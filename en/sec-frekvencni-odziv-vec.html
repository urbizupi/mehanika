Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.10 Harmonic excitation and the frequency response matrix (harmonično vzbujanje in matrika frekvenčnega odziva)

We attach a shaker (stresalnik) to the upper floor of frame A from section 10.1, a machine that pushes it with the force \(100\ \text{N}\cdot\cos\Omega t\). The frame has two rigid floors of mass \(m = 500\) kg, each storey has stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, so \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and \(\mat K = k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), the coordinate \(q_1\) is the displacement of the lower floor and \(q_2\) that of the upper floor. The damping is Rayleigh damping (definition 10.45) with damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) in both modes, \(a_0 = 0{,}56668\) 1/s and \(a_1 = 5{,}6469\cdot 10^{-4}\) s (section 10.9). The natural frequencies of the frame are \(3{,}116\) and \(8{,}158\) Hz. We ask two questions. How far does each floor move at each shaker frequency? And why does one of the floors stand still at some frequency although we push it with \(100\) N?

For a single oscillator in section 9.6 we obtained the response with the complex notation. The cosine is the real part of a complex exponential function, and the derivative of that function is only a product with \(\mathrm{i}\Omega\) (theorem 9.28), so the differential equation for \(q = Z\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) turns into an ordinary equation for the number \(Z\). We do the same with a column. A complex column (kompleksen stolpec) is a column with complex elements, a complex matrix is a matrix with complex elements, and we compute the product as for real ones (definition 1.98), with addition and multiplication of complex numbers. The real part of a column \(\vek z\) is the column of the real parts of its elements, \(\mathrm{Re}\,\vek z\), and the derivative of a column is the column of derivatives. For a real matrix \(\mat A\) we have \(\mathrm{Re}(\mat A\vek z) = \mat A\,\mathrm{Re}\,\vek z\), because the element \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}z_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a sum of products of real and complex numbers and its real part is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{Re}\,z_{\mathrm{j}}\). We try \(\vek q = \vek z\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) with a constant complex column \(\vek z\). Each derivative multiplies by \(\mathrm{i}\Omega\), and the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) becomes \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M + \mathrm{i}\Omega\mat C)\,\vek z = \vek Q_0\). For \(n = 1\) the matrix in brackets has the form \(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c\,\Omega\), which is the characteristic polynomial from section 9.6 at \(s = \mathrm{i}\Omega\). Its inverse gets a name.

Definition 10.51 Frequency response matrix (matrika frekvenčnega odziva)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) be real matrices of order \(n\) as in theorem 10.2 and \(\Omega > 0\) a circular frequency. The complex matrix of order \(n\)

\[\mat S(\Omega) = \mat K - \Omega^2\mat M + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat C \tag{10.65}\]

is the dynamic stiffness matrix (dinamična togostna matrika). If it has an inverse matrix (definition 1.107), the frequency response matrix is

\[\mat H(\Omega) = \mat S(\Omega)^{-1} = \big(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat C\big)^{-1} . \tag{10.66}\]

It is a complex matrix of order \(n\), its element \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(\Omega)\) has the unit of the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) divided by the unit of the force \(Q_{\mathrm{j}}\), for displacements m/N. For \(n = 1\) it is the frequency response of definition 9.51.

Source: GR, equations (2.80) to (2.83) (PDF p. 102): GR writes the matrix \(\mat K - \omega^2\mat M\) without damping and calls it the impedance matrix or dynamic stiffness matrix, and its inverse the admittance matrix or dynamic flexibility. The term with \(\mat C\) is not from the source. CK, equation (11.31) (PDF p. 350) gives the element \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) as a sum over the modes, as in theorem 10.53.

When does the inverse matrix exist? We wrote the proof of lemma 8.21 on a matrix with a full set of pivots for real matrices, but it uses only addition, multiplication and division by a number that is not zero (in step 2 we divide by \(U_{\mathrm{kk}}\)), and these operations obey the same rules for complex numbers (lemma 9.20). So the lemma holds word for word also for complex matrices and columns, including the last claim about the transposed matrix. We use it for \(\mat S(\Omega)\).

Lemma 10.52 Existence of the frequency response matrix (obstoj matrike frekvenčnega odziva)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric positive definite, \(\mat K\) a symmetric and \(\mat C\) a symmetric positive semidefinite real matrix of order \(n\) and \(\Omega > 0\). The matrix \(\mat S(\Omega)\) has no inverse matrix exactly when there is a real column \(\vek\varphi \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \Omega^2\mat M\vek\varphi\) and \(\mat C\vek\varphi = \vek 0\).

Proof

We must show both directions. For the inverse we use lemma 8.21 for complex matrices, and for the other direction the real and imaginary parts of the equation \(\mat S\vek w = \vek 0\).

  1. If \(\vek\varphi\) is real, \(\vek\varphi \neq \vek 0\), \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \Omega^2\mat M\vek\varphi\) and \(\mat C\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), then \(\mat S\vek\varphi = \mat K\vek\varphi - \Omega^2\mat M\vek\varphi + \mathrm{i}\Omega\mat C\vek\varphi = \vek 0\). If \(\mat S\) had an inverse matrix, we would have \(\vek\varphi = \mat S^{-1}\mat S\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), which is false, so it has none.
  2. Conversely let \(\mat S\) have no inverse matrix. By lemma 8.21 there is a complex column \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat S\vek w = \vek 0\). Write \(\vek w = \vek x + \mathrm{i}\vek y\) with real columns \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\), not both zero.
  3. Multiply \(\mat S\vek w = \vek 0\) from the left by the row \(\bar{\vek w}^\T = \vek x^\T - \mathrm{i}\vek y^\T\). For a real symmetric matrix \(\mat A\) we have \(\bar{\vek w}^\T\mat A\vek w = \vek x^\T\mat A\vek x + \vek y^\T\mat A\vek y + \mathrm{i}\,(\vek x^\T\mat A\vek y - \vek y^\T\mat A\vek x)\) and the last bracket is zero, because \(\vek y^\T\mat A\vek x = (\vek x^\T\mat A^\T\vek y)^\T = \vek x^\T\mat A\vek y\) is a number. Hence
    \[0 = \big(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x + \vek y^\T\mat K\vek y\big) - \Omega^2\big(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x + \vek y^\T\mat M\vek y\big) + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\big(\vek x^\T\mat C\vek x + \vek y^\T\mat C\vek y\big) .\]
  4. The imaginary part is zero: \(\Omega\,(\vek x^\T\mat C\vek x + \vek y^\T\mat C\vek y) = 0\). Since \(\Omega > 0\) and both terms are nonnegative (\(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite), \(\vek x^\T\mat C\vek x = 0 = \vek y^\T\mat C\vek y\), so by lemma 7.16 \(\mat C\vek x = \vek 0\) and \(\mat C\vek y = \vek 0\).
  5. Therefore \(\mat S\vek w = (\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)\vek w = \vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M\) is real, so its product with \(\vek w\) splits into the real part \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)\vek x\) and the imaginary part \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)\vek y\), both zero. One of \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\) is not zero, and it is the wanted \(\vek\varphi\).

Source: The lemma is not from a source.

The lemma says when the frequency response matrix is missing. Without damping (\(\mat C = \mat 0\)) it is missing at \(\Omega^2 = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is at every natural frequency, which is resonance. With a damper it is missing only if the damper has no force on a natural mode, \(\mat C\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). For system A with Rayleigh damping \(\mat C\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (a_0 + a_1\lambda_{\mathrm{r}})\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \neq \vek 0\), so \(\mat H(\Omega)\) exists at every frequency.

What does the matrix tell us about the motion? The answer is a theorem. It states that the response to a harmonic force is the complex product \(\mat H\vek Q_0\), that every initial condition in addition dies out, and that the element \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equals \(H_{\mathrm{ji}}\).

Theorem 10.53 Response to a harmonic force with several degrees of freedom (odziv na harmonično silo pri več prostostnih stopnjah)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) be as in lemma 10.52, \(\vek Q_0\) a complex column with \(n\) elements, \(\Omega > 0\), and let \(\mat S(\Omega)\) have an inverse matrix. For the equation

\[\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \mathrm{Re}\big(\vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) \tag{10.67}\]

the following holds:

  1. The column \(\vek q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\mat H(\Omega)\,\vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big)\) is a solution.
  2. Every solution of equation (10.67) is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{p}}\) plus a solution of the free equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x = \vek 0\), and every such sum is a solution.
  3. The matrix \(\mat H(\Omega)\) is symmetric, \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = H_{\mathrm{ji}}\).
  4. Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite as well, \(\mat C\) classical damping (definition 10.44) with all ratios \(0 < \zeta_{\mathrm{r}} < 1\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}\). Then \(\mat H(\Omega)\) exists at every \(\Omega > 0\),
    \[\mat H(\Omega) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{n} \frac{\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T}{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Omega} , \tag{10.68}\]
    and for every solution \(\vek q\) of equation (10.67) we have \(\vek q(t) - \vek q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) \to \vek 0\) as \(t \to \infty\).
Proof

We must show that the complex column solves the complex equation, that its real part solves the real one, that the difference of two solutions is a free motion, and that the matrix is symmetric. Then the modal matrix \(\mat\Phi\) diagonalizes \(\mat S\), and the free motion dies out in modal coordinates.

  1. Let \(\vek z(t) = \mat H(\Omega)\,\vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\). By theorem 9.28 \(\dot{\vek z} = \mathrm{i}\Omega\,\vek z\) and \(\ddot{\vek z} = -\Omega^2\vek z\), since the derivative of a column is the column of derivatives and the constant complex factor stays.
  2. Substitute: \(\mat M\ddot{\vek z} + \mat C\dot{\vek z} + \mat K\vek z = (\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M + \mathrm{i}\Omega\mat C)\,\vek z = \mat S\,\mat H\,\vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = \vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), because \(\mat S\mat H = \mat I\).
  3. The matrices \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\), \(\mat K\) are real, so the real part of the left side equals \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{p}} + \mat C\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{p}} + \mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{p}}\) with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{Re}\,\vek z\) (the real part of a product with a real matrix is the matrix times the real part, as we said before the definition, and the real part of a derivative is the derivative of the real part, definition 9.29). The real part of the right side is the force. This proves point 1.
  4. If \(\vek q\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{p}}\) are solutions, the difference \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is a solution of the free equation, because the left side is linear in \(\vek q\). Conversely the sum \(\vek q_{\mathrm{p}} + \vek x\) is a solution if \(\vek x\) is a solution of the free equation. This is point 2.
  5. The matrices \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\) are symmetric, so \(\mat S^\T = \mat S\). By the last claim of lemma 8.21 (for complex matrices) \(\mat H = \mat S^{-1} = (\mat S^\T)^{-1} = (\mat S^{-1})^\T = \mat H^\T\). This is point 3.
  6. For point 4 we use the modal matrix of definition 10.15, \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}})\) (lemma 10.16), and the classical damping \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_1\omega_1, \dots, 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). Then \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat S\mat\Phi = \mat B\) with diagonal elements \(B_{\mathrm{rr}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Omega\). The imaginary part \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Omega\) is positive, so \(B_{\mathrm{rr}} \neq 0\) and \(\mat B\) has the inverse matrix \(\mat B^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(1/B_{\mathrm{rr}})\).
  7. From \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\) (lemma 10.16) we get \(\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^\T\mat M = \mat I\), so \(\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^\T = \mat M^{-1}\) and \(\mat M\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^\T = \mat I\). Multiply \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat S\mat\Phi = \mat B\) from the left by \(\mat M\mat\Phi\). We get \(\mat S\mat\Phi = \mat M\mat\Phi\mat B\).
  8. Let \(\mat X = \mat\Phi\mat B^{-1}\mat\Phi^\T\). By step 7, \(\mat S\mat X = \mat M\mat\Phi\,\mat B\mat B^{-1}\mat\Phi^\T = \mat M\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^\T = \mat I\). The matrix \(\mat X\) is symmetric, because \(\mat B^{-1}\) is diagonal, so by lemma 1.101 \(\mat X\mat S = (\mat S^\T\mat X^\T)^\T = (\mat S\mat X)^\T = \mat I\). So \(\mat S\) is invertible with inverse matrix \(\mat X\) at every \(\Omega > 0\). The element \((i, j)\) of \(\mat X\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} \varphi_{\mathrm{ir}}\varphi_{\mathrm{jr}}/B_{\mathrm{rr}}\), which is (10.68).
  9. Let \(\vek q\) be a solution, \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_{\mathrm{p}}\) the free motion by point 2 and \(\vek\eta = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\vek x\) its modal coordinates. By theorem 10.49 (at zero force) every solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) solves \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), which is the free motion of an oscillator with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\). By theorem 9.52, point 3, \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t) \to 0\). Hence \(\vek x = \mat\Phi\vek\eta = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}} \to \vek 0\).

Source: GR, equations (2.80) to (2.85) (PDF pp. 102-103): the frequency response matrix and its expansion in the undamped modes. CK, equations (11.30) and (11.31) (PDF pp. 349-350): \(\overline{H}_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \phi_{\mathrm{ir}}\phi_{\mathrm{jr}}/K_{\mathrm{r}} \cdot 1/((1 - r_{\mathrm{r}}^2) + \mathrm{i}\,2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} r_{\mathrm{r}})\), in our case with modes normalized by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\), where \(K_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) is the modal stiffness. The symmetry in step 5 and the convergence in step 9 are not from the source.

The proof showed three things. The steady response is a single cosine in each coordinate with amplitude \(|(\mat H\vek Q_0)_{\mathrm{i}}|\) and phase \(\arg(\mat H\vek Q_0)_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the phasor of the displacement in coordinate \(i\) is the complex number \((\mat H\vek Q_0)_{\mathrm{i}}\) (phasors from section 9.6). The free motion that joins the steady response because of the initial values dies out, because each mode is an oscillator from chapter 9 with its own damping ratio. And the matrix \(\mat H\) is a sum of \(n\) simple terms, one for each mode. For a force \(F\) on coordinate \(j\) the amplitude of coordinate \(i\) is \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}|\,F\), so we call \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the response of coordinate \(i\) to a unit force in coordinate \(j\) at frequency \(\Omega\).

The symmetry \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = H_{\mathrm{ji}}\) says that the response of coordinate \(i\) to a force in \(j\) equals the response of coordinate \(j\) to the same force in \(i\), in amplitude and in phase. This is dynamic reciprocity (dinamična vzajemnost), the vibration form of Maxwell's theorem (theorem 4.140), which Rayleigh extended, as the history box in chapter 4 says. At \(\Omega = 0\) we indeed have \(\mat H(0) = \mat K^{-1}\), the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) of lemma 10.3, and the symmetry is the symmetry of the flexibility matrix. For system A, \(\mat H(0) = \frac{1}{k}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}9930 & 1{,}9930 \\ 1{,}9930 & 3{,}9860 \end{bmatrix}\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N. At \(\Omega = \omega_1 = 19{,}578\) 1/s we have \(|H_{12}| = |H_{21}| = 58{,}330\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N and the difference \(|H_{12} - H_{21}|\) is \(7\cdot 10^{-21}\) m/N, which is the rounding error of the computer (numerical computation).

Example 10.54 Frame A under a shaker on the upper floor (okvir A pri stresalniku na zgornji plošči)

The force \(100\ \text{N}\cdot\cos\Omega t\) acts on the upper floor, so \(\vek Q_0 = (0, 100)^\T\) N. By theorem 10.53 the amplitudes of the floors are \(|H_{12}|\cdot 100\ \text{N}\) and \(|H_{22}|\cdot 100\ \text{N}\), and the lag of the upper floor is \(-\arg H_{22}\). The computation with \(\mat H = (\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M + \mathrm{i}\Omega\mat C)^{-1}\) (numerical computation) gives:

\(f = \Omega/(2\pi)\) (Hz) \(\vert q_1\vert\) (mm) \(\vert q_2\vert\) (mm) lag of \(q_2\)
\(0\) \(0{,}19930\) \(0{,}39860\) \(0^\circ\)
\(1{,}558\) \(0{,}27569\) \(0{,}52506\) \(1{,}48^\circ\)
\(3{,}116 = \omega_1/(2\pi)\) \(5{,}8330\) \(9{,}4394\) \(89{,}85^\circ\)
\(5{,}042\) \(0{,}19901\) \(0{,}19927\) \(176{,}93^\circ\)
\(7{,}130\) \(0{,}19717\) \(0{,}01496\) \(97{,}19^\circ\)
\(8{,}158 = \omega_2/(2\pi)\) \(0{,}85133\) \(0{,}53109\) \(96{,}97^\circ\)
\(12{,}237\) \(0{,}01105\) \(0{,}04298\) \(178{,}55^\circ\)
  1. At \(f = 0\) the force is slow, \(\mat H(0) = \mat K^{-1}\) and the floors move by the static displacements \(0{,}19930\) and \(0{,}39860\) mm, because each storey carries \(100\) N, so the lower one moves \(100/k = 0{,}19930\) mm and the upper one by as much again (matrix \(\mat D\) from section 10.1).
  2. At \(f = 3{,}116\) Hz, that is at \(\omega_1\), the upper floor swings by \(9{,}4394\) mm, which is \(23{,}7\) times the static displacement \(0{,}39860\) mm. The ratio \(|q_1|/|q_2| = 0{,}6179\) is almost exactly the ratio of the components of the first mode \(1/1{,}618034 = 0{,}6180\): near resonance the sum in equation (10.68) is dominated by the term \(r = 1\), which is \(\vek\varphi_1\,(\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek Q_0)\) times a scalar, so it has the shape of the mode, no matter where the shaker stands.
  3. At \(f = 8{,}158\) Hz the same holds for the second mode. The ratio of magnitudes \(|q_1|/|q_2| = 1{,}603\) is close to the ratio of the components of the second mode \(1{,}618\) and the floors move almost in opposite directions (the phases differ by \(189{,}7^\circ\)). The peak is lower, \(0{,}531\) mm, because the term \(r = 2\) equals \(\varphi_{22}^2/(2\zeta\lambda_2)\cdot 100\ \text{N} = 0{,}526\) mm, with \(\varphi_{22}^2 = 5{,}53\cdot 10^{-4}\) 1/kg instead of \(\varphi_{21}^2 = 1{,}447\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/kg and \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\) 1/s\(^2\) instead of \(383{,}31\) 1/s\(^2\). The rest up to \(0{,}531\) mm is added by the term of the first mode, which at \(\omega_2\) is real and adds at a right angle to the purely imaginary term \(r = 2\).
  4. At \(f = 12{,}237\) Hz, \(1{,}5\,\omega_2\), the excitation is above both frequencies, and the upper floor lags by almost \(180^\circ\) and moves only \(0{,}043\) mm. The inertia of the floors dominates the springs, as for the oscillator at \(r = 2\) in section 9.6.

At each resonance, then, one mode determines the motion. How exactly? We estimate the peak of the upper floor at \(\omega_1\) with a single term of the sum (10.68). The term \(r = 1\) at \(\Omega = \omega_1\) is purely imaginary, \(\varphi_{21}^2/(2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta\omega_1^2)\), with \(\varphi_{21} = 0{,}038042\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) its magnitude is \(\varphi_{21}^2/(2\zeta\lambda_1) = 0{,}0014472/(2\cdot 0{,}02\cdot 383{,}31) = 9{,}4389\cdot 10^{-5}\) m/N, which is \(9{,}4389\) mm at \(100\) N. The term of the second mode at \(\Omega = \omega_1\) is almost real, \(2{,}46\cdot 10^{-7}\) m/N, and adds to the first at a right angle in the complex plane, so it adds only \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\) relatively to the magnitude. The full value \(|H_{22}(\omega_1)|\cdot 100\ \text{N}\) is \(9{,}4394\) mm, and the largest value over all frequencies is \(9{,}4418\) mm, because the peak is at \(3{,}1146\) Hz, a little below \(\omega_1\): there the real part of the term of the first mode is positive like the term of the second mode, and the two add. The estimate with one term is smaller than the largest value by \(0{,}03\ \%\).

Let us look at the second question. In the table at \(7{,}130\) Hz the upper floor is almost at rest (\(0{,}01496\) mm), and the lower one moves \(0{,}19717\) mm, although the shaker pushes the upper one with \(100\) N. At \(5{,}042\) Hz the lower floor would stand still if we put the shaker on it. The frequency at which the principal element \(H_{\mathrm{kk}}\) is zero is called the antiresonance (antiresonanca) of coordinate \(k\). For two degrees of freedom we find it directly from the inverse of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix (lemma 4.49).

Theorem 10.55 Antiresonance of a system with two degrees of freedom (antiresonanca sistema z dvema prostostnima stopnjama)

Let \(n = 2\), \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(m_1, m_2)\) with \(m_1, m_2 > 0\), \(\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} k_{11} & k_{12} \\ k_{12} & k_{22} \end{bmatrix}\) positive definite with \(k_{12} \neq 0\) and \(\mat C = \mat 0\). Let \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\) be the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) (theorem 8.125) and \(\Omega > 0\) with \(\Omega^2 \neq \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). With \(x = \Omega^2\) and \(\Delta(x) = (k_{11} - m_1x)(k_{22} - m_2x) - k_{12}^2\) the following holds:

  1. \(\mat H(\Omega) = \dfrac{1}{\Delta(x)}\begin{bmatrix} k_{22} - m_2x & -k_{12} \\ -k_{12} & k_{11} - m_1x \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\Delta(x) = m_1m_2\,(x - \lambda_1)(x - \lambda_2)\).
  2. The element \(H_{12}\) has no zero. The element \(H_{11}\) is zero exactly at \(x = k_{22}/m_2\) and \(H_{22}\) exactly at \(x = k_{11}/m_1\).
  3. \(\lambda_1 < k_{22}/m_2 < \lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_1 < k_{11}/m_1 < \lambda_2\).
  4. On \(\lambda_1 < x < \lambda_2\) we have \(\Delta < 0\), and \(H_{11}\) is negative at \(x < k_{22}/m_2\), positive at \(x > k_{22}/m_2\) and tends to \(-\infty\) as \(x \to \lambda_1\) and to \(+\infty\) as \(x \to \lambda_2\). The same holds for \(H_{22}\) with \(k_{11}/m_1\).
Proof

We must show the form of the matrix, the places of the zeros and their position between the eigenvalues.

  1. The dynamic stiffness matrix is \(\mat S = \begin{bmatrix} k_{11} - m_1x & k_{12} \\ k_{12} & k_{22} - m_2x \end{bmatrix}\) and its determinant (definition 2.50) is \(\Delta(x)\). The coefficient at \(x^2\) is \(m_1m_2\). The eigenvalues solve \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M) = 0\) (theorem 8.125), that is \(\Delta(\lambda) = 0\). For \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\) there would not be two different roots, but \(\Delta(k_{22}/m_2) = -k_{12}^2 < 0\) for \(k_{12} \neq 0\) and \(m_1m_2 > 0\) means that \(\Delta\) has two different real roots, so \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2\) and \(\Delta = m_1m_2(x - \lambda_1)(x - \lambda_2)\). Because \(\Omega^2 \neq \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) we have \(\Delta \neq 0\).
  2. By lemma 4.49 \(\mat H = \mat S^{-1}\) is the matrix of point 1.
  3. The numerators in \(H_{11}\) and \(H_{22}\) are \(k_{22} - m_2x\) and \(k_{11} - m_1x\), and the numerator in \(H_{12}\) is \(-k_{12} \neq 0\) and constant. So \(H_{12}\) has no zero, \(H_{11}\) is zero exactly at \(x = k_{22}/m_2\) and \(H_{22}\) at \(x = k_{11}/m_1\). This is point 2.
  4. The parabola \(\Delta\) has a positive leading coefficient, so it is negative exactly between the roots. Since \(\Delta(k_{22}/m_2) = -k_{12}^2 < 0\) by step 1 and \(\Delta(k_{11}/m_1) = -k_{12}^2 < 0\), the numbers \(k_{22}/m_2\) and \(k_{11}/m_1\) lie between \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), strictly, where point 3 of theorem 8.125 states only inequalities. This is point 3.
  5. On \(\lambda_1 < x < \lambda_2\) we have \(\Delta < 0\), so \(H_{11}\) has the sign of the numerator with the opposite sign. The numerator \(k_{22} - m_2x\) is positive at \(x < k_{22}/m_2\) and negative at \(x > k_{22}/m_2\), so \(H_{11}\) is first negative and then positive, with the zero at \(x = k_{22}/m_2\). As \(x \to \lambda_1\), \(\Delta \to 0\) from the negative side and the numerator is positive by point 3, so \(H_{11} \to -\infty\), as \(x \to \lambda_2\) the numerator is negative, so \(H_{11} \to +\infty\). For \(H_{22}\) the computation is the same. This is point 4.

Source: GR, §2.6.2 and example 2.7 (PDF pp. 103-105): for any order GR states that the principal element \(h_{\mathrm{kk}}\) increases throughout with \(\omega^2\) and that an antiresonance lies between two resonances, and that the antiresonances are the natural frequencies of the system with the coordinate \(k\) held. The proof for \(n = 2\) is not from the source.

The proof showed that between two resonances the element \(H_{11}\) changes sign, at a frequency that the system itself determines. Which frequency is it? The quantity \(k_{22}/m_2\) is the eigenvalue of the system in which we hold coordinate \(1\), since the mass \(m_2\) remains on a spring of stiffness \(k_{22}\) (the stiffness \(k_{22}\) is the force in coordinate \(2\) that holds \(q_2 = 1\) at \(q_1 = 0\), as in section 10.1). Similarly \(k_{11}/m_1\) is the eigenvalue of the system with coordinate \(2\) held. At the antiresonance of coordinate \(1\) the mass \(m_2\) therefore oscillates as if free and its spring force on the mass \(m_1\) exactly balances the shaker, so the mass \(m_1\) stands still. For general order the same picture is given by the sum (10.68) at \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\): the derivative of the principal element with respect to \(x = \Omega^2\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} \varphi_{\mathrm{kr}}^2/(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - x)^2 > 0\), so between neighbouring resonances at which \(\varphi_{\mathrm{kr}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{r}+1}\) are not zero the element goes from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\) and crosses zero exactly once. We do not prove this for \(n > 2\), but GR states it and calls it the antiresonance of coordinate \(k\).

Example 10.56 Antiresonances of frame A (antiresonanci okvira A)

For A we have \(m_1 = m_2 = m\), \(k_{11} = 2k\), \(k_{12} = -k\), \(k_{22} = k\), so the antiresonance of coordinate \(1\) lies at \(x = k/m\) and that of coordinate \(2\) at \(x = 2k/m\), that is at \(\Omega = 31{,}678\) 1/s (\(5{,}042\) Hz) and \(\Omega = 44{,}800\) 1/s (\(7{,}130\) Hz). The eigenvalues are \((3 \mp \sqrt5)/2\cdot k/m = 0{,}382\,k/m\) and \(2{,}618\,k/m\) (section 10.2) and both antiresonances lie between them, as point 3 of the theorem claims.

  1. At \(x = k/m\) we have \(\Delta = (2k - k)(k - k) - k^2 = -k^2\) and \(\mat H = \frac{1}{-k^2}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & k \\ k & k \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{k}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\). The force \(100\) N on the lower floor gives \(\vek q = \mat H\,(100, 0)^\T\,\text{N} = (0, -100\ \text{N}/k)^\T = (0, -0{,}19930)^\T\) mm: the lower floor stands still, the upper one moves by \(-0{,}19930\) mm. The spring between the floors is then shortened by \(0{,}19930\) mm and pushes the lower floor with the force \(k\,(q_2 - q_1) = -100\) N, exactly against the shaker. The upper storey acts as a tuned damper of the lower one, because the free upper mass on the spring \(k\) has exactly the frequency \(\sqrt{k/m}\). We shall use the same idea in the next section.
  2. At \(x = 2k/m\) we again have \(\Delta = 0\cdot(-k) - k^2 = -k^2\) and \(\mat H = \frac{1}{k}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\). The force on the upper floor gives \(\vek q = (-100/k, 0)^\T\): the upper floor stands still, the lower one moves by \(-0{,}19930\) mm. The lower storey, held by both springs with the total stiffness \(2k\), has as a free system the frequency \(\sqrt{2k/m}\) and we excite it exactly at it. The same value \(2007{,}04\) 1/s\(^2\) is the eigenvalue of the frame with the upper floor held, which we met in corollary 10.29 on constraints.
  3. At \(x = k/m\) and a force on the upper floor, however, by step 1 \(\vek q = (-1, -1)^\T\cdot 100\ \text{N}/k\): both floors move equally and the upper spring is not stretched. The antiresonance is not a property of the frequency alone, but of the coordinate on which the force acts.
  4. With damping (\(\zeta = 0{,}02\)) the antiresonance does not reach zero. For a force on the lower floor \(|H_{11}(5{,}042\ \text{Hz})| = 0{,}0712\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N, which is \(3{,}6\ \%\) of the static value \(1{,}9930\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N, and the lower floor moves \(0{,}00712\) mm. For a force on the upper floor \(|H_{22}(7{,}130\ \text{Hz})| = 0{,}1496\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N, which is \(3{,}8\ \%\) of the static value \(3{,}9860\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N (table, \(0{,}01496\) mm).

The figures show the same as a function of frequency. Figure 10.31 shows \(|H_{12}|\) and \(|H_{22}|\) on logarithmic axes, figure 10.32 the phases, and figure 10.33 the shapes that the floors take at five frequencies from the table.

Figure 10.33 tells what the shaker does to the frame. At \(3{,}12\) and \(8{,}16\) Hz the frame sways in the shape of a mode, at \(5{,}04\) Hz the floors move as one mass, because the upper spring remains unstretched (step 3 of example 10.56), and at \(7{,}13\) Hz only the lower floor moves. The frequency response is therefore a map of the system: the peaks are its modes, the valleys its subsystems with one coordinate held, and the low frequencies its static flexibility \(\mat K^{-1}\).
