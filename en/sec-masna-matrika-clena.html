Chapter 28 Flexible robot links

28.4 The mass matrix of the link

In section 28.3 we wrote the velocity of every point of the link, \(\dot{\vek r} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot\theta\,\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p} + \mat R\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) (lemma 28.8). Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.99), however, need the kinetic energy as a function of the coordinates and their derivatives on the way to the equations of motion. For a system of rigid bodies it is the quadratic form \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}\) with a mass matrix (theorem 8.97). What matrix do we get for a link that both turns and bends? A comparison with the rigid body (theorem 8.92) predicts two things: the mass of the link does not change, but its inertia changes with the bend, and the turning of the frame is coupled to the elastic coordinates.

This section uses the following idealizations of the model.

  1. The link is a straight bar with the axis \(\bar x \in [0, L]\) in the undeformed state. Its mass is distributed along the axis with the mass per unit length \(\mu(\bar x) = \rho A\), which is continuous and positive (we simply write \(\mu\) and write the variable as \(x\)). We neglect the rotary inertia of the cross-sections. By lemma 13.36 the two parts of the kinetic energy are \(\tfrac12\int\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (the motion of the cross-sections) and \(\tfrac12\int\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (their turning). In a vibration in a mode with the shape \(\varphi(x)\) the ratio of the second to the first is \(\dfrac{I}{A}\,\dfrac{\int\varphi'^2\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int\varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x} = \dfrac{h^2}{12}\,\dfrac{\int\varphi'^2\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int\varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x}\), where \(h\) is the section height in the plane of motion. For the steel strip of example A (\(h = 6\) mm, \(L = 1\) m) the quotient of the integrals is \(4{,}648/L^2\) at the first mode of the cantilever and \(142{,}9/L^2\) at the fourth, and the ratio is \(1{,}4\cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(4{,}3\cdot 10^{-4}\). So the idealization is good for low modes.
  2. The position of a point of the axis is \(\vek r = \vek o + \mat R(\theta)\,(\bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) by definition 28.4 in the floating frame (plavajoči koordinatni sistem, definition 28.3), where \(\bar{\vek r}_0 = (x, 0)\) is the position of the point at \(x\) in the undeformed state, and the matrix \(\mat\Psi(x)\) with \(2\) rows and \(n\) columns is the collection of shapes, known from the trial functions of the Ritz method (definition 6.32). The coordinates of the link are therefore \(\vek o\) (two numbers), \(\theta\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (\(n\) numbers), \(n + 3\) in all. The subscript \(d\) stands for deformation, because \(f\) is used in chapter 7 for the free unknowns.
  3. The matrix \(\mat J = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) is the quarter turn of section 28.3. It is not a moment of inertia: we write the moments of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\).

For a general planar link \(\mat\Psi\) has the rows \(\mat\Psi_1\) (displacements along the axis of the frame) and \(\mat\Psi_2\) (displacements perpendicular to the axis), each with \(n\) functions. For an Euler-Bernoulli link with an inextensible axis (definition 5.14) we have \(\mat\Psi_1 = \vek 0\) and \(\mat\Psi_2 = \vek\psi^\T\), where \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) are continuous functions on \([0, L]\) and the displacement of the axis is \(v(x) = \vek\psi(x)^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). The axis stays along the frame at its own \(x\). This is an idealization: the bent axis is longer than \(L\) by \(\int_0^L\big(\sqrt{1 + v'^2} - 1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), which is at most \(\tfrac12\int_0^L v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) by the inequality \(\sqrt{1 + p^2} - 1 \le p^2/2\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.149). For a slope \(|v'| \le 0{,}03\) this is no more than \(4{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,L\). The correction is of second order and this section does not need it, but at fast spin it becomes important.

We build figure 28.30 as one would draw it on a board. First the axes of the frame: \(\bar x\) along the link and \(\bar y\) perpendicular to it, with the origin at the hub. On them we place the undeformed axis, the dashed line of length \(L\). The link is clamped in the hub, so the shape is clamped at \(x = 0\) too: \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\). These are the reference conditions of the clamped frame (definition 28.5). The bent link is the graph of the function \(v(x)\). We choose the point at \(x\) to see what velocity is assigned to it. The frame turns with the angular velocity \(\dot\theta\), so the point at the position vector \(\bar{\vek p}\) from the hub moves with the velocity \(\dot\theta\,\mat J\bar{\vek p}\), perpendicular to \(\bar{\vek p}\) (every point of a rigid body circles about the centre of rotation, section 28.3). This is the orange arrow, in the frame \((-\dot\theta\,v,\ \dot\theta\,x)\), and it is almost perpendicular to the axis. At the same time the point moves relative to the frame because of the bend: the blue arrow \(\psi(x)\,\dot q\) is perpendicular to the axis. Both velocities point the same way, so their sum is longer than either alone, and the square of the length of the sum is not the sum of the squares. The difference is the mixed product \(2\,\dot\theta\,x\,\psi(x)\,\dot q\). The sum of this mixed product over the points of the link is the coupling of turning and bending, which a rigid body does not have.

The square of the length of the velocity has six terms. With the notation \(\bar{\vek p} = \bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) they are

\[|\dot{\vek r}|^2 = |\dot{\vek o}|^2 + \dot\theta^2\,|\bar{\vek p}|^2 + |\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}|^2 + 2\,\dot\theta\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p} + 2\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} - 2\,\dot\theta\,\bar{\vek p}^\T\mat J\,\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} . \tag{28.17}\]

Each term is a product of velocities of coordinates, which do not depend on \(x\), and a function of \(x\) that we must integrate along the link with the weight \(\mu\). The right half of figure 28.30 shows three such functions for the shape \(\psi\) on the axis \(\xi = x/L\) from \(0\) to \(1\): \(x^2\) from the term with \(\dot\theta^2\), \(x\,\psi\) from the mixed term and \(\psi^2\) from the term with \(\dot q^2\). The area under the orange curve is the integral of the mixed term. All three areas are numbers that we compute once, because they do not depend on the coordinates. We collect all such integrals and name them.

Definition 28.12 Inertia shape integrals (vztrajnostni integrali oblike)

Let the link have the mass per unit length \(\mu(x) > 0\) on \([0, L]\), the undeformed position of the point \(\bar{\vek r}_0(x) = (x, 0)\) and the shape \(\mat\Psi(x)\), a matrix with two rows and \(n\) columns whose entries are continuous functions. The inertia shape integrals of the link are

\[\begin{aligned} m &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mathrm{d}x , & \vek s_0 &= \int_0^L \mu\,\bar{\vek r}_0\,\mathrm{d}x = (s, 0) , & J_{\mathrm{link}} &= \int_0^L \mu\,x^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ \mat S &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mat\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x , & \vek c &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mat\Psi^\T\bar{\vek r}_0\,\mathrm{d}x , & \vek b &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mat\Psi^\T\mat J\,\bar{\vek r}_0\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ \mat Z &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mat\Psi^\T\mat J\mat\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x , & \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} &= \int_0^L \mu\,\mat\Psi^\T\mat\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x , \end{aligned} \tag{28.18}\]

where \(s = \int_0^L\mu\,x\,\mathrm{d}x\). For a transverse shape \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\), \(\vek b\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) are the areas under the curves \(x^2\), \(x\,\psi\) and \(\psi^2\) in figure 28.30, each with the weight \(\mu\). The numbers are \(m\) (unit kg), \(s\) (kg m) and \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\) (kg m\(^2\)), \(\vek s_0\) is a vector with two components, \(\mat S\) is a \(2 \times n\) matrix, \(\vek c\) and \(\vek b\) are columns with \(n\) entries, \(\mat Z\) is an \(n \times n\) matrix (antisymmetric, \(\mat Z^\T = -\mat Z\), because \(\mat J^\T = -\mat J\)) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. For a link with a transverse shape (\(\mat\Psi_1 = \vek 0\), \(\mat\Psi_2 = \vek\psi^\T\)) we have \(\vek c = \vek 0\), \(\mat Z = \mat 0\), the first row of \(\mat S\) is zero and the second is \(\int\mu\,\vek\psi^\T\mathrm{d}x\), and

\[\vek b = \int_0^L \mu\,x\,\vek\psi\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \int_0^L \mu\,\vek\psi\vek\psi^\T\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{28.19}\]

Source: SHABANA (5.94) and (5.95) (PDF p. 219): the same set of integrals for a planar body, written with volume integrals. The notation with the right-angle matrix \(\mat J\) and with integrals along the link is ours, because the link has its mass on the axis.

The names have reasons. The integrals are called inertia integrals because they measure inertia: mass, moment of mass and moment of inertia. They are called shape integrals because besides the distribution of mass they contain the shape \(\mat\Psi\), so we cannot compute them until the shape is chosen. The vector \(\vek s_0\) is the moment of mass (moment mase): mass multiplied by the lever arm and summed along the link, just as the moment of a force is force times lever arm. The quantity \(s/m\) is the position of the centre of mass of the link on the axis, because the centre of mass is the average of the position with the weight of mass. The column \(\vek b\) tells how strongly the turning of the frame "drives" each shape: \(\mat J\,\bar{\vek r}_0 = (0, x)\) is the direction and size of the velocity that turning with unit angular velocity gives, and \(\mat\Psi^\T\) maps it onto each shape, which for a transverse shape is the orange integrand in figure 28.30. All three curves have the value \(1\) at \(\xi = 1\), because \(\psi(L) = 1\).

For the steel strip of example A (from section 28.1: \(L = 1\) m, \(\mu = 1{,}872\) kg/m) and the static shape \(\psi = (3\xi^2 - \xi^3)/2\), which is the deflection curve of a cantilever under a force at the free end, normalized to a unit end displacement (theorem 5.57), we have \(m = 1{,}872\) kg, \(s = 0{,}9360\) kg m, \(J_{\mathrm{link}} = 0{,}6240\) kg m\(^2\), \(\vek b = \mu L^2\cdot 0{,}275 = 0{,}5148\) kg m (area \(0{,}275\) in the figure), \(M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \mu L\cdot 0{,}2357 = 0{,}4413\) kg and \(\int\mu\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}7020\) kg. The unit of \(\vek b\) is kg m, because \(\psi\) has no unit and is multiplied by the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{d}}\) in metres.

Now we write the kinetic energy with them.

Theorem 28.13 Kinetic energy of a flexible link (kinetična energija člena)

Let the link be as in definition 28.12 with the coordinates \(\vek x = (\vek o, \theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) and let \(\mat R = \mat R(\theta)\). Then the kinetic energy of the link is \(T = \tfrac12\int_0^L \mu\,|\dot{\vek r}|^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek x}^\T\mat M(\theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\,\dot{\vek x}\) with the mass matrix (masna matrika)

\[\mat M = \begin{bmatrix} m\,\mat I & \mat R\mat J\,(\vek s_0 + \mat S\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}) & \mat R\mat S \\ \ast & m_{\theta\theta} & \vek b^\T - \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat Z \\ \ast & \ast & \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} \end{bmatrix} , \qquad m_{\theta\theta} = J_{\mathrm{link}} + 2\,\vek c^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} , \tag{28.20}\]

where the asterisks mean the transposed entries above the diagonal, so \(\mat M\) is symmetric. The matrix depends on \(\theta\) only in the first row of blocks, and on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) only in the first two.

Proof

We have to show that \(T\) is a quadratic form in the velocities of the coordinates with the coefficients of definition 28.12. We expand the square of the velocity and write every product with velocities that do not depend on \(x\).

  1. The velocity of the point is \(\dot{\vek r} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot\theta\,\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p} + \mat R\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) with \(\bar{\vek p} = \bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (lemma 28.8). The second term is the turning, the third the elastic motion.
  2. The matrix satisfies \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), because \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\) (lemma 8.11), and \(\mat J^\T\mat J = \mat I\) by computation: \(\mat J^\T\mat J = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} = \mat I\). A turn preserves length, since the turning arrow is perpendicular to \(\bar{\vek p}\) and \(\dot\theta\) times as long. Hence \(|\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p}|^2 = |\bar{\vek p}|^2\) and \((\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p})^\T\mat R\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \bar{\vek p}^\T\mat J^\T\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = -\bar{\vek p}^\T\mat J\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), since \(\mat J^\T = -\mat J\). The square of the sum of three vectors with these equalities is (28.17).
  3. We multiply by \(\tfrac12\mu\) and integrate over \([0, L]\). The velocities \(\dot{\vek o}\), \(\dot\theta\), \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) and the coordinates \(\theta\), \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) do not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral (lemma 3.54) we take them out. The term \(|\dot{\vek o}|^2\) gives \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek o}|^2\), the term \(|\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}|^2 = \dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat\Psi^\T\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) gives \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), which is the area under the curve \(\psi^2\) in the figure.
  4. The two mixed terms with \(\dot{\vek o}\): \(\dot\theta\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat J\int\mu\,\bar{\vek p}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\int\mu\,\mat\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\). The first integral is \(\int\mu\,(\bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek s_0 + \mat S\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), the second is \(\mat S\). These are the first row of blocks in (28.20).
  5. The term with \(\dot\theta^2\): \(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2\int\mu\,|\bar{\vek p}|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(|\bar{\vek p}|^2 = |\bar{\vek r}_0|^2 + 2\,\bar{\vek r}_0^\T\mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat\Psi^\T\mat\Psi\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). After integration this is \(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2\,m_{\theta\theta}\) with \(\int\mu\,|\bar{\vek r}_0|^2 = J_{\mathrm{link}}\) (the area under the curve \(x^2\)) and \(\int\mu\,\bar{\vek r}_0^\T\mat\Psi = \vek c^\T\).
  6. The term with \(\dot\theta\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\): \(-\dot\theta\int\mu\,\bar{\vek p}^\T\mat J\mat\Psi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\bar{\vek p}^\T\mat J\mat\Psi = \bar{\vek r}_0^\T\mat J\mat\Psi + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat\Psi^\T\mat J\mat\Psi\). Transposition (lemma 1.102) gives \(\bar{\vek r}_0^\T\mat J\mat\Psi = -(\mat\Psi^\T\mat J\bar{\vek r}_0)^\T\), because \(\mat J^\T = -\mat J\). After integration the term is \(\dot\theta\,(\vek b^\T - \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat Z)\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), where \(\vek b\) is the area under the curve \(x\,\psi\), the mixed term of the figure.
  7. The sum of steps 3 to 6 is a quadratic form in \(\dot{\vek x}\) with the blocks stated in (28.20), and with the same blocks below the diagonal if the mixed term \(\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{ij}}\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{j}} + \dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{ij}}^\T\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{i}}\) is split into two equal halves. The symmetric matrix of a quadratic form is determined uniquely.

Source: SHABANA (5.39)-(5.47) (PDF pp. 211-213) for the definition and the block structure of the mass matrix of a body, (5.78)-(5.93) (PDF pp. 217-219) for the planar case. The notation with integrals along the link and with the quarter turn \(\mat J\), and the proof, are ours.

The matrix has three groups of blocks, and figure 28.31 separates them. The grid lines divide the matrix into blocks by the coordinates \(\vek o\), \(\theta\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). The two blue blocks \(m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) are constant. The first is the mass of the link, the same as for a rigid body, the second is the matrix of masses of all the shapes, as a beam has it without any motion of the frame. The orange blocks, \(m_{\theta\theta}\) and the row \(\vek b^\T - \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat Z\), depend only on the elastic coordinates. The grey blocks in the first row of blocks depend on \(\theta\) too, because \(\mat R\) turns the contribution of the frame into the axes of the world. The lower block is the transpose of the upper one, and the headers show which velocities each column multiplies.

We test the theorem on an example of the book that we can compare with its numbers. Example 5.3 in SHABANA (PDF pp. 220 to 222) has a link with \(m = 1{,}236\) kg, \(l = 0{,}5\) m and the shape \(\mat\Psi = \begin{bmatrix}\xi & 0\\ 0 & 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3\end{bmatrix}\) (\(\xi = x/l\)), at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = (0{,}001,\ 0{,}01)\). The inertia integrals from the definition are \(\vek s_0 = (0{,}309,\ 0)\) kg m, \(\mat S = 0{,}618\,\mat I\) kg, \(\vek c = (0{,}206,\ 0)\), \(\vek b = (0,\ 0{,}2163)\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \operatorname{diag}(0{,}412,\ 0{,}4591)\) kg. By (28.20) the blocks of the first row are \(\mat R\mat J(\vek s_0 + \mat S\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}) = (-0{,}1602,\ 0{,}2650)\) and \(\mat R\mat S = \begin{bmatrix}0{,}5352 & -0{,}309\\ 0{,}309 & 0{,}5352\end{bmatrix}\), which agrees in all digits with the example on PDF p. 222. The lower row of blocks \(\vek b^\T - \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat Z = (-0{,}004326,\ 0{,}2167)\) agrees with the printed \(-4{,}326\cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(0{,}2167\). For \(m_{\theta\theta}\) we get \(0{,}103 + 4{,}12\cdot 10^{-4} + 4{,}63\cdot 10^{-5} = 0{,}1035\) kg m\(^2\), and the book prints \(0{,}1031\). The difference is in the book itself: its term \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is \(2{,}316\cdot 10^{-5}\), half of the correct \(4{,}63\cdot 10^{-5}\), because in the computation the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is multiplied by \(\tfrac12\) (PDF p. 222), in contrast to (5.88) on PDF p. 219 and to its matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(0{,}412,\ 0{,}4591)\) on the same page. The sum of the printed terms (\(0{,}103 + 4{,}12\cdot 10^{-4} + 2{,}3\cdot 10^{-5} = 0{,}1034\)) is not the printed value either. We also checked the theorem with an independent computation: the kinetic energy by numerical integration of \(\tfrac12\int\mu|\dot{\vek r}|^2\mathrm{d}x\) at random velocities is \(0{,}88455359\) J and equals \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek x}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek x}\) to the eighth digit.

At every test we ask what the theorem returns in a known case. The known case is the rigid link.

We build figure 28.32 as at the board. First the hub with the axes of the frame, turned by the angle \(\theta\) against the axes of the world. From the hub we draw the rigid link as a straight bar of length \(L\) along the axis \(\bar x\). On it lies the centre of mass \(G\) at the arm \(s/m\) from the hub, because the centre of mass is the average of the position weighted by the mass. The hub moves with the velocity \(\dot{\vek o}\), so the centre of mass first has this part of the velocity, the same for all points of the link. By the turning about the hub it gets the perpendicular part \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\mat J\,\vek s_0/m\), proportional to the arm. The sum of both is the velocity of the centre of mass \(\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{G}}\).

Corollary 28.14 Rigid link (togi člen)

Let \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) and \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\). Then the kinetic energy of the rigid link from theorem 28.13 is

\[T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek o}|^2 + \dot\theta\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat J\,\vek s_0 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{link}}\,\dot\theta^2 , \tag{28.21}\]

and it equals \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\) for a rigid bar (theorem 8.92) with the centre of mass \(G\) at \(\vek o + \mat R\,\vek s_0/m\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = J_{\mathrm{link}} - s^2/m\).

Proof

We have to show that (28.21) equals theorem 8.92. At \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) the link is a rigid body and the matrix (28.20) becomes a matrix with three rows of blocks without the elastic ones.

  1. Theorem 28.13 with \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) gives \(T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek o}|^2 + \dot\theta\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat J\vek s_0 + \tfrac12\,m_{\theta\theta}\dot\theta^2\) with \(m_{\theta\theta} = J_{\mathrm{link}}\). This is (28.21).
  2. The centre of mass is at \(\vek r_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek o + \mat R\vek s_0/m\) (the position of a point with the mass \(m\) whose moment of mass is \(\vek s_0\)). Its velocity is \(\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{G}} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot\theta\,\mat R\mat J\vek s_0/m\) (the time derivative with lemma 28.8 at \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\)).
  3. The square of the sum of the displacement of the hub and the turning part: \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 = \tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek o}|^2 + \dot\theta\,\dot{\vek o}^\T\mat R\mat J\vek s_0 + \tfrac12\,\dot\theta^2\,|\vek s_0|^2/m\), because \(|\mat R\mat J\vek s_0| = |\vek s_0|\) (step 2 of the previous proof).
  4. Steiner's theorem (lemma 8.93) for the arm \(s/m\) from the hub to the centre of mass gives \(J_{\mathrm{link}} = J_{\mathrm{G}} + m\,(s/m)^2\), so \(\tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{link}}\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12\,\dot\theta^2\,s^2/m\). With step 2, (28.21) equals \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\).

Source: SHABANA (5.48)-(5.50) (PDF p. 213) for the rigid case. The proof is not from the source.

For example A, where the centre of mass is at the middle of the bar (arm \(s/m = 0{,}5\) m): \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m L^2/12 = 0{,}1560\) kg m\(^2\), \(s^2/m = 0{,}4680\) and the sum \(0{,}6240 = J_{\mathrm{link}}\), which is also the moment of inertia of a bar about its end. The more important case is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} \ne \vek 0\), and the next lemma treats it.

Lemma 28.15 Inertia of a link with a transverse shape (vztrajnost člena s prečno obliko)

Let the link have a transverse shape \(\mat\Psi = \begin{bmatrix}\vek 0\\ \vek\psi^\T\end{bmatrix}\) with linearly independent functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (definition 6.22) and let \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) be arbitrary. Then in (28.20) we have \(\vek c = \vek 0\), \(\mat Z = \mat 0\), the second row of the matrix \(\mat S\) is \(\vek\sigma^\T\) with the column \(\vek\sigma = \int_0^L\mu\,\vek\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\) (the first row is zero), and

\[m_{\theta\theta} = J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = J_{\mathrm{link}} + \int_0^L \mu\,v^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ \ge\ J_{\mathrm{link}} , \qquad \vek b^\T - \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat Z = \vek b^\T . \tag{28.22}\]

The moment of inertia of the link about the hub is therefore larger than for a rigid link, and the difference is quadratic in the displacement. In figure 28.30 \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\) is the area under \(x^2\), \(\int\mu\,v^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) the area under \(\psi^2\) and \(\vek b\) the area under \(x\,\psi\). The column \(\vek b\) does not depend on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), and

\[\vek b^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\vek b \ \le\ J_{\mathrm{link}} , \tag{28.23}\]

with equality only if \(x = \vek a^\T\vek\psi(x)\) for some column \(\vek a\), that is, if the function \(x\) is a linear combination of the shapes.

Proof

We have to show the forms of the blocks and the inequality, which is the Cauchy-Schwarz one: the area under \(x\,\psi\) squared does not exceed the product of the area under \(x^2\) and the area under \(\psi^2\). We compute the integrals with the rows \(\mat\Psi_1 = \vek 0\) and \(\mat\Psi_2 = \vek\psi^\T\).

  1. With \(\mat\Psi_1 = \vek 0\) we have \(\vek c = \int\mu\,\mat\Psi^\T\bar{\vek r}_0\,\mathrm{d}x = \int\mu\,x\,\mat\Psi_1^\T\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek 0\) and \(\mat J\mat\Psi = \begin{bmatrix}-\mat\Psi_2\\ \mat\Psi_1\end{bmatrix}\), so \(\mat\Psi^\T\mat J\mat\Psi = -\mat\Psi_1^\T\mat\Psi_2 + \mat\Psi_2^\T\mat\Psi_1 = \mat 0\). Hence \(\mat Z = \mat 0\) and from (28.20) follow \(m_{\theta\theta}\) and the row \(\vek b^\T\) of (28.22). For \(\vek b\) we have \(\mat J\bar{\vek r}_0 = (0, x)\), so \(\vek b = \int\mu\,x\,\vek\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\) as in (28.19).
  2. With \(v = \vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \int\mu\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\vek\psi\vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int\mu\,v^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), since \(\mu > 0\) (lemma 3.54, item 4): the curve \(\psi^2\) lies above the axis, so its area is not negative. This is (28.22).
  3. Let \(\vek a\) be an arbitrary column and \(f = x\), \(g = \vek\psi^\T\vek a\) (for one shape and \(a = 1\), \((f, f)\), \((f, g)\) and \((g, g)\) are the areas under \(x^2\), \(x\,\psi\) and \(\psi^2\)). The inner product \((f, g) = \int\mu\,f\,g\,\mathrm{d}x\) has the properties of lemma 6.19, so \((\vek a^\T\vek b)^2 = (f, g)^2 \le (f, f)\,(g, g) = J_{\mathrm{link}}\,\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek a\).
  4. By step 2 and the independence of the functions, the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is positive definite: \(\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek a = \int\mu\,(\vek\psi^\T\vek a)^2 > 0\) for \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\), because otherwise the area under \(\psi^2\) would be zero, so the function \(\vek\psi^\T\vek a\) would be zero everywhere (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 6.7), which independence excludes. So \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\) exists and \(\vek a = \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek b\) is admissible. Step 3 gives \((\vek b^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek b)^2 \le J_{\mathrm{link}}\,\vek b^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek b\), and after dividing by \(\vek b^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek b > 0\) (for \(\vek b \ne \vek 0\)) we get (28.23). Equality holds in step 3 only if \(f\) is a multiple of \(g\).

Source: SHABANA (5.84)-(5.89) (PDF pp. 218-219) for \(m_{\theta\theta}\) as a sum of three terms. The inequality (28.23) and its proof are not from the source.

For example A and the static shape, \(m_{\theta\theta} = 0{,}6240 + 0{,}4413\,q^2\) kg m\(^2\), where \(0{,}6240\) is the area under \(x^2\) times \(\mu L^2\) and \(0{,}4413\) the area under \(\psi^2\) times \(\mu L\). At an end displacement \(q = 10\) mm the increase is \(4{,}4\cdot 10^{-5}\) kg m\(^2\), which is \(7{,}1\cdot 10^{-5}\) of \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\), and at \(100\) mm it is \(7{,}1\cdot 10^{-3}\). For the first mode of the cantilever of example A (theorem 13.52), which is a different shape, this share at \(10\) mm is \(7{,}5\cdot 10^{-5}\). It is small enough that in dynamics staying at small displacements the moment of inertia of the link may be taken as constant, but only under the condition that the end displacement stays below about \(10\) % of the length, where the increase is below \(1\) %. The coupling \(\vek b\) (area under \(x\,\psi\)), however, is not small: \(b/\sqrt{J_{\mathrm{link}}M_{\mathrm{dd}}} = 0{,}981\), close to the upper bound \(1\) that inequality (28.23) gives for one shape. The rotation of the hub therefore strongly "drives" the elastic coordinate, and this is the main effect in the equations of motion. Here \(J_{\mathrm{link}} - b^2/M_{\mathrm{dd}} = 0{,}0234\) kg m\(^2\) (\(3{,}8\) % of \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\)), the quantity that the bound (28.23) leaves.

Now we can check whether the matrix is positive definite. We expect it, because a moving mass has positive kinetic energy unless each of its points is at rest. The question is whether the rest of all points of the link really implies the rest of all coordinates, and the answer depends on the reference conditions.

Theorem 28.16 Positive definiteness of the mass matrix of a link (pozitivna definitnost masne matrike člena)

Let the link have a transverse shape with continuous functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'\) that are linearly independent, and let them satisfy the reference conditions of the clamped frame (the bent link touches the axis of the frame at the hub) \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(0) = 0\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\) (definition 28.5). Then the mass matrix \(\mat M(\theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) of theorem 28.13 is positive definite (definition 6.27) for every \(\theta\) and every \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\).

Proof

We have to show that zero kinetic energy implies that all velocities of coordinates are zero. In the figure this means that the sum of both parts of the velocity is zero at every point of the link. Let \(\dot{\vek x} = (\dot{\vek o}, \dot\theta, \dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}})\) and \(\dot{\vek x}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek x} = 0\).

  1. The matrix is symmetric (theorem 28.13) and \(\dot{\vek x}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek x} = \int_0^L\mu\,|\dot{\vek r}|^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\). At the value zero the continuous non-negative function \(\mu\,|\dot{\vek r}|^2\) is zero everywhere, otherwise its integral over a neighbourhood of a point where it is positive would have a positive lower bound (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 6.7). Hence \(\dot{\vek r}(x) = \vek 0\) for every \(x\).
  2. At \(x = 0\) (the hub) we have \(\bar{\vek p}(0) = (0, v(0)) = \vek 0\) and \(\mat\Psi(0) = \mat 0\), so \(\dot{\vek r}(0) = \dot{\vek o}\) (lemma 28.8). Hence \(\dot{\vek o} = \vek 0\).
  3. What remains is that the turning and the elastic motion cancel: \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\mat J\bar{\vek p} + \mat R\mat\Psi\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\). We multiply by \(\mat R^\T\) and insert \(\bar{\vek p} = (x, v)\), \(\mat J\bar{\vek p} = (-v, x)\): the second component is \(\dot\theta\,x + \vek\psi(x)^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\).
  4. We differentiate with respect to \(x\) at \(x = 0\), where the slope of the link is zero: \(\dot\theta + \vek\psi'(0)^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \dot\theta = 0\), because \(\vek\psi'(0) = \vek 0\).
  5. Now the elastic motion \(\vek\psi(x)^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) everywhere, and by linear independence (definition 6.22) \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\). Hence \(\dot{\vek x} = \vek 0\).

Source: SHABANA section 6.2 (PDF pp. 286-287) for the reference conditions in finite elements, but the positive definiteness of the mass matrix is not proved in the source. The proof is not from the source.

Each of the conditions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(0) = 0\) (a point on the axis of the frame) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\) (the link touches the axis) is used in the proof in one step: the first in step 2 (the hub is a point of the frame), the second in step 4 (the direction of the axis at the hub is the direction of the frame). What happens if we leave one out? With \(\psi(0) = 0\) without the other condition the proof stops at step 4, and a case of this kind follows.

Let us first draw what this shape does. At \(\psi = x/L\) the link is a straight line from the hub to the point \((L, q)\), so the elastic displacement \(q\) is a displacement of the end perpendicular to the axis of the frame. We get nearly the same position in another way: we turn the frame by the angle \(\arctan(q/L)\) so that its axis lies in the direction of the left link, and there is no elastic displacement. Such a link has the same direction, but its length is \(L\), while the left one is \(L\sqrt{1 + q^2/L^2}\) long. At \(q = 0{,}01\) m the two lengths differ by \(5{,}0\cdot 10^{-5}\,L\), so we write the sign \(\approx\) between the two positions and not an equals sign. Two coordinates, \(\theta\) and \(q\), therefore describe the same position of the body to first order in \(q/L\). An exact pair, \((\theta, q)\) and \((\theta + 2\beta, -q)\) with \(\tan\beta = q/L\), we found in section 28.2.1. Because the turn of the frame and the elastic displacement move the points of the link in the same way to first order, the matrix for this case is bad.

Lemma 28.17 A shape without the slope condition: a singular mass matrix (oblika brez pogoja za naklon)

Let \(n = 1\), \(\psi = x/L\) and \(\mu\) constant, so that \(\psi(0) = 0\) but \(\psi'(0) = 1/L \ne 0\). Then \(\vek b = J_{\mathrm{link}}/L\) and \(M_{\mathrm{dd}} = J_{\mathrm{link}}/L^2\), the block of the mass matrix for the coordinates \((\theta, q)\) is

\[\begin{bmatrix} J_{\mathrm{link}}\,(1 + q^2/L^2) & J_{\mathrm{link}}/L \\ J_{\mathrm{link}}/L & J_{\mathrm{link}}/L^2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \det = \frac{J_{\mathrm{link}}^2\,q^2}{L^4} . \tag{28.24}\]

At \(q = 0\) the matrix is singular with the null vector \((\dot\theta, \dot q) = (1, -L)\) (the turn of the frame and the elastic displacement cancel), and for example A (\(J_{\mathrm{link}} = 0{,}6240\) kg m\(^2\), \(L = 1\) m) its determinant at \(q = 0{,}01\) m equals \(3{,}9\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the smallest eigenvalue \(3{,}1\cdot 10^{-5}\) kg m\(^2\) against the largest \(1{,}248\) kg m\(^2\).

Proof

We have to show the blocks, the determinant and the null vector and connect them with the motion: the elastic displacement and the turn of the frame give the same position.

  1. The integrals: \(\vek b = \int\mu\,x\,(x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = J_{\mathrm{link}}/L\) and \(M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \int\mu\,(x/L)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = J_{\mathrm{link}}/L^2\) (definition 28.12). By lemma 28.15 \(m_{\theta\theta} = J_{\mathrm{link}} + q^2M_{\mathrm{dd}}\). This is the matrix in (28.24).
  2. The determinant: \(J_{\mathrm{link}}(1 + q^2/L^2)\,J_{\mathrm{link}}/L^2 - J_{\mathrm{link}}^2/L^2 = J_{\mathrm{link}}^2\,q^2/L^4\).
  3. At \(q = 0\) the matrix is \(\tfrac{J_{\mathrm{link}}}{L^2}\begin{bmatrix}L^2 & L\\ L & 1\end{bmatrix}\) and multiplying by \((1, -L)\) gives \(\tfrac{J_{\mathrm{link}}}{L^2}\,(L^2 - L^2,\ L - L) = (0, 0)\).
  4. Physical meaning (the turn of the frame and the elastic displacement have opposite effects): at \(q = 0\) the velocity of the point is \(\dot{\vek r} = \mat R\,(\dot\theta\,\mat J\bar{\vek r}_0 + \psi\,\dot q\,\vek e_2) = \mat R\,x\,(\dot\theta + \dot q/L)\,\vek e_2\) (lemma 28.8), and it is zero for every \(x\) at \(\dot q = -L\,\dot\theta\).
  5. For example A: \(J_{\mathrm{link}}^2q^2/L^4 = 0{,}6240^2\cdot 10^{-4} = 3{,}9\cdot 10^{-5}\), the trace is \(1{,}248\) kg m\(^2\), so the smallest eigenvalue is approximately \(\det/\mathrm{trace} = 3{,}1\cdot 10^{-5}\) kg m\(^2\).

Source: Not from the source. SHABANA PDF pp. 280-281 mentions that the element field contains rigid motion, and p. 286 the conditions that remove it.

At \(q = 0\) the matrix is singular (the link on the left and on the right almost coincide) and at \(q = 10\) mm nearly singular: the ratio of the smallest and the largest eigenvalue is \(2{,}5\cdot 10^{-5}\), so a computation with eight digits would lose five digits. At the same time the elastic energy is zero, because \(\int EI\,\psi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for a straight shape (energy of a beam, lemma 5.77), and the coordinate \(q\) has no restoring force. This is one more reason why the reference conditions require \(\psi'(0) = 0\) as well: with it \(\theta\) and \(q\) are separated, which is the difference between rigid and elastic motion. Theorem 28.16 is therefore exactly as strong as we need, and not weaker.

What have we shown? The kinetic energy of the link is a quadratic form in the velocities of the coordinates \((\dot{\vek o}, \dot\theta, \dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}})\) (theorem 28.13), whose coefficients are the blocks of the mass matrix, eight integrals along the link, computed once (definition 28.12). For a rigid link it becomes the energy of a rigid body (corollary 28.14), and for a transverse shape it has a moment of inertia that grows quadratically with the bend and a coupling \(\vek b\) that does not depend on the bend (lemma 28.15). The matrix is positive definite if the shapes satisfy the reference conditions (theorem 28.16) and singular if a shape contains a rigid turn (lemma 28.17). In the next section we add the elastic energy and the generalized forces, and the matrix obtained enters Lagrange's equations.
