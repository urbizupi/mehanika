1.14 Mathematics: the mean value theorem

At the end of section 1.7.3 we needed two facts for a fibre in a general field. The first is about functions whose input is one number and whose output is one number, like the displacement \(u(x)\) of a bar: if a function has a derivative at every point between two numbers and no jump at the end points, then its change between the numbers equals the derivative at some point between them times the difference of the two numbers. For the function \(f(t) = t^3\) between \(0\) and \(1\) we found such a point in section 1.8.1, at \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3\), and saw that it does not exist among the fractions (figure 1.111). In this section we state this fact precisely and prove it for every such function.

Let us draw the route step by step, from left to right. In each of the four graphs the axes show the value of the function vertically and the number \(t\) horizontally, and on the axis \(t\) the endpoints of the interval are marked with the labels \(a\) and \(b\). The blackwhite curve is the graph of a function on this interval, a different invented one in each graph. The double arrows between the graphs mean: the next follows from the previous.

In the first graph the blue dot is at the highest point of the graph. The grey dashed line drops it to the axis and the label \(x_{\max}\) says that this is the number at which the value is largest. The blackwhite dot at the lowest point, its dashed line and the label \(x_{\min}\) say the same for the smallest value. The second graph is the same graph. The dashed line and dot at \(x_0\) show the largest value inside the interval. The blue horizontal line is the tangent at this point. It is horizontal by lemma 1.82 on the derivative at an interior extremum.

In the third graph the endpoint values are equal. So they are joined by a grey dashed horizontal line, with a blackwhite dot at each end. By Rolle's theorem 1.83 there is a point \(\xi\) in between with a horizontal tangent. At \(\xi\) the peak of the graph is drawn, with a dashed line and a dot. The blue tangent is horizontal there again. In the fourth graph the endpoint values are not equal, so the grey dashed line between the endpoints is tilted. The blue tangent at the point \(\xi\) is tilted the same as this line, so it is parallel to it.

The route is short, because the tools of the previous sections do the main work (figure 1.266). By the extreme value theorem 1.73, a continuous function on a closed interval attains a largest and a smallest value. We will show that the tangent to the graph at a point where the function attains its largest value is horizontal (the same for the smallest), if that point is not an end point of the interval and the function has a derivative there. Together these give the statement for functions that have the same value at both end points: somewhere in between the tangent is horizontal. We then get the general statement by subtracting from the function the straight line through the points of the graph above the end points, and so we get a tangent parallel to this line. For this last step we must be able to differentiate the difference of two functions, so we first derive the rules by which we differentiate sums, multiples and products. They will also spare us the long computations of derivatives from sections 1.3.4 and 1.8.1. The rule for products needs the fact that a differentiable function has no jumps, and this is where we begin. At the end we derive two consequences that we will use for strains.

About the numbers we assume the same as in section 1.8.1: the arithmetic rules from school, division of integers with remainder, the fact that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element, and the completeness axiom 1.32. The completeness axiom enters this section in one place only, through the extreme value theorem, and we will say where. All functions in this section have one number as input and one number as output.

Under the horizontal line is the denominator: the horizontal distance between the numbers \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), in the row from left to right \(\Delta x = 2\), \(1\) and \(0{,}5\). Next to the point \(Q\) is the numerator: the vertical distance between \(P\) and \(Q\), which is \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\), that is \(1{,}6\), \(0{,}65\) and \(0{,}2875\). The segment is blue because the quotient is what interests us, while the numerator and the denominator are only its parts. The point \(Q\) moves toward \(P\), and so both distances shrink.

In section 1.3.4, for the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, we noticed that the numerator \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) and the denominator \(\Delta x\) of the difference quotient go to zero together. Figure 1.267 shows the same: the numerator and the denominator get smaller and smaller as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller, while the quotient approaches \(0{,}5\). Let us turn the question around. Suppose that the function \(f\) has a derivative at the point \(x\). Must the numerator \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\) then go to zero? It must. For \(\Delta x \neq 0\) the numerator is the quotient times the denominator,

\[f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \cdot \Delta x ,\]

and as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, the first factor stays close to the derivative \(f'(x)\), while the second goes to zero. So the value \(f(x + \Delta x)\) approaches the value \(f(x)\), and the function has no jump at the point \(x\). For the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight, the numerator and the denominator are the elongation and the length of a piece. The piece of length 0.001 m at the top gets longer by \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m, which is its length times the strain \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (section 1.3.4). A piece ten times shorter gets longer by about ten times less:

\[\Delta x = 10^{-3}\ \text{m} \ \Rightarrow\ 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \text{m} , \qquad \Delta x = 10^{-4}\ \text{m} \ \Rightarrow\ 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-10}\ \text{m} .\]

So the elongation goes to zero together with the length. Why a product goes to zero if one factor stays close to a number and the other goes to zero, the proof below shows exactly.

Continuity from section 1.12 expresses exactly this. By the definition of continuity 1.58 a function \(f\) defined on a set \(D\) is continuous at a point \(x\) of \(D\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(y) - f(x)| < \eta\) for every \(y\) in \(D\) with \(|y - x| < \delta\). So the values at points close to \(x\) are close to \(f(x)\), and the function has no jump at \(x\).

By definition 1.9 the derivative is defined at a point around which the function is defined on both sides, and as a one-sided derivative at an endpoint of the domain. So we state the claim for a function on any interval (definition 1.43): at a point between the endpoints the derivative is the ordinary one, at an endpoint that belongs to the interval it is one-sided. In this way we may use it on a closed interval too, for example for the displacement of the hanging bar on \([0, L]\), which has a one-sided derivative at the top and at the lower end (section 1.4).

The proof will show that the graph near the point \(x\) cannot leave the wedge bounded by two lines through the point of the graph above \(x\), which narrows toward this point, and so it stays within the allowed error, the blue band around \(f(x)\) (figure 1.268). In the proof \(\Delta x\) may only be such that \(x + \Delta x\) is still in the interval, and there are fewer of these at the endpoints (figure 1.269).

Let us draw the figure. The axes show the number \(t\) horizontally and the value \(f\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the function \(f\). The blackwhite dot on it is the point above the chosen number \(x\), a grey dashed line drops it to the axis, and the value \(f(x)\) is read off on the vertical axis. The dashed blackwhite line through this point is the tangent with the slope \(f'(x)\). In the figure \(f'(x) = 0{,}4\).

The proof traps the graph in a wedge, the shape between two grey lines through the point above \(x\), with labels of their slopes at the right end. The slopes are \(1 + |f'(x)|\) and \(-(1 + |f'(x)|)\), in the figure \(1{,}4\) and \(-1{,}4\). Both are steeper than the tangent, because we add the error \(1\) to the derivative. By the definition of the derivative there is a \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that for \(|\Delta x| < \delta_1\) the difference quotient differs from \(f'(x)\) by less than \(1\) (step 3 of the proof). So there its absolute value is less than \(1 + |f'(x)|\), and the graph cannot move away from the point above \(x\) faster than along these two lines, one valid for rising, the other for falling. The wedge is drawn only for \(|\Delta x| < \delta_1\). This limit is marked under the axis with the numbers \(x - \delta_1\) and \(x + \delta_1\), in the figure \(2\) and \(4\). The lines end there because only there the quotient surely stays within the error \(1\). The blue band is the allowed error: the values between \(f(x) - \eta\) and \(f(x) + \eta\), in the figure \(\eta = 0{,}7\), that is from \(1{,}3\) to \(2{,}7\). We must trap the continuous function in it. At \(\delta = \eta / (1 + |f'(x)|) = 0{,}5\) the wedge at the numbers \(x - \delta\) and \(x + \delta\) just reaches the edge of the band, because \(1{,}4 \cdot 0{,}5 = 0{,}7\). So in this part the wedge is inside the band, and with it the graph, drawn in thick blue.

Lemma 1.79 A differentiable function is continuous (odvedljiva funkcija je zvezna)

Let the function \(f\) be defined on an interval \(I\) and differentiable at the point \(x \in I\), at an endpoint of the interval that belongs to \(I\) with the one-sided derivative of definition 1.9. Then \(f\) is continuous at the point \(x\).

Proof

We write the definition of continuity 1.58 out for the function \(f\) on the set \(D = I\) at the point \(x\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(y) - f(x)| < \eta\) for every \(y \in I\) with \(|y - x| < \delta\). We will write a point \(y \neq x\) as \(y = x + \Delta x\).

  1. We choose an arbitrary error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta > 0\).
  2. The difference quotient \(q(\Delta x) = (f(x + \Delta x) - f(x))/\Delta x\) is defined for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\) for which \(x + \Delta x\) is in \(I\). We call such \(\Delta x\) admissible.
  3. By the definition of the derivative 1.9, \(q\) approaches \(f'(x)\). We use this with the error \(1\): there is a number \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|q(\Delta x) - f'(x)| < 1\) for every admissible \(\Delta x\) with \(|\Delta x| < \delta_1\). If \(x\) is not an endpoint of the interval, this is the limit of definition 1.5, and we may take \(\delta_1\) so small that all \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta_1\) are admissible (section 1.3.3). If \(x\) is the left endpoint and belongs to \(I\), only positive \(\Delta x\) are admissible, and this is the limit from the right of definition 1.8, which looks at exactly such \(\Delta x\). At the right endpoint only negative \(\Delta x\) are admissible, and this is the limit from the left. The error \(1\) is an arbitrary choice, any positive number would do equally well.
  4. For such a \(\Delta x\) we bound the quotient by a number that does not depend on \(\Delta x\). By the triangle inequality for numbers \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1)
    \[\begin{aligned} |q(\Delta x)| &= \big|\big(q(\Delta x) - f'(x)\big) + f'(x)\big| \\ &\le |q(\Delta x) - f'(x)| + |f'(x)| \\ &< 1 + |f'(x)| . \end{aligned}\]
  5. The numerator is the quotient times \(\Delta x\): \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = q(\Delta x)\,\Delta x\). The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, item 2), so by step 4, for every admissible \(\Delta x\) with \(|\Delta x| < \delta_1\),
    \[|f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)| = |q(\Delta x)|\,|\Delta x| < \big(1 + |f'(x)|\big)\,|\Delta x| .\]
  6. The right side is smaller than \(\eta\) if \(|\Delta x| < \eta / (1 + |f'(x)|)\). So for \(\delta\) we take the smaller of the positive numbers \(\delta_1\) and \(\eta / (1 + |f'(x)|)\).
  7. We check. Let \(y \in I\) and \(|y - x| < \delta\). If \(y = x\), then \(|f(y) - f(x)| = 0 < \eta\). If \(y \neq x\), then \(\Delta x = y - x\) is admissible, because \(y\) is in \(I\), and \(|\Delta x| < \delta \le \delta_1\), so by step 5
    \[|f(y) - f(x)| < \big(1 + |f'(x)|\big)\,|\Delta x| < \big(1 + |f'(x)|\big) \cdot \frac{\eta}{1 + |f'(x)|} = \eta .\]
  8. We chose the error arbitrarily and found a \(\delta\) for it. So the function \(f\) is continuous at \(x\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 4.1.6 (PDF p. 157), for a function on any interval, also at an endpoint. The source proves it with the limit of a product. The proof by bounding the quotient is not from the source.

What did the proof show? The inequality of step 5 says that close to the point \(x\) the graph cannot leave the wedge between the straight lines through the point of the graph above \(x\) with slopes \(1 + |f'(x)|\) and \(-(1 + |f'(x)|)\) (figure 1.268). Towards this point the wedge narrows to a tip from both sides, so the graph approaches the point from both sides and cannot jump. At an endpoint of the interval the graph lies on one side of the point only, and the wedge catches it from that side. Step 6 then only chose a \(\delta\) so small that the wedge inside the band \(|\Delta x| < \delta\) lies within the blue band. For the displacement of the hanging bar \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) on \([0, L]\) the lemma turns the one-sided derivative \(2aL\) at the top into continuity at \(x = 0\), which we checked directly in section 1.12.1.

The converse is false: a continuous function need not have a derivative. An example is the absolute value, the function that assigns \(|x|\) to the number \(x\), at the point \(0\) (figure 1.270, left). It is continuous at \(0\), because \(\big||x| - |0|\big| = |x|\) is smaller than \(\eta\) for every \(x\) with \(|x| < \eta\), that is, with the band \(\delta = \eta\). But it has no derivative at \(0\). The difference quotient is

\[\frac{|0 + \Delta x| - |0|}{\Delta x} = \frac{|\Delta x|}{\Delta x} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{for } \Delta x > 0 , \\ -1 & \text{for } \Delta x < 0 \end{cases}\]

(figure 1.270, right). If it had a limit \(d\), the definition of the limit with the error \(\eta = 1\) would give a band \(\delta\) in which all values of the quotient differ from \(d\) by less than \(1\). The band contains the point \(\Delta x = \delta/2\) with the value \(1\) and the point \(\Delta x = -\delta/2\) with the value \(-1\), so \(|1 - d| < 1\) and \(|-1 - d| < 1\). By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) we would then have

\[2 = |1 - (-1)| = \big|(1 - d) + (d + 1)\big| \le |1 - d| + |d + 1| < 1 + 1 = 2 ,\]

that is, \(2 < 2\), which cannot hold. So there is no limit. The graph has a sharp corner at \(0\): the straight lines through the point of the graph above \(0\) and the points to its left have slope \(-1\), those through the points to its right slope \(1\), and no number is the limit of both slopes (LEBL1 Example 4.1.5, PDF p. 157). The slope of the graph of the displacement of a bar is the strain. A corner means that the slopes just to the left and just to the right of the point differ, so the material just above the point is stretched differently from the material just below it, for example at the joint of two bars of different materials that stretch differently under the same force (chapter 3). At the joint the graph of the displacement continues without a jump, but it has a corner, and at the point of the joint the strain is not defined.

Let us read the figure too. The left part has the axes, horizontal for the number \(x\) and vertical for the value, with the numbers \(-1\) and \(1\) on the horizontal axis. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the function \(|x|\): for negative \(x\) it falls, for positive \(x\) it rises, because \(|x|\) equals \(-x\) for negative \(x\) and \(x\) for positive \(x\). The blackwhite dot at the point \(0\) is the corner where the lines meet. The right part has other axes: horizontal for the change \(\Delta x\) and vertical for the quotient \(q\). For \(\Delta x > 0\) the quotient is \(1\), so the horizontal line at height \(1\) is to the right of the vertical axis. For \(\Delta x < 0\) it is \(-1\), so the horizontal line at height \(-1\) is to the left of it. Each line ends with an empty circle at \(\Delta x = 0\), because the quotient is not defined there. No point joins the left and right lines, and this is the jump because of which the quotient has no limit at \(0\).

1.14.2 Rules of differentiation

Section 1.3.4 ended with the promise that we would compute derivatives from the definition until we derived rules that do this work once and for all. Each rule says how we get the derivative of a combined function, for example of the sum or the product of two functions, from the derivatives of its parts. The task is the same for every rule: we must show that the difference quotient of the combined function has a limit, and compute it. We will write the quotient of the combined function with quotients we already know, for example as their sum. So we need a rule that gives, from the limits of two functions, the limit of their sum, and similarly for products. We do not yet have such rules for limits of functions. But we have them for continuous functions: by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, the sum, a multiple and the product of functions that are continuous at some point are again continuous at that point. So we will translate the limit into continuity.

How? The difference quotient is not defined at \(\Delta x = 0\). Its graph has a hole above \(\Delta x = 0\), as in figure 1.106, where an empty circle said that a fibre of length zero does not exist. If the quotient has the limit \(d\), we fill the hole with the point at height \(d\) (figure 1.271). The function we get is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and since the values nearby approach \(d\), it has no jump at the point \(0\). For the bar of length \(L\) hanging under its own weight, with the axis \(x\) from the top downwards and the displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), the difference quotient of the displacement at the point \(x\) is, by (1.6),

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x \qquad (\Delta x \neq 0) .\]

When we fill the hole with the limit \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), we get a function equal to \(a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\) for every \(\Delta x\), also for \(\Delta x = 0\). Its graph is a whole straight line, without a hole.

The blackwhite curve is the graph of the quotient, invented so that the example is smooth. The quotient is defined for \(0 < |h| < r\), so the grey dashed verticals at \(-r\) and \(r\) bound the region where the curve exists. The empty circle on the axis \(g\) is the hole at \(h = 0\), because the quotient is not defined there. The grey dotted line at height \(d\) reaches from the label \(d\) to the empty circle and shows the limit toward which the values of the quotient approach. In the right part are other axes, and on the vertical one is now the value \(G\). The same curve is the graph of the function \(G\). The verticals and the dotted line of the limit are also the same. The blue dot stands at height \(d\) and fills the hole, so \(G(0) = d\) and the curve has no hole. The bottom part is the one-sided case. The axes show \(h\) and \(G\). The blackwhite curve is the graph for \(h \ge 0\) only, the dashed vertical at \(r\) bounds the interval \([0, r)\) and the dotted line shows the height \(d\). We state the claim for any function \(g\) that is defined near zero, except at zero. We use the letter \(h\) as in the definition of the limit 1.5.

Lemma 1.80 Limit and continuity (limita in zveznost)

Let \(d\) be a number.

  1. Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and let \(G\) be the function on the open interval \((-r, r)\) that has the value \(G(h) = g(h)\) for \(h \neq 0\) and the value \(G(0) = d\) at the point \(0\). Then \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) if and only if \(G\) is continuous at the point \(0\).
  2. Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and let \(G\) be the function on the interval \([0, r)\) that has the value \(G(h) = g(h)\) for \(h > 0\) and the value \(G(0) = d\) at the point \(0\). Then \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} g(h) = d\) if and only if \(G\) is continuous at the point \(0\). The same holds for the limit from the left, with the function \(g\) defined for all \(h\) with \(-r < h < 0\) and with the interval \((-r, 0]\).
Proof

We must show both items. We reduce the first to lemma 1.59 on continuity and the limit, and prove the second directly from the definition of continuity.

1. Limit.

  1. The function \(G\) is defined on the interval \((-r, r)\), that is, at all numbers \(h\) with \(|h - 0| < r\). So lemma 1.59 on continuity and the limit applies to it at the point \(c = 0\): \(G\) is continuous at \(0\) if and only if \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} G(0 + h) = G(0)\), that is, \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} G(h) = d\).
  2. The definition of the limit 1.5 looks only at numbers \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\), that is, never at the number \(h = 0\). For all such \(h\) with \(|h| < r\) we have \(G(h) = g(h)\). So the condition of the definition of the limit for the function \(G\) and the number \(d\) is the same as for the function \(g\) and the number \(d\), when we take \(\delta \le r\), which the explanation after the definition of the limit allows. Therefore \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} G(h) = d\) if and only if \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\).
  3. Steps 1 and 2 together say: \(G\) is continuous at \(0\) if and only if \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\).

2. One-sided limit.

  1. We write the definition of continuity 1.58 out for the function \(G\) on the set \(D = [0, r)\) at the point \(0\): for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|G(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h \in [0, r)\) with \(|h| < \delta\).
  2. In the condition of continuity and in the condition of the limit from the right we may take a band \(\delta \le r\), because a condition that holds for some band also holds for every narrower band (section 1.3.3).
  3. At \(h = 0\) we have \(|G(0) - d| = |d - d| = 0 < \eta\), so the condition of continuity holds there for every \(\eta\).
  4. For \(\delta \le r\) the numbers \(h \in [0, r)\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) are exactly the numbers \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\), and for them \(G(h) = g(h)\).
  5. By steps 3 and 4 a band \(\delta \le r\) satisfies the condition of continuity if and only if \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\). This is the condition of the limit from the right of definition 1.8. So \(G\) is continuous at \(0\) if and only if \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} g(h) = d\).
  6. For the limit from the left the steps are the same, with the interval \((-r, 0]\) and with the numbers \(h\) for which \(-\delta < h < 0\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.2.2 (ii) (PDF p. 122) in a more general form: at a point that points of the domain approach arbitrarily closely, a function is continuous if and only if it has a limit there equal to its value at that point. Item 2 is the same statement for the set \([0, r)\), on which the limit at the point \(0\) is the limit from the right (LEBL1 Def. 3.1.16, PDF p. 119). LEBL1 uses the difference quotient completed with the derivative in the proof of Prop. 4.1.10 (PDF p. 159).

The lemma says nothing new about the limit, it only translates it into the language of continuity. It is useful because for continuous functions we already know that sums, multiples and products behave well. So we will prove all the rules for sums, multiples and products by the same recipe. We write the difference quotient of the combined function with the completed quotients of its parts, which by the lemma are continuous at \(0\). The function we get is continuous at \(0\) by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, and by the lemma its value at \(0\) is the limit of the quotient, that is, the derivative.

Figure 1.272 shows how quotients add. In the left part the increment \(\Delta x\) is horizontal and the same for all the quotients. The grey segment is the secant of \(f\) with slope \(q_{\mathrm{f}} = 0{,}95\), and its height increment \(\Delta f\) is measured to the right of it. The second grey segment is the secant of \(g\) with slope \(q_{\mathrm{g}} = 0{,}9\). It is placed at the end of the first, so that the height increments add. The blue segment from the start of the first to the end of the second is the secant of the sum \(f + g\). Its height increment is \(\Delta f + \Delta g = 1{,}85\), so its slope at \(\Delta x = 1\) equals \(q_{\mathrm{f}} + q_{\mathrm{g}}\). In the right part the increment \(\Delta x\) is again the same. The grey segment is the secant of \(f\), and the blue segment the secant of the multiple \(2f\). The height increment of the multiple is twice as large, \(2\,\Delta f = 1{,}9\), so the slope is twice as large too.

For the product the area of a rectangle helps (figure 1.273). Let us picture the product of two positive numbers as the area of a rectangle with sides \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\). When \(x\) changes to \(x + \Delta x\), both sides change, by \(\Delta f = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\) and by \(\Delta g = g(x + \Delta x) - g(x)\). The area grows by two strips. The top strip has the area \(f(x + \Delta x)\,\Delta g\), the right one \(\Delta f\,g(x)\):

\[f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\,g(x) = f(x + \Delta x)\,\Delta g + \Delta f\,g(x) .\]

In the proof we will check this equality by a computation that holds for all numbers, not only for positive ones. When we divide it by \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta g / \Delta x\) approaches the derivative \(g'(x)\), \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) the derivative \(f'(x)\), and the factor \(f(x + \Delta x)\) the value \(f(x)\), because by lemma 1.79 that a differentiable function is continuous, \(f\) has no jump at \(x\).

Its sides are \(f(x)\) horizontally and \(g(x)\) vertically, drawn with grey dimension lines, because each line measures the distance between its endpoints. At \(x + \Delta x\) the horizontal side is longer by \(\Delta f\), and the vertical one by \(\Delta g\). The outer rectangle is the grown rectangle, and its horizontal side is \(f(x + \Delta x)\). The difference of the areas is two strips. The top strip is coloured blue, because it has height \(\Delta g\) and reaches across the whole new width, so its area is \(f(x + \Delta x)\,\Delta g\). The right strip is coloured grey, because it has width \(\Delta f\) and reaches only up to the height of the original rectangle, so its area is \(\Delta f\,g(x)\). The small corner at the top right belongs to the top strip and not to the right one, so that the strips do not overlap.

As in section 1.12.5, for every number \(t\) the power \(t^0\) is \(1\), and for a natural number \(n\), \(t^n\) is the product of \(n\) factors \(t\). Lemma 1.81 Rules of differentiation (pravila odvajanja)

Let the functions \(f\) and \(g\) be defined on an interval \(I\) and differentiable at the point \(x \in I\), at an endpoint of the interval that belongs to \(I\) with the one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), and let \(c\) be a number. At an endpoint the derivatives in items 1 to 3 are one-sided too.

  1. The sum \(f + g\), which assigns \(f(t) + g(t)\) to the number \(t\), is differentiable at \(x\) and \((f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)\).
  2. The multiple \(c f\), which assigns \(c\,f(t)\) to the number \(t\), is differentiable at \(x\) and \((c f)'(x) = c\,f'(x)\).
  3. The product \(f g\), which assigns \(f(t)\,g(t)\) to the number \(t\), is differentiable at \(x\) and
    \[(f g)'(x) = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x) . \tag{1.29}\]

For functions defined for all numbers, moreover:

  1. A constant function (stalna funkcija), which has the same value \(d\) for every number, has the derivative \(0\) at every point. The function that assigns \(t\) to every number \(t\) has the derivative \(1\) at every point.
  2. For every natural number \(n\) the function that assigns \(t^n\) to every number \(t\) has at every point \(x\) the derivative
    \[n\,x^{n-1} . \tag{1.30}\]
Proof

We must show five items. We prove the first three (sum, multiple and product) by the recipe with completed quotients, the fourth (constant function) with the lemma on the limit of a number plus a multiple, the fifth (powers) by induction.

Preparation.

  1. If \(x\) is not an endpoint of the interval \(I\), we take for \(r\) the smaller of the distances from \(x\) to the two endpoints. Then all points at a distance less than \(r\) from \(x\) lie in \(I\), and we let \(J = (-r, r)\). If \(x\) is the left endpoint and belongs to \(I\), let \(r\) be the length of the interval and \(J = [0, r)\), and if it is the right endpoint, \(J = (-r, 0]\). In all cases \(x + \Delta x\) is in \(I\) for every \(\Delta x \in J\).
  2. For \(\Delta x \in J\) with \(\Delta x \neq 0\) we denote the difference quotients of the functions \(f\) and \(g\) at the point \(x\):
    \[q_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta x) = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} , \qquad q_{\mathrm{g}}(\Delta x) = \frac{g(x + \Delta x) - g(x)}{\Delta x} .\]
  3. Let \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) be the function on \(J\) that equals \(q_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta x)\) for \(\Delta x \neq 0\) and has the value \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}(0) = f'(x)\) at \(0\). In the same way let \(Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) equal \(q_{\mathrm{g}}\) for \(\Delta x \neq 0\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{g}}(0) = g'(x)\). The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the limit of the quotient \(q_{\mathrm{f}}\), one-sided at an endpoint (definition 1.9), so by lemma 1.80 on limit and continuity, item 1 or 2, the function \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) is continuous at \(0\). For the same reason \(Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) is continuous at \(0\). Figure 1.271 shows the quotient with a hole at \(0\) and the completed function.

1. Sum.

  1. We rearrange the numerator of the quotient of the sum so that the terms of each function stand together: \(\big(f(x + \Delta x) + g(x + \Delta x)\big) - \big(f(x) + g(x)\big) = \big(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\big) + \big(g(x + \Delta x) - g(x)\big)\). When we divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\), the quotient of the sum equals \(q_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta x) + q_{\mathrm{g}}(\Delta x)\).
  2. The function \(Q_{\mathrm{f}} + Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) on \(J\) equals the quotient of the sum for \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and at \(0\) it has the value \(f'(x) + g'(x)\).
  3. By corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, \(Q_{\mathrm{f}} + Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) is continuous at \(0\).
  4. By lemma 1.80 on limit and continuity, item 1 or 2, the quotient of the sum has the limit \(f'(x) + g'(x)\), one-sided at an endpoint. So the sum is differentiable at \(x\), and \((f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x)\).

2. Multiple.

  1. The numerator of the quotient of the multiple is \(c\,f(x + \Delta x) - c\,f(x) = c\,\big(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\big)\), so the quotient equals \(c\,q_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta x)\).
  2. The function \(c\,Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) equals this quotient for \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and at \(0\) it has the value \(c\,f'(x)\). By corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions it is continuous at \(0\).
  3. By lemma 1.80 on limit and continuity, item 1 or 2, \(c f\) is differentiable at \(x\), and \((c f)'(x) = c\,f'(x)\).

3. Product.

  1. In the numerator \(f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\,g(x)\) both factors change. We add and subtract the term \(f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x)\), so that in each pair only one factor changes, and take out the common factors:
    \[\begin{aligned} f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\,g(x) &= f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x + \Delta x) - f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x) \\ &\quad + f(x + \Delta x)\,g(x) - f(x)\,g(x) \\ &= f(x + \Delta x)\,\big(g(x + \Delta x) - g(x)\big) \\ &\quad + \big(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\big)\,g(x) . \end{aligned}\]
    This is the equality of areas from figure 1.273.
  2. We divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The quotient of the product is \(f(x + \Delta x)\,q_{\mathrm{g}}(\Delta x)\) \(+\) \(g(x)\,q_{\mathrm{f}}(\Delta x)\).
  3. Let \(F\) be the function on \(J\) given by \(F(\Delta x) = f(x + \Delta x)\). Let us show that it is continuous at \(0\). By lemma 1.79 that a differentiable function is continuous, \(f\) is continuous at \(x\): for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(y) - f(x)| < \eta\) for every \(y \in I\) with \(|y - x| < \delta\). For \(\Delta x \in J\) with \(|\Delta x| < \delta\) the point \(y = x + \Delta x\) lies in \(I\) by step 1 of the preparation, and \(|y - x| = |\Delta x| < \delta\), so \(|F(\Delta x) - F(0)| = |f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)| < \eta\). This is the condition of the definition of continuity 1.58 for \(F\) on the set \(J\) at the point \(0\).
  4. By step 2 the function \(F\,Q_{\mathrm{g}} + g(x)\,Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) on \(J\) equals the quotient of the product for \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and at \(0\) it has the value \(F(0)\,Q_{\mathrm{g}}(0) + g(x)\,Q_{\mathrm{f}}(0) = f(x)\,g'(x) + g(x)\,f'(x)\).
  5. By corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions the product \(F\,Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) is continuous at \(0\), so is the multiple \(g(x)\,Q_{\mathrm{f}}\), and therefore also their sum.
  6. By lemma 1.80 on limit and continuity, item 1 or 2, \(f g\) is differentiable at \(x\), and \((f g)'(x) = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)\).

4. A constant function and the function that assigns \(t\) to the number \(t\).

  1. For the constant function with the value \(d\) the numerator at every point \(x\) is \(d - d = 0\), so the quotient is \(0\) for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\). This has the form \(0 + 0 \cdot \Delta x\), and by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple the limit is \(0\).
  2. For the function that assigns \(t\) to the number \(t\) the numerator is \((x + \Delta x) - x = \Delta x\), so the quotient is \(\Delta x / \Delta x = 1\) for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\). This has the form \(1 + 0 \cdot \Delta x\), and by the same lemma the limit is \(1\).

5. Powers.

  1. We use the principle of induction 1.44 for the statement \(P(n)\): the function that assigns \(t^n\) to the number \(t\) has the derivative \(n\,x^{n-1}\) at every point \(x\).
  2. Basis. For \(n = 1\) this is the function that assigns \(t\) to the number \(t\). By item 4 it has the derivative \(1\) at every point, and this is \(1 \cdot x^0\).
  3. Induction step. Let \(P(n)\) hold. For every number \(t\), \(t^{n+1} = t \cdot t^n\), so the function that assigns \(t^{n+1}\) to the number \(t\) is the product of the function that assigns \(t\) to it and the function that assigns \(t^n\) to it. Both are defined for all numbers, hence on the open interval \((x - 1, x + 1)\). The first has the derivative \(1\) at \(x\) by item 4, the second the derivative \(n\,x^{n-1}\) by the statement \(P(n)\).
  4. By item 3 the derivative of the product at \(x\) is \(1 \cdot x^n\) \(+\) \(x \cdot n\,x^{n-1}\) \(= x^n + n\,x^n = (n + 1)\,x^n\), which is the statement \(P(n + 1)\). Here we used \(x \cdot x^{n-1} = x^n\), which for \(n = 1\) holds because \(x^0 = 1\). For the first powers, \(t^2\) and \(t^3\), the step looks like this:
    \[\begin{aligned} (t^2)' &= 1 \cdot x + x \cdot 1 = 2x , \\ (t^3)' &= 1 \cdot x^2 + x \cdot 2x = 3x^2 , \\ (t^4)' &= 1 \cdot x^3 + x \cdot 3x^2 = 4x^3 . \end{aligned}\]
  5. By the principle of induction \(P(n)\) holds for every natural number \(n\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 4.1.7 and 4.1.8 (PDF pp. 157-158), for functions on any interval, powers in Exercise 4.1.3 (PDF p. 160). The source leaves the rule for products and the powers to the reader. LEBL1 uses the completed quotient in the proof of Prop. 4.1.10 (PDF p. 159), and LEBL1 Figure 4.2 (PDF p. 158) shows the areas for the product.

Now let us use the rules on the polynomials of definition 1.63. With the variable \(t\) we write a polynomial as \(b_0 + b_1 t + b_2 t^2 + \dots + b_{\mathrm{m}} t^m\), where \(b_0, b_1, \dots, b_{\mathrm{m}}\) are given numbers. It is a sum of a constant function and multiples of powers. By item 4 the constant term has the derivative \(0\), and by items 2 and 5 the term \(b_{\mathrm{k}} t^k\) has the derivative \(k\,b_{\mathrm{k}}\,x^{k-1}\). For \(m = 2\) the derivative of each term is visible at once:

\[\underbrace{b_0}_{\text{odvod } 0} + \underbrace{b_1 t}_{\text{odvod } \mathrm{b}_1} + \underbrace{b_2 t^2}_{\text{odvod } 2 \mathrm{b}_2 \mathrm{x}} \ \Rightarrow\ b_1 + 2 b_2\,x .\]

In general we build the polynomial by adding one term after another, and by item 1, used at each addition, the polynomial has at every point \(x\) the derivative

\[b_1 + 2b_2\,x + 3b_3\,x^2 + \dots + m\,b_{\mathrm{m}}\,x^{m-1} .\]

The displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(t) = a\,(2Lt - t^2) = 2aL \cdot t + (-a) \cdot t^2\), is a polynomial with \(b_0 = 0\), \(b_1 = 2aL\) and \(b_2 = -a\). So its derivative is

\[u'(x) = 2aL + 2 \cdot (-a)\,x = 2a\,(L - x) ,\]

the strain that we computed from the definition in twelve steps in section 1.3.4. The function \(t^3\) from section 1.8.1 has by item 5 with \(n = 3\) the derivative \(3x^2\), for which we needed ten steps there. The rules did the work with the definition of the limit in their proofs, once and for all.

1.14.3 The derivative at the largest value

Let a function on some interval attain its largest value at a point \(x_0\) that is not an end point of the interval. Then there are points of the interval on both sides of \(x_0\), and at none of them is the value larger than \(f(x_0)\). What does this tell us about the difference quotients at \(x_0\) (figure 1.276)?

Let us draw the extremum. The axes show the number \(t\) horizontally and the value \(f\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph on the interval that has the end points \(a\) and \(b\), each with a dot and a dashed line to the axis. The dot at the top with the dashed line at \(x_0\) is the point where the function has its largest value, so in the figure it is the highest point of the graph. Through this point we draw grey lines to points of the graph to the right of it, each with a grey dot. The lines fall, because these points are lower than the peak. Likewise the grey lines to points to the left of the peak rise, when we read them from left to the peak. Each line is drawn a little beyond its dot so that its direction can be seen. The blue horizontal line is the tangent at the peak, the limit of both kinds of lines. The two measure lines below the axis show the number \(r\), the distance from \(x_0\) to the nearer end point, which in the figure is equal on both sides, \(2{,}1\).

To the right of \(x_0\) we have \(\Delta x > 0\), to the left \(\Delta x < 0\). The numerator \(f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)\) is nowhere positive, because \(f(x_0)\) is the largest value. The signs are

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} & \text{števec} & \text{imenovalec} & \text{količnik} \\ \hline \Delta x > 0 & \le 0 & > 0 & \le 0 \\ \Delta x < 0 & \le 0 & < 0 & \ge 0 \end{array}\]

The straight lines through the point of the graph above \(x_0\) and the points of the graph to its right therefore fall or are horizontal, while the lines to its left rise or are horizontal. The tangent is the limit of both kinds of lines, so it can neither rise nor fall. It is horizontal.

Recall that the largest and the smallest value of a function, its maximum and its minimum, are called by one word an extremum (definition 1.70). When a function attains an extremum at a point that is not an end point of the interval, we speak of an interior extremum (notranji ekstrem). Let us write the reasoning above precisely. We will choose the error \(\eta\) in the definition of the limit equal to the size of the derivative, so that the band around the derivative lies on one side of zero.

Let us draw both cases. In the left part the derivative is positive. The axes show the change \(\Delta x\) horizontally and the difference quotient \(q\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the quotient, with a circled hole at \(\Delta x = 0\), where the quotient is not defined. The dotted line at height \(f'(x_0)\) shows the limit. The blue band is the allowed error: the values that differ from \(f'(x_0)\) by less than \(\eta = f'(x_0)\) lie between \(0\) and \(2f'(x_0)\), so above zero. The dashed verticals at \(-\delta\) and \(\delta\) bound the \(\Delta x\) for which the quotient stays in the band. The orange region holds the values that the quotient may have for \(0 < \Delta x < r\) by the signs: it is not positive. The number \(r\) marks the right edge of this region. The blackwhite dot stands above \(\Delta x^*\), half of the smaller of the numbers \(\delta\) and \(r\). The quotient is in the blue band there, so positive, and it cannot be in the orange region, and both cannot hold at once. The right part is a mirror image, with a negative derivative. The axes and the curve are the same, the dotted line is at \(f'(x_0) < 0\) below the axis \(\Delta x\), the blue band lies between \(2f'(x_0)\) and \(0\), so below zero, and the dashed verticals bound the \(\Delta x\) of the definition of the limit. The orange region holds the values that the quotient may have for \(-r < \Delta x < 0\): it is not negative. The number \(-r\) marks its left edge and the blackwhite dot stands above the negative \(\Delta x^*\).

We will reduce the smallest value to the largest by reflecting the graph across the axis \(t\) (figure 1.278).

Let us draw the reflection. The graph of \(f\) lies above the axis \(t\) and has its lowest point at \(x_0\), with a blue tangent. The graph of \(-f\) is its image across the axis \(t\), so at the same \(x_0\) it has the highest point and the tangent is the mirror image of the previous one.

Lemma 1.82 Derivative at an interior extremum (odvod v notranjem ekstremu)

Let the function \(f\) be defined on an interval with end points \(a < b\) that is closed, open or half-open, and let it attain its largest or smallest value on this interval at a point \(x_0\) with \(a < x_0 < b\). If \(f\) is differentiable at \(x_0\), then \(f'(x_0) = 0\).

Proof

First we treat the largest value, then we reduce the smallest value to it.

1. Largest value.

  1. By the definition of the maximum 1.70, \(f(y) \le f(x_0)\) for every point \(y\) of the interval.
  2. Let \(r\) be the smaller of the positive numbers \(x_0 - a\) and \(b - x_0\). For \(0 < |\Delta x| < r\) the point \(x_0 + \Delta x\) lies strictly between \(a\) and \(b\), hence in the interval, and by step 1, \(f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0) \le 0\).
  3. So the difference quotient \(q(\Delta x) = (f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0))/\Delta x\) is nonpositive for \(0 < \Delta x < r\), because we divide a nonpositive number by a positive one. For \(-r < \Delta x < 0\) it is nonnegative, because we divide a nonpositive number by a negative one.
  4. Suppose that \(f'(x_0) > 0\). The definition of the limit 1.5 with the error \(\eta = f'(x_0)\) gives a band \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|q(\Delta x) - f'(x_0)| < f'(x_0)\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\). From the left half of the inequality \(-f'(x_0) < q(\Delta x) - f'(x_0) < f'(x_0)\) it follows that \(q(\Delta x) > 0\) for all such \(\Delta x\).
  5. Let \(\Delta x\) be half of the smaller of the numbers \(\delta\) and \(r\). This \(\Delta x\) is positive and smaller than both, so by step 4 \(q(\Delta x) > 0\), and by step 3 \(q(\Delta x) \le 0\). Both cannot hold, so \(f'(x_0) > 0\) is false.
  6. Suppose that \(f'(x_0) < 0\). The definition of the limit with the error \(\eta = -f'(x_0) > 0\) gives a band \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|q(\Delta x) - f'(x_0)| < -f'(x_0)\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\). From the right half of the inequality \(f'(x_0) < q(\Delta x) - f'(x_0) < -f'(x_0)\) it follows that \(q(\Delta x) < 0\) for all such \(\Delta x\).
  7. Let \(\Delta x\) be the negative of half of the smaller of the numbers \(\delta\) and \(r\). This \(\Delta x\) is negative and \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\), so by step 6 \(q(\Delta x) < 0\), and by step 3 \(q(\Delta x) \ge 0\). So \(f'(x_0) < 0\) is false too.
  8. What remains is \(f'(x_0) = 0\).

2. Smallest value.

  1. Let \(f\) attain its smallest value at \(x_0\), that is, \(f(y) \ge f(x_0)\) for every point \(y\) of the interval. Then \(-f(y) \le -f(x_0)\), and the function \(-f\), which assigns \(-f(t)\) to the number \(t\), attains its largest value at \(x_0\).
  2. We denote the restriction of the function \(f\) to the open interval \((a, b)\) that contains \(x_0\) again by \(f\). The difference quotient at \(x_0\) uses only values near \(x_0\) that are in \((a, b)\), so the derivative is the same as on the original interval. By lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, item 2 with \(c = -1\), \(-f\) is differentiable at \(x_0\) and \((-f)'(x_0) = -f'(x_0)\).
  3. By the first part of the proof \((-f)'(x_0) = 0\), so \(f'(x_0) = 0\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 4.2.2 (PDF pp. 162-163), for an extremum that is the largest or smallest value only among the points close to \(x_0\). The source argues with sequences. The proof by direct use of the definition of the limit is not from the source.

The proof used points on both sides of \(x_0\), to the right in step 5 and to the left in step 7. At an end point of the interval there are no points on one side, and the lemma says nothing there. An example is the strain of a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight, with the axis \(x\) from the top downwards: \(\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\) on the interval \([0, L]\), where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) is the constant from the displacement of the bar, not an end point from the lemma, and the end points of the interval are \(0\) and \(L\). It is largest at the top, at \(x = 0\), where it is \(2aL\) (figure 1.241). By the rules of differentiation the polynomial \(2aL - 2a\,x\), defined for all numbers, has the derivative \(-2a\) at every point. The function \(\varepsilon\) is defined only on \([0, L]\), so its derivative at \(x = 0\) is one-sided, a limit from the right (definition 1.9). A limit on both sides of zero is also a limit from the right (section 1.3.3), so this one-sided derivative equals \(-2a\), and it is not zero. The tangent at the largest value is horizontal only when that value is attained in the interior of the interval.

The converse is false too: a zero derivative does not yet mean an extremum (figure 1.279, left). The function \(t^3\) has the derivative \(3 \cdot 0^2 = 0\) at the point \(0\), but on no interval around \(0\) does it have its largest or smallest value at \(0\), because it is negative to the left of \(0\) and positive to the right, for example \((-0{,}1)^3 = -0{,}001 < 0 < 0{,}001 = 0{,}1^3\) (LEBL1, PDF p. 166). The lemma only tells where an extremum can be: at a point where the derivative is zero, at a point where there is no derivative, and at an end point (LEBL1, PDF p. 163). The absolute value \(|x|\), for example, has its smallest value at \(0\), where it has no derivative.

Let us draw both examples, each at its own scale. On the left are the axes and the blackwhite curve, the graph of the function \(t^3\). The blue dot at \(0\) with the horizontal blue line is the point where the tangent is horizontal. The tangent lies exactly on the axis \(t\), because it has slope zero and passes through the origin, so the blue line is drawn over part of the axis. The curve crosses the axis there from the negative to the positive side, so \(0\) is neither the largest nor the smallest value. On the right are other axes and the blackwhite curve, the graph of the function \(t^3 - t\) on \([0, 1]\). The blackwhite dots with the labels \(0\) and \(1\) are the end points, where the value is \(0\). The grey dashed line downward from the axis \(t\) and the horizontal grey dashed line to the axis \(g\) show where the lowest point is: at \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3\) with the value \(-0{,}3849\). The empty blue circle marks this point. It is empty because \(\xi\) is not a fraction, and the blue horizontal line through the circle is the tangent there.

1.14.4 Rolle's theorem

From history

Michel Rolle (1652 to 1719) looked for limits between which lie the places where the value of a polynomial is zero. His theorem did not arise from graphs but from equations. It is not yet in his Traité d'algèbre of 1690, it appears in his Méthode pour résoudre les égalités (Paris, 1691) (CAJORI, PDF p. 240). He transformed the polynomial and arranged it by powers, and equated the coefficients of the powers to zero. He called these equations cascades (kaskade). They are the derivative of the original polynomial, the derivative of the derivative and so on, each put equal to zero. He solved them from the lowest degree upward, which is very laborious (CAJORI, PDF p. 240-241). He started from a statement that in modern form reads: between two successive places where the derivative is zero there is no more than one place where the value of the polynomial is zero (CAJORI, PDF p. 241). This statement follows from another one: between two places where the value of the polynomial is zero lies a place where the derivative is zero, that is, a place with a horizontal tangent. If two places with value zero lay between successive places with derivative zero, there would have to be one more place with derivative zero between them, and the places would not be successive. At first Rolle rejected the differential calculus and held it to be a collection of ingenious fallacies (BALL, PDF p. 346), and he had a controversy about it with Pierre Varignon (CAJORI, PDF p. 236). He obtained the cascades, which are derivatives in content, by arranging in powers, that is, by algebra and not by calculus.

The statement about the horizontal tangent that Rolle used for polynomials we can now prove for a function that has the same value at both end points of an interval. Its graph begins and ends at the same height. If the function is not constant, somewhere in between it rises above this height or falls below it, and then it must come back. At the highest or lowest place in between the tangent is horizontal (figure 1.281). That the highest place exists is guaranteed by the extreme value theorem 1.73, and that the tangent is horizontal there by lemma 1.82 on the derivative at an interior extremum.

Let us draw the figure. The axes show \(t\) horizontally and the value \(f\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the function, drawn only on \([a, b]\). The blackwhite dots above \(a\) and \(b\) are its start and end, with dashed verticals to the axis. The grey dashed horizontal line is drawn through both dots. The dots are at the same height, because \(f(a) = f(b)\). The blue dot above \(\xi_1\) is the highest point of the graph, the blue dot above \(\xi_2\) the lowest, and each is dropped to the axis by a grey dashed line. In both, a blue horizontal tangent is drawn. At the highest and the lowest point it is horizontal, because by lemma 1.82 on the derivative at an interior extremum the tangent at a point of largest or smallest value that is not an end point is horizontal.

A function is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at each of its points (definition of continuity 1.58), and differentiable on an interval if it is differentiable at each of its points, at an endpoint that belongs to the interval with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9). The theorem requires continuity on the closed interval because the extreme value theorem needs it, and a derivative only at the points between the end points because the lemma on the derivative at an interior extremum speaks only about them. The theorem does not need one-sided derivatives at the end points. But a function that is differentiable on the whole closed interval is also continuous on it by lemma 1.79 that a differentiable function is continuous, so both hypotheses hold for it.

Theorem 1.83 Rolle's theorem (Rolleov izrek)

Let the function \(f\) be continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) and differentiable at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\), and let \(f(a) = f(b)\). Then there is a point \(\xi \in (a, b)\) at which \(f'(\xi) = 0\).

Proof

We must show that \(f\) has a zero derivative at some point between \(a\) and \(b\). We will look for it among the points where \(f\) attains its largest and its smallest value.

  1. The function \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\), so by the extreme value theorem 1.73 there are points \(x_{\max}\) and \(x_{\min}\) in \([a, b]\) such that \(f(x_{\min}) \le f(y) \le f(x_{\max})\) for every \(y \in [a, b]\).
  2. Let \(K = f(a) = f(b)\). At least one of three cases holds: (A) there is a \(y \in [a, b]\) with \(f(y) > K\), (B) there is a \(y \in [a, b]\) with \(f(y) < K\), (C) \(f(y) = K\) for every \(y \in [a, b]\). Indeed, if neither (A) nor (B) holds, every value is at once at most \(K\) and at least \(K\), which is (C).
  3. Case (A). By step 1, \(f(x_{\max}) \ge f(y) > K\), so \(f(x_{\max})\) differs from \(f(a)\) and from \(f(b)\). So the point \(x_{\max}\) is neither \(a\) nor \(b\), but lies in \((a, b)\). There \(f\) is differentiable, and by lemma 1.82 on the derivative at an interior extremum \(f'(x_{\max}) = 0\). For \(\xi\) we take \(x_{\max}\), which is the point \(\xi_1\) in the figure.
  4. Case (B). By step 1, \(f(x_{\min}) \le f(y) < K\), so \(x_{\min}\) lies in \((a, b)\), and by the same lemma \(f'(x_{\min}) = 0\). For \(\xi\) we take \(x_{\min}\), which is the point \(\xi_2\) in the figure.
  5. Case (C). For \(\xi\) we take the midpoint \(\tfrac12(a + b)\). For \(0 < |\Delta x| < \tfrac12(b - a)\) the point \(\xi + \Delta x\) lies in \((a, b)\), the numerator of the difference quotient is \(K - K = 0\), and the quotient is \(0 + 0 \cdot \Delta x\). By lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, \(f'(\xi) = 0\).
  6. In each of the cases we have found \(\xi \in (a, b)\) with \(f'(\xi) = 0\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 4.2.3 (PDF pp. 163-164).

From history

The name Rolle's theorem was first used by Moritz Wilhelm Drobisch in 1834 and by Giusto Bellavitis in 1846 (CAJORI, PDF p. 240).

Where does the completeness axiom enter the proof of Rolle's theorem? In step 1. The extreme value theorem rests on the supremum and on the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57, and both rest on the completeness axiom. The other steps use only the arithmetic rules and the definition of the limit, which hold for fractions too. That we cannot do without the axiom is shown by the function \(g(t) = t^3 - t\) on the interval \([0, 1]\). The values at the end points are equal, \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g(1) = 1 - 1 = 0\). The function is a polynomial, so it is continuous by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions and differentiable by lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, with the derivative \(g'(t) = 3t^2 - 1\). The derivative is zero when \(t^2 = \tfrac13\). On \((0, 1)\) the only such point is \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3 = 0{,}577\dots\), because by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, there is only one nonnegative number with the square \(\tfrac13\). This is the same point as in figure 1.111, and in section 1.8.1 we showed that it is not a fraction. If we knew only fractions, the function \(g\) would have the value \(0\) at both end points, but its derivative would be zero at no point. The conclusion of the theorem would be false, and since the other steps hold among fractions, the proof would break down exactly in step 1. The lowest point of the graph of the function \(g\) is namely at \(\xi\) (figure 1.279, right), which is not a fraction. Among fractions the values of the function \(g\) come arbitrarily close to the lowest value, but none attains it, so there is no smallest value among fractions.

Among the real numbers the point \(\xi\) exists, and the proof finds it in case (B). The value there is

\[g(\xi) = \xi\,(\xi^2 - 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 3} \cdot \Big({-\frac23}\Big) = -\frac{2\sqrt 3}{9} = -0{,}3849\dots ,\]

less than the value \(0\) at the end points. By the extreme value theorem, \(g\) attains a smallest value somewhere on \([0, 1]\). It is not at an end point, because \(g(\xi)\) is smaller than the values at the end points. So by the lemma on the derivative at an interior extremum it is at a point with zero derivative, and the only such point is \(\xi\).

Both hypotheses of the theorem are needed (figure 1.283).

For each example let us write down where the derivative is and which hypothesis fails:

  • Left, \(|x|\) on \([-1, 1]\): the derivative is \(-1\) left of \(0\) and \(1\) right of it, at \(0\) there is none. Differentiability fails.
  • Right, the function with the value \(x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(0\) at \(1\): the derivative is \(1\) at every point of \((0, 1)\), and at \(1\) there is a jump. Continuity fails.

Let us look at the computations. In the first example, the absolute value \(|x|\) on the interval \([-1, 1]\), the function has equal values at the end points, \(|-1| = |1| = 1\). For \(x > 0\) and \(0 < |\Delta x| < x\) we have \(x + \Delta x > 0\), so \(|x + \Delta x| - |x| = \Delta x\), the quotient is \(1\), and by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple the derivative is \(1\). In the same way the derivative is \(-1\) for \(x < 0\). At \(0\) there is no derivative, as we showed in section 1.14.1, but the function is continuous there. At every other point it is continuous by lemma 1.79, because it has a derivative there. So the function is continuous on \([-1, 1]\), but it has no point with zero derivative. The hypothesis that fails is differentiability at every point of \((-1, 1)\): the function attains its smallest value exactly at the corner, where the lemma on the derivative at an interior extremum says nothing. The second example is the function that equals \(x\) for \(0 \le x < 1\) and has the value \(0\) at \(1\). The values at the end points are equal, both \(0\), and at every point of \((0, 1)\) the derivative is \(1\). Again there is no point with zero derivative. Here continuity fails at the end point \(1\): the values just left of \(1\) are close to \(1\), but the value at \(1\) is \(0\). So the function has no largest value on \([0, 1]\), and step 1 of the proof fails. Indeed, at every point \(m < 1\) the value \(m\) is smaller than the value \(\tfrac12(m + 1)\) at the point \(\tfrac12(m + 1)\), which lies between \(m\) and \(1\), and the value \(0\) at the point \(1\) is smaller than the value \(\tfrac12\) at the point \(\tfrac12\). Exactly such a jump is excluded by the requirement from section 1.7.3 that the function has no jump at the end points.

1.14.5 The mean value theorem

Rolle's theorem is a special case of the fact we are looking for. When \(f(a) = f(b)\), the straight line through the points of the graph above the end points is horizontal, and a point with a horizontal tangent is a point where the tangent is parallel to this line. But what if the line is tilted (figure 1.240)? The idea is to straighten it. From each value \(f(t)\) we subtract the height of the line above \(t\). What remains are the vertical distances between the graph and the line (the figure shows them twice: on the left next to the line, on the right hanging from the axis \(t\)), which are zero at both end points (figure 1.284). Rolle's theorem holds for the new function, and the point where its tangent is horizontal is the point where the tangent of the old graph is parallel to the line.

Let us draw both parts. On the left are the axes and the blackwhite curve, the graph of the function \(t^3\). The blackwhite dots are the points of the graph above \(0\) and above \(1\), with a grey dashed vertical at \(1\). The grey dashed line \(\ell\) passes through both dots. Three grey measures show the vertical distance between the line and the graph at \(t = 0{,}2\), \(0{,}4\) and \(0{,}8\). The line through \((0, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\) is at height \(t\) above the number \(t\), the graph is at height \(t^3\), so the distance is the height of the line minus the height of the graph, \(t - t^3\), that is \(0{,}192\), \(0{,}336\) and \(0{,}288\). The blue segment in the left part is the tangent of the graph at \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3\), with a blue dot and a grey dashed line to the axis. It is parallel to the line \(\ell\), because it has the slope \(3\xi^2 = 1\), as the line has. On the right are other axes and the blackwhite curve, the graph of the function \(g(t) = t^3 - t\), that is the graph minus the line. The same three distances now hang from the axis \(t\). They point downward, because the graph is below the line and \(g\) is negative. The dots at \(0\) and \(1\) are now on the axis, because at both end points the difference is zero. The blue horizontal line with the blue dot is the tangent at the lowest point, at \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3\), where \(g = -0{,}3849\).

The straight line through the points of the graph above \(a\) and above \(b\) has the slope

\[k = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} ,\]

and above the point \(t\) it is at the height \(\ell(t) = f(a) + k\,(t - a)\): at the point \(a\) at the height \(f(a)\), and every step to the right raises it by \(k\) times the length of the step.

Theorem 1.84 Mean value theorem (izrek o srednji vrednosti)

Let the function \(f\) be continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) and differentiable at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\). Then there is a point \(\xi \in (a, b)\) for which

\[f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a) . \tag{1.31}\]
Proof

We must show that at some point \(\xi\) the tangent has the slope \(k = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\) of the straight line through the points of the graph above the end points. We subtract this line from \(f\) and use Rolle's theorem.

  1. Let \(\ell(t) = f(a) + k\,(t - a)\) be the height of the line above the point \(t\). At the end points \(\ell(a) = f(a) + k \cdot 0 = f(a)\) and \(\ell(b) = f(a) + k\,(b - a) = f(a) + \big(f(b) - f(a)\big) = f(b)\).
  2. Let \(g(t) = f(t) - \ell(t)\) for \(t \in [a, b]\), which is the signed distance between the graph and the line. By step 1, \(g(a) = f(a) - f(a) = 0\) and \(g(b) = f(b) - f(b) = 0\).
  3. The function \(\ell\) is a polynomial, \(\ell(t) = (f(a) - k\,a) + k\,t\), so by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions it is continuous at every point. It is also continuous as a function on \([a, b]\), because the definition of continuity 1.58 on a smaller set checks only some of the same points. Since \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), by the same corollary the difference \(g = f + (-1)\,\ell\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) too.
  4. By lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, items 1, 2 and 4, \(\ell\) has the derivative \(0 + k \cdot 1 = k\) at every point.
  5. Let \(x \in (a, b)\). The functions \(f\) and \(\ell\) are defined on the open interval \((a, b)\) and differentiable at \(x\). By items 1 and 2 of the same lemma, \(g\) is differentiable at \(x\) and \(g'(x) = f'(x) + (-1) \cdot k = f'(x) - k\).
  6. The function \(g\) satisfies the hypotheses of Rolle's theorem 1.83: it is continuous on \([a, b]\) by step 3, differentiable at every point of \((a, b)\) by step 5, and \(g(a) = g(b)\) by step 2. So there is a \(\xi \in (a, b)\) with \(g'(\xi) = 0\), that is, \(f'(\xi) - k = 0\) and \(f'(\xi) = k\).
  7. We multiply the equality \(f'(\xi) = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\) by \(b - a\) and get \(f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a)\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 4.2.4 (PDF pp. 164-165).

The quotient \((f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\) is the average change of the function per unit change of \(t\) between \(a\) and \(b\), and the derivative is the change per unit just at a point. The theorem says that the average is attained at some point in between. Hence the name: the mean value here is the average. When driving a car, where \(f(t)\) is the distance travelled up to the time \(t\) (section 1.3.2), the theorem says that at some instant the car was going exactly at the average speed of the whole trip (LEBL1, PDF p. 165, figure 1.285). The theorem is also called Lagrange's theorem (Lagrangeov izrek).

Let us draw the drive. The axes show the time \(t\) in hours and the distance travelled \(s\) in kilometres. The blackwhite curve is the distance, made up so that the car travelled \(150\) km in two hours. The grey dashed line goes from the start to the point at \(t = 2\), so its slope is the average speed, \(150/2 = 75\) km/h. The blue segment is the tangent at the time \(\xi = 1\) h. Its slope is the speed at that instant, \(s'(1) = 45 + 30 = 75\) km/h, so it is parallel to the dashed line.

Now we can read the first fact of section 1.7.3 precisely. "A derivative at every point between two numbers" is differentiability at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\). "No jump at the end points" is continuity at \(a\) and at \(b\). Continuity at the points between \(a\) and \(b\) need not be required separately, because there the derivative guarantees it by lemma 1.79. Both hypotheses are needed, as the examples of figure 1.283 show, which are examples for this theorem too.

The theorem does not say where the point \(\xi\) is, only that it exists. Let us look at two examples where we can compute it. For \(f(t) = t^3\) on \([0, 1]\), \(k = (1 - 0)/(1 - 0) = 1\), and from \(f'(\xi) = 3\xi^2 = 1\) it follows that \(\xi = 1/\sqrt 3 = 0{,}577\dots\), the point of figure 1.111. For this function the proof subtracted the line \(\ell(t) = t\) and got the function \(g(t) = t^3 - t\), which we met with Rolle's theorem (figure 1.284).

Next the hanging bar (figure 1.286). A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points from the top downwards, and the displacement of the point at depth \(x\) is \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (section 1.2). The displacement is a polynomial, so it satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem on every interval. For the whole bar, on \([0, L]\),

\[\frac{u(L) - u(0)}{L} = \frac{a\,(2L^2 - L^2) - 0}{L} = aL = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

This is the total elongation divided by the length, that is, the average strain \(\Delta L / L\) of section 1.2. The theorem says that it equals the strain \(u'(\xi) = 2a\,(L - \xi)\) at some point \(\xi\) of the bar. From \(2a\,(L - \xi) = aL\) it follows that \(L - \xi = L/2\), so \(\xi = L/2 = 5\) m (the blue dot in the right part of the figure), where the tangent in the left part is parallel to the line. We noticed this already in section 1.4: the average strain of the bar equals the strain at its middle.

Let us draw both parts. In the left part the axes show the depth \(x\) horizontally, although the bar hangs downward, and the displacement \(u\) vertically. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The blackwhite dots at \(0\) and at the bottom at \(10\) are its end points, the lower one with a grey dashed line to the axis, and above \(x = 10\) the displacement is \(19{,}52\ \mu\text{m}\). The grey dashed line passes through both dots. Its slope is the total elongation divided by the length, that is the average strain \(aL\). The blue tangent is drawn at \(x = 5\), where the blue dot stands with a grey dashed line to the axis, and it is parallel to the line, because the theorem asks for a point with the same slope.

In the right part the axes show \(x\) and the strain \(\varepsilon\). The blackwhite straight line is the strain \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\). It falls evenly, because the displacement is a quadratic polynomial, and at the top it has the value \(2aL\). The grey dashed horizontal line at height \(aL\) is the average strain of the whole bar. It meets the blackwhite line at \(x = 5\) m, where the blue dot is, and this is the same number as in the left part. The short blue segment above the interval from \(0\) to \(1\) m is the average strain of the top piece, \(19a = 3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The blue dot on it, at \(x = 0{,}5\), shows that this is the strain at the middle of the piece.

The same holds for every piece. For the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), by (1.6),

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = 2a\,\Big(L - \big(x + \tfrac12 \Delta x\big)\Big) = u'\big(x + \tfrac12 \Delta x\big) ,\]

so \(\xi\) is always the middle of the piece. For the top piece of length 1 m the strain of the piece is \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the first number in the table of section 1.2, and this is the strain at the point \(x = 0{,}5\) m (top piece), \(2a\,(10 - 0{,}5)\ \text{m} = 19\ \text{m} \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} = 3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Why exactly the middle? The strain \(2a\,(L - x)\) decreases evenly along the bar, by \(2a\) per metre, as the weight carried by a cross section does. So at a point some distance above the middle of the piece the strain is larger than the strain at the middle by as much as it is smaller at the point the same distance below the middle. The two differences are equally large and of opposite sign, so they cancel in the sum, and the average of the strain over the piece is the value at the middle, which the computation above with \(u'\big(x + \tfrac12 \Delta x\big)\) also tells us. For \(t^3\) the derivative \(3t^2\) does not grow evenly, and \(\xi = 0{,}577\dots\) is not the middle of the interval \([0, 1]\).

What does the theorem mean for strains? The strain of a piece, that is, its elongation divided by its length, is exactly the strain at some point of the piece, whenever the displacement satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem:

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = u'(\xi) \qquad \text{za neki } \xi \text{ med } x \text{ in } x + \Delta x .\]

In section 1.2 we took the strain of a piece as an approximation of the strain just at the point \(x\), \(\varepsilon \approx (u(x + \Delta x) - u(x))/\Delta x\) ((1.2)). So this approximation is off only by the difference between the strains at \(\xi\) and at \(x\). The shorter the piece, the closer \(\xi\) is to \(x\). The theorem cannot be used, and its conclusion can fail, when the displacement has a jump in the piece, for example at a crack (razpoka), where the material is torn apart, or when at some point of the piece it has no derivative, as at the joint of two different materials.

1.14.6 Two consequences

The mean value theorem connects the change of a function over a whole interval with the derivative at one point. So what we know about the derivative at all points we can carry over to the change. Two consequences follow, which we will use often for strains.

The first answers the question from section 1.4. There we saw that a displacement that is the same for all points does not change the strain, and that therefore the displacement cannot be computed from the strain completely. Is this the only freedom? If two displacements of a bar have the same strain at every point, do they differ only by such a constant shift? A constant function is, as in lemma 1.81, item 4, a function that has the same value at all points (figure 1.288, left).

The proof is shown in figure 1.287.

Let us draw the graph. The axes show \(t\) and \(f\). The blackwhite segment is the graph of a function with zero derivative, drawn horizontal because the proof shows that it is. The point \(a\), the point \(t\) and the end point \(b\) each have a dot on the graph and a dashed line to the axis. The measure line below the axis is the interval \([a, t]\), on which we use the mean value theorem. The blue dot is the point \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(t\), where the derivative is zero and the tangent lies on the graph itself. The label beside the axis \(f\) says that the values at \(a\) and at \(t\) are equal.

Corollary 1.85 A function with zero derivative is constant (funkcija z ničelnim odvodom je stalna)

Let the function \(f\) be continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\), differentiable at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\), and let \(f'(t) = 0\) for every \(t \in (a, b)\). Then \(f(t) = f(a)\) for every \(t \in [a, b]\).

Proof

We must show that the value at every point \(t\) equals the value at \(a\). We use the mean value theorem on the interval \([a, t]\).

  1. For \(t = a\) there is nothing to prove. Let \(a < t \le b\).
  2. The function \(f\) is continuous on \([a, t]\). The definition of continuity 1.58 on the smaller set \([a, t]\) checks only some of the same points as on \([a, b]\), so the condition that holds on \([a, b]\) holds on \([a, t]\) too.
  3. The function \(f\) is differentiable at every point \(s \in (a, t)\) and \(f'(s) = 0\), because \(s\) also lies in \((a, b)\). The difference quotient at \(s\) uses only values at points close to \(s\), so the derivative is the same whether we regard \(f\) on \([a, b]\) or on \([a, t]\).
  4. By the mean value theorem 1.84 on the interval \([a, t]\) there is a \(\xi \in (a, t)\) such that \(f(t) - f(a)\) \(= f'(\xi)\,(t - a)\).
  5. The point \(\xi\) lies in \((a, b)\), so \(f'(\xi) = 0\), \(f(t) - f(a) = 0\) and \(f(t) = f(a)\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 4.2.6 (PDF p. 165), for a function that is differentiable on the whole interval.

Let us use it on a bar of length \(L\) with the axis \(x\) along the bar. Let \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) be two displacements, continuous on \([0, L]\), that have the same strain at every point \(x \in (0, L)\), \(u_1'(x) = u_2'(x)\). The difference \(f = u_1 + (-1)\,u_2\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, and by lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, items 1 and 2, it has the derivative \(u_1'(x) - u_2'(x) = 0\) at every point \(x \in (0, L)\). By corollary 1.85 it is constant:

\[u_1(x) - u_2(x) = u_1(0) - u_2(0) \qquad \text{for every } x \in [0, L] .\]

So the two displacements differ only by the same shift of all points, by a rigid-body displacement along the bar. Once this shift is fixed, for example by the condition \(u(0) = 0\) for a fixed end, the strain determines the displacement exactly. For the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight with its top fixed, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) is therefore the only displacement with the strain \(2a\,(L - x)\) and with \(u(0) = 0\). A bar that has zero strain at every point does not deform, it only moves as a whole (figure 1.288, left). The argument does not need the strains at the ends of the bar, which are one-sided derivatives (definition 1.9), but it needs the displacements to be continuous at the ends. One of the following two is enough:

  • the displacements are continuous on \([0, L]\) and \(u_1' = u_2'\) on \((0, L)\),
  • or \(u_1' = u_2'\) everywhere on \([0, L]\), the ends included, and then by lemma 1.79 that a differentiable function is continuous the displacements are continuous on \([0, L]\), so continuity is automatic.

Continuity is essential here. Take a bar of length \(L\) lying on a table, with the axis \(x\) along it from the left end. The bar breaks at its middle, and we move the right part away by \(s\) without stretching it (figure 1.288, right). Each part moves as a whole, so the strain is zero at every point except at the break. But the distance between the ends of the bar has grown by \(s\). At the break the displacement has no single value, because the two faces of the break move differently, so the displacement is not a continuous function on \([0, L]\). The corollary does not hold here, because its hypothesis does not hold. Across a crack we cannot infer the displacements from the strains, and this is why we will assume that displacement fields have no jumps.

Let us draw both examples, the left and the right one. The blackwhite bar with marks at the top is the bar before the movement. Its vertical ticks are marks at equal spacing, so that it can be seen whether a piece of the bar stretches. The orange bar below it is the same bar after the movement, with the same marks. It is moved by \(s\) to the right. The dimension lines \(s\) between the rows show the movement of the first and the last mark, each with two grey dashed lines, down from the mark before the movement and up from the mark after it. Both dimension lines are equally long, because each mark moved by the same distance and the bar did not stretch. Below are the axes for \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and for the displacement \(u\). The horizontal blackwhite line at height \(s\) is the graph of the displacement: it is constant, because each point moves by \(s\).

On the right the bar before the movement with the marks is the same. The orange bar has a gap: the left part stays, the right part moves by \(s\), and the marks go with them. The dimension lines \(s\) at the middle and at the right end are equal. The first shows the gap that appeared at the break, the second the movement of the right end. Below it are the axes with the label \(L/2\) at the break. The lower blackwhite line is the displacement of the left part, zero, so it lies on the axis \(x\). The upper one is the displacement of the right part, \(s\). The empty circles at \(L/2\) say that the displacement there is neither zero nor \(s\), so it cannot be written with a single value.

The second consequence bounds the change of a function when all we know about the derivative is that its size does not exceed some bound.

Corollary 1.86 Bounded derivative (omejen odvod)

Let the function \(f\) be continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) and differentiable at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\), and let \(M\) be a number for which \(|f'(t)| \le M\) for every \(t \in (a, b)\). Then

\[|f(b) - f(a)| \le M\,(b - a) .\]
Proof

We must show that the size of the change is not larger than the bound of the derivative times the length of the interval.

  1. By the mean value theorem 1.84 there is a \(\xi \in (a, b)\) such that \(f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a)\).
  2. The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values (lemma 1.6, item 2), and \(b - a > 0\), so \(|f(b) - f(a)|\) \(= |f'(\xi)|\,(b - a)\).
  3. By the hypothesis \(|f'(\xi)| \le M\). We multiply this inequality by the positive number \(b - a\), which does not reverse it, and get \(|f'(\xi)|\,(b - a) \le M\,(b - a)\).
  4. By steps 2 and 3, \(|f(b) - f(a)| \le M\,(b - a)\).

Source: LEBL1 Exercise 4.2.3 (PDF p. 169), where the claim is left to the reader as an exercise, stated as Lipschitz continuity (LEBL1 Def. 3.4.7, PDF p. 141).

For a bar the corollary says: if the strain at every point of a piece is at most \(M\) in size, the piece gets longer or shorter by at most \(M\) times its length. For the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight the strain \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\) is at most \(M = 2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for every \(x \in (0, L)\). So the total elongation is at most

\[M\,L = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}\]

(figure 1.289). The actual elongation, \(u(L) = aL^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, is half as large, because the strain is that large only at the top and falls evenly to zero towards the bottom. The estimate is rough, but it needs no displacement, only a bound on the strain. This is also how we will use the corollary: when we do not know the displacement exactly but know that its derivative is not too large.

Let us draw the figure. The axes show the depth \(x\) horizontally, although the bar hangs downward, and the displacement \(u\) vertically, at a different scale from \(x\), because the displacements are much smaller than the length. The blackwhite curve is the graph of the displacement, which ends with a dot at \(x = L\). The strain is nowhere larger than \(M = 2aL\), so the slope of the graph is everywhere between \(-M\) and \(M\). Two grey dashed lines through the origin have exactly these slopes, and the blue wedge between them is the region that the graph cannot leave. At \(x = L\) the upper line is at height \(ML = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m. This is the upper dimension line, which is the whole bound on the elongation. The lower dimension line is the actual displacement \(u(L) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m. It is shorter, because the strain is that large only at the top.

With this the first fact from the end of section 1.7.3 is proved. The road to it led from the hole among the fractions through the completeness axiom, sequences and their subsequences to functions without jumps and their largest value.
