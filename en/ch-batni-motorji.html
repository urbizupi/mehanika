The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

Here the motion of a system of bodies, which we wrote with equations in chapter 8, meets the vibration that we treated on models in chapters 9, 10 and 13. From the first we have the equations of motion of a rigid body and Lagrange's equations, from the others the response to forced excitation and the natural frequencies. The chapter applies them to a machine everyone knows: the engine with pistons.

We explain the words of the name in turn and draw the machine they speak of as we go (figure 23.1, left). An engine (motor) is a machine that turns the energy of the fuel burnt in it into the turning of a shaft, which drives the wheels of a car, a pump or a generator. The Slovenian name motor comes from the Latin verb movere, to move. The shaft points perpendicular out of the drawing, so we draw it as the point \(O\). At this point the shaft sits in the frame, a heavy housing that holds all the other parts, and can turn in it. Reciprocating (batni) means that pistons (bati) drive the shaft, and the English word itself says that they move back and forth. In the frame is a cylinder, a tube closed at its upper end, and in it slides a piston, a piece of metal that fits the wall of the cylinder tightly. Above the piston the fuel burns, and the hot gas pushes the piston towards the shaft. The piston moves back and forth in the cylinder, while the shaft turns. The motion along a line is turned into rotation by a rod, joined to the piston by a hinge, and an arm (ročica), a short piece that is part of the shaft and to which the other end of the rod is hinged. When the rod pushes the end of the arm, the arm turns the shaft in the direction of the arrow. In the same way a cyclist pushes a pedal with the foot, and the pedal turns the pedal axle through the pedal arm.

Dynamics (dinamika) is the description of motion together with the forces that cause it. The name comes from the Greek word dynamis, power or force. Balancing (uravnoteženje) means that we distribute the mass of the machine, or add weights to it, so that the forces with which the rotating parts and the parts that move back and forth act on the frame vanish or become smaller. On the wheel at bottom right, the mass \(m\) that is too much at one place is evened out by an equal mass on the opposite side. The name recalls a balance, the scale that stays level when the weights on its two sides are equal. Vibration isolation (izolacija nihanj) means that we separate the frame from the ground with springs, so that only a small part of its shaking passes on to the ground. The name comes from the Latin word insula, island: on its springs the frame stands apart from the ground like an island.

Why does this matter? The wheel of a car that is badly balanced shakes the steering wheel at high speed (figure 23.1, top right). At one place of the wheel there is a mass \(m\) too much, say because the tyre is thicker there. This mass runs with the wheel on a circle. Without a force it would move on along a straight line (Newton's first law), so the wheel must pull it towards the axle all the time. By Newton's third law the mass pulls the axle towards itself through the wheel with a force of the same size. This pull turns with the wheel: a quarter of a turn later it points up to the left, half a turn later down to the left. So in every turn the axle is pulled in all directions, and through the suspension of the car we feel this as a shaking of the steering wheel. The mechanic fixes a weight of the same mass on the opposite side of the rim (bottom right). It pulls the axle with a force of the same size in the opposite direction, the two forces cancel and the axle feels nothing.

An engine whose parts move back and forth shakes its frame even when all its rotation is perfectly balanced (figure 23.1, left). The piston stops at each end of its path and then speeds up in the opposite direction. For this it needs a force (Newton's second law), which the frame gives it through the rod, the arm and the shaft. By Newton's third law the moving parts therefore push the frame with the opposite force, which points now up, now down. If we bolted the frame to the ground, this force would shake the ground and everything standing on it. So we place it on rubber or spring mounts, springs between the frame and the ground. On them the frame rises above its rest position under the force and sinks below it. The force with which a spring pushes on the ground changes in proportion to how much the spring is further compressed or stretched. A heavy frame, however, moves only a little under fast shaking, since a large motion back and forth in a short time would need a much larger force (Newton's second law). So the springs are compressed and stretched only a little and pass only a small part of the force on to the ground. Under slow shaking the frame follows the force instead, and the springs pass all of it on, near resonance even enlarged. Section 23.8 shows where the limit lies.

The evenness of the shaft's turning decides whether the machine runs smoothly or in jerks. The gas pushes the piston hard only for a short time after the fuel burns, so the shaft receives its turning moment in jerks and turns now faster, now slower. A heavy disk on the shaft, turning with it, evens out these jerks: during a jerk it takes up part of the kinetic energy and gives it back when there is none.

Section by section we shall do the following. First we balance a rigid body turning on two bearings, like the wheel at right, only longer. Then we describe the motion of the piston and compute the forces that one cylinder exerts on the frame, then the turning moment on the shaft and how a disk that turns with the shaft evens out its jerks. Next we show what can be balanced with one cylinder and what with several. After that we compute how an engine on mounts passes its shaking on to the ground, and finally at which speeds the shaft twists in resonance.
