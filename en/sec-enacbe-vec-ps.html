Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.1 Equations of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom (gibalne enačbe sistema z več prostostnimi stopnjami)

What equation describes small motions of a system with several coordinates about equilibrium, and where do the matrices in it come from? Chapter 9 showed this for one coordinate (theorem 9.3) and chapter 8 for \(n\) coordinates without damping and without an external force (theorem 8.136). Here we combine the two. First we look at a simple system with two coordinates, on which we shall test everything as we go.

10.1.1 The frame with two floors (okvir z dvema stropoma)

Frame A is our laboratory model (figure 10.3). Two rigid floors (strop) have a mass \(m = 500\) kg each. The lower floor stands on the ground on two steel columns (stebri), the upper one on the lower one, again on two columns. All columns are \(h = 1\) m long and have the \(20\ \text{mm} \times 40\ \text{mm}\) cross section from chapter 5. The section is mounted so that the column bends in the direction of the 40 mm side, so \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\) and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2\), as for the cantilever in section 5.9.2. Each column is clamped at both ends: at the bottom into the ground or into the lower floor, at the top into the upper floor, and it cannot rotate in the clamp. The floors move only horizontally. The displacement of the lower floor from equilibrium is denoted \(q_1\), that of the upper floor \(q_2\), both positive to the right.

How much force does a floor need to move by \(q\)? Take a single column (figure 10.4, (c)), of length \(h\). At the lower clamp it can neither move nor rotate. The upper end moves by \(v\) with the floor, but it cannot rotate, because the rigid floor holds it. Such a column is the cantilever from section 5.9.5, loaded at the free end by a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple \(M_0\), with which the floor prevents the rotation. In the figure the force is labelled \(F\), and the numbers in it hold for \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) kN.

We build the displacement of the end from two parts, each in its own panel.

  1. The force alone, panel (a). The force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) moves the end of the cantilever by \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h^3/(3EI) = 15{,}94\) mm and tilts it with the slope \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h^2/(2EI)\) (equation (5.64) with \(M_0 = 0\)).
  2. The couple alone, panel (b). The couple \(M_0\) moves the end by \(M_0 h^2/(2EI)\) and tilts it by \(M_0 h/(EI)\) (the same equation with \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\)).
  3. Both together. The equation of the deflection line is linear, so the two parts add (section 5.9.5). The displacement and the slope of the end are
    \[v(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{3EI} + \frac{M_0\,h^2}{2EI} , \qquad v'(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^2}{2EI} + \frac{M_0\,h}{EI} .\]
  4. The condition of the floor. The end does not rotate, \(v'(h) = 0\), so \(M_0 = -F_{\mathrm{y}} h/2\). The sign says that the couple turns the end back, against the tilt that the force gives. Its part of the displacement is \(-F_{\mathrm{y}} h^3/(4EI)\), which is 11,96 mm back towards the initial axis.
  5. The sum, panel (c):
    \[v(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{3EI} - \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{4EI} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{12EI} .\]
    At \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) kN this is \(15{,}944 - 11{,}958 = 3{,}986\) mm (rounded in the figure).

The force that moves the end of the column by \(v\) is therefore \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = (12EI/h^3)\,v\), which is the force of a spring with the stiffness

\[k_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{12EI}{h^3} = 250\,880\ \text{N/m} .\]

This is four times the stiffness of a cantilever with a free end, \(3EI/h^3 = 62\,720\) N/m: the couple of the floor takes away three quarters of the displacement that the force alone would give. The two columns between the ground and the lower floor, or between the lower and the upper floor, together we call a storey (etaža). Their forces add, so the stiffness of a storey (storey stiffness, togost etaže) is

\[k = 2k_{\mathrm{s}} = 501\,760\ \text{N/m} .\]

The same holds for the upper storey: both ends of its columns move but do not rotate, and the force depends only on the difference of the displacements, since a rigid displacement of the columns causes no force.

Why does the axis have the shape of an S? There is no load along the column, so the shear force is constant, \(V = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), and by the differential relations (corollary 5.11) the moment satisfies \(M' = -V = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). At the end \(M(h) = M_0 = -F_{\mathrm{y}} h/2\), so \(M(x) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,(h/2 - x)\). The moment is zero at \(x = h/2\) and has opposite signs on the two sides of this point. The curvature is proportional to the moment, \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35), so the lower half of the column curves to one side, the upper half to the other.

Our model has three idealizations. For each we say why we may make it here, when we may not, and how much it changes the result.

  1. The columns have no mass. The lower and the upper columns have the mass \(\rho A h = 7800 \cdot 8\cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1\ \text{kg} = 6{,}24\) kg each, so a pair has 12,48 kg, which is 2,5 % of the mass of a floor. We therefore assign all the mass to the floors. If we add the mass of each half of a column to the floor it is clamped in (\(512{,}48\) kg and \(506{,}24\) kg), the natural frequencies of the frame (definition 8.140) decrease by \(0{,}8\ \%\) and \(1{,}1\ \%\) (NumPy). The idealization fails when the mass of the columns is no longer small against the mass of the floors: then it must be divided between the ends of the columns, as section 10.8 will do for a bar.
  2. The floors do not rotate. A floor is so stiff that the end of a column stays vertical. Panel (d) shows what happens if it is not: the floor rotates by the angle \(\theta\) and the end of the column moves more at the same force. Let the floor resist the rotation with the moment \(M_0 = -\beta\,(EI/h)\,v'(h)\), where the number \(\beta\) measures the stiffness of the floor against the stiffness of the column. The computation is the same as above, only this relation replaces the condition \(v'(h) = 0\) of step 4: the slope is \(v'(h) = F_{\mathrm{y}} h^2/(2EI\,(1 + \beta))\) and the displacement
    \[v(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{12EI}\cdot\frac{4 + \beta}{1 + \beta}\]
    (SymPy). The stiffness of the column is therefore \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\,(1 + \beta)/(4 + \beta)\): at \(\beta = 100\) this is \(97{,}1\ \%\) of ours, at \(\beta = 20\) \(87{,}5\ \%\), at \(\beta = 2\) a half (\(7{,}97\) mm instead of \(3{,}99\) mm) and at a hinge, \(\beta = 0\), a quarter. Our model is the limit \(\beta \to \infty\). It is good for a thick slab or a deep beam on slender columns, but not for a thin slab on strong columns.
  3. We do not count the axial forces in the columns. The shortening of a column along its axis is of second order for small displacements, so the floors stay at the same height. The columns do carry the weight of the floors, however: each lower column \(2m g/2 = 4905\) N, each upper one \(m g/2 = 2452{,}5\) N. A compressive axial force \(P\) reduces the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) of a column approximately by \(P\int_0^h \psi'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 6P/(5h)\), where \(\psi = 3(x/h)^2 - 2(x/h)^3\) is the shape of the axis of a column without axial force that does not rotate at its ends (CK (8.33b), PDF p. 254, integral with SymPy). The share is \(P h^2/(10EI)\): \(2{,}35\ \%\) of the stiffness of a column in the lower storey and \(1{,}17\ \%\) in the upper one, and the natural frequencies of the frame decrease by \(1{,}0\ \%\) and \(0{,}75\ \%\) (NumPy). The idealization fails for heavy floors: at \(P = 10EI/h^2 = 209\) kN the stiffness would by this estimate drop to zero and the column would no longer hold the floor upright. Our force of \(4905\) N is \(2{,}3\ \%\) of this value. Chapter 12 will treat this term.

The errors of all three idealizations are therefore of the order of one per cent of the frequency, all in the same direction: the real frame vibrates somewhat more slowly than the model.

Now we can write the equations of motion of the frame. First with Newton, then with Lagrange.

Example 10.1 Equation of motion of the frame with two floors (gibalna enačba okvira z dvema stropoma)

Frame A as above, on floor \(i\) let a horizontal external force \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) act, positive to the right. We look for the equations for \(q_1(t)\) and \(q_2(t)\).

First way, forces on the floors.

  1. A floor is a rigid body that moves horizontally, so by theorem 8.82 the mass times the acceleration equals the sum of the horizontal forces: \(m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum F_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The ends of the lower pair of columns are displaced relative to each other by \(q_1\) (the lower end is in the ground). The pair acts on the lower floor with the force \(-k\,q_1\), since the force on the end of a column equals the stiffness times the relative displacement of the ends and points towards equilibrium.
  3. The ends of the upper pair of columns are displaced relative to each other by \(q_2 - q_1\). The pair acts on the upper floor with the force \(-k\,(q_2 - q_1)\), so by the law of action and reaction (section 2.1) the upper floor acts on the pair with the force \(+k\,(q_2 - q_1)\). The pair has no mass, so the sum of the forces on it is zero and the lower floor acts on it with the force \(-k\,(q_2 - q_1)\). By the law of action and reaction the pair acts on the lower floor with the force \(+k\,(q_2 - q_1)\).
  4. The equation of the lower floor is \(m\ddot{q}_1 = -k q_1 + k (q_2 - q_1) + Q_1\), of the upper one \(m\ddot{q}_2 = -k (q_2 - q_1) + Q_2\). We move the terms with \(q\) to the left:
    \[\begin{aligned} m\ddot{q}_1 + 2k\,q_1 - k\,q_2 &= Q_1 , \\ m\ddot{q}_2 - k\,q_1 + k\,q_2 &= Q_2 . \end{aligned}\]

Second way, Lagrange's equations.

  1. The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot{q}_1^2 + \tfrac12 m\dot{q}_2^2\) (theorem 8.97).
  2. Each pair of columns behaves as a spring with the stiffness \(k\) with respect to the relative displacement of its ends. If the relative displacement is \(\delta\), the force grows from zero to \(k\delta\), its work is \(\int_0^\delta k s\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12 k\delta^2\) (section 4.1) and this work is stored as potential energy. The relative displacement of the lower pair is \(q_1\), of the upper \(q_2 - q_1\), so \(V = \tfrac12 k q_1^2 + \tfrac12 k (q_2 - q_1)^2\).
  3. The derivatives are \(\partial V/\partial q_1 = k q_1 - k(q_2 - q_1) = 2k q_1 - k q_2\) and \(\partial V/\partial q_2 = k(q_2 - q_1)\). The external force on floor \(i\) has the generalized force \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}\), because its point of application moves by \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (definition 8.51).
  4. The kinetic energy does not contain the coordinates, so \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}} = m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\). By theorem 8.101 therefore \(m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}\), which equals the equations of step 4.

In matrix form, where \(\mat M\) holds the masses of the floors and \(\mat K\) the stiffnesses of the storeys,

\[\begin{aligned} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{M}}\begin{bmatrix} \ddot{q}_1 \\ \ddot{q}_2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{K}}\begin{bmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} Q_1 \\ Q_2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad k = 501\,760\ \text{N/m} , \ m = 500\ \text{kg} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.1}\]

The matrix \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix of the potential \(V\) (definition 8.69): \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_1^2 = 2k\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_1\partial q_2 = -k\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_2^2 = k\) (SymPy). The equation is exactly linear, because \(V\) is a quadratic function and \(\mat M\) is constant, so the remainder from theorem 8.136 is zero.

Source: CK section 8.1 and Example 8.1 (PDF p. 230): Newton's laws for a system of springs and masses, among the models a building with three floors, and the matrix form (8.1) (PDF p. 229). We did not find a frame with two floors in the sources. The model, the storey stiffness from section 5.9.5 and the numbers are ours.

Why does \(\mat K\) have this form? The element \(K_{22} = k\) is the stiffness of one storey, because the upper floor is held only by the upper pair of columns. The element \(K_{11} = 2k\) is the sum of both stiffnesses, because a displacement of the lower floor shifts the ends of both pairs of columns. The off-diagonal element \(K_{21} = -k\) says that when we move the lower floor to the right, the upper pair of columns pulls the upper floor to the right with the force \(k\), so we must hold it with the force \(-k\), that is to the left.

10.1.2 The chain of masses on springs (veriga mas na vzmeteh)

Frame A has two floors. How do we write the equations of a system with arbitrarily many parts in a row? The simplest such system is the chain of masses on springs (veriga mas na vzmeteh, figure 10.5). The masses, \(N\) of them, slide without friction on the horizontal ground. A spring joins neighbouring masses, and a spring ties the first and the last mass to a wall. There are therefore \(N + 1\) springs: the spring \(k_1\) between the left wall and the first mass, the spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\) between mass \(i - 1\) and mass \(i\), the spring \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1}\) between the last mass and the right wall. The coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the displacement of mass \(i\) from the equilibrium position, in which all springs are unstretched, positive to the right. The model has three idealizations: a mass moves only along the chain, the springs are linear and massless, there is no friction. What a massless spring costs is told by the number in section 10.6 (one percent of the force at a buffer of 200 kg). Frame A is such a chain with \(N = 2\): the floors are the masses, the pairs of columns are the springs \(k_1 = k_2 = k\), and there is no right wall and no spring \(k_3\).

Let us write Newton's law for mass \(i\) in the middle of the chain. Only the springs attached to it and the external force act on it.

  1. The spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\) on the left lengthens by the difference of the displacements of its ends, \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) minus \(q_{\mathrm{i}-1}\). The lengthened spring pulls mass \(i\) back, to the left, with the force \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}-1})\).
  2. The spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) on the right lengthens by \(q_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) minus \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) and pulls mass \(i\) to the right with the force \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}+1} - q_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  3. The mass times the acceleration equals the sum of the forces on the free mass, where \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) is the external force, positive to the right:
    \[m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{i}} = -k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}-1}) + k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}+1} - q_{\mathrm{i}}) + Q_{\mathrm{i}} .\]
  4. We move the terms with the displacements to the left and order them by the displacements:
    \[m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{i}} - k_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}-1} + (k_{\mathrm{i}} + k_{\mathrm{i}+1})\,q_{\mathrm{i}} - k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\,q_{\mathrm{i}+1} = Q_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{10.2}\]

This is row \(i\) of the matrix equation, drawn below the chain. It has only three nonzero elements. On the diagonal is the sum of the stiffnesses of the two springs attached to mass \(i\): if we move only this mass, one spring lengthens and the other shortens, and both push it back. Left of the diagonal is \(-k_{\mathrm{i}}\) and right of it \(-k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\): a neighbour that moves to the right drags mass \(i\) along, and on the left side of the equation this force has the sign minus. All other elements of the row are zero, because no spring ties mass \(i\) to masses farther away. For the first and the last mass the neighbour is a wall, which does not move, so the same row holds with \(q_0 = 0\) and \(q_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\) respectively. A matrix with such rows is called tridiagonal (tridiagonalna): it has nonzero elements only on the diagonal and right next to it.

Theorem 10.2 Equation of motion of the chain of masses (gibalna enačba verige mas)

Let a chain of masses be given: \(N \ge 1\) masses \(m_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) that move along one line, with the displacements \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the position in which the springs are unstretched. Let linear massless springs have the stiffnesses \(k_1 \ge 0\) (between the left wall and mass 1), \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) for \(i = 2, \dots, N\) (between the masses \(i - 1\) and \(i\)) and \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} \ge 0\) (between mass \(N\) and the right wall), where the stiffness zero means that there is no spring and no wall. Let an external force \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) along the line act on mass \(i\). Let \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{N}})\) and let \(\mat K\) be the tridiagonal matrix of order \(N\) with the elements \(K_{\mathrm{ii}} = k_{\mathrm{i}} + k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), \(K_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}+1} = K_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{i}} = -k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) and zero elsewhere. Then:

  1. The motion of the chain solves the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q(t)\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), since it is a sum of stiffnesses times squared stretches of springs,
    \[\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = k_1\,q_1^2 + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=2}^{N} k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}-1})^2 + k_{\mathrm{N}+1}\,q_{\mathrm{N}}^2 . \tag{10.3}\]
  3. If the chain hangs on at least one wall (\(k_1 > 0\) or \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} > 0\)), then \(\mat K\) is positive definite (definition 6.27) and \(\mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) holds only for \(\vek q = \vek 0\).
  4. If there is no wall (\(k_1 = k_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\)), then \(\mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) exactly when all displacements are equal, \(\vek q = c\,(1, \dots, 1)\). The sum of the elements of every row of \(\mat K\) is then zero and \(\mat K\) has no inverse.
Proof

We must show the equation from Newton's law for every mass, and then the properties of the matrix from the stretches of the springs. The steps point at figure 10.5.

  1. For mass \(i\) equation (10.2) holds, derived in steps 1 to 4 before the theorem from theorem 8.82, for the first mass with \(q_0 = 0\) and for the last with \(q_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\). The coefficients of the displacements are by definition the elements of row \(i\) of \(\mat K\), so these \(N\) equations are the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q\). This is point 1.
  2. The symmetry follows from the definition, \(K_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}+1} = K_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{i}}\): the same spring \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) pulls mass \(i\) and mass \(i + 1\) with forces of equal size.
  3. We expand the right side of (10.3). The square \(q_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) appears in two terms, with the coefficients \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), together \(K_{\mathrm{ii}}\). The product \(q_{\mathrm{i}-1}q_{\mathrm{i}}\) appears only in the term \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(q_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}-1})^2\), with the coefficient \(-2k_{\mathrm{i}} = K_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}} + K_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i}-1}\). These are exactly the terms of the sum \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}} q_{\mathrm{j}}\). Every term on the right is a stiffness times the square of the stretch of one spring, so the sum is not negative. This is point 2.
  4. Let \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = 0\). A sum of nonnegative terms is zero only if every term is zero. Since \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) for \(i = 2, \dots, N\), we have \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) for all these \(i\): all displacements equal the same number \(c\) and no inner spring is stretched.
  5. Let \(k_1 > 0\). From \(k_1 q_1^2 = 0\) follows \(q_1 = 0\), so \(c = 0\) and \(\vek q = \vek 0\). The same for \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} > 0\) from \(q_{\mathrm{N}} = 0\). For \(\vek q \ne \vek 0\) therefore \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q > 0\), which is positive definiteness. If \(\mat K\vek q = \vek 0\), then also \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = 0\) and so \(\vek q = \vek 0\). This is point 3.
  6. Let \(k_1 = k_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\) and \(\vek s = (1, \dots, 1)\). Element \(i\) of the column \(\mat K\vek s\) is the sum of row \(i\), that is \(-k_{\mathrm{i}} + (k_{\mathrm{i}} + k_{\mathrm{i}+1}) - k_{\mathrm{i}+1} = 0\) (in the first row \(k_1 = 0\) is missing, in the last \(k_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\)). So \(\mat K\,(c\,\vek s) = \vek 0\). Conversely, from \(\mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) follows \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = 0\) and by step 4 \(\vek q = c\,\vek s\).
  7. If \(\mat K\) had an inverse (definition 1.110), from \(\mat K\vek s = \vek 0\) would follow \(\vek s = \mat K^{-1}\mat K\vek s = \vek 0\), which is not true. This is point 4.

Source: CK Example 8.1 (PDF p. 231 to 232): Newton's laws for three masses on springs and the matrix form. The general \(N\), the conditions on the springs and the proof of points 2 to 4 are not from the source.

The proof read all the properties from a single picture: \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) is the sum of the squares of the stretches of the springs, and a displacement that stretches no spring requires no force. This conclusion will return with the rigid modes in section 10.5.

Chain V. Our standing example of a chain has \(N = 3\) equal masses \(m = 2\) kg and four equal springs \(k = 5000\) N/m. On it we compare three kinds of ends (figure 10.6): two walls, one wall and none. We assemble the matrices by the row rule (10.2), and they differ only in the corner elements: \(K_{33} = 2k\) with the right wall and \(K_{33} = k\) without it, because a single spring then acts on the last mass.

A single experiment shows what separates the three cases: we move all three masses from the equilibrium position by the same \(s\), for example by \(s = 10\) mm, so that the inner springs do not stretch. The forces that hold this displacement are \(\mat K\,(s, s, s)\), that is \(s\) times the row sums of the matrix on the right.

  • Two walls, panel (a). At the walls the left spring lengthens and the right one shortens, both by \(s\). We must hold the first and the last mass, each with the force \(k s = 50\) N, and not the middle one. The row sums are \((k, 0, k)\).
  • One wall, panel (b). Only the left spring stretches, and we hold only the first mass with \(k s = 50\) N. The row sums are \((k, 0, 0)\). The matrix is still positive definite (theorem 10.2, point 3).
  • No walls, panel (c). No spring changes its length and the displacement requires no force. The row sums are \((0, 0, 0)\), and the matrix has no inverse (point 4): the chain can move as a rigid body.

The sum of row \(i\) is therefore the stiffness of the spring that ties mass \(i\) to a wall. The walls also affect the vibration. The natural circular frequencies (definition 8.140) of chain V are, in the unit \(\sqrt{k/m} = 50\) rad/s, equal to \(0{,}765\), \(1{,}414\) and \(1{,}848\) with two walls, \(0{,}445\), \(1{,}247\) and \(1{,}802\) with one, and \(0\), \(1\) and \(1{,}732\) without walls (NumPy). Every wall taken away lowers all three, and for the chain without walls the first is zero: this is the rigid displacement that section 10.5 treats. How the frequencies are computed, section 10.2 tells.

In the lectures the displacements of the masses are \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), the column of displacements \(\{x\}\), and the matrices \(\boldsymbol M\) and \(\boldsymbol K\). The masses are given there in the unit \(m\), the springs in the unit \(k\) and the frequencies in the unit \(\sqrt{k/m}\), as above.

10.1.3 The equation of motion for \(n\) coordinates (gibalna enačba za \(n\) koordinat)

The frame and the chain are special cases. What equation holds for a system with \(n\) coordinates that also has dampers? Damper \(k\) (definition 9.1) has a coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) and joins the points \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the system at the distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}|\) (figure 10.7, (a)). A damper resists only the change of its length. Of the velocities of the two points therefore only that part of their difference counts which points along the damper, in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\): this is the rate \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) at which the length changes. The damper acts on the two points with equal and opposite forces along its axis, on \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) and on \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\). They are drawn for a damper that is lengthening, so they pull the points together.

The distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a function of all coordinates, so the rate of its change \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a sum of contributions of all velocities \(\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\), each with its own coefficient \(\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}\). For the damper \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) of frame A in panel (b) we have \(\ell = \ell_0 + q_1\), so \(\partial\ell/\partial q_1 = 1\) and \(\partial\ell/\partial q_2 = 0\). The force of a damper is proportional to \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\), so we expect the generalized force of the dampers to be a linear function of the velocities, with a square table of coefficients. We announce its elements in advance:

\[C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad s, j = 1, \dots, n . \tag{10.4}\]

The matrix \(\mat C\) with these elements is symmetric, since \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) does not change if we swap \(s\) and \(j\). The theorem will show that indeed \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\). The proof derives this from the definition of a damper (definition 9.1), from the generalized force (definition 8.51) and from the chain rule.

Theorem 10.3 Equation of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom (gibalna enačba sistema z več prostostnimi stopnjami)

Let a system have \(n\) degrees of freedom with generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) on an open set \(U \subset \mathbb R^n\) (definition 8.46), ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) by theorem 8.97 with continuously differentiable \(\mat M\). Let the applied forces be conservative with a three times continuously differentiable potential \(V(\vek q)\). Let the viscous dampers (definition 9.1) have coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) and join points with twice continuously differentiable positions \(\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\) and \(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\), whose distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}|\) is positive everywhere on \(U\). Let the external forces have the generalized force \(\vek Q(t)\), a continuous function of time. Let \(\mat C(\vek q)\) be the matrix with the elements (10.4). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\mat C(\vek q)\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), continuously differentiable, the generalized force of all dampers on the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\) and equals \(-\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\) with the dissipation function \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q}\).
  2. A twice continuously differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) is a motion of the system if and only if for \(s = 1, \dots, n\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} \\ &= Q_{\mathrm{s}}(t) , \\ &g_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\Big(\frac{\partial M_{\mathrm{sj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}} \\ &\quad - \frac12\,\frac{\partial M_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big)\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{k}} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.5}\]
  3. Along the motion \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(T + V) = \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q} \le \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q}\).
  4. In the plane of coordinates (a) let \(\vek q_0 \in U\) be an equilibrium, \(\nabla V(\vek q_0) = \vek 0\), and let \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek q_0)\), \(\mat C_0 = \mat C(\vek q_0)\) and \(\mat K\) the matrix of second derivatives of \(V\) at \(\vek q_0\). Then \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat K\) is symmetric and \(\mat C_0\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. For the displacement \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0\) there exist numbers \(\rho > 0\) and \(c\) such that for every time at which \(|\vek x| \le \rho\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C_0\,\dot{\vek x} \\ &\quad + \mat K\,\vek x = \vek Q(t) + \vek R , \\ &|R_{\mathrm{s}}| \le c\,\big(|\vek x|^2 + |\dot{\vek x}|^2 \\ &\quad + |\vek x|\,|\dot{\vek x}| + |\vek x|\,|\ddot{\vek x}|\big) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.6}\]
Proof

We must show that the dampers contribute the generalized force \(-\mat C\dot{\vek q}\), that Lagrange's equations with it give (10.5), and then bound the terms that are not linear in the displacement and its derivatives by the remainder \(\vek R\). Let \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\) be the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\).

  1. Let \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})/\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the unit vector from \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) towards \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) (panel (a)). From \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\) and the product rule (lemma 1.82) follows \(2\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = 2\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\), so \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\). The function \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is twice continuously differentiable, because it is the root of a positive twice continuously differentiable function.
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, point 1) for the function \(t \mapsto \ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q(t))\) we have \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\). By definition 9.1 the force on \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) and on \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) it is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By definition 8.51 the generalized force of both forces of damper \(k\) on the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}\cdot\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}}\cdot\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}) = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) by step 1. By step 2 this is \(-\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the sum over all dampers is \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. Since \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a linear function of the velocities by step 2, \(\partial\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = -Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}}\) by step 3. From step 2 we also have \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q}\).
  5. The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric, because the product \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric in \(s\) and \(j\). For every column \(\vek z\) we have \(\vek z^\T\mat C\vek z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,z_{\mathrm{s}}\big)^2 \ge 0\) (term \(k\) is the square of the rate of change of the length \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) at the velocity \(\vek z\)), so \(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite. The elements \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) are continuously differentiable, because the \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) are continuously differentiable. This is point 1. The generalized force of the forces that are not in the potential is \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\).
  6. By theorem 8.101 the motion is a motion of the system if and only if for \(\mathcal L = T - V\) we have \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}}\) (the right side is the generalized force of the dampers and of the external forces). The left side depends only on \(T\) and \(V\), so steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 8.136 stay the same (they do not contain the right side) and give \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}}\). When we move the term with \(\mat C\) to the left, we get (10.5). All steps are equivalences, so the converse holds too. This is point 2.
  7. Theorem 8.121 with \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\) from step 5 gives \(\mathrm{d}(T + V)/\mathrm{d}t = \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q}\). The term with \(\mat C\) is not positive by step 5. This is point 3.
  8. The displacement \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0\) shifts the coordinates by a constant, so \(\dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek q}\), \(\ddot{\vek x} = \ddot{\vek q}\). The functions \(\tilde V(\vek x) = V(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\), \(\tilde M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek x) = M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\) and \(\tilde C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek x) = C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\) have the same derivatives as \(V\), \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) and \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\), and \(\vek x = \vek 0\) is an equilibrium. Let \(\rho\) be so small that the closed ball \(|\vek x| \le \rho\) lies in \(\{\vek x : \vek q_0 + \vek x \in U\}\). In panel (b) the horizontal axis \(x\) runs along the displacement, the vertical axis \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) shows the derivative, and the marks \(-\rho\) and \(\rho\) bound the displacements inside the ball. The estimates from steps 5 to 7 of the proof of theorem 8.136 do not contain the right side of the equation and hold unchanged for \(\tilde V\) and \(\tilde M_{\mathrm{sj}}\): \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}x_{\mathrm{j}} + r_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(|r_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac12 n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}}|\vek x|^2\), \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\,\ddot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}|\vek x|\,|\ddot{\vek x}|\) and \(|g_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac32 n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}|\dot{\vek x}|^2\), where \(c_{\mathrm{V}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\) are the largest absolute values of the third derivatives of \(V\) and of the first derivatives of \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball.
  9. We estimate the difference \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0)\) like the difference of \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) in step 6 of the same proof: by the mean value theorem 1.85 on the function \(\tau \mapsto \tilde C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek x)\) and lemma 8.29. Let \(c_{\mathrm{C}}\) be the largest absolute value of the derivatives \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball (theorem 8.27, the derivatives are continuous by step 5). Then \(|C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0)| \le n\,c_{\mathrm{C}}\,|\vek x|\) and \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0))\,\dot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{C}}\,|\vek x|\,|\dot{\vek x}|\).
  10. In (10.5) we insert \(M_{\mathrm{sj}} = M_{0,\mathrm{sj}} + (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\), the same for \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\), and step 8. We get (10.6) with \(R_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\ddot{x}_{\mathrm{j}} - g_{\mathrm{s}} - r_{\mathrm{s}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} (C_{\mathrm{sj}} - C_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\dot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\). By steps 8 and 9 the inequality for \(R_{\mathrm{s}}\) holds with \(c = n^2\max\big(\tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{V}},\ \tfrac32 c_{\mathrm{M}},\ c_{\mathrm{C}}\big)\), since \(n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}} \le \tfrac32 n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\).
  11. The matrix \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite by theorem 8.97, \(\mat K\) is symmetric because mixed derivatives commute (theorem 8.31). The matrix \(\mat C_0\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite by step 5. This is point 4.

The matrix \(\mat C\) is not necessarily positive definite. In frame A the horizontal damper between the post on the ground and the lower floor has the length \(\ell = \ell_0 + q_1\), so \(\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} c_{\mathrm{d}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vek z^\T\mat C\vek z = 0\) for \(\vek z = (0, 1)^\T\): the damper does not resist a motion of the upper floor on its columns while the lower one is at rest, because its length does not change in that motion.

The theorem says what the model is. The exact equation (10.5) is in general nonlinear, because \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\mat M\) and \(\mat C\) are nonlinear functions of the coordinates, and the dependence of \(\mat M\) on the coordinates also gives the quadratic form of the velocities \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\). When the displacement is small, a linear equation remains in the unknown \(\vek x\) with a remainder of second order, as for one coordinate. Chapter 8 already warned that the theorem does not bound the error of the solution in time, but the difference between the equations at each moment, and so we take the linear equation as a model whose validity we check with numbers. The displacement from equilibrium we from now on again write \(\vek q\), as in chapter 9, and the equation

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t) \tag{10.7}\]

we call the equation of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom. The matrix \(\mat M\) is the mass matrix (masna matrika), \(\mat K\) the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) and \(\mat C\) the damping matrix (dušilna matrika), all with \(n \times n\) elements. For \(n = 1\) this is equation (9.8) from chapter 9: the matrices correspond to the numbers \(m\), \(c\) and \(k\).

For coordinates with different units, such as a length and an angle, different elements of the matrices have different units, as we saw for the bar on springs (section 8.13.3). The quadratic form \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) always has the unit J, the same as energy, and the quadratic form \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) the unit W, the same as power.

10.1.4 Stiffness and flexibility coefficients (togostni in podajnostni koeficienti)

In the theorem the matrix \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix of the potential. For a structure of linearly elastic material we do not need to compute the potential and differentiate it twice. Chapter 7 defined the matrix as the matrix of forces that hold the state (definition 7.4): the element \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the force in the direction of unknown \(i\) that we need so that \(q_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) and all other displacements are zero. The two descriptions are the same matrix. The state \(\vek q\) is an equilibrium state under the forces \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\), so by Castigliano's first theorem (theorem 4.92) the force in the direction of \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) that holds this state is \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(U(\vek q)\) is the strain energy of the structure. The potential of the structure differs from \(U\) by a term that is linear in \(\vek q\) (the work of constant loads) and does not change the second derivatives. Therefore \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} = \partial F_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}\), and since \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\) is a linear function by definition 7.4, the derivative is \(\partial F_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} = K_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The elements \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are called stiffness coefficients (togostni koeficienti), as CK (8.16) (PDF p. 248) calls them, in the spirit of the flexibility coefficients of chapter 4. Since \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) says how a displacement at place \(j\) influences the force at place \(i\), they are also called stiffness influence coefficients (togostni vplivni koeficienti), and the elements \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the flexibility matrix flexibility influence coefficients (definition 4.139). The columns of \(\mat K\) we get by the column rule: we move one coordinate by a unit and hold all the others at zero, and the forces that hold this state are a column of the matrix.

In frame A the first column is the force we need to move the lower floor by 1 m while the upper one stays. The relative displacement of the lower pair is \(1\) m, of the upper one \(-1\) m. The columns act on the lower floor with the force \(-k\cdot 1 + k\cdot(-1) = -2k\) and on the upper one with the force \(-k\cdot(-1) = k\), so the floors must be held by the forces \(2k\) and \(-k\). The column \((2k, -k)\) is indeed the first column of the matrix from (10.1). The second column (upper floor 1 m, lower one at rest) is \((-k, k)\) (figure 10.9, top).

The converse question is: how much does a point move if a unit force acts on it? Chapter 4 introduced the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) for it (definition 4.139), whose columns are the displacements due to unit forces. In frame A we get \(\mat D\) without computing with matrices. A force \(F\) on the upper floor runs through both pairs of columns, because the upper pair holds the upper floor and the lower pair holds both. The lower floor therefore moves by \(F/k\) and the upper one by \(F/k + F/k = 2F/k\). A force \(F\) on the lower floor loads only the lower pair, so both floors move by \(F/k\). The first column is \((1, 1)/k\) and the second \((1, 2)/k\), so

\[\mat D = \frac1k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.8}\]

The two matrices are inverse to each other, which we shall use in computations.

Lemma 10.4 Stiffness and flexibility matrix (togostna in podajnostna matrika)

Let a structure be of linearly elastic material, with its state determined by a column \(\vek q\) of displacements of \(n\) chosen points in chosen directions (the joints of a truss or of a frame of massless beams), so that every column \(\vek q\) gives a state that respects the supports. Let \(\mat K\) be the stiffness matrix (definition 7.4) for these displacements. Let there be, for every column of forces \(\vek F\) at these points and in these directions, exactly one state that the forces \(\vek F\) hold. For this the supports must prevent every small rigid motion, which chapter 4 showed for the truss of two bars. Let \(\mat D\) be the matrix whose column \(j\) is the column of displacements in the state held by the unit force \(\vek F = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\). This is the flexibility matrix (definition 4.139), with points in place of patches. Then \(\mat K\mat D = \mat D\mat K = \mat I\), so \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\). If \(\mat K\) is symmetric, \(\mat D\) is symmetric too.

Proof

We must show that the forces \(\vek F\) hold exactly the state \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\). We use that the state for given forces is unique.

  1. Column \(j\) of \(\mat D\) is \(\mat D\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) (definition 1.97) and by assumption it is the state held by the force \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) (in frame A the displacements of the floors under a unit force). By definition 7.4 this state is held by the forces \(\mat K\mat D\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\mat K\mat D\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. Column \(j\) of \(\mat K\mat D\) is \((\mat K\mat D)\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat K(\mat D\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})\) (lemma 1.100), by step 1 this is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) for every \(j\), and \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\).
  3. Let \(\vek q\) be an arbitrary column of displacements. The state \(\vek q\) is held by the forces \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\) (definition 7.4), as the forces on the floors hold the unit displacement of the lower floor. The state \(\mat D\vek F\) is held by the forces \(\mat K(\mat D\vek F) = (\mat K\mat D)\vek F = \vek F\) by step 2. For the forces \(\vek F\) the state is unique by assumption, so \(\vek q = \mat D\vek F = \mat D\mat K\vek q\). For \(\vek q = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) column \(j\) of \(\mat D\mat K\) equals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\mat D\mat K = \mat I\).
  4. Steps 2 and 3 show \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\) by definition 1.110. Let \(\mat K^\T = \mat K\). From \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\) follows \(\mat D^\T\mat K = \mat I\) by lemma 1.102, so \(\mat D^\T = \mat D^\T(\mat K\mat D) = (\mat D^\T\mat K)\mat D = \mat D\) (lemma 1.100).

Source: Not from a source. CK (11.49) and footnote 4 (PDF p. 367) use \(\mat K^{-1}\) as the flexibility matrix and remark that it does not exist for systems with rigid modes. Check on a truss of two bars: chapter 4, verified with SymPy.

The lemma says when the flexibility matrix exists: when the supports prevent every rigid motion. For a system without supports, such as an aeroplane or a machine in space, the matrix \(\mat K\) has no inverse and the lemma does not hold. The chapter will treat such systems separately in section 10.5.

The same holds for the truss from chapter 4, where the product of the matrices was checked with SymPy.

Chain W. Frame A has only two coordinates, so both its matrices are full and do not show how \(\mat K\) and \(\mat D\) differ. Take chain V with two walls and change its springs: from left to right let them have the stiffnesses \(2k\), \(k\), \(3k\) and \(k\), with \(k = 5000\) N/m (figure 10.10). This is chain W. By the row rule (10.2) the diagonal holds the sum of the neighbouring springs, and next to it stands the spring between the two masses with a minus:

\[\mat K = k\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & -3 \\ 0 & -3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.9}\]

The top row of the figure assembles the same matrix by the column rule. In every panel we move one mass from the equilibrium position by a unit, hold the other two at rest, and draw the forces with which we must hold all three.

  1. The first column. We move the first mass by 1 to the right. The spring \(2k\) on its left lengthens by 1 and pulls it back with the force \(2k\), the spring \(k\) on its right shortens by 1 and pushes it back with the force \(k\). We therefore hold it with the force \(2k + k = 3k\) to the right. The shortened spring \(k\) also pushes the second mass, to the right, so we hold the second mass with the force \(k\) to the left, that is \(-k\). The springs at the third mass did not change their length, so the third mass needs no force. The column is \((3k, -k, 0)\). For a displacement of 10 mm these are the forces \(150\) N, \(-50\) N and \(0\).
  2. The second column. We move the middle mass. The spring \(k\) lengthens, the spring \(3k\) shortens, so we hold the middle mass with \(k + 3k = 4k\), the first with \(-k\) and the third with \(-3k\). The column is \((-k, 4k, -3k)\).
  3. The third column. We move the third mass. The spring \(3k\) lengthens, the spring \(k\) at the wall shortens, so we hold the third mass with \(4k\) and the middle one with \(-3k\), and the first needs no force. The column is \((0, -3k, 4k)\).

Where do the zeros in the corners of the matrix come from? A unit state moves a single mass. Only the two springs attached to it change their length, so only this mass and its two neighbours feel forces. A mass that is not a neighbour feels nothing, and \(K_{31} = K_{13} = 0\). This is why the stiffness matrix of a chain is tridiagonal for every number of masses.

A common mistake with this rule is to move one mass and leave the other two free. The words "we hold the other two" mean forces: the arrows above the neighbouring masses are part of the state. If we move the first mass of chain W by 1 and do not hold the others, they move too, by \(4/7\) and \(3/7\), and the force on the first mass is only \(17k/7 = 2{,}43\,k\) instead of \(K_{11} = 3k\) (the numbers follow from the bottom row of the figure). The element \(K_{11}\) is therefore not the stiffness we feel when we push the first mass of the free chain, but the force for its unit displacement with the other masses held.

The bottom row of the figure assembles the flexibility matrix. A force \(F\) acts on the first mass and we hold no mass. The force has two paths to the walls, and we compute the displacements in four steps.

  1. The left path is the spring \(2k\). The right path is the springs \(k\), \(3k\) and \(k\) one after another. The same force runs through all three and their shortenings add, so the flexibilities add: \(1/k + 1/(3k) + 1/k = 7/(3k)\). The stiffness of the right path is \(3k/7\).
  2. Both paths start at the first mass and so have the same displacement \(q_1\), while their forces add: \(F = (2k + 3k/7)\,q_1 = (17k/7)\,q_1\). Therefore \(q_1 = 7F/(17k)\).
  3. Through the right path runs the force \((3k/7)\,q_1 = 3F/17\). The last spring \(k\) shortens under it by \(3F/(17k)\), and this is the displacement of the third mass, \(q_3 = 3F/(17k)\). The spring \(3k\) shortens by \(F/(17k)\), so \(q_2 = 4F/(17k)\).
  4. Check: the spring \(k\) between the first and the second mass shortens by \(q_1 - q_2 = 3F/(17k)\), which is indeed the force \(3F/17\) divided by \(k\).

The displacements \((7, 4, 3)/(17k)\) at a unit force are the first column of \(\mat D\). At \(F = 100\) N they are \(8{,}24\) mm, \(4{,}71\) mm and \(3{,}53\) mm. In the same way we get the second column from a force on the middle mass and its displacements, and the third from a force on the third mass:

\[\mat D = \frac{1}{17k}\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 4 & 3 \\ 4 & 12 & 9 \\ 3 & 9 & 11 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat K\mat D = \mat I \tag{10.10}\]

(SymPy). Comparing the two rows of the figure shows three things.

  • The matrix \(\mat K\) is sparse, the matrix \(\mat D\) is full. A unit displacement loads only the two neighbouring springs, but a unit force runs through all the springs to both walls and moves all the masses. This holds for every chain with \(N \ge 3\) masses that hangs on at least one wall (without walls there is no \(\mat D\), theorem 10.2, point 4). For \(N = 2\), as in frame A, a tridiagonal matrix has no room for a zero.
  • The element \(D_{13} = 3/(17k)\) is not zero although \(K_{13} = 0\): the third mass needs no force at a unit displacement of the first, but it moves at a unit force on the first. The inverse matrix is not the matrix of the reciprocals of the elements. They do not agree elsewhere either: \(1/K_{11} = 1/(3k)\) against \(D_{11} = 7/(17k)\), and \(1/K_{12} = -1/k\) against \(D_{12} = 4/(17k)\), which even has the other sign.
  • The matrix \(\mat D\) is symmetric, as lemma 10.4 says: the displacement of the third mass due to a force on the first equals the displacement of the first due to a force on the third, both times \(3/(17k)\). This is Maxwell's theorem from section 4.8.5.

In the lectures the stiffness influence coefficients are \(k_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The flexibility matrix is there called prožnostna matrika \(\boldsymbol P\), and its elements \(p_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the flexibility influence coefficients (prožnostni vplivni koeficienti). The book writes \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(\mat D\) and \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\), because in section 10.2.3 \(\mat P\) is the matrix of a change of coordinates.

The flexibility matrix gives the equation of motion a second form.

Corollary 10.5 Flexibility form of the equation of motion (podajnostna oblika gibalne enačbe)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\), \(\mat K\) and \(\vek Q\) be as in equation (10.7) and let \(\mat K\) be invertible with the inverse matrix \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\). Then \(\vek q(t)\) is a solution of equation (10.7) if and only if

\[\mat D\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat D\mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek q = \mat D\vek Q(t) . \tag{10.11}\]
Proof

We must show that one equation follows from the other by multiplication with an invertible matrix.

  1. If (10.7) holds, we multiply it from the left by \(\mat D\). Since \(\mat D\mat K = \mat I\) by the definition of the inverse matrix (definition 1.110), we get (10.11).
  2. If (10.11) holds, we multiply it from the left by \(\mat K\). Since \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\), we get (10.7).

Source: CK (11.49) (PDF p. 367) for the flexibility matrix. The proof is not from a source.

What is the second form good for? When it is easier to compute the displacements due to unit forces than the forces for unit displacements, we do not need the matrix \(\mat K\) at all. In frame A we read \(\mat D\) directly from the unit forces and their displacements and (10.8). The matrix \(\mat D\mat M\) is not necessarily symmetric, even if \(\mat D\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric. Its transpose is \(\mat M\mat D\) by lemma 1.102, which equals \(\mat D\mat M\) only if the matrices commute. In frame A \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and \(\mat D\mat M = (m/k)\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is symmetric, but for the machine with coordinates of different units below it is not. The motion in which all coordinates move in step, \(\vek q = \vek\varphi\cos\omega t\), which we shall look for in the next section, we insert into equation (10.11) without damping and force and get \(-\omega^2\,\mat D\mat M\vek\varphi + \vek\varphi = \vek 0\). We keep the search for motions in step in the symmetric form with the matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\), and the flexibility form says the same.

An example in which the way through \(\mat D\) is shorter is a beam with masses. Take a cantilever of length \(L\) with the bending stiffness \(EI\), clamped in a wall, that carries the point masses \(m_1\) at the middle and \(m_2\) at the end (figure 10.11). The coordinates \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are the deflections of the beam at the masses, measured downwards from the unloaded axis.

Example 10.6 Flexibility matrix of a cantilever with two masses (podajnostna matrika konzole z dvema masama)

The cantilever as above, the beam has no mass. We look for \(\mat D\) and \(\mat K\) for the deflections \((q_1, q_2)\). Numbers: \(L = 1\) m and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2\), as for a column of frame A.

By equation (5.57) its deflection at a place \(x \le a\) has the magnitude \(F x^2 (3a - x)/(6EI)\), at the force \(F a^3/(3EI)\), and the slope at the force is \(F a^2/(2EI)\). Beyond the force the moment is zero, so the curvature \(v'' = M/(EI)\) is zero there (theorem 5.35) and the beam stays straight, with the slope it has at the force.

  3. A unit force at \(m_2\), \(a = L\). The deflection at the middle is \((L/2)^2\,(3L - L/2)/(6EI) = 5L^3/(48EI) = 5c\), the deflection of the end \(L^3/(3EI) = 16c\).

    \[\mat D = \frac{L^3}{48EI}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 5 & 16 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}993 & 4{,}982 \\ 4{,}982 & 15{,}944 \end{bmatrix}10^{-6}\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{N}} .\]

    \[\mat K = \mat D^{-1} = \frac{48EI}{7L^3}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -5 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\,293\,764 & -716\,801 \\ -716\,801 & 286\,720 \end{bmatrix}\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} .\]

This is the same difference as in chain W between \(K_{11}\) and \(1/D_{11}\).

Why is the computation linear if the bars tilt under the weight? The computation from chapter 4 expresses the elongation of a bar as a projection of the displacement, \(\vek n\cdot\vek q\), so to first order. The exact geometry would add to the Hessian matrix a term due to the force in the bar: a bar with force \(N\) and length \(L\) resists a transverse displacement like a taut string with the stiffness \(N/L\). The weight loads bar \(AC\) with a compressive force of \(13\,080\) N and bar \(BC\) with a tensile force of \(16\,350\) N. Compression reduces the transverse stiffness, tension increases it, by \(|N|/L = 3270\) N/m. This changes the natural frequencies (definition 8.140) by \(-0{,}0094\ \%\) and \(+0{,}0010\ \%\) (NumPy), which is much less than the uncertainty in \(EA\). Where this term mattered, we would have to keep it. Chapter 12 will treat it.

Example 10.8 Equation of motion of the machine that also rocks (gibalna enačba stroja, ki se tudi zibla)

Static and dynamic coupling. In machine F the off-diagonal term is in \(\mat K\) and not in \(\mat M\). Is this a property of the machine or of the choice of coordinates? Let us draw a rigid body on two springs in general (figure 10.14). A body with the mass \(M\) and the moment of inertia \(J\) about the centre of mass \(G\) stands on the ground on the spring \(k_1\), attached at the distance \(l_1\) left of the centre of mass, and on the spring \(k_2\) at the distance \(l_2\) right of it. Machine F of step 6 is such a body with \(l_1 = 0{,}6\) m, \(l_2 = 0{,}4\) m and \(k_1 = k_2 = k_{\mathrm{p}}\), since there \(x_1 = -l_1\) and \(x_2 = l_2\).

Definition 10.9 Static and dynamic coupling (statična in dinamična vezanost)

Let \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q(t)\) be the equation of motion (10.7) in the coordinates \(\vek q\). The coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(i \ne j\), are statically coupled (statično vezani) if \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} \ne 0\), and dynamically coupled (dinamično vezani) if \(M_{\mathrm{ij}} \ne 0\). The system is statically coupled in the coordinates \(\vek q\) if \(\mat K\) is not diagonal, and dynamically coupled if \(\mat M\) is not diagonal. Coordinates with diagonal \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) are called uncoupled (nesklopljene, nevezane).

Source: CK, the text after Example 8.1 (PDF p. 232): stiffness coupling for off-diagonal terms of \(\mat K\), and after Example 8.3 (PDF p. 236): inertia coupling for off-diagonal terms of \(\mat M\). The names static and dynamic and the example with machine F are not from the source.

The names say by what experiment we find the coupling. Static coupling we feel in a slow experiment without accelerations: we move one coordinate and hold it, and a force appears at the other one too. Dynamic coupling shows only during acceleration, since the term \(M_{\mathrm{ij}}\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\) vanishes at rest.

The coordinates \((y, \varphi)\), panel (a). We lift the body from the equilibrium position by \(y\) without rotation. Both springs lengthen by \(y\) and pull the body down, the left one with the force \(k_1 y\) and the right one with the force \(k_2 y\). The left force acts \(l_1\) left of the centre of mass and turns the body counterclockwise, the right one acts \(l_2\) right of it and turns it clockwise. Together they give the moment \((k_1 l_1 - k_2 l_2)\,y\) about the centre of mass. To keep the body from rotating we must hold it with the opposite moment \((k_2 l_2 - k_1 l_1)\,y\), and by the column rule this is the element \(K_{\varphi \mathrm{y}}\,y\). The other elements follow as in step 4 of example 10.8:

\[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 & k_2 l_2 - k_1 l_1 \\ k_2 l_2 - k_1 l_1 & k_1 l_1^2 + k_2 l_2^2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \begin{bmatrix} M & 0 \\ 0 & J \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.14}\]

This is also the Hessian matrix of the potential \(V = \tfrac12 k_1\,(y - l_1\varphi)^2 + \tfrac12 k_2\,(y + l_2\varphi)^2\) (SymPy). In the coordinates \((y, \varphi)\) the body is statically coupled and dynamically uncoupled. For machine F and a lift by \(y = 10\) mm the forces of the springs are \(1233{,}7\) N each, their moment is \((0{,}6 - 0{,}4)\ \text{m}\cdot 1233{,}7\ \text{N} = 246{,}7\) N m, and we must hold with \(-246{,}7\) N m, which is \(K_{\varphi \mathrm{y}}\,y\) with \(K_{\varphi \mathrm{y}} = -24\,674\) N from (10.13).

When does the coupling vanish? The off-diagonal term is zero exactly when

\[k_1 l_1 = k_2 l_2 . \tag{10.15}\]

Then the moments of the two forces about the centre of mass cancel: the resultant of the spring forces in a lift passes through the centre of mass and does not turn the body. Equal springs at equal distances, as in step 5, are a special case. Machine F with its centre of mass off the middle we uncouple with different mounts, the stiffer one closer to the centre of mass: \(k_2 = k_1 l_1/l_2 = 1{,}5\,k_{\mathrm{p}} = 185\,055\) N/m gives \(\mat K = \mathrm{diag}(308\,425\ \text{N/m},\ 74\,022\ \text{N m})\), a bounce with the frequency \(5{,}590\) Hz and rocking with \(7{,}071\) Hz.

The coordinates \((y_1, y_2)\), panel (b). The position of the same body can also be given by the vertical displacements of the attachments of the springs, \(y_1\) on the left and \(y_2\) on the right. For a small rotation \(y_1 = y - l_1\varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + l_2\varphi\). Conversely, with \(L = l_1 + l_2\),

\[y = \frac{l_2\,y_1 + l_1\,y_2}{L} , \qquad \varphi = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{L} . \tag{10.16}\]

In the figure we lift the left attachment by \(y_1\) and the right one is at rest. The body rotates about the right attachment by \(-y_1/L\) and the centre of mass rises by \((l_2/L)\,y_1\), as (10.16) says. We get the matrices in four steps.

  1. The potential. Each spring feels only the displacement of its own attachment, the left one \(y_1\) and the right one \(y_2\), so \(V = \tfrac12 k_1 y_1^2 + \tfrac12 k_2 y_2^2\) and \(\mat K' = \mathrm{diag}(k_1, k_2)\) for any springs and distances.
  2. The kinetic energy. Into \(T = \tfrac12 M\dot{y}^2 + \tfrac12 J\dot\varphi^2\) we insert (10.16):
    \[T = \frac{M}{2L^2}\,(l_2\,\dot{y}_1 + l_1\,\dot{y}_2)^2 + \frac{J}{2L^2}\,(\dot{y}_2 - \dot{y}_1)^2 .\]
  3. The mass matrix is the matrix of the coefficients of this quadratic form (theorem 8.97):
    \[\mat M' = \frac{1}{L^2}\begin{bmatrix} M l_2^2 + J & M l_1 l_2 - J \\ M l_1 l_2 - J & M l_1^2 + J \end{bmatrix} \tag{10.17}\]
    (SymPy).
  4. For machine F \(\mat M' = \begin{bmatrix} 77{,}5 & 22{,}5 \\ 22{,}5 & 127{,}5 \end{bmatrix}\) kg and \(\mat K' = \mathrm{diag}(123\,370,\ 123\,370)\) N/m. Check: the sum of all four elements of \(\mat M'\) is \(250\) kg, the mass of the machine, because the motion with \(y_1 = y_2\) is a pure lift with \(T = \tfrac12 M\dot{y}^2\).

In the coordinates \((y_1, y_2)\) the same body is statically uncoupled and dynamically coupled. Why? If we accelerate the left attachment and hold the right one, the centre of mass accelerates and the body turns, and both need a force at the right attachment as well. The term vanishes only for \(J = M l_1 l_2\), which for machine F is \(60\ \text{kg m}^2\), while its \(J\) is \(37{,}5\ \text{kg m}^2\).

Coupling is therefore a property of the description and not of the system. Machine F has the same natural frequencies (definition 8.140) in both descriptions, \(4{,}791\) Hz and \(6{,}736\) Hz (NumPy). That a change of coordinates does not change the frequencies is proved in section 10.2.3, and section 10.4 finds the coordinates in which both matrices are diagonal at once.

In the lectures the displacement of the centre of mass is \(y_{\mathrm{T}}\), and the coordinates are \((y_{\mathrm{T}}, \varphi)\) and \((y_1, y_2)\) as here.

The block and the cylinder. The third system has a body that rolls (figure 10.15, (a)). A block with the mass \(m_1\) slides without friction on the ground, and the spring \(k_1\) ties it to the wall. A solid cylinder with the mass \(m_2\) and the radius \(r\) rolls on the ground without slipping. Two horizontal springs tie it to the block: the spring \(k_2\) is attached at the point of the cylinder \(r/2\) above the centre, the spring \(k_3\) at the point \(r/2\) below it. The coordinates are the displacement \(x_1\) of the block and the displacement \(x_2\) of the centre of the cylinder, both to the right, measured from the position in which the springs are unstretched. Numbers: \(m_1 = 3\) kg, \(m_2 = 4\) kg, \(r = 0{,}1\) m, \(k_1 = 3000\) N/m, \(k_2 = 2000\) N/m, \(k_3 = 1000\) N/m.

Example 10.10 Equation of motion of a block and a rolling cylinder (gibalna enačba klade in valja, ki se kotali)

The block and the cylinder as above. We look for \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) for the coordinates \((x_1, x_2)\).

  1. The rolling constraint. The point of contact of the cylinder with the ground is at rest (section 8.1.2), so the cylinder rotates about it. When the centre moves by \(x_2\), the cylinder rotates by the angle \(\theta = x_2/r\) clockwise: the vertical diameter tilts. The rotation is not a new coordinate, so the system has two degrees of freedom.
  2. The displacements of the points of the cylinder, panel (b). For a small rotation the point of the diameter at the height \(h\) above the ground moves horizontally by \(h\,\theta = (h/r)\,x_2\), in proportion to the height. The attachment of the spring \(k_3\) at the height \(r/2\) moves by \(0{,}5\,x_2\), the attachment of the spring \(k_2\) at the height \(3r/2\) by \(1{,}5\,x_2\).
  3. The stretches of the springs are the differences of the displacements of their ends: \(k_1\) lengthens by \(x_1\), \(k_2\) by \(1{,}5\,x_2 - x_1\) and \(k_3\) by \(0{,}5\,x_2 - x_1\). In panel (a) \(x_2 = 2x_1\), so the spring \(k_3\) is unstretched there, and the spring \(k_2\) is lengthened by \(2x_1\).
  4. The potential is \(V = \tfrac12 k_1 x_1^2 + \tfrac12 k_2\,(1{,}5\,x_2 - x_1)^2 + \tfrac12 k_3\,(0{,}5\,x_2 - x_1)^2\), and its Hessian matrix is
    \[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 + k_3 & -1{,}5\,k_2 - 0{,}5\,k_3 \\ -1{,}5\,k_2 - 0{,}5\,k_3 & 2{,}25\,k_2 + 0{,}25\,k_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6000 & -3500 \\ -3500 & 4750 \end{bmatrix}\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}\]
    (SymPy). The spring \(k_2\) enters \(K_{22}\) with the factor \(1{,}5^2\): once because it stretches by \(1{,}5\,x_2\) at the displacement \(x_2\), and a second time because its force acts at a point that moves \(1{,}5\) times as far as the centre, and so does \(1{,}5\) times as much work (definition 8.51).
  5. The kinetic energy. The block has \(\tfrac12 m_1\dot{x}_1^2\). The cylinder has, by theorem 8.92, \(\tfrac12 m_2\dot{x}_2^2 + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{G}}\dot\theta^2\). The moment of inertia of a solid cylinder about its axis is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V = \tfrac12 m_2 r^2\) (an integral over rings, SymPy), and \(\dot\theta = \dot{x}_2/r\), so
    \[T = \tfrac12 m_1\dot{x}_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot{x}_2^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 m_2 r^2\cdot\frac{\dot{x}_2^2}{r^2} = \tfrac12 m_1\dot{x}_1^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32 m_2\,\dot{x}_2^2 .\]
    Rolling increases the inertia of the cylinder by a half, and the radius drops out of the computation: \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(m_1,\ \tfrac32 m_2) = \mathrm{diag}(3,\ 6)\) kg.
  6. The rolling constraint is ideal (definition 8.3), so the friction force at the point of contact does not appear in the equations, and by theorem 10.3 without damping
    \[\begin{aligned} 3\,\ddot{x}_1 + 6000\,x_1 - 3500\,x_2 &= Q_1 , \\ 6\,\ddot{x}_2 - 3500\,x_1 + 4750\,x_2 &= Q_2 . \end{aligned} \tag{10.18}\]
  7. The assumption of a small rotation. Exactly, the attachment of the spring \(k_2\) moves horizontally by \(x_2 + (r/2)\sin\theta\) and drops by \((r/2)(1 - \cos\theta)\). The linear computation takes \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and neglects the drop. At \(x_2 = 10\) mm we have \(\theta = 0{,}1\) rad, the horizontal displacement differs from \(1{,}5\,x_2\) by \(\theta^2/18 = 0{,}056\ \%\), and the drop is \(0{,}25\) mm. The computation holds as long as \(x_2\) is small against \(r\). For \(x_2\) of the order of \(r\) the springs tilt and the potential is no longer a quadratic function.

Source: Not from a source. Rolling as an ideal constraint is from section 8.1.2, the kinetic energy from theorem 8.92, the numbers are ours.

The drum with a load. In all systems so far we counted the coordinates from the position with unstretched springs, or we computed the equilibrium under the weight separately. The last system shows why the weight need not be written into the equations for the displacements from equilibrium. The drum of a hoist with the radius \(r = 0{,}25\) m and the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{b}} = 1{,}25\ \text{kg m}^2\) rotates about its axis. The shaft to the motor, which is at rest, holds it as a torsion spring with the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = 40\,000\) N m/rad. On the drum is wound a rope, which is a spring with the stiffness \(k = 200\,000\) N/m, and on it hangs a load with the mass \(m_{\mathrm{b}} = 300\) kg. The coordinates are the rotation \(\varphi\) of the drum in the direction in which the rope unwinds and the descent \(x\) of the load, measured from the position in which the shaft and the rope are unstretched (figure 10.16).

Example 10.11 Equation of motion of a drum with a load on a rope (gibalna enačba bobna z bremenom na vrvi)

The drum and the load as above. We look for \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\), the equilibrium position and the equation for the displacements from it.

  1. The stretch of the rope. When the drum rotates by \(\varphi\), the length \(r\varphi\) unwinds from it. The load descends by \(x\). The rope lengthens by the difference, \(x - r\varphi\).
  2. The energies. \(T = \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{b}}\dot\varphi^2 + \tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{b}}\dot{x}^2\) and \(V = \tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi^2 + \tfrac12 k\,(x - r\varphi)^2 - m_{\mathrm{b}} g\,x\). The last term is the potential of the weight, with a minus because \(x\) is positive downwards.
  3. The matrices for \(\vek q = (\varphi, x)\) are
    \[\mat M = \begin{bmatrix} J_{\mathrm{b}} & 0 \\ 0 & m_{\mathrm{b}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}25 & 0 \\ 0 & 300 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat K = \begin{bmatrix} k_{\mathrm{t}} + k r^2 & -k r \\ -k r & k \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 52\,500 & -50\,000 \\ -50\,000 & 200\,000 \end{bmatrix}\]
    in the units kg m\(^2\), kg, N m, N and N/m (the Hessian matrix of \(V\), SymPy). The weight is a linear term in \(V\) and contributes nothing to \(\mat K\).
  4. The equation of motion in the coordinates \((\varphi, x)\) has the weight on the right: \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek f + \vek Q(t)\) with the constant column \(\vek f = (0,\ m_{\mathrm{b}} g) = (0,\ 2943\ \text{N})\).
  5. The equilibrium without an external force is \(\mat K\vek q_0 = \vek f\). The second row says that the rope carries the weight, \(k\,(x_0 - r\varphi_0) = m_{\mathrm{b}} g\), so it is lengthened by \(14{,}7\) mm. The first row says that the shaft carries the moment of the rope, \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi_0 = m_{\mathrm{b}} g\,r\). Therefore \(\varphi_0 = 18{,}39\) mrad, which is \(1{,}05^\circ\), and \(x_0 = 19{,}31\) mm.
  6. The displacement from equilibrium we denote by \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0 = (\varphi - \varphi_0,\ x - x_0)\). Since \(\vek q_0\) is constant, \(\ddot{\vek q} = \ddot{\vek x}\), and since \(\mat K\vek q = \mat K\vek q_0 + \mat K\vek x = \vek f + \mat K\vek x\), the column \(\vek f\) cancels on the two sides:
    \[\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x = \vek Q(t) .\]
    The weight no longer appears in the equation for the displacements. It is carried by the constant stretch of the rope and the twist of the shaft in equilibrium.
  7. The limit of the model. A rope carries only tension, so it must stay taut: the total stretch, that is \(14{,}7\) mm plus the change of the stretch during the vibration, must stay positive. When the vibration reduces the stretch of the rope by more than \(14{,}7\) mm, the rope goes slack, and the linear equation no longer holds.

Source: Not from a source. The model and the numbers are ours.

Step 6 holds for every system with constant matrices and constant loads, not only for the drum.

Lemma 10.12 Displacements from the static equilibrium position (odmiki od statične ravnovesne lege)

Let a system with \(n\) coordinates \(\vek q\) have constant matrices \(\mat M\) and \(\mat C\), the potential \(V(\vek q) = \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q - \vek f\cdot\vek q\) with a constant symmetric matrix \(\mat K\) and a constant column \(\vek f\) (the generalized force of the weights and other constant loads), and the generalized force \(\vek Q(t)\) of the other external forces. Let there be a column \(\vek q_0\) with \(\mat K\vek q_0 = \vek f\). Then \(\vek q_0\) is an equilibrium of the system without the forces \(\vek Q\), and \(\vek q(t)\) is a motion of the system exactly when the displacement \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0\) solves the equation

\[\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x = \vek Q(t) ,\]

in which \(\vek f\) does not appear.

Proof

We must show that the constant column \(\vek f\) cancels when we count the coordinates from \(\vek q_0\). The steps point at the drum of figure 10.16.

  1. The derivative of the potential is \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}q_{\mathrm{j}} - f_{\mathrm{s}}\), because \(\mat K\) is symmetric. For a constant \(\mat M\) we have \(g_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) in equation (10.5), so a motion is exactly a solution of the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek f + \vek Q(t)\) (theorem 10.3, point 2). For the drum \(\vek f\) is the weight of the load.
  2. For \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) the constant \(\vek q(t) = \vek q_0\) is a solution of this equation, because \(\mat K\vek q_0 = \vek f\). This is the equilibrium: the rope and the shaft carry the weight.
  3. Let \(\vek q = \vek q_0 + \vek x\). Since \(\vek q_0\) is constant, \(\dot{\vek q} = \dot{\vek x}\) and \(\ddot{\vek q} = \ddot{\vek x}\). Since multiplication by a matrix is linear, \(\mat K\vek q = \mat K\vek q_0 + \mat K\vek x = \vek f + \mat K\vek x\). The equation of step 1 is therefore \(\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x + \vek f = \vek f + \vek Q(t)\), and \(\vek f\) cancels. Every step holds in both directions.

Source: Not from a source. For one coordinate the same conclusion is in section 9.8, where the mounts in equilibrium carry the weight with the static sag \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}}\).

The lemma has two assumptions that must be checked for every system.

  1. The load is constant, so its potential is linear in the coordinates. For the load on the rope this is so, because the work of the weight is \(m_{\mathrm{b}} g\,x\). It is not so when the centre of mass moves along an arc in a rotation. For a pendulum the potential of the weight is \(m g\,l\,(1 - \cos\theta)\) and the weight is the only source of stiffness. For machine F with the mounts at the bottom of the box the weight gave, in step 7 of example 10.8, the term \(-Mg\,a = -736\) N m in \(K_{\varphi\varphi}\), that is \(-1{,}06\ \%\). In such systems the weight stays in \(\mat K\) also for displacements from equilibrium.
  2. The matrix \(\mat K\) is constant, so the springs are linear. For a spring that stiffens or softens with its stretch, the stiffness in equilibrium depends on how far the weight has already stretched it. Then the weight drops out of the equation for the displacements, but it decides which stiffness we insert.

With this we have the equation of motion (10.7) for frame A, the chains V and W, bracket B, machine F, the block with the cylinder and the drum with a load, all without damping (we added a damper only to frame A, in the example of the matrix \(\mat C\)).
