Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.1 Equations of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom (gibalne enačbe sistema z več prostostnimi stopnjami)

What equation describes small motions of a system with several coordinates about equilibrium, and where do the matrices in it come from? Chapter 9 showed this for one coordinate (theorem 9.3) and chapter 8 for \(n\) coordinates without damping and without an external force (theorem 8.120). Here we combine the two. First we look at a simple system with two coordinates, on which we shall test everything as we go.

10.1.1 The frame with two floors (okvir z dvema stropoma)

Frame A is our laboratory model (figure 10.1). Two rigid floors (strop) have a mass \(m = 500\) kg each. The lower floor stands on the ground on two steel columns (stebri), the upper one on the lower one, again on two columns. All columns are \(h = 1\) m long and have the \(20\ \text{mm} \times 40\ \text{mm}\) cross section from chapter 5. The section is mounted so that the column bends in the direction of the 40 mm side, so \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\) and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2\), as for the cantilever in section 5.9.2. Each column is clamped at both ends: at the bottom into the ground or into the lower floor, at the top into the upper floor, and it cannot rotate in the clamp. The floors move only horizontally. The displacement of the lower floor from equilibrium is denoted \(q_1\), that of the upper floor \(q_2\), both positive to the right.

Our model has two idealizations: the columns have no mass and the floors do not rotate. Real columns have the mass \(\rho A h = 7800 \cdot 8\cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1\ \text{kg} = 6{,}24\) kg, two columns below a floor together 12,48 kg, which is 2,5 % of the mass of a floor. A real floor has beams that bend a little and soften the clamp of the column somewhat, so the real stiffness is somewhat smaller than ours. We do not count the shortening of the columns along the axis, which is of second order for small displacements.

How much force does a floor need to move by \(q\)? A column that cannot move or rotate at the lower clamp, and moves at the upper clamp without rotating, is the cantilever from section 5.9.5, loaded at the free end with a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and with a couple \(M_0\) that prevents the rotation. By equation (5.59) the displacement and the slope of the end are

\[v(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{3EI} + \frac{M_0\,h^2}{2EI} , \qquad v'(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^2}{2EI} + \frac{M_0\,h}{EI} .\]

The end does not rotate, \(v'(h) = 0\), so \(M_0 = -F_{\mathrm{y}} h/2\) and

\[v(h) = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{3EI} - \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{4EI} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{y}}\,h^3}{12EI} .\]

The force that moves the end of the column by \(v\) is therefore \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = (12EI/h^3)\,v\), which is the force of a spring with the stiffness

\[k_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{12EI}{h^3} = 250\,880\ \text{N/m} .\]

This is four times the stiffness of a cantilever with a free end, \(3EI/h^3 = 62\,720\) N/m (the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h^3/(3EI)\) from the equation for \(v(h)\) without the couple). The reason is the couple \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h/2\), which keeps the end parallel to the initial position: its part of the displacement is opposite to the displacement due to the force and reduces it from \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h^3/(3EI)\) to \(F_{\mathrm{y}} h^3/(12EI)\) (figure 10.2). The two columns between the ground and the lower floor, or between the lower and the upper floor, together we call a storey (etaža). Their forces add, so the stiffness of a storey (storey stiffness, togost etaže) is

\[k = 2k_{\mathrm{s}} = 501\,760\ \text{N/m} .\]

The same holds for the upper storey: both ends of its columns move but do not rotate, and the force depends only on the difference of the displacements, since a rigid displacement of the columns causes no force.

Why does the axis have the shape of an S? There is no load along the column, so the shear force is constant, \(V = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), and by the differential relations (corollary 5.11) the moment satisfies \(M' = -V = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). At the end \(M(h) = M_0 = -F_{\mathrm{y}} h/2\), so \(M(x) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,(h/2 - x)\). The moment is zero at \(x = h/2\) and has opposite signs on the two sides of this point. The curvature is proportional to the moment, \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35), so the lower half of the column curves to one side, the upper half to the other.

Now we can write the equations of motion of the frame. First with Newton, then with Lagrange.

Example 10.1 Equation of motion of the frame with two floors (gibalna enačba okvira z dvema stropoma)

Frame A as above, on floor \(i\) let a horizontal external force \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) act, positive to the right. We look for the equations for \(q_1(t)\) and \(q_2(t)\).

First way, forces on the floors.

  1. A floor is a rigid body that moves horizontally, so by theorem 8.74 the mass times the acceleration equals the sum of the horizontal forces: \(m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum F_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The ends of the lower pair of columns are displaced relative to each other by \(q_1\) (the lower end is in the ground). The pair acts on the lower floor with the force \(-k\,q_1\), since the force on the end of a column equals the stiffness times the relative displacement of the ends and points towards equilibrium.
  3. The ends of the upper pair of columns are displaced relative to each other by \(q_2 - q_1\). It acts on the upper floor with the force \(-k\,(q_2 - q_1)\) and on the lower floor with the opposite force \(+k\,(q_2 - q_1)\) by the law of action and reaction (section 2.1).
  4. The equation of the lower floor is \(m\ddot{q}_1 = -k q_1 + k (q_2 - q_1) + Q_1\), of the upper one \(m\ddot{q}_2 = -k (q_2 - q_1) + Q_2\). We move the terms with \(q\) to the left:
    \[\begin{aligned} m\ddot{q}_1 + 2k\,q_1 - k\,q_2 &= Q_1 , \\ m\ddot{q}_2 - k\,q_1 + k\,q_2 &= Q_2 . \end{aligned}\]

Second way, Lagrange's equations.

  1. The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot{q}_1^2 + \tfrac12 m\dot{q}_2^2\) (theorem 8.86).
  2. Each pair of columns behaves as a spring with the stiffness \(k\) with respect to the relative displacement of its ends. If the relative displacement is \(\delta\), the force grows from zero to \(k\delta\), its work is \(\int_0^\delta k s\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12 k\delta^2\) (section 4.1) and this work is stored as potential energy. The relative displacement of the lower pair is \(q_1\), of the upper \(q_2 - q_1\), so \(V = \tfrac12 k q_1^2 + \tfrac12 k (q_2 - q_1)^2\).
  3. The derivatives are \(\partial V/\partial q_1 = k q_1 - k(q_2 - q_1) = 2k q_1 - k q_2\) and \(\partial V/\partial q_2 = k(q_2 - q_1)\). The external force on floor \(i\) has the generalized force \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}\), because its point of application moves by \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (definition 8.50).
  4. The kinetic energy does not contain the coordinates, so \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}} = m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\). By theorem 8.90 therefore \(m\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}\), which equals the equations of step 4.

In matrix form

\[\begin{aligned} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{M}}\begin{bmatrix} \ddot{q}_1 \\ \ddot{q}_2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{K}}\begin{bmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} Q_1 \\ Q_2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad k = 501\,760\ \text{N/m} , \ m = 500\ \text{kg} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.1}\]

The matrix \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix of the potential \(V\) (definition 8.61): \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_1^2 = 2k\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_1\partial q_2 = -k\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_2^2 = k\) (SymPy). The equation is exactly linear, because \(V\) is a quadratic function and \(\mat M\) is constant, so the remainder from theorem 8.120 is zero.

Source: CK section 8.1 and Example 8.1 (PDF p. 230): Newton's laws for a system of springs and masses, among the models a building with three floors, and the matrix form (8.1) (PDF p. 229). We did not find a frame with two floors in the sources. The model, the storey stiffness from section 5.9.5 and the numbers are ours.

Why does \(\mat K\) have this form? The element \(K_{22} = k\) is the stiffness of one storey, because the upper floor is held only by the upper pair of columns. The element \(K_{11} = 2k\) is the sum of both stiffnesses, because a displacement of the lower floor shifts the ends of both pairs of columns. The off-diagonal element \(K_{21} = -k\) says that when we move the lower floor to the right, the upper pair pulls the upper floor after the lower one with the force \(k\), so we must hold it with the force \(-k\).

10.1.2 The equation of motion for \(n\) coordinates (gibalna enačba za \(n\) koordinat)

The frame is a special case. What equation holds for a system with \(n\) coordinates that also has dampers? Damper \(k\) (definition 9.1) has a coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) and joins the points \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the system at the distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}|\). This distance is a function of all coordinates, so the rate of its change \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a sum of contributions of all velocities \(\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\), each with its own coefficient \(\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}\). The force of a damper is proportional to \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\), so we expect the generalized force of the dampers to be a linear function of the velocities, with a square table of coefficients. We announce its elements in advance:

\[C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad s, j = 1, \dots, n . \tag{10.2}\]

The matrix \(\mat C\) with these elements is symmetric, since \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) does not change if we swap \(s\) and \(j\). The theorem will show that indeed \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\). The proof derives this from the definition of a damper, from the generalized force (definition 8.50) and from the chain rule.

Theorem 10.2 Equation of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom (gibalna enačba sistema z več prostostnimi stopnjami)

Let a system have \(n\) degrees of freedom with generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) on an open set \(U \subset \mathbb R^n\) (definition 8.45), ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) by theorem 8.86 with continuously differentiable \(\mat M\). Let the applied forces be conservative with a three times continuously differentiable potential \(V(\vek q)\). Let the viscous dampers (definition 9.1) have coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) and join points with twice continuously differentiable positions \(\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\) and \(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\), whose distance \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}|\) is positive everywhere on \(U\). Let the external forces have the generalized force \(\vek Q(t)\), a continuous function of time. Let \(\mat C(\vek q)\) be the matrix with the elements (10.2). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\mat C(\vek q)\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), continuously differentiable, the generalized force of all dampers on the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\) and equals \(-\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\) with the dissipation function \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q}\).
  2. A twice continuously differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) is a motion of the system if and only if for \(s = 1, \dots, n\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} \\ &= Q_{\mathrm{s}}(t) , \\ &g_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\Big(\frac{\partial M_{\mathrm{sj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}} \\ &\quad - \frac12\,\frac{\partial M_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big)\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{k}} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.3}\]
  3. Along the motion \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(T + V) = \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q} \le \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q}\).
  4. Let \(\vek q_0 \in U\) be an equilibrium, \(\nabla V(\vek q_0) = \vek 0\), and let \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek q_0)\), \(\mat C_0 = \mat C(\vek q_0)\) and \(\mat K\) the matrix of second derivatives of \(V\) at \(\vek q_0\). Then \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat K\) is symmetric and \(\mat C_0\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. For the displacement \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0\) there exist numbers \(\rho > 0\) and \(c\) such that for every time at which \(|\vek x| \le \rho\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C_0\,\dot{\vek x} \\ &\quad + \mat K\,\vek x = \vek Q(t) + \vek R , \\ &|R_{\mathrm{s}}| \le c\,\big(|\vek x|^2 + |\dot{\vek x}|^2 \\ &\quad + |\vek x|\,|\dot{\vek x}| + |\vek x|\,|\ddot{\vek x}|\big) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.4}\]
Proof

We must show that the dampers contribute the generalized force \(-\mat C\dot{\vek q}\), that Lagrange's equations with it give (10.3), and then bound the terms that are not linear in the displacement and its derivatives by the remainder \(\vek R\). Let \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\) be the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\).

  1. Let \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})/\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the unit vector from \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) towards \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\). From \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\) and the product rule (lemma 1.81) follows \(2\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = 2\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\), so \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}})\). The function \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is twice continuously differentiable, because it is the root of a positive twice continuously differentiable function.
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, point 1) for the function \(t \mapsto \ell_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q(t))\) we have \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\). By definition 9.1 the force on \(B_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) and on \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\) it is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By definition 8.50 the generalized force of damper \(k\) on the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}\cdot\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}}\cdot\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{B}_\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\vek x_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{k}}) = -c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) by step 1. By step 2 this is \(-\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the sum over all dampers is \(Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. Since \(\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a linear function of the velocities by step 2, \(\partial\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}} = -Q^{\mathrm d}_{\mathrm{s}}\) by step 3. From step 2 we also have \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\,\dot{\vek q}\).
  5. The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric, because the product \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric in \(s\) and \(j\). For every column \(\vek z\) we have \(\vek z^\T\mat C\vek z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\,\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\,z_{\mathrm{s}}\big)^2 \ge 0\), so \(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite. The elements \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) are continuously differentiable, because the \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}}\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) are continuously differentiable. This is point 1. The generalized force of the forces that are not in the potential is \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\dot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\).
  6. By theorem 8.90 the motion is a motion of the system if and only if for \(\mathcal L = T - V\) we have \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot{q}_{\mathrm{s}}} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}}\). The left side depends only on \(T\) and \(V\), so steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 8.120 stay the same (they do not contain the right side) and give \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\ddot{q}_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{s}}\). When we move the term with \(\mat C\) to the left, we get (10.3). All steps are equivalences, so the converse holds too. This is point 2.
  7. Theorem 8.106 with \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\) from step 5 gives \(\mathrm{d}(T + V)/\mathrm{d}t = \vek Q\cdot\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q}\). The term with \(\mat C\) is not positive by step 5. This is point 3.
  8. The displacement \(\vek x = \vek q - \vek q_0\) shifts the coordinates by a constant, so \(\dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek q}\), \(\ddot{\vek x} = \ddot{\vek q}\). The functions \(\tilde V(\vek x) = V(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\), \(\tilde M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek x) = M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\) and \(\tilde C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek x) = C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0 + \vek x)\) have the same derivatives as \(V\), \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) and \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\), and \(\vek x = \vek 0\) is an equilibrium. Let \(\rho\) be so small that the closed ball \(|\vek x| \le \rho\) lies in \(\{\vek x : \vek q_0 + \vek x \in U\}\). The estimates from steps 5 to 7 of the proof of theorem 8.120 do not contain the right side of the equation and hold unchanged for \(\tilde V\) and \(\tilde M_{\mathrm{sj}}\): \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}x_{\mathrm{j}} + r_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(|r_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac12 n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}}|\vek x|^2\), \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\,\ddot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}|\vek x|\,|\ddot{\vek x}|\) and \(|g_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac32 n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}|\dot{\vek x}|^2\), where \(c_{\mathrm{V}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\) are the largest absolute values of the third derivatives of \(V\) and of the first derivatives of \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball.
  9. We estimate the difference \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0)\) like the difference of \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) in step 6 of the same proof: by the mean value theorem 1.84 on the function \(\tau \mapsto \tilde C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek x)\) and lemma 8.28. Let \(c_{\mathrm{C}}\) be the largest absolute value of the derivatives \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} C_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball (theorem 8.26, the derivatives are continuous by step 5). Then \(|C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0)| \le n\,c_{\mathrm{C}}\,|\vek x|\) and \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - C_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q_0))\,\dot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{C}}\,|\vek x|\,|\dot{\vek x}|\).
  10. In (10.3) we insert \(M_{\mathrm{sj}} = M_{0,\mathrm{sj}} + (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\), the same for \(C_{\mathrm{sj}}\), and step 8. We get (10.4) with \(R_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}} - M_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\ddot{x}_{\mathrm{j}} - g_{\mathrm{s}} - r_{\mathrm{s}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} (C_{\mathrm{sj}} - C_{0,\mathrm{sj}})\dot{x}_{\mathrm{j}}\). By steps 8 and 9 the inequality for \(R_{\mathrm{s}}\) holds with \(c = n^2\max\big(\tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{V}},\ \tfrac32 c_{\mathrm{M}},\ c_{\mathrm{C}}\big)\), since \(n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}} \le \tfrac32 n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\).
  11. The matrix \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite by theorem 8.86, \(\mat K\) is symmetric because mixed derivatives commute (theorem 8.30). The matrix \(\mat C_0\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite by step 5. This is point 4.

Source: CK (8.1) (PDF p. 229) and (8.9) (PDF p. 241): \(\mat M\ddot{\vek u} + \mat C\dot{\vek u} + \mat K\vek u = \vek p\) and Lagrange's equations. GR section 2.1 (PDF p. 77-79): linear vibrations about equilibrium. GR (1.53) and (1.54) (PDF p. 53): the dissipation function and the generalized force of dampers. The damping matrix from the distances \(\ell_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the bound on the remainder are not from a source.

The matrix \(\mat C\) is not necessarily positive definite. In frame A a horizontal damper between the ground and the lower floor has the distance \(\ell = \ell_0 + q_1\), so \(\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} c_{\mathrm{d}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vek z^\T\mat C\vek z = 0\) for \(\vek z = (0, 1)^\T\): the damper does not resist a motion of the upper floor while the lower one is at rest.

The theorem says what the model is. The exact equation (10.3) is nonlinear, because the mass matrix depends on the coordinates, and this shows in the quadratic form of the velocities \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\). When the displacement is small, a linear equation remains in the unknown \(\vek x\) with a remainder of second order, as for one coordinate. Chapter 8 already warned that the theorem does not bound the error of the solution in time, but the difference between the equations at each moment, and so we take the linear equation as a model whose validity we check with numbers. The displacement from equilibrium we from now on again write \(\vek q\), as in chapter 9, and the equation

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t) \tag{10.5}\]

we call the equation of motion of a system with several degrees of freedom. The matrix \(\mat M\) is the mass matrix (masna matrika), \(\mat K\) the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) and \(\mat C\) the damping matrix (dušilna matrika), all with \(n \times n\) elements. For \(n = 1\) this is equation (9.8) from chapter 9: the matrices correspond to the numbers \(m\), \(c\) and \(k\).

The matrices have \(n \times n\) elements. For coordinates with different units, such as a length and an angle, different elements have different units, as we saw for the bar on springs (section 8.13.3). The quadratic form \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) always has the unit J, the same as energy, and the quadratic form \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) the unit W, the same as power.

10.1.3 Stiffness and flexibility coefficients (togostni in podajnostni koeficienti)

In the theorem the matrix \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix of the potential. For a structure of linearly elastic material we do not need to compute the potential and differentiate it twice. Chapter 7 defined the matrix as the matrix of forces that hold the state (definition 7.4): the element \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the force in the direction of unknown \(i\) that we need so that \(q_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) and all other displacements are zero. The two descriptions are the same matrix. The state \(\vek q\) is an equilibrium state under the forces \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\), so by Castigliano's first theorem (theorem 4.92) the force in the direction of \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) that holds this state is \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(U(\vek q)\) is the strain energy of the structure. The potential of the structure differs from \(U\) by a term that is linear in \(\vek q\) (the work of constant loads) and does not change the second derivatives. Therefore \(\partial^2 V/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} = \partial F_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}\), and since \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\) is a linear function by definition 7.4, the derivative is \(\partial F_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} = K_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The elements \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are called stiffness coefficients (togostni koeficienti), as CK (8.16) (PDF p. 248) calls them, in the spirit of the flexibility coefficients of chapter 4. Their columns we get by the column rule.

In frame A the first column is the force we need to move the lower floor by 1 m while the upper one stays. The relative displacement of the lower pair is \(1\) m, of the upper one \(-1\) m. The columns act on the lower floor with the force \(-k\cdot 1 + k\cdot(-1) = -2k\) and on the upper one with the force \(-k\cdot(-1) = k\), so the floors must be held by the forces \(2k\) and \(-k\). The column \((2k, -k)\) is indeed the first column of the matrix from (10.1). The second column (upper floor 1 m, lower one at rest) is \((-k, k)\) (figure 10.3, top).

The converse question is: how much does a point move if a unit force acts on it? Chapter 4 introduced the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) for it (definition 4.138), whose columns are the displacements due to unit forces. In frame A we get \(\mat D\) without computing with matrices. A force \(F\) on the upper floor runs through both pairs of columns, because the upper pair holds the upper floor and the lower pair holds both. The lower floor therefore moves by \(F/k\) and the upper one by \(F/k + F/k = 2F/k\). A force \(F\) on the lower floor loads only the lower pair, so both floors move by \(F/k\). The first column is \((1, 1)/k\) and the second \((1, 2)/k\), so

\[\mat D = \frac1k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.6}\]

The two matrices are inverse to each other, which we shall use in computations.

Lemma 10.3 Stiffness and flexibility matrix (togostna in podajnostna matrika)

Let a structure be of linearly elastic material as in definition 4.138 (for a truss the point is a joint), and let its state be determined by a column \(\vek q\) of displacements of \(n\) chosen points in chosen directions, so that every column \(\vek q\) gives a state that respects the supports. Let the supports prevent every small rigid motion of the structure. Let \(\mat K\) be the stiffness matrix (definition 7.4) and \(\mat D\) the flexibility matrix (definition 4.138) for these displacements and forces at the same points and in the same directions. Then \(\mat K\mat D = \mat D\mat K = \mat I\), so \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\), and \(\mat D\) is symmetric.

Proof

We must show that the displacement due to forces \(\vek F\) is exactly \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\). We use the uniqueness of the solution for given forces.

  1. Let \(\vek F\) be an arbitrary column of sufficiently small forces at the chosen points and in the chosen directions. By definition 4.138 there is a solution with small deformations for each single force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\), and by the principle of superposition (lemma 4.134) the sum of these solutions is a solution for all forces at once. Its displacements of the chosen points are \(\vek q = \mat D\vek F\), since column \(j\) of \(\mat D\) is the displacement due to the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\), divided by \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. In this state exactly the forces \(\vek F\) act at the chosen points, there are no other loads. By definition 7.4 therefore \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q = \mat K\mat D\vek F\).
  3. Since \(\vek F\) is arbitrary, we take \(\vek F = \varepsilon\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) with a small \(\varepsilon > 0\) for each \(j\). From step 2 follows \(\varepsilon\,\mat K\mat D\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \varepsilon\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so column \(j\) of \(\mat K\mat D\) equals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) for each \(j\) and \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\).
  4. Let \(\vek q\) be an arbitrary column of sufficiently small displacements. By assumption a state with these displacements exists, and the forces that hold it are \(\vek F = \mat K\vek q\) (definition 7.4). This state is a solution for these forces. The supports prevent every small rigid motion, so the solution for given forces is unique (lemma 4.133), and it equals the solution from step 1. So \(\vek q = \mat D\vek F = \mat D\mat K\vek q\). Since \(\vek q\) is arbitrary, \(\mat D\mat K = \mat I\).
  5. Steps 3 and 4 show \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\) by definition 1.107. The matrix \(\mat D\) is symmetric by Maxwell's theorem (theorem 4.140).

Source: Not from a source. CK (11.49) and footnote 4 (PDF p. 367) use \(\mat K^{-1}\) as the flexibility matrix and remark that it does not exist for systems with rigid modes. Check on a truss of two bars: chapter 4, verified with SymPy.

The lemma says when the flexibility matrix exists: when the supports prevent every rigid motion. For a system without supports, such as an aeroplane or a machine in space, the matrix \(\mat K\) has no inverse and the lemma does not hold. The chapter will treat such systems separately in section 10.5.

For frame A \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\) is a computational check: \(k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\cdot\dfrac1k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 1 & 2 - 2 \\ -1 + 1 & -1 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \mat I\). The same holds for the truss from chapter 4, where the product of the matrices was checked with SymPy.

The flexibility matrix gives the equation of motion a second form.

Corollary 10.4 Flexibility form of the equation of motion (podajnostna oblika gibalne enačbe)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\), \(\mat K\) and \(\vek Q\) be as in equation (10.5) and let \(\mat K\) be invertible with the inverse matrix \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\). Then \(\vek q(t)\) is a solution of equation (10.5) if and only if

\[\mat D\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat D\mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek q = \mat D\vek Q(t) . \tag{10.7}\]
Proof

We must show that one equation follows from the other by multiplication with an invertible matrix.

  1. If (10.5) holds, we multiply it from the left by \(\mat D\). Since \(\mat D\mat K = \mat I\) by the definition of the inverse matrix (definition 1.107), we get (10.7).
  2. If (10.7) holds, we multiply it from the left by \(\mat K\). Since \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\), we get (10.5).

Source: CK (11.49) (PDF p. 367) for the flexibility matrix. The proof is not from a source.

What is the second form good for? When it is easier to compute the displacements due to unit forces than the forces for unit displacements, we do not need the matrix \(\mat K\) at all. In frame A we read \(\mat D\) directly from (10.6). The matrix \(\mat D\mat M\) is not necessarily symmetric, even if \(\mat D\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric. Its transpose is \(\mat M\mat D\) by lemma 1.101, which equals \(\mat D\mat M\) only if the matrices commute. In frame A \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and \(\mat D\mat M = (m/k)\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is symmetric, but for the machine with coordinates of different units below it is not. The motion in which all coordinates move in step, \(\vek q = \vek\varphi\cos\omega t\), which we shall look for in the next section, we insert into equation (10.7) without damping and force and get \(-\omega^2\,\mat D\mat M\vek\varphi + \vek\varphi = \vek 0\). We keep the search for motions in step in the symmetric form with the matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\), and the flexibility form says the same.

10.1.4 The bracket and the machine (konzola in stroj)

We test the theorem on two more systems that are not a frame of floors.

Bracket B. The truss of two bars from chapter 4 (figure 10.4): bar \(AC\), horizontal and 4 m long, bar \(BC\), 5 m long, pins \(A\) and \(B\) fixed to the wall, as in section 4.8.1. Both bars have \(EA = 19{,}6\) MN. At the joint \(C\) let there be a point mass \(m = 1000\) kg, which is our model: its weight 9,81 kN is close to the load 10 kN of chapter 4. The bars have no mass. Together they have 7,02 kg, which is 0,7 % of the mass \(m\). The coordinates are the displacements of the joint \(C\), \(\vek q = (u, v)\), \(u\) to the right and \(v\) upwards, as in chapter 4.

Example 10.5 Equation of motion of the bracket with a mass at the joint (gibalna enačba konzole z maso v vozlišču)

Bracket B as above. We look for \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) for the coordinates \((u, v)\) and check \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\).

  1. Bar \(k\) with the unit vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) from the wall towards \(C\) and length \(L_{\mathrm{k}}\) elongates by \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek q\) at the joint displacement \(\vek q\) (chapter 4, section 4.8.1, step 4). The axial force is \((EA/L_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek q\), and the bar acts on the joint with the force \(-(EA/L_{\mathrm{k}})(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek q)\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. The force that holds the joint in the state \(\vek q\) is opposite to the sum of these forces, \(\vek F = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (EA/L_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\,\vek q\). By the column rule (definition 7.4) therefore \(\mat K = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (EA/L_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\).
  3. For bar \(AC\) we have \(\vek n = (1, 0)\) and \(EA/L = 4{,}9\cdot 10^6\) N/m, for bar \(BC\) \(\vek n = (4, -3)/5\) and \(EA/L = 3{,}92\cdot 10^6\) N/m. The first gives \(4{,}9\cdot 10^6\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), the second \(3{,}92\cdot 10^6\cdot\frac1{25}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 \\ -12 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2{,}5088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{bmatrix}10^6\), and the sum is
    \[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 7{,}4088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{bmatrix}10^6\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} .\]
  4. The flexibility matrix of chapter 4 (equation (4.82)) is \(\mat D = \dfrac{1\ \text{m}}{EA}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 16/3 \\ 16/3 & 21 \end{bmatrix}\), and \(\mat K\mat D = \mat I\) up to rounding (SymPy), as lemma 10.3 says.
  5. The kinetic energy of the mass is \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot{u}^2 + \dot{v}^2)\), so \(\mat M = m\mat I = 1000\,\mat I\) kg. The potential is \(V = \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q + m g\,v\) for displacements from the unloaded position. The equilibrium \(\nabla V = \vek 0\) is at \(\vek q_0 = -\mat K^{-1}(0, mg)\), that is \(\vek q_0 = \mat D\,(0, -mg)\) with the components \(u_0 = -2{,}669\) mm and \(v_0 = -10{,}511\) mm, and the Hessian matrix is \(\mat K\) everywhere, because \(V\) is a quadratic function of the displacements.
  6. For the displacement from equilibrium, by theorem 10.2 without damping,
    \[\begin{aligned} &1000\,\ddot{u} + 7{,}4088\cdot 10^6\,u \\ &\quad - 1{,}8816\cdot 10^6\,v = Q_{\mathrm{u}} , \\ &1000\,\ddot{v} - 1{,}8816\cdot 10^6\,u \\ &\quad + 1{,}4112\cdot 10^6\,v = Q_{\mathrm{v}} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.8}\]

Source: Not from a source. The matrix \(\mat K\) is from section 4.6.7 and from definition 7.4, the numbers are ours.

Why is the computation linear if the bars tilt under the weight? The computation from chapter 4 expresses the elongation of a bar as a projection of the displacement, \(\vek n\cdot\vek q\), so to first order. The exact geometry would add to the Hessian matrix a term due to the force in the bar: a bar with force \(N\) and length \(L\) resists a transverse displacement like a taut string with the stiffness \(N/L\). The weight loads bar \(AC\) with a compressive force of \(13\,080\) N and bar \(BC\) with a tensile force of \(16\,350\) N. Compression reduces the transverse stiffness, tension increases it, by \(|N|/L = 3270\) N/m. This changes the natural frequencies (definition 8.124) by \(-0{,}0094\ \%\) and \(+0{,}0010\ \%\) (NumPy), which is much less than the uncertainty in \(EA\). Where this term mattered, we would have to keep it. Chapter 12 will treat it.

Machine F. Chapter 9 allowed the machine on mounts only a vertical displacement (a guide) and mentioned that a real machine would also rock. Now we do it. Machine F is a rigid body of mass \(M = 250\) kg in the shape of a box \(1{,}2\ \text{m} \times 0{,}6\ \text{m}\) and with the moment of inertia about the centre of mass \(J = M(1{,}2^2 + 0{,}6^2)/12 = 37{,}5\ \text{kg m}^2\). It stands on two pairs of mounts (podložke), each pair has the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{p}} = 123\,370\) N/m, together \(246\,740\) N/m, like the four mounts of the machine in chapter 9. The mounts are springs that act only vertically, which is an idealization: real mounts also have a shear stiffness. The machine moves vertically and rotates in the plane, like the bar on springs in section 8.13.3 (figure 10.5). The coordinates are the vertical displacement of the centre of mass \(y\) (upwards) and the rotation \(\varphi\) (counterclockwise), measured from the position in which the mounts are unstrained. A point at the horizontal distance \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the centre of mass moves vertically by \(y + x_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\varphi \approx y + x_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi\).

Example 10.6 Equation of motion of the machine that also rocks (gibalna enačba stroja, ki se tudi zibla)

Machine F as above, the mounts with vertical stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{p}}\) are at the horizontal distances \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) from the centre of mass. We look for \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) for \((y, \varphi)\).

  1. The kinetic energy of a rigid body in the plane is \(T = \tfrac12 M\dot{y}^2 + \tfrac12 J\dot\varphi^2\) (theorem 8.86, as for the bar on springs), so \(\mat M = \operatorname{diag}(M, J)\) with the units kg and kg m\(^2\).
  2. The potential of the springs and the weight is \(V = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{p}}\,(y + x_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\varphi)^2 + M g\,y\).
  3. The Hessian matrix at \(y = \varphi = 0\), where the mounts are unstrained: \(\partial^2 V/\partial y^2 = 2k_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial y\partial\varphi = k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1 + x_2)\), \(\partial^2 V/\partial\varphi^2 = k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1^2 + x_2^2)\). So
    \[\mat K = k_{\mathrm{p}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & x_1 + x_2 \\ x_1 + x_2 & x_1^2 + x_2^2 \end{bmatrix} .\]
  4. The same elements we get by the column rule. We move the machine by \(y = 1\) m without rotation: each pair of mounts acts on the machine with the force \(-k_{\mathrm{p}}\), so it must be held by the forces \(2k_{\mathrm{p}}\) together and the moment about the centre of mass \(k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1 + x_2)\). This is the first column. We rotate the machine by \(\varphi = 1\) rad without lifting: mount \(i\) moves by \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the force that holds the machine is \(k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1 + x_2)\) and the moment is \(k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1^2 + x_2^2)\). This is the second column. The force for the rotation and the moment for the displacement are equal, \(k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1 + x_2)\), as it must be for a symmetric matrix.
  5. Mounts at \(x_1 = -0{,}5\) m and \(x_2 = 0{,}5\) m (centre of mass in the middle): \(\mat K = \operatorname{diag}(246\,740\ \text{N/m},\ 61\,685\ \text{N m})\), the matrix is diagonal and the coordinates are not coupled. The bounce has the frequency \(\sqrt{246\,740/250}/(2\pi) = 5{,}000\) Hz, as in chapter 9, the rocking \(\sqrt{61\,685/37{,}5}/(2\pi) = 6{,}455\) Hz.
  6. The centre of mass 0,1 m from the middle between the mounts, mounts at \(x_1 = -0{,}6\) m and \(x_2 = 0{,}4\) m: \(x_1 + x_2 = -0{,}2\) m and \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 0{,}52\) m\(^2\), so
    \[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 246\,740 & -24\,674 \\ -24\,674 & 64\,152{,}4 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \begin{bmatrix} 250 & 0 \\ 0 & 37{,}5 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{10.9}\]
    in the units N/m, N, N m, kg and kg m\(^2\). The off-diagonal term is not zero, because the centre of mass is not in the middle between the mounts: when the machine is lifted, both mounts resist with the same force, but their resultant lies 0,1 m from the centre of mass and gives a moment about it. The motions are coupled (sklopljeni), as for the bar on two different springs in section 8.13.3.
  7. The equilibrium of these mounts is at \(y_0 = -10{,}34\) mm and \(\varphi_0 = -3{,}98\) mrad (SymPy from \(\nabla V = \vek 0\) for the exact \(V\) with \(\sin\varphi\)). The Hessian matrix of the exact \(V\) at it differs from (10.9) by \(4{,}1\cdot 10^{-6}\) of the largest element, so we keep it. By theorem 10.2 for the displacement from equilibrium \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q\) holds with the matrices from (10.9).

Source: Not from a source. The same model is the bar on two springs from section 8.13.3. The numbers are ours.

For this machine the flexibility form is an example of a matrix that is not symmetric. For (10.9) the matrix \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1}\) is symmetric with \(D_{12} = 1{,}621\cdot 10^{-6}\) (NumPy), but in \(\mat D\mat M\) we have \((\mat D\mat M)_{12} = D_{12}\,J = 6{,}08\cdot 10^{-5}\) and \((\mat D\mat M)_{21} = D_{21}\,M = 4{,}05\cdot 10^{-4}\) in SI base units. The masses \(M\) and \(J\) are different, so the two elements differ, and the matrix is not symmetric.

With this we have for three systems the equation of motion (10.5), and without damping for two. In the next section we ask when all coordinates move in step.
