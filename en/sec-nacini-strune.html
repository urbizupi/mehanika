Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.2 Modes of the string and the sine series

A steel guitar string of diameter \(0{,}4\) mm and length \(L = 0{,}65\) m is tightened with a force \(T = 70\) N and plucked. We hear a note and above it the sound of higher notes. Which frequencies does the string have at all, and why are they in the ratio \(1 : 2 : 3\)? How do we compute, from the shape in which we release the string, how much of each note is in the sound?

Figure 13.9 shows what vibrates. The string lies along the axis \(x\) between two ends that are fixed and so at rest. The force \(T\) tightens it, pulling at both ends. The distance \(L\) is the length of the string, and the distance \(x\) picks the point we watch. At time \(t\) the string has the blue shape, and the point \(x\) is displaced by \(w(x, t)\). At the start we release it from the dashed shape \(w_0(x)\) and with the velocity \(v_0(x)\).

The model is the string of section 13.1 (definition 13.2): the displacement \(w(x, t)\) of the point \(x \in [0, L]\) at time \(t\) is transverse, the tension \(T\) is constant, the slope is small and the bending stiffness is zero. We write \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \partial^2 w/\partial t^2\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2 w/\partial x^2\). The motion obeys the wave equation (13.2),

\[w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad c = \sqrt{T/\mu} ,\]

with the wave speed \(c\) (definition 13.4) and the mass per length \(\mu\). For our string \(\mu = \rho\,\pi d^2/4 = 9{,}802 \cdot 10^{-4}\) kg/m with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ and \(c = 267{,}24\) m/s. We add that the ends are at rest and that the initial shape \(w_0\) and the initial velocity \(v_0\) are known:

\[w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0 , \tag{13.4}\]
\[w(x, 0) = w_0(x) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0) = v_0(x) \quad (0 \le x \le L) . \tag{13.5}\]

The model has no damping: a real string loses energy while it vibrates, which we treat later (chapter 20).

For systems with finitely many degrees of freedom we found the modes (definition 8.140), special motions in which all coordinates vibrate with the same frequency and in a fixed ratio, \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\). Let us try the same for the string: every point vibrates with the time dependence \(\cos\omega t\), and the shape along the string is carried by a function \(\varphi(x)\).

Definition 13.6 Mode of a continuous system (način nihanja zveznega sistema)

A continuous system is a string, a bar, a shaft or a beam of length \(L\) that has an equation of motion and boundary conditions at both ends. Let \(w(x, t)\) be its displacement: the deflection of the string, the axial displacement of the bar, the twist of the shaft or the deflection of the beam. For the string these are equation (13.2) and boundary conditions (13.4), which we use here. For the bar and the shaft section 13.3 writes them down, for the beam sections 13.5 and 13.6. Let \(\omega > 0\) be a number and \(\varphi : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) a function that is not zero everywhere and as many times continuously differentiable as the order of the equation of motion requires (twice for the string). The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode (način nihanja) if the function

\[w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\]

solves the equation of motion of the system and satisfies its boundary conditions (for the string equation (13.2) and conditions (13.4)). The number \(\omega\) is the natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) and \(\varphi\) the mode shape (oblika načina). A point \(x\) where \(\varphi(x) = 0\) is a node (vozel) of the mode.

Source: The notion follows GR section 2.2 (PDF p. 85-87) for finitely many degrees of freedom, see the mode in definition 8.140. KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 571-573) calls the same notion "eigenfunction" and "normal mode" and obtains it by separating the variables \(w = F(x)G(t)\). The form of the definition with \(\cos\omega t\) is not from the source.

Does a mode \((\omega, \varphi)\) exist, and how many are there? When we insert \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) into the equation, time cancels and an ordinary differential equation for \(\varphi\) remains, with conditions at the ends. Before we solve it, let us ask which shape may breathe at all, so that all points go up together and down together (figure 13.10).

We draw the axis and on it the two fixed ends. Between them is the arc of a sine, and at a chosen point the displacement \(\varphi\) from the axis. A piece of string is pushed by its curvature: the force on it is \(T\) times the change of slope, per unit length \(T\varphi''\). In the middle the arc is most curved, so the force \(T\varphi''\) is largest there and points towards the axis. Nearer the ends the arc is less curved and the forces are shorter, each exactly as long as the displacement below it. If the force on every point is proportional to its displacement, every point is like a mass on a spring with the same stiffness per unit mass: all have the same frequency and return to the axis together. So the curvature must be proportional to minus the displacement, \(\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi\) (equation (13.7) in step 1 of the proof below). Below the first axis is a second axis and on it the triangular shape of a plucked string: we hold the string at \(L/5\) and pull it aside by \(1\) mm, and the slope is at most \(0{,}0077\). Such a shape cannot do this: the force acts only at the kink and is zero on the straight parts, so the kink would return to the axis before the straight parts and the shape would not stay the same. So the triangle is not a mode. Where did we assume anything here? That the force is only \(T\varphi''\), because the string has no bending stiffness. This is good for our steel string, since the ratio of the bending force to the tension is \(EI/(TL^2) = 8{,}3\cdot 10^{-6}\) (section 13.1). Where this ratio is not small, for example for a ten times thicker wire (\(0{,}083\)), the bending force of a beam is added to \(T\varphi''\) and the equation for \(\varphi\) is no longer this one.

We find the solutions of the equation without guessing, with an energy argument for uniqueness. The solution will be a sine, so we first look at its zeros (figure 13.11).

Figure 13.11 shows \(\sin\theta\) for the angle \(\theta = kx\). On the axis we measure \(\theta\). The sine is positive on \((0, \pi)\) and on \((2\pi, 3\pi)\) and negative on \((\pi, 2\pi)\). The zeros are at \(0\), \(\pi\), \(2\pi\) and \(3\pi\). The negative arc is the mirror image of the positive one: the values at \(\theta\) and at \(2\pi - \theta\) are opposite. On a string of length \(L\) the angle at the right end is \(kL\), in our figure \(3\pi\).

Theorem 13.7 Modes of the string (načini nihanja strune)

Let \(L > 0\) and \(c > 0\).

  1. A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the string with ends at rest if and only if, for some natural number \(n\),
    \[\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{n\pi c}{L} , \qquad \varphi(x) = C\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \tag{13.6}\]
    with a number \(C \ne 0\).
  2. For every \(n\) and any numbers \(A\), \(B\) the function \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t) = \sin(n\pi x/L)\,\big(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\big)\) is a solution of equation (13.2) that satisfies the boundary conditions (13.4).
  3. Mode \(n\) has \(n - 1\) nodes inside the string, at the points \(x = jL/n\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\).
Proof

We must show three points. For the first we insert \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) into the equation, obtain an equation for \(\varphi\) and solve it with the energy \(\psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\), which is conserved. The second is a check by substitution, the third a count of the zeros of the sine.

  1. Let \((\omega, \varphi)\) be a mode and \(w = \varphi\cos\omega t\). Then \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2\varphi\cos\omega t\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varphi''\cos\omega t\) (theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine, 8.13). At \(t = 0\) we have \(\cos 0 = 1\), so equation (13.2) gives \(-\omega^2\varphi(x) = c^2\varphi''(x)\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\). With \(k = \omega/c > 0\),
    \[\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi . \tag{13.7}\]
    This is the condition of figure 13.10: the force \(T\varphi''\) is proportional to the displacement \(\varphi\), with the coefficient \(-k^2\), which is the same everywhere for a given mode, and points towards the axis.
  2. The boundary conditions at \(t = 0\) give \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\).
  3. Let \(\sigma(x) = \dfrac{\varphi'(0)}{k}\sin kx\) and \(\psi = \varphi - \sigma\). The function \(\sigma\) solves equation (13.7) (theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine, 8.13, \(\sigma'' = -k^2\sigma\)), so \(\psi'' = -k^2\psi\), and \(\psi(0) = \varphi(0) - 0 = 0\), \(\psi'(0) = \varphi'(0) - \varphi'(0) = 0\).
  4. Let \(E = \psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\). By the product rule (lemma 1.82), \(E' = 2\psi'\psi'' + 2k^2\psi\psi' = 2\psi'(\psi'' + k^2\psi) = 0\). By the corollary on a function with zero derivative (1.86), \(E\) is constant, \(E(x) = E(0) = 0\). A sum of two squares is zero only if each is zero, so \(\psi = 0\) and \(\varphi = \sigma\). Why this \(E\)? The equation \(\psi'' = -k^2\psi\) is the equation of a mass on a spring with the space variable \(x\) in place of time: \(\psi\) is the displacement, \(\psi'\) the velocity, and \(E\) is (up to a factor) the energy, which a spring conserves: the point \((\psi, \psi'/k)\) runs on a circle, since \(E/k^2 = \psi^2 + (\psi'/k)^2\) is the square of its radius. A mass at rest in equilibrium (\(\psi = 0\), \(\psi' = 0\)) would never leave it, and this is what \(E(0) = 0\) says, radius zero, the point at the origin: a shape with no initial displacement and no slope is zero.
  5. If \(\varphi'(0) = 0\) then \(\varphi = 0\), which the definition excludes. Hence \(\varphi'(0) \ne 0\). The condition \(\varphi(L) = 0\) is then \(\sin kL = 0\). The sine is zero at \(0\) and \(\pi\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.11, item 2), positive on \((0, \pi)\) (item 3) and, by oddness and periodicity (item 4), negative on \((\pi, 2\pi)\), since there \(\sin\theta = -\sin(2\pi - \theta)\). So the zeros of the sine are exactly the multiples of \(\pi\), and \(kL = n\pi\) with a natural number \(n\), because \(kL > 0\). Hence \(\omega = kc = n\pi c/L\) and \(\varphi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with \(C = \varphi'(0)/k \ne 0\). This is the direction "only if" of item 1.
  6. Conversely, for \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\varphi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) we have \(\varphi'' = -(n\pi/L)^2\varphi\) and \(c^2(n\pi/L)^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). So \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(n\pi/L)^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 w\) for \(w = \varphi\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), and \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} = w_{\mathrm{tt}}\). The same computation holds for \(\varphi\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), so by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82) \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) of item 2 is a solution too. At \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) we have \(\sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.11, item 2, and periodicity), so the boundary conditions hold. This is item 2 and the direction "if" of item 1.
  7. A point \(x \in (0, L)\) is a node of mode \(n\) when \(\sin(n\pi x/L) = 0\), that is \(n\pi x/L = j\pi\) for an integer \(j\) by step 5. The condition \(0 < x < L\) gives \(0 < j < n\), so \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\) and \(x = jL/n\).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 572-573) separates the variables and shows that the separation constant must be negative, then obtains \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = cn\pi/L\) and the functions \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\). The number of nodes \(n - 1\) is there too (PDF p. 573, figure 287). The proof with the energy \(\psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\) is not from the source.

We draw figure 13.12 as follows. First the axes: the displacement \(\psi\) horizontal, \(\psi'/k\) vertical, which is the slope divided by \(k\), so that it has the same unit of length as \(\psi\). The point \((\psi, \psi'/k)\) at each \(x\) tells where the shape is and how steeply it changes. Since \(E/k^2\) is the squared distance of this point from the origin, the point always lies on a circle of constant radius. At \(E(0) = 0\) the radius is zero, the circle is just the origin, the point cannot leave it and \(\psi = 0\) everywhere.

Item 2 also says how the points vibrate in mode \(n\): each with the time dependence \(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), which is a vibration with the natural frequency \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(2\pi) = nc/(2L)\) (definition 9.4). Figure 13.13 shows the first three shapes. The nodes are the points that stay at rest, and in mode \(n\) the stretches between them have equal length \(L/n\). Why is the ratio of the frequencies a whole number? Because the shape \(\varphi\) of equation (13.7) must be zero at both ends: \(\sin kx\) with \(k = \omega/c\) has zeros at multiples of the distance \(\pi/k\), and the end \(L\) must be one of them, so \(kL = n\pi\), and the frequency \(\omega = kc\) is a multiple of the fundamental one.

Each row of figure 13.13 is one mode. The first mode is one arc, the second two and the third three. The ends are at rest in every mode. The dashed shape is the same string half a period later. In the second and third mode the nodes are points that stay at rest. To the right of each row is the natural frequency of the mode, and the distance \(L\) below them is the length of the string.

Example 13.8 Frequencies of the guitar string (frekvence kitarske strune)

For the string from the start of the section, \(c = 267{,}24\) m/s and \(L = 0{,}65\) m, equation (13.6) gives the circular frequency of the first mode \(\omega_1 = \pi c/L = 1291{,}6\) 1/s and the frequencies

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{n}} &= \frac{n c}{2L} = n \cdot 205{,}57\ \text{Hz} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3) , \\ f_1 &= 205{,}57 , \ f_2 = 411{,}13 , \ f_3 = 616{,}70\ \text{Hz} . \end{aligned}\]

The second mode has one node in the middle, \(x = 0{,}325\) m, the third has two, at \(0{,}2167\) and \(0{,}4333\) m (SymPy and NumPy). The frequency grows with the square root of the tension: at \(T = 77\) N, which is \(10\) % more, \(f_1 = 205{,}57\sqrt{77/70} = 215{,}60\) Hz, so \(4{,}88\) % more.

The last number corrects an error in a source. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 574) writes that the frequency is proportional to the tension, which is not true. Since \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac{n}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}\), it is proportional to the square root of the tension, which the number also shows: a tension \(1{,}1\) times larger gives a frequency only \(\sqrt{1{,}1} = 1{,}0488\) times larger. The same source adds that the first mode is called the fundamental and the others overtones (višji toni), and that the frequency \(2f_1\) musically corresponds to the octave and \(3f_1\) to the octave plus a fifth (PDF p. 573).

The definition of a mode requires two things at once: the function \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) solves the equation and satisfies the boundary conditions. So a pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is not a mode if either one fails. Let us look at both possibilities on the string of example 13.8, with \(\omega_1 = \pi c/L = 1291{,}6\) 1/s and \(\varepsilon = 1\) mm.

  1. Right shape, wrong frequency. Let \(\varphi = \varepsilon\sin(\pi x/L)\) and \(\omega = 1{,}1\,\omega_1 = 1420{,}8\) 1/s. The boundary conditions hold, \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\). The equation, however, requires \(-\omega^2\varphi = c^2\varphi''\) (step 1 of the proof of theorem 13.7). Here \(c^2\varphi'' = -\omega_1^2\varphi\), so the difference is \(-\omega^2\varphi + \omega_1^2\varphi = -0{,}21\,\omega_1^2\varphi\). At \(x = L/2\) this is \(-0{,}21\cdot 1291{,}6^2\cdot 10^{-3} = -350{,}3\) m/s\(^2\), not zero. The equation does not hold, so this is not a mode.
  2. The frequency is right for the equation, the shape is wrong for the condition. Let \(\varphi = \varepsilon\sin(1{,}5\pi x/L)\) and \(\omega = 1{,}5\,\omega_1 = 1937{,}4\) 1/s. With \(k = 1{,}5\pi/L\) we have \(\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi\) and \(c^2 k^2 = \omega^2\), so the equation holds. The condition at \(x = 0\) holds too, but at \(x = L\) we have \(\varphi(L) = \varepsilon\sin(1{,}5\pi) = -1\) mm, not zero. The end of the string would move, and it is fixed (figure 13.14). So the frequency \(1{,}5\cdot 205{,}57 = 308{,}35\) Hz is not a frequency of the string, although it lies between the first and the second.

Figure 13.14 puts the wrong shape next to the right ones. The first three modes of the string are drawn in blue: mode \(n = 1\) has no node, mode \(n = 2\) has one, mode \(n = 3\) has two, and each returns to the axis at both ends. Below is the shape \(\sin(1{,}5\pi x/L)\), with the same exaggerated height, which also solves the equation. At the right end, however, it does not reach the axis but ends at the value \(-1\) below it, at the distance \(L\) from the left end: the end of the string would move, and it is fixed.

The equation alone has the solution \(\sin kx\) for every \(k\), hence at every frequency. Only the boundary condition at \(x = L\) picks \(\sin kL = 0\) (step 5 of the proof), and from the continuous set of frequencies only \(n\cdot 205{,}57\) Hz remain.

A string released from the shape \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) vibrates in one mode only. A plucked string has an initial shape that is not a sine. The wave equation is linear, so a sum of solutions is a solution again, and we try to build the initial shape and velocity from sines. There are three questions. Does such a series converge, and is its sum really a solution that meets the initial conditions (13.5)? Which \(w_0\) can be expanded this way? How much of each mode is in a given state? The first two questions are settled by the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11), the third by the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12).

To expand in sines on \([0, L]\) we need a function on the whole axis, since the Fourier series of section 9.11.4 (definition 9.110) describes periodic functions. We extend a function \(h\) on \([0, L]\) with zeros at the ends so that it becomes odd and periodic with period \(2L\). Oddness brings only sines, because cosines are even. Figure 13.15 shows this for two functions.

The top row shows a function with zeros at the ends. On the axis are the points \(x\), and the dashed lines are at \(-L\), \(0\), \(L\) and \(2L\). The blue triangular \(h\) lies between \(0\) and \(L\) and is zero at both joints. The orange one is its mirror image, turned downward, on \([-L, 0]\) and repeats on \([L, 2L]\): this is an odd function with period \(2L\), which we call \(h^*\). The bottom row has on its axis the function \(h = x/L\), which equals \(1\) at \(L\): there the extension breaks off with a jump of size \(2\).

Definition 13.9 Odd periodic extension and sine coefficients (lihi periodični podaljšek in sinusni koeficienti)

Let \(h : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\). Its odd periodic extension (lihi periodični podaljšek) is the function \(h^* : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) that equals \(h(x)\) for \(0 \le x \le L\), equals \(-h(-x)\) for \(-L \le x < 0\) and is \(2L\)-periodic, \(h^*(x + 2L) = h^*(x)\). The sine coefficients (sinusni koeficienti) of \(h\) are the numbers

\[h_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L h(x)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x \qquad (n = 1, 2, \dots) . \tag{13.8}\]

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576): the coefficients \(B_{\mathrm{n}}\) of equation (14) and the odd periodic extension with period \(2L\) (figure 289). The notations \(h^*\) and \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\) are ours.

The function \(h^*\) is zero at both ends \(0\) and \(L\), so it is continuous at the joints of the extension too. Does its Fourier series really converge to \(h\), and what are its coefficients?

Lemma 13.10 Sine series of a function on an interval (sinusna vrsta funkcije na odseku)

Let \(h\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) (definition 6.11) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\). Then \(h^*\) is continuous, \(2L\)-periodic and piecewise continuously differentiable, its Fourier coefficients (definition 9.110 with \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2L\), \(\Omega = \pi/L\)) are \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \ge 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = h_{\mathrm{n}}\), and

\[\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}|h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \infty , \qquad \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \to h^*(x) \quad (N \to \infty) \tag{13.9}\]

uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\), so on \([0, L]\) to \(h\).

Proof

We must show the continuity of the extension, then the coefficients by oddness, and finally apply the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.119).

  1. On \([0, L]\) the function \(h^*\) equals \(h\), on \([-L, 0)\) it equals \(x \mapsto -h(-x)\), which is piecewise continuously differentiable because \(h\) is and reflecting the argument is linear. At the point \(0\) the left limit is \(-h(0) = 0\) and \(h^*(0) = h(0) = 0\). At the point \(L\) we have \(h^*(L) = h(L) = 0\), and to the right of it \(h^*(L + s) = h^*(-L + s) = -h(L - s) \to -h(L) = 0\) as \(s \to 0^+\). So \(h^*\) is continuous at all joints, and by periodicity everywhere.
  2. By the lemma on shift, reflection and period in the integral (9.108, item 3), the integral of a \(2L\)-periodic function over \([0, 2L]\) equals the integral over \([-L, L]\). For \(g(x) = h^*(x)\cos(n\pi x/L)\) we have \(g(-s) = -h(s)\cos(n\pi s/L)\) for \(s \in [0, L]\), so by reflection (lemma on shift, reflection and period in the integral, 9.108, item 2) \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}}^{0}g\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L h(s)\cos(n\pi s/L)\,\mathrm{d}s\), which cancels \(\int_0^L g\,\mathrm{d}x\). So \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \ge 0\).
  3. For \(g(x) = h^*(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with the odd extension we have \(g(-s) = (-h(s))(-\sin(n\pi s/L)) = g(s)\), so the two parts are equal and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}g\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{L}\cdot 2\int_0^L h\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = h_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  4. The function \(h^*\) is continuous, \(2L\)-periodic and piecewise continuously differentiable, so by the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.119) \(\sum(|a_{\mathrm{n}}| + |b_{\mathrm{n}}|) = \sum|h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \infty\) and \(S_{\mathrm{N}} h^* \to h^*\) uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since all \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(S_{\mathrm{N}} h^*(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576) expands \(f\) in a sine series with the same coefficients and uses the odd extension for convergence, but does not prove uniform convergence. The proof with the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.119) is not from the source.

The condition \(h(L) = 0\) in the lemma on the sine series is not a formality. Let \(h(x) = x/L\), which is smooth and has \(h(0) = 0\) but \(h(L) = 1\). The lemma does not apply to it, and the series indeed does not converge uniformly.

  1. The extension. To the left of \(L\) we have \(h^*(x) = x/L \to 1\), to the right \(h^*(L + s) = -h(L - s) \to -1\) as \(s \to 0^+\) (step 1 of the proof of the lemma). The extension has a jump of \(2\) at \(L\), it is not continuous, step 1 fails and theorem 9.119 cannot be used.
  2. The coefficients. Steps 2 and 3 of the proof use only oddness, so they still hold: \(h_{\mathrm{n}} = 2(-1)^{n+1}/(n\pi)\), that is \(0{,}6366\), \(-0{,}3183\), \(0{,}2122\), \(-0{,}1592\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 4\) (SymPy). They decay like \(1/n\), not like \(1/n^2\) as for the triangular shape of a plucked string, computed in example 13.14 below.
  3. The sum \(\sum|h_{\mathrm{n}}| = (2/\pi)\sum 1/n\) is not finite. The group of terms \(1/n\) from \(n = 2^k + 1\) to \(n = 2^{k+1}\) has \(2^k\) terms, each at least \(2^{-(k+1)}\), so the group gives at least \(2^k\cdot 2^{-(k+1)} = 1/2\). There are infinitely many groups, so the sum exceeds every bound. The partial sums are \(3{,}74\) at \(N = 200\) and \(5{,}21\) at \(N = 2000\).
  4. The partial sums \(S_{\mathrm{N}}(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}} h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) are all zero at \(x = L\), since \(\sin n\pi = 0\), not \(h(L) = 1\). Since \(S_{\mathrm{N}}\) is continuous, near \(L\) the difference \(|S_{\mathrm{N}} - h|\) is close to \(1\) for every \(N\), so the largest deviation does not tend to zero: the convergence is not uniform. Just below \(L\) the partial sum overshoots \(h\): the maximum is \(1{,}174\) at \(N = 200\) (at \(x = 0{,}995\,L\)) and \(1{,}178\) at \(N = 2000\) (at \(x = 0{,}9995\,L\)). The overshoot stays near \(9\) % of the jump \(2\), as in the Gibbs phenomenon (section 9.11.7, figure 13.17).

The triangular shape of a plucked string in example 13.14 below is zero at both ends, its extension is continuous and its coefficients decay like \(1/n^2\). So the condition \(h(L) = 0\) selects a continuous joint of the extension, and without it the joint is a jump.

The theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11) below requires the smoothness of the extension, not only of the function itself, because the sum must be twice differentiable to solve the wave equation. If the extension \(w_0^*\) is twice continuously differentiable, then \(w_0''(0) = w_0''(L) = 0\). The reason is oddness. We differentiate \(w_0^*(-x) = -w_0^*(x)\) twice by the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 3) and get \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(-x) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x)\). At \(x = 0\) this is \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(0) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(0)\), so \(w_0''(0) = 0\). At \(L\) likewise: by the period and the oddness, \(w_0^*(L + s) = w_0^*(s - L) = -w_0^*(L - s)\) for every \(s\), and the second derivative with respect to \(s\) at \(s = 0\) gives \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(L) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(L)\). The condition is not merely technical, though: the equation itself requires it. At the end \(w(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\), so \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}(0, t) = 0\) and by equation (13.2) also \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0\), so at \(t = 0\) also \(w_0''(0) = 0\), and likewise at \(L\). So every solution with continuous \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) has zero curvature at a fixed end. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 576) states the same conditions as sufficient for a twice differentiable extension.

That the condition \(w_0''(0) = w_0''(L) = 0\) is not a formality shows a smooth shape that violates it. Take the parabola \(w_0 = 4h\,x(L - x)/L^2\) with \(h = 1\) mm and \(v_0 = 0\). It is zero at both ends and smooth between them, but \(w_0'' = -8h/L^2 = -0{,}0189\) 1/m, so \(w_0''(0) \ne 0\). Figure 13.18 shows the parabola, its extension and the second derivative.

At the top, on the axis, is the parabola \(w_0\), zero at the ends and bulging upward: its curvature is negative. The extension \(w_0^*\) below the axis is the mirror image and is curved upward: its curvature is positive. At the joint at \(0\) the curvature jumps. At the bottom the axes are the time \(t\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the point \(0{,}1\) m. The course is \(-0{,}0189\) 1/m until \(0{,}374\) ms, then zero, the jump is \(0{,}0189\) 1/m, and the times are \(0{,}374\) and \(2{,}06\) ms.

So to the left of \(0\) we have \(w_0^{*\prime\prime} = +0{,}0189\) 1/m, to the right \(-0{,}0189\) 1/m. The function \(w_0^*\) is not twice continuously differentiable, the second derivative jumps by \(16h/L^2 = 0{,}0379\) 1/m, and likewise at \(L\).
  2. By formula (13.11) with \(v_0 = 0\), where the second derivatives exist, \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big)\) (step 4 of the proof of theorem 13.11). Take \(x = 0{,}1\) m. As long as \(x - ct > 0\), that is until \(t = x/c = 0{,}374\) ms, both arguments are in \((0, L)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}0189\) 1/m. From then until \(t = (L - x)/c = 2{,}06\) ms we have \(x - ct < 0\) and \(x + ct < L\), so \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12(+0{,}0189 - 0{,}0189) = 0\).
  3. The second derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) jumps by \(0{,}0189\) 1/m across the line \(x = ct\), and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by \(c^2\cdot 0{,}0189 = 1352\) m/s\(^2\). Definition 13.2 requires continuous \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), so this motion is not a string in its sense, although the initial shape is smooth.
  4. The reason is at the end. Since \(w(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}(0, t) = 0\) and by the equation also \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0\). The parabola has \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, 0) = -0{,}0189\) 1/m. The curvature at time zero and later do not agree at the end, and the jump runs along the string as a wave.

So the condition on the second derivative rejects a shape that is zero at the ends but has curvature there. Formula (13.11) still gives a generalized solution for the parabola, as for the triangular shape of a plucked string (example 13.14 below), but not a solution with continuous second derivatives.

One more idea leads to the same form of the solution without series. A wave that travels along the string with unchanged shape is \(w = F(x - ct)\). By the lemma on the travelling wave (13.5), it solves the equation \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for every twice continuously differentiable function \(F\). The same holds for \(F(x + ct)\), which travels in the opposite direction. The sum of two such waves, whose shapes we adapt to the initial conditions, is d'Alembert's solution. The theorem says that this solution and the series of modes are one and the same function.

Theorem 13.11 Expansion of the motion of the string in modes and d'Alembert's form (razvoj gibanja strune po načinih in d'Alembertova oblika)

Let \(L > 0\) and \(c > 0\). Let \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) be functions on \([0, L]\) with zeros at the ends, \(w_0(0) = w_0(L) = 0\) and \(v_0(0) = v_0(L) = 0\), and let the odd periodic extension \(w_0^*\) be twice continuously differentiable and \(v_0^*\) continuously differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}\) (definition 13.9). Let \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the sine coefficients of \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi c/L\). Then for every \(x\) and \(t\) the series

\[w(x, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big(p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + \frac{q_{\mathrm{n}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\Big) \tag{13.10}\]

converges, its sum is

\[w(x, t) = \frac12\big(w_0^*(x - ct) + w_0^*(x + ct)\big) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{13.11}\]

the derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) exist and are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the function \(w\) solves equation (13.2), satisfies the boundary conditions (13.4) and the initial conditions (13.5). Term \(n\) of the series is the solution of item 2 of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7), so the solution \(w\) is a sum of modes.

Proof

We must show that the partial sums of the series equal the partial sums of the sine series of \(w_0^*\) and \(v_0^*\) at the arguments \(x \mp ct\), that these converge (lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval, 13.10), and that the resulting function is smooth and solves the equation.

  1. The functions \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) are piecewise continuously differentiable with one partition (the extensions are continuously differentiable), so by the lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10), for every \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) we have \(\sum p_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi y/L) = w_0^*(y)\) and \(\sum q_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi y/L) = v_0^*(y)\), with uniform convergence on \(\mathbb{R}\). Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{N}}\) be the largest deviations of the partial sums from \(w_0^*\) and \(v_0^*\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}, \eta_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\).

  2. The partial sum \(W_{\mathrm{N}}\) of the series (13.10). Because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = n\pi ct/L\), the addition theorems give (right and left wave) \(\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = \tfrac12\big(\sin\frac{n\pi(x - ct)}{L} + \sin\frac{n\pi(x + ct)}{L}\big)\). By the theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine (8.13) and the fundamental theorem of calculus (3.55, item 1), \(\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}\sin\frac{n\pi s}{L}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{L}{n\pi}\big(\cos\frac{n\pi(x - ct)}{L} - \cos\frac{n\pi(x + ct)}{L}\big) = \frac{2L}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). Since \(\frac{1}{2c}\cdot\frac{2L}{n\pi} = \frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\),

    \[W_{\mathrm{N}}(x, t) = \frac12\big(S_{\mathrm{N}} w_0^*(x - ct) + S_{\mathrm{N}} w_0^*(x + ct)\big) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}S_{\mathrm{N}} v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s ,\]

    where \(S_{\mathrm{N}}\) is the partial sum of the sine series.

  3. As \(N \to \infty\) the first two terms tend to \(\frac12(w_0^*(x - ct) + w_0^*(x + ct))\) by step 1. For the integral, by the lemma on the estimate of the integral (9.36), \(\big|\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}(v_0^* - S_{\mathrm{N}} v_0^*)\,\mathrm{d}s\big| \le 2c|t|\,\eta_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\). So \(W_{\mathrm{N}}(x, t)\) converges to the right-hand side of equation (13.11). This is the first and second claim.

  4. Let \(G(y) = \int_0^y v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \(v_0^*\) is continuously differentiable, by the fundamental theorem of calculus (3.55, item 2) \(G' = v_0^*\) and \(G'' = v_0^{*\prime}\) is continuous, and \(\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}v_0^*\,\mathrm{d}s = G(x + ct) - G(x - ct)\).

    By the chain rule for a function of one variable (lemma 8.30, item 3), applied to the continuously differentiable functions \(w_0^*\), \(w_0^{*\prime}\), \(G\) and \(G'\),

\[\begin{aligned} w_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \tfrac12\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{1}{2c}\big(v_0^{*\prime}(x + ct) - v_0^{*\prime}(x - ct)\big) , \\ w_{\mathrm{tt}} &= \tfrac{c^2}{2}\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{c}{2}\big(v_0^{*\prime}(x + ct) - v_0^{*\prime}(x - ct)\big) . \end{aligned}\]

Both are continuous, and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). 5. Every term of the series (13.10) is zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), because \(\sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.11, item 2). So \(W_{\mathrm{N}}(0, t) = W_{\mathrm{N}}(L, t) = 0\) and in the limit \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\). 6. At \(t = 0\) we have \(w(x, 0) = \tfrac12\big(w_0^*(x) + w_0^*(x)\big) + 0 = w_0(x)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The derivative in \(t\) from step 4 is \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = \tfrac{c}{2}\big(-w_0^{*\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac12\big(v_0^*(x + ct) + v_0^*(x - ct)\big)\), and at \(t = 0\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0) = 0 + v_0^*(x) = v_0(x)\). 7. Term \(n\) is \(\sin(n\pi x/L)(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\) with \(A = p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(B = q_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), so it has the form of item 2 of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576) expands the solution in the series (12) with the coefficients (14), (15), sums it for \(v_0 = 0\) to \(\frac12(f^*(x - ct) + f^*(x + ct))\) and requires a twice differentiable \(f\) with zero second derivatives at the ends. D'Alembert's form and the solution with an initial velocity are in section 12.4 (PDF p. 579-581). The proof that the series equals d'Alembert's form by uniform convergence (lemma 13.10) is not from the source. We do not prove uniqueness of the solution.

The theorem says more than that the series is a solution. The term of each mode vibrates with its own frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) are the initial displacement and the initial velocity in mode \(n\), the first in the unit of displacement, the second in the unit of velocity. The shape \(w_0\) is found as the sum of all modes at time zero. D'Alembert's form shows the same motion differently: the string is the sum of two half shapes that move apart with speed \(c\). Figure 13.19 shows this for a plucked string.

Figure 13.19 shows four moments. The top row \(ct = 0\) is the start. The axis of each row is the string at rest, the vertical segments are the ends \(0\) and \(L\). The blue wave moves to the right, the orange one to the left, each of half the height, and their blackwhite sum is the string. The second, third and fourth row show the times \(0{,}1\,L\), \(0{,}2\,L\) and \(0{,}4\,L\), and the distance \(L\) is the length between the ends.

At time \(ct = 0{,}2\,L\) the peak of the orange wave reaches the end \(x = 0\), and at the same time the inverted part of the blue wave, which belongs to the odd extension, enters the string from the left. The sum is zero at the end at all times. This is the reflection at a fixed end: the wave turns over because \(w_0^*\) is odd. After the time \(L/c\) the shape is \(-w_0(L - x)\), inverted and mirrored, after \(2L/c = 4{,}86\) ms it is the same as at the start again, and this is the period of the first mode \(1/f_1\).

The triangular shape, however, does not satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem, because it has a kink at the peak and \(w_0^*\) is not twice differentiable. The lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10) holds for it, since it requires only piecewise continuous differentiability. At the kink, expression (13.11) is a generalized solution (posplošena rešitev) in the sense of KREYSZIG (PDF p. 576): the equation holds everywhere except on the lines where \(x - ct\) or \(x + ct\) equals a kink of the extension, that is \(\pm x_0 + 2kL\) with an integer \(k\), where the second derivatives do not exist. That the series of modes converges to expression (13.11) here too, however, we have already proved. Steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 13.11 use only the uniform convergence of the sine series of the initial shape, which lemma 13.10 gives for a piecewise continuously differentiable \(w_0\) and \(v_0 = 0\). Only step 4, which needs the second derivatives, fails. The numbers in example 13.14 check this.

How much of each mode is in the shape \(w_0\)? The square of the coefficient \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a measure of the share of mode \(n\), if the sum of all squares is known. That this sum is twice the mean square of the displacement, \(\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), is stated by the following lemma, and the factor \(2\) comes from the mean of \(\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\) over \([0, L]\) being \(\tfrac12\). The proof of the lemma starts from the uniform convergence of the sine series (lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval, 13.10), so it does not need the general Parseval equality, which chapter 9 only states (section 9.11.7).

Lemma 13.12 Parseval equality for the sine series (Parsevalova enakost za sinusno vrsto)

Let \(h\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\) and sine coefficients \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then

\[\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L h(x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{13.12}\]
Proof

We must show that the integral of the product of \(h\) with a partial sum of its sine series is expressed by the coefficients, and that it tends to \(\int h^2\) because the partial sums converge uniformly.

  1. Let \(S_{\mathrm{N}}(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\). By linearity of the integral (lemma on the properties of the integral of piecewise continuous functions, 6.10, item 2) and by equation (13.8), \(\int_0^L h\,S_{\mathrm{N}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\int_0^L h\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).
  2. Let \(M\) be the maximum of the function \(|h|\) on \([0, L]\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}} = \sup|h - S_{\mathrm{N}}|\), which tends to zero by the lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10). By the lemma on the estimate of the integral (9.36), \(\big|\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L h\,S_{\mathrm{N}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big| = \big|\int_0^L h\,(h - S_{\mathrm{N}})\,\mathrm{d}x\big| \le L\,M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(\frac{L}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) tends to \(\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so the series \(\sum h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) converges and equation (13.12) holds.

Source: LEBL2 theorem 11.8.13 (PDF p. 204) proves the equality for every \(2\pi\)-periodic Riemann integrable function (for period \(2L\) after a change of variable, which is our addition) with the Stone-Weierstrass theorem (LEBL2 theorem 11.7.12, PDF p. 185), which this book does not prove. The proof for a uniformly convergent sine series in three steps is not from the source.

From the equality it follows that the sines miss nothing: no nonzero shape is orthogonal to all of them.

Corollary 13.13 Completeness of the sines (popolnost sinusov)

Let \(h\) be piecewise continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\) and \(\int_0^L h(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every \(n \ge 1\). Then \(h = 0\) on \([0, L]\).

Proof

We must show that zero coefficients give a zero integral of the square, and that this gives a zero function.

  1. All \(h_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), so by the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12), \(\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L}{2}\sum h_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 0\).
  2. The function \(h^2\) is nonnegative and continuous, so by the lemma on the properties of the integral of piecewise continuous functions (6.10, item 3) it is zero on every closed piece, so \(h = 0\) everywhere.

Source: The corollary is ours, from the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12). KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574) only uses the sine functions.

The corollary says that the coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) determine the shape \(w_0\): two shapes with equal coefficients are equal, since their difference has all coefficients zero and is therefore zero by the corollary. So no modes are missing: no admissible shape of the string escapes the sum of sines. For shapes that are not piecewise continuously differentiable we do not claim this.

Example 13.14 Plucked guitar string (zabrenkana kitarska struna)

We pull the string of example 13.8 at \(x_0 = L/5 = 0{,}13\) m by \(h = 1\) mm and release it without velocity. The initial shape is triangular, \(w_0 = h\,x/x_0\) for \(x \le x_0\) and \(w_0 = h\,(L - x)/(L - x_0)\) for \(x \ge x_0\), \(v_0 = 0\). The slope is at most \(h/x_0 = 0{,}0077\), below the bound \(0{,}01\) of section 13.1, at which the cosine of the slope angle differs from \(1\) by less than \(5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) (here \(3 \cdot 10^{-5}\)). By formula (13.11) the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is half the sum of two slopes of the extension, so at every time it is at most \(0{,}0077\) in absolute value, where it exists.

  1. By equation (13.8) the coefficients are \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{2}{L}\displaystyle\int_0^L w_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{2h L^2\sin(n\pi x_0/L)}{\pi^2 n^2 x_0 (L - x_0)}\) (SymPy). The factor \(1/n^2\) comes from the kink. With the jump of the slope at the kink, \(s = h/x_0 + h/(L - x_0) = hL/(x_0(L - x_0))\), we have \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{2Ls}{\pi^2 n^2}\sin\dfrac{n\pi x_0}{L}\): at the same \(x_0\) every \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) is proportional to the jump \(s\), so the shares of the modes do not depend on \(h\), while the decay stays \(1/n^2\). How strong the higher modes are relative to the first is set by the factor \(\sin(n\pi x_0/L)\), that is by the place where we hold the string.
  2. The factor \(\sin(n\pi x_0/L) = \sin(n\pi/5)\) is zero at \(n = 5, 10, 15, \dots\): the point where we hold the string, \(x_0 = L/5 = jL/n\) with \(j = n/5\), is a node of every mode whose \(n\) is a multiple of \(5\) (step 7 of the proof of the theorem on the modes of the string, 13.7). These modes are not excited.
  3. The numbers: \(p_1 = 0{,}7444\) mm, \(p_2 = 0{,}3011\) mm, \(p_3 = 0{,}1338\) mm, \(p_4 = 0{,}0465\) mm, \(p_5 = 0\), \(p_6 = -0{,}0207\) mm.
  4. The Parseval equality (13.12) gives \(\sum p_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{2h^2}{3} = 0{,}6667\) mm² (integral of the triangle, \(\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x = h^2L/3\), checked numerically). The share of mode \(n\) in the mean square of the displacement is \(p_{\mathrm{n}}^2/(2h^2/3)\): \(83{,}13\) %, \(13{,}60\) %, \(2{,}69\) %, \(0{,}32\) %, \(0\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 5\). The first three modes together carry \(99{,}42\) %, the first ten \(99{,}96\) %.
  5. A check of the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11) on this shape (which does not meet its hypotheses): the series (13.10) with \(20\,000\) terms gives the displacement \(0{,}2056\) mm at \(x = 0{,}30\) m, \(t = 1\) ms, d'Alembert's formula (13.11) gives the same number, and at \(x = 0{,}50\) m, \(t = 2{,}5\) ms both give \(-0{,}9615\) mm (NumPy).

The slope \(0{,}0077\) is the bound for this height, not for every pluck. Let us pull the same string at the same \(x_0\) by \(h = 2\) mm instead of \(1\) mm and check the bound \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) of section 13.1.

  1. The slope of the first segment is \(h/x_0 = 0{,}002/0{,}13 = 0{,}0154\), above the bound \(0{,}01\).
  2. The cosine of the slope angle differs from \(1\) by \(1 - \cos(\arctan 0{,}0154) = 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4}\), more than twice the bound \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\) (at \(h = 1\) mm it is \(2{,}96\cdot 10^{-5}\)).
  3. The stretch of the string on the steep piece here is \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}0154^2} - 1 = 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4}\), by Hooke's law the force increases by at most \(EA\cdot 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4} = 24{,}63\ \text{kN}\cdot 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4} = 2{,}91\) N, that is \(4{,}2\) % of \(T\), and the wave speed by at most \(2{,}1\) %. At the bound \(0{,}01\) idealization 3 allowed \(1{,}8\) % and \(0{,}9\) %.

So at \(h = 2\) mm the model is outside its range, although the example differs in one number only. The largest height at which the slope is still within the bound is \(h = 0{,}01\,x_0 = 1{,}3\) mm.

The sound of a plucked guitar is thus almost entirely the fundamental, and its colour is the higher harmonics, which are chosen by the place where we excite the string. At the middle (\(x_0 = L/2\)) all even modes would drop out, because they have a node there. We showed that the solution which the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11) builds from the initial shape and velocity is a sum of the modes of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7), which we proved with a uniformly convergent sine series and d'Alembert's form. We have not proved that it is the only solution. We also showed that the coefficients determine the initial shape (corollary on the completeness of the sines, 13.13) and that half the sum of their squares is the mean square of the displacement (Parseval equality for the sine series, 13.12). The same procedure, the expansion in the proper shapes and the time dependence of each one separately, will be used for the bar, the shaft and the beam. The bar and the shaft obey a wave equation of the same form, so travelling waves remain there too (lemma 13.5). For the beam d'Alembert's form is gone and only the modes and the expansion in them remain.

13.2.1 The string as beads on a cord

We obtained the modes of the string from the wave equation. The road of chapter 10 leads to them too, with a few masses and Newton's law for each. We replace the string by \(N\) beads on a massless cord and ask how many beads we need to hit the frequency \(205{,}57\) Hz of example 13.8. The answer also shows where the sines come from.

We stretch the cord between two walls with the force \(T\), as we did the string. Its length is \(L\). We thread two equal beads of mass \(m\) onto it, so that they divide the cord into three equal pieces of length \(h = L/3\). With \(N\) beads there are \(N + 1\) pieces and \(h = L/(N + 1)\). At rest the cord lies on the grey dashed line. We displace the beads sideways, the first by \(w_1\) and the second by \(w_2\), and the cord takes the orange broken shape of three straight pieces. On the right is the first bead alone. The left piece pulls it with the force \(T\) towards the left wall, the middle piece with the force \(T\) towards the second bead. Only the vertical parts of these forces bring it back to rest, \(T\,w_1/h\) and \(T\,(w_1 - w_2)/h\), which we derive in the proof below. The restoring force of a piece is thus the tension times the slope of the piece, \(T\,\Delta w/\Delta x\), and each piece acts as a spring of stiffness \(T/h\).

The model has three assumptions.

  1. The mass is gathered in the beads, the pieces of cord are massless. This is why the pieces are straight: by Newton's law the forces on a massless piece add up to zero, so the forces at its two ends are equal and opposite and lie on one line, the line of the piece. The assumption is good when the beads are many compared with the number of arcs of the mode. Its effect is frequencies that are too low: with two beads the first is \(22{,}0\) % below the frequency of the string, the second \(32{,}5\) %.
  2. The beads move only sideways and the slopes of the pieces are small, \(|\Delta w|/h \le 0{,}01\), as for the string (definition 13.2). Since a bead has no horizontal acceleration, the horizontal part of the force is the same in all pieces. We call it \(H\). The vertical part of the force of a piece is then exactly \(H\,\Delta w/h\), because the horizontal and the vertical part of the force are in the same ratio as the legs of the piece, \(h\) and \(\Delta w\). The small slope is used only for \(H = T\): the force along the piece is \(H/\cos\alpha\), and at the slope \(0{,}01\) it differs from \(H\) by \(5 \cdot 10^{-5}\). The assumption fails when the displacement is comparable with \(h\). Then the cord stretches noticeably, the tension grows and the equation is no longer linear (CK equation (6.62), PDF p. 190).
  3. The weight is not in the equations. We measure the displacements from the static equilibrium position, in which the weight and the forces of the cord already add up to zero (lemma 10.12). For the guitar string the weight is \(\mu L g = 6{,}25\) mN against \(T = 70\) N, so this position is almost straight.
Theorem 13.15 Frequencies of beads on a taut cord (frekvence kroglic na napeti vrvici)

Let \(N \ge 1\). On a massless cord fixed at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) let there be \(N\) beads of equal mass \(m > 0\) at the points \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = jh\), \(j = 1, \dots, N\), where \(h = L/(N + 1)\). Let the beads move only sideways, with the displacements \(w_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\), and let the horizontal part of the force in every piece of the cord be \(T > 0\) (assumptions 1 and 2 above).

  1. On a bead \(j\) the vertical parts of the forces of the two neighbouring pieces pull back, so the equations of motion are
    \[m\,\ddot w_{\mathrm{j}} = -\frac{T}{h}\,\big(-w_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}+1}\big) \quad (j = 1, \dots, N) , \qquad w_0 = w_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0 , \tag{13.13}\]
    so \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat K = \tfrac{T}{h}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)\). This is the chain of equal masses of corollary 10.56 with the springs \(k = T/h\).
  2. The natural circular frequencies and the components of the eigenvectors are, for \(r = 1, \dots, N\),
    \[\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)} = 2\sqrt{\frac{T}{mh}}\,\sin\frac{r\pi}{2(N + 1)} , \qquad \phi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{j}} = \sin\frac{jr\pi}{N + 1} = \sin\frac{r\pi x_{\mathrm{j}}}{L} . \tag{13.14}\]
    The beads in mode \(r\) lie on the shape \(\sin(r\pi x/L)\) of mode \(r\) of the string.
  3. Let the total mass of the beads equal the mass of the string, \(Nm = \mu L\), and let \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi c/L\) with \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) be the frequency of the string from equation (13.6). Then
    \[\sqrt{\frac{N}{N + 1}}\,\sqrt{1 - \frac{\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2}{12}} \ \le\ \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{(N)}}{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}} \ \le\ \sqrt{\frac{N}{N + 1}} \ <\ 1 , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{r\pi}{N + 1} . \tag{13.15}\]
    The frequencies of the beads lie below those of the string, and for a fixed \(r\) both bounds tend to \(1\) as \(N\) grows.
Proof

We must show three points. The first comes from Newton's law for one bead, the second from the corollary on the chain of equal masses, the third from the estimate of the cosine.

  1. The piece between the beads \(j - 1\) and \(j\) has the horizontal leg \(h\) and the vertical leg \(w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}-1}\). The force of the piece on bead \(j\) lies along the piece (assumption 1), so its horizontal and vertical parts are in the ratio \(h : (w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}-1})\). The horizontal part is \(T\) and points towards bead \(j - 1\). The vertical part is therefore \(-T\,(w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}-1})/h\): for \(w_{\mathrm{j}} > w_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) it pulls the bead down.
  2. In the same way the piece between the beads \(j\) and \(j + 1\) acts on bead \(j\) with the vertical part \(-T\,(w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}+1})/h\). For the first and the last bead the neighbour is a wall, which is at rest, so \(w_0 = w_{\mathrm{N}+1} = 0\).
  3. Newton's law in the transverse direction for bead \(j\) is \(m\,\ddot w_{\mathrm{j}} = -T\,(w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}-1})/h - T\,(w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}+1})/h\), which is equation (13.13). It is equation (10.79) with \(k = T/h\) and \(q_{\mathrm{j}} = w_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the matrices follow. This is item 1.
  4. By corollary 10.56 (item 1) the eigenpairs are \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}})\) with \(\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}\) from equation (10.81), where \(k/m = T/(mh)\), and with the components \(\sin j\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) from equation (10.80). Since \(x_{\mathrm{j}}/L = j/(N + 1)\), we have \(j\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi x_{\mathrm{j}}/L\): component \(j\) is the value of the sine of the string at the point \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\). This is item 2.
  5. With \(m = \mu L/N\) and \(h = L/(N + 1)\) we have \(\dfrac{T}{mh} = \dfrac{T\,N\,(N + 1)}{\mu L^2} = N(N + 1)\,\dfrac{c^2}{L^2}\). By equation (10.81), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \dfrac{2T}{mh}\,(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}})\).
  6. The estimate of the cosine (lemma 10.53) gives \(\tfrac12\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,(1 - \theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/12) \le 1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}} \le \tfrac12\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). We multiply by \(2T/(mh)\) and use step 5:
    \[N(N + 1)\,\frac{c^2}{L^2}\,\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\Big(1 - \frac{\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2}{12}\Big) \ \le\ \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ \le\ N(N + 1)\,\frac{c^2}{L^2}\,\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 .\]
  7. Since \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = r^2\pi^2/(N + 1)^2\), we have \(N(N + 1)\,\dfrac{c^2}{L^2}\,\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = \dfrac{N}{N + 1}\,\dfrac{r^2\pi^2c^2}{L^2} = \dfrac{N}{N + 1}\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). We divide the inequalities of step 6 by \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) and take square roots, which is allowed because \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} < \pi\) and hence \(1 - \theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/12 > 0\). This gives equation (13.15), and \(N/(N + 1) < 1\). The ratio is the height of a point below the line \(1\).
  8. For a fixed \(r\), \(N/(N + 1) = 1 - 1/(N + 1)\) is as close to \(1\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi/(N + 1)\) as close to \(0\) as we like once \(N\) is large enough, so both bounds are as close to \(1\) as we like. This is item 3.

Source: CK section 6.3, equation (6.63) (PDF p. 190, crop read): one mass at the middle of a taut string, \(m\ddot u + (2T_0/L)\,u = p(t)\), where \(L\) in CK is half the span. This is our case \(N = 1\) with \(h\) in place of \(L\). The frequencies of the chain follow GR section 4.3.1, as in corollary 10.56. The comparison with the string and the bounds (13.15) are not from the source.

Example 13.17 Largest span of a cable with two loads (največji razpon kabla z dvema bremenoma)

A cable with the mass per length \(\mu_{\mathrm{k}} = 0{,}4\) kg/m is tightened with the horizontal force \(T = 5000\) N. At the thirds of the span hang two loads of mass \(m_{\mathrm{d}} = 25\) kg each. The sideways vibration, horizontal and perpendicular to the cable, must not have a frequency below \(f_{\min} = 1\) Hz. What is the largest span \(L\)?

  1. Model. In the horizontal transverse direction the weight does not act and the cable is straight in plan, so figure 13.20 holds, seen from above. We divide the mass of the cable between two beads at the loads, \(m = m_{\mathrm{d}} + \mu_{\mathrm{k}} L/2\), and \(h = L/3\).
  2. The first frequency by equation (13.14) with \(N = 2\), \(r = 1\) and \(2\sin(\pi/6) = 1\) is
    \[\omega_1^2 = \frac{T}{mh} = \frac{3T}{L\,(m_{\mathrm{d}} + \mu_{\mathrm{k}} L/2)} .\]
  3. The requirement \(\omega_1 \ge 2\pi f_{\min}\) is \(L\,(m_{\mathrm{d}} + \mu_{\mathrm{k}} L/2) \le A\) with \(A = 3T/(2\pi f_{\min})^2 = 379{,}95\) kg m. The left side grows with \(L\), so the largest span is the positive root of \(\tfrac12\mu_{\mathrm{k}} L^2 + m_{\mathrm{d}} L - A = 0\):
    \[L_{\max} = \frac{-m_{\mathrm{d}} + \sqrt{m_{\mathrm{d}}^2 + 2\mu_{\mathrm{k}} A}}{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{-25 + \sqrt{625 + 303{,}96}}{0{,}4} = 13{,}70\ \text{m} .\]
  4. Check: \(m = 25 + 0{,}4 \cdot 13{,}70/2 = 27{,}74\) kg, \(h = 4{,}566\) m, \(\omega_1 = \sqrt{5000/(27{,}74 \cdot 4{,}566)} = 6{,}283\) 1/s, that is \(1{,}000\) Hz.
  5. What does assumption 1 cost? A finer model with \(599\) beads that carry the mass of the cable, and the two loads at the thirds, gives the frequency \(1{,}0225\) Hz at \(L = 13{,}70\) m and reaches the limit \(1\) Hz at \(14{,}29\) m (NumPy). The model with two beads is thus on the safe side, it underestimates the span by \(4{,}1\) %. Without the loads it would give \(43{,}59\) m in place of the exact \(c/(2f_{\min}) = 55{,}90\) m, the same \(22{,}0\) % too little as for the guitar string. With the loads the error is smaller, because \(50\) kg of the \(55{,}5\) kg of mass really are gathered in two points.
  6. Why only the sideways vibration? The weight of the loads and the cable is \((2 \cdot 25 + 0{,}4 \cdot 13{,}70) \cdot 9{,}81 = 544\) N, the slope of the cable at the supports in the vertical plane is \(544/(2 \cdot 5000) = 0{,}054\) and the sag about \(0{,}24\) m. In the vertical vibration of a sagging cable the cable stretches already at small displacements and the force in it oscillates with the displacement, so the model with the constant force \(T\) is not enough there. We do not treat that case.

13.2.2 The string with a spring at the end

A fixed end is an idealization: the bridge of a guitar gives a little under the string. What happens to the frequencies if the end \(x = L\) is held not by a wall but by a spring? The answer joins the fixed and the free end into one family, and shows that the frequency ratio \(1 : 2 : 3\) holds only for fixed ends.

We fix the string at \(x = 0\) to a wall. We tie the other end to a small ring, which slides without friction on a vertical guide at \(x = L\). The guide takes the horizontal force \(T\), so the string stays taut. The ring is held by a spring of stiffness \(k\), which is unloaded when the ring is on the rest line. The string of length \(L\) has at time \(t\) the orange shape, and the ring is displaced by \(w(L, t)\). At the top right is the ring alone. The string pulls it with the force \(T\) along its tangent. The vertical part of this force is \(-T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\), tension times slope as for the beads: in the figure the string falls towards the ring, \(w_{\mathrm{x}} < 0\), and this part points up. The spring is compressed by \(w(L, t)\) and pushes the ring back with the force \(k\,w(L, t)\). The guide holds it only horizontally. The ring has no mass, so by Newton's law the vertical forces on it add up to zero, \(-T\,w_{\mathrm{x}} - k\,w = 0\):

\[T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) + k\,w(L, t) = 0 . \tag{13.16}\]

The condition has a simple geometry. The slope at the end is \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = -w/(T/k)\), so the tangent at the end meets the rest line at the distance \(T/k\) beyond the end, for every displacement and at every time. With a stiff spring \(T/k\) is small against \(L\) and the string behaves as if it were fixed a little further on. With a soft spring \(T/k\) is large, the tangent almost horizontal, and this is the free end \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\).

The condition has three assumptions.

  1. The ring and the spring have no mass. With a ring of mass \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) the sum of the forces would be \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and the condition in a mode of frequency \(\omega\) would be \(T\,w_{\mathrm{x}} + (k - M_{\mathrm{k}}\omega^2)\,w = 0\). The mass is negligible when \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\omega^2\) is small against \(k\). For the guitar string with \(k = 300\) N/m a ring of mass \(0{,}01\) g (\(M_{\mathrm{k}}\omega^2 = 9{,}8\) N/m) lowers the first frequency by \(0{,}57\) %, a ring of \(0{,}1\) g (\(88\) N/m) already by \(5{,}9\) % (NumPy).
  2. The guide is frictionless. Friction would add a force against the motion of the ring, that is damping (chapter 20), which is absent here.
  3. The slope is small at the end too, \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)| \le 0{,}01\). By condition (13.16) this means \(|w(L, t)| \le 0{,}01\,T/k\), so \(2{,}3\) mm for \(k = 300\) N/m.
Theorem 13.18 Frequency equation of a string with a spring at the end (frekvenčna enačba strune z vzmetjo na koncu)

Let \(L\), \(T\), \(\mu\) and \(k\) be positive numbers, \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) and \(\kappa = kL/T\). Let the string satisfy equation (13.2), the condition \(w(0, t) = 0\) and condition (13.16) at \(x = L\).

  1. The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode (definition 13.6) if and only if \(\omega = s\,c/L\) and \(\varphi(x) = C\sin(s\,x/L)\) with \(C \ne 0\), where \(s > 0\) is a solution of the frequency equation, that is the abscissa of an intersection of a branch of the tangent with the line
    \[s\cos s + \kappa\sin s = 0 , \qquad \text{that is} \qquad \frac{T\omega}{kc} + \tan\frac{\omega L}{c} = 0 . \tag{13.17}\]
  2. The frequency equation has in every interval \(\big((2n - 1)\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ n\pi\big)\), \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), exactly one solution \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) and no other on \((0, \infty)\).
  3. For every \(n\)
    \[n\pi\,\frac{\kappa}{1 + \kappa} \ <\ s_{\mathrm{n}} \ <\ n\pi , \qquad (2n - 1)\frac{\pi}{2} \ <\ s_{\mathrm{n}} \ <\ (2n - 1)\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{2\kappa}{(2n - 1)\pi} . \tag{13.18}\]
    So as \(k \to \infty\), \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) tends to \(n\pi\), the frequency of the fixed end, with a relative decrease below \(1/(1 + \kappa) < T/(kL)\). As \(k \to 0\), \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) tends to \((2n - 1)\pi/2\), the frequency of the free end, \(\omega = (2n - 1)\pi c/(2L)\).
Proof

We must show three points. The shape comes as for the fixed string, the condition at the end gives the frequency equation, the roots are counted by the sign and the monotonicity of one function, and the bounds follow from the inequality \(\sin\delta > \delta\cos\delta\).

  1. Let \((\omega, \varphi)\) be a mode and \(\beta = \omega/c > 0\). By step 1 of the proof of theorem 13.7, \(\varphi'' = -\beta^2\varphi\), the condition at \(x = 0\) gives \(\varphi(0) = 0\), and by steps 3 and 4 of the same proof, which use only these two facts, \(\varphi = C\sin\beta x\) with \(C = \varphi'(0)/\beta\). Since \(\varphi\) is not zero everywhere, \(C \ne 0\).
  2. Condition (13.16) at \(t = 0\) gives \(T\varphi'(L) + k\varphi(L) = 0\), that is \(C\,(T\beta\cos\beta L + k\sin\beta L) = 0\). We divide by \(C\), multiply by \(L/T\) and write \(s = \beta L\): \(s\cos s + \kappa\sin s = 0\). The slope at the end and the displacement of the end have opposite signs.
  3. Conversely, for such an \(s\) the function \(w = C\sin(sx/L)\cos\omega t\) with such a shape with \(\omega = sc/L\) solves equation (13.2) (the computation of step 6 of the proof of theorem 13.7 with \(s/L\) in place of \(n\pi/L\)), \(w(0, t) = 0\) and \(T w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) + k\,w(L, t) = C\cos\omega t\,\tfrac{T}{L}\,(s\cos s + \kappa\sin s) = 0\). At a solution \(\cos s \ne 0\), otherwise the equation would give \(\kappa\sin s = 0\) with \(\sin s = \pm 1\). So we may divide by \(\kappa\cos s\) and obtain \(s/\kappa + \tan s = 0\), where \(s/\kappa = T\omega/(kc)\). This is item 1.
  4. To count the roots we write \(s = (n - 1)\pi + \sigma\) with \(n = 1, 2, \dots\) and \(\sigma \in (0, \pi]\). By the addition theorem \(\sin s = (-1)^{n-1}\sin\sigma\) and \(\cos s = (-1)^{n-1}\cos\sigma\), so the left side of the frequency equation is \((-1)^{n-1}\,q_{\mathrm{n}}(\sigma)\) with \(q_{\mathrm{n}}(\sigma) = \big((n - 1)\pi + \sigma\big)\cos\sigma + \kappa\sin\sigma\).
  5. On \((0, \pi/2]\) we have \(\cos\sigma \ge 0\) and \(\sin\sigma > 0\) (lemma 8.11, item 3), so \(q_{\mathrm{n}}(\sigma) \ge \kappa\sin\sigma > 0\): there is no root on the first half of any branch, where \(\tan s\) and \(s/\kappa\) are both positive.
  6. On \((\pi/2, \pi)\) we have \(q_{\mathrm{n}}'(\sigma) = (1 + \kappa)\cos\sigma - \big((n - 1)\pi + \sigma\big)\sin\sigma < 0\), because \(\cos\sigma < 0\) and \(\sin\sigma > 0\) there (the rules of differentiation, lemma 1.82, and theorem 8.13). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) strictly decreases on \([\pi/2, \pi]\). Since \(q_{\mathrm{n}}(\pi/2) = \kappa > 0\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}(\pi) = -n\pi < 0\), by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) it has exactly one zero \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} \in (\pi/2, \pi)\). So \(s_{\mathrm{n}} = (n - 1)\pi + \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the only root between \((2n - 1)\pi/2\) and \(n\pi\). This is item 2.
  7. An auxiliary inequality: for \(\delta \in (0, \pi/2)\), \(\sin\delta > \delta\cos\delta\). The function \(p(\delta) = \sin\delta - \delta\cos\delta\) has \(p(0) = 0\) and \(p'(\delta) = \delta\sin\delta > 0\) on \((0, \pi/2)\), so by the mean value theorem it strictly increases and is positive there.
  8. The bound for a stiff spring. Let \(\delta = n\pi - s_{\mathrm{n}} \in (0, \pi/2)\), the distance of the root from \(n\pi\). Then \(\cos s_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n\cos\delta\) and \(\sin s_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^{n+1}\sin\delta\), and the frequency equation is \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\delta = \kappa\sin\delta\). By step 7, \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\delta > \kappa\,\delta\cos\delta\), so \(s_{\mathrm{n}} > \kappa\,(n\pi - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(s_{\mathrm{n}} > n\pi\,\kappa/(1 + \kappa)\). The relative decrease is \(1 - s_{\mathrm{n}}/(n\pi) < 1/(1 + \kappa) < 1/\kappa = T/(kL)\).
  9. The bound for a soft spring. Let \(\varepsilon = s_{\mathrm{n}} - (2n - 1)\pi/2 \in (0, \pi/2)\), the distance of the root from the asymptote. Then \(\cos s_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n\sin\varepsilon\) and \(\sin s_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^{n-1}\cos\varepsilon\), and the frequency equation is \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\varepsilon = \kappa\cos\varepsilon\). By step 7, \(\kappa\cos\varepsilon > s_{\mathrm{n}}\,\varepsilon\cos\varepsilon\), so \(\varepsilon < \kappa/s_{\mathrm{n}} < 2\kappa/\big((2n - 1)\pi\big)\). This is equation (13.18).
  10. As \(k \to \infty\), \(\kappa/(1 + \kappa)\) tends to \(1\), so by the first double inequality \(s_{\mathrm{n}} \to n\pi\). As \(k \to 0\), \(\kappa\) tends to \(0\), so by the second \(s_{\mathrm{n}} \to (2n - 1)\pi/2\). This is item 3.

CK in note 4 (PDF p. 422) mentions springs and masses at the ends and leaves the derivation to the problems. The shape \(\sin\beta x\) follows KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF pp. 572-573). The lower bound \(n\pi\,\kappa/(1 + \kappa)\) has a picture. A string with fixed ends and the length \(L + T/k\) has by equation (13.6) the frequencies \(n\pi c/(L + T/k)\), in units of \(c/L\) exactly \(n\pi\,\kappa/(1 + \kappa)\), since \(L/(L + T/k) = \kappa/(1 + \kappa)\). So the frequency of the string with a spring lies between the frequency of the string lengthened by \(T/k\), up to the point where the tangent meets the rest line, and the frequency of the fixed string of length \(L\). Why is the true frequency higher than that of the lengthened string? The sine of the shape, continued beyond the end, bends below the tangent and reaches the rest line before the point at the distance \(T/k\). Its half wave is therefore shorter than \(L + T/k\), and a shorter half wave means a higher frequency.

We draw figure 13.23 part by part. On the horizontal axis is \(s = \omega L/c\). The blue branches are \(\tan s\), each starts at \(-\infty\) to the right of a dashed asymptote at \((2n - 1)\pi/2\) and passes through zero at \(n\pi\), where the open circles are. The right side of the equation, \(-s/\kappa\), we draw as a line through the origin, which falls the more steeply the softer the spring: steep for \(k = 100\) N/m, in between for \(300\) N/m and almost horizontal for \(3000\) N/m. The roots are the intersections. The line is below the axis, and the tangent function is negative only between an asymptote and \(n\pi\), so on every branch there is exactly one intersection and it lies there. With the stiff spring the intersections are just before the open circles, so at the fixed string. With the soft one they slide down the branches towards the asymptotes, so to the free end. With the middle spring they are in between, and the distances between the roots are no longer equal.

Example 13.19 Guitar string with a spring at the end (kitarska struna z vzmetjo na koncu)

The string of example 13.8 has \(T/L = 107{,}69\) N/m and \(c/(2\pi L) = 65{,}43\) Hz, so \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 65{,}43\,s_{\mathrm{n}}\) Hz. We find the roots of equation (13.17) as intersections by bisection on the intervals of item 2 of the theorem (NumPy).

  1. Soft spring, \(k = 100\) N/m: \(\kappa = 0{,}9286\), \(s = 2{,}0046\), \(4{,}8997\), \(7{,}9700\) and \(f = 131{,}17\), \(320{,}61\), \(521{,}51\) Hz.
  2. Middle spring, \(k = 300\) N/m: \(\kappa = 2{,}7857\), \(s = 2{,}4253\), \(5{,}2039\), \(8{,}1821\) and \(f = 158{,}69\), \(340{,}51\), \(535{,}39\) Hz. This is the shape in figure 13.22, with \(T/k = 0{,}233\) m.
  3. Stiff spring, \(k = 3000\) N/m: \(\kappa = 27{,}857\), \(s = 3{,}0331\), \(6{,}0687\), \(9{,}1088\) and \(f = 198{,}47\), \(397{,}10\), \(596{,}02\) Hz.
  4. The limits: the fixed end gives \(205{,}57\), \(411{,}13\), \(616{,}70\) Hz, the free one \(102{,}78\), \(308{,}35\), \(513{,}92\) Hz. All frequencies of steps 1 to 3 lie between them.
  5. The ratio \(f_2/f_1\) is \(2{,}444\), \(2{,}146\) and \(2{,}0008\): the overtones are no longer whole multiples of the fundamental, except in the limit of a stiff spring.
  6. With the stiff spring the first frequency is lower by \(1 - s_1/\pi = 3{,}45\) %. The bound of item 3 is \(1/(1 + \kappa) = 3{,}47\) %, the rough estimate \(T/(kL) = 3{,}59\) %. The string lengthened by \(T/k = 23{,}3\) mm would have \(198{,}44\) Hz, only \(0{,}03\) Hz below the true value.

Example 13.20 Shaft with a torsional spring made of two springs: the stiffness for a frequency \(30\) % lower (gred s torzijsko vzmetjo iz dveh vzmeti: togost za \(30\) % nižjo frekvenco)

The steel shaft of example 13.29, \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), \(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, is fixed at \(x = 0\). At the end \(x = L\) it carries a rigid arm across of length \(2a = 0{,}30\) m, whose tips are held by two equal springs \(k\). What must \(k\) be for the first frequency to be \(30\) % lower than for the shaft fixed at both ends?

  1. The torsional spring. In the view along the axis the cross-section of the shaft is at the middle of the arm. The arm goes from the horizontal position to the turned one when the end of the shaft twists by \(\varphi\). A tip of the arm at the distance \(a\) from the axis moves by \(a\varphi\), the right one up and the left one down. Each spring is therefore stretched or compressed by \(a\varphi\) and acts on the arm with the force \(k\,a\varphi\) against the displacement. Each force has the lever arm \(a\), both moments turn back, together \(-2ka^2\varphi\). The springs are thus a torsional spring of stiffness \(k_\varphi = 2ka^2\).
  2. The assumptions of this step. The twist is small: the displacement of a tip is \(a\sin\varphi\), and at \(\varphi = 0{,}01\) it differs from \(a\varphi\) by \(1{,}7 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of its value. The arm is rigid and massless, which we check in step 7. The springs are unloaded in the rest position.
  3. The boundary condition. The arm has no mass, so the moments on it add up to zero. The shaft acts on it with the moment \(-G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\), as on the disk in equation (13.25), the springs with \(-k_\varphi\,\varphi(L, t)\):
    \[G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) + k_\varphi\,\varphi(L, t) = 0 .\]
    This is condition (13.16) with \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) in place of \(T\) and \(k_\varphi\) in place of \(k\), the equation of motion is the wave equation with \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\) in place of \(c\), and \(\varphi(0, t) = 0\). Theorem 13.18 therefore holds with \(\kappa = k_\varphi L/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and \(\omega = s\,c_{\mathrm{T}}/L\).
  4. Data: \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi d^4/32 = 2{,}5133 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\), \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L = 18\,946\) N m/rad, \(c_{\mathrm{T}} = 3108{,}8\) m/s. The shaft fixed at both ends has \(s = \pi\) and \(f_1 = c_{\mathrm{T}}/(2L) = 1554{,}4\) Hz, with a free end \(s = \pi/2\) and \(777{,}2\) Hz. A spring can lower the frequency by at most \(50\) %, so \(30\) % can be reached.
  5. The requirement is \(s_1 = 0{,}7\,\pi = 2{,}1991\), in the interval \((\pi/2, \pi)\) of item 2 of the theorem, and \(f_1 = 0{,}7 \cdot 1554{,}4 = 1088{,}1\) Hz. This time we solve the frequency equation (13.17) for \(\kappa\):
    \[\kappa = -\frac{s_1}{\tan s_1} = -\frac{2{,}1991}{\tan 2{,}1991} = 1{,}5978 .\]
  6. The stiffness: \(k_\varphi = \kappa\,G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L = 30\,271\) N m/rad and \(k = k_\varphi/(2a^2) = 30\,271/(2 \cdot 0{,}15^2) = 6{,}727 \cdot 10^5\) N/m, that is \(672{,}7\) N/mm for each spring. Check: at this \(\kappa\) the roots are \(2{,}1991\), \(5{,}0205\), \(8{,}0499\) and the frequencies \(1088{,}1\), \(2484{,}1\), \(3983{,}0\) Hz (NumPy). The bound (13.18) gives \(\pi\kappa/(1 + \kappa) = 1{,}932 < 2{,}199 < \pi\).
  7. What does the massless arm cost? With the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the arm the condition in a mode is \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} + (k_\varphi - J_{\mathrm{r}}\omega^2)\,\varphi = 0\), as for the ring with mass. A steel arm with the cross-section \(10 \times 20\) mm has the mass \(0{,}468\) kg and \(J_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}468 \cdot 0{,}30^2/12 = 3{,}51 \cdot 10^{-3}\) kg m\(^2\). At the target frequency \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\omega^2 = 1{,}64 \cdot 10^5\) N m/rad, \(5{,}4\) times \(k_\varphi\). The assumption fails here: with the springs of step 6 the first frequency would be \(543{,}3\) Hz, not \(1088{,}1\) Hz. The target needs \(k_\varphi = 30\,271 + 164\,056 = 194\,328\) N m/rad, so \(k = 4{,}318 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(6{,}4\) times more (NumPy). The computation with a massless arm holds when \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\omega^2\) is small against \(k_\varphi\), for example for a light arm made of a tube.

In the lectures the frequency equation of the string with a spring is written as \(\dfrac{T\omega}{kc} + \tan\dfrac{\omega L}{c} = 0\), the second form of equation (13.17), with the tension \(P\) or \(N\) and the displacement \(y(x, t)\).
