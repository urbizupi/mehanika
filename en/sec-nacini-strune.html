Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.2 Modes of the string and the sine series

A steel guitar string of diameter \(0{,}4\) mm and length \(L = 0{,}65\) m is tightened with a force \(T = 70\) N and plucked. We hear a note and above it the sound of higher notes. Which frequencies does the string have at all, and why are they in the ratio \(1 : 2 : 3\)? How do we compute, from the shape in which we release the string, how much of each note is in the sound?

Figure 13.3 shows what vibrates. The string lies along the axis \(x\) between two ends that are fixed and so at rest. The force \(T\) tightens it, pulling at both ends. The distance \(L\) is the length of the string, and the distance \(x\) picks the point we watch. At time \(t\) the string has the blue shape, and the point \(x\) is displaced by \(w(x, t)\). At the start we release it from the dashed shape \(w_0(x)\) and with the velocity \(v_0(x)\).

The model is the string of section 13.1 (definition 13.2): the displacement \(w(x, t)\) of the point \(x \in [0, L]\) at time \(t\) is transverse, the tension \(T\) is constant, the slope is small and the bending stiffness is zero. We write \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \partial^2 w/\partial t^2\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2 w/\partial x^2\). The motion obeys the wave equation (13.2),

\[w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad c = \sqrt{T/\mu} ,\]

with the wave speed \(c\) (definition 13.4) and the mass per length \(\mu\). For our string \(\mu = \rho\,\pi d^2/4 = 9{,}802 \cdot 10^{-4}\) kg/m with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ and \(c = 267{,}24\) m/s. We add that the ends are at rest and that the initial shape \(w_0\) and the initial velocity \(v_0\) are known:

\[w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0 , \tag{13.4}\]
\[w(x, 0) = w_0(x) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0) = v_0(x) \quad (0 \le x \le L) . \tag{13.5}\]

The model has no damping: a real string loses energy while it vibrates, which we treat later (chapter 20).

For systems with finitely many degrees of freedom we found the modes (definition 8.124), special motions in which all coordinates vibrate with the same frequency and in a fixed ratio, \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\). Let us try the same for the string: every point vibrates with the time dependence \(\cos\omega t\), and the shape along the string is carried by a function \(\varphi(x)\).

Definition 13.6 Mode of a continuous system (način nihanja zveznega sistema)

A continuous system is a string, a bar, a shaft or a beam of length \(L\) that has an equation of motion and boundary conditions at both ends. Let \(w(x, t)\) be its displacement: the deflection of the string, the axial displacement of the bar, the twist of the shaft or the deflection of the beam. For the string these are equation (13.2) and boundary conditions (13.4), which we use here. For the bar and the shaft section 13.3 writes them down, for the beam sections 13.5 and 13.6. Let \(\omega > 0\) be a number and \(\varphi : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) a function that is not zero everywhere and as many times continuously differentiable as the order of the equation of motion requires (twice for the string). The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode (način nihanja) if the function

\[w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\]

solves the equation of motion of the system and satisfies its boundary conditions (for the string equation (13.2) and conditions (13.4)). The number \(\omega\) is the natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) and \(\varphi\) the mode shape (oblika načina). A point \(x\) where \(\varphi(x) = 0\) is a node (vozel) of the mode.

Source: The notion follows GR section 2.2 (PDF p. 85-87) for finitely many degrees of freedom, see the mode in definition 8.124. KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 571-573) calls the same notion "eigenfunction" and "normal mode" and obtains it by separating the variables \(w = F(x)G(t)\). The form of the definition with \(\cos\omega t\) is not from the source.

Does a mode \((\omega, \varphi)\) exist, and how many are there? When we insert \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) into the equation, time cancels and an ordinary differential equation for \(\varphi\) remains, with conditions at the ends. We find its solutions without guessing, with an energy argument for uniqueness. The solution will be a sine, so we first look at its zeros (figure 13.4).

Figure 13.4 shows \(\sin\theta\) for the angle \(\theta = kx\). On the axis we measure \(\theta\). The sine is positive on \((0, \pi)\) and on \((2\pi, 3\pi)\) and negative on \((\pi, 2\pi)\). The zeros are at \(0\), \(\pi\), \(2\pi\) and \(3\pi\). The negative arc is the mirror image of the positive one: the values at \(\theta\) and at \(2\pi - \theta\) are opposite. On a string of length \(L\) the angle at the right end is \(kL\), in our figure \(3\pi\).

Theorem 13.7 Modes of the string (načini nihanja strune)

Let \(L > 0\) and \(c > 0\).

  1. A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the string with ends at rest if and only if, for some natural number \(n\),
    \[\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{n\pi c}{L} , \qquad \varphi(x) = C\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \tag{13.6}\]
    with a number \(C \ne 0\).
  2. For every \(n\) and any numbers \(A\), \(B\) the function \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t) = \sin(n\pi x/L)\,\big(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\big)\) is a solution of equation (13.2) that satisfies the boundary conditions (13.4).
  3. Mode \(n\) has \(n - 1\) nodes inside the string, at the points \(x = jL/n\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\).
Proof

We must show three points. For the first we insert \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) into the equation, obtain an equation for \(\varphi\) and solve it with the energy \(\psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\), which is conserved. The second is a check by substitution, the third a count of the zeros of the sine.

  1. Let \((\omega, \varphi)\) be a mode and \(w = \varphi\cos\omega t\). Then \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2\varphi\cos\omega t\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varphi''\cos\omega t\) (theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine, 8.12). At \(t = 0\) we have \(\cos 0 = 1\), so equation (13.2) gives \(-\omega^2\varphi(x) = c^2\varphi''(x)\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\). With \(k = \omega/c > 0\),
    \[\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi . \tag{13.7}\]
  2. The boundary conditions at \(t = 0\) give \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\).
  3. Let \(\sigma(x) = \dfrac{\varphi'(0)}{k}\sin kx\) and \(\psi = \varphi - \sigma\). The function \(\sigma\) solves equation (13.7) (theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine, 8.12, \(\sigma'' = -k^2\sigma\)), so \(\psi'' = -k^2\psi\), and \(\psi(0) = \varphi(0) - 0 = 0\), \(\psi'(0) = \varphi'(0) - \varphi'(0) = 0\).
  4. Let \(E = \psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\). By the product rule (lemma 1.81), \(E' = 2\psi'\psi'' + 2k^2\psi\psi' = 2\psi'(\psi'' + k^2\psi) = 0\). By the corollary on a function with zero derivative (1.85), \(E\) is constant, \(E(x) = E(0) = 0\). A sum of two squares is zero only if each is zero, so \(\psi = 0\) and \(\varphi = \sigma\).
  5. If \(\varphi'(0) = 0\) then \(\varphi = 0\), which the definition excludes. Hence \(\varphi'(0) \ne 0\). The condition \(\varphi(L) = 0\) is then \(\sin kL = 0\). The sine is zero at \(0\) and \(\pi\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.10, item 2), positive on \((0, \pi)\) (item 3) and, by oddness and periodicity (item 4), negative on \((\pi, 2\pi)\), since there \(\sin\theta = -\sin(2\pi - \theta)\). So the zeros of the sine are exactly the multiples of \(\pi\), and \(kL = n\pi\) with a natural number \(n\), because \(kL > 0\). Hence \(\omega = kc = n\pi c/L\) and \(\varphi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with \(C = \varphi'(0)/k \ne 0\). This is the direction "only if" of item 1.
  6. Conversely, for \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\varphi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) we have \(\varphi'' = -(n\pi/L)^2\varphi\) and \(c^2(n\pi/L)^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). So \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(n\pi/L)^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 w\) for \(w = \varphi\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), and \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} = w_{\mathrm{tt}}\). The same computation holds for \(\varphi\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), so by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) of item 2 is a solution too. At \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) we have \(\sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.10, item 2, and periodicity), so the boundary conditions hold. This is item 2 and the direction "if" of item 1.
  7. A point \(x \in (0, L)\) is a node of mode \(n\) when \(\sin(n\pi x/L) = 0\), that is \(n\pi x/L = j\pi\) for an integer \(j\) by step 5. The condition \(0 < x < L\) gives \(0 < j < n\), so \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\) and \(x = jL/n\).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 572-573) separates the variables and shows that the separation constant must be negative, then obtains \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = cn\pi/L\) and the functions \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\). The number of nodes \(n - 1\) is there too (PDF p. 573, figure 287). The proof with the energy \(\psi'^2 + k^2\psi^2\) is not from the source.

Item 2 also says how the points vibrate in mode \(n\): each with the time dependence \(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), which is a vibration with the natural frequency \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(2\pi) = nc/(2L)\) (definition 9.4). Figure 13.5 shows the first three shapes. The nodes are the points that stay at rest, and in mode \(n\) the stretches between them have equal length \(L/n\). Why is the ratio of the frequencies a whole number? Because the shape \(\varphi\) of equation (13.7) must be zero at both ends: \(\sin kx\) with \(k = \omega/c\) has zeros at multiples of the distance \(\pi/k\), and the end \(L\) must be one of them, so \(kL = n\pi\), and the frequency \(\omega = kc\) is a multiple of the fundamental one.

Each row of figure 13.5 is one mode. The first mode is one arc, the second two and the third three. The ends are at rest in every mode. The dashed shape is the same string half a period later. In the second and third mode the nodes are points that stay at rest. To the right of each row is the natural frequency of the mode, and the distance \(L\) below them is the length of the string.

Example 13.8 Frequencies of the guitar string (frekvence kitarske strune)

For the string from the start of the section, \(c = 267{,}24\) m/s and \(L = 0{,}65\) m, equation (13.6) gives the circular frequency of the first mode \(\omega_1 = \pi c/L = 1291{,}6\) 1/s and the frequencies

\[\begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{n}} &= \frac{n c}{2L} = n \cdot 205{,}57\ \text{Hz} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3) , \\ f_1 &= 205{,}57 , \ f_2 = 411{,}13 , \ f_3 = 616{,}70\ \text{Hz} . \end{aligned}\]

The second mode has one node in the middle, \(x = 0{,}325\) m, the third has two, at \(0{,}2167\) and \(0{,}4333\) m (SymPy and NumPy). The frequency grows with the square root of the tension: at \(T = 77\) N, which is \(10\) % more, \(f_1 = 205{,}57\sqrt{77/70} = 215{,}60\) Hz, so \(4{,}88\) % more.

The last number corrects an error in a source. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 574) writes that the frequency is proportional to the tension, which is not true. Since \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac{n}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}\), it is proportional to the square root of the tension, which the number also shows: a tension \(1{,}1\) times larger gives a frequency only \(\sqrt{1{,}1} = 1{,}0488\) times larger (figure 13.6). The same source adds that the first mode is called the fundamental and the others overtones (višji toni), and that the frequency \(2f_1\) musically corresponds to the octave and \(3f_1\) to the octave plus a fifth (PDF p. 573).

On the axes are the tension \(T\) and the frequency \(f_1\). The blue curve is \(f_1 = 205{,}57\sqrt{T/70}\) and bends over as it grows, the orange dashed line would be proportionality to \(T\). Both pass through the point at \(T = 70\) N. At \(T = 77\) N the curve is at \(215{,}60\) Hz, the line would give \(226{,}12\) Hz.

The definition of a mode requires two things at once: the function \(\varphi\cos\omega t\) solves the equation and satisfies the boundary conditions. So a pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is not a mode if either one fails. Let us look at both possibilities on the string of example 13.8, with \(\omega_1 = \pi c/L = 1291{,}6\) 1/s and \(\varepsilon = 1\) mm.

  1. Right shape, wrong frequency. Let \(\varphi = \varepsilon\sin(\pi x/L)\) and \(\omega = 1{,}1\,\omega_1 = 1420{,}8\) 1/s. The boundary conditions hold, \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\). The equation, however, requires \(-\omega^2\varphi = c^2\varphi''\) (step 1 of the proof of theorem 13.7). Here \(c^2\varphi'' = -\omega_1^2\varphi\), so the difference is \(-\omega^2\varphi + \omega_1^2\varphi = -0{,}21\,\omega_1^2\varphi\). At \(x = L/2\) this is \(-0{,}21\cdot 1291{,}6^2\cdot 10^{-3} = -350{,}3\) m/s\(^2\), not zero. The equation does not hold, so this is not a mode.
  2. The frequency is right for the equation, the shape is wrong for the condition. Let \(\varphi = \varepsilon\sin(1{,}5\pi x/L)\) and \(\omega = 1{,}5\,\omega_1 = 1937{,}4\) 1/s. With \(k = 1{,}5\pi/L\) we have \(\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi\) and \(c^2 k^2 = \omega^2\), so the equation holds. The condition at \(x = 0\) holds too, but at \(x = L\) we have \(\varphi(L) = \varepsilon\sin(1{,}5\pi) = -1\) mm, not zero. The end of the string would move, and it is fixed (figure 13.7). So the frequency \(1{,}5\cdot 205{,}57 = 308{,}35\) Hz is not a frequency of the string, although it lies between the first and the second.

Figure 13.7 puts the wrong shape next to the right ones. The first three modes of the string are drawn in blue: mode \(n = 1\) has no node, mode \(n = 2\) has one, mode \(n = 3\) has two, and each returns to the axis at both ends. Below is the shape \(\sin(1{,}5\pi x/L)\), with the same exaggerated height, which also solves the equation. At the right end, however, it does not reach the axis but ends at the value \(-1\) below it, at the distance \(L\) from the left end: the end of the string would move, and it is fixed.

The equation alone has the solution \(\sin kx\) for every \(k\), hence at every frequency. Only the boundary condition at \(x = L\) picks \(\sin kL = 0\) (step 5 of the proof), and from the continuous set of frequencies only \(n\cdot 205{,}57\) Hz remain.

A string released from the shape \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) vibrates in one mode only. A plucked string has an initial shape that is not a sine. The wave equation is linear, so a sum of solutions is a solution again, and we try to build the initial shape and velocity from sines. There are three questions. Does such a series converge, and is its sum really a solution that meets the initial conditions (13.5)? Which \(w_0\) can be expanded this way? How much of each mode is in a given state? The first two questions are settled by the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11), the third by the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12).

To expand in sines on \([0, L]\) we need a function on the whole axis, since the Fourier series of section 9.11.4 (definition 9.88) describes periodic functions. We extend a function \(h\) on \([0, L]\) with zeros at the ends so that it becomes odd and periodic with period \(2L\). Oddness brings only sines, because cosines are even. Figure 13.8 shows this for two functions.

The top row shows a function with zeros at the ends. On the axis are the points \(x\), and the dashed lines are at \(-L\), \(0\), \(L\) and \(2L\). The blue triangular \(h\) lies between \(0\) and \(L\) and is zero at both joints. The orange one is its mirror image, turned downward, on \([-L, 0]\) and repeats on \([L, 2L]\): this is an odd function with period \(2L\), which we call \(h^*\). The bottom row has on its axis the function \(h = x/L\), which equals \(1\) at \(L\): there the extension breaks off with a jump of size \(2\).

Definition 13.9 Odd periodic extension and sine coefficients (lihi periodični podaljšek in sinusni koeficienti)

Let \(h : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\). Its odd periodic extension (lihi periodični podaljšek) is the function \(h^* : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) that equals \(h(x)\) for \(0 \le x \le L\), equals \(-h(-x)\) for \(-L \le x < 0\) and is \(2L\)-periodic, \(h^*(x + 2L) = h^*(x)\). The sine coefficients (sinusni koeficienti) of \(h\) are the numbers

\[h_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L h(x)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x \qquad (n = 1, 2, \dots) . \tag{13.8}\]

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576): the coefficients \(B_{\mathrm{n}}\) of equation (14) and the odd periodic extension with period \(2L\) (figure 289). The notations \(h^*\) and \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\) are ours.

The function \(h^*\) is zero at both ends \(0\) and \(L\), so it is continuous at the joints of the extension too. Does its Fourier series really converge to \(h\), and what are its coefficients?

Lemma 13.10 Sine series of a function on an interval (sinusna vrsta funkcije na odseku)

Let \(h\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) (definition 6.11) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\). Then \(h^*\) is continuous, \(2L\)-periodic and piecewise continuously differentiable, its Fourier coefficients (definition 9.88 with \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2L\), \(\Omega = \pi/L\)) are \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \ge 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = h_{\mathrm{n}}\), and

\[\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}|h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \infty , \qquad \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \to h^*(x) \quad (N \to \infty) \tag{13.9}\]

uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\), so on \([0, L]\) to \(h\).

Proof

We must show the continuity of the extension, then the coefficients by oddness, and finally apply the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.95).

  1. On \([0, L]\) the function \(h^*\) equals \(h\), on \([-L, 0)\) it equals \(x \mapsto -h(-x)\), which is piecewise continuously differentiable because \(h\) is and reflecting the argument is linear. At the point \(0\) the left limit is \(-h(0) = 0\) and \(h^*(0) = h(0) = 0\). At the point \(L\) we have \(h^*(L) = h(L) = 0\), and to the right of it \(h^*(L + s) = h^*(-L + s) = -h(L - s) \to -h(L) = 0\) as \(s \to 0^+\). So \(h^*\) is continuous at all joints, and by periodicity everywhere.
  2. By the lemma on shift, reflection and period in the integral (9.86, item 3), the integral of a \(2L\)-periodic function over \([0, 2L]\) equals the integral over \([-L, L]\). For \(g(x) = h^*(x)\cos(n\pi x/L)\) we have \(g(-s) = -h(s)\cos(n\pi s/L)\) for \(s \in [0, L]\), so by reflection (lemma on shift, reflection and period in the integral, 9.86, item 2) \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}}^{0}g\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L h(s)\cos(n\pi s/L)\,\mathrm{d}s\), which cancels \(\int_0^L g\,\mathrm{d}x\). So \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \ge 0\).
  3. For \(g(x) = h^*(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with the odd extension we have \(g(-s) = (-h(s))(-\sin(n\pi s/L)) = g(s)\), so the two parts are equal and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}g\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{L}\cdot 2\int_0^L h\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = h_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  4. The function \(h^*\) is continuous, \(2L\)-periodic and piecewise continuously differentiable, so by the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.95) \(\sum(|a_{\mathrm{n}}| + |b_{\mathrm{n}}|) = \sum|h_{\mathrm{n}}| < \infty\) and \(S_{\mathrm{N}} h^* \to h^*\) uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since all \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(S_{\mathrm{N}} h^*(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576) expands \(f\) in a sine series with the same coefficients and uses the odd extension for convergence, but does not prove uniform convergence. The proof with the theorem on the uniform convergence of the Fourier series (9.95) is not from the source.

The condition \(h(L) = 0\) in the lemma on the sine series is not a formality. Let \(h(x) = x/L\), which is smooth and has \(h(0) = 0\) but \(h(L) = 1\). The lemma does not apply to it, and the series indeed does not converge uniformly.

  1. The extension. To the left of \(L\) we have \(h^*(x) = x/L \to 1\), to the right \(h^*(L + s) = -h(L - s) \to -1\) as \(s \to 0^+\) (step 1 of the proof of the lemma). The extension has a jump of \(2\) at \(L\), it is not continuous, step 1 fails and theorem 9.95 cannot be used.
  2. The coefficients. Steps 2 and 3 of the proof use only oddness, so they still hold: \(h_{\mathrm{n}} = 2(-1)^{n+1}/(n\pi)\), that is \(0{,}6366\), \(-0{,}3183\), \(0{,}2122\), \(-0{,}1592\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 4\) (SymPy). They decay like \(1/n\), not like \(1/n^2\) as for the triangular shape of a plucked string, computed in example 13.14 below.
  3. The sum \(\sum|h_{\mathrm{n}}| = (2/\pi)\sum 1/n\) is not finite. The group of terms \(1/n\) from \(n = 2^k + 1\) to \(n = 2^{k+1}\) has \(2^k\) terms, each at least \(2^{-(k+1)}\), so the group gives at least \(2^k\cdot 2^{-(k+1)} = 1/2\). There are infinitely many groups, so the sum exceeds every bound. The partial sums are \(3{,}74\) at \(N = 200\) and \(5{,}21\) at \(N = 2000\).
  4. The partial sums \(S_{\mathrm{N}}(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}} h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) are all zero at \(x = L\), since \(\sin n\pi = 0\), not \(h(L) = 1\). Since \(S_{\mathrm{N}}\) is continuous, near \(L\) the difference \(|S_{\mathrm{N}} - h|\) is close to \(1\) for every \(N\), so the largest deviation does not tend to zero: the convergence is not uniform. Just below \(L\) the partial sum overshoots \(h\): the maximum is \(1{,}174\) at \(N = 200\) (at \(x = 0{,}995\,L\)) and \(1{,}178\) at \(N = 2000\) (at \(x = 0{,}9995\,L\)). The overshoot stays near \(9\) % of the jump \(2\), as in the Gibbs phenomenon (section 9.11.7, figure 13.10).

The top row shows the coefficients \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\) of \(h = x/L\): the bars are the values for \(n = 1, \dots, 8\), the axis is zero and the numbers are \(n\). The sign alternates and the size decays like \(1/n\). The middle row has on its axis the sine coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) of the triangular shape of the plucked string from example 13.14 below, which decay faster. In the bottom row are, on the axis, the terms \(1/n\): the blocks gather the terms from \(2^k + 1\) to \(2^{k+1}\): each block has the height of its smallest term and area \(1/2\), and the bars in it together have area at least \(1/2\).

Figure 13.10 magnifies the neighbourhood of the end. On the axes, \(h\) is shown from \(0{,}8\,L\) on. The line \(h = x/L\) is what the sum should follow, and the vertical line is the end \(x = L\). The blue sum \(S_5\) is smooth and falls towards zero. The orange \(S_{20}\) and the blackwhite \(S_{100}\) follow the line better on the left, but just below \(L\) both jump over it to a peak near \(1{,}18\), the blackwhite one with a narrower peak of the same height. At the end every sum passes through the open circle at \(0\), while the value of the function is the filled dot at \(1\).

The triangular shape of a plucked string in example 13.14 below is zero at both ends, its extension is continuous and its coefficients decay like \(1/n^2\). So the condition \(h(L) = 0\) selects a continuous joint of the extension, and without it the joint is a jump.

The theorem requires the smoothness of the extension, not only of the function itself, because the sum must be twice differentiable to solve the wave equation. If the extension \(w_0^*\) is twice continuously differentiable, then \(w_0''(0) = w_0''(L) = 0\). The reason is oddness. We differentiate \(w_0^*(-x) = -w_0^*(x)\) twice by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 3) and get \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(-x) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x)\). At \(x = 0\) this is \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(0) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(0)\), so \(w_0''(0) = 0\). At \(L\) likewise: by the period and the oddness, \(w_0^*(L + s) = w_0^*(s - L) = -w_0^*(L - s)\) for every \(s\), and the second derivative with respect to \(s\) at \(s = 0\) gives \(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(L) = -w_0^{*\prime\prime}(L)\). The condition is not merely technical, though: the equation itself requires it. At the end \(w(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\), so \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}(0, t) = 0\) and by equation (13.2) also \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0\), so at \(t = 0\) also \(w_0''(0) = 0\), and likewise at \(L\). So every solution with continuous \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) has zero curvature at a fixed end. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 576) states the same conditions as sufficient for a twice differentiable extension.

That the condition \(w_0''(0) = w_0''(L) = 0\) is not a formality shows a smooth shape that violates it. Take the parabola \(w_0 = 4h\,x(L - x)/L^2\) with \(h = 1\) mm and \(v_0 = 0\). It is zero at both ends and smooth between them, but \(w_0'' = -8h/L^2 = -0{,}0189\) 1/m, so \(w_0''(0) \ne 0\). Figure 13.11 shows the parabola, its extension and the second derivative.

At the top, on the axis, is the parabola \(w_0\), zero at the ends and bulging upward: its curvature is negative. The extension \(w_0^*\) below the axis is the mirror image and is curved upward: its curvature is positive. At the joint at \(0\) the curvature jumps. At the bottom the axes are the time \(t\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the point \(0{,}1\) m. The course is \(-0{,}0189\) 1/m until \(0{,}374\) ms, then zero, the jump is \(0{,}0189\) 1/m, and the times are \(0{,}374\) and \(2{,}06\) ms.

  1. The extension for \(-L \le x < 0\) is \(w_0^*(x) = -w_0(-x) = 4h\,x(L + x)/L^2\), which has the second derivative \(+8h/L^2\). So to the left of \(0\) we have \(w_0^{*\prime\prime} = +0{,}0189\) 1/m, to the right \(-0{,}0189\) 1/m. The function \(w_0^*\) is not twice continuously differentiable, the second derivative jumps by \(16h/L^2 = 0{,}0379\) 1/m, and likewise at \(L\).
  2. By formula (13.11) with \(v_0 = 0\), where the second derivatives exist, \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big)\) (step 4 of the proof of theorem 13.11). Take \(x = 0{,}1\) m. As long as \(x - ct > 0\), that is until \(t = x/c = 0{,}374\) ms, both arguments are in \((0, L)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}0189\) 1/m. From then until \(t = (L - x)/c = 2{,}06\) ms we have \(x - ct < 0\) and \(x + ct < L\), so \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12(+0{,}0189 - 0{,}0189) = 0\).
  3. The second derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) jumps by \(0{,}0189\) 1/m across the line \(x = ct\), and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by \(c^2\cdot 0{,}0189 = 1352\) m/s\(^2\). Definition 13.2 requires continuous \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), so this motion is not a string in its sense, although the initial shape is smooth.
  4. The reason is at the end. Since \(w(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}(0, t) = 0\) and by the equation also \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0\). The parabola has \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, 0) = -0{,}0189\) 1/m. The curvature at time zero and later do not agree at the end, and the jump runs along the string as a wave.

So the condition on the second derivative rejects a shape that is zero at the ends but has curvature there. Formula (13.11) still gives a generalized solution for the parabola, as for the triangular shape of a plucked string (example 13.14 below), but not a solution with continuous second derivatives.

One more idea leads to the same form of the solution without series. A wave that travels along the string with unchanged shape is \(w = F(x - ct)\). By the lemma on the travelling wave (13.5), it solves the equation \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for every twice continuously differentiable function \(F\). The same holds for \(F(x + ct)\), which travels in the opposite direction. The sum of two such waves, whose shapes we adapt to the initial conditions, is d'Alembert's solution. The theorem says that this solution and the series of modes are one and the same function.

Theorem 13.11 Expansion of the motion of the string in modes and d'Alembert's form (razvoj gibanja strune po načinih in d'Alembertova oblika)

Let \(L > 0\) and \(c > 0\). Let \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) be functions on \([0, L]\) with zeros at the ends, \(w_0(0) = w_0(L) = 0\) and \(v_0(0) = v_0(L) = 0\), and let the odd periodic extension \(w_0^*\) be twice continuously differentiable and \(v_0^*\) continuously differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}\) (definition 13.9). Let \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the sine coefficients of \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi c/L\). Then for every \(x\) and \(t\) the series

\[w(x, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big(p_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + \frac{q_{\mathrm{n}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\Big) \tag{13.10}\]

converges, its sum is

\[w(x, t) = \frac12\big(w_0^*(x - ct) + w_0^*(x + ct)\big) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{13.11}\]

the derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) exist and are continuous on \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the function \(w\) solves equation (13.2), satisfies the boundary conditions (13.4) and the initial conditions (13.5). Term \(n\) of the series is the solution of item 2 of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7), so the solution \(w\) is a sum of modes.

Proof

We must show that the partial sums of the series equal the partial sums of the sine series of \(w_0^*\) and \(v_0^*\) at the arguments \(x \mp ct\), that these converge (lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval, 13.10), and that the resulting function is smooth and solves the equation.

  1. The functions \(w_0\) and \(v_0\) are piecewise continuously differentiable with one partition (the extensions are continuously differentiable), so by the lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10), for every \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) we have \(\sum p_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi y/L) = w_0^*(y)\) and \(\sum q_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi y/L) = v_0^*(y)\), with uniform convergence on \(\mathbb{R}\). Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{N}}\) be the largest deviations of the partial sums from \(w_0^*\) and \(v_0^*\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}}, \eta_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\).

  2. The partial sum \(W_{\mathrm{N}}\) of the series (13.10). Because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = n\pi ct/L\), the addition theorems give (right and left wave) \(\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = \tfrac12\big(\sin\frac{n\pi(x - ct)}{L} + \sin\frac{n\pi(x + ct)}{L}\big)\). By the theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine (8.12) and the fundamental theorem of calculus (3.40, item 1), \(\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}\sin\frac{n\pi s}{L}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{L}{n\pi}\big(\cos\frac{n\pi(x - ct)}{L} - \cos\frac{n\pi(x + ct)}{L}\big) = \frac{2L}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). Since \(\frac{1}{2c}\cdot\frac{2L}{n\pi} = \frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\),

    \[W_{\mathrm{N}}(x, t) = \frac12\big(S_{\mathrm{N}} w_0^*(x - ct) + S_{\mathrm{N}} w_0^*(x + ct)\big) + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}S_{\mathrm{N}} v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s ,\]

    where \(S_{\mathrm{N}}\) is the partial sum of the sine series.

  3. As \(N \to \infty\) the first two terms tend to \(\frac12(w_0^*(x - ct) + w_0^*(x + ct))\) by step 1. For the integral, by the lemma on the estimate of the integral (9.31), \(\big|\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}(v_0^* - S_{\mathrm{N}} v_0^*)\,\mathrm{d}s\big| \le 2c|t|\,\eta_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\). So \(W_{\mathrm{N}}(x, t)\) converges to the right-hand side of equation (13.11). This is the first and second claim.

  4. Let \(G(y) = \int_0^y v_0^*(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \(v_0^*\) is continuously differentiable, by the fundamental theorem of calculus (3.40, item 2) \(G' = v_0^*\) and \(G'' = v_0^{*\prime}\) is continuous, and \(\int_{\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{ct}}^{x+ct}v_0^*\,\mathrm{d}s = G(x + ct) - G(x - ct)\).

    By the chain rule for a function of one variable (lemma 8.29, item 3), applied to the continuously differentiable functions \(w_0^*\), \(w_0^{*\prime}\), \(G\) and \(G'\),

\[\begin{aligned} w_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \tfrac12\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{1}{2c}\big(v_0^{*\prime}(x + ct) - v_0^{*\prime}(x - ct)\big) , \\ w_{\mathrm{tt}} &= \tfrac{c^2}{2}\big(w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{c}{2}\big(v_0^{*\prime}(x + ct) - v_0^{*\prime}(x - ct)\big) . \end{aligned}\]

Both are continuous, and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). 5. Every term of the series (13.10) is zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), because \(\sin 0 = \sin n\pi = 0\) (lemma on the basic properties of sine and cosine, 8.10, item 2). So \(W_{\mathrm{N}}(0, t) = W_{\mathrm{N}}(L, t) = 0\) and in the limit \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\). 6. At \(t = 0\) we have \(w(x, 0) = \tfrac12\big(w_0^*(x) + w_0^*(x)\big) + 0 = w_0(x)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The derivative in \(t\) from step 4 is \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = \tfrac{c}{2}\big(-w_0^{*\prime}(x - ct) + w_0^{*\prime}(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac12\big(v_0^*(x + ct) + v_0^*(x - ct)\big)\), and at \(t = 0\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0) = 0 + v_0^*(x) = v_0(x)\). 7. Term \(n\) is \(\sin(n\pi x/L)(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\) with \(A = p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(B = q_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), so it has the form of item 2 of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7).

Source: KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574-576) expands the solution in the series (12) with the coefficients (14), (15), sums it for \(v_0 = 0\) to \(\frac12(f^*(x - ct) + f^*(x + ct))\) and requires a twice differentiable \(f\) with zero second derivatives at the ends. D'Alembert's form and the solution with an initial velocity are in section 12.4 (PDF p. 579-581). The proof that the series equals d'Alembert's form by uniform convergence (lemma 13.10) is not from the source. We do not prove uniqueness of the solution.

The theorem says more than that the series is a solution. The term of each mode vibrates with its own frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) are the initial displacement and the initial velocity in mode \(n\), the first in the unit of displacement, the second in the unit of velocity. The shape \(w_0\) is found as the sum of all modes at time zero. D'Alembert's form shows the same motion differently: the string is the sum of two half shapes that move apart with speed \(c\). Figure 13.12 shows this for a plucked string.

Figure 13.12 shows four moments. The top row \(ct = 0\) is the start. The axis of each row is the string at rest, the vertical segments are the ends \(0\) and \(L\). The blue wave moves to the right, the orange one to the left, each of half the height, and their blackwhite sum is the string. The second, third and fourth row show the times \(0{,}1\,L\), \(0{,}2\,L\) and \(0{,}4\,L\), and the distance \(L\) is the length between the ends.

At time \(ct = 0{,}2\,L\) the peak of the orange wave reaches the end \(x = 0\), and at the same time the inverted part of the blue wave, which belongs to the odd extension, enters the string from the left. The sum is zero at the end at all times. This is the reflection at a fixed end: the wave turns over because \(w_0^*\) is odd. After the time \(L/c\) the shape is \(-w_0(L - x)\), inverted and mirrored, after \(2L/c = 4{,}86\) ms it is the same as at the start again, and this is the period of the first mode \(1/f_1\).

The triangular shape, however, does not satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem, because it has a kink at the peak and \(w_0^*\) is not twice differentiable. The lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10) holds for it, since it requires only piecewise continuous differentiability. At the kink, expression (13.11) is a generalized solution (posplošena rešitev) in the sense of KREYSZIG (PDF p. 576): the equation holds everywhere except on the lines where \(x - ct\) or \(x + ct\) equals a kink of the extension, that is \(\pm x_0 + 2kL\) with an integer \(k\), where the second derivatives do not exist. That the series of modes converges to expression (13.11) here too, however, we have already proved. Steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 13.11 use only the uniform convergence of the sine series of the initial shape, which lemma 13.10 gives for a piecewise continuously differentiable \(w_0\) and \(v_0 = 0\). Only step 4, which needs the second derivatives, fails. The numbers in example 13.14 check this.

How much of each mode is in the shape \(w_0\)? The square of the coefficient \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a measure of the share of mode \(n\), if the sum of all squares is known. That this sum is twice the mean square of the displacement, \(\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), is stated by the following lemma, and the factor \(2\) comes from the mean of \(\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\) over \([0, L]\) being \(\tfrac12\). The proof of the lemma starts from the uniform convergence of the sine series (lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval, 13.10), so it does not need the general Parseval equality, which chapter 9 only states (section 9.11.7).

Lemma 13.12 Parseval equality for the sine series (Parsevalova enakost za sinusno vrsto)

Let \(h\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\) and sine coefficients \(h_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then

\[\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L h(x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{13.12}\]
Proof

We must show that the integral of the product of \(h\) with a partial sum of its sine series is expressed by the coefficients, and that it tends to \(\int h^2\) because the partial sums converge uniformly.

  1. Let \(S_{\mathrm{N}}(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\). By linearity of the integral (lemma on the properties of the integral of piecewise continuous functions, 6.10, item 2) and by equation (13.8), \(\int_0^L h\,S_{\mathrm{N}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}\int_0^L h\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).
  2. Let \(M\) be the maximum of the function \(|h|\) on \([0, L]\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}} = \sup|h - S_{\mathrm{N}}|\), which tends to zero by the lemma on the sine series of a function on an interval (13.10). By the lemma on the estimate of the integral (9.31), \(\big|\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L h\,S_{\mathrm{N}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big| = \big|\int_0^L h\,(h - S_{\mathrm{N}})\,\mathrm{d}x\big| \le L\,M\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{N}} \to 0\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(\frac{L}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{n} \le \mathrm{N}}h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) tends to \(\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so the series \(\sum h_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) converges and equation (13.12) holds.

Source: LEBL2 theorem 11.8.13 (PDF p. 204) proves the equality for every \(2\pi\)-periodic Riemann integrable function (for period \(2L\) after a change of variable, which is our addition) with the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, which the book does not prove. The proof for a uniformly convergent sine series in three steps is not from the source.

From the equality it follows that the sines miss nothing: no nonzero shape is orthogonal to all of them.

Corollary 13.13 Completeness of the sines (popolnost sinusov)

Let \(h\) be piecewise continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) with \(h(0) = h(L) = 0\) and \(\int_0^L h(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every \(n \ge 1\). Then \(h = 0\) on \([0, L]\).

Proof

We must show that zero coefficients give a zero integral of the square, and that this gives a zero function.

  1. All \(h_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), so by the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12), \(\int_0^L h^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L}{2}\sum h_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 0\).
  2. The function \(h^2\) is nonnegative and continuous, so by the lemma on the properties of the integral of piecewise continuous functions (6.10, item 3) it is zero on every closed piece, so \(h = 0\) everywhere.

Source: The corollary is ours, from the Parseval equality for the sine series (13.12). KREYSZIG section 12.3 (PDF p. 574) only uses the sine functions.

The corollary says that the coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) determine the shape \(w_0\): two shapes with equal coefficients are equal, since their difference has all coefficients zero and is therefore zero by the corollary. So no modes are missing: no admissible shape of the string escapes the sum of sines. For shapes that are not piecewise continuously differentiable we do not claim this.

Example 13.14 Plucked guitar string (zabrenkana kitarska struna)

We pull the string of example 13.8 at \(x_0 = L/5 = 0{,}13\) m by \(h = 1\) mm and release it without velocity. The initial shape is triangular, \(w_0 = h\,x/x_0\) for \(x \le x_0\) and \(w_0 = h\,(L - x)/(L - x_0)\) for \(x \ge x_0\), \(v_0 = 0\). The slope is at most \(h/x_0 = 0{,}0077\), below the bound \(0{,}01\) of section 13.1, at which the cosine of the slope angle differs from \(1\) by less than \(5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) (here \(3 \cdot 10^{-5}\)). By formula (13.11) the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is half the sum of two slopes of the extension, so at every time it is at most \(0{,}0077\) in absolute value, where it exists.

  1. By equation (13.8) the coefficients are \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{2}{L}\displaystyle\int_0^L w_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{2h L^2\sin(n\pi x_0/L)}{\pi^2 n^2 x_0 (L - x_0)}\) (SymPy). The factor \(1/n^2\) comes from the kink. With the jump of the slope at the kink, \(s = h/x_0 + h/(L - x_0) = hL/(x_0(L - x_0))\), we have \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{2Ls}{\pi^2 n^2}\sin\dfrac{n\pi x_0}{L}\): at the same \(x_0\) every \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) is proportional to the jump \(s\), so the shares of the modes do not depend on \(h\), while the decay stays \(1/n^2\). How strong the higher modes are relative to the first is set by the factor \(\sin(n\pi x_0/L)\), that is by the place where we hold the string.
  2. The factor \(\sin(n\pi x_0/L) = \sin(n\pi/5)\) is zero at \(n = 5, 10, 15, \dots\): the point where we hold the string, \(x_0 = L/5 = jL/n\) with \(j = n/5\), is a node of every mode whose \(n\) is a multiple of \(5\) (step 7 of the proof of the theorem on the modes of the string, 13.7). These modes are not excited.
  3. The numbers: \(p_1 = 0{,}7444\) mm, \(p_2 = 0{,}3011\) mm, \(p_3 = 0{,}1338\) mm, \(p_4 = 0{,}0465\) mm, \(p_5 = 0\), \(p_6 = -0{,}0207\) mm.
  4. The Parseval equality (13.12) gives \(\sum p_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{2h^2}{3} = 0{,}6667\) mm² (integral of the triangle, \(\int_0^L w_0^2\,\mathrm{d}x = h^2L/3\), checked numerically). The share of mode \(n\) in the mean square of the displacement is \(p_{\mathrm{n}}^2/(2h^2/3)\): \(83{,}13\) %, \(13{,}60\) %, \(2{,}69\) %, \(0{,}32\) %, \(0\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 5\). The first three modes together carry \(99{,}42\) %, the first ten \(99{,}96\) %.
  5. A check of the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11) on this shape (which does not meet its hypotheses): the series (13.10) with \(20\,000\) terms gives the displacement \(0{,}2056\) mm at \(x = 0{,}30\) m, \(t = 1\) ms, d'Alembert's formula (13.11) gives the same number, and at \(x = 0{,}50\) m, \(t = 2{,}5\) ms both give \(-0{,}9615\) mm (NumPy).

The slope \(0{,}0077\) is the bound for this height, not for every pluck. Let us pull the same string at the same \(x_0\) by \(h = 2\) mm instead of \(1\) mm and check the bound \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) of section 13.1.

  1. The slope of the first segment is \(h/x_0 = 0{,}002/0{,}13 = 0{,}0154\), above the bound \(0{,}01\).
  2. The cosine of the slope angle differs from \(1\) by \(1 - \cos(\arctan 0{,}0154) = 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4}\), more than twice the bound \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\) (at \(h = 1\) mm it is \(2{,}96\cdot 10^{-5}\)).
  3. The stretch of the string on the steep piece here is \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}0154^2} - 1 = 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4}\), by Hooke's law the force increases by at most \(EA\cdot 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4} = 24{,}63\ \text{kN}\cdot 1{,}18\cdot 10^{-4} = 2{,}91\) N, that is \(4{,}2\) % of \(T\), and the wave speed by at most \(2{,}1\) %. At the bound \(0{,}01\) idealization 3 allowed \(1{,}8\) % and \(0{,}9\) %.

So at \(h = 2\) mm the model is outside its range, although the example differs in one number only. The largest height at which the slope is still within the bound is \(h = 0{,}01\,x_0 = 1{,}3\) mm.

The sound of a plucked guitar is thus almost entirely the fundamental, and its colour is the higher harmonics, which are chosen by the place where we excite the string. At the middle (\(x_0 = L/2\)) all even modes would drop out, because they have a node there. We showed that the solution which the theorem on the expansion of the motion of the string in modes (13.11) builds from the initial shape and velocity is a sum of the modes of the theorem on the modes of the string (13.7), which we proved with a uniformly convergent sine series and d'Alembert's form. We have not proved that it is the only solution. We also showed that the coefficients determine the initial shape (corollary on the completeness of the sines, 13.13) and that half the sum of their squares is the mean square of the displacement (Parseval equality for the sine series, 13.12). The same procedure, the expansion in the proper shapes and the time dependence of each one separately, will be used for the bar, the shaft and the beam. The bar and the shaft obey a wave equation of the same form, so travelling waves remain there too (lemma 13.5). For the beam d'Alembert's form is gone and only the modes and the expansion in them remain.
