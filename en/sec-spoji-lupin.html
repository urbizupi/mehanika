Chapter 22 Shells

22.7 Junctions of shells

The wall of a pressure vessel has two parts that we know from sections 22.5 and 22.6: a cylinder and the hemisphere that closes it. We know each part alone: both by membrane theory, the cylinder also by the bending of the cylinder. What happens on the circle where they meet? Does the membrane state stay there, or does an edge effect appear as at a clamped edge? And if it appears, how large is it?

Take the vessel of section 22.5.1: a steel tube with mid-surface radius \(R = 50\) mm and thickness \(h = 2\) mm, closed by a hemisphere of the same radius and thickness, filled with oil at pressure \(p = 3\) MPa. The steel has \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). An axisymmetric load means that everything repeats the same way around the axis, so the axial section (osni prerez) is enough. We name the idealizations: the joint of the two shells has no notch and no stiff ring, the material is linearly elastic and homogeneous, displacements are small compared with the thickness (the largest displacement, \(16{,}263\) μm, is \(0{,}008\) of the thickness \(2\) mm). The wall is thin in the sense of section 22.3, and the ratio \(h/(2R) = 0{,}02\) bounds the neglected factor \(1 + z/R\).

Figure 22.23 builds the answer in three rows. In each row we draw the upper half of the axial section, so each shell is a single line, its middle surface.

We start with the axis of revolution, dashed in every row. Then we draw the position before loading: the straight line of the cylinder and the quarter circle of the hemisphere, meeting at a point at height \(R\) above the axis. The first row shows the pressure acting on each shell alone. The cylinder widens under it, because the oil presses on the wall from all sides and pushes it outward. The hemisphere widens less. Why? By section 22.5 the cylinder has the hoop force \(N_\theta = pR\) and the hemisphere \(N_\theta = pR/2\), because the sphere is curved in both directions and the two forces carry the pressure equally, while the cylinder is curved only around its circumference and the hoop force alone carries the pressure. When a ring widens by \(w\), its circumference \(2\pi R\) grows by \(2\pi w\), that is by the fraction \(w/R\), so the smaller hoop force means the smaller displacement. By lemma 22.35

\[w_\mathrm{c} = \frac{pR^2}{Eh}\Big(1 - \frac{\nu}{2}\Big) = 16{,}263\ \mu\text{m} , \qquad w_\mathrm{s} = \frac{pR^2}{2Eh}(1 - \nu) = 6{,}696\ \mu\text{m} , \tag{22.48}\]

and the difference, which we write as the gap \(\Delta\), is

\[\Delta = w_\mathrm{c} - w_\mathrm{s} = \frac{pR^2}{Eh}\Big(1 - \frac{\nu}{2} - \frac{1 - \nu}{2}\Big) = \frac{pR^2}{2Eh} = 9{,}566\ \mu\text{m} . \tag{22.49}\]

The two shells have to be joined somehow, there is no gap in the wall. At the junction each shell carries the axial force \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = pR/2\) (by theorem 22.32 on the equilibrium of a shell of revolution, from the equilibrium of the cap above the junction circle, the same on both sides), so the two axial forces balance each other. Radially, however, the ends do not match: the end of the cylinder would widen by \(\Delta\) more than the end of the hemisphere. Since the wall must not tear, the junction has to push the ends together with radial forces. In the middle row we therefore cut the junction and move the shells apart, as with a free body in statics. The end of the cylinder stays higher than the end of the hemisphere. The junction that holds them together pushes the end of the cylinder inward and the end of the hemisphere outward, each toward the position of the other. The two forces \(Q_0\) are equally large, because they are a pair of action and reaction between the shells (Newton's third law), and we measure them in N/mm of length of the junction circle. The two axial forces point from each shell toward the junction, each pulls its own end toward the other shell. We do not know the moment at the junction yet, it will show itself in the calculation.

The last row shows the result we shall compute: the wall is a single line that joins the two parts, and at the junction the displacement is the same for both sides. Grey is again the position before loading. The departure from the membrane displacements is visible only near the junction, far from it the wall behaves as a membrane. Whether this is really so, the calculation will show.

From history

In many problems of thin shells a solution without bending is enough, which assumes stresses uniformly distributed through the thickness, and this was investigated for surfaces of revolution, especially for the sphere and the cylinder (TIMO, PDF p. 350). The general theory of bending of shells was given by Aron and Love, and its first engineering application was Stodola's analysis of stresses in conical shells of constant thickness (TIMO, PDF p. 351). The symmetrically loaded spherical shell of constant thickness was investigated by Reissner. He showed that bending stresses are produced mainly by reactive forces along the fixed edge, and derived equations for calculating these forces (TIMO, PDF p. 351). At our junction this role is played by the force \(Q_0\) and the moment \(M_0\), forced by the difference of the membrane displacements. Meissner and his pupils obtained exact solutions of several practical problems with Reissner's equations. Their usefulness depends on the speed of convergence of the series, and this is poorer for thinner shells (TIMO, PDF p. 351). The source does not give the reason, we see it from our numbers: at \(h = 2\) mm we have \(\beta R = 6{,}43\) and the edge effect dies out within \(\pi/\beta = 24{,}4\) mm, that is \(28{,}0^\circ\) of the meridian, while at \(R/h = 400\) we have \(\beta R = 25{,}7\) and this part is only \(7{,}0^\circ\). A series that has to follow such a narrow layer needs more terms. The difficulty was bypassed by the approximate methods of Blumenthal and Geckeler (TIMO, PDF p. 351). The edge effect, first introduced by Love, was applied by Geckeler to symmetrically loaded spherical shells, and he reduced two coupled differential equations to two independent ones, assuming that for very thin shells the derivatives of the functions are larger than the functions themselves (VK, PDF p. 303).

Let us return to the vessel with the cylinder and the hemisphere and to the gap \(\Delta\) between their membrane displacements. This section does not treat the full equation of an axisymmetric shell, so we take Geckeler's approximation: near its edge the hemisphere is treated as a cylinder.

Definition 22.47 Geckeler's approximation (Geckelerjev približek)

Let a spherical shell of radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\) carry an axisymmetric load and let its edge lie at the angle \(\varphi_\mathrm{e}\) between the axis and the normal (for a hemisphere \(\varphi_\mathrm{e} = 90^\circ\)). Let \(s \ge 0\) be the distance from the edge along the meridian. In Geckeler's approximation (a) in the edge-effect equations we neglect the functions and their first derivatives against the higher derivatives and (b) we take the radii of curvature \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) as constant near the edge. The displacement \(w_\mathrm{e}(s)\) of the edge effect (robni učinek), that is the departure from the membrane displacement caused by the forces and the moment at the edge, then solves the same equation as the displacement of a cylinder of radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\) by theorem 22.42, without the right-hand side,

\[D\,w_\mathrm{e}'''' + \frac{Eh}{R^2}\,w_\mathrm{e} = 0 , \tag{22.50}\]

where \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\) and \(\beta\) is the same as for the cylinder (theorem 22.43).

Source: The approximation is after VK section 16.4 (PDF pp. 465-466): conditions (a) and (b) are on PDF pp. 465 and 466, the edge-effect equation (16.41) and its analogy with equation (15.44) for the cylinder on PDF p. 466. VK writes it for another unknown, with the parameter (16.42), which for a sphere equals \(\beta R\). VK attributes the approximation to H. Reissner and Geckeler, and Meissner extended it to shells of revolution with an arbitrary meridian (PDF p. 466). The error of the formulas is according to VK of the order \(\sqrt{h\cot^2\varphi_\mathrm{e}/R}\), and they are usable for \(\varphi_\mathrm{e} > 35^\circ\) (PDF p. 469). The approximation is not proved, see the remark below.

What the approximation neglects and how well, the numbers of the vessel tell us. Each derivative of the solution \(e^{-\beta s}\cos\beta s\) brings a factor of order \(\beta = 0{,}12854\) 1/mm, while the function itself changes over the radius by \(1/R = 0{,}02\) 1/mm. The first derivative \(w_\mathrm{e}'\) is therefore of order \(\beta w_\mathrm{e}\), which is \(\beta R = 6{,}43\) times more than \(w_\mathrm{e}/R\), and the second derivative \((\beta R)^2 = 41\) times more than \(w_\mathrm{e}/R^2\). This is not very large, and so the approximation is only an approximation. A comparison is in VK (example 16.1, PDF p. 472): for a hemisphere with the edge at \(\varphi_\mathrm{e} = 90^\circ\) the result of the approximation agrees with the results of the exact equations (16.30) from another source, which VK attributes to \(\varphi_\mathrm{e} = 90^\circ\) and the constant radii of curvature. We have not proved it, and in this section we use it as a given rule.

Now we have everything for the calculation at the junction.

Theorem 22.48 Junction of a cylinder and a hemisphere of equal thickness (spoj valja in polkrogle iste debeline)

Let the cylinder and the hemisphere be of the same material (\(E\), \(\nu\)), both with mid radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\), joined without a notch and without a ring, and let them carry the internal pressure \(p\) with the vessel closed. Let \(x\) be the coordinate along the meridian with its origin at the junction, \(x > 0\) in the cylinder and \(x < 0\) in the hemisphere, so that \(|x|\) is the distance from the junction, \(w(x)\) the radial displacement outward and \(\Delta = pR^2/(2Eh)\) the difference of the membrane displacements from equation (22.49). By Geckeler's approximation (definition 22.47):

  1. The edge part of the displacement is \(w_\mathrm{e}(x) = -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(x)\,e^{-\beta|x|}\cos\beta x\), and the whole displacement is \(w = w_\mathrm{m} + w_\mathrm{e}\) with \(w_\mathrm{m} = w_\mathrm{c}\) for \(x > 0\) and \(w_\mathrm{m} = w_\mathrm{s}\) for \(x < 0\).
  2. The moment at the junction is \(M_0 = 0\), and the radial force per unit length of the ring is \(Q_0 = D\beta^3\Delta = p/(8\beta)\). The end of the cylinder is pushed inward, the end of the hemisphere outward.
  3. The displacement at the junction is the mean of the membrane displacements, \(w(0) = \tfrac12(w_\mathrm{c} + w_\mathrm{s})\).
  4. The moment along the meridian, \(M = D\,w''\) as in theorem 22.42, is \(M(x) = -(Q_0/\beta)\,e^{-\beta|x|}\sin\beta x\), it has opposite signs in the cylinder and the hemisphere and is largest in size at \(\beta|x| = \pi/4\), where \(|M| = M_{\max} = \dfrac{Q_0}{\beta}\,\dfrac{e^{-\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\).
  5. The hoop force at the junction is \(N_\theta(0) = \tfrac34\,pR\).
Proof

We must show that the four conditions at the junction determine exactly the four constants of the two edge solutions. We build the solutions from the edge solution of the cylinder, place them at the junction, apply the junction conditions and compute.

  1. On each side the displacement is the sum of the membrane part, which is constant (equation (22.48)), and the edge part, which by definition 22.47 solves equation (22.50). This is the equation of the cylinder without the right-hand side, and its general solution that does not grow with the distance from the junction is given by theorem 22.43: \(w_\mathrm{e} = e^{-\beta x}(A_1\cos\beta x + A_2\sin\beta x)\) for \(x > 0\) and \(w_\mathrm{e} = e^{\beta x}(B_1\cos\beta x + B_2\sin\beta x)\) for \(x < 0\) (in the second case \(|x|\) is the distance from the junction, the same form as for the cylinder).
  2. At \(x = 0\) we compute \(w_\mathrm{e}\) and its derivatives by the product rule (lemma 1.81) and the theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine (8.12). For \(x > 0\) the values are \(A_1\), \(\beta(A_2 - A_1)\), \(-2\beta^2A_2\), \(2\beta^3(A_1 + A_2)\), for \(x < 0\) they are \(B_1\), \(\beta(B_1 + B_2)\), \(2\beta^2B_2\), \(2\beta^3(B_2 - B_1)\) for the derivatives of order zero to three (SymPy).
  3. There are no external loads at the junction: the pressure acts on the two shells over their area, which on a ring without width gives no force per unit length, and the axial forces \(pR/2\) are equal (compare figure 22.23) and so give no radial force. The wall neither tears nor kinks at the junction, so the displacement and the slope are continuous. A ring without width, however, is in equilibrium only if one side acts on it with the same force and the same moment as the other, in the opposite direction, so the moment \(M = D\,w''\) and the transverse force \(Q = -D\,w'''\) are continuous too (theorem 22.42, item 3, for the hemisphere by Geckeler's approximation). The membrane part is constant and contributes nothing to the slope, the moment or the transverse force.
  4. The slope condition gives \(\beta(A_2 - A_1) = \beta(B_1 + B_2)\), the moment condition \(-2\beta^2A_2 = 2\beta^2B_2\), the transverse force condition \(2\beta^3(A_1 + A_2) = 2\beta^3(B_2 - B_1)\). From the moment, \(B_2 = -A_2\). From the transverse force, \(B_1 = -A_1 - 2A_2\). We insert this into the slope: \(A_2 - A_1 = -A_1 - 2A_2 - A_2\), so \(A_2 = -3A_2\) and \(A_2 = 0\). Hence \(B_2 = 0\) and \(B_1 = -A_1\).
  5. The displacement condition \(w_\mathrm{c} + A_1 = w_\mathrm{s} + B_1\) gives \(B_1 - A_1 = \Delta\), so \(-2A_1 = \Delta\), \(A_1 = -\Delta/2\) and \(B_1 = \Delta/2\). This is item 1 of the theorem, since \(e^{\beta x}\cos\beta x\) for \(x < 0\) equals \(e^{-\beta|x|}\cos\beta x\).
  6. The moment is \(M_0 = D\,w_\mathrm{e}''(0) = -2D\beta^2A_2 = 0\). The transverse force is \(-D\,w_\mathrm{e}'''(0) = -2D\beta^3(A_1 + A_2) = D\beta^3\Delta\). Since \(4D\beta^4 = Eh/R^2\) (the definition of \(\beta\) in theorem 22.43), we have \(D\beta^3 = Eh/(4R^2\beta)\) and \(Q_0 = \dfrac{Eh}{4R^2\beta}\cdot\dfrac{pR^2}{2Eh} = \dfrac{p}{8\beta}\). The edge part on the cylinder is negative at \(x = 0\) (\(A_1 < 0\)), so the junction pushes the end of the cylinder inward, and on the hemisphere (\(B_1 > 0\)) outward. This is item 2.
  7. The displacement at the junction is \(w(0) = w_\mathrm{c} + A_1 = w_\mathrm{c} - \Delta/2 = \tfrac12(w_\mathrm{c} + w_\mathrm{s})\), since \(\Delta = w_\mathrm{c} - w_\mathrm{s}\). This is item 3.
  8. For \(x > 0\) we have \(w_\mathrm{e} = -\tfrac\Delta2e^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\) and \(w_\mathrm{e}'' = -\Delta\beta^2e^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\), so \(M = D\,w_\mathrm{e}'' = -D\beta^2\Delta\,e^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x = -(Q_0/\beta)\,e^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\). For \(x < 0\) we have \(w_\mathrm{e}'' = -\Delta\beta^2e^{\beta x}\sin\beta x\) in the same notation, where \(\sin\beta x < 0\), and so \(M(x) = -(Q_0/\beta)e^{-\beta|x|}\sin\beta x\), in the hemisphere with the opposite sign to the cylinder. The derivative of \(e^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\) is \(\beta e^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x - \sin\beta x)\) and is zero at \(\tan\beta x = 1\), that is \(\beta x = \pi/4\) (the first zero, here \(|M|\) is largest, since \(M(0) = 0\) and \(M < 0\) up to \(\beta x = \pi\), and at the next zeros \(\beta x = \pi/4 + k\pi\) the value \(|e^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x|\) is smaller by the factor \(e^{-k\pi}\)). There \(\sin(\pi/4) = 1/\sqrt2\), hence \(M_{\max}\). This is item 4.
  9. The axial force stays \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = pR/2\), since the equilibrium of the cap above the junction circle (theorem 22.32) does not depend on the displacement. By Hooke's law for shells (theorem 22.28) \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = C(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} + \nu\,w/R)\) and \(N_\theta = C(w/R + \nu\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}})\) with \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\), since the hoop strain equals \(w/R\). From the first, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = N_{\mathrm{x}}/C - \nu w/R\), which we insert into the second: \(N_\theta = C(1 - \nu^2)\,w/R + \nu N_{\mathrm{x}} = Eh\,w/R + \nu N_{\mathrm{x}}\). At \(x = 0\) we have \(w(0) = w_\mathrm{c} - \Delta/2\) and \(Eh\,w_\mathrm{c}/R = pR(1 - \nu/2)\) and \(Eh\,\Delta/(2R) = pR/4\), so \(N_\theta(0) = pR(1 - \nu/2) - pR/4 + \nu pR/2 = \tfrac34\,pR\). This is item 5.

Source: The result \(M_0 = 0\) and \(Q_0 = pR^2D\beta^3/(2Eh) = p\sqrt{Rh}\,/\big(8\sqrt[4]{3(1 - \nu^2)}\big)\) is in VK, example 16.2 (PDF pp. 472 to 474), there from the conditions of continuity of displacement and slope with unit loads. The value \(\tfrac34pR\) for the hoop force is also there ("\(1{,}125\,p\)" at \(R = 1{,}5\) m, PDF p. 474), and \(M_{\max}\) with the same formula. The proof by symmetry of the edge solutions is not from the source. Geckeler's approximation itself is quoted, not proved.

What have we shown? With equal thickness and equal radius the junction carries only the radial force \(Q_0\) and no moment. The reason is the equality of the two edge solutions: both have the same \(\beta\), the same stiffness \(D\) and the same radius, so each end moves by half the gap, each in its own direction, and the slopes of the two ends are equal already without a moment (step 4). With another thickness or another radius of the hemisphere the two solutions would have different \(\beta\) and \(D\) and \(M_0 \ne 0\), which we do not compute here.

Example 22.49 Stresses at the junction of the vessel (napetosti na spoju posode)

For the vessel from the start of the section we have, by SymPy and NumPy, \(D = 143\,590\) N mm, \(\beta = 0{,}12854\) 1/mm and \(1/\beta = 7{,}780\) mm. The radial force at the junction is \(Q_0 = p/(8\beta) = 2{,}917\) N/mm. The displacement at the junction is \(11{,}480\) μm, the mean of \(16{,}263\) and \(6{,}696\) μm. The moment is largest in size, \(M_{\max} = 7{,}317\) N mm/mm, at \(\beta|x| = \pi/4\), that is \(6{,}11\) mm from the junction, and belongs to the bending stress \(6M_{\max}/h^2 = 10{,}98\) MPa. In the cylinder this stress stretches the outer face in the axial direction (axial stress \(37{,}50 + 10{,}98 = 48{,}48\) MPa) and reduces the inner one (\(37{,}50 - 10{,}98 = 26{,}52\) MPa), in the hemisphere it is the other way round. The hoop stress at the junction is \(\tfrac34\cdot 75{,}00 = 56{,}25\) MPa, less than the membrane \(75{,}00\) MPa in the cylinder. The moment dies out within \(\pi/\beta = 24{,}44\) mm, that is \(28{,}0^\circ\) of the meridian of the hemisphere, and at \(20\) mm we have \(e^{-\beta x} = 0{,}0765\). The hoop stress on the middle surface of the cylinder, \((Eh\,w/R + \nu N_{\mathrm{x}})/h\) by step 9 of the proof, is largest where the displacement \(w\) is largest. This is at \(\beta x = 3\pi/4\), \(18{,}33\) mm from the junction, where \(w\) exceeds \(w_\mathrm{c}\) by a little, and the hoop stress there is \(76{,}26\) MPa, only \(1{,}7\) % above the \(75{,}00\) MPa far from the junction.

Let us look at the course (figure 22.24), to see how what we computed is distributed along the meridian.

Far from the junction the displacement \(w\) lies on the membrane values \(w_\mathrm{s}\) and \(w_\mathrm{c}\). At the junction it is exactly halfway between them and bridges the gap \(\Delta\) over a length of order \(1/\beta\). The moment is zero at the junction and far from it, and in between it reaches its largest absolute value \(M_{\max}\) near the junction, positive in the hemisphere and negative in the cylinder. The moment is odd about the junction, because the edge part of the displacement is odd (item 1 of the theorem): the end of the cylinder gives way inward by \(\Delta/2\), the end of the hemisphere outward by \(\Delta/2\). So in the cylinder the outer face is stretched more in the axial direction, and in the hemisphere the inner one. On the \(x\) axis two points \(\pm\pi/\beta\) are marked, where the moment first crosses zero again.

The theorem requires the shells to be equal. Let us look at two cases that violate this by a little, to see what the theorem really needs.

A near miss: a rigid end. If the cylinder is closed by a flat end whose stiffness is much larger than the stiffness of the wall, the junction behaves as a rigid clamp: displacement and slope at the edge are zero (lemma 22.45 in section 22.6). The end of the cylinder must now not move, so the edge part has to return all of \(w_\mathrm{c}\) and not only half of the gap. The flat end, being a thin plate, is not perfectly rigid, its true stiffness is treated by chapter 18, so we do not compute stresses of a flat end here. The clamp is a limiting case that shows how far the junction with a hemisphere is from this extreme.

hemisphere (theorem 22.48) rigid clamp (lemma 22.45)
displacement at the junction \(11{,}480\) μm \(0\)
\(M_0\) \(0\) \(77{,}167\) N mm/mm
\(Q_0\) \(2{,}917\) N/mm \(19{,}838\) N/mm
largest bending stress \(10{,}98\) MPa \(115{,}75\) MPa
axial stress on the face \(48{,}48\) MPa \(153{,}25\) MPa

The reason for the difference is the cause that forces the wall to bend: with a hemisphere the edge part of the cylinder has to reduce the displacement by only \(\Delta/2 = 4{,}78\) μm (step 5 of the proof), with a rigid end by the whole \(w_\mathrm{c} = 16{,}263\) μm, that is \(3{,}4\) times more. The transverse force is \(6{,}8\) times larger (\(19{,}838/2{,}917\)): \(3{,}4\) times because of the larger displacement, and \(2\) times more because the clamp also has to return the slope. At the clamp \(Q(0) = p(1 - \nu/2)/\beta = 4D\beta^3w_\mathrm{c}\) (lemma 22.45), with the hemisphere \(Q_0 = D\beta^3\Delta = 2D\beta^3\cdot\Delta/2\) (step 6 of the proof). The largest bending stress is \(10{,}5\) times larger. The largest axial stress at the rigid end, \(153{,}25\) MPa, is \(2{,}04\) times the hoop stress \(75{,}00\) MPa far from the end, while with the hemisphere it stays below it at \(48{,}48\) MPa. How an end really answers is the task of the plate (chapter 18).

A near miss: a cone. We close the cylinder with a cone instead of a hemisphere. The theorem does not hold here: in step 3 of its proof we claimed that there is no radial force at the junction, but the membrane force of the cone has a radial component. The thickness \(h\), the radius \(R\) at the junction, the material and the pressure stay the same, only the direction of the head at the junction differs.

First we draw the axis of revolution and the wall of the cylinder with the head on the right, which meets the cylinder at the junction \(K\). In the left figure the head is a hemisphere: its tangent at \(K\) is parallel to the axis, so the head pulls the ring to the right with \(pR/2\), the cylinder to the left with the same \(pR/2\), and the forces balance, \(H = 0\). In the right figure the head is a cone that runs from \(K\) downward toward the axis along a generatrix. The meridian force of the cone \(N_{\mathrm{s}}\) is now inclined and has two components: an axial one that balances the force of the cylinder, and a radial one, \(H\), inward, which has nothing to balance it. How large the two are is told by the equilibrium of the cap.

Lemma 22.50 Radial force at the cone-to-cylinder junction (radialna sila na spoju stožca in valja)

Let a cylinder of mid radius \(R\) be closed by a cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) between the axis and the generatrix (\(0 \le \alpha < 90^\circ\)), loaded by the internal pressure \(p\) with the vessel closed. By membrane theory (section 22.4) the meridian force of the cone at the junction is \(N_{\mathrm{s}} = pR/(2\cos\alpha)\), its axial component is \(pR/2\) and equals the axial force of the cylinder, and the radial component, which has no opposing force, is

\[H = N_{\mathrm{s}}\sin\alpha = \frac{pR\tan\alpha}{2} \tag{22.51}\]

per unit length of the junction circle, directed toward the axis. For a hemisphere \(H = 0\).

Proof

We must show the axial equilibrium of the cap above the junction and then split the force \(N_{\mathrm{s}}\) into components.

  1. We cut off the cap of the cone above the junction circle, as the cap in theorem 22.32. The pressure \(p\) on its inner side has the axial resultant \(p\pi R^2\), since the projection of the inner surface onto the plane of the junction circle is a disc of radius \(R\).
  2. The force \(N_{\mathrm{s}}\) per unit length of the circle points along the generatrix, which makes the angle \(\alpha\) with the axis, and over the whole circumference of length \(2\pi R\) its axial component is \(2\pi R\,N_{\mathrm{s}}\cos\alpha\).
  3. Axial equilibrium of the cap: \(2\pi R\,N_{\mathrm{s}}\cos\alpha = p\pi R^2\), so \(N_{\mathrm{s}} = pR/(2\cos\alpha)\) and the axial component \(N_{\mathrm{s}}\cos\alpha = pR/2\), equal to the axial force of the cylinder.
  4. The radial component is \(N_{\mathrm{s}}\sin\alpha = pR\tan\alpha/2\). For a hemisphere the tangent of the meridian at the junction is parallel to the axis, so \(\alpha = 0\) and \(H = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. That the horizontal component of the meridian force at the edge of a shell requires a supporting ring is in VK (PDF p. 380) for a dome, and in example 16.3 (PDF pp. 475 to 476) the horizontal component \(T_0\) of the membrane force at the edge of the head is completed by an equal and opposite thrust among the forces of the edge effect.

At \(p = 3\) MPa and \(R = 50\) mm the force is \(H = 43{,}30\), \(75{,}00\) and \(129{,}90\) N/mm at \(\alpha = 30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\) (SymPy and NumPy), the axial component is always \(75{,}00\) N/mm. For the hemisphere the whole radial force that the junction has to carry is \(Q_0 = 2{,}917\) N/mm. For the cone of \(45^\circ\) the force \(H\) is already \(75{,}00/2{,}917 = 25{,}7\) times larger before any response of the junction. The transverse force condition in step 3 of the proof of theorem 22.48 turns for the cone from continuity into a jump: the radial force of the edge effect on the end of the head differs from the one on the end of the cylinder by \(H\) (this is how VK divides the forces in example 16.3 for a head shaped as a spherical segment, PDF p. 476). We do not compute the solution, because it requires a separate calculation of the edge effect of the cone.

A remark on what stays open. Geckeler's approximation is quoted and not proved, so the proof of the theorem is conditional on it. A proof would need the full edge equation of an axisymmetric shell of revolution (VK, sections 16.2 to 16.3, PDF pp. 454 to 465) and an estimate of the error, which VK gives only as the order \(\sqrt{h\cot^2\varphi_\mathrm{e}/R}\) (PDF p. 469). A different thickness or radius, and a spherical segment instead of a hemisphere, are treated by VK in example 16.3 (PDF pp. 475 to 477).
