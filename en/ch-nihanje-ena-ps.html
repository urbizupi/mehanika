Chapter 8 ended, for a system with one degree of freedom, with the equation \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = 0\) and a motion that never stops: the displacement is a cosine of constant amplitude (theorem 8.120). Real systems behave differently. A cantilever with a weight on it, struck with a hammer, vibrates and dies out. A machine on mounts, driven by a rotating unbalanced mass, shakes strongly at some speeds and hardly at all at others. What does one equation predict if we add a force that opposes the motion in proportion to the velocity, and an outer force that changes with time? And how do we read the numbers of this equation from a record of the motion?

The answer has three parts. The first is the equation itself: when one number describes the motion of a body with infinitely many points, and how energies give the equation for that number. The second is its solution: how fast the vibration dies out after a blow, how the system answers a force that oscillates with one frequency, and how it answers a short impulse or a force of any shape. The third is the inverse task: to determine from a measured record of the motion how fast the system vibrates by itself and how fast the vibration dies out.

The route of the chapter is this. In the first section (9.1) we introduce the force that opposes the motion and write the equation that holds for all systems with this model. To solve it we need three pieces of mathematics that the book does not have yet, and we introduce each right before its first use: a function that turns equal steps of time into equal quotients (section 9.2), the numbers with which we write the roots of a quadratic equation that has no solution among the real numbers (section 9.3), and the theorem that with them we capture every solution (section 9.4). Then we solve the motion after a blow without an outer force and ask when a system returns to rest fastest (section 9.5). A force that oscillates with one frequency follows: at which frequencies the system answers strongly and at which hardly (section 9.6), and where the energy goes meanwhile (section 9.7). We apply the same to the machine on mounts to see how much the mounts reduce the force on the floor (section 9.8). Then we treat a short impulse (section 9.9) and a force of any shape, which we cut into a sequence of impulses (section 9.10). A force that repeats with the same shape splits into simple forces, each oscillating with its own frequency (sections 9.11 and 9.12). At the end we measure from a record of the motion how fast the system vibrates by itself and how fast the vibration dies out (section 9.13).

Three systems will accompany us (figure 9.3 in section 9.1). The first is the steel cantilever of chapter 5 with a weight of mass 50 kg on its free end, which we shall call system A. The second is a machine on mounts, driven by a rotating unbalanced mass. The third is the pendulum of chapter 8. At the end of the chapter we add to system A a force that switches on and off in equal times. All three systems are our models, and their numbers are chosen to be comparable with the calculations of the previous chapters. Where the choice is ours, we say so.
