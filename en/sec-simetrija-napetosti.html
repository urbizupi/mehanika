Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.8 When forces do not turn a body

By definition 2.3, equilibrium of a body has two parts. The resultant of the forces on the body must be the zero vector, and the forces must not tend to turn the body. We wrote the first part with numbers in (2.2) and applied it in section 2.7 to a small cube, which gave the equilibrium equations. The second part is still written only in words. Three things wait for it. The ruler in figure 2.3 turns although the resultant of the forces on it is zero. For pure shear in section 2.6.6 we promised to show why the forces on the four faces of the cube must be equally large. And at the end of the same section a question remained: how does the second condition of equilibrium relate the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\)?

In this section we write the second condition of equilibrium with numbers and derive from it that at every point of a body \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). The route is this. First we need a quantity that says about which axis a force turns a body and how strongly. It will be a vector along the axis. The axis is perpendicular to the force, so we need a new operation that makes from two vectors a vector perpendicular to both. For it we also need an agreement on how the axes of a coordinate system are placed in space. Then we test the second condition on the ruler. Finally we cut a small cube out of a body in thought and show that it is in equilibrium only if the shear components of stress are equal in pairs.

2.8.1 Mathematics: the vector product

Let us return to the door in figure 2.2, right. The door turns about a vertical axis through the hinge, while the force at the handle is horizontal. The segment from the hinge to the handle is horizontal too. So the axis about which the force turns the door is perpendicular to the force and to the segment from the axis to the point where the force acts. How strongly the force turns the door we describe with a number, the axis of the turn with a direction. Together they are a vector along the axis. So we need a rule that makes from two vectors a third one, perpendicular to both. The scalar product of section 2.3 cannot do this, since its result is a number.

The rule that makes a third vector from two vectors is the second part of Hamilton's product from section 2.3. There we saw that the product of the vectors \(a_{\mathrm{x}} i + a_{\mathrm{y}} j + a_{\mathrm{z}} k\) and \(b_{\mathrm{x}} i + b_{\mathrm{y}} j + b_{\mathrm{z}} k\) contains, besides the number, terms with \(i\), \(j\) and \(k\), and that \(ij = k\) and \(ji = -k\). The units \(i\) and \(j\) are unit vectors along the axes \(x\) and \(y\), and \(k\) along the axis \(z\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 156-157). The product of the units along the axes \(x\) and \(y\) is therefore the unit along the axis \(z\), which is perpendicular to both. Exchanging the order reverses the direction. Of the two opposite directions perpendicular to both vectors, one is thus chosen by the order of the factors.

From history

A similar product was also developed by Grassmann (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158), and Gibbs wrote it with the sign \(\times\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158), as we do too.

Let us return to the vector we are looking for, which is to be perpendicular to both given vectors. Two nonzero vectors that do not point in the same or in opposite directions have two opposite directions perpendicular to both. For the door the axis of the turn points up or down. Which of the two directions we choose is an agreement, and this agreement is tied to how the axes of the coordinate system are placed in space. Definition 1.12 only requires the axes to be pairwise perpendicular and each to have a chosen positive direction. Once the axes \(x\) and \(y\) are placed, the positive axis \(z\) can point to one side or to the other of the plane in which they lie (figure 2.38). Both systems satisfy the definition, but each is the mirror image of the other, like a right and a left hand.

Definition 2.29 Right-handed coordinate system (desnosučni koordinatni sistem)

A rectangular Cartesian coordinate system is right-handed (desnosučni) when the positive directions of the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), in this order, point like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand, spread so that they are pairwise perpendicular. When the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the left hand point like this, the system is left-handed (levosučni).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3, Figs. 186 and 188 (PDF p. 395), BOWER Appendix A.1.3 (PDF p. 739).

Every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system is of one of the two kinds. When we point the thumb and the index finger of the right hand along the positive axes \(x\) and \(y\), the middle finger points to one side of the plane of these two axes. If the positive axis \(z\) points to the same side, the system is right-handed. Otherwise it points to the opposite side, where the middle finger of the left hand points when its thumb and index finger are held the same way, and the system is left-handed. We can also check the difference without hands (figure 2.38). We look from the tip of the positive axis \(z\) toward the origin and turn the positive axis \(x\) by a quarter turn into the positive axis \(y\). In a right-handed system we see this turn counterclockwise, in a left-handed one clockwise. KREYSZIG says the same with a right-handed screw: if we turn it from the axis \(x\) toward the axis \(y\), it advances in the direction of the positive axis \(z\) (KREYSZIG §9.3, Fig. 187, PDF p. 395).

We cannot read off right-handedness from components. In both systems of figure 2.38 the unit vectors of the axes have the components \((1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0)\) and \((0, 0, 1)\). The two systems differ in how they are placed in the space they describe, not in the numbers. KREYSZIG uses the same notion for any three vectors, not only for the unit vectors of perpendicular axes, and calls a triple of vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) right-handed when, in this order, they point like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand (KREYSZIG §9.3, PDF p. 395). The definition with numbers that we will use, we will give in section 2.10.

The system in figure 1.22 and the system of the sheared cube in figure 1.33 are right-handed. From now on a coordinate system in this book is always right-handed, unless we say otherwise. We have already met one exception. In part 2 of the proof of Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24) we reversed every axis along which the normal has a negative component. Reversing a single axis turns a right-handed system into a left-handed one, as figure 2.38 shows for the axis \(z\). It did no harm there, because that proof does not use the operation we introduce now.

Now let us look for a vector perpendicular to two given vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). We start with a simple case, where both lie in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\). Every vector of the form \((0, 0, c)\) is perpendicular to both, since

\[(a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\cdot(0, 0, c) = a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot c = 0 ,\]

and the same for \(\vek b\). Which number \(c\) should we choose? Let the length of the new vector tell us something about the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), for example the area of the parallelogram they span when drawn from the same point (figure 2.39). For \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\) on the axis \(x\) the parallelogram has the base \(|a_{\mathrm{x}}|\) and the height \(|b_{\mathrm{y}}|\), the distance of the tip of \(\vek b\) from the axis \(x\). By the school formula its area is the base times the height, \(|a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}}|\). For any two vectors in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) the area is \(|a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}|\), which we will show below. So we choose

\[c = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

The sign of the number \(c\) tells to which side of the plane the new vector points. For \(\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek b = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) we get \(c = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1\), and the new vector is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and the new vector then point, in this order, like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand, because the system is right-handed.

For vectors with three components we keep this formula for the component \(z\). The formulas for the components \(x\) and \(y\) we get by exchanging the letters in the cycle \(x \to y \to z \to x\). From \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\) for the component \(z\) this gives \(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}}\) for the component \(x\), and from that \(a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\) for the component \(y\). That the vector obtained in this way is indeed perpendicular to both we prove in the lemma below.

Definition 2.30 Vector product (vektorski produkt)

The vector product (vektorski produkt) of the vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\), written in a chosen right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, is the vector

\[\vek a\times\vek b = (a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}},\ \ a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}},\ \ a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}) . \tag{2.32}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3 (2) (PDF p. 394), CM (2.2.12), (2.2.49), (2.2.52) (PDF pp. 43, 54-55). Both define it by its length and direction and derive the formula with components.

The result is a vector, hence the name. Books also call it the cross product (križni produkt) (KREYSZIG §9.3, PDF p. 394, CM, PDF p. 43). Grassmann called it the external product (zunanji produkt) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158). Each component is the difference of two products in which only the other two letters appear. In the component \(x\) these are \(y\) and \(z\). KREYSZIG warns that in a left-handed system each component would have to be multiplied by \(-1\) to get the same vector (KREYSZIG §9.3, PDF p. 394). That is why we agreed above on right-handed systems.

Let us test the formula on the fly's flight \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\) and the vector \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\) of section 2.3, which is perpendicular to it. By (2.32)

\[\vek a\times\vek b = \big(2 \cdot 0 - (-2)(-1),\ \ (-2) \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0,\ \ 1 \cdot (-1) - 2 \cdot 2\big) = (-2, -4, -5) .\]

The scalar products \(\vek a\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = -2 - 8 + 10 = 0\) and \(\vek b\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = -4 + 4 + 0 = 0\) confirm that it is perpendicular to both. Its length is \(\sqrt{4 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{45}\). Since \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) are perpendicular, their parallelogram is a rectangle with the sides \(|\vek a| = 3\) and \(|\vek b| = \sqrt5\), with the area \(3\sqrt5 = \sqrt{45}\). The length of the vector product is indeed the area of the parallelogram. Items 4 and 5 of the next lemma say this in general.

Lemma 2.31 Properties of the vector product (lastnosti vektorskega produkta)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) written in a chosen right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, and all numbers \(k\):

  1. \(\vek b\times\vek a = -(\vek a\times\vek b)\), so \(\vek a\times\vek a = \vek 0\),
  2. \(\vek a\times(\vek b + \vek c) = \vek a\times\vek b + \vek a\times\vek c\) and \((\vek a + \vek b)\times\vek c = \vek a\times\vek c + \vek b\times\vek c\),
  3. \((k\,\vek a)\times\vek b = k\,(\vek a\times\vek b) = \vek a\times(k\,\vek b)\),
  4. \(\vek a\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = 0\) and \(\vek b\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = 0\),
  5. \(|\vek a\times\vek b|^2 = |\vek a|^2\,|\vek b|^2 - (\vek a\cdot\vek b)^2\),
  6. \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\).
Proof

We write each rule with components by (2.32) and reduce it to rules for numbers. The components \(y\) and \(z\) in (2.32) arise from the component \(x\) by exchanging the letters in the cycle \(x \to y \to z \to x\). For items 1 to 3 it is therefore enough to check the component \(x\), because the same computation with exchanged letters gives the other two.

1. Exchanging the order.

  1. By (2.32) the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek b\times\vek a\) is \(b_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{z}} - b_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  2. In a product of numbers the order does not matter, so this is \(a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} = -(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\), the negative of the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\times\vek b\).
  3. For \(\vek b = \vek a\) we get \(\vek a\times\vek a = -(\vek a\times\vek a)\). Every component \(q\) of this vector satisfies \(q = -q\), so \(2q = 0\) and \(q = 0\). Therefore \(\vek a\times\vek a = \vek 0\).

2. Splitting a sum.

  1. The sum \(\vek b + \vek c\) has the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.14), so the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\times(\vek b + \vek c)\) is \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\,(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}}) - a_{\mathrm{z}}\,(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}})\).
  2. We multiply out the brackets and rearrange the terms into \((a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}}) + (a_{\mathrm{y}} c_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} c_{\mathrm{y}})\). This is the sum of the components \(x\) of the vectors \(\vek a\times\vek b\) and \(\vek a\times\vek c\).
  3. The second equality follows from item 1, the first equality and item 1 once more:
    \[(\vek a + \vek b)\times\vek c = -\big(\vek c\times(\vek a + \vek b)\big) = -(\vek c\times\vek a) - (\vek c\times\vek b) = \vek a\times\vek c + \vek b\times\vek c .\]

3. Taking out a number.

  1. The vector \(k\,\vek a\) has the components \(k a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(k a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(k a_{\mathrm{z}}\), so the component \(x\) of the vector \((k\,\vek a)\times\vek b\) is \((k a_{\mathrm{y}})\,b_{\mathrm{z}} - (k a_{\mathrm{z}})\,b_{\mathrm{y}} = k\,(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\). This is the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  2. In the same way the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\times(k\,\vek b)\) is \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\,(k b_{\mathrm{z}}) - a_{\mathrm{z}}\,(k b_{\mathrm{y}}) = k\,(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\).

4. Perpendicularity.

  1. By (2.6) and (2.32)
    \[\vek a\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = a_{\mathrm{x}}\,(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}}) + a_{\mathrm{y}}\,(a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) + a_{\mathrm{z}}\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]
  2. After multiplying out the brackets these are six terms, \(a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The terms \(a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(-a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\) cancel, and so do \(-a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}}\), and \(a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-a_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The sum is zero.
  3. By item 1, \(\vek a\times\vek b = -(\vek b\times\vek a)\), and by item 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12), \(\vek b\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = -\,\vek b\cdot(\vek b\times\vek a)\). This is zero by steps 1 and 2, with the roles of the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) exchanged.

5. Length.

  1. By (1.10) the square of the length is the sum of the squares of the components:
    \[|\vek a\times\vek b|^2 = (a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}})^2 + (a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}})^2 + (a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}})^2 .\]
  2. We expand each square by the school formula for the square of a difference. We get six squares and three double products:
    \[a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 b_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 b_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 2\,a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} .\]
  3. By (1.10), \(|\vek a|^2\,|\vek b|^2 = (a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2)(b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\). Multiplied out, these are nine terms, each the square of one component of the vector \(\vek a\) times the square of one component of the vector \(\vek b\). Three of them have the same letter twice, \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 b_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). Six have two different letters, and these are exactly the six squares of step 2.
  4. By (2.6) and the school formula for the square of a sum of three numbers,
    \[(\vek a\cdot\vek b)^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 b_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\,a_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} .\]
  5. When we subtract \((\vek a\cdot\vek b)^2\) from \(|\vek a|^2\,|\vek b|^2\), the three terms with the same letter twice cancel. What remains are the six squares with different letters and the three double products with a minus sign, which is exactly the expression of step 2.

6. Unit vectors of the axes.

  1. By (2.32) with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0)\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0,\ 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0) = (0, 0, 1) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. In the same way \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0) = (1, 0, 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0,\ 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0,\ 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1) = (0, 1, 0) = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3 (3)-(6), Theorem 1 (PDF pp. 396-397), Problem 4, equation (12) (PDF p. 400), CM (2.2.17), (2.2.18) (PDF pp. 44-45), BOWER Appendix A.1.2 (PDF p. 739). KREYSZIG proves the splitting of a sum by components (PDF p. 397), the other proofs are not from the sources.

The proof reduced every property to a computation with components, as for the scalar product. Items 1 to 3 tell us how to compute with the vector product. We may multiply out brackets and take out numbers as when multiplying numbers, but we may not exchange the order of the factors, because this changes the sign (figure 2.39). Nor may we move the brackets in a product of three factors. By items 1, 3 and 6, for example,

\[(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek 0\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\]

(KREYSZIG §9.3 (7), PDF p. 397).

Item 4 says that the vector product is indeed perpendicular to both factors. Item 5 says how long it is. Let us show that this is the area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). Let \(\vek a \neq \vek 0\) and let \(\vek e = \vek a/|\vek a|\) be the unit vector in its direction. The area of a parallelogram is the base times the height. The base is the side \(\vek a\), with the length \(|\vek a|\). The height \(v\) is the length of the segment that is perpendicular to the line through the side \(\vek a\) and reaches the tip of the vector \(\vek b\). This is the length of the part of the vector \(\vek b\) perpendicular to \(\vek e\), so by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector \(v^2 = |\vek b|^2 - (\vek b\cdot\vek e)^2\). Since \(\vek b\cdot\vek e = (\vek a\cdot\vek b)/|\vek a|\) by items 1 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product,

\[\big(|\vek a|\,v\big)^2 = |\vek a|^2\left(|\vek b|^2 - \frac{(\vek a\cdot\vek b)^2}{|\vek a|^2}\right) = |\vek a|^2\,|\vek b|^2 - (\vek a\cdot\vek b)^2 = |\vek a\times\vek b|^2 ,\]

the last equality by item 5. Both numbers \(|\vek a|\,v\) and \(|\vek a\times\vek b|\) are nonnegative and have the same square, so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square they are equal. The length of the vector product is therefore the area of the parallelogram. So our definition agrees with the definitions in KREYSZIG (§9.3 (1), PDF p. 394) and CM (PDF p. 43), which take the length of the vector product to be the area of the parallelogram. For two vectors in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) the vector product is \((0, 0, a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}})\), and its length \(|a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is the area we promised above. When \(\vek b = k\,\vek a\) points in the direction of the vector \(\vek a\) or opposite to it, the parallelogram is flattened into a segment, and indeed by items 3 and 1, \(\vek a\times(k\,\vek a) = k\,(\vek a\times\vek a) = \vek 0\).

Into which of the two perpendicular directions does the vector product point? KREYSZIG and CM say this with the right-hand rule (pravilo desne roke): the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and \(\vek a\times\vek b\), in this order, point like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand, or, in other words, a right-handed screw turned from \(\vek a\) toward \(\vek b\) the shorter way advances in the direction of \(\vek a\times\vek b\) (KREYSZIG §9.3, PDF pp. 394-395, CM, PDF p. 43). The sources take the rule as the definition of the direction. For us the direction is fixed by formula (2.32), and the rule is a statement about this formula. For the unit vectors of the axes item 6 of the lemma confirms it, since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) means exactly that the system is right-handed. The right-hand rule describes the position of fingers in space, so we cannot prove it with components until we write it with numbers. We will do so in section 2.10.1, with the sign of a number computed from the components of three vectors, and there we will prove the statement for any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) with \(\vek a\times\vek b \neq \vek 0\). In our examples the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) will mostly lie in the plane of two axes, where we can check it directly, as for \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\).

2.8.2 The moment of a force

From history

A bar with a support, which we push down at one end and so raise a load at the other, is a lever (vzvod). Aristotle knew that the lever works, but he could not establish its mathematical theory. He described a body at the end of a lever by two motions, one along the tangent to the circle and one along the radius, the first according to nature and the second contrary to nature. Such notions did not fit the facts and made it impossible to perceive the true reason for the properties of the lever (CAJORI, PDF p. 53). A small book on mechanics, sometimes ascribed to Aristotle but probably not his (BALL, PDF p. 76), was closer to the truth. It states that what is gained in power is lost in speed, and therefore that two weights which keep a lever in equilibrium are inversely proportional to the arms of the lever. This is why it is easier to extract teeth with a pair of pincers than with the fingers (BALL, PDF p. 77). We see the reason on a lever with arms of 2 m and 1 m (figure 2.40). Turned through the same angle, the end of the longer arm describes an arc twice as long as the end of the shorter one, so the load on the longer arm moves twice as far. What we gain in distance we lose in force, so the weight on the longer arm must be half as large.

A rigorous proof of the conditions under which a lever is in equilibrium was given by Archimedes (287 to 212 BC, TIMO, PDF p. 3). In his work on mechanics he derived it for two weights \(P\) and \(Q\) at the points \(A\) and \(B\) of a light bar. He looked for the point \(O\) of the bar at which we support the bar so that it stays at rest, and he wanted to prove \(P \cdot OA = Q \cdot OB\) (BALL, PDF p. 102). Let us take \(P = 2\), \(Q = 1\), \(A = 0\) and \(B = 3\) (figure 2.41). He replaced the weight \(P\) by a uniformly heavy bar \(LH\) of weight \(P\) with its middle at \(A\), and the weight \(Q\) by a bar \(HK\) of weight \(Q\) with its middle at \(B\). The pull of each weight acts at the point where we hold it, and the weight of a uniform bar acts at its middle, so the replacement does not change the effect. He chose the lengths so that the bars have the same weight per unit length: \(L = -2\), \(H = 2\), \(K = 4\), so \(LH = 4\) and \(HK = 2\), in the ratio \(2 : 1 = P : Q\), and both weigh \(0{,}5\) per unit length. Together they are one uniform bar \(LK\), whose centre of gravity is at its middle \(O = 1\). Indeed \(P \cdot OA = 2 \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot 2 = Q \cdot OB\) (BALL, PDF p. 102). With arms in the ratio 1 to 1000, weights in the ratio 1000 to 1 thus balance, and so Archimedes is credited with the saying that with a firm support he could move the whole Earth (BALL, PDF p. 102, CAJORI, PDF p. 53). Mach later objected to the proof that from the mere assumption that equal weights at equal distances are in equilibrium it derives the inverse proportionality of weights and arms (CAJORI, PDF p. 53). The reason for the objection is the step with the replacement. That the weight of a uniform bar acts like one weight at its middle is a statement of the same kind as the one the proof is looking for.

Statics rested on the theory of the lever of Archimedes until Stevin, of whom we told in section 2.1 (BALL, PDF pp. 102, 274). Galileo wrote down for machines the principle that what we gain in force we lose in speed, in the same ratio (BALL, PDF p. 277). As we saw in chapter 1, he treated a bar built into a wall as a lever about the edge of the wall (TIMO, PDF pp. 19-20). A force turns a body the more strongly, the larger it is and the farther from the support it acts, measured perpendicular to its direction (EP §1.2, PDF p. 8).

Let us return to the door and write down how a force turns a body about a point. With the door in figure 2.2 we saw that the size of the force counts, and how far from the hinge it acts. The vector product captures both.

Definition 2.32 Moment of a force (moment sile)

Let a force \(\vek F\) act at a point \(A\), and let \(O\) be a point. The moment (moment) of the force \(\vek F\) about the point \(O\) is the vector

\[\vek M_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek r\times\vek F , \qquad \vek r = \overrightarrow{OA} , \tag{2.33}\]

where \(\vek r\) is the vector from the point \(O\) to the point \(A\) at which the force acts.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.3 (8), Example 3 (PDF p. 397), CM (2.2.9)-(2.2.11) (PDF pp. 42-43).

The vector \(\vek r\) has components in metres and the force in newtons, so by (2.32) every component of the moment has the unit newton metre, N m. The force tends to turn the vector \(\vek r\) toward the direction of the force (CM, PDF pp. 42-43). The moment points along the axis of this turn, by the right-hand rule: \(\vek r\), \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) point like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand (KREYSZIG §9.3, Example 3, PDF p. 397), when \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is not the zero vector. That the rule holds for any two vectors whose vector product is not zero is proved in section 2.10.1.

Let us test the definition on the door, seen from above (figure 2.42). We put the origin \(O\) at the hinge, the axis \(x\) along the door, the axis \(y\) horizontal and perpendicular to the door, and the axis \(z\) vertically up, toward the viewer. This system is right-handed. Let the door be 80 cm wide, and let the handle be right at its free end, at the point \(A = (80, 0, 0)\) cm. We measure lengths in centimetres so that the components are whole numbers, and the moment therefore in N cm, \(1\) N cm \(= 0{,}01\) N m. We push the door at the handle, perpendicular to it, with the force \(\vek F = (0, 10, 0)\) N. By (2.32)

\[\begin{aligned} \vek M_{\mathrm{O}} &= (80, 0, 0)\times(0, 10, 0) \\ &= (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 10,\ \ 0 \cdot 0 - 80 \cdot 0,\ \ 80 \cdot 10 - 0 \cdot 0) = (0, 0, 800)\ \text{N cm} . \end{aligned}\]

The moment points vertically up, along the axis of the hinge, and its size is \(800\) N cm \(= 8\) N m. From the tip of the axis \(z\), that is, from above, we see the door turn from the axis \(x\) toward the axis \(y\), counterclockwise. The same force acting 5 cm from the hinge, at the point \((5, 0, 0)\) cm, gives the moment \((5, 0, 0)\times(0, 10, 0) = (0, 0, 50)\) N cm, sixteen times smaller. A force that pulls the door along itself toward the hinge, \(\vek F = (-10, 0, 0)\) N, gives \((80, 0, 0)\times(-10, 0, 0) = \vek 0\) and does not turn the door at all.

What if the force is not perpendicular to the door? We push it at the handle with the force \(\vek F = (-6, 8, 0)\) N. It has the same size \(\sqrt{36 + 64} = 10\) N, but points obliquely, partly toward the hinge. Then

\[\begin{aligned} \vek M_{\mathrm{O}} &= (80, 0, 0)\times(-6, 8, 0) \\ &= (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 8,\ \ 0 \cdot (-6) - 80 \cdot 0,\ \ 80 \cdot 8 - 0 \cdot (-6)) = (0, 0, 640)\ \text{N cm} , \end{aligned}\]

less than for the perpendicular force. What matters is not the distance from \(O\) to the point \(A\) but the distance from \(O\) to the line on which the force lies. The line through the point \(A\) in the direction of the force is called the line of action (premica delovanja) of the force. If \(\vek e = \vek F/|\vek F|\) is the unit vector in the direction of the force, its points are \(A + t\,\vek e\), where \(t\) is any number. The smallest distance from the point \(O\) to the points of the line of action is called the lever arm (ročica) of the force about \(O\) (CM (2.2.9), PDF p. 42, KREYSZIG §9.3, Example 3, PDF p. 397). Lemma 2.33, which follows, says that this smallest distance exists, how large it is and how it is related to the moment.

Lemma 2.33 Lever arm and the magnitude of the moment (ročica in velikost momenta)

Let a force \(\vek F \neq \vek 0\) act at a point \(A\), let \(\vek e = \vek F/|\vek F|\) be the unit vector in its direction, \(O\) a point and \(\vek r = \overrightarrow{OA}\). Then:

  1. Among the distances from \(O\) to the points \(A + t\,\vek e\) of the line of action there is a smallest one, the lever arm,
    \[\ell = \sqrt{|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2} , \tag{2.34}\]
    attained at \(t = -\vek r\cdot\vek e\).
  2. The size of the moment is the size of the force times the lever arm, \(|\vek r\times\vek F| = |\vek F|\,\ell\).
  3. If we move the force along its line of action to the point \(A' = A + t\,\vek e\), its moment about \(O\) does not change: \(\overrightarrow{OA'}\times\vek F = \vek r\times\vek F\).
Proof

We must show three statements. Throughout we use that \(\vek e\) is a unit vector, so \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = |\vek e|^2 = 1\) by item 4 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12).

1. The smallest distance.

  1. The vector from \(O\) to the point \(A + t\,\vek e\) is \(\vek r + t\,\vek e\), since the components of the vector from \(O\) to \(A\) and of the vector \(t\,\vek e\) add up to the differences of coordinates ((1.8)).
  2. By the formula for the square of the length of a sum (lemma 2.9) and by items 3 and 4 of the properties of the scalar product, the square of the distance is
    \[|\vek r + t\,\vek e|^2 = |\vek r|^2 + 2t\,(\vek r\cdot\vek e) + t^2 .\]
  3. The square \((t + \vek r\cdot\vek e)^2 = t^2 + 2t\,(\vek r\cdot\vek e) + (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2\) differs from the right side only in its last term, so
    \[|\vek r + t\,\vek e|^2 = (t + \vek r\cdot\vek e)^2 + |\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2 .\]
  4. The first term is never negative and is zero exactly at \(t = -\vek r\cdot\vek e\). The square of the distance is therefore smallest at this \(t\), and there it equals \(|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2\).
  5. Distances are nonnegative numbers, so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, the smallest square belongs to the smallest distance. This is \(\ell = \sqrt{|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2}\).

2. The size of the moment.

  1. By lemma 1.19 on the unit vector in the direction of a vector, \(\vek F = |\vek F|\,\vek e\).
  2. By item 5 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, \(|\vek r\times\vek F|^2 = |\vek r|^2\,|\vek F|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek F)^2\).
  3. By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product, \(\vek r\cdot\vek F = |\vek F|\,(\vek r\cdot\vek e)\), so \(|\vek r\times\vek F|^2 = |\vek F|^2\,\big(|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2\big) = |\vek F|^2\,\ell^2\).
  4. The numbers \(|\vek r\times\vek F|\) and \(|\vek F|\,\ell\) are nonnegative and have the same square, so by lemma 1.28 they are equal.

3. Moving along the line.

  1. By step 1 of part 1 the vector from \(O\) to \(A' = A + t\,\vek e\) is \(\vek r + t\,\vek e\).
  2. By items 2 and 3 of the lemma on the properties of the vector product, \((\vek r + t\,\vek e)\times\vek F = \vek r\times\vek F + t\,(\vek e\times\vek F)\).
  3. Since \(\vek F = |\vek F|\,\vek e\), items 3 and 1 of the same lemma give \(\vek e\times\vek F = |\vek F|\,(\vek e\times\vek e) = \vek 0\). What remains is \(\vek r\times\vek F\).

Source: CM (2.2.9), (2.2.10) (PDF p. 42), KREYSZIG §9.3, Example 3 (PDF p. 397). The sources write the size of the moment with the lever arm and with the angle between \(\vek r\) and \(\vek F\). The proof through the smallest distance is not from the sources.

The lemma says that the moment does not depend on where on its line of action the force acts, only on the line itself and on the force. Its size is the force times the lever arm, which is the formula with which CM and KREYSZIG introduce the moment. Let us check this on the oblique force \(\vek F = (-6, 8, 0)\) N on the door. The unit vector in its direction is \(\vek e = (-\tfrac35, \tfrac45, 0)\), and \(\vek r\cdot\vek e = 80 \cdot (-\tfrac35) = -48\) cm. By (2.34) the lever arm is

\[\ell = \sqrt{80^2 - 48^2}\ \text{cm} = \sqrt{6400 - 2304}\ \text{cm} = \sqrt{4096}\ \text{cm} = 64\ \text{cm} .\]

By item 1 the nearest point \(N\) of the line of action lies at \(t = 48\) cm, that is, 48 cm from \(A\) in the direction of the force (figure 2.42). By item 2 the size of the moment is \(10\ \text{N} \cdot 64\ \text{cm} = 640\) N cm, exactly the component \(z\) we computed above. The oblique force turns the door less strongly because its lever arm is 64 cm, shorter than 80 cm. For a force acting along the door toward the hinge, the line of action passes through \(O\), the lever arm is zero, and so is the moment.

2.8.3 The second condition of equilibrium

Now we can write as an equation what it means that the forces do not tend to turn a body. Let forces \(\vek F_1, \vek F_2, \dots, \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) act on a body at points \(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\), and let \(O\) be a chosen point. The forces do not tend to turn the body when the sum of their moments about \(O\) is the zero vector:

\[\vek r_1\times\vek F_1 + \vek r_2\times\vek F_2 + \dots + \vek r_{\mathrm{k}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0 , \tag{2.35}\]

where \(\vek r_1 = \overrightarrow{OA_1}\) is the vector from \(O\) to the point at which the force \(\vek F_1\) acts, and the same for the other forces. This is the second condition of equilibrium of definition 2.3, written with numbers (SADD §3.7, (3.7.6), PDF pp. 77-78, EP §2.2.2, p. 54). When a force is distributed over a part of the surface or over all the material, like the weight, it is by section 2.1 the sum of many small forces, one on each bit, and its moment is the sum of their moments. Equation (2.35) is a vector equation, so it means three equations for numbers, one for each component.

Where does this condition come from? Like the condition for the forces, it is a law of mechanics that we do not derive from others. CM writes it for every part of a body, also for a body that moves: the sum of the moments of all forces on a part of a body equals the rate at which the sum of the moments of the momenta (gibalne količine) of its bits changes, the momentum of a bit being its mass times its velocity. CM calls it the principle of balance of angular momentum (načelo o vrtilni količini) (CM §5.3.3, (5.3.28), PDF pp. 232-233). For a body at rest all velocities are zero, the right side is zero, and (2.35) remains. We cannot get it from the condition for the forces, as the ruler shows.

Let the ruler in figure 2.3 be 30 cm long. We put the origin \(O\) at its centre, the axis \(x\) along the ruler, the axis \(y\) horizontally across it and the axis \(z\) vertically up (figure 2.43). The ends are \(A_1 = (15, 0, 0)\) cm and \(A_2 = (-15, 0, 0)\) cm. The force \(\vek F = (0, 5, 0)\) N acts on the right end, the force \(-\vek F\) on the left end. The resultant is zero, and the first condition of equilibrium holds. The moments about \(O\), however, are

\[(15, 0, 0)\times(0, 5, 0) = (0, 0, 75)\ \text{N cm} , \qquad (-15, 0, 0)\times(0, -5, 0) = (0, 0, 75)\ \text{N cm} .\]

Their sum \((0, 0, 150)\) N cm is not the zero vector, and the second condition does not hold. The moment points vertically up. From the tip of the axis \(z\), from above, we see the ruler turn from the axis \(x\) toward the axis \(y\), counterclockwise, as the grey arrow in figure 2.3 shows. The forces along the ruler, \((5, 0, 0)\) N at the right end and \((-5, 0, 0)\) N at the left end, have the moments

\[(15, 0, 0)\times(5, 0, 0) = \vek 0 , \qquad (-15, 0, 0)\times(-5, 0, 0) = \vek 0 ,\]

because the vector to the point of action points in the direction of the force or opposite to it. The line of action therefore passes through \(O\), and the lever arm is zero (lemma 2.33 on the lever arm and the magnitude of the moment). Such forces do not turn the ruler. In section 2.1 we claimed this for bars pulled along their axis, and now it is proved.

About which point \(O\) should we compute the moments? For the ruler, let us choose the left end \(A_2\) instead of the centre. The vector from \(A_2\) to \(A_1\) is \((30, 0, 0)\) cm, and the vector from \(A_2\) to \(A_2\) is the zero vector, so the sum of the moments is

\[(30, 0, 0)\times(0, 5, 0) + \vek 0\times(0, -5, 0) = (0, 0, 150)\ \text{N cm} ,\]

the same as about the centre. This is no coincidence.

Lemma 2.34 The moment does not depend on the point (moment ni odvisen od točke)

Let forces \(\vek F_1, \vek F_2, \dots, \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) act at points \(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\), and let their resultant be the zero vector, \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0\). Then for any two points \(O\) and \(Q\)

\[\overrightarrow{QA_1}\times\vek F_1 + \dots + \overrightarrow{QA_{\mathrm{k}}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \overrightarrow{OA_1}\times\vek F_1 + \dots + \overrightarrow{OA_{\mathrm{k}}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
Proof

We write each vector from \(Q\) to one of the points \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\) as a path through the point \(O\) and use the splitting of a sum.

  1. Let \(\vek d = \overrightarrow{QO}\). By (1.8), \(\overrightarrow{QA_1} = \vek d + \overrightarrow{OA_1}\), since for example \(x_{\mathrm{A}_1} - x_{\mathrm{Q}} = (x_{\mathrm{O}} - x_{\mathrm{Q}}) + (x_{\mathrm{A}_1} - x_{\mathrm{O}})\), and the same for the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\). The same holds for the other points.
  2. By item 2 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, \(\overrightarrow{QA_1}\times\vek F_1 = \vek d\times\vek F_1 + \overrightarrow{OA_1}\times\vek F_1\), and the same for the other forces.
  3. We add all \(k\) equalities. The terms with \(\vek d\) give, by the same item 2 applied to the second factor, \(\vek d\times\vek F_1 + \dots + \vek d\times\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek d\times(\vek F_1 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}) = \vek d\times\vek 0\).
  4. By item 3 of the same lemma, applied with the number zero, \(\vek d\times\vek 0 = \vek d\times(0\,\vek d) = 0\,(\vek d\times\vek d) = \vek 0\). What remains is the sum of the moments about \(O\).

Source: SADD §3.7 (PDF p. 78) says that the point about which the moments are computed can be chosen arbitrarily. The lemma and the proof are not from the sources.

So when the resultant of the forces is zero, the second condition of equilibrium does not depend on the choice of the point \(O\). If the sum of the moments is zero about one point, it is zero about every point. We can therefore choose the point that makes the computation shortest, for example one through which as many lines of action as possible pass, since forces on these lines have no moment. For the ruler the lemma says that the pair of forces \(\vek F\) and \(-\vek F\) turns it with the moment \((0, 0, 150)\) N cm no matter about which point we compute it.

2.8.4 Moments on a small cube

We now apply the second condition of equilibrium to the same small cube to which we applied the first condition in section 2.7. Let the body be in equilibrium and \(P\) a point in its interior. We put the origin of a right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system at \(P\). In thought we cut out of the body the cube with centre \(P\), side \(h\) and edges parallel to the axes. Its faces lie in the planes \(x = \pm h/2\), \(y = \pm h/2\) and \(z = \pm h/2\). The cube is a part of a body in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself (section 2.1), and by (2.35) the sum of the moments of all forces on it about \(P\) is the zero vector. Two kinds of forces act on the cube. The rest of the body acts on it across its six faces, and the body force (definition 2.26), for example the weight, acts on all its material. Its density \(\vek b\) is the force per unit volume.

We know the force across a face from the stress components. As in section 2.7 we write the point with the traction vector, \(\vek t(Q, \vek n)\), because we compare traction vectors at different points. On the face \(x = h/2\) the outward normal of the cube is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and at a point \(Q\) of this face the traction vector \(\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q), \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q), \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(Q)\big)\) acts per unit area (definition 2.25). On the opposite face \(x = -h/2\) the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut the traction at its point \(Q\) is \(\vek t(Q, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\). The same holds on the other four faces. The stress components change from point to point, so the force is not necessarily distributed evenly over a face.

We treat a distributed force as in sections 2.1 and 2.7, as the sum of many small forces on small patches. We divide a face of the cube by lines parallel to its sides into equal small rectangles. On a rectangle with the area \(\Delta A\) and the centre \(Q\) the force \(\vek t(Q)\,\Delta A\) acts at \(Q\), where \(\vek t(Q)\) is the force per unit area at \(Q\), on the face \(x = h/2\) therefore \(\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\). The moment of the distributed force is the sum of the moments of these small forces. In the same way we divide the volume of the cube into equal small cubes. This is a simplification, and in section 2.7 we took the same summation as an assumption about the forces on the faces of the cube. SADD and CM write the moment of a distributed force exactly, by exact adding up over a surface and over a body (SADD (3.7.6), PDF p. 78, CM (5.3.28), PDF p. 233), which we will meet in chapter 4. Until then we compute with sums. We need two facts about them, collected in lemma 2.35.

Lemma 2.35 Moment of a distributed force (moment porazdeljene sile)

Let a force act on a flat face with the area \(A\), equal per unit area to \(\vek t(Q)\) at the point \(Q\). We divide the face into small bits. On a bit with the area \(\Delta A\) the force \(\vek t(Q)\,\Delta A\) acts at a chosen point \(Q\) of the bit, and the moment of the distributed force about a point \(O\) is the sum of the moments of these forces. Then:

  1. If the face is a rectangle with the centre \(S\), divided by lines parallel to its sides into equal rectangles, the points \(Q\) are their centres, and the force is distributed evenly, \(\vek t(Q) = \vek t_0\) for all \(Q\), then its moment about \(O\) equals \(\overrightarrow{OS}\times(A\,\vek t_0)\), the moment of the resultant \(A\,\vek t_0\) acting at the centre \(S\).
  2. If \(|\vek t(Q)| \le c\) for all chosen points \(Q\) and every point of the face is at most \(r_{\max}\) away from \(O\), then every component of the moment is at most \(c\,A\,r_{\max}\) in absolute value.

Item 2 holds in the same way for a force distributed over a volume \(V\), equal per unit volume to \(\vek b(Q)\), with \(|\vek b(Q)| \le c\) at all chosen points \(Q\) of the bits of volume. Every component of its moment is then at most \(c\,V\,r_{\max}\).

Proof

We show both statements on the sum of the moments of the small forces.

1. An evenly distributed force.

  1. Lines parallel to the sides divide each side into equal parts, so the division is the same on both sides of the centre \(S\). A rectangle whose centre is moved from \(S\) by a vector \(\vek s\) has a partner whose centre is moved from \(S\) by \(-\vek s\). When the number of rectangles is odd in both directions, the middle rectangle has its centre at \(S\) and is its own partner.
  2. All rectangles have the same area \(\Delta A\), so the same force \(\vek f = \vek t_0\,\Delta A\) acts on each. The vectors from \(O\) to the centres of a pair are \(\overrightarrow{OS} + \vek s\) and \(\overrightarrow{OS} - \vek s\).
  3. By items 2 and 3 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product, the sum of the moments of the pair is
    \[(\overrightarrow{OS} + \vek s)\times\vek f + (\overrightarrow{OS} - \vek s)\times\vek f = 2\,\overrightarrow{OS}\times\vek f + \vek s\times\vek f - \vek s\times\vek f = 2\,\overrightarrow{OS}\times\vek f .\]
    The middle rectangle, if there is one, has the moment \(\overrightarrow{OS}\times\vek f\).
  4. Each rectangle therefore contributes \(\overrightarrow{OS}\times\vek f\) to the sum, as if its force acted at \(S\). By item 2 of the same lemma the sum is \(\overrightarrow{OS}\times(\vek f + \vek f + \dots + \vek f)\). The sum of the forces is \(\vek t_0\) times the sum of the areas, that is, \(A\,\vek t_0\).

2. A bound for the moment.

  1. Take one bit of the division with the chosen point \(Q\) and the force \(\vek f = \vek t(Q)\,\Delta A\), and let \(\vek r = \overrightarrow{OQ}\). By item 5 of the lemma on the properties of the vector product, \(|\vek r\times\vek f|^2 = |\vek r|^2\,|\vek f|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek f)^2 \le |\vek r|^2\,|\vek f|^2\), so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|\vek r\times\vek f| \le |\vek r|\,|\vek f|\).
  2. We have \(|\vek r| \le r_{\max}\), and by (1.10) \(|\vek f| = |\vek t(Q)|\,\Delta A \le c\,\Delta A\), because \(\Delta A\) is a positive number. Therefore \(|\vek r\times\vek f| \le c\,r_{\max}\,\Delta A\).
  3. Every component of a vector is at most its length in absolute value, since for example \(q_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le q_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + q_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + q_{\mathrm{z}}^2\), as in step 3 of the proof of Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24).
  4. A component of the moment of the distributed force is the sum of the same components of the moments of all bits. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) its absolute value is at most the sum of the absolute values, and by steps 2 and 3 this is at most \(c\,r_{\max}\) times the sum of the areas, that is, \(c\,A\,r_{\max}\).
  5. For a force distributed over a volume we repeat steps 1 to 4 with bits of volume \(\Delta V\) and forces \(\vek b(Q)\,\Delta V\).

Source: The lemma and the proof are not from the sources.

Item 1 says that an evenly distributed force turns a body as if all of it acted at the centre of the face (figure 2.44). The reason is symmetry. Every bit on one side of the centre has a partner on the other side, and the contributions of the vectors \(\vek s\) and \(-\vek s\) cancel in the sum of the moments. In section 2.7 we used the same idea of pairs of patches symmetric about the centre for the force across a face. Item 2 says that a small force per unit area on a small face near \(O\) gives a small moment.

First let us compute what the moment would be if the stress components at all points of the cube were the same as at \(P\). On the face \(x = h/2\) an evenly distributed force with the traction \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) per unit area would then act. The face is a square with the area \(h^2\) and the centre \((h/2, 0, 0)\), so by item 1 of lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force and by (2.32) its moment about \(P\) is

\[\Big(\frac h2, 0, 0\Big)\times h^2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) = \frac{h^3}{2}\,(0,\ -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}) .\]

On the opposite face \(x = -h/2\) both factors are opposite, the vector to the centre \((-h/2, 0, 0)\) and the force \(-h^2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\). By item 3 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product the signs cancel, \((-1)(-1) = 1\), and the moment is the same. We compute the other faces in the same way:

faces moment of each of the two faces
\(x = \pm h/2\) \(\tfrac{h^3}{2}\,(0,\ -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\)
\(y = \pm h/2\) \(\tfrac{h^3}{2}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}},\ 0,\ -\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}})\)
\(z = \pm h/2\) \(\tfrac{h^3}{2}\,(-\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}},\ 0)\)

We count each row twice, once for each face, so the sum of all six moments is

\[h^3\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}},\ \ \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\big) . \tag{2.36}\]

The normal components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) do not appear in the sum. Their forces point along the axis on which the centre of the face lies, so the line of action passes through \(P\), the lever arm is zero, and so is the moment (figure 2.45, right). Only the shear components appear. Let us look at the component \(z\). The forces \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}h^2\) on the faces \(x = \pm h/2\) point in opposite directions on opposite sides of \(P\), each with the lever arm \(h/2\), and turn the cube about the axis \(z\) counterclockwise. The forces \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}h^2\) on the faces \(y = \pm h/2\) turn it clockwise (figure 2.45, left and middle). The component \(z\) of the sum is zero only if the two pairs are equally strong, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\).

What about everything we left out? The moments in (2.36) are proportional to \(h^3\): the force is proportional to the area of the face \(h^2\) and the lever arm to half the side \(h/2\). The weight of the cube is proportional to its volume \(h^3\), and every bit of the cube is at most as far from \(P\) as a corner, \(\sqrt{3\,(h/2)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\). By item 2 of lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force the moment of the weight is therefore at most proportional to \(h^4\). The stress components also change across the cube. For a continuously differentiable field the difference between the value at a point of a face and the value at \(P\) is at most proportional to the distance, that is, to \(h\), which we will show in the proof below. The force due to this difference is therefore proportional to \(h \cdot h^2\), and its moment to \(h \cdot h^2 \cdot h = h^4\), with the size of the cube, the area and the lever arm. When we shrink the cube, \(h^4\) decreases faster than \(h^3\).

How much faster, let us show on a steel cube with \(\rho g = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\) N/m³, as for the tetrahedron in section 2.6.2. In it the density of the weight is the same everywhere in the cube, so the moment of the weight about the centre \(P\) is in fact exactly zero. We divide the cube into equal small cubes. A small cube whose centre is shifted from \(P\) by the vector \(\vek s\) has a partner whose centre is shifted by \(-\vek s\), the same force \(\vek f\) acts on both, and by item 3 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product the sum of their moments is \(\vek s\times\vek f + (-\vek s)\times\vek f = \vek 0\), as in item 1 of lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force. We need the bound of item 2 for a body force whose density varies across the cube, and the table shows how fast such a bound shrinks when the density is at most \(\rho g\) in size. Suppose \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) were larger than \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) by 1 MPa. By (2.36) the moment of the pairs would be \(h^3 \cdot 1\) MPa, and the bound for the moment of the body force, by the same item 2, the force \(\rho g\,h^3\) times the largest distance \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\). We computed with NumPy:

\(h\) \(h^3 \cdot 1\) MPa \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\rho g\,h^4\) ratio
1 mm \(1 \cdot 10^{-3}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-5}\)
0,1 mm \(1 \cdot 10^{-6}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-12}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,01 mm \(1 \cdot 10^{-9}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-16}\) N m \(6{,}627 \cdot 10^{-7}\)

When we decrease \(h\) by a factor of 10, the moment of the pairs decreases by a factor of 1000, the bound for the moment of the body force by a factor of 10 000, and their ratio falls by a factor of 10 (figure 2.46). In a small enough cube the body force cannot balance any difference between \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) other than zero. The same holds for the change of stress across the cube. In the hanging bar the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) changes over the distance \(h\) by \(\rho g\,h\), for \(h = 1\) mm by \(76{,}52\) Pa, and the moment that such a change gives is again at most proportional to \(h^4\). The only possibility left is that the differences in (2.36) are zero. This is the heart of the derivation, which we now write down precisely. As with the tetrahedron, two quantities shrink together, but at different rates, and their ratio decides.

2.8.5 Symmetry of stress

We state the theorem with the assumptions we used in section 2.7. The first is that the stress components are continuously differentiable fields (definition 1.90). We will need only what theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step uses of it, and we write that into the theorem. The second concerns the body force. Its density is to be bounded near \(P\). This holds for the weight, whose density has the size \(\rho g\) everywhere. It holds also for every body force density that is continuous at \(P\), since then near \(P\) each of its components differs from its value at \(P\) by less than 1 N/m³ (definition 1.67 with the error 1 N/m³). The third assumption is the way we compute the moments of distributed forces.

Theorem 2.36 Symmetry of stress (simetrija napetosti)

Let a body be in equilibrium, \(P\) a point in its interior and \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) the axes of a right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. Assume:

  1. every stress component is defined at all points that are less than some number \(r_1 > 0\) away from \(P\), has all three partial derivatives there, and these are continuous at \(P\),
  2. there are numbers \(B\) and \(r_0 > 0\) such that the body force density \(\vek b\) has a size of at most \(B\) at every point less than \(r_0\) away from \(P\),
  3. only forces across its surface and the body force act on any part of the body, and their moments are computed as sums of the moments of small forces, as in lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force, where a face that is a rectangle is divided by lines parallel to its sides into equal rectangles with the points at their centres.

Then at the point \(P\)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} . \tag{2.37}\]
Proof

We must show that the differences \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) at \(P\) are zero. We write the equilibrium of moments of a small cube with the centre \(P\). The moment given by the values of stress at \(P\) is proportional to \(h^3\), everything else at most proportional to \(h^4\).

  1. We put the origin at \(P\). For \(h > 0\) we take the cube with centre \(P\), side \(h\) and faces in the planes \(x = \pm h/2\), \(y = \pm h/2\) and \(z = \pm h/2\). Every point of the cube has coordinates that are at most \(h/2\) in absolute value, so by (1.10) it is at most \(\sqrt{3\,(h/2)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\) away from \(P\).
  2. We apply theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step to the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), with the error \(\eta_1 = 1\) Pa/m. Its hypotheses are assumption 1. So there is \(\delta_{\mathrm{xy}} > 0\) such that for every point \(Q\) with the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)\) and \(0 < |\overrightarrow{PQ}| < \delta_{\mathrm{xy}}\)
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(P) = \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x}\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y}\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial z}\,\Delta z + R , \qquad |R| \le \eta_1\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}| ,\]
    with the partial derivatives at \(P\).
  3. Each of the components \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\) is at most \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\) in absolute value. By the triangle inequality for numbers therefore
    \[|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(P)| \le G_{\mathrm{xy}}\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}| , \qquad G_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Big|\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x}\Big| + \Big|\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y}\Big| + \Big|\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial z}\Big| + \eta_1 .\]
    At \(Q = P\) the inequality holds because both sides are zero.
  4. We do the same for all nine stress components. Let \(G\) be the largest of the nine numbers \(G_{\mathrm{xx}}, G_{\mathrm{xy}}, \dots, G_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and \(\delta_1\) the smallest of the nine numbers \(\delta_{\mathrm{xx}}, \delta_{\mathrm{xy}}, \dots, \delta_{\mathrm{zz}}\). For every point \(Q\) with \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}| < \delta_1\) each stress component at \(Q\) then differs from its value at \(P\) by at most \(G\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).
  5. Since \(P\) is in the interior of the body, we can choose \(h\) so small that the whole cube lies in the body and that \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\) is smaller than \(\delta_1\) and than \(r_0\). By step 1 the bound of step 4 and assumption 2 then hold at every point of the cube.
  6. The cube is a part of a body in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself (section 2.1), and by (2.35) the sum of the moments of all forces on it about \(P\) is the zero vector. By assumption 3 these are the forces across the six faces and the body force.
  7. On the face \(x = h/2\) the outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the force per unit area at its point \(Q\) is the traction vector \(\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\) (definition 2.25). We write it as the sum \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}} + \vek q(Q)\), where \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) with the values at \(P\), and \(\vek q(Q)\) is the difference. By steps 4 and 5 each component of the difference is at most \(G\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\) in absolute value, so by (1.10) \(|\vek q(Q)| \le \sqrt3 \cdot G\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h = \tfrac32\,G\,h\).
  8. The force on each rectangle of the division of the face is the sum of the forces \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\,\Delta A\) and \(\vek q(Q)\,\Delta A\), so by item 2 of lemma 2.31 on the properties of the vector product the moment of the force on the face is the sum of two moments. The first is the moment of the evenly distributed force \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\), and by item 1 of lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force it equals \(\tfrac{h^3}{2}\,(0, -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\), as in the table before the theorem. The second is the moment of the force \(\vek q\). By item 2 of the same lemma with \(c = \tfrac32\,G\,h\), \(A = h^2\) and \(r_{\max} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\), each of its components is at most \(\tfrac32\,G\,h \cdot h^2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}\,G\,h^4\) in absolute value.
  9. We proceed in the same way on the other five faces. On the face \(x = -h/2\) the force per unit area is, by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(-\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and on the faces \(y = \pm h/2\) and \(z = \pm h/2\) it is \(\pm\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\pm\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). The moments of the even parts are in the table before the theorem, and their sum is (2.36). The moments of the differences are again at most \(\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}\,G\,h^4\) in each component.
  10. By step 5 the body force density has a size of at most \(B\) at every point of the cube. By item 2 of the lemma on the moment of a distributed force for the volume \(V = h^3\) and \(r_{\max} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h\), each component of its moment is at most \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,B\,h^4\) in absolute value.
  11. The component \(z\) of the sum of all moments is zero. It consists of the component \(z\) of the vector (2.36), \(h^3\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}})\), the six components of the moments of the differences from steps 8 and 9, and the component of the moment of the body force from step 10. So \(h^3\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}})\) is the negative of the sum of the other seven numbers, and by the triangle inequality for numbers
    \[h^3\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}| \le 6 \cdot \frac{3\sqrt3}{4}\,G\,h^4 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,B\,h^4 = K\,h^4 , \qquad K = \frac{9\sqrt3}{2}\,G + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,B .\]
  12. We divide the inequality by the positive number \(h^3\) and get \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}| \le K\,h\). This holds for every \(h > 0\) that satisfies step 5.
  13. We choose any number \(\eta > 0\). We choose \(h\) so that it satisfies step 5 and \(K\,h \le \eta\). For \(K > 0\) it is enough to require also \(h \le \eta/K\), for \(K = 0\) every \(h\) of step 5 will do. Then \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}| \le \eta\).
  14. So the nonnegative number \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}|\) is not larger than any positive number \(\eta\). By lemma 1.34 on an inequality tested with all positive numbers it is zero, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\).
  15. The components \(x\) and \(y\) of the sum of the moments give, by the same steps, \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}| \le K\,h\) and \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}| \le K\,h\), so also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\).

Source: SADD (3.7.7) (PDF p. 79), CM (5.3.31) (PDF p. 234), EP (2.2.17) (p. 54), BOWER §2.3.2 (PDF p. 78). SADD, CM and BOWER derive the theorem from the equilibrium of moments on a finite part of the body with integrals, EP with moments on a small box, much as we do. The proof with bounds is not from the sources.

Next to Cauchy's formula, this is the second result on which the whole theory of stress rests, so let us remember it. The proof showed why it holds. The shear forces on opposite faces of a small cube form pairs that turn the cube, and the moment of each pair is proportional to the area of the face and to the lever arm, that is, to \(h^3\). Everything else, the body force and the change of stress across the cube, gives moments at most proportional to \(h^4\). In a small cube the pairs of shear forces must therefore balance each other. The idea is the same as with the tetrahedron in section 2.6.2, where the weight disappeared because it shrank faster than the forces across the faces.

What does the theorem mean for the material? A shear component never appears alone. When a shear component in the direction of the axis \(y\) acts on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), an equally large shear component in the direction of the axis \(x\) acts on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) through the same point. In figure 2.45 the forces on the left and in the middle must therefore be equally large, and the cube does not turn.

Where does the theorem fail? Assumption 3 excludes bodies on which, besides forces, distributed moments act that are not moments of forces. CM and EP call them body couples (telesni momenti) and say that without them the second condition of equilibrium gives the symmetry of stress (CM §5.3.3, PDF pp. 232-234, EP §2.2.2, p. 54). For materials whose inner structure carries such moments too, CM writes additional equations (CM §5.3.3.2, PDF p. 234). We do not treat such materials in this book. Assumption 1 excludes points at which the stress changes by a jump. We proved the theorem for a body in equilibrium. CM derives the symmetry also for a body that moves (CM (5.3.28)-(5.3.31), PDF pp. 233-234), much as in Cauchy's formula the term with the acceleration disappeared in the same way as the weight.

2.8.6 Pure shear again

Let us return to the cube in pure shear of section 2.6.6 (figure 2.28). The cube has the side \(s\), the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(z\) up, and the axis \(y\) into the picture, away from the viewer, so that the system is right-handed. We pull the top face to the right and the bottom face to the left with the force \(\tau_0\) per unit area. We pull the right face up and the left face down, now with the force \(\tau_1\) per unit area, and ask how large \(\tau_1\) must be. The first condition of equilibrium does not tell, since for every \(\tau_1\) the forces on the top and the bottom face are opposite, and so are the forces on the side faces.

The forces are distributed evenly over the faces, so by item 1 of lemma 2.35 on the moment of a distributed force each of them turns the cube as if it acted at the centre of its face. We put the origin at the centre \(P\) of the cube (figure 2.47). The force on the top face is \(\tau_0 s^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \((0, 0, s/2)\), on the bottom face \(-\tau_0 s^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \((0, 0, -s/2)\), on the right face \(\tau_1 s^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the point \((s/2, 0, 0)\) and on the left face \(-\tau_1 s^2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the point \((-s/2, 0, 0)\). By (2.32) their moments about \(P\) are

\[\begin{aligned} \Big(0, 0, \frac s2\Big)\times(\tau_0 s^2, 0, 0) &= \Big(0,\ \frac{\tau_0 s^3}{2},\ 0\Big) , \\ \Big(0, 0, -\frac s2\Big)\times(-\tau_0 s^2, 0, 0) &= \Big(0,\ \frac{\tau_0 s^3}{2},\ 0\Big) , \\ \Big(\frac s2, 0, 0\Big)\times(0, 0, \tau_1 s^2) &= \Big(0,\ -\frac{\tau_1 s^3}{2},\ 0\Big) , \\ \Big(-\frac s2, 0, 0\Big)\times(0, 0, -\tau_1 s^2) &= \Big(0,\ -\frac{\tau_1 s^3}{2},\ 0\Big) . \end{aligned}\]

The sum is \(\big(0,\ (\tau_0 - \tau_1)\,s^3,\ 0\big)\). The second condition of equilibrium (2.35) requires it to be zero, so \(\tau_1 = \tau_0\). This is what we promised in section 2.6.6: the cube is in equilibrium exactly when all four forces per unit area are equally large. The moment of the pair on the top and the bottom face points in the direction of the axis \(y\), into the picture, and the viewer sees the cube turn clockwise. The moment of the pair on the side faces points out of the picture, and this pair turns the cube the other way. For \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa and \(s = 10\) mm the force on the top face is \(\tau_0 s^2 = 50 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 = 5000\) N, and the moment of the pair is \(\tau_0 s^3 = 50 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3 = 50\) N m. Without the forces on the side faces a moment of 50 N m would turn the cube.

In terms of stress components, \(\tau_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), the component \(x\) of the traction on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\tau_1 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), the component \(z\) of the traction on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The condition \(\tau_1 = \tau_0\) is therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), the symmetry of stress (2.37) in this example. On the ruler, two forces with zero resultant formed a pair that turns the ruler. On the cube in pure shear there are two such pairs, and they balance each other.

Theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress says that among the nine stress components at a point at most six are different: three normal ones, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and three shear ones, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) (SADD, PDF p. 79, EP, p. 54). The same is true of strain, which at a point is described by six numbers (definition 1.115). The reason, though, is different. For strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\) holds by definition, because both are half of the same sum \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\). For stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) are components of forces on two different faces, and they are equal because of a law of mechanics, the second condition of equilibrium.

The table of the nine numbers of section 2.6.5, in which each row is one traction vector, is therefore symmetric about the diagonal:

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} & x & y & z \\ \hline \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{array}\]

As a matrix (definition 1.93) it equals its transposed matrix, so it is symmetric (definition 1.102). For Cauchy's formula this means a simplification. By (2.24) we got the component \(x\) of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) from the column \(x\) of the table, \(t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Because of the symmetry the column equals the row of the same name, and

\[\begin{aligned} t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{y}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{z}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned}\]

With this the difference between our order of indices and that of CM, which exchanges the two indices (section 2.6.5), also disappears, since exchanging the indices does not change a stress component.

Of the three questions at the end of section 2.6.6 we have answered the last. The first two remain: how the six numbers change when we turn the axes, and on which face through the point the normal stress is largest. We take up the first in the next section, where we write the table of stress as a matrix and use it to turn the axes.
