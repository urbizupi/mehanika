Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.6 Stress on a face of any tilt

At the end of the previous section we asked whether we have to know the traction vector at a point separately for each of the infinitely many tilts of the face. The answer is no. Three traction vectors are enough, on three faces through the point that are perpendicular to the axes of the coordinate system. From them every other traction vector is computed with a simple formula. First we will notice the formula in the bar and see on a small wedge cut from it why it holds. Then we will cut a small tetrahedron out of an arbitrary body and show from its equilibrium that the formula holds in every body. At the end we will write the three vectors with nine numbers and try the formula on three examples.

2.6.1 A hint from the bar

Take the bar from section 2.4, pulled by the axial force \(N\). The axis \(x\) lies along the bar, the axis \(y\) points up and the axis \(z\) towards us, as in figure 2.39. The stress in the cross section is \(\sigma = N/A\). Let the point \(P\) be far from the ends of the bar. At it we know the traction vectors on three faces that are perpendicular to the axes.

  1. On the cross section the unit normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by (2.10) at \(\theta = 0\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) through \(P\) is the lengthwise cut at \(\theta = 90^\circ\), which divides the bar into an upper and a lower part. At the end of section 2.4 we saw that no force has to be carried across such a cut, since each part carries a share of the force proportional to its area, like the thin lengthwise bars in section 2.2. This only says that the sum of the forces across the cut can be zero. For the bar we assume more: no force acts across the lengthwise cut at any point, so \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\). This assumption holds only far from the ends, which is why we chose the point \(P\) there. Why it fails at an end is shown by the end of the bar (figure 2.62, left). At the grips the force \(N\) is concentrated on a smaller part of the cross section (section 2.2), in the figure on the middle third. The upper lengthwise strip lies outside that part, and the lengthwise cut with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) separates it from the rest of the bar. Let us draw the strip alone (right). At the end no force acts on it. Far from the end the rest of the bar pulls it with its share of the force, proportional to its area. The strip is at rest, so another force must balance this share. At the top, the front and the back the strip is bounded by the surface of the bar, on which nothing acts, so only the neighbouring strip, across the lengthwise cut, can give it this force. So near the end \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) \neq \vek 0\). The books give \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\) in their solutions for a bar pulled evenly at its ends (SADD Example 8.1, (8.1.10), PDF p. 169) and for the hanging bar (CM (7.3.17), PDF p. 305). SADD adds that for a bar whose ends are in fact pulled unevenly the solution holds only away from the ends.
  3. The face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) through \(P\) is the lengthwise cut that divides the bar into a front and a back part. The argument is the same: each part carries a share of the force proportional to its area, and no force has to be carried across the cut. With the same assumption, also \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0\).

On the oblique cut with the normal \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), though, the traction vector is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (section 2.5). The component \(x\) of the normal is \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\), so we can write this vector with the three vectors from the list:

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek 0 + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek 0 = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

The traction vector on the oblique cut is the sum of the traction vectors on three faces perpendicular to the axes, and each of them is multiplied by the corresponding component of the normal. Is this a coincidence that holds only for the bar?

What holds this sum together is shown by a small wedge that we cut in thought out of the bar at the point \(P\) (figure 2.63, on the left the wedge in the bar, on the right enlarged). The wedge is bounded by five plane faces: the cross section through \(P\), the lengthwise cut through \(P\) with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), the oblique cut with the normal \(\vek n\) a little away from \(P\), and two faces perpendicular to the axis \(z\), at the front and at the back. A piece of a body with such faces, two equal triangles and three rectangles, is called a triangular prism (tristrana prizma). Which forces act on the wedge?

  1. The wedge lies on the side of larger \(x\) of the cross section, so the outward normal of the wedge on this face is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). By lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, the traction vector there is \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The face has the area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force is distributed over it evenly, and the rest of the bar acts on it with the force \(-\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points to the left.
  2. On the oblique face with the area \(\Delta A\) the outward normal is \(\vek n\), and the rest of the bar acts on it with the force \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points to the right. The angle \(\theta\) is the angle between the cross section and the oblique face.
  3. On the lengthwise cut with the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and on the faces at the front and at the back the traction vectors are zero, so there are no forces there. We neglect the weight of the wedge. We may, since the weight is proportional to the volume of the wedge, while the two forces on the faces are proportional to the areas: if we halve the wedge, the weight falls to an eighth, the forces only to a quarter. For a wedge whose cross-sectional face is 1 mm high, with the angle \(\theta\) \(= 30^\circ\), in a steel bar of density 7800 kg/m³ and stress \(0{,}765\) MPa (the top of the hanging bar), the weight is \(0{,}003\) % of the force on the cross-sectional face, and for a smaller wedge even less. For a large piece, for example the whole hanging bar, the weight may not be neglected, since there it is the only load. The neglect removes the weight term from the sum of forces in step 4.
  4. The wedge is part of a bar in equilibrium: the bar is at rest, so every piece of it is at rest, and by Newton's laws the forces on a piece at rest add up to zero. The forces from steps 1 and 2 are therefore equal and opposite. For \(\sigma \neq 0\), \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A = \sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\) gives
    \[\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\,\Delta A = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A .\]

So the equilibrium of the wedge is consistent with the ratio of areas we found in (2.9), which gave the factor \(\cos\theta\) in step 2. In (2.9) the cross section with the area \(A\) is smaller than the oblique cut with the area \(A_\theta\) by the factor \(\cos\theta\). The cross-sectional face of the wedge is the shadow of the oblique face cast by light directed along the axis \(x\), and the shadow has an area smaller by the factor \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.44). So the equilibrium of the wedge says this. The force on the oblique face must balance the forces on the other faces. These forces are traction vectors times areas, and the areas are the area of the oblique face times components of the normal. When we divide the force on the oblique face by its area, we get the traction vectors multiplied by components of the normal. This argument does not need the bar. It needs only a small piece of a body with faces in the right ratio, and equilibrium. Let us carry it out for every body.

2.6.2 A small tetrahedron

In a general body we do not know in advance what the traction vectors are, nor whether they are the same over the faces. So we take a piece of the body that is so small that the traction vector over each of its faces differs only a little from the traction vector at the point \(P\) for the same normal. In the bar the normal \(\vek n\) lay in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), so besides the oblique face we closed the wedge at \(P\) with two faces through \(P\) perpendicular to the axes \(x\) and \(y\), and with the faces at the front and at the back, perpendicular to the axis \(z\). Only the face perpendicular to the axis \(x\) carried force, because \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) are zero. For a normal with three components different from zero we need three faces through \(P\) perpendicular to the axes. Why three? A plane with such a normal that does not pass through \(P\) cuts all three axes, so it closes off a piece of the body at \(P\) only when we add a face in each of the three coordinate planes. The piece of the body is then a tetrahedron (tetraeder), a body with four plane triangular faces, or in other words a pyramid with a triangular base.

Let the body be in equilibrium and \(P\) a point in its interior, that is, a point around which there is still some material of the body in all directions. We put the origin of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system at \(P\). Let \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) be a unit vector that has, for now, all three components positive. We choose a small positive number \(h\) and on the axes the points

\[A = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad B = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad C = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{z}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{2.20}\]

The points \(P\), \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are the vertices (oglišča) of the tetrahedron \(PABC\), and its faces are the triangles \(PBC\), \(PCA\), \(PAB\) and \(ABC\) (figure 2.64). The vertices lie on the axes, and the faces at \(P\) lie in the coordinate planes. The edges \(PA\), \(PB\) and \(PC\) are dashed in the figure, because the face \(ABC\) hides them. The first three faces lie in planes perpendicular to the axes. The triangle \(PBC\) lies in the plane \(x = 0\), because all three vertices have the coordinate \(x\) equal to zero, \(PCA\) in the plane \(y = 0\) and \(PAB\) in the plane \(z = 0\). Why did we put the vertices \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) exactly there? Because then \(\vek n\) is the normal of the fourth face \(ABC\), as we will show below. And because all distances between the vertices are proportional to \(h\). When we make \(h\) smaller, the tetrahedron shrinks towards \(P\) while its shape stays the same (figure 2.67, right).

For the equilibrium we will need the areas of the faces and the volume of the tetrahedron. The next lemma expresses them with the area of the face \(ABC\) and with the components of the normal. The idea of the proof is simple: we compute the volume of the tetrahedron in two ways, once with the base \(ABC\), once with the base \(PBC\), and set the two values equal.

Lemma 2.24 Faces and volume of the tetrahedron (ploskve in prostornina tetraedra)

Let \(P\) be the origin of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) a unit vector with three positive components and \(h > 0\). For the tetrahedron \(PABC\) with the vertices (2.20):

  1. \(\vek n\) is a unit normal of the face \(ABC\).
  2. The height of the tetrahedron on the face \(ABC\), the distance from \(P\) to the plane of this face, is \(h\), and \(\vek n\) points away from \(P\), out of the tetrahedron.
  3. The areas of the faces \(PBC\), \(PCA\) and \(PAB\) are
    \[\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A , \qquad \Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A , \qquad \Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\Delta A , \tag{2.21}\]
    where \(\Delta A\) is the area of the face \(ABC\).
  4. The volume of the tetrahedron is \(\Delta V = h\,\Delta A/3\).
Proof

We compute items 1 and 2 with the scalar product, and items 3 and 4 from the school formula for the volume of a pyramid, which we use twice.

1. The normal of the face \(ABC\).

  1. By (1.8) the vectors from \(A\) to \(B\) and from \(A\) to \(C\) are \(B - A = (-h/n_{\mathrm{x}},\ h/n_{\mathrm{y}},\ 0)\) and \(C - A = (-h/n_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0,\ h/n_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  2. By (2.6), \(\vek n\)\(\cdot(B - A) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(-h/n_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = -h + h = 0\). Likewise \(\vek n\cdot(C - A) = -h + h = 0\). So we drew figure 2.65 as we see it along \(\vek n\): \(\vek n\) points out of the picture towards us, perpendicular to both blue arrows.
  3. The points of the plane through \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are the points \(A + s\,(B - A) + w\,(C - A)\) for arbitrary numbers \(s\) and \(w\), just as the points of the oblique cut of the bar were the points \(O + s\,\vek d + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (section 2.4). The triangle \(ABC\) consists of those among them for which \(s \ge 0\), \(w \ge 0\) and \(s + w \le 1\).
  4. The vector between the points of the plane at the numbers \(s_1, w_1\) and \(s_2, w_2\) is, by the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15), \((s_2 - s_1)\,(B - A) + (w_2 - w_1)\,(C - A)\). By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) and by step 2, its scalar product with \(\vek n\) is \((s_2 - s_1) \cdot 0 + (w_2 - w_1) \cdot 0 = 0\).
  5. The vector \(\vek n\) is a unit vector and perpendicular to every vector between two points of the face \(ABC\), so it is a unit normal of this face (definition 2.17).

2. The height and the direction of the normal.

  1. Let \(F\)\( = P + h\,\vek n\). We show that \(F\) is a point of the plane from step 3 of item 1, namely at \(s = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) and \(w = n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). The component \(x\) of the point \(A + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\,(B - A) + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\,(C - A)\) is
    \[\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} - n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\,\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} - n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\,\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} = \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\big(1 - n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 - n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big) = \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]
    because \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = |\vek n|^2 = 1\). The component \(y\) is \(n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 \cdot h/n_{\mathrm{y}} = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the component \(z\) is \(n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 \cdot h/n_{\mathrm{z}} = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the point is \(P + h\,\vek n = F\).
  2. Since \(s + w = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = 1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 < 1\), the point \(F\) even lies on the triangle \(ABC\).
  3. The vector from \(F\) to \(P\) is \(-h\,\vek n\). By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product and by step 4 of item 1 it is perpendicular to every vector between points of the plane, so the segment \(PF\) is perpendicular to the plane of the face \(ABC\). Its length is \(|{-h\,\vek n}| = h\,|\vek n| = h\). This is the distance from \(P\) to the plane, the height of the tetrahedron on the face \(ABC\).
  4. From \(P = F - h\,\vek n\) we see that we reach \(P\) if we move from a point of the face by \(h\) in the direction opposite to the normal. So the normal \(\vek n\) points away from \(P\), out of the tetrahedron.

3. and 4. The areas and the volume.

  1. A tetrahedron is a pyramid with a triangular base. The volume of a pyramid is, from school, one third of the product of the area of the base and the height, the distance from the opposite vertex to the plane of the base. CM (4.2.5) writes exactly this formula for our tetrahedron. We may take any of the four faces as the base, so we stand the same tetrahedron on two of them (figure 2.66).
  2. For the base \(ABC\) the opposite vertex is \(P\), and by item 2 the height is \(h\). So \(\Delta V = \tfrac13\,h\,\Delta A\). This is item 4.
  3. For the base \(PBC\) in the plane \(x = 0\) the opposite vertex is \(A\). The vector from \(P\) to \(A\) is \((h/n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Every vector between two points of the plane \(x = 0\) has the component \(x\) equal to zero, so it has the form \((0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\). The segment \(PA\) is therefore perpendicular to the plane \(x = 0\), and the height is its length \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\). So \(\Delta V = \tfrac13\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  4. The two values of the volume are equal, \(\tfrac13\,h\,\Delta A = \tfrac13\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\). We multiply both sides by the positive number \(3n_{\mathrm{x}}/h\) and get \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A\).
  5. We do the same with the base \(PCA\), which has the height \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and with the base \(PAB\), which has the height \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}}\). We get \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A\) and \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\Delta A\).

Source: CM (4.2.4), (4.2.5) (PDF pp. 181-182), BOWER, Appendix E.1 (PDF pp. 792-793). CM gets the ratios of the areas from a theorem on closed surfaces, BOWER with the vector product. The proof that computes the volume twice is not from the sources.

Item 3 is the ratio we noticed on the wedge, and it also has a picture without calculation (figure 2.67, left). We light the tetrahedron along the axis \(x\), so that the rays run parallel to the axis \(x\) towards the plane \(x = 0\). The slanted face \(ABC\) casts a shadow on this plane. The vertex \(A\) falls onto \(P\), and the vertices \(B\) and \(C\) stay where they are, because they already lie in the plane \(x = 0\). The shadow is the face \(PBC\), and by item 3 its area is smaller by the factor \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\). The extreme cases show that this is right. If the face is turned straight towards the rays, then \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), and the shadow is as large as the face. If the face lies along the rays, then \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and the shadow is only a segment with area zero. In between the factor is \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\).

Let us test the lemma on numbers. Let \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), which is a unit vector since \(\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac49 = 1\), and \(h = 1\) mm. The vertices are at the distances \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}} = 3\) mm, \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}} = 1{,}5\) mm and \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}} = 1{,}5\) mm from \(P\). The face \(PBC\) is a right triangle with legs of \(1{,}5\) mm, so it has the area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 1{,}5/2 = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^2\), and the faces \(PCA\) and \(PAB\) have \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = \Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 3/2 = 2{,}25\ \text{mm}^2\). By item 3, \(\Delta A = \Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/n_{\mathrm{x}} = 3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\), and indeed \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}/n_{\mathrm{y}} = 2{,}25 \cdot \tfrac32 = 3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\) too. By item 4 the volume is \(\Delta V = 1 \cdot 3{,}375/3 = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^3\), which is also one third of the product of \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^2\) and the height \(|PA| = 3\) mm.

Now let us write the equilibrium of the tetrahedron (figure 2.68). Four forces across the faces and the weight act on it. We denote by \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}\) the force with which the rest of the body acts on the tetrahedron across the face \(ABC\). The forces across the faces \(PBC\), \(PCA\) and \(PAB\), whose outward normals are \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), we denote by \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}\). The index tells the outward normal of the face. Let the material have the same density \(\rho\) everywhere near \(P\), as in the theorem below. The mass of the tetrahedron is then \(\rho\,\Delta V\), and by (2.1) its weight is a vector \(\Delta\vek W\) of size \(\rho g\,\Delta V\), pointing down. The tetrahedron is part of a body in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself (section 2.1), and by (2.2)

\[\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}} + \Delta\vek W = \vek 0 . \tag{2.22}\]

What happens when we shrink the tetrahedron towards \(P\)? The forces across the faces are roughly traction vectors times areas, and the areas are proportional to \(h^2\). The weight is proportional to the volume, and the volume to \(h^3\). For a steel tetrahedron with the normal \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\) and \(\rho g = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\ \text{N/m}^3\) we computed this with NumPy:

\(h\) \(\Delta A\) \(\Delta V\) \(\vert\Delta\vek W\vert\) \(\vert\Delta\vek W\vert/\Delta A\)
1 mm \(3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(1{,}125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-5}\) N \(25{,}51\) Pa
0,1 mm \(0{,}03375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(0{,}001125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N \(2{,}551\) Pa
0,01 mm \(0{,}0003375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(0{,}000001125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-11}\) N \(0{,}2551\) Pa

Why so differently? On the right of figure 2.67, with the same axes and the same \(P\), the \(h\) is halved. Every edge is half as long, and since both directions of the slanted face are halved, the smaller face has a quarter of the area of the larger one. The volume has three directions, so it falls to an eighth. When we make \(h\) smaller by the factor 10, the area therefore becomes smaller by the factor \(10 \cdot 10 = 100\), but the volume and the weight by the factor \(10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 1000\). The weight per unit area of the slanted face is, by item 4 of the lemma, \(\rho g\,\Delta V/\Delta A = \rho g h/3\), and it falls towards zero as \(h\) gets smaller, from \(25{,}51\) Pa at \(h = 1\) mm to \(0{,}2551\) Pa at \(h = 0{,}01\) mm. Stresses in steel are measured in millions of pascals. The forces across the faces per unit area, however, do not get smaller towards zero, because, as we will assume below, they are close to the traction vectors at \(P\). For a small tetrahedron the weight therefore no longer counts, and the equilibrium relates only the forces across the faces. This is the heart of the derivation. As with the derivative, two quantities get smaller together, but at different rates, and their ratio is what decides.

To write this precisely, we divide equation (2.22) by the area \(\Delta A\). We divide the forces across the faces by the areas. We write the quotient \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A\) as \((\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A)\,(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}})\), and by (2.21), \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A = n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). We get

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}}{\Delta A} + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}} + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}} + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}}} + \frac{\Delta\vek W}{\Delta A} = \vek 0 . \tag{2.23}\]

Each of the first four quotients is the force across a face divided by the area of that face, that is, the average force per unit area on the face, like the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) in definition 2.20 of the traction vector.

Where do these quotients go as \(h\) approaches zero? We expect each of them to approach the traction vector at \(P\) for the outward normal of its face: \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}/\Delta A\) the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and so on. We cannot derive this from the definition of the traction vector. The definition speaks about square patches centred at \(P\), but the faces of the tetrahedron are triangles, three of them have \(P\) at a vertex, and the face \(ABC\) does not contain the point \(P\) at all, since it is the height \(h\) away from it.

The expectation rests on the assumption that the traction vector changes continuously from point to point, without jumps (figure 2.69, left). In a piece of material we draw a face through \(P\) from the side, as a line. At its points we draw the force per unit area with which the material on the right pulls the material on the left. Since it changes continuously, all the arrows are close to the one at \(P\), which has 20 MPa, and so is the average. It does not matter whether the piece of the face has \(P\) in its middle, like the patch of the definition, or at a corner, like the faces of the tetrahedron. At the drawn size \(h\) the two averages are 20 MPa and 20,5 MPa, and as \(h\) gets smaller both approach 20 MPa. CM assumes that in a continuum all quantities, stresses included, vary continuously (CM §1.1, PDF p. 30), and then simply shrinks the tetrahedron to a point (CM, PDF p. 182). We too will take the expectation as an assumption. It holds in matter that varies smoothly near \(P\).

The assumption does not hold at a point where a point force acts, at the tip of a crack or at the contact of two materials, where the stress changes abruptly (section 2.7.8). On the right of figure 2.69 two materials are bonded along a horizontal line through \(P\), and the face cuts across both. Above the joint the force per unit area is 30 MPa, below it 10 MPa, so it jumps at the joint. The piece of the face with its centre at \(P\) has, for every \(h\), half of its area above the joint and half below, so its average is 20 MPa. The piece that has \(P\) at a corner and lies above the joint has the average 30 MPa for every \(h\). The error caused by the shape of the face is 10 MPa here, half of 20 MPa, and it does not vanish as \(h\) gets smaller. At such a point the quotients therefore need not approach the traction vectors at \(P\), and the proof below does not hold there.

2.6.3 Mathematics: the limit of a sum

In the last step we will let \(h\) approach zero and conclude from equation (2.23), which holds for every \(h\), an equation between limits. On the left stands a sum of five vectors, and three of them are multiplied by the numbers \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\). So we need the rule that the limit of such a sum equals the sum of the limits, multiplied by the same numbers. We compute the limit of a vector by components (section 2.5), so it is enough to prove the rule for functions whose input is a number \(h\) and whose output is one number.

The idea is the same as for the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7). The difference between the sum and the sum of the limits is a sum of differences, each of which is small. If there are \(k\) differences, we allow each of them a \(k\)-th part of the error (figure 2.70 for \(k = 3\)).

Lemma 2.25 Limit of a sum (limita vsote)

Let the functions \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{\mathrm{k}}\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and let \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g_{\mathrm{i}}(h) = d_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, k\). Then for all numbers \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_{\mathrm{k}}\)

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \big(c_1\,g_1(h) + c_2\,g_2(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}}\,g_{\mathrm{k}}(h)\big) = c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}} . \tag{2.24}\]

The same holds for the limit from the right, when the functions are defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < r\).

Proof

We write the definition of the limit 1.5 for the sum and the number \(d = c_1 d_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}}\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|c_1 g_1(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. We choose an arbitrary error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. For each \(i\) we take the error \(\eta_{\mathrm{i}} = \eta/\big(k\,(|c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1)\big)\). The added one makes sure that we do not divide by zero when \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). Since \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the limit of the function \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\), there is a band \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) such that \(|g_{\mathrm{i}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{i}}| < \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. For \(\delta\) we take the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1, \dots, \delta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(r\). The number \(r\) is included so that all the functions are defined in the band, as in the proof of the uniqueness of the limit. For every \(h\) on the axis with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) all inequalities from step 2 hold at once.
  4. We rearrange the difference between the sum and the number \(d\) by the rules of arithmetic with numbers: \(c_1 g_1(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d = c_1\,(g_1(h) - d_1) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}}\,(g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  5. By the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\), used \(k - 1\) times, and by the rule \(|c\,a| = |c|\,|a|\) (lemma 1.6, items 1 and 2), the absolute value of this sum is at most \(|c_1|\,|g_1(h) - d_1| + \dots + |c_{\mathrm{k}}|\,|g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{k}}|\).
  6. By step 2 each term \(e_{\mathrm{i}} = |c_{\mathrm{i}}|\,|g_{\mathrm{i}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{i}}|\) is at most \(|c_{\mathrm{i}}|\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}} = \dfrac{|c_{\mathrm{i}}|}{|c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1} \cdot \dfrac{\eta}{k}\), which is less than \(\eta/k\), because \(|c_{\mathrm{i}}| < |c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1\). Each term is shorter than its part.
  7. There are \(k\) terms, so the sum is less than \(k \cdot \eta/k = \eta\). The condition of the definition of the limit is satisfied.
  8. For the right-hand limit we repeat all the steps with the band \(0 < h < \delta\) instead of \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Cor. 3.1.12 (PDF p. 118), for two functions and without the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). The source leaves the proof to the reader. The proof that divides the error is not from the source.

The lemma holds for each component of a vector separately, so it also holds for a sum of vectors multiplied by numbers. We will also need that a function which is zero for every \(h\) has the limit zero. The definition of the limit confirms it: the difference \(|0 - 0| = 0\) is smaller than every error \(\eta\), for every band \(\delta\).

2.6.4 Cauchy's formula

Now we can state and prove the main result of the section.

Theorem 2.26 Cauchy's formula (Cauchyjeva formula)

Let a body be in equilibrium, \(P\) a point in its interior and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the unit vectors of the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. Let the weight be the only force that acts on all the matter of the body, and let the matter near \(P\) have the same density \(\rho\) everywhere. Assume that the average force per unit area on a plane face approaches the traction vector at \(P\) for the outward normal of this face when we shrink the face so that all its points come ever closer to \(P\). Then for every unit normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) at the point \(P\)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) . \tag{2.25}\]
Proof

We prove the formula first for a normal with three positive components, then for a normal with negative components, and finally for a normal with some component equal to zero.

1. Three positive components.

  1. For every \(h > 0\) we take the tetrahedron \(PABC\) with the vertices (2.20). The point \(P\) is in the interior of the body, so for a small enough \(h\) the whole tetrahedron lies in the body. It is in equilibrium, so the four forces across the faces and the weight balance, (2.22).
  2. We divide the equation by the area \(\Delta A\) and use the ratios of areas (2.21). We get (2.23), which holds for every small enough \(h > 0\).
  3. By item 4 of lemma 2.24 on the faces and volume of the tetrahedron, the last term has the length \(\rho g\,\Delta V/\Delta A = \rho g h/3\). Each component of a vector is in absolute value at most its length, since for example \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). For an error \(\eta > 0\) we take \(\delta = 3\eta/(\rho g)\). For \(0 < h < \delta\) each component of the last term is in absolute value less than \(\rho g\,\delta/3 = \eta\), so the right-hand limit of the last term is the zero vector. Figure 2.71 shows this for steel, \(\rho g = 76\,518\) N/m³. On the horizontal axis we plot \(h\), on the vertical one a component of the last term. Every component lies between the lines \(\rho g h/3\) and \(-\rho g h/3\), which open out from the origin. The lines leave the error band \(\eta = 51\) Pa only at \(h = \delta = 2{,}0\) mm, so for every smaller \(h\) both lines and everything between them lie in the band. For the narrower band \(\eta/2\) the \(\delta\) is half as large, \(1{,}0\) mm, and for every band, however narrow, we find such a \(\delta\).
  4. Every point of the tetrahedron is \(P + a\,(A - P) + b\,(B - P) + c\,(C - P)\) with \(a, b, c \ge 0\) and \(a + b + c \le 1\), so by the triangle inequality its distance from \(P\) is at most \((a + b + c)\) times the largest of the distances \(|PA|\), \(|PB|\), \(|PC|\). So the points of the tetrahedron are at most as far from \(P\) as the largest of the numbers \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}}\). As \(h\) approaches zero, all points of all four faces therefore come ever closer to \(P\). By the assumption the quotients approach the traction vectors at \(P\): \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}/\Delta A\) the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  5. By lemma 2.25 on the limit of a sum, used on each component separately, the left side of (2.23) approaches the vector \(\vek t(\vek n) + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) + \vek 0\).
  6. The left side is the zero vector for every \(h\), so its limit is also the zero vector. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7), which also holds for the right-hand limit (step 5 of the proof of lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut), the limits from steps 5 and 6 are equal:
    \[\vek t(\vek n) + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0 .\]
  7. By lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\)\( = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). We substitute and move three terms to the right side. We get (2.25).

2. Negative components, none of them zero.

  1. Let, for example, \(n_{\mathrm{x}} < 0\), so the normal \(\vek n\) points to the negative side of the axis \(x\). We take the coordinate system with the same origin and the same axes, only on the axis \(x\) we choose the opposite positive direction. This is again a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, since definition 1.12 requires only that a positive direction is chosen on each axis. The system may be left-handed, which is allowed here, because the proof of item 1 uses only the scalar product, lengths and the volume of a pyramid, none of which depends on handedness. Its unit vector of the axis \(x\) is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The component of the vector \(\vek n\) along the new axis \(x\) is its projection onto \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 2.13), that is, \(\vek n\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -n_{\mathrm{x}} > 0\), the opposite of the projection onto the old axis. The other two components stay \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. In the same way we reverse every axis along which \(\vek n\) has a negative component. In the new system \(\vek n\) has all three components positive, and by item 1, for example with the reversed axis \(x\),
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = (-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]
  4. By the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \((-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\big({-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})}\big) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\). Likewise for every other reversed axis. So in the old system the formula has the form (2.25).

3. Components that are zero.

  1. If two components are zero, \(\vek n\) is \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the formula reads \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and for \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it reads \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), which is the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut. Likewise for \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. Let only one component be zero, for example \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and let the other two be positive by item 2. Then the plane through \(A\) and \(B\) with the normal \(\vek n\) does not meet the axis \(z\), and there is no tetrahedron. Instead of it we take a triangular prism, like the wedge in figure 2.63, and draw it in figure 2.73 along the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) with the origin at \(P\). One of its equal triangular faces is the triangle \(PAB\) in the plane \(z = 0\), with \(A\) and \(B\) as in (2.20), so \(|PA| = h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(|PB| = h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\). The other is the same triangle moved by \(h\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Between them are three rectangular faces: the slanted one with the normal \(\vek n\) and two at \(P\), in the planes \(x = 0\) and \(y = 0\).
  3. By the Pythagorean theorem the side \(AB\) has the length \(\sqrt{h^2/n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + h^2/n_{\mathrm{y}}^2} = h\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{x}}^2}/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}) = h/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\), because \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = 1\). The slanted face is a rectangle with the sides \(h/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(h\), so \(\Delta A = h^2/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\). The normal \(\vek n\) is perpendicular to \(B - A\) as in item 1 of the lemma and to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so it is a unit normal of this face. It points out of the prism. Every point of the prism is \(X = P + a\,(A - P) + b\,(B - P) + c\,h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with \(a, b \ge 0\), \(a + b \le 1\) and \(0 \le c \le 1\), so \(\vek n\cdot(X - P) = (a + b)\,h \le h\). On the slanted face \(a + b = 1\), for example at \(A\) we have \(\vek n\cdot(A - P) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/n_{\mathrm{x}} = h\). So the whole prism lies on the side of the slanted face where \(P\) is, and \(\vek n\) points away from it.
  4. The face in the plane \(x = 0\) is a rectangle with the sides \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h\), so \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = h^2/n_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A\). Likewise \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A\) for the face in the plane \(y = 0\). The triangular faces have the area \(\tfrac12\,(h/n_{\mathrm{x}})(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) = \tfrac12\,\Delta A\), and the volume of the prism is the area of the triangle times \(h\), that is, \(h\,\Delta A/2\).
  5. We divide the equilibrium of the prism by \(\Delta A\) and repeat steps 3 to 6 of item 1, with two changes. The weight per unit area is now \(\rho g\,\Delta V/\Delta A = \rho g h/2\), so we take \(\delta = 2\eta/(\rho g)\). By the triangle inequality the point \(X\) of step 3 is at most \((a + b)\max(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}, h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) + c\,h\) from \(P\), so at most \(\max(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}, h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) + h\), and this goes to zero with \(h\). The triangular faces have the outward normals \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so in the limit they contribute \(\tfrac12\,\big(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) + \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\big)\), which by the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut is the zero vector. What remains is \(\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\), which is (2.25) at \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). If we flipped an axis in step 2, we return to the old system as in step 4 of item 2.

Source: CM (4.2.2)-(4.2.7) (PDF pp. 181-182), SADD (3.2.5) (PDF p. 66), BOWER §2.2.2-2.2.3 (PDF pp. 68-70). The sources prove the formula only for the tetrahedron. Items 2 and 3 of the proof are not from the sources.

This is one of the results on which all of continuum mechanics stands, so let us remember it. Three pictures tell why it holds. The faces at \(P\) are shadows of the slanted face, so their areas are the area of the slanted face times components of the normal. The equilibrium relates the forces on the four faces. The weight does not count for a small tetrahedron, because it shrinks faster than the areas. What remains is a relation between traction vectors at a single point.

We derived the formula for a body in equilibrium. What about a body that moves? The bits of the tetrahedron then need not all have the same acceleration. For a collection of bits of matter Newton's law says that the resultant of the forces on it is the sum of the masses of the bits times their accelerations (CM (5.3.6), (5.3.9), PDF p. 227, where this sum is written as an exact summation over the volume). If the acceleration changes continuously from point to point, then in a small tetrahedron the accelerations of all the bits are close to the acceleration of the point \(P\), and the sum is close to \(\rho\,\Delta V\) times the acceleration of the point \(P\). This vector stands on the right side of (2.22) instead of the zero vector, because the four forces no longer balance. CM carries out the derivation exactly this way (CM (4.2.2), PDF p. 181). The mass is proportional to the volume, so after division by \(\Delta A\) this term vanishes just like the weight (CM (4.2.6), (4.2.7), PDF p. 182). The same holds for every other force that acts on all the matter, as long as its magnitude on a small piece near \(P\) is at most a fixed number times the volume of the piece. So the single density in the theorem is not necessary either. We asked for it only so that the weight is simply \(\rho g\,\Delta V\). If the density near \(P\) merely does not exceed some number \(\rho_{\max}\), the weight per unit area of the slanted face is at most \(\rho_{\max} g h/3\) and falls towards zero just as in step 3 of the proof. Cauchy's formula therefore holds also in a body that moves or changes its shape.

2.6.5 Stress components

Cauchy's formula says that the stress at a point is described by three vectors, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). In a chosen coordinate system each of them has three components. So the stress at a point is described by nine numbers.

Definition 2.27 Stress components (komponente napetosti)

Let \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) be the traction vectors at the point \(P\) on the faces that are perpendicular to the axes of a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. The stress components (komponente napetosti) at \(P\) are nine numbers, the components of these three vectors:

\[\begin{aligned} \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) , \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}) , \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{2.26}\]

The first index tells the face, perpendicular to the axis of that name, and the second index the component of the traction vector on it.

Source: SADD (3.2.2), (3.2.3) (PDF pp. 65-66), BOWER §2.2.3 (PDF pp. 70-71), EP §1.3.1 (pp. 28-29).

Why two indices? For the strain in chapter 1 the indices of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) told two directions, between which we measure the change of a right angle. Here the first index selects the face and the second the component of the force on it. The number \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the component \(y\) of the force per unit area on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

What do the numbers mean? On the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the normal stress is, by definition 2.18, the projection of the traction vector onto the normal, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The shear part is \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (0, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\). It lies in the face, because its component \(x\) is zero, and its length is the shear stress on this face, \(\tau = \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2}\). Likewise on the other two faces. The three numbers with equal indices, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), are therefore the normal stresses on the three faces. The six numbers with different indices are the components of the shear parts, so we call them shear components (strižne komponente). Figure 2.74 shows them on a small cube around the point \(P\), with the axis \(x\) toward us, \(y\) to the right and \(z\) up. On the front face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the blue arrow \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) points perpendicular out of the face, and the blackwhite arrows \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) lie in the face, along the axes \(y\) and \(z\). Likewise on the right face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) perpendicular, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) in the face) and on the top face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) perpendicular, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) in the face).

What are the signs? A positive component on the face with the outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) points in the positive direction of its axis, like the arrows \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) in figure 2.74. That figure hides the opposite faces, so let us also look at the cube along the axis \(z\) (figure 2.75). We see it as a square, the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(y\) up. The right face has the outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the left one \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). On the left the traction vector is, by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), so there positive components point in the negative directions of the axes. On the right \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) points to the right and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) up, on the left \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) points to the left and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) down (SADD, PDF p. 66). Likewise on the top and bottom faces with the outward normals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). A positive normal stress on both opposite faces pulls outward from the cube, so it means tension, as in the bar.

Books write the components differently. SADD writes the normal components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}\) and the shear ones \(\tau_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and so on (SADD (3.2.5), PDF p. 66). BOWER and EP write the indices as we do, the first for the face and the second for the direction (BOWER, PDF p. 71, EP, pp. 28-29). CM swaps the indices: for it \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) means the component in the direction of the axis \(i\) on the face perpendicular to the axis \(j\) (CM, PDF p. 183). When reading CM, the indices must therefore be swapped.

Let us write Cauchy's formula with components.

  1. Into (2.25) we substitute the vectors from (2.26):
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) .\]
  2. We compute multiples and the sum of vectors by components (definition 1.14). The component \(x\) we get from the components \(x\) of all three vectors, the component \(y\) from the components \(y\), the component \(z\) from the components \(z\):
    \[\begin{aligned} t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{y}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{z}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{2.27}\]

This is how SADD (3.2.5) (PDF p. 66) and BOWER (PDF p. 70) write it. The nine numbers are easier to remember in a table in which each row is one traction vector:

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} & x & y & z \\ \hline \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{array}\]

The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) we get from the column \(x\): we multiply the first number by \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), the second by \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), the third by \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and add the products. Likewise for the columns \(y\) and \(z\).

Books call these nine numbers the stress tensor (napetostni tenzor) at the point \(P\) (SADD §3.2, PDF pp. 64-66, CM §4.2.3, PDF pp. 182-183). What the word tensor means we will say later, when we see how the nine numbers change when we turn the axes. Until then the stress components are for us nine numbers in a chosen coordinate system.

In all the examples that follow, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). This is no coincidence. We will show that it follows from the second condition of equilibrium, that the forces do not turn the body, which we have not yet written with numbers. The proof is in section 2.8.5. For Cauchy's formula we did not use this condition, so the formula does not need the equality. CM too stresses that its derivation does not assume it (CM, PDF p. 183).

2.6.6 Three examples

A bar, any face. In a bar pulled by an axial force, by the hint at the beginning of the section, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0\). The only nonzero stress component is therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\), and by (2.27)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = (\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0,\ 0)\]

for every face, not only for the faces tilted about the axis \(z\) that we treated in section 2.4. Take the face with the normal \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), which is not tilted about any of the coordinate axes. Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = (\sigma/3, 0, 0)\), the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = \sigma/3 \cdot 1/3 = \sigma/9\), and the shear stress, by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector,

\[\tau = \sqrt{|\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{9} - \frac{\sigma^2}{81}} = \frac{2\sqrt2}{9}\,\sigma = 0{,}3143\,\sigma .\]

For our bar with \(\sigma = 127{,}32\) MPa (unrounded, section 2.2) let us draw the face from the side, in the plane spanned by the normal \(\vek n\) and the traction vector (figure 2.76). The traction vector points along the axis of the bar and has the length \(|\vek t| = 42{,}44\) MPa. Its projection onto the normal is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 14{,}15\) MPa, only a third of the length, because the cosine of the angle between \(\vek t\) and \(\vek n\) is \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac13\). The remainder along the face is \(\tau = 40{,}01\) MPa, almost the whole vector. Without Cauchy's formula this face would be hard to treat, since it does not pass through the bar as simply as the cuts in figure 2.45.

Pure shear. We load a cube only with forces along its faces (figure 2.77, left). Let the axis \(x\) point to the right and the axis \(z\) up. We label the corners of the front face as in section 1.6.2: \(A\) at the bottom left, \(B\) at the bottom right, \(C\) at the top right and \(D\) at the top left. We pull the top face to the right and the bottom face to the left, as with the deck of cards in figure 1.12. If only these two forces acted, they would turn the cube, like the forces on the ruler in figure 2.7. So we pull the right face up and the left face down, and these two forces turn the cube the other way. Let all four forces per unit area be equally large, \(\tau_0\), and the cube is in equilibrium. Why exactly at equal sizes, we will show in section 2.8.8, when we write the second condition of equilibrium with numbers. The front and the back face are not loaded. We leave out the weight of the cube. Why is this justified? For a steel cube with the side \(s = 10\) mm the weight per unit area of the bottom face is \(\rho g s = 765\) Pa, only \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of the stress \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa that we take below. At this stress the weight would reach 1 % of \(\tau_0\) only at a side of \(6{,}5\) m. We will check this more closely in section 2.7.9. As in the bar, we assume that the stress is the same at all points of the cube. Why may we? The force per unit area is the same at every point of every face, \(\tau_0\), and the same stress at all points fits all six faces at once. That it also satisfies the equations of equilibrium we will check in section 2.7.9. When does it not hold? If we pulled a face unevenly, for example only in its middle, the force per unit area would differ over the face, and with it the stress over the cube. What does the assumption give us? The numbers below hold at every point of the cube, not only at one. Then

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\), and all other components are zero. Such a state is called pure shear (čisti strig). On the faces of the cube there are only shear stresses, no normal ones. But what about the faces that cut the cube along a diagonal? By (2.25)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}},\ 0,\ n_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]
  1. The face perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\) has the normal \(\vek n\)\( = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2 = \tau_0\,\vek n\). The traction vector points in the direction of the normal, so the shear part is zero, and the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \tau_0\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = \tau_0\), tension.
  2. The face perpendicular to the diagonal \(BD\) has the normal \(\vek n = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(1, 0, -1)/\sqrt2 = -\tau_0\,\vek n\), the shear part is again zero, and the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = -\tau_0\), compression.

So shear on the faces of the cube is the same as tension and compression on the diagonal faces, depending only on which faces we look at. At \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa the material is pulled with 50 MPa in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\) and pressed with 50 MPa in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\). We saw the same pattern for the sheared cube in section 1.6.2: the diagonal \(AC\) got longer, the diagonal \(BD\) shorter (figure 1.95). How stress and strain are related we will see in chapter 3. Let us also check the equilibrium of the lower left triangular piece \(ABD\) of the cube with side \(s\), cut off by the diagonal face (figure 2.77, right). On the bottom face acts the force \(-\tau_0 s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) per unit depth, on the left one \(-\tau_0 s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and on the diagonal face of length \(s\sqrt2\) the force \(\tau_0\,\vek n\,s\sqrt2 = \tau_0 s\,(1, 0, 1)\). The sum is \(\tau_0 s\,\big((-1, 0, 0) + (0, 0, -1) + (1, 0, 1)\big) = \vek 0\).

A fluid at rest. A fluid (tekočina), such as water or air, differs from a solid in that it cannot resist shear. A shear force deforms a fluid continuously, so a fluid at rest carries only pressure and no shear stress (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). At a point of still water the shear part of the traction vector is therefore zero on every face. On the faces perpendicular to the axes the traction vector points along the normal and towards the face, because the water pushes:

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -p_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = -p_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = -p_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} ,\]

where \(p_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{z}}\) are positive numbers, the pressures (tlaki) on the three faces. Are these pressures equal? Cauchy's formula says they must be. Let us show it on a small wedge of water (figure 2.78), drawn as if \(p_{\mathrm{x}}\) were larger than \(p_{\mathrm{y}}\).

  1. Take a wedge with one face perpendicular to the axis \(x\), another perpendicular to the axis \(y\), and a third, slanted one with the normal \(\vek n = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\). On the first the water pushes with the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{x}}\), on the second with the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{y}}\). By (2.25), on the slanted face \(\vek t(\vek n) = -(p_{\mathrm{x}}, p_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)/\sqrt2\), and since \(p_{\mathrm{x}}\) is larger, this vector is more parallel to the axis \(x\) than the normal is.
  2. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = -(p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}})/2\), and the shear part is
    \[\vek t(\vek n) - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(-p_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}}}{2},\ -p_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}}}{2},\ 0\right) = \frac{p_{\mathrm{y}} - p_{\mathrm{x}}}{2\sqrt2}\,(1, -1, 0) .\]
  3. On this face too the shear part must be zero, since water cannot resist shear, so \(p_{\mathrm{x}} = p_{\mathrm{y}}\). The dashed arrow in the figure then shrinks to nothing, and the traction vector falls onto the normal.
  4. In the same way the wedge, in which the axes \(x\) and \(y\) become \(y\) and \(z\), so the face with the normal \((0, 1, 1)/\sqrt2\), gives \(p_{\mathrm{y}} = p_{\mathrm{z}}\). All three pressures are equal, and we denote them by \(p\).
  5. For every face therefore \(\vek t(\vek n) = -p\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - p\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} - p\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = -p\,\vek n\).

In a fluid at rest the pressure pushes perpendicular to every face and is the same in all directions (figure 2.79). On a small body immersed in water all the arrows are perpendicular to the surface and equally long, however the side is tilted. That is why water presses on the wall of a dam perpendicular to it, however the wall is tilted, and why a diver feels the same pressure on the eardrum wherever he turns his head. CM writes \(\vek t = -p\,\vek n\) directly (CM (8.1.1), PDF p. 384). That \(p\) is the same in all directions we have derived here from Cauchy's formula. The stress components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\), and the shear components are zero.

We have described the stress at a point with nine numbers in a chosen coordinate system. Three questions remain open. How do these nine numbers change when we turn the axes? On which face through the point is the normal stress largest, and on which the shear stress? For the bar we found the answer only among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), and in pure shear we saw that faces with normal stresses only exist even where the faces of the cube have none. And how does the second condition of equilibrium relate \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\)?
