Chapter 1 Strain

1.16 Mathematics: matrices

In section 1.15 we showed how the displacement changes over a short step in any direction. Let us recall the result. Let \(P\) be an interior point of a body, that is, a point with some material around it in all directions, and let the displacement field be continuously differentiable, that is, let it have partial derivatives that exist and change without jumps (definition 1.90). From \(P\) we take a short step to the point \(Q\), so that \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)\). By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, written for each component of the displacement separately, the three equalities (1.36) of section 1.15.7 hold. Let us write them out once more:

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{x}} , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{y}} , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z + R_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned}\]

The remainders \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\) are negligible compared with the length of the step: each of them, divided by the length of the step \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\), goes to zero as the step gets shorter. More precisely, for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le \eta\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\) for every step with \(0 < |\overrightarrow{PQ}| < \delta\), and the same for \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\).

These three lines contain nine partial derivatives and the same pattern three times. Each line takes the three partial derivatives of one component of the displacement, multiplies each by the matching component of the step and adds the products. In the next section we will compute the strain of a fibre in any direction from these lines and rewrite them several times along the way. Nine partial derivatives written out again each time would hide what is going on. In chapter 2, too, we will describe with nine numbers how the parts of a body act on each other, and we will have to say how these numbers change when we turn the axes. So we need a way of writing in which the nine numbers are a single table and the threefold pattern is a single operation.

From history

Calculating with tables of numbers as with a single object was developed in 1858 by Arthur Cayley in a paper that, in the opinion of James Joseph Sylvester, began the reign of Algebra the Second (Algebra druga), that is, a new era in algebra (CAJORI, PDF p. 355-356). The origin of such calculating actually lies with Hamilton of section 1.5.2, but Cayley's theory is more general and does not mention Hamilton (CAJORI, PDF p. 356).

First we will define tables such as the table of nine partial derivatives of the displacement, and add them. Then we will multiply a table by a vector, so that the three lines (1.36) become a single equation, and we will try this on the sheared cube and on the hanging bar. From two multiplications by two tables one after the other we will derive the multiplication of two tables. Then come the exchange of rows and columns, the splitting of a table into a part that does not change under this exchange and a part that changes its sign under it, and a table that leaves a vector unchanged and a table that undoes the multiplication by another table. In the next section the splitting will separate the stretching of the material from its turning. All of this is algebra. We assume only the school rules of arithmetic for numbers and the rules of vector arithmetic of section 1.5.3. We need no limits in this section.

1.16.1 A table of numbers

We already collected the nine partial derivatives of (1.36) at the end of section 1.15 in a table with three rows and three columns, just as in section 1.7.2 we collected the partial derivatives of the hanging bar. A row holds a component of the displacement, a column the variable we differentiate with respect to. Now we enclose the table in square brackets:

\[\begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}(P) \end{bmatrix} .\]

The first row of the table is exactly the three partial derivatives of the first line of (1.36), in the same order, the second row holds the partial derivatives of the second line and the third row those of the third. So the table holds everything the three equations say about the field at the point \(P\), apart from the remainders. Such tables get a name.

Definition 1.93 Matrix (matrika)

A matrix (matrika) of size \(m \times n\) is a table of real numbers with \(m\) rows (vrstice) and \(n\) columns (stolpci). The number in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is the entry (element) of the matrix at the position \((i, j)\). We denote matrices by bold capital letters, for example \(\mat A\), and their entries by the same letter with two indices, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The first index gives the row, the second the column:

\[\mat A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} & \cdots & A_{1\mathrm{n}} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{2\mathrm{n}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ A_{\mathrm{m}1} & A_{\mathrm{m}2} & \cdots & A_{\mathrm{mn}} \end{bmatrix} .\]

Two matrices are equal if they have the same size and agree in all entries at the same positions. A matrix with as many rows as columns, \(n \times n\), is a square matrix (kvadratna matrika) of order \(n\), and its entries \(A_{11}, A_{22}, \dots, A_{\mathrm{nn}}\) lie on the main diagonal (glavna diagonala). A matrix of size \(n \times 1\) is a column (stolpec), a matrix of size \(1 \times n\) a row (vrstica). For square matrices of order \(3\) that describe quantities in space we label the rows and columns by the letters \(x, y, z\) instead of the numbers \(1, 2, 3\). When we calculate with matrices, we write a vector \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\) as the column

\[\vek v = \begin{bmatrix} v_{\mathrm{x}} \\ v_{\mathrm{y}} \\ v_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} .\]

Source: KREYSZIG §7.1, (2) (PDF pp. 283-285), CM §2.3.1 (PDF pp. 60-61).

From history

The word matrix was introduced by Sylvester in September 1850, for a table of numbers from which certain expressions can be formed, while it is not itself such an expression (MUIR, PDF p. 582-584). We shall meet these expressions in chapter 2.

A matrix is by definition \(m \cdot n\) numbers arranged in rows and columns, and the arrangement matters. Two matrices with the same numbers in different positions are different matrices, just as the points \((3, 2, 2)\) and \((2, 3, 2)\) of section 1.5.1 are different points. The table of partial derivatives at the beginning of the section is a square matrix of order \(3\). Its entry in row \(x\) and column \(y\) is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at the point \(P\) (figure 1.125, right). The two indices of an entry follow each other in the same order as the letters in the symbol of the partial derivative, read from left to right: the first gives the component of the displacement, the second the variable we differentiate with respect to.

Why do we label the rows and columns of square matrices of order \(3\) by letters? So that an index tells a direction. The entry \(A_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and in the table of partial derivatives it says how fast the component of the displacement in the direction of the axis \(x\) changes as we move in the direction of the axis \(z\). Many books number the axes and write the same entry as \(A_{13}\) (CM §2.3.1, PDF p. 60), as we mentioned with the unit vectors \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) in section 1.5.5.

Until now we have written a vector as a triple in round brackets, \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\). When we calculate with matrices, we write it vertically, as a column. It is the same vector with the same three components, only arranged differently on paper. Two columns are equal exactly when they are equal vectors, since they must agree in the same three numbers. Why vertically, we will see below, when we multiply a matrix by a vector. Where there is no risk of confusion, we will also write columns in the text as triples in round brackets, to save space.

Matrices can have any size. A system of \(m\) equations in \(n\) unknowns, in which the unknowns are only multiplied by numbers and added, has for example a matrix of coefficients of size \(m \times n\) (KREYSZIG §7.1, Example 1, PDF p. 283). The entries can also be functions (KREYSZIG §7.1, PDF p. 283), but we will need only real numbers. Square matrices of order \(3\) and columns with three entries are the most common case in this book, but not the only one. As soon as we multiply matrices we will also need rows of size \(1 \times 3\).

In the equations (1.36) the remainders are added to the products. When we write them with matrices, we will therefore add columns. Later we will also add square matrices. We add them as we add vectors, entry by entry.

Definition 1.94 Sum and multiple of matrices (vsota in večkratnik matrik)

For matrices \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) of the same size \(m \times n\) and a number \(c\), the sum (vsota) \(\mat A + \mat B\), the difference (razlika) \(\mat A - \mat B\) and the multiple (večkratnik) \(c\,\mat A\) are the matrices of size \(m \times n\) with the entries

\[(\mat A + \mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ij}} + B_{\mathrm{ij}} , \qquad (\mat A - \mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ij}} - B_{\mathrm{ij}} , \qquad (c\,\mat A)_{\mathrm{ij}} = c\,A_{\mathrm{ij}} .\]

The zero matrix (ničelna matrika) \(\mat 0\) has all entries equal to zero.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.1 (PDF p. 286), CM (2.3.2) (PDF p. 61).

The notation \((\mat A + \mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}}\) means the entry of the matrix \(\mat A + \mat B\) at the position \((i, j)\). So every entry of the sum depends only on the entries of both matrices at the same position. We do not add matrices of different sizes, because some entries of one would have no partner in the other. For example

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix} .\]

For two columns with three entries, the sum, the difference and the multiple are exactly the sum, the difference and the multiple of vectors of definition 1.14, only written vertically. Since we calculate entry by entry, the same rules hold for matrices as for vectors.

Lemma 1.95 Rules of matrix arithmetic (računska pravila za matrike)

For all matrices \(\mat A\), \(\mat B\), \(\mat C\) of the same size and all numbers \(c\), \(k\):

  1. \(\mat A + \mat B = \mat B + \mat A\),
  2. \((\mat A + \mat B) + \mat C = \mat A + (\mat B + \mat C)\),
  3. \(\mat A + \mat 0 = \mat A\) and \(\mat A - \mat A = \mat 0\),
  4. \(c\,(\mat A + \mat B) = c\,\mat A + c\,\mat B\) and \((c + k)\,\mat A = c\,\mat A + k\,\mat A\),
  5. \(c\,(k\,\mat A) = (c\,k)\,\mat A\) and \(1\,\mat A = \mat A\).
Proof

We must show that in each rule the matrices on the left and on the right are equal.

  1. Two matrices are equal if they have the same size and agree in all entries at the same positions (definition 1.93). The sum, the difference and the multiple of matrices of size \(m \times n\) are matrices of size \(m \times n\) (definition 1.94), so both sides of every rule have the same size. It remains to check each rule for the entry at an arbitrary position \((i, j)\).
  2. All operations in the rules are defined entry by entry, so the entry \((i, j)\) of each side is computed only from the entries \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(C_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and from the numbers \(c\), \(k\).
  3. Rules 1 and 2. The entry \((i, j)\) of the left and the right side is \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + B_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{ij}} + A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), or \((A_{\mathrm{ij}} + B_{\mathrm{ij}}) + C_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + (B_{\mathrm{ij}} + C_{\mathrm{ij}})\). In both cases the numbers are equal, because the order of adding numbers does not affect the sum and when adding three numbers it does not matter which two we add first.
  4. Rule 3. The entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat A + \mat 0\) is \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + 0 = A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and the entry of the matrix \(\mat A - \mat A\) is \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} - A_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), which is the entry of the zero matrix.
  5. Rule 4. The entry \((i, j)\) of the left side is \(c\,(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + B_{\mathrm{ij}})\) or \((c + k)\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), that of the right side \(c\,A_{\mathrm{ij}} + c\,B_{\mathrm{ij}}\) or \(c\,A_{\mathrm{ij}} + k\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The numbers are equal by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number.
  6. Rule 5. The entry \((i, j)\) of the left side is \(c\,(k\,A_{\mathrm{ij}})\) or \(1 \cdot A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), that of the right side \((c\,k)\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) or \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The numbers are equal, because when multiplying three numbers it does not matter which two we multiply first, and because multiplying by \(1\) does not change a number.
  7. Both sides of every rule agree in all entries, so by step 1 they are equal.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.1, (3) and (4) (PDF pp. 286-287), CM §2.3.2 (PDF p. 61).

The proof is the same as the proof of lemma 1.15 on the rules of vector arithmetic, only that instead of three components we check all entries. Matrices of the same size, with their addition and multiplication by numbers, therefore meet all the requirements of a vector space (KREYSZIG §7.9, Example 1, PDF p. 337), which definition 1.16 lists. There we mentioned that tables of numbers of size \(2 \times 2\) are vectors in this sense, and the lemma confirms it for all sizes. So we may calculate with matrices just as with vectors: move the terms of a sum around, shift brackets and take numbers out as factors.

1.16.2 A matrix times a vector

Now let us write the three sums. Look at the first line of (1.36) without the remainder:

\[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,\Delta y + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,\Delta z .\]

Its first factors are the first row of the matrix of partial derivatives, its second factors are the components of the step, which we write as a column. We get the sum by walking along the row and down the column at the same time, multiplying the numbers at matching positions and adding the products (figure 1.126). The second line of (1.36) is the same sum with the second row of the matrix and the same column, the third with the third row. This operation gets a name.

Definition 1.96 Product of a matrix and a vector (produkt matrike in vektorja)

Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix of size \(m \times n\) and \(\vek v\) a column with \(n\) entries \(v_1, \dots, v_{\mathrm{n}}\). The product (produkt) \(\mat A\vek v\) is the column with the \(m\) entries

\[\begin{aligned} (\mat A\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}} &= \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = A_{\mathrm{i}1}\,v_1 + A_{\mathrm{i}2}\,v_2 + \dots + A_{\mathrm{in}}\,v_{\mathrm{n}} , \\ &\qquad i = 1, \dots, m . \end{aligned} \tag{1.38}\]

The sign \(\sum\) means the sum of the terms \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n\), as the written-out form shows. For a square matrix of order \(3\) and a vector \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\) this is

\[\mat A\vek v = \begin{bmatrix} A_{\mathrm{xx}} & A_{\mathrm{xy}} & A_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ A_{\mathrm{yx}} & A_{\mathrm{yy}} & A_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ A_{\mathrm{zx}} & A_{\mathrm{zy}} & A_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_{\mathrm{x}} \\ v_{\mathrm{y}} \\ v_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}} \\ A_{\mathrm{yx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{yy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{yz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}} \\ A_{\mathrm{zx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{zy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{zz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} .\]

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (1) and Example 2 (PDF pp. 289-290), CM (2.3.6) (PDF p. 63).

The sign \(\sum\), the capital Greek letter sigma, is short for a sum. The expression \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\) means that in the expression \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\) we put in turn \(1, 2, \dots, n\) for \(j\) and add the \(n\) terms we get. The index \(j\) under the sign is called the summation index (indeks seštevanja). For a square matrix of order \(3\) it runs over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{xj}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\). We will always write sums with this sign or written out, and we will never leave the sign out, as some books do (CM §2.2.5, PDF pp. 52-53).

So the entry \(i\) of the product is row \(i\) of the matrix times the column: we multiply each entry of the row by the entry of the column at the matching position and add the products. That is why we write the vector vertically. The product exists only if the matrix has as many columns as the column has entries, since every entry of the row must get its partner. The result has as many entries as the matrix has rows.

With the product, the three lines (1.36) are a single equation. On the left stands the column of differences of displacements, on the right the product of the matrix of partial derivatives and the column of the step, to which we add the column of remainders:

\[\begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) \\[1mm] u_{\mathrm{y}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{y}}(P) \\[1mm] u_{\mathrm{z}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{z}}(P) \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}(P) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}(P) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}(P) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\[1mm] \Delta y \\[1mm] \Delta z \end{bmatrix} \\ &\quad + \begin{bmatrix} R_{\mathrm{x}} \\[1mm] R_{\mathrm{y}} \\[1mm] R_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} . \end{aligned} \tag{1.39}\]

Let us check that this really is the same. By definition 1.96 the entry \(x\) of the product is the first row of the matrix times the column of the step, which is the sum in the first line of (1.36). When by definition 1.94 we add the entry \(x\) of the column of remainders, \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), we get the right side of the first line of (1.36). The left side is the entry \(x\) of the column on the left. The same holds for the entries \(y\) and \(z\). The column on the left is the difference of the displacement vectors \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\), written vertically, and the column of the step is the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\). Nine numbers and three sums are now one matrix and one product.

Let us try the notation first on the sheared cube of section 1.6.2. We glued a steel cube with an edge of 10 mm, that is, \(10\,000\) µm, to a table by its bottom face and pushed its top face sideways by 10 µm. The origin is at the lower front left corner \(A\), the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(z\) vertically up, and we measure lengths in micrometres. The displacement field is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = 0{,}001\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) = 0\]

((1.16)). We compute the partial derivatives as in section 1.7.2, treating the variables we do not differentiate with respect to as fixed numbers. The component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) changes only with \(z\), at the rate \(0{,}001\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = 0{,}001\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = 0\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero everywhere, so all their partial derivatives are zero. The matrix of partial derivatives is therefore the same at every point of the cube. We denote it by \(\mat K\):

\[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The only nonzero entry is in row \(x\) and column \(z\). The component of the displacement in the direction of the axis \(x\) changes as we go in the direction of the axis \(z\), upward, as in the deck of cards, whose layers slide the more the higher they lie.

For the cube there are no remainders. The difference \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = 0{,}001\,z_{\mathrm{Q}} - 0{,}001\,z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0{,}001\,\Delta z\) is exactly the product of the partial derivative and the component of the step, because the field has no squares or products of coordinates, and the other two differences are zero. The difference of displacements of the ends of every fibre of the cube is therefore exactly \(\mat K\) times the vector of the fibre. Let us check this on the vertical edge \(AD\) with the vector \(\overrightarrow{AD} = (0, 0, 10\,000)\):

\[\mat K \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 10\,000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0{,}001 \cdot 10\,000 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 10\,000 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 10\,000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

This is exactly \(\vek u(D) - \vek u(A) = (10, 0, 0) - (0, 0, 0)\): the top of the edge moves by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\), the bottom does not move. We get the same for the diagonal \(AC\), \(\mat K\,\overrightarrow{AC} = \mat K\,(10\,000, 0, 10\,000) = (10, 0, 0)\), and for the bottom edge, \(\mat K\,\overrightarrow{AB} = \mat K\,(10\,000, 0, 0) = (0, 0, 0)\). By (1.15) the vector of a fibre after loading is its vector before loading plus the difference of displacements of its ends. For the edge \(AD\) this is \((0, 0, 10\,000) + (10, 0, 0) = (10, 0, 10\,000)\), as in the table of section 1.6.2. So the whole deformation of the cube is stored in a single number, \(0{,}001\), in the right place of the matrix \(\mat K\).

Two properties of the product follow from the rules for numbers, and we will use them all the time.

Lemma 1.97 Linearity of the product with a matrix (linearnost produkta z matriko)

For every matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(m \times n\), all columns \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) with \(n\) entries and every number \(c\)

\[\mat A\,(\vek v + \vek w) = \mat A\vek v + \mat A\vek w , \qquad \mat A\,(c\,\vek v) = c\,(\mat A\vek v) .\]
Proof

We must show that the columns on the left and on the right of each equality agree in every entry.

  1. Both sides of each equality are columns with \(m\) entries, because the product with the matrix \(\mat A\) has as many entries as \(\mat A\) has rows (definition 1.96), and the sum and the multiple of columns with \(m\) entries have \(m\) entries (definition 1.94). We compare the entry \(i\).
  2. The entry \(j\) of the column \(\vek v + \vek w\) is \(v_{\mathrm{j}} + w_{\mathrm{j}}\) (definition 1.94). By (1.38) the entry \(i\) of the left side of the first equality is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(v_{\mathrm{j}} + w_{\mathrm{j}})\).
  3. We expand each term by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(v_{\mathrm{j}} + w_{\mathrm{j}}) = A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} + A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. A sum of \(2n\) terms does not depend on the order of adding, so we arrange the terms in two sums, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  5. By (1.38) the first sum is the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat A\vek v\) and the second the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat A\vek w\). Their sum is the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat A\vek v + \mat A\vek w\) (definition 1.94). The first equality holds.
  6. The entry \(j\) of the column \(c\,\vek v\) is \(c\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\), so by (1.38) the entry \(i\) of the left side of the second equality is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  7. In each term we change the order of the factors, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = c\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\), and take the common factor \(c\) out in front of the sum by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. We get \(c \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  8. This is the number \(c\) times the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat A\vek v\), that is, the entry \(i\) of the column \(c\,(\mat A\vek v)\) (definition 1.94). The second equality holds.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (2a) and (2d) (PDF p. 290), §7.9, (10) and (11) (PDF p. 340).

What does the lemma say about fibres? In the cube take a fibre with the vector \(\vek v\) and a fibre pointing in the same direction and half as long, with the vector \(\tfrac12\,\vek v\) (figure 1.127, left). By the second equality of the lemma, the difference of displacements of the ends of the shorter fibre is \(\mat K\,(\tfrac12\,\vek v) = \tfrac12\,\mat K\vek v\), half as large. For a fibre along the diagonal \(AC\) and half as long as it, this is \(\tfrac12\,(10, 0, 0) = (5, 0, 0)\), as we found without matrices in section 1.6.2. The first equality says that differences of displacements add up along a path (figure 1.127, right). From the point \(P\) we get to the point \(S\) directly, with the vector \(\vek v + \vek w\), or first with the vector \(\vek v\) to the point \(Q\) and then with the vector \(\vek w\) to \(S\). The difference of displacements between \(P\) and \(S\) is \(\mat K\,(\vek v + \vek w)\), the sum of the differences over the two parts of the path is \(\mat K\vek v + \mat K\vek w\). The lemma says they are equal. And so they must be, since \(\vek u(S) - \vek u(P) = \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) + \big(\vek u(S) - \vek u(Q)\big)\), as in the splitting of a step in equation (1.22).

Multiplication by a matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(m \times n\) is a rule whose input is a column \(\vek v\) of \(n\) numbers and whose output is the column \(\mat A\vek v\) of \(m\) numbers. So it is a function in the sense of definition 1.4, with both sets being sets of columns. A function whose input is an element of some vector space and whose output is an element of the same or another vector space, and which has both properties of lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, written with the addition and the multiplication by numbers of these spaces, is called a linear map (linearna preslikava) in mathematics (KREYSZIG §7.9, (10), PDF pp. 339-340). Every multiplication by a matrix is a linear map, with both spaces being spaces of columns. The converse also holds: every linear map whose input is \(n\) numbers and whose output is \(m\) numbers is the multiplication by some matrix of size \(m \times n\) (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF pp. 340-341). So a matrix has three roles. It is a table of numbers (definition 1.93), it is an element of a vector space (lemma 1.95), and it represents a linear map (lemma 1.97).

Now the hanging bar, where the remainders are not zero. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a diameter of 10 mm hangs under its own weight. As in section 1.7.1 it is held in an idealized way: only the point \(O\), the centre of the top end, is fixed, and a force pulling evenly upward on the top face carries the weight of the bar. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from \(O\), the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally. The point \(P\) lies at a depth of 5 m, 4 mm to the right of the axis of the bar, so \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 0{,}004\) m and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\). We computed the table of partial derivatives at the point \(P\) in section 1.7.2. Let us write it as a matrix and denote it by \(\mat V\), after the initial of the Slovenian word viseča, hanging:

\[\mat V = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} & -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & 0 \\ 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} .\]

From \(P\) we draw a fibre of length \(h\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\) of section 1.7.3, whose length is \(\sqrt{1/9 + 4/9 + 4/9} = 1\). The vector of the fibre is \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). By (1.39) and the second equality of lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix,

\[\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) = \mat V\,(h\,\vek n) + \vek R = h\,(\mat V\vek n) + \vek R ,\]

where \(\vek R\) is the column of the remainders \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\). We divide both sides by \(h\):

\[\frac{1}{h}\,\big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) = \mat V\vek n + \frac{1}{h}\,\vek R .\]

The remainders divided by the length of the fibre go to zero as the fibre gets shorter (theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step), so the left side approaches the column \(\mat V\vek n\). Let us compute it row by row, by (1.38).

  1. Row \(x\): \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \tfrac13 + (-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}) \cdot \tfrac23 + 0 \cdot \tfrac23 = 6{,}5067 \cdot 10^{-7} - 3{,}123 \cdot 10^{-10} = 6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\).
  2. Row \(y\): \(4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot \tfrac13 + (-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}) \cdot \tfrac23 + 0 \cdot \tfrac23 = 1{,}562 \cdot 10^{-10} - 3{,}9040 \cdot 10^{-7} = -3{,}9024 \cdot 10^{-7}\).
  3. Row \(z\): \(0 \cdot \tfrac13 + 0 \cdot \tfrac23 + (-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}) \cdot \tfrac23 = -3{,}9040 \cdot 10^{-7}\).

The first number is the value \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-7}\) that we approached in section 1.7.3 with ever shorter fibres. There we computed it only for the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), while the product gives all three at once. What do they tell us? A fibre of length 1 mm, \(h = 0{,}001\) m, has a difference of displacements of its ends of about \(h\,\mat V\vek n\), that is, \(6{,}5035 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m in the direction of the axis \(x\) and \(-3{,}9024 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m and \(-3{,}9040 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m in the directions of the axes \(y\) and \(z\) (figure 1.128). The end \(Q\) lies lower than \(P\), where the bar hanging under its own weight moves down more, so the component \(x\) is positive and the fibre gets longer along the axis of the bar. The end \(Q\) also lies farther from the axis of the bar than \(P\), and the bar gets thinner, so \(Q\) moves toward the axis more than \(P\): the components \(y\) and \(z\) are negative.

Why are the components \(y\) and \(z\) nearly equal, but not quite? The main part of both is \(-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \tfrac23 = -3{,}9040 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The bar thins by the same amount in both transverse directions, which the two equal entries \(-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\) on the main diagonal in rows \(y\) and \(z\) express, and the vector \(\vek n\) has the same component \(\tfrac23\) in both directions. The component \(y\) gets the extra term \(1{,}562 \cdot 10^{-10}\) from the entry in row \(y\) and column \(x\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y_{\mathrm{P}}\), where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m⁻¹ and the number \(0{,}6\) is the thinning ratio \(0{,}3\) of steel times the \(2\) of the strain \(2a\,(L - x)\) along the bar (section 1.7.1). The end \(Q\) lies \(h/3\) deeper than \(P\), where the bar thins a little less, so a point at the distance \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) from the axis moves toward the axis a little less there. The component \(z\) has no such term. The point \(P\) lies in the plane \(z = 0\), where \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z\) is zero at every depth, so there \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\).

For the cube the equalities of lemma 1.97 are exact, because there are no remainders. For the hanging bar, however, the lemma holds only for the part of the difference of displacements that the matrix \(\mat V\) gives, and the remainders do not follow it. For the fibre from the point \(P\) in the direction \(\vek n\) we computed for the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) in section 1.7.3: at a length of 1 mm the part with the partial derivatives is \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-10}\) m and the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}} = -7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-14}\) m, at a length of 0,1 mm they are \(6{,}504 \cdot 10^{-11}\) m and \(-7{,}374 \cdot 10^{-16}\) m. For a fibre ten times shorter the part with the matrix is ten times smaller, as the second equality of the lemma requires, while the remainder is a hundred times smaller, because each of its terms is a product of two components of the step. So the lemma describes exactly the part of the difference of displacements that dominates for short fibres.

1.16.3 The product of matrices

Let us return to the sheared cube, glued to the table, whose top face we pushed sideways by a thousandth of its height. Its matrix \(\mat K\) has a single nonzero entry, \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and for every fibre with the vector \(\vek v\) it gives the difference of displacements of its ends \(\mat K\vek v\) with the components \(0{,}001\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(0\) and \(0\). The vector of the fibre after loading is its vector before loading plus this difference: \(\vek v + \mat K\vek v\) has the components \(v_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\). This vector, too, is the product with a single matrix. The entry \(x\) of the result is \(1 \cdot v_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 \cdot v_{\mathrm{y}} + 0{,}001 \cdot v_{\mathrm{z}}\), which is the row with the entries \(1\), \(0\) and \(0{,}001\) times the column \(\vek v\), and we get the other two rows in the same way:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_{\mathrm{x}} \\ v_{\mathrm{y}} \\ v_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,v_{\mathrm{z}} \\ v_{\mathrm{y}} \\ v_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} .\]

Such a matrix tells where the vector of every fibre goes. But what if we deform a piece of material twice in a row, first in one way and then in another? The vector of a fibre after the first deformation is the input of the second. Is the combined rule again the product with a single matrix, and with which one?

Let us take a simple example with large numbers, so that the picture is clear. Let the first rule double the component \(x\) of every vector and leave the other two. This is a stretching in the direction of the axis \(x\) to twice the length. Let the second rule add the component \(z\) to the component \(x\) and leave the other two. This is a shear like the shear of the cube, only with the ratio \(1\) instead of \(0{,}001\). By (1.38) we write them with the matrices

\[\mat D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

since \(\mat D\vek v = (2\,v_{\mathrm{x}},\ v_{\mathrm{y}},\ v_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\mat G\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{z}},\ v_{\mathrm{y}},\ v_{\mathrm{z}})\). Figure 1.129 shows a square in the plane \(x\)-\(z\) that we first shear and then stretch, and a square that we first stretch and then shear.

Let us find the matrix for two rules one after the other in general. Let \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) be square matrices of order \(3\). We first multiply the vector \(\vek v\) by \(\mat B\) and get \(\vek y = \mat B\vek v\), then we multiply \(\vek y\) by \(\mat A\) and get \(\vek w = \mat A\vek y = \mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\). Let us express the entry \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) through the components of the vector \(\vek v\).

  1. By (1.38), \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,y_{\mathrm{x}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y_{\mathrm{y}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,y_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. By the same equation the entries of the column \(\vek y = \mat B\vek v\) are \(y_{\mathrm{x}} = B_{\mathrm{xx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + B_{\mathrm{xy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + B_{\mathrm{xz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(y_{\mathrm{y}} = B_{\mathrm{yx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + B_{\mathrm{yy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + B_{\mathrm{yz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(y_{\mathrm{z}} = B_{\mathrm{zx}}\,v_{\mathrm{x}} + B_{\mathrm{zy}}\,v_{\mathrm{y}} + B_{\mathrm{zz}}\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. We put these three expressions into step 1 and expand each product by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. We get nine terms, each the product of one entry of the matrix \(\mat A\), one entry of the matrix \(\mat B\) and one component of the vector \(\vek v\).
  4. We collect the three terms with \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) and take \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) out: \((A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,B_{\mathrm{xx}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,B_{\mathrm{yx}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,B_{\mathrm{zx}})\,v_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the same way we collect the terms with \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\), which give \((A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,B_{\mathrm{xy}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,B_{\mathrm{yy}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,B_{\mathrm{zy}})\,v_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the terms with \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\), which give \((A_{\mathrm{xx}}\,B_{\mathrm{xz}} + A_{\mathrm{xy}}\,B_{\mathrm{yz}} + A_{\mathrm{xz}}\,B_{\mathrm{zz}})\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  5. The number in front of \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) is row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat A\) times column \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat B\), since we walk along the row \(A_{\mathrm{xx}}, A_{\mathrm{xy}}, A_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and down the column \(B_{\mathrm{xx}}, B_{\mathrm{yx}}, B_{\mathrm{zx}}\). The number in front of \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the same row times column \(y\) of the matrix \(\mat B\), and the number in front of \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\) the same row times column \(z\).
  6. For \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{z}}\) the calculation is the same, only with row \(y\) or \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat A\) in place of row \(x\).

So the vector \(\mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\) is the product of a single matrix with the vector \(\vek v\). The entry of this matrix in row \(i\) and column \(k\) is row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A\) times column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat B\), that is, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\). This matrix is the product of the two matrices.

Definition 1.98 Matrix product (produkt matrik)

Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix of size \(m \times n\) and \(\mat B\) a matrix of size \(n \times p\). Their product (produkt) \(\mat A\mat B\) is the matrix of size \(m \times p\) with the entries

\[\begin{aligned} (\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ik}} &= \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}} = A_{\mathrm{i}1}\,B_{1\mathrm{k}} + A_{\mathrm{i}2}\,B_{2\mathrm{k}} + \dots + A_{\mathrm{in}}\,B_{\mathrm{nk}} , \\ &\qquad i = 1, \dots, m , \quad k = 1, \dots, p . \end{aligned} \tag{1.40}\]

So the entry at the position \((i, k)\) is row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A\) times column \(k\) of the matrix \(\mat B\). For \(p = 1\) the matrix \(\mat B\) is a column, and \(\mat A\mat B\) is the product of a matrix and a vector of definition 1.96.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (1) (PDF p. 289), the idea with two rules one after the other on PDF pp. 291-292, CM (2.3.6) (PDF p. 63).

The product \(\mat A\mat B\) exists only if \(\mat A\) has as many columns as \(\mat B\) has rows, since every entry of a row of the matrix \(\mat A\) must get its partner in a column of the matrix \(\mat B\). We read \(\mat A\mat B\) from right to left: first \(\mat B\) acts, then \(\mat A\), as in \(\mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\). Let us compute both products of the matrices \(\mat D\) and \(\mat G\). The entry in row \(x\) and column \(z\) of the product \(\mat D\mat G\) is row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat D\), \((2, 0, 0)\), times column \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat G\), \((1, 0, 1)\), that is, \(2 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 2\). The same entry of the product \(\mat G\mat D\) is row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat G\), \((1, 0, 1)\), times column \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat D\), \((0, 0, 1)\), that is, \(1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1\). When we compute all nine entries, we get

\[\mat D\mat G = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat G\mat D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The matrices differ in the entry we computed. In figure 1.129 we see this on the left vertical edge with the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1)\). With \(\mat D\mat G\) the shear first tilts it to \((1, 0, 1)\), and then the stretching doubles its horizontal part too, so the edge ends as \((2, 0, 1)\). With \(\mat G\mat D\) the stretching does not change the vertical edge, because it has no horizontal part, and the shear then tilts it to \((1, 0, 1)\). Stretching followed by shear is not the same as shear followed by stretching.

Which laws of arithmetic for numbers hold for the matrix product as well, and which do not, the next lemma tells.

Lemma 1.99 Properties of the matrix product (lastnosti produkta matrik)

For matrices whose sizes allow the sums and products below, and for every number \(c\):

  1. \((\mat A\mat B)\,\vek v = \mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\) for every column \(\vek v\),
  2. \((\mat A\mat B)\,\mat C = \mat A\,(\mat B\mat C)\),
  3. \(\mat A\,(\mat B + \mat C) = \mat A\mat B + \mat A\mat C\), \((\mat A + \mat B)\,\mat C = \mat A\mat C + \mat B\mat C\) and \((c\,\mat A)\,\mat B = c\,(\mat A\mat B) = \mat A\,(c\,\mat B)\),
  4. there are square matrices \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) of the same order with \(\mat A\mat B \neq \mat B\mat A\).
Proof

We must show four items. The first three are equalities of matrices, so we check them entry by entry (definition 1.93), the fourth with an example.

1. Products one after the other.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be of size \(m \times n\), \(\mat B\) of size \(n \times p\) and \(\vek v\) a column with \(p\) entries. Both sides are columns with \(m\) entries. We compare the entry \(i\).
  2. By (1.38) the entry \(j\) of the column \(\mat B\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By the same equation the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat A\,(\mat B\vek v)\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  4. We multiply the factor \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) into the inner sum by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. We get a sum of \(n \cdot p\) terms \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\), one for each pair of indices \(j\) and \(k\).
  5. A sum of finitely many numbers does not depend on the order of adding, so we add the terms in another order, first over \(j\) and then over \(k\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  6. In the inner sum \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a common factor of all terms, so we take it out: \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\big)\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  7. By (1.40) the bracket is the entry \((\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ik}}\), so by (1.38) the sum is the entry \(i\) of the column \((\mat A\mat B)\,\vek v\).

2. Grouping.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be of size \(m \times n\), \(\mat B\) of size \(n \times p\) and \(\mat C\) of size \(p \times q\). Both sides are matrices of size \(m \times q\) (definition 1.98). We compare the entry \((i, l)\).
  2. By (1.40), used twice, the entry \((i, l)\) of the left side is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\big)\,C_{\mathrm{kl}}\).
  3. We multiply the factor \(C_{\mathrm{kl}}\) into the bracket. We get the sum of the terms \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,C_{\mathrm{kl}}\) over all pairs \(j\) and \(k\).
  4. We change the order of adding as in item 1, step 5: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,C_{\mathrm{kl}}\).
  5. In the inner sum \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is a common factor, so we take it out: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{p} B_{\mathrm{jk}}\,C_{\mathrm{kl}}\big)\).
  6. The bracket is the entry \((\mat B\mat C)_{\mathrm{jl}}\), so the sum is the entry \((i, l)\) of the matrix \(\mat A\,(\mat B\mat C)\) ((1.40)).

3. Distributing and multiples.

  1. By (1.40) and definition 1.94 the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat A\,(\mat B + \mat C)\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(B_{\mathrm{jk}} + C_{\mathrm{jk}})\).
  2. We expand each term, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(B_{\mathrm{jk}} + C_{\mathrm{jk}}) = A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}} + A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,C_{\mathrm{jk}}\), and arrange the terms in two sums, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,C_{\mathrm{jk}}\). This is \((\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ik}} + (\mat A\mat C)_{\mathrm{ik}}\), the entry of the matrix \(\mat A\mat B + \mat A\mat C\).
  3. In the same way the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \((\mat A + \mat B)\,\mat C\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (A_{\mathrm{ij}} + B_{\mathrm{ij}})\,C_{\mathrm{jk}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,C_{\mathrm{jk}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} B_{\mathrm{ij}}\,C_{\mathrm{jk}}\), the entry of the matrix \(\mat A\mat C + \mat B\mat C\). We need both equalities separately, because we may not change the order of the factors in a matrix product, as item 4 shows.
  4. The entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \((c\,\mat A)\,\mat B\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\), that of the matrix \(\mat A\,(c\,\mat B)\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\). In both sums \(c\) is a common factor of all terms. Taking it out, we get \(c \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\), the entry of the matrix \(c\,(\mat A\mat B)\).

4. The order of the factors.

  1. Take the square matrices of order \(3\)
    \[\mat D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]
  2. By (1.40) the entry in row \(x\) and column \(z\) of the product \(\mat D\mat G\) is the row \((2, 0, 0)\) times the column \((1, 0, 1)\), that is, \(2 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 2\).
  3. The entry at the same position of the product \(\mat G\mat D\) is the row \((1, 0, 1)\) times the column \((0, 0, 1)\), that is, \(1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1\).
  4. The matrices \(\mat D\mat G\) and \(\mat G\mat D\) do not agree in all entries at the same positions, so they are not equal (definition 1.93). The matrices \(\mat A = \mat D\) and \(\mat B = \mat G\) are the pair we wanted.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (2a) to (2d) and Example 4 (PDF p. 290), CM §2.3.5, remarks (4) and (7) (PDF p. 64).

Item 1 is the reason for the definition: the matrix product is a single matrix that does the same as both matrices one after the other. Item 2 says that with three rules one after the other it does not matter which two we combine into one matrix first, so we write no brackets and write \(\mat A\mat B\mat C\). Item 4, however, warns of a difference from numbers. For numbers \(2 \cdot 3 = 3 \cdot 2\), but for matrices we may not in general change the order of the factors, because two deformations one after the other in the reverse order are a different deformation, as we saw in figure 1.129. For some pairs of matrices the product is the same in both orders, for example for two matrices whose nonzero entries all lie on the main diagonal, like \(\mat D\). Such matrices, call them \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\), stretch a vector along the axes, and it does not matter which stretching we do first. By calculation: in the sum \((\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ik}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,B_{\mathrm{jk}}\) at most the term with \(j = i = k\) is nonzero, so the product has nonzero entries only on the main diagonal, namely \(A_{\mathrm{ii}}\,B_{\mathrm{ii}}\). For the product \(\mat B\mat A\) these are \(B_{\mathrm{ii}}\,A_{\mathrm{ii}}\), the same numbers.

1.16.4 The transposed matrix and the split

In section 1.7.2 we noticed in the table of partial derivatives of the hanging bar pairs off the main diagonal that are equal in size and opposite in sign: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\). We showed that such a pair means a small turn of the material without shear. The two entries of such a pair lie in the matrix as mirror images across the main diagonal: the first in row \(x\) and column \(y\), the second in row \(y\) and column \(x\). To compare a matrix with its mirror image, we need a matrix in which the rows and the columns exchange their roles.

Definition 1.100 Transposed matrix (transponirana matrika)

For a matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(m \times n\) the transposed matrix (transponirana matrika) \(\mat A^\T\) is the matrix of size \(n \times m\) with the entries

\[(\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}} , \qquad i = 1, \dots, n , \quad j = 1, \dots, m .\]

So row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A^\T\) is column \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A\). A column \(\vek v\) with \(n\) entries thereby becomes the row \(\vek v^\T = [\,v_1\ \ v_2\ \cdots\ v_{\mathrm{n}}\,]\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (9) (PDF pp. 292-293), CM §2.3.3 (PDF p. 62).

For a square matrix, transposition replaces every entry by its mirror image across the main diagonal, while the entries on the main diagonal stay in place, because for them \(i = j\) (figure 1.130). For the sheared cube, the only nonzero entry of its matrix \(\mat K\) moves from row \(x\) and column \(z\) to row \(z\) and column \(x\):

\[\mat K^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0{,}001 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

For the hanging bar, the two entries of the pair in the matrix \(\mat V\) of its partial derivatives at the point \(P\) (section 1.16.2) swap places: in \(\mat V^\T\) the entry \(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\) is in row \(y\) and column \(x\), and \(4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\) in row \(x\) and column \(y\). We read \(\mat A^\T\) as "A transposed".

Transposing twice gives back the original matrix, since every entry is mirrored twice. With sums and products it gets along as follows.

Lemma 1.101 Transposing a sum, a multiple and a product (transponiranje vsote, večkratnika in produkta)

For matrices whose sizes allow the sums and products below, and for every number \(c\):

  1. \((\mat A^\T)^\T = \mat A\),
  2. \((\mat A + \mat B)^\T = \mat A^\T + \mat B^\T\) and \((c\,\mat A)^\T = c\,\mat A^\T\),
  3. \((\mat A\mat B)^\T = \mat B^\T\mat A^\T\),
  4. for a column \(\vek v\) with \(m\) entries, a matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(m \times n\) and a column \(\vek w\) with \(n\) entries, \(\vek v^\T\mat A\vek w\) is a matrix of size \(1 \times 1\) whose only entry is
    \[\vek v^\T\mat A\vek w = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{m} \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} v_{\mathrm{i}}\,A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}} . \tag{1.41}\]
Proof

We must show four items. The first three are equalities of matrices, which we check entry by entry (definition 1.93), and in the fourth we compute the only entry.

1. Transposing twice.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be of size \(m \times n\). Then \(\mat A^\T\) is of size \(n \times m\) and \((\mat A^\T)^\T\) of size \(m \times n\), the same as \(\mat A\).
  2. By definition 1.100, used twice, \(\big((\mat A^\T)^\T\big)_{\mathrm{ij}} = (\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{ji}} = A_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

2. Sum and multiple.

  1. By definition 1.100 and definition 1.94, \(\big((\mat A + \mat B)^\T\big)_{\mathrm{ij}} = (\mat A + \mat B)_{\mathrm{ji}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}} + B_{\mathrm{ji}}\).
  2. By definition 1.100, \(A_{\mathrm{ji}} + B_{\mathrm{ji}} = (\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}} + (\mat B^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which is the entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat A^\T + \mat B^\T\).
  3. In the same way, \(\big((c\,\mat A)^\T\big)_{\mathrm{ij}} = (c\,\mat A)_{\mathrm{ji}} = c\,A_{\mathrm{ji}} = c\,(\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the entry of the matrix \(c\,\mat A^\T\).

3. Product.

  1. Let \(\mat A\) be of size \(m \times n\) and \(\mat B\) of size \(n \times p\). The matrix \(\mat A\mat B\) is of size \(m \times p\), so \((\mat A\mat B)^\T\) is of size \(p \times m\). The matrix \(\mat B^\T\) is of size \(p \times n\) and \(\mat A^\T\) of size \(n \times m\), so the product \(\mat B^\T\mat A^\T\) exists and is of size \(p \times m\).
  2. By definition 1.100, \(\big((\mat A\mat B)^\T\big)_{\mathrm{ik}} = (\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ki}}\).
  3. By (1.40), \((\mat A\mat B)_{\mathrm{ki}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{kj}}\,B_{\mathrm{ji}}\).
  4. In each term we change the order of the factors, which we may do for numbers: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} B_{\mathrm{ji}}\,A_{\mathrm{kj}}\).
  5. By definition 1.100, \(B_{\mathrm{ji}} = (\mat B^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{kj}} = (\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{jk}}\), so the sum equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} (\mat B^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}}\,(\mat A^\T)_{\mathrm{jk}}\).
  6. By (1.40) this is the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat B^\T\mat A^\T\).

4. The double sum.

  1. The column \(\mat A\vek w\) has \(m\) entries, and by (1.38) its entry \(i\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. The row \(\vek v^\T\) is a matrix of size \(1 \times m\), so by definition 1.98 the product \(\vek v^\T\,(\mat A\vek w)\) is a matrix of size \(1 \times 1\). By item 2 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product it equals the matrix \((\vek v^\T\mat A)\,\vek w\), so we write no brackets.
  3. By (1.40) its only entry is the row \(\vek v^\T\) times the column \(\mat A\vek w\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{m} v_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. We multiply the factor \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) into the inner sum by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number and get (1.41).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (10a) to (10d) (PDF p. 293), CM §2.3.3 and §2.3.5, remark (9) (PDF pp. 62 and 64), KREYSZIG §8.4, (7) (PDF p. 369) for item 4.

In item 3 the order is reversed. Why? Transposition exchanges rows and columns, and an entry of a product is a row of the first matrix times a column of the second. After the exchange the rows of the first matrix become columns, and for the product to be row times column again, the transposed first matrix must stand on the right.

We will identify the matrix of size \(1 \times 1\) of item 4 with its only entry, since a table with a single number holds exactly that number and we calculate with it as with the number. The double sum (1.41) has \(m \cdot n\) terms, one for each entry of the matrix. Each entry \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is multiplied by the entry \(i\) of the column on the left and the entry \(j\) of the column on the right. Without a matrix in between, the product of a row and a column with three entries is by (1.40) \(\vek v^\T\vek w = v_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,w_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{z}}\), and for \(\vek w = \vek v\) it is \(\vek v^\T\vek v = v_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + v_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + v_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = |\vek v|^2\) by (1.10). The sum of the products of matching components of two vectors will appear in chapter 2 as a concept of its own with a geometric meaning.

Now we can name the matrices that do not change under transposition or only change their sign.

Definition 1.102 Symmetric and antisymmetric matrix (simetrična in antisimetrična matrika)

A square matrix \(\mat A\) is symmetric (simetrična) if \(\mat A^\T = \mat A\), that is, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = A_{\mathrm{ji}}\) for all \(i\) and \(j\), and antisymmetric (antisimetrična) if \(\mat A^\T = -\mat A\), that is, \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = -A_{\mathrm{ji}}\) for all \(i\) and \(j\). For a square matrix \(\mat A\) the matrix \(\tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\) is its symmetric part (simetrični del) and the matrix \(\tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\) its antisymmetric part (antisimetrični del).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (11) (PDF p. 294), CM §2.3.4 (PDF p. 62).

So a symmetric matrix equals its mirror image across the main diagonal, while in an antisymmetric matrix the mirror image of every entry has the opposite sign. KREYSZIG calls an antisymmetric matrix skew-symmetric (KREYSZIG §7.2, PDF p. 294). The matrix \(\mat K\) of the cube is neither symmetric nor antisymmetric: the entry \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\) has the mirror image \(0\), which is neither \(0{,}001\) nor \(-0{,}001\). The matrix \(\mat V\) of the hanging bar is neither one nor the other either. Its pair off the diagonal is antisymmetric, but the entries on the main diagonal are not zero, which an antisymmetric matrix does not allow, as we will show in the next lemma. Every square matrix, however, can be written as the sum of a symmetric and an antisymmetric one.

Lemma 1.103 Split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts (razcep na simetrični in antisimetrični del)

For every square matrix \(\mat A\):

  1. its symmetric part \(\tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\) is a symmetric matrix, its antisymmetric part \(\tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\) is an antisymmetric matrix, and their sum is \(\mat A\):
    \[\mat A = \tfrac12\,(\mat A + \mat A^\T) + \tfrac12\,(\mat A - \mat A^\T) , \tag{1.42}\]
  2. the split is unique: if \(\mat A = \mat B + \mat C\), where \(\mat B\) is a symmetric and \(\mat C\) an antisymmetric matrix, then \(\mat B = \tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\) and \(\mat C = \tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\),
  3. the entries on the main diagonal of an antisymmetric matrix are zero.
Proof

We must show three items. We use the rules of matrix arithmetic (lemma 1.95) and the rules for transposition (lemma 1.101).

1. The split.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 1.101, \(\big(\tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\big)^\T = \tfrac12\big(\mat A^\T + (\mat A^\T)^\T\big)\).
  2. By item 1 of the same lemma, \((\mat A^\T)^\T = \mat A\), and by rule 1 of lemma 1.95, \(\mat A^\T + \mat A = \mat A + \mat A^\T\). So the symmetric part equals its transposed matrix and is symmetric.
  3. In the same way, \(\big(\tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\big)^\T = \tfrac12\,(\mat A^\T - \mat A)\), where we transpose the difference as the sum \(\mat A + (-1)\,\mat A^\T\) by item 2 of lemma 1.101.
  4. The matrix \(\mat A^\T - \mat A\) has the entries \(A_{\mathrm{ji}} - A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which are the opposites of the entries \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} - A_{\mathrm{ji}}\) of the matrix \(\mat A - \mat A^\T\) (definition 1.94). So \(\tfrac12\,(\mat A^\T - \mat A) = -\tfrac12\,(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\), and the antisymmetric part is antisymmetric.
  5. By rules 4 and 5 of lemma 1.95 the sum of the two parts is \(\tfrac12\mat A + \tfrac12\mat A^\T + \tfrac12\mat A - \tfrac12\mat A^\T = (\tfrac12 + \tfrac12)\,\mat A + (\tfrac12 - \tfrac12)\,\mat A^\T = \mat A\).

2. Uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\mat A = \mat B + \mat C\), where \(\mat B^\T = \mat B\) and \(\mat C^\T = -\mat C\).
  2. We transpose the equation. By item 2 of lemma 1.101, \(\mat A^\T = \mat B^\T + \mat C^\T = \mat B - \mat C\). This gives a second equation for the matrices \(\mat B\) and \(\mat C\).
  3. We add the two equations. The matrices \(\mat C\) and \(-\mat C\) cancel, and \(\mat A + \mat A^\T = 2\,\mat B\) remains. Multiplying by \(\tfrac12\) gives \(\mat B = \tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\).
  4. We subtract the second equation from the first. What remains is \(\mat A - \mat A^\T = 2\,\mat C\), and after multiplying by \(\tfrac12\), \(\mat C = \tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\).

3. The main diagonal of an antisymmetric matrix.

  1. Let \(\mat C\) be antisymmetric. By definition 1.102, \(C_{\mathrm{ij}} = -C_{\mathrm{ji}}\) for all \(i\) and \(j\), so also for \(j = i\): \(C_{\mathrm{ii}} = -C_{\mathrm{ii}}\).
  2. We add \(C_{\mathrm{ii}}\) to both sides and get \(2\,C_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\), so \(C_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\).

Source: CM (2.3.3) and §2.3.4 (PDF p. 62), KREYSZIG §7.2, (11) (PDF p. 294). The proof of uniqueness is not from a source.

The split into parts is like writing a number as a sum, only here the two parts are determined: the symmetric part is the average of the matrix and its mirror image, the antisymmetric part half of their difference. The entry of the symmetric part in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is \(\tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + A_{\mathrm{ji}})\), the entry of the antisymmetric part \(\tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{ij}} - A_{\mathrm{ji}})\).

For the hanging bar at the point \(P\), the symmetric part of the matrix \(\mat V\) is just its main diagonal, since the two entries of the pair, \(\mp 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\), cancel in the average, and the zeros stay zeros. The antisymmetric part is the pair itself:

\[\begin{aligned} \mat V &= \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} \\ &\quad + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & 0 \\ 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} . \end{aligned}\]

This is exactly what we learned about the bar in section 1.7.2. The symmetric part describes the stretching along the bar and the thinning across it, the antisymmetric part a small turn of the material close to the point \(P\) without shear.

For the cube the split is more instructive, because there the same shear splits into two parts that we can draw. By (1.42),

\[\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}0005 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0{,}0005 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}0005 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -0{,}0005 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

What does each part do to the front face \(ABCD\) of the cube (figure 1.131)? In micrometres the corners are \(A = (0, 0, 0)\), \(B = (10\,000, 0, 0)\), \(C = (10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) and \(D = (0, 0, 10\,000)\), and we multiply each part by the vectors of the edges and the diagonals from \(A\).

  1. The symmetric part moves the end of the edge \(AB\) by \((0, 0, 5)\), upward, and the end of the edge \(AD\) by \((5, 0, 0)\), to the right. Each edge tilts toward the other by five ten-thousandths of its length, and the right angle at \(A\) closes equally from both sides. It moves the end of the diagonal \(AC\) by \((5, 0, 5)\), which is \(0{,}0005\) times the vector \((10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\): the diagonal gets longer by five ten-thousandths. The vector of the diagonal \(BD\), \((-10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\), however, gives \((5, 0, -5)\), which is \(-0{,}0005\) times this vector: the diagonal gets shorter by five ten-thousandths.
  2. The antisymmetric part moves the end of the edge \(AB\) by \((0, 0, -5)\), downward, and the end of the edge \(AD\) by \((5, 0, 0)\), to the right. Both edges tilt by five ten-thousandths of their length in the same sense, clockwise when we look at the face with the axis \(x\) to the right and the axis \(z\) up, as if we turned the face. The end of the diagonal \(AC\) moves by \((5, 0, -5)\), in the direction of the other diagonal, which is perpendicular to \(AC\), because the diagonals of a square are perpendicular. So the diagonal turns but does not get longer.
  3. The sum of the two movements is the movement due to the matrix \(\mat K\). The end of the edge \(AB\) moves by \((0, 0, 5) + (0, 0, -5) = (0, 0, 0)\), the end of the edge \(AD\) by \((5, 0, 0) + (5, 0, 0) = (10, 0, 0)\). This is the shear of section 1.6.2.

So the shear of the cube is a stretching along one diagonal and a shortening along the other, to which a small turn of the whole face is added. How exactly is the antisymmetric part a turn? A true turn does not change lengths at all. The edge \(AB\), however, becomes \((10\,000, 0, 0) + (0, 0, -5) = (10\,000, 0, -5)\) under the antisymmetric part, with the length \(\sqrt{10\,000^2 + 5^2} = 10\,000{,}00125\) µm, which is \(1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of the length more. This is the square of the number \(0{,}0005\) divided by \(2\), a term of the same kind as the strain \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of the edge \(AD\) in section 1.6.2, which was the square of the ratio \(0{,}001\) divided by \(2\) and grew with the square of the push. So the antisymmetric part is a turn only up to such negligible terms.

The double sum (1.41) with the same column on both sides has one more property, which we will need in section 1.17.

Corollary 1.104 The double sum and the antisymmetric part (dvojna vsota in antisimetrični del)

Let \(\vek v\) be a column with \(n\) entries. Then:

  1. for every antisymmetric matrix \(\mat C\) of order \(n\), \(\vek v^\T\mat C\vek v = 0\),
  2. for every square matrix \(\mat A\) of order \(n\), \(\vek v^\T\mat A\vek v = \vek v^\T \big(\tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\big)\,\vek v\).
Proof

We must show that an antisymmetric matrix contributes nothing to the double sum, and that therefore only the symmetric part contributes.

1. An antisymmetric matrix.

  1. By (1.41), \(\vek v^\T\mat C\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} v_{\mathrm{i}}\,C_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\), a sum of \(n^2\) terms, one for each pair of indices \((i, j)\).
  2. The terms with \(i = j\) are zero, because by item 3 of lemma 1.103 the entries \(C_{\mathrm{ii}}\) on the main diagonal of an antisymmetric matrix are zero.
  3. We divide the terms with \(i \neq j\) into pairs: the term for the pair \((i, j)\) and the term for the pair \((j, i)\). The sum of the two is \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\,C_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} + v_{\mathrm{j}}\,C_{\mathrm{ji}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = v_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\,(C_{\mathrm{ij}} + C_{\mathrm{ji}})\), after we rearrange the factors and take out \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. The matrix \(\mat C\) is antisymmetric, so \(C_{\mathrm{ji}} = -C_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 1.102), and the sum of each pair is zero.
  5. Every term is either zero or in a pair whose sum is zero, so the whole sum is zero.

2. The symmetric part.

  1. By item 1 of lemma 1.103, \(\mat A = \mat B + \mat C\), where \(\mat B = \tfrac12(\mat A + \mat A^\T)\) is symmetric and \(\mat C = \tfrac12(\mat A - \mat A^\T)\) antisymmetric.
  2. By item 3 of lemma 1.99, used twice, \(\vek v^\T\,(\mat B + \mat C)\,\vek v = \vek v^\T\mat B\vek v + \vek v^\T\mat C\vek v\).
  3. By item 1, \(\vek v^\T\mat C\vek v = 0\), so \(\vek v^\T\mat A\vek v = \vek v^\T\mat B\vek v\).

Source: KREYSZIG §8.4, (7) and Example 5 (PDF p. 369) says that in the double sum \(\vek v^\T\mat A\vek v\) the matrix may be replaced by a symmetric one. The proof is not from a source.

Books call the expression \(\vek v^\T\mat A\vek v\) a quadratic form (kvadratna forma) (KREYSZIG §8.4, (7), PDF p. 369), because each of its terms is a product of two components of the vector. Let us try the corollary on the cube. Take the unit vector along the diagonal \(AC\), \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), whose length is \(\sqrt{1/2 + 0 + 1/2} = 1\). In the double sum with the matrix \(\mat K\) only the term with the entry \(K_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is nonzero:

\[\vek n^\T\mat K\vek n = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,K_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \cdot 0{,}001 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = 0{,}0005 .\]

The symmetric part gives the same, from two terms of \(0{,}00025\) each, and the antisymmetric part gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}0005 - \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}0005 = 0\). For the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), \(\vek n = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), we get \(-0{,}0005\). These are the strains of the diagonals of section 1.6.2, \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\), up to the small terms with the square of the push. The agreement is no coincidence. In section 1.17 we will show that the strain of a short fibre in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek n\) is approximately such a double sum with the matrix of partial derivatives. The corollary tells why the turn does not count there: the antisymmetric part moves the end of a fibre across it, as for the diagonal \(AC\) above, so it turns the fibre but does not lengthen it.

Books write matrices in different ways. CM writes a matrix in square brackets, \([A]\), and its entries with a small letter, \(a_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (CM §2.3.1, PDF p. 60), while KREYSZIG writes it with a bold letter \(\mathbf A\) and its entries as \(a_{\mathrm{jk}}\) (KREYSZIG §7.1, PDF p. 284). SADD writes the matrix of partial derivatives of the displacement as \(u_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\), where a comma in the index means the partial derivative with respect to the variable with the index \(j\) (SADD (2.1.4), (2.1.5), PDF p. 40), and CM with the sign \(\nabla\) and a transposition (CM (3.3.8), PDF p. 120). We will write \(\mat A\) and \(A_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and partial derivatives always with the sign \(\partial\).

1.16.5 The identity matrix and the inverse matrix

For the sheared cube, whose matrix \(\mat K\) has the only nonzero entry \(0{,}001\) in row \(x\) and column \(z\), we wrote the vector of a fibre after loading as \(\vek v + \mat K\vek v\). We would like to write it as a single product with a matrix, just as in section 1.16.3 we wrote the vector with the components \(v_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,v_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\). For this we need a matrix that leaves the vector \(\vek v\) unchanged, and we add it to the matrix \(\mat K\).

Definition 1.105 Identity matrix (enotska matrika)

The identity matrix (enotska matrika) of order \(n\), \(\mat I\), is the square matrix of order \(n\) that has ones on the main diagonal, while all other entries are zero:

\[I_{\mathrm{ij}} = 1 \ \text{for}\ i = j , \qquad I_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0 \ \text{for}\ i \neq j .\]

For \(n = 3\) it is

\[\mat I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2 (PDF p. 294), CM §2.3.1 (PDF p. 60).

The columns of the identity matrix of order \(3\) are the unit vectors of the axes \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) ((1.12)), written vertically. When the order matters we write it as an index, \(\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\), otherwise we read it off the matrices that we add to \(\mat I\) or multiply by it.

Lemma 1.106 Multiplication by the identity matrix (množenje z enotsko matriko)

For every column \(\vek v\) with \(n\) entries, \(\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek v = \vek v\). For every matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(m \times n\), \(\mat I_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat A = \mat A\) and \(\mat A\,\mat I_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat A\).

Proof

We must show that the two sides of each equality agree in all entries.

  1. By (1.38) the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek v\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} I_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. By definition 1.105, \(I_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) in this sum for every \(j \neq i\), so all terms except the term with \(j = i\) are zero. That term is \(I_{\mathrm{ii}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = 1 \cdot v_{\mathrm{i}} = v_{\mathrm{i}}\). So the entry \(i\) of the column \(\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek v\) is \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\), and \(\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek v = \vek v\).
  3. By (1.40) the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat I_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat A\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{m} I_{\mathrm{ij}}\,A_{\mathrm{jk}}\). As in step 2, only the term with \(j = i\) remains, which is \(A_{\mathrm{ik}}\).
  4. The entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat A\,\mat I_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n} A_{\mathrm{ij}}\,I_{\mathrm{jk}}\). Only the term with \(j = k\) remains, which is \(A_{\mathrm{ik}} \cdot 1 = A_{\mathrm{ik}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.2, (13) (PDF p. 294), CM §2.3.5, remark (8) (PDF p. 64).

In the multiplication of matrices the identity matrix plays the role that the number \(1\) plays in the multiplication of numbers. For the cube we now have \(\vek v + \mat K\vek v = \mat I\vek v + \mat K\vek v = (\mat I + \mat K)\,\vek v\), by lemma 1.106 and item 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, used for the column \(\vek v\). The matrix

\[\mat I + \mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

is exactly the matrix we guessed at the beginning of section 1.16.3. It sends the vector of every fibre before loading to its vector after loading. For the edge \(AD\), \((\mat I + \mat K)\,(0, 0, 10\,000) = (10, 0, 10\,000)\).

Can we reverse the way and compute the vector of a fibre before loading from its vector after loading? For the cube the answer is simple: we push the top face back by 10 µm. This rule subtracts \(0{,}001\) times the component \(z\) from the component \(x\), and its matrix is \(\mat I - \mat K\). For the edge \(AD\) the component \(x\) of the product \((\mat I - \mat K)\,(10, 0, 10\,000)\) is \(10 - 0{,}001 \cdot 10\,000 = 0\), and the other two components are \(0\) and \(10\,000\). So the product is \((0, 0, 10\,000)\), the edge before loading. Let us check that this holds for every fibre by computing the product of the two matrices.

  1. By item 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, \((\mat I - \mat K)(\mat I + \mat K) = \mat I\mat I + \mat I\mat K - \mat K\mat I - \mat K\mat K\).
  2. By lemma 1.106, \(\mat I\mat I = \mat I\), \(\mat I\mat K = \mat K\) and \(\mat K\mat I = \mat K\), so the two middle terms cancel, and \(\mat I - \mat K\mat K\) remains.
  3. The entry \((i, k)\) of the product \(\mat K\mat K\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,K_{\mathrm{jk}}\) ((1.40)). Only a term with \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} \neq 0\) could be nonzero, that is, a term with \(i = x\) and \(j = z\). That term is \(K_{\mathrm{xz}}\,K_{\mathrm{zk}}\), and \(K_{\mathrm{zk}} = 0\) for every \(k\), because row \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) is zero. So \(\mat K\mat K = \mat 0\).
  4. Therefore \((\mat I - \mat K)(\mat I + \mat K) = \mat I\). In the same way \((\mat I + \mat K)(\mat I - \mat K) = \mat I + \mat K - \mat K - \mat K\mat K = \mat I\).

So the matrix \(\mat I - \mat K\) undoes the action of the matrix \(\mat I + \mat K\), in both orders (figure 1.132, top). By the way, \(\mat K\mat K = \mat 0\) although \(\mat K\) is not the zero matrix. With numbers nothing like this happens, with matrices it does. A matrix that undoes another gets a name.

Definition 1.107 Inverse matrix (inverzna matrika)

Let \(\mat A\) be a square matrix of order \(n\). A square matrix \(\mat B\) of order \(n\) for which

\[\mat A\mat B = \mat B\mat A = \mat I \tag{1.43}\]

is an inverse matrix (inverzna matrika) of the matrix \(\mat A\). A matrix that has an inverse matrix is invertible (obrnljiva).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.8, (1) (PDF p. 327), CM §2.3.6 (PDF p. 65).

The definition asks for both equalities, \(\mat A\mat B = \mat I\) and \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\), because the matrix product is not commutative (item 4 of lemma 1.99), and we do not know in advance that one follows from the other. By the calculation above, the matrix \(\mat I - \mat K\) is an inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat I + \mat K\). The name invertible says that the action of such a matrix can be reversed: from \(\mat A\vek v\) we get \(\vek v\) back, since by item 1 of lemma 1.99 and lemma 1.106 \(\mat B\,(\mat A\vek v) = (\mat B\mat A)\,\vek v = \mat I\vek v = \vek v\). Books also call an invertible matrix nonsingular (nesingularna), and a matrix without an inverse singular (singularna) (KREYSZIG §7.8, PDF p. 327). We define the inverse matrix only for square matrices, as both sources do (KREYSZIG §7.8, PDF p. 327, CM §2.3.6, PDF p. 65). Can a matrix have two different inverse matrices? It cannot.

Lemma 1.108 Uniqueness of the inverse matrix (enoličnost inverzne matrike)

If \(\mat B\) and \(\mat C\) are inverse matrices of a square matrix \(\mat A\), then \(\mat B = \mat C\).

Proof

We must show that \(\mat B\) and \(\mat C\) are equal. We turn the matrix \(\mat B\) into \(\mat C\) by a chain of equalities.

  1. By lemma 1.106, \(\mat B = \mat B\,\mat I\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat C\) is an inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat A\), so \(\mat A\mat C = \mat I\) ((1.43)), and \(\mat B = \mat B\,(\mat A\mat C)\).
  3. By item 2 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, \(\mat B\,(\mat A\mat C) = (\mat B\mat A)\,\mat C\).
  4. The matrix \(\mat B\) is an inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat A\), so \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\), and \((\mat B\mat A)\,\mat C = \mat I\,\mat C = \mat C\) by lemma 1.106.
  5. Steps 1 to 4 give \(\mat B = \mat C\).

Source: CM §2.3.6 (PDF p. 65), KREYSZIG §7.8 (PDF p. 327).

Since the inverse matrix is unique, we may denote it by a single symbol, \(\mat A^{-1}\). For the cube, \((\mat I + \mat K)^{-1} = \mat I - \mat K\). The proof used only the grouping of factors and the identity matrix, nothing else.

Does every square matrix have an inverse matrix? No. Take the matrix

\[\mat N = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

for which \(\mat N\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\). It pushes every vector into the horizontal plane \(z = 0\), as if we pressed a piece of material into a plate with no thickness. Let us show that \(\mat N\) is not invertible.

  1. Suppose that \(\mat B\) is a matrix of order \(3\) with \(\mat B\mat N = \mat I\).
  2. By (1.38), \(\mat N\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 0)\). Row \(x\) of the matrix \(\mat N\), \((1, 0, 0)\), times the column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1)\) gives \(0\), and so does row \(y\), \((0, 1, 0)\), while row \(z\) is zero.
  3. By item 1 of lemma 1.99 therefore \((\mat B\mat N)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \mat B\,(\mat N\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \mat B\,\vek 0\), and every entry of the product \(\mat B\,\vek 0\) is a sum of products with zero, so zero.
  4. By assumption and lemma 1.106, however, \((\mat B\mat N)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \mat I\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1)\), which is not the zero vector. This contradicts step 3.
  5. So there is no matrix \(\mat B\) with \(\mat B\mat N = \mat I\), and \(\mat N\) has no inverse matrix.

Why it fails is shown in figure 1.132, bottom. The matrix \(\mat N\) sends the vectors \(\vek a = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek b = (1, 0, 1)\), which are different, to the same vector \((1, 0, 0)\). From the result we therefore cannot tell which vector it came from, and no rule can bring it back. In the deformation of a body this does not happen. A fibre that has a positive length before loading cannot have the length zero after loading, because that is physically impossible, and CM concludes from this that the matrices which send the vector of every fibre before loading to its vector after loading are invertible (CM §3.3.1, PDF pp. 119-120). That a matrix which sends no nonzero vector to the zero vector really has an inverse matrix, and how to compute that inverse matrix, we will show in chapter 2. There we will compute from the entries of a square matrix of order \(3\) a single number that tells whether the matrix is invertible.

Let us sum up how we arrived at these tools. We wrote the nine partial derivatives of (1.36) as a matrix and the three sums as the product of the matrix and the column of the step, and got the single equation (1.39). On the cube we saw that one entry, \(0{,}001\), of the matrix \(\mat K\) carries the whole deformation, and that the vector of every fibre after loading is the matrix \(\mat I + \mat K\) times its vector before loading. On the bar, the product \(\mat V\vek n\) gave us in one calculation all three components of the difference of displacements of a fibre per unit length. We derived the product of two matrices from two rules one after the other and saw that the order matters. The split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts divided the shear of the cube into a stretching along the diagonals and a turn, and the double sum does not see the antisymmetric part. In the next section we will use these tools to compute the strain of a short fibre in any direction for every continuously differentiable displacement field. The vector that a short fibre has after loading will be approximately the product of the matrix of partial derivatives plus the identity matrix and the vector before loading. We will write the square of its length with transposition, and the split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts will separate the stretching of the material from its turning.
