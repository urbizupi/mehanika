Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.7 Piecewise linear trial fields

The Ritz method (section 6.4) finds the best field in a family of fields that we choose. For the hanging bar we built the family from polynomials that run over the whole length. This choice has two weaknesses. First, a single polynomial cannot have a kink, such as the displacement of a bar with a step in its cross section has (section 6.1.4), because a polynomial has a continuous derivative everywhere. Second, the functions \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) become more and more alike on the interval, so the system of equations for the coefficients becomes harder and harder to solve, as we saw in section 6.3. What happens if we cut the bar into pieces and take a straight line on each piece?

Figure 6.114 shows both weaknesses. On the left are the axes for \(x\) and the displacement \(u\), the displacement of the bar with a step has a kink, and a smooth polynomial does not catch it. On the right, on axes, are the powers \(x, x^2, x^3, x^4\) on \([0, 1]\), which become alike as the degree grows.

We will find the answer in four steps. First we look for the functions from which any continuous function made of straight pieces is built. With them we solve the hanging bar that we already know, and look at how wrong the approximation is. Then we apply the same idea to the bar with a step, where polynomials fail. Finally we show why the idea does not carry over to the beam.

6.7.1 Hat functions

The idea of taking the pieces separately did not arise with bars. Let us see how it arose and what it solved.

From history

The Rayleigh-Ritz method and Galerkin's weighted-residual approach reduced, according to GR (PDF p. 354), a problem with infinitely many degrees of freedom to a finite number of unknowns. In their original form, however, they lacked the flexibility that systems of complex shape with arbitrary boundary conditions and arbitrary load distribution demand (GR, PDF p. 354). According to COOK (PDF p. 170), the classical Rayleigh-Ritz method regarded the entire structure as a single domain and spanned it with a single trial function. According to GR (PDF p. 354), the era of finite elements formally started with a lecture by R. Courant in 1941. In it he introduced, for the classical problem of torsion, which chapter 16 treats, the key idea of spatial subdivision of the domain (GR, PDF p. 354). The written form of the lecture is Courant's paper of 1943 (COOK, PDF p. 24). In it Courant divided the cross section of a hollow shaft into triangles and interpolated the stress function linearly over each triangle from its values at chosen points, which we now call nodes (COOK, PDF p. 24). With this he determined the torsional rigidity of the hollow shaft (COOK, PDF p. 24). Courant himself wrote that the method suggests a wide generalization which provides great flexibility and seems to have considerable practical value (COOK, PDF p. 24). He did not pursue the idea, because computers were still largely unavailable for his research (GR, PDF p. 354). Practical use appeared only later, when aeronautical engineers developed the method, according to COOK (PDF p. 24) apparently without knowing of Courant's work. The real development was triggered, according to GR (PDF p. 354), by the needs of the aerospace industry and by the progress of computers and matrix computation. In 1954 J. H. Argyris published a monograph on matrix structural analysis, and in 1956 R. W. Clough and his colleagues formulated the finite element method as a natural extension of matrix structural analysis (GR, PDF p. 354).

The sources give the year of Courant's introduction of triangles as 1946 (ONATE1, PDF p. 63) and as 1943 (ONATE1, PDF p. 472, COOK, PDF p. 24).

Let us return to the hanging bar, which we have so far solved with polynomials over the whole length. Courant's idea, carried over to the bar, is simple: we cut the interval \([0, L]\) into the elements of a mesh and take a straight line on each. We met the building blocks of such fields, the hat functions, in section 6.3.3 (definitions 6.24 and 6.25). There, for the bar fixed at \(x = 0\), we took only \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(\psi_0(0) = 1\). Here we take all of them, from \(\psi_0\) to \(\psi_{\mathrm{n}}\), so that the lemma that follows holds.

The function \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is zero outside the two elements next to the node \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), so it touches only its neighbours. For \(i = 0\) and \(i = n\) it has a single element, since an end node has only one neighbour. Each element, for example \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\), is carried by exactly two hat functions, \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\). On this element they equal \((x_{\mathrm{k}} - x)/h_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \((x - x_{\mathrm{k}-1})/h_{\mathrm{k}}\). In chapter 7 we will call them the shape functions of the element (oblikovni funkciji elementa). Figure 6.115 shows a mesh with four elements and shows how the values at the nodes combine into a function. On the \(x\) axis are the nodes \(x_0, \dots, x_4\), the distance between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) is the element length \(h_2\). The dots are the values \(u_1, \dots, u_4\) at the nodes. The blue dashed triangles are the hat functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) multiplied by \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), and their sum is the blackwhite broken line \(u\).

The figure shows what the next lemma says in general. Every continuous function that runs along a straight line on each element (blackwhite broken line) is a sum of hat functions multiplied by the values at the nodes, and these numbers cannot be chosen differently.

Lemma 6.48 Continuous piecewise linear functions (zvezne funkcije po kosih po premicah)

Let \(x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a mesh of the interval \([0, L]\) and \(\psi_0, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) its hat functions. For every continuous function \(u : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) that runs along a straight line on each element (broken line),

\[u = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n} u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}} , \tag{6.32}\]

and if \(u = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) for numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\), then \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every \(i\).

Proof

We must show that the difference between \(u\) and the sum of hat functions is zero everywhere and that the coefficients are unique.

  1. Let \(w = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n} u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Each hat function is continuous and runs along a straight line on each element (definition 6.25). A sum and a multiple of such functions is again such a function, since the sum of two straight lines on an element is again a straight line. So \(w\) is continuous and runs along a straight line on each element. In figure 6.115, \(w\) is the sum of the blue triangles.
  2. At the node \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\), so \(w(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = u(x_{\mathrm{j}})\) for every \(j\): at a node only one triangle differs from zero, and its apex is the dot \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. Let \(d = w - u\). The function \(d\) is continuous, runs along a straight line on each element \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\), and by step 2 is zero at both ends of the element.
  4. A straight line \(d(x) = \alpha + \beta x\) on an element with \(d(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) = d(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = 0\) has slope \(\beta = (d(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - d(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}))/(x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) = 0\), so it is a constant, and since it is zero at an end point, it is zero everywhere on the element. This holds for every element, also for the element between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), so \(d = 0\) and \(u = w\).
  5. Let \(u = \sum c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). At the node \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(u(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = c_{\mathrm{j}}\), because \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for \(i \ne j\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\). So \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = u(x_{\mathrm{j}})\). The same computation is the proof of the independence of hat functions (lemma 6.26).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The statement is described in ONATE1 §2.4, (2.30) (PDF p. 76 to 77).

The lemma says that hat functions span exactly all continuous functions made of straight pieces, and that each coefficient is a displacement at a node. With polynomials the coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) tells us nothing about what happens at any particular point. Here \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), a number with the unit of length, which we can read as the displacement of the node, the height of the dot above the node.

6.7.2 The hanging bar with hat functions

Now we use hat functions in the Ritz method. Take the same bar as in section 4.6.8: a steel hanging bar of length \(L = 10\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\), with the axis \(x\) pointing down. It has axial stiffness \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N, its only load is its own weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m per unit length, and the lower end is free. The true field is \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(u(L) = 19{,}52\) µm. For the bar (section 6.1.3) we have \(B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\). The condition \(u(0) = 0\) is essential and every trial field must satisfy it, while at the lower end the condition \(EA\,u'(L) = 0\) is natural and we do not require it (definition 6.32).

We divide the bar into \(n\) equal elements of length \(h = L/n\). The hat function \(\psi_0\) of the node \(x_0 = 0\) is not in the trial field, because \(\psi_0(0) = 1\) and it would violate \(u(0) = 0\). Since the condition \(u(0) = 0\) is homogeneous, the function \(\psi_0\) of the Ritz method (definition 6.32) is here the zero function. The trial space \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) is spanned by the hat functions \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\). By lemma 6.48 these are all continuous functions that run along a straight line on each element and are zero at \(x = 0\). The coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the Ritz approximation are therefore the displacements of the nodes, \(u_{\mathrm{n}}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = c_{\mathrm{i}}\).

First let us compute the smallest case, \(n = 2\), \(h = 5\) m, with three nodes \(x_0 = 0\), \(x_1 = 5\) m, \(x_2 = 10\) m. Figure 6.116 shows the two hat functions we need. On the axes are the depth \(x\) and the value of the function, \(\psi_1\) is above, \(\psi_2\) below, and under them are the first and the second element.

  1. The derivatives of the hat functions are constant on the elements. A hat function rises from \(0\) to \(1\) or falls from \(1\) to \(0\) over the length \(h\), so its slope is \(1/h\) or \(-1/h\). The function \(\psi_1\) has slope \(1/h\) on the first element and \(-1/h\) on the second, the function \(\psi_2\) is zero on the first element and has slope \(1/h\) on the second.
  2. We compute the entries of the matrix \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \int_0^L EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\) element by element. For \(i = j = 1\) each element contributes \(EA\,h/h^2 = EA/h\), so \(K_{11} = 2EA/h\). For \(i = 1\), \(j = 2\) the hat functions overlap only on the second element, where \(\psi_1' \psi_2' = -1/h^2\), so \(K_{12} = K_{21} = -EA/h\). The function \(\psi_2\) has only the second element, so \(K_{22} = EA/h\).
  3. The entries of the right-hand side \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \int_0^L q\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) are the areas under \(q\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(q\) is constant, each entry is \(q\) times the area under \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The triangle \(\psi_1\) has base \(2h\) and height 1, so area \(h\), the triangle \(\psi_2\) has base \(h\), so area \(h/2\). Hence \(f_1 = q\,h\) and \(f_2 = q\,h/2\).
  4. The system \(K\,c = f\) is
    \[\frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} q\,h \\ q\,h/2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.33}\]
    The sum of the two equations gives \((EA/h)\,c_1 = \tfrac32\,q\,h\), so \(c_1 = 3\,q\,h^2/(2EA)\). The second equation gives \(c_2 = c_1 + q\,h^2/(2EA) = 2\,q\,h^2/EA\).
  5. With \(q/EA = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (the ratio of the unrounded values) we have \(q\,h^2/EA = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 25\ \text{m} = 9{,}760\) µm, so \(c_1 = 14{,}64\) µm and \(c_2 = 19{,}52\) µm. These are the dots at \(x = 5\) m and \(x = 10\) m in figure 6.117, joined by the polyline \(n = 2\).

The true displacement is \(u(5\ \text{m}) = a\,(100 - 25) = 14{,}64\) µm and \(u(10\ \text{m}) = 19{,}52\) µm. Both nodal displacements are exact. On the axes of figure 6.117 are the depth \(x\) and the displacement \(u\). The figure shows the true parabola and the approximations for \(n = 1\), \(n = 2\) and \(n = 4\), which all pass through points on the parabola.

We can also build the matrix and the right-hand side from the contributions of the elements. An element of length \(h\) with the hat functions of its two nodes contributes \(\frac{EA}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) to the matrix, at the rows and columns of its two nodes, and \(q\,h/2\) to the right-hand side at each of them, since the area under half a hat function on the element is \(h/2\). The first element contributes only to node 1, because node 0 is not among the unknowns, and the second element contributes to both. The sum gives \(K_{11} = EA/h + EA/h\), \(K_{12} = -EA/h\), \(K_{22} = EA/h\) and \(f_1 = q\,h/2 + q\,h/2\), \(f_2 = q\,h/2\), as above. This procedure, which we call assembly (sestavljanje), is the core of the finite element method and we will develop it for an arbitrary mesh in chapter 7. The equal length of the elements only made the computation easier: an element of length \(h_{\mathrm{k}}\) contributes \(EA/h_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(q\,h_{\mathrm{k}}/2\), and the procedure is the same. The constant \(EA\) and \(q\) allowed us to read the integrals from slopes and areas. With variable ones we would have to integrate over each element separately.

For \(n = 1\) we have \(K = EA/L\), \(f = q\,L/2\) and \(c_1 = q\,L^2/(2EA) = 19{,}52\) µm. This is the best straight line from section 4.6.8, which we found there as the minimum of the energy. The straight line is therefore the Ritz approximation with one hat function. With each halving of the elements the family gains a new function and the old fields remain in it, because a continuous function that runs along a straight line on each element also runs along a straight line on both halves of an element: the polyline \(n = 2\) is also one of the polylines of the mesh with four elements, only not the best among them, the best is the polyline \(n = 4\). The Ritz solution therefore cannot get worse (theorem 6.35).

What about an arbitrary \(n\)? For a mesh with equal elements and unknowns \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\), the same computation as above gives \(K_{\mathrm{ii}} = 2EA/h\), \(K_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{i} \pm 1} = -EA/h\) and other entries zero, except \(K_{\mathrm{nn}} = EA/h\), because the last hat function has only one element. The right-hand side is \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = q\,h\) for \(i < n\) and \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = q\,h/2\). The matrix therefore has nonzero entries only on the main diagonal and the two neighbouring ones (figure 6.76), so each equation couples only three neighbouring unknowns. With \(c_0 = 0\) the equations are

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{EA}{h}\,(2c_{\mathrm{i}} - c_{\mathrm{i}-1} - c_{\mathrm{i}+1}) &= q\,h \quad (1 \le i \le n - 1) , \\ \frac{EA}{h}\,(c_{\mathrm{n}} - c_{\mathrm{n}-1}) &= \frac{q\,h}{2} . \end{aligned} \tag{6.34}\]

Their solution consists of the true nodal displacements, \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), for every \(n\) (the dots on the parabola), as theorem 6.38 says (section 6.5.2): for a constant \(EA\) the unit-force field at a node is continuous, runs along a straight line on each element and is zero at \(x = 0\), so by lemma 6.48 it lies in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\). The same theorem is the reason why for the straight line of section 4.6.8 the displacement at the end was exact.

Between the nodes, however, the approximation is wrong, since the elements are straight lines and the true function is a parabola. The approximation is the interpolation of the true field, so the error on an element is \(a\,(x - x_{\mathrm{k}-1})(x_{\mathrm{k}} - x)\), largest at the middle of the element, \(a\,h^2/4\) (section 6.5.4). Figure 6.118 shows it with blue distances. On the axes are again \(x\) and \(u\), the true parabola passes through the nodes, the dashed vertical lines are at the middles of the elements. For \(n = 1\) we have \(h = 10\) m and the straight line is \(4{,}880\) µm below the parabola in the middle. For \(n = 2\) we have \(h = 5\) m and the polyline is \(1{,}220\) µm below it at the middles of both elements. Half the \(h\) gives a quarter of the error, because \(a\,h^2/4\) contains \(h^2\). In figure 6.117 too the true parabola lies above the polyline.

By section 6.5.4 the energy of the error is \(97{,}76/n^2\) µJ: each element contributes \(EA\,a^2 h^3/6\), and all \(n = L/h\) elements \(EA\,a^2 L\,h^2/6\). For \(n = 1\) this is the number of section 4.6.8, and with each doubling of the number of elements it falls to a quarter. By theorem 6.34 therefore \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{h}}) = -391{,}03 + 97{,}76/n^2\) µJ, a sequence that falls towards the true value from above (figure 6.93).

Another reason why hat functions are a good choice is the matrix: its condition number grows only about as \(1{,}7\,n^2\), while for powers it grows by a factor of about 30 with each term (section 6.3.4). Each hat function touches only its neighbours, so it is not nearly dependent on the others.

The axial force in the approximation. So far we have looked at the displacement. What about the axial force \(N_{\mathrm{h}} = EA\,u_{\mathrm{h}}'\)? On each element \(u_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is constant, so \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) is constant too. The displacements at the ends of the element are exact, so the slope of the approximation \(u_{\mathrm{h}}' = \big(u(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{k}-1})\big)/h = \tfrac1h\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} u'\,\mathrm{d}x\) is by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) the mean of the true slope, and \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) the mean of the true axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) over the element. This is the same phenomenon as for the straight line in section 4.6.8, where the strain of the line was the mean of the true one: now we have it on each element separately. For the hanging bar \(N = q\,(L - x)\) is linear, so \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) on an element equals the true force at its middle. On the axes are the depth \(x\) and the axial force \(N\). For \(n = 4\) (figure 6.119) the values on the elements are 52,58 N, 37,56 N, 22,54 N and 7,512 N, and the true force falls from 60,10 N at the top to zero at the bottom.

The true axial force is continuous, but the approximation is not, it has jumps between the steps. The jump at the node \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the difference of the left and right values. From equation (6.34) we have \((EA/h)(c_{\mathrm{i}} - c_{\mathrm{i}-1}) - (EA/h)(c_{\mathrm{i}+1} - c_{\mathrm{i}}) = q\,h\), so the jump equals \(q\,h\), the weight of the halves of the two elements next to the node, which is the right-hand side \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\). The force jumps because the node must carry its weight, and the approximation does not distribute the weight along the element but gathers it at the node. The error of the force, the vertical distance between a step and the straight line, is largest at the ends of an element, \(q\,h/2 = 7{,}51\) N for \(n = 4\), and shrinks with \(h\). It is worst at the clamp. The reaction of the clamp is the axial force at \(x = 0\), which is the weight of the whole bar, 60,10 N, the top of the true line. The force in the first element, the first step, is 52,58 N, which is 12,5 % less. We therefore do not read the reaction from the force in the first element, and in chapter 7 we will see how to obtain it from the equilibrium of the node. The displacement at a node is exact but the force is not, because the force is the derivative of the displacement, and the derivative of the approximation agrees on an element only with the mean of the true one. ONATE1 (PDF p. 363) says the same: the solution with two-node bars is exact at the nodes, while the stresses are constant on each element.

6.7.3 A step in the mesh

The bar with a step is a case where polynomials fail. Take the bar from section 6.1.4 (in figure 6.121 the upper piece of area \(A\) and the lower piece of area \(A/4\), fixed at the top): steel, of length \(L = 10\) m, fixed at the top, with the axis \(x\) pointing down, its only load is its own weight and the lower end is free. The upper 5 m have a diameter of 10 mm, cross-sectional area \(A\), stiffness \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N and weight \(q = 6{,}010\) N/m per unit length. The lower 5 m have a diameter of 5 mm. The area there is \(A/4\), because it is proportional to the square of the diameter, \((5/10)^2 = 1/4\). The lower piece therefore has stiffness \(EA/4\) and weight \(q/4\) per unit length. We computed the true field in section 6.1.4: the axial force is continuous and equals \(7{,}512\) N at \(x = 5\) m, the derivative of the displacement jumps there from \(4{,}880 \cdot 10^{-7}\) to \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), by a factor of 4, because under the same force the lower piece is four times softer, and the displacements are \(u(5\ \text{m}) = 7{,}320\) µm and \(u(10\ \text{m}) = 12{,}20\) µm.

On the axes of figure 6.121 are the depth \(x\) and the displacement \(u\). The true field is continuous, but its derivative has a jump at \(x = 5\) m, so a kink. A polynomial cannot do this, but straight pieces can. We place a node at the step (blue broken line), so that each element has its own stiffness. The mesh has \(n = 2\) elements of length \(h = 5\) m with nodes \(x_0 = 0\), \(x_1 = 5\) m, \(x_2 = 10\) m.

  1. On the first element the stiffness is \(EA\) and the weight \(q\), on the second \(EA/4\) and \(q/4\). The hat functions have slopes \(\pm 1/h\) as before. An element of length \(h\) and stiffness \(EA_{\mathrm{e}}\) contributes the matrix \(\frac{EA_{\mathrm{e}}}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(K_{11} = EA/h + EA/(4h)\), \(K_{12} = -EA/(4h)\), \(K_{22} = EA/(4h)\).
  2. The right-hand side is half the weight of each element at each of its nodes: \(f_1 = q\,h/2 + (q/4)\,h/2 = \tfrac58\,q\,h\) and \(f_2 = (q/4)\,h/2 = \tfrac18\,q\,h\).
  3. The second equation is \(\frac{EA}{4h}\,(c_2 - c_1) = \tfrac18\,q\,h\), so the difference in height of the polyline vertices is \(c_2 - c_1 = q\,h^2/(2EA)\). The sum of the two equations gives \(\frac{EA}{h}\,c_1 = \tfrac34\,q\,h\), so \(c_1 = \tfrac34\,q\,h^2/EA\).
  4. With \(h = 5\) m, the vertices of the polyline are \(c_1 = \frac{q}{EA}\,\tfrac{75}{4}\ \text{m}^2 = 7{,}320\) µm and \(c_2 = c_1 + \frac{q}{EA}\,\tfrac{25}{2}\ \text{m}^2 = 12{,}20\) µm.

Both nodal displacements are exact, as theorem 6.38 predicts (section 6.5.2). The unit-force field at the node \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(\int_0^{\min(x, x_{\mathrm{i}})} \frac{1}{EA(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t\). Since \(EA\) is constant on each element, this is a function that runs along a straight line on each element, also when it has a kink at the step, and it lies in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\).

What would we get with a single straight line over the whole bar (figure 6.121)? Its displacement of the end is \(13{,}66\) µm (section 6.2.2), the true one \(12{,}20\) µm, so it is too large by 12 %. The cause is in the slope. The straight line has slope \(13{,}66\ \text{µm}/10\ \text{m} = 1{,}366 \cdot 10^{-6}\) everywhere, while the true slope varies and has a mean of \(7{,}320\ \text{µm}/5\ \text{m} = 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-6}\) in the upper half of the bar and \(4{,}880\ \text{µm}/5\ \text{m} = 9{,}76 \cdot 10^{-7}\) in the lower. In the upper half the true field stretches by 7,320 µm, the line by 6,832 µm. In the lower half the true field stretches by 4,880 µm and the line again by 6,832 µm, since the line cannot know that the force in the lower piece is small, from 7,512 N at the step down to zero. The excess stretch below (1,952 µm too much) outweighs the deficit above (0,488 µm too little), and together it is 1,464 µm too much.

On the field with two elements the work of the weight is \(183{,}3\) µJ and \(\Pi = -\tfrac12\,\ell = -91{,}65\) µJ (SymPy), on the line \(179{,}6\) µJ and \(-89{,}81\) µJ, on the true field \(213{,}8\) µJ and \(-106{,}92\) µJ (sections 6.2.2 and 6.4.4). The energy of two elements is lower than that of the line, as theorem 6.35 requires, since the line is one of the fields of two elements. Neither of the two is lower than the true one, as theorem 6.34 requires. The line has the displacement of the end 12 % too large, but this does not contradict the theorem: it is the work of the loads that is bounded, while an individual displacement can be too large or too small (section 6.4.4).

The position of the node matters too. On the axes are the depth \(x\) and the displacement \(u\), the blackwhite curve is the true field. If instead of at the step we put the node at \(x = 4\) m, the mesh has the elements \([0, 4]\) and \([4, 10]\) m. The displacement at the node is still exact, \(u(4\ \text{m}) = 6{,}637\) µm, since the unit-force field at it is a straight line up to \(x = 4\) m and a constant beyond, which is a function that runs along a straight line on both elements. The displacement of the end is 11,71 µm instead of 12,20 µm, so 4,0 % too small. The unit-force field at \(x = 10\) m namely has a kink at the step, which lies inside the second element, and a straight line cannot follow it. The kink must be at a node.

We therefore need a node at the step for two reasons. First, by theorem 6.38 the displacements at the nodes are certainly exact if the unit-force field lies in the space, which means that \(EA\) has a constant value on each element. If the step is not at a node, the exactness of all nodes is no longer guaranteed, as the example with \(x = 4\) m shows, where the displacement at the node \(x = 10\) m is inexact. Second, the error estimate in theorem 6.41 requires a continuous second derivative on each element, which means that the steps of stiffness are at nodes.

6.7.4 Why hat functions are not for the beam

Hat functions proved themselves for the bar. Can we take them for the deflection of a beam too? An attempt shows that we cannot, and the reason is instructive. The energy of a beam is \(U = \tfrac12 \int_0^L EI\,(v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (definition 5.76). On the axes of the left part of figure 6.122 are \(x\) and the deflection \(v\), on the axes of the right one the length \(s\) and the energy \(E\). A deflection made of straight pieces (left part of figure 6.122, slope \(m\) and slope \(m + \theta\)) has \(v'' = 0\) on each element and a kink at a node. The derivative \(v'\) jumps there, so \(v''\) at the node is not a function and the integral \(\int EI\,(v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is not defined. If we compute it element by element, it is zero, which is wrong, since the kink of a beam is not free. Let us see what it costs.

In a true beam the slope does not jump. If it changes by \(\theta\) over a short length \(s\) (rounded part), then \(\theta = v'(x_0 + s) - v'(x_0)\) is the difference of slopes, a number without a unit, because the slope is the ratio of two lengths. The cheapest transition has \(v''\) constant over this length, \(v'' = \theta/s\), and zero outside it.

  1. The energy of the transition is \(\tfrac12\,EI\,(\theta/s)^2\,s = EI\,\theta^2/(2s)\).
  2. No other transition is cheaper. For any transition over the length \(s\) \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{x_0+s} v''\,\mathrm{d}x = \theta\), and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for functions (lemma 6.19) with \(f = 1\) and \(g = v''\) we have \(\theta^2 \le s \int v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so the energy of the transition is at least \(EI\,\theta^2/(2s)\).

For the beam of chapter 5 with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2\) and a kink \(\theta = 0{,}01\) (1 mm over 10 cm) the energy is 10,45 J at \(s = 10\) cm, 104,5 J at \(s = 1\) cm and 1045 J at \(s = 1\) mm. For comparison: a cantilever of length 1 m with a force of 500 N at the end has, by section 5.12.1, a total strain energy of \(F^2 L^3/(6EI) = 1{,}993\) J. A kink of the slope by one hundredth, which we can barely see, costs at \(s = 10\) cm five times as much as the whole cantilever. As \(s\) goes to zero, the energy goes to infinity (figure 6.122). A field with a kink would have infinite energy, so the Ritz method cannot use it.

The weak form of the beam (section 6.1.5) contains the integral \(\int EI\,v''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x\) and therefore requires the deflection and the test function to have a continuous slope and a piecewise continuous second derivative (section 6.2.1). The trial fields of a beam must have a continuous slope (figure 6.123).

The deflection made of straight lines has a kink at the node, which we excluded above. On each element we therefore take a cubic polynomial with prescribed deflection and slope at both ends, since four conditions determine four coefficients. Neighbouring elements share the values at the node, so the tangents agree and the axis is smooth.

The continuity of the slope is explained by ONATE2 (chapter 1, PDF p. 36): the virtual work of the beam contains the second derivatives of the deflection, so the deflection and the slope must be continuous, and the slope equals the inclination of the axis of the beam, which must be smooth. Such Hermite polynomials on the element will be the core of chapter 12, here we only announce them.

6.7.5 What follows

The question from the start of the section has its answer. If we cut the bar into pieces and take a straight line on each, the unknowns are the displacements of the nodes, each equation couples only three neighbouring unknowns, kinks of the displacement are allowed if we put them at nodes, and the error in the energy falls with the square of the element length. For the hanging bar the displacement at the nodes is exact. For the beam the idea fails in this form, because the deflection requires a continuous slope. Chapter 7 adds what is missing for a computer calculation: element matrices and assembly for an arbitrary mesh, reactions, trusses in the plane and in space, numerical integration, and the steps of a complete finite element analysis. Chapter 12 brings Hermite elements for beams and shear locking, chapter 15 elements for two- and three-dimensional bodies. We develop the idea of the Ritz method for natural frequencies in chapter 13.
