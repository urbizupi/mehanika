Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.6 Orthogonality and expansion in modes

In section 26.3 we found the exact modes (načini) of the moving string, the complex functions \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}} x}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with frequencies \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (theorem 26.13). For the string at rest we expanded the initial shape and the initial velocity in modes (theorem 13.11), and the coefficients came from the orthogonality of the modes (theorem 13.43). The question is whether the same holds for a string that runs through its two eyelets with speed \(v\). The answer has two parts. Ordinary orthogonality in the mass fails, but orthogonality survives in another product, which has to be found first. This section finds it, proves the orthogonality and derives the coefficients of the expansion from it.

From history

Methods for axially moving materials date to the nineteenth century, and the eigenfunctions of the moving string and beam had been calculated before. Wickert and Mote (1990) state that orthogonality relations among the eigenfunctions had not been derived until then (WM1990, PDF p. 1). The generalized coordinates in an expansion in eigenfunctions therefore remained coupled, and the classical modal analysis and the Green's function method, which had been applied to the stationary string and beam, were not applicable to axially moving materials. The response was known only for special excitations, for example a constant transverse point force (Chonan, 1986) or harmonic motion of a support (Miranker, 1960) (WM1990, PDF p. 1). For discrete systems Meirovitch (1974, 1975) had studied the matrix eigenvalue problem of gyroscopic systems and derived a corresponding modal analysis, in which a transformation of the generalized coordinates and momenta decouples the equations (WM1990, PDF p. 2). Wickert and Mote wrote the equations of the moving string and beam in first-order form with one symmetric and one skew-symmetric differential operator and showed that the eigenfunctions are orthogonal with respect to both (WM1990, PDF p. 1 and 2).

The moving string that we last looked at in section 26.3 therefore has exact modes, but we have not been able to compute the coefficients of an expansion in them. The story showed two things: the difficulty is not in the eigenfunctions but in the product with respect to which they are orthogonal, and the way to that product is to write the equation in first-order form. We do it here on the string we already know, and prove everything with lemma 6.13.

26.6.1 State and first-order form

The string equation of theorem 26.4 is of second order in time, so the shape \(w(\cdot, t)\) alone does not suffice to continue the motion from time \(t\). Newton's second law connects the force with the acceleration, that is with the second derivative in time, so the motion is determined only when the displacement and the velocity of all particles at one time are known, as for the pendulum with one degree of freedom (chapter 9). Whoever sees only the shape of the string does not know whether a particle is on its way up or down. The displacement and the velocity together we call the state (stanje), because all later motion follows from them, as for the damped systems of section 20.8. We draw it on the string of mode 1 from the example of section 26.3. We choose units so that \(L = \mu = T = 1\), hence \(c = 1\), and take \(v = 1/2\) (the ratio \(v/c\) as in section 26.3). Then \(\omega_1 = \pi\,(1 - v^2/c^2) = 2{,}356\).

We build figure 26.18 as one draws it on the board. First we draw the axis \(x\) along which the string is stretched, and on it the two eyelets at \(0\) and \(L\), which are fixed in space (the idealization of section 26.1). Then we draw the shape \(w\) that the string has in mode 1 at time \(t = 0\), that is \(w(x, 0) = \mathrm{Re}\,\varphi_1(x) = \sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi x/2)\). Last, at four particles we add the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\): the particle at \(x\) has at this time the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = \mathrm{Re}\,(\mathrm{i}\omega_1\varphi_1) = -\omega_1\,\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi_1(x)\). At \(x = 0{,}6\) this gives \(w = 0{,}559\) and \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -2{,}356\cdot 0{,}769 = -1{,}813\). The velocity is not in the outline of the string, so we write the state as a pair of functions.

We use the pair \((w_{\mathrm{t}}, w)\) for a second purpose: we write the string equation as a pair of first-order equations, because this separates a symmetric part from a skew-symmetric one, which we did not need for the string at rest. The equation is (section 26.1)

\[\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + \mathcal G\,w_{\mathrm{t}} + \mathcal K\,w = 0 , \qquad \mathcal G f = 2\mu v\,f' , \qquad \mathcal K f = -\kappa\,f'' , \qquad \kappa = T - \mu v^2 = \mu\,(c^2 - v^2) , \tag{26.30}\]

since \(\mu(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}) = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The operator \(\mathcal G\) comes from the Coriolis term \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) (named in section 26.1), the operator \(\mathcal K\) from the tension reduced by the centripetal term \(\mu v^2\). For \(v < c\) we have \(\kappa > 0\). We need this condition in lemma 26.30. In this section the letters \(\mathcal G\) and \(\mathcal K\) mean operators, not the matrices of section 26.5 (the matrices \(\mat G\) and \(\mat K\) arise from them by the Galerkin method).

Definition 26.29 State and first-order form (stanje in oblika prvega reda)

Let a moving string with \(v < c\) and eyelets fixed in space (section 26.1) have mass per length \(\mu\) and tension \(T\) on \([0, L]\). The state space \(S\) is the set of pairs \(\vek z = (p, w)\) of complex functions \(p, w\colon [0, L] \to \mathbb C\) that are twice continuously differentiable and have \(p(0) = p(L) = w(0) = w(L) = 0\). The state product (produkt stanj) of two states is the complex number

\[\langle \vek z_1, \vek z_2\rangle = \int_0^L \big(p_1\,\bar p_2 + w_1\,\bar w_2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{26.31}\]

where the bar means the complex conjugate. The operators \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) act on a state \(\vek z = (p, w)\) as

\[\mathcal A\,\vek z = \begin{pmatrix} \mu\,p \\ \mathcal K\,w \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mathcal B\,\vek z = \begin{pmatrix} \mathcal G\,p + \mathcal K\,w \\ -\mathcal K\,p \end{pmatrix} , \tag{26.32}\]

with \(\mathcal G\) and \(\mathcal K\) from equation (26.30). The state of the moving string at time \(t\) is \(\vek z(t) = (w_{\mathrm{t}}, w)\).

Source: WM1990 (8a), (8c), (11) (PDF p. 2). The notation with \(\mu\), \(\kappa\) and dimensions is ours, in WM1990 the quantities are dimensionless (\(\mathcal M = I\)).

The conjugation in the product is needed so that \(\langle \vek z, \vek z\rangle = \int (|p|^2 + |w|^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is real and nonnegative also for the complex states that we shall obtain as eigenstates. The function \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) is zero at both eyelets at every time, because \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) for all \(t\), so \((w_{\mathrm{t}}, w) \in S\) if \(w\) is smooth enough. That equation (26.30) is equivalent to the first-order form is shown by the following computation: for \(\vek z = (w_{\mathrm{t}}, w)\) we have \(\mathcal A\,\vek z_{\mathrm{t}} + \mathcal B\,\vek z = (\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + \mathcal G\,w_{\mathrm{t}} + \mathcal K\,w,\ \mathcal K\,w_{\mathrm{t}} - \mathcal K\,w_{\mathrm{t}})\). The second component is zero without any assumption, the first is the left side of equation (26.30). The string therefore moves by equation (26.30) exactly when

\[\mathcal A\,\vek z_{\mathrm{t}} + \mathcal B\,\vek z = \vek 0 . \tag{26.33}\]

The second row is there so that the operators get the properties that follow: without it the first row would be equation (26.30), but the operators would be neither symmetric nor skew-symmetric.

What should symmetry and skew symmetry mean for an operator? For a matrix, \(\mat A^{\mathsf T} = \mat A\) means \(\vek u^{\mathsf T}\mat A\,\vek v = \vek v^{\mathsf T}\mat A\,\vek u\), that is the product does not depend on the order (chapter 10). We ask the same of an operator with respect to the state product (26.31), and for skew symmetry the swap of order changes the sign, as for the matrix \(\mat B^{\mathsf T} = -\mat B\).

Lemma 26.30 Symmetry of \(\mathcal A\) and skew symmetry of \(\mathcal B\) (simetrija operatorja \(\mathcal A\) in poševna simetrija operatorja \(\mathcal B\))

Let \(\vek z_1, \vek z_2 \in S\) be states of definition 26.29. Then

\[\langle \mathcal A\vek z_1, \vek z_2\rangle = \langle \vek z_1, \mathcal A\vek z_2\rangle , \qquad \langle \mathcal B\vek z_1, \vek z_2\rangle = -\,\langle \vek z_1, \mathcal B\vek z_2\rangle . \tag{26.34}\]

For every nonzero state \(\vek z = (p, w) \in S\)

\[\langle \mathcal A\vek z, \vek z\rangle = \int_0^L \big(\mu\,|p|^2 + \kappa\,|w'|^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x > 0 , \tag{26.35}\]

and the number \(\langle \mathcal B\vek z, \vek z\rangle\) is purely imaginary.

Idea

The operator \(\mathcal K\) is symmetric by lemma 6.13, like the second derivative in lemma 13.42, and the operator \(\mathcal G\), a first derivative, is skew-symmetric for the same reason: in integration by parts a derivative moves from one function to the other and changes sign. This is also the real reason why the Coriolis force does no work: its power \(\int p\,\mathcal G p\,\mathrm{d}x\) equals \(\mu v\,[p^2]_0^L = 0\) for real \(p\).

Proof

We must show three equalities, each with one transfer of a derivative. We start with \(\mathcal K\) and \(\mathcal G\).

  1. For functions \(f, g\) of \(S\) (twice continuously differentiable, zero at the eyelets) we have \(\int_0^L (\mathcal K f)\,\bar g\,\mathrm{d}x = \kappa\int_0^L f'\,\bar g'\,\mathrm{d}x\). By lemma 6.13, \(-\kappa\int f''\,\bar g = -\kappa\,[f'\,\bar g]_0^L + \kappa\int f'\,\bar g'\), and the boundary term is zero because \(\bar g(0) = \bar g(L) = 0\). The right side is symmetric in \(f\) and \(g\) up to conjugation, because \(\kappa\) is real: \(\int (\mathcal K f)\,\bar g = \int f\,\overline{\mathcal K g}\).
  2. For \(\mathcal G\) the same: \(\int (\mathcal G f)\,\bar g = 2\mu v\int f'\,\bar g = 2\mu v\,[f\,\bar g]_0^L - 2\mu v\int f\,\bar g' = -\int f\,\overline{\mathcal G g}\), the boundary term is zero.
  3. Symmetry of \(\mathcal A\). By definition \(\langle\mathcal A\vek z_1, \vek z_2\rangle = \int \mu\,p_1\,\bar p_2 + \int (\mathcal K w_1)\,\bar w_2\). By step 1 the second term equals \(\int w_1\,\overline{\mathcal K w_2}\), and the first equals \(\int p_1\,\overline{\mu p_2}\). Together this is \(\langle\vek z_1, \mathcal A\vek z_2\rangle\).
  4. Skew symmetry of \(\mathcal B\). The sum \(\langle\mathcal B\vek z_1, \vek z_2\rangle + \langle\vek z_1, \mathcal B\vek z_2\rangle\) has four terms with \(\mathcal K\) and two with \(\mathcal G\). By step 2 the two terms with \(\mathcal G\) cancel: \(\int (\mathcal G p_1)\,\bar p_2 + \int p_1\,\overline{\mathcal G p_2} = 0\). The four terms with \(\mathcal K\) are \(\int (\mathcal K w_1)\,\bar p_2 - \int w_1\,\overline{\mathcal K p_2} + \int p_1\,\overline{\mathcal K w_2} - \int (\mathcal K p_1)\,\bar w_2\), and by step 1 the first cancels the second and the third the fourth. The sum is zero.
  5. Positivity. For \(\vek z_1 = \vek z_2 = \vek z\) step 1 gives \(\langle\mathcal A\vek z, \vek z\rangle = \mu\int |p|^2 + \kappa\int |w'|^2\), which is equation (26.35). For \(\mu > 0\) and \(\kappa > 0\) (that is \(v < c\)) the sum is nonnegative. If it is zero, then \(p = 0\) and \(w' = 0\), so \(w\) is constant, and because \(w(0) = 0\) we get \(w = 0\), hence \(\vek z = \vek 0\).
  6. By step 4 for \(\vek z_1 = \vek z_2 = \vek z\) we have \(\langle\mathcal B\vek z, \vek z\rangle = -\langle\vek z, \mathcal B\vek z\rangle = -\overline{\langle\mathcal B\vek z, \vek z\rangle}\), since \(\langle\vek a, \vek b\rangle = \overline{\langle\vek b, \vek a\rangle}\). A number equal to minus its own conjugate is purely imaginary.

Source: WM1990 (12) (PDF p. 2): symmetry of \(\mathcal A\) and skew symmetry of \(\mathcal B\), positive definiteness of \(\mathcal A\) for \(v < v_{\mathrm{c}}\) in the Appendix (PDF p. 2 and 7). The proof by integration by parts is ours.

Lemma 26.30 shows what happens at \(v = c\): then \(\kappa = 0\), the product \(\langle\mathcal A\vek z, \vek z\rangle\) is zero for \(\vek z = (0, w)\) with \(w \neq 0\), and \(\mathcal A\) is no longer positive. This is exactly the critical speed of section 26.4 in another dress: with the positivity of \(\mathcal A\) the theorem that follows disappears, and with it everything that follows from it.

Eigenstates are states that change only in size and phase when we write the time part as \(\vek z = \mathrm{Re}\,(\vek\zeta\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t})\). We insert this ansatz in equation (26.33) and obtain (since \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) do not differentiate in time) the eigenvalue problem

\[\lambda\,\mathcal A\,\vek\zeta + \mathcal B\,\vek\zeta = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek\zeta \in S , \ \vek\zeta \neq \vek 0 , \tag{26.36}\]

with eigenvalue \(\lambda \in \mathbb C\). The pair \((\lambda, \vek\zeta)\) we call an eigenpair (lasten par) of the operators \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\). The connection with the modes of section 26.3 is simple.

Lemma 26.31 Eigenstates and modes of the moving string (lastna stanja in načini gibajoče se strune)

A pair \((\lambda, \vek\zeta)\) with \(\vek\zeta = (q, \varphi)\) is an eigenpair of (26.36) exactly when \(q = \lambda\varphi\) and \(\varphi \neq 0\) solves

\[\lambda^2\mu\,\varphi + \lambda\,\mathcal G\varphi + \mathcal K\varphi = 0 , \qquad \varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0 . \tag{26.37}\]

For \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) this is the equation \(\mu\,(-\omega^2\varphi + 2\mathrm{i}\omega v\,\varphi' + v^2\varphi'') = T\varphi''\), so the pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the moving string (definition 26.12) and \(\vek\zeta = (\mathrm{i}\omega\varphi, \varphi)\).

Proof

We must show that the second row of equation (26.36) gives \(q = \lambda\varphi\) and the first row equation (26.37).

  1. The second row is \(\lambda\,\mathcal K\varphi - \mathcal K q = 0\), so \(\mathcal K(\lambda\varphi - q) = -\kappa\,(\lambda\varphi - q)'' = 0\). The function \(y = \lambda\varphi - q\) therefore has \(y'' = 0\), that is \(y = a + bx\), and \(y(0) = y(L) = 0\) gives \(y = 0\), which is \(q = \lambda\varphi\).
  2. The first row is \(\lambda\mu\,q + \mathcal G q + \mathcal K\varphi = 0\). With \(q = \lambda\varphi\) this is equation (26.37). Conversely, if \(\varphi\) solves (26.37), both rows are satisfied.
  3. For \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) we insert \(\mathcal G\varphi = 2\mu v\,\varphi'\) and \(\mathcal K\varphi = -(T - \mu v^2)\,\varphi''\): \(-\omega^2\mu\varphi + 2\mathrm{i}\omega\mu v\,\varphi' - (T - \mu v^2)\,\varphi'' = 0\), which is the equation in the statement.

The equation in the statement is the one that the ansatz \(w = \mathrm{Re}\,(\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\) solved in theorem 26.13. The eigenpairs with \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) are therefore exactly the states \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = (\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\) with the modes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) of that theorem.

26.6.2 Orthogonality of the complex modes

For the matrices of section 26.5 we did the same computation with \(\mat M\), \(\mat G\) and \(\mat K\) (theorem 26.27): the eigenvalues are purely imaginary because \(\mat G\) is skew and \(\mat K\) is positive definite. Here the same proof runs on operators. Its idea: with the eigenvalue equation (26.36) we rewrite \(\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\) twice, once from the left argument and once from the right, and the skew symmetry says that the two forms differ by the factor \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Theorem 26.32 Orthogonality of the complex modes of the moving string (ortogonalnost kompleksnih načinov gibajoče se strune)

Let a moving string with \(v < c\) and fixed eyelets be given (definition 26.29). Then:

  1. Every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of (26.36) is purely imaginary, \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) with real \(\omega\), and for an eigenpair \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle > 0\) and \(\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle = -\lambda\,\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle\).
  2. If \((\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}})\) are eigenpairs with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} \neq \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\), then
    \[\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0 . \tag{26.38}\]
Idea

For the string at rest the proof of theorem 13.43 subtracts two equations. Here we subtract in the same way, but in the state space, where the eigenvalue equation connects \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\). The purely imaginary eigenvalues come from the positivity of \(\mathcal A\) and the skew symmetry of \(\mathcal B\) (lemma 26.30).

Proof

We show first item 1 and then the difference \((\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{m}})\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0\).

  1. We multiply the eigenvalue equation (26.36) in the state product (26.31) by \(\vek\zeta\), which is linear in the first argument: \(\lambda\,\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle + \langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle = 0\).
  2. By lemma 26.30, \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle\) is real and greater than zero, and \(\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle\) is purely imaginary. From step 1, \(\lambda = -\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle/\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle\) is therefore a purely imaginary number. This is item 1.
  3. For two eigenpairs we write \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = -\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\) (the eigenvalue equation for \(n\), multiplied by \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\)). By skew symmetry (lemma 26.30), \(-\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = \langle\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\).
  4. The eigenvalue equation for \(m\) is \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(\langle\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = \langle\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, -\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = -\bar\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\,\langle\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\), because the product is conjugate-linear in the second argument.
  5. By item 1, \(\bar\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\), and by the symmetry of \(\mathcal A\), \(\langle\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = \langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\). Steps 3 and 4 together give \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle\).
  6. For \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} \neq \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) this gives \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0\), and by step 3 also \(\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = -\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\cdot 0 = 0\).

Source: WM1990 (14b), (16) (PDF p. 3): the eigenvalues are imaginary, \(\langle\mathcal A\phi_{\mathrm{n}}, \phi_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = \delta_{\mathrm{nm}}\) and \(\langle\mathcal B\phi_{\mathrm{n}}, \phi_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0\) for \(n \neq m\) (Meirovitch, 1974). Proved here by integration by parts from lemma 26.30, WM1990 does not give the proof.

The theorem gives orthogonality in two products at once, as for matrices (theorem 10.13, definition 10.10) in mass and stiffness. The difference is that the products are no longer the mass and the stiffness but \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\), in which mass and stiffness are mixed, as equation (26.32) shows.

We test the theorem on modes 1 and 2 with \(v/c = 1/2\) and compare it with the ordinary mass product \(\int\mu\,\varphi_1\bar\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x\) of the string at rest. The real and imaginary part of the mode are \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = \sin(n\pi x)\cos(n\pi x/2)\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = \sin(n\pi x)\sin(n\pi x/2)\), therefore

\[\int_0^1\varphi_1^{\mathrm R}\varphi_2^{\mathrm R}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}16168 , \qquad \int_0^1\varphi_1^{\mathrm I}\varphi_2^{\mathrm I}\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}06467 , \qquad \mathrm{Re}\int_0^1\varphi_1\bar\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}09701 \neq 0 . \tag{26.39}\]

The mass product of the modes is not zero. For the string at rest theorem 13.43 gave zero because the eigenvalue \(\omega^2\) is only a factor of the mass in the equation \(-T\varphi'' = \omega^2\mu\,\varphi\) (at \(v = 0\) we have \(\kappa = T\)). For the moving string equation (26.37) has the further term \(\lambda\mathcal G\varphi\) and the eigenvalue enters in two powers, so the same computation does not give zero. Zero is, by theorem 26.32, the state product (26.31), in which the mass part is accompanied by a stiffness part:

\[\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1, \vek\zeta_2\rangle = \underbrace{\mu\,\omega_1\omega_2\!\int_0^1\varphi_1\bar\varphi_2\,\mathrm{d}x}_{= \,1{,}0771 + 1{,}0771\,\mathrm{i}} + \underbrace{\kappa\!\int_0^1\varphi_1'\,\bar\varphi_2'\,\mathrm{d}x}_{= \,-1{,}0771 - 1{,}0771\,\mathrm{i}} = 0 . \tag{26.40}\]

The mass part is \(\omega_1\omega_2\) times the mass product, because \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) (lemma 26.31) and \(\mu\,p_1\bar p_2 = \mu\,\omega_1\omega_2\varphi_1\bar\varphi_2\), with \(\omega_1\omega_2 = 1{,}125\,\pi^2\). The computation is numerical (trapezoidal rule on 20001 points), the remainder is \(10^{-8}\).

In figure 26.19 we first draw the axis \(x\) between the eyelets. Then the mass part, which has area \(0{,}09701\cdot 1{,}125\,\pi^2 = 1{,}0771\): if we judged orthogonality by it alone, we would reject it. So we add the stiffness part with the opposite area. Last we add the two, the blackwhite curve is their sum: its area is zero, although the sum is not zero at any point except at the ends and at its crossings. This is the orthogonality the theorem claims, and it is not orthogonality point by point.

For the expansion we need real states. Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) is purely imaginary and \(\mathcal A\), \(\mathcal B\) map real states to real states, \(\bar{\vek\zeta}_{\mathrm{n}}\) is an eigenstate together with \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\), with eigenvalue \(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). We write \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\). By lemma 26.31, \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = (-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R})\) and \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = (\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I})\), since \(\mathrm{i}\omega(\varphi^{\mathrm R} + \mathrm{i}\varphi^{\mathrm I}) = -\omega\varphi^{\mathrm I} + \mathrm{i}\omega\varphi^{\mathrm R}\). The real part of the mode in time is then (with \(\omega t\) as the phase) \(w = \mathrm{Re}\,(\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}) = \varphi^{\mathrm R}\cos\omega t - \varphi^{\mathrm I}\sin\omega t\). Let us see what this means for the particles of the string.

We build figure 26.20 over the times. First we draw the first panel with \(\omega t = 0\): the shape is \(\varphi_1^{\mathrm R}\) and it is nowhere zero in the interior. On it we mark three particles at \(0{,}25\), \(0{,}5\) and \(0{,}75\). For the second panel with \(\omega t = \pi/8\) we use \(w = \varphi^{\mathrm R}\cos\omega t - \varphi^{\mathrm I}\sin\omega t\): the particle at \(0{,}75\) is now on the axis, because there \(\varphi^{\mathrm R}\cos\omega t = \varphi^{\mathrm I}\sin\omega t\). The particle at \(x\) passes its rest position at \(\omega t = \pi/2 - k_1 x\), which with \(k_1 = \pi/2\) for \(x = 0{,}75\), \(0{,}5\) and \(0{,}25\) is exactly \(\pi/8\), \(\pi/4\) and \(3\pi/8\), as the third panel and the fourth panel show. The particles of the string therefore do not pass through their rest positions together. For the string at rest \(\varphi^{\mathrm I} = 0\) and they all pass together at \(\omega t = \pi/2\). Wickert and Mote point out that the complex eigenfunctions give a response with varying phase, so the particles do not pass their rest positions simultaneously (WM1990, PDF p. 3). They pass at \(0{,}0625\), \(0{,}125\) and \(0{,}1875\) of the period \(\tau_1 = 2\pi/\omega_1 = 2{,}667\).

Corollary 26.33 Real pairs of modes (realni pari načinov)

Let \((\mathrm{i}\omega, \vek\zeta)\) be an eigenpair with \(\omega > 0\) and \(\vek\zeta = \vek\zeta^{\mathrm R} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}\), where \(\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}\) and \(\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}\) are real states. With the real product \((\vek a, \vek b) = \int (a_1b_1 + a_2b_2)\,\mathrm{d}x\) we have:

  1. \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R} = \omega\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}\) and \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I} = -\omega\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}\),
  2. \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}) = (\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}) = \tfrac12\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle\) and \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}) = 0\),
  3. for two eigenpairs with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \neq \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) (both positive) all four products \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{a}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{b})\) and \((\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{a}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{b})\), \(a, b \in \{\mathrm R, \mathrm I\}\), are zero.
Proof

We must show that the real and imaginary part of theorem 26.32 separate.

  1. The eigenvalue equation with \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) is \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta = -\mathrm{i}\omega\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta\). Since \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) do not mix real and imaginary parts (they have real coefficients), \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R} + \mathrm{i}\,\mathcal B\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I} = \omega\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I} - \mathrm{i}\omega\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}\). Comparing real and imaginary parts gives item 1.
  2. The conjugate state \(\bar{\vek\zeta}\) is an eigenstate with \(-\mathrm{i}\omega \neq \mathrm{i}\omega\) (for \(\omega > 0\)). Theorem 26.32 gives \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \bar{\vek\zeta}\rangle = \int(\mathcal A\vek\zeta)^{\mathsf T}\vek\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). Written out in real and imaginary part this is \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}) - (\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}) + 2\mathrm{i}\,(\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}) = 0\), because \(\mathcal A\) is symmetric also for the real product. The real and imaginary part give the first two equalities, and the sum \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta, \vek\zeta\rangle = (\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm R}) + (\mathcal A\vek\zeta^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta^{\mathrm I})\) gives the half.
  3. For \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \neq \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) the numbers \(\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) are different. Theorem 26.32 for the pairs \((\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}})\) and \((\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \bar{\vek\zeta}_{\mathrm{m}})\) gives \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0\) and \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}, \bar{\vek\zeta}_{\mathrm{m}}\rangle = 0\). The real and imaginary parts of both give four equations: \((R, R) + (I, I) = 0\), \((I, R) - (R, I) = 0\), \((R, R) - (I, I) = 0\) and \((I, R) + (R, I) = 0\), where \((a, b)\) means \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{a}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{b})\). All four are zero. The same for \(\mathcal B\).

Source: WM1990 (15), (16) (PDF p. 3): the structure \(\phi_{\mathrm{n}} = \phi_{\mathrm{n}}^R + \mathrm{i}\phi_{\mathrm{n}}^I\) and the orthogonality. Items 1 to 3 are our proof.

For mode 1 the same numerical computation gives \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm R}) = (\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm I}) = 3{,}70110\) and \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta_1^{\mathrm I}) = -10^{-8}\), which is zero up to the error of the rule and is item 2 of the corollary. Together \(\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1, \vek\zeta_1\rangle = 7{,}40220\), \(\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_1, \vek\zeta_1\rangle = -17{,}44103\,\mathrm{i}\) and so \(-\langle\mathcal B\vek\zeta_1, \vek\zeta_1\rangle/\langle\mathcal A\vek\zeta_1, \vek\zeta_1\rangle = 2{,}35619\,\mathrm{i} = \mathrm{i}\omega_1\), which is item 1 of the theorem for this mode.

26.6.3 Expansion in modes

The goal is to solve the initial value problem: at \(t = 0\) the string has shape \(w_0\) and velocity \(w_1\), so the state \(\vek z_0 = (w_1, w_0)\). Which motion follows? For the string at rest (theorem 13.11) we wrote the motion as a sum of modes with coefficients from orthogonality. Here there are two coordinates per mode, \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\), because each mode has two real parts. We write

\[\vek z(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^\infty\Big(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(t)\,\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} + \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(t)\,\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\Big) . \tag{26.41}\]

The theorem that follows says which coefficients this must have. We do not prove the completeness of the expansion, that is that every state of \(S\) is such a sum: Wickert and Mote assume it (WM1990, PDF p. 3, "it is assumed that the expansion is complete"). For the string at rest theorem 13.11 proves it from Fourier series, for the moving string the proof remains open and we mark it as cited, not proved.

Theorem 26.34 Modal coordinates of the moving string (modalne koordinate gibajoče se strune)

Let a moving string with \(v < c\) and fixed eyelets be given, \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = (\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\) its modes with distinct \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\) (theorem 26.13) and \(N_{\mathrm{n}} = (\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R})\). Let \(\vek z(t) = (w_{\mathrm{t}}, w)\) be a motion that the sum (26.41) represents at every time \(t\) so that the operators \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\), the derivative in time and the integral may be applied term by term. Then

\[\dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} , \qquad \dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} , \tag{26.42}\]

so \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(t) = \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0)\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(t) = \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t - \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0)\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), with the initial values

\[\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0) = \frac{(\mathcal A\vek z_0, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R})}{N_{\mathrm{n}}} , \qquad \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0) = \frac{(\mathcal A\vek z_0, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I})}{N_{\mathrm{n}}} . \tag{26.43}\]
Idea

We insert the sum in equation (26.33) and multiply it by one of the real states. Because of theorem 26.32 and corollary 26.33 one term remains, and the equation for the coefficient is the equation of an oscillator.

Proof

We must show that multiplication by \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}\) and \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}\) gives (26.42) and that the initial condition gives (26.43).

  1. Equation (26.33) for the sum (26.41) gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}}\big(\dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} + \dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} + \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}\,\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} + \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\,\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\big) = \vek 0\). By corollary 26.33 (item 1) \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\) and \(\mathcal B\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}\), so the sum reads \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}}\big((\dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} - \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I})\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} + (\dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R})\,\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\big) = \vek 0\).
  2. We multiply this by \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}\). By corollary 26.33 (items 2 and 3) only the term with \(n = m\) and with \(\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}\) remains of the sum, and gives \(N_{\mathrm{m}}\,(\dot\xi_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R} - \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\xi_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}) = 0\). Multiplying by \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}\) leaves \(N_{\mathrm{m}}\,(\dot\xi_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I} + \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\xi_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}) = 0\), because \((\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}) = N_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\). This gives (26.42).
  3. From (26.42), \(\ddot\xi^{\mathrm R}_{\mathrm{n}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\xi^{\mathrm R}_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + b\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t\) (theorem 9.36), and \(\dot\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0) = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0)\) gives \(b = \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0)\). The same for \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}\).
  4. Initial condition: \(\vek z(0) = \vek z_0\), so \((\mathcal A\vek z_0, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}}\big(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0)\,(\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}) + \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0)\,(\mathcal A\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}, \vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R})\big) = N_{\mathrm{m}}\,\xi_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm R}(0)\) by items 2 and 3 of corollary 26.33. The same for \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm I}\). This is (26.43).

Source: WM1990 (19), (20), (21b) (PDF p. 3): modal coordinates and their initial values (Meirovitch, 1975). The proof and the choice of \(N_{\mathrm{n}}\) are ours, the completeness of the expansion is assumed (WM1990, PDF p. 3) and not proved.

Let us see whether this works on an example. We take the string with \(L = \mu = T = 1\) and \(v/c = 1/2\) from the previous examples and give it \(w_0 = \sin\pi x\), \(w_1 = 0\), so the state \(\vek z_0 = (0, \sin\pi x)\) (the initial shape is the first sine of chapter 13, not a mode of the moving string, since \(\varphi_1^{\mathrm R} = \sin\pi x\cos(\pi x/2)\)). By equation (26.43) the coefficients are

\[\xi_1^{\mathrm R}(0) = \xi_1^{\mathrm I}(0) = 0{,}67906 , \quad \xi_2^{\mathrm R}(0) = 0{,}125 , \ \xi_2^{\mathrm I}(0) = 0 , \quad \xi_3^{\mathrm R}(0) = -\xi_3^{\mathrm I}(0) = 0{,}02646 , \ \ldots \tag{26.44}\]

and the sum of the first \(N\) terms at \(t = 0\) gives the displacement \(w_0\) and velocity zero with the following errors (largest deviation over \(x\)):

\(N\) 1 2 4 8 12
\(\max\lvert w - w_0\rvert\) \(0{,}0850\) \(0{,}0397\) \(0{,}0036\) \(0{,}00042\) \(0{,}00012\)
\(\max\lvert w_{\mathrm{t}}\rvert\) \(0{,}616\) \(0{,}323\) \(0{,}036\) \(0{,}010\) \(0{,}005\)

The error falls with \(N\), which is observed, not proved: the convergence of the expansion is not proved, as said above. The motion at later times is obtained by rotating the pair \((\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}, \xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I})\) with angular velocity \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) by equation (26.42). For the steel band of section 26.1 (dimensions, tension and speeds are our choice) the frequency of the first mode at \(v = c/2 = 56{,}6\) m/s is \(f_1 = (1 - v^2/c^2)\cdot 56{,}61 = 0{,}75\cdot 56{,}61 = 42{,}5\) Hz, where \(56{,}61\) Hz is the frequency of the first mode at \(v = 0\) (section 26.3), and this expansion would write any initial displacement of the band as a sum of sines of these frequencies with shifted phases.

Let us also look at the way back to the string at rest (section 26.1 has \(v = 0\) as a special case). For \(v = 0\) we have \(\mathcal G = 0\), \(k_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = 0\) and \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R} = (0, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\), \(\vek\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I} = (\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}, 0)\). Equation (26.43) gives \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm R}(0) \propto \int\kappa\,w_0'\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \kappa\,(\pi n/L)^2\int w_0\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) (by lemma 6.13) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm I}(0) \propto \mu\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\int w_1\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). These are the coefficients from the initial displacement and the initial velocity, as the expansion in modes of the string at rest gives them (theorem 13.11).

What has this section given us? The orthogonality of the moving string does not go by the mass product but by the state product (26.31) with the operators \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) (theorem 26.32), which we showed by integration by parts and the eigenvalue equation. The expansion in modes (theorem 26.34) writes the response to an initial condition with the same steps as for the string at rest. We already met a first-order form for damping (section 20.8), there with two symmetric matrices, here with one symmetric and one skew-symmetric operator. Later we shall need the two operators and the state product for the beam in section 26.7, where the second order is replaced by the fourth. The completeness of the expansion and the convergence in the example remain unproved. We do not yet treat the Green's function, which WM1990 derives from the same orthogonality (PDF p. 3 and 4), nor the response to a moving force, which WM1990 computes for a monocable aerial tramway with a payload (enovrvna žičnica, PDF p. 4 and 5).
