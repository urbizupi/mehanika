Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.9 Torsional vibration of the shaft line and critical speeds (torzijsko nihanje gredi in kritični vrtljaji)

The sections so far treated the crankshaft as one rigid body that turns through the angle \(\theta = \Omega t\). Such a model gives the torque and the size of the flywheel (section 23.5), but it cannot say what happens because the shaft is not rigid. The torque of the cylinders does not appear everywhere at once. A cylinder presses it into its own crank pin, and the flywheel and the generator feel it only through the shaft, which twists as it passes it on. Chapter 13 promised that we would use the twist of the shaft and its stiffness for several disks. This section keeps the promise in three steps. First we build a model of shafts and disks with at least two degrees of freedom and show that it always has one rigid-body mode with zero frequency. In between we bring a drive with a gear pair back to the same chain and find the frequencies in a second way, by walking along the chain from disk to disk. Then we show how an engine torque of order \(n\) excites a single mode, and finally we use this to compute the speeds at which the response of the model is unbounded.

23.9.1 Chain of disks (veriga diskov)

Take a drive line of the kind a piston engine set has: the engine, a flywheel on the shaft, and then a generator. Draw it as in figure 23.73. We start with the common axis about which everything turns. On it we put three rigid disks, disk \(J_1\) for the engine with its crankshaft, disk \(J_2\) for the flywheel and disk \(J_3\) for the generator. We call them disks because from each body we need only one number, its moment of inertia about the axis (definition 8.89). The shape and size of a disk are therefore drawn only roughly. For the disks we compute with, \(J_1 = 0{,}05\), \(J_2 = 0{,}5\) and \(J_3 = 0{,}3\) kg m\(^2\). These three numbers are chosen for the example, they are not data of any engine, and the flywheel \(J_2\) is not the one that section 23.5 computed for \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}02\).

Between the disks we put shafts. Each is a circular bar to which chapter 13 assigns a torsional stiffness \(k_1\) and \(k_2\), \(k_{\mathrm{i}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L_{\mathrm{i}}\) by lemma 13.23, where the lengths \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) are measured between the centres of the disks. The steel has \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa and density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), the diameter is \(d = 40\) mm and \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi d^4/32 = 2{,}5133\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) (example 13.29), so \(k_1 = 94\,731{,}6\) and \(k_2 = 37\,892{,}6\) N m/rad. The shafts have no mass. This is an idealization, because a disk has much more inertia than a shaft: the moment of inertia of the second shaft about the axis is \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}} L_2 = 0{,}00098\) kg m\(^2\) against \(0{,}3\) kg m\(^2\) of the generator, that is \(0{,}33\) %. The error of such an idealization is already measured by corollary 13.28 (for the shaft with a disk of example 13.29 \(0{,}65\) %), and below we shall see that for FPGL it is less than \(0{,}5\) %. The idealization fails when the shaft is no longer light against the disks, for instance a long thick shaft between two small disks: then the shaft itself vibrates too and we treat it as continuous (theorem 13.27).

The rigidity of the disks is an idealization too. For the flywheel and the rotor of the generator it is good, because they are short and thick against the shaft and hardly twist themselves. For the engine it is rough: the crankshaft twists between the cylinders, so an engine with several cylinders gets one disk per cylinder (the example with two cylinders below is built so). A single disk \(J_1\) leaves out the modes in which the crankshaft itself twists, while the two modes we compute here have almost all their twist in the shafts between the disks.

Each disk has its own twist \(\varphi_1\), \(\varphi_2\) and \(\varphi_3\), the angle between the present and the reference position of a line on the disk, measured about the axis in the same direction. These are three coordinates: three rigid disks on one axis have three degrees of freedom by definition 8.46. The twist \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) may be many revolutions, because the line turns. Only the difference of the twists of neighbouring disks has to be small, that is the twist of the shaft \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\), since lemma 13.23 holds for a linearly elastic shaft. The chain has its name for a reason: the disks are linked one after another by shafts like the links of a chain, and each link carries only a torque between two neighbours.

What it means that a shaft twists is shown in figure 23.74 on the left. Take the second shaft between the flywheel and the generator, which turn about the axis. On the surface of the shaft at rest we draw a straight line along the axis. When the generator turns further than the flywheel, the right end of the line moves along the circumference and the line lies obliquely. The difference of the twists of the two ends is the twist of the shaft \(\delta_2 = \varphi_3 - \varphi_2\). The shaft resists the twist like a spring: it acts on the disks with the torque \(M_2 = k_2\delta_2\), pulling the generator back and the flywheel forward. Why are the torques at the two ends equally large? A massless shaft has no moment of inertia, so the sum of the torques on it must be zero, otherwise it would get an unbounded angular acceleration.

On the right side of figure 23.74 we draw the axes, the twist of the shaft horizontally and the torque in it vertically. Lemma 13.23 gives a straight line through the origin with slope \(k_2 = 37{,}89\) N m/mrad. How large is the twist in our drive? The mean engine torque \(84{,}61\) N m (section 23.5) goes to the generator through this shaft and twists it steadily by \(84{,}61/k_2 = 2{,}23\) mrad, which is the point on the line. The work the torque does up to this point is the area of the triangle under the line, that is the energy stored in the shaft. In the vibration of order \(3\) that we compute below, the torque changes about this point by \(\pm 88{,}86\) N m and the twist by \(\pm 2{,}35\) mrad (\(0{,}13^\circ\)), which is the orange part of the line. A rigid line would load the same shaft only by \(\pm 11{,}26\) N m, which we compute in example 23.71. The largest twist is therefore \(2{,}23 + 2{,}35 = 4{,}58\) mrad. The shear stress at the surface of the shaft is \(M_2 d/(2 I_{\mathrm{p}})\): \(6{,}73\) MPa from the mean torque and \(7{,}07\) MPa from the vibration, together \(13{,}8\) MPa. The shaft is still very far from the limit of linearity, so the straight line holds.

Definition 23.56 Chain of disks (veriga diskov)

A chain of disks (veriga diskov) is a system of \(N \ge 2\) rigid disks with moments of inertia \(J_1, \dots, J_{\mathrm{N}} > 0\) about a common axis, linked in order by \(N - 1\) massless shafts, where shaft \(i\) between disks \(i\) and \(i + 1\) has torsional stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) (lemma 13.23). The state is given by the column of twists \(\vek\varphi = (\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{N}})\) of the disks about the axis, measured in the same direction. An external torque \(Q_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\) about the axis acts on disk \(k\). The chain is free if there is no other support about the axis.

Source: FPGL §9.4 (PDF pp. 405 to 406): disks with inertia \(I_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}}\), shafts as torsional springs \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\), twist of a disk \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\). The name and the symbols are ours: FPGL writes \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\theta\), we write \(J\) and \(\varphi\), because \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is here the second moment of area of the shaft section (lemma 13.23).

The symbol \(J_{\mathrm{k}}\) here means the moment of inertia of a disk about its axis. It is neither \(J(\theta)\), the moment of inertia of the crank mechanism of section 23.4, nor \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) about the centre of mass from chapter 8, and \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the torsional stiffness of a shaft in N m/rad, not the stiffness of a spring of chapter 9. We write eigenvalues as \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), because in this chapter \(\lambda\) already stands for the ratio \(a/l\).

Do we get the equation of motion of the chain from our theorems? We get it from Lagrange's equations, as for the chains of masses on springs in chapter 10.

Lemma 23.57 Equation of motion of the chain of disks (gibalna enačba verige diskov)

Let a chain of disks be given by definition 23.56, \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(J_1, \dots, J_{\mathrm{N}})\) and \(\mat K\) the tridiagonal matrix of order \(N\) with entries \(K_{\mathrm{kk}} = k_{\mathrm{k}-1} + k_{\mathrm{k}}\) (where \(k_0 = k_{\mathrm{N}} = 0\)), \(K_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{k}+1} = K_{\mathrm{k}+1,\mathrm{k}} = -k_{\mathrm{k}}\) and zero elsewhere. Then:

  1. A motion \(\vek\varphi(t)\) of the chain solves the equation
    \[\mat M\ddot{\vek\varphi} + \mat K\vek\varphi = \vek Q(t) . \tag{23.46}\]
  2. The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric positive definite, the matrix \(\mat K\) symmetric positive semidefinite and \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{N-1}k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})^2\).
  3. \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) if and only if all twists are equal, \(\varphi_1 = \dots = \varphi_{\mathrm{N}}\). The kernel of \(\mat K\) is therefore the line spanned by the column \(\vek s = (1, \dots, 1)\).
Proof

We have to show the equation from the kinetic and potential energy, and then the properties of the matrices from the form of the potential.

  1. Disk \(k\) turns about a fixed axis with angular velocity \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) and has its centre of mass on the axis, so by theorem 8.92 its kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{k}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}^2\). The kinetic energy of the chain is \(T = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \tfrac12\dot{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek\varphi}\), of the form of theorem 8.97 with a constant matrix \(\mat M\).
  2. For a twist of its ends \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), shaft \(i\) is a linear torsional spring with torque \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) (lemma 13.23). The torque grows linearly with the twist from \(0\) to \(k_{\mathrm{i}}\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) (the straight line, drawn for the second shaft), so its work over the twist \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the average value \(\tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{i}}\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) times \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) (the area of the triangle under the torque-twist line). This is the potential energy of the shaft. The sum over the shafts is \(V = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})^2\).
  3. Expanding the square gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1}^2 - 2\sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (k_{\mathrm{k}-1} + k_{\mathrm{k}})\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}^2 - 2\sum_{\mathrm{i}} k_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), because \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) appears at shaft \(k - 1\) (as \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1}^2\)) and at shaft \(k\) (as \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^2\)). This is \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi\) with the stated matrix, so \(V = \tfrac12\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi\), the matrix is symmetric by construction, and \(\partial V/\partial\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat K\vek\varphi)_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. The work of the external torques in a virtual twist \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) of a disk (the twists in the figure) is \(Q_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\), so the \(Q_{\mathrm{k}}\) are generalized forces (lemma 8.52). By theorem 8.101, for \(T\) and \(V\) above, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(J_{\mathrm{k}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}) + (\mat K\vek\varphi)_{\mathrm{k}} = Q_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is equation (23.46).
  5. The matrix \(\mat M\) has \(J_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) on its diagonal, so it is symmetric positive definite. From step 3, \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi = 2V \ge 0\) because \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), so \(\mat K\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15).
  6. The sum \(\sum k_{\mathrm{i}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})^2\) is zero if and only if every term is zero, that is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}+1} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) for every \(i\) because \(k_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\). By lemma 7.16, \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) if and only if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi = 0\), which is point 3.

Source: FPGL §9.4 (PDF p. 406): the equations are as equation (9.2), with the matrices (9.5) for four disks. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says what the rigid motion of the chain is. All disks turn through the same angle (mode 1 in the figure) and no shaft twists, so there is no potential energy. This is the turn of the whole line as one body, as section 23.5 used it. Because \(\mat K\) is semidefinite with a one-dimensional kernel, everything we proved about semidefinite matrices in chapter 10 can be used.

Corollary 23.58 A free chain of disks has exactly one rigid-body mode (prosta veriga diskov ima en togi način)

Let a chain of disks be free by definition 23.56 and let \((\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\), \(r = 1, \dots, N\), be the eigenpairs of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) by theorem 10.21, \(\omega_1 \le \dots \le \omega_{\mathrm{N}}\), with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal modes. Then:

  1. Exactly one eigenvalue is zero, \(\omega_1 = 0\), and \(\omega_2 > 0\). The rigid-body mode is \(\vek\varphi_1 = \vek s/\sqrt{J_\mathrm{c}}\) with \(J_\mathrm{c} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\), in which all disks turn through the same angle.
  2. Every elastic mode (\(r \ge 2\)) satisfies \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = 0\). The weighted mean twist \(\bar\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}/J_\mathrm{c}\), with the moments of inertia as weights, is therefore zero in an elastic mode.
  3. For any torques \(Q_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\), \(J_\mathrm{c}\,\ddot{\bar\varphi} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Q_{\mathrm{k}}\): the chain as a whole turns like one disk with inertia \(J_\mathrm{c}\) under the sum of the torques.
Proof

We have to show that the kernel is exactly one-dimensional, and then we use theorem 10.34.

  1. By lemma 23.57 (point 3) the set \(\mathcal N\) of columns with \(\mat K\vek w = \vek 0\) is the line \(\{c\,\vek s\}\), so it is one-dimensional. By theorem 10.34 (point 2) the number of zero eigenvalues is \(z = 1\), so \(\omega_1 = 0\) and \(\omega_2 > 0\) (point 1 of the same theorem).
  2. The mode \(\vek\varphi_1\) lies in \(\mathcal N\), so \(\vek\varphi_1 = c\,\vek s\), and \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_1 = c^2 J_\mathrm{c} = 1\) gives \(c = 1/\sqrt{J_\mathrm{c}}\) (up to sign).
  3. By theorem 10.34 (point 3), \(\vek s^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\varphi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for \(r \ge 2\). The modal coordinate \(\eta_1 = \vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek\varphi = \sqrt{J_\mathrm{c}}\,\bar\varphi\) solves, by theorem 10.28, the equation \(\ddot\eta_1 = \vek\varphi_1^\T\vek Q\) (from \(\omega_1 = 0\)), that is \(J_\mathrm{c}\,\ddot{\bar\varphi} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} Q_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: FPGL (PDF p. 406): "the torsional system invariably has one" rigid-body mode. The proof is not from the source.

Point 3 is exactly the model of section 23.5, now as a consequence. When this model is good, the theorem below tells: for an excitation with frequency \(\omega\) far below \(\omega_2\), the denominator \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \omega^2\) of the elastic modes is almost \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), which is the static response.

23.9.2 Shafts in series and the gear pair (zaporedni gredi in zobniški par)

The chain of definition 23.56 has one shaft between neighbouring disks and all disks on one axis. Real drives differ from it in two things. The shaft between two disks is often made of two pieces, and the generator often does not run at the speed of the engine, but is driven through two gears by a second, parallel shaft. We bring both back to the chain of disks. First we ask how stiff a shaft of two pieces is. Draw it as in figure 23.77.

Take the shafts of our chain and leave out the flywheel. The first shaft with stiffness \(k_1\) and the second shaft with stiffness \(k_2\) are firmly joined at the joint, and at the free ends there are disks. We hold the left disk and turn the right one with the torque \(M = 84{,}61\) N m, the mean engine torque. On the surface of the unloaded shafts we draw a straight line. Under the torque the line lies obliquely. How much does each shaft twist? Cut the shaft in thought anywhere and look at the piece to the right of the cut. It is at rest, and only the torque \(M\) at its right end and the torque in the cut act on it, so the two are equally large. The same torque \(M\) therefore passes through every section of both shafts. The first shaft thus twists by \(\delta_1 = M/k_1 = 0{,}89\) mrad, and the second, from the joint on, by a further \(\delta_2 = M/k_2 = 2{,}23\) mrad. The right disk turns against the left one by the sum, \(\delta = 3{,}13\) mrad. A single equivalent shaft is to twist under the same torque by the same angle \(\delta = M/k^*\). Its stiffness \(k^*\) is what we are looking for.

Lemma 23.61 Shafts in series (zaporedna vezava gredi)

Let two massless shafts with torsional stiffnesses \(k_1 > 0\) and \(k_2 > 0\) (lemma 13.23) be connected in series: the right end of the first is firmly joined to the left end of the second, there is no disk at the joint and no external torque acts on it. Let a torque \(M\) about the axis act on the right end of the second shaft. Then:

  1. The torque in every section of both shafts is \(M\).
  2. The right end of the second shaft turns against the left end of the first by \(\delta = M/k^*\), where the equivalent stiffness (nadomestna togost) \(k^*\) is given by
    \[\frac{1}{k^*} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}, \qquad k^* = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2} . \tag{23.47}\]
  3. The equivalent stiffness is smaller than both, \(k^* < k_1\) and \(k^* < k_2\).
Proof

We have to show that the same torque passes through both shafts and that the twists add. The steps are shown in figure 23.77.

  1. The second shaft has no mass, so it has no moment of inertia, and the sum of the torques on it about the axis is zero during motion too. The torque \(M\) at the right end and the torque of the first shaft at the joint act on it, so the latter is \(-M\).
  2. By the law of action and reaction the second shaft acts on the first at the joint with the torque \(M\). There is no disk and no external torque at the joint, so this is the only torque at the right end of the first shaft. Both shafts are therefore loaded by the torque \(M\), which is the first point.
  3. By lemma 13.23 the right end of the first shaft turns against its left end by \(\delta_1 = M/k_1\) and the right end of the second against the joint by \(\delta_2 = M/k_2\).
  4. The joint is firm, so the twist of the right end against the left end is the sum of the two, \(\delta = \delta_1 + \delta_2\) \(= M\,(1/k_1 + 1/k_2)\).
  5. The equivalent stiffness is the ratio \(k^* = M/\delta\), so \(1/k^* = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\). A common denominator gives \(1/k^* = (k_1 + k_2)/(k_1 k_2)\), which is the second point.
  6. Because \(k_2/(k_1 + k_2) < 1\), we have \(k^* = k_1\cdot k_2/(k_1 + k_2) < k_1\). In the same way, with the indices exchanged, \(k^* < k_2\). This is the third point.

Source: FPGL equations (2.6) to (2.9) (PDF p. 39) for two springs connected in series: the force in both springs is equal, the extensions add, \(1/k_{\mathit{eff}} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\). The transfer to shafts and the proof are ours.

We test the lemma in figure 23.77. For our shafts

\[k^* = \frac{94\,731{,}6\cdot 37\,892{,}6}{94\,731{,}6 + 37\,892{,}6} = 27\,066{,}2\ \text{N m/rad} ,\]

and the twist \(M/k^* = 84{,}61/27\,066{,}2 = 3{,}13\) mrad is indeed the sum of \(0{,}89\) and \(2{,}23\) mrad. The number has one more test. Both shafts have the same diameter, so together they are one shaft of length \(L_1 + L_2 = 0{,}7\) m, and lemma 13.23 gives for it \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}/0{,}7 = 27\,066{,}2\) N m/rad, the same. This is why the orange line in the figure is straight: a shaft of constant section twists under the same torque by the same amount on every centimetre of its length. With shafts of different diameters the line would have a kink at the joint, and the lemma would hold all the same.

From here comes a rule worth remembering on an example. Shafts with stiffnesses \(k/2\) and \(k\) give in series

\[k^* = \frac{\tfrac12 k\cdot k}{\tfrac12 k + k} = \frac{k}{3} .\]

We see the reason on the two shafts of the figure: with equal sections the shaft with stiffness \(k/2\) is twice as long as the shaft with stiffness \(k\), so together they are three times as long, and a shaft three times as long is three times softer. A series connection is always softer than the softer shaft (point 3), because every added piece adds its own twist.

In the lectures the torsional stiffness of a shaft is written \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) and the equivalent stiffness with a star, \(k^*\), as here.

When the lemma does not hold is shown by our chain with the flywheel at the joint (figure 23.73, mode 2). In the second mode the twist of the first shaft is \(0{,}0618\) and of the second \(1{,}5398\) per unit twist of the generator, so the torques in the shafts are \(k_1\cdot 0{,}0618 = 5850\) and \(k_2\cdot 1{,}5398 = 58\,349\) N m per radian. They differ by the factor \(9{,}97\), because the flywheel uses up the difference for its angular acceleration. The condition of the lemma that there is no disk at the joint is therefore not a formality. Without the flywheel the disks \(J_1\) and \(J_3\) on the equivalent shaft would vibrate with frequency \(\sqrt{k^*(J_1 + J_3)/(J_1 J_3)}/(2\pi) = 126{,}48\) Hz, with the flywheel at the joint the first positive frequency is \(70{,}19\) Hz.

Now the gear pair. Let the drive be as in figure 23.78 at the top left. On the axis of the engine there are the engine disk \(J_1\) and, after the shaft \(k_1\), the flywheel with gear \(a\), which together have the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{a}}\). Below it runs the parallel axis of the generator. On it there is gear \(b\) with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{b}}\), which meshes with gear \(a\), and after the shaft \(k_{\mathrm{g}}\) the rotor of the generator \(J_{\mathrm{g}}\). The gears touch on circles with radii \(r_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(r_{\mathrm{b}}\). These are the working radii: the radii of the circles that roll on each other without slip as the gears mesh.

What the gears do with the twists is shown in the right part of figure 23.78, the end view. Gear \(a\) and gear \(b\) touch at one point, to which we draw the radii. Turn gear \(a\) by the angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\). A point on its rim moves along an arc of length \(r_{\mathrm{a}}\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\). Teeth cannot pass through teeth, so every tooth of gear \(a\) that goes through the contact point pushes one tooth of gear \(b\) through it. The rim of gear \(b\) therefore moves by an equally long arc, and because the two rims move in the same direction at the contact point, gear \(b\) turns the opposite way. Equal arcs give \(r_{\mathrm{a}}\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} = -r_{\mathrm{b}}\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\). The smaller gear thus turns by the larger angle: in the figure gear \(b\) has half the radius and turns by \(-40^\circ\) when gear \(a\) turns by \(20^\circ\). Because the two gears have teeth of the same size, the number of teeth is proportional to the circumference, so the ratio of the radii equals the ratio of the numbers of teeth.

Definition 23.62 Gear pair and gear ratio (zobniški par in prestavno razmerje)

A gear pair (zobniški par) is two rigid disks \(a\) and \(b\) on parallel axes with working radii \(r_{\mathrm{a}} > 0\) and \(r_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\) and numbers of teeth \(z_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{b}}\), which mesh without slip and without backlash: their twists \(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\) about their own axes, measured in the same direction, satisfy \(r_{\mathrm{a}}\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} = -r_{\mathrm{b}}\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\) at every time. The gear ratio (prestavno razmerje) of the pair is the number

\[\gamma = \frac{r_{\mathrm{a}}}{r_{\mathrm{b}}} = \frac{z_{\mathrm{a}}}{z_{\mathrm{b}}} > 0, \qquad \text{so that}\quad \varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\,\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} . \tag{23.48}\]

Source: FPGL §9.6.1 (PDF pp. 408 to 409): for a perfect gear pair the tangential velocities are equal, \(R_2\dot\phi_2 = -R_3\dot\phi_3\), the gear ratio is \(\gamma = R_2/R_3 = N_2/N_3\) and \(\phi_3 = -\gamma\phi_2\). The labels \(a\) and \(b\) for the gears are ours (FPGL counts them as disks \(2\) and \(3\)).

The ratio \(\gamma\) says how many turns the shaft of gear \(b\) makes for one turn of the shaft of gear \(a\). With \(z_{\mathrm{a}} = 60\) and \(z_{\mathrm{b}} = 30\) teeth \(\gamma = 2\): when the engine runs at \(1500\) 1/min, the generator runs at \(3000\) 1/min. The definition has two idealizations, which we name here because they carry the whole theorem. The teeth are rigid: a tooth is short and thick against a shaft half a metre long, so under the same force it bends much less than the shaft twists. If we do not assume this, there is one more spring between the gears and the drive has four degrees of freedom instead of three (the more formal approach of FPGL §9.6.2 allows for it). There is no backlash: the flanks of the teeth touch all the time and pass the force \(F\) to each other. This holds as long as the torque through the mesh does not change sign. When it does, the flanks of real gears separate for a moment, equation (23.48) does not hold then and neither does the theorem below.

The disk \(J_1\) and the flywheel are a chain of two disks, gear \(b\) and the generator another. The gear pair ties them into one system. Four disks have four twists, but equation (23.48) fixes one of them from another, so there are three degrees of freedom. Is this again a chain of three disks? It is, if we convert the quantities on the generator shaft correctly to the engine shaft. We call this referring the drive to one shaft.

Theorem 23.63 Referring a drive with a gear pair to one shaft (redukcija pogona z zobniškim parom na eno gred)

Let disks with moments of inertia \(J_1 > 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{a}} > 0\) on the first axis be joined by a massless shaft with stiffness \(k_1 > 0\) (lemma 13.23), and disks \(J_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{g}} > 0\) on a second, parallel axis by a massless shaft with stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{g}} > 0\). Let the disks \(a\) and \(b\) be a gear pair with gear ratio \(\gamma\) (definition 23.62). The twists \(\varphi_1\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}\) are measured about their own axes in the same direction, and external torques \(Q_1\), \(Q_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{g}}\) act on the disks. Let the referred twist of the generator be \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* = -\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}/\gamma\). Then:

  1. The kinetic and the potential energy of the drive are
    \[T = \tfrac12 J_1\dot\varphi_1^2 + \tfrac12\,(J_{\mathrm{a}} + \gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{b}})\,\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + \tfrac12\,\gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{g}}\,(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*)^2, \qquad V = \tfrac12 k_1\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} - \varphi_1)^2 + \tfrac12\,\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}})^2 . \tag{23.49}\]
  2. The column \((\varphi_1, \varphi_{\mathrm{a}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*)\) solves the equation of motion (23.46) of a chain of three disks (definition 23.56) with moments of inertia \(J_1\), \(J_{\mathrm{a}} + \gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(\gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{g}}\), stiffnesses \(k_1\), \(\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}\) and torques \(Q_1\), \(Q_{\mathrm{a}} - \gamma Q_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(-\gamma Q_{\mathrm{g}}\). We call this chain the equivalent chain.
  3. The natural frequencies of the drive are the natural frequencies of the equivalent chain. The true twists are \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} = -\gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\), and the torque in the generator shaft is \(k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{b}}) = -\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\,\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}})\), in size \(1/\gamma\) of the torque in the second shaft of the equivalent chain.
Proof

We have to show that the energies of the drive, written with three independent twists, have the form of the energies of a chain of three disks. The parts of the drive are shown in figure 23.78.

  1. By equation (23.48), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\) at every time (the equal arcs on the rims), so also \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\). Gear \(b\) turns about a fixed axis and by theorem 8.92 has the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{b}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}^2 = \tfrac12\gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{b}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}^2\).
  2. From \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} = -\gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\) follows \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} = -\gamma\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\), so the kinetic energy of the generator is \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{g}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^2 = \tfrac12\gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{g}}(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*)^2\).
  3. The twist of the generator shaft is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* + \gamma\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} = -\gamma\,(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}})\). Its potential energy is (step 2 of the proof of lemma 23.57) \(\tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{b}})^2 = \tfrac12\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}})^2\).
  4. The energies of the disk \(J_1\), the flywheel with gear \(a\) and the shaft \(k_1\) are \(\tfrac12 J_1\dot\varphi_1^2\), \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{a}}\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}^2\) and \(\tfrac12 k_1(\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} - \varphi_1)^2\) (steps 1 and 2 of the same proof). The sum with steps 1 to 3 is equation (23.49), which is the first point.
  5. The virtual work of the external torques is \(Q_1\delta\varphi_1 + Q_{\mathrm{a}}\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} + Q_{\mathrm{b}}\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} + Q_{\mathrm{g}}\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}\). With \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}} = -\gamma\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} = -\gamma\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\) this is \(Q_1\delta\varphi_1 + (Q_{\mathrm{a}} - \gamma Q_{\mathrm{b}})\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{a}} + (-\gamma Q_{\mathrm{g}})\,\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\), so the coefficients are the generalized forces of the coordinates \((\varphi_1, \varphi_{\mathrm{a}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*)\) (lemma 8.52).
  6. The tooth forces are equal and opposite by the law of action and reaction and act at the contact point, where the two rims have the same virtual displacement because the gears mesh without slip. Their total virtual work is therefore zero and the constraint is ideal (definition 8.3).
  7. The energies of point 1 have the form of the energies \(T\) and \(V\) of steps 1 and 2 of the proof of lemma 23.57 for a chain of three disks with the stated moments of inertia and stiffnesses. The coordinates \((\varphi_1, \varphi_{\mathrm{a}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*)\) are independent, so by theorem 8.101 we get the same equations as in step 4 of that proof, with the torques of step 5. This is the second point.
  8. The same equation has the same eigenproblem, hence the same natural frequencies of the equivalent chain. The twists follow from the definitions of \(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^*\) and \(\gamma\). The expression of step 3, multiplied by \(k_{\mathrm{g}}\), gives \(k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{b}}) = -\gamma k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}}) = -\tfrac1\gamma\,\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{g}}^* - \varphi_{\mathrm{a}})\). This is the third point.

Source: FPGL §9.6.1, equations (9.6) to (9.9) (PDF pp. 409 to 410): four disks with a gear pair, elimination of the force between the gears, the "referred form of the model" with the mass matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(I_1,\ I_2 + \gamma^2 I_3,\ \gamma^2 I_4)\), the inertias and stiffnesses on the lower shaft are multiplied by \(\gamma^2\), the torques and angles by \(-\gamma\). FPGL eliminates the tooth force from the four equations of motion. The proof with the energies is ours.

The theorem says that a shaft that turns \(\gamma\) times faster counts with \(\gamma^2\). Why with the square? The first view is energy. The generator turns \(\gamma\) times faster than the disk that replaces it, and kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed. In the same way the generator shaft twists by \(\gamma\) times as much, and potential energy grows with the square of the twist. The equivalent disk \(\gamma^2 J_{\mathrm{g}}\) and the equivalent shaft \(\gamma^2 k_{\mathrm{g}}\) are chosen so that at the twists of the engine shaft they have the same energy as the true parts at their own twists.

The second view shows where each of the two factors \(\gamma\) comes from, which the energy hides. Look at the tooth forces in the right part of figure 23.78. Gear \(b\) acts on gear \(a\) with the force \(F\), gear \(a\) on it with an equal and opposite one. The same force has the lever arm \(r_{\mathrm{b}}\) and the torque \(F r_{\mathrm{b}}\) about the axis of gear \(b\), and the lever arm \(r_{\mathrm{a}}\) and the torque \(F r_{\mathrm{a}} = \gamma\,F r_{\mathrm{b}}\) about the axis of gear \(a\). On its way from the generator shaft to the engine shaft the torque is multiplied by \(\gamma\). This is the first factor. The second is the twist: gear \(b\) turns by \(\gamma\) times the twist of gear \(a\), so for the same twist of the engine shaft the generator shaft returns a torque \(\gamma\) times larger. Stiffness is torque per unit twist, so it gets both factors, \(\gamma\cdot\gamma = \gamma^2\). The same holds for the moment of inertia with the angular acceleration in place of the twist.

In the lectures the referred quantities are written with a star, \(J^* = J\cdot(\text{ratio})^2\) and \(k^* = k\cdot(\text{ratio})^2\), and the ratio with the radii of the gears. The ratio is always the speed of the shaft we refer, divided by the speed of the shaft we refer to, with us \(\gamma = r_{\mathrm{a}}/r_{\mathrm{b}}\).

Gear \(b\) turns twice as much as gear \(a\) and the opposite way, as equation (23.48) requires. The model with a rigid mesh therefore fails precisely near the critical speeds, where the vibration of the torque is largest.

23.9.3 Transfer matrices for torsion (prenosne matrike za torzijo)

We got the natural frequencies of the chain from the matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\), which treat all disks at once. The same question can be asked differently, as if we inspected the chain link by link. We choose a frequency \(\omega\), choose the twist of the first disk at the free left end and go along the chain to the right: at every disk and at every shaft we compute what is on the other side. At the right end the torque must be zero, because there is nothing there that could supply it. If a torque is left over, the chosen \(\omega\) is not a natural frequency. This view adds two things. We compute with only two numbers at a time, not with a matrix of order \(N\), so the procedure can be done by hand for a long chain too. And it shows what is special about a natural frequency: only at it can the chain vibrate without anyone turning it from outside. The procedure of trying frequencies on a torsional chain bears Holzer's name (Holzer, Analysis of Torsional Vibration, 1921), and the notation with matrices is called the transfer matrix method (metoda prenosnih matrik). The same idea holds for the chain of masses on springs of chapter 10, where displacement replaces the twist and force the torque.

The assumptions are those of definition 23.56, and one is new. The chain vibrates freely and synchronously: all disks vibrate with the same frequency and pass through the equilibrium position together, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}(t) = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\cos\omega t\). This is no restriction, since by lemma 10.14 every such motion is an eigenpair and conversely. The number \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the amplitude of the twist of disk \(k\). The torque in shaft \(k\) then also varies as \(\cos\omega t\), and we write its amplitude \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = k_{\mathrm{k}}(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \Phi_{\mathrm{k}})\). A positive \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) means that shaft \(k\) turns disk \(k\) forward and disk \(k + 1\) back.

Draw a chain of three disks and cut it in thought just to the right of every disk (figure 23.79). At every cut we are interested in two numbers, the twist of the disk to the left of the cut and the torque in the shaft we have cut. Together they are the state at the cut. To the left of the first disk there is nothing, so the state there is \(\vek z_0 = (\Phi_1, 0)\): the twist of the first disk and zero torque. After the first disk the state is \(\vek z_1 = (\Phi_1, M_1)\), after the second \(\vek z_2 = (\Phi_2, M_2)\), after the last \(\vek z_3 = (\Phi_3, R)\), where \(R\) is the torque that a shaft to the right of the last disk would have to supply, if there were one.

How do we get from one state to the next? The bottom left part of figure 23.79 takes us across a disk. On disk \(k\) there act the left shaft with the torque \(M_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) backward and the right shaft with the torque \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) forward. The law of angular momentum for the disk is \(J_{\mathrm{k}}\ddot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = M_{\mathrm{k}} - M_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), and because \(\ddot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = -\omega^2\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) in harmonic vibration, \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = M_{\mathrm{k}-1} - \omega^2 J_{\mathrm{k}}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\). The twist is the same on both sides of the disk, while the torque changes by as much as the disk needs for its angular acceleration. The bottom right part takes us across a shaft. Shaft \(k\) has no mass, so the torque is the same at both ends. The line on its surface is straight before and oblique after: the right end turns against the left one by \(M_{\mathrm{k}}/k_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}} + M_{\mathrm{k}}/k_{\mathrm{k}}\). Across a shaft the torque does not change, but the twist does. Both passages are linear in the twist and the torque, so we write them with two matrices of order \(2\).

Definition 23.65 State and transfer matrices for torsion (stanje in prenosni matriki za torzijo)

Let a chain of disks be given by definition 23.56 and let \(\omega > 0\) be a number. The state (stanje) after disk \(k\) is the column of two real numbers \(\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = (\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}, M_{\mathrm{k}})\), the amplitude of the twist of disk \(k\) and the amplitude of the torque in the shaft to its right. The field matrix (matrika polja) of shaft \(k\) and the point matrix (matrika točke) of disk \(k\) at the frequency \(\omega\) are the matrices of order \(2\)

\[\mat P_{\mathrm{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/k_{\mathrm{k}} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mat D_{\mathrm{k}}(\omega) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\omega^2 J_{\mathrm{k}} & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{23.50}\]

The transfer matrix (prenosna matrika) of the chain at the frequency \(\omega\) is the product

\[\mat T(\omega) = \mat D_{\mathrm{N}}(\omega)\,\mat P_{\mathrm{N}-1}\,\mat D_{\mathrm{N}-1}(\omega)\cdots\mat P_1\,\mat D_1(\omega) , \tag{23.51}\]

in which the matrices follow from right to left as the parts of the chain follow from left to right.

Source: GR Problem 4.9 (PDF p. 351): the state (displacement, axial force) at the end of a bar is related to the state at its beginning by a matrix of order \(2\), as for discrete systems. Holzer (1921) is listed in the references of chapter 2 of GR (PDF p. 166). The matrices for a disk and a shaft and the symbols are ours, derived from lemma 23.57.

In the lectures the state is the column \(V\), the field matrix is \(P\) and the point (mass) matrix \(M\). We write the point matrix as \(\mat D\), because with us \(\mat M\) is the mass matrix and \(M\) a torque.

Theorem 23.66 Frequency equation of a free chain from the transfer matrix (frekvenčna enačba proste verige iz prenosne matrike)

Let a chain of disks be free by definition 23.56, \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) the matrices of lemma 23.57, \(\omega > 0\) and \(\mat T(\omega)\) the transfer matrix by definition 23.65 with elements \(T_{11}\), \(T_{12}\), \(T_{21}\), \(T_{22}\). For a number \(\Phi_1\) let the numbers \(\Phi_2, \dots, \Phi_{\mathrm{N}}\) and \(M_1, \dots, M_{\mathrm{N}}\) be determined in turn by \(M_0 = 0\) and

\[M_{\mathrm{k}} = M_{\mathrm{k}-1} - \omega^2 J_{\mathrm{k}}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}} \quad (k = 1, \dots, N), \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{M_{\mathrm{k}}}{k_{\mathrm{k}}} \quad (k = 1, \dots, N - 1) . \tag{23.52}\]

Then:

  1. The states \(\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = (\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}, M_{\mathrm{k}})\) satisfy \(\vek z_1 = \mat D_1\vek z_0\) with \(\vek z_0 = (\Phi_1, 0)\) and \(\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat D_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat P_{\mathrm{k}-1}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) for \(k = 2, \dots, N\), so \(\vek z_{\mathrm{N}} = \mat T(\omega)\,\vek z_0\) and the residual torque (ostanek momenta) is \(R(\omega) = M_{\mathrm{N}} = T_{21}(\omega)\,\Phi_1\).
  2. The column \(\boldsymbol\Phi = (\Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_{\mathrm{N}})\) satisfies the first \(N - 1\) rows of the equation \((\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\boldsymbol\Phi = \vek 0\), and the last row equals \(R(\omega)\).
  3. The number \(\omega\) is a natural angular frequency of the chain if and only if \(T_{21}(\omega) = 0\). The mode that belongs to it is the column \(\boldsymbol\Phi\) with \(\Phi_1 = 1\), and \(\Phi_1 \ne 0\) in every mode.
  4. The residual is \(R(\omega) = -\omega^2\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{N} J_{\mathrm{k}}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\).
Proof

We have to show that the steps (23.52) are exactly the rows of the eigenproblem, written with the torques in the shafts. The steps are shown in figure 23.79.

23.9.4 Torque of order \(n\) and the modal force (moment reda \(n\) in modalna sila)

The engine drives the line with a torque that is not constant, and presses it into the engine disk. In a four-stroke engine it repeats after two turns, that is after the angle \(4\pi\) (section 23.5). Over this period the torque is expanded in a Fourier series (lemma 9.122) with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 4\pi/\Omega\), so with terms \(c_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m\Omega t/2}\). The term with \(m = 2n\) has frequency \(n\Omega\) and is called order \(n\) (red \(n\)): it repeats \(n\) times in one turn of the crankshaft, so \(n\) says how many times per turn. In a four-stroke engine \(n\) is a multiple of \(\tfrac12\), \(n = \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \dots\) If \(g(\theta)\) is the torque that a cylinder gives at the angle \(\theta\) of its crank, and \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2c_{2\mathrm{n}}\) is the complex amplitude of order \(n\) (lemma 9.122, \(2c_{2\mathrm{n}} = a_{2\mathrm{n}} - \mathrm{i}b_{2\mathrm{n}}\)), its term of order \(n\) is \(\mathrm{Re}\big(T_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\big)\) with amplitude \(|T_{\mathrm{n}}|\).

Cylinder \(k\) fires with a lag \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (firing phase, zamik vžiga), that is by the crank angle by which its cycle is delayed against the cylinder with \(\psi = 0\). Its torque on disk \(k\) (in figure 23.73 this is, for the engine, disk \(J_1\)) is therefore \(g(\Omega t - \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) and order \(n\) is \(\mathrm{Re}\big(T_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\big)\). The torque of order \(n\) thus has the same frequency \(\omega = n\Omega\) on every disk and a different phase. For even firing \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) is fixed in section 23.7. The question is how much each factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\) contributes to a given mode. It is like pushing a swing: a push in time with its own swing drives it, while two children who push it at the same moment from opposite sides cancel.

Theorem 23.68 Response of the chain of disks to a torque of order n (odziv verige diskov na moment reda n)

Let a chain of disks be free by definition 23.56 with eigenpairs \((\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) by corollary 23.58 (\(\omega_1 = 0\)), without damping, and let \(\vek Q(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\vek q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\) with \(\omega = n\Omega > 0\) and a complex column \(\vek q\), for instance \(q_{\mathrm{k}} = T_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\) on the disks with cylinders and \(q_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) elsewhere. The modal force of order \(n\) of mode \(r\) is

\[F_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{N}\varphi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\,q_{\mathrm{k}} . \tag{23.53}\]

Then:

  1. If \(\omega \ne \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) for all \(r\), equation (23.46) has the solution \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\vek x\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\) with
    \[\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{N}\frac{F_{\mathrm{r}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \omega^2}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} , \tag{23.54}\]
    and every other solution differs from it by a free motion. In the rigid-body mode \(r = 1\) the term is \(-F_1\vek\varphi_1/\omega^2\), where \(F_1 = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} q_{\mathrm{k}}/\sqrt{J_\mathrm{c}}\).
  2. If \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) for some \(r \ge 2\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\), the equation has no bounded solution: the modal coordinate \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) contains a term \(\mathrm{Re}\big(F_{\mathrm{r}}\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}/(2\mathrm{i}\omega)\big)\) with amplitude \(|F_{\mathrm{r}}|\,t/(2\omega)\), which grows with \(t\) without bound.
  3. If \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), mode \(r\) is not excited and the solution (23.54) (without the term \(r\)) holds as in point 1.
Proof

We have to show that the equation decouples into modes and that each modal equation under a harmonic force offers a solution in closed form. Here \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is real, so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q = \mathrm{Re}\big(F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\).

  1. By theorem 10.28, equation (23.46) is equivalent to the equations \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(F_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\), one for each mode, for the modal coordinates \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi\) (definition 10.27).
  2. For \(\omega \ne \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) we try \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\). By theorem 9.34 (point 3), \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(-\omega^2A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\), so \((\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \omega^2)A = F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(A = F_{\mathrm{r}}/(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \omega^2)\). For \(r = 1\) (\(\omega_1 = 0\)), \(A = -F_1/\omega^2\).
  3. Because \(\vek\varphi = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) (definition 10.27), we get (23.54), a sum of the mode shapes, each with its own amplitude. The difference of two solutions solves the equation without force (lemma 9.6, point 2), that is a free motion. This settles the first point.
  4. For \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) we try \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(B\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\). By theorem 9.34 (point 3) and the product rule, \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(B\,(2\mathrm{i}\omega - \omega^2 t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\), so \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \omega^2\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(2\mathrm{i}\omega B\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\) and \(B = F_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\mathrm{i}\omega)\). Every other solution differs from this one by a free vibration of constant amplitude, while \(|B|\,t\) grows, so it is not bounded. This is the second point.
  5. For \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) the equation for \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is homogeneous, its solutions are free vibrations, and the other modes have the same form by step 2. This is the third point.

Point 1 says what each mode does with the excitation (figure 23.73, the modes below). The modal force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the scalar product of the mode and the excitation, so it measures how much the excitation presses into the shape of the mode, and on each disk it counts with the weight that the mode has there (the values of mode \(2\): a torque on the engine disk counts with \(-0{,}60\), an equal torque on the generator would count with \(1\)). The denominator \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \omega^2\) is the stiffness of the mode \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) (for an \(\mat M\)-orthonormal mode \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\)), reduced by the inertia term \(\omega^2\) at the frequency of the excitation, so the response grows as \(\omega\) approaches \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). For the rigid-body mode the denominator is \(-\omega^2\): the response of order \(n\) in the rigid-body mode is a twist with amplitude \(|\sum_{\mathrm{k}} q_{\mathrm{k}}|/(J_\mathrm{c}\,\omega^2)\) on every disk. This is the oscillation of the twist of the line as a whole with inertia \(J_\mathrm{c}\), as for a flywheel. For order \(3\) of our example (\(T_3 = 31{,}9\) N m on the engine disk, a value from section 23.5, which is our model of engine E, at \(1500\) 1/min) it is \(31{,}9/(0{,}85\cdot 471{,}24^2) = 0{,}169\) mrad.

What if the excitation is exactly at the frequency of a mode and yet nothing happens? The third point of the theorem shows this when the line has several disks with cylinders. Take two equal disks (figure 23.81), \(J = 0{,}5\) kg m\(^2\), joined by a shaft \(k = 2\cdot 10^4\) N m/rad, with a cylinder on each, and look at the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\varphi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\) for the mode in which the disks twist in opposite senses.

First the left side, order \(n = 1\). The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\) is \(1\) for \(\psi_1 = 0\) and also \(1\) for \(\psi_2 = 2\pi\), so the exciting torques on the two disks are in phase. The mode in which the disks twist oppositely is \(\vek\varphi_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt{2J}\) (the sign is arbitrary), here \((1, -1)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) m\(^{-1}\). The term of the first disk is \(+1\) (blue arrow to the right), of the second \(-1\) (orange, to the left), the sum is zero (open circle): the mode does not feel a torque that is pushed at the same moment from both sides, like two children on a swing. Then the right side, order \(n = \tfrac12\): the second factor is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi} = -1\), the term of the second disk is \((-1)\cdot(-1) = +1\), the orange arrow turns and points the same way as the blue one, and the sum is \(2\) (blackwhite arrow). In a formula, \(F_2 = T_{\mathrm{n}}\,(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}n})/\sqrt{2J}\) and \(|F_2| = 2\,|T_{\mathrm{n}}|\,|\sin\pi n|/\sqrt{2J}\), which is zero for whole \(n\) and \(2|T_{\mathrm{n}}|/\sqrt{2J}\) for odd \(2n\).

Example 23.71 Running speed 1500 1/min and orders 2,5 and 3 (delovna hitrost 1500 1/min in redi 2,5 in 3)

The running speed \(1500\) 1/min is \(25\) Hz, which is \(2{,}8\) times less than \(f_2 = 70{,}19\) Hz, so it would seem that the line is stiff and that the running speed is far from all critical speeds. Yet orders \(2{,}5\) and \(3\) lie near: \(N_{\mathrm{r},3} = 1404\) is \(6{,}4\) % below the running speed and \(N_{\mathrm{r},2{,}5} = 1685\) is \(12{,}3\) % above it (figure 23.82: \(1404\) to the left and \(1685\) to the right of the running speed). At the running speed orders \(2{,}5\) and \(3\) excite with angular frequencies \(392{,}70\) and \(471{,}24\) 1/s, closer to \(\omega_2 = 441{,}02\) 1/s than any other order.

In figure 23.82 the orders \(2{,}5\) and \(3\) are blackwhite. The torque on the engine disk has, by section 23.5, amplitudes \(T_{2{,}5} = 49{,}9\) and \(T_3 = 31{,}9\) N m. By theorem 23.68 the modal force is \(F_2 = T_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{2,1}\), where \(\varphi_{2,1}\) is the first element of the \(\mat M\)-normalized mode. The ratio \(\omega_2^2/|\omega_2^2 - \omega^2|\) is \(4{,}83\) at order \(2{,}5\) and \(7{,}05\) at order \(3\): the modal part of the response is that many times larger than for slow, static excitation. The twists of the disks at order \(3\) (\(\varphi_1\), \(\varphi_2\) and \(\varphi_3\)) have amplitudes \(0{,}764\), \(1{,}011\) and \(1{,}334\) mrad. The flywheel and the generator vibrate in opposite senses, as in mode \(2\), so the twist of the second shaft is the sum \(1{,}011 + 1{,}334 = 2{,}345\) mrad.

The torque in the second shaft (figure 23.73, the second shaft) is \(k_2\,(\varphi_3 - \varphi_2)\) with amplitude \(88{,}86\) N m, which is \(2{,}79\) times the exciting torque \(31{,}9\) N m and comparable to the mean engine torque \(84{,}61\) N m (section 23.5). If the line were rigid, it would have an angular acceleration of amplitude \(T_3/J_\mathrm{c}\) and the generator would need \(J_3\) times that as its torque. The second shaft would then carry \(J_3 T_3/J_\mathrm{c} = 11{,}26\) N m, the dynamic torque is \(7{,}9\) times larger. At order \(2{,}5\) the torque of the second shaft is \(91{,}80\) N m (\(1{,}84\,T_{\mathrm{n}}\)) against \(17{,}61\) N m for a rigid line. A shaft that is strong enough for the mean torque can be loaded by the vibration with several times the exciting torque, because the frequencies came close.

A near miss (figure 23.82, order \(2\) and its crossing): "critical speeds are only where an order and a frequency coincide". For order \(2\) (amplitude \(50{,}7\) N m) the ratio is only \(2{,}03\) and the torque of the second shaft is \(38{,}13\) N m, \(2{,}1\) times more than for a rigid line, although \(N_{\mathrm{r},2} = 2106\) 1/min is \(40\) % above the running speed. The factor \(\omega_2^2/|\omega_2^2 - \omega^2|\) grows as the order approaches the critical speed, so the critical speed is not a sharp boundary.

Damping is left out of the theorem. If an elastic mode has a damping ratio \(\zeta\) (modal damping, theorem 10.66), the dynamic factor at frequency ratio \(1\) is by theorem 9.66 (point 3) exactly \(1/(2\zeta)\): for \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) this is \(50\), for \(0{,}02\) it is \(25\). The ratio \(\zeta\) is an assumption here, not data. Damping limits the height of the peak at the line \(f_2\) but does not remove the peak.

Two omissions are named. The first is the variable moment of inertia of the crank mechanism \(J(\theta)\) (section 23.4): within the \(0{,}05\) kg m\(^2\) of disk \(J_1\) it varies from \(0{,}002094\) to \(0{,}007024\) kg m\(^2\), that is by \(\pm 0{,}0025\) kg m\(^2\) (\(\pm 4{,}9\) %), and this moves \(f_2\) by less than \(0{,}1\) % (by the formula for two disks \(0{,}08\) %, by the exact computation with three disks \(0{,}096\) %, NumPy). Its effect as a parametric excitation is a separate phenomenon that we do not treat here. The second is damping, which the theorem and the example take only as \(\zeta\).

What is proved here and what is not? Lemma 23.57, corollary 23.58, lemma 23.61, theorem 23.63, theorem 23.66 and theorem 23.68 are proved for a chain of disks without damping and without shaft mass, with teeth that are rigid and without backlash. FPGL Example 9.4.1 and the computation of the chain engine, flywheel, generator are checked. We do not prove how well the model with disk \(J_1\) replaces a real engine, nor how accurate the chosen \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) is. We do not treat the mass of the shaft, the variable \(J(\theta)\) or damping. The structure of the excitation for several cylinders with even firing is completed by section 23.7, other shaft sections to which lemma 13.23 does not apply by chapter 16, and the extension to many disks by chapter 21.
