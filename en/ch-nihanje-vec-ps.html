Chapter 9 described a system with a single number: one coordinate, one equation and one frequency. Such a model is enough for a weight on a cantilever or for a machine on mounts that moves only vertically. Real systems, however, have several parts that move at the same time. A structure with two floors (stropa) has two horizontal displacements, one for each floor. A pin in which two bars meet can move in two directions. A machine on mounts also tilts, besides bouncing. A bar cut into sixteen finite elements from chapter 7 has sixteen nodes and each has its own displacement. How many numbers describe the motion of such a system? How do these numbers move if we displace the system once and release it? And how do we compute the motion caused by a force of arbitrary shape?

The answer rests on an idea we already know from section 8.13.3. Among all motions some are simple: all coordinates move in step, with the same frequency, and reach zero together. Such motions are modes of vibration (definition 8.122). The chapter will show that a system with \(n\) degrees of freedom has \(n\) such modes, that every free motion is their sum, and that a motion driven by a force also splits into motions along the modes. Each mode obeys the equation with one degree of freedom from chapter 9, so all we know about one degree of freedom is used once for each mode.

What is new here? Chapter 8 found the modes of order two with a \(2 \times 2\) determinant. For order \(n\) the book has no determinants (it treats them only for orders two and three), and we do not need them: we shall prove the theorem on modes by finding a smallest value, as in chapter 2 we proved the theorem on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix (theorem 2.61). Chapter 9 added damping and force for one coordinate, here we get them for \(n\) coordinates. It is also new that we must be able to assemble the mass matrix, not only the stiffness matrix, and that the orders \(n\) of computer models reach into the tens and hundreds.

The route is this. In the first section (10.1) we write the equation of motion for \(n\) coordinates and show where the matrices in it come from. In the second (10.2) we ask at which frequencies and in which shapes the system can vibrate so that all coordinates move in step. For an order larger than two the answer needs a theorem, which we prove in the third section (10.3). In the fourth (10.4) we use it to split the free motion, in the fifth (10.5) we treat systems that can move as a rigid body. The sixth section (10.6) shows how to estimate a frequency from an approximate shape without solving the whole problem. The seventh (10.7) shows how the mass of a bar is shared among its nodes, the eighth (10.8) uses the estimates for approximate computations. In the ninth section (10.9) we add damping and a force of arbitrary shape, in the tenth (10.10) a harmonic force, in the eleventh (10.11) we show how an additional mass on a spring calms the vibration of a cantilever, and in the last (10.12) how to compute the motion step by step in time when splitting into modes is not enough.

Four models of ours will accompany us, and we fix their numbers when we use them: a frame with two floors, the cantilever of two bars from chapter 4 with a mass at the joint, a steel bar of finite elements, and a machine on mounts that also rocks besides bouncing. The first section introduces the first, the second and the last.
