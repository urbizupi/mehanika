Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.3 Mesh convergence and extrapolation

In chapter 7 we computed the force at the clamp of a bar with \(1\), \(2\), \(4\), \(8\) and \(16\) elements and obtained \(40{,}06\), \(55{,}09\), \(58{,}85\), \(59{,}78\) and \(60{,}02\) N, while the exact value is \(60{,}10\) N. For this bar we know the exact value. For a real structure we do not, and yet we must know whether the mesh is fine enough and how much is still missing to the true value. This section answers the question on three meshes, without the exact value.

The idea is one picture. We draw the values of three meshes as three points on a number line, here for the first eigenvalue of a bar, which we compute further on.

On the axis is the value of the quantity and every mesh is one point: the coarse mesh \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) at \(12{,}000\), the twice finer \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\) at \(10{,}387\) and the four times finer \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4}\) at \(9{,}997\). The exact value \(Q\) we do not see in a real problem, here we draw it dashed so that the conclusion can be checked. What we do see are the differences: \(d_1 = 1{,}613\) between the first two meshes and \(d_2 = 0{,}390\) between the second two. The second is about a quarter of the first. What we do not see are the errors, the distances from the points to the dashed line: \(e(h) = 2{,}130\), \(e(h/2) = 0{,}517\) and \(e(h/4) = 0{,}127\). Each of them is also about a quarter of the one before, and that is exactly why the differences shrink by the same factor: a difference is the segment between two errors.

Two conclusions of the section follow. The first: the factor by which the differences shrink tells how fast the error falls, here \(4 = 2^2\). The second is in part (b), on the same axis with the same exact value. If \(e(h)\) is four times \(e(h/2)\), then the difference \(d_1\) is three times the error that is left. So from \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\) we step on by \(d_1/3 = 0{,}538\) and land at \(\hat Q = 9{,}849\), only \(0{,}021\) from the exact value, while the mesh itself was \(e(h/2) = 0{,}517\) away from it. Part (c) shows the same picture for a quantity whose differences do not yet shrink by a constant factor, and its turn comes at the end of the section. Everything that follows makes this picture exact: when the differences really shrink by a constant factor, how good the step is and when it fails.

Of the three errors of definition 17.2 we treat here only the discretization error (diskretizacijska napaka), the difference between the finite-element solution and the exact solution of the same model. A finer mesh does not reduce the modelling error: even if we brought the discretization error to zero, the error of the model remains (COOK, PDF p. 18), and a mesh on a simplified geometry, without the rounded corner, can converge to a wrong result (COOK, PDF p. 345). A finer mesh does not reduce the numerical rounding error either: the condition number of the stiffness matrix (definition 6.31) grows with \(n\), which is the topic of a later section of this chapter. So for every number it must be clear which error we reduced. Notation: \(h\) is the length of an element as in definition 6.24, while the height of a cross section (chapter 5) is written \(H\) in this chapter. The load of chapter 7, which we called \(q\) there, is written \(q_{\mathrm{L}}\) here, because \(q\) is reserved for the order of convergence.

17.3.1 Order of convergence

Take one quantity \(Q\) that the model gives as a single number: an eigenvalue, a support force, a displacement at a point. Let \(Q\) be its exact value in the model and \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) the value that the finite-element method gives on a mesh of equal elements of length \(h\). The meshes follow each other by halving: \(h\), \(h/2\), \(h/4\), and each keeps the nodes of the previous one (nested meshes, consequence 10.60). The error is \(e(h) = Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q\). The quantity must be the same on every mesh: at the same place of the model and at the same place relative to the element (COOK, PDF p. 330). The reason: only then is the number in front of \(h^q\) the same on all meshes. For a point that lies near a node on one mesh and in the middle of an element on another, this number changes from mesh to mesh (in the example of part 17.3.7 between \(0{,}16\) and \(0{,}24\)), the errors jump around the line of slope \(2\) and the quotient of differences gives the order \(1\), \(3\), \(1\) instead of \(2\). How fast \(e(h)\) falls is told by the number \(q\), which we call the order, because it is the "order" of the notation \(O(h^q)\), "of order \(h^q\)" (COOK, PDF p. 327).

Definition 17.11 Order of convergence (red konvergence)

Let \(Q\) be a real number and \((Q_{\mathrm{h}})\) the values of the same quantity on nested meshes of element length \(h\) from the set \(\{h_0, h_0/2, h_0/4, \dots\}\). A number \(q > 0\) is the order of convergence of the quantity \(Q\) if there exist a real number \(C \ne 0\), a number \(M \ge 0\) and a length \(h^* > 0\) such that for every \(h \le h^*\) of the set

\[e(h) = Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q = C\,h^q + R(h) , \qquad |R(h)| \le M\,h^{q+1} . \tag{17.6}\]

The number \(C\) is the error constant (konstanta napake) and \(R(h)\) the remainder (ostanek).

Source: COOK section 9.6 (PDF p. 327) knows only the upper bound \(O(h^q)\) and in section 9.7 (PDF p. 329) assumes that convergence is monotone and that \(q\) is known. The notation with the remainder \(R(h)\) and with \(C \ne 0\) is ours: we take it because lemma 17.12 and theorem 17.14 need it.

What do we already have? Chapters 6, 7 and 10 proved only upper bounds: the energy of the error falls at least as \(h^2\) (theorem 6.41), the values at the nodes and between them at least as \(h^2\) (lemma 6.40), the ratio of frequencies at least as \(\theta^2 \propto h^2\) (consequence 10.54). A bound says that the error is not larger, but not that it equals \(C h^q\) with a constant \(C \ne 0\). For a general quantity we assume the equality after COOK (PDF pp. 327 and 329). This is the main assumption of the section, so let us say three things about it. Why it is sensible: most of the error is given by the lowest term that the polynomial of the element leaves out, and this is proportional to a power of \(h\). In figure 17.24 these are the errors, each about a quarter of the one before. When it does not hold: when the quantity does not exist in the model (the displacement under a point force), when it is not at the same place relative to the element and when the mesh is still too coarse for the lowest term to dominate. All three cases are computed in part 17.3.7. What it costs: the remainder \(R(h)\) is exactly what is left to the extrapolation as its error. For the eigenvalue of the figure, at \(n = 4\) the term is \(C h^2 = 0{,}507\) and the remainder \(R = 0{,}0097\), just under \(2\) % of the error, and the extrapolated value misses by \(0{,}021\) instead of \(0{,}517\). The equality is proved here only on the examples that we compute.

17.3.2 Order from three meshes

In a real problem we do not know \(Q\), so we cannot read \(q\) from \(e(h)\). The difference of two neighbouring meshes, however, does not contain the exact value: \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} = e(h) - e(h/2)\). For \(e = C h^q\) the difference is \(e(h) - e(h/2) = C h^q\,(1 - 2^{-q})\), the next one is \(C (h/2)^q (1 - 2^{-q})\), so their quotient is \(2^q\), in which neither \(C\) nor \(Q\) appears. This is how we arrive at the order \(q\) from three meshes. COOK (PDF p. 327) says the same: when \(h\) is halved, an error of order \(2\) falls about four times. Since the definition also contains a remainder, we must know how well the quotient of differences tells \(q\).

Lemma 17.12 Order from three meshes (red iz treh mrež)

Let \(Q\) have order \(q\) by definition 17.11 with the numbers \(C\), \(M\), \(h^*\). Let \(h \le h^*\) and let the differences of three meshes be

\[d_1 = Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} , \qquad d_2 = Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q_{\mathrm{h}/4} .\]

If \(h \le 2|C|\,(1 - 2^{-q})/(3M)\) (for \(M = 0\) every \(h\)), then \(d_2 \ne 0\) and

\[\left|\frac{d_1}{d_2} - 2^q\right| \le K\,h , \qquad K = \frac{9\cdot 2^{q-1}\,M}{|C|\,(1 - 2^{-q})} . \tag{17.7}\]

Therefore \(\log_2(d_1/d_2)\) tends to \(q\) as \(h \to 0\), and for \(M = 0\) we have \(d_1/d_2 = 2^q\) exactly.

Proof

We must show that in \(d_1 - 2^q d_2\) the term with \(C\) cancels and only the remainder stays, and that \(d_2\) is large enough to divide by it. In figure 17.24, part (a), \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are the blue distances between the points of the three meshes, there for \(q = 2\).

  1. Let \(a = C h^q (1 - 2^{-q})\). By equation (17.6), \(d_1 = e(h) - e(h/2) = a + r_1\) with \(r_1 = R(h) - R(h/2)\) and \(d_2 = e(h/2) - e(h/4) = 2^{-q} a + r_2\) with \(r_2 = R(h/2) - R(h/4)\), since \(C (h/2)^q (1 - 2^{-q}) = 2^{-q} a\). In the figure \(d_1\) is the segment between the ends of the errors \(e(h)\) and \(e(h/2)\), and \(d_2\) the segment between the ends of \(e(h/2)\) and \(e(h/4)\).
  2. Estimate of the remainders: \(|r_1| \le M h^{q+1} + M (h/2)^{q+1} \le \tfrac32 M h^{q+1}\) and \(|r_2| \le M (h/2)^{q+1} + M (h/4)^{q+1} \le \tfrac32 M h^{q+1}\,2^{-(q+1)}\), because \(2^{-(q+1)} < \tfrac12\).
  3. The difference is \(d_1 - 2^q d_2 = r_1 - 2^q r_2\), so \(|d_1 - 2^q d_2| \le \tfrac32 M h^{q+1}\,(1 + \tfrac12) = \tfrac94 M h^{q+1}\). In the figure \(d_1 - 4\,d_2 = 1{,}613 - 4 \cdot 0{,}390 = 0{,}055\), little against \(d_1\).
  4. The condition on \(h\) gives \(|r_2| \le \tfrac12\,2^{-q} |a|\), so \(|d_2| \ge 2^{-q}|a| - |r_2| \ge 2^{-q-1} |C|\,h^q\,(1 - 2^{-q}) > 0\).
  5. The quotient: \(|d_1/d_2 - 2^q| = |d_1 - 2^q d_2|/|d_2| \le \tfrac94 M h^{q+1}\cdot 2^{q+1}/\big(|C|\,h^q (1 - 2^{-q})\big) = K h\). The function \(\log_2\) is continuous at \(2^q > 0\), so \(\log_2(d_1/d_2) \to q\). In the figure \(d_1/d_2\) \(= 1{,}613/0{,}390 = 4{,}14\) and \(\log_2 4{,}14 = 2{,}05\).

Source: The idea of three meshes is in COOK (PDF p. 330): "at least three different meshes are needed so that we may distinguish a curve from a straight line". The lemma and its proof with the bound \(K h\) are not from the source.

17.3.3 The first eigenvalue of a bar

Let us try it on an eigenvalue, where we know the exact value, so that we can check the estimate. A bar of length \(L = 1\) with \(EA = \rho A = 1\) is clamped at both ends, divided into \(n\) equal elements, with a consistent mass (chapter 10). The exact first eigenvalue is \(\omega_1^2 = \pi^2 = 9{,}869604\), because \(c^2 = E/\rho = 1\). For the finite-element method we take steps \(1\) and \(2\) of the proof of theorem 10.52 with \(\theta = \pi/n\): the vector \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin(j\theta)\) is then zero at \(j = 0\) and at \(j = n\), so every row \(j = 1, \dots, n-1\) gives the same \(\lambda\). The eigenvalue is

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{6}{h^2}\,\frac{1 - \cos(\pi h)}{2 + \cos(\pi h)} , \qquad h = \frac1n . \tag{17.8}\]

For \(r = 1, \dots, n-1\) the angle \(\theta = r\pi/n\) lies in \((0, \pi)\) and the eigenvalue grows with \(\theta\) (the function \(g(u) = u/(3 - u)\) from the proof of consequence 10.54 grows), so \(r = 1\) is the lowest. The expansion in \(h\) is given by SymPy: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}} = \pi^2 + \tfrac{\pi^4}{12}\,h^2 + \tfrac{\pi^6}{360}\,h^4 + \dots\) (the book does not prove the expansion, as with equation (10.77)). So \(q = 2\), \(C = \pi^4/12 = 8{,}117\) and \(R(h) \approx 2{,}67\,h^4\). On the meshes of the table \(|R|/h^3 \le 0{,}81\), which we take for \(M\).

The quotient of differences approaches \(4 = 2^2\) as \(h\) decreases, as lemma 17.12 claims. The first row is figure 17.24: \(d_1\) divided by \(d_2\) is \(1{,}613/0{,}390 = 4{,}14\). The bound of the lemma, \(K h\) with \(K = 2{,}4\) (equation (17.7) with \(M = 0{,}81\)), is \(1{,}2\) for the first triple, while the actual difference is \(0{,}14\): the bound is reliable but pessimistic.

We draw the errors of the table. Whoever reads them on a logarithmic scale sees the order as a slope: from \(|e| = |C|\,h^q\) we get \(\log|e| = \log|C| + q\log h\), a straight line with slope \(q\) against \(\log h\). With \(h = 1/n\) the same line has slope \(-q\) against \(\log n\): when \(n\) doubles, the error falls \(2^q\) times.

Let us draw it as on a whiteboard. First the axes of the left part: horizontally the number of elements \(n\), every doubling is the same length, vertically the error relative to the error at \(n = 2\), every decade the same height, so all three lines start at \(1\). The first line, the eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\), we computed above: at every doubling it falls by a factor \(4\). The second, the energy of the error of the linear element, is taken from the table of chapter 7 and falls almost the same. What we have proved is only a bound: the energy of the error is half the square of the energy norm of the error (theorem 6.34), and the norm is at most proportional to \(h\) (theorem 6.41), so the energy of the error falls at least as fast as \(h^2\). The third, the energy of the error of the quadratic element, falls by \(16 = 2^4\) at every doubling. The slope is shown by the grey triangles: over the same width, that is half a doubling of \(n\), the second falls twice as deep as the first. Why is \(q\) twice as large for the quadratic element? The energy of the error is the square of the norm of the error, and the norm measures the error of the derivative. The linear element leaves out the quadratic term of the field, so its error of the derivative is proportional to \(h\) and the energy to \(h^2\). The quadratic element leaves out only the cubic term, the error of the derivative is proportional to \(h^2\) and the energy to \(h^4\) (the numbers are in part 17.3.6). The right part of the figure, with its axes, points and the line of slope \(2\), shows a quantity that is not at the same place relative to the element on every mesh: its errors jump around the line, which we compute in part 17.3.7.

17.3.4 Richardson extrapolation

From history

In computing the stress function with a grid of points and difference equations, the mesh must be refined for greater accuracy, which gives more equations (TIMO, PDF p. 338). L. F. Richardson used difference equations successfully on plane problems of elasticity, where the stress function must satisfy an equation of fourth order, and published this in 1910 (TIMO, PDF p. 338). The formula that computes an improved value from two meshes is called the Richardson extrapolation formula (COOK, PDF p. 330, ONATE1, PDF p. 92). The sources do not say that he introduced it himself in 1910: they say only that the formula carries his name and that he worked with grids on a similar difficulty, an approximate computation in the plane.

Let us return to the first eigenvalue of the bar and to extrapolation with two meshes. For \(n = 4\) and \(8\) we have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}} = 10{,}386642\) and \(9{,}997081\). The error falls as \(h^2\), so for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}}\) we draw a graph against \(h^2\): the points lie almost on a line, which we extend to \(h^2 = 0\). The intersection is an estimate of the exact value. This idea carries Richardson's name and has an exact form.

Theorem 17.14 Richardson extrapolation (Richardsonova ekstrapolacija)

Let \(Q\) have order \(q\) by definition 17.11 with \(C\), \(M\), \(h^*\), let \(h \le h^*\) and let the extrapolated value be

\[\hat Q = Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} + \frac{Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q_{\mathrm{h}}}{2^q - 1} . \tag{17.9}\]
  1. The error of the extrapolated value is \(|\hat Q - Q| \le \dfrac{3M}{2\,(2^q - 1)}\,h^{q+1}\). For \(M = 0\) we have \(\hat Q = Q\).
  2. If instead of \(q\) we take a number \(q' > 0\), \(q' \ne q\), and \(\hat Q'\) by equation (17.9) with \(q'\), then
    \[\hat Q' - Q = \gamma\,C\,(h/2)^q + r , \quad \gamma = 1 - \frac{2^q - 1}{2^{q'} - 1} , \quad |r| \le M h^{q+1}\Big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3}{2\,(2^{q'} - 1)}\Big) . \tag{17.10}\]
    The number \(\gamma\) is \(0\) only for \(q' = q\). The term \(C (h/2)^q\) is the leading term of the error of the mesh \(h/2\), so \(\hat Q'\) multiplies this term by \(\gamma\), and for \(|\gamma| > 1\) the extrapolated value is, for small enough \(h\), worse than the value on the finer mesh.
Proof

We must show that in \(\hat Q - Q\) the term with \(C\) cancels, and estimate what is left with the remainder \(R\). In figure 17.24, part (b), this is the step from \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\) to \(\hat Q\), there for \(q = 2\).

  1. Since \(\hat Q - Q = e(h/2) + \big(e(h/2) - e(h)\big)/(2^q - 1)\), we have \(\hat Q - Q = \big(2^q e(h/2) - e(h)\big)/(2^q - 1)\). In the figure: from the error \(e(h/2)\) of the finer mesh we subtract the step \(d_1/(2^q - 1)\), because \(e(h/2) - e(h) = -d_1\).
  2. By equation (17.6), \(2^q e(h/2) = C h^q + 2^q R(h/2)\) and \(e(h) = C h^q + R(h)\). The terms with \(C\) cancel and \(\hat Q - Q = \big(2^q R(h/2) - R(h)\big)/(2^q - 1)\) remains. In the figure this means: if \(e(h)\) were exactly \(2^q\) times \(e(h/2)\), the difference \(d_1\) would be exactly \((2^q - 1)\) times the error that is left, and the step would land on \(Q\).
  3. Estimate: \(|2^q R(h/2) - R(h)| \le 2^q M (h/2)^{q+1} + M h^{q+1} = \tfrac32 M h^{q+1}\). This is point 1, and for \(M = 0\) we have \(R = 0\). In the figure this rest is the distance \(0{,}021\) between \(\hat Q\) and the dashed line.
  4. For point 2, \(\hat Q' - Q = e(h/2) + \big(e(h/2) - e(h)\big)/(2^{q'} - 1)\). We insert \(e(h/2) = C (h/2)^q + R(h/2)\) and \(e(h/2) - e(h) = C h^q (2^{-q} - 1) + R(h/2) - R(h) = -C (h/2)^q\,(2^q - 1) + R(h/2) - R(h)\). The terms with \(C\) together give \(C (h/2)^q\,\big(1 - (2^q - 1)/(2^{q'} - 1)\big)\).
  5. The remainder is \(r = R(h/2) + \big(R(h/2) - R(h)\big)/(2^{q'} - 1)\) with \(|r| \le M (h/2)^{q+1} + \big(M (h/2)^{q+1} + M h^{q+1}\big)/(2^{q'} - 1) \le M h^{q+1}\big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3}{2(2^{q'} - 1)}\big)\).
  6. The factor \(\gamma = 0\) means \(2^{q'} = 2^q\), so \(q' = q\), because \(2^x\) is strictly increasing.
  7. For \(|\gamma| > 1\) we have \(|\hat Q' - Q| \ge |\gamma|\,|C|\,(h/2)^q - |r|\) and \(|e(h/2)| \le |C|\,(h/2)^q + M (h/2)^{q+1} \le |C|\,(h/2)^q + \tfrac12 M h^{q+1}\). With the bound for \(|r|\) from step 5, \(|\hat Q' - Q| - |e(h/2)| \ge (|\gamma| - 1)\,|C|\,(h/2)^q - M h^{q+1}\big(1 + \tfrac{3}{2(2^{q'} - 1)}\big)\), which is positive for \(h < (|\gamma| - 1)\,|C|\,2^{-q}\big/\big(M\,(1 + \tfrac{3}{2(2^{q'} - 1)})\big)\), and for every \(h\) when \(M = 0\). In figure 17.27, part (c), this is the error \(-0{,}0640\) for \(q' = 1\) against the error of the mesh \(+0{,}0317\).

Source: COOK section 9.7 (PDF pp. 329 to 330, equation 9.7-1) and ONATE1 (PDF pp. 91 to 92, equations 2.60 and 2.61) give the formula for a known order, and COOK also says that with a wrong \(q\) the extrapolated value "may be less accurate than the result at either data point" (PDF p. 330). The proof with bounds is ours.

For \(q = 2\) we have \(\hat Q = Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} + (Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q_{\mathrm{h}})/3\), and this is the point where the line through two points of the graph of \(Q\) against \(h^2\) meets the axis \(h^2 = 0\), as we drew it. The figure shows both, the right and the wrong \(q\).

First the axes of part (a): \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}}\) vertically, \(h^2\) horizontally. Then the points, one for each mesh, \(n = 2\) on the right and \(n = 32\) close to the axis. For \(q = 2\) they lie on the line through \(n = 4\) and \(8\), which we continue to \(h^2 = 0\), to the extrapolated value \(9{,}867227\). The exact value \(\pi^2 = 9{,}869604\) is only \(0{,}0024\) away from it. The points further to the left lie on the line even better. Part (d) shows this. On its axes \(n\) is horizontal and the size of the error vertical, both logarithmic. The error of the extrapolated value goes from \(-2{,}07 \cdot 10^{-2}\) (from \(n = 2, 4\)) through \(-2{,}38 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(-1{,}60 \cdot 10^{-4}\) to \(-1{,}01 \cdot 10^{-5}\) (from \(n = 16, 32\)), while the error of the mesh alone at \(n = 32\) is \(7{,}93 \cdot 10^{-3}\). The blue line falls with slope \(2\), the orange one with a slope close to \(4\), as the triangles show. Theorem 17.14 guarantees the order \(q + 1 = 3\), but here the error falls by a factor close to \(16 = 2^4\). The reason: in equation (17.8) \(h\) appears only in \(h^2\) and in \(\cos(\pi h)\), and both stay the same when \(h\) is replaced by \(-h\). So the expansion knows only even powers of \(h\) and the remainder is of order \(h^4\), not \(h^3\). The theorem is a guarantee, not an exact prediction.

Part (b) is the case in which we took a wrong \(q' = 1\) (the second part of the theorem). There the same set of points is drawn on axes against \(h\) and is not a line but curved. The line through \(n = 8\) and \(16\) gives the extrapolated value \(9{,}805627\), below the exact value. The distances in part (b) are too small to see, so part (c) enlarges them. On the vertical axis the exact value is dashed. The mesh \(n = 16\) is above it by \(3{,}17 \cdot 10^{-2}\), and the value extrapolated with \(q' = 1\) below it by \(6{,}40 \cdot 10^{-2}\), twice as far and on the other side. The value extrapolated with the right \(q = 2\) from the same two meshes lies on the dashed line, with the error \(-1{,}6 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The theorem predicts this: for \(q = 2\) and \(q' = 1\) we have \(\gamma = 1 - 3/1 = -2\) and \(-2 \cdot 3{,}17 \cdot 10^{-2} = -6{,}35 \cdot 10^{-2}\). Extrapolation with a wrong order is therefore twice as bad as the finer mesh and has the opposite sign. Other wrong choices are milder: for \(q = 1\) and \(q' = 2\) we have \(\gamma = 2/3\), and the error decreases by only a third.

The estimate that extrapolation gives is not an exact number. COOK (PDF p. 330) suggests taking the difference \(|\hat Q - Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}|\) as an estimate of the error of the mesh \(h/2\). For \(\lambda_1\) from \(n = 16\) and \(32\) we have \(|\hat Q - Q_{32}|/\hat Q = 0{,}0804\) %, and the true error of the mesh \(n = 32\) is \(0{,}0803\) %. Why they agree so well is shown in figure 17.27, part (d): the last orange point is almost three decades below the last blue one, so \(\hat Q\) is a thousand times closer to the exact value than the mesh is, and the distance from the mesh to \(\hat Q\) is almost the same as the distance from the mesh to the exact value.

17.3.5 The clamp force and the order of a quantity

The same procedure on the example of chapter 7 shows that the order is not a property of the mesh but of the quantity. The bar has \(L = 10\) m, \(EA = 1{,}5394 \cdot 10^7\) N, is fixed at the top and loaded with the distributed load \(q_{\mathrm{L}}\,x/L\), which is zero at the top (\(q_{\mathrm{L}} = 12{,}019\) N/m, twice the self-weight of the bar per metre, so that the total load \(q_{\mathrm{L}} L/2 = 60{,}097\) N equals the weight of the bar, the axis \(x\) pointing down). The true displacement is a cubic polynomial, the axial force is \(N(x) = q_{\mathrm{L}}\,(L^2 - x^2)/(2L)\) and \(N(0) = 60{,}097\) N. We are interested in the force in the first element at the clamp, \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\).

Example 17.15 Order of the clamp force for linear and quadratic elements (red sile v vpetju pri linearnem in kvadratnem elementu)

For the linear element the displacements of the nodes are exact (table in chapter 7), so \(N_{\mathrm{h}} = EA\,\big(u(h) - u(0)\big)/h\) is the mean of the exact force over the first element:

\[N_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac1h\int_0^h N(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{q_{\mathrm{L}}}{2L}\Big(L^2 - \frac{h^2}{3}\Big) , \qquad e(h) = -\frac{q_{\mathrm{L}}}{6L}\,h^2 . \tag{17.11}\]

The error is exactly \(C h^2\) with \(C = -q_{\mathrm{L}}/(6L) = -0{,}2003\) N/m² and \(R = 0\). For the quadratic element lemma 7.27 gives the displacement error \(u - u_{\mathrm{h}} = a\,s\,(s^2 - h^2/4)\) with \(a = u'''/6\) and \(u''' = -q_{\mathrm{L}}/(EA\,L)\) on an element with ends at \(s = \mp h/2\). The strain error at the end \(s = -h/2\) is the derivative \(a\,(3h^2/4 - h^2/4) = a h^2/2\), so

\[N_{\mathrm{h}} = N(0) + \frac{q_{\mathrm{L}}}{12\,L}\,h^2 , \qquad e(h) = +\frac{q_{\mathrm{L}}}{12L}\,h^2 . \tag{17.12}\]

Here too \(R = 0\), the error is \(+0{,}1002\,h^2\) N/m². The values, confirmed in both cases by a direct computation with elements (NumPy):

\(n\) \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) linear (N) error \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) quadratic (N) error
1 \(40{,}065\) \(-20{,}032\) \(70{,}113\) \(+10{,}016\)
2 \(55{,}089\) \(-5{,}008\) \(62{,}601\) \(+2{,}504\)
4 \(58{,}845\) \(-1{,}252\) \(60{,}723\) \(+0{,}626\)
8 \(59{,}784\) \(-0{,}313\) \(60{,}254\) \(+0{,}157\)

Lemma 17.12 gives \(q = 2{,}0000\) for both on every triple of meshes, and Richardson with \(q = 2\) gives exactly \(60{,}097\) N from any pair. For the quadratic element and a wrong \(q' = 4\) (the order of the energy from figure 17.26) the pair \(n = 2, 4\) gives the value \(60{,}598\) N with the error \(0{,}501\) N, while the mesh \(n = 4\) has the error \(0{,}626\) N: extrapolation with a wrong order is almost useless.

Let us draw both forces, to see where the errors and their signs come from.

In part (a) there are the axes: \(x\) from the clamp to the free end, the axial force vertically. The exact force is a parabola that is horizontal at the clamp, because the load is zero there. The linear element has a constant force over the element, the average of the exact one: with one element this is \(40{,}065\) N, with two it is \(55{,}089\) N in the first element. The average is below the value at the clamp, because the force falls away from the clamp: the error is \(-20{,}03\) N and after halving the element \(-5{,}01\) N, a quarter. In part (b), on the same axes and with the same exact force, the force of the quadratic element is a straight line over the element: with one element from \(70{,}113\) N at the clamp to \(10{,}016\) N at the free end, with two a broken line. The line crosses the parabola twice in every element and is above it at the ends: the error at the clamp is \(+10{,}02\) N and after halving \(+2{,}50\) N, again a quarter.

The error of the quadratic element is therefore only half the error of the linear element (with the opposite sign), not \(h^2\) times smaller. The same quadratic element reduced the energy of the error from \(h^2\) to \(h^4\) (figure 17.26). The reason is that the error of the \(r\)-th derivative of the field is of order \(p + 1 - r\) (COOK, PDF p. 327): the force is the first derivative of the displacement, \(r = 1\), so for \(p = 2\) the order is \(2\), for the field \(3\) and for the energy \(4\). For the clamp force the error is \(q = 2\) for both elements, which is not a general rule for the linear element but a consequence of the load being zero at the top: for the linear element \(N_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the mean of the exact force over the element, and to first order the difference to \(N(0)\) is \(N'(0)\,h/2\), where \(-N'(0)\) is the load at the top, here zero: the parabola is horizontal at the clamp, so the average over the first element falls short only by a term with \(h^2\). For a load that is not zero at the top the error is of order \(1\) (COOK, PDF p. 327: the strain error is \(O(h)\)). If we chose another quantity or another load we would have another \(q\), so we check every order with lemma 17.12 on the quantity that we need.

17.3.6 h and p improvement

A mesh can be improved in two ways. In h refinement (h-izboljšava) we add elements of the same kind and \(h\) falls, in p refinement (p-izboljšava) we raise the degree \(p\) of the highest complete polynomial in the element, and the number of elements stays (COOK, section 9.8, PDF pp. 332 to 333). The letters are as in the notation: \(h\) is the element length and \(p\) the degree of the polynomial. The quadratic element of chapter 7 is a p step from the linear (\(p = 1\)) to the quadratic (\(p = 2\)). Let us compare an h step and a p step on the mesh \(n = 2\) for the same bar, with the energy of the error \(\Pi_{\mathrm{h}} - \Pi\) from the table in chapter 7 (µJ):

step unknowns energy of the error factor
start: linear, \(n = 2\) \(2\) \(30{,}957\)
h: linear, \(n = 4\) \(4\) \(8{,}0446\) \(3{,}85\)
p: quadratic, \(n = 2\) \(4\) \(0{,}40732\) \(76\)

At the same additional computing cost, four unknowns, the p step reduced the error \(76\) times and the h step \(3{,}85\) times. The reason is the order: COOK (PDF p. 327) gives \(O(h^{p+1})\) for the field, \(O(h^{p+1-r})\) for the \(r\)-th derivative and \(O(h^{2(p+1-m)})\) for the energy, where \(2m\) is the order of the derivative in the equation. For the bar \(m = 1\), so \(O(h^{2p})\): \(2\) for the linear and \(4\) for the quadratic element, in agreement with the quotients \(4\) and \(16\) of the table in chapter 7. In figure 17.26 these are the slopes: the linear element falls four times when \(n\) doubles, the quadratic one sixteen times. The orders above hold only without singularities (COOK, PDF p. 328), and for derivatives above the degree of the polynomial the smoothness of the true field decides. For a cubic field the cubic element gives zero error, because the field lies in the space of trial functions and by theorem 6.37 \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = u\). Then \(C = 0\) and the order by definition 17.11 is not defined. ONATE1 (PDF p. 361) warns that the p method is hard to implement in a program written for h refinement and that a combination of both is also possible.

17.3.7 When extrapolation fails

Theorem 17.14 has assumptions: the quantity is the same on all meshes, the meshes are proper halvings and the error has the form of definition 17.11. Each of them has a case in which it is missing and the conclusion fails.

The quantity is not at the same place relative to the element. A bar clamped at both ends is loaded with a uniform distributed load \(q_{\mathrm{L}}\) and \(u(x) = q_{\mathrm{L}}\,x\,(L - x)/(2EA)\). The displacements of the nodes are exact (lemma 7.21), and between the nodes \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a line joining the exact values. The exact displacement is higher than this line on the element \([x_{\mathrm{i}}, x_{\mathrm{i}+1}]\) by \(q_{\mathrm{L}}\,(x - x_{\mathrm{i}})(x_{\mathrm{i}+1} - x)/(2EA)\), because this is the quadratic function that has zeros at the ends and second derivative \(-q_{\mathrm{L}}/EA\). For the point \(x = 0{,}4\,L\), \(s = 0{,}4\,n - \lfloor 0{,}4\,n\rfloor\) is the position of the point in the element and

\[e(h) = -\frac{q\,h^2}{2EA}\,s\,(1 - s) = -\frac{q L^2}{2EA}\cdot\frac{s\,(1 - s)}{n^2} , \qquad s = \{0{,}4\,n\} . \tag{17.13}\]

The error is proportional to \(h^2\), but every time it is multiplied by \(s(1 - s)\), which jumps: for \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\) we have \(s = 0{,}8\), \(0{,}6\), \(0{,}2\), \(0{,}4\), \(0{,}8\) and \(s(1 - s) = 0{,}16\), \(0{,}24\), \(0{,}16\), \(0{,}24\), \(0{,}16\). The errors in units of \(q_{\mathrm{L}} L^2/(2EA)\) are \(-0{,}04\), \(-0{,}015\), \(-0{,}0025\), \(-0{,}00094\), \(-0{,}00016\) (figure 17.26). In the right part of figure 17.26 the error axis is the same as before, and the error points lie in a zigzag around the reference line of slope \(2\). The quotients of differences \(d_1/d_2\) are \(2{,}00\), \(8{,}00\), \(2{,}00\) and lemma 17.12 would give \(q = 1\), \(3\), \(1\) instead of \(2\). The constant \(C\) is not a constant but depends on \(h\), and so definition 17.11 is not satisfied. Richardson with \(q = 2\) gives the errors \(-0{,}0067\) (from \(n = 2, 4\)), \(+0{,}0017\), \(-0{,}00042\), \(+0{,}00010\): the sign alternates and the value is not reliable. COOK (PDF p. 330) requires this explicitly: the quantity must be at a fixed place of the model and at a fixed place relative to the element (always at a node, for example), otherwise convergence is not monotone and the formula does not hold. For a value at a node the error in this problem is zero.

The quantity does not exist in the model. Under a point force on a plane body the displacement does not exist: theorem 17.9 of section 17.2 shows that this force has unbounded energy, and consequence 17.10 that the displacement under the force on nested meshes grows without bound. Here we compute this on the simplest model with the same energy. Let \(w(r)\) be a function of the radius on a circle of radius \(R\) with \(w(R) = 0\) and the energy

\[\Pi(w) = \tfrac12\,T\int_{\text{circle}} |\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A - F\,w(0) , \tag{17.14}\]

where \(T > 0\) is a constant with the unit N/m and \(F\) the force at the centre. This is not an elastic body but a scalar model with the energy \(\int |\nabla w|^2\), which appears in the proof of theorem 17.9. The mesh consists of \(n\) rings of width \(h = R/n\) with nodes \(r_{\mathrm{i}} = i h\), on each ring \(w\) is a linear function of \(r\), and the node values are \(w_0, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\).

Lemma 17.16 Displacement under the force in the ring model (pomik pod silo v modelu obročev)

In the model of equation (17.14):

  1. The energy of the \(i\)-th ring is \(\tfrac12\,g_{\mathrm{i}}\,(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1})^2\) with \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = \pi T\,(2i + 1)\), independent of \(h\).
  2. The finite-element solution satisfies \(g_{\mathrm{i}}\,(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1}) = F\) for every \(i = 0, \dots, n-1\), and \(w_0 = \dfrac{F}{2\pi T}\,S_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = \displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{i + \frac12}\).
  3. For every \(n\) we have \(\tfrac12 < S_{2\mathrm{n}} - S_{\mathrm{n}} < 1\). Therefore \(S_{2^\mathrm{k}} > S_1 + k/2\) for \(k \ge 1\) and \(w_0\) grows beyond every bound as \(n \to \infty\).
Proof

We must show that the energy of a ring has the form of a spring, that the forces of the rings are equal, and that the sum of differences at a doubling stays between \(\tfrac12\) and \(1\).

  1. On a ring \(w\) is a linear function of \(r\), so \(|\nabla w| = |w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1}|/h\) is constant. The area of the ring is \(\pi (r_{\mathrm{i}+1}^2 - r_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = \pi h^2\,(2i + 1)\). The energy of the ring is \(\tfrac12 T\,(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1})^2 h^{-2}\cdot\pi h^2\,(2i + 1) = \tfrac12\,g_{\mathrm{i}}\,(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1})^2\).
  2. The energy of the mesh is \(\Pi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n-1}\tfrac12\,g_{\mathrm{i}}\,(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1})^2 - F w_0\), a quadratic function of \(w_0, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) with a positive definite matrix, because all \(g_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), so its stationary point is a minimum (theorem 6.34). The derivative with respect to \(w_0\) gives \(g_0 (w_0 - w_1) - F = 0\), and with respect to \(w_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(1 \le j \le n-1\) it gives \(g_{\mathrm{j}} (w_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}+1}) - g_{\mathrm{j}-1} (w_{\mathrm{j}-1} - w_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\). So all forces \(g_{\mathrm{i}} (w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1})\) are equal and by the first equation equal to \(F\), as in a chain of springs through which the same force passes.
  3. From the differences of nodes \(w_{\mathrm{i}} - w_{\mathrm{i}+1} = F/g_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) we get \(w_0 = F \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n-1} 1/g_{\mathrm{i}} = \dfrac{F}{2\pi T}\,S_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  4. The difference \(S_{2\mathrm{n}} - S_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=\mathrm{n}}^{2n-1} 1/(i + \tfrac12)\) has \(n\) terms, each at most \(1/(n + \tfrac12)\) and larger than \(1/(2n)\), so the difference lies between \(n/(2n) = \tfrac12\) and \(n/(n + \tfrac12) < 1\).
  5. From step 4, after \(k \ge 1\) doublings \(S_{2^\mathrm{k}} > S_1 + k/2\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. The result explains why COOK (PDF p. 138), for a stress singularity, states that "repeated mesh refinement never achieves infinite stress".

What do the numbers show? The values under the force, \(2\pi T\,w_0/F = S_{\mathrm{n}}\), are \(3{,}35238\), \(4{,}04360\), \(4{,}73626\), \(5{,}42929\), \(6{,}12240\), \(6{,}81554\) for \(n = 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\) (NumPy, num-03.py). We draw them together with the profiles.

First the radius axis \(r\) and the deflection axis \(w\): \(r\) from the centre to the edge \(R\), \(w\) downward. At the centre the force \(F\) acts, we draw it downward from the point where it acts. Then the mesh with \(4\) rings: five nodes with segments between them, like springs in a row. Then the mesh with \(8\) rings and with \(16\). For \(r > 0\) the curves almost overlap and follow the exact solution \(w = F\ln(R/r)/(2\pi T)\). It follows from equilibrium: for a circle of radius \(r\) the force \(F\) at the centre must balance the force on the circumference, \(2\pi r\,T\,|w'| = F\) (free-body diagram, Newton), whence \(|w'| = F/(2\pi T r)\). Under the force \(w\) is larger on every mesh: for \(n = 16\) the first node is at \(4{,}74\), for \(n = 4\) at \(3{,}35\).

The right part has the axes with \(n\) horizontally, every doubling the same length, and \(2\pi T\,w/F\) vertically. On them is the value under the force against \(n\) (orange). The points lie on a line: every doubling adds an increment \(S_{2\mathrm{n}} - S_{\mathrm{n}}\), which for \(n = 4, 8, 16, 32\) equals \(0{,}69122\), \(0{,}69266\), \(0{,}69303\), \(0{,}69312\), so it approaches \(\ln 2 = 0{,}69315\) (the increment is the sum of the values of \(1/x\) at the midpoints of the \(n\) unit steps between \(n\) and \(2n\), so it approximates the area under \(1/x\) between \(n\) and \(2n\), which is \(\ln 2\)). The quotient of differences of lemma 17.12 on the triples \(n = 4, 8, 16\), \(8, 16, 32\) and \(16, 32, 64\) equals \(0{,}99792\), \(0{,}99947\), \(0{,}99987\), so it tends to \(1 = 2^0\): \(q = 0\), the differences do not fall. Formula (17.9) with \(q' = 1\) gives from the pairs \(n = 4, 8\), \(8, 16\), \(16, 32\) and \(32, 64\) the numbers \(4{,}7348\), \(5{,}4289\), \(6{,}1223\) and \(6{,}8155\): finite, each larger by about \(\ln 2\), because they simply predict the value of the mesh that follows. These numbers mean nothing, because under the force there is no value. With \(q' = 2\) we get \(4{,}2740\), \(4{,}9671\), \(5{,}6603\), \(6{,}3534\), again a sequence without a limit. The same model at a place where the exact value exists behaves calmly. At the radius \(R/2\) we have \(w = F \ln 2/(2\pi T)\), so in units of \(F/(2\pi T)\) the value is \(\ln 2 = 0{,}693147\). The finite-element values, \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=\mathrm{n}/2}^{n-1} 1/(i + \tfrac12)\), are \(0{,}685714\), \(0{,}691220\), \(0{,}692661\), \(0{,}693025\), \(0{,}693117\) for \(n = 4, \dots, 64\), with errors \(-7{,}4 \cdot 10^{-3}\), \(-1{,}9 \cdot 10^{-3}\), \(-4{,}9 \cdot 10^{-4}\), \(-1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-4}\), \(-3{,}1 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and observed \(q = 1{,}934\), \(1{,}982\), \(1{,}995\) against \(q = 2\). The same model, two quantities: for one the order is \(2\), for the other there is none.

For stresses at sharp corners the meaning is the same: when the quantity in the model is infinite, the mesh does not return a limit but an ever larger number (COOK, PDF p. 138). We look for the cause in the model, not in the mesh, and so the check whether the quantity exists, before extrapolation, is part of the analysis plan.

A wrong order on a pipe. For the pipe of chapter 11 (\(a = 40\), \(b = 60\) mm, oil \(30\) MPa, plane strain) the hoop stress on the inner surface is not given by the displacement but by a derivative that we compute from the elements (the procedure is in section 17.4). The exact value is \(78{,}00\) MPa. The estimate from the stress at the Gauss points of the first element for \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\) linear elements (c17.out) is \(84{,}39\), \(83{,}96\), \(81{,}92\), \(80{,}23\), \(79{,}19\), \(78{,}61\) MPa. We draw them as at the start of the section, as points on the stress axis (figure 17.24, part (c)): the values of the meshes approach the exact value from one side, and the differences of neighbouring meshes are \(2{,}04\), \(1{,}69\), \(1{,}04\) and \(0{,}58\) MPa. The quotients of neighbouring differences, \(1{,}21\), \(1{,}62\) and \(1{,}81\), are not constant, as they were for the eigenvalue in part (a). Lemma 17.12 gives on the triples \(n = 2, 4, 8\), \(4, 8, 16\) and \(8, 16, 32\) the order \(0{,}281\), \(0{,}695\) and \(0{,}857\): the order is not yet settled and approaches \(1\). As long as two consecutive triples do not give about the same number, the claim about the order is not proved, and the rule is to take one more mesh. With \(q = 1\) from \(n = 8, 16\) we get \(78{,}147\) MPa (error \(0{,}147\)), with \(q = 1\) from \(16, 32\) we get \(78{,}038\) MPa, and with a wrong \(q' = 2\) from \(8, 16\) we get \(78{,}841\) MPa (error \(0{,}841\)). The error of the mesh \(n = 16\) is \(1{,}19\) MPa, so the wrong \(q'\) removes only \(0{,}35\) MPa (a factor \(0{,}71\) against the predicted \(\gamma = 2/3\) from theorem 17.14), and the right \(q\) removes \(1{,}04\) MPa.

17.3.8 Summary

Before extrapolation we check four things: that the quantity exists in the model (the ring example), that it is the same at the same place relative to the element (the example of the point that is not a node), that we have at least three nested meshes and that the observed order \(\log_2(d_1/d_2)\) is settled and agrees with the order that the degree of the polynomial predicts (the pipe example and the table of eigenvalues). Only then does \(\hat Q\) of equation (17.9) hold, and we take the difference \(|\hat Q - Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}|\) as an estimate of the error of the finer mesh. An error of order \(q + 1\) and an estimate of the error do not reduce the modelling error and the numerical error: the sections that follow give them second place.
