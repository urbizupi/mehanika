Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.7 Internal damping and instability

In sections 24.1 and 24.2 the shaft was free of losses. The damping \(c\) in the equation of the response to unbalance (theorem 24.7) acted on the disk centre from the surroundings, as air drag or as friction in the bearings. A real shaft, however, loses energy by itself. The shaft material, friction in the bolted joints between its parts and a laminated build of the rotor (a rotor stacked from thin sheets) turn part of the work into heat at every contraction and stretch (FPGL, PDF p. 336). A fibre of the shaft that bends and straightens again as the shaft turns goes through one cycle of loading and unloading. For a steady deflection of a shaft that turns with angular speed \(\Omega\) this is one cycle per revolution, so \(\Omega/(2\pi)\) cycles per second, and for a whirl (definition 24.3) of frequency \(\omega\) the motion of the fibre relative to the shaft is slower or faster by \(\omega\).

We draw the bent shaft with a fibre: the upper fibre is stretched, the lower one compressed, and when the shaft turns through half a revolution they swap. So the fibre is stretched once and compressed once in one cycle. Stress against strain of the fibre traces a loop, the hysteresis loop, because strain lags behind stress. The area of the loop is the work lost to heat in one cycle.

We want to know what this loss does to the rotor. The thought that damping always calms the motion is wrong here: above the first critical speed (definition 24.8) internal damping can drive the motion. The section settles the question on the rotor of section 24.1, in the example R1 (steel shaft of diameter 40 mm, bearings 1 m apart, a steel disk 400 × 50 mm at midspan, \(m = 49{,}009\) kg, \(k = 1{,}1822\cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 155{,}32\) 1/s, 1483 rev/min). Because a treatment in fixed axes would hide the nature of internal damping, we first write the motion in axes that turn with the shaft.

From history

Steam turbines gave engineers several vibration troubles, among them the whirl of a shaft with a disk. The influence of hysteresis on the whirl of a shaft was treated by A. L. Kimball (TIMO, PDF p. 358). Hysteresis (Greek hysteresis, lagging behind) is the lag of strain behind stress: in every cycle of loading and unloading part of the work is lost in the material. Hysteresis in a shaft is therefore one source of what we call internal damping (FPGL, PDF p. 336 lists material damping among the sources).

Kimball's question is ours: what does internal damping do to the whirl. We already know that by theorem 24.7 the disk moves on a circle under unbalance and that this solution is always possible by itself. Now we ask whether such a solution is also stable.

The model is the Jeffcott rotor (definition 24.1) with the following named idealizations. The shaft is massless and round (isotropic), the bearings are rigid and short, the angular speed \(\Omega\) is constant and prescribed, the displacements are small so that the equations are linear. The internal damping is viscous, that is proportional to velocity. FPGL stresses that modelling internal damping is difficult and that the viscous model is taken as an illustration (PDF p. 336). A different law can give a different threshold or none, so theorem 24.43 holds only for the viscous law.

We draw the disk on the shaft between the bearings, turning with angular speed \(\Omega\). The internal damping \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a damper in the shaft that turns with it, the external \(c_{\mathrm{e}}\) a damper between the disk and the fixed surroundings.

24.7.1 Axes that turn with the shaft

The internal force of the shaft depends on how the shaft deforms, that is on the displacement of the disk centre relative to the shaft material. The material turns. So we need coordinates in which the shaft is at rest.

Figure 24.81 builds them. First we draw the fixed axes \(v\) and \(w\) in the plane of the disk, as in section 24.1. In this plane lies the disk centre \(P\), whose position we write with the complex number \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) (definition 9.24, see also D2 in the introduction of the chapter on notation), and the arrow \(p\) runs from the bearing axis \(O\) to \(P\). Then on the cross-section of the shaft we draw the axes \(\xi\) and \(\eta\): they are fixed to the shaft and turn with it. The shaft turns with constant angular speed \(\Omega\), so at time \(t\) the axes are rotated by the angle \(\Omega t\) relative to \(v\) and \(w\). Finally from the point \(P\) we drop perpendiculars onto the turning axes: their feet are \(\xi\) and \(\eta\), the coordinates of the disk centre in the axes that turn with the shaft. We write \(q = \xi + \mathrm{i}\,\eta\).

The first question is what the kinetic energy of the disk is if we take \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) as coordinates. By theorem 8.97 the kinetic energy in these coordinates has three parts, \(T_2 + T_1 + T_0\), of second, first and zeroth degree in the velocities, and chapter 8 promised that turning systems would show transport and Coriolis forces. Here the promise is kept.

Lemma 24.39 The Jeffcott rotor in rotating axes (Jeffcottov rotor v vrtečih se oseh)

The Jeffcott rotor without damping has a disk of mass \(m\) and a shaft of stiffness \(k\) (definition 24.1) and turns with constant angular speed \(\Omega\). Let \(q = \xi + \mathrm{i}\,\eta\) be the coordinate of the disk centre in the axes that turn with the shaft (figure 24.81). Then \(p = q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), the kinetic energy of the disk centre is \(T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0\) with

\[T_2 = \tfrac12 m(\dot\xi^2 + \dot\eta^2) , \quad T_1 = m\Omega(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi) , \quad T_0 = \tfrac12 m\Omega^2(\xi^2 + \eta^2) , \tag{24.38}\]

and the equations of motion are

\[\begin{aligned} m\ddot\xi - 2m\Omega\,\dot\eta + (k - m\Omega^2)\,\xi &= 0 , \\ m\ddot\eta + 2m\Omega\,\dot\xi + (k - m\Omega^2)\,\eta &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{24.39}\]

Written together as one complex equation (the first equation plus the second multiplied by \(\mathrm{i}\)) they give

\[m\ddot q + 2\mathrm{i}\,m\Omega\,\dot q + (k - m\Omega^2)\,q = 0 . \tag{24.40}\]

Substituting \(q = p\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) returns \(m\ddot p + k p = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the kinetic energy and the Lagrange equations in the coordinates \(\xi\), \(\eta\) give the stated equations and that they agree with the equation in the fixed axes.

  1. A point with coordinates \((\xi, \eta)\) in the axes rotated by \(\Omega t\) has in the fixed axes \(v = \xi\cos\Omega t - \eta\sin\Omega t\) and \(w = \xi\sin\Omega t + \eta\cos\Omega t\) (figure 24.81, projections on \(v\) and \(w\)). These are the real and imaginary parts of the product \((\xi + \mathrm{i}\,\eta)(\cos\Omega t + \mathrm{i}\sin\Omega t)\), so \(p = q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) by definition 9.33.
  2. By the product rule and theorem 9.34 (point 3), \(\dot p = (\dot q + \mathrm{i}\Omega\,q)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\). Since a turn through the angle \(\Omega t\) does not change a length, that is \(|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}| = 1\) (point 2 of the same theorem), \(|\dot p|^2 = |\dot q + \mathrm{i}\Omega\,q|^2 = (\dot\xi - \Omega\eta)^2 + (\dot\eta + \Omega\xi)^2\), because \(\dot q + \mathrm{i}\Omega q = (\dot\xi - \Omega\eta) + \mathrm{i}(\dot\eta + \Omega\xi)\).
  3. Besides the motion of its centre the disk only turns about its own axis, and its contribution \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2\) by theorem 8.92 does not depend on \(\xi\), \(\eta\) and does not appear in the Lagrange equations (the derivative of a constant is zero). So \(T = \tfrac12 m|\dot p|^2\). We expand both squares of step 2: \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot\xi^2 + \dot\eta^2) + m\Omega(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi) + \tfrac12 m\Omega^2(\xi^2 + \eta^2)\). The term with two velocities is \(T_2\), with one \(T_1\), with none \(T_0\), which is (24.38).
  4. The potential energy of the shaft is \(V = \tfrac12 k|p|^2 = \tfrac12 k(\xi^2 + \eta^2)\), because \(|p| = |q|\) (step 2, \(|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}| = 1\)) and the shaft is round: the energy depends only on the size of the deflection, not on its direction.
  5. For \(\xi\) by theorem 8.101 with \(\mathcal L = T - V\): \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\xi = m\dot\xi - m\Omega\eta\), its time derivative is \(m\ddot\xi - m\Omega\dot\eta\), and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\xi = m\Omega\dot\eta + m\Omega^2\xi - k\xi\). The difference is \(m\ddot\xi - 2m\Omega\dot\eta - m\Omega^2\xi + k\xi = 0\), the first equation of (24.39). For \(\eta\): \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\eta = m\dot\eta + m\Omega\xi\), derivative \(m\ddot\eta + m\Omega\dot\xi\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\eta = -m\Omega\dot\xi + m\Omega^2\eta - k\eta\), and the difference is the second equation.
  6. The first equation plus \(\mathrm{i}\) times the second: the terms with \(\Omega\dot{}\) are \(2m\Omega(-\dot\eta + \mathrm{i}\dot\xi) = 2\mathrm{i}\,m\Omega(\dot\xi + \mathrm{i}\dot\eta) = 2\mathrm{i}\,m\Omega\,\dot q\), which gives (24.40).
  7. From \(q = p\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) it follows that \(\dot q = (\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) and \(\ddot q = (\ddot p - 2\mathrm{i}\Omega\dot p - \Omega^2 p)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) (point 3 of theorem 9.34 twice). We substitute into (24.40) and divide by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\): \(m\ddot p - 2\mathrm{i}m\Omega\dot p - m\Omega^2 p + 2\mathrm{i}m\Omega\dot p + 2m\Omega^2 p + kp - m\Omega^2 p = m\ddot p + kp\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. The split \(T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0\) into transport and complementary (Coriolis or gyroscopic) forces: GR (1.36)-(1.40) (PDF pp. 46 to 47). Step 7 and SymPy checked that the equation in the rotating axes agrees with equation (24.40) without damping in the fixed axes.

The lemma says what the turning of the axes brings, and each term has its cause.

We draw the axes \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) that turn with the shaft and in them the disk centre with its velocity. The shaft pulls it toward the axis with the elastic force, the turning of the axes pushes it away with the centrifugal force and sideways with the Coriolis force, perpendicular to the velocity.

  • The term \(-m\Omega^2\xi\) is a transport force (it comes from \(T_0\)): in turning axes the disk needs, to move on a circle, an acceleration toward the centre of \(\Omega^2\) per unit of radius, and since an observer in these axes turns together with the disk, he feels his acceleration toward the centre as a force \(m\Omega^2 q\) away from the centre. We call it the centrifugal force (Latin centrum and fugere, to flee from the centre). It reduces the effective stiffness from \(k\) to \(k - m\Omega^2\).
  • The terms \(\mp 2m\Omega\dot\eta\) and \(\pm 2m\Omega\dot\xi\) (the term \(T_1\)) are a force proportional to velocity and turned by a right angle relative to it. We call it the Coriolis force (after Coriolis) or the gyroscopic force. Because the coefficients are skew-symmetric (\(+2m\Omega\) and \(-2m\Omega\)), the power is \(\dot\xi\cdot 2m\Omega\dot\eta - \dot\eta\cdot 2m\Omega\dot\xi = 0\): the force does no work. The gyroscopic matrix of the disk from section 24.3 (lemma 24.19) has the same structural property, only here the cause is the turning of the coordinates, not the tilt of the disk.

The first consequence is the same as for the critical speed. At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) we have \(k - m\Omega^2 = 0\) and equations (24.39) allow rest in the turning axes at every \(q \neq 0\): the disk centre turns with the shaft at exactly the same angular speed, on a circular orbit. In a synchronous forward whirl \(q\) is constant, because the orbit turns together with the axes. This is the speed at which, in the turning axes, the balance between the elastic and the centrifugal force holds.

24.7.2 Internal and external damping

When then does internal damping act? The shaft loses energy when its fibre deforms, that is when the disk centre moves relative to the turning axes.

Definition 24.40 Internal and external damping (notranje in zunanje dušenje)

Let the Jeffcott rotor be as in lemma 24.39. Internal damping is a force of the shaft on the disk centre proportional to the velocity of the centre relative to the turning axes: in components of the turning axes \(F_\xi = -c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot\xi\), \(F_\eta = -c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot\eta\), in complex form \(F^{\mathrm{vrt}}_{\mathrm{i}} = -c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q\), with a coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) in N s/m. External damping is a force of the surroundings (bearings, air) on the disk centre proportional to the velocity of the centre relative to the fixed axes: \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = -c_{\mathrm{e}}\dot p\) with \(c_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\). We write both coefficients with the damping ratios (definition 9.5) \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = c_{\mathrm{e}}/(2m\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2m\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), where \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\).

Source: FPGL §7.5 (PDF pp. 336 to 337): damping in the rotor (\(c_{\mathrm{i}}\)) and damping outside the rotor (\(c_{\mathrm{e}}\), in bearings and foundation, also air drag), (7.58) for the force \(-c_{\mathrm{i}}\) times the velocity in the turning axes. The name is by where the force acts: internal acts inside the turning part, external between it and the surroundings.

What does the condition "relative to the turning axes" mean in the fixed axes in which we shall compute? By step 2 of lemma 24.39 the velocity of the centre relative to the turning axes is \(\dot q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = \dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p\): from the velocity \(\dot p\) of the centre we subtract the velocity \(\mathrm{i}\Omega p\) that a point fixed to the shaft at the same place would have (the vector \(p\) rotated forward by a right angle and multiplied by \(\Omega\)).

Lemma 24.41 Equation of the rotor with internal and external damping (enačba rotorja z notranjim in zunanjim dušenjem)

The Jeffcott rotor with the dampings of definition 24.40 and angular speed \(\Omega\) has in the fixed axes the equation of motion

\[m\ddot p + (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\,\dot p - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\,p + k p = 0 . \tag{24.41}\]

Written out in the components \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) these are the equations

\[\begin{aligned} m\ddot v + (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\dot v + \Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\,w + k v &= 0 , \\ m\ddot w + (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\dot w - \Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\,v + k w &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{24.42}\]
Proof

We must show that the force of internal damping in the fixed axes is \(-c_{\mathrm{i}}(\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p)\) and that it is to be added to the right-hand side of the equation without damping.

  1. Without damping the Lagrange equations in the coordinates \(v\), \(w\) with \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2)\) and \(V = \tfrac12 k(v^2 + w^2)\) give by theorem 8.101 \(m\ddot v + kv = Q_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(m\ddot w + kw = Q_{\mathrm{w}}\). For a virtual displacement \((\delta v, \delta w)\) of the disk centre the work of a force \((F_{\mathrm{v}}, F_{\mathrm{w}})\) is \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\delta v + F_{\mathrm{w}}\delta w\), so \(Q_{\mathrm{v}} = F_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{w}} = F_{\mathrm{w}}\). With \(F = F_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}F_{\mathrm{w}}\) we have \(m\ddot p + kp = F\).
  2. The components of a vector in the turning and in the fixed axes are related like the coordinates of a point (step 1 of lemma 24.39): \(F_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}F_{\mathrm{w}} = (F_\xi + \mathrm{i}F_\eta)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\). For the internal force therefore \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = -c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), and by step 2 of the lemma \(\dot q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = \dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p\). So \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = -c_{\mathrm{i}}(\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p)\) (SymPy confirms the same).
  3. The external force is \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = -c_{\mathrm{e}}\dot p\) by definition. We insert \(F = F_{\mathrm{e}} + F_{\mathrm{i}}\) in step 1 and move to the left: \(m\ddot p + c_{\mathrm{e}}\dot p + c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} p + kp = 0\), which is (24.41).
  4. We split into real and imaginary parts: \(-\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}(v + \mathrm{i}w) = \Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} w - \mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} v\), so the real part brings \(+\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} w\) and the imaginary part \(-\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} v\), which is (24.42).

Source: FPGL §7.5 (PDF pp. 336 to 337), (7.58) to (7.62): the same equation written with \(u\), \(v\) and in matrix form. The proof by steps is not from the source.

Let us read equations (24.42). Besides the usual damping \(c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}}\) they have a term \(\pm\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\): a force on the disk that is proportional to the displacement but perpendicular to it. It has the structural property of a stiffness matrix with a skew-symmetric part, and this part grows with \(\Omega\) (FPGL, PDF p. 348). The skew-symmetric part of the stiffness is the one that does not conserve energy: a force perpendicular to the displacement that accompanies it can supply work to the disk instead of taking it. Let us see what this means for the orbit.

Figure 24.83 shows it for a disk on a circular orbit, whirled forward with frequency \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). We draw the orbit around the axis \(O\) and on it the disk centre \(P\). On \(P\) act first the elastic force of the shaft \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\), directed toward the axis, of size \(k|p|\). Then the external force \(F_{\mathrm{e}}\), always opposite to the velocity, that is against the direction of the whirl, so we draw it tangentially backward. The internal force \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = -c_{\mathrm{i}}(\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p)\) depends on the difference of two velocities, which we draw below: \(\dot p\) of the disk centre and \(\mathrm{i}\Omega p\) of the point of the shaft at the same place. At \(\Omega = \omega\) (left) they are equal, the relative velocity is zero and there is no internal force: the centre turns with the shaft as a whole, the fibre does not change its deformation and no energy is dissipated. At \(\Omega = 4\omega\) (right) the shaft runs ahead of the whirl, the point of the shaft at the same place moves four times faster, the relative velocity \(\dot p - \mathrm{i}\Omega p\) points backward, and the force \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) therefore points forward, in the direction of the whirl. The shaft thus drags the disk forward by friction and above the whirl frequency drives it instead of braking it.

A computation confirms this. On a circular orbit \(p = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) we have \(\dot p = \mathrm{i}\omega p\). The external force is \(-c_{\mathrm{e}}\mathrm{i}\omega p\), along the velocity in the opposite sense, the internal one \(-c_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{i}(\omega - \Omega)p = c_{\mathrm{i}}(\Omega - \omega)\,\mathrm{i}p\), along the velocity (\(\mathrm{i}p\) points forward) if \(\Omega > \omega\). The power of these forces is the scalar product of force and velocity. The sum of the damping forces is \(-c_{\mathrm{e}}\dot p - c_{\mathrm{i}}\dot p + \mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} p = \big(c_{\mathrm{i}}\Omega - (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\omega\big)\,\mathrm{i}p\). So it lies on the tangent and has in the forward direction, the direction of \(\mathrm{i}p\) of length \(|p|\), the component \(\big(c_{\mathrm{i}}\Omega - (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\omega\big)|p|\). The velocity \(\dot p = \mathrm{i}\omega p\) points the same way with size \(\omega|p|\), and the elastic force \(-kp\) is perpendicular to it and does no work. So the power is

\[\mathcal P = \big(c_{\mathrm{i}}\Omega - (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})\,\omega\big)\,\omega\,|p|^2 . \tag{24.43}\]

It is positive at \(\Omega > \omega(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}})\). The orbit gains more energy than it loses and its amplitude grows. The energy does not come from nothing: the drive that holds \(\Omega\) constant (which is why we took \(\Omega\) as prescribed) must turn the shaft against the friction of the fibre, and through the force \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) on figure 24.83 on the right (pointing forward) that work passes into the whirl. At \(\omega \approx \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the threshold is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}})\). So from the law of work (power is force times velocity) we arrived at a number that the theorem below proves from the roots.

One consequence holds at once and links this section with section 24.2.

Corollary 24.42 Internal damping does not affect the response to unbalance (notranje dušenje ne vpliva na odziv na neuravnoteženost)

Let the rotor of lemma 24.41 also have the unbalance force \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) on the right (definition 24.6). If \(c_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) or \(\Omega \ne \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), one solution is the circular orbit \(p = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), with an amplitude that depends only on the external damping:

\[P = \frac{m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2}{k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c_{\mathrm{e}}\Omega} . \tag{24.44}\]

This is the response of theorem 24.7 with \(c = c_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Proof

We must show that \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) drops out of the equation.

  1. We insert \(p = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) into (24.41) with the right-hand side \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\). Since \(\dot p = \mathrm{i}\Omega p\) and \(\ddot p = -\Omega^2 p\), we get \(\big(-m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\Omega(c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}}) - \mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} + k\big)P = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\).
  2. The terms \(\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(-\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\) cancel, and \((k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}c_{\mathrm{e}}\Omega)P = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) remains, which is (24.44).

Source: The proof is not from the source. The same reason as for figure 24.83 on the left: at \(\omega = \Omega\) there is no internal force.

For R1 at \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\) we have \(|p| = 4{,}1886\), \(25{,}0000\) and \(5{,}6394\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) at \(\Omega = 0{,}9\), \(1\) and \(1{,}1\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), and we obtain the same numbers if we set \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) to zero (computation in num-07.py). The response of section 24.2 thus stays as it was. The consequence does not say, however, that the orbit is stable: this solution makes sense only if a disturbance around it does not grow, and that is decided by the theorem in the next part.

24.7.3 The stability threshold

From the law of work we guessed for the threshold \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}})\). The theorem proves it without the approximation \(\omega \approx \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), with the zeros of the characteristic polynomial of equation (24.41).

The zeros lie in the complex plane. We draw the real and imaginary axes, the left half-plane where solutions decay and the right one where they grow. The zero \(s_-\) stays on the left at every \(\Omega\). The zero \(s_+\) moves to the right as \(\Omega\) grows and crosses the imaginary axis at \(\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) when \(\Omega = \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\).

Theorem 24.43 Stability threshold of the Jeffcott rotor with internal damping (meja stabilnosti Jeffcottovega rotorja z notranjim dušenjem)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) and let \(\Omega\) be real. Let \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and the threshold

\[\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Big(1 + \frac{c_{\mathrm{e}}}{c_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) . \tag{24.45}\]

The characteristic polynomial of equation (24.41) is \(\chi(s) = m s^2 + (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})s + k - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\). Its zeros are \(s_\pm = \big(-(c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}}) \pm \sqrt\Delta\big)/(2m)\), where \(\Delta = (c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}})^2 - 4m(k - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(\sqrt\Delta\) is the complex root with nonnegative real part (for real \(\Delta < 0\), which happens only at \(\Omega = 0\), the one with positive imaginary part). Then \(\operatorname{Re}s_- < 0\) at every \(\Omega\) and:

  1. The equilibrium \(p = 0\) is asymptotically stable (definition 9.52 for the coordinates \(v\), \(w\)) if and only if \(|\Omega| < \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. For \(|\Omega| > \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}s_+ > 0\) and the equilibrium is not stable (definition 8.130).
  3. At \(\Omega = \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(s_+ = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\): the solution \(\varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) neither decays nor grows. For \(\Omega > 0\) the zero \(s_+\) has a positive imaginary part, which is a forward whirl (definition 24.3), and \(s_-\) a negative one, a backward whirl.
Proof

We must show that the sign of \(\operatorname{Re}s_+\) is decided by the comparison of \(|\Omega|\) and \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\). First we reduce stability to the zeros through the characteristic polynomial, then we compute them.

  1. Equation (24.41) is a linear equation of order 2 with constant complex coefficients for a complex function \(p\). By theorem 9.44 all solutions have the form \(p = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} p_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) the zeros of the polynomial \(\chi\), \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) polynomials of degree less than the multiplicity of the zero, and by the choice of coefficients we reach any initial values. If both zeros have a negative real part, every term \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\) tends to zero, and so does its derivative, so \(p\) and \(\dot p\) tend to zero for every start. The coefficients of the polynomials \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) are linear functions of the initial values \(p(0)\) and \(\dot p(0)\) (the solution of a system of two linear equations from theorem 9.44). Since every term decays and is therefore bounded, there is a number \(C\) with \(|p(t)| + |\dot p(t)| \le C\,(|p(0)| + |\dot p(0)|)\) for all \(t \ge 0\). A small start therefore stays small, the equilibrium is stable (definition 8.130) and, with the limit zero, asymptotically stable. If \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) for some zero, then \(p = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\) is a solution (since \(\chi(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}) = 0\)) with \(|p(0)| = \varepsilon\) and \(|p(t)| = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{(\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}) t}\), which at \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) does not tend to zero and at \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) grows without bound for arbitrarily small \(\varepsilon\). The equilibrium is then not asymptotically stable and at \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) not even stable.
  2. We write \(c = c_{\mathrm{e}} + c_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), \(A = c^2 - 4mk\) and \(B = 4m\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}\). The zeros are \(s_\pm = (-c \pm \sqrt\Delta)/(2m)\) with \(\Delta = A + \mathrm{i}B\), which is the \(\Delta\) of the theorem. We write the root of the theorem as \(\sqrt\Delta = a + \mathrm{i}b\) with \(a \ge 0\). From \(a^2 - b^2 = A\) and \(2ab = B\) we get \(b^2 = a^2 - A\) and \(4a^2(a^2 - A) = B^2\), so \(x = a^2\) is a zero of the polynomial \(x^2 - Ax - B^2/4\) with \(x \ge 0\) and \(x \ge A\) (because \(b^2 = x - A \ge 0\)). The zeros of this polynomial are \(\big(A \pm \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\big)/2\). The zero with the minus is \(\ge 0\) only if \(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} \le A\), and \(\ge A\) only if \(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} \le -A\). Both hold together only at \(A = B = 0\), where the two zeros are equal. Hence \(a^2 = \big(A + \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\big)/2\).
  3. The real parts of the zeros are \(\operatorname{Re}s_\pm = (-c \pm a)/(2m)\). The zero with the minus has \(\operatorname{Re}s_- = -(c + a)/(2m) < 0\) at every \(\Omega\), because \(c > 0\) and \(a \ge 0\). For the zero with the plus: \(\operatorname{Re}s_+ < 0\) exactly when \(a < c\), and since \(c > 0\) and \(a \ge 0\) this is the same as \(a^2 < c^2\), that is \(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} < 2c^2 - A = c^2 + 4mk\). Both sides are positive, so this is the same as \(A^2 + B^2 < (c^2 + 4mk)^2\), that is \(B^2 < (c^2 + 4mk)^2 - (c^2 - 4mk)^2 = 16\,m k c^2\). With \(B^2 = 16 m^2\Omega^2 c_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) this is \(\Omega^2 < (k/m)(c/c_{\mathrm{i}})^2\), so \(|\Omega| < \omega_{\mathrm{n}} c/c_{\mathrm{i}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}}) = \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\). The same steps with \(=\) and \(>\) instead of \(<\) give \(\operatorname{Re}s_+ = 0\) at \(|\Omega| = \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\operatorname{Re}s_+ > 0\) at \(|\Omega| > \Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\). On figure 24.86 the threshold is where the curve crosses zero (case \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\)).
  4. Points 1 and 2 of the theorem follow from steps 1 and 3.
  5. At \(\Omega = \Omega_{\mathrm{s}} > 0\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}s_+ = 0\), so \(s_+ = \mathrm{i}\sigma\) with real \(\sigma\). We insert into \(\chi(s) = 0\): the real part \(-m\sigma^2 + k = 0\) and the imaginary part \(c\sigma - \Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). The first gives \(\sigma = \pm\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), the second \(\sigma = \Omega c_{\mathrm{i}}/c = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\). So \(s_+ = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the solution \(\varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) of step 1 has constant \(|p| = \varepsilon\).
  6. At \(\Omega > 0\) we have \(B > 0\), so \(a > 0\) (if \(a = 0\) then \(B = 2ab = 0\)) and \(b = B/(2a) > 0\). The imaginary parts of the zeros are \(\pm b/(2m)\), so \(\operatorname{Im}s_+ > 0\) and \(\operatorname{Im}s_- < 0\). A solution \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) with \(\operatorname{Im}s > 0\) is \(p = \mathrm{e}^{(\operatorname{Re}s) t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\operatorname{Im}s) t}\), which turns in the same direction as the turning of the shaft (forward, definition 24.3), because \(\Omega > 0\).

Source: FPGL (7.72) and (7.73) (PDF p. 338): \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} R = \zeta_{\mathrm{e}} + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and stability for \(\Omega < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + \zeta_{\mathrm{e}}/\zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\), which is the same, because \(c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}} = \zeta_{\mathrm{e}}/\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\). FPGL proves it with the Routh-Hurwitz criterion or from the zeros, here we name it and do not use it. The proof by steps is not from the source.

Let us test the theorem on R1 with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\), so \(c_{\mathrm{e}} = 304{,}5\) N s/m and \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 152{,}2\) N s/m. The threshold is \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = 3\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 465{,}95\) 1/s, that is 4449 rev/min, three times the first critical speed. At \(0{,}99\,\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(s_+ = -0{,}0466 + 155{,}32\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s and at \(1{,}01\,\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(+0{,}0466 + 155{,}32\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s, so the real part changes sign and the zero stays at almost the same frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). A rotor just above the threshold whirls forward with frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and not \(\Omega\), so subsynchronously, and its amplitude is multiplied by \(\mathrm{e}^{0{,}0466\,t}\). Growth is this slow because 1 % above the threshold is little. Figure 24.85 shows the difference at 20 % from the threshold.

In the figure the coordinate axes \(v\) and \(w\) are common, the start is on a circle of radius 1,15 cm. At \(\Omega = 0{,}8\,\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) the orbit shrinks and in 0,8 s only 47 % of the initial amplitude remains. At \(\Omega = 1{,}2\,\Omega_{\mathrm{s}}\) the orbit widens to 211 %. In the linear model the amplitude grows without bound, as \(\mathrm{e}^{0{,}93\,t}\). What happens at large amplitudes is decided by nonlinearities that we do not treat.

The threshold of stability is determined by the ratio \(c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}}\) and not by the size of \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). Figure 24.86 shows the real part of the zero \(s_+\) as a function of \(\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\) and three external dampings.

We draw the axes: \(\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) on the horizontal and \(\operatorname{Re}s_+\) on the vertical. First the case \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\): the line starts at \(-4{,}66\) 1/s, that is \(-(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), because at \(\Omega = 0\) the rotor is not turning and both kinds of damping act as usual, and it crosses zero at the threshold at 3. The case \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}05\) starts lower (\(-9{,}32\)) and crosses zero at 6, the case \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) at 1, that is at the first critical speed. The grey dashed line is the rotor with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\) and no internal damping: its real part is \(-\zeta_{\mathrm{e}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = -3{,}106\) 1/s at every speed and never crosses zero. All three curves with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\) are almost parallel with slope \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). The reason is equation (24.43): the growth of the amplitude has the rate \(\mathcal P\) divided by the energy \(m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2|p|^2\) of the orbit, so \(2\operatorname{Re}s_+ \approx \mathcal P/(m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2|p|^2)\) and

\[\operatorname{Re}s_+ \approx \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Omega - (\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} + \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \tag{24.46}\]

(with \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\)). At \(\Omega = 4\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the approximation is \(1{,}553\) 1/s, the exact value from step 3 of the theorem \(1{,}551\) 1/s. At \(7\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) it is \(6{,}21\) against \(6{,}19\) 1/s. The approximation holds for small \(\zeta\), because we wrote the energy of the orbit as for an undamped circular orbit. The theorem is exact, the approximation explains the cause.

External damping does not remove the threshold, it only raises it, internal damping lowers it, and since \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) at every \(c_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\), the rotor is always stable below the first critical speed. For operation at \(\Omega\) we need \(c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}} > \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} - 1\).

We now test the theorem on the cases whose hypotheses it excludes.

Example 24.44 External damping only (samo zunanje dušenje)

At \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) the term \(\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} p\) in (24.41) vanishes and the equation is the ordinary damped equation of theorem 9.53 for each plane separately. The threshold of (24.45) with \(c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}} \to \infty\) goes to infinity, which agrees. For R1 with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}02\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}s = -\zeta_{\mathrm{e}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = -3{,}106\) 1/s at every \(\Omega\) (the grey dashed line in figure 24.86), at \(\Omega = 3\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) just as at \(0\). The theorem does not apply, because it assumes \(c_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\). The reason is not that external damping guarantees stability, but that there is no term that drives the motion: the force \(\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} p\) is the only source of energy in the equation.

Example 24.45 Internal damping only (samo notranje dušenje)

At \(c_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) we have \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). R1 with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}01\) is stable at \(\Omega = 0{,}975\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(\operatorname{Re}s_+ = -0{,}0388\) 1/s) and unstable at \(1{,}025\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(+0{,}0388\) 1/s). At the speed \(1{,}1\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) it is \(+0{,}155\) 1/s. The threshold is at the first critical speed: a rotor that has only internal damping is stable only below it (FPGL, PDF p. 338, the worst case). Fast growth is prevented here only by \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) being small, not by some property of the model. The example warns that "damping always helps" does not hold for a rotor that has internal damping and no external damping.

Example 24.46 Check against the source's equations (preizkus z enačbami vira)

FPGL, example 7.5.1 (PDF pp. 338 to 339), takes \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 100\) 1/s and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{e}} = \zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}1\), so the threshold is \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = 2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(R = 2\) in the source). Our equation (24.41) gives at \(R = 1{,}95\) the zeros \(-0{,}48 + 99{,}90\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-39{,}52 - 99{,}90\,\mathrm{i}\) and at \(R = 2{,}05\) the zeros \(+0{,}48 + 100{,}10\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-40{,}48 - 100{,}10\,\mathrm{i}\) (rad/s), which are the numbers of table 7.1 in the source. The zero that becomes unstable has a positive imaginary part, that is forward whirl, as the source text says (PDF p. 339).

The section showed three things. First, in rotating axes the kinetic energy splits into transport and Coriolis terms (lemma 24.39), because the coordinates turn. Second, internal damping is a force relative to the shaft and not to the bearings, so in fixed axes it gets a term \(-\mathrm{i}\Omega c_{\mathrm{i}} p\) perpendicular to the displacement (lemma 24.41), and the synchronous response of section 24.2 stays unchanged (corollary 24.42). Third, above \(\Omega_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}(1 + c_{\mathrm{e}}/c_{\mathrm{i}})\) this term drives a forward whirl with frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (theorem 24.43). What this means for design is short: above the first critical speed the rotor should have a lot of external damping in the bearings and as little internal damping as possible, which comes for example from bolted joints and laminated rotors (FPGL, PDF p. 336).

The idealizations of the section are listed once more so that the reader knows what the theorem really covers. Viscous internal damping (the theorem holds for this law). A shaft without mass and isotropic: for a shaft with mass and more complex rotors FPGL says that internal damping shows up in finite elements as a skew-symmetric part of the stiffness that grows with \(\Omega\) (§7.7, PDF pp. 348 to 349). For the overhung rotor of FPGL, example 7.7.1 (PDF p. 350), the second eigenvalue becomes unstable between 1000 and 2000 rev/min. The section does not prove this, it cites it. Hydrodynamic bearings and asymmetric rotors are only named in the chapter.
