Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.7 The thick-walled tube

A steel tube of a hydraulic system has inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm, and it holds oil at pressure \(p = 30\) MPa. This is the tube of section 11.1 (figure 11.1), made of the same steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). How do the stresses vary through the wall? Where does the steel start to yield first and at what pressure? And how good is the thin-wall formula, by which the stress in the hoop direction of the tube equals the pressure times the radius divided by the wall thickness? The wall is \(b - a = 20\) mm thick, half the inner radius, so we must not just assume that the formula holds.

From history

Lamé and Clapeyron, two engineers, wrote a memoir on the interior equilibrium of homogeneous solid bodies while in service in St. Petersburg. It was reviewed by Poinsot and Navier in 1828 and printed in 1833 (TIMO, PDF p. 123). TIMO ascribes the importance of the memoir to the fact that it contains not only a derivation of the equations of equilibrium, which Navier and Cauchy already knew, but also their application to problems of practical value (TIMO, PDF p. 123). Among them is the hollow circular cylinder under uniform internal and external pressure, for which they derive formulas for the stresses. With them they calculate the necessary wall thickness for given pressures. In doing so they use the maximum stress theory (teorija največje napetosti), but they state explicitly that each element of the cylinder is in a two-dimensional state of stress and that the elastic limit from a simple tensile test may not apply to such a case (TIMO, PDF p. 124). After that they treat the torsion of circular shafts, a sphere under gravitational forces directed toward its centre and a spherical shell under uniform internal or external pressure. For all these problems TIMO states that the formulas are correct and that they have since found many applications (TIMO, PDF p. 124). The two memoirs of Poisson that TIMO cites are from 1829 and 1831 (TIMO, PDF p. 119). In one of them Poisson treats, as a simple example of a three-dimensional problem, the stresses and deformations in a hollow sphere under internal or external uniform pressure (TIMO, PDF p. 120).

We asked how the stresses vary through the wall of a tube with radii 40 mm and 60 mm under pressure 30 MPa and how good the thin-wall formula is. History tells us that such tubes were already calculated in the time of Lamé and Clapeyron, and that they were aware that the state of stress in the wall is two-dimensional. Let us first ask how much equilibrium alone tells us about the stresses.

11.7.1 Equilibrium of half a tube

We cut the tube by a plane through the axis and consider the upper half of length one unit (figure 11.17). For now assume that the stresses do not change along the axis, which we shall show in theorem 11.27. Then the forces on the two axial sections of the piece are opposite and cancel. The oil pushes the wall of the bore outward. The forces on the curved wall point in all directions, but the horizontal components on the left and right halves of the bore are opposite, so only the vertical component remains. At the arc at angle \(\theta\) from the horizontal axis, where the normal of the bore is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), the vertical component of the force per unit area is \(p\sin\theta\). We describe the arc by the angle \(\theta\) and the length \(z\) along the axis. The surface vector has length \(a\) (section 4.3.6, equation (4.34)), so the resultant per unit length is the integral over \(\theta\) of the force per unit area times \(a\):

\[\int_0^{\pi} p\,\sin\theta\;a\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\,p\,a ,\]

which is the pressure times the width \(2a\) of the half bore. At the two sections (\(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\)) the lower part holds the half. The normal of the section there is \(\mp\vek e_\theta\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) is vertical, so the vertical component of the force per unit area has magnitude \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \vek e_\theta\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) (definition 11.19) and points downward. The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) points horizontally and does not affect vertical equilibrium. The component \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is called the hoop stress (obodna napetost) because it points along the circumference of the tube, and the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) the radial stress (radialna napetost). The section has width \(b - a\) per unit length, so vertical equilibrium at \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) gives

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = p\,a . \tag{11.32}\]

The mean hoop stress through the wall is therefore \(p\,a/(b - a)\). If the hoop stress is equal to \(\sigma\) throughout the wall, then \(\sigma\,(b - a) = p\,a\), so

\[\sigma = \frac{p\,a}{b - a} = \frac{p\,a}{t} , \qquad t = b - a . \tag{11.33}\]

This is the thin-wall formula. For our tube \(\sigma = 30 \cdot 40/20 = 60\) MPa. If we replace the radius in the numerator by the mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = (a + b)/2 = 50\) mm, we get \(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t = 75\) MPa (SADD (8.4.6), PDF p. 190). The two numbers, 60 and 75 MPa, are quite different. Which of them means what? We shall see when we know the hoop stress through the whole wall.

Equilibrium of half a tube tells us only the mean of the hoop stress through the wall and not how it is distributed. Equation (11.32) is one equation for the function \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(r)\), so infinitely many functions with the same mean satisfy it. The distribution is determined only by the link with displacements that the boundary value problem contains. We shall guess the field by the semi-inverse method, just like the field of the hanging bar in section 11.4.

11.7.2 Lamé's solution

The tube is rotationally symmetric and the load is the same on every circumference. We therefore expect each ring of the tube to expand uniformly: a point at radius \(r\) moves radially by \(u(r)\), independent of the angle \(\theta\) and of the height \(z\). Along the axis the tube may stretch or shrink uniformly, \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0\,z\), with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\) that we shall determine. The field is therefore

\[\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

This is a guess, which the following steps will check. If the field satisfies all three requirements of definition 11.1, it is a solution by theorem 11.4, and there is no other.

But what about the ends of the tube? A tube is always closed, clamped or open somewhere, and this changes the axial stress. So let us prescribe at both ends \(z = \pm l\) a uniform normal stress \(S\), that is, a force per unit area \(S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the upper and \(-S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the lower end, and see how \(S\) affects the solution. Three values matter.

  1. Open ends (odprta konca): the ends are free, \(S = 0\) (figure 11.1, left).
  2. Closed ends (zaprta konca): plugs close the ends. The oil with pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) pushes a plug of radius \(a\) with axial force \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\pi a^2\), and the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) acts on the outer surface with force \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\pi b^2\). The resultant \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\pi a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}}\pi b^2\) is by assumption spread uniformly over the annulus of the end with area \(\pi(b^2 - a^2)\) (figure 11.1, middle). The stress is then \(S = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\).
  3. Plane strain (ravninska deformacija, section 11.9): \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\), so the distance between the ends does not change, as if they were held by smooth rigid walls. The stress \(S\) is here a result that we shall compute. The walls are not part of our problem, because section 11.1 does not use smooth supports. Instead of them we prescribe this stress, and the problem of forces with it has the same solution.

The stresses at the ends of a real tube are not perfectly uniform, because a plug or a wall does not spread the force uniformly. A uniform \(S\) is an idealization. How large the differences are and how far from the end they reach we shall see at Saint-Venant's principle later in this chapter. For now let us compute the field.

Theorem 11.27 Lamé solution for a tube (Laméjeva rešitev za cev)

Let the body be the tube \(a \le r \le b\), \(-l \le z \le l\) with radii \(0 < a < b\) and half-length \(l > 0\), cut by two planes through the axis into four curved boxes, as the tube with radii 40 mm and 60 mm is cut in section 4.3.7 of chapter 4 (the same for general \(a\), \(b\) and \(l\)). Let the material be isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.48). There is no body force and no supports, so this is a problem of forces (definition 11.1). On the surface \(r = a\) let the pressure be \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) and on \(r = b\) the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\), that is, force per unit area \(-p\,\vek n\) with outward normal \(\vek n\), and at the ends \(z = \pm l\) the force \(\pm S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Let

\[A = \frac{p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2}{b^2 - a^2} , \qquad B = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^2 b^2}{b^2 - a^2} , \qquad \varepsilon_0 = \frac{S - 2\nu A}{E} . \tag{11.34}\]

Then:

  1. the displacement field
    \[\vek u = \Big(\frac{(1 - \nu)A - \nu S}{E}\,r + \frac{(1 + \nu)B}{E}\,\frac1r\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} \tag{11.35}\]
    is a solution of this problem, and its components of stress in local axes (definition 11.19) are
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - \frac{B}{r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{11.36}\]
  2. every solution of this problem has the same stresses and strains and differs from the field (11.35) by a small rigid displacement (theorem 11.4, item 2).
Proof

We must show that the field (11.35) satisfies the three requirements of definition 11.1, and then use the uniqueness theorem. We work with the guess \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with an unknown function \(u\) and a constant \(\varepsilon_0\). Hooke's law in local axes has the same form as in Cartesian ones (corollary 11.26).

1. The equation for \(u\).

  1. Strains from corollary 11.21 for \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u(r)\), \(u_\theta = 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0 z\): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0\). The other three components are zero, because in their formulas \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta\), \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r\), \(u_\theta\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial r\), \(\partial u_\theta/\partial z\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial\theta\) are all zero.
  2. Hooke's law (equation (3.13), Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) from definition 3.10) with \(\Theta = u' + u/r + \varepsilon_0\):
    \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u' + \lambda\,\frac{u}{r} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \lambda\,u' + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\frac{u}{r} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,(u' + \tfrac{u}{r}) + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_0 , \end{aligned}\]
    and the shear stresses are \(2\mu\) times the zero shear strains, hence zero.
  3. Equilibrium equations in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.24, \(\vek b = \vek 0\)). The second and third, because of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0\) and independence of \(\theta\), read \(0 = 0\) and \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z = 0\), which holds because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) does not depend on \(z\). The first is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\).
  4. Insert the expressions from step 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'' + \lambda\,u'/r - \lambda\,u/r^2\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 2\mu\,(u' - u/r)/r\). In the sum the terms \(\lambda\,u'/r\) and \(2\mu\,u'/r\) combine into \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'/r\), and the terms \(-\lambda\,u/r^2\) and \(-2\mu\,u/r^2\) into \(-(\lambda + 2\mu)\,u/r^2\), so
    \[(\lambda + 2\mu)\,\Big(u'' + \frac{u'}{r} - \frac{u}{r^2}\Big) = 0 .\]
    The factor \(\lambda + 2\mu = E\,(1 - \nu)/((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu))\) is positive for \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), so \(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2 = 0\).
  5. By the product rule \(((1/r)(r\,u)')' = (u/r + u')' = u'/r - u/r^2 + u''\), so the equation of step 4 is \(((1/r)(r\,u)')' = 0\). By corollary 1.85 \((1/r)(r\,u)' = 2C_1\) with a constant \(C_1\), so \((r\,u - C_1 r^2)' = 0\) and again by this corollary \(r\,u - C_1 r^2 = C_2\) with a constant \(C_2\). The solution is
    \[u = C_1\,r + \frac{C_2}{r} .\]

2. Stresses and boundary conditions.

  1. In the expressions of step 2, \(u' = C_1 - C_2/r^2\) and \(u/r = C_1 + C_2/r^2\). Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - B/r^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) with \(A = 2(\lambda + \mu)\,C_1 + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0\) and \(B = 2\mu\,C_2\), both constants. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A\) is constant. Hooke's law (equation (3.12)) gives \(\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}))/E\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu A + E\,\varepsilon_0\) is constant throughout the tube.
  2. At \(r = a\) the outward normal of the body is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24) with zero shear stresses gives \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = -\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), which must equal the force per unit area \(-p_{\mathrm{a}}\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\). At \(r = b\) the normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). We get two linear equations \(A - B/a^2 = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(A - B/b^2 = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). Subtract them: \(B\,(1/a^2 - 1/b^2) = p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(B = (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\), and \(A = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + B/a^2 = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\). These are the constants (11.34).
  3. At the ends \(z = \pm l\) the normal is \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\ten\sigma\,(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \pm\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\) are zero. The prescribed force is \(\pm S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S\) is required. By step 6 this is \(2\nu A + E\,\varepsilon_0 = S\), so \(\varepsilon_0 = (S - 2\nu A)/E\) as in (11.34).
  4. We return to \(u\). From \(u/r = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and Hooke's law (equation (3.12)) \(u = r\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E = r\,\big((1 - \nu)A - \nu S + (1 + \nu)B/r^2\big)/E\). This is the field (11.35), of the form \(C_1 r + C_2/r\) with \(C_1 = ((1 - \nu)A - \nu S)/E\) and \(C_2 = (1 + \nu)B/E\). For these constants \(2(\lambda + \mu)\,C_1 + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 = A\) and \(2\mu\,C_2 = B\) (insert \(\lambda = E\nu/((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu))\), \(\mu = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) and \(\varepsilon_0\) from step 8, checked with SymPy), so the field (11.35) indeed gives the stresses (11.36) by step 6, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu A + E\varepsilon_0 = S\).
  5. In Cartesian components \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(r)\,x/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u(r)\,y/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0 z\) with \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\). The functions \(r\) and \(1/r\) are continuously differentiable for \(r > 0\), so the field is continuously differentiable on the open set \(r > 0\), which contains the body, and so are its stresses by step 6. The equilibrium equations hold by step 3, the conditions on the surface by steps 7 and 8. The field is therefore a solution. This is item 1.
  6. The body is cut into pieces between graphs and curved boxes, the material is by assumption within the limits of theorem 3.48, so theorem 11.4 holds. By its item 2 every solution has the same stresses and strains, and the difference of displacements is a small rigid displacement. This is item 2.

Source: SADD Example 8.6, (8.4.1)-(8.4.6) (PDF pp. 188-190), where the tube is treated in plane strain with a stress function, CM Example 7.5.1 (PDF pp. 324-325), where the displacement \(U(r)\) is used, BOWER §4.1.9 (PDF pp. 233-235), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = F_{\mathrm{z}}/(\pi E (b^2 - a^2)) - 2\nu\,(p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(E\,(b^2 - a^2))\) with the axial force \(F_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the ends (in our notation \(F_{\mathrm{z}} = S\,\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\)), and for closed ends \(F_{\mathrm{z}} = \pi\,(p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)\) (PDF p. 234), BARBER §9.3.2 (PDF p. 145). The proof is ours: equations in displacements and direct integration of both derivatives instead of a stress function. The form \(u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the displacement field is prescribed by CM and BOWER, while SADD and BARBER start from a stress function.

The proof showed that the field we guessed satisfies the field equations and both conditions on the surface, and that by the uniqueness theorem there is no other field. Now let us test it on our tube.

The three values of \(S\) from the previous section give the following in the theorem. For open ends \(S = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_0 = -2\nu A/E\). For closed ends \(S = A\), because \(S = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\) is exactly \(A\). For plane strain \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\) and \(S = 2\nu A\). The end of the tube affects only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and the displacements. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) do not depend on it, nor on \(E\) and \(\nu\): the form \(A \mp B/r^2\) follows from the equation in step 4, in which \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) cancel, and the constants \(A\) and \(B\) are determined only by the two pressures in step 7. The displacements, however, contain \(E\) and \(\nu\) (SADD, PDF p. 189).

Why is the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A\) constant through the wall? In step 5, \((1/r)(r\,u)' = u' + u/r = 2C_1\), so the sum \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) is constant. The part \(C_2/r\) of the field has zero sum \(u' + u/r = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\): what a fibre stretches in the hoop direction it shrinks in the radial direction, and the area of a small piece in the plane of the cross-section does not change. The sum of stresses follows from Hooke's law.

For our tube \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = p = 30\) MPa, \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\), \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm. By (11.34)

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{p\,a^2}{b^2 - a^2} = \frac{30 \cdot 1600}{3600 - 1600} = 24\ \text{MPa} , \\ B &= \frac{p\,a^2 b^2}{b^2 - a^2} = \frac{30 \cdot 1600 \cdot 3600}{2000} = 86\,400\ \text{MPa mm}^2 , \end{aligned}\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 24 - 86\,400/r^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 24 + 86\,400/r^2\) in MPa with \(r\) in mm. The values (with SymPy and NumPy) are in the table and in figure 11.18.

\(r\) (mm) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (MPa)

50 \(-10{,}56\) 58,56 48

First we check the equilibrium of half a tube. The mean of the hoop stress through the wall is \(\frac1{b - a}\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = A + B/(a b) = 24 + 86\,400/2400 = 60\) MPa, which is exactly \(p\,a/t\) of (11.33). The equilibrium of half a tube is satisfied, as it must be, and the distribution that this equilibrium could not give is now given: the hoop stress is \(78\) MPa at the inner edge and falls toward the outer one, where it is \(48\) MPa. The radial stress is \(-30\) MPa at the inner edge, where the oil presses, and zero on the free outer surface.

Why does the hoop stress fall? With open ends the bore moves by \(u(a) = 17{,}755\) µm, while the outer edge moves only by \(u(b) = 14{,}694\) µm (by (11.35), with \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(E = 196\) GPa). The hoop strain is \(u/r\), so at the inner edge \(17{,}755/40\,000 = 4{,}44\cdot10^{-4}\) and at the outer \(14{,}694/60\,000 = 2{,}45\cdot10^{-4}\). The fibre next to the bore stretches around the axis \(4{,}44/2{,}45 = 1{,}8\) times as much as the fibre next to the outer surface, so it carries a larger stress. The wall thickness therefore cannot be neglected, because different fibres are not equally loaded. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A = 48\) MPa is constant through the wall, as we saw above.

11.7.3 How good is the thin-wall formula

The formula (11.33) with the inner radius gives the mean \(60\) MPa, and this is exact for every thickness, because it follows from the equilibrium of half a tube. The formula with the mean radius gives \(75\) MPa. This is \(25\,\%\) above the mean, but only \(3{,}8\,\%\) below the largest hoop stress \(78\) MPa. Whoever designs a tube by this formula uses it for the stress that is compared with the yield stress, that is, the largest one. Let us therefore compare it with the stress at the inner edge, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + B/a^2 = p\,(a^2 + b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\). The ratio is

\[\frac{p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t}{\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)} = \frac{p\,(a + b)^2/(2\,(b^2 - a^2))}{p\,(a^2 + b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)} = \frac{(a + b)^2}{2\,(a^2 + b^2)} = 1 - \frac{(b - a)^2}{2\,(a^2 + b^2)} .\]

The ratio is smaller than \(1\) for every thickness, so the formula with the mean radius always underestimates the largest stress. For our tube it is \(1 - 400/10\,400 = 0{,}9615\). For thinner walls the error is quadratic in the relative thickness \(t/a\):

\(t/a\) 0,5 (our tube) 0,2 0,1 0,02
\(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t\) relative to \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) 0,9615 0,9918 0,9977 0,9999
\(p\,a/t\) relative to \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) 0,7692 0,9016 0,9502 0,9900

At \(t/a = 0{,}1\) the formula with the mean radius is accurate to \(0{,}23\,\%\), which suffices for design. The formula with the inner radius is worse, because it is always the mean and not the maximum. For our tube \(t/a = 0{,}5\) and the formula with the mean radius underestimates the largest stress by \(3{,}8\,\%\). Lamé's solution is therefore needed when this error is too large, and for all loads where we are interested in more than the largest hoop stress, for example the radial stress, which is negligible in a thin wall but here reaches \(-30\) MPa.

11.7.4 A thin ring is solved exactly

The thin ring of section 1.18.5 with radii 40 mm and 60 mm and thickness 2 mm is a tube with \(2l = 2\) mm and free faces \(z = \pm l\), so a case of open ends, \(S = 0\). By theorem 11.27, under pressure in the bore it is solved exactly in three dimensions: the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are as in the table, and the axial stress is zero through the whole thickness. The state of plane stress (section 11.9), in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) everywhere, is therefore not an approximation here. The axial strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E = -2\nu A/E = -7{,}347\cdot10^{-5}\) and is constant through the ring, because the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is constant. The thickness of the ring decreases by \(2\ \text{mm}\cdot 7{,}347\cdot10^{-5} = 146{,}9\) nm. If the sum of stresses varied through the wall, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) would vary too, and a field with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) everywhere would not necessarily be exact. We shall see an example in the rotating ring.

11.7.5 Where the tube starts to yield

By theorem 11.27 the matrix of stress in local axes is diagonal, so its diagonal components are the principal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.76) is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{\tfrac12\big((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})^2 + (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})^2 + (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})^2\big)} ,\]

and the steel starts to yield when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) with yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (section 2.12.5 of chapter 2). All three stresses are proportional to the pressure \(p\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) is proportional to it as well, and the yield pressure is \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 30\ \text{MPa}\cdot 220/\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\). The computation through the whole wall (NumPy) gives the largest value for all three ends at the inner edge \(r = a\), where \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}|\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are largest. There, at \(p = 30\) MPa:

ends of the tube \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) at the bore (MPa) yield pressure \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) (MPa)
open, \(S = 0\) 0 96,56 68,35
plane strain, \(S = 2\nu A\) 14,4 94,02 70,20
closed, \(S = A\) 24 93,53 70,57

At \(30\) MPa the tube therefore does not yield, the safety against yielding is \(68{,}35/30 = 2{,}28\) for open and \(70{,}57/30 = 2{,}35\) for closed ends. The three values differ by \(3{,}2\,\%\). The axial stress enters \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}^2\) only through the sum \((\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})^2 + (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})^2\), while the first term \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})^2 = 108^2 = 11\,664\) is always the same. The sum is \(78^2 + 30^2 = 6984\) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(54^2 + 54^2 = 5832\) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 24\) MPa. At \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = A = 24\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is exactly in the middle between \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), where the sum is smallest, so closed ends have the smallest equivalent stress and the largest yield pressure. Whoever calculates by Tresca's condition (section 2.12.5 of chapter 2: yielding when \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\)) gets for all three ends the same difference of the largest and smallest principal stress, \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 108\) MPa, and the yield pressure \(30\cdot220/108 = 61{,}1\) MPa. The difference between the Mises and Tresca criteria (\(12\,\%\) to \(15\,\%\)) is larger than the difference between the ends. At \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) the layer next to the bore starts to yield, and our solution for an elastic body holds only up to this pressure.

11.7.6 Interference fit

A common way to fasten a tube onto a shaft or into another tube is an interference fit (nasedanje): we make the inner part larger by a small \(\delta\) than the bore of the outer one and assemble them by heating or cooling one until they go together (BOWER §4.1.11, PDF p. 236). Take a tube \(20/40\) mm in a tube \(40/60\) mm, both of steel with the same constants and with open ends, and let the radius of the outer edge of the inner tube be larger by \(\delta = 20\) µm than the radius of the bore of the outer tube (figure 11.19, top). Let the contact carry no friction, only the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) (contact pressure, kontaktni tlak), and let there be no external load. What is \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and what stresses does it cause?

For each tube separately we know the solution. If \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is known, each tube is a problem of forces with known data, so by theorem 11.27 there is a single solution (by theorem 11.4). Only \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is unknown. It is determined by the condition that the surfaces touch.

  1. Inner tube (\(a = 20\) mm, \(b = 40\) mm): inner pressure zero, outer pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\). By (11.34) \(A_{\mathrm{i}} = -p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot 1600/1200 = -\tfrac43\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{i}} = -p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot 400\cdot1600/1200 = -533{,}3\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) (in mm\(^2\)). For open ends \(S = 0\), and by (11.35) \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = 40\,\big((1 - \nu)A_{\mathrm{i}} + (1 + \nu)B_{\mathrm{i}}/1600\big)/E = -0{,}2789\ \mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa}\).
  2. Outer tube (\(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm): inner pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\), outer zero. Here \(A_{\mathrm{o}} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot1600/2000 = 0{,}8\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{o}} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot1600\cdot3600/2000 = 2880\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\), and \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) = 0{,}5918\ \mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa}\).
  3. Before assembly the outer edge of the inner tube has radius \(40\ \text{mm} + \delta\) and the bore of the outer tube \(40\) mm. After assembly the radius of the outer edge of the inner tube is \(40\ \text{mm} + \delta + u_{\mathrm{i}}(40)\) and the radius of the bore of the outer tube \(40\ \text{mm} + u_{\mathrm{o}}(40)\). Because they touch, they are equal, so \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = \delta\). The displacements are small, \(20\ \mu\text{m}/40\ \text{mm} = 5\cdot10^{-4}\), so we compute the stresses on the original shape as before.
  4. We get \((0{,}5918 + 0{,}2789)\,\mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa} = 20\ \mu\text{m}\), so \(p_{\mathrm{c}} = 22{,}969\) MPa. Check: \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) = 13{,}594\) µm and \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = -6{,}406\) µm, difference \(20{,}000\) µm.

Uniqueness holds for each part separately at a known \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\). That \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is exactly this one follows from the contact condition. We do not prove uniqueness of the contact problem itself, where \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is not given. The outer tube expands and the inner one shrinks, as it must. The stresses are (by (11.36)) in the outer tube at the bore \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\,(60^2 + 40^2)/(60^2 - 40^2) = 59{,}72\) MPa and at the outer edge \(36{,}75\) MPa, and in the inner tube \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -38{,}28\) MPa at radius 40 mm and \(-61{,}25\) MPa at the inner edge at 20 mm. The radial stress at 40 mm is \(-22{,}97\) MPa in both, because the contact pressure is the same for both tubes. The hoop stress, however, jumps at the contact from \(-38{,}28\) to \(59{,}72\) MPa (figure 11.19, bottom): the inner tube is compressed, the outer one stretched. At these stresses neither tube yields, the highest equivalent stress is \(73{,}9\) MPa at the bore of the outer tube.

11.7.7 The rotating ring

A steel ring \(40/60\) mm rotates about its axis at \(10\,000\) revolutions per minute, so with angular velocity \(\omega = 2\pi\cdot10\,000/60 = 1047{,}2\) rad/s, with no external forces. There is no body force, but there is acceleration. A point at radius \(r\) moves on a circle with position \(\vek x = r\,(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)\), so by definition 8.69 its acceleration is \(\vek a = \partial^2\vek x/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2\,\vek x = -\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (derivatives of sine and cosine, theorem 8.12). The equation of motion (definition 8.70, equation (8.59)) is \(\mathrm{div}\,\ten\sigma = \rho\,\vek a\), so \(\mathrm{div}\,\ten\sigma + \rho\,\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). This is an equilibrium equation with body force \(\vek b = \rho\,\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), which points outward. The same conclusion is d'Alembert's principle (theorem 8.71): instead of the acceleration we take the force \(-\rho\vek a\) and treat the body as in equilibrium.

The ring is thin (2 mm thick), so we assume plane stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The field is rotationally symmetric, \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), as for the tube.

  1. Hooke's law (equation (3.12), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E\). Solving for the stresses: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}})/(1 - \nu^2)\), with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\) by corollary 11.21.
  2. The radial equilibrium equation (corollary 11.24 with \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = \rho\,\omega^2 r\)): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r + \rho\,\omega^2 r = 0\). The remaining two hold as for the tube.
  3. Insert step 1. The terms with \(\nu\) have the same form as for the tube: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = E\,(u'' + \nu\,(u'/r - u/r^2))/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = E\,(1 - \nu)\,(u' - u/r)/(r\,(1 - \nu^2))\). The sum is \(E\,(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2)/(1 - \nu^2)\), so
    \[u'' + \frac{u'}{r} - \frac{u}{r^2} = -\frac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\omega^2}{E}\,r .\]
  4. We look for a particular solution. For \(u_{\mathrm{p}} = k\,r^3\), \(u_{\mathrm{p}}'' + u_{\mathrm{p}}'/r - u_{\mathrm{p}}/r^2 = (6 + 3 - 1)\,k\,r = 8\,k\,r\), so \(u_{\mathrm{p}} = -\dfrac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\omega^2}{8\,E}\,r^3\) is a solution. The difference of two solutions solves the homogeneous equation \(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2 = 0\) of step 4 of the proof of theorem 11.27, whose general solution is by step 5 of that proof \(C_1 r + C_2/r\). So the general solution is \(u = u_{\mathrm{p}} + C_1 r + C_2/r\). The solution is checked with SymPy.
  5. Boundary conditions: both edges are free, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\). The two linear equations for \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are solved with SymPy:
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2\,(r^2 - a^2)\,(b^2 - r^2)}{8\,r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\rho\,\omega^2}{8}\Big((3 + \nu)\Big(a^2 + b^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2}\Big) - (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.37}\]

For steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 27{,}80\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = 16{,}68\) MPa and the largest radial stress is \(1{,}41\) MPa at \(r = 49\) mm (figure 11.20). Let us compare with the estimate for a thin ring. If the radial stress is negligible, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\), the radial equation of step 2 gives \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r + \rho\,\omega^2 r = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \rho\,\omega^2 r^2\). At the mean radius \(50\) mm this is \(21{,}38\) MPa. The estimate expects the hoop stress to grow with \(r\), while our solution falls. Why? The radial equation without the approximation is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \rho\,\omega^2 r^2 + (r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})'\). The first term is \(13{,}69\) MPa at the bore and \(30{,}79\) MPa at the outer edge. The second term is \(+14{,}11\) MPa at the bore and \(-14{,}11\) MPa at the outer edge, because the radial stress, although only \(1{,}41\) MPa, rises from zero over a short distance and falls back to zero, and its derivative has opposite signs at the two edges. This term exceeds the growth of the first, so the hoop stress falls. The estimate lies in the middle of the range from \(16{,}68\) to \(27{,}80\) MPa, and the largest stress is \(30\,\%\) above it, because the wall is half the inner radius.

With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) at the bore (both other principal stresses are zero there) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 27{,}80\) MPa. The stress grows with \(\omega^2\), so the ring starts to yield at \(10\,000\cdot\sqrt{220/27{,}80} \approx 28\,100\) revolutions per minute, that is, at \(2{,}8\) times the speed. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is, however, not constant here but is \(27{,}80\) MPa at the bore and \(16{,}68\) MPa at the outer edge. The axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\) therefore varies through the wall, \(-4{,}26\cdot10^{-5}\) at the bore and \(-2{,}55\cdot10^{-5}\) at the outer edge. For the tube of theorem 11.27 it was constant and the field was exact in three dimensions. Here it is not exact: plane stress is a model for a thin ring, not a solution of the three-dimensional problem. How good a model it is is shown by the exact field of a long rotating tube with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (CM Example 7.5.1, PDF pp. 324-325), in which in step 1 the coefficients \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu E/(1 - \nu^2)\) are replaced by \(\lambda + 2\mu\) and \(\lambda\) (equation (3.13) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), and in step 3 \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\) is replaced by \(\lambda + 2\mu\). There \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 28{,}35\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = 16{,}13\) MPa, so \(2{,}0\,\%\) above and \(3{,}3\,\%\) below the values of our model.

11.7.8 What we have shown

Equilibrium of half a tube gave only the mean of the hoop stress, \(p\,a/t = 60\) MPa, and the displacement field \(u = C_1 r + C_2/r\) gave its distribution, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) from \(78\) to \(48\) MPa. The same field solves three kinds of ends, and for all of them theorem 11.4 says that there is no other solution. The thin-wall formula with the mean radius is at \(t/a = 0{,}5\) by \(3{,}8\,\%\) below the largest stress, and is below it for every thickness. The tube starts to yield at \(68\) to \(71\) MPa depending on the ends, an interference fit of \(20\) µm gives a contact pressure of \(22{,}97\) MPa, and the rotating ring starts to yield at about \(28\,100\) revolutions per minute. We shall obtain the same stresses of the tube later in this chapter from the Airy function in polar coordinates. In the next section we use the same procedure, guessing a radial displacement and integrating twice, for the spherical shell, which chapter 4 cut into six curved boxes.
