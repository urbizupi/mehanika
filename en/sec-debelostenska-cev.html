Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.7 The thick-walled tube

A steel tube of a hydraulic system has inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm, and it holds oil at pressure \(p = 30\) MPa. This is the tube of section 11.1 (figure 11.3), made of the same steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). How do the stresses vary through the wall? Where does the steel start to yield first and at what pressure? And how good is the thin-wall formula, by which the stress in the hoop direction of the tube equals the pressure times the radius divided by the wall thickness? The wall is \(b - a = 20\) mm thick, half the inner radius, so we must not just assume that the formula holds.

From history

Lamé and Clapeyron, two engineers, wrote a memoir on the interior equilibrium of homogeneous solid bodies while in service in St. Petersburg. It was reviewed by Poinsot and Navier in 1828 and printed in 1833 (TIMO, PDF p. 123). TIMO ascribes the importance of the memoir to the fact that it contains not only a derivation of the equations of equilibrium, which Navier and Cauchy already knew, but also their application to problems of practical value (TIMO, PDF p. 123). Among them is the hollow circular cylinder under uniform internal and external pressure, for which they derive formulas for the stresses. With them they calculate the necessary wall thickness for given pressures. In doing so they use the maximum stress theory (teorija največje napetosti), but they state explicitly that each element of the cylinder is in a two-dimensional state of stress and that the elastic limit from a simple tensile test may not apply to such a case (TIMO, PDF p. 124). After that they treat the torsion of circular shafts, a sphere under gravitational forces directed toward its centre and a spherical shell under uniform internal or external pressure. For all these problems TIMO states that the formulas are correct and that they have since found many applications (TIMO, PDF p. 124). The two memoirs of Poisson that TIMO cites are from 1829 and 1831 (TIMO, PDF p. 119). In one of them Poisson treats, as a simple example of a three-dimensional problem, the stresses and deformations in a hollow sphere under internal or external uniform pressure (TIMO, PDF p. 120).

Let us return to our tube with radii 40 mm and 60 mm under pressure 30 MPa. Such tubes were already calculated in the time of Lamé and Clapeyron, and they were aware that the state of stress in the wall is two-dimensional. Let us first ask how much equilibrium alone tells us about the stresses.

11.7.1 Equilibrium of half a tube

We cut the tube by a plane through the axis and consider the upper half of length one unit (figure 11.56). For now assume that the stresses do not change along the axis. This is justified far from the ends: the tube and the pressure are the same everywhere along the axis, so every slice of the tube is in the same situation as its neighbour (for our field theorem 11.28 will show it). Near the ends, where a plug closes the tube or a clamp holds it, the assumption does not hold. What does it bring? The forces on the two axial sections of the piece are opposite and cancel, so in vertical equilibrium only the pressure and the stresses on the lengthwise section remain. Without it the equation we are about to get would hold only for the mean along the length of the tube. The oil pushes the wall of the bore outward. The forces on the curved wall point in all directions, but the horizontal components on the left and right halves of the bore are opposite, so only the vertical component remains. At the arc at angle \(\theta\) from the horizontal axis through the centre \(O\), where the normal of the bore is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), the vertical component of the force per unit area is \(p\sin\theta\). We describe the arc by the angle \(\theta\) and the length \(z\) along the axis. The surface vector has length \(a\) (section 4.3.6, equation (4.34)), so the resultant per unit length is the integral over \(\theta\) of the force per unit area times \(a\):

\[\int_0^{\pi} p\,\sin\theta\;a\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\,p\,a ,\]

which is the pressure times the width \(2a\) of the half bore. For our tube this is \(2 \cdot 30 \cdot 40 = 2400\) N for every millimetre of length of the tube. At the two sections (\(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\)) the lower part holds the half. The normal of the section there is \(\mp\vek e_\theta\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) is vertical, so the vertical component of the force per unit area has magnitude \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \vek e_\theta\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) (definition 11.19) and points downward. The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) points horizontally and does not affect vertical equilibrium. The component \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is called the hoop stress (obodna napetost) because it points along the circumference of the tube, and the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) the radial stress (radialna napetost). Each of the two sections reaches from the radius \(a\) to the radius \(b\), so it has width \(t = b - a\) per unit length, and it carries half of the resultant, so vertical equilibrium with a free outer surface gives

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = p\,a . \tag{11.35}\]

The mean hoop stress through the wall is therefore \(p\,a/(b - a)\). Equilibrium tells no more than the mean, so we drew the arrows on both sections equally long. If the hoop stress is equal to \(\sigma\) throughout the wall, then \(\sigma\,(b - a) = p\,a\), so

\[\sigma = \frac{p\,a}{b - a} = \frac{p\,a}{t} , \qquad t = b - a . \tag{11.36}\]

This is the thin-wall formula. For our tube \(\sigma = 30 \cdot 40/20 = 60\) MPa. If we replace the radius in the numerator by the mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = (a + b)/2 = 50\) mm, we get \(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t = 75\) MPa (SADD (8.4.6), PDF p. 190). The numbers 60 and 75 MPa differ by a quarter. Which of them means what? We shall see when we know the hoop stress through the whole wall, in figure 11.58, where both are drawn as horizontal lines.

Equilibrium of half a tube tells us only the mean of the hoop stress through the wall and not how it is distributed. Equation (11.35) is one equation for the function \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(r)\), so infinitely many functions with the same mean satisfy it. The distribution is determined only by the link with displacements that the boundary value problem contains. We shall guess the field by the semi-inverse method, just like the field of the hanging bar in section 11.4.

11.7.2 Lamé's solution

The tube is rotationally symmetric and the load is the same on every circumference. We therefore expect each ring of the tube to expand uniformly: a point at radius \(r\) moves radially by \(u(r)\), independent of the angle \(\theta\) and of the height \(z\). Let us draw a ring of the tube before loading grey and dashed around the axis through \(O\), and after it orange, with the displacements enlarged enough to be seen (figure 11.57, left). The assumption rests on symmetry: the geometry, the material and the pressure are the same at every angle, so no direction along the circumference is preferred and a point has nowhere to move except along the radius. It does not hold when the load is not the same around the circumference, for example for a tube that lies on supports and bends under its own weight, nor for a material that is not isotropic. What does it bring? Instead of three unknown functions of three variables one function \(u(r)\) of one variable remains, and the shear strains are zero. It brings no error if the field in the end satisfies all the equations, since there is only one solution. Along the axis the tube may stretch or shrink uniformly, \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0\,z\), with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\) that we shall determine. The field is therefore

\[\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

This is a guess, which the following steps will check. If the field satisfies all three requirements of definition 11.1, it is a solution by theorem 11.4, and there is no other.

The form of the field alone tells why the inner fibres carry more. A fibre that runs around the axis along the circle of radius \(a\) has length \(2\pi a\). When all its points move outward by \(u(a)\), it lies on the circle of radius \(a + u(a)\) and has length \(2\pi\,(a + u(a))\). It has become longer by \(2\pi\,u(a)\), so its hoop strain, the elongation divided by the length, is \(u(a)/a\). The same holds at every radius: \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u(r)/r\). The fibre next to the outer surface is longer by a half than the fibre next to the bore. Even if both edges moved by the same amount, the shorter inner fibre would stretch relatively more. For our tube we shall compute below that the bore moves by \(17{,}8\) µm and the outer surface by \(14{,}7\) µm. The hoop strain is therefore \(4{,}44\cdot10^{-4}\) at the bore and \(2{,}45\cdot10^{-4}\) at the outer surface. By Hooke's law a fibre that is stretched more is held by a larger stress, so we expect the hoop stress to fall from the bore outward. How fast is determined by Hooke's law, in which the radial stress appears as well, and by equilibrium. This is the computation in the proof of theorem 11.28.

But what about the ends of the tube? A tube is always closed, clamped or open somewhere, and this changes the axial stress. So let us prescribe at both ends \(z = \pm l\) a uniform normal stress \(S\), that is, a force per unit area \(S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the upper and \(-S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) at the lower end, and see how \(S\) affects the solution. Three values matter.

  1. Open ends (odprta konca): the ends are free, \(S = 0\) (figure 11.3, left).
  2. Closed ends (zaprta konca): plugs close the ends. The oil with pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) pushes a plug of radius \(a\) with axial force \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\pi a^2\), and the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) acts on the outer surface with force \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\pi b^2\). The resultant \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\pi a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}}\pi b^2\) is by assumption spread uniformly over the annulus of the end with area \(\pi(b^2 - a^2)\) (figure 11.3, middle). The stress is then \(S = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\).
  3. Plane strain (ravninska deformacija, section 11.9): \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\), so the distance between the ends does not change, as if they were held by smooth rigid walls. The stress \(S\) is here a result that we shall compute. The walls are not part of our problem, because section 11.1 does not use smooth supports. Instead of them we prescribe this stress, and the problem of forces with it has the same solution.

The stresses at the ends of a real tube are not perfectly uniform, because a plug or a wall does not spread the force uniformly. A uniform \(S\) is an idealization. It is justified because the real load of the end has the same resultant, and the difference of the two is a load that is in equilibrium with itself and dies out quickly with the distance from the end. For the straight strip of section 11.11.2 later in this chapter it falls to \(1{,}5\,\%\) at a distance of one height of the strip. Our solution therefore holds in the part of the tube that is a few wall thicknesses away from the ends, and not right at the plug. It affects the result little: the theorem will show that \(S\) appears only in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and in the displacements, while the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are the same for all three kinds of ends. Now let us compute the field.

Theorem 11.28 Lamé solution for a tube (Laméjeva rešitev za cev)

Let the body be the tube \(a \le r \le b\), \(-l \le z \le l\) with radii \(0 < a\) and \(b\), \(a < b\) and half-length \(l > 0\), cut by two planes through the axis into four curved boxes, as the tube with radii 40 mm and 60 mm is cut in section 4.3.7 of chapter 4 (the same for general \(a\), \(b\) and \(l\)). Let the material be isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64). There is no body force and no supports, so this is a problem of forces (definition 11.1). On the surface \(r = a\) let the pressure be \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) and on \(r = b\) the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\), that is, force per unit area \(-p\,\vek n\) with outward normal \(\vek n\), and at the ends \(z = \pm l\) the force \(\pm S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Let

\[A = \frac{p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2}{b^2 - a^2} , \qquad B = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^2 b^2}{b^2 - a^2} , \qquad \varepsilon_0 = \frac{S - 2\nu A}{E} . \tag{11.37}\]

Then:

  1. the displacement field
    \[\vek u = \Big(\frac{(1 - \nu)A - \nu S}{E}\,r + \frac{(1 + \nu)B}{E}\,\frac1r\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} \tag{11.38}\]
    is a solution of this problem, and its components of stress in local axes (definition 11.19) are
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - \frac{B}{r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{11.39}\]
  2. every solution of this problem has the same stresses and strains and differs from the field (11.38) by a small rigid displacement (theorem 11.4, item 2).
Proof

We must show that the field (11.38) satisfies the three requirements of definition 11.1, and then use the uniqueness theorem. We work with the guess \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with an unknown function \(u\) and a constant \(\varepsilon_0\). Hooke's law in local axes has the same form as in Cartesian ones (corollary 11.27).

1. The equation for \(u\).

  1. Strains from corollary 11.21 for \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u(r)\), \(u_\theta = 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0 z\): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0\). The other three components are zero, because in their formulas \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta\), \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r\), \(u_\theta\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial z\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial r\), \(\partial u_\theta/\partial z\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial\theta\) are all zero. The figure shows the hoop strain \(u/r\): the circle of radius \(r\) becomes longer by \(2\pi\,u\) when it moves outward by \(u\).
  2. Hooke's law (equation (3.28), Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) from definition 3.18) with \(\Theta = u' + u/r + \varepsilon_0\):
    \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u' + \lambda\,\frac{u}{r} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \lambda\,u' + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\frac{u}{r} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,(u' + \tfrac{u}{r}) + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_0 , \end{aligned}\]
    and the shear stresses are \(2\mu\) times the zero shear strains, hence zero.
  3. Equilibrium equations in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.25, \(\vek b = \vek 0\)). The second and third, because of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0\) and independence of \(\theta\), read \(0 = 0\) and \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z = 0\), which holds because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) does not depend on \(z\). The first is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\). Multiplied by \(r\) it reads \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). This is the equilibrium of half a tube of section 11.7.1, written for half of a shell between the radius \(r\) and the outer radius \(b\), cut out of the wall around the axis through \(O\). The inner part of the wall pushes it outward with the force per unit area \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\), as the oil pushes the bore, and the lower half holds it with \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\). Indeed, the integral of the equation from \(r\) to \(b\) is \(b\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) - r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r) = \int_{\mathrm{r}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}s\), which with a free outer surface is equation (11.35) with the pressure \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\) at the radius \(r\).
  4. Insert the expressions from step 2 into the equilibrium of the shell from step 3: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'' + \lambda\,u'/r - \lambda\,u/r^2\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 2\mu\,(u' - u/r)/r\). In the sum the terms \(\lambda\,u'/r\) and \(2\mu\,u'/r\) combine into \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'/r\), and the terms \(-\lambda\,u/r^2\) and \(-2\mu\,u/r^2\) into \(-(\lambda + 2\mu)\,u/r^2\), so
    \[(\lambda + 2\mu)\,\Big(u'' + \frac{u'}{r} - \frac{u}{r^2}\Big) = 0 .\]
    The factor \(\lambda + 2\mu = E\,(1 - \nu)/((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu))\) is positive for \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), so \(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2 = 0\).
  5. By the product rule \(((1/r)(r\,u)')' = (u/r + u')' = u'/r - u/r^2 + u''\), so the equation of step 4 is \(((1/r)(r\,u)')' = 0\). By corollary 1.86 \((1/r)(r\,u)' = 2C_1\) with a constant \(C_1\), so \((r\,u - C_1 r^2)' = 0\) and again by this corollary \(r\,u - C_1 r^2 = C_2\) with a constant \(C_2\). The solution, which gives the displacement of the bore and the displacement of the outer surface, is
    \[u = C_1\,r + \frac{C_2}{r} .\]

2. Stresses and boundary conditions.

  1. In the expressions of step 2, \(u' = C_1 - C_2/r^2\) and \(u/r = C_1 + C_2/r^2\). Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - B/r^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) with \(A = 2(\lambda + \mu)\,C_1 + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0\) and \(B = 2\mu\,C_2\), both constants. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A\) is constant. Hooke's law (equation (3.27)) gives \(\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}))/E\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu A + E\,\varepsilon_0\) is constant throughout the tube.
  2. At \(r = a\) the outward normal of the body is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) with zero shear stresses gives \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = -\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), which must equal the force per unit area \(-p_{\mathrm{a}}\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) with which the oil pushes the wall of the bore. Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\). At \(r = b\) the normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). We get two linear equations \(A - B/a^2 = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(A - B/b^2 = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). Subtract them: \(B\,(1/a^2 - 1/b^2) = p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(B = (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\), and \(A = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + B/a^2 = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\). These are the constants (11.37).
  3. At the ends \(z = \pm l\) the normal is \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\ten\sigma\,(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \pm\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\) are zero. The prescribed force is \(\pm S\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (the stress at the end of the tube), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S\) is required. By step 6 this is \(2\nu A + E\,\varepsilon_0 = S\), so \(\varepsilon_0 = (S - 2\nu A)/E\) as in (11.37).
  4. We return to the displacement \(u\). From \(u/r = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and Hooke's law (equation (3.27)) \(u = r\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E = r\,\big((1 - \nu)A - \nu S + (1 + \nu)B/r^2\big)/E\). This is the field (11.38), of the form \(C_1 r + C_2/r\) with \(C_1 = ((1 - \nu)A - \nu S)/E\) and \(C_2 = (1 + \nu)B/E\). For these constants \(2(\lambda + \mu)\,C_1 + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0 = A\) and \(2\mu\,C_2 = B\) (insert \(\lambda = E\nu/((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu))\), \(\mu = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) and \(\varepsilon_0\) from step 8, checked with SymPy), so the field (11.38) indeed gives the stresses (11.39) by step 6, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu A + E\varepsilon_0 = S\).
  5. In Cartesian components \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(r)\,x/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u(r)\,y/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varepsilon_0 z\) with \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\). The functions \(r\) and \(1/r\) are continuously differentiable for \(r > 0\), so the field is continuously differentiable on the open set \(r > 0\), which contains the body (the axis through \(O\) is excluded), and so are its stresses by step 6. The equilibrium equations hold by step 3, the conditions on the surface by steps 7 and 8. The field is therefore a solution. This is item 1.
  6. The body is cut into pieces between graphs and curved boxes, the material is by assumption within the limits of theorem 3.64, so theorem 11.4 holds. By its item 2 every solution has the same stresses and strains, and the difference of displacements is a small rigid displacement, by which the ring after the load would only be moved. This is item 2.

The proof showed that the field we guessed satisfies the field equations and both conditions on the surface, and that by the uniqueness theorem there is no other field. Now let us test it on our tube.

The three values of \(S\) from the previous section give the following in the theorem. For open ends \(S = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_0 = -2\nu A/E\). For closed ends \(S = A\), because \(S = (p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\) is exactly \(A\). For plane strain \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\) and \(S = 2\nu A\). The end of the tube affects only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and the displacements. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) do not depend on it, nor on \(E\) and \(\nu\): the form \(A \mp B/r^2\) follows from the equation in step 4, in which \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) cancel, and the constants \(A\) and \(B\) are determined only by the two pressures in step 7. The displacements, however, contain \(E\) and \(\nu\) (SADD, PDF p. 189).

Why is the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A\) constant through the wall? In step 5, \((1/r)(r\,u)' = u' + u/r = 2C_1\), so the sum \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) is constant. The part \(C_2/r\) of the field has zero sum \(u' + u/r = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\): what a fibre stretches in the hoop direction it shrinks in the radial direction, and the area of a small piece in the plane of the cross-section does not change. The sum of stresses follows from Hooke's law.

For our tube \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = p = 30\) MPa, \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\), \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm. By (11.37)

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{p\,a^2}{b^2 - a^2} = \frac{30 \cdot 1600}{3600 - 1600} = 24\ \text{MPa} , \\ B &= \frac{p\,a^2 b^2}{b^2 - a^2} = \frac{30 \cdot 1600 \cdot 3600}{2000} = 86\,400\ \text{MPa mm}^2 , \end{aligned}\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 24 - 86\,400/r^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 24 + 86\,400/r^2\) in MPa with \(r\) in mm. The values (with SymPy and NumPy) are in the table and in figure 11.58. In the figure the horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from the bore at 40 mm to the outer surface at 60 mm, the vertical one the stress in MPa. The blackwhite curve is the hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), the blue one the radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\). The dots are the values of the table at the bore, in the middle of the wall and at the outer surface. The axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S\) is constant through the wall, so each of the three kinds of ends has a horizontal line: zero for open ends, \(2\nu A = 14{,}4\) MPa for plane strain and \(A = 24\) MPa for closed ends. First we check the equilibrium of half a tube. The mean of the hoop stress through the wall is \(\frac1{b - a}\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = A + B/(a b) = 24 + 86\,400/2400 = 60\) MPa, which is exactly \(p\,a/t\) of (11.36), the dashed horizontal line at 60 MPa. The equilibrium of half a tube is satisfied, as it must be, and the distribution that this equilibrium could not give is now given: the hoop stress is \(78\) MPa at the inner edge and falls toward the outer one, where it is \(48\) MPa. The radial stress is \(-30\) MPa at the inner edge, where the oil presses, and zero on the free outer surface. In the same way we check half of the shell between \(r = 50\) mm and the outer surface. The inner part of the wall pushes it with \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(50) = 10{,}56\) MPa at the radius \(50\) mm, which gives \(10{,}56 \cdot 50 = 528\) N/mm for each section. That is also \(\int_{50}^{60}\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = 24 \cdot 10 + 86\,400\,(1/50 - 1/60) = 240 + 288 = 528\) N/mm.

That the hoop stress falls we predicted from the form of the field. Now we have the numbers. With open ends the bore moves by \(u(a) = 17{,}755\) µm, while the outer edge moves only by \(u(b) = 14{,}694\) µm (by (11.38), with \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(E = 196\) GPa). The hoop strain \(u/r\) is \(17{,}755/40\,000 = 4{,}44\cdot10^{-4}\) at the inner edge and \(14{,}694/60\,000 = 2{,}45\cdot10^{-4}\) at the outer. The fibre next to the bore stretches around the axis \(4{,}44/2{,}45 = 1{,}8\) times as much as the fibre next to the outer surface. The wall thickness therefore cannot be neglected, because different fibres are not equally loaded. Because the bore moves more than the outer edge, the wall gets thinner: its width decreases by \(17{,}755 - 14{,}694 = 3{,}061\) µm. In the radial direction the wall is compressed, which agrees with the negative radial stress. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A = 48\) MPa is constant through the wall, as we saw above. So the two curves of the figure are mirror images about the horizontal line at \(A = 24\) MPa: the hoop stress is \(B/r^2\) above it, the radial one just as much below it.

11.7.3 How good is the thin-wall formula

The formula (11.36) with the inner radius gives the mean \(60\) MPa, and this is exact for every thickness, because it follows from the equilibrium of half a tube. The formula with the mean radius gives \(75\) MPa. This is \(25\,\%\) above the mean, but only \(3{,}8\,\%\) below the largest hoop stress \(78\) MPa. Whoever designs a tube by this formula uses it for the stress that is compared with the yield stress, that is, the largest one. Let us therefore compare it with the stress at the inner edge, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + B/a^2 = p\,(a^2 + b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)\). The ratio is

\[\frac{p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t}{\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)} = \frac{p\,(a + b)^2/(2\,(b^2 - a^2))}{p\,(a^2 + b^2)/(b^2 - a^2)} = \frac{(a + b)^2}{2\,(a^2 + b^2)} = 1 - \frac{(b - a)^2}{2\,(a^2 + b^2)} .\]

The ratio is smaller than \(1\) for every thickness, so the formula with the mean radius always underestimates the largest stress. For our tube it is \(1 - 400/10\,400 = 0{,}9615\), which is the ratio of \(75\) to \(78\) MPa. For thinner walls the error is quadratic in the relative thickness \(t/a\):

\(t/a\) 0,5 (our tube) 0,2 0,1 0,02
\(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t\) relative to \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) 0,9615 0,9918 0,9977 0,9999
\(p\,a/t\) relative to \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) 0,7692 0,9016 0,9502 0,9900

At \(t/a = 0{,}1\) the formula with the mean radius is accurate to \(0{,}23\,\%\), which suffices for design. The formula with the inner radius is worse, because it is always the mean and not the maximum. For our tube \(t/a = 0{,}5\) and the formula with the mean radius underestimates the largest stress by \(3{,}8\,\%\). Lamé's solution is therefore needed when this error is too large, and for all loads where we are interested in more than the largest hoop stress, for example the radial stress, which is negligible in a thin wall but here reaches \(-30\) MPa.

11.7.4 A thin ring is solved exactly

The thin ring of section 1.18.5 with radii 40 mm and 60 mm and thickness 2 mm is a tube with \(2l = 2\) mm and free faces \(z = \pm l\), so a case of open ends, \(S = 0\). By theorem 11.28, under pressure in the bore it is solved exactly in three dimensions: the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are as in the table and the figure, and the axial stress is zero through the whole thickness. The state of plane stress (section 11.9), in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) everywhere, is therefore not an approximation here. The axial strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E = -2\nu A/E = -7{,}347\cdot10^{-5}\) and is constant through the ring, because the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is constant. The thickness of the ring decreases by \(2\ \text{mm}\cdot 7{,}347\cdot10^{-5} = 146{,}9\) nm, which the section of the wall in the plane \(r\), \(z\) shows. If the sum of stresses varied through the wall, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) would vary too, and a field with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) everywhere would not necessarily be exact. We shall see an example in the rotating ring.

11.7.5 Where the tube starts to yield

By theorem 11.28 the matrix of stress in local axes is diagonal, so its diagonal components are the principal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.107) is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{\tfrac12\big((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})^2 + (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})^2 + (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})^2\big)} ,\]

and the steel starts to yield when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) with yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (section 2.12.6 of chapter 2). All three stresses are proportional to the pressure \(p\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) is proportional to it as well, and the yield pressure is \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 30\ \text{MPa}\cdot 220/\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\). The computation through the whole wall (NumPy) gives the largest value for all three ends at the inner edge \(r = a\), where \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}|\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are largest. There, at \(p = 30\) MPa:

ends of the tube \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) at the bore (MPa) yield pressure \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) (MPa)
open, \(S = 0\) 0 96,56 68,35
plane strain, \(S = 2\nu A\) 14,4 94,02 70,20
closed, \(S = A\) 24 93,53 70,57

At \(30\) MPa the tube therefore does not yield, the safety against yielding is \(68{,}35/30 = 2{,}28\) for open and \(70{,}57/30 = 2{,}35\) for closed ends. The three values differ by \(3{,}2\,\%\). The axial stress enters \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}^2\) only through the sum \((\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})^2 + (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})^2\), while the first term \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})^2 = 108^2 = 11\,664\) is always the same. The sum is \(78^2 + 30^2 = 6984\) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(54^2 + 54^2 = 5832\) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 24\) MPa. At \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = A = 24\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is exactly in the middle between \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), where the sum is smallest, so closed ends have the smallest equivalent stress and the largest yield pressure. Whoever calculates by Tresca's condition (section 2.12.6 of chapter 2: yielding when \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\)) gets for all three ends the same difference of the largest and smallest principal stress, \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 108\) MPa, and the yield pressure \(30\cdot220/108 = 61{,}1\) MPa. The difference between the Mises and Tresca criteria (\(12\,\%\) to \(15\,\%\)) is larger than the difference between the kinds of ends (\(3{,}2\,\%\)). At \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) the layer next to the bore starts to yield, and our solution for an elastic body holds only up to this pressure.

11.7.6 Interference fit

A common way to fasten a tube onto a shaft or into another tube is an interference fit (nasedanje): we make the inner part larger by a small \(\delta\) than the bore of the outer one and assemble them by heating or cooling one until they go together (BOWER §4.1.11, PDF p. 236). Take a tube \(20/40\) mm in a tube \(40/60\) mm, both of steel with the same constants and with open ends, and let the radius of the outer edge of the inner tube be larger by \(\delta = 20\) µm than the radius of the bore of the outer tube (figure 11.59, top). Let the contact carry no friction, only the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) (contact pressure, kontaktni tlak), and let there be no external load. What is \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and what stresses does it cause?

For each tube separately we know the solution. If \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is known, each tube is a problem of forces with known data, so by theorem 11.28 there is a single solution (by theorem 11.4). Only \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is unknown. It is determined by the condition that the surfaces touch.

  1. Inner tube (\(a = 20\) mm, \(b = 40\) mm): inner pressure zero, outer pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\). By (11.37) \(A_{\mathrm{i}} = -p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot 1600/1200 = -\tfrac43\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{i}} = -p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot 400\cdot1600/1200 = -533{,}3\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) (in mm\(^2\)). For open ends \(S = 0\), and by (11.38) \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = 40\,\big((1 - \nu)A_{\mathrm{i}} + (1 + \nu)B_{\mathrm{i}}/1600\big)/E = -0{,}2789\ \mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa}\).
  2. Outer tube (\(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm): inner pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\), outer zero. Here \(A_{\mathrm{o}} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot1600/2000 = 0{,}8\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{o}} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot1600\cdot3600/2000 = 2880\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\), and \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) = 0{,}5918\ \mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa}\).
  3. Before assembly the outer edge of the inner tube has radius \(40\ \text{mm} + \delta\) and the bore of the outer tube \(40\) mm. After assembly the radius of the outer edge of the inner tube is \(40\ \text{mm} + \delta + u_{\mathrm{i}}(40)\) and the radius of the bore of the outer tube \(40\ \text{mm} + u_{\mathrm{o}}(40)\). Because they touch, they are equal, so \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = \delta\). The displacements are small, \(20\ \mu\text{m}/40\ \text{mm} = 5\cdot10^{-4}\), so we compute the stresses on the original shape as before.
  4. We get \((0{,}5918 + 0{,}2789)\,\mu\text{m}\cdot p_{\mathrm{c}}/\text{MPa} = \delta\), so \(p_{\mathrm{c}} = 22{,}969\) MPa. Check: \(u_{\mathrm{o}}(40) = 13{,}594\) µm and \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(40) = -6{,}406\) µm, difference \(20{,}000\) µm.

Uniqueness holds for each part separately at a known \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\). That \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is exactly this one follows from the contact condition. We do not prove uniqueness of the contact problem itself, where \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is not given. The outer tube expands and the inner one shrinks, as it must. The stresses along the radius are (by (11.39)) in the outer tube at the bore \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = p_{\mathrm{c}}\,(60^2 + 40^2)/(60^2 - 40^2) = 59{,}72\) MPa and at the outer edge \(36{,}75\) MPa, and in the inner tube \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -38{,}28\) MPa at radius 40 mm and \(-61{,}25\) MPa at the inner edge at 20 mm. The radial stress at 40 mm is \(-22{,}97\) MPa in both, because the contact pressure is the same for both tubes. The hoop stress, however, jumps at the contact from \(-38{,}28\) to \(59{,}72\) MPa (figure 11.59, bottom): the inner tube is compressed, the outer one stretched. At these stresses neither tube yields, the highest equivalent stress is \(73{,}9\) MPa at the bore of the outer tube.

11.7.7 The rotating ring

A steel ring \(40/60\) mm rotates about the axis through \(O\) at \(10\,000\) revolutions per minute, so with angular velocity \(\omega = 2\pi\cdot10\,000/60 = 1047{,}2\) rad/s, with no external forces (figure 11.60). There is no body force, but there is acceleration. The point \(P\) at radius \(r\) moves on a circle with position \(\vek x = r\,(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)\), so by definition 8.77 its acceleration is \(\vek a = \partial^2\vek x/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2\,\vek x = -\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (derivatives of sine and cosine, theorem 8.13). It points toward the axis. The equation of motion (definition 8.78, equation (8.74)) is \(\mathrm{div}\,\ten\sigma = \rho\,\vek a\), so \(\mathrm{div}\,\ten\sigma + \rho\,\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). This is an equilibrium equation with body force \(\vek b = \rho\,\omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), which points outward and grows with the radius. The same conclusion is d'Alembert's principle (theorem 8.79): instead of the acceleration we take the force \(-\rho\vek a\) and treat the body as in equilibrium.

The ring is thin (2 mm thick), so we assume plane stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). What this assumption costs we say at the end of the section: the exact solution of a long tube differs from the model by at most \(3{,}3\,\%\). The field is rotationally symmetric, \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), as for the tube.

  1. Hooke's law (equation (3.27), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)) gives
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{E} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}}{E} .\]
    Solving for the stresses:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{1 - \nu^2} , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{E\,(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}})}{1 - \nu^2} ,\]
    with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\) by corollary 11.21.
  2. The radial equilibrium equation (corollary 11.25 with \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = \rho\,\omega^2 r\)): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r + \rho\,\omega^2 r = 0\). The remaining two hold as for the tube.
  3. Insert step 1. The terms with \(\nu\) have the same form as for the tube: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = E\,(u'' + \nu\,(u'/r - u/r^2))/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = E\,(1 - \nu)\,(u' - u/r)/(r\,(1 - \nu^2))\). The sum is \(E\,(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2)/(1 - \nu^2)\), so with the force outward on the right-hand side
    \[u'' + \frac{u'}{r} - \frac{u}{r^2} = -\frac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\omega^2}{E}\,r .\]
  4. We look for a particular solution that answers the force outward. For \(u_{\mathrm{p}} = k\,r^3\), \(u_{\mathrm{p}}'' + u_{\mathrm{p}}'/r - u_{\mathrm{p}}/r^2 = (6 + 3 - 1)\,k\,r = 8\,k\,r\), so \(u_{\mathrm{p}} = -\dfrac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\omega^2}{8\,E}\,r^3\) is a solution. The difference of two solutions solves the homogeneous equation \(u'' + u'/r - u/r^2 = 0\) of step 4 of the proof of theorem 11.28, whose general solution is by step 5 of that proof \(C_1 r + C_2/r\). So the general solution is \(u = u_{\mathrm{p}} + C_1 r + C_2/r\). The solution is checked with SymPy.
  5. Boundary conditions: both edges of the ring are free, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\). The two linear equations for \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are solved with SymPy:
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2\,(r^2 - a^2)\,(b^2 - r^2)}{8\,r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\rho\,\omega^2}{8}\Big((3 + \nu)\Big(a^2 + b^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2}\Big) - (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.40}\]

Figure 11.61 shows the stresses by (11.40) for steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\). The horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from 40 to 60 mm, the vertical one the stress in MPa. The blackwhite curve is the hoop stress, the blue one the radial stress. The hoop stress is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 27{,}80\) MPa at the bore and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = 16{,}68\) MPa at the outer edge, and the largest radial stress is \(1{,}41\) MPa at \(r = 49\) mm. Let us compare with the estimate for a thin ring. If the radial stress is negligible, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\), the radial equation of step 2 gives \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r + \rho\,\omega^2 r = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \rho\,\omega^2 r^2\). At the mean radius \(50\) mm this is \(21{,}38\) MPa, the orange horizontal line. The estimate expects the hoop stress to grow with \(r\), while our solution falls. Why? The radial equation without the approximation is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \rho\,\omega^2 r^2 + (r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})'\). The first term is \(13{,}69\) MPa at the bore and \(30{,}79\) MPa at the outer edge. The second term is \(+14{,}11\) MPa at the bore and \(-14{,}11\) MPa at the outer edge, because the radial stress, although only \(1{,}41\) MPa, rises from zero over a short distance and falls back to zero, and its derivative has opposite signs at the two edges. This term exceeds the growth of the first, so the hoop stress falls. The estimate lies within the range from \(16{,}68\) to \(27{,}80\) MPa (the middle of the range is \(22{,}24\) MPa), and the largest stress is \(30\,\%\) above it, because the wall is half the inner radius.

At the bore both other principal stresses are zero, so there \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 27{,}80\) MPa. A computation over the whole wall (NumPy) shows that this is the largest equivalent stress in the ring. The stress grows with \(\omega^2\), so the ring starts to yield at \(10\,000\cdot\sqrt{220/27{,}80} \approx 28\,100\) revolutions per minute, that is, at \(2{,}8\) times the speed. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is, however, not constant here but is \(27{,}80\) MPa at the bore and \(16{,}68\) MPa at the outer edge, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) at both edges. The axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\) therefore varies through the wall, \(-4{,}26\cdot10^{-5}\) at the bore and \(-2{,}55\cdot10^{-5}\) at the outer edge. For the tube of theorem 11.28 it was constant and the field was exact in three dimensions. Here it is not exact: plane stress is a model for a thin ring, not a solution of the three-dimensional problem. How good a model it is is shown by the exact field of a long rotating tube with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (CM Example 7.5.1, PDF pp. 324-325), in which in step 1 the coefficients \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu E/(1 - \nu^2)\) are replaced by \(\lambda + 2\mu\) and \(\lambda\) (equation (3.28) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), and in step 3 \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\) is replaced by \(\lambda + 2\mu\). There \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 28{,}35\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = 16{,}13\) MPa, so \(2{,}0\,\%\) above and \(3{,}3\,\%\) below the values of our model.

11.7.8 The solid circular disk

What changes if the ring has no hole? Take a round steel plate of radius \(b = 60\) mm and thickness \(2\) mm, a solid disk with centre \(O\) and radius \(b\), which we compress all around its edge with the pressure \(p = 30\) MPa (figure 11.62, top left). A thin flat body loaded in its own plane is called a disk (stena), and a round plate or an annulus a circular disk (krožna stena). In the lectures this problem is called the axisymmetrically loaded circular disk. The ring of section 11.7.4 is a circular disk with a hole, here we have it without one. Next to it let us draw an annulus with the same outer radius and with a small hole of radius \(a = 6\) mm, under the same pressure (top right). We want to know what the stresses in the solid disk are and whether a small hole changes them.

We already have the general solution. Step 5 of the proof of theorem 11.28 holds on every interval of radii \(r > 0\) and gives \(u = C_1 r + C_2/r\). For the ring the two constants were determined by two edges, the bore and the outer surface. A solid disk has a single edge. The second condition comes from the centre. All directions from the centre are equivalent, so the point at the centre has nowhere to move: \(u(0) = 0\). What each of the two terms does we draw on three equal disks with centre \(O\) (figure 11.63). With the term \(C_1 r\) the circles around the centre move in proportion to the radius: a circle twice as large twice as far. The centre stays in place and the order of the circles is kept. The term \(C_2/r\), however, would grow beyond all bounds near the centre. Of two circles the one ten times smaller would move ten times as far, a circle of radius \(1\) µm a thousand times as far as a circle of radius \(1\) mm. For \(C_2 > 0\) the small circle would jump past the larger one and a hole would open at the centre, which the disk does not have. For \(C_2 < 0\) the small circle would pass through the centre to the other side and land outside the larger circle, the material would overlap itself. So \(C_2 = 0\), and because \(B = 2\mu\,C_2\) (step 6 of the same proof), also \(B = 0\). What remains is \(u = C_1 r\). In the stresses (11.39) the term \(B/r^2\) is gone, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are equal to the same constant \(A\). The edge requires \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = -p\), so \(A = -p\). The stress in the plane of the disk is \(-p\) everywhere.

This reasoning tells what field to expect. That it really is a solution and the only one we show as for the tube: we check the field and use uniqueness.

Theorem 11.29 Solid disk under edge pressure (polna krožna stena pod tlakom na obodu)

Let the body be the cylinder \(r \le b\), \(-l \le z \le l\) with radius \(b > 0\) and half-thickness \(l > 0\), that is, a prism with a circular cross-section like the hanging bar in section 11.4. Let the material be isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64). There is no body force and no supports, so this is a problem of forces (definition 11.1). On the lateral surface \(r = b\) let the pressure be \(p\), that is, force per unit area \(-p\,\vek n\) with outward normal \(\vek n\), and let the faces \(z = \pm l\) be free. Then:

  1. the displacement field with Cartesian components
    \[u_{\mathrm{x}} = -\frac{(1 - \nu)\,p}{E}\,x , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{(1 - \nu)\,p}{E}\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{2\nu\,p}{E}\,z \tag{11.41}\]
    is a solution of this problem, and its stresses in local axes (definition 11.19) are at every point off the axis
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = -p , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0 , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{11.42}\]
  2. every solution of this problem has the same stresses and strains and differs from the field (11.41) by a small rigid displacement (theorem 11.4, item 2).
Proof

We write the field in Cartesian components, because the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is not defined on the axis, while the centre is part of the body. Off the axis this is the field \(\vek u = -c\,r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + (2\nu p/E)\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) with \(c = (1 - \nu)\,p/E\), so of the form \(u = C_1 r\) of the reasoning before the theorem.

  1. Smoothness. The components (11.41) are linear functions of the coordinates, so they are continuously differentiable everywhere, also on the axis, at the centre.
  2. Strains. Each component depends only on its own coordinate, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = -c\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -c\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 2\nu p/E\), and the shear strains are zero. The disk shrinks equally in all directions of its plane.
  3. Stresses. Hooke's law (equation (3.27)) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -p\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and zero shear stresses gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E = (-p + \nu p)/E = -c\), likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -c\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E = 2\nu p/E\). These are the strains of step 2. Hooke's law assigns a single stress to each strain (equation (3.28)), so these are the stresses of our field.
  4. Local components. By step 3, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek m = -p\,\vek m\) for every vector \(\vek m\) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek 0\). Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = -p\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \vek e_\theta\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = -p\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = -p\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = 0\) (definition 11.19), while the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\) are zero. These are the stresses (11.42), the same at every point.
  5. Equilibrium. The stresses are constant, so all their derivatives are zero, and the equilibrium equations (2.38) without body force hold.
  6. Surface. On the lateral surface the outward normal is \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), a vector in the plane \(x\), \(y\), so by step 4 \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = -p\,\vek n\), which is the prescribed pressure. On the faces \(z = \pm l\), \(\ten\sigma\,(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0\), the faces are free. The field is therefore a solution. This is item 1.
  7. Uniqueness. The cylinder is a body between graphs, the material is within the limits of theorem 3.64, so theorem 11.4 holds. By its item 2 every solution has the same stresses and strains, and the difference of displacements is a small rigid displacement. This is item 2.

Source: The field is Lamé's solution with \(B = 0\) (theorem 11.28, SADD (8.4.1)-(8.4.3), PDF pp. 188-189). The requirement that the stresses at the centre of a solid disk stay bounded, so that the constant of \(1/r^2\) is zero, is written by SADD for the rotating disk (PDF p. 221). The proof is ours: we check the field in Cartesian components and use uniqueness, because theorem 11.28 requires \(a > 0\).

The theorem says more than the reasoning before it. For the solid disk plane stress is exact, as for the ring under pressure: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero at every point, and the axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu p/E = 9{,}18\cdot10^{-5}\) is the same throughout the disk. The edge of the disk moves toward the centre by \((1 - \nu)\,p\,b/E = 0{,}7 \cdot 30 \cdot 60/196\,000\ \text{mm} = 6{,}43\) µm. In the graph below the two sketches the horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from the centre to the edge at \(60\) mm, the vertical one the stress in MPa. Both stresses of the solid disk are a single horizontal line at \(-30\) MPa.

Why is the stress the same in all directions? A picture we already know helps. Cut the disk along any diameter, as we cut the tube in section 11.7.1, and take the upper half (figure 11.64, left). The pressure on its semicircular edge has, by the same integral as the resultant of the pressure in the bore, the resultant \(2\,p\,b = 3600\) N/mm per unit thickness, only that it now points toward the cut. The cut has length \(2b = 120\) mm, so the mean normal stress on it is \(-2pb/(2b) = -p = -30\) MPa: the lower half pushes the upper one. A cut along an oblique diameter gives the same resultant and the same mean (middle), because the disk and the pressure all around its edge look the same from every side. This holds for every diameter, so for every direction in the plane. Equilibrium again tells only the mean, the theorem tells that the stress is \(-p\) at every point as well. A small square of material around the same point has the pressure \(p\) on every side and no shear stress, however we turn it (right). This is step 4 of the proof, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek m = -p\,\vek m\) for every direction \(\vek m\) in the plane.

Now the annulus with the hole. Theorem 11.28 holds for it with \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) and \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = p\). The constants (11.37) are \(A = -p\,b^2/(b^2 - a^2) = -30{,}30\) MPa and \(B = -p\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2) = -1090{,}9\) MPa mm\(^2\). In the graph we marked the radius of the hole by a vertical line at \(6\) mm. The radial stress starts at zero on the free wall of the hole and quickly approaches \(-30\) MPa. The hoop stress, however, is at the wall of the hole

\[\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + \frac{B}{a^2} = -\frac{2\,p\,b^2}{b^2 - a^2} , \tag{11.43}\]

which is \(-60{,}61\) MPa, twice as much as in the solid disk. Toward the edge it falls to \(-30{,}61\) MPa. The term \(B/r^2\), by which the annulus differs from the solid disk, is \(30{,}3\) MPa at the wall of the hole, at \(r = 30\) mm, that is, at five radii of the hole, still \(1{,}2\) MPa, and at the edge \(0{,}3\) MPa. The hole changes the stress only in its neighbourhood, but there it doubles it. How the factor depends on the size of the hole is shown by the table.

radius of the hole \(a\) (mm) 40 20 6 0,6
\(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) relative to \(-p\), that is, \(2b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\) 3,6 2,25 2,02 2,0002

The factor never falls below \(2\), however small the hole. At every point with fixed \(r > 0\) the stresses of the annulus tend to the stresses of the solid disk as \(a\) tends to zero, because \(A\) tends to \(-p\) and \(B\) to zero. The exception is the wall of the hole itself: there the double stress stays. Why exactly double? The wall of the hole is free, so there \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\). A small square of material at the wall of the hole therefore has no pressure on its side at the hole, but carries \(60{,}6\) MPa on its hoop sides. A small square far from the hole has about \(30\) MPa on every side (figure 11.65, left). The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = (A - B/r^2) + (A + B/r^2) = 2A\), however, is constant throughout the annulus, as for the tube. The bars of the two stresses at \(r = 6\), \(12\), \(30\) and \(60\) mm are therefore all equally long, \(2A = -60{,}6\) MPa (right). For a small hole \(2A\) is almost \(-2p\), as much as in the solid disk. What the radial stress does not carry at the wall of the hole, the hoop stress takes over. For the sphere with a small cavity this factor was \(1{,}5\) (section 11.8).

For yielding this means a lot. In the solid disk the principal stresses in the plane are both \(-p\) and the third is zero, so the von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.107) equals \(p\) and steel with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa starts to yield at \(p = 220\) MPa. At the wall of the hole the only nonzero principal stress is \(-2{,}02\,p\), so there the steel starts to yield already at \(220/2{,}02 = 108{,}9\) MPa (NumPy: this is the largest equivalent stress in the annulus). A small hole therefore halves the pressure that the disk carries without yielding.

The rotating solid disk. Now let the same disk rotate without pressure at \(10\,000\) revolutions per minute, like the ring in section 11.7.7, with the force per unit volume \(\rho\,\omega^2 r\) outward. The assumptions are the same as there: plane stress and the field \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Steps 1 to 4 there do not use the edges, so the general solution is the same, \(u = u_{\mathrm{p}} + C_1 r + C_2/r\). For the same reason as under pressure \(C_2 = 0\), because the displacement at the centre must be zero. The only edge is free, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\), and it determines \(C_1\) (SymPy):

\[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2}{8}\,\big(b^2 - r^2\big) , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\rho\,\omega^2}{8}\Big((3 + \nu)\,b^2 - (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.44}\]

These are the formulas SADD (8.4.81) (PDF p. 221, where the radius of the disk is denoted by \(a\)). They agree with (11.40) at \(a = 0\). Figure 11.66 shows the stresses.

The horizontal axis is again the radius \(r\) from the centre to the edge at \(60\) mm, the vertical one the stress in MPa. The hoop stress and the radial stress of the solid disk start at the centre at the same value, \((3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2 b^2/8 = 12{,}70\) MPa. The radial one falls to zero at the free edge, the hoop one to \((1 - \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2 b^2/4 = 5{,}39\) MPa. The largest stress is at the centre, although the force \(\rho\,\omega^2 r\) is zero there (SADD, PDF p. 221). Each ring of material pulls outward also everything that is inside it, so the force accumulates toward the centre.

The annulus with the hole of radius \(6\) mm has by (11.40) \(25{,}46\) MPa at the wall of the hole. In the limit \(a \to 0\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = (3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\omega^2 b^2/4 = 25{,}40\) MPa, exactly twice as much as at the centre of the solid disk (SADD, PDF p. 221, states approximately twice for a very small hole). The hoop stress of the annulus then approaches the curve of the solid disk and is \(5{,}64\) MPa at the edge. The radial one starts at zero at the wall of the hole, reaches \(10{,}29\) MPa at \(r = 19\) mm and then approaches the radial stress of the solid disk. The reason for the double stress is the same as under pressure. Near the centre of the solid disk the stress is almost the same in all directions, \(12{,}70\) MPa. The wall of the hole, however, is free and carries no radial stress, so the hoop stress takes it over.

The solid disk starts to yield at the centre, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 12{,}70\) MPa, because both principal stresses in the plane are equal and the third is zero. This happens at \(10\,000\cdot\sqrt{220/12{,}70} \approx 41\,600\) revolutions per minute. The annulus with the hole starts to yield at the wall of the hole at \(10\,000\cdot\sqrt{220/25{,}46} \approx 29\,400\) revolutions per minute (NumPy along the whole radius). Plane stress is a model here and not an exact solution, for the same reason as for the rotating ring: the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is \(25{,}40\) MPa at the centre and \(5{,}39\) MPa at the edge, so the axial strain is not constant through the disk. The model holds for a thin disk. For a long rotating cylinder with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the same formulas hold by SADD with \(\nu/(1 - \nu)\) in place of \(\nu\) (PDF p. 221), which gives at the centre \((3 + 0{,}4286)/(3 + 0{,}3) = 1{,}039\) times the stress, that is, \(3{,}9\,\%\) more.

11.7.9 What we have shown

Equilibrium of half a tube gave only the mean of the hoop stress, \(p\,a/t = 60\) MPa, and the displacement field \(u = C_1 r + C_2/r\) gave its distribution, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) from \(78\) to \(48\) MPa. The same field solves three kinds of ends, and for all of them theorem 11.4 says that there is no other solution. The thin-wall formula with the mean radius is at \(t/a = 0{,}5\) by \(3{,}8\,\%\) below the largest stress, and is below it for every thickness. The tube starts to yield at \(68\) to \(71\) MPa depending on the ends, an interference fit of \(20\) µm gives a contact pressure of \(22{,}97\) MPa, and the rotating ring starts to yield at about \(28\,100\) revolutions per minute. A solid circular disk under pressure on its edge has the stress \(-p\) everywhere in all directions of its plane, and a small hole doubles it at its wall, under pressure and under rotation. We shall obtain the same stresses of the tube later in this chapter from the Airy function in polar coordinates. In the next section we use the same procedure, guessing a radial displacement and integrating twice, for the spherical shell, which chapter 4 cut into six curved boxes.
