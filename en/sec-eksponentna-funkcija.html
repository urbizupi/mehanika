Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.2 Mathematics: the exponential function and the logarithm

We strike system A, the cantilever with a mass of example 9.9, and let it vibrate (figure 9.16).

Let us draw the record of the motion: above the axis of time \(t\) the displacement \(q\) of the tip, which vibrates about zero and slowly dies down. We measure displacements in parts of the first peak \(q_0\). At the damping ratio (razmernik dušenja) \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) (definition 9.5) every peak is \(0{,}9391\) times the previous one, so the second peak is \(0{,}9391\,q_0\). We will derive this number in section 9.5, here we take it as given. After eleven peaks \(0{,}9391^{11} = 0{,}5010\) of the initial value remains, after twelve \(0{,}9391^{12} = 0{,}4705\). The half \(q_0/2\) is therefore reached somewhere between the eleventh and the twelfth peak. When exactly? And what fraction remains after half a cycle, when the record is at the bottom of a trough and not at a peak?

For the record of the displacement we would like a function \(f(t)\) of time \(t\) for which equal steps of time give equal ratios: \(f(t + s)/f(t)\) should be the same number for every \(t\), depending only on the step \(s\). For a step of one cycle, from peak to peak, this number is \(0{,}9391\), for a step of half a cycle, from a peak to a trough, it is the number whose square is \(0{,}9391\), because two half steps make one whole step. The school answer is a power with a real exponent, \(0{,}9391^{t/T_{\mathrm{d}}}\), where \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the time between two peaks. The book knows powers only with natural exponents, so such a function has no definition yet. This section builds it and answers two questions: which function turns equal steps of time into equal ratios, and what is its derivative.

The route is the same as for sine and cosine in section 8.2, so only with what the book already has: the integral and the fundamental theorem of calculus from chapter 3, and derivatives and the mean value theorem from chapter 1. First we look for a function that turns a product into a sum, and we define it by an area. Its inverse function is the one we are looking for. We show that the derivative of this function equals the function itself, that the unique solution of the equation \(y' = a y\) follows from this, and that equal steps indeed give equal ratios. At the end we show that this function multiplied by a power \(t^n\) decays to zero, and with this we introduce the area under a graph up to arbitrarily large times that sections 9.10 and 9.12 will need. The procedure follows the idea of LEBL1 (§5.4, PDF pp. 207-211), the notation and proofs are ours where the source does not say otherwise.

9.2.1 The logarithm

We will turn equal ratios into equal differences with a function that turns a product into a sum, \(f(x y) = f(x) + f(y)\). With such a function, multiplying by the same number \(y\) always adds the same increment \(f(y)\), no matter where we start. What must it look like if it is differentiable at \(1\)? For \(x = y = 1\), \(f(1) = f(1) + f(1)\) gives \(f(1) = 0\). Let \(y > 0\) and \(0 < |k| < y\), so that \(y + k > 0\) too. We write \(y + k = y\,(1 + u)\) with \(u = k/y\). Then \(f(y + k) = f(y) + f(1 + u)\) and the difference quotient is

\[\frac{f(y + k) - f(y)}{k} = \frac{1}{y}\cdot\frac{f(1 + u) - f(1)}{u} .\]

As \(k\) tends to zero, so does \(u\), and the quotient on the right tends to \(f'(1)\). Hence

\[f'(y) = \frac{f'(1)}{y} . \tag{9.21}\]

The derivative must be a constant multiple of the function \(1/y\). If we choose the multiple \(1\), then by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) \(f\) is the area under the graph of the function \(1/t\). This is not a proof that such a function exists, since we assumed what we would like to prove. It only showed where to look. We set up the definition with the area (figure 9.17) and then prove the properties.

Definition 9.15 Natural logarithm (naravni logaritem)

The function \(1/t\) is continuous at every point \(c > 0\) (the computation in section 3.5.4, written there for \(c \in (0, 1]\), uses only \(c > 0\)), so by theorem 3.53 it is integrable on every closed interval in \((0, \infty)\). For \(x > 0\) the natural logarithm of the number \(x\) (figure 9.17) is the number

\[\ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t \quad \text{for } x \ge 1 , \qquad \ln x = -\int_{\mathrm{x}}^1 \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t \quad \text{for } 0 < x < 1 . \tag{9.22}\]

Source: LEBL1 proposition 5.4.1 (PDF p. 207), where the function is \(L(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\) for \(x > 0\). The minus for \(x < 1\) is written out here, because the book defines the integral only for an upper limit that is not smaller than the lower one (definition 3.49). The motivation with equation (9.21) is not from the source.

We draw the axes \(t\) and \(y\) and on them the graph of the function \(y = 1/t\) (figure 9.17). For \(x > 1\), \(\ln x\) is the area under this graph between \(1\) and \(x\), for \(x < 1\) it is the negative of the area between \(x\) and \(1\). Hence \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln x < 0\) for \(x < 1\). For \(x = 2\) the definition means the blue area between \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\), \(\ln 2 = 0{,}693147\) (SymPy), for \(x = 1/2\) it gives \(\ln\tfrac12 = -0{,}693147\). This example already shows a rule that we prove below: a number and its reciprocal have opposite logarithms.

Why does the area under \(1/t\) turn a product into a sum? Let us look before we prove it. We again draw the axes and the graph of the function \(1/t\), and under it three strips: from \(1\) to \(2\), from \(2\) to \(4\) and from \(4\) to \(8\). Every boundary is twice as far as the previous one. The heights of the graph at the boundaries are \(1\), \(\frac12\), \(\frac14\) and \(\frac18\), each half of the previous one, because \(1/(2t)\) is half of the number \(1/t\). Every strip is therefore twice as wide and half as high as the previous one.

What this does to the area we see on one rectangle. The rectangle over \([1, \frac32]\) with height \(\frac23\), which is the value of \(1/t\) at \(t = \frac32\), lies under the graph and has the area \(\frac12\cdot\frac23 = \frac13\). We stretch it to double width, over \([2, 3]\). The graph there is half as high, so the stretched rectangle is only \(\frac13\) high and has the area \(1\cdot\frac13 = \frac13\), the same as before. We get such a pair for every rectangle under the graph in the first strip, so all three strips have the same area \(\ln 2 = 0{,}6931\). This is the idea, the exact proof is item 2 of the lemma below. Multiplying the boundary by \(2\) therefore always adds the same amount to the area: \(\ln 4 = 2\ln 2 = 1{,}3863\) and \(\ln 8 = 3\ln 2 = 2{,}0794\) (SymPy). The same holds for every factor \(y\) in place of \(2\): the strip from \(x\) to \(x y\) is the strip from \(1\) to \(y\), stretched \(x\) times in width and squeezed \(x\) times in height. This is the rule \(\ln(x y) = \ln x + \ln y\).

Lemma 9.16 Properties of the logarithm (lastnosti logaritma)
  1. \(\ln 1 = 0\). The function \(\ln\) is differentiable on \((0, \infty)\) with derivative \(\ln' x = 1/x\), hence it is continuous and strictly increasing (figure 9.17).
  2. For all \(x, y > 0\), \(\ln(x y) = \ln x + \ln y\). It follows that \(\ln(1/x) = -\ln x\) and \(\ln(x^n) = n \ln x\) for every natural number \(n\) (figure 9.18).
  3. \(\ln 2 \ge 7/12 > 1/2\) (the two rectangles). For every real number \(y\) there is exactly one number \(x > 0\) with \(\ln x = y\).
Proof

We must show three items. The derivative comes from the fundamental theorem of calculus, the product from the fact that the function \(x \mapsto \ln(x y) - \ln x\) has zero derivative, the last item from an estimate of the area (the two rectangles and the strips) and the intermediate value theorem.

1. Derivative.

  1. The function \(1/t\) is continuous at every point \(c > 0\) (section 3.5.4, as in definition 9.15).
  2. Let \(x_0 > 0\). We choose \(a\) and \(b\) with \(0 < a < \min\{x_0, 1\}\) and \(b > \max\{x_0, 1\}\). The function \(1/t\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) by step 1, so \(G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\) is differentiable on \([a, b]\) by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 2), with derivative \(G'(x) = 1/x\). The area under the graph from \(a\) to \(x\) therefore grows as fast as the graph is high above \(x\).
  3. For \(x \in [a, b]\), \(\ln x = G(x) - G(1)\). For \(x > 1\), additivity (lemma 3.54, item 2, with the intermediate point \(1\)) gives \(G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^1 + \int_1^x\), hence \(G(x) - G(1) = \int_1^x\). For \(x = 1\), \(\ln 1 = \int_1^1 \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\) by the convention of definition 3.49, and \(G(1) - G(1) = 0\). For \(x < 1\), \(a \le x < 1\) and additivity gives \(G(1) = G(x) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^1\) (for \(x = a\), \(G(a) = 0\) and this holds by the convention of definition 3.49), hence \(G(x) - G(1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}}^1\).
  4. By step 3, \(\ln\) and \(G\) differ on \([a, b]\) by the constant \(G(1)\), so \(G - G(1)\) is differentiable at \(x_0\) with derivative \(1/x_0\) (lemma 1.82, items 1 and 4). The point \(x_0\) lies inside \((a, b)\), so for small enough steps the difference quotient of \(\ln\) at \(x_0\) uses only values on \([a, b]\), where \(\ln = G - G(1)\). The derivative of \(\ln\) at \(x_0\) is therefore \(1/x_0\), the height of the graph of \(1/t\) above \(x_0\). The point \(x_0\) was arbitrary. A differentiable function is continuous (lemma 1.80).
  5. Let \(0 < x < y\). The function \(\ln\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable in its interior, so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) there is \(\xi \in (x, y)\) with \(\ln y - \ln x = (y - x)/\xi > 0\). The function is strictly increasing. In figure 9.17, \(\ln y - \ln x\) is the area under the graph between \(x\) and \(y\), and the graph lies above the axis \(t\).

2. Product.

  1. Let \(y > 0\) be fixed and \(h(x) = \ln(x y) - \ln x\) for \(x > 0\). In figure 9.18, \(h(x)\) is the area of the strip from \(x\) to \(x y\), for \(x = y = 2\) of the strip from \(2\) to \(4\). Let \(k \ne 0\) and \(u = y k \ne 0\). The difference quotient of the function \(x \mapsto \ln(x y)\) is \(\big(\ln(x y + u) - \ln(x y)\big)/k = y\,\big(\ln(x y + u) - \ln(x y)\big)/u\). By item 1 the quotient in \(u\) converges to \(1/(x y)\). As in theorem 8.13 (part 4, with \(u = \omega h\)), \(x \mapsto \ln(x y)\) is therefore differentiable with derivative \(y/(x y) = 1/x\): for an error \(\eta\) we take a band \(\delta'\) in which the quotient in \(u\) differs from \(1/(x y)\) by less than \(\eta/y\), and \(|k| < \delta'/y\).
  2. Hence \(h'(x) = 1/x - 1/x = 0\) for every \(x > 0\). For \(t > 1\), corollary 1.86 on \([1, t]\) gives \(h(t) = h(1)\), for \(t < 1\) it gives \(h(1) = h(t)\) on \([t, 1]\). So \(h\) is constant, equal to \(h(1) = \ln y - \ln 1 = \ln y\). The strip from \(x\) to \(x y\) therefore has, for every \(x\), the same area as the strip from \(1\) to \(y\).
  3. From \(h(x) = \ln y\) we get \(\ln(x y) = \ln x + \ln y\). For \(y = 1/x\), \(0 = \ln 1 = \ln x + \ln(1/x)\). For \(y = x^{n}\) and induction on \(n\) (start \(\ln x^1 = \ln x\)), \(\ln(x^{n+1}) = \ln(x^n) + \ln x = (n + 1)\ln x\). For \(x = 2\) the strips show this: \(\ln 8 = \ln(2^3) = 3\ln 2\).

3. Values.

  1. On \([1, 3/2]\), \(1/t \ge 2/3\), and on \([3/2, 2]\), \(1/t \ge 1/2\) (the dashed rectangles under the graph). By additivity and the bound of the integral (lemma 3.54, items 2 and 3), \(\ln 2 \ge \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac{7}{12} > \tfrac12\).
  2. By item 2, \(\ln(2^n) = n \ln 2 \ge n/2\) and \(\ln(2^{-n}) = -n \ln 2 \le -n/2\) for natural \(n\). Every strip up to the next power of \(2\) adds \(\ln 2 > 1/2\). Let \(y\) be a real number. By the Archimedean property (theorem 1.36) there is a natural number \(n > 2|y|\), and then \(\ln(2^{-n}) < y < \ln(2^n)\).
  3. The function \(\ln\) is continuous on \([2^{-n}, 2^n]\) (item 1), so by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) there is \(x\) with \(\ln x = y\). Since \(\ln\) is strictly increasing, such an \(x\) is unique. The graph of the function \(\ln\) therefore crosses every horizontal line exactly once.

Source: LEBL1 proposition 5.4.1 (PDF pp. 207-208): the derivative from the fundamental theorem, the product by the change of variable \(u = y t\), the values by the estimate of \(\ln 2\) and the intermediate value theorem. Here the product follows from the zero derivative and corollary 1.86 instead of a change of variable. The details of the proof are ours.

We check the product rule on numbers: \(\ln 2 + \ln 3 = 0{,}693147 + 1{,}098612 = 1{,}791759 = \ln 6\) (SymPy). For the factor \(2\) we saw this on the strips, here the factor is \(3\). This is the property we were looking for. Item 3 says that \(\ln\) takes every real number exactly once. Hence it has an inverse function.

From history

In 1614 John Napier published the work Mirifici logarithmorum canonis descriptio, in which he described logarithms and gave a table of the logarithms of the sines of the angles of the first quadrant from minute to minute (CAJORI, PDF p. 166, BALL, PDF p. 223). CAJORI on p. 165 says that he constructed them before exponents were used, and that the connection between logarithms and the notation with powers was not observed until much later. With Napier the logarithm therefore came first, as in this section. He reached the logarithm with two points that start moving at the same moment: one along a ray with uniform speed, the other along a segment with a speed proportional to the distance it still has to the end of the segment. The path of the first point is the logarithm of the remaining distance of the second (CAJORI, PDF p. 165). The remaining distance decreases in geometric progression, that is by the same factor each time, while the path increases in arithmetic progression, that is by the same increment each time (CAJORI, PDF p. 166). The reason is the speed of the second point: since it is proportional to the remaining distance, in every short time step the point travels the same fraction of that distance. Equal time steps therefore give equal ratios of the remaining distances, which is the same relation as between the peaks of the record in the introduction of the section. By BALL (PDF p. 223) and CAJORI (PDF p. 166), Napier's logarithm of the number \(n\) equals \(10^7 \ln(10^7/n)\), so it decreases as the number increases and the logarithm of the number \(10^7\) is zero. Our \(\ln\) increases and has \(\ln 1 = 0\). Napier's logarithms are therefore not natural logarithms, as CAJORI on p. 166 stresses explicitly.

In 1616 Henry Briggs visited Napier and urged a decimal base, which Napier recognized as an improvement (BALL, PDF p. 224). CAJORI (PDF p. 167) tells in more detail how it went. Briggs proposed that the whole sine (celi sinus), that is \(\sin 90^\circ\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 166), keep the logarithm \(0\) and that the tenth part of the whole sine get the logarithm \(10^{10}\). Napier answered that he had already thought of this change himself, so by CAJORI the decimal logarithms occurred to both independently. Napier improved the proposal further: the number \(1\) should have the logarithm \(0\) and the whole sine the logarithm \(10^{10}\). Only then does the system have a base, since by CAJORI (PDF p. 166) the notion of a base demands that \(1\) have the logarithm \(0\), as our \(\ln\) has. Briggs admitted this to be more convenient (CAJORI, PDF p. 167). In 1617 he published a table of the logarithms of the numbers from 1 to 1000 to fourteen decimal places (BALL, PDF p. 224). By BALL on the same page, J. Bürgi, it would seem independently of Napier, had constructed before 1611 a table of antilogarithms, that is of the numbers belonging to given logarithms, published in 1620. By 1630 tables of logarithms were in general use (BALL, PDF p. 225). The reason is item 2 of lemma 9.16: the logarithm replaces multiplication by addition, as with the strips, where multiplying the boundary by \(2\) adds the area \(\ln 2\). CAJORI (PDF p. 165) writes that shortening the labour doubled the life of the astronomer.

The box at the fundamental theorem (section 3.5.7) told that around 1645 Bond recognized Wright's table as a table of logarithms of tangents (CAJORI, PDF p. 205). By CAJORI (PDF p. 204), St. Vincent in Book VII of his Opus geometricum (Antwerp, 1647) deduced a property of the hyperbola \(x y = 1\), that is of the graph of the function \(1/x\), which comes ever closer to the axes \(x\) and \(y\): if the areas of successive figures between the hyperbola and the axis \(x\), bounded by verticals, are equal, then the lengths of the verticals form a geometric progression, in the figure \(1\), \(\frac12\), \(\frac14\), \(\frac18\). The reason is item 2 of lemma 9.16: the area under \(1/t\) between \(x\) and \(r x\) is \(\ln(r x) - \ln x = \ln r\), independent of \(x\). By the same source, Alfons Anton de Sarasa was apparently the first to state this in the language of logarithms, and Mercator showed that the construction of logarithmic tables reduces to computing areas under the hyperbola (CAJORI, PDF p. 204). Lord Brouncker obtained the first infinite series for the area under the hyperbola \(x y = 1\) between the verticals at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 2\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 204), that is for our area \(\ln 2\).

We return to the search for a function that turns equal steps of time into equal ratios. The logarithm, built from the area under \(1/t\), turns products into sums, so ratios into differences: the boundaries of the strips \(1, 2, 4, 8\) are in the ratio \(2\), while the areas up to them differ by \(\ln 2\). Its inverse function will therefore turn differences, that is steps, into ratios.

9.2.2 The exponential function

By item 3 of lemma 9.16, \(\ln\) is a bijection from \((0, \infty)\) onto the set of all real numbers, so it has an inverse function. We will get its graph by mirroring the graph of the function \(\ln\). We define it next.

Definition 9.17 Exponential function and the number e (eksponentna funkcija in število e)

The exponential function \(\exp\) assigns to every real number \(y\) the number \(\exp(y) > 0\) for which \(\ln(\exp(y)) = y\). Euler's number \(\mathrm{e}\) is \(\mathrm{e} = \exp(1)\), that is the number for which \(\int_1^{\mathrm{e}} \frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1\). For every real number \(y\) we write \(\mathrm{e}^y = \exp(y)\) (figure 9.20).

Source: LEBL1 proposition 5.4.2 and the definition on PDF pp. 209-210 (inverse function of the logarithm), the number \(\mathrm{e}\) and the notation \(\mathrm{e}^x\) on PDF p. 211. The justification of the notation \(\mathrm{e}^y\) is in theorem 9.18.

The number \(\mathrm{e}\) exists because by lemma 9.16, \(\ln\) takes the value \(1\) exactly once. We compute \(\mathrm{e} = 2{,}718282\) (SymPy), and the area under \(1/t\) between \(1\) and \(2{,}718282\), blue and grey together, indeed gives \(1{,}000000\). What are the properties of the function \(\exp\)? The derivative comes from the inverse rule, as for the cosine (lemma 8.9), the product from the logarithm.

Theorem 9.18 Properties of the exponential function (lastnosti eksponentne funkcije)
  1. The function \(\exp\colon \mathbb R \to (0, \infty)\) is a bijection, strictly increasing, \(\exp(0) = 1\), and \(\ln(\exp y) = y\) and \(\exp(\ln x) = x\) for all \(y\) and \(x > 0\) (figure 9.20).
  2. The function \(\exp\) is differentiable at every point (the tangents) and
    \[\exp'(y) = \exp(y) . \tag{9.23}\]
    Hence it is continuously differentiable. For real numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) the function \(t \mapsto \exp(\alpha t + \beta)\) has at every point \(t\) the derivative \(\alpha\exp(\alpha t + \beta)\).
  3. For all real \(a\) and \(b\), \(\exp(a + b) = \exp(a)\exp(b)\) (figure 9.21). It follows that \(\exp(-a) = 1/\exp(a)\) and \(\exp(n a) = \exp(a)^n\) for every integer \(n\), where for \(n < 0\) the power is \(x^n = 1/x^{-n}\).
  4. For an integer \(p\), \(\exp(p)\) is the power \(\mathrm{e}^p\) of the number \(\mathrm{e}\). For integers \(p\) and \(q > 0\), \(\exp(p/q)\) is the only positive number whose \(q\)-th power is \(\mathrm{e}^p\).
Proof

We must show four items. The first comes from lemma 9.16, the second from lemma 8.9 (mirroring a tangent), the third from the fact that \(\ln\) turns a product into a sum, the fourth from the third.

1. Bijection.

  1. By lemma 9.16, items 1 and 3, \(\ln\) is a strictly increasing bijection onto all real numbers, so its inverse function \(\exp\) is defined for every \(y\) and bijective. The equality \(\ln(\exp y) = y\) is the definition, \(\exp(\ln x) = x\) follows from bijectivity.
  2. From \(\ln 1 = 0\) (the point \((1, 0)\)), \(\exp(0) = 1\) (the point \((0, 1)\)). If \(y < y'\) and \(\exp(y) \ge \exp(y')\), then \(y = \ln(\exp y) \ge \ln(\exp y') = y'\), because \(\ln\) is increasing, which is false. So \(\exp\) is strictly increasing.

2. Derivative.

  1. Let \(y_0\) be a real number and \(x_0 = \exp(y_0)\). As in step 2 of item 3 of lemma 9.16 there is a natural number \(n > 2|y_0|\) for which \(\ln(2^{-n}) \le -n/2 < y_0 < n/2 \le \ln(2^n)\).
  2. Lemma 8.9 holds for the continuous strictly monotone function \(\ln\) on the closed interval \([2^{-n}, 2^n]\). The inverse function on the interval \(J\) with end points \(\ln(2^{-n})\) and \(\ln(2^n)\) is \(\exp\). The point \(x_0\) lies inside \([2^{-n}, 2^n]\), because \(\ln\) is strictly increasing and \(y_0\) lies between the end points.
  3. The derivative \(\ln' (x_0) = 1/x_0 \ne 0\) (item 1 of lemma 9.16). So by lemma 8.9, item 2, the inverse function on \(J\) is differentiable at \(y_0\) with derivative \(1/\ln'(x_0) = x_0\). The point \(y_0\) lies inside \(J\), so for small enough steps the difference quotient of \(\exp\) at \(y_0\) uses only values on \(J\). Hence \(\exp'(y_0) = x_0 = \exp(y_0)\). In figure 9.20 this is the mirroring of a tangent: the tangent of the graph of \(\ln\) at the point \((\mathrm{e}, 1)\) has, for a vertical step \(h\), the horizontal step \(\mathrm{e}\,h\), so the slope \(1/\mathrm{e}\). Mirroring swaps the horizontal and the vertical step, so the tangent of the graph of \(\exp\) at the point \((1, \mathrm{e})\) has the slope \(\mathrm{e}\), the reciprocal.
  4. The derivative is \(\exp\) itself, which is continuous because it is differentiable (lemma 1.80). Hence \(\exp\) is continuously differentiable.
  5. Let \(\alpha = 0\). Then \(t \mapsto \exp(\beta)\) is constant and has the derivative \(0 = \alpha\exp(\beta)\) (lemma 1.82, item 4).
  6. Let \(\alpha \ne 0\), \(s = \alpha t + \beta\), \(h \ne 0\) and \(u = \alpha h \ne 0\). The difference quotient is \(\big(\exp(s + u) - \exp(s)\big)/h = \alpha\,\big(\exp(s + u) - \exp(s)\big)/u\). By step 3 the quotient in \(u\) converges to \(\exp(s)\). For an error \(\eta\) let \(\delta'\) be a band around \(u = 0\) in which the quotient in \(u\) differs from \(\exp(s)\) by less than \(\eta/|\alpha|\). For \(0 < |h| < \delta'/|\alpha|\), \(0 < |u| < \delta'\), and the quotient in \(h\) differs from \(\alpha\exp(s)\) by less than \(\eta\). The derivative is therefore \(\alpha\exp(\alpha t + \beta)\), as in theorem 8.13, part 4.

Above is the quotient in \(u\) over the axis \(u\), which tends to the value \(\exp(s)\) as \(u \to 0\). Around it is the band of height \(\eta/|\alpha|\) that the quotient enters when \(|u| < \delta'\). Below it, the quotient in \(h\) is multiplied by \(\alpha\), so the band becomes \(\alpha\) times higher, that is \(\eta\), around \(\alpha\exp(s)\), and the horizontal step on the axis \(h\) is \(\alpha\) times smaller, so \(|h| < \delta'/|\alpha|\). The dashed arrows carry the ends of the interval from the axis \(u\) to the axis \(h\), because \(h = u/\alpha\).

3. Product.

  1. Let \(x = \exp(a)\) and \(y = \exp(b)\). By lemma 9.16, item 2, \(\ln(x y) = \ln x + \ln y = a + b\). The definition gives \(\exp(a + b) = x y\).
  2. For \(b = -a\), \(\exp(a)\exp(-a) = \exp(0) = 1\), so \(\exp(-a) = 1/\exp(a)\). For natural \(n\) induction gives \(\exp((n + 1) a) = \exp(n a)\exp(a) = \exp(a)^{n+1}\), for \(n = 0\), \(\exp(0) = 1\), and for \(n = -m < 0\), \(\exp(-m a) = 1/\exp(m a) = 1/\exp(a)^m\).

4. Rational exponent.

  1. By item 3 with \(a = 1\), \(\exp(p) = \exp(1)^p = \mathrm{e}^p\), a power with an integer exponent.
  2. By item 3 with \(a = p/q\) and \(n = q\), \(\exp(p/q)^q = \exp(p) = \mathrm{e}^p\). The number \(\exp(p/q)\) is positive by item 1.

9.2.3 Equal steps, equal ratios

Now we can answer the question from the beginning of the section. By theorem 9.18, item 2, the derivative of the function \(y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{a t}\) is \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{a t}\), so \(y' = a y\): the rate of change is proportional to the value. In a time \(\Delta t\) the value is multiplied by the number \(\mathrm{e}^{a (t + \Delta t)}/\mathrm{e}^{a t} = \mathrm{e}^{a\,\Delta t}\) (theorem 9.18, item 3), so it changes by the fraction \(\mathrm{e}^{a\,\Delta t} - 1\), however large it is. For \(a < 0\) this is a loss of the same fraction. For a short \(\Delta t\) this fraction is close to \(a\,\Delta t\), because \(\exp'(0) = 1\) and the graph near the point \((0, 1)\) almost coincides with the tangent of slope \(1\): at \(a\,\Delta t = 0{,}1\) it is \(0{,}10517\), which is 5,2 % more than \(a\,\Delta t\), and at \(a\,\Delta t = -0{,}1\) it is \(-0{,}09516\), which is 4,8 % less in size. The graph lies above the tangent on both sides, so the growth is somewhat larger and the loss somewhat smaller than \(a\,\Delta t\). Is this the only such function?

Lemma 9.19 The equation of exponential growth and decay (enačba za eksponentno rast in upadanje)

Let \(I\) be an interval (definition 1.44), \(a\) a real number, \(t_0 \in I\) and \(y_0\) a real number (an example in figure 9.21).

  1. The function \(y(t) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\) is differentiable on \(I\), \(y' = a y\) and \(y(t_0) = y_0\). If \(y\) is any function differentiable on \(I\) with \(y' = a y\) and \(y(t_0) = y_0\), then \(y(t) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\) for every \(t \in I\).
  2. For a solution with \(y_0 \ne 0\) and all \(t\), \(t + s\) in \(I\), \(y(t + s)/y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{a s}\). The ratio depends only on the step \(s\) (the arcs).
  3. Let \(y\) be a differentiable function on \(\mathbb R\) that is nowhere zero, and let the ratio \(y(t + s)/y(t)\) not depend on \(t\). Then \(y' = a y\) for some number \(a\) (figure 9.21).
Proof

We must show three items. In the first, the ratio of a solution to the function \(\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\) is constant because its derivative is zero, the second is a rewriting of the first, the third expresses the derivative through the ratio per step.

1. Unique solution.

  1. Let \(\varphi(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-a (t - t_0)}\). By theorem 9.18, item 2, with \(\alpha = -a\) and \(\beta = a t_0\), \(\varphi'(t) = -a\,\varphi(t)\). With \(\alpha = a\), \(\beta = -a t_0\) and the rule for a multiple (lemma 1.82, item 2), \(Y(t) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\) is differentiable with derivative \(a\,Y(t)\), and \(Y(t_0) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^0 = y_0\). This proves the first part of the first item.
  2. Now let \(y\) be a solution and \(u(t) = y(t)\,\varphi(t)\). By the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3), \(u'(t) = y'(t)\varphi(t) + y(t)\varphi'(t) = a y\,\varphi - a y\,\varphi = 0\) on \(I\).
  3. For \(t > t_0\) in \(I\), \(u\) is continuous on \([t_0, t]\) and \(u' = 0\) in the interior, so by corollary 1.86, \(u(t) = u(t_0) = y_0\). For \(t < t_0\) the same holds on \([t, t_0]\) and \(u(t_0) = u(t)\).
  4. Hence \(y(t)\varphi(t) = y_0\). Since \(\varphi(t) > 0\) and \(\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)} = \mathrm{e}^{0} = 1\) (theorem 9.18, item 3), \(y(t) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\).

2. Ratio.

  1. By item 1 and theorem 9.18, item 3, \(y(t + s) = y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a (t - t_0)}\,\mathrm{e}^{a s} = y(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{a s}\). We divide by \(y(t) \ne 0\). For \(a = -0{,}354175\) 1/s and \(s = 1\) s this is the number \(0{,}7018\) above the arcs.

3. The ratio determines the derivative.

  1. Let \(\rho(s) = y(t + s)/y(t)\), independent of \(t\). For \(t = 0\), \(\rho(s) = y(s)/y(0)\), so by the rule for a multiple (lemma 1.82, item 2) \(\rho\) is differentiable at \(0\) with \(\rho'(0) = y'(0)/y(0)\). We call this number \(a\).
  2. For every \(t\), \(y(t + h) = \rho(h)\,y(t)\) and \(\rho(0) = 1\), so the difference quotient is \((y(t + h) - y(t))/h = y(t)\,(\rho(h) - \rho(0))/h\). As \(h\) tends to \(0\), this quotient converges to \(y(t)\,\rho'(0) = a\,y(t)\). Hence \(y'(t) = a\,y(t)\).

9.2.4 Growth and the integral to infinity

For \(a > 0\) the function \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) decays to zero. But how fast? Solutions of the vibration equation will also contain products \(t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\), for example a motion of the form \((c_1 + c_2 t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) at critical damping \(c = c_{\mathrm{kr}}\) (definition 9.5), which we will derive in section 9.5. There a decaying function is multiplied by a growing one. Which one wins? Figure 9.23 below shows two examples: the function \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\) and the function \(t^3\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\) first grow, then decay towards zero. That this holds for every power follows from an estimate by a polynomial.

Lemma 9.20 Growth of the exponential function (rast eksponentne funkcije)
  1. For every integer \(n \ge 0\) and every \(t \ge 0\) (the polynomials under \(\mathrm{e}^s\)),
    \[\mathrm{e}^t \ge 1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2!} + \dots + \frac{t^n}{n!} . \tag{9.24}\]
  2. For every integer \(n \ge 0\) and every \(a > 0\), the function \(t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) converges to zero as \(t\) grows beyond all bounds (the example \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\)). This means: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is \(T\) such that \(t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t} < \eta\) for every \(t > T\).
Proof

We must show both items. We prove the first by induction, in which we integrate an inequality (the areas under the two curves), and we get the second from the first with a suitable choice of \(n\) (the bound \(2/t\)).

1. Estimate.

  1. Start, \(n = 0\). For \(t \ge 0\), \(\exp(t) \ge \exp(0) = 1 = P_0\), because \(\exp\) is increasing (theorem 9.18, item 1).
  2. Induction step. Let \(\mathrm{e}^s \ge P_{\mathrm{n}}(s) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^n s^k/k!\) hold for some \(n\) and every \(s \ge 0\). By monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.54, item 4), for \(t > 0\), \(\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^s\,\mathrm{d}s \ge \int_0^t P_{\mathrm{n}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). In the figure this is the case \(n = 1\): the area under \(\mathrm{e}^s\) up to \(t\), grey and blue together, is larger than the grey area under the line \(P_1\), because the curve \(\mathrm{e}^s\) lies above it.
  3. By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 1) the left integral is \(\mathrm{e}^t - \mathrm{e}^0 = \mathrm{e}^t - 1\), because \(\exp' = \exp\). The function \(s^{k+1}/(k+1)!\) has the derivative \(s^k/k!\) (lemma 1.82, items 5 and 2), and a sum of such functions has the sum of the derivatives (item 1), so the right integral is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^n t^{k+1}/(k+1)! = P_{\mathrm{n}+1}(t) - 1\). For \(n = 1\) and \(t = 1{,}5\) the grey area is \(1{,}5 + 1{,}5^2/2 = 2{,}625 = P_2(1{,}5) - 1\), and grey and blue together are \(\mathrm{e}^{1{,}5} - 1 = 3{,}4817\).
  4. Hence \(\mathrm{e}^t - 1 \ge P_{\mathrm{n}+1}(t) - 1\): the curve \(P_2\) lies under \(\mathrm{e}^s\). At \(t = 0\) the inequality is an equality.

2. Convergence.

  1. Let \(\eta > 0\), \(n \ge 0\) an integer and \(a > 0\). By the first item with \(n + 1\) in place of \(n\) and \(at\) in place of \(t\), \(\mathrm{e}^{a t} \ge (a t)^{n+1}/(n + 1)!\) for \(t \ge 0\), because all terms of the sum are nonnegative and we dropped all but the last (the last term \(s^2/2\) lies lower still than \(P_2\)).
  2. Hence for \(t > 0\), by theorem 9.18, item 3, \(0 < t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t} = t^n/\mathrm{e}^{a t} \le (n + 1)!\,/\,(a^{n+1}\,t)\). For \(n = 1\) and \(a = 1\) this is the bound \(2/t\) above the function \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\).
  3. We choose \(T = (n + 1)!\,/\,(a^{n+1}\eta)\). For \(t > T\) the right side is smaller than \(\eta\). In the figure, for \(\eta = 0{,}25\) the limit \(T = 2/\eta = 8\): to the right of it the bound and the function are below the height \(\eta\).

Definition 9.21 Integral to infinity (integral do neskončnosti)

Let \(g\) be a function piecewise continuous on every closed interval \([0, T]\) with \(T > 0\) (definition 6.8), which we write briefly as piecewise continuous on \([0, \infty) = \{t \in \mathbb R : t \ge 0\}\). A continuous function is a special case with a single partition. Then the integral \(\int_0^T g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists (definition](9.24:pas1,t01) 6.9). We say that the integral \(\int_0^\infty g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists and equals the number \(I\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is \(T_0\) such that \(\left|\int_0^T g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t - I\right| < \eta\) for every \(T > T_0\) ([the band). We write \(\int_0^\infty g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = I\) or \(I = \lim_{\mathrm{T}\to\infty}\int_0^T g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).

Source: LEBL1 definition 5.5.1 (PDF p. 214): the improper integral as a limit of integrals. The formulation with the error \(\eta\) is ours.

Lemma 9.22 Existence of an integral to infinity (obstoj integrala do neskončnosti)

Let \(g\) and \(G\) be piecewise continuous functions on \([0, \infty)\) with \(|g(t)| \le G(t)\) for every \(t \ge 0\) that is not a break point (the values at break points do not affect the integral). If the integral \(\int_0^\infty G(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists, then \(\int_0^\infty g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists too (figure 9.25).

Proof

We must show that \(\int_0^T g\) settles for large \(T\). We split the function \(g\) into two nonnegative parts, the positive part \(g^+\) and the negative part \(g^-\), each of which has an increasing integral bounded from above. We work with piecewise continuous functions, so on every closed piece of a partition we compute with a continuous function that agrees with the given one on the open piece.

  1. Let \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) be a piece of the common partition of \(g\) and \(G\) on \([0, T]\) and \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(G_{\mathrm{k}}\) the continuous functions of the piece. The function \(|g_{\mathrm{k}}|\) is continuous, because \(\big|\,|g(t)| - |g(c)|\,\big| \le |g(t) - g(c)|\) by the triangle inequality (lemma 1.6, item 1), applied to \(g(t) = (g(t) - g(c)) + g(c)\) and to \(g(c) = (g(c) - g(t)) + g(t)\). The functions \(g^+ = (|g| + g)/2\) and \(g^- = (|g| - g)/2\), in the figure the arcs above the axis and the mirrored arcs below it, have the continuous functions \((|g_{\mathrm{k}}| \pm g_{\mathrm{k}})/2\) on the piece (corollary 1.65), so they are piecewise continuous with the same partition, \(g = g^+ - g^-\) and \(0 \le g^\pm \le |g| \le G\) on open pieces, and by continuity on closed ones too.
  2. Let \(L = \int_0^\infty G\) and \(F(T) = \int_0^T G\). Since \(G \ge 0\), for \(T < T'\), on a partition with \(T\) as a break point (item 1 of lemma 6.10), \(F(T') - F(T) = \int_{\mathrm{T}}^{T'} G \ge 0\) by nonnegativity (item 3 of the same lemma), so \(F\) is increasing. If \(F(T_1) > L\) for some \(T_1\), then \(F(T) - L \ge F(T_1) - L > 0\) for all \(T > T_1\), which contradicts definition 9.21 with \(\eta = F(T_1) - L\). Hence \(F(T) \le L\) for every \(T\). In the figure the curve \(F\) rises towards the level \(L\) and never exceeds it.
  3. For \(h \in \{g^+, g^-\}\) let \(H(T) = \int_0^T h\). As in step 2, \(H\) is increasing, and \(H(T) \le F(T) \le L\), because \(F - H = \int_0^T (G - h) \ge 0\) by linearity and nonnegativity (items 2 and 3 of lemma 6.10). The sequence \(H(1), H(2), \dots\) is increasing and bounded, so by theorem 1.52 it converges to its supremum \(s\). In the figure these are the stepped curves \(H^+\) and \(H^-\): \(H^+\) grows only on the arcs above the axis, \(H^-\) only on the arcs below it, both under \(F\). The points \(H^+(1), H^+(2), \dots\) pile up under the level \(s^+\).
  4. Let \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 1.34, item 2, there is a natural number \(n_0\) with \(H(n_0) > s - \eta\). For every \(T \ge n_0\), \(H(n_0) \le H(T)\), and for a natural \(n \ge T\), which exists by the Archimedean property (theorem 1.36), \(H(T) \le H(n) \le s\). Hence \(|H(T) - s| < \eta\) and \(\int_0^\infty h = s\). The curve \(H^+\) thus comes arbitrarily close to the level \(s^+\) and stays there.
  5. Let \(s^+\) and \(s^-\) be the numbers for \(g^+\) and \(g^-\). By linearity (lemma 6.10, item 2), \(\int_0^T g = \int_0^T g^+ - \int_0^T g^-\). For \(\eta > 0\) we choose by step 4 a \(T_0\) such that \(|\int_0^T g^+ - s^+| < \eta/2\) and \(|\int_0^T g^- - s^-| < \eta/2\) for every \(T > T_0\). For \(T > T_0\), \(|\int_0^T g - (s^+ - s^-)| < \eta\). In the figure \(\int_0^T g\) is the difference of the heights of the curves \(H^+\) and \(H^-\), which settles at \(s^+ - s^- = 0{,}9461 - 0{,}8603 = 0{,}0859\).

What if the displacement does not decay? Take the same form with \(a = 0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 1\ \text{1/s}\), that is \(g(t) = \cos t\). We show that for this function the integral to infinity does not exist. The definition says that a number \(I\) is not the integral if there is an error \(\eta\) such that for every \(T_0\) there is a \(T > T_0\) with \(\left|\int_0^T g - I\right| \ge \eta\). Here \(\int_0^T \cos t\,\mathrm{d}t = \sin T\) (theorem 3.55, item 1, and theorem 8.13). In figure 9.24 the positive and negative areas under \(\cos t\) alternate, so the area up to \(T\) swings. At \(T = \pi/2 + 2\pi j\) we have \(\sin T = 1\), at \(T = 3\pi/2 + 2\pi j\) we have \(\sin T = -1\), and both kinds of points reach beyond every \(T_0\). If both values were less than \(1/2\) away from \(I\), we would have \(2 = |1 - (-1)| \le |1 - I| + |I + 1| < 1\), which is impossible. Lemma 9.22 does not help here, since the only upper bound is \(G = 1\), and its integral \(\int_0^T G = T\) is not bounded. Decay of the function alone is not enough either: \(g(t) = 1/(1 + t)\) tends to zero, but \(\int_0^T g = \ln(1 + T)\). With the error \(\eta = 1\) and an arbitrary \(I\) we have \(\ln(1 + T) \ge I + 1\) for every \(T \ge \mathrm{e}^{I+1}\), so no \(T_0\) fits: the curve leaves the band \(I \pm 1\) and does not return to it. For the displacement of system A the upper bound \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) has a bounded integral, so the lemma holds.

For power functions the integral can be computed exactly.

Lemma 9.23 The integral of a power times a decaying exponential to infinity (integral potence s padajočo eksponentno funkcijo do neskončnosti)

For every integer \(n \ge 0\) and every \(a > 0\) the integral \(\int_0^\infty t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists (figure 9.24) and equals

\[\int_0^\infty t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{n!}{a^{n+1}} . \tag{9.25}\]
Proof

We must show that the integrand has an antiderivative of the form (polynomial) times \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) and that its term at the upper limit vanishes: this is the area to the right of \(T\), which tends to zero. The antiderivative is found by repeated integration by parts (corollary 3.56), here we write it down and check it by differentiation.

  1. Let \(P_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^n \dfrac{n!}{k!\,a^{n-k+1}}\,t^k\) and \(F(t) = -\mathrm{e}^{-a t}P_{\mathrm{n}}(t)\). The function \(\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) has the derivative \(-a\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\) (theorem 9.18, item 2), so \(F'(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-a t}\big(a P_{\mathrm{n}}(t) - P_{\mathrm{n}}'(t)\big)\) by the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3).
  2. The term \(k\) of the polynomial \(a P_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(\dfrac{n!}{k!\,a^{n-k}}\,t^k\). The term \(k\) of the polynomial \(P_{\mathrm{n}}'\) is \(\dfrac{n!}{(k-1)!\,a^{n-k+1}}\,t^{k-1}\) for \(k \ge 1\), after renaming \(k - 1 \to k\) this is the term \(\dfrac{n!}{k!\,a^{n-k}}\,t^k\) for \(k = 0, \dots, n - 1\). The difference \(a P_{\mathrm{n}} - P_{\mathrm{n}}'\) therefore keeps only the term with \(k = n\), which is \(t^n\). Hence \(F'(t) = t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\).
  3. By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 1), \(\int_0^T t^n\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}\,\mathrm{d}t = F(T) - F(0) = \dfrac{n!}{a^{n+1}} - \mathrm{e}^{-a T}P_{\mathrm{n}}(T)\), because \(P_{\mathrm{n}}(0) = n!/a^{n+1}\). For \(n = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(T = 3\) this is \(1 - 4\,\mathrm{e}^{-3} = 0{,}8009\), and the term \(\mathrm{e}^{-3}P_1(3) = 4\,\mathrm{e}^{-3} = 0{,}1991\) is the area to the right of \(T\).
  4. The last term is a sum of \(n + 1\) terms of the form \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\,T^k\,\mathrm{e}^{-a T}\) with constants \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\), each of which converges to zero by item 2 of lemma 9.20 (the term \(k = 0\) is \(T^0\,\mathrm{e}^{-a T}\)). For an error \(\eta\) we choose \(T_0\) such that \(T^k\,\mathrm{e}^{-a T} < \eta/((n + 1)(|c_{\mathrm{k}}| + 1))\) for every \(k\) and every \(T > T_0\) (the largest of the \(n + 1\) thresholds of the lemma). Then \(|\mathrm{e}^{-a T}P_{\mathrm{n}}(T)| < \eta\), and by definition 9.21 the integral equals \(n!/a^{n+1}\). In the figure the remainder shrinks towards zero as \(T\) grows.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

Two examples: for \(n = 1\) and \(a = 1\), \(\int_0^T t\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1 - (1 + T)\,\mathrm{e}^{-T}\), in figure 9.24 the curve. At \(T = 10\) this is \(0{,}9995\), and the limit is \(1 = 1!/1^2\). For \(n = 3\), \(\int_0^\infty t^3\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t = 3! = 6\) (SymPy), as equation (9.25) says. Sections 9.10 and 9.12 use both lemmas: for the convolution over the whole past and for its existence under a periodic force.

At the beginning we asked which function turns equal steps into equal ratios and what its derivative is. The answer is \(y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a t}\) with the derivative \(a\,y_0\,\mathrm{e}^{a t}\), for system A the envelope of the peaks, and we built the function from the area under \(1/t\), without powers with real exponents and without series.
