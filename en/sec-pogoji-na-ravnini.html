Chapter 19 Symmetry, periodicity and constraints

19.2 Conditions on the plane of symmetry and the half model

Section 19.1 showed that the solution of a symmetric problem cannot differ from its mirror image: for a symmetric load theorem 19.4 gives \(\vek u(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\), for an antisymmetric load \(\vek u(\vek x) = -\mat Z\,\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\), and for the frame model theorem 19.6 gives \(\vek d = \mat P\vek d\) or \(\vek d = -\mat P\vek d\). What does this save us in the computation? If the right half is the image of the left half, we need not compute the right one. But we cannot simply cut out the left half and leave it free, because at the cut, in the plane of symmetry (the plane of reflection \(\Pi\), with respect to which the structure is mirror symmetric), it was held by the right half. The question of this section is therefore: with what support at the cut do we replace the removed half, so that the left half receives the same displacements and forces as in the whole? And what happens to the load and the stiffness that lie on the plane itself?

We test the answer on the portal frame of section 19.1 (figure 19.5): columns \(AB\) and \(EC\) \(1\) m high, clamped at \(A\) and \(E\), beam \(BC\) \(1\) m long with node \(D\) in the middle, steel \(E = 196\) GPa, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm (height \(40\) mm in the plane of the frame), \(EA = 1{,}568 \cdot 10^8\) N and \(EI = 20\,907\) N m\(^2\), the columns each made of four frame elements, the beam of eight, four on each half \(BD\) and \(DC\), in all \(17\) nodes and \(45\) free unknowns. The model is the one of chapter 12: an Euler-Bernoulli frame with small displacements and neglected shear deformation. We check smallness on the solution below, for a horizontal force of \(500\) N at \(B\): the largest displacement is \(1{,}43\) mm over a length of \(1\) m (in the figure the deformation, magnified \(100\) times), that is \(1{,}4 \cdot 10^{-3}\) of the length, and the largest rotation is \(1{,}9 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad (in the columns). Equilibrium on the undeformed shape neglects that the deformation moves the points of application of the forces by the displacements and turns the directions of the elements by the rotations. The neglected terms are therefore of order \(2 \cdot 10^{-3}\) of the kept ones, and the computation is accurate to a few parts per thousand. For this frame we write \(\mat P\) for the mirror matrix (definition 19.5) and \(\mat Z\) for the reflection of points of space.

Let us draw the frame part by part. First the frame: columns \(AB\) and \(EC\) are clamped at \(A\) and \(E\), the beam joins \(B\) and \(C\), and its midpoint is \(D\). Through \(D\) we place the plane of symmetry \(\Pi\), vertical and perpendicular to the plane of the frame, so in the figure we see it as a line. We call it so because we can set it up as a mirror: the frame in the mirror is the same frame. The mirror arrow shows where the mirror sends the nodes: \(B\) to \(C\), \(A\) to \(E\), and \(D\) to \(D\), since it lies on the plane. Finally the force \(H\) at \(B\), directed towards \(C\). In the middle and right figures it is the same frame (middle, right) with the same plane and with the deformation that the computation gives for the load of the right figure. The midpoint \(D\) of the beam lies in the plane before the deformation and moves horizontally, perpendicular to the plane, as the frame deforms.

The load \(H\) is neither symmetric nor antisymmetric. The mirror would send the force at \(B\) to a force at \(C\) in the opposite direction, which is neither the same load nor its opposite. How to split such a load into two parts, each symmetric or antisymmetric by itself, is the subject of the next section. Here we only add: a pair of forces that mirror each other, \(\tfrac12 H = 250\) N at \(B\) to the right and \(250\) N at \(C\) to the left, is mapped by the mirror into itself, a pair of forces in the same direction, \(250\) N at \(B\) and \(250\) N at \(C\) to the right, is mapped by the mirror into its opposite, since the mirror image would point at \(C\) and \(B\) to the left. The sum at \(B\) is \(250 + 250 = 500\) N, at \(C\) it is \(-250 + 250 = 0\), so this is the original load \(H\).

What does the computation of the whole frame show on the plane? We build the mirror matrix \(\mat P\) by the rule: node \(i\) goes along the mirror arrow to the mirror node \(\bar i\), the horizontal displacement changes sign because the mirror reverses the direction of the \(x\) axis, the vertical one stays, and the rotation changes sign because a counterclockwise rotation is seen in the mirror as a clockwise one (a wheel turning left turns right in the mirror). So \((\mat P\vek d)_{\bar{i}} = (-u_{\mathrm{i}},\ v_{\mathrm{i}},\ -\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\), the block \(\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, -1)\) at each node. A NumPy computation confirms \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) and \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\). For the two parts of the load the computed solutions at \(D\) are:

symmetric \(< 10^{-19}\) m \(-0{,}00020\) mm \(< 10^{-19}\)
antisymmetric \(1{,}4247\) mm \(< 10^{-16}\) m \(0{,}000424\)

For the symmetric load \(u_{\mathrm{D}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}\) are zero but \(v_{\mathrm{D}}\) is not. For the antisymmetric load it is exactly the opposite, \(v_{\mathrm{D}}\) is zero. Is this an accident of this frame? No: why, the theorem shows, first for a body, then for a frame.

For the body we ask what the mirror image and Newton's third law said about a point on the plane. A point \(\vek x\) on the plane is its own mirror image. If the load is symmetric, then under reflection everything that is displaced coincides with its image, so the point cannot move perpendicular to the plane: a displacement to one side would have its image on the other side, but both would be the displacement of the same point. It can slide along the plane. The same holds for the force with which the right half acts on the left. The force of the left half on the right is, by Newton's third law, its opposite, and its mirror image must be that force, since the state is symmetric. A force that is the opposite of its mirror image is perpendicular to the plane, since reflection leaves the tangential component and reverses the perpendicular one. The tangential (shear) component of the force on the plane is therefore zero. For an antisymmetric load the mirror image of the state is the opposite state, so everything is reversed: the point slides only perpendicular to the plane and the force has no component perpendicular to it. The picture of this argument is the displacement of a point on the plane: a vector equal to its own mirror image lies in the plane, a vector equal to the opposite of its image is perpendicular to it. We carry out the proof without further pictures, from theorem 19.4.

Theorem 19.7 Conditions on the plane of symmetry (pogoji na ravnini simetrije)

Let the boundary value problem be symmetric or antisymmetric with respect to the plane \(\Pi\) with unit normal \(\vek m\) and reflection \(\mat Z\) (definition 19.3), and let it satisfy the hypotheses of theorem 19.4. Let \(\vek u\) be the solution with stress \(\ten\sigma\), continuous up to the plane, \(\vek x\) a point on \(\Pi\) and \(\vek t = \ten\sigma(\vek x)\,\vek m\) the traction vector on a surface with normal \(\vek m\). Then:

  1. for a symmetric load \(\vek m\cdot\vek u(\vek x) = 0\) (the displacement has no perpendicular component) and \(\vek t = (\vek m\cdot\vek t)\,\vek m\) (the shear component of the traction vector is zero),
  2. for an antisymmetric load \(\vek u(\vek x) = (\vek m\cdot\vek u)\,\vek m\) (the displacement has no component in the plane) and \(\vek m\cdot\vek t = 0\) (the perpendicular component of the traction vector is zero).
Proof

We must show that the equality of stresses from theorem 19.4 at a point on the plane contradicts itself unless the stated components vanish. The sign \(\pm\) means \(+\) for a symmetric and \(-\) for an antisymmetric load.

  1. Displacement. For \(\vek x\in\Pi\) we have \(\mat Z\vek x = \vek x\) (lemma 19.2, item 2), so by item 1 of theorem 19.4 \(\vek u(\vek x) = \pm\,\mat Z\,\vek u(\vek x)\). By definition (19.1) \(\mat Z\vek u = \vek u - 2\vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek u)\). For the sign \(+\) it follows that \(\vek m\cdot\vek u = 0\), for the sign \(-\) that \(\vek u = -\vek u + 2\vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek u)\), so \(\vek u = \vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek u)\). This is item 3 of theorem 19.4, which is here the first part of the claim.
  2. Stress. By item 2 of theorem 19.4 \(\ten\sigma(\vek x) = \pm\,\mat Z\,\ten\sigma(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\). For \(\vek x\in\Pi\) this is \(\ten\sigma = \pm\,\mat Z\ten\sigma\mat Z\).
  3. Multiply by \(\vek m\) and use \(\mat Z\vek m = \vek m - 2\vek m = -\vek m\) (lemma 19.2): \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\vek m = \pm\,\mat Z\ten\sigma\mat Z\vek m = \mp\,\mat Z\,\ten\sigma\vek m = \mp\,\mat Z\,\vek t\).
  4. For the sign \(+\) (symmetric case) we have \(\vek t = -\mat Z\vek t = -\vek t + 2\vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek t)\), so \(\vek t = \vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek t)\). For the sign \(-\) (antisymmetric case) we have \(\vek t = \mat Z\vek t = \vek t - 2\vek m(\vek m\cdot\vek t)\), so \(\vek m\cdot\vek t = 0\). This is the second part of the claim.

Source: COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69): the rules S1 "Translational motion has no component normal to a plane of symmetry" and A1 "Translational motion has no component in a plane of antisymmetry", ONATE1 §9.6.1 (PDF p. 347) the same rules. The conditions for the traction vector and the proof are not from the source.

The theorem says what the half we keep must have at the cut. The symmetric case: the removed half is replaced by a smooth wall. Its wall with rollers allows sliding along the plane, so the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\) along the plane is free and the displacement perpendicular to it is zero (first part of the theorem), and the rollers can push only perpendicular to the wall, with a force \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\), so the shear component of the force is zero. The antisymmetric case is the twin: the removed half is replaced by a rod in a sleeve, which allows only a displacement perpendicular \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the sleeve pushes it only along the plane, with a force \(t_{\mathrm{t}}\). The first support acts as a roller, the second as a rod in a sleeve, and each allows what the other forbids. The plane \(\Pi\) is dashed in both figures, since it is absent after the cut.

The conditions hold only if the stiffness is symmetric as well. The frame with a rotated right column from section 19.1 has symmetric geometry, supports and load, but the stiffness is not (\(\mat P\mat K\mat P \ne \mat K\)), and under a vertical force of \(500\) N at \(D\) the point \(D\) moves horizontally by \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = 0{,}2134\) mm. The condition \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\), which the table showed for equal columns and a symmetric load, therefore fails: theorem 19.6 requires \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\), so the solution is not symmetric and the condition \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\) has no basis. A half model that enforced \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\) would miss all of the horizontal motion of the point \(D\), that is \(0{,}2134\) mm.

In a frame the unknowns are not a displacement field but \(u\), \(v\) and \(\varphi\) of the nodes, so we prove the theorem for a frame directly with the matrix \(\mat P\). The rotation in the plane of the frame is a rotation vector along the \(z\) axis, which lies in \(\Pi\), so the rule S2 of COOK ("rotation vectors have no components in a plane of symmetry") forbids a rotation in the symmetric case. Let us prove it.

We build the picture for both cases. The plane \(\Pi\) runs through the node \(J\). Left of it is the element \(e_1\), right of it its mirror image \(e_2\). In the left picture, for the symmetric load, the rotation of the node is forbidden and so is the horizontal displacement, only the vertical one is free. The force \(\vek F_1\) of the element \(e_1\) on \(J\) and the force \(\vek F_2\) of the element \(e_2\) have the same vertical component and opposite horizontal ones, so in the sum the horizontal part and the moment cancel and only the vertical force \(2Y_1\) remains. In the right picture, for the antisymmetric load, the vertical displacement is forbidden. The force \(\vek F_1\) and the force \(\vek F_2\) now have the same horizontal component and the same moment, the vertical components are opposite, so in the sum the vertical part cancels and the horizontal force \(2X_1\) and the moment \(2M_1\) remain.

Corollary 19.8 Conditions at a frame node on the plane of symmetry (pogoji v vozlišču okvirja na ravnini simetrije)

Let a plane frame (definition 5.93), divided into frame elements (definition 12.17) with no loads on the elements, be symmetric with respect to a vertical plane \(\Pi\) perpendicular to the plane of the frame, and let \(\vek d\) be the solution of theorem 12.20 for a symmetric (\(\mat P\vek f = \vek f\)) or antisymmetric (\(\mat P\vek f = -\vek f\)) load. Let \(J\) be a node on \(\Pi\) with no external force, in which exactly two elements meet, \(e_1\) left of \(\Pi\) and its mirror image \(e_2\). Let \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} = (X_{\mathrm{i}}, Y_{\mathrm{i}}, M_{\mathrm{i}})^\T\) be the column of the components of force and moment with which the element \(e_{\mathrm{i}}\) acts on the node \(J\) (global axes, item 3 of theorem 12.20). Then:

  1. for a symmetric load \(u_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\) and \(\vek F_2 = (-X_1, Y_1, -M_1)^\T\) and \(Y_1 = 0\),
  2. for an antisymmetric load \(v_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\) and \(\vek F_2 = (X_1, -Y_1, M_1)^\T\) and \(X_1 = 0\) and \(M_1 = 0\).

If the member \(e_1e_2\) is horizontal, as in example A, then \(X\) is the axial force, \(Y\) the shear force and \(M\) the bending moment of the member at \(J\) (up to sign).

Proof

We must show that the mirror image of the solution at the node \(J\) gives an equality that requires the stated components to vanish, and that the equilibrium of the node requires the stated forces to vanish.

  1. By theorem 19.6 \(\vek d = \pm\,\mat P\vek d\) (\(+\) for a symmetric load). The node \(J\) is its own mirror node, so \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{J}}) = \pm(-u_{\mathrm{J}},\ v_{\mathrm{J}},\ -\varphi_{\mathrm{J}})\).
  2. For the sign \(+\) (symmetric part) we have \(u_{\mathrm{J}} = -u_{\mathrm{J}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}} = -\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\), so \(u_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\). For the sign \(-\) (antisymmetric part) we have \(v_{\mathrm{J}} = -v_{\mathrm{J}}\), so \(v_{\mathrm{J}} = 0\).
  3. The element \(e_2\) is the mirror image of the element \(e_1\): the same length, cross-section and material. We number the two nodes of \(e_2\) in the same order as their mirror images in \(e_1\). By steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 19.6 the energy of the element \(e_2\) at the column \(\vek d_2\) equals the energy of the element \(e_1\) at the column \(\vek d_1 = \mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_2\), where \(\mat P_{\mathrm{e}} = \mathrm{diag}(\mat D, \mat D)\) and \(\mat D = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, -1)\). The matrix \(\mat k_2\) is therefore the matrix of the quadratic form \(\vek d_2 \mapsto \vek d_1^\T\mat k_1\vek d_1\), so \(\mat k_2 = \mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\mat k_1\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\) (two symmetric matrices with the same quadratic form are equal, step 5 of the proof of theorem 19.6).
  4. By step 1 the displacements of the element \(e_2\) are the mirrored displacements of \(e_1\): \(\vek d_2 = \pm\,\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_1\). Hence \(\vek F_2^{\text{el}} = \mat k_2\vek d_2 = \mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\mat k_1\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}(\pm\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_1) = \pm\,\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat k_1\vek d_1 = \pm\,\mat P_{\mathrm{e}}\vek F_1^{\text{el}}\). The components of these six numbers at the node \(J\) are \(\vek F_2 = \pm\,\mat D\,\vek F_1\), which is, as the forces \(\vek F_1\) and \(\vek F_2\) show, \((-X_1, Y_1, -M_1)\) for the sign \(+\) and \((X_1, -Y_1, M_1)\) for the sign \(-\).
  5. The node \(J\) has no external force, so by item 1 of theorem 12.20 \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 = \vek 0\). For the sign \(+\) (the sum) this is \((0,\ 2Y_1,\ 0) = \vek 0\), so \(Y_1 = 0\). For the sign \(-\) (the sum) it is \((2X_1,\ 0,\ 2M_1) = \vek 0\), so \(X_1 = 0\) and \(M_1 = 0\).

Source: COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69): the rules S2 "Rotation vectors have no components in a plane of symmetry" and A2 "Rotation vectors have no components normal to a plane of antisymmetry", ONATE1 §9.6.1 (PDF p. 347). The proof and the forces in the member are not from the source.

On example A: for the symmetric part the shear force of the beam at \(D\) is zero, the axial force is \(-249{,}90\) N (the beam is compressed by almost exactly \(\tfrac12 H = 250\) N, the difference of \(0{,}10\) N is the shear force of the column, which resists the motion of \(B\) towards the middle) and the moment is \(0{,}033\) N m, and neither is zero. For the antisymmetric part the axial force and the moment are zero, the shear force is \(214{,}19\) N. The computation on the whole frame gives the same: the left and the right element act on \(D\) with opposite forces and opposite moments, and the sum is zero in both cases, as step 5 requires.

Now we have the conditions the half needs. But what about the load and the stiffness that lie on the plane itself? Chapter 7 already saw this with the bridge: the bar \(b_3t_3\) on the axis of symmetry had half the cross-section in the half model, and the load at \(b_3\) was halved (lemma 7.39). Why a half? Take a node \(J\) on the plane. Its row in the system \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f\) contains the contributions of all the elements that meet at \(J\): the elements on the left, their mirror images on the right, and the elements that lie in the plane. The left and right groups contribute equally, so twice as much as the left one alone. If the half model keeps only the left group, the row must stay the same, so the load must be halved and the contribution of the elements in the plane must be halved, and this is exactly the rule of the bridge. We prove it for every mirror symmetric frame model and both types of load.

Let us draw what the row of the node \(J\) contains. The plane \(\Pi\) is a vertical dashed line, and the node \(J\) lies on it. Left of it is the left node \(i\), held by the elements \(L\), right of it the elements \(R\), their mirror images, and on the plane itself the element \(\Pi\), which is its own image. At \(J\) act the elements \(L\) with the force \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}\), the elements \(R\) with the equal force \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{R}}\) and the element \(\Pi\) with the force \(\Sigma_\Pi\). The half model \(H\) keeps only the left half, and on the plane a support whose reaction \(r_{\mathrm{c}}\) replaces the removed half.

Lemma 19.9 Half model of a symmetric frame (polovični model simetričnega okvirja)

Let a plane frame model be mirror symmetric with respect to the plane \(\Pi\) (definition 19.5), with the matrix \(\mat K\), the displacement column \(\vek d\) and the mirror matrix \(\mat P\), so that \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) (theorem 19.6). Let the elements be left (\(L\), both nodes left of \(\Pi\) or on it, at least one left), their mirror images right (\(R\)), or lie on \(\Pi\) (\(\Pi\)-elements, both nodes on \(\Pi\), the mirror image is the element itself). Let the load be \(\vek f\) with \(\mat P\vek f = \pm\vek f\) (\(+\) symmetric, \(-\) antisymmetric). By item 3 of the definition the prescribed unknowns have zero values and map into themselves. Let the reduced matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) of the whole structure be positive definite (theorem 7.18). The half model \(H\) (polovični model), so called because it has only half of the structure, has the nodes from the left and from the plane, the elements \(L\) with stiffnesses \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), the elements \(\Pi\) with stiffnesses \(\tfrac12\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), the load \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the left nodes and \(\tfrac12\vek f_{\mathrm{J}}\) at the nodes \(J\) on \(\Pi\), the prescribed unknowns of the left half and, on the plane, those unknowns that \(\mat P\) multiplies by \(\mp 1\), that is \(u = \varphi = 0\) for the sign \(+\) and \(v = 0\) for the sign \(-\) (corollary 19.8). Then:

  1. the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}^H\) of the model \(H\) is positive definite and its solution \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) is unique,
  2. \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) is the restriction of the solution \(\vek d\) of the whole structure to the left and plane nodes, and on the right \(\vek d = \pm\,\mat P\vek d\),
  3. if only elements \(L\) and \(R\) meet at a node \(J\) on the plane, the reaction of the model \(H\) in the unknown \(c\) that the plane forbids is equal to \(-\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}\), so up to sign the force with which the left elements act on \(J\) (notation in step 2 of the proof). The support in \(H\) replaces the removed half.
Proof

We must show that \(\vek d\) solves the rows of the model \(H\), and use uniqueness, which we prove by energy.

  1. Let \(i\) be a left node and \(a\) its free unknown. In the row \(a\) of the system \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f + \vek r\) only the elements that meet at \(i\) appear, these are elements \(L\) (elements \(R\) and \(\Pi\) have no left nodes). The row is therefore equal to the row of the model \(H\) with the same column \(\vek d\) and the same right-hand side \(f_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  2. Let \(J\) be a node on \(\Pi\) and \(c\) its unknown that is not prescribed in \(H\) (\(v\) for the sign \(+\), \(u\) and \(\varphi\) for the sign \(-\)). The row \(c\) of the system contains the contribution of the elements \(L\), the elements \(R\) and the elements \(\Pi\) at \(J\): \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}} + \Sigma_{\mathrm{R}} + \Sigma_\Pi = f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\). As in step 4 of the proof of corollary 19.8, which requires only that the element \(R\) is the mirror image of the element \(L\), the force of the element \(R\) on \(J\) is \(\pm\,\mat D\) times the force of its mirror element \(L\). For the sign \(+\) the component \(c = v\) is multiplied by \(+1\), for the sign \(-\) the components \(c = u, \varphi\) are multiplied by \((-1)(-1) = +1\), so \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{R}} = \Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}\). The row is \(2\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}} + \Sigma_\Pi = f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\), that is \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}} + \tfrac12\Sigma_\Pi = \tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\), the row of the model \(H\).
  3. The prescribed unknowns of the model \(H\) are held by \(\vek d\) by step 2 of the proof of corollary 19.8, which needs only \(\vek d = \pm\,\mat P\vek d\) (the plane), and by assumption (the supports on the left). The rows of all free unknowns of the model \(H\) are satisfied by steps 1 and 2, so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d|_{\mathrm{H}}\) solves \(H\).
  4. Positive definiteness. Let \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) be a column of unknowns of the model \(H\) with zero prescribed values. We extend it to the right half with \(\mat P\): \(\hat{\vek w} = \vek w\) at the left and plane nodes, \(\hat{\vek w} = \pm\,\mat P\vek w\) at the right ones. This is well defined, because on the plane \(\vek w = \pm\,\mat P\vek w\) holds: the components that would change sign under reflection are prescribed and zero. \(\hat{\vek w} \ne \vek 0\) satisfies the prescribed conditions of the whole structure (the supports map into themselves with zero values).
  5. Energy: \(\hat{\vek w}^\T\mat K\hat{\vek w} = \sum_{\mathrm{L}}\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} + \sum_{\mathrm{R}}(\dots) + \sum_{\Pi}\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\) (item 2 of theorem 7.13). The elements \(R\) contribute the same as their images \(L\) (step 3 of the proof of corollary 19.8), so the sum is \(2\sum_{\mathrm{L}} + \sum_\Pi = 2\big(\sum_{\mathrm{L}} + \tfrac12\sum_\Pi\big) = 2\,\vek w^\T\mat K^H\vek w\). The left side is positive by the assumption on \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\), so also \(\vek w^\T\mat K^H_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek w > 0\). By theorem 6.29 \(H\) has exactly one solution, so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d|_{\mathrm{H}}\). These are items 1 and 2 (the right side by step 1 of the corollary).
  6. Let \(c\) be the unknown of the node \(J\) that the plane forbids and \(r_{\mathrm{c}}\) the reaction of the model \(H\). Its row \(c\) is \(\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}} + r_{\mathrm{c}} = \tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\). The component \(f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\) is zero, because \(\mat P\vek f = \pm\vek f\) at this component (which changes sign) gives \(f_{\mathrm{Jc}} = -f_{\mathrm{Jc}}\). So \(r_{\mathrm{c}} = -\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}\). This is item 3.

ONATE1 §9.6.1 (PDF p. 346-347). The proof is not from the source.

What have we shown? A half with the right supports at the cut does not merely approximate but exactly reproduces the solution of the whole, provided the load and the stiffness on the plane are halved. Chapter 7 proved this for the bridge with the bar \(b_3t_3\) on the axis of symmetry and symmetric vertical loads. Now it holds for every mirror symmetric frame model with supports that the mirror maps into themselves, and for both types of load. The bridge of chapter 7 is a truss with nonsymmetric supports, fixed at \(b_0\) and movable at \(b_6\), so it also needs the rigid shift from the proof of lemma 7.38.

We check it on example A. We keep the left half, that is the column \(AB\) and half of the beam \(BD\): \(9\) nodes and \(27\) unknowns. The symmetric model has a clamp at \(A\) and at \(D\) a guide where \(u = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\) and \(v\) is free, so \(27 - 3 - 2 = 22\) unknowns. The antisymmetric model has at \(D\) a roller that allows \(u\) and \(\varphi\) and forbids \(v\), so \(27 - 3 - 1 = 23\) unknowns. The sum \(22 + 23 = 45\) is the number of free unknowns of the whole, and this is no accident: every column \(\vek d\) splits in exactly one way into \(\tfrac12(\vek d + \mat P\vek d)\) and \(\tfrac12(\vek d - \mat P\vek d)\), the first symmetric, the second antisymmetric. Both halves carry the load \(\tfrac12 H\) at \(B\).

The solutions of the models agree with the whole. Symmetric model: \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 0{,}0008\) mm, \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}00020\) mm, the reaction of the guide at \(D\) is an axial force \(249{,}90\) N and a moment \(0{,}033\) N m, which is the force of the removed half (item 3 of the lemma). Antisymmetric model: \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}4247\) mm, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{D}} = 0{,}000424\) and the reaction of the roller at \(D\) is a shear force \(214{,}19\) N. The sum \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 0{,}0008 + 1{,}4247 = 1{,}4255\) mm is the horizontal displacement of \(B\) in the whole frame with \(H = 500\) N. Two computations with \(22\) and \(23\) unknowns replace one with \(45\).

What if we do not halve the stiffness of an element on the plane? Let us add to the frame a short column \(DG\) in the plane of symmetry: \(1\) m long, the same cross-section, clamped at \(G\), four elements, in all \(21\) nodes. In the whole the load \(F = 500\) N acts downwards at \(D\) on the plane. The column lies in the plane and \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) still holds (NumPy). The displacement is \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}00315\) mm: the column takes \(493{,}72\) N, each part of the beam \(3{,}14\) N of shear force (\(493{,}72 + 2 \cdot 3{,}14 = 500{,}00\) N). So little, because the column is very stiff axially, \(EA/L = 1{,}568 \cdot 10^8\) N/m, and by itself it would give \(500/(1{,}568 \cdot 10^8) = 3{,}19 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m.

Let us compare three computations. The correct half model has the left half of the frame, the column with halved stiffness, a guide at \(D\) and a force of \(250\) N, and gives \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}00315\) mm, as the whole. The wrong model has the same half of the frame and the same force, but the column with full stiffness, and gives \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}00158\) mm, half of the true displacement (ratio \(0{,}503\)). Why almost exactly half? The column carries almost the entire load, so the displacement is about the load divided by the stiffness of the column. The wrong model has half the load and full stiffness, hence half the displacement.

The same happens for the antisymmetric part of \(H\), where \(v = 0\) on the plane and the column acts in bending. The whole frame has \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 0{,}8049\) mm and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}000096\), the model with halved stiffness the same, and the model with full stiffness \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 0{,}6148\) mm (\(24\) % too small) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}000233\). Without the column \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}4247\) mm: the column, clamped at \(G\), holds back the midpoint of the beam.

Is the halved matrix really the stiffness of half of the column? If we cut the column lengthwise by the plane \(\Pi\), each strip has cross-section \(A/2\), so the axial stiffness \(EA/2\) is exactly halved. In bending about the axis through the centroid of the whole cross-section the strip has, by Steiner's theorem (theorem 5.28), \(20\cdot 20^3/12 + 400\cdot 10^2 = 13\,333 + 40\,000 = 53\,333\) mm\(^4 = I/2\), since its centroid is \(10\) mm from the axis and its area is \(400\) mm\(^2\). The halving is therefore also in bending the stiffness of a strip that remains connected to the axis of the whole column. If we took the strip as an independent beam with its own axis, we would get only \(I/8\) (\(13\,333\) mm\(^4\)) and an error.

The error is not caught by the sum of reactions, since the model with the wrong stiffness is again a correctly solved system, as with the wrong support of the bridge (chapter 7). It is caught by the comparison with the whole and by the rule of the lemma: an element on the plane gets half of the stiffness, just as it gets half of the load.

The conditions on the plane hold for each part of the load separately. The next section therefore answers the question that example A only hinted at: how to split an arbitrary load into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part and how to add the solutions.
