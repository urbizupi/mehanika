Chapter 1 Strain

1.11 Mathematics: subsequences

Section 1.10 ended with bisection, which found the number \(\sqrt 3\). At every step we knew which half to keep, and the left endpoints converged to \(\sqrt 3\). In this section we apply bisection to a sequence that does not converge. We cannot get a limit out of such a sequence, but we can choose from it infinitely many terms that converge. We will prove that this is possible for every bounded sequence. This theorem is a step on the road to the statement from section 1.7.3, which we wrote on the board in section 1.8:

\[f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a) \quad \text{for some number } \xi \text{ between } a \text{ and } b .\]

In figure 1.113 the horizontal measure is the difference \(b - a\), the vertical measure the change \(f(b) - f(a)\), and the blue tangent at the point above \(\xi\) is parallel to the grey line through both points of the graph, so it has the slope \(f'(\xi)\).

Why do we need this theorem? The proof of the equation from the board will need a point at which the value of the function on the interval is largest. Why exactly the largest? Let us draw a hill (figure 1.197).

The axes are \(t\) horizontally and \(f\) vertically, the curve is the graph of the function on the interval between \(a\) and \(b\). The dashed horizontal line is the largest value \(s\), the height of the top. If the point at the top, let us call it \(c\), is not an endpoint of the interval, no value to the left or right of it is larger. The lines from the top to the points of the graph to the right of it therefore fall, and those to the points to the left of it rise, and the tangent at the top is horizontal: its slope, the derivative, is zero there. We prove this precisely later in the chapter, in the section on the derivative at a largest value, and for now the figure is enough. The proof of the equation from the board first treats functions with equal values at the endpoints, for which the top lies in the interior of the interval and the tangent there is horizontal, and it gets the general equation by subtracting from the function the line through the points of the graph above the endpoints. We will not be able to compute the point \(c\). We will find a sequence of points \(t_1, t_2, \dots\) at which the values of the function approach the largest one. Such sequences exist: the largest value \(s\) is the supremum of the values, so \(s - 1/n\) is not an upper bound and for every \(n\) there is a point \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(s - 1/n < f(t_{\mathrm{n}}) \le s\), so \(f(t_{\mathrm{n}}) \to s\). From the sequence we then choose terms that converge. The figure shows one such choice, the blue points \(t_1, t_3, t_5, \dots\) with odd indices, which approach \(c\) from the left. That such a choice exists for every bounded sequence is told by the theorem in this section. The limit of the chosen points will be the point \(c\) we are looking for.

The question has a physical side too. Let us take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m that stretches under its own weight (figure 1.198). We hold the point \(O\) at the centre of the top end at rest. The weight of the bar is carried by a force \(F\) that pulls the top face evenly upward. This is a simplification (idealization): in a real bar the weight would be carried by the fixing (section 1.7.1). We measure the depth \(x\) from \(O\) downward (axis \(x\)). The strain along the axis at the depth \(x\) is

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) , \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\]

(section 1.4), on the graph the line. The constant \(a\) is a datum of the steel, and its value does not matter to us here: we know only that \(a > 0\). From the formula we see that it is largest at the top, where \(x = 0\) and \(\varepsilon = 2aL\), and at \(x = L\) it is zero. When we have no formula, for example for a bar made of several different materials or with a changing cross-section, we must first prove that a point where the material is stretched most exists at all.

We draw the figure in parts. On the left is the bar (a grey rectangle) of length \(L\). It hangs from its top end, at the point \(O\) in its centre it is fixed. Two arrows point up, because the weight of the bar is carried by a force \(F\) that pulls it up. They stand at the edges of the top face, because the force pulls the whole face evenly. To the right of the bar is the axis \(x\), which points down, its start \(0\) is at the height of \(O\). It points down because we measure the depth downward: \(x = 0\) at the top and \(x = L\) at the bottom. The graph of the strain stands beside the same axis. The value \(\varepsilon = 0\) lies on the axis \(x\), and the horizontal axis under the graph, with the scale \(0\) and \(2aL\), says that the offset from the axis \(x\) is the strain \(\varepsilon\). The line falls from the top to the bottom, because \(\varepsilon\) is a linear function of \(x\). At the top it is the largest, \(2aL\), and a grey dashed line shows at which height this is. At the bottom it is zero, there the line touches the axis \(x\). The scales are not equal: the length of the bar and the values of the strain are each drawn in their own scale, the graph shows only the course.

We will look for the point of the largest value also in chapter 2, where among all planes that we mentally put through a point of a body we will look for the one on which the forces pull the material apart most strongly. What these forces are, we will say there.

The route of the section is as follows. First we give the chosen terms a name and show two things: the \(k\)-th chosen term stands at least at the place \(k\) in the sequence, in symbols \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), and if the sequence converges, the chosen terms converge to the same limit. Then we say precisely what it means that a set holds infinitely many terms, and we show that at least one of the two halves of an interval has this property. At the end we carry out bisection on an example and prove the theorem. About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.10: the rules of school arithmetic and the properties of the integers, the fact that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element, and the completeness axiom 1.32.

1.11.1 Subsequence

Let us take the sequence

\[x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n \Big(1 + \frac1n\Big) ,\]

that is, \(x_1 = -2\), \(x_2 = \tfrac32\), \(x_3 = -\tfrac43\), \(x_4 = \tfrac54\), \(x_5 = -\tfrac65\) and so on. Let us draw it (figure 1.199). The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis shows the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). The grey dashed horizontal lines are at \(1\) and at \(-1\), these are the values that the terms at even and at odd places approach. The dots at even places are blue and lie above \(1\), the dots at odd places are blackwhite and lie below \(-1\). The orange dashed lines are the bounds \(2\) and \(-2\), all dots lie between them, and the first dot, \(x_1 = -2\), lies exactly on the lower one.

The factor \((-1)^n\) is \(1\) for even \(n\) and \(-1\) for odd ones, as in the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) from section 1.9.3. The factor \(1 + 1/n\) is larger than \(1\) and approaches \(1\). So the terms at even places are \(1 + 1/n\) and approach \(1\) from above, and the terms at odd places are \(-(1 + 1/n)\) and approach \(-1\) from below. The sequence is bounded. Since \(0 < 1/n \le 1\), we have \(1 < 1 + 1/n \le 2\), so \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| = 1 + 1/n \le 2\) and \(-2 \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le 2\) for every \(n\). But it does not converge: the terms at even places are close to \(1\), the terms at odd places close to \(-1\), and no number is close to both, much like the sequence \(((-1)^n)\). For now the figure tells us this, the proof will be in section 1.11.2. If we take only the terms at even places, \(x_2, x_4, x_6, \dots\), we get the sequence \(\tfrac32, \tfrac54, \tfrac76, \dots\), which approaches \(1\). From a sequence that does not converge we have chosen terms that approach a number. We write such a choice precisely through the places of the chosen terms.

Definition 1.52 Subsequence (podzaporedje)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and let \((n_{\mathrm{k}})\) be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers, that is, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} < n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for every natural number \(k\), or \(n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \dots\) The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) whose \(k\)-th term is \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), that is, the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}_1}, x_{\mathrm{n}_2}, x_{\mathrm{n}_3}, \dots\), is a subsequence (podzaporedje) of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.16 (PDF p. 58).

A subsequence is again a sequence: its input is a natural number \(k\), its output the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\). The indices \(k\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) have different jobs. The number \(k\) counts the chosen terms: \(x_{\mathrm{n}_1}\) is the first chosen term, \(x_{\mathrm{n}_2}\) the second. The number \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) tells at which place of the original sequence the \(k\)-th chosen term stands. For the terms at even places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\): the first chosen term is \(x_2\), the second \(x_4\), the \(k\)-th \(x_{2\mathrm{k}}\). For \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\) the places are \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2, 4, 6, 8\) and the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac32, \tfrac54, \tfrac76, \tfrac98\). The sequence of places \(2, 4, 6, \dots\) is strictly increasing, because \(2(k + 1) = 2k + 2 > 2k\). In figure 1.200 the chosen places are \(2, 3, 6, 7, 10\) and \(12\), irregularly spaced. The chosen terms are renumbered: the subsequence is a new sequence \(y_{\mathrm{k}} = x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), that is \(y_1 = x_{\mathrm{n}_1}\), \(y_2 = x_{\mathrm{n}_2}\) and so on. The term \(x_6\) is the third chosen term, so in the subsequence it stands at the place \(k = 3\): \(x_{\mathrm{n}_3} = x_6 = \tfrac76\).

Let us draw this in two parts. At the top is the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 12\): the horizontal axis counts the places \(1, \dots, 12\), each with its own label. The vertical axis shows the terms. We mark the chosen terms in blue, the blue dots are at the places \(2, 3, 6, 7, 10\) and \(12\). The grey dots are the terms we did not choose. Under the labels of the places there are the places marked in blue \(n_1, \dots, n_6\) of the chosen terms, for example \(n_5\) under the number \(10\). Below is the subsequence as a sequence of its own. The axis \(k\) counts the chosen terms, the vertical axis shows \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\). The dots are the same terms, renumbered in order: the \(k\)-th chosen term stands above the number \(k\). The term \(x_6\) is the third chosen one, so below it is above \(k = 3\). The same in a table, where we count \(k\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) separately:

\(k\) 1 2 3 4 5 6
\(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) 2 3 6 7 10 12
\(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) \(\tfrac32\) \(-\tfrac43\) \(\tfrac76\) \(-\tfrac87\) \(\tfrac{11}{10}\) \(\tfrac{13}{12}\)

The top row counts the chosen terms, the second tells their places in the original sequence, the third their values, which are the blue dots below.

For the examples that follow, let us also draw the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) (figure 1.201).

The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis shows the terms. The grey dashed horizontal lines are the values \(1\) and \(-1\), because the sequence takes only these two. The blue dots are the terms at the even places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\). All of them are at \(1\), because \((-1)^{2k} = 1\). The grey dots are the terms at the odd places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\). All of them are at \(-1\), because \((-1)^{2k-1} = -1\).

What is the requirement that the places increase strictly for? Let us look at three choices of places from the sequence \(x_1, \dots, x_8\) (figure 1.202).

Let us draw three choices of places. Each row holds the places \(1, \dots, 8\) of the original sequence, a dot is a chosen place, and the number above it says which chosen term this was, that is, \(k\). To the right of the row the sequence of chosen places is written, in the order of choosing. The top row has the places \(2, 4, 6, 8\). The numbers above the dots, \(1, 2, 3, 4\), increase together with the places, so this is a subsequence with \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\). In the middle row the first chosen term is at the place \(2\), the second at the place \(1\), the third at the place \(4\) and the fourth at the place \(3\). So in every pair the places fall instead of increasing, and the choice is not a subsequence. In the bottom row there is a single dot at the place \(1\), with \(1, 2, 3\) above it: the same term is chosen three times, and the places \(1, 1, 1\) do not increase strictly.

The chosen terms keep the order they have in the sequence, and no term is chosen twice. So the subsequence moves ever further along the sequence, and this is exactly what we will need when we go from the limit of the sequence to the limit of the chosen terms. The sequence \(x_2, x_1, x_4, x_3, \dots\), the middle row, is not a subsequence of \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\), because it swaps the order. Nor is the sequence \(x_1, x_1, x_1, \dots\), the bottom row, which takes the same term several times, since the places \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) do not increase strictly. Values may repeat, though. The sequence \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) is a subsequence of the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\), because \((-1)^{2k} = 1\). So what counts is the place of a term, not only its value: the choice of places \(2, 4, 6, \dots\) is a subsequence although all the chosen terms are equal to \(1\), while the choice of places \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) is not.

Since the places are natural numbers and each is larger than the previous one, with every chosen term we move at least one place forward. The first chosen term stands at least at the place \(1\), the second at least at the place \(2\), and so on. We will need this in the next section, when we go from the place \(N\) to the places of the chosen terms. Let us write it on the board as a statement

\[P(k):\quad n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k .\]

This is a claim for all natural numbers \(k\), so we prove it by induction.

Let us draw the proof on three number lines. The top one shows the start: the natural numbers start at \(1\), the blue part of the line is the region \(n \ge 1\), and the number \(n_1\) lies in it. The middle one shows the step. The blue part starts at \(k\), the number \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) lies in it, and the number \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is to the right of \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\), because the places increase strictly, which the measure \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} - n_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) between them shows. So it is also to the right of \(k\). The bottom one shows why this is already \(k + 1\) or more: the grey segment between \(k\) and \(k + 1\) contains no integer, so an integer to the right of \(k\) lies in the orange part from \(k + 1\) on.

Lemma 1.53 Indices of a subsequence (indeksi podzaporedja)

If \((n_{\mathrm{k}})\) is a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers, then \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Proof

We must show that the \(k\)-th chosen place is not before the place \(k\). We use the principle of induction 1.44 for the statement \(P(k)\): \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\).

  1. Basis. The number \(n_1\) is natural, and the natural numbers are \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), so \(n_1 \ge 1\).
  2. Induction step. Let \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) hold. The sequence of places increases strictly, so \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > n_{\mathrm{k}}\). The inequalities form a chain, \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > k\).
  3. The difference \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} - k\) is an integer and by step 2 it is larger than \(0\), so it is a natural number. The natural numbers are \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} - k \ge 1\). So an integer larger than \(k\) is at least \(k + 1\): there is no integer between \(k\) and \(k + 1\). Hence \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} \ge k + 1\), which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Source: LEBL1 states the claim in the proof of Prop. 2.1.17 (PDF p. 58) and leaves the proof by induction to the reader. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says that the \(k\)-th chosen term does not stand before the place \(k\) (figure 1.204). For the places of figure 1.200, \(n_1 = 2 \ge 1\), \(n_3 = 6 \ge 3\) and \(n_6 = 12 \ge 6\). We get the equality \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k\) at every \(k\) only if we choose all terms, and then the subsequence is just the sequence itself. Every skipped term pushes all the following chosen places one place further from \(k\), so

\[n_{\mathrm{k}} = k + (\text{number of skipped places before } n_{\mathrm{k}}) .\]

The places \(1\) to \(12\) of figure 1.200, with the chosen ones in a box:

\[1,\ \boxed{2},\ \boxed{3},\ 4,\ 5,\ \boxed{6},\ \boxed{7},\ 8,\ 9,\ \boxed{10},\ 11,\ \boxed{12} .\]

There are six places without a box, namely the skipped places \(1, 4, 5, 8, 9\) and \(11\). Up to and including the place \(12\) there are twelve places, six of them chosen and six skipped, so \(n_6 - 6 = 12 - 6 = 6\) and \(n_6 = 6 + 6 = 12\).

Both axes have the same scale, so the grey dashed line \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k\) is the diagonal. The dots would lie on it if we chose all the terms, since then the \(k\)-th chosen term would be exactly the \(k\)-th term. The blue dots are the places \(2, 3, 6, 7, 10\) and \(12\) from the previous figure, drawn at \(k = 1, \dots, 6\). All are above the line or on it, because the \(k\)-th chosen term cannot stand before the place \(k\), and every skipped term lifts them one place higher.

1.11.2 The limit of a subsequence

If a sequence converges, its terms from some place on are close to the limit. A subsequence takes only some of these terms, so we expect it to converge to the same limit. The lemma on the indices of a subsequence tells us why this is so: the chosen terms from the \(N\)-th on stand at places from \(N\) on (figure 1.205).

Lemma 1.54 Limit of a subsequence (limita podzaporedja)

If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to the number \(d\), then every subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) of it also converges to \(d\).

Proof

We must show that from some \(k\) on all terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) are closer to the number \(d\) than a given error.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\).
  3. Let \(k \ge N\). By lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k \ge N\): the condition \(k \ge N\) on the axis of chosen terms turns into the condition \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge N\) on the axis of places.
  4. By step 2, used for the place \(n = n_{\mathrm{k}}\), we have \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - d| < \eta\). This holds for every \(k \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(d\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 2.1.17 (PDF p. 58).

The proof used for the subsequence the same place \(N\) as for the sequence. That \(k \ge N\) guarantees \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge N\) is exactly the lemma on the indices of a subsequence. In figure 1.205, \(N = 4\). The fourth chosen term stands at the place \(n_4 = 7 \ge 4\), and the chosen terms from the fourth on, at the places \(7\), \(10\) and \(12\), are in the band. The third chosen term is in the band too, because it stands at the place \(6 \ge N\). The proof does not need it: the bound \(k \ge N\) is only a safe choice that works for every subsequence.

The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis shows the terms. The grey dashed line is the limit \(d\), around it is a blue band of width \(\eta\) on each side. The vertical line \(N\) is the place \(N = 4\), from which on all the terms are in the band. The blue dots with a blackwhite edge are the chosen terms at the places \(n_1, \dots, n_6 = 2, 3, 6, 7, 10, 12\), which are labelled under the axis. The blackwhite edge is there so that a blue dot can be told apart from the blue band. The grey dots are the terms we did not choose. The sequence is the same as in figure 1.158: a convergent sequence that is in the band around \(d\) from the place \(4\) on.

The converse does not hold. The claim "if some subsequence converges, the sequence converges" is false: a convergent subsequence does not yet mean that the sequence converges (LEBL1 Example 2.1.18, PDF p. 58, shows this with the sequence \(0, 1, 0, 1, \dots\)). We already have an example before us: the subsequence \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) of the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) converges, but the sequence itself does not. But the lemma offers a simple way to show that a sequence does not converge: we find two subsequences with different limits. Let us try it first on the sequence \(((-1)^n)\), whose divergence we proved in lemma 1.40 on a sequence without a limit with the error \(\eta = \tfrac12\).

Let us draw the reason. Each row is a number line with the numbers \(-1\) and \(1\) marked. The top row shows the sequence \(((-1)^n)\): the subsequence \(x_{2\mathrm{k}}\) is constant at \(1\) (blue dot), the subsequence \(x_{2\mathrm{k}-1}\) constant at \(-1\) (blackwhite dot). We will need the middle row only for the second proof and the bottom one at step 3 of the first, so we draw them there.

  1. The subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\) has the terms \((-1)^{2k} = 1\). This is the constant sequence \(1, 1, 1, \dots\), which converges to \(1\) (section 1.9.2).
  2. The subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\) has the terms \((-1)^{2k-1} = -1\), so it converges to \(-1\). The places \(2k - 1\) increase strictly, because \(2(k + 1) - 1 = (2k - 1) + 2\).
  3. Suppose that the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) converges to some number \(d\). The bottom row of the figure shows what would follow: the number \(d\) is drawn twice, blackwhite at \(-1\) and blue at \(1\), because it is one single number that would have to be at both at the same time. Then by lemma 1.54 on the limit of a subsequence both subsequences converge to \(d\).
  4. By lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence, each of the two subsequences has only one limit, so \(d = 1\) by step 1 and \(d = -1\) by step 2. Then \(1 = -1\), which is not true, and the measure under the line shows that these numbers are \(2\) apart. So the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) does not converge.

This proof is shorter than the first one, because the work with the error \(\eta\) has already been done in the proofs of the lemma on the limit of a subsequence and of the lemma on the uniqueness of the limit. In the same way we show that the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) does not converge, except that its subsequences are not constant. They are shown by the middle row of figure 1.206: the blue terms at even places approach the number \(1\) from the right, the blackwhite terms at odd places approach the number \(-1\) from the left.

  1. At an even place \(n = 2k\) we have \(x_{2\mathrm{k}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2k}\). Let us take the sequence \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = 1 + 1/n\). The distance of its term from \(1\) is \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - 1| = 1/n = |1/n - 0|\). By lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n, for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a place \(N\) such that \(|1/n - 0| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\), and so for the same \(N\) we have \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - 1| < \eta\). So the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(1\).
  2. The terms \(x_{2\mathrm{k}} = y_{2\mathrm{k}}\) form a subsequence of the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\). So by the lemma on the limit of a subsequence they converge to \(1\).
  3. At an odd place \(n = 2k - 1\) we have \(x_{2\mathrm{k}-1} = -\big(1 + \frac{1}{2k-1}\big)\). The sequence \(z_{\mathrm{n}} = -(1 + 1/n)\) converges to \(-1\), because \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - (-1)| = |-1/n| = 1/n\), and for the same \(N\) as in step 1 we have \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - (-1)| < \eta\). The terms \(x_{2\mathrm{k}-1} = z_{2\mathrm{k}-1}\) form a subsequence of the sequence \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\), so they converge to \(-1\).
  4. If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converged to a number \(d\), then by the lemma on the limit of a subsequence both subsequences of steps 2 and 3 would converge to \(d\), and by the uniqueness of the limit we would have \(d = 1\) and \(d = -1\). So \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) does not converge.

Figure 1.199 shows both subsequences, the even one with blue dots and the odd one with blackwhite ones. From a bounded sequence that does not converge we have chosen a convergent subsequence, because we knew the formula for its terms. Can this be done for every bounded sequence, also when we do not know a formula?

1.11.3 Infinitely many terms

How would we find a convergent subsequence without a formula? Bisection suggests itself. All terms of a bounded sequence lie in some interval. We halve it and keep the half in which infinitely many terms lie, then we halve this half, and so on. For the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) all the terms are in the interval \([-2, 2]\) and the kept intervals are

\[[-2, 2] \supset [-2, 0] \supset [-2, -1] \supset [-1{,}5, -1] \supset \dots\]

The intervals shrink, and in each of them there are enough terms to choose from it the next term of the subsequence at a later place than the previous one. Before we carry this out, we must say precisely what it means that a half holds infinitely many terms, and show that at least one of the two halves has this property.

In a sequence we count places, not values. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) has terms at infinitely many places in the set \(\{1\}\), which has a single element, because every term at an even place equals \(1\). So that a set holds infinitely many terms will mean that terms at arbitrarily late places lie in it.

Definition 1.55 Infinitely many terms in a set (neskončno mnogo členov v množici)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and \(A\) a set of real numbers. The set \(A\) holds infinitely many terms (neskončno mnogo členov) of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) if for every natural number \(N\) there is a natural number \(n \ge N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A\).

The definition is a game of two players, like the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38. The first picks a place \(N\), however late. The second must find a term in \(A\) at the place \(N\) or later. Let us try it on the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) and on two intervals chosen for the example: the first holds the terms at even places, the second only the first term (figure 1.207).

The interval \(A = [1, 2]\) holds infinitely many terms. For every \(N\) the place \(n = 2N\) is even and at least \(N\), and the term at it is \(1 + \frac{1}{2N}\), which lies between \(1\) and \(\tfrac32\), so in \([1, 2]\). The interval \(B = [-\tfrac52, -\tfrac32]\), though, holds only the term \(x_1 = -2\). The terms at even places are positive, and the terms at odd places from the third on are \(-(1 + 1/n) \ge -(1 + \tfrac13) = -\tfrac43\), which is more than \(-\tfrac32\). So for \(N = 2\) there is no place \(n \ge 2\) with its term in \(B\), and \(B\) does not hold infinitely many terms.

The example of the interval \(B\) shows what it means that a set does not hold infinitely many terms. The definition requires a term in the set for every place \(N\). If this does not hold, there is at least one place \(N\) for which there is no such term, that is, no term at the place \(N\) or later lies in the set. So a set does not hold infinitely many terms exactly when there is a place from which on no term lies in it:

\[\text{there is } N: \quad x_{\mathrm{n}} \notin A \ \text{ for every } n \ge N .\]

The terms that lie in it are then all among \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\).

In bisection we split an interval into two halves, and every number of the interval lies in at least one of them. We need a name for this situation. The union (unija) of the sets \(A\) and \(B\), written \(A \cup B\), is the set of all numbers that lie in \(A\) or in \(B\) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.4, PDF p. 10). The word or allows both here, so the union also contains the numbers that lie in both sets. The closed interval \([a, b]\) with the midpoint \(m = \tfrac12(a + b)\) is the union of its halves,

\[[a, b] = [a, m] \cup [m, b] .\]

Indeed, every number \(t\) with \(a \le t \le b\) satisfies \(t \le m\) or \(t \ge m\), so it lies in the left or in the right half, and every number of either half lies in \([a, b]\). The midpoint \(m\) lies in both halves.

On the number line the upper grey segment is the interval \([a, b]\), the blue its left half \([a, m]\) and the blackwhite the right half \([m, b]\). Each segment has filled dots at both endpoints, because the halves are closed intervals. The grey dashed vertical is at the midpoint \(m\) and passes through the filled dots in which the halves meet. Above the upper segment are the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) of the whole interval. Every number of the upper segment lies above at least one of the lower two.

If the union of two sets holds infinitely many terms, does one of the two sets hold infinitely many terms? We expect so. If each of the sets held only terms before some place, both together would also hold only terms before some place. Let us show this precisely (figure 1.209).

Lemma 1.56 One of the halves (ena od polovic)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and \(A\) and \(B\) sets of real numbers. If the union \(A \cup B\) holds infinitely many terms of the sequence, then \(A\) or \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.

Proof

We must show that terms at arbitrarily late places lie in at least one of the sets \(A\) and \(B\).

  1. We argue by contradiction. Suppose that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.
  2. Since \(A\) does not hold infinitely many terms, the condition of definition 1.55 of infinitely many terms in a set does not hold for every place. So there is a place \(N_1\) for which there is no \(n \ge N_1\) with \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A\): from the place \(N_1\) on no term lies in \(A\).
  3. In the same way there is a place \(N_2\) from which on no term lies in \(B\).
  4. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\), and let \(n \ge N\). Then \(n \ge N_1\) and \(n \ge N_2\), so by steps 2 and 3, \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) lies neither in \(A\) nor in \(B\). So it does not lie in \(A \cup B\) either, which contains only numbers from \(A\) or from \(B\).
  5. The set \(A \cup B\) holds infinitely many terms, so by the definition, used with this place \(N\), there is an \(n \ge N\) with \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A \cup B\). This contradicts step 4. The assumption of step 1 is false, so \(A\) or \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.

From history

Bernard Bolzano was a professor of the philosophy of religion in Prague and held advanced views on variables and limits (CAJORI, PDF p. 383). Mathematicians overlooked his writings until the German mathematician Hermann Hankel called attention to them. In Hankel's words Bolzano has everything that places him beside the French mathematician Cauchy in the treatment of infinite sums, except that he lacks the art of the French to work out their ideas and to communicate them in the most suitable and attractive way. So he remained unknown and was soon forgotten (CAJORI, PDF p. 383-384). In 1872 Hermann Amandus Schwarz looked upon Bolzano as the inventor of a line of reasoning that Weierstrass developed further (CAJORI, PDF p. 384). The theorem that follows bears the names of both. The story is here so that we know where the name comes from, we do not need it for the proof.

The theorem is named after the Czech mathematician Bolzano and the German mathematician Weierstrass (LEBL1, PDF p. 73).

Theorem 1.57 Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (Bolzano-Weierstrassov izrek)

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

Proof

We must show that from a bounded sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) we can choose a subsequence that converges.

The starting interval.

Let us draw the graph: the horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical one shows the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). The grey dashed lines are the lower bound \(a = -2\) and the upper bound \(b = 2\). The blackwhite dots are the terms, and all lie between the bounds. The dot \(x_1 = -2\) is on the lower bound, because it is attained, while the upper bound is \(\tfrac12\) away from the largest term \(1{,}5\). The vertical dashed line is an arbitrary place \(N\), here \(N = 7\), and the blue dot on it is the term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\), which lies between the bounds.

  1. The sequence is bounded, so the set of its terms has a lower bound \(a\) and an upper bound \(b\) (definitions 1.37 and 1.29). So \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\).

Let us draw this case. A single dot is a term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) of a constant sequence, which has all its terms at the same point. This point is then both a lower and an upper bound, so \(a = b\), and the bounds \(a\) and \(b\) may be equal although they are arbitrary. We move the upper bound by \(1\) to \(b + 1\), which the measure above the line shows, and so get \(a < b\).

  1. From \(a \le x_1 \le b\) it follows that \(a \le b\). If \(a = b\), we replace the upper bound by \(b + 1\), which is an upper bound as well. So we may take \(a < b\), as the definition of an interval 1.43 requires.
  2. All terms lie in \([a, b]\), so it holds infinitely many terms: for every place \(N\) the term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\) lies in \([a, b]\), and this is a term at the place \(n = N \ge N\), as definition 1.55 of infinitely many terms in a set requires.

Building the intervals and the places. We build the intervals \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) step by step, so that at every \(k\) two requirements hold. The first: \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) holds infinitely many terms. The second: \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \in [a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\).

  1. Basis. Let \(a_1 = a\), \(b_1 = b\) and \(n_1 = 1\). We have \(a_1 < b_1\) by step 2, the first requirement by step 3, and the second requirement, because \(x_1 \in [a, b]\) by step 1.
  2. For some \(k\) let \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) be already determined so that both requirements hold, and let \(m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}})\) be the midpoint of the interval. The interval is the union of its halves, \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}] = [a_{\mathrm{k}}, m_{\mathrm{k}}] \cup [m_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\), so by the first requirement the union of the halves holds infinitely many terms. By lemma 1.56 on one of the halves at least one half holds infinitely many terms.
  3. If the left half holds infinitely many terms, let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\). Otherwise the right half holds infinitely many terms by step 5, and let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\). In both cases the first requirement holds for \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\), and \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} < b_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), because the midpoint lies strictly between the endpoints: \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}}) < b_{\mathrm{k}}\), because \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) (section 1.10.1).

  1. We use the first requirement for \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\) with the place \(N = n_{\mathrm{k}} + 1\): there is a place \(n \ge n_{\mathrm{k}} + 1\) with the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in [a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\).   1. By step 7, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} < n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for every \(k\). So the places increase strictly, and \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) (definition 1.52).

Let us draw steps 10 and 11. The four grey segments are the intervals \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) for \(k = 1, \dots, 4\) with the endpoints marked, \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the left and \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the right. Each interval lies in the previous one: the left endpoint does not move left, the right one does not move right, which the dashed vertical lines show, carrying each new endpoint to the row above it. The measure under a segment, with a number to the right of it, is the length of the interval: \(4\), \(2\), \(1\) and \(\tfrac12\). Each is half of the previous one, because every step keeps half of the interval.

  1. The left endpoints do not decrease and the right ones do not increase, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), because a new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\), which lies between the old endpoints.
  2. The length of the interval is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = (b - a)/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) the length is \(b - a = (b - a)/2^0\). Every step halves the length, because each half has the length \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = b_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the formula at \(k\) gives the formula at \(k + 1\), \(\tfrac12 \cdot (b - a)/2^{k-1} = (b - a)/2^k\), and by the principle of induction the formula holds for every \(k\), just like the third property of bisection in section 1.10.2.

The limit.

Let us draw steps 12 and 13. The upper line shows the interval \([a_2, b_2] = [-2, 0]\) and in it the number \(c\), here \(c = -1\). The lower line shows the left endpoints: \(a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = -2\), \(a_4 = -1{,}5\), \(a_5 = -1{,}25\) and the following ones, which pile up to the left of \(c\). The figure is made for \(k = 2\) as an example, for another \(k\) it would be the same. The dashed line at \(b_2 = 0\) is an upper bound of all the left endpoints, none is to the right of it, for another \(k\) it would stand at \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\). The number \(c\) is drawn again below, at the same value: no endpoint is to the right of it. The supremum is exactly this least upper bound, so \(c\) lies in every interval.

  1. The sequence of left endpoints \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) is increasing by step 10. It is bounded, because \(a = a_1 \le a_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}} \le b_1 = b\) by step 8 and by the same lemma for the decreasing sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\). Together we get the chain
    \[a = a_1 \le a_2 \le \dots \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}} \le \dots \le b_2 \le b_1 = b .\]
    So by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51 it converges to the number \(c = \sup\{a_{\mathrm{k}} : k \in \mathbb N\}\).
  2. The number \(c\) lies in every interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\), as in step 3 of section 1.10. Since the supremum is an upper bound, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\). The number \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an upper bound of all left endpoints: for \(j \le k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\), and for \(j > k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} < b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), by step 8 and by the lemma on the terms of a monotone sequence. The supremum \(c\) is not larger than any upper bound, so \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The grey segment is the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with the endpoints \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\). Above the segment is its length \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\), between the marked ends, because we measure from one endpoint to the other. The blue dot \(c\) is the number that lies in the interval. The proof will get it as the supremum of the left endpoints, here it is drawn only as an example. The blackwhite dot \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) is the chosen term, which also lies in it. The distance \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c|\) is below the segment and is not longer than the length of the interval, because both points lie between the endpoints.

  1. The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) lies in \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) by the second requirement, and the number \(c\) by step 13. Two numbers \(x\) and \(y\) in the same interval are at most its length apart, \(|x - y| \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\). For \(x = x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) and \(y = c\) we show this as follows. From \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\) it follows that \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\), and from \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) that \(c - x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\). So by step 11
    \[|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{b - a}{2^{k-1}} .\]
  2. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half, the sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\) converges to \(0\). We use the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 for it with the error \(\eta/(b - a)\), which is positive because \(b > a\). There is a place \(N\) such that \(|1/2^{k-1} - 0| = 1/2^{k-1} < \eta/(b - a)\) for every \(k \ge N\). We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(b - a\) and get \((b - a)/2^{k-1} < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\).

Let us draw this step. The blue band around the number \(c\) has the radius of the error \(\eta\). The shorter interval is \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) at \(k \ge N\), its length \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\) is smaller than \(\eta\) by step 15, so the interval does not stick out of the band. In it lie the number \(c\) and the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), and their distance is by step 14 at most as large as the length of the interval, so smaller than \(\eta\).

  1. By steps 14 and 15, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\):
    \[|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| \le \frac{b - a}{2^{k-1}} < \eta .\]
    We picked the error freely, so the subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(c\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 2.3.8, second proof (PDF pp. 78-79). The source shows that the two sequences of endpoints have the same limit and finishes with the squeeze lemma. We, as in section 1.10.5, show that the limit of the left endpoints lies in every interval, and we bound the distance of the chosen term from it by the length of the interval. The choice of the smallest place is not from the source.

What did the proof show? Bisection with the lemma on one of the halves builds intervals that shrink and each of which holds infinitely many terms. From each interval we choose a term at a later place than the previous one. The completeness axiom, hidden in the monotone convergence theorem, supplies the number \(c\), which lies in all the intervals (step 13). The chosen terms are at most the length of the interval away from it, and this length goes to zero. For the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) we have \(c = -1\). The left endpoints \(-2, -2, -2, -\tfrac32, -\tfrac54, \dots\) approach \(-1\), because from \(k = 3\) on \(b_{\mathrm{k}} = -1\) and the length \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\) goes to zero. So \(c\) is their limit \(-1\), which agrees with \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}} = -1\) (figure 1.214).

The theorem guarantees that a convergent subsequence exists, but it does not say which one or to which limit. If the rule gave preference to the right half, then for our sequence we would keep the interval \([0, 2]\), in which all terms at even places lie, already at the first step, and we would get a subsequence with the limit \(1\).

Let us draw both choices. The upper grey segment is the starting interval \([-2, 2]\). The grey tick on each row is the midpoint of its interval, at \(k = 1\) it is \(0\). The next row is the half that the rule chooses. The blue segments are the rule from the text, which takes the left half if it has infinitely many terms: \([-2, 0]\), \([-2, -1]\) and \([-1{,}5, -1]\) at \(k = 2, 3, 4\). At the passage from \(k = 3\) to \(k = 4\) the rule takes the right half, because the left half \([-2, -1{,}5]\) of the interval \([-2, -1]\) holds only the term \(x_1\). They shrink toward the number \(-1\), the grey dashed line. The blackwhite segments are the rule that prefers the right half: \([0, 2]\), \([1, 2]\) and \([1, 1{,}5]\). The interval \([1, 2]\) is the right half of the interval \([0, 2]\), in which all terms at even places lie. In \([1, 2]\) the right half \([1{,}5, 2]\) holds only the term \(x_2 = 1{,}5\), so the rule takes the left half \([1, 1{,}5]\). They shrink toward the number \(1\), the grey dashed line.

The limit lies in the starting interval. At \(k = 1\) step 13 has already shown \(a_1 \le c \le b_1\), that is, \(a \le c \le b\). We will need this in section 1.13, where a point found as a limit must lie in the interval in which we search.

The theorem needs two things, the boundedness of the sequence and the completeness axiom. Without boundedness it fails. The sequence \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), that is, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = n\), has no convergent subsequence. Its subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) has the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = n_{\mathrm{k}}\). For every number \(B\) there is by the Archimedean property 1.35 a natural number \(k > B\), and then by the lemma on the indices of a subsequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k > B\). So the subsequence is not bounded, while a convergent sequence is bounded by lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence. Without the completeness axiom the theorem fails as well. The left endpoints of the bisection for \(\sqrt 3\) from section 1.10.1 are a bounded sequence of fractions that converges to \(\sqrt 3\). By the lemma on the limit of a subsequence every subsequence of it converges to \(\sqrt 3\), which by lemma 1.27 is not a fraction. Among the fractions alone the limit must also be a fraction, this is \(d\) in the definition of the limit. A fraction is a real number, so by the uniqueness of the limit it would be equal to \(\sqrt 3\), which is not a fraction. So among the fractions alone this sequence has no convergent subsequence.

The grey dashed line is \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k\) and the dots are above it, because \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). The horizontal dashed line is an arbitrary number \(B\), here \(B = 8\). The blackwhite dots are below \(B\), the orange ones from \(k = 5\) on are above \(B\), and all the following ones stay above it. The first six chosen places are from the beginning of the section, the rest were added. On the right is the line of fractions with blue dots, the left endpoints \(a_4, a_5, a_6\) and \(a_8\) of the bisection for \(\sqrt 3\). The endpoint \(a_7\) is equal to \(a_6\), so it is the same dot. At \(\sqrt 3\) the line has a gap with a small circle, because \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction: the dots come ever closer to the gap, but never get into it.

The theorem is one of the results that carry this chapter, because it guarantees the existence of a point that we cannot compute. We will always use it in the same pattern: first we find a sequence with the desired property, then we choose from it a subsequence that converges (LEBL1, PDF p. 130). When we look for the point where the value of a function is largest, the theorem will choose, from a sequence of points at which the values approach the largest one, a subsequence of points that converge to some point \(c\). Let \(t_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) be the chosen points and \(s\) the largest value. Since \(f(t_{\mathrm{n}}) \to s\) (the introductory paragraph of the section), by the lemma on the limit of a subsequence also \(f(t_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) \to s\). If we also had

\[t_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \to c \quad \Rightarrow \quad f(t_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) \to f(c) ,\]

then by the uniqueness of the limit we would get \(f(c) = s\), so \(c\) would be the point of the largest value we are looking for. This requirement, though, is a property of the function that we have so far described only in words: its graph has no jumps, like the graph in figure 1.197. The graph with a jump is in the next section (figure 1.225), where we define this property precisely.
