Chapter 1 Strain

1.11 Mathematics: subsequences

Section 1.10 ended with bisection, which found the number \(\sqrt 3\). At every step we knew which half to keep, and the left endpoints converged to \(\sqrt 3\). In this section we apply bisection to a sequence that does not converge. We cannot get a limit out of such a sequence, but we can choose from it infinitely many terms that converge. We will prove that this is possible for every bounded sequence. This theorem is one of the results that carry the road to the statement about the change of a function between two numbers from section 1.7.3.

Why do we need it? The proof of that statement will need a point at which the value of a function on an interval is largest. We will not be able to compute such a point. We will find a sequence of points at which the values of the function approach the largest one, and choose from it terms that converge. Their limit will be the point we are looking for. The question has a physical side too. Let us take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m that stretches under its own weight. It is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of its top end, and its weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly, which is an idealization (section 1.7.1). The strain along its axis at the depth \(x\) is \(2a\,(L - x)\), where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (section 1.4). From the formula we see that it is largest at the top, where \(x = 0\). When we have no formula, we must first prove that a point where the material is stretched most exists at all. We will need the same tool in chapter 2, where among all planes through a point of a body we will look for the one on which the forces pull the material apart most strongly.

The route of the section is as follows. First we give the chosen terms a name and show two things: the \(k\)-th chosen term stands at least at the place \(k\) in the sequence, and if the sequence converges, the chosen terms converge to the same limit. Then we say precisely what it means that a set holds infinitely many terms, and we show that at least one of the two halves of an interval has this property. At the end we carry out bisection on an example and prove the theorem. About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.10: the rules of school arithmetic and the properties of the integers, the fact that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element, and the completeness axiom 1.32.

1.11.1 Subsequence

Let us take the sequence

\[x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n \Big(1 + \frac1n\Big) ,\]

that is, \(x_1 = -2\), \(x_2 = \tfrac32\), \(x_3 = -\tfrac43\), \(x_4 = \tfrac54\), \(x_5 = -\tfrac65\) and so on (figure 1.74). The factor \((-1)^n\) is \(1\) for even \(n\) and \(-1\) for odd \(n\), as in the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) from section 1.9.3. The factor \(1 + 1/n\) is larger than \(1\) and approaches \(1\). So the terms at even places are \(1 + 1/n\) and approach \(1\) from above, and the terms at odd places are \(-(1 + 1/n)\) and approach \(-1\) from below. The sequence is bounded. Since \(0 < 1/n \le 1\), we have \(1 < 1 + 1/n \le 2\), so \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| = 1 + 1/n \le 2\) and \(-2 \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le 2\) for every \(n\). But it does not converge, because it jumps between values close to \(1\) and values close to \(-1\), much like the sequence \(((-1)^n)\). We will prove this in section 1.11.2. If we take only the terms at even places, \(x_2, x_4, x_6, \dots\), we get the sequence \(\tfrac32, \tfrac54, \tfrac76, \dots\), which approaches \(1\). From a sequence that does not converge we have chosen terms that approach a number. We write such a choice precisely through the places of the chosen terms.

Definition 1.52 Subsequence (podzaporedje)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and let \((n_{\mathrm{k}})\) be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers, that is, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} < n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for every natural number \(k\), or \(n_1 < n_2 < n_3 < \dots\) The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) whose \(k\)-th term is \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), that is, the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}_1}, x_{\mathrm{n}_2}, x_{\mathrm{n}_3}, \dots\), is a subsequence (podzaporedje) of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.16 (PDF p. 58).

A subsequence is again a sequence: its input is a natural number \(k\), its output the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\). The indices \(k\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) have different jobs. The number \(k\) counts the chosen terms: \(x_{\mathrm{n}_1}\) is the first chosen term, \(x_{\mathrm{n}_2}\) the second. The number \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) tells at which place of the original sequence the \(k\)-th chosen term stands. For the terms at even places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\): the first chosen term is \(x_2\), the second \(x_4\), the \(k\)-th \(x_{2\mathrm{k}}\). The sequence of places \(2, 4, 6, \dots\) is strictly increasing, because \(2(k + 1) = 2k + 2 > 2k\). In figure 1.75 the chosen places are \(2, 3, 6, 7, 10\) and \(12\), irregularly spaced. The chosen terms are renumbered. The term \(x_6\) is the third chosen term, so in the subsequence it stands at the place \(k = 3\): \(x_{\mathrm{n}_3} = x_6 = \tfrac76\).

What is the requirement that the places increase strictly for? The chosen terms keep the order they have in the sequence, and no term is chosen twice. So the subsequence moves ever further along the sequence, and this is exactly what we will need when we go from the limit of the sequence to the limit of the chosen terms. The sequence \(x_2, x_1, x_4, x_3, \dots\) is not a subsequence of \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\), because it swaps the order. Nor is the sequence \(x_1, x_1, x_1, \dots\), which takes the same term several times, since the places \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) do not increase strictly. Values may repeat, though. The sequence \(1, 1, 1, \dots\) is a subsequence of the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\), because \((-1)^{2k} = 1\). As with the sequence itself, what counts is the place of a term, not only its value.

Since the places are natural numbers and each is larger than the previous one, with every chosen term we move at least one place forward. The first chosen term stands at least at the place \(1\), the second at least at the place \(2\), and so on. This is a claim for all natural numbers \(k\), so we prove it by induction.

Lemma 1.53 Indices of a subsequence (indeksi podzaporedja)

If \((n_{\mathrm{k}})\) is a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers, then \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Proof

We must show that the \(k\)-th chosen place is not before the place \(k\). We use the principle of induction 1.44 for the statement \(P(k)\): \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\).

  1. Basis. The number \(n_1\) is natural, and the natural numbers are \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), so \(n_1 \ge 1\).
  2. Induction step. Let \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) hold. The sequence of places increases strictly, so \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > n_{\mathrm{k}}\). The inequalities form a chain, \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > k\).
  3. The numbers \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(k\) are integers, and there is no integer between the integers \(k\) and \(k + 1\) (school properties of the integers, section 1.8.1). So an integer larger than \(k\) is at least \(k + 1\). Hence \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} \ge k + 1\), which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Source: LEBL1 states the claim in the proof of Prop. 2.1.17 (PDF p. 58) and leaves the proof by induction to the reader. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says that the \(k\)-th chosen term does not stand before the place \(k\) (figure 1.76). For the places of figure 1.75, \(n_1 = 2 \ge 1\), \(n_3 = 6 \ge 3\) and \(n_6 = 12 \ge 6\). We get the equality \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k\) at every \(k\) only if we choose all terms, and then the subsequence is just the sequence itself. Every skipped term pushes all the following chosen places one place further from \(k\). In the figure \(n_6 - 6 = 6\), because six places are skipped before the place \(12\), namely \(1, 4, 5, 8, 9\) and \(11\).

1.11.2 The limit of a subsequence

If a sequence converges, its terms from some place on are close to the limit. A subsequence takes only some of these terms, so we expect it to converge to the same limit. The lemma on the indices of a subsequence tells us why this is so: the chosen terms from the \(N\)-th on stand at places from \(N\) on (figure 1.77).

Lemma 1.54 Limit of a subsequence (limita podzaporedja)

If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to the number \(d\), then every subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) of it also converges to \(d\).

Proof

We must show that from some \(k\) on all terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) are closer to the number \(d\) than a given error.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\).
  3. Let \(k \ge N\). By lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge N\).
  4. By step 2, used for the place \(n = n_{\mathrm{k}}\), we have \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - d| < \eta\). This holds for every \(k \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(d\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 2.1.17 (PDF p. 58).

The proof used for the subsequence the same place \(N\) as for the sequence. That \(k \ge N\) guarantees \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge N\) is exactly the lemma on the indices of a subsequence. In figure 1.77, \(N = 4\), and the chosen terms from the fourth on, at the places \(7\), \(10\) and \(12\), are in the band. The third chosen term is in the band too, because it stands at the place \(6 \ge N\). The proof does not need it: the bound \(k \ge N\) is only a safe choice that works for every subsequence.

The converse does not hold. A convergent subsequence does not yet mean that the sequence converges (LEBL1 Example 2.1.18, PDF p. 58, shows this with the sequence \(0, 1, 0, 1, \dots\)). But the lemma offers a simple way to show that a sequence does not converge: we find two subsequences with different limits. Let us try it first on the sequence \(((-1)^n)\), whose divergence we proved in lemma 1.40 on a sequence without a limit with the error \(\eta = \tfrac12\).

  1. The subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\) has the terms \((-1)^{2k} = 1\). This is the constant sequence \(1, 1, 1, \dots\), which converges to \(1\) (section 1.9.2).
  2. The subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\) has the terms \((-1)^{2k-1} = -1\), so it converges to \(-1\). The places \(2k - 1\) increase strictly, because \(2(k + 1) - 1 = (2k - 1) + 2\).
  3. Suppose that the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) converges to some number \(d\). Then by lemma 1.54 on the limit of a subsequence both subsequences converge to \(d\).
  4. By lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence, each of the two subsequences has only one limit, so \(d = 1\) by step 1 and \(d = -1\) by step 2. Then \(1 = -1\), which is not true. So the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) does not converge.

This proof is shorter than the first one, because the work with the error \(\eta\) has already been done in the proofs of the lemma on the limit of a subsequence and of the lemma on the uniqueness of the limit. In the same way we show that the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) does not converge, except that its subsequences are not constant.

  1. At an even place \(n = 2k\) we have \(x_{2\mathrm{k}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2k}\). Let us take the sequence \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = 1 + 1/n\). The distance of its term from \(1\) is \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - 1| = 1/n = |1/n - 0|\). By lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n, for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a place \(N\) such that \(|1/n - 0| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\), and so for the same \(N\) we have \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - 1| < \eta\). So the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(1\).
  2. The terms \(x_{2\mathrm{k}} = y_{2\mathrm{k}}\) form a subsequence of the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k\). So by the lemma on the limit of a subsequence they converge to \(1\).
  3. At an odd place \(n = 2k - 1\) we have \(x_{2\mathrm{k}-1} = -\big(1 + \frac{1}{2k-1}\big)\). The sequence \(z_{\mathrm{n}} = -(1 + 1/n)\) converges to \(-1\), because \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - (-1)| = |-1/n| = 1/n\), and for the same \(N\) as in step 1 we have \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - (-1)| < \eta\). The terms \(x_{2\mathrm{k}-1} = z_{2\mathrm{k}-1}\) form a subsequence of the sequence \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\), so they converge to \(-1\).
  4. If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converged to a number \(d\), then by the lemma on the limit of a subsequence both subsequences of steps 2 and 3 would converge to \(d\), and by the uniqueness of the limit we would have \(d = 1\) and \(d = -1\). So \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) does not converge.

Figure 1.74 shows both subsequences, the even one with blue dots and the odd one with the others. From a bounded sequence that does not converge we have chosen a convergent subsequence, because we knew the formula for its terms. Can this be done for every bounded sequence, also when we do not know a formula?

1.11.3 Infinitely many terms

How would we find a convergent subsequence without a formula? Bisection suggests itself. All terms of a bounded sequence lie in some interval. We halve it and keep the half in which infinitely many terms lie, then we halve this half, and so on. The intervals shrink, and in each of them there are enough terms to choose from it the next term of the subsequence at a later place than the previous one. Before we carry this out, we must say precisely what it means that a half holds infinitely many terms, and show that at least one of the two halves has this property.

In a sequence we count places, not values. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) has terms at infinitely many places in the set \(\{1\}\), which has a single element, because every term at an even place equals \(1\). So that a set holds infinitely many terms will mean that terms at arbitrarily late places lie in it.

Definition 1.55 Infinitely many terms in a set (neskončno mnogo členov v množici)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and \(A\) a set of real numbers. The set \(A\) holds infinitely many terms (neskončno mnogo členov) of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) if for every natural number \(N\) there is a natural number \(n \ge N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A\).

Source: LEBL1, in the second proof of Thm 2.3.8 (PDF p. 78), speaks of infinitely many places \(n\) at which \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) lies in an interval, without defining the notion separately. The definition is not from the source.

The definition is a game of two players, like the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38. The first picks a place \(N\), however late. The second must find a term in \(A\) at the place \(N\) or later. Let us try it on the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) (figure 1.78). The interval \(A = [1, 2]\) holds infinitely many terms. For every \(N\) the place \(n = 2N\) is even and at least \(N\), and the term at it is \(1 + \frac{1}{2N}\), which lies between \(1\) and \(\tfrac32\), so in \([1, 2]\). The interval \(B = [-\tfrac52, -\tfrac32]\), though, holds only the term \(x_1 = -2\). The terms at even places are positive, and the terms at odd places from the third on are \(-(1 + 1/n) \ge -(1 + \tfrac13) = -\tfrac43\), which is more than \(-\tfrac32\). So for \(N = 2\) there is no place \(n \ge 2\) with its term in \(B\), and \(B\) does not hold infinitely many terms.

The example of the interval \(B\) shows what it means that a set does not hold infinitely many terms. The definition requires a term in the set for every place \(N\). If this does not hold, there is at least one place \(N\) for which there is no such term, that is, no term at the place \(N\) or later lies in the set. So a set does not hold infinitely many terms exactly when there is a place from which on no term lies in it. The terms that lie in it are then all among \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\).

In bisection we split an interval into two halves, and every number of the interval lies in at least one of them. We need a name for this situation. The union (unija) of the sets \(A\) and \(B\), written \(A \cup B\), is the set of all numbers that lie in \(A\) or in \(B\) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.4, PDF p. 10). The word or allows both here, so the union also contains the numbers that lie in both sets. The closed interval \([a, b]\) with the midpoint \(m = \tfrac12(a + b)\) is the union of its halves,

\[[a, b] = [a, m] \cup [m, b] .\]

Indeed, every number \(t\) with \(a \le t \le b\) satisfies \(t \le m\) or \(t \ge m\), so it lies in the left or in the right half, and every number of either half lies in \([a, b]\). The midpoint \(m\) lies in both halves.

If the union of two sets holds infinitely many terms, does one of the two sets hold infinitely many terms? We expect so. If each of the sets held only terms before some place, both together would also hold only terms before some place. Let us show this precisely.

Lemma 1.56 One of the halves (ena od polovic)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of real numbers and \(A\) and \(B\) sets of real numbers. If the union \(A \cup B\) holds infinitely many terms of the sequence, then \(A\) or \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.

Proof

We must show that terms at arbitrarily late places lie in at least one of the sets \(A\) and \(B\).

  1. We argue by contradiction. Suppose that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.
  2. Since \(A\) does not hold infinitely many terms, the condition of definition 1.55 of infinitely many terms in a set does not hold for every place. So there is a place \(N_1\) for which there is no \(n \ge N_1\) with \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A\): from the place \(N_1\) on no term lies in \(A\).
  3. In the same way there is a place \(N_2\) from which on no term lies in \(B\).
  4. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\), and let \(n \ge N\). Then \(n \ge N_1\) and \(n \ge N_2\), so by steps 2 and 3, \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) lies neither in \(A\) nor in \(B\). So it does not lie in \(A \cup B\) either, which contains only numbers from \(A\) or from \(B\).
  5. The set \(A \cup B\) holds infinitely many terms, so by the definition, used with this place \(N\), there is an \(n \ge N\) with \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in A \cup B\). This contradicts step 4. The assumption of step 1 is false, so \(A\) or \(B\) holds infinitely many terms.

Source: LEBL1 uses the conclusion in the second proof of Thm 2.3.8 (PDF p. 78) without proving it. The proof is not from the source.

The proof again used the larger of two places, like the proof of lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence. From the place \(N_1\) on no term lies in \(A\), from the place \(N_2\) on none in \(B\), and from the larger of the two places on none in either (figure 1.79). Let us try the lemma on the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) with the interval \([-2, -1] = [-2, -\tfrac32] \cup [-\tfrac32, -1]\). The interval \([-2, -1]\) holds infinitely many terms, because all terms at odd places lie in it, \(-2 \le -(1 + 1/n) < -1\). The left half holds only the term \(x_1 = -2\), as we saw for the interval \(B\). So by the lemma the right half \([-\tfrac32, -1]\) holds infinitely many terms, and indeed all terms at odd places from the third on lie in it. But the lemma does not say which half has this property, and both may have it. All terms at odd places lie in \([-2, 0]\), all terms at even places in \([0, 2]\).

1.11.4 The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem

Now let us carry out bisection on the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\). All its terms lie in the interval \([a_1, b_1] = [-2, 2]\). For some natural number \(k\) let the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\), which holds infinitely many terms, be already determined, and let \(m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}})\) be its midpoint.

  • If the left half \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, m_{\mathrm{k}}]\) holds infinitely many terms, the next interval is the left half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  • Otherwise the next interval is the right half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\). By lemma 1.56 on one of the halves it holds infinitely many terms.

From every interval we also choose a term of the subsequence. The first chosen term is \(x_1\), that is, \(n_1 = 1\). When the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\) is determined, we take for \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) the smallest place after the place \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) at which the term lies in this interval. Such a place exists, because the interval holds infinitely many terms. The table shows the first seven steps, and figure 1.80 shows the intervals with the terms in them.

1 \(-2\) \(2\) 1 \(-2\) \(0\) all at odd places left half
2 \(-2\) \(0\) 3 \(-1{,}3333\dots\) \(-1\) all at odd places left half
3 \(-2\) \(-1\) 5 \(-1{,}2\) \(-1{,}5\) only \(x_1\) right half

5 \(-1{,}25\) \(-1\) 9 \(-1{,}1111\dots\) \(-1{,}125\) \(x_5\), \(x_7\) right half
6 \(-1{,}125\) \(-1\) 11 \(-1{,}0909\dots\) \(-1{,}0625\) \(x_9\), \(x_{11}\), \(x_{13}\), \(x_{15}\) right half
7 \(-1{,}0625\) \(-1\) 17 \(-1{,}0588\dots\) \(-1{,}03125\) \(x_{17}\), \(x_{19}\), \(\dots\), \(x_{31}\) right half

The endpoints and midpoints are fractions with denominators \(1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\) and are written exactly in the table. The chosen terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = -(1 + 1/n_{\mathrm{k}})\) that end in three dots are cut off after four decimals.

How do we know whether the left half holds infinitely many terms? Here, from the formula. The terms at even places are larger than \(1\). The terms at odd places are \(-1 - 1/n\) and lie below \(-1\), ever closer to \(-1\). At \(k = 1\) and \(k = 2\) the left half contains all terms at odd places. From \(k = 3\) on the right endpoint is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} = -1\), but the left half does not reach \(-1\), because its right endpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is smaller than \(-1\). The term \(-1 - 1/n\) lies in the left half only if \(-1 - 1/n \le m_{\mathrm{k}}\), that is, only if \(1/n \ge -1 - m_{\mathrm{k}}\). This holds only for the places \(n \le 1/(-1 - m_{\mathrm{k}})\), and from some place on for none. At \(k = 3\) we have \(m_3 = -\tfrac32\) and \(-1 - m_3 = \tfrac12\), so only the places \(n \le 2\) come into question, and the only odd one among them is \(n = 1\). The right half, on the other hand, contains all terms at odd places from some place on.

For a general sequence we have no formula. In bisection for \(\sqrt 3\) we recognized the half by the square of the midpoint, with one computation. Here no check of finitely many terms tells us whether a half holds terms at arbitrarily late places. So the proof of the theorem will not be a procedure for computing. It only tells us that at least one half has this property, and it does so by the lemma on one of the halves, by contradiction.

The chosen places are \(1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 17, \dots\) and are not evenly spaced. At \(k = 7\) the interval has the endpoints \(-1{,}0625\) and \(-1\). The terms \(x_{13} = -\tfrac{14}{13} = -1{,}0769\dots\) and \(x_{15} = -\tfrac{16}{15} = -1{,}0666\dots\) lie below \(-1{,}0625\), so the first term after the place \(11\) that lies in the interval is only \(x_{17}\). Every place is at least as large as \(k\), as lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence requires. The intervals shrink toward \(-1\), and the chosen terms with them (figure 1.81). The number \(-1\) is not a term of the sequence, but the chosen terms approach it, just as the left endpoints of the bisection approached the number \(\sqrt 3\), which is not a fraction.

The bisection for \(\sqrt 3\) caught the point, although we did not know it in advance. We shall do the same for an arbitrary bounded sequence whose terms cannot be written with a formula.

From history

Bernard Bolzano was a professor of the philosophy of religion in Prague and held advanced views on variables and limits (CAJORI, PDF p. 383). Mathematicians overlooked his writings until Hermann Hankel called attention to them. In Hankel's words Bolzano has everything that places him beside Cauchy in the treatment of infinite sums, except that he lacks the art of the French to work out their ideas and to communicate them in the most suitable and attractive way. So he remained unknown and was soon forgotten (CAJORI, PDF p. 383-384). In 1872 Hermann Amandus Schwarz looked upon Bolzano as the inventor of a line of reasoning that Weierstrass developed further (CAJORI, PDF p. 384). The theorem that follows bears the names of both.

Now let us write the bisection procedure that caught \(\sqrt 3\) for an arbitrary bounded sequence and prove that the chosen terms converge. The proof has four parts. First we find the starting interval. Then we build the intervals and the places by induction. Then we check the properties of the intervals, the same as in bisection for \(\sqrt 3\). At the end we show that the chosen terms converge. There we get the limit as the supremum of the left endpoints, by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51, just as in section 1.10.5. The theorem is named after the Czech mathematician Bolzano (1781-1848) and the German mathematician Weierstrass (1815-1897) (LEBL1, PDF p. 73).

Theorem 1.57 Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (Bolzano-Weierstrassov izrek)

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

Proof

We must show that from a bounded sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) we can choose a subsequence that converges.

The starting interval.

  1. The sequence is bounded, so the set of its terms has a lower bound \(a\) and an upper bound \(b\) (definitions 1.37 and 1.29). So \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\).
  2. From \(a \le x_1 \le b\) it follows that \(a \le b\). If \(a = b\), we replace the upper bound by \(b + 1\), which is an upper bound as well. So we may take \(a < b\), as the definition of an interval 1.43 requires.
  3. All terms lie in \([a, b]\), so it holds infinitely many terms: for every place \(N\) the term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\) lies in \([a, b]\), and this is a term at the place \(n = N \ge N\), as definition 1.55 of infinitely many terms in a set requires.

Building the intervals and the places. We build the intervals \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) step by step, so that at every \(k\) two requirements hold. The first: \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) holds infinitely many terms. The second: \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \in [a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\).

  1. Basis. Let \(a_1 = a\), \(b_1 = b\) and \(n_1 = 1\). We have \(a_1 < b_1\) by step 2, the first requirement by step 3, and the second requirement, because \(x_1 \in [a, b]\) by step 1.
  2. For some \(k\) let \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) be already determined so that both requirements hold, and let \(m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}})\) be the midpoint of the interval. The interval is the union of its halves, \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}] = [a_{\mathrm{k}}, m_{\mathrm{k}}] \cup [m_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\), so by the first requirement the union of the halves holds infinitely many terms. By lemma 1.56 on one of the halves at least one half holds infinitely many terms.
  3. If the left half holds infinitely many terms, let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\). Otherwise the right half holds infinitely many terms by step 5, and let \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\). In both cases the first requirement holds for \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\), and \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} < b_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), because the midpoint lies strictly between the endpoints (section 1.10.1).
  4. We use the first requirement for \([a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\) with the place \(N = n_{\mathrm{k}} + 1\): there is a place \(n \ge n_{\mathrm{k}} + 1\) with the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in [a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\). The set of such places is a nonempty set of natural numbers, so it has a smallest element (section 1.8.1). This element is \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). So \(n_{\mathrm{k}+1} > n_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{k}+1}} \in [a_{\mathrm{k}+1}, b_{\mathrm{k}+1}]\), which is the second requirement at \(k + 1\).
  5. Step 4 is the basis, and steps 5 to 7 are the induction step. So by the principle of induction 1.44, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) are determined for every natural number \(k\), and both requirements hold at every \(k\).

Properties.

  1. By step 7, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} < n_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for every \(k\). So the places increase strictly, and \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) (definition 1.52).
  2. The left endpoints do not decrease and the right ones do not increase, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), because a new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\), which lies between the old endpoints.
  3. The length of the interval is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = (b - a)/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) the length is \(b - a = (b - a)/2^0\). Every step halves the length, because each half has the length \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = b_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the formula at \(k\) gives the formula at \(k + 1\), \(\tfrac12 \cdot (b - a)/2^{k-1} = (b - a)/2^k\), and by the principle of induction the formula holds for every \(k\), just like the third property of bisection in section 1.10.2.

The limit.

  1. The sequence of left endpoints \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) is increasing by step 10. It is bounded, because \(a = a_1 \le a_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}} \le b_1 = b\) by step 8 and by the same lemma for the decreasing sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\). So by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51 it converges to the number \(c = \sup\{a_{\mathrm{k}} : k \in \mathbb N\}\).
  2. The number \(c\) lies in every interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\). Since the supremum is an upper bound, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\). Let us also show \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let \(j\) be any natural number. For \(j \le k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the lemma on the terms of a monotone sequence and by step 8. For \(j > k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} < b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by step 8 and by the same lemma for the decreasing sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the number \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an upper bound of all left endpoints, and the supremum \(c\) is not larger than any upper bound. So \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) lies in \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) by the second requirement, and the number \(c\) by step 13. Two numbers in the same interval are at most its length apart. From \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\) it follows that \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\), and from \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\) that \(c - x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\). So by step 11
    \[|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{b - a}{2^{k-1}} .\]
  4. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half, the sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\) converges to \(0\). We use the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 for it with the error \(\eta/(b - a)\), which is positive because \(b > a\). There is a place \(N\) such that \(|1/2^{k-1} - 0| = 1/2^{k-1} < \eta/(b - a)\) for every \(k \ge N\). We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(b - a\) and get \((b - a)/2^{k-1} < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\).
  5. By steps 14 and 15, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(c\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 2.3.8, second proof (PDF pp. 78-79). The source shows that the two sequences of endpoints have the same limit and finishes with the squeeze lemma. We, as in section 1.10.5, show that the limit of the left endpoints lies in every interval, and we bound the distance of the chosen term from it by the length of the interval. The choice of the smallest place is not from the source.

What did the proof show? Bisection with the lemma on one of the halves builds intervals that shrink and each of which holds infinitely many terms. From each interval we choose a term at a later place than the previous one. The completeness axiom, hidden in the monotone convergence theorem, supplies the number \(c\), which lies in all the intervals. The chosen terms are at most the length of the interval away from it, and this length goes to zero. For the sequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n (1 + 1/n)\) we have \(c = -1\). From \(k = 3\) on \(b_{\mathrm{k}} = -1\), and the left endpoints \(-2, -2, -2, -\tfrac32, -\tfrac54, \dots\) approach \(-1\) (figure 1.81).

The theorem guarantees that a convergent subsequence exists, but it does not say which one or to which limit. If the rule gave preference to the right half, then for our sequence we would keep the interval \([0, 2]\), in which all terms at even places lie, already at the first step, and we would get a subsequence with the limit \(1\).

The limit lies in the starting interval. All chosen terms lie in \([a, b]\), so by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, also \(a \le c \le b\). We will need this in section 1.13, where a point found as a limit must lie in the interval in which we search.

The theorem needs two things, the boundedness of the sequence and the completeness axiom. Without boundedness it fails. The sequence \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), that is, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = n\), has no convergent subsequence. Its subsequence with the places \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) has the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = n_{\mathrm{k}}\). For every number \(B\) there is by the Archimedean property 1.35 a natural number \(k > B\), and then by the lemma on the indices of a subsequence \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} = n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k > B\). So the subsequence is not bounded, while a convergent sequence is bounded by lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence. Without the completeness axiom the theorem fails as well. The left endpoints of the bisection for \(\sqrt 3\) from section 1.10.1 are a bounded sequence of fractions that converges to \(\sqrt 3\). By the lemma on the limit of a subsequence every subsequence of it converges to \(\sqrt 3\), which by lemma 1.27 is not a fraction. A limit that was a fraction would be equal to \(\sqrt 3\) by the uniqueness of the limit. So among the fractions alone this sequence has no convergent subsequence.

The theorem is one of the results that carry this chapter, because it guarantees the existence of a point that we cannot compute. We will always use it in the same pattern: first we find a sequence with the desired property, then we choose from it a subsequence that converges (LEBL1, PDF p. 130). When we look for the point where the value of a function is largest, the theorem will choose, from a sequence of points at which the values approach the largest one, a subsequence of points that converge to some point \(c\). But for the values of the function at the chosen points to approach the value at \(c\) too, the function must have a property that we have so far described only in words: its graph has no jumps. The next section defines this property precisely.
