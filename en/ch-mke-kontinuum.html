This chapter continues the finite element method. In chapter 7 we used it to compute bars, which have one dimension, and in chapter 6 we proved that the Ritz approximation is the best one in its space. Now we apply both to a body made of material in the plane or in space, for example a plate, a tube or a sphere. In chapter 11 we described such a body with equations, but we solved it only for a few simple shapes.

Let us look at the words of the title. A finite element (končni element) is a piece of a body of finite size that can be described by a few numbers. A continuum (kontinuum) is a body that we treat as material without gaps, in which every point has its own temperature, its own displacement and its own stress. Finite elements of a continuum are therefore pieces of such material from which we assemble a body that we cannot compute in one piece.

Two machines show why this matters. A thick tube with radii 40 mm and 60 mm is under an internal pressure of 30 MPa. A metal plate that we heat and prevent from expanding develops stresses without a single external force. In both cases we want to know where in the body the stresses are largest, and the answer must come from a computation, not an estimate.

The chapter promises three things. First we start with the simplest piece, the triangle, and with a field of one number at each node, such as temperature, and then compute displacements on the same triangle. Then we show how pieces with curved edges and quadrilaterals give more accurate elements, and how an integral over an element is replaced by a sum of values at a few points. Finally we say when an element fails and how to test for it, and compute the tube and the sphere also in space, each result compared with the exact solution from chapter 11.
