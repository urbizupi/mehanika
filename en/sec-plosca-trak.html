Chapter 18 Plates

18.4 The plate as a strip: cylindrical bending

A ditch carrying water is covered with a steel plate (figure 18.37). The plate is several metres long, but across the ditch it measures only \(L = 500\) mm, and it is \(h = 10\) mm thick, fifty times less. It rests on the two long edges of the ditch and is loaded by water or snow with a uniform pressure of \(10\) kPa downward, about what a metre-high column of water makes. The material is the steel of the book, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) (section 3.1.2). The plate equation (theorem 18.12) has two variables, \(x\) and \(y\), and conditions on the whole edge. Is there a case in which this equation shrinks to the beam of chapter 5, and if there is, is the stiffness of the plate there the same \(EI\)? The answer matters for everything that follows: only in such a case can we test the plate against something we already know.

We look at the plate from the side, along the direction \(y\) (figure 18.37, top). We draw the axis \(x\) along the span and the axis \(z\) upward, as in section 18.1. The deflection \(w\) and the load \(q\) are positive in the direction \(z\), as in the plate equation. The load on our cover points down, so \(q = -10\) kPa and the deflection is negative. In this view the middle plane of the plate (definition 18.1) is a line, horizontal before loading. The plate lies on two supports that prevent only the vertical displacement: the left one is a hinge, the right one a roller. This is an idealization, the true support is the edge of the ditch. A hinge is the right choice because the plate only lies on the edge of the ditch: nothing holds its edge against turning, so the edge carries no moment. It does not hold when the plate is welded or bolted to the edge. Then it is closer to clamping and the deflection is up to five times smaller (corollary 18.26 below). Friction on the edge of the ditch only keeps the edge from sliding horizontally, which counts only once the middle plane lengthens, for our cover by \(2{,}3\) % of the bending strain (the paragraph after the corollary). If the edge of the ditch is slightly compressed under the plate, the plate sinks as a whole, equally at both edges, and therefore bends no more. On it acts the load \(q\) per unit area, equal along the whole span and pointing down. Under it the middle plane bends downward, at the middle by \(|w(L/2)|\), across the span \(L\) between the supports.

The load does not change in the direction \(y\), so the side view is the same at every \(y\). The lower half of the figure shows the same plate obliquely. The middle plane before loading is a flat rectangle that continues far back in the direction of the axis \(y\). After loading the curved surface is made of the same curves, one for each \(y\), and is not curved in the direction \(y\). In geometry, a surface traced by parallel lines moving along a curve is called a cylinder. Here the lines are parallel to the axis \(y\) and the curve is the deflection \(w(x)\). Hence the name cylindrical bending. The word strip we know from everyday life, a narrow long piece. A plate strip is a piece of plate of width \(1\) (one metre) that lies along \(x\) and extends over the whole span. The width \(1\) means that forces and moments per unit length of edge (\(\mathrm{N/m}\) and \(\mathrm{N\,m/m}\)) equal in value those carried by the strip.

Definition 18.24 Cylindrical bending and plate strip (valjasti upogib in trak plošče)

Let the plate be Kirchhoff (definition 18.2) with a middle plane that is the band \(\Omega = \{(x, y) : 0 < x < L,\ y \in \mathbb{R}\}\) of width \(L\), unbounded in the direction \(y\). The band is not bounded, so this is an idealization of a long plate: definition 18.1 holds for every bounded piece of the band, and the characteristic plan dimension \(a\) is \(L\). The plate is supported along the edges \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), and the load per unit area \(q = q(x)\) is a function of \(x\) only. Cylindrical bending is a deflection in which the deflection function depends only on \(x\), that is \(w(x, y) = w(x)\) for every \(y\). The plate strip is the band \(\{(x, y) : 0 \le x \le L,\ y_1 \le y \le y_1 + 1\}\) of width \(1\) (one metre) and arbitrary \(y_1\). The function \(w : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) is a real function of one variable.

Source: The notion follows RPS section 4.1 (PDF p. 143), where the plate is long in the direction \(y\) and \(w\) is a function of \(x\) only, and VK section 3.2.1 (PDF p. 48), where all strips of unit width parallel to the axis \(x\) bend equally. The symbol \(\Omega\) and the unboundedness in the direction \(y\) as an idealization are ours.

The unboundedness in the direction \(y\) is an idealization (the base plane in figure 18.37 reaches infinitely far back). A real plate has an end, at which the strip is no longer equal to its neighbours. How long it must be for this end to be negligible is shown by the test at the end of the section: for an error in the deflection at the middle below \(1\) %, \(4{,}5\) times the span suffices (the limit is at \(4{,}3\)), for our ditch \(2{,}25\) m, which a cover several metres long exceeds.

The simplest guess is that the strip equals a beam of width one metre. Then for the deflection the deflection curve equation (5.33) would hold, \(EI\,v'''' = q\), with \(I = h^3/12\) per metre of width. Is that true? Exactly here we see what the plate adds to the beam. Think first with what we know from everyday life. If we squeeze an eraser in the hand, it bulges at the sides: what we squeeze in one direction widens in the perpendicular ones. When a beam bends (section 5.1), the upper fibres are compressed (if the bending points down), so they would widen in the direction \(y\), and the lower ones are stretched and would narrow. A beam can do that: its side face is free. We met the same effect in section 18.1.4, where the curvature was given. Here the load is given, and from the effect we get the whole deflection of the strip. Look at the cross-section at midspan from the other side, along the axis \(x\) (figure 18.38).

In panel (a) is the cross-section of a narrow beam, before loading a dashed rectangle with width \(b\) and thickness \(h\). After loading (orange) the upper edge widens, the lower one narrows and the cross-section curves into an arc turned opposite to the bending along \(x\): the lower edge rises at the middle above its chord (the derivation is in example 18.27). In panel (b) is a strip of a wide plate, of thickness \(h\). In a plate every strip bends equally to its neighbour, since the load and the supports do not depend on \(y\). If the upper fibres of the strip widened sideways, the fibres of the neighbour would have to widen too and would be pushed together. Since they are equal, no part moves sideways relative to its neighbour: the displacement in the direction \(y\) is the same everywhere, so distances in the direction \(y\) do not change. This is not a special property of the strip, it is a consequence of the equality of all strips. What holds the strip? We draw it as a free body: we move the two neighbouring strips away and replace them by the forces with which they acted on it. By Newton's third law the strip and its neighbour press on each other with equal forces, at the upper face as compression, at the lower as tension. The force per unit area of the side face is the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). At the middle plane \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is zero, because there the fibre is neither compressed nor stretched and would not widen at all, and towards the surfaces it grows as the compression or tension of the fibres grows. The compression at the top and the tension at the bottom are equal and opposite, so their sum is zero, but a turning effect remains, a couple, the moment \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\). The pair of curved arrows shows it: on the left face clockwise, on the right face counterclockwise, so the turning effects on the two faces cancel and the strip as a whole does not turn. The effect of the couple is that the curvature \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}}\) stays zero. A beam is allowed the sideways motion, so the stiffness of the strip is not the stiffness of the beam.

What does this mean for a small piece of material? In panel (c) we look from above, with the axis \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up, at a small piece of the upper surface of the strip at midspan. Before loading it is a square. Along \(x\) it is compressed by the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), because the upper surface is compressed when the plate bends downward, and along \(y\) by the sideways pressure \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) of the neighbours of panel (b). After loading the piece is shorter in the direction \(x\) and equally wide in the direction \(y\), because the neighbours do not let it widen. The two numbers at the arrows are computed for our cover after corollary 18.26.

This is exactly what the next lemma says (the strip stays flat). It uses the plate equation of theorem 18.12, the displacement field of the plate and Hooke's law of a layer in plane stress.

Lemma 18.25 Cylindrical bending is a beam with stiffness \(D\) (valjasti upogib je nosilec s togostjo \(D\))

Let the plate be Kirchhoff with modulus \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), let \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\) be its bending rigidity (definition 18.6) and let the plate bend cylindrically (definition 18.24) with \(w \in C^4([0, L])\). Then:

  1. The deflection solves \(D\,w'''' = q\) on \((0, L)\), which is the deflection curve equation (5.33) with \(EI\) replaced by \(D\).
  2. The strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -z\,w''\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), the curvature in the direction \(y\) is zero.
  3. In every layer \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,z\,w''\).
  4. \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,w''\) (sign as for the beam, \(M = EI\,v''\) in theorem 5.35), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu\,M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  5. The largest stress is on the surface \(z = \pm h/2\) and is \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}| = 6\,|M_{\mathrm{x}}|/h^2\), \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}| = \nu\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|\).
Proof

We show that from \(w = w(x)\) follow the equation, the strains, the stresses and the moments: the plate equation gives the first point, the displacement field the second, Hooke's law the third, the integral over the thickness the fourth and fifth. We draw steps 3 to 5 on a piece and a section of our cover at midspan (figure 18.39), and we calculate the numbers in the figure below for the corollary.

  1. Since \(w(x, y) = w(x)\) for every \(y\) (the curved surface is made of equal curves and is not curved in the direction \(y\)), all partial derivatives of \(w\) with respect to \(y\) are zero: \(w_{\mathrm{y}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}} = w_{\mathrm{xy}} = w_{\mathrm{xyy}} = w_{\mathrm{yyyy}} = 0\). Therefore \(\nabla^4 w = w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\,w_{\mathrm{xxyy}} + w_{\mathrm{yyyy}} = w''''\) and the plate equation \(D\,\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12) gives \(D\,w'''' = q\).
  2. By theorem 18.3 \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -z\,w_{\mathrm{x}} = -z\,w'\) (the normal tilts by the slope and a point at height \(z\) moves sideways by \(z\,w'\)), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -z\,w_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) (the strip does not move sideways) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = w\). By equations (1.71) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = -z\,w''\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = 0\). The curvature in the direction \(y\) is \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) (definition 18.4).
  3. Every layer is in plane stress (definition 18.2, theorem 11.37), and its strains in the plane are by equations (11.50) with \(E' = E\) and \(\nu' = \nu\): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\). From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) follows \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the sideways pressure on the piece that does not let it widen. We insert this into \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\). In the figure this is the sum in panel (a), we lay the shortenings one after another on the axis: the compression \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) alone shortens the piece by \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|/E\), the sideways pressure lengthens it by \(\nu\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}|/E = \nu^2\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|/E\), so the shortening \((1 - \nu^2)\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|/E\) remains. Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/(1 - \nu^2) = -E\,z\,w''/(1 - \nu^2)\). From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) follows also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  4. The moments per unit length are the integrals of the stresses multiplied by \(z\) over the thickness \([-h/2, h/2]\) (definition 18.5). In panel (b) is the section with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), linear over the thickness. We cut it into thin layers: the layer at height \(z\) with thickness \(\mathrm{d}z\) carries the force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}z\) at the lever arm \(z\) to the middle plane, and the moments of all layers add up to the integral. Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are in the ratio \(\nu\) at every point, the variation of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is the same, only \(\nu\) times shorter, so also their moments are in the ratio \(\nu\): \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu\,M_{\mathrm{x}}\). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) on the side face of the strip, linear over the thickness, are exactly the couple \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\). The force of a layer grows with \(z\) and so does the lever arm, so \(z^2\) stands in the integral. For the magnitude \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} z^2\,\mathrm{d}z = h^3/12\) (theorem 3.55), so \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}| = \dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,|w''|\,\dfrac{h^3}{12} = D\,|w''|\). We get the same without an integral: each of the two stress triangles has the resultant \(F = |\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(h/2)|\,h/4\), that is the area of the triangle, at its centroid \(h/3\) from the middle plane. The distance between the resultants is \(2h/3\), so their couple is \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}| = F \cdot 2h/3 = |\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(h/2)|\,h^2/6\). The sign is set by the convention as for the beam, see theorem 18.7, which gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{yy}}) = D\,w''\). The moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is proportional to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so it is zero.
  5. From step 3, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -12\,D\,w''\,z/h^3\), because \(E/(1 - \nu^2) = 12\,D/h^3\), that is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -12\,M_{\mathrm{x}}\,z/h^3\). Its magnitude is largest at \(|z| = h/2\), on both surfaces, where also the longest arrows for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are, and is there \(6\,|M_{\mathrm{x}}|/h^2\) (corollary 18.8), which gives also the couple of resultants from step 4. By step 3, \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}| = \nu\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|\).

Source: RPS section 4.2 (PDF p. 144), where equation (4.2.3) holds for the plate strip and has the same form as for the beam, with \(D_{11}\) instead of \(EI\). VK section 3.2.1 (PDF pp. 48-50), where equation (3.1) \(\mathrm{d}^4 w/\mathrm{d}x^4 = p/D\) coincides with the beam equation if we replace \(EI\) by \(D\), and \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu M_{\mathrm{x}}\). The proof is ours, from layers in plane stress, and shows the cause of the factor \(1 - \nu^2\).

The lemma says two things. First, the equation is the same as for the beam, so we know how to solve it. Second, \(D\) stands in place of \(EI\). The reason is in step 3: the strip cannot widen, so a stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) appears, and by Hooke's law it reduces the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by the factor \(1 - \nu^2\) at the same stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Why does it reduce it? The sideways pressure \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) squeezes the piece in the direction \(y\), and like an eraser that we squeeze, the piece therefore lengthens in the direction \(x\), by \(\nu\,|\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}|/E\). This lengthening takes away part of the shortening made by the compression \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and the piece shortens less than in a beam, only \(0{,}91\) times as much:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} - \nu\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{E} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} - \nu^2\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} = (1 - \nu^2)\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} .\]

In a hinged strip the moment is fixed by equilibrium alone, the same as in the beam, so the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) on the surface is the same too. A smaller shortening means a smaller curvature \(w'' = -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/z\), and the deflection is obtained from the curvature by integrating twice, so the deflection of the strip is \((1 - \nu^2)\) times that of the beam. Since \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(1 - \nu^2 = 0{,}91\) and \(D = EI/0{,}91\), so \(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m, while \(EI = E\,h^3/12 = 16\,333{,}3\) N m per metre of width. We know the same factor from chapter 11: the modulus \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) of plane strain (theorem 11.33). The strip really is a plane strain in the plane \(x z\), since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \approx 0\) (RPS section 4.1, PDF p. 143: cylindrical bending is a plane strain, a beam is a plane stress state).

Now we can solve the strip for the two supports we meet most often (figure 18.40).

In panel (a) the strip lies on a hinge and a roller like our cover, the load \(q\) presses it down, the middle plane before loading is dashed. Orange is the strip after loading, and for comparison grey a beam of the same steel with the same load, which sags somewhat deeper. The supports hold the strip with the reactions upward. The boundary conditions are those of definition 18.19: at the support \(w = 0\), because the support does not let the edge go down, and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), because the hinge does not hold the edge against turning (by the lemma this is \(w'' = 0\)). Under the strip is the diagram of the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\): above the axis \(x\) we draw a positive moment, below it a negative one. In panel (b) both edges are clamped in a wall, with the same load. The wall holds the edge in place and horizontal, so \(w = 0\) and \(w' = 0\), and the strip starts horizontally at the wall. Under it is its moment diagram above its own axis. The numbers in the figure are computed for our plate after the corollary.

Corollary 18.26 Strip deflection, simply supported and clamped (poves traku pri členkasti podpori in pri vpetju)

Let a plate strip with the properties of lemma 18.25 carry a constant load \(q\).

  1. For a hinged support on both edges (\(w = w'' = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\))
    \[w(x) = \frac{q}{24\,D}\,x\,(L - x)\,(L^2 + L\,x - x^2) , \tag{18.30}\]
    the deflection at the middle, largest in absolute value, is \(w(L/2) = \dfrac{5\,q\,L^4}{384\,D}\), \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is largest at the middle, \(\dfrac{|q|\,L^2}{8}\), and zero at the edge. The reaction of each support is \(-q\,L/2\) per metre of edge (in the direction \(z\)).
  2. For clamping on both edges (\(w = w' = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\))
    \[w(x) = \frac{q}{24\,D}\,x^2\,(L - x)^2 , \tag{18.31}\]
    the deflection at the middle, largest in absolute value, is \(w(L/2) = \dfrac{q\,L^4}{384\,D}\), \(|M_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is \(\dfrac{|q|\,L^2}{12}\) at the edge and \(\dfrac{|q|\,L^2}{24}\) at the middle.
  3. In each of the two cases this is the only solution of the lemma with the given boundary conditions.
Proof

We solve the ordinary equation \(D\,w'''' = q\) of lemma 18.25 with four conditions and show uniqueness.

  1. The general solution is \(w = \dfrac{q\,x^4}{24\,D} + c_1\,x^3 + c_2\,x^2 + c_3\,x + c_4\): the fourth derivative is \(q/D\) (rule for the derivative of a power, lemma 1.82) and a polynomial of degree three has fourth derivative zero. Conversely, the difference of two solutions has zero fourth derivative and a function with zero derivative is constant (corollary 1.86), four times in a row, so the difference is a polynomial of degree three. The four constants are fixed by the four boundary conditions.
  2. Hinged support (the conditions at the edge): \(w(0) = 0\) gives \(c_4 = 0\), \(w''(0) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = 0\) (\(w'' = \dfrac{q\,x^2}{2\,D} + 6\,c_1\,x + 2\,c_2\)). From \(w''(L) = 0\) we get \(c_1 = -\dfrac{q\,L}{12\,D}\), from \(w(L) = 0\) we get \(c_3 = \dfrac{q\,L^3}{24\,D}\). We insert and factor: \(\dfrac{q}{24\,D}\,(x^4 - 2\,L\,x^3 + L^3\,x) = \dfrac{q}{24\,D}\,x\,(L - x)\,(L^2 + L\,x - x^2)\), which is (18.30) (multiply out the brackets and compare coefficients). At \(x = L/2\) we have \(\tfrac{L}{2}\cdot\tfrac{L}{2}\cdot\tfrac54 L^2 = \tfrac{5}{16}L^4\), so \(w(L/2) = 5\,q\,L^4/(384\,D)\). The derivative \(w' = \dfrac{q}{24\,D}\,(2\,x - L)\,(2\,x^2 - 2\,L\,x - L^2)\) is zero on \((0, L)\) only at \(x = L/2\) (the strip is horizontal only at the middle), since the second factor has its zeros \(L\,(1 \pm \sqrt3)/2\) outside \([0, L]\). Since \(w(0) = w(L) = 0\), \(|w|\) is largest at \(x = L/2\). The moment is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,w'' = -\dfrac{q}{2}\,x\,(L - x)\), whose absolute value is largest at \(x = L/2\), with value \(|q|\,L^2/8\) (the parabola \(x(L-x)\) has its vertex at \(L/2\)). The two reactions are equal by symmetry and together balance the load \(q\,L\) per metre of width by equilibrium of the whole strip, so each is \(-q\,L/2\).
  3. Clamping (the conditions at the wall): \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\) gives \(c_4 = c_3 = 0\). The conditions \(w(L) = w'(L) = 0\) are \(c_1\,L^3 + c_2\,L^2 = -\dfrac{q\,L^4}{24\,D}\) and \(3\,c_1\,L^2 + 2\,c_2\,L = -\dfrac{q\,L^3}{6\,D}\). We multiply the first equation by \(2/L\) and subtract it from the second: \(c_1\,L^2 = -\dfrac{q\,L^3}{6\,D} + \dfrac{q\,L^3}{12\,D} = -\dfrac{q\,L^3}{12\,D}\). So \(c_1 = -\dfrac{q\,L}{12\,D}\), and from the first equation \(c_2\,L^2 = -\dfrac{q\,L^4}{24\,D} + \dfrac{q\,L^4}{12\,D}\), hence \(c_2 = \dfrac{q\,L^2}{24\,D}\). We insert: \(\dfrac{q}{24\,D}\,(x^4 - 2\,L\,x^3 + L^2\,x^2) = \dfrac{q}{24\,D}\,x^2\,(L - x)^2\), which is (18.31). At \(x = L/2\) we have \(x^2(L - x)^2 = L^4/16\), so \(w(L/2) = q\,L^4/(384\,D)\), and since \(x\,(L - x) \ge 0\) has its vertex at \(L/2\), \(|w|\) is largest there. The moment is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,w'' = \dfrac{q}{12}\,(6\,x^2 - 6\,L\,x + L^2)\). At \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) it is \(\dfrac{q\,L^2}{12}\) (at the wall), at \(x = L/2\) it is \(-\dfrac{q\,L^2}{24}\) (at the middle). The signs are opposite because the clamping reverses the curvature at the edge: at the wall the strip is convex upward, at the middle downward.
  4. Uniqueness: the difference of two solutions with the same boundary conditions is the deflection of a strip without load on the same supports. We show that this strip is straight, like the middle plane before loading. The difference solves \(D\,d'''' = 0\) with homogeneous conditions, so it is a polynomial \(d = c_1\,x^3 + c_2\,x^2 + c_3\,x + c_4\) (step 1 with \(q = 0\)). For the hinged support \(c_4 = c_2 = 0\), and from \(d''(L) = 6\,c_1\,L = 0\) follows \(c_1 = 0\), then from \(d(L) = c_3\,L = 0\) also \(c_3 = 0\). For clamping \(c_4 = c_3 = 0\) and the system \(c_1\,L^3 + c_2\,L^2 = 0\), \(3\,c_1\,L^2 + 2\,c_2\,L = 0\) has determinant \(2\,L^4 - 3\,L^4 = -L^4 \ne 0\), so \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\).

Source: RPS sections 4.3.2 and 4.3.3 (PDF pp. 145-147), equations (4.3.12) and (4.3.17), where for clamping \(w_{\max} = q_0 a^4/(384\,D_{11})\) and \(M_{\max} = -q_0 a^2/12\) at the edge. The computations are checked with SymPy (outlines/ch-plosce/num-04.py). Uniqueness is our proof, because the sources do not check that the solution is the only one.

Now the numbers for our plate (\(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m, \(q = -10\) kPa, \(L = 500\) mm, table in the script num-04). For the hinged support \(w(L/2) = 5\cdot(-10^4)\cdot 0{,}5^4/(384\cdot 17\,948{,}7)\) m \(= -0{,}4534\) mm, the plate sags at the middle by \(0{,}4534\) mm. For clamping the deflection is five times smaller, \(0{,}0907\) mm (the ratio \(5\) is the ratio of the coefficients \(5/384\) and \(1/384\)). The moment at the middle of the hinged strip is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -q\,L^2/8 = 312{,}50\) N m/m, positive because the plate is compressed on top, the stress on the surface is by the lemma \(6\cdot 312{,}5/0{,}01^2 = 18{,}75\) MPa, so \(8{,}5\) % of the yield strength of the steel (\(220\) MPa, definition 2.7). The same is given by the couple of the resultants on the section: each resultant is \(18{,}75\) MPa \(\cdot\,h/4 = 46{,}875\) kN/m, their distance is \(2h/3 = 6{,}667\) mm, and their couple \(46{,}875\) kN/m \(\cdot\,6{,}667\) mm \(= 312{,}5\) N m/m. The transverse moment is \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu\,M_{\mathrm{x}} = 93{,}75\) N m/m and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 5{,}625\) MPa: the strip carries stress in the direction in which nothing bends, which a beam would not have. For clamping the moment at the edge is \(q\,L^2/12 = -208{,}33\) N m/m (stress \(12{,}5\) MPa in absolute value) and at the middle \(-q\,L^2/24 = 104{,}17\) N m/m (\(6{,}25\) MPa). Why exactly these? In both strips \(M_{\mathrm{x}}'' = D\,w'''' = q\), so the difference of their moments has second derivative zero and is a straight line, by symmetry a horizontal one, so a constant. The walls add the same moment \(q\,L^2/12\) at both edges, which lowers the whole parabola of panel (a) by \(208{,}33\) N m/m, and from the edge to the top there remain \(312{,}5\) N m/m as for the hinged support. Clamping therefore reduces the deflection five times and the largest stress by a third, but moves it to the edge.

The ratio of deflection to thickness \(|w(L/2)|/h = 0{,}453/10 = 0{,}045\) is below the bound \(0{,}2\) of idealization 2 (definition 18.2), under which we treat the middle plane as unstrained. When the deflection is comparable with the thickness, the middle plane lengthens, which is a nonlinear term in the axial strain (for beams treated in EP §2.5.4, von Kármán theory), and with immovable edges an axial force arises in the plane of the plate. For our cover this stretch is small. The bent middle plane is an arc, longer than the chord, as in panel (a) of figure 18.4 (the arc above the chord), here by \(1{,}0\ \mu\)m on \(500\) mm (SymPy, length of the arc of the deflection curve (18.30) minus the chord). This is a stretch of \(2{,}0\cdot 10^{-6}\), which is \(2{,}3\) % of the bending strain of the surface at the middle, \(8{,}705\cdot 10^{-5}\). The model neglects this, and the theory of large deflections is in this chapter only named.

Example 18.27 A narrow beam is not a plate strip (ozek nosilec ni trak plošče)

Let a beam of width \(b\), thickness \(h = 10\) mm and span \(L = 500\) mm of the same steel be hinged and carry a uniform load \(q = -10\) kPa per unit area like the cover. The faces \(y = \pm b/2\) are free, so by Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) on them. If \(b\) is comparable with \(h\), then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) also inside (as in assumption 6 of theorem 5.35).

  1. By Hooke's law \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). With \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -z\,w''\) we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,z\,w''\), which is not zero: the upper edge widens, the lower one narrows.
  2. The fibre on the surface \(z = h/2\) at midspan has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -18{,}75\) MPa (compression), the same as the strip, because in both the moment is fixed by equilibrium. Therefore \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}| = 9{,}566\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the free lateral strain is \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}| = 2{,}870\cdot 10^{-5}\).
  3. The transverse curvature is \(-\nu\,\kappa_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}01913\) m\(^{-1}\) (radius \(52{,}3\) m), so \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}005740\) m\(^{-1}\) (radius \(174\) m). For width \(b = h = 10\) mm, as for the narrow beam in section 18.1.4, the lower edge of the cross-section rises above its chord by \(|\kappa_{\mathrm{y}}|\,b^2/8 = 7{,}2\cdot 10^{-8}\) m \(= 0{,}072\ \mu\)m, which is \(0{,}014\) % of the beam deflection.
  4. The deflection of the beam at the middle is \(5\,q\,L^4/(384\,E\,I) = -0{,}4982\) mm, in absolute value \(9{,}9\) % more than the strip (\(-0{,}4534\) mm). The ratio is exactly \(1 - \nu^2 = 0{,}91\) (lemma 18.25).

The lemma requires \(w\) to be a function of \(x\) only. For a narrow beam this is not true, because \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{y}} \ne 0\) and the lemma does not hold.

The example says something unexpected. The cross-section of a narrow beam of \(b = 10\) mm curves by barely \(0{,}072\ \mu\)m (in the figure magnified about twenty thousand times), the shape hardly changes. For the strip we forbid this small change and pay for it with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 5{,}6\) MPa, which is \(30\) % of the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The forbidden strain is only \(0{,}003\) %, but because of the stress it demands the strip is \(9{,}9\) % stiffer (its deflection is \(9\) % smaller). The difference between the beam and the strip is therefore the difference between whether a transverse displacement is allowed or not, and does not depend on how large it would be.

What remains is a test of the model. If the strip really is the limit of a long plate, a rectangular plate hinged on all four edges and loaded equally, \(a \times b\) with \(b/a \to \infty\), must approach the strip. The solution of such a plate is in section 18.5 and is proved there, here we use its numbers only as a test, not as a proof. The plan in the figure is a rectangle \(a \times b\), supported on all four edges, in which lies a strip along the shorter side \(a\). The measures are \(a\) and \(b\). On the right is a graph: the horizontal axis is the ratio \(b/a\), the vertical axis the fraction of the value of the strip. The blue points are the deflection at the centre, the orange ones the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the centre, and the dashed limit \(1\) is the strip.

All three columns approach the value of the strip: \(w_{\mathrm{c}}/w_{\mathrm{s}} \to 1\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}/M_{\mathrm{s}} \to 1\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}/M_{\mathrm{x}} \to \nu = 0{,}3\), which by lemma 18.25 must be so. For the square the deflection at the centre is only \(31\) % of the strip, because the load is carried also in the direction \(y\), and \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = M_{\mathrm{x}}\) by the symmetry of exchanging \(x\) and \(y\). At \(b/a = 3\) the deviation is still \(6\) %, at \(5\) it is \(0{,}4\) %. For an error below \(1\) % at the centre \(b \ge 4{,}5\,a\) suffices, for our ditch \(2{,}25\) m: the series gives a deviation of \(0{,}78\) % at \(b/a = 4{,}5\) and still \(1{,}55\) % at \(b/a = 4\) (NumPy, odd \(m, n \le 401\)). This is the measure for the idealization of the infinite strip. By VK (PDF p. 50) pure cylindrical bending of a plate of finite length requires a moment \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the edges parallel to the axis \(x\), and without it the surface departs from the cylinder over a part of the length. For hinged edges \(y = \pm b/2\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), whereas the strip would have \(\nu\,M_{\mathrm{x}}\) there, so near the ends the plate departs from the strip. The table measures how far into the interior this departure reaches.

In the limit of a long plate the plate therefore behaves as a beam, but with \(D\) instead of \(EI\), so the strip and the beam differ only by the factor \(1 - \nu^2\).

Notation. Here \(w\) is the deflection function as in chapter 13 (in chapter 5 the same quantity was denoted by the letter \(v\)), \(q\) is the load per unit area in N/m\(^2\) (for the beam it was per unit length), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) are moments per unit length in N m/m (for the beam \(M\) is in N m), \(h\) is the thickness of the plate, not the length of an element as in chapter 12, and \(D\) is the bending rigidity of the plate (not the elasticity matrix \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of chapter 4).
