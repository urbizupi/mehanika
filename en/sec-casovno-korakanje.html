Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.13 Numerical integration in time (numerično reševanje v času)

So far we have written the motion of a system with several degrees of freedom with natural modes. With classical damping (section 10.10) every mode solves the equation of chapter 9, and the motion is the sum of these solutions. This route has two limits. The first is damping that couples the modes. For frame A with one damper in the first storey the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\,\mat\Phi\) has the off-diagonal entries \(1{,}267\) 1/s, so the equations by modes do not separate (section 10.10). The second limit is a force that is known only as a sequence of measured numbers, for example one every thousandth of a second. The Duhamel integral (theorem 9.96) cannot be evaluated with a formula, because there is no formula for the force. In both cases we solve the equation of motion step by step: from the state at one time we compute the state at a slightly later time (figure 10.135 below). The question of the section is how a computer makes such a step, how it makes it so that the error does not grow beyond all bounds, and when this fails.

The starting point is the equation of motion of theorem 10.3,

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t) , \tag{10.116}\]

with a positive definite mass matrix \(\mat M\), symmetric matrices \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) of order \(n\), the force \(\vek Q(t)\) and the initial columns \(\vek q(0) = \vek q_0\), \(\dot{\vek q}(0) = \vek v_0\). We choose a time step (časovni korak) \(\Delta t > 0\) and the times \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = k\,\Delta t\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) Instead of functions of time we look for sequences of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) that are meant to approximate \(\vek q(t_{\mathrm{k}})\), \(\dot{\vek q}(t_{\mathrm{k}})\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}(t_{\mathrm{k}})\). We require equation (10.116) at the times \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\),

\[\mat M\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat C\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat K\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q(t_{\mathrm{k}}) . \tag{10.117}\]

This is one equation for three unknown columns. A rule is still missing that tells how the displacement, velocity and acceleration change in one step. The rule we choose is called a scheme (shema). Different rules give different schemes, and the question is which one is good.

We shall first test each scheme on a single natural mode, the equation \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\) with \(q(0) = 1\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Its exact solution is \(q = \cos\omega t\) with the period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) (chapter 9). For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) the system splits in the scheme into such equations by modes, which we shall show in the proofs of theorems 10.96 and 10.99. A test on one mode is therefore a test on every mode.

10.13.1 The trapezoidal rule (trapezno pravilo)

How the velocity and the displacement should change in one step we look at on the test equation \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\) with \(\omega = 1\) 1/s, \(q_0 = 1\), \(v_0 = 0\) and the step \(\Delta t = T/10 = 0{,}62832\) s.

We draw three time axes one above the other, the top one for the acceleration \(a\), the middle one for the velocity \(v\), the bottom one for the displacement \(q\), and on them the times \(t_0\), \(t_1\), \(t_2\), \(\Delta t\) apart. The grey curves are the exact solution, from top to bottom \(-\cos t\), \(-\sin t\) and \(\cos t\). The scheme does not know it, we draw it for comparison. Velocity is the integral of acceleration, displacement the integral of velocity. On one step we know the acceleration only at its end points, \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). If we replace the acceleration on the step by their mean value, the velocity changes by \(\Delta t\) times this mean acceleration, which is the area of a rectangle. In the first step the mean is \((a_0 + a_1)/2 = (-1 - 0{,}82034)/2 = -0{,}91017\) 1/s\(^2\) and the change of the velocity \(-0{,}572\) 1/s. We do the same with the displacement: we replace the velocity by the mean of its values at the end points, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), and the displacement changes by the area of the trapezoid under the straight line between them, in the first step by \(-0{,}180\). Hence the name. The displacements \(q_0\), \(q_1\), \(q_2\) arise by adding these areas. We cannot compute the velocity and the displacement until we know the acceleration at the end of the step, and that is determined by the equation of motion at the end of the step. So the value \(a_1 = -0{,}82034\) 1/s\(^2\) in the figure is known only when the step has been solved. The unknowns are therefore obtained together from a system of equations.

Definition 10.92 Trapezoidal rule (trapezno pravilo)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) symmetric matrices of order \(n\), \(\vek Q(t)\) a column of forces, \(\Delta t > 0\) a time step and \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\) initial columns. The trapezoidal rule (or the average acceleration scheme, shema s povprečnim pospeškom) is the sequence of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) that is determined by the initial values \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\), by equation (10.117) at \(k = 0\), which gives \(\vek a_0\), and for every \(k \ge 0\) by the equations

  1. The displacement changes by the area of the trapezoid:
    \[\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) , \tag{10.118}\]
  2. The velocity changes by the area of the rectangle:
    \[\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) , \tag{10.119}\]
  3. equation (10.117) at \(k + 1\), that is \(\mat M\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \mat C\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \mat K\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\).

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 430 to 432): "average acceleration" is Newmark's scheme, "often called the trapezoidal rule", because \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\) is the area of a trapezoid (PDF pp. 431 to 432). BATHE §9.2.3 (9.18) and (9.19), PDF p. 794: Newmark's scheme with the parameters \(\delta = 1/2\) and \(\alpha = 1/4\) is the "constant-average-acceleration method (also called trapezoidal rule)". The notation with equations (10.118) and (10.119) is ours, from both equations in the source follows COOK's (11.13-3) with \(\gamma = 1/2\), \(\beta = 1/4\) (see lemma 10.93).

Let us determine how a computer actually makes one step. We express the unknowns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) from equations (10.118), (10.119) and the third equation of the definition by \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), and one system of linear equations with the unknown \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) remains. Its matrix does not depend on \(k\), so for the given \(\Delta t\) we factorize it once (solving large systems is in chapter 17) and at every step only apply the factorization (COOK PDF p. 432).

Lemma 10.93 One step of the trapezoidal rule (en korak trapeznega pravila)

In definition 10.92 let \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) be positive semidefinite and \(\Delta t\) the step. The matrix

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}} = \mat K + \frac{2}{\Delta t}\,\mat C + \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,\mat M \tag{10.120}\]

is positive definite, hence invertible. The columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) satisfy equations (10.118), (10.119) and the third equation of the definition exactly when

\[\begin{aligned} &\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}+1} \\ &\quad + \mat M\Big(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{4}{\Delta t}\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\qquad + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) + \mat C\Big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) \end{aligned} \tag{10.121}\]

and

\[\begin{aligned} &\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}\,(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} , \\ &\qquad \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\quad - \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.122}\]

The trapezoidal rule therefore has exactly one sequence for every start \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\) and every force.

Proof

We show that the equations of the definition can be expressed by equations (10.122) and that equation (10.121) remains after substitution.

  1. We solve equation (10.118) for \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). With \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) we get the first equation (10.122).
  2. We solve equation (10.119) for \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\): \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\). We insert step 1 into it: \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}\big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - 2\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\big) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is the second equation (10.122).
  3. We insert steps 1 and 2 into the third equation of the definition. The term \(\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is \(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mat M\big(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{4}{\Delta t}\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\), the term \(\mat C\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is \(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\mat C\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mat C\big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\). Terms with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) go left, the rest right: equation (10.121) with the matrix (10.120).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 are reversible, since each solves one equation for one unknown. The equations of the definition therefore hold exactly when (10.121) and (10.122) hold.
  5. For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek x^\T\mat K\vek x + \frac{2}{\Delta t}\vek x^\T\mat C\vek x + \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x \ge \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x > 0\), because \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) are positive semidefinite and \(\mat M\) is positive definite.
  6. If \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek 0\) held with \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), then also \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = 0\), a contradiction.
  7. A square matrix whose equation \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek 0\) has only the zero solution is invertible (lemma 8.22). Hence equation (10.121) has exactly one solution.

Source: COOK (11.13-3) to (11.13-5), PDF p. 432: the same system with \(\gamma = 1/2\) and \(\beta = 1/4\). It says there that \([\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}]\) is nonsingular if \([\mat M]\) is positive definite, even for an unsupported structure, and that a diagonal mass matrix is of no use, because \([\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}]\) contains \([\mat K]\) (PDF p. 432). The proof is not from the source.

A test on one mode. For \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\), \(\omega = 1\) 1/s, \(q_0 = 1\), \(v_0 = 0\) and the step \(\Delta t = T/10 = 0{,}62832\) s we have \(a_0 = -\omega^2 q_0 = -1\) 1/s\(^2\) and \(K_{\mathrm{ef}} = 1 + 4/\Delta t^2 = 11{,}1321\) 1/s\(^2\). The right-hand side of equation (10.121) is \(4/\Delta t^2 + a_0 = 9{,}1321\) 1/s\(^2\), so \(q_1 = 0{,}82034\). The exact value is \(\cos 0{,}62832 = 0{,}80902\), the error of the first step is \(0{,}011\), which is \(1{,}4\) %.

The scheme stands on one assumption: that the mean of the accelerations at the end points of a step is a good mean of the acceleration over the whole step, and likewise for the velocity. This is exactly true when the acceleration changes linearly with time during the step, and nearly true when the step is short compared with the period, because a short piece of the grey curve is nearly straight. At \(\Delta t = T/10\) the error of the first step is \(0{,}011\), at \(T/20\) only \(0{,}0008\). The assumption fails when the motion reverses within one step: at \(\Delta t = T/2\) the scheme gives \(q_1 = -0{,}423\), while the exact value is \(\cos\pi = -1\). Even then the scheme does not blow up (theorem 10.95 below), but the result is no longer of use.

A question follows that the first step does not open: what happens after a thousand steps? The error may accumulate slowly, and then the scheme is merely inaccurate. But the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) may also grow beyond all bounds (figure 10.142 in section 10.13.2, which for comparison also has a sequence that stays bounded), although the exact motion of the undamped system is bounded, since the energy is conserved (section 10.4). Such a failure is a different kind of error than inaccuracy, and we call it instability.

Definition 10.94 Stability of a scheme (stabilnost sheme)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be positive definite matrices of order \(n\) and let a scheme (the trapezoidal rule or central differences) for the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with the step \(\Delta t\) determine a sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) from the initial values. The scheme is stable (stabilna) at this step if for every choice of initial values the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded, that is, if there is a number \(B\) with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le B\) for every \(k\). The scheme is unconditionally stable (brezpogojno stabilna) if it is stable at every \(\Delta t > 0\). The scheme is conditionally stable (pogojno stabilna) with the critical time step (kritični korak) \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) if it is stable exactly for \(\Delta t < \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\). The initial values are \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek v_0\) for the trapezoidal rule and \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) for central differences (section 10.13.2).

Source: COOK §11.13, PDF p. 430: "those in common use are unconditionally stable: even very large values of Δt do not make calculations blow up (but may destroy accuracy)". BATHE §9.2.1, PDF p. 790: "conditionally stable". Boundedness in the norm \(\sqrt{\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q}\) is our choice: since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, by theorem 8.27 we have \(\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q \ge \mu\,|\vek q|^2\) with \(\mu > 0\), so a bounded norm is the same as bounded components.

The trapezoidal rule has a property that is rare among schemes: it conserves energy exactly (figure 10.145 in section 10.13.3). The energy for \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) is \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) and is constant for the exact solution (section 10.4). We write its discrete version with the values of the scheme, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Theorem 10.95 Energy of the trapezoidal rule (energija pri trapeznem pravilu)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite and \(\mat K\), \(\mat C\) symmetric matrices of order \(n\), let \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and let the trapezoidal rule (definition 10.92) exist. With \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) we have for every \(k \ge 0\) and every \(\Delta t > 0\)

\[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = -\frac{\Delta t}{4}\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1})^\T\mat C\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) . \tag{10.123}\]

Therefore:

  1. For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) we have \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathcal{E}_0\) for every \(k\).
  2. If \(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}}\) does not increase with the step.
  3. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, \(\mat C\) positive semidefinite and \(\lambda_1\) the smallest eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\), then \(\tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\) and \(\tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0/\lambda_1\) for every \(k\) and every \(\Delta t\). For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) the trapezoidal rule is unconditionally stable.
Proof

We transform the energy difference into products of sums and differences, replace the differences by the rules of the scheme and use the equation of motion.

  1. Since \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\), because on expanding the product the mixed terms \(-\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(+\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) cancel (they are equal numbers, since \(\mat M\) is symmetric). The same holds for \(\mat K\) and \(\vek q\). Hence \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) + \tfrac12(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  2. We replace the differences by equations (10.119) and (10.118): \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). We get \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{4}\big[(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}) + (\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\big]\).
  3. We add the equation of motion (10.117) with \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) at \(k\) and \(k + 1\): \(\mat M(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = -\mat C(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  4. This gives the first term of step 2: \(-\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat C(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). The second term is \(+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\), and it is the opposite of the term with \(\mat K\), because \(\mat K\) is symmetric. The terms with \(\mat K\) cancel and equation (10.123) remains.
  5. Points 1 and 2 are special cases of equation (10.123): for \(\mat C = \mat 0\) the right-hand side is zero, for a positive semidefinite \(\mat C\) we have \(\vek w^\T\mat C\vek w \ge 0\) for \(\vek w = \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and the right-hand side is at most zero.
  6. Point 3. By point 2 we have \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). Both terms of the energy are nonnegative (\(\mat M\) positive definite, \(\mat K\) positive definite), so each of them is at most \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}}\), hence \(\tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). By theorem 10.42 we have \(\lambda_1\,\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q \le \vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) for every \(\vek q\), so \(\tfrac12\lambda_1\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). The bounds do not contain \(\Delta t\). The sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is therefore bounded at every step, which by definition 10.94 means unconditional stability for \(\mat C = \mat 0\).

Source: COOK §11.14 (PDF pp. 435 to 437) proves stability by analysing one mode, \(Z = \lambda^n\), and also mentions "energy stability analysis", which it does not carry out (PDF p. 435). The energy computation above is not from the source.

The proof has shown why the energy stays constant. In exact time the derivative of the energy is \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = \dot{\vek q}^\T(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q) = 0\), because the equation of motion cancels the bracket. The trapezoidal rule repeats this computation with values averaged over the step, and therefore the terms with \(\mat K\) and with \(\mat M\) cancel exactly, not only approximately. The theorem, however, does not say whether the state is at the right place on the path of constant energy. The next theorem says that.

For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) the scheme also splits by modes. Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite and let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be the eigenpairs of \((\mat K, \mat M)\) from theorem 10.21. The modal coordinate is \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and its velocity \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\). For the exact solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t + \xi_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{-1}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) (theorem 10.31). We write the pair \((\eta_{\mathrm{r}}, \xi_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\) as the complex number \(z_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \mathrm{i}\,\xi_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) (section 9.3). The exact solution is then \(z_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = z_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t - \mathrm{i}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t)\): the number rotates in the complex plane with constant size and with the turn \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\) per step (lemma 9.30).

In figure 10.136 on the right we draw the complex plane with the circle on which \(z_{\mathrm{r}}\) moves. It turns clockwise, as the arrow beside the circle shows, because the factor is \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t - \mathrm{i}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\). The start \(z_0\) lies on the real axis. At the step \(\Delta t = T/10\) the exact solution returns exactly to \(z_0\) after ten steps. Where the scheme arrives after ten steps is what the theorem says.

Theorem 10.96 Turn per step of the trapezoidal rule (zasuk na korak pri trapeznem pravilu)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C = \mat 0\), \(\vek Q = \vek 0\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) as in theorem 10.21, \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \mathrm{i}\,\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n\). For the trapezoidal rule with the step \(\Delta t\) and \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2\) we have

\[\begin{aligned} z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} &= \frac{1 - \mathrm{i}\,x_{\mathrm{r}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\,x_{\mathrm{r}}}\,z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} \\ &= \big(\cos 2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} - \mathrm{i}\sin 2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\big)\,z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} , \\ &\qquad \tan\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} = x_{\mathrm{r}} , \ \ \alpha_{\mathrm{r}} \in (0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.124}\]

The size \(|z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}|\) is therefore constant, and the turn per step is \(2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} = 2\arctan(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2)\) instead of the exact \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\).

Proof

We show that at every step the complex number \(z_{\mathrm{r}}\) is multiplied by the same number of size \(1\).

  1. Splitting by modes. We multiply equations (10.118) and (10.119) by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\). We get \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} = \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). Also \(\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} = -\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\). By corollary 10.22 we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), and by theorem 10.21 \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(s = r\), \(0\) otherwise, so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s = r\) and \(0\) otherwise. So \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} = -\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. With \(p_{\mathrm{k}} = \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(x = x_{\mathrm{r}}\) step 1 gives \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \eta_{\mathrm{k}} = x\,(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(p_{\mathrm{k}+1} - p_{\mathrm{k}} = -x\,(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  3. With \(z = \eta + \mathrm{i}\,p\) we have \(z_{\mathrm{k}+1} - z_{\mathrm{k}} = x(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \mathrm{i}\,x(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1})\), which is \(-\mathrm{i}\,x\big[(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}) + \mathrm{i}(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1})\big] = -\mathrm{i}\,x\,(z_{\mathrm{k}} + z_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  4. From step 3, \((1 + \mathrm{i}\,x)\,z_{\mathrm{k}+1} = (1 - \mathrm{i}\,x)\,z_{\mathrm{k}}\). By definition 9.29 the number \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x\) has the polar form \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x = \rho(\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)\) with \(\rho = \sqrt{1 + x^2}\) and \(\alpha \in (-\pi, \pi]\). For it \(\rho\cos\alpha = 1\) and \(\rho\sin\alpha = x\), so \(\cos\alpha > 0\) and \(\sin\alpha > 0\). Since \(\sin\alpha > 0\), \(\alpha \in (0, \pi)\), and since \(\cos\alpha > 0\), \(\alpha < \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) (lemma 8.11, point 3). Hence \(\alpha \in (0, \tfrac{\pi}{2})\) and \(\tan\alpha = x\). The number \(1 - \mathrm{i}\,x\) is its conjugate, \(\rho(\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)\). Figure 10.136 on the left shows this for \(x = 0{,}6\): in the complex plane we draw an arc of the unit circle and its tangent at the point \(1\). The arc of length \(x\) is the exact half turn, because the full turn per step is \(\omega\Delta t = 2x\). The number \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x\) lies on the tangent at the height \(x\), it is the blue point above the axis, and \(\alpha\) is the angle between the real axis and the segment from the origin to it. The conjugate \(1 - \mathrm{i}\,x\) is its mirror image below the axis.
  5. The quotient is \(\dfrac{\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha}\). Since \((\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)(\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha) = \cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha = 1\), the quotient equals \((\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)^2 = \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha - 2\mathrm{i}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha = \cos 2\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin 2\alpha\) (corollary 8.16). This is equation (10.124). The square of the size of this number is \(\cos^2 2\alpha + \sin^2 2\alpha = 1\), so \(|z_{\mathrm{k}+1}| = |z_{\mathrm{k}}|\). In the figure: division by \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x\) turns by \(-\alpha\), multiplication by \(1 - \mathrm{i}\,x\) turns by \(-\alpha\) once more, the lengths \(\rho\) cancel, so the total turn is the angle \(2\alpha\) between the two blue segments.

10.13.2 Central differences (centralne diference)

The trapezoidal rule must solve a system with the matrix (10.120) at every step. In fast phenomena, such as the propagation of a shock wave, there are very many steps, and then it matters that every step is cheap (COOK PDF p. 423: "Cost per time step is small"). The idea of the second scheme is not to use the velocity at all and to replace the acceleration at the time \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the second difference of three consecutive displacements.

Why this makes sense we see on the cosine.

On the time axis we mark three times, \(\Delta t\) apart, and on the curve \(\cos t\) three points \(q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), here at \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = 0{,}9\) s and \(\Delta t = T/10\), with the values \(0{,}96332\), \(0{,}62161\) and \(0{,}04247\). We join the outer points by a chord. Its midpoint has the height \((q_{\mathrm{k}-1} + q_{\mathrm{k}+1})/2 = 0{,}50289\). The second difference \(q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2q_{\mathrm{k}} + q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) is twice the amount by which the midpoint of the chord lies below the middle point: \(2\,(0{,}50289 - 0{,}62161) = -0{,}23743\). So it measures how much the curve bends away from a straight line between the three points, and this is also the meaning of the second derivative. Divided by \(\Delta t^2 = 0{,}39478\) s\(^2\) it gives \(-0{,}6014\), while the second derivative of the cosine there is \(-\cos 0{,}9 = -0{,}6216\). That this is no accident of these three points is shown by a computation. By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) \(\cos\omega(t + \Delta t) + \cos\omega(t - \Delta t) = 2\cos\omega\Delta t\,\cos\omega t\), hence

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac{\cos\omega(t + \Delta t) - 2\cos\omega t + \cos\omega(t - \Delta t)}{\Delta t^2} \\ &\quad = -\frac{2\,(1 - \cos\omega\Delta t)}{\Delta t^2}\,\cos\omega t = -\omega^2 R\cos\omega t , \\ &\quad R = \frac{2(1 - \cos\omega\Delta t)}{(\omega\Delta t)^2} , \qquad 1 - \frac{(\omega\Delta t)^2}{12} \le R \le 1 . \end{aligned} \tag{10.125}\]

The second difference of the cosine is therefore the second derivative \(-\omega^2\cos\omega t\) with a relative error between \(0\) and \((\omega\Delta t)^2/12\) (by lemma 10.53). In the example of the figure \(R = 0{,}6014/0{,}6216 = 0{,}9675\), and the lower bound is \(1 - 0{,}62832^2/12 = 0{,}9671\).

How the error shrinks with the step is shown in figure 10.138. On the horizontal axis we put \(\omega\Delta t\), on the vertical one the ratio \(R\), strongly magnified between \(0{,}9\) and \(1\). The horizontal line \(R = 1\) is the exact second derivative, the curve is \(R\) from equation (10.125), which falls below \(1\) as the step grows. At the step \(T/10\), \(\omega\Delta t = 0{,}628\), the error is \(1 - R = 0{,}0325\), and right beside it the bound \((\omega\Delta t)^2/12 = 0{,}0329\) is a little longer. At half the step, \(T/20\), \(\omega\Delta t = 0{,}314\), the error is \(0{,}00820\) and the bound \(0{,}00822\), both four times shorter. The bound of the error is of order \(\Delta t^2\): with half the step \((\omega\Delta t)^2/12\) is a quarter of what it was, and the error follows it.

Definition 10.97 Central differences (centralne diference)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat K\) a symmetric matrix of order \(n\), \(\Delta t > 0\) and \(\vek Q(t)\) a column of forces. For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) central differences are the sequence of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) that for \(k \ge 1\) and with the given initial columns \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) satisfies

\[\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1} = \Delta t^2\,\mat M^{-1}\big(\vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} - \mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q(t_{\mathrm{k}}) . \tag{10.126}\]

The start is usually \(\vek q_1 = \vek q_0 + \Delta t\,\vek v_0 + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\,\vek a_0\) with \(\mat M\vek a_0 = \vek Q_0 - \mat K\vek q_0\). The velocity, if we want it, is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1})/(2\Delta t)\).

Source: COOK §11.12 (PDF pp. 423 to 425), (11.12-1a) and (11.12-1b): "classical central differences", velocity and acceleration from displacements. BATHE §9.2.1 (PDF pp. 787 to 789), (9.3) to (9.10) and Table 9.1: the same scheme and the start with \(\vek q_{-1} = \vek q_0 - \Delta t\,\vek v_0 + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\,\vek a_0\) (PDF p. 788). For \(\mat C \ne \mat 0\) COOK (11.12-3) and (11.12-6), not treated here.

The scheme is explicit (eksplicitna): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) follows from the already known \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), without solving a system of equations with a full matrix. If \(\mat M\) is diagonal (lumped mass, definition 10.48), multiplication by \(\mat M^{-1}\) is division of each component by its mass. The matrix \(\mat K\) then need not even be assembled, because the product \(\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) can be summed element by element (BATHE PDF pp. 788 to 789, COOK PDF pp. 425 to 426). The trapezoidal rule, by contrast, is implicit (implicitna): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is determined by an equation that contains \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) itself, and we must solve it.

The price is a stability condition. To find it we need the recurrence that the scheme has in each mode.

We will reduce the scheme to a sequence of numbers \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) in which every term follows from the two before it, \(u_{\mathrm{k}+1} = 2b\,u_{\mathrm{k}} - u_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), with a constant number \(b\). In figure 10.139 we draw the terms as dots above the axis of indices \(k\) for three values of \(b\), all at the same scale. On the left \(b = 0{,}803\), which belongs to the step \(T/10\), with the start \(u_0 = u_1 = 1\). The dots follow a sampled cosine and stay in the blue band \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| \le 1{,}33\). In the middle \(b = -1\) with the start \(u_0 = 1\), \(u_1 = 0\). The dots change sign and move away from zero along the straight lines \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| = k - 1\), by the same length per step. On the right \(b = -1{,}02005\), which belongs to \(\omega\Delta t = 2{,}01\), with the start \(u_0 = 1\), \(u_1 = -1{,}2213\). The dots also change sign, but each is \(1{,}2213\) times farther from zero than the one before, so they follow the curves \(\pm 1{,}2213^k\) and grow ever faster. The lemma says that this is no accident of the chosen numbers: for \(|b| < 1\) every solution is bounded, while for \(|b| = 1\) and for \(|b| > 1\) there is a solution that grows linearly or geometrically.

Lemma 10.98 Second-order recurrence (rekurzija drugega reda)

Let \(b\) be a real number and \((u_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{k} \ge 0}\) a sequence of real numbers with

\[u_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2b\,u_{\mathrm{k}} + u_{\mathrm{k}-1} = 0 \qquad (k \ge 1) . \tag{10.127}\]

The sequence is determined by \(u_0\) and \(u_1\).

  1. For \(|b| < 1\) let \(\psi \in (0, \pi)\) be the angle with \(\cos\psi = b\). Then \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = u_0\cos k\psi + c\sin k\psi\) with \(c = (u_1 - u_0\cos\psi)/\sin\psi\). Every solution is bounded, \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| \le |u_0| + |c|\).
  2. For \(|b| = 1\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = b^k\big(u_0 + k\,(b\,u_1 - u_0)\big)\). The solution is bounded exactly when \(u_1 = b\,u_0\), otherwise \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}|\) grows linearly with \(k\).
  3. For \(|b| > 1\) let \(z = b + \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{b^2 - 1}\), so that \(|z| > 1\). The sequence \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = z^k\) is a solution with \(u_0 = 1\), \(u_1 = z\) and is unbounded: \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| = |z|^k\).
Proof

We show that the stated sequences solve equation (10.127) and have the right initial values.

  1. From equation (10.127), \(u_{\mathrm{k}+1} = 2b\,u_{\mathrm{k}} - u_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), so \(u_0\) and \(u_1\) determine all terms by induction. Two solutions with the same initial values are therefore equal.
  2. For \(|b| < 1\) the angle \(\psi\) exists by theorem 2.79, because \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\) (lemma 8.11), and \(\sin\psi > 0\). By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) \(\cos(k + 1)\psi + \cos(k - 1)\psi = 2\cos k\psi\,\cos\psi = 2b\cos k\psi\) and likewise for the sine, so \(\cos k\psi\) and \(\sin k\psi\) are solutions, and so is their combination (equation (10.127) is linear). For \(k = 0\) it gives \(u_0\), for \(k = 1\) it gives \(u_0\cos\psi + c\sin\psi = u_1\). By step 1 this is the required solution. The estimate follows from \(|\cos| \le 1\) and \(|\sin| \le 1\).
  3. For \(|b| = 1\) we have \(b^2 = 1\). We insert \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = b^k(a + c\,k)\): \(b^{k+1}(a + c(k+1)) - 2b\cdot b^k(a + ck) + b^{k-1}(a + c(k-1))\) equals \(b^{k-1}\big[(a + ck + c) - 2(a + ck) + (a + ck - c)\big] = 0\). The initial values \(u_0 = a\) and \(u_1 = b(a + c)\) give \(a = u_0\) and \(c = b\,u_1 - u_0\) (since \(b^2 = 1\)). The solution is bounded exactly for \(c = 0\), that is for \(u_1 = b\,u_0\), otherwise \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge k|c| - |a|\) and grows linearly.
  4. For \(|b| > 1\) we have \(z^2 - 2bz + 1 = 0\), because \(z = b + s\) with \(s = \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{b^2 - 1}\) and \(z^2 - 2bz + 1 = (b + s)^2 - 2b(b + s) + 1 = s^2 - b^2 + 1 = 0\). Hence \(z^{k+1} - 2b\,z^k + z^{k-1} = z^{k-1}(z^2 - 2bz + 1) = 0\). The size: \(|z| = |b| + \sqrt{b^2 - 1} > 1\).

Theorem 10.99 Stability of central differences (stabilnost centralnih diferenc)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat K\) positive semidefinite with the pairs \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) of theorem 10.21, \(0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\max}^2 > 0\). Let \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and let the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(k \ge 0\)) satisfy equation (10.126) for \(k \ge 1\) with arbitrary initial displacements \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\).

  1. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t < 2\) every mode with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) rotates with bounded amplitude, with the angle \(2\arcsin(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2)\) per step. The mode with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) moves as \(a + c\,k\) with constants \(a\) and \(c\), that is linearly, like the rigid-body motions in theorem 10.34.
  2. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t = 2\) there is a solution that grows linearly with \(k\).
  3. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t > 2\) there is a solution with \(\sqrt{\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}}\) growing like \(\rho^k\) with \(\rho > 1\). For positive definite \(\mat K\) the scheme is therefore conditionally stable with the critical time step \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2/\omega_{\max}\) (definition 10.94).
Proof

We show that the scheme splits into the recurrences of lemma 10.98, one for each mode, and apply the lemma.

  1. We write equation (10.126) for \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) as \(\mat M(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}) = -\Delta t^2\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and multiply it by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\). By corollary 10.22 we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by theorem 10.21 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\). Hence \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} - 2b_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}-1} = 0\) with \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t^2/2\).
  2. The initial \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},1}\) are arbitrary, because \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) are. The recurrences for different \(r\) are independent. The sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded exactly when all sequences \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) are bounded, because \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}^2\) (theorem 10.21).
  3. We have \(b_{\mathrm{r}} \le 1\), and \(b_{\mathrm{r}} > -1\) exactly when \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t^2 < 4\), that is \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t < 2\). The largest \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(\omega_{\max}\) and gives the smallest \(b\). For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t < 2\) we therefore have \(|b_{\mathrm{r}}| < 1\) for every \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\), and by point 1 of the lemma the solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded, with the angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{r}} = b_{\mathrm{r}}\). From \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 - 2\sin^2(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2)\) (corollary 8.16) we get \(\sin^2(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2) = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\Delta t^2/4\) and, since \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2 \in (0, \pi/2)\), \(\sin(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2) = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2\). For \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) we have \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) and by point 2 of the lemma \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},0} + k(\eta_{\mathrm{r},1} - \eta_{\mathrm{r},0})\). This is point 1.
  4. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t \ge 2\) we have \(b_{\mathrm{n}} \le -1\). 10.13.3 Choice of step and limits of the computation (izbira koraka in meje računa)

The trapezoidal rule is unconditionally stable for frame A (theorem 10.95), so we can run it with steps larger than \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\). Take \(\Delta t = T_2/2 = 0{,}06129\) s, which is more than the critical step of central differences, \(0{,}03902\) s. We start from rest at the position \(\vek q_0 = (5,\ 10)\) mm, \(\vek v_0 = \vek 0\) and make 1000 steps. The energy is \(\mathcal{E}_0 = 12{,}544\) J (section 10.4) and after 1000 steps it differs from it by less than \(3\cdot 10^{-13}\) relatively, so only by rounding (figure 10.145). In the first 30 steps the kinetic and strain energy take turns, while their sum stays \(12{,}544\) J. The energy is conserved even when the step is so large that only two steps fall on an oscillation of the second mode.

But conserved energy is not the exact solution. By theorem 10.96 the first mode turns by \(2\arctan(\omega_1\Delta t/2) = 1{,}08083\) rad per step instead of \(\omega_1\Delta t = 1{,}19998\) rad, so its period is longer by \(11{,}0\) %. The second mode is at \(\omega_2\Delta t = \pi\) and \(2\arctan(\pi/2) = 2{,}00777\) rad instead of \(3{,}14159\) rad, its period is longer by \(56{,}5\) %. The energy of each mode is right, but its phase is not. Conserved energy therefore guarantees stability, but not accuracy.

Let us look at the case for which we introduced stepping: a damper in the first storey, which couples the modes. The damping matrix is \(\mat C = \operatorname{diag}(1416{,}70,\ 0)\) N s/m (section 10.10), the start the same as above. The choice of mass is helped by COOK (PDF pp. 439 to 440). Lumped mass usually lowers the frequencies (for bar C always, section 10.8) and thus lengthens the periods, which partly cancels the shrinking of the period in central differences. Consistent mass raises the frequencies and shortens the periods, which partly cancels the elongation in the trapezoidal rule. Lumped mass with central differences is therefore according to COOK favourable both for accuracy and for cost, and consistent mass with the trapezoidal rule for accuracy, while it does not harm the cost much (PDF p. 440: "not very detrimental").

The stability condition of central differences, \(\Delta t < 2/\omega_{\max}\), refers to the highest mode of the mesh, even if the force does not excite it. The trapezoidal rule has no such condition. COOK (PDF p. 423) therefore says that explicit methods are best for wave propagation, where accuracy alone already requires a small step, because the "cost per time step is small, but so is the critical time step", and that they are not suitable for structural dynamics ("not well suited to structural dynamics problems"). BATHE (PDF p. 789) says the same: for waves \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) does not require an unusually small step, but for structures a step "much larger than" the critical one would suffice for accuracy.

All that we have shown so far holds for linear equations. Equations that are not linear need one more scheme, and it is in the next section.

10.13.4 The Runge-Kutta method of fourth order

Pendulum A from section 8.9 has the equation of motion \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\), which is not linear. The trapezoidal rule would need the solution of a nonlinear equation for \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) in every step (chapter 17), and central differences have an error of order \(\Delta t^2\) (figure 10.151 below), which is not enough for checking other computations. In example 8.100 and in section 8.13 we therefore computed with a method that is explicit like central differences and far more accurate, and we did not explain it there. It is the Runge-Kutta method of fourth order (Runge-Kuttova metoda četrtega reda), RK4 for short. It is named after the mathematicians Runge and Kutta, and the form we use was given by Kutta (KREYSZIG PDF p. 931, footnote 1). What fourth order means is shown below.

The method is written for equations that contain only the first derivative of the unknowns. So we first rewrite an equation of second order. We stack the displacements and the velocities into one column \(\vek y = (\vek q, \vek v)\) with \(2n\) components and call it the state (stanje). The name says that it holds all there is to know about the system at this instant: if we know the state at one time, the motion from there on is determined (corollary 8.108), and the past no longer matters. For pendulum A the state is the pair \((\theta, \dot\theta)\), so it is a point in a plane with the angle on the horizontal axis and the velocity on the vertical one (figure 10.147 below). The derivative of the state is \(\dot{\vek y} = (\dot{\vek q}, \ddot{\vek q})\). Its first half is the velocity, which is already part of the state. The second half comes from the equation of motion (10.116), solved for the acceleration:

\[\dot{\vek y} = \vek f(t, \vek y) , \qquad \vek y = \begin{pmatrix} \vek q \\ \vek v \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f(t, \vek y) = \begin{pmatrix} \vek v \\ \mat M^{-1}\big(\vek Q(t) - \mat C\,\vek v - \mat K\,\vek q\big) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.128}\]

The map \(\vek f\) assigns to every time and state the direction and the speed with which the state moves there. For pendulum A it is \(\vek f = \big(\dot\theta,\ -(g/l)\sin\theta\big)\). In the lectures the state is written as \([x, \dot x]\).

In nonlinear systems the mass matrix often depends on the position. The equations of motion then have the form \(\mat M(\vek q)\,\ddot{\vek q} = \vek F(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q})\), where \(\vek F\) collects all the other terms (theorem 8.97 and the proof of corollary 8.108). The accelerations are mixed together on the left-hand side, while \(\vek f\) needs each of them separately. So at every evaluation of \(\vek f\) we first solve the linear system \(\mat M(\vek q)\,\vek a = \vek F\) for the accelerations \(\vek a\), with the matrix computed at the position \(\vek q\) of that state. The solution exists and is unique because \(\mat M\) is positive definite, by the same argument as in step 5 of the proof of lemma 10.93. With one degree of freedom this is a division by the mass: mechanism D with equation (8.114) and the mass \(m(\theta) = \tfrac13 + 2\sin^2\theta\) kg m\(^2\) has \(\vek f = \big(\dot\theta,\ -(\tfrac12 m'(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 + V'(\theta))/m(\theta)\big)\), and this is how we computed it in section 8.13. With several degrees of freedom we do not write the inverse matrix as a formula. We solve the system numerically every time the method asks for \(\vek f\), which is four times per step.

The simplest step would be to move from the state \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) by \(\Delta t\) in the direction that \(\vek f\) shows at the start of the step. The direction changes during the step, so such a step misses. The Runge-Kutta method looks at the direction in four places within the step and moves with their mean.

Definition 10.100 Runge-Kutta method of fourth order (Runge-Kuttova metoda četrtega reda)

Let \(N \ge 1\), let \(\vek f\) be a map that assigns to a time \(t\) and a column \(\vek y \in \mathbb R^N\) a column \(\vek f(t, \vek y) \in \mathbb R^N\), let \(\Delta t > 0\) be the step, \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = k\,\Delta t\) and \(\vek y_0 \in \mathbb R^N\) the initial state. The Runge-Kutta method of fourth order for the system \(\dot{\vek y} = \vek f(t, \vek y)\) is the sequence of columns \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), determined for every \(k \ge 0\) by the four directions

\[\begin{aligned} \vek k_1 &= \vek f\big(t_{\mathrm{k}},\ \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \\ \vek k_2 &= \vek f\big(t_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t,\ \vek y_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t\,\vek k_1\big) , \\ \vek k_3 &= \vek f\big(t_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t,\ \vek y_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t\,\vek k_2\big) , \\ \vek k_4 &= \vek f\big(t_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t,\ \vek y_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,\vek k_3\big) \end{aligned} \tag{10.129}\]

and the step

\[\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek y_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{\Delta t}{6}\,\big(\vek k_1 + 2\,\vek k_2 + 2\,\vek k_3 + \vek k_4\big) . \tag{10.130}\]

The bold \(\vek k_1\) to \(\vek k_4\) are columns and are not the step counter \(k\). For the equation of motion (10.116) we have \(N = 2n\) and \(\vek f\) from equation (10.128).

Source: KREYSZIG §21.1, Table 21.3 (PDF p. 931): "Classical Runge–Kutta Method of Fourth Order", there with \(k_{\mathrm{i}} = h f(\dots)\), so with the step already inside the directions. CK (6.39) and (6.40), PDF pp. 182 to 183: the same method, "RK4 Method", also with the step inside the directions and with the state before the time in \(f\). Systems of equations: KREYSZIG §21.3 (PDF p. 941). An equation of second order written with a state: CK (6.20) to (6.22), PDF p. 177.

Let us take one step with pendulum A (\(l = 1\) m, \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\)), released from rest at \(90°\), so from the state \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}} = (\pi/2,\ 0)\). The step \(\Delta t = 0{,}5\) s is large on purpose, so that the four directions can be told apart in the figure: the period at this amplitude is \(2{,}3678\) s (computed numerically). The angle is in radians, the velocity in 1/s.

In the state plane the horizontal axis carries the angle \(\theta\) and the vertical one the velocity \(\dot\theta\) divided by \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l} = 3{,}1321\) 1/s, so that the two axes have the same scale. The grey curve is the path of the exact solution: from the initial state \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) the pendulum falls, the angle decreases, the velocity is negative and grows in size. The arrows of the directions are drawn shortened.

  1. The first direction \(\vek k_1\) is \(\vek f\) at the initial state, \((0,\ -9{,}81)\). The pendulum is at rest, so the angle does not change yet, while the velocity falls with the acceleration \(-(g/l)\sin(\pi/2) = -9{,}81\) 1/s\(^2\).
  2. With this direction we go half a step, to the state \((1{,}5708,\ -2{,}4525)\), and there we compute the second direction \(\vek k_2\) \(= (-2{,}4525,\ -9{,}81)\). Now the angle already decreases.
  3. We return to \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and go half a step with the direction \(\vek k_2\), to \((0{,}9577,\ -2{,}4525)\). The third direction \(\vek k_3\) is \((-2{,}4525,\ -8{,}0231)\). The acceleration is smaller in size, because \(\sin 0{,}9577 = 0{,}8179\) is less than \(1\).
  4. Again from \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) we go a whole step with the direction \(\vek k_3\), to \((0{,}3445,\ -4{,}0116)\), where the fourth direction \(\vek k_4\) \(= (-4{,}0116,\ -3{,}3135)\).
  5. The mean with the weights \(1\), \(2\), \(2\), \(1\) is \((-2{,}3036,\ -8{,}1316)\), and the step with this direction gives \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1} = (0{,}4190,\ -4{,}0658)\).

The exact state at this time is \((0{,}4018,\ -4{,}2494)\). The step with the first direction alone would give \((1{,}5708,\ -4{,}905)\): the angle would not change at all in half a second, because the first direction does not know that the pendulum will move.

Where do the weights \(1\), \(2\), \(2\), \(1\) and the division by \(6\) come from? The weights add up to \(6\), so the expression in equation (10.130) is a mean of the directions. Why these weights is seen in the case where \(f\) does not depend on the state but only on time. Then \(k_2\) and \(k_3\) are equal and the step is

\[y_{\mathrm{k}+1} - y_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{\Delta t}{6}\,\Big(f(t_{\mathrm{k}}) + 4 f\big(t_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t\big) + f(t_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t)\Big) , \tag{10.131}\]

an approximation of the integral of \(f\) over the step from its values at the start, in the middle and at the end. This is Simpson's rule (KREYSZIG PDF p. 931). We test it on the powers \(f = t^p\) on the step from \(0\) to \(\Delta t\), where the exact integral is \(\Delta t^{p+1}/(p + 1)\) and the rule gives \(\Delta t^{p+1}\,(4/2^p + 1)/6\):

\(p\) 1 2 3 4
exact \(1/(p+1)\) \(1/2\) \(1/3\) \(1/4\) \(1/5\)
rule \((4/2^p + 1)/6\) \(1/2\) \(1/3\) \(1/4\) \(5/24\)

The rule is exact up to the third power and misses only the fourth. That is why the error of one step is small. The weight \(4\) of the middle of the step is shared in the method by the middle directions \(\vek k_2\) and \(\vek k_3\), each with the weight \(2\).

How accurate the method is for vibration we measure, as with the previous schemes, on one mode.

Theorem 10.101 The RK4 step on one mode (korak RK4 na enem načinu)

Let \(\omega > 0\), \(\Delta t > 0\) and \(x = \omega\Delta t\). We write the equation \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\) in the form (10.128) with the state \(\vek y = (q, v)\) and \(\vek f = (v,\ -\omega^2 q)\). Let \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}} = (q_{\mathrm{k}}, v_{\mathrm{k}})\) be the sequence of definition 10.100 and \(z_{\mathrm{k}} = q_{\mathrm{k}} + \mathrm{i}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}/\omega\). Then

\[z_{\mathrm{k}+1} = P(x)\,z_{\mathrm{k}} , \qquad P(x) = 1 - \mathrm{i}\,x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \mathrm{i}\,\frac{x^3}{6} + \frac{x^4}{24} \tag{10.132}\]

and

\[|P(x)|^2 = 1 - \frac{x^6\,(8 - x^2)}{576} . \tag{10.133}\]

Hence:

  1. For \(\omega\Delta t < 2\sqrt2\) we have \(|P(x)| < 1\): with every step the size \(|z_{\mathrm{k}}|\) is multiplied by \(|P(x)| < 1\) and the sequence is bounded.
  2. For \(\omega\Delta t = 2\sqrt2\) the size \(|z_{\mathrm{k}}|\) is constant.
  3. For \(\omega\Delta t > 2\sqrt2\) we have \(|P(x)| > 1\) and \(|z_{\mathrm{k}}| = |P(x)|^k\,|z_0|\) grows beyond all bounds if \(z_0 \ne 0\).
Proof

We show that a step of the method multiplies the number \(z\) by \(P(x)\), and we compute the size of this number.

  1. The equation for \(z\). From \(\dot q = v\) and \(\dot v = -\omega^2 q\) we get \(\dot z = v - \mathrm{i}\,\omega\,q = -\mathrm{i}\,\omega\,(q + \mathrm{i}\,v/\omega) = -\mathrm{i}\,\omega\,z\). To the column \(\vek y = (q, v)\) we assign the number \(q + \mathrm{i}\,v/\omega\). This assignment is linear and \(\vek f\) is linear, so the directions \(\vek k_{\mathrm{j}}\) of equation (10.129) become numbers \(\kappa_{\mathrm{j}}\) that satisfy the same equations with the function \(z \mapsto -\mathrm{i}\,\omega\,z\) in place of \(\vek f\), and equation (10.130) becomes \(z_{\mathrm{k}+1} = z_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}(\kappa_1 + 2\kappa_2 + 2\kappa_3 + \kappa_4)\).
  2. The first direction: \(\kappa_1 = -\mathrm{i}\,\omega\,z_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The proof showed where the accuracy comes from. The exact solution multiplies \(z\) in one step by \(\cos x - \mathrm{i}\sin x\) (a turn by \(x\), as in theorem 10.96). The real part of \(P\), \(1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24\), is the upper bound of the cosine from lemma 10.53, and the imaginary part is the start of the sine with the opposite sign. At \(\Delta t = T/10\), so \(x = 0{,}62832\), we get \(P = 0{,}80910 - 0{,}58698\,\mathrm{i}\) against the exact \(0{,}80902 - 0{,}58779\,\mathrm{i}\). The turn per step is \(0{,}62761\) rad instead of \(0{,}62832\) rad, so the point of the method lags by \(0{,}00070\) rad, and in one oscillation of ten steps a lag of \(10 \cdot 0{,}00070\) rad \(= 0{,}40°\) accumulates. The trapezoidal rule had \(11{,}2°\) at the same step and central differences \(6{,}2°\). The comparison at the same step is not fair, because RK4 evaluates \(\vek f\) four times in every step. At a step four times smaller, \(T/40\), the trapezoidal rule has a lag of \(0{,}74°\) per oscillation, still more.

The theorem also says where RK4 differs from the previous schemes. The size \(|z|\) is not conserved: at \(T/10\) we have \(|P| = 0{,}99959\), the point of the method lies \(0{,}00041\) inside the circle, and the amplitude decreases in one oscillation to \(0{,}99959^{10} = 0{,}9959\), so by \(0{,}41\) %. The method damps the vibration slightly, although the equation has no damping. With a small step this loss is negligible: per step it falls with the sixth power of the step, per oscillation with the fifth. And the method is conditionally stable, with the critical step \(2\sqrt2/\omega_{\max} = 2{,}83/\omega_{\max}\), which is \(1{,}41\) times longer than for central differences (theorem 10.99). For a linear system without damping this follows from the expansion in modes, done as in the proof of theorem 10.96.

Now we can say what fourth order means. The expansion (SymPy) gives the turn of the number \(P\) as \(x - x^5/120 + \dots\) and its size as \(1 - x^6/144 + \dots\), so the error of one step is of order \(x^5\). There are \(2\pi/x\) steps per oscillation, so the error after one oscillation is of order \(x^4\): with half the step it is sixteen times smaller. A scheme has order (red) \(p\) if the error at a chosen time falls as \(\Delta t^p\). The trapezoidal rule and central differences have order \(2\), RK4 has order \(4\). For a general \(\vek f\) with enough continuous derivatives the same holds: KREYSZIG (PDF pp. 930 to 931) calls the method "of fourth order", and shows the argument from the error of a step to the error at a chosen time on a simpler method (PDF p. 928). We do not give this proof, and we test the order on a nonlinear example.

Order \(4\) has a condition: \(\vek f\) must be smooth during the step. When the force has a jump or a kink the error falls more slowly, which the last example of the section shows.

How do we choose the step when we do not know the exact solution? We take the same step twice, once with \(\Delta t\) and once with two half steps, and draw both results and the exact value on an axis. The error of one step is of order \(\Delta t^5\). If the step is so small that the error is almost exactly \(C\,\Delta t^5\) with the same number \(C\) in both computations, the error of the whole step is \(E = C\,\Delta t^5\), and the two half steps together have about \(2\,C\,(\Delta t/2)^5 = E/16\), because the errors of the two halves add up. The difference of the two results is therefore about \(15/16\) of the error of the whole step, which is fifteen times the error of the more accurate result. So we estimate the error of the more accurate result by the difference divided by \(15\) (KREYSZIG (10), PDF p. 933, there with the steps \(h\) and \(2h\)). In our step with the pendulum one step of \(0{,}5\) s gives the angle \(0{,}41900\), two steps of \(0{,}25\) s give \(0{,}40370\). The estimate of the error is \((0{,}40370 - 0{,}41900)/15 = -0{,}0010\) rad, and the true error of the more accurate result is \(0{,}40177 - 0{,}40370 = -0{,}0019\) rad (the exact state). The estimate gets the sign and the order of magnitude, not the digits, because the step \(0{,}5\) s is too large for an argument with the order \(\Delta t^5\): the error of the whole step, \(0{,}01723\) rad, is only \(8{,}9\) times the error of the half steps, not \(16\) times.

The adaptive step (prilagodljivi korak) stands on this estimate. The user gives a tolerance (toleranca), the largest allowed estimate of the error of one step. If the estimate is larger than the tolerance, the step is rejected and repeated with a shorter one. If it is much smaller, the next step is longer. The step is thus short where the motion changes fast and long elsewhere (the blue dots in figure 10.153 below are dense during the pulse). Library routines work on the same idea, except that they get the estimate more cheaply: with the same directions they compute two results of different order and subtract them (the idea of Fehlberg with formulas of fourth and fifth order, which together need six directions per step, KREYSZIG PDF p. 933). In the lectures and exercises this is the function solve_ivp of the SciPy library, which takes the tolerance as two numbers, the relative rtol and the absolute atol. The tolerance bounds the error of a single step, not the error at the end of the computation, because the errors of the steps add up. So we always check the result by repeating it with a tolerance ten times smaller, or with the energy, as in section 8.9.

The adaptive step has a trap that catches every scheme: the force is read only at the times at which the scheme evaluates \(\vek f\). Take a system with one degree of freedom \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = p(t)\) with \(\omega = 1\) 1/s and \(T = 6{,}2832\) s, where \(p\) is the force divided by the mass, in m/s\(^2\), and \(q\) the displacement in m. The system is at rest until a short pulse hits it.

We draw two time axes, the upper one for the force, the lower one for the displacement, both up to the time \(T\). The pulse has the shape of half a sine wave with the largest value \(1\) m/s\(^2\), it starts at \(3{,}3\) s and lasts \(t_{\mathrm{p}} = T/20 = 0{,}314\) s. The Runge-Kutta method is therefore the right choice for nonlinear equations with few degrees of freedom and a smooth force, where we want high accuracy. For large linear systems the trapezoidal rule, which the step does not limit (the energy stays bounded also at a step above the critical one), and central differences, where a step is cheap but limited by the critical step, remain. Implicit schemes for nonlinear systems, in which every step is solved by iteration, and schemes with numerical damping (algorithmic damping, numerično dušenje) are in chapter 17. Other members of Newmark's family, which have other parameters and numerical damping, are in COOK §11.13 (PDF pp. 432 to 434) and BATHE §9.2.3 (PDF p. 794).
