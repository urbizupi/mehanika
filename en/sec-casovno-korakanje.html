Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.12 Numerical integration in time (numerično reševanje v času)

So far we have written the motion of a system with several degrees of freedom with natural modes. With classical damping (section 10.9) every mode solves the equation of chapter 9, and the motion is the sum of these solutions. This route has two limits. The first is damping that couples the modes. For frame A with one damper in the first storey the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\,\mat\Phi\) has the off-diagonal entries \(1{,}267\) 1/s, so the equations by modes do not separate (section 10.9). The second limit is a force that is known only as a sequence of measured numbers, for example one every thousandth of a second. The Duhamel integral (theorem 9.79) cannot be evaluated with a formula, because there is no formula for the force. In both cases we solve the equation of motion step by step: from the state at one time we compute the state at a slightly later time. The question of the section is how a computer makes such a step, how it makes it so that the error does not grow beyond all bounds, and when this fails.

The starting point is the equation of motion of theorem 10.2,

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t) , \tag{10.72}\]

with a positive definite mass matrix \(\mat M\), symmetric matrices \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) of order \(n\), the force \(\vek Q(t)\) and the initial columns \(\vek q(0) = \vek q_0\), \(\dot{\vek q}(0) = \vek v_0\). We choose a time step (časovni korak) \(\Delta t > 0\) and the times \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = k\,\Delta t\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) Instead of functions of time we look for sequences of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) that are meant to approximate \(\vek q(t_{\mathrm{k}})\), \(\dot{\vek q}(t_{\mathrm{k}})\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}(t_{\mathrm{k}})\). We require equation (10.72) at the times \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\),

\[\mat M\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat C\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat K\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q(t_{\mathrm{k}}) . \tag{10.73}\]

This is one equation for three unknown columns. A rule is still missing that tells how the displacement, velocity and acceleration change in one step. The rule we choose is called a scheme (shema). Different rules give different schemes, and the question is which one is good.

We shall first test each scheme on a single natural mode, the equation \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\) with \(q(0) = 1\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\). Its exact solution is \(q = \cos\omega t\) with the period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) (chapter 9). For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) the system splits in the scheme into such equations by modes, which we shall show in the proofs of theorems 10.67 and 10.70. A test on one mode is therefore a test on every mode.

10.12.1 The trapezoidal rule (trapezno pravilo)

Velocity is the integral of acceleration, displacement the integral of velocity. On one step we know the acceleration only at its end points, \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). If we replace the acceleration on the step by their mean value, the velocity changes by \(\Delta t\) times this mean acceleration. We do the same with the displacement: we replace the velocity by the mean of its values at the end points, and the displacement changes by the area of the trapezoid under the straight line between \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). Hence the name. We cannot compute the velocity and the displacement until we know the acceleration at the end of the step, and that is determined by the equation of motion at the end of the step. The unknowns are therefore obtained together from a system of equations.

Definition 10.63 Trapezoidal rule (trapezno pravilo)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) symmetric matrices of order \(n\), \(\vek Q(t)\) a column of forces, \(\Delta t > 0\) a time step and \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\) initial columns. The trapezoidal rule (or the average acceleration scheme, shema s povprečnim pospeškom) is the sequence of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) that is determined by the initial values \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\), by equation (10.73) at \(k = 0\), which gives \(\vek a_0\), and for every \(k \ge 0\) by the equations

  1. \[\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) , \tag{10.74}\]
  2. \[\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{\Delta t}{2}\,(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) , \tag{10.75}\]
  3. equation (10.73) at \(k + 1\), that is \(\mat M\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \mat C\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \mat K\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\).

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 430 to 432): "average acceleration" is Newmark's scheme, "often called the trapezoidal rule", because \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\) is the area of a trapezoid (PDF pp. 431 to 432). BATHE §9.2.3 (9.18) and (9.19), PDF p. 794: Newmark's scheme with the parameters \(\delta = 1/2\) and \(\alpha = 1/4\) is the "constant-average-acceleration method (also called trapezoidal rule)". The notation with equations (10.74) and (10.75) is ours, from both equations in the source follows COOK's (11.13-3) with \(\gamma = 1/2\), \(\beta = 1/4\) (see lemma 10.64).

Let us determine how a computer actually makes one step. We express the unknowns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) from equations (10.74), (10.75) and the third equation of the definition by \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), and one system of linear equations with the unknown \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) remains. Its matrix does not depend on \(k\), so for the given \(\Delta t\) we factorize it once (solving large systems is in chapter 17) and at every step only apply the factorization (COOK PDF p. 432).

Lemma 10.64 One step of the trapezoidal rule (en korak trapeznega pravila)

In definition 10.63 let \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) be positive semidefinite. The matrix

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}} = \mat K + \frac{2}{\Delta t}\,\mat C + \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,\mat M \tag{10.76}\]

is positive definite, hence invertible. The columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) satisfy equations (10.74), (10.75) and the third equation of the definition exactly when

\[\begin{aligned} &\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}+1} \\ &\quad + \mat M\Big(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{4}{\Delta t}\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\qquad + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) + \mat C\Big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) \end{aligned} \tag{10.77}\]

and

\[\begin{aligned} &\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}\,(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} , \\ &\qquad \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\,(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\quad - \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}} . \end{aligned} \tag{10.78}\]

The trapezoidal rule therefore has exactly one sequence for every start \(\vek q_0\), \(\vek v_0\) and every force.

Proof

We show that the equations of the definition can be expressed by equations (10.78) and that equation (10.77) remains after substitution.

  1. We solve equation (10.74) for \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). With \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) we get the first equation (10.78).
  2. We solve equation (10.75) for \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\): \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\). We insert step 1 into it: \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{2}{\Delta t}\big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}}) - 2\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\big) - \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is the second equation (10.78).
  3. We insert steps 1 and 2 into the third equation of the definition. The term \(\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is \(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mat M\big(\frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \frac{4}{\Delta t}\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\), the term \(\mat C\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is \(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\mat C\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mat C\big(\frac{2}{\Delta t}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\). We collect the terms with the unknown \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) on the left, the rest on the right, and obtain equation (10.77) with the matrix (10.76).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 are reversible, since each solves one equation for one unknown. The equations of the definition therefore hold exactly when (10.77) and (10.78) hold.
  5. For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek x^\T\mat K\vek x + \frac{2}{\Delta t}\vek x^\T\mat C\vek x + \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x \ge \frac{4}{\Delta t^2}\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x > 0\), because \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) are positive semidefinite and \(\mat M\) is positive definite. If \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek 0\) held with \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), then also \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = 0\), a contradiction. A square matrix whose equation \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\vek x = \vek 0\) has only the zero solution is invertible (lemma 8.21). Hence equation (10.77) has exactly one solution.

Source: COOK (11.13-3) to (11.13-5), PDF p. 432: the same system with \(\gamma = 1/2\) and \(\beta = 1/4\). It says there that \([\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}]\) is nonsingular if \([\mat M]\) is positive definite, even for an unsupported structure, and that a diagonal mass matrix is of no use, because \([\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}]\) contains \([\mat K]\) (PDF p. 432). The proof is not from the source.

A test on one mode. For \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\), \(\omega = 1\) 1/s, \(q_0 = 1\), \(v_0 = 0\) and the step \(\Delta t = T/10 = 0{,}62832\) s we have \(a_0 = -\omega^2 q_0 = -1\) 1/s\(^2\) and \(K_{\mathrm{ef}} = 1 + 4/\Delta t^2 = 11{,}1321\) 1/s\(^2\). The right-hand side of equation (10.77) is \(4/\Delta t^2 + a_0 = 9{,}1321\) 1/s\(^2\), so \(q_1 = 0{,}82034\). The exact value is \(\cos 0{,}62832 = 0{,}80902\), the error of the first step is \(1{,}4\) %.

A question follows that the first step does not open: what happens after a thousand steps? The error may accumulate slowly, and then the scheme is merely inaccurate. But the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) may also grow beyond all bounds, although the exact motion of the undamped system is bounded, since the energy is conserved (section 10.4). Such a failure is a different kind of error than inaccuracy, and we call it instability.

Definition 10.65 Stability of a scheme (stabilnost sheme)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be positive definite matrices of order \(n\) and let a scheme (the trapezoidal rule or central differences) for the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with the step \(\Delta t\) determine a sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) from the initial values. The scheme is stable (stabilna) at this step if for every choice of initial values the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded, that is, if there is a number \(B\) with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le B\) for every \(k\). The scheme is unconditionally stable (brezpogojno stabilna) if it is stable at every \(\Delta t > 0\). The scheme is conditionally stable (pogojno stabilna) with the critical time step (kritični korak) \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) if it is stable exactly for \(\Delta t < \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\). The initial values are \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek v_0\) for the trapezoidal rule and \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) for central differences (section 10.12.2).

Source: COOK §11.13, PDF p. 430: "those in common use are unconditionally stable: even very large values of Δt do not make calculations blow up (but may destroy accuracy)". BATHE §9.2.1, PDF p. 790: "conditionally stable". Boundedness in the norm \(\sqrt{\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q}\) is our choice: since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, by theorem 8.26 we have \(\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q \ge \mu\,|\vek q|^2\) with \(\mu > 0\), so a bounded norm is the same as bounded components.

The trapezoidal rule has a property that is rare among schemes: it conserves energy exactly. The energy for \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) is \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) and is constant for the exact solution (section 10.4). We write its discrete version with the values of the scheme, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Theorem 10.66 Energy of the trapezoidal rule (energija pri trapeznem pravilu)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite and \(\mat K\), \(\mat C\) symmetric matrices of order \(n\), let \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and let the trapezoidal rule (definition 10.63) exist. With \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) we have for every \(k \ge 0\) and every \(\Delta t > 0\)

\[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = -\frac{\Delta t}{4}\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1})^\T\mat C\,(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) . \tag{10.79}\]

Therefore:

  1. For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) we have \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathcal{E}_0\) for every \(k\).
  2. If \(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}}\) does not increase with the step.
  3. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, \(\mat C\) positive semidefinite and \(\lambda_1\) the smallest eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\), then \(\tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\) and \(\tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0/\lambda_1\) for every \(k\) and every \(\Delta t\). For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) the trapezoidal rule is unconditionally stable.
Proof

We transform the energy difference into products of sums and differences, replace the differences by the rules of the scheme and use the equation of motion.

  1. Since \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\), because on expanding the product the mixed terms \(-\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(+\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) cancel (they are equal numbers, since \(\mat M\) is symmetric). The same holds for \(\mat K\) and \(\vek q\). Hence \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}}) + \tfrac12(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  2. We replace the differences by equations (10.75) and (10.74): \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). We get \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{4}\big[(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat M(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}}) + (\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\big]\).
  3. We add the equation of motion (10.73) with \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) at \(k\) and \(k + 1\): \(\mat M(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = -\mat C(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  4. We insert this into the first term of step 2. The first term is then \(-\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat C(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). The second term of step 2 is \(+\tfrac{\Delta t}{4}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}})^\T\mat K(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}})\), and it equals the term with \(\mat K\) just obtained with the opposite sign, because \(\mat K\) is symmetric (a number equals its transpose). The terms with \(\mat K\) cancel and equation (10.79) remains.
  5. Points 1 and 2 are special cases of equation (10.79): for \(\mat C = \mat 0\) the right-hand side is zero, for a positive semidefinite \(\mat C\) we have \(\vek w^\T\mat C\vek w \ge 0\) for \(\vek w = \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and the right-hand side is at most zero.
  6. Point 3. By point 2 we have \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). Both terms of the energy are nonnegative (\(\mat M\) positive definite, \(\mat K\) positive definite), so each of them is at most \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}}\), hence \(\tfrac12\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). By theorem 10.26 we have \(\lambda_1\,\vek q^\T\mat M\vek q \le \vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) for every \(\vek q\), so \(\tfrac12\lambda_1\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \le \mathcal{E}_0\). The bounds do not contain \(\Delta t\). The sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is therefore bounded at every step, which by definition 10.65 means unconditional stability for \(\mat C = \mat 0\).

Source: COOK §11.14 (PDF pp. 435 to 437) proves stability by analysing one mode, \(Z = \lambda^n\), and also mentions "energy stability analysis", which it does not carry out (PDF p. 435). The energy computation above is not from the source.

The proof has shown why the energy stays constant. In exact time the derivative of the energy is \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = \dot{\vek q}^\T(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q) = 0\), because the equation of motion cancels the bracket. The trapezoidal rule repeats this computation with values averaged over the step, and therefore the terms with \(\mat K\) and with \(\mat M\) cancel exactly, not only approximately. The theorem, however, does not say whether the state is at the right place on the path of constant energy. The next theorem says that.

For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) the scheme also splits by modes. Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite and let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be the eigenpairs of \((\mat K, \mat M)\) from theorem 10.13. The modal coordinate is \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and its velocity \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\). For the exact solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t + \xi_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^{-1}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) (theorem 10.21). We write the pair \((\eta_{\mathrm{r}}, \xi_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\) as the complex number \(z_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \mathrm{i}\,\xi_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) (section 9.3). The exact solution is then \(z_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = z_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t - \mathrm{i}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t)\): the number rotates in the complex plane with constant size and with the turn \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\) per step (lemma 9.25).

Theorem 10.67 Turn per step of the trapezoidal rule (zasuk na korak pri trapeznem pravilu)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C = \mat 0\), \(\vek Q = \vek 0\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) as in theorem 10.13, \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \mathrm{i}\,\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n\). For the trapezoidal rule with the step \(\Delta t\) and \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2\) we have

\[\begin{aligned} z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} &= \frac{1 - \mathrm{i}\,x_{\mathrm{r}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\,x_{\mathrm{r}}}\,z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} \\ &= \big(\cos 2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} - \mathrm{i}\sin 2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\big)\,z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} , \\ &\qquad \tan\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} = x_{\mathrm{r}} , \ \ \alpha_{\mathrm{r}} \in (0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.80}\]

The size \(|z_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}|\) is therefore constant, and the turn per step is \(2\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} = 2\arctan(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2)\) instead of the exact \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\).

Proof

We show that at every step the complex number \(z_{\mathrm{r}}\) is multiplied by the same number of size \(1\).

  1. Splitting by modes. We multiply equations (10.74) and (10.75) by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\). We get \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} = \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). The equation of motion is \(\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} = -\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\). By corollary 10.14 we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), and by theorem 10.13 \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(s = r\), \(0\) otherwise, so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s = r\) and \(0\) otherwise. Hence \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} = -\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. With \(p_{\mathrm{k}} = \xi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(x = x_{\mathrm{r}}\) step 1 gives \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \eta_{\mathrm{k}} = x\,(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1})\) and \(p_{\mathrm{k}+1} - p_{\mathrm{k}} = -x\,(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  3. With \(z = \eta + \mathrm{i}\,p\) we have \(z_{\mathrm{k}+1} - z_{\mathrm{k}} = x(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \mathrm{i}\,x(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1})\), which is \(-\mathrm{i}\,x\big[(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}) + \mathrm{i}(p_{\mathrm{k}} + p_{\mathrm{k}+1})\big] = -\mathrm{i}\,x\,(z_{\mathrm{k}} + z_{\mathrm{k}+1})\).
  4. From step 3, \((1 + \mathrm{i}\,x)\,z_{\mathrm{k}+1} = (1 - \mathrm{i}\,x)\,z_{\mathrm{k}}\). By definition 9.24 the number \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x\) has the polar form \(1 + \mathrm{i}\,x = \rho(\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)\) with \(\rho = \sqrt{1 + x^2}\) and \(\alpha \in (-\pi, \pi]\). For it \(\rho\cos\alpha = 1\) and \(\rho\sin\alpha = x\), so \(\cos\alpha > 0\) and \(\sin\alpha > 0\). Since \(\sin\alpha > 0\), \(\alpha \in (0, \pi)\), and since \(\cos\alpha > 0\), \(\alpha < \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) (lemma 8.10, point 3). Hence \(\alpha \in (0, \tfrac{\pi}{2})\) and \(\tan\alpha = x\). The number \(1 - \mathrm{i}\,x\) is its conjugate, \(\rho(\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)\).
  5. The quotient is \(\dfrac{\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha}\). Since \((\cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)(\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha) = \cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha = 1\), the quotient equals \((\cos\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha)^2 = \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha - 2\mathrm{i}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha = \cos 2\alpha - \mathrm{i}\sin 2\alpha\) (corollary 8.15). This is equation (10.80). The size of this number is \(\cos^2 2\alpha + \sin^2 2\alpha = 1\), so \(|z_{\mathrm{k}+1}| = |z_{\mathrm{k}}|\).

Source: COOK §11.14 (PDF pp. 437 to 439) derives the same by analysing one mode: the roots \(\lambda_{1,2}\) are conjugate complex numbers with \(|\lambda| = 1\), "period elongation" for average acceleration (11.14-21) and "free of amplitude error" (PDF p. 440). Our proof with the number \(z\) and the turn \(2\arctan(\omega\Delta t/2)\) is not from the source.

The period in the scheme is the time in which \(z\) turns by \(2\pi\), that is \(2\pi\Delta t/(2\alpha)\), while the exact one is \(2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\Delta t/(2x)\). Their ratio is \(x/\arctan x\) and \(\arctan x < x\), so the period in the scheme is longer than the true one. The expansion (SymPy) is \(x/\arctan x = 1 + x^2/3 - 4x^4/45 + \dots\), so for small \(\omega\Delta t\) the elongation is about \((\omega\Delta t)^2/12\). The values for four steps are in the table in the next section.

The turn in the scheme per step at \(\Delta t = T/10\) (\(x = \pi/10 = 0{,}31416\)) is \(2\arctan 0{,}31416 = 0{,}60879\) rad instead of \(\omega\Delta t = 0{,}62832\) rad. By the end of the first oscillation a lag of \(0{,}0195\) rad \(\cdot\, 10 = 0{,}195\) rad accumulates, which is \(11{,}2°\). At the end of three oscillations (figure 10.37) it is \(0{,}586\) rad, which is \(33{,}6°\).

10.12.2 Central differences (centralne diference)

The trapezoidal rule must solve a system with the matrix (10.76) at every step. In fast phenomena, such as the propagation of a shock wave, there are very many steps, and then it is cheaper if every step is cheap (COOK PDF p. 423: "Cost per time step is small"). The idea of the second scheme is not to use the velocity at all and to replace the acceleration at the time \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the second difference of three consecutive displacements.

Why this makes sense we see on the cosine. By the addition theorem (corollary 8.15) \(\cos\omega(t + \Delta t) + \cos\omega(t - \Delta t) = 2\cos\omega\Delta t\,\cos\omega t\), hence

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac{\cos\omega(t + \Delta t) - 2\cos\omega t + \cos\omega(t - \Delta t)}{\Delta t^2} \\ &\quad = -\frac{2\,(1 - \cos\omega\Delta t)}{\Delta t^2}\,\cos\omega t = -\omega^2 R\cos\omega t , \\ &\quad R = \frac{2(1 - \cos\omega\Delta t)}{(\omega\Delta t)^2} , \qquad 1 - \frac{(\omega\Delta t)^2}{12} \le R \le 1 . \end{aligned} \tag{10.81}\]

The second difference of the cosine is therefore the second derivative \(-\omega^2\cos\omega t\) with a relative error between \(0\) and \((\omega\Delta t)^2/12\) (by lemma 10.37). The error is at most of order \(\Delta t^2\): with half the step it is four times smaller.

Definition 10.68 Central differences (centralne diference)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat K\) a symmetric matrix of order \(n\), \(\Delta t > 0\) and \(\vek Q(t)\) a column of forces. For \(\mat C = \mat 0\) central differences are the sequence of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) that for \(k \ge 1\) and with the given initial columns \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) satisfies

\[\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1} = \Delta t^2\,\mat M^{-1}\big(\vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} - \mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \qquad \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek Q(t_{\mathrm{k}}) . \tag{10.82}\]

The start is usually \(\vek q_1 = \vek q_0 + \Delta t\,\vek v_0 + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\,\vek a_0\) with \(\mat M\vek a_0 = \vek Q_0 - \mat K\vek q_0\). The velocity, if we want it, is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1})/(2\Delta t)\).

Source: COOK §11.12 (PDF pp. 423 to 425), (11.12-1a) and (11.12-1b): "classical central differences", velocity and acceleration from displacements. BATHE §9.2.1 (PDF pp. 787 to 789), (9.3) to (9.10) and Table 9.1: the same scheme and the start with \(\vek q_{-1} = \vek q_0 - \Delta t\,\vek v_0 + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\,\vek a_0\) (PDF p. 788). For \(\mat C \ne \mat 0\) COOK (11.12-3) and (11.12-6), not treated here.

The scheme is explicit (eksplicitna): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) follows from the already known \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), without solving a system of equations with a full matrix. If \(\mat M\) is diagonal (lumped mass, definition 10.32), multiplication by \(\mat M^{-1}\) is division of each component by its mass. The matrix \(\mat K\) then need not even be assembled, because the product \(\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) can be summed element by element (BATHE PDF pp. 788 to 789, COOK PDF pp. 425 to 426). The trapezoidal rule, by contrast, is implicit (implicitna): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is determined by an equation that contains \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) itself, and we must solve it.

The price is a stability condition. To find it we need the recurrence that the scheme has in each mode.

Lemma 10.69 Second-order recurrence (rekurzija drugega reda)

Let \(b\) be a real number and \((u_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{k} \ge 0}\) a sequence of real numbers with

\[u_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2b\,u_{\mathrm{k}} + u_{\mathrm{k}-1} = 0 \qquad (k \ge 1) . \tag{10.83}\]

The sequence is determined by \(u_0\) and \(u_1\).

  1. For \(|b| < 1\) let \(\psi \in (0, \pi)\) be the angle with \(\cos\psi = b\). Then \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = u_0\cos k\psi + c\sin k\psi\) with \(c = (u_1 - u_0\cos\psi)/\sin\psi\). Every solution is bounded, \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| \le |u_0| + |c|\).
  2. For \(|b| = 1\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = b^k\big(u_0 + k\,(b\,u_1 - u_0)\big)\). The solution is bounded exactly when \(u_1 = b\,u_0\), otherwise \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}|\) grows linearly with \(k\).
  3. For \(|b| > 1\) let \(z = b + \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{b^2 - 1}\), so that \(|z| > 1\). The sequence \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = z^k\) is a solution with \(u_0 = 1\), \(u_1 = z\) and is unbounded: \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| = |z|^k\).
Proof

We show that the stated sequences solve equation (10.83) and have the right initial values.

  1. From equation (10.83), \(u_{\mathrm{k}+1} = 2b\,u_{\mathrm{k}} - u_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), so \(u_0\) and \(u_1\) determine all terms by induction. Two solutions with the same initial values are therefore equal.
  2. For \(|b| < 1\) the angle \(\psi\) exists by theorem 2.57, because \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\) (lemma 8.10), and \(\sin\psi > 0\). By the addition theorem (corollary 8.15) \(\cos(k + 1)\psi + \cos(k - 1)\psi = 2\cos k\psi\,\cos\psi = 2b\cos k\psi\) and likewise for the sine, so \(\cos k\psi\) and \(\sin k\psi\) are solutions, and so is their combination (equation (10.83) is linear). For \(k = 0\) it gives \(u_0\), for \(k = 1\) it gives \(u_0\cos\psi + c\sin\psi = u_1\). By step 1 this is the required solution. The estimate follows from \(|\cos| \le 1\) and \(|\sin| \le 1\).
  3. For \(|b| = 1\) we have \(b^2 = 1\). We insert \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = b^k(a + c\,k)\): \(b^{k+1}(a + c(k+1)) - 2b\cdot b^k(a + ck) + b^{k-1}(a + c(k-1))\) equals \(b^{k-1}\big[(a + ck + c) - 2(a + ck) + (a + ck - c)\big] = 0\). The initial values \(u_0 = a\) and \(u_1 = b(a + c)\) give \(a = u_0\) and \(c = b\,u_1 - u_0\) (since \(b^2 = 1\)). The solution is bounded exactly for \(c = 0\), that is for \(u_1 = b\,u_0\), otherwise \(|u_{\mathrm{k}}| \ge k|c| - |a|\) and grows linearly.
  4. For \(|b| > 1\) we have \(z^2 - 2bz + 1 = 0\), because \(z = b + s\) with \(s = \operatorname{sgn}(b)\sqrt{b^2 - 1}\) and \(z^2 - 2bz + 1 = (b + s)^2 - 2b(b + s) + 1 = s^2 - b^2 + 1 = 0\). Hence \(z^{k+1} - 2b\,z^k + z^{k-1} = z^{k-1}(z^2 - 2bz + 1) = 0\). The size: \(|z| = |b| + \sqrt{b^2 - 1} > 1\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. The same idea (look for a solution in the form of a power, and with a double root with a factor \(k\)) is in lemma 9.35 for continuous time. The same idea, a solution of the form \(\lambda^n\) with \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = 1\), is in COOK §11.14, PDF pp. 435 to 436.

Central differences for \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) also split by modes, and every mode is a recurrence from the lemma. The parameter \(b\) for the mode \(r\) equals \(1 - \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\Delta t^2/2\).

Theorem 10.70 Stability of central differences (stabilnost centralnih diferenc)

Let \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat K\) positive semidefinite with the pairs \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) of theorem 10.13, \(0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\max}^2 > 0\). Let \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and let the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(k \ge 0\)) satisfy equation (10.82) for \(k \ge 1\) with arbitrary initial displacements \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\).

  1. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t < 2\) every mode with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) rotates with bounded amplitude, with the angle \(2\arcsin(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2)\) per step. The mode with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) moves as \(a + b\,k\), that is linearly, like the rigid-body motions in theorem 10.23.
  2. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t = 2\) there is a solution that grows linearly with \(k\).
  3. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t > 2\) there is a solution with \(\sqrt{\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}}\) growing like \(\rho^k\) with \(\rho > 1\). For positive definite \(\mat K\) the scheme is therefore conditionally stable with the critical time step \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2/\omega_{\max}\) (definition 10.65).
Proof

We show that the scheme splits into the recurrences of lemma 10.69, one for each mode, and apply the lemma.

  1. We write equation (10.82) for \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) as \(\mat M(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}) = -\Delta t^2\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and multiply it by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\). By corollary 10.14 we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by theorem 10.13 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\). Hence \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} - 2b_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} + \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}-1} = 0\) with \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t^2/2\).
  2. The initial \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},1}\) are arbitrary numbers, because \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek q_1\) are arbitrary and the expansion by modes is a unique replacement of a column by the numbers \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\). The recurrences for different \(r\) are independent. The sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded exactly when all sequences \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) are bounded, because \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}^2\) (orthonormality of the modes, theorem 10.13).
  3. We have \(b_{\mathrm{r}} \le 1\), and \(b_{\mathrm{r}} > -1\) exactly when \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t^2 < 4\), that is \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t < 2\). The largest \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(\omega_{\max}\) and gives the smallest \(b\). For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t < 2\) we therefore have \(|b_{\mathrm{r}}| < 1\) for every \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\), and by point 1 of the lemma the solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\) is bounded, with the angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{r}} = b_{\mathrm{r}}\). From \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1 - 2\sin^2(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2)\) (corollary 8.15) we get \(\sin^2(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2) = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\Delta t^2/4\) and, since \(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2 \in (0, \pi/2)\), \(\sin(\psi_{\mathrm{r}}/2) = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t/2\). For \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) we have \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) and by point 2 of the lemma \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r},0} + k(\eta_{\mathrm{r},1} - \eta_{\mathrm{r},0})\). This is point 1.
  4. For \(\omega_{\max}\Delta t \ge 2\) we have \(b_{\mathrm{n}} \le -1\). We take \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) and for \(\eta_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{k}}\) the solution of point 2 of the lemma (for \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = -1\), \(u_0 = 1\), \(u_1 = 0\) we have \(c = b_{\mathrm{n}} u_1 - u_0 = -1 \ne 0\), so linear growth) or of point 3 of the lemma (for \(b_{\mathrm{n}} < -1\) we have \(\rho = |z| > 1\)). Since \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \eta_{\mathrm{n},\mathrm{k}}^2\), this gives points 2 and 3. By definition 10.65 conditional stability follows for positive definite \(\mat K\).

Source: COOK §11.12, PDF p. 425: "Equation 11.12-3 is conditionally stable", \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{cr}} = 2/\omega_{\max}\) "independent of damping", "limiting stability" at \(\Delta t = 2/\omega_{\max}\) ("divergence ... only in arithmetic fashion"), above it "exponential" (equation (11.12-9)). 10.12.3 Choice of step and limits of the computation (izbira koraka in meje računa)

The trapezoidal rule is unconditionally stable for frame A (theorem 10.66), so we can run it with steps larger than \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\). Take \(\Delta t = T_2/2 = 0{,}06129\) s, which is more than the critical step of central differences, \(0{,}03902\) s. We start from the static position \(\vek q_0 = (5,\ 10)\) mm, \(\vek v_0 = \vek 0\) and make 1000 steps. The energy is \(\mathcal{E}_0 = 12{,}544\) J (section 10.4) and after 1000 steps it differs from it by less than \(3\cdot 10^{-13}\) relatively, so only by rounding (figure 10.40). The energy is conserved even when the step is so large that only two steps fall on an oscillation of the second mode.

But conserved energy is not the exact solution. By theorem 10.67 the first mode turns by \(2\arctan(\omega_1\Delta t/2) = 1{,}08083\) rad per step instead of \(\omega_1\Delta t = 1{,}20000\) rad, so its period is longer by \(11{,}0\) %. The second mode is at \(\omega_2\Delta t = \pi\) and \(2\arctan(\pi/2) = 2{,}00777\) rad instead of \(3{,}14159\) rad, its period is longer by \(56{,}5\) %. The energy of each mode is right, but its phase is not. Conservation of energy is therefore a condition for stability, not for accuracy.

Let us look at the case for which we introduced stepping: a damper in the first storey, which couples the modes. The damping matrix is \(\mat C = \operatorname{diag}(1416{,}70,\ 0)\) N s/m (section 10.9), the start the same as above. For comparison we write the exact solution as a sum of four terms \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) along the eigenvectors of the state matrix of order \(2n = 4\) (NumPy), with the roots \(s = -0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02493 \pm 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s from section 10.9. We apply the trapezoidal rule by equation (10.77), with the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}\) for \(\mat C \ne \mat 0\). The largest error \(|q_1 - q_1^{\mathrm{nat}}|\) between \(0\) and \(1\) s is (the largest displacement of the first floor in this time is \(5{,}70\) mm):

With every halving of the step the error decreases about four times, which is order \(\Delta t^2\). Such an order follows from theorem 10.67: the error of the turn per step is \(2(x - \arctan x) = 2x^3/3 + \dots\), there are \(\pi/x\) steps per oscillation, so the error per oscillation is of order \(x^2\). For an error below \(1\) % of the largest displacement this example needs the step \(T_2/40\).

From this follows a rule for the choice of step. We choose \(\Delta t \approx T/20\), where \(T\) is the period of the highest mode that the force still excites significantly. By the table the period error is then \(0{,}8\) % for the trapezoidal rule and \(0{,}4\) % for central differences, so below \(1\) %. The rule is a guideline, because the period error is repeated in every oscillation. For \(T/20\) and the trapezoidal rule we have \(x = \pi/20\) and the turn per oscillation is short by \(20 \cdot 2(x - \arctan x) = 0{,}0509\) rad, which is \(2{,}9°\), too little. After ten oscillations the phase is shifted by \(29°\). Whoever wants to follow many oscillations must reduce the step. On frame A the error in the first second (\(8\) oscillations of the second mode) is below \(1\) % only at \(T_2/40\), although by the rule \(T_2/20\) would suffice.

The choice of mass is helped by COOK (PDF pp. 439 to 440). Lumped mass usually lowers the frequencies (for bar C always, section 10.7) and thus lengthens the periods, which partly cancels the shrinking of the period in central differences. Consistent mass raises the frequencies and shortens the periods, which partly cancels the elongation in the trapezoidal rule. Lumped mass with central differences and consistent mass with the trapezoidal rule are therefore according to COOK favourable both for accuracy and for cost (PDF p. 440).

The stability condition of central differences, \(\Delta t < 2/\omega_{\max}\), refers to the highest mode of the mesh, even if the force does not excite it. The trapezoidal rule has no such condition. COOK (PDF p. 423) therefore says that explicit methods are best for wave propagation, where accuracy alone already requires a small step, because the "cost per time step is small, but so is the critical time step", and that they are not suitable for structural dynamics ("not well suited to structural dynamics problems"). BATHE (PDF p. 789) says the same: for waves \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) does not require an unusually small step, but for structures a step "much larger than" the critical one would suffice for accuracy.

All that we have shown holds for linear equations. The Runge-Kutta methods, which we used for checking in section 8.13, are a third family that we do not treat. Nonlinear systems and the choice of schemes with numerical damping (algorithmic damping, numerično dušenje) are in chapter 17. Other members of Newmark's family, which have other parameters and numerical damping, are in COOK §11.13 (PDF pp. 432 to 434) and BATHE §9.2.3 (PDF p. 794).
