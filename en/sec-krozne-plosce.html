Chapter 18 Plates

18.6 Circular plates

The bottom of a pressurised vessel, a manhole cover, a pump membrane and a turbine disc share one property: their middle plane is a circle (VK, PDF p. 100). Such a plate is called a circular plate (krožna plošča), after the shape of its middle plane, a circle of radius \(a\). The square plate of section 18.5 needed a double series, but for the circle there is a hope that the computation simplifies a great deal: a circle looks the same from every side, so its solution should be the same too, if the load and the support do not change along the rim. Let us see what this says, and whether the computation is really so simple.

The model is the plate of sections 18.1 to 18.3, that is the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) with its named idealizations: the plate is thin, its deflection is small compared with the thickness, the normals of the middle plane stay straight and perpendicular to it, and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is neglected. The running example is the circle inscribed in the square of example B of section 18.5: a steel plate of radius \(a = 250\) mm and thickness \(h = 10\) mm (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) is loaded by a uniform pressure \(q = 10\) kPa that acts in the same direction as the displacement \(w\). The bending stiffness is \(D = E h^3/(12(1-\nu^2)) = 17\,948{,}7\) N m (definition 18.6). The idealizations have a measure: the ratio is \(h/a = 0{,}04\), that is \(2a/h = 50\). EP (p. 392) calls a plate thin if the ratio of its smallest in-plane dimension to the thickness is at least \(20\), and the largest displacement (of the simply supported plate) will be \(0{,}139\) mm, which is \(1{,}4\ \%\) of the thickness, so indeed much less than \(h\).

From history

Poisson rewrote the plate equation in polar coordinates for a load that depends only on the radius and gave a complete solution of this problem, and then applied it to a uniform load for a simply supported and for a clamped edge (TIMO, PDF p. 121). The reason for this success is our reading: when everything depends on the radius alone, the equation in the plane becomes an ordinary differential equation in one variable, which can be integrated step by step.

We repeat Poisson's step: the plate equation of theorem 18.12 is written for a field that depends only on the radius \(r\) and is integrated. On the way we will see what must hold at the centre, and the boundary conditions fix the constants. We will test the result on our plate and then show what happens when the force acts at one point, where this condition at the centre no longer holds.

Let us draw what we will compute (figure 18.17). On the left is the plate seen from above, a circle of radius \(a\) with the origin at the centre. The radius \(r\) to a point is the length \(r\), the point has the angle \(\theta\) with the horizontal axis, and the radius \(a\) reaches the rim. At the point on the radius \(r\) we place two direction vectors: \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) points outward along the radius and \(\vek e_\theta\) perpendicular to it, along the circle of radius \(r\). With a dashed circle we mark the piece of plate that we will treat: a disc of radius \(r < a\). On the right is the section through the axis, with the thickness greatly exaggerated. The plate is supported at both ends, the load \(q\) acts on top, and the arrows end on the surface because the pressure presses on the plate. The vertical dashed lines show where we cut the disc out. Where we cut it out, the rest of the plate is replaced by what it exerts on the rim of the disc: the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) per unit length of the rim and the bending moment \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) per unit length of the rim. Below the section are the displacement \(w\) and the radius \(r\) of the disc: \(w\) points downward, in the direction of the load. The arrows show how the force and the moment actually act under our load. The shear force carries the disc upward, against the load, and the moment bends it so that the top, loaded surface is compressed and the bottom one stretched. The sign is as in section 18.1: \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is positive in the direction of \(w\), and a moment is positive for compression on the surface toward which \(w\) points, here the bottom one. Hence \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) in the figure are negative, which the computation will confirm.

How do we express all the quantities with the radius? Section 18.2 gave us the plate equation \(D\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12) and the third section the moments, shear forces and boundary conditions in Cartesian coordinates. We need two steps: the operator \(\nabla^2\) in polar coordinates (we computed it in chapter 11, corollary 11.18), and then the moments and the shear force in polar form. Both follow from the next lemma.

Lemma 18.34 An axisymmetric plate (osnosimetrična plošča)

Let \(w(x, y) = F(r)\) with \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), where \(F\) is a four times continuously differentiable function on the interval \(0 < r \le a\). We write \(F' = \mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}r\). Then:

  1. \(\nabla^2 w = F'' + F'/r = \frac1r\,(r F')'\) and \(\nabla^4 w = \frac1r\,\big(r\,(\nabla^2 w)'\big)'\).
  2. At the point \((r, 0)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F'/r\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). Hence in local axes whose axis \(x\) points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (and axis \(y\) along \(\vek e_\theta\)) the moments per unit length are
    \[M_{\mathrm{r}} = D\Big(F'' + \nu\,\frac{F'}{r}\Big) , \qquad M_\theta = D\Big(\frac{F'}{r} + \nu\,F''\Big) , \qquad M_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0 , \tag{18.39}\]
    and the shear force is \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D\,(\nabla^2 w)'\), \(Q_\theta = 0\).
Proof

We must show the form of the Laplace operator for fields without angular dependence and express the second derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) at a point on the axis \(x\) with derivatives of the function \(F\).

  1. The function \(w\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and \(z\), so in corollary 11.18 the terms with derivatives in \(\theta\) and \(z\) vanish and \(\nabla^2 w = F'' + F'/r\). Since \((rF')' = F' + rF''\), this equals \(\frac1r(rF')'\).
  2. The function \(\psi = \nabla^2 w\) again depends only on \(r\) and is twice continuously differentiable, because \(F\) is four times. Corollary 11.18 applied to \(\psi\) gives \(\nabla^4 w = \nabla^2\psi = \frac1r(r\psi')'\). This is item 1.
  3. With \(\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) we have \(\partial\rho/\partial x = x/\rho\) and \(\partial\rho/\partial y = y/\rho\). The chain rule gives \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = F'\,x/\rho\) and
    \[w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\,\frac{x^2}{\rho^2} + F'\Big(\frac1\rho - \frac{x^2}{\rho^3}\Big) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F''\,\frac{y^2}{\rho^2} + F'\Big(\frac1\rho - \frac{y^2}{\rho^3}\Big) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Big(F'' - \frac{F'}{\rho}\Big)\frac{xy}{\rho^2} .\]
  4. At the point \((r, 0)\) we have \(y = 0\), \(x = \rho = r\). Substituting: \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F'/r\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  5. The function \(w\) depends only on the distance from the centre, so it does not change when the axes are rotated about the centre. Every point at the distance \(r\) can therefore be treated as the point \((r, 0)\) in rotated axes whose axis \(x\) points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Theorem 18.7 with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{xy}} = w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{yy}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = D(w_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{xx}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D(1-\nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and with step 4 equations (18.39).
  6. By theorem 18.12 the shear force is \(\vek Q = -D\,\nabla(\nabla^2 w)\), that is the gradient of a function of \(r\) alone, so it points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and its component along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(-D\,(\nabla^2 w)'\).

Source: EP (pp. 405-406, equations (7.2.32) to (7.2.37)) gives the equation, stresses, moments and shear force for an axisymmetric circular plate, VK (PDF p. 101) derives the Laplace operator in polar coordinates with the derivative rule. The proof with corollary 11.18 and the moments from the Cartesian form is not from the source.

Lemma 18.34 says that the plate equation for a field depending on \(r\) alone is an ordinary equation of fourth order. Before integrating, one more question: why should the solution for the circle really depend on \(r\) alone? The load and the support do not change along the rim, so a rotation about the centre leaves them unchanged. If we find a field \(w(r)\) that solves the equation and the boundary conditions, it is by theorem 18.23 the only solution, so a field that would depend on \(\theta\) as well is excluded. The uniqueness theorem thus spares us the question whether the solution is axisymmetric: it is enough to find it.

How do we solve the equation? The first integration will give the equilibrium of the disc of figure 18.17: the load on the disc is just the integral of the load, and the constant that appears in the integration will be a point force at the centre. The other three integrations give the displacement.

Theorem 18.35 An axisymmetric circular plate (osnosimetrična krožna plošča)

Let a circular plate of radius \(a\) be loaded by a load per unit area \(q(r)\) that is a continuous function on \([0, a]\), and let \(w(r)\) be a displacement, four times continuously differentiable on \(0 < r \le a\), that solves the equation \(D\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12). Denote

\[\Phi(r) = \frac1D\int_0^r s\,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]

Then:

  1. There exist constants \(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4\) and a function \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) (a particular solution) such that
    \[w(r) = w_{\mathrm{p}}(r) + c_1 + c_2 r^2 + c_3\ln r + c_4\,r^2\ln r . \tag{18.40}\]
    For a uniform load \(q_0\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = q_0 r^4/(64D)\).
  2. For every \(r > 0\) we have \(2\pi r\,Q_{\mathrm{r}}(r) + \int_0^r q(s)\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s = -8\pi D\,c_4\). The left side is the sum of the forces in the direction of \(w\) on the disc of radius \(r\), the shear force along the rim and the load, as in equation (18.12) of lemma 18.10, which the same argument gives for the disc. Since the disc is in equilibrium, the right side is the negative of the point force at the centre. If there is no point force at the centre, \(c_4 = 0\), and if a force \(P\) acts in the direction of \(w\), \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\).
  3. If \(w\) is a continuously differentiable function of \(x\) and \(y\) also at the centre, then \(c_3 = 0\). A plate without a point force at the centre therefore has \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}} + c_1 + c_2 r^2\), two constants that the two boundary conditions at \(r = a\) determine.
Proof

We must show that the equation can be integrated four times and what the constants mean. Denote \(g = \nabla^2 w\).

  1. By lemma 18.34, \(g = \frac1r(rw')'\) and the equation is \(\frac1r(rg')' = q/D\), so \((rg')' = rq/D\).
  2. The function \(s\,q(s)\) is continuous, so \(\Phi\) is differentiable with \(\Phi' = rq/D\) by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40). The difference \(rg' - \Phi\) has derivative zero, so it is a constant \(C_1\): \(rg' = \Phi + C_1\).
  3. We have \(g' = \Phi/r + C_1/r\). Let \(G(r) = \int_0^r \Phi(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \(\Phi(s) = O(s^2)\) as \(s \to 0\), \(\Phi/s\) is continuous and \(G\) is differentiable with \(G' = \Phi/r\). From \((\ln r)' = 1/r\) it follows that \(g = G + C_1\ln r + C_2\).
  4. Let \(H(r) = \int_0^r sG(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). From \((rw')' = rg\) and \((r^2\ln r/2 - r^2/4)' = r\ln r\) it follows that \(rw' = H + C_1\big(\tfrac12 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2\big) + \tfrac12 C_2 r^2 + C_3\).
  5. We divide by \(r\) and let \(K(r) = \int_0^r H(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \((\tfrac14 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2)' = \tfrac12 r\ln r - \tfrac14 r\), we get \(w = K + C_1\big(\tfrac14 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2\big) + \tfrac14 C_2 r^2 + C_3\ln r + C_4\). With \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = K\), \(c_1 = C_4\), \(c_2 = (C_2 - C_1)/4\), \(c_3 = C_3\), \(c_4 = C_1/4\) this is equation (18.40). For \(q = q_0\) we have \(\Phi = q_0r^2/(2D)\) and \(\nabla^2(r^4) = 16r^2\), \(\nabla^4(r^4) = 64\) (lemma 18.34), so \(D\nabla^4(q_0r^4/(64D)) = q_0\).
  6. For item 2: by lemma 18.34, \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -Dg'\), so \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} = -2\pi D\,(\Phi + C_1)\). The term \(2\pi D\,\Phi(r) = \int_0^r q(s)\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s\) is the load on the disc, so \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} + \int_0^r q\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s = -2\pi D\,C_1 = -8\pi D\,c_4\). If a force \(P\) also acts on the disc at the centre, the equilibrium of the disc by the same argument as in equation (18.12) is \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} + \int_0^r q\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s + P = 0\), so \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\). Without the force \(c_4 = 0\).
  7. For item 3: in steps 4 and 5 all terms of the derivative \(w'\) except \(c_3/r\) tend to zero as \(r \to 0\): \(K' = H/r = O(r^3)\), \(c_2r \to 0\) and \((r^2\ln r)' = 2r\ln r + r \to 0\). Since \(w\) is continuously differentiable in \(x\) and \(y\) and \(w(x, 0) = F(|x|)\) is an even function of \(x\), we have \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, 0) = 0\) and hence \(F'(r) = w_{\mathrm{x}}(r, 0) \to 0\). The term \(c_3/r\) with \(c_3 \ne 0\) is not bounded, so \(c_3 = 0\).

Source: EP (p. 406, equations (7.2.39) to (7.2.43)) obtains the same general solution by successive integrations and says that symmetry at the centre requires \(c_3 = 0\). VK (PDF p. 105) says that the logarithmic terms in a full plate without a point force give an infinite displacement, moment and shear force, so these constants are zero. The proof with the equilibrium of the disc for \(c_4\) and with the derivative for \(c_3\) is not from the source.

The theorem said two things: the plate equation for an \(r\)-dependent load is solved with four constants, and two of them vanish if the plate is whole and has no point force. Each hypothesis has its role. Without the continuity of the derivative at the centre the constant \(c_3\) would remain, and the plate would have a hole or a peak. Without the condition on the point force \(c_4\) remains, which we will see right away in the last lemma.

Now we can solve the example. We determine \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) from the boundary conditions at \(r = a\) (definition 18.19), first for a clamped edge, where \(w = 0\) and \(\partial w/\partial r = 0\).

Corollary 18.36 A clamped circular plate (vpeta krožna plošča)

A circular plate of radius \(a\) with a clamped edge, \(w(a) = 0\) and \(w'(a) = 0\), loaded by a uniform pressure \(q\), has

\[w = \frac{q\,(a^2 - r^2)^2}{64D} , \qquad w(0) = \frac{q a^4}{64D} , \tag{18.41}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{q}{16}\,\big((3+\nu)\,r^2 - (1+\nu)\,a^2\big) , \qquad M_\theta = \frac{q}{16}\,\big((1+3\nu)\,r^2 - (1+\nu)\,a^2\big) , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{qr}{2} . \tag{18.42}\]

The largest absolute value of the moments is at the edge, \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = qa^2/8\). There \(M_\theta(a) = \nu\,qa^2/8\).

Proof

We must show that the function of theorem 18.35 with two constants satisfies both conditions at the edge.

  1. By item 3 of theorem 18.35, \(w = qr^4/(64D) + c_1 + c_2r^2\), since there is no point force at the centre and the plate is whole.
  2. The derivative is \(w' = qr^3/(16D) + 2c_2r\). The condition \(w'(a) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = -qa^2/(32D)\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -qa^4/(64D) - c_2a^2 = qa^4/(64D)\).
  4. Substituting: \(w = \frac{q}{64D}(r^4 - 2a^2r^2 + a^4) = \frac{q}{64D}(a^2 - r^2)^2\). This is equation (18.41).
  5. Derivatives: \(w' = qr(r^2 - a^2)/(16D)\) and \(w'' = q(3r^2 - a^2)/(16D)\). Equations (18.39) give \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{q}{16}\big((3r^2 - a^2) + \nu(r^2 - a^2)\big)\) and \(M_\theta = \frac{q}{16}\big((r^2 - a^2) + \nu(3r^2 - a^2)\big)\), which is (18.42).
  6. \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D(\nabla^2 w)'\), \(\nabla^2 w = q(2r^2 - a^2)/(8D)\) and \((\nabla^2 w)' = qr/(2D)\).
  7. At the edge \(r = a\) we have \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac q{16}(3 + \nu - 1 - \nu)a^2 = qa^2/8\) and \(M_\theta = \frac q{16}(1 + 3\nu - 1 - \nu)a^2 = \nu qa^2/8\).

Source: EP Example 7.2.1 (pp. 407-408) solves the same problem with the same constants and states that the largest moment is at the clamped edge. VK (PDF p. 106) gives the clamped edge under uniform load in equation (4.26) and the following. The notation with item 3 of the theorem and the steps are ours.

Let us look at the numbers for our plate (\(a = 250\) mm, \(q = 10\) kPa, \(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m), computed in outlines/ch-plosce/num-06.py. The central deflection is \(w(0) = qa^4/(64D) = 0{,}03401\) mm, which is \(0{,}34\ \%\) of the thickness. At the edge \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = qa^2/8 = 78{,}13\) N m/m and at the centre \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -(1+\nu)\,qa^2/16 = -50{,}78\) N m/m. At the edge the top surface is stretched, at the centre compressed, so the two moments have opposite signs. By corollary 18.8 the stress is \(\sigma_{\max} = 6|M|/h^2\), so \(4{,}69\) MPa at the edge and \(3{,}05\) MPa at the centre. The shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -qr/2\) is \(-1250\) N/m at the edge, and the rim gives a total of \(2\pi a\cdot 1250 = 1963{,}5\) N, which is exactly the pressure \(q\) times the area \(\pi a^2\), as it must be by the equilibrium of the whole plate. Why is \(M_\theta(a) = \nu M_{\mathrm{r}}(a)\)? Because the edge is clamped, \(w'(a) = 0\) and the curvature in the circumferential direction \(w'/r\) is zero there, and from equations (18.39) it follows that \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = Dw''\) and \(M_\theta = \nu Dw''\). For a plate in the form of a strip the curvature in one direction is zero for another reason, but the result is the same: \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu M_{\mathrm{x}}\) (section 18.4).

Now a simply supported edge, where \(w = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) (definition 18.19). This edge is circular and therefore smooth: unlike the square there are no corners on which a force \(2M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) would act (corollary 18.22), and because of \(M_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) (lemma 18.34) the effective shear force at the edge is simply \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Corollary 18.37 A simply supported circular plate (prosto podprta krožna plošča)

A circular plate of radius \(a\) with a simply supported edge, \(w(a) = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = 0\), loaded by a uniform pressure \(q\), has

\[w = \frac{q\,(a^2 - r^2)}{64D}\Big(\frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\,a^2 - r^2\Big) , \qquad w(0) = \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\,\frac{qa^4}{64D} , \tag{18.43}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{(3+\nu)\,q}{16}\,(a^2 - r^2) , \qquad M_\theta = -\frac q{16}\big((3+\nu)\,a^2 - (1+3\nu)\,r^2\big) , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{qr}2 . \tag{18.44}\]

The largest absolute value of both moments is at the centre, \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -(3+\nu)\,qa^2/16\). The difference of the displacements of the supported and the clamped plate is the displacement caused by a uniform moment \(M_{\mathrm{a}} = -qa^2/8\) along the edge:

\[w_{\mathrm{podprta}} - w_{\mathrm{vpeta}} = -\frac{M_{\mathrm{a}}\,(a^2 - r^2)}{2D(1+\nu)} , \qquad M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = M_{\mathrm{a}} . \tag{18.45}\]
Proof

We must show that the function of theorem 18.35 with two constants satisfies both conditions and that the difference from the clamped plate is a field with a constant moment.

  1. By item 3 of theorem 18.35, \(w = qr^4/(64D) + c_1 + c_2r^2\), \(w' = qr^3/(16D) + 2c_2r\), \(w'' = 3qr^2/(16D) + 2c_2\).
  2. By equation (18.39) the condition \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = 0\) is \(w''(a) + \nu w'(a)/a = 0\), that is \(\frac{(3+\nu)\,qa^2}{16D} + 2(1+\nu)c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = -\frac{(3+\nu)\,qa^2}{32(1+\nu)D}\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -\frac{qa^4}{64D} - c_2a^2 = \frac{qa^4}{64D}\cdot\frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\).
  4. By substitution \(w = \frac{q}{64D}\Big(r^4 - \frac{2(3+\nu)}{1+\nu}a^2r^2 + \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}a^4\Big)\). The product in (18.43) is the same polynomial: the coefficient at \(a^2r^2\) is \(-1 - \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu} = -\frac{2(3+\nu)}{1+\nu}\).
  5. Moments from equations (18.39): \(w'/r = qr^2/(16D) + 2c_2\), so \(w'' + \nu w'/r = \frac{(3+\nu)\,qr^2}{16D} + 2(1+\nu)\,c_2 = \frac{(3+\nu)\,q}{16D}\,(r^2 - a^2)\) with \(c_2\) from step 2, and \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = D(w'' + \nu w'/r) = -\frac{(3+\nu)q}{16}(a^2 - r^2)\). \(M_\theta\) follows in the same way from \(w'/r + \nu w''\). The shear force is the same as for the clamped plate (\(\nabla^2 w\) differs only by a constant, the derivative does not).
  6. For equation (18.45): the difference is \(w_{\mathrm{podprta}} - w_{\mathrm{vpeta}} = \frac{qa^2(a^2 - r^2)}{16(1+\nu)D}\) (we subtract the polynomials). The field \(c\,(a^2 - r^2)\) has \(w'' = -2c\) and \(w'/r = -2c\), so by equations (18.39) \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -2cD(1+\nu)\). With \(c = -\frac{M_{\mathrm{a}}}{2D(1+\nu)}\) this is \(M_{\mathrm{a}}\), and for \(M_{\mathrm{a}} = -qa^2/8\) we get \(c = \frac{qa^2}{16(1+\nu)D}\).

Source: EP (p. 405, table 7.2.1) lists the conditions for a simply supported edge (\(w = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{rr}} = M_{\mathrm{a}}\)) and VK (PDF pp. 105-106, equations (4.24) to (4.28)) solves the same problem with constants \(C_3\), \(C_4\). The difference as a constant moment (step 6) is not from the source.

The numbers for our plate: \(w(0) = 0{,}13864\) mm, which is \(1{,}4\ \%\) of the thickness (where the Kirchhoff assumption of a small deflection \(w \ll h\) still holds well), and at the centre \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -128{,}91\) N m/m, which is a stress of \(7{,}73\) MPa. The ratio of the deflections \(0{,}13864/0{,}03401 = 4{,}077\) equals \((5+\nu)/(1+\nu)\).

Figure 18.18 shows where the ratio comes from. On the left is the section through the centre from edge to edge, the displacement drawn downward. For the clamped plate the displacement at the centre is \(0{,}0156\) (in units of \(qa^4/D\)), the clamped curve is blue, the supported curve is blackwhite and reaches \(0{,}0637\). Their difference is the dashed orange curve and is exactly the displacement under a constant edge moment \(M_{\mathrm{a}}\) from equation (18.45): \(0{,}0481\) at the centre, \(0{,}0156 + 0{,}0481 = 0{,}0637\). A simply supported plate is thus a clamped plate from which we take the clamping away: the moment \(qa^2/8\) that the clamping exerted at the edge is replaced by zero, that is we add the moment \(-qa^2/8\). On the right this is seen in the moments. The vertical axis carries \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\), so that bending in the direction of the load is upward, and the values are in units of \(qa^2\). The clamped moment goes from \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = -0{,}0813\) at the centre to \(0{,}125\) at the edge, the simply supported one from \(-0{,}2063\) to \(0\), and the two curves are separated by the constant value \(0{,}125 = 1/8\). For \(\nu = 0\) the ratio \((5+\nu)/(1+\nu)\) is exactly \(5\), as for the beam with the ratio \(5qL^4/(384EI)\) to \(qL^4/(384EI)\) (section 18.4).

What does this mean in practice? VK (PDF p. 110) says that the true support lies between the two extremes and that it is sensible to compute both. For us they give: a deflection between \(0{,}034\) and \(0{,}139\) mm, a stress between \(4{,}69\) MPa (clamped, at the edge) and \(7{,}73\) MPa (supported, at the centre). The simply supported plate therefore has a \(4{,}08\) times larger deflection and a \(1{,}65\) times larger stress. Clamping is therefore most valuable for the deformation and less for the stress.

Example 18.38 A circle in a square (krog v kvadratu)

We compare the circular plate \(a = 250\) mm with the square plate \(500\) mm × \(500\) mm of section 18.5, of the same thickness \(h = 10\) mm and the same pressure \(q = 10\) kPa. Both are simply supported. The square has the central deflection \(0{,}1414\) mm, the circle \(0{,}1386\) mm. The ratio is \(0{,}980\): the circle has \(\pi/4 = 78{,}5\ \%\) of the area of the square, but only a \(2\ \%\) smaller deflection. For the clamped edge the deflection of the circle is \(0{,}0340\) mm, that of the square by Ritz's method (chapter 6, the number is from a later section of this chapter) \(0{,}0440\) mm, the ratio \(0{,}772\). Why the square and the inscribed circle have almost the same deflection for the simple support, we cannot show in one step: this is an observation from comparing numbers, not a theorem.

Let us return to the hypothesis on the point force. So far the load was a function \(q(r)\), which means that the force on every piece was smaller than the full force. What if the plate is loaded by a force \(P\) at one point, the centre? A point force is an idealization: in reality it acts on a small circle, and in the model there is nothing on it. Theorem 18.35 (item 2) predicts that the constant \(c_4\) will not be zero. Let us solve.

Lemma 18.39 A point force at the centre of a circular plate (točkovna sila v središču krožne plošče)

Let a clamped circular plate of radius \(a\) have a point force \(P\) in the direction of \(w\) at the centre and no load elsewhere. For \(0 < r \le a\),

\[w = \frac{P}{16\pi D}\Big(a^2 - r^2 + 2r^2\ln\frac ra\Big) , \qquad w(0) = \frac{Pa^2}{16\pi D} , \tag{18.46}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac P{4\pi}\Big(1 - (1+\nu)\ln\frac ar\Big) , \qquad M_\theta = \frac P{4\pi}\Big(\nu - (1+\nu)\ln\frac ar\Big) , \qquad 2\pi r\,Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -P . \tag{18.47}\]

The displacement at the centre is finite, but the absolute values of the moments grow beyond all bounds as \(r \to 0\), like \(\ln(a/r)\).

Proof

We must show which term of the general solution remains and what happens to the derivatives.

  1. A point force \(P\) at the centre: by item 2 of theorem 18.35, \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\). The centre is smooth, so by item 3, \(c_3 = 0\). There is no load outside the centre, so \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(w = c_1 + c_2r^2 + c_4r^2\ln r\).
  2. The derivative is \(w' = 2c_2r + c_4(2r\ln r + r)\). The condition \(w'(a) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = -c_4(2\ln a + 1)/2\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -c_2a^2 - c_4a^2\ln a = c_4a^2/2\).
  4. Substituting: \(w = \frac{c_4}2\big(a^2 - r^2 + 2r^2\ln(r/a)\big)\) and \(c_4/2 = P/(16\pi D)\). This is equation (18.46). As \(r \to 0\) we have \(r^2\ln r \to 0\), so \(w(0) = Pa^2/(16\pi D)\).
  5. Derivatives: \(w' = \frac{P}{4\pi D}\,r\ln(r/a)\) and \(w'' = \frac P{4\pi D}\big(\ln(r/a) + 1\big)\). Equations (18.39) give \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{P}{4\pi}\big((1+\nu)\ln(r/a) + 1\big)\) and \(M_\theta = \frac P{4\pi}\big((1+\nu)\ln(r/a) + \nu\big)\), which is (18.47).
  6. \(\ln(a/r) \to \infty\) as \(r \to 0\), so \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(M_\theta\) fall below all bounds, and their absolute values grow without bound. From item 2 of theorem 18.35 it follows that \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} = -P\), which is the equilibrium of a disc with the force \(P\).

Source: EP Example 7.2.1(b) (pp. 407-408) gives the same solution for a point force on a clamped plate. RPS (PDF p. 190, equations (5.3.54) and (5.3.55)) solves the same problem for a simply supported edge. The proof with theorem 18.35 is ours.

Numbers for \(P = 1\) kN: \(w(0) = 0{,}06928\) mm, at the edge \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = P/(4\pi) = 79{,}6\) N m/m. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) at a distance \(r\) from the centre is \(-86{,}9\), \(-253{,}4\), \(-491{,}6\) and \(-729{,}8\) N m/m at \(r = 50\), \(10\), \(1\) and \(0{,}1\) mm, which is a stress of \(5{,}22\), \(15{,}21\), \(29{,}50\) and \(43{,}79\) MPa (figure 18.19). The figure has \(r\) on the horizontal axis in a logarithmic scale, and \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\), bending in the direction of the load, on the vertical one. Since \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a linear function of \(\ln(a/r)\), the graph is a straight line, and every tenfold decrease of \(r\) adds \(P(1+\nu)\ln 10/(4\pi) = 238{,}2\) N m/m to \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the points lie on the same line). At the edge \(-M_{\mathrm{r}} = -79{,}6\) N m/m. The plate thickness \(h\) is at \(r = 10\) mm, and the orange segment shows the distances smaller than it.

May we believe such numbers? Not always. A point force is not real, and the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) is not a model for distances smaller than the thickness either, since there the normals do not stay straight with such a fast change of deflection. The distance at which the model still holds must be larger than \(h\), so \(r > 10\) mm: the stresses \(29{,}50\) MPa at \(r = 1\) mm and \(43{,}79\) MPa at \(r = 0{,}1\) mm are a consequence of the model and not a prediction for a real steel plate. EP (p. 406) therefore prescribes, after Nadai and Westergaard, that the largest moment under a point force on a small circle be computed with an equivalent radius \(r_{\mathrm{e}}\) that depends on \(h\). The energy stays finite: the energy (definition 18.14) contains \((\nabla^2 w)^2\), so a term of order \(\ln^2 r\), and the integral \(\int_0^a r\ln^2 r\,\mathrm{d}r\) is finite. Hence a point force is, in the energy sense, an admissible load, in spite of the infinite moment at one point.

We have seen how the circle is a special shape that demands only an ordinary equation. The clamped and the simply supported plate are two extremes of the true support, the difference between them is one edge moment, and the point force is a case where the hypothesis of the theorem does not hold and the logarithm remains. For the square (section 18.5) we therefore needed a series, for the circle only four integrals. What remained unsolved is a plate that is not full: an annular plate with a hole has all four constants \(c_1\) to \(c_4\) and EP treats it with a table of boundary conditions (EP, p. 405), we do not compute it. The buckling of a circular plate we cite and do not prove (EP, pp. 411-414, section 7.2.2.2).
