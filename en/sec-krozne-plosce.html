Chapter 18 Plates

18.6 Circular plates

The bottom of a pressurised vessel, a manhole cover, a pump membrane and a turbine disc share one property: their middle plane is a circle (VK, PDF p. 100). Such a plate is called a circular plate (krožna plošča), after the shape of its middle plane, a circle of radius \(a\). The square plate of section 18.5 needed a double series, but for the circle there is a hope that the computation simplifies a great deal: a circle looks the same from every side, so its solution should be the same too, if the load and the support do not change along the rim. Let us see what this says, and whether the computation is really so simple.

The model is the plate of sections 18.1 to 18.3, that is the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) with its named idealizations: the plate is thin, its deflection is small compared with the thickness, the normals of the middle plane stay straight and perpendicular to it, and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is neglected. The running example is the circle inscribed in the square of example B of section 18.5: a steel plate of radius \(a = 250\) mm and thickness \(h = 10\) mm (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) is loaded by a uniform pressure \(q = 10\) kPa that acts in the same direction as the displacement \(w\). The bending stiffness is \(D = E h^3/(12(1-\nu^2)) = 17\,948{,}7\) N m (definition 18.6). The idealizations have a measure: the ratio is \(h/a = 0{,}04\), that is \(2a/h = 50\). EP (p. 392) calls a plate thin if the ratio of its smallest in-plane dimension to the thickness is at least \(20\), and the largest displacement (of the simply supported plate) will be \(0{,}139\) mm, which is \(1{,}4\ \%\) of the thickness, so indeed much less than \(h\).

From history

Poisson rewrote the plate equation in polar coordinates for a load that depends only on the radius and gave a complete solution of this problem, and then applied it to a uniform load for a simply supported and for a clamped edge (TIMO, PDF p. 121). The reason for this success is our reading: when everything depends on the radius alone, the equation in the plane becomes an ordinary differential equation in one variable, which can be integrated step by step.

We repeat Poisson's step: the plate equation of theorem 18.12 is written for a field that depends only on the radius \(r\) and is integrated. On the way we will see what must hold at the centre, and the boundary conditions fix the constants. We will test the result on our plate and then show what happens when the force acts at one point, where this condition at the centre no longer holds.

Let us draw what we will compute (figure 18.53). On the left is the plate seen from above, a circle of radius \(a\) with the origin at the centre. The radius \(r\) to a point is the length \(r\), the point has the angle \(\theta\) with the horizontal axis, and the radius \(a\) reaches the rim. At the point on the radius \(r\) we place two direction vectors: \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) points outward along the radius and \(\vek e_\theta\) perpendicular to it, along the circle of radius \(r\). With a dashed circle we mark the piece of plate that we will treat: a disc of radius \(r < a\). On the right is the section through the axis, with the thickness greatly exaggerated. The plate is supported at both ends, the load \(q\) acts on top, and the arrows end on the surface because the pressure presses on the plate. The vertical dashed lines show where we cut the disc out. Where we cut it out, the rest of the plate is replaced by what it exerts on the rim of the disc: the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) per unit length of the rim and the bending moment \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) per unit length of the rim. Below the section are the displacement \(w\) and the radius \(r\) of the disc: \(w\) points downward, in the direction of the load. The arrows show how the force and the moment actually act under our load. The shear force carries the disc upward, against the load, and the moment bends it so that the top, loaded surface is compressed and the bottom one stretched. The sign is as in section 18.1: \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is positive in the direction of \(w\), and a moment is positive for compression on the surface toward which \(w\) points, here the bottom one. Hence \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) in the figure are negative, which the computation will confirm.

How do we express all the quantities with the radius? Section 18.2 gave us the plate equation \(D\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12) and the third section the moments, shear forces and boundary conditions in Cartesian coordinates. We need two steps: the operator \(\nabla^2\) in polar coordinates (we computed it in chapter 11, corollary 11.18), and then the moments and the shear force in polar form. Both follow from the next lemma.

Lemma 18.34 An axisymmetric plate (osnosimetrična plošča)

Let \(w(x, y) = F(r)\) with the radius \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), where \(F\) is a four times continuously differentiable function on the interval \(0 < r \le a\). We write \(F' = \mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}r\). Then:

  1. Derivatives along the radius give \(\nabla^2 w = F'' + F'/r = \frac1r\,(r F')'\) and \(\nabla^4 w = \frac1r\,\big(r\,(\nabla^2 w)'\big)'\).
  2. At the point \((r, 0)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F'/r\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). Hence in local axes whose axis \(x\) points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (and axis \(y\) along \(\vek e_\theta\)) the moments per unit length are
    \[M_{\mathrm{r}} = D\Big(F'' + \nu\,\frac{F'}{r}\Big) , \qquad M_\theta = D\Big(\frac{F'}{r} + \nu\,F''\Big) , \qquad M_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0 , \tag{18.39}\]
    and the shear force is \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D\,(\nabla^2 w)'\), \(Q_\theta = 0\).
Proof

We must show the form of the Laplace operator for fields without angular dependence and express the second derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) at a point on the axis \(x\) with derivatives of the function \(F\).

  1. The function \(w\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and \(z\), so in corollary 11.18 the terms with derivatives in \(\theta\) and \(z\) vanish and \(\nabla^2 w = F'' + F'/r\). Since \((rF')' = F' + rF''\), this equals \(\frac1r(rF')'\).
  2. The function \(\psi = \nabla^2 w\) again depends only on \(r\) and is twice continuously differentiable, because \(F\) is four times. Corollary 11.18 applied to \(\psi\) gives \(\nabla^4 w = \nabla^2\psi = \frac1r(r\psi')'\). This is item 1.
  3. With \(\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) we have \(\partial\rho/\partial x = x/\rho\) and \(\partial\rho/\partial y = y/\rho\). The step \(\mathrm{d}x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) namely increases the distance to the centre by its projection on the direction of the radius, \(\frac x\rho\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(x/\rho\) is the cosine of the angle between the radius and the axis \(x\). The chain rule gives \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = F'\,x/\rho\) and
    \[w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\,\frac{x^2}{\rho^2} + F'\Big(\frac1\rho - \frac{x^2}{\rho^3}\Big) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F''\,\frac{y^2}{\rho^2} + F'\Big(\frac1\rho - \frac{y^2}{\rho^3}\Big) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Big(F'' - \frac{F'}{\rho}\Big)\frac{xy}{\rho^2} .\]
  4. At the point \((r, 0)\) we have \(y = 0\), \(x = \rho = r\). Substituting: \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}} = F'/r\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The figure shows why. A step along the axis \(x\) stays on the radius, the distance changes by exactly \(\mathrm{d}x\), so \(w\) changes like \(F\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''\). A step \(\mathrm{d}y\) perpendicular to the radius goes along the tangent to the circle and leaves it only by \(\sqrt{r^2 + (\mathrm{d}y)^2} - r \approx (\mathrm{d}y)^2/(2r)\). Hence \(w \approx F(r) + F'(r)\,(\mathrm{d}y)^2/(2r)\), and the second derivative with respect to \(y\) is \(F'/r\). The steps \(\mathrm{d}y\) and \(-\mathrm{d}y\) give the same distance, so \(w_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) on the whole axis \(x\) and its derivative with respect to \(x\), \(w_{\mathrm{xy}}\), is zero.
  5. The function \(w\) depends only on the distance from the centre, so it does not change when the axes are rotated about the centre. Every point at the distance \(r\) can therefore be treated as the point \((r, 0)\) in rotated axes whose axis \(x\) points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Theorem 18.7 with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{xy}} = w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{yy}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = D(w_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu w_{\mathrm{xx}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D(1-\nu)\,w_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and with step 4 equations (18.39).
  6. By theorem 18.12 the shear force is \(\vek Q = -D\,\nabla(\nabla^2 w)\), that is the gradient of a function of \(r\) alone, so it points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and its component along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(-D\,(\nabla^2 w)'\).

Lemma 18.34 says that the plate equation for a field depending on \(r\) alone is an ordinary equation of fourth order. Before integrating, one more question: why should the solution for the circle really depend on \(r\) alone? The load and the support do not change along the rim, so a rotation about the centre leaves them unchanged. If we find a field \(w(r)\) that solves the equation and the boundary conditions, it is by theorem 18.23 the only solution, so a field that would depend on \(\theta\) as well is excluded. The uniqueness theorem thus spares us the question whether the solution is axisymmetric: it is enough to find it.

Why is the curvature in the circumferential direction exactly \(F'/r\), although \(w\) does not change along the circle at all? The computation of step 3 does not say, figure 18.55 does (the slope in it is greatly exaggerated). The bent middle plane is a surface of revolution: we turn the curve \(w = F(r)\) about the axis of the plate. At the point at radius \(r\) the curve has a tangent with the slope angle \(\varphi\), \(\tan\varphi = F'\), and perpendicular to it the normal. The first curvature is the curvature of this curve in the section: the circle that fits it most closely at the point has its centre \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) on the normal and the radius \(R_{\mathrm{r}}\). For the second curvature we look at the plate from above. All points of the circle of radius \(r\) are tilted towards the axis in the same way, so their normals point towards the axis and meet at one point \(S_\theta\) on it. Along the circle the surface therefore fits a sphere with the centre \(S_\theta\), and its radius is the length of the normal to the axis, \(R_\theta = r/\sin\varphi\). For a small slope \(\sin\varphi \approx \tan\varphi = F'\), so the curvature in the circumferential direction is \(1/R_\theta = F'/r\), and the curvature of the curve in the section is \(1/R_{\mathrm{r}} = F''\), as for a beam (definition 5.16). How small is the slope in our case? The largest slope of our simply supported plate will be \(8{,}4\cdot 10^{-4}\), and the difference between \(\sin\varphi\) and \(\tan\varphi\) is then \(\varphi^2/2 = 3{,}5\cdot 10^{-7}\) of the value. For a deflection comparable with the thickness this approximation would no longer hold, but there the Kirchhoff model itself already fails. The figure says two things that the computation does not show: the plate is curved in the circumferential direction only because it is tilted, and where it is horizontal (\(F' = 0\)) this curvature is absent. At the centre of a full plate every direction is a radial direction, so the two curvatures are equal there and always \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta\).

How do we solve the equation? The first integration will give the equilibrium of the disc of figure 18.53, which at the cut is carried by the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\): the load on the disc is just the integral of the load, and the constant that appears in the integration will be a point force at the centre. The other three integrations give the displacement.

Theorem 18.35 An axisymmetric circular plate (osnosimetrična krožna plošča)

Let a circular plate of radius \(a\) be loaded by a load per unit area \(q(r)\) that is a continuous function on \([0, a]\), and let \(w(r)\) be a displacement, four times continuously differentiable on \(0 < r \le a\), that solves the equation \(D\nabla^4 w = q\) (theorem 18.12). Denote

\[\Phi(r) = \frac1D\int_0^r s\,q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]

Then:

  1. There exist constants \(c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4\) and a function \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) (a particular solution) such that
    \[w(r) = w_{\mathrm{p}}(r) + c_1 + c_2 r^2 + c_3\ln r + c_4\,r^2\ln r . \tag{18.40}\]
    For a uniform load \(q_0\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = q_0 r^4/(64D)\). The shapes of the four terms are shown by four sections.
  2. For every \(r > 0\) we have \(2\pi r\,Q_{\mathrm{r}}(r) + \int_0^r q(s)\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s = -8\pi D\,c_4\). The left side is the sum of the forces in the direction of \(w\) on the disc of radius \(r\), the shear force along the rim and the load, as in equation (18.12) of lemma 18.10, which the same argument gives for the disc. Since the disc is in equilibrium, the right side is the negative of the point force at the centre. If there is no point force at the centre, \(c_4 = 0\), and if a force \(P\) acts in the direction of \(w\), \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\).
  3. If \(w\) is a continuously differentiable function of \(x\) and \(y\) also at the centre (no spike), then \(c_3 = 0\). A plate without a point force at the centre therefore has \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}} + c_1 + c_2 r^2\), two constants that the two boundary conditions at \(r = a\) determine.
Proof

We must show that the equation can be integrated four times and what the constants mean. Denote \(g = \nabla^2 w\).

  1. By lemma 18.34, \(g = \frac1r(rw')'\) and the equation is \(\frac1r(rg')' = q/D\), so \((rg')' = rq/D\).
  2. The function \(s\,q(s)\) is continuous, so \(\Phi\) is differentiable with \(\Phi' = rq/D\) by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55). The difference \(rg' - \Phi\) has derivative zero, so it is a constant \(C_1\): \(rg' = \Phi + C_1\).
  3. We have \(g' = \Phi/r + C_1/r\). Let \(G(r) = \int_0^r \Phi(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\). The load is continuous on \([0, a]\), so bounded, \(|q| \le q_{\max}\), and \(\Phi\) lies between the parabolas \(\pm q_{\max}s^2/(2D)\), so \(\Phi(s) = O(s^2)\) as \(s \to 0\). Therefore \(\Phi/s\) lies in the wedge \(\pm q_{\max}s/(2D)\), which narrows to a point at \(s = 0\): \(\Phi(s)/s \to 0\). With the value \(0\) at \(s = 0\), \(\Phi/s\) is continuous on \([0, a]\) and \(G\) is differentiable with \(G' = \Phi/r\). From \((\ln r)' = 1/r\) it follows that \(g = G + C_1\ln r + C_2\).
  4. Let \(H(r) = \int_0^r sG(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). From \((rw')' = rg\) and \((r^2\ln r/2 - r^2/4)' = r\ln r\) it follows that \(rw' = H + C_1\big(\tfrac12 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2\big) + \tfrac12 C_2 r^2 + C_3\).
  5. We divide by \(r\) and let \(K(r) = \int_0^r H(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \((\tfrac14 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2)' = \tfrac12 r\ln r - \tfrac14 r\), we get \(w = K + C_1\big(\tfrac14 r^2\ln r - \tfrac14 r^2\big) + \tfrac14 C_2 r^2 + C_3\ln r + C_4\). With \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = K\), \(c_1 = C_4\), \(c_2 = (C_2 - C_1)/4\), \(c_3 = C_3\), \(c_4 = C_1/4\) this is equation (18.40). For \(q = q_0\) we have \(\Phi = q_0r^2/(2D)\) and \(\nabla^2(r^4) = 16r^2\), \(\nabla^4(r^4) = 64\) (lemma 18.34), so \(D\nabla^4(q_0r^4/(64D)) = q_0\).
  6. For item 2: by lemma 18.34, \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -Dg'\), so \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} = -2\pi D\,(\Phi + C_1)\). This is the shear force per unit length of rim, added up along the whole rim of the disc. The term \(2\pi D\,\Phi(r) = \int_0^r q(s)\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s\) is the load on the disc, so \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} + \int_0^r q\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s = -2\pi D\,C_1 = -8\pi D\,c_4\). If a force \(P\) also acts on the disc at the centre, the equilibrium of the disc by the same argument as in equation (18.12) is \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} + \int_0^r q\,2\pi s\,\mathrm{d}s + P = 0\), so \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\). Without the force \(c_4 = 0\).
  7. For item 3: in steps 4 and 5 all terms of the derivative \(w'\) except \(c_3/r\) tend to zero as \(r \to 0\): \(K' = H/r = O(r^3)\), \(2c_2r\) and \((r^2\ln r)' = 2r\ln r + r\) are in every band \(\pm\delta\) around zero when \(r\) is smaller than some \(r_\delta\). Since \(w\) is continuously differentiable in \(x\) and \(y\) and \(w(x, 0) = F(|x|)\) is an even function of \(x\), we have \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, 0) = 0\) and hence \(F'(r) = w_{\mathrm{x}}(r, 0) \to 0\). The term \(c_3/r\) with \(c_3 \ne 0\), however, escapes from every band, it is not bounded (on the plate this is a spike). Since \(c_3/r = F' - (\text{other terms}) \to 0\), \(c_3 = 0\).

Source: EP (p. 406, equations (7.2.39) to (7.2.43)) obtains the same general solution by successive integrations and says that symmetry at the centre requires \(c_3 = 0\). VK (PDF p. 105) says that the logarithmic terms in a full plate without a point force give an infinite displacement, moment and shear force, so these constants are zero. The theorem said two things: the plate equation for an \(r\)-dependent load is solved with four constants, and two of them vanish if the plate is whole and has no point force. Each hypothesis enters at its own place, so for each let us say why it holds here, when it does not, and what it costs. The shape that each of the four terms gives the plate is shown in figure 18.62 in subsection 18.6.1.

  • The continuity of the load \(q(r)\) from the centre to the edge is needed in step 2 of the proof, for \(\Phi\) to be differentiable. With our pressure \(q = 10\) kPa over the whole plate it holds. It does not hold if the pressure covers only a part of the plate or if a force acts along a circle: there the single formula (18.40) no longer describes the whole plate. The cost: we cut the plate into fields in which the load is continuous and use the theorem in each of them, with four constants per field instead of two for the whole plate (subsection 18.6.1).
  • Smoothness at the centre gives \(c_3 = 0\). It holds for a full plate, because the centre is a point of the plate like any other, and the term \(c_3\ln r\) would make a spike there, with the slope \(c_3/r\) growing beyond all bounds. It does not hold for a plate with a hole: there is no centre there and the funnel stays.
  • The absence of a point force at the centre gives \(c_4 = 0\). It holds under a pressure: on a circle of radius \(1\) mm about the centre our pressure acts with the force \(q\pi r^2 = 0{,}031\) N, which goes to zero with \(r\). It does not hold if a rod or a bolt pushes on the plate at the centre: then the term that carries a force stays, with \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\). The cost is a moment that grows beyond all bounds towards the centre, which we will compute in lemma 18.39.

Now we can solve the example. We determine \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) from the boundary conditions at \(r = a\) (definition 18.19), first for a clamped edge, where \(w = 0\) and \(\partial w/\partial r = 0\).

Corollary 18.36 A clamped circular plate (vpeta krožna plošča)

A circular plate of radius \(a\) with a clamped edge, \(w(a) = 0\) and \(w'(a) = 0\), loaded by a uniform pressure \(q\), has

\[w = \frac{q\,(a^2 - r^2)^2}{64D} , \qquad w(0) = \frac{q a^4}{64D} , \tag{18.41}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{q}{16}\,\big((3+\nu)\,r^2 - (1+\nu)\,a^2\big) , \qquad M_\theta = \frac{q}{16}\,\big((1+3\nu)\,r^2 - (1+\nu)\,a^2\big) , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{qr}{2} . \tag{18.42}\]

The largest absolute value of the moments is at the edge, \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = qa^2/8\). There \(M_\theta(a) = \nu\,qa^2/8\).

Proof

We must show that the function of theorem 18.35 with two constants satisfies both conditions at the edge.

  1. By item 3 of theorem 18.35, \(w = qr^4/(64D) + c_1 + c_2r^2\), since there is no point force at the centre and the plate is whole.
  2. The derivative is \(w' = qr^3/(16D) + 2c_2r\). The condition \(w'(a) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = -qa^2/(32D)\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -qa^4/(64D) - c_2a^2 = qa^4/(64D)\).
  4. Substituting: \(w = \frac{q}{64D}(r^4 - 2a^2r^2 + a^4) = \frac{q}{64D}(a^2 - r^2)^2\). This is equation (18.41).
  5. Derivatives: \(w' = qr(r^2 - a^2)/(16D)\) and \(w'' = q(3r^2 - a^2)/(16D)\). Equations (18.39) give \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{q}{16}\big((3r^2 - a^2) + \nu(r^2 - a^2)\big)\) and \(M_\theta = \frac{q}{16}\big((r^2 - a^2) + \nu(3r^2 - a^2)\big)\), which is (18.42).
  6. \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D(\nabla^2 w)'\), \(\nabla^2 w = q(2r^2 - a^2)/(8D)\) and \((\nabla^2 w)' = qr/(2D)\).
  7. At the edge \(r = a\) we have \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac q{16}(3 + \nu - 1 - \nu)a^2 = qa^2/8\) and \(M_\theta = \frac q{16}(1 + 3\nu - 1 - \nu)a^2 = \nu qa^2/8\).

Let us look at the numbers for our plate (\(a = 250\) mm, \(q = 10\) kPa, \(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m), computed in outlines/ch-plosce/num-06.py, and draw them as we go into a section through the axis (figure 18.57, top). The flat plate before loading is drawn grey and dashed from edge to edge, with the radius \(a = 250\) mm and the direction of the displacement \(w\) at the side. The edge is clamped, drawn as a hatched wall. The pressure \(q\) pushes on the plate and the plate bends into the orange line. The deflection is drawn a thousand times larger than the radius, otherwise we would not see it. The centre goes down by \(w(0) = qa^4/(64D) = 0{,}03401\) mm, which is \(0{,}34\ \%\) of the thickness. At the edge the plate stays horizontal, \(w' = 0\), because the wall does not let it rotate. For that the wall must twist the edge with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = qa^2/8 = 78{,}13\) N m/m, while at the centre \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -(1+\nu)\,qa^2/16 = -50{,}78\) N m/m. At the edge the orange line bulges upwards, so the top surface is stretched there, in the middle it bulges downwards and the top surface is compressed. Hence the two moments have opposite signs. By corollary 18.8 the stress is \(\sigma_{\max} = 6|M|/h^2\), so \(4{,}69\) MPa at the edge and \(3{,}05\) MPa at the centre. The shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -qr/2\) is \(-1250\) N/m at the edge: the wall carries the plate upwards with \(1250\) N on every metre of rim. The whole rim gives a total of \(2\pi a\cdot 1250 = 1963{,}5\) N, which is exactly the pressure \(q\) times the area \(\pi a^2\), as it must be by the equilibrium of the whole plate. Why is \(M_\theta(a) = \nu M_{\mathrm{r}}(a)\)? Because the edge is clamped, \(w'(a) = 0\), the plate is horizontal there, and the curvature in the circumferential direction \(w'/r\) is zero there (in figure 18.55 the normals of a horizontal plate do not point towards the axis). From equations (18.39) it follows that \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = Dw''\) and \(M_\theta = \nu Dw''\). For a plate in the form of a strip the curvature in one direction is zero for another reason, but the result is the same: \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu M_{\mathrm{x}}\) (section 18.4).

Now a simply supported edge, where \(w = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) (definition 18.19). This edge is circular and therefore smooth: unlike the square there are no corners on which a force \(2M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) would act (corollary 18.22), and because of \(M_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) (lemma 18.34) the effective shear force at the edge is simply \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Corollary 18.37 A simply supported circular plate (prosto podprta krožna plošča)

A circular plate of radius \(a\) with a simply supported edge, \(w(a) = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = 0\), loaded by a uniform pressure \(q\), has

\[w = \frac{q\,(a^2 - r^2)}{64D}\Big(\frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\,a^2 - r^2\Big) , \qquad w(0) = \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\,\frac{qa^4}{64D} , \tag{18.43}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{(3+\nu)\,q}{16}\,(a^2 - r^2) , \qquad M_\theta = -\frac q{16}\big((3+\nu)\,a^2 - (1+3\nu)\,r^2\big) , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{qr}2 . \tag{18.44}\]

The largest absolute value of both moments is at the centre, \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -(3+\nu)\,qa^2/16\). The difference of the displacements of the supported and the clamped plate is the displacement caused by a uniform moment \(M_{\mathrm{a}} = -qa^2/8\) along the edge:

\[w_{\mathrm{podprta}} - w_{\mathrm{vpeta}} = -\frac{M_{\mathrm{a}}\,(a^2 - r^2)}{2D(1+\nu)} , \qquad M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = M_{\mathrm{a}} . \tag{18.45}\]
Proof

We must show that the function of theorem 18.35 with two constants satisfies both conditions and that the difference from the clamped plate is a field with a constant moment.

  1. By item 3 of theorem 18.35, \(w = qr^4/(64D) + c_1 + c_2r^2\), \(w' = qr^3/(16D) + 2c_2r\), \(w'' = 3qr^2/(16D) + 2c_2\).
  2. By equation (18.39) the condition \(M_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = 0\) is \(w''(a) + \nu w'(a)/a = 0\), that is \(\frac{(3+\nu)\,qa^2}{16D} + 2(1+\nu)c_2 = 0\), so \(c_2 = -\frac{(3+\nu)\,qa^2}{32(1+\nu)D}\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -\frac{qa^4}{64D} - c_2a^2 = \frac{qa^4}{64D}\cdot\frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\).
  4. By substitution \(w = \frac{q}{64D}\Big(r^4 - \frac{2(3+\nu)}{1+\nu}a^2r^2 + \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}a^4\Big)\). The product in (18.43) is the same polynomial: the coefficient at \(a^2r^2\) is \(-1 - \frac{5+\nu}{1+\nu} = -\frac{2(3+\nu)}{1+\nu}\).
  5. Moments from equations (18.39): \(w'/r = qr^2/(16D) + 2c_2\), so \(w'' + \nu w'/r = \frac{(3+\nu)\,qr^2}{16D} + 2(1+\nu)\,c_2 = \frac{(3+\nu)\,q}{16D}\,(r^2 - a^2)\) with \(c_2\) from step 2, and \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = D(w'' + \nu w'/r) = -\frac{(3+\nu)q}{16}(a^2 - r^2)\). \(M_\theta\) follows in the same way from \(w'/r + \nu w''\). The shear force is the same as for the clamped plate (\(\nabla^2 w\) differs only by a constant, the derivative does not).
  6. For equation (18.45): the difference is \(w_{\mathrm{podprta}} - w_{\mathrm{vpeta}} = \frac{qa^2(a^2 - r^2)}{16(1+\nu)D}\) (we subtract the polynomials). The field \(c\,(a^2 - r^2)\) has \(w'' = -2c\) and \(w'/r = -2c\), so by equations (18.39) \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -2cD(1+\nu)\). With \(c = -\frac{M_{\mathrm{a}}}{2D(1+\nu)}\) this is \(M_{\mathrm{a}}\), and for \(M_{\mathrm{a}} = -qa^2/8\) we get \(c = \frac{qa^2}{16(1+\nu)D}\).

Source: EP (p. 405, table 7.2.1) lists the conditions for a simply supported edge (\(w = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{rr}} = M_{\mathrm{a}}\)) and VK (PDF pp. 105-106, equations (4.24) to (4.28)) solves the same problem with constants \(C_3\), \(C_4\). The difference as a constant moment (step 6) is not from the source.

The numbers for our plate are in the lower section of figure 18.57. The edge now only rests on a support, which we draw as a triangle: the support carries the edge but does not hinder its rotation. So the plate tilts at the edge, \(w'(a) = -qa^3/(8(1+\nu)D) = -8{,}4\cdot 10^{-4}\), there is no moment at the edge, and the bent plate bulges downwards everywhere. The centre goes down by \(w(0) = 0{,}13864\) mm, which is \(1{,}4\ \%\) of the thickness (where the Kirchhoff assumption of a small deflection \(w \ll h\) still holds well), and at the centre \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = -128{,}91\) N m/m, which is a stress of \(7{,}73\) MPa. The support carries as much as the wall did before, \(1250\) N per metre of rim, since \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the same. The ratio of the deflections \(0{,}13864/0{,}03401 = 4{,}077\) equals \((5+\nu)/(1+\nu)\).

Figure 18.58 shows where the ratio comes from. On the left is the section through the centre from edge to edge, the displacement drawn downward. For the clamped plate the displacement at the centre is \(0{,}0156\) (in units of \(qa^4/D\)), the clamped curve is blue, the supported curve is blackwhite and reaches \(0{,}0637\). Their difference is the dashed orange curve and is exactly the displacement under a constant edge moment \(M_{\mathrm{a}}\) from equation (18.45): \(0{,}0481\) at the centre, \(0{,}0156 + 0{,}0481 = 0{,}0637\). A simply supported plate is thus a clamped plate from which we take the clamping away: the moment \(qa^2/8\) that the clamping exerted at the edge is replaced by zero, that is we add the moment \(-qa^2/8\). On the right this is seen in the moments. The vertical axis carries \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\), so that bending in the direction of the load is upward, and the values are in units of \(qa^2\). The clamped moment goes from \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = -0{,}0813\) at the centre to \(0{,}125\) at the edge, the simply supported one from \(-0{,}2063\) to \(0\), and the two curves are separated by the constant value \(0{,}125 = 1/8\). For \(\nu = 0\) the ratio \((5+\nu)/(1+\nu)\) is exactly \(5\), as for the beam with the ratio \(5qL^4/(384EI)\) to \(qL^4/(384EI)\) (section 18.4).

What does this mean in practice? VK (PDF p. 110) says that the true support lies between the two extremes and that it is sensible to compute both. For us they give: a deflection between \(0{,}034\) and \(0{,}139\) mm, a stress between \(4{,}69\) MPa (clamped, at the edge) and \(7{,}73\) MPa (supported, at the centre). The simply supported plate therefore has a \(4{,}08\) times larger deflection and a \(1{,}65\) times larger stress. Clamping is therefore most valuable for the deformation and less for the stress.

Example 18.38 A circle in a square (krog v kvadratu)

We compare the circular plate \(a = 250\) mm with the square plate \(500\) mm × \(500\) mm of section 18.5, of the same thickness \(h = 10\) mm and the same pressure \(q = 10\) kPa. Both are simply supported. The square has the central deflection \(0{,}1414\) mm, the circle \(0{,}1386\) mm. The ratio is \(0{,}980\): the circle has \(\pi/4 = 78{,}5\ \%\) of the area of the square, but only a \(2\ \%\) smaller deflection. For the clamped edge the deflection of the circle is \(0{,}0340\) mm, that of the square by Ritz's method (chapter 6, the number is from a later section of this chapter) \(0{,}0440\) mm, the ratio \(0{,}772\). Why the square and the inscribed circle have almost the same deflection for the simple support, we cannot show in one step: this is an observation from comparing numbers, not a theorem.

Let us return to the hypothesis on the point force. So far the load was a function \(q(r)\), which means that the force on every piece was smaller than the full force. What if the plate is loaded by a force \(P\) at one point, the centre? A point force is an idealization: in reality it acts on a small circle, and in the model there is nothing on it. Theorem 18.35 (item 2) predicts that the constant \(c_4\) will not be zero. Let us solve.

Lemma 18.39 A point force at the centre of a circular plate (točkovna sila v središču krožne plošče)

Let a clamped circular plate of radius \(a\) have a point force \(P\) in the direction of \(w\) at the centre and no load elsewhere. For \(0 < r \le a\),

\[w = \frac{P}{16\pi D}\Big(a^2 - r^2 + 2r^2\ln\frac ra\Big) , \qquad w(0) = \frac{Pa^2}{16\pi D} , \tag{18.46}\]
\[M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac P{4\pi}\Big(1 - (1+\nu)\ln\frac ar\Big) , \qquad M_\theta = \frac P{4\pi}\Big(\nu - (1+\nu)\ln\frac ar\Big) , \qquad 2\pi r\,Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -P . \tag{18.47}\]

The displacement at the centre is finite, but the absolute values of the moments grow beyond all bounds as \(r \to 0\), like \(\ln(a/r)\).

Proof

We must show which term of the general solution remains and what happens to the derivatives.

  1. A point force \(P\) at the centre: by item 2 of theorem 18.35, \(c_4 = P/(8\pi D)\). The centre is smooth, so by item 3, \(c_3 = 0\). There is no load outside the centre, so \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(w = c_1 + c_2r^2 + c_4r^2\ln r\).
  2. The derivative is \(w' = 2c_2r + c_4(2r\ln r + r)\). The condition \(w'(a) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = -c_4(2\ln a + 1)/2\).
  3. The condition \(w(a) = 0\) gives \(c_1 = -c_2a^2 - c_4a^2\ln a = c_4a^2/2\).
  4. Substituting: \(w = \frac{c_4}2\big(a^2 - r^2 + 2r^2\ln(r/a)\big)\) and \(c_4/2 = P/(16\pi D)\). This is equation (18.46). As \(r \to 0\) we have \(r^2\ln r \to 0\), so \(w(0) = Pa^2/(16\pi D)\).
  5. Derivatives: \(w' = \frac{P}{4\pi D}\,r\ln(r/a)\) and \(w'' = \frac P{4\pi D}\big(\ln(r/a) + 1\big)\). Equations (18.39) give \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{P}{4\pi}\big((1+\nu)\ln(r/a) + 1\big)\) and \(M_\theta = \frac P{4\pi}\big((1+\nu)\ln(r/a) + \nu\big)\), which is (18.47).
  6. \(\ln(a/r) \to \infty\) as \(r \to 0\), so \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(M_\theta\) fall below all bounds, and their absolute values grow without bound. From item 2 of theorem 18.35 it follows that \(2\pi rQ_{\mathrm{r}} = -P\), which is the equilibrium of a disc with the force \(P\).

Source: EP Example 7.2.1(b) (pp. 407-408) gives the same solution for a point force on a clamped plate. RPS (PDF p. 190, equations (5.3.54) and (5.3.55)) solves the same problem for a simply supported edge. The proof with theorem 18.35 is ours.

Numbers for \(P = 1\) kN: \(w(0) = 0{,}06928\) mm, at the edge \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = P/(4\pi) = 79{,}6\) N m/m. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) at a distance \(r\) from the centre is \(-86{,}9\), \(-253{,}4\), \(-491{,}6\) and \(-729{,}8\) N m/m at \(r = 50\), \(10\), \(1\) and \(0{,}1\) mm, which is a stress of \(5{,}22\), \(15{,}21\), \(29{,}50\) and \(43{,}79\) MPa (figure 18.60). The figure has \(r\) on the horizontal axis in a logarithmic scale, and \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\), bending in the direction of the load, on the vertical one. Since \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a linear function of \(\ln(a/r)\), the graph is a straight line, and every tenfold decrease of \(r\) adds \(P(1+\nu)\ln 10/(4\pi) = 238{,}2\) N m/m to \(-M_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the points lie on the same line). At the edge \(-M_{\mathrm{r}} = -79{,}6\) N m/m. The plate thickness \(h\) is at \(r = 10\) mm, and the orange segment shows the distances smaller than it.

May we believe such numbers? Not always. A point force is not real, and the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) is not a model for distances smaller than the thickness either, since there the normals do not stay straight with such a fast change of deflection. The distance at which the model still holds must be larger than \(h\), so \(r > 10\) mm: the stresses \(29{,}50\) MPa at \(r = 1\) mm and \(43{,}79\) MPa at \(r = 0{,}1\) mm, the last two points on the line, which lie in the orange segment, are a consequence of the model and not a prediction for a real steel plate. EP (p. 406) therefore prescribes, after Nadai and Westergaard, that the largest moment under a point force on a small circle be computed with an equivalent radius \(r_{\mathrm{e}}\) that depends on \(h\). The energy stays finite: the energy (definition 18.14) contains \((\nabla^2 w)^2\), so a term of order \(\ln^2 r\), and the integral \(\int_0^a r\ln^2 r\,\mathrm{d}r\) is finite. Hence a point force is, in the energy sense, an admissible load, in spite of the infinite moment at one point.

18.6.1 A plate of several fields

Which circular plates can we solve by now? Those for which one formula holds from the centre to the edge: the full plate under a uniform pressure and the plate with a force at the centre. A manhole cover with a wheel standing on it, a flange with a pipe through its middle, the bottom of a vessel held by a ring at half its radius, and a piston with a thickened middle do not meet this: at some radius something changes. As long as this happens on a circle around the centre, the problem is still axisymmetric, and we will show that one procedure solves all such cases. We cut the plate at these radii into fields, use the general solution (18.40) in every field, and glue the fields together with conditions at the cuts. So everything that is built of rings can be solved: a load that is a piecewise continuous function of the radius, forces and supports along circles, a thickness that changes in steps, and a hole in the middle. This procedure does not solve what is not axisymmetric: a force away from the centre, a support on only a part of the circumference, or a plate that is not a circle.

Let us draw a plate that has all of this at once (figure 18.61). We draw half of the section through the axis, from the axis of the plate on the left to the edge on the right, because the other half is its mirror image. The plate is thicker in the middle, so it has the stiffness \(D_1\) there and \(D_2\) elsewhere. On the thickened middle lies a pressure \(q\). A little further out a ring presses on the plate along a circle with a force \(p\) per unit length of the circle, and further still a support along a circle holds it from below. The outer edge at the radius \(a\) is free, the plate reaches beyond the support. We cut the plate at the radii \(r_1\), \(r_2\) and \(r_3\), where the thickening with the pressure ends, where the ring acts and where the support is. The cuts are only in our minds, the plate stays whole. We get four fields: field 1 is the full circle up to \(r_1\), fields 2, 3 and 4 are rings.

The model stays the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2): a thin plate, a displacement small against the thickness, normals straight and perpendicular to the mid-plane. We add two idealizations. The force along a circle acts exactly on a line, as if an infinitely thin ring carried it, and the support along a circle holds the plate exactly on a line. A real ring has a width. The idealization is good as long as this width is small against the radius of the circle, and it fails where the point force fails: closer than the thickness \(h\) to the line we do not trust the model. It is milder than the point force, though, since we will see that the moments at the line stay finite.

Definition 18.40 Field of a circular plate (polje krožne plošče)

Let a circular plate of radius \(a\) be loaded and supported axisymmetrically. Let \(0 \le r_0 < r_1 < \dots < r_{\mathrm{n}} = a\) be radii chosen so that between two consecutive ones the load per unit area \(q(r)\) is continuous and the bending stiffness \(D\) is constant, and forces and supports along circles are only on the circles \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\). For a full plate \(r_0 = 0\), for a plate with a hole of radius \(b\) it is \(r_0 = b > 0\).

  1. Field \(i\) (polje), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), is the set of points of the plate with \(r_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le r \le r_{\mathrm{i}}\). It is a ring, and for \(r_0 = 0\) field 1 is a full circle.
  2. A ring line force (obročna linijska sila) on the circle \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a load distributed uniformly along this circle. It is given by the number \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\), the force per unit length of the circle in the direction of \(w\), with the unit N/m. Its total force is \(2\pi r_{\mathrm{i}}\,p_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. A ring support (obročna podpora) on the circle \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a support that prescribes \(w = 0\) there and does not hinder the rotation. It acts on the plate with a ring line force that is not given but is an unknown.
  4. An annular plate (obročasta plošča), also a ring or a plate with a hole, is a plate with \(r_0 = b > 0\). Its mid-plane is the ring \(b \le r \le a\) with the inner edge \(r = b\) and the outer edge \(r = a\) (the section in the figure).

Source: VK (PDF pp. 111-112, example 4.2) divides a plate with a line force along the circle \(r = b\) into an inner solid plate and an outer annular plate, VK (PDF pp. 112-113, section 4.3.2) defines the annular plate with the outer radius \(a\) and the inner radius \(b\). The notation for any number of fields is ours.

The word field here means a piece of the plate, like a field of a farm, not a function of position as in the displacement field. In every field the load is continuous and the stiffness constant, so the plate equation can be integrated there as in theorem 18.35, only that every field gets its own four constants. We write them \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(C_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\), with the index of the field:

\[w_{\mathrm{i}}(r) = w_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}}(r) + A_{\mathrm{i}} + B_{\mathrm{i}}\,r^2 + C_{\mathrm{i}}\ln\frac ra + K_{\mathrm{i}}\,r^2\ln\frac ra .\]

We write the logarithm as \(\ln(r/a)\) instead of \(\ln r\), so that its argument has no unit and it is zero at the outer edge. Since \(\ln(r/a) = \ln r - \ln a\), the two forms differ only by a constant and by a multiple of \(r^2\), which go into \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{i}}\). The constants at the logarithms are the same as before: \(C_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(c_3\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(c_4\) of theorem 18.35.

What do these four constants do to the plate? We need the answer to understand why a ring keeps all four. We draw every term alone, as a section through the axis (figure 18.62). The flat mid-plane is grey and dashed, the displacement points down. For every term we compute the moments from equations (18.39) and the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D(\nabla^2 w)'\) (lemma 18.34):

  • The term \(1\) only moves the plate as a rigid body. There is no curvature, so there are neither moments nor a shear force.
  • The term \(r^2\) is a bowl with \(w'' = w'/r = 2\), curved equally in all directions and everywhere. So the moment is the same everywhere, \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = M_\theta = 2D(1+\nu)\), and since \(\nabla^2 r^2 = 4\) is a constant, there is no shear force. This is a plate bent by a uniform moment along its edge.
  • The term \(\ln r\) is a funnel with the slope \(w' = 1/r\), which grows beyond all bounds towards the centre: at the centre there is a spike. Since \(w'' = -1/r^2\), the moments are equal and opposite, \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = -M_\theta = -D(1-\nu)/r^2\), and since \(\nabla^2\ln r = 0\), this term carries no shear force either.
  • The term \(r^2\ln r\) is the only one that carries a force. For it \(\nabla^2 w = 4\ln r + 4\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -4D/r\), so it passes the same force through every circle of radius \(r\), \(2\pi r\,Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -8\pi D\). This force can only come from inside: in the figure it is the force \(8\pi D\) at the centre and its reaction at the edge.

Now the answer is short. In the full plate the last two terms vanished because of the centre: a spike must not be there and there was no force at the centre. A ring does not contain the centre, so it does not have these two reasons. On a ring the funnel is a perfectly tame function, and the term that carries a force is even needed by the ring: through it flows towards the support all the force that acts inside it. The constants \(C_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) therefore stay in every field that does not contain the centre.

The four fields thus have \(16\) constants and we need \(16\) conditions. Where do we take them from? At the outer edge there are two, as before. At the centre there are two, which we have just repeated. The rest must come from the cuts, where two fields meet. We invented the cut ourselves, so the conditions on it only say that the plate on both sides is one and the same plate: that it stays whole and that every piece of it is in equilibrium. Let us look at both in figure 18.63, which has three parts.

The left part shows what a kink would mean. Field \(i\) on the left and field \(i+1\) on the right meet at the radius \(r_{\mathrm{i}}\), the thickness is drawn exaggerated. If the two fields had different slopes there, a gap would open on one surface of the plate, and on the other the material would overlap. The middle part is the section of a thin ring that we cut out around the cut: the ring reaches \(\varepsilon\) to each side of the radius \(r_{\mathrm{i}}\), the neighbouring fields are dashed. On it act the ring line force \(p\), on the outer face the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\) and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\) of field \(i+1\), on the inner face the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\) and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\) of field \(i\). All are drawn in the positive sense: \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is positive in the direction of \(w\) on the face whose normal points along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), so on the opposite face it points against \(w\). The right part is the same ring from above: from it we take the piece between the angles \(-\alpha\) and \(\alpha\), the blue dashed circle is the cut \(r_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the orange line is the tangent to it, about which we will count the moments.

Theorem 18.41 A circular plate of several fields (krožna plošča iz več polj)

Let a Kirchhoff circular plate be divided into the fields \(1, \dots, n\) by definition 18.40. In field \(i\) let the stiffness \(D_{\mathrm{i}}\) be constant and the load \(q_{\mathrm{i}}(r)\) continuous on \([r_{\mathrm{i}-1}, r_{\mathrm{i}}]\), let the thickness change at the cuts symmetrically about the mid-plane, and let no prescribed moment act along the circles. Denote the displacement in field \(i\) by \(w_{\mathrm{i}}(r)\). Let it be four times continuously differentiable on \(r_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le r \le r_{\mathrm{i}}\) (for \(r_0 = 0\) on \(0 < r \le r_1\)) and let it solve \(D_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}}\) there. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\) and the shear force \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\) of field \(i\) are computed from equations (18.39) and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -D\,(\nabla^2 w)'\) with its stiffness \(D_{\mathrm{i}}\) and its displacement \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then:

  1. Constants. In every field
    \[w_{\mathrm{i}}(r) = w_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}}(r) + A_{\mathrm{i}} + B_{\mathrm{i}}\,r^2 + C_{\mathrm{i}}\ln\frac ra + K_{\mathrm{i}}\,r^2\ln\frac ra \tag{18.48}\]
    with the four constants of the field and a particular solution \(w_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}}\), which for a constant load \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) equals \(q_{\mathrm{i}} r^4/(64D_{\mathrm{i}})\). There are \(4n\) constants in all.
  2. Transition conditions (prehodni pogoji). At every cut \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(1 \le i \le n-1\), with a ring line force \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) (without a force \(p_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\)), at \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\)
    \[w_{\mathrm{i}} = w_{\mathrm{i}+1} , \qquad w_{\mathrm{i}}' = w_{\mathrm{i}+1}' , \qquad M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} = M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} - Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} = p_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{18.49}\]
    If there is a ring support at the cut, \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) is unknown. The fourth equation is then replaced by the condition \(w_{\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\), which with the first also gives \(w_{\mathrm{i}+1}(r_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\), and after the solution the fourth equation gives the force of the support \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. Count. The cuts give \(4(n-1)\) conditions (the last row of the figure). The outer edge gives two: clamped \(w = 0\) and \(w' = 0\), simply supported \(w = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), free \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) (with a line force \(p\) along a free outer edge \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = p\)). The centre of a full plate gives two: \(C_1 = 0\) and \(K_1 = P/(8\pi D_1)\), where \(P\) is the point force at the centre (\(P = 0\) if there is none). The inner edge of an annular plate also gives two, of the same kind as the outer one, only that at a free inner edge with a line force \(p\) the condition is \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = -p\). There are \(4(n-1) + 2 + 2 = 4n\) conditions, as many as constants.
Proof

We must show three things: that the integration in a ring gives the same four terms as in the full circle, that the four equations (18.49) hold at a cut, and that the conditions and the constants count up to the same number.

Constants.

  1. Let \(i\) be a field with \(r_{\mathrm{i}-1} > 0\) and \(g = \nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{i}}\). By lemma 18.34 the plate equation is \((r g')' = r q_{\mathrm{i}}/D_{\mathrm{i}}\), as in step 1 of the proof of theorem 18.35.
  2. We repeat steps 2 to 5 of that proof, only that all integrals start at \(r_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) instead of at \(0\): \(\Phi(r) = \frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{i}}}\int_{\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^r s\,q_{\mathrm{i}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), \(G(r) = \int_{\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^r \Phi(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\), \(H(r) = \int_{\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^r s\,G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) and \(K(r) = \int_{\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^r H(s)/s\,\mathrm{d}s\). Since \(s \ge r_{\mathrm{i}-1} > 0\), all integrands are continuous and the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55) holds in every step. We get \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = K + c_1 + c_2r^2 + c_3\ln r + c_4r^2\ln r\), the sum of a particular solution and the four terms of the figure.
  3. We insert \(\ln r = \ln(r/a) + \ln a\): \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = K + (c_1 + c_3\ln a) + (c_2 + c_4\ln a)\,r^2 + c_3\ln(r/a) + c_4\,r^2\ln(r/a)\). This is equation (18.48) with \(w_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}} = K\), \(A_{\mathrm{i}} = c_1 + c_3\ln a\), \(B_{\mathrm{i}} = c_2 + c_4\ln a\), \(C_{\mathrm{i}} = c_3\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}} = c_4\).
  4. Let \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) be constant. The function \(q_{\mathrm{i}}r^4/(64D_{\mathrm{i}})\) solves \(D_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla^4 w = q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (step 5 of the proof of theorem 18.35), so the difference \(w_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}}r^4/(64D_{\mathrm{i}})\) solves the equation with zero load. For it steps 2 and 3 with \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), so with \(K = 0\), give a sum of the four terms. Hence we may take \(w_{\mathrm{p},\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}}r^4/(64D_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  5. For field 1 of a full plate (\(r_0 = 0\)) item 1 of theorem 18.35 gives the same form, and step 3 rewrites it with \(\ln(r/a)\). There are \(n\) fields, with four constants in each, \(4n\) in all.

Transition conditions.

  1. Displacement. The point of the mid-plane at the cut belongs to both fields, and the plate is whole there. If \(w_{\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}}) \ne w_{\mathrm{i}+1}(r_{\mathrm{i}})\), the same point would move by two different lengths, the plate would be sheared apart. So \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = w_{\mathrm{i}+1}\).
  2. Slope. By theorem 18.3 (equation (18.2)) a point at the height \(z\) from the mid-plane moves in the radial direction by \(-z\,w'\), because the normal stays perpendicular to the mid-plane. On the surface \(z = -h/2\) the point at the cut moves by \(\frac h2 w_{\mathrm{i}}'\) as a part of field \(i\), and by \(\frac h2 w_{\mathrm{i}+1}'\) as a part of field \(i+1\). The difference \(\frac h2\,(w_{\mathrm{i}+1}' - w_{\mathrm{i}}')\) is the width of the gap, and on the opposite surface \(z = h/2\) the material overlaps by the same length. A whole plate has neither a gap nor an overlap, so \(w_{\mathrm{i}}' = w_{\mathrm{i}+1}'\). The thickness changes symmetrically, so the mid-plane of both fields is the same and we measure \(z\) from the same plane on both sides.
  3. Force. The piece of the ring between the angles \(-\alpha\) and \(\alpha\), \(0 < \alpha < \pi/2\), is in equilibrium. The forces on it in the direction of \(w\) are: \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\) on the outer face, an arc of length \(2\alpha\,(r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\), \(-Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\) on the inner one, an arc of length \(2\alpha\,(r_{\mathrm{i}} - \varepsilon)\), the line force \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) on an arc of length \(2\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the load \(q\) on the area of the piece \(4\alpha\,\varepsilon\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\). On the straight side faces of the piece \(Q_\theta = 0\) (lemma 18.34). Let \(q_{\max}\) be the largest absolute value of the load in the two fields. The balance of forces, the same argument as in equation (18.12) for a rectangle, gives
    \[2\alpha\,\Big( (r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\,Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}(r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon) - (r_{\mathrm{i}} - \varepsilon)\,Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}} - \varepsilon) + r_{\mathrm{i}}\,p_{\mathrm{i}} \Big) + R = 0 , \qquad |R| \le 4\alpha\,\varepsilon\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\max} .\]
  1. We make the ring thinner, \(\varepsilon \to 0\). The term \(R\) lies in the wedge \(\pm 4\alpha\,\varepsilon\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\max}\), which narrows to a point at \(\varepsilon = 0\), so it goes to zero, and with it the sum of the forces in the bracket, which equals \(-R\). The shear forces are continuous in their fields up to the cut, so this sum has the limit \(2\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\,\big(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} - Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} + p_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\) at \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\), and this limit is the only point of the wedge at \(\varepsilon = 0\), zero. Hence \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} - Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} = p_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. Moment. The arc has its centre at \(O\). We count the moments on the same piece about the tangent to the circle \(r = r_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the angle \(0\) (on the right picture, the axis). The moment \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\) on the outer face turns, at the angle \(\theta\), about the tangent at this angle, which makes the angle \(\theta\) with our axis, so it contributes the share \(\cos\theta\) to the sum. A piece of the arc of length \((r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\) is mapped on the axis to the length \((r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\), and the pieces add up on the axis to the chord. Along the arc from \(-\alpha\) to \(\alpha\) we have \(\int_{-\alpha}^{\alpha}\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\sin\alpha\), so the outer face gives \(2\sin\alpha\,(r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\,M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}(r_{\mathrm{i}} + \varepsilon)\) and the inner one, with the opposite sense, \(-2\sin\alpha\,(r_{\mathrm{i}} - \varepsilon)\,M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}} - \varepsilon)\). There is no prescribed moment along the circle, \(M_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) (lemma 18.34).
  3. The other contributions go to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\), each in its own narrowing wedge, like \(R\) in step 9. The moments \(M_\theta\) act on the two straight side faces of the piece, which are \(2\varepsilon\) long. The load \(q\) acts on the area \(4\alpha\,\varepsilon\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\). The three forces of step 8 act at the same angle \(\theta\) at radii that differ by at most \(\varepsilon\), so their lever arms to the axis differ by at most \(\varepsilon\), and their sum goes to zero by step 9. Hence their total moment goes to zero as well.
  4. The balance of moments about the axis, the same argument as in equation (18.14), gives for \(\varepsilon \to 0\) the equation \(2\sin\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{i}}\,\big(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} - M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}}\big) = 0\). Since \(\sin\alpha \ne 0\), we have \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} = M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\). With steps 6, 7 and 9 these are equations (18.49).
  5. Ring support. The support prescribes \(w_{\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) (definition 18.40). Steps 6 to 12 still hold, only that \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) in step 9 is not given. The equation \(Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} - Q_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1} = p_{\mathrm{i}}\) therefore does not bind the constants but determines \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) once the constants are known. In its place stands the condition \(w_{\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\), and the number of conditions at the cut stays four (the third cut in the figure).

The proof has shown that the theorem has two kinds of conditions. The first two, displacement and slope, are geometry: the plate stays whole, without a gap or an overlap. The other two are the equilibrium of the thin ring: what enters it on one side must leave on the other, except for what the line force presses on it. That is why \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) jumps at a line force by exactly \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\), while the moment does not jump, because a thin ring has no lever arm on which a force would make a moment.

The hypotheses of the theorem each enter at their own place:

  • Constant stiffness in a field. We need it in step 1, so that the equation is \(D_{\mathrm{i}}\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}}\). It holds for a stepped thickness. For a thickness that changes continuously it holds in no field, however narrow: then the plate can only be approximated by steps, and the error shrinks with the number of fields.
  • A symmetric change of thickness. We need it in step 7, so that there is one mid-plane, as in the figure, where the plate thickens equally upward and downward. With a step on only one side of the plate the mid-planes of the fields do not match, bending couples with stretching in the plane and our conditions do not suffice.
  • No prescribed moment along a circle. We need it in step 10. If a moment \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) per unit length acts on the circle, for example from a welded pipe, it enters the balance of the piece next to the moments of the two fields and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) jumps at the cut by \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\), just as \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) jumps by \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  • The moment is continuous, not the curvature. The condition is \(M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}} = M_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{i}+1}\), not \(w_{\mathrm{i}}'' = w_{\mathrm{i}+1}''\). Since \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = D\,(w'' + \nu w'/r)\) and \(w'\) is continuous, \(w''\) is continuous exactly when \(D_{\mathrm{i}} = D_{\mathrm{i}+1}\). At the step of thickness at \(r_1\) \(w''\) breaks: the thicker field curves less under the same moment. For a field twice as thick the stiffness is \(2^3 = 8\) times larger and \(w'' + \nu w'/r\) is eight times smaller on the thicker side.

An equal number of conditions and constants does not yet say that the system can be solved. It can be solved, and uniquely, when the plate is supported somewhere, by theorem 18.23. A plate that is supported nowhere has all edges free and no ring support: then the constant \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\), the rigid shift, appears in no condition, and the load must be in equilibrium by itself.

Let us return to the four fields and read the lower part of figure 18.61. At the centre \(C_1 = 0\) and \(K_1 = 0\), because the plate is whole there and without a point force. At the first cut, where the pressure and the thickening end, there is neither a line force nor a support, so all four quantities are continuous: \(w\), \(w'\), \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\). At the second cut the ring acts, so \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) jumps by \(p\) and the other three are continuous. At the third cut there is the ring support: the displacement is zero from both sides, \(w'\) and \(M_{\mathrm{r}}\) are continuous, and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is free and tells after the solution with what force the support carries the plate. At the free outer edge \(M_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). The count works out: \(2 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 16\) conditions for \(4 \cdot 4 = 16\) constants. This is a system of sixteen linear equations, which a computer solves. With one or two fields we solve it by hand, and this is what we do now.

We test the solution on two results that we already have.

  1. Pressure over the whole plate, \(b = a\): we move the cut to the edge. Field 2 disappears and field 1 must be the simply supported plate of corollary 18.37. For \(b = a\) we have \(\lambda = 0\), and the formula for \(w_1(0)\) gives \(\dfrac{qa^4}{64D\,(1+\nu)}\,(12 + 4\nu - 7 - 3\nu) = \dfrac{5+\nu}{1+\nu}\,\dfrac{qa^4}{64D}\), the value of equation (18.43). The formula for \(M_0\) gives \(-\dfrac{qa^2}{4}\cdot\dfrac{3+\nu}{4} = -\dfrac{(3+\nu)\,qa^2}{16}\), as in the corollary. SymPy confirms that for \(b = a\) the whole \(w_1(r)\) agrees with equation (18.43).
  2. We shrink the circle of the load at the same total force \(P\), so \(q = P/(\pi b^2)\) grows. Field 2 can be written as
    \[w_2 = \frac{P}{16\pi D}\Big(\frac{3+\nu}{1+\nu}\,(a^2 - r^2) + 2r^2\ln\frac ra\Big) - \frac{P\,b^2}{32\pi D}\Big(2\ln\frac ar + \frac{1-\nu}{1+\nu}\,\frac{a^2 - r^2}{a^2}\Big) .\]
    The second term has the factor \(b^2\) and goes to zero as \(b \to 0\). What remains is the first term, which is the simply supported plate with a point force \(P\) at the centre. It differs from the clamped plate of lemma 18.39 (equation (18.46)) by \(\dfrac{P\,(a^2 - r^2)}{8\pi D\,(1+\nu)}\). This is the bowl with the uniform moment \(-P/(4\pi)\), which cancels the edge moment \(P/(4\pi)\) of the clamped plate of the lemma, just as in equation (18.45) for the pressure. If we solve the same example with a clamped edge (condition 8 is replaced by \(w_2'(a) = 0\)), the limit \(b \to 0\) gives exactly equation (18.46), and the deflection at the centre is, for every \(b\), equal to \(\dfrac{qb^2}{16D}\Big(a^2 - \dfrac34 b^2 + b^2\ln\dfrac ba\Big)\), which is the result VK (4.43).

We have seen how the circle is a special shape that demands only an ordinary equation. The clamped and the simply supported plate are two extremes of the true support, the difference between them is one edge moment, and the point force is a case where the hypothesis of the theorem does not hold and the logarithm remains. For the square (section 18.5) we therefore needed a series, for the circle only four integrals. A plate that is not the same from the centre to the edge we cut into fields: every field has its own four constants, the transition conditions bind them at the cuts (theorem 18.41), and the annular plate with a hole is the case of a single field, for which EP has a table of boundary conditions (EP, p. 405).
