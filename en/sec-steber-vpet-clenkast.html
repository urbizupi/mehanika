Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.11 Column with a fixed and a pinned end

Let us return to the column of section 8.14.3, which is clamped at the bottom, at \(x = 0\), and has at the top, at \(x = L\), a pin support that slides along the axis and that the compressive force \(P\) pushes towards the clamp. The model is the same as there: the axis is inextensible, the column is perfectly straight up to buckling and has a constant bending stiffness \(EI > 0\), the force acts exactly along the axis and keeps its direction, the deflections from the straight line are small. Section 8.14 listed these idealizations with bounds on their errors. For the steel strip of chapter 5 with \(L = 2\) m the axis, under the force \(26{,}383\) kN that we shall obtain below as the threshold, actually shortens by the ratio \(P/(EA) = 1{,}68\cdot 10^{-4}\), which by the estimate of section 8.14 shifts the threshold by the order of twice this ratio, about \(3\cdot 10^{-4}\). We describe the deflection of the axis from the straight line by the function \(\eta(x)\). The clamp prescribes \(\eta(0) = 0\) and \(\eta'(0) = 0\), the pin prescribes \(\eta(L) = 0\) and leaves the rotation there free. The energy of the column is quadratic, and its second variation (definition 5.72) at the straight position is the expression (8.156), \(\int_0^L (EI\,\eta''^2 - P\,\eta'^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\), only with other boundary conditions. The first term is twice the energy that bending into the shape \(\eta\) stores in the column. The second is twice the work of the force \(P\), because the top comes down when the column bends: the bent axis is as long as the straight one, but its vertical extent is shorter, by about \(\tfrac12\int_0^L\eta'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (lemma 8.149, the drop of the top of the cantilever). The straight position is stable as long as the difference is positive for every admissible \(\eta \ne 0\). Then every small bending stores more energy than the work of the force \(P\) as the top comes down, so the column does not bend by itself.

What do we already know about this column? Section 8.14.3 computed with a determinant that the equilibrium equation \(EI\,v'''' + P\,v'' = 0\) with these boundary conditions first has a curved solution at \(\tan kL = kL\) with \(k^2 = P/EI\), that is at \(kL = 4{,}4934\) and \(P = 20{,}19\,EI/L^2\). It showed that above this threshold the second variation is not positive for some \(\eta\). Below the threshold, however, it proved positivity only for \(P < \pi^2 EI/L^2\), which is \(0{,}489\) of the threshold, and in between positivity was supported only by computation. For the same reason theorem 12.53 in chapter 12 gave for this column only the bound \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge \pi^2 EI/L^2\), for the strip with \(L = 2\) m thus \(12{,}896\) kN instead of \(26{,}383\) kN. This section closes the gap: we shall prove that the second variation is positive for every \(P\) below the threshold.

Why are the two routes of section 8.14 not enough here? The split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts (theorem 8.151) needs a column that is the same when turned upside down, and this one has different ends. Division by the sine (lemma 8.143) divides by the shape at which the quotient is smallest, and this shape must not vanish in the interior, since there we would divide by zero. For this column the role of that shape is played by the slope of the buckling shape, and the slope changes sign in the interior. So we take another route, in two steps: we write the second variation with the slope of the axis, and we expand the slope in the modes of a bar.

Let \(w = \eta'\), the slope of the axis. The second variation is then

\[\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta) = EI\int_0^L w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x - P\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{13.99}\]

The boundary conditions carry over to the slope. The clamp gives \(w(0) = \eta'(0) = 0\). The pin gives, by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55), \(\int_0^L w\,\mathrm{d}x = \eta(L) - \eta(0) = 0\). The last condition says that the top returns to the straight line: as far as the axis moves away from the line on one part, it must come back on another, so the blue area, where the slope is positive, equals the grey one, where it is negative. Without this condition the expression (13.99) with \(w(0) = 0\) would be exactly the second variation of the cantilever column of section 8.14.2, with the slope \(w\) in the role of the angle \(\theta\), and the threshold would be \(\pi^2 EI/(4L^2) = 2{,}467\,EI/L^2\). The pin at the top thus adds a single condition to the cantilever, but this condition raises the threshold \(8{,}18\) times, to \(20{,}19\,EI/L^2\).

The lower end, hatched at \(x = 0\), is clamped, so the axis cannot have a slope there. The upper end is a pin on a slider, which holds the top on the line and does not hold the rotation. The arrow points down, because the column feels the force \(P\) as a push towards the clamp. The blackwhite curve is the buckling shape \(\eta\), strongly enlarged so that it can be seen. On the right the vertical axis is the same height \(x\) as on the left and the horizontal axis is the slope \(w\). The blackwhite curve is \(w = \eta'\). For the cantilever the deciding function was the sine \(\sin(\pi x/2L)\), which has the smallest quotient \(\int w'^2/\int w^2 = (\pi/2L)^2\) among functions with \(w(0) = 0\) (lemma 8.143, case (b)). It is the first mode of a bar that is fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) (theorem 13.24). The modes of this bar are the sines \(\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\) with \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} = (2j - 1)\pi/(2L)\), which have a quarter of a wave over the length \(L\), three quarters, five quarters and so on, so for short we call them quarter sines (četrtinski sinusi). For the column with the pin a single sine is not enough, because the slope must also satisfy the condition \(\int w = 0\), so we expand \(w\) in all of them. That the series in these sines converges uniformly is item 2 of theorem 13.72. We also need the integral of the square of the function, the integral of the function itself and an estimate of the integral of the square of the derivative, so the next lemma collects everything we shall use and proves what the theorem does not say.

The figure shows the functions that the lemma calls \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), without the constant factor. On the left is the horizontal axis from \(0\) to \(L\). The blackwhite curve is \(\sin\beta_1 x\), a quarter of a wave. The blue one is \(\sin\beta_2 x\), three quarters of a wave, and the grey one is \(\sin\beta_3 x\), five quarters. The dot at \(x = 0\) shows that all three are zero there. On the right the axis is the same. The blackwhite curve is \(\cos\beta_1 x\), the blue one is \(\cos\beta_2 x\) and the grey one is \(\cos\beta_3 x\). The dot at \(x = L\) shows that all three cosines are zero there, because each has an odd multiple of a quarter wave at \(L\).

What it means that two sines are orthogonal we see on the product. On the left is the horizontal axis from \(0\) to \(L\). The blackwhite curve is the product of the first and the second sine. Above the axis it is positive, up to \(x = 2L/3\), and below the axis it is negative. The two areas are equal, so the integral of the product is zero. On the right the axis is the same. The square of one sine is nowhere negative, so the area under it is positive. With the factor \(2/L\) it equals \(1\).

The left graph shows what uniform convergence means. The horizontal and vertical axes are \(x\) and \(w\). The blackwhite curve is the function \(w\), which is zero at \(x = 0\). The blue curve is its partial sum \(S_{\mathrm{N}} w\), here with a single term so that the difference can be seen. The grey band has the width \(\varepsilon\) on each side of \(w\). Uniform convergence means that for large enough \(N\) the whole blue curve lies in this band, not only at one point.

Lemma 13.93 Expansion in quarter sines (razvoj po četrtinskih sinusih)

Let \(L > 0\) and for \(j = 1, 2, \dots\) let \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} = (2j - 1)\pi/(2L)\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) (the square of the number \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\), an eigenvalue of the equation \(-\varphi'' = \mu\varphi\) for the quarter sines, not the mass per length of section 13.1), with the shapes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) of figure 13.158,

\[\begin{aligned} \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(x) &= \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x , \qquad \psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\cos\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x , \\ s_{\mathrm{j}} &= \int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x . \end{aligned} \tag{13.100}\]
  1. For all \(i\) and \(j\) the integrals \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\int_0^L\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) equal \(1\) for \(i = j\) and \(0\) for \(i \ne j\). Further \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0\), \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L) = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}' = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}' = -\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2/L}\,/\beta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\).
  2. Let \(w\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) (definition 6.11) with \(w(0) = 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L w\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Then the partial sums \(S_{\mathrm{N}} w = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N c_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) converge uniformly to \(w\) on \([0, L]\), all three series in
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x &= \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^\infty c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 , \\ \int_0^L w\,\mathrm{d}x &= \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^\infty c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} , \\ \int_0^L w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x &\ge \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^\infty \mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \end{aligned} \tag{13.101}\]
    converge, and both equalities and the inequality hold.
Proof

We must show item 1 with the addition formulas, then the uniform convergence from the expansion in the modes of the bar, both equalities from uniform convergence, and the inequality from integration by parts and the nonnegativity of a square.

Item 1.

  1. By the addition formula (corollary 8.16) with \(\sin\pi = 0\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\) (lemma 8.11, item 2), \(\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta\) and \(\cos(\theta + \pi) = -\cos\theta\) for every \(\theta\). From \(\sin 0 = 0\), \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\) it follows that \(\sin m\pi = 0\) and \(\cos m\pi = (-1)^m\) for every integer \(m \ge 0\), and \(\cos\beta_{\mathrm{j}} L = \cos\big((j - 1)\pi + \pi/2\big) = 0\). For an integer \(m < 0\), \(\sin m\pi = -\sin(-m\pi) = 0\) because the sine is odd (item 4 of the lemma).
  2. From the addition formulas it follows, as before lemma 9.109, that \(\sin a\sin b = \tfrac12\big(\cos(a - b) - \cos(a + b)\big)\) and \(\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12\big(\cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)\big)\). For \(a = \beta_{\mathrm{i}} x\) and \(b = \beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\) we have \(a - b = (i - j)\pi x/L\) and \(a + b = (i + j - 1)\pi x/L\).
  3. For an integer \(m \ne 0\) the function \(x \mapsto L\sin(m\pi x/L)/(m\pi)\) is an antiderivative of \(\cos(m\pi x/L)\) (theorem 8.13), so by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 1) \(\int_0^L\cos(m\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = L\sin(m\pi)/(m\pi) = 0\) by step 1.
  4. For \(i \ne j\) both \(i - j\) and \(i + j - 1\) are nonzero integers, so by step 3 the integrals of both products of step 2 are zero. For \(i = j\) we have \(a - b = 0\) and \(\cos 0 = 1\), \(i + j - 1 = 2j - 1 \ne 0\), so \(\int_0^L\sin^2\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L\cos^2\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\,\mathrm{d}x = L/2\). With the factor \(2/L\) we get \(1\).
  5. We have \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin 0 = 0\) and by step 1 \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\cos\beta_{\mathrm{j}} L = 0\). By theorem 8.13, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}' = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}' = -\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The function \(-\cos(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x)/\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) is an antiderivative of \(\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\), so by the fundamental theorem \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2/L}\,(1 - \cos\beta_{\mathrm{j}} L)/\beta_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2/L}\,/\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Item 2.

  1. By theorem 13.72, item 2, with \(v = w\), the partial sums \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{J}} d_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\) with \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L w\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x\,\mathrm{d}x\) converge uniformly to \(w\) on \([0, L]\). Since \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2/L}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\), we have \(d_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\beta_{\mathrm{j}} x = c_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(x)\), and these partial sums are \(S_{\mathrm{J}} w\). For \(\varepsilon > 0\) let \(N_0\) be the index of definition 9.106, so \(|w(x) - S_{\mathrm{N}} w(x)| < \varepsilon\) for all \(N \ge N_0\) and all \(x \in [0, L]\).
  2. As in the proof of lemma 13.12: by linearity \(\int_0^L w\,S_{\mathrm{N}} w\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\int_0^L w\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). Let \(M\) be the largest value of \(|w|\) on \([0, L]\), which exists because \(w\) is continuous on a closed interval (section 1.13.1). For \(N \ge N_0\), by lemma 9.36 and lemma 3.54, item 3, \(\big|\int_0^L w^2 - \int_0^L w\,S_{\mathrm{N}} w\big| \le \int_0^L |w|\,|w - S_{\mathrm{N}} w| \le L\,M\,\varepsilon\), because the difference is everywhere inside the band of width \(\varepsilon\). The partial sums of the series \(\sum c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) therefore converge to \(\int_0^L w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. By linearity \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L S_{\mathrm{N}} w\,\mathrm{d}x\). For \(N \ge N_0\) of step 6, by lemma 9.36 and lemma 3.54, item 3, \(\big|\int_0^L w\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L S_{\mathrm{N}} w\,\mathrm{d}x\big| \le \int_0^L |w - S_{\mathrm{N}} w|\,\mathrm{d}x \le L\,\varepsilon\), again because the difference is inside the band. The partial sums of the series \(\sum c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\) therefore converge to \(\int_0^L w\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. Let \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L w'\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). The function \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuously differentiable, so it is piecewise continuously differentiable on the partition of \(w\). Lemma 6.13 with the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(w\) and item 1 give \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L)\,w(L) - \psi_{\mathrm{j}}(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^L \psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \beta_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\), because \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L) = 0\) and \(w(0) = 0\).
  5. For every \(N\) the function \(\big(w' - \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N a_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\big)^2\) is piecewise continuous and nonnegative, so it has a nonnegative integral (lemma 6.10, item 3). We expand the square and use linearity (item 2 of the same lemma) and item 1: \(0 \le \int_0^L w'^2 - 2\sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\int_0^L w'\psi_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\int_0^L\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L w'^2 - \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}}a_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). This is Bessel's inequality as in theorem 9.114, here for the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  6. By step 9, \(a_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), so the partial sums \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{N}}\mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) increase and by step 10 are bounded by \(\int_0^L w'^2\). By theorem 1.52 they converge, and by lemma 1.49 the limit is at most \(\int_0^L w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The uniform convergence is item 2 of theorem 13.72, the equality for \(\int w^2\) is, for the mass \(\rho A = 1\), also case (b) of lemma 13.80.

The lemma showed that the slope \(w\) is written with the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) so that the integral of the square is kept, while the integral of the slope is a linear expression in the coefficients. For the derivative we have only an inequality, because we know Parseval's equality only for uniformly convergent series (lemma 13.12). The series in the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) of a continuous \(w'\) with \(w'(L) \ne 0\) does not converge uniformly to \(w'\), since all \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) vanish at \(x = L\) and so would a uniform limit. For the proof of positivity the inequality is enough, because we only need the second variation to be at least as large as the sum with the coefficients.

We see the inequality for the derivative on the left half of the figure. We picture functions as arrows. The horizontal line stands for the functions \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{N}}\) and everything built from them. The blackwhite arrow is \(w'\). The blue arrow is its perpendicular shadow on this line, \(\sum a_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The orange dashed segment is the remainder \(w' - \sum a_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The right angle is there because we chose \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) so that we subtract from \(w'\) exactly what lies along the \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By Pythagoras the blue arrow is therefore shorter than the blackwhite one, which is \(\sum a_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \int w'^2\).

The lemma requires \(w(0) = 0\). Let us see what happens without it, on the function \(w = 1\) (\(L = 1\), the right graph of figure 13.160), which is continuous and piecewise differentiable, but \(w(0) = 1\). The coefficients are \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^1\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2}/\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\). The first two relations hold: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} 8/\big((2j - 1)^2\pi^2\big) = 1 = \int w^2\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} 2/\beta_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 1 = \int w\), because \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} 1/(2j - 1)^2 = \pi^2/8\) (example 9.120). The third fails. The derivative is \(w' = 0\), so \(\int w'^2 = 0\), whereas \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \cdot 2/\beta_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 2\) for every \(j\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \infty\). The inequality \(0 \ge \infty\) does not hold. The cause is step 9 of the proof. There the boundary term \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(0)\,w(0)\) vanishes only because \(w(0) = 0\). Here \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \int w'\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\), whereas \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} = \sqrt{2}\), and the difference \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(0)\,w(0) = \sqrt{2}\) is exactly the boundary term. There is no uniform convergence either: every partial sum \(S_{\mathrm{N}} w\) has the value \(0\) at \(x = 0\) and the function has \(1\), so \(\max|w - S_{\mathrm{N}} w| \ge 1\) for every \(N\). The condition \(w(0) = 0\) means \(\eta'(0) = 0\), that is the clamp of the column at \(x = 0\), and without it the second variation would not be bounded below by the sum \(\sum(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\).

Let us first show what the expansion says on the first two quarter sines, \(w = c_1\varphi_1 + c_2\varphi_2\). Then \(w' = c_1\beta_1\psi_1 + c_2\beta_2\psi_2\), and by item 1 of lemma 13.93, \(\int w^2 = c_1^2 + c_2^2\) and \(\int w'^2 = \mu_1 c_1^2 + \mu_2 c_2^2\). With \(\lambda = P/EI\) therefore

\[\frac{\delta^2\Pi}{EI} = (\mu_1 - \lambda)\,c_1^2 + (\mu_2 - \lambda)\,c_2^2 , \qquad \int_0^L w\,\mathrm{d}x = s_1 c_1 + s_2 c_2 = 0 .\]

For \(\lambda \le \mu_1\) both coefficients are nonnegative and the second is positive, while the condition does not allow \(c_2 = 0\) with \(c_1 \ne 0\), so the second variation is positive. The interesting band \(\mu_1 < \lambda < \mu_2\), where the first coefficient is negative. From the condition \(c_2 = -s_1 c_1/s_2\), and when we insert this,

\[\frac{\delta^2\Pi}{EI} = \frac{c_1^2}{s_2^2}\Big((\mu_1 - \lambda)\,s_2^2 + (\mu_2 - \lambda)\,s_1^2\Big) = \frac{c_1^2}{s_2^2}\,(\mu_1 - \lambda)(\mu_2 - \lambda)\Big(\frac{s_1^2}{\mu_1 - \lambda} + \frac{s_2^2}{\mu_2 - \lambda}\Big) .\]

The product \((\mu_1 - \lambda)(\mu_2 - \lambda)\) is negative in the band, so the second variation is positive for every \(c_1 \ne 0\) exactly when the sum of fractions in the last bracket is negative. By item 1 of the lemma, \(s_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 2/(L\mu_{\mathrm{j}})\). In dimensionless numbers \(\mu_1 L^2 = \pi^2/4 = 2{,}467\), \(\mu_2 L^2 = 9\pi^2/4 = 22{,}207\), \(s_1^2/L = 8/\pi^2 = 0{,}811\) and \(s_2^2/L = 8/(9\pi^2) = 0{,}0901\). The sum of fractions is zero at

\[\lambda = \frac{s_1^2\mu_2 + s_2^2\mu_1}{s_1^2 + s_2^2} = \frac{2{,}05\,\pi^2}{L^2} = \frac{20{,}233}{L^2} ,\]

only \(0{,}21\) % above \(20{,}19/L^2\). Two sines thus almost give the threshold already. With more sines the same computation gives (NumPy)

sines \(\lambda L^2\) above the threshold \(20{,}1907\)
2 \(20{,}233\) \(0{,}21\) %
3 \(20{,}202\) \(0{,}055\) %
5 \(20{,}193\) \(0{,}011\) %
10 \(20{,}1910\) \(0{,}0014\) %
100 \(20{,}1907\) \(1{,}4 \cdot 10^{-6}\) %

The values are always above the threshold and ever closer. They are above the threshold because every shape from a smaller family is also a shape of the column: where the second variation is positive for all shapes of the column, it is positive for the shapes of the family, so the family cannot give a lower threshold.

The computation differs in three ranges of the force, so we draw them on an axis. The horizontal axis is \(\lambda L^2\), the dimensionless force. The first marked value is \(\mu_1L^2 = 2{,}467\) and the third is \(\mu_2L^2 = 22{,}207\). Between them is the threshold \(\lambda_1L^2 = 20{,}19\) of section 8.14.3. The dark blue range is \(\lambda \le \mu_1\), where all coefficients are nonnegative. The light blue range is \(\mu_1 < \lambda < \lambda_1\), where only the first one is negative. The grey range is \(\lambda \ge \lambda_1\), where the second variation is no longer positive, which we shall prove. The dashed mark \(\pi^2 = 9{,}870\) is the bound up to which section 8.14.3 proved positivity, and the blue brace is the gap between it and the threshold, which this section closes.

With all sines the lemma gives a second variation of at least \(EI\sum_{\mathrm{j}}(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\,c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), with the condition at the top \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). Here we cannot express \(c_2\) through \(c_1\), because the condition ties infinitely many numbers. Instead we estimate all higher terms at once. For given \(c_1\) the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 2}s_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} = -s_1 c_1\) is given, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality says that then \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 2}(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\,c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) is at least \(s_1^2 c_1^2\big/\sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 2}s_{\mathrm{j}}^2/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\). As with two sines, the sum of fractions decides, now of all of them,

\[F(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^\infty \frac{s_{\mathrm{j}}^2}{\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda} .\]

The fractions \(s_{\mathrm{j}}/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\) are the coefficients of a function that we know in closed form, so we can add up the sum.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality has a picture on the right half of figure 13.161. The vector \(u\) has the components \(\sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the vector \(v\) has \(s_{\mathrm{j}}/\sqrt{\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda}\), the angle between them is \(\theta\). Their scalar product is \(\sum c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\). It equals the length of \(v\) times the length of the perpendicular projection of \(u\) on \(v\), and this is not longer than \(u\) itself. So the product is at most the length of \(u\) times the length of \(v\), which is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Lemma 13.94 The sum of fractions and the auxiliary function of the column (vsota ulomkov in pomožna funkcija stebra)

Let \(L\), \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) be as in lemma 13.93, \(\lambda > 0\), \(k = \sqrt\lambda\) and \(\cos kL \ne 0\). Let

\[g(x) = \frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\frac{\cos k(L - x)}{\cos kL} - 1\Big) , \qquad 0 \le x \le L . \tag{13.102}\]
  1. The function \(g\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(-g'' - \lambda g = 1\) on \([0, L]\), \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(L) = 0\) (at \(\lambda = x_1^2/L^2\) it is, up to a constant factor, the blackwhite curve on the right of figure 13.157).
  2. For every \(j\), \(\lambda \ne \mu_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\int_0^L g\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = s_{\mathrm{j}}/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\).
  3. The series \(F(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^\infty s_{\mathrm{j}}^2/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\) converges and
    \[F(\lambda) = \int_0^L g\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\sin kL - kL\cos kL}{k^3\cos kL} = \frac{\tan kL - kL}{k^3} . \tag{13.103}\]
Proof

We must show the equation for \(g\) by differentiation, the coefficients by integrating by parts twice, then the sum from item 2 of lemma 13.93 and the integral with an antiderivative.

  1. By theorem 8.13 (equation (8.9) with \(\omega = -k\) and \(c = kL\)), \(g'(x) = \sin k(L - x)/(k\cos kL)\) and \(g''(x) = -\cos k(L - x)/\cos kL\), which is continuous. Therefore \(-g'' - \lambda g = \cos k(L - x)/\cos kL - \cos k(L - x)/\cos kL + 1 = 1\). At the ends \(g(0) = (\cos kL/\cos kL - 1)/\lambda = 0\) and \(g'(L) = \sin 0/(k\cos kL) = 0\).
  2. By item 1 of lemma 13.93, \(\sqrt{2/L}\,\cos\beta_{\mathrm{j}} L = \psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L) = 0\), while by assumption \(\cos kL \ne 0\). Hence \(k \ne \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\), and since both numbers are positive, \(\lambda = k^2 \ne \beta_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \mu_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. Let \(g_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L g\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) gives \(\int_0^L g''\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \big[g'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\big]_0^L - \int_0^L g'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}' = -\int_0^L g'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\), because \(g'(L) = 0\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0\). Once more: \(\int_0^L g'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}' = \big[g\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\big]_0^L - \int_0^L g\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'' = -\int_0^L g\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}''\), because \(g(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'(L) = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(L) = 0\). By item 1 of the lemma, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'' = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}' = -\mu_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\int_0^L g''\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = -\mu_{\mathrm{j}} g_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. We multiply the equation of step 1 by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and integrate: \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^L(-g'' - \lambda g)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mu_{\mathrm{j}} g_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda g_{\mathrm{j}}\) by step 3. By step 2 we may divide by \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda\), so \(g_{\mathrm{j}} = s_{\mathrm{j}}/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\).
  5. The function \(g\) is continuously differentiable with \(g(0) = 0\), so by item 2 of lemma 13.93, \(\int_0^L g = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} g_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\), the series converges, and by step 4, \(g_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} = s_{\mathrm{j}}^2/(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\).
  6. The function \(-\sin k(L - x)/k\) is an antiderivative of \(\cos k(L - x)\) (theorem 8.13), so by the fundamental theorem \(\int_0^L g = \frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\frac{\sin kL}{k\cos kL} - L\Big) = \frac{\sin kL - kL\cos kL}{k^3\cos kL}\), where we used \(\lambda = k^2\). With \(\tan kL = \sin kL/\cos kL\) this is \((\tan kL - kL)/k^3\).

What is the function \(g\)? Take a cantilever column, clamped at \(x = 0\), with a free top that is pushed, besides the vertical force \(P\), horizontally by a force \(R\). Look at the part of the column between the cross section \(x\) and the top.

We draw the column part by part. The lower end is clamped. The blackwhite curve is the axis of the column \(v(x)\), strongly enlarged. At the free top act the vertical force \(P\), which pushes the column along the axis, and the horizontal force \(R\), which pushes it sideways. The dot is the cross section at height \(x\), for which we look at the upper part.

About the point of the axis in the cross section \(x\), the vertical force has the lever arm \(v(L) - v(x)\) and the horizontal force the lever arm \(L - x\), because the deflections are small and we neglect the shortening of the axis as in lemma 8.149. The true lever arm of the force \(R\) is the vertical distance from the cross section to the top, which for an inextensible axis is shorter than the arc length \(L - x\) by a fraction that at the slope \(v'\) is about \(\tfrac12 v'^2\): at the slope \(0{,}05\) by \(0{,}125\) %, at the slope \(0{,}3\) by \(4{,}5\) %. The correction of the moment is proportional to \(R\) and to the square of the slope, hence of third order in the deflection, so the linear equation leaves it out, while for large deflections the elastica keeps it (figure 8.231). The cross section \(x\) carries the moment of both forces, so \(EI\,v''(x) = R\,(L - x) + P\,\big(v(L) - v(x)\big)\), with the sign for which a force \(R > 0\) at \(P = 0\) gives \(v'' > 0\), bending in the direction of the force. The force \(P\) bends in the same direction when the top is farther from the line than the cross section, \(v(L) > v(x)\). Differentiating with respect to \(x\) gives \(EI\,v''' + P\,v' = -R\). For the slope \(w = v'\) this is \(-w'' - \lambda w = R/EI\), with \(w(0) = 0\) because of the clamp and \(w'(L) = 0\), because the moment at the free top is zero. The function \((R/EI)\,g\) satisfies the same equations (item 1 of the lemma). The difference \(u\) of the two solutions satisfies \(u'' + \lambda u = 0\), so by theorem 8.18 \(u = u(0)\cos kx + (u'(0)/k)\sin kx\). From \(u(0) = 0\) it follows that \(u'(L) = u'(0)\cos kL\), and since this is zero and \(\cos kL \ne 0\), \(u'(0) = 0\) and \(u = 0\). Hence \(w = (R/EI)\,g\) and the deflection of the top is \(v(L) = \int_0^L w = (R/EI)\,F(\lambda)\). The number \(F(\lambda)/EI\) is thus the deflection of the top of the cantilever column under a unit horizontal force at the top. At \(P = 0\) we have \(g = Lx - x^2/2\) (the solution of \(-g'' = 1\) with \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(L) = 0\)) and \(\int_0^L g = L^3/3\), which is the deflection \(L^3/(3EI)\) of the end of the cantilever under a unit force from equation (5.65), multiplied by \(EI\). The column with the pin is this cantilever, whose top the pin holds on the line with the force \(R\). At \(F(\lambda) = 0\) a force \(R \ne 0\) bends the column but does not move the top off the line, so the curved position is an equilibrium. This is the threshold.

Lemma 13.95 The first positive zero of sin x − x cos x (prva pozitivna ničla funkcije sin x − x cos x)

Let \(H(x) = \sin x - x\cos x\).

  1. On the interval \((0, 3\pi/2]\), \(H\) has exactly one zero \(x_1\). It lies between \(\pi\) and \(3\pi/2\), and \(H(x) > 0\) for \(0 < x < x_1\) and \(H(x) < 0\) for \(x_1 < x \le 3\pi/2\).
  2. Let \(L > 0\), \(\lambda > 0\) and \(k = \sqrt\lambda\) with \(\pi/2 < kL < 3\pi/2\). Then \(\cos kL < 0\), and the sum \(F(\lambda)\) of lemma 13.94 is negative for \(kL < x_1\), zero for \(kL = x_1\) and positive for \(kL > x_1\).
Proof

We must show the sign of the derivative of \(H\) on both parts of the interval, then the monotonicity and the zero with the intermediate value theorem, and finally the sign of the sum from equation (13.103).

  1. By the product rule (lemma 1.82) and theorem 8.13, \(H'(x) = \cos x - \cos x + x\sin x = x\sin x\).
  2. By lemma 8.11, item 3, \(\sin x > 0\) for \(0 < x < \pi\). For \(\pi < x < 2\pi\), by step 1 of the proof of lemma 13.93, \(\sin x = -\sin(x - \pi) < 0\). So \(H' > 0\) on \((0, \pi)\) and \(H' < 0\) on \((\pi, 3\pi/2]\).
  3. For \(0 \le a < b \le \pi\), by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85), \(H(b) - H(a) = H'(\xi)\,(b - a) > 0\) with \(\xi \in (a, b)\), so \(H\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \pi]\). Since \(H(0) = 0\), \(H > 0\) on \((0, \pi]\), and \(H(\pi) = 0 - \pi\cdot(-1) = \pi\).
  4. In the same way \(H\) is strictly decreasing on \([\pi, 3\pi/2]\). By step 1 of the proof of lemma 13.93, \(\sin(3\pi/2) = -\sin(\pi/2) = -1\) and \(\cos(3\pi/2) = -\cos(\pi/2) = 0\), so \(H(3\pi/2) = -1\). The number \(0\) lies between \(H(\pi) = \pi\) and \(H(3\pi/2) = -1\), so by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) there is \(x_1 \in (\pi, 3\pi/2)\) with \(H(x_1) = 0\). Since \(H\) is strictly decreasing there, this zero is the only one, \(H > 0\) on \([\pi, x_1)\) and \(H < 0\) on \((x_1, 3\pi/2]\). Together with step 3 this is item 1.
  5. For \(\pi/2 < x < 3\pi/2\) we have \(|x - \pi| < \pi/2\), so by lemma 8.11, items 3 and 4 (the cosine is even and positive on \([0, \pi/2)\)), \(\cos(x - \pi) > 0\), and by step 1 of the proof of lemma 13.93, \(\cos x = -\cos(x - \pi) < 0\).
  6. By step 5, \(\cos kL < 0\), so \(\cos kL \ne 0\), and by equation (13.103), \(F(\lambda) = H(kL)/(k^3\cos kL)\). The denominator is negative, so \(F(\lambda)\) has the opposite sign of \(H(kL)\). Item 1 gives item 2.

Theorem 13.96 Column with a fixed and a pinned end (steber z vpetim in členkastim koncem)

Let \(L > 0\), \(EI > 0\) and \(P\) be real numbers and let \(x_1\) be the zero of lemma 13.95. The second variation

\[\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta) = \int_0^L \big(EI\,\eta''^2 - P\,\eta'^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\]

is positive for every twice continuously differentiable function \(\eta \ne 0\) on \([0, L]\) with \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = \eta(L) = 0\) exactly when

\[P < P_{\mathrm{cr}} = \frac{x_1^2\,EI}{L^2} . \tag{13.104}\]

For \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) it is also positive for every field with kinks of the second kind \(\eta \ne 0\) (lemma 12.10) with these three conditions.

Proof

We must show positivity below the threshold for fields with kinks of the second kind, which include the twice continuously differentiable functions, by expanding the slope, and a shape with a non-positive second variation above the threshold. Let \(\lambda = P/EI\) and \(\lambda_1 = x_1^2/L^2\), so that \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) exactly when \(\lambda < \lambda_1\). All integrals are over \([0, L]\), the notation \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\mu_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) is that of lemma 13.93.

  1. Let \(\eta \ne 0\) be a field with kinks of the second kind with \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = \eta(L) = 0\). A twice continuously differentiable function is such a field with a single piece (definition 6.11). Let \(w = \eta'\). By the definition of the field, \(w\) is piecewise continuously differentiable, \(w(0) = 0\), and by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 1, \(\eta\) is continuously differentiable) \(\int w = \eta(L) - \eta(0) = 0\). If \(w = 0\), then \(\eta\) would be constant (corollary 1.86), so \(\eta = \eta(0) = 0\). Hence \(w \ne 0\). The second variation is \(EI\,\big(\int w'^2 - \lambda\int w^2\big)\), as in equation (13.99).
  2. By item 2 of lemma 13.93 with the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\int w^2 = \sum c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), \(\int w'^2 \ge \sum\mu_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) and \(\sum c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} = \int w = 0\). With \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = \mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda\) therefore \(\delta^2\Pi/EI \ge \sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), and the series converges as the difference of convergent series (lemma 1.61). Not all \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) are zero, since otherwise \(\int w^2 = 0\) and by lemma 6.10, item 3, \(w = 0\), contrary to step 1.
  3. The numbers \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) increase with \(j\), so \(\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \mu_3 < \dots\)
  4. Let \(\lambda \le \mu_1\). Then \(d_1 \ge 0\) and \(d_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\) for \(j \ge 2\) by step 3. If \(c_{\mathrm{j}} \ne 0\) for some \(j \ge 2\), then \(\sum d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \ge d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 > 0\). Otherwise \(c_1 \ne 0\) by step 2, while the condition \(\sum c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) gives \(c_1 s_1 = 0\), which is impossible because \(s_1 > 0\). Hence \(\delta^2\Pi > 0\).
  5. Let \(\mu_1 < \lambda < \lambda_1\) and \(k = \sqrt\lambda\). Then \(\pi/2 < kL < x_1 < 3\pi/2 = \beta_2 L\), so \(\lambda < \mu_2\), \(d_1 < 0\) and \(d_{\mathrm{j}} \ge d_2 > 0\) for \(j \ge 2\). By item 2 of lemma 13.95, \(\cos kL \ne 0\) and \(F(\lambda) < 0\). The series \(F(\lambda)\) converges (lemma 13.94), so \(S = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 2}s_{\mathrm{j}}^2/d_{\mathrm{j}} = F(\lambda) - s_1^2/d_1\) converges too, and \(S > 0\) because all its terms are positive.
  6. Let \(D = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 2}d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). For \(N \ge 2\) we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (lemma 6.19) for the sum of products on \(\mathbb R^{N-1}\), as in step 6 of the proof of theorem 9.119, with the components \(\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{j}}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{j}}/\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{j}}}\): \(\big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=2}^N c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\big)^2 \le \big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=2}^N d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\big)\big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=2}^N s_{\mathrm{j}}^2/d_{\mathrm{j}}\big) \le D\,S\). The last step holds because a partial sum of a series with nonnegative terms is not larger than the sum (theorem 1.52). As \(N \to \infty\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=2}^N c_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{j}}\) tends to \(-c_1 s_1\) by the condition of step 2, so by lemma 1.49 \(c_1^2 s_1^2 \le D\,S\).
  7. By step 6, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = d_1 c_1^2 + D \ge d_1 c_1^2 + c_1^2 s_1^2/S = \dfrac{c_1^2}{S}\,\big(d_1 S + s_1^2\big) = \dfrac{c_1^2\,d_1}{S}\Big(S + \dfrac{s_1^2}{d_1}\Big) = \dfrac{c_1^2\,d_1\,F(\lambda)}{S}\). By step 5, \(d_1\) and \(F(\lambda)\) are negative and \(S\) is positive. If \(c_1 \ne 0\), therefore \(\sum d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 > 0\). If \(c_1 = 0\), then by step 2 \(c_{\mathrm{j}} \ne 0\) for some \(j \ge 2\) and \(\sum d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = D \ge d_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}^2 > 0\). Hence \(\delta^2\Pi > 0\).
  8. Steps 4 and 5 to 7 cover all \(\lambda < \lambda_1\). For \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) the second variation is therefore positive for every field with kinks of the second kind and so for every twice continuously differentiable function with the three conditions.
  9. Let \(\lambda \ge \lambda_1\). Let \(g_1\) be the function (13.102) at \(\lambda_1\), that is with \(k_1 = x_1/L\). By item 1 of lemma 13.95, \(\pi < x_1 < 3\pi/2\), by its item 2, \(\cos x_1 \ne 0\) and \(F(\lambda_1) = 0\), and by item 3 of lemma 13.94, \(\int g_1 = F(\lambda_1) = 0\). Let \(\eta^*(x) = \int_0^x g_1(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 2), \(\eta^{*\prime} = g_1\) and \(\eta^{*\prime\prime} = g_1'\), so \(\eta^*\) is twice continuously differentiable, and \(\eta^*(0) = 0\), \(\eta^{*\prime}(0) = g_1(0) = 0\), \(\eta^*(L) = \int g_1 = 0\). The function \(g_1\) is not zero, since otherwise the equation \(-g_1'' - \lambda_1 g_1 = 1\) would give \(0 = 1\). Hence \(\eta^* \ne 0\).
  10. Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) and item 1 of lemma 13.94 give \(\int g_1'^2 = \big[g_1 g_1'\big]_0^L - \int g_1 g_1'' = \int g_1\,(1 + \lambda_1 g_1) = \int g_1 + \lambda_1\int g_1^2 = \lambda_1\int g_1^2\), because \(g_1(0) = g_1'(L) = 0\) and \(\int g_1 = 0\). Therefore \(\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta^*) = EI\,\big(\int g_1'^2 - \lambda\int g_1^2\big) = EI\,(\lambda_1 - \lambda)\int g_1^2 \le 0\). For \(P \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) the second variation is therefore not positive for every twice continuously differentiable function \(\eta \ne 0\).

The theorem showed that the threshold \(20{,}19\,EI/L^2\), which section 8.14.3 computed as the first force with a curved equilibrium, is also the limit of positivity of the second variation. The proof had three parts. The slope replaced the deflection, and the pin became a single linear condition \(\int w = 0\). The expansion in quarter sines estimated the second variation by the sum \(\sum(\mu_{\mathrm{j}} - \lambda)\,c_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), in which below \(\mu_1\) every term is nonnegative and above \(\mu_1\) only the first one is negative. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality estimated the higher terms at once, and the decision fell to the sum \(F(\lambda)\), which is the deflection of the top of the cantilever under a horizontal force and is computed by the function \(g\). The shape \(\eta^*\) of step 9 is \(v/k_1^3\), where \(v = k_1(L - x) + \sin k_1 x - k_1 L\cos k_1 x\) is the curved solution of the equilibrium equation of section 8.14.3 at the threshold (SymPy). This \(v\) satisfies \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\) and \(v(L) = v''(L) = 0\), because \(\sin x_1 = x_1\cos x_1\). The shape and its slope are drawn in figure 13.157. Only the number \(x_1 = 4{,}4934\) remained computed, the existence and uniqueness of the threshold are proved. At the force \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) itself the second variation is zero in the direction \(\eta^*\). Whether the straight position is stable there, the quadratic energy does not decide, as for the other supports in section 8.14.3.

We see that the threshold is sharp when we test the same field \(\eta^*\) a little below and a little above the force \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\). From step 10, \(\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta^*) = EI\,(\lambda_1 - \lambda)\int g_1^2\) and \(\int g_1'^2 = \lambda_1\int g_1^2\), so

\[\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta^*) = \Big(1 - \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{cr}}}\Big)\,EI\int_0^L \eta^{*\prime\prime 2}\,\mathrm{d}x ,\]

since \(\eta^{*\prime\prime} = g_1'\). By step 10, therefore, also \(\lambda_1\int g_1^2 = \tfrac12\), so \(\delta^2\Pi(0, \eta^*)/EI = \tfrac12\,(1 - P/P_{\mathrm{cr}})\). At \(P = 0{,}99\,P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) this is \(+0{,}0050\), at \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) it is zero and at \(P = 1{,}01\,P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) it is \(-0{,}0050\), which is \(+1\) %, \(0\) and \(-1\) % of the term \(EI\int\eta^{*\prime\prime 2}\), twice the bending energy \(\tfrac12 EI\int\eta^{*\prime\prime 2}\) of equation (8.155). For the strip with \(L = 2\) m and \(EI = 5226{,}67\) N m², \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) \(= 20{,}1907 \cdot 5226{,}67/4\) N \(= 26{,}383\) kN, and the second variation in the direction \(\eta^*\) is positive at \(26{,}119\) kN and negative at \(26{,}646\) kN. The positivity below the threshold, which the theorem proves for every shape, is thus one percent of the force away from the limit, and the shape \(\eta^*\) already destroys it at one percent above.

The threshold of the strip is \(2{,}0457\) times the \(12{,}896\) kN for pinned ends. The same theorem also closes the gap of chapter 12, because it holds for fields with kinks of the second kind, such as the fields of Hermite elements.

Corollary 13.97 Finite element buckling load of the column with a fixed and a pinned end (kritična sila modela stebra z vpetim in členkastim koncem)

For support (iv) of theorem 12.53, a clamp at \(x = 0\) and a pin support at \(x = L\), \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}} = x_1^2 EI/L^2\) for every mesh.

Proof

We must show that the Rayleigh quotient of every field of the model is at least \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\), with theorem 13.96.

  1. By steps 1, 3 and 4 of the proof of theorem 12.53, \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the smallest value of the quotient \(R(\vek d) = EI\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\big/\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\) over \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\), where \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a field with kinks of the second kind and \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 > 0\). The clamp prescribes \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}'(0) = 0\), the pin \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(L) = 0\). From \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 > 0\) it follows that \(v_{\mathrm{h}} \ne 0\).
  2. Let \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}}\). By theorem 13.96, \(EI\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2 - P\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 > 0\), and division by the positive \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\) gives \(R(\vek d) > P\).
  3. If \(R(\vek d) < P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) for some \(\vek d\), step 2 with \(P = R(\vek d)\) would give \(R(\vek d) > R(\vek d)\). Hence \(R(\vek d) \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) for every \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\) and \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The values of the table in section 12.10.6, \(39{,}200\), \(27{,}060\), \(26{,}437\) and \(26{,}386\) kN for \(n = 1\), \(2\), \(4\) and \(8\) elements, are therefore above the proved bound \(26{,}383\) kN and not only above a computed one. For all four supports of theorem 12.53 the buckling load of the finite element model is thus an upper bound of the true one.
