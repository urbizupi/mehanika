Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.8 Vibration isolation

A machine of mass 250 kg stands in a building and has a rotor that turns 1500 times a minute. The rotor is not perfectly balanced, so the machine shakes the floor. If we bolt it directly to the floor, the floor holds it with the force that the rotor produces and feels all of it. If we put it on soft mounts, the floor feels only what the mounts transmit. The question is which mounts send less force into the floor, and what happens at start-up, when the speed of rotation rises from zero to 1500 1/min. The answer stays within the equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t)\) of section 9.1 and the response to a harmonic force of section 9.6. At the end we use the same mathematics for the opposite case: it is not the machine that shakes the floor, but the floor moves and carries a machine or a measuring instrument with it.

9.8.1 A machine with an unbalanced rotor

We call the machine machine \(B\). The model is ours, the numbers are chosen for this section. The rigid frame of the machine with everything that turns or rests, except the unbalanced mass, has the mass \(M_{\mathrm{f}}\). The rotor has a horizontal axis. We think of the unbalance of the rotor as a point mass \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) at the distance \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) from the axis, while the rest of the rotor is balanced, so its centre of mass lies on the axis. The product \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) is called the unbalance (neuravnoteženost) of the rotor. The forces that such an unbalance causes are present in every rotating machine and oscillate with the frequency of rotation (FPGL, PDF p. 21). For machine \(B\) we have \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\,a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}02\) kg m, which is, for example, a mass of 20 g at the distance 1 m from the axis or 200 g at the distance 0,1 m. The total mass is \(M = M_{\mathrm{f}} + m_{\mathrm{u}} = 250\) kg. The motor keeps the rotor at a constant angular velocity \(\Omega\), so the angle of the unbalanced mass is \(\Omega t\), a known function of time. Such a constraint is rheonomic (definition 8.42). At 1500 1/min we have \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s, which is a frequency of 25 Hz.

The machine stands on mounts (podložke), that is, on elements that connect it elastically to the floor. We model each mount as a spring and a viscous damper (definition 9.1) acting in parallel between the machine and the floor. The four mounts together have the stiffness \(k\) and the damping coefficient \(c\). By an idealization that we call the guide (vodilo), the machine has only the vertical displacement \(x\). A real machine would also sway and tilt, which would need more coordinates (chapter 10). The displacement \(x\) is measured upward from the position in which the machine rests under its own weight. Figure 9.29 (left) shows the machine, its mount and the unbalanced mass.

We derive the equation of motion of the machine from Lagrange's equations. We compute in the generalized coordinate \(x\).

Example 9.64 Equation of motion of a machine with an unbalanced rotor (enačba gibanja stroja z neuravnoteženim rotorjem)

Machine \(B\) as above: the mass \(M_{\mathrm{f}}\) and the unbalanced mass \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) with the angle \(\Omega t\) at the distance \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) from the axis, mounts with \(k\) and \(c\), the coordinate \(x\) upward. We look for the equation for \(x(t)\).

  1. The position of the unbalanced mass is vertically \(x + a_{\mathrm{u}}\cos\Omega t\), so its velocity is \(\dot x - a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin\Omega t\). The frame moves with the velocity \(\dot x\).
  2. The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{f}}\,\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{u}}(\dot x - a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin\Omega t)^2\). With \(M = M_{\mathrm{f}} + m_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have
    \[T = \underbrace{\tfrac12 M\,\dot x^2}_{\mathrm{T}_2} \underbrace{{} - m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin(\Omega t)\,\dot x}_{\mathrm{T}_1} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}^2\Omega^2\sin^2\Omega t}_{\mathrm{T}_0} .\]
    This is the decomposition of theorem 8.84. The terms \(T_1\) and \(T_0\) are not zero because the constraint contains time. The kinetic energy of the rotation of the rotor about its axis is a constant for a prescribed \(\Omega\) and does not enter the derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(\dot x\).
  3. In the equilibrium position the mounts are compressed by the static sag (poves) \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = Mg/k\), since they must carry the weight. The potential energy of the spring is therefore \(\tfrac12 k\,(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} - x)^2\), and that of the weights \(M g\,x + m_{\mathrm{u}} g\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\cos\Omega t\). The derivative of the sum with respect to \(x\) is \(-k\,(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} - x) + Mg = k\,x\), because \(k\,\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = Mg\). The term \(m_{\mathrm{u}} g\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\cos\Omega t\) depends only on time and vanishes when we differentiate with respect to \(x\). Only the spring and the damper therefore act, the weight cancels because the coordinate is measured from the equilibrium position.
  4. The damper gives the non-conservative generalized force \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\,\dot x\) (definition 9.1).
  5. By theorem 8.88 we have \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\partial T/\partial\dot x\big) - \partial T/\partial x + k\,x = -c\,\dot x\). Here \(\partial T/\partial\dot x = M\dot x - m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin\Omega t\), its time derivative is \(M\ddot x - m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\), and \(\partial T/\partial x = 0\). The moment with which the motor maintains the angle \(\Omega t\) is the reaction of an ideal rheonomic constraint: its work in a virtual displacement at frozen time is zero, so it does not enter the generalized force for \(x\) (virtual displacement for a rheonomic constraint, theorem 8.86). We obtain
    \[M\,\ddot x + c\,\dot x + k\,x = m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\Omega^2\cos\Omega t . \tag{9.54}\]

SymPy confirms step 5. This is equation (9.7) of theorem 9.3 with the mass \(m = M\), the force \(Q(t) = Q_0\cos\Omega t\) and \(Q_0 = m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\), for which the remainder is \(R = 0\) because the equation of the machine is exactly linear.

Source: Not from a source. The unbalance as a source of force on a machine is named by FPGL (PDF p. 21), exercise 2.12 (PDF p. 90) asks for the equation of motion of two contra-rotating machines on mounts with an unbalanced mass \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) at the radius \(r\), its equation is not legible in the text of the source. The derivation in five steps is ours.

Why is the amplitude of the force \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\)? The unbalanced mass moves on a circle of radius \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\), so it has the acceleration \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) toward the axis. To give it this acceleration, the axis must pull it with the force \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\). By the law of action and reaction the mass pulls the axis outward with the same force, and its vertical part is \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\). This agrees with the right-hand side of equation (9.54). For machine \(B\) we have \(Q_0 = 0{,}02\cdot 157{,}08^2 = 493{,}48\) N, which is a fifth of the weight of the machine (\(Mg = 2452{,}5\) N). Because the angular velocity enters squared, at twice the speed of rotation the force would be four times larger.

Equation (9.54) has the same form as the equation in section 9.6. So by theorem 9.52 on the response to a harmonic force the steady state has the amplitude \(X = Q_0\,|H(\Omega)|\). With \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), the dynamic amplification factor \(D(r) = k\,|H|\) (definition 9.53), \(Q_0 = m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 r^2\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k/M\) we have

\[X = \frac{m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}}{M}\,r^2 D(r) . \tag{9.55}\]

We will need this later.

9.8.2 Transmissibility

The floor does not feel the force \(Q\) that the rotor produces, but the force of the mounts. If the machine is higher than its equilibrium position by \(x\), the spring is compressed less and presses on the floor by \(k\,x\) less than in equilibrium, which is a change of the force on the floor of \(k\,x\) upward. The damper, whose ends move apart with the velocity \(\dot x\), pulls the floor upward with the force \(c\,\dot x\). The mounts are massless, so the force on the floor, besides the constant weight of the machine, is

\[f_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,x + c\,\dot x . \tag{9.56}\]

This force oscillates with the same frequency as the excitation. We compare its amplitude with the amplitude of the exciting force. Without the damper we would have only the spring force, but with the damper the floor also feels its force, so it is not clear in advance whether the damper helps or harms.

Definition 9.65 Transmissibility (prenosnost)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\) and \(Q_0 > 0\), \(\Omega > 0\) such that \(k - m\Omega^2 + i\,c\,\Omega \ne 0\). Let \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(Q_0\,H(\Omega)\,e^{i\Omega t}\big)\) be the steady state of the response to a harmonic force from theorem 9.52 for the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\) and \(f_{\mathrm{t}}(t) = k\,q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) + c\,\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\) the force that the spring and the damper transmit to the base. Let \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = \max_{\mathrm{t}} |f_{\mathrm{t}}(t)|\) be its largest absolute value. The transmissibility is the number

\[\mathrm{TR} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{t}}}{Q_0} , \tag{9.57}\]

that is, the ratio of the amplitude of the force that the mount transmits to the base to the amplitude of the exciting force.

Source: CK (4.39)-(4.44), PDF p. 115, where TR is the ratio of the amplitude of the transmitted force to the force \(p_0 = k\,U_0\) that the spring would transmit under a static load \(p_0\). Here we divide by \(Q_0\), because we are interested in the force into the floor relative to the force that the source produces.

A mount reduces forces when \(\mathrm{TR} < 1\) and amplifies them when \(\mathrm{TR} > 1\). When does the first hold?

Theorem 9.66 Transmissibility of a single-degree-of-freedom system (prenosnost sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

In the setting of definition 9.65 let \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\). Then

\[\mathrm{TR} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = \sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}\,D(r) . \tag{9.58}\]

For every \(\zeta \ge 0\) we have \(\mathrm{TR} = 1\) at \(r = \sqrt2\), \(\mathrm{TR} < 1\) exactly for \(r > \sqrt2\) and \(\mathrm{TR} > 1\) for \(0 < r < \sqrt2\). For \(r > \sqrt2\) the value \(\mathrm{TR}\) strictly increases with increasing \(\zeta\), for \(0 < r < \sqrt2\) it strictly decreases with increasing \(\zeta > 0\).

Proof

We must show the form (9.58) and then the behaviour of \(\mathrm{TR}^2\) with respect to \(r\) and \(\zeta\).

  1. By theorem 9.28 we have \(\dot q_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(i\Omega\,Q_0 H\,e^{i\Omega t}\big)\). Since \(k\) and \(c\) are real, \(f_{\mathrm{t}} = \mathrm{Re}\big((k + i\,c\,\Omega)\,Q_0 H\,e^{i\Omega t}\big)\).
  2. Let \(A = (k + i c\Omega)\,Q_0 H\) and \(A = |A|\,(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)\) by definition 9.24. Then \(\mathrm{Re}\big(A e^{i\Omega t}\big) = |A|\cos(\Omega t + \alpha)\) (definition 9.27 and addition formula 8.15), which has the largest absolute value \(|A|\). So \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = |A| = |k + i c\Omega|\,|H|\,Q_0\) (\(|zw| = |z||w|\), definition 9.21).
  3. From \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km}\) and \(\Omega = r\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = r\sqrt{k/m}\) it follows that \(c\,\Omega = 2\zeta r\,k\). Therefore \(|k + i c\Omega| = k\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}\).
  4. By definition 9.53 we have \(|H| = D(r)/k\). We insert into step 2: \(\mathrm{TR} = F_{\mathrm{t}}/Q_0 = \sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}\,D(r)\), which is (9.58).
  5. Let \(N = 1 + 4\zeta^2 r^2\) and \(S = (1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 r^2\), so that \(\mathrm{TR}^2 = N/S\) and \(S > 0\). The difference is \(S - N = (1 - r^2)^2 - 1 = r^4 - 2r^2 = r^2 (r^2 - 2)\), so
    \[\mathrm{TR}^2 - 1 = \frac{N - S}{S} = \frac{r^2\,(2 - r^2)}{S} .\]
    For \(r > 0\) the right-hand side is positive exactly for \(r < \sqrt2\), zero at \(r = \sqrt2\) and negative for \(r > \sqrt2\). Since \(\mathrm{TR} \ge 0\), the same sign holds for \(\mathrm{TR} - 1\).
  6. Let \(w = \zeta^2\). Then \(N\) and \(S\) are differentiable in \(w\) with \(\partial N/\partial w = \partial S/\partial w = 4r^2\). By the quotient rule
    \[\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\frac{N}{S} = \frac{4r^2\,S - N\,4r^2}{S^2} = \frac{4r^2\,(S - N)}{S^2} = \frac{4r^4\,(r^2 - 2)}{S^2} .\]
    For \(r > \sqrt2\) this derivative is positive, for \(0 < r < \sqrt2\) negative. Since \(w = \zeta^2\) is a strictly increasing function of \(\zeta \ge 0\), and \(\mathrm{TR}\) is an increasing function of \(\mathrm{TR}^2\), the claim about \(\zeta\) follows.

Source: CK (4.44) and figure 4.11 (PDF p. 115-116): CK states that the transmitted force is smaller than the static one only for \(r > \sqrt2\) and that for such \(r\) it decreases when the damping decreases. The proof in six steps is ours, SymPy checked \(\mathrm{TR}^2 - 1\) and the derivative with respect to \(\zeta\).

Figure 9.30 shows \(\mathrm{TR}\) against \(r\) for five values of \(\zeta\). All the curves cross in the point \((\sqrt2, 1)\) and lie below one to the right of it.

Meaning: for \(r < \sqrt2\) the mount amplifies forces, for \(r > \sqrt2\) it reduces them. At \(r = \sqrt2\) we have \(\mathrm{TR} = 1\) for every damping, because then the right-hand side in step 5 vanishes. Isolation therefore needs mounts that are soft enough that the natural frequency of the machine on them is smaller than \(1/\sqrt2\) of the exciting frequency. Damping helps only near resonance and harms far above it. We see the reason if we ask what the machine does at large \(r\). Then the acceleration of the machine is \(\Omega^2 X\), so the inertial force \(M\Omega^2 X\) is much larger than the spring force \(k X\) (their ratio is \(r^2\)), and the machine moves almost like a free mass under the force \(Q_0\cos\Omega t\). Its displacement therefore has the amplitude approximately \(X \approx Q_0/(M\Omega^2)\). The spring force is then \(k\,Q_0/(M\Omega^2) = Q_0/r^2\), and the damper force, whose velocity has the amplitude \(\Omega X\), is \(c\,\Omega\,Q_0/(M\Omega^2) = 2\zeta\,Q_0/r\). The forces \(k\,x\) and \(c\,\dot x\) are shifted by a quarter of the period (one is a cosine, the other a sine), so their amplitudes add by Pythagoras and \(\mathrm{TR} \approx \sqrt{1/r^4 + 4\zeta^2/r^2}\). The spring contributes with \(1/r^2\), the damper with \(2\zeta/r\), which falls more slowly with \(r\). At \(r = 5\) and \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) the approximation is \(0{,}0447\), the exact value from equation (9.58) is \(0{,}0466\).

Let us test this on machine \(B\).

Example 9.67 Soft and stiff mounts for machine B (mehke in trde podložke za stroj B)

Machine \(B\) of example 9.64, \(M = 250\) kg, \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}02\) kg m, \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s, \(Q_0 = 493{,}48\) N. Let the mounts have the natural frequency \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz, so \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi\cdot 5 = 31{,}416\) 1/s and the stiffness \(k = M\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 246\,740\) N/m (\(61\,685\) N/m for each of the four mounts). The ratio is \(r = 25/5 = 5\). From equation (9.58) and the force \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = Q_0\,\mathrm{TR}\) we obtain (computed with Python, NumPy):

\(\zeta\) \(\mathrm{TR}\) \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) in N amplitude of the machine \(X\) in mm
0 \(0{,}04167\) \(20{,}56\) \(0{,}08333\)
\(0{,}05\) \(0{,}04657\) \(22{,}98\) \(0{,}08332\)
\(0{,}1\) \(0{,}05887\) \(29{,}05\) \(0{,}08326\)
\(0{,}2\) \(0{,}09285\) \(45{,}82\) \(0{,}08305\)

Without damping the force into the floor is \(493{,}48/24 = 20{,}56\) N, which is \(1/(r^2 - 1)\) of the exciting force. The floor therefore receives only \(1/24\) of the force of the rotor, besides the constant weight \(2452{,}5\) N. At \(\zeta = 0{,}2\) the force is \(45{,}82\) N, \(2{,}2\) times as much as without damping, because the term \(2\zeta r = 2\) in equation (9.58) is no longer small. The amplitude of the machine hardly changes, because by equation (9.55) we have \(X \to m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}/M = 0{,}08\) mm for large \(r\), regardless of \(k\) and \(c\). The frame would have the same displacement if the centre of mass of the whole machine were at rest: from \(M x + m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\cos\Omega t = 0\) it follows that \(x = -(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}/M)\cos\Omega t\). At large \(r\) the mounts therefore no longer hold the machine, the centre of mass is at rest and the frame moves opposite to the unbalanced mass.

If the mounts were stiffer, for example \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 50\) Hz (\(k = 24{,}67\) MN/m, \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = 0{,}099\) mm, \(r = 0{,}5\)), we would have \(\mathrm{TR} = 1/(1 - 0{,}25) = 1{,}3333\) at \(\zeta = 0\) and the force into the floor \(657{,}97\) N. This is more than the \(493{,}48\) N that the rotor produces. A stiff mount is therefore not a safe mount: at \(r < \sqrt2\) the mount amplifies forces, because the exciting frequency is close to the natural one. If we bolted the machine to the floor (\(k \to \infty\), \(r \to 0\)), we would have \(\mathrm{TR} \to 1\) and the floor would feel the whole force of the rotor.

How do we choose the mounts? The spring carries the weight of the machine, so its stiffness is tied to the sag. In equilibrium \(k\,\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = M g\), so \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k/M = g/\delta_{\mathrm{st}}\) and

\[f_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta_{\mathrm{st}}}} . \tag{9.59}\]

We measure such a natural frequency if we load the mount with the weight and measure the compression (CK, example 3.2, PDF p. 91). Conversely, if we want \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz, the sag must be \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = g/(2\pi\cdot 5)^2 = 9{,}94\) mm. For \(\mathrm{TR} < 1\) at 25 Hz it is enough that \(f_{\mathrm{n}} < 25/\sqrt2 = 17{,}7\) Hz, so a sag above \(0{,}795\) mm. The price of soft isolation is a large sag: at \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz almost a centimetre, at \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 1\) Hz already \(248{,}5\) mm.

9.8.3 Starting the machine

At the working speed (\(r = 5\)) all is well. But the machine reaches it from rest and passes through resonance at \(r = 1\). The exciting force \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) is still small there, but resonance amplifies it. How large is the force into the floor on the way? At the speed \(\Omega\) the amplitude of the machine is \(X\) from equation (9.55), and the amplitude of the force into the floor is \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = Q_0\,\mathrm{TR} = m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,r^2\,\mathrm{TR}(r)\). These two values describe a start-up in which the speed changes slowly, so that at every time the machine is approximately in the steady state of that speed. What "slowly" means we say below.

The largest amplitude of the machine is quickly found. In equation (9.55) we have \(r^2 D(r) = 1/\big((u - 1)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u\big)^{1/2}\) with \(u = 1/r^2\), because we divide the numerator and the denominator by \(r^2\). The expression under the root is a quadratic function of \(u\), whose derivative \(2(u - 1) + 4\zeta^2\) is zero at \(u = 1 - 2\zeta^2\), so at \(r = 1/\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}\) (for \(\zeta < 1/\sqrt2\)). There the expression has the value \(4\zeta^2(1 - \zeta^2)\) and

\[X_{\max} = \frac{m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}}{M}\,\frac{1}{2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} . \tag{9.60}\]

The largest force into the floor is found slightly elsewhere (numerically we look for the largest value of \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,r^2\,\mathrm{TR}(r)\) on a dense grid of \(r\)):

\(\zeta\) \(r\) at \(X_{\max}\) \(X_{\max}\) in mm largest \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) in N at the speed \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) at 1500 1/min
\(0{,}05\) \(1{,}0025\) \(0{,}8010\) \(198{,}63\) at \(300{,}8\) 1/min \(22{,}98\) N
\(0{,}1\) \(1{,}0102\) \(0{,}4020\) \(101{,}20\) at \(303{,}2\) 1/min \(29{,}05\) N
\(0{,}2\) \(1{,}0426\) \(0{,}2041\) \(54{,}60\) at \(315{,}0\) 1/min \(45{,}82\) N

Figure 9.31 shows the amplitude of the machine and the force into the floor against the speed. At \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) the force at the passage through resonance is \(8{,}6\) times larger than at the working speed, at \(\zeta = 0{,}2\) only \(1{,}2\) times. Damping, which harms at the working speed (\(22{,}98\) N against \(45{,}82\) N), helps at start-up (\(198{,}63\) N against \(54{,}60\) N). The choice of damping is therefore a compromise that cannot be settled with a single number. The peak of the force is slightly higher than the peak of the amplitude (at \(\zeta = 0{,}2\) at \(r = 1{,}0498\) instead of \(1{,}0426\)), because \(\mathrm{TR}\) still grows with \(r\) there.

When is the start-up slow enough? We obtain an estimate here, not a proof. The free vibration of the machine decays as \(e^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) (point 3 of theorem 9.52), so it has the decay time \(\tau = 1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). For machine \(B\) with \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) we have \(\tau = 1/(0{,}05\cdot 31{,}416) = 0{,}64\) s. We expect that the motion of the machine depends only on the last few \(\tau\), because the rest has already died out, and that the steady state therefore holds if in this time the speed changes very little compared with the width of the resonance. By theorem 9.62 this width is \(\Omega_2 - \Omega_1 = 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 3{,}14\) 1/s. If the speed increases uniformly with \(\alpha = \dot\Omega\), it changes by \(\alpha\tau\) in the time \(\tau\), so we estimate that we need \(\alpha\tau \ll 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is

\[\alpha \ll 2\,(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 = 4{,}93\ \mathrm{1/s^2} .\]

This is a consideration of sizes. We have no theorem that would claim, for a changing speed, that the motion is close to the steady one and say how close, so the criterion must not be read as a proven limit. If the motor reaches \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s in the time \(T_{\mathrm{r}}\), then \(\alpha = 157{,}08/T_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the estimate says that \(T_{\mathrm{r}}\) must substantially exceed \(157{,}08/4{,}93 = 32\) s.

We test the estimate numerically, which is a test on one machine and one damping, not a proof. At a changing speed the angle is no longer \(\Omega t\), but \(\theta(t) = \alpha t^2/2\). If in step 2 of example 9.64 we replace \(\Omega t\) by \(\theta\), then \(T_1 = -m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\dot\theta\sin\theta\,\dot x\) and its time derivative in step 5 gives the right-hand side \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}(\dot\theta^2\cos\theta + \ddot\theta\sin\theta)\) with \(\dot\theta = \alpha t\) and \(\ddot\theta = \alpha\). We solved this equation from rest (\(x = 0\), \(\dot x = 0\) at \(t = 0\)) step by step with a computer (the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, which we do not develop in this book, steps of 0,2 ms and 0,4 ms, the result does not change when the step is halved) for \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) and several start-up times \(T_{\mathrm{r}}\). A faster start-up reduces the peak, because the amplitude has no time to build up, but the force into the floor even for a start-up in three seconds is still \(100\) N, which is \(4{,}4\) times as much as at the working speed (\(22{,}98\) N). The passage through resonance therefore must not be neglected, even if it is short. An exact computation of a fast passage needs the response to a force whose frequency changes, which is a task for the integral that builds the response to a force of arbitrary shape from the responses to short pulses (section 9.10), and we do not compute it here.

9.8.4 Motion of the base

So far the source of vibration has been the machine and the base has stood still. Often it is the other way round: a vehicle drives on a rough road, an earthquake shakes a building, an instrument stands on a machine that shakes. The base then moves by a known function, \(y(t) = Y\cos\Omega t\), and on it is a mass \(M\) on a spring \(k\) and a damper \(c\) (figure 9.29, right). The absolute displacement of the mass is denoted by \(q\), and the relative displacement with respect to the base is \(z = q - y\). The displacements are measured from the equilibrium position in which the spring carries the weight of the mass, so here too we do not write the weight (as in step 3 of example 9.64).

Theorem 9.68 Base motion (gibanje podlage)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\) and \(\Omega > 0\) such that \(k - m\Omega^2 + i\,c\,\Omega \ne 0\). Let the base move by \(y(t) = Y\cos\Omega t\) and let the mass \(m\) be on it on a spring \(k\) and a damper \(c\), in parallel between the mass and the base. Let \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\). The steady state (section 9.6) has

  1. the relative displacement \(z = q - y\) with the amplitude \(r^2 D(r)\,Y\),
  2. the absolute displacement \(q\) with the amplitude \(\mathrm{TR}\cdot Y\), where \(\mathrm{TR}\) is the expression from theorem 9.66.
Proof

We must show that \(z\) satisfies the equation with the force \(m\Omega^2 Y\cos\Omega t\) and that \(q\) satisfies the equation with the complex amplitude \((k + i c\Omega)Y\).

  1. Let the coordinate \(z\) be the generalized coordinate. The position of the mass is \(z + y(t)\), so the kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot z + \dot y)^2 = \tfrac12 m\dot z^2 + m\dot y\,\dot z + \tfrac12 m\dot y^2\), which is the decomposition of theorem 8.84 with the terms \(T_1\) and \(T_0\), because \(y\) depends on time. The spring is stretched by \(z\), so \(V = \tfrac12 k z^2\), and the damper acts with \(-c\,\dot z\) (definition 9.1).
  2. By theorem 8.88 we have \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(m\dot z + m\dot y) + k z = -c\dot z\), so \(m\ddot z + c\dot z + k z = -m\ddot y = m\Omega^2 Y\cos\Omega t\).
  3. This is the equation of theorem 9.3 with the force \(Q_0\cos\Omega t\), \(Q_0 = m\Omega^2 Y\). By theorem 9.52 on the response to a harmonic force the steady state is \(z = \mathrm{Re}\big(m\Omega^2 Y\,H\,e^{i\Omega t}\big)\) with the amplitude \(m\Omega^2 Y\,|H| = m\Omega^2 Y\,D/k = r^2 D\,Y\), because \(m\Omega^2/k = r^2\) (\(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k/m\)). This is claim 1.
  4. The equation of step 2 reads \(m(\ddot z + \ddot y) + c\dot z + k z = 0\). With \(z = q - y\) we have \(\ddot z + \ddot y = \ddot q\), so \(m\ddot q + c(\dot q - \dot y) + k(q - y) = 0\) and \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = k\,y + c\,\dot y\). The right-hand side is \(kY\cos\Omega t - c\Omega Y\sin\Omega t = \mathrm{Re}\big((k + i c\Omega)\,Y\,e^{i\Omega t}\big)\) (theorem 9.28).
  5. Let \(\hat Q\) be a complex number. The function \(\hat Q H\,e^{i\Omega t}\) solves the complex equation with the right-hand side \(\hat Q\,e^{i\Omega t}\), because \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}e^{i\Omega t} = i\Omega e^{i\Omega t}\) and the left part is \(P(i\Omega)\,\hat Q H\,e^{i\Omega t} = \hat Q\,e^{i\Omega t}\). By lemma 9.32 its real part is a solution for the right-hand side \(\mathrm{Re}(\hat Q e^{i\Omega t})\). The other solutions differ from it by a free motion (point 2 of theorem 9.52 on the response to a harmonic force, its proof uses only linearity), which for \(\zeta > 0\) dies out, so this solution is the steady state. Its amplitude is \(|\hat Q|\,|H|\) as in step 2 of the proof of theorem 9.66. For \(\hat Q = (k + i c\Omega)\,Y\) this is \(|k + i c\Omega|\,|H|\,Y = k\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}\,(D/k)\,Y = \mathrm{TR}\cdot Y\) (steps 3 and 4 of the same proof). This is claim 2.

Source: CK (4.45)-(4.51), PDF p. 116-117: the equations of absolute and relative motion and the ratios \(U/Z\) and \(W/Z\), CK remarks that \(U/Z\) equals \(\mathrm{TR}\) (PDF p. 117). The proof in five steps (with Lagrange's equations in the relative coordinate) is ours.

An example: the same machine \(B\) on the same mounts (\(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz) stands in a building whose floor shakes with the amplitude \(Y = 0{,}1\) mm at 25 Hz (this is \(r = 5\)). By theorem 9.68 the absolute displacement of the machine has the amplitude \(\mathrm{TR}\cdot Y\), which is \(4{,}657\) \(\mu\)m at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) and \(9{,}285\) \(\mu\)m at \(\zeta = 0{,}2\) (the values of \(\mathrm{TR}\) from example 9.67). The mounts thus protect the machine from the shaking of the floor as they protect the floor from the machine. The relative displacement, that is, the compression of the mounts, is \(r^2 D\cdot Y = 104{,}1\) \(\mu\)m (\(\zeta = 0{,}05\)), almost as much as the displacement of the floor: the machine is at rest, the floor moves under it.

9.8.5 Seismic instruments

Theorem 9.68 also tells how we measure motion. A seismic transducer (potresni merilnik) is a mass on a spring and a damper in a casing that we fix firmly to the body whose motion we measure. The casing moves with the body, so its displacement is \(y(t)\), and the instrument shows the relative displacement \(z\) of the mass with respect to the casing (CK, PDF p. 120). For a harmonic motion \(y = Y\cos\Omega t\) the amplitude of what the instrument shows is, by theorem 9.68, \(r^2 D(r)\,Y\). Figure 9.32 shows this function. What the instrument measures depends on \(r\), that is, on the natural frequency of the instrument relative to the frequency of the motion.

A vibrometer (vibrometer) is to show the displacement of the body. For \(r \gg 1\) we have \(r^2 D = 1/\sqrt{(1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u}\) with \(u = 1/r^2 \to 0\), so \(r^2 D \to 1\). Then \(H \approx -1/(m\Omega^2)\), because \(-m\Omega^2\) dominates in the denominator \(k - m\Omega^2 + i c\Omega\), and from step 3 of the proof of theorem 9.68 it follows that \(z \approx -Y\cos\Omega t = -y\). The mass is at rest, the body moves under it, and the pointer shows the displacement of the body with the opposite sign. At \(\zeta = 0\) we have \(r^2 D = 1{,}3333\), \(1{,}1250\), \(1{,}0417\) at \(r = 2\), \(3\), \(5\), and at \(\zeta = 0{,}7\) we have \(0{,}9747\), \(0{,}9961\), \(1{,}0000\). The price is a soft spring and a large mass: for a motion with the frequency 5 Hz and \(r = 5\) we have \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 1\) Hz, so by equation (9.59) \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = 248{,}5\) mm. Vibrometers are therefore rarely used, except in some seismic measurements (CK, PDF p. 121).

An accelerometer (merilnik pospeška) is to show the acceleration of the body \(\ddot y = -\Omega^2 Y\cos\Omega t\), whose amplitude is \(A = \Omega^2 Y\). For \(r \ll 1\) we have \(H \approx 1/k\) and \(z \approx m\Omega^2 Y\cos\Omega t/k = -\ddot y/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), so \(z\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 \approx -\ddot y\). The instrument is a good accelerometer if this ratio is the same for all frequencies. Exactly, the ratio between the amplitude of the reading \(z\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) and the true amplitude of the acceleration is \(r^2 D\,Y\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2/(\Omega^2 Y) = D(r)\). At \(\zeta = 0\) we have \(D = 1{,}0101\), \(1{,}0417\), \(1{,}1905\), \(1{,}5625\) at \(r = 0{,}1\), \(0{,}2\), \(0{,}4\), \(0{,}6\), so five percent error at \(r \approx 0{,}22\). At \(\zeta = 0{,}7\) the values are \(1{,}00015\), \(1{,}00000\), \(0{,}99054\), \(0{,}94694\). The error is then at \(r = 0{,}6\) only \(5{,}3\,\%\), so the range of use widens to about \(0{,}6\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (CK, PDF p. 122).

Why exactly \(\zeta \approx 0{,}7\)? The square of the denominator of \(D\) is \((1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 r^2 = 1 + (4\zeta^2 - 2)\,r^2 + r^4\). If \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt2 = 0{,}7071\), the term with \(r^2\) vanishes and \(D = 1/\sqrt{1 + r^4}\), which for small \(r\) equals \(1 - r^4/2\) up to the fourth power. The deviation from one is then slow, because there is no term with \(r^2\). At \(\zeta = 0{,}7\) the term with \(r^2\) is only \(-0{,}04\,r^2\). At \(\zeta = 0\) this term is \(-2\,r^2\) and \(D \approx 1 + r^2\), so the error grows as \(r^2\).

A good accelerometer must, in addition, delay all components of the motion by the same time and amplify them by the same factor, otherwise it distorts the shape of the record (CK, PDF p. 121). We have checked the amplitudes, we do not compute the delays here.

What remains open here goes elsewhere. The balancing of the rotor, which reduces the unbalance \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\), and the crankshaft are in chapter 23, and the bending vibration of rotating shafts with their critical speeds in chapter 24. The torsional vibration of a disc on a shaft has the same equation as machine \(B\) with the moment of inertia and the torsional stiffness in place of \(M\) and \(k\) (section 9.1).
