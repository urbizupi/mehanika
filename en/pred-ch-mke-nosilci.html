Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

Chapter 12Lecture: Beam and frame finite elements

1 A beam element has a deflection and a rotation at each node

For the bar we described each piece of the structure by one number per node, the displacement. Now we have a beam, which bends. The question for today: why does a piece of a beam need two numbers at each node, and what do the sixteen numbers of its stiffness matrix mean?

Let us draw the axis of the beam, straight and grey dashed, as it is before loading. On it we mark three nodes, with the pieces (1) and (2) between them. A node (vozlišče) is the point where two pieces meet. The Slovenian name comes from vozel, the knot, because that is where we tie the pieces together. Now we bend the beam. Let each piece be a straight line as for the bar, and let the neighbouring pieces share only the deflection (poves) of the middle node. We get an orange tent, two straight lines meeting at the middle node. What is wrong with this picture? At the middle node piece (1) has its own slope and piece (2) another. The deflection has a kink (pregib) there.

On the right we draw the same axis with the same nodes, but this time the pieces also share the slope. The deflection is a smooth curve through the same points. At the middle node both pieces have one single tangent. The angle between the horizontal and this tangent is the rotation \(\varphi\), the distance from the axis to the curve is the deflection \(v\). These are the two numbers of the node.

The claim in one sentence: a beam stores energy by curving, so neighbouring pieces share deflection and slope, every node carries two numbers \((v, \varphi)\), the field on a piece is a polynomial of degree three made of four Hermite functions, and column \(j\) of the stiffness matrix is the four end forces and couples that hold the piece in the shape where number \(j\) is 1 and the others 0. Each part of this claim is one question "why". Let us take them in order.

1.1 Why is the deflection of the node not enough?

What happens to the curvature where the two straight lines meet? Let us look at the kink up close. Left of the node the beam points along the first blue line, right of it along the second. The direction turns by a finite angle over zero length. Curvature (ukrivljenost) tells how much the direction turns per unit length. A finite angle divided by zero length is no number at all. The curvature there grows without bound.

Why is that fatal? The energy of a beam is \(\tfrac12\int EI\,(v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and for small slopes the second derivative \(v''\) is exactly the curvature. If we round off the kink over a short length and shrink that length, the energy goes to infinity. In the book: 12.1.1.

Where is the ground of this argument? In the hand. We can bend a steel ruler into an arc, but we cannot get it into a sharp corner with our hands without bending it for good. So the kink is not a state a beam can be in, while the smooth curve is. For it to be smooth, the tangent must be shared by both pieces. Its slope therefore cannot be something each piece chooses for itself. It must become a number of the node that the pieces share, just as they share the deflection. This is how we get the rotation \(\varphi\) next to the deflection \(v\).

Why the name rotation (zasuk)? Because it tells how much the cross-section of the beam turns at the node. A first assumption hides in that sentence. Let us draw it.

Again we start with the straight axis and draw one cross-section on it, an upright line across the beam. We bend the beam into the orange curve. The section goes with it and stays perpendicular to the bent axis. At this point the axis has a tangent with slope \(v'\). Since the section is perpendicular to the tangent, it turns away from the vertical by the same angle. So \(\varphi = v'\). This is the Euler-Bernoulli beam model.

Why is that right here? The section could lag behind the perpendicular by an angle \(\gamma\). That is shear deformation (strižna deformacija). In a slender beam it is small: for our flat bar \(20 \times 40\) mm, 1 m long, the shear part of the deflection is only 0,125 % of the bending part. When does it fail? For a short, stubby beam. For the same bar 80 mm long the shear part is already 19,5 %. What does it do to the result? An element that does not know the angle \(\gamma\) is too stiff by that share. The third part of the lecture lifts this assumption. In the book: 12.7.5.

1.2 Why a polynomial of degree three?

How many numbers does one piece have now? Let us count. Two ends, at each a deflection and a rotation, four conditions in all. A straight line has two coefficients, a parabola three, a polynomial of degree three four. One coefficient per condition, so a polynomial of degree three. Let us write it so that each number of a node switches on its own part of the field, as for the bar.

Let us take this element from \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) to \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\), drawn as a dashed line that is also the value zero. The first shape is \(H_1\). At the left end it has the value 1, at the right end 0, and a horizontal slope at both ends. It is the shape in which only the left node rises. The second is \(H_2\). Both values are zero, but at the left end it has slope 1. In this shape the left node only turns. \(H_2\) carries the unit of length because it turns a rotation, which has no unit, into a deflection. \(H_3\) and \(H_4\) do the same for the right node.

Each shape switches on exactly one of the four numbers and keeps the other three at zero. So the field of the element is

\[v_{\mathrm{h}} = v_1 H_1 + \varphi_1 H_2 + v_2 H_3 + \varphi_2 H_4 .\]

We call them Hermite functions (Hermitove funkcije) because they are an interpolation that prescribes values and derivatives at the ends, and that is Hermite interpolation. In the book: 12.1, 12.2.

1.3 What does a column of the stiffness matrix mean?

The stiffness matrix of the element turns the four numbers of the nodes into four forces and couples at the ends. What does one of its columns mean? Let us do an experiment.

Let us draw the element in its position before the displacement, grey dashed. We lift the left node by \(v_1 = 1\) and hold everything else at zero. The right node stays in place, both ends stay horizontal. The element bends into the shape \(H_1\). What must the surroundings do to hold it like that? It lifts the left node with the force \(12EI/h^3\), pushes the right one down with the same force, and at both ends it acts with the couple \(6EI/h^2\) counterclockwise. A couple (dvojica) is a pair of equal and opposite forces that turns a piece but does not shift it. The force \(12EI/h^3\) for the displacement 1 is a stiffness (togost), the force per unit of displacement. A stiffer piece needs a larger force for the same displacement. These four values, force, couple, force, couple, are the first column of the matrix.

How much is that for our piece, \(h = 1\) m and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², when lifted by 1 m? The force is 250 880 N, the couple 125 440 N m.

Below we instead turn the left node by \(\varphi_1 = 1\). The element bends into the shape \(H_2\). The surroundings now need the couple \(4EI/h\) at the left node, that is 83 627 N m, the couple \(2EI/h\) at the right one, 41 813 N m, and the force \(6EI/h^2\), that is 125 440 N, up at the left and down at the right. That is the second column. The third and fourth are the same for the right node. In the book: 12.1.3, (12.3).

1.4 Why do the energy route and the equilibrium route agree?

We get the matrix in two ways. From the energy as \(k_{\mathrm{ij}} = EI\int H_{\mathrm{i}}''H_{\mathrm{j}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\), or from equilibrium, with the forces that really hold the piece. Why do they give the same? Let us look at the upper piece once more. Only the forces and the couples at the ends act on it, there is nothing along the piece.

Why is the Hermite field then the true deflection? Let us draw the proof.

Let us take the element on which nothing acts along its length. We cut it anywhere at two places and draw the piece between the cuts on its own. The neighbouring parts act on it with a shear force and a moment. The piece is at rest because we apply the load slowly. In a vibration the piece accelerates and this argument does not hold. Since it is at rest, by Newton the vertical forces on it balance, the shear force \(V\) is the same in both cuts. Since we put the cuts anywhere, \(V\) is constant along the whole element. Now the moments about the left cut: the moments \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) differ by exactly the moment of the force \(V\) at the right cut, and that grows in proportion to the distance between the cuts. So the moment is linear. The curvature \(v'' = M/EI\) is therefore linear and the deflection is a polynomial of degree three. But there is only one polynomial of degree three with four given values, exactly \(H_1\). For a piece without load the Hermite field is not an approximation, it is the true deflection. In the book: steps 6 and 7 of the proof of 12.4.

1.5 When does the element store no energy?

Does every motion of the nodes need a force? Let us try two. We draw the position before the motion. We lift the element as a whole by \(a\), say by 1 cm. Nothing curves, so no force is needed. On the right we turn it about the left node by the angle \(b\), say 0,01, and the right node rises by \(bh\). Again nothing curves and again all four forces and couples are zero. These are the only motions without energy, the rigid body motions (togi gibanji). In the book: 12.5.

1.6 What does all this stand on?

Four statements with no further "why" behind them:

  1. We cannot bend a ruler into a sharp corner, a kink is not a state of a beam.
  2. Counting: four conditions, four coefficients, four shapes.
  3. The meaning of the word stiffness: force per unit of displacement. One unit shape, four forces and couples at the ends.
  4. Newton: a piece on which nothing acts between two cuts carries the same shear force in both cuts, like the upper piece.

1.7 How do we build the element from the ground?

From the first statement a node carries a deflection and a rotation. From the second there are four shapes, one per number. From the third we hold one shape and read the four forces and couples at the ends, which is one column. Four columns are the matrix. From the fourth these are the true forces for a piece without load. Let us return to the first picture. The shared tangent and its rotation \(\varphi\) are exactly the number the bar element did not have.

1.8 The geometric view

Whoever has only a few minutes for the explanation should look at the tent and the smooth curve side by side. The tent has two slopes at the node, the curve one. That one slope is the second number of the node, everything else follows from counting. This view adds the reason for the second number without a single equation.

1.9 The view by hand

Let us take a ruler in both hands. We hold the right end still and horizontal and lift the left end without tilting it. The ruler bends like \(H_1\). We feel that the hands do not only push but also twist. Without twisting the ends would tilt. This view adds why even a pure lift has the couples \(6EI/h^2\) in the matrix.

1.10 The view by numbers

On the upper piece there are 250 880 N up and 250 880 N down. The forces balance, \(F_3 = -F_1\). Moments about the left node: the force on the right with the lever arm 1 m gives \(-250\,880\) N m, the couples give \(2 \cdot 125\,440 = 250\,880\) N m. The sum is zero, the piece is in equilibrium, as it must be. Below, the couple on the left, 83 627 N m, is twice the couple on the right, 41 813 N m. This view adds the sizes and a check of equilibrium.

1.11 The algebraic view

The entry \(k_{\mathrm{ij}} = EI\int H_{\mathrm{i}}''H_{\mathrm{j}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\) tells how much the curvatures of the shapes \(H_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{j}}\) overlap. The two indices in the product can be swapped, so the matrix is symmetric. In the picture of the columns this shows as the same value \(6EI/h^2\) for the couple in the first column and the force in the second. This view adds the symmetry, seen without any calculation. In the book: 12.4.

1.12 Which assumptions did we make?

  1. Euler-Bernoulli, the section stays perpendicular to the axis and \(\varphi = v'\). Right for slender beams, the shear part is 0,125 % at a length of 1 m. It fails for short ones, 19,5 % at 80 mm. Effect: the element is too stiff by that share, the third part of the lecture repairs it.
  2. \(EI\) is constant on the piece. Right when the section does not change between the nodes, our bar is \(20 \times 40\) mm everywhere. Where the section changes, we put a node there. Effect: \(EI\) comes out of the integral and the matrix has a closed form, the values on the arrows.
  3. There is no load along the piece. Then only forces and couples at the ends act and the polynomial of degree three is the true deflection. Under the self-weight of the cantilever it is not any more. The deflection at the nodes stays exact, but the clamp moment from the field is 17 % too small with one element. That is the second part of the lecture.
  4. The rotations are small, so the slope \(v'\) stands for the angle. We took this over from chapter 5. In the picture the angle is strongly exaggerated.

A frame is this element together with the bar, both turned into the direction of the beam, and the mass matrix and the geometric stiffness use the same Hermite field. In the book: 12.4, 12.5, 12.9, 12.10.

2 Loads into the nodes and why the nodes are exact

Let us take a steel flat bar \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m long, and clamp one end into a wall. Nothing loads it except its own weight. The true deflection of this cantilever is a polynomial of degree four. The beam element of the previous part can only do degree three. One element therefore cannot hit this curve. And yet the tip deflection comes out exact, \(-0{,}3660\) mm, while the moment at the wall is 17 % too small. Is that luck?

First we draw the wall the cantilever is clamped into, at \(x = 0\). A single element has two nodes, at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = 1\) m. Everything we draw is measured from the horizontal zero line. Now we draw by how much the deflection of the finite element differs from the true one. This is the error of the deflection. Look at its ends: at both nodes the error is zero. It is largest in the middle, \(-7{,}6\) µm. Below we draw the moment. The true moment is a parabola. The moment we compute from the curvature of the field is a straight line from \(-25{,}51\) to \(5{,}10\) N m. At the wall we also put an orange dot at \(-30{,}61\) N m, which lies exactly on the parabola. We will compute that dot in a different way at the end.

The claim of this part is one: if we replace the load by forces and couples at the nodes that do the same work, the deflection and the rotation at every node are exact for any load. The error lives only inside the elements, and the true internal forces are read from the end forces of the elements, not from the curvature of the field. This claim holds five things that are not self-evident. Each is the next question why.

2.1 How does a load along the element get to the nodes?

How do we tell the system of equations about a load that lies between the nodes? The system knows only numbers at the nodes, so the load must be translated for it. We draw the element between node 1 and node 2. Its length is \(h\). On it acts a uniform load \(q\), a force per metre of length. What do we put at the nodes instead?

The answer comes from the word work. The work of a force is the force times the displacement of its point along the force. The work of a couple is its moment times the angle it turns through. We demand: the equivalent forces and couples must do the same work as the load on every shape the element can take. The element can take four Hermite shapes, so we get four numbers. We draw the same element without the load. At node 1 we put a force \(q\,h/2\), at node 2 the same force \(q\,h/2\). Each node carries half the load, as with the bar. Now comes something new: at node 1 a couple \(q\,h^2/12\), at node 2 a couple of the same size in the opposite sense. In the figure the load points up. For a load pointing down, \(q = -1000\) N/m, and \(h = 1\) m all four arrows turn round: the forces are \(500\) N each downward, and the couples are \(83{,}33\) N m each. These forces are called consistent (konsistentne sile), because in work they agree with the load. In the book: 12.6 and (12.5).

2.2 Why couples, when the load does not twist?

The load does not twist anything. So where does a couple come from? We draw the element and on it only one shape, \(H_2\). This is the shape in which node 1 is rotated by one unit, the tangent with slope 1, and everything else stays zero. Look at what the element does: to the right of node 1 it rises. A load pointing down resists this rise, so it does work on this shape. Work on the rotation of a node can only be done at that node by a couple. Hence the couple. The forces come in the same way from the shapes \(H_1\) and \(H_3\), which lift one node by one unit. At node 2 the element goes down when the node rotates, the work has the opposite sign, and so the second couple is reversed. Under a uniform load and with elements of equal length, at an inner node the couples of the two neighbouring elements cancel, because they are equal in size and opposite.

2.3 Why is everything exact when loads act only at nodes?

When does the mesh hit the true deflection everywhere, not only at the nodes? We start with the easiest case. We draw a cantilever clamped at \(x = 0\), with the nodes \(x_0\), \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), so with two elements. The only load is a unit upward force at the node \(x_1\). Along the elements there is nothing. What is then the true deflection of each element? We know it from the first part of the lecture: a piece with nothing acting on it between the cuts has a constant shear force, a linear moment and therefore a cubic deflection. This is exactly the shape of a finite element. The mesh can therefore take the true deflection.

Could the solution of the mesh be some other cubic deflection? No. The same ruler under the same weight sags the same way every time. A beam with given supports and loads has a single deflection. The solution of the mesh meets every condition of this problem: the rows of the system are the balance of forces and couples at the nodes, and the cubic pieces are the balance of the elements. So the difference below is zero, along the whole beam, not only at the nodes. In the book: 12.13, uniqueness 12.12.

2.4 Why does the node stay exact under a load along the element?

Now the harder case, the cantilever under its own weight of the first figure. Here the true deflection is not cubic and the mesh cannot take it. So how is the node exact? The trick is to write the nodal value as a work.

The deflection of the node \(x_1\) equals the work that a unit force at \(x_1\) does on that deflection. One times the deflection is the deflection. So we measure the error at the node by letting the unit force do work on it. This work can also be written differently: as the work of the moments of the deflection under the unit force on the curvature of the error. That form is symmetric, since it holds only \(EI\) times the curvature of one times the curvature of the other, so it is the same as the work of the moments of the error on the curvature of that deflection.

And what does the system of equations say? It has one row for each shape of the mesh, and each row says: the moments of the error do no work on this shape. The previous question showed that the deflection under a unit force at a node is one of the shapes of the mesh. So the error does no work on it. The work of the unit force on the error is therefore zero, and the error at the node is zero. For the rotation we do the same with a unit couple. In the book: 12.14, (12.9).

Where does this proof stop? We put the unit force at a point \(P\) inside the second element, at \(x = 0{,}75\) m. Its deflection has a jump of the shear force at \(P\) and is not cubic on the element. The mesh cannot take it. The field of the mesh with the same values at the nodes parts from it. The difference on the second element is an arch that is zero at the nodes. At the point \(P\) it is \(0{,}46\) % of the deflection. Exactness is a property of the nodes, not of every point.

2.5 Where is the error then, and how do we get the true internal forces?

The nodes are exact, so the error is inside the element. What does it look like? We draw one element with a straight axis and on it the load \(q\) pointing down. The ends of the element are the nodes \(a\) and \(b\). The true deflection \(v\) passes through their exact values. The slopes at the ends are exact too.

The Hermite field we draw below, on the same nodes. It has the same values and the same slopes, because the nodes are exact. In between it is cubic and misses the true curve. What is missing? The difference has value zero and slope zero at both ends. That is exactly a piece clamped at both ends. We load it with the same load. It sags into the arch \(v_0\). In the middle the arch is largest, \(q\,h^4/(384\,EI)\). This is the error of the field. For the cantilever under its own weight it was \(-7{,}6\) µm.

Now the forces. The clamps hold the piece with the forces \(q\,h/2\) and the couples \(q\,h^2/12\). Compare them with the consistent forces: the same sizes, the opposite senses. This is not a coincidence, it holds for every load. The forces that hold the true element are therefore the forces of the Hermite field, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the forces of the clamps, \(-\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). Together \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). In the book: 12.15.

For the cantilever under its own weight this gives at the wall \(M(0) = -30{,}61\) N m, the orange dot on the parabola. The line of the field differs from it by \(q\,h^2/12 = -5{,}10\) N m, because the moment of the field is only the curvature of the cubic part, without the arch \(v_0\). Hence the 17 %. In the book: 12.16, (12.10).

2.6 Back up

Now we build everything from the ground. The work of a force is force times displacement, the work of a couple is moment times rotation: this gives the consistent forces and couples. A piece with nothing acting between two cuts sags into a cubic, and a beam has a single deflection: so with loads at the nodes the difference is zero. A unit force at a node is a load at a node, its deflection is a shape of the mesh, the error does no work on it: the node is exact. Back to the first figure. The two zeros of the error are the theorem. The bar in the middle is the arch of the clamped piece that remains. The dot against the line says why we read moments from the end forces.

2.7 What does a finer mesh buy?

One more view, by numbers. We take a simply supported beam, \(L = 2\) m, \(q = -1\) kN/m, and look at the first element of two, between the nodes \(x_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}} = 1\) m, each part from its own zero line. The true deflection and the deflection of the field barely part. The error is an arch, largest in the middle. Below, the true moment is a parabola, and the moment of the field is a straight line that shows \(83{,}33\) N m at the support, although the moment there is zero. Only the orange dots are exact, \(0\) and \(500\) N m. What happens when we halve the elements? We write it on the board:

\(n\) gap of the field moment at \(L/2\) (\(q\,h^2/12\), N m) deflection error at the middle of an element (mm)
2 83,33 \(-0{,}124562\)
4 20,83 \(-0{,}007785\)
8 5,21 \(-0{,}000487\)

The deflection error falls by a factor of 16 at every halving, because it grows with \(h^4\). The moment gap falls by a factor of 4, because it grows with \(h^2\). A finer mesh therefore buys the field between the nodes and its moments. It does not need to buy the nodes, which are exact already with two elements.

2.8 Assumptions: when this holds and what it costs if not

First assumption: the nodal forces are consistent, with couples. What if we leave the couples out? We draw the beam on two supports with the nodes of two elements. The forces \(500\), \(1000\) and \(500\) N are in both systems. The couples, \(83{,}33\) N m at the supports and zero at the inner node, are only in the consistent system. On the right we draw the axes: the number of elements \(n\) and the ratio \(\rho\), the midspan deflection divided by the true one. Consistent forces give \(\rho = 1\) at every \(n\). Lumped forces give \(0{,}80\) with two elements, \(0{,}95\) with four, \(0{,}9875\) with eight. Why? The lumped system is a different beam with loads at the nodes, the mesh solves it exactly, but it is not our beam. Effect: without the couples we lose the exactness of the nodes and keep only convergence. In the book: 12.3.4.

Second assumption: the stiffness \(EI\) is constant on every element, and a node stands at every support and at every change of section. Why is it needed? Only then is the deflection under a unit force at a node cubic on every element and a shape of the mesh. When does it fail? For a cantilever whose stiffness grows linearly, \(EI\,(1 + x/L)\), with a force at the tip. We write the effect on the board:

\(n\) tip deflection (mm)
1 \(-6{,}4389\)
2 \(-6{,}5124\)
4 \(-6{,}5187\)
true \(-6{,}5192\)

Exactness becomes an approximation that improves quickly with halving. If a jump of the stiffness lies inside an element and we take the average stiffness, the tip deflection is 18,5 % too large. With a node at the jump it is exact again. In the book: 12.3.2.

Third assumption: the point we look at is a node. Inside an element the theorem does not hold, there the error is \(0{,}46\) % at the point \(P\).

Fourth: exact means exact for the Euler-Bernoulli model, in which the rotation of the section equals the slope of the axis. For our bar, 1 m long, the shear part of the tip deflection is 0,125 % of the bending part, so the model is good here. The zeros of the error are zeros with respect to the model, not with respect to real steel.

The same procedure for internal forces, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\) on every element, is used for frames, where the element only gets a rotation of its axis in addition. In the book: 12.4.

3 Shear locking and one Gauss point

Take the same steel flat bar as before, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and shear stiffness \(kGA = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N. We clamp it at the left end and hang \(500\) N on the free end. Now we compute it with the simplest element that also knows shear. What do we get?

First we draw the clamp at the left end and the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(1\) m. We have stretched it vertically: \(1\) mm of deflection is half a centimetre on the drawing, a metre of length only \(8\) cm. The true deflection drops by \(7{,}982\) mm at the end. Almost on top of it lies the Euler-Bernoulli deflection, because shear is small in such a long bar. Now the finite element solutions. One element lies practically on the axis. Only the magnification below shows that the end goes down by \(0{,}0396\) mm, which is \(0{,}00497\) of the true value. Four elements give \(0{,}590\) mm, sixteen \(4{,}478\) mm, still only \(0{,}56\) of the true value.

For a short cantilever, \(L = 80\) mm, the same element does work, as we will soon see on a graph. Same steel, same cross-section, same element. Only the slenderness differs. That the element stays on the axis is called shear locking (strižno zaklepanje): the element is too stiff because shear does not let it bend. And stranger still: the locking disappears when we compute the shear part of the matrix with one Gauss point instead of two. Why?

The claim we will build: in a slender beam the shear stiffness \(kGA\) acts like a very stiff spring that holds the shear strain \(\gamma = v' - \theta\) at zero wherever it is measured. An element made of straight lines, measured at two points, can therefore only stay straight, like one element in the figure. Measured at one point, the middle, it can bend.

3.1 What is different in the Timoshenko element?

What must an element know in order to know shear at all? In the Euler-Bernoulli element the rotation of the section was the derivative of the deflection. In Timoshenko's model the tilt of the section (nagib prereza) \(\theta\) is an unknown of its own. Let us look at one element with chosen numbers.

We draw the undeformed axis of the element and on it the nodes 1 and 2. The deflection \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a straight line between the nodes, raised on the right by \(v_2\). At every point stands a section, turned by \(\theta_{\mathrm{h}}\), which grows linearly from node 1 to node 2, from \(0{,}05\) to \(0{,}45\). Why straight lines? Because the energy of a Timoshenko beam holds only first derivatives of \(v\) and \(\theta\), it is enough that both fields are continuous, a kink is allowed. The simplest such field is a straight line, as for the bar.

Below, above the axis \(x\), we draw the two quantities we will compare. The slope of the axis \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is one single number, because a straight line has one slope. The tilt of the section \(\theta_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a line that grows. The vertical distance between them is the shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}} = v_{\mathrm{h}}' - \theta_{\mathrm{h}}\): how far the section is from perpendicular to the axis. Here it is \(0{,}15\) at node 1 and \(-0{,}25\) at node 2. In the book: 12.7.2, definition 12.31.

3.2 Why can a straight-line element not bend without shear?

What does bending mean at all? The sections spread like a fan: the tilt \(\theta\) changes along the piece. Let us draw this on one element of length \(6\) cm.

We draw the same picture twice, in two columns that will differ only at the Gauss points. In grey dashes we draw the undeformed axis, then the deformed axis between nodes 1 and 2. The axis is a straight line with slope \(0{,}2\). We draw the sections in orange: the first stands upright, the middle one is tilted by \(0{,}2\), the last by \(0{,}4\). That is bending. We chose the slope of the axis equal to the mean tilt, \(0{,}2\), so that the element shears as little as possible.

Now, below, above the axis \(x\) between the ends of the element, we draw the shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\). At the left end it is \(0{,}2 - 0 = 0{,}2\), in the middle \(0{,}2 - 0{,}2 = 0\), at the right end \(0{,}2 - 0{,}4 = -0{,}2\). Could we have chosen another slope of the axis and avoided the shear? No: the slope of the axis is one number, while the tilt changes. One number cannot equal a changing tilt everywhere. Bending of an element made of straight lines therefore always carries shear that the truly bent beam does not have. In the book: 12.8.3.

3.3 Why is this fatal for a slender beam?

Shear costs energy. How much? First let us recall how a very stiff spring works.

We draw the inclined support and the direction \(\vek n\) perpendicular to it, with the direction \(\vek t\) along it, then the end of the frame with node \(C\). Between the support and the node stands a spring of stiffness \(\alpha\). At a displacement \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) it stores the energy \(\tfrac12\,\alpha\,u_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). When \(\alpha\) is very large, every displacement is expensive and the penetration, the distance from the position of the node with the spring unstressed to the node, stays small, but not zero. This is experience: a very stiff spring can hardly be compressed. This is how the penalty method (kazenska metoda) replaces a constraint, "\(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) shall be zero". In the book: 12.6.3, definition 12.27.

In the Timoshenko element the shear energy is \(\tfrac12\,kGA\int\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). It has the same form: the square of what should be zero, times a large stiffness. The shear stiffness \(kGA\) is the penalty number, the stiffness of the spring, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) is its stretch. How large is this bill for our cantilever \(L = 1\) m with one element? Let us draw what the element would have to do to hit the true deflection and the true tilt at the end.

Over the axis \(x\) with nodes 1 and 2 we draw the slope of the axis. A straight line has one slope, and the true deflection \(7{,}982\) mm over the length \(1\) m gives \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = -0{,}007982\). The tilt of the section grows from zero at the clamp to the true tilt at the end. The difference between them is the shear strain: at node 1 it is \(-0{,}007982\), at node 2 it is \(+0{,}003976\). The true shear strain of this cantilever is \(-9{,}95 \cdot 10^{-6}\), between \(400\) and \(800\) times smaller than these two distances. In the figure it would be thinner than a line. What does such shear cost? On the right, over a common zero line, we draw two bars to the same scale. The shear energy of such a field is \(400{,}2\) J. The work of the force \(500\) N on the deflection \(7{,}982\) mm is only \(2{,}0\) J, a bar we can hardly see.

A field that costs \(200\) times more than the force can pay for is not what the element chooses. It bends far less. In the book: 12.7.4, lemma 12.35.

When is the penalty large? What decides is the ratio of the bending and the shear flexibility of a piece of length \(h\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{h}} = 12\,EI/(kGA\,h^2)\) (equation (12.40)). A slender piece means a small \(\beta_{\mathrm{h}}\). Let us look at the graph.

Horizontally we draw the slenderness \(1/\beta_{\mathrm{L}}\), growing to the right, vertically the ratio \(r\) of the deflection with one element to the true one. The blue curve is the element with two points. On the left, for short and thick beams, it is close to one, then it drops steeply towards zero. The cantilever \(L = 80\) mm has \(1/\beta_{\mathrm{L}} = 1{,}28\) and ratio \(0{,}438\), still on the steep part. The cantilever \(L = 1\) m has \(1/\beta_{\mathrm{L}} = 200\) and ratio \(0{,}00497\), almost zero. The more slender the beam, the stiffer the spring \(kGA\) compared with bending, and the more the element locks. In the book: 12.8.4, theorem 12.39.

3.4 Why do two points leave nothing and one point leaves bending?

Where does the penalty see the shear strain at all? The computer does not evaluate the integral \(\int\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) everywhere. It takes the values of \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) at the Gauss points and adds them with weights. So the penalty sees only those points. Let us draw what that means for a straight line.

We start on the left. We draw the axis \(x\) and the ends of the element. On the axis we put the two Gauss points \(g_1\) and \(g_2\), at \(0{,}2113\,h\) and \(0{,}7887\,h\). Now we take the sloping shear strain from the previous figure, the one that bending carries. At both points it has a value other than zero, \(+0{,}1155\) and \(-0{,}1155\), and the penalty counts them with \(kGA \cdot 0{,}1155^2\). Which line is zero at both points? Through two points goes one single line, so it is the axis itself: \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) everywhere. But zero shear strain everywhere means that the tilt equals the slope of the axis everywhere, so it is constant. That is not bending. We saw the same two values in the previous figure as the blue dots in the left column.

Now the right, again the axis \(x\) and the ends of the element, but only one point \(g\), in the middle. Through one point goes a whole fan of lines. Each of them is zero in the middle and the penalty leaves it alone. Among them is the sloping line of bending from the previous figure. One point only asks that the slope of the axis equals the mean tilt of the section, equation (12.41). In the previous figure this is the blue dot in the middle of the right column, where \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) is zero.

Let us count on the whole beam too, as we would count equations on the board. A cantilever with \(n\) elements has \(2n\) unknowns, a deflection and a tilt at every free node.

  • Two points: two zeros per element, \(2n\) constraints on \(2n\) unknowns. Nothing is left, the beam is locked.
  • One point: one zero per element, \(n\) constraints on \(2n\) unknowns. \(n\) numbers stay free, the tilts, and the deflection follows their integral.

One zero per element is exactly the Euler-Bernoulli relation \(v' = \theta\), satisfied on average on every element. In the book: 12.8.2, 12.8.3, lemmas 12.36 and 12.37.

3.5 Is this cheating?

We computed the shear part less accurately. What did we pay for it? Let us go back to the slenderness graph.

The orange curve is the element with one point. It does not fall towards zero but towards the value \(0{,}75\), below the true value \(1\). At \(L = 80\) mm it gives \(0{,}791\), at \(L = 1\) m \(0{,}750\). So one element does not lock, but it is not exact either. What happens with more elements?

Horizontally we draw the number of elements \(n\), vertically the ratio \(r\). The blue line of two points stays below \(0{,}08\) up to \(n = 4\) and reaches \(0{,}95\) only at \(64\) elements. The orange one of one point starts at \(0{,}75\) and from \(n = 8\) on is above \(0{,}996\). The dots are the solutions, at \(n = 1, 2, 4\) the ratios \(0{,}750\), \(0{,}938\) and \(0{,}984\). Exactly \(1/(4n^2)\) of the bending part of the deflection, \(P\,L^3/(3\,EI)\), is missing. The error, the distance of the orange dots from the value \(1\), is \(P\,L^3/(12\,EI\,n^2)\): \(1{,}993\) mm with one element, \(0{,}498\) mm with two. It falls like \(h^2\) and does not depend on \(kGA\), so not on the slenderness. It is an ordinary discretization error, not locking (equation (12.45)).

Where exactly does this error come from? The one-point element is an exact piece of a beam whose shear flexibility is smaller by \(h^2/(12\,EI)\), that is \(1/kGA - h^2/(12\,EI)\). For a slender piece this flexibility is even negative, so it is no real beam, the statement holds only for the equations of the element. If we correct it with the substitute shear stiffness (nadomestna strižna togost) \(kGA^*\) of equation (12.44), we get the blackwhite dashed line at \(1\) for every \(n\). With two elements the deflection with one point is \(-7{,}4836\) mm, with the substitute stiffness \(-7{,}9819\) mm, exactly the true value. In the book: 12.8.5, theorem 12.41.

And what did we lose? The one-point element is no longer the Ritz method for the true energy. So the energy estimate and the bound that the deflection is not too large are not proved for it. For the cantilever, equation (12.45) shows that the orange dots always stay below \(1\), but we have no general bound. In the book: 12.8.6.

3.6 The ground and the way back

Where did we reach the bottom? At four things that need no further reason:

  • a straight line has one slope (the meaning of the word),
  • through two points goes one line, through one point infinitely many (the geometry of a ruler),
  • a very stiff spring hardly gives, what it guards stays near zero (experience),
  • the measured numbers \(0{,}00497\) and \(0{,}56\) of the test.

Now we build back up. Bending spreads the sections, a straight element has one slope, so it carries a false shear. In a slender beam the spring \(kGA\) does not allow it. With two measuring points only the zero line is allowed, there is no bending and the beam stays on the axis like one element. With one point a whole fan is allowed and bending is free. In the first figure the four and sixteen elements slowly peel off the axis. Why, if with two points there are always as many constraints as unknowns? Because the piece is shorter: \(\beta_{\mathrm{h}}\) grows like \(n^2\) and the spring becomes weaker and weaker compared with the bending of the piece.

3.7 A deck of cards

The shortest explanation is a deck of cards. When we bend the deck, the cards slide one over another, that is shear between the layers. The sections are such cards. If we glue the cards, which corresponds to a very large \(kGA\), they cannot slide, and the deck can bend only with the cards staying perpendicular to the axis. An element made of straight lines cannot do that, because its axis has one slope. A glued deck made of such pieces stays straight. What does this view add? That locking is not a property of the steel but a limit on the shapes the element can take.

3.8 The same spring elsewhere

The spring on the inclined support and the shear at the Gauss points are the same mechanism: a large stiffness holds some quantity near zero. If there are as many such constraints as unknowns, nothing is left and the solution locks. We will meet the same in plates and in nearly incompressible bodies, and we met it already with the hanging bar in the chapter on the weak form. What does this view add? Locking is not a peculiarity of beams but one mechanism, which we always check by counting constraints and unknowns.

3.9 Assumptions

What did we assume and what does it cost?

  • Straight lines for \(v\) and \(\theta\). Why: the simplest allowed field, the energy holds only first derivatives (both lines). When it hurts: in slender beams, as \(\beta_{\mathrm{h}}\) goes to zero. What it costs: at \(L = 1\) m with two points \(0{,}00497\) with one element and \(0{,}56\) with sixteen, at \(L = 80\) mm already \(0{,}438\) with one.
  • One Gauss point for the shear part only. One point integrates the bending part exactly, because the tilt is a straight line and the curvature is constant on the element. In the quadratic bar element one point gave a spurious zero-energy mode. Why is there none here? The bending energy holds one combination of unknowns, \(\theta_2 - \theta_1\), the shear energy with one point another one, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{h}}\) in the middle. Two constraints on four unknowns leave two numbers free, exactly as many as there are rigid motions, a shift and a rotation. So there is no spurious mode (lemma 12.38). What it costs: \(0{,}750\) instead of \(1\) with one element, and no proved bound.
  • Reach of the thin-limit theorem: it holds for meshes in which every row of shear constraints has its pivot, such as the cantilever with two zeros per element. A cantilever with an extra support at the end under two points has more constraints than unknowns and lies outside the theorem. The theorem says nothing about it.
  • Choice of model. Euler-Bernoulli is enough when the shear part of the deflection is small: \(0{,}125\) % at \(L = 1\) m, which is why the true and the Euler-Bernoulli deflections are almost the same curve. At \(L = 80\) mm it is \(19{,}5\) % and the Timoshenko element is needed. In the book: 12.7.5.

The section on the penalty method is the ground of this lecture, we took only the spring from it, and we do not explain the error of the penalty method or the conditioning here. In the book: 12.6.
