Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.1 A beam on an elastic foundation

A rail lies on sleepers, and the sleepers lie on gravel. When a wheel rolls onto the rail, the rail bends and sinks into the foundation, and the foundation pushes it back up. How far does the rail sink under the wheel, where is the bending moment largest, and how far from the wheel is the effect still felt? This section answers these questions with the simplest model of a foundation. The chapter will then replace the foundation step by step with a more real one: first with a half-space, which sinks also around the loaded point, then with the contact of two rounded bodies, and finally with a rolling bearing, where the foundation is a ring on which steel balls roll.

A foundation (podlaga) is what lies under the beam and carries it. It is elastic because it returns to its original state when unloaded, like a spring. The model carries Winkler's name, and Zimmermann simplified his calculation for rails (VK, PDF p. 81, TIMO, PDF p. 370).

From history

When railways were built, people asked how a rail bends under a wheel. Winkler took the rail as a continuous beam on rigid supports (TIMO, PDF p. 370). A sleeper, however, is not a rigid support: the gravel under it gives way. Zimmermann therefore prepared tables that simplified Winkler's calculation of a beam on an elastic foundation, and applied the theory to the deflection of sleepers. In his picture the rail lies on elastic supports, and at the usual distances between sleepers the vertical load is spread over several sleepers, so the separate elastic supports can be replaced by an equivalent continuous elastic foundation (TIMO, PDF p. 370). The source gives only the initial H. and no full name.

The story showed a step that we shall take ourselves: a row of elastic supports is replaced by a foundation that is elastic at every point. Why this makes sense was told by Zimmermann, and how well it works we shall test with numbers at the end of the section. First we set up the model.

Take the beam of chapter 5, steel, 20 mm wide and 40 mm high, with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) (theorem 5.35). The axis \(x\) runs along the beam, the axis \(y\) points up, and the deflection \(v(x)\) is the displacement of the axis from its straight position, measured upward. We lay the beam on a row of springs so close together that we cannot tell them apart.

Let us draw the figure part by part. First the ground, a hatched surface, and above it a straight line showing where the axis of the beam would lie if nothing acted on it. On the ground we put the springs, each with one end on the ground and the other on the beam. The springs stand side by side and do not influence one another: this is the essence of the model. The beam has a shape that we shall compute: it is lowest under the force, then rises above the line and gradually dies out. The force \(P\) presses from above downward, so the arrow ends at the point where it acts, like every push. The deflection is the distance between the straight position and the beam. Last the force of the foundation: a spring that the beam has compressed pushes the beam upward, so the arrow ends on the beam. The length of the arrow is proportional to the deflection and is longest under the force.

We state the model also as a definition, since it gets its name and its numbers there.

Definition 25.1 Elastic or Winkler foundation (elastična podlaga)

Let \(k > 0\) be a number in N/m\(^2\), called the foundation modulus (modul podlage). A foundation with modulus \(k\) acts on the beam of chapter 5, which has axis \(x\) and deflection \(v(x)\) upward, with a force per unit length

\[f(x) = -k\,v(x) \tag{25.1}\]

in the direction of the axis \(y\). The function \(f\) takes the number \(v(x)\) and returns the number \(f(x)\), at each point of the axis separately. The force of the foundation on the piece of the axis between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) is the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} f\,\mathrm{d}t\). The model has six idealizations. The foundation is linear: the force is proportional to the deflection. Every point is independent: \(f(x)\) depends only on \(v(x)\) and not on the deflection of neighbouring points. The foundation acts on both sides: where \(v > 0\) it pulls the beam down, it does not only push. The foundation transmits no longitudinal forces (no friction). The modulus \(k\) is constant along the beam. The deflection is small, within the limits of the Euler-Bernoulli beam model (\(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\), chapter 5).

Source: COOK §8.8 (PDF p. 301, read): the model requires that the foundation resists only the displacement normal to its surface, with a pressure proportional to the displacement, and that the surrounding foundation is "unaffected". VK §3.7 (PDF p. 81, read) calls \(k\) the foundation modulus and measures it in force per area per displacement, while our \(k\) is force per length per displacement, as in COOK. The list of idealizations and their limits are ours.

The unit of \(k\) is N/m\(^2\): a force per unit length in N/m, divided by a deflection in metres. The choice already made by section 9.4.7 of chapter 9 is \(k = 1{,}0\cdot 10^{6}\) N/m\(^2\). At a deflection of 1 mm the foundation gives \(1000\) N/m, which on a face 20 mm wide is a pressure of 50 kPa. A row of springs of stiffness \(K_{\mathrm{t}}\) at distance \(s\) has \(k = K_{\mathrm{t}}/s\), as with the sleepers of the story.

Symbols are occupied in several places of the book. The letter \(k\) is the thermal conductivity in chapter 14, the shear factor in chapter 5, the stiffness of a spring in chapters 9 and 10. Here \(k\) is always the foundation modulus. The letter \(\beta\) is as in section 9.4.7: \(\beta = (k/(4\,EI))^{1/4}\). The force on the beam is written \(P\) as in chapter 9, because \(F\) appears later in the chapter for the contact of bodies.

First we derive the equation that the deflection satisfies. Let us draw a piece of the beam between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) and everything that acts on it.

Let us draw figure 25.5. The piece is the part of the axis from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), which we imagine cut off from the rest. Below it is the foundation with its springs. From above presses the external load \(q\) in N/m, drawn downward. The force of the foundation \(-k\,v\) points upward, because we drew a place where \(v < 0\). The piece is cut off, so we also draw the forces that held it: the shear force \(V(x_2)\) on the right face points in the direction \(+y\), on the left face it is opposite by lemma 5.10, \(-V(x_1)\). At the bottom are the ends of the piece, \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), with the distance between them.

The sum of the forces on the piece is zero. We add up the forces of the springs over the piece: the sum \(-k\,v(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) over a partition of the piece is a Riemann sum of the continuous function \(-k\,v\), and its limit is the integral. The load \(q - k\,v\) is continuous, because \(v\) is continuous. Lemma 5.10 gives for this piece

\[V(x_2) - V(x_1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\big(q(t) - k\,v(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{25.2}\]

On the right is the load that the piece carries, springs included, on the left the change of the shear force across the piece. The equation holds for every piece, not only a short one. What follows is the equation for \(v\) at every point.

Theorem 25.2 Equation of a beam on a foundation (enačba nosilca na podlagi)

Let the beam be prismatic and satisfy the assumptions of theorem 5.35, and let the foundation be as in definition 25.1 with modulus \(k\) along the interval \((a, b)\). On \((a, b)\) let there be no point forces and couples, let the load \(q\) be continuous and \(v\) four times continuously differentiable. Then equation (25.2) holds for the piece between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), all \(a < x_1 < x_2 < b\), if and only if

\[EI\,v'''' + k\,v = q \quad \text{on } (a, b) . \tag{25.3}\]
Proof

We must show that the difference of the two sides of equation (25.2) is the integral of one continuous function over the piece, and then use that a continuous function with zero integral over every piece vanishes everywhere.

  1. By theorem 5.35 \(M = EI\,v''\), and by corollary 5.11 the shear force is \(V = -M'\). Hence \(V = -EI\,v'''\).
  2. Insert the shear forces at the ends of the piece and the load with the foundation force into (25.2) and multiply by \(-1\): \(EI\,\big(v'''(x_2) - v'''(x_1)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}(q - k\,v)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. The function \(v''''\) is continuous, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, part 1), over the piece, \(EI\,\big(v'''(x_2) - v'''(x_1)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} EI\,v''''\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  4. Let \(f = EI\,v'''' + k\,v - q\), a continuous function. By steps 2 and 3, (25.2) for the piece \([x_1, x_2]\) is equal to \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} f\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
  5. If (25.3) holds, \(f = 0\), so the integral is zero for every piece and (25.2) holds.
  6. Conversely let (25.2) hold for every piece. Choose \(x_0 \in (a, b)\) and \(G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{x} f\,\mathrm{d}t\). By step 4, \(G = 0\) everywhere on \((a, b)\). The function \(G\) is differentiable with derivative \(f\) (theorem 3.55, part 2), because \(f\) is continuous, and it is zero everywhere, so \(f = G' = 0\).

Source: The equilibrium of the piece is lemma 5.10. The same equation follows from example 3 under theorem 5.70, where the term \(\tfrac12\,k\,v^2\) means the energy of the springs. COOK §8.8 (PDF p. 301, read) takes \(\tfrac12\,k\,w^2\) for the energy of the foundation, VK (3.65)-(3.67) (PDF pp. 81-82, read) uses the same model for a plate. The proof with a finite piece is not from a source.

We obtained the equation from the equilibrium of a piece, and the route of chapter 5 gives it from energy. A spring per unit length has energy \(\tfrac12\,k\,v^2\) (like a spring of stiffness \(k\) and displacement \(v\)), its derivative with respect to the deflection is the force \(k\,v\), and the Euler-Lagrange equation gives the same term \(k\,v\) in the equation. The two routes agree, which is a test of the model.

Let us test the theorem on numbers. Let the beam have its own weight \(q = -61{,}21\) N/m (section 5.1.3, chapter 5) and let the deflection be constant, \(v_0 = q/k = -0{,}0612\) mm. Then \(v_0'''' = 0\) and equation (25.3) says \(0 + k\,v_0 = q\), which holds. By equation (25.2), \(V(x_2) - V(x_1) = 0\) and \(-\int(q - k\,v_0)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), since the springs carry exactly the weight, \(k\,v_0 = q\).

The theorem rejects a different deflection. Let \(v_1 = -0{,}1\) mm be constant. Then \(v_1'''' = 0\), but \(k\,v_1 = -100\) N/m and not \(q = -61{,}21\) N/m. The springs push up with \(100\) N/m, the weight pulls down with \(61{,}21\) N/m, and \(38{,}79\) N/m upward remains. On a piece 1 m long this is \(38{,}79\) N, while the shear force does not change at a constant deflection, \(V(x_2) - V(x_1) = 0\). Equation (25.2) does not hold, the piece would accelerate upward: a deflection of \(-0{,}1\) mm with no other forces is not an equilibrium.

Now we solve the most important case: a long beam with one force. A rail under a wheel is such a case, the beam is much longer than the region where it bends. We take it to be infinite so that the end does not matter, and later in the section we check how long a real one must be.

Equation (25.3) at \(q = 0\) is linear with constant coefficients, of the kind solved in section 9.4.7. There the four solutions are \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta x}\cos\beta x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta x}\sin\beta x\) (figure 9.41), and four constants are to be fixed by conditions that we set up now. Under the force the conditions are given by lemma 5.44: \(v\), \(v'\) and \(M\) are continuous, and the shear force jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}} = P\). Far from the force the deflection must stay bounded, because the beam is not loaded there.

Theorem 25.3 Infinite beam under a point force (neskončen nosilec s točkovno silo)

Let the beam be prismatic with \(EI\) and a foundation \(k\) along the whole axis, \(\beta = (k/(4\,EI))^{1/4}\). At the point \(x = 0\) let a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -P\) (\(P > 0\), downward) act, and no other loads. Let \(v\) be four times continuously differentiable on \(x \neq 0\), let it solve (25.3) with \(q = 0\) on each side, let it be bounded on \(\mathbb{R}\) and let it satisfy the conditions of lemma 5.44 at \(x = 0\). Then there is exactly one such function and

\[v(x) = -\frac{P\beta}{2k}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta |x|}\big(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta |x|\big) . \tag{25.4}\]
Proof

On each side we write the general solution, boundedness removes two constants on each side, and the conditions under the force fix the remaining four.

  1. The equation is \(v'''' + 4\beta^4 v = 0\), because \(k = 4\,EI\,\beta^4\). By section 9.4.7 (theorem 9.44 and corollary 9.45) all real solutions on \(x > 0\) are \(v = \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(A\cos\beta x + B\sin\beta x) + \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}(a\cos\beta x + b\sin\beta x)\), and on \(x < 0\) they are \(v = \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}(C\cos\beta x + D\sin\beta x) + \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(a'\cos\beta x + b'\sin\beta x)\).
  2. Let \((a, b) \neq (0, 0)\) and \(r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} > 0\). There is an angle \(\varphi\) with \(a\cos\beta x + b\sin\beta x = r\cos(\beta x - \varphi)\). At the points \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (\varphi + 2\pi n)/\beta\) (for \(n\) so large that \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\)) the second term equals \(r\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta x_{\mathrm{n}}}\), which grows beyond every bound with \(n\), while the first term is at most \(|A| + |B|\) in absolute value. The function \(v\) is not bounded. Boundedness on \(x > 0\) therefore requires \(a = b = 0\).
  3. On \(x < 0\) the same argument at the points \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (\varphi' - 2\pi n)/\beta \to -\infty\) gives \(a' = b' = 0\). What remains is \(v = \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(A\cos\beta x + B\sin\beta x)\) for \(x > 0\) and \(v = \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}(C\cos\beta x + D\sin\beta x)\) for \(x < 0\).
  4. The derivatives at \(x = 0\) (checked with SymPy): from the right \(v = A\), \(v' = \beta(B - A)\), \(v'' = -2B\beta^2\), \(v''' = 2\beta^3(A + B)\). From the left \(v = C\), \(v' = \beta(C + D)\), \(v'' = 2D\beta^2\), \(v''' = 2\beta^3(D - C)\).
  5. Continuity of \(v\) gives \(C = A\). The moment \(M = EI\,v''\) is continuous, so \(-2B = 2D\), \(D = -B\). The slope \(v'\) is continuous: \(B - A = C + D = A - B\), so \(B = A\) and \(D = -A\).
  6. The shear force is \(V = -EI\,v'''\) and it jumps by \(V(0^+) - V(0^-) = -2\,EI\,\beta^3\big((A + B) - (D - C)\big) = -2\,EI\,\beta^3\cdot 4A = -8\,EI\,\beta^3 A\), where we inserted \(B = A\), \(D = -A\), \(C = A\). The jump must be \(P\), so \(A = -P/(8\,EI\,\beta^3)\). Since \(EI\,\beta^3 = k/(4\beta)\), \(A = -P\beta/(2k)\).
  7. For \(x > 0\), \(v = A\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\), and for \(x < 0\), \(v = A\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}(\cos\beta x - \sin\beta x)\). Because \(\cos\beta x = \cos\beta|x|\) and \(\sin\beta x = -\sin\beta|x|\), on \(x < 0\) this equals \(A\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|}(\cos\beta|x| + \sin\beta|x|)\). This is (25.4).
  8. The constants are determined uniquely (steps 2 to 6). Steps 5 and 6 are equivalent to the four conditions under the force, so the function of step 7 satisfies them all. It is bounded: \(|v| \le \sqrt{2}\,|A|\), because \(|\cos t + \sin t| \le \sqrt{2}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|} \le 1\).

Source: TIMO (PDF p. 370, read) treats the rail as an infinitely long beam on a foundation and gives the maximum moment and stress, but the formulas are not in the text that was read, so there is no comparison. The proof with boundedness and with lemma 5.44 is not from a source.

Figure 25.6 shows the theorem. Let us draw the first panel. The horizontal axis is \(t = \beta x\), a dimensionless distance measured in units of \(1/\beta = 0{,}538\) m, and it is the same in all three panels. The unit \(1/\beta\) is natural because \(k/(4\,EI)\) has unit \(\mathrm{m}^{-4}\), so \(\beta\) has unit \(\mathrm{m}^{-1}\), and it is the only length that \(EI\) and \(k\) determine. The deflection is the curve \(v/|v(0)|\) against \(t\). It is lowest under the force and decays fast: at \(t = \pi\), that is at \(x = 1{,}69\) m, only \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432\) of the largest deflection is left. At \(P = 10\) kN, \(v(0) = -P\beta/(2k) = -9{,}298\) mm. Since \(EI\,v''\) is the bending moment, the second derivative of equation (25.4) gives \(M(x) = \dfrac{P}{4\beta}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|}\big(\cos\beta x - \sin\beta|x|\big)\). This is the moment in the third panel. It is largest under the force, \(M(0) = P/(4\beta) = 1344{,}4\) N m. Its zero is at \(t = \pi/4\), marked on the axis, so at \(x = 0{,}422\) m. Between \(t = \pi/4\) and \(t = 5\pi/4\) the moment is negative, and its smallest value is \(-0{,}208\,M(0)\) at \(t = \pi/2\).

A test of the model on numbers: the largest bending stress, from the moment \(M(0)\), is \(M(0)\cdot(h/2)/I = 1344{,}4\cdot 0{,}02/(1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}) = 252\) MPa, above the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (definition 2.7). At \(P = 10\) kN the linear model therefore no longer holds. Since the stress is proportional to \(P\), it holds up to \(P = 8{,}73\) kN. We keep the example because the numbers are clear, and the ratios in the figure do not depend on \(P\). The slope stays within the limits of the model: the largest \(v'\) is \(0{,}0111\) at \(t = \pi/4\), which gives \(v'^2 = 1{,}2\cdot 10^{-4}\), and the limit \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\) of chapter 5 is met.

The theorem has the hypothesis of boundedness. What happens without it? The function \(w = \cosh\beta x\,\cos\beta x\) is smooth on the whole axis and solves \(w'''' + 4\beta^4 w = 0\), because it is the real part of \(\cosh\big((1 + \mathrm{i})\beta x\big)\), whose fourth derivative is \((1 + \mathrm{i})^4\beta^4 = -4\beta^4\) times the same function. Being smooth, it has no jumps at \(x = 0\) and changes none of the four conditions under the force. So \(v + \epsilon\,w\) is another solution with all four conditions, for every \(\epsilon\). Let \(\epsilon = 1\) µm. At \(x = 2\pi/\beta = 3{,}38\) m, \(\epsilon\cosh 2\pi = 0{,}268\) mm, and at \(4\pi/\beta = 6{,}76\) m already \(\epsilon\cosh 4\pi = 143\) mm. The deflection would grow far from the force, though nothing acts there. The hypothesis of boundedness is no decoration: without it there is no uniqueness.

Equilibrium says that the foundation must carry the whole force \(P\). Let us check that our solution gives this.

Lemma 25.4 Reaction of the foundation (reakcija podlage)

For the deflection (25.4) the total force of the foundation on the beam equals the force: \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-k\,v)\,\mathrm{d}x = P\), where \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\) is the sum of the integrals over \((-\infty, 0]\) and \([0, \infty)\).

Proof

By definition 9.21 the integral to infinity is the limit of integrals to \(T\), and the integral over \((-\infty, 0]\) is the limit of integrals from \(-T\) to \(0\). The function \(v\) is even, so both integrals are equal and it suffices to compute the integral over \(x > 0\).

  1. The derivative of \(-\tfrac1\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\) is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x + \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\) (rules for a product and a composite function, lemma 1.82).
  2. By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, part 1), the area under the curve from \(0\) to \(T\) is \(\int_0^T\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac1\beta\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\beta T}\cos\beta T\big)\).
  3. Since \(|\mathrm{e}^{-\beta T}\cos\beta T| \le \mathrm{e}^{-\beta T}\), which tends to zero, the limit is \(1/\beta\).
  4. The integral is the area under the curve of figure 25.6, multiplied by \(-k\). Both halves together: \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-k\,v)\,\mathrm{d}x = k\,\dfrac{P\beta}{2k}\cdot\dfrac{2}{\beta} = P\).

Source: The computation is not from a source.

The lemma tells how far the effect of the force reaches. If the deflection were everywhere equal to \(|v(0)|\), the foundation would need the length \(P/(k\,|v(0)|) = 2/\beta = 1{,}076\) m to carry the force \(P\). The deflection decreases, so the foundation carries the force over a longer stretch, and on the curve of figure 25.6 we see that this is a few units of \(1/\beta = 0{,}54\) m.

One thing about the theorem surprised us: the function in (25.4) changes sign. What does this mean for the foundation? Hertz found the same pattern for another foundation.

From history

At the beginning of 1883 Hertz was occupied with the bending of an infinite plate floating on water and loaded normally at one point. He found that under the force the plate bends downward, but at a certain distance from the force the deflection becomes negative, at a still larger distance it is positive again, and so on. The surface is therefore wavy, and the height of the waves quickly decreases with the distance from the force. He arrived at the paradoxical conclusion that a plate heavier than water can be kept afloat by loading it at its centre. The explanation is that the bending turns the plate into a shell, which presses out of the water more than the weight of the plate (TIMO, PDF p. 288, read).

Hertz's plate on water has the same pattern as our beam: a deflection under the force, then a sign that alternates, and an amplitude that decreases quickly. Water is a foundation of our kind for the plate: when the plate sinks by an additional depth \(w\), the buoyancy grows by \(\rho_{\mathrm{w}}\,g\,w\) per unit area, so in proportion to the displacement (this inference is ours, not the source's). The pattern is therefore not a property of our number or material, but of the law that the force is proportional to the deflection. Let us look at it on the beam.

Lemma 25.5 The foundation pulls the beam back (podlaga vleče nosilec nazaj)

For the deflection (25.4), \(v(x) > 0\) if and only if \(\beta|x|\) lies in one of the intervals \(\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{4} + 2j\pi,\ \tfrac{7\pi}{4} + 2j\pi\big)\), \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) There the foundation pulls the beam down with a force \(-k\,v < 0\). The largest pull is at \(\beta|x| = \pi\) and equals \(k\,|v(0)|\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}\).

Proof

We must show where the function \(g(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t}(\cos t + \sin t)\) has negative values for \(t \ge 0\) and where it is smallest.

  1. Since \(v = -\frac{P\beta}{2k}\,g(\beta|x|)\) and \(P\beta/(2k) > 0\), \(v > 0\) exactly where \(g(\beta|x|) < 0\).
  2. We have \(\cos t + \sin t = \sqrt{2}\,\sin(t + \pi/4)\), because \(\sin(t + \pi/4) = (\sin t + \cos t)/\sqrt{2}\). This expression is zero at \(t + \pi/4 = n\pi\), that is at \(t = 3\pi/4,\ 7\pi/4,\ 11\pi/4,\ \dots\)
  3. The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\) is positive, so \(g\) is negative exactly where \(\sin(t + \pi/4) < 0\), that is on the intervals \((3\pi/4 + 2j\pi,\ 7\pi/4 + 2j\pi)\). There \(v > 0\).
  4. The derivative is \(g' = -\mathrm{e}^{-t}(\cos t + \sin t) + \mathrm{e}^{-t}(\cos t - \sin t) = -2\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin t\), zero at \(t = n\pi\), and \(g(n\pi) = (-1)^n\mathrm{e}^{-n\pi}\). The negative values of \(g\) have local minima at odd \(n\) with value \(-\mathrm{e}^{-n\pi}\), which decreases in absolute value with \(n\). The largest pull is therefore at \(t = \pi\): \(v = |v(0)|\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}\) and \(-k\,v = -k\,|v(0)|\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}\).

Source: The computation is not from a source. Hertz's paradox for a plate on water shows the alternation of sign (TIMO, PDF p. 288).

Let us look at figure 25.6. The deflection in the first panel rises above the axis at \(t = 3\pi/4\), but at the scale of the panel it is hardly visible. The second panel therefore shows the same deflection magnified 20 times, only to the right of the force. The zeros \(3\pi/4\) and \(7\pi/4\) are marked on the axis, and between them is the shaded region where \(v > 0\). At \(P = 10\) kN and \(k = 1{,}0\cdot 10^6\) N/m\(^2\) this region lies between \(x = 1{,}267\) m and \(2{,}956\) m, of length \(\pi/\beta = 1{,}689\) m. The highest point is \(0{,}4018\) mm above the axis at \(x = 1{,}689\) m. There the foundation pulls the beam down with the force \(k\cdot 0{,}4018\) mm \(= 401{,}8\) N/m. The next region starts at \(11\pi/(4\beta) = 4{,}646\) m, and at its highest point it is \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi} = 0{,}0019\) times as high as the first.

Would the beam's own weight remove this? The lemma holds for a beam without weight. The weight \(\rho g A = 61{,}21\) N/m (chapter 5) lowers the beam by a further \(0{,}0612\) mm, so at \(t = \pi\) the total deflection is still \(+0{,}4018 - 0{,}0612 = +0{,}3406\) mm, and the foundation together with the weight still pulls, with \(340{,}6\) N/m. The pull at \(t = \pi\) equals \(P\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi}/2\), and the weight exceeds it only when it is smaller than \(61{,}21\) N/m, that is at \(P < 1524\) N. At a force of 10 kN the pull is 6,6 times the weight.

A foundation that pulls is not a foundation on which the beam only lies. The ground under a sleeper cannot pull, and at \(P = 10\) kN the beam would lift off the ground between \(x = 1{,}27\) m and \(2{,}96\) m. The real problem then has two parts: where the beam touches, the foundation acts as a spring, elsewhere the force is zero, and the boundary between them is unknown. We set up the contact conditions in section 25.5, but a beam that lifts off the foundation is not solved in this chapter. The model solved here is therefore correct for a foundation that is attached to the beam and can pull (a beam bolted to springs). For a foundation that only supports the beam it is correct as long as the total force of the foundation, that is the force that carries the weight plus \(-k\,v\), is not negative, so at \(P < 1524\) N.

What remains is a beam of finite length. Its ends are free: the shear force and the moment are zero there.

Corollary 25.6 A finite beam on a foundation (končni nosilec na podlagi)

Let the beam of length \(L\) be prismatic with \(EI\) and a foundation \(k\) along the whole length \([-L/2, L/2]\), \(\beta = (k/(4\,EI))^{1/4}\). Let the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -P\) (\(P > 0\), downwards) act at the point \(x = 0\), and let there be no other loads. We seek a function \(v\) that is four times continuously differentiable separately on \([-L/2, 0]\) and on \([0, L/2]\) (with one-sided derivatives at the endpoints), solves (25.3) with \(q = 0\) on each side, meets the conditions of lemma 5.44 at \(x = 0\) and has free ends, \(V = M = 0\), that is \(v'' = v''' = 0\) at \(x = \pm L/2\). Then there is only one such function. It is even, and for \(x \ge 0\) it equals

\[v = c_1\cosh\beta x\cos\beta x + c_2\sinh\beta x\sin\beta x + c_3\cosh\beta x\sin\beta x + c_4\sinh\beta x\cos\beta x , \tag{25.5}\]

where the constants are the unique solution of the four linear equations \(v'(0) = 0\), \(-EI\,v'''(0^+) = P/2\), \(v''(L/2) = 0\), \(v'''(L/2) = 0\). The foundation carries the whole force: \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2}(-k\,v)\,\mathrm{d}x = P\).

Proof

We prove uniqueness with energy, get the symmetry from uniqueness, then write the system for the constants, show that a solution of the system gives a solution of the problem, and check the reaction.

  1. Let \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) be two solutions. The difference \(u = v_1 - v_2\) solves \(EI\,u'''' + k\,u = 0\) on each side of the force. The conditions under the force are the same for \(v_1\) and \(v_2\): \(v\), \(v'\) and \(v''\) are continuous, and \(v'''\) jumps by \(-P/EI\), because \(V = -EI\,v'''\). So \(u\), \(u'\), \(u''\) and \(u'''\) have equal limits from the left and from the right at \(x = 0\). At the ends \(u'' = u''' = 0\).
  2. Multiply the equation by \(u\) and integrate separately over \([-L/2, 0]\) and over \([0, L/2]\). Integrating by parts twice (corollary 3.56), on each half \(\int EI\,u''''u\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EI\,u'''u - EI\,u''u'\big] + \int EI\,u''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The boundary terms at the ends \(\pm L/2\) are zero because \(u'' = u''' = 0\). The terms at \(x = 0\) from the two halves cancel, because the limits of \(u\), \(u'\), \(u''\), \(u'''\) from the left and from the right are equal (step 1). The sum of the two halves is \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2}\big(EI\,u''^2 + k\,u^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  3. The integrals over the two halves are non-negative and their sum is zero, so each is zero. On each half the integrand is continuous and non-negative and its integral is zero, so it is zero everywhere. Hence \(u = 0\) and the solution is unique.
  4. The mirror image \(x \mapsto v(-x)\) solves the same problem, because the load, the foundation and the conditions do not change under reflection. By uniqueness \(v(-x) = v(x)\).
  5. The functions \(\cosh t\cos t\), \(\sinh t\sin t\), \(\cosh t\sin t\), \(\sinh t\cos t\) are real and imaginary parts of \(\cosh\big((1 + \mathrm{i})t\big)\) and \(\sinh\big((1 + \mathrm{i})t\big)\), which solve \(y'''' = -4y\). Their initial data \((y, y', y'', y''')\) at \(t = 0\) are \((1, 0, 0, 0)\), \((0, 0, 2, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0, 2)\) and \((0, 1, 0, -2)\) (checked with SymPy), so they are linearly independent. By theorem 9.41 every solution on \(x > 0\) is determined by its initial data, so (25.5) is the general solution.
  6. The derivatives at \(x = 0\) are \(v'(0) = \beta(c_3 + c_4)\) and \(v'''(0) = 2\beta^3(c_3 - c_4)\). Evenness gives \(v'(0) = 0\), and \(V(0^+) = P/2\), because \(V\) jumps by \(P\) and \(V\) is odd. So \(c_4 = -c_3\) and \(-EI\cdot 4\beta^3 c_3 = P/2\), that is \(c_3 = -P\beta/(2k)\).
  7. Let the constants satisfy \(c_4 = -c_3\), \(c_3 = -P\beta/(2k)\) and the conditions \(v''(L/2) = v'''(L/2) = 0\). Then the even extension, \(v(x) = v(-x)\) for \(x < 0\), is a solution of the problem. On \(x < 0\) it solves the equation, because the equation contains only the even derivative \(v''''\). The functions \(v\) and \(v''\) are even, so their limits from the left and from the right at \(x = 0\) are equal. The function \(v'\) is odd with \(v'(0^+) = \beta(c_3 + c_4) = 0\), so \(v'(0^-) = 0\) too. The shear force \(V = -EI\,v'''\) is odd with \(V(0^+) = -EI\cdot 4\beta^3 c_3 = P/2\), so it jumps by \(P/2 - (-P/2) = P = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\), as lemma 5.44 requires. At the left end \(v''(-L/2) = v''(L/2) = 0\) and \(v'''(-L/2) = -v'''(L/2) = 0\).
  8. The two conditions at the end \(x = L/2\) give two linear equations for \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), and their matrix does not depend on \(P\). The matrix is invertible. Otherwise the system at \(P = 0\), where \(c_3 = c_4 = 0\), would have a solution \((c_1, c_2) \neq (0, 0)\). By step 7 its even extension would be a solution of the problem with \(P = 0\), not zero by the independence of the functions (step 5). The function \(v = 0\) is also a solution of the problem with \(P = 0\), which steps 1 to 3 exclude. So the system has exactly one solution, and by step 7 it gives a solution of the problem.
  9. On each side of the force \(-k\,v\)\( = EI\,v''''\). The integral over \([-L/2, 0)\) and \((0, L/2]\) gives \(EI\,\big(v'''(0^-) - v'''(-L/2)\big) + EI\,\big(v'''(L/2) - v'''(0^+)\big)\). The terms at the ends are zero, what remains is \(-EI\,\big(v'''(0^+) - v'''(0^-)\big) = V(0^+) - V(0^-) = P\).

Source: The proof with energy and four conditions is not from a source. Section 9.4.7 only found the four solutions and promised that the constants are fixed by the conditions at the ends, here we do it.

Let us compute an example: the same beam, \(P = 10\) kN, length \(L = 4\) m and \(L = 1\) m. The first means \(\beta L = 7{,}438\), the second \(\beta L = 1{,}860\).

Let us draw figure 25.7. The zero line is the position without load. On it lies the grey curve of the infinite beam from figure 25.6. The blue curve is the beam of length 4 m: near the middle it almost coincides with the grey one, and at the ends it rises above the zero line. The orange curve is the beam of length 1 m: its middle is considerably lower than for the infinite beam. Both follow equation (25.5) with the constants from the system of the corollary. For comparison the dashed lines show the deflection \(-P/(kL)\) of a beam that does not bend at all and lies on the springs like a rigid board: \(-2{,}5\) mm at \(L = 4\) m and \(-10\) mm at \(L = 1\) m.

The system of the corollary gives the following. At \(L = 4\) m the deflection under the force is \(-9{,}3142\) mm, the infinite beam has \(-9{,}2979\) mm (ratio \(1{,}0018\)). The ends rise by \(0{,}7554\) mm above the zero line. The infinite beam behaves in the same way: the end is at \(x = L/2 = 2\) m, in the region from \(1{,}267\) m to \(2{,}956\) m, where the infinite beam lies above the zero line (figure 25.6). The end of the beam with \(L = 1\) m is at \(x = 0{,}5\) m, before this region, and lies below the zero line. The total force of the foundation is \(10\,000{,}00\) N, as the corollary says. At \(L = 1\) m the deflection under the force is \(-11{,}371\) mm, that is \(22{,}3\) % more than for the infinite beam, and the ends sink by \(7{,}960\) mm. The beam is neither a rigid board nor an infinite beam: the deflection of the board, \(-10\) mm, is 12 % smaller than the true \(-11{,}371\) mm, because the beam does bend after all, and the infinite beam has \(-9{,}298\) mm because it has no ends that could sink.

The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta L/2}\), by which the envelope of the solution decays towards the end of the beam, is \(0{,}0243\) at \(L = 4\) m and \(0{,}3946\) at \(L = 1\) m. In the first case the middle differs from the infinite beam by \(0{,}18\) %, in the second by \(22\) %. These are two measured points, not a general limit.

The question from the story remains: how good is the replacement of springs by a continuous foundation? We also computed the same beam of length 4 m with \(P = 10\) kN on sleepers. The springs of stiffness \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,s\) are at distance \(s\), and the force acts midway between two sleepers. The computation was done with the finite elements of chapter 12 (the matrix of lemma 12.4) with nodes at the sleepers, at the ends and under the force. The force of the spring at a sleeper is a nodal force, and between the nodes the beam carries no load, so the true deflection of the sleeper model is a polynomial of degree three on each element (\(EI\,v'''' = 0\)), which the finite elements capture exactly (as in theorem 12.13). So there is no element error: the table shows the exact deflections of the sleeper model. A check confirms this, since four elements between neighbouring nodes give the same deflections to six decimal places in millimetres.

The test shows when the continuous foundation holds. At a spacing of \(0{,}25\) m, which is below half of \(1/\beta = 0{,}538\) m, the difference is \(0{,}07\) %, at a spacing of \(0{,}5\) m, about \(1/\beta\), already \(1{,}4\) %, and with four sleepers on \(4\) m (\(\beta s = 1{,}86\)) it is \(29\) %: there the deflection is \(12{,}0\) mm and the continuous model underestimates it by \(22\) %, since it would give \(9{,}3\) mm. Zimmermann's step from the story therefore holds for the usual closely spaced sleepers and not for four sleepers on a beam.

We have thus derived, solved and tested the model. The solution shows that the effect of the force dies out over a distance of a few \(1/\beta\), that the foundation carries the whole force, and that the sign of the deflection alternates, so the foundation sometimes pulls the beam. The last is the key for the rest of the chapter: a foundation in which every point is independent cannot represent a body that sinks also around the loaded point, and it does not know when contact would break. In the next sections we therefore replace the Winkler foundation by a half-space.
