Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.5 Gyroscopic systems

We found the exact modes of the moving string in section 26.3 with an ansatz containing a phase factor. For a belt with bending stiffness (section 26.7), with finite elements (section 26.8) and in rotating machines (chapter 24) there is no such closed formula, but we do have finitely many coordinates. So in this section we look at what system of ordinary equations we get from the moving string if we describe it with finitely many sines, and at what we can prove about such a system. First we put the sines into the equation and obtain three matrices. One of them is skew-symmetric and is called gyroscopic (žiroskopska). Then we show that gyroscopic forces do no work, that for a positive definite stiffness matrix every eigenvalue is purely imaginary, and, on an example, where this condition fails. We finish with numbers for the band of example A and with the question of why the sines converge poorly at high speed (figures 26.30 to 26.38).

26.5.1 Sines in the equation of the moving string

Take the string of section 26.1: length between the eyelets \(L\), mass per length \(\mu\), tension \(T\), axial speed \(v\), transverse displacement \(w(x, t)\), and the eyelets at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) fixed in space (the ends of the rest lines in figure 26.30 below). By theorem 26.4, \(\mu\,(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}) = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We move the term \(\mu v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) to the right-hand side and obtain

\[\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} - S\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0 , \qquad S = T - \mu v^2 . \tag{26.23}\]

The letter \(S\) is in this section only an abbreviation for a force in newtons. Since \(S/\mu = c^2 - v^2\) with \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) (definition 13.4), it is positive for \(v < c\). We take only \(v < c\). Why we may: the band of example A runs at \(v = 40\) m/s with \(c = 113{,}23\) m/s, far below \(c\). When not: at \(v = c\), \(S = 0\) and the string has no transverse stiffness left (section 26.4). What the assumption does to the result: because \(S > 0\) the stiffness matrix below is positive definite, and point 2 of theorem 26.27 rests on this.

We look for the solution in a family we already know. The sines \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(x) = \sin(j\pi x/L)\) are zero at both eyelets, and by lemma 13.10 there are enough of them to approximate uniformly any smooth shape that is zero at the eyelets, and we take the first \(N\) of them (figure 26.30). We try

\[w(x, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} q_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}(x) , \tag{26.24}\]

where \(q_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\) are unknown functions of time in metres. Such a sum does not satisfy equation (26.23) exactly. What a finite \(N\) costs is shown by the numbers at the end of the section: with one sine the first frequency of the band of example A is \(6{,}89\,\%\) too high, with eight it is \(0{,}01\,\%\) too high (the blackwhite curve in figure 26.38). So we use the same requirement as the Galerkin method (definition 6.42): the residual of the equation, that is the left-hand side \(r\) of equation (26.23), must at every instant be orthogonal to every function \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(i = 1, \dots, N\), which means \(\int_0^L r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). In the static problem the definition applied this requirement to a bilinear form, here we apply it to the residual of the dynamic equation. By lemma 6.43 it suffices to write it for the \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) alone, because \(\int_0^L r\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\) is linear in the test function \(u\).

What will be in the equation we get? Figure 26.30 shows what we shall need. We draw the first four shapes \(\varphi_1\) to \(\varphi_4\), each with its own rest line between the eyelets. In between we draw a dashed midpoint \(L/2\). The shapes with odd \(j\) (blue) are mirror images of themselves about the midpoint, the shapes with even \(j\) (orange) are mirrored about the midpoint and change sign. For our computation what matters is what happens to the derivative: the slope of a mirror-symmetric curve has opposite signs on the two sides of the midpoint, so the derivative of an even function is an odd function and conversely.

Lemma 26.24 Galerkin system of the moving string (Galerkinov sistem gibajoče strune)

Let the string be that of theorem 26.4 with the eyelets at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), and let \(v < c\). Let \(w\) be as in equation (26.24) with twice differentiable \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and with the sine shapes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin(j\pi x/L)\), and the entries \(G_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the values of the matrix below. The residual of equation (26.23) is orthogonal to \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{N}}\) at every instant exactly when

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat G\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek 0 , \tag{26.25}\]

where \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{N}})^\T\) and

\[M_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac{\mu L}{2}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \qquad K_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac{S\,j^2\pi^2}{2L}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \qquad G_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2\mu v\int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \begin{cases} \dfrac{4\mu v\,i\,j}{i^2 - j^2} & i + j \text{ odd} , \\[1ex] 0 & i + j \text{ even} . \end{cases} \tag{26.26}\]
Proof

We must show what each term of the residual becomes after integration with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. The residual is \(r = \mu w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} - S\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (equation (26.23)). From equation (26.24), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \ddot q_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}''\), since the sine shapes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) are differentiable and the sum is finite.
  2. For the shapes the product of sines is \(\sin a\,\sin b = \tfrac12\big(\cos(a - b) - \cos(a + b)\big)\). For \(a = i\pi x/L\) and \(b = j\pi x/L\) the integral \(\int_0^L\cos(m\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is zero for integer \(m \ne 0\) (the integral of a cosine over whole half-periods). Hence \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{L}{2}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'' = -(j\pi/L)^2\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) also \(-\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}''\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = (j\pi/L)^2\,\tfrac L2\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  3. For the term with the mixed derivative (the curve \(\varphi_2\varphi_1'\) is an example), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}' = (j\pi/L)\cos(j\pi x/L)\). We use \(\sin a\,\cos b = \tfrac12\big(\sin(a + b) + \sin(a - b)\big)\) and \(\int_0^L\sin(m\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{L}{m\pi}\big(1 - (-1)^m\big)\) for an integer \(m \ne 0\), which is \(2L/(m\pi)\) for odd and zero for even \(m\).
  4. For \(i + j\) even, \(i + j\) and \(i - j\) are both even (or \(i - j = 0\), where \(\sin 0 = 0\)), so \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). For \(i = 1\), \(j = 3\) figure 26.31 shows it: the areas above the line and below it cancel. For \(i + j\) odd both are odd and \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{j\pi}{L}\cdot\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{2L}{(i + j)\pi} + \tfrac{2L}{(i - j)\pi}\Big) = j\Big(\tfrac{1}{i + j} + \tfrac{1}{i - j}\Big) = \dfrac{2ij}{i^2 - j^2}\). For \(i = 2\), \(j = 1\) this is \(4/3\), the sum of two equal areas in figure 26.31.
  5. Row \(i\) of the condition \(\int_0^L r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) is, by steps 2 to 4, equal to \(\mu\tfrac L2\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} G_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}} + S\,i^2\pi^2/(2L)\,q_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). This is equation (26.25), in which the entries \(G_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the values of row \(i\) of the matrix. The converse holds because the steps are equalities.

Source: The procedure is ours: the sines after section 13.2, the residual requirement after definition 6.42. WM1990 (PDF p. 6) mentions the choice of the eigenfunctions of the string at rest as expansion functions for moving systems. The numbers in equation (26.26) are checked by quadrature in num-05.py.

The reason \(\mat G\) has entries only for \(i + j\) odd is shown by figures 26.30 and 26.31. The integrand \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\) is the product of the shape \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the slope \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\), and the slope has the opposite symmetry to the shape \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). We draw the product \(\varphi_1\varphi_3'\) between the eyelets, a case of equal parity. Across the midpoint the curve is mirrored and changes sign: the areas \(+0{,}19\) and \(+1{,}69\) above the line have their twins \(-1{,}69\) and \(-0{,}19\) below the line on the other side of the midpoint, so the integral is zero. Below it we draw the product \(\varphi_2\varphi_1'\), a case of different parity. This curve is its own mirror image about the midpoint, the areas \(+0{,}67\) on the left and on the right add up to \(4/3\), which is the value \(2ij/(i^2 - j^2)\) of step 4 for \(i = 2\), \(j = 1\). So the first shape does not couple to itself but couples to the second. Physically: motion in the first shape produces, through the term \(2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), a force in the second shape, because the slope of the first shape (the derivative of an even shape is odd) has the same symmetry as the second shape.

Figure 26.32 shows all the values for \(N = 4\). We draw the frame of the matrix with row \(i\) and column \(j\) and write in the values \(4ij/(i^2 - j^2)\) from equation (26.26). The cells with zeros are where \(i + j\) is even, so a checkerboard. On the diagonal are only zeros, because \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,[\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^2]_0^L = 0\) (the eyelet is fixed). Every element below the diagonal is the negative of its mirror element above it: \(G_{21} = 2\mu v \cdot 4/3 = +2{,}67\,\mu v\) (the area \(4/3\) of figure 26.31) and \(G_{12} = -2{,}67\,\mu v\). This is no accident, since integration by parts with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}(0) = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}(L) = 0\) gives \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), and swapping \(i\) and \(j\) changes the sign. It is exactly this property of the matrix that carries the rest of the section.

26.5.2 The gyroscopic matrix and the gyroscopic system

System (26.25) has the same structure as the system of GR (example 1.8, PDF p. 54 to 56), where a mass on springs in a wheel turning with constant angular velocity is described in the coordinates of the wheel. GR writes there that the equation of motion acquires a term \(\mat G\,\dot{\vek q}\), that this term belongs to the Coriolis or gyroscopic forces, and that these are apparent forces, because they arise only from the description in a moving frame (GR PDF p. 47 and 56, remark 1.1). The name gyroscopic comes from the gyroscope, a device with a spinning wheel (Greek gyros, circle), since such forces show up precisely in spinning bodies. Why GR uses it is not written, so this is the name in use and not our conclusion. In our string nothing spins. The term \(2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) is the one that section 26.1 calls Coriolis, because in the acceleration of a particle it arises from the simultaneous motion along the string with speed \(v\) and the changing slope. In the coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\) it has the same property as for the wheel: it is linear in the velocities \(\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and has skew-symmetric coefficients. So we keep the same name for the matrix that is skew-symmetric, as figure 26.32 shows.

Definition 26.25 Gyroscopic matrix and gyroscopic system (žiroskopska matrika in žiroskopski sistem)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrices with \(\mat M\) positive definite (definition 6.27). A real \(n \times n\) matrix \(\mat G\) is gyroscopic (skew-symmetric, poševno simetrična) if \(\mat G^\T = -\mat G\), that is \(G_{\mathrm{ji}} = -G_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (a pair of values across the diagonal) and \(G_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\) (the diagonal). The system of ordinary differential equations for \(\vek q : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n\),

\[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat G\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek 0 , \tag{26.27}\]

is a (linear conservative) gyroscopic system. The matrix \(\mat G\) is called the gyroscopic matrix and the vector \(\mat G\,\dot{\vek q}\) the gyroscopic forces.

Source: GR (2.242), PDF p. 137, and (1.39), (1.40), PDF p. 47, where the coefficients of the gyroscopic forces are skew-symmetric. The matrix \(\mat G\) has nothing to do with the shear modulus \(G\) (a scalar), which it does not meet.

By lemma 26.24 the Galerkin system of the moving string is a gyroscopic system: \(\mat M\) is a positive definite matrix with the number \(\mu L/2\) on the diagonal, \(\mat K\) is positive definite because \(S > 0\) for \(v < c\), and \(\mat G\) is skew-symmetric by figure 26.32.

Lemma 26.26 Gyroscopic forces do no work (žiroskopske sile ne opravijo dela)

Let \(\vek q(t)\) be a twice continuously differentiable solution of the gyroscopic system (26.27). Then the power of the gyroscopic forces (the scalar product of force and velocity) \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat G\,\dot{\vek q}\) is zero at every instant and the quantity

\[E = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q \tag{26.28}\]

is constant.

Proof

We must show that the scalar \(\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x\) is zero and that the derivative of \(E\) is zero.

  1. For every column \(\vek x\), \(\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x\) is a number, hence equal to its transpose: \(\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x = (\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x)^\T = \vek x^\T\mat G^\T\vek x = -\,\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x\). A number equal to its own negative is zero. For \(\vek x = \dot{\vek q}\) the first claim follows. With two coordinates this is figure 26.33: the force is perpendicular to the velocity.
  2. Because \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat K\,\vek q = \dot{\vek q}^\T(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q)\).
  3. By equation (26.27), \(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = -\mat G\,\dot{\vek q}\), so \(\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}t = -\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat G\,\dot{\vek q} = 0\) by step 1.

Source: GR (2.243), PDF p. 137: gyroscopic forces neither add nor remove energy, they only transfer it between coordinates. The proof follows GR.

With two coordinates the lemma can be seen by eye (figure 26.33). We draw the plane of the coordinates \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) and in it the path along which the state point \((q_1, q_2)\) moves. At the chosen instant the point has the velocity \(\dot{\vek q} = (2, 1)\), tangent to the path (we choose the units so that the numbers are simple). Every skew-symmetric \(2 \times 2\) matrix has the form \(\mat G = g\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). For \(g = 1\) the gyroscopic force is \(-\mat G\,\dot{\vek q} = (-\dot q_2, \dot q_1) = (-1, 2)\): the components of the velocity swap places and one gets a minus, which is a turn by a right angle. The scalar product of the force and the velocity is \((-1) \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = 0\). A force perpendicular to the velocity bends the path but does not change the size of the velocity (for \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\), as for the string, for a general \(\mat M\) the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}\) is conserved). This is how a string pulls a stone that we whirl in a circle: it pulls towards the hand, yet the stone comes no closer to the hand, so the string does no work (work is force times the path in the direction of the force). This view adds what the calculation of step 1 does not show: the gyroscopic force turns the motion. With more than two coordinates the plane cannot be drawn, but the conclusion \(\vek x^\T\mat G\,\vek x = 0\) remains.

What is \(E\) for our string? From step 2 of the proof of lemma 26.24, \(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac L2\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) and \(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac L2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (j\pi/L)^2 q_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) (integration by parts with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}(0) = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}(L) = 0\) gives \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}'\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}''\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\)), so \(E = \tfrac12\int_0^L\big(\mu w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + S\,w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the functional of theorem 26.21, and lemma 26.26 says that it is conserved already in the Galerkin system (checked on a random state with five sines and the band of example A, both sides equal to six places). It is also the energy integral \(\mathcal{E}^* = T_2 - T_0 + V\) of theorem 8.124. The kinetic energy of the transverse motion of the string is \(\tfrac12\mu\int_0^L (w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}})^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (the velocity of a particle from lemma 26.1, the axial part \(\tfrac12\mu v^2 L\) is constant and does not enter the equations), so by theorem 8.97 it splits into \(T_2 = \tfrac12\mu\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(T_1 = \mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(T_0 = \tfrac12\mu v^2\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), the potential energy is \(V = \tfrac12 T\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(T_2 - T_0 + V = E\). The term \(T_1\) is linear in the velocities. From the definition of \(G_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(T_1 = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat G\,\vek q\), and in Lagrange's equations \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tfrac{\partial T_1}{\partial\dot{\vek q}} - \tfrac{\partial T_1}{\partial\vek q} = \tfrac12\mat G\,\dot{\vek q} - \tfrac12\mat G^\T\dot{\vek q} = \mat G\,\dot{\vek q}\). The gyroscopic force is thus the part of the equations that \(T_1\) brings (GR (1.39), (1.40), PDF p. 47), and it does not change the energy by itself, as the skew-symmetry of the matrix in figure 26.32 shows.

26.5.3 Eigenvalues of a gyroscopic system

The trial \(\vek q = \vek x\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\) solves equation (26.27), because the derivative of a complex exponential is a product with \(\lambda\) (theorem 9.34), if \((\lambda^2\mat M + \lambda\mat G + \mat K)\,\vek x = \vek 0\) with \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) from \(\mathbb{C}^n\). For \(\mat G = \mat 0\), \(\mat K\,\vek x = -\lambda^2\mat M\,\vek x\), so by lemma 10.26 \(-\lambda^2\) is real and, for positive definite \(\mat K\), positive, and \(\lambda\) is purely imaginary. Does it stay so when \(\mat G \ne \mat 0\)? The answer depends on the stiffness matrix, as figure 26.36 shows.

Theorem 26.27 Eigenvalues of a gyroscopic system (lastne vrednosti žiroskopskega sistema)

Let \((\mat M, \mat G, \mat K)\) be a gyroscopic system (definition 26.25) and let \(\lambda \in \mathbb{C}\) (one of the points of figure 26.36) with \(\vek x \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{\vek 0\}\) solve \((\lambda^2\mat M + \lambda\mat G + \mat K)\,\vek x = \vek 0\). Denote \(m = \bar{\vek x}^\T\mat M\,\vek x\), \(k = \bar{\vek x}^\T\mat K\,\vek x\) and \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G\,\vek x = \mathrm{i}g\). Then \(m > 0\), \(k\) and \(g\) are real numbers, and

  1. \(\lambda\) is purely imaginary exactly when \(g^2 + 4mk \ge 0\). Then \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega = \big(-g \pm \sqrt{g^2 + 4mk}\big)/(2m)\).
  2. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, \(k > 0\), and so every eigenvalue is purely imaginary and different from zero. The system then has no solution that grows exponentially.
  3. If \(g^2 + 4mk < 0\), \(\lambda\) has a nonzero real part and the system is not stable (it has a solution that grows exponentially).
Proof

We must show that the equation with the eigenvector reduces to a quadratic equation for \(\lambda\) with real coefficients.

  1. Write \(\vek x = \vek a + \mathrm{i}\vek b\). For a real symmetric \(\mat A\), \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat A\,\vek x = \vek a^\T\mat A\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat A\,\vek b + \mathrm{i}\,(\vek a^\T\mat A\,\vek b - \vek b^\T\mat A\,\vek a)\) and the remainder is zero (lemma 10.26). Hence \(m\) and \(k\) are real, and \(m > 0\), because \(\mat M\) is positive definite and \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\).
  2. For \(\mat G\), \(\overline{(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G\,\vek x)} = \vek x^\T\mat G\,\bar{\vek x}\), which is a number equal to its transpose \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G^\T\vek x = -\,\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G\,\vek x\). A number equal to the negative of its conjugate is purely imaginary: \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G\,\vek x = \mathrm{i}g\) with real \(g\).
  3. We multiply the equation \((\lambda^2\mat M + \lambda\mat G + \mat K)\,\vek x = \vek 0\) from the left by \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\): \(m\lambda^2 + \mathrm{i}g\,\lambda + k = 0\). The solutions are \(\lambda = \big(-\mathrm{i}g \pm \sqrt{-(g^2 + 4mk)}\big)/(2m)\).
  4. For \(g^2 + 4mk \ge 0\) the root is \(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{g^2 + 4mk}\), so \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\,\big(-g \pm \sqrt{g^2 + 4mk}\big)/(2m)\) is purely imaginary, like the points on the imaginary axis in the example of figure 26.36. For \(g^2 + 4mk < 0\) the root is real and nonzero, so \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda = \pm\sqrt{|g^2 + 4mk|}/(2m) \ne 0\), like the points on the circle off the imaginary axis. This is point 1.
  5. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, \(k = \vek a^\T\mat K\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat K\,\vek b > 0\) by step 1, so \(g^2 + 4mk > 0\) and by step 4 \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\). The product of the solutions \(\omega\) is \(-k/m < 0\), so \(\omega\) and the other root have different signs and \(\omega \ne 0\). No solution grows either: by lemma 26.26, \(\tfrac12\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q \le E(0)\) for every \(t\), and since \(\vek u^\T\mat K\,\vek u \ge \kappa\,\vek u^\T\vek u\) with \(\kappa > 0\), the smallest value of \(\vek u^\T\mat K\,\vek u\) at \(\vek u^\T\vek u = 1\) (it exists by the extreme value theorem), we get \(\vek q^\T\vek q \le 2E(0)/\kappa\). This is point 2.
  6. The determinant \(\Delta(\lambda) = \det(\lambda^2\mat M + \lambda\mat G + \mat K)\) equals the determinant of the transposed matrix, \(\det(\lambda^2\mat M - \lambda\mat G + \mat K) = \Delta(-\lambda)\), because \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric and \(\mat G^\T = -\mat G\) (GR (2.246), PDF p. 138). If \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue, so is \(-\lambda\): the dots in figure 26.36 lie in pairs, mirrored through the origin. For \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda \ne 0\) one of the values \(\lambda\), \(-\lambda\) therefore has \(a = \mathrm{Re} > 0\) and an eigenvector \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\). The real part \(\vek q(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\vek y\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\big)\) is a solution, because the system is real. It is not zero: for \(\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda = 0\), \(\vek q = \mathrm{e}^{at}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\vek y\) and we may take \(\vek y\) real (the kernel of a real matrix is closed under real and imaginary parts), and for \(\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda = \beta \ne 0\), at \(\beta t = 0\) and \(\beta t = \pi/2\) the values of \(\mathrm{e}^{-at}\vek q\) are \(\mathrm{Re}\,\vek y\) and \(-\mathrm{Im}\,\vek y\), at least one of which is nonzero. The solution grows as \(\mathrm{e}^{at}\). This is point 3.

Source: GR (2.244) to (2.247), PDF p. 137 to 138: the eigenvalues come in pairs \(\lambda\), \(-\lambda\), and a real eigenvalue is possible only if the effective stiffness matrix is not positive definite. GR concludes on p. 138 that the eigenvalues are therefore only real or purely imaginary, which is not so: the pairs \(\lambda\), \(-\lambda\) and \(\lambda\), \(\bar\lambda\) also give quadruples \(\pm a \pm b\,\mathrm{i}\), as at \(g = 1\) in figure 26.36. The notation with \(m\), \(g\), \(k\) and the condition \(g^2 + 4mk \ge 0\) is ours.

How many eigenvalues does the system have for positive definite \(\mat K\)? The polynomial \(\Delta(\lambda)\) has degree \(2n\), because \(\mat M\) is positive definite, so there are \(2n\) eigenvalues (with multiplicity), all \(\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). The eigenvalues \(\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) lie on the imaginary axis of figure 26.36. The eigenvector for \(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the conjugate of the eigenvector for \(\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), because the system is real. For \(\mat G = \mat 0\) the eigenvector can be real. For \(\mat G \ne \mat 0\) it is in general complex, as in the stabilization example below with \(\vek x = (1, \pm\mathrm{i})^\T\). This is the mathematical form of what we saw in the moving string as the phase factor \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\) in theorem 26.13: the shapes are no longer standing, the waves travel. We prove the orthogonality of these modes in section 26.6.

As a special case take \(N = 1\). Then \(\mat G = (0)\), because \(G_{11} = 0\), and \(\omega_1^2 = K_{11}/M_{11} = S\,\pi^2/(\mu L^2)\), so \(\omega_1 = (\pi/L)\sqrt{c^2 - v^2}\). For the band of example A (0,9 × 50 mm, \(L = 1\) m, tension 100 MPa, steel \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(c = 113{,}23\) m/s, the dimensions and speed are our choice) at \(v = 40\) m/s this is \(f_1 = 52{,}963\) Hz, while the exact value from theorem 26.13 is \(49{,}548\) Hz: the approximation is \(6{,}89\,\%\) too high (the first dot of the blackwhite curve in figure 26.38). What is missing? A single shape cannot represent the coupling between the shapes \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) that \(G_{12} \ne 0\) brings. Take \(N = 2, 4, 8\): \(f_1 = 49{,}783\) (\(+0{,}47\,\%\)), \(49{,}584\) (\(+0{,}07\,\%\)) and \(49{,}554\) Hz (\(+0{,}01\,\%\)), the next three dots of the same curve. For \(N = 2\), \(f_2 = 112{,}695\) Hz against the exact \(99{,}097\) Hz. The error decreases at our \(v/c = 0{,}353\), but not uniformly over the frequencies: the second frequency needs more shapes than the first (figure 26.38).

26.5.4 Gyroscopic stabilization

Theorem 26.27 requires a positive definite \(\mat K\). What if it is not? For \(\mat G = \mat 0\), theorem 8.135 says that the equilibrium is unstable if there is a \(\vek u\) with \(\vek u^\T\mat K\,\vek u < 0\). Does the same hold when \(\mat G \ne \mat 0\)? Let us look first at the example from GR, then at an example that we can compute to the end by hand and that is drawn in figure 26.36.

Example 26.28 Inertia wheel: stability when positive definiteness is lost (kolo z maso: stabilnost ob izgubi pozitivne definitnosti)

GR (example 2.9, PDF p. 139 to 141) takes a mass \(m = 1\) on springs of stiffness \(k_1 = 50\) and \(k_2 = 98\) in a wheel turning with constant angular velocity \(\Omega\) (the numbers are GR's). The matrices are \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\), \(\mat G = 2m\Omega\begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat K^* = \mathrm{diag}(k_1 - \Omega^2 m,\ k_2 - \Omega^2 m)\) (GR PDF p. 140). The matrix \(\mat K^*\) is positive definite for \(\Omega < \omega_1 = \sqrt{50} = 7{,}0711\). The eigenvalues for three speeds (computed in num-05.py):

\(\Omega\) \(\mat K^*\) \(\det\mat K^*\) eigenvalues \(\lambda\)
\(5\) \(\mathrm{diag}(25, 73)\) \(1825\) \(\pm 3{,}1131\,\mathrm{i}\), \(\pm 13{,}7226\,\mathrm{i}\)
\(8\) \(\mathrm{diag}(-14, 34)\) \(-476\) \(\pm 1{,}3092\), \(\pm 16{,}6648\,\mathrm{i}\)
\(12\) \(\mathrm{diag}(-94, -46)\) \(4324\) \(\pm 3{,}1865\,\mathrm{i}\), \(\pm 20{,}636\,\mathrm{i}\)

For \(\Omega = 5\) theorem 26.27 applies, everything is purely imaginary. For \(\Omega = 8\), \(\mat K^*\) has one negative eigenvalue, \(\det\mat K^* < 0\), and we get the real pair \(\pm 1{,}3092\), so instability, as theorem 26.27 (point 3) allows. For \(\Omega = 12\) both eigenvalues of \(\mat K^*\) are negative, \(\mat K^*\) is negative definite and \(\det\mat K^* > 0\), yet all eigenvalues are again purely imaginary: the system is stable although \(\mat K^*\) is not positive definite. GR PDF p. 141 ascribes this precisely to the gyroscopic forces.

Theorem 8.135 assumes \(\mat G = \mat 0\), and the example shows that the conclusion fails for \(\mat G \ne \mat 0\). The next example shows the mechanism in its simplest form, with one number \(g\) (figure 26.36).

We take a dimensionless system with two coordinates, unit mass \(\mat M = \mat I\), negative definite stiffness matrix \(\mat K = -\mat I\) and \(\mat G = g\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\) with a number \(g > 0\). Without the gyroscopic term (\(g = 0\)), \(\lambda^2 = 1\) and \(\lambda = \pm 1\) twice: the system is unstable, as theorem 8.135 says. In the figure the axes show the real and imaginary parts of \(\lambda\). At \(g = 0\) the eigenvalues are on the real axis at \(\pm 1\). The characteristic equation is

\[\det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^2 - 1 & g\lambda\\ -g\lambda & \lambda^2 - 1\end{pmatrix} = (\lambda^2 - 1)^2 + g^2\lambda^2 = \lambda^4 + (g^2 - 2)\,\lambda^2 + 1 = 0 . \tag{26.29}\]

For \(\nu = \lambda^2\), \(\nu^2 + (g^2 - 2)\nu + 1 = 0\) with discriminant \((g^2 - 2)^2 - 4 = g^2(g^2 - 4)\).

  • For \(g < 2\) the discriminant is negative, \(\nu\) is not real and so \(\lambda\) is neither real nor purely imaginary. Since the product of the roots \(\nu\) is \(1\), \(|\nu| = 1\) and \(|\lambda| = 1\): the eigenvalues lie on a circle of radius \(1\). At \(g = 1\), \(\lambda = \pm 0{,}866 \pm 0{,}5\,\mathrm{i}\) (num-05.py), so \(\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda = 0{,}866 > 0\) and the solution grows as \(\mathrm{e}^{0{,}866\,t}\) (theorem 26.27, point 3).
  • At \(g = 2\) the discriminant is zero, \(\nu = -1\) doubly and \(\lambda = \pm\mathrm{i}\) doubly: the eigenvalues arrive at the imaginary axis. The double eigenvalue has only one eigenvector, so the solution released from rest at \((1, 0)\) grows as \(|\vek q| = \sqrt{1 + t^2}\).
  • For \(g > 2\) the discriminant is positive, the sum of the roots is \(-(g^2 - 2) < 0\) and the product \(1 > 0\), so both \(\nu\) are real and negative, hence all four eigenvalues are purely imaginary. At \(g = 3\), \(\nu^2 + 7\nu + 1 = 0\), \(\nu = (-7 \pm \sqrt{45})/2 = -0{,}1459\) and \(-6{,}854\), \(\lambda = \pm 0{,}382\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(\pm 2{,}618\,\mathrm{i}\). Computing the eigenvalues of the state matrix gives the same.

Why the gyroscopic term holds the system can be seen in the plane of the coordinates (figure 26.37). The stiffness \(\mat K = -\mat I\) means a force \(-\mat K\,\vek q = +\vek q\) that pushes the state point away from the origin, the harder the farther it is, like a ball off the top of a dome. The gyroscopic force is perpendicular to the velocity (figure 26.33) and bends the path. Let us ask whether the point can circle. We draw a circle of radius \(r = 1\) and on it a point circling anticlockwise with angular velocity \(\omega\), so with the velocity \(\omega r\) along the tangent. On it act the outward force of size \(r\) and the gyroscopic force of size \(g\,\omega r\), which points to the centre because it is perpendicular to the tangent. The circling \(\vek q = r\,(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)^\T\) has the acceleration \(\ddot{\vek q} = -\omega^2\vek q\), so of size \(\omega^2 r\) towards the centre, and with unit mass the surplus of force towards the centre must supply it: \(g\,\omega r - r = \omega^2 r\), which is

\[\omega^2 - g\,\omega + 1 = 0 , \qquad \omega = \frac{g \pm \sqrt{g^2 - 4}}{2} . \tag{26.30}\]

A real \(\omega\) exists exactly for \(g \ge 2\). This is the same limit as from the discriminant above, this time from the three forces in the figure. At \(g = 3\), \(\omega = 0{,}382\) or \(2{,}618\), the sizes of the eigenvalues \(\pm 0{,}382\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(\pm 2{,}618\,\mathrm{i}\). The slower circling is drawn: the gyroscopic force is \(3 \cdot 0{,}382 = 1{,}146\), the outward force \(1\), and the surplus \(0{,}146\) is exactly \(\omega^2 r = 0{,}382^2\). For \(g < 2\) there is no such \(\omega\), because \(\omega^2 - g\,\omega + 1 > 0\) for every \(\omega\): at every speed of circling the gyroscopic force \(g\,\omega r\) is smaller than the sum \(r + \omega^2 r\) that it would have to supply, and the point does not stay on the circle.

Panel (b) of figure 26.37 shows, in the plane \(q_1\), \(q_2\), what happens when we release the point from rest at \((1, 0)\). Without the gyroscopic term it goes straight away and at \(t = 2\) it is at \(\cosh 2 = 3{,}76\). At \(g = 1\) the gyroscopic force bends the path, but too little: at \(t = 2\) the point is \(3{,}32\) from the origin and moves away along a spiral. At \(g = 3\) the force turns it back before it escapes, and the path stays between the circles of radii \(1\) and \(1{,}34\). The motion there is the sum of the two circlings of equation (26.30) with the shares \(1{,}171\) and \(-0{,}171\) (from the initial conditions), so the largest distance is \(1{,}171 + 0{,}171 = 1{,}342\) and the smallest \(1{,}171 - 0{,}171 = 1\).

The matrix \(\mat K = -\mat I\) is negative definite, yet for \(g > 2\) the system stays bounded: this is gyroscopic stabilization (žiroskopska stabilizacija). The reason is in the condition of theorem 26.27: for the eigenvector \(\vek x = (1, \pm\mathrm{i})^\T/\sqrt2\), \(m = 1\), \(k = -1\) and \(\bar{\vek x}^\T\mat G\,\vek x = \pm\mathrm{i}g\), so \(g^2 + 4mk = g^2 - 4\), which is nonnegative exactly for \(g \ge 2\) (then the points lie on the imaginary axis, for \(g < 2\) off it) (the labels \(m\), \(k\), \(g\) are those of the theorem). The gyroscopic term must be large enough that the expression \(g^2 + 4mk\) under the root in step 4 of the proof is not negative. Positive definiteness is therefore a sufficient, but not a necessary condition for purely imaginary eigenvalues. We shall meet the same structure in the moving beam: in section 26.7, for \(T L^2/EI = 100\) the system is stable between \(v = 11{,}810\) and \(v = 12{,}176\) (dimensionless), although \(\mat K\) is not positive definite there.

26.5.5 Convergence of the Galerkin frequencies

How well does the Galerkin system predict the frequencies of the moving string, where we know the exact value? Take \(L = c = \mu = 1\) (dimensionless) and the ratios \(v/c = 0{,}5\) and \(0{,}9\). The exact frequencies are \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi\,(1 - v^2/c^2)\) (theorem 26.13): \(\omega_1 = 2{,}35619\) and \(\omega_2 = 4{,}71239\) at \(0{,}5\), \(\omega_1 = 0{,}59690\) and \(\omega_2 = 1{,}19381\) at \(0{,}9\). We write system (26.25) as a first-order system of dimension \(2N\) with the state vector \((\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) (GR §2.11.3, PDF p. 139) and the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\mat 0 & \mat I\\ -\mat M^{-1}\mat K & -\mat M^{-1}\mat G\end{pmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are \(\lambda\), and solve it with \(N = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\) sines. The largest absolute real part of the eigenvalues is everywhere below \(4 \cdot 10^{-15}\), as theorem 26.27 demands. The relative errors \(\varepsilon_1\) and \(\varepsilon_2\) of the first and second frequency are shown in figure 26.38.

We draw the axes: the number of sines \(N\) from \(1\) to \(32\) and the error \(\varepsilon\) from \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{1}\), both logarithmic, so that the powers have the same length. First the blue curve: at \(v/c = 0{,}5\), \(\varepsilon_1 = 1{,}37 \cdot 10^{-2}\) at \(N = 2\) and \(3{,}95 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at \(N = 32\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 0{,}315\) at \(N = 2\) and \(1{,}51 \cdot 10^{-5}\) at \(N = 32\). Then the orange curve at \(v/c = 0{,}9\): \(\varepsilon_1 = 0{,}111\) and \(1{,}34 \cdot 10^{-5}\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 3{,}74\) (frequency \(5{,}65\) against the exact \(1{,}19\)) and \(5{,}38 \cdot 10^{-5}\). The blackwhite curve is the band of example A at \(v/c = 0{,}353\), from \(6{,}89 \cdot 10^{-2}\) with one sine to \(1{,}93 \cdot 10^{-6}\) with \(32\). The labels say which colour is which speed. At both speeds the error decreases about eightfold with every doubling of \(N\) (for example \(\varepsilon_1\) at \(0{,}5\): \(1{,}73 \cdot 10^{-3} \to 2{,}31 \cdot 10^{-4} \to 3{,}06 \cdot 10^{-5} \to 3{,}95 \cdot 10^{-6}\)), but at \(v/c = 0{,}9\) more shapes are needed to get onto this path: \(\varepsilon_2\) at \(N = 4\) is \(0{,}120\) at \(0{,}9\) and \(6 \cdot 10^{-3}\) at \(0{,}5\). All computed errors are positive, so the approximations are above the exact values. This is an observation on the computed cases, not a proof: Ritz's upper-bound theorem (theorem 10.59) requires \(\mat G = \mat 0\), and for gyroscopic systems we do not prove an upper bound.

Why so poor at \(v/c = 0{,}9\)? Take the shape of mode \(n = 1\) from theorem 26.13, that is \(\psi_1(x) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_1x}\sin(\pi x/L)\) with \(k_1 = \pi v/(cL)\) (the theorem calls it \(\varphi\), here it is \(\psi_1\), since \(\varphi_1\) is the first sine in this section). Its real and imaginary parts are the shapes of the string at two instants, a quarter of a period apart, figure 26.39. The string moves as \(w = \mathrm{Re}(\psi_1\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_1 t})\), so at \(t = 0\) the shape is \(\mathrm{Re}\,\psi_1\), and a quarter of a period earlier, at \(\omega_1 t = -\pi/2\), it is \(\mathrm{Re}(-\mathrm{i}\,\psi_1) = \mathrm{Im}\,\psi_1\).

We draw the rest line between the eyelets, a distance \(L\) apart, and above it the shape at rest \(\sin(\pi x/L)\), which is the first sine. Over it we lay the real part of the mode: the factor \(\cos(0{,}9\,\pi x/L)\) falls at \(x = 0{,}5\,L\) to \(0{,}156\) (at \(v/c = 0{,}5\) it would be \(0{,}707\)), so at this instant the middle of the string is nearly on the rest line and the crest moves towards the left eyelet. Next we draw the imaginary part, which is the shape of the string a quarter of a period earlier and has its crest towards the right eyelet. Since neither of these shapes resembles a single sine, the first sine \(\sin(\pi x/L)\) captures only the fraction \(0{,}083\) of the squared norm of the real part and \(0{,}968\) of the imaginary part (the squared norm over \(x \in [0, L]\)), and at \(v/c = 0{,}5\) it captures \(0{,}922\) of both. The first two sines together capture at \(v/c = 0{,}9\) \(0{,}998\) of the real and \(0{,}975\) of the imaginary part (recomputed in num-05.py), so the real part lies almost wholly in the second sine. So the first mode at \(v/c = 0{,}9\) needs at least two sines, and the higher modes more. WM1990 (PDF p. 6) writes the same: at high transport speed the eigenfunctions of the string at rest, which it calls solutions with constant phase, are a poor approximation of the motion and many of them are needed for a sufficiently accurate computation. For the string we avoid this because the complex modes are known (section 26.3). For systems that have no closed formula, for example finite elements (section 26.8), we must take enough shapes, and we use the eigenfunctions of the moving string for orthogonality in section 26.6.

Let us summarize what we showed and how. With the sines and the residual requirement (lemma 26.24) we reduced the moving string to the system \(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat G\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek 0\) with a matrix \(\mat G\) that is skew-symmetric because of the fixed eyelets, and, because of the parity of the shapes (figure 26.30), nonzero only for \(i + j\) odd. From the skew-symmetry we derived that gyroscopic forces do no work (lemma 26.26) and that for positive definite \(\mat K\) all eigenvalues are purely imaginary (theorem 26.27). Positive definiteness is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one: example 26.28 has a negative definite \(\mat K\) and a stable system. For our string \(\mat K\) is positive definite exactly when \(S = T - \mu v^2 > 0\), that is for \(v < c\) (section 26.4). In the computed cases the frequencies lie above the exact ones and converge, more slowly at high speed, because the sines are not adapted to the travelling waves.
