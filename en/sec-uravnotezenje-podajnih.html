Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.8 Balancing flexible rotors (uravnoteženje podajnih rotorjev)

In section 24.2 we saw that unbalance excites a whirl of the shaft whose response grows at the critical speed. Now we ask what to do about it. We cannot remove the unbalance, because we do not know where on the shaft it is, but we can add or remove mass at places we can reach, so that the effects add up to zero. This is called balancing (uravnoteženje). The word has the same origin as balance: a rotor is balanced when the forces that the turning causes on its masses are in equilibrium like the pans of a scale, and the shaft feels no force that turns with it. A rotor is flexible (podajen, from "podati", to give way under load) when it visibly bends under these forces. A rigid rotor (togi rotor), which does not bend and moves as a rigid body, is balanced in two planes by the procedure of chapter 23. Here we show why this is not enough for a flexible rotor, and derive two procedures that are: influence coefficients (vplivni koeficienti), which need no model of the shaft, and modal balancing (modalno uravnoteženje), which needs the mode shapes of the shaft. We prove both with the response of section 24.2 and the models of chapters 10 and 13.

From history

According to the source FPGL (PDF p. 358) the rotor of smaller machines is usually rigid and moves as a rigid body. In recent decades there has been a trend towards flexible rotors, which deform under the forces and moments of unbalance, and these are common in larger units, for example turbogenerators (FPGL, PDF p. 358). Many balancing techniques were introduced in the 1950s and 1960s ("1950s and 1960s"), and a historical survey, especially for modal balancing, is given by the papers of Bishop and Gladwell (1959) and Parkinson (1965, 1966, 1967) (FPGL, PDF p. 358). The source does not say what these papers proved, so we cite them only as a survey.

The question of the section is now sharper: where on a flexible shaft should we add masses, and how do we compute them from measurements. First we set up a model (section 24.8.1), then we compute the response of the flexible shaft by modes, because linearity follows from it, and every procedure stands on linearity (section 24.8.2). Then comes the influence coefficient procedure for one plane and for several planes (sections 24.8.3 and 24.8.4), and last the case in which two planes are not enough, and the modal procedure (sections 24.8.5 and 24.8.6).

24.8.1 Model: what we add and what we measure

Take a steel shaft of length \(L = 1\) m and diameter \(40\) mm, turning in two rigid short bearings (chapter 13, the same as in section 24.2). On it we choose three balance planes (ravnine uravnoteženja), transverse planes at \(0{,}1L\), \(0{,}5L\) and \(0{,}9L\), in which we can fix or remove mass. The added or removed mass, for example a bolt or a drilled hole, is a point mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) at radius \(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\). In the figure the trial mass is in plane 2. On the shaft there is a mark, a short stretch that the probe detects once per revolution, and this gives us the phase. So we do not describe the mass by clock time but by the angle from the mark. To look at one plane exactly, we step to the end of the shaft and look at it from one side. From the mark we measure the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{c}}\) to the mass, in the sense of rotation, and the radius \(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\) to the axis. The position of the mass is \(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\,e^{i\theta_{\mathrm{c}}}\) in the complex plane. By section 24.2 its unbalance (neuravnoteženost, definition 24.6) is the complex number

\[U = m_{\mathrm{c}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\,e^{i\theta_{\mathrm{c}}} . \tag{24.47}\]

Why is this the right measure? The shaft turns with angular velocity \(\Omega\), so the mass on a circle of radius \(\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\) must feel a force towards the axis \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\Omega^2\) (Newton's second law for motion on a circle, chapter 8), and by the third law the mass pulls the shaft outwards with the same force. The force has magnitude \(|U|\Omega^2\), turns together with the mass, and so the whole exciting force that the mass causes is, in complex form, \(U\Omega^2 e^{i\Omega t}\). The magnitude and the angle of \(U\) are therefore all we need to know about the mass.

The probe stands next to the shaft, in the figure at \(0{,}3L\), and measures the displacement of its centre from the bearing axis. Since the supports are equal in all directions (isotropic), the orbit of the centre is a circle (section 24.2), and the measurement is the complex amplitude \(r\) of the circular orbit, that is \(p(t) = r\,e^{i\Omega t}\) for the complex displacement \(p = v + i w\) of section 24.1. Its angle \(\arg r\) tells by how much the centre leads the mark at the moment the mark passes the probe. The letter \(r\) here means the reading, and not the frequency ratio \(\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) of chapter 9, which we do not use in this section.

The model has these assumptions, all named:

  • The response is linear in the unbalance: twice the unbalance gives twice the reading, and the readings of two unbalances add. This is the assumption of all balancing procedures (FPGL, PDF p. 357). With bearings of nonlinear stiffness it holds only approximately and the procedure must be repeated (FPGL, PDF p. 357). For our model we prove it in section 24.8.2.
  • The shaft is uniform with the models of chapter 13 (Euler-Bernoulli beam, rotary inertia neglected, which by section 24.5 changes the critical speed by \(0{,}049\) %).
  • The damping is modal with ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}02\) in every mode (definition 10.47), which is a choice of the model and not a measurement. Internal damping is not included (section 24.7).
  • The probe and the mark are ideal, without noise. Noise we show in section 24.8.3.

24.8.2 Response of the flexible shaft by modes

Section 24.2 treated one disk on a massless shaft. The unbalance of a flexible shaft is distributed along its length, so we must first describe it. The centre of mass of the cross-section at \(x\) is displaced from the geometric centre of the shaft by \(a(x)\,e^{i\Omega t}\), where \(a(x)\) is the complex eccentricity (izsrednost), that is, the displacement at the moment when the mark is on the real axis. By chapter 13 each mode \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x) = \sin(n\pi x/L)\) measures how much unbalance it feels, and the response of the shaft to a load by modes follows from theorem 13.46. So we first define how much unbalance a shape feels.

Definition 24.47 Unbalance with respect to a shape (neuravnoteženost glede na obliko)

Let a shaft of length \(L\) have mass per unit length \(\rho A\) (constant), eccentricity \(a\colon [0, L] \to \mathbb C\) (a continuous function), and let \(K\) masses with unbalances \(b_1, \dots, b_{\mathrm{K}} \in \mathbb C\) by equation (24.47) be fixed in the planes \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{K}}\). For a continuous function \(\varphi\colon [0, L] \to \mathbb R\), which we call a shape, the unbalance with respect to the shape (a complex number in kg m) is

\[U(\varphi) = \int_0^L \rho A\,a(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{K}b_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi(x_{\mathrm{j}}) . \tag{24.48}\]

For \(\varphi = \varphi_{\mathrm{n}} = \sin(n\pi x/L)\) we write it \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) and call it the modal unbalance (modalna neuravnoteženost) of mode \(n\). For \(\varphi = 1\) it is the total unbalance (from it comes the force \(\Omega^2\,U(1)\) that the rotor excites as a rigid body), and for \(\varphi = x - L/2\) it is the moment of this unbalance about the midpoint.

Source: FPGL (8.40), PDF p. 382, where it is the scalar product \(\mathbf u_\ell^{\mathsf T}\mathbf b\) of the mode and the unbalance vector. The notation with an integral and a shape \(\varphi\) is ours.

The reason for the name: for \(\varphi = 1\) and \(\varphi = x - L/2\) we get the quantities that the rigid balancing in two planes removes (chapter 23), and for \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) the quantity that by the next theorem excites mode \(n\). The theorem says that these numbers alone decide the response.

Theorem 24.48 Response of the flexible shaft by modes (odziv podajne gredi po načinih)

Let a simply supported shaft of length \(L\) have constant \(EI > 0\) and \(\rho A > 0\), as in theorem 13.46, and let it have eccentricity \(a(x)\) and masses in planes as in definition 24.47. Let it turn with \(\Omega > 0\) and let its centre describe a circular orbit \(p(x, t) = P(x)\,e^{i\Omega t}\) with complex displacement \(p = v + i w\). Let \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = (n\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI/\rho A}\), \(M_{\mathrm{n}} = \rho A L/2\) and \(\Omega \ne \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\). Then \(P(x) = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty}C_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with

\[C_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{\Omega^2\,U_{\mathrm{n}}}{M_{\mathrm{n}}\,\big(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2\big)} . \tag{24.49}\]

With modal damping of ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\) the denominator is \(M_{\mathrm{n}}(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2i\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega)\). The response \(P(x)\) is therefore linear in the eccentricity \(a\) and in the unbalances \(b_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Idea

The shaft has two bendings, one in each plane. We solve each with theorem 13.46 and combine them in one complex equation. The load is the inertia of the unbalanced masses.

Proof

We must show equation (24.49) for the real and imaginary parts of \(P\), then add the point masses and the damping.

  1. The centre of mass of the cross-section at \(x\) has coordinates \(v + \mathrm{Re}(a\,e^{i\Omega t})\) and \(w + \mathrm{Im}(a\,e^{i\Omega t})\), where \(v\), \(w\) are the displacements of the geometric centre in the two planes. The inertia force per unit length is mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass (theorem 8.71), and the bending term depends on the displacement of the axis (chapter 13, equation (13.31)). Hence
    \[\rho A\,v_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,v'''' = \rho A\,\Omega^2\,\mathrm{Re}(a\,e^{i\Omega t}) , \qquad \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w'''' = \rho A\,\Omega^2\,\mathrm{Im}(a\,e^{i\Omega t}) ,\]
    because \(\partial_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{Re}(a\,e^{i\Omega t}) = -\Omega^2\,\mathrm{Re}(a\,e^{i\Omega t})\) and this term is moved to the right.
  2. We multiply the second equation by \(i\) and add it to the first. Since the operators are real, \(p = v + i w\) is a solution of the equation \(\rho A\,p_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,p'''' = \rho A\,\Omega^2\,a\,e^{i\Omega t}\).
  3. We insert \(p = P(x)\,e^{i\Omega t}\). We get \(EI\,P'''' - \rho A\,\Omega^2\,P = \rho A\,\Omega^2\,a\) with \(P = P'' = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). This is equation (13.52) with right-hand side \(Q = \rho A\,\Omega^2\,a\). Theorem 13.46 holds for the real and for the imaginary part separately, so for the coefficients \(C_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L P\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x\) we have \(\rho A(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2)\,C_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{L}\rho A\,\Omega^2\int_0^L a\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. Let the mass in plane \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) be spread over a short stretch \([x_{\mathrm{j}} - \eta, x_{\mathrm{j}} + \eta]\) with the same unbalance \(b_{\mathrm{j}}\) and with the triangular density \(g_\eta(x) = \max\big(0, \eta - |x - x_{\mathrm{j}}|\big)/\eta^2\), which is continuous, nonnegative and has integral \(1\) (a triangle with base \(2\eta\) and height \(1/\eta\)). The load \(Q = \Omega^2 b_{\mathrm{j}}\,g_\eta\) is then continuous, as theorem 13.46 requires. Its contribution to the right-hand side of step 3 is \(\frac{2}{L}\,\Omega^2 b_{\mathrm{j}}\int_0^L g_\eta\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Since the integral of \(g_\eta\) is \(1\), this contribution differs from \(\frac{2}{L}\,\Omega^2 b_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x_{\mathrm{j}})\) by at most \(\frac{2}{L}\,\Omega^2|b_{\mathrm{j}}|\max_{|\mathrm{x} - \mathrm{x}_\mathrm{j}| \le \eta}|\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x) - \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x_{\mathrm{j}})|\). As \(\eta \to 0\) this bound tends to zero by the continuity of \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\), so the contribution is \(\frac{2}{L}\Omega^2 b_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x_{\mathrm{j}})\).
  5. We divide step 3 by \(\rho A\,L/2 = M_{\mathrm{n}}\) and sum the contributions of the planes. The right-hand side is \(\Omega^2\,U_{\mathrm{n}}/M_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) from equation (24.48), which is equation (24.49). The series converges, because the \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) are bounded and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 \sim n^4\).
  6. Damping: with modal damping the equation of the mode is \(M_{\mathrm{n}}\ddot q_{\mathrm{n}} + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} M_{\mathrm{n}}\dot q_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 q_{\mathrm{n}} = \) force (definition 10.47), and for the response \(q_{\mathrm{n}} = C_{\mathrm{n}} e^{i\Omega t}\) the factor \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2\) is replaced by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2i\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega\) (theorem 9.52).
  7. The right-hand side is linear in \(a\) and \(b_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the denominators do not depend on them, so \(P\) is linear in them.

Source: Expansion by modes: theorem 13.46 (GR section 4.2.3, PDF pp. 252-253). Model of the shaft with eccentricity and unbalances in planes: FPGL section 6.3 (PDF pp. 269-275). The proof is not from the sources.

The theorem gives three things that we will need. First, the linearity that every procedure assumes is proved and not only assumed. Second, the numbers \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) decide the response, so two equally unbalanced shafts differ only in these numbers. Third, for \(\Omega\) near \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) one term of the series dominates, because the denominator is small.

Example 24.49 A shaft with uniform eccentricity

Take a shaft without a disk (\(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³), where \(\rho A = 9{,}8018\) kg/m and \(\omega_1 = 494{,}74\) 1/s, that is \(4724\) rev/min. The eccentricity is uniform and real, \(a(x) = a_0 = 20\) μm, so the total unbalance is \(\rho A\,a_0\,L = 196{,}04\) g mm. By equation (24.48) \(U_{\mathrm{n}} = \rho A\,a_0\,L\,(1 - \cos n\pi)/(n\pi)\), because \(\int_0^L\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x = L(1 - \cos n\pi)/(n\pi)\). The even modes are zero (\(U_2 = 0\)), the odd ones give \(U_1 = \rho A a_0 L\cdot 2/\pi = 124{,}80\) g mm, \(U_3 = 41{,}60\) g mm and \(U_5 = 24{,}96\) g mm. At \(\Omega = \omega_1\) with \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) mode 1 dominates by theorem 24.48, and at midspan \(|P(L/2)| = 4a_0/(2\pi\zeta) = 0{,}6366\) mm, which with nine modes is \(0{,}6366\) mm. The displacement \(0{,}64\) mm is \(32\) times the eccentricity, since the ratio at resonance is \(4/(2\pi\zeta)\).

24.8.3 One plane is balanced with an influence coefficient

How do we find \(U_{\mathrm{c}}\) without knowing where the unbalance is? We measure how much the reading changes when we add a known mass, and from it we compute what mass we would have to add to make the reading vanish. The change of the reading per unit of added unbalance we call the influence coefficient, because it tells how much the added unbalance influences the reading.

Definition 24.50 Influence coefficient (vplivni koeficient)

Let a rotor have a balance plane and a probe, and let the reading \(r\) at spin speed \(\Omega\) after fixing an unbalance \(U\) in the plane be written as \(r(U) = r_0 + \alpha\,U\), where \(r_0 \in \mathbb C\) is the reading without added mass. The complex number \(\alpha\) (unit m per kg m), which does not depend on \(U\), is the influence coefficient (vplivni koeficient) of the plane and the probe at speed \(\Omega\). The notation makes sense by theorem 24.48, which proves that the reading is linear in the unbalances, and by theorem 24.7 for the disk, where \(\alpha = \Omega^2/(k - m\Omega^2 + ic\Omega)\).

Source: FPGL (8.10)-(8.13), PDF p. 364, where \(R(\Omega)\) is the response function. The content is the same, the notation is ours.

Theorem 24.51 Single-plane balancing (uravnoteženje v eni ravnini)

Let \(r(U) = r_0 + \alpha U\) by definition 24.50, and let a trial unbalance \(U_{\mathrm{t}} \ne 0\) give a reading \(r_1\) with \(r_1 \ne r_0\). Then \(\alpha = (r_1 - r_0)/U_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the only unbalance for which the reading is zero is

\[U_{\mathrm{c}} = -\,\frac{r_0}{\alpha} = -\,\frac{r_0\,U_{\mathrm{t}}}{r_1 - r_0} . \tag{24.50}\]

If the whole original unbalance \(U_0\) is in this plane, then \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = -U_0\) and the rotor is balanced at every spin speed.

Idea

The line in the complex plane \(U \mapsto r_0 + \alpha U\) is determined by two points. The trial mass gives the second point, and we compute the zero of the line.

Proof
  1. From \(r_1 = r_0 + \alpha U_{\mathrm{t}}\) it follows that \(\alpha = (r_1 - r_0)/U_{\mathrm{t}}\), and \(\alpha \ne 0\) because \(r_1 \ne r_0\).
  2. Because \(\alpha \ne 0\), the equation \(r_0 + \alpha U = 0\) has exactly one solution \(U = -r_0/\alpha\). We insert \(\alpha\) from step 1 and get equation (24.50).
  3. Let all the excitation be the unbalance \(U_0\) in the plane. A rotor without any unbalance reads zero, and the reading is linear in the unbalance of the plane (theorem 24.48). So \(r_0 = \alpha\,U_0\) with the same \(\alpha\) as for the added mass, since \(U_0\) lies in the same plane. By step 2 \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = -r_0/\alpha = -U_0\). The total unbalance \(U_0 + U_{\mathrm{c}}\) is zero, so all \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) are zero, and by theorem 24.48 the response is zero at every \(\Omega\) (for the disk by theorem 24.7).

Source: FPGL (8.14), PDF p. 364, and example 8.4.1, PDF p. 365. The proof is not from the source.

Example 24.52 The rotor R1 at 1187 rev/min

Take the rotor R1 of section 24.2: a disk of \(49{,}009\) kg at midspan with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 155{,}32\) 1/s (\(1483\) rev/min), \(\zeta = 0{,}02\). We want to balance it at \(\Omega = 0{,}8\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is \(1187\) rev/min. The unbalance is in the plane of the disk, but the balancer does not know it. We proceed:

  1. We draw the axes of the complex readings and the axes of the unbalance. Without added mass the reading \(r_0\) is \(0{,}0885\) mm at \(24{,}9^\circ\) ahead of the mark. The hidden unbalance is \(U_0\), \(2450\) g mm at \(30^\circ\).
  2. We fix a trial mass of \(10\) g at radius \(150\) mm at \(0^\circ\), so \(U_{\mathrm{t}} = 1500\) g mm. The reading is \(r_1\) \(= 0{,}1382\) mm at \(13{,}6^\circ\).
  3. The change of the reading is \(r_{\mathrm{d}} = r_1 - r_0 = 0{,}0542\) mm at \(-5{,}1^\circ\), and the influence coefficient is \(\alpha = r_{\mathrm{d}}/U_{\mathrm{t}} = 36{,}13\) mm per kg m at \(-5{,}1^\circ\). The formula \(\Omega^2/(k - m\Omega^2 + ic\Omega)\) of section 24.2 gives the same value.
  4. The reading is to be zero, so the target is the change of reading \(-r_0\). We reach it if we turn the trial mass by \(150^\circ\) clockwise and enlarge it by \(|r_0|/|r_{\mathrm{d}}| = 1{,}634\). In the plane of the masses this is the correction \(U_{\mathrm{c}}\), \(2450\) g mm at \(-150^\circ\) (the angle between \(U_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(U_{\mathrm{c}}\)), that is \(16{,}34\) g at radius \(150\) mm at \(-150^\circ\). Indeed \(U_{\mathrm{c}} = -U_0\).

So with exact readings the reading becomes zero. But every real measurement has an error, so we test the procedure on a wrong reading.

Example 24.53 The first trial reading is 3 % too high

For the rotor R1 let the reading \(r_1\) be \(3\) % too large because of a probe error, \(r_1' = 1{,}03\,r_1\) (the phase is correct). By equation (24.50) we get \(U_{\mathrm{c}}'\), and the unbalance \(U_0 + U_{\mathrm{c}}' = 175\) g mm remains, which is \(7{,}1\) % of the original. The reading at \(0{,}8\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) after the correction is \(0{,}0063\) mm instead of zero. Why is the error \(2{,}4\) times larger than \(3\) %? Let \(r_1' = r_1 + \delta\) with \(\delta = 0{,}03\,r_1\) and \(r_{\mathrm{d}}' = r_{\mathrm{d}} + \delta\). Since \(r_0 = \alpha U_0\) and \(r_{\mathrm{d}} = \alpha U_{\mathrm{t}}\),

\[U_0 + U_{\mathrm{c}}' = U_0\Big(1 - \frac{r_{\mathrm{d}}}{r_{\mathrm{d}} + \delta}\Big) = U_0\,\frac{\delta}{r_{\mathrm{d}}'} , \qquad \frac{|U_0 + U_{\mathrm{c}}'|}{|U_0|} = 0{,}03\,\frac{|r_1|}{|r_{\mathrm{d}}'|} = 0{,}03 \cdot \frac{0{,}1382}{0{,}0581} = 0{,}0713 . \tag{24.51}\]

The error \(\delta\) is proportional to the reading \(r_1\), but it is divided by the small change \(r_{\mathrm{d}}'\): the trial mass of \(10\) g changes the reading by only \(0{,}61\,|r_0|\) (\(|r_{\mathrm{d}}| = 0{,}0542\) mm against \(|r_0| = 0{,}0885\) mm), so the error grows. With a trial mass of \(25\) g the remainder by equation (24.51) is \(4{,}6\) %, with \(50\) g it is \(3{,}7\) %, because the ratio \(|r_1|/|r_{\mathrm{d}}'|\) falls from \(2{,}38\) to \(1{,}53\) and \(1{,}25\). FPGL (PDF p. 364) therefore recommends a trial mass for which \(|r_{\mathrm{d}}|\) is similar to \(|r_0|\). A phase error of \(3^\circ\) with the same \(10\) g mass, however, leaves \(13{,}9\) %. A turn by \(3^\circ\), that is \(0{,}052\) rad, moves the tip of \(r_1\) by \(\delta\) with \(|\delta| = 0{,}052\,|r_1|\), which is \(1{,}75\) times more than the magnitude error of \(3\) %. Equation (24.51) holds for every \(\delta\), and this displacement too is divided by the small \(|r_{\mathrm{d}}'| = 0{,}0521\) mm.

So the procedure has limits, which the theorem hides in the assumption \(r_1 \ne r_0\): if the trial mass changes the reading too little, \(\alpha\) is poorly determined and the correction is not reliable.

24.8.4 Several planes and several probes

The unbalance of a flexible shaft is usually not in any of the planes in which we can make corrections. So we balance the readings and not the unbalance: with \(N\) planes we can null \(N\) readings (\(N\) probes at one speed or fewer probes at several speeds, FPGL, PDF p. 369). Each plane influences each reading.

Theorem 24.54 Multi-plane balancing (uravnoteženje v več ravninah)

Let a rotor have \(N\) planes and \(N\) readings \(\mathbf r \in \mathbb C^N\) at spin speed \(\Omega\), and let \(\mathbf r(\mathbf b) = \mathbf r_0 + \mathbf R\,\mathbf b\) hold for every unbalance vector \(\mathbf b \in \mathbb C^N\) in the planes, with a matrix \(\mathbf R \in \mathbb C^{N \times N}\) of influence coefficients. If a trial unbalance \(U_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{j}}\ne 0\) in plane \(j\) only gives readings \(\mathbf r^{(j)}\), then column \(j\) of the matrix \(\mathbf R\) equals \((\mathbf r^{(j)} - \mathbf r_0)/U_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{j}}\). If \(\det\mathbf R \ne 0\), the equation \(\mathbf r(\mathbf b) = 0\) has exactly one solution

\[\mathbf b_{\mathrm{c}} = -\,\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r_0 , \tag{24.52}\]

and after the correction all readings are zero. If \(\det\mathbf R = 0\), a solution does not exist in general.

Proof
  1. In trial \(j\) we have \(\mathbf b = U_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{j}}\,\mathbf e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\mathbf r^{(j)} = \mathbf r_0 + U_{\mathrm{t},\mathrm{j}}\,\mathbf R\mathbf e_{\mathrm{j}}\), and \(\mathbf R\mathbf e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is column \(j\).
  2. For \(\det\mathbf R \ne 0\) the matrix \(\mathbf R\) is invertible, and \(\mathbf r_0 + \mathbf R\mathbf b = 0\) is equivalent to \(\mathbf b = -\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r_0\). Insertion gives \(\mathbf r(\mathbf b_{\mathrm{c}}) = \mathbf r_0 - \mathbf R\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r_0 = 0\).
  3. For \(\det\mathbf R = 0\) the image of \(\mathbf R\) is a proper subspace of \(\mathbb C^N\), and \(-\mathbf r_0\) lies in it only for a special \(\mathbf r_0\).

Source: FPGL (8.27)-(8.32), PDF p. 370. The proof is not from the source.

For \(N = 1\) this is theorem 24.51. When there are more readings than planes, or when we read at several speeds and with several trial masses, the equations have no solution in general and we look for the \(\mathbf b\) that gives the smallest weighted sum of squares of the readings. The procedure with a pseudoinverse we quote from the source (FPGL (8.37)-(8.39), PDF pp. 371-372) and do not prove.

Example 24.55 Two planes and two probes on a shaft with uniform eccentricity

Take the shaft of example 24.49 at \(\Omega = 0{,}9\,\omega_1\) (\(4252\) rev/min), planes at \(0{,}25L\) and \(0{,}75L\), probes at \(0{,}3L\) and \(0{,}5L\). The readings are \(0{,}0863\) and \(0{,}1066\) mm. Two trials with \(100\) g mm in each plane give \(\mathbf R\) with condition number \(96\) (the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value). The corrections by equation (24.52) are \(76{,}3\) g mm and \(100{,}6\) g mm, both in the direction opposite to the eccentricity. The readings at both probes are zero (below \(10^{-5}\) mm). The remaining displacement at \(0{,}7L\), where there is no probe, is \(0{,}0005\) mm, since the planes and probes have already nearly covered the first mode. Two things, however, the correction does not guarantee. First, the reading vanishes only at the measured points and at the measured speed. At \(9\,\omega_1\) (the third critical speed) the displacement at midspan grows from \(0{,}2136\) mm to \(0{,}4257\) mm, because the modal unbalance \(U_3\) increases in absolute value from \(41{,}60\) to \(83{,}43\) g mm. The corrections lie opposite to the eccentricity, at \(0{,}25L\) and \(0{,}75L\), where \(\sin(3\pi x/L) = 0{,}7071\), and contribute \(-0{,}7071\,(76{,}27 + 100{,}55) = -125{,}03\) g mm, so \(U_3 = 41{,}60 - 125{,}03 = -83{,}43\) g mm. Second, planes close together (\(0{,}45L\) and \(0{,}55L\)) give \(\mathbf R\) with condition number \(420\), and a \(3\) % error in the first trial reading leaves readings \(0{,}0053\) and \(0{,}0065\) mm instead of \(0{,}0030\) and \(0{,}0036\) mm for planes \(0{,}25L\) and \(0{,}75L\). A badly conditioned \(\mathbf R\) means that the planes influence the readings almost equally (FPGL, PDF p. 370).

24.8.5 Why two planes are not enough for a flexible rotor

A rigid rotor is balanced in two planes, whatever the supports (FPGL, PDF p. 363): only the force and the moment must be corrected. For a flexible shaft this does not hold, as definition 24.47 shows: the force and the moment are the unbalances with respect to the shapes \(1\) and \(x - L/2\), while the response is determined by the unbalances with respect to the modes, which are other numbers.

Lemma 24.56 Rigid balancing of a flexible shaft leaves modal unbalance (togo uravnoteženje podajne gredi pusti modalno neuravnoteženost)

Let a simply supported shaft have uniform real eccentricity \(a_0\) and let \(\rho A\,a_0\,L\) be its total unbalance. Let there be in the planes \(0{,}1L\) and \(0{,}9L\) masses with unbalances \(-\rho A\,a_0\,L/2\) (opposite to the direction of the eccentricity). Then \(U(1) = 0\) and \(U(x - L/2) = 0\), but

\[U_1 = \rho A\,a_0\,L\,\Big(\frac{2}{\pi} - \sin\frac{\pi}{10}\Big) , \tag{24.53}\]

which is \(0{,}5146\) of the original value \(\rho A\,a_0\,L\cdot 2/\pi\).

Proof
  1. \(U(1) = \rho A\,a_0\,L - 2\cdot\rho A\,a_0\,L/2 = 0\).
  2. \(\int_0^L (x - L/2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), and the planes have equal unbalances at \(\pm 0{,}4L\) from the midpoint, so the contribution of the planes is \(-\frac{\rho A a_0 L}{2}\,(-0{,}4L) - \frac{\rho A a_0 L}{2}\,(0{,}4L) = 0\).
  3. By equation (24.48) \(U_1 = \rho A\,a_0\int_0^L \sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x - \frac{\rho A\,a_0\,L}{2}\big(\sin\frac{\pi}{10} + \sin\frac{9\pi}{10}\big)\). The first term is \(\rho A\,a_0\,L\cdot 2/\pi\), in the second \(\sin(9\pi/10) = \sin(\pi/10)\), which gives equation (24.53).
  4. Numbers: \(2/\pi = 0{,}63662\), \(\sin(\pi/10) = 0{,}30902\), the difference \(0{,}32760\) and the ratio \(0{,}32760/0{,}63662 = 0{,}5146\).

Source: FPGL, PDF p. 363, where this is a statement for a rigid rotor. The lemma and the proof are not from the source.

By theorem 24.48 the displacement at midspan at \(\Omega = \omega_1\) is proportional to \(U_1\), so it decreases only to \(0{,}5146\) of its value: from \(0{,}6366\) mm to \(0{,}3276\) mm. At the third mode it is even worse. By equation (24.48) \(U_3 = \rho A\,a_0\,L\,\big(2/(3\pi) - \sin(3\pi/10)\big) = -117{,}00\) g mm, in absolute value \(2{,}8\) times more than before the correction (\(41{,}60\) g mm), and at \(9\,\omega_1\) the displacement at midspan grows from \(0{,}2136\) mm to \(0{,}5971\) mm. Two planes null the unbalance with respect to the functions \(1\) and \(x - L/2\), but not with respect to the odd modes \(1\) and \(3\). The even modes have \(U_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) already before the correction and after it, because the eccentricity and the correction are symmetric about the middle of the shaft.

24.8.6 Modal balancing

The aim of modal balancing is to null, by theorem 24.48, the terms of the series that we want to remove, and not the readings. The shaft has an axis and mode 1 and mode 3, which have different values in the planes (vertical lines with the values of mode 1 as dots). The rigid correction is \(98{,}0\) g mm at \(0{,}1L\) and \(0{,}9L\) (by lemma 24.56). The correction in three planes is different, because it satisfies more conditions: \(U(1) = 0\), \(U(x - L/2) = 0\) and \(U_1 = 0\). The columns show what remains.

Theorem 24.57 Modal balancing (modalno uravnoteženje)

Let the shaft be as in theorem 24.48, and let us want to eliminate modes \(1, \dots, N\). Let the number of planes be \(K = N + 2\) at \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{K}}\) and let the matrix \(\mathbf S \in \mathbb R^{K\times K}\), whose rows are the values of the functions \(1\), \(x - L/2\), \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{N}}\) in the planes \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{K}}\), be invertible. Then there is exactly one choice of unbalances \(b_1, \dots, b_{\mathrm{K}}\) in the planes for which \(U(1) = 0\), \(U(x - L/2) = 0\) and \(U_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \le N\), namely \(\mathbf b = -\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf u_0\), where the components of \(\mathbf u_0\) are the values of these \(N + 2\) quantities for the eccentricity without masses in the planes. With this choice the terms \(C_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for \(n \le N\) in the response \(P(x)\) by theorem 24.48 at every speed \(\Omega\) and every damping, and only the modes \(n > N\) remain.

Proof
  1. The quantities \(U(1)\), \(U(x - L/2)\) and \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) are by equation (24.48) the sum of the contribution of the eccentricity \(u_{0}\) and of \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} b_{\mathrm{j}}\varphi(x_{\mathrm{j}})\), so \(\mathbf S\mathbf b + \mathbf u_0\).
  2. The condition that all are zero is \(\mathbf S\mathbf b = -\mathbf u_0\), which for invertible \(\mathbf S\) has exactly one solution \(-\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf u_0\).
  3. By equation (24.49) \(C_{\mathrm{n}} \propto U_{\mathrm{n}}\) with a denominator independent of \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(U_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) gives \(C_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for every \(\Omega\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Source: FPGL section 8.5.2, (8.40)-(8.44), PDF pp. 381-383, where the condition \(\mathbf u_\ell^{\mathsf T}\mathbf b = 0\) is for the modes and there are as many planes as modes (PDF p. 383). The source does not set the conditions of force and moment there. We took them from rigid balancing (FPGL, PDF p. 363), the reason is below the theorem. The proof is not from the source.

Why do we add the force and the moment? For the displacement \(P\) in our model with rigid bearings they are not needed, since only the numbers \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) enter \(P\) (theorem 24.48). They are needed, however, for the forces in the bearings. The bearings carry the whole inertial force of the rotating shaft (theorem 8.71), which is \(\Omega^2\big(U(1) + \int_0^L \rho A\,P\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\). At a speed small compared with \(\omega_1\), \(P\) is small, since by equation (24.49) every \(C_{\mathrm{n}}\) has \(\Omega^2\) in the numerator, while the denominator is close to \(M_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). What remains is the force \(\Omega^2\,U(1)\) with the moment \(\Omega^2\,U(x - L/2)\) about the midpoint. The conditions \(U(1) = 0\) and \(U(x - L/2) = 0\) therefore keep the rigid balance (theorem 23.2): at low speed the bearings feel no rotating force. On flexible bearings, however, the rotor can also move as a rigid body, by translation, the bounce mode (premik), and by tilting, the tilt mode (nagib). These two patterns of displacement have the shapes \(1\) and \(x - L/2\), so they are excited exactly by \(U(1)\) and \(U(x - L/2)\), which the source calls static and dynamic unbalance (FPGL, PDF p. 362). For the modal procedure FPGL (PDF p. 383) uses as many planes as modes, without these two conditions. That they are needed on flexible bearings we do not prove here.

Example 24.58 Three planes for the first mode

For the shaft of example 24.49 we have \(N = 1\) and \(K = 3\), planes at \(0{,}1L\), \(0{,}5L\), \(0{,}9L\). The matrix \(\mathbf S\) has rows \((1, 1, 1)\), \((-0{,}4, 0, 0{,}4)L\) and \((0{,}309, 1, 0{,}309)\), and its determinant is \(-0{,}5528\) (in units \(L = 1\) m). The solution is \(b_1 = -51{,}55\), \(b_2 = -92{,}94\) and \(b_3 = -51{,}55\) g mm. Result: \(U(1) = 0\), \(U(x - L/2) = 0\), \(U_1 = 0\). The response at midspan at \(\omega_1\) is \(0{,}0002\) mm instead of \(0{,}6366\) mm, and at \(0{,}5\,\omega_1\) it is \(0{,}048\) μm instead of \(8{,}46\) μm.

Let us also test what the theorem does not guarantee. The modes \(n > N\) remain, and balancing one mode can make them worse.

Example 24.59 The third mode gets worse after balancing the first

For the shaft of the previous example, after the correction \(U_3 = 51{,}14\) g mm, before \(41{,}60\) g mm, and \(U_5 = -171{,}08\) g mm, before \(24{,}96\) g mm. At \(\Omega = 9\,\omega_1\) the displacement at midspan is \(0{,}2610\) mm instead of \(0{,}2136\) mm, so \(1{,}22\) times larger. With the rigid correction (lemma 24.56) it is \(0{,}5971\) mm. If the machine runs through the third critical speed, we must have \(N = 3\) and \(K = 5\) planes. The higher modes must therefore not be neglected if the machine excites them.

In practice we do not know the unbalance, and we balance each mode step by step from the lowest, with a pattern \(\mathbf e_{\mathrm{n}}\) that satisfies \(U(1) = U(x - L/2) = 0\) and \(U_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbf e_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0\) for all other \(k \le N\). At a speed near \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) one term of the series dominates (theorem 24.48), so the reading is approximately the reading of mode \(n\), and the scalar multiplier of the pattern is found with theorem 24.51. When there are several readings, \(\mathbf r_0\) and \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{d}}\) are vectors, and if the trial pattern is \(\delta\,\mathbf e_{\mathrm{n}}\) with a real \(\delta > 0\), the multiplier is \(-\delta\,\mathbf r_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathsf H}\mathbf r_0/(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{d}}^{\mathsf H}\mathbf r_{\mathrm{d}})\) (FPGL (8.45), PDF p. 383). By the source we start at the lowest mode and do not change the lower ones already balanced (FPGL, PDF p. 382). The reason: to reach the critical speed of a higher mode we must accelerate the rotor through the critical speeds of the lower ones, and if the lower modes are strongly excited, this is often impossible without damaging the machine.

24.8.7 What is proved

The response of a flexible shaft to unbalance is linear, and the numbers \(U_{\mathrm{n}}\) decide it (theorem 24.48). It follows that the influence coefficient is determined with one trial run per plane, and that the correction is exact with exact readings (theorems 24.51 and 24.54). Two planes do not balance the first mode (lemma 24.56), while \(N + 2\) planes balance the modes up to \(N\) at every speed (theorem 24.57). The proof holds for the model with a simply supported uniform shaft, modal damping and isotropic rigid bearings, without internal damping. Not proved: the behaviour of nonlinear bearings, the weighted least squares of FPGL, the necessity of force and moment with flexible supports, and the complete system of modes for other supports. Automatic balancing and balancing without a phase reference we only name (FPGL sections 8.6-8.7, PDF pp. 387-390).
