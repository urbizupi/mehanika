Chapter 18 Plates

18.8 Mindlin plates

The Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) requires the normal to stay straight and perpendicular to the middle plane. It therefore knows no transverse shear: the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero, and the transverse forces \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) are found from equilibrium (theorem 18.12). Such an idealization holds only for a thin plate, \(h/a \le 0{,}05\) (definition 18.1), while for moderately thick (\(0{,}05 \le h/a < 0{,}10\)) and very thick plates (\(h/a \ge 0{,}10\)) the normal, according to ONATE2 (PDF p. 268), is increasingly distorted during deformation and is no longer perpendicular to the middle plane (section 18.1.2). We have seen the same story for the beam. The Euler-Bernoulli model has cross-sections perpendicular to the axis, the Timoshenko model rotates them by an angle \(\theta\) independent of the slope of the axis (definition 5.84, theorem 5.88). This section takes the same step for the plate. The rotations of the normal \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) become unknowns besides the deflection \(w\), and the shear part of the energy gets the shear correction factor \(k = 5/6\) of definition 5.86. The name comes from Reissner and Mindlin (ONATE2 §6.2, PDF p. 323: Reissner-Mindlin plate theory), in this chapter we write Mindlin plate (Mindlinova plošča). The section has five parts: the kinematics and the plate quantities, the equations from the energy, the relation to the Kirchhoff plate for a simply supported polygon, the clamped circle, for which this relation fails, and the question of when to use which theory.

18.8.1 The normal rotates

Let us look at the plate from the side. Figure 18.23 shows a piece of the plate in the \(xz\) plane through the point \(P\) of the middle plane. We first draw the axes, \(x\) horizontal and \(z\) vertical upwards, and the plate: a band of thickness \(h\) whose middle plane is the thick line through \(P\). At the point \(P\) the plate is inclined, for a clear figure by an angle of \(30^\circ\), in reality the slope of the middle plane is a few hundredths of a radian. Its slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the angle between the \(x\) axis and the middle plane. For small angles the angle in radians is approximately equal to its tangent, that is to the slope (at \(0{,}05\) rad they differ by less than \(0{,}1\,\%\)), so we write the angle and the derivative \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial w/\partial x\) with the same letter (definition 1.116).

Through \(P\) passes the normal of the middle plane, a segment across the thickness of the plate. Kirchhoff requires it to stay perpendicular to the middle plane. This is the dashed line in the figure, inclined by \(30^\circ\) from the vertical. Mindlin gives up this requirement. The normal stays straight and keeps its length, but it rotates by its own angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) from the vertical, which need not equal the slope. In the figure the normal according to Mindlin is the thick orange line, rotated by \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = 18^\circ\). The angle between it and the Kirchhoff normal is the shear angle (strižni kot) \(\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{x}} = 12^\circ\). If it is zero, we have Kirchhoff. Why the name shear angle? For \(\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} \ne 0\) the material on the normal does not turn as a whole together with the middle plane, instead the layers slide one relative to another, which is a shear strain.

The displacement of a point on the normal at height \(z\) is therefore the horizontal displacement \(-z\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), because the normal rotates by \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and its point at height \(z\) is at the distance \(z\) from the middle plane. The minus sign has the same reason as for Kirchhoff (theorem 18.3): a positive \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) tilts the upper part of the normal towards smaller \(x\).

Let us write all this as a definition.

Definition 18.50 Mindlin plate (Mindlinova plošča)

Let the plate \(P\) of definition 18.1 be made of a homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\), shear modulus \(G\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). A Mindlin plate (Mindlinova plošča) has the same idealizations as the Kirchhoff plate (definition 18.2) except the third: the normal stays straight and keeps its length, but it is not perpendicular to the middle plane. Its displacement field is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = w(x, y) , \tag{18.59}\]

where \(w\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) are three fields on the middle plane \(\Omega\) with continuous derivatives up to second order: the deflection \(w\) and the rotations of the normal (zasuka normale) \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\), without a unit (\(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the rotation in the \(xz\) plane and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) in the \(yz\) plane). The field of rotations is the vector field \(\vek\theta = (\theta_{\mathrm{x}}, \theta_{\mathrm{y}})\). The shear angles (strižna kota) are

\[\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} - \theta_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{y}} = \frac{\partial w}{\partial y} - \theta_{\mathrm{y}} , \tag{18.60}\]

which we write as \(\vek\gamma = \nabla w - \vek\theta\). For \(\vek\gamma = \vek 0\) the plate is a Kirchhoff plate with \(\vek\theta = \nabla w\).

Source: ONATE2 §6.2.1 (PDF p. 324): the displacement field in \(w\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the same as for Kirchhoff, the rotations are independent variables, since they cannot be expressed by \(w\) alone. ONATE2 (PDF p. 325): for zero shear strains it follows that \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial w/\partial x\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial w/\partial y\). EP §7.3.3 (p. 493) writes the rotations as \(\phi_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\phi_{\mathrm{y}}\) and shows the deformation of the normal in figure 7.3.1. Equations (7.3.27) are in the text as an image, so we do not compare their sign with ours.

The strains follow from the displacement field (18.59). The plate quantities are integrals of the stresses over the thickness as for Kirchhoff (definitions 18.5 and 18.9), but here there is a new difficulty, which we met already for the beam: by the model the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\) would not depend on \(z\), yet on the faces \(z = \pm h/2\) it must be zero. We know what to do (section 5.13.2): we keep \(\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\) constant over the thickness and relate the force to it by a factor \(k\), chosen so that the energy is the same.

Lemma 18.51 Stress resultants of a Mindlin plate (količine Mindlinove plošče)

Let the plate be a Mindlin plate (definition 18.50) with bending stiffness \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\) (definition 18.6), and let \(S = k\,G\,h\) with \(k = 5/6\) be the shear stiffness of the plate (strižna togost plošče), in N/m. The nonzero strains are

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\tfrac12\,z\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}) , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12\,\gamma_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{18.61}\]

The moments per unit length are

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + \nu\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}) , \quad M_{\mathrm{y}} = D\,(\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + \nu\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}) , \quad M_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,D\,(1 - \nu)\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}) , \tag{18.62}\]

and the transverse forces, which by definition we take with the energetically equivalent factor \(k\), are

\[Q_{\mathrm{x}} = S\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{y}} = S\,\gamma_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{18.63}\]

The shear correction factor \(k = 5/6\) is the same as for a beam with a rectangular cross-section.

Proof

We must show the strains from the displacement field, the moments as for Kirchhoff with the curvatures replaced, and the shear factor as for the beam.

  1. Strains from equation (1.63): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -z\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = -\tfrac12 z\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}})\). Further \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = \tfrac12(-\theta_{\mathrm{x}} + w_{\mathrm{x}}) = \tfrac12\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\), and likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  2. The in-plane strains are the same as for Kirchhoff with \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xy}}\) replaced by \(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\tfrac12(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}})\). The proof of theorem 18.7 (a layer in plane stress, Hooke's law, the integral \(-\int z\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}z\) with \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} z^2\,\mathrm{d}z = h^3/12\)) uses only the form of these strains, so it gives (18.62) with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} \to \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} \to \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{xy}} \to \tfrac12(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}})\).
  3. By Hooke's law the shear stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\), constant in \(z\), and gives \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}z = G\,h\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\). The true stress is the parabola \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac{3Q_{\mathrm{x}}}{2h}(1 - 4z^2/h^2)\), which is zero on the faces (section 18.2.5). Its energy per unit area is \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2}\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2/(2G)\,\mathrm{d}z = \tfrac{9Q_{\mathrm{x}}^2}{4h^2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2G}\cdot\tfrac{8h}{15} = \tfrac65\,\tfrac{Q_{\mathrm{x}}^2}{2Gh}\), since \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2}(1 - 4z^2/h^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}z = h\,(1 - \tfrac23 + \tfrac15) = \tfrac{8h}{15}\). The energy of the model with \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = S\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}^2/(2S) = Q_{\mathrm{x}}^2/(2k\,G\,h)\). Equating the two gives \(1/k = 6/5\), that is \(k = 5/6\), as for the rectangular cross-section of a beam (lemma 5.80, where \(b\) is here a unit width and the height is \(h\)). The same holds for \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: EP (7.3.40)-(7.3.43) (p. 495) has the same structure, with \(K_{\mathrm{s}}\) for the factor. ONATE2 §6.2.2 (PDF p. 324-325): the shear strains are independent of \(z\), equivalent \(\phi_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\phi_{\mathrm{y}}\). The signs are converted to ours.

For steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), hence \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu)) = 75{,}38\) GPa) and \(h = 10\) mm we have \(S = 5/6 \cdot 75{,}38 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 0{,}010 = 6{,}28 \cdot 10^8\) N/m and \(D = 17\,948{,}7\) N m. Their ratio \(D/S = h^2/(6(1 - \nu)\,k) = 0{,}2857\,h^2\) is the square of a length and appears in everything that follows: the shear correction will be large when \(h\) is comparable with the size of the plate (as the ratio of the angles in figure 18.23 also suggests). For \(E\) and \(\nu\) we have \(2(1 + \nu) = E/G\) from definition 3.9, so the ratio depends on the material only through \(\nu\).

18.8.2 Equations from the energy

For the beam we obtained the Timoshenko equations by two routes: from the equilibrium of a slice and from virtual work (theorem 5.88). We do the same for the plate. The equilibrium of a piece of the plate (lemma 18.10) is written with stresses only and uses no kinematics, so it holds for the Mindlin plate as well. Steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 18.12 used only this lemma and give

\[Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = -q , \qquad M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = -Q_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = -Q_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{18.64}\]

These three equations hold for every plate with continuous derivatives of its quantities. Kirchhoff used them for \(Q\) and \(M\) from \(w\), here \(M\) and \(Q\) are given by equations (18.62) and (18.63) from the three fields \(w\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\). For virtual work we also need the energy: per unit area it is

\[\tfrac12\,(M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}) + Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{y}}) ,\]

which is the bending energy (lemma 18.15, with the curvatures replaced by the derivatives of the rotations) and the shear energy \(\tfrac12\,S\,|\vek\gamma|^2\) from step 3 of lemma 18.51. For the section we need one more notion, which we take from section 18.3: on an edge with arc length \(s\) (the distance measured along the edge) and outward normal \(\vek n\) the tangent is \(\vek s = (-n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{x}})\), the components of the rotation are \(\theta_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek\theta\cdot\vek n\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\theta\cdot\vek s\), the vector of edge moments \(\vek t = (M_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}} n_{\mathrm{y}},\ M_{\mathrm{xy}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\) has the components \(M_{\mathrm{nn}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n\) and \(M_{\mathrm{ns}} = \vek t\cdot\vek s\), and the transverse force of the edge is \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\). Because of the relation \(\vek t\cdot\delta\vek\theta = M_{\mathrm{nn}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{ns}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\), the pairs \((w, Q_{\mathrm{n}})\), \((\theta_{\mathrm{n}}, M_{\mathrm{nn}})\) and \((\theta_{\mathrm{s}}, M_{\mathrm{ns}})\) act like the pairs \((v, V)\) and \((\theta, M)\) of the beam: on every edge the support prescribes exactly one member of each pair.

Theorem 18.52 Mindlin plate equations (enačbe Mindlinove plošče)

Let \(\Omega\) be a polygon for which the divergence theorem in the plane holds (a rectangle by lemma 18.17, every polygon that can be cut into pieces between two graphs by theorem 4.39 and corollary 4.40), and let the Mindlin plate (definition 18.50) have constant \(D\), \(\nu\), \(S\) and a load \(q\) continuous on \(\Omega\). Let the fields \(w\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) have continuous derivatives up to second order on an open set that contains \(\Omega\) with its edge, and let \(M\) and \(Q\) be given by equations (18.62) and (18.63). Then:

  1. The equilibrium (18.64) holds if and only if the system
    \[\begin{aligned} S\,(\nabla^2 w - \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}) &= -q , \\ \tfrac12 D\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\theta_{\mathrm{x}} + (1 + \nu)\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}})\big) + S\,(w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{x}}) &= 0 , \\ \tfrac12 D\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\theta_{\mathrm{y}} + (1 + \nu)\,\partial_{\mathrm{y}}(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}})\big) + S\,(w_{\mathrm{y}} - \theta_{\mathrm{y}}) &= 0 \end{aligned} \tag{18.65}\]
    holds.
  2. Let \(\delta w\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) be three functions with continuous derivatives up to second order on \(\Omega\) with its edge, which on every part of the edge are zero in those components \(\delta w\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) that the support prescribes. If (18.65) holds and for every component that the support does not prescribe the corresponding edge quantity is zero (natural conditions \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{nn}} = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{ns}} = 0\)), then the virtual work
    \[\int_\Omega \big( M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \delta\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}) + Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\delta w_{\mathrm{x}} - \delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}) + Q_{\mathrm{y}}\,(\delta w_{\mathrm{y}} - \delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}) \big)\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_\Omega q\,\delta w\,\mathrm{d}A \tag{18.66}\]
    holds.
  3. Conversely, if (18.66) holds for every triple \(\delta w\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) that is zero outside some rectangle in the interior of \(\Omega\) (and has continuous derivatives up to second order), then (18.64) holds at every interior point.
Proof

We must show that every term of (18.66), after integration by parts, is a sum of the expressions (18.64) with test functions and of an edge integral. We use the divergence theorem in the plane in the form \(\int_\Omega f_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint f\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}s\), and the same for \(y\).

  1. Point 1: we insert (18.62) and (18.63) into (18.64). The first equation is \(S\,(w_{\mathrm{xx}} - \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + w_{\mathrm{yy}} - \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}) = -q\). In the second, \(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = D\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{yx}} + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{yy}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{xy}}))\), since the mixed derivatives are equal (theorem 8.30). With \(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{yy}} = \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\theta_{\mathrm{x}} + \tfrac12(1 + \nu)\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\nu\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{xy}} + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(1 + \nu)\,\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{xy}}\) this is \(\tfrac12 D\,((1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\theta_{\mathrm{x}} + (1 + \nu)\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}))\). With \(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = -Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -S\,(w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{x}})\) we obtain the second line. The third is the same with \(x \leftrightarrow y\) exchanged. Since all the substitutions work both ways, the converse holds as well.
  2. Point 2: the expression \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\) is \(\partial_{\mathrm{x}}(M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}) - M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by the divergence theorem \(\int_\Omega M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint M_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}s - \int_\Omega M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). Likewise \(\int M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = \oint M_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}} - \int M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\int M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} = \oint M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}} - \int M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\int M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}} = \oint M_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}} - \int M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\). For the transverse forces: \(\int Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta w_{\mathrm{x}} = \oint Q_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta w - \int Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\,\delta w\), and the same for \(y\).
  3. The sum of all terms of the left side of (18.66) is
    \[-\int_\Omega (Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}})\,\delta w\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_\Omega (M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} + Q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_\Omega (M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + Q_{\mathrm{y}})\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \oint \big(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta w + \vek t\cdot\delta\vek\theta\big)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]
    Here \(\vek t\cdot\delta\vek\theta = (M_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}} + (M_{\mathrm{xy}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. By (18.64) the three integrals equal \(\int q\,\delta w\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(0\) and \(0\). In the edge integral \(\vek t\cdot\delta\vek\theta = M_{\mathrm{nn}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{n}} + M_{\mathrm{ns}}\,\delta\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\). On every edge and for every pair, either the test function is zero (the support prescribes the component) or the edge quantity is zero (natural condition). The edge integral is zero and the right side of (18.66) remains.
  5. Point 3: we take \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and \(\delta w = \varphi(x, y) = q_{\alpha\beta}(x)\,q_{\gamma\delta}(y)\) for a rectangle \([\alpha, \beta] \times [\gamma, \delta]\) in \(\Omega\), where \(q_{\alpha\beta}(t) = (t - \alpha)^4(\beta - t)^4\) on \([\alpha, \beta]\) and zero elsewhere (lemma 4.122). The function \(\varphi\) and its derivatives up to third order are zero outside the interior of the rectangle. Since the test triple is zero at the edge and so the edge integral is zero, step 3 gives \(\int (Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q)\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). For the function \(g = Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + q\), which does not depend on a third coordinate, this is point 3 of lemma 4.122, and hence \(g = 0\) in \(\Omega\). Likewise, with \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \varphi\) and the other two zero we obtain the second equation of (18.64), with \(\delta\theta_{\mathrm{y}} = \varphi\) the third.

Source: The structure of the proof is the proof of theorem 5.88 in two dimensions. EP (7.3.33)-(7.3.39) (p. 494-495) has the same Euler-Lagrange equations and natural boundary conditions in its variables, ONATE2 §6.2 (PDF p. 323-331) the same from virtual work. We have omitted the converse direction for the boundary conditions, which would give the natural conditions from virtual work: it is proved as in section 5.11.4 with test functions concentrated at the edge. The section does not need it.

What have we obtained? Equations (18.65) are three equations of second order for three functions, so together of order six instead of four for Kirchhoff. Two edge quantities on every edge become three, and this is why a support can prescribe, besides the deflection \(w\) and the rotation \(\theta_{\mathrm{n}}\), also the rotation \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) along the edge, which for Kirchhoff is already fixed by the deflection along the edge (\(\theta_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial w/\partial s\)). The Kirchhoff plate is a limiting case, and let us write this down before we go on.

If in the first equation of (18.65) \(S \to \infty\) for a given \(q\) and the transverse forces \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) stay bounded, then \(\vek\gamma = \nabla w - \vek\theta = (Q_{\mathrm{x}}, Q_{\mathrm{y}})/S \to \vek 0\). The boundedness is an assumption here. For the simply supported polygon we will see it below, where \(Q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}}\) are the Kirchhoff transverse forces, independent of \(S\). For \(\vek\theta = \nabla w\) we have \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{yy}})\) as in theorem 18.7, and \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = -(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}}) = -D\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}\nabla^2 w\) by step 4 of the proof of theorem 18.12. The first line of (18.64) then gives \(D\,\nabla^4 w = q\), the plate equation (18.17). So Kirchhoff is a limiting case. For a finite \(S\), however, the transverse force \(Q = S\,\gamma\) is finite and the shear angle \(\gamma = Q/S\) is not zero wherever \(Q \ne 0\), as figure 18.23 suggested. To close the section, let us write down what the supports determine. The solution is unique, as a lemma shows that is the same computation as lemma 4.133 and as the uniqueness of the solution for the beam.

Lemma 18.53 Uniqueness for the Mindlin plate (enoličnost rešitve Mindlinove plošče)

Let \(\Omega\) be a polygon as in theorem 18.52. If \((w_1, \vek\theta_1)\) and \((w_2, \vek\theta_2)\) are two solutions of equations (18.65) with the same load \(q\) and the same supports (\(w\) is prescribed on the edge, \(w = 0\), and for every other pair of edge quantities the support prescribes the same component or the natural condition is zero), then \(w_1 = w_2\) and \(\vek\theta_1 = \vek\theta_2\).

Proof

We must show that the difference gives the integral of a nonnegative form that is zero only for a zero difference.

  1. The difference \((w, \vek\theta) = (w_1 - w_2, \vek\theta_1 - \vek\theta_2)\) is a solution with \(q = 0\), \(w = 0\) on the edge and homogeneous conditions in the other components (the prescribed value of the difference is zero, the natural condition is zero). We use (18.66) with \(\delta w = w\), \(\delta\vek\theta = \vek\theta\): this triple is zero on the edge in every prescribed component. The right side is zero, since \(q = 0\).
  2. By equations (18.62) and (18.63) the left side is the integral of the density \(D\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}^2 + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}^2 + 2\nu\,\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}})^2) + S\,|\vek\gamma|^2\). With \(s = \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\), \(d = \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\), \(e = \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} + \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}\) the first term is \(D\,(\tfrac12(1 + \nu)\,s^2 + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)\,(d^2 + e^2))\), since \(\tfrac12(1 + \nu)(a + b)^2 + \tfrac12(1 - \nu)(a - b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2\nu\,ab\) for \(a = \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}}\) and \(b = \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}}\). Because \(-1 < \nu < 1\) all coefficients are positive, and the density is nonnegative.
  3. The integral of a continuous nonnegative function is zero only if the function is zero everywhere: if it were positive at a point \(P\), it would be larger than half of this value on a small rectangle around \(P\), and the integral would exceed a positive number (lemma 3.39). Hence \(s = d = e = 0\) and \(\vek\gamma = \vek 0\) in \(\Omega\).
  4. From \(s = d = 0\) we get \(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} = \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = 0\). Then \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) does not depend on \(x\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(y\), and \(e = 0\) means \(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} = -\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}}\). The left side is a function of \(y\) only, the right side of \(x\) only, so both are constant: \(\theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} = -\omega\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}} = \omega\). From \(\vek\gamma = \vek 0\) we have \(\vek\theta = \nabla w\), and the mixed derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{xy}} = \theta_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y}} = -\omega\) and \(w_{\mathrm{yx}} = \theta_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{x}} = \omega\) are equal (theorem 8.30), so \(\omega = 0\). The rotations are constant, \(w\) is a linear function, zero at three points of the edge that are not on one line, hence \(w = 0\), and \(\vek\theta = \nabla w = \vek 0\).

18.8.3 The Marcus moment and two Dirichlet problems

Where does the Mindlin plate differ from the Kirchhoff plate? For a special kind of plate the answer hides in a quantity that Kirchhoff's theory has and has not used. Let us take the Kirchhoff plate with the equation \(D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = q\). The sum \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}} = D\,(1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}}\) follows from theorem 18.7, because \(\nu\) enters both in the same way. The Marcus moment (moment Marcusa) is

\[M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -\frac{M_{\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}}{1 + \nu} , \tag{18.67}\]

in N m/m. Why the minus sign? For \(q > 0\) the load \(q\) points in the direction of \(w\), the deflections are positive, \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\) has its largest value in the middle of the plate and there \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} < 0\), so \(M_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) in the middle for a load \(q > 0\). EP §7.4.3 (p. 516) uses the same quantity under the name Marcus moment or moment sum, with the sign of its own moments, which is opposite to ours (remark at definition 18.5), and therefore EP's Marcus moment is our \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\).

On the edge of a simply supported polygon the moment \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) has a very simple value. Let us first look at figure 18.24, which is drawn for \(q > 0\). On the left is the moment \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) along the centre line \(y = a/2\) of the simply supported square, on the right along a radius of the clamped circle. We draw the horizontal axis \(x/a\) from the edge to the centre and the vertical axis \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(q a^2)\). The result of the Navier series of section 18.5 is the curve that starts with zero at the edge \(x = 0\) and rises to \(0{,}0737\) at the centre, which is \(0{,}0737 \cdot 10\,000 \cdot 0{,}25 = 184{,}2\) N m/m for \(q = 10\) kPa and \(a = 500\) mm. The square has \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on every edge. On the right is the circle: the axis \(r/R\) from the centre to the edge and \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(q R^2)\). For the clamped circle \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = q\,(R^2 - 2r^2)/8\), which we compute in step 1 of lemma 18.57. The curve starts at \(0{,}125\) at the centre, passes through zero at \(r = R/\sqrt2 = 0{,}707\,R\) and at the edge is not zero but \(-0{,}125\). This difference is the core of the section.

Let us prove that \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) is indeed zero on the edge of a simply supported polygon and that two Poisson problems follow from this. For this we need the uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem (Dirichletov problem: the solution of an equation in the interior with a value prescribed in advance on the edge).

Lemma 18.54 Uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem of the Laplace operator (enoličnost Dirichletovega problema za Laplaceov operator)

Let \(\Omega\) be a connected polygon for which the divergence theorem in the plane holds as in theorem 18.52, and \(g\) a continuous function on \(\Omega\). If \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(\Omega\) with its edge, with \(\nabla^2 f_{\mathrm{i}} = g\) in \(\Omega\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\), then \(f_1 = f_2\).

Proof

We must show that the difference \(f = f_1 - f_2\) with \(\nabla^2 f = 0\) and \(f = 0\) on the edge has a zero gradient, and is therefore zero everywhere.

  1. The field \((f\,f_{\mathrm{x}},\ f\,f_{\mathrm{y}})\) is continuously differentiable and has the divergence \(f_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + f_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + f\,\nabla^2 f = |\nabla f|^2\). By the divergence theorem in the plane, \(\int_\Omega |\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint f\,(f_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + f_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), since \(f = 0\) on the edge.
  2. The continuous nonnegative function \(|\nabla f|^2\) with zero integral is zero everywhere, as in step 3 of lemma 18.53. Hence \(\nabla f = \vek 0\) in \(\Omega\).
  3. A function with zero gradient is constant along every segment in \(\Omega\) (corollary 1.85), and since \(\Omega\) is connected, along polygonal lines that join any two points. So \(f\) is constant in \(\Omega\) and by continuity equal to its value on the edge, which is zero.

Source: The proof is ours, it is the usual one in textbooks of potential theory. The same result is planned in chapter 14, we do not refer to its block.

We will use the lemma for two equations, which for the Kirchhoff plate we obtain from \(D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = q\) by introducing \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\): \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\) and \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -M_{\mathrm{K}}/D\). These are two Poisson problems, if we know the edge values. For a simply supported edge we know them, as the next statement shows, in which we also compute what the Mindlin plate does with the Marcus moment. The key step is a general solution of equations (18.65) from a given solution \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\) of the plate equation, independent of the supports.

Lemma 18.55 Solutions of the Mindlin equations from a Kirchhoff solution (rešitve Mindlinovih enačb iz Kirchhoffove rešitve)

Let \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\) be four times continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(\Omega\) with its edge, with \(D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = q\) (equation (18.17)), \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) given by equation (18.67), and \(c\) an arbitrary constant. Then the triple

\[w = w_{\mathrm{K}} + \frac{M_{\mathrm{K}}}{S} + c , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{K}} , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{K}} \tag{18.68}\]

solves equations (18.65). The shear angles are \(\vek\gamma = \nabla M_{\mathrm{K}}/S\), the moments are the Kirchhoff moments of \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\), the transverse forces are the Kirchhoff transverse forces \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}^K = \partial_{\mathrm{n}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\).

Proof

We must show that the triple satisfies (18.64), which by point 1 of theorem 18.52 is equivalent to (18.65).

  1. The rotations are \(\vek\theta = \nabla w_{\mathrm{K}}\), so by equation (18.62) the moments \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D\,(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{yy}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy}} = D\,(1 - \nu)\,w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{xy}}\) equal the Kirchhoff moments of theorem 18.7. For them, by steps 2 to 4 of the proof of theorem 18.12, \(M_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} = D\,\partial_{\mathrm{x}}\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -\partial_{\mathrm{x}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + M_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = -\partial_{\mathrm{y}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\). The second and third equations of (18.64) therefore require \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial_{\mathrm{y}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\).
  2. By (18.60) the shear angles are \(\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}}(w_{\mathrm{K}} + M_{\mathrm{K}}/S + c) - \partial_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{K}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}} M_{\mathrm{K}}/S\), since the constant \(c\) has no derivative, and likewise \(\gamma_{\mathrm{y}}\). By (18.63) \(Q_{\mathrm{x}} = S\,\gamma_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial_{\mathrm{y}} M_{\mathrm{K}}\). This is the requirement of step 1.
  3. The first equation of (18.64): \(Q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{x}} + Q_{\mathrm{y},\mathrm{y}} = \nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The idea that the Mindlin solution is expressed by the Kirchhoff solution and the Marcus moment is in EP §7.4.3 (p. 516-519), where the equations are in the variables \(w\) and \(\mathcal{M}\).

The lemma says nothing about the supports. The supports decide which solution \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\) of the plate equation is taken and how large the constant \(c\) is. For a simply supported polygon everything is determined.

Theorem 18.56 Mindlin against Kirchhoff for a simply supported polygon (Mindlin proti Kirchhoffu pri prosto podprtem mnogokotniku)

Let \(\Omega\) be a connected polygon as in lemma 18.54, with sides that are segments. Let the plate be simply supported along its whole edge, that is:

  • Kirchhoff: \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\),
  • Mindlin with hard simple support (trda podpora): \(w = 0\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{nn}} = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\).

Let the Kirchhoff problem have a solution \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\), four times continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(\Omega\) with its edge, for a continuous load \(q\). Then:

  1. \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\), hence \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) is the only solution of the problem \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\) in \(\Omega\), \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\), and \(w_{\mathrm{K}}\) the only solution of the problem \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -M_{\mathrm{K}}/D\), \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on \(\partial\Omega\).
  2. The Mindlin plate has exactly one solution, and it is
    \[w_{\mathrm{M}} = w_{\mathrm{K}} + \frac{M_{\mathrm{K}}}{S} , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{K}} , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{y}} = \partial_{\mathrm{y}} w_{\mathrm{K}} . \tag{18.69}\]
  3. At every point \(w_{\mathrm{M}} - w_{\mathrm{K}} = M_{\mathrm{K}}/S\), and on the edge \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/S = 0\). At a point where \(M_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) the Mindlin deflection is larger than the Kirchhoff one.
Proof

We must show that \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on the edge, that the Dirichlet problems follow from this, and that (18.69) by lemma 18.55 satisfies the boundary conditions, then the uniqueness.

  1. On a side of the polygon \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) along the side, so the derivative along the side is zero: \(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{ss}} = 0\). By theorem 18.7 the moments are \(D\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\mathrm{Hess}\,w_{\mathrm{K}} + \nu\,\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}}\,\mathbb{1}\big)\) as a tensor, and \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = \vek n\cdot\vek t\) is its component in the direction \(\vek n\). In the coordinates \(n\), \(s\) along the side, therefore, \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = D\,(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{nn}} + \nu\,w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{ss}})\), since the Hessian matrix and the identity matrix are written the same way under a rotation of the coordinates. From \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = 0\) and \(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{ss}} = 0\) it follows that \(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{nn}} = 0\). Then \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{nn}} + w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{ss}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on the side. The vertices are limits of points of the sides, and \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) is continuous.
  2. The equation \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\) with the boundary condition \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) is a Dirichlet problem, and by lemma 18.54 it has only one solution. Likewise \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -M_{\mathrm{K}}/D\) with \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\). This is point 1.
  3. The triple (18.69) solves equations (18.65) by lemma 18.55 with \(c = 0\). Boundary conditions: \(w_{\mathrm{M}} = w_{\mathrm{K}} + M_{\mathrm{K}}/S = 0 + 0 = 0\) on the edge by step 1. The rotation \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial_{\mathrm{s}} w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\), since \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) along the side. The edge moment \(M_{\mathrm{nn}} = M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = 0\), since the moments are the Kirchhoff ones (lemma 18.55) and the Kirchhoff condition holds.
  4. Uniqueness: the edge components are \(w\) (prescribed, zero), \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) (prescribed, zero) and \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}\) (natural condition, zero), one from each pair \((w, Q_{\mathrm{n}})\), \((\theta_{\mathrm{s}}, M_{\mathrm{ns}})\), \((\theta_{\mathrm{n}}, M_{\mathrm{nn}})\). By lemma 18.53 the solution is unique. Point 3 of the theorem follows from (18.69) and step 1.

Source: EP §7.4.3 (p. 516-519): for simply supported polygonal plates with the Marcus moment zero on the edge (EP (7.4.56), p. 519) the deflection of the first-order theory is the deflection of the classical theory plus a term with the Marcus moment. EP's text mentions the hard support with a prescribed tangential rotation (p. 518). The proof is ours: with lemma 18.55, without EP's equations, which are in the text as images. EP example 7.4.4 (p. 519) uses the same for the equilateral triangle.

The meaning of the theorem is that the Mindlin deflection can be computed from the Kirchhoff one with one additional Poisson problem, in which \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) appears as the right side. The sign is as follows: the Poisson equation with the uniform load \(q > 0\) has \(M_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) in the interior. The reason: \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) is continuous on the closed polygon and attains its smallest value there. At an interior minimum we would have \(M_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{xx}} \ge 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{yy}} \ge 0\), hence \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} \ge 0\), which contradicts \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -q < 0\). The smallest value is therefore on the edge, where \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\), and every interior point with \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) would be an interior minimum, so \(M_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) in the interior. Therefore the Mindlin deflection is larger than the Kirchhoff one. The shear strain adds to the plate one more deflection, which Kirchhoff does not know.

Let us test this on the example of section 18.5: the simply supported square, \(a = 500\) mm, steel, \(q = 10\) kPa. By the Navier series \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0{,}004062\,q a^4/D\) at the centre and \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0{,}07367\,q a^2 = 184{,}2\) N m/m (SymPy and NumPy, num-08). For \(a/h = 50\) (\(h = 10\) mm, \(S = 6{,}28\cdot 10^8\) N/m) we have \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0{,}14146\) mm and \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/S = 184{,}2/(6{,}28 \cdot 10^8) = 2{,}93 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m \(= 0{,}00029\) mm, hence \(w_{\mathrm{M}} = 0{,}14175\) mm and the ratio \(w_{\mathrm{M}}/w_{\mathrm{K}} = 1{,}0021\). We check the same term by term of the series: for one mode \(w = W\,\sin(\alpha x)\sin(\beta y)\) with hard support the system (18.65) gives (SymPy) \(W = q_{\mathrm{mn}}/(D\lambda^2) + q_{\mathrm{mn}}/(S\lambda)\) with \(\lambda = \alpha^2 + \beta^2\), which is the Kirchhoff term and the shear term. The shear term is \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/S\) term by term of the series, since for this mode \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = q_{\mathrm{mn}}/\lambda\). For five values of \(a/h\) the ratio \(w_{\mathrm{M}}/w_{\mathrm{K}}\) at the centre is:

\(a/h\) 5 10 20 50 100
\(w_{\mathrm{M}}/w_{\mathrm{K}}\) 1,2073 1,0518 1,0130 1,0021 1,0005

The deviation from one is, for these five numbers, \(0{,}2073\), \(0{,}0518\), \(0{,}01295\), \(0{,}00207\), \(0{,}00052\), and it is always \(5{,}1815\,(h/a)^2\). This is no coincidence, because \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(S\,w_{\mathrm{K}})\) is proportional to \((h/a)^2\): \(M_{\mathrm{K}} \propto q a^2\), \(w_{\mathrm{K}} \propto q a^4/D \propto h^{-3}\) and \(S \propto h\), so \((M_{\mathrm{K}}/S)/w_{\mathrm{K}} = (0{,}07367/0{,}0040624)\cdot D/(S a^2) = 18{,}135 \cdot 0{,}2857\,h^2/a^2 = 5{,}1815\,(h/a)^2\). EP in table 7.3.1 (p. 500) has for \(\nu = 0{,}25\) and uniform load \(\bar w = 0{,}4259\), \(0{,}4111\), \(0{,}4070\), \(0{,}4060\) at \(a/h = 10\), \(20\), \(50\), \(100\) and \(0{,}4062\) by the classical theory. Our theorem gives with \(\nu = 0{,}25\) (\(c = 4{,}836\)) the ratios \(1{,}0484\), \(1{,}0121\), \(1{,}0019\), \(1{,}0005\), and the products with the Kirchhoff value \(0{,}40624\) are \(0{,}4259\), \(0{,}4111\), \(0{,}4070\), \(0{,}4064\). The value \(0{,}4060\) is therefore an error in EP's table on p. 500, the correct value is \(0{,}4064\).

18.8.4 The clamped circle: the Marcus relation fails

The theorem succeeded for the simply supported polygon because \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on the edge. Step 1 of its proof used two things: that the edge is a segment (\(w_{\mathrm{K},\mathrm{ss}} = 0\)) and that \(M_{\mathrm{nn}}^K = 0\). What if one of them is missing? Let us take a plate for which the hypothesis does not hold. A circle of radius \(R\) is not a polygon, and on the clamped edge \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = -qR^2/8 \ne 0\) (the right curve in figure 18.24). The theorem does not apply. Does its result hold, at least approximately? Let us see.

Lemma 18.57 Clamped circular plate according to Mindlin (vpeta krožna plošča po Mindlinu)

A circular plate of radius \(R\) is clamped along its whole edge (\(w = 0\) and \(\vek\theta = \vek 0\) at \(r = R\)) and loaded by a uniform \(q\). Let \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = q\,(R^2 - r^2)^2/(64\,D)\) be the Kirchhoff deflection (theorem 18.35 and corollary 18.36). Then the solution of the Mindlin plate is

\[w_{\mathrm{M}} = w_{\mathrm{K}} + \frac{q\,(R^2 - r^2)}{4\,S} , \qquad \vek\theta = \nabla w_{\mathrm{K}} , \tag{18.70}\]

and it is the only one. The deflection at the centre is \(w_{\mathrm{M}}(0) = w_{\mathrm{K}}(0)\,\big(1 + 16\,D/(S R^2)\big)\). The Marcus formula (18.69) with \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = q\,(R^2 - 2r^2)/8\) gives at the centre only \(w_{\mathrm{K}}(0)\,\big(1 + 8\,D/(S R^2)\big)\), that is half of the correction, and on the edge \(-qR^2/(8S) \ne 0\).

Proof

We must show that the triple (18.70) satisfies the equations and the clamping, and compute the correction.

  1. \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = \tfrac1r(r\,w_{\mathrm{K}}')'\) by corollary 11.18. From \(w_{\mathrm{K}}' = -q\,r\,(R^2 - r^2)/(16\,D)\) we have \(r\,w_{\mathrm{K}}' = -q\,(R^2 r^2 - r^4)/(16\,D)\) and \((r\,w_{\mathrm{K}}')' = -q\,(2R^2 r - 4 r^3)/(16\,D)\), so \(\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\,(R^2 - 2r^2)/(8\,D)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = -D\,\nabla^2 w_{\mathrm{K}} = q\,(R^2 - 2r^2)/8\). From \(M_{\mathrm{K}}' = -qr/2\) we get \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = \tfrac1r(r M_{\mathrm{K}}')' = \tfrac1r\,(-q r^2/2)' = -q\), hence \(D\,\nabla^4 w_{\mathrm{K}} = -\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = q\), as the plate equation requires.
  2. By lemma 18.55 \(w_{\mathrm{K}} + M_{\mathrm{K}}/S + c\) with \(\vek\theta = \nabla w_{\mathrm{K}}\) is a solution of the equations. The clamping requires \(\vek\theta = \vek 0\) at \(r = R\), that is \(w_{\mathrm{K}}'(R) = 0\) (indeed: the factor \(R^2 - r^2\) is zero), and \(w = 0\) at \(r = R\). Since \(w_{\mathrm{K}}(R) = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{K}}(R) = -qR^2/8\), the condition is \(-qR^2/(8S) + c = 0\), that is \(c = qR^2/(8S)\).
  3. Then \(w = w_{\mathrm{K}} + q\,(R^2 - 2r^2)/(8S) + qR^2/(8S) = w_{\mathrm{K}} + q\,(R^2 - r^2)/(4S)\), which is (18.70). Uniqueness follows from lemma 18.53: with clamping, all three components of the edge pairs are prescribed.
  4. Centre: \(w_{\mathrm{K}}(0) = qR^4/(64D)\), the correction is \(qR^2/(4S)\), the ratio of the correction is \(\big(qR^2/(4S)\big)\big(64 D/(qR^4)\big) = 16\,D/(S R^2)\). With the Marcus formula the correction is \(M_{\mathrm{K}}(0)/S = qR^2/(8S)\), half of it, and on the edge \(M_{\mathrm{K}}(R)/S = -qR^2/(8S)\), so the Marcus formula does not satisfy \(w = 0\) on the edge.

The solution for the clamped circle in the form Kirchhoff plus a correction is in EP §7.3.2 (p. 488-492) for the axisymmetric Mindlin plate, in EP's variables and with other boundary conditions. 18.8.5 When to use which theory

Figure 18.25 is the answer to the question of the section. We first draw the axes: horizontally \(t = h/l\), where \(l\) is the side of the square or the diameter of the circle, from \(0\) to \(0{,}2\), vertically \(w_{\mathrm{M}}/w_{\mathrm{K}}\) at the centre from \(1\) to \(1{,}8\). The simply supported square gives the parabola \(1 + 5{,}18\,t^2\), given by theorem 18.56. The clamped circle gives the steeper parabola \(1 + 18{,}3\,t^2\) from lemma 18.57, and the dashed curve \(1 + 9{,}14\,t^2\) is the Marcus formula, which we would have if the theorem held here too. Two vertical lines show the limits: \(t = 0{,}02\) and \(t = 0{,}10\). The first is the ratio \(a/h = 50\) of our plate, the second the limit of the very thick plate according to ONATE2 (PDF p. 268), where for the square the Kirchhoff theory is already off by \(5{,}2\,\%\) (that is \(1 + 5{,}18 \cdot 0{,}01\)) and for the circle by \(18{,}3\,\%\).

From the figure we read when the Kirchhoff theory is good enough. For the simply supported square the deviation is \(5{,}1815\,(h/a)^2\): below \(1\,\%\) for \(a/h \ge 22{,}8\) and below \(5\,\%\) for \(a/h \ge 10{,}2\) (SymPy). The limit \(h/a \le 0{,}05\) of definition 18.1, which we took from two sources, is therefore indeed such for the square that the Kirchhoff error does not exceed \(1{,}3\,\%\) (\(5{,}1815/400\)). For the clamped circle the same limit (\(2R/h = 20\)) is worse: \(1{,}0457\), that is \(4{,}6\,\%\), and the deviation would be below \(5\,\%\) for \(2R/h \ge 19{,}1\). The limit therefore depends on the supports, not only on the ratio \(h/l\), as we saw in the previous section. ONATE2 §6.16 (PDF p. 410-411) confirms the same: for simply supported plates the central deflection of the thick plate always converges to larger values than Kirchhoff gives, and the soft support (\(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) free) gives an even larger deflection than the hard one. For a rhombic plate with \(L/t = 100\) and soft support the deflection according to ONATE2 is \(4\,\%\) larger than the Kirchhoff one, and Babuška and Scapolla confirm with three-dimensional elasticity that Mindlin is closer to reality. We have taken only the hard support, because the result of theorem 18.56 depends on it. The soft support, in which \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) is free and \(M_{\mathrm{ns}}\) is prescribed, is treated in ONATE2 (§6.3, PDF p. 336-337) as a different boundary condition and is outside the scope of the section.

A last test shows that the Mindlin theory is not always better. For a point force \(P\) at the centre of the simply supported square (\(P = 1\) kN, \(h = 10\) mm) the Kirchhoff deflection is finite, \(0{,}1616\) mm (Navier series). The addition due to shear in the series that (18.65) gives term by term is the sum of the terms \(4P/(\pi^2 S)\sum 1/(m^2 + n^2)\) over odd \(m\), \(n\), and this sum does not converge: with terms up to \(N = 11\), \(41\), \(161\), \(641\) it is \(0{,}00076\), \(0{,}00108\), \(0{,}00142\), \(0{,}00177\) mm, each time about \(0{,}00035\) mm more for a fourfold \(N\), which is \(P/(2\pi S)\,\ln 4 = 0{,}00035\) mm (NumPy). This is a numerical demonstration, not a theorem: the Mindlin plate has an infinite deflection under a point force, and ONATE2 (PDF p. 411-412) writes the same for the thick plate, with the remark that three-dimensional elasticity predicts this too. In finite elements the deflection under the force therefore depends on the mesh, and ONATE2 recommends comparing the total strain energy. The Kirchhoff theory has a finite deflection under a point force, but an infinite moment (lemma 18.33), the Mindlin theory in addition an infinite deflection. Both theories are idealizations, and the point force is an idealization of the load that neither of them carries.

A summary of the section, with how each result was shown. In the Mindlin plate the normal rotates (\(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}}\)) and the shear angle \(\gamma = \nabla w - \vek\theta\) is an independent unknown (definition, figure 18.23). The plate quantities are computed from the strains and from the energetically equivalent \(k = 5/6\) (lemma 18.51). Equations (18.65) come from equilibrium and from virtual work, which are equivalent (theorem 18.52), and Kirchhoff is the limiting case \(S \to \infty\). For a simply supported polygon the solution is the Kirchhoff one with the Marcus correction, proved with lemma 18.55 and uniqueness (theorem 18.56, numbers in the example and in the table). For the clamped circle the relation fails because \(M_{\mathrm{K}} \ne 0\) on the edge, and the true correction is twice as large at the centre (lemma 18.57, figure 18.25). The Mindlin plate is compatible with elements that have only first derivatives in the energy, so it appears in section 18.9, where for a thin plate the shear term locks the mesh.
