Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.2 A body made of elements

In section 15.1 we cut a flat body into triangles. On each triangle the field is linear, and on the common edge of two triangles the neighbouring fields agree (lemma 15.7), so the field on the whole body is continuous but has kinks. We tested the Ritz method of chapter 6 on a bar, where everything was one dimensional. Now we ask: do its theorems hold for a body made of triangles and tetrahedra as well? The proofs of chapter 6 need two things. The first is the energy of the field, which for a body is an integral over the body, while the field has kinks and its strain jumps at the edges of elements, so the integrand is not continuous. The second is that the true solution satisfies the weak form for every test field, not only for smooth ones. For a body we get this from the divergence theorem, which we proved in chapter 4 for curved boxes and for bodies between graphs, but not for a triangle or a tetrahedron. This section fills both gaps. We must fill the second gap first, because the first one will use the same cutting.

15.2.1 The triangle and the tetrahedron as a body of curved boxes

We know the divergence theorem for a curved box (theorem 4.67) and for a body cut into such boxes (corollary 4.68). A triangle is not a box, but we can cut it into pieces that are each the image of a square. Take the triangle with three vertices \(P_1 = (0, 0)\), \(P_2 = (40, 0)\) and \(P_3 = (10, 30)\) mm, of area \(600\) mm\(^2\) (the numbers are in num-02.out, part C). On the edges we choose the midpoints \(M_{12}\), \(M_{13}\) and \(M_{23}\), the points in the middle of the sides, as the name says. From the centroid \(G = (P_1 + P_2 + P_3)/3\) (the centroid has this name because a uniformly dense triangular plate would balance on the tip of a needle at this point, but we need it only as the average of the vertices) we start three segments that lead to the midpoints, and the triangle falls apart into three quadrilaterals. Take the quadrilateral at the vertex \(P_1\), with vertices \(P_1\), \(M_{12}\), \(G\) and \(M_{13}\).

The piece is the image of the unit square \(0 \le s \le 1\), \(0 \le t \le 1\), where the vertex \((0, 0)\) goes to \(P_1\), the vertex \((1, 0)\) to \(M_{12}\), the vertex \((1, 1)\) to \(G\) and the vertex \((0, 1)\) to \(M_{13}\). The four images of the vertices determine a bilinear map (bilinearna preslikava): the value at the point \((s, t)\) is the weighted average of the four images whose weights are \((1 - s)(1 - t)\), \(s(1 - t)\), \(st\) and \((1 - s)t\), which for every \((s, t)\) in the square are non-negative and add up to \(1\). Let \(\vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the position vector of the vertex \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\). The midpoints are \((\vek p_1 + \vek p_2)/2\) and \((\vek p_1 + \vek p_3)/2\), the centroid \((\vek p_1 + \vek p_2 + \vek p_3)/3\). The coefficient at \(\vek p_2\) is \(\tfrac12 s(1 - t) + \tfrac13 st = s(3 - t)/6\), at \(\vek p_3\) it is \(t(3 - s)/6\), and at \(\vek p_1\) it is the difference to \(1\). The map is therefore

\[\vek x(s, t) = \vek p_1 + \frac{s(3 - t)}{6}\,(\vek p_2 - \vek p_1) + \frac{t(3 - s)}{6}\,(\vek p_3 - \vek p_1) . \tag{15.8}\]

At \(s = t = 0\) this is \(\vek p_1\), at \(s = 1\), \(t = 0\) it is \(\vek p_1 + \tfrac12(\vek p_2 - \vek p_1)\), that is \(M_{12}\). The map is an affine image of the map for \(\vek p_1 = (0, 0)\), \(\vek p_2 = (1, 0)\), \(\vek p_3 = (0, 1)\), whose components are \(x = s(3 - t)/6\) and \(y = t(3 - s)/6\). The other two vertices give equal pieces, in the same order.

Plane problems have a body that is not a triangle but a prism: a triangle with thickness \(h\) in the direction \(z\) (definition 11.32 of chapter 11 has such plates). We cut the prism in the same way, the piece at \(P_1\) is the image of the box \([0, 1]^3\) by the map \((s, t, \lambda) \mapsto (\vek x(s, t),\, h\lambda)\). The three dimensional element is the tetrahedron. We cut it too, into four hexahedra. Take a tetrahedron with vertices \(P_1, \dots, P_4\). Every edge has a midpoint, every face a face centroid, and the body has the centroid \(G\) (the name has the same reason as for the triangle). The hexahedron at \(P_1\) has as vertices \(P_1\), the midpoints of the three edges that meet in \(P_1\), the centroids of the three faces through \(P_1\) and the centroid \(G\). The weights of the eight vertices of the cube are now products of three factors, \((1 - s)(1 - t)(1 - u)\), \(s(1 - t)(1 - u)\) and so on. At \(\vek p_2\) the coefficient is made of the four weights whose vertex has the image \(P_2\): \(\tfrac12 s(1 - t)(1 - u) + \tfrac13 st(1 - u) + \tfrac13 s(1 - t)u + \tfrac14 stu = s\,(6 - 2t - 2u + tu)/12\), at \(\vek p_3\) and \(\vek p_4\) analogously. The map of the cube \([0, 1]^3\) is

\[\vek x(s, t, u) = \vek p_1 + \frac{s\,(6 - 2t - 2u + tu)}{12}\,(\vek p_2 - \vek p_1) + \frac{t\,(6 - 2s - 2u + su)}{12}\,(\vek p_3 - \vek p_1) + \frac{u\,(6 - 2s - 2t + st)}{12}\,(\vek p_4 - \vek p_1) . \tag{15.9}\]

For the divergence theorem we need the conditions of definition 4.65: the map must be twice continuously differentiable, injective on an open set around the cube and have a positive Jacobian determinant. The first condition holds for polynomials. For the other two a property helps that we shall test on our maps. Its name is diagonal dominance (diagonalna prevlada): in every row of the Jacobian matrix the diagonal element is larger than the sum of the absolute values of the others. What this says is shown by the example \(x = s(3 - t)/6\), \(y = t(3 - s)/6\). The first row of the Jacobian matrix is \(\big((3 - t)/6,\, -s/6\big)\), the diagonal element \((3 - t)/6\) is for \(t, |s| \le 1\) at least \(1/3\), and the other one is in absolute value at most \(1/6\).

Lemma 15.10 Diagonal dominance gives injectivity (diagonalna prevlada zagotovi injektivnost)

Let \(D\) be an open convex set of points in space, \(\Phi = (\Phi_1, \Phi_2, \Phi_3)\) a continuously differentiable map on \(D\), and let at every point of \(D\) for every \(i = 1, 2, 3\)

\[\frac{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} > \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}} \Big|\frac{\partial\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big| . \tag{15.10}\]

Then \(\Phi\) is injective on \(D\) and its Jacobian determinant \(J_\Phi\) is positive at every point of \(D\).

Proof

We must show two things. Injectivity follows from the fact that when we move along a segment, the fastest changing coordinate of the map really changes, and positivity of the determinant from the fact that when the off-diagonal terms are removed gradually the matrix stays invertible.

  1. Let \(P \ne Q\) be points of \(D\) and \(\vek\delta = Q - P\). The segment \(\gamma(\tau) = P + \tau\vek\delta\) for \(\tau \in [0, 1]\) lies in \(D\), because \(D\) is convex. Choose an index \(i\) for which \(|\delta_{\mathrm{i}}|\) is the largest of the three absolute values, \(|\delta_{\mathrm{i}}| > 0\). If \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} < 0\) we swap the roles of \(P\) and \(Q\), so let \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\).
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43), \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(\gamma(\tau)) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(\gamma(\tau))\,\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\), and this function is continuous. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40), \(\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(Q) - \Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = \int_0^1 \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(\gamma(\tau))\,\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\).
  3. The integrand is at every \(\tau\) at least \(\partial_{\mathrm{i}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}}|\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}|\,|\delta_{\mathrm{j}}|\). Because \(|\delta_{\mathrm{j}}| \le \delta_{\mathrm{i}}\), this is at least \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\big(\partial_{\mathrm{i}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}}|\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}|\big)\), which is positive by (15.10). A continuous positive function on \([0, 1]\) has a positive integral (as for the bar in step 1 of the proof of lemma 6.7), so \(\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(Q) \ne \Phi_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\) and \(\Phi(Q) \ne \Phi(P)\).
  4. Let \(\mat J\) be the Jacobian matrix at a point \(P\) of \(D\) and \(\mat J_\tau\) the matrix with the same diagonal elements and off-diagonal elements multiplied by \(\tau \in [0, 1]\). If \(\mat J_\tau\vek v = \vek 0\) held for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), then at the index \(i\) with the largest \(|v_{\mathrm{i}}|\) we would have \(\partial_{\mathrm{i}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}\,|v_{\mathrm{i}}| = \tau\,\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}}\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le \sum_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}}|\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\Phi_{\mathrm{i}}|\,|v_{\mathrm{i}}|\), which (15.10) excludes. So \(\det\mat J_\tau \ne 0\) for every \(\tau \in [0, 1]\).
  5. The determinant \(\det\mat J_\tau\) is a polynomial in \(\tau\), hence continuous, and at \(\tau = 0\) it is the product of the diagonal elements (definition 2.50), which is positive. A continuous function on \([0, 1]\) without a zero does not change sign (intermediate value theorem, 2.57), so \(J_\Phi(P) = \det\mat J_1 > 0\).

Source: The lemma is not from a source. The condition (15.10) for the Jacobian matrix at every point is called strict diagonal dominance by rows in the literature on matrices, here with positive diagonal elements. We prove the lemma only for three coordinates.

The lemma is simple but has a limit. If one diagonal element is smaller than the sum of the others, the map may fail to be injective, which is exactly what happens for the folded quadrilateral in section 15.5. Here we use it on the pieces.

Theorem 15.11 Divergence theorem for a tetrahedron and a triangular prism (izrek o divergenci za tetraeder in trikotno prizmo)

Let \(T\) be one of two sets of points:

(a) a tetrahedron with vertices \(P_1, P_2, P_3, P_4\), labelled so that \(\det[\vek p_2 - \vek p_1,\, \vek p_3 - \vek p_1,\, \vek p_4 - \vek p_1] > 0\), that is the set of points \(T = \{\vek p_1 + \xi_2(\vek p_2 - \vek p_1) + \xi_3(\vek p_3 - \vek p_1) + \xi_4(\vek p_4 - \vek p_1) : \xi_2, \xi_3, \xi_4 \ge 0,\ \xi_2 + \xi_3 + \xi_4 \le 1\}\), the spatial version of the triangle (15.1),

(b) a triangular prism \(T = \{(x, y, z) : (x, y) \text{ in the triangle } P_1P_2P_3,\ 0 \le z \le h\}\) with \(h > 0\) and the vertices of the triangle labelled so that \(\det[\vek p_2 - \vek p_1,\, \vek p_3 - \vek p_1] > 0\).

For the tetrahedron the pieces are images of the cube \(K = [0, 1]^3\) by the map (15.9). For the prism the pieces are images of the box \(K\) by the map \((s, t, \lambda) \mapsto (\vek x(s, t), h\lambda)\), where \(\vek x\) is from (15.8). For each piece at the vertex \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) we take \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) as the first vertex and the others in an order such that the determinant is positive. Then:

  1. Every piece is a curved box (definition 4.65), on the open set \(D = (-\tfrac1{10}, \tfrac{11}{10})^3\) around \(K\).
  2. (Stated, not proved.) The four (for the prism three) pieces together give \(T\) and no interior point of any lies in another.
  3. For every common face of two pieces the fluxes of any continuous field through their two pieces of the face are opposite.
  4. If \(\vek v\) is a field continuously differentiable on an open set \(E \supset T\), then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{T}} \operatorname{div}\vek v\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek v\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{15.11}\]
    where \(S\) is the surface and \(\vek n\) its outward unit normal, and the integral over a face that is a triangle or a quadrilateral is the sum of the integrals over the pieces of the face that this cutting gives.
Idea

Every piece has the form that lemma 15.10 covers, so it is a curved box and the theorem of chapter 4 holds for it. What remains is that the interior fluxes cancel, which follows from corollary 4.64, because the two descriptions of the common face are mirror images.

Proof

We must show points 1, 3 and 4. Point 2 is stated. First we do the computation for a reference case, which has as vertices \((0, 0, 0)\) and the unit vectors, then we carry it over to a general element.

  1. The reference tetrahedron has vertices \(\vek p_1 = \vek 0\) and \(\vek p_{2,3,4}\) the unit vectors. Then the map (15.9) equals \(\vek x_{\mathrm{ref}} = \big(s(6 - 2t - 2u + tu),\, t(6 - 2s - 2u + su),\, u(6 - 2s - 2t + st)\big)/12\). Its partial derivatives we compute in turn. The first row of the Jacobian matrix is \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} x = (6 - 2t - 2u + tu)/12\), \(\partial_{\mathrm{t}} x = s(u - 2)/12\), \(\partial_{\mathrm{u}} x = s(t - 2)/12\), the other two are obtained by swapping the variables.
  2. On \(D\) the numbers \(t\) and \(u\) lie between \(-\tfrac1{10}\) and \(\tfrac{11}{10}\), so \(2 - t\) and \(2 - u\) are positive and \(|\partial_{\mathrm{t}} x| + |\partial_{\mathrm{u}} x| = |s|\,(4 - t - u)/12\). For \(s \in (-\tfrac1{10}, \tfrac{11}{10})\) we have \(|s| < \tfrac{11}{10}\) and the difference of the diagonal element and the sum of the others is more than \(\big(6 - 2t - 2u + tu - \tfrac{11}{10}(4 - t - u)\big)/12 = \big(\tfrac85 - \tfrac9{10}t - \tfrac9{10}u + tu\big)/12\). This expression is linear in \(t\) and in \(u\) separately, so it reaches its smallest value at one of the four vertices of the square \([-\tfrac1{10}, \tfrac{11}{10}]^2\): \(1{,}79\), \(0{,}59\), \(0{,}59\) and \(0{,}83\), divided by \(12\). The smallest is \(0{,}59/12 = 0{,}0492\), which agrees with the numerical check on a grid of points (num-02.out, part B). The diagonal element is positive, because it is larger than a non-negative sum. The first row therefore satisfies (15.10), and by the symmetry of the formulas so do the second and the third.
  3. For the reference prism \(x = s(3 - t)/6\), \(y = t(3 - s)/6\), \(z = h\lambda\). The diagonal elements are \((3 - t)/6\), \((3 - s)/6\) and \(h\), the off-diagonal ones \(|s|/6\) and \(|t|/6\). For \(|s|, |t| < \tfrac{11}{10}\) we have \(3 - t - |s| > 0{,}8\) and \(3 - s - |t| > 0{,}8\), so (15.10) holds (part B).
  4. The set \(D\) is open and convex. By lemma 15.10, \(\vek x_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is injective on \(D\) with \(J > 0\). For a general element \(\vek x = \vek p_1 + \mat H\,\vek x_{\mathrm{ref}}\), where the columns of the matrix \(\mat H\) are the vectors \(\vek p_{\mathrm{i}} - \vek p_1\) and \(\det\mat H > 0\) by assumption. The matrix \(\mat H\) is invertible, so \(\vek x\) is injective, and by the chain rule and theorem 2.52 \(J = \det\mat H\,J_{\mathrm{ref}} > 0\). The components are polynomials, so they have continuous partial derivatives of all orders. This proves point 1 for the piece at \(P_1\).
  5. For the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) we do the same with the vertices in an order that has a positive determinant. Of the two orders of the remaining vertices that differ by a swap of two, exactly one has a positive determinant, because swapping two columns changes the sign (lemma 2.51). The computation is the same.
  6. A piece and the fluxes through its faces do not depend on which of the orders with a positive determinant we choose. A cyclic permutation of the other three vertices in (15.9) gives the same map composed with a cyclic permutation of the variables \(s, t, u\), which maps the cube onto itself with Jacobian determinant \(1\), because the three coefficients in (15.9) are the same function with the variables permuted. The common face of the pieces at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) has the vertices \(M_{\mathrm{ab}}\), the centroids of the faces through the edge \(P_{\mathrm{a}}P_{\mathrm{b}}\), and \(G\). For the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) take the order \((a, b, c, d)\) with a positive determinant, for the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) the order \((b, a, d, c)\). This differs from the first by two swaps, so its determinant has the same sign (lemma 2.51). For both pieces the common face is the face \(s = 1\). From (15.9) at \(s = 1\) we get \(\vek x(1, t, u) = \alpha\,(\vek p_{\mathrm{a}} + \vek p_{\mathrm{b}}) + \beta\,\vek p_{\mathrm{c}} + \gamma\,\vek p_{\mathrm{d}}\) with \(\alpha = (6 - 2t - 2u + tu)/12\), \(\beta = t(4 - u)/12\) and \(\gamma = u(4 - t)/12\), since the weight of the vertex \(\vek p_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(1 - \alpha - \beta - \gamma\), equals \(\alpha\). For the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) the roles of \(\vek p_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(\vek p_{\mathrm{b}}\) and the roles of \(\vek p_{\mathrm{c}}\), \(\vek p_{\mathrm{d}}\) are swapped, so its description is \(\vek y(1, t, u) = \vek x(1, u, t)\). The descriptions are related by the swap \(\tau(t, u) = (u, t)\) with \(J_\tau = -1\), which maps the square onto itself, so by corollary 4.64, point 3, the fluxes of every continuous field are opposite. For the prism the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) has the order \((a, b, c)\) and the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) the order \((b, c, a)\), both cyclic shifts of the order \((1, 2, 3)\) and therefore with a positive determinant. From (15.8), \(\vek x(1, t) = \tfrac{3 - t}{6}(\vek p_{\mathrm{a}} + \vek p_{\mathrm{b}}) + \tfrac t3\,\vek p_{\mathrm{c}}\), and for the second piece \(\vek y(s, 1) = \vek x(1, s)\). The common face is the face \(s = 1\) of the first piece with the vector \(\partial_{\mathrm{t}}\Phi \times \partial_\lambda\Phi\), and the face \(t = 1\) of the second with the vector \(\partial_\lambda\Phi \times \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\Phi\) (definition 4.65). The same point of the face has the same pair of derivatives in both, in the reverse order, so the face vectors are opposite and so are the fluxes. This is point 3.
  7. By points 1 to 3, \(T\) is a body of curved boxes that satisfies the assumptions of corollary 4.68. The outer pieces are the pieces of the faces of the element: a face that is a triangle is cut into three quadrilaterals (one on each of the three pieces that touch it), the quadrilateral face of the prism into two rectangles. By point 2 of corollary 4.68 the theorem (15.11) is proved.

Source: KREYSZIG §10.7 (PDF p. 482): a body cut by interior surfaces into pieces has the divergence theorem as the sum of the theorems over the pieces. COOK §6.5 (PDF p. 231): hexahedral isoparametric finite elements are images of a cube whose faces are images of the faces of the cube. The cutting and the proof are ours, point 2 is not proved.

Point 2 is not proved. The proof requires that the images of the cubes fit into the tetrahedron without gaps and overlaps, a geometric fact that we have not written down with the tools of the book. We say what we checked. The volumes of the four pieces of the reference tetrahedron add up to \(0{,}166667 = 1/6\), which is the volume of the tetrahedron (num-02.out, part E), and the vector areas of the six common faces cancel to zero for all pairs of pieces. This is a check, not a proof. For a general tetrahedron with vertices \((0, 0, 0)\), \((40, 0, 0)\), \((10, 30, 0)\) and \((10, 10, 30)\) mm we have \(\det\mat H = 36\,000\) mm\(^3\), volume \(6000\) mm\(^3\) and each piece has \(1500\) mm\(^3\). For the triangle at the beginning of the section \(\det\mat H = 1200\) mm\(^2\), the Jacobian determinant of the piece is \(J = 1200\,(3 - s - t)/12\) mm\(^2\), that is \(300\) mm\(^2\) at \(P_1\) (\(s = t = 0\)) and \(100\) mm\(^2\) at the centroid (\(s = t = 1\)), the area of the piece is \(200\) mm\(^2\) and the three together give \(600\) mm\(^2\) (part C).

Why the centroid? Replace it by a point \(Q\) in the reference triangle \((0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1)\) near the vertex, \(Q = (0{,}1, 0{,}1)\). The quadrilateral \(P_1M_{12}QM_{13}\) is not convex, it folds inward at \(Q\). The Jacobian determinant at the vertices of the square is \(0{,}25\), \(0{,}05\), \(-0{,}15\) and \(0{,}05\) (in the triangle from the beginning of the section \(300\), \(60\), \(-180\) and \(60\) mm\(^2\)), so at the point \((1, 1)\) it is negative, and definition 4.65 is not satisfied. At the centroid the values are \(300\), \(200\), \(100\) and \(200\) mm\(^2\), positive everywhere (part F). The centroid is therefore chosen so that the Jacobian determinant of all pieces is positive.

15.2.2 Mesh and piecewise smooth fields

We cut one element for the sake of the divergence theorem. Now we assemble the body from several elements, as we assembled it from bars in chapter 7. Bars met in nodes, but elements of a body meet along faces and edges, so we must say how.

Definition 15.12 Mesh of elements (mreža elementov)

The body \(V\) of definition 11.1 is cut into a mesh of elements (mreža elementov) \(V_1, \dots, V_{\mathrm{m}}\) if it is the union of the elements and:

  1. Every element is a tetrahedron, a triangular prism or a curved box (definition 4.65), with labelled vertices as in theorem 15.11. Its faces are quadrilaterals (for a box and a prism) or triangles.
  2. No interior point of any element lies in another.
  3. Every face of an element, as a whole set of points, lies either on the surface \(S\) of the body or is a face of exactly one other element. (A face that is only a part of a face of the neighbour is not allowed. This is the hanging node of section 15.1.) A face of a curved box is shared only with another curved box, and the two descriptions of the common face from definition 4.65 are related by a map \(\tau\) with \(J_\tau < 0\) as in corollary 4.64.
  4. Every two elements are connected by a chain of elements in which neighbours have a common face.
  5. The parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) are made of whole outer faces of elements.

For every \(k\) let the function \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) be continuous on \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\). On a common face the two neighbouring functions may have different values. The integral over the body of the function \(f\) that has the values \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) on each element \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the sum of the integrals over the elements, \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} f\,\mathrm{d}V = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{k}} f_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}V\). For a function \(f\) that is continuous on \(V\) this is, by corollary 4.68, point 1, applied to the pieces of the elements, its integral over \(V\). The integral over a face of an element is the sum of the integrals over the pieces of the face that the cutting of theorem 15.11 gives (for a curved box by definition 4.65).

Source: ONATE1 §3.8.1 (PDF pp. 124-125) requires that the field agrees on the common faces. The definition is ours, it rests on definition 4.21 and corollary 4.68.

Point 3 is the reason that the hanging node of section 15.1 is an error: if one element has a node on the edge that the neighbour does not have, the face of the neighbour is not a whole face of the first and the field jumps along the edge. Point 4 forbids that two parts of the body are connected only through an edge or a node, and we need it for the positivity of the energy. A field on such a mesh we describe as follows.

Definition 15.13 Piecewise smooth field (odsekoma gladko polje)

Let \(V\) be cut into a mesh of elements \(V_1, \dots, V_{\mathrm{m}}\). A field \(\vek w = (w_1, w_2, w_3)\) on \(V\), which takes a point of the body as input and gives three numbers as output, is a piecewise smooth field (odsekoma gladko polje) if:

  1. it is continuous on \(V\),
  2. on every element \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) it agrees with a field that is continuously differentiable on an open set containing \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) (definition 1.90).

Its strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\) (equation (1.51)) we compute on each element with this field, on the common faces it is not unique.

Source: The definition is ours, a threefold extension of the piecewise continuously differentiable function (definition 6.11). ONATE1 §3.8.1 (PDF p. 125): if the virtual work contains derivatives of order \(m\), the field must be continuous with derivatives up to \(m - 1\), and for bodies in the plane and in space continuity of the field is enough, because the strains are finite, although they jump at the joints.

Why is continuity of the field enough, and not continuity of the strain too? The strain is the first derivative of the field, the first derivative appears in the virtual work, and the integral of a function with finite jumps is finite. For a field that jumps, the derivative at the joint does not exist. The energy computed element by element stays finite, but the weak form gets a term at the joint, as the example with two blocks later in this section shows. We apply the definition to the linear field of section 15.1: a field that is linear on each triangle and continuous on the common edges (lemma 15.7) is a piecewise smooth field, because on each triangle it is a polynomial.

Lemma 15.14 Energy element by element (energija po elementih)

Let \(V\) be cut into a mesh of elements, \(\vek u\) and \(\vek w\) piecewise smooth fields (definition 15.13) and

\[B(\vek u, \vek w) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}',\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijk}'\mathrm{l}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}'\mathrm{l}}(\vek u)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{15.12}\]

where \(C_{\mathrm{ijk}'\mathrm{l}}\) are the constants of Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) and the strain on each element is computed with its field. Then:

  1. \(B\) is a symmetric bilinear form, and if \(\vek u\) and \(\vek w\) are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(V\), then \(B(\vek u, \vek w)\) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum C_{\mathrm{ijk}'\mathrm{l}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}'\mathrm{l}}(\vek u)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  2. Let the material be isotropic with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and let the only rigid motion \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the zero one. If \(\vek w\) is a piecewise smooth field with \(\vek w = \vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(B(\vek w, \vek w) = 0\), then \(\vek w = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show both points. The first we get from the properties of the integral over pieces, the second from the fact that zero energy means that the field is rigid on each element and that the rigid motions of neighbouring elements agree.

  1. Each term of the sum is bilinear in \(\vek u\) and \(\vek w\) by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.22, point 1) and the linearity of strain, and symmetric because of \(C_{\mathrm{ijk}'\mathrm{l}} = C_{\mathrm{k}'\mathrm{lij}}\) (theorem 3.47, point 1). If both fields are smooth on all of \(V\), the integrand is continuous and by corollary 4.68, point 1, the sum of the integrals over the elements is the integral over \(V\).
  2. Let \(B(\vek w, \vek w) = 0\). By theorem 3.48, point 1, the energy density is positive for every strain that is not zero, so every term of the sum is non-negative and all are zero. On an element the integrand is continuous and non-negative, so it is zero at every point (as in step 3 of the proof of lemma 6.7), hence \(\varepsilon(\vek w) = 0\) everywhere on the element.
  3. Theorem 1.134 needs a connected open set, so we apply it to the interior of the element. For a tetrahedron this is the set of points with \(\xi_2, \xi_3, \xi_4 > 0\) and \(\xi_2 + \xi_3 + \xi_4 < 1\), for a prism the points with \((x, y)\) in the interior of the triangle and \(0 < z < h\). This set is open, because the \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are continuous functions of the point, and convex, so two of its points are joined by a segment in it. For a curved box we take the image of the open cube, and we state without proof that it is a connected open set. On the interior \(\vek w = \vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\vek r\). Every point of the element is a limit of interior points (for a tetrahedron the points \((1 - \eta)P + \eta Q\) with an interior point \(Q\) and \(\eta \to 0\)), and \(\vek w\) is continuous, so the equality holds on all of \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let the elements \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(V_{\mathrm{l}}\) have a common face. The field \(\vek w\) is continuous, so the displacements agree at all points of the face, hence at three points \(\vek r_0\), \(\vek r_1\), \(\vek r_2\) that are not on one line (the face is a triangle or a quadrilateral). The difference \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) with \(\vek a = \vek a_{\mathrm{k}} - \vek a_{\mathrm{l}}\), \(\mat W = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}} - \mat W_{\mathrm{l}}\) is therefore zero at these points. From this \(\mat W\vek e_1 = \mat W\vek e_2 = \vek 0\) for the independent vectors \(\vek e_1 = \vek r_1 - \vek r_0\), \(\vek e_2 = \vek r_2 - \vek r_0\). Because \(\mat W\) is antisymmetric, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat W\vek v = -\vek v\cdot\mat W\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for every \(\vek v\), so \(\mat W\vek v\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_2\) and parallel to \(\vek n = \vek e_1 \times \vek e_2\). The vector \(\mat W\vek n\) is parallel to \(\vek n\) and perpendicular to it (\(\vek n\cdot\mat W\vek n = 0\) by antisymmetry), so it is zero. Every \(\vek v\) is a combination of \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek n\), so \(\mat W\vek v = \vek 0\), \(\mat W = \mat 0\) and \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
  4. By point 4 of definition 15.12, \(\vek w\) is therefore the same rigid motion on all elements. This is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), and by assumption \(\vek w = \vek 0\).

Source: EP §6.2.5 (pp. 283, 287) for the positivity of the energy of a body, lemma 6.7. The proof element by element is not from a source.

Point 2 is the condition of the Ritz method of chapter 6: \(B(\vek w, \vek w) > 0\) for every test field \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\). Without the connectedness of the elements it would not hold. Take two cubes with edge \(20\) mm, \(V_1 = [0, 20]^3\) and \(V_2 = [20, 40] \times [20, 40] \times [0, 20]\) mm, which touch only along the edge \(x = y = 20\) mm, and let \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the face \(x = 0\). The only rigid motion of the whole body that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the zero one. Let the field \(\vek w\) be zero on \(V_1\) and on \(V_2\) a rotation about the common edge by \(0{,}001\) rad, \(\vek w = \mat W(\vek r - \vek r_0)\) with \(\vek r_0 = (20, 20, 0)\) mm and \(\mat W\) the antisymmetric matrix with \(W_{\mathrm{yx}} = -W_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0{,}001\). Points of the edge have \(\vek r - \vek r_0 = (0, 0, z)\), which \(\mat W\) maps to zero, so the field is continuous. The strain is zero on both elements, so \(B(\vek w, \vek w) = 0\), while the vertex \((40, 40, 0)\) mm moves by \((-0{,}02, 0{,}02, 0)\) mm, by \(0{,}028\) mm. The field is not zero but has zero energy, because point 4 of definition 15.12 is missing.

15.2.3 The true solution solves the weak form on every piecewise smooth field

Now to the second gap. The solution \(\vek u\) of the boundary value problem (definition 11.1) has continuously differentiable stresses on the whole body, so the equality \(B(\vek u, \vek w) = \ell(\vek w)\) from the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) held for continuously differentiable fields \(\vek w\). The trial fields of the finite element method, however, are not continuously differentiable but piecewise smooth. We check first on an example whether the equality still holds, and when it does not.

Take a steel bar \(40 \times 20 \times 20\) mm with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), pulled on the face \(x = 40\) mm with the stress \(\sigma_0 = 100\) MPa, the other four faces free. On the face \(x = 0\) the displacement \(\bar{\vek u} = (0, -\nu\sigma_0 y/E, -\nu\sigma_0 z/E)\) is prescribed. This is an idealized support that allows the transverse contraction, a real rigidly fixed one would cause different stresses at the clamping. The material is linearly elastic and the strains are small (\(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\)). The solution is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\) everywhere, the other stresses are zero, and \(\vek u = (\sigma_0/E)\,(x, -\nu y, -\nu z)\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 5{,}102 \cdot 10^{-4}\) (part D). We cut the bar at \(x = 20\) mm into two blocks, \(V_1\) and \(V_2\), which we move apart in the drawing to see both contact faces, and we treat each as an element by theorem 15.11. The test field is not a displacement of the bar but an imagined displacement, so its strain \(1\) does not violate small strains. Let it have only the component \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is zero at \(x = 0\): \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = x\) up to \(x = 20\) mm and \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 20\) mm beyond. The field is continuous with one kink, so piecewise smooth. Because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(\vek w) = 1\) on \(V_1\) and \(0\) on \(V_2\), we get \(B(\vek u, \vek w) = \sigma_0 \cdot 1 \cdot 8000\ \text{mm}^3 = 8 \cdot 10^5\) N mm. The work of the external force is \(\ell(\vek w) = \sigma_0 \cdot 400\ \text{mm}^2 \cdot w_{\mathrm{x}}(40) = 8 \cdot 10^5\) N mm. The equality holds.

Where did what could remain cancel? On the contact face the element \(V_2\) pulls the element \(V_1\) with the force \(\sigma_0 \cdot 400\ \text{mm}^2 = 4 \cdot 10^4\) N in the direction \(+x\), and by Newton's third law \(V_1\) pulls \(V_2\) with an equally large force in the direction \(-x\). Each is the stress \(\sigma\vek n\) on the outward normal of its element, multiplied by the area. Their work on the field \(\vek w\) is \(\sigma_0 \cdot 400 \cdot w_{\mathrm{x}}(20^-) = +8 \cdot 10^5\) N mm on \(V_1\) and \(-\sigma_0 \cdot 400 \cdot w_{\mathrm{x}}(20^+) = -8 \cdot 10^5\) N mm on \(V_2\). Because \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(20\) mm on both sides, the work cancels to zero. Now we move the second block so that the field jumps on \(V_2\): \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 30\) mm, on \(V_1\) it stays \(20\) mm. The field is no longer continuous, so it is not piecewise smooth. The energy is still \(B(\vek u, \vek w) = 8 \cdot 10^5\) N mm, and the work of the external force is now \(\sigma_0 \cdot 400 \cdot 30 = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^6\) N mm. The equality does not hold, the difference is \(-4 \cdot 10^5\) N mm. This is exactly the work of the forces at the contact, \(+8 \cdot 10^5 - 1{,}2 \cdot 10^6 = -4 \cdot 10^5\) N mm, which is \(-\sigma_0 \cdot 400 \cdot 10\) mm, the stress multiplied by the jump of the field (part D). A jump of the field therefore breaks the weak form by exactly the term at the contact. Without continuity of the field the contact term remains.

Theorem 15.15 Weak form on piecewise smooth fields (šibka oblika na odsekoma gladkih poljih)

Let \(V\) be cut into a mesh of elements (definition 15.12) and \(\vek u\) the solution of the boundary value problem of elasticity (definition 11.1) for given \(\vek b\), \(\bar{\vek t}\), \(\bar{\vek u}\). Let \(\vek w\) be a piecewise smooth field (definition 15.13) that is zero at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). Then

\[B(\vek u, \vek w) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A = \ell(\vek w) , \tag{15.13}\]

where \(B\) is the form (15.12).

Idea

We do the same computation as in the proof of theorem 4.82 on each element separately, and the terms at the contacts cancel, because the field \(\vek w\) is continuous and the stress of the solution is continuous.

Proof

We must show that the sum of the parts over elements equals the whole and that the interior ones cancel.

  1. Let \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) be an element. The field \(v_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,w_{\mathrm{i}}\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\), because the \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(V\) (definition 11.1, point 2), and the field \(\vek w\) by definition 15.13 on \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. The divergence is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial_{\mathrm{j}} v_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} (\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}})\,w_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\partial_{\mathrm{j}} w_{\mathrm{i}}\). By the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28) the first term is \(-\vek b\cdot\vek w\), and because of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) the second term is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\).
  3. Theorem (15.11) (or corollary 4.68 for a curved box) for the element \(V_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the field \(\vek v\) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{k}} \sum\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{k}} \vek b\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\partial \mathrm{V}_\mathrm{k}} (\ten\sigma\vek n_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A\), where \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the outward normal of the element and \(\vek v\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek w\cdot\ten\sigma\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.24).
  4. We sum over \(k\). The left side is \(B(\vek u, \vek w)\), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum C_{\mathrm{ijk}'\mathrm{l}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{k}'\mathrm{l}}(\vek u)\) (theorem 3.11). The first term of the right side is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  5. Interior faces appear in pairs (definition 15.12, point 3). On a common face the fields \(\vek w\) from both elements are equal (continuity) and the stress \(\ten\sigma\) is the same (continuous), so the field \(\vek v\) from both sides is the same continuous field on the face. The fluxes of this field through the two pieces of the face are opposite. For curved boxes this is given by corollary 4.64, point 3, with the map \(\tau\) from point 3 of definition 15.12. For tetrahedra and prisms each of the two elements cuts the common face by its vertices, midpoints of edges and centroid, so the pieces of the face are the same sets. For two tetrahedra with the common face \(P_{\mathrm{a}}P_{\mathrm{b}}P_{\mathrm{c}}\) the piece at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) of the first element has the order \((a, b, c, d)\) and on the face \(u = 0\) the description \(\vek x(s, t, 0) = \vek p_{\mathrm{a}} + \tfrac{s(3 - t)}6(\vek p_{\mathrm{b}} - \vek p_{\mathrm{a}}) + \tfrac{t(3 - s)}6(\vek p_{\mathrm{c}} - \vek p_{\mathrm{a}})\). The fourth vertex \(P_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the second lies on the other side of the face, so the order \((a, b, c, e)\) has a negative determinant and \((a, c, b, e)\) a positive one, and the description is \(\vek x(t, s, 0)\). The descriptions are related by \(\tau(s, t) = (t, s)\) with \(J_\tau = -1\), and by corollary 4.64, point 3, the fluxes are opposite. For the pieces at \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{c}}\) the computation is the same. For two prisms with a common vertical face over the edge \(P_{\mathrm{a}}P_{\mathrm{b}}\) the face is described for both pieces at \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) (and likewise at \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\)) with the same two derivatives, \(\tfrac12(\vek p_{\mathrm{b}} - \vek p_{\mathrm{a}})\) in the plane and \((0, 0, h)\), once on the face \(t = 0\) with the vector \(-\partial_\lambda\Phi \times \partial_{\mathrm{s}}\Phi\), once on the face \(s = 0\) with the vector \(-\partial_{\mathrm{t}}\Phi \times \partial_\lambda\Phi\) (definition 4.65), which are opposite. So the fluxes cancel.
  6. The outer faces remain. On \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\ten\sigma\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) (definition 11.1, point 3), on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\vek w = \vek 0\). The sum of the outer terms is \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}}\bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A\), which gives (15.13).

Source: The proof is not from a source, it is the same argument as for theorem 4.82. ONATE1 §3.8.1 (PDF pp. 124-125) on the continuity of the field at the joints of elements.

The proof showed that step 5 uses the continuity of the field. For the jumping field of the example the field \(\vek v\) is \(\sigma_0 \cdot 20\) on one side of the contact and \(\sigma_0 \cdot 30\) on the other, and the fluxes are no longer opposite, the term \(-\sigma_0 \cdot 400 \cdot 10 = -4 \cdot 10^5\) N mm remains. The same happens if along a common edge there is a node at only one element: then the field on a part of the edge is not continuous, the face by point 3 of definition 15.12 is not a whole face of the other element either, and the pairs in step 5 do not exist (section 15.1).

The assumption that \(\vek u\) has continuously differentiable stresses all the way to the surface is an assumption of the definition of the boundary value problem, which the divergence theorem needs. For every body it is not necessarily satisfied, when it is, chapter 17 treats. Here we claim nothing about bodies for which it does not hold.

15.2.4 Consequence for the Ritz method

The proofs of chapter 6 (theorems 6.33, 6.34 and 6.37) were written for a bar, a beam or a body, but they use only four properties: \(B\) is a symmetric bilinear form, \(B(\vek w, \vek w) > 0\) for every test field \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\), the test fields form a vector space that contains the differences of admissible fields and the trial functions, and the true solution satisfies the weak form for every test field. For a mesh of elements we have all four.

Corollary 15.16 Ritz theorems for families of elements (Ritzovi izreki za družine elementov)

Let \(V\) be cut into a mesh of elements (definition 15.12), \(\vek u\) the solution of the boundary value problem (definition 11.1), the material isotropic with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), and let the only rigid motion that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the zero one. Let the test fields be all piecewise smooth fields (definition 15.13) that are zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), and the admissible fields piecewise smooth fields with the values \(\bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). Let \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a trial space spanned by \(n\) independent test fields and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}} \in \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) the Ritz solution (definition 6.32) with the energy from (15.12). Then the statements of theorems 6.33, 6.34 and 6.37 hold: the matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge \Pi(\vek u)\) with \(\Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{n}}) - \Pi(\vek u) = \tfrac12\|\vek u - \vek u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2\), and \(\|\vek u - \vek u_{\mathrm{n}}\|_{\mathrm{E}} \le \|\vek u - \vek v\|_{\mathrm{E}}\) for every \(\vek v \in \psi_0 + \mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Proof

We must show that the four properties that the statements use are satisfied.

  1. \(B\) is a symmetric bilinear form by lemma 15.14, point 1.
  2. By point 2 of the same lemma, \(B(\vek w, \vek w) > 0\) for every test field \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) (the energy \(B(\vek w, \vek w) \ge 0\) is given by a sum of non-negative terms).
  3. The sum and a multiple of piecewise smooth fields with zero displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) are again such fields. The difference of two admissible fields is a test field.
  4. By theorem 15.15, \(B(\vek u, \vek w) = \ell(\vek w)\) for every test field \(\vek w\), in particular for every field of \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{n}}\) and for the difference \(\vek v - \vek u\). Because the proofs of the theorems of chapter 6 use only these four properties, they hold here too.

Source: The proof is not from a source, except for the conclusion about repeating the theorems of chapter 6. We do not carry over Cea's estimate 6.39, because it is proved for a bar with two stiffness bounds.

From history

That the finite element method is a form of the Rayleigh-Ritz method we told in chapter 6 and do not repeat. For a body made of elements this is exactly this corollary: the field of the finite element method is the Ritz solution in the space of piecewise smooth fields, its total potential energy is greater than or equal to the true one, and the error in the energy norm is the smallest among all fields of the same space.

Let us return to what we proved: the Ritz theorems hold for a body made of triangles and tetrahedra. The condition is that the field is continuous across the joints (the lemma on continuity across an edge in section 15.1, for isoparametric elements section 15.5). Chapter 11 left open how supports are assembled from several pieces of the body. For a mesh, point 5 of definition 15.12 settles this: the parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) are made of whole outer faces of elements. The next section computes the matrix that the energy on one triangle gives, for a scalar field (temperature), where the unknown at each node is a single number.
