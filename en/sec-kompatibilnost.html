Chapter 1 Strain

1.18 Do the strains fit together

In section 1.17 we computed from the displacement field of a body how the material is strained close to each point. When the partial derivatives of the displacements are small, this is described by six numbers, the components of the small strain tensor ((1.51)): three normal strains and three shear strains. Let us write them out once more:

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.63}\]

On the right-hand sides only three functions appear, the displacement components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\), while on the left there are six. In this section we ask the opposite question. If six functions are given, is there a displacement field from which we get them by (1.63)? We will see that there is not always one. Six functions that come from three cannot be arbitrary: they must satisfy six equations. First, on the example of a strip, we look at how strains can fail to fit together. Then we meet derivatives of partial derivatives and show that for them the order in which we differentiate does not matter. With this we derive the six equations and say what they mean. Then we show that the strains determine the displacements up to a shift and a small rotation of the body. Finally we look at when the equations are enough for a displacement field to exist, and why they are not enough for a body with a hole.

1.18.1 Six functions from three

Why would anyone know the strains but not the displacements? In this chapter we always went from displacements to strains. In chapter 3, though, we will learn how the strain of a piece of material depends on the forces acting on it, and on whether it is heated. Then we will often know the strains before the displacements. In a body that is heated unevenly, for instance, every piece wants to stretch in its own way, as much as it was heated. If these stretches do not fit together, the pieces cannot stretch as they would like to, and they push on each other or pull on each other. So we must be able to check whether prescribed strains fit together.

For the bar this question did not come up. There the strain was a single function \(\varepsilon(x)\), and we get the displacement of a point by adding up the elongations of all pieces from the fixed end to the point (we will write this precisely in section 3.5). In space, though, three functions give six, and this is the reason why the six functions cannot be arbitrary.

What it means that strains do not fit together is easiest to picture like this (CM §3.7.1, PDF p. 149). Before loading we cut the body into small cubes. We strain each cube separately as the given strains prescribe, and then try to put the cubes back together into a whole body. If the strains are arbitrary, the cubes do not fit together without gaps or overlaps. They fit only if the strain of every cube agrees with the strains of its neighbours.

Let us try this on an example. A thin steel strip of length 200 mm and height 20 mm lies in a plane in which we place the axes \(x\) and \(y\) (figure 1.142). The axis \(x\) points along the strip, the axis \(y\) across it, and the origin is at the middle of the left end. So the strip reaches from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 200\) mm and from \(y = -10\) mm to \(y = 10\) mm. No force acts on it. This is a thought experiment: we prescribe a strain to every piece of the strip and ask whether the strip as a whole can take it. Let us prescribe

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0 , \tag{1.64}\]

and let the other three components be zero. The number \(k\) is a constant. Since \(y^2\) has the unit m² and a strain has no unit, \(k\) has the unit m⁻². Let us take \(k = -10\) m⁻², so that the strains at the edges are a thousandth in size, which by section 1.1 is the upper limit for the strains of steel structures. The longitudinal fibre in the middle of the strip, where \(y = 0\), is to keep its length. The longitudinal fibres at the edges, where \(y = \pm 10\) mm, are to shorten by \(10\ \text{m}^{-2} \cdot (0{,}01\ \text{m})^2 = 0{,}001\), by a thousandth. The fibres at the height \(y = \pm 5\) mm are to shorten by \(10\ \text{m}^{-2} \cdot (0{,}005\ \text{m})^2 = 0{,}00025\), four times less, because \(y\) is half as large there and so \(y^2\) is four times smaller. The transverse fibres are to keep their length, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), and the right angles between longitudinal and transverse fibres are to stay right, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).

Do pieces strained like this fit together into a strip? Figure 1.143 on the right shows the strip cut into five horizontal bands of squares. We strained each band as (1.64) prescribes at its height and placed all bands on top of each other at the left end, so that they touch there. The band in the middle stays straight. The two bands above it curve upward, the two below it downward, and the farther a band lies from the middle, the more it curves. So gaps open between the bands, wider and wider toward the right end. The pieces do not fit together.

For comparison, figure 1.143 in the middle shows the same strip with the prescribed strain

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{y}{R} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0 , \qquad R = 10\ \text{m} ,\]

and the other components zero. The longitudinal fibres above the middle get longer and those below it shorter, in proportion to the distance from the middle, at the edges by a thousandth, since \(0{,}01\ \text{m} / 10\ \text{m} = 0{,}001\). Here all bands curve in the same way, like parts of rings around the same centre, and they fit together without gaps. A bent beam, which we will treat in chapter 5, curves in a similar way.

For the second strip a displacement field exists. It is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{x\,y}{R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{x^2}{2R} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 .\]

Let us check it with (1.63). We compute partial derivatives as ordinary derivatives in which the other variables are treated as numbers, with the rules of lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation.

  1. In \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) the factor \(y/R\) is a constant, and the derivative of \(x\) with respect to \(x\) is \(1\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\).
  2. The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on \(y\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = 0\).
  3. We have \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = x/R\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = -2x/(2R) = -x/R\). The sum is zero, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(x/R - x/R) = 0\).
  4. The component \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) is zero, and \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) do not depend on \(z\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero.

The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -x^2/(2R)\) does not depend on the height \(y\). So all longitudinal fibres curve into the same shape, and that is exactly why the bands fit together (figure 1.143, middle). The right end of the strip at \(x = 0{,}2\) m drops by \(0{,}2^2/(2 \cdot 10)\ \text{m} = 0{,}002\) m, by 2 mm.

For the first strip, though, we will not find such a field. Before we show this, let us write down precisely what we are looking for.

Definition 1.123 Compatible strains (kompatibilne deformacije)

Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) be functions of three variables defined at the interior points of a body. These functions are compatible strains (kompatibilne deformacije) if there is a continuously differentiable displacement field \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) (definition 1.90), defined at the same points, such that the equations (1.63) hold at each of them.

Source: BARBER §2.2.1 (PDF p. 46), CM §3.7.1 (PDF p. 149).

The word compatibility means the ability to go together: strains are compatible if the strained pieces go together into a whole body. By definition 1.23 a displacement field assigns to every point a single displacement vector. This matters: pieces that do not fit together would mean that the same point had to get two different displacements, one as part of one piece and another as part of its neighbour. The strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\) are compatible, since we found a displacement field for them. For the strains (1.64) we will show in section 1.18.3 that they are not.

Why do the bands of the first strip curve differently (figure 1.143, right)? The longitudinal fibres at different heights shorten differently, and we will see that this makes the strip curve, and the faster the strain changes with height, the more strongly it curves. To write this precisely we need the derivative of a partial derivative and the fact that it does not matter in which order we differentiate twice.

1.18.2 Second partial derivatives

Let us return to the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a diameter of 10 mm hangs and stretches under its own weight. It is held in an idealized way: only the point \(O\) at the centre of the top end is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force spread evenly over the top face. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down, the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally. By (1.18) the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the displacement, along the axis \(y\), is

\[u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} ,\]

and by the table in section 1.7.2 its partial derivatives with respect to \(y\) and to \(x\) are

\[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}(x, y, z) = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) , \qquad \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}(x, y, z) = 0{,}6\,a\,y .\]

In section 1.7.3 we saw that the slope \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\) at the depth \(x + \Delta x\) is slightly different from that at the depth \(x\), because lower down the bar thins less (figure 1.48). So a partial derivative is again a function of the point, and we can ask how fast it changes as we move. This is a derivative of a derivative.

Let us compute how fast \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\) changes with depth. We split the expression \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) into \(-0{,}6\,a\,L + 0{,}6\,a\,x\). The first term is a constant, with derivative zero, and by lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation the second has the derivative \(0{,}6\,a\). So

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}\Big) = 0{,}6\,a = 1{,}171 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

When we go down by 1 m, the slope \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\) grows by \(1{,}171 \cdot 10^{-7}\). Since it is negative, its size decreases: lower down the bar thins less.

Definition 1.124 Second partial derivatives (drugi parcialni odvodi)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables, and let its partial derivative \(\partial f / \partial x\) exist at all points whose distance from the point \(P\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). Then \(\partial f / \partial x\) is again a function of three variables, defined at these points. Its partial derivative with respect to \(y\) at the point \(P\), if it exists, is the second partial derivative (drugi parcialni odvod) of the function \(f\), first with respect to \(x\) and then with respect to \(y\):

\[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(P) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big)(P) . \tag{1.65}\]

In the same way we define the second partial derivative for every pair of variables from \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), also for a pair of equal variables, which we write \(\partial^2 f / \partial x^2\). A second partial derivative with respect to two different variables is a mixed partial derivative (mešani odvod). Partial derivatives of third order are obtained in the same way from second partial derivatives, for example

\[\frac{\partial^3 f}{\partial z\,\partial y\,\partial x}(P) = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\Big)(P) .\]

Source: LEBL2 §8.6 (PDF p. 60), for functions of \(n\) variables and derivatives of any order.

A second partial derivative at a point is a number. As a function of the point it is again a function of three variables, which we can differentiate further. A function of three variables has nine second partial derivatives, one for each of the nine choices of the first and the second variable. The notation \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x\) is read from right to left: first we differentiate with respect to the variable standing closest to \(f\), that is \(x\), and then with respect to \(y\). Some books write a mixed derivative with subscripts, \(f_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and then the subscripts stand in the opposite order to our notation (LEBL2, PDF p. 60). As with partial derivatives, we do not use subscripts. The condition that \(\partial f / \partial x\) exists at all points near \(P\) is needed because the definition of the partial derivative 1.25 needs the function \(\partial f / \partial x\) at the points \((x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + \Delta y, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) for all small enough \(\Delta y\).

For the hanging bar we computed above the mixed derivative \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\,\partial y = 0{,}6\,a\). Let us compute the other second partial derivatives of the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) too. The derivative in a row is the first derivative, and the column says with respect to which variable we differentiate it the second time. Differentiating \(0{,}6\,a\,y\) with respect to \(y\) gives \(0{,}6\,a\), and with respect to \(x\) and to \(z\) zero, because \(x\) and \(z\) do not appear in the expression. The derivative of \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) with respect to \(x\) we computed above, and with respect to \(y\) and to \(z\) it is zero. The derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z\) is zero at every point, so all its derivatives are zero as well.

w.r.t. \(x\) w.r.t. \(y\) w.r.t. \(z\)
\(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0{,}6\,a\,y\) \(0\) \(0{,}6\,a\) \(0\)
\(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\) \(0{,}6\,a\) \(0\) \(0\)
\(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z = 0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\)

The two mixed derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) are equal (figure 1.144):

\[\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(0{,}6\,a\,y\big) = 0{,}6\,a , \qquad \frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\big) = 0{,}6\,a .\]

The first says how fast \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) changes with the distance from the axis, that is, the rate at which the displacement toward the axis decreases as we go down. The second says how fast \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\) changes with depth, that is, how strongly the bar thins. These two things sound different, yet they are equal. For the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\), too, the two mixed derivatives are equal for every pair of variables. For \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), for example, \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\,\partial x = \partial / \partial y\,\big(2a\,(L - x)\big) = 0\) and \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\,\partial y = \partial / \partial x\,(-0{,}6\,a\,y) = 0\).

Is the equality a coincidence? Let us look at four values of a function \(f\) at the corners of a small rectangle whose sides are parallel to the axes \(x\) and \(y\) (figure 1.145). Let the coordinate \(z\) be \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\) everywhere, so we leave it out of the notation and write \(f(x, y)\) instead of \(f(x, y, z_{\mathrm{P}})\). The corners are \((x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\), \((x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}})\), \((x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t)\) and \((x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t)\), where \(s\) and \(t\) are positive numbers. We add the values with alternating signs:

\[\begin{aligned} g(s, t) &= f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) \\ &\quad - f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}}) + f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.66}\]

We call the number \(g(s, t)\) the double difference. Its terms can be grouped in two ways.

  1. The first two and the last two: \(\big(f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t)\big) - \big(f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}}) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\big)\). This is the change of \(f\) along the top side, in the direction of the axis \(x\), minus the change along the bottom side. Divided by \(s\), it gives approximately \(\partial f / \partial x\) at the height \(y_{\mathrm{P}} + t\) minus \(\partial f / \partial x\) at the height \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\). Divided further by \(t\), it gives approximately how fast \(\partial f / \partial x\) changes with \(y\), that is \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x\).
  2. The first and the third, and the second and the fourth: \(\big(f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}})\big) - \big(f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\big)\). This is the change along the right side, in the direction of the axis \(y\), minus the change along the left side. Divided by \(t\) and by \(s\), it is approximately \(\partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\).

So the same number \(g(s, t)/(s\,t)\) approximates both mixed derivatives, and we expect them to be equal. For the component \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\) of the hanging bar, which does not depend on \(z\), the double difference is

\[\begin{aligned} g(s, t) &= -0{,}6\,a\,\big[(L - x_{\mathrm{P}} - s)(y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - (L - x_{\mathrm{P}})(y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) \\ &\qquad - (L - x_{\mathrm{P}} - s)\,y_{\mathrm{P}} + (L - x_{\mathrm{P}})\,y_{\mathrm{P}}\big] . \end{aligned}\]

In the square bracket the first two terms together give \(-s\,(y_{\mathrm{P}} + t)\), and the last two give \(s\,y_{\mathrm{P}}\). The sum is \(-s\,t\), so \(g(s, t) = 0{,}6\,a\,s\,t\) and \(g(s, t)/(s\,t) = 0{,}6\,a\) for all \(s\) and \(t\), exactly the two mixed derivatives. For a general function, though, the quotient \(g(s, t)/(s\,t)\) need not equal either of the mixed derivatives at \(P\). With the mean value theorem 1.84, used twice, we show that it equals a mixed derivative at some point inside the rectangle. As the rectangle shrinks, this point approaches \(P\), and if the mixed derivative is continuous, its value approaches the value at \(P\).

Theorem 1.125 Symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (zamenljivost mešanih odvodov)

Let \(f\) be a function of three variables defined at all points whose distance from the point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). Let the partial derivatives \(\partial f / \partial x\) and \(\partial f / \partial y\) and the mixed derivatives \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x\) and \(\partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\) exist at all these points, and let the two mixed derivatives be continuous at the point \(P\) (definition 1.67). Then

\[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(P) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(P) . \tag{1.67}\]

The same holds for the variables \(x\) and \(z\) and for the variables \(y\) and \(z\).

Proof

We must show that the two mixed derivatives at \(P\) are the same number. Throughout the proof the coordinate \(z\) equals \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\), so we leave it out of the notation: \(f(x, y)\) means \(f(x, y, z_{\mathrm{P}})\), and likewise for the partial derivatives.

  1. We pick positive numbers \(s\) and \(t\) with \(s^2 + t^2 < r^2\). Every point \((x_{\mathrm{P}} + v, y_{\mathrm{P}} + w)\) with \(0 \le v \le s\) and \(0 \le w \le t\) is at the distance \(\sqrt{v^2 + w^2} \le \sqrt{s^2 + t^2} < r\) from \(P\) ((1.10)). So the rectangle with the corners \((x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \((x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t)\) lies entirely among the points at which, by assumption, we know all the derivatives used. Let \(g(s, t)\) be the double difference (1.66).
  2. For numbers \(v\) between \(0\) and \(t\) we define the function of one variable \(\varphi_1(v) = f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + v) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + v)\). This is the change of \(f\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) at the height \(y_{\mathrm{P}} + v\). Putting in \(v = t\) and \(v = 0\) we get \(\varphi_1(t) - \varphi_1(0) = g(s, t)\), since these are exactly the four terms of (1.66).
  3. Each of the two terms of \(\varphi_1\) is the function \(f\) along a line in the direction of the axis \(y\). Its derivative with respect to \(v\) is, by the definition of the partial derivative 1.25, the partial derivative \(\partial f / \partial y\), which exists by assumption. The derivative of a difference is the difference of the derivatives (lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation), so \(\varphi_1\) has a derivative at every point of the interval \([0, t]\), a one-sided one at the end points (definition 1.9). Since the inequality \(\sqrt{s^2 + t^2} < r\) is strict, the points just outside the rectangle are also closer to \(P\) than \(r\), so the partial derivative exists at the end points too as a limit on both sides, and this is also a limit from one side (section 1.3.3):
    \[\varphi_1'(v) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + v) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + v) .\]
    By lemma 1.79, that a differentiable function is continuous, \(\varphi_1\) is continuous on the interval \([0, t]\).
  4. By the mean value theorem 1.84 there is a number \(t_1\) between \(0\) and \(t\) such that \(\varphi_1(t) - \varphi_1(0) = \varphi_1'(t_1)\,t\). By steps 2 and 3, therefore,
    \[g(s, t) = t\,\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_{\mathrm{P}} + s, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_1) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_1)\Big) .\]
  5. For numbers \(w\) between \(0\) and \(s\) we define \(\psi_1(w) = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x_{\mathrm{P}} + w, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_1)\). The bracket of step 4 is \(\psi_1(s) - \psi_1(0)\). The function \(\psi_1\) is the function \(\partial f / \partial y\) along the horizontal segment at the height \(y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_1\). Its derivative with respect to \(w\) is, by the definition of second partial derivatives 1.124, the mixed derivative \(\partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\), which exists by assumption, so \(\psi_1\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, s]\), one-sidedly at the end points, since the mixed derivative exists there as a limit on both sides, as in step 3. So by lemma 1.79 it is continuous on \([0, s]\).
  6. By the mean value theorem there is a number \(s_1\) between \(0\) and \(s\) such that \(\psi_1(s) - \psi_1(0) = \psi_1'(s_1)\,s\). Let \(Q_1 = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + s_1, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_1)\). With step 4 we get
    \[g(s, t) = s\,t\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(Q_1) .\]
  7. We proceed in the same way with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) swapped. For numbers \(w\) between \(0\) and \(s\) let \(\varphi_2(w) = f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + w, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + w, y_{\mathrm{P}})\), the change in the direction of the axis \(y\) at the place \(x_{\mathrm{P}} + w\). For it, too, \(\varphi_2(s) - \varphi_2(0) = g(s, t)\). Steps 3 to 6 with the variables swapped give a number \(s_2\) between \(0\) and \(s\), a number \(t_2\) between \(0\) and \(t\) and the point \(Q_2 = (x_{\mathrm{P}} + s_2, y_{\mathrm{P}} + t_2)\) such that
    \[g(s, t) = s\,t\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(Q_2) .\]
  8. The right-hand sides of steps 6 and 7 are equal, because both equal \(g(s, t)\). We divide them by \(s\,t \neq 0\):
    \[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(Q_1) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(Q_2) .\]
  9. Now we use continuity. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). Since \(\partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\) is continuous at \(P\), for the error \(\eta/2\) there is a \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that the value at every point whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(\delta_1\) differs from the value at \(P\) by less than \(\eta/2\). Likewise, for \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x\) there is a \(\delta_2 > 0\).
  10. Let \(\rho\) be the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\), and take \(s = t = \rho/2\). Then \(s^2 + t^2 = \rho^2/2 < r^2\), so step 1 applies. The points \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) are at a distance less than \(\sqrt{s^2 + t^2} = \rho/\sqrt 2 < \rho\) from \(P\), since their coordinates lie strictly between the coordinates of the corners. Therefore
    \[\Big|\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(P) - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(Q_1)\Big| < \frac{\eta}{2} , \qquad \Big|\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(Q_2) - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(P)\Big| < \frac{\eta}{2} .\]
  11. By step 8 the difference of the two mixed derivatives at \(P\) is the sum of the differences of step 10:
    \[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(P) - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(P) = \Big(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(P) - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}(Q_1)\Big) + \Big(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(Q_2) - \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}(P)\Big) .\]
    By the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1), and by step 10 the absolute value of the left-hand side is less than \(\eta/2 + \eta/2 = \eta\).
  12. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely. The absolute value of the difference of the two mixed derivatives at \(P\) is not negative and is smaller than every positive number, so by lemma 1.34 on an inequality tested with all positive numbers it equals zero. The mixed derivatives at \(P\) are equal.
  13. For the variables \(x\) and \(z\) and for \(y\) and \(z\) steps 1 to 12 are the same, only with the variables renamed.

Source: LEBL2 Prop. 8.6.2 (PDF pp. 60-62). The source assumes that all partial derivatives up to second order are continuous at all points of an open set, and remarks that continuity of the two mixed derivatives is enough. The proof uses continuity only at the point \(P\). Steps 9 to 12 replace the limit of a function of two variables used by the source and are not from the source.

The proof wrote the double difference twice as the product of \(s\,t\) and a mixed derivative, once with one order of differentiation and once with the other, each time at some point inside the rectangle. As the rectangle shrinks, both points approach \(P\), and the continuity of the mixed derivatives makes their values approach the values at \(P\). The mean value theorem did the same job here, twice, as for the change of a field over a short step in section 1.15: it turned a difference of values into a derivative at a point in between.

Is the assumption of continuity needed? It is. In Exercise 8.6.2 (PDF p. 62), LEBL2 gives the function of two variables

\[f(x, y) = \frac{x\,y\,(x^2 - y^2)}{x^2 + y^2} \quad \text{for } (x, y) \neq (0, 0) , \qquad f(0, 0) = 0 ,\]

whose first order partial derivatives exist and are continuous, while the mixed derivatives exist but are not continuous at the origin and differ there. On the axis \(y\) the difference quotient with respect to \(x\) is \(\big(f(h, y) - f(0, y)\big)/h = y\,(h^2 - y^2)/(h^2 + y^2)\), and as \(h\) goes to zero with \(y \neq 0\), the numerator approaches \(-y^3\), the denominator \(y^2\), and the quotient \(-y\). So \(\partial f / \partial x\,(0, y) = -y\), and this holds at \(y = 0\) too, since \(f\) is zero everywhere on the axis \(x\). In the same way we get the value \(\partial f / \partial y\,(x, 0) = x\) on the axis \(x\). Therefore \(\partial^2 f / \partial y\,\partial x\,(0, 0) = -1\), \(\partial^2 f / \partial x\,\partial y\,(0, 0) = 1\). For the hanging bar and for the strips of this section the displacement components are polynomials. Their partial derivatives of all orders are polynomials again, and these are continuous everywhere by corollary 1.69, that polynomials in \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are continuous.

In the equations we are looking for we will differentiate three times and swap the order in several places. So we extend the theorem to third derivatives.

Corollary 1.126 Independence of the order of differentiation (neodvisnost od vrstnega reda odvajanja)

Let \(D\) be a set of points in which every point is interior, that is, around every point \(P\) of \(D\) there is a number \(r > 0\) such that all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\) are in \(D\). Let a function \(f\) have partial derivatives of first, second and third order at every point of \(D\), and let all these derivatives be continuous at every point of \(D\). Then at every point of \(D\) the second and third partial derivatives of \(f\) do not depend on the order in which we differentiate.

Proof

We must show that for second and third partial derivatives a swap of the order of differentiation does not change the value. Let \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) be variables, each of them \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\).

1. Second derivatives.

  1. Let \(P\) be a point of \(D\) and \(r\) its number from the assumption. The function \(f\) is defined at all points whose distance from \(P\) is less than \(r\), its first and second partial derivatives exist there, and the mixed derivatives are continuous at \(P\). By theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, \(\partial^2 f / \partial a\,\partial b\,(P) = \partial^2 f / \partial b\,\partial a\,(P)\).

2. Third derivatives.

  1. Swapping the inner pair. By the first part, the functions \(\partial^2 f / \partial b\,\partial c\) and \(\partial^2 f / \partial c\,\partial b\) are equal at every point of \(D\). Equal functions have equal difference quotients and so equal partial derivatives, therefore \(\partial^3 f / \partial a\,\partial b\,\partial c = \partial^3 f / \partial a\,\partial c\,\partial b\) at every point of \(D\).
  2. Swapping the outer pair. Let \(h = \partial f / \partial c\). Its first partial derivatives are second partial derivatives of \(f\), and its second partial derivatives are third partial derivatives of \(f\), which exist and are continuous. By theorem 1.125 applied to \(h\), \(\partial^2 h / \partial a\,\partial b = \partial^2 h / \partial b\,\partial a\), that is, \(\partial^3 f / \partial a\,\partial b\,\partial c = \partial^3 f / \partial b\,\partial a\,\partial c\) at every point of \(D\).
  3. From the order \(a\,b\,c\) we get all six orders by a chain of swaps, each time swapping either the outer or the inner pair: \(a\,b\,c \to b\,a\,c \to b\,c\,a \to c\,b\,a \to c\,a\,b \to a\,c\,b\). By steps 1 and 2 no swap changes the value, so all orders give the same value. If two of the variables are equal, there are fewer different orders, but the chain still covers them all.

Source: LEBL2 Exercise 8.6.3 (PDF p. 62) states the claim for derivatives of any order. The proof is not from the source.

A function whose partial derivatives up to third order exist and are continuous at every point of a set is called by LEBL2 a three times continuously differentiable function (LEBL2 Def. 8.6.1, PDF p. 60). For a displacement field this is more than the continuous differentiability of definition 1.90 requires. The body has no cracks, and the strains and their first and second partial derivatives change from point to point without jumps. We will need this assumption for the equations we are looking for. It fails, for example, at a crack or at a point where a force concentrated at one point acts on the body (section 1.15).

1.18.3 Compatibility equations

Let us return to the strip of section 1.18.1. A thin steel strip of length 200 mm and height 20 mm lies in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), the axis \(x\) along the strip, the origin at the middle of the left end. We prescribed the strains (1.64) to it, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), with \(k = -10\) m⁻². Let us show that there is no displacement field with these strains. We argue by contradiction: suppose that at the interior points of the strip there is a displacement field whose components have continuous partial derivatives of first and second order and which gives these strains.

  1. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = 0\) at every point, \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) has derivative zero along every transverse segment on which \(x\) and \(z\) are constant. By corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative, applied on the interval between \(0\) and \(y\), we have \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0, z)\) for every \(y\). All points of a transverse segment move equally in the direction of the axis \(y\).
  2. So \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) and \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2\) do not depend on \(y\) either. Indeed, at the points \((x, y, z)\) and \((x, 0, z)\) the difference quotients with respect to \(x\) are equal, because the values are equal, and so their limits are equal too.
  3. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), by (1.63) \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 0\), that is, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = -\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) at every point.
  4. Equal functions have equal partial derivatives with respect to \(x\): \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2 = -\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\,\partial y\).
  5. By theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\,\partial y = \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\,\partial x = \partial / \partial y\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x) = \partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y\).
  6. For the prescribed strain \(\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y = \partial(k\,y^2)/\partial y = 2k\,y\) (lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation). With steps 4 and 5,
    \[\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x^2} = -2k\,y .\]
  7. By step 2 the left-hand side does not depend on \(y\), but the right-hand side does. At \(y = 0\) the right-hand side is \(0\), at \(y = 5\) mm it is \(-2 \cdot (-10\ \text{m}^{-2}) \cdot 0{,}005\ \text{m} = 0{,}1\ \text{m}^{-1}\). This is a contradiction, so there is no displacement field with the prescribed strains.

The computation also tells us why the bands in figure 1.143 curve. The partial derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) is the slope of a longitudinal fibre after straining: how much the fibre rises per unit length. The second derivative \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2\) says how fast this slope changes along the fibre, that is, how strongly the fibre curves. Steps 3 to 6 say that in a strip without shear \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2 = -\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y\): the longitudinal fibre at the height \(y\) curves the more strongly, the faster the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) changes with height there. Steps 1 and 2 say that when the transverse fibres keep their length, all longitudinal fibres curve equally. Both requirements can hold together only if \(\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y\) is the same at all heights. For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\) we have \(\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y = 1/R\) everywhere, and indeed \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x^2 = \partial^2(-x^2/(2R))/\partial x^2 = -1/R\). For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\), though, \(2k\,y\) differs at different heights.

Why a fibre curves when the strain changes with height is shown in figure 1.146. Take two short longitudinal fibres of length \(\Delta x\) at the heights \(y\) and \(y + \Delta y\) that start on the same transverse fibre. The upper one lengthens by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(y + \Delta y)\,\Delta x\), the lower one by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(y)\,\Delta x\). If the two strains differ, the right ends move by different amounts, and the transverse fibre on the right tilts relative to the one on the left. Since the right angles are to stay right, the longitudinal fibres turn together with the tilted transverse fibre. The next piece in the row turns a little more, and so the row of pieces curves.

What if the transverse fibres do not keep their length, or the right angles change? Then the longitudinal fibres at different heights can curve differently. If, for example, the transverse fibres lengthen by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -k\,x^2\), that is, more and more toward the right end of the strip, the strains

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -k\,x^2 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\]

come from the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = k\,x\,y^2\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -k\,x^2\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). Indeed \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = k\,y^2\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y = -k\,x^2\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x = 2k\,x\,y - 2k\,x\,y = 0\). Transverse fibres that lengthen differently move the longitudinal fibres apart just enough for them to curve differently. With \(k = -10\) m⁻², however, this example is no longer one of small strains. At the right end of the strip, at \(x = 200\) mm, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 10\ \text{m}^{-2} \cdot (0{,}2\ \text{m})^2 = 0{,}4\), four hundred times the bound \(0{,}001\) that the small deformation assumption sets for steel structures (section 1.17.3), and there the small strain tensor no longer tells accurately how much the fibres lengthen. The number \(k\) is therefore strongly exaggerated. We kept it only so that the strip can be compared with the first strip. The condition that we will test on the strips is an exact relation between second partial derivatives of the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which the components computed from a displacement field satisfy, whatever the size of \(k\). The size of \(k\) decides only whether these components still describe the lengthening of the fibres accurately. All computations of this section are linear in \(k\): at another \(k\) the strains, the displacements and all their derivatives are only multiplied by the same number. With \(k = -0{,}025\) m⁻² the largest strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) would be exactly \(0{,}001\), while \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the edges would be only \(2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and everything that follows would hold in the same way. The general condition, which says how much the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and the shear \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) must make up for fibres at different heights curving differently, is the first of the six equations in the following theorem.

From history

The relations given by the theorem below were first obtained by Saint-Venant in 1864, without using rotations. The proof that he indicated was developed later by Gustav Robert Kirchhoff, and the proof through rotations is the work of Eugenio Beltrami, published in 1889 (LOVE, PDF p. 68-69).

The six equations that the components of the strain, calculated from the displacement field, satisfy are given by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.127 Compatibility equations (enačbe kompatibilnosti)

Let \(D\) be a set of points in which every point is interior, and let the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) of a displacement field have, at every point of \(D\), partial derivatives of first, second and third order that are continuous at every point of \(D\). Then the strain components (1.63) satisfy, at every point of \(D\), the equations

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial y^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial z^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial y^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial z^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} . \end{aligned} \tag{1.68}\]
Proof

We must show that each of the six equations holds when we put the components (1.63) into it. The strain components are first partial derivatives of the displacements or halves of their sums, so their second partial derivatives are third partial derivatives of the displacements. By corollary 1.126 on the independence of the order of differentiation, the third partial derivatives of the displacements do not depend on the order of differentiation. We compute partial derivatives as ordinary derivatives, so the two rules of lemma 1.81 hold for them: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and a constant factor can be taken out.

1. The first equation.

  1. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\), \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2\) is a third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), once with respect to \(x\) and twice with respect to \(y\). By the same corollary we can write it as
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}\Big) .\]
  2. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\), \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} / \partial x^2\) is a third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), once with respect to \(y\) and twice with respect to \(x\). By the same corollary,
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) .\]
  3. We add the equalities of steps 1 and 2. The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, so
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) .\]
  4. By (1.63) the sum in the bracket is \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). We take out the constant factor \(2\) and get the first equation of (1.68).

2. The second and third equations.

  1. We rename the axes: \(x\) to \(y\), \(y\) to \(z\) and \(z\) to \(x\). The equations (1.63) then turn into each other. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial y\), from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\), which by definition equals \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The assumption of the theorem does not change either. So the steps of the first part hold with the renamed axes, and the first equation becomes the second.
  2. When we rename the axes once more in the same way, the second equation becomes the third. In it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) appears, and by corollary 1.126 on the independence of the order of differentiation the order of differentiation in \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} / \partial z\,\partial x\) does not matter.

3. The fourth equation.

  1. We write each term with the displacements by (1.63). The first term on the left is a third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), with respect to \(x\), to \(y\) and to \(z\):
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y\,\partial z}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}\Big) .\]
  2. The second term on the left is
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x^2} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z}\Big) + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}\Big) ,\]
    the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) twice with respect to \(x\) and once with respect to \(z\), and the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) twice with respect to \(x\) and once with respect to \(y\), each with the factor \(\tfrac12\).
  3. The first term on the right is
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}\Big) + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big) ,\]
    half of the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and half of the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) twice with respect to \(x\) and once with respect to \(y\).
  4. The second term on the right is
    \[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial z}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}\Big) + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial z}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) ,\]
    half of the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and half of the third partial derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) twice with respect to \(x\) and once with respect to \(z\).
  5. By corollary 1.126 on the independence of the order of differentiation, third derivatives of the same component with respect to the same variables are equal, whatever the order. On the right-hand side the two halves of the derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) therefore add up to the whole derivative, which equals the first term on the left from step 1. The half of the derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) from step 3 equals the second half from step 2, and the half of the derivative of \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) from step 4 equals the first half from step 2. So the right-hand side equals the left-hand side, and the fourth equation holds.

4. The fifth and sixth equations.

  1. When we rename the axes in the fourth equation as in the second part, \(x\) to \(y\), \(y\) to \(z\) and \(z\) to \(x\), and use \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and the fact that the order of differentiation does not matter, we get the fifth equation. One more renaming gives the sixth.

Source: CM (3.7.4)-(3.7.9) (PDF pp. 150-151), SADD (2.6.2) (PDF p. 51), BARBER (2.8)-(2.9) (PDF p. 45). CM derives the first equation and only states the others, SADD derives them in index notation.

The equations (1.68) are called the Saint-Venant compatibility equations (CM, PDF p. 149, SADD, PDF p. 51). The proof showed where they come from: the strain components are derivatives of only three functions, and when we differentiate them twice more, the same third derivatives of the displacements appear in several places. The equations are exactly these repetitions, written without the displacements. The first three equations relate the normal strains and the shear in one plane, \(x\)-\(y\), \(y\)-\(z\) or \(z\)-\(x\). The other three relate the normal strain in one direction to the shears in all three planes.

Let us test the equations on both strips. For the strip with the strains (1.64), \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2 = \partial(2k\,y)/\partial y = 2k\), while the other two terms of the first equation are zero, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are zero. The first equation would require \(2k + 0 = 0\), but for \(k = -10\) m⁻² the left-hand side is \(-20\) m⁻². The equation fails, so by the theorem no displacement field with continuous derivatives up to third order gives these strains, as we already showed above. For the strip with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\), on the other hand, \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2 = 0\), and the first equation holds, \(0 + 0 = 0\). For the repaired strip with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -k\,x^2\) we have \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} / \partial x^2 = -2k\), and the first equation holds, \(2k - 2k = 0\). The other five equations hold for all three strips, because no component depends on \(z\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero.

What does the first equation say in words? By the computation on the strip, \(-\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y\) is how strongly a longitudinal fibre curves when there is no shear. So the term \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2\) says how fast this curving changes with height. The term \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} / \partial x^2\) says the same for the transverse fibres, with the roles of the axes swapped. The shear term on the right says how much the change of right angles lets the fibres curve differently. The left-hand side must equal the right-hand side, otherwise the pieces do not fit together.

For the hanging bar the test is short. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) pointing down from the centre \(O\) of the top end. By (1.63) and the table of partial derivatives in section 1.7.2 its strains are

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2a\,(L - x) , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]

The shear strains are zero because the pairs of partial derivatives off the diagonal of the table cancel, for example \(\tfrac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y) = 0\). In the first equation \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2\) and \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} / \partial x^2\) are zero. The first is zero because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) does not depend on \(y\). The second is zero because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is a straight line in \(x\) with the derivative \(0{,}6\,a\), which is constant and has derivative zero. The right-hand side is zero because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is zero. The equation holds, \(0 + 0 = 0\). In the same way, in all the other equations all second derivatives of the strains are zero, because the strains depend only on \(x\), and at most linearly. It must be so, since we got the strains from a displacement field whose components are polynomials and so have continuous derivatives of all orders.

A few remarks on the equations.

  1. In plane problems, where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) depend only on \(x\) and \(y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero and no strain depends on \(z\). Only the first equation remains, and the other five turn into \(0 = 0\) (BARBER, PDF p. 45).
  2. Books that use the engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) write the first equation as \(\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} / \partial y^2 + \partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} / \partial x^2 = \partial^2 \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} / \partial x\,\partial y\), without the factor \(2\). CM writes the components \(\varepsilon_{11}\), \(\varepsilon_{12}\) and the coordinates \(X_1\), \(X_2\), SADD writes \(e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(e_{\mathrm{xy}}\), both with half the shear as we do.
  3. We might expect three equations, since six functions come from three. With partial derivatives, though, counting does not work as with equations for numbers: none of the six equations can be derived from the other five (BARBER, PDF p. 45), but there are relations among them involving higher derivatives (SADD, PDF p. 51).
  4. The equations hold for the small strain tensor. For the Green-Lagrange strain tensor (definition 1.112) there are conditions for fitting together too. CM writes them with the deformation gradient, while the form with the Green-Lagrange tensor is so involved that it does not derive it (CM, PDF pp. 149 and 154).
  5. We need the equations only when we solve a problem with strains or with forces in the material, not with displacements. When we first find the displacements and compute the strains from them, the strains fit together by themselves (CM, PDF p. 154, SADD, PDF p. 54). We will use them in chapter 11.

1.18.4 A field without strain

Theorem 1.127 gave a condition that the strains satisfy when they come from a displacement field. Before we ask whether the condition is also enough, let us answer another question. Suppose that a displacement field with the given strains exists. Is it the only one? For the bar we got the answer in section 1.14.6: two displacements of the bar that have the same strain at every point differ only by the same shift of all points (corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative). In space we expect more freedom. We can shift a body as a whole and also turn it, without changing the distance between any two of its points (section 1.6). Such a rigid-body displacement changes the length of no fibre, so we can add it to every displacement field.

The small strain tensor, though, notices a true rotation. In section 1.17.6 we turned a square plate about the axis \(z\) with the numbers \(s = 0{,}001\) and \(c = \sqrt{1 - s^2}\) and got \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c - 1 = -5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\), although the plate was not strained. Which displacements have a small strain tensor of exactly zero? In section 1.17.5 we mentioned that a displacement given by an antisymmetric matrix that is the same at all points is a rigid rotation when the entries of the matrix are small (CM, PDF p. 143). Let us add to it a shift of all points by the same vector. We get the field

\[\vek u(P) = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}} ,\]

where \(\vek a\) is a constant vector, \(\mat W\) a constant antisymmetric matrix (definition 1.102), and \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}} = \overrightarrow{OP} = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) the vector from the origin \(O\) to the point \(P\), whose components are the coordinates of \(P\). The component \(\ix{i}\) of the displacement is \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = a_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}(P)\), where as before \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\) and \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\). The partial derivative of this component with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(W_{\mathrm{ij}}\): the number \(a_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant, the coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the derivative \(1\) with respect to itself, and the other two coordinates are constant in this differentiation (lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation). So the displacement gradient is \(\mat W\) at every point, and the small strain tensor is \(\tfrac12(\mat W + \mat W^\T) = \tfrac12(\mat W - \mat W) = \mat 0\). The small rotation tensor is \(\tfrac12(\mat W - \mat W^\T) = \mat W\).

The goal of this section is to show the converse: a displacement field whose small strain tensor is zero at every point of a body has the form \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\). From this the answer to the question at the start will follow. Two displacement fields that have the same strains at every point differ only by \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\), that is, by a shift of all points and a small rotation of the body as a whole. There are three assumptions. The displacement field is continuously differentiable (definition 1.90), as we have assumed all along since section 1.15.6. We look at it at the interior points of the body, which form an open set (lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point). The body is in one piece. We will state the last assumption precisely, because without it the claim fails. The route is this. First we look at what the strains say about the rotation. Then we say when a set of points is in one piece, and show that a function with zero partial derivatives on such a set is constant. With both we prove the theorem, test it on the turned plate, and say what it means for the hanging bar and for a body with a hole.

The partial derivatives of the displacement contain the strain and the rotation, \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\) (section 1.17.5). What do the strains say about the rotation? Not its values, since the field \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) has zero strains and the rotation \(\mat W\), which can be any antisymmetric matrix. How the rotation changes from point to point, though, is determined by the strains, when the displacement field has continuous second partial derivatives.

Lemma 1.128 Derivatives of the rotation from derivatives of the strains (odvodi zasuka iz odvodov deformacij)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points, and let the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) of a displacement field have at every point of \(D\) partial derivatives of first and second order that are continuous at every point of \(D\). Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the components of the small strain tensor (definition 1.115) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the components of the small rotation tensor (definition 1.116), where the indices \(\ix{i}\), \(\ix{j}\) and \(\ix{k}\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and let \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\) and \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\). Then at every point of \(D\), for all \(\ix{i}\), \(\ix{j}\) and \(\ix{k}\):

  1. the partial derivatives of the rotation are differences of partial derivatives of the strains,
    \[\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} , \tag{1.69}\]
  2. the second partial derivatives of the displacements are sums of partial derivatives of the strains and the rotation,
    \[\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} + \frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} + \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
Proof

We have to show that the two sides of both equalities agree when the components of the strain and the rotation are written with partial derivatives of the displacements. We compute partial derivatives as ordinary derivatives, so the two rules of lemma 1.81 hold for them: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and a constant factor can be taken out.

1. Derivatives of the rotation.

  1. By definition 1.116, \(\omega_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} - \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\). We differentiate with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) and get, by definition 1.124,
    \[\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  2. By definition 1.115, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\). We differentiate the first with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), the second with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and write the difference:
    \[\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} - \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}} - \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} .\]
  3. Let \(P\) be a point of \(D\). Since \(D\) is open, all points at a distance less than some number \(r > 0\) from \(P\) are in \(D\). At them the first and second partial derivatives of the displacements exist, and at \(P\) they are continuous. So by theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, the order of two differentiations does not matter at \(P\). With two equal variables there is nothing to swap. The second and the fourth term on the right of step 2 are therefore equal and cancel, and in the first and the third we swap the order:
    \[\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ik}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{jk}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  4. The right side is the right side of step 1, so (1.69) holds.

2. Second derivatives of the displacements.

  1. By section 1.17.5, \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\), entry by entry \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} + \omega_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  2. We differentiate both sides with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\). The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, so we get the first equality of item 2. We get the second when we replace \(\partial\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\) by (1.69).

Source: SADD (2.6.6) (PDF p. 52), written with commas for partial derivatives, \(\omega_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{k}} = e_{\mathrm{ik},\mathrm{j}} - e_{\mathrm{jk},\mathrm{i}}\), and with the same sign of the rotation as ours (SADD (2.1.7), PDF p. 40). The proof follows the source, and we justify swapping the order of differentiation by the theorem on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives.

The lemma says that the rotation cannot change arbitrarily: how fast it changes in the direction of the axis \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) is determined by derivatives of the strains. Let us test it on the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre \(O\) of the top end, and \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). From the table of partial derivatives in section 1.7.2 we have at every point

\[\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) = \frac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,y - 0{,}6\,a\,y) = -0{,}6\,a\,y ,\]

with the derivative \(-0{,}6\,a\) with respect to \(y\). The lemma with \(\ix{i} = x\), \(\ix{j} = y\) and \(\ix{k} = y\) gives the same from the strains of the bar, which we computed in section 1.18.3, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\):

\[\frac{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x} = 0 - 0{,}6\,a = -1{,}171 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

Figure 1.45 shows the meaning. The bar thins more at the top than at the bottom, since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) grows with depth at the rate \(0{,}6\,a\). So the vertical fibres tilt, the more the farther from the axis they lie, and the material turns the more, the farther from the axis it is (figure 1.147). At the point \(P\), 4 mm from the axis, \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}6\,a \cdot 0{,}004\ \text{m} = -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\), as in section 1.17.5.

The lemma also gives a short route to the goal of the section. If the strains are zero at every point, so are their derivatives, and by item 2 of the lemma all second partial derivatives of the displacements are zero. This route, however, needs the second partial derivatives of the displacements to exist and be continuous. In chapter 4 we will need the theorem for the difference of two displacement fields, about which we will only know that it is continuously differentiable. So we prove it with first partial derivatives only. The idea is geometric and has three steps (figure 1.148).

  1. Zero strain means that no short fibre gets longer to first order. We will show that therefore not even a long segment in the body gets longer to first order. For the segment from \(A\) to \(B\) with the vector \(\vek d = \overrightarrow{AB}\) we will have \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(u_{\mathrm{i}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(A)\big)\,d_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). When we expand the square of the length of the segment after loading, as in section 1.17.5, this sum is exactly the first-order term. A sum of products of corresponding components of two vectors that is zero means that the vectors are perpendicular, which we will meet precisely in chapter 2. So all points of the segment move equally far along the segment, like the points of a rigid rod.
  2. In a small ball around a point \(C\) this holds for all three sides of every triangle with the corner \(C\). No side gets longer to first order, so the triangle only moves and turns. From this we will derive that the difference of displacements \(\vek u(C + \vek v) - \vek u(C)\) is the product of an antisymmetric matrix with the vector \(\vek v\).
  3. So the displacement gradient is constant near every point. For it to be the same in the whole body we need that from every point of the body we can reach every other one along a path in the body.

What it means for a body to be in one piece, and why it matters, is shown by an example (figure 1.149). Two steel cubes with an edge of 10 mm lie on a table, separated by a gap. The axis \(x\) points from the left cube toward the right one. We leave the left cube in place and shift the right one as a whole by 1 mm in the direction of the axis \(x\). The displacement field is \((0, 0, 0)\) at the points of the left cube and \((1, 0, 0)\) in millimetres at the points of the right one. Around every point the displacement is constant, so all partial derivatives and all strains are zero. But the field is not of the form \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) with the same \(\vek a\) and \(\mat W\) in both cubes. If it were, then for the points \(P\) and \(P + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) in the left cube we would have \(\vek u(P + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}) - \vek u(P) = \mat W\,(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})\). The left side is \(\vek 0\), the right side is \(h\) times the column \(\ix{j}\) of the matrix \(\mat W\) (definition 1.96). All columns would be zero, \(\mat W = \mat 0\), and from \(\vek 0 = \vek a + \mat 0\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) it would follow that \(\vek a = \vek 0\). The displacement in the right cube, though, is not \(\vek 0\). Each cube moves rigidly, but each in its own way. The theorem must therefore require the body to be in one piece, and we will state this with segments.

The segment from a point \(A\) to a point \(B\) is the set of points \(A + t\,\overrightarrow{AB}\) for the numbers \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\). At \(t = 0\) we are at \(A\), at \(t = 1\) at \(B\) ((1.8)). How far are the points of the segment from a given point \(C\)? We will need the answer for the ball in which we carry out the second step of the proof, and for the ring.

Lemma 1.129 Distance to the points of a segment (razdalja do točk daljice)

Let \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) be points, \(t\) a number and \(T = A + t\,\overrightarrow{AB}\). Then:

  1. the square of the distance from \(C\) to \(T\) is
    \[|CT|^2 = (1 - t)\,|CA|^2 + t\,|CB|^2 - t\,(1 - t)\,|AB|^2 , \tag{1.70}\]
  2. \(t\,(1 - t) \le \tfrac14\), so \(|CT|^2 \ge (1 - t)\,|CA|^2 + t\,|CB|^2 - \tfrac14\,|AB|^2\),
  3. if \(0 \le t \le 1\) and \(A\) and \(B\) are at a distance less than some number \(\rho > 0\) from \(C\), then \(T\) is also at a distance less than \(\rho\) from \(C\).
Proof

We have to show the equality of item 1 and then get from it the inequalities of items 2 and 3.

1. The equality.

  1. The coordinate \(x\) of the point \(T\) is \(x_{\mathrm{A}} + t\,(x_{\mathrm{B}} - x_{\mathrm{A}})\) (definition 1.14). Let \(p = x_{\mathrm{A}} - x_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(q = x_{\mathrm{B}} - x_{\mathrm{C}}\). Then \(x_{\mathrm{B}} - x_{\mathrm{A}} = q - p\) and \(x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{C}} = p + t\,(q - p) = (1 - t)\,p + t\,q\).
  2. We square: \((x_{\mathrm{T}} - x_{\mathrm{C}})^2 = (1 - t)^2\,p^2 + 2t\,(1 - t)\,p\,q + t^2\,q^2\).
  3. The right side of (1.70) has for the coordinate \(x\) the part \((1 - t)\,p^2 + t\,q^2 - t\,(1 - t)\,(q - p)^2\). We expand \((q - p)^2 = q^2 - 2p\,q + p^2\) and collect the terms with \(p^2\), with \(q^2\) and with \(p\,q\). The terms with \(p^2\) give \((1 - t)\,p^2 - t\,(1 - t)\,p^2 = (1 - t)^2\,p^2\), the terms with \(q^2\) give \(t\,q^2 - t\,(1 - t)\,q^2 = t^2\,q^2\), and the term with \(p\,q\) is \(2t\,(1 - t)\,p\,q\). This is the expression of step 2.
  4. The same holds for the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\). The square of the distance is the sum of the squares of the differences of the coordinates ((1.10)), so we add the three equalities and get (1.70).

2. Lower bound.

  1. We expand \(\tfrac14 - t\,(1 - t) = t^2 - t + \tfrac14 = \big(t - \tfrac12\big)^2\). A square is not negative, so \(t\,(1 - t) \le \tfrac14\).
  2. The number \(|AB|^2\) is not negative, so \(t\,(1 - t)\,|AB|^2 \le \tfrac14\,|AB|^2\). Putting this into (1.70) gives the inequality of item 2.

3. A point in the ball.

  1. For \(0 \le t \le 1\) the numbers \(t\) and \(1 - t\) are not negative, so the last term in (1.70) is not positive, and \(|CT|^2 \le (1 - t)\,|CA|^2 + t\,|CB|^2\).
  2. By the assumption and by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|CA|^2 < \rho^2\) and \(|CB|^2 < \rho^2\). The numbers \(1 - t\) and \(t\) are not negative and at least one of them is positive, so \((1 - t)\,|CA|^2 + t\,|CB|^2 < (1 - t)\,\rho^2 + t\,\rho^2 = \rho^2\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(|CT|^2 < \rho^2\), and by the same lemma \(|CT| < \rho\).

Source: The lemma is not from a source.

Item 3 says that a ball, that is, the set of all points at a distance less than \(\rho\) from a point \(C\), contains together with every two of its points also the segment between them. Item 1 says something more about a circle. If \(A\) and \(B\) are different points at the distance \(\rho\) from \(C\), then for \(0 < t < 1\) the distance \(|CT|^2 = \rho^2 - t\,(1 - t)\,|AB|^2\) is smaller than \(\rho^2\). All points of the segment between them are therefore closer to \(C\) than \(\rho\), and none except the ends lies on the circle or the sphere of radius \(\rho\).

Now we can state when a set is in one piece. We want to get from point to point along a path of straight pieces that does not leave the set.

Definition 1.130 Connected open set (povezana odprta množica)

A broken line (lomljena črta) from a point \(P\) to a point \(Q\) is a sequence of finitely many points \(P = A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{m}} = Q\) together with the segments \(A_0 A_1\), \(A_1 A_2\), ..., \(A_{\mathrm{m}-1} A_{\mathrm{m}}\), where the segment \(AB\) is the set of points \(A + t\,\overrightarrow{AB}\) with \(0 \le t \le 1\). An open set of points \(D\) is connected (povezana) if every two of its points \(P\) and \(Q\) can be joined by a broken line that lies entirely in \(D\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.6 (PDF p. 419), where this explains what a connected open set in space means, and KREYSZIG §13.3 (PDF p. 646) for sets in the plane.

This is one form of the general notion of connectedness. A more general form, for sets in which a distance is defined, says that the set cannot be divided into two nonempty parts in such a way that every point of each part has around it a ball containing no point of the other part (LEBL1 Def. 7.2.13 and Prop. 7.2.14, PDF p. 268). For open sets in space this is the same as our form. KREYSZIG describes the connectedness of an open set exactly with broken lines (KREYSZIG §9.6, PDF p. 419), and LEBL2 states for connected open sets that every two points can be joined by a path of smooth pieces (LEBL2 §9.3, PDF p. 83), leaving the proof to the reader. We do not prove the equivalence, because we will use only our form. Our form, though, suits only open sets. On a circle, for example, by item 1 of lemma 1.129 no segment with two different ends lies, so two of its points cannot be joined by a broken line in the circle, although the circle is in one piece.

Which bodies are connected? If a set contains together with every two points also the segment between them, a broken line of a single segment is enough. This is so for a ball by item 3 of lemma 1.129. It is so for a box with edges along the axes, because the coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{A}} + t\,(x_{\mathrm{B}} - x_{\mathrm{A}})\) of a point of the segment lies between \(x_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) for \(0 \le t \le 1\), and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). It is also so for a cylinder with axis \(x\), for example the round hanging bar. The coordinate \(x\) of a point of the segment lies between the coordinates of the ends, and for the distance from the axis we use item 3 of the lemma on the points \((0, y_{\mathrm{A}}, z_{\mathrm{A}})\), \((0, y_{\mathrm{B}}, z_{\mathrm{B}})\) and \(C = O\). The two separate cubes of figure 1.149 are not connected. Suppose the left cube reaches from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 10\) mm and the right one from \(x = 20\) mm to \(x = 30\) mm. All corners of a broken line in the set lie in one of the cubes. If the line starts in the left cube and ends in the right one, the first corner in the right cube has before it a corner in the left one. On the segment between them the coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{A}} + t\,(x_{\mathrm{B}} - x_{\mathrm{A}})\) goes from less than \(10\) mm to more than \(20\) mm and equals \(15\) mm at some \(t\), and the point with this coordinate is in neither cube. A body with a hole, though, can be connected too. Such is the ring, the simplest body with a hole (figure 1.150). We will meet it in the next section. There we will show that in a body with a hole the compatibility equations are not enough: the ring will have strains that satisfy them at every point, and no displacement field. The proof will rest on the cut ring, the ring whose hole we cut through with a slit, being determined by its strains up to a rigid motion, which holds for connected bodies, as we will see. Without the connectedness of the cut ring the slit might close in some way and the proof would fail. So we prove it now, together with the connectedness of the whole ring, which we need in chapter 4.

Lemma 1.131 The ring is connected (obroč je povezan)

Lengths are measured in millimetres, \(c > 0\) is a number, and \(\rho_{\mathrm{P}} = \sqrt{x_{\mathrm{P}}^2 + y_{\mathrm{P}}^2}\) is the distance of a point \(P\) from the axis \(z\). Let the ring be the set of points \(P\) with \(40 < \rho_{\mathrm{P}} < 60\) and \(|z_{\mathrm{P}}| < c\), and the cut ring the ring without the points with \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) and \(x_{\mathrm{P}} > 0\). Then both sets are open and connected (definition 1.130).

Proof

We have to show that every point of both sets is an interior point and that every two points can be joined by a broken line in the set. First we join the points of the cut ring, where we avoid the cut, and then the points on the cut. We will use eight points on the circle of radius \(50\) in the plane \(z = 0\),

\[\begin{aligned} K_1 &= (50, 0, 0) , & K_2 &= (30, 40, 0) , & K_3 &= (0, 50, 0) , & K_4 &= (-30, 40, 0) , \\ K_5 &= (-50, 0, 0) , & K_6 &= (-30, -40, 0) , & K_7 &= (0, -50, 0) , & K_8 &= (30, -40, 0) , \end{aligned}\]

for which \(30^2 + 40^2 = 50^2\) (figure 1.150).

1. Both sets are open.

  1. The functions \(f(Q) = x_{\mathrm{Q}}^2 + y_{\mathrm{Q}}^2\) and \(z_{\mathrm{Q}}\) are polynomials in the coordinates, so they are continuous at every point (corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space, item 2). So are the coordinates \(x_{\mathrm{Q}}\) and \(y_{\mathrm{Q}}\).
  2. Let \(P\) be a point of the ring. By lemma 1.28, \(1600 < f(P) < 3600\). Let \(\eta\) be the smallest of the positive numbers \(f(P) - 1600\), \(3600 - f(P)\) and \(c - |z_{\mathrm{P}}|\). By the definition of continuity 1.67 there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that for every point \(Q\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\) the values \(f(Q)\) and \(z_{\mathrm{Q}}\) differ from \(f(P)\) and \(z_{\mathrm{P}}\) by less than \(\eta\).
  3. For such a point, \(1600 < f(Q) < 3600\), so \(40 < \rho_{\mathrm{Q}} < 60\), and by the triangle inequality for numbers \(|z_{\mathrm{Q}}| \le |z_{\mathrm{P}}| + |z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}| < c\). The point \(Q\) is in the ring, so \(P\) is an interior point of the ring.
  4. For a point \(P\) of the cut ring we also reduce \(\eta\) to \(|y_{\mathrm{P}}|\) if \(y_{\mathrm{P}} \neq 0\), and otherwise to \(|x_{\mathrm{P}}|\), since then \(x_{\mathrm{P}} < 0\). We choose the number \(\delta\) for this \(\eta\) so that at the points \(Q\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\) also the coordinates \(x_{\mathrm{Q}}\) and \(y_{\mathrm{Q}}\) differ from \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\) by less than \(\eta\) (step 1). The points \(Q\) with \(|PQ| < \delta\) then have \(y_{\mathrm{Q}}\) of the same sign as \(y_{\mathrm{P}}\), or else \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} < 0\), so they are not on the cut.

2. The cut ring and the ring are connected.

  1. From a point \(P\) of the cut ring we go straight to the point \(M = \lambda_1\,(x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, 0)\) with \(\lambda_1 = 50/\rho_{\mathrm{P}}\), which lies on the circle of radius \(50\) in the plane \(z = 0\). The points of this segment are \(\big(\lambda\,x_{\mathrm{P}},\ \lambda\,y_{\mathrm{P}},\ (1 - t)\,z_{\mathrm{P}}\big)\) with \(\lambda = (1 - t) + t\,\lambda_1 > 0\). Their distance from the axis \(z\) is \(\lambda\,\rho_{\mathrm{P}} = (1 - t)\,\rho_{\mathrm{P}} + 50\,t\) and lies between \(\rho_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(50\), and \(|(1 - t)\,z_{\mathrm{P}}| \le |z_{\mathrm{P}}| < c\), so they are in the ring. The coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) are multiplied by the positive number \(\lambda\) and keep their signs, so the segment does not meet the cut.
  2. Let \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) be points in the plane \(z = 0\) at the distance \(50\) from \(O\), with \(|M_1 M_2|^2 < 3600\). For a point \(T\) of the segment between them, by items 1 and 2 of lemma 1.129 with \(C = O\),
    \[2500 - \tfrac14\,|M_1 M_2|^2 \le |OT|^2 \le 2500 ,\]
    so \(1600 < |OT|^2 \le 2500\). The point \(T\) lies in the plane \(z = 0\), so \(|OT| = \rho_{\mathrm{T}}\), and \(40 < \rho_{\mathrm{T}} \le 50\). The segment lies in the ring.
  3. The squares of the distances between consecutive points \(K_1, K_2, \dots, K_8, K_1\) are \(2000\) or \(1000\), for example \(|K_1 K_2|^2 = 20^2 + 40^2 = 2000\) and \(|K_2 K_3|^2 = 30^2 + 10^2 = 1000\). By step 2 all these segments are in the ring. A coordinate of a point of a segment lies between the coordinates of the ends. The segments \(K_2 K_3\) and \(K_3 K_4\) therefore have \(y > 0\) everywhere, the segments \(K_4 K_5\) and \(K_5 K_6\) have \(x < 0\) everywhere, and the segments \(K_6 K_7\) and \(K_7 K_8\) have \(y < 0\) everywhere. The broken line \(K_2, K_3, \dots, K_8\) is therefore in the cut ring, since it does not touch the cut.
  4. Let \(M\) be a point of the cut ring on the circle of radius \(50\) in the plane \(z = 0\), as in step 1. Either \(|x_{\mathrm{M}}| \ge |y_{\mathrm{M}}|\) or \(|y_{\mathrm{M}}| \ge |x_{\mathrm{M}}|\). Let first \(x_{\mathrm{M}} \ge |y_{\mathrm{M}}|\). Then \(2x_{\mathrm{M}}^2 \ge x_{\mathrm{M}}^2 + y_{\mathrm{M}}^2 = 2500\), so \(x_{\mathrm{M}}^2 \ge 1250 > 35^2\) and \(x_{\mathrm{M}} > 35\). The point \(M\) is not on the cut, so \(y_{\mathrm{M}} \neq 0\). For \(y_{\mathrm{M}} > 0\) we join it to \(K_2\):
    \[\begin{aligned} |M K_2|^2 &= (x_{\mathrm{M}} - 30)^2 + (y_{\mathrm{M}} - 40)^2 = 5000 - 60\,x_{\mathrm{M}} - 80\,y_{\mathrm{M}} \\ &< 5000 - 60 \cdot 35 = 2900 , \end{aligned}\]
    and for \(y_{\mathrm{M}} < 0\) to \(K_8\), where \(|M K_8|^2 = 5000 - 60\,x_{\mathrm{M}} + 80\,y_{\mathrm{M}} < 2900\). By step 2 the segment lies in the ring, and since both ends have \(y > 0\) or both have \(y < 0\), the whole segment has that sign, so it does not meet the cut. For \(y_{\mathrm{M}} \ge |x_{\mathrm{M}}|\) we have \(y_{\mathrm{M}} > 35\) and \(|M K_3|^2 = 5000 - 100\,y_{\mathrm{M}} < 1500\), and the segment has \(y > 0\) everywhere. Likewise the segments to \(K_5\) and to \(K_7\) in the other two cases, \(-x_{\mathrm{M}} \ge |y_{\mathrm{M}}|\) and \(-y_{\mathrm{M}} \ge |x_{\mathrm{M}}|\), are in the ring and have \(x < 0\) and \(y < 0\) everywhere.
  5. For points \(P\) and \(Q\) of the cut ring we put together a broken line from step 1 from \(P\) to \(M_{\mathrm{P}}\), from step 4 from \(M_{\mathrm{P}}\) to one of the points \(K_2, \dots, K_8\), by step 3 to the point \(K_{\mathrm{j}}\) that belongs to the point \(Q\), and from steps 4 and 1 in reverse order to \(Q\). The cut ring is connected.
  6. Let \(P\) be a point of the ring. If \(P\) is not on the cut, it is in the cut ring. If it is on the cut, by part 1 there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that all points with \(|PQ| < \delta\) are in the ring, among them \(P' = P + (0, \delta/2, 0)\) with \(y_{\mathrm{P}'} > 0\). The segment \(PP'\) is in the ring, because its points are at most \(\delta/2\) from \(P\), and \(P'\) is in the cut ring. So every point of the ring is joined to the cut ring by a short segment or by none, and in the cut ring step 5 applies. The ring is connected.

Now we need one more tool, for the first step of the proof. Along the segment from \(P\) in the direction of the vector \(\vek d\) a field is a function of one variable \(t\), which tells where on the segment we are. For a step along an axis its derivative was a partial derivative (lemma 1.87 on the change over a step along an axis). In an arbitrary direction we expect, as in corollary 1.92 on the difference of displacements along a fibre, the sum of products of the partial derivatives with the components of the direction.

Lemma 1.132 Derivative along a segment (odvod vzdolž daljice)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points and \(f\) a function of three variables that is continuously differentiable on \(D\) (definition 1.90). Let \(P\) be a point and \(\vek d = (d_{\mathrm{x}}, d_{\mathrm{y}}, d_{\mathrm{z}})\) a vector. For every number \(t\) for which the point \(P + t\,\vek d\) is in \(D\), let \(g(t) = f(P + t\,\vek d)\). Then for every such number \(t\):

  1. the function \(g\) is defined at all numbers at a distance less than some positive number from \(t\), and it is differentiable at \(t\) with the derivative
    \[\begin{aligned} g'(t) &= \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(P + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{j}} \\ &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(P + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(P + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{y}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}(P + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{z}} , \end{aligned} \tag{1.71}\]
  2. the derivative \(g'\) is continuous at \(t\).
Proof

We have to show that the derivative exists, its value, and its continuity. Let \(t\) be a number for which \(S = P + t\,\vek d\) is in \(D\).

1. The derivative.

  1. If \(\vek d = \vek 0\), then \(g\) is the constant function with the value \(f(P)\), defined for all numbers. By lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, item 4, its derivative is \(0\), which is also the right side of (1.71). From now on let \(\vek d \neq \vek 0\).
  2. Since \(D\) is open, there is an \(r > 0\) such that all points at a distance less than \(r\) from \(S\) are in \(D\). The point \(P + (t + s)\,\vek d = S + s\,\vek d\) is at the distance \(\sqrt{s^2 d_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + s^2 d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + s^2 d_{\mathrm{z}}^2} = |s|\,|\vek d|\) from \(S\) ((1.10)). For \(|s| < r/|\vek d|\) it is therefore in \(D\), and \(g\) is defined at all numbers \(t + s\) with \(|s| < r/|\vek d|\).
  3. Let \(0 < |s| < r/|\vek d|\) and \(Q = S + s\,\vek d\). The components of the step from \(S\) to \(Q\) are \(s\,d_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(s\,d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(s\,d_{\mathrm{z}}\). The function \(f\) is defined at all points at a distance less than \(r\) from \(S\), its partial derivatives exist there and are continuous at \(S\), so theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step holds. By (1.34),
    \[g(t + s) - g(t) = f(Q) - f(S) = m\,s + R(Q) , \qquad m = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(S)\,d_{\mathrm{j}} .\]
  4. We divide by \(s\). The difference quotient of the function \(g\) at \(t\) is \(m + R(Q)/s\).
  5. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By the theorem on the change of a field with the error \(\eta/(2|\vek d|)\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R(Q)| \le \tfrac{\eta}{2|\vek d|}\,|SQ|\) for every point \(Q\) with \(0 < |SQ| < \delta\). Let \(\delta'\) be smaller than the numbers \(\delta/|\vek d|\) and \(r/|\vek d|\). For \(0 < |s| < \delta'\) we have \(0 < |SQ| = |s|\,|\vek d| < \delta\), so
    \[\Big|\frac{g(t + s) - g(t)}{s} - m\Big| = \frac{|R(Q)|}{|s|} \le \frac{\eta}{2|\vek d|} \cdot \frac{|s|\,|\vek d|}{|s|} = \frac{\eta}{2} < \eta .\]
  6. By the definition of the limit 1.5 the limit of the difference quotient is \(m\). By the definition of the derivative 1.9, \(g\) is differentiable at \(t\) and \(g'(t) = m\), which is (1.71).

2. Continuity of the derivative.

  1. For \(\vek d = \vek 0\) the derivative \(g'\) is zero everywhere and therefore continuous. Let \(\vek d \neq \vek 0\). By step 2 of the first part all numbers \(t + s\) with \(|s| < r/|\vek d|\) are such that \(P + (t + s)\,\vek d\) is in \(D\), so by the first part \(g'\) is defined at all of them.
  2. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous at \(S\), so by definition 1.67 there are bands \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\) for the error \(\eta/(3|\vek d|)\). Let \(\delta'\) be the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}/|\vek d|\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}/|\vek d|\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}/|\vek d|\) and \(r/|\vek d|\).
  3. For \(|s| < \delta'\) the point \(S + s\,\vek d\) is at the distance \(|s|\,|\vek d|\) from \(S\), which is less than all three bands. Each partial derivative at it differs from its value at \(S\) by less than \(\eta/(3|\vek d|)\).
  4. No component of a vector is longer than the vector, \(|d_{\mathrm{j}}| \le |\vek d|\) (as in step 4 of the proof of theorem 1.88). By the triangle inequality for numbers and by step 3,
    \[\begin{aligned} |g'(t + s) - g'(t)| &\le \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(S + s\,\vek d) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(S)\Big|\,|d_{\mathrm{j}}| \\ &< 3 \cdot \frac{\eta}{3|\vek d|} \cdot |\vek d| = \eta . \end{aligned}\]
  5. We picked the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily and found a band \(\delta'\) for it, so by definition 1.58 the derivative \(g'\) is continuous at \(t\).

Source: LEBL2 §8.3 (PDF pp. 41-42) for the directional derivative of a differentiable function, and Prop. 8.4.6 (PDF pp. 48-49), which shows that a function with continuous partial derivatives is differentiable. The proof from the theorem on the change of a field over a short step is not from the source.

For \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) the sum in (1.71) is the partial derivative with respect to \(y\), as in the lemma on the change over a step along an axis. Now let us show that a function without change in any direction is constant on a connected set, just as for the bar a function with zero derivative is constant on an interval.

Lemma 1.133 Zero partial derivatives on a connected set (ničelni parcialni odvodi na povezani množici)

Let \(D\) be a connected open set (definition 1.130) and \(f\) a function of three variables defined on \(D\) whose partial derivatives \(\partial f/\partial x\), \(\partial f/\partial y\) and \(\partial f/\partial z\) exist and are equal to zero at every point of \(D\). Then \(f\) is constant on \(D\): \(f(Q) = f(P)\) for every two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of \(D\).

Proof

We have to show that \(f\) does not change along any segment in \(D\), and carry this over to broken lines.

  1. The function \(f\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\). Its partial derivatives exist at every point of \(D\) and are zero, and the function that is zero everywhere is continuous, since the difference of its values is always \(0\), less than any error \(\eta\).
  2. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be points for which the segment \(AB\) lies in \(D\), and \(\vek d = \overrightarrow{AB}\). Let \(g(t) = f(A + t\,\vek d)\) for every \(t\) for which \(A + t\,\vek d\) is in \(D\), among them all \(t\) with \(0 \le t \le 1\). By lemma 1.132 on the derivative along a segment, item 1, for every such \(t\) the function \(g\) is defined at all numbers near \(t\), differentiable at \(t\), and \(g'(t) = 0 \cdot d_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 \cdot d_{\mathrm{y}} + 0 \cdot d_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  3. By lemma 1.79 that a differentiable function is continuous, \(g\) is continuous at every \(t\) with \(0 \le t \le 1\). The condition of continuity on the interval \([0, 1]\) checks only numbers of this interval, so \(g\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\) (as in step 4 of the proof of lemma 1.87).
  4. By corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative, \(g(1) = g(0)\), that is, \(f(B) = f(A)\).
  5. Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be points of \(D\). By definition 1.130 there is a broken line \(P = A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{m}} = Q\) whose segments lie in \(D\). By step 4, \(f(A_{\mathrm{k}}) = f(A_{\mathrm{k}-1})\) for every \(k\) from \(1\) to \(m\), so \(f(Q) = f(A_{\mathrm{m}}) = f(A_{\mathrm{m}-1}) = \dots = f(A_0) = f(P)\).

Source: LEBL2 Cor. 8.4.4 (PDF p. 47), for connected open sets in the general sense, with a different proof. The proof with a broken line is not from the source.

Theorem 1.134 A displacement field without strain (polje brez deformacij)

Let \(D\) be a connected open set of points (definition 1.130) and \(\vek u\) a displacement field that is continuously differentiable on \(D\) (definition 1.90). For a point \(P\) let \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}} = \overrightarrow{OP} = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) be the vector from the origin \(O\) to \(P\). Then:

  1. if the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek u\) (definition 1.115) is the zero matrix at every point of \(D\), there are a constant vector \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) such that
    \[\vek u(P) = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}} \qquad \text{for every point } P \text{ of } D , \tag{1.72}\]
  2. conversely, every field of the form (1.72) with a constant vector \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) has at every point the displacement gradient \(\mat W\) and a zero small strain tensor.
Proof

We have to show that zero strains give the form (1.72), and conversely. The indices \(\ix{i}\), \(\ix{j}\) and \(\ix{k}\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\), and \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(P) = \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,(P)\) are the entries of the displacement gradient \(\mat H(P)\) at the point \(P\) (definition 1.109).

1. Zero strains give the form (1.72).

  1. A segment in \(D\). Let \(A\) and \(B\) be points for which the segment \(AB\) lies in \(D\), and \(\vek d = \overrightarrow{AB}\). For every \(t\) for which \(A + t\,\vek d\) is in \(D\), let \(\varphi(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} u_{\mathrm{i}}(A + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. By lemma 1.132 on the derivative along a segment, item 1, used for each component \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), and by the rules for the derivative of a sum and of a multiple (lemma 1.81), for every \(t\) with \(0 \le t \le 1\) the function \(\varphi\) is defined at all numbers near \(t\), differentiable at \(t\), and
    \[\varphi'(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}(A + t\,\vek d)\,d_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek d^\T\,\mat H(A + t\,\vek d)\,\vek d ,\]
    where we wrote the double sum by (1.41).
  3. By corollary 1.104 on the double sum and the antisymmetric part, item 2, \(\vek d^\T\mat H\,\vek d = \vek d^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek d\), where \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) is the small strain tensor. It is zero at the point \(A + t\,\vek d\), which lies in \(D\). So \(\varphi'(t) = 0\) for every \(t\) with \(0 \le t \le 1\).
  4. As in steps 3 and 4 of the proof of lemma 1.133, \(\varphi\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\) and \(\varphi(1) = \varphi(0)\) (corollary 1.85). Putting in \(\varphi(1) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} u_{\mathrm{i}}(B)\,d_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} u_{\mathrm{i}}(A)\,d_{\mathrm{i}}\) we get
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(u_{\mathrm{i}}(B) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(A)\big)\,d_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 . \tag{1.73}\]
  5. A ball. Let \(C\) be a point of \(D\). Since \(D\) is open, there is a number \(\rho > 0\) such that all points at a distance less than \(\rho\) from \(C\) are in \(D\). For a vector \(\vek v\) with \(|\vek v| < \rho\) the point \(C + \vek v\) is at the distance \(|\vek v|\) from \(C\), so it is in \(D\). Let \(\vek g(\vek v) = \vek u(C + \vek v) - \vek u(C)\), with the components \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\).
  6. Let \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) be vectors with \(|\vek v| < \rho\) and \(|\vek w| < \rho\), \(X = C + \vek v\) and \(Y = C + \vek w\). The points \(C\), \(X\) and \(Y\) are at a distance less than \(\rho\) from \(C\), so by lemma 1.129 on the distance to the points of a segment, item 3, the segments \(CX\), \(CY\) and \(XY\) lie in the ball and hence in \(D\).
  7. For the segment from \(C\) to \(X\), \(\vek d = \vek v\) and \(\vek u(X) - \vek u(C) = \vek g(\vek v)\). By (1.73), \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). Likewise \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\,w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  8. For the segment from \(X\) to \(Y\), \(\vek d = \vek w - \vek v\) and \(\vek u(Y) - \vek u(X) = \vek g(\vek w) - \vek g(\vek v)\). By (1.73), \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w) - g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\big)(w_{\mathrm{i}} - v_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\). We expand into four sums, \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\,w_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\,v_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,w_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). The first and the last are zero by step 7, so
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,w_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{1.74}\]
  9. A sum. Let \(\vek v\), \(\vek w\) and \(\vek v + \vek w\) be shorter than \(\rho\), and let \(\vek m\) be any vector with \(|\vek m| < \rho\). By (1.74), used three times,
    \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v + \vek w)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} &= -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek m)\,(v_{\mathrm{i}} + w_{\mathrm{i}}) \\ &= -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek m)\,v_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek m)\,w_{\mathrm{i}} \\ &= \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} , \end{aligned}\]
    so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \big(g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v + \vek w) - g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v) - g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek w)\big)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  10. Let \(h = \rho/2\). For \(\vek m = h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) only the term with \(\ix{i} = x\) in the sum of step 9 is nonzero, so \(h\,\big(g_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek v + \vek w) - g_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek v) - g_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek w)\big) = 0\), and the bracket is zero. Likewise with \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). So \(\vek g(\vek v + \vek w) = \vek g(\vek v) + \vek g(\vek w)\).
  11. A multiple. Let \(\vek v\) be shorter than \(\rho\) and \(\lambda\) a number for which \(\lambda\,\vek v\) is also shorter than \(\rho\). By (1.74), used twice, for every \(\vek m\) with \(|\vek m| < \rho\)
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\lambda\,\vek v)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek m)\,\lambda\,v_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v)\,m_{\mathrm{i}} .\]
    With \(\vek m = h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) as in step 10 we get \(\vek g(\lambda\,\vek v) = \lambda\,\vek g(\vek v)\).
  12. A matrix. Let \(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\) be the matrix with the entries \((\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = g_{\mathrm{i}}(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})/h\). Let \(|\vek v| < \rho\). By lemma 1.21, \(\vek v = v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The vectors \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) have the lengths \(|v_{\mathrm{x}}|\), \(|v_{\mathrm{y}}|\), \(|v_{\mathrm{z}}|\) and \(\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + v_{\mathrm{y}}^2}\), which are not larger than \(|\vek v|\) (lemma 1.28) and are therefore smaller than \(\rho\). By step 10, used first for \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), then for \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek g(\vek v) = \vek g(v_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + \vek g(v_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + \vek g(v_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). The vector \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \((v_{\mathrm{j}}/h)\) times the vector \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) and has the length \(|v_{\mathrm{j}}| < \rho\), so by step 11, \(\vek g(v_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}) = (v_{\mathrm{j}}/h)\,\vek g(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})\). The component \(\ix{i}\) is
    \[g_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{g_{\mathrm{i}}(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})}{h}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} (\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\,\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}} .\]
  13. Antisymmetry. In (1.74) we put \(\vek v = h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek w = h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\). The vector \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) has only the component \(\ix{i}\) different from zero, and \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) only the component \(\ix{j}\), so of the sum on the left only the term with the component \(\ix{i}\) remains, and of the sum on the right only the term with the component \(\ix{j}\). The left side is \(h\,g_{\mathrm{i}}(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}) = h^2\,(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the right side \(-h\,g_{\mathrm{j}}(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}) = -h^2\,(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ji}}\). So \((\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = -(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ji}}\), and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\) is antisymmetric.
  14. The gradient near \(C\). By step 12, \(\vek u(X) = \vek u(C) + \mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\,\overrightarrow{CX}\) for every point \(X\) at a distance less than \(\rho\) from \(C\). Let \(X\) be at a distance less than \(\rho/4\) from \(C\). By lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point, all points at a distance less than \(\rho/4\) from \(X\) are at a distance less than \(\rho\) from \(C\). For \(0 < |\Delta| < \rho/4\) we therefore have, by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix,
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{i}}(X + \Delta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}) - u_{\mathrm{i}}(X) &= \big(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\,(\overrightarrow{CX} + \Delta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})\big)_{\mathrm{i}} - \big(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\,\overrightarrow{CX}\big)_{\mathrm{i}} \\ &= \Delta\,(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}} . \end{aligned}\]
    The difference quotient is \((\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for every such \(\Delta\), so by lemma 1.26 on the limit of a number plus a multiple, \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(X) = (\mat W_{\mathrm{C}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The displacement gradient equals \(\mat W_{\mathrm{C}}\) at all points at a distance less than \(\rho/4\) from \(C\).
  15. Zero derivatives of the gradient. By step 14 each function \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is constant at all points at a distance less than \(\rho/4\) from \(C\). For \(0 < |\Delta| < \rho/4\) we therefore have \(\big(H_{\mathrm{ij}}(C + \Delta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}) - H_{\mathrm{ij}}(C)\big)/\Delta = 0\), and the partial derivative \(\partial H_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\) is zero at \(C\). We picked the point \(C\) arbitrarily, so each of the nine functions \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) has all partial derivatives equal to zero at every point of \(D\).
  16. A constant gradient. By lemma 1.133 on zero partial derivatives on a connected set, each function \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is constant on \(D\). We choose a point \(C_0\) of \(D\) and write \(\mat W = \mat H(C_0)\). By step 14 at \(X = C_0\), \(\mat W = \mat W_{\mathrm{C}_0}\), which is antisymmetric by step 13. So \(\mat H(P) = \mat W\) for every point \(P\) of \(D\).
  17. A shift. Let \(f_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}(P)\) for \(P\) in \(D\). The partial derivative of the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}\) with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(W_{\mathrm{ik}}\) (lemma 1.81), so \(\partial f_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = H_{\mathrm{ik}} - W_{\mathrm{ik}} = 0\) at every point of \(D\). By the same lemma on zero partial derivatives, each function \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant on \(D\). Let \(\vek a = (f_{\mathrm{x}}(C_0), f_{\mathrm{y}}(C_0), f_{\mathrm{z}}(C_0))\). Then \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = f_{\mathrm{i}}(P) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}(P) = a_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}(P)\), so \(\vek u(P) = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) for every point \(P\) of \(D\).

2. The converse.

  1. The component \(\ix{i}\) of the field (1.72) is \(u_{\mathrm{i}}(P) = a_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{ij}}\,x_{\mathrm{j}}(P)\). The number \(a_{\mathrm{i}}\) has the derivative zero, and the coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the derivative \(1\) with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(0\) with respect to the other coordinates (lemma 1.81). So \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = W_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at every point, and the displacement gradient is \(\mat W\).
  2. The small strain tensor is \(\tfrac12(\mat W + \mat W^\T)\). Since \(\mat W^\T = -\mat W\), this is \(\tfrac12(\mat W - \mat W) = \mat 0\).

Source: SADD (2.2.9) and (2.2.10) (PDF pp. 44-45) writes the most general small rigid-body displacement with components and says for the plane form (2.2.9) that it gives zero strains. That with zero strains the displacements are at most such a motion, SADD states without proof (§6.2, PDF p. 133). CM (PDF p. 143) shows that a displacement with a constant antisymmetric matrix is a small rigid rotation. The proof is not from the sources.

The proof had three parts, as announced. In steps 1 to 4 the derivative along a segment, together with the corollary on a function with zero derivative, showed that no segment in the body gets longer to first order. In steps 5 to 13 the triangle in the ball turned this perpendicularity into linearity: the equality (1.74), which swaps the roles of two vectors at the cost of a sign, gave the sum, the multiple and the antisymmetric matrix. In steps 14 to 17 we passed from the ball to the whole set by broken lines, just as for the bar we concluded from a zero derivative that a function is constant on an interval. The proof needs no second derivatives. We will meet the derivative along a segment again in chapter 4, where we will also use the theorem.

Let us test the theorem on the square plate of section 1.17.6. Lengths are measured in centimetres, the side of the plate is \(5\), and the corners are \(O = (0, 0, 0)\), \(A = (5, 0, 0)\), \(B = (5, 5, 0)\) and \(C = (0, 5, 0)\). Take \(\vek a = \vek 0\) and the antisymmetric matrix with a single pair of entries, \(W_{\mathrm{xy}} = -s\) and \(W_{\mathrm{yx}} = s\), with \(s = 0{,}001\). The field is \(\vek u = \mat W\,\vek r = (-s\,y,\ s\,x,\ 0)\). In micrometres, in which the side of the plate is \(50\,000\), the displacements of the corners are

\[\vek u(A) = (0, 50, 0) , \qquad \vek u(B) = (-50, 50, 0) , \qquad \vek u(C) = (-50, 0, 0) .\]

The sum (1.73) for the segment \(OB\) with \(\vek d = (5, 5, 0)\) is \(-50 \cdot 5 + 50 \cdot 5 + 0 = 0\), and for the segment \(AC\) with \(\vek d = (-5, 5, 0)\) it is \((-50 - 0) \cdot (-5) + (0 - 50) \cdot 5 = 0\). Each corner moves perpendicular to its segment from \(O\) (figure 1.151). The lengths, though, change by a term with a square. The segment \(OA\) of \(50\,000\) µm becomes the segment to the point \((50\,000, 50, 0)\) with the length

\[\sqrt{50\,000^2 + 50^2}\ \text{µm} = 50\,000{,}025\ \text{µm} ,\]

which is \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\) of the length more. This is \(s^2/2\). In general, for every vector of a segment \(\vek d\) the new vector is \(\vek d + \mat W\vek d\), and when we expand the square of its length as in section 1.17.5,

\[|\vek d + \mat W\vek d|^2 = |\vek d|^2 + 2\,\vek d^\T\mat W\vek d + |\mat W\vek d|^2 = |\vek d|^2 + |\mat W\vek d|^2 ,\]

because \(\vek d^\T\mat W\vek d = 0\) by corollary 1.104 on the double sum and the antisymmetric part, item 1. The exact rotation with the same \(s\) from section 1.17.6, which moves the corner \(A\) to \((5c, 5s, 0)\), differs from the field \(\mat W\vek r\) only by a term with a square: it moves the corner \(A\) by \(0{,}025\) µm less in the direction of the axis \(x\), since \(5 \cdot (1 - c) = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) cm. The exact rotation keeps the lengths and has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = c - 1 = -5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\), while the field \(\mat W\vek r\) has a zero small strain tensor and lengthens by \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\). For steel structures, where we are interested in strains around \(0{,}001\), the two rigid motions are equally good.

The theorem gives the answer to the question at the start of the section.

Corollary 1.135 Two fields with equal strains (polji z enakimi deformacijami)

Let \(D\) be a connected open set of points (definition 1.130), and let \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be displacement fields, continuously differentiable on \(D\), that have the same small strain tensor at every point of \(D\). Then there are a constant vector \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) such that

\[\tilde{\vek u}(P) = \vek u(P) + \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}} \qquad \text{for every point } P \text{ of } D .\]
Proof

We have to show that the difference of the fields satisfies the assumptions of theorem 1.134, item 1.

  1. Let \(\vek v = \tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\). The partial derivative of a difference is the difference of the partial derivatives (lemma 1.81), and the difference of continuous functions is continuous (corollary 1.69, item 1), so \(\vek v\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\).
  2. The entry \(\ix{i}\ix{j}\) of the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek v\) is \(\tfrac12\big(\partial v_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial v_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\), by step 1 the difference of the same entries of the fields \(\tilde{\vek u}\) and \(\vek u\), which are equal by assumption. The small strain tensor of the field \(\vek v\) is therefore zero at every point of \(D\).
  3. By theorem 1.134, item 1, \(\vek v(P) = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) with a constant \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\), which is the claim.

Source: SADD (PDF p. 54), BOWER (PDF p. 61), BARBER §9.1.1 (PDF p. 139), where the claim is stated without proof: by integrating the strains we get the displacements only up to a rigid-body motion. The proof from the theorem is not from the sources.

So the displacement is determined by the strains up to six numbers: the three components of the vector \(\vek a\) and the three entries \(W_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{yz}}\) above the diagonal of the matrix \(\mat W\), since the entries below the diagonal are their opposites and the diagonal holds zeros (lemma 1.103, item 3). Six numbers are fixed by six conditions. The steel hanging bar hangs under its own weight and is fixed in the model only at the centre \(O\) of the top face. Its interior points form a cylinder, which is connected, so every other continuously differentiable field with the same strains differs from the field (1.18) at the interior points by an addition \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\). The point \(O\) lies on the surface, but it is the limit of the interior points \((1/n, 0, 0)\) with \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), with the coordinates in metres. When both fields are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the bar with its surface, the same relation therefore holds at \(O\) too, for the displacements and for their partial derivatives (lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points and lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence). The addition has at every point the displacement \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the small rotation tensor \(\mat W\), as we computed at the start of the section. At the point \(O\), where \(\vek r_{\mathrm{O}} = \vek 0\), it moves the material by \(\vek a\) and turns it by \(\mat W\). CM therefore requires that the displacement at \(O\) is zero and that the material next to \(O\) does not turn (CM (7.3.16) and PDF p. 306). The field of the bar satisfies both conditions: \(\vek u(O) = \vek 0\), and the entries of its small rotation tensor are \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}6\,a\,y\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = -0{,}6\,a\,z\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), which are zero at \(y = z = 0\). An addition that does not spoil the conditions must have \(\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W = \mat 0\). The field of the bar is therefore the only field with these strains that satisfies the conditions. CM writes the second condition so that all partial derivatives of the displacement off the diagonal of the gradient are zero at \(O\), which for the bar, which has no shear strains at \(O\), means the same.

The theorem and the corollary only require the body to be connected, not to be free of holes. The ring is connected by lemma 1.131, so two displacement fields of the ring with the same strains are equal up to \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\), although the ring has a hole. For the two separate cubes of figure 1.149, though, the corollary does not hold, because the set is not connected. The corollary is about how many fields with given strains there are when there is one. Whether there is one is another question, and for the ring the answer will be different. We look at this in the next section.

1.18.5 Bodies with holes

Theorem 1.127 says that strains that come from a displacement field satisfy the compatibility equations. So the equations are a necessary condition: if they fail, there is no displacement field. But are they also a sufficient condition? Does a displacement field exist for every six functions that satisfy the equations? The answer depends on the shape of the body.

How would we find the displacement from the strains? We pick a point \(P\) and the displacement there, for example zero. We get the displacement at another point \(Q\) by walking from \(P\) to \(Q\) along some curve in the body and adding up the changes of the displacement over short steps. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, the change over a short step is almost exactly the sum of the products of the partial derivatives with the components of the step. The partial derivatives of the displacement are not only strains, though: they also contain the rotation of the material (definition 1.116). So the rotation too has to be obtained by adding up its changes along the path, and these changes are determined by derivatives of the strains (lemma 1.128 on derivatives of the rotation from derivatives of the strains, SADD (2.6.3)-(2.6.7), PDF p. 52). The compatibility equations make the changes along every small closed path add up to zero. So a small change of the path does not change the result (BARBER, PDF p. 46). If the path from \(P\) to \(Q\) can be turned into every other path between the same two points by small changes, we get the same displacement along all paths, and a displacement field exists. In a ring, though, the path to the left of the hole cannot be turned into the path to the right of it without passing through the hole, that is, outside the body (figure 1.152). The displacement obtained along one path can differ from the displacement obtained along the other.

A body with a hole differs from a body without one also in the displacement, which is not necessarily a single-valued function of the point. Love describes a hollow cylinder from which we cut a narrow wedge between two planes through the axis, and we bring the two faces together and join them (LOVE, PDF p. 240). After the joining the parts of the body pull and push on one another, although no external force acts on it. If we go along a path around the axis, we return to the same point, but the displacement on returning is not equal to the displacement at the start, because we went across the joint, where the material moved so that the two faces coincided (LOVE, PDF p. 240). That the displacement may fail to be a single-valued function of the point was first indicated by G. Weingarten in 1901. Two-dimensional bodies, such as a plane circular ring, were treated by A. Timpe in 1906. A more general theory was developed in a series of notes by Vito Volterra, who called such deformations distorsioni. Love calls them dislocations (dislokacije) (LOVE, PDF p. 240). Volterra published a comprehensive account in 1907 (LOVE, PDF p. 240, TIMO, PDF p. 345). The region with a hole is turned by a cut into a region without a hole, and the displacement may jump across the cut (LOVE, PDF p. 241).

The property that separates these two bodies has its own name.

Definition 1.136 Simply connected body (enostavno povezano telo)

Let \(D\) be a set of points of space.

  1. A closed curve (sklenjena krivulja) in \(D\) is a rule \(\gamma\) whose input is a number \(s\) from the interval \([0, 1]\) and whose output is a point \(\gamma(s)\) of \(D\), such that every coordinate of the point \(\gamma(s)\) is a continuous function of the number \(s\) on \([0, 1]\) (definition 1.58) and \(\gamma(0) = \gamma(1)\).
  2. A closed curve \(\gamma\) can be shrunk continuously to a point in \(D\) if there is a rule \(H\) whose input is a point \((s, t)\) of the square \([0, 1] \times [0, 1]\) and whose output is a point \(H(s, t)\) of \(D\), such that every coordinate of the point \(H(s, t)\) is a continuous function of the point \((s, t)\) of the square (definition 1.67, written for points of the plane), and such that \(H(s, 0) = \gamma(s)\) for every \(s\), \(H(s, 1)\) is the same point for all \(s\) and \(H(0, t) = H(1, t)\) for every \(t\).
  3. The set \(D\) is simply connected (enostavno povezana) if every closed curve in \(D\) can be shrunk continuously to a point in \(D\). A body is simply connected if the set of its interior points is simply connected. A set or a body that is not simply connected is multiply connected (večkratno povezana).

Source: SADD §2.6 (PDF p. 53), KREYSZIG §10.2 (PDF p. 449), where the definition is given in words. The formulation with the rule \(H\) is not from these sources.

The definition writes precisely what we describe with a rubber band. A closed curve is a line that ends where it starts. The number \(s\) says where on the line we are: as \(s\) runs from \(0\) to \(1\), the point \(\gamma(s)\) runs along the whole line and returns to the starting point. Continuity of the coordinates means that the line has no jumps. We think of the number \(t\) in the rule \(H\) as the time of the shrinking (figure 1.153). For every fixed \(t\) the rule \(s \mapsto H(s, t)\) is again a closed curve, since \(H(0, t) = H(1, t)\): this is the rubber band at time \(t\). At time \(0\) it is the curve \(\gamma\), at time \(1\) a single point. All points \(H(s, t)\) lie in \(D\), so the rubber band does not leave the set while it shrinks. The coordinates are continuous functions of the point \((s, t)\), so the rubber band neither tears nor jumps, neither along the line nor in time. For a body we look at the interior points, because the strains and their derivatives are defined there. The definition is written for sets of points of space, and for sets in the plane it reads the same. LEBL2 (PDF p. 86) describes a simply connected domain as a connected open set in which every path between two points can be continuously deformed into every other path between the same two points, and KREYSZIG (PDF p. 449) writes the definition for domains. We require connectedness separately in the theorems (definition 1.130).

A cube, a cylinder and a ball are simply connected: we shrink a curve by moving each of its points at the same time along a segment towards one chosen point. We prove this precisely in chapter 4, section 4.7.8, for all bodies in which every point can be joined to a chosen point by a straight segment inside the body. A cylinder with a spherical cavity inside is simply connected too, because every curve around the cavity can be slid past it (SADD, PDF p. 53), and so is a thick spherical shell, the body between two spheres with a common centre (KREYSZIG, PDF p. 449). We prove both in the same section of chapter 4. A ring, however, is not simply connected, because a curve around the hole cannot be shrunk without passing through the hole (SADD, PDF p. 53, KREYSZIG, PDF p. 449). We prove this too in the same section, for the ring with radii 40 mm and 60 mm and any height, hence also for such a tube, which is only a tall ring. If we cut a ring, it becomes simply connected, which we prove there as well.

Theorem 1.137 Sufficiency of the compatibility equations (zadostnost enačb kompatibilnosti)

Let the set of interior points of a body be connected (definition 1.130) and simply connected, and let six functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) be defined at the interior points of the body, with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order. If they satisfy the compatibility equations (1.68) at every interior point, they are compatible strains: there is a displacement field from which we get them by (1.63).

Source: SADD §2.6 (PDF pp. 52-53), CM §3.7.2 (PDF p. 151). We prove the theorem in full in chapter 4, section 4.7.9. SADD proves it by adding up changes of the displacement along a curve and with a theorem about such sums around closed curves (Stokes' theorem), without stating the smoothness requirements separately. Continuous derivatives up to second order are what his proof uses of the strains.

We prove the theorem in chapter 4, once we can add up small changes precisely (section 3.5). First, in section 4.7.7, we prove it for bodies in which every point can be joined to one chosen point by a straight segment that does not leave the body. The box, the cylinder and the ball are such bodies, the ring is not. Then, in section 4.7.9, we build paths from several segments that avoid the holes, and with simple connectedness we show that the result does not depend on the choice of the path. The books make this step with Stokes' theorem about sums around closed curves (SADD, PDF p. 53, KREYSZIG §10.2, Theorem 3, PDF p. 449), but we will manage with straight segments in small balls. The displacement is determined by the strains up to a rigid-body displacement: in a connected body two fields with the same strains differ only by a shift and a small rotation of the body as a whole, \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) (corollary 1.135 on two fields with equal strains, SADD, PDF p. 54). This agrees with what we saw in section 1.6: a rigid-body displacement does not change distances, so it does not change the strains.

Why the body has to be simply connected is shown by an example (figure 1.154). A thin ring with an inner radius of 40 mm and an outer radius of 60 mm lies in a plane, with its centre at the origin \(O\). Let us prescribe to it a strain that we describe in words. Every short fibre lying along the ring, that is, around the hole, is to shorten by a thousandth. Every short fibre pointing toward the centre is to keep its length. The right angles between these two kinds of fibres are to stay right. The example is not from a source, but it follows the idea of BARBER §2.2.1 (PDF pp. 47-48).

First we cut the ring along a segment from the inner to the outer edge (figure 1.154, left). The cut ring is simply connected (the proof is in chapter 4, section 4.7.8). It takes the prescribed strain like this. Every circle around the centre keeps its radius, and its arc shortens by a thousandth, like a fan that we close a little. The segments pointing toward the centre only turn about the centre. Their length stays the same, and they stay perpendicular to the circles, since a radius is always perpendicular to its circle. This is exactly the prescribed strain. The two ends of the cut, though, move apart (figure 1.154, right). A circle of radius \(r\) has the circumference \(2\pi r\), so its arc shortens by \(0{,}001 \cdot 2\pi r\), and by that much the ends of the cut move apart on this circle. On the inner edge this is \(0{,}001 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 40\) mm \(= 0{,}251\) mm, on the outer edge \(0{,}001 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 60\) mm \(= 0{,}377\) mm. The gap is wider outside than inside, in proportion to the radius, so it has the shape of a wedge. One end of the cut is turned about the centre relative to the other.

Since the cut ring has a displacement field, by theorem 1.127 the prescribed strains satisfy the compatibility equations at every interior point of the cut ring. We could have made the cut anywhere, and the strain would be the same, so the equations hold at every point of the ring. Can the whole ring take these strains? The cut ring is connected (lemma 1.131), so by corollary 1.135 on two fields with equal strains its displacement is determined by the strains up to a rigid-body displacement (SADD, PDF p. 54). Every other displacement field of the cut ring with the same strains differs from ours only by \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\), a shift and a small rotation of the cut ring as a whole. The field \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) is continuous across the cut as well. At the cut it therefore approaches the same vector from both sides and does not change the difference between the displacements of the two ends of the cut. So the gap in figure 1.154 cannot close. The whole ring cannot take these strains, even though the compatibility equations hold at every one of its points.

If we cut such a ring, strained it and forced the ends of the cut together, the pieces would have to strain further so that the ends meet. Displacement fields with a jump across a cut are used for cracks and for dislocations, defects in the structure of metal crystals (SADD, PDF p. 54). For a body with a hole we add to the compatibility equations conditions that the ends of such a cut match. That the two ends of the cut of the ring can differ only by a rigid-body displacement, described by six numbers, three shifts and three rotations, we prove in chapter 4, section 4.7.9. There we also show that the whole ring has a displacement field exactly when this rigid-body displacement is zero. There we also say which properties of the ring the proof uses, so that it holds for every body with one hole and a cut with these properties. BARBER (PDF p. 48) says that the same holds for every hole and that every hole in a body therefore adds six conditions. SADD (PDF pp. 53-54) writes the same conditions with sums along a curve around the hole.

With this we have answered the question from the start of the section. The six strain components cannot be arbitrary, because they come from three displacement components. We found how they are related by differentiating them twice and using the theorem on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives, which we proved with the mean value theorem used twice. On the strip with the prescribed \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) we saw that pieces with strains that do not satisfy the equations do not fit together. In a body without holes the equations are enough, and in the ring we saw that they are not. When a displacement field exists, the strains in a connected body determine it up to a shift and a small rotation. We showed this by treating every segment in the body as a rigid rod and then passing from a small ball to the whole body along broken lines. In chapter 2 we turn to the forces that strain a body.
