Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.9 The weak form of steady heat conduction

So far we found the temperature with series, in a bar and in a rectangular plate (sections 14.5 and 14.6). A series needs a body of simple shape, and for a casting, an engine head or a bracket with holes there is none. For displacements, chapter 6 showed the way for such bodies: we write the problem as an equality of integrals that holds for every test function (the weak form), and look for the best approximation in a chosen family (the Ritz and Galerkin methods), and chapter 7 built the family from finite elements. Does the same way work for temperature? Chapter 6 mentioned steady conduction only as a problem for which the Galerkin method works and "the Ritz method does not". That is not true, and in this section we show what is true: steady conduction has a weak form with the same structure as the bar, the beam and the body, a symmetric bilinear form and a functional that the solution minimizes, so the Ritz and Galerkin methods give the same equations (theorem 6.44). Along the way we must tell apart a problem that has exactly one solution from a problem that has none or infinitely many.

The path is this. First we write the strong form of the steady problem and test it on the fin of section 14.4. Then we derive the weak form from it, for the bar by integration by parts and for a body with the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14), and prove that the two forms are equivalent. Next we show that the bilinear form is positive and with it that the solution minimizes a functional. At the end we show what happens when only heat fluxes are prescribed.

New in this section are the weak form of conduction and its functional. Everything else comes from earlier chapters and sections: the bilinear and linear forms (definitions 6.4 and 6.5), the Ritz method and its properties (chapter 6), the divergence theorem (chapter 4) and the heat flux with the energy balance (sections 14.1 and 14.2).

The symbols are from those sections: temperature \(T\), heat flux \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) (definition 14.3), conductivity \(k\), heat source \(Q\) per unit volume and film coefficient \(h\) (equation (14.12)). Two letters have another meaning in the chapters we build on: in chapters 6 and 7 \(h\) is the element length while here it is the film coefficient (the element length will be written \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) later), and \(\Pi\) is here a functional and not the potential energy. The letters \(\ell\) and \(B\) are the forms of definitions 6.5 and 6.4. Steady (stacionarno) means that the temperature does not change with time, \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), as after a long wait.

14.9.1 Strong form and the fin

We start with the simplest body, a bar, namely the fin of section 14.4.3.

The bar lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\), has a constant cross-section of area \(A\) and perimeter \(P\) and a constant conductivity \(k\). At \(x = 0\) it is attached to a wall at temperature \(T_0\). Along its side surface it exchanges heat with surroundings at temperature \(T_\infty\) by Newton's law (equation (14.12)): per unit of side area \(h\,(T_\infty - T)\) flows into the bar. We draw the arrows into the bar, because here every flux is counted into the body. When the bar is warmer than the surroundings this flux is negative and heat leaves. The model has the idealizations of section 14.4.3, each with its bound and number: \(h\) is constant, and the temperature in a cross-section is a single number, \(T(x)\), which for a round bar of radius \(R\) with \(hR/k \le 0{,}1\) holds to within \(5\ \%\). We add two things to the fin that it did not have there. The first is a source \(Q\) per unit volume, for example the heat produced by an electric current through the bar. The second is the end \(x = L\), which need not be insulated: let a prescribed flux \(\bar q\) per unit of cross-section area flow into it from outside.

The equation comes from the energy balance of a finite piece \([a, b]\), as in theorem 14.22, part 1, only with a source added. In the steady state the stored heat does not grow, so the sum of the contributions is zero. The flux is \(q = -k\,T'\) and \(q\,A\) flows through a cross-section, so along the axis the heat \(-kA\,T'(a)\) enters the piece at \(a\) and the heat \(-kA\,T'(b)\) leaves at \(b\). So \(kA\,T'(b) - kA\,T'(a)\) more enters than leaves, which by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (kA\,T')'\,\mathrm{d}x\). Through the side surface \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b hP\,(T_\infty - T)\,\mathrm{d}x\) enters, and the source gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b A\,Q\,\mathrm{d}x\). Hence

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \Big( (kA\,T')' + hP\,(T_\infty - T) + A\,Q \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \qquad \text{for every piece } [a, b] \subseteq [0, L] . \tag{14.48}\]

The integrand is continuous when \(T\) is twice continuously differentiable and \(Q\) is continuous. A continuous function whose integral over every piece is zero is zero everywhere (step 3 of the proof of theorem 14.22). We get the equation of the fin

\[(kA\,T')' - hP\,T = -hP\,T_\infty - A\,Q , \tag{14.49}\]

which is equation (12.1-10) of COOK, PDF p. 471, without the time term.

At the ends we prescribe two conditions. At the wall the temperature is prescribed, \(T(0) = T_0\). At the end \(x = L\) the heat \(A\,\bar q\) flows into the bar from outside. The axial flux \(q(L)\) is counted along the axis \(x\), that is, out of the bar, so \(-q(L)\,A = kA\,T'(L)\) flows into the bar and the condition is \(k\,T'(L) = \bar q\). For an insulated end \(\bar q = 0\).

For tests we take the fin of section 14.4.3 with an insulated end, \(\bar q = 0\), \(Q = 0\), and with the excess over the surroundings \(\theta = T - T_\infty\), which writes equation (14.49) without a right-hand side, \(kA\,\theta'' = hP\,\theta\), with \(\theta(0) = \theta_0\). With \(m^2 = hP/(kA)\) (equation (14.19)) and \(mL = 1\) the solution is \(\theta(x) = \theta_0\,\cosh m(L - x)/\cosh mL\) (theorem 14.22), which at the insulated end falls to \(1/\cosh 1 = 0{,}6481\) of its value at the base.

14.9.2 The body

We write the same for a body. Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) with surface \(S\) and outward normal \(\vek n\), contained in an open set \(D\).

We draw the body \(V\) as a rectangle cut by a plane. Heat is produced in it by the source \(Q\) per unit volume (dots), for example by a current through a conductor. The surface \(S\) is divided into parts that do not overlap, and on each part one condition holds, since at the same point we cannot prescribe both the temperature and the flux (COOK, PDF p. 472). The temperature on a face already fixes how much heat flows through it: for the fin we prescribe the temperature at the wall, and the heat through the base comes out of the solution (theorem 14.22, part 3) and cannot be chosen separately. On the left face the temperature \(\bar T\) is prescribed, this is the part \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\). On the right the flux \(\bar q\) per unit area flowing into the body is prescribed, the part \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\). Here we count the flux, as COOK does (PDF p. 472), positive into the body, while in equation (14.11) we counted it outward, so \(\bar q = -\bar q_{\mathrm{n}}\). By Fourier's law heat flows in the direction \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\), so through a surface with outward normal \(\vek n\) there enters per unit area \(-\vek q\cdot\vek n = k\,\nabla T\cdot\vek n = k\,\partial T/\partial n\) (where \(\partial T/\partial n\) is the derivative of the temperature in the direction of the normal), and the condition is \(k\,\partial T/\partial n = \bar q\). On the top face there is convection, the part \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\), where \(h\,(T_\infty - T)\) enters the body by Newton's law, so \(k\,\partial T/\partial n = h\,(T_\infty - T)\). The bottom face is insulated, which is the flux condition with \(\bar q = 0\), and belongs to \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\). The normal points out of the body.

Definition 14.48 Strong form of steady heat conduction (močna oblika stacionarnega prevajanja toplote)

Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes with surface \(S\), which is the union of parts \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) that overlap only along the edges of faces, and let \(D\) be an open set containing \(V\). The conductivity \(k > 0\) and the film coefficient \(h \ge 0\) are numbers, the source \(Q\) is a continuous function on \(V\), and \(\bar T\), \(\bar q\) and \(T_\infty\) are continuous functions on the corresponding parts of the surface. The strong form is the problem: find a function \(T\), twice continuously differentiable on \(D\), that satisfies

\[-k\,\nabla^2 T = Q \ \text{ in } V , \qquad T = \bar T \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{T}} , \qquad k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial n} = \bar q \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{q}} , \qquad k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial n} = h\,(T_\infty - T) \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{h}} . \tag{14.50}\]
\[-k\,T_{,\mathrm{ii}} = Q \ \text{ in } V , \qquad T = \bar T \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{T}} , \qquad k\,T_{,\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = \bar q \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{q}} , \qquad k\,T_{,\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = h\,(T_\infty - T) \ \text{ on } S_{\mathrm{h}} . \tag{14.50}\]

The Laplace operator \(\nabla^2\) is from definition 11.5.

Source: COOK §12.1, (12.1-8), (12.1-14) and (12.1-15) (PDF pp. 471-472), CM §8.3.1 (PDF p. 402). With theorem 14.8 the first equation is the steady case of the conduction equation at constant conductivity.

The first equation is the conduction equation (theorem 14.8) with \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\) and constant \(k\): \(0 = \nabla\cdot(k\,\nabla T) + Q = k\,\nabla^2 T + Q\). As idealizations, let us say what we assumed (section 14.2): the body is isotropic, \(k\) is constant over the range of temperatures that the problem covers (if \(k\) changes with temperature the problem is nonlinear, COOK, PDF p. 469), heat moves only by conduction and convection, radiation is outside the model (COOK, PDF pp. 476 to 478), and the body is at rest. For radiation let us say when this is justified and what it costs. COOK (12.3-4), PDF p. 477, writes radiation as a convection that would add on the face \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) a flux of the same form with the coefficient \(h_\mathrm{rad} = \psi\,\sigma\,(T_2^2 + T_1^2)\,(T_2 + T_1)\). Here \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are the absolute temperatures of the surface and the surroundings, \(\sigma = 5{,}670 \cdot 10^{-8}\) W/(m² K⁴) (COOK, PDF p. 469), and \(\psi\) contains the emissivities and the position of the surfaces, for blackwhite surfaces that see each other completely \(\psi = 1\) (COOK, PDF p. 476). With \(\psi = 1\) and both temperatures \(300\) K, \(h_\mathrm{rad} = 6{,}1\) W/(m² K), with a surface at \(600\) K it is \(23\) W/(m² K) (NumPy). A convective \(h\) lies between \(5\) and \(10^5\) W/(m² K) (COOK, PDF p. 469). In still air with \(h = 5\) W/(m² K) at room temperature the omitted radiation would be more than half of the heat given off, \(6{,}1/(5 + 6{,}1) = 55\ \%\), with forced flow at \(h = 100\) W/(m² K) it would be \(6\ \%\). So we may omit radiation at a large \(h\) and moderate temperatures, but not for a hot body in still air. With it the problem would become nonlinear, since \(h_\mathrm{rad}\) depends on the temperature (COOK, PDF p. 477). The requirement "twice continuously differentiable on \(D\)" is a simplification: a body made of boxes has corners on its surface, where the solution is in general not so smooth. What the general theory does about it is said in section 6.2.3, here we stay with smooth solutions.

14.9.3 The weak form

The strong form asks for the second derivative of the temperature at every point. The trial fields of finite elements are piecewise linear, the slope jumps at a node and the second derivative does not exist there, so they do not have one. How do we get rid of it? We multiply the conduction equation by a test function \(w\) and integrate over the body, as for the bar in chapter 6, and move one derivative onto \(w\). For the bar integration by parts moves it, for a body the divergence theorem does. The move also gives boundary terms, and these themselves tell which conditions are essential and which natural.

Definition 14.49 Weak form of steady heat conduction (šibka oblika stacionarnega prevajanja toplote)

Let the body, the conductivity, the film coefficient and the data be as in definition 14.48. An admissible field is a function \(T\), continuously differentiable on \(D\), with \(T = \bar T\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\). A test function is a function \(w\), continuously differentiable on \(D\), with \(w = 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\). The test functions form a vector space \(W\). For functions \(T\) and \(w\), continuously differentiable on \(D\), let

\[B(T, w) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,T\,w\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{14.51}\]
\[\ell(w) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{q}} \bar q\,w\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,T_\infty\,w\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{14.52}\]

The weak form is the problem: find an admissible field \(T\) such that

\[B(T, w) = \ell(w) \qquad \text{for every test function } w \in W . \tag{14.53}\]

The form \(B\) is bilinear because the derivative and the integral are linear, and symmetric because \(\nabla T\cdot\nabla w = \nabla w\cdot\nabla T\) and \(h\,T\,w = h\,w\,T\), and \(\ell\) is linear (definitions 6.4 and 6.5). So this is a weak form in the sense of definition 6.6. For the bar of section 14.9.1 it is

\[B(T, w) = \int_0^L \big( kA\,T'\,w' + hP\,T\,w \big)\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \ell(w) = \int_0^L \big( A\,Q + hP\,T_\infty \big)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + A\,\bar q\,w(L) , \tag{14.54}\]

with admissible fields \(T(0) = T_0\) and test functions with \(w(0) = 0\).

Source: COOK §12.2, (12.2-1) and §5.1 (PDF pp. 473, 194). The notation with the forms \(B\) and \(\ell\) is as in definition 6.6.

Let us see which condition sits where in the weak form. The temperature on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is in the set of admissible fields, so it is an essential condition, like a displacement at a support, and therefore the test function \(w\) is zero there, as the curve below the body shows. The flux \(\bar q\) and the convection are in the forms \(B\) and \(\ell\) as terms over \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\), so they are natural conditions. For the fin \(\ell\) contains the term \(A\,\bar q\,w(L)\) and the value \(T_0\) is in the set of admissible fields. The pattern is the same as for the bar (section 6.1.3), except that the displacement is replaced by the temperature and the axial force by the heat flux.

We test the definition on the fin of section 14.9.1, in units \(kA = 1\) and \(L = 1\), so \(hP = m^2 = 1\) (since \(mL = 1\)), \(\bar q = 0\) and \(\theta(0) = \theta_0 = 1\). The form is \(B(\theta, w) = \int_0^1 (\theta'\,w' + \theta\,w)\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(\ell = 0\), and \(\theta = \cosh(1 - x)/\cosh 1\). The test function \(w = x\) is zero at \(x = 0\) and so it is admissible.

In the left part of the figure the derivative \(\theta' = -\sinh(1 - x)/\cosh 1\) is always negative and its area is \(\int_0^1 \theta'\,\mathrm{d}x = \theta(1) - \theta(0) = 0{,}6481 - 1 = -0{,}3519\). The product \(x\,\theta\) is positive and its area is \(\int_0^1 x\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}3519\) (SymPy). The sum is zero, as it must be, and the same holds for \(w = \sin(\pi x/2)\) (zero at \(x = 0\), SymPy). The weak form holds.

Now let us choose \(w = 1\), which is not zero at the prescribed temperature and therefore is not a test function. Its derivative is \(w' = 0\), so in the right part of the figure the product \(\theta'\,w'\) is zero and only the area under \(\theta\) remains, \(B(\theta, 1) = \int_0^1 \theta\,\mathrm{d}x = \tanh 1 = 0{,}7616\), which is not zero and differs from \(\ell(1) = 0\). The weak form does not hold for this \(w\). The reason is in a step that the theorem below carries out: integration by parts leaves a boundary term \(-kA\,\theta'(0)\,w(0)\), and it is zero only when \(w(0) = 0\). For \(w = 1\) it equals \(-\theta'(0) = \tanh 1\) and it is exactly the heat that flows through the base (section 14.4, \(mL\tanh mL\) in units \(kA\,\theta_0/L\), with \(mL = 1\): \(0{,}7616\)). A prescribed temperature therefore requires \(w = 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), and it takes along the heat that the weak form computes separately.

The balance of the whole fin gives the same number. The area under \(\theta\) is, in units \(hP = 1\), the heat that leaves through the side surface, and in the steady state it equals the heat that enters through the base, \(0{,}7616\). The weak form with \(w = 1\) is therefore the balance of the whole fin with the inflow through the base missing.

This view holds for every piece of the fin. Let us take for \(w\) a weight that equals \(1\) on the piece \([a, b]\), is zero outside the slightly wider interval \([a - \varepsilon, b + \varepsilon]\), and rises and falls smoothly on ramps of width \(\varepsilon\), so that it is continuously differentiable. On the ramp at \(a\) we have \(w' \ge 0\) and its integral is \(1\), since \(w\) rises from \(0\) to \(1\). The term \(-\int kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) over this ramp is therefore an average of the axial heat \(-kA\,T'\) on the ramp, weighted by \(w'\). As \(\varepsilon\) goes to zero, this average goes to the heat that enters the piece at \(a\). The ramp at \(b\) gives in the same way the heat that leaves at \(b\), and the terms with \(hP\) and \(A\,Q\) go to integrals over \([a, b]\). In the limit the equation \(B(T, w) = \ell(w)\) becomes

\[-kA\,T'(a) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big( hP\,(T_\infty - T) + A\,Q \big)\,\mathrm{d}x = -kA\,T'(b) ,\]

which reads: what enters at \(a\), along the side and from the source leaves at \(b\). This is the balance (14.48). The weak form is therefore an energy balance in which the piece has no sharp boundaries and every part of the bar counts with the weight \(w\). This also says why the weak form needs only one derivative: the balance of a piece needs only the heat at its boundaries, so \(T'\), not \(T''\). In a body it is the same, with a region in place of the piece and the flux through its surface.

The theorem below says that the two forms are equivalent. The proof of the converse uses test functions that are nonzero only on a small part of the bar from \(0\) to \(L\) or of the body \(V\).

Theorem 14.50 Strong and weak form of heat conduction (močna in šibka oblika prevajanja toplote)

Let the body, the data and the forms be as in definitions 14.48 and 14.49.

  1. Bar: let \(T\) be twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and \(A\,Q\) continuous. The function \(T\) is a solution of the strong form, equation (14.49) with \(T(0) = T_0\) and \(k\,T'(L) = \bar q\), exactly when it is an admissible field and a solution of the weak form with the forms (14.54).
  2. Body: if \(T\) is a solution of the strong form (definition 14.48), it is a solution of the weak form (definition 14.49). Conversely, if \(T\) is twice continuously differentiable on \(D\) and a solution of the weak form, it satisfies the first equation at every interior point of the body, and the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) at every point that has a neighbourhood in \(S\) inside that part, and by continuity on the whole part. The condition on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is in the admissibility.
Proof

We must show four things: that the weak form follows from the strong form in the bar and in the body, and that the equation and the boundary conditions follow from the weak form in both cases. The bar is treated with integration by parts, the body with the divergence theorem, which is the same step in three dimensions. For the converse in the body we use a test function that is nonzero only in a small ball.

  1. Bar, strong to weak. Let \(w\) be a test function, \(w(0) = 0\). We multiply equation (14.49), written as \((kA\,T')' - hP\,T + hP\,T_\infty + A\,Q = 0\), by \(w\) and integrate over \([0, L]\), the whole bar. The functions are continuous, so the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals (lemma 3.54).
  2. By integration by parts (corollary 3.56, with \(kA\,T'\) in place of \(f\), which is continuously differentiable, and \(w\) in place of \(g\)) we have \(\int_0^L (kA\,T')'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = kA\,T'(L)\,w(L) - kA\,T'(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\). Here \(w(0) = 0\) at the wall and \(kA\,T'(L) = A\,\bar q\) at the end, so the right-hand side is \(A\,\bar q\,w(L) - \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. We insert step 2 into step 1 and move the terms \(\int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\int_0^L hP\,T\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\) to the left: this gives equation (14.53) with the forms (14.54). The field \(T\) is admissible, since \(T(0) = T_0\) at the wall.
  4. Bar, weak to strong. Let \(T\) be a solution of the weak form. By step 2 read backwards, for every test function \(B(T, w) - \ell(w) = \big(kA\,T'(L) - A\,\bar q\big)\,w(L) - \int_0^L R\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), where \(R = (kA\,T')' - hP\,T + hP\,T_\infty + A\,Q\) is a continuous function. Let \(R(x_0) > 0\) at some interior point \(x_0\). By continuity there is \(\delta > 0\) with \(R > R(x_0)/2\) on \([a, b] = [x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta] \subset (0, L)\). The function \(w = (x - a)^2\,(b - x)^2\) on \([a, b]\) and zero elsewhere is continuously differentiable (its derivative is zero at \(a\) and \(b\) and continuous), zero at \(x = 0\), with \(w(L) = 0\), and positive inside \([a, b]\). Since \(w\) is zero outside \([a, b]\), \(\int_0^L R\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b R\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\), the area under the product \(R\,w\). On \([a, b]\) we have \(R > R(x_0)/2\) and \(w \ge 0\), so the curve \(R\,w\) lies above the curve \(\tfrac12 R(x_0)\,w\) and \(\int_0^L R\,w\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac12 R(x_0)\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b w\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\) (lemma 3.54, monotonicity), a contradiction, because this integral is zero (since \(w(L) = 0\) it is all that remains in the equation). The same for \(R(x_0) < 0\) with \(-R\): both areas then lie below the axis and \(\int_0^L R\,w\,\mathrm{d}x \le \tfrac12 R(x_0)\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b w\,\mathrm{d}x < 0\). So \(R = 0\) in the interior, by continuity everywhere, and this is equation (14.49).
  5. With \(R = 0\) what remains is \(\big(kA\,T'(L) - A\,\bar q\big)\,w(L) = 0\) for every test function. We choose \(w = x\), which is zero at \(0\) and has \(w(L) = L \ne 0\). We get \(k\,T'(L) = \bar q\), the condition at the end. The condition \(T(0) = T_0\) is in the admissibility. This is part 1.
  6. Body, strong to weak. Let \(w\) be a test function. The field \(\vek v = w\,k\,\nabla T\) is continuously differentiable on \(D\) (a product of continuously differentiable functions, \(T\) is twice continuously differentiable), so by the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \nabla\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek v\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\), with the outward normal \(\vek n\). By the product rule \(\nabla\cdot\vek v = k\,\nabla w\cdot\nabla T + w\,k\,\nabla^2 T\), and \(\vek v\cdot\vek n = w\,k\,\partial T/\partial n\). Hence \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} w\,k\,\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial n}\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,k\,\nabla^2 T\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  7. The first equation of (14.50) gives \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,k\,\nabla^2 T\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,w\,\mathrm{d}V\). On \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) we have \(w = 0\) and the term drops out, on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) we have \(k\,\partial T/\partial n = \bar q\), on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) we have \(k\,\partial T/\partial n = h\,T_\infty - h\,T\). Inserting everything into step 6 we get \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,T\,w\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{q}} \bar q\,w\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,T_\infty\,w\,\mathrm{d}A\), which is equation (14.53).
  8. Body, weak to strong. Steps 6 and 7 read backwards, for a solution of the weak form, give for every test function \(B(T, w) - \ell(w) = -\int_{\mathrm{V}} R\,w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{q}} (k\,\partial T/\partial n - \bar q)\,w\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} (k\,\partial T/\partial n - h\,T_\infty + h\,T)\,w\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), where \(R = k\,\nabla^2 T + Q\). Let \(R(P) \ne 0\) at an interior point \(P\) of the body. For a small enough radius \(\rho\) the ball \(|X - P| < \rho\) lies in the interior of the body and \(R\) has the same sign in it. The function \(w(X) = (\rho^2 - |X - P|^2)^2\) in the ball and zero outside is continuously differentiable (the derivative \(-4\,(\rho^2 - |X - P|^2)\,(X - P)\) is zero on the surface of the ball), zero on all faces (the ball does not reach them) and positive inside. Then \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} R\,w\,\mathrm{d}V \ne 0\) for the same reason as in step 4, a contradiction. So \(R = 0\) in the interior, by continuity everywhere, and this is the first equation.
  9. With \(R = 0\) the surface terms remain. Let \(P_{\mathrm{q}}\) be a point of \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\) that has a neighbourhood in \(S\) inside \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\), and \(g = k\,\partial T/\partial n - \bar q\), continuous on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\), with \(g(P_{\mathrm{q}}) \ne 0\). For small enough \(\rho\) the ball \(|X - P_{\mathrm{q}}| < \rho\) meets the surface \(S\) only in this neighbourhood, so the function \(w\) of step 8, centred at \(P_{\mathrm{q}}\), is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\), hence a test function, and the term over \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) is zero. What remains is \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{q}} g\,w\,\mathrm{d}A\). For small enough \(\rho\), \(g\) has the sign of \(g(P_{\mathrm{q}})\) on the part of the surface inside the ball, and \(w\) is positive there except on the boundary of the ball. So \(g\,w\) has the sign of \(g(P_{\mathrm{q}})\) everywhere on this part and is not zero over a part of the surface of positive area, and the integral is not zero. This is a contradiction, so \(g(P_{\mathrm{q}}) = 0\) at every such point and, by continuity, everywhere on \(S_{\mathrm{q}}\). The same argument on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) gives \(k\,\partial T/\partial n = h\,(T_\infty - T)\). This is part 2.

Source: COOK §12.2 (PDF p. 473) and §4.7 (PDF p. 165), EP §6.3.6.2 for the relation of the strong and weak form (pp. 323-327). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

What did the proof show? The two forms give the same temperature when it is smooth enough, and the weak one asks for one derivative less. Steps 2 and 6 showed what the conditions are: the boundary term that integration by parts brings is the heat flux times \(w\), like \(A\,\bar q\,w(L)\) at the end of the fin, so the flux is built into the weak form and the temperature is not. For the converse in the body we assumed that \(T\) is twice continuously differentiable. For a piecewise linear finite element field in the plane or in space the integral over the body splits into a sum over pieces (corollary 4.40) and steps 6 and 7 gain terms on the faces between the pieces, which we treat in chapter 15. For the bar we did this completely already in chapter 6 (lemma 6.13).

14.9.4 Positivity and the functional

The weak form is easy to write as an equation, but the solution need not be unique. The Ritz method (definition 6.32) and theorem 6.34 require \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every \(w \ne 0\). For displacements it is twice the strain energy (lemma 6.7). For temperature \(B(w, w) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} h\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), a sum of two nonnegative terms. When is it zero? The proof of the lemma below takes place in the body \(V\), the proof of the next lemma on the graph of the difference of the functional.

Lemma 14.51 Positivity of the conduction form (pozitivnost forme prevajanja toplote)

Let the interior of the body \(V\) be a connected open set (definition 1.136), \(h > 0\) constant on \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\), and let \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) or \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) have positive area. Then \(B(w, w) \ge 0\) for every test function \(w\), and \(B(w, w) = 0\) only for \(w = 0\).

Proof

We must show that a zero sum of two nonnegative terms means a zero field. First the gradient, then the constant.

  1. The first integrand \(k\,|\nabla w|^2\), over the body, is continuous and nonnegative, and so is the second, \(h\,w^2\), over the surface \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\). Hence both integrals are nonnegative (lemma 4.32, part 2), the lower bar and the upper bar stand above zero, and their sum is \(B(w, w) \ge 0\).
  2. Let \(B(w, w) = 0\). Both nonnegative terms are zero, since their sum is zero: bars that stand above zero and have total height zero both have height zero. If \(|\nabla w|^2 > 0\) at some interior point, by continuity it would be positive and larger than some positive bound in a neighbourhood of that point, and the integral over the neighbourhood would be positive (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 6.7), a contradiction. So \(\nabla w = \vek 0\) at every interior point.
  3. Let \(P\) and \(P'\) be interior points and \(P = A_0, \dots, A_{\mathrm{m}} = P'\) a broken line in the interior (definition 1.136). It is broken because the straight segment between \(P\) and \(P'\) may leave the body. On every segment \(A_{\mathrm{j}-1}A_{\mathrm{j}}\) the function \(t \mapsto w(A_{\mathrm{j}-1} + t\,\overrightarrow{A_{\mathrm{j}-1}A_{\mathrm{j}}})\) is differentiable with derivative \(\nabla w\cdot\overrightarrow{A_{\mathrm{j}-1}A_{\mathrm{j}}} = 0\), so by corollary 1.86 it is constant. The values on consecutive segments agree at \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(w(P) = w(P')\). The field \(w\) is therefore constant, \(w = c\), in the interior, and by continuity on all of \(V\).
  4. If the part of \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) where \(w = 0\) has positive area, then \(c = 0\), since by continuity \(w = c\) on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) as well. If \(S_{\mathrm{h}}\) has positive area, the second term is \(h\,c^2\) times this area, which is zero only for \(c = 0\) (and \(h > 0\)). In both cases \(w = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. For displacements it is lemma 6.7, here is the scalar version.

The connectedness assumption is needed. If the body consists of two separate pieces, \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) touches only the first and the second has no convection, then the function \(w\) that is zero on the first piece and \(1\) on the second piece is a test function with \(\nabla w = \vek 0\) everywhere. Then \(B(w, w) = 0\) with \(w \ne 0\), and the temperature of the second piece is not determined, as in the problem with fluxes only in section 14.9.5.

The lemma gives what we need. The Ritz solution exists and is unique (theorem 6.34 and its corollary on the matrix \(\mat K\)), and theorem 6.44 says that the Ritz and Galerkin methods are the same, because \(B\) is symmetric. It says something more: the solution of the weak form is the lowest point of a functional.

Lemma 14.52 Functional of steady conduction (funkcional stacionarnega prevajanja)

Let the assumptions of lemma 14.51 hold. For an admissible field \(v\) let

\[\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v) - \ell(v) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \Big( \tfrac12\,k\,|\nabla v|^2 - Q\,v \Big)\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{h}} \Big( \tfrac12\,h\,v^2 - h\,T_\infty\,v \Big)\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{q}} \bar q\,v\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{14.55}\]

Then \(T\) is a solution of the weak form exactly when \(\Pi(T) \le \Pi(v)\) for every admissible field \(v\). For the solution \(\Pi(v) - \Pi(T) = \tfrac12\,B(v - T, v - T)\), with equality at \(v = T\).

Proof

We must show the difference of the functionals and the converse. The first comes from bilinearity, the second from the quadratic term.

  1. Let \(T\) be a solution of the weak form and \(v\) an admissible field. The difference \(w = v - T\) is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) and is a test function. By theorem 6.34, part 1, \(\Pi(v) - \Pi(T) = \tfrac12\,B(w, w) \ge 0\), with equality only for \(w = 0\) (lemma 14.51).
  2. Converse. Let \(T\) be an admissible field with \(\Pi(T) \le \Pi(v)\) for all admissible fields \(v\). For a test function \(w\) and a number \(\alpha\) the field \(T + \alpha w\) is admissible, so \(\Pi(T + \alpha w) - \Pi(T) = \alpha\,\big( B(T, w) - \ell(w) \big) + \tfrac12\,\alpha^2\,B(w, w) \ge 0\) (from bilinearity and symmetry, as in section 6.1.6). As a function of the number \(\alpha\) this is a parabola through the origin with slope \(B(T, w) - \ell(w)\) at \(\alpha = 0\).
  3. Let \(w \ne 0\) and \(r = B(T, w) - \ell(w)\). We choose \(\alpha = -r/B(w, w)\), the lowest point of the parabola, which is possible since \(B(w, w) > 0\) (lemma 14.51). Then \(\Pi(T + \alpha w) - \Pi(T) = -r^2/B(w, w) + \tfrac12\,r^2/B(w, w) = -\tfrac12\,r^2/B(w, w)\), which is negative if \(r \ne 0\), and this contradicts minimality. The lowest point is at the origin only when \(r = 0\). So \(r = 0\) for every \(w \ne 0\), and for \(w = 0\) the claim is trivial.

Source: COOK §12.2, (12.2-1) (PDF p. 473) has the same functional with all terms, for the flux \(f_{\mathrm{B}}\) into the body and the film coefficient. The proof is not from the sources.

It is called a functional because we give it a whole field \(v\) as input and get one number, in the figure the height of the parabola at a chosen \(\alpha\). How large is that number? The unit of \(\tfrac12 k\,|\nabla v|^2\) is W/(m K) · K²/m² = W K/m³, so \(\Pi\) is in W K. It is not the energy of any quantity stored in the body, and COOK says for nonstructural problems that a functional may have no clear physical meaning (PDF p. 193). It is enough that it is a number with its lowest value at the true temperature.

With the lemma we check a Ritz approximation on the fin, in units \(kA = 1\), \(L = 1\), \(mL = 1\), \(\theta(0) = 1\). The trial field with one term is \(\theta_1 = 1 + c\,x\), \(\psi_0 = 1\) and \(\psi_1 = x\) (zero at \(x = 0\)). The Ritz equation (definition 6.32) has \(K_{11} = B(x, x) = \int_0^1 (1 + x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 4/3\) and \(f_1 = \ell(x) - B(1, x) = 0 - \int_0^1 x\,\mathrm{d}x = -1/2\), so \(c = -3/8\) and \(\theta_1 = 1 - 3x/8\).

In the figure the horizontal axis carries the position \(x\) along the fin and the vertical one the excess temperature \(\theta\), starting at \(0{,}5\) so that the difference shows. The true temperature is a curve that flattens to slope zero at the insulated end, and the Ritz approximation is a straight line that satisfies only the condition at the base. At the end \(x = 1\) the approximation is \(0{,}625\) against the true \(0{,}6481\), \(3{,}6\ \%\) too low, and the difference is modest. The natural condition, slope zero, the line does not satisfy, since its slope is \(-0{,}375\). The weak form does not impose it on the trial fields: the true solution satisfies it (theorem 14.50, step 5), an approximation only as far as its family of trial fields allows.

By lemma 14.52, for the Ritz line \(\Pi(\theta_1) = \tfrac12\,B(\theta_1, \theta_1) = \tfrac12\int_0^1 (\theta_1'^2 + \theta_1^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}40625\), and the true solution has \(\Pi(\theta) = \tfrac12\,B(\theta, \theta) = 0{,}38080\) (SymPy), so \(\Pi(\theta_1) \ge \Pi(\theta)\) and the difference \(0{,}02545\) is \(\tfrac12\,B(\theta_1 - \theta, \theta_1 - \theta)\), the energy of the error, as theorem 6.34 states. What does this say about the heat through the base? Since \(\theta - 1\) is a test function, \(B(\theta, \theta - 1) = 0\), so \(B(\theta, \theta) = B(\theta, 1)\) and \(\Pi(\theta) = \tfrac12 B(\theta, 1) = \tfrac12\tanh 1\), half the heat through the base. For the Ritz field the same holds, \(B(\theta_1, \theta_1) = B(\theta_1, 1)\). We can get the heat through the base from a field in two ways, by the balance of the whole fin, \(B(\cdot, 1)\), the area under the curve, or by the slope at the base:

true \(\theta\) Ritz \(\theta_1\)
balance, \(B(\cdot, 1)\) \(0{,}7616\) \(0{,}8125\)
slope at the base, \(-\theta'(0)\) \(0{,}7616\) \(0{,}375\)

For the true temperature the two ways agree, the tangent of the true curve at the base has slope \(-0{,}7616\). For the Ritz field the balance gives \(0{,}8125\), more than the true value, and this is guaranteed, since \(\Pi(\theta_1) = \tfrac12 B(\theta_1, 1) \ge \Pi(\theta) = \tfrac12 B(\theta, 1)\). The slope of the line gives \(0{,}375\), less than half the true value. In this example the balance is much closer, and for finite elements, which we treat later in the chapter, this finding tells us on which side we err in the heat through the base.

14.9.5 Heat fluxes only

What if we prescribe only fluxes, so \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is empty and \(h = 0\)? For displacements this is a body with no supports, which cannot be held (chapter 11, part 2 of theorem 11.4). For heat it is similar, and the condition here is the heat balance, what flows in must flow out.

Lemma 14.53 Problem with heat fluxes only (naloga s samimi toplotnimi tokovi)

Let \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) be empty and \(h = 0\) everywhere, so that only the source \(Q\) and the flux \(\bar q\) on \(S = S_{\mathrm{q}}\) are given (definition 14.49). Then:

  1. \(B(w, w) = 0\) for \(w = 1\) (a shift of the polyline upwards), so lemma 14.51 does not hold.
  2. The weak form has a solution only if \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), that is, if the heat that is produced in the body and flows into it also flows out.
  3. If \(T\) is a solution, so is \(T + c\) for every number \(c\). For every admissible field \(v\) and every number \(c\), \(\Pi(v + c) = \Pi(v) - c\,\big(\int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q\,\mathrm{d}A\big)\).
Proof

We must show that a constant is in the kernel of the form, and then derive the balance from its effect on both sides of the equation.

  1. The function \(w = 1\) is continuously differentiable and, since \(S_{\mathrm{T}}\) is empty, a test function. Its gradient is \(\vek 0\) and \(h = 0\), so \(B(1, 1) = 0\) and \(w \ne 0\).
  2. Let \(T\) be a solution. We insert \(w = 1\) into the weak form: \(B(T, 1) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} k\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla 1\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\) and \(\ell(1) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} Q\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}} \bar q\,\mathrm{d}A\). The equality gives claim 2: what flows in must also flow out.
  3. The gradient of \(T + c\) is the gradient of \(T\) and \(h = 0\), so \(B(T + c, w) = B(T, w)\) for every \(w\), and \(T + c\) is a solution, as the family of broken lines shows. For the same reason \(B(v + c, v + c) = B(v, v)\) for an admissible field \(v\), and \(\ell(v + c) = \ell(v) + c\,\ell(1)\), so \(\Pi(v + c) = \Pi(v) - c\,\ell(1)\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. COOK, PDF p. 474 stresses that temperature and flux cannot be prescribed at the same place.

Let us look at this on a bar of two linear elements, in units \(kA = 1\) and \(L = 1\). The nodes are at \(0\), \(1/2\) and \(1\). The element matrix is the matrix of a bar element from lemma 7.5 with \(kA\) in place of \(EA\) (we justify this later in the chapter), an element of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = 1/2\) has \(kA/h_{\mathrm{e}} = 2\), and the assembled matrix is \(\mat K = 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(0\), \(2\) and \(6\) (NumPy) and determinant zero. In the figure, in its left part, heat \(1\) enters the left end of the bar and \(1\) leaves the right one (the arrows), \(\vek f = (1, 0, -1)\). The equations \(\mat K\,\vek T = \vek f\) determine the differences \(T_0 - T_1 = 0{,}5\) and \(T_1 - T_2 = 0{,}5\), but not the sum: a family of broken lines shifted upwards is an equally good solution, as claim 3 says. This is not an error of the computation but the fact that the flux between two parts depends only on the temperature differences: by Fourier's law the flux is proportional to the slope, and shifting the broken line upwards does not change the slope. But if \(1\) enters the left end and nothing leaves the right one, \(\vek f = (1, 0, 0)\), the sum of the rows of the matrix is zero and the sum of the right-hand side is \(1\), and the system has no solution (the smallest residual is \(0{,}577\), NumPy). In a body the temperature would keep rising, since heat flows in and does not flow out and the stored heat grows (the balance of section 14.2), and that is an unsteady problem. In the middle part of the figure heat \(1\) enters the bar without leaving (the arrow), and the functional along the constant field is \(\Pi(c) = -c\), a straight line without a lowest point, as claim 3 says.

What mends this? We prescribe a temperature somewhere, or add convection. With convection \(hA = 1\) at both ends the matrix is \(\mat K + \mat H\) with \(\mat H = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0, 1)\), the determinant is \(12\) and the smallest eigenvalue \(0{,}628\) (NumPy). For \(\vek f = (1, 0, -1)\) there is one solution only, \(T_\infty + 1/3\), \(T_\infty\) and \(T_\infty - 1/3\). The balance confirms it: of the heat \(1\) that enters the left end, \(hA \cdot 1/3 = 1/3\) leaves by convection and \(2/3\) flows along the bar, which is the difference \(1/3\) times \(kA/h_{\mathrm{e}} = 2\). At the right end \(1/3\) comes in from the surroundings and \(1\) leaves in all. The matrix is positive definite, as lemma 14.51 requires with \(h > 0\) on a part of the surface. Prescribed fluxes alone are therefore not enough, just as for displacements forces alone are not enough when the supports do not hold the body and \(B(w, w) > 0\) of theorem 6.34 fails (section 6.4.4). A prescribed temperature or convection must anchor the temperature.

14.9.6 What we have gained

Steady conduction has a weak form of the same structure as the bar, the beam and the body (theorem 14.50, definition 14.49). The temperature and the heat flux are divided by essential and natural conditions like the displacement and the force. Because the form \(B\) is positive (lemma 14.51), the problem has a functional that the solution minimizes (lemma 14.52), the Ritz and Galerkin methods are the same (theorem 6.44), and theorem 6.34 holds with all its consequences. Without a prescribed temperature or convection what happens is what happens for displacements without supports: the solution is not unique, or does not exist at all (lemma 14.53). We tested what we showed on the fin, where the Ritz approximation is \(0{,}625\) against the true \(0{,}6481\) at the tip. The next task is to write the same by elements, which is the same procedure as for the bar of chapter 7, and chapter 15 continues in the plane and in space.
