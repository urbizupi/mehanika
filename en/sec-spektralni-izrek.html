Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.3 Mathematics: the spectral theorem

In section 10.2 we obtained, for frame A, two natural frequencies and two modes from a determinant of order 2, and vibration in one mode was a motion in which all coordinates move in step (lemma 10.14). For a system with 16 coordinates, such as the bar made of finite elements will be, we have no determinant of order 16, since the book has determinants only up to order 3 (definition 2.72). Even if we had one, we would obtain an equation of degree 16 for \(\lambda\), and we would not know whether it has 16 real solutions. So four questions arise for a pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(n\).

  1. Does the pair always have \(n\) eigenpairs (definition 10.13)?
  2. Are the eigenvalues real, even if we admit complex numbers? A complex eigenvalue would give a motion in which vibration is mixed with growth or decay.
  3. How are the modes related to each other?
  4. Is every column a sum of modes, so that every motion can be built from them?

The answer is theorem 10.21 of this section. We prove it without determinants, with the smallest value, along the same path by which we proved theorem 2.83 for a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with the largest value in chapter 2.

10.3.1 Idea: successive minima

For the principal directions we looked for the unit vector at which \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) is largest and showed that it is an eigenvector. We do the same here, with three changes. First: instead of the unit sphere \(\vek v^\T\vek v = 1\) we take the set of columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\). At velocities \(\dot{\vek q}\) the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.97), so all columns of this set, read as velocities, have the same kinetic energy. By lemma 10.14 the eigenvalue is the quotient \(\lambda = \vek\varphi^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi/\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi\), the ratio of twice the potential energy at displacements \(\vek\varphi\) to twice the kinetic energy at velocities \(\vek\varphi\). So we expect the lowest frequency to belong to the shape that strains the structure least at a given kinetic energy, and we look for the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) on this set. Second change: we look for the smallest, not the largest value, because the lowest frequency interests us first. Third change: we cannot get the third direction from a cross product, which we do not have for more coordinates, so we will need another argument, lemma 10.16. All three changes can be seen in figure 10.30: the set \(\vek y^\T\mat M\,\vek y = 1\) and the curve of the smallest value that touches it.

Let us look at the set and its smallest value for frame A. In the coordinates \((q_1, q_2)\) of section 10.1 we have \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and the set \(\vek q^\T\mat M\,\vek q = 1\) is a circle. The circle comes from the equal masses of the two floors and from this choice of coordinates, not from a general rule. So we take coordinates in which neither matrix is diagonal: the displacement of the upper floor \(y_1 = q_2\) and the drift of the upper storey \(y_2 = q_2 - q_1\). From \(\vek q = \mat P\,\vek y\) with \(\mat P = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) we get \(\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q = \vek y^\T(\mat P^\T\mat K\mat P)\,\vek y\) (lemma 1.102), hence

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat P^\T\mat K\mat P = k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat P^\T\mat M\mat P = m\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{10.32}\]

with \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(m = 500\) kg. The set \(\vek y^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = 1\) is an ellipse (figure 10.30). The curves of constant value \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = c\) are ellipses about the origin that grow as \(c\) increases. The smallest value of \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y\) on the ellipse is reached by the smallest ellipse of this family that still reaches the ellipse (it touches it from inside), and the largest by the largest one that still reaches it (it touches it from outside). The two touchings occur at \(c = 383{,}311\) 1/s\(^2\) (blue) and \(c = 2627{,}249\) 1/s\(^2\) (orange), which are exactly the eigenvalues of section 10.2. The points of contact are \(\pm\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\pm\vek\varphi_2\) with \(\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}038042,\ 0{,}014531)\) and \(\vek\varphi_2 = (-0{,}023511,\ -0{,}061554)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). At \(c = 900\) 1/s\(^2\) (grey dashed) the curve cuts the ellipse at four points. The smallest value is not reached there, because from an intersection point we can move along the ellipse to the side where \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y < 900\) 1/s\(^2\).

At the point of contact \(\vek\varphi_1\) must be an eigencolumn: if the two curves crossed at this point as the grey curve and the ellipse do, we could move along the ellipse to the side with a smaller value. This is the idea of section 2.11.2, here for a pair of matrices. The proof will make it precise and for every order \(n\).

The assumptions are as follows. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric with real entries, while \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) may have either sign, so that we also cover unstable equilibrium (\(\lambda < 0\)) as well as systems that the supports do not hold and mechanisms (\(\lambda = 0\)). The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive definite (definition 6.27), both of order \(n\), as in theorem 10.3. Columns have \(n\) real entries. Positive definiteness of \(\mat M\) means that the set \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) is an ellipse, a closed curve, and not a hyperbola reaching to infinity.

10.3.2 Tools: more unknowns than equations, and \(\mat M\)-orthogonality

In theorem 2.83 we obtained the third direction as the cross product of two perpendicular vectors. For any order \(n\) we need another statement: when we impose fewer conditions than there are unknowns, something nonzero still satisfies them.

Let us first see what this says in the plane and in space (figure 10.31). In the plane of the unknowns \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) the equation \(x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\) is a line through the origin, and the equation \(4x_1 + 5x_2 = 0\) is another such line. Two different lines through the origin share only the point \(O\), that is only the solution \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\). So with two equations in two unknowns a nonzero solution need not exist. With one more unknown each equation is a plane through the origin. Let us draw the plane of the columns perpendicular to a column \(\vek\varphi_1\) and the plane of the columns perpendicular to a column \(\vek\varphi_2\). The two columns are perpendicular to each other, we will meet them as two modes of the frame with three storeys. Two planes through the same point \(O\) cannot meet in that point alone, just as two walls of a room do not meet in one point but along an edge. They share a whole line, and every point of it except \(O\) is a nonzero solution of both equations. This is the statement for two equations and three unknowns. For more unknowns we have no picture, so we prove the statement by elimination. Its outcome is shown in part (a) of the figure for three equations and five unknowns: the matrix \(\mat U\) with three rows and five numbered columns, in it two pivots \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), zeros to the left of a pivot and below it, a row of zeros only and the remaining entries, which may be anything. The shaded columns \(2\), \(4\) and \(5\) have no pivot. Below the matrix is the solution \(\vek w\): we write \(1\) into one of the shaded columns and \(0\) into the other two, and we compute the unknowns in the pivot columns from the last one to the first.

Lemma 10.16 Homogeneous system with more unknowns than equations (homogeni sistem z več neznankami kot enačbami)

Let \(\mat A\) be a real matrix with \(k\) rows and \(n\) columns, \(n > k\). Then there is a column \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) entries for which \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\). In figure 10.31 (a) \(\vek w\) exists because of a column without a pivot, in which the unknown stays free.

Proof

We must show that the elimination of definition 8.21 leaves some column without a pivot, and that the unknown of this column can be set to \(1\). The idea comes from the proof of lemma 8.22.

  1. Gaussian elimination with row exchanges as in definition 8.21 finds \(r\) pivots, one in each of the rows \(1, \dots, r\), in the columns \(c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_{\mathrm{r}}\) (each pivot lies to the right of the previous one). In figure 10.31 (a) we have \(k = 3\), \(n = 5\) and \(r = 2\), and the pivots are in the columns \(c_1 = 1\) and \(c_2 = 3\). There are \(k\) rows, so \(r \le k\). Each step of the elimination, an exchange of two rows or the subtraction of a multiple of one row from another, gives a system with the same solutions (step 1 of the proof of lemma 8.22). We obtain a system \(\mat U\,\vek w = \vek 0\) with the same set of solutions as \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\).
  2. By definition 8.21 the pivot \(U_{\mathrm{i} \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}}\) in row \(i \le r\) is nonzero, and all entries of row \(i\) to its left are zero (the two zeros to the left of \(p_2\)). Indeed, column \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the first column to the right of \(c_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) that has a nonzero number in the rows from \(i\) on. The columns before it have zeros in these rows: the pivot columns because of the subtractions in earlier steps, the columns in between because of the choice of the pivot. Later steps do not spoil these zeros, because they exchange and subtract only rows that already have zeros in these columns. If \(r < k\), the rows \(r + 1, \dots, k\) are all zeros (the third row in the figure) for the same reason: the elimination stopped because none of these rows has a nonzero number in the columns to the right of \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) (for \(r = 0\) in all columns), and in the columns up to \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) they have zeros from earlier steps.
  3. There are \(r \le k < n\) pivots and \(n\) columns, so there is a column \(j\) that is not a pivot column (the shaded columns, we choose \(j = 4\)). We set \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) and \(w_{\mathrm{l}} = 0\) for every other column \(l\) without a pivot (the row below the matrix).
  4. We determine the unknowns in the pivot columns in the order \(i = r, r - 1, \dots, 1\) (back substitution). Row \(i\) contains only unknowns \(w_{\mathrm{l}}\) with \(l \ge c_{\mathrm{i}}\), so equation \(i\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{l} > \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{il}}\,w_{\mathrm{l}}/U_{\mathrm{i} \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}}\), where all \(w_{\mathrm{l}}\) with \(l > c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are already known: either they come from step 3, or they are unknowns \(w_{\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{i}'}}\) of rows \(i' > i\), determined earlier. In the figure the second row first gives \(w_3\), then the first row gives \(w_1\), and the coefficients \(U_{\mathrm{il}}\) are the stars to the right of the pivot.
  5. The rows \(1, \dots, r\) are thus satisfied, and the rows \(r + 1, \dots, k\) are all zeros and are satisfied for every \(\vek w\). Hence \(\mat U\,\vek w = \vek 0\), by step 1 also \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\), and \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) says \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\). In the figure these are the pivots and the zero row.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.5, Theorem 3 (PDF p. 317) states the claim. The proof is not from the source.

We test the statement on a system of two equations in three unknowns, \(x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\) and \(4x_1 + 5x_2 + 6x_3 = 0\). Subtracting four times the first row from the second gives \(-3x_2 - 6x_3 = 0\). The pivots are in columns \(1\) and \(2\), column \(3\) is free, so we set \(x_3 = 1\). The second row gives \(x_2 = -2\), the first \(x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_3 = 1\). The column \((1, -2, 1)\) indeed solves both equations: \(1 - 4 + 3 = 0\) and \(4 - 10 + 6 = 0\). Each of the equations is a plane through the origin, and the multiples of the column \((1, -2, 1)\) are their common line, like the line in which the two planes meet in figure 10.31 (c).

Without the third unknown the statement fails. Take \(x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\) and \(4x_1 + 5x_2 = 0\), where \(n = k = 2\). Subtracting four times the first row from the second gives \(-3x_2 = 0\), so \(x_2 = 0\), and then the first row gives \(x_1 = 0\). The only solution is the zero column, the point \(O\), the only common point of the two lines. Both columns have a pivot (\(1\) and \(-3\)), no unknown is free, so none can be set to \(1\). For \(n > k\) there are at most \(k < n\) pivots and a column without a pivot always remains, which the proof of the lemma uses.

For \(\mat M = \mat I\) we measured the perpendicularity of columns with the scalar product. For the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) it is replaced by the product with the matrix \(\mat M\), because \(\mat M\) determines the kinetic energy. The columns of equal length in this product form an ellipse, not a circle.

Definition 10.17 M-orthogonality and M-normalization (M-ortogonalnost in M-normiranost)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Real columns \(\vek\varphi\) and \(\vek\psi\) with \(n\) entries are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal (\(\mat M\)-ortogonalna), if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = 0\). A column \(\vek\varphi\) is \(\mat M\)-normalized (\(\mat M\)-normiran), if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 1\). A set of columns is \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (\(\mat M\)-ortonormirana), if all its columns are \(\mat M\)-normalized and any two different ones are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal, that is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) for \(r \ne s\). In frame A the columns \(\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\vek\varphi_2\) are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal, although they are not perpendicular in the figure.

Source: CK (10.20) and the text below it, "orthogonal with respect to the mass matrix" (PDF p. 307), CK (10.31) for the normalization (PDF p. 312).

The condition does not depend on the order of the two columns. For a symmetric matrix \(\mat A\) of order \(n\) and columns \(\vek v\), \(\vek w\) with \(n\) entries, \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w\) is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, which equals its transpose, so by lemma 1.102 (points 1 and 3)

\[\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w = (\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w)^\T = \vek w^\T\mat A^\T\vek v = \vek w^\T\mat A\,\vek v . \tag{10.33}\]

This is equation (2.79) of chapter 2 for any order \(n\). For \(\mat A = \mat M\) it says that \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = 0\) exactly when \(\vek\psi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 0\). For \(\mat M = \mat I\) this is ordinary perpendicularity, for frame A in the coordinates \(\vek q\) (\(\mat M = m\mat I\)) too, but for frame A in the coordinates \(\vek y\) it is not (figure 10.30). We check with the modes \(\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}038042,\ 0{,}014531)\) and \(\vek\varphi_2 = (-0{,}023511,\ -0{,}061554)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) of the figure. The ordinary product is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek\varphi_2 = -0{,}001789\) kg\(^{-1}\), which is not zero, so they are not perpendicular. The product with the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\) from (10.32), however, is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_2 = 1{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\), zero up to the rounding of the six-digit entries (for comparison \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_1 = 1{,}0000\)). In the coordinates \(\vek q\) the modes \((1;\ 1{,}618)\) and \((1;\ -0{,}618)\) are perpendicular, \(m\,(1 - 1{,}618 \cdot 0{,}618) = 0\) up to rounding. If we change the second column to \((1;\ -0{,}6)\), then \(m\,(1 - 1{,}618 \cdot 0{,}6) = 14{,}6\) kg, which is not zero, and there is no \(\mat M\)-orthogonality. The unit of the entries of an \(\mat M\)-normalized column is kg\(^{-1/2}\), because \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 1\) is a number without a unit. Meaning of the normalization: the velocities \(\dot{\vek q} = \sqrt{1\ \text{J}}\,\vek\varphi\) have kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} = \tfrac12\) J.

Where does ordinary perpendicularity hide in the ellipse? Let us look at the two modes once more, from two sides (figure 10.32). In the coordinates \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) the set \(\vek y^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = 1\) is an ellipse. Its longer semi-axis measures \(0{,}0724\) kg\(^{-1/2}\), as much as the radius of the circle that touches it from outside, and the shorter one \(0{,}0276\) kg\(^{-1/2}\), as much as the radius of the circle that touches it from inside. The mode \(\vek\varphi_1\) and the line of the mode \(\vek\varphi_2\) make an angle of \(48{,}2^\circ\), not \(90^\circ\). Now we change the coordinates so that the ellipse becomes a circle. We look for a matrix \(\mat C\) with \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat C\,\mat C^\T\). For frame A we read it from (10.32): since \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat P^\T(m\mat I)\,\mat P = m\,\mat P^\T\mat P\), the choice \(\mat C^\T = \sqrt m\,\mat P\) works, and the multiplication confirms it:

\[\mat C^\T = \sqrt m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat C\,\mat C^\T = m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = m\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mat M_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{10.34}\]

The new coordinates are \(\vek z = \mat C^\T\vek y\). For columns \(\vek\varphi\) and \(\vek\psi\), by lemma 1.102,

\[\vek\varphi^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\psi = \vek\varphi^\T\mat C\,\mat C^\T\vek\psi = (\mat C^\T\vek\varphi)^\T(\mat C^\T\vek\psi) , \tag{10.35}\]

so the product with the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the ordinary scalar product of the images \(\mat C^\T\vek\varphi\) and \(\mat C^\T\vek\psi\). For \(\vek\varphi = \vek\psi = \vek y\) this says \(\vek y^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = |\vek z|^2\), so in the coordinates \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) the same set is a circle of radius \(1\). The images of the modes are \(\mat C^\T\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}5257,\ 0{,}8507)\) and \(\mat C^\T\vek\varphi_2 = (0{,}8507,\ -0{,}5257)\). Both are unit columns and perpendicular to each other, since \(0{,}5257 \cdot 0{,}8507 - 0{,}8507 \cdot 0{,}5257 = 0\). So \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is ordinary perpendicularity in the coordinates in which the mass no longer distorts the picture. In these coordinates the two curves of constant value of \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y\) are an ellipse with its axes along the images of the two modes that touches the circle from inside and another such ellipse that touches it from outside. This is the picture of the principal directions of chapter 2: in the coordinates \(\vek z\) the matrix \(\mat M\) is the identity matrix and what remains is the eigenproblem of a single symmetric matrix. For frame A \(\vek z = \sqrt m\,\mat P\,\vek y = \sqrt m\,\vek q\), so the coordinates \(\vek z\) are the floor displacements multiplied by \(\sqrt m\). With different floor masses we would multiply each displacement by the square root of its own mass. For a general symmetric positive definite \(\mat M\) we find the factorization \(\mat M = \mat C\,\mat C^\T\) later in this chapter, where this change of coordinates is used to compute large problems. Here the change is only another view of the same picture, the proof of the theorem does not need it.

But why are the modes \(\mat M\)-orthogonal? For frame A we computed it. The reason is short and holds for every pair of symmetric matrices: we compute the number \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) in two ways and compare the results. Let us draw the two computations (figure 10.33). On the horizontal axis we put the number \(x = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), which we want to show is zero, and on the vertical axis the number \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). The first computation will give \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,x\), a line through the origin with slope \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), the second \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,x\), a line with slope \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\). Since both compute the same number, \(x\) must be where the two lines have the same height.

Lemma 10.18 Orthogonality of modes with different eigenvalues (ortogonalnost načinov z različnima lastnima vrednostma)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric matrices of order \(n\) with real entries. For real numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) and real columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(n\) entries let \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\). Then

\[(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{s}})\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 . \tag{10.36}\]

If \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\), then therefore \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). In figure 10.33 (a) the left side of equation (10.36) is the difference of the heights of the two lines at the same \(x\).

Proof

We must show equation (10.36) and the two consequences for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\). We compute the number \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) once with the equation of mode \(r\) and once with the equation of mode \(s\). The number is the height on the line \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,x\) and at the same time on the line \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,x\).

  1. We multiply the equation \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) from the left by the row \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\) (lemma 1.100, point 3): \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the line \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,x\).
  2. We multiply the equation \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) from the left by the row \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\): \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. Here symmetry enters. Since \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric, by (10.33) we may swap the columns: \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). The equation of step 2 is therefore \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), the line \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,x\).
  4. The left sides of the equations of steps 1 and 3 are the same number, so we subtract the equation of step 3 from the equation of step 1: \(0 = (\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{s}})\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). In the figure this is the difference of the heights of the two lines at the same \(x\), which must be zero.
  5. Here \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ne \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) enters. A product of two numbers is zero only if one of them is zero, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ne 0\), so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). Two lines with different slopes have the same height only at the origin. By step 1 then also \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \cdot 0 = 0\).

Source: CK (10.16) to (10.21) (PDF p. 307), the repeated natural frequency in §10.1.6 (PDF pp. 307 and 308).

The lemma showed that two modes with different natural frequencies are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal and at the same time \(\mat K\)-orthogonal, with just two multiplications and one subtraction. For frame A \(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 = 383{,}311 - 2627{,}249 = -2243{,}938\) 1/s\(^2\), which is not zero, so \(\vek\varphi_2^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_1 = 0\) must hold, as we computed above. Two assumptions carry the proof, each in one step. For different eigenvalues the lines have different slopes, so they have the same height only at the origin.

Symmetry enters in step 3. In vibration it holds because \(\mat K\) is the matrix of the second derivatives of the potential energy and \(\mat M\) the matrix of the kinetic energy (theorem 10.3, point 4). The theorem shows this for conservative forces, which have a potential \(V\), so for forces without a potential this reason is gone. Without symmetry the conclusion fails. Take the nonsymmetric matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) in place of \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M = \mat I\). Its eigenpairs are \(\lambda = 1\) with the column \((1;\ 0)\) and \(\lambda = 2\) with the column \((1;\ 1)\), since \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\). The eigenvalues are different, yet the product of the columns is \(1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1 = 1\), not zero. It is exactly step 3 that fails: the first column, multiplied by the matrix and the second column, gives \(2\), in the reverse order it gives \(1\), so the two lines no longer compute the same number.

The different eigenvalues enter in step 5. For \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) the two lines coincide (in the same axes as before, through the same origin), equation (10.36) reads \(0 \cdot x = 0\) and holds for every \(x\), so the lemma says nothing about \(x\). An example is two equal floors, each on its own pair of steel columns and with no connection between them: \(\mat K = k\mat I\), \(\mat M = m\mat I\) with \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(m = 500\) kg. Each floor vibrates on its own with the same \(\lambda = k/m = 1003{,}52\) 1/s\(^2\), so every nonzero column is an eigencolumn. The columns \((1;\ 0)\) and \((1;\ 1)\) are both eigencolumns with the same eigenvalue, but their product with \(\mat M\) is \(x = m\,(1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1) = 500\) kg, not zero. We can, however, choose the columns \((1;\ 1)\) and \((1;\ -1)\), for which \(m\,(1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1) = 0\). So for equal eigenvalues \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is not given by itself, but we reach it by the choice of the columns.

The lemma also does not say whether eigenpairs exist at all, or how many there are. That is why the theorem of this section builds the modes in another way, as successive smallest values: each new column is sought only among the columns that are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to those already found, so orthogonality is built into the construction and holds for equal eigenvalues as well. The lemma remains the shortest answer to the question why two modes with different frequencies are orthogonal.

How many nonzero pairwise \(\mat M\)-orthogonal columns can there be? For frame A we see it in the coordinates \(\vek z\) of figure 10.32 (b), where by equation (10.35) the product with the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the ordinary scalar product of the images \(\mat C^\T\vek\psi\). Let us draw it (figure 10.34). In the plane of the coordinates \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) we draw the unit circle. On it lie the images of the two modes, the arrow \(\vek a_1 = (0{,}5257;\ 0{,}8507)\) and the arrow \(\vek a_2 = (0{,}8507;\ -0{,}5257)\), which are perpendicular. We stretch the arrow \(\vek a_1\) by the factor \(c_1 = 1{,}3\) and the arrow \(\vek a_2\) by the factor \(c_2 = 1{,}2\) and obtain the sides \(c_1\vek a_1\) and \(c_2\vek a_2\) of a rectangle. Their sum is the diagonal \(c_1\vek a_1 + c_2\vek a_2\). We project the diagonal perpendicularly onto the direction of \(\vek a_1\) and obtain exactly the side \(c_1\vek a_1\) with length \(1{,}3\), because the other side stands perpendicular to \(\vek a_1\) and adds nothing to the projection. The scalar product with the unit arrow \(\vek a_1\) is this projection:

\[\vek a_1^\T(c_1\vek a_1 + c_2\vek a_2) = c_1\,\vek a_1^\T\vek a_1 + c_2\,\vek a_1^\T\vek a_2 = c_1 \cdot 1 + c_2 \cdot 0 = 1{,}3 .\]

If the diagonal were the zero arrow, its projection would be zero too, so \(c_1 = 0\), and the projection onto \(\vek a_2\) gives \(c_2 = 0\) in the same way. This is point 1 of the corollary below.

In part (b) we add a third arrow \(\vek a_3 = (1;\ 0{,}5)\) to the arrows \(\vek a_1\) and \(\vek a_2\). Its projections onto \(\vek a_1\) and \(\vek a_2\) are \(\vek a_1^\T\vek a_3 = 0{,}5257 + 0{,}8507 \cdot 0{,}5 = 0{,}951\) and \(\vek a_2^\T\vek a_3 = 0{,}8507 - 0{,}5257 \cdot 0{,}5 = 0{,}588\). The arrows \(0{,}951\,\vek a_1\), \(0{,}588\,\vek a_2\) and \(-\vek a_3\), laid one after another, return to the starting point, so \(0{,}951\,\vek a_1 + 0{,}588\,\vek a_2 - \vek a_3 = \vek 0\) with coefficients that are not all zero. Every three arrows \(\vek u_1\), \(\vek u_2\), \(\vek u_3\) in the plane have such a sum, since the equation \(c_1\vek u_1 + c_2\vek u_2 + c_3\vek u_3 = \vek 0\) is a system of two equations (one for each coordinate) in three unknowns, which by lemma 10.16 has a nonzero solution. By part (a), however, the sum of pairwise perpendicular nonzero arrows is not zero unless all coefficients are zero. So three nonzero arrows in the plane are never pairwise perpendicular. This is point 2. An arrow perpendicular to \(\vek a_1\) and to \(\vek a_2\) would have both projections zero, and since an arrow is the sum of its projections, like \(\vek a_3 = 0{,}951\,\vek a_1 + 0{,}588\,\vek a_2\), it would be the zero arrow. This is point 3.

Corollary 10.19 Number of nonzero M-orthogonal columns (število neničelnih M-ortogonalnih stolpcev)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}}\) nonzero, pairwise \(\mat M\)-orthogonal columns with \(n\) entries. For frame A (\(\mat M = \mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\)) the images \(\mat C^\T\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) of such columns are perpendicular arrows in the coordinates \(\vek z\) of figure 10.32 (b). Then:

  1. From \(c_1\vek\psi_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{p}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek 0\) it follows that \(c_1 = \dots = c_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\). In figure 10.34 (a) the sum of perpendicular arrows has the projection \(c_1\) onto the direction of \(\vek a_1\), so it is zero only for \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\).
  2. \(p \le n\). In the plane (\(n = 2\)) there are no three nonzero pairwise perpendicular arrows, since three arrows in the plane always have a zero sum with coefficients that are not all zero.
  3. If \(p = n\) and the column \(\vek w\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all of \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{n}}\), then \(\vek w = \vek 0\). In the plane the only column perpendicular to both arrows is the zero column.
Proof

We must show three points. The first is a computation with the product with \(\mat M\), the second and third follow from lemma 10.16.

  1. Let \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\). For each \(j\) we multiply the equation from the left by the row \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\) (lemma 1.100, point 3), and since \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(i \ne j\), there remains \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). Since \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} \ne \vek 0\) and \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\), hence \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). In the plane this is the projection of the sum onto the direction of the first arrow: only the product of an arrow with itself remains, the square of its length.
  2. Suppose \(p > n\). The matrix \(\mat A\) with the columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}}\) has \(n\) rows and \(p > n\) columns, so by lemma 10.16 there is \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) with \(\mat A\,\vek c = \vek 0\). By (1.39) entry \(l\) of the column \(\mat A\,\vek c\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} (\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{l}}\,c_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \(\mat A\,\vek c = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\). This contradicts point 1. The matrix \(\mat A\) has more columns than rows, so it has columns without a pivot. For \(n = 2\) and \(p = 3\) the column \(\vek c\) is like the coefficients \(0{,}951\), \(0{,}588\) and \(-1\) of the closed triangle.
  3. Let \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) be \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek w\) is a set of \(n + 1\) nonzero pairwise \(\mat M\)-orthogonal columns, which contradicts point 2. In the plane \(\vek w\) would be a third arrow perpendicular to both, and there is none.

The proof of point 1 uses both assumptions of the corollary, both in the same step. The columns must be nonzero, otherwise the coefficient is not determined: a zero arrow has projection zero for every coefficient, so for example \(0 \cdot \vek a_1 + 5 \cdot \vek 0 = \vek 0\) with the coefficient \(5\). The matrix \(\mat M\) must be positive definite, so that the product of a nonzero column with itself, in the coordinates \(\vek z\) the square of the length of the arrow, is positive. For the symmetric matrix \(\mat M = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\), which is not positive definite, the nonzero columns \((1;\ 1)\) and \((2;\ 2)\) have the product \(1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 2 = 0\), and yet \(2\,(1;\ 1) - (2;\ 2) = \vek 0\). Step 1 fails because also \((1;\ 1)^\T\mat M\,(1;\ 1) = 1 - 1 = 0\). In vibration the assumption holds by theorem 10.3 (point 4): the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}\) is positive for every velocity \(\dot{\vek q} \ne \vek 0\).

Lemma 10.20 The set of M-normalized columns (množica M-normiranih stolpcev)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek b_1, \dots, \vek b_{\mathrm{k}}\) arbitrary columns with \(n\) entries, \(k < n\). The set

\[S = \{\vek v :\ \vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1 ,\ \vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0 \text{ for } j = 1, \dots, k\}\]

is nonempty, closed and bounded. For \(k = 0\) there are no conditions \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0\). For \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) they are the \(\mat M\)-orthogonality conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\), since \(\mat M\) is symmetric. For frame A in the coordinates \(\vek y\) (\(n = 2\), \(\mat M = \mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\)) and \(k = 0\) the set \(S\) is the ellipse, for \(n = 3\), \(\mat M = \mat I\), \(k = 1\) and \(\vek b_1 = \vek\varphi_1\) it is the circle in the plane perpendicular to \(\vek\varphi_1\).

Proof

We must show three properties. Nonemptiness comes from lemma 10.16, boundedness from the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) on the unit sphere, closedness from the continuity of polynomials.

  1. Nonempty. The conditions \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek w = 0\) are \(k\) equations in \(n > k\) unknowns \(w_1, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\), with the rows \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\) as coefficients. By lemma 10.16 there is \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) that satisfies them. For \(k = 0\) there are no conditions and we take \(\vek w = \vek e_1\). Since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek w > 0\), and \(\vek v = \vek w/\sqrt{\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek w}\) lies in \(S\), since \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) and the orthogonality conditions are homogeneous. For frame A in the coordinates \(\vek y\) and for \(k = 0\) the set \(S\) is the whole ellipse.
  2. Bounded. As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 8.71, the polynomial \(\vek u \mapsto \vek u^\T\mat M\,\vek u\) has a smallest value \(\mu\) on the unit sphere \(|\vek u| = 1\) (theorem 8.27, lemma 8.25, points 3 and 4), and \(\mu > 0\) because \(\mat M\) is positive definite. Every \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) is \(|\vek v|\,\vek u\) with \(\vek u = \vek v/|\vek v|\) on the sphere, and since the quadratic form is multiplied by \(c^2\) when the column is multiplied by a number \(c\), \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = |\vek v|^2\,\vek u^\T\mat M\,\vek u \ge \mu\,|\vek v|^2\). For \(\vek v\) in \(S\) therefore \(1 \ge \mu\,|\vek v|^2\), hence \(|\vek v| \le 1/\sqrt\mu\). For frame A in the coordinates \(\vek y\) we have \(\mu = 190{,}98\) kg and \(1/\sqrt\mu = 0{,}0724\) kg\(^{-1/2}\), the radius of the circle in which the ellipse lies.
  3. Closed. Each of the functions \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v\) is a polynomial in the coordinates \(v_1, \dots, v_{\mathrm{n}}\), hence continuous (lemma 8.25, point 3). By point 4 of the same lemma the sets where a continuous function has the value \(1\) or \(0\) are closed. By the same point the intersection of these \(k + 1\) sets is closed too, since we obtain it by \(k\) successive intersections of two closed sets. Like the whiteblack squares on the circle and their limit in figure 1.296, convergent points of the ellipse approach a point that again lies on the ellipse: the value \(\vek v^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek v\) is \(1\) at all of them, so by continuity it is \(1\) at the limit too.

10.3.3 The theorem

Theorem 10.21 Spectral theorem for the stiffness and mass matrix (spektralni izrek za togostno in masno matriko)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric matrix of order \(n\) with real entries and \(\mat M\) a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Then there are real numbers \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and real columns \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(n\) entries that are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (definition 10.17) and for which

\[\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \qquad (r = 1, \dots, n) . \tag{10.37}\]

The number \(\lambda_1\) is the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) among the columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\). The number \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of the same expression among the columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) that are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\). In frame A (figure 10.30), \(\lambda_1\) is the value of the blue curve, which touches the ellipse at the point \(\vek\varphi_1\), and \(\lambda_2\) the value of the orange one, which touches it at the point \(\vek\varphi_2\).

Proof

We find the eigenpairs one after the other, as in theorem 2.83, but with the smallest value. We must show that the smallest value on the set \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) exists, that the column \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) at which it is reached is an eigencolumn, and that the procedure runs up to \(r = n\). We write \(q(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) and \(p(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\). Let \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) be the set of columns \(\vek v\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for \(j < r\) (\(W_1\) is the set of all columns) and \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) the set of columns in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(p(\vek v) = 1\). The induction step \(r\) assumes that \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\) have already been chosen, \(\mat M\)-orthonormal, with \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). For \(r = 1\) there is no assumption. The set \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the set \(S\) of lemma 10.20 with \(k = r - 1\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). We follow the steps on frame A, where \(S_1\) is the ellipse of figure 10.30.

  1. By lemma 10.20 \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is nonempty, closed and bounded when \(r - 1 < n\), and \(q\) is continuous (lemma 8.25, point 3). By theorem 8.27 there is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) such that \(q(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le q(\vek v)\) for every \(\vek v\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\). Since \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\). This is the last sentence of the theorem. For frame A and \(r = 1\) this is the point \(\vek\varphi_1\), where the ellipse is touched by the curve with the value \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}311\) 1/s\(^2\).
  2. Since \(q(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,q(\vek v)\) and \(p(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,p(\vek v)\), every nonzero \(\vek v\) in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives \(\vek v/\sqrt{p(\vek v)}\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the set \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) is closed under multiplication by a number, \(p(\vek v) > 0\)). The division shrinks or stretches the column along the line through the origin until it reaches the ellipse. By step 1 \(q(\vek v)/p(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Let \(\mat B = \mat K - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\), a symmetric matrix (lemma 1.102, point 2). By lemma 1.100, point 3, then
    \[\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v = q(\vek v) - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,p(\vek v) \ge 0 \quad \text{for every } \vek v \text{ in } W_{\mathrm{r}} , \qquad \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 ,\]
    where for \(\vek v = \vek 0\) both sides of the inequality are zero.
  3. Let \(\vek z\) be a column in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(t\) a number. Then \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\), because the conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) are linear in \(\vek v\). We expand with the properties of the matrix product (lemmas 1.100 and 1.102). The first term of the expansion is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) by step 2, the two middle terms are equal by (10.33), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek z = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by step 2
    \[(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z)^\T\mat B\,(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z) = 2t\,\alpha + t^2\beta \ge 0 , \qquad \alpha = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} , \quad \beta = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek z \ge 0 ,\]
    for every number \(t\). As in step 6 of the proof of theorem 2.83 it follows that \(\alpha = 0\): if \(\alpha \ne 0\), the expression would have the value \(-\alpha^2(\beta + 2)/(\beta + 1)^2 < 0\) at \(t = -\alpha/(\beta + 1)\) (figure 2.249). Hence \(\vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(\vek z\) in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\). In figure 10.30 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z\) is a move from the point \(\vek\varphi_1\) in the direction \(\vek z\), which the division of step 2 brings back to the ellipse. If \(\alpha \ne 0\), the point of the ellipse obtained in this way would lie below the value \(\lambda_1\) for a small \(t\) of the opposite sign to \(\alpha\). The blue curve would cross the ellipse, as the grey curve crosses it, and not only touch it.
  4. Let \(\vek w\) be any column, \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w\) for \(j < r\) and \(\vek z = \vek w - \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By \(\mat M\)-orthonormality \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek z = c_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = c_{\mathrm{i}} - c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for every \(i < r\), so \(\vek z\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\). For \(\mat M = \mat I\), \(n = 3\) and \(r = 2\) this is the splitting of the column \(\vek w\) into a part \(\vek z\) in the plane \(W_2\) and a part \(c_1\vek\varphi_1\) along its normal \(\vek\varphi_1\). For frame A and \(r = 2\) we have \(\mat M = \mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\), and figure 10.35 shows the split in the coordinates \(\vek y\) with the axes \(y_1\) and \(y_2\). Let us take the column \(\vek w = (0{,}05,\ -0{,}02)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). With \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_1 = (30{,}777,\ -11{,}756)\) kg\(^{1/2}\) we get \(c_1 = (\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_1)^\T\vek w = 30{,}777 \cdot 0{,}05 + (-11{,}756) \cdot (-0{,}02) = 1{,}774\), so the part along \(\vek\varphi_1\) is \(c_1\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}0675,\ 0{,}0258)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). The rest, \(\vek z = \vek w - c_1\vek\varphi_1 = (-0{,}0175,\ -0{,}0458)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\), lies on the line \(W_2\) by the computation above. The column \(\vek w\) is the diagonal of the parallelogram with the sides \(c_1\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\vek z\), and the sides are parallel to the line through \(\vek\varphi_1\) and to the line \(W_2\). The perpendicular projection of the column \(\vek w\) onto the line through \(\vek\varphi_1\) would give a different, shorter part \((0{,}0370,\ 0{,}0141)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\), because \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\)-orthogonality is not ordinary perpendicularity.
  1. For \(j < r\) we have \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). The matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric, so by lemma 1.102 and the induction assumption \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}})^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}})^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and this is zero because \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\). Also \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \cdot 0\).
  2. By step 4 \(\vek w = \vek z + \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), so by lemma 1.102, point 2, lemma 1.100, point 3, and steps 3 and 5, for every column \(\vek w\)
    \[\vek w^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 .\]
    For frame A and \(r = 2\) (figure 10.35), the part \(\vek z\) on the line \(W_2\) gives zero by step 3 and the part \(c_1\vek\varphi_1\) gives zero by step 5. For \(\vek w = \mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) this is \((\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})^\T(\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = |\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}|^2 = 0\). A sum of squares of entries is zero only if every entry is zero, so \(\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). Step \(r\) is complete.
  3. Since \(W_{\mathrm{r}} \subset W_{\mathrm{r}-1}\), also \(S_{\mathrm{r}} \subset S_{\mathrm{r}-1}\), and the smallest value on the smaller set is not smaller: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}-1} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). For frame A \(S_2\) is the pair of points \(\pm\vek\varphi_2\) (one of them) on the ellipse \(S_1\), and \(383{,}311 \le 2627{,}249\).
  4. By lemma 10.20 \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is nonempty for every \(r \le n\), so all the steps \(r = 1, 2, \dots, n\) can be carried out. From history

Rayleigh, in his treatise Theory of Sound, published in 1877, introduced the fundamental concept of oscillation of a linear system about an equilibrium configuration and showed that eigenmodes and eigenfrequencies exist for discrete and for continuous systems (GR, PDF p. 19). He was concerned with acoustics, not with structural mechanics (GR, PDF p. 19). The pioneers of aeronautics were the first structural designers who had to get vibration under control, because they constantly aimed to reduce the weight of flying structures (GR, PDF p. 19). From the 1920s onwards aeronautical engineers had to admit the importance of the mechanics of vibration for predicting the aeroelastic behaviour of aircraft (GR, PDF p. 19). During the next forty years they had to limit the scope of their analysis to methods that the computational means of the time could handle (GR, PDF p. 19). The structural models were therefore either analytical or described by a small number of degrees of freedom, obtained by transfer (prenosnimi) or Rayleigh-Ritz methods (GR, PDF p. 19). The appearance and spread of computers since 1960 led to a reconsideration of all methods of analysis, and the traditional methods were replaced by matrix methods arising from the discretization of variational expressions (GR, PDF p. 19).

We met the methods of Rayleigh and Ritz in section 6.4.1, and the beginnings of the finite element method in section 7.2.2. Back to the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) and the theorem. For a symmetric \(\mat K\) and a positive definite \(\mat M\) of order \(n\) there are \(n\) real eigenpairs with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal modes, which the proof found one after the other as successive smallest values of the quotient of energies on the sets \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\). In frame A the set \(S_1\) is the ellipse and the set \(S_2\) a pair of points.

10.3.5 Expansion in modes and the modal matrix

The answer to the fourth question: every column is a sum of modes.

Corollary 10.22 Expansion theorem for any number of coordinates (razvoj po načinih za poljubno število koordinat)

Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as in theorem 10.21. Then:

  1. Every column \(\vek v\) with \(n\) entries is \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the coefficients are unique, \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\). For frame A this is the parallelogram of the shares of the two modes.
  2. If \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\) for a real number \(\lambda\) and a column \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), then \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for some \(r\), and \(\vek\varphi\) is a sum of multiples of those \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) for which \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\). For two equal floors this is the circle, in which every arrow is an eigenvector.
  3. The number of indices \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) does not depend on the choice of columns in the proof of the theorem: every other choice according to the theorem gives the same list of eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\). With the second pair on the circle the list \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\) is the same as with the first.
Proof

We must show the expansion, then what follows for other eigenpairs, and finally that the list of eigenvalues does not depend on the choice.

  1. Let \(\vek w = \vek v - \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v)\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). By \(\mat M\)-orthonormality \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v - \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for every \(s\). The columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are nonzero, since they are \(\mat M\)-normalized, so by point 3 of corollary 10.19 \(\vek w = \vek 0\). If \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), we multiply from the left by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\) and obtain \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) (again by \(\mat M\)-orthonormality). For frame A, \(\vek w = \vek 0\) means that the parallelogram closes at the tip of the column.
  2. By point 1 \(\vek\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi\). Since \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric, by lemma 1.102 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi = (\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\vek\varphi = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,c_{\mathrm{r}}\), while by assumption \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,c_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda)\,c_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(r\). Since \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), some \(c_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\), and for this \(r\) we have \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Only the \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) can be nonzero. On the circle \(\vek\varphi\) is a sum of the arrows of the pair with the same \(\lambda\).
  3. Let \(E_\lambda\) be the set of solutions of \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\) (including \(\vek 0\)) and \(p\) the number of indices \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\). By step 2 every nonzero element of \(E_\lambda\) is a sum of multiples of the columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\). For \(p = 0\) therefore \(E_\lambda = \{\vek 0\}\). For the circle \(E_\lambda\) is the whole plane, for the ellipse of frame A only the lines through the touching points.
  4. Let \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}'}\) be \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns in \(E_\lambda\). We show \(p' \le p\). For \(p = 0\) this holds, because an \(\mat M\)-normalized column is not \(\vek 0\). Let \(p \ge 1\) and suppose \(p' > p\). By step 3 \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), a sum over the \(p\) indices \(j\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\). The system \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,x_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) has \(p\) equations and \(p' > p\) unknowns, so by lemma 10.16 there is \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) for which \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,x_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\). This contradicts point 1 of corollary 10.19. Hence \(p' \le p\). On the circle there can be two \(\mat M\)-orthonormal arrows, never a third.
  5. Let another choice of columns in the proof of the theorem give the eigenvalues \(\lambda'_1 \le \dots \le \lambda'_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the columns \(\vek\varphi'_{\mathrm{r}}\). The columns \(\vek\varphi'_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal and lie in \(E_\lambda\), so by step 4 there are at most \(p\) of them. The other choice satisfies the theorem as well, so steps 1 to 4 also hold with the roles of the two choices exchanged, and there are at least \(p\) of them. Every real number therefore appears equally often in both lists. Both lists have \(n\) members and are in increasing order, so they are equal. The second pair has the same \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) as the first, although the arrows differ.

Definition 10.23 Modal matrix (modalna matrika)

Corollary 10.25 Spectral theorem for a symmetric matrix (spektralni izrek za simetrično matriko)

A symmetric matrix \(\mat A\) of order \(n\) with real entries has \(n\) real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(n\) pairwise perpendicular unit columns \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(\mat A\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). Every column \(\vek v\) is \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek v)\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). For \(\mat M = \mat I\) the set is a circle and the modes are perpendicular unit arrows.

Proof

We must show that this is theorem 10.21 and corollary 10.22 for a special choice.

  1. The matrix \(\mat I\) is symmetric and positive definite, since \(\vek v^\T\mat I\,\vek v = |\vek v|^2 > 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\).
  2. For \(\mat K = \mat A\) and \(\mat M = \mat I\), \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is ordinary perpendicularity, \(\mat M\)-normalization is length \(1\), and the theorem gives the statement on the eigenpairs. Corollary 10.22 gives the expansion. The set \(\vek v^\T\vek v = 1\) is a circle of radius 1, and \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is a right angle.

Source: The proof is not from a source. CM §2.5.6.3 (PDF p. 93), SADD §1.6 (PDF pp. 21 and 22), as for theorem 2.83.

For \(n = 3\) this is theorem 2.83 with the eigenvalues in reverse order: there they are ordered from the largest, here from the smallest. In figure 10.31 (c) these are \(\vek\varphi_1\), \(\vek\varphi_2\) and \(\vek\varphi_3\).

10.3.6 A common mistake: coefficients without the mass matrix

By lemma 10.24 \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\). So we get the coefficients of the expansion of a column \(\vek q\) in modes by a multiplication, \(\vek c = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\vek q\) (this is the column of the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q\) of corollary 10.22), and we put the column back together from them by \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\,\vek c\). In the next section these coefficients will be the new coordinates of the system. In a computation by hand the matrix \(\mat M\) is easily left out, \(\tilde{\vek c} = \mat\Phi^\T\vek q\), as if the modes were perpendicular in the ordinary sense. For frame A with its equal masses the mistake hides, so we show it on a frame with different masses, once right and once wrong (figure 10.42).

The model. We take frame A and put another \(500\) kg on the upper floor (our model). The lower floor has mass \(m\), the upper one \(2m\), with \(m = 500\) kg. The steel columns and the ground stay, so \(\mat K\) does not change. The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\,m\,\dot q_1^2 + \tfrac12\,(2m)\,\dot q_2^2\), so in the mass matrix the second diagonal entry doubles (theorem 8.97):

\[\mat K = k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.39}\]

We compute in units as for the frame with three storeys: \(\lambda\) in the unit \(k/m = 1003{,}52\) 1/s\(^2\) and the entries of the modes in the unit \(m^{-1/2}\), so that \(\mat M\) plays the role of the matrix with the diagonal \(1\) and \(2\), and \(\mat K\) the role of the matrix \(\mat K/k\). We find the eigenpairs as in section 10.2, with a determinant of order 2: \((2 - \lambda)(1 - 2\lambda) - 1 = 2\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 1 = 0\). We get (NumPy)

\[\lambda_1 = \frac{5 - \sqrt{17}}{4} = 0{,}219224 , \qquad \lambda_2 = \frac{5 + \sqrt{17}}{4} = 2{,}280776 , \qquad \mat\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}369048 & 0{,}929410 \\ 0{,}657192 & -0{,}260956 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{10.40}\]

The modes are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal, \(0{,}369048 \cdot 0{,}929410 - 2 \cdot 0{,}657192 \cdot 0{,}260956 = 0\) up to rounding, but not perpendicular in the ordinary sense, \(0{,}369048 \cdot 0{,}929410 - 0{,}657192 \cdot 0{,}260956 = 0{,}1715\). This is just lemma 10.18: the eigenvalues are different, so it is the product with \(\mat M\) that is zero, not the ordinary product.

The initial shape. We move the lower floor by \(q_1 = 10\) mm and the upper one by \(q_2 = 5\) mm from the equilibrium position, so \(\vek q = (10;\ 5)\) mm. In the plane \(q_1\), \(q_2\) this is the arrow \(\vek q\), and the modes are two lines through the origin that are not perpendicular.

Right, with the matrix \(\mat M\) (part (b) of the figure).

  1. First \(\mat M\,\vek q = (1 \cdot 10;\ 2 \cdot 5) = (10;\ 10)\) mm. The coefficients are \(c_1 = 0{,}369048 \cdot 10 + 0{,}657192 \cdot 10 = 10{,}262\) and \(c_2 = 0{,}929410 \cdot 10 - 0{,}260956 \cdot 10 = 6{,}685\), in the unit mm \(m^{1/2}\).
  2. The parts of the modes are \(c_1\vek\varphi_1 = (3{,}787;\ 6{,}744)\) mm and \(c_2\vek\varphi_2 = (6{,}213;\ -1{,}744)\) mm, each along the line of its mode.
  3. The sum of the parts is \((3{,}787 + 6{,}213;\ 6{,}744 - 1{,}744) = (10{,}000;\ 5{,}000)\) mm. The parallelogram closes at the tip of the arrow \(\vek q\), we got back the column we started with.

Wrong, without the matrix \(\mat M\) (part (c) of the figure, the same axes, the same lines of the modes and the same arrow \(\vek q\)).

  1. The coefficients are \(\tilde c_1 = 0{,}369048 \cdot 10 + 0{,}657192 \cdot 5 = 6{,}976\) and \(\tilde c_2 = 0{,}929410 \cdot 10 - 0{,}260956 \cdot 5 = 7{,}989\).
  2. The parts are \(\tilde c_1\vek\varphi_1 = (2{,}575;\ 4{,}585)\) mm and \(\tilde c_2\vek\varphi_2 = (7{,}425;\ -2{,}085)\) mm.
  3. The sum of the parts is \(\tilde{\vek q} = (10{,}000;\ 2{,}500)\) mm. The parallelogram closes at the point \(\tilde{\vek q}\), not at the tip of the arrow \(\vek q\): the displacement of the upper floor is \(2{,}5\) mm too small.

The second entry is exactly half of the right one, and this is no accident. From \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\) it follows that \(\mat I = \mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\). In our units \(\mat M\) has the diagonal \(1\) and \(2\), and its inverse matrix \(\mat M^{-1}\) the diagonal \(1\) and \(\tfrac12\). We multiply the equation from the right by \(\mat M^{-1}\):

\[\mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^\T = \mat M^{-1} , \qquad \tilde{\vek q} = \mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^\T\vek q = \mat M^{-1}\vek q = \left(\frac{10}{1};\ \frac{5}{2}\right) \text{ mm} . \tag{10.41}\]

So the route without \(\mat M\) divides each displacement by the mass of its floor. We get \(\vek q\) back only when \(\mat M = \mat I\). For frame A \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and the route without \(\mat M\) gives \(\vek q/m\): the ratio of the displacements is right, but the size is wrong by the factor \(m = 500\) kg, which hides when the mass is measured in the unit \(m\). With different masses the shape is spoiled as well, as the point \(\tilde{\vek q}\) shows. The unit already gives the mistake away: \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\vek q\) has the unit kg\(^{-1/2} \cdot\) kg \(\cdot\) mm \(=\) kg\(^{1/2}\) mm, while \(\mat\Phi^\T\vek q\) has kg\(^{-1/2}\) mm. In the lectures the column of displacements is \(\{x\}\), the modal matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf X}\) and the column of coefficients \(\{\eta\}\), and the correct relation is \(\{\eta\} = \tilde{\mathbf X}^\T\mat M\,\{x\}\).

10.3.7 The eigenvalues are real

The theorem speaks of real columns and real numbers \(\lambda\). Could some complex pair \((\lambda, \vek\varphi)\) show a vibration that the theorem does not cover? We introduced complex numbers in section 9.3: a complex column has complex entries, \(\vek\varphi = \vek a + \mathrm i\,\vek b\) with real columns \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). We compute the product of a real matrix with a complex column by equation (1.39), with complex numbers in place of real ones. Such a pair would give an oscillation with growing amplitude, seen with a point \(s\) off the axes.

Let us draw what a complex pair would give (figure 10.43). On the left is the plane of the number \(s\) with the real axis \(\sigma\) and the imaginary axis \(\tau\). For \(\lambda > 0\) we have \(s = \pm\mathrm i\omega\), so two points on the imaginary axis, and the motion is an oscillation with constant amplitude. For \(\lambda < 0\) the number \(s\) is real, so two points on the real axis, and the motion is exponential growth or decay. On the right are the time courses against the time \(t\) on the horizontal axis. A complex \(\lambda\) would give \(s\) off both axes and an oscillation with growing amplitude. The lemma below shows that there is no such point.

Lemma 10.26 The eigenvalues are real (lastne vrednosti so realne)

Let \(\mat K\) be symmetric and \(\mat M\) symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), both with real entries. If for a complex number \(\lambda\) and a complex column \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\), then \(\lambda\) is a real number. In figure 10.43 this means that \(s\) lies on the imaginary or on the real axis, not off them.

Proof

We must show that the imaginary part of \(\lambda\) is zero. We split the equation into its real and imaginary parts and eliminate the same expression by the symmetry of the matrices.

  1. Let \(\lambda = \sigma + \mathrm i\tau\) and \(\vek\varphi = \vek a + \mathrm i\,\vek b\). Since the matrices are real, \(\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \mat M\,\vek a + \mathrm i\,\mat M\,\vek b\), and by the rules of arithmetic (lemma 9.25) \(\lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi = (\sigma\,\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\mat M\,\vek b) + \mathrm i\,(\tau\,\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek b)\), and \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \mat K\,\vek a + \mathrm i\,\mat K\,\vek b\) (the matrix is real). The real parts and the imaginary parts are equal: \(\mat K\,\vek a = \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\mat M\,\vek b\) and \(\mat K\,\vek b = \tau\,\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek b\).
  2. We multiply the first equation from the left by \(\vek b^\T\), the second by \(\vek a^\T\): \(\vek b^\T\mat K\,\vek a = \sigma\,\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b\) and \(\vek a^\T\mat K\,\vek b = \tau\,\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek b\).
  3. The matrices are symmetric, so \(\vek b^\T\mat K\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\mat K\,\vek b\) and \(\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek b\) (equation (10.33)). When we subtract the second equation of step 2 from the first, the left sides and the terms with \(\sigma\) cancel: \(0 = -\tau\,(\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b)\).
  4. Since \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) or \(\vek b \ne \vek 0\). Since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b > 0\), hence \(\tau = 0\).

Source: CM (2.5.34) to (2.5.36) (PDF pp. 92-93) proves the same for a symmetric matrix (\(\mat M = \mat I\)) with complex conjugate pairs. The proof above with real and imaginary parts is not from a source.

Meaning: we look for solutions \(\vek q = \mathrm e^{st}\vek\varphi\) of \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with complex \(s\). Since \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\mathrm e^{st} = s\,\mathrm e^{st}\) and \(\mathrm e^{st} \ne 0\) (theorem 9.34, points 2 and 3), we need \((s^2\mat M + \mat K)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), hence \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = -s^2\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\). By the lemma \(\lambda = -s^2\) is real. With \(s = \sigma + \mathrm i\tau\) we have \(s^2 = \sigma^2 - \tau^2 + 2\mathrm i\,\sigma\tau\), so \(\sigma\tau = 0\): \(s\) is real or purely imaginary. For \(\lambda > 0\), \(s = \pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda\), a pure vibration. For \(\lambda = 0\), \(s = 0\). For \(\lambda < 0\), \(s = \pm\sqrt{-\lambda}\) is real, growth or decay without vibration. So an undamped system has no solution \(\mathrm e^{st}\vek\varphi\) with an \(s\) whose real and imaginary parts are both nonzero. With damping, later in this chapter, this will change. No pair thus gives a point \(s\) off the axes, and only oscillation and growth or decay remain.

The spectral theorem has thus answered all four questions from the beginning of the section. A pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(n\) has \(n\) real eigenpairs, the modes are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal, every column is their sum, and the eigenvalues are successive minima of the quotient of energies. In the next section we use them to decouple the equations of motion, and later in the chapter we estimate the eigenvalues without computing them.
