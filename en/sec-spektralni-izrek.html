Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.3 Mathematics: the spectral theorem

In section 10.2 we obtained, for frame A, two natural frequencies and two modes from a determinant of order 2, and vibration in one mode was a motion in which all coordinates move in step (lemma 10.8). For a system with 16 coordinates, such as the bar made of finite elements will be, we have no determinant of order 16, since the book has determinants only up to order 3 (definition 2.50). Even if we had one, we would obtain an equation of degree 16 for \(\lambda\), and we would not know whether it has 16 real solutions. So four questions arise for a pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(n\).

  1. Does the pair always have \(n\) eigenpairs (definition 10.7)?
  2. Are the eigenvalues real, even if we admit complex numbers? A complex eigenvalue would give a motion in which vibration is mixed with growth or decay.
  3. How are the modes related to each other?
  4. Is every column a sum of modes, so that every motion can be built from them?

The answer is theorem 10.13 of this section. We prove it without determinants, with the smallest value, along the same path by which we proved theorem 2.61 for a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with the largest value in chapter 2.

10.3.1 Idea: successive minima

For the principal directions we looked for the unit vector at which \(\vek n^\T\mat A\,\vek n\) is largest and showed that it is an eigenvector. We do the same here, with three changes. First: instead of the unit sphere \(\vek v^\T\vek v = 1\) we take the set of columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\). At velocities \(\dot{\vek q}\) the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.86), so all columns of this set, read as velocities, have the same kinetic energy. By lemma 10.8 the eigenvalue is the quotient \(\lambda = \vek\varphi^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi/\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi\), the ratio of twice the potential energy at displacements \(\vek\varphi\) to twice the kinetic energy at velocities \(\vek\varphi\). So we expect the lowest frequency to belong to the shape that strains the structure least at a given kinetic energy, and we look for the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) on this set. Second change: we look for the smallest, not the largest value, because the lowest frequency interests us first. Third change: we cannot get the third direction from a cross product, which we do not have for more coordinates, so we will need another argument, lemma 10.9.

Let us look at the set and its smallest value for frame A. In the coordinates \((q_1, q_2)\) of section 10.1 we have \(\mat M = m\mat I\) and the set \(\vek q^\T\mat M\,\vek q = 1\) is a circle. In the coordinates \((q_1, q_2 - q_1)\) of section 10.2 \(\mat K\) is a multiple of the identity matrix, the curves of constant value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) are circles, and the modes \((1,\ 0{,}618)\) and \((1,\ -1{,}618)\) are perpendicular, since \(1 \cdot 1 + 0{,}618 \cdot (-1{,}618) = 0\) up to rounding. In both cases this comes from the coordinates, not from a general rule. So we take coordinates in which neither matrix is diagonal: the displacement of the upper floor \(y_1 = q_2\) and the drift of the upper storey \(y_2 = q_2 - q_1\). From \(\vek q = \mat P\,\vek y\) with \(\mat P = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) we get \(\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q = \vek y^\T(\mat P^\T\mat K\mat P)\,\vek y\) (lemma 1.101), hence

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat P^\T\mat K\mat P = k\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat P^\T\mat M\mat P = m\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{10.18}\]

with \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(m = 500\) kg. The set \(\vek y^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = 1\) is an ellipse with semi-axes \(0{,}0724\) and \(0{,}0276\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) (figure 10.9). The curves of constant value \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y = c\) are ellipses about the origin that grow as \(c\) increases. The smallest value of \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y\) on the ellipse is reached by the smallest ellipse of this family that still reaches the ellipse (it touches it from inside), and the largest by the largest one that still reaches it (it touches it from outside). The two touchings occur at \(c = 383{,}311\) 1/s\(^2\) (blue) and \(c = 2627{,}249\) 1/s\(^2\) (orange), which are exactly the eigenvalues of section 10.2. The points of contact are \(\pm\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\pm\vek\varphi_2\) with \(\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}038042,\ 0{,}014531)\) and \(\vek\varphi_2 = (-0{,}023511,\ -0{,}061554)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). At \(c = 900\) 1/s\(^2\) (grey dashed) the curve cuts the ellipse at four points. The smallest value is not reached there, because from an intersection point we can move along the ellipse to the side where \(\vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek y < 900\) 1/s\(^2\).

At the point of contact \(\vek\varphi_1\) must be an eigencolumn: if the two curves crossed at this point, we could move along the ellipse to the side with a smaller value. This is the idea of section 2.11.2, here for a pair of matrices. The proof will make it precise and for every order \(n\).

The assumptions are as follows. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric with real entries, while \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) may have either sign, so that we also cover unstable equilibrium (\(\lambda < 0\)) and a mechanism (\(\lambda = 0\)). The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive definite (definition 6.27), both of order \(n\), as in theorem 10.2. Columns have \(n\) real entries.

10.3.2 Tools: more unknowns than equations, and \(\mat M\)-orthogonality

In theorem 2.61 we obtained the third direction as the cross product of two perpendicular vectors. For any order \(n\) we need another statement: when we impose fewer conditions than there are unknowns, something nonzero still satisfies them.

Lemma 10.9 Homogeneous system with more unknowns than equations (homogeni sistem z več neznankami kot enačbami)

Let \(\mat A\) be a real matrix with \(k\) rows and \(n\) columns, \(n > k\). Then there is a column \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) entries for which \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\).

Proof

We must show that the elimination of definition 8.20 leaves some column without a pivot, and that the unknown of this column can be set to \(1\). The idea comes from the proof of lemma 8.21.

  1. Gaussian elimination with row exchanges as in definition 8.20 finds \(r\) pivots, one in each of the rows \(1, \dots, r\), in the columns \(c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_{\mathrm{r}}\) (each pivot lies to the right of the previous one). There are \(k\) rows, so \(r \le k\). Each step of the elimination, an exchange of two rows or the subtraction of a multiple of one row from another, gives a system with the same solutions (step 1 of the proof of lemma 8.21). We obtain a system \(\mat U\,\vek w = \vek 0\) with the same set of solutions as \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\).
  2. By definition 8.20 the pivot \(U_{\mathrm{i} \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}}\) in row \(i \le r\) is nonzero, and all entries of row \(i\) to its left are zero. Indeed, column \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the first column to the right of \(c_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) that has a nonzero number in the rows from \(i\) on. The columns before it have zeros in these rows: the pivot columns because of the subtractions in earlier steps, the columns in between because of the choice of the pivot. Later steps do not spoil these zeros, because they exchange and subtract only rows that already have zeros in these columns. If \(r < k\), the rows \(r + 1, \dots, k\) are all zeros for the same reason: the elimination stopped because none of these rows has a nonzero number in the columns to the right of \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) (for \(r = 0\) in all columns), and in the columns up to \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) they have zeros from earlier steps.
  3. There are \(r \le k < n\) pivots and \(n\) columns, so there is a column \(j\) that is not a pivot column. We set \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) and \(w_{\mathrm{l}} = 0\) for every other column \(l\) without a pivot.
  4. We determine the unknowns in the pivot columns in the order \(i = r, r - 1, \dots, 1\) (back substitution). Row \(i\) contains only unknowns \(w_{\mathrm{l}}\) with \(l \ge c_{\mathrm{i}}\), so equation \(i\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{l} > \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}} U_{\mathrm{il}}\,w_{\mathrm{l}}/U_{\mathrm{i} \mathrm{c}_\mathrm{i}}\), where all \(w_{\mathrm{l}}\) with \(l > c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are already known: either they come from step 3, or they are unknowns \(w_{\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{i}'}}\) of rows \(i' > i\), determined earlier.
  5. The rows \(1, \dots, r\) are thus satisfied, and the rows \(r + 1, \dots, k\) are all zeros and are satisfied for every \(\vek w\). Hence \(\mat U\,\vek w = \vek 0\), by step 1 also \(\mat A\,\vek w = \vek 0\), and \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) says \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.5, Theorem 3 (PDF p. 317) states the claim. The proof is not from the source.

We test the statement on a system of two equations in three unknowns, \(x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\) and \(4x_1 + 5x_2 + 6x_3 = 0\). Subtracting four times the first row from the second gives \(-3x_2 - 6x_3 = 0\). The pivots are in columns \(1\) and \(2\), column \(3\) is free, so we set \(x_3 = 1\). The second row gives \(x_2 = -2\), the first \(x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_3 = 1\). The column \((1, -2, 1)\) indeed solves both equations: \(1 - 4 + 3 = 0\) and \(4 - 10 + 6 = 0\).

Without the third unknown the statement fails. Take \(x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\) and \(4x_1 + 5x_2 = 0\), where \(n = k = 2\). Subtracting four times the first row from the second gives \(-3x_2 = 0\), so \(x_2 = 0\), and then the first row gives \(x_1 = 0\). The only solution is the zero column. Both columns have a pivot (\(1\) and \(-3\)), no unknown is free, so none can be set to \(1\). For \(n > k\) there are at most \(k < n\) pivots and a column without a pivot always remains, which the proof of the lemma uses.

For \(\mat M = \mat I\) we measured the perpendicularity of columns with the scalar product. For the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) it is replaced by the product with the matrix \(\mat M\), because \(\mat M\) determines the kinetic energy.

Definition 10.10 Orthogonality and normalization with respect to the mass matrix (ortogonalnost in normiranost glede na masno matriko)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Real columns \(\vek\varphi\) and \(\vek\psi\) with \(n\) entries are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal (\(\mat M\)-ortogonalna), if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = 0\). A column \(\vek\varphi\) is \(\mat M\)-normalized (\(\mat M\)-normiran), if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 1\). A set of columns is \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (\(\mat M\)-ortonormirana), if all its columns are \(\mat M\)-normalized and any two different ones are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal, that is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) for \(r \ne s\).

Source: CK (10.20) and the text below it, "orthogonal with respect to the mass matrix" (PDF p. 307), CK (10.31) for the normalization (PDF p. 312).

The condition does not depend on the order of the two columns. For a symmetric matrix \(\mat A\) of order \(n\) and columns \(\vek v\), \(\vek w\) with \(n\) entries, \(\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w\) is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, which equals its transpose, so by lemma 1.101 (points 1 and 3)

\[\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w = (\vek v^\T\mat A\,\vek w)^\T = \vek w^\T\mat A^\T\vek v = \vek w^\T\mat A\,\vek v . \tag{10.19}\]

This is equation (2.60) of chapter 2 for any order \(n\). For \(\mat A = \mat M\) it says that \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = 0\) exactly when \(\vek\psi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 0\). For \(\mat M = \mat I\) this is ordinary perpendicularity, for frame A in the coordinates \(\vek q\) (\(\mat M = m\mat I\)) too, but for frame A in the coordinates \(\vek y\) it is not (figure 10.9). We check with the modes \(\vek\varphi_1 = (0{,}038042,\ 0{,}014531)\) and \(\vek\varphi_2 = (-0{,}023511,\ -0{,}061554)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) from the caption of the figure. The ordinary product is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek\varphi_2 = -0{,}001789\) kg\(^{-1}\), which is not zero, so they are not perpendicular. The product with the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\) from (10.18), however, is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_2 = 1{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\), zero up to the rounding of the six-digit entries (for comparison \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek\varphi_1 = 1{,}0000\)). In the coordinates \(\vek q\) the modes \((1;\ 1{,}618)\) and \((1;\ -0{,}618)\) are perpendicular, \(m\,(1 - 1{,}618 \cdot 0{,}618) = 0\) up to rounding. If we change the second column to \((1;\ -0{,}6)\), then \(m\,(1 - 1{,}618 \cdot 0{,}6) = 14{,}6\) kg, which is not zero, and there is no \(\mat M\)-orthogonality. The unit of the entries of an \(\mat M\)-normalized column is kg\(^{-1/2}\), because \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 1\) is a number without a unit. Meaning of the normalization: the velocities \(\dot{\vek q} = \sqrt{1\ \text{J}}\,\vek\varphi\) have kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} = \tfrac12\) J.

Corollary 10.11 At most as many nonzero mass-orthogonal columns as coordinates (največ toliko neničelnih masno ortogonalnih stolpcev, kolikor je koordinat)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}}\) nonzero, pairwise \(\mat M\)-orthogonal columns with \(n\) entries. Then:

  1. From \(c_1\vek\psi_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{p}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek 0\) it follows that \(c_1 = \dots = c_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\).
  2. \(p \le n\).
  3. If \(p = n\) and the column \(\vek w\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all of \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{n}}\), then \(\vek w = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show three points. The first is a computation with the product with \(\mat M\), the second and third follow from lemma 10.9.

  1. Let \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\). For each \(j\) we multiply the equation from the left by the row \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\) (lemma 1.99, point 3), and since \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(i \ne j\), there remains \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). Since \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} \ne \vek 0\) and \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\), hence \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\).
  2. Suppose \(p > n\). The matrix \(\mat A\) with the columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}}\) has \(n\) rows and \(p > n\) columns, so by lemma 10.9 there is \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) with \(\mat A\,\vek c = \vek 0\). By (1.38) entry \(l\) of the column \(\mat A\,\vek c\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} (\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{l}}\,c_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \(\mat A\,\vek c = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\). This contradicts point 1.
  3. Let \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) be \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{n}}, \vek w\) is a set of \(n + 1\) nonzero pairwise \(\mat M\)-orthogonal columns, which contradicts point 2.

Source: The proof is not from a source. CK (PDF p. 313) states that the modes are linearly independent and refers to Problem 10.8.

For \(\mat M = \mat I\) and \(n = 3\) the corollary says that space has no four nonzero pairwise perpendicular vectors. Before we state the theorem, we need the set on which we look for the minimum. It must be nonempty, closed and bounded, so that theorem 8.26 guarantees that the minimum exists.

Lemma 10.12 The set of mass-normalized columns (množica masno normiranih stolpcev)

Let \(\mat M\) be a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek b_1, \dots, \vek b_{\mathrm{k}}\) arbitrary columns with \(n\) entries, \(k < n\). The set

\[S = \{\vek v :\ \vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1 ,\ \vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0 \text{ for } j = 1, \dots, k\}\]

is nonempty, closed and bounded. For \(k = 0\) there are no conditions \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0\). For \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) they are the \(\mat M\)-orthogonality conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\), since \(\mat M\) is symmetric.

Proof

We must show three properties. Nonemptiness comes from lemma 10.9, boundedness from the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) on the unit sphere, closedness from the continuity of polynomials.

  1. Nonempty. The conditions \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek w = 0\) are \(k\) equations in \(n > k\) unknowns \(w_1, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\), with the rows \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\) as coefficients. By lemma 10.9 there is \(\vek w \ne \vek 0\) that satisfies them. For \(k = 0\) there are no conditions and we take \(\vek w = \vek e_1\). Since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek w > 0\), and \(\vek v = \vek w/\sqrt{\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek w}\) lies in \(S\), since \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) and the orthogonality conditions are homogeneous.
  2. Bounded. As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 8.63, the polynomial \(\vek u \mapsto \vek u^\T\mat M\,\vek u\) has a smallest value \(\mu\) on the unit sphere \(|\vek u| = 1\) (theorem 8.26, lemma 8.24, points 3 and 4), and \(\mu > 0\) because \(\mat M\) is positive definite. Every \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) is \(|\vek v|\,\vek u\) with \(\vek u = \vek v/|\vek v|\) on the sphere, and since the quadratic form is multiplied by \(c^2\) when the column is multiplied by a number \(c\), \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = |\vek v|^2\,\vek u^\T\mat M\,\vek u \ge \mu\,|\vek v|^2\). For \(\vek v\) in \(S\) therefore \(1 \ge \mu\,|\vek v|^2\), hence \(|\vek v| \le 1/\sqrt\mu\).
  3. Closed. Each of the functions \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v\) is a polynomial in the coordinates \(v_1, \dots, v_{\mathrm{n}}\), hence continuous (lemma 8.24, point 3). By point 4 of the same lemma the sets where a continuous function has the value \(1\) or \(0\) are closed. By the same point the intersection of these \(k + 1\) sets is closed too, since we obtain it by \(k\) successive intersections of two closed sets.

10.3.3 The theorem

Theorem 10.13 Spectral theorem for the stiffness and mass matrix (spektralni izrek za togostno in masno matriko)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric matrix of order \(n\) with real entries and \(\mat M\) a symmetric, positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Then there are real numbers \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and real columns \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(n\) entries that are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (definition 10.10) and for which

\[\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \qquad (r = 1, \dots, n) . \tag{10.20}\]

The number \(\lambda_1\) is the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) among the columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\). The number \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of the same expression among the columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) that are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\).

Proof

We find the eigenpairs one after the other, as in theorem 2.61, but with the smallest value. We must show that the smallest value on the set \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) exists, that the column \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) at which it is reached is an eigencolumn, and that the procedure runs up to \(r = n\). We write \(q(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) and \(p(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\). Let \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) be the set of columns \(\vek v\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for \(j < r\) (\(W_1\) is the set of all columns) and \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) the set of columns in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(p(\vek v) = 1\). The induction step \(r\) assumes that \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\) have already been chosen, \(\mat M\)-orthonormal, with \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). For \(r = 1\) there is no assumption. The set \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the set \(S\) of lemma 10.12 with \(k = r - 1\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\).

  1. By lemma 10.12 \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is nonempty, closed and bounded when \(r - 1 < n\), and \(q\) is continuous (lemma 8.24, point 3). By theorem 8.26 there is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) such that \(q(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le q(\vek v)\) for every \(\vek v\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\). Since \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\). This is the last sentence of the theorem.
  2. Since \(q(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,q(\vek v)\) and \(p(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,p(\vek v)\), every nonzero \(\vek v\) in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives \(\vek v/\sqrt{p(\vek v)}\) in \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the set \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) is closed under multiplication by a number, \(p(\vek v) > 0\)). By step 1 \(q(\vek v)/p(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Let \(\mat B = \mat K - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\), a symmetric matrix (lemma 1.101, point 2). By lemma 1.99, point 3, then
    \[\vek v^\T\mat B\,\vek v = q(\vek v) - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,p(\vek v) \ge 0 \quad \text{for every } \vek v \text{ in } W_{\mathrm{r}} , \qquad \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 ,\]
    where for \(\vek v = \vek 0\) both sides of the inequality are zero.
  3. Let \(\vek z\) be a column in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(t\) a number. Then \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\), because the conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) are linear in \(\vek v\). We expand with the properties of the matrix product (lemmas 1.99 and 1.101). The first term of the expansion is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) by step 2, the two middle terms are equal by (10.19), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat B\,\vek z = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by step 2
    \[(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z)^\T\mat B\,(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + t\,\vek z) = 2t\,\alpha + t^2\beta \ge 0 , \qquad \alpha = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} , \quad \beta = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek z \ge 0 ,\]
    for every number \(t\). As in step 6 of the proof of theorem 2.61 it follows that \(\alpha = 0\): if \(\alpha \ne 0\), the expression would have the value \(-\alpha^2(\beta + 2)/(\beta + 1)^2 < 0\) at \(t = -\alpha/(\beta + 1)\) (figure 2.74). Hence \(\vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(\vek z\) in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  4. Let \(\vek w\) be any column, \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w\) for \(j < r\) and \(\vek z = \vek w - \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By \(\mat M\)-orthonormality \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek z = c_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = c_{\mathrm{i}} - c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for every \(i < r\), so \(\vek z\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  5. For \(j < r\) we have \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). The matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric, so by lemma 1.101 and the induction assumption \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}})^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}})^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and this is zero because \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\). Also \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), so \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \cdot 0\).
  6. By step 4 \(\vek w = \vek z + \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), so by lemma 1.101, point 2, and steps 3 and 5, for every column \(\vek w\)
    \[\vek w^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek z^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0 .\]
    For \(\vek w = \mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) this is \((\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})^\T(\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = |\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}|^2 = 0\). A sum of squares of entries is zero only if every entry is zero, so \(\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). Step \(r\) is complete.
  7. Since \(W_{\mathrm{r}} \subset W_{\mathrm{r}-1}\), also \(S_{\mathrm{r}} \subset S_{\mathrm{r}-1}\), and the smallest value on the smaller set is not smaller: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}-1} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  8. By lemma 10.12 \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) is nonempty for every \(r \le n\), so all the steps \(r = 1, 2, \dots, n\) can be carried out. This gives \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), the \(\mat M\)-orthonormal \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the equations (10.20).

Without determinants, the proof has shown that every pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(n\) has as many real eigenpairs with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal eigencolumns as there are coordinates: an eigencolumn is the point at which \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) takes the smallest value that the extreme value theorem guarantees, and equation (10.20) follows from its being the smallest. Step 3 is the precise form of the idea of figure 10.9: if \(\mat B\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) were not zero, we could move along the set \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\) to the side where \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) is smaller. For the first eigenpair (\(r = 1\)) \(W_1\) is the set of all columns and step 3 holds for every \(\vek z\), for the later ones only for columns in \(W_{\mathrm{r}}\). Later in this chapter we will use the last sentence of the theorem to estimate eigenvalues: the eigenvalues are the smallest values of the quotient of energies.

From history

Rayleigh, in his treatise Theory of Sound, published in 1877, introduced the fundamental concept of oscillation of a linear system about an equilibrium configuration and showed that eigenmodes and eigenfrequencies exist for discrete and for continuous systems (GR, PDF p. 19). He was concerned with acoustics, not with structural mechanics (GR, PDF p. 19). The pioneers of aeronautics were the first structural designers who had to get vibration under control, because they constantly aimed to reduce the weight of flying structures (GR, PDF p. 19). From the 1920s onwards aeronautical engineers had to admit the importance of the mechanics of vibration for predicting the aeroelastic behaviour of aircraft (GR, PDF p. 19). During the next forty years they had to limit the scope of their analysis to methods that the computational means of the time could handle (GR, PDF p. 19). The structural models were therefore either analytical or described by a small number of degrees of freedom, obtained by transfer (prenosnimi) or Rayleigh-Ritz methods (GR, PDF p. 19). The appearance and spread of computers since 1960 led to a reconsideration of all methods of analysis, and the traditional methods were replaced by matrix methods arising from the discretization of variational expressions (GR, PDF p. 19).

We met the methods of Rayleigh and Ritz in section 6.4.1, and the beginnings of the finite element method in section 7.2.2. Back to the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) and the theorem. For a symmetric \(\mat K\) and a positive definite \(\mat M\) of order \(n\) there are \(n\) real eigenpairs with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal modes, which the proof found one after the other as successive smallest values of the quotient of energies on the sets \(S_{\mathrm{r}}\).

10.3.5 Expansion in modes and the modal matrix

The answer to the fourth question: every column is a sum of modes.

Corollary 10.14 Expansion theorem for any number of coordinates (razvoj po načinih za poljubno število koordinat)

Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as in theorem 10.13. Then:

  1. Every column \(\vek v\) with \(n\) entries is \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the coefficients are unique, \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\).
  2. If \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\) for a real number \(\lambda\) and a column \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), then \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for some \(r\), and \(\vek\varphi\) is a sum of multiples of those \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) for which \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\).
  3. The number of indices \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) does not depend on the choice of columns in the proof of the theorem: every other choice according to the theorem gives the same list of eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\).
Proof

We must show the expansion, then what follows for other eigenpairs, and finally that the list of eigenvalues does not depend on the choice.

  1. Let \(\vek w = \vek v - \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v)\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). By \(\mat M\)-orthonormality \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v - \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for every \(s\). The columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are nonzero, since they are \(\mat M\)-normalized, so by point 3 of corollary 10.11 \(\vek w = \vek 0\). If \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), we multiply from the left by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\) and obtain \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) (again by \(\mat M\)-orthonormality).
  2. By point 1 \(\vek\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi\). Since \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are symmetric, by lemma 1.101 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi = (\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\vek\varphi = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,c_{\mathrm{r}}\), while by assumption \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,c_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda)\,c_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(r\). Since \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), some \(c_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\), and for this \(r\) we have \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Only the \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) can be nonzero.
  3. Let \(E_\lambda\) be the set of solutions of \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\) (including \(\vek 0\)) and \(p\) the number of indices \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\). By step 2 every nonzero element of \(E_\lambda\) is a sum of multiples of the columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\). For \(p = 0\) therefore \(E_\lambda = \{\vek 0\}\).
  4. Let \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{p}'}\) be \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns in \(E_\lambda\). We show \(p' \le p\). For \(p = 0\) this holds, because an \(\mat M\)-normalized column is not \(\vek 0\). Let \(p \ge 1\) and suppose \(p' > p\). By step 3 \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\), a sum over the \(p\) indices \(j\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\). The system \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,x_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) has \(p\) equations and \(p' > p\) unknowns, so by lemma 10.9 there is \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) for which \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{ji}}\,x_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\). This contradicts point 1 of corollary 10.11. Hence \(p' \le p\).
  5. Let another choice of columns in the proof of the theorem give the eigenvalues \(\lambda'_1 \le \dots \le \lambda'_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the columns \(\vek\varphi'_{\mathrm{r}}\). The columns \(\vek\varphi'_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal and lie in \(E_\lambda\), so by step 4 there are at most \(p\) of them. The other choice satisfies the theorem as well, so steps 1 to 4 also hold with the roles of the two choices exchanged, and there are at least \(p\) of them. Every real number therefore appears equally often in both lists. Both lists have \(n\) members and are in increasing order, so they are equal.

Source: CK (10.33) and (10.34) (PDF p. 313): "An expansion theorem can be stated for MDOF systems", GR (PDF p. 93) for the choice of \(\mat M\)-orthonormal modes for a repeated eigenvalue. The proof is not from a source.

The corollary says that there are no more than \(n\) eigenvalues and that we find them nowhere else than in the list of the theorem. For a repeated eigenvalue the choice of modes is not unique: for \(\mat K = k\mat I\) and \(\mat M = m\mat I\) we have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = k/m\) for every \(r\), and every \(\mat M\)-orthonormal set of columns is a valid choice. The list of eigenvalues, however, is uniquely determined (point 3).

Definition 10.15 Modal matrix (modalna matrika)

Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as in theorem 10.13. The modal matrix (modalna matrika) \(\mat\Phi\) is the matrix of order \(n\) whose columns are \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\mat\Phi = [\vek\varphi_1\ \dots\ \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}]\). We write \(\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}})\) for the diagonal matrix with the entries \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) on the diagonal.

Lemma 10.16 Properties of the modal matrix (lastnosti modalne matrike)

For the modal matrix \(\mat\Phi\), \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Phi = \mat I\), \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}})\), the matrix \(\mat\Phi\) is invertible and \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\).

Proof

We must show two equalities, invertibility and the form of the inverse matrix. The equalities are a statement about the entries, invertibility follows from lemma 8.21.

  1. The entry \((r, s)\) of the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Phi\) is \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) (definition 1.98), which by \(\mat M\)-orthonormality is \(1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) for \(r \ne s\), the entry \((r, s)\) of the identity matrix.
  2. The entry \((r, s)\) of the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Phi\) is by (10.20) \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), which by step 1 is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) for \(r \ne s\).
  3. Let \(\mat\Phi\,\vek w = \vek 0\). By step 1 and lemma 1.99, point 1, then \(\vek w = \mat I\,\vek w = (\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Phi)\,\vek w = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Phi\,\vek w) = \vek 0\). By lemma 8.21 \(\mat\Phi\) is invertible, with the inverse matrix \(\mat\Phi^{-1}\), for which \(\mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat I\).
  4. By step 1 and lemma 1.99, point 2, \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M = (\mat\Phi^\T\mat M)\,(\mat\Phi\,\mat\Phi^{-1}) = (\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Phi)\,\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{-1}\).

Corollary 10.17 Spectral theorem for a symmetric matrix (spektralni izrek za simetrično matriko)

We must show that this is theorem 10.13 and corollary 10.14 for a special choice.

  2. For \(\mat K = \mat A\) and \(\mat M = \mat I\), \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is ordinary perpendicularity, \(\mat M\)-normalization is length \(1\), and the theorem gives the statement on the eigenpairs. Corollary 10.14 gives the expansion.

10.3.6 The eigenvalues are real

The theorem speaks of real columns and real numbers \(\lambda\). Could some complex pair \((\lambda, \vek\varphi)\) show a vibration that the theorem does not cover? We introduced complex numbers in section 9.3: a complex column has complex entries, \(\vek\varphi = \vek a + \mathrm i\,\vek b\) with real columns \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\). We compute the product of a real matrix with a complex column by equation (1.38), with complex numbers in place of real ones.

Lemma 10.18 The eigenvalues are real (lastne vrednosti so realne)

Let \(\mat K\) be symmetric and \(\mat M\) symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), both with real entries. If for a complex number \(\lambda\) and a complex column \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\), then \(\lambda\) is a real number.

Proof

We must show that the imaginary part of \(\lambda\) is zero. We split the equation into its real and imaginary parts and eliminate the same expression by the symmetry of the matrices.

  1. Let \(\lambda = \sigma + \mathrm i\tau\) and \(\vek\varphi = \vek a + \mathrm i\,\vek b\). Since the matrices are real, \(\mat M\,\vek\varphi = \mat M\,\vek a + \mathrm i\,\mat M\,\vek b\), and by the rules of arithmetic (lemma 9.20) \(\lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi = (\sigma\,\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\mat M\,\vek b) + \mathrm i\,(\tau\,\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek b)\), and \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = \mat K\,\vek a + \mathrm i\,\mat K\,\vek b\) (the matrix is real). The real parts and the imaginary parts are equal: \(\mat K\,\vek a = \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\mat M\,\vek b\) and \(\mat K\,\vek b = \tau\,\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\mat M\,\vek b\).
  2. We multiply the first equation from the left by \(\vek b^\T\), the second by \(\vek a^\T\): \(\vek b^\T\mat K\,\vek a = \sigma\,\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek a - \tau\,\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b\) and \(\vek a^\T\mat K\,\vek b = \tau\,\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \sigma\,\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek b\).
  3. The matrices are symmetric, so \(\vek b^\T\mat K\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\mat K\,\vek b\) and \(\vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek b\) (equation (10.19)). When we subtract the second equation of step 2 from the first, the left sides and the terms with \(\sigma\) cancel: \(0 = -\tau\,(\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b)\).
  4. Since \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\), \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) or \(\vek b \ne \vek 0\). Since \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek a^\T\mat M\,\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat M\,\vek b > 0\), hence \(\tau = 0\).

Source: CM (2.5.34) to (2.5.36) (PDF pp. 92-93) proves the same for a symmetric matrix (\(\mat M = \mat I\)) with complex conjugate pairs. The proof above with real and imaginary parts is not from a source.

Meaning: we look for solutions \(\vek q = \mathrm e^{st}\vek\varphi\) of \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with complex \(s\). Since \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\mathrm e^{st} = s\,\mathrm e^{st}\) and \(\mathrm e^{st} \ne 0\) (theorem 9.28, points 2 and 3), we need \((s^2\mat M + \mat K)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), hence \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi = -s^2\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\). By the lemma \(\lambda = -s^2\) is real. With \(s = \sigma + \mathrm i\tau\) we have \(s^2 = \sigma^2 - \tau^2 + 2\mathrm i\,\sigma\tau\), so \(\sigma\tau = 0\): \(s\) is real or purely imaginary. For \(\lambda > 0\), \(s = \pm\mathrm i\sqrt\lambda\), a pure vibration. For \(\lambda = 0\), \(s = 0\). For \(\lambda < 0\), \(s = \pm\sqrt{-\lambda}\) is real, growth or decay without vibration. So an undamped system has no solution \(\mathrm e^{st}\vek\varphi\) with an \(s\) whose real and imaginary parts are both nonzero. With damping, later in this chapter, this will change.

The spectral theorem has thus answered all four questions from the beginning of the section. A pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(n\) has \(n\) real eigenpairs, the modes are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal, every column is their sum, and the eigenvalues are successive minima of the quotient of energies. In the next section we use them to decouple the equations of motion, and later in the chapter we estimate the eigenvalues without computing them.
