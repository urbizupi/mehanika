Chapter 16 Torsion

16.3 Prandtl's function

In section 16.2 we led the twisting of a prism to the warping function \(\psi(y, z)\), harmonic in the cross section \(S\) with a boundary condition on its derivative (theorem 16.18). What interests us above all is where in the section the stresses are large and how large a moment it carries. Is there a function from which the stresses can be read directly, and which has a simpler condition on the boundary than \(\psi\)? Such a condition would be the value of the function on the boundary, not its derivative. We shall call this function Prandtl's and use it in this section to solve the ellipse, the equilateral triangle and, once more, the circle.

We take the stresses from section 16.2. For small twists and Hooke's law (theorem 3.19), by lemma 16.16 only two shear stresses are nonzero. They are expressed with the shear modulus \(G\) (definition 3.17), the twist per unit length \(\alpha\) and the warping function:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 . \tag{16.25}\]

Here \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}}\) mean the partial derivatives \(\partial\psi/\partial y\) and \(\partial\psi/\partial z\). No stress depends on \(x\), so the equilibrium equation in the direction \(x\) (theorem 2.31, without body force) remains the only one:

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial z} = 0 . \tag{16.26}\]

This equation connects only two stresses. We shall find one function that carries both of them, as in section 4.7.8 we looked for a function from its derivatives. But first let us see what the free lateral surface demands of the stresses.

Take the section \(S\) and draw it together with the axes \(y\) and \(z\) [figure 16.28]. The outline of the section is the curve that bounds it, and we call it the boundary. On the boundary we choose a point \(P\). In it the outward normal \(\vek n\) is the unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) that points out of the section and is perpendicular to the boundary, and the tangent is the line that touches the boundary at \(P\). Next to the point \(P\) a small piece of material lies on the lateral surface of the prism. The surface carries no load, since it is free (definition 16.1), so the stress vector on it is zero. By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) the component of this vector in the direction of the bar's axis, for the normal \((0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) of the lateral surface, equals \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and therefore

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 \quad \text{on the boundary.} \tag{16.27}\]

This is the scalar product of the pair \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) with the normal \(\vek n\). The shear stress \(\boldsymbol\tau\) is this pair, drawn as an arrow in the plane of the section. If the scalar product is zero, it is perpendicular to \(\vek n\), so it lies along the tangent. An oblique arrow would have a component along the normal, and the force in the direction of the bar's axis would then have to be carried by a lateral surface that holds nothing. Such stresses do not exist on a free boundary: the shear stress on the boundary is always tangent to the boundary.

The equilibrium equation (16.26) and the boundary condition (16.27) speak of both stresses together. Let \(\Phi\) be a function whose derivatives are the stresses. If \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\), the left side of equation (16.26) equals \(\Phi_{\mathrm{zy}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which is zero because the mixed derivatives of a smooth function are equal. Equilibrium is then satisfied by itself, and the boundary condition becomes simple: the tangent to the boundary has the direction \(\vek t = (-n_{\mathrm{z}}, n_{\mathrm{y}})\) (the normal turned by a right angle counterclockwise), so the derivative of \(\Phi\) along the boundary is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} t_{\mathrm{y}} + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} t_{\mathrm{z}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} n_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\), which by equation (16.27) is zero. The function \(\Phi\) does not change its value along the boundary.

How do we picture \(\Phi\)? As the height of a hill above the section, with height zero at the boundary (figure 16.39, explained in detail for the ellipse). The stress at each point runs along the line of constant height, just as a path along a contour line of a map stays at the same height, and it is larger the steeper the hill is there, since its length equals the slope, that is the length of the gradient. Where the hill is flat the stress is small, where it is steep it is large. How much moment the section carries can therefore be read from the volume of the hill, as follows.

Definition 16.22 Prandtl stress function (Prandtlova funkcija)

Let \(S\) be the section of a prism in Saint-Venant torsion (definition 16.1) with the stresses (16.25). A function \(\Phi : S \to \mathbb{R}\) with values in N/mm, twice continuously differentiable in the interior of the section, is the Prandtl stress function (Prandtlova funkcija) of the torsion if at every point of the section

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y} . \tag{16.28}\]

The function assigns one number to every point of the section. Its values are a stress multiplied by a length, so we measure them in N/mm.

Explanation

It is named after Prandtl, who in 1903 showed that the same equations hold for it as for a membrane under pressure (section 16.4). It is called a stress function because the stresses are obtained by differentiation. The letter \(\Phi\) has several meanings in the book: \(\varphi\) is the rotation of a section (chapter 13) and Airy's function (chapter 11), and the capital \(\Phi\) is a local name for a mapping in the proofs of chapter 4 and in section 16.1. SADD writes \(\phi\) for Prandtl's function. From here on, \(\Phi\) in this chapter means only this function.

Source: SADD section 9.3.1, equation (9.3.8) (PDF p. 248), the name after Prandtl and the year 1903 in section 9.3.4 (PDF p. 254). SADD has the bar's axis \(z\), the section in the plane \(x, y\) and the twist \(\alpha\), we have the axis \(x\) and the section in the plane \(y, z\): SADD's \((x, y)\) is our \((y, z)\) and its \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) is our \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) with the sign according to our axes.

Does such a function exist at all for the stresses (16.25)? From equilibrium it follows that it does. The theorem on a field from partial derivatives (theorem 4.115) is exactly this step: if the mixed derivatives of a pair of functions agree and the region is simply connected (definition 1.142), the pair comes from one function. A section with a hole, such as a tube, does not guarantee this. So in this section we restrict ourselves to sections without holes and leave tubes for section 16.8.

We look at the difference in a figure (figure 16.29) with the axes \(y\) and \(z\) of the section plane. On the left is a section without a hole and in it a loop that we can shrink to a point without leaving the section. On the right is a tube with a hole, and a loop around the hole does not shrink, since it would have to cross the hole.

The proof of the lemma takes place in a cylinder over the section (figure 16.30). We draw the cylinder \(D\) standing over the open set \(E\), and in it the section \(S\) with its boundary, where the function will be constant. In the cylinder we take a segment along the axis \(x\), on which the function \(\varphi\) will be constant, and a loop that we shrink. At the lower right are two functions with the same derivatives that differ by a constant.

Lemma 16.23 Existence of the Prandtl function (obstoj Prandtlove funkcije)

Let \(S\) be a bounded section whose interior is connected and simply connected (definition 1.142) and whose boundary is one closed curve with a piecewise continuously differentiable tangent, let the warping function \(\psi\) of theorem 16.18 be twice continuously differentiable on an open set \(E\) that contains \(S\) and is connected (definition 1.136) and simply connected (for the circle, the ellipse and the triangle \(\psi\) is a polynomial and \(E\) is the whole plane, for the rectangle see section 16.5), and let \(G\alpha \ne 0\). Then:

  1. A Prandtl function \(\Phi\) of the section exists (definition 16.22), twice continuously differentiable on \(E\).
  2. The function \(\Phi\) is determined up to an added constant, and it has a constant value on the boundary.
  3. Since the stresses contain only derivatives, we can choose \(\Phi = 0\) on the boundary.
Idea

We show that the field \((0, -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\) satisfies the condition of the theorem on a field from derivatives, because that condition is equilibrium (16.26). Constancy on the boundary is an expression of the boundary condition (16.27).

Proof
  1. Let \(D = E \times \mathbb{R}\) be the cylinder over the set \(E\). It is an open and connected set. It is also simply connected: a loop in \(D\) is contracted by contracting its projection into \(E\) by definition 1.142 and at the same time pulling the coordinate \(x\) to a constant value.
  2. The field \(\vek w = (0, -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\) is continuously differentiable on the cylinder \(D\), since the stresses (16.25) have continuous first derivatives (\(\psi\) has continuous second ones). Because it does not depend on \(x\), the derivatives with respect to \(x\) are zero. The condition of theorem 4.115 is the equality of mixed derivatives, \(\partial w_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z = \partial w_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y\), and the other pairs are zero. The first two are \(-\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z\) and \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y\) and they are equal exactly by equation (16.26). By the theorem a continuously differentiable function \(\varphi\) exists on \(D\) with \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial z = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = 0\).
  3. The derivative with respect to \(x\) is zero on every segment along the axis \(x\) in \(D\), so by corollary 1.86 \(\varphi\) is constant along it and is a function of \((y, z)\) only. We call it \(\Phi\). Since the first derivatives \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are continuously differentiable on \(E\), \(\Phi\) is twice continuously differentiable on \(E\), and so also at the points of the boundary of the section. Equation (16.28) holds.
  4. If \(\tilde\Phi\) is another such function on \(E\), the difference \(\tilde\Phi - \Phi\) has both derivatives zero on the connected set \(E\), so it is constant.
  5. On the boundary, by what was written before the definition, \(\Phi\) has the derivative along the tangent \(\vek t\) equal to \(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} t_{\mathrm{y}} + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} t_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) by equation (16.27). The boundary is one connected curve, and a function with zero derivative along it is constant. Point 3 follows, because \(\Phi - \Phi_{\text{boundary}}\) gives the same stresses.

Source: SADD section 9.3.1 (PDF p. 248-249): at (9.3.8) and (9.3.12) it says that the function is constant on the boundary and that the constant is chosen to be zero. The proof of existence with the theorem on a field from derivatives is not from the source. The existence of the warping function for a general section is stated by SADD and we do not prove it (section 16.2).

What equation does \(\Phi\) satisfy in the interior? Equilibrium is already used up. What remains is a requirement that the warping function satisfies by itself, but \(\Phi\) must be given it: the shear strains must come from one displacement. From equations (16.25),

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial z} = G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{zy}} + 1 - \psi_{\mathrm{yz}} + 1) = 2G\alpha , \tag{16.29}\]

since the mixed derivatives of \(\psi\) are equal. The left side does not contain \(\psi\), so this is a condition on the stresses themselves. We call it the compatibility condition (pogoj kompatibilnosti) of torsion, since like the compatibility equations (theorem 1.133) it ensures that the strains come from one displacement. Written with \(\Phi\) it is the equation of the theorem that follows.

What does equation (16.29) say, shown on a figure? Around a small square of the section in the axes \(y\) and \(z\) [figure 16.31] we go round the loop and add the stress along the sides. On the lower and left side the stress has the value at the corner, on the upper and right side it differs by the derivative times the side length, on the upper one with the opposite sign because the circuit runs against the axis \(y\) there. So the sum does not cancel, and what remains is the difference of derivatives times the area. The equation therefore says: the circulation of the stress around every small square is \(2G\alpha\,\Delta A\), the same everywhere, since it is twice the rotation rate \(\alpha\) of the material, multiplied by \(G\). If \(\Phi\) did not satisfy this, the strains would not come from one displacement.

We look at the moment in a figure (figure 16.32). At a point of the section two components of the shear stress act, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) along \(y\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) along \(z\). Their arms about the axis \(x\) are \(z\) and \(y\). The position vector \((y, z)\) points from the axis to the point, and the gradient \(\nabla\Phi\) points toward the top of the hill, perpendicular to the stress.

How do we compute from \(\Phi\) the moment that the section carries? The moment is the sum of the moments of the shear forces of the section about the axis \(x\). The force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) at the point \((y, z)\) has the arm \(y\) and the force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) the arm \(z\) with the opposite sign, so

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \big( y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\mathrm{S}} \big( y\,\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{16.30}\]

The expression \(y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the scalar product of the position vector \((y, z)\) with the gradient \(\nabla\Phi = (\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\). This product is a part of the divergence of the field \(\Phi\,(y, z)\). The divergence theorem (theorem 4.14, section 4.2.5) turns the divergence into an integral over the boundary, where \(\Phi = 0\). Since the section is a figure in the plane, we need the theorem in the plane, lemma 16.17, which we proved in section 16.2.

Why is the moment exactly twice the volume? Cut the hill into thin layers between heights \(h\) and \(h + \Delta h\) (figure 16.39). A layer is a strip around the level line of height \(h\), which encloses the area \(A(h)\). The strip has width \(\Delta n\), the stress in it is tangent and of size \(\Delta h/\Delta n\) (the slope of the hill), so the force on a piece of the strip of length \(\Delta s\) is \((\Delta h/\Delta n)\,\Delta n\,\Delta s = \Delta h\,\Delta s\). Its moment about the axis is \(\Delta h\) times the arm \(r_\perp\,\Delta s\), and the sum around the closed level line is \(\Delta h\sum r_\perp\,\Delta s = 2A(h)\,\Delta h\), because \(r_\perp\,\Delta s\) is twice the area of the narrow wedge from the axis to the piece of line. Adding all layers gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_0^{\Phi_{\max}}A(h)\,\mathrm{d}h\), and \(\int A(h)\,\mathrm{d}h\) is the volume under the hill. The factor 2 is thus twice the wedge area, not a computational extra. The same result without the divergence follows in the theorem.

Now we have everything for the theorem. It contains three claims: the equation for \(\Phi\), the moment, and a converse which says that every solution of the equation really is a torsion.

For the proof we look at a figure (figure 16.33). On the left is the section \(S\) and in it a vertical strip, on the right the height of the hill \(\Phi\) along the strip, the profile. At the ends of the strip the section is at its boundary and \(\Phi = 0\), so the sum of increments along the strip is zero. At the top the profile curves downward: the curvature \(\Phi_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is negative, and together with \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}}\) the sum equals \(-2G\alpha\).

Theorem 16.24 Prandtl's equation (Prandtlova enačba)

Let \(S\) be a section as in lemma 16.23, and let \(\Phi\) be the Prandtl function of the section with the value zero on the boundary, twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\) (for a rectangle this does not happen at the corners, section 16.5).

  1. The curvature of the hill is the same everywhere:
    \[\nabla^2\Phi = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2} = -2G\alpha \quad \text{in } S , \qquad \Phi = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial S . \tag{16.31}\]
  2. The resultant of the forces of the section is zero, and its moment about the axis \(x\) is twice the volume under the hill:
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} \Phi\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{16.32}\]
    So \(J = (2/G\alpha)\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\) is the torsion constant of theorem 16.18, \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\alpha\).
  3. Conversely, let \(\Phi\) be a twice continuously differentiable function with the properties (16.31). Then the field (16.28) satisfies equilibrium and the free lateral surface, and a warping function \(\psi\) exists (harmonic, with the boundary condition of theorem 16.18) that gives the same stresses.
Idea

The first claim is the compatibility condition (16.29), written with \(\Phi\). The second is the divergence theorem in the section, because the boundary term cancels owing to \(\Phi = 0\). The converse goes by the same theorem on a field from derivatives, this time for \(\psi\).

Proof
  1. From equation (16.28), \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial y = -\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial z = \Phi_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The left side of equation (16.29) is therefore \(-\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nabla^2\Phi\) and equals \(2G\alpha\). The boundary condition is point 3 of lemma 16.23.
  2. The force in the direction \(y\): \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). By lemma 16.17 with the field \(\vek v = (0, \Phi)\) this is \(\oint\Phi n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), because \(\Phi = 0\) on the boundary. Similarly with the field \((\Phi, 0)\) for the force in the direction \(z\): \(-\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -\oint\Phi n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). There are no normal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so there is no force in the direction \(x\). Since the shear resultants are zero too, the moments about \(y\) and \(z\) are zero as well (as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 16.18).
  3. The field \(\vek v = (y\,\Phi, z\,\Phi)\) has the divergence \(\Phi + y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + \Phi + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\Phi + y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\). By lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}(2\Phi + y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint\Phi\,(y\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), since \(\Phi = 0\) on the boundary.
  4. By equation (16.30), \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\). The same moment for \(\psi\) equals \(GJ\alpha\) (theorem 16.18), whence \(J\).
  5. The converse. Let \(\Phi\) be as in point 3 of the theorem. The field \(\vek w = (0, -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}) = (0, \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\) is by construction the gradient of the function \(\Phi\), so equilibrium (16.26) is satisfied (mixed derivatives of \(\Phi\)). The derivative of \(\Phi\) along the boundary is zero, because \(\Phi = 0\) on the boundary. This derivative is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{y}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\), so (16.27) holds.
  6. We look for \(\psi\) with \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}/(G\alpha) + z\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}/(G\alpha) - y\) (equations (16.25) rewritten). The mixed derivatives: \(\partial_{\mathrm{z}}\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{zz}}/(G\alpha) + 1\) and \(\partial_{\mathrm{y}}\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}}/(G\alpha) - 1\). They are equal exactly when \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} + \Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = -2G\alpha\), which holds. By theorem 4.115 (as in lemma 16.23) \(\psi\) therefore exists. For \(\psi\) we have \(\psi_{\mathrm{yy}} + \psi_{\mathrm{zz}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{zy}}/(G\alpha) - \Phi_{\mathrm{yz}}/(G\alpha) = 0\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = (\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{y}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}})/(G\alpha) + zn_{\mathrm{y}} - yn_{\mathrm{z}} = zn_{\mathrm{y}} - yn_{\mathrm{z}}\), since \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{y}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the derivative along the boundary and is zero.

Source: SADD section 9.3.1, (9.3.9), (9.3.12), (9.3.15)-(9.3.18) (PDF p. 248-251). The converse and the use of the theorem on a field from derivatives are not from the source.

We proved the theorem when \(\Phi\) is twice continuously differentiable up to the boundary. For the circle, the ellipse and the triangle, which we compute in this section, the solutions are polynomials and the condition holds. The rectangle has corners. Along the side \(y = a\) we have \(\Phi = 0\), so there \(\Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} = -2G\alpha\), while along the side \(z = b\) we have \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). The second derivative \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}}\) therefore has two different limits at the corner. The mixed derivative \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yz}}\) is not even bounded near the corner: the series of section 16.5 gives on the diagonal, at the distances \(0{,}1\), \(0{,}01\) and \(0{,}001\) mm from the corner, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yz}}/(G\alpha) = 5{,}72\), \(8{,}65\) and \(11{,}58\), larger by \((4/\pi)\ln 10 = 2{,}93\) for each ten times smaller distance. So at the corner \(\Phi\) is not twice continuously differentiable, and the proof with the divergence theorem would need a cut-out near the corner and a limit. For sections with corners we do not prove it here. SADD (PDF p. 248-251) uses Green's theorem without this condition. The rectangle will be solved in section 16.5 with a series and with checked numbers.

We look at this in a figure (figure 16.34). On the left is the corner with the vertical and the horizontal side, along which \(\Phi = 0\), and the diagonal toward the corner. On the right is the graph of the mixed derivative along the diagonal: at every ten times smaller offset it grows by a constant step, so it is unbounded.

Is the solution of equation (16.31) unique? If there were several, the same torsion would have several stress fields. We show that this is not so, by energy, as in chapter 11 we proved the uniqueness of the boundary value problem.

We draw what two solutions would mean (figure 16.35). On the right are the solutions \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) along a line through the section, and since they have the same boundary, both are zero at the ends. The difference is zero there. On the left are the axes \(y\) and \(z\), the boundary, a disc around a point \(P\) at which the slope of the difference would be nonzero, the field \(w\,\nabla w\), which vanishes at the boundary, and a path from \(P\) to the boundary.

Lemma 16.25 Uniqueness of Prandtl's equation (enoličnost Prandtlove enačbe)

Let \(S\) be a section whose interior is connected and for which lemma 16.17 holds. Let \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) be functions, twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\), with \(\nabla^2f_1 = \nabla^2f_2 = -2G\alpha\) in \(S\) and \(f_1 = f_2 = 0\) on \(\partial S\). Then \(f_1 = f_2\).

Idea

The difference \(w = f_1 - f_2\) is harmonic and zero on the boundary. The energy \(\int|\nabla w|^2\) is then zero.

Proof
  1. Let \(w = f_1 - f_2\). Then \(\nabla^2w = 0\) in \(S\) and \(w = 0\) on the boundary.
  2. The field \(\vek v = w\,\nabla w = (w\,w_{\mathrm{y}}, w\,w_{\mathrm{z}})\) is continuously differentiable and has the divergence \(w_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + w\,\nabla^2w = |\nabla w|^2\) (product rule). By lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint w\,(w_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + w_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), since \(w = 0\) on the boundary.
  3. A continuous nonnegative function \(|\nabla w|^2\) with zero integral is zero everywhere (if it were positive at some point, it would be positive in a disc around it and the integral would be positive). So \(\nabla w = 0\).
  4. A function with zero gradient on a connected set is constant, and on the boundary \(w = 0\), so \(w = 0\).

Source: The same energy argument for the Laplace operator is in CM section 7.5.7 (PDF p. 340-342). The proof is not from the source.

The lemma means that for a given section there is at most one Prandtl function with zero on the boundary. If we find it by any means (by guessing, or by a series when it is twice continuously differentiable up to the boundary), it is the right one. For the rectangle, where this fails at the corners, see section 16.5. The stress field therefore does not depend on how we found \(\Phi\). The displacement field too is by theorem 11.4 unique with it, up to a rigid motion.

Let us try this on the ellipse, where \(\Phi\) can be guessed. Let the section have the semi-axis \(a\) along \(y\) and \(b\) along \(z\). The boundary is the curve \(y^2/a^2 + z^2/b^2 = 1\), and the function \(1 - y^2/a^2 - z^2/b^2\) is zero on it and positive in the interior. Take \(\Phi = c\,(1 - y^2/a^2 - z^2/b^2)\) with a constant \(c\). The boundary has the value zero for every \(c\), and we choose \(c\) so that \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\). Let us first draw this function [figure 16.36]. Above the boundary of the section we draw the profile along the axis \(y\), a parabola that falls from the top to zero at \(y = \pm a\), and the profile along the axis \(z\), which falls to zero at \(z = \pm b\). The height \(c\) is the top of the hill.

The volume under the hill is written in the theorem as \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\). We can compute it by the change of variables theorem (theorem 4.59): we write a point of the section as \((y, z) = (a\,u,\ b\,v)\), where \((u, v)\) lies in the disc \(K\) of radius \(1\) centred at the origin. This linear mapping stretches the disc by the factor \(a\) along \(y\) and by the factor \(b\) along \(z\), so it maps it onto the ellipse, and it is injective on the whole plane. Its Jacobian determinant is constant, \(a\,b\): every piece of the disc is mapped into a piece of the ellipse with an \(ab\) times larger area.

We draw the mapping (figure 16.37). On the left is the disc \(K\) of radius \(1\). On the right is the ellipse \(S\) onto which it is mapped by \((u, v) \mapsto (a\,u,\ b\,v)\): along \(y\) we stretch it by \(a\) and along \(z\) by \(b\). A small piece of the disc goes into a piece of the ellipse with an \(ab\) times larger area. On the ellipse there is also the point \((a, 0)\), where the stress will be largest, and an inner ellipse on which \(\Phi\) is constant.

Theorem 16.26 Torsion of an ellipse (torzija elipse)

Let the section be an ellipse with semi-axes \(a\) along the axis \(y\) and \(b\) along the axis \(z\), \(a \le b\), and let \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the moment.

  1. The Prandtl function of the ellipse with zero on the boundary is
    \[\Phi = G\alpha\,\frac{a^2b^2}{a^2 + b^2}\Big( 1 - \frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{z^2}{b^2} \Big) . \tag{16.33}\]
  2. From the volume of the hill we get the torsion constant:
    \[J = \frac{\pi\,a^3b^3}{a^2 + b^2} , \qquad \alpha = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{GJ} . \tag{16.34}\]
  3. The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -2M_{\mathrm{x}} z/(\pi a b^3)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2M_{\mathrm{x}} y/(\pi a^3 b)\), the largest is at the ends of the shorter axis and equals
    \[\tau_{\max} = \frac{2M_{\mathrm{x}}}{\pi\,a^2b} . \tag{16.35}\]
  4. The warping function is \(\psi = k\,yz\) with \(k = (b^2 - a^2)/(a^2 + b^2)\).
  5. For \(a = b = R\) this agrees with the circle: \(J = \pi R^4/2\) and \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\).
Idea

The function is a polynomial, so it has a constant Laplace operator and zero on the boundary. The constant \(c\) is chosen from the Laplace value, \(J\) from the moment, the stresses from the derivatives. By lemma 16.25 this is the only solution.

Proof
  1. Let \(\Phi = c\,(1 - y^2/a^2 - z^2/b^2)\). On the boundary \(\Phi = 0\). The second derivatives are \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} = -2c/a^2\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = -2c/b^2\), so \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2c\,(a^2 + b^2)/(a^2b^2)\). The condition \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) gives \(c = G\alpha\,a^2b^2/(a^2 + b^2)\). This is equation (16.33). By lemma 16.25 this is the function of the ellipse, and by theorem 16.24 (point 3) it belongs to a torsion of the section.
  2. We compute the integrals over the ellipse with the mapping \((u, v) \mapsto (au, bv)\) of the introduction and with lemma 16.2 for the disc \(K\) of radius \(R = 1\). The area: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{d}A = ab\int_{\mathrm{K}}\mathrm{d}A = \pi ab\), since \(K\) has the area \(\pi\) (the proof of lemma 16.2 with the function \(1\) in place of \(r^2\), where \(\int_0^1 r\,\mathrm{d}r = 1/2\)). The term \(y^2 = a^2u^2\) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = a^3b\int_{\mathrm{K}}u^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \pi a^3b/4\) and similarly \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = ab^3\int_{\mathrm{K}}v^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \pi ab^3/4\).
  3. Hence \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = c\,(\pi ab - \pi ab/4 - \pi ab/4) = c\,\pi ab/2\) and by equation (16.32) \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = c\,\pi ab = G\alpha\,\pi a^3b^3/(a^2 + b^2)\). This is \(GJ\alpha\) with \(J\) from equation (16.34).
  4. From \(c = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(\pi ab)\) we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = -2cz/b^2 = -2M_{\mathrm{x}}z/(\pi ab^3)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} = 2cy/a^2 = 2M_{\mathrm{x}}y/(\pi a^3b)\). The magnitude: \(\tau^2 = (2M_{\mathrm{x}}/\pi ab)^2\,(y^2/a^4 + z^2/b^4)\).
  5. The maximum is on the boundary: with \(y = a\cos\theta\), \(z = b\sin\theta\) we have \(y^2/a^4 + z^2/b^4 = \cos^2\theta/a^2 + \sin^2\theta/b^2\), which for \(a \le b\) is largest at \(\cos^2\theta = 1\), at the point \((a, 0)\). There \(\tau = 2M_{\mathrm{x}}/(\pi ab)\cdot 1/a\), equation (16.35). An interior point has a smaller stress, since \(y^2/a^4 + z^2/b^4\) under the ellipse \(y^2/a^2 + z^2/b^2 = s^2 \le 1\) is at most \(s^2/a^2\).
  6. The warping function: from equations (16.25), \(G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z) = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = -2G\alpha\,a^2z/(a^2 + b^2)\) and \(G\alpha\,(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y) = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} = 2G\alpha\,b^2y/(a^2 + b^2)\). With \(\psi = k\,yz\) we have \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z = (k - 1)z\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y = (k + 1)y\), so \(k - 1 = -2a^2/(a^2 + b^2)\) and \(k + 1 = 2b^2/(a^2 + b^2)\), both satisfied with \(k = (b^2 - a^2)/(a^2 + b^2)\). SymPy checked them.
  7. For \(a = b = R\) we have \(k = 0\), \(J = \pi R^6/(2R^2) = \pi R^4/2\) and \(\tau^2 = (2M_{\mathrm{x}}/\pi R^2)^2\,r^2/R^4\), so \(\tau = 2M_{\mathrm{x}}r/(\pi R^4) = M_{\mathrm{x}}r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) (lemma 16.2). This is theorem 16.3.

Source: SADD example 9.1 (PDF p. 257-258) and ONATE2 figure 4.11 (PDF p. 201): \(J = \pi a^3b^3/(a^2 + b^2)\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}} = 2M/(\pi a b^2)\) for ONATE's larger semi-axis \(a\) and smaller \(b\), which are our \(b\) and \(a\). SADD has the semi-axes \(a\) along \(x\) and \(b\) along \(y\) and the validity \(a > b\). The proof with the uniqueness lemma and the linear mapping of the disc is not from the source.

We draw the warping function of the ellipse (figure 16.38). On the left \(\psi = k\,yz\) has alternating signs in the quadrants, it is constant on the hyperbolas \(yz = \text{constant}\), and the displacement is largest at the boundary point at \(t = 45°\). On the right is the circle, where \(k = 0\) and \(\psi = 0\).

Example 16.27 Ellipse 10 by 20 mm (elipsa 10 × 20 mm)

For an ellipse with \(a = 10\) mm along \(y\) and \(b = 20\) mm along \(z\) and a steel bar (\(G = 75\,385\) MPa) at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m: \(J = 50\,265\) mm\(^4\), \(\alpha = 0{,}01320\) rad/m, \(G\alpha = 0{,}9947\) N/mm\(^3\), the top of the hill \(c = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(\pi ab) = 79{,}58\) N/mm. By equation (16.35) the largest stress is \(\tau_{\max} = 15{,}92\) MPa. The section bulges: the displacement along the bar's axis is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\psi = \alpha k\,yz\) with \(k = 0{,}600\) (point 4 of the theorem). On the boundary \(yz = \tfrac12 ab\sin 2t\) at the point \((a\cos t,\ b\sin t)\), so the displacement is largest at \(t = 45°\), at the point \((7{,}07;\ 14{,}14)\) mm, and there \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}792\ \mu\)m.

Let us look at where the stress flows in this ellipse [figure 16.39]. We draw the axes \(y\) and \(z\) and the boundary of the ellipse, where \(\Phi = 0\). Inside we draw the lines on which the function (16.33) has a constant value. Each is an ellipse similar to the boundary, with the equation \(y^2/a^2 + z^2/b^2 = s^2\), and on it \(\Phi = c\,(1 - s^2)\). They are drawn for \(s = 0{,}75\), \(0{,}5\) and \(0{,}25\), where \(\Phi\) equals \(7/16\), \(3/4\) and \(15/16\) of the value \(c\). In the center \(\Phi = c\). The stress \(\boldsymbol\tau = (\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}, -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}})\) by equation (16.28) we draw as an arrow, \(0{,}1\) cm per MPa. At the end of the shorter axis, at \((a, 0)\), it is \(15{,}92\) MPa long. At the end of the longer axis, at \((0, b)\), it is half as long, \(7{,}96\) MPa. At three interior points it is shorter, since \(y\) and \(z\) are smaller there. At one point we also draw the gradient \(\nabla\Phi = (\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}, \Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\), the vector that points in the direction of steepest ascent of the function: toward the top, perpendicular to the stress, and of the same length.

The drawn lines show that the stress always runs along a line where \(\Phi\) is constant, and that the gradient is perpendicular to it. Such lines are called level lines (plastnice), like the lines of equal height on a map: they join points on the same layer of terrain (in Slovenian plast means layer), and the boundary of the section is the level line with the value zero. We saw the pattern on the ellipse, now we show that it holds everywhere.

Lemma 16.28 Stress along the level lines (napetost vzdolž plastnic)

Let \(\Phi\) be the Prandtl function of the section and \(P\) a point at which \(\nabla\Phi \ne \vek 0\). Then:

  1. The shear stress \(\boldsymbol\tau = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) at \(P\) is perpendicular to the gradient \(\nabla\Phi\).
  2. Its magnitude is \(|\boldsymbol\tau| = |\nabla\Phi|\).
  3. The stress is tangent to the level line \(\{\Phi = \text{constant}\}\) through \(P\).
Proof
  1. By equation (16.28), \(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\nabla\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  2. \(|\boldsymbol\tau|^2 = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = |\nabla\Phi|^2\).
  3. Since \(\nabla\Phi \ne \vek 0\) and \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable, near \(P\) the level line is by the implicit function theorem (theorem 8.35, with \(N = 2\) and \(m = 1\)) the graph of a continuously differentiable function of one coordinate, so a curve with a tangent. Let \(\vek t\) be the tangent vector of the level line at \(P\). The derivative of \(\Phi\) along the level line is zero, so \(\nabla\Phi\cdot\vek t = 0\). In the plane, for \(\nabla\Phi \ne \vek 0\) only vectors of one direction are perpendicular to it, so \(\boldsymbol\tau\) is parallel to \(\vek t\).

Source: SADD section 9.3.4 and figure 9.8 (PDF p. 254-258) use this property for the membrane and for the ellipse. The proof is not from the source.

Why is the stress at the end of the shorter axis twice as large as at the end of the longer? By the lemma the stress is the magnitude of the slope of the hill. The function falls from \(c\) to zero over the distance \(a\) along the axis \(y\) and over the distance \(b\) along the axis \(z\). The slope at the boundary is \(2c/a\) and \(2c/b\), the ratio is \(b/a = 2\), so the stress is larger where the boundary is nearer to the top. The stress is therefore not largest at the point farthest from the axis.

The ellipse is also a near miss for the conjecture of section 16.1: for the circle the stress is proportional to the distance from the axis, \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and is largest on the boundary. If we assumed the same for the ellipse, we would look for the stress at the farthest boundary, at the end of the longer axis, and obtain \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}} b/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) with \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi ab\,(a^2 + b^2)/4 = 78\,540\) mm\(^4\), so \(50\,000 \cdot 20/78\,540 = 12{,}73\) MPa. The true stress there is \(7{,}96\) MPa, and at the end of the shorter axis \(15{,}92\) MPa: the conjecture is wrong at both points, at the end of the longer axis it is too large by 60 %, and it misses the largest stress by 20 %. The circle is a special case where \(\psi = 0\) and the sections stay plane. For the ellipse \(k = 0{,}600\) and the section bulges.

A function that is zero on the boundary is not yet a Prandtl function: by theorem 16.24 it must in addition satisfy the equation \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\). Let us take the simplest function that is zero on the boundary of a rectangle and check what it lacks. Let the rectangle have the half-sides \(a = 10\) mm along the axis \(y\) and \(b = 20\) mm along the axis \(z\), equal to the section 20 by 40 mm that we shall solve in section 16.5, and the function is

\[\Phi_{\mathrm{t}} = (a^2 - y^2)\,(b^2 - z^2) .\]
Example 16.29 A function that is zero on the boundary and is not the solution (funkcija, ki je na robu nič in ni rešitev)

The function \(\Phi_{\mathrm{t}}\) is zero on all four sides, \(y = \pm a\) and \(z = \pm b\). Its Laplace operator is \(\nabla^2\Phi_{\mathrm{t}} = -2\,(a^2 + b^2 - y^2 - z^2)\), which is not constant. The condition \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) however demands the same number everywhere. We scale it so that it equals \(1\) at the center. Then the value of \(-\nabla^2\Phi_{\mathrm{t}}\) divided by the value at the center is \(1\) at the center, \(0{,}8\) at the middle of the longer side \((a, 0)\), \(0{,}2\) at the middle of the shorter \((0, b)\) and \(0\) at the corners. The lines of equal value are circles about the center with radii \(10\), \(20\) and \(\sqrt{500} = 22{,}36\) mm.

What would this mean? If we accepted \(\Phi_{\mathrm{t}}\), the left side of equation (16.29), which \(\psi\) must satisfy, would be zero at the corners instead of \(2G\alpha\), and at the middle of the longer side \(0{,}8\cdot 2G\alpha\). The warping function of step 6 in the proof of theorem 16.24 would demand different mixed derivatives, which for a function with continuous second derivatives are equal. So there is no warping function, and the pieces of material at a corner could not be put together into a whole without gaps and overlaps. Zero on the boundary is a necessary, not a sufficient condition. The function \(\Phi_{\mathrm{t}}\) returns in section 16.9 as a trial function for the lower bound of \(J\), with an error of \(-2{,}82\) %, since there it does not solve the equation but looks for the best function among such ones.

The ellipse and the circle are two sections where \(\Phi\) is guessed from the shape of the boundary. The same trick works for the equilateral triangle. The section is bounded by three lines, on each one of the three distances \(d_1\), \(d_2\), \(d_3\) to the sides has the value zero, and the product is zero on the boundary. Let us try it [figure 16.41]: we draw a triangle with side \(s\) and the distances \(d_{\mathrm{k}} = \rho - \vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot(y, z)\) from the \(k\)-th side, where \(\rho = s/(2\sqrt{3})\) is the radius of the inscribed circle and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) the outward normal. The centroid is at the origin of coordinates, the side \(d_1 = 0\) is at the bottom, and

\[d_1 = \rho + z , \qquad d_2 = \rho - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,y - \tfrac{1}{2}\,z , \qquad d_3 = \rho + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,y - \tfrac{1}{2}\,z .\]
Example 16.30 Equilateral triangle (enakostranični trikotnik)

Let \(\Phi = c\,\)\(d_1d_2d_3\). After multiplying,

\[d_1d_2d_3 = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,s^3}{72} - \frac{\sqrt{3}\,s}{8}\,(y^2 + z^2) + \frac{z^3 - 3y^2z}{4} .\]

The term \(z^3 - 3y^2z\) is harmonic (\(6z - 6z = 0\)), the term with \(y^2 + z^2\) has the Laplace operator \(-\sqrt{3}\,s/2\), so \(\nabla^2(d_1d_2d_3) = -\sqrt{3}\,s/2\), constant. The condition \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) gives \(c = 4G\alpha/(\sqrt{3}\,s)\). The function is zero on the boundary, because at every point of a side one of the distances is zero. By lemma 16.25 this is the Prandtl function of the triangle. The integral over the triangle (SymPy) gives \(J = \dfrac{2c}{G\alpha}\int_{\mathrm{S}} d_1d_2d_3\,\mathrm{d}A = \sqrt{3}\,s^4/80\), which agrees with ONATE2 (figure 4.11, PDF p. 201). The area is \(A = \sqrt{3}\,s^2/4\), so \(J/A^2 = \sqrt{3}/15 = 0{,}1155\). For \(A = 800\) mm\(^2\) we have \(s = 42{,}98\) mm, \(J = 73\,901\) mm\(^4\) and the largest stress at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m is \(12{,}59\) MPa at the middle of a side. At the corners the stress is zero: there two distances in the product are zero, so both derivatives of \(\Phi\) are zero.

The level lines are not similar triangles, but become rounder toward the center. On the sides \(\Phi = 0\). By lemma 16.28 the stress is along the level lines, at the corners it is zero and it is largest at the middle of the sides (SADD, PDF p. 260). The corners therefore carry the least, as we shall also see for the rectangle (section 16.5).

Which section is stiffest in twisting, if all have the same area and the same material? We choose the area \(800\) mm\(^2\) (which the rectangle 20 by 40 mm has) and compare six sections [figure 16.42]. We draw a circle, a square, an ellipse 1 : 2, an equilateral triangle, a strip 1 : 2 and a strip 1 : 5, each with area \(800\) mm\(^2\). To the right of each we draw a horizontal line whose length is proportional to the torsion constant \(J\) of the section. A longer line means a smaller twist at the same moment, since \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ)\).

Example 16.31 The same material, different stiffness (isti material, različna togost)

At \(A = 800\) mm\(^2\) the torsion constants are: circle \(101\,859\), square \(89\,969\), ellipse 1 : 2 \(81\,487\), equilateral triangle \(73\,901\), strip 1 : 2 \(73\,178\) and strip 1 : 5 \(37\,288\) mm\(^4\). The ratio to the circle is \(1\), \(0{,}883\), \(0{,}800\), \(0{,}726\), \(0{,}718\) and \(0{,}366\). The circle, the ellipse and the triangle are from this section. The square and the strips are from the series for the rectangle in section 16.5. The square agrees with ONATE2 (figure 4.11, PDF p. 201), which has the rounded \(J = 2{,}25\,a^4\) with half-side \(a\), so \(90\,000\) mm\(^4\).

Among these six the circle is stiffest, and the more stretched the section, the less stiff it is. That this holds for every section of the same area we neither prove nor claim here. Why is the strip 1 : 5 so soft? At the same twist \(\alpha\) the function \(\Phi\) is everywhere near the boundary, where it is zero, so the hill is low, and its volume is by equation (16.32) the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\). In section 16.4 we shall see this on the membrane.

Prandtl's function is thus a second way to the same torsion. The warping function is the solution of an equation with a boundary condition on the derivative and gives displacements. Prandtl's function solves the Poisson equation with zero on the boundary and gives stresses. Both ways give the same torsion constant \(J\) (theorem 16.24, point 2).
