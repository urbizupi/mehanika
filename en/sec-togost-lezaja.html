Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.8 Stiffness of a bearing (togost ležaja)

A shaft on a bearing with nine balls (diameter \(D = 12{,}7\) mm, zero clearance) presses with a force \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N and moves by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}54\) µm (section 25.7). Now let the force change a little, for example because of an unbalanced mass rotating with the shaft. How far does the shaft move? We would first try the quotient \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 86{,}6\) N/µm, as if the bearing were an ordinary spring. As we shall see, this quotient is not the right one. The bearing is a spring that stiffens with load (hardening spring, utrjujoča se vzmet), and for small changes about the working point we need the slope of its curve, not the quotient. This slope is the stiffness of the bearing (togost ležaja). The section computes it first for one displacement, then for the displacement vector of the ring in the plane, then for a preloaded pair of bearings, and finally shows how the stiffness fluctuates a little during rotation.

Let us draw the axis \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) horizontally in micrometres and \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) vertically in newtons. By section 25.7 the bearing force at zero clearance, when the sum over the balls is replaced by the integral \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\), is \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = Z\,Q_{\max}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}\) (equation (25.34)) with \(Q_{\max} = K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\), so \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = c\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\) with \(c = Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}\,K_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is the curve that climbs ever more steeply. The number \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) for a ball in a raceway groove would require the elliptic contact, which the book states but does not prove (section 25.6). We therefore take Palmgren's equation, which HK (8.1) (PDF p. 179) gives for ball bearings under radial load at low to moderate speeds:

\[\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = C_{\mathrm{P}}\,\frac{Q_{\max}^{2/3}}{D^{1/3}\cos\alpha} , \qquad C_{\mathrm{P}} = 4{,}36\cdot 10^{-4} , \tag{25.37}\]

with \(Q_{\max}\) in newtons, \(D\) in millimetres and \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) in millimetres. The equation is empirical and the book does not prove it. HK does not state the units on the page, but with millimetres and newtons the stiffness agrees with FPGL (5.90) to \(1{,}0\,\%\) (the comparison below). At \(\alpha = 0\) and \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta^{3/2}\) we get \(K_{\mathrm{n}} = D^{1/2}/C_{\mathrm{P}}^{3/2} = 391\,450\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\). This is three times the \(127\,928\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\) of a ball between two flat plates (section 25.7), because the raceway fits the ball and the gap near the contact is smaller (sum of curvatures, definition 25.30). For our bearing \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2) = 0{,}2288\), so \(c = 9 \cdot 0{,}2288 \cdot 391\,450 = 8{,}06\cdot 10^5\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\), that is \(25{,}49\) N/µm\(^{3/2}\).

We mark the working point: at \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N we have \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}54\) µm (equation (25.37) with \(Q_{\max} = 485{,}6\) N). The secant from the origin to it has slope \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 86{,}62\) N/µm. This is the quotient we first tried. The tangent has slope \(129{,}93\) N/µm, which is \(1{,}5\) times more, and cuts the \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) axis at a third of \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), at \(3{,}85\) µm. Which slope is the right one is shown by a trial with a number. Let us raise the force from \(1000\) N to \(1100\) N and magnify this step in the box. Along the curve \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 25{,}49\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\) the shaft moves by \(0{,}76\) µm, from \(11{,}54\) to \(12{,}30\) µm. The tangent predicts \(0{,}77\) µm, that is \(100/129{,}93\), and misses by \(1{,}7\,\%\). The secant predicts \(1{,}15\) µm, that is \(100/86{,}62\), which is too much by a half. Why does the secant miss? Its slope is the average steepness of the curve from the origin to the working point, where the curve was flatter, while the force changes at the working point, where the curve is steepest. If the force changes by a small \(\Delta F\), the displacement is therefore about \(\Delta F/129{,}93\) N/µm, not \(\Delta F/86{,}62\) N/µm. The natural frequency of a shaft on a spring is proportional to the square root of the stiffness, so the secant would give a frequency with the factor \(\sqrt{86{,}62/129{,}93} = 0{,}8165\), that is \(18{,}4\,\%\) too low. The secant is the right answer only when the curve is a straight line. For small deviations in the interval of width \(2x\) about the working point the tangent suffices, and how small they must be is stated by the estimate below.

25.8.1 The stiffness matrix of a bearing

The shaft does not move only in the direction of the force. We therefore need the stiffness in every direction of the plane perpendicular to the axis, and since a force in one direction can cause a displacement in another, a matrix. For the bearing of section 25.7 we choose coordinates in the plane of the ring: \(v\) along the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(h\) perpendicular to it. What the letters mean is collected in the remark at the end of the section.

We draw the two rings and the balls between them, as in the previous section. Let the inner ring move as a whole by the vector \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{v}}, u_{\mathrm{h}})\), drawn additionally dashed in the figure and greatly exaggerated for display. Ball \(j\) lies at the angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = \psi_0 + 2\pi j/Z\), measured from the direction \(v\) (the angle), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = (\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\) is the unit vector from the centre towards it. When the ring moves by \(\vek u\), the distance between the raceway centres at ball \(j\) changes by the projection of the displacement on \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so the approach of the ball is

\[\delta_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek u) = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek u - \frac{P_{\mathrm{d}}}{2} , \tag{25.38}\]

which is the geometry of step 1 in the proof of theorem 25.35, only with an arbitrary direction of the displacement. The error of this geometry is of second order and for a compressed ball at most \(|\vek u|^2/(2R)\), where \(R = 23\) mm is the raceway radius assumed in section 25.7. At \(|\vek u| = 33{,}76\) µm (at \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 5000\) N) this is \(0{,}025\) µm, \(0{,}073\,\%\) of the displacement, and at \(11{,}54\) µm it is \(2{,}9\) nm, \(0{,}025\,\%\) (numerical computation of this section). The ball carries the force \(Q_{\mathrm{j}} = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\max(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}, 0)^n\) by the law (25.28) and its force on the ring lies on the line \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\).

On the right side of the figure the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) points from the centre downward, and the left and right positions of the cage differ in whether a ball is on the direction of the force (three compressed) or two are beside it (four compressed). The stiffness will therefore depend also on the position of the cage, which the last part of the section treats.

Definition 25.39 Force function and stiffness matrix of a bearing (silna funkcija in togostna matrika ležaja)

Let the bearing of definition 25.32 have rigid rings, \(Z\) balls at angles \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the law \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^n\) with \(n > 1\). The bearing force function (silna funkcija ležaja) \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\colon \mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R^2\) assigns to every displacement \(\vek u\) of the inner ring the vector

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{Z} K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\max\big(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek u), 0\big)^n\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} , \tag{25.39}\]

the force the shaft must exert on the ring so that it is in equilibrium with the balls at the displacement \(\vek u\). The tangent stiffness matrix of the bearing (togostna matrika ležaja) at the point \(\vek u_0\) is the \(2 \times 2\) matrix of partial derivatives

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u_0) = \Big[\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{a}}}{\partial u_{\mathrm{c}}}(\vek u_0)\Big]_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{c} \in \{\mathrm{v}, \mathrm{h}\}} , \tag{25.40}\]

its units are N/m. The diagonal entries are written \(k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}\), the off-diagonal one \(k_{\mathrm{vh}}\).

Source: The notion follows FPGL section 5.5.3 (PDF pp. 202-203): the stiffness is the "slope of the force-deflection function", and HK section 14.3.3 (PDF p. 352): a spring with the slope of the curve at the normal load. The matrix in this form is not from the source.

Theorem 25.40 Stiffness matrix of a bearing (togostna matrika ležaja)

For the bearing of definition 25.39 with \(n > 1\) the function \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) is continuously differentiable on \(\mathbb R^2\) and

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{Z} n\,K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\max\big(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek u), 0\big)^{n-1} \begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\psi_{\mathrm{j}} & \cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}} \\ \cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}} & \sin^2\psi_{\mathrm{j}} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{25.41}\]

The sum runs only over compressed balls (\(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\)). Moreover:

  1. \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (each compressed ball contributes a nonnegative term), \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} nK_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{j},+}^{n-1}(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x)^2\), where \(\delta_{\mathrm{j},+} = \max(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}, 0)\).
  2. \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is positive definite exactly when at least two balls are compressed.
  3. For \(\vek u_0\) and small \(\vek x\), \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u_0 + \vek x) = \vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u_0) + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u_0)\,\vek x + \vek r\), and for \(n = 3/2\) and a ball with \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\) the contribution of this ball to the remainder is bounded by \(|\vek r_{\mathrm{j}}| \le \frac{\sqrt2}{4}\,\frac{|\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x|}{\delta_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\big|nK_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{j}}^{n-1}(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x)\big|\) if \(|\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x| \le \delta_{\mathrm{j}}/2\).
Idea

Each ball gives a contribution that depends only on its approach, and that is a linear function of the displacement. The derivative of the function \(\max(t,0)^n\) is continuous for \(n > 1\), so a ball that has just started to touch does not spoil the matrix.

Proof

We must show the form of the matrix, the continuity of the derivative and three further claims.

  1. The function \(g(t) = K_{\mathrm{n}}\max(t, 0)^n\) has derivative \(nK_{\mathrm{n}}t^{n-1}\) for \(t > 0\), derivative zero for \(t < 0\), and at \(t = 0\) both one-sided limits are zero because \(n - 1 > 0\). So \(g\) is continuously differentiable with \(g'(t) = nK_{\mathrm{n}}\max(t,0)^{n-1}\).
  2. The approach \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek u) = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek u - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) is an affine function with derivatives \(\partial\delta_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial u_{\mathrm{c}} = e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{c}}\). By the chain rule \(\partial\big(g(\delta_{\mathrm{j}})\,e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{a}}\big)/\partial u_{\mathrm{c}} = g'(\delta_{\mathrm{j}})\,e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{a}}\,e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{c}}\).
  3. The sum over \(j\) gives equation (25.41), since \(e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{v}} = \cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{h}} = \sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). All derivatives are continuous (step 1), so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) is continuously differentiable.
  4. The matrix \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\) is symmetric, hence so is the sum. For a column \(\vek x\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} g'(\delta_{\mathrm{j}})(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x)^2 \ge 0\), because \(g' \ge 0\). This proves claim 1. In another way: \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the gradient of the energy \(\Pi(\vek u) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{n}}\max(\delta_{\mathrm{j}},0)^{n+1}/(n+1)\), which is twice continuously differentiable, and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is its Hessian matrix (definition 8.69).
  5. For claim 2: \(\vek x^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek x = 0\) exactly when \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x = 0\) for all compressed balls. Two compressed balls \(j \ne k\) cannot have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} = -\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), since then \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} + \delta_{\mathrm{k}} = -P_{\mathrm{d}} \le 0\), in contradiction with \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}, \delta_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\). Since the vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} \ne \vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) are different, they are independent, and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x = \vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek x = 0\) gives \(\vek x = \vek 0\). With one compressed ball, \(\vek x \perp \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), a displacement perpendicular to the line of the ball that does not compress it to first order, is nonzero with zero quadratic form, and the matrix is singular.
  6. For claim 3 (small displacement) we apply theorem 8.68 to the function \(g\) at \(t_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\) with increment \(\xi = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x\), \(|\xi| \le \delta_{\mathrm{j}}/2\). There is a \(\theta\) between \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} + \xi\) with \(g(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} + \xi) - g(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}) - g'(\delta_{\mathrm{j}})\xi = \tfrac12 g''(\theta)\xi^2\), where \(g''(\theta) = n(n-1)K_{\mathrm{n}}\theta^{n-2}\). For \(n = 3/2\), \(g''(\theta) = \tfrac34K_{\mathrm{n}}\theta^{-1/2} \le \tfrac34K_{\mathrm{n}}(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}/2)^{-1/2}\), so the remainder is \(\le \tfrac38\sqrt2\,K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{j}}^{-1/2}\xi^2\). We divide by \(|g'(\delta_{\mathrm{j}})\xi| = \tfrac32K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{j}}^{1/2}|\xi|\): the quotient is \(\le \tfrac{\sqrt2}{4}|\xi|/\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\). The remainder in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is exactly the remainder of the scalar \(g\).

Source: The form of \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) follows FPGL (PDF pp. 202-203), where the stiffness of a bearing comes from the slope of the force-deflection function, but formula (5.90) there holds only for \(k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) and has a different form. The theorem and proof are not from the source. Numerical check: the difference between equation (25.41) and finite differences of \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) is at most \(1{,}6\cdot 10^{-10}\) at \(Z = 9\) (relative to \(Q_{\max}\), NumPy).

What does the theorem say? The matrix is a sum of contributions of the compressed balls, each with stiffness \(nK_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_{\mathrm{j}}^{n-1}\) along its line \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) and zero perpendicular to it. Balls at the edge of the zone, where \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) is small, are soft, and the ball under the shaft is the stiffest. The third claim also says how small the displacement must be. For \(n = 3/2\) the error of one ball is about \(|\xi|/(4\delta_{\mathrm{j}})\) of the stiffness term (numerically at \(x/\delta = 0{,}05\): \(1{,}24\,\%\), at \(0{,}1\): \(2{,}46\,\%\), at \(0{,}2\): \(4{,}85\,\%\)). For a vibration of amplitude \(1\) µm about \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}54\) µm the error of the linear model is therefore about \(2{,}2\,\%\). This is a sensible measure, and at larger amplitudes the linear model does not suffice. An estimate for the whole bearing would also have to count the balls that start or stop touching during the vibration, but these carry little, so the estimate is only for single balls.

Example 25.41 Stiffness of the nine-ball bearing at 1000 N (togost ležaja z devetimi kroglicami)

The bearing of the introduction has zero clearance, \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N and a ball on the direction of the force. Five balls are compressed (\(0^\circ\), \(\pm 40^\circ\), \(\pm 80^\circ\)). The sum over these five balls in equation (25.41) with \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) from equation (25.37) gives \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}57\) µm, \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = 129{,}63\) N/µm, \(k_{\mathrm{vh}} = 0\) and \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}/k_{\mathrm{vv}} = 0{,}746\). The integral model with \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) from the introduction gives \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}54\) µm and \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = 1{,}5\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 129{,}93\) N/µm (next corollary), a difference of \(0{,}2\,\%\). The first direction is stiffer than the second because the balls under the shaft lie in the direction \(v\) and support it more than in the direction \(h\). For \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 284\) N, \(1000\) N and \(5000\) N the stiffnesses by the integral model are \(85{,}40\), \(129{,}93\) and \(222{,}18\) N/µm (NumPy), at the approaches \(4{,}99\), \(11{,}54\) and \(33{,}76\) µm.

At zero clearance the matrix has one more special property, called homogeneity. Since \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) is then proportional to \(\vek u\), we have \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\lambda\vek u) = \lambda^n\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u)\) for \(\lambda > 0\). This is the same relation between \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in the previous section.

Corollary 25.42 Stiffness along the load (togost v smeri sile)

For a bearing with zero clearance (\(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\)) and the displacement \(\vek u = (\delta_{\mathrm{r}}, 0)\) in the direction \(v\),

\[k_{\mathrm{vv}} = \frac{n\,F_{\mathrm{v}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{r}}} , \qquad k_{\mathrm{vh}} = \frac{n\,F_{\mathrm{h}}}{\delta_{\mathrm{r}}} , \tag{25.42}\]

where \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{h}}\) are the components of \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u)\). For a position of the cage that is symmetric about the direction \(v\), \(F_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = F_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = \tfrac32 F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) at \(n = 3/2\). The ratio \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}/k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) does not depend on the size of the load.

Proof

We must show equation (25.42) and the independence of the ratio.

  1. At \(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) we have \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}(\lambda\vek u)\) = \lambda,\delta_j(\vek u)\( and \)\max(\lambda\delta_j, 0)^n = \lambda^n\max(\delta_j, 0)^n\( for \)\lambda > 0\(. So \)\vek F_b(\lambda\vek u) = \lambda^n\vek F_b(\vek u)$.
  2. We differentiate with respect to \(\lambda\), that is along the displacement, at \(\lambda = 1\). The left side gives \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u)\,\vek u\) (chain rule, theorem 25.40), the right side \(n\,\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u)\). So \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek u = n\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\).
  3. At \(\vek u = (\delta_{\mathrm{r}}, 0)\) we have \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek u = \delta_{\mathrm{r}}(k_{\mathrm{vv}}, k_{\mathrm{vh}})\), which gives equation (25.42). For a symmetric position of the cage the mirror image of the balls about the axis \(v\) is the same, so \(F_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\), and \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the equilibrium of the ring.
  4. From equation (25.41), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\lambda\vek u) = \lambda^{n-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek u)\), since every term is proportional to \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}^{n-1}\). All entries of the matrix are multiplied by the same factor, so the ratio \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}/k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) stays.

Source: The independence of the ratio from the load follows FPGL (PDF p. 203): "this ratio is independent of the static load". Equation (25.42) is not from the source. Numerically: at \(\vek u = (0{,}7, 0{,}2)\) we have \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek u = 1{,}5\,\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) (NumPy, \(Z = 9\), cage position \(0{,}3\) rad), and at the cage position \(0{,}3\) rad \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = 3{,}0977 = 1{,}5 F_{\mathrm{v}}\).

At \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N, therefore, \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 1000/11{,}54 = 129{,}9\) N/µm, which figure 25.63 shows as the slope of the tangent. Since \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} \propto F_{\mathrm{r}}^{2/3}\), we have \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = \tfrac32 F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}} \propto F_{\mathrm{r}}^{1/3}\): the stiffness grows with the load, but more slowly than the force (the bearing is a hardening spring). At five times the force, at the point at \(5000\) N and \(33{,}76\) µm, the tangent is steeper: the stiffness is \(222{,}18/129{,}93 = 1{,}71\) times larger, not five times.

FPGL (5.90) writes the same in the form \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = \kappa\,Z^{2/3}d^{1/3}F_{\mathrm{s}}^{1/3}\cos^{5/3}\alpha\) with the constant \(\kappa = 13\cdot 10^6\) N\(^{2/3}\) m\(^{-4/3}\) (PDF p. 202, equation read from the crop). From \(k_{\mathrm{vv}} = \tfrac32 F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) and equation (25.37) our model gives the form \(\kappa = \tfrac32 J_{\mathrm{r}}^{2/3}/(4{,}36\cdot 10^{-8}) = 12{,}87\cdot 10^6\), so the two forms agree to \(1{,}0\,\%\) (at \(1000\) N: \(131{,}23\) against \(129{,}93\) N/µm). We do not know the cause of the difference, since FPGL does not state how \(\kappa\) was determined.

FPGL quotes the ratio \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}/k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) after Kramer (PDF p. 203) for \(8\), \(12\) and \(16\) balls: \(0{,}46\), \(0{,}64\), \(0{,}73\). Our model with a ball on the direction of the force gives \(0{,}457\), \(0{,}555\) and \(0{,}595\), and with the direction between two balls \(0{,}734\), \(0{,}701\) and \(0{,}688\). For \(8\) balls the first position agrees with Kramer, for \(12\) and \(16\) neither does. Kramer's load, clearance and definition of the ratio are not on the page, so we do not claim that the results agree or disagree, only that they cannot be compared on this basis.

25.8.2 A preloaded pair of bearings

At zero load every \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} \le 0\) in the model of theorem 25.40, so \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}} = \mat 0\): the bearing has zero stiffness at \(\vek u = \vek 0\) and \(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\). Why would this be a problem? The theorem holds here and correctly gives \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}} = \mat 0\). But the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek x)\) grows like \(|\vek x|^{3/2}\), so the zero line is a correct first-order approximation, yet a useless one: the linear model has no restoring force, and without load the shaft would have no natural frequency, although the bearing under a small force still holds the shaft. What fails is the bound of point 3 of the theorem, which needs \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\) and \(|\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x| \le \delta_{\mathrm{j}}/2\). At \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) this allows only \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\vek x = 0\), so the linear model has no range where its error is bounded. This is the near miss of point 3: without load no ball has \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\). A bearing avoids this by preload (predobremenitev).

We draw the walls left and right, between them two bearings as springs, bearing \(2\) on the left and bearing \(1\) on the right, and the shaft block between them, grey dashed before loading. The springs are compressed against each other, each with force \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) and approach \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) (the preload). An external force \(F_{\mathrm{e}}\) acts on the block towards the right and the block moves by \(s\) towards bearing \(1\), and we draw the new position orange. Bearing \(1\) is compressed to \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s\), bearing \(2\) is relieved to \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s\). Each follows the law \(F = c\,\delta^{3/2}\) (a simplification: the contact angle does not change, HK (8.13) to (8.16), PDF pp. 181-183, also treats the change of angle in angular-contact bearings, which the book does not). In the example below the bearings are the radial bearing of the introduction, pressing on the shaft block from opposite sides in the radial direction, as in the figure, so \(c = 25{,}49\) N/µm\(^{3/2}\). HK in section 8.3.1 (PDF pp. 180-181) preloads a pair of angular-contact bearings in the axial direction. There the law relates the axial force to the axial displacement and has another constant, and the lemma holds for every law of this form.

Lemma 25.43 Stiffness of a preloaded pair (togost predobremenjenega para)

Let two identical bearings follow the law \(F = c\,\delta^{3/2}\) for \(\delta > 0\) and \(F = 0\) for \(\delta \le 0\), with preload \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = c\,\delta_{\mathrm{p}}^{3/2}\). For a displacement \(s\) of the block with \(0 \le s \le \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) the external force is \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = c\big[(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s)^{3/2} - (\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s)^{3/2}\big]\) and the stiffness of the pair is \(k = dF_{\mathrm{e}}/ds = \tfrac32c\big[(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s)^{1/2} + (\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s)^{1/2}\big]\). Then:

  1. At \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\), \(k = 2k_{\mathrm{p}}\) with \(k_{\mathrm{p}} = \tfrac32c\,\delta_{\mathrm{p}}^{1/2}\) the stiffness of one bearing at the preload.
  2. \(k\) decreases with \(s\) and at \(s = \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) we have \(k = \sqrt2\,k_{\mathrm{p}}\) at \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 2^{3/2}F_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  3. At \(F_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 2^{3/2}F_{\mathrm{p}}\) bearing \(2\) is free, the pair behaves as one bearing with \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = c(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s)^{3/2}\), and the stiffness is the continuous continuation.
Proof

We must show three claims.

  1. For \(s \le \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) the approaches \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} \pm s \ge 0\). Equilibrium of the block: the force of bearing \(1\) on the block points towards \(-\), that of bearing \(2\) towards \(+\), so \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = F_1 - F_2\). This gives the stated equation, and its derivative with respect to \(s\) gives \(k\).
  2. At \(s = 0\), \(k = \tfrac32c\cdot 2\delta_{\mathrm{p}}^{1/2} = 2k_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  3. The function \(\sqrt t\) is concave, so \(\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s} + \sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s} \le 2\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{p}}}\), and the sum decreases for \(s > 0\) (derivative \(\tfrac12(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s)^{-1/2} - \tfrac12(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s)^{-1/2} < 0\)). At \(s = \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\): \(k = \tfrac32c\sqrt{2\delta_{\mathrm{p}}} = \sqrt2k_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = c(2\delta_{\mathrm{p}})^{3/2} = 2^{3/2}F_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  4. For \(s > \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) we have \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} - s < 0\), bearing \(2\) has no force, \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = c(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} + s)^{3/2}\), and at \(s = \delta_{\mathrm{p}}\) the value and the stiffness are the same as in step 3.

Source: The phenomenon follows HK section 8.3.1 (PDF pp. 180-183): "the unit acts as a single bearing under thrust load" after the preload is lost. The lemma and equations are not from the source, HK writes them for angular-contact bearings with \(\alpha\) (8.13)-(8.16).

On the right of the figure are the axes: horizontally the external force \(F_{\mathrm{e}}\) in newtons, vertically the stiffness \(k\) of the pair in N/µm. The example with the bearing of the introduction (\(F_{\mathrm{p}} = 1000\) N, \(\delta_{\mathrm{p}} = 11{,}54\) µm, \(k_{\mathrm{p}} = 129{,}93\) N/µm, NumPy): at \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) the stiffness of the pair is \(259{,}86\) N/µm, twice that of one bearing. It is double because a displacement \(s\) of the block raises the force of bearing \(1\) and lowers the force of bearing \(2\), and both changes push the block back. At \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 1000\) N the stiffness of the pair is \(256{,}08\) N/µm and at \(2000\) N \(241{,}83\) N/µm, and at \(2828\) N (\(= 2^{3/2}\cdot 1000\) N) \(183{,}75\) N/µm, which is \(\sqrt2 \cdot 129{,}93\). The displacement of the pair at \(1000\) N is \(3{,}87\) µm against \(11{,}54\) µm for one bearing. For one bearing without preload the stiffness at \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) is zero (the grey curve starts at the origin), at \(1000\) N it is \(129{,}93\) and at \(2000\) N \(163{,}70\) N/µm. Above \(2828\) N the two graphs meet: the pair becomes one bearing, so at \(5000\) N the stiffness is \(222{,}18\) N/µm as for one bearing. The blue curve is therefore higher than the grey one up to this point, but the preload comes at a full price: each bearing carries a constant \(1000\) N. At a force above \(2{,}83\) kN the gain is lost.

25.8.3 Stiffness fluctuation during rotation

So far the position of the cage was fixed. In reality the cage rotates and the balls travel past the direction of the force, so the two positions of figure 25.64, a ball on the direction of the force and the direction of the force between two balls, follow one another. If the cage turns the position \(\psi_0\) forward, the set \(\{\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\}\) maps onto itself when \(\psi_0\) increases by a multiple of \(2\pi/Z\).

Lemma 25.44 Stiffness fluctuation due to ball passage (nihanje togosti zaradi prehajanja kroglic)

For a bearing of definition 25.39 let the displacement \(\vek u\) be constant and the cage position \(\psi_0 = \omega_{\mathrm{c}} t\). Then \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) are periodic in time with period \(2\pi/(Z\omega_{\mathrm{c}})\). At \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{v}}, 0)\), \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}\) are even functions of \(\psi_0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{vh}}\) odd ones, about \(0\) and about \(\pi/Z\).

Proof
  1. At \(\psi_0 \to \psi_0 + 2\pi/Z\) the angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) changes to \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) (index modulo \(Z\)), so the set of angles is the same. The sums (25.39) and (25.41) are sums over the set and do not change. The period in \(\psi_0\) is \(2\pi/Z\), in time \(2\pi/(Z\omega_{\mathrm{c}})\).
  2. At the position \(-\psi_0\) the balls are at the angles \(-\psi_0 + 2\pi j/Z = -\big(\psi_0 + 2\pi(Z - j)/Z\big) + 2\pi\), so they are the mirror images about the direction \(v\) of the balls at the position \(\psi_0\). At \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{v}}, 0)\) we have \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}} = u_{\mathrm{v}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), which is the same for the mirrored ball, since \(\cos\) is even. In the sum (25.39) \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is therefore kept and \(\sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) changes sign: \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\) is an even and \(F_{\mathrm{h}}\) an odd function of \(\psi_0\). In the sum (25.41) \(\cos^2\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\sin^2\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) are kept and \(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) changes sign: \(k_{\mathrm{vv}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}\) are even, \(k_{\mathrm{vh}}\) is odd.
  3. About \(\pi/Z\): for an even function \(f\) with period \(2\pi/Z\) we have \(f(\pi/Z + t) = f(-\pi/Z + t) = f(\pi/Z - t)\), for an odd one \(f(\pi/Z + t) = f(-\pi/Z + t) = -f(\pi/Z - t)\).

Source: The frequency of the fluctuation follows HK section 14.3.3 (PDF p. 352): equal to the cage rotation frequency times the number of balls, "the frequency of balls passing the outer raceway". The proof is not from the source.

The lemma does not give the size of the fluctuation, so we compute it. At the displacement \(\vek u = (1, 0)\) and the same \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), the ratio of the force at the position between two balls to the force at the position with a ball on the direction of the force is: \(Z = 8\): \(0{,}9898\) (\(-1{,}02\,\%\)), \(Z = 9\): \(1{,}0064\), \(Z = 12\): \(0{,}9977\), \(Z = 16\): \(0{,}9992\). For \(Z = 9\) and \(12\) balls the deviations are therefore \(0{,}64\,\%\) and \(0{,}23\,\%\). FPGL (PDF p. 203) states: "the fluctuation in the stiffness is typically less than 1 percent", which agrees, except for \(8\) balls with \(1{,}02\,\%\) at equal displacement. At equal force the displacement at the position between balls is \(0{,}69\,\%\) larger than with a ball on the direction (\(Z = 8\)), and the stiffness is \(0{,}68\,\%\) smaller. At \(Z = 16\) the deviation of the force at equal \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(0{,}08\,\%\), that of the displacement at equal force \(0{,}06\,\%\).

HK (PDF p. 352) gives an example of bearing 204 with \(D = 7{,}938\) mm, \(Z = 8\) and \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 4450\) N: displacement \(0{,}001702\) in with a ball on the direction of the force and \(0{,}001714\) in at the position between two. In millimetres the page prints \(0{,}04232\) mm and \(0{,}04353\) mm. The second pair agrees (\(0{,}001714\) in \(= 0{,}04354\) mm), the first does not: \(0{,}001702\) in is \(0{,}04323\) mm, so two digits are swapped in \(0{,}04232\). With the correct values the difference is \(0{,}71\,\%\). Our model gives \(0{,}03937\) and \(0{,}03964\) mm with the same data, a difference of \(0{,}69\,\%\). The direction and size of the fluctuation therefore agree (the bearing is stiffer with a ball on the direction of the force). The displacements, however, are \(8{,}9\,\%\) smaller than HK's at both positions. HK does not say on the page how the displacement was computed (for example the clearance or the constant), so we cannot determine the cause of this difference. The fluctuation of stiffness is also a source of excitation, since the shaft moves at \(Z\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\) although the force is constant.

Notation of this section. The letter \(k\) with indices (\(k_{\mathrm{vv}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{hh}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{vh}}\)) is the stiffness of the bearing, not the foundation modulus of section 25.1 and not the conductivity of the chapter on heat. The letter \(v\) here is the index of the direction of the force, not the deflection of a beam. The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the stiffness matrix of the bearing, and \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a number, the contact constant of equation (25.29). The letter \(\kappa\) is the constant from FPGL and not a curvature. The letter \(x\) is a small displacement about the working point and not a beam coordinate, the letter \(s\) the displacement of the block between the bearings and not an arc length. The letter \(\alpha\) is the contact angle.

We have computed how the force of a bearing changes under a small displacement. The next section (25.9) builds this stiffness into a finite element model of the shaft. Since \(\vek F_{\mathrm{b}}\) is nonlinear, the equilibrium must first be found by iteration, and only then the vibration about the resulting position with the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) of this section.
