The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

In chapter 9 we described vibration with one degree of freedom by a viscous damper, and in chapter 10 we carried this over to several degrees of freedom and chose the damping so that the modes still decoupled. We used all of it with one silent assumption: the damper exists and has a known constant. This chapter tests that assumption. It also builds on chapter 3, which promised that a material does not return all its work, and on the chapters on finite elements (7, 12).

The word damping (dušenje) means everything that takes energy from a vibration, so that the amplitude fades with time. The word model means a simplified description that we can put into a calculation: a rule for how the force we feel as damping depends on displacement and velocity. Damping models are therefore different rules for the same experience, that a vibration dies out. The real causes differ, such as friction in a clamp and the inside of steel, but we can calculate with only one rule at a time.

Why does it matter? A cantilever carrying a mass of 50 kg, struck once, loses some energy in every period, yet no damper hangs under it. A machine on rubber mounts passes a smaller force into the ground when the mounts are soft, and damping in the mounts can make this worse. A two-storey frame with one damper under the first floor has a vibration that classical damping cannot describe. In each of these cases the choice of model changes the number the designer gets.

The chapter promises to test the damping model every time, not merely assume it. First we find a measure that lets us compare all the causes, namely the energy a vibration loses in one period. Then we show when another cause may be replaced by a viscous damper and when not, and how damping that arises in the material behaves under vibration at one frequency. Next we describe a material that responds slowly to a load, first with models made of springs and dampers, then with the history of the load. Finally we put this into finite elements, write the vibration in a form that also gives damping that the classical model cannot describe, and look at the damping of a beam and of a string.
