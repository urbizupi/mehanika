Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.9 Plane strain and plane stress (ravninsko deformacijsko in ravninsko napetostno stanje)

The thick tube (debela cev) of section 11.7 and the thin ring (tanek obroč) of section 11.7.4 have the same cross-section, radii 40 mm and 60 mm, and the same load, pressure 30 MPa in the bore. The radial and hoop stresses are the same in both, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A\,(1 - b^2/r^2)\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A\,(1 + b^2/r^2)\) with \(A = p\,a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 24\) MPa (theorem 11.28). The axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is different. For the ring, which is thin and has free faces, it is zero. For a long tube whose ends are held by smooth rigid walls it is \(14{,}4\) MPa. When may we treat a body as plane, that is, compute with stresses and displacements in the plane of the cross-section only, and what is exact about this and what is an approximation? The answer is not the same for the two cases. For the long tube the plane treatment is an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations, for the thin ring plane stress is a model that is exact only under a condition, which we will derive and which holds for the ring.

The path is this. First we treat the two states separately, the long prism and the thin plate, and write down the equations that remain for each. Both lead to the same algebra, so we do it once. Then we show that the equations of the two states differ only in two constants, and at the end we say in what sense plane stress is really a solution.

11.9.1 Equations in the plane (enačbe v ravnini)

In both states the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero, and three stress components in the plane remain, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. In the long prism between rigid walls the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is nonzero as well, but it is fixed by \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and enters the equations in the plane only through the coefficients \(E'\) and \(\nu'\) below (theorem 11.33). Of the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31), in which the derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, two remain,

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{y}} = 0 . \tag{11.49}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx},\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy},\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}} = 0 . \tag{11.49}\]

Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) also has the same form in both states, only with coefficients that we call \(E'\) and \(\nu'\) for now, and fill with numbers for each state,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{E'} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E'} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{1 + \nu'}{E'}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} . \tag{11.50}\]

Let us write the same three equations also as a matrix that maps the column of three stresses to the column of three strains, and the other way round, as a matrix that gives the stresses from the strains,

\[\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{E'}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\nu' & 0 \\ -\nu' & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 + \nu' \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \nu' & 0 \\ \nu' & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 - \nu' \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{11.51}\]

The first matrix holds the coefficients of equations (11.50), copied row by row. The second is its inverse matrix and exists for \(\nu' \ne \pm 1\): the product of the two is the identity matrix, since for example in the first row \(\frac{1}{1 - \nu'^2}\,(1 \cdot 1 - \nu' \cdot \nu') = 1\) and \(\frac{1}{1 - \nu'^2}\,(1 \cdot \nu' - \nu' \cdot 1) = 0\), and in the third \(\frac{(1 + \nu')(1 - \nu')}{1 - \nu'^2} = 1\). The column of strains carries here the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of the tensor. Voigt notation (definition 3.27) carries the shear angle \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) in this place, so in it the first matrix has \(2\,(1 + \nu')\) in the corner and the second \(\tfrac12\,(1 - \nu')\), and in this form we shall meet it with finite elements in chapter 15. In the lectures the axes are numbered and the column is \((\varepsilon_{11}, \varepsilon_{22}, \varepsilon_{12})\), with the component of the tensor as here, so the corner there is \(1 + \nu\) and \(1 - \nu\).

Of the six compatibility equations (theorem 1.133) the first remains, the one that contains only components in the plane,

\[S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = 0 .\]
\[S_1 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx},\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy},\mathrm{xx}} - 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} = 0 .\]

The question is what this equation says about the stresses. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), which will appear again and again, we write \(\Theta_2\) (plane trace, ravninska sled), so as not to confuse it with the trace \(\Theta\) of theorem 11.9, which has three terms. By \(\nabla^2\) we denote the Laplace operator (definition 11.5), which for functions without dependence on \(z\) is the sum of two second derivatives, and by \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek b = \partial b_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x + \partial b_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) the divergence of the plane body force.

Lemma 11.31 Plane compatibility condition (ravninski pogoj skladnosti)

Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be functions of the variables \(x\), \(y\) with continuous partial derivatives up to second order on an open set of the plane, and \(b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}}\) functions with continuous first derivatives that satisfy the equilibrium equations (11.49). Let \(E' > 0\) and \(\nu'\) be arbitrary numbers and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) defined by (11.50). Then

\[E'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu')\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b , \qquad \Theta_2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} . \tag{11.52}\]
Proof

We must show that the left side \(E'\,S_1\) can be written as the right side after using equilibrium. The second derivative of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) with respect to \(x\) and the second derivative of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with respect to \(y\) we express with the shear stress.

  1. We insert (11.50) into \(S_1\). The factor \(1/E'\) is constant, so
    \[E'\,S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} - \nu'\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - \nu'\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,(1 + \nu')\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} .\]
  2. We differentiate the first equation (11.49) with respect to \(x\) and the second with respect to \(y\). The order of differentiation of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is exchanged (theorem 1.131), because the second derivatives are continuous. We get
    \[\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} - \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , \qquad \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y^2} = -\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} - \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} .\]
  3. The sum of these two equalities is \(\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y^2 = -2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y - \mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). The terms with \(\nu'\) from step 1 are therefore together \(\nu'\,(2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b)\) and
    \[E'\,S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \nu'\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b .\]
    The terms with \(\nu'\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) cancelled against \(-2\,(1 + \nu')\), and \(-2\) remained.
  4. The Laplace operator of the sum is \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2\). We replace the first two terms by step 3 and get \(\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b\).
  5. We insert this into step 3: \(E'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \nu'\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), which is (11.52).

Source: SADD (7.1.7) and (7.2.7) (PDF pp. 151, 154) and CM (7.5.32), (7.5.33) (PDF p. 329) write this result separately for each state, and we write it for general constants. The derivation is ours. SymPy checked step 5 for general functions.

The lemma says that the compatibility condition in the plane does not contain the individual components, only their sum \(\Theta_2\). The constant \(\nu'\) enters only in the term with the body force. Without body force the condition is \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = 0\) regardless of \(E'\) and \(\nu'\).

11.9.2 Plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Take a long tube or any other long cylinder that has the same cross-section along its whole length, and let it be loaded by forces on the lateral surface that do not depend on \(z\) and have no component in the direction \(z\) (figure 11.96, left). Let us assume two things: that all cross-sections deform in the same way and that they do not move along the axis. The first is right because every cross-section is in the same position as its neighbour: if we shift the body and the load along the axis \(z\), nothing changes, so there is no reason for one cross-section to deform differently from another. This holds far from the ends, and near an end only if the end spoils nothing either. How far is far is told by the rule of thumb that BOWER (PDF p. 230) gives for a cylinder: about three radii from the end. How the disturbance of an end fades with distance we show in section 11.9.3. The second is ensured by smooth rigid walls at the ends, which allow neither lengthening nor shortening. Where there are no walls, the second assumption fails, and how much that changes we shall measure on the tube: the axial stress by \(14{,}4\) MPa, the radial displacement by \(5\,\%\), and the stresses in the plane of the cross-section not at all (section 11.9.3). Such a body we call a prism with axis \(z\) (prizma z osjo \(z\)): we get it when the plane region \(R\) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) is moved along the axis \(z\) from \(z = 0\) to \(z = \ell\).

Definition 11.32 Plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Let the body be a prism with axis \(z\) and cross-section \(R\), an open set in the plane \(x\), \(y\). The displacement field \(\vek u\) of the body is a plane strain state (plane strain) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) if it has the form

\[\vek u = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y),\ 0\big) , \tag{11.53}\]

where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) are continuously differentiable functions of two variables on \(R\). The body force has the form \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), b_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\).

Source: SADD §7.1 (7.1.1) (PDF p. 149), CM (7.5.1) (PDF p. 323). TIMO (PDF pp. 289-290) says the same in words: the displacements lie in planes perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder.

What are the stresses for such a displacement field? Hooke's law, equilibrium and compatibility give the answer.

Theorem 11.33 Equations of plane strain (enačbe ravninskega deformacijskega stanja)

Let a prism with axis \(z\) and cross-section \(R\) have an isotropic and homogeneous material with \(E\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), and let \(\vek u\) be a plane strain state (definition 11.32) that has continuous partial derivatives up to third order (then the strains and stresses have continuous second derivatives) and whose body force has continuous first derivatives. Then:

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and the remaining three strain components depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\).
  3. The strains in the plane follow from (11.50) with \(E' = E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu' = \nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\), that is, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)\) and similarly for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\).
  4. Of the equilibrium equations, (11.49) hold, and the third holds by itself.
  5. Of the six compatibility equations the first remains, and it is equivalent to the equation
    \[\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = -\frac{1}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.54}\]
Proof

We must show five claims. The first four are consequences of the form of the displacement, the fifth follows from lemma 11.31.

  1. The displacement (11.53) does not depend on \(z\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). Therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (definition 1.121). The remaining components are derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. By Hooke's law (3.27), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), and likewise \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). From \(0 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E\) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\).
  3. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) into \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E\). We get \(\big((1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)/E = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)\), which is (11.50) with \(E' = E^*\) and \(\nu' = \nu^*\), since \((1 - \nu^2)/E = 1/E^*\) and \(\nu/(1 - \nu) = \nu^*\). For the shear, \((1 + \nu^*)/E^* = \frac{1}{1 - \nu}\cdot\frac{1 - \nu^2}{E} = (1 + \nu)/E\), which is Hooke's law for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
  4. By step 2 all stresses depend only on \(x\) and \(y\) (also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is a function of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\)), so in the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31) all derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by step 2. The first two equations are (11.49), and the third reads \(0 + 0 + 0 + b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and is satisfied because \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  5. Five of the six equations (1.76) have in every term a derivative with respect to \(z\) or one of the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), so by step 1 they vanish (as remark 1 after theorem 1.133 says). The theorem applies because \(\vek u\) has continuous derivatives up to third order. The first remains, \(S_1 = 0\). By lemma 11.31 with \(E^*\) and \(\nu^*\), \(E^*\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu^*)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). Since \(1 + \nu^* = 1/(1 - \nu)\), \(S_1 = 0\) is equivalent to (11.54).

Source: SADD (7.1.2) to (7.1.7) (PDF pp. 149-151), CM (7.5.1) to (7.5.5) (PDF p. 323). Point 5 is derived with lemma 11.31, not with the table of the source.

Points 1 and 2 are written in figure 11.96 on the left, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\). On the right, for the thin plate with axes \(x\) and \(z\), the edge load lies in the plane of the plate and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) holds on the free faces. We treat this state later. The proof showed that in plane strain, of the fifteen equations of the boundary value problem (section 11.1) a system in the plane remains with unknowns \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) follows from them. The state is not an approximation. The equations of the three-dimensional problem are all satisfied, so the field is an exact solution if the conditions on the surface are satisfied too. On the lateral surface they are, if the force and the normal there have no component \(z\) and do not depend on \(z\), as we assumed. At the ends \(z = 0\) and \(z = \ell\) the displacement in the direction \(z\) is zero and the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero, which corresponds exactly to ends held by smooth rigid walls without friction (SADD, PDF p. 151). The force with which the walls hold the prism is obtained by integrating \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) at an end over the cross-section \(R\) (SADD, PDF pp. 151-152), so we know it only after the solution. A prism with free ends does not have this state exactly, because at the ends we would have to remove \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) with a corrective solution that has zero on the lateral surface and the opposite force at the ends, and its exact form is usually very hard to find. SADD (PDF p. 152) therefore replaces the correction by a statically equivalent distribution by Saint-Venant's principle and uses the solution approximately for a long prism with arbitrary ends. The principle is proved only in special cases, so in section 11.11 we treat it as a principle with evidence, not as a theorem.

Point 3 of the theorem gives the strains from the stresses. The inverse relation, stresses from strains, we read from the second matrix of (11.51) with \(E' = E^*\) and \(\nu' = \nu^*\). The three numbers we need are

  1. \(1 - \nu^{*2} = \dfrac{(1 - \nu)^2 - \nu^2}{(1 - \nu)^2} = \dfrac{1 - 2\nu}{(1 - \nu)^2}\), so \(\dfrac{E^*}{1 - \nu^{*2}} = \dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\cdot\dfrac{(1 - \nu)^2}{1 - 2\nu} = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\),
  2. \(\dfrac{E^*}{1 - \nu^{*2}}\,\nu^* = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\cdot\dfrac{\nu}{1 - \nu} = \dfrac{E\,\nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\),
  3. \(\dfrac{E^*}{1 - \nu^{*2}}\,(1 - \nu^*) = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\cdot\dfrac{1 - 2\nu}{1 - \nu} = \dfrac{E}{1 + \nu}\).

We take out the common factor \(E/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) and get the stiffness matrix of plane strain in \(E\) and \(\nu\),

\[\begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\begin{bmatrix} 1 - \nu & \nu & 0 \\ \nu & 1 - \nu & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 - 2\nu \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{11.55}\]

With the Voigt shear angle \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) the corner is \(\tfrac12\,(1 - 2\nu)\). Lamé's form (3.28) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives the same matrix: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with \(\lambda = E\,\nu/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) and \(2\mu = E/(1 + \nu)\).

For steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) the three different numbers of the matrix (11.55) are \(263{,}846\) GPa, \(113{,}077\) GPa and \(150{,}769\) GPa. The first is the ratio \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) when \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero, and it is \(34{,}6\,\%\) larger than \(E\). Where the difference comes from is shown in figure 11.97. Take a steel cube with side \(L\), which we draw in two views, on top along the axis \(z\) and below along the axis \(y\). Three times we stretch it in the direction \(x\) by the same elongation \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,L\). The free cube contracts in the directions \(y\) and \(z\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 196\ \text{GPa}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) suffices, that is \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). In the second case we put it between smooth rigid walls in the direction \(y\), which allow it neither to contract nor to expand in that direction but let it slide along them. The cube would contract in the direction \(y\), so the walls pull it outwards with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). This follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})/E = 0\), since in the direction \(z\) the cube is free and contracts, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E = (1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and the ratio is \(E/(1 - \nu^2) = 215{,}385\) GPa. In the third case we add walls in the direction \(z\), like the two that hold the ends of the long prism, and the cube contracts in no transverse direction. The ratio is \(263{,}846\) GPa, the first number of the matrix. So the same elongation needs ever longer arrows \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The held direction \(y\) contributes the factor \(1/(1 - \nu^2) = 1{,}099\), the walls in the direction \(z\) a further factor \((1 - \nu)^2/(1 - 2\nu) = 1{,}225\), together \(1{,}346\). At the same \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), a piece that can neither lengthen nor shorten along the axis \(z\) is therefore \(22{,}5\,\%\) stiffer in the plane than a piece that is free in the direction \(z\).

Hooke's law in Lamé's form, which gave us the matrix (11.55) above, also gives a second expression for the axial stress. The third row of (3.28) at \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) = \frac{E\,\nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) .\]

The axial stress is thus found in two ways: from the stresses in the plane, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\), or from the strains in the plane. The two expressions are the same equation, since the sum of the first two rows of (11.55) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\), which multiplied by \(\nu\) gives \(\lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\).

The equation (11.54) we already know. Theorem 11.9, applied to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with \(\Theta = (1 + \nu)\,\Theta_2\) and \(\partial^2\Theta/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\Theta/\partial y^2 = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta_2\), gives in the sum of both equations \(2\,\nabla^2\Theta_2 = -\frac{2\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - 2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), that is, \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = -\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu)\). Both paths therefore give the same equation.

We test the state on the tube. A tube with inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm made of steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), pressure \(p = 30\) MPa in the bore) is long, and its ends are held by smooth rigid walls. Its displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,x/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,y/r\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) have the form (11.53). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) from theorem 11.28 give the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A = 48\) MPa at every point of the wall, so by point 2 of the theorem the axial stress is constant,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}) = 0{,}3 \cdot 48 = 14{,}4\ \text{MPa} .\]

The second way, from the strains, gives the same: in the local axes of the tube \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E = 1{,}3 \cdot 0{,}4 \cdot 48/196\,000 = 1{,}2735 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(\lambda = 113\,077\) MPa, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 113\,077\ \text{MPa} \cdot 1{,}2735 \cdot 10^{-4} = 14{,}4\) MPa. Since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is tensile, the walls must pull the tube outwards at each end, with a force \(14{,}4\ \text{MPa} \cdot \pi\,(60^2 - 40^2)\ \text{mm}^2 = 90{,}48\) kN. Without them a tube \(1\) m long would shorten by \(73{,}5\ \mu\)m. The radial displacement of the inner surface is by point 3 equal to \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = a\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = a\,\frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\big) = 16{,}873\ \mu\)m. For a tube with free ends, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a)\) \(= a\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E = 17{,}755\ \mu\)m. The walls reduced the displacement by \(5\,\%\). Why, is shown in section 11.9.3, where we draw the two tubes one below the other. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are the same in both cases, as figure 11.98 shows, with the radius \(r\) in mm on the horizontal axis and the stress in MPa on the vertical one. What differs are the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and the axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\). For free ends, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\), and between the walls it is zero.

For temperature the same path holds. In plane strain with temperature change \(\Delta T\), the condition \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives from the thermoelastic Hooke's law (theorem 3.23) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), since \(0 = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\) (SADD §12.3.1, PDF pp. 380-381, BARBER §14.1, PDF p. 234). The first term is the same as without temperature. Figure 11.99 shows the second on the simplest case: we heat a prism with the axis \(z\) horizontal and a free lateral surface uniformly by \(\Delta T\). Without walls the prism of length \(\ell\) lengthens by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\ell\) past the old end. Between smooth rigid walls, however, it stays \(\ell\) long: the walls push it back by the whole elongation, from where the free prism would reach. The smooth walls let the cross-section expand freely in the plane \(x\), \(y\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) are zero and only the second term remains, the compressive stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\). It just cancels the thermal elongation, since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T = 0\). BOWER (PDF p. 99, formulas as an image) expresses the stresses with the strains in the plane and has in \(\sigma_{11}\), \(\sigma_{22}\) the term \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\), and in \(\sigma_{33}\) the term \(+E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\). Both signs cannot come out of \(\varepsilon_{33} = 0\) at the same time: if we insert the stresses in the plane in terms of strains into \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), the temperature term that remains is \(-2\nu\,E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\). The plus sign at \(\sigma_{33}\) is a misprint, the correct formula is above.

11.9.3 Generalized plane strain (posplošeno ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Where does the axial stress \(14{,}4\) MPa come from, and what happens if there are no walls? In figure 11.100 we put the same steel tube three times, each time in longitudinal section, with the axis \(z\) horizontal and the radius \(r\) vertical.

The tube without pressure we draw dashed, it is \(\ell = 1\) m long. The pressure \(p = 30\) MPa pushes on the surface of the bore. Let us start with the middle tube (b), which nobody holds at the ends, so in every cross-section the axial force \(N\) is zero. In the tube the axial stress is the same over the whole cross-section, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S\) (theorem 11.28), and since the free ends carry nothing, \(S = 0\) and with it \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The pressure widens it: the inner surface moves away from the axis by \(17{,}755\ \mu\)m. At the same time the tube shortens. The cause is Poisson's ratio: a material that we stretch in one direction contracts in the directions perpendicular to it, and the other way round (definition 3.8). The tensile hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) therefore shortens the tube along the axis, the compressive radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) lengthens it, and since their sum \(48\) MPa is positive, the shortening wins,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})}{E} = -\frac{0{,}3 \cdot 48}{196\,000} = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5} .\]

On a metre of length this is a shortening by \(73{,}5\ \mu\)m.

Now let us grip the ends of this tube and pull it back to its original length. We must add the axial strain \(+7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\), the same over the whole cross-section, and for that, by Hooke's law for uniaxial tension (lemma 3.12), we need the axial stress

\[E \cdot 7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5} = 196\,000\ \text{MPa} \cdot 7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5} = 14{,}4\ \text{MPa} .\]

We have obtained the top tube (a) between smooth rigid walls: the length does not change, and the walls pull each end with a force \(90{,}48\) kN. The axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\) of plane strain is thus exactly the tension that cancels the shortening of the free tube. The added tension narrows the tube laterally, so the radial displacement of the bore is smaller, \(16{,}873\ \mu\)m instead of \(17{,}755\ \mu\)m. The difference \(0{,}882\ \mu\)m is \(\nu \cdot 7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 40\ \text{mm}\), the lateral contraction of the radius \(a\) under the added tension, and this is those \(5\,\%\). We were allowed to add because the equations are linear, and the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) stayed the same because uniform axial tension does not load the lateral surface: its stress vector on a surface whose normal has no component \(z\) is zero.

The walls are not the only possibility. We can pull the ends with any force. The bottom tube (c) has closed ends, like a pressure vessel. The pressure pushes on the caps with a force \(p\,\pi a^2 = 150{,}80\) kN, and this force is carried by the wall of the tube as axial tension \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 150{,}80\ \text{kN}/\big(\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\big) = 24\) MPa. This is more than the \(14{,}4\) MPa that keep the length, so the tube lengthens by \(49{,}0\ \mu\)m, and the bore widens by \(16{,}286\ \mu\)m. The three tubes differ in a single number, the axial strain, which each time is the same over the whole cross-section:

ends axial force \(N\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) \(\Delta\ell\) at \(\ell = 1\) m \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a)\)
(a) smooth rigid walls \(90{,}48\) kN \(14{,}4\) MPa \(0\) \(0\) \(16{,}873\ \mu\)m
(b) free \(0\) \(0\) \(-7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\) \(-73{,}5\ \mu\)m \(17{,}755\ \mu\)m
(c) closed \(150{,}80\) kN \(24\) MPa \(4{,}898 \cdot 10^{-5}\) \(49{,}0\ \mu\)m \(16{,}286\ \mu\)m

State (a) we know. States (b) and (c) generalize it by replacing the zero in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) with a constant number, and hence the name.

Definition 11.34 Generalized plane strain (posplošeno ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Let the body be a prism with axis \(z\) and cross-section \(R\), an open set in the plane \(x\), \(y\), and let \(\varepsilon_0\) be a real number. A displacement field \(\vek u\) of the body is a generalized plane strain state (generalized plane strain, in Slovenian abbreviated GRDS) with axial strain \(\varepsilon_0\) if it has the form

\[\vek u = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y),\ \varepsilon_0\,z\big) , \tag{11.56}\]

where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) are continuously differentiable functions of two variables on \(R\). The body force has the form \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), b_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\). For \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\) this is plane strain (definition 11.32).

Source: BOWER §4.1.7 (PDF p. 230) for the axially symmetric cylinder: displacement \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero for plane strain and constant for generalized plane strain. The form for a prism with an arbitrary cross-section in Cartesian axes is ours.

The definition covers a constant axial strain, that is, a prism whose ends are only pulled or pushed. A wider form allows an axial strain that is an affine function, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\), which besides tension also describes bending of the prism by moments at the ends. BARBER (§3.1.1, PDF p. 55) obtains such a state by adding to a plane strain state a beam with the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = B + C\,x + D\,y\) (3.5), the axial force (3.6) and the moments (3.7), since the axial strain \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/E\) of such a beam is affine. We do not need this form here. In the lectures the state is written by the condition \(\varepsilon_{33} = \text{const} \ne 0\). The name must not be confused with generalized plane stress of section 11.9.6, which is something else: an average through the thickness of a thin plate.

Which of the equations of plane strain change? On the tube we saw that only the axial stress and the lateral contraction do. The theorem says that this is so for every prism, and why. We can already see the idea of the proof on the free tube if we split its displacement into two parts.

Take the tube between walls, which we know from section 11.9.2. The pressure widens the bore by \(16{,}873\ \mu\)m, the length stays, and the walls pull the tube with an axial stress of \(14{,}4\) MPa. Next to it we draw the same tube without pressure, which we only compress uniformly along the axis with the axial strain \(\varepsilon_0 = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\). It shortens by \(73{,}5\ \mu\)m, because of Poisson's ratio its cross-section widens and the bore moves away from the axis by \(\nu\,|\varepsilon_0|\,a = 0{,}882\ \mu\)m. At every point the same axial stress \(E\,\varepsilon_0 = -14{,}4\) MPa acts, the other stresses are zero. We add the two displacements, which we may because the equations are linear. The sum is the free tube: the shortening of \(73{,}5\ \mu\)m comes from the second part, the bore \(16{,}873 + 0{,}882 = 17{,}755\ \mu\)m from both, and the axial stress \(14{,}4 - 14{,}4 = 0\) is a column in which the blue part cancels the blackwhite one. For the closed tube we have \(\varepsilon_0 = 4{,}898 \cdot 10^{-5}\), the blue part points up, \(E\,\varepsilon_0 = 9{,}6\) MPa, and the column is \(24\) MPa high. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are carried only by the left part, since the middle one has only axial stress, so they are the same in all tubes. The theorem says that this split works for every prism.

Theorem 11.35 Equations of generalized plane strain (enačbe posplošenega ravninskega deformacijskega stanja)

Let a prism with axis \(z\) and a bounded cross-section \(R\) of area \(A_{\mathrm{R}}\) have an isotropic and homogeneous material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), and let \(\vek u\) be a generalized plane strain state with axial strain \(\varepsilon_0\) (definition 11.34) that has continuous partial derivatives up to third order and whose body force has continuous first derivatives. Let \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\). Then:

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and the remaining three strain components depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. The field \(\vek u^0 = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_0\,x,\ u_{\mathrm{y}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_0\,y,\ 0\big)\) is a plane strain state with the same body force and with the same stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) as \(\vek u\).
  3. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) + E\,\varepsilon_0\).
  4. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu^*\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{E^*} - \nu\,\varepsilon_0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu^*\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E^*} - \nu\,\varepsilon_0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\).
  5. The stresses in the plane satisfy the equilibrium equations (11.49) and equation (11.54), the same as in plane strain.
  6. If the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\mathrm{d}A\) exists, the axial force \(N = \int_{\mathrm{R}} \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) is the same in all cross-sections and
    \[N = \nu \int_{\mathrm{R}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,A_{\mathrm{R}}\,\varepsilon_0 , \qquad \text{so} \qquad \varepsilon_0 = \frac{1}{E\,A_{\mathrm{R}}}\Big(N - \nu \int_{\mathrm{R}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\mathrm{d}A\Big) . \tag{11.57}\]
Proof

We must show six claims. We split the displacement into a plane strain state and a uniform axial tension, as in figure 11.101 we built the free tube from the tube between the walls and the uniformly compressed tube, and for the first part we use theorem 11.33.

  1. We differentiate (11.56) (definition 1.121). The third component \(\varepsilon_0\,z\) depends only on \(z\), the first two only on \(x\) and \(y\). So \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial(\varepsilon_0\,z)/\partial z = \varepsilon_0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial(\varepsilon_0\,z)/\partial x\big) = 0\) and likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The remaining components are derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\). This is point 1.
  2. We write the displacement as a sum \(\vek u = \vek u^0 + \vek u^1\) with \(\vek u^1 = \varepsilon_0\,(-\nu\,x,\ -\nu\,y,\ z)\). The field \(\vek u^0 = \vek u - \vek u^1\) has third component zero and the first two depending only on \(x\) and \(y\), so it has the form (11.53), and it is as smooth as \(\vek u\). In figure 11.101, \(\vek u^0\) is the tube between the walls, and \(\vek u^1\) is the uniform change of length together with the lateral change of the cross-section that goes with it: for \(\varepsilon_0 < 0\) the cross-section widens, for \(\varepsilon_0 > 0\) it narrows.
  3. The strains of the field \(\vek u^1\) are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_0\), the shear strains are zero. By lemma 3.12 on uniaxial stress these are the strains of uniaxial tension \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = E\,\varepsilon_0\) along the axis \(z\), and since Hooke's law (3.28) determines the stresses uniquely from the strains, \(\vek u^1\) has exactly these stresses: \(\sigma^1_{\mathrm{zz}} = E\,\varepsilon_0\), all other components are zero.
  4. Strains are derivatives of displacements, and stresses are by (3.28) linear in the strains, so the stresses of the sum \(\vek u^0 + \vek u^1\) are the sum of the stresses of the two fields. The field \(\vek u^1\) contributes only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), so \(\vek u^0\) has the same \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) as \(\vek u\). The stresses of the field \(\vek u^1\) are constant, so they contribute nothing to the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31) and \(\vek u^0\) is in equilibrium with the same body force. This is point 2.
  5. For the plane strain state \(\vek u^0\) theorem 11.33 holds. By its point 2, \(\sigma^0_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma^0_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma^0_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\). We add the stresses of the field \(\vek u^1\) from step 3, as in the figure we stack the blue part \(E\,\varepsilon_0\) on the blackwhite part \(\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\), and get point 3.
  6. By point 3 of the same theorem the strains of the field \(\vek u^0\) in the plane are \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu^*\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E^*\), \((\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu^*\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})/E^*\) and \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\). We add the strains of the field \(\vek u^1\) from step 3, \(-\nu\,\varepsilon_0\) for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and zero for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and get point 4.
  7. By points 4 and 5 of the same theorem the stresses of the field \(\vek u^0\) in the plane satisfy (11.49) and (11.54). By step 4 these are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\). This is point 5.
  8. By point 3, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is a function of \(x\) and \(y\) only, so the integral over the cross-section is the same at every \(z\). We integrate point 3 over \(R\). The number \(E\,\varepsilon_0\) is constant and \(\int_{\mathrm{R}} \mathrm{d}A = A_{\mathrm{R}}\), so \(N = \nu \int_{\mathrm{R}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,A_{\mathrm{R}}\,\varepsilon_0\). Since \(E\,A_{\mathrm{R}} > 0\), we solve the equation for \(\varepsilon_0\). This is point 6.

Source: BOWER §4.1.7 to §4.1.9 (PDF pp. 230-234) for the axially symmetric cylinder: Hooke's law with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) constant, the condition \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b 2\pi r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm{d}r = F_{\mathrm{z}}\) for the axial strain, and for the tube under pressure \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = F_{\mathrm{z}}/\big(\pi E\,(b^2 - a^2)\big) - 2\nu\,(p_{\mathrm{a}} a^2 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^2)/\big(E\,(b^2 - a^2)\big)\), which is (11.57) for the tube. The split into a plane strain state and uniaxial tension and the form for an arbitrary cross-section are ours. SymPy checked points 3 and 4 with Hooke's law (3.27).

The proof showed that generalized plane strain is plane strain to which a uniform axial tension or compression is added. The stresses in the plane of the cross-section are therefore the same as we would get between walls, and we compute them from the same equations. Two things change: the axial stress shifts by \(E\,\varepsilon_0\), and the displacements in the plane get the term \(-\nu\,\varepsilon_0\,(x, y)\), which narrows or widens the cross-section uniformly. The number \(\varepsilon_0\) is fixed by the axial force at the end through equation (11.57).

Let us test it on the tube. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 48\) MPa is constant and \(A_{\mathrm{R}} = \pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\), so \(\varepsilon_0 = \big(N/A_{\mathrm{R}} - 0{,}3 \cdot 48\ \text{MPa}\big)/E\). For free ends \(N = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_0 = -14{,}4/196\,000 = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\), for closed ends \(N/A_{\mathrm{R}} = 24\) MPa and \(\varepsilon_0 = (24 - 14{,}4)/196\,000 = 4{,}898 \cdot 10^{-5}\), as in the table. This is the constant \(\varepsilon_0 = (S - 2\nu A)/E\) of theorem 11.28 with \(S = N/A_{\mathrm{R}}\) and \(2A = 48\) MPa: the Lamé solution for a tube with axial stress \(S\) at the ends is a generalized plane strain state, only we did not name it there.

When is this state an exact solution? On the lateral surface the conditions are satisfied as in plane strain, because the added tension does not load the lateral surface. At the ends the force per unit area must equal \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) + E\,\varepsilon_0\) at every point of the cross-section. For the tube this expression is constant, so a uniformly loaded or free end is satisfied exactly.

For a cross-section in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) varies, equation (11.57) takes care only of the right total force \(N\). Figure 11.102 shows a long prism with cross-section height \(h\) with a free end on the right, where the prescribed axial stress is zero. The obtained \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is not zero there, so we must add to the end the difference between the prescribed and the obtained distribution. Its total force is zero, because \(N\) is right. In case (a) the difference is tensile at the edges and compressive in the middle, symmetric about the middle, so its moment is zero too. Such a load we feel only near the end, up to a distance of about \(h\), and not farther from the end. This is Saint-Venant's principle, which we treat later in this chapter. BARBER (PDF p. 55) expects the stresses of such a difference only within a distance from the end comparable with the dimensions of the cross-section, and BOWER (PDF p. 230) gives for a cylinder the rule that the solution is applicable about three radii from the ends. In case (b) the difference is tensile at the top and compressive at the bottom. The force is again zero, but the moment is not, and this moment bends the straight prism along its whole length. A constant axial strain does not capture this, only the wider form with an affine axial strain does, with which BARBER adds the two moments as well. The assumption of a constant axial strain is therefore right in the middle part of a long prism, and fails near the ends and where the difference at the end or the load on the end carries a moment that bends the prism. In the tube there is no moment, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is constant. The effect of the assumption compared with the walls is, for our tube, a shift of the axial stress from \(14{,}4\) MPa to \(0\) or \(24\) MPa and of the radial displacement of the bore by at most \(0{,}882\ \mu\)m, which is \(5{,}2\,\%\) of the displacement between the walls.

11.9.4 Plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje)

The ring of section 1.18.5 is 2 mm thick and its radius is 40 to 60 mm. It is therefore a thin plate, loaded only on its edge in its own plane (figure 11.96, right). The axis \(z\) is perpendicular to the plate, with the origin on its middle surface. The ring's faces \(z = \pm h\) with \(h = 1\) mm are free, so there is no force per unit area there, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The plate is thin, so the three stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which are zero on both faces, cannot grow to a large value over the short path \(2h\) between the faces. We expect them to be near zero throughout the thickness, and the other three components to be almost independent of \(z\) (SADD, PDF p. 152). May we assume this? Let us assume it first as a model and only then ask what it means.

A thin plate that we load only in its own plane is in Slovenian engineering usage also called a wall (stena). The name says how it carries: like a wall, which passes the load above it down by stresses in its own plane and does not bend in doing so. From chapter 18 on, the word plate will mean a thin flat body loaded perpendicular to its plane, which carries by bending. The thickness we write \(2h\) here, because the origin of the axis \(z\) is on the middle surface and the free faces are at \(z = \pm h\). Chapter 15 writes the whole thickness \(t\), and chapter 18 writes \(h\), so the \(h\) there equals the \(2h\) here.

Definition 11.36 Plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Let a plate loaded in its own plane (a wall, stena) be the set of points \(\{(x, y, z)\ :\ (x, y) \in R,\ -h \le z \le h\}\) with an open set \(R\) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) and a small thickness \(2h\) compared with the dimensions of \(R\). The stress state of the plate is a plane stress state (plane stress) as a model if

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 \tag{11.58}\]

and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. The body force has the form \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), b_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\).

Source: SADD §7.2 (7.2.1) (PDF p. 152), TIMO (PDF p. 289) for a thin plate loaded on the edge in its plane. The word "model" is ours and stresses that the definition does not claim that a three-dimensional solution exists.

The definition demands the state and does not claim that the three-dimensional equations allow it. What follows from it and where it disagrees with the equations is told by the theorem.

Theorem 11.37 Equations of plane stress (enačbe ravninskega napetostnega stanja)

Let a plate have an isotropic and homogeneous material with \(E\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and let the stress state of the plate be a plane stress state (definition 11.36) whose stresses have continuous partial derivatives up to second order and whose body force has continuous first derivatives. Let the strains be those that follow from the stresses by Hooke's law. Then:

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\), and the strains in the plane are (11.50) with \(E' = E\) and \(\nu' = \nu\). All strains depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. Of the equilibrium equations, (11.49) hold, and the third holds by itself.
  3. If the stresses satisfy the equilibrium equations of point 2, the first compatibility equation is equivalent to the equation
    \[\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = -(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.59}\]
  4. Of the remaining five compatibility equations the fourth and the fifth are satisfied by themselves, and the second, third and sixth read \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = 0\), \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 = 0\) and \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). All three hold exactly when \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is an affine function, that is, of the form \(\alpha_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\) with \(\alpha_0\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) constants (on the connected set \(R\)). For \(\nu \ne 0\) this is exactly when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is such a function, for \(\nu = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all three always hold.
Proof

We must show four claims. The first three are similar to the proof for plane strain, the fourth is new and says where the model deviates from the three-dimensional equations.

  1. From (11.58) and Hooke's law (3.27), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E = 0\), likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\). For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + 0))/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) this is (11.50) with \(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\), the shear is the same. The strains are functions of stresses that depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. As in step 4 of the proof of theorem 11.33: the derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  3. Lemma 11.31, whose condition is the equilibrium equations of point 2, with \(E' = E\) and \(\nu' = \nu\) gives \(E\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), so \(S_1 = 0\) is equivalent to (11.59).
  4. By step 1 all strains are independent of \(z\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). In the second equation (1.76) therefore \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial z^2\) and \(2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y\,\partial z\) are zero and \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = 0\) remains. The third gives likewise \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 = 0\) and the sixth \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). Every term of the fourth and fifth equation has a derivative with respect to \(z\) or contains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) or \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), so they are satisfied. If all three second derivatives of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero, both first derivatives are constant (a function with zero gradient on a connected set is constant), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\). Conversely, such a function has all three second derivatives zero. By step 1, for \(\nu \ne 0\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\nu\), and for \(\nu = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\).

The proof showed that stresses which satisfy the plane equations (11.49) and the equation (11.59) satisfy three-dimensional equilibrium and the first compatibility equation, which have the same form in the plane as in plane strain, and the remaining three compatibility equations only when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and with it the axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), is an affine function (for \(\nu \ne 0\)). When these three equations do not hold, the strains that Hooke's law determines from the stress field are not the strains of any displacement field with continuous third derivatives (theorem 1.133), so the stress field is not the stress field of any displacement. If in a simply connected plate the equation of point 3 and the condition of point 4 hold, all six compatibility equations are satisfied and the strains belong to a displacement field (theorem 4.120). The model is then no longer a model but an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations (equilibrium and the conditions on the faces are satisfied), namely for the edge load that the model gives: the traction vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on the edge, which does not vary over the thickness. For the ring this is true, because the internal pressure acts equally over the whole thickness. If, however, an edge force acts only near one face, the model describes only its resultant correctly and is not an exact solution.

What it means that \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is an affine function we see on its graph (figure 11.103, middle). Over the plane of the plate we draw at every point \((x, y)\) the height \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}(x, y)\). The graph of an affine function \(\alpha_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\) is a tilted plane. On it we draw two lines in the direction \(y\) at two different \(x\). Both are straight and have the same slope \(\gamma\). Since they are straight, \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = 0\), and since the slope is the same at every \(x\), \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). The lines in the direction \(x\) are straight on the plane too, so \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 = 0\). These are the three equations of point 4.

A ring with free faces is therefore an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations and not only a model. Let us look at it from above, in figure 11.104 on the right, where we see the top face \(y = c\) with the axis \(x\) along the cantilever and the axis \(z\) across the thickness, \(2h = 20\) mm. The shear, however, gets the term \(-\tfrac12\,z^2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = -\tfrac12\,h^2 \cdot 7{,}175 \cdot 10^{-3} = -3{,}59 \cdot 10^{-7}\) at \(z = \pm h\), because the parabola at height \(y\) is bowed in proportion to \(y\) and neighbouring layers therefore shift against each other, which is \(5{,}8\,\%\) of the largest shear strain of the model, \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E = 6{,}22 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Plane stress is therefore useful in practice for a beam, but it must be known as a model and not as an exact solution.

11.9.5 Change of constants (zamenjava konstant)

Let us compare theorems 11.33 and 11.37, that is, the prism between walls and the thin plate. In both, the equilibrium equations (11.49), Hooke's law (11.50) and the compatibility equation \(S_1 = 0\) hold, which by lemma 11.31 is written with the constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\). The difference is only in which numbers we insert for \(E'\) and \(\nu'\). Therefore a solution of one state with other constants becomes a solution of the other.

Corollary 11.38 Change of constants (zamenjava konstant)

Let \(E\) and \(\nu\) be constants of the material. The plane system is the system of equations (11.49), (11.50) and \(S_1 = 0\) with constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\), for prescribed forces and displacements in the plane.

  1. Plane strain of a material with \(E\), \(\nu\) solves the same plane system as plane stress (model) with the constants \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) in the places of \(E'\), \(\nu'\), for the same body force and the same conditions in the plane. The restriction \(\nu^* < \tfrac12\) of theorem 3.64 is not important here, because we use only the equations. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) of a solution of one system are therefore a solution of the other. That the solution of each is unique, the corollary does not claim.
  2. Plane stress of a material with \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(\nu > -\tfrac12\) solves the same plane system as plane strain of a material with \(E_{\mathrm{p}} = E\,(1 + 2\nu)/(1 + \nu)^2\) and \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} = \nu/(1 + \nu)\).

Proof

We must show that both states lead to the same system of equations in the plane.

On replacing \(E \to E^*\) and \(\nu \to \nu^*\) in the stress state the system is the same. The conditions in the plane are the same: displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the edge and the force in the plane \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) with \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).

11.9.6 Generalized plane stress (posplošeno ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Point 4 of theorem 11.37 showed that plane stress is in general only a model. Does it have an exact meaning? It does: it is not the field in the plate, but the average of the field over the thickness (figure 11.106). For a function \(f(x, y, z)\) on the plate between the faces \(z = -h\) and \(z = h\), so of thickness \(2h\), the thickness average (povprečje po debelini) is

\[\langle f\rangle (x, y) = \frac{1}{2h}\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h} f(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}z . \tag{11.60}\]

The figure draws the profile \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(z)\) at a chosen point \((x, y)\) to the right of the plate, with \(z\) on the vertical axis and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) on the horizontal one. The vertical blue line is its average: the rectangle between it and the vertical axis has the same area as the region between the profile and the vertical axis, because both areas equal \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}z\). The theorem says that the averages satisfy the plane equations exactly, if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) really holds in the plate.

Theorem 11.39 Generalized plane stress (posplošeno ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Let \(\vek u\) be a solution of the three-dimensional problem in the plate \(\{(x, y, z)\ :\ (x, y) \in R,\ -h \le z \le h\}\) of an isotropic homogeneous material, with free faces (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) on \(z = \pm h\)), with body force \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components \(b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}}\) have continuous first derivatives, and with displacement with continuous partial derivatives up to third order. Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) hold at every point of the plate. On the edge of the plate with normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) let the stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) be prescribed. Then for the averages \(\langle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}\rangle\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\rangle\), \(\langle u_\alpha\rangle\) and \(\langle b_\alpha\rangle\) (\(\alpha, \beta = x, y\)) the following hold:

  1. \(\partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle/\partial x + \partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/\partial y + \langle b_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle = 0\) and \(\partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/\partial x + \partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle/\partial y + \langle b_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle = 0\).
  2. \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle = (\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle - \nu\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle)/E\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle = (\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle - \nu\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle)/E\) and \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle = (1 + \nu)\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/E\), that is, (11.50) with \(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\).
  3. \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle = \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle/\partial x\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle = \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle/\partial y\) and \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle = \tfrac12\,(\partial\langle u_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle/\partial y + \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle/\partial x)\).
  4. On the edge, \(\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \langle\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle\) and \(\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \langle\bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle\).

The averages therefore satisfy all equations of plane stress, including \(S_1 = 0\) and (11.59), with the edge load averaged over the thickness.

Proof

We must show four claims and conclude about \(S_1\) from them. The average is linear and the derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) commute with it, so every linear equation carries over to the averages, if it has derivatives with respect to \(z\) only in a term that we know how to average.

  1. We average the equilibrium equation \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). The derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) we exchange with the integral (lemma 4.19, the limits \(\pm h\) are constant, the integrand and its derivatives are continuous). For the term with \(z\), by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55), \(\frac{1}{2h}\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h}\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{2h}\,[\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}]_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h} = 0\), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) on both free faces. In figure 11.105 on the left is an example of the profile \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(z)\) over the thickness, which is zero on both faces. To its right is its derivative \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z\): in the lower half, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) increases, it is positive, in the upper half negative. The areas above and below the axis are equal, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) returns to zero, so the integral of the derivative is zero. The first equation of point 1 remains, the second is the same with \(y\) in place of \(x\).
  2. Hooke's law (3.27) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\) at every point of the plate. These equations are linear, so we average them without losing anything.
  3. The strains in the plane are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\) (definition 1.121). We average and exchange the derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) with the integral as in step 1.
  4. The boundary condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\) (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.26, with \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\)) and the similar one for \(y\) we average over \(z\). The normal does not depend on \(z\), so it is taken out of the average.
  5. Points 1 to 3 are the equilibrium equations, Hooke's law and the relation of strains to displacements of plane stress. The derivatives of \(\langle u_\alpha\rangle\) up to third order are continuous (lemma 4.19), so by theorem 1.133 \(S_1 = 0\) holds for the averages, and from lemma 11.31 the equation (11.59) follows.

Source: SADD §7.3 (7.3.1) to (7.3.7) (PDF pp. 155-156). The source states the theorem without steps, we derived steps 1 to 5 ourselves. That we do not need the symmetry of the load in \(z\) is our finding: symmetry is the reason why \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) is plausible (SADD, PDF pp. 155-156), not a condition of the proof.

The proof showed that the thickness averages are an exact solution of the plane problem if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero throughout the plate. Generalized plane strain (definition 11.34) has a similar name, but the two generalizations are not related: there the generalization is a constant axial strain of a long prism and the equations hold at every point, here the generalization is the average through the thickness of a thin plate and the equations hold for the averages. The assumption \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) in the interior is the approximation: on both faces it is zero, in the interior it is not necessarily. For \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) on the faces, from equilibrium \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) it follows that \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z\) is zero on the faces (the terms with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero there, because they are zero on the whole face), and SADD (PDF p. 156) concludes from a Taylor series that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is of order \(h^2\) for a thin plate (for a bounded second derivative). This bound can be written as a number. Let \(|\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z^2| \le M\) over the whole thickness. In figure 11.105 on the right is the profile \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}(z)\), which has the value zero and the derivative zero on both faces, so it touches the axis \(z\) there. Hooke's law of point 2 then lacks the term \(-\nu\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\rangle/E\), for which \(|\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\rangle| \le \frac{1}{2h}\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h}\tfrac12\,M\,(h - |z|)^2\,\mathrm{d}z = M\,h^2/6\). We test the theorem on a plate of height \(2c = 40\) mm and thickness \(2h = 20\) mm with stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y + m\,z\) and the other components zero. The field is linear, so it is in equilibrium without body force and satisfies the compatibility equations, and the faces \(z = \pm h\) are free. A thin plate loaded in its plane is otherwise in plane stress only as a model. This state is exact when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is an affine function, the plate is simply connected or we know the displacement field, as for the ring with internal pressure (theorem 11.37, point 4), and the edge load is uniform over the thickness. Otherwise the thickness average is a solution if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero throughout the plate (theorem 11.39), which for a thin plate is good but not exact. For the cantilever of chapter 5 we saw that the field of the model is not exactly a three-dimensional field, and that the deviation is by estimate negligible, \(7{,}2\) nm against \(239{,}2\ \mu\)m.

We have thus made the approximation on two different sides of the model. On the physical side we replace the body or its supports by simpler ones and solve the equations of the replaced body exactly: the free ends of a long tube are replaced by walls or by a uniform axial force, and the error stays near the ends. On the mathematical side we keep the body and do not satisfy all the equations: in plane stress we drop three compatibility equations, and the error is all over the plate: for the cantilever the bowing of the line across the thickness is the same at every \(x\), because the term \(-\tfrac12\,z^2\,\nu\,F\,y/(E I)\) does not contain \(x\), and the stress correction is \(0{,}06\,\%\) of the largest stress. For every model, a computer model too, it is worth asking on which side its approximation lies.

The plane states are not the only plane theories. One more is antiplane strain (antiravninsko stanje), which we draw in figure 11.107 on the left. Let us take a long prism with the axis \(z\) along it and choose two flat cross-sections in it. Every point of a cross-section moves only along the axis, by \(w(x, y)\), so the displacement is \(\vek u = (0, 0, w(x, y))\) (SADD §7.4, PDF p. 157). Since all arrows are parallel to the axis, no point moves in the plane of the cross-section, and since \(w\) does not depend on \(z\), both cross-sections bow into the same surface. The restriction to \(w\) gives a single equation for a single function, without body force the Laplace equation \(\nabla^2 w = \partial^2 w/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 w/\partial y^2 = 0\) (SADD, PDF p. 157). The example in the figure is \(w = k\,x\,y\) with a constant \(k\). Its lines in the directions \(x\) and \(y\) are straight on the bowed surface, so both second derivatives are zero and the equation holds. In torsion, which is treated in chapter 16, the displacement of the points of a cross-section along the axis satisfies the same equation, except that there the cross-section also rotates in its plane (figure 11.107 on the right, SADD, PDF pp. 247 and 251).

For the tube, plane strain and plane stress gave the same stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and a different radial displacement, by \(5{,}2\,\%\) at the inner surface. The same stresses are no accident. The stresses in the tube do not contain \(E\) and \(\nu\), and for plane problems without body force in a simply connected region on whose edge only forces are prescribed this holds for every solution: the stresses in the plane do not depend on \(E\) and \(\nu\), the displacements do (SADD, PDF p. 159). The cross-section of the tube is, however, not simply connected. For such a region Michell requires in addition that the resultant of the forces on each boundary is zero (TIMO, PDF p. 292), and for the tube the resultant of the pressure in the bore is zero, because the two forces at the ends of every diameter cancel. If displacements are prescribed on part of the edge, the stresses in general depend on \(E\) and \(\nu\) too. We show this with the Airy stress function in section 11.10.
