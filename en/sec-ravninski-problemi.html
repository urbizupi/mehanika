Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.9 Plane strain and plane stress (ravninsko deformacijsko in ravninsko napetostno stanje)

The thick tube (debela cev) of section 11.7 and the thin ring (tanek obroč) of section 1.18.5 have the same cross-section, radii 40 mm and 60 mm, and the same load, pressure 30 MPa in the bore. The radial and hoop stresses are the same in both, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A\,(1 - b^2/r^2)\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A\,(1 + b^2/r^2)\) with \(A = p\,a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 24\) MPa (theorem 11.27). The axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is different. For the ring, which is thin and has free faces, it is zero. For a long tube whose ends are held by smooth rigid walls it is \(14{,}4\) MPa. When may we treat a body as plane, that is, compute with stresses and displacements in the plane of the cross-section only, and what is exact about this and what is an approximation? The answer is not the same for the two cases. For the long tube the plane treatment is an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations, for the thin ring plane stress is a model that is exact only under a condition, which we will derive and which holds for the ring.

The path is this. First we treat the two states separately, the long prism and the thin plate, and write down the equations that remain for each. Both lead to the same algebra, so we do it once. Then we show that the equations of the two states differ only in two constants, and at the end we say in what sense plane stress is really a solution.

11.9.1 Equations in the plane (enačbe v ravnini)

Both states have three nonzero stress components, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. Of the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28), in which the derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, two remain,

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y} + b_{\mathrm{y}} = 0 . \tag{11.42}\]

Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) also has the same form in both states, only with coefficients that we call \(E'\) and \(\nu'\) for now, and fill with numbers for each state,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{E'} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E'} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{1 + \nu'}{E'}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} . \tag{11.43}\]

Of the six compatibility equations (theorem 1.127) the first remains, the one that contains only components in the plane,

\[S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = 0 .\]

The question is what this equation says about the stresses. The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), which will appear again and again, we write \(\Theta_2\) (plane trace, ravninska sled), so as not to confuse it with the trace \(\Theta\) of theorem 11.9, which has three terms. By \(\nabla^2\) we denote the Laplace operator (definition 11.5), which for functions without dependence on \(z\) is the sum of two second derivatives, and by \(\mathrm{div}\,\vek b = \partial b_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x + \partial b_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) the divergence of the plane body force.

Lemma 11.29 Plane compatibility condition (ravninski pogoj skladnosti)

Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be functions of the variables \(x\), \(y\) with continuous partial derivatives up to second order on an open set of the plane, and \(b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}}\) functions with continuous first derivatives that satisfy the equilibrium equations (11.42). Let \(E' > 0\) and \(\nu'\) be arbitrary numbers and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) defined by (11.43). Then

\[E'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu')\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b , \qquad \Theta_2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} . \tag{11.44}\]
Proof

We must show that the left side \(E'\,S_1\) can be written as the right side after using equilibrium. The second derivative of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) with respect to \(x\) and the second derivative of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with respect to \(y\) we express with the shear stress.

  1. We insert (11.43) into \(S_1\). The factor \(1/E'\) is constant, so
    \[E'\,S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} - \nu'\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - \nu'\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,(1 + \nu')\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} .\]
  2. We differentiate the first equation (11.42) with respect to \(x\) and the second with respect to \(y\). The order of differentiation of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is exchanged (theorem 1.125), because the second derivatives are continuous. We get
    \[\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} - \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , \qquad \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y^2} = -\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} - \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} .\]
  3. The sum of these two equalities is \(\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y^2 = -2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y - \mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). The terms with \(\nu'\) from step 1 are therefore together \(\nu'\,(2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b)\) and
    \[E'\,S_1 = \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} - 2\,\frac{\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \nu'\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b .\]
    The terms with \(\nu'\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) cancelled against \(-2\,(1 + \nu')\), and \(-2\) remained.
  4. The Laplace operator of the sum is \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2\). We replace the first two terms by step 3 and get \(\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 - 2\,\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b\).
  5. We insert this into step 3: \(E'\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + \mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \nu'\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), which is (11.44).

Source: SADD (7.1.7) and (7.2.7) (PDF pp. 151, 154) and CM (7.5.32), (7.5.33) (PDF p. 329) write this result separately for each state, and we write it for general constants. The derivation is ours. SymPy checked step 5 for general functions.

The lemma says that the compatibility condition in the plane does not contain the individual components, only their sum \(\Theta_2\). The constant \(\nu'\) enters only in the term with the body force. Without body force the condition is \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = 0\) regardless of \(E'\) and \(\nu'\).

11.9.2 Plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Take a long tube or any other long cylinder that has the same cross-section along its whole length, and let it be loaded by forces on the lateral surface that do not depend on \(z\) and have no component in the direction \(z\) (figure 11.24, left). Every cross-section is then in the same position as its neighbour, so it is reasonable to assume that all cross-sections deform in the same way and do not move along the axis. Such a body we call a prism with axis \(z\) (prizma z osjo \(z\)): we get it when the plane region \(R\) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) is moved along the axis \(z\) from \(z = 0\) to \(z = \ell\).

Definition 11.30 Plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje)

Let the body be a prism with axis \(z\) and cross-section \(R\), an open set in the plane \(x\), \(y\). The displacement field \(\vek u\) of the body is a plane strain state (plane strain) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) if it has the form

\[\vek u = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y),\ 0\big) , \tag{11.45}\]

where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) are continuously differentiable functions of two variables on \(R\). The body force has the form \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), b_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\).

Source: SADD §7.1 (7.1.1) (PDF p. 149), CM (7.5.1) (PDF p. 323). TIMO (PDF pp. 289-290) says the same in words: the displacements lie in planes perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder.

What are the stresses for such a displacement field? Hooke's law, equilibrium and compatibility give the answer.

Theorem 11.31 Equations of plane strain (enačbe ravninskega deformacijskega stanja)

Let a prism with axis \(z\) and cross-section \(R\) have an isotropic and homogeneous material with \(E\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.48), and let \(\vek u\) be a plane strain state (definition 11.30) that has continuous partial derivatives up to third order (then the strains and stresses have continuous second derivatives) and whose body force has continuous first derivatives. Then:

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and the remaining three strain components depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\).
  3. The strains in the plane follow from (11.43) with \(E' = E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu' = \nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\), that is, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)\) and similarly for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\).
  4. Of the equilibrium equations, (11.42) hold, and the third holds by itself.
  5. Of the six compatibility equations the first remains, and it is equivalent to the equation
    \[\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = -\frac{1}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.46}\]
Proof

We must show five claims. The first four are consequences of the form of the displacement, the fifth follows from lemma 11.29.

  1. The displacement (11.45) does not depend on \(z\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). Therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (definition 1.115). The remaining components are derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. By Hooke's law (3.12), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), and likewise \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). From \(0 = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E\) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\).
  3. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) into \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E\). We get \(\big((1 - \nu^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)/E = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)\), which is (11.43) with \(E' = E^*\) and \(\nu' = \nu^*\), since \((1 - \nu^2)/E = 1/E^*\) and \(\nu/(1 - \nu) = \nu^*\). For the shear, \((1 + \nu^*)/E^* = \frac{1}{1 - \nu}\cdot\frac{1 - \nu^2}{E} = (1 + \nu)/E\), which is Hooke's law for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
  4. By step 2 all stresses depend only on \(x\) and \(y\) (also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is a function of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\)), so in the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28) all derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by step 2. The first two equations are (11.42), and the third reads \(0 + 0 + 0 + b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and is satisfied because \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  5. Five of the six equations (1.68) have in every term a derivative with respect to \(z\) or one of the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), so by step 1 they vanish (as remark 1 after theorem 1.127 says). The theorem applies because \(\vek u\) has continuous derivatives up to third order. The first remains, \(S_1 = 0\). By lemma 11.29 with \(E^*\) and \(\nu^*\), \(E^*\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu^*)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\). Since \(1 + \nu^* = 1/(1 - \nu)\), \(S_1 = 0\) is equivalent to (11.46).

Source: SADD (7.1.2) to (7.1.7) (PDF pp. 149-151), CM (7.5.1) to (7.5.5) (PDF p. 323). Point 5 is derived with lemma 11.29, not with the table of the source.

The proof showed that in plane strain, of the fifteen equations of the boundary value problem (section 11.1) a system in the plane remains with unknowns \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) follows from them. The state is not an approximation. The equations of the three-dimensional problem are all satisfied, so the field is an exact solution if the conditions on the surface are satisfied too. On the lateral surface they are, if the force and the normal there have no component \(z\) and do not depend on \(z\), as we assumed. At the ends \(z = 0\) and \(z = \ell\) the displacement in the direction \(z\) is zero and the shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero, which corresponds exactly to ends held by smooth rigid walls without friction (SADD, PDF p. 151). The force of the walls on the prism is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) at the ends and we compute it after the solution. A prism with free ends does not have this state exactly, because at the ends we would have to remove \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) with a corrective solution that has zero on the lateral surface and the opposite force at the ends, and its exact form is usually very hard to find. SADD (PDF p. 152) therefore replaces the correction by a statically equivalent distribution by Saint-Venant's principle and uses the solution approximately for a long prism with arbitrary ends. The principle is proved only in special cases and we treat it later in this chapter as a principle with evidence.

The equation (11.46) we already know. Theorem 11.9, applied to \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) with \(\Theta = (1 + \nu)\,\Theta_2\) and \(\partial^2\Theta/\partial x^2 + \partial^2\Theta/\partial y^2 = (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta_2\), gives in the sum of both equations \(2\,\nabla^2\Theta_2 = -\frac{2\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - 2\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), that is, \(\nabla^2\Theta_2 = -\mathrm{div}\,\vek b/(1 - \nu)\). Both paths therefore give the same equation.

We test the state on the tube. A tube with inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm made of steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), pressure \(p = 30\) MPa in the bore) is long, and its ends are held by smooth rigid walls. Its displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,x/r\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,y/r\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) have the form (11.45). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) from theorem 11.27 give the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2A = 48\) MPa at every point of the wall, so by point 2 of the theorem the axial stress is constant,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}) = 0{,}3 \cdot 48 = 14{,}4\ \text{MPa} .\]

The walls must hold the tube with a force \(14{,}4\ \text{MPa} \cdot \pi\,(60^2 - 40^2)\ \text{mm}^2 = 90{,}48\) kN at each end. The radial displacement of the inner surface is by point 3 equal to \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = a\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = a\,\frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\big) = 16{,}873\ \mu\)m. For a tube with free ends, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), it is \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = a\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E = 17{,}755\ \mu\)m. The walls reduced the displacement by \(5\,\%\). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are the same in both cases, as figure 11.25 shows, and what differs are the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and the axial strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\). For free ends \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\), and between the walls it is zero.

For temperature the same path holds. In plane strain with temperature change \(\Delta T\), the condition \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives from the thermoelastic Hooke's law (theorem 3.15) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), since \(0 = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\) (SADD §12.3.1, PDF pp. 380-381, BARBER §14.1, PDF p. 234). BOWER (PDF p. 99, formulas as an image) expresses the stresses with the strains in the plane and has in \(\sigma_{11}\), \(\sigma_{22}\) the term \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\), and in \(\sigma_{33}\) the term \(+E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\). Both signs cannot come out of \(\varepsilon_{33} = 0\) at the same time: if we insert the stresses in the plane in terms of strains into \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), the temperature term that remains is \(-2\nu\,E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\). The plus sign at \(\sigma_{33}\) is a misprint, the correct formula is above.

11.9.3 Plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje)

The ring of section 1.18.5 is 2 mm thick and its radius is 40 to 60 mm. Its faces \(z = \pm h\) with \(h = 1\) mm are free, so there is no force per unit area there, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The plate is thin, so the three stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which are zero on both faces, cannot grow to a large value over the short path \(2h\) between the faces. We expect them to be near zero throughout the thickness, and the other three components to be almost independent of \(z\) (SADD, PDF p. 152). May we assume this? Let us assume it first as a model and only then ask what it means.

Definition 11.32 Plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Let a plate be the set of points \(\{(x, y, z)\ :\ (x, y) \in R,\ -h \le z \le h\}\) with an open set \(R\) in the plane \(x\), \(y\) and a small thickness \(2h\) compared with the dimensions of \(R\). The stress state of the plate is a plane stress state (plane stress) as a model if

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 \tag{11.47}\]

and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. The body force has the form \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), b_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\).

Source: SADD §7.2 (7.2.1) (PDF p. 152), TIMO (PDF p. 289) for a thin plate loaded on the edge in its plane. The word "model" is ours and stresses that the definition does not claim that a three-dimensional solution exists.

The definition demands the state and does not claim that the three-dimensional equations allow it. What follows from it and where it disagrees with the equations is told by the theorem.

Theorem 11.33 Equations of plane stress (enačbe ravninskega napetostnega stanja)

Let a plate have an isotropic and homogeneous material with \(E\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and let the stress state of the plate be a plane stress state (definition 11.32) whose stresses have continuous partial derivatives up to second order and whose body force has continuous first derivatives. Let the strains be those that follow from the stresses by Hooke's law. Then:

  1. \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\), and the strains in the plane are (11.43) with \(E' = E\) and \(\nu' = \nu\). All strains depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. Of the equilibrium equations, (11.42) hold, and the third holds by itself.
  3. The first compatibility equation is equivalent to the equation
    \[\nabla^2(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) = -(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b . \tag{11.48}\]
  4. Of the remaining five compatibility equations the fourth and the fifth are satisfied by themselves, and the second, third and sixth read \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = 0\), \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 = 0\) and \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). All three hold exactly when \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is an affine function, that is, of the form \(\alpha_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\) with \(\alpha_0\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) constants (on the connected set \(R\)). For \(\nu \ne 0\) this is exactly when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is such a function, for \(\nu = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all three always hold.
Proof

We must show four claims. The first three are similar to the proof for plane strain, the fourth is new and says where the model deviates from the three-dimensional equations.

  1. From (11.47) and Hooke's law (3.12), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E = 0\), likewise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E = -\nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\). For \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + 0))/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) this is (11.43) with \(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\), the shear is the same. The strains are functions of stresses that depend only on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. As in step 4 of the proof of theorem 11.31: the derivatives with respect to \(z\) drop out, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  3. Lemma 11.29 with \(E' = E\) and \(\nu' = \nu\) gives \(E\,S_1 = \nabla^2\Theta_2 + (1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\), so \(S_1 = 0\) is equivalent to (11.48).
  4. By step 1 all strains are independent of \(z\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). In the second equation (1.68) therefore \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial z^2\) and \(2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y\,\partial z\) are zero and \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y^2 = 0\) remains. The third gives likewise \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x^2 = 0\) and the sixth \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = 0\). Every term of the fourth and fifth equation has a derivative with respect to \(z\) or contains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) or \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), so they are satisfied. If all three second derivatives of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero, both first derivatives are constant (a function with zero gradient on a connected set is constant), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha_0 + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\). Conversely, such a function has all three second derivatives zero. By step 1, for \(\nu \ne 0\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\nu\), and for \(\nu = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\).

Source: SADD (7.2.2) to (7.2.7) (PDF pp. 153-154). SADD says on p. 154 that the three compatibility equations with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{z}}\) are usually neglected, which gives plane stress an approximate character, and leaves them to exercise 7.9, which we do not use here. Point 4 is our derivation.

The proof showed that plane stress always satisfies equilibrium and the first compatibility equation, which are the same in the plane as in plane strain, and the remaining three compatibility equations only when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is an affine function (for \(\nu \ne 0\)). When these three equations do not hold, the strains that Hooke's law determines from the stress field are not the strains of any displacement field with continuous third derivatives (theorem 1.127), so the stress field is not the stress field of any displacement. If in a simply connected plate point 3 and point 4 are satisfied, all six compatibility equations hold, the strains belong to a displacement field (theorem 4.119), and if the strains are smooth enough, the model is no longer a model but an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations (equilibrium and the conditions on the faces are satisfied).

We test the condition on both bodies. For the ring, by Lamé \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = 48\) MPa is constant, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}3 \cdot 48/196\,000 = -7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5}\) is constant and all six compatibility equations hold. The ring is not simply connected, but we have the displacement field: \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) from theorem 11.27 for a tube with free ends. A ring with free faces is therefore an exact solution of the three-dimensional equations and not only a model. Its thickness \(2\) mm decreases by \(2\ \text{mm}\cdot 7{,}347 \cdot 10^{-5} = 146{,}9\) nm. The cantilever of chapter 5 has in the model \(\Theta_2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = F\,(L - x)\,y/I\) and

\[\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac{\nu\,F}{E\,I} = \frac{0{,}3 \cdot 500\ \text{N}}{20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2} = 7{,}175 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-2} \ne 0 ,\]

so the condition of point 4 is not satisfied. This agrees with section 11.3, where the equation \(xy\) of theorem 11.9 gave the same number for the cantilever.

What does this mean for the cantilever (cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, thickness \(2h = 20\) mm)? Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) from the model hold. From \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z\) it follows that \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = z\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}(x, y) + w_0(x, y)\) (since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) does not depend on \(z\)) (we take \(w_0 = 0\), the middle surface of the plate stays in its plane), and from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = 0\) it follows that \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = -z\,\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x - \partial w_0/\partial x\). The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) therefore depends on \(z\) through the term \(-\tfrac12\,z^2\,\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\), and likewise \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) through the term \(-\tfrac12\,z^2\,\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial y\). At \(z = h = 10\) mm and \(y = c = 20\) mm the first term has absolute value \(\tfrac12\,h^2\,\nu\,F\,c/(E I) = 7{,}2\) nm, while the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the top edge at the free end due to the rotation of the cross-section is \(F L^2 c/(2 E I) = 239{,}2\ \mu\)m. The first is thus \(3 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of the second. The terms \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) do not change, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is linear in each of the variables \(x\), \(y\) separately. The shear, however, gets the term \(-\tfrac12\,z^2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial x\,\partial y = -\tfrac12\,h^2 \cdot 7{,}175 \cdot 10^{-3} = -3{,}59 \cdot 10^{-7}\) at \(z = \pm h\), which is \(5{,}8\,\%\) of the largest shear strain of the model, \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E = 6{,}22 \cdot 10^{-6}\). In stresses this is \(2\mu \cdot 3{,}59 \cdot 10^{-7} = 0{,}054\) MPa, against a bending stress \(93{,}75\) MPa at the clamp. The model of the cantilever is therefore not a three-dimensional field, and the correction of the shear stress is by this estimate only \(0{,}06\,\%\) of the largest stress. This is an estimate of size, not a bound on the error, since we left the equilibrium of the corrected field aside. Plane stress is therefore useful in practice for a beam, but it must be known as a model and not as an exact solution.

11.9.4 Change of constants (zamenjava konstant)

Let us compare theorems 11.31 and 11.33. In both, the equilibrium equations (11.42), Hooke's law (11.43) and the compatibility equation \(S_1 = 0\) hold, which by lemma 11.29 is written with the constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\). The difference is only in which numbers we insert for \(E'\) and \(\nu'\). Therefore a solution of one state with other constants becomes a solution of the other.

Corollary 11.34 Change of constants (zamenjava konstant)

Let \(E\) and \(\nu\) be constants of the material. The plane system is the system of equations (11.42), (11.43) and \(S_1 = 0\) with constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\), for prescribed forces and displacements in the plane.

  1. Plane strain of a material with \(E\), \(\nu\) solves the same plane system as plane stress (model) with the constants \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) in the places of \(E'\), \(\nu'\), for the same body force and the same conditions in the plane. The restriction \(\nu^* < \tfrac12\) of theorem 3.48 is not important here, because we use only the equations. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) of a solution of one system are therefore a solution of the other. That the solution of each is unique, the corollary does not claim.
  2. Plane stress of a material with \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(\nu > -\tfrac12\) solves the same plane system as plane strain of a material with \(E_{\mathrm{p}} = E\,(1 + 2\nu)/(1 + \nu)^2\) and \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} = \nu/(1 + \nu)\).
  3. For \(\nu = 0\) we have \(E^* = E_{\mathrm{p}} = E\) and \(\nu^* = \nu_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\): the two states are the same.
Proof

We must show that both states lead to the same system of equations in the plane.

  1. By points 3 to 5 of theorem 11.31, plane strain of the material \(E\), \(\nu\) solves (11.42), (11.43) with \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) and \(S_1 = 0\). By points 1 to 3 of theorem 11.33, plane stress of the material with \(E\) and \(\nu\) solves the same system with \(E\) and \(\nu\) in the places \(E'\) and \(\nu'\). On replacing \(E \to E^*\) and \(\nu \to \nu^*\) in the stress state the system is the same. The conditions in the plane are the same: displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the edge and the force in the plane \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) with \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  2. We look for \(E_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}}\) with \(E = E_{\mathrm{p}}/(1 - \nu_{\mathrm{p}}^2)\) and \(\nu = \nu_{\mathrm{p}}/(1 - \nu_{\mathrm{p}})\). The second gives \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} = \nu/(1 + \nu)\), and \(1 - \nu_{\mathrm{p}}^2 = \big((1 + \nu)^2 - \nu^2\big)/(1 + \nu)^2 = (1 + 2\nu)/(1 + \nu)^2\) gives \(E_{\mathrm{p}} = E\,(1 + 2\nu)/(1 + \nu)^2\). For \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}}\) from \(-1 < \nu_{\mathrm{p}} < \tfrac12\) we need \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} > -1\), which is \(\nu > -\tfrac12\), since \(1 + \nu > 0\) (theorem 3.48). The upper bound \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} < \tfrac12\) is equivalent to \(\nu < 1\) and holds.
  3. For \(\nu = 0\) we insert into the formulas of points 1 and 2.

Source: CM §7.5.4 (7.5.29) to (7.5.34) (PDF p. 329), SADD Table 7.1 (PDF p. 154, the table is not in the text, the content is told by the text on pp. 154-155). The reverse direction and the restriction \(\nu > -\tfrac12\) are ours.

It is not necessary that \(E^*\) and \(\nu^*\) are constants of some material: \(\nu^*\) can be larger than \(\tfrac12\), which does not matter, because these are only numbers in the equations. For steel \(E^* = 215{,}385\) GPa and \(\nu^* = 0{,}4286\). Conversely, plane stress of steel has the same plane system as plane strain of a material with \(E_{\mathrm{p}} = 185{,}562\) GPa and \(\nu_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}2308\).

We test the corollary on the tube. In plane strain we got \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = 16{,}873\ \mu\)m. Plane stress with \(E^*\) and \(\nu^*\) gives \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(a) = a\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu^*\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E^* = 40\ \text{mm}\cdot(78 + 0{,}4286 \cdot 30)\ \text{MPa}/215\,385\ \text{MPa} = 16{,}873\ \mu\)m. Both paths give the same number, as they must.

11.9.5 Generalized plane stress (posplošeno ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Point 4 of theorem 11.33 showed that plane stress is in general only a model. Does it have an exact meaning? It does: it is not the field in the plate, but the average of the field over the thickness. For a function \(f(x, y, z)\) on the plate \(-h \le z \le h\) the thickness average (povprečje po debelini) is

\[\langle f\rangle (x, y) = \frac{1}{2h}\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h} f(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}z . \tag{11.49}\]

The theorem says that the averages satisfy the plane equations exactly, if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) really holds in the plate.

Theorem 11.35 Generalized plane stress (posplošeno ravninsko napetostno stanje)

Let \(\vek u\) be a solution of the three-dimensional problem in the plate \(\{(x, y, z)\ :\ (x, y) \in R,\ -h \le z \le h\}\) of an isotropic homogeneous material, with free faces (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) on \(z = \pm h\)), with body force \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components \(b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}}\) have continuous first derivatives, and with displacement with continuous partial derivatives up to third order. Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) hold at every point of the plate. On the edge of the plate with normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) let the stress vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) be prescribed. Then for the averages \(\langle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}\rangle\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta}\rangle\), \(\langle u_\alpha\rangle\) and \(\langle b_\alpha\rangle\) (\(\alpha, \beta = x, y\)) the following hold:

  1. \(\partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle/\partial x + \partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/\partial y + \langle b_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle = 0\) and \(\partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/\partial x + \partial\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle/\partial y + \langle b_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle = 0\).
  2. \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle = (\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle - \nu\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle)/E\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle = (\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle - \nu\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle)/E\) and \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle = (1 + \nu)\,\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle/E\), that is, (11.43) with \(E' = E\), \(\nu' = \nu\).
  3. \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle = \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle/\partial x\), \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle = \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle/\partial y\) and \(\langle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle = \tfrac12\,(\partial\langle u_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle/\partial y + \partial\langle u_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle/\partial x)\).
  4. On the edge, \(\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \langle\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle\) and \(\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \langle\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\rangle\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \langle\bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle\).

The averages therefore satisfy all equations of plane stress, including \(S_1 = 0\) and (11.48), with the edge load averaged over the thickness.

Proof

We must show four claims and conclude about \(S_1\) from them. The average is linear and the derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) commute with it, so every linear equation carries over to the averages, if it has derivatives with respect to \(z\) only in a term that we know how to average.

  1. We average the equilibrium equation \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). The derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) we exchange with the integral (lemma 4.19, the limits \(\pm h\) are constant, the integrand and its derivatives are continuous). For the term with \(z\), by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40), \(\frac{1}{2h}\int_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h}\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{1}{2h}\,[\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}]_{-\mathrm{h}}^{h} = 0\), because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) on both free faces. The first equation of point 1 remains, the second is the same with \(y\) in place of \(x\).
  2. Hooke's law (3.12) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\) at every point of the plate. These equations are linear, so we average them without losing anything.
  3. The strains in the plane are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\) (definition 1.115). We average and exchange the derivatives with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) with the integral as in step 1.
  4. The boundary condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} = \bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\) (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.24, with \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\)) and the similar one for \(y\) we average over \(z\). The normal does not depend on \(z\), so it is taken out of the average.
  5. Points 1 to 3 are the equilibrium equations, Hooke's law and the relation of strains to displacements of plane stress. The derivatives of \(\langle u_\alpha\rangle\) up to third order are continuous (lemma 4.19), so by theorem 1.127 \(S_1 = 0\) holds for the averages, and from lemma 11.29 the equation (11.48) follows.

Source: SADD §7.3 (7.3.1) to (7.3.7) (PDF pp. 155-156). The source states the theorem without steps, we derived steps 1 to 5 ourselves. That we do not need the symmetry of the load in \(z\) is our finding: symmetry is the reason why \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) is plausible (SADD, PDF pp. 155-156), not a condition of the proof.

The proof showed that the thickness averages are an exact solution of the plane problem if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero throughout the plate. The assumption \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) in the interior is the approximation: on both faces it is zero, in the interior it is not necessarily. For \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) on the faces, from equilibrium \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) it follows that \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z\) is zero on the faces (the terms with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero there, because they are zero on the whole face), and SADD (PDF p. 156) concludes from a Taylor series that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is of order \(h^2\) for a thin plate (for a bounded second derivative). How large the error is, the sources do not say. SADD (PDF p. 157) says there himself that the accuracy gained is not worth much in applications, so the model is used in practice.

We test the theorem on a plate of height \(2c = 40\) mm and thickness \(2h = 20\) mm with stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y + m\,z\) and the other components zero (figure 11.26). The field is linear, so it is in equilibrium without body force and satisfies the compatibility equations, and the faces \(z = \pm h\) are free. The thickness average is \(\langle\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\rangle = k\,y\) (the term \(m\,z\) is an odd function of \(z\) and has average zero). This is the pure bending of chapter 5, which is a solution of plane stress. The term \(m\,z\), bending about the axis \(y\), is lost by the average: the plane problem does not see it.

11.9.6 Which model when (kdaj kateri model)

A long prism with constant cross-section and a load that does not depend on \(z\) is in plane strain exactly if its ends are held by smooth rigid walls, and approximately far from the ends if the ends are free, about which Saint-Venant's principle speaks later in this chapter. A thin plate loaded in its plane is in plane stress as a model. This state is exact when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is an affine function and the condition is in addition satisfied on a plate that is simply connected or has a displacement, as for the ring with internal pressure (theorem 11.33, point 4). Otherwise the thickness average is a solution if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero throughout the plate (theorem 11.35), which for a thin plate is good but not exact. For the cantilever of chapter 5 we saw that the field of the model is not exactly a three-dimensional field, and that the deviation is by estimate negligible.

The plane states are not the only plane theories. One more is antiplane strain (antiravninsko stanje) with displacement \(\vek u = (0, 0, w(x, y))\), where the only nonzero component is the displacement in the direction of the axis (SADD §7.4, PDF p. 157). The restriction to \(w\) gives a single equation for a single function, and this is the setting of torsion, which is treated in chapter 16.

For the tube, plane strain and plane stress gave the same stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and a different radial displacement, by \(5\,\%\) at the inner surface. The same stresses are no accident. The Lamé stresses do not contain \(E\) and \(\nu\), and for plane problems without body force in a simply connected region this holds for every solution: the stresses in the plane do not depend on \(E\) and \(\nu\), the displacements do. We show this with the Airy stress function in section 11.10.
