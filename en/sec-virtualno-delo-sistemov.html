Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.1 Virtual work for systems of rigid bodies (virtualno delo za sisteme togih teles)

Theorem 4.73 gives the equilibrium of one rigid body from the work of forces on its rigid virtual displacements. The two-bar toggle mechanism, however, has two bars, a pin, a hinge and a slider. For a given position of the mechanism, can we compute the load that holds it in equilibrium without computing the forces in the pin, the hinge and the guide? In this section we answer: we can, if the constraints are ideal. First we set up the mechanism, then we say which displacements of the system the constraints allow and which constraints do no work on such displacements, finally we prove the theorem and test it.

8.1.1 The toggle mechanism (kolenasti mehanizem)

Mechanism D (figure 8.2) lies in a vertical plane. The axis \(x\) points horizontally to the right, the axis \(y\) vertically up. Bar \(AB\) is fixed by a pin (zatič) at the point \(A\), which is the origin, and bar \(BC\) is joined to bar \(AB\) by a hinge (zgib) at the point \(B\). The end \(C\) of bar \(BC\) is a slider (drsnik) on a horizontal guide (vodilo) through \(A\). Between \(A\) and \(C\) there is a spring. A load \(P\) hangs at the hinge \(B\). Blue is the virtual displacement for a small rotation of bar \(AB\): the point \(B\) moves perpendicular to \(AB\) by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}}\), and the slider \(C\) moves along the guide by \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}}\). This is our model, not an example from a source. Let us list its assumptions and say for each what a real machine would change.

  1. The bars are rigid (definition 1.11), each of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m. We neglect their weight. For steel bars of mass 2 kg each the total weight of both would be \(39{,}2\) N, about 7 % of the load \(552{,}54\) N that we will compute. Only half of this affects the result, however, as figure 8.3 shows. In it we draw the two bars separately, grey dashed before the displacement and orange after it. The rotation is enlarged so that it can be seen. The weight of a bar acts at its centroid, which is at the middle of the bar. Bar \(AB\) turns about the pin \(A\), so its centroid \(T_1\) is always at half the height of the point \(B\) and rises by \(\tfrac12 h\) when the point \(B\) rises by \(h\). The end \(C\) of bar \(BC\) slides along the guide and stays at the same height, so the centroid \(T_2\), midway between \(B\) and \(C\), rises by the average of the two rises, also by \(\tfrac12 h\). The weights of the two bars therefore do the same work as the weight of one bar at the point \(B\). They would join the load as the weight of one bar, \(19{,}62\) N, and the load would be \(532{,}92\) N, 3,6 % less. Later in this chapter we will give the bars a mass.
  2. The pin \(A\) is firmly fixed, the hinge \(B\) and the slider \(C\) have no friction, the guide is smooth. This is nearly true when the pins and the slider are lubricated or run on bearings, and it is not true for a dry slider under a large force. Friction would take from the constraints the property that will let us leave them out of the computation. How much it would change the result we compute at the end of the example: with a friction force of up to 10 N on the slider the mechanism would be at rest under every load in a band of \(\pm 1{,}1\) % around the computed one, not only under one.
  3. The spring is linear with stiffness \(k = 10\) kN/m. When it is stretched, it pulls its two ends toward itself with the force \(k\,(\text{length} - \text{length without tension})\). It is without tension at \(\theta_0 = 30° = 0{,}5236\) rad, when its length is \(2a\cos\theta_0 = 0{,}8660\) m. Linearity is a property of the chosen spring. It does not follow from the spring returning to its original length after unloading: a spring that stiffens as it is stretched also returns, yet it is not linear. Here the spring will be stretched by \(0{,}0893\) m, about 10 % of its length, and we assume that the chosen spring is linear at least up to this stretch. For a spring that stiffens as it is stretched, the spring force would be larger and the load with it in the same ratio, since the result will be proportional to the spring force. With a spring force 5 % larger the load would be \(580{,}17\) N instead of \(552{,}54\) N.
  4. The load \(P\) acts at the hinge \(B\) vertically downward and is constant, the mechanism is at rest. Constant means that the force does not change during a small displacement, so its work is the force multiplied by the displacement in the direction of the force. Rest means that there are no forces demanded by the acceleration of masses. We add these in the second part of the chapter, until then the results hold only for a mechanism that is at rest or moves very slowly. An example shows what this assumption costs. If the load is the weight of a mass and the point \(B\) accelerates upward at \(0{,}1\ \text{m/s}^2\), by Newton's second law the mass needs an additional force that is \(0{,}1/9{,}81\) of its weight, about 1 %, and the result would be off by as much. In the computation of virtual work we attribute the load to bar \(AB\). The two bars move equally at \(B\), so the work is the same.
  5. The angle \(\theta\) between bar \(AB\) and the horizontal we measure like the rotation \(\delta\varphi\) of a rigid body: as the ratio of the length of a circular arc to the radius, so as a dimensionless number. Why this choice is the right one is told by the section that follows this one. Until then we take the values of \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) from school.

The question is: how large must the load \(P\) be at a given position \(\theta\) for the mechanism to be at rest? With the method of chapter 4 we would cut each bar out of the system and write its equilibrium. Let us do this in figure 8.4. We draw bar \(AB\) and bar \(BC\) apart, so that the two forces in the hinge \(B\) can be seen, which is why it is drawn twice. The slider is the end \(C\) of bar \(BC\). On bar \(AB\) act the pin force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\) with components \(A_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{y}}\), the load \(P\) and the hinge force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\) with components \(B_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{y}}\), with which bar \(BC\) pushes it at \(B\). On bar \(BC\) act the opposite hinge force \(-\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\), the spring force \(F_{\mathrm{s}}\), which we know for every position, and the guide force \(G\), which is vertical because a smooth guide cannot give a force along itself. We draw the forces of the pin, the hinge and the guide in orange. In blue we draw the virtual displacements of the points at which the forces act: the point \(A\) stays at rest, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}} = \vek 0\), the point \(B\) moves by the same \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}}\) in both bars, and the slider \(C\) by \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}}\) along the guide. Each bar has three equilibrium equations in the plane, for the force components in the directions \(x\) and \(y\) and for the moment, six together. There are six unknowns too: \(A_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(G\) and \(P\). Yet we need only one of them, \(P\). Virtual work will give the same number from one equation in which only \(P\) appears. For this we need two things: what a displacement of the system that respects the constraints means, and what the constraints do to the work of their forces.

8.1.2 Constraints and virtual displacements of a system (vezi in virtualni pomiki sistema)

In definition 4.70, chapter 4 said that a virtual displacement respects the constraints (vezi), that is, the conditions that restrict the displacements of points. Now we write these conditions for a system of bodies. The system consists of \(K\) rigid bodies. Each body \(k\) has its own small rigid displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(P) = \delta\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\times\overrightarrow{O_{\mathrm{k}}P}\) with respect to a chosen point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the body (lemma 4.72). In the plane each body has three numbers: the two components of the vector \(\delta\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\), since the vector \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) points along the axis \(z\). The constraints are conditions that these numbers must satisfy. As in section 4.4.2 we respect them up to terms of first order, so these conditions are linear equations in the virtual quantities. Let us list four constraints that we will use. We draw each right after the definition.

Definition 8.1 Admissible virtual displacement of a system (dopusten virtualni pomik sistema)

Let a system of rigid bodies \(1, \dots, K\) be in a given position. A virtual displacement of a system (virtualni pomik sistema) is a choice of a small rigid displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}\) for each body, with vectors \(\delta\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (lemma 4.72). It is admissible (dopusten) if it satisfies the conditions of all constraints of the system up to terms of first order:

  1. Hinge of two bodies or pin of a body on the ground (zgib, zatič): the point \(P\) belongs to bodies \(k\) and \(l\). Then \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(P) = \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{l}}(P)\). If the body \(l\) is the ground, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{l}}(P) = \vek 0\).
  2. Smooth guide (gladko vodilo): the point \(P\) of body \(k\) stays on a given line or curve. Then \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\) is along the tangent of the guide.
  3. Rolling without slipping (kotaljenje brez spodrsavanja): bodies \(k\) and \(l\) touch at the point \(Q\). The points of the two bodies that are at \(Q\) in the given position move equally: \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(Q) = \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{l}}(Q)\). If the body \(l\) is a ground at rest, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(Q) = \vek 0\).
  4. Inextensible taut rope (neraztegljiva napeta vrv): the rope leaves body \(k\) at its point \(P\) and body \(l\) at its point \(R\), where it is attached to them or unwinds from them without sliding, and in between it may run over pulleys with an axis at rest. Let \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\) be the unit vector that points from \(P\) along the rope, and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{R}}\) the unit vector that points from \(R\) along the rope. Then \(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(P) + \vek t_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{l}}(R) = 0\): as far as one end moves along the rope toward it, so far the other moves away from it.

Source: GR §1.2.2 (PDF p. 36-37) for constraints between particles, CK §2.4 (PDF p. 52-58) for the concept of a virtual displacement. SPONG §14.3, example 14.6 (PDF p. 509) for the rolling condition that the velocity of the contact point on a ground at rest is zero. The notation for a system of rigid bodies, rolling on a body that moves and the condition for the rope are not from the sources.

A constraint not only restricts displacements, it also acts with a force. A pin holds the point \(A\) at rest, so it acts on it with a force that we do not know in advance. The forces that constraints exert on bodies we call constraint forces (sile vezi). In figure 8.4 they are orange. All other forces on bodies, that is the load, the weight and the spring force, we call applied forces (uporabljene sile). In the lectures they are called active forces (aktivne sile). We count the spring force among the applied ones because it is determined by the stretch of the spring, which we know for every position.

Why did constraint forces not appear for the lever in chapter 4? The pin acted at a point that did not move. This is not a peculiarity of the lever. Let us see what the three constraints of the definition do to the sum of the work of the forces they exert (figure 8.5).

Hinge. The hinge acts on bar 1 with a force \(\vek F_1\) and on bar 2 with a force \(\vek F_2\). We count the pin of the hinge as part of bar 1, since it moves together with it. For this we do not need to assume that the pin has no mass: we add its weight to the applied forces at \(P\). Then \(\vek F_2\) is the force of bar 1 on bar 2 and \(\vek F_1\) the force of bar 2 on bar 1, and by the law of action and reaction (section 2.1), \(\vek F_2 = -\vek F_1\). By item 1 of the definition the virtual displacement of the point \(P\) is the same in both bars, \(\delta\vek u\). The work of the two forces is \(\vek F_1\cdot\delta\vek u + \vek F_2\cdot\delta\vek u = \vek F_1\cdot\delta\vek u - \vek F_1\cdot\delta\vek u = 0\), whatever the force \(\vek F_1\) is. For a pin on the ground \(\delta\vek u = \vek 0\), so the work of the pin force is zero.

Guide. Smooth means that the guide does not act with a force along itself. The guide force \(\vek N\) is therefore perpendicular to the guide, while the virtual displacement of the point \(C\) is along the guide by item 2, and \(\vek N\cdot\delta\vek u = 0\). In mechanism D the guide is horizontal, the guide force on the slider is \((0, G)\), the displacement of the slider is \((\delta x_{\mathrm{C}}, 0)\), and \(0\cdot\delta x_{\mathrm{C}} + G\cdot 0 = 0\). Smoothness is an assumption of the model. A guide with friction also acts on the slider along itself, and this force does work on the displacement of the slider, which we compute for mechanism D later in this section.

Rolling. The wheel rolls on the ground, which is at rest. The force of the ground on the wheel, normal and along the ground, acts at the contact point \(Q\). We draw it as one force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{Q}}\) that ends at \(Q\) from below. By item 3 this point does not move, so the work of the force is zero. The other points of the wheel do move. Since \(Q\) is at rest, by lemma 4.72 with the chosen point \(Q\) the displacement of a point \(X\) of the wheel is \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\overrightarrow{QX}\), perpendicular to \(\overrightarrow{QX}\) and proportional to the distance from \(Q\). The centre \(O\) is one radius away from \(Q\) and moves by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{O}}\) along the ground. The top \(T\) is twice as far, so it moves by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{T}} = 2\,\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{O}}\).

Rolling on a body that moves. What if the ground itself moves? Let us stack three bodies on one another (figure 8.6). On the ground, which is at rest, we put two equal rollers 1 of radius \(r\), on them the plate 2, and on the plate the cylinder 3 of radius \(\rho\). Nothing slips anywhere. What does this mean at the contact \(K\) of cylinder 3 and the plate, when both move? Slipping is the difference between the displacements of two points that touch, the point of the cylinder and the point of the plate. If there is no difference, the two points move equally. This is what item 3 of the definition says. A ground at rest is its special case, in which the displacement of the point of the ground is zero.

We build the displacements from the ground up. Let the rollers 1 turn by \(\delta\beta\) clockwise. The contact point \(Q_1\) of a roller with the ground is at rest. The centre of the roller is \(r\) above it and moves to the right by \(r\,\delta\beta\). The top of the roller is \(2r\) above it and moves by \(2r\,\delta\beta\), like the top \(T\) of the wheel above. The plate touches the top of the roller without slipping, so its point there moves equally. The plate lies on two equal rollers and does not turn, so all its points move by \(\delta x_2 = 2r\,\delta\beta\). The plate goes twice as far as the rollers under it. Now cylinder 3. Its contact point \(K\) moves like the plate, by \(\delta x_2\). The cylinder also turns by \(\delta\alpha\) clockwise. By lemma 4.72 with the chosen point \(K\) the centre, which is \(\rho\) above \(K\), moves by \(\delta x_2\) like the point \(K\) and in addition by \(\rho\,\delta\alpha\) because of the rotation. Together,

\[\delta x_3 = \delta x_2 + \rho\,\delta\alpha = 2r\,\delta\beta + \rho\,\delta\alpha .\]

Take \(r = 0{,}05\) m and \(\rho = 0{,}08\) m. Then \(\delta x_2 = 0{,}10\,\delta\beta\) m and \(\delta x_3 = 0{,}10\,\delta\beta + 0{,}08\,\delta\alpha\) m. At \(\delta\alpha = 0\) cylinder 3 rides with the plate without turning. At \(\delta\alpha = -1{,}25\,\delta\beta\) we get \(\delta x_3 = 0\): the cylinder turns backward just enough for its centre to stay in place, while the plate drives away from under it.

The same ratios hold for velocities. The velocity of a point tells by how much the point moves in a unit of time, we define it exactly later in this chapter. If all the displacements happen in the same short time, the ratios between them do not change: the plate has twice the velocity of the centres of the rollers, and the velocity of the centre of cylinder 3 is the sum of the velocity of the plate and the contribution of the rotation. In the lectures this is written \(v = 2r\dot\beta\) and \(v_{\mathrm{t}3} = v_{\mathrm{t}2} + r\dot\alpha\), where the dot over a letter means the change of the quantity in a unit of time, and \(r\) in the second equation is the radius of the upper cylinder, our \(\rho\).

Is such a contact an ideal constraint, that is, does the work of its forces drop out as for the hinge? Let us look at the contact \(K\) from close by and draw the bodies apart (right part of the figure). The plate acts on cylinder 3 at its point \(K_3\) with a force \(\vek F\), which has a part perpendicular to the plate and a part along it. By the law of action and reaction (section 2.1) the cylinder acts on the plate at its point \(K_2\) with the force \(-\vek F\). The two points move equally, by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{K}}\) on the cylinder and by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{K}}\) on the plate. The sum of the works is

\[\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{K}} + (-\vek F)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{K}} = 0 ,\]

whatever the force \(\vek F\) is. Each force by itself does work, though. For \(\vek F = (-20, 50)\) N and \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{K}} = (0{,}10\,\delta\beta, 0)\) m the work of the force on the cylinder is \(-2{,}0\,\delta\beta\) N m, and the work of the force on the plate is \(+2{,}0\,\delta\beta\) N m. Only the sum is zero. This is why the definition of an ideal constraint below will speak of the sum of the works of all forces that the constraint exerts, not of the work of one force.

The assumption that nothing slips enters right here, so let us say when it holds. A contact transmits a force along the surface only up to a limit, which we meet with friction further on in this section. As long as the force along the surface that equilibrium or motion demands is below this limit, the contact points stay together. This is so for rough dry surfaces and moderate forces. It is not so on ice, on an oiled surface or at a jerky start. Then the points slide along each other, the displacement and the rotation of the body are no longer tied and the system gains one more free number (SHABANA §1.6, PDF p. 34). The force along the surface does work on the difference of the displacements that is not zero, and the constraint is no longer ideal. For the force \(\vek F = (-20, 50)\) N from above the contact must carry 20 N along the surface at 50 N normal to it, so the coefficient of friction must be at least \(0{,}4\).

Rolling along a straight line. Let us return to the wheel on a ground at rest and look at two things: what the rolling condition says about the position of the wheel, not only about a small displacement, and how points that are not on the rim move. In the left part of figure 8.7 we roll a wheel of radius \(r\) along the ground from its starting position to the right, to the position with the centre \(O\) and the contact point \(Q\). We mark the point of the rim that touched the ground at the start. At the start it was at \(M_0\), now it is at \(M\). The wheel has turned by the angle \(\varphi\), which we measure as the ratio of arc to radius. During the rolling every point of the arc from \(Q\) to \(M\), which is \(r\varphi\) long, touched the ground in turn. Since nothing slips, the arc unwinds onto the ground without sliding, like adhesive tape from a roll. So the path of the contact point along the ground is equally long:

\[x = r\varphi . \tag{8.1}\]

In the figure \(\varphi = 1{,}2\) rad. For \(r = 0{,}04\) m, \(x = 0{,}048\) m. The radius to the contact point is perpendicular to the ground that the wheel touches, so the centre is always vertically above the contact point and has moved by the same \(x\). This is a relation between positions, not only between small displacements: whoever knows the rotation knows the position. A wheel that rolls along a straight line therefore has a single free number, although without the constraint we would need two in this direction, \(x\) and \(\varphi\) (SHABANA §1.6, PDF p. 34, and example 3.11, PDF p. 152). The small displacement follows from the same relation, \(\delta x = r\,\delta\varphi\), which is the displacement of the centre \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{O}}\) from before. It is not always this simple. Later in this chapter we meet a disk that rolls on a plane and turns as it goes, and for it the rolling condition cannot be summed into a relation between positions.

Now the points that are not on the rim. In the right part of the figure there is a stepped cylinder. Its axle of radius \(r\) rolls on a rail, and on it sits a drum of radius \(2r\), which reaches below the height of the rail beside it. On the vertical diameter let us mark four points: the contact point \(Q\), the centre \(O\), the top of the drum \(T\) and the bottom of the drum \(D\). Let us turn the cylinder by \(\delta\varphi\) clockwise. The point \(Q\) is at rest, so by lemma 4.72 with the chosen point \(Q\) the displacement of every point of the cylinder is perpendicular to the line joining it with \(Q\) and proportional to the distance from \(Q\). The points of the vertical diameter therefore move horizontally. The centre is \(r\) above \(Q\) and moves by \(r\,\delta\varphi\). The top of the drum is \(3r\) above \(Q\) and moves by \(3r\,\delta\varphi\). What counts is the distance from \(Q\), not the distance \(2r\) from the centre. The bottom of the drum is \(r\) below \(Q\) and moves by \(r\,\delta\varphi\) backward, in the opposite direction. The tips of all the displacements lie on one line through \(Q\): a point at the height \(h\) above \(Q\) moves by \(h\,\delta\varphi\). For \(r = 0{,}04\) m the displacements of the points \(O\), \(T\) and \(D\) are \(0{,}04\,\delta\varphi\) m, \(0{,}12\,\delta\varphi\) m and \(-0{,}04\,\delta\varphi\) m.

At this instant the cylinder moves as if it turned about the point \(Q\). We therefore call it the instantaneous centre of rotation (trenutno središče vrtenja), in the lectures the velocity pole (pol hitrosti). It is instantaneous because the contact point changes during rolling: at the next instant another point of the cylinder already touches the rail. The same picture holds for velocities. The velocity of a point is proportional to its distance from \(Q\), so the line is also the profile of velocity over the diameter.

A common error is to measure the distance from the centre \(O\). In figure 8.8 the two cylinders are equal. On the left is the right profile with a line through \(Q\), on the right the wrong one with a line through the centre \(O\). The top of the drum would then get \(2r\,\delta\varphi\) instead of \(3r\,\delta\varphi\), the bottom \(2r\,\delta\varphi\) backward instead of \(r\,\delta\varphi\) backward, and the centre would stay in place, although the cylinder rolls and the centre should move by \(r\,\delta\varphi\). The contact point gives the error away. In the right profile \(Q\) is at rest, in the wrong one it would go \(r\,\delta\varphi\) backward. The cylinder would thus slip on the rail, which item 3 of definition 8.1 forbids.

Rope and pulley. The fourth constraint does not act at one point, it joins two distant bodies. In figure 8.9 the stepped cylinder from before is on the rail. A rope is wound on the drum and leaves it horizontally at the top \(T\). The rope runs over a pulley (škripec), that is a disk that turns about a fixed axle, and on it hangs a load of mass 2 kg and weight \(W = 19{,}62\) N. The cylinder is held back by a horizontal force \(H\) at the centre \(O\).

Let the rope be inextensible and taut. Its piece between the cylinder and the load then has a constant length: as much rope as goes toward the pulley at one end, so much comes from it at the other. Item 4 of the definition writes this with the displacements of the two ends along the rope. When the cylinder turns by \(\delta\varphi\), the point \(T\) moves toward the pulley by \(3r\,\delta\varphi\), as we computed in the previous figure. The load therefore sinks by the same \(3r\,\delta\varphi\).

This also holds exactly between positions, not only for a small step. In figure 8.10 we roll the cylinder from its starting position along the rail by the angle \(\varphi\). The centre \(O\) and the top \(T\) of the drum move by \(r\varphi\) toward the pulley by (8.1). The centre stays at the same height, so the rope still leaves the drum horizontally at the top, and its piece from \(T\) to the pulley is shorter by \(r\varphi\). The drum of radius \(2r\) has turned by \(\varphi\), so an arc \(2r\varphi\) has unwound from it. This piece of rope now lies between \(T\) and the mark \(Z\), which was at the top of the drum at the start. The mark has therefore moved by \(r\varphi + 2r\varphi = 3r\varphi\) toward the pulley. The rope is inextensible, so just as much rope comes out on the other side of the pulley and the load sinks by \(s = 3r\varphi\), for \(r = 0{,}04\) m and \(\varphi = 1{,}2\) rad by \(0{,}144\) m. The displacement \(3r\,\delta\varphi\) from before is the same relation for a small rotation. If the rope turned another drum of radius \(r_{\mathrm{C}}\) instead of the load, that drum would turn by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}\) with \(r_{\mathrm{C}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = 3r\varphi\). Such a relation between the rotations or displacements of two bodies is always the first step in problems with ropes.

What does the rope do to the work? A rope can only pull. It acts on the cylinder at \(T\) with the force \(S\) along the rope toward the pulley, on the load with the force \(S\) upward. This is why we drew the first piece of the rope at each body as a force. The point \(T\) moves in the direction of the force, so the work is \(+S\cdot 3r\,\delta\varphi\). The load moves against the force, so the work is \(-S\cdot 3r\,\delta\varphi\). The sum is zero, whatever the force \(S\) is. The force of the rail on the cylinder \(\vek F_{\mathrm{Q}}\) acts at the contact point \(Q\), which is at rest, and does no work.

Why is the force the same at both ends of the rope? A straight piece of rope without weight is in equilibrium under the forces at its ends, so they are equal and opposite. There remains the pulley, which turns the rope around. Let us cut it out (right part of the figure). On the disk act the rope forces \(S_1\) and \(S_2\), each tangent to the disk, and the axle force \(\vek R\), which passes through the centre. The rope forces have the same lever arm \(\rho\) about the axle, the radius of the disk, and turn in opposite senses. The disk is at rest, so the sum of the moments about the axle is zero (definition 2.3):

\[S_1\,\rho - S_2\,\rho = 0 , \qquad \text{so} \qquad S_1 = S_2 .\]

The axle force has no moment because it passes through the axle, and it does no work because the axle does not move. A rope over a pulley is therefore an ideal constraint. In general the rope pulls body \(k\) at \(P\) with the force \(S\,\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\) and body \(l\) at \(R\) with the force \(S\,\vek t_{\mathrm{R}}\). The sum of the works is \(S\,(\vek t_{\mathrm{P}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}(P) + \vek t_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{l}}(R))\), zero by item 4 of definition 8.1. This reasoning holds five assumptions.

  1. The bearing of the pulley has no friction. For a rolling bearing the friction moment is small against the moment \(S\rho\). With a friction moment \(M_{\mathrm{t}}\) we would have \(S_1 - S_2 = M_{\mathrm{t}}/\rho\). For \(M_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}01\) N m and \(\rho = 0{,}02\) m the difference is \(0{,}5\) N, which at the rope force \(19{,}62\) N is about 2,5 %. The sum of the works of the rope forces is then not zero but \(-M_{\mathrm{t}}\) times the rotation of the disk.
  2. The pulley has no mass. At rest we do not need this assumption: the moment equation holds for every disk at rest, and its weight is carried by the axle. It is needed for motion in the second part of the chapter. A disk with mass demands a difference of the forces \(S_1 - S_2\) to accelerate its rotation. We neglect it when the mass of the disk is small against the masses of the bodies that the rope joins, otherwise the disk is one more body of the system.
  3. The rope is inextensible. A steel wire of diameter 2 mm and length 1 m stretches under the force \(19{,}62\) N by equation (3.2) by \(0{,}030\) mm (with \(E = 210\) GPa), which is \(0{,}02\) % of the sinking of the load \(0{,}144\) m. The assumption does not hold for a rubber rope or a very long one. Such a rope we treat as a spring: its force is an applied force with its own work and the system has one more free number.
  4. The rope is taut. A rope cannot push. If the computation gives \(S < 0\), the rope in fact goes slack, the condition of item 4 of the definition drops and the two bodies move independently, so that the system gains one more free number. Such a constraint, which holds in one direction only, is called one-sided (enostranska).
  5. The rope has no weight. A steel wire of diameter 2 mm and length 1 m has, at the density 7800 kg/m³ (chapter 2), the mass \(0{,}0245\) kg and the weight \(0{,}24\) N, which is 1,2 % of the rope force \(19{,}62\) N. By at most this much the force at the upper end of the vertical piece of the rope differs from the force at its lower end. For a heavy chain or a rope tens of metres long we may not neglect the weight. Then the weight of the rope is one more applied force with its own work, since part of the rope moves down as the load sinks.

Friction spoils this. So far the contact was smooth, or the contact points did not move along each other. When a body slides on a surface, the surface also acts on it along itself, and this force does work on the displacement. How large it is and where it points is told by a measured law.

Let body 1, on which body 2 lies, slide on a horizontal ground to the right. The grey arrow \(v\) means only the direction of sliding. We drew the bodies lifted off the ground, so that the two forces with which the ground acts on body 1 can be seen. The first is the normal force \(N\), perpendicular to the surface. With it the ground prevents the body from sinking into it, so it is a constraint force. The second is the friction force \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) along the surface. Measurements show two things. The friction force points against the direction of sliding. Its magnitude, for a given pair of surfaces, is proportional to the normal force and nearly independent of how fast the body slides (CK equation (3.45), PDF p. 89, NORTON §10.11, PDF p. 593).

Definition 8.2 Coulomb's law of sliding friction (Coulombov zakon drsnega trenja)

Let a body slide on a dry surface of another body or of the ground. We resolve the force of the surface on the body into the normal force (normalna sila) of magnitude \(N \ge 0\), perpendicular to the surface, and the friction force (sila trenja) along the surface. Coulomb's law of sliding friction is a model of the friction force: its magnitude is

\[F_{\mathrm{t}} = \mu N , \tag{8.2}\]

and its direction is opposite to the direction in which the body slides relative to the surface. The number \(\mu \ge 0\) is the coefficient of friction (koeficient trenja), a dimensionless number that we measure for the given pair of surfaces. The body acts on the surface with the two opposite forces of equal size.

Source: CK equation (3.45) (PDF p. 89) for \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = \mu_{\mathrm{k}} N\) and the direction against the velocity, NORTON §11.5 (PDF p. 633) for \(f = \pm\mu N\) and the direction against the velocity in a sliding joint, BARBER equation (12.78) (PDF p. 201) for the slip region in a contact.

The letter \(\mu\) here is not the Lamé constant of chapter 3, the context tells the meaning. In the lectures the law is written \(F_{\mathrm{tr}} = \mu N\).

Where does the normal force come from? The law needs \(N\), and this is a constraint force that we do not know in advance. We obtain it from equilibrium perpendicular to the surface. We cut out bodies 1 and 2 together. In the vertical direction the weight \(m_1 g\), the weight \(m_2 g\) and \(N\) act on them, and since they do not move vertically,

\[N = (m_1 + m_2)\,g .\]

The normal force holds the weight of all bodies above the sliding surface, not only of the body that touches the surface. For \(m_1 = 4\) kg, \(m_2 = 2\) kg and \(\mu = 0{,}3\) we get \(N = 58{,}86\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 17{,}66\) N.

On an incline of slope \(\alpha\) (right part of the figure) a body of mass \(m\) slides downward. We resolve the weight \(mg\) into a part along the incline and a part \(mg\cos\alpha\) perpendicular to it. Perpendicular to the incline the body does not move, so \(N = mg\cos\alpha\). The friction force \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = \mu\,mg\cos\alpha\) points up the incline, against the sliding. For \(m = 4\) kg, \(\alpha = 20°\) and \(\mu = 0{,}3\) we get \(N = 36{,}87\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 11{,}06\) N.

The law holds only when the body slides. This assumption must be checked in every problem. A body that is at rest on a surface is held by the surface with a force along itself that is exactly as large as equilibrium demands and at most \(\mu N\) in magnitude (BARBER equation (12.77), PDF p. 201). On the incline the part of the weight along the incline is \(mg\sin\alpha\). In figure 8.12 this part is drawn as a function of the slope, and next to it the largest force \(\mu N = \mu\,mg\cos\alpha\) with which the surface holds a body at rest. At \(\alpha = 20°\) the part of the weight is \(13{,}42\) N, more than \(\mu N = 11{,}06\) N. The body really slides and the law holds. At \(\alpha = 15°\) the part of the weight along the incline is \(10{,}16\) N, while \(\mu N\) would be \(11{,}37\) N. The body stays at rest and the friction force is \(10{,}16\) N. Whoever inserted \(\mu N\) here would get a force that pulls a body at rest up the incline. The border between the two cases is \(\tan\alpha = \mu\), here at \(\alpha = 16{,}7°\). To the left of it the body is at rest and the friction force equals the part of the weight along the incline, to the right the body slides and the friction force is \(\mu N\). For real surfaces the limiting force at rest is somewhat larger than the force of sliding (NORTON §10.11, PDF p. 593), which this model neglects. If the limiting force at rest is, for example, 20 % larger, that is \(0{,}36\,N\) instead of \(0{,}3\,N\), the border moves from \(16{,}7°\) to \(19{,}8°\). The body at \(20°\) still slips, since \(0{,}36\cdot 36{,}87 = 13{,}27\) N is less than \(13{,}42\) N. The law also does not hold when a layer of lubricant separates the two surfaces. Then the force comes from shearing the lubricant in the gap, and it grows with the speed of sliding (NORTON §10.11, PDF p. 593).

How does friction enter virtual work? The friction force is exerted by a constraint, but we cannot leave it out like the hinge force, because its work does not drop out. We therefore count it among the applied forces: it goes into the sum with the term force times the virtual displacement of the point on which it acts. If we move body 1 by \(\delta x\) in the direction of sliding, the work of the friction force is \(-\mu N\,\delta x = -17{,}66\,\delta x\) N. The peculiarity is that its magnitude contains \(N\), a constraint force, which the equation of virtual work does not give. We therefore compute the normal force separately and first: from equilibrium perpendicular to the surface as above, or by removing the constraint in thought and keeping its force, as we will do with the guide force in the example below. When later in this chapter we convert the forces to numbers we choose ourselves, friction will enter in the same way.

In mechanism D a friction force of 10 N on the slider \(C\), directed toward \(+x\) because the slider slides toward \(A\), would on the admissible displacement \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}} = -0{,}2955\,\delta\theta\) m (computed in the example below) do the work \(10\cdot(-0{,}2955)\,\delta\theta = -2{,}955\,\delta\theta\) N m. This is not zero, so with friction the work of the constraint does not drop out, as it does in the four previous cases. The guide force on the slider will be \(G = 276{,}3\) N in the example, so the force 10 N corresponds to the coefficient of friction \(\mu = 10/276{,}3 = 0{,}036\).

Definition 8.3 Ideal constraint (idealna vez)

A constraint of a system of rigid bodies is ideal (idealna) if the sum of the virtual works of the forces that the constraint exerts on the bodies is zero for every admissible virtual displacement of the system (definition 8.1).

Source: GR §1.2.1 (PDF p. 35-36) for the rigid constraint between two particles, where the forces cancel because the points have the same displacement in the direction of the constraint. The definition for a system of rigid bodies and the examples of hinge, guide, rolling and rope are not from the sources.

By the reasoning above, the hinge and the pin on the ground, the smooth guide, rolling without slipping on a body at rest or in motion and the inextensible taut rope over a pulley without friction are ideal constraints, and sliding friction is not. In this chapter the constraints will be ideal, except where we say otherwise. The friction force we will then count among the applied forces.

8.1.3 The principle of virtual displacements for a system (načelo virtualnih pomikov za sistem)

We say that a system of rigid bodies is in equilibrium when each body is in equilibrium under all forces on it, applied and constraint forces (definition 2.3).

Theorem 8.4 Principle of virtual displacements for a system of rigid bodies (načelo virtualnih pomikov za sistem togih teles)

Let a system of rigid bodies with ideal constraints be in equilibrium. Then the virtual work of the applied forces is zero for every admissible virtual displacement of the system.

Proof

We must show that the constraint forces cancel in the sum of the work. We write the sum of the work of all forces on all bodies in two ways. Figure 8.4 shows the steps on mechanism D, while the proof holds for every system. In it the constraint forces are orange.

  1. Body \(k\) is in equilibrium under all forces on it, applied and constraint forces. By theorem 4.73 the virtual work of all forces on body \(k\) is zero for all \(\delta\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\). We take them from an admissible virtual displacement of the system. In the figure the bodies are bar \(AB\) with the forces \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\), \(P\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\) and bar \(BC\) with the forces \(-\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(G\).
  2. We add the virtual works over all bodies \(k = 1, \dots, K\). The sum is zero because each term is zero.
  3. The sum is the virtual work of all forces on the system. We split it into the virtual work of the applied forces and the virtual work of the constraint forces, because each force is either an applied force or a constraint force.
  4. By definition 8.3 the virtual work of the constraint forces is zero for every admissible virtual displacement. In the figure: the pin force acts at a point that does not move, the hinge forces are opposite and act on the same displacement, the guide force is perpendicular to the displacement of the slider.
  5. From steps 2 and 4 it follows that the virtual work of the applied forces is zero.

Source: GR §1.2.1 (PDF p. 35-36) for a system of particles, EP §4.2.1 (p. 167). The proof is not from the sources.

The proof showed where the advantage comes from. The equilibrium equations of the individual bodies contain the constraint forces. On an admissible displacement their work drops out, and only the work of the forces we know remains. The converse, that zero virtual work implies equilibrium, we do not prove in this section. Why is it not as simple? From the sum being zero it does not yet follow that each term is zero. For each body separately we must find constraint forces that balance it, and show that such forces exist. We will obtain them later in this chapter, when we prove the theorem that tells when a condition between numbers determines one by the other. We state and prove the converse even later, when we describe the position of the system with numbers we choose ourselves. Until then we check the result by virtual work with a computation of equilibrium joint by joint.

We test the theorem at once on the cylinder with the rope of figure 8.9. How large must the force \(H\) be for the cylinder to be at rest? The constraints are rolling on the rail and the rope over the pulley. Both are ideal, so we need neither the force of the rail nor the rope force. One number describes the admissible displacements, the rotation of the cylinder \(\delta\varphi\) clockwise. The applied forces are the weight of the load \(W = 19{,}62\) N, the force \(H\) and the weight of the cylinder. The load sinks by \(3r\,\delta\varphi\) in the direction of the weight, so the work of the weight is \(+W\cdot 3r\,\delta\varphi\). The centre \(O\) moves by \(r\,\delta\varphi\) to the right, against the force \(H\), so its work is \(-H\,r\,\delta\varphi\). The weight of the cylinder acts at the centre \(O\), which moves horizontally, perpendicular to the weight, so the weight of the cylinder does no work. By theorem 8.4 the sum is zero for every \(\delta\varphi\):

\[3r\,W - r\,H = 0 , \qquad \text{so} \qquad H = 3W = 58{,}86\ \text{N} .\]

The radius \(r\) has dropped out. Why three times? The point \(T\), at which the rope pulls, moves three times as far as the centre, at which the force \(H\) holds, so a third of the force suffices for the same work. Let us check with the equilibrium of the cylinder. We take the moments about the contact point \(Q\), because the force of the rail passes through it and has no lever arm. The weight of the cylinder passes through \(Q\) too, since \(Q\) is vertically below the centre \(O\). The rope force \(S\) has the lever arm \(3r\), the force \(H\) the lever arm \(r\), so \(3r\,S = r\,H\). The load is at rest, so \(S = W\), and again \(H = 3W\). The computation by equilibrium needed the rope force, virtual work did not.

8.1.4 Example: the toggle mechanism (zgled: kolenasti mehanizem)

Now let us compute mechanism D of section 8.1.1 at \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad. First we find the admissible virtual displacements. Bar \(AB\) has three numbers, \(\delta\vek a_1\) (two components) and \(\delta\varphi_1\), and bar \(BC\) has \(\delta\vek a_2\) and \(\delta\varphi_2\). Together that is six numbers. The constraints give five conditions: the pin \(A\) two, the hinge \(B\) two, the guide at \(C\) one. One free number remains if the conditions are independent. For mechanism D this holds when \(\cos\theta \ne 0\), as step 4 shows. At \(\cos\theta = 0\) the bars are vertical, the guide condition does not determine \(\delta\varphi_2\) and two free numbers remain. At \(\cos\theta \ne 0\) a single number determines the position of the mechanism near the given position, the angle \(\theta\). The bars have the length \(a\), so it determines the hinge \(B\) and the slider at \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\). At the same \(\theta\) the slider could also be at \(A\), where \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\), so this holds only near the given position. That the number of free virtual displacements tells in general how many numbers determine the position is the theorem we prove later in this chapter. We choose \(\delta\theta := \delta\varphi_1\), since the angle \(\theta\) changes by the rotation of bar \(AB\). The point \(A\) is the origin and the chosen point \(O_1\) of bar \(AB\).

  1. The pin \(A\) is at rest (item 1 of definition 8.1), so \(\delta\vek a_1 = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\vek u_1(P) = \delta\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\overrightarrow{AP}\).
  2. The hinge \(B\) is at \(\vek r_{\mathrm{B}} = (a\cos\theta, a\sin\theta, 0)\). By formula (2.39), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r_{\mathrm{B}} = (-a\sin\theta, a\cos\theta, 0)\), so \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = a\,\delta\theta\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\) and it is perpendicular to \(AB\). At \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = \delta\theta\,(-0{,}1478, 0{,}4777, 0)\) m.
  3. For bar \(BC\) we take the point \(B\) as the chosen point \(O_2\). The hinge \(B\) (item 1) requires \(\delta\vek a_2 = \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}}\). The end \(C\) is from \(B\) by \(\overrightarrow{BC} = (a\cos\theta, -a\sin\theta, 0)\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\overrightarrow{BC} = (a\sin\theta, a\cos\theta, 0)\). Therefore \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} + \delta\varphi_2\,(a\sin\theta, a\cos\theta, 0) = \big(a\sin\theta\,(\delta\varphi_2 - \delta\theta),\ a\cos\theta\,(\delta\theta + \delta\varphi_2),\ 0\big)\).
  4. The guide is horizontal (item 2), so the component \(y\) of \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) is zero. Since \(\cos\theta \ne 0\), it follows that \(\delta\varphi_2 = -\delta\theta\). Bar \(BC\) turns opposite to bar \(AB\).
  5. We insert \(\delta\varphi_2 = -\delta\theta\) and obtain \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (-2a\sin\theta\,\delta\theta, 0, 0)\). At \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad, \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}} = -0{,}2955\,\delta\theta\) m. When \(B\) rises, \(C\) moves toward \(A\), as figure 8.2 shows.

The guide condition from step 4 really rejects wrong displacements. Take at \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad \(\delta\varphi_2 = 0\) instead of \(-\delta\theta\), so bar \(BC\) does not turn but only moves along with \(B\). The displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) is not admissible unless it lies along the guide, that is if it has a component \(y\) that is not zero. The pin \(A\) and the hinge \(B\) are satisfied as before, since steps 1 and 3 are the same, only the guide fails. By step 3, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = \delta\theta\,(-0{,}1478, 0{,}4777, 0)\) m, which is the same \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}}\) as in step 2. Its component \(y\) is \(a\cos\theta\,\delta\theta = 0{,}4777\,\delta\theta\) m, different from zero for every \(\delta\theta \ne 0\), so the slider would leave the guide (figure 8.14). The component \(y\) from step 3 is \(a\cos\theta\,(\delta\theta + \delta\varphi_2)\) and is zero only at \(\delta\varphi_2 = -\delta\theta\). So there is exactly one admissible \(\delta\varphi_2\) for a given \(\delta\theta\).

Now let us compute the virtual work of the applied forces. These are the load \((0, -P, 0)\) on bar \(AB\) at \(B\) and the force of the spring on \(C\). How large the spring force is, figure 8.15 shows. Grey dashed we draw the mechanism in the position \(\theta_0\), in which the spring is without tension and \(2a\cos\theta_0 = 0{,}8660\) m long. BlackWhite we draw it in the position \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad. This position is flatter, so the slider on the guide is farther from the pin \(A\) and the spring is \(2a\cos\theta = 0{,}9553\) m long. The difference is the stretch of the spring \(0{,}0893\) m. The spring force is \(F_{\mathrm{s}} = k\cdot 0{,}0893\ \text{m} = 893{,}1\) N and it pulls \(C\) toward \(A\), in the direction \(-x\). The other end of the spring is at \(A\), which does not move, so the spring does no work there. We do not know the forces in the pin, the hinge and the guide, and we do not need them.

  1. Load: \((0, -P, 0)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = -P\,a\cos\theta\,\delta\theta\). At \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad this is \(-0{,}4777\,P\,\delta\theta\) m.
  2. Spring: \((-F_{\mathrm{s}}, 0, 0)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = F_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot 2a\sin\theta\,\delta\theta = 893{,}1\cdot 0{,}2955\,\delta\theta = 263{,}9\,\delta\theta\) N m.
  3. By theorem 8.4 the sum is zero for every \(\delta\theta\), so \(-0{,}4777\,P + 263{,}9 = 0\). The solution is \(P = 552{,}54\) N (computed with more digits).

In symbols: \(F_{\mathrm{s}} = k\,(2a\cos\theta - 2a\cos\theta_0)\), so \(-P\,a\cos\theta + 2k a\,(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)\cdot 2a\sin\theta = 0\), and after dividing by \(a\cos\theta\),

\[P = 4 k a\,(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)\tan\theta ,\]

where \(\tan\theta = \sin\theta/\cos\theta\). For our data, \(4\cdot 10\,000\cdot 0{,}5\cdot 0{,}08931\cdot 0{,}30934 = 552{,}54\) N. Why such a result? The point \(B\) rises by \(0{,}4777\,\delta\theta\), while the point \(C\) moves by only \(0{,}2955\,\delta\theta\), in the ratio \(2\tan\theta = 0{,}6187\). The works of the load and the spring cancel if \(0{,}4777\,P = 0{,}2955\,F_{\mathrm{s}}\), that is \(P = 0{,}6187\,F_{\mathrm{s}}\). The mechanism is a lever that transmits the spring force to the load with the ratio \(2\tan\theta\), which changes with the position.

We check the same result with equilibrium joint by joint (figure 8.16), as in chapter 4. Here we hang the load on the hinge \(B\), which is a point without mass. The bars have no weight and on each only forces at the ends act, so by lemma 4.5 the forces point along the bar. Both bars are compressed. Let \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) be the magnitudes of the compressive forces in bars \(AB\) and \(BC\). Hinge \(B\): the load \((0, -P)\), bar \(AB\) pushes the hinge along \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) with the force \(N_1\), bar \(BC\) along \((-\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) with the force \(N_2\). The component \(x\) is \(N_1\cos\theta - N_2\cos\theta = 0\), so \(N_1 = N_2 = N\). The component \(y\) is \(2N\sin\theta = P\), so \(N = P/(2\sin\theta)\). Slider \(C\): bar \(BC\) pushes it along \((\cos\theta, -\sin\theta)\) with the force \(N\), the spring pulls it toward \(A\) with the force \(F_{\mathrm{s}}\), the guide acts vertically with the force \(G\). The component \(x\) is \(N\cos\theta = F_{\mathrm{s}}\), so \(P\cos\theta/(2\sin\theta) = F_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(P = 2F_{\mathrm{s}}\tan\theta = 2\cdot 893{,}1\cdot 0{,}30934 = 552{,}54\) N. This is the same number as by virtual work. The component \(y\) at \(C\) gives in addition \(G = N\sin\theta = P/2 = 276{,}3\) N, and the pin force on bar \(AB\) is \((N\cos\theta, N\sin\theta) = (893{,}1, 276{,}3)\) N, because the pin pushes the compressed bar toward \(B\).

The computation joint by joint required the equilibrium of the hinge and the slider and the forces in the bars. Virtual work gave \(P\) from one equation. To see that the theorem really works, we check it on the numbers with figure 8.4. On bar \(AB\) act the pin force \((893{,}1, 276{,}3)\) N at \(A\), the load \((0, -552{,}54)\) N at \(B\) and the hinge force, which by the equilibrium of the bar is \((-893{,}1, 276{,}3)\) N. The work of the pin force is zero, because \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}} = \vek 0\). The work of the load is \(-263{,}9\,\delta\theta\), and the work of the hinge force on the displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}}\) is \((-893{,}1)(-0{,}1478) + 276{,}3\cdot 0{,}4777 = +263{,}9\), the sum is zero, as theorem 4.73 requires for one bar. On bar \(BC\) acts the hinge force \((893{,}1, -276{,}3)\) N at \(B\), opposite to the hinge force on bar \(AB\), with the work \(-263{,}9\,\delta\theta\). At \(C\) act the spring force with the work \(+263{,}9\,\delta\theta\) and the guide force \((0, 276{,}3)\) N with the work \(0\), because \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}}\) is horizontal. The sum for bar \(BC\) is zero too.

This is the proof of the theorem, carried out with numbers. In figure 8.17 we draw these six works as arrows on a number line, each next one at the end of the previous one, so that the end of the last one shows the sum. On the left we order them by bodies, as in steps 1 and 2 of the proof: the arrows of bar \(AB\) end at zero, and so do the arrows of bar \(BC\) at zero. On the right we order the same arrows by the kind of force, as in step 3. The work of the constraint forces, that is the pin force (zero), the two hinge forces (\(+263{,}9\) and \(-263{,}9\)) and the guide force (zero), is zero in sum, as the definition of an ideal constraint requires (step 4). The work of the forces we know, the load \(-263{,}9\) and the spring \(+263{,}9\), is therefore zero in sum (step 5), and this exactly at \(P = 552{,}54\) N.

The guide force can also be obtained by virtual work, without writing the equilibrium of the hinge (figure 8.18). We remove the guide in thought and keep its force \((0, G)\) as an applied force on \(C\). The system then has two free numbers and a displacement is admissible in which bar \(AB\) on the pin \(A\) does not move, the angle \(\theta\) stays the same, \(\delta\theta = 0\), and bar \(BC\) rotates about \(B\) by \(\delta\varphi_2\). By step 3, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = \delta\varphi_2\,(a\sin\theta, a\cos\theta, 0)\), perpendicular to \(BC\), with a horizontal part and a vertical part. The load does no work because \(B\) does not move. The work of the spring is \((-F_{\mathrm{s}}, 0, 0)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -F_{\mathrm{s}}\,a\sin\theta\,\delta\varphi_2\), and the work of the guide force is \((0, G, 0)\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = G\,a\cos\theta\,\delta\varphi_2\). The sum is zero for every \(\delta\varphi_2\), so \(G = F_{\mathrm{s}}\tan\theta = 893{,}1\cdot 0{,}30934 = 276{,}3\) N. This is the same force as by the joints. The idea of removing a constraint and keeping its force as a known one is the seed of the unit virtual displacement method of chapter 4. The computation needs only the proved direction of the theorem. The mechanism is at rest, so each bar is in equilibrium under the same forces also in the system without the guide, in which \(G\) is an applied force and the pin and the hinge are ideal constraints. By theorem 8.4 the virtual work is therefore zero also for this displacement.

Now we can say how much friction on the slider would change the result, as we promised at assumption 2. A slider at rest is held by the guide along itself with a force that is exactly as large as equilibrium demands, up to a limit (section 8.1.2). Take the limit 10 N, which at the guide force \(G = 276{,}3\) N corresponds to \(\mu = 0{,}036\). The friction force \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) on the slider in the direction \(x\) is then any number between \(-10\) N and \(+10\) N. We count it among the applied forces. On the displacement \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}} = -0{,}2955\,\delta\theta\) m it does the work \(-0{,}2955\,F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\delta\theta\), and the equation of step 8 becomes

\[-0{,}4777\,P + 263{,}9 - 0{,}2955\,F_{\mathrm{t}} = 0 , \qquad \text{so} \qquad P = 552{,}54\ \text{N} - 0{,}6187\,F_{\mathrm{t}} .\]

In figure 8.19 we plot the friction force \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) horizontally, the load \(P\) vertically. The equation is a line, which we draw only between the limits \(\pm 10\) N. At \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = +10\) N we get \(P = 546{,}36\) N, at \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = -10\) N we get \(558{,}73\) N. If the mechanism with friction is at rest in the position \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad, the load therefore lies between these two values, that is in a band of \(\pm 6{,}19\) N or \(\pm 1{,}1\) % around \(552{,}54\) N. That it is really at rest under every load in the band is the converse of the theorem, which we have not proved yet, so we check it joint by joint. The equilibrium of the slider in the direction \(x\) with the friction force \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) and with \(N = P/(2\sin\theta)\) gives \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = F_{\mathrm{s}} - P/(2\tan\theta)\). This is the same line, read from \(P\) to \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\): \(+10{,}00\) N at \(546{,}36\) N, \(-10{,}00\) N at \(558{,}73\) N and in between for the loads between them, so the friction force stays within the limit. We took the limit of 10 N as constant. If the limit were \(\mu G\), it would change with \(G = P/2\) by \(\pm 1{,}1\) % across the band, and the ends of the band would move by only \(0{,}07\) N, to \(546{,}43\) N and \(558{,}80\) N. Friction does not shift the result, it widens one number into a band.

8.1.5 Example: a bar on two springs (zgled: palica na dveh vzmeteh)

One more example, with a single rigid body, as a test of theorem 4.73, on which the system rests (figure 8.20). A uniform bar of length \(L = 2\) m and mass \(m = 30\) kg lies horizontally on two vertical springs below it, the left with stiffness \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m and the right with \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m, which stand on the ground. The horizontal motion of the bar we exclude with a smooth guide, which is an idealization: for a real bar the ends would shift a little sideways when it tilts. The axis \(y\) points up. The springs are without tension when the bar is in the position \(y = 0\), \(\varphi = 0\). Under the weight the bar sinks and tilts. The displacement of the centroid \(G\) is \(y\), the rotation of the bar is \(\varphi\) (counterclockwise, as a ratio of arc to radius), and the virtual displacements are \(\delta y\) and \(\delta\varphi\). The model is ours, not from a source.

By lemma 4.72 the ends of the bar, which are \(\mp L/2\) horizontally from the centroid, move vertically by \(y_1 = y - \tfrac{L}{2}\varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + \tfrac{L}{2}\varphi\). For the vector from the centroid to the left end \((-L/2, 0, 0)\) we have \(\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times(-L/2, 0, 0) = (0, -\tfrac{L}{2}\varphi, 0)\), and for the right end the opposite. May we neglect that the ends also move horizontally? At \(\varphi = 1{,}2\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad (computed below) the distance between the ends changes by the lemma at most by \(\tfrac12 L\varphi^2 = 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\) m, which is 0,03 % of the vertical displacements of the ends, which are of the order of 5 mm. The springs remain vertical. The applied forces on the bar are the weight \((0, -m g)\) at \(G\) with \(m g = 294{,}3\) N and the spring forces upward \(-k_1 y_1\) and \(-k_2 y_2\) at the ends. The other end of each spring is on the ground and is at rest. The guide forces are horizontal and do no work. The virtual work is

\[-m g\,\delta y - k_1 y_1\,\Big(\delta y - \tfrac{L}{2}\delta\varphi\Big) - k_2 y_2\,\Big(\delta y + \tfrac{L}{2}\delta\varphi\Big) = 0\]

for all \(\delta y\) and \(\delta\varphi\). Since they are independent, each of their coefficients must be zero. At \(\delta y\) we get \(m g + k_1 y_1 + k_2 y_2 = 0\), at \(\delta\varphi\) we get \(\tfrac{L}{2}(k_1 y_1 - k_2 y_2) = 0\). So \(k_1 y_1 = k_2 y_2 = -m g/2 = -147{,}15\) N and \(y_1 = -7{,}3575\) mm, \(y_2 = -4{,}905\) mm. The centroid sinks by \(\tfrac12(7{,}3575 + 4{,}905)\) mm \(= 6{,}1313\) mm and the bar turns by \((y_2 - y_1)/L = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad. This is also the result of the equilibrium of the bar by definition 2.3: the sum of the forces in the direction \(y\) is \(k_1 y_1 + k_2 y_2 + m g = 0\), and the sum of the moments about the centroid is \(\tfrac{L}{2}(k_1 y_1 - k_2 y_2) = 0\). These two equations are the same as above, which is expected for one body. The softer spring is the left one, and since the forces in both springs are equal, the left one is compressed more: \(7{,}36\) mm against \(4{,}91\) mm.

The section showed the following. For a system of rigid bodies with ideal constraints, the virtual work of the applied forces in equilibrium is zero for every admissible virtual displacement, and the constraint forces do not appear in this equation. We proved it by adding the principle for one body over the bodies and eliminating the constraint forces with the definition of an ideal constraint. Ideal are the hinge, the smooth guide, rolling without slipping and the inextensible taut rope. Friction is not ideal, so we count it among the applied forces, when sliding with the magnitude \(\mu N\), at rest with any magnitude up to this limit, as for the slider of mechanism D. For mechanism D we obtained the load \(552{,}54\) N at \(\theta = 0{,}3\) rad from one equation, and the computation joint by joint gave the same number. For the bar on springs we obtained the sinkings of the ends from two equations. For mechanism D one number \(\delta\theta\) described the admissible virtual displacements, and in \(\delta x_{\mathrm{C}} = -2a\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\) we recognized the derivative of the position \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\) with respect to \(\theta\), multiplied by \(\delta\theta\). That the derivative of \(\cos\theta\) is really \(-\sin\theta\) we have not proved so far, since we used both as in school. We do that in the next section.
