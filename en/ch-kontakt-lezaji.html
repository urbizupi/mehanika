The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

We begin this chapter with a question the earlier chapters leave open: what a body does to the support it presses on. In chapter 5 we loaded a beam with forces and moments, in chapter 11 we wrote the equilibrium equations and boundary conditions of an elastic body, and in chapter 12 we assembled beams into finite elements. In all of this the supports and loads were given. Here we compute them: how a foundation sinks, and how a force arises where two bodies touch.

The words of the title tell what we will do. A contact (kontakt, stik) is the place where two bodies touch and pass a force to each other. Hertz contact is named after Heinrich Hertz, who solved how two rounded bodies are squeezed when pressed together. The expression rolling bearing (kotalni ležaj) has two parts: a bearing (ležaj) is the part of a machine that carries a rotating shaft and passes its force to the housing, and rolling means that balls or rollers roll between the rings instead of the shaft sliding in the housing. A ball touches the rings only over a small area, so the contact is decisive here.

The contact decides two things. The first is strength: a steel ball of diameter 12.7 mm pressed with 100 N on a flat steel plate touches the plate in a circle of radius about 0.16 mm, so the pressure in this circle is far larger than the pressure we would get by spreading the force over the whole ball. The second is stiffness: a bearing with nine such balls is a spring that is not linear, and its stiffness decides at which frequency the shafts and machines carried by the bearing vibrate.

The chapter promises three things. First, we replace the foundation by an ever more real one: first a row of independent springs under a beam, then a solid elastic half-space that sinks also around the loaded point, and then the contact of two rounded bodies, where we compute the size of the contact area, the pressure and the approach of the bodies. Second, from this we compute how a bearing distributes its load over the balls and what its stiffness is. Third, we show how a bearing enters a calculation by the finite element method, and connect the chapter to those before it, also to the vibration of chapter 9. Where a result cannot be proved in the book, we cite it with its source and say that it is cited.

We start with the simplest foundation, the one we know from rails on sleepers.
