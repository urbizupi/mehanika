We begin the chapter with the vibration of bodies that we treat entirely as a continuum (chapter 1). It stands on four earlier chapters: the vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (9) and with several degrees of freedom (10), Hamilton's principle (8) and the Navier-Lamé equation (11). The finite elements of chapters 7 and 12 are checked here.

The chapter is named by three words. Vibration (nihanje) is a motion that repeats around an equilibrium position. A system (sistem) is a collection of bodies or parts of a body that we treat as a whole. A system is continuous (zvezen) when we imagine its matter without gaps, so that every point has its own displacement. The Latin word continuus means connected, unbroken. There are infinitely many such points, so a continuous system has infinitely many coordinates and infinitely many natural frequencies (definition 9.4). The systems of the earlier chapters had finitely many coordinates, and were therefore called discrete.

A system with one degree of freedom (chapter 9) and a system with several degrees of freedom (chapter 10) have finitely many coordinates and therefore finitely many natural frequencies (definition 9.4). A stretched cord, a bar, a shaft and a beam have a displacement at every point, so they have infinitely many coordinates. Finite elements (chapters 7 and 12) replaced them by a few nodes and gave finitely many frequencies, which are approximations. We saw on examples how good they are, but a few claims were only stated or announced, without proof. So how exactly do a stretched cord, a bar, a shaft and a beam vibrate, and how close do finite elements come to these results?

The chapter makes two promises. For a stretched cord, a bar, a shaft and a beam we write the equation of motion, compute the exact frequencies and modes and the response to an initial condition and to a harmonic force, and say for each model what we neglected. We also prove what was left without proof: that the modes are orthogonal to each other, that a Ritz approximation does not lie below the true frequency, how the turning of cross sections lowers the frequencies of a beam, and that a column clamped at one end and pinned at the other is stable up to the load \(20{,}19\,EI/L^2\). For the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends we prove the Ritz bound under the assumption that every sufficiently smooth shape of these beams is a sum of modes, which for these two supports we cite from a source and do not prove. We test the calculations on the bodies of earlier chapters, the steel cantilever with a \(20 \times 40\) mm section and a length of 1 m from chapter 5, and the bar and the beam with finite elements from chapters 7 and 12, which have a known approximation. Where a result is proved, the proof is in the text, where it is not, we say so.

The path is this. First we derive, from Newton's law for a finite piece of a stretched cord, the equation that the displacement of the cord satisfies (this section), then we solve it by separation of variables and with the Fourier series of chapter 9 and obtain the vibrations in which all points of the cord oscillate together with one frequency (the modes). We do the same for a bar and a shaft, where we obtain exact frequencies and compare them with the finite-element models. Then we show that the same equations follow from the Navier-Lamé equation of chapter 11 once inertia is added to it. A beam has an equation of fourth order, which we derive from Hamilton's principle of chapter 8. We compute its modes, prove that they are orthogonal to each other, and obtain the response to a harmonic force. That every sufficiently smooth shape can be expanded in the modes we prove for the stretched cord, for the bar and the shaft fixed at both ends or at one end, and for the simply supported beam, and for other supports of the beam we cite it from the sources. From the expansion it follows that a Ritz approximation of a natural frequency does not lie below the true value. At the end we include the turning of cross sections and shear (the Timoshenko beam of chapter 5) and prove the bound for the column, which chapter 8 only computed.

What is new in this chapter is therefore the equation that the vibration of a stretched cord satisfies and its solutions, the exact frequencies and modes of continuous systems, their orthogonality and the expansion of a motion in these vibration patterns, the Navier-Lamé equation with inertia added, Hamilton's principle applied to a beam, the Rayleigh quotient and the Ritz upper bound for continuous systems, the turning of cross sections, and the column. Everything else comes from earlier chapters: Fourier series and natural frequencies from chapter 9, eigenpairs and the Rayleigh quotient from chapter 10, Hamilton's principle from chapter 8, the finite-element matrices from chapters 7 and 12.
