Chapter 20 Damping models

20.5 Hereditary integral

In section 20.4 we obtained for springs and dashpots the relaxation modulus (relaksacijski modul) \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\) (definition 20.20): the stress that a bar shows at time \(t\) after we suddenly stretched it at time zero by a unit strain and held it so. The standard linear solid gave \(E_{\mathrm{rel}} = E_\infty + (E_0 - E_\infty)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) (theorem 20.27). But a machine has no step. A bar in a bearing, in a spring washer or in a rubber element experiences a strain that changes in any way: a ramp, a cycle, a vibration. The question of this section is therefore: if we know the whole history of the strain \(\varepsilon(s)\) for \(0 \le s \le t\), what is the stress \(\sigma(t)\)? The answer will not depend on which springs and dashpots the model is built from, only on \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\).

Let us give a reason for the name. The formula that we will obtain is hereditary (dedni): CM calls it so (hereditary integral), because the strain or stress at some time depends not only on that moment but on everything that happened before, in contrast to an elastic material, where they depend only on the moment (CM, PDF p. 438). The Slovenian word comes from the verb "dedovati" (to inherit): the stress now inherits all earlier strains. We use the full name only at the theorem.

The model that we use. The body is a straight bar under uniaxial stress: one stress \(\sigma\) and one strain \(\varepsilon\) appear, each a number at every time (as in section 20.4). We prescribe the strain, for example by moving the jaw of a testing machine, and compute the stress. Up to time zero the bar is at rest: \(\varepsilon = 0\) and no internal change has yet happened. We name the idealizations. First, small strains, as in section 3.1.4 (point 1): for them stress and strain are proportional, so stresses add. How small they must be is told by experiment (a doubled history must give a doubled stress), and we do not quote numbers for polymers from memory: all numbers of this section are dimensionless numbers of the chosen model, not material data (the standard linear solid of the example in section 20.4 with \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\), so \(E_1 = E_\infty\), which we call model D). Second, constant temperature, because BOWER derives the law for constant temperature up to time \(t\) (BOWER, PDF p. 137). Third, at time zero we start from rest, as we did for the Duhamel integral (theorem 9.79).

20.5.1 Idea: the history as steps

Before we prove anything, let us think as if drawing on a board. We replace a strain history that is not a step by steps, because for these we know the response. For the history we take the function \(\varepsilon(s) = \varepsilon_0\,(1 + 0{,}5\sin(2s/\tau))\) for times from \(0\) to \(3\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the relaxation time of model D. The letter \(\tau\) here is a time, not a shear stress (there is no shear stress in the chapter).

On the top axis we draw time \(s\) and the strain \(\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0\), then the given history, which starts at \(\varepsilon(0) = \varepsilon_0\). Until time zero the bar was at rest, so the history starts with a jump from \(0\) to \(\varepsilon_0\). We replace the history by steps: at times \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\) the strain jumps to the value that the history has at that point and stays constant until the next time. The step heights are the differences \(\Delta_0 = \varepsilon(0)\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = \varepsilon(s_{\mathrm{i}}) - \varepsilon(s_{\mathrm{i}-1})\) for \(i \ge 1\). The negative \(\Delta_2\) means unloading, the strain between \(\tau\) and \(2\tau\) falls. The bottom axis shows the same time \(t\), and above it we draw the stress.

How does the bar respond to one step? By definition 20.20 a bar stretched at the instant \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) behaves at time \(t \ge s_{\mathrm{i}}\) like a bar that has already been stretched for the time \(t - s_{\mathrm{i}}\): the stress is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\). This reasoning uses two properties. The law is linear, so a doubled strain gives a doubled stress and the stresses of successive steps add (this is the Boltzmann superposition principle, CM, PDF pp. 436-438). The law has constant coefficients, so the response to a step at time \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the same as the response at time zero, only shifted by \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\). In the figure the responses of the steps are thin blue lines: each starts at its own step, and the response to the negative step \(\Delta_2\) lies below the axis. The stress is the sum of the responses,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{h}}(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{i} \,:\, \mathrm{s}_\mathrm{i} \le \mathrm{t}} E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} ,\]

so the figure shows the blackwhite line as the sum of the blue lines, and the jump of the blackwhite line at the start of each step is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(0)\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = E_0\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) (right after a step the bar responds with the instantaneous modulus \(E_0\) that section 20.4 defined). The first term, the jump at time zero, is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0)\) and we must not forget it: at \(t = 3\tau\) it is \(1{,}0498\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\), while the other three steps together give only \(-0{,}1459\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The values of the four terms at \(t = 3\tau\) are \(1{,}0498\), \(0{,}5162\), \(-1{,}1395\) and \(0{,}4774\), the sum is \(0{,}9038\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The same sum is given by the law of the material that we write in section 20.5.2, if we compute it for the stepwise strain piece by piece (in every piece the strain is constant, so the computation is an exponential function, and at a jump \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) stays continuous, since the dashpot does not move in an instant): \(0{,}903841\) in both computations (num-05.out).

The true stress (dashed line) at \(t = 3\tau\) is \(0{,}9804\), so with three steps the error is \(0{,}0766\), that is \(7{,}8\) %. Narrower steps do better, with the same computation (NumPy, steps at times \(s_{\mathrm{i}} = 3\tau i/N\)):

For \(N \ge 24\) the error roughly halves with every doubling of \(N\) (the ratios of successive errors are \(0{,}58\), \(0{,}53\) and \(0{,}52\)), so for narrow steps it is roughly proportional to the step width \(h\). What is the sum when we narrow the steps? The step height is the difference of the values of a differentiable function at the ends of a piece of width \(h\), and by the mean value theorem \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = \dot\varepsilon(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,h\) for some point \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) in the piece. The sum is therefore like a Riemann sum

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{h}}(t) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0) + \sum_{\mathrm{i} \ge 1} E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s_{\mathrm{i}})\,\dot\varepsilon(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,h ,\]

which has for its limit \(h \to 0\) the integral \(\int_0^t E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) (the mark at the end of the integral only closes it, it is not a factor). This is an idea, not a proof: we do not know that the limit really is the stress of the model, and for a general linear material it is the Boltzmann principle, which we cite without proof (CM, equations (9.3.7)-(9.3.9), PDF p. 438, BOWER, PDF p. 137). A proof for the models that the section treats we get as with the Duhamel integral: we write the formula as a candidate and check that it solves the equations of the model.

20.5.2 Prony material

Which models have equations that we can check? The standard linear solid of section 20.4 is a spring \(E_\infty\) in parallel with one Maxwell element (definition 20.22). Why not more elements? BOWER says that simple models are too far from the measurements of a polymer over a longer time, and so he connects in parallel several Maxwell elements, each with its own time, and a spring in parallel with all of them (BOWER, PDF p. 139). The text layer of the book on this page says that the elements are connected "in series", but the formula for \(G(t)\) is the relaxation modulus of a parallel connection (a misprint of the book, PDF p. 139): at a common strain the stresses add, in a series connection the compliances would add. We take the parallel connection, which theorem 20.30 below confirms.

We write the law of one Maxwell element with the strain of the dashpot, so that the equation has no derivative of the strain \(\varepsilon\), since at a jump it does not exist. A Maxwell element is a spring of stiffness \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\) in series with a dashpot in which the stress is proportional to the rate of its strain, \(\sigma = \eta_{\mathrm{j}}\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) with the coefficient \(\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\). Let \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\) be the strain of the dashpot, so that the strain of the spring is \(\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\). Both elements carry the same stress (series connection, the elements are massless): \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}) = \eta_{\mathrm{j}}\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\). With \(\tau_{\mathrm{j}} = \eta_{\mathrm{j}}/E_{\mathrm{j}}\) we get \(\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = (\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}})/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). At time zero the dashpot has not yet moved, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0\). We call the time \(\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) the relaxation time (relaksacijski čas) of element \(j\): it has the unit second and tells in what time, after a step, the strain of the spring \(\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) decreases by a factor \(\mathrm{e}\) (which lemma 20.29 proves).

Definition 20.28 Prony series and Prony material (Pronyjeva vrsta in Pronyjev material)

Let \(N \ge 1\) be an integer, \(E_\infty \ge 0\) and \(E_1, \dots, E_{\mathrm{N}} > 0\) moduli in pascals, and \(\tau_1, \dots, \tau_{\mathrm{N}} > 0\) times in seconds. The function \(E_{\mathrm{rel}} : [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\),

\[E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} , \tag{20.18}\]

is a Prony series (Pronyjeva vrsta). Let \(T > 0\) and \(\varepsilon : [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}\) a continuous function (the strain after time zero, the value \(\varepsilon(0)\) is the strain right after the start, before time zero the strain is zero). A Prony material (Pronyjev material) has the stress

\[\sigma(t) = E_\infty\,\varepsilon(t) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\big(\varepsilon(t) - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\big) , \tag{20.19}\]

where the internal variables (notranje spremenljivke) \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} : [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}\) are solutions of the equations

\[\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = \frac{\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0 \qquad (j = 1, \dots, N) . \tag{20.20}\]

Since \(\varepsilon\) is continuous, each equation has by lemma 9.34 (with \(s = -1/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(f = \varepsilon/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\)) exactly one solution, which is differentiable everywhere, so \(\sigma\) is thereby a uniquely determined continuous function.

Source: A series of exponential terms and a spring in parallel with the Maxwell elements are BOWER, PDF p. 139 ("Prony series", the text says "in series", see above). The notation with the strain of the dashpot as an internal variable is ours. We quote the name "Prony" as BOWER uses it, we have not read the reason for it.

For \(N = 1\) and \(E_1 = E_0 - E_\infty\) the Prony series has the same \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) as the standard linear solid. We check that this function really is the response to a step.

Lemma 20.29 Relaxation modulus of a Prony material (relaksacijski modul Pronyjevega materiala)

Let a Prony material have the strain \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0\) for every \(t \in [0, T]\) (a step at time zero). Then \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t) = \varepsilon_0\,(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}})\) and \(\sigma(t) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon_0\) with \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) from equation (20.18). The function \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) is positive and strictly decreasing, \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(0) = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}} =: E_0\) and \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) \to E_\infty\) for \(t \to \infty\).

Proof

We must show that the announced \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) solves equation (20.20), then compute the sum (20.19).

  1. The function \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t) = \varepsilon_0\,(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}})\) has \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0\) and \(\dot\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = \varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). On the other hand \((\varepsilon_0 - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}})/\tau_{\mathrm{j}} = \varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). The function solves equation (20.20), and by uniqueness (lemma 9.34) it is \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. We insert into equation (20.19): \(\varepsilon_0 - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = \varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\), so \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0\,\big(E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon_0\).
  3. All terms \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\) are positive and \(E_\infty \ge 0\), so \(E_{\mathrm{rel}} > 0\). The derivative \(\dot E_{\mathrm{rel}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (E_{\mathrm{j}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} < 0\), so it is strictly decreasing. At \(t = 0\) we have \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\). Since the exponential function with a negative argument decreases toward zero, the limit is \(E_\infty\).

Source: The formula is BOWER, PDF p. 139. CM, PDF p. 438, demands of the relaxation modulus non-negativity, monotone decrease and a finite limit, which we prove here for the series (20.18). The proof is ours.

The lemma tells two things that we use. First, \(E_0\) is the modulus right after a step, \(E_\infty\) the modulus after a long time. This suggests that a bar is stiffer when we load it faster, which is the promise of the section on linear elasticity for polymers such as nylon (BOWER, PDF pp. 96, 120). The example in section 20.5.6 shows it with a number. Second, \(\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) really is the time in which the stress of element \(j\), \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\varepsilon_0\), falls by a factor \(\mathrm{e}\), since \(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mathrm{e}^{-1}\).

20.5.3 Hereditary integral

Now we have everything to prove the idea of figure 20.12. We insert the candidate that the idea announces into the equations (20.20) and check that it satisfies them. For this we need the derivative of an integral in which \(t\) appears both in the upper limit and in the integrand (lemma 9.77 from chapter 9), and the theorem on uniqueness of the solution of a first-order equation (lemma 9.34). The strain may be piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11), which covers a ramp that stops after time \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\), and a jump in the rate.

Theorem 20.30 Hereditary integral (dedni integral)

Let the material be a Prony material (definition 20.28) with relaxation modulus \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\), \(T > 0\) and \(\varepsilon : [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}\) a continuous function that is piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11), with a piecewise continuous derivative \(\dot\varepsilon\). Then the stress (20.19) for every \(t \in [0, T]\) is

\[\sigma(t) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0) + \int_0^t E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s . \tag{20.21}\]
Proof

We must show that every term of the sum (20.19) has the form (20.21). The term with \(E_\infty\) is the fundamental theorem of calculus. For the term with index \(j\) we take the candidate \(R_{\mathrm{j}}\) that the idea announces, and show that it has the same equation and the same initial value as \(\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\).

  1. Let \(E_\infty \ge 0\). By lemma 6.12 (a continuous piecewise continuously differentiable function) \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon(0) + \int_0^t\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), so \(E_\infty\,\varepsilon(t) = E_\infty\,\varepsilon(0) + \int_0^t E_\infty\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. Let \(j\) be fixed and \(\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). Let \(R(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\varepsilon(0) + G(t)\) with \(G(t) = \int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)/\tau}\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The kernel \(g(t, s) = \mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)/\tau}\) is continuous on the square \([0, T]^2\), differentiable in \(t\) with the derivative \(\partial g/\partial t = -g/\tau\), which is continuous, and \(g(t, t) = 1\).
  3. By lemma 9.77 \(G\) is continuous and at every point \(t\) that is not a division point of \(\dot\varepsilon\) differentiable with the derivative \(G'(t) = g(t, t)\,\dot\varepsilon(t) + \int_0^t (-g(t, s)/\tau)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \dot\varepsilon(t) - G(t)/\tau\). The derivative of the first term of \(R\) is \(-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\varepsilon(0)/\tau\). So \(R\) is continuous, \(R(0) = \varepsilon(0)\) and at these points \(\dot R = \dot\varepsilon - R/\tau\). The derivative is piecewise continuous.
  4. Let \(D = \varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\). The function \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuously differentiable (lemma 9.34, \(f = \varepsilon/\tau\) continuous), so \(D\) is continuous and \(D(0) = \varepsilon(0) - 0\). At the same points as in step 3 we have \(\dot D = \dot\varepsilon - (\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}})/\tau = \dot\varepsilon - D/\tau\).
  5. Let \(W = D - R\). It is continuous, \(W(0) = 0\) and at every point outside finitely many division points \(\dot W = -W/\tau\), that is \(\dot W - sW = 0\) for \(s = -1/\tau\). By uniqueness in lemma 9.34 (\(f = 0\), \(y_0 = 0\)) \(W = 0\), so \(\varepsilon - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = R\).
  6. We multiply step 5 by \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\) and add over \(j\) together with step 1. By linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10, point 2) \(\sigma(t) = \big(E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big)\,\varepsilon(0) + \int_0^t \big(E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), and this is equation (20.21).

Source: The formula is CM, equations (9.3.7)-(9.3.8), PDF p. 438, and BOWER, PDF p. 137, for an arbitrary linear material (Boltzmann superposition principle, not proved there and not here either). The proof for a Prony material by verification and first-order uniqueness is not from the source, it is the same as the path of the proof of theorem 9.79.

What did the theorem show and what not? It showed that for a Prony material the stress at time \(t\) is the integral of the strain rate \(\dot\varepsilon(s)\) multiplied by the weight \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\), and that the jump at the start contributes its own term. It did not show that the same holds for an arbitrary linear material (this is the Boltzmann principle, which we cite), nor for a history with jumps later than at time zero: then the expression gets a further term \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) for every jump (as in figure 20.12, but we do not prove this here. CM, PDF p. 438, equation (9.3.6), writes the initial jump together with the integral as a Stieltjes integral, for the creep compliance). The weight \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\) tells with what modulus a change of strain at time \(s\) still contributes to the stress at time \(t\). Since \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) decreases (lemma 20.29), older changes count less than newer ones.

We test the theorem on the same example as in figure 20.12, now for \(t = 3\tau\). We draw the axis \(s\) and on it the strain rate \(\dot\varepsilon(s)/\varepsilon_0 = \cos(2s/\tau)\), which is positive at the start, negative from \(0{,}79\,\tau\) to \(2{,}36\,\tau\) and again positive. Across it we draw the weight \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(3\tau - s)\): at \(s = 0\) it is \(1{,}0498\), because \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(3\tau) = E_\infty(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-3})\), and at \(s = t\), where the integral ends, it is \(2\), because \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(0) = E_0\). Below is the product of the two. Its signed area is \(\int_0^{3\tau}E_{\mathrm{rel}}(3\tau - s)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = -0{,}0694\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\): the positive parts (\(s < 0{,}79\,\tau\) and \(s > 2{,}36\,\tau\)) together do not offset the negative part, so the integral is negative. We add the jump \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0) = 1{,}0498\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\), and get \(\sigma(3\tau) = 0{,}9804\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The same follows from the law with the internal variable if we solve equation (20.20) with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (4000 steps): \(0{,}980390\), the difference from the integral is \(4 \cdot 10^{-14}\). The data are in num-05.out.

20.5.4 An example that fails the test: without the jump

We could regard the term \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0)\) in the theorem as unnecessary, since the integral holds all the strain rate. Let us test this. We try the simplest history, the step \(\varepsilon(s) = \varepsilon_0\) for all \(s \in [0, T]\), which is continuous and has \(\dot\varepsilon = 0\). The formula with only the integral would give \(\sigma(t) = \int_0^t E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\cdot 0\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). A bar that we stretched at time zero and hold would have no stress. But lemma 20.29 says that the stress is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon_0\), that is \(1{,}6065\), \(1{,}3679\) and \(1{,}1353\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\) at \(t = 0{,}5\tau\), \(\tau\) and \(2\tau\) (model D). The difference is all of the stress. The reason is that the whole jump from zero to \(\varepsilon_0\) at time zero is collected in the value \(\varepsilon(0)\): the history on \([0, T]\) is a constant and its derivative is zero everywhere, and the jump before the start the integral \(\int_0^t\dot\varepsilon\) does not see. Theorem 20.30 says this with the term \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0)\) and in the case \(\varepsilon \equiv \varepsilon_0\) the whole answer is this term. In figure 20.12 it is the same: the jump \(\Delta_0\) gives the first term of the sum, and without it the sum at \(3\tau\) would be \(-0{,}1459\) instead of \(0{,}9038\).

A second example that fails the test is the Kelvin-Voigt element: a spring and a dashpot in parallel have no relaxation modulus as a function. If we stretch it in an instant, the dashpot would have to cover the finite elongation in an instant, the rate would be infinite and so would the stress in the dashpot. This is why BOWER says that the Kelvin-Voigt model has no well-defined relaxation modulus (BOWER, PDF p. 138). Such an element is not a Prony material and theorem 20.30 does not hold for it. Its response to a stress history is computed by CM from the compliance (CM, example 9.3.1, PDF p. 439), the reverse question, which we do not treat here.

20.5.5 Memory in \(N\) numbers

Formula (20.21) demands the whole history: in the sum of steps we must store all steps and at every time step multiply them all by a new weight, so there is more work with every step. A Prony material, however, has a short memory: the strain history influences the stress only through \(N\) numbers \(\alpha_1(t), \dots, \alpha_{\mathrm{N}}(t)\). Let us show this on one time step.

Lemma 20.31 Update of the internal variables (posodobitev notranjih spremenljivk)

Let the material be a Prony material, \(0 \le t_{\mathrm{n}} < t_{\mathrm{n}+1} \le T\), \(h = t_{\mathrm{n}+1} - t_{\mathrm{n}}\), and let \(\varepsilon\) be linear on \([t_{\mathrm{n}}, t_{\mathrm{n}+1}]\), \(\varepsilon(t_{\mathrm{n}} + u) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,u\) with \(r = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}+1} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}})/h\). Then for every \(j\)

\[\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}+1}) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}+1} - r\,\tau_{\mathrm{j}} + \big(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}}) - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{-h/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} , \tag{20.22}\]

and the stress at \(t_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) is equation (20.19) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) and the \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}+1})\) so obtained. The history before \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) influences the stress at \(t_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) only through the \(N\) numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}})\).

Proof

We must show that the expression in \(u\) solves equation (20.20) on the piece and has the right initial value, then use uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(a = \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(y(u) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,u - r\,\tau + (a - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-u/\tau}\) for \(u \in [0, h]\). Then \(y(0) = a\) and \(y'(u) = r - (a - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-u/\tau}/\tau\).
  2. On the other hand \((\varepsilon(t_{\mathrm{n}} + u) - y(u))/\tau = \big(r\,\tau - (a - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} + r\,\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-u/\tau}\big)/\tau\), which is exactly the same as \(y'(u)\).
  3. The function \(u \mapsto \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}} + u)\) solves the same equation with the same initial value \(a\) (the coefficients do not depend on time, chain rule, as in step 10 of the proof of theorem 9.79). By uniqueness (lemma 9.34, with a shift of time) \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}(t_{\mathrm{n}} + u) = y(u)\). At \(u = h\) this is equation (20.22).

Source: The proof is not from a source, it is derived from lemma 9.34.

A test. Let us take the history of figure 20.13, but now piece by piece linear between the values \(\varepsilon(s_{\mathrm{n}})\) at steps \(h = 0{,}5\tau\), from \(0\) to \(3\tau\). Six updates by equation (20.22) give \(\sigma(3\tau) = 0{,}9625824501\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\), and the hereditary integral of the same piecewise linear history gives \(0{,}9625824501\), the difference is in computer precision. Let us compare also with a simpler route. If we solve equation (20.20) with the explicit Euler method with the same step, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} \leftarrow \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} + h\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}})/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\), we get \(1{,}0202\), \(6\) % too much. The reason is the memory factor: instead of \(\mathrm{e}^{-h/\tau} = 0{,}6065\) Euler takes \(1 - h/\tau = 0{,}5\) (for \(\tau = \tau_1\)), so the bar forgets too fast, because the step \(0{,}5\,\tau\) is not small against the relaxation time. Equation (20.22) has the exponent exactly. The comparison is not a proof, it only shows that a crude difference scheme must not be used when the step is comparable to the relaxation time.

20.5.6 Example: loading rate

The promise of the section on the linearly elastic material, that the behaviour of polymers depends on the loading rate, we now fulfil with a number. We stretch the bar uniformly to \(\varepsilon_0\) in the time \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\): \(\varepsilon(s) = \varepsilon_0 s/t_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s \le t_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(\varepsilon(0) = 0\) and \(\dot\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0/t_{\mathrm{r}}\). There is no jump and theorem 20.30 gives

\[\sigma(t_{\mathrm{r}}) = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{t_{\mathrm{r}}}\int_0^{t_{\mathrm{r}}} E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t_{\mathrm{r}} - s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \varepsilon_0\Big(E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{t_{\mathrm{r}}}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big)\Big) ,\]

since \(\int_0^{t_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(t_{\mathrm{r}}-s)/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\mathrm{d}s = \tau_{\mathrm{j}}(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}})\) (lemma 9.78, point 1). For \(t_{\mathrm{r}} \to 0\) we have \((\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/t_{\mathrm{r}})(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}) \to 1\) (the derivative of the function \(x \mapsto \mathrm{e}^{-x}\) at \(x = 0\) is \(-1\), so \((1 - \mathrm{e}^{-x})/x \to 1\) for \(x \to 0\), here with \(x = t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\)), the stress is \(E_0\,\varepsilon_0\) as for a step. For \(t_{\mathrm{r}} \to \infty\) we have \((\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/t_{\mathrm{r}})(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}) \le \tau_{\mathrm{j}}/t_{\mathrm{r}} \to 0\), and the stress is \(E_\infty\,\varepsilon_0\).

In the figure there is the axis \(t_{\mathrm{r}}/\tau\) in logarithmic form, the limits \(E_0\) and \(E_\infty\) as dashed lines and the curve between them. For model D the stress at the end for three ramps is \(1{,}9516\), \(1{,}6321\) and \(1{,}1000\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\), when \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) equals \(0{,}1\,\tau\), \(\tau\) and \(10\,\tau\). The same strain gives, if we reach it in a tenth of the relaxation time, \(1{,}77\) times as much stress (\(1{,}95\)) as if we reach it in ten times the relaxation time (\(1{,}10\)). At \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = \tau\) the formula gives \(1 + 1\cdot(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-1}) = 1{,}6321\), which is a point on the curve. The modulus of a polymer is therefore undetermined without the stated loading rate.

20.5.7 What follows

A strain history that is not a step demands weights. For a material whose relaxation modulus is a sum of exponential terms the hereditary integral (20.21) is exact (theorem 20.30), not only an approximation by steps. The memory of the material is in \(N\) internal variables (lemma 20.31). What the integral gives for an oscillating strain is shown in section 20.6: it takes \(\varepsilon(s) = \varepsilon_0\cos\Omega s\) and computes the phase of the stress against the strain. Sources of the section: CM §9.3.1 (PDF pp. 436-439), BOWER §3.6.2 (PDF pp. 136-137) and §3.6.4 (PDF p. 139). The paths of the proofs (verification and uniqueness) are from theorem 9.79, the numbers are in outlines/ch-dusenje/num-05.out.
