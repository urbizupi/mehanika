Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.6 The Jacobian matrix of a robot (Jacobijeva matrika robota)

We draw the robot as a chain. The base is fixed to the floor, link 1 turns about joint 1, link 2 about joint 2. Each motor has its own rate, \(\dot q_1\) and \(\dot q_2\), drawn as an arc about the joint. At the end of the chain is the end effector. Its velocity shows where it moves, its rotation how fast it turns. We look for the relation between the two arcs on one side and the velocity and rotation of the end effector on the other.

So far we have given the robot a position: the joint variables (spremenljivke zglobov) \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) (section 27.1) give, by direct kinematics (definition 27.31), the origin \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}(\vek q)\) and the orientation matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}(\vek q)\) of the end effector (končni efektor, definition 27.8). Motors, however, do not turn by a fixed angle. Each has its own rate \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The question is how fast the end effector moves and how fast it turns. We need the answer three times: to find the positions where the robot can no longer move in every direction (section 27.7), for the joint forces that balance a load on the end effector (section 27.7), and for the kinetic energy of the links (section 27.8).

The model is the one used before (section 27.1): rigid links (definition 1.11), ideal joints with one degree of freedom held by motors, and an open chain. Friction and motor inertia do not appear. The variables \(q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) are twice continuously differentiable functions of time, like the entries of the matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) in definition 8.39. Notation: the orientation matrix of link \(n\) relative to frame \(0\) is written in short \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat R^0{}_{\mathrm{n}}\). Its columns are the axes of frame \(n\) in frame \(0\) (definition 27.8), so it is the transpose of the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) of definition 2.44, where the axes are in the rows. It is not the polar factor of chapter 2 and not \(\mat R(\varphi)\) of chapter 8. The origin of frame \(i\) in frame \(0\) is \(\vek o_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek o^0{}_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the unit vector of the \(z\) axis of frame \(i\) in frame \(0\) is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k\) with \(\vek k = (0, 0, 1)\). The matrix we shall build is written \(\mat J(\vek q)\). The inertia tensor of chapter 8, written \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) there, is written \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) in this chapter, so that the two symbols never meet.

27.6.1 Angular velocity of a link (kotna hitrost člena)

We draw a single link. Frame 0 is at rest, the link is a rigid body with its own axes at the point \(\vek o\). We pick a point of the link \(\vek x\) and the vector \(\vek x - \vek o\) from the origin to it. The origin moves with velocity \(\dot{\vek o}\), and the link turns about an axis through the origin with angular velocity \(\vek\omega\). Because of the turning the point runs along a circle and has velocity \(\vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek o)\), tangent to the circle. The sum of both velocities is the velocity of the point \(\dot{\vek x}\).

First we need the velocities of the points of one link. For the motion \(\vek x = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\), chapter 8 showed that all points of a rigid body have the velocity \(\dot{\vek c} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek c)\) (lemma 8.87) with one single angular velocity \(\vek\omega\) (definition 8.86). Link \(n\) is such a body. A point with constant coordinates \(\vek p^n\) in the frame of the link has the position \(\vek x = \vek o_{\mathrm{n}} + \mat R_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek p^n\) in frame \(0\) by lemma 27.9, which is the form \(\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\) with \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}\) in place of \(\mat Q\). The matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}\) need not equal the unit matrix at time \(0\), so we repeat the proof of lemma 8.85 for \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Why \(\mat\Omega\) must be antisymmetric is seen on two arrows fixed to the link. At time \(t\), the arrow \(\vek u\) and the arrow \(\vek v\) equal the axes \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_2\) of frame \(0\), they have unit length and are perpendicular. The link turns with the angular velocity \(\omega\) about an axis perpendicular to the page. At time \(t + \Delta t\) both have turned by the same angle \(\omega\,\Delta t\), and their tips stay on the unit circle. An arrow \(\vek y = \mat R\,\vek p\) fixed to the link has, by step 4 of the proof below, the velocity \(\dot{\vek y} = \dot{\mat R}\,\vek p = \mat\Omega\,\vek y\). So \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat\Omega\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the component along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the velocity of the arrow that equals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) at time \(t\). The velocity \(\dot{\vek u}\) is tangent to the circle and perpendicular to \(\vek u\), so \(\Omega_{11} = 0\). The arrow \(\vek u\) tilts towards \(\vek v\), so \(\Omega_{21} = \omega\). The arrow \(\vek v\) tilts away from \(\vek u\) by the same angle, its velocity \(\dot{\vek v}\) points along \(-\vek e_1\) and \(\Omega_{12} = -\omega\). If \(\Omega_{11}\) were not \(0\), \(\vek u\) would grow longer or shorter. If \(\Omega_{12}\) were not \(-\Omega_{21}\), the right angle would open or close. The proof says the same with matrices: the orthogonality of the matrix \(\mat R\) means exactly that lengths and angles are kept.

Lemma 27.37 Derivative of the orientation matrix and angular velocity of a link (odvod matrike orientacije in kotna hitrost člena)

Let \(\mat R(t)\) be the orientation matrix of a link relative to frame \(0\), with twice continuously differentiable entries, and \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T\). Then \(\mat\Omega\) is antisymmetric, for \(\vek\omega = (\Omega_{32},\ \Omega_{13},\ \Omega_{21})\) we have \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y = \vek\omega\times\vek y\) for every vector \(\vek y\), and \(\dot{\mat R} = \mat\Omega\,\mat R\). A point with constant coordinates \(\vek p\) in the frame of the link, which has the position \(\vek x = \vek o + \mat R\,\vek p\) in frame \(0\), has the velocity

\[\dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek o} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek o) . \tag{27.21}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\mat\Omega\) is antisymmetric and then use that \(\vek p\) is constant.

  1. The matrix \(\mat R\) is orthogonal (lemma 27.9), so \(\mat R\,\mat R^\T = \mat I\) at every time. By the product rule (lemma 1.82), applied to every entry, \(\dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T + \mat R\,\dot{\mat R}^\T = \mat 0\).
  2. By lemma 1.102, \(\mat\Omega^\T = \mat R\,\dot{\mat R}^\T\), which by step 1 equals \(-\mat\Omega\). The matrix \(\mat\Omega\) is antisymmetric.
  3. Steps 3 and 4 of the proof of lemma 8.85 use only \(\mat\Omega^\T = -\mat\Omega\) and (2.39), so they give \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y = \vek\omega\times\vek y\).
  4. From \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\) (lemma 27.9), \(\dot{\mat R} = \dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat\Omega\,\mat R\).
  5. Since \(\vek p\) is constant, \(\dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot{\mat R}\,\vek p\). By step 4 and step 3 with \(\vek y = \mat R\,\vek p = \vek x - \vek o\), \(\dot{\mat R}\,\vek p = \mat\Omega\,\mat R\,\vek p = \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek o)\).

Source: SPONG (4.18) (PDF p. 129) for \(\dot{\mat R} = \mat S(\vek\omega)\,\mat R\), (4.32) and (4.35) (PDF p. 132) for the velocity of a point. The step-by-step proof for \(\mat R\) is a copy of the proof of lemma 8.85 and is not from the source.

For the motion treated in lemma 8.87, \(\mat Q(t) = \mat R(t)\,\mat R(0)^\T\), because it has the value \(\mat I\) at time \(0\) and turns the point \(\vek X\) of the frame as \(\mat R(t)\,\vek p\) does. Hence \(\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T = \dot{\mat R}\,\mat R(0)^\T\mat R(0)\,\mat R^\T = \dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T\), and the angular velocity of lemma 27.37 is exactly the angular velocity of definition 8.86. The motion of the points of a link is thus described by two vectors, \(\dot{\vek o}\) and \(\vek\omega\), that is, by six numbers. The connection between them and the joint rates \(\dot{\vek q}\) is sought in the next subsection.

We draw three links. Frame 0 is at rest, frame 1 is attached to link 1, which turns with angular velocity \(\vek\omega_1\) relative to frame 0. Link 2 turns relative to link 1 with rate \(\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2}\), which is measured in frame 1, so we draw it converted to frame 0. Relative to frame 0 its angular velocity \(\vek\omega_2\) is the sum of the first vector and the second. We repeat this for link 3: its relative rate is added and gives the total rate \(\vek\omega_3\).

A robot has several links that turn relative to each other, so angular velocities have to be added. We know the angular velocity of link \(1\) relative to frame \(0\) and that of link \(2\) relative to link \(1\). What is the angular velocity of link \(2\) relative to frame \(0\)? Vectors written in different frames must not be added, so we first need an identity that tells how the vector product behaves under a rotation.

The left part shows a rotation. We take vector \(\vek a\), vector \(\vek b\) and their vector product \(\vek a\times\vek b\), perpendicular to the plane of both. The rotation \(\mat R\) turns all three together as a rigid triple: \(\mat R\,\vek a\), \(\mat R\,\vek b\) and \(\mat R\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\) result. For the proof we take an arbitrary vector \(\vek c\) and its preimage \(\vek u = \mat R^\T\vek c\) before the rotation. The box on \(\vek u\), \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) and the box on \(\vek c\), \(\mat R\,\vek a\), \(\mat R\,\vek b\) have the same volume, since a rotation keeps volume. The right part shows why we need \(\det\mat R = 1\): for the reflection \(\mat R = -\mat I\) we have the vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and their product \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the reversed vectors and their product, again \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), but the reversed original product points to \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Lemma 27.38 The vector product and a rotation (vektorski produkt in zasuk)

Let \(\mat R\) be an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix with \(\det\mat R = 1\). Then for all vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\)

\[(\mat R\,\vek a)\times(\mat R\,\vek b) = \mat R\,(\vek a\times\vek b) . \tag{27.22}\]

If an antisymmetric matrix \(\mat\Omega\) satisfies \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y = \vek a\times\vek y\) for every \(\vek y\), then \(\mat R\,\mat\Omega\,\mat R^\T\vek y = (\mat R\,\vek a)\times\vek y\) for every \(\vek y\).

Proof

We must show that the two vectors in (27.22) have the same scalar product with every vector, and then use this.

  1. Let \(\vek c\) be any vector and \(\vek u = \mat R^\T\vek c\), so \(\vek c = \mat R\,\vek u\) (lemma 27.9).
  2. The scalar triple product is the determinant of the matrix with the vectors in its columns ((2.71)), so \(\vek c\cdot\big((\mat R\,\vek a)\times(\mat R\,\vek b)\big) = \det(\mat R\,\vek u,\ \mat R\,\vek a,\ \mat R\,\vek b)\).
  3. The columns of \(\mat R\,\mat M\) are \(\mat R\) times the columns of \(\mat M\) ((1.39)). So the determinant of step 2 is \(\det\big(\mat R\,(\vek u, \vek a, \vek b)\big) = \det\mat R\cdot\det(\vek u, \vek a, \vek b)\) by theorem 2.74, that is, \(\vek u\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  4. By lemma 1.102, \(\vek u\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = (\mat R^\T\vek c)\cdot(\vek a\times\vek b) = \vek c\cdot\mat R\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\).
  5. The difference \(\vek d = (\mat R\,\vek a)\times(\mat R\,\vek b) - \mat R\,(\vek a\times\vek b)\) therefore satisfies \(\vek c\cdot\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek c\). For \(\vek c = \vek d\) we get \(|\vek d|^2 = 0\) and \(\vek d = \vek 0\). This is (27.22).
  6. Second part: \(\mat R\,\mat\Omega\,\mat R^\T\vek y = \mat R\,(\vek a\times\mat R^\T\vek y)\) by assumption, which by (27.22) equals \((\mat R\,\vek a)\times(\mat R\,\mat R^\T\vek y) = (\mat R\,\vek a)\times\vek y\).

Source: SPONG (4.9) and (4.8) (PDF p. 127): both identities are stated in the source with "verified by direct calculation". The proof is ours.

The condition \(\det\mat R = 1\) is needed. The reflection \(\mat R = -\mat I\) has \(\det\mat R = -1\) and is orthogonal, but for \(\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek b = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) we get \((\mat R\,\vek a)\times(\mat R\,\vek b) = (-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\times(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), whereas \(\mat R\,(\vek a\times\vek b) = -\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (num-06.out N4). Step 3 of the proof is where the factor \(\det\mat R = -1\) would show. Rotations preserve right-handedness (lemma 27.9), reflections do not.

Lemma 27.39 Addition of angular velocities (seštevanje kotnih hitrosti)

Let \(\mat R_2 = \mat R_1\,\mat R^1{}_2\) by lemma 27.12, all three matrices with twice continuously differentiable entries. Let \(\vek\omega_1\) be the angular velocity of \(\mat R_1\) and \(\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2}\) that of \(\mat R^1{}_2\) by lemma 27.37 (the components of the first in frame \(0\), of the second in frame \(1\), because \(\mat R^1{}_2\) is measured in it). Then the angular velocity of \(\mat R_2\) is

\[\vek\omega_2 = \vek\omega_1 + \mat R_1\,\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2} . \tag{27.23}\]

For a chain of \(n\) links, \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n \mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek\omega^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\mat R_0 = \mat I\).

Proof

We must show that \(\dot{\mat R}_2\,\mat R_2^\T\) is the sum of two antisymmetric matrices that we know, and then read off the vector.

  1. By the product rule (lemma 1.82), \(\dot{\mat R}_2 = \dot{\mat R}_1\,\mat R^1{}_2 + \mat R_1\,\dot{\mat R}^1{}_2\).
  2. By lemma 1.102, \(\mat R_2^\T = (\mat R^1{}_2)^\T\mat R_1^\T\). Multiplying and using \(\mat R^1{}_2(\mat R^1{}_2)^\T = \mat I\) gives \(\dot{\mat R}_2\,\mat R_2^\T = \dot{\mat R}_1\,\mat R_1^\T + \mat R_1\,\big(\dot{\mat R}^1{}_2(\mat R^1{}_2)^\T\big)\,\mat R_1^\T\).
  3. The first term is \(\mat\Omega_1\), with angular velocity \(\vek\omega_1\). In the second, the bracket is by lemma 27.37 the matrix with \(\vek y \mapsto \vek\omega^1{}_{1,2}\times\vek y\). By the second part of lemma 27.38, the second term is the map \(\vek y \mapsto (\mat R_1\,\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2})\times\vek y\).
  4. So the matrix \(\mat\Omega_2\) acts as \(\vek y \mapsto (\vek\omega_1 + \mat R_1\,\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2})\times\vek y\). If a matrix acts as \(\vek y \mapsto \vek a\times\vek y\), then \(\Omega_{32} = (\vek a\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})_{\mathrm{z}} = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Omega_{13} = (\vek a\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Omega_{21} = (\vek a\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{y}} = a_{\mathrm{z}}\), so its vector by definition 8.86 is exactly \(\vek a\). This is (27.23).
  5. For a chain we use (27.23) with induction on \(n\): \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{n}-1}\,\mat R^{n-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}\) (lemma 27.12) gives \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}-1} + \mat R_{\mathrm{n}-1}\,\vek\omega^{n-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}-1,\mathrm{n}}\), and \(\vek\omega_1 = \mat R_0\,\vek\omega^0{}_{0,1}\) is the start.

Source: SPONG (4.20)-(4.26) (PDF p. 130-131) and (4.28)-(4.30) (PDF p. 131). The step-by-step proof is ours, the source uses (4.8) and \(\mat S(\vek a) + \mat S(\vek b) = \mat S(\vek a + \vek b)\).

The sum (27.23) is an addition of vectors, correct only when all components are written in the same frame: \(\mat R_1\,\vek\omega^1{}_{1,2}\) is the angular velocity of link \(2\) relative to link \(1\), converted into frame \(0\) (lemma 27.9). That is why the last step of the proof has the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) in front of every term.

On the left is a revolute joint. The axis \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) runs through the joint, link \(i-1\) is at rest relative to it, link \(i\) turns about it along the arc \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The angular velocity vector lies along the axis and has length \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\). In the middle is a prismatic joint: the slider moves along the axis with the sliding rate, there is no turning, so the angular velocity is zero. On the right we look at the turning from above: the axes before the turn and the axes after a small turn \(\mathrm{d}\theta\), the vector \(\vek y\) and the vector \(\vek k\times\vek y\), which is turned by \(90^\circ\) in the direction of the rotation.

What is the angular velocity of one joint? By section 27.4, \(\mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{x},\alpha_\mathrm{i}}\) (lemma 27.28, definition 27.10). At a revolute joint \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the variable \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (or \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) plus a constant) and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant, at a prismatic joint \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant.

Lemma 27.40 Angular velocity of a joint (kotna hitrost zgloba)

Let \(\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\mat K\,\vek y = \vek k\times\vek y\) for every \(\vek y\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta} = \mat K\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\). At a revolute joint \(i\), \(\vek\omega^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}} = \dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k\), at a prismatic joint \(\vek\omega^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\).

Proof

We must show both derivatives and then compute \(\dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T\).

  1. By (2.39), \(\vek k\times\vek y = (0\cdot y_3 - 1\cdot y_2,\ 1\cdot y_1 - 0\cdot y_3,\ 0) = (-y_2,\ y_1,\ 0) = \mat K\,\vek y\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) has the entries \(\cos\theta\), \(-\sin\theta\), \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\) and \(1\). The derivatives are \(\sin' = \cos\) and \(\cos' = -\sin\) (theorem 8.13), so the derivative of the matrix with respect to \(\theta\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} -\sin\theta & -\cos\theta & 0 \\ \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), which is \(\mat K\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) by the definition of the matrix product (definition 1.99).
  3. At a revolute joint, \(\dot{\mat R}^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}} = \dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mat K\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\mat R_{\mathrm{x},\alpha_\mathrm{i}}\) by the chain rule and step 2. By lemma 1.102, \((\mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}})^\T = \mat R_{\mathrm{x},\alpha_\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}^\T\), hence \(\dot{\mat R}^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}(\mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}})^\T = \dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mat K\), since both rotations are orthogonal (lemma 27.9). By step 1 it acts as \(\vek y \mapsto \dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k\times\vek y\), and the vector of definition 8.86 is \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k\) (step 4 of the proof of lemma 27.39).
  4. At a prismatic joint \(\mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant, its derivative is \(\mat 0\), and so \(\vek\omega^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\).

Source: SPONG (4.43) and (4.44) (PDF p. 134). The step-by-step proof is ours.

Each revolute joint thus turns the next link about its axis \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) at the rate \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and a prismatic joint does not turn it. The next subsection builds the angular part of the Jacobian matrix from this.

27.6.2 The matrix that maps joint rates to the velocity of the end effector (matrika, ki preslika hitrosti zglobov v hitrost efektorja)

Let us see how the velocity of the end effector is assembled from the motor rates on robot A, the planar arm with two links, \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, met in section 27.1 (the link masses are our choice, we do not need them here). Draw the arm at \(\vek q = (30^\circ;\ 60^\circ)\) (figure 27.57). The base is at the origin. Link 1 goes from the base to the second joint at the angle \(30^\circ\), link 2 continues at the angle \(q_1 + q_2 = 90^\circ\), vertically upward, to the tip of the end effector. The axes of both joints, \(\vek z_0\) and \(\vek z_1\), point perpendicular to the page toward us, because the arm turns in the plane of the page.

Now let only motor 1 turn, at the rate \(\dot q_1\) (left part of the figure). Motor 2 is at rest, so both links behave as one rigid body that turns about the axis \(\vek z_0\) with the angular velocity \(\dot q_1\,\vek z_0\) (lemma 27.40). The tip, on the lever arm \(\vek r_1 = \vek o_2 - \vek o_0\) from the axis to the tip, has by lemma 27.37 (with the fixed origin \(\vek o_0\)) the velocity \(\dot q_1\,\vek z_0\times\vek r_1\). The vector product is perpendicular to the lever arm (lemma 2.34, item 4), which also has a reason in the picture: a point of a turning body moves on a circle about the axis, so its velocity lies along the tangent of that circle, and the tangent is perpendicular to the radius, which here is the lever arm. The length of the product is \(|\vek r_1| = \sqrt{0{,}866^2 + 1^2} = 1{,}3229\) m times \(\dot q_1\). For \(\dot q_1 = 1\) rad/s this is the velocity \(\vek J_1\), drawn from the tip. In plain words: at the same angular velocity a point on a long lever arm moves faster than a point on a short one, and the direction of motion is perpendicular to the lever arm. In the second part (middle of the figure) only motor 2 turns, at the rate \(\dot q_2\). Link 1 is at rest, link 2 turns about the axis \(\vek z_1\) through the second joint, the lever arm \(\vek r_2\) is only the length of link 2, \(|\vek o_2 - \vek o_1| = 0{,}5\) m, and the tip moves with the velocity \(\vek J_2\) \(= \vek z_1\times\vek r_2\) at \(\dot q_2 = 1\) rad/s. In the right part both motors turn at once, each with its own rate. The velocity of the tip is the time derivative of its position, and this position is a function of both angles, so by the chain rule (lemma 8.30) the velocity is the sum of the two contributions, each multiplied by its motor rate, \(\vek v = \vek J_1\,\dot q_1 + \vek J_2\,\dot q_2\). The numbers \(\dot q_1\) and \(\dot q_2\) thus determine the tip velocity through a matrix that has \(\vek J_1\) and \(\vek J_2\) as columns.

The name Jacobian matrix is already borne by the matrix of derivatives of a map from chapter 4 (definition 4.42). This matrix is also a matrix of derivatives: column \(i\) is the derivative of the tip position with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (step 3 of theorem 27.42), which is why it is so called. For the angular velocity there are no such derivatives (lemma 27.44), so we define the matrix for both parts together by its task.

Definition 27.41 Jacobian matrix of a robot (Jacobijeva matrika robota)

Let a robot have \(n\) joints, joint variables \(\vek q\) and the end-effector orientation matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}(\vek q)\). Let \(\vek v = \dot{\vek o}_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the velocity of the end-effector origin, \(\vek\omega = \vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the angular velocity of \(\mat R_{\mathrm{n}}\) by lemma 27.37 (both in frame \(0\)) and \(\vek\xi = (\vek v;\ \vek\omega)\) the column of six numbers. The Jacobian matrix of the robot is the \(6 \times n\) matrix \(\mat J(\vek q)\) that is a function of the variables \(\vek q\) only and satisfies, for every motion \(\vek q(t)\),

\[\vek\xi = \mat J(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q} . \tag{27.24}\]

We call the upper \(3\) rows \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) and the lower \(3\) rows \(\mat J_\omega\), so \(\vek v = \mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\vek\omega = \mat J_\omega\,\dot{\vek q}\). Column \(i\) is called \(\mat J_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: SPONG (4.37)-(4.42) (PDF p. 133), where \(\vek\xi\) is called a "body velocity" and the matrix is written as \(6 \times n\). Here the matrix is defined by property (27.24), existence and uniqueness are in theorem 27.42.

Units: \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is in rad/s for a revolute and in m/s for a prismatic joint, so the matrix \(\mat J\) has columns with different units (the column of a revolute joint has m above and 1 below, that of a prismatic joint 1 above and 0 below). The definition does not say whether such a matrix exists and what it is. The figure shows that column \(i\) is the velocity obtained when only joint \(i\) turns. The theorem proves it.

We draw a general chain, the links from the base with its origin \(\vek o_0\). The vector \(\vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) points to joint \(i\), through which the axis \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) runs. The lever \(\vek r\) leads from the joint axis to the end effector \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}\). If only joint \(i\) turns with rate \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), the angular velocity has the direction of the axis and the end effector has velocity perpendicular to the lever and to the axis. Below is a prismatic joint: its axis points in the sliding direction, and the velocity of the end effector is just the axis vector, with no turning.

Theorem 27.42 Columns of the Jacobian matrix (stolpci Jacobijeve matrike)

Let \(\vek q(t)\) be a twice continuously differentiable motion of a robot with \(n\) joints and DH frames \(0, \dots, n\) (section 27.4). Let \(\rho_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) for a revolute and \(\rho_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for a prismatic joint \(i\). Then the Jacobian matrix of the robot exists, is unique, and has the columns

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{i}} = \begin{pmatrix} \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1}) \\ \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1} \end{pmatrix} \text{ (revolute joint)} , \qquad \mat J_{\mathrm{i}} = \begin{pmatrix} \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1} \\ \vek 0 \end{pmatrix} \text{ (prismatic joint)} . \tag{27.25}\]
Proof

We must show first that \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\), then that \(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) equals the upper part of column \(i\), and finally use the chain rule.

  1. By lemma 27.40, \(\vek\omega^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{i}} = \rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k\). By lemma 27.39, \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek k = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\). This is \(\mat J_\omega\,\dot{\vek q}\) with the column \(\rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\), which is the lower part of (27.25).
  2. The positions are composed by lemma 27.22 from \(\mat T^0{}_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat T^0{}_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\mat T^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mat T^i{}_{\mathrm{n}}\). Hence \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1} + \mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{n}} + \vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\). By lemma 27.28 every \(\mat T^{j-1}{}_{\mathrm{j}}\) contains only the variable \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) do not contain \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and neither does \(\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 1), \(\dot{\vek o}_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \dfrac{\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The vector \(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is thus column \(i\) of \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) and is the end-effector velocity when \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) and all other \(\dot q_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\).
  4. Prismatic joint: \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = d_{\mathrm{i}}\) and by lemma 27.28 \(\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\,(a_{\mathrm{i}},\ 0,\ d_{\mathrm{i}})\), while \(\mat R^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\) does not depend on \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\). Hence \(\partial\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial d_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\,\vek k = \vek k\) and by step 2 \(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial d_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek k = \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\).
  5. Revolute joint: \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}}\) or \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) plus a constant, which does not change the derivative. By lemma 27.28, \(\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\,\vek w\) with \(\vek w = (a_{\mathrm{i}},\ 0,\ d_{\mathrm{i}}) + \mat R_{\mathrm{x},\alpha_\mathrm{i}}\,\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{n}}\), which does not depend on \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) (step 2). By lemma 27.40, \(\partial\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}/\partial\theta = \mat K\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) and \(\mat K\,\vek y = \vek k\times\vek y\). Hence \(\partial\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial\theta_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek k\times\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  6. By step 2 and by (27.22), \(\dfrac{\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,(\vek k\times\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}) = (\mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek k)\times(\mat R_{\mathrm{i}-1}\,\vek o^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{n}}) = \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\).
  7. Steps 1 and 3 together with 4 and 6 give \(\vek\xi = \mat J\,\dot{\vek q}\) with (27.25). Uniqueness: if \(\mat J'\) also satisfies (27.24), we take the motion \(\vek q + t\,\vek u\) with a vector \(\vek u\) and get \(\mat J'\,\vek u = \mat J\,\vek u\) for every \(\vek u\). For \(\vek u = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) the columns \(i\) are equal.

Source: SPONG (4.43)-(4.47) (PDF p. 134) for the angular part, (4.48)-(4.54) (PDF p. 135-137) for the velocity part and (4.55)-(4.58) (PDF p. 137) for the columns. The source uses figures 4.1 and 4.2 (PDF p. 135-136) instead of a computation with partial derivatives. The step-by-step proof is ours, the source has the same idea of one joint at a time.

The column of a revolute joint is thus what the figure drew: the angular velocity \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and the tip velocity \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times\vek r\) with the lever arm \(\vek r = \vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) from the joint axis to the tip. The column of a prismatic joint is the velocity \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) along the axis and no angular velocity. We look at three examples.

A frequent mistake is the lever arm from the base instead of from the joint axis. For robot A it would mean \(\mat J_2 = \vek k\times(\vek o_2 - \vek o_0)\), which is \((-1;\ 0{,}866)\) m instead of \((-0{,}5;\ 0)\) m (left and middle part of figure 27.57). At \(\dot{\vek q} = (1;\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s the tip would get the velocity \((-1{,}5;\ 1{,}299)\) m/s instead of \((-1{,}25;\ 0{,}866)\) m/s, which is \((-0{,}25;\ 0{,}433)\) m/s too much (num-06.out N3). Joint \(2\) turns only link \(2\) about its own axis, so the lever arm is the distance to that axis, as the theorem says.

Example A, the planar arm. At \(\vek q = (30^\circ;\ 60^\circ)\) (figure 27.57) the origins are \(\vek o_0 = (0, 0, 0)\), \(\vek o_1 = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}5;\ 0)\) m, \(\vek o_2 = (0{,}866;\ 1;\ 0)\) m and the axes \(\vek z_0 = \vek z_1 = \vek k\) (the plane \(xy\), turning about the axis \(z\)). By (27.25), \(\mat J_1 = \vek k\times(\vek o_2 - \vek o_0) = \vek k\times(0{,}866;\ 1;\ 0) = (-1;\ 0{,}866;\ 0)\) m, because \(\vek k\times\vek y = (-y_2,\ y_1,\ 0)\), and \(\mat J_2 = \vek k\times(0;\ 0{,}5;\ 0) = (-0{,}5;\ 0;\ 0)\) m above, and \(\vek k\) below in both columns. The matrix is

\[\mat J = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -0{,}5 \\ 0{,}866 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The first column is exactly \(\vek J_1\) from the left and the second \(\vek J_2\) from the middle of the figure. At \(\dot{\vek q} = (1;\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s, \(\vek\xi = \mat J\,\dot{\vek q} = (-1{,}25;\ 0{,}866;\ 0;\ 0;\ 0;\ 1{,}5)\), the tip velocity is \((-1{,}25;\ 0{,}866)\) m/s (right part of the figure) and the angular velocity of the end effector is \((\dot q_1 + \dot q_2)\,\vek k = 1{,}5\,\vek k\) rad/s. We checked the computation with central differences of the tip position and of the matrix \(\mat R_2\) at the step \(10^{-6}\) s: the tip velocity agrees to \(10^{-10}\) m/s, and \(\dot{\mat R}_2\,\mat R_2^\T\) gives \(\Omega_{21} = 1{,}5\) rad/s (num-06.out A4, A5). The upper \(2 \times 2\) matrix is the matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial\vek o_2/\partial\vek q\) (definition 4.42), and its determinant is \(0{,}433 = l_1\,l_2\sin q_2\) m\(^2\). The bottom row of ones says that the end effector turns with the sum of the two motor rates, which equals the sum of the angles \(q_1 + q_2 = 90^\circ\) of the position.

Example C, a prismatic joint. The arm with one revolute and one prismatic joint (figure 27.59) has its base at the origin, and the axis \(\vek z_0\) points out of the page. The sleeve turns about the base through the angle \(q_1\), the rod slides in it and is at the distance \(d\) from the base, drawn as a distance with end bars. The revolute joint has the rate \(\dot q_1\), shown by an arc, the prismatic joint the rate \(\dot d\) along the rod. We choose \(q_1 = 30^\circ\) and \(d = 1{,}2\) m. The axis of the prismatic joint is the direction of the rod, so \(\vek z_1 = (\cos q_1,\ \sin q_1,\ 0) = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}5;\ 0)\), which we get from a DH table with the row \((a, \alpha, d, \theta) = (0, 90^\circ, 0, q_1 + 90^\circ)\) (\(\mat R_1 = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{q}_1 + 90^\circ}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\) has the third column \((\cos q_1,\ \sin q_1,\ 0)\), and the constant \(90^\circ\) does not change the derivative). The tip is at \(\vek o_2 = d\,\vek z_1 = (1{,}039;\ 0{,}6;\ 0)\) m, the origins \(\vek o_0 = \vek o_1\) are at the base. By (27.25), \(\mat J_1 = (\vek k\times\vek o_2;\ \vek k) = (-0{,}6;\ 1{,}039;\ 0;\ 0;\ 0;\ 1)\) and \(\mat J_2 = (\vek z_1;\ \vek 0) = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}5;\ 0;\ 0;\ 0;\ 0)\). At \(\dot q_1 = 0{,}5\) rad/s and \(\dot d = 0{,}2\) m/s, \(\vek v = (-0{,}127;\ 0{,}620;\ 0)\) m/s, the contribution of turning \((-0{,}3;\ 0{,}520)\) m/s and the contribution of sliding \((0{,}173;\ 0{,}1)\) m/s lie along the tangent and along the rod, and their sum is the tip velocity. The direct derivative \(\dot d\,\vek z_1 + d\,\dot q_1\,(-\sin q_1,\ \cos q_1,\ 0)\) gives the same (num-06.out C2, C3). The prismatic column has no lever-arm term, because a motion along the axis involves no turning.

We draw a spatial arm with three revolute joints. The base carries the vertical axis \(\vek z_0\) of the first joint. Link 1 is vertical up to the origin \(\vek o_1\). The axes \(\vek z_1\) and \(\vek z_2\) of the second and third joint are parallel, link 2 and link 3 lie in a plane perpendicular to them. The tip \(\vek o_3\) is the end of link 3. The Jacobian matrix does not hold only for the end effector. The velocity of any point rigidly attached to link \(k\) is the derivative of its position by the chain rule, and the steps of theorem 27.42 can be repeated.

Corollary 27.43 Jacobian matrix of a point of a link (Jacobijeva matrika točke člena)

Let \(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}} = \vek o_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek p\) be the position of a point with constant coordinates \(\vek p\) in the frame of link \(k\). Then \(\dot{\vek o}_{\mathrm{c}} = \mat J_{\mathrm{v}}^{(c)}\,\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat J_\omega^{(k)}\,\dot{\vek q}\), where the \(3 \times n\) matrices have, for \(i \le k\), the columns \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\) and \(\rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) at a revolute joint and \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(\vek 0\) at a prismatic one, and zero columns for \(i > k\). For the centre of mass \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}\) of link \(k\) these are the matrices \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the proof of theorem 8.97.

Proof

We must show that steps 1 to 7 of theorem 27.42 hold if \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}\) is replaced by \(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}}\) and the chain is shortened to \(k\) links.

  1. The point \(\vek c\) has in frame \(i \le k\) the position \(\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{c}} = \mat R^i{}_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek p + \vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{k}}\), which contains only \(q_{\mathrm{i}+1}, \dots, q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (step 2 of theorem 27.42). So steps 2 and 4 to 6 of the theorem can be repeated with \(\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{c}}\) in place of \(\vek o^i{}_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. For \(i > k\) the position \(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}} = \vek o_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek p\) contains only \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\). The same holds for \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i} \le \mathrm{k}}\rho_{\mathrm{i}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) by step 1 of theorem 27.42 with the shortened chain.
  3. In the proof of theorem 8.97 (step 1) the velocity of the centre of mass is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) and the angular velocity \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) for scleronomic constraints, such as the joints of a robot. A \(3 \times n\) matrix with this property is unique (the same argument as in step 7), so \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat J_{\mathrm{v}}^{(c)}\) for the \(\vek p\) of the centre of mass and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat J_\omega^{(k)}\).

Source: SPONG example 4.6 (PDF p. 138-139): "the usual Jacobian with \(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}}\) in place of \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}\)". The connection with \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) is ours.

We apply the corollary to the centres of mass of the links of robot A. They are \(\vek c_1 = (0{,}433;\ 0{,}25;\ 0)\) m (the middle of link 1) and \(\vek c_2 = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}75;\ 0)\) m (the middle of link 2, which points vertically upward). By corollary 27.43, \(\mat A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}25 & 0 \\ 0{,}433 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (link 1 does not depend on \(q_2\)) and \(\mat A_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}75 & -0{,}25 \\ 0{,}866 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (upper two rows). The matrices \(\mat W_1\) and \(\mat W_2\) have zero first two rows, and their third rows are \((1,\ 0)\) and \((1,\ 1)\). At \(\dot{\vek q} = (1;\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s the velocities of the centres are \((-0{,}25;\ 0{,}433)\) and \((-0{,}875;\ 0{,}866)\) m/s, equal to central differences of the position (num-06.out A7, A8). These are the matrices that section 27.8 will insert into the mass matrix.

27.6.3 The angular velocity is not the derivative of any three angles (kotna hitrost ni odvod nobenih treh kotov)

The upper rows of the matrix \(\mat J\) are the matrix of partial derivatives (definition 4.42) of the map \(\vek q \mapsto \vek o_{\mathrm{n}}\), the derivative of something we measure. Is the lower part \(\vek\omega = \mat J_\omega\,\dot{\vek q}\) also the derivative of some three numbers that describe the orientation, for example three angles, just as \(\dot{\vek o}_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the derivative of the three coordinates of the tip? If it were, we could take \(\mat J_\omega\) as a matrix of partial derivatives and save the separate treatment. The answer is no, and it holds however the angles are chosen.

Imagine a single rigid frame that we turn four times about the axes of the base frame, each time by \(90^\circ\) between time \(0\) and \(1\) s at a constant angular velocity: about the axis \(x\) by \(+90^\circ\), about \(z\) by \(+90^\circ\), about \(x\) by \(-90^\circ\), about \(z\) by \(-90^\circ\). In each turn about an axis of the base frame the orientation matrix is multiplied from the left (lemma 27.12). Figure 27.62 shows the states \(t_0\) to \(t_4\) of the frame, that is, the columns of the matrix \(\mat R\) in frame \(0\), and the four turns between them. We view the frame obliquely: in state \(t_0\) the axis \(x\) points toward us (drawn slanting down to the left), the axis \(y\) to the right and the axis \(z\) up. The first turn, about the axis \(x\) by \(90^\circ\), raises the axis \(y\) up and turns the axis \(z\) to the left, which is state \(t_1\). The second, about the axis \(z\) by \(90^\circ\), turns the axis \(x\) to the right and the axis \(z\) toward us, which is state \(t_2\). The third, about the axis \(x\) by \(-90^\circ\), turns the axis \(x\) down and the axis \(y\) to the right, which is state \(t_3\). The fourth, about the axis \(z\) by \(-90^\circ\), gives the last state. The end is not the start: the last frame has its axis \(x\) down, its axis \(y\) toward us and its axis \(z\) to the left, while the initial one, drawn dashed, has \(x\) toward us, \(y\) to the right and \(z\) up.

The figure shows what the proof below says. On the left is the plane of the components \(x\) and \(z\) of the sum \(\int_0^t\vek\omega\,\mathrm{d}\tau\), whose component \(y\) stays \(0\). The first turn moves the sum by \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) along the axis \(x\), the second by \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) along the axis \(z\), the third and the fourth by the same amounts back. After the four turns the sum is back at the origin. If \(\vek\omega\) were the derivative of three numbers \(\vek\varphi(\mat R)\), then by the fundamental theorem the difference \(\vek\varphi(\mat R(t)) - \vek\varphi(\mat I)\) would walk exactly this path and return to \(\vek 0\). On the right is the frame itself: the last one does not coincide with the first one, and the tip of its axis \(x\) has moved by \(0{,}1414\) m. A closed path on the left and an open one on the right cannot both hold, and this is the contradiction of the proof.

Lemma 27.44 The angular velocity is not the derivative of an orientation (kotna hitrost ni odvod orientacije)

There is no function \(\vek\varphi(\mat R)\) that assigns three numbers to every orientation matrix so that \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\vek\varphi(\mat R(t)) = \vek\omega(t)\) for every motion and that two different matrices have different triples.

Proof

We must show that such a function would imply that the four turns of figure 27.62 return the frame to the start, which does not hold.

  1. In the first part, let the turn about the axis \(x\) move as \(\mat R(t) = \mat R_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{s}(\mathrm{t})}\,\mat R_0\) with \(\mat R_0\) at the start of the part and \(s(t) = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,t\). Then \(\dot{\mat R}\,\mat R^\T = \dot{\mat R}_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{s}}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{s}}^\T\) (as in lemma 27.40 for the axis \(z\)), which is \(\dot s\) times the matrix that acts as \(\vek y \mapsto \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek y\). So \(\vek\omega = \dot s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) by lemma 27.37. The same holds for the axis \(z\) with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. On each of the four parts \(\vek\omega\) is constant, so the integrals of \(\vek\omega\) over the time of the parts are, in order, \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Their sum is \(\vek 0\).
  3. If \(\vek\omega = \dot{\vek\varphi}\), then by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55), applied to each part separately, the change of \(\vek\varphi\) on each part would equal the integral of \(\vek\omega\) over that part. By step 2 the changes over the four parts add up to \(\vek 0\), so \(\vek\varphi(\mat R_{\text{end}}) = \vek\varphi(\mat I)\). By the assumption of different triples, \(\mat R_{\text{end}} = \mat I\).
  4. The end is \(\mat R_{\text{end}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},-90^\circ}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{x},-90^\circ}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq \mat I\). The point \((0{,}1;\ 0;\ 0)\) m of the end effector goes to \((0;\ 0;\ -0{,}1)\) m, \(0{,}1414\) m from the start. This is a contradiction.

Source: SPONG (PDF p. 133 and 162): "this velocity vector is not the derivative of a position variable, since the angular velocity vector is not the derivative of any particular time-varying quantity" (stated without proof). The proof and the computation are ours, the numbers are from num-06.out N2.

What the lemma says matters for the following sections: the relation \(\vek\xi = \mat J\,\dot{\vek q}\) cannot be integrated into \(\vek\xi = \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\ldots)\), and the orientation cannot be read off from the integral of the angular velocity. For the orientation we would need three numbers, for example the Euler angles of section 27.2, and their derivatives are connected to \(\vek\omega\) by a matrix that is not the unit matrix and has no inverse at \(\sin\theta = 0\) (SPONG, PDF p. 144). At \(\theta = 0\) the same orientation matrix belongs to all pairs \(\varphi\), \(\psi\) with the same sum (section 27.2). The matrix of derivatives of such angles is called by the source the analytical Jacobian matrix (SPONG section 4.8, PDF p. 143), which we neither prove nor use here. We stay with the geometric matrix \(\mat J\) and with \(\vek\omega\) as the angular velocity of a body by definition 8.86.

We began this section with the question of how fast the end effector moves. The answer is equation (27.24) with a matrix whose column \(i\) is the velocity when joint \(i\) alone turns (theorem 27.42). The same matrix forms the basis of what follows: section 27.7 will look in it for the positions where the robot loses a direction of motion and use it for the joint forces, and section 27.8 will use the matrices \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) of corollary 27.43 for the kinetic energy.
