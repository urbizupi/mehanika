Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.1 The boundary value problem of elasticity

Take a steel tube with an inner radius of 40 mm and an outer radius of 60 mm, filled with oil at a pressure of 30 MPa (figure 11.1). The radii are the same as for the ring of section 1.18.5 and for the thick spherical shell of section 4.3.7. The tube is made of steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). Which equations and which conditions on the surface determine the field of displacements, strains and stresses in its wall? And do they determine it uniquely, or could the same data give two different fields?

First let us count the unknowns and the equations. The unknowns are the three components of displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\), the six components of the tensor of small strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the six components of stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), fifteen functions of the point of the body in all. There are just as many equations (SADD §5.1, PDF p. 103-104). Six connect the strains with the displacements (definition 1.115),

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) ,\]

where the indices \(i\), \(j\) are each equal to \(x\), \(y\) or \(z\) and \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\). Six connect the stresses with the strains, this is Hooke's law for an isotropic material (theorem 3.11),

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} ,\]

where \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is one for \(i = j\) and zero otherwise. Three are the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.28),

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 , \qquad i = x, y, z ,\]

where \(\vek b\) is the body force. Six plus six plus three is fifteen, so there are as many equations as unknowns. The compatibility equations (theorem 1.127) are not among them. Strains computed from displacements satisfy them automatically, and we need them only when the strains or the stresses are the unknowns and we look for the displacements afterwards (SADD §5.1, PDF p. 104). The constants \(E\) and \(\nu\) and the body force are given in advance.

The equations alone do not determine the problem, however. Every tube, every beam and every body has the same equations, but a different field of displacements, because it is held and loaded differently. The data on holding and loading are conditions on the surface, as in section 4.5.1. On a part of the surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) a displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) is prescribed, and on the remaining part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) a force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\) is prescribed. Chapter 4 always took the prescribed displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) to be zero. Here we may prescribe any displacement, for example a support that settles by a known distance. According to what is prescribed where, we distinguish three kinds of problems (figure 11.2, SADD §5.2, PDF p. 108-109). In a traction problem (problem sil) the force is prescribed on the whole surface and \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty. In a displacement problem (problem pomikov) the displacement is prescribed on the whole surface and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is empty. In a mixed problem (mešani problem) both are nonempty. The tube of figure 11.1 is a traction problem. There is one more kind of condition, which we only name: at the same point a component of displacement in one direction and a component of force in another are prescribed, for example for a body sliding along a smooth rigid wall, or on a plane of symmetry (SADD §5.2, PDF p. 104). We will not use it.

Now we can write everything said so far as one problem. The data about the body are the same as in section 4.5.1, so here we only list them.

Definition 11.1 Boundary value problem of elasticity (robni problem elastičnosti)

Let the body \(V\) be as in section 4.5.1: a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) or a body cut into pieces between two graphs and curved boxes, its interior points form a connected open set (definition 1.130), every point of the body is the limit of a sequence of interior points, and the surface \(S\) is the union of two parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) that do not overlap and are made of whole outer pieces of the surface. Let the material be linearly elastic, with stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}\) (definition 3.16, for an isotropic material theorem 3.11). The data are the body force \(\vek b\), continuous on \(V\), the prescribed force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\), continuous on each outer face of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the prescribed displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\), a field continuously differentiable (definition 1.90) on an open set that contains \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). A displacement field \(\vek u\) is a solution of the boundary value problem of elasticity (rešitev robnega problema elastičnosti) for the data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\) if:

  1. \(\vek u\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface, and at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) it equals \(\bar{\vek u}\),
  2. the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), computed from the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the tensor of small strains of the field \(\vek u\), are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface, and at every interior point of the body they satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31),
  3. at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) holds, where \(\vek n\) is the outer unit normal of the surface.

If \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty this is a traction problem, if \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is empty a displacement problem, if both are nonempty a mixed problem.

Source: SADD §5.2, (5.2.1) and (5.2.2) (PDF p. 108-109). The requirements of continuous differentiability are ours, so that the proved principles of chapter 4 apply.

For \(\bar{\vek u} = \vek 0\) we already had this definition (definition 4.132). Only the value of \(\bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is new.

May we prescribe any load we like? If we push the tube of figure 11.1 at one end with a force of 100 kN and do not hold it at the other, it starts to move, and the equilibrium equations have no solution. The condition that excludes this is in fact a consequence of the equations.

Lemma 11.2 Equilibrium of the whole body (ravnovesje celega telesa)

Let \(\vek u\) be a solution of the boundary value problem of elasticity (definition 11.1) and \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) the stress vector on the surface \(S\) (on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) this is \(\bar{\vek t}\)). Let \(\vek r\) be the position vector of a point from a chosen fixed point \(O\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0 , \qquad \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek r\times\vek t\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek r\times\vek b\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0 . \tag{11.1}\]
Proof

We must show six equalities, three for the force and three for the moment. We obtain each by multiplying an equilibrium equation by a suitable function, integrating over the body and using the divergence theorem.

  1. The stresses of the solution are symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) (lemma 3.17, point 2, as in the text after definition 4.132). At every interior point \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) holds (requirement 2). Both sides are continuous on \(V\) and every point of the body is a limit of interior points, so this equation holds at every point of the body.
  2. The field \(\vek v = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}})\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(V\), and by step 1 its divergence equals \(-b_{\mathrm{x}}\). By the divergence theorem 4.14 for bodies made of boxes, or by corollary 4.68 for other bodies, it follows that \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek v\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  3. By symmetry \(\vek v\cdot\vek n = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = t_{\mathrm{x}}\), because \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) (Cauchy's formula, theorem 2.24), hence \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{V}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\). For the components \(y\) and \(z\) we repeat steps 2 and 3 with the fields \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}})\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\). This is the first equality.
  4. The component \(x\) of the moment is \((\vek r\times\vek t)_{\mathrm{x}} = y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}}\) (equation (2.32)). Take the field \(\vek v\) with components \(v_{\mathrm{j}} = y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{jz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{jy}}\) for \(j = x, y, z\). It is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(V\), and by symmetry \(\vek v\cdot\vek n = y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  5. By the product rule its divergence is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial v_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = y\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{jz}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} - z\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{jy}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), because \(\partial y/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) equals one for \(j = y\) and zero otherwise, and likewise for \(z\). By step 1 the two sums are \(-b_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(-b_{\mathrm{y}}\), and by symmetry \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) cancel. What remains is \(-(y\,b_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,b_{\mathrm{y}})\).
  6. The divergence theorem as in step 2 gives \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} (y\,b_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,b_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} (y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A\), which is the component \(x\) of the second equality. The components \(y\) and \(z\) follow by exchanging the letters in the cycle \(x \to y \to z \to x\), as for the vector product.

Source: SADD §3.7, (3.7.1)-(3.7.3) (PDF p. 77-78), where the argument is reversed: from the equilibrium of every part the equilibrium equations follow. The condition on the data of the traction problem is not stated in SADD §5.2. The proof with the divergence theorem is not from the source. The force for a body made of boxes was already proved by corollary 4.41.

The lemma says that the data of a traction problem may not be chosen arbitrarily. When \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty, \(S = S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and the data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t})\) must satisfy both equalities by themselves. In a mixed problem they satisfy them only together with the forces of the supports on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), which we do not know in advance. Let us check this on three bodies.

The tube of figure 11.1 is a traction problem. There is no body force. The pressure in the bore acts on the wall equally from all sides, so the forces on opposite sides of the bore cancel and the resultant of the pressure is zero, and so is its moment about a point on the axis. If the ends of the tube are free there is no other load and both equalities hold. If a plug closes each end of the tube, the plug receives from the oil in the bore of radius \(a = 40\) mm the force \(p\pi a^2 = 150{,}8\) kN and passes it on to the tube. Let this force be distributed uniformly over the annulus of the end face with area \(\pi(b^2 - a^2)\), where \(b = 60\) mm is the outer radius. The force per unit area is then \(p a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 24\) MPa. The lower annulus pulls the tube downwards, the upper one upwards, each with the force \(24\ \text{MPa} \cdot 6{,}283 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2 = 150{,}8\) kN, and the sum of the forces is zero. The moment about the axis is zero because the force on an annulus is distributed uniformly along its circumference, and about a point on the axis it is zero because both forces lie on the axis and have equal magnitude and opposite direction. The plug is an idealization here: we do not compute its field, we only prescribe the force it passes on. How a real plug would change the field near the ends we will say later in this chapter.

The hanging bar of chapter 1, 10 m long with a diameter of 10 mm, is also a traction problem. The body force is the weight \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), where the axis \(x\) points downwards, and its resultant is \(\rho g A L = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot 10 = 60{,}10\) N. The top face is pulled upwards uniformly by the force per unit area \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa, which on the area \(A = 78{,}54\) mm\(^2\) gives exactly 60,10 N. The lateral surface and the bottom face are free. The weight and the force on the top face cancel. The moment about the centre of the top face is zero, because the weight and the force on the top face are distributed symmetrically about the axis.

The cantilever of chapter 5 is a mixed problem. The face of the clamp is \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the displacement there is zero, the remaining faces are \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and at the end a force of 500 N acts downwards. The data on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) do not balance by themselves: a force of 500 N and its moment of 500 N m about the clamp remain, because the arm is 1 m. The lemma says that the clamp must supply a force of 500 N upwards and a moment of 500 N m that cancel them. We did not prescribe these forces, the solution must supply them itself.

Now the question we posed at the tube: is the field only one? Let us see what would happen if there were two. Their difference would be a displacement field without load and without prescribed displacement, and chapter 4 showed that such a field has no strain. For this we first need that solutions may be subtracted.

Corollary 11.3 Superposition for boundary value problems (seštevanje rešitev robnih problemov)

Let \(\vek u^{(1)}\) be a solution of the boundary value problem of elasticity for the data \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}, \bar{\vek u}^{(1)})\) and \(\vek u^{(2)}\) a solution for the data \((\vek b^{(2)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(2)}, \bar{\vek u}^{(2)})\) of the same body with the same parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and let \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) be numbers. Then \(\alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) is a solution for the data \((\alpha\,\vek b^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek b^{(2)},\ \alpha\,\bar{\vek t}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek t}^{(2)},\ \alpha\,\bar{\vek u}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek u}^{(2)})\), and its strains and stresses are the same linear combinations.

Proof

We must show the three requirements of definition 11.1. The only difference from lemma 4.134 is the value of the field on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\).

  1. By lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, the field \(\alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) is continuously differentiable on the intersection of the two open sets that contain the body together with its surface, and this intersection is an open set with the same property. On \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) the field equals \(\alpha\,\bar{\vek u}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek u}^{(2)}\). This is requirement 1.
  2. Steps 2, 3 and 4 of the proof of lemma 4.134 on the principle of superposition show that the strains and stresses of the field \(\alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) are the same linear combinations, that the stresses are continuously differentiable and that they satisfy the equilibrium equations with the body force \(\alpha\,\vek b^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek b^{(2)}\). These steps use only the rules for derivatives and the linearity of the elastic constants, not the value of the field on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). This is requirement 2.
  3. Step 5 of the same lemma gives on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the equality \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \alpha\,\bar{\vek t}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\) and does not use the field on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) either. This is requirement 3.

Source: EP §4.6.1, (4.6.1)-(4.6.4) (p. 218), where prescribed displacements are also part of the data. The proof is an adaptation of lemma 4.134, not from the source.

Theorem 11.4 Uniqueness of the boundary value problem (enoličnost robnega problema)

Let the body be as in definition 11.1 and the material ideally elastic (definition 3.43) with a strain energy density that is positive for every small strain that is not zero (for an isotropic material theorem 3.48 on the bounds on the elastic constants guarantees this). Let \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be solutions of the boundary value problem for the same data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\). Then:

  1. the strains and stresses of the two solutions are equal at every point of the body,
  2. the difference \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a small rigid displacement (definition 1.11) that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), and for every such displacement \(\vek w\) the field \(\vek u + \vek w\) is also a solution,
  3. if the only small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the field \(\vek 0\), then \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\).
Proof

We must show that the difference of the two solutions solves the problem with zero data, and apply the lemma of chapter 4 about such a problem.

  1. By corollary 11.3 with \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\beta = -1\) the field \(\vek w = \tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a solution for the data \((\vek 0, \vek 0, \bar{\vek u} - \bar{\vek u}) = (\vek 0, \vek 0, \vek 0)\). Its strains and stresses are the differences of the strains and stresses of the fields \(\tilde{\vek u}\) and \(\vek u\).
  2. The field \(\vek 0\) is also a solution for the data \((\vek 0, \vek 0, \vek 0)\): its strains and stresses are zero, the equilibrium equations are \(0 + 0 = 0\), and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\).
  3. By lemma 4.133 (valid for bodies made of curved boxes by the remark at the end of section 4.5.4), applied to the solutions \(\vek w\) and \(\vek 0\) for the same load \((\vek 0, \vek 0)\), the strains and stresses of the field \(\vek w\) equal those of the field \(\vek 0\), so they are zero, and \(\vek w\) is a small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\).
  4. The strains of the field \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u + \vek w\) are the sum of the strains of the fields \(\vek u\) and \(\vek w\), so they equal the strains of the field \(\vek u\), and the stresses are equal by Hooke's law. This is point 1. The first part of point 2 is step 3.
  5. Let \(\vek w\) be a small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). The field \(\vek w = \vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\) is a polynomial in the coordinates, hence continuously differentiable, and its tensor of small strains is \(\tfrac12(\mat W + \mat W^\T) = \mat 0\), because \(\mat W\) is antisymmetric. The stresses are zero by Hooke's law, the equilibrium equations are \(0 + 0 = 0\) and the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is \(\vek 0 = \vek 0\), so \(\vek w\) is a solution for zero data. By corollary 11.3, \(\vek u + \vek w\) is therefore a solution for the data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u} + \vek 0)\). This is the second part of point 2.
  6. If the only small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the field \(\vek 0\), then by step 3 \(\vek w = \vek 0\), so \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\). This is point 3.

Source: SADD §6.2, (6.2.1)-(6.2.5) (PDF p. 133), CM §7.3.6, (7.3.38)-(7.3.45) (PDF p. 313-314), BARBER §33.8 (PDF p. 515-516), where the difference of the solutions is inserted into the strain energy. The reduction to lemma 4.133 is ours.

The proof showed that no new energy estimate is needed: the difference of two solutions solves the problem with zero data, and that has no strain, which we proved in chapter 4. Let us see what the theorem says about the bodies.

For the cantilever, \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the face of the clamp, on which the displacement is zero. A small rigid displacement \(\vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\) is zero at three points \(P_1\), \(P_2\), \(P_3\) of the face that do not lie on one line only if \(\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W = \mat 0\). We show this as follows. From \(\vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{i}}} = \vek 0\) for \(i = 1, 2, 3\) it follows by subtraction that \(\mat W\,\vek d_2 = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W\,\vek d_3 = \vek 0\), where \(\vek d_2 = \overrightarrow{P_1P_2}\) and \(\vek d_3 = \overrightarrow{P_1P_3}\) are independent vectors. Let \(\vek d_1\) be a vector that is not in their plane. Since \(\mat W\) is antisymmetric, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\mat W\,\vek d_1 = -(\mat W\,\vek d_{\mathrm{j}})\cdot\vek d_1 = 0\) for \(j = 2, 3\) and \(\vek d_1\cdot\mat W\,\vek d_1 = 0\) (corollary 1.104, point 1). The vector \(\mat W\,\vek d_1\) is therefore perpendicular to the three independent vectors \(\vek d_1\), \(\vek d_2\), \(\vek d_3\), which span space, so it is perpendicular to every vector, itself included, and it is \(\vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat W\) maps three independent vectors to zero, so it is the zero matrix, and \(\vek a = \vek 0\). The cantilever therefore satisfies point 3 and the field of displacements is unique. The same holds for a beam on three supports, which is also a mixed problem: each support is a piece of a flat surface with an area, which contains three points not on one line. The equilibrium equations of the whole beam do not determine the three support forces, because there are more unknowns than equations, but the elastic field is nevertheless unique, and with it the support forces.

For the tube, for the hanging bar and for every traction problem \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty. All small rigid displacements are then allowed, point 3 does not apply, point 2 does: the stresses and strains are unique, but the displacements only up to a small rigid displacement (figure 11.3). The tube can move and turn as a whole, and every such displacement adds a new solution to a solution. The hanging bar is fixed only at the centre \(O\) of the top face. The condition \(\vek u(O) = \vek 0\) removes the translation but not the rotation about \(O\), so \(\mat W = \mat 0\) must also be required (CM, PDF p. 306).

And existence? The theorem says that there cannot be two solutions, not that there is at least one. A proof of existence in general is beyond this book, because it requires Sobolev spaces and the Lebesgue integral, which we only named in section 6.2.3, and theorems of functional analysis. The sources say the following. BOWER §5.1.4 (PDF p. 279) states that the boundary value problem with displacements prescribed on the whole surface always has a solution and that it is unique, that with mixed conditions a static solution exists and is unique if the prescribed displacements prevent rigid motion, and that with tractions only a solution exists only if the tractions are in equilibrium, which is just our lemma 11.2. BARBER §33.8 (PDF p. 516), after the proof of uniqueness, says that the question of existence is much more challenging and that he will not pursue it, and cites Sokolnikoff, according to whom existence has been satisfactorily resolved for domains of great generality. BATHE §4.3.4 (PDF p. 253-255) shows what stands behind this: the bilinear form of the problem (definition 6.4) has two properties on the space of functions that are zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and have integrable squares of themselves and of their first derivatives. Continuity says that the value of the form for two fields is bounded above by the product of their sizes, ellipticity says that the value of the form for one field in both arguments is at least a constant multiple of the square of its size. Ellipticity holds because the structure is properly supported, and for the proof BATHE refers to Ciarlet. That existence follows from these properties is a theorem of functional analysis, which BATHE does not summarize on these pages, so we do not prove existence here and do not claim it for every body. In this chapter every solution is constructed: we find a field that satisfies all three requirements of definition 11.1, and by theorem 11.4 it is the solution. Where we would really need general existence, we will say so as a conjecture.

From history

The proof that the equations for the equilibrium of an elastic body have no more than one solution was first published by Kirchhoff in a memoir of 1858 (TP2B, PDF p. 18). Neumann repeated it in the tenth section of his Vorlesungen, which is entitled Kirchhoffs allgemeine Lehrsätze (Kirchhoff's general theorems). Paragraph 60 reproduces Kirchhoff's proof for equilibrium, and the section stems from lectures of 1859 and 1860 and is therefore older than Clebsch's textbook (TP2B, PDF p. 18). In §61 Neumann extended the proof to the vibration of a body. TP2B thinks that neither Kirchhoff nor Clebsch had done this before him (TP2B, PDF p. 18). Neumann assumes an isotropic material, takes two solutions and finds that the difference of their displacements \(U\), \(V\), \(W\) satisfies the equations without body force and surface force. He considers the quadruple integral (over the body and over time) of a sum of three terms: the equation of motion of the difference in the direction of each axis, multiplied by the time derivative of the displacement in that direction. The integral is zero because of the equations in the interior of the body. He integrates the stress terms by parts, the surface integrals vanish because of the conditions on the surface, and after inserting the relation between stresses and strains the whole is a complete differential with respect to time. After integrating over time he obtains the equality that the sum of two integrals of squared terms (squares of the time derivatives of the displacements and squared terms of the strain energy) equals zero. Hence every squared term must be zero at every point of the body, the displacements do not depend on time and can express only a translation and a rotation of the body as a whole (TP2B, PDF p. 18-19).

We were looking at whether the equations and the conditions on the surface determine the field uniquely. The conclusion that two solutions can differ only by a translation and a rotation of the body as a whole is, at rest, exactly point 2 of our theorem: the stresses and strains are unique, and the displacements up to a small rigid displacement, which the supports remove if they prevent it.

For the tube of figure 11.1 this means the following. The tube with open ends and the tube with ends closed by a plug are two different traction problems, because the data at the ends differ, and each has a single field of stresses and strains. How to find this field we will see later in this chapter. For now we know that it is enough to find one field that satisfies all three requirements of the definition, because that field is the one. We use exactly this in every solution of the chapter.
