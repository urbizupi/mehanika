Chapter 20 Damping models

20.3 Hysteretic damping

In section 20.1 we measured the loss in one period by the area of the loop of the damping force against displacement (figure 9.83), and in section 20.2 we replaced every mechanism by a viscous damper that loses the same energy. The viscous damper has a property that often fails in built structures: its loss in a period, \(\pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\), at the same amplitude \(X\) grows in proportion to the frequency \(\Omega\) (theorem 9.73). GR (§3.2.2, PDF p. 181, footnote 3) mentions structures with joints, where the mechanical joints cause dry friction and damping forces that do not grow linearly with frequency in harmonic motion. For such friction the reason is simple, and the rectangular loop of figure 20.11 shows it: the force is constant, \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the path covered in a period is \(4X\) (twice \(2X\), there and back), so the work is \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\) however fast the motion goes. For cantilever \(A\) with \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N and \(X = 13{,}914\) mm this area is \(0{,}1113\) J at every frequency (computation in num-03.py). We ask whether there is a linear model of damping whose loss in a period does not depend on frequency.

From history

CK (§4.8, PDF p. 129) calls such damping structural and writes that it is often used in structural dynamics, for example in studies of aircraft vibration and flutter, and that Theodorsen and Garrick used it in an early study of flutter. CK introduces it with the notation of the complex frequency response of his section 4.3 and says that this notation is especially suitable for this damping.

Let us return to the question that opened the section. We draw the loop that the point \((q, f_{\mathrm{D}})\) runs through in a period, as before, for cantilever \(A\) at amplitude \(X = 15{,}944\) mm (figure 20.21). We hold the amplitude constant so that only the loops are compared, although the driving force would have to be adjusted at every frequency.

First the axes: displacement \(q\) horizontally, force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\) vertically, on the horizontal axis the limit displacements \(-X\) and \(X\). The viscous damper of cantilever \(A\) (\(m = 50\) kg, \(k = 62\,720\) N/m) with \(\zeta = 0{,}01\), that is \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km} = 35{,}42\) N s/m, at resonance, \(r = 1\), gives an ellipse of height \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X = 20\) N and area \(\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2 = 1{,}00\) J, which is the energy lost in a period (theorem 9.73). If we halve the frequency, the amplitude stays the same and the velocity is half, so the damper force is half and the ellipse is lower, \(10\) N, and its area is \(0{,}50\) J. If we double the frequency, the ellipse is \(40\) N high and its area is \(2{,}00\) J. The point \((q, f_{\mathrm{D}})\) runs along them counterclockwise. The area grows with frequency because the force of a viscous damper is proportional to velocity, and its amplitude \(c\,\Omega X\) is therefore proportional to \(\Omega\).

We look for a different mechanism. On the right are the axes and a loop of the same height at every frequency. The height of the loop is the amplitude of the damping force. Since we want a linear model, let it be proportional to the displacement amplitude \(X\), that is \(\gamma k X\). The number \(\gamma\) has no unit, and the stiffness \(k\) makes the result a force. For \(A\) we take \(\gamma = 0{,}02\), which gives \(\gamma k X = 20\) N, the same as for the viscous damper at resonance. The loop is an ellipse with semi-axes \(X\) and \(\gamma k X\), its area is \(\pi\,X\cdot\gamma k X = \pi\gamma k X^2 = 1{,}00\) J at every frequency, and it is run counterclockwise as well. We call the loop hysteretic because the forces at the same displacement on the way there and on the way back differ. At the displacement \(q = X/2\) the force is \(-17{,}32\) N on the way there and \(17{,}32\) N on the way back, that is \(\pm\gamma k X\sqrt{1 - \tfrac14} = \pm 20\cdot 0{,}8660\) N, because the damping force always points against the velocity. The force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\) lags behind the displacement by a quarter period (the Greek word hysterein means to lag behind). CK (PDF p. 127, figure 4.17) calls the loop of the total force of a spring and a viscous damper a "hysteresis loop" and says that it is characteristic of forces that dissipate energy. So the left loops are hysteretic too, but their height depends on frequency. The name hysteretic damping stayed for the model in which the loop height does not depend on frequency (GR, PDF p. 180: the damping force is independent of frequency, proportional to amplitude and in phase with velocity, where GR counts the force acting on the damper, that is \(-f_{\mathrm{D}}\)).

Proportionality to the amplitude is an assumption, so let us say what it means, why we take it and when it fails. It means that at half the amplitude, \(X/2\), the loop is half as high, \(10\) N, and has a quarter of the area, \(0{,}25\) J, because both semi-axes are halved. We take it because only then does the equation of motion stay linear: twice the driving force gives twice the displacement and a loop of the same shape. It fails for dry friction, where the height of the loop, \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\), is the same at every amplitude, so at half the amplitude the loss is halved and does not drop to a quarter. One \(\gamma\) fits such a joint only at one amplitude, which the end of the section shows with numbers. Whether the assumption holds for a given structure is told by measuring the loss in a period at two amplitudes: at twice the amplitude the loss must be four times larger, as for the parabola \(\pi c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2\), and not twice, as for the friction line \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\).

What damper gives this loop (right in figure 20.21)? A viscous damper with coefficient \(c\) has at amplitude \(X\) the loop height \(c\,\Omega X\). Let this be \(\gamma k X\), so \(c = \gamma k/\Omega\). The coefficient is different at every frequency, as the left loops are different at every frequency, so the model holds only for steady motion with a single frequency. We write this down.

Definition 20.12 Hysteretic damping (histerezno dušenje)

Let \(m > 0\) and \(k > 0\) be real numbers and \(\gamma \ge 0\) a number without unit (the loss factor, as lemma 20.13 shows). Hysteretic damping with loss factor \(\gamma\) is a rule for the system \(m\ddot q + k q = Q\), valid only for steady harmonic motion with angular frequency \(\Omega > 0\): besides the spring, a force acts on the mass

\[f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c_{\mathrm{h}}(\Omega)\,\dot q , \qquad c_{\mathrm{h}}(\Omega) = \frac{\gamma k}{\Omega} ,\]

that is, a viscous damper (definition 9.1) with a coefficient for which the amplitude of the damping force \(c_{\mathrm{h}}\,\Omega X = \gamma k X\) is proportional to the displacement amplitude \(X\) and independent of \(\Omega\). The equation of motion at frequency \(\Omega\) is

\[m\ddot q + \frac{\gamma k}{\Omega}\,\dot q + k q = Q_0\cos\Omega t . \tag{20.10}\]

The complex number \(\hat k = k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) is the complex stiffness: it is complex because besides the real part \(k\) it has the imaginary part \(\gamma k\), which represents damping. The model is also called structural damping.

Source: CK §4.8 (PDF p. 129): equation (4.79) with \(\gamma\) as the "structural damping factor" and the complex stiffness \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\). GR §3.2.2 (PDF pp. 180-181): "hysteretic damping", equation (3.31), damping force independent of frequency, proportional to amplitude and in phase with velocity, "also sometimes called structural damping". The notation with the coefficient \(\gamma k/\Omega\) is ours, the sources give it in complex notation. The letter \(\gamma\) here is the loss factor of section 20.1, not the shear angle.

What does this damper lose, the one that gives the right loop? The first thing we show is that \(\gamma\) really is the loss factor of section 20.1.

Lemma 20.13 Loss per period under hysteretic damping (izguba v periodi pri histereznem dušenju)

Let \(m, k > 0\), \(\gamma > 0\) and \(\Omega > 0\), and let \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) be a steady motion of the system with hysteretic damping (definition 20.12) with amplitude \(X > 0\). Then:

  1. In the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) the damping force loses the energy \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \pi\,\gamma\,k\,X^2\), whatever \(\Omega\) and \(\varphi\) are.
  2. In a period the point \((q, f_{\mathrm{D}})\) runs around an ellipse with semi-axes \(X\) and \(\gamma k X\), and its area is \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\).
  3. Let \(U = \tfrac12 k X^2\) be the largest energy of the spring. Then \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}/(2\pi U) = \gamma\), which is the loss factor of definition 20.3.
Proof

We must show the loss, the shape of the loop and the ratio. At a chosen frequency the system is a viscous damper with \(c = c_{\mathrm{h}}(\Omega)\), so we use the theorems for the viscous damper.

  1. The coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{h}} = \gamma k/\Omega\) is real and positive, the imaginary part of the denominator \(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c_{\mathrm{h}}\Omega = k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\gamma k\) is \(\gamma k > 0\), so it is different from zero. The assumptions of theorem 9.73 hold.
  2. By theorem 9.73 the loss in a period is \(\pi\,c_{\mathrm{h}}\,\Omega X^2 = \pi\,\dfrac{\gamma k}{\Omega}\,\Omega X^2 = \pi\gamma k X^2\). The frequency cancels, \(\varphi\) does not occur in the expression. This is point 1.
  3. The damping force is \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c_{\mathrm{h}}\dot q = c_{\mathrm{h}}\Omega X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi) = \gamma k X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\) (theorem 8.13). From \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) (lemma 8.11, point 1) we get \((q/X)^2 + (f_{\mathrm{D}}/(\gamma k X))^2 = 1\), which is an ellipse with semi-axes \(X\) and \(\gamma k X\). Its area is \(\pi X\cdot\gamma k X = \pi\gamma k X^2\), as in the text before figure 9.83. This is point 2.
  4. Dividing point 1 by \(2\pi U = \pi k X^2\) gives \(\gamma\). This is point 3.

Source: The proof is not from a source. CK (4.75) and figure 4.17 (PDF p. 127) connect the loss in a period with the area of the loop, CK (4.83) (PDF p. 129) sets \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma/2\).

The lemma says that damping with loss factor \(\gamma\), as the right loop in figure 20.21 shows, is described equally well at every frequency by the number \(\gamma\), and that this is exactly the number that section 20.1 computes from the loop. The same holds in the complex-stiffness notation: when we write steady motion as \(z = Z\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), we have \(\dot z = \mathrm{i}\Omega z\), so the term \(\dfrac{\gamma k}{\Omega}\dot z\) equals \(\mathrm{i}\gamma k\,z\) and together with the spring gives \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)z\). How large the displacement is and when it lags behind the force is told by the next theorem. We read it from one picture. The number that multiplies \(z\) in this notation is \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) - m\Omega^2\), because \(m\ddot z = -m\Omega^2 z\). We divide it by \(k\) and use \(m\Omega^2 = k r^2\) with \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), which gives \((1 - r^2) + \mathrm{i}\gamma\). We draw this number as a point in the plane (figure 20.22), for \(\gamma = 0{,}4\) so that everything is easy to see.

Let us draw the axes: the real part horizontally, the imaginary part vertically. The real part \(1 - r^2\) comes from the spring and the mass, the imaginary part \(\gamma\) from the damping. At \(r = 0{,}5\) the real part is \(1 - r^2 = 0{,}75\) and the imaginary part is \(\gamma = 0{,}4\), and the arrow from the origin to the point has, by the Pythagorean theorem, the length \(\sqrt{0{,}75^2 + 0{,}4^2} = 0{,}85\). The amplitude is \(Q_0/k\) divided by this length, so here it is \(1/0{,}85 = 1{,}176\) times the static displacement. The angle of the arrow to the horizontal axis, \(28{,}1^\circ\), is the phase lag \(\varphi\): its cosine is the ratio of the real part to the length, its sine the ratio of the imaginary part to the length (theorem 9.62). As the frequency grows, \(1 - r^2\) gets smaller while \(\gamma\) stays the same, so the point slides to the left along the horizontal line at height \(\gamma\). It starts at \(r = 0\) with the angle \(\arctan 0{,}4 = 21{,}8^\circ\). At \(r = 1\) it is exactly above the origin, where the arrow is shortest, of length \(\gamma = 0{,}4\), and vertical, \(\varphi = 90^\circ\). At \(r = 1{,}5\) it is to the left of the origin, the arrow has length \(1{,}312\) and the angle is \(162{,}3^\circ\). The shortest arrow means the largest amplitude, so the peak is exactly at \(r = 1\) and its height is \(1/\gamma = 2{,}5\). The point of viscous damping with \(\zeta = \gamma/2 = 0{,}2\) has the imaginary part \(2\zeta r\), which grows with \(r\), so it moves along the blue curve: at \(r = 0\) it starts on the horizontal axis, at \(r = 0{,}5\) it is at height \(0{,}2\), at \(r = 1\) it meets the hysteretic point at height \(0{,}4\), and at \(r = 1{,}5\) it is higher, at \(0{,}6\). The theorem says this in equations.

Theorem 20.14 Response of a system with hysteretic damping (odziv sistema s histereznim dušenjem)

Let \(m, k > 0\), \(\gamma > 0\), \(Q_0\) a real number, \(\Omega > 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). For equation (20.10):

  1. The number \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) - m\Omega^2\) is different from zero. The steady state is \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{Re}\big(Q_0\,\hat H\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) with frequency response
    \[\hat H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) - m\Omega^2} , \tag{20.11}\]
    and the function \(z = Q_0\hat H\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) solves the complex equation
    \[m\ddot z + k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,z = Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} . \tag{20.12}\]
  2. The amplitude is \(X = \dfrac{Q_0}{k}\,D_{\mathrm{h}}(r)\) with
    \[D_{\mathrm{h}}(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2}} . \tag{20.13}\]
    The phase lag \(\varphi\in(0, \pi)\) is determined by \(\tan\varphi = \gamma/(1 - r^2)\) for \(r \ne 1\) and is \(\pi/2\) at \(r = 1\). As \(r \to 0\), \(\varphi\) tends to \(\arctan\gamma > 0\).
  3. For every \(r \ge 0\), \(D_{\mathrm{h}}(r) \le 1/\gamma\), with equality exactly at \(r = 1\).
  4. At \(r = 1\), \(D_{\mathrm{h}}(1) = 1/\gamma = D(1)\) and \(\varphi = \pi/2\) for a viscous system with \(\zeta = \gamma/2\) (definition 9.64). For \(r \ne 1\) the denominators differ: for the viscous one the damping term is \((2\zeta r)^2\), for the hysteretic one \(\gamma^2 = (2\zeta)^2\).
Proof

We must show that for equation (20.10) theorem 9.62 holds with \(c = c_{\mathrm{h}}(\Omega)\), and then read off the amplitude, the lag and the largest value.

  1. The coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{h}} = \gamma k/\Omega \ge 0\) is real and \(c_{\mathrm{h}}\Omega = \gamma k\). The characteristic polynomial \(P(s) = ms^2 + c_{\mathrm{h}} s + k\) has \(P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) = k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,\gamma k = k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) - m\Omega^2\), its imaginary part \(\gamma k\) is positive, so \(P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) \ne 0\). The assumptions of theorem 9.62 hold, and it gives \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{Re}(Q_0 H\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) with \(H = 1/P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) = \hat H\).
  2. For \(z = Q_0\hat H\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), by theorem 9.34 \(\ddot z = -\Omega^2 z\), so \(m\ddot z + k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)z = \big(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) - m\Omega^2\big)Q_0\hat H\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = Q_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), because \(P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)\hat H = 1\). This proves point 1.
  3. From \(m\Omega^2 = k r^2\) we get \(|P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)|^2 = (k - m\Omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2k^2 = k^2\big((1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2\big)\), which is \(k^2\) times the square of the length of the arrow with legs \(1 - r^2\) and \(\gamma\). By theorem 9.62, \(X = Q_0/|P|\), which is \((Q_0/k)D_{\mathrm{h}}\).
  4. By the same theorem \(\cos\varphi = (k - m\Omega^2)/|P|\) and \(\sin\varphi = c_{\mathrm{h}}\Omega/|P| = \gamma k/|P| > 0\), so \(\varphi \in (0, \pi)\): the angle of the arrow is above the horizontal axis, because the point is always above it. Division gives \(\tan\varphi = \gamma k/(k - m\Omega^2) = \gamma/(1 - r^2)\) for \(r \ne 1\). At \(r = 1\), \(\cos\varphi = 0\) and \(\sin\varphi > 0\), so \(\varphi = \pi/2\). As \(r \to 0\), \(\cos\varphi \to 1/\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}\) and \(\sin\varphi \to \gamma/\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}\), so \(\tan\varphi \to \gamma\). This proves point 2.
  5. The expression \((1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2\) is the sum of a square and \(\gamma^2\), so it is \(\ge \gamma^2\) with equality exactly at \(1 - r^2 = 0\), that is at \(r = 1\) for \(r \ge 0\). The function \(x \mapsto x^{-1/2}\) is strictly decreasing on \(x > 0\), so \(D_{\mathrm{h}} \le 1/\gamma\) with equality exactly at \(r = 1\). In the figure this is the point of the line nearest to the origin, the one above it. This is point 3.
  6. For \(\zeta = \gamma/2\), \(D(1) = 1/(2\zeta) = 1/\gamma\) by definition 9.64, and \(\varphi = \pi/2\) by theorem 9.66 (point 3). The denominators of equations (9.66) and (20.13) are \((1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2\) and \((1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta)^2\), which agree exactly at \(r = 1\) (or \(\zeta = 0\)), where the blue curve and the line cross. This is point 4.

Source: CK (4.81)-(4.85) and (4.83) (PDF p. 129): \(\bar H_0 = 1/((1 - r^2) + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\), amplitude \(1/[(1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2]^{1/2}\), \(\tan\alpha = \gamma/(1 - r^2)\), \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma/2\) (CK sets \(\bar U/U_0 = -\mathrm{i}/\gamma\) at \(r = 1\), PDF p. 130). The proof by steps from theorem 9.62 is ours.

The reason: at \(r = 0{,}9\), \((1 - r^2)^2 = 0{,}0361\), while the damping terms are only \((2\zeta r)^2 = 0{,}000324\) and \(\gamma^2 = 0{,}0004\), which is \(0{,}9\ \%\) and \(1{,}1\ \%\) of the first, and their difference \(0{,}000076\) is \(0{,}2\ \%\) of the denominator. The substitution \(\zeta = \gamma/2\) is therefore an approximation for light damping. Far from resonance both damping terms are small compared with \((1 - r^2)^2\), as at \(r = 0{,}9\) above: the arrow is long there mainly because of the real part. What happens when the damping or \(r\) is large is shown by machine \(B\).

Machine \(B\) of chapter 9 stands on pads that transmit force to the ground (figure 9.94). A pad is a spring and a damper, so the force it transmits to the ground is \(f_{\mathrm{t}} = k q + c\,\dot q\) (definition 9.77). For a hysteretically damped pad the damper is the one of definition 20.12.

Corollary 20.16 Transmissibility with hysteretic damping (prenosnost s histereznim dušenjem)

Let \(\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}} = F_{\mathrm{t}}/Q_0\) be the transmissibility (definition 9.77) of a system with hysteretic damping at frequency \(\Omega\), under the conditions of theorem 20.14 and with \(Q_0 > 0\). Then

\[\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}\,D_{\mathrm{h}}(r) , \tag{20.14}\]

\(\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}} = 1\) at \(r = \sqrt2\) for every \(\gamma\), \(\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}} < 1\) exactly for \(r > \sqrt2\), for \(r > \sqrt2\) \(\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}}\) strictly increases with increasing \(\gamma\), for \(0 < r < \sqrt2\) it strictly decreases.

Proof

We must show the form (20.14), and take the rest over from theorem 9.78.

  1. At frequency \(\Omega\) the system is viscous with \(c = c_{\mathrm{h}}\), its damping ratio is \(\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} = c_{\mathrm{h}}/(2\sqrt{km}) = \gamma k/(2\Omega\sqrt{km})\). From \(\Omega = r\sqrt{k/m}\), \(\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} = \gamma/(2r)\), so \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} r = \gamma\).
  2. Theorem 9.78 gives \(\mathrm{TR} = \sqrt{1 + (2\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} r)^2}\,D(r)\) with \(D = 1/\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} r)^2}\). With \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} r = \gamma\) this is (20.14).
  3. Theorem 9.78 also says: \(\mathrm{TR} = 1\) at \(r = \sqrt2\) for every damping ratio, \(\mathrm{TR} < 1\) exactly for \(r > \sqrt2\), for \(r > \sqrt2\) it grows with the damping ratio and for \(0 < r < \sqrt2\) it falls. At a given \(r\), \(\zeta_{\mathrm{h}} = \gamma/(2r)\) is a strictly increasing function of \(\gamma\), so the same holds for \(\gamma\).

Source: CK (4.44) (PDF p. 115) for the viscous transmissibility. The proof that the same holds for hysteretic damping is ours, SymPy checked \(\mathrm{TR}_{\mathrm{h}}^2 - 1 = r^2(2 - r^2)/\big((1 - r^2)^2 + \gamma^2\big)\).

At \(r = 1\) the models are equal, as figure 20.23 shows. At \(r = 5\) viscous damping with \(\zeta = 0{,}2\) increases the force into the ground \(2{,}23\) times, from \(20{,}56\) N to \(45{,}82\) N, hysteretic with the same \(\gamma = 2\zeta\) only \(1{,}077\) times, to \(22{,}14\) N.

The reason is the term \(2\zeta r\) of corollary 20.16 and theorem 9.78. The force into the ground is the sum of the spring force and the damper force, which is a quarter period ahead of it, so their phasors are perpendicular and the force into the ground is the hypotenuse (figure 9.95). Since only the ratio matters, we divide every force by the spring force \(kX\) in the same motion: the spring force is the horizontal leg, of length \(1\). For the viscous damper the ratio of the damper force \(c\,\Omega X\) to the spring force \(kX\) is \(2\zeta r\), because \(c\,\Omega = 2\zeta r\,k\). At \(r = 1\) it is \(0{,}4\), at \(r = 5\) it is \(2\), five times more, so its contribution to the root in the numerator is \((2\zeta r)^2 = 4\). For hysteretic damping the ratio is \(\gamma k X/(kX) = \gamma = 0{,}4\) at every \(r\), because the loop height does not depend on frequency, and the contribution is \(\gamma^2 = 0{,}16\). The factors are the lengths of the hypotenuses, \(\sqrt{1 + 4} = 2{,}236\) and \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}16} = 1{,}077\). The denominator hardly changes, \((1 - r^2)^2 = 576\) grows to \(580\) and \(576{,}16\), so the ratios of the forces are \(2{,}228\) and \(1{,}077\).

The conclusion on isolation from chapter 9 (figure 9.94), that damping far above resonance hurts, holds here too: by corollary 20.16 hysteretic damping increases the transmissibility at every \(r > \sqrt2\). How much it hurts depends on the model. Whether the viscous or the hysteretic model is closer to the truth for a given pad is decided by a measurement: if the loss of the pad in a period at the same amplitude does not change with frequency, the hysteretic model is more suitable, if it grows in proportion to frequency, the viscous one. How the loss factor of a real material depends on frequency is shown by section 20.6: it is constant only approximately and only over a limited range of frequencies.

The computation so far held only for steady harmonic motion, into which the vibration passes after the transient (figure 9.72). Let us try to use the same notation for free vibration, when there is no force and no steady state. In complex notation the equation of free vibration is \(m\ddot z + k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)z = 0\), which is equation (20.12) without the right-hand side. Why is the model not meant for time? The term \(\mathrm{i}\gamma k z\) meant \((\gamma k/\Omega)\,\dot z\), where \(\Omega\) is the frequency with which the motion goes. In free vibration there is no force with frequency \(\Omega\) and the motion is not steady harmonic, so definition 20.12 does not apply to it and does not determine the coefficient \(\gamma k/\Omega\). Let us see what the equation itself does, first in a picture of the zeros of its characteristic polynomial for the pads of machine \(B\) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi\cdot 5 = 31{,}416\) 1/s (figure 20.25).

A zero \(s\) of the polynomial gives the motion \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\), whose size is \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{Re}(s)\,t}\) (definition 9.33): it decays if \(\mathrm{Re}\,s < 0\) and grows if \(\mathrm{Re}\,s > 0\). The half-plane where it grows we shade. On the left we draw the axes and the zeros of the viscous system with \(\zeta = 0{,}2\), the pair \(-6{,}28 \pm 30{,}78\,\mathrm{i}\) (theorem 9.48). A polynomial with real coefficients has for every zero \(s\) also \(\bar s\) as a zero, because \(P(\bar s) = \overline{P(s)}\), so the zeros are mirror images across the horizontal axis and both lie on the left, where the motion decays. On the right are the same axes for the equation with complex stiffness at \(\gamma = 0{,}4\). Its coefficient \(\mathrm{i}\gamma k\) is complex and the rule of the conjugate pair does not hold for it. The polynomial \(P(s) = s^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) contains only \(s^2\), so \(P(-s) = P(s)\) and with the zero \(s_1 = -6{,}17 + 32{,}02\,\mathrm{i}\) also \(s_2 = -s_1\) is a zero, its mirror image through the origin, which falls into the shaded half-plane. The conjugate \(\bar s_1\), however, is not a zero: from \(\bar s_1^2 = \overline{s_1^2} = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(1 - \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) we get \(P(\bar s_1) = 2\mathrm{i}\gamma\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 \ne 0\). So a growing part belongs to the decaying one. The lemma shows this by computation for every \(\gamma > 0\).

Lemma 20.18 Complex stiffness in the time domain (kompleksna togost v časovnem zapisu)

Let \(m, k > 0\), \(\gamma > 0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\). Let

\[a = \sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2} + 1}{2}} , \qquad b = \sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2} - 1}{2}} , \qquad \alpha = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} b , \quad \beta = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} a\]

and let \(z\) be a complex function with \(m\ddot z + k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)z = 0\) (the zeros of the polynomial are \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) in figure 20.25). Then:

  1. The characteristic polynomial of the equation has the zeros \(s_1 = -\alpha + \mathrm{i}\beta\) and \(s_2 = -s_1 = \alpha - \mathrm{i}\beta\), and \(\alpha > 0\).
  2. The unique solution with \(z(0) = 1\), \(\dot z(0) = 0\) is \(z = \tfrac12\big(\mathrm{e}^{s_1t} + \mathrm{e}^{s_2t}\big)\) and its real part is \(\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t} + \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}\big)\cos\beta t\).
  3. At the instants \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\pi j/\beta\) with integer \(j \ge 0\), \(\mathrm{Re}\,z(t_{\mathrm{j}}) \ge \tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}}\), so \(\mathrm{Re}\,z(t_{\mathrm{j}})\) grows beyond all bounds as \(j \to \infty\).
Proof

We must show the zeros of the polynomial, the solution with the given initial values and its growth.

  1. For \(z = \mathrm{e}^{st}\), by theorem 9.34 \(\ddot z = s^2 z\), and after dividing by \(m\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) and with \(k = m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) the characteristic polynomial is \(s^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\).
  2. By lemma 9.31 with \(d = 1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma\) (\(p = 1\), \(q = \gamma > 0\), \(|d| = \sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}\)), \(w = a + \mathrm{i}b\) is a number with \(w^2 = 1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma\), and \(-w\) is the only other one. The figure shows the plane divided by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), at \(\gamma = 0{,}4\). Multiplying by a number turns an arrow by the angle of that number and stretches it by its length (lemma 9.30), so \(w^2 = w\cdot w\) has twice the angle and the square of the length of the arrow \(w\), and the arrow \(w\) has half the angle of the arrow \(d\), \(10{,}9^\circ\) against \(21{,}8^\circ\), and the length \(\sqrt{1{,}077} = 1{,}038\) (figure 9.32). Since \((\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} w)^2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 w^2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\), the zeros of the polynomial are \(\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} w\). Multiplying by \(\mathrm{i}\) turns the arrow by a right angle (figure 9.30), so for \(+\), \(\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(a + \mathrm{i}b) = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} b + \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a = s_1\), for \(-\) it is \(s_2 = -s_1\) (at true scale in figure 20.25 \(s_1\) and \(s_2\)).
  3. For \(\gamma > 0\), \(\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2} > 1\), so \(b > 0\): the arrow \(w\) lies above the real axis, because its half angle is positive. The turn by a right angle carries this height to the left of the vertical axis, so \(\alpha = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} b > 0\) and \(s_2\) lies in the half-plane \(\mathrm{Re}\,s > 0\). This proves point 1.
  4. Each of the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2t}\) solves the equation (step 1), so their half-sum does too. We have \(z(0) = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1\) and \(\dot z(0) = \tfrac12(s_1 + s_2) = 0\). By theorem 9.41, which holds for complex coefficients, this is the only solution.
  5. By definition 9.33, \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1t} = \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}(\cos\beta t + \mathrm{i}\sin\beta t)\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2t} = \mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t - \mathrm{i}\sin\beta t)\). The real part of the half-sum is \(\tfrac12(\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t} + \mathrm{e}^{\alpha t})\cos\beta t\). This is point 2.
  6. At \(t_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\beta t_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\pi j\) and \(\cos\beta t_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) (lemma 8.11, points 2 and 4), so \(\mathrm{Re}\,z(t_{\mathrm{j}}) = \tfrac12(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}} + \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}}) \ge \tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}}\). Since \(\alpha > 0\) and \(t_{\mathrm{j}} \to \infty\), the exponential function grows beyond all bounds (lemma 9.20, point 1 with \(n = 1\): \(\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}} \ge 1 + \alpha t_{\mathrm{j}}\)). This is point 3.

The first, \(\tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{s_1t}\), decays as in a damped oscillator: its rate \(\alpha = 6{,}1656\) 1/s is close to \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = (\gamma/2)\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 6{,}2832\) 1/s (\(0{,}981\) times, and \(0{,}9998\) times for \(\gamma = 0{,}04\)). The model of hysteretic damping is therefore used only as defined: for steady harmonic motion at one frequency, in the frequency domain. If we need the response in time, we replace it by a viscous damper with coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma k/\Omega_0\) at the frequency \(\Omega_0\) that interests us (section 20.2, at \(\Omega_0 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) this is \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \gamma/2\) of theorem 20.14), or we use a material model that has causality built in: the viscoelastic model of section 20.4. There are two idealizations. The first is harmonic motion with one frequency. The second is a constant \(\gamma\): for friction in a joint the loss in a period \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\) is proportional to \(X\), not to \(X^2\), so \(\gamma\) is the same only at one amplitude, \(\gamma = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi k X)\), as the loss factors above the friction loops show, which fall with the amplitude. For \(A\) with \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N this is \(0{,}00292\) at \(X = 13{,}914\) mm and \(0{,}00146\) at \(27{,}828\) mm, so half at double the amplitude. Hysteretic damping is therefore suitable when the motion is near one amplitude and one frequency, for example the vibration of a structure at resonance, but not for start-up or decay. GR (PDF p. 181, footnote 3) says that the model is quantitatively usable in a limited range of frequencies for many kinds of real structures, among them built structures with joints with dry friction.
