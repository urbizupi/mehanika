Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.7 The deflection curve equation (enačba upogibnice)

For the cantilever and the simply supported beam we got the reactions from the equilibrium of the whole beam, the internal forces \(V\) and \(M\) from the equilibrium of parts, and the curvature from \(M = EI\,v''\). The deflection \(v\) came last. Is there another way, in which we compute the deflection directly from the load? There is, and the path is drawn in figure 5.108 for the simply supported beam: from the load at the top to the deflection at the bottom lead four steps, each one integration. We read the figure row by row in subsection 5.7.1. First let us see where we need this path: for a beam where equilibrium alone does not suffice, and such a beam is the propped cantilever (podprta konzola).

Take the same steel strip \(20 \times 40\) mm as before, of length \(L = 2\) m, with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² (figure 5.109, top, with the axes \(x\) and \(y\)). Let it be clamped (vpetje) at \(x = 0\) and held up at \(x = L\) by a roller support (pomična členkasta podpora), and let it carry the uniform load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, downward. The clamp gives two forces and a couple, the support one force, four unknown reactions in all, while there are only three equilibrium equations in the plane (definition 5.6). Because the load points vertically, the horizontal equation is satisfied if the horizontal reaction at the clamp is zero. What remains are the equations for the vertical forces and for the moments about the clamp. We remove the supports and draw in their place what they exert on the beam (middle picture): let \(A\) be the vertical reaction at the clamp, \(M_{\mathrm{v}}\) the couple at the clamp (positive if it turns counterclockwise) and \(R\) the reaction at the support, both forces upward. Equilibrium gives

\[A + R - q_0 L = 0 , \qquad M_{\mathrm{v}} + R\,L - q_0 L\,\frac{L}{2} = 0 , \tag{5.32}\]

so \(A = 2000 - R\) and \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 2000 - 2R\) (forces in N, moment in N m). Every \(R\) gives a pair that satisfies equilibrium. For \(R = 0\) this is the cantilever without a support with \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = q_0 L^2/2 = 2000\) N m. For \(R = 1000\) N we get \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), the clamp gives no couple and the two reactions are those of the simply supported beam. Which value of \(R\) is the right one, equilibrium does not say.

The deformation says it, and the bottom picture shows it: the wall holds the beam clamped, and at the end, in place of the support, a force \(R\) that we choose lifts it. We computed the two end deflections with the deflection curves of section 5.9, here we only look at them. Without the force, \(R = 0\), the end sinks by \(95{,}7\) mm below the height of the clamp. The force \(R = 1000\) N is too large: it lifts the end \(31{,}9\) mm above this height. Every newton of the force \(R\) lifts the end by the same amount, \(0{,}128\) mm, so between the two values there is exactly one for which the end stays at the height of the support. The support at \(x = L\) does just this, it prevents the end of the beam from sinking or rising, so \(v(L) = 0\). This is a condition on the deflection, so we must find the deflection from the load.

From history

Navier's equation \(E I\,\mathrm{d}^2 y/\mathrm{d}x^2 = M\) had been used since Euler's time for the deflection of cantilevers and symmetrically loaded simply supported beams (TIMO, PDF p. 83). Navier applied it to any transverse load: since the moment differs in different parts of the beam, the deflection in different parts is described by different equations (TIMO, PDF p. 83). For a beam loaded at a point \(C\), whose deflection curve is drawn in the figure, he denoted the angle that the tangent at \(C\) makes with the horizontal by \(\alpha\) and expressed the deflections of both ends with this angle. From the equality of the two deflections he obtained \(\tan\alpha\) (TIMO, PDF p. 83). He knew the deflection of a cantilever, so he could write the deflection equations for both parts of the beam (TIMO, PDF p. 83).

Navier was the first to develop a general method for statically indeterminate problems. He showed that problems are indeterminate only as long as the bodies are considered perfectly rigid. When elasticity is taken into account, equations expressing the conditions of deformation can always be added to the equations of statics, and so we get enough equations for all unknowns (TIMO, PDF p. 83). He explained this with the example of a load carried by several bars in one plane. On the left, panel (a) the bars are rigid, on the right, panel (b) elastic. If the bars are perfectly rigid, we may choose arbitrary values for the forces in all bars except two and compute the last two from statics, so the problem is indeterminate. If elasticity is taken into account, the elongations of the bars and the forces in them are functions of the two components of the displacement of the point where the bars meet. Two equations of statics then determine the displacement and from it all the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 83-84). For bending he started with a beam built in at one end and simply supported at the other (TIMO, PDF p. 84). He denoted the unknown support force at the simply supported end by \(Q\) and for each part of the beam wrote the deflection equation with a moment that depends on \(Q\). By integrating, and by requiring that the two parts have a common ordinate and tangent where they meet, he obtained the deflection, and \(Q\) from the condition that the deflection at the simply supported end is zero (TIMO, PDF p. 84-85). His calculation has no moment and shear force diagrams, which might explain why he sometimes fixed the place of the largest moment wrongly (TIMO, PDF p. 85).

Let us return to the question of the section: how we get the deflection of a beam built in at one end and simply supported at the other directly from the load, when equilibrium alone does not suffice. Navier's path is the same as ours: we express the moment with the unknown, integrate the deflection equation and determine the unknowns from the conditions at the ends and at the joints.

5.7.1 From the load to the deflection

We already know every link of the path from the load to the deflection, each one separately, and each is one row of figure 5.108. At the top is the simply supported beam with its load, below it are the functions above the same axis \(x\). The load \(q\) changes the shear force: a piece of beam without point forces is in equilibrium if \(V\) changes over the piece by the opposite of the load force, and this is said by the relation \(V' = -q\) (corollary 5.11). For the simply supported beam with \(q = -q_0\) we have \(V' = q_0 = 1000\) N/m, so over \(2\) m \(V\) grows from \(-1000\) N to \(+1000\) N. The shear force changes the moment, \(M' = -V\) (the same corollary): the moment grows while \(V\) is negative and falls when it becomes positive. It is largest, \(500\) N m, in the middle, where \(V = 0\). The moment gives the curvature, \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35), in the middle \(500/20\,906{,}7 = 0{,}0239\) m\(^{-1}\). The curvature is the change of the slope \(v'\): since it is positive everywhere, the slope grows all the way, from \(-0{,}0159\) above the left support through zero in the middle to \(+0{,}0159\) above the right one. And the slope is the change of the deflection \(v\): the deflection falls while the slope is negative and is deepest, \(-9{,}965\) mm, where the slope is zero. We compute the values of the slope and of the deflection in section 5.7.2, once we know the supports. The graph of the function \(v\), the curve into which the axis of the beam bends, is called the deflection curve (upogibnica). If we know \(q\), we get it by four integrations.

The signs in the relations \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\) are a consequence of the choice of the side of the cross section with which we defined \(V\) and \(M\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8). Let us check them on the cantilever with the force \(F = 500\) N downward at the end: there \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\), a straight line that rises over \(1\) m from \(-500\) N m to zero, so \(M' = F = 500\) N and indeed \(M' = -V\). The load \(q\) is zero for the cantilever, \(V' = 0\) and \(M\) is linear, as it must be.

The relation \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and both relations from the section on internal forces hold under conditions that we now collect in a theorem. The theorem is the equilibrium of a piece of beam, written with one unknown deflection.

Theorem 5.42 Deflection curve equation (enačba upogibnice)

Let the beam be straight and prismatic (the cross section and the material do not change along the axis, so \(EI\) and \(EA\) are constant) and satisfy the assumptions of theorem 5.35 (Euler-Bernoulli model, linearly elastic material, axis through the centroids of the cross sections). On an open interval \((a, b)\) of the axis let there be no point forces and couples and no distributed couples (\(m = 0\)), let the load \(q\) be continuous on \((a, b)\), and let the function \(v\) be four times differentiable. Then

\[EI\,v'''' = q \quad \text{on } (a, b) . \tag{5.33}\]

If the axial load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is also continuous and \(u\) is twice differentiable, then on \((a, b)\) also

\[-\big(EA\,u'\big)' = q_{\mathrm{x}} . \tag{5.34}\]

Here \(q\) is positive upward and \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) positive in the direction of the axis \(x\).

Proof

We show that the equations are only another way of writing the relations among \(q\), \(V\), \(M\) and \(v\) from theorem 5.35 and corollary 5.11.

  1. By theorem 5.35 \(M = EI\,v''\) on \((a, b)\). So the function \(EI\,v''\) equals the moment \(M\) on the whole interval \((a, b)\).
  2. The load \(q\) is continuous on \((a, b)\), so by corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13) with \(m = 0\)) \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The signs follow from the definition of \(V\) and \(M\) on the side of larger \(x\), the check is on the cantilever above.
  3. From step 2 it follows that \(M'' = (M')' = (-V)' = -V' = q\).
  4. Insert \(M = EI\,v''\) from step 1: \((EI\,v'')'' = M'' = q\). Since the beam is prismatic, \(EI\) is constant and \((EI\,v'')'' = EI\,v''''\). This is (5.33).
  5. On the bar with its axis \(x\) we take the piece between the markers. For the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\), where \(u\) is the displacement of the section (theorem 5.35), and by corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13), \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) continuous on \((a, b)\)) \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the equilibrium of a piece of the axis, as lemma 4.75 makes it for a bar. Since \(EA\) is constant, \(-EA\,u'' = -(EA\,u')' = -N' = q_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is (5.34).

Source: EP (2.5.17) and (2.5.18) (p. 75), CM (7.3.32) (PDF p. 310). The source CM (7.3.32) writes both equations with variable \(EI(x)\) and \(EA(x)\), EP (2.5.18) the first one with variable \(EI\). We wrote them for the prismatic beam. The sources use the opposite sign of \(M\) to ours (EP \(M = \int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) with the axis \(z\) upward, CM \(M = \int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\)), and the same \(V\) as we do. Since the sign of \(M\) and the sign of the relation between \(M\) and \(v''\) both flip, the equation is the same in all three notations (EP: \(-M_{\mathrm{EP}}'' = q\) and \(M_{\mathrm{EP}} = -M\)). The proof from \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\) is not literally from the sources, they derive the equation from the equilibrium of a slice.

Extension to a variable cross section (a named simplification). If the cross section changes along the axis, \(EI(x)\) and \(EA(x)\) are functions of \(x\) and EP (2.5.18) (p. 75) writes \(\big(EI(x)\,v''\big)'' = q\). Our proof stays the same up to step 4, where \(EI\) may not be taken out of the derivative. The extension needs a cross section that changes slowly along the axis: we proved theorem 5.35 and the relation \(M = EI\,v''\) for a prismatic beam, and for a variable cross section they remain good only approximately, if the height of the cross section changes little over a length of one height. How little, this book does not estimate, and it does not use the extension: all beams in this chapter are prismatic.

The proof showed that the deflection curve equation is the same statement as the chain in figure 5.108, written with one unknown function. The first part goes from the load through the shear force to the moment: \(q \to V \to M\), and this is equilibrium. The second part is kinematics and material: \(M \to v''\). From the curvature through the slope to the deflection there are two more integrations. The last claim of the theorem is the bar of chapter 4. Equation (5.34) is \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) with \(N = EA\,u'\) and constant \(EA\), so the equilibrium of the bar (lemma 4.75). The axial and the bending equation are not coupled, because the axial force and the moment come separately from theorem 5.35.

Equation (5.33) connects the unknown function \(v\) with its derivatives and not only with its values. The highest derivative in it is the fourth. What this means, when it has a solution and when only one, we say in the next section (section 5.8.1), which also names equations of this kind. Already now we can see what else we need to know. Every integration in the chain adds a constant: the integral of \(q\) is \(V\) up to a constant, the integral of \(V\) is \(M\) up to a constant, and then follow two more integrations for \(v'' \to v' \to v\). Altogether there are four constants.

Let us try this on the simply supported beam with a uniform load (\(L = 2\) m, \(q = -q_0\), the beam at the top of the figure). We already know the moment from the equilibrium of parts, \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\), so we start at \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and have two integrations before us:

\[\begin{aligned} v'' &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\,\big(L x - x^2\big) , \\ v' &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right) + c_1 , \\ v &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L x^3}{6} - \frac{x^4}{12}\right) + c_1\,x + c_2 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.35}\]

The two steps of the chain, from \(q\) to \(V\) and from \(V\) to \(M\), would add two more constants. Here the equilibrium of the whole beam determined them (the reactions are \(1\) kN). The constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) stay open: they cannot be got from the load alone, since we add \(c_1 x + c_2\), which is a displacement and a small (linearized) rotation of the beam as a rigid body (definition 1.11), and the function \(v''\) stays the same. They are determined by the position of the beam, that is, by the supports.

We read the chain from the top down, from the load to the deflection. Often we read it the other way: we know the deflection, because we measured or computed it, and ask what happens in the beam. The answer is given by the derivatives of the deflection in turn, and in figure 5.108 beside every row it is written which derivative it is. The third relation, the shear force from the third derivative, we have not written so far, so we set all four side by side as one statement.

Corollary 5.43 Meaning of the derivatives of the deflection curve (pomen odvodov upogibnice)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.42 hold: the beam is prismatic, with constant \(EI\), and satisfies the Euler-Bernoulli model with a linearly elastic material, on the open interval \((a, b)\) of the axis there are no point forces and couples and no distributed couples (\(m = 0\)), the load \(q\) is continuous on \((a, b)\), and the deflection \(v\) is four times differentiable. The axis \(y\) points upward, the deflection \(v\) and the load \(q\) are positive upward, \(V\) and \(M\) are the shear force and the bending moment by definitions 5.7 and 5.8. Then on \((a, b)\)

\[V = -EI\,v''' \tag{5.36}\]

and the four derivatives of the deflection mean in turn

\[\begin{aligned} v' &= \text{slope of the deflection curve} , \\ EI\,v'' &= M \quad \text{(bending moment)} , \\ -EI\,v''' &= V \quad \text{(shear force)} , \\ EI\,v'''' &= q \quad \text{(load)} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.37}\]

The slope (naklon) \(v'(x)\) is the ratio of the rise to the horizontal step of the tangent to the deflection curve at the point \(x\). In the Euler-Bernoulli model it is also the small angle in radians by which the cross section at \(x\) rotates.

Proof

We show each of the four lines of equation (5.37) separately, they are the slope, the moment, the shear force and the load. All of them are relations already proved, written with the deflection.

  1. The first line. The derivative \(v'(x)\) is by definition 1.9 the limit of the ratios of the change of the deflection to the step along the axis, which is the slope of the tangent to the graph of the function \(v\). By theorem 5.15 (equation (5.16)) the longitudinal displacement of a point of the cross section is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u - y\,v'\): the point at the height \(y\) moves back by \(y\,v'\), which is the displacement in a small rotation of the cross section by the angle \(v'\) about the axis \(z\).
  2. The second line (the moment). By theorem 5.35 (equation (5.24)) \(M = EI\,v''\) on \((a, b)\).
  3. The third line (the shear force). The load \(q\) is continuous and \(m = 0\), so by corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13)) \(M' = -V\), hence \(V = -M'\). Insert step 2: \(V = -(EI\,v'')'\). The beam is prismatic, \(EI\) is constant, so \((EI\,v'')' = EI\,v'''\) and \(V = -EI\,v'''\). This is (5.36).
  4. The fourth line (the load). This is the deflection curve equation (5.33) of theorem 5.42. From step 3 we get it with the second relation of the same corollary, \(V' = -q\): \(q = -V' = (EI\,v''')' = EI\,v''''\).

Source: EP (2.5.5) (p. 74), \(V = \mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{EP}}/\mathrm{d}x\), and (2.5.12) (p. 75), \(M_{\mathrm{EP}} = -EI\,w''\), give \(V = -EI\,w'''\). The deflection \(w\) of EP is our \(v\), its moment is \(M_{\mathrm{EP}} = -M\), and its shear force is the same as ours, so this is our third line. The fourth line is EP (2.5.18) (p. 75). Setting the four relations side by side is ours.

The sign in the third line comes from \(M' = -V\), that is, from the convention that we measure \(V\) and \(M\) on the side of larger \(x\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8). A book that counts the moment or the deflection in the opposite direction has another sign in the second or the third line, so with every book we first look where its axis and its positive moment point.

Let us try the corollary on the simply supported beam of figure 5.108. We have just written its deflection in equation (5.35), with the constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), which we do not know yet. We differentiate it twice, three times and four times:

\[\begin{aligned} EI\,v'' &= \frac{q_0}{2}\,\big(L x - x^2\big) , \\ -EI\,v''' &= -\frac{q_0}{2}\,(L - 2x) = q_0\Big(x - \frac{L}{2}\Big) , \\ EI\,v'''' &= -q_0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.38}\]

The second line of the corollary gives the moment, in the middle \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1000 \cdot (2 \cdot 1 - 1^2) = 500\) N m. The third gives the shear force, above the left support \(1000 \cdot (0 - 1) = -1000\) N, in the middle zero and above the right support \(+1000\) N. The fourth gives the load \(q = -q_0 = -1000\) N/m. These are exactly the functions we started from. The constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) vanished at the second differentiation: the moment, the shear force and the load do not feel a rigid displacement of the beam, we need the supports only for the slope and the deflection.

The corollary can also be read by eye, without computing, and this is its second view. Where the deflection curve is most curved, the moment is largest, since \(M = EI\,v''\). Where the deflection curve is deepest, its tangent is horizontal, the slope is zero. Where the moment has its largest value, the shear force is zero, because \(V = -M'\). For our beam all three happen in the middle, because the beam and the load are symmetric.

5.7.2 Conditions at the ends of the beam

The open constants are determined by conditions at the ends of the beam. In chapter 4 we met the same for the bar: the equation \(N' + q = 0\) holds in the interior, and the conditions \(u(0) = 0\) at the fixed end and \(N(L) = F\) at the free end are at the ends. We called the first essential or geometric (bistveni ali geometrijski), the second natural or force (naravni ali silni) condition (section 4.4.7). In the next section we shall collect such conditions at the ends together with the equation. For a beam the unknown is \(v\) and the conditions also concern its derivatives. So that we do not write them just by feeling, let us ask what a support prescribes at its end.

Let us start on the right, at the free end \(x = L\) in figure 5.114. We draw the axis before loading grey and dashed, and after loading orange, bent. The end moves along the axis by \(u\), upward by \(v\) and rotates: the tangent to the axis tilts and its slope is \(v'\). Below we draw the forces on the same end: the force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the axis, the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) upward and the couple \(M_0\). Every force lies on the line of its own movement. The force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) does work on the displacement \(u\), the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) on the deflection \(v\), and the couple \(M_0\) on the rotation of the cross section, which for small slopes equals \(v'\) (a point at height \(y\) moves by \(-y\,v'\), theorem 5.15 and figure 5.56). So at every end of the beam there are three pairs of quantities: displacement and axial force \((u, N)\), deflection and shear force \((v, V)\), and slope and moment \((v', M)\). The product of the members of a pair is the work that the force does on this movement if it stays constant during it, as in virtual work (section 4.4.7). A force that grows from zero in proportion to the movement during slow loading does only half the product, the triangle under the sloping line instead of the whole rectangle. Nothing holds the free end, so the forces are prescribed, and the movements are a result that we get only from the computation.

On the left is the clamped end \(x = 0\). The wall holds it, so the end does not move and does not rotate: the axis leaves the wall horizontally and \(u = 0\), \(v = 0\), \(v' = 0\) are prescribed. If we think the wall away and replace it by the forces with which it acts on the end, we get the force \(-N(0)\), the force \(-V(0)\) and the couple \(-M(0)\). We do not know their sizes, they are reactions that we get only from the computation. So at an end we cannot prescribe both members of a pair: where a support holds the end, the movement is prescribed and the force is unknown, where nothing holds the end, the force is prescribed and the movement is unknown. So for the idealized supports of this chapter (assumption 10) exactly one member of each pair is prescribed at every end. CM (7.3.33), (7.3.34) says this too.

The assumption is good when the support is much stiffer than the beam. It does not hold for a support that gives a little under the force: there neither the movement nor the force is prescribed but a relation between them, as for a spring, and this chapter does not treat such supports. How much this changes the result depends on how the support is made (section 5.15.1). In figure 5.115 we draw a cantilever of length \(1\) m, grey and dashed before loading, and the force of \(500\) N downward at the free end. With a rigid clamp the axis leaves the wall horizontally and the end descends by the deflection \(7{,}97\) mm (section 5.9.2). Now let the clamp give so that the axis at the wall has the slope \(v'(0) = -0{,}001\) instead of zero. The moment in the cantilever is fixed by equilibrium and does not change, so the curvature \(v'' = M/(EI)\) also stays the same. Only the condition at the clamp changes: the whole cantilever rotates as a rigid body and the new axis is lower than the axis with the rigid clamp by \(0{,}001\,x\) at every \(x\). The free end descends by an additional \(0{,}001 \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 1\) mm. This is 12,5 % of the deflection with the rigid clamp.

We get the signs from the definitions 5.7 and 5.8, where \(V\) and \(M\) are the force and the moment with which the part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). At the right end \(x = L\) the cross section is an outer cross section with the normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The part on the side of larger \(x\) is there whatever acts on the end from outside, so \(V(L)\) is simply the force that acts on the end, and \(M(L)\) simply the couple that acts on the end. If the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) (positive upward) and the couple \(M_0\) (positive counterclockwise) act on the end, then

\[V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad M(L) = M_0 . \tag{5.39}\]

In the same way \(N(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\). At the left end \(x = 0\) the outer cross section has the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). There the part on the side of larger \(x\) is the beam itself, so \(V(0)\) is the force with which the beam acts on the wall. By the law of action and reaction the wall acts on the beam with the opposite force, so the applied force there is \(-V(0)\) and the couple \(-M(0)\). CM (7.3.33) writes the same pairs with its own signs and uses \(-v'\) for the rotation. We derive the signs from our definitions and do not copy them.

The conditions of the beams that we know, one by one:

  • clamp (vpetje): \(v = 0\) (the end does not move) and \(v' = 0\) (the cross section does not rotate: by theorem 5.15 a point at height \(y\) moves in the direction \(x\) by \(-y\,v'\), so the rotation of the cross section is tied to \(v'\) and for \(v' = 0\) there is none). The reaction \(V\) and the moment \(M\) are unknown,
  • pin or roller support (nepomična ali pomična členkasta podpora): \(v = 0\), and the rotation is free. The joint is a smooth pin, its forces pass through the centre of the pin and have no lever arm about it, so the support acts on the end with no couple and \(M = 0\), which by theorem 5.35 means \(v'' = 0\). The reaction \(V\) is unknown,
  • free end: \(V = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M = M_0\) (equation (5.39)), \(v\) and \(v'\) are unknown.

So an end has two conditions, the two ends together four. This is exactly the number given by the fourth derivative in equation (5.33), as we counted four constants. For our beams the conditions are these:

  • cantilever (\(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N, \(M_0 = 0\)): \(v(0) = 0\), \(v'(0) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\), \(V(L) = -500\) N,
  • simply supported beam: \(v(0) = 0\), \(M(0) = 0\), \(v(L) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\),
  • propped cantilever: \(v(0) = 0\), \(v'(0) = 0\), \(v(L) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\).

For the cantilever both conditions with forces are prescribed at the free end, \(V(L)\) and \(M(L)\). From there downward along the chain of section 5.7.1 we get \(V\) and \(M\) without constants, and the deflection then from the conditions at the clamp. For the propped cantilever only \(M(L) = 0\) is prescribed at the end \(x = L\), and the force \(V(L) = R\) is unknown. The chain from \(q\) to \(M\) therefore cannot be computed before we know \(R\). The missing datum is given by the condition \(v(L) = 0\). This is the difference between a statically determinate and an indeterminate beam (definition 5.6), seen in the conditions: the cantilever has three unknown reactions, the propped cantilever four. The solution of the propped cantilever is found in section 5.9.4. For checking we state that we get \(R = 750\) N, \(A = 1250\) N and \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 500\) N m, in agreement with equation (5.32) (\(2000 - 2 \cdot 750 = 500\)).

Let us also try the conditions on the simply supported beam of equation (5.35), of length \(L = 2\) m, with the load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. We draw the axis before loading grey and dashed, and the bent axis orange, with the deflection magnified 40 times. The condition \(v(0) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = 0\), since at \(x = 0\) only \(c_2\) remains in the expression for \(v\). The condition \(v(L) = 0\) gives

\[\frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L^4}{6} - \frac{L^4}{12}\right) + c_1 L = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad c_1 = -\frac{q_0 L^3}{24\,EI} = -0{,}01594 . \tag{5.40}\]

This is the slope of the beam above the left support, \(v'(0) = c_1\). It is negative, because the beam descends from the support: for every metre in the direction \(x\) the tangent descends by \(15{,}9\) mm. The conditions for the moment, \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\), are already satisfied, since \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\) and this expression already took the equilibrium with the reactions into account. The deflection at the middle is

\[v\!\left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = -\frac{5\,q_0 L^4}{384\,EI} = -9{,}965\ \text{mm} , \tag{5.41}\]

which is 0,5 % of the length of the beam. The largest slope, above the supports, is \(0{,}0159\) and its square \(2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so the assumption of small slopes (\(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\)) from section 5.3.3 is satisfied. The exact curvature of the graph is \(v''/(1 + v'^2)^{3/2}\) (SADD, PDF p. 578). With \(v'^2 = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) the denominator is at most \(1{,}0004\), so \(v''\) differs from the exact curvature by less than 0,04 %. The result was given by equilibrium, the relation \(M = EI\,v''\) (theorem 5.35) and two conditions.

So far the load was continuous everywhere. What happens under a point force, where theorem 5.42 does not hold? A point force cannot be written as a load \(q\), since a force on zero length is not a force per unit length. So we cut the beam into two parts, solve each with equation (5.33), and join the pieces with conditions at the point. Let us first look on an example at what happens under the force.

Take the simply supported beam of length \(L = 2\) m with the force \(F = 1\) kN downward at the middle, \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N at \(x_0 = 1\) m. The reactions are \(500\) N each. The shear force is \(V = -500\) N to the left and \(V = +500\) N to the right of the force, the moment is \(M = 500\,x\) on the left and \(M = 500\,(L - x)\) on the right, \(500\) N m in the middle (figure 5.118). We computed these values in section 5.2.2. The shear force jumps by \(+1000\) N, the moment is continuous with a pointed peak. What of this holds in every beam with a point force is told by the lemma below. First let us draw what the lemma assumes.

We cut the beam at \(x_0\) into the left piece and the right piece, which share the cross section at \(x_0\). The force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) presses on the top surface of the beam, as does every load that we give the beam from outside. A point force is an idealization, a finite force on an area of zero, and right at the point where it is applied the displacement field need not be continuously differentiable. The lemma claims nothing about this point. It uses only the interior points of the cross section, which are away from it and at which the field is continuously differentiable as everywhere in the interior (section 1.15.6). A real load presses on a small area and only next to it do the stresses differ from the stresses of the model. The beam model does not see this difference (assumption 1 in section 5.15.1). How far it reaches is told by Saint-Venant's principle: right next to the point of application the force is gathered, a little farther it is already more even and far from it almost even. At figure 5.44 we said that the deviation decays within one to two heights of the cross section. For the beam of figure 5.118, of height \(h = 40\) mm, this is \(40\) to \(80\) mm from the force, which is 2 to 4 % of the beam length of \(2\) m.

Lemma 5.44 Matching conditions at a point force (pogoji pod točkovno silo)

Let the beam satisfy the assumptions of theorem 5.35. At an interior point \(x_0\) of the axis let a point force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) act and no couple, and on both sides of \(x_0\) let the load \(q\) extend to a continuous function on the closed interval up to \(x_0\) (as in lemma 5.12) and \(m = 0\). Let the point where the force is applied lie on the surface of the beam, and let the displacement field be continuously differentiable at all interior points of the beam (section 1.15.6). Then \(v\) and \(v'\) and \(M\) are continuous at \(x_0\), and the shear force jumps by

\[V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{5.42}\]
Proof

We show that \(v\) and \(v'\) come from the continuity of the displacement field, and \(M\) and \(V\) from the lemma on the jump.

  1. We apply theorem 5.15 separately to the piece of the beam left of \(x_0\) and to the piece right of it. Each is a box as in the theorem, and its interior points are interior points of the beam, where the field is continuously differentiable. So for \(x \ne x_0\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\) with the same function \(v\) on each side.
  2. The displacement field is continuously differentiable also at the interior points of the cross section at \(x_0\) (the point where the force is applied lies on the surface), and so continuous (lemma 1.92). The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0, 0) = v(x)\), the displacement of the point of the axis, is therefore continuous also at \(x_0\), so the limits \(v(x_0^-)\) and \(v(x_0^+)\) are both equal to \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x_0, 0, 0)\). So \(v\) is continuous.
  3. Take two heights inside the cross section, \(y_1\) and \(y_2\), \(y_1 \ne y_2\), such that the points \((x_0, y_1, 0)\) and \((x_0, y_2, 0)\) are interior points of the cross section and do not lie on the surface. The cross section is vertical before loading, tilted after it and stays plane. By step 1 the difference of the horizontal displacements of its points is \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_1, 0) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_2, 0) = -(y_1 - y_2)\,v'(x)\) for \(x \ne x_0\). The left side is a continuous function of \(x\) also at \(x_0\), because the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous at interior points (step 2). So the limits \(v'(x_0^-)\) and \(v'(x_0^+)\) are both equal to \(-(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y_1, 0) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y_2, 0))/(y_1 - y_2)\).
  1. It remains to show that the derivative \(v'(x_0)\) exists and equals this common limit, since step 1 gives \(v'\) only for \(x \ne x_0\). Figure 5.120 shows the graph of \(v\) around \(x_0\) with the point \((x_0, v(x_0))\). For an \(x\) to the right of \(x_0\), \(v\) is continuous on \([x_0, x]\) (at \(x_0\) by step 2, elsewhere because it is differentiable) and differentiable between \(x_0\) and \(x\) (step 1). So the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) gives a point \(\xi\) between them at which the tangent is parallel to the secant: \((v(x) - v(x_0))/(x - x_0) = v'(\xi)\). As \(x\) approaches \(x_0\), \(\xi\) is trapped between \(x_0\) and \(x\) and tends to \(x_0\) from the right. So \(v'(\xi)\) tends to the limit \(v'(x_0^+)\) of step 3. From the left it is the same, with \(x < x_0\) and \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x_0\). The quotient \((v(x) - v(x_0))/(x - x_0)\) therefore has the same limit from both sides. So the derivative \(v'(x_0)\) exists and equals the two limits of step 3. Thus \(v'\) is continuous at \(x_0\).
  2. The load and the point force satisfy lemma 5.12, which writes the equilibrium of a short piece around \(x_0\) under the forces of the neighbouring parts and the point force. It gives \(M(x_0^+) - M(x_0^-) = -M_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). So the moment is continuous and the shear force jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: The continuity of the displacement field at interior points is an assumption from section 1.15.6, and the jump is lemma 5.12 (EP p. 74, relation of \(V\) and \(q\)). The proof is not from a source.

Let us look at the consequences on the beam with the central force. The jump of \(V\) is \(1000\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N, so indeed \(V^+ - V^- = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). Since \(M\) is continuous, the curvature \(v'' = M/(EI)\) is continuous too, and its derivative jumps, because \((EI\,v'')' = M' = -V\) and \(V\) jumps. So \(v''\) has a pointed peak at \(x_0\), the same as \(M\), the graph of \(v'\) has no corners and the graph of \(v\) has no sharp corner: under the force the axis of the beam gets no kink, only the rate of change of the curvature jumps. The slope is \(v' = 0\) at \(x_0\) by symmetry and the beam has its lowest point there, \(v(x_0) = -F L^3/(48\,EI) = -7{,}972\) mm.

If there were a point couple \(M_0\) on the beam, the moment would jump, not the shear force (lemma 5.12). The conditions under the force (continuity of \(v\), \(v'\), \(M\) and the jump of \(V\) by equation (5.42)) are four at the joint of two pieces, just as there are four conditions at the two ends of the beam together. With two pieces we have eight constants and eight conditions: four at the ends and four under the force. We write the solution piece by piece in section 5.8.4.
