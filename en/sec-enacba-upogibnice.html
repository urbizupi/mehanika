Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.7 The deflection curve equation (enačba upogibnice)

For the cantilever and the simply supported beam we got the reactions from the equilibrium of the whole beam, the internal forces \(V\) and \(M\) from the equilibrium of parts, and the curvature from \(M = EI\,v''\). The deflection \(v\) came last. Is there another way, in which we compute the deflection directly from the load? We need the answer for a beam where equilibrium alone does not suffice, and such a beam is the propped cantilever (podprta konzola).

Take the same steel strip \(20 \times 40\) mm as before, of length \(L = 2\) m, with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m². Let it be clamped (vpetje) at \(x = 0\) and held up at \(x = L\) by a roller support (pomična členkasta podpora), and let it carry the uniform load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, downward. The clamp gives two forces and a couple, the support one force, four unknown reactions in all, while there are only three equilibrium equations in the plane (definition 5.6). Because the load points vertically, the horizontal equation is satisfied if the horizontal reaction at the clamp is zero. What remains are the equations for the vertical forces and for the moments about the clamp. Let \(A\) be the vertical reaction at the clamp, \(M_{\mathrm{v}}\) the couple at the clamp (positive if it turns counterclockwise) and \(R\) the reaction at the support, both forces upward. Equilibrium gives

\[A + R - q_0 L = 0 , \qquad M_{\mathrm{v}} + R\,L - q_0 L\,\frac{L}{2} = 0 , \tag{5.32}\]

so \(A = 2000 - R\) and \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 2000 - 2R\) (forces in N, moment in N m). Every \(R\) gives a pair that satisfies equilibrium. For \(R = 0\) this is the cantilever without a support with \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = q_0 L^2/2 = 2000\) N m. For \(R = 1000\) N we get \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\), the clamp acts as a pin and we have the simply supported beam. Which value of \(R\) is the right one, equilibrium does not say. The deformation says it: the support at \(x = L\) prevents the end of the beam from sinking, so \(v(L) = 0\). This is a condition on the deflection, so we must find the deflection from the load.

From history

Navier's equation \(E I\,\mathrm{d}^2 y/\mathrm{d}x^2 = M\) had been used since Euler's time for the deflection of cantilevers and symmetrically loaded simply supported beams (TIMO, PDF p. 83). Navier applied it to any transverse load: since the moment differs in different parts of the beam, the deflection in different parts is described by different equations (TIMO, PDF p. 83). For a beam loaded at a point \(C\) he denoted the angle that the tangent at \(C\) makes with the horizontal by \(\alpha\) and expressed the deflections of both ends with this angle. From the equality of the two deflections he obtained \(\tan\alpha\) (TIMO, PDF p. 83). He knew the deflection of a cantilever, so he could write the deflection equations for both parts of the beam (TIMO, PDF p. 83).

Navier was the first to develop a general method for statically indeterminate problems. He showed that problems are indeterminate only as long as the bodies are considered perfectly rigid. When elasticity is taken into account, equations expressing the conditions of deformation can always be added to the equations of statics, and so we get enough equations for all unknowns (TIMO, PDF p. 83). He explained this with the example of a load carried by several bars in one plane. If the bars are perfectly rigid, we may choose arbitrary values for the forces in all bars except two and compute the last two from statics, so the problem is indeterminate. If elasticity is taken into account, the elongations of the bars and the forces in them are functions of the two components of the displacement of the point where the bars meet. Two equations of statics then determine the displacement and from it all the forces (TIMO, PDF p. 83-84). For bending he started with a beam built in at one end and simply supported at the other (TIMO, PDF p. 84). He denoted the unknown support force at the simply supported end by \(Q\) and for each part of the beam wrote the deflection equation with a moment that depends on \(Q\). By integrating, and by requiring that the two parts have a common ordinate and tangent where they meet, he obtained the deflection, and \(Q\) from the condition that the deflection at the simply supported end is zero (TIMO, PDF p. 84-85). His calculation has no moment and shear force diagrams, which might explain why he sometimes fixed the place of the largest moment wrongly (TIMO, PDF p. 85).

Let us return to the question of the section: how we get the deflection of a beam built in at one end and simply supported at the other directly from the load, when equilibrium alone does not suffice. Navier's path is the same as ours: we express the moment with the unknown, integrate the deflection equation and determine the unknowns from the conditions at the ends and at the joints.

5.7.1 From the load to the deflection

We already know every link of the path from the load to the deflection, each one separately. The load \(q\) changes the shear force: a piece of beam without point forces is in equilibrium if \(V\) changes over the piece by the opposite of the load force, and this is said by the relation \(V' = -q\) (corollary 5.11). For the simply supported beam with \(q = -q_0\) we have \(V' = q_0 = 1000\) N/m, so over \(2\) m \(V\) grows from \(-1000\) N to \(+1000\) N. The shear force changes the moment, \(M' = -V\) (the same corollary): the moment grows while \(V\) is negative and falls when it becomes positive. The moment gives the curvature, \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35). The curvature is the change of the slope \(v'\), and the slope is the change of the deflection \(v\). The graph of the function \(v\), the curve into which the axis of the beam bends, is called the deflection curve (upogibnica). If we know \(q\), we get it by four integrations (figure 5.44).

The signs in the relations \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\) are a consequence of the choice of the side of the cross section with which we defined \(V\) and \(M\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8). Let us check them on the cantilever with the force \(F = 500\) N downward at the end: there \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\), so \(M' = F = 500\) N and indeed \(M' = -V\). The load \(q\) is zero for the cantilever, \(V' = 0\) and \(M\) is linear, as it must be.

The relation \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and both relations from the section on internal forces hold under conditions that we now collect in a theorem. The theorem is the equilibrium of a piece of beam, written with one unknown deflection.

Theorem 5.42 Deflection curve equation (enačba upogibnice)

Let the beam be straight and prismatic (the cross section and the material do not change along the axis, so \(EI\) and \(EA\) are constant) and satisfy the assumptions of theorem 5.35 (Euler-Bernoulli model, linearly elastic material, axis through the centroids of the cross sections). On an open interval \((a, b)\) of the axis let there be no point forces and couples and no distributed couples (\(m = 0\)), let the load \(q\) be continuous on \((a, b)\), and let the function \(v\) be four times differentiable. Then

\[\big(EI\,v''\big)'' = q \quad \text{on } (a, b) , \tag{5.33}\]

If the axial load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is also continuous and \(u\) is twice differentiable, then on \((a, b)\) also

\[-\big(EA\,u'\big)' = q_{\mathrm{x}} . \tag{5.34}\]

Here \(q\) is positive upward and \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) positive in the direction of the axis \(x\).

Proof

We show that the equations are only another way of writing the relations among \(q\), \(V\), \(M\) and \(v\) from theorem 5.35 and corollary 5.11.

  1. By theorem 5.35 \(M = EI\,v''\) on \((a, b)\). So the function \(EI\,v''\) equals the moment \(M\) on the whole interval \((a, b)\).
  2. The load \(q\) is continuous on \((a, b)\), so by corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13) with \(m = 0\)) \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The signs follow from the definition of \(V\) and \(M\) on the side of larger \(x\), the check is on the cantilever above.
  3. From step 2 it follows that \(M'' = (M')' = (-V)' = -V' = q\).
  4. Insert \(M = EI\,v''\) from step 1: \((EI\,v'')'' = M'' = q\). Since the beam is prismatic, \(EI\) is constant and \((EI\,v'')'' = EI\,v''''\). This is (5.33).
  5. For the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) (theorem 5.35) and by corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13), \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) continuous on \((a, b)\)) \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the equilibrium of a piece of the axis, as lemma 4.75 makes it for a bar. Since \(EA\) is constant, \(-EA\,u'' = -(EA\,u')' = -N' = q_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is (5.34).

Source: EP (2.5.17) and (2.5.18) (p. 75), CM (7.3.32) (PDF p. 310). The source CM (7.3.32) writes both equations with variable \(EI(x)\) and \(EA(x)\), EP (2.5.18) the first one with variable \(EI\). We wrote them for the prismatic beam. The sources use the opposite sign of \(M\) to ours (EP \(M = \int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) with the axis \(z\) upward, CM \(M = \int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\)), and the same \(V\) as we do. In the second derivative the sign of \(M\) cancels, so the equation is the same in all three notations (EP: \(-M_{\mathrm{EP}}'' = q\) and \(M_{\mathrm{EP}} = -M\)). The proof from \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\) is not literally from the sources, they derive the equation from the equilibrium of a slice.

Extension to a variable cross section (a named simplification). If the cross section changes along the axis, \(EI(x)\) and \(EA(x)\) are functions of \(x\) and EP (2.5.18) (p. 75) writes \(\big(EI(x)\,v''\big)'' = q\). Our proof stays the same up to step 4, where \(EI\) may not be taken out of the derivative. The extension needs a cross section that changes slowly along the axis: we proved theorem 5.35 and the relation \(M = EI\,v''\) for a prismatic beam, and for a variable cross section they remain good only approximately, if the height of the cross section changes little over a length of one height. How little, this book does not estimate, and it does not use the extension: all beams in this chapter are prismatic.

The proof showed that the deflection curve equation is the same statement as the chain in figure 5.44, written with one unknown function. The first part goes from the load to the moment: \(q \to V \to M\), and this is equilibrium. The second part is kinematics and material: \(M \to v''\). The last claim of the theorem is the bar of chapter 4. Equation (5.34) is \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) with \(N = EA\,u'\) and constant \(EA\), so the equilibrium of the bar (lemma 4.75). The axial and the bending equation are not coupled, because the axial force and the moment come separately from theorem 5.35.

Equation (5.33) connects the unknown function \(v\) with its derivatives and not only with its values. The highest derivative in it is the fourth. What this means, when it has a solution and when only one, we say in the next section (section 5.8.1), which also names equations of this kind. Already now we can see what else we need to know. Every integration in the chain adds a constant: the integral of \(q\) is \(V\) up to a constant, the integral of \(V\) is \(M\) up to a constant, and then follow two more integrations for \(v'' \to v' \to v\). Altogether there are four constants.

Let us try this on the simply supported beam with a uniform load (\(L = 2\) m, \(q = -q_0\)). We already know the moment from the equilibrium of parts, \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\), so we start at \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and have two integrations before us:

\[\begin{aligned} v'' &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\,\big(L x - x^2\big) , \\ v' &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right) + c_1 , \\ v &= \frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L x^3}{6} - \frac{x^4}{12}\right) + c_1\,x + c_2 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.35}\]

The two steps of the chain, from \(q\) to \(V\) and from \(V\) to \(M\), would add two more constants. Here the equilibrium of the whole beam determined them (the reactions are \(1\) kN). The constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) stay open: they cannot be got from the load alone, since we add \(c_1 x + c_2\), which is a displacement and a rotation of the beam as a rigid body (definition 1.11), and the function \(v''\) stays the same. They are determined by the position of the beam, that is, by the supports.

5.7.2 Conditions at the ends of the beam

The open constants are determined by conditions at the ends of the beam. In chapter 4 we met the same for the bar: the equation \(N' + q = 0\) holds in the interior, and the conditions \(u(0) = 0\) and \(N(L) = F\) are at the ends. We called the first essential or geometric (bistveni ali geometrijski), the second natural or force (naravni ali silni) condition (section 4.4.7). In the next section we shall collect such conditions at the ends together with the equation. For a beam the unknown is \(v\) and the conditions also concern its derivatives. So that we do not write them just by feeling, let us ask what a support prescribes at its end.

At every end of the beam there are three pairs of quantities: displacement and axial force \((u, N)\), deflection and shear force \((v, V)\), and slope and moment \((v', M)\). The product of the members of a pair is work: the axial force on the displacement, the shear force on the deflection, the moment on the slope (a couple on the angle of rotation, the slope is a ratio of lengths without a unit). At an end we cannot prescribe both members of a pair: if the support holds the end in place, the displacement is prescribed, and the force is a reaction that we get only from the computation. If nothing holds the end, the force is prescribed and the displacement is the result. So at every end exactly one member of each pair is prescribed. CM (7.3.33), (7.3.34) says this too.

We get the signs from the definitions 5.7 and 5.8, where \(V\) and \(M\) are the force and the moment with which the part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). At the right end \(x = L\) the cross section is an outer cross section with the normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so there \(V(L)\) is simply the force that acts on the end, and \(M(L)\) simply the couple that acts on the end. If the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) (positive upward) and the couple \(M_0\) (positive counterclockwise) act on the end, then

\[V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad M(L) = M_0 . \tag{5.36}\]

At the left end \(x = 0\) the outer cross section has the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The force that acts on the end is, by the law of action and reaction, opposite to \(V(0)\), so the applied force there is \(-V(0)\) and the couple \(-M(0)\). CM (7.3.33) writes the same pairs with its own signs and uses \(-v'\) for the rotation. We derive the signs from our definitions and do not copy them.

The conditions of the beams that we know, one by one:

  • clamp (vpetje): \(v = 0\) (the end does not move) and \(v' = 0\) (the cross section does not rotate: by theorem 5.15 a point at height \(y\) moves in the direction \(x\) by \(-y\,v'\), so the rotation of the cross section is tied to \(v'\) and for \(v' = 0\) there is none). The reaction \(V\) and the moment \(M\) are unknown,
  • pin or roller support (nepomična ali pomična členkasta podpora): \(v = 0\), the rotation is free, so there is no couple and \(M = 0\), which by theorem 5.35 means \(v'' = 0\). The reaction \(V\) is unknown,
  • free end: \(V = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M = M_0\) (equation (5.36)), \(v\) and \(v'\) are unknown.

So an end has two conditions, the two ends together four. This is exactly the number given by the fourth derivative in equation (5.33), as we counted four constants. For our beams the conditions are these:

  • cantilever (\(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N, \(M_0 = 0\)): \(v(0) = 0\), \(v'(0) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\), \(V(L) = -500\) N,
  • simply supported beam: \(v(0) = 0\), \(M(0) = 0\), \(v(L) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\),
  • propped cantilever: \(v(0) = 0\), \(v'(0) = 0\), \(v(L) = 0\), \(M(L) = 0\).

For the cantilever both conditions with forces are prescribed at the free end, \(V(L)\) and \(M(L)\). From there downward along the chain of section 5.7.1 we get \(V\) and \(M\) without constants, and the deflection then from the conditions at the clamp. For the propped cantilever only \(M(L) = 0\) is prescribed at the end \(x = L\), and the force \(V(L) = R\) is unknown. The chain from \(q\) to \(M\) therefore cannot be computed before we know \(R\). The missing datum is given by the condition \(v(L) = 0\). This is the difference between a statically determinate and an indeterminate beam (definition 5.6), seen in the conditions. The solution of the propped cantilever is found in section 5.9.4. For checking we state that we get \(R = 750\) N, \(A = 1250\) N and \(M_{\mathrm{v}} = 500\) N m, in agreement with equation (5.32) (\(2000 - 2 \cdot 750 = 500\)).

Let us also try the conditions on the simply supported beam of equation (5.35). The condition \(v(0) = 0\) gives \(c_2 = 0\). The condition \(v(L) = 0\) gives

\[\frac{q_0}{2EI}\left(\frac{L^4}{6} - \frac{L^4}{12}\right) + c_1 L = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad c_1 = -\frac{q_0 L^3}{24\,EI} = -0{,}01594 . \tag{5.37}\]

This is the slope of the beam above the left support, \(v'(0) = c_1\), and it is negative, because the beam descends from the support. The conditions for the moment, \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\), are already satisfied, since \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\). The deflection at the middle is

\[v\!\left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = -\frac{5\,q_0 L^4}{384\,EI} = -9{,}965\ \text{mm} , \tag{5.38}\]

which is 0,5 % of the length of the beam. The largest slope is \(0{,}0159\) and its square \(2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so the assumption of small slopes (\(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\)) from section 5.3.3 is satisfied. The result was given by equilibrium, theorem 5.42 and two conditions.

So far the load was continuous everywhere. What happens under a point force, where theorem 5.42 does not hold? The function \(q\) is not a function there, since a force on zero length is not a force per unit length. So we cut the beam into two parts, solve each with equation (5.33), and join the pieces with conditions at the point. Take the simply supported beam of length \(L = 2\) m with the force \(F = 1\) kN downward at the middle, \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N at \(x_0 = 1\) m. The reactions are \(500\) N each. The shear force is \(V = -500\) N to the left and \(V = +500\) N to the right of the force, the moment is \(M = 500\,x\) on the left and \(M = 500\,(L - x)\) on the right, \(500\) N m in the middle (figure 5.46). The shear force jumps by \(+1000\) N, the moment is continuous with a pointed peak.

Lemma 5.43 Matching conditions at a point force (pogoji pod točkovno silo)

Let the beam satisfy the assumptions of theorem 5.35. At an interior point \(x_0\) of the axis let a point force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) act and no couple, and on both sides of \(x_0\) let the load \(q\) be continuous and bounded near \(x_0\) and \(m = 0\). Let the point where the force is applied lie on the surface of the beam, and let the displacement field be continuously differentiable at all interior points of the beam (section 1.15.6). Then \(v\), \(v'\) and \(M\) are continuous at \(x_0\), and the shear force jumps by

\[V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{5.39}\]
Proof

We show that \(v\) and \(v'\) come from the continuity of the displacement field, and \(M\) and \(V\) from the lemma on the jump.

  1. We apply theorem 5.15 separately to the piece of the beam left of \(x_0\) and to the piece right of it. Each is a box as in the theorem, and its interior points are interior points of the beam, where the field is continuously differentiable. So for \(x \ne x_0\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\) with the same function \(v\) on each side.
  2. The displacement field is continuously differentiable also at the interior points of the cross section at \(x_0\) (the point where the force is applied lies on the surface), and so continuous (lemma 1.91). The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0, 0) = v(x)\) is therefore continuous also at \(x_0\), so the limits \(v(x_0^-)\) and \(v(x_0^+)\) are both equal to \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x_0, 0, 0)\). So \(v\) is continuous.
  3. For two heights \(y_1 \ne y_2\) of the cross section \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_1, 0) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y_2, 0) = -(y_1 - y_2)\,v'(x)\) for \(x \ne x_0\). The left side is a continuous function of \(x\) also at \(x_0\), because the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous (step 2). So the limits \(v'(x_0^-)\) and \(v'(x_0^+)\) are both equal to \(-(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y_1, 0) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(x_0, y_2, 0))/(y_1 - y_2)\) and \(v'\) is continuous.
  4. The load and the point force satisfy lemma 5.12. It gives \(M(x_0^+) - M(x_0^-) = -M_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) and \(V(x_0^+) - V(x_0^-) = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). So the moment is continuous and the shear force jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: The continuity of the displacement field at interior points is an assumption from section 1.15.6, and the jump is lemma 5.12 (EP p. 74, relation of \(V\) and \(q\)). The proof is not from a source.

Let us look at the consequences on the beam with the central force. The jump of \(V\) is \(1000\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N, so indeed \(V^+ - V^- = -F_{\mathrm{y}}\). Since \(M\) is continuous, the curvature \(v'' = M/(EI)\) is continuous too, and its derivative jumps, because \((EI\,v'')' = M' = -V\) and \(V\) jumps. So \(v''\) has a pointed peak at \(x_0\), the same as \(M\), the graph of \(v'\) has no corners and the graph of \(v\) has no sharp corner: under the force the beam does not break, only the rate of change of the curvature jumps. The slope is \(v' = 0\) at \(x_0\) by symmetry and the beam has its lowest point there, \(v(x_0) = -F L^3/(48\,EI) = -7{,}972\) mm.

If there were a point couple \(M_0\) on the beam, the moment would jump, not the shear force (lemma 5.12). The conditions under the force (continuity of \(v\), \(v'\), \(M\) and the jump of \(V\) by equation (5.39)) are four at the joint of two pieces, just as the conditions at an end are four at the end of the beam. With two pieces we have eight constants and eight conditions: four at the ends and four under the force. We write the solution piece by piece in section 5.8.4.
