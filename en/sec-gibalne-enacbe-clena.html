Chapter 28 Flexible robot links

28.6 Equations of motion of the link

So far we have described the link with two groups of coordinates, the reference coordinates \(o\) and \(\theta\) and the elastic coordinates \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (definitions 28.3 and 28.4). We have computed its kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 28.13) and its elastic energy \(U = \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 28.18). We now ask how the link moves when loads act on it. The answer is the equations of motion (gibalne enačbe), the equations that connect the coordinates, their velocities and accelerations with the loads at every time. This section derives them, then applies them to a link driven by a motor on a hub and shows two things: how the frequencies of such a link interlace with the frequencies of the link held by the hub, and why we must not compute the elastic coordinates separately from the rigid motion.

28.6.1 Equations of motion of a general planar link

Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.88) are the road we already know: from the kinetic energy we have and the generalized forces they give the equations of motion without computing the forces in the constraints. The link brings one novelty. It is not finitely many points and rigid bodies but infinitely many points whose positions are determined by the coordinates \(\vek q = (\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = (o_{\mathrm{x}}, o_{\mathrm{y}}, \theta)\). The proof of theorem 8.88 uses for each point only that its position is a function of the coordinates (steps 3 and 4), and for the body also that the derivatives with respect to the coordinates go under the integral (step 5), so it holds here too. The elastic forces in the link are internal forces with a potential \(U\). We know that \(U\) depends only on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 28.18), which is also all the dependence on the position of the frame that we need.

As in theorem 8.120 we separate the derivatives of the mass matrix from Lagrange's equations. The resulting term with squares of velocities is named the quadratic velocity vector (vektor kvadratnih hitrosti) \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{v}}\). For the link these are the forces that no external load causes but that appear because the mass matrix changes with position: centrifugal, Coriolis and gyroscopic forces (SHABANA PDF p. 235, equations 5.142 and 5.144, where it is named this way).

Theorem 28.20 Equations of motion of a planar flexible link (gibalne enačbe ravninskega člena)

Let a planar link have coordinates \(\vek q = (\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) made of \(n\) numbers, where \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = (o_{\mathrm{x}}, o_{\mathrm{y}}, \theta)\) are the reference coordinates and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) the elastic coordinates under the reference conditions of definition 28.5. Let the kinetic energy be \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\dot{\vek q}\) with a matrix \(\mat M\) that is symmetric, positive definite and continuously differentiable (theorem 28.13), let the elastic energy be \(U = \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), and let \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the vector of generalized forces of the applied loads (definition 8.50). Then a twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) satisfies Lagrange's equations of the link if and only if for \(s = 1, \dots, n\)

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + (\mat K\vek q)_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{s}} , \qquad Q_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\Big(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}} - \tfrac12\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\Big)\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} , \tag{28.31}\]

where \(\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} \mat 0 & \mat 0 \\ \mat 0 & \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\). In matrix form \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek Q_{\mathrm{v}}\) with \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{v}} = -\dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\,\big[\partial(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q})/\partial\vek q\big]^\T\).

Proof

We must show that Lagrange's equations of the link read as (28.31). The link has many points, so we first say what theorem 8.88 means here.

  1. The position of the point of the link at \(x\) is \(\vek X(x, \vek q) = \vek o + \mat R(\theta)\,(\bar{\vek r}_0(x) + \mat\Psi(x)\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) (definition 28.4). It is a twice continuously differentiable function of the coordinates, because \(\mat R(\theta)\) is built from sine and cosine and \(\vek X\) is linear in \(\vek o\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). So for every point steps 3 and 4 of the proof of theorem 8.88 hold: \(\ddot{\vek X}\cdot\partial\vek X/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) equals the difference between the time derivative of \(\dot{\vek X}\cdot\partial\dot{\vek X}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and the quantity \(\dot{\vek X}\cdot\partial\dot{\vek X}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and each of these is a derivative of half the squared velocity.
  2. The summation over points in step 5 of that proof is replaced by the integral over the link, \(T = \tfrac12\int\rho A\,|\dot{\vek X}|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the derivatives with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) go under the integral (lemma 8.31). The inertial work is therefore \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\) for every \(s\).
  3. The elastic forces of the link are internal forces with a potential \(U\) (theorem 28.18, which follows from definition 4.85), so their generalized force is \(-\partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (definition 8.53). Since \(U\) does not depend on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(-\partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = -(\mat K\vek q)_{\mathrm{s}}\) by theorem 28.18. The applied loads give \(Q_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{s}}\) (definition 8.50).
  4. D'Alembert's principle gives by step 6 of the proof of theorem 8.88 the equality \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{s}} - (\mat K\vek q)_{\mathrm{s}}\) for every \(s\), because the coordinates are independent.
  5. From \(T = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}M_{\mathrm{jk}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the same computation as in steps 2 to 4 of the proof of theorem 8.120 (symmetry of the matrix \(\mat M\) and the chain rule, lemma 8.29) we get \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}} - \tfrac12\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}})\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\). We insert it into step 4 and move the term with squares of velocities to the right-hand side. This is (28.31).

Source: SHABANA section 5.5, PDF pp. 234-237: equation 5.144 (\(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q + \mat C_{\mathrm{q}}^\T\vek\lambda = \vek Q_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek Q_{\mathrm{v}}\), here without constraints) and 5.142 (the quadratic velocity vector, an expression with \(\dot{\mat M}\) and the derivative with respect to \(\vek q\), which we checked on the image of the equation). The proof is not from the source: it only announces the equation, and the derivation from theorem 8.88 and theorem 8.120 is ours.

The theorem holds for a free link. A link connected to other links by joints gets one more term \(\mat C_{\mathrm{q}}^\T\vek\lambda\) with the multipliers of the constraints, as equation 5.144 in SHABANA, PDF p. 235, says. We have the multipliers of constraints in theorem 8.95. Assembling several links with constraints is outside this chapter (SHABANA sections 5.4 and 5.8 to 5.10, PDF pp. 230-233 and 251-260). The vector \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{v}}\) is the part of the equations that the rigid body of chapter 8 would not have: because \(\mat M\) depends on \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), elastic displacements affect the rotation, and the rotation affects the elastic displacements.

28.6.2 The link on a hub

For the first joint of a robot this theorem is too general, so we narrow it to the case we will keep until the end of the chapter. The link is the steel strip from the section on running examples (length \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 141{,}12\) N m², \(\rho A = 1{,}872\) kg/m, \(J_{\mathrm{link}} = mL^2/3 = 0{,}624\) kg m²), and a motor turns it about an axis at the point \(O\), which we take as the origin. This case is our choice and not from a source (SHABANA has no link on a hub with numbers), so we vary the inertia of the hub so that the conclusion does not hang on it.

Let us draw it. The motion is in the horizontal plane \(xy\), so weight does no work, since it is perpendicular to every displacement (definition 8.50). First the fixed axes \(x\) and \(y\), measured from the resting ground. About the point \(O\) turns the hub, named after the wheel, where the hub is its central part on which the axle sits. Here it is a rigid body with inertia \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\) about the axis, into which the rotor of the motor and the gearbox are converted. For the hub we take radius zero, and the link is attached to the axis (an idealization from the chapter outline: a hub of larger radius would change the forces in the link, which the section on steady rotation says). The frame \(\bar x\bar y\) is the one of definition 28.3, with origin at \(O\), and it is turned by the angle \(\theta\) from the axis \(x\). The reference conditions of definition 28.5 are clamped conditions at \(x = 0\): the axis \(\bar x\) is the tangent of the link at the hub, \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\). In the frame the link bends by \(v(x)\) perpendicular to the axis \(\bar x\), the tip bending is \(v(L)\). Finally the motor torque \(\tau\) acts on the hub in the direction of increasing \(\theta\). Its work is \(\tau\,\delta\theta\), because the angle of the hub is \(\theta\) and the bending angle does not affect it: by definition 8.50 \(Q_\theta = \tau\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{k}} = 0\). This is why the torque with a clamped frame enters only the equation for \(\theta\).

The simplification of the elastic field is this: a point on the axis at \(x\) moves only transversally, \(\bar{\vek r}_0 = (x, 0)\) and \(\bar{\vek u} = (0, v)\) with \(v(x) = \mat\Psi_2(x)\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(x)\,q_{\mathrm{k}}\). The displacement along the axis, the shortening of the axis due to bending, we neglect. For a tip of 10 mm in the first mode the shortening is \(\tfrac12\int v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0{,}058\) mm, which is 0,6 % of the tip, so in this section it is negligible. In fast rotation we must not forget it, because there the centrifugal force opposes exactly this shortening. The next section therefore brings this work back. Let the \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) be free of rigid motions, that is \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}(0) = \psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\), and independent, and let \(\vek b = \int_0^L\rho A\,x\,\vek\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\) be the coupling column and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \int_0^L\rho A\,\vek\psi\vek\psi^\T\mathrm{d}x\) (both as in theorem 28.13 and lemma 28.15).

The motor is an ideal source of torque: its electrical dynamics and the flexibility of the joint itself (a hub that twists under torque) are not taken into account in this chapter. Robots with flexible joints are treated by SPONG in his chapter on the control of such robots (PDF p. 461).

Corollary 28.21 Equations of motion of a link on a hub (gibalne enačbe člena na pestu)

Let a link with a transverse elastic field \(v = \vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) have a clamped frame and let the hub have inertia \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\) and torque \(\tau\), as described above. Let \(\vek q = (\theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\). Then the equations of motion are

\[\big(J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big)\,\ddot\theta + \vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + 2\,\dot\theta\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \tau , \tag{28.32}\]
\[\vek b\,\ddot\theta + \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \big(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \dot\theta^2\,\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\big)\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0 . \tag{28.33}\]
Proof

We must show what the kinetic energy of the link and the hub is (step 2), and then insert it into theorem 28.20.

  1. The point of the link at \(x\) has in the frame the position \(\bar{\vek u}_{\mathrm{tot}} = (x, v)\), and by lemma 28.8 its velocity in the axes of the frame is \(\dot\theta\,\mat J(x, v)^\T + (0, \dot v)^\T = (-\dot\theta v,\ \dot\theta x + \dot v)\). Because \(\mat R(\theta)\) is an orthogonal matrix (lemma 2.40), \(|\dot{\vek r}|^2 = \dot\theta^2v^2 + (\dot\theta x + \dot v)^2\), that is \(\dot\theta^2(x^2 + v^2) + 2\dot\theta x\dot v + \dot v^2\). The end \(O\) is at rest.
  2. The kinetic energy of the link is \(\tfrac12\int_0^L\rho A\,|\dot{\vek r}|^2\mathrm{d}x\). With \(v = \vek\psi^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), \(\int\rho A x^2\mathrm{d}x = J_{\mathrm{link}}\) (this is \(mL^2/3\) for constant \(\rho A\)), \(\vek b\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) this is
    \[T_{\mathrm{link}} = \tfrac12\big(J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big)\dot\theta^2 + \dot\theta\,\vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} .\]
    The hub contributes \(\tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{h}}\dot\theta^2\). Together \(T\) equals
    \[T = \tfrac12\big(J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\big)\dot\theta^2 + \dot\theta\,\vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} . \tag{28.34}\]
  3. The elastic energy is \(U = \tfrac12\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 28.18). The generalized forces are \(Q_\theta = \tau\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{k}} = 0\) (above). Lagrange's equations of the link (theorem 28.20) are Lagrange's equations with \(T\), \(-\partial U/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  4. The coordinate \(\theta\). \(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial\dot\theta} = (J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\dot\theta + \vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\). Its time derivative by the product rule is \((J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\ddot\theta + 2\,\dot\theta\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), since \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}) = 2\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) (symmetry of the matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\)). Because \(T\) does not contain \(\theta\), \(\partial T/\partial\theta = 0\), and \(\partial U/\partial\theta = 0\). We get (28.32).
  5. The coordinates \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). \(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}} = \dot\theta\,\vek b + \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), its time derivative is \(\ddot\theta\,\vek b + \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), because \(\vek b\) is constant. The derivatives are \(\partial T/\partial\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \dot\theta^2\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\partial U/\partial\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). Lagrange's equation is \(\vek b\ddot\theta + \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} - \dot\theta^2\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\), which is (28.33).

Source: The derivation is not from a source: SHABANA has no link on a hub, he has the general equations 5.144 and 5.147 (PDF pp. 235-237). Here they are derived from theorem 28.20 for the transverse field.

Let us see what the equations say. The first, (28.32), is the rotation: the torque \(\tau\) accelerates the total inertia \(J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}}\), and the term \(\vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) we will turn to below, because it says that the bending of the link itself turns the hub. The second, (28.33), is the bending: \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the elastic force, \(\vek b\ddot\theta\) is the inertial force due to the acceleration of the hub (as a vehicle pulling away pushes us back into the seat, the link lags behind the accelerating hub), and \(-\dot\theta^2\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the transverse part of the centrifugal force, which pulls the bent link still further from the axis \(\bar x\) and reduces its stiffness. The axial part of the centrifugal force stretches the link and increases its stiffness, but it is not in the equation, because we neglected the shortening of the axis (section 28.7).

How small are the terms we will now drop? The first class are the terms with \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\). For a tip of 10 mm in the first mode, \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = 4{,}68\cdot10^{-5}\) kg m², which is \(7{,}5\cdot10^{-5}\) of \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\). The second class is \(\dot\theta^2\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) against \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\): the ratio is \(\dot\theta^2/\omega_1^2\) with \(\omega_1 = 30{,}53\) rad/s. At \(\dot\theta = 3\) rad/s it is \(0{,}0097\), at \(\dot\theta = 10\) rad/s it is \(0{,}107\). Linearization is therefore good for rotation around 3 rad/s (1 % in stiffness), at 10 rad/s it is already 11 %.

28.6.3 Linearization and angular momentum

The state in which the link is at rest and straight, \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) and \(\dot\theta = 0\), is an equilibrium at every angle \(\theta\): every angle is equally good, because the rotation of the whole link is not resisted. Theorem 8.120 reads the equilibrium at \(\vek q = \vek 0\), and since \(\mat M\) and \(U\) do not contain \(\theta\), we may take \(\theta = 0\).

Corollary 28.22 Linearized equations of a link on a hub (linearizirane enačbe člena na pestu)

Let \(\tau = 0\) (a free joint) and \(\vek q = (\theta, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) as in corollary 28.21. Let \(\mat M_0\) be the matrix with cells \(J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}}\), \(\vek b^\T\), \(\vek b\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) and \(\mat K = \mathrm{diag}(0, \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}})\). Then \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, and every twice differentiable motion of the link satisfies the equation

\[\begin{bmatrix} J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} & \vek b^\T \\ \vek b & \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\ddot\theta \\ \ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \vek 0^\T \\ \vek 0 & \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\theta \\ \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\end{bmatrix} = \vek R , \tag{28.35}\]

where the remainder \(\vek R\) satisfies the estimate (8.108) of theorem 8.120.

Proof

We must show that the assumptions of theorem 8.120 hold, and read off \(\mat M_0\) and \(\mat K\).

  1. Lagrange's equations of the link are Lagrange's equations with the potential \(V = U\) (theorem 8.90), because at \(\tau = 0\) there are no non-elastic forces. \(U\) is a quadratic form, so arbitrarily differentiable, \(\nabla U(\vek 0) = \vek 0\). The elements of \(\mat M(\vek q)\) from (28.34) are polynomials in \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), hence continuously differentiable.
  2. The matrix of second derivatives of \(U\) at \(\vek 0\) is \(\mat K = \mathrm{diag}(0, \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}})\). \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is symmetric and positive definite, because \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \int_0^L EI\,(v'')^2\mathrm{d}x\) (lemma 5.76) and this is zero only for \(v'' \equiv 0\), that is \(v\) linear, and because of \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\) then \(v \equiv 0\), so \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) (independence of the \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\)). The matrix \(\mat K\) is therefore semidefinite, \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q \ge 0\).
  3. \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\) has the cells written by (28.34). For every column \(\vek\xi = (\xi_\theta, \vek\xi_{\mathrm{d}})\) we have \(\vek\xi^\T\mat M_0\vek\xi = J_{\mathrm{h}}\xi_\theta^2 + \int_0^L\rho A\,(\xi_\theta x + \vek\psi^\T\vek\xi_{\mathrm{d}})^2\mathrm{d}x\), which follows from the definitions of \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\), \(\vek b\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\). This is zero only if \(\xi_\theta x + \vek\psi^\T\vek\xi_{\mathrm{d}} \equiv 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\xi_\theta = 0\). The derivative at \(x = 0\) gives \(\xi_\theta + 0 = 0\), because \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}'(0) = 0\), then \(\vek\psi^\T\vek\xi_{\mathrm{d}} \equiv 0\) and \(\vek\xi_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\). So \(\mat M_0\) is positive definite. It is symmetric by construction.
  4. Theorem 8.120 now gives (28.35) with the remainder satisfying (8.108).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The form of \(\mat M_0\) and \(\mat K\) corresponds to the splitting of equation 5.159 in SHABANA, PDF p. 241.

The corollary has behind it a law we know, which is very useful in this system. The coordinate \(\theta\) is cyclic (ciklična), because \(T\) does not contain it (equation (28.34)). By corollary 8.112 the quantity \(p_\theta = \partial T/\partial\dot\theta\) is conserved at \(\tau = 0\).

Lemma 28.23 Conservation of angular momentum of a link on a hub (ohranitev kotne količine člena na pestu)

At \(\tau = 0\) the quantity \(p_\theta = (J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\dot\theta + \vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) in equations (28.32) and (28.33) is constant, and it is the angular momentum of the hub and the link about the axis \(O\). In the linear equations (28.35) \(p_\theta = (J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}})\dot\theta + \vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) and therefore \((J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}})\,\ddot\theta = -\vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\).

Proof

We must show that \(p_\theta\) is constant and that it is the angular momentum.

  1. The coordinate \(\theta\) is cyclic and \(Q_\theta = 0\), so by corollary 8.112 \(\mathrm{d}p_\theta/\mathrm{d}t = 0\) with \(p_\theta = \partial T/\partial\dot\theta\).
  2. From step 2 of the proof of corollary 28.21, \(\partial T/\partial\dot\theta = J_{\mathrm{h}}\dot\theta + \int_0^L\rho A\,\big(\dot\theta(x^2 + v^2) + x\dot v\big)\mathrm{d}x\). A point with position \((x, v)\) and velocity \((-\dot\theta v,\ \dot\theta x + \dot v)\) in the axes of the frame has angular momentum per unit mass \(x(\dot\theta x + \dot v) - v(-\dot\theta v) = \dot\theta(x^2 + v^2) + x\dot v\). The integral with \(\rho A\) is the angular momentum of the link, \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\dot\theta\) is the angular momentum of the hub.
  3. In linearization (corollary 28.22) \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\,\dot\theta\) are of higher order, so \(p_\theta = (J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}})\dot\theta + \vek b^\T\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\). If we differentiate this constant quantity with respect to time, we get \((J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}})\ddot\theta + \vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\), which is the first row of equation (28.35).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The physical reason we know from school: without an external torque about an axis, the angular momentum about that axis does not change. If the elastic coordinates of the link start to oscillate, \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} \ne \vek 0\), the hub must turn so that the sum stays the same. The hub and the link in a free joint are thus coupled.

28.6.4 Example: free vibration of a link on a hub

Example 28.24 First vibration of the link on a hub with \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) kg m² (prvo nihanje člena na pestu)

We look for the vibration with a free joint, \(\tau = 0\), without rigid motion, at \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) kg m². We take one elastic coordinate, the amplitude of the first cantilever mode (chapter 13, theorem 13.37, \(\omega_1' = 30{,}53\) rad/s), mass normalized, \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = 1\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} = \omega_1'^2\), \(\vek b = b_1 = 0{,}77827\) kg\(^{1/2}\) m, which corresponds to \(\int\rho A\,x\,\varphi_1\,\mathrm{d}x\) for this mode.

We write (28.35) with \(\tau = 0\): \(J_{\mathrm{tot}}\ddot\theta + b_1\ddot q = 0\) and \(b_1\ddot\theta + \ddot q + \omega_1'^2 q = 0\), where \(J_{\mathrm{tot}} = J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}} = 0{,}824\) kg m². We solve the first equation for \(\ddot\theta\) (lemma 28.23) and insert it into the second. We get

\[\Big(1 - \frac{b_1^2}{J_{\mathrm{tot}}}\Big)\ddot q + \omega_1'^2\,q = 0 . \tag{28.36}\]

The matrix \(\mat M_0\) is positive definite (corollary 28.22), so \(\det\mat M_0 = J_{\mathrm{tot}} - b_1^2 > 0\) and the factor in the parentheses lies between 0 and 1. At \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\), \(b_1^2/J_{\mathrm{tot}} = 0{,}7351\), the factor is \(0{,}2649\), and

\[\omega = \frac{\omega_1'}{\sqrt{0{,}2649}} = 59{,}31\ \text{rad/s} .\]

With 20 finite elements (chapter 12) it is \(59{,}147\) rad/s, with two modes \(59{,}150\). A single mode thus hits the frequency to 0,3 %.

Let us draw what this means.

In the extreme position the hub is turned by the angle \(\theta\), negative in the picture, and the bent link lies in blue in the turned frame, which is shown by a dashed line. The tip is displaced by \(v(L)\) from this line. The hub turns against the bending. From \(J_{\mathrm{tot}}\ddot\theta = -b_1\ddot q\) it follows for oscillation about rest that \(\theta = -b_1 q/J_{\mathrm{tot}}\) (the constants of integration are zero, because the hub and the link are on average at rest). With one mode this is \(-b_1/(J_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\varphi_1(L)) = -0{,}646\) rad per metre of tip bending, where \(\varphi_1(L) = 1{,}4618\) kg\(^{-1/2}\) is the tip bending of the mass-normalized mode. The whole model with 20 elements, in which \(\theta = -\vek b^\T\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}/J_{\mathrm{tot}}\) (lemma 28.23), gives \(-0{,}6373\) rad per metre. At a tip of 10 mm the turn of the hub is \(-6{,}4\) mrad (\(-0{,}37^\circ\)).

The example has two more limiting cases. At \(J_{\mathrm{h}} \to \infty\) the ratio \(b_1^2/J_{\mathrm{tot}} \to 0\) and \(\omega \to \omega_1'\): a heavy hub does not turn and the link vibrates as a cantilever. At \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) the factor is \(1 - b_1^2/J_{\mathrm{link}} = 1 - 0{,}9707 = 0{,}0293\) and \(\omega = 178{,}3\) rad/s with one mode, while the exact value is \(133{,}87\) rad/s (20 elements), because one mode is not enough for such strong coupling. 28.6.5 Interlacing of frequencies

The hub releases the rotation, and so we cannot get the free vibration simply from the cantilever: at \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) the first vibration is at \(59{,}15\) rad/s, the cantilever has \(30{,}53\) rad/s. The question is whether anything holds for all hubs. The answer is a theorem that follows from corollary 10.29: locking the hub is one constraint.

Theorem 28.25 Interlacing of the frequencies of a link (prepletanje frekvenc člena)

Let a link in a free joint be as in corollary 28.22 with \(n_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 1\) elastic coordinates, and let \(0 \le \omega_1 \le \omega_2 \le \dots \le \omega_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{d}+1}\) be the nonnegative roots of the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M_0)\), that is the frequencies of the free joint. Let \(\omega_1' \le \dots \le \omega_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{d}}'\) be the frequencies of the link with the hub locked, \(\theta = 0\), that is the eigenfrequencies of the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})\) (the cantilever). Then \(\omega_1 = 0\) and for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{d}}\)

\[\omega_{\mathrm{r}} \le \omega_{\mathrm{r}}' \le \omega_{\mathrm{r}+1} . \tag{28.37}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\theta = 0\) matches the constraint of corollary 10.29 with \(\mat G\vek u = (0, \vek u)\), and that the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\) agrees with \((\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})\).

  1. The pair \((\mat K, \mat M_0)\) has \(\mat K\) symmetric and \(\mat M_0\) symmetric and positive definite (corollary 28.22), so by theorem 10.13 the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) exist and theorem 10.26 holds. The column \(\vek e_\theta = (1, \vek 0)\) satisfies \(\mat K\vek e_\theta = \vek 0\), so \(R(\vek e_\theta) = 0\). Since \(\mat K\) is positive semidefinite, \(R \ge 0\) everywhere, and by theorem 10.26 \(\lambda_1 \le R(\vek e_\theta) = 0\), hence \(\lambda_1 = 0\) and \(\omega_1 = 0\). This is the rigid rotation of definition 10.22.
  2. The lock \(\theta = 0\) is a constraint \(\vek b_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\vek q = 0\) with the column \(\vek b_{\mathrm{c}} = \vek e_\theta\) and \(b_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{p}} = 1\) for the index \(p\) that corresponds to \(\theta\). The matrix \(\mat G\) of corollary 10.29 assigns to every column \(\vek u\) of \(n_{\mathrm{d}}\) numbers the column \((0, \vek u)\), because \(v_{\mathrm{p}} = -\frac{1}{b_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i} \ne \mathrm{p}}b_{\mathrm{i}}u_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), since all \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(i \ne p\) are zero.
  3. Then \(\mat K' = \mat G^\T\mat K\mat G = \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) and \(\mat M' = \mat G^\T\mat M_0\mat G = \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\), as block multiplication shows. The eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}'\) are therefore the squares of the cantilever frequencies, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}' = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}'^2\).
  4. Corollary 10.29 gives \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}' \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}+1}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{d}}\). Since the frequencies are nonnegative roots (all \(\lambda \ge 0\)), (28.37) follows.

Source: The theorem is ours. GR section 2.10.6, equations 2.230-2.240 (PDF pp. 135-136) has the theorem on constraints that we use (corollary 10.29). Nothing in the sources is about a link on a hub.

Now let us test it on four hubs.

Four axes, one for each hub inertia: \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\), \(0{,}2\), \(2\) and \(100\) kg m² (the axis \(\omega\) is shown by the scale in rad/s). First on each axis the orange circles: these are the cantilever frequencies, which are the same for every \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\), since the hub is at rest when locked. Then the blue dots: the free joint has one path more, so the first dot is at \(0\), the rigid rotation, and the next ones depend on \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\). At \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) the first nonzero blue dot is at \(133{,}87\) rad/s, between the circles \(30{,}53\) and \(191{,}31\). Inequality (28.37) with \(r = 1\) is \(\omega_1 = 0 \le \omega_1' = 30{,}53 \le \omega_2 = 133{,}87\), and with \(r = 2\) it is \(\omega_2 = 133{,}87 \le \omega_2' = 191{,}31 \le \omega_3 = 433{,}82\). For larger \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\) the dots move toward the circles, at \(100\) kg m² the difference is \(0{,}09\) rad/s. The same follows from the example: \(\omega_2 \to \omega_1'\) as \(J_{\mathrm{h}} \to \infty\).

The theorem says two things about the first vibration frequency of a link with a free joint. First, the cantilever frequency \(\omega_1' = 30{,}53\) rad/s is a lower bound for it: \(\omega_2 \ge \omega_1'\). Second, it does not exceed the second cantilever frequency: \(\omega_2 \le \omega_2' = 191{,}31\) rad/s. A reader might expect that a lock, which is a constraint, raises all frequencies, so \(\omega_1' \ge \omega_2\). At \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) kg m² this does not hold: \(\omega_2 = 59{,}15 > \omega_1' = 30{,}53\) rad/s. The constraint does raise the frequencies, but each cantilever frequency is compared with the free-joint number with the same index: \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} \le \omega_{\mathrm{r}}'\), which at \(r = 1\) means \(0 \le 30{,}53\). The first free-joint frequency is the rigid rotation with frequency zero, so the first nonzero free-joint frequency has the index 2, and (28.37) compares it with \(\omega_1'\) and \(\omega_2'\): \(\omega_1' \le \omega_2 \le \omega_2'\).

28.6.6 What linear elastodynamics says

SHABANA describes a road that was used for a long time: first we compute the link as rigid, from the rigid motion we get the inertial and reaction forces, and these we put into a problem of linear elasticity, which gives the deformations. It is called the linear theory of elastodynamics (linearna teorija elastodinamike) and says: in the equation for the reference coordinates we neglect the coupling \(\mat m_{\mathrm{rf}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{f}}\), and the matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and the vector \(\bar{\vek Q}_{\mathrm{r}}\) must not depend on the deformation (equations 5.160 and 5.161, PDF p. 241). The elastic equation is then solved with the known acceleration of the reference motion. The author warns that this assumption does not hold for fast, light systems (PDF p. 240). The example of the link on a hub shows how good it is.

Example 28.26 Linear elastodynamics of a link on a hub (linearna elastodinamika člena na pestu)

At \(\tau = 0\) let us write equations 5.161 for our link in the notation of this section. We neglect \(\vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) in the first equation of (28.35).

  1. After neglecting, the first equation is \((J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}})\ddot\theta = 0\), so the hub does not turn (\(\ddot\theta = 0\), and at rest \(\theta = 0\)).
  2. In the second equation \(\vek b\ddot\theta\) therefore cancels: \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\). This is the cantilever, its first frequency is \(\omega_1' = 30{,}53\) rad/s regardless of \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\).
  3. The coupled model (corollary 28.22) has for the same link the frequency \(\omega_2\) from the table: \(133{,}87\), \(59{,}15\), \(34{,}81\) and \(30{,}62\) rad/s at \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\), \(0{,}2\), \(2\) and \(100\) kg m².
  4. The error of the linear theory, relative to the coupled model, is \(77{,}2\ \%\), \(48{,}4\ \%\), \(12{,}3\ \%\) and \(0{,}3\ \%\).

The figure shows the error by the inertia of the hub.

First we draw the axes: horizontally \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\) on a logarithmic scale, because it changes over several orders of magnitude, vertically \(\omega\) in rad/s. The orange line is the answer of the linear theory, the same for all hubs, \(30{,}53\) rad/s. The blue curve is the coupled model: it falls from \(133{,}87\) rad/s at a small hub toward the orange line. On the curve are three points from the table, and the dashed segments show the difference to the orange line.

Why does the theory fail? The term \(\vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) that it dropped is the moment that the vibrating link gives to the hub. By lemma 28.23 the vibration of a link without torque must turn the hub. By how much, figure 28.15 says: \(0{,}6373\) rad per metre of tip bending at \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) kg m². The theory stays exact when the motion of the hub is prescribed in advance (a fast servo that fulfils \(\theta(t)\) exactly): then \(\ddot\theta\) is not an unknown but a known function, and equation (28.33) with the given \(\ddot\theta\) is precisely linear elastodynamics. Section 28.9 shows that the linearized equation (28.33) in the cantilever modes splits into one equation for each mode, driven by the force \(-b_{\mathrm{r}}\ddot\theta\), proportional to the acceleration of the hub. If the motor gives a torque and the link decides where the hub turns, the error remains. With a heavy hub the two models are equal, because \(\vek b^\T\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}/(J_{\mathrm{h}} + J_{\mathrm{link}}) \to 0\).

28.6.7 What we have shown

The equations of motion of the link (theorem 28.20) are Lagrange's equations with the kinetic energy \(T\) and the elastic energy \(U\). For a link on a hub they are equations (28.32) and (28.33), linearized in (28.35). The frequencies of a link with a free joint interlace with the frequencies of the cantilever (theorem 28.25, (28.37)) and differ from them by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1 - b_1^2/J_{\mathrm{tot}}}\) for one mode. Linear elastodynamics, which drops the coupling, computes the cantilever, and its error at \(J_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}2\) kg m² is 48 %. The next section returns to the term \(-\dot\theta^2\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) in (28.33): at constant rotation rate it gives a falling frequency and a predicted instability at \(\dot\theta = \omega_1'\), which is false, because we dropped the shortening of the axis.
