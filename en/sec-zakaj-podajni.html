Chapter 28 Flexible robot links

28.1 Why robot links bend

A robot arm is a chain of links (členi) joined by joints (sklepi) and driven by motors. The word link means one piece of a chain, and in the arm it is one stretch between two joints. Until now we treated each such piece as a rigid body (chapter 8, definition 8.38): the distances between its points do not change. In this chapter the link also bends while it turns. Such a link is called a flexible link (podajni člen), because under load it yields, it gives way, instead of staying straight. For a link on a hub the chapter will write equations of motion whose unknowns are the angle of rotation and the elastic coordinates, compute frequencies that the rigid model cannot predict, and show what steady rotation does to its stiffness. This first section answers two questions: when must we take the bending into account at all, and why can we not describe it with the tools of the chapter on deformation that we have used so far.

Take the link that we follow through the whole chapter: a steel strip of length \(L = 1\) m, \(b = 40\) mm wide perpendicular to the plane of motion and \(h = 6\) mm thick in the plane of motion, with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ as the steel of chapter 5. The strip is rigidly fixed at one end to the hub (pest), which for it is a clamp. The hub is the part to which the link is fixed and which the motor turns. The same name is used for the part of a wheel into which the axle is fixed. The hub turns about a vertical axis, the plane of motion is horizontal, and so the weight acts in the direction of \(b\), where the strip is stiff. This example is our own choice and not taken from a source, because no book in the library treats a robot link with numbers. We shall therefore vary the inertia of the hub later, so that the conclusions do not depend on our choice.

The model is the beam model of chapter 5 with three simplifications, which we name at once. First: the motion is planar, and a spatial link (SHABANA, section 6.6, PDF pp. 303-308) is only named. Second: the link is an Euler-Bernoulli beam (definition 5.14), cross sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis. Third: the axis does not lengthen. This is a simplification and not a fact. The axial stiffness \(EA/L\) of the strip is \(4{,}704 \cdot 10^7\) N/m, the bending stiffness of the tip \(3EI/L^3\) is \(423{,}4\) N/m, and the ratio is \(1{,}11 \cdot 10^5\). A force of \(10 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 423{,}4 = 4{,}23\) N bends the tip by 10 mm. If the same force acted along the axis, it would lengthen it by only \(4{,}23/(4{,}704 \cdot 10^{7}) \approx 9 \cdot 10^{-8}\) m, which is below \(10^{-7}\) of the length. The own weight in the direction of \(b\), with \(I = h\,b^3/12 = 32\,000\) mm⁴ (lemma 5.27, roles of the sides exchanged), lowers the tip by \(0{,}366\) mm by theorem 5.56, item 2. This deflection is perpendicular to the plane of motion, so the planar model does not contain it.

28.1.1 How much the link bends

First look at a link at rest, loaded. Let us draw it in the upper half of figure 28.1.

The hub is a circle with the axis as a dot pointing towards us. The link starts at it. The coordinate axes \(x\) and \(y\) at the bottom left give the directions: \(x\) runs along the link from the hub to the tip, \(y\) is perpendicular to it in the plane of motion, and the deflection \(v\) is the displacement of a point of the link in the direction \(y\). The dashed line is the axis of the link before loading, and the orange curve is the same axis after it. A rope pulls the tip of the link in the plane of motion with the force \(F = 10\) N. The force starts at the point of the tip and points down in the figure, because it pulls. The deflection \(v(L)\) is the distance between the dashed and the orange position of the tip, drawn with bars at both ends.

How large is the deflection? For a cantilever clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), theorem 5.56, item 1, equation (5.54), gives \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI)\). The bending stiffness of definition 5.36 is \(EI = 196 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 720 \cdot 10^{-12} = 141{,}12\) N m². The second moment of area \(I = b\,h^3/12 = 40 \cdot 6^3/12 = 720\) mm⁴ is by lemma 5.27, because \(h\) lies in the plane of bending. So

\[|v(L)| = \frac{10\ \text{N} \cdot (1\ \text{m})^3}{3 \cdot 141{,}12\ \text{N m}^2} = 23{,}62\ \text{mm} . \tag{28.1}\]

This is \(2{,}4\,\%\) of the length. The slope of the tip is by the same equation \(|v'(L)| = F L^2/(2EI) = 0{,}0354\) rad, at which the sine and the tangent of the angle differ from the angle by less than \(0{,}1\,\%\), so the small-displacement model of chapter 5 is accurate enough here.

28.1.2 When the static calculation is not enough

But the arm does not move slowly. How fast must a load change for the static calculation to fail? The link has a natural frequency. By theorem 13.37 \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\omega_0\) with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)}\) and \(x_1 = 1{,}8751\). With \(\rho A = 7800 \cdot 240 \cdot 10^{-6} = 1{,}872\) kg/m we get \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{141{,}12/1{,}872} = 8{,}682\) 1/s and

\[\omega_1 = 1{,}8751^2 \cdot 8{,}682 = 30{,}53\ \text{rad/s} , \qquad f_1 = \frac{\omega_1}{2\pi} = 4{,}86\ \text{Hz} , \qquad T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = 0{,}206\ \text{s} . \tag{28.2}\]

A freely vibrating link would oscillate almost five times a second. A movement of the arm that lasts about \(0{,}2\) s is therefore as long as one oscillation of the link.

What does this mean for the response? Take a force that rises linearly from zero to a final value \(Q_0\) in the time \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) and then stays constant. For a system with one degree of freedom without damping and with natural frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), lemma 9.70 gives the largest value of the response

\[q_{\max} = \frac{Q_0}{k}\Big(1 + \frac{|\sin\theta|}{\theta}\Big) , \qquad \theta = \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,t_{\mathrm{r}}}{2} , \tag{28.3}\]

where \(Q_0/k\) is the static value. For \(t_{\mathrm{r}} \to 0\) we have \(\theta \to 0\) and the expression tends to \(2\), as for a step (theorem 9.69). Inserting \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_1 = 30{,}53\) rad/s gives the ratio of the largest response to the static value: \(1{,}98\) for \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}02\) s, \(1{,}91\) for \(0{,}05\) s, \(1{,}65\) for \(0{,}1\) s and \(1{,}00\) for \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = T_1 = 0{,}206\) s, and again \(1{,}22\) for \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}29\) s. At \(t_{\mathrm{r}} = T_1\) the overshoot is zero only because \(\sin\theta = \sin\pi = 0\), for a longer \(t_{\mathrm{r}}\) it rises again. A reliable bound comes from \(|\sin\theta| \le 1\): the response exceeds the static value by at most \(1/\theta\) of that value, so by less than \(10\,\%\) for \(t_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0{,}66\) s (\(\theta \ge 10\)) and by less than \(5\,\%\) for \(t_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 1{,}31\) s (\(\theta \ge 20\)). The static calculation therefore holds when the load rises over at least a few oscillations of the link, for \(10\,\%\) accuracy over at least \(3{,}2\) oscillations. When it rises in less than one oscillation, the largest response is up to twice the static one. The values hold for the part of the response in the first mode. That each mode of the link really is a system with one degree of freedom under a load proportional to the acceleration of the hub we shall prove in section 28.6, and the higher modes add less (numbers in section 28.9).

28.1.3 The load that the hub produces

A load on the link can also arise without an external force, only because the hub accelerates. Let us draw it in the lower half of figure 28.1. The hub turns with the angular acceleration \(\alpha = 10\) rad/s², drawn as the curved arrow, counterclockwise. First we take the link as straight, as if it were rigid, and compute which forces its points need for this acceleration.

  1. A point of the link at distance \(s\) from the axis has by lemma 8.40, with \(\vek o = \vek 0\) and \(\vek r_0 = (s, 0)\), the position \(s\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\). We differentiate it twice in time (definition 8.69): the acceleration is \(s\,\big(\ddot\theta\,(-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta) - \dot\theta^2\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\big)\). At the instant when \(\dot\theta = 0\) it is perpendicular to the link and has magnitude \(s\,\alpha\).
  2. The part of the link from \(x\) to the end has the mass \(\rho A\,(L - x)\), and each of its points has the acceleration \(s\,\alpha\) upwards. In this calculation this part moves as a rigid body, so by theorem 8.74 (first equation of (8.62)) the resultant of the forces on it is the integral of the density times the acceleration, \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L \rho A\,\alpha\,s\,\mathrm{d}s = \rho A\,\alpha\,(L^2 - x^2)/2\), upwards. It is supplied by the rest of the link through the cross section \(x\), and the part from \(x\) to the end acts back with the same force downwards. The shear force (force of the larger \(x\) on the smaller) is therefore \(V(x) = -\rho A\,\alpha\,(L^2 - x^2)/2\) and its derivative is \(V' = \rho A\,\alpha\,x\). By the relation \(V' = -q\) (corollary 5.11) this is the same as if a distributed load \(q(x) = -\rho A\,\alpha\,x\) acted on the link, downwards. These are the arrows of the load. They end on the link, because they push, and they are proportional to \(x\), so the dashed line above joins them into a triangle.
  3. The moment with which the hub acts on the link (arrow \(M_0\)) is, by the second equation of (8.62) for the whole link as a rigid body, the moment of all these forces about the axis, \(M_0 = \int_0^L s \cdot \rho A\,\alpha\,s\,\mathrm{d}s = \rho A\,\alpha\,L^3/3\). The moment of inertia of the link about the axis is by definition 8.73 \(J_{\mathrm{link}} = \int_0^L \rho A\,s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \rho A\,L^3/3 = 0{,}624\) kg m², so \(M_0 = J_{\mathrm{link}}\,\alpha = 6{,}24\) N m. This is the moment that the hub must give to a rigid link.
\[M_0 = \frac{\rho A\,\alpha\,L^3}{3} = 1{,}872 \cdot 10 \cdot \frac{1}{3}\ \text{N m} = 6{,}24\ \text{N m} . \tag{28.4}\]

Now this moment acts on the link as bending. By theorem 5.35 \(M = EI\,v''\) and by theorem 5.15 the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -y\,v''\) (equation (5.17) with \(u' = 0\)). At the root it is largest at the edge of the strip, \(y = h/2 = 3\) mm:

\[|\varepsilon_{\max}| = \frac{M_0\,h/2}{EI} = \frac{6{,}24 \cdot 0{,}003}{141{,}12} = 1{,}327 \cdot 10^{-4} . \tag{28.5}\]

By theorem 5.35 the stress at the root is \(M_0\,(h/2)/I = 26{,}0\) MPa. We get the deflection from the deflection curve equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) (theorem 5.42) with the load \(q = -\rho A\,\alpha\,x\), downwards. The boundary conditions are the clamp, \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\), and the free end, \(v''(L) = v'''(L) = 0\) (zero moment and shear force). Four integrations give the deflection curve

\[v(x) = -\frac{\rho A\,\alpha}{120\,EI}\,\big(x^5 - 10\,L^2 x^3 + 20\,L^3 x^2\big) , \qquad v(L) = -\frac{11\,\rho A\,\alpha\,L^5}{120\,EI} = -12{,}16\ \text{mm} , \tag{28.6}\]

which we check. At \(x = 0\) both \(v\) and \(v'\) are zero, because the bracket has no terms with \(x^0\) and \(x^1\). The derivatives are \(v'' = -\dfrac{\rho A\alpha}{120\,EI}\,(20\,x^3 - 60\,L^2 x + 40\,L^3)\) and \(v''' = -\dfrac{\rho A\alpha}{120\,EI}\,(60\,x^2 - 60\,L^2)\). At \(x = L\) both are zero, and at \(x = 0\) we have \(EI\,v''(0) = -\rho A\,\alpha\,L^3/3 = -M_0\) from equation (28.4). The fourth derivative is \(EI\,v'''' = -\rho A\,\alpha\,x = q\). The deflection is \(1{,}2\,\%\) of the length.

The calculation was simplified: we computed the load with a straight and rigid link. We left out two things. The first is the acceleration of the bending \(\ddot v\). The deflection \(12{,}16\) mm is the static response to a load that has acted for a long time. If the acceleration of the hub is switched on faster than one oscillation of the link, by section 28.1.2 the deflection can nearly double, to about \(24\) mm. The second is the rotation of the tip. The real link bends and its tip turns by the slope \(v'(L) = \rho A\alpha L^4/(8EI) = 0{,}0166\) rad, \(0{,}95^\circ\). By this angle the acceleration of the tip is turned relative to the bent axis of the link. The load therefore gets an axial component of \(\sin 0{,}0166 \approx 1{,}7\,\%\) of the transverse load, while the transverse load decreases only by \(1 - \cos 0{,}0166 = 1{,}4 \cdot 10^{-4}\) of its value. This suffices for the estimate in this section. The feedback of the bending on the motion will be the main subject of sections 28.4 to 28.6.

The meaning of the two numbers: a deflection of 12 mm and a strain of \(1{,}3 \cdot 10^{-4}\) are small, as the beam model requires (definition 5.14). The angle through which the hub turns the link, however, can be large: a robot arm turns by tens of degrees and more in one move, and in this chapter we also compute with \(30^\circ\). The link therefore performs two motions at once. It turns a lot as a whole and bends a little. Let us see what this does to the description of displacements from the chapter on deformation, which measures displacements from the position before loading in fixed axes.

28.1.4 The false stretch of a rotation

To begin with, take without bending a square \(OABC\) with side \(L\) and the point \(O\) on the axis of the hub, which the hub turns through the angle \(\theta\) as a rigid body. Let us draw it in figure 28.2, with \(\theta = 30^\circ\).

The dashed square is the position before the motion and the orange one the same plate after the turn. The angle \(\theta\) is the angle between \(OA\) and \(OA'\). Every point with position vector \(\vek r\) from \(O\) goes to \(\mat R(\theta)\,\vek r\) (lemma 8.40), so its displacement is \(\vek u = (\mat R(\theta) - \mat I)\,\vek r\), where \(\mat I\) is the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix. In the figure this is the arrow from \(A\) to \(A'\) and from \(C\) to \(C'\). The plate has not deformed, every segment has the same length after the turn. What does the small strain tensor say about this displacement?

Lemma 28.1 False stretch of a rotation (lažni raztezek zasuka)

Let \(\theta\) be an angle and \(\mat R(\theta)\) the rotation matrix in the plane \(xy\) (lemma 8.40). A rigid rotation about the point \(O\) is the displacement field \(\vek u = (\mat R(\theta) - \mat I)\,\vek r\) in the plane \(xy\), with \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(\vek r\) the position vector from \(O\). We write the matrices \(\mat H\), \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\mat E\) only with their elements in the plane \(xy\), the other elements are zero, and \(\mat I\) is the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix. Then:

  1. the displacement gradient is \(\mat H = \mat R(\theta) - \mat I\) at every point,
  2. the small strain tensor (definition 1.115) is in the plane \(\ten\varepsilon = (\cos\theta - 1)\,\mat I\), so the small strain tensor assigns to a fibre in every direction \(\vek n\) in the plane the strain \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \cos\theta - 1\),
  3. the Green-Lagrange strain tensor (definition 1.112) is \(\mat E = \mat 0\).
Proof

We must show three items: the gradient is the matrix that writes the field, and then we compute both tensors from it.

  1. The components of the field are \(u_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} (R_{\mathrm{ij}} - \delta_{\mathrm{ij}})\,x_{\mathrm{j}}\) with constant \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = R_{\mathrm{ij}} - \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (the partial derivative of a linear function is its coefficient). Hence \(\mat H = \mat R - \mat I\) (definition 1.109).
  2. By definition 1.115, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T) = \tfrac12(\mat R + \mat R^\T) - \mat I\). For \(\mat R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\) we have \(\mat R + \mat R^\T = 2\cos\theta\,\mat I\) (the off-diagonal elements cancel), so \(\ten\varepsilon = (\cos\theta - 1)\,\mat I\). With the small strain tensor the strain of a fibre in direction \(\vek n\) is approximated by \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) (theorem 1.114 and corollary 1.122), and this is \((\cos\theta - 1)\,\vek n^\T\vek n = \cos\theta - 1\), because \(\vek n\) is a unit vector.
  3. By definition 1.110, \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \mat R\). By definition 1.112, \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat R^\T\mat R - \mat I)\). The rotation matrix is orthogonal, \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\) (lemma 2.40), which we also see directly from the elements \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\) (lemma 8.10, item 1) on the diagonal and \(-\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\) off it. Hence \(\mat E = \mat 0\).

Source: The lemma keeps the promise of chapter 1 at figure 1.465 and theorem 1.134. The proof is not from the sources.

What does this mean in the figure? The small strain tensor claims that every fibre has shortened by \(1 - \cos\theta\). At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) we have \(\cos\theta - 1 = -0{,}134\). According to the tensor the fibre \(OA'\) would have the length \((1 + \varepsilon)\,L = 0{,}866\,L\), while the true length is \(L\): we did not squeeze the plate, we only turned it. The same calculation in numbers for \(\theta = 30^\circ\): \(\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}134 & -0{,}5 \\ 0{,}5 & -0{,}134 \end{bmatrix}\), its symmetric part is \(-0{,}134\,\mat I\), as the lemma says, and the Green-Lagrange tensor is \(\mat 0\). Hooke's law (chapter 3) would assign to a fibre in uniaxial stress from this strain the stress \(E \cdot 0{,}134 = 26{,}3\) GPa, which is a thousand times the stress of \(26\) MPa from the true bending that we computed above.

At which angle is the false strain larger than the true one? For the true strain at the root \(\varepsilon = 1{,}327 \cdot 10^{-4}\) from equation (28.5) the following corollary answers.

Corollary 28.2 Angle at which the false strain dominates (meja zasuka, pri kateri lažna deformacija prevlada)

Let \(0 < \varepsilon < 2\) and \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\). The false strain \(|\cos\theta - 1| = 1 - \cos\theta\) of lemma 28.1 is less than or equal to \(\varepsilon\) exactly when \(\theta \le \theta_\varepsilon\), where \(\theta_\varepsilon \in (0, \pi)\) is the unique angle with \(\cos\theta_\varepsilon = 1 - \varepsilon\).

Proof

We must show that the angle \(\theta_\varepsilon\) exists and that the comparison \(1 - \cos\theta \le \varepsilon\) means exactly \(\theta \le \theta_\varepsilon\).

  1. The condition \(1 - \cos\theta \le \varepsilon\) is equivalent to \(\cos\theta \ge 1 - \varepsilon\).
  2. The cosine is differentiable (theorem 8.12), so by lemma 1.79 it is continuous, and by lemma 8.10 strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\), with \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\) (items 2 and 3). Since \(-1 < 1 - \varepsilon < 1\), by the intermediate value theorem 2.57 there is \(\theta_\varepsilon \in (0, \pi)\) with \(\cos\theta_\varepsilon = 1 - \varepsilon\), and it is unique by strict monotonicity.
  3. Since the cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\), \(\cos\theta \ge \cos\theta_\varepsilon\) holds exactly for \(\theta \le \theta_\varepsilon\).

Source: The corollary is ours. The proof is not from the sources.

For \(\varepsilon = 1{,}327 \cdot 10^{-4}\) we get \(\theta_\varepsilon = 0{,}01629\) rad \(= 0{,}933^\circ\). For a turn by more than \(0{,}93^\circ\) the false strain is therefore larger than the true one, and at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) it is three orders of magnitude larger. The values for a few angles are:

\(\theta\) \(0{,}01\) rad \(0{,}1\) rad \(30^\circ\) \(90^\circ\)
\(\cos\theta - 1\) \(-5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\) \(-5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-3}\) \(-0{,}134\) \(-1\)

At \(90^\circ\) the small strain tensor would claim that all fibres have length zero.

Does this contradict theorem 1.134, which says that a field \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) with an antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) has a zero small strain tensor? It does not, and this very example shows where the theorem holds. We split the rotation matrix into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts,

\[\mat R - \mat I = (\cos\theta - 1)\,\mat I + \sin\theta\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{28.7}\]

where the first term is not antisymmetric if \(\theta \ne 0\). The matrix \(\mat R - \mat I\) is therefore not of the form \(\mat W\) of the theorem, so the theorem cannot be applied to a finite rotation. The theorem stays true, only its hypothesis is not met here. For a small angle the first term is small: at \(\theta = 0{,}01\) it is \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\), which is \(\theta^2/2\) at the digits shown. For the square plate of chapter 1 (figure 1.465) the angle was \(s = 0{,}001\) and the error \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\), and there the theorem was good enough. For our link the true strain is \(10^{-4}\), so the error \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\) at \(0{,}57^\circ\) is already more than a third of the true strain, and at \(30^\circ\) it is a thousand times larger.

The consequence for the link is this. We must not measure the displacement of a turned link in fixed axes and compute small strains from it, because the false stretch would swamp the true strain, which is three orders of magnitude smaller. If instead we split the displacement into a large rotation, described by an angle and a position (lemma 8.40), and a small elastic displacement, measured in axes that turn with the link, the gradient of this displacement will be small and the strain will make sense, as we shall prove in the next section. The source also uses this idea: it describes the position and orientation of a chosen point of the body with one group of coordinates and the deformation relative to it with another (SHABANA, PDF pp. 199-200). Rods, beams, plates and shells have an infinite number of degrees of freedom (SHABANA, PDF p. 200), so we write the deformation with a finite number of coordinates by the Ritz method (definition 6.32, SHABANA, PDF p. 201). The axes that turn with the link and the coordinates in them are defined in section 28.2.
