Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

Chapter 25Lecture: Hertz contact and rolling bearings

1 How a support answers a force: from a row of springs to a half-space

We press on one single point. How far around is it felt? And what happens right under the force? We look for the answer on two supports at once, drawn side by side.

On the left we first draw the ground and above it a straight line, where the beam lies as long as nobody loads it. Between the ground and the beam we put a row of springs, each on its own. We press on the middle with the force \(P\). What happens? The beam bends: under the force it sinks deepest, a little further away it is back on the straight line. The influence of the force reaches only a few lengths that we call \(1/\beta\). For the steel beam of the book this length is \(0.538\) m.

On the right, instead of springs, we take a solid body, for example a piece of steel, and press on it with the same force. Now the surface sinks everywhere, steeper and steeper toward the force. The curve is \(w = C/r\) and right under the force it has no bottom, so we cut it off there. Let us compare the same distance from the force on both sides. On the left the spring there is hardly pressed, on the right the surface is still sunk by \(w\).

So the claim of this part of the lecture is this. A row of independent springs answers a force only where the beam carries the force to it, over a length \(1/\beta\) that the beam and the springs set together. A solid body sinks around the force as well, like \(1/r\), and right under a point force the answer is infinite. That is why a real contact cannot be a point, it must be a patch.

1.1 Why does a beam on springs sink only near the force?

What do we know from experience about one single spring? Press it twice as far and it pushes back twice as hard. That is a measured fact, Hooke's law for a spring, and everything that follows stands on it.

We draw the ground and the straight position of the beam. We put the springs so close together that we no longer tell them apart. The force \(P\) presses on the beam. The beam bends, and the deflection \(v\) is the distance between the straight position and the beam. Each spring the beam presses pushes back with the force \(f = -k\,v\) per unit length ((25.1)). The number \(k\) is the foundation modulus (modul podlage). The arrow is longest under the force, because the deflection is largest there.

But why do several springs carry the force and not only the one under it? Because the beam resists bending and shares the force among the neighbours: the arrows of the springs are there to the left and right of the force too. A stiff beam on soft springs spreads it far, a soft beam on stiff springs spreads it little. One single length records this trade, \(1/\beta\) of the first figure, with \(k = 4\,EI\,\beta^4\). More on the model in the book: 25.1, 25.1.

Assumption: the springs are independent. The force of a spring depends only on the deflection at its own point, not on its neighbours. Why is this right here? For a rail on sleepers each sleeper sits on its own gravel. When does it fail? For any solid support, as we will see below. What does it do to the result? Far from the force the surface does not sink at all in the model, on the left of the first figure the spring there is hardly pressed, while a solid body would be sunk there by \(C/r\).

1.2 Why exactly this equation?

How do we get from springs to an equation for the deflection? We cut out of the beam a piece between the sections \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). Below it is the foundation. From above presses the load \(q\), from below the springs with \(-k\,v\). We have cut the piece off, so we also draw the shear forces with which its neighbours held it.

What do we know about the piece? It is at rest. Newton's law says that the forces on a body at rest add up to zero. That is all there is in the equation (25.2): the shear forces at the two cuts differ by exactly what the load and the springs give together on the piece. When we add the beam law, \(M = EI\,v''\), we get for every point

\[EI\,v'''' + k\,v = q .\]

The first term is the resistance of the beam to bending, the second the resistance of the springs. The idea of the proof is that equilibrium holds for every piece between two sections, also an arbitrarily short one, and so the equation holds at every point. In the book: 25.2.

1.3 How far does the influence of the force reach?

How far from the force does the beam still sink? We solve the equation for a long beam with one force. We expect a deflection that is largest under the force and dies out with distance, as on the first figure. First we draw the horizontal axis. On it we measure the distance from the force in units of \(1/\beta = 0.538\) m. Then the deflection. The solution is (25.4) and it has two parts. The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta|x|}\) makes the deflection die out. The factor \(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta|x|\) is a wave, which makes the curve cross the axis and rise a little beyond it. At \(P = 10\) kN the deflection under the force is \(9.298\) mm. At \(t = \pi\), that is \(1.69\) m from the force, only \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0.0432\) of the deflection is left. In the book: 25.3.

And this small rise? Let us enlarge it 20 times, to the right of the force. The curve goes above the axis at the zeros \(3\pi/4\) and \(7\pi/4\). In the shaded zone \(v > 0\), the spring is stretched and pulls the beam down.

Assumption: the foundation is two-sided. The springs of the model not only push, in the shaded zone they also pull. Why do we accept it? The pull is small: the highest point of the curve is \(0.4018\) mm above the axis, \(1.689\) m from the force, and there the foundation pulls with \(401.8\) N/m. When does it fail? A beam that merely lies on the ground lifts off there, since the ground cannot pull. What does it mean for the result? For a beam that only lies on the ground the solution is right only for \(P < 1524\) N, when the self-weight covers the pull. In the book: 25.5.

Assumption: the beam is infinite. How long must a real beam be for this not to matter? We draw the zero line and on it the grey curve of the infinite beam, which we already know. The blue curve is a beam of length \(4\) m with free ends: the deflection under the force is \(9.3142\) mm instead of \(9.2979\) mm, a difference of \(0.18\) %. The orange curve is a beam of length \(1\) m: \(11.371\) mm, \(22\) % more. A beam \(7.4\) lengths \(1/\beta\) long is therefore almost infinite, a beam \(1.9\) lengths long is not. The dashed lines show the other extreme, a rigid board that does not bend at all: \(-2.5\) mm at \(4\) m and \(-10\) mm at \(1\) m. The short beam is closer to the board than to the infinite beam. These are two computed points, not a general bound.

1.4 By numbers: the model itself tells where it stops being valid

Take the steel beam of the book: \(EI = 20\,906.7\) N m\(^2\), \(k = 1.0\cdot 10^6\) N/m\(^2\), \(P = 10\) kN. Look at the third panel, the bending moment. It is largest under the force, \(M(0) = 1344.4\) N m, and has its zero at \(x = 0.422\) m. From \(M(0)\) we get the bending stress \(252\) MPa. The yield stress of the steel is \(220\) MPa.

Assumption: the material is linear. What does this tell us? At \(10\) kN the steel would already yield under the force, where the moment is largest, so the linear model no longer holds there. Since the stress is proportional to the force, it holds up to \(P = 8.73\) kN. The shape on the figure does not depend on \(P\), so we can keep reading it. This view adds an important habit: we check every result with numbers against the assumptions it came from.

1.5 Why is a real support not a row of springs?

Press a fist into a mattress. What do we see? The mattress sinks not only under the fist but around it too. In a solid body each bit of material is tied to its neighbours. When we push one point, it drags its surroundings along.

Let us draw it. The surface is a horizontal line, the body is below it. We call such a body a half-space (polprostor), because we cut space in half with a plane and kept one half. The force \(F\) presses on the point \(O\), and the axis \(z\) goes from there into the body. The surface sinks along the curve \(w = C/r\), which we draw orange. What does \(1/r\) mean? We draw two rectangles with one corner at \(O\) and the opposite corner on the curve. They have the same area \(C\). Four times further away, the deflection is four times smaller. For steel and the force \(1000\) N the deflection is \(1.478\) µm at a distance of \(1\) mm and \(0.148\) µm at \(10\) mm. In the book: 25.4, 25.16.

Assumption: the body is a half-space. Why is this right? The body below the surface is much larger than the region where we press, so its edge far away has no influence. When does it fail? For a thin plate or near an edge. What does it do to the result? The size of the body does not enter the result at all.

Assumption: the strains are small. Linear theory requires displacement derivatives below about \(0.001\). On the surface the largest derivative is \(C/r^2\), the steepness of the deflection curve. At \(r = 1\) mm it is \(1.5\cdot 10^{-3}\), at \(0.1\) mm already \(0.148\). So for our force the solution holds for \(r \gtrsim 1.2\) mm, and not closer to the force.

1.6 Why is the stress under the force infinite?

How large is the stress right under the force? Before we compute, let us think how large an area the force has to pass through. Take the simplest case, where the force does not press on a point but along a long line, like a cylinder on a table. We call such a force a line force (linijska sila) \(F'\), in newtons per metre. We draw the surface and through the point \(O\) the axis \(y\) into the body. At \(O\) presses the force \(F'\). A point of the body is described by the distance \(r\) and the angle \(\theta\).

Around \(O\) we draw a half-circle of radius \(a\). The half-circle of material is at rest. So the rest of the body must act on it with stresses on the arc that together give exactly \(F'\) upward. The arc is \(\pi a\) long. What happens if we halve the radius? The same force now crosses an arc half as long. Stress is force per area, so it doubles. The stress therefore grows like \(1/r\).

Let \(a\) go to zero. The arc becomes arbitrarily short, while the force on it stays \(F'\). So the stress is unbounded. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm the pressure under the force, on the axis \(y\), is \(63.7\) MPa at a depth of \(1\) mm, \(636.6\) MPa at \(0.1\) mm and \(6366\) MPa at \(0.01\) mm. In the book: 25.11, 25.13.

Assumption: the force acts on a line with no width. This is a model, not a mistake: a force on a line means a force on zero area. When does it hold? Away from the line. For steel and \(F' = 100\) N/mm it holds at depths \(y \geq 0.30\) mm, closer the stress exceeds the limit of linear theory.

1.7 Geometric view: circles of equal stress

We can see the same \(1/r\) law as a picture. We draw the surface with the force at \(O\). Through \(O\) we draw three circles that touch the surface at \(O\), with diameters \(1\), \(2\) and \(4\) mm. On each the stress is the same everywhere, and its values are \(63.66\), \(31.83\) and \(15.92\) MPa. A circle twice as large, half the stress. This view adds a picture we can draw by hand. On the right we see that the stress at depth \(a\) is gathered under the force, at \(x = a\) a quarter is left.

1.8 Historical view: rails on sleepers

Why did engineers accept springs at all? Look again at the row of springs. When railways were built, Winkler took the rail as a continuous beam on rigid supports. But sleepers are not rigid, the gravel sinks under them. Zimmermann therefore simplified Winkler's calculation of a beam on an elastic foundation (elastična podlaga) with tables and applied it to the sleepers. Since at the usual sleeper spacing the load spreads over several sleepers, he replaced the individual elastic supports with a continuous foundation. A row of springs of stiffness \(K_{\mathrm{t}}\) at spacing \(s\) has \(k = K_{\mathrm{t}}/s\). This view adds the reason for the model: sleepers really are separate supports.

1.9 Back up: a force never lands on a point

Now let us return to the first figure. From the half-circle we know that under a point force the stress is unbounded, and so is the deflection of the surface, since \(C/r\) grows past every bound as \(r\) goes to zero. No real body shows this. So what does it do? The two bodies flatten at the contact until the patch is large enough to carry the force at a finite pressure. How large that patch is, is the question of the next part.

We do not throw away the springs on the left either. The bearing will again be a spring for the shaft, only not one that pushes in proportion to the displacement.

1.10 By hand: a fist in a mattress

For anyone who wants the whole story in one minute, let us press with a fist once more, once into a mattress of separate springs and once into foam. With separate springs only the spring under the fist is pressed, its neighbour stays as it was, unless a board ties them together, and that board is our beam. With foam the surroundings sink too. This view adds what the word independent means, and it is exactly what the comparison at the same distance shows: almost nothing on the left, still \(w\) on the right.

2 Hertz contact: the patch that the force decides

We press a steel ball of diameter \(12.7\) mm onto a steel plate, first with a force of \(100\) N, then with \(1000\) N. How large is the circle in which they touch? How high is the pressure in it? And why does a ten times larger force not give a ten times larger circle? One picture carries the answer.

Let us draw the left panel. At the bottom is the flat body, on it the sphere of radius \(R\), touching it at one single point \(O\). From \(O\) we measure horizontally the distance \(r\). Between the sphere and the plate is the gap \(g_0(r)\), which grows with the distance. Now we press. On the right is the plate after loading: it has sunk under the ball. The sphere has flattened at the bottom and its centre has moved down by the approach \(h\) (približevanje). In the contact circle of radius \(a\) the two surfaces are one surface, outside it the gap opens again. All of this is done by the force \(F\).

The claim in one sentence: the ball and the plate flatten just enough for the circle to be large enough. Its radius grows as \(a^3 = 3FR/(4E^*)\), the approach is \(h = a^2/R\), and therefore

\[F = \tfrac43\,E^*\sqrt R\;h^{3/2} .\]

This is a spring that gets stiffer as we press it (25.27, (25.25)). Why exactly so?

2.1 Why a patch and not a point?

What would happen if the ball really touched the plate at one single point? In the previous part we saw what a force at one single point of a half-space does: the sink under it is infinite and so is the pressure. No steel can take that. So the surfaces give way until they share a circle. How large is this circle? We do not know in advance. The force itself decides it. This is what makes contact different from everything we did before: for the bar, the beam and the tube we knew where the force acts. Here the place where it acts is itself an unknown. In the book: 25.6.

2.2 What must hold in the contact and outside it?

What can we say about two bodies that touch, before we compute anything? Four plain requirements, all visible on the right panel. First, the bodies do not pass through each other: the gap is \(g \ge 0\) everywhere. Second, the contact does not pull, surfaces that are not glued can only push: the pressure is \(p \ge 0\). Third, there is pressure only where they touch: in the circle \(g = 0\), outside \(p = 0\), so \(g\,p = 0\) everywhere. Fourth, the pressure adds up to the force \(F\). None of this is theory. Two solid bodies cannot be put in the same place and an unglued surface does not pull. These are the contact conditions (stični pogoji) of 25.19.

Here the first assumption enters: no friction, the force in the contact is normal to the surface. Why may we take it? Hertz assumed that the load is normal to the surface, and our force is: it acts along the axis, normal to the plate. When does it fail? When the contact also carries a force along the surface, that is friction. What does it do to the result? For the sphere the book does not put a number on it.

Note one more thing. We will show that the Hertz solution, the circle with the pressure in it, meets all four requirements. That it is the only one that meets them, neither the book nor we prove (25.19, the source note under 25.27).

2.3 How far must the ball come closer?

Back to the left panel. How high is the sphere above the plate at the distance \(r\)? The right triangle with hypotenuse \(R\) and legs \(r\) and \(R - g_0\) gives \(g_0 = R - \sqrt{R^2 - r^2}\). For \(r\) much smaller than \(R\) this is

\[g_0(r) \approx \frac{r^2}{2R} ,\]

the gap is a parabola ((25.22)). Twice as far from \(O\), four times as high. This is the geometry of a circle and nothing else.

What does this say about the approach? At the edge of the circle the gap is \(a^2/(2R)\). It must vanish, so the sphere must come down at least that far, and a little more, because the surface also gives way at the edge and uses up part of the motion there. The order of magnitude is therefore \(h \sim a^2/R\). The exact factor comes at the end.

Here the second assumption enters: a parabola instead of the circle. Why may it? At \(1000\) N the true gap at the edge of the contact is \(9.8545\ \mu\)m, the parabola gives \(9.8468\ \mu\)m, which is \(0.078\ \%\) less. When does it fail? When \(a\) is comparable with \(R\). What does it do to the result? The relative error is of order \(a^2/(4R^2)\), here \(7.8\cdot 10^{-4}\).

2.4 Why does the material resist, and how much?

The ball has to come down, and for that the material under it has to give way. How much does it give way under a given pressure? Let us first take one single force.

The force \(F\) presses on the surface of the half-space and it gives way along the curve \(w = C/r\): four times as far from the force, a quarter of the sink. The pressure on a circle we cut into small patches. Each carries a small force, the pressure times its area, and each makes the surface give way along the same curve. The sinks add up (25.17). How large is the sum? A pressure \(p\) on a circle of radius \(a\) is a force of order \(p\,a^2\) at a distance of order \(a\), so the sink is of order \(p\,a^2/(E^*a) = p\,a/E^*\). Claim 2 of 25.26 gives it exactly: at the centre \(u(0) = \pi p_0 a/(2E^*)\).

There are two bodies, the plate and the ball. What do we do with both? By Newton's third law they get the same pressure, each gives way as a half-space and their sinks add. So one modulus is enough,

\[\frac1{E^*} = \frac{1 - \nu_{\mathrm{A}}^2}{E_{\mathrm{A}}} + \frac{1 - \nu_{\mathrm{B}}^2}{E_{\mathrm{B}}} ,\]

the effective modulus (učinkoviti modul, (25.24)). The gaps add as well, so one radius is enough, \(1/R = 1/R_{\mathrm{A}} + 1/R_{\mathrm{B}}\) ((25.23)). For our ball on a plane these are \(R = 6.35\) mm and \(E^* = 107.692\) GPa.

Here the third assumption enters: near the contact each body is a half-space. Is the circle really that small?

Let us draw the ball in true proportion, with radius \(R = 6.35\) mm and modulus \(E^*\). Where is the contact? This short orange line at the bottom is the contact circle at \(1000\) N, to scale: \(a/R = 0.056\). On the right we enlarge the frame around it and look from above, we will come back to this zoom. Why is the assumption right? The radius of the ball is almost eighteen times the radius of the contact, so seen from the contact the ball and the plate reach as far as if they were infinite. When does it fail? In a conforming contact, for example a ball in a groove of nearly its own radius (25.18). What does it do to the result? The shape of the bodies far from the contact drops out, only \(R\) and \(E^*\) remain.

And the fourth: linear elasticity. The strains under the contact are of order \(p_0/E\), at \(1000\) N this is \(3818/196\,000 = 0.019\), at \(100\) N \(0.009\). Linear theory assumes the order \(10^{-3}\). This error is more than an order of magnitude larger than the error of the parabola, and the book therefore takes the elastic result as an approximation. More than that: the book's soft steel with yield stress \(220\) MPa starts to yield already at \(F = 0.80\) N (25.60). Rolling bearings are therefore made of steel hardened to \(61\) to \(65\) Rockwell C, whose yield stress our sources do not give.

2.5 Why a semi-ellipsoid of pressure?

Now we put both together. The gap is a parabola. For it to be zero in the circle, the sink of both surfaces there must be exactly \(u = h - r^2/(2R)\), also a parabola. Which pressure gives such a sink?

On the left is the surface. On it we put a pressure that is largest in the middle, \(p_0\), and falls to zero at the edge, the semi-ellipsoid \(p_0\sqrt{1 - r^2/a^2}\). Beside it, for comparison, is the cylinder of height \(p_0\) and diameter \(2a\). The volume of the semi-ellipsoid is two thirds of the cylinder, so the force is \(F = \tfrac23\,p_0\,\pi a^2\) (claim 1 of 25.26).

What does this pressure do to the surface? On the right, the axes carry the distance \(r/a\) and the sink divided by \(h\). The blue curve is the sink. In the contact it is a parabola from \(1\) in the middle to \(\tfrac12\) at the edge, exactly the one we need. And a uniform pressure? Its sink falls at the edge only to \(2/\pi = 0.6366\) of the central one, the blackwhite dot. At half the radius such a pressure would leave a gap of \(0.0251\,u(0)\), so the surfaces would not touch inside the contact. A uniform pressure presses the edge too hard and the middle too little.

And outside the circle? The orange curve is the gap. It stays above zero for every \(r > a\), which the book proves in step 4 of the proof of 25.27. The requirements \(g \ge 0\), \(p \ge 0\) and \(g\,p = 0\) are thus met.

The idea of the proof: in polar coordinates around a point of the surface the integral for the sink falls apart into areas of half circles. The book proves claims 1, 2 and 4, claim 3, the parabola in the circle, is cited and checked numerically to ten digits at \(r/a = 0\), \(0.3\), \(0.6\), \(0.9\) and \(0.99\). In the book: 25.26.

2.6 Where does the exponent 3/2 come from?

We have the parabola of the gap and the parabola of the sink. How do we get the force from them? We put them next to each other and require their sum to be zero in the circle:

\[\frac{r^2}{2R} + \frac{\pi p_0}{4E^*a}\,(2a^2 - r^2) - h = 0 .\]

It holds for every \(r\) in the circle, so each term must vanish by itself. The \(r^2\) term gives the height of the pressure \(p_0 = 2E^*a/(\pi R)\). The constant term gives the approach \(h = a^2/R\). The force, the volume of the semi-ellipsoid, gives \(F = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}p_0a^2 = 4E^*a^3/(3R)\). Three lines:

\[F \propto a^3 , \qquad h \propto a^2 , \qquad F \propto h^{3/2} .\]

In the book: 25.27, steps 1 to 3 and 5.

The second line can be read straight off the pressure picture. In the middle there is no gap, so the sink there equals the approach, \(u(0) = h\). At the edge the sink is half of it, \(h/2\). The gap at the edge, \(a^2/(2R)\), must cover the difference \(h - h/2\), so \(h = a^2/R\). This is the exact factor we left open before.

Why \(3/2\) and not \(1\)? Look at the zoom. The circle at \(100\) N has radius \(164.1\ \mu\)m. We press harder and the circle at \(1000\) N has radius \(353.6\ \mu\)m. Pressing deeper does two things at once: it squeezes the material already in contact harder, and it brings new material into contact, this ring. A plain spring has only the first. Every new ring carries part of the force, so each next micrometre of approach needs more force than the one before. The book says it briefly: at a larger force the contact patch is larger and so the contact stiffer.

2.7 Back up: the ball in numbers

Now we have all three grounds: the gap is a parabola, the sink is of order \(p\,a/E^*\), and the only pressure that gives a parabola is the semi-ellipsoid. We put in the ball. The radii of the circle we already know. At \(100\) N we get \(h = 4.24\ \mu\)m and \(p_0 = 1772\) MPa. At \(1000\) N we get \(h = 19.69\ \mu\)m and \(p_0 = 3818\) MPa. A force of \(1000\) N gives a pressure of almost four gigapascals.

What happens when we multiply the force by ten? The radius grows only by \(10^{1/3} = 2.154\), the area by \(2.154^2 = 4.64\), so the pressure only by \(10/4.64 \approx 2.154\). This is the answer to the question at the start. Back to the first picture: the contact circle is what grew, and because it grew, the pressure only a little more than doubled.

2.8 The spring view: stiffness is the slope

Let us draw the law as the graph of a spring. On the axes are the approach \(h\) in \(\mu\)m and the force \(F\) in N. The blue curve is \(F = \tfrac43E^*\sqrt R\,h^{3/2}\). Stiffness is the slope. The tangent at \(100\) N has slope \(35.35\) N/\(\mu\)m, the tangent at \(1000\) N \(76.17\) N/\(\mu\)m, again \(2.154\) times more. A linear spring would be the dashed line with \(50.8\) N/\(\mu\)m. The derivative gives \(k_{\mathrm{c}} = 2E^*a = 3F/(2h)\) (25.28). And at zero force? There \(a = 0\), the curve starts horizontal and an unloaded contact has no stiffness. This view adds that stiffness is the slope of the curve and that it grows with the force. What this means for a bearing we see in the next part.

2.9 Under the surface: where the steel gives way

Where is the steel loaded most? At the surface? Let us look. Vertically is the depth \(z/a\). The orange curve is the vertical pressure, \(p_0\) at the surface. The grey one is the horizontal stress, \(0.8\,p_0\) at the surface. The shear is half the difference, the blue curve: only \(0.1\,p_0\) at the surface, but at most \(0.310\,p_0\) at \(z = 0.481\,a\). On the right in true proportion: the contact of width \(2a\) and the point of largest shear below it, at \(1000\) N \(170\ \mu\)m deep. This view adds the place of the largest load: it is under the contact, not in it. In the book: 25.29.

2.10 Line contact in one breath

And a roller in a roller bearing? We draw the lower body with radius \(R_2\), a piece of the race, and lay on it a cylinder with radius \(R_1\), seen in section. They are pressed together by the forces \(F'\) per unit length. The bodies touch not in a circle but along a line, which under the force widens into a strip of half-width \(b\). On the right is the equivalent problem with the axes \(x\) and \(z\): a half-plane with modulus \(E^*\) and a rigid cylinder of radius \(R\), the same replacement as for the sphere. The conditions are the same four and the pressure is again a half-ellipse, now in section (25.24). A roller \(R_1 = 5\) mm on a race \(R_2 = 25\) mm at \(100\) N/mm gives a strip \(0.14\) mm wide. One difference remains: the approach of two cylinders is not fixed by the local solution (25.25). This view adds rollers: the same path, only a strip instead of a circle. In the book: 25.5.

3 The bearing: which balls carry, and how stiff it is

A shaft presses on a bearing with nine balls with a force of \(1000\) N. Does each ball carry a ninth, \(111.1\) N? And when the force changes a little, how far does the shaft move? We will read the answer off one picture.

We cut the bearing at right angles to the shaft and draw the outer ring. It is fixed in the housing and does not move. Between the rings we place nine balls. In the middle comes the inner ring, on which the shaft sits. The shaft pushes it with a force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) downwards, \(1000\) N. What does the ring do? It moves down by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\). In the picture this is exaggerated, in reality it is \(11.54\) µm. We find each ball by its angle \(\psi\) from the vertical. The balls below are squeezed, the ones above get room. Only the balls in the load zone (območje obremenitve) are squeezed.

The claim in one sentence: the ring moves as one rigid piece, so each ball is squeezed by a part of that same displacement, \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\), each squeezed ball answers with a Hertz force \(Q = K\delta^{3/2}\), and the vertical parts of these forces together give \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). So the bottom ball carries \(485.6\) N, not \(111.1\) N, and the bearing is a spring \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = c\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\) whose stiffness for small changes is \(129.93\) N/µm, not \(86.62\) N/µm.

3.1 Why do not all the balls carry?

There are nine balls and one force. Do all of them carry? Look again at the shift of the ring. The ring goes down. Below, the gap between the rings narrows and the ball is squeezed. Above, the gap opens. Could the ball above pull the ring back? No. A ball can only push, like a hand on a table: the table supports the hand, but it cannot glue it down. This is the contact condition from the previous part, the pressure is never negative. So only the balls in the lower half carry, all those above have zero force.

3.2 How much is a ball at angle \(\psi\) squeezed?

The ring moves straight down. Is a ball that lies off to the side squeezed as much as the bottom one? Take one single ball, at the angle \(40^\circ\), and magnify it.

First the outer raceway (steza), which stays still. Against it lies the ball. Under the ball is the inner raceway as it was before the load, with the bearing centre \(O\). Through \(O\) and the ball's centre we draw the ball's line. The ball pushes along this line, because a contact force without friction is normal to the surface and so passes through the centre of the sphere. The angle \(\psi\) separates it from the vertical.

Assumption: no friction, no inertia of the balls, the load is static. Why does it hold here? The balls roll, so friction is small, and speeds of rotation are usually not so high that the inertia of the balls would change the distribution. When does it fail? In a fast bearing the centrifugal force changes the load on the outer raceway. What does it do to the result? The force of a ball goes exactly along its line and the ball is in equilibrium. We do not compute the fast bearing in this chapter.

Now we move the inner ring down by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\). The whole ring moves as one piece, so the raceway moves by exactly as much and in the same direction, drawn orange. How much closer has the raceway come to the ball? Not by the whole \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), since the ball is not under the ring but off to the side. Only the part of the shift along the ball's line counts. From the end of the shift we drop a perpendicular onto the line and get a right triangle. Its hypotenuse is \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), the angle at \(O\) is \(\psi\), and the adjacent leg is \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\). By exactly this much the raceway digs into the outline of the ball: this is the squeeze of the ball, \(\delta_\psi\).

Assumption: the rings are rigid. They do not deform, they move as a whole, so one single shift describes the whole ring. When does it fail? Real rings bend, and with light housings this shows. What does it do to the result? The compliance of the ring and the housing is neglected. The book gives no number for it.

Is this exact? In the picture the overlap differs a little from the blue leg, because the shift is drawn far too large. The difference is a second order term, at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/(2R_{\mathrm{p}})\), where \(R_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the radius of the circle of ball centres. At \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11.54\) µm and \(R_{\mathrm{p}} = 23\) mm this is \(2.9\) nm, \(0.025\,\%\) of the shift, and at \(5000\) N it is \(0.025\) µm, \(0.073\,\%\). In the book: 25.35, step 1 of the proof.

At the bottom: \(\psi = 0\), \(\cos\psi = 1\), the whole shift is squeeze. At \(\psi = 90^\circ\), \(\cos\psi = 0\), no squeeze. Above that \(\cos\psi\) is negative and the gap opens: the edge of the load zone is at \(\pm 90^\circ\). If the bearing has a clearance \(P_{\mathrm{d}}\), we also subtract \(P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), the gap that has to close first: \(\delta_\psi = \delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). Today we take \(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\).

3.3 What force does a squeezed ball give?

We know the ball from the previous part, where it had one contact. How many does it have in a bearing? We draw the ball. It has two contacts. From below the inner raceway squeezes it by \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\), from above the outer one by \(\delta_{\mathrm{o}}\). How large are the forces in the two contacts? We cut the ball out and draw the forces on it. The ball is at rest, so by Newton the forces add up to zero. Two forces that add up to zero are equal and opposite. The same force \(Q\) goes through both contacts, as through two springs hung one below the other.

The approaches add up to \(\delta = \delta_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta_{\mathrm{o}}\). Why may we add them? The contact is small: at \(1000\) N the radius of the contact patch is \(5.6\,\%\) of the ball's radius, so the deformation at the lower contact does not reach the upper one. For a ball squeezed almost to its middle this would not hold.

Each contact is a Hertz contact, \(Q = K\delta^{3/2}\) ((25.28)), and we know the power \(3/2\) from the previous part. Two Hertz contacts in a row are again a law with the power \(3/2\) for the total squeeze, only with the constant \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) ((25.29)). For our ball between two flat steel plates \(K_{\mathrm{n}} = 127\,928\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\). In the book: 25.33, proof by scaling.

3.4 How do the ball forces add up to \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\)?

Each ball pushes in its own direction. How do forces in different directions make one force downwards? Back to the right half of L25.17. We draw the inner ring alone. The shaft pushes it with the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) downwards. What holds it? We add a pair of balls at \(\pm\psi\). Each pushes the ring along its own line towards the centre with the force \(Q_\psi\), the arrow ends at the contact point because the force is a push. The angle from the vertical is again \(\psi\).

We split each force into two parts. The horizontal parts \(Q_\psi\sin\psi\) point at each other. The two balls are squeezed equally, being at the same \(|\psi|\), so the parts are equal and cancel. The vertical parts \(Q_\psi\cos\psi\) both point up and add up. The ring is at rest, so the vertical parts of all the balls together must give exactly \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Now the whole ring: the ring, the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and all five ball forces. All pass through the centre, so there is no moment. One equation is left, the sum of the vertical parts is \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\):

\[F_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} .\]

In the book: (25.32).

3.5 Why does the bottom ball carry more than four times the average?

The average is \(111.1\) N per ball. Why does the bottom one carry so much more? Two cosines work against the side balls. We see both in L25.17. The first: a ball off to the side is squeezed only by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\), and by Hertz it therefore gives \(\cos^{3/2}\psi\) of the bottom ball's force. The second: of that force only the vertical part counts for carrying, another \(\cos\psi\). So a side ball contributes \(\cos^{5/2}\psi\) of what the bottom ball does. The numbers are in the counting below.

So the load falls mainly on the bottom ball. The book replaces the sum over the balls by an integral over the angle, \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2) = 0.2288\), and gets

\[Q_{\max} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{r}}}{Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}} = \frac{1000}{9 \cdot 0.2288} = 485.6\ \text{N} = 4.37\,\frac{F_{\mathrm{r}}}{Z} .\]

This is the longest arrow, \(485.6\) N. The arrows at \(\pm 40^\circ\) are \(325.6\) N, those at \(\pm 80^\circ\) only \(35.1\) N.

Assumption: the sum is replaced by an integral. How wrong does this make us? With nine balls and a ball on the load line, a little: the counting below gives \(4.385\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\), the integral is \(0.3\,\%\) lower. When does it fail? When the load line is between two balls, then the deviation of the largest force reaches \(9\,\%\), and when only a few balls are in a narrow zone. The theorem gives the general bound \(2/Z = 0.222\), which is crude. In the book: 25.35, claim 4.

Assumption: zero clearance. When does it fail? At \(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 20\) µm and a light load (\(\chi = 0.1\)) the load zone shrinks to \(\pm 36.87^\circ\). What does it do to the result? The integral gives \(Q_{\max} = 8.653\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\), twice as much as without clearance. With nine balls there are one or two in such a narrow zone, so the exact value is \(9.000\) or \(4.789\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\).

3.6 Why is the stiffness not \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\)?

A force of \(1000\) N moves the shaft by \(11.54\) µm. By how much does one more newton move it? We draw the axes, the shift \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) in micrometres across, the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) in newtons up. Every ball is a Hertz spring, so the whole bearing is also a curve \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = c\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\) with \(c = 25.49\) N/µm\(^{3/2}\), rising ever more steeply. On it we mark the operating point, \(1000\) N at \(11.54\) µm. First thought: the stiffness is \(1000/11.54 = 86.62\) N/µm. That is the slope of the secant from the origin. But where does the shaft go when the force grows a little? Along the curve, not along the secant. So what counts is the slope of the tangent, \(129.93\) N/µm, exactly \(3/2\) times more. Why exactly \(3/2\)? The derivative of \(c\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\) is \(\tfrac32 c\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{1/2} = \tfrac32 F_{\mathrm{r}}/\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\). So the tangent crosses the axis at a third of the shift, \(3.85\) µm. The tangent holds in a narrow band around the operating point. Whoever took the secant would get the natural frequency of the shaft \(18.4\,\%\) too low.

The book gets the constant \(c\) from Palmgren's equation ((25.37)), which is measured, not derived. A check against another book gives \(131.2\) N/µm against our \(129.9\) N/µm, a difference of \(1.0\,\%\), and that is how uncertain the slope of the tangent is. In the book: 25.8.

3.7 Back up

Now we go from the ground back to the top. One single shift of the ring \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\). Geometry turns it into nine squeezes, \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\). Hertz turns the squeezes into nine forces, four of which are zero. Equilibrium adds their vertical parts into \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). Read backwards: at \(1000\) N the ring sits \(11.54\) µm lower and the bottom ball carries \(485.6\) N. That answers the question at the top.

What follows for a machine? The stiffness is the slope of the tangent, and at zero force that is zero. An unloaded bearing does not hold the shaft stiffly. So designers push two bearings against each other.

We draw the walls, between them two bearings as springs and a block of the shaft. Each bearing already squeezes it with \(1000\) N before any load, the preload (predobremenitev). Then an external force \(F_{\mathrm{e}}\) acts on the block and moves it by \(s\). One bearing is squeezed more, the other less, and both work. On the axes on the right we draw the stiffness. The pair starts at twice the stiffness of one bearing, \(2 \cdot 129.93 = 259.9\) N/µm, one bearing without preload at zero. At \(F_{\mathrm{e}} = 2.83\) kN the second bearing comes free and the pair behaves as one bearing. In the book: 25.43.

3.8 Perspective: let us count

For those who find the integral one step too many: we count right on the arrows of the ring. One ball on the direction of the force, nine balls \(40^\circ\) apart. Each ball contributes \(\cos^{5/2}\psi\) of the bottom one:

balls \(\psi\) \(\cos^{5/2}\psi\) total
1 \(0^\circ\) \(1\) \(1\)
2 \(\pm 40^\circ\) \(0.5136\) \(1.0272\)
2 \(\pm 80^\circ\) \(0.0126\) \(0.0251\)
4 above \(0\) \(0\)
sum \(2.052\)

Equilibrium says \(Q_{\max} \cdot 2.052 = F_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(Q_{\max} = 1000/2.052 = 487.2\) N, that is \(4.385\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\). This is exactly the number the book's table has for \(Z = 9\). What does this view add? No integral, only five arrows and a sum that can be done on the fingers.

3.9 Perspective: bars under a curve

We draw the axes, the angle \(\psi\) across, the load up. The curve \(\cos^{3/2}\psi\) says how much a ball at each angle would carry. But the balls sit only at some angles, so we draw them as bars: at \(0^\circ\) height \(1\), at \(\pm 40^\circ\) height \(0.6705\), at \(\pm 80^\circ\) height \(0.0724\). Here is also the idea of the proof of the bound \(2/Z\): the sum over the balls is the bars, the integral is the area under the continuous curve, and since the curve rises once and falls once, they differ by at most two bars. What happens when the cage (kletka) turns by \(20^\circ\)? The balls slide to new angles, \(\pm 20^\circ\) and \(\pm 60^\circ\), with heights \(0.9109\) and \(0.3536\). Now two balls share the load, the largest force at a given \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(3.969\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\). What does this view add? When the bearing turns, the balls move across the angles and the load on each changes all the time between the two pictures. That is where the stiffness ripple during rotation comes from, 25.8.3.

3.10 Perspective: three springs

To finish, we put three springs side by side. The Winkler foundation from the first part is a line through the origin and can also pull. The one-sided spring only pushes, like our upper balls, which carry nothing. The Hertz law pushes and stiffens with the load. For it the secant and the tangent differ by \(3/2\), the same as for the bearing. What does this view add? It ties all three parts of the chapter into one picture: a bearing is a Hertz spring made of balls that only push. Where the bearing goes next, into a rotor model, it enters with its tangent stiffness, 25.9.
