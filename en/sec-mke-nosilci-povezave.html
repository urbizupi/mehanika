Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.11 Connections

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19.

  • Hermite elements and nodal exactness: a beam element gives a deflection with a continuous slope, and theorem 12.14 gives the exact deflection and rotation at the nodes when the stiffness \(EI\) is constant on each element and the element forces are consistent. For vibration, section 12.9 only computed that the frequencies with consistent mass lie above the exact ones. We proved only that on nested meshes they do not increase when the elements are divided (corollary 12.46 with theorem 10.42). That they are not below the exact ones is proved by the upper bound of the Ritz method for continuous systems (DSK, chapter 13). The same chapter derives the exact frequencies of a beam with rotary inertia and shear, as the Timoshenko model includes them, and the mass matrix of an element with rotary inertia. For plates a continuous slope is much harder to get, because the elements meet along edges and the slope must be continuous along the whole edge, not only at the nodes (MK, AK, chapter 18). For shells the cylinder element, whose matrix is the beam element matrix with an added hoop stiffness, no longer has nodal exactness, and flat elements replace the curved shell by plane facets (MK, AK, chapter 22).
  • Frames: theorem 12.20 and corollary 12.24 assemble a plane and a space frame from frame elements. The symmetry of a structure, constraints between several nodes and the coupling of different kinds of elements continue the multipliers and the penalty method of section 12.6 (MK, AK, chapter 19). Mesh convergence and good modelling practice are in chapter 17 (AK). The torsion constant of a section, which we took as a given number in the space frame, and the warping of a section are explained in chapter 16 (MK). Rotors are written with beam elements (DSK, chapter 24), and flexible links of robots with beam elements and vibration modes (DSK, chapter 28).
  • Locking: shear locking, which we showed in section 12.8 as a penalty system (lemma 12.35), repeats in Mindlin plates with two directions of shear (MK, AK, chapter 18). In nearly incompressible solids the condition that the volume does not change plays the role of the penalty and locks the elements in the same way (AK, chapter 15).
  • Mass matrices: the consistent mass matrix and the lumped mass of section 12.9 are the basis of damping matrices, which in practice are built from mass and stiffness (DSK, chapter 20). Guyan reduction splits the unknowns into kept and dependent ones and expresses the dependent ones by the kept ones, as lemma 12.45 expressed the rotations without inertia by the displacements. For rotations without mass this step is exact, for unknowns with mass only approximate. The assembly of substructures splits the unknowns into interior and boundary ones (DSK, chapter 21).
  • Geometric stiffness: theorem 12.53 proves that the critical force of the finite element model \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) is not below the true critical force \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) for a column with constant stiffness that has both ends pinned or both fixed or is a cantilever. For a column with one end fixed and the other pinned it gives only the weaker bound \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge \pi^2 EI/L^2\). Chapter 8 computed the threshold of this column with a determinant and did not prove that the second variation stays positive below it, so in section 12.10.5 \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) is only computed for it. The threshold of this column, and with it the bound \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\), is proved in chapter 13 (DSK). Linear buckling analysis assumes a perfectly straight column and small displacements. Stability in modelling, imperfections and nonlinear buckling are in chapter 17 (AK).
