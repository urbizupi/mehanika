Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.7 Subspace iteration (iteracija podprostora)

Inverse iteration from section 21.6 finds one mode. For the first \(p\) modes we must repeat it \(p\) times and in every step remove the modes already found from the column (lemma 21.28). The speed depends on the ratio of the wanted eigenvalue to the next one, and if the two are close it is slow. Could we iterate several columns at once and leave the orthogonality to someone else? The Ritz approximation of chapter 10 already does this job: from any independent columns it makes \(\mat M\)-orthonormal Ritz shapes (lemma 10.40). This section therefore takes \(q\) columns, makes one step of inverse iteration on each, and then a Ritz step, which orthonormalizes the columns and sorts them by eigenvalue. The procedure is called subspace iteration (iteracija podprostora) because no single column converges, but the space the columns span: in every step this space comes closer to the space of the first \(q\) modes. The section fixes what the procedure does, proves how fast the Ritz values approach the eigenvalues, and shows on the cantilever of chapter 12 which \(q\) pays off and when the procedure fails. The path is the same as for all earlier reduced models: every step is a Ritz approximation (definition 10.41), so the upper bound of theorem 10.42 holds, the only difference being that the basis \(\mat\Psi\) improves itself (CK section 15.3, PDF p. 498).

We read the figure by rows. Every graph has the clamp on the left, the beam axis and the exact mode \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) as a grey dashed line: the first has one bend, the second one zero, the third two. In the row \(k = 0\) the start column, taken from the first three columns of the start block, is a random curve that resembles anything more than a mode. After one step the three blue curves are already close to the grey dashed ones, and after two they nearly cover them in the figure. This section tells why this happens and how fast.

21.7.1 The procedure

A step of inverse iteration is a static problem: from a column of shape \(\vek x\) we form the inertia forces \(\mat M\,\vek x\) and find the displacements \(\vek y\) these forces cause, \(\mat K\,\vek y = \mat M\,\vek x\) (CK, step 6 in table 15.1, PDF p. 500 and remark 4, PDF p. 501). For \(q\) columns we solve \(q\) such static problems with the same matrix \(\mat K\), which we factorize once (lemma 7.47), and every further static problem is only forward and back substitution. Instead of normalizing the columns one by one, we now send them together into a Ritz step.

Definition 21.31 Subspace iteration (iteracija podprostora)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\) (definition 6.27), \(1 \le q \le n\) and \(\mat X_0\) a matrix with \(n\) rows and \(q\) linearly independent columns (definition 6.22), the start block (začetni blok). Subspace iteration (iteracija podprostora) is the sequence of matrices \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(k = 1, 2, \dots\), obtained by doing the following in step \(k\):

  1. Solve \(q\) static problems, \(\mat K\,\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\).
  2. Form the reduced matrices \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\,\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) of order \(q\).
  3. Solve the reduced eigenproblem \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat I\) and \(\tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_1, \dots, \tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{q}}\big)\), \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{q}}\) (the columns of the matrix \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) are \(\vek a_1, \dots, \vek a_{\mathrm{q}}\) of lemma 10.40).
  4. Set \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The numbers \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the estimates of the eigenvalues, the columns of the matrix \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\) the estimates of the modes after \(k\) steps.

Source: CK section 15.3 (PDF pp. 498-501, table 15.1 on p. 500): the table has steps 0 to 7 with the choice of the start loads, the static problem, the reduced matrices, the reduced eigenproblem, the estimates of the modes and the improved loads. The symbols \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\) are ours. GR section 6.8.1 (PDF pp. 482-484, (6.112) to (6.116)) has the same steps with normalized \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{k}}\) and other symbols. BATHE section 11.6.2 (PDF pp. 975-976, (11.151) to (11.155)).

The Ritz step is always the same here: the reduced basis \(\mat\Psi = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) has \(q\) columns, and the estimates are Ritz values (definition 10.41). Whether the start is enough for \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) to stay independent and for the estimates to be really Ritz values, we check in the lemma.

Lemma 21.32 Steps of subspace iteration are Ritz steps (koraki iteracije podprostora so Ritzovi koraki)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(q\), \(\mat X_0\), \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as in definition 21.31 and \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). Let \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the column space of the matrix \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\) (the set of all linear combinations). Then for every \(k \ge 1\):

  1. The columns of the matrix \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) are independent, so step 3 is well defined and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) is invertible.
  2. \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat I\) and \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\). The columns are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal, hence independent.
  3. \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}} = (\mat K^{-1}\mat M)\,\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), so \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the column space of the matrix \((\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\mat X_0\).
  4. The numbers \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the Ritz values of the basis \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and stay the same for every other basis of the space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\). They satisfy \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, q\), and \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\).
Proof

We must show that lemma 10.40 really applies to the basis \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\), then read off the orthonormality and the space from it, and finally show that \(\tilde\lambda\) depends only on the space.

  1. By induction on \(k\) let \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) have independent columns (for \(k = 1\) this is the assumption). If \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek b = \vek 0\), then \(\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\vek b = \mat K\,\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\vek b = \vek 0\). For \(\vek w = \mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\vek b\) we get \(\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek w = 0\), hence \(\vek w = \vek 0\) (definition 6.27), and since the columns of \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) are independent, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The columns of the matrix \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) are independent. By lemma 10.40 \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\) is positive definite and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the required properties exists.
  2. By lemma 10.40, point 2, \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat I\) and \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat K\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\), because \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat I\). If \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\vek b = \vek 0\), we multiply by \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\) and get \(\vek b = \vek 0\). So the columns of \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\) are independent, which completes the induction of step 1.
  3. If \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\vek b = \vek 0\), then \(\vek b = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\vek b = \vek 0\). By lemma 8.21 \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) is invertible, so \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat X_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^{-1}\) and \(\mat X_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) have the same column space. Since \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), we have \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}} = (\mat K^{-1}\mat M)\,\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), and by induction \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the column space of the matrix \((\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\mat X_0\).
  4. The Ritz values of the basis \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\) are \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) by definition 10.41, and \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) by theorem 10.42, point 1. Let \(\mat\Psi' = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\mat T\) be another basis of the space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(q\) independent columns, each a combination of the columns of \(\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}\), and \(\mat T\) square). From \(\mat T\vek b = \vek 0\) it follows that \(\mat\Psi'\vek b = \vek 0\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\), so \(\mat T\) is invertible (lemma 8.21). The reduced matrices of the basis \(\mat\Psi'\) are \(\mat T^\T\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat T\) and \(\mat T^\T\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}}\mat T\), and \(\mat T^\T(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}} - \lambda\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}})\mat T\,\vek a = \vek 0\) exactly when \((\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}} - \lambda\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{k}})\,\mat T\vek a = \vek 0\) (\(\mat T^\T\) is invertible). The map \(\vek a \mapsto \mat T\vek a\) therefore takes eigencolumns of one problem to eigencolumns of the other with the same eigenvalue, and independent ones to independent ones, so the ordered lists of eigenvalues are equal (as in step 8 of the proof of theorem 10.42). Finally, by step 1 of definition 21.31, \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}^\T(\mat K\,\mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}) = \mat Y_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\,\mat X_{\mathrm{k}-1}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. CK (PDF p. 499) says that the step \(\tilde{\mat K}\), \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is a "Ritz approximation", and BATHE (PDF p. 977) that "one subspace iteration ... in fact, a Ritz analysis", so the estimates are upper bounds.

The last point gives two things. Every step has all the advantages of a Ritz approximation: the estimates are upper bounds (definition 10.41 and theorem 10.42), and the space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) is what counts, the basis in it does not change the values. And \(\tilde{\mat K}_{\mathrm{k}}\) needs no new multiplication by \(\mat K\): we get it from the columns already computed, it costs only \(q^2\) scalar products and saves multiplying a large matrix by \(q\) columns.

21.7.2 Why the space converges

Look at the space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) in modal coordinates. If \(\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) (corollary 10.14), then \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) (from \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} = R(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}) > 0\) because \(\mat K\) is positive definite), so \((\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{-k}c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\). Every step multiplies the mode \(s\) by \(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\), so the low modes grow relative to the high ones. This is already so in inverse iteration (theorem 21.27), but now all \(q\) columns would grow towards the first mode and become more and more parallel, were it not for the Ritz step that sorts them again every time. The speed with which the space approaches the first \(q\) modes is set by the ratio of the largest eigenvalue we want to the smallest one we must suppress. The theorem writes this for Ritz values.

Theorem 21.33 Convergence of subspace iteration (konvergenca iteracije podprostora)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite of order \(n\), \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) (theorem 10.13) and \(q < n\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{q}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}\). Let \(\mat\Phi_1\) be the matrix of the first \(q\) columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\mat\Phi_2\) the matrix of the others, \(\mat X_0\) a start block with independent columns, \(\mat C_1 = \mat\Phi_1^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0\), \(\mat C_2 = \mat\Phi_2^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0\) and \(\mat C_1\) invertible (the start has an invertible part on the first \(q\) modes). Let \(\mat G = \mat C_2\mat C_1^{-1}\) and \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in definition 21.31. Then for every \(r = 1, \dots, q\) and \(k \ge 1\)

\[0 \le \tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le B_{\mathrm{r}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}}\right)^{2k} , \qquad B_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}=\mathrm{q}+1}^n\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^r G_{\mathrm{sj}}^2 , \tag{21.45}\]

where \(G_{\mathrm{sj}}\) is the element \((s - q, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat G\) (we number the rows of \(\mat G\) with \(s = q+1, \dots, n\)).

Proof

We must show that the same space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) contains a basis that is ever closer to the first \(q\) modes, and then find how large the error of the Ritz value is on this basis.

  1. Let \(\mat\Phi = [\mat\Phi_1\ \mat\Phi_2]\), and \(\mat\Lambda_1\) and \(\mat\Lambda_2\) the diagonal matrices of the eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{q}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), and \(\mat\Lambda\) the diagonal matrix of all \(n\) eigenvalues. By corollary 10.14, point 1, \(\mat X_0 = \mat\Phi\,\mat C\) with \(\mat C = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0 = \begin{pmatrix}\mat C_1 \\ \mat C_2\end{pmatrix}\). From \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) it follows that \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{k}} := (\mat K^{-1}\mat M)^k\mat X_0 = \mat\Phi\,\mat\Lambda^{-k}\mat C\), and by lemma 21.32, point 3, \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the column space of the matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. Let \(\mat T_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat C_1^{-1}\mat\Lambda_1^k\), an invertible matrix of order \(q\). Then \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{k}}\mat T_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat\Phi_1\mat\Lambda_1^{-k}\mat C_1\mat C_1^{-1}\mat\Lambda_1^k + \mat\Phi_2\mat\Lambda_2^{-k}\mat C_2\mat C_1^{-1}\mat\Lambda_1^k = \mat\Phi_1 + \mat\Phi_2\mat E_{\mathrm{k}} =: \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(\mat E_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat\Lambda_2^{-k}\mat G\,\mat\Lambda_1^k\) and elements \((\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}} = G_{\mathrm{sj}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}})^k\). The columns of \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) are combinations of the columns of \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{k}}\) with an invertible matrix, so \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) has the same column space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(q\) independent columns. By lemma 21.32, point 4, the \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the Ritz values of the basis \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\), and \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the left estimate.
  3. Let \(\vek t \ne \vek 0\) be a column of \(q\) numbers with \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(j > r\) and \(\vek v = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\vek t\). Write \(e_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}}(\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}}\,t_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(s > q\). Then \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s}>\mathrm{q}}e_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), and by \(\mat M\)-orthonormality and \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = \sum t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 + \sum e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 + \sum_{\mathrm{s}>\mathrm{q}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\).
  4. Write \(a = \sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 > 0\). Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(j \le r\), the numerator of the quotient \(R(\vek v)\) is at most \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,a + \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), the denominator is at least \(a\) and the numerator is not negative, so \(R(\vek v) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s}>\mathrm{q}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2/a\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (lemma 8.23, point 1) for the sum \(e_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}(\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}}t_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(e_{\mathrm{s}}^2 \le a\sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}(\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}}^2\), so \(R(\vek v) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s}>\mathrm{q}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}\le \mathrm{r}}(\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}}^2\).
  5. For \(j \le r \le q < s\) we have \(0 < \lambda_{\mathrm{j}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}\), so \((\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{sj}}^2 \le G_{\mathrm{sj}}^2(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1})^{2k}\) and \(R(\vek v) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} + B_{\mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1})^{2k}\) for every such \(\vek v\).
  6. The reduced pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\) of the basis \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) has a symmetric matrix \(\tilde{\mat K}\) and a symmetric positive definite matrix \(\tilde{\mat M}\) (lemma 10.40, point 1), as theorem 10.28 requires. Its eigenvalues are \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the quotient of the pair is \(R(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\vek t)\) (lemma 10.40, point 3). By theorem 10.28, point 2, for the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\) and the columns \(\vek e_1, \dots, \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the first \(r\) columns of the identity matrix of order \(q\)) we have \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}} \le \max R(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\vek t)\) over all \(\vek t \ne \vek 0\) with \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(j > r\). By step 5 this largest quotient is at most \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} + B_{\mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1})^{2k}\). This is the right estimate.

Source: The proof is not from a source. BATHE (PDF p. 976) says that "the \(i\)th column ... converges linearly to \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the convergence rate is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{p}+1}\)" and that more vectors raise the speed, GR (PDF p. 485, (6.117)) has the same ratio with the mode \(p + 1\), CK (PDF pp. 500-501) does not analyse the speed. The theorem proves the speed of the square of this ratio for the eigenvalues, and the constant \(B_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is not in the sources.

The proof gave three things. The Ritz values of subspace iteration are upper bounds, and their error falls at least as fast as \(B_{\mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1})^{2k}\), so with the square of the ratio of one value already in the subspace to the first one that is not (step 5). The ratio depends on \(q + 1\), not on \(r + 1\): with a larger \(q\) we raise the denominator \(\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}\), the ratio gets smaller and for the same \(r\) the speed is greater (these are the "buffer vectors" of GR, PDF p. 485). And the constant \(B_{\mathrm{r}}\) depends on the start through \(\mat G = \mat C_2\mat C_1^{-1}\): if \(\mat C_1\) is nearly singular, \(\mat G\) is usually large and so is the bound, and the error can stay large for several steps (the frame at the end of the section). If \(\mat C_1\) is singular, the condition of the theorem is not met. The speed for the shape, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}\), as stated by BATHE and GR, is not proved here. 21.7.3 The cantilever with six columns

Take the cantilever of chapter 12 with \(16\) elements, which has \(32\) free coordinates (deflection and rotation at \(16\) nodes, clamped on the left), in units \(EI = \rho A = L = 1\). We want the first three modes, \(p = 3\). The eigenvalues from NumPy are \(\lambda_1 = 12{,}3624\), \(\lambda_2 = 485{,}524\), \(\lambda_3 = 3806{,}85\), \(\lambda_4 = 14\,621{,}7\), \(\lambda_7 = 174\,481\), the angular frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3 = 3{,}51602\), \(22{,}0346\), \(61{,}6997\) and \(\omega_4 = 120{,}920\). The start is random: \(q = 6\) columns from the normal distribution (the NumPy generator with seed \(1\)). The matrix \(\mat C_1 = \mat\Phi_1^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0\) has \(|\det\mat C_1| = 4{,}3\cdot10^{-3}\) (the sign depends on the signs of the modes) and the matrix \(\mat G\) has elements of absolute value at most \(2{,}60\). So the start has an invertible part on the first six modes, as the theorem requires. In the basis \(\mat\Phi_1 + \mat\Phi_2\mat G\) of the same space (step 2 of the proof at \(k = 0\)) every column has the share \(1\) of its own low mode and at most \(2{,}6\) of every higher one.

Example 21.34 Subspace iteration on the cantilever, \(p = 3\), \(q = 6\)

We make the steps of definition 21.31 for \(q = 6\) and read \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_1\), \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_2\) and \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_3\) from the diagonal of the matrix \(\tilde{\mat\Lambda}_{\mathrm{k}}\). The table gives the relative error \(\tilde\lambda^{(k)}_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - 1\), which by lemma 21.32, point 4, is nowhere negative (up to rounding):

\(k\) \(r = 1\) \(r = 2\) \(r = 3\)
1 \(1{,}12\cdot10^{-5}\) \(5{,}90\cdot10^{-3}\) \(5{,}78\cdot10^{-2}\)
2 \(< 10^{-11}\) \(4{,}20\cdot10^{-8}\) \(1{,}45\cdot10^{-5}\)
3 \(< 10^{-11}\) \(< 10^{-11}\) \(2{,}93\cdot10^{-9}\)
4 \(< 10^{-11}\) \(< 10^{-11}\) \(7{,}9\cdot10^{-13}\)

An entry \(< 10^{-11}\) means an error we can no longer tell from rounding. The computer rounds every number by about \(10^{-16}\) relative (double precision, chapter 12). At \(q = 6\) the iterated values differ from values computed to \(40\) digits (mpmath) by at most \(5\cdot10^{-11}\), with both signs, which at \(\lambda_1 = 12{,}4\) is \(4\cdot10^{-12}\) relative. The exact \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for the table are therefore computed to \(40\) digits. The usual computation with NumPy already misses \(\lambda_1\) by \(5{,}4\cdot10^{-11}\) relative, and with such a comparison the column \(r = 1\) would show only this error from \(k = 2\) on (we do not analyse rounding further). The ratios of successive errors of the third value are \(2{,}5\cdot10^{-4}\), \(2{,}0\cdot10^{-4}\) and \(2{,}7\cdot10^{-4}\). In the bound of the theorem the factor per step is \((\lambda_3/\lambda_7)^2 = 0{,}02182^2 = 4{,}76\cdot10^{-4}\). The actual ratios are of the same order of magnitude, for this start smaller by a factor of \(1{,}8\) to \(2{,}4\), so the bound catches the speed. The theorem does not bound the ratio of two successive errors, though: with other seeds of the generator we also got \(5{,}2\cdot10^{-4}\) and \(5{,}9\cdot10^{-4}\). The constant is \(B_3 = 1{,}34\cdot10^8\), and \(B_3/\lambda_3 = 35\,088\). The bound of the relative error is therefore \(16{,}7\), \(7{,}95\cdot10^{-3}\) and \(3{,}78\cdot10^{-6}\) at \(k = 1, 2, 3\), which is \(290\) times, \(550\) times and \(1290\) times more than the measured values. In the first step the bound allows \(\tilde\lambda_3\) up to \(17{,}7\,\lambda_3\) and tells nothing of practical use, and from the second step on it tells how large the error is at most.

The first test of the theorem therefore succeeds: the error falls by a factor of \(3700\) to \(4900\) per step, and the order of magnitude of this factor is set by \(\lambda_3\) and \(\lambda_7\), not by the random start (with four other seeds of the generator it was between \(1700\) and \(4700\)). What happens to the shape in the process is shown by the next figure.

We expand the third Ritz shape in the modes \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) (corollary 10.14) and read in the figure how much of each it contains. The open circle is the third mode, which takes up \(1\). The vertical line separates the first six modes, present in the space \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{k}}\) by design, from the modes \(7\) to \(12\), which the iteration must suppress. The vertical axis is logarithmic. After the first step the share of the modes \(7\) to \(12\) is from \(6\cdot10^{-4}\) to \(1{,}4\cdot10^{-2}\), after the second from \(4{,}5\cdot10^{-6}\) to \(3{,}7\cdot10^{-4}\) and after the third from \(1{,}5\cdot10^{-8}\) to \(4{,}9\cdot10^{-6}\). The share of mode \(7\) at \(k = 1, 2, 3\) is \(5{,}1\cdot10^{-3}\), \(2{,}2\cdot10^{-4}\) and \(4{,}9\cdot10^{-6}\). We divide it by \((\lambda_3/\lambda_7)^k = 0{,}0218^k\) and get \(0{,}234\), \(0{,}465\) and \(0{,}468\). The last two values match the element \(|G_{73}| = 0{,}468\) of step 2 of the proof of the theorem: the third column of the basis \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) has at mode \(7\) the share \((\mat E_{\mathrm{k}})_{73} = G_{73}(\lambda_3/\lambda_7)^k\). In this share the third Ritz shape is already nearly equal to the third column of the basis \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) after two steps. So the share of mode \(7\) in the shape falls here by the factor \(\lambda_3/\lambda_7\) per step, as BATHE and GR state the speed of the shape, and the square of this factor holds for the eigenvalue, which the theorem proves.

Let us also compare three sizes of the block, \(q = 3\), \(6\) and \(11\), for the third eigenvalue. The ratio \(\lambda_3/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1}\) is in the second column of the table, the relative error after the first three steps in the last.

The figure puts the table on the axis with the step \(k\) horizontal and the logarithmic error vertical. The orange curve (\(q = 3\)) falls more slowly in the first steps and from \(k = 4\) on it meets the prediction with the slope \(0{,}0678\) per step (a factor of \(15\)). The blue curve (\(q = 6\)) falls by about \(3{,}6\) orders of magnitude per step and lies below the prediction, because its ratios of successive errors, \(2{,}0\) to \(2{,}7\cdot10^{-4}\), are smaller than \(\rho = 4{,}76\cdot10^{-4}\). The blackwhite curve (\(q = 11\)) is steeper still, but it has only two points, because the third value is at the rounding limit. Its second point lies a factor of \(2{,}2\) above the prediction: the error fell by the factor \(1{,}0\cdot10^{-5}\) and not \(\rho = 4{,}7\cdot10^{-6}\). At about \(20\) static problems the most accurate is therefore the middle choice \(q = 6\). The static problems are only part of the cost: every step needs also \(q^2\) scalar products for the matrices \(\tilde{\mat K}\), \(\tilde{\mat M}\) and a reduced eigenproblem of order \(q\) with full matrices, which grows as \(q^3\). For a large \(n\) the static problems dominate, for a large \(q\) the reduced problem. CK (PDF p. 501) recommends for \(N_{\mathrm{d}}\) wanted modes \(N_{\mathrm{s}} = \min\{2N_{\mathrm{d}},\ N_{\mathrm{d}} + 8\}\) vectors, GR the same (PDF p. 485, citing Bathe and Wilson): at \(p = 3\) we have \(q = \min\{6,\ 11\} = 6\), which we took. The rule means that we add \(p\) spare vectors as long as \(p \le 8\), and \(8\) for a larger \(p\). BATHE (section 11.6.2, PDF p. 977) writes \(\max\{2p,\ p + 8\}\) in the text, but its cost table (PDF p. 982) has \(\min\{2p,\ p + 8\}\). The books disagree, we follow the \(\min\) of three places, because \(\max\) at \(p = 100\) would give \(q = 200\) and not \(108\). The recommendation comes from experience and is not proved. Only the speed at a given \(q\) is proved.

21.7.4 Near miss: a start without the second mode

The theorem requires \(\mat C_1\) to be invertible. What happens if it is not? We try the frame of chapter 10 with three floors, units \(k/\bar m\), eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = 0{,}198062\), \(\lambda_2 = 1{,}554958\), \(\lambda_3 = 3{,}246980\). We take \(q = 2\), so we want \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), and three starts.

Example 21.35 The frame, \(q = 2\), a start without the second mode

The first start has the columns \((1, 1, 1)^\T\) and \((0, 1, 3)^\T\). Its matrix \(\mat C_1\) (the rows are \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0\) and \(\vek\varphi_2^\T\mat M\,\mat X_0\)) has \(|\det\mat C_1| = 3{,}72\), so it is invertible, and the second Ritz value after \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\) steps is \(1{,}55524\), \(1{,}55502\), \(1{,}55497\) and \(1{,}55496\) in turn: it approaches \(\lambda_2 = 1{,}554958\) from above.

The second start has the columns \(\vek\varphi_1 + \vek\varphi_3\) and \(\vek\varphi_1 + 2\vek\varphi_3\). Both are combinations of the first and third mode only, so the second row of the matrix \(\mat C_1\) is zero, only the first one is nonzero, and the condition of the theorem is not met. The space of all combinations of the modes \(\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\vek\varphi_3\) is invariant in this case: because \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\), every step stays in it, and we never get the second mode. The Ritz values are for all \(k\) exactly \(0{,}198062\) and \(3{,}246980\), that is \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_3\), and \(\lambda_2\) is nowhere. This holds in a computation without rounding, what rounding does is told by the paragraph after figure 21.26.

The third start has the columns \(\vek\varphi_1 + \vek\varphi_3\) and \(\vek\varphi_1 + 2\vek\varphi_3 + 10^{-3}\vek\varphi_2\): a small share of the second mode. Now \(\mat C_1\) is invertible (\(\det\mat C_1 = 10^{-3}\)), but \(\mat G = (1,\ 1000)\) and the constant is \(B_2 = 3{,}2\cdot10^6\). The second Ritz value is \(3{,}24697\) at \(k = 1\), \(3{,}24637\) at \(k = 4\), \(3{,}0514\) at \(k = 8\), \(2{,}0406\) at \(k = 10\), \(1{,}5900\) at \(k = 12\), \(1{,}55684\) at \(k = 14\), \(1{,}55506\) at \(k = 16\) and \(1{,}554958\) with the relative error \(1{,}8\cdot10^{-7}\) at \(k = 20\) and \(1{,}1\cdot10^{-10}\) at \(k = 25\).

The figure collects all three starts. The step \(k\) is horizontal, \(\tilde\lambda_2\) vertical. The blue curve lies on \(\lambda_2\) from the first step. The blackwhite line stays at \(\lambda_3\) for all \(30\) steps: the space is two-dimensional and invariant, and the reduced problem in it has only \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_3\). The orange curve starts at \(\lambda_3\) and falls towards \(\lambda_2\) only after \(k \approx 10\). Why just there? The difference of the two columns is \(\vek\varphi_3 + \varepsilon\,\vek\varphi_2\) and lies in the space of the start. The share of the second mode in it is \(\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\), the share of the third is \(1\), and every step multiplies them by \(1/\lambda_2\) and \(1/\lambda_3\), so the ratio of the parts changes by \(\lambda_3/\lambda_2 = 2{,}088\) per step. The second mode becomes equal to the third at \(\varepsilon\,(2{,}088)^k = 1\), that is at \(k = \ln 10^3/\ln 2{,}088 = 9{,}4\), and so it is in the figure. Later the curve falls at the predicted speed: the mean ratio of successive errors between \(k = 20\) and \(25\) is \((1{,}119\cdot10^{-10}/1{,}763\cdot10^{-7})^{1/5} = 0{,}229\), which is the \((\lambda_2/\lambda_3)^2 = 0{,}229\) of the theorem. The slow start does not contradict the theorem, which puts it down to the constant \(B_2 = 3{,}2\cdot10^6\): the bound of the relative error is \(0{,}84\) at \(k = 10\) and \(3{,}4\cdot10^{-7}\) at \(k = 20\), above the measured \(0{,}31\) and \(1{,}8\cdot10^{-7}\). The same computation explains what happens to the blackwhite line in the computer. Rounding creates in the columns a share of the second mode of the order \(10^{-16}\), which becomes equal to the third at \(k = \ln 10^{16}/\ln 2{,}088 = 50\). The second Ritz value of the second start is still \(3{,}24698\) at \(k = 40\), it is \(1{,}794\) at \(k = 50\) and already \(1{,}55496\) at \(k = 60\). CK (PDF p. 500, remark 1) requires that the initial loads excite all the low modes we are interested in. In our notation this is the invertibility of the matrix \(\mat C_1\), and by CK choosing such loads needs experience.

21.7.5 Summary

Subspace iteration makes a step of inverse iteration on \(q\) columns and then a Ritz step (definition 21.31). Every step is a Ritz approximation (lemma 21.32), so the estimates of the eigenvalues are upper bounds, as for the Guyan and Craig-Bampton bases, the only difference being that we improve the basis ourselves each time. By theorem 21.33 the bound of the error of the \(r\)th estimate shrinks by the factor \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{q}+1})^2\) per step, when the start has an invertible part on the first \(q\) modes. For the cantilever at \(q = 6\) and \(r = 3\) the factor is \(4{,}8\cdot10^{-4}\), at \(q = 3\) it is \(0{,}068\), and six columns give the error \(3\cdot10^{-9}\) after three steps, three columns \(5\cdot10^{-7}\) after eight. Without a share of the second mode in the start the procedure stays, in a computation without rounding, in the wrong subspace and does not find \(\lambda_2\) (the blackwhite line in figure 21.26). In the computer it finds it only after about \(50\) steps, when rounding creates this share and the steps enlarge it.

The cost is \(q\) static problems and a reduced problem of order \(q\) in every step. The Lanczos procedure in the next section builds the subspace from a single column and gets every further vector with one static problem. The Ritz steps and the upper bounds remain.
