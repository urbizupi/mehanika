The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

Chapter 6 gave the hanging bar an approximation made of straight pieces. We cut the bar into the elements of a mesh, took a straight line on each, and obtained a system of equations from the displacements of the nodes. At the end we noticed that the matrix of the system is built from small blocks, one for each element, and that the right-hand side is a sum of contributions of the elements (section 6.7.2). Chapters 4 and 5 wrote the same idea down by hand for a truss of two bars, first with a matrix with two rows (section 4.6.7), then with three (equation (5.94)). Both times we derived each row separately, with that one structure in mind. A computer has no such insight. So how do we write the same computation for a bar with any load and supports, or for a truss of a hundred bars, in the plane or in space, so that a computer can carry it out, and how do we know that the numbers we obtain are right?

The answer is the finite element method (metoda končnih elementov), as used by computer programs for structures. Let us look at the words in the title. Method comes from Greek (methodos, from meta and hodos), literally a way to a goal: a prescription of steps that we can repeat every time. A finite element (končni element) is a piece of a structure of finite size. The pieces of a Riemann sum in an integral are shrunk towards zero, a finite element is not: we build the structure from such pieces as they are. A bar (palica) is a straight body loaded only along its axis, and a truss (paličje) is a structure of several bars joined at nodes. FEM is the abbreviation for this method. It matters because we use it for every structure too large for a hand computation, and bars and trusses are its simplest case: each node has only one unknown, and chapter 6 already knows the true answer for the bar, with which we compare.

The chapter promises this: we shall be able to describe all steps of an analysis with finite elements, from the model and the mesh to the forces in the bars and the checking, and every step will be proved or computed once. We do not repeat the proofs of chapter 6, for example that the displacement at a node is exact or that the error falls as the square of the element length, but we cite them.

The method differs from the computation in chapter 6 in five things. First, the computation is written piece by piece: each piece of the bar has its own small matrix, which we compute once, and the matrix of the whole structure is obtained by adding. Second, we take the supports into account only when the pieces are joined, so the same equations also give the forces in the supports and in the bars. Third, a piece may have more than two nodes, and we compute the integrals by a rule that a computer can carry out. Fourth, an oblique bar has its stiffness along its own axis, while the structure needs common axes, so we rotate the axes. Fifth, we check every model with computations that expose a wrong model without our knowing the true answer. The route is as follows. First we treat a single piece of bar: what it does with the displacements of its ends and how we replace the loads along it by forces at the ends. Then we join the pieces into the whole bar and show that the result is the same matrix as in chapter 6. Next we add the supports, solve the system and compute the forces in the supports. A piece with three nodes follows, with the rule by which a computer computes integrals. The chapter then moves on to trusses in the plane and in space, to a larger example, a bridge truss, and to the mathematics of matrices in which most entries are zero. At the end we treat heated bars and bars that are too long, and we collect the steps of the analysis and the checking of the model. Trusses add to the bar only geometry, the rotation of the axes.
