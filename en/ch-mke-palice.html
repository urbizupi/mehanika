The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

Chapter 6 gave the hanging bar an approximation made of straight pieces. We cut the bar into the elements of a mesh, took a straight line on each, and obtained a system of equations from the displacements of the nodes. At the end we noticed that the matrix of the system is built from small blocks, one for each element, and that the right-hand side is a sum of contributions of the elements (section 6.7.2). Chapters 4 and 5 wrote the same idea down by hand for a truss of two bars, first with a matrix with two rows (section 4.6.7), then with three (equation (5.86)). Both times we derived each row separately, with that one structure in mind. A computer has no such insight. So how do we write the same computation for a bar with any load and supports, or for a truss of a hundred bars, in the plane or in space, so that a computer can carry it out, and how do we know that the numbers we obtain are right?

The answer is the finite element method (metoda končnih elementov), as used by computer programs for structures. It differs from the computation in chapter 6 in five things. First, the computation is written piece by piece: each piece of the bar has its own small matrix, which we compute once, and the matrix of the whole structure is obtained by adding these matrices. Second, we take the supports into account only when the pieces are already joined into a whole, so the same equations also give the forces in the supports and the forces in the bars. Third, a piece of bar may have more than two nodes, and we compute the integrals by a rule that a computer can carry out. Fourth, a bar that lies obliquely has its axial stiffness along its own axis, while the structure needs common axes, so we have to rotate the axes. Fifth, we check every model with computations that expose a wrong model without our knowing the true answer.

The route is as follows. First we treat a single piece of bar: what it does with the displacements of its ends and how we replace the loads along it by forces at the ends. Then we join the pieces into the whole bar and show that the result is the same matrix as in chapter 6. Next we add the supports, solve the system and compute the forces in the supports. A piece with three nodes follows, with the rule by which a computer computes integrals. The chapter then moves on to trusses in the plane and in space, to a larger example, a bridge truss, and to the mathematics of matrices in which most entries are zero. At the end we treat heated bars and bars that are too long, and we collect the steps of the analysis and the checking of the model. Everything that is new in the method can be seen on the bar, because each node has only one unknown and chapter 6 can tell us the true answer for the bar, with which we compare. Trusses then add only geometry, the rotation of the axes.

The goal of the chapter is that we can describe all steps of an analysis with finite elements, from the model and the mesh to the forces in the bars and the checking, and that every step is proved or computed once. We do not repeat the proofs of chapter 6, for example that the displacement at a node is exact or that the error falls as the square of the element length, but we cite them.
