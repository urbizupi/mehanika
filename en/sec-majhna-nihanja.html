Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.13 Small oscillations about an equilibrium (majhna nihanja okoli ravnovesja)

A pendulum that we deflect by a few degrees and release swings with a period that hardly changes when we increase the deflection. At a deflection of 5° one swing lasts 2,0070 s, at 10° it lasts 2,0099 s, so only 0,14 % longer. The pendulum equation, \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\), has no solution in the functions we know. The equation \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\,\theta = 0\), however, we can solve with the sine and cosine of section 8.2. In section 8.6 we called an equilibrium stable when the energy \(V\) has a strict local minimum there, and in section 8.12 we proved that a motion that starts near such an equilibrium stays near. Now we ask what this motion looks like. Which simpler equation does it follow for any system of this chapter, and how close is it to the true equation?

The route is this. First, from Lagrange's equations about an equilibrium we derive an equation that is linear in the displacements and their derivatives, and we bound what is discarded on the way. Then we solve it completely for one degree of freedom and compare it with the pendulum, whose period we can compute exactly. With two degrees of freedom something new appears: the system has two frequencies and its own characteristic oscillation for each. We show this on a rigid bar on two springs. At the end we test what a load does to the oscillation, on an inverted bar with a spring and on the toggle mechanism. The frequency drops to zero exactly where the equilibrium loses its stability. The same question, when a loaded column bends, we will pose again in section 8.14 without time, with energy.

8.13.1 Linearization (linearizacija)

Take the system of section 8.9: \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) (definition 8.45), ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and conservative forces with a potential \(V(\vek q)\) (definition 8.53). The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.86), where \(\mat M(\vek q)\) is a symmetric positive definite matrix that changes with the coordinates. By corollary 8.58 an equilibrium is a position \(\vek q_0\) in which all derivatives \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are zero. We shift the coordinates so that we measure displacements from the equilibrium, write \(\vek q - \vek q_0\) again as \(\vek q\), and the equilibrium is at \(\vek q = \vek 0\). A shift by a constant does not change any derivative of the functions \(V\) and \(\mat M\).

For the pendulum such a shift is unnecessary, because the equilibrium is already at \(\theta = 0\). With the mass matrix \(M = m\,l^2\) and \(V = -m g l\cos\theta\) Lagrange's equation is \(m l^2\,\ddot\theta + m g l\sin\theta = 0\). What do we discard if we replace \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\)? By lemma 8.19 on small angles, \(|\sin\theta - \theta| \le |\theta|^3/6\), so the discarded term compared with the kept one is smaller than \(\theta^2/6\). At \(10°\), that is \(0{,}1745\) rad, this bound is \(0{,}1745^2/6 = 0{,}51\) %. The actual error is \((\theta - \sin\theta)/\theta = 0{,}507\) %. At \(5°\) it is 0,13 %, at \(20°\) it is 2,0 %.

For a general system the idea is the same, only with more terms. We expand both energies about the equilibrium by Taylor (theorem 8.60). For the potential the linear term drops out, because \(\nabla V(\vek 0) = \vek 0\), and what remains is

\[V(\vek q) \approx V(\vek 0) + \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q , \qquad K_{\mathrm{sj}} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}(\vek 0) , \tag{8.106}\]

where \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix (definition 8.61), symmetric because mixed derivatives can be exchanged (theorem 8.30). For the kinetic energy \(\mat M(\vek q) \approx \mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\), so \(T \approx \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M_0\,\dot{\vek q}\). Both energies are quadratic forms, as in a system of springs and masses. The quadratic form \(\tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q\) is the energy of a spring, because its gradient \(\mat K\,\vek q\) has the form of a linear force, and the force is \(-\mat K\,\vek q\). This idea tells us which equation to expect. So that we really know the discarded terms and do not merely estimate them, we will write them out from the exact equations.

Theorem 8.120 Linearization of the equations of motion about an equilibrium (linearizacija gibalnih enačb okoli ravnovesja)

Let a mechanical system with \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) be as in theorem 8.90: ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and conservative forces with a potential \(V\). Let \(\vek q = \vek 0\) be an equilibrium, \(\nabla V(\vek 0) = \vek 0\). Let \(V\) be three times continuously differentiable and \(\mat M\) continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of the point \(\vek 0\). Let \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\) and let \(\mat K\) be the matrix of second derivatives of \(V\) at \(\vek 0\) as in (8.106). Then \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat K\) is symmetric, and every twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) of the system satisfies the equation

\[\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek R , \tag{8.107}\]

where the remainder \(\vek R\) satisfies the following. There are a number \(\rho > 0\) and a number \(c\), which depend on \(V\) and \(\mat M\), such that for every time at which \(|\vek q| \le \rho\) we have for every \(s\)

\[|R_{\mathrm{s}}| \le c\,\big(|\vek q|^2 + |\dot{\vek q}|^2 + |\vek q|\,|\ddot{\vek q}|\big) . \tag{8.108}\]
Proof

We must write the exact equations of motion, separate from them the terms that we keep, and then bound the remainder. Let \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\), and let \(\rho\) be so small that the closed ball \(|\vek q| \le \rho\) lies in an open set where \(V\) and \(\mat M\) are defined, \(V\) has continuous derivatives up to the third order and \(\mat M\) has continuous first derivatives. The notation \(|\cdot|\) is the length of a column, so \(|q_{\mathrm{j}}| \le |\vek q|\) for every element.

  1. The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} - V\). By theorem 8.90, for conservative forces \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0\) for every \(s\).
  2. Because \(\mat M\) is symmetric, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\). The element \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q(t))\) changes with time by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1), \(\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{sj}}/\mathrm{d}t = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. Because \(V\) does not depend on the velocities, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\).
  4. We insert steps 2 and 3 into step 1. We obtain the exact equation
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = 0 , \qquad g_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\Big(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}} - \tfrac12\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\Big)\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
    The term \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\) is a quadratic form of the velocities: a term with the square of the velocity that "mass times acceleration" alone would not give (section 8.9).
  5. We expand the derivative \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) along a segment. The function \(\gamma(\tau) = \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\tau\,\vek q)\) for \(\tau \in [0, 1]\) is twice continuously differentiable: we apply lemma 8.28 to \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) and then to \(\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\), which are continuously differentiable because \(V\) is three times continuously differentiable. We have \(\gamma(0) = \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\vek 0) = 0\) and \(\gamma'(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}}\). By theorem 8.60, \(\gamma(1) = \gamma(0) + \gamma'(0) + \tfrac12\,\gamma''(\tau_*)\) for some \(\tau_* \in (0, 1)\), with \(\gamma''(\tau_*) = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\tau_*\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\). All third derivatives are continuous on the closed bounded ball, so they have a largest absolute value there, let \(c_{\mathrm{V}}\) be the largest of them (theorem 8.26, applied to the derivative and to its negative). Because \(|q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}| \le |\vek q|^2\),
    \[\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}} + r_{\mathrm{s}} , \qquad |r_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac12\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}}\,|\vek q|^2 .\]
  6. We estimate the difference \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0)\) with the mean value theorem 1.84 on the function \(\tau \mapsto M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek q)\): it equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau_*\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{k}}\) for some \(\tau_*\), because the derivative of this function is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (lemma 8.28). Let \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\) be the largest absolute value of the derivatives \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball (theorem 8.26, as in step 5). Then \(|M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0)| \le n\,c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\vek q|\) and \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0))\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\vek q|\,|\ddot{\vek q}|\).
  7. From step 4 with steps 5 and 6 we get \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{0,\mathrm{sj}}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(R_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0))\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} - g_{\mathrm{s}} - r_{\mathrm{s}}\). By step 4 the term \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\) is a sum of \(n^2\) terms, each of which has absolute value at most \(\tfrac32\,c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\dot{\vek q}|^2\), so \(|g_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac32\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\dot{\vek q}|^2\). The inequality (8.108) holds with \(c = \max\big(\tfrac12\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}},\ \tfrac32\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}},\ n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\big)\).
  8. The matrices \(\mat M_0\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric: the first by theorem 8.86, the second because mixed derivatives can be exchanged (theorem 8.30). The positive definiteness of \(\mat M_0\) is part of the same theorem.

Source: GR (2.6)-(2.12) (PDF p. 79-80): expansion of both energies to second order and the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\). The exact equation in step 4 and the bound on the remainder are not from the source.

We proved the theorem by writing out every discarded term and finding a bound for it. We expanded \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) and \(\mat M\) in the same way as section 1.15 of chapter 1 expanded a field over a short step, and this is exactly what chapter 1 promised for the equations of motion near an equilibrium in linear form. The remainder is of second order: if \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}\) are of order \(\varepsilon\), then \(\vek R\) is of order \(\varepsilon^2\), while the kept terms are of order \(\varepsilon\). In the energy the discarded part is of third order and in the equation of second, because differentiation lowers the order by one. For the pendulum the remainder is even of third order, \(m g l\,(\theta - \sin\theta)\), because the third derivative of the potential at \(\theta = 0\) is zero. The theorem does not guarantee this, because it holds for every potential.

Definition 8.121 Linearized equation of motion (linearizirana enačba gibanja)

For the system and the equilibrium of theorem 8.120, the linearized equation of motion about the equilibrium is the equation

\[\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek 0 \tag{8.109}\]

for the column \(\vek q(t)\) of displacements from the equilibrium. We call the matrix \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\) the mass matrix (masna matrika), and the matrix \(\mat K\) of second derivatives of the potential at the equilibrium the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) of the linearized equation. Both are symmetric matrices with \(n \times n\) elements, and \(\mat M_0\) is positive definite.

Source: GR (2.12) (PDF p. 80): "the fundamental equation describing the free vibration of a conservative system around an equilibrium position".

Whether the equilibrium is really stable is decided by the stiffness matrix. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite (definition 6.27), then by theorem 8.63 \(V\) has a strict local minimum at the equilibrium, the equilibrium is stable (theorem 8.115), and all statements about frequencies proved below hold. We test the pivots of the matrix \(\mat K\) by lemma 8.62.

How large the error of a computation with such an equation is, the theorem does not say. It bounds by how much the equations differ at each moment, but not by how much the two solutions differ over the time \(t\). For this we would need an estimate of how a small remainder accumulates in the solution at each moment, and the book does not have such an estimate. The chapters on vibrations therefore take the linear equation as a model of the system, not as a proven approximation of its motion: we check its validity with numbers. The answer for the pendulum is in the next section. For the toggle mechanism of example D (section 8.13.4) we will test it with numbers.

8.13.2 One degree of freedom (ena prostostna stopnja)

For \(n = 1\) the mass and stiffness matrices are numbers, \(m = M_0 > 0\) and \(k = V''(0)\), and the linearized equation is \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = 0\). This is the equation that section 8.2 prepared: we look for the solution with a cosine and a sine, and theorem 8.17 covers all solutions. The pendulum has \(m = m l^2 = 1\) kg m\(^2\) and \(k = m g l = 9{,}81\) J, so \(k/m = g/l\).

What do we expect? For a spring of stiffness \(k\) and mass \(m\) the force is \(-k q\), the acceleration \(-(k/m)\,q\), and the cosine with \(\omega^2 = k/m\) has second derivative \(-\omega^2\) times itself. So we try \(q = \cos\omega t\). For \(k < 0\) such a function does not solve the equation, as we will see.

Theorem 8.122 Small oscillations of a system with one degree of freedom (majhna nihanja pri eni prostostni stopnji)

Let \(m > 0\) and \(k\) be numbers, and let \(q(t)\) be a twice differentiable solution of the equation \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = 0\) on an interval \(I\) that contains \(0\), with initial values \(q(0)\) and \(\dot q(0)\).

  1. If \(k > 0\), let \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\). The solution is unique and for \(t \in I\) it is
    \[q(t) = q(0)\,\cos\omega t + \frac{\dot q(0)}{\omega}\,\sin\omega t . \tag{8.110}\]
    The right-hand side is defined for every \(t\) and for every \(t\) we have \(q(t + 2\pi/\omega) = q(t)\), and the period (nihajni čas) \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) does not depend on the initial values. The displacement stays in absolute value below the amplitude (amplituda) \(a = \sqrt{q(0)^2 + (\dot q(0)/\omega)^2}\).
  2. If \(k < 0\), \(q(0) = \varepsilon > 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\), then at every time \(t \ge 0\) in \(I\) we have \(|q(t)| \ge \varepsilon\,\sqrt{|k|/m}\,t\).
Proof

For point 1 we show that the formula solves the equation and that it is the only solution. For point 2 we use the energy.

Point 1.

  1. We divide by \(m\): \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\), because \(k/m = \omega^2\).
  2. Denote the right-hand side of (8.110) by \(q_*(t)\). By theorem 8.12 (second expression, \(c = 0\)), \(q_*' = -\omega\,q(0)\sin\omega t + \dot q(0)\cos\omega t\) and \(q_*'' = -\omega^2 q(0)\cos\omega t - \omega\,\dot q(0)\sin\omega t = -\omega^2 q_*\). The values are \(q_*(0) = q(0)\) and \(q_*'(0) = \dot q(0)\), because \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\sin 0 = 0\).
  3. By theorem 8.17 with \(t_0 = 0\), every solution of the equation \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) on \(I\) equals \(y(0)\cos\omega t + (y'(0)/\omega)\sin\omega t\). For \(y = q\) this is \(q_*\), so \(q = q_*\) on \(I\) and the solution is unique.
  4. Cosine and sine have period \(2\pi\) (lemma 8.10, point 4), so \(\cos\omega(t + 2\pi/\omega) = \cos(\omega t + 2\pi) = \cos\omega t\) and the same for the sine. The period \(2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) contains only \(m\) and \(k\).
  5. The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,m\dot q^2 + \tfrac12\,k q^2\) has derivative \(\dot q\,(m\ddot q + k q) = 0\), so it is constant (corollary 1.85). At \(t = 0\), \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,m\dot q(0)^2 + \tfrac12\,k q(0)^2 = \tfrac12\,k\,\big(q(0)^2 + (\dot q(0)/\omega)^2\big) = \tfrac12\,k a^2\), because \(m = k/\omega^2\). Because \(\tfrac12\,m\dot q^2 \ge 0\), we have \(\tfrac12\,k q^2 \le \tfrac12\,k a^2\), so \(|q| \le a\).

Point 2. Let \(f(t) = \tfrac12\,m q^2\).

  1. The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}}\) is constant as in step 5 and at \(t = 0\) it is \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,k\varepsilon^2 = -\tfrac12\,|k|\,\varepsilon^2 < 0\).
  2. The first derivative is \(f' = m q\dot q\) and the second \(f'' = m\dot q^2 + m q\ddot q = m\dot q^2 - k q^2\). From \(k q^2 = 2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} - m\dot q^2\) it follows that \(f'' = 2 m\dot q^2 - 2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} \ge -2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = |k|\,\varepsilon^2\).
  3. The function \(h(t) = f'(t) - |k|\varepsilon^2 t\) has a nonnegative derivative by step 7, so \(h(t) - h(0) = h'(\xi)\,t \ge 0\) for \(t \ge 0\) (theorem 1.84), and \(h(0) = f'(0) = m\varepsilon\cdot 0 = 0\). So \(f' \ge |k|\varepsilon^2 t\) for \(t \ge 0\). By the same reasoning \(f(t) - \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2 \ge f(0) = \tfrac12\,m\varepsilon^2 > 0\), because this function has the derivative \(f' - |k|\varepsilon^2 t \ge 0\), so \(f(t) \ge \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2\).
  4. From \(\tfrac12\,m q^2 \ge \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2\) it follows that \(|q| \ge \varepsilon\,\sqrt{|k|/m}\,t\).

Source: KREYSZIG §2.4 (PDF p. 89): the solution of the equation \(m\ddot y + k y = 0\) as a harmonic oscillation. CK example 3.1 (PDF p. 87-88): with negative stiffness the solution is not oscillating but grows without oscillation ("divergence"). GR PDF p. 87: without positive definiteness the solution would grow exponentially. The proof by steps is not from the source, point 2 is the same argument as theorem 8.117, written for the linear equation.

Point 2 says that the solution leaves every neighbourhood of the equilibrium when \(k < 0\), as theorem 8.117 says for the true equation. How fast, the theorem does not say: the growth is in fact faster than linear. The exact solution contains a function that the book does not have yet and will introduce with damping in chapter 9. The pendulum in the upper position \(\theta = \pi\) has \(k = -m g l = -9{,}81\) J and \(k/m = -9{,}81\) 1/s\(^2\), so by point 2 the displacement grows at least by \(\varepsilon\cdot 3{,}132\,t\) (1/s) in the time \(t\).

For the pendulum about the equilibrium \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l} = 3{,}1321\) 1/s and period \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2{,}0061\) s. The linear solution for a deflection \(\theta_0\) and initial velocity zero is \(\theta = \theta_0\cos\omega_0 t\). The actual period we obtain from the conservation of energy, which for a conservative system is guaranteed by theorem 8.106.

Theorem 8.123 Period of the pendulum (nihajni čas nihala)

A point mass on a rigid weightless rod of length \(l\) in a gravitational field \(g\) has the equation of motion \(\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\sin\theta\). Let \(\theta(t)\) be a motion, defined for every time \(t\) (corollary 8.94 does not guarantee that such a motion exists, so this is an assumption), with \(\theta(0) = 0\) and \(\dot\theta(0) > 0\), in which \(\dot\theta\) first becomes zero at a time \(t_{\mathrm{a}} > 0\) at a deflection \(a = \theta(t_{\mathrm{a}}) \in (0, \pi)\), which we call the amplitude. With \(k = \sin(a/2)\) the time in which the deflection grows from \(0\) to \(a\) is

\[t_{\mathrm{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}}\,\mathrm{d}u ,\]

the motion repeats after the time \(T = 4\,t_{\mathrm{a}}\), which is the period, and

\[T = 4\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}}\,\mathrm{d}u . \tag{8.111}\]

As \(a \to 0\), \(T\) tends to \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

Proof

We must show that \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) is an integral, one that is not singular at the endpoint, that \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{a}}\) and that \(T \to T_0\). We obtain the time as the inverse function of the deflection, and the integral by a change of variable whose every step we prove.

  1. The energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\,m l^2\dot\theta^2 - m g l\cos\theta\) is constant (theorem 8.106). At \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) we have \(\dot\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = a\), so \(\mathcal{E} = -m g l\cos a\) and for every time \(\dot\theta^2 = \dfrac{2g}{l}\,(\cos\theta - \cos a)\).
  2. The function \(\dot\theta\) is continuous, positive at \(0\) and has no zero on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\), so by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.57) it is positive on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.84), \(\theta\) is strictly increasing on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}}]\), so \(0 < \theta < a\) on \((0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\). By lemma 8.8 the inverse function \(t(\theta)\) is continuous on \([0, a]\) and differentiable on \((0, a)\) with derivative \(t'(\theta) = 1/\dot\theta = 1\big/\sqrt{(2g/l)(\cos\theta - \cos a)}\) by step 1, with values \(t(0) = 0\) and \(t(a) = t_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  3. From the addition formula with \(\beta = \alpha\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) (corollary 8.15, lemma 8.10, point 1) we have \(\cos x = 1 - 2\sin^2(x/2)\), so \(\cos\theta - \cos a = 2\big(k^2 - \sin^2(\theta/2)\big)\).
  4. For \(\theta \in [0, a)\) we have \(0 \le \sin(\theta/2)/k < 1\), because \(\sin\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \pi/2]\) (its derivative \(\cos\) is positive on \([0, \pi/2)\), theorem 8.12, lemma 8.10, point 3) and \(\theta/2 < a/2 < \pi/2\). Let \(h\) be the inverse function of the sine on \([0, \pi/2]\), continuous with values \(h(0) = 0\) and \(h(1) = \pi/2\) and differentiable on \((0, 1)\) with derivative \(h'(y) = 1/\cos h(y)\) (lemma 8.8, theorem 2.57 for existence). Let \(u(\theta) = h\big(\sin(\theta/2)/k\big)\), so \(\sin u = \sin(\theta/2)/k\) and \(u \in [0, \pi/2)\) for \(\theta \in [0, a)\). The function \(u\) is continuous and differentiable on \((0, a)\) with derivative \(u' = \dfrac{1}{\cos u}\cdot\dfrac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2k}\), where \(\cos u > 0\). As \(\theta \to a\), \(\sin(\theta/2)/k \to 1\) and hence \(u \to h(1) = \pi/2\).
  5. By step 4, \(\cos^2(\theta/2) = 1 - k^2\sin^2 u\), and by step 3, \(\cos\theta - \cos a = 2k^2(1 - \sin^2 u) = 2k^2\cos^2 u\). So \(\sqrt{(2g/l)(\cos\theta - \cos a)} = 2k\cos u\sqrt{g/l}\) and \(t'(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{2k\cos u}\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\).
  6. Let \(F(u) = \int_0^u \mathrm{d}v\big/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 v}\). Because \(k = \sin(a/2) < 1\) (since \(a/2 < \pi/2\)), we have \(1 - k^2\sin^2 v \ge 1 - k^2 > 0\), the integrand is continuous on \([0, \pi/2]\) and \(F' = 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.40). The function \(w(\theta) = \sqrt{l/g}\,F(u(\theta))\) has by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 3) the derivative \(w' = \sqrt{l/g}\,\dfrac{u'}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}} = \sqrt{l/g}\,\dfrac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2k\cos u}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\cos(\theta/2)} = \dfrac{1}{2k\cos u}\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\), where we used step 4 for \(u'\) and step 5 for the root, and \(\cos(\theta/2) > 0\) because \(\theta/2 < \pi/2\). So \(w' = t'\) on \((0, a)\) by step 5, both functions are continuous on \([0, a)\) and \(w(0) = t(0) = 0\). The function \(t - w\) has zero derivative, so on every \([0, \theta_1]\) with \(\theta_1 < a\) it is constant, that is zero (corollary 1.85): \(t(\theta) = w(\theta)\) for \(\theta \in [0, a)\).
  7. As \(\theta \to a\), \(t(\theta) \to t_{\mathrm{a}}\) (continuity, step 2) and \(w(\theta) \to \sqrt{l/g}\,F(\pi/2)\) (step 4 and continuity of \(F\)). Hence \(t_{\mathrm{a}} = \sqrt{l/g}\,F(\pi/2)\), which is the first formula of the theorem.
  8. The equation of motion does not change if we replace \(\theta(t)\) by \(-\theta(-t)\) or by \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t)\), because \(\sin\) is an odd function and the equation contains only the second derivative. The motion \(-\theta(-t)\) has at \(t = 0\) the state \((0, \dot\theta(0))\), the same as \(\theta\), and the motion \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t)\) has at \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) the state \((a, 0)\), the same as \(\theta\). By corollary 8.94 on the uniqueness of the motion for a given state they equal \(\theta(t)\): \(\theta(-t) = -\theta(t)\) and \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t) = \theta(t)\). Hence \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} + s) = \theta(-s) = -\theta(s)\) and \(\theta(t + 4t_{\mathrm{a}}) = -\theta(t + 2t_{\mathrm{a}}) = \theta(t)\): the motion repeats after \(4t_{\mathrm{a}}\), and in the intermediate time \(2t_{\mathrm{a}}\) the deflection is opposite.
  9. From \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{a}}\) we get (8.111). For \(0 \le k < 1\) we have \(1 \le 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u} \le 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2}\), so \(\pi/2 \le F(\pi/2) \le (\pi/2)/\sqrt{1 - k^2}\). As \(a \to 0\), \(k = \sin(a/2) \to 0\) (continuity of the sine), so \(F(\pi/2) \to \pi/2\) and \(T \to 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

The error of the linear equation has one more form that theorem 8.120 does not touch. The small difference of the periods \(T - T_0\) accumulates with every swing. The linear solution \(\theta_0\cos\omega_0 t\) and the actual motion differ after \(N\) swings in phase by \(N\,(T - T_0)/T_0\) of a swing, and are in opposite phase when this is \(\tfrac12\). At \(10°\) this is after \(N = T_0/\big(2(T - T_0)\big) = 262\) swings, so after 527 s. In the first 10 s the difference of the deflections does not exceed \(0{,}569°\) (numerical solution of the equation of motion with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method). At a deflection of \(90°\), however, the two solutions differ in 10 s, that is in 4,2 swings, by up to \(178{,}8°\), almost opposite in phase. The linear equation is therefore good as long as the deflections are small and the time is short compared with \(N\,T\).

8.13.3 Two degrees of freedom (dve prostostni stopnji)

With one degree of freedom there is a single oscillation. What happens when the system can move in two ways? Take a rigid bar of length \(L = 2\) m and mass \(m = 30\) kg that lies horizontally on two vertical springs (example C from sections 8.4 and 8.8). The left end is held by a spring of stiffness \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m, the right by a spring of stiffness \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m. A guide excludes horizontal displacement, which is an idealization: a real support would also allow horizontal motion. The coordinates are the vertical displacement of the centre of mass \(y\) (upward) and the rotation \(\varphi\) (counterclockwise). The moment of inertia of the bar about the centre of mass is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m L^2/12 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), and the kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,m\dot y^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\dot\varphi^2\) (theorem 8.86), so \(\mat M = \operatorname{diag}(30, 10)\) in the units kg and kg m\(^2\). The potential is

\[V = \tfrac12\,k_1\big(y - \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\big)^2 + \tfrac12\,k_2\big(y + \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\big)^2 + m g y ,\]

where \(y \mp \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\) are the vertical displacements of the left and right ends. The equilibrium is not at \(y = 0\), because the bar hangs on the springs under its own weight: from \(\partial V/\partial y = \partial V/\partial\varphi = 0\) we get \(y = -6{,}1313\) mm and \(\varphi = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad (the ends are 7,3575 mm and 4,9050 mm lower, which is \(-mg/(2k_1)\) and \(-mg/(2k_2)\), as if each spring carried half of the weight, because the ends are equally far from the centre of mass, and the equilibrium of a rigid body, definition 2.3, requires that each spring carry half of the weight). From now on we count the coordinates \(y\) and \(\varphi\) from this equilibrium. The Hessian matrix of the potential at it is \(\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 50\,000 & 9999{,}99 \\ 9999{,}99 & 49\,999{,}9 \end{bmatrix}\), which differs from the matrix at the point \(y = \varphi = 0\), \(\begin{bmatrix} 50\,000 & 10\,000 \\ 10\,000 & 50\,000 \end{bmatrix}\), by at most about \(0{,}075\) N m, that is by \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\) of its value. A computation with that matrix therefore gives the same result, and we write it with the elements \(K_{\mathrm{yy}} = k_1 + k_2 = 50\,000\) N/m, \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} = (k_2 - k_1)\,L/2 = 10\,000\) N and \(K_{\varphi\varphi} = (k_1 + k_2)\,(L/2)^2 = 50\,000\) N m. The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite: its pivots are \(50\,000\) and \(50\,000 - 10\,000^2/50\,000 = 48\,000\) (lemma 8.62).

Why is \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} \ne 0\)? Because the springs are different. If they were equal, \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} = 0\): the springs would resist the upward displacement of the centre of mass with equal forces on equal arms, so without a moment about the centre of mass. With different springs the coordinates are linked, coupled (sklopljeni): the generalized force in the coordinate \(y\), \(-K_{\mathrm{yy}}\,y - K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi}\,\varphi\), also contains \(\varphi\).

We ask whether the bar can oscillate so that \(y\) and \(\varphi\) move with the same frequency and reach zero together, that is \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) for a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\). With one degree of freedom this is the only form of the solution. The following definition names what we are looking for.

Definition 8.124 Natural frequency and mode shape (lastna frekvenca in način nihanja)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be symmetric matrices with \(n \times n\) elements, \(\mat M\) positive definite, and let the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) be given (definition 8.121). A number \(\omega > 0\) is a natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) of the system if there is a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) elements for which

\[\mat K\,\vek u = \omega^2\,\mat M\,\vek u . \tag{8.112}\]

The column \(\vek u\) is then a mode shape (način nihanja) at the frequency \(\omega\), and the motion \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) is an oscillation in this mode. A point of the system that does not move in some mode is a node (vozel) of this mode. The frequency in hertz is \(f = \omega/(2\pi)\), the number of oscillations per second.

Source: GR section 2.2, (2.22)-(2.33) (PDF p. 85-87): synchronous motions with the same time dependence of all coordinates, the eigenproblem, "eigenmode", "circular frequency" and the relation \(\nu = \omega/2\pi\). The concept of a node and the form of the definition are not from the source.

For \(n = 1\) this is the equation \(k\,u = \omega^2 m\,u\), so \(\omega^2 = k/m\) as in theorem 8.122. For \(n = 2\) we transform it into a condition on a determinant.

Theorem 8.125 Natural frequencies of a system with two degrees of freedom (lastne frekvence sistema z dvema prostostnima stopnjama)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be symmetric positive definite matrices with \(2 \times 2\) elements (definition 6.27). Then:

  1. The motion \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) with a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) is a solution of the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) exactly when \((\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\,\vek u = \vek 0\). Such a \(\vek u\) exists exactly when \(\det(\mat K - \omega^2\mat M) = 0\).
  2. The equation \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\,\mat M) = 0\) for \(\lambda\) is quadratic and has two real roots \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\), both positive. The natural frequencies are \(\omega_1 = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\) and \(\omega_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_2}\).
  3. We have \(\lambda_1 \le \min(K_{11}/M_{11},\ K_{22}/M_{22})\) and \(\lambda_2 \ge \max(K_{11}/M_{11},\ K_{22}/M_{22})\).
Proof

Point 1 we get by substitution, points 2 and 3 from the properties of a quadratic polynomial.

  1. For \(\vek q = \vek u\cos\omega t\) we have by theorem 8.12 \(\ddot{\vek q} = -\omega^2\vek u\cos\omega t\), so \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = (\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\,\vek u\cos\omega t\). This column is zero for every \(t\) exactly when it is zero at \(t = 0\), where \(\cos 0 = 1\).
  2. Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with \(2 \times 2\) elements and \(\det\mat A = A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21}\). Lemma 2.53 of chapter 2 says this for \(3 \times 3\) matrices, here we prove it for \(2 \times 2\). If \(\mat A\vek u = \vek 0\) and \(u_1 \ne 0\), we multiply the first row \(A_{11}u_1 + A_{12}u_2 = 0\) by \(A_{22}\) and the second \(A_{21}u_1 + A_{22}u_2 = 0\) by \(A_{12}\) and subtract: \((A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21})\,u_1 = 0\), so \(\det\mat A = 0\). If \(u_2 \ne 0\), the same with multiplication by \(A_{21}\) and \(A_{11}\). Conversely, let \(\det\mat A = 0\). If the first row is not zero, \(\vek u = (A_{12}, -A_{11}) \ne \vek 0\) solves the first row, and the second row gives \(A_{21}A_{12} - A_{22}A_{11} = -\det\mat A = 0\). If the first row is zero and the second is not, we take \(\vek u = (A_{22}, -A_{21})\), if both are zero, any \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\). This proves point 1, because \(\mat A = \mat K - \omega^2\mat M\).
  3. The polynomial \(p(\lambda) = \det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\) equals \((K_{11} - \lambda M_{11})(K_{22} - \lambda M_{22}) - (K_{12} - \lambda M_{12})^2\) and also \(a\lambda^2 - b\lambda + c\) with \(a = M_{11}M_{22} - M_{12}^2 = \det\mat M\), \(b = K_{11}M_{22} + K_{22}M_{11} - 2K_{12}M_{12}\) and \(c = \det\mat K\).
  4. By lemma 8.62, for a positive definite \(2 \times 2\) matrix the first diagonal element and the determinant are positive, so \(a > 0\), \(c > 0\), \(K_{11} > 0\) and \(M_{11} > 0\). Also \(K_{22} > 0\) and \(M_{22} > 0\) from definition 6.27 with the column \((0, 1)\).
  5. Let \(\lambda_* = K_{11}/M_{11} > 0\). The first factor in \(p(\lambda_*)\) is zero, so \(p(\lambda_*) = -(K_{12} - \lambda_* M_{12})^2 \le 0\).
  6. For a quadratic polynomial \(4a\,p(\lambda) = (2a\lambda - b)^2 - \Delta\) with \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\) (completing the square). By step 5 and \(a > 0\), \((2a\lambda_* - b)^2 \le \Delta\), so \(\Delta \ge 0\) and the solutions \(\lambda_{1,2} = (b \mp \sqrt\Delta)/(2a)\) are real. The inequality \(p(\lambda) \le 0\) holds exactly for \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda \le \lambda_2\), so \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_* \le \lambda_2\).
  7. The product of the solutions is \(c/a > 0\), so the solutions have the same sign. Because \(\lambda_2 \ge \lambda_* > 0\), both are positive. This proves point 2, because by point 1 every positive solution \(\lambda = \omega^2\) gives a frequency.
  8. Point 3 for \(K_{11}/M_{11}\) is step 6. For \(K_{22}/M_{22}\) we repeat the procedure with the indices exchanged \(1 \leftrightarrow 2\), which does not change \(p\).

Source: The proof for \(2 \times 2\) is not from the source. GR (2.25)-(2.31) (PDF p. 86) shows for a general order that the solutions are real and positive, by multiplying the eigenproblem by the conjugate column.

The proof derived more than we asked. Point 3 says where the frequencies fall relative to what we would get if we treated each coordinate by itself: the lower one is below both, the upper one above both. The coupling pushes the frequencies apart.

What does this mean for the bar on springs? The determinant is \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M) = (50\,000 - 30\lambda)(50\,000 - 10\lambda) - 10\,000^2 = 300\lambda^2 - 2\cdot 10^6\lambda + 2{,}4\cdot 10^9\), and its zeros are

\[\lambda_1 = 1569{,}50\ \text{1/s}^2 , \quad \lambda_2 = 5097{,}17\ \text{1/s}^2 , \qquad \omega_1 = 39{,}617\ \text{1/s} , \quad \omega_2 = 71{,}394\ \text{1/s} ,\]

that is \(f_1 = 6{,}305\) Hz and \(f_2 = 11{,}363\) Hz. The steps of the proof indeed come true: \(a = 300\) and \(c = 2{,}4\cdot 10^9\) are positive, \(\lambda_* = 50\,000/30 = 1666{,}7\) lies between the zeros, and \(p(\lambda_*) = -(10\,000 - 0)^2 = -10^8\). If the springs were equal, \(k_1 = k_2 = 25\) kN/m, the matrix \(\mat K\) would be diagonal with elements \(50\,000\) and \(50\,000\) and the frequencies \(\sqrt{50\,000/30} = 40{,}825\) and \(\sqrt{50\,000/10} = 70{,}711\) 1/s, that is pure bouncing and pure rotation. The different springs move them to \(39{,}617\) and \(71{,}394\) 1/s, the lower one down by 1,2 1/s, the upper one up by 0,68 1/s. Point 3 of the theorem says that this direction of the shift is always so.

We get the modes from the first row of equation (8.112), as in step 2 of the proof: \(\vek u = (K_{12} - \lambda M_{12},\ -(K_{11} - \lambda M_{11})) = (10\,000,\ -(50\,000 - 30\lambda))\). We normalize them to \(y = 1\) m to see the rotation:

\[\vek u_1 = (1\ \text{m},\ -0{,}2915\ \text{rad}) , \qquad \vek u_2 = (1\ \text{m},\ 10{,}2915\ \text{rad}) .\]

The second rows of both are satisfied (the residual is below \(10^{-100}\), computation in SymPy). In mode 1 the centre of mass and the ends move in the same direction: at \(y = 1\) m the ends are at heights \(y - \varphi\cdot 1\,\text{m} = 1{,}2915\) m and \(0{,}7085\) m, so it is mostly bouncing with a small tilt. A point of the bar at distance \(x\) from the centre of mass moves by \(y + x\varphi\), and this is zero at \(x = -y/\varphi\), that is \(3{,}4305\) m to the right of the centre of mass, outside the bar (the end is at \(1\) m). In mode 2 the ends are at \(-9{,}2915\) m and \(11{,}2915\) m, so in opposite directions, it is mostly rotation about a node that is \(0{,}0972\) m to the left of the centre of mass. The node lies near the centre of mass, because the rotation is mostly about the middle, and slightly to the left, because the right spring is stiffer (\(k_2 > k_1\)) and holds that side of the bar more. Figure 8.37 shows both modes.

To conclude this statement we still need that the two modes really span all motions. If \(\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2\), the following holds.

Corollary 8.126 Motion as a sum of modes (gibanje kot vsota načinov)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\) be as in theorem 8.125 and \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2\), and let \(\vek u_1\), \(\vek u_2\) be the modes at \(\omega_1\), \(\omega_2\). Then \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\,\vek u_2 = 0\), and the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with initial values \(\vek q(0)\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0)\) has exactly one solution,

\[\begin{aligned} \vek q(t) &= \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{2}\Big(\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t \\ &\quad + \beta_{\mathrm{r}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\Big)\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}} , \\ \alpha_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q(0)}{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}} , \\ \beta_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0)}{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}} . \end{aligned} \tag{8.113}\]
Proof

We must show the orthogonality of the modes in the sense of \(\mat M\), from it the expansion of an arbitrary column, then the solution and its uniqueness.

  1. From \(\mat K\vek u_1 = \lambda_1\mat M\vek u_1\) we multiply from the left by \(\vek u_2^\T\), from \(\mat K\vek u_2 = \lambda_2\mat M\vek u_2\) by \(\vek u_1^\T\): \(\vek u_2^\T\mat K\vek u_1 = \lambda_1\vek u_2^\T\mat M\vek u_1\) and \(\vek u_1^\T\mat K\vek u_2 = \lambda_2\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2\). The left-hand sides are equal, because a number equals its transpose and \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) are symmetric. Because also \(\vek u_2^\T\mat M\vek u_1 = \vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2\), we have \((\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2 = 0\), so \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2 = 0\).
  2. The columns \(\vek u_1\), \(\vek u_2\) are independent: if \(c_1\vek u_1 + c_2\vek u_2 = \vek 0\), we multiply from the left by \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\) and get \(c_1\,\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_1 = 0\). Because \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_1 > 0\), so \(c_1 = 0\) and likewise \(c_2 = 0\). Two independent columns with two elements span all columns, so every \(\vek v = c_1\vek u_1 + c_2\vek u_2\), and by multiplying with \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\) we get by step 1 \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v\,/\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. Every term of the sum (8.113) is a solution of the equation: for \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) by theorem 8.125, point 1, for \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) likewise, because the sine has the same second derivative \(-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) times itself (theorem 8.12). The equation is linear, so the sum is also a solution.
  4. At \(t = 0\) the sum equals \(\alpha_1\vek u_1 + \alpha_2\vek u_2 = \vek q(0)\) by step 2, and its derivative \(\omega_1\beta_1\vek u_1 + \omega_2\beta_2\vek u_2 = \dot{\vek q}(0)\) by the same step with \(\vek v = \dot{\vek q}(0)\).
  5. Uniqueness. Let \(\vek q\) and \(\tilde{\vek q}\) be solutions with the same initial values and \(\vek w = \vek q - \tilde{\vek q}\). The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek w}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek w} + \tfrac12\,\vek w^\T\mat K\vek w\) has derivative \(\dot{\vek w}^\T(\mat M\ddot{\vek w} + \mat K\vek w) = 0\) (symmetry of both matrices), so it is constant (corollary 1.85), and at \(t = 0\) it is zero. Because both quadratic forms are positive definite, we must have \(\dot{\vek w} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek w = \vek 0\) at every time.

Source: GR section 2.3, (2.39), (2.40) (PDF p. 88): the same orthogonality by subtracting two equations. GR PDF p. 87: an arbitrary combination of modes is a solution that is usually not synchronous. The proof by steps is not from the source.

For \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\) the matrix \(\mat K\) would be a multiple of the matrix \(\mat M\), which we do not treat here. A general order \(n\) needs the theorem on eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix, which is brought by chapter 10.

We test the expansion on the bar. Let the bar be released at \(t = 0\) from \(y = 10\) mm, \(\varphi = 0\), without velocity. By equation (8.113), \(\alpha_1\vek u_1 = (9{,}725\ \text{mm},\ -2{,}835\cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{rad})\) and \(\alpha_2\vek u_2 = (0{,}275\ \text{mm},\ +2{,}835\cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{rad})\), and \(\beta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). The sum gives \(y = 10\) mm and \(\varphi = 0\) at \(t = 0\). The centre of mass oscillates mostly in mode 1 with amplitude 9,725 mm, with a small influence of mode 2 (0,275 mm, 2,7 % of the whole), and the rotation is the difference of two oscillations of equal size with different frequencies: zero at \(t = 0\), then different from zero.

The coordinates \(y\) and \(\varphi\) are not the only possible choice. Take instead the vertical displacements of the ends, \(y_1 = y - \varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + \varphi\) (\(L/2 = 1\) m, \(\sin\varphi \approx \varphi\) by lemma 8.19). Then \(\vek q = \mat P\,\vek q'\) for \(\vek q' = (y_1, y_2)\) and \(\mat P = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). The energies are written in the new coordinates with the transformed matrices \(\mat K' = \mat P^\T\mat K\mat P\) and \(\mat M' = \mat P^\T\mat M\mat P\):

\[\mat K' = \begin{bmatrix} 20\,000 & 0 \\ 0 & 30\,000 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M' = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 5 & 10 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The coupling has moved from the stiffness matrix to the mass matrix: the springs are now each at its own end and independent, while the masses are felt at both ends. The determinant is \(75\lambda^2 - 5\cdot 10^5\lambda + 6\cdot 10^8 = 0\), which is the same polynomial divided by \(4\), and the frequencies are equal. Why? Because \(\mat P\) is invertible (determinant \(\tfrac12 \ne 0\)), \(\mat P^\T(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\mat P\,\vek u' = \vek 0\) exactly when \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\mat P\vek u' = \vek 0\). The same mode is therefore \(\vek u = \mat P\vek u'\) in the old coordinates, and the frequencies do not depend on the choice of coordinates. The coordinates determine only how we write the modes, not the physics.

8.13.4 Loaded structures: the frequency drops (obremenjene konstrukcije: frekvenca pada)

What does a load do to the oscillation? The inverted rigid bar with a spring (example B from section 8.6) has the equilibrium \(\theta = 0\) at every load \(P\), as we saw. Section 8.6 showed that the equilibrium is stable for \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}/L\), where \(V''(0) = k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L\). Now we put a point mass \(m = 10\) kg at the end of the bar. The bar of length \(L = 1\) m is weightless, the rotational spring has stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 2000\) N m, the load \(P\) is vertical and constant. This is our model, not from a source. The closest model in a source is CK example 3.1 (PDF p. 87-88), an inverted pendulum on two side springs with natural frequency \(\sqrt{2k/m - g/L}\), which has the same form: the stiffness of the springs minus a term proportional to the load.

The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,m L^2\dot\theta^2\), so \(M_0 = m L^2 = 10\) kg m\(^2\) and \(\mat M\) does not depend on the angle. The potential is \(V(\theta) = \tfrac12\,k_{\mathrm{r}}\theta^2 + P L\cos\theta\), where \(P L\cos\theta\) is the potential of the load (the height of the end of the bar is \(L\cos\theta\)). By theorem 8.120 the linearized equation is

\[\begin{aligned} m L^2\,\ddot\theta + (k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)\,\theta &= 0 , \\ \omega^2 &= \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L}{m L^2} \\ &= \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{m L^2}\Big(1 - \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{cr}}}\Big) \\ &= 200\,\Big(1 - \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{cr}}}\Big)\ \text{1/s}^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.114}\]

The term \(-P L\) in the stiffness matrix comes from \(P L\cos\theta \approx P L - \tfrac12\,P L\,\theta^2\): the load descends when the bar tilts and releases energy, which reduces the restoring force.

The square of the frequency falls along a line with slope \(-200/P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) and reaches zero at \(P_{\mathrm{cr}} = 2000\) N (figure 8.38). The frequency itself falls ever faster, because \(\omega = \sqrt{200\,(1 - P/P_{\mathrm{cr}})}\) is a root: at 0,9 \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) it is still 31,6 % of the original, at 0,99 only 10 %. The period then grows toward infinity: the bar returns ever more slowly. At exactly \(P = P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) the linear equation gives \(\ddot\theta = 0\) and says nothing about stability: a higher derivative decides (section 8.6.2, where \(V''''(0) = k_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) and the equilibrium is indeed stable). For \(P > P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) we have \(k < 0\) and by point 2 of theorem 8.122 the displacement grows: at \(P = 1{,}05\,P_{\mathrm{cr}}\), \(k/m = -10\) 1/s\(^2\) and \(|\theta| \ge \varepsilon\cdot 3{,}16\,t\). We can therefore use the frequency as a measure of the distance from the loss of stability: GR writes that at \(\omega = 0\) we get an eigenproblem with critical loads (GR (5.43), (5.44), PDF p. 364).

The same happens with the toggle mechanism (example D from sections 8.5 and 8.8). Two bars of \(a = 0{,}5\) m each, a pin at \(A\), a spring \(k = 10\) kN/m, a load \(P\) at the hinge \(B\). The potential is \(V(\theta) = \tfrac12\,k\,(2a)^2\,(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)^2 + P a\sin\theta\) with \(\theta_0 = 30°\), and the mass is \(m(\theta) = \tfrac13 + 2\sin^2\theta\) kg m\(^2\) (bars of 2 kg each, slider 1 kg). Lagrange's equation is \(m(\theta)\,\ddot\theta + \tfrac12\,m'(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 + V'(\theta) = 0\), and the term with the square of the velocity is exactly \(g\) from theorem 8.120. At \(P = 100\) N there are three equilibria (section 8.6): \(28{,}95°\) stable (\(V'' = 2239{,}3\) J), \(2{,}15°\) unstable (\(V'' = -1319{,}6\) J) and \(-30{,}94°\) stable. We measure the displacement from the equilibrium angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\), at the equilibrium \(\theta_{\mathrm{e}} = 28{,}95°\) we have \(M_0 = m(\theta_{\mathrm{e}}) = 0{,}80185\) kg m\(^2\), not \(m(30°) = 0{,}8333\) kg m\(^2\), because the equilibrium is at a different angle than the unstretched spring. Therefore

\[\omega = \sqrt{\frac{V''(\theta_{\mathrm{e}})}{m(\theta_{\mathrm{e}})}} = \sqrt{\frac{2239{,}3}{0{,}80185}} = 52{,}846\ \text{1/s} , \qquad T = 0{,}11890\ \text{s} .\]

At the unstable equilibrium \(2{,}15°\) we have \(k/m = -1319{,}6/0{,}33614 = -3926\) 1/s\(^2\), and by point 2 of theorem 8.122 the displacement grows at least as \(62{,}66\,\varepsilon\,t\) (1/s).

How close is the linear period to the true one? We integrated the equation \(m(\theta)\ddot\theta + \tfrac12\,m'(\theta)\dot\theta^2 + V'(\theta) = 0\) with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method and measured the period from successive largest displacements. At an amplitude of 0,01 rad, that is \(0{,}57°\), the measured period is \(0{,}11892\) s, which is 1,00016 times the linear one. At an amplitude of 0,1 rad (5,7°) it is \(0{,}12138\) s, 1,0209 times, that is 2,1 % longer. For the pendulum at \(0{,}1745\) rad (10°) it was 0,19 %. For the pendulum the correction at 0,1 rad would be 0,06 % (by \(1 + a^2/16\)), here it is 33 times larger. Where from? At ten times smaller amplitude the correction decreases 130 times, close to 100 times, as expected of a correction proportional to the square of the amplitude. We integrated the equation at the amplitude 0,1 rad also in two modified forms. With the mass frozen at \(m(\theta_{\mathrm{e}})\) at \(\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the true potential the period is 1,0400 times the linear one. With the mass \(m(\theta)\) and the potential replaced by the parabola \(\tfrac12\,V''(\theta_{\mathrm{e}})\,(\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{e}})^2\) it is only 1,00055 times. The correction therefore comes from the potential, whose third derivative \(V''' = 12\,706\) J and fourth \(V'''' = 13\,705\) J are significant at this amplitude compared with \(V'' = 2239\) J (\(V''' a/V'' = 0{,}57\)), while the potential of the pendulum has \(V''' = 0\) and \(V'''' = -m g l\). The variable mass reduces the correction (4,0 % to 2,1 %).

The frequency also falls as the load grows along the stable branch. For the stable equilibrium at different \(P\) the natural frequencies are \(54{,}772\) (at \(P = 0\)), \(52{,}846\) (\(100\) N), \(47{,}328\) (\(300\) N), \(34{,}003\) (\(500\) N), \(24{,}564\) (\(540\) N), \(17{,}260\) (\(550\) N) and \(11{,}147\) 1/s (\(552{,}5\) N), which goes toward zero at the limiting force \(P_{\max} = 553{,}01\) N (section 8.6). There the stable and the unstable equilibrium merge and \(V''\) is zero. A limit point (mejna točka) loses stability differently from the bifurcation of the bar with a spring, but the frequency drops to zero in the same way. The bar with a spring and the toggle mechanism are thus two different losses of stability with the same sign in the frequency.

8.13.5 Where next (kam naprej)

With this we have gone from Lagrange's equations to the equation \(\mat M_0\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\), solved it for one and two degrees of freedom and seen that a load reduces the frequencies. Three things remain for the next chapters. First, damping and forces: the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = f(t)\), its solution in closed form and the response to an impulse, which are in chapter 9. Second, order \(n\): for arbitrary \(n\) we need the theorem on eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix, the normalization of modes and the expansion of the motion in them, which is in chapter 10, where the \(2 \times 2\) determinant is replaced by the eigenproblem of matrices. Third, continuous systems: a string, a shaft and a beam have infinitely many natural frequencies, which is in chapter 13. Section 8.14, however, will find the critical force of a column without time, as the limit at which the second variation of the energy stops being positive, and this is the same condition at which the frequency here is zero.
