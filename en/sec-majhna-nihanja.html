Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.13 Small oscillations about an equilibrium (majhna nihanja okoli ravnovesja)

A pendulum that we deflect by a few degrees and release swings with a period that hardly changes when we increase the deflection. At a deflection of 5° one swing lasts 2,0070 s, at 10° it lasts 2,0099 s, so only 0,14 % longer. The pendulum equation, \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\), has no solution in the functions we know. The equation \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\,\theta = 0\), however, we can solve with the sine and cosine of section 8.2. In section 8.6 we called an equilibrium stable when the energy \(V\) has a strict local minimum there, and in section 8.12 we proved that a motion that starts near such an equilibrium stays near. Now we ask what this motion looks like. Which simpler equation does it follow for any system of this chapter, and how close is it to the true equation?

The route is this. First, from Lagrange's equations about an equilibrium we derive an equation that is linear in the displacements and their derivatives, and we bound what is discarded on the way. Then we solve it completely for one degree of freedom and compare it with the pendulum, whose period we can compute exactly. With two degrees of freedom something new appears: the system has two frequencies and its own characteristic oscillation for each. We show this on a rigid bar on two springs. At the end we test what a load does to the oscillation, on an inverted bar with a spring and on the toggle mechanism. The frequency drops to zero exactly where the equilibrium loses its stability. The same question, when a loaded column bends, we will pose again in section 8.14 without time, with energy.

8.13.1 Linearization (linearizacija)

Take the system of section 8.9: \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) (definition 8.46), ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and conservative forces with a potential \(V(\vek q)\) (definition 8.58). For the bar on two springs the coordinates are \(y\) and \(\varphi\), for the toggle mechanism there is a single one, the angle \(\theta\). The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.97), where \(\mat M(\vek q)\) is a symmetric positive definite matrix that changes with the coordinates. By corollary 8.63 an equilibrium is a position \(\vek q_0\) in which all derivatives \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are zero. We shift the coordinates so that we measure displacements from the equilibrium, write \(\vek q - \vek q_0\) again as \(\vek q\), and the equilibrium is at \(\vek q = \vek 0\). A shift by a constant does not change any derivative of the functions \(V\) and \(\mat M\).

For the pendulum such a shift is unnecessary, because the equilibrium is already at \(\theta = 0\). With the mass matrix \(M = m\,l^2\) and \(V = -m g l\cos\theta\) Lagrange's equation is \(m l^2\,\ddot\theta + m g l\sin\theta = 0\). What do we discard if we replace \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\)? In figure 8.145, top right, the horizontal axis is the angle \(\theta\), the blackwhite curve is the term \(m g l\sin\theta\) for \(m = 1\) kg and \(l = 1\) m, and the dashed blue line is the term \(k\,\theta\) with \(k = m g l = 9{,}81\) J, which we keep. The line is the tangent of the curve at \(\theta = 0\), so the curve fits it near zero. The discarded term is the vertical distance \(r\) between them, at \(60°\) already 1,78 N m. By lemma 8.20 on small angles, \(|\sin\theta - \theta| \le |\theta|^3/6\), so the discarded term compared with the kept one is smaller than \(\theta^2/6\). This ratio is shown by the graph at the bottom right: the blackwhite curve is the actual share \((\theta - \sin\theta)/\theta\), the dashed blue one is the bound \(\theta^2/6\), which lies just above it. At \(10°\), that is \(0{,}1745\) rad, this bound is \(0{,}1745^2/6 = 0{,}51\) %. The actual error is \((\theta - \sin\theta)/\theta = 0{,}507\) %. At \(5°\) it is 0,13 %, at \(20°\) it is 2,0 %.

For a general system the idea is the same, only with more terms. We expand both energies about the equilibrium by Taylor (theorem 8.68). For the potential the linear term drops out, because \(\nabla V(\vek 0) = \vek 0\), and what remains is

\[V(\vek q) \approx V(\vek 0) + \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q , \qquad K_{\mathrm{sj}} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}(\vek 0) , \tag{8.139}\]

where \(\mat K\) is the Hessian matrix (definition 8.69), symmetric because mixed derivatives can be exchanged (theorem 8.31). For the pendulum this is the graph at the top left: the blackwhite curve is \(V(\theta) - V(0) = m g l\,(1 - \cos\theta)\), the dashed blue parabola is \(\tfrac12\,k\,\theta^2\) with \(k = V''(0) = m g l\). The bottom of the valley is close to a parabola, they separate only at large angles, at \(90°\) by 2,29 J. For the kinetic energy \(\mat M(\vek q) \approx \mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\), so \(T \approx \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M_0\,\dot{\vek q}\). For the pendulum \(M = m l^2\) is constant, for the toggle mechanism the mass changes with the angle. At the bottom left is its mass \(m(\theta)\) from equation (8.100), and the dashed blue line is the value \(M_0\) at the equilibrium \(\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\), which the linearization keeps for all positions. Both energies are quadratic forms, as in a system of springs and masses. The quadratic form \(\tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q\) is the energy of a spring, because its gradient \(\mat K\,\vek q\) has the form of a linear force, and the force is \(-\mat K\,\vek q\). For the pendulum the gradient is the line \(k\,\theta\). This idea tells us which equation to expect. So that we really know the discarded terms and do not merely estimate them, we will write them out from the exact equations.

Theorem 8.136 Linearization of the equations of motion about an equilibrium (linearizacija gibalnih enačb okoli ravnovesja)

Let a mechanical system with \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) be as in theorem 8.101: ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and conservative forces with a potential \(V\). Let \(\vek q = \vek 0\) be an equilibrium, \(\nabla V(\vek 0) = \vek 0\). For the pendulum this is the bottom of the valley, for the crank mechanism the equilibrium angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\). Let \(V\) be three times continuously differentiable and \(\mat M\) continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of the point \(\vek 0\). Let \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\) and let \(\mat K\) be the matrix of second derivatives of \(V\) at \(\vek 0\) as in (8.139). Then \(\mat M_0\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat K\) is symmetric, and every twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) of the system satisfies the equation

\[\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek R , \tag{8.140}\]

where the remainder \(\vek R\) satisfies the following. There are a number \(\rho > 0\) and a number \(c\), which depend on \(V\) and \(\mat M\), such that for every time at which \(|\vek q| \le \rho\) we have for every \(s\)

\[|R_{\mathrm{s}}| \le c\,\big(|\vek q|^2 + |\dot{\vek q}|^2 + |\vek q|\,|\ddot{\vek q}|\big) . \tag{8.141}\]
Proof

We must write the exact equations of motion, separate from them the terms that we keep, and then bound the remainder. Let \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\), and let \(\rho\) be so small that the closed ball \(|\vek q| \le \rho\) lies in an open set where \(V\) and \(\mat M\) are defined, \(V\) has continuous derivatives up to the third order and \(\mat M\) has continuous first derivatives. The notation \(|\cdot|\) is the length of a column, so \(|q_{\mathrm{j}}| \le |\vek q|\) for every element. For the pendulum the remainder will be the distance to the tangent, bounded by the funnel.

  1. The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} - V\). By theorem 8.101, for conservative forces \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0\) for every \(s\).
  2. Because \(\mat M\) is symmetric, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\). The element \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q(t))\) changes with time by the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 1), \(\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{sj}}/\mathrm{d}t = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\). For the crank mechanism this is the slope of the mass curve times the velocity.
  3. Because \(V\) does not depend on the velocities, \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} - \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\).
  4. We insert steps 2 and 3 into step 1. We obtain the exact equation
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q)\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{s}} + \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V = 0 , \qquad g_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\Big(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}} - \tfrac12\,\partial_{\mathrm{s}} M_{\mathrm{jk}}\Big)\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
    The term \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\) is a quadratic form of the velocities: a term with the square of the velocity that "mass times acceleration" alone would not give (section 8.9). For the toggle mechanism it is \(\tfrac12\,m'(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2\), proportional to the slope of the curve \(m(\theta)\), for the pendulum it does not exist because the mass is constant.
  5. We expand the derivative \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) along a segment. The function \(\gamma(\tau) = \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\tau\,\vek q)\) for \(\tau \in [0, 1]\) is twice continuously differentiable: we apply lemma 8.29 to \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) and then to \(\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\), which are continuously differentiable because \(V\) is three times continuously differentiable. We have \(\gamma(0) = \partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\vek 0) = 0\) and \(\gamma'(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}}\). By theorem 8.68, \(\gamma(1) = \gamma(0) + \gamma'(0) + \tfrac12\,\gamma''(\tau_*)\) for some \(\tau_* \in (0, 1)\), with \(\gamma''(\tau_*) = \sum_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{j}}\partial_{\mathrm{k}}\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\tau_*\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\). All third derivatives are continuous on the closed bounded ball, so they have a largest absolute value there, let \(c_{\mathrm{V}}\) be the largest of them (theorem 8.27, applied to the derivative and to its negative). Because \(|q_{\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{k}}| \le |\vek q|^2\),
    \[\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}} + r_{\mathrm{s}} , \qquad |r_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac12\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}}\,|\vek q|^2 .\]
    For the pendulum (\(n = 1\)) the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the tangent \(k\,\theta\), \(r_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the distance \(r\) from it to the curve, and the bound \(\tfrac12\,c_{\mathrm{V}}\,\theta^2\) with \(c_{\mathrm{V}} = m g l\) (the largest \(|V'''| = m g l\,|\sin\theta|\) on \(|\theta| \le \pi/2\)) is a funnel around the tangent, which the curve does not leave.
  6. We estimate the difference \(M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0)\) with the mean value theorem 1.85 on the function \(\tau \mapsto M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek q)\): it equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau_*\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{k}}\) for some \(\tau_*\), because the derivative of this function is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\tau\,\vek q)\,q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (lemma 8.29). Let \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\) be the largest absolute value of the derivatives \(\partial_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sj}}\) on the ball (theorem 8.27, as in step 5). Then \(|M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0)| \le n\,c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\vek q|\) and \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0))\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}}\big| \le n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\vek q|\,|\ddot{\vek q}|\). For the toggle mechanism the difference is the vertical distance between the curve \(m(\theta)\) and the line \(M_0\), the bound \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{e}}|\) with \(c_{\mathrm{M}} = 2\) kg m\(^2\) (the largest \(|m'(\theta)| = |2\sin 2\theta|\)) is a funnel with its apex at the equilibrium.
  7. From step 4 with steps 5 and 6 we get \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} M_{0,\mathrm{sj}}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{sj}}\,q_{\mathrm{j}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(R_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek q) - M_{\mathrm{sj}}(\vek 0))\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} - g_{\mathrm{s}} - r_{\mathrm{s}}\). By step 4 the term \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\) is a sum of \(n^2\) terms, each of which has absolute value at most \(\tfrac32\,c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\dot{\vek q}|^2\), so \(|g_{\mathrm{s}}| \le \tfrac32\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\dot{\vek q}|^2\). The inequality (8.141) holds with \(c = \max\big(\tfrac12\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{V}},\ \tfrac32\,n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}},\ n^2 c_{\mathrm{M}}\big)\). For the pendulum the remainder is the distance \(r\) between the curve and the tangent, for the crank mechanism the difference is the distance between the mass curve and the line \(M_0\).
  8. The matrices \(\mat M_0\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric: the first by theorem 8.97, the second because mixed derivatives can be exchanged (theorem 8.31). The positive definiteness of \(\mat M_0\) is part of the same theorem.

Source: GR (2.6)-(2.12) (PDF p. 79-80): expansion of both energies to second order and the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\). The exact equation in step 4 and the bound on the remainder are not from the source.

We proved the theorem by writing out every discarded term and finding a bound for it. We expanded \(\partial_{\mathrm{s}} V\) and \(\mat M\) in the same way as section 1.15 of chapter 1 expanded a field over a short step, and this is exactly what chapter 1 promised for the equations of motion near an equilibrium in linear form. The remainder is of second order: if \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}\) are of order \(\varepsilon\), then \(\vek R\) is of order \(\varepsilon^2\), while the kept terms are of order \(\varepsilon\). In the energy the discarded part is of third order and in the equation of second, because differentiation lowers the order by one. For the pendulum the remainder is even of third order, \(m g l\,(\theta - \sin\theta)\), because the third derivative of the potential at \(\theta = 0\) is zero. The theorem does not guarantee this, because it holds for every potential. In the figure the funnel around the tangent is four times narrower at half the angle.

Every assumption of the theorem costs something when it does not hold.

  1. Small displacements. The remainder is of second order, so the linear equation is good as long as \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}\) are small. For the pendulum at \(10°\) we discard 0,51 % of the kept term, while at an amplitude of \(90°\) the period is already 18,0 % longer than the linear one (the dot at \(90°\) in section 8.13.2).
  2. Conservative forces. Friction or damping would add to the equation a term with the velocity that equation (8.140) does not have, and the oscillation would decay. Such a term is added by chapter 9.
  3. Scleronomic constraints. With a rheonomic constraint (definition 8.43), for example a support that moves with time, the kinetic energy also has terms linear in the velocities, and the matrix \(\mat M\) is no longer the whole kinetic energy. The theorem does not cover such a system.
  4. Smoothness. The third derivatives of \(V\) bound the funnel around the tangent. A spring that is stretched on one side of the equilibrium and slack on the other has a jump of the second derivative of the energy at the equilibrium, the matrix \(\mat K\) is not defined there, and there is no linear equation.
Definition 8.137 Linearized equation of motion (linearizirana enačba gibanja)

For the system and the equilibrium of theorem 8.136, the linearized equation of motion about the equilibrium is the equation

\[\mat M_0\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek 0 \tag{8.142}\]

for the column \(\vek q(t)\) of displacements from the equilibrium. We call the matrix \(\mat M_0 = \mat M(\vek 0)\) the mass matrix (masna matrika), and the matrix \(\mat K\) of second derivatives of the potential at the equilibrium the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) of the linearized equation. Both are symmetric matrices with \(n \times n\) elements, and \(\mat M_0\) is positive definite.

Source: GR (2.12) (PDF p. 80): "the fundamental equation describing the free vibration of a conservative system around an equilibrium position".

Whether the equilibrium is really stable is decided by the stiffness matrix. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite (definition 6.27), then by theorem 8.71 \(V\) has a strict local minimum at the equilibrium, for one coordinate a valley instead of a hill, the equilibrium is stable (theorem 8.131), and all statements about frequencies proved below hold. We test the pivots of the matrix \(\mat K\) by lemma 8.70.

How large the error of a computation with such an equation is, the theorem does not say. It bounds by how much the equations differ at each moment, but not by how much the two solutions differ over the time \(t\). For this we would need an estimate of how a small remainder accumulates in the solution at each moment, and the book does not have such an estimate. The chapters on vibrations therefore take the linear equation as a model of the system, not as a proven approximation of its motion: we check its validity with numbers. The answer for the pendulum is in the next section. For the toggle mechanism of example D (section 8.13.4) we will test it with numbers.

8.13.2 One degree of freedom (ena prostostna stopnja)

For \(n = 1\) the mass and stiffness matrices are numbers, \(m = M_0 > 0\) and \(k = V''(0)\), and the linearized equation is \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = 0\). This is the equation that section 8.2 prepared: we look for the solution with a cosine and a sine, and theorem 8.18 covers all solutions. The pendulum has \(m = m l^2 = 1\) kg m\(^2\) and \(k = m g l = 9{,}81\) J, the second derivative of the parabola \(\tfrac12\,k\,\theta^2\) in figure 8.145, so \(k/m = g/l\).

What do we expect? For a spring of stiffness \(k\) and mass \(m\) the force is \(-k q\), the acceleration \(-(k/m)\,q\), and the cosine with \(\omega^2 = k/m\) has second derivative \(-\omega^2\) times itself. So we try \(q = \cos\omega t\). Figure 8.146 shows what happens for both signs of \(k\). At the top left, on the axes, is the energy \(\tfrac12\,k q^2\) for \(k > 0\), a valley with its bottom at the equilibrium, in which the ball returns towards the bottom. At the bottom left is the pendulum from this valley, the deflection \(q = a\cos\omega t\) with \(a = 10°\), which stays in the band between \(-a\) and \(a\). At the extreme position the acceleration \(\ddot q = -(k/m)\,q\) points towards the axis, so the deflection returns. For \(k < 0\) the energy is a hill (top right, axes), and the acceleration \(-(k/m)\,q = (|k|/m)\,q\) has the same sign as the displacement: at the initial position \(\varepsilon\) it points away from the axis (bottom right, axes). The cosine, however, always curves back towards the axis, so it does not solve the equation then: the trial cosine returns across zero, while the true solution moves away from zero ever faster and stays above the line \(\varepsilon\sqrt{|k|/m}\,t\). The theorem states both cases exactly.

Theorem 8.138 Small oscillations of a system with one degree of freedom (majhna nihanja pri eni prostostni stopnji)

Let \(m > 0\) and \(k\) be numbers, and let \(q(t)\) be a twice differentiable solution of the equation \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = 0\) on an interval \(I\) that contains \(0\), with initial values \(q(0)\) and \(\dot q(0)\). Figure 8.146 shows both cases: for \(k > 0\) the energy is a valley, for \(k < 0\) a hill.

  1. If \(k > 0\), let \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\). The solution is unique and for \(t \in I\) it is
    \[q(t) = q(0)\,\cos\omega t + \frac{\dot q(0)}{\omega}\,\sin\omega t . \tag{8.143}\]
    The right-hand side is defined for every \(t\) and for every \(t\) we have \(q(t + 2\pi/\omega) = q(t)\), and the period (nihajni čas) \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) does not depend on the initial values. In absolute value the displacement never exceeds the amplitude (amplituda) \(a = \sqrt{q(0)^2 + (\dot q(0)/\omega)^2}\). In the figure this is the cosine, which repeats after the time \(T\).
  2. If \(k < 0\), \(q(0) = \varepsilon > 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\), then at every time \(t \ge 0\) in \(I\) we have \(|q(t)| \ge \varepsilon\,\sqrt{|k|/m}\,t\). In the figure the displacement grows without oscillating.
Proof

For point 1 we show that the formula solves the equation and that it is the only solution. For point 2 we use the energy. In part 1 we look at the valley, in part 2 at the hill.

Point 1.

  1. We divide by \(m\): \(\ddot q + \omega^2 q = 0\), because \(k/m = \omega^2\).
  2. Denote the right-hand side of (8.143) by \(q_*(t)\). By theorem 8.13 (second expression, \(c = 0\)), \(q_*' = -\omega\,q(0)\sin\omega t + \dot q(0)\cos\omega t\) and \(q_*'' = -\omega^2 q(0)\cos\omega t - \omega\,\dot q(0)\sin\omega t = -\omega^2 q_*\). The values are \(q_*(0) = q(0)\) and \(q_*'(0) = \dot q(0)\), because \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\sin 0 = 0\). The cosine in the figure always curves toward the axis, so its second derivative is the opposite of itself.
  3. By theorem 8.18 with \(t_0 = 0\), every solution of the equation \(y'' + \omega^2 y = 0\) on \(I\) equals \(y(0)\cos\omega t + (y'(0)/\omega)\sin\omega t\). For \(y = q\) this is \(q_*\), so \(q = q_*\) on \(I\) and the solution is unique. A second solution with the same initial values would be the same curve in the figure.
  4. Cosine and sine have period \(2\pi\) (lemma 8.11, point 4), so \(\cos\omega(t + 2\pi/\omega) = \cos(\omega t + 2\pi) = \cos\omega t\) and the same for the sine. The period \(2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) contains only \(m\) and \(k\).
  5. The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,m\dot q^2 + \tfrac12\,k q^2\) has derivative \(\dot q\,(m\ddot q + k q) = 0\), so it is constant (corollary 1.86). At \(t = 0\), \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,m\dot q(0)^2 + \tfrac12\,k q(0)^2 = \tfrac12\,k\,\big(q(0)^2 + (\dot q(0)/\omega)^2\big) = \tfrac12\,k a^2\), because \(m = k/\omega^2\). Because \(\tfrac12\,m\dot q^2 \ge 0\), we have \(\tfrac12\,k q^2 \le \tfrac12\,k a^2\), so \(|q| \le a\): the energy level cuts the valley at \(-a\) and \(a\), and the displacement stays between them.

Point 2. Let \(f(t) = \tfrac12\,m q^2\). We look at the hill and the energy level below its top.

  1. The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}}\) is constant as in step 5 and at \(t = 0\) it is \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,k\varepsilon^2 = -\tfrac12\,|k|\,\varepsilon^2 < 0\). The energy level is below the top of the hill and cuts it at \(-\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon\).
  2. The first derivative is \(f' = m q\dot q\) and the second \(f'' = m\dot q^2 + m q\ddot q = m\dot q^2 - k q^2\). From \(k q^2 = 2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} - m\dot q^2\) it follows that \(f'' = 2 m\dot q^2 - 2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} \ge -2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = |k|\,\varepsilon^2\).
  3. The function \(h(t) = f'(t) - |k|\varepsilon^2 t\) has a nonnegative derivative by step 7, so \(h(t) - h(0) = h'(\xi)\,t \ge 0\) for \(t \ge 0\) (theorem 1.85), and \(h(0) = f'(0) = m\varepsilon\cdot 0 = 0\). So \(f' \ge |k|\varepsilon^2 t\) for \(t \ge 0\). By the same reasoning \(f(t) - \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2 \ge f(0) = \tfrac12\,m\varepsilon^2 > 0\), because this function has the derivative \(f' - |k|\varepsilon^2 t \ge 0\), so \(f(t) \ge \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2\). The displacement in the figure grows at least as fast as this bound says.
  4. From \(\tfrac12\,m q^2 \ge \tfrac12\,|k|\varepsilon^2 t^2\) it follows that \(|q| \ge \varepsilon\,\sqrt{|k|/m}\,t\). This proves the lower bound for the growth of the displacement.

Source: KREYSZIG §2.4 (PDF p. 89): the solution of the equation \(m\ddot y + k y = 0\) as a harmonic oscillation. CK example 3.1 (PDF p. 87-88): with negative stiffness the solution is not oscillating but grows without oscillation ("divergence"). GR PDF p. 87: without positive definiteness the solution would grow exponentially. The proof by steps is not from the source, point 2 is the same argument as theorem 8.133, written for the linear equation.

Point 2 says that the solution leaves every neighbourhood of the equilibrium when \(k < 0\), as theorem 8.133 says for the true equation. How fast, the theorem does not say: the growth is in fact faster than linear. The exact solution contains a function that the book does not have yet and will introduce with damping in chapter 9. The pendulum in the upper position \(\theta = \pi\) has \(k = -m g l = -9{,}81\) J and \(k/m = -9{,}81\) 1/s\(^2\), so by point 2 the displacement from the upper position at the time \(t\) is at least \(\varepsilon\cdot 3{,}132\,t\) (1/s). In the figure this is the displacement leaving the hill.

For the pendulum about the equilibrium \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l} = 3{,}1321\) 1/s and period \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2{,}0061\) s. The linear solution for a deflection \(\theta_0\) and initial velocity zero is \(\theta = \theta_0\cos\omega_0 t\). The actual period we obtain from the conservation of energy, which for a conservative system is guaranteed by theorem 8.121. The linear period \(T_0\) is the time of one oscillation at \(k = m g l\).

Figure 8.147 shows what the theorem will compute, on a pendulum with \(l = 1\) m and amplitude \(a = 90°\). On the left, on the axes, is the deflection \(\theta\) as a function of time, the dashed lines are the extreme deflections \(a\) and \(-a\). The pendulum passes through \(\theta = 0\) with positive velocity and reaches the deflection \(a\) at time \(t_{\mathrm{a}} = 0{,}5920\) s: this is the blue quarter. The theorem computes only this one. The second quarter is its mirror image across the vertical line at \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\), the third and fourth are the first two with the opposite sign. So the times \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(2t_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(3t_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{a}}\) are equally spaced and \(T = 4\,t_{\mathrm{a}} = 2{,}3678\) s. On the right, on the axes, are two forms of the same time \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) as an area under a curve. The blackwhite curve is \(\mathrm{d}t/\mathrm{d}\theta = 1/\dot\theta\), the time per unit of angle. At \(\theta = a\) the pendulum stops, so the curve grows beyond all bounds there, and theorem 3.55, which requires a continuous integrand on a closed interval, does not apply to it. The blue curve is the same time per unit of a new variable \(u\), \(\mathrm{d}t/\mathrm{d}u\), which stays between the bounds \(\sqrt{l/g} = 0{,}3193\) s and \(\sqrt{l/g}/\sqrt{1 - k^2} = 0{,}4515\) s. The area under it is \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\), and this is the integral of the theorem.

Theorem 8.139 Period of the pendulum (nihajni čas nihala)

A point mass on a rigid weightless rod of length \(l\) in a gravitational field \(g\) has the equation of motion \(\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\sin\theta\). Figure 8.147 shows one oscillation between the extreme angles \(\pm a\). Let \(\theta(t)\) be a motion, defined for every time \(t\) (corollary 8.108 does not guarantee that such a motion exists, so this is an assumption), with \(\theta(0) = 0\) and \(\dot\theta(0) > 0\), in which \(\dot\theta\) first becomes zero at a time \(t_{\mathrm{a}} > 0\) at a deflection \(a = \theta(t_{\mathrm{a}}) \in (0, \pi)\), which we call the amplitude. With \(k = \sin(a/2)\) the time in which the deflection grows from \(0\) to \(a\) is

\[t_{\mathrm{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}}\,\mathrm{d}u ,\]

the motion repeats after the time \(T = 4\,t_{\mathrm{a}}\), which is the period, and

\[T = 4\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}}\,\mathrm{d}u . \tag{8.144}\]

As \(a \to 0\), \(T\) tends to \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

Proof

We must show that \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) is an integral, one that is not singular at the endpoint, that \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{a}}\) and that \(T \to T_0\). We obtain the time as the inverse function of the deflection, and the integral by a change of variable whose every step we prove. The time \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) is the area under the blue curve, and the period is four times the first quarter.

  1. The energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\,m l^2\dot\theta^2 - m g l\cos\theta\) is constant (theorem 8.121). At \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) we have \(\dot\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = a\), so \(\mathcal{E} = -m g l\cos a\) and for every time \(\dot\theta^2 = \dfrac{2g}{l}\,(\cos\theta - \cos a)\). At \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) the pendulum is at the extreme angle \(a\), where it rests.
  2. The function \(\dot\theta\) is continuous, positive at \(0\) and has no zero on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\), so by the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) it is positive on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85), \(\theta\) is strictly increasing on \([0, t_{\mathrm{a}}]\) (blue quarter), so \(0 < \theta < a\) on \((0, t_{\mathrm{a}})\). By lemma 8.9 the inverse function \(t(\theta)\) is continuous on \([0, a]\) and differentiable on \((0, a)\) with derivative (blackwhite curve) \(t'(\theta) = 1/\dot\theta = 1\big/\sqrt{(2g/l)(\cos\theta - \cos a)}\) by step 1, with values \(t(0) = 0\) and \(t(a) = t_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  3. From the addition formula with \(\beta = \alpha\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) (corollary 8.16, lemma 8.11, point 1) we have \(\cos x = 1 - 2\sin^2(x/2)\), so \(\cos\theta - \cos a = 2\big(k^2 - \sin^2(\theta/2)\big)\).
  4. For \(\theta \in [0, a)\) we have \(0 \le \sin(\theta/2)/k < 1\), because \(\sin\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \pi/2]\) (its derivative \(\cos\) is positive on \([0, \pi/2)\), theorem 8.13, lemma 8.11, point 3) and \(\theta/2 < a/2 < \pi/2\). Let \(h\) be the inverse function of the sine on \([0, \pi/2]\), continuous with values \(h(0) = 0\) and \(h(1) = \pi/2\) and differentiable on \((0, 1)\) with derivative \(h'(y) = 1/\cos h(y)\) (lemma 8.9, theorem 2.79 for existence). Let \(u(\theta) = h\big(\sin(\theta/2)/k\big)\), so \(\sin u = \sin(\theta/2)/k\) and \(u \in [0, \pi/2)\) for \(\theta \in [0, a)\). The function \(u\) is continuous and differentiable on \((0, a)\) with derivative \(u' = \dfrac{1}{\cos u}\cdot\dfrac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2k}\), where \(\cos u > 0\). As \(\theta \to a\), \(\sin(\theta/2)/k \to 1\) and hence \(u \to h(1) = \pi/2\). When \(\theta\) reaches \(a\), \(u\) reaches \(\pi/2\): the integrand in \(u\) stays bounded there, the blackwhite curve does not.
  5. By step 4, \(\cos^2(\theta/2) = 1 - k^2\sin^2 u\), and by step 3, \(\cos\theta - \cos a = 2k^2(1 - \sin^2 u) = 2k^2\cos^2 u\). So \(\sqrt{(2g/l)(\cos\theta - \cos a)} = 2k\cos u\sqrt{g/l}\) and \(t'(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{2k\cos u}\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\). The zero of the denominator \(\cos u\) at \(u = \pi/2\) is why the blackwhite curve grows without bound at \(\theta = a\).
  6. Let \(F(u) = \int_0^u \mathrm{d}v\big/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 v}\). Because \(k = \sin(a/2) < 1\) (since \(a/2 < \pi/2\)), we have \(1 - k^2\sin^2 v \ge 1 - k^2 > 0\), the integrand is continuous on \([0, \pi/2]\) and \(F' = 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.55). The function \(w(\theta) = \sqrt{l/g}\,F(u(\theta))\) has by the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 3) the derivative \(w' = \sqrt{l/g}\,\dfrac{u'}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u}} = \sqrt{l/g}\,\dfrac{\cos(\theta/2)}{2k\cos u}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\cos(\theta/2)} = \dfrac{1}{2k\cos u}\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\), where we used step 4 for \(u'\) and step 5 for the root, and \(\cos(\theta/2) > 0\) because \(\theta/2 < \pi/2\). So \(w' = t'\) on \((0, a)\) by step 5, both functions are continuous on \([0, a)\) and \(w(0) = t(0) = 0\). The function \(t - w\) has zero derivative, so on every \([0, \theta_1]\) with \(\theta_1 < a\) it is constant, that is zero (corollary 1.86): \(t(\theta) = w(\theta)\) for \(\theta \in [0, a)\).
  7. As \(\theta \to a\), \(t(\theta) \to t_{\mathrm{a}}\) (continuity, step 2) and \(w(\theta) \to \sqrt{l/g}\,F(\pi/2)\) (step 4 and continuity of \(F\)). Hence \(t_{\mathrm{a}} = \sqrt{l/g}\,F(\pi/2)\), the area under the blue curve, which is the first formula of the theorem.
  8. The equation of motion does not change if we replace \(\theta(t)\) by \(-\theta(-t)\) or by \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t)\), because \(\sin\) is an odd function and the equation contains only the second derivative. The motion \(-\theta(-t)\) has at \(t = 0\) the state \((0, \dot\theta(0))\), the same as \(\theta\), and the motion \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t)\) has at \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\) the state \((a, 0)\), the same as \(\theta\). By corollary 8.108 on the uniqueness of the motion for a given state they equal \(\theta(t)\): \(\theta(-t) = -\theta(t)\) and \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} - t) = \theta(t)\). The second equality is the reflection across the vertical line at \(t_{\mathrm{a}}\). Hence \(\theta(2t_{\mathrm{a}} + s) = \theta(-s) = -\theta(s)\) and \(\theta(t + 4t_{\mathrm{a}}) = -\theta(t + 2t_{\mathrm{a}}) = \theta(t)\): the motion repeats after \(4t_{\mathrm{a}}\), and in the intermediate time \(2t_{\mathrm{a}}\) the deflection is opposite (third and fourth quarter).
  9. From \(T = 4t_{\mathrm{a}}\) we get (8.144). For \(0 \le k < 1\) we have \(1 \le 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u} \le 1/\sqrt{1 - k^2}\), so \(\pi/2 \le F(\pi/2) \le (\pi/2)/\sqrt{1 - k^2}\), because the blue curve lies between the bounds. As \(a \to 0\), \(k = \sin(a/2) \to 0\) (continuity of the sine), so \(F(\pi/2) \to \pi/2\) and \(T \to 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

Source: GR example 1.7, (E1.7.f), (E1.7.g), (E1.7.h) (PDF p. 49-50): the same energy equation, the same substitution \(\sin(\theta/2) = k\sin\phi\) and the same form of the integral, the symmetry of the quarters from the symmetry of the phase curve. The proof by steps, including the derivative of the inverse function in steps 2 and 4 and the symmetry in step 8, is not from the source.

The integral is not an elementary function, so we compute it by numerical integration. The periods of a pendulum with \(l = 1\) m and their ratios to the linear period are in the table. The last column is the approximation that we get by writing, for small \(k\), \(1/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 u} \approx 1 + \tfrac12\,k^2\sin^2 u\) and integrating. Because \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2 u\,\mathrm{d}u = \pi/4\) (from \(\sin^2 u = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2u)\)), we get \(T \approx T_0\,(1 + k^2/4)\), and with \(k \approx a/2\) also \(T \approx T_0\,(1 + a^2/16)\) (GR (E1.7.h), PDF p. 50). 8.13.3 Two degrees of freedom (dve prostostni stopnji)

With one degree of freedom there is a single oscillation. What happens when the system can move in two ways? Take a rigid bar of length \(L = 2\) m and mass \(m = 30\) kg that lies horizontally on two vertical springs (example C from sections 8.4 and 8.8). The left end is held by a spring of stiffness \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m, the right by a spring of stiffness \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m. A guide excludes horizontal displacement, which is an idealization: a real support would also allow horizontal motion. The coordinates are the vertical displacement of the centre of mass \(y\) (upward) and the rotation \(\varphi\) (counterclockwise). The moment of inertia of the bar about the centre of mass is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m L^2/12 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), and the kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,m\dot y^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\dot\varphi^2\) (theorem 8.97), so \(\mat M = \operatorname{diag}(30, 10)\) in the units kg and kg m\(^2\). The potential is

\[V = \tfrac12\,k_1\big(y - \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\big)^2 + \tfrac12\,k_2\big(y + \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\big)^2 + m g y ,\]

where \(y \mp \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\varphi\) are the vertical displacements of the left and right ends. The equilibrium is not at \(y = 0\), because the bar hangs on the springs under its own weight: from \(\partial V/\partial y = \partial V/\partial\varphi = 0\) we get \(y = -6{,}1313\) mm and \(\varphi = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad. From now on we count the coordinates \(y\) and \(\varphi\) from this equilibrium. The Hessian matrix of the potential at it is \(\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 50\,000 & 9999{,}99 \\ 9999{,}99 & 49\,999{,}9 \end{bmatrix}\), which differs from the matrix at the point \(y = \varphi = 0\), \(\begin{bmatrix} 50\,000 & 10\,000 \\ 10\,000 & 50\,000 \end{bmatrix}\), by at most about \(0{,}075\) N m, that is by \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\) of its value. A computation with that matrix therefore gives the same result, and we write it with the elements \(K_{\mathrm{yy}} = k_1 + k_2 = 50\,000\) N/m, \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} = (k_2 - k_1)\,L/2 = 10\,000\) N and \(K_{\varphi\varphi} = (k_1 + k_2)\,(L/2)^2 = 50\,000\) N m. The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite: its pivots are \(50\,000\) and \(50\,000 - 10\,000^2/50\,000 = 48\,000\) (lemma 8.70).

The left drawing of figure 8.150 shows where the equilibrium position comes from. The springs \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) stand on the ground, the dashed grey line is the position of the bar on unstretched springs, the orange one is the equilibrium. The weight \(m g = 294{,}3\) N acts at the centre of mass \(G\), which is equally far from both springs, \(L/2 = 1\) m. The equilibrium of a rigid body (definition 2.3) requires that the sum of the moments about \(G\) is zero, so the spring forces are equal, each \(m g/2 = 147{,}15\) N. The softer spring compresses more under the same force: the left end descends by \(147{,}15/20\,000\) m \(= 7{,}36\) mm, the right one by \(147{,}15/30\,000\) m \(= 4{,}91\) mm. The bar is therefore not horizontal in the equilibrium. The centre of mass is lower by the average, \(6{,}13\) mm, and the rotation is the difference of the descents divided by the length, \(2{,}45\ \text{mm}/2\ \text{m} = 1{,}23\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad.

Why is \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} \ne 0\)? Because the springs are different. In the middle drawing of figure 8.150 we lift the bar from the equilibrium by \(y\) without rotation, to the orange position. The springs are less compressed and push upwards less than in the equilibrium. The change is a force downwards, \(k_1 y\) at the left end and \(k_2 y\) at the right, at \(y = 1\) mm these are 20 N and 30 N. On equal arms \(L/2 = 1\) m they give about the centre of mass the moment \((k_2 - k_1)\,y\,L/2 = 10\) N m clockwise: a lift without rotation turns the bar. The moment per unit of lift is the element \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} = (k_2 - k_1)\,L/2 = 10\,000\) N. If the springs were equal, \(K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi} = 0\): the springs would resist the upward displacement of the centre of mass with equal forces on equal arms, so without a moment about the centre of mass. With different springs the coordinates are linked, coupled (sklopljeni): the generalized force in the coordinate \(y\), \(-K_{\mathrm{yy}}\,y - K_{\mathrm{y}\varphi}\,\varphi\), also contains \(\varphi\).

We ask whether the bar can oscillate so that \(y\) and \(\varphi\) move with the same frequency and reach zero together, that is \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) for a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\). With one degree of freedom every solution that starts from rest has this form: it is equation (8.143) with \(\dot q(0) = 0\). The following definition names what we are looking for. In the figure this would mean that the bar in the equilibrium position returns to zero in both coordinates at the same time.

Definition 8.140 Natural frequency and mode shape (lastna frekvenca in način nihanja)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be symmetric matrices with \(n \times n\) elements, \(\mat M\) positive definite, and let the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) be given (definition 8.137). For the bar on springs the coordinates are the rise and the rotation. A number \(\omega > 0\) is a natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) of the system if there is a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) elements for which

\[\mat K\,\vek u = \omega^2\,\mat M\,\vek u . \tag{8.145}\]

The column \(\vek u\) is then a mode shape (način nihanja) at the frequency \(\omega\), and the motion \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) is an oscillation in this mode. A point of the system that does not move in some mode is a node (vozel) of this mode. The frequency in hertz is \(f = \omega/(2\pi)\), the number of oscillations per second.

Source: GR section 2.2, (2.22)-(2.33) (PDF p. 85-87): synchronous motions with the same time dependence of all coordinates, the eigenproblem, "eigenmode", "circular frequency" and the relation \(\nu = \omega/2\pi\). The concept of a node and the form of the definition are not from the source.

For \(n = 1\) this is the equation \(k\,u = \omega^2 m\,u\), so \(\omega^2 = k/m\) as in theorem 8.138. For \(n = 2\) we transform it into a condition on a determinant. This is the oscillation of one degree of freedom.

Figure 8.151 shows why the determinant decides about modes. Each row of the equation \(\mat A\vek u = \vek 0\) is a line through the origin in the plane \((u_1, u_2)\). On the left, on the axes, the first row and the second row are different lines, so they meet only at the origin and the only solution there is \(\vek u = \vek 0\). On the right, on the axes, both rows give the same line and every column \(\vek u\) on it solves both: this is the case \(\det\mat A = 0\).

Theorem 8.141 Natural frequencies of a system with two degrees of freedom (lastne frekvence sistema z dvema prostostnima stopnjama)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be symmetric positive definite matrices with \(2 \times 2\) elements (definition 6.27). The example is the bar on two springs. Then:

  1. The motion \(\vek q(t) = \vek u\cos\omega t\) with a column \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) is a solution of the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) exactly when \((\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\,\vek u = \vek 0\). Such a \(\vek u\) exists exactly when \(\det(\mat K - \omega^2\mat M) = 0\). Figure 8.151: this is the common line on which the column \(\vek u\) lies.
  2. The equation \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\,\mat M) = 0\) for \(\lambda\) is quadratic and has two real roots \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\), both positive. The natural frequencies are \(\omega_1 = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\) and \(\omega_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_2}\). In the figure the quadratic polynomial is the parabola with two zeros.
  3. We have \(\lambda_1 \le \min(K_{11}/M_{11},\ K_{22}/M_{22})\) and \(\lambda_2 \ge \max(K_{11}/M_{11},\ K_{22}/M_{22})\). The number \(K_{11}/M_{11}\) is the point on the axis between the zeros.
Proof

Point 1 we get by substitution, points 2 and 3 from the properties of a quadratic polynomial.

  1. For \(\vek q = \vek u\cos\omega t\) we have by theorem 8.13 \(\ddot{\vek q} = -\omega^2\vek u\cos\omega t\), so \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = (\mat K - \omega^2\mat M)\,\vek u\cos\omega t\). This column is zero for every \(t\) exactly when it is zero at \(t = 0\), where \(\cos 0 = 1\).
  2. Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with \(2 \times 2\) elements and \(\det\mat A = A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21}\). Lemma 2.75 of chapter 2 says this for \(3 \times 3\) matrices, here we prove it for \(2 \times 2\). If \(\mat A\vek u = \vek 0\) and \(u_1 \ne 0\), we multiply the first row \(A_{11}u_1 + A_{12}u_2 = 0\) by \(A_{22}\) and the second \(A_{21}u_1 + A_{22}u_2 = 0\) by \(A_{12}\) and subtract: \((A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21})\,u_1 = 0\), so \(\det\mat A = 0\). If \(u_2 \ne 0\), the same with multiplication by \(A_{21}\) and \(A_{11}\). Conversely, let \(\det\mat A = 0\). If the first row is not zero, \(\vek u = (A_{12}, -A_{11}) \ne \vek 0\) solves the first row, and the second row gives \(A_{21}A_{12} - A_{22}A_{11} = -\det\mat A = 0\). If the first row is zero and the second is not, we take \(\vek u = (A_{22}, -A_{21})\), if both are zero, any \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\). This proves point 1, because \(\mat A = \mat K - \omega^2\mat M\). For \(\det\mat A \ne 0\) the rows meet only at the origin, for \(\det\mat A = 0\) they are the same line.
  3. The polynomial \(p(\lambda) = \det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\) equals \((K_{11} - \lambda M_{11})(K_{22} - \lambda M_{22}) - (K_{12} - \lambda M_{12})^2\) and also \(a\lambda^2 - b\lambda + c\) with \(a = M_{11}M_{22} - M_{12}^2 = \det\mat M\), \(b = K_{11}M_{22} + K_{22}M_{11} - 2K_{12}M_{12}\) and \(c = \det\mat K\).
  4. By lemma 8.70, for a positive definite \(2 \times 2\) matrix the first diagonal element and the determinant are positive, so \(a > 0\), \(c > 0\), \(K_{11} > 0\) and \(M_{11} > 0\). Also \(K_{22} > 0\) and \(M_{22} > 0\) from definition 6.27 with the column \((0, 1)\).
  5. Let \(\lambda_* = K_{11}/M_{11} > 0\). The first factor in \(p(\lambda_*)\) is zero, so \(p(\lambda_*) = -(K_{12} - \lambda_* M_{12})^2 \le 0\). In figure 8.152 this is the blue distance below the axis.
  6. For a quadratic polynomial \(4a\,p(\lambda) = (2a\lambda - b)^2 - \Delta\) with \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\) (completing the square). By step 5 and \(a > 0\), \((2a\lambda_* - b)^2 \le \Delta\), so \(\Delta \ge 0\) and the solutions \(\lambda_{1,2} = (b \mp \sqrt\Delta)/(2a)\) are real. The inequality \(p(\lambda) \le 0\) holds exactly for \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda \le \lambda_2\), so \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_* \le \lambda_2\): the parabola is below the axis only between the roots.
  7. The product of the solutions is \(c/a > 0\), so the solutions have the same sign. Because \(\lambda_2 \ge \lambda_* > 0\), both are positive. This proves point 2, because by point 1 every positive solution \(\lambda = \omega^2\) gives a frequency. Both zeros, \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), lie to the right of the origin.
  8. Point 3 for \(K_{11}/M_{11}\) is step 6. For \(K_{22}/M_{22}\) we repeat the procedure with the indices exchanged \(1 \leftrightarrow 2\), which does not change \(p\) (lower window).

Corollary 8.142 Motion as a sum of modes (gibanje kot vsota načinov)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\) be as in theorem 8.141 and \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2\), and let \(\vek u_1\), \(\vek u_2\) be the modes at \(\omega_1\), \(\omega_2\). Figure 8.154 shows the modes as columns and every column \(\vek v\) as their sum. Then \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\,\vek u_2 = 0\), and the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) with initial values \(\vek q(0)\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0)\) has exactly one solution,

\[\begin{aligned} \vek q(t) &= \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{2}\Big(\alpha_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t \\ &\quad + \beta_{\mathrm{r}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\Big)\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}} , \\ \alpha_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q(0)}{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}} , \\ \beta_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0)}{\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}} . \end{aligned} \tag{8.146}\]
Proof

We must show the orthogonality of the modes in the sense of \(\mat M\), from it the expansion of an arbitrary column, then the solution and its uniqueness. The orthogonality is the right angle between the modes, and the expansion is the diagonal of the parallelogram.

  1. From \(\mat K\vek u_1 = \lambda_1\mat M\vek u_1\) we multiply from the left by \(\vek u_2^\T\), from \(\mat K\vek u_2 = \lambda_2\mat M\vek u_2\) by \(\vek u_1^\T\): \(\vek u_2^\T\mat K\vek u_1 = \lambda_1\vek u_2^\T\mat M\vek u_1\) and \(\vek u_1^\T\mat K\vek u_2 = \lambda_2\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2\). The left-hand sides are equal, because a number equals its transpose and \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) are symmetric. Because also \(\vek u_2^\T\mat M\vek u_1 = \vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2\), we have \((\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2 = 0\), so \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_2 = 0\). The result is the right angle between \(\vek u_1\) and \(\vek u_2\) in the metric of \(\mat M\).
  2. The columns \(\vek u_1\), \(\vek u_2\) are independent: if \(c_1\vek u_1 + c_2\vek u_2 = \vek 0\), we multiply from the left by \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\) and get \(c_1\,\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_1 = 0\). Because \(\mat M\) is positive definite, \(\vek u_1^\T\mat M\vek u_1 > 0\), so \(c_1 = 0\) and likewise \(c_2 = 0\). Two independent columns with two elements span all columns, so every \(\vek v = c_1\vek u_1 + c_2\vek u_2\), and by multiplying with \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\) we get by step 1 \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v\,/\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\). In the figure \(\vek v\) is the diagonal of the parallelogram with sides \(c_1\vek u_1\) and \(c_2\vek u_2\).
  3. Every term of the sum (8.146) is a solution of the equation: for \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) by theorem 8.141, point 1, for \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) likewise, because the sine has the same second derivative \(-\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) times itself (theorem 8.13). The equation is linear, so the sum is also a solution. In the figure the part in mode 1 and the part in mode 2 are each a solution, and so is their sum.
  4. At \(t = 0\) the sum equals \(\alpha_1\vek u_1 + \alpha_2\vek u_2 = \vek q(0)\) by step 2, and its derivative \(\omega_1\beta_1\vek u_1 + \omega_2\beta_2\vek u_2 = \dot{\vek q}(0)\) by the same step with \(\vek v = \dot{\vek q}(0)\). At \(t = 0\) the sum equals the initial displacement.
  5. Uniqueness. Let \(\vek q\) and \(\tilde{\vek q}\) be solutions with the same initial values and \(\vek w = \vek q - \tilde{\vek q}\). The energy \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{lin}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek w}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek w} + \tfrac12\,\vek w^\T\mat K\vek w\) has derivative \(\dot{\vek w}^\T(\mat M\ddot{\vek w} + \mat K\vek w) = 0\) (symmetry of both matrices), so it is constant (corollary 1.86), and at \(t = 0\) it is zero. Because both quadratic forms are positive definite, we must have \(\dot{\vek w} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek w = \vek 0\) at every time. Two solutions with the same initial values would have the same blackwhite curve, and the difference \(\vek w\) would stay at zero.

Source: GR section 2.3, (2.39), (2.40) (PDF p. 88): the same orthogonality by subtracting two equations. GR PDF p. 87: an arbitrary combination of modes is a solution that is usually not synchronous. The proof by steps is not from the source.

For \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\) the matrix \(\mat K\) would be a multiple of the matrix \(\mat M\), which we do not treat here. A general order \(n\) needs the theorem on eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix, which is brought by chapter 10.

Because \(\mat P\) is invertible (determinant \(\tfrac12 \ne 0\)), \(\mat P^\T(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\mat P\,\vek u' = \vek 0\) exactly when \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\,\mat P\vek u' = \vek 0\). The same mode is therefore \(\vek u = \mat P\vek u'\) in the old coordinates, and the frequencies do not depend on the choice of coordinates. The coordinates determine only how we write the modes, not the physics.

8.13.4 Loaded structures: the frequency drops (obremenjene konstrukcije: frekvenca pada)

What does a load do to the oscillation? The inverted rigid bar with a spring (example B from section 8.6) has the equilibrium \(\theta = 0\) at every load \(P\), as we saw. Section 8.6 showed that the equilibrium is stable for \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}/L\), where \(V''(0) = k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L\). Now we put a point mass \(m = 10\) kg at the end of the bar. The bar of length \(L = 1\) m is weightless, the rotational spring has stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 2000\) N m, the load \(P\) is vertical and constant. This is our model, not from a source. The closest model in a source is CK example 3.1 (PDF p. 87-88), an inverted pendulum on two side springs with natural frequency \(\sqrt{2k/m - g/L}\), which has the same form: the stiffness of the springs minus a term proportional to the load. Figure 8.156 shows the upright position and the tilted position.

Figure 8.156 draws this model. The bar stands on the joint \(O\) on the ground, where it is held by the rotational spring \(k_{\mathrm{r}}\). The upright position of length \(L\) is the equilibrium, the tilted position is rotated from it by the angle \(\theta\). At the top \(A\) are the mass \(m\) and the load \(P\), which stays vertical and of equal size, like a weight. It therefore has a potential, and theorem 8.136 holds. A force that would turn with the bar has no potential, and this computation does not hold for it.

The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\,m L^2\dot\theta^2\), so \(M_0 = m L^2 = 10\) kg m\(^2\) and \(\mat M\) does not depend on the angle. The potential is \(V(\theta) = \tfrac12\,k_{\mathrm{r}}\theta^2 + P L\cos\theta\), where \(P L\cos\theta\) is the potential of the load (the height of the end of the bar is \(L\cos\theta\)). By theorem 8.136 the linearized equation is

\[\begin{aligned} m L^2\,\ddot\theta + (k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)\,\theta &= 0 , \\ \omega^2 &= \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L}{m L^2} \\ &= \frac{k_{\mathrm{r}}}{m L^2}\Big(1 - \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{cr}}}\Big) \\ &= 200\,\Big(1 - \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{cr}}}\Big)\ \text{1/s}^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.147}\]

The term \(-P L\) in the stiffness matrix comes from \(P L\cos\theta \approx P L - \tfrac12\,P L\,\theta^2\): when the bar tilts, the top with the load descends by \(L\,(1 - \cos\theta) \approx \tfrac12\,L\,\theta^2\), and the load does the work \(\tfrac12\,P L\,\theta^2\). The spring stores \(\tfrac12\,k_{\mathrm{r}}\theta^2\) at the same rotation. The work of the load is part of this energy, so the hand that tilts the bar has to add only the difference \(\tfrac12\,(k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)\,\theta^2\): the bar behaves like a spring of stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L\). At \(\theta = 0{,}1\) rad and \(P = 1000\) N the top descends by 5,0 mm, the load does 5,0 J of work, the spring stores 10 J, the difference is 5,0 J, half of that without the load.

8.13.5 Where next (kam naprej)

With this we have gone from Lagrange's equations to the equation \(\mat M_0\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\), solved it for one and two degrees of freedom and seen that a load reduces the frequencies. Three things remain for the next chapters. First, damping and forces: the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = f(t)\), its solution in closed form and the response to an impulse, which are in chapter 9. Second, order \(n\): for arbitrary \(n\) we need the theorem on eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix, the normalization of modes and the expansion of the motion in them, which is in chapter 10, where the \(2 \times 2\) determinant is replaced by the eigenproblem of matrices. Third, continuous systems: a string, a shaft and a beam have infinitely many natural frequencies, which is in chapter 13. Section 8.14, however, will find the critical force of a column without time, as the limit at which the second variation of the energy stops being positive, and this is the same condition at which the frequency here is zero.
