Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.6 Balancing the single-cylinder engine (uravnoteženje enovaljnega motorja)

In section 23.3 we computed, for the single-cylinder engine \(E\) (our model, not data from a source: crank radius \(a = 50\) mm, conrod \(l = 200\) mm, reciprocating mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}8\) kg, rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}9\) kg, constant speed \(1500\) 1/min, \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s), the shaking force (definition 23.17), the force with which the moving parts shake the frame. The first harmonic of the reciprocating mass has amplitude \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N, the second \(564{,}1\) N, and the rotating mass gives the force \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 = 1110{,}3\) N. These are the forces that the bearings and the base carry, and that shake everything attached to them at every turn. Now we ask what the designer can do to make the force smaller. The simplest possibility is to load the shaft with a mass that turns together with it. We have to find out what such a mass removes, what it does not, and why.

The word balancing (uravnoteženje) comes from equilibrium, as figure 23.45 shows: a scale is balanced when the effects on both sides cancel. Here the inertia forces of the moving parts, which shake the frame, are to cancel. On the right is the rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) at the pin \(A\), and the weight that we add on the opposite side of the crank is called a counterweight (protiutež), because it acts against the mass that we want to cancel.

The model is the one of section 23.3. The links are rigid, the pins frictionless, the speed \(\Omega\) constant. The crank, the conrod and the cylinder lie in one plane perpendicular to the crankshaft axis, so the engine is a single-plane device (naprava z eno ravnino, NORTON, PDF p. 732). The mass of the crank itself is taken as already balanced, its centre of mass lies on the axis. This is reasonable here because a crank is balanced separately when it is made, and it matters only this far: any unbalance \(m_{\mathrm{r}} e_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the crank would act as one more rotating mass, and the same counterweight would cover it by growing by \(m_{\mathrm{r}} e_{\mathrm{r}}/a\). So it changes no conclusion, only the numbers of the masses. The conrod is replaced by two point masses by lemma 23.15: \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) at the crank pin \(A\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) at the piston pin \(B\) (with the piston). The gas has mass zero and weight is not counted, so by lemma 23.18 the shaking force equals \(-\sum m\,\vek a\) over the moving masses.

We build figure 23.46 piece by piece. The frame holds the shaft in the bearing \(O\) and the piston in the cylinder. We lay the \(x\) axis along the cylinder axis and measure the angle \(\theta = \Omega t\) from it, as in section 23.2. The crank from \(O\) to the crank pin \(A\) has length \(a\), and the conrod joins the pin \(A\) to the piston pin \(B\), which moves in the cylinder only along \(x\). At the pin \(A\) sits the rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\). What does the frame receive from it? By lemma 23.18 it is \(-m_{\mathrm{A}}\vek a_{\mathrm{A}}\). The point \(A\) goes round a circle of radius \(a\) at constant speed, its position is \(a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), and its second time derivative is \(-\Omega^2\) times the position (theorem 8.13 with \(\theta = \Omega t\)). The force of the rotating mass is therefore \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), directed away from the axis, as everyday experience teaches: a washing machine with an unbalanced load of laundry wants to fly off its axis. The force of the piston is different: the piston moves only along a line, so its force is \(F_{\mathrm{B}} = -m_{\mathrm{B}}\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\) along \(x\) and changes direction, and its magnitude at \(\theta = 50^\circ\) is \(1338{,}1\) N.

The designer can add a mass to the crank that turns with it. We add a counterweight of mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) on the circle of radius \(a\) opposite the pin \(A\), at the point \(A'\), that is at the angle \(\theta + \pi\). Its position is \(-a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), so its force is \(-m_{\mathrm{c}}\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). The forces at the pin and at the counterweight lie on the same line through \(O\) and point in opposite directions. If they are equally large, they cancel.

The radius of the counterweight could also differ from \(a\). What counts is the product of mass and radius \(m\,e\), the unbalance (neuravnoteženost, definition 23.1). The mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) at radius \(a\) has unbalance \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\), a counterweight at the same radius must have the same mass, and a counterweight with a smaller radius must be heavier. For simplicity we keep the radius \(a\).

Lemma 23.32 Counterweight of a rotating mass (protiutež vrteče se mase)

Let a mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) go round a circle of radius \(a\) about an axis, and a mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) round a circle of radius \(a\) about the same axis, so that it always lies opposite the first (angle \(\theta + \pi\)), at constant speed \(\Omega\) and \(\theta = \Omega t\). The two circles lie in planes perpendicular to the axis, which may be different. Then their contribution to the shaking force is

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{rot}}(\theta) = (m_{\mathrm{A}} - m_{\mathrm{c}})\,a\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \tag{23.29}\]

and it is zero for every \(\theta\) if and only if \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\). For a counterweight of radius \(r_{\mathrm{c}} \ne a\) the same holds with \(m_{\mathrm{c}} r_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}} a\).

Proof

We have to show the form of the sum of the forces of both masses and that the vector \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) is never zero.

  1. The position of the mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) relative to the axis is \(a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), the position of the mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) is \(a(\cos(\theta + \pi), \sin(\theta + \pi)) = -a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), because \(\cos(\theta + \pi) = -\cos\theta\) and \(\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta\) (corollary 8.16 with \(\beta = \pi\), \(\cos\pi = -1\), \(\sin\pi = 0\)).
  2. By theorem 8.13 the second time derivative of the position is \(-\Omega^2\) times the position. The acceleration of the mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) is therefore \(-\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), that of the mass \(m_{\mathrm{c}}\) is \(+\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\).
  3. By lemma 23.18 the shaking force is \(-\sum m\,\vek a\), that is \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) - m_{\mathrm{c}}\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), which is equation (23.29).
  4. The length of the vector \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) is \(1\) (lemma 8.11, item 1), so the sum of the two forces is zero if and only if \((m_{\mathrm{A}} - m_{\mathrm{c}})\,a\Omega^2 = 0\), that is \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\).
  5. For the radius \(r_{\mathrm{c}}\) the counterweight has \(r_{\mathrm{c}}\) instead of \(a\) in step 1, and the sum is \((m_{\mathrm{A}} a - m_{\mathrm{c}} r_{\mathrm{c}})\,\Omega^2(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). It is zero if and only if \(m_{\mathrm{c}} r_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}} a\).

Source: NORTON section 13.10 (PDF p. 732) demands the same mass balance, \(m_{\mathrm{bal}}\vek R_{\mathrm{bal}} = -m_{\mathrm{A}}\vek R_{\mathrm{A}}\) (the equation in the picture on PDF p. 732). The proof in steps is ours.

For the rotating masses we have thus obtained what section 23.1 gives us: an unbalance \(U = m\,e\) cancels with an opposite one, and by definition 23.3 the rotor is statically balanced. But the lemma says nothing about the planes of the two masses. Does it really not matter in which plane the counterweight sits?

Example 23.33 Counterweight in another plane (protiutež v drugi ravnini)

Engine \(E\) has a counterweight \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}9\) kg at radius \(a = 50\) mm, but fixed on the shaft \(d = 30\) mm along the axis from the plane of the crank pin \(A\). What is the force on the frame, and what do the bearings do?

  1. By lemma 23.32 the force on the frame is zero, because \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\) and the masses go round the same axis at opposite positions. The lemma does not require the planes to coincide.
  2. The forces \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 = 0{,}045\ \mathrm{kg\,m}\cdot 24\,674\ \mathrm{1/s^2} = 1110{,}3\) N act in opposite directions along lines that are \(d\) apart. Together they are a couple with moment \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 d = 1110{,}3\ \mathrm{N}\cdot 0{,}03\ \mathrm{m} = 33{,}3\) N m (the moment of a couple is the force times the distance between the lines).
  3. This couple turns with the shaft. The bearings take it up as two equal and opposite forces that turn with the shaft: with a bearing distance of \(0{,}2\) m (an assumption of this example) that is \(33{,}3/0{,}2 = 166{,}5\) N at each.

The rotor is statically balanced (the sum of forces is zero) but not dynamically: by theorem 23.2 the bearing forces depend on the unbalance \(U\) and on its moment \(W\) (definition 23.1). Here \(U = 0\), but the moment \(W\) is not zero, because the two masses lie in different planes. This is why real crankshafts have counterweights on both sides of the crank pin, arranged symmetrically about the plane of the pin, so that the moments cancel. For such shafts the single-plane calculation of this section holds.

Source: The example is ours. The idealization of a single-plane device is stated by NORTON section 13.10 (PDF p. 732).

With the counterweight \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\) the rotating mass is balanced. What remains is the force of the piston from section 23.3, \(-m_{\mathrm{B}}\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\) along the cylinder. The difficulty is the form of this force: a vector that turns (the force of the counterweight) and a vector that only oscillates along a line (the force of the piston) cannot cancel. But an oscillation along a line is the sum of two turnings, since

\[\tfrac12\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) + \tfrac12\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta) = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, 0) . \tag{23.30}\]

The first term equals the force of the mass \(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{B}}\) at the pin \(A\): it turns with the crank. The second turns in the opposite direction. A counterweight on the crank, which turns with the shaft, can remove only the first. What fraction \(p\) of the reciprocating mass should the counterweight carry, and what is left? The theorem answers. Figure 23.49 above this paragraph shows the two turnings, each in its own direction, and figure 23.50 before the theorem shows the ellipses they make.

Let the counterweight have mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}} + p\,m_{\mathrm{B}}\) at radius \(a\) opposite the pin \(A\). For \(p = 0\) this balances only the rotating mass (lemma 23.32). For \(p > 0\) it also carries a fraction \(p\) of the reciprocating mass, and we call this overbalancing (preuravnoteženje), because the counterweight is larger than needed for the rotating mass. For \(p = 1\) the counterweight would be as large as if the whole reciprocating mass were screwed onto the pin.

Theorem 23.34 Counterweight of the reciprocating mass (protiutež nihajoče mase)

Let a single-cylinder engine of the model of section 23.3 (rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) at the pin \(A\), reciprocating mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\), constant speed \(\Omega\), everything in one plane) have on its crank a counterweight with unbalance \((m_{\mathrm{A}} + p\,m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\) opposite the pin \(A\), \(p\) any real number. Write \(\theta = \Omega t\) and \(F_0 = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\).

  1. The first harmonic of the shaking force, that is its part proportional to \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\), is
    \[\vek F_1(\theta) = F_0\,\big((1 - p)\cos\theta,\ -p\sin\theta\big) . \tag{23.31}\]
    All remaining harmonics (\(2\Omega\), \(4\Omega\), ...) are independent of \(p\).
  2. Its magnitude is \(|\vek F_1|^2 = F_0^2\big[(1 - p)^2\cos^2\theta + p^2\sin^2\theta\big]\), and its largest value over one turn is
    \[\max_\theta |\vek F_1| = F_0\,\max\big(|1 - p|,\ |p|\big) . \tag{23.32}\]
  3. For every \(p\) we have \(\max(|1 - p|, |p|) \ge \tfrac12\), and equality holds only for \(p = \tfrac12\). Then \(|\vek F_1| = \tfrac12 F_0\) at every \(\theta\), the vector \(\vek F_1\) describes a circle.
Idea

By equation (23.30) the first harmonic of the piston is the sum of two turnings, each of amplitude \(\tfrac12 F_0\). The counterweight turns with the crank, so it changes only the first: its amplitude becomes \((\tfrac12 - p)F_0\). The other, which turns against the crank, stays \(\tfrac12 F_0\) for every \(p\). The sum of two turnings in opposite directions is an ellipse, longest when the two vectors are parallel, so \(|\tfrac12 - p| + \tfrac12\) long, and never shorter than \(\tfrac12\). We write this as a direct computation.

Proof

We have to show three items, each from the sum of forces by the definition of the shaking force.

  1. By lemma 23.18 the shaking force is \(-\sum m\,\vek a\) over all moving masses. The force of the rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) is \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\Omega^2 a(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), the force of the counterweight with unbalance \((m_{\mathrm{A}} + p\,m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\) is \(-(m_{\mathrm{A}} + p\,m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\Omega^2(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) (steps 1 to 3 of lemma 23.32 with \(m_{\mathrm{c}} r_{\mathrm{c}} = (m_{\mathrm{A}} + p\,m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\)). Their sum is \(-p\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = -pF_0\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\).
  2. The piston moves along the line \(x\), so its contribution is \(-m_{\mathrm{B}}\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (by lemma 23.15 the sum of the forces of the conrod and the piston is the same as if the pins carried the masses \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\)). By theorem 23.12 (items 1 to 4, which hold for the acceleration too) \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = -a\Omega^2\cos\theta + (\text{even harmonics})\): the first-order coefficient of the acceleration is \(-a\Omega^2\), there are no sine terms and no odd harmonics from the third on, and the series converges uniformly. The contribution of the piston is therefore \(F_0\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R(\theta)\), where \(\vek R\) contains only the harmonics \(2\Omega\), \(4\Omega\), ... and does not depend on \(p\).
  3. The sum of steps 1 and 2: the first harmonic is \(F_0\big(\cos\theta - p\cos\theta,\ -p\sin\theta\big)\), which is equation (23.31). The remaining harmonics are \(\vek R\), independent of \(p\). This proves item 1.
  1. The square of the magnitude is \(F_0^2\big[(1 - p)^2\cos^2\theta + p^2\sin^2\theta\big]\). With \(q = \cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta = 1 - q\) (lemma 8.11, item 1) this is \(F_0^2\big[(1 - p)^2 q + p^2(1 - q)\big]\), a linear function of the number \(q\) on the interval \(0 \le q \le 1\). A linear function on an interval takes its largest value at an endpoint: at \(q = 1\) (\(\theta = 0\)) the value \((1 - p)^2\), at \(q = 0\) (\(\theta = \pi/2\)) the value \(p^2\). Both endpoints are really occupied (they are the squares of the semi-axes of the ellipse), so the largest value is \(\max\big((1 - p)^2, p^2\big)\), and taking square roots gives equation (23.32). This proves item 2.
  1. By the triangle inequality \(|1 - p| + |p| \ge |(1 - p) + p| = 1\): the way from \(p\) to \(0\) and the way from \(p\) to \(1\) together are not shorter than the segment from \(0\) to \(1\). If both numbers were smaller than \(\tfrac12\), their sum would be smaller than \(1\). The larger of the two is therefore at least \(\tfrac12\).
  2. Let the larger be exactly \(\tfrac12\). Both numbers are then at most \(\tfrac12\) and their sum is at least \(1\), so both equal \(\tfrac12\). From \(|p| = \tfrac12\) and \(|1 - p| = \tfrac12\) follows \(p = \tfrac12\), the point in the middle of the segment. For \(p = \tfrac12\) we have \(|\vek F_1|^2 = \tfrac14 F_0^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = \tfrac14 F_0^2\), the same for every \(q\). This proves item 3.
Explanation

No counterweight on the crank can reduce the first harmonic below one half, because the half that turns against the crank remains for every \(p\). For \(p = \tfrac12\) this half is all that remains: the force has constant magnitude and turns. This is the best \(p\) for the first harmonic. The other half can be removed only by something that turns against the crank.

Source: NORTON section 13.10 (PDF p. 735) states in words that the reciprocating unbalanced force cannot be completely removed with one rotating counterweight, and that overbalancing reduces the force along the \(x\) axis at the price of a transverse force. Equations (23.31) and (23.32) and the proof are not from the source.

What does this look like in figure 23.50? On the left we draw the tip of the vector \(\vek F_1\) during a turn in the plane \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}})\), the \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) axis along the cylinder and the \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) axis across as in figure 23.46, with the unit \(F_0\). For \(p = 0\) we have \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and the vector oscillates along the axis between \(\pm 1\): this is the first harmonic of the unbalanced force of the piston. For \(p = 1\) we have \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and the vector oscillates across, again \(\pm 1\): we have swapped the direction, not the size. In between, the tip of the vector describes an ellipse with semi-axes \(|1 - p|\) along \(x\) and \(|p|\) along \(y\), for \(p = \tfrac34\) mirrored. For \(p = \tfrac12\) the semi-axes are equal and the ellipse is a circle of radius \(\tfrac12\): the largest vector is the shortest. On the right the axis \(F\) over \(p\) shows the largest magnitude of the first harmonic \(\max(|1 - p|, |p|)\): two lines that meet in the lowest point at \(p = \tfrac12\), which is the bound of item 3.

The first harmonic is only part of the force. The orange curve shows the whole force of engine \(E\) with all harmonics of the piston, and its lowest point is not at \(\tfrac12\). Let us look at numbers.

Of the pin forces, the counterweight changes only the force in the main bearing. By NORTON the forces in the crank pin and in the piston pin stay the same, because of the pin-force equations only the one for the main bearing contains the mass of the crank, to which the counterweight belongs (PDF pp. 732 and 736). With the counterweight \(p = \tfrac12\) we are thus left with a circle of radius \(\tfrac12 F_0\). By equation (23.30) this is the half of the first harmonic that turns against the crank. Something that turns in the opposite direction must remove it, and the next lemma tells us how.

Lemma 23.36 Two counter-rotating masses (dve protivrteči masi)

Let two masses \(m\) turn with constant angular velocity \(\omega\) each about its own axis (the axes are parallel, the circles lie in one plane perpendicular to them), the first in the positive direction with angle \(\psi_1 = \omega t + \alpha\), the second in the negative one with angle \(\psi_2 = -\omega t + \beta\), both at radius \(e\) from their own axis. With \(s = (\alpha + \beta)/2\) and \(\vek u = (\cos s, \sin s)\) their joint shaking force is

\[\vek F(t) = 2\,m e\omega^2\,\cos\!\Big(\omega t + \frac{\alpha - \beta}{2}\Big)\,\vek u . \tag{23.33}\]

The force always lies in the same direction \(\vek u\) and oscillates along it with circular frequency \(\omega\) and amplitude \(2\,m e\omega^2\). If the masses are not equal, \(m_1 \ne m_2\), the joint force is

\[\vek F = e\omega^2\Big[(m_1 + m_2)\cos d\,\vek u + (m_1 - m_2)\sin d\,\vek u^{\perp}\Big] , \qquad d = \omega t + \frac{\alpha - \beta}{2} , \tag{23.34}\]

with \(\vek u^\perp = (-\sin s, \cos s)\), and it describes an ellipse with semi-axes \((m_1 + m_2)\,e\omega^2\) and \(|m_1 - m_2|\,e\omega^2\). When both masses turn in the same direction with equal phase angles (\(\alpha = \beta\)), the sum is a circle of radius \((m_1 + m_2)\,e\omega^2\).

Idea

A mass that goes round a circle uniformly has acceleration \(-\omega^2\) times its position relative to the axis, so its force on the frame is \(m\omega^2\) times the position. The positions of the two masses are symmetric about the line with angle \(s\), so the components perpendicular to this line cancel and along it they add. This is what the addition formulas say.

Proof

We have to show the form of the sum from the positions of the two masses.

  1. With \(d = \omega t + (\alpha - \beta)/2\) we have \(\psi_1 = s + d\) and \(\psi_2 = s - d\), because \(s + d = \omega t + \alpha\) and \(s - d = -\omega t + \beta\).
  2. By theorem 8.13 the second derivative of the position of a mass, measured from its axis, \(e(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)\), is \(-\omega^2\) times the position, also for \(\psi_2\), where the derivative of the angle is \(-\omega\) and \((-\omega)^2 = \omega^2\). By lemma 23.18 the force of each mass on the frame is \(m\omega^2 e(\cos\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \sin\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). The positions of the axes do not matter, because the acceleration depends only on the position relative to the axis, so we may draw both forces from one point.
  3. By corollary 8.16 we have for the sums of the two forces \(\cos(s + d) + \cos(s - d) = 2\cos s\cos d\) and \(\sin(s + d) + \sin(s - d) = 2\sin s\cos d\).
  4. The sum of the forces of equal masses is therefore \(m\omega^2 e\cdot 2\cos d\,(\cos s, \sin s)\), which is equation (23.33).
  5. For different masses \(m_1\cos(s + d) + m_2\cos(s - d) = (m_1 + m_2)\cos s\cos d - (m_1 - m_2)\sin s\sin d\) and \(m_1\sin(s + d) + m_2\sin(s - d) = (m_1 + m_2)\sin s\cos d + (m_1 - m_2)\cos s\sin d\) (corollary 8.16). This is \((m_1 + m_2)\cos d\,\vek u + (m_1 - m_2)\sin d\,\vek u^\perp\), equation (23.34). Since \(\vek u\) and \(\vek u^\perp\) are perpendicular of length \(1\), this is an ellipse with the stated semi-axes.
  6. For the same direction of turning the angles are \(\omega t + \alpha\) and \(\omega t + \beta\), and the sum is \(e\omega^2\) times \(m_1(\cos(\omega t + \alpha), \sin(\omega t + \alpha)) + m_2(\cos(\omega t + \beta), \sin(\omega t + \beta))\). For \(\alpha = \beta\) this is \((m_1 + m_2)\,e\omega^2(\cos(\omega t + \alpha), \sin(\omega t + \alpha))\) with constant length, that is a circle.

Source: NORTON section 14.9 (PDF p. 779) describes the same arrangement in words: the forces cancel the horizontal components and add the vertical ones to twice the value. The proof in steps and the ellipse for unequal masses are not from the source.

We illustrate the lemma in figure 23.49. We take a circle and put two equal masses on it: the blue one, which turns counter-clockwise, and the orange one, which turns clockwise. Their forces on the frame, drawn radially outward, together have a component only along the \(x\) axis: at \(\omega t = 0\) both point the same way and the sum is twice the force of one mass (\(2\,m e\omega^2\)). At \(\omega t = 60^\circ\) they point above and below the axis, the vertical components cancel, the horizontal ones are \(\tfrac12 m e\omega^2\) each and together \(m e\omega^2\). At \(120^\circ\) the sum reverses. At \(180^\circ\) it is twice again, but in the opposite direction. The sum therefore moves along the axis as \(2\,m e\omega^2\cos\omega t\), as the lemma says.

The lower pair of axes shows what happens if we break the lemma. For equal masses the sum stays on the segment along the axis. If the masses differ, \(m_1 = \tfrac32 m_2\) (in units of \(m_1 e\omega^2\) the semi-axes are \(1 + \tfrac23 = 1{,}667\) and \(1 - \tfrac23 = 0{,}333\)), the segment widens into an ellipse: the transverse component \((m_1 - m_2)\,e\omega^2\sin d\) does not vanish. For the same direction of turning the sum is a circle of radius \(2\), which is what we have with the counterweight on the crank. For engine \(E\), unbalances of \(0{,}045\) and \(0{,}030\) kg m instead of two equal ones would give semi-axes of \(1850{,}6\) N along and \(370{,}1\) N across: a transverse force that was not there before would replace the oscillation along a line by an ellipse. The masses really must be equal.

From history

NORTON says on PDF p. 735 that the force of one piston cannot be completely removed with a counterweight on the crank, and that it would be removed only by a second reciprocating mass that oscillates in the opposite phase: an additional piston and cylinder. With one cylinder there is no such possibility. The solution on PDF p. 779 is a pair of counter-rotating shafts with eccentric masses set \(180^\circ\) apart: the horizontal components of the centrifugal forces cancel, the vertical ones add to twice the force of one mass. Such an arrangement is called a Lanchester balancer (Lanchesterjev uravnoteževalnik) after its English inventor, who developed it before the First World War (about the year 1913), and according to NORTON it is still used in various machines and engines to cancel inertia forces (PDF p. 779). The reason is exactly lemma 23.36: two equal masses that turn in opposite directions give a force on one line. NORTON adds on PDF p. 779 that for the first harmonic one usually needs two shafts at crank speed, of which one can be the crankshaft itself, and for the second harmonic at least two shafts at twice the speed, driven by chain or gears. For a four-cylinder inline engine (PDF p. 783) shafts at twice the speed remove the second harmonic of the force that the ordinary inline four leaves (section 23.7).

Let us return to engine \(E\) with the counterweight and ask what the Lanchester pairs achieve. A counter-rotating pair at speed \(\Omega\) removes the first harmonic of the force, a pair at \(2\Omega\) the second, by lemma 23.36 each on the cylinder axis.

The primary pair must give the force \(-F_0\cos\theta\) along \(x\). By the lemma the amplitude is \(2\,m e\omega^2\) with \(\omega = \Omega\), so \(2\,m e = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\) and \(m e = \tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\) for each mass. This is exactly the excess of the unbalance of the counterweight at \(p = \tfrac12\) over \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\). One of the two masses is therefore already replaced by the crank with its counterweight, as NORTON also says (PDF p. 779: one of the shafts can be the crankshaft itself). Only one more shaft is needed, turning against the crank, with the same unbalance \(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{B}} a = 0{,}045\) kg m. The secondary pair turns with \(\omega = 2\Omega\) and must give the force \(-c_2 F_0\cos 2\theta\), so \(2\,m e\,(2\Omega)^2 = c_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\) and \(m e = c_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a/8\), which for engine \(E\) is \(0{,}002858\) kg m per mass (for example \(57{,}2\) g at radius \(50\) mm). We choose the phases so that at \(\theta = 0\) the masses are on the side of the axis opposite the piston: \(\alpha = \beta = \pi\) in the lemma gives \(s = \pi\), \(\vek u = (-1, 0)\) and the force \(-2\,m e\omega^2\cos\omega t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

Example 23.37 Engine \(E\) with Lanchester pairs (motor \(E\) s Lanchesterjevima paroma)

Engine \(E\) has on the crank a counterweight with \(p = 0\), that is \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\). We add a primary pair with unbalance \(0{,}045\) kg m per shaft and a secondary pair with \(0{,}002858\) kg m per shaft, each divided into two halves on both sides of the cylinder plane. What remains?

  1. Without pairs the force along \(x\) is \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = F_0[\cos\theta + c_2\cos 2\theta + c_4\cos 4\theta + c_6\cos 6\theta]\) with \(c_2 = 0{,}254025\), \(c_4 = -0{,}004098\) and \(c_6 = 0{,}000074\) (theorem 23.19), at most \(2775{,}8\) N.
  2. The primary pair adds \(-F_0\cos\theta\) (lemma 23.36). What remains is \(F_0[c_2\cos 2\theta + c_4\cos 4\theta + c_6\cos 6\theta]\), at most \(573{,}4\) N, at \(\theta = 90^\circ\): \(F_0\,\lvert{-0{,}254025} - 0{,}004098 - 0{,}000074\rvert = 2220{,}7\cdot 0{,}258197 = 573{,}4\) N.
  3. The secondary pair adds \(-c_2F_0\cos 2\theta\). What remains is \(F_0[c_4\cos 4\theta + c_6\cos 6\theta]\), at most \(9{,}3\) N, that is \(0{,}3\ \%\) of the original value.
  4. Dividing a pair into two halves on both sides of the cylinder plane is needed when the masses of the pair cannot be placed in the cylinder plane. The reason is the moment, as in example 23.33. The force \(F\) of a pair acts in a plane at distance \(d\) from the cylinder plane along the crankshaft. Its moment about a transverse axis through the cylinder plane is \(F\,d\) (force times distance). If we divide the pair into two halves at distances \(+d\) and \(-d\), the moment is \(\tfrac12 F\,d - \tfrac12 F\,d = 0\), and the sum of the forces stays \(F\).

The price is additional shafts with a drive, chain or gears (NORTON, PDF p. 779): four in this example, two at speed \(\Omega\) and two at \(2\Omega\), or three if the crankshaft with a counterweight \(p = \tfrac12\) replaces one of the primary ones. For a four-cylinder inline engine with a displacement below about \(2{,}0\) litres the secondary forces may, by NORTON, be small enough to ignore, above it a balancer is needed (PDF p. 783, section 23.7).

Source: The example is ours, for our model. NORTON section 14.9 (PDF p. 779) also mentions two pairs of eccentrics displaced along the shaft from one another, which gives a moment. The division of a pair into two halves so that the moment vanishes is our computation.

We see the result in figure 23.54. The \(\theta\) axis and the \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) axis in units of \(F_0\) show one turn. The blue curve is the force of engine \(E\) with the counterweight \(p = 0\): at \(\theta = 0\) it is \(1{,}25\) (the sum \(1 + c_2 + c_4 + c_6\), the piston at top dead centre), at \(\theta = 180^\circ\) it is \(-0{,}75\), because there the first harmonic changes sign and the higher ones do not: \(-1 + c_2 + c_4 + c_6\). If we add the primary pair, the orange curve remains, with waves twice as fast. If we also add the secondary pair, the force is the blackwhite line, which hardly differs from the \(\theta\) axis: at most \(0{,}004\,F_0\).

What we have shown. With one cylinder, a counterweight on the crank can completely remove the force of the rotating mass (lemma 23.32, in one plane), but the first harmonic of the reciprocating one at most by half (theorem 23.34), because the turning in the opposite direction remains. Overbalancing with \(p\) around \(0{,}5\) to \(0{,}6\) reduces the largest force, which for engine \(E\) means \(1665{,}5\) to \(1465{,}9\) N instead of \(2775{,}8\) N. Complete removal of the first and second harmonics is given by counter-rotating pairs (lemma 23.36), at the price of at least three additional shafts. The other way is an additional piston: section 23.7 deals with it, where we choose the phase angles of the cranks so that the first and second harmonics cancel between the cylinders. There we shall therefore also compute the moments that this section mentions.

The bearing forces caused by the couple of a counterweight outside the plane of the pin are computed by theorem 23.2 of section 23.1. The flexibility of the rotor and balancing in operation are the subject of chapter 24.
