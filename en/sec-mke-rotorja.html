Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.5 Finite elements of a rotor

The Jeffcott rotor (section 24.1) has one mass on a massless shaft, and a tilting disk (sections 24.3 and 24.4) adds two angular unknowns. The running rotor R1 is a steel shaft of diameter 40 mm between two bearings 1 m apart, with a disk of diameter 400 mm and thickness 50 mm at the middle. The shaft has mass \(9{,}802\) kg, the disk \(49{,}009\) kg, so the shaft weighs \(20{,}0\) % of the disk, and the Rayleigh quotient (theorem 13.54) predicted a frequency \(4{,}53\) % lower for this mass. For a rotor with several disks, or with a shaft whose cross-section changes along its length, such an estimate by hand is not enough. So we do what we did for the beam in chapter 12: we divide the shaft into elements, put the disk into a node and assemble the equations from energies. The only new thing is that the shaft turns. This brings two consequences. The bending takes place in two planes at once, and every piece of the shaft has, besides the rotary inertia (section 13.10), a gyroscopic contribution that couples the two planes, just as for the disk in section 24.3.

24.5.1 Model and unknowns

The aim of this section is an equation of the form \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat G\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek f\) for a rotor of any length with any number of disks, with matrices assembled element by element. The model has named idealizations.

  • The shaft is round with a constant diameter within an element, and we treat it as a Rayleigh beam (definition 13.58): the cross-section stays plane and perpendicular to the axis and turns as a whole, shear is neglected. The Timoshenko element, which adds shear, is only named (FPGL, section 5.4.2, PDF pp. 181 to 185).
  • The spin speed \(\Omega\) is constant, as for the isochronous machine (izo-stroj) in example 9.64. The slopes of the shaft \(v'\) and \(w'\) are small, and the kinetic energy is written to second order in the slopes, as for the disk (lemma 24.15).
  • The shaft is massless only in sections 24.1 to 24.4. Here it has mass and rotary inertia, while shear and internal damping do not appear (that is section 24.7).
  • The bearings are short and rigid (the displacement at the bearing is zero, the slope is free), except where we name a spring of stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\). Hydrodynamic bearings and seals are only named (FPGL, section 5.5, PDF pp. 194 to 212). The stiffness of a rolling bearing from Hertz contact is treated in chapter 25.
  • The rotor and the bearings are isotropic: the same stiffness in both planes. A case where this does not hold is in section 24.2.

The notation is the same as before and is only collected here, because some letters meet their other meanings. The letter \(\Omega\) is the spin speed of the shaft (in chapter 9 the excitation frequency, and for unbalance the same number). The letter \(\omega\) is the whirl frequency, and the bold \(\boldsymbol\omega\) of chapter 8 (angular velocity) does not appear in this section. The polar and transverse mass moments of inertia of the disk are \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) (in chapter 13 \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\) is our \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\)), and the second moment of area of the shaft cross-section is \(I\) (definition 5.26) and the polar one \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\), both in m\(^4\). The matrix \(\mat G\) is the gyroscopic matrix (definition 24.16), while \(G\) without bold type is the shear modulus, which does not appear here. The radius of gyration of the cross-section (definition 13.61) is written as \(\sqrt{I/A}\) without a letter of its own, because the letter \(r\) means the ratio \(\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) from chapter 9.

We draw the mesh on rotor R1 with four elements (figure 24.16). We draw the axis of the shaft, a horizontal line \(x\) that joins the centres of the cross-sections. We divide it into four equal elements \(e_1\) to \(e_4\) of length \(h = L/n\), where \(n\) is the number of elements. The boundaries between them are the nodes 0 to 4. A node is so called because the ends of neighbouring elements are held together in it like a rope in a knot, and an element is called finite because it is a piece of finite length \(h\) and not an infinitely small one (chapter 12). At node 2, in the middle, sits the disk of mass \(m\) and moments \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\). At the ends of the shaft the bearings are held by springs of stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\). A rigid bearing is the limiting case \(k_{\mathrm{b}} \to \infty\), and we get it by prescribing the displacement at the node to be zero.

At every node there are two unknowns for each plane, a displacement and a slope. In the plane \(xy\) these are \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = v'(x_{\mathrm{j}})\), in the plane \(xz\) they are \(w_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = w'(x_{\mathrm{j}})\). In figure 24.17 the plane \(xy\) and the plane \(xz\) each carry one element, in blue and orange, and each shows the displacements \(v_1\), \(v_2\) (respectively \(w_1\), \(w_2\)) and the slopes \(\varphi_1\), \(\varphi_2\) (respectively \(\psi_1\), \(\psi_2\)) of both nodes. The deflection between the nodes is a polynomial of third degree built from Hermite functions (definition 12.3), and the planes do not influence each other as long as the cross-section is round. In a turning shaft, however, a gyroscopic term couples them, and so we join them into one complex number, as in section 24.1: the point of the axis in the plane of the cross-section has the coordinates \(v\) and \(w\), and we assign to it \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\). The slope of the complex point is \(p' = v' + \mathrm{i}\,w' = \varphi + \mathrm{i}\,\psi\). The nodal unknown (vozliščna neznanka) of node \(j\) is the pair of complex numbers \((p_{\mathrm{j}}, p_{\mathrm{j}}')\), and the whole unknown of the rotor is a column \(\vek q\) of them, ordered by nodes as for the beam: \(\vek q = \vek d_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\), where \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) are the real columns of nodal quantities of the planes \(xy\) and \(xz\). The rotation \(\Omega t\) of the shaft about its axis does not appear in \(\vek q\), because only the cross-sections carry it and the axis stays in place (the axis is the same axis at every time).

24.5.2 Shaft element

What is the kinetic energy of a piece of a turning shaft? We know the disk from lemma 24.15: a rigid body of mass \(m\) and moments \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) has, for small slopes \(\alpha = v'\) and \(\beta = w'\), the energy \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2) + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{t}}(\dot\alpha^2 + \dot\beta^2) + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2 - J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\,\alpha\dot\beta\). The shaft is spread along its length, so we cannot assign a single mass to it. But we can cut it into \(N\) pieces of length \(\ell = h/N\), take each for a rigid cylinder and add them up, and then let \(\ell\) go to zero. This is the same step as for a sum that passes into an integral (Riemann sum), and it contains no quantity that is itself "infinitely small". The lemma is derived on this path.

Lemma 24.27 Element of a rotating shaft (element vrteče se gredi)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a beam element of length \(h\) (definition 12.3), with a round cross-section of constant diameter \(d\), area \(A\) and second moment of area \(I\) (definition 5.26), with modulus \(E\) and density \(\rho\). The shaft turns with constant speed \(\Omega\) about the axis \(x\), the cross-sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis (Rayleigh model), the slopes are small. The deflection of the axis in both planes is \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) with the nodal columns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} = (v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}} = (w_1, \psi_1, w_2, \psi_2)^\T\). Then:

  1. The polar second moment of area \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\) is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 2I\).
  2. The kinetic energy of the element is, up to the term \(\tfrac12\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2 h\), which does not depend on the displacements,
    \[T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \Omega\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\,\mat G_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} , \qquad \mat M_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat m_{\mathrm{e}} + \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} , \quad \mat G_{\mathrm{e}} = 2\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} , \tag{24.27}\]
    where \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\) are the matrices of lemmas 12.43 and 13.65.
  3. The strain energy of the element is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} + \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) with the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) of lemma 12.4.

The matrices \(\mat M_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\mat G_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) are real and symmetric.

Proof

We must show three things: the polar moment of the circle, the kinetic energy as a limit of sums of rigid pieces, and the strain energy of both planes. For the energy we use the disk of lemma 24.15, every piece is such a disk.

  1. A circle is mapped onto itself by a rotation through a right angle, which takes \(y\) into \(z\), so \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = 2I\). For \(d = 40\) mm, \(I = 1{,}2566\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 2{,}5133\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\).
  2. We cut the element into \(N\) pieces of length \(\ell = h/N\) with centres \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\). A piece is a round cylinder of mass \(\rho A\ell\). Its polar mass moment is \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = \rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\ell = 2\rho I\ell\) by step 1. The transverse moment about a diameter through the centre is \(\int\rho\,(y^2 + s^2)\,\mathrm{d}V = \rho\,(I\ell + A\ell^3/12)\), because \(s \in [-\ell/2, \ell/2]\) is the axial distance and \(\int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2} s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \ell^3/12\).
  3. By lemma 24.15 the piece has the energy \(\tfrac12\rho A\ell\,(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2) + \tfrac12\rho(I\ell + A\ell^3/12)\,(\dot v'^2 + \dot w'^2) + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2 - 2\rho I\ell\,\Omega\,v'\dot w'\), all values at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\). The sum over \(j\) is the kinetic energy of the element, provided the third-order pieces are not too large.
  4. The functions \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\dot w_{\mathrm{h}}\) and their derivatives in \(x\) are polynomials and therefore continuous. The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} f(x_{\mathrm{j}})\,\ell\) of a continuous function goes, as \(\ell \to 0\), to \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} f\,\mathrm{d}x\) (Riemann sum). The term with \(A\ell^3/12\) is \(\tfrac{A\ell^2}{12I}\) times the term with \(I\ell\), so its sum is bounded by \(\ell^2\) times a constant and goes to zero. The limit is \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\big[\tfrac12\rho A(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2 + \dot w_{\mathrm{h}}^2) + \tfrac12\rho I(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 + \dot w_{\mathrm{h}}'^2) - 2\rho I\,\Omega\,v_{\mathrm{h}}'\dot w_{\mathrm{h}}'\big]\mathrm{d}x + \tfrac12\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega^2h\).
  5. We insert \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\). The first two terms give \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\dot{\vek d}\) for each plane (steps 1 and 2 of lemma 12.43), the next two give \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\dot{\vek d}\) (steps 1 and 2 of lemma 13.65). The mixed term is \(2\rho I\,\Omega\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'\dot w_{\mathrm{h}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \Omega\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\big(2\int\rho I\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} = \Omega\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\,2\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}\), because \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\dot w_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}\) are numbers. With the minus sign this is equation (24.27).
  6. The axis of the shaft is a principal axis of the section in every direction (circle, step 1), so bending in the plane \(xy\) and in the plane \(xz\) each has its own energy \(\tfrac12\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\tfrac12\int EI\,(w_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) without a mixed term (theorem 5.35 and lemma 5.59). By lemma 12.4 each integral is \(\tfrac12\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d\).
  7. Symmetry: \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) are symmetric (lemmas 12.4 and 12.43), \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\) is symmetric because of equation (13.74).

Source: The proof is not from the source. FPGL (section 5.4.5, PDF pp. 188 to 190, equations 5.53 to 5.60) derives the gyroscopic shaft element from the same energy as for the disk and says that the polar moment of a thin disk cut from the shaft is twice the transverse one, hence "the factor 2". The matrix there is skew-symmetric, and the signs of the terms follow from FPGL's axes (the axis \(z\) for the shaft, figure 5.4, PDF p. 187), not from our choice of the axis \(x\).

The lemma says how the shaft element is made: the mass matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the bending matrix (lemma 12.43) with the addition of rotary inertia (lemma 13.65), and the gyroscopic matrix \(\mat G_{\mathrm{e}}\) is twice that addition. The factor two is not mysterious: for a round shaft \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 2I\), so turning about the axis is twice as hard as turning about a diameter, which we already see for the disk drawn as a flat plate (\(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 2J_{\mathrm{t}}\) for a thin plate).

The disk and the bearing are elements too. For the disk, the energy is already given by lemma 24.15, and we write it in the form that we shall assemble.

Definition 24.28 Disk and bearing element (element diska in ležaja)

A rigid disk of mass \(m\), transverse mass moment \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and polar mass moment \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) is fixed to the shaft at node \(j\). Its element has matrices that act on the complex unknown \((p_{\mathrm{j}}, p_{\mathrm{j}}')\) and are real \(2\times 2\),

\[\mat M_{\mathrm{d}} = \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & J_{\mathrm{t}} \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat G_{\mathrm{d}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & J_{\mathrm{p}} \end{pmatrix} , \tag{24.28}\]

so the mass acts on the displacement, and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) act on the slope. A bearing with isotropic stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) (N/m) at node \(j\) is a spring with energy \(\tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{j}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{j}}^2)\). It adds \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) to the term of the matrix \(\mat K\) at the displacement \(p_{\mathrm{j}}\) and nothing elsewhere.

Source: FPGL (section 5.3, PDF pp. 175 to 177, equations 5.7 to 5.9) derives the disk elements from the kinetic energy with \(I_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) (our \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\)), and adds the bearings (section 5.5, PDF p. 194) as diagonal terms.

The matrices of equation (24.28) have the same form as the matrices of a piece of shaft from lemma 24.27, when the piece is shrunk towards zero: there the mass is \(\rho A\ell\), \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = \rho I\ell + \ldots\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\rho I\ell\). This is also the reason why the shaft and the disk can be put into the same sum.

24.5.3 Assembling the rotor

Now we have the elements. For the beam, theorem 12.9 said that the matrix of the whole is obtained from the matrices of the elements with selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), which pick out of the whole column of unknowns those that belong to the element. We do the same for the rotor, except that we also have a sum for the disks and the bearings, and that we make one complex equation out of two real ones.

Theorem 24.29 Assembly of a rotor from elements (sestavljanje rotorja)

Let the shaft be divided into \(n\) elements of lemma 24.27 with selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definitions 7.10 and 7.11), let the nodes carry disks and bearings of definition 24.28, and in rigid bearings the displacement at the node is zero (we omit the corresponding rows and columns). Let

\[\mat K = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} + \sum_{\text{bearings}} k_{\mathrm{b}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}^\T , \quad \mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} + \sum_{\text{disks}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{d}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{j}} , \quad \mat G = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat G_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} + \sum_{\text{disks}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat G_{\mathrm{d}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{j}} , \tag{24.29}\]

where \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the unit column at the displacement of node \(j\) and \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{j}}\) the selection matrix of \((p_{\mathrm{j}}, p_{\mathrm{j}}')\). Then:

  1. The kinetic energy of the rotor is, up to a constant, \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \tfrac12\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \Omega\,\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat G\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}\), and the strain energy is \(U = \tfrac12\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} + \tfrac12\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}^\T\mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\).
  2. Lagrange's equations for forces \(\vek f_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\vek f_{\mathrm{w}}\) in the planes are
    \[\mat M\ddot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \Omega\,\mat G\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} + \mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} , \qquad \mat M\ddot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \Omega\,\mat G\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{w}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{w}} . \tag{24.30}\]
  3. For \(\vek q = \vek d_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) and \(\vek f = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek f_{\mathrm{w}}\) the two equations are one complex equation:
    \[\mat M\,\ddot{\vek q} - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat G\,\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek f . \tag{24.31}\]
  4. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive definite if the bearings hold the shaft (at least two different bearings or two prescribed displacements). The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, and positive definite if the shaft has mass. The matrix \(\mat G\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Therefore the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\mat 0 & \mat G \\ -\mat G & \mat 0\end{pmatrix}\) of the real form is skew-symmetric, and the equations have the form of theorem 24.21, so all frequencies are real.
  5. The unbalance of the disk at node \(j\) (definition 24.6) gives in \(\vek f\), at the displacement \(p_{\mathrm{j}}\), the term \(m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\Omega^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,\Omega t}\) (up to a phase angle), as in theorem 24.7.
Idea

We add up the energy element by element as for the beam, then compute Lagrange's equations for \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) separately and join them.

Proof

We must show that the energy is a sum over the elements, that both real equations follow from it, that these combine, and that the matrices have the properties of item 4.

  1. By theorem 12.9, item 1, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat A\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat A\vek d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{e}}\) for every matrix \(\mat A\), and likewise for \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\) and for the mixed term \(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat G_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}\). The sum over the elements of lemma 24.27 with the disks (kinetic energy of the disk from lemma 24.15) and the springs \(\tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{j}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{j}}^2)\) is item 1.
  2. Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.88) for the coordinate \(d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{I}}\) are \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\partial T/\partial\dot d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{I}} - \partial T/\partial d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{I}} + \partial U/\partial d_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{I}} = f_{\mathrm{v},\mathrm{I}}\). From item 1: \(\partial T/\partial\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} = \mat M\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}}\), \(\partial T/\partial\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} = -\Omega\,\mat G\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}\) and \(\partial U/\partial\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} = \mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\). This is the first equation (24.30).
  3. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\): \(\partial T/\partial\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} = \mat M\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \Omega\,\mat G^\T\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\mat G^\T = \mat G\), so \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\) of it is \(\mat M\ddot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \Omega\,\mat G\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}}\). The term \(T\) does not contain \(\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\), so \(\partial T/\partial\vek d_{\mathrm{w}} = \vek 0\), and \(\partial U/\partial\vek d_{\mathrm{w}} = \mat K\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}\). This is the second equation.
  4. We add the first equation to the second multiplied by \(\mathrm{i}\): \(\mat M(\ddot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\ddot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}) + \Omega\,\mat G\,(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \mathrm{i}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}}) + \mat K\,(\vek d_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\vek d_{\mathrm{w}}) = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\vek f_{\mathrm{w}}\). Because \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}} - \mathrm{i}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} = -\mathrm{i}\,(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{w}}) = -\mathrm{i}\dot{\vek q}\), this is equation (24.31).
  5. The matrices \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat G\) are by equation (24.29) sums of real symmetric matrices, hence symmetric. For \(\mat K\): \(\vek d^\T\mat K\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum k_{\mathrm{b}}\,v_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \ge 0\) (theorem 12.9, item 3). If it is zero, then \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'' = 0\) on every element, so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a polynomial of degree at most one on the whole beam (continuity of the slope) that is zero at two points or has a prescribed displacement, hence it is zero, and \(\vek d = \vek 0\). For \(\mat M\): \(\vek d^\T\mat M\vek d \ge \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\int\rho A\,v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\) (lemmas 12.43 and 13.65, step 5 of the first), if it is zero then \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) on every element (continuous nonnegative integrand), so \(\vek d = \vek 0\). For \(\mat G\): \(\vek d^\T\mat G\vek d = 2\sum_{\mathrm{e}}\int\rho I\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\text{disks}} J_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \ge 0\), where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the slope in \(\vek d\) at the node of the disk (equation (13.74)).
  6. The real form of equation (24.30) with \(\vek x = (\vek d_{\mathrm{v}}, \vek d_{\mathrm{w}})\) is \(\hat{\mat M}\ddot{\vek x} + \Omega\,\hat{\mat G}\dot{\vek x} + \hat{\mat K}\vek x = \vek f\) with blocks \(\hat{\mat G} = \begin{pmatrix}\mat 0 & \mat G \\ -\mat G & \mat 0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\hat{\mat G}^\T = -\hat{\mat G}\) because of \(\mat G^\T = \mat G\), and with \(\hat{\mat M}\), \(\hat{\mat K}\) symmetric and positive definite. Step 5 fulfils the assumptions of theorem 24.21. Item 5 of the theorem is the unbalance force of the disk, which by theorem 24.7 has the form \(m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\Omega^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), and which by the definition \(\vek f = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\vek f_{\mathrm{w}}\) we place at the displacement of the disk node.

Source: FPGL (section 5.7, PDF pp. 214 to 215, equation 5.109) assembles the same matrices element by element and with selection matrices and omits the rows for rigid bearings. The proof of the theorem is not from the source.

The theorem says that the whole rotor is described by the same complex equation as the Jeffcott rotor, only with a column \(\vek q\) in place of the number \(p\) and with matrices in place of numbers: \(m\to\mat M\), \(k\to\mat K\), and the term \(-\mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat G\dot{\vek q}\) brings the gyroscopic coupling. For rotor R1 with \(n = 16\) elements, \(\vek q\) is a column of 32 complex unknowns (17 nodes times two, minus two displacements at the bearings), which is 64 real ones. We look for the whirl frequencies as solutions \(\vek q = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\), that is \((\mat K + \Omega\,\omega\,\mat G - \omega^2\mat M)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), and \(\omega > 0\) means forward whirl, \(\omega < 0\) backward (definition 24.3). This is a quadratic eigenvalue problem in \(\omega\). We solve it numerically by an extension to a matrix of twice the size (the states and their derivatives), which chapter 20 treats as the state form. Everything that the book proves uses only theorem 24.21 or the synchronous whirl of section 24.4.

24.5.5 A test on a uniform shaft

For R1 the influence of the shaft hides in the margin of \(0{,}009\) %, because the disk at midspan covers its influence. For a shaft without disks it is visible. We take a simply supported shaft without a disk, that is a uniform shaft of length \(L = 1\) m and diameter 40 mm, and for it we can compute the exact solution in closed form. This solution is the yardstick for the mesh and at the same time a counterexample to the rule of chapter 13: rotary inertia always lowers the frequency, by theorem 13.62. In a turning rotor this does not hold for forward whirl.

Lemma 24.31 Uniform rotating shaft (enakomerna vrteča se gred)

A simply supported round shaft of length \(L\) with constant \(E\), \(\rho\), \(A\), \(I\) turns with \(\Omega\). Let \(\beta = n\pi/L\) (here \(n = 1, 2, \ldots\) is the number of the mode, not the number of elements), \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = \beta^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\) and \(\varepsilon = \beta^2 I/A\), and let \(\varepsilon < 1\) (the radius of gyration \(\sqrt{I/A}\) of definition 13.61, \(\varepsilon = (n\pi\sqrt{I/A}/L)^2\)). The equations of motion of the Rayleigh model of a rotating shaft are \(\rho A\,v_{\mathrm{tt}} - \rho I\,v_{\mathrm{xxtt}} + EI\,v_{\mathrm{xxxx}} - 2\rho I\,\Omega\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}} = 0\) and \(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\rho I\,\Omega\,v_{\mathrm{xxt}} = 0\) with the boundary conditions \(v = v_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(w = w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at both ends. Then:

  1. The complex function \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w = P\sin(\beta x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) (\(P \ne 0\), \(\omega\) real) solves both equations and both conditions exactly when
    \[(1 + \varepsilon)\,\omega^2 - 2\varepsilon\,\Omega\,\omega - \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 = 0 . \tag{24.32}\]
  2. Equation (24.32) has one positive root (forward whirl) and one negative (backward whirl), \(\omega_\pm = \big(\varepsilon\,\Omega \pm \sqrt{\varepsilon^2\Omega^2 + (1 + \varepsilon)\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2}\big)/(1 + \varepsilon)\). At \(\Omega = 0\) they are \(\pm\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\) (theorem 13.62, item 2), and as \(\Omega\) grows the forward frequency grows and the absolute value of the backward frequency falls.
  3. Synchronous forward whirl, \(\omega_+ = \Omega\), occurs exactly at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}/\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}\), which is the critical speed (definition 24.8). It is higher than \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\) by the factor \(\sqrt{(1 + \varepsilon)/(1 - \varepsilon)}\) and higher than the Euler-Bernoulli value \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\).
Idea

We get the equations of motion as in theorem 13.59, except that in the action the third term is gyroscopic. The function \(\sin\beta x\) solves all spatial derivatives, so only the quadratic equation in \(\omega\) remains.

Proof

We must show that the assumed form is a solution exactly for equation (24.32), that this equation has roots as item 2 says, and that item 3 follows from \(\omega = \Omega\).

  1. The kinetic energy of a piece of shaft is by lemma 24.27 (the limit from step 4) \(\int\big[\tfrac12\rho A(v_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{t}}^2) + \tfrac12\rho I(v_{\mathrm{xt}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2) - 2\rho I\,\Omega\,v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\big]\mathrm{d}x\) up to a constant, and the strain energy is \(\tfrac12\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\). The action is their difference integrated over time.
  2. The variation \(v \to v + \alpha\eta\) (\(\eta\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) continuous, \(\eta = 0\) at \(x = 0, L\) and at \(t_1\), \(t_2\)) gives by steps 1 to 5 of the proof of theorem 13.59 the same result as for the Rayleigh beam, except that an additional term \(-2\rho I\,\Omega\int\!\!\int\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\) comes along. Integrating by parts in \(x\) it equals \(+2\rho I\,\Omega\int\!\!\int\eta\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\), because the bracket \([\eta\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}]_0^L\) is zero (\(\eta = 0\) at the ends). So \(R_{\mathrm{v}} = -\rho A\,v_{\mathrm{tt}} + \rho I\,v_{\mathrm{xxtt}} - EI\,v_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\rho I\,\Omega\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}}\) and the first equation is \(R_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\). The natural boundary condition that step 8 of the same theorem gives is \(v_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), since the additional term contributes nothing at the ends.
  3. The variation \(w \to w + \alpha\zeta\): the term \(-2\rho I\,\Omega\,v_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) gives, by parts in \(t\) (because \(\zeta = 0\) at \(t_1\), \(t_2\)) and then in \(x\), the term \(-2\rho I\,\Omega\int\!\!\int\zeta\,v_{\mathrm{xxt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(R_{\mathrm{w}} = -\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} - 2\rho I\,\Omega\,v_{\mathrm{xxt}}\). This is the second equation, and the boundary condition is \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\).
  4. The complex equation: we add the first equation to the second multiplied by \(\mathrm{i}\). The terms with \(\Omega\) give \(-2\rho I\,\Omega\,(w_{\mathrm{xxt}} - \mathrm{i}\,v_{\mathrm{xxt}}) = -2\rho I\,\Omega\,(-\mathrm{i})\,(v_{\mathrm{xxt}} + \mathrm{i}\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}}) = 2\mathrm{i}\,\rho I\,\Omega\,p_{\mathrm{xxt}}\), so \(\rho A\,p_{\mathrm{tt}} - \rho I\,p_{\mathrm{xxtt}} + EI\,p_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + 2\mathrm{i}\,\rho I\,\Omega\,p_{\mathrm{xxt}} = 0\).
  5. We insert \(p = P\sin(\beta x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\). We have \(p_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2p\), \(p_{\mathrm{xxtt}} = \beta^2\omega^2p\), \(p_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \beta^4p\) and \(p_{\mathrm{xxt}} = -\mathrm{i}\,\beta^2\omega\,p\). The conditions \(p = p_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at \(x = 0, L\) hold because \(\sin(n\pi) = 0\). The equation gives \(\big(-\rho A\,\omega^2 - \rho I\,\beta^2\omega^2 + EI\,\beta^4 + 2\rho I\,\Omega\,\beta^2\omega\big)\,p = 0\). Division by \(\rho A\) with \(\varepsilon = \beta^2I/A\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 = EI\beta^4/(\rho A)\) gives \(-(1 + \varepsilon)\,\omega^2 + 2\varepsilon\,\Omega\,\omega + \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 = 0\), which is equation (24.32). Since \(\sin(\beta x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) is nonzero everywhere except at isolated points, this is also a necessary condition (item 1).
  6. Item 2: the product of the roots of the quadratic equation is \(-\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(1 + \varepsilon) < 0\), so one root is positive and one negative, and \(\omega_\pm\) are as in the formula. At \(\Omega = 0\) they are \(\pm\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\). The derivative \(\mathrm d\omega_\pm/\mathrm d\Omega = \big(\varepsilon \pm \varepsilon^2\Omega/\sqrt{\varepsilon^2\Omega^2 + (1 + \varepsilon)\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2}\big)/(1 + \varepsilon)\), and since the fraction with the root in its denominator is smaller than \(\varepsilon\) for every \(\Omega\) (the root is larger than \(\varepsilon\,\Omega\)), the derivative for \(\omega_+\) is positive (the forward frequency grows) and for \(\omega_-\) also positive, so the negative \(\omega_-\) grows towards zero and its absolute value falls.
  7. Item 3: \(\omega = \Omega\) in equation (24.32) gives \((1 + \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon)\,\Omega^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2\), so \(\Omega^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(1 - \varepsilon)\). For \(\varepsilon < 1\) this is one positive solution, and \(\omega_- < 0 < \Omega\) never coincides with \(\Omega > 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. FPGL (section 6.8.1, PDF p. 295, equation 6.67) sets up the critical speeds of a rotor as an eigenvalue problem for \(\Omega\), in which under synchronous excitation the gyroscopic matrix enters together with the mass matrix under the same factor \(\Omega^2\), which is our \(\mat M - \mat G\). Item 2 at rest is theorem 13.62.

Meaning: rotary inertia has two effects on a shaft, and they have opposite signs in synchronous forward whirl. Without turning it lowers the frequency by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\) (as for the beam), and the gyroscopic term adds \(+2\varepsilon\,\Omega\,\omega\) in forward whirl, so that the total effect is \((1 + \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon) = 1 - \varepsilon\) with the opposite sign. This is the same that we saw for the disk in the value \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\) (section 24.4): for the disk \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 2J_{\mathrm{t}}\) for a thin disk, and for a thin slice of shaft similarly \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\rho I\), so for synchronous whirl \(\rho I - 2\rho I = -\rho I\).

What have we shown? A mesh of beam elements with the complex equation (24.31) gives the Jeffcott rotor, the four degrees of freedom of the disk (section 24.4) and a rotor with shaft mass, and it agrees with the closed form of the uniform shaft, which cannot be proved with the Euler-Bernoulli beam formula alone. The matrices are three, real and symmetric, and therefore theorem 24.21 holds for every rotor that we assemble. In section 24.6 we use them for the critical speeds of a rotor with several disks, and in section 24.8 for balancing.
