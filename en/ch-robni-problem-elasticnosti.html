The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

In the first three chapters we collected three kinds of equations. Strains follow from displacements (chapter 1), stresses must balance the load (chapter 2), and Hooke's law connects stresses with strains (chapter 3). Chapter 4 derived the energy principles from them and showed that a load has at most one solution. Chapter 5 solved beams with them, but with an assumption about the form of the field: how a cross-section moves was assumed in advance.

How do we find the field of a real body, for example the steel tube of a hydraulic cylinder, a plate with a hole or a curved hook, without assuming in advance how it deforms? This is the question of this chapter.

The route is the following. First we collect all the equations and all the conditions on the surface into one problem and show that it has only one solution. Without this, every solution we found would be only one of several possible ones. Then we reduce the problem in two ways to fewer unknowns, to displacements or to stresses, and on the hanging bar we show how a solution is really found: we guess the field and check whether it satisfies all the equations. Round bodies, such as the tube and the sphere, call for coordinates that fit them. With them we solve the thick tube and the thick sphere. Plane problems we reduce to a single function, the Airy stress function (Airyjeva napetostna funkcija), which chapter 4 already named. With it we solve the beams of chapter 5 once more, this time as solutions of the equations of elasticity. Here the question of why we may replace real supports and loads by their resultants arises by itself, and the answer will be Saint-Venant's principle, which we will test on numbers. With all this we solve the plate with a hole and the curved beam, and at the end the body on several separate supports, which chapter 4 promised.

What is new in this chapter is thus five things: the complete set of equations as one problem, two ways to reduce it, coordinates that fit round bodies, plane problems with one function and the principle that justifies replacing the real load by its resultant.
