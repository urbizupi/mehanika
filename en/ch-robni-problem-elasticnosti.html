The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

In the first three chapters we collected three kinds of equations. Strains follow from displacements (chapter 1), stresses must balance the load (chapter 2), and Hooke's law connects stresses with strains (chapter 3). Chapter 4 derived the energy principles from them and showed that a load determines the strains and stresses uniquely. Chapter 5 solved beams with them, but with an assumption about the form of the field: how a cross-section moves was assumed in advance. Now for the first time we put everything together into one problem and solve it for a body of any shape.

Let us look at the words of the title. A boundary value problem is a problem in which the equations hold in the interior of the body and the conditions are given on its boundary, that is on the surface: there the load or the displacement is prescribed. Hence the name. Linear means that displacements and strains are small, so the equations are linear and solutions add up. Small means small compared with the size of the body and with one: for steel with \(E = 196\) GPa up to a yield stress of about 200 MPa the strain is at most about 0.001, so the error of the linear treatment is of the order of 0.1 %. For rubber at a strain of 0.5, or a thin rod with a large bending, it would not be small and the equations of this chapter would not hold. The word comes from the Latin linea, thread, line. Elasticity is the property of a body to return to its original shape after unloading, and the word comes from the Greek elastos, ductile, pliable.

This matters because the beam is a simplification that we may not simply assume for the tube of a hydraulic cylinder or a plate with a hole. Only the solution of the boundary value problem tells where the body is loaded most, for example at the hole, and by how much the stress there exceeds the average.

How do we find the field of a real body, for example the steel tube of a hydraulic cylinder, a plate with a hole or a curved hook, without assuming in advance how it deforms? This is the question of this chapter, and we promise to answer it.

The route is the following. First we collect all the equations and all the conditions on the surface into one problem and show that it has only one solution. Without this, every solution we found would be only one of several possible ones. Then we reduce the problem in two ways to fewer unknowns, to displacements or to stresses, and on the hanging bar we show how a solution is really found: we guess the field and check whether it satisfies all the equations. Round bodies, such as the tube and the sphere, call for coordinates that fit them. With them we solve the thick tube and the thick sphere. Plane problems we reduce to a single function, the Airy stress function (Airyjeva napetostna funkcija), which chapter 4 already named. With it we solve the beams of chapter 5 once more, this time as solutions of the equations of elasticity. Here the question of why we may replace real supports and loads by their resultants arises by itself, and the answer will be Saint-Venant's principle, which we will test on numbers. With all this we solve the plate with a hole and the curved beam, and at the end the body on several separate supports, which chapter 4 promised.

What is new in this chapter is thus five things: the complete set of equations as one problem, two ways to reduce it, coordinates that fit round bodies, plane problems with one function and the principle that justifies replacing the real load by its resultant.
