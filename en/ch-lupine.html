The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

We start with bodies we know from everyday life: an eggshell, a tin can, a pressure vessel, the roof of a dome. All of them are thin compared with their other two dimensions, and all are curved. In chapter 5 we saw how structures are divided by shape: a surface structure has one dimension, the thickness, much smaller than the other two, and if it is flat it is called a plate (chapter 18), if it is curved, a shell (lupina). This chapter is its treatment.

In everyday language a shell is the thin hard cover of an egg or a nut. Engineers use the word for every thin curved body, because it has the same property: the cover is thin and therefore light, yet it carries a lot, because it is curved. The Slovenian word lupina has the same everyday meaning. Why curvature is so useful we will compute on a pressure vessel: a tube with a mean radius of 50 mm and a thickness of 2 mm, with oil at 3 MPa in it, has a wall only \(1/25\) of the radius thick, and the chapter shows that the pressure loads it differently from a flat wall, by tension instead of bending.

The chapter promises three things. First, we will be able to describe any curved wall: where a point of the wall is, how far apart two points are along the wall, at which angle two lines on the wall meet, and how curved the wall is. Second, for a thin wall we will derive from chapter 11 the strains, the internal forces per unit length and the energy, with the limit up to which a thin wall is really thin, and compute the stresses in vessels and domes with numbers from the earlier chapters. Third, we will show where the simple calculation fails, at edges and junctions, and how the finite elements known from chapters 7 and 12 deal with it. What we cannot prove from the book we will state with a source and say that it is stated.

First geometry. Without it we cannot even say where a point of the shell is.
