Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.4 Four degrees of freedom and the Campbell diagram

A disk at midspan only translates, so the Jeffcott rotor of section 24.1 had one complex coordinate. A disk that sits elsewhere also tilts the shaft, and by section 24.3 a spinning disk answers a tilt with a gyroscopic couple. The rotor therefore has four coordinates, two displacements and two slopes, and its natural frequencies are no longer numbers but change with the spin speed \(\Omega\). The graph that shows this change is called the Campbell diagram (Campbellov diagram). The section answers three questions: what frequencies such a rotor has (theorem 24.21), how they change with \(\Omega\) (definition 24.23, lemma 24.24), and at which speeds they meet the spin speed, which gives the critical speeds of the disk (theorem 24.25 and lemma 24.26). It matters because the Campbell diagram shows in one graph at which speeds a machine must not run.

24.4.1 A disk off centre

Rotor \(R_2\) is the same as rotor \(R_1\) of section 24.3: a steel shaft of diameter \(40\) mm on two rigid bearings \(L = 1\) m apart, with \(EI = 24\,630\) N m\(^2\), and a steel disk of diameter \(0{,}4\) m and thickness \(0{,}05\) m with mass \(m = 49{,}009\) kg, \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}9802\) kg m\(^2\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}5003\) kg m\(^2\). The only difference is that the disk is not at midspan but at \(a = 0{,}25\) m from the left bearing, so \(b = 0{,}75\) m from the right one. The shaft is massless (an idealization that section 24.5 removes), the bearings are rigid simple supports and \(\Omega\) is constant. Figure 24.12 shows the model.

At the top we look at the shaft from the side, in the plane \(x y\), at the bottom in the plane \(x z\), as the coordinate crosses on the left show. The bearings are rigid simple supports, the grey dashed line between them is the axis of the shaft at rest. The disk sits at distance \(a\) from the left bearing and \(b\) from the right one. In the plane \(x y\) the shaft displaces the disk by \(v\), and the tangent of the shaft at the disk turns by the angle \(\alpha = v'\). At the bottom the bearings, the axis at rest, the disk and the distances \(a\) and \(b\) are drawn in the same way. In the plane \(x z\) the same happens with displacement \(w\) and slope \(\beta = w'\). On the right we look along the shaft. The axes \(y\) and \(z\) meet in the axis of the shaft at rest, where the cross-section of the shaft is drawn. The centre of the disk is at the point that the complex displacement \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) points to, with component \(v\) along the axis \(y\) and \(w\) along the axis \(z\). When the rotor spins, this point circles around the axis of the shaft. For a disk at midspan displacement and slope are independent. Here they are not, because a force on the disk also tilts the shaft. The next lemma gives the relation between force, couple, displacement and slope.

Lemma 24.20 Stiffness matrix of the shaft at the disk (togostna matrika gredi v disku)

Let a simply supported shaft with bending stiffness \(EI\) and length \(L = a + b\) carry at \(x = a\) a force \(F\) and a couple \(C\) (in the plane \(x y\), \(C\) is a couple about the axis \(z\)). Displacement \(v\) and slope \(\alpha = v'\) at \(x = a\) are

\[\begin{pmatrix} v \\ \alpha \end{pmatrix} = \mat D \begin{pmatrix} F \\ C \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat D = \frac{1}{3EIL}\begin{pmatrix} a^2 b^2 & a b\,(b - a) \\ a b\,(b - a) & a^2 - a b + b^2 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{24.21}\]

the matrix \(\mat D\) is symmetric and positive definite, and its inverse is

\[\mat K = \mat D^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} k_{\mathrm{T}} & k_{\mathrm{C}} \\ k_{\mathrm{C}} & k_{\mathrm{R}} \end{pmatrix} , \quad k_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{3EI\,L\,(a^2 - ab + b^2)}{a^3 b^3} , \quad k_{\mathrm{C}} = \frac{3EI\,(a^2 - b^2)}{a^2 b^2} , \quad k_{\mathrm{R}} = \frac{3EI\,L}{a b} . \tag{24.22}\]
Idea

On each side of the disk the shaft is a beam without load, so a cubic polynomial. Force and couple at the disk fix the jumps of the internal quantities, which we get from the principle of virtual work.

Proof

We must show that the cubic pieces, the end conditions and the conditions at the disk give exactly the matrix (24.21).

  1. By theorem 5.49, \(EI\,v'''' = 0\) on each piece, so \(v\) is a cubic polynomial. At \(x = 0\) we have \(v = 0\) and \(M = EI\,v'' = 0\) (simple support), so \(v_1 = c_1 x^3 + c_2 x\) on \([0, a]\). At \(x = L\) likewise \(v_2 = c_3 (L - x)^3 + c_4 (L - x)\) on \([a, L]\).
  2. The virtual work of the load on a virtual displacement \(\delta v\) with \(\delta v(0) = \delta v(L) = 0\) equals the virtual strain energy: \(\int_0^L EI\,v''\,\delta v''\,\mathrm{d}x = F\,\delta v(a) + C\,\delta v'(a)\).
  3. Split the integral into the two pieces and integrate each twice by parts. Since \(EI\,v'''' = 0\), only boundary terms at \(x = a\) remain: \(\bigl(EI\,v''(a^-) - EI\,v''(a^+)\bigr)\delta v'(a) + \bigl(EI\,v'''(a^+) - EI\,v'''(a^-)\bigr)\delta v(a)\). The terms at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) vanish because of \(\delta v = 0\) and \(v'' = 0\).
  4. Since \(\delta v(a)\) and \(\delta v'(a)\) are arbitrary, the jumps are \(EI\,[v''] = -C\) and \(EI\,[v'''] = F\). In addition \(v\) and \(v'\) are continuous at \(x = a\).
  5. These are four linear equations for \(c_1, \dots, c_4\). SymPy solves them and gives the matrix (24.21) from \(v(a)\) and \(v'(a)\), and its inverse gives (24.22) (num-04.out, the closed forms agree, the remainder is zero).
  6. Positive definiteness: \(D_{11} = a^2 b^2/(3EIL) > 0\) and \(\det\mat D = a^3 b^3/\bigl(9(EI)^2L^2\bigr) > 0\). Hence \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x = D_{11}\bigl(x_1 + x_2 D_{12}/D_{11}\bigr)^2 + x_2^2\det\mat D/D_{11} > 0\) for every \(\vek x \neq \vek 0\), and \(\mat D\) is positive definite (definition 6.27). The same holds for its inverse \(\mat K\), because \(\vek y^\T\mat K\vek y = \vek x^\T\mat D\vek x\) for \(\vek x = \mat K\vek y\).

Source: FPGL §3.5.3 (PDF pp. 109 to 113) and Appendix 2 use the same stiffnesses for a shaft with a disk. The derivation by virtual work is ours.

The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric, so the force on the disk due to the slope (\(k_{\mathrm{C}}\,\alpha\)) and the couple due to the displacement (\(k_{\mathrm{C}}\,v\)) carry the same coefficient. If the disk sits at midspan (\(a = b = L/2\)), then \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) and \(k_{\mathrm{T}} = 48EI/L^3\), the stiffness of the Jeffcott rotor. For \(R_2\) (\(a = 0{,}25\) m, \(b = 0{,}75\) m) we get \(k_{\mathrm{T}} = 4{,}9041\cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = -1{,}0509\cdot 10^6\) N and \(k_{\mathrm{R}} = 3{,}9408\cdot 10^5\) N m, and \(k_{\mathrm{T}} k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = 8{,}283\cdot 10^{11}\) N\(^2\) is positive, as the lemma promises (num-04.out).

24.4.2 Equations of motion and real frequencies

The shaft acts on the disk with the opposite of the force the disk exerts on the shaft: force and couple on the disk in the plane \(x y\) are \(F_{\mathrm{v}} = -(k_{\mathrm{T}} v + k_{\mathrm{C}}\alpha)\) and \(M_\alpha = -(k_{\mathrm{C}} v + k_{\mathrm{R}}\alpha)\). In the plane \(x z\) the shaft is the same because of its round section, so the same law holds for the generalized coordinates \(w\) and \(\beta\). We insert this in theorem 24.17.

Real equations:

\[\begin{aligned} m\ddot v + k_{\mathrm{T}} v + k_{\mathrm{C}}\alpha &= 0 , & J_{\mathrm{t}}\ddot\alpha + J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\dot\beta + k_{\mathrm{C}} v + k_{\mathrm{R}}\alpha &= 0 , \\ m\ddot w + k_{\mathrm{T}} w + k_{\mathrm{C}}\beta &= 0 , & J_{\mathrm{t}}\ddot\beta - J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\dot\alpha + k_{\mathrm{C}} w + k_{\mathrm{R}}\beta &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{24.23}\]

In matrix form \(\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \Omega\,\mat G\dot{\vek x} + \mat K_4\vek x = \vek 0\) for \(\vek x = (v, w, \alpha, \beta)^\T\), with \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(m, m, J_{\mathrm{t}}, J_{\mathrm{t}})\), \(\mat G\) from equation (24.17) and \(\mat K_4\) with the entries \(k_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{C}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{R}}\) at the places \((v,v)\), \((w,w)\), \((v,\alpha)\), \((w,\beta)\) and \((\alpha,\alpha)\), \((\beta,\beta)\). We add the first equation to the second multiplied by \(\mathrm{i}\), and the third to the fourth multiplied by \(\mathrm{i}\). With \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) and \(\vartheta = \alpha + \mathrm{i}\,\beta\) (as in equation (24.19)) we get

\[m\ddot p + k_{\mathrm{T}} p + k_{\mathrm{C}}\vartheta = 0 , \qquad J_{\mathrm{t}}\ddot\vartheta - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega J_{\mathrm{p}}\dot\vartheta + k_{\mathrm{C}} p + k_{\mathrm{R}}\vartheta = 0 . \tag{24.24}\]

We look for a whirl \(p = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\), \(\vartheta = \Theta\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\). For \(\omega > 0\) the centre of the disk circles in the sense of the spin (forward), for \(\omega < 0\) against it (backward), definition 24.3. Inserting gives

\[(k_{\mathrm{T}} - m\omega^2)P + k_{\mathrm{C}}\Theta = 0 , \qquad k_{\mathrm{C}} P + (k_{\mathrm{R}} - J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2 + J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\omega)\Theta = 0 . \tag{24.25}\]

A nonzero solution exists exactly when the determinant is zero:

\[f(\omega, \Omega) = (k_{\mathrm{T}} - m\omega^2)(k_{\mathrm{R}} - J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2 + J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\omega) - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = 0 . \tag{24.26}\]

This is a polynomial of fourth degree in \(\omega\) with coefficients \(J_{\mathrm{t}} m\), \(-J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega m\), \(-(k_{\mathrm{T}} J_{\mathrm{t}} + k_{\mathrm{R}} m)\), \(k_{\mathrm{T}} J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\) and \(k_{\mathrm{T}} k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2\). The question to settle is whether all its roots are real. If it had a complex root \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}} + \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{i}}\), then \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) would grow or decay, and in an undamped rotor nothing can grow or decay. We prove this more generally, for arbitrary matrices.

Theorem 24.21 Real frequencies of a gyroscopic system (realne frekvence žiroskopskega sistema)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be real symmetric positive definite matrices, \(\mat G\) a real skew-symmetric matrix, \(\mat G^\T = -\mat G\), and \(\Omega\) a real number. If the equation \((\lambda^2\mat M + \lambda\,\Omega\,\mat G + \mat K)\,\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) has a solution \(\vek\varphi \neq \vek 0\) (a complex vector, \(\lambda\) a complex number), then \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega\) real.

Idea

Multiply the equation from the left by \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\). This gives a quadratic equation for \(\lambda\) with real \(m\), \(k\) and a purely imaginary \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat G\vek\varphi\).

Proof

We must show that \(\lambda\) is purely imaginary.

  1. Multiply the equation by \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\): \(\lambda^2 m_\varphi + \lambda\,\Omega\,\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat G\vek\varphi + k_\varphi = 0\) with \(m_\varphi = \bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi\) and \(k_\varphi = \bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi\).
  2. Because \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are real, symmetric and positive definite, \(m_\varphi > 0\) and \(k_\varphi > 0\) (definition 6.27, for a complex \(\vek\varphi = \vek a + \mathrm{i}\vek b\) we have \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi = \vek a^\T\mat M\vek a + \vek b^\T\mat M\vek b\), since the mixed terms cancel by symmetry).
  3. The number \(s = \bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat G\vek\varphi\) is purely imaginary: its conjugate is \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat G\bar{\vek\varphi}\), a scalar, which equals its transpose \(\bar{\vek\varphi}^\T\mat G^\T\vek\varphi = -s\). Write \(s = \mathrm{i}g\) with \(g\) real.
  4. The equation is \(m_\varphi\lambda^2 + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega g\,\lambda + k_\varphi = 0\). Its solution is \(\lambda = \dfrac{-\mathrm{i}\,\Omega g \pm\sqrt{-\Omega^2 g^2 - 4m_\varphi k_\varphi}}{2m_\varphi} = \mathrm{i}\,\dfrac{-\Omega g \pm\sqrt{\Omega^2 g^2 + 4m_\varphi k_\varphi}}{2m_\varphi}\).
  5. The discriminant \(\Omega^2 g^2 + 4m_\varphi k_\varphi\) is positive, so the root is real and \(\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega\) real.

Source: GR §2.11 (PDF pp. 137 to 148) states the same result for conservative rotating systems. The proof in steps is ours.

The matrices \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K_4\) of equation (24.23) are symmetric and positive definite (the first because \(m\), \(J_{\mathrm{t}} > 0\), the second because it consists of two copies of the matrix \(\mat K\), which is positive definite by lemma 24.20), and \(\mat G\) is skew-symmetric (definition 24.16), so the theorem holds for rotor \(R_2\). Check: in the matrix form with \(\lambda\) as the eigenvalue, the largest \(|\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda|\) at \(0\), \(3000\) and \(30\,000\) rev/min is below \(10^{-13}\), which is rounding error (num-04.out).

The theorem says that all roots are real, but not how many there are or where. The next lemma answers this and gives a second proof of reality for \(R_2\).

Lemma 24.22 Four frequencies, one in each interval (štiri frekvence, ena v vsakem intervalu)

Let \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \neq 0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{T}}/m}\). At every speed \(\Omega\) equation (24.26) has exactly four real roots, one in each of the intervals \((-\infty, -\omega_{\mathrm{T}})\), \((-\omega_{\mathrm{T}}, 0)\), \((0, \omega_{\mathrm{T}})\) and \((\omega_{\mathrm{T}}, \infty)\). The rotor therefore has at every speed two frequencies of forward whirl and two of backward whirl.

Proof

We must show four sign changes of the polynomial \(f(\cdot, \Omega)\).

  1. At \(\omega = 0\) we have \(f = k_{\mathrm{T}} k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 > 0\) by lemma 24.20, independent of \(\Omega\).
  2. At \(\omega = \pm\omega_{\mathrm{T}}\) the first factor is zero, so \(f = -k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 < 0\), again independent of \(\Omega\).
  3. The leading coefficient \(J_{\mathrm{t}} m\) is positive, so \(f > 0\) for \(\omega \to \pm\infty\).
  4. A continuous function with the values \(+, -, +, -, +\) at \(-\infty\), \(-\omega_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(\infty\) has, by the intermediate value theorem, at least one zero in each of the four intervals. A polynomial of fourth degree has at most four roots, so exactly one in each.

Source: Our proof. FPGL §3.5.3 (PDF pp. 109 to 113) states the same structure of the frequencies without proof.

For \(R_2\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} = 316{,}33\) 1/s, that is \(50{,}35\) Hz. At \(3000\) rev/min the roots are \(-110{,}25\), \(-27{,}88\), \(+35{,}08\) and \(+201{,}01\) Hz, one in each interval (num-04.out). At rest, \(\Omega = 0\), they are \(\pm 31{,}77\) and \(\pm 146{,}55\) Hz, that is \(1906\) and \(8793\) rev/min.

24.4.3 The Campbell diagram

The frequencies thus lie in four intervals and move with \(\Omega\). We draw the movement.

Definition 24.23 Campbell diagram (Campbellov diagram)

The Campbell diagram of a rotor is the graph of the absolute values of the natural whirl frequencies \(|\omega|/2\pi\) (in Hz) against the spin speed \(n = 60\,\Omega/2\pi\) (in rev/min), with the line \(\omega = \Omega\) drawn on it, the frequency of the synchronous excitation due to unbalance. The curves of forward whirl (\(\omega > 0\)) and backward whirl (\(\omega < 0\)) are different.

Source: FPGL §3.7 (PDF pp. 122 to 125) calls this graph a natural frequency map, the name Campbell diagram is introduced in §6.1 (PDF pp. 246 to 250), where the excitation lines are drawn on the same graph.

Figure 24.13 shows the diagram of rotor \(R_2\) up to \(6000\) rev/min. On the horizontal axis is the spin speed \(n\) in rev/min, on the vertical one the frequency \(f = |\omega|/2\pi\) in Hz. At \(n = 0\) the lower forward curve \(\omega_{1+}\) and the lower backward curve \(\omega_{1-}\) meet at \(31{,}77\) Hz, and the upper forward one \(\omega_{2+}\) and the upper backward one \(\omega_{2-}\) at \(146{,}55\) Hz, since without spin there is no difference between the senses. With growing \(\Omega\) the forward frequency rises and the backward frequency falls. The lower forward frequency rises slowly, from \(31{,}77\) through \(35{,}08\) (3000 rev/min) to \(37{,}68\) Hz (6000 rev/min), and stays below \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}/2\pi = 50{,}35\) Hz by lemma 24.22. The upper forward frequency goes from \(146{,}55\) through \(201{,}01\) to \(271{,}04\) Hz, the lower backward one from \(31{,}77\) to \(27{,}88\) and \(23{,}87\) Hz, the upper backward one from \(146{,}55\) to \(110{,}25\) and \(88{,}93\) Hz. The line \(\omega = \Omega\) crosses a forward curve only once.

We prove why the forward frequencies rise and the backward ones fall for the simplest case, when the coupling \(k_{\mathrm{C}}\) drops out.

Lemma 24.24 Pure tilt (čisti nagib)

Let \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}, J_{\mathrm{p}}, k_{\mathrm{R}} > 0\). The tilt equation \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2 - J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\omega - k_{\mathrm{R}} = 0\) has for \(\Omega > 0\) one positive root \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}\) (forward) and one negative root \(-\omega_{\mathrm{b}}\) (backward), \(\omega_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\). We have \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} - \omega_{\mathrm{b}} = J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{b}} = k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}\) increases with \(\Omega\) and exceeds \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{b}}\) decreases with \(\Omega\) and is smaller than \(k_{\mathrm{R}}/(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega)\). At \(\Omega = 0\) both equal \(\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}}\).

Idea

Roots of a quadratic equation in \(\omega\) and the derivative with respect to \(\Omega\).

Proof

We must show four relations.

  1. From \(-J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2 + J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\omega + k_{\mathrm{R}} = 0\) (the second equation of (24.25) without \(k_{\mathrm{C}}\)) and \(s = \sqrt{(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega)^2 + 4J_{\mathrm{t}}k_{\mathrm{R}}}\) the roots are \(\omega = (J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega \pm s)/(2J_{\mathrm{t}})\). Since \(s > J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\), one is positive, \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} = (J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega + s)/(2J_{\mathrm{t}})\), and one negative, \(-\omega_{\mathrm{b}}\) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{b}} = (s - J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega)/(2J_{\mathrm{t}})\).
  2. Difference and product: \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} - \omega_{\mathrm{b}} = J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}\omega_{\mathrm{b}} = (s^2 - J_{\mathrm{p}}^2\Omega^2)/(4J_{\mathrm{t}}^2) = k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}\) (Vieta's formula).
  3. Since \(\omega_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} = J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}} + \omega_{\mathrm{b}} > J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\). Since \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} > J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}\omega_{\mathrm{b}} = k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}\), we get \(\omega_{\mathrm{b}} = k_{\mathrm{R}}/(J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega_{\mathrm{f}}) < k_{\mathrm{R}}/(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega)\).
  4. The derivatives are \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathrm{d}\Omega} = \dfrac{J_{\mathrm{p}}}{2J_{\mathrm{t}}}\Bigl(1 + \dfrac{J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega}{s}\Bigr) > 0\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathrm{b}}}{\mathrm{d}\Omega} = \dfrac{J_{\mathrm{p}}}{2J_{\mathrm{t}}}\Bigl(\dfrac{J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega}{s} - 1\Bigr) < 0\), because \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega < s\). At \(\Omega = 0\), \(s = 2\sqrt{J_{\mathrm{t}}k_{\mathrm{R}}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}} = \omega_{\mathrm{b}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}}\).

Source: FPGL §3.5.3 (PDF pp. 109 to 113) treats the tilt frequencies with the gyroscopic term. The proof in steps is ours.

In figure 24.14 the axes mean the same as in figure 24.13. At rest both frequencies equal \(\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{R}}/J_{\mathrm{t}}}/2\pi = 141{,}25\) Hz. The forward frequency approaches the asymptote \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega/J_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the backward frequency falls toward zero below the curve \(k_{\mathrm{R}}/(J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega)\), which it approaches as \(\Omega\) grows. For \(R_2\) we have \(J_{\mathrm{p}}/J_{\mathrm{t}} = 1{,}959\), so the asymptote is steeper than the line \(\omega = \Omega\), which will be essential in the next part. Numbers: at \(3000\) rev/min \(198{,}48\) Hz forward and \(100{,}52\) Hz backward, at \(30\,000\) rev/min \(999{,}55\) and \(19{,}96\) Hz (num-04.out). For a disk at midspan \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) and equations (24.24) separate: besides the Jeffcott whirl the disk has the tilt frequencies of lemma 24.24, with its own stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{R}} = 12EI/L\) (equation (24.22) at \(a = b = L/2\)). For \(R_2\) we have \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \neq 0\), so figure 24.14 is only an approximation and the true curves are in figure 24.13: the upper pair is close to the pure tilt, but not equal to it.

Check of theorem 24.21: the skew-symmetry of \(\mat G\) is essential. If instead of \(\Omega\mat G\) we take a symmetric matrix (for example damping \(c\) in one degree of freedom with \(m = 1\), \(k = 1\), \(c = 0{,}5\)), then \(\lambda = -0{,}25 \pm 0{,}968\,\mathrm{i}\) is complex. If the matrix \(\mat K\) is not positive definite (\(k = -1\) without \(\mat G\)), then \(\lambda = \pm 1\) is real, but not purely imaginary, and the motion grows (num-04.out). The theorem therefore needs both, a skew-symmetric \(\mat G\) and a positive definite \(\mat K\).

An example from FPGL confirms the model. A shaft of diameter \(38\) mm and length \(1{,}1\) m with a disk of diameter \(0{,}65\) m and thickness \(0{,}1\) m at \(0{,}8\) m from one bearing, \(E = 211\) GPa, \(\rho = 7810\) kg/m\(^3\) (FPGL Example 3.9.1, PDF p. 133). Our model gives \(m = 259{,}2\) kg, \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 13{,}687\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = 7{,}059\) kg m\(^2\), \(k_{\mathrm{T}} = 2{,}526\cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 6{,}19\cdot 10^5\) N and \(k_{\mathrm{R}} = 2{,}97\cdot 10^5\) N m, and at \(900\) rev/min the frequencies \(8{,}31\) backward, \(11{,}88\) forward, \(25{,}55\) backward and \(51{,}06\) Hz forward. At rest they are \(10{,}34\) and \(34{,}72\) Hz. FPGL in Example 3.9.1 (PDF p. 135) states the same four values at \(900\) rev/min (num-04.out).

24.4.4 Critical speeds of the disk

The unbalance of the disk circles together with the disk, so forward with frequency \(\Omega\). A speed is therefore critical (the formal definition is in section 24.6) if a forward whirl frequency meets \(\Omega\). In equation (24.26) we insert \(\omega = \Omega\) and get \((k_{\mathrm{T}} - m\Omega^2)(k_{\mathrm{R}} - (J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}})\Omega^2) - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = 0\), a quadratic equation in \(x = \Omega^2\).

Theorem 24.25 Synchronous critical speeds of the disk (sinhrone kritične hitrosti diska)

Let \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \neq 0\) and \(J = J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\). The critical speeds of forward whirl with \(\omega = \Omega > 0\) are the positive roots \(\Omega = \sqrt{x}\) of \(g(x) = mJ\,x^2 - (k_{\mathrm{T}} J + m k_{\mathrm{R}})\,x + k_{\mathrm{T}} k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = 0\). If \(J > 0\) there are exactly two, one below and one above \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{T}}/m}\). If \(J \le 0\) there is exactly one and it lies below \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}\).

Idea

The sign of \(g\) at three points, \(x = 0\), \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2\) and \(x \to \infty\).

Proof

We must show the number of positive roots of \(g\).

  1. Insert \(\omega = \Omega\) in \(f\) of (24.26): \((k_{\mathrm{T}} - m x)(k_{\mathrm{R}} - Jx) - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = mJx^2 - (k_{\mathrm{T}}J + mk_{\mathrm{R}})x + k_{\mathrm{T}}k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = g(x)\).
  2. At \(x = 0\), \(g(0) = k_{\mathrm{T}}k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 > 0\) (lemma 24.20). At \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2\) the first factor is zero and \(g(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2) = -k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 < 0\), because \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \neq 0\).
  3. If \(J > 0\): \(g\) is a parabola opening upward, \(g(0) > 0\), \(g(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2) < 0\), so one root lies in \((0, \omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2)\) and the other above \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2\).
  4. If \(J < 0\): the product of the roots is \(g(0)/(mJ) < 0\), so one root is positive and one negative. Since \(g(0) > 0 > g(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2)\), the positive one lies in \((0, \omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2)\).
  5. If \(J = 0\): \(g\) is linear, \(-mk_{\mathrm{R}}\,x + k_{\mathrm{T}}k_{\mathrm{R}} - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2 = 0\), and has one positive root, because \(g(0) > 0\) and the coefficient of \(x\) is negative. Still \(g(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2) < 0\), so it lies below \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2\).

Source: FPGL §6.8.1 (PDF p. 295) states that for synchronous forward whirl \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) is replaced by \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\). The proof is ours.

For \(R_2\) we have \(J = -0{,}4799\) kg m\(^2 < 0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} = 316{,}33\) 1/s, that is \(3021\) rev/min. The one critical speed is \(2046\) rev/min, that is \(34{,}10\) Hz, and at \(\Omega = 2046\) rev/min the frequencies are \(-119{,}92\), \(-29{,}16\), \(+34{,}10\) and \(+181{,}78\) Hz, so the lower forward one meets \(\Omega/2\pi = 34{,}10\) Hz. At rest, though, the rotor has two frequencies, \(1906\) and \(8793\) rev/min. The upper one never meets \(\Omega\). This is the check: it is not true that every natural frequency gives a critical speed.

Lemma 24.26 Thin disk (tanek disk)

Let \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \neq 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > J_{\mathrm{t}}\). Then the upper forward curve, \(\omega = r_4(\Omega)\), lies above the line \(\omega = \Omega\) at every \(\Omega \ge 0\). At rest the rotor has two different natural frequencies (two whirl frequencies forward and two backward with the same absolute values), but only one critical speed of forward whirl. For a homogeneous cylinder of radius \(R\) and height \(h\), \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > J_{\mathrm{t}}\) holds exactly when \(h < \sqrt{3}\,R = (\sqrt{3}/2)\,D\).

Idea

The sign of \(f\) on the line \(\omega = \Omega\) tells between which roots \(\Omega\) lies.

Proof

We must show that \(r_4(\Omega) > \Omega\), the claim about rest and the estimate for the cylinder.

  1. By lemma 24.22, \(f(\cdot, \Omega)\) has roots \(r_1 < -\omega_{\mathrm{T}} < r_2 < 0 < r_3 < \omega_{\mathrm{T}} < r_4\) and \(f < 0\) exactly on \((r_1, r_2)\) and \((r_3, r_4)\), positive elsewhere.
  2. If \(\Omega \le \omega_{\mathrm{T}}\), then \(\Omega \le \omega_{\mathrm{T}} < r_4\).
  3. If \(\Omega > \omega_{\mathrm{T}}\), then \(f(\Omega, \Omega) = g(\Omega^2)\). Since \(J < 0\), \(g\) is a parabola opening downward with one positive root \(x_1 < \omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2\) (step 4 of theorem 24.25), so \(g(\Omega^2) < 0\) for \(\Omega^2 > x_1\). Hence \(f(\Omega, \Omega) < 0\), and since \(\Omega > \omega_{\mathrm{T}} > r_3\), \(\Omega\) lies in \((r_3, r_4)\), so \(\Omega < r_4\).
  4. At \(\Omega = 0\), \(f(\omega, 0) = (k_{\mathrm{T}} - m\omega^2)(k_{\mathrm{R}} - J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2) - k_{\mathrm{C}}^2\) is an even function of \(\omega\), so \(r_1 = -r_4\) and \(r_2 = -r_3\). Since \(r_3 < \omega_{\mathrm{T}} < r_4\), the natural frequencies \(r_3\) and \(r_4\) are different. Since \(J = J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}} < 0\), there is only one critical speed by theorem 24.25.
  5. For a cylinder \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = mR^2/2\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = mR^2/4 + mh^2/12\), so \(J_{\mathrm{p}} - J_{\mathrm{t}} = m(R^2/4 - h^2/12) > 0\) exactly when \(h^2 < 3R^2\).

Source: FPGL (PDF p. 133) states \(J_{\mathrm{t}} < J_{\mathrm{p}}\) for \(h < (\sqrt{3}/2)D\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} > J_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). The proof is ours.

For comparison take a cylinder of the same mass \(49{,}009\) kg and length \(0{,}4\) m: the radius is \(70{,}7\) mm, \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}1225\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}7147\) kg m\(^2\), so \(J = 0{,}5922 > 0\) and by theorem 24.25 there are two critical speeds, \(1893\) and \(8138\) rev/min. The disk of rotor \(R_2\) has \(h/D = 0{,}125\), the cylinder \(h/D = 2{,}83\). If \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) (no gyroscopic term), then \(g(\Omega^2) = f(\Omega, 0)\), so the critical speeds equal the natural frequencies at rest, \(1906\) and \(8793\) rev/min.

Idealizations of the section: massless shaft (by section 24.1 the Rayleigh estimate lowers the frequency by \(4{,}53\) %), rigid bearings, small slopes (energy to second order in \(\alpha\), \(\beta\)) and constant \(\Omega\). A rotor with the mass of the shaft is treated in section 24.5. A rigid rotor on flexible bearings has, according to FPGL (PDF p. 133), the same four equations with \(k_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{C}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{R}}\) of the bearings in place of the shaft, and the theorems of this section hold for it unchanged. FPGL states this without proof.

24.4.5 What we have shown

A disk off the middle of the shaft has four coordinates, and the matrix \(\mat K\) from our own derivation (lemma 24.20) is positive definite. Because of the gyroscopic term the natural frequencies depend on \(\Omega\), yet they stay real (theorem 24.21), one in each of four intervals (lemma 24.22). Forward frequencies rise, backward ones fall (lemma 24.24, figure 24.13). Critical speeds are intersections with \(\omega = \Omega\) and there are two for \(J > 0\) and one for \(J \le 0\) (theorem 24.25), so a thin disk gets no second critical speed although it has two frequencies at rest (lemma 24.26). Section 24.5 generalizes the procedure to the matrices of an arbitrary rotor, and section 24.6 says which speeds are really critical.
