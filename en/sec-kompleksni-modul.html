Chapter 20 Damping models

20.6 Complex modulus and loss factor of a material

In section 20.3 we wrote the stiffness for steady vibration as \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) with a constant loss factor \(\gamma\), but that was not a model in time: one root of the characteristic polynomial had a positive real part, so the solution grew in time (lemma 20.18). In sections 20.4 and 20.5 we looked at a material with memory, in which the stress follows from the whole past history of the deformation. Now we join the two routes. What does such a material do when we stretch and compress it steadily with frequency \(\Omega\), as a vibrating structure does? How much energy does it lose in one period, and what does that depend on? Do we really get the constant \(\gamma\) of section 20.3 from its model, and on what band of frequencies does it hold (figure 20.51)?

The model is this. We watch a point of the material in a uniaxial stress state: the stress \(\sigma(t)\) and the strain \(\varepsilon(t)\) are numbers, tied together by the relaxation modulus \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\) of definition 20.20. The material is linearly viscoelastic (definition 20.19), and its relaxation modulus is a Prony series (definition 20.28),

\[E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad E_\infty \ge 0 ,\ E_{\mathrm{j}} > 0 ,\ \tau_{\mathrm{j}} > 0 , \tag{20.23}\]

with finitely many terms. Each term is the relaxation of one branch made of a spring \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\) and a dashpot \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) in series (a Maxwell element, definition 20.22), and \(E_\infty\) is a spring that does not give way. The sum \(E_0 = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(0) = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the initial modulus. The standard linear solid of section 20.4 is the case \(N = 1\) with \(E_\infty > 0\). The symbol \(\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) here is a relaxation time, as in section 20.4, and has nothing to do with shear stress. Linearity is the assumption we need here: twice the strain gives twice the stress. Because of it the number we are looking for will not depend on \(\varepsilon_0\) and the loop will be an ellipse. It holds as long as the material fits definition 20.19, which is at small strains. Where it no longer fits, the loop is not an ellipse and one number per frequency is not enough. We impose the strain from \(t = 0\) on, \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0\cos\Omega t\) for \(t \ge 0\) and \(\varepsilon(t) = 0\) before, with real \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(\Omega > 0\). At \(t = 0\) there is a jump \(\varepsilon_0\), for which the hereditary integral has its own term (theorem 20.30). We will draw the response as a loop of stress against strain (figure 20.47).

We will write the answer with one complex number. The expression for the stress will have two parts: a vibration with frequency \(\Omega\) that does not end, and terms that die out with \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\). The first gets a name (its loop is shown in figure 20.47).

Definition 20.32 Complex modulus (kompleksni modul)

Let a material under the strain \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0\cos\Omega t = \mathrm{Re}(\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) (the loop is shown in figure 20.47), after the transient terms that tend to zero have died out, give the stress

\[\sigma(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\hat E(\Omega)\,\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) \tag{20.24}\]

with a complex number \(\hat E(\Omega)\) that does not depend on \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(t\). The number \(\hat E(\Omega) = E_{\mathrm{shr}}(\Omega) + \mathrm{i}\,E_{\mathrm{izg}}(\Omega)\) is the complex modulus (kompleksni modul) at frequency \(\Omega\), its real part \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) is the storage modulus (shranjevalni modul), its imaginary part \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the loss modulus (izgubni modul), and the quotient

\[\gamma(\Omega) = \frac{E_{\mathrm{izg}}(\Omega)}{E_{\mathrm{shr}}(\Omega)} \qquad (E_{\mathrm{shr}} > 0) \tag{20.25}\]

is the loss factor of the material (faktor izgub materiala). If for \(\varepsilon_0 > 0\) we write the stress as \(\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\cos(\Omega t + \delta)\) with amplitude \(\sigma_0 > 0\) and a phase shift \(\delta\) by which the stress leads the strain, then \(\hat E = (\sigma_0/\varepsilon_0)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\delta}\), so \(\gamma = \tan\delta\).

Explanation

The names have a reason. The modulus is "complex" because it is a number with a real and an imaginary part. The part in phase with the strain, \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\), acts like a spring: the loss in a period does not contain it (lemma 20.34). The part that leads the strain by a quarter of a period, \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\), loses energy. The hat over \(E\) separates the modulus from \(E^*\) of chapter 11, which is the plane-strain modulus, and the primes of the sources (\(E'\), \(E''\)) mean a derivative, so we do not use them. Compare with \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) of section 20.3: there \(\gamma\) is constant, here it is a function of frequency, as the lower curve of figure 20.49 shows.

Source: GR equations (3.32) and (3.33) (PDF p. 181) write \(E_{\mathrm{hysteretic}} = E' + \mathrm{i}E''\) and \(E'(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) with "storage modulus", "loss modulus" and "loss factor" \(E''/E'\). BOWER section 3.6.1.2 (PDF p. 134) introduces the same for the shear modulus, \(G^* = \tau_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\delta}/2\varepsilon_0 = G' + \mathrm{i}G''\) (the factor 2 comes from the tensor shear strain, \(G = \tau/2\varepsilon\) on PDF p. 132). The same page prints \(G' = \tau_0\cos\delta/\varepsilon_0\) and \(G'' = \tau_0\sin\delta/\varepsilon_0\) without the factor 2. This is a misprint, since the real and imaginary parts of \(G^*\) are \(\tau_0\cos\delta/2\varepsilon_0\) and \(\tau_0\sin\delta/2\varepsilon_0\). Here is the uniaxial form, and the symbols \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) are the book's choice.

Does the number of the definition exist, and how large is it? For a Prony series we compute it from the hereditary integral of section 20.5, and we draw the result in figure 20.49.

Theorem 20.33 Complex modulus of a Prony series (kompleksni modul Pronyjeve vrste)

Let a material have the relaxation modulus (20.23) and let \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0\cos\Omega t\) for \(t \ge 0\) and \(0\) before, \(\varepsilon_0, \Omega > 0\). Then for \(t > 0\)

\[\sigma(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\hat E(\Omega)\,\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) + \varepsilon_0\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N}\frac{E_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} , \tag{20.26}\]

where

\[\hat E(\Omega) = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}\,\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} . \tag{20.27}\]

So the material has the complex modulus (20.27) (curves: figure 20.49), and

\[E_{\mathrm{shr}} = E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} E_{\mathrm{j}}\frac{\Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2}{1 + \Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2} , \qquad E_{\mathrm{izg}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N} E_{\mathrm{j}}\frac{\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2} . \tag{20.28}\]
Idea

We write the hereditary integral of section 20.5 for the complex strain \(\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), because the exponential function integrates as in the real case. Each term of the series gives two parts: a vibration with frequency \(\Omega\) and a remainder \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\). We take the real part at the end, and we see the loop it traces in figure 20.47.

Proof

We must show the form (20.26). We write the stress with the hereditary integral, insert the series and integrate each term with the fundamental theorem.

  1. For \(t \ge 0\) we have \(\varepsilon(t) = \mathrm{Re}(\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) and \(\varepsilon(0+) = \varepsilon_0\). The derivative of the complex exponential function is \((\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s})' = \mathrm{i}\Omega\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}\) (theorem 9.34, item 3), so \(\dot\varepsilon(s) = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\Omega\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}) = -\Omega\varepsilon_0\sin\Omega s\) (definition 9.33). By theorem 20.30
    \[\sigma(t) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon_0 + \int_0^t E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\,\dot\varepsilon(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]
  2. The function \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) is real, and the integral of a complex function is the integral of its real part plus \(\mathrm{i}\) times the integral of its imaginary part, so \(\sigma = \mathrm{Re}\,Z\) for
    \[Z(t) = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon_0 + \int_0^t E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t - s)\,\mathrm{i}\Omega\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}\,\mathrm{d}s .\]
  3. After inserting the series (20.23) we have \(Z = Z_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Z_{\mathrm{j}}\). The term with \(E_\infty\) is \(Z_\infty = E_\infty\varepsilon_0\big(1 + \int_0^t\mathrm{i}\Omega\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}\,\mathrm{d}s\big)\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55) and step 1 the integral is \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} - 1\), so \(Z_\infty = E_\infty\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\).
  4. For term \(j\) we have \(Z_{\mathrm{j}} = E_{\mathrm{j}}\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} + E_{\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{i}\Omega\varepsilon_0\,I_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(I_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_0^t\mathrm{e}^{-(t-s)/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}\,\mathrm{d}s\). Let \(\lambda = 1/\tau_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i}\Omega \ne 0\). By theorem 9.34, item 1, the integrand is \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda s}\), and the antiderivative is \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda s}/\lambda\) by item 3. The fundamental theorem gives \(I_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}})/\lambda\), since \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), and \(1/\lambda = \tau_{\mathrm{j}}/(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}})\).
  5. Therefore
    \[Z_{\mathrm{j}} = E_{\mathrm{j}}\varepsilon_0\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\big)\Big] = E_{\mathrm{j}}\varepsilon_0\Big[\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} + \frac{1}{1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big] ,\]
    because \(1 - \dfrac{\mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\).
  6. The sum is \(Z = \hat E\,\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} + \varepsilon_0\sum_{\mathrm{j}} E_{\mathrm{j}}\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}/(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}})\) with \(\hat E\) from (20.27). Since \(1/(1 + \mathrm{i}y) = (1 - \mathrm{i}y)/(1 + y^2)\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\) is real, the real part of the second term equals the term in (20.26). This is the difference between the curves in the figure after the theorem.
  7. For the split of \(\hat E\) we use \(\mathrm{i}y/(1 + \mathrm{i}y) = \mathrm{i}y(1 - \mathrm{i}y)/(1 + y^2) = (y^2 + \mathrm{i}y)/(1 + y^2)\) with \(y = \Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). This gives (20.28). The transient terms are \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}\) and tend to zero (from \(5\tau\) on the figure no longer shows them), and the number \(\hat E\) does not depend on \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(t\), so definition 20.32 holds.
Explanation

The remainder is not small at once. After the time \(5\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) (vertical line) we have \(\mathrm{e}^{-5} = 0{,}0067\), so below \(0{,}7\ \%\) of the original value of term \(j\), and the "steady" state makes sense only from there on. The core of the theorem is that all terms of the forced part, \(E_\infty\) and \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}})\), are multipliers of the same \(\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\). The jump at \(t = 0\) affects only the remainder: at \(t = 0\) we get \(\sigma(0+) = \mathrm{Re}\hat E\varepsilon_0 + \varepsilon_0\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}/(1 + \Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2) = \varepsilon_0(E_\infty + \sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}) = E_0\varepsilon_0\) (orange point), as it must be for a jump of strain (initial modulus \(E_0\)). The loop of the steady part is shown in figure 20.47.

Source: The theorem is proved from the hereditary integral for Prony series, which theorem 20.30 provides. BOWER section 3.6.1.2 (PDF p. 134) only states the complex modulus. The proof is not from the source.

Let us draw the response of the theorem for a standard linear solid with \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) and \(\Omega\tau = 1\). Here \(\hat E = (1{,}5 + 0{,}5\,\mathrm{i})\,E_\infty\), and the remainder is \(0{,}5\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) (figure 20.46).

On the axes are time in units of \(\tau\) and stress in units of \(E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The grey dashed curve is the steady part \(\mathrm{Re}(\hat E\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\), which starts at \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon_0 = 1{,}5\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\) and oscillates with the amplitude \(|\hat E|\varepsilon_0 = 1{,}5811\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The orange curve is the whole stress. It starts at the point \(E_0\varepsilon_0 = 2\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\), \(0{,}5\) above the steady part: this is the remainder \(\varepsilon_0E_1/(1 + \Omega^2\tau^2)\) with \(E_1 = E_\infty\). The difference falls like \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\). At \(t = 5\tau\) it is \(0{,}5\cdot 0{,}0067 = 0{,}0034\), and the curves coincide in the figure. From there on the stress is the one of definition 20.32. The three blackwhite dots are the check of the computation in step 5 of the proof: the hereditary integral, computed by the trapezoidal sum on \(40\,000\) pieces, gives at \(t/\tau = 0{,}5\), \(2\) and \(7\) the values \(1{,}379926\), \(-1{,}011201\) and \(0{,}802816\), and formula (20.26) gives the same to six digits.

What do \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) mean in terms of energy? In section 20.1 we defined the loss factor by the energy lost in a period (definition 20.3). Now we check that \(\gamma\) of (20.25) is the same quantity.

Lemma 20.34 Loss per period and the loss factor of the material (izguba v periodi in faktor izgub materiala)

Let \(\sigma(t) = \mathrm{Re}(\hat E\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) with \(\hat E = E_{\mathrm{shr}} + \mathrm{i}E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) and \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\cos\Omega t\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) (loop: figure 20.47). The work of the stress per unit volume in one period (definition 3.57), that is the energy that turns into heat in one period per unit volume, is

\[w_{\mathrm{izg}} = \int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sigma\,\dot\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}t = \pi\,E_{\mathrm{izg}}\,\varepsilon_0^2 . \tag{20.29}\]

With the stored energy \(U = \tfrac12E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon_0^2\) (the energy of an elastic material with modulus \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) at the largest strain, like \(\tfrac12kX^2\) in definition 20.3) we have \(w_{\mathrm{izg}}/(2\pi U) = \gamma\).

Proof

We must show (20.29) with integrals of sine and cosine over a period.

  1. From \(\hat E\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = (E_{\mathrm{shr}} + \mathrm{i}E_{\mathrm{izg}})(\cos\Omega t + \mathrm{i}\sin\Omega t)\) the real part is \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\cos\Omega t - E_{\mathrm{izg}}\sin\Omega t)\). The derivative of the strain is \(\dot\varepsilon = -\Omega\varepsilon_0\sin\Omega t\) (theorem 8.13). The first term of the stress is the stress on the line, the second is the departure from it, which at \(\varepsilon = 0\) equals \(\pm E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\).
  2. The product is \(\sigma\dot\varepsilon = -\Omega\varepsilon_0^2\big(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\sin\Omega t\cos\Omega t - E_{\mathrm{izg}}\sin^2\Omega t\big)\). By lemma 9.72 (item 3 with \(a = 0\)) the integral of the first term is zero, by item 2 we have \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sin^2\Omega t\,\mathrm{d}t = T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). The first term is the work on the line: what the stress does on the way out to \(\varepsilon_0\), the triangle \(U\), it gets back on the way to zero, so nothing is left after a period. The second term is the area that the loop encloses.
  3. Therefore \(w_{\mathrm{izg}} = \Omega\varepsilon_0^2E_{\mathrm{izg}}T_{\mathrm{p}}/2 = \pi E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0^2\), because \(\Omega T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi\). The quotient with \(2\pi U = \pi E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon_0^2\) is \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}/E_{\mathrm{shr}}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The form \(\gamma = W/(2\pi U)\) is definition 20.3. CK (PDF p. 127) reads the loss of viscous damping per period, \(\pi c\Omega U^2\), as the area of the ellipse of force against displacement, and introduces \(\gamma\) in (4.79) (PDF p. 129).

The loss is therefore proportional to \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\), not to the whole of \(\hat E\): the complex number carries two roles, storage and loss, and each has its own part. This split can be seen in figure 20.47.

We build the figure as at a board. Horizontally we draw the \(\varepsilon\) axis and vertically the \(\sigma\) axis. If the material were elastic with modulus \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\), the point \((\varepsilon, \sigma)\) would move along the line through the origin with slope \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\), since then \(\sigma = E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon\). The strain oscillates between \(-\varepsilon_0\) and \(\varepsilon_0\), which we mark with the distance \(\varepsilon_0\) and the vertical dashed lines where the line reaches the extreme positions. For our material the stress \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\cos\Omega t - E_{\mathrm{izg}}\sin\Omega t)\) is not only \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon\): the added term \(-E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\sin\Omega t = E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\cos(\Omega t + \pi/2)\) leads the strain by a quarter of a period, since it reaches its largest value a quarter of a period before the strain does. So the point does not run along the line but along a loop. Since \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) (lemma 8.11, item 1), we have

\[\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\sigma - E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon}{E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0}\Big)^2 = 1 . \tag{20.30}\]

In the coordinates \(\varepsilon\) and \(\sigma - E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon\) this is an ellipse with semi-axes \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\). In the plane \((\varepsilon, \sigma)\) adding \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon\) tilts it so that it lies along the line. At \(\varepsilon = 0\) we have \(\sigma = \pm E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\): these are the orange points. Each lies \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0 = 0{,}5\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0\) from the line, so there the loop is \(2E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0 = E_\infty\varepsilon_0\) wide. The point travels clockwise, opposite to figure 9.75. That figure shows the force \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c\,\dot q\) with which the dashpot acts on the mass, while here we have the stress with which the surroundings pull on the material. By the law of action and reaction the two have opposite signs: the part of the stress that loses energy is \(-E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0\sin\Omega t = (E_{\mathrm{izg}}/\Omega)\,\dot\varepsilon\), proportional to \(+\dot\varepsilon\) and not to \(-\dot q\). The opposite sign mirrors the loop across the horizontal axis and reverses the direction of travel. The area of this ellipse is \(\pi\cdot\varepsilon_0\cdot E_{\mathrm{izg}}\varepsilon_0 = 1{,}5708\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0^2\) (the map \((\varepsilon, \sigma) \mapsto (\varepsilon, \sigma - E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon)\), which tilts the ellipse, has determinant \(1\) and does not change the area) and it equals the loss (20.29): both routes give the same number. The stored energy is the area of the grey triangle under the line, \(U = \tfrac12\cdot\varepsilon_0\cdot E_{\mathrm{shr}}\varepsilon_0 = 0{,}75\,E_\infty\varepsilon_0^2\), and \(\gamma = 1{,}5708/(2\pi\cdot 0{,}75) = 0{,}3333\).

Why does the loop have a width at all? We look at the branch of the standard linear solid, its Maxwell element with \(E_1 = E_0 - E_\infty\). It has a spring \(E_1\) and a dashpot \(E_1\tau\) in series. By step 5 of the proof the stress of the branch has the amplitude \(E_1\varepsilon_0\,\Omega\tau/\sqrt{1 + \Omega^2\tau^2}\), and the dashpot stretches with a rate whose amplitude is (the stress of the branch divided by its coefficient \(E_1\tau\)) \(\Omega\varepsilon_0/\sqrt{1 + \Omega^2\tau^2}\). The power of the dashpot is force times velocity (definition 8.59), so the dashpot loses much energy only when both are large at once. Figure 20.48 shows both as lengths: the stretch of the spring, which is the stress of the branch divided by \(E_1\), and the stroke of the dashpot, which is its rate divided by \(\Omega\). At \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}1\) the stress of the branch is \(0{,}0995\,E_1\varepsilon_0\) (short bar under the spring) and the rate of the dashpot is \(0{,}995\,\Omega\varepsilon_0\) (long arrow under the dashpot): the dashpot moves with almost the whole rate of the strain, but with almost no force, since the branch has time to give way. At \(\Omega\tau = 10\) the stress of the branch is \(0{,}995\,E_1\varepsilon_0\) (long arrow under the spring) and the rate only \(0{,}0995\,\Omega\varepsilon_0\) (short bar under the dashpot): the force hardly moves the dashpot, so the spring takes almost all the stretch of the branch. In the middle, at \(\Omega\tau = 1\), both are \(0{,}7071\) of their size (spring, dashpot), and the loss is largest.

We read the figure row by row. Each row has the same branch, a spring \(E_1\) and a dashpot \(E_1\tau\) in series. Both carry the same stress, and their stretches add up to the stretch of the branch. The stroke of the dashpot lags the stretch of the spring by a quarter of a period: the rate of the dashpot is proportional to the stress, and the stress to the stretch of the spring, so the dashpot is farthest out when the stress passes through zero. So the amplitudes do not add like numbers but like the legs of a right triangle, \(0{,}0995^2 + 0{,}9950^2 = 1\). On the right we draw this. The sides of the rectangle are the two amplitudes, the diagonal is \(\varepsilon_0\) and its end lies on the arc with radius \(\varepsilon_0\). The loss of the branch in a period is, by lemma 20.34 and equations (20.28), equal to \(\pi E_1\varepsilon_0^2\,\Omega\tau/(1 + \Omega^2\tau^2)\), that is \(\pi E_1\) times the area of the rectangle. In the first row the rectangle is lying and thin with the area \(0{,}099\,\varepsilon_0^2\), in the third it is standing and thin with the same area, in the middle one it is a square with the area \(0{,}5\,\varepsilon_0^2\). Among rectangles with the same diagonal the square has the largest area, because \(ab \le (a^2 + b^2)/2\). So the loss of the branch is largest at \(\Omega\tau = 1\). This is the view by hand and eye of item 3 of corollary 20.36, which follows.

Example 20.35 Complex modulus of the standard solid (kompleksni modul standardnega trdnika)

For a standard linear solid with \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) (a model, not a substance, as in section 20.4, curves in figure 20.49) we have \(N = 1\), \(E_1 = E_0 - E_\infty = E_\infty\) and \(x = \Omega\tau\). From equations (20.28) we get \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}/E_\infty = 1 + x^2/(1 + x^2)\), \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}/E_\infty = x/(1 + x^2)\) and \(\gamma = x/(1 + 2x^2)\). For three frequencies:

\(\Omega\tau\) \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}/E_\infty\) \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}/E_\infty\) \(\gamma\)
\(0{,}1\) \(1{,}0099\) \(0{,}0990\) \(0{,}0980\)
\(1\) \(1{,}5000\) \(0{,}5000\) \(0{,}3333\)
\(10\) \(1{,}9901\) \(0{,}0990\) \(0{,}0498\)

Source: The computation is in num-06.py, SymPy and NumPy. The model is the book's choice.

Slowly (\(\Omega\tau = 0{,}1\)) the material behaves almost like an elastic material with modulus \(E_\infty \approx E_{\mathrm{shr}}\), quickly (\(\Omega\tau = 10\)) again like an elastic material, but with a stiffer modulus \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} \to E_0 = 2E_\infty\). Between the two, \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) climbs from \(E_\infty\) to \(E_0\), as the \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) curve of the next figure shows, and the \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) curve has a hump in it.

We read the figure from top to bottom. The axes are on a logarithmic scale, because the frequencies differ by decades. The upper curve \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) starts at \(1\) and ends at \(2\), in units of \(E_\infty\). Below it is \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\), which is close to zero at both ends. At the bottom is the loss factor \(\gamma\), the quotient of the two, with a peak between the decades, and the three points of the table are marked on it. The dashed curve, the point \(A\) and the blue bars by the peak are explained below, where we need them. This quotient is just what the model of section 20.3 needs. Where is the peak and how high is it?

Corollary 20.36 Peak of the loss factor of the standard solid (vrh faktorja izgub standardnega trdnika)

Let a material have the relaxation modulus \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = E_\infty + (E_0 - E_\infty)\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) with \(E_0 > E_\infty > 0\) and \(x = \Omega\tau\) (curves: figure 20.49). Then:

  1. \(\gamma(\Omega) = \dfrac{E_0 - E_\infty}{E_\infty/x + E_0x}\) (lower curve).
  2. \(\gamma(\Omega) \le \gamma_{\max} = \dfrac{E_0 - E_\infty}{2\sqrt{E_0E_\infty}}\), with equality exactly at \(x = \sqrt{E_\infty/E_0}\) (peak). With the retardation time \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau E_0/E_\infty\) of theorem 20.27 this is \(\Omega = 1/\sqrt{\tau\,\tau_\varepsilon}\).
  3. \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}(\Omega) \le (E_0 - E_\infty)/2\), with equality exactly at \(x = 1\) (peak of the \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) curve).
Proof

We must show three items: the first by computation from theorem 20.33, the second and third by the inequality of the square of a difference.

  1. With \(N = 1\) and \(E_1 = E_0 - E_\infty\) we have by equations (20.28) \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} = E_\infty + E_1x^2/(1 + x^2) = (E_\infty + E_0x^2)/(1 + x^2)\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} = E_1x/(1 + x^2)\). The quotient is \(\gamma = E_1x/(E_\infty + E_0x^2)\), which after dividing by \(x\) gives item 1.
  2. The square of a difference is nonnegative: \(\big(\sqrt{E_0x} - \sqrt{E_\infty/x}\big)^2 \ge 0\), so \(E_0x + E_\infty/x \ge 2\sqrt{E_0E_\infty}\), and equality holds exactly when \(E_0x = E_\infty/x\), that is \(x^2 = E_\infty/E_0\). The denominator of item 1 is thus at least \(2\sqrt{E_0E_\infty}\), which gives the inequality. In the figure this is the peak of the lower curve.
  3. At \(x = \sqrt{E_\infty/E_0}\) we have \(\Omega = (1/\tau)\sqrt{E_\infty/E_0}\) and \(\tau\tau_\varepsilon = \tau^2E_0/E_\infty\), so \(1/\sqrt{\tau\tau_\varepsilon} = \Omega\).
  4. From \((1 - x)^2 \ge 0\) we have \(1 + x^2 \ge 2x\), so \(x/(1 + x^2) \le 1/2\) with equality at \(x = 1\). We multiply by \(E_1\). In the figure this is the peak with the value \(0{,}5\), since there \(E_1 = E_\infty\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The peak at \(x = 1/\sqrt2\) and the value \(0{,}35355\) for \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) are confirmed by SymPy (num-06.py).

For \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) the peak is at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}7071\) with the value \(\gamma_{\max} = 1/(2\sqrt2) = 0{,}35355\) (figure 20.49). Here is also a case that almost succeeds: the peak of the loss modulus is at \(\Omega\tau = 1\) (item 3) with the value \(0{,}5\,E_\infty\), and there \(\gamma = 0{,}3333\), less than \(0{,}35355\). The peaks differ because the storage modulus in the denominator of the quotient between them grows, from \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} = 1{,}3333\,E_\infty\) at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}7071\) to \(1{,}5\,E_\infty\) at \(\Omega\tau = 1\), and the quotient decreases although the numerator still grows a little.

The position of the peak has an explanation in the two times of the standard linear solid. The frequency \(1/\tau\) tells how fast the stress relaxes at constant strain, the frequency \(1/\tau_\varepsilon\) how fast the strain settles at constant stress (section 20.4). For \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) we have \(1/\tau_\varepsilon = 0{,}5/\tau\), and the peak at \(0{,}7071/\tau\) is their geometric mean, since \(\sqrt{1\cdot 0{,}5} = 0{,}7071\). On a logarithmic axis the geometric mean lies halfway: the peak is equally far from \(0{,}5\) and from \(1\).

The standard linear solid therefore has a peak, and above it and below it the losses fall. The Kelvin-Voigt element, which in section 20.4 is the second simple model, is different. Its law is \(\sigma = E\varepsilon + \eta\dot\varepsilon\) with \(\eta = E\tau\) (definition 20.24, where \(\tau = \eta/E\) is called the retardation time \(\tau_\varepsilon\)), without memory, so there are no transient terms and we get the complex modulus directly.

Lemma 20.37 Complex modulus of the Kelvin-Voigt element (kompleksni modul Kelvin-Voigtovega člena)

Let \(\varepsilon(t) = \mathrm{Re}(\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) and \(\sigma = E\varepsilon + E\tau\dot\varepsilon\) with \(E, \tau > 0\) (comparison with the standard linear solid: figure 20.49). Then for every \(t\)

\[\sigma(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(E(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau)\,\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) , \tag{20.31}\]

so \(\hat E = E(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau)\), \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} = E\), \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} = E\Omega\tau\) and \(\gamma = \Omega\tau\).

Proof

We must show only the derivative of the strain.

  1. By theorem 9.34 (item 3) we have \(\dot\varepsilon = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\Omega\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\).
  2. Since the real part is linear, \(\sigma = \mathrm{Re}\big((E + E\tau\,\mathrm{i}\Omega)\varepsilon_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big)\). This is (20.31). From \(E + \mathrm{i}\,E\Omega\tau\) we read \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} = E\), \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} = E\Omega\tau\) and \(\gamma = \Omega\tau\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The law of the element is in definition 20.24.

The loss factor \(\gamma = \Omega\tau\) grows with \(\Omega\) without bound, because the loss modulus \(E\Omega\tau\) grows while the storage modulus \(E\) stays. A comparison with the standard linear solid from example 20.35 shows the difference: at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}1\) the Kelvin-Voigt \(\gamma\) is \(0{,}1\) (standard linear solid \(0{,}0980\)), at \(1\) it is \(1\) (against \(0{,}3333\)), at \(10\) it is \(10\) (against \(0{,}0498\)). The Kelvin-Voigt element does not stiffen under fast stretching, but stays at the same modulus \(E\) and the dashpot holds it back more and more: the dashed curve \(\gamma = \Omega\tau\) exceeds the peak of the standard linear solid already at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}3536\) and at \(\Omega\tau = 1\), where the figure no longer shows it, is three times as high as the curve of the standard linear solid (\(1\) against \(0{,}3333\)).

We can check the same on a dashpot that we know. For cantilever \(A\) (section 9.6) the dashpot \(c = 35{,}4175\) N s/m is parallel to the spring \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, which is a Kelvin-Voigt element with \(\tau = c/k = 5{,}647\cdot 10^{-4}\) s. At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s we get \(\gamma = \Omega\tau = 0{,}0200\), and by lemma 20.4 we have \(\gamma = 2\zeta r = 2\cdot 0{,}01\cdot 1 = 0{,}0200\). The same number from two directions. At \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}02\), at the point \(A\) close to the left edge of the figure, the Kelvin-Voigt element and the standard linear solid have almost the same \(\gamma\) (each \(0{,}0200\)), since there \(\gamma = x/(1 + 2x^2) \approx x\). A Kelvin-Voigt material with the same \(\tau\) in all elements also gives damping proportional to stiffness, \(\mathbf{C} = a_1\mathbf{K}\) of definition 10.62: the coefficient \(a_1\) is the time \(\tau\), and the loss factor \(\gamma = a_1\Omega\) grows with \(\Omega\), as the lemma says.

Does any Prony material have a constant \(\gamma\), as section 20.3 assumes (figure 20.51)? The answer is no, on the whole frequency axis. The reason lies in the two functions of which, by equations (20.28), both moduli are made (figure 20.50).

On the axes we have \(x = \Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) horizontally and the value of the function vertically, both on a logarithmic scale, so that both ends can be seen. The blackwhite curve is \(x/(1 + x^2)\), the share of term \(j\) in \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\), divided by \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\). On the left it hugs the line \(x\), on the right the line \(1/x\), and it is nowhere above them: at \(x = 0{,}01\) and at \(x = 100\) it is \(0{,}01\), and at most it reaches \(\tfrac12\) at \(x = 1\). The blue curve is \(x^2/(1 + x^2)\), the share of term \(j\) in \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} - E_\infty\), divided by \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\): on the left it falls even faster, like \(x^2\), and on the right it stays above the level \(\tfrac12\). The lemma adds these bounds up over the terms.

Lemma 20.38 A Prony series has no constant loss factor (Pronyjeva vrsta nima stalnega faktorja izgub)

Let a material have the relaxation modulus (20.23) (curves: figure 20.51) with finite \(N \ge 1\), \(E_{\mathrm{j}}, \tau_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\), \(\tau_{\min} = \min_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\tau_{\max} = \max_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). Then:

  1. If \(E_\infty > 0\), then \(\gamma(\Omega) \le \Omega\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/E_\infty\) for every \(\Omega > 0\), and for \(\Omega \ge 1/\tau_{\min}\) we have \(\gamma(\Omega) \le \dfrac{\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}}{\Omega\,(E_\infty + \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}})}\). So \(\gamma \to 0\) as \(\Omega \to 0\) and as \(\Omega \to \infty\), as figure 20.51 shows left and right of the band.
  2. If \(E_\infty = 0\) (a liquid), then for \(0 < \Omega \le 1/\tau_{\max}\) we have \(\gamma(\Omega) \ge 1/(2\Omega\tau_{\max})\), so \(\gamma \to \infty\) as \(\Omega \to 0\).

In neither case is \(\gamma\) constant on \((0, \infty)\) (figure 20.51).

Idea

For each term, \(x/(1 + x^2)\) is at most \(x\) and at most \(1/x\) (blackwhite curve under the grey lines), which is small for small and for large \(x\) respectively. So the loss modulus is small at both ends of the frequency axis, while the storage modulus stays at least \(E_\infty\). The result is seen at the edges of figure 20.51.

Proof

We must show the estimates from theorem 20.33 (equations (20.28)) and the conclusion that \(\gamma\) is not constant. Let \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = \Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\).

  1. Since \(1 + x^2 \ge 1\) and \(1 + x^2 \ge x^2\), we have \(x/(1 + x^2) \le x\) (left line) and \(x/(1 + x^2) \le 1/x\) (right line). Therefore \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} \le \Omega\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} \le (1/\Omega)\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}/\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. For \(E_\infty > 0\) we have \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} \ge E_\infty\), so \(\gamma \le \Omega\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}/E_\infty\).
  3. For \(\Omega \ge 1/\tau_{\min}\) every \(x_{\mathrm{j}} \ge 1\), so \(x_{\mathrm{j}}^2/(1 + x_{\mathrm{j}}^2) \ge \tfrac12\) (blue curve above the level \(\tfrac12\)) and \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} \ge E_\infty + \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\). With the estimate of step 1 the second inequality of item 1 follows.
  4. For \(E_\infty = 0\) and \(\Omega \le 1/\tau_{\max}\) every \(x_{\mathrm{j}} \le 1\), so \(1 + x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le 2\) and \(x_{\mathrm{j}}/(1 + x_{\mathrm{j}}^2) \ge x_{\mathrm{j}}/2\) (left of \(x = 1\) the blackwhite curve does not fall below half of the line \(x\)). Therefore \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} \ge \tfrac12\Omega\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). At the same time \(x_{\mathrm{j}}^2/(1 + x_{\mathrm{j}}^2) \le x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \Omega^2\tau_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \Omega^2\tau_{\max}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(E_{\mathrm{shr}} \le \Omega^2\tau_{\max}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}E_{\mathrm{j}}\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\). The quotient is at least \(1/(2\Omega\tau_{\max})\).
  5. Since \(N \ge 1\) and \(E_{\mathrm{j}}, \tau_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\), both \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) are positive for every \(\Omega > 0\), so \(\gamma(\Omega) > 0\). A constant \(\gamma = c\) would have \(c > 0\), but by item 1 \(\gamma \to 0\) and by item 2 \(\gamma \to \infty\). Therefore in neither case is \(\gamma\) constant.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The lemma says (figure 20.51) that the constant \(\gamma\) of section 20.3 is not a property of any Prony material on the whole frequency axis: at slow motion the material (since it has the firm spring \(E_\infty\)) is almost elastic, at fast motion just as much, and a liquid has more and more loss at slow motion. But it is possible to approximate a constant \(\gamma\) on a band of frequencies. We take \(N\) terms with times \(\tau_{\mathrm{j}}\) spread evenly on a logarithmic scale from \(1\) to \(0{,}01\) (for \(N = 1\) only \(\tau_1 = 0{,}1\), time in an arbitrary unit, \(\Omega\) in its inverse), \(E_\infty = 1\), and look for weights \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\) such that \(\gamma = 0{,}1\) on the band \(1 \le \Omega \le 100\). The condition \(E_{\mathrm{izg}} = 0{,}1\,E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) is by equations (20.28) linear in \(E_{\mathrm{j}}\),

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N}E_{\mathrm{j}}\,\frac{x_{\mathrm{j}} - 0{,}1\,x_{\mathrm{j}}^2}{1 + x_{\mathrm{j}}^2} = 0{,}1\,E_\infty , \qquad x_{\mathrm{j}} = \Omega\tau_{\mathrm{j}} ,\]

which we require at 400 points spread evenly on a logarithmic scale and solve by the method of least squares.

In the figure there are the axes, \(\Omega\) horizontally and \(\gamma\) vertically, and the band with the target \(\gamma = 0{,}1\). Above them is the smaller graph of the storage modulus, to which we return in the next paragraph. The curve for \(N = 1\), so the standard linear solid, has a peak of \(0{,}1345\) in the band and \(0{,}0233\) at its edge, that is \(+35\ \%\) and \(-77\ \%\) relative to the target. Three terms follow the target in the band between \(0{,}0952\) and \(0{,}1047\), that is \(-4{,}8\ \%\) and \(+4{,}7\ \%\), and five terms between \(0{,}0952\) and \(0{,}1018\), \(-4{,}8\ \%\) and \(+1{,}8\ \%\). All weights are positive (for \(N = 3\): \(E_{\mathrm{j}}/E_\infty = 0{,}1847\), \(0{,}1792\) and \(0{,}2574\), for \(N = 5\) at least \(0{,}0552\)), so this is a real material (lemma 20.38 has the condition \(E_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\)). Outside the band all three curves fall, as the lemma requires: for \(N = 3\) we have \(\gamma(0{,}1) = 0{,}0203\) and \(\gamma(1000) = 0{,}0170\), that is a fifth and a sixth of the target. The bounds of the lemma hold: at \(\Omega = 0{,}01\) the upper bound of item 1 is \(0{,}00205\) and \(\gamma = 0{,}00205\) (the bound is almost reached, because all \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) are small), at \(\Omega = 10^4\) the bound is \(0{,}00211\) and \(\gamma = 0{,}00171\).

The band is therefore an approximation and not an equality, and its price has two sides. The first is the storage modulus: for \(N = 3\) we have \(E_0/E_\infty = 1{,}621\) and \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) is not constant on the band, but grows from \(1{,}094\,E_\infty\) to \(1{,}491\,E_\infty\), so by \(36\ \%\). The model \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) of section 20.3 has both constant, which is the second idealization that the band brings with it. The other side is a gain: the stress of a Prony material at time \(t\) depends, by the hereditary integral of section 20.5, only on the strain at times \(s \le t\), so it does not have the problem that hysteretic damping had in time (lemma 20.18). A constant \(\gamma\) therefore makes sense as a description of the frequency response on the band in which the structure moves, and not as a law of the substance.

In section 20.7 we will insert \(\hat E(\Omega)\), that is \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) on the band, into the stiffness matrices of elements. There \(\gamma\) will no longer be the number of one substance, but a property of each element.
