Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.2 Internal forces in a beam (notranje sile v nosilcu)

In chapter 2 we cut a beam clamped in a wall in thought (figure 2.11). One part of the beam acts on the other across the cut with forces that are distributed over the whole cross section and have a longitudinal and a vertical part. The chapter promised that we would describe them with three numbers and define them exactly in this chapter. We do this now. There are two questions. What are these numbers and how do we compute them from the load? How do they change along the beam? The answer to the first says exactly what we are describing, the answer to the second says how to find these quantities in all cross sections at once, without cutting the beam anew for every cross section.

The beam lies along the axis \(x\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\), the axes are as for the two beams above (section 5.1). The cross section is a rectangle with height \(h\) in the direction \(y\) and width \(b\) in the direction \(z\), its centre (središče) lies on the axis of the beam. The centre of a rectangle is the intersection of its diagonals. We compute an integral over a cross section as an iterated integral over a rectangle (definition 4.8), so we state the lemma on internal forces for a rectangular cross section. The mathematics section on the cross section, which follows in this chapter, extends the integral to cross sections made of rectangles, such as the letter T and the double T. There we shall see that the centre of a rectangle is also the mean position of its points, and that for composite cross sections this mean position replaces the centre. The lemma holds for such cross sections too, as corollary 5.19 in section 5.4 shows. All loads lie in the plane \(xy\), which is a plane of symmetry of the cross section: it divides the cross section into two mirror halves, one at \(z > 0\) and the other at \(z < 0\). The displacements are small compared with the lengths, so we write equilibrium on the shape of the beam before loading, as in chapter 4. Why we may, how large the error is and when we must not compute this way, we show with a figure and the numbers of the cantilever in section 5.2.2, where we first use the assumption.

From history

Galileo considered how a bar built into a wall breaks at the wall, as we saw in chapter 1 (figure 1.14). He then asked what happens when the bar is not built in but lies on two supports and the force acts at any point between them (TIMO, PDF p. 22). The upper part of figure 5.19 shows this.

The bar lies on two supports, the force \(F\) acts at the distance \(a\) from the left and \(b\) from the right support. Galileo found that the effect of the force on breaking is greatest under the force and proportional to the product \(a b\) (TIMO, PDF p. 22). We can see the reason from equilibrium. For a support spacing \(a + b\) the left support carries the force \(F b/(a + b)\), and its moment about the point under the force is this force times the lever arm \(a\), \(F a b/(a + b)\). The spacing does not change, so this moment is proportional to \(a b\). The product of two numbers with a given sum is largest when the numbers are equal, since \((a + b)^2/4 - a b = \big((a - b)/2\big)^2 \ge 0\). In the figure \(a b\) is the area of the rectangle with sides \(a\) and \(b\), and \((a + b)^2/4\) is the area of the square with side \((a + b)/2\), which has the same perimeter. The strip of the rectangle outside the square and the strip of the square above the rectangle are equal, both measure \(b \cdot (a - b)/2\). The square is left with the small square with side \((a - b)/2\), by that much the square is larger, and the small square vanishes only at \(a = b\). The smallest force that breaks the bar is therefore the one acting at the middle between the supports (TIMO, PDF p. 22).

Because the effect of the force on breaking is smaller near the supports than at the middle, Galileo noticed that material can be saved if the cross section is made smaller toward the supports (TIMO, PDF p. 22). For a built-in bar of rectangular cross section he also derived the shape in which the strength is the same everywhere. For a force at the free end, the effect of the force on breaking grows with the distance \(d\) from the free end, and the resistance of a cross section grows with the square of its depth \(h\). The depth must therefore grow with the square root of the distance, \(h^2\) must be proportional to \(d\), which gives the shape of a parabola (TIMO, PDF p. 22). So the effect of the force on breaking is not the same in all cross sections of the bar, and we must know it in each cross section separately.

Let us return to the question of the section: which forces and moments act across one cross section of a beam, and how they change from cross section to cross section along the beam.

5.2.1 Three numbers in a cross section (tri števila v prerezu)

We cut the beam at \(x\) and look at the face of the cut that belongs to the part on the side of smaller \(x\). Its outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on this part across the face, and per unit area at a point \(Q\) of the face acts the traction vector (definition 2.20)

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\]

with the components of stress from definition 2.27. The components are functions of the point \(Q\) on the face and also of the place \(x\). We chose the same side for the axial force (definition 2.5), so we need not remember anything new: the force that we describe is always the force of the part on the side of larger \(x\) on the part on the side of smaller \(x\).

Besides the resultant (rezultanta) of these forces, that is the internal force (definition 2.4), we are interested in how strongly the forces try to rotate the part. The resultant does not tell this. A pulling force \(N\) at the centre of the cross section \(C\) only pulls the part. The same force at the upper edge of the part has about \(C\) the lever arm \(h/2\) and also turns the part, like a door that we push far from its hinges. So we add to the resultant the moment of the distributed forces. We compute it about the centre of the cross section \(C(x) = (x, 0, 0)\). The moment of a force depends on the point about which we compute it, because the resultant is in general different from zero (lemma 2.39 holds only for a zero resultant). The centre of the cross section lies on the axis of the beam. We shall need this when we move the moment from one cross section to another.

Definition 5.7 Shear force (prečna sila)

Let \(\vek F(x)\) be the internal force (definition 2.4) in the cross section of the beam at \(x\), that is the resultant of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across the cross section on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). Let us write it by components, \(\vek F(x) = (N,\ V_{\mathrm{y}},\ V_{\mathrm{z}})\). We called the first component \(N\) the axial force (definition 2.5). The shear forces (prečni sili) are the components \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\), perpendicular to the axis of the beam. In a plane problem, where all loads lie in the plane \(xy\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and we denote the shear force \(V := V_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309), SADD app. D (PDF pp. 576-577). All three know the shear force only in the plane. EP and CM have the same sign as we do, SADD the opposite one (see the paragraph on the books below). The vector form is ours.

Definition 5.8 Bending moment (upogibni moment)

Let \(\vek M(x)\) be the internal moment (notranji moment) in the cross section of the beam at \(x\), that is the moment about the centre of the cross section \(C(x)\) of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across the cross section on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). As in lemma 2.40, it is the sum of the moments of the small forces \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A\) over the pieces of the face, as the pieces are made smaller. Let us write it by components, \(\vek M(x) = (M_{\mathrm{x}},\ M_{\mathrm{y}},\ M_{\mathrm{z}})\). The components \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the bending moments (upogibna momenta), since they bend the beam about the axes \(y\) and \(z\). The component \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the moment about the axis of the beam, the torque (torzijski moment), which twists the beam, and chapter 16 deals with it. In a plane problem \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and we denote the bending moment \(M := M_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309), SADD app. D (PDF pp. 576-577). All three know only the component about the axis perpendicular to the plane of bending. EP and CM have the opposite sign to ours, SADD the same one (see the paragraph on the books below). The vector form is ours.

The definitions are written with a resultant and a moment, while the stresses are distributed over the cross section. Which stresses make up \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\)? The answer is in lemma 5.9. The cross section is a rectangle, so the integral over the area is the integral over a rectangle from definition 4.8. The lemma and its proof are accompanied by figure 5.21, in which the cross section \(A\) is drawn three times, seen from the side of larger \(x\).

The lemma needs a hypothesis on the forces across small pieces of the cross section. The integral adds the forces over the pieces of a grid. Definition 2.20, however, speaks only of squares centred at the point \(P\), while the piece \(R\) of the grid only contains the point \(P\) and has its centre elsewhere. The hypothesis (H1) below therefore requires that the average force per unit area tends to the traction vector on such pieces too.

Lemma 5.9 Internal forces from stresses (notranje sile iz napetosti)

Let the cross section of the beam at \(x\) be the rectangle \(A = \{(x, y, z) : -h/2 \le y \le h/2,\ -b/2 \le z \le b/2\}\) with centre \(C = (x, 0, 0)\), and let the components of stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be continuous functions on \(A\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\). For a rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) with sides parallel to those of \(A\), let \(\vek F(R)\) be the force and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R)\) the moment about a point \(P\) of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across \(R\) on the part on the side of smaller \(x\), and \(|R|\) the area. We assume:

  • (H1) For every point \(P \in A\) and every rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) of this kind that contains \(P\), \(|\vek F(R)/|R| - \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})(P)| \to 0\) when the diameter of \(R\) tends to 0. The average force per unit area on a small patch tends to the traction vector, as in theorem 2.26.
  • (H2) For the same \(P\) and \(R\), \(\vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R)/|R| \to 0\). The forces across a small patch act at the patch, so their moment about a point of the patch is by point 2 of lemma 2.40 at most force density times area times diameter, which means there are no couple stresses. CM (PDF pp. 198-199) excludes body couples, a related assumption about volume.
  • Forces and moments add up: if \(R\) is split into rectangles \(R_1, \dots, R_{\mathrm{m}}\), then \(\vek F(R) = \sum \vek F(R_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}(R) = \sum \vek M_{\mathrm{O}}(R_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every point \(O\), since this is the resultant and the moment of the forces on the pieces (definitions 2.2 and 2.37).

Then the stresses give the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the moment \(M\):

\[N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad V_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad V_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.4}\]
\[\begin{aligned} M_{\mathrm{x}} &= \int_{\mathrm{A}} (y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\,\mathrm{d}A , \\ M_{\mathrm{y}} &= \int_{\mathrm{A}} z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \\ M_{\mathrm{z}} &= -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A . \end{aligned} \tag{5.5}\]

So in a plane problem \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Proof

A limit at each point says nothing about how fast it is approached, so we cannot assemble the result from pieces by summing errors. Bisection finds the point where the claim would fail, and there we reach a contradiction with (H1). We work with one component at a time.

  1. For a component \(k\) and a rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) let \(G(R) = F_{\mathrm{k}}(R) - \int_{\mathrm{R}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) with \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}))_{\mathrm{k}}\). If \(R\) is split into rectangles \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\), then \(G(R) = \sum G(R_{\mathrm{i}})\), since \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\) is additive and the integral is additive by point 4 of lemma 4.32 (area instead of volume).
  2. Suppose \(|G(R_0)| = \varepsilon\,|R_0|\) with \(\varepsilon > 0\) for some rectangle \(R_0\), \(R_0 \subseteq A\). We halve both sides into four congruent rectangles. By step 1 at least one of them, \(R_1\), has \(|G(R_1)| \ge \varepsilon\,|R_1|\), otherwise the sum of the four absolute values would be less than \(\varepsilon\,|R_0|\). We repeat and get nested rectangles \(R_0 \supseteq R_1 \supseteq R_2 \supseteq \dots\) with \(|G(R_{\mathrm{n}})| \ge \varepsilon\,|R_{\mathrm{n}}|\), whose sides are halved each time.
  3. The coordinates \((y_{\mathrm{n}}, z_{\mathrm{n}})\) of the corner of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) with the smallest coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) (in the figure the lower right one, because the axis \(z\) points to the left) form non-decreasing sequences bounded by \(h/2\) and \(b/2\), so by theorem 1.52 they converge to a point \(P\). For \(m \ge n\) the corner of \(R_{\mathrm{m}}\) lies in \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(P\) lies in the closed rectangle \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\), hence in all of them. The sides of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) are \(2^{-n}\) times those of \(R_0\). Since \(2^n \ge n\), we have \(2^{-n} \le 1/n\), so the diameter of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) tends to 0 (lemma 1.40).
  4. By (H1), as \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) shrinks to the point \(P\), \(F_{\mathrm{k}}(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\). Since \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous at \(P\), point 3 of lemma 4.32 gives \(\big|\int_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}}(t_{\mathrm{k}} - t_{\mathrm{k}}(P))\,\mathrm{d}A\big| \le \max_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}}|t_{\mathrm{k}} - t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)|\,|R_{\mathrm{n}}|\), so \(\frac{1}{|R_{\mathrm{n}}|}\int_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A \to t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\). Hence \(G(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to 0\), which contradicts \(|G(R_{\mathrm{n}})|/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \ge \varepsilon\). So \(F_{\mathrm{k}}(R) = \int_{\mathrm{R}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) for every rectangle \(R \subseteq A\), and for \(R = A\) this is (5.4), with \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\).
  5. The vector from \(C\) to \(Q = (x, y, z)\) is \(\vek r = (0, y, z)\) and
    \[\vek r\times\vek t = (y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \ z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - 0\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \ 0\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})\]
    by definition 2.37 and equation (2.39). Each component is continuous on \(A\), since it is a sum of products of continuous functions with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\).
  6. For a component \(k\) let now \(G(R) = M_{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{k}}(R) - \int_{\mathrm{R}} (\vek r\times\vek t)_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). It is additive as in step 1, and steps 2 and 3 repeat unchanged. For \(R_{\mathrm{n}} \ni P\) (the point \(P\) in \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\)) we have \(\vek M_{\mathrm{C}}(R_{\mathrm{n}}) = \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek F(R_{\mathrm{n}}) + \vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R_{\mathrm{n}})\), since the vectors from \(C\) to the points of the forces are the sum of the vector \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) from \(C\) to \(P\) and the vector from \(P\) to those points, which is at most as long as the diameter of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\), and point 2 of lemma 2.34 applies. By step 4, (H2) and the continuity of \(\vek r\times\vek t\) at \(P\), \(G(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek t(P) + \vek 0 - \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek t(P) = 0\) (component \(k\)), again a contradiction. Hence \(\vek M(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \vek r\times\vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\), which by components is (5.5).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74) and CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309) give the same integral for \(N\) and \(V\) and for \(M\) with the opposite sign, SADD (PDF p. 576) has \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}A\) like us. The resultant force on a surface as the integral of the traction vector: BOWER (PDF p. 66), the moment of the surface forces as \(\int \vek y\times\vek T\,\mathrm{d}A\): BOWER (PDF p. 78). The absence of body couples, which gives a symmetric stress tensor: CM (4.5.6)-(4.5.9) (PDF pp. 198-199). The hypothesis (H2) on the limit of the moment across a small patch is not from a source. CM does not mention couple stresses, it puts only the traction vector on a surface. The proof is not from a source.

Let us test the lemma on a stress that in the cross section \(b = 20\) mm, \(h = 40\) mm is linear in the height, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y\), with no shear stresses. In the lower part of the figure is the part on the side of smaller \(x\) with the centre \(C\) of the face, the axis \(x\) points right, \(y\) up. For \(k > 0\) the upper part of the cross section (\(y > 0\)) is in tension, the lower in compression. By the lemma \(N = k\int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2}\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z = 0\), since the contributions of the upper and of the lower part cancel. In the moment they do not cancel. The strip at the height \(y\) carries the force \(k\,y \cdot b\,\Delta y\) on the lever arm \(y\), so \(y\) appears twice, once in the force and once in the lever arm. Below the centre the force and the lever arm both have the opposite sign, so all strips turn the part clockwise. The lemma writes this with the integral of \(y^2\):

\[M_{\mathrm{z}} = -k\,b\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = -k\,\frac{b\,h^3}{12} , \qquad \frac{b\,h^3}{12} = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4 .\]

For \(k = 4{,}6875\cdot 10^{9}\) Pa/m the stress at the upper edge, where \(y = 0{,}02\) m, equals \(93{,}75\) MPa, and \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = -500\) N m. So a stretched upper edge means a negative moment. A positive bending moment means the opposite, the lower edge is stretched (figure 5.25 below). The cantilever that we load at the free end with a force \(500\) N downward has exactly the moment \(-500\) N m at the clamp, as equilibrium shows in the next section. That the stress is really linear in \(y\) is shown by the section on stresses in bending, here we only tested the lemma.

The plane problem now gets its justification. Why three of the six numbers remain is shown by the cross section in figure 5.22.

We look at the cross section from the side of larger \(x\), the axis \(x\) points toward the reader, \(y\) up and \(z\) to the left. Let us assume that the loads, the supports and the material are mirror symmetric about the plane of symmetry of the cross section \(z = 0\), and that the picture of the stresses is mirror symmetric about the plane \(xy\) as well. Take the mirror points \(Q\) and \(Q'\), the first at \(z\) and the second at \(-z\). The normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) have the same value at both, because mirroring across the plane \(z = 0\) does not change the directions \(x\) and \(y\). The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which points in the direction \(z\), has the opposite one, because mirroring reverses the direction \(z\). In the lemma the integrands for \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) are therefore odd functions of \(z\) (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\)): the contributions of the points \(Q\) and \(Q'\) cancel, so their integral over \(z \in [-b/2, b/2]\) is zero. What remains is

\[N(x), \qquad V(x), \qquad M(x) ,\]

the axial force, the shear force and the bending moment, the three numbers that chapter 2 announced (figure 5.23 below). Each is a function of the place \(x\) with one number as input and one as output. We also call them stress resultants (rezultante napetosti), because we get them from the stresses. The symmetry of the stresses is an assumption that we shall check when we know the stresses. The zeros \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = M_{\mathrm{x}} = M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) also follow from equilibrium of the cut-off part, whenever all loads and reactions lie in the plane \(xy\). The cut-off part is at rest, so the forces on it in the direction \(z\) add up to zero, and without an external force in the direction \(z\) we get \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). A force in the plane \(xy\) also has no moment about the axes \(x\) and \(y\) about the centre of the cross section, which lies in the same plane, so \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) are zero.

When does the assumption fail? When the load does not lie in the plane of symmetry, or the cross section has no plane of symmetry at all. A force of \(500\) N down at the upper corner of the cross section, \(b/2 = 10\) mm away from the plane, has about the axis of the beam through \(C\) the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}01\ \text{m} \cdot 500\ \text{N} = 5\) N m, which twists the beam. This is 1 % of the bending moment \(500\) N m at the clamp of the cantilever, but the plane model does not see it. Chapter 16 deals with twisting.

In the figure, on the left, is the part of the beam on the side of smaller \(x\), and the axes point \(x\) horizontally and \(y\) up. On its right face acts the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), drawn at five points. On the right is the same part, and the stresses are equivalently replaced by the resultant at the centre \(C\), that is the axial force \(N\) along the axis, the shear force \(V\) vertically and the moment \(M\) about \(C\).

The books write these numbers differently (figure 5.24). EP has the axis \(z\) up and CM the axis \(y\) up, and they define \(M\) as \(\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) respectively (EP (2.5.6), p. 74, CM (7.3.28), PDF p. 309). Their \(M\) is therefore \(-M_{\mathrm{z}}\) by our definitions, and their shear force equals \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\) (in EP with the symbol \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) for the vertical shear stress). CM has in (7.3.26) and (7.3.27) text about the direction \(z\) and the axis \(y\), although (7.3.28) uses \(y\) for the vertical direction: the symbols are not consistent (PDF p. 309). SADD has the same \(M\) as we do, and its shear force is \(-V_{\mathrm{y}}\) (SADD (D.5), (D.6), (D.8), PDF pp. 576-578). For every borrowed equation we therefore derive the signs from our definitions and do not copy them from the book. Why such a choice? We describe all three quantities on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), like the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xj}}\) in chapter 2 and the axial force, and all other signs we get by computation.

5.2.2 Internal forces from equilibrium (notranje sile iz ravnovesja)

How do we compute these forces if we do not know the stresses? From equilibrium. If a beam is in equilibrium, so is each of its parts (section 2.1), also when we cut it off in thought. Why? The cut-off part is at rest, and on a body at rest, by Newton's laws, neither a net force nor a net moment acts. On the cut-off part act the external loads and the forces of the cross section that we are looking for. We show the procedure right on the cantilever of figure 5.26.

  1. We cut the beam at \(x\) and choose the part on the side of larger \(x\), from the cut to the end \(x = L\).
  2. We draw all forces and moments on it. On the face of the cut the part on the side of smaller \(x\) acts on our part. The face has the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so by lemma 2.21 the force on it is \(-\vek F(x)\), and the moment about the centre \(C(x)\) is \(-\vek M(x)\) (each vector \(\vek r\times\vek t\) changes sign with \(\vek t\)).
  3. We write equilibrium of forces (2.2) and of moments (2.45). We compute moments about the centre of the cross section \(C(x)\). The force of the cross section acts at \(C(x)\), so its lever arm about it is zero and it drops out of the equation of moments. One unknown is left in the equation, the internal moment.

Why do we choose the part on the side of larger \(x\)? For a cantilever clamped at \(x = 0\) the end at \(x = L\) is free and has no support. The cut-off part therefore carries only known loads and we do not need the reactions of the clamp.

Cantilever. A cantilever of length \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), loaded at the free end \(x = L\) with a force \(500\) N downward, so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (0, -500, 0)\) N in the axes \(x\) and \(y\) (section 5.1.3). The cut is at the distance \(x\) from the clamp, the part from the cut to the end has length \(L - x\). On it act the force of the cross section \(-\vek F(x) = (-N, -V, 0)\) and the force at the end \((0, -500, 0)\) N.

  1. Equilibrium of forces (2.2), component \(x\): no external force on the cut-off part has a horizontal part, so \(-N = 0\), so \(N = 0\).
  2. Component \(y\): the force of the cross section \(-V\) and the force at the end must cancel, \(-V - 500 = 0\), so \(V = -500\) N. This holds in every cross section, since there is no other force between the cut and the end.
  3. The moment of the force at the end about \(C(x)\): the vector from \(C(x)\) to the point of application is \((L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), this is the lever arm \(L - x\), and by (2.39) it is \((L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times(-500\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = -500\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) N m. The moment of the cross section is \(-\vek M(x) = (0, 0, -M)\).
  4. Equilibrium of moments (2.45), component \(z\): the moment of the section and the moment of the end force with the lever arm \(L - x\) cancel, \(-M - 500\,(L - x) = 0\), so
    \[M(x) = -500\,(L - x)\ \text{N m} = -F\,(L - x) . \tag{5.6}\]

Let us draw both functions under the beam with the clamp and the force at the end, positive values upward. In the axes of the diagram of \(V\) the shear force is a horizontal line at \(-500\) N. In the axes of the diagram of \(M\) the moment is a straight line from \(M(0) = -500\) N m at the clamp to \(M(L) = 0\) at the free end \(L\). The shear force is negative because the part at the wall must hold the cut-off part up: the force of the cross section on it is \(-V = +500\) N, as figure 2.11 already shows.

The hand says the same. We hold a stick horizontally, with a bucket hanging on its other end. The hand is to the stick what the part at the wall is to the cut-off part. It must carry the stick, this is the shear force, and at the same time twist it upward so that it does not droop, this is the moment. We carry the same weight wherever we grip the stick, so \(V\) is constant. The twist, however, grows with the lever arm \(L - x\) to the bucket: at the lever arm \(0{,}5\) m it is \(M = -500\cdot 0{,}5 = -250\) N m, at the clamp, at the lever arm \(1\) m, twice as much. This view gives the shape of both diagrams without computing.

What the negative moment does to the beam is shown in figure 5.28, in which the deflection is strongly exaggerated.

We draw the unloaded cantilever grey and dashed, the loaded one in orange over it. The force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\) pushes the free end down, while the wall holds the other end horizontal. The beam therefore bends so that at the wall the upper fibres stretch and the lower ones shorten. A stretched upper edge is the mark of the negative moment from the test of the stress above. The stress with \(93{,}75\) MPa at the upper edge from the test therefore has exactly the moment \(-500\) N m that the cantilever carries at the clamp.

Assumption: we write equilibrium on the unbent beam. In step 3 we took the lever arm of the force from the unloaded beam, at the clamp this is the whole length \(L\). But the force acts on the bent beam. Its end drops and thereby moves a little towards the wall. The axis does not get longer in bending (section 5.5.2), and a tilted piece of the same length reaches less far horizontally than a straight one. The true lever arm is therefore shorter than \(L\).

  • Why may we leave the difference out here? In our cantilever the end drops by \(7{,}97\) mm (section 5.9.2). Let us take a short piece of the axis of length \(\Delta s\), straight and tilted with the slope \(v'\). The tilted piece is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the horizontal leg \(\Delta x\) and the vertical one \(v'\,\Delta x\). By Pythagoras' theorem \(\Delta s^2 = (1 + v'^2)\,\Delta x^2\), so \(\Delta x = \Delta s/\sqrt{1 + v'^2} \approx (1 - v'^2/2)\,\Delta s\) for a small \(v'\). The tilted piece covers horizontally a share \(v'^2/2\) less than the straight one, and at the largest slope \(0{,}012\) this is \(0{,}007\) %. The sum along the whole length gives a shift of \(0{,}038\) mm, which is \(0{,}0038\) % of the length.
  • What does this do to the result? With the lever arm \(L\) we get \(500\) N m, with the truly shorter arm \(499{,}98\) N m, the difference is \(0{,}019\) N m.
  • When does the assumption fail? In a thin ruler that we bend so that the end drops by a third of the length, the lever arm is shorter by about \(7\) % already, and the moment at the clamp is smaller by as much. And when we also compress the beam along its axis (right part of the figure), the axial force gets a lever arm on the deflection and adds a moment that the computation on the straight beam does not see.

To the end of the chapter we write equilibrium on the shape before loading (all assumptions are collected in section 5.15.1).

Simply supported beam with a force at the middle. A beam of length \(L = 2\) m lies on a fixed pin support at \(x = 0\) and on a roller support at \(x = L\) (definition 5.5, section 5.1.2). At the middle acts a force \(1\) kN downward, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (0, -1000, 0)\) N at \(x_0 = 1\) m, both distances are measured from the left support. There are no horizontal loads, so by equilibrium of forces in the direction \(x\) the horizontal reaction of the fixed pin support is zero. The reactions are vertical, \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\) at \(x = 0\) and \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) at \(x = L\), each in the direction \(+y\). From equilibrium of the whole beam, moments about \(x = 0\) (component \(z\)): \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\,L - 1000\cdot 1 = 0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 500\) N, and from the forces \(R_{\mathrm{A}} + R_{\mathrm{B}} - 1000 = 0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{A}} = 500\) N. We choose the point \(x = 0\) because \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\) has zero lever arm about it and only one unknown is left in the equation. The halves are what we expect: the force stands at the middle, so the supports share it equally. A reaction is an external force on the beam, like the force at the end of the cantilever.

The internal forces have different forms to the left and to the right of the force at the middle, since the cut-off part from the cut to the end contains the force at the middle only for \(x < 1\) m. So we cut twice, to the left of the force and to the right of it.

  • A cut at \(x > 1\) m. The cut-off part, from \(x\) to \(L\), has only the reaction \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\). Forces: the force of the cross section \(-V\) and the reaction cancel, \(-V + R_{\mathrm{B}} = 0\), so \(V = 500\) N. Moments about \(C(x)\): the reaction has the lever arm \(L - x\), the moment of the cross section is \(-M\), and \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = 0\), so \(M = 500\,(2 - x)\) N m.
  • A cut at \(x < 1\) m. The cut-off part contains also the force \(1\) kN at the middle. Forces: \(-V\)\(\,+ R_{\mathrm{B}} - 1000 = 0\), so \(V = -500\) N. Moments: the force at the middle has the lever arm \(x_0 - x = 1 - x\), the reaction the lever arm \(L - x\), and with the moment of the cross section \(-M\) it is \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) - 1000\,(1 - x) = 0\), so \(M = 500\,(2 - x) - 1000\,(1 - x) = 500\,x\) N m.

In the axes of the diagram of \(V\) the shear force is \(-500\) N on the left half and \(+500\) N on the right half. In the axes of the diagram of \(M\) the moment grows from zero at the pin to \(500\) N m at the middle and falls to zero at the roller support. Why is it zero at the supports? The cut-off part at \(x = L\) has no length left, so the reaction \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) has no lever arm, and the pin itself does not twist the beam. At \(x = 0\) the cut-off part is the whole beam, and \(M(0) = R_{\mathrm{B}}\,L - 1000 \cdot 1 = 0\) is exactly the moment equilibrium of the whole beam about \(x = 0\) from which we found \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\). The moment is positive everywhere, since under the force the beam sags like the piece in the figure of the signs: the lower fibres are stretched. The jump of the shear force at the force at the middle is \(500 - (-500) = 1000\) N, as much as the force, and the moment at \(x = 1\) m is continuous, \(500\cdot 1 = 500\cdot(2 - 1) = 500\) N m. Why this is so is shown by the lemma on the jump of the shear force in the section on a piece of a beam below. We get the same result with the part on the side of smaller \(x\), since the internal forces in the same cross section are the same. Then we would need the reaction \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\), and the force of the cross section on this part would be \(+\vek F(x)\) as on the left part of the figure of the signs and not \(-\vek F(x)\) as on the right one. SADD (D.5) (PDF p. 576) gives for the same beam, with a force \(P\) at \(a\) and \(b = L - a\), \(V_{\mathrm{SADD}} = P\,b/L\) to the left of the force and \(M_{\mathrm{SADD}} = P\,b\,x/L\), which for \(P = 1000\) N, \(a = b = 1\) m, \(L = 2\) m is exactly our \(-V\) and \(M\).

Simply supported beam with a uniform load. We load the same beam on a pin and a roller support with a uniformly distributed load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, pointing downward (definition 5.2). We cut it at \(x\) and look at the part between \(x\) and \(L\) with the load on it. The force of the load on this part is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\,(L - x)\). Its moment about \(C(x)\) we put together from pieces. We divide the part into short pieces, the piece at \(t_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the length \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) carries the force \(q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\), which lies at the distance \(t_{\mathrm{i}} - x\) from \(C(x)\). The moment of the load is the limit of the sums of the moments of these forces. Since \((t - x)\,q(t)\) is a continuous function, the sums are Riemann sums, and the moment is the integral

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\,\frac{(L - x)^2}{2} , \tag{5.7}\]

in the direction \(z\). This is the moment of the force \(-q_0\,(L - x)\) that would act at the middle of the cut-off part, at the distance \((L - x)/2\) from \(C(x)\), since \(-q_0\,(L - x)\cdot (L - x)/2 = -q_0\,(L - x)^2/2\). Why exactly at the middle? Two pieces that lie equally far to the left and to the right of the middle have lever arms that are larger and smaller than the middle one by the same amount, so together they give as much as two pieces at the middle. The reactions are equal by symmetry, \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\) \(=\) \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) \(= q_0\,L/2 = 1000\) N.

  1. Forces on the cut-off part from \(x\) to \(L\): the force of the cross section \(-V\), the reaction and the load, \(-V + R_{\mathrm{B}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), so \(V = R_{\mathrm{B}} - q_0\,(L - x) = q_0\,(x - L/2)\).
  2. Moments about \(C(x)\), component \(z\): the moment of the cross section \(-M\), the reaction with the lever arm \(L - x\) and the moment of the load (5.7), \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) - q_0\,(L - x)^2/2 = 0\), so
    \[M = \frac{q_0\,L}{2}\,(L - x) - \frac{q_0}{2}\,(L - x)^2 = \frac{q_0}{2}\,x\,(L - x) . \tag{5.8}\]

In the axes of the diagram of \(V\) the shear force is a straight line: \(V(0) = -1000\) N, \(V(L/2) = 0\), \(V(L) = +1000\) N. At the cut \(x = 0{,}5\) m it is \(V = -500\) N. In the axes of the diagram of \(M\) the moment is a parabola, zero at the supports, at \(x = 0{,}5\) m it is \(375\) N m. Where is it largest? We shall find out when we know the relation between \(M\) and \(V\) (example below). All three loads are chosen so that the largest absolute value of the moment is the same, \(500\) N m: a force \(500\) N on a cantilever of length \(1\) m (at the clamp), a force \(1\) kN at the middle of a beam of length \(2\) m (the peak, moment \(F\,L/4\)) and a load \(1\) kN/m on the same beam (the top of the parabola, moment \(q_0 L^2/8\)). This is a choice and not a law. It lets us compare later the stresses and displacements of the three beams at the same largest moment.

Self weight. So far we have left out the weight of the beam itself. How much we miss by that is shown in figure 5.32.

A steel bar \(20\times 40\) mm, clamped in the wall, has the cross-sectional area \(A = 8\cdot 10^{-4}\) m\(^2\), the density of steel is \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) and \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\). Its weight per unit length is the load \(q = -\rho g A = -61{,}21\) N/m, uniform and downward. For a cantilever of length \(1\) m the same procedure as for a uniform load gives \(V(x) = -61{,}21\,(L - x)\) N and \(M(x) = -61{,}21\,(L - x)^2/2\) N m. At the clamp \(V(0) = -61{,}21\) N and, in the axes of the diagram, \(M(0) = -30{,}61\) N m, which is \(6{,}1\) % of the moment due to the force \(500\) N, both fall to zero at the free end. A simply supported beam of length \(2\) m has \(M_{\max} = \rho g A\,L^2/8 = 30{,}61\) N m, the same share. When we leave the self weight out, we therefore miss about \(6\) % of the moment in our two beams. The share is \(\rho g A\,L/(2F)\) and grows with the length, since the moment of the weight grows with the square of the length and the moment of the force only with the length. In a long beam with a small load, such as a bridge, the self weight is the main load.

5.2.3 Equilibrium of a piece of a beam (ravnovesje kosa nosilca)

For the three beams we got \(V\) and \(M\) as functions of the place. For a uniform load \(V\) is linear and \(M\) a parabola, for a force at the middle \(V\) is constant on each half and \(M\) linear. Is the relation between them and the load the same for all? Let us differentiate and write everything in a table, \(L\) is the length of the beam.

beam \(q\) \(V\) \(V'\) \(M\) \(M'\)
uniform load \(-q_0\) \(q_0\,(x - L/2)\) \(q_0\) \((q_0/2)\,x\,(L - x)\) \(-q_0\,(x - L/2)\)
cantilever with a force at the end \(0\) \(-500\) N \(0\) \(-500\,(L - x)\) N m \(500\) N
force at the middle, left \(0\) \(-500\) N \(0\) \(500\,x\) N m \(500\) N
force at the middle, right \(0\) \(500\) N \(0\) \(500\,(L - x)\) N m \(-500\) N

In every row \(V'\) equals \(-q\) and \(M'\) equals \(-V\). For the cantilever and for the force at the middle \(q = 0\), since between the supports and the force there is no distributed load. So the pattern is \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). On the diagrams the second part is seen like this: where \(V\) is negative, \(M\) grows, where \(V\) is positive, \(M\) falls, for example to the left and to the right of the middle of the beam with a uniform load. Why exactly this pattern, each time with a minus? The answer is shown by the equilibrium of a short piece of a beam, which does not depend on the particular load. The approach is the same as for the axial force in lemma 4.75, only that now besides the axial force we have also the shear force and the moment.

First let us say what acts on the piece from outside (figure 5.33).

We describe the load with a force per unit length that acts on the axis of the beam, \(\vek q(x) = (q_{\mathrm{x}}, q, q_{\mathrm{z}})\). The component \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the axial load (definition 4.74), \(q\) is the transverse load in the direction \(+y\) (definition 5.2), \(q_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the transverse load in the direction \(z\), defined in the same way. Each component is a function of the place \(x\).

Often, however, the load does not act on the axis but on the upper edge, for example the wheels of a car on a bridge. Let us draw a piece of a beam with its axis at mid-height. The upper edge is \(h/2\) above the axis. The vertical load \(q\) on the edge points down, and the line on which each of its forces lies passes through the axis. We may move the force along this line to the axis, since the lever arm about any point changes only by a vector parallel to the force, and the vector product of parallel vectors is zero. So the moment of the force does not change.

It is different with a horizontal load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the upper edge, for example when a braking wheel drags the road surface forward. Its line is \(h/2\) above the axis. We move it onto the axis by adding \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the axis, which cancel. The original \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the edge and \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the axis are then a couple (definition 5.3), with the moment per unit length, by lemma 5.4,

\[\tfrac{h}{2}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\times q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\tfrac{h}{2}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

What remains is \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the axis and the distributed couple (porazdeljeni moment) \(\vek m(x)\), the moment of couples per unit length, here \(m = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/2\). It points clockwise, because a pull to the right along the upper edge turns the piece that way. The vertical \(q\) goes onto the axis with nothing added. In our examples there is no horizontal load and \(\vek m = \vek 0\).

Let the load be continuous, and let there be no point forces and couples on the piece under consideration. Why this condition? The lemma will add up the load on the piece into an integral, and Riemann sums settle when the load is continuous. At a point force the force on a short piece around it does not fall toward zero as we shrink the piece, so the shear force jumps there, as for the beam with a force at the middle. We treat such places (supports, wheels, point forces and couples) separately at the end of the section, and between them the lemma holds on every segment.

Now let us cut out of the beam the piece between the cross sections \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) and draw everything that acts on it (figure 5.34).

At the top is the piece from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), with the centres of the cross sections on the axis. On the right face acts the part of the beam to the right of it, with the resultant \(\vek F(x_2)\) at the centre and with the moment \(\vek M(x_2)\). On the left face acts the part to the left of it, with \(-\vek F(x_1)\) and \(-\vek M(x_1)\), with a minus, because the outward normal there is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). From above acts the load \(\vek q\), from which we pick a small force \(\vek q(t)\,\Delta t\) on a short part of length \(\Delta t\) at \(t\).

Lever arms such as \(t - x_1\) are measured on the shape of the beam before loading (assumption 3 of section 5.2). We may do this because bending moves the points of application along the axis very little: for the cantilever with the force \(500\) N at the end, \(L = 1\) m, the free end comes closer to the wall by only \(0{,}04\) mm, the lever arm changes by \(4 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of its length and the moment at the clamp by just as much (computed with SymPy from the shape of the bent axis, which we compute later). We may not do it when a large axial force compresses the beam at the same time. On the bent axis this force gets a lever arm equal to the offset of the axis and adds a moment that the lemma does not contain.

Lemma 5.10 Equilibrium of a piece of a beam (ravnovesje kosa nosilca)

Let the beam be in equilibrium and let the continuous distributed load \(\vek q\) and the distributed couple \(\vek m\) act on the piece between the cross sections \(x_1 < x_2\), without point forces and couples. Let the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be continuous on every cross section of the piece and satisfy (H1) and (H2) of lemma 5.9. The internal force \(\vek F\) and the internal moment \(\vek M\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8) are then continuous functions on \([x_1, x_2]\) and

\[\vek F(x_2) - \vek F(x_1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{5.9}\]
\[\vek M(x_2) - \vek M(x_1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \big(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(t) + \vek m(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{5.10}\]

In a plane problem, \(\vek F = (N, V, 0)\), \(\vek M = (0, 0, M)\), \(\vek q = (q_{\mathrm{x}}, q, 0)\), \(\vek m = (0, 0, m)\), we have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, 0, V)\) and

\[\begin{aligned} N(x_2) - N(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}t , \\ V(x_2) - V(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q\,\mathrm{d}t , \\ M(x_2) - M(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \big(V(t) + m(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t . \end{aligned} \tag{5.11}\]
Proof

We write equilibrium of the piece, first the forces, then the moments about the centre of the left cross section. In the moments a term with \(\vek F(x_2)\) appears, which we transform into an integral with the derivative and the fundamental theorem of calculus. In the figure this term is a rectangle that the graph of the shear force divides into two parts. At the end we read the plane case from the parallelogram of the vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek F\).

  1. On the piece act: across the right face, with the outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force \(\vek F(x_2)\) and the moment \(\vek M(x_2)\) about \(C(x_2)\). Across the left face, with the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the traction vector is by lemma 2.21 the opposite one, so the force is \(-\vek F(x_1)\) and the moment \(-\vek M(x_1)\) about \(C(x_1)\). The load: the force \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\), by components by definitions 4.74 and 5.2, and the moment \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek m\,\mathrm{d}t\), by components by the definition of the distributed couple above.
  2. Equilibrium of the forces on the piece (2.2): \(\vek F(x_2) - \vek F(x_1) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). This is (5.9).
  3. The moment of the forces on the right face about \(C(x_1)\) is the sum of the moments of the small forces \(\vek t\,\Delta A\). The vector from \(C(x_1)\) to the point of application is \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(+\) \(\vek r\), where \(\vek r\) is the vector from \(C(x_2)\) to the point of application. By items 2 and 3 of lemma 2.34 the sum of the moments is \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\sum\vek t\,\Delta A + \sum\vek r\times\vek t\,\Delta A\), in the limit \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \vek M(x_2)\). The limits exist by steps 4 and 6 of lemma 5.9 since the stresses are continuous and satisfy (H1) and (H2) of that lemma by assumption.
  4. The small force of the load \(\vek q(t)\,\Delta t\) has the vector from \(C(x_1)\) to the point of application on the axis equal to \((t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The function \((t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\) is continuous, so the sums of the moments are Riemann sums and the moment of the load is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} (t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  5. Equilibrium of moments about \(C(x_1)\) (2.45), the sum of steps 1, 3 and 4: \(\vek M(x_2) - \vek M(x_1) + (x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}(t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek m\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
  6. Steps 1 and 2 hold for every piece inside \([x_1, x_2]\). Let \(x \in [x_1, x_2]\). For the piece from \(x_1\) to \(x\) we get \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\): from \(x_1\) to \(x\) the shear force rises by the area under \(-q\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 2, each component is continuous on \([x_1, x_2]\) and differentiable with the derivative \(-\vek q\) (a narrow strip of width \(\Delta x\) at \(x\) has the area about \(-q(x)\,\Delta x\)), so \(\vek F' = -\vek q\).
  7. By the product rule (lemma 1.82, items 3 and 4) \(\big((t - x_1)\,\vek F(t)\big)' = \vek F(t) - (t - x_1)\,\vek q(t)\), and this derivative is continuous. By the fundamental theorem, item 1, \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek F(x_2) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek F\,\mathrm{d}t - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}(t - x_1)\,\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). For the component \(V\) this equality has a picture: the rectangle with the area \((x_2 - x_1)\,V(x_2)\) is the sum of the area under the graph of \(V\) and the area above it. The horizontal strip above the graph at \(t\) has the height \(-q(t)\,\Delta t\), the force of the load on \(\Delta t\), and the length \(t - x_1\), its lever arm about \(C(x_1)\). The area above the graph is therefore the moment of the load, in the example \(1000 - 500 = 500\) N m.
  8. The vector product with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is linear (lemma 2.34, items 2 and 3) and the integral is linear (lemma 3.54, item 1), by components. Step 7 therefore gives \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \int(t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q = \int\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F\,\mathrm{d}t\), the rectangle without the area above the graph is the area below it. We insert it into step 5 and get (5.10). In the example \(M(2) - M(1) = 0 - 500 = -500\) N m, minus the area under the graph of \(V\). The continuity of \(\vek M\) follows from (5.10) for \(x_1\) and \(x\) and from the fundamental theorem.
  9. In a plane problem \(\vek F = N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), so by items 2 and 3 of lemma 2.34 \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). The axial force lies on the line of the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), their parallelogram is flat, and by item 1 \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\). The shear force is perpendicular to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), by item 6 \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). So \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, 0, V)\), the same as by (2.39), \((1, 0, 0)\times(N, V, 0) = (0\cdot 0 - 0\cdot V,\ 0\cdot N - 1\cdot 0,\ 1\cdot V - 0\cdot N)\). The components of (5.9) and (5.10) are therefore (5.11).

The lemma says that between two cross sections the shear force changes by the load on this piece, and the moment by the area under \(V\), both times with a minus. An integral is the area under a diagram, so this is a second view of the same lemma: we read the change of \(V\) and \(M\) off the picture without integrating anything, and we can draw the diagrams from left to right (figure 5.37).

Let us test it on the beam with a uniform load, where \(V = q_0\,(x - L/2)\) and \(M = (q_0/2)\,x\,(L - x)\). Let us draw one under another the diagrams of the load \(q\), the shear force \(V\) and the moment \(M\), and on all three mark the piece from \(x_1 = 0{,}5\) m to \(x_2 = 1{,}5\) m. The area under \(q\) on the piece is \(-1000\) N/m times \(1\) m, that is \(-1000\) N. The shear force rises on the piece from \(V(0{,}5) = -500\) N to \(V(1{,}5) = 500\) N, so by \(1000\) N, which is exactly \(-\int q\,\mathrm{d}t = 1000\) N.

Now the moment. Under \(V\) on the piece there is the area \(-125\) N m to the left of the middle and the area \(+125\) N m to the right of it. The sum is zero, so \(M(1{,}5) - M(0{,}5) = 0\), and indeed \(M(0{,}5)\) and \(M(1{,}5)\) are both \(375\) N m. On the piece from \(x_1 = 0\) to \(x_2 = 1\) m the area under \(V\) equals \(-500\) N m, so the moment rises by \(500\) N m, from \(M(0) = 0\) to \(M(1) = 500\) N m.

We do not need the piece to be arbitrarily long. When we shrink it, we get the derivative. Let us take a short piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) (figure 5.38).

Let us draw the short piece of length \(\Delta x\), with the load \(q\) on the axis, drawn downward, because in our examples \(q < 0\). On the left face acts the part to the left of it with \(-N\), \(-V\) and \(-M\), on the right face the part to the right of it with \(N + \Delta N\), \(V + \Delta V\) and \(M + \Delta M\). The sign \(\Delta\) says by how much the quantity changes from the left face to the right one.

Corollary 5.11 Differential relations between the load, the shear force and the moment (diferencialne zveze)

Let \(\vek q\) and \(\vek m\) be continuous on an open interval \((a, b)\), without point forces and couples on it, and let the assumptions of lemma 5.10 on the stresses hold. Then \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) are differentiable on \((a, b)\) and

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}\vek F}{\mathrm{d}x} + \vek q = \vek 0 , \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\vek M}{\mathrm{d}x} + \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m = \vek 0 . \tag{5.12}\]

In a plane problem

\[N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad V' = -q , \qquad M' = -V - m . \tag{5.13}\]
Proof

We get the derivative from the lemma on a piece whose right cross section we move, and from the fundamental theorem of calculus.

  1. Let \(x \in (a, b)\) and \([x_1, x_2] \subset (a, b)\) a piece that contains \(x\). By lemma 5.10, applied to the piece from \(x_1\) to \(x\), \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 2, \(\vek F\) is differentiable with the derivative \(-\vek q\) (continuous), a narrow strip at \(x\) adds the area about \(-q(x)\,\Delta x\).
  2. The function \(t \mapsto \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(t) + \vek m(t)\) is continuous, and by the lemma \(\vek M(x) = \vek M(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\). By the fundamental theorem \(\vek M' = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F - \vek m\). On a short piece the term \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F\) is the moment of the pair of forces \(-V\) and \(V + \Delta V\) with the lever arm \(\Delta x\), divided by \(\Delta x\), in the limit \(\Delta x \to 0\), as the paragraph after the corollary shows.
  3. In a plane problem \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, 0, V)\) (step 9 of the lemma), which gives (5.13).

Why does the relation \(M' = -V\) have a minus? Let us look at a short piece \([x, x + \Delta x]\) and a positive \(V\) on the right face (figure 5.38). The force \(V\) on the right face points up and has, about the centre of the left face, the lever arm \(\Delta x\) in the direction \(+x\). Its moment is \(\Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = V\,\Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Together with the opposite force \(-V\) on the left face it is a couple that turns the piece counterclockwise. The piece is in equilibrium, so for \(m = 0\), as in our examples, this turning is balanced by the difference of the internal moments \(M + \Delta M\) and \(M\): \(M(x + \Delta x) - M(x) + V\,\Delta x \approx 0\). This is the reason for the minus. The moment is not constant by itself, but changes by the moment of the shear force of the right face about the left one. What remains is the term of the load, which falls with the square of \(\Delta x\) (step 5 of the proof of lemma 5.10): the load on the piece is about \(q\,\Delta x\), its lever arm about \(\Delta x/2\), the product \(q\,\Delta x^2/2\), and after division by \(\Delta x\) it vanishes as we shrink \(\Delta x\). For \(m = 0\) from (5.13) follows also \(M'' = -V' = q\). For a uniform load downward, \(q = -q_0\), we therefore have \(M'' = -q_0 < 0\). The slope \(M' = -V\) falls steadily, because \(V\) rises steadily, so \(M\) first grows, then falls, and is a parabola with the peak up, as we got it. The moment is, up to a linear term, the double integral of the load. Later we shall link the moment also with how strongly the axis of the beam curves.

Where is the moment largest? For the beam with a uniform load \(M\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), so by theorem 1.74 it attains its largest value. This is not at the end points, where \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\), since \(M(0{,}5\ \text{m}) = 375\) N m. The largest value is therefore at an interior point \(x^*\), where \(M\) is differentiable, and by lemma 1.83 \(M'(x^*) = 0\). By (5.13) \(V(x^*) = 0\) there. The shear force \(V = q_0\,(x - L/2)\) is zero only at \(x^* = L/2 = 1\) m, where \(M = q_0 L^2/8 = 500\) N m. An extremum of \(M\) at an interior point where \(M\) is differentiable occurs only at \(V = 0\). An extremum can also lie at an end (for the cantilever \(|M|\) is largest at the clamp, where \(V = -500\) N) or at a point force. For the beam with a force at the middle \(V\) is nowhere zero and \(M\) has a peak at the force, where it is not differentiable.

What about a point force? The lemma on a piece requires that there be no point forces on the piece. For the beam with a force at the middle we saw that the shear force jumps at the force and that the moment is continuous. Why is shown by a short piece around the force (figure 5.39).

At the top is the magnified short piece of width \(\Delta x\) around \(x_0\). From above the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\) presses on it. The neighbouring parts act on it with the forces of the cross sections, the left one with \(-V(x_0^-)\), the right one with \(V(x_0^+)\). Below it are the diagrams of the whole beam: on the axes the shear force with a jump, on the axes the continuous moment with a peak at \(x_0\). The general statement is the lemma.

Lemma 5.12 Jump of the shear force and the moment (skok prečne sile in momenta)

Let a point force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, F_{\mathrm{z}})\) and a point couple with the moment \(\vek M_{\mathrm{p}}\) act at an interior point \(x_0\) of the axis, and let continuous \(\vek q\) and \(\vek m\) act on the intervals \([x_1, x_0)\) and \((x_0, x_2]\), which extend to continuous functions on \([x_1, x_0]\) and \([x_0, x_2]\), without other point loads, and let the assumptions of lemma 5.10 on the stresses hold. Then the left and right limits \(\vek F(x_0^{\pm})\) and \(\vek M(x_0^{\pm})\) exist and

\[\vek F(x_0^+) - \vek F(x_0^-) = -\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} , \qquad \vek M(x_0^+) - \vek M(x_0^-) = -\vek M_{\mathrm{p}} . \tag{5.14}\]

In a plane problem with a point force \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) in the direction \(y\) and a couple with the moment \(M_0\) in the direction \(z\): \(V\) jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(M\) jumps by \(-M_0\). For a point force alone, without a couple, \(M\) is continuous.

Proof

On a short piece around \(x_0\) we write equilibrium with the point loads and let the piece shrink to the point.

  1. Existence of the limits. By lemma 5.10, applied to the piece \([x, x_2]\), for \(x_0 < x < x_2\) we have \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). As \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) from the right, the right-hand side approaches, by the continuity of the integral (fundamental theorem, item 2, for \(\vek q\) extended by a continuous function on \([x_0, x_2]\)), \(\vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). So \(\vek F(x_0^+)\) exists. In the same way, with the lemma on the piece \([x_1, x]\) for \(x_1 < x < x_0\), \(\vek F(x_0^-)\) exists. In the figure these are the heights of the diagram of \(V\) just to the left and just to the right of the jump. The function \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m\) therefore extends on each side to a continuous function on the closed interval. By (5.10) \(\vek M(x) = \vek M(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x_2}(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m)\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(\vek M(x_0^{\pm})\) exist too.
  2. Let \(x_1 \le a < x_0 < b \le x_2\). Equilibrium of forces on the piece \([a, b]\), which contains the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\): \(\vek F(b) - \vek F(a) + \vek F_{\mathrm{p}} + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^{b}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). We split the integral at \(x_0\) into the integrals of the continuous extensions over \([a, x_0]\) and \([x_0, b]\) (item 2 of lemma 3.54). Each component of each is by item 3 of the same lemma, with the bounds \(-c\) and \(c\), at most \(c\,(b - a)\) in absolute value, where \(c\) is the largest absolute value of the components of the extensions, and tends to zero. When \(a \to x_0^-\) and \(b \to x_0^+\), we get the first equation (5.14).
  3. Moments about \(C(a)\): as in step 5 of lemma 5.10 the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\) has the lever arm \((x_0 - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the couple the moment \(\vek M_{\mathrm{p}}\) (the moment of a couple is the same about every point, lemma 5.4), so \(\vek M(b) - \vek M(a) + (b - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(b) + (x_0 - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} + \vek M_{\mathrm{p}} + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^{b}\big((t - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q + \vek m\big)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
  4. When \(a \to x_0^-\) and \(b \to x_0^+\): \(\vek F(b)\) is bounded by step 1, so \((b - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(b)\) tends to zero, so does \((x_0 - a)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} \to \vek 0\), and the integral tends to zero by item 3 of the lemma on the properties of the integral. What remains is \(\vek M(x_0^+) - \vek M(x_0^-) + \vek M_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek 0\), the second equation of (5.14).

What about a couple? We load the same simply supported beam at the middle with a couple with the moment \(M_0 = 100\) N m in the direction \(z\), counterclockwise, with no other loads (figure 5.41).

On the beam of length \(L = 2\) m the couple \(M_0\) acts at \(x_0 = 1\) m. By itself it would turn the beam counterclockwise, the right end would rise, the left one sink. The supports therefore hold it with a pair of forces clockwise: from equilibrium of the beam \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\,L + M_0 = 0\) (component \(z\) about \(x = 0\)), so \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = -50\) N and \(R_{\mathrm{A}} = +50\) N. The support \(A\) pushes the beam upward with \(50\) N, and the roller support \(B\) pulls it downward with \(50\) N. The pair of \(50\) N on the lever arm \(2\) m has the moment \(100\) N m and balances \(M_0\).

By the procedure with equilibrium of a part \(V = -50\) N along the whole length. On the right half \(M = R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = -50\,(2 - x)\) N m, on the left, where the part contains the couple, \(M = M_0 + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = 50\,x\) N m. At \(x = 1\) m \(M(1^-) = 50\) N m and \(M(1^+) = -50\) N m. The jump is \(-100\) N m \(= -M_0\), as the lemma claims, and \(V\) is continuous. A couple therefore has a different effect from a force: a force shifts \(V\), a couple shifts \(M\).

The same reasoning gives the conditions at the ends of the beam. At the free end \(x = L\) act a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple with the moment \(M_0\). On the piece from \(x\) to \(L\) act, besides the force of the cross section \(-\vek F(x)\), these two as well, so \(V(x) = F_{\mathrm{y}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t\) and, when \(x \to L\), \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), since the load on an ever shorter piece falls toward zero. In the same way from the moments about \(C(x)\): \(M(x) = M_0 + (L - x)\,F_{\mathrm{y}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,q\,\mathrm{d}t\), which tends to \(M_0\) as \(x \to L\), because the lever arm \(L - x\) tends to zero. For the cantilever with a force \(500\) N downward \(V(L) = -500\) N \(= F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M(L) = 0 = M_0\), as we computed. At the clamped end \(x = 0\) the wall acts on the beam with the force \(-\vek F(0)\) and the moment \(-\vek M(0)\), since the outward normal of the beam there is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For the cantilever this is a force \(500\) N upward and the moment \(+500\) N m, counterclockwise, which holds the beam against the load at the end.
