Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.2 Internal forces in a beam (notranje sile v nosilcu)

In chapter 2 we cut a beam clamped in a wall in thought (figure 2.5). One part of the beam acts on the other across the cut with forces that are distributed over the whole cross section and have a longitudinal and a vertical part. The chapter promised that we would describe them with three numbers and define them exactly in this chapter. We do this now. There are two questions. What are these numbers and how do we compute them from the load? How do they change along the beam? The answer to the first says exactly what we are describing, the answer to the second says how to find these quantities in all cross sections at once, without cutting the beam anew for every cross section.

The beam lies along the axis \(x\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\), the axis \(y\) points up, the axis \(z\) toward the reader (section 5.1). The cross section is a rectangle with height \(h\) in the direction \(y\) and width \(b\) in the direction \(z\), its centre (središče) lies on the axis of the beam. The centre of a rectangle is the intersection of its diagonals. For now we can compute an integral over the area of a cross section only over a rectangle (definition 4.8), so we state the lemma on internal forces for a rectangular cross section. The mathematics section on the cross section, which follows in this chapter, extends the integral to cross sections made of rectangles, such as the letter T and the double T. There we shall see that the centre of a rectangle is also the mean position of its points, and that for composite cross sections this mean position replaces the centre. The lemma holds for such cross sections too, as corollary 5.19 in this chapter shows. All loads lie in the plane \(xy\), which is a plane of symmetry of the cross section: it divides the cross section into two mirror halves, one at \(z > 0\) and the other at \(z < 0\). The displacements are small compared with the lengths, so we write equilibrium on the shape of the beam before loading, as in chapter 4.

From history

Galileo broke a bar built into a wall, as we saw in chapter 1 (figure 1.6). He then asked what happens when the bar is not built in but lies on two supports and the force acts at any point between them (TIMO, PDF p. 22). He found that the effect of the force on breaking is greatest under the force and proportional to the product \(a b\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are the distances of the force from the two supports (TIMO, PDF p. 22). We can see the reason from equilibrium. For a support spacing \(a + b\) the left support carries the force \(F b/(a + b)\), and its moment about the point under the force is \(F a b/(a + b)\). The spacing does not change, so this moment is proportional to \(a b\). The product of two numbers with a given sum is largest when the numbers are equal, since \((a + b)^2/4 - a b = \big((a - b)/2\big)^2 \ge 0\). The smallest force that breaks the bar is therefore the one acting at the middle between the supports (TIMO, PDF p. 22).

Because the effect of the force on breaking is smaller near the supports than at the middle, Galileo noticed that material can be saved if the cross section is made smaller toward the supports (TIMO, PDF p. 22). For a cantilever of rectangular cross section he also derived the shape in which the strength is the same everywhere. The effect of the force on breaking grows with the distance from the free end, and the resistance of a cross section grows with the square of its depth. The depth must therefore grow with the square root of the distance, which gives a parabola (TIMO, PDF p. 22). So the effect of the force on breaking is not the same in all cross sections of the bar, and we must know it in each cross section separately.

Let us return to the question of the section: which forces and moments act across one cross section of a beam, and how they change from cross section to cross section along the beam.

5.2.1 Three numbers in a cross section (tri števila v prerezu)

We cut the beam at \(x\) and look at the face of the cut that belongs to the part on the side of smaller \(x\). Its outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on this part across the face, and per unit area at a point \(Q\) of the face acts the traction vector (definition 2.20)

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\]

with the components of stress from definition 2.25. The components are functions of the point \(Q\) on the face and also of the place \(x\). We chose the same side for the axial force (definition 2.5), so we need not remember anything new: the force that we describe is always the force of the part on the side of larger \(x\) on the part on the side of smaller \(x\).

Besides the resultant (rezultanta) of these forces, that is the internal force (definition 2.4), we are interested in how strongly the forces try to rotate the part. The resultant does not tell this: the same resultant, acting at the centre of the cross section or at its edge, rotates differently. So we add to it the moment of the distributed forces. We compute it about the centre of the cross section \(C(x) = (x, 0, 0)\). The moment of a force depends on the point about which we compute it, since the resultant is different from zero (lemma 2.34 holds only for a zero resultant). The centre of the cross section lies on the axis of the beam. We shall need this when we move the moment from one cross section to another.

Definition 5.7 Shear force (prečna sila)

Let \(\vek F(x)\) be the internal force (definition 2.4) in the cross section of the beam at \(x\), that is the resultant of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across the cross section on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). Let us write it by components, \(\vek F(x) = (N,\ V_{\mathrm{y}},\ V_{\mathrm{z}})\). We called the first component \(N\) the axial force (definition 2.5). The shear forces (prečni sili) are the components \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\), perpendicular to the axis of the beam. In a plane problem, where all loads lie in the plane \(xy\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and we denote the shear force \(V := V_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309), SADD app. D (PDF pp. 576-577). All three know the shear force only in the plane. EP and CM have the same sign as we do, SADD the opposite one (see the paragraph on the books below). The vector form is ours.

Definition 5.8 Bending moment (upogibni moment)

Let \(\vek M(x)\) be the internal moment (notranji moment) in the cross section of the beam at \(x\), that is the moment about the centre of the cross section \(C(x)\) of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across the cross section on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). As in lemma 2.35, it is the sum of the moments of the small forces \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\,\Delta A\) over the pieces of the face, as the pieces are made smaller. Let us write it by components, \(\vek M(x) = (M_{\mathrm{x}},\ M_{\mathrm{y}},\ M_{\mathrm{z}})\). The components \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the bending moments (upogibna momenta), since they bend the beam about the axes \(y\) and \(z\). The component \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the moment about the axis of the beam, the torque (torzijski moment), which twists the beam, and chapter 16 deals with it. In a plane problem \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and we denote the bending moment \(M := M_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309), SADD app. D (PDF pp. 576-577). All three know only the component about the axis perpendicular to the plane of bending. EP and CM have the opposite sign to ours, SADD the same one (see the paragraph on the books below). The vector form is ours.

The definitions are written with a resultant and a moment, while the stresses are distributed over the cross section. Which stresses make up \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\)? The answer is in lemma 5.9. The cross section is a rectangle, so the integral over the area is the integral over a rectangle from definition 4.8. The lemma needs a hypothesis on the forces across small patches of the section. Definition 2.20 speaks only of squares centred at the point, while the pieces of a grid only contain the point and have their centres elsewhere.

Lemma 5.9 Internal forces from stresses (notranje sile iz napetosti)

Let the cross section of the beam at \(x\) be the rectangle \(A = \{(x, y, z) : -h/2 \le y \le h/2,\ -b/2 \le z \le b/2\}\) with centre \(C = (x, 0, 0)\), and let the components of stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be continuous functions on \(A\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\). For a rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) with sides parallel to those of \(A\), let \(\vek F(R)\) be the force and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R)\) the moment about a point \(P\) of the forces with which the part of the beam on the side of larger \(x\) acts across \(R\) on the part on the side of smaller \(x\), and \(|R|\) the area. We assume:

  • (H1) For every point \(P \in A\) and every rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) of this kind that contains \(P\), \(|\vek F(R)/|R| - \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})(P)| \to 0\) when the diameter of \(R\) tends to 0. The average force per unit area on a small patch tends to the traction vector, as in theorem 2.24.
  • (H2) For the same \(P\) and \(R\), \(\vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R)/|R| \to 0\). The forces across a small patch act at the patch, so their moment about a point of the patch is by point 2 of lemma 2.35 at most force density times area times diameter, which means there are no couple stresses. CM (PDF pp. 198-199) likewise assumes the absence of body couples.
  • Forces and moments add up: if \(R\) is split into rectangles \(R_1, \dots, R_{\mathrm{m}}\), then \(\vek F(R) = \sum \vek F(R_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{O}}(R) = \sum \vek M_{\mathrm{O}}(R_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every point \(O\), since this is the resultant and the moment of the forces on the pieces (definitions 2.2 and 2.32).

Then

\[N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad V_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad V_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.4}\]
\[\begin{aligned} M_{\mathrm{x}} &= \int_{\mathrm{A}} (y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\,\mathrm{d}A , \\ M_{\mathrm{y}} &= \int_{\mathrm{A}} z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A , \\ M_{\mathrm{z}} &= -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A . \end{aligned} \tag{5.5}\]

So in a plane problem \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Proof

A limit at each point says nothing about how fast it is approached, so we cannot assemble the result from pieces by summing errors. Bisection finds the point where the claim would fail, and there we reach a contradiction with (H1). We work with one component at a time.

  1. For a component \(k\) and a rectangle \(R \subseteq A\) let \(G(R) = F_{\mathrm{k}}(R) - \int_{\mathrm{R}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) with \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = (\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}))_{\mathrm{k}}\). If \(R\) is split into rectangles \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\), then \(G(R) = \sum G(R_{\mathrm{i}})\), since \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\) is additive and the integral is additive by point 4 of lemma 4.32 (area instead of volume).
  2. Suppose \(|G(R_0)| = \varepsilon\,|R_0|\) with \(\varepsilon > 0\) for some rectangle \(R_0 \subseteq A\). We halve both sides into four congruent rectangles. By step 1 at least one of them, \(R_1\), has \(|G(R_1)| \ge \varepsilon\,|R_1|\), otherwise the sum of the four absolute values would be less than \(\varepsilon\,|R_0|\). We repeat and get nested rectangles \(R_0 \supseteq R_1 \supseteq R_2 \supseteq \dots\) with \(|G(R_{\mathrm{n}})| \ge \varepsilon\,|R_{\mathrm{n}}|\), whose sides are halved each time.
  3. The coordinates \((y_{\mathrm{n}}, z_{\mathrm{n}})\) of the lower left corner of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) form non-decreasing sequences bounded by \(h/2\) and \(b/2\), so by theorem 1.51 they converge to a point \(P\). For \(m \ge n\) the corner of \(R_{\mathrm{m}}\) lies in \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(P\) lies in the closed rectangle \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\), hence in all of them. The sides of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) are \(2^{-n}\) times those of \(R_0\), so the diameter of \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) tends to 0 (lemma 1.39).
  4. By (H1), \(F_{\mathrm{k}}(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\). Since \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous at \(P\), point 3 of lemma 4.32 gives \(\big|\int_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}}(t_{\mathrm{k}} - t_{\mathrm{k}}(P))\,\mathrm{d}A\big| \le \max_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}}|t_{\mathrm{k}} - t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)|\,|R_{\mathrm{n}}|\), so \(\frac{1}{|R_{\mathrm{n}}|}\int_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{n}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A \to t_{\mathrm{k}}(P)\). Hence \(G(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to 0\), which contradicts \(|G(R_{\mathrm{n}})|/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \ge \varepsilon\). So \(F_{\mathrm{k}}(R) = \int_{\mathrm{R}} t_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) for every rectangle \(R \subseteq A\), and for \(R = A\) this is (5.4), with \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\).
  5. The vector from \(C\) to \(Q = (x, y, z)\) is \(\vek r = (0, y, z)\) and
    \[\vek r\times\vek t = (y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \ z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - 0\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \ 0\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} - y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})\]
    by definition 2.32 and equation (2.32). Each component is continuous on \(A\), since it is a sum of products of continuous functions with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\).
  6. For a component \(k\) let now \(G(R) = M_{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{k}}(R) - \int_{\mathrm{R}} (\vek r\times\vek t)_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). It is additive as in step 1, and steps 2 and 3 repeat unchanged. For \(R_{\mathrm{n}} \ni P\) we have \(\vek M_{\mathrm{C}}(R_{\mathrm{n}}) = \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek F(R_{\mathrm{n}}) + \vek M_{\mathrm{P}}(R_{\mathrm{n}})\), since the vectors from \(C\) to the points of the forces are the sum of the vector \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) from \(C\) to \(P\) and the vector from \(P\) to those points, and point 2 of lemma 2.31 applies. By step 4, (H2) and the continuity of \(\vek r\times\vek t\) at \(P\), \(G(R_{\mathrm{n}})/|R_{\mathrm{n}}| \to \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek t(P) + \vek 0 - \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek t(P) = 0\) (component \(k\)), again a contradiction. Hence \(\vek M(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \vek r\times\vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\), which by components is (5.5).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74) and CM (7.3.28) (PDF p. 309) give the same integral for \(N\) and \(V\) and for \(M\) with the opposite sign, SADD (PDF p. 576) has \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}A\) like us. The resultant force on a surface as the integral of the traction vector: BOWER (PDF p. 66), the moment of the surface forces as \(\int \vek y\times\vek T\,\mathrm{d}A\): BOWER (PDF p. 78). The absence of body couples, which gives a symmetric stress tensor: CM (4.5.6)-(4.5.9) (PDF pp. 198-199). The hypothesis (H2) on the limit of the moment across a small patch is not from a source, the sources only assume that there are no couple stresses. The proof is not from a source.

Let us test the lemma on a stress that in the cross section \(b = 20\) mm, \(h = 40\) mm is linear in the height, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y\), with no shear stresses. For \(k > 0\) the upper part of the cross section (\(y > 0\)) is in tension, the lower in compression. By the lemma \(N = k\int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2}\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z = 0\), since the contributions of the upper and of the lower part cancel, and the moment is

\[M_{\mathrm{z}} = -k\,b\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = -k\,\frac{b\,h^3}{12} , \qquad \frac{b\,h^3}{12} = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4 .\]

For \(k = 4{,}6875\cdot 10^{9}\) Pa/m the stress at the upper edge, where \(y = 0{,}02\) m, equals \(93{,}75\) MPa, and \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = -500\) N m. So a stretched upper edge means a negative moment. A positive bending moment means the opposite, the lower edge is stretched. The cantilever has the same picture at the clamp when we load it at the free end with a force \(500\) N downward: the upper fibres stretch, the lower ones shorten. That the cantilever has the moment \(-500\) N m there is shown by equilibrium in the next section. That the stress is really linear in \(y\) is shown by the section on stresses in bending, here we only tested the lemma.

The plane problem now gets its justification. The loads lie in the plane of symmetry of the cross section, so the picture of the stresses is mirror symmetric about the plane \(xy\) (figure 5.9). The normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) have the same value at \(z\) and \(-z\), the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), which points in the direction \(z\), the opposite one. In the lemma the integrands for \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) are therefore odd functions of \(z\) (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\)), and their integral over \(z \in [-b/2, b/2]\) is zero. What remains is

\[N(x), \qquad V(x), \qquad M(x) ,\]

the axial force, the shear force and the bending moment, the three numbers that chapter 2 announced. Each is a function of the place \(x\) with one number as input and one as output. We also call them stress resultants (rezultante napetosti), because we get them from the stresses. The symmetry of the stresses is an assumption that we shall check when we know the stresses.

The books write these numbers differently. EP has the axis \(z\) up and CM the axis \(y\) up, and they define \(M\) as \(\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) respectively (EP (2.5.6), p. 74, CM (7.3.28), PDF p. 309). Their \(M\) is therefore \(-M_{\mathrm{z}}\) by our definitions, and their shear force equals \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\) (in EP with the symbol \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) for the vertical shear stress). CM has in (7.3.26) and (7.3.27) text about the direction \(z\) and the axis \(y\), although (7.3.28) uses \(y\) for the vertical direction: the symbols are not consistent (PDF p. 309). SADD has the same \(M\) as we do, and its shear force is \(-V_{\mathrm{y}}\) (SADD (D.5), (D.6), (D.8), PDF pp. 576-578). For every borrowed equation we therefore derive the signs from our definitions and do not copy them from the book. Why such a choice? We describe all three quantities on the side that we chose for the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xj}}\) in chapter 2, that is on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). So there is no special rule for beams to remember, all other signs we get by computation.

The positive directions of \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) on both sides of the cut are shown in figure 5.10. On the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) a positive \(N\) and \(V\) point in the direction \(+x\) and \(+y\), a positive \(M\) in the direction \(+z\), that is toward the reader. On the opposite face, with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the stress is by lemma 2.21 the opposite one, and so all three point in the opposite directions.

5.2.2 Internal forces from equilibrium (notranje sile iz ravnovesja)

How do we compute these forces if we do not know the stresses? From equilibrium. If a beam is in equilibrium, so is each of its parts (section 2.1), also when we cut it off in thought. On the cut-off part act the external loads and the forces of the cross section that we are looking for. The procedure is this.

  1. We cut the beam at \(x\) and choose the part on the side of larger \(x\), from the cut to the end \(x = L\).
  2. We draw all forces and moments on it. On the face of the cut the part on the side of smaller \(x\) acts on our part. The face has the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so by lemma 2.21 the force on it is \(-\vek F(x)\), and the moment about the centre \(C(x)\) is \(-\vek M(x)\) (each vector \(\vek r\times\vek t\) changes sign with \(\vek t\)).
  3. We write equilibrium of forces (2.2) and of moments (2.35). We compute moments about the centre of the cross section \(C(x)\), since then the internal moment appears by itself.

Why do we choose the part on the side of larger \(x\)? For a cantilever clamped at \(x = 0\) the end at \(x = L\) is free and has no support. The cut-off part therefore carries only known loads and we do not need the reactions of the clamp.

Cantilever. A cantilever of length \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), loaded at the free end \(x = L\) with a force \(500\) N downward, so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (0, -500, 0)\) N (section 5.1.3, figure 5.11). The part from the cut at \(x\) to the end has length \(L - x\). On it act the force of the cross section \(-\vek F(x) = (-N, -V, 0)\) and the force at the end \((0, -500, 0)\) N.

  1. Equilibrium of forces (2.2), component \(x\): \(-N = 0\), so \(N = 0\).
  2. Component \(y\): \(-V - 500 = 0\), so \(V = -500\) N, in every cross section.
  3. The moment of the force at the end about \(C(x)\): the vector from \(C(x)\) to the point of application is \((L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by (2.32) it is \((L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times(-500\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = -500\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) N m. The moment of the cross section is \(-\vek M(x) = (0, 0, -M)\).
  4. Equilibrium of moments (2.35), component \(z\): \(-M - 500\,(L - x) = 0\), so
    \[M(x) = -500\,(L - x)\ \text{N m} = -F\,(L - x) . \tag{5.6}\]

At the clamp \(M(0) = -500\) N m, at the free end \(M(L) = 0\). The shear force is negative because the part at the wall must hold the cut-off part up: the force of the cross section on it is \(-V = +500\) N, as figure 2.5 shows. The moment is negative, so the beam is stretched at the top, as in the test of the stress above. The stress with \(93{,}75\) MPa at the upper edge from the test therefore has exactly the moment that the cantilever carries at the clamp.

Simply supported beam with a force at the middle. A beam of length \(L = 2\) m lies on a fixed pin support at \(x = 0\) and on a roller support at \(x = L\) (definition 5.5, section 5.1.2). At the middle acts a force \(1\) kN downward, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (0, -1000, 0)\) N at \(x_0 = 1\) m. There are no horizontal loads, so by equilibrium of forces in the direction \(x\) the horizontal reaction of the fixed pin support is zero. The reactions are vertical, \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\) at \(x = 0\) and \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\) at \(x = L\), each in the direction \(+y\). From equilibrium of the whole beam, moments about \(x = 0\) (component \(z\)): \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\,L - 1000\cdot 1 = 0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = 500\) N, and from the forces \(R_{\mathrm{A}} + R_{\mathrm{B}} - 1000 = 0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{A}} = 500\) N. A reaction is an external force on the beam, like the force at the end of the cantilever.

The internal forces have different forms to the left and to the right of the force at the middle, since the cut-off part from the cut to the end contains the force at the middle only for \(x < 1\) m (figure 5.13).

  • A cut at \(x > 1\) m. The cut-off part, from \(x\) to \(L\), has only the reaction \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\). Forces: \(-V + R_{\mathrm{B}} = 0\), so \(V = 500\) N. Moments about \(C(x)\): \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = 0\), so \(M = 500\,(2 - x)\) N m.
  • A cut at \(x < 1\) m. The cut-off part contains also the force \(1\) kN at the middle. Forces: \(-V + R_{\mathrm{B}} - 1000 = 0\), so \(V = -500\) N. Moments: the force at the middle has the lever arm \((1 - x)\), and \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) - 1000\,(1 - x) = 0\), so \(M = 500\,(2 - x) - 1000\,(1 - x) = 500\,x\) N m.

The shear force is \(-500\) N on the left half and \(+500\) N on the right half, and the moment grows from zero at the pin to \(500\) N m at the middle and falls to zero at the roller support. The jump of the shear force at the force at the middle is \(500 - (-500) = 1000\) N, as much as the force, and the moment at \(x = 1\) m is continuous, \(500\cdot 1 = 500\cdot(2 - 1) = 500\) N m. Why this is so is shown by the lemma on the jump of the shear force in the section on a piece of a beam below. We get the same result with the part on the side of smaller \(x\), since the internal forces in the same cross section are the same. Then we would need the reaction \(R_{\mathrm{A}}\), and the force of the cross section on this part would be \(+\vek F(x)\) and not \(-\vek F(x)\). SADD (D.5) (PDF p. 576) gives for the same beam, with a force \(P\) at \(a\) and \(b = L - a\), \(V_{\mathrm{SADD}} = P\,b/L\) to the left of the force and \(M_{\mathrm{SADD}} = P\,b\,x/L\), which for \(P = 1000\) N, \(a = b = 1\) m, \(L = 2\) m is exactly our \(-V\) and \(M\).

Simply supported beam with a uniform load. We load the same beam with a uniformly distributed load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, pointing downward (definition 5.2). The force of the load on the part between \(x\) and \(L\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\,(L - x)\). Its moment about \(C(x)\) is the limit of the sums of the moments of the forces \(q(t_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) on the pieces of the segment, which lie at the distance \(t - x\) from \(C(x)\). Since \((t - x)\,q(t)\) is a continuous function, the sums are Riemann sums, and the moment is the integral

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,\mathrm{d}t = -q_0\,\frac{(L - x)^2}{2} , \tag{5.7}\]

in the direction \(z\). This is the moment of the force \(-q_0\,(L - x)\) that would act at the middle of the cut-off part, at the distance \((L - x)/2\) from \(C(x)\), since \(-q_0\,(L - x)\cdot (L - x)/2 = -q_0\,(L - x)^2/2\). The reactions are equal by symmetry, \(R_{\mathrm{A}} = R_{\mathrm{B}} = q_0\,L/2 = 1000\) N.

  1. Forces on the cut-off part from \(x\) to \(L\): \(-V + R_{\mathrm{B}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), so \(V = R_{\mathrm{B}} - q_0\,(L - x) = q_0\,(x - L/2)\).
  2. Moments about \(C(x)\), component \(z\): \(-M + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) - q_0\,(L - x)^2/2 = 0\), so
    \[M = \frac{q_0\,L}{2}\,(L - x) - \frac{q_0}{2}\,(L - x)^2 = \frac{q_0}{2}\,x\,(L - x) . \tag{5.8}\]

The shear force is a linear function (figure 5.14): \(V(0) = -1000\) N, \(V(L/2) = 0\), \(V(L) = +1000\) N. The moment is a parabola, zero at the supports, at \(x = 0{,}5\) m it is \(375\) N m. Where is it largest? We shall find out when we know the relation between \(M\) and \(V\) (example below). All three loads are chosen so that the largest moment is the same, \(500\) N m: a force \(500\) N on a cantilever of length \(1\) m, a force \(1\) kN at the middle of a beam of length \(2\) m (moment \(F\,L/4\)) and a load \(1\) kN/m on the same beam (moment \(q_0 L^2/8\)). This is a choice and not a law. It lets us compare later the stresses and displacements of the three beams at the same largest moment.

Self weight. A steel bar \(20\times 40\) mm has the cross-sectional area \(A = 8\cdot 10^{-4}\) m\(^2\), the density of steel is \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) and \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\). Its weight per unit length is the load \(q = -\rho g A = -61{,}21\) N/m, uniform and downward. For a cantilever of length \(1\) m the same procedure as for a uniform load gives \(V(x) = -61{,}21\,(L - x)\) N and \(M(x) = -61{,}21\,(L - x)^2/2\) N m. At the clamp \(V(0) = -61{,}21\) N and \(M(0) = -30{,}61\) N m, which is \(6{,}1\) % of the moment due to the force \(500\) N. A simply supported beam of length \(2\) m has \(M_{\max} = \rho g A\,L^2/8 = 30{,}61\) N m.

5.2.3 Equilibrium of a piece of a beam (ravnovesje kosa nosilca)

For the three beams we got \(V\) and \(M\) as functions of the place. For a uniform load \(V\) is linear and \(M\) a parabola, for a force at the middle \(V\) is constant on each half and \(M\) linear. Is the relation between them and the load the same for all? Let us differentiate. For a uniform load \(V' = q_0 = -q\) and \(M' = (q_0/2)\,(L - 2x) = -q_0\,(x - L/2) = -V\). For the cantilever with a force at the end \(V' = 0 = -q\), since between the clamp and the end there is no load, and \(M' = 500\) N \(= -V\). For a force at the middle on each half \(V' = 0\) and \(M' = \pm 500 = -V\) (the upper sign on the left, the lower on the right). So the pattern is \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). Why exactly this, each time with a minus? The answer is shown by the equilibrium of a short piece of a beam, which does not depend on the particular load. The approach is the same as for the axial force in lemma 4.75, only that now besides the axial force we have also the shear force and the moment.

We describe the load with a force per unit length that acts on the axis of the beam, \(\vek q(x) = (q_{\mathrm{x}}, q, q_{\mathrm{z}})\). The component \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the axial load (definition 4.74), \(q\) is the transverse load in the direction \(+y\) (definition 5.2), \(q_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the transverse load in the direction \(z\), defined in the same way. Each component is a continuous function of the place \(x\). A load can also act off the axis, for example at the upper edge. Then we replace it by a force on the axis and a distributed couple (porazdeljeni moment) \(\vek m(x)\), the moment of couples per unit length (definition 5.3). In our examples \(\vek m = \vek 0\). Let the load be continuous, and let there be no point forces and couples on the piece under consideration.

Lemma 5.10 Equilibrium of a piece of a beam (ravnovesje kosa nosilca)

Let the beam be in equilibrium and let the continuous distributed load \(\vek q\) and the distributed couple \(\vek m\) act on the piece between the cross sections \(x_1 < x_2\), without point forces and couples. The internal force \(\vek F\) and the internal moment \(\vek M\) (definitions 5.7 and 5.8) are then continuous functions on \([x_1, x_2]\) and

\[\vek F(x_2) - \vek F(x_1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{5.9}\]
\[\vek M(x_2) - \vek M(x_1) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \big(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(t) + \vek m(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{5.10}\]

In a plane problem, \(\vek F = (N, V, 0)\), \(\vek M = (0, 0, M)\), \(\vek q = (q_{\mathrm{x}}, q, 0)\), \(\vek m = (0, 0, m)\), we have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, 0, V)\) and

\[\begin{aligned} N(x_2) - N(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}t , \\ V(x_2) - V(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q\,\mathrm{d}t , \\ M(x_2) - M(x_1) &= -\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} \big(V(t) + m(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t . \end{aligned} \tag{5.11}\]
Proof

We write equilibrium of the piece, first the forces, then the moments about the centre of the left cross section. In the moments a term with \(\vek F(x_2)\) appears, which we transform into an integral with the derivative and the fundamental theorem of calculus.

  1. On the piece act: across the right face, with the outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force \(\vek F(x_2)\) and the moment \(\vek M(x_2)\) about \(C(x_2)\). Across the left face, with the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the traction vector is by lemma 2.21 the opposite one, so the force is \(-\vek F(x_1)\) and the moment \(-\vek M(x_1)\) about \(C(x_1)\). The load: the force \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\), by components by definitions 4.74 and 5.2, and the moment \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek m\,\mathrm{d}t\), by components by the definition of the distributed couple above.
  2. Equilibrium of forces (2.2): \(\vek F(x_2) - \vek F(x_1) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). This is (5.9).
  3. The moment of the forces on the right face about \(C(x_1)\) is the sum of the moments of the small forces \(\vek t\,\Delta A\). The vector from \(C(x_1)\) to the point of application is \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek r\), where \(\vek r\) is the vector from \(C(x_2)\) to the point of application. By items 2 and 3 of lemma 2.31 the sum of the moments is \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\sum\vek t\,\Delta A + \sum\vek r\times\vek t\,\Delta A\), in the limit \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \vek M(x_2)\). The limits exist by steps 2 and 4 of lemma 5.9 when the stresses are continuous.
  4. The small force of the load \(\vek q(t)\,\Delta t\) has the vector from \(C(x_1)\) to the point of application on the axis equal to \((t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The function \((t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\) is continuous, so the sums of the moments are Riemann sums and the moment of the load is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} (t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  5. Equilibrium of moments (2.35): \(\vek M(x_2) - \vek M(x_1) + (x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}(t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek m\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
  6. Steps 1 and 2 hold for every piece inside \([x_1, x_2]\). Let \(x \in [x_1, x_2]\). For the piece from \(x_1\) to \(x\) we get \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 2, each component is continuous on \([x_1, x_2]\) and differentiable with the derivative \(-\vek q\), so \(\vek F' = -\vek q\).
  7. By the product rule (lemma 1.81, items 3 and 4) \(\big((t - x_1)\,\vek F(t)\big)' = \vek F(t) - (t - x_1)\,\vek q(t)\), and this derivative is continuous. By the fundamental theorem, item 1, \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek F(x_2) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek F\,\mathrm{d}t - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}(t - x_1)\,\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  8. The vector product with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is linear (lemma 2.31, items 2 and 3) and the integral is linear (lemma 3.39, item 1), by components. Step 7 therefore gives \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + \int(t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q = \int\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F\,\mathrm{d}t\). We insert it into step 5 and get (5.10). The continuity of \(\vek M\) follows from (5.10) for \(x_1\) and \(x\) and from the fundamental theorem.
  9. In a plane problem by (2.32) \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (1, 0, 0)\times(N, V, 0) = (0\cdot 0 - 0\cdot V,\ 0\cdot N - 1\cdot 0,\ 1\cdot V - 0\cdot N) = (0, 0, V)\). The components of (5.9) and (5.10) are therefore (5.11).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Equilibrium of an element: EP (2.5.3)-(2.5.5) (p. 74), CM (7.3.25)-(7.3.27) (PDF p. 309), SADD (PDF p. 576), with the opposite sign for \(V\) and \(M\) in the first two, as described at the definitions.

The lemma says that between two cross sections the shear force changes by the load on this piece, and the moment by the area under \(V\). Let us test it on the beam with a uniform load, where \(V = q_0\,(x - L/2)\) and \(M = (q_0/2)\,x\,(L - x)\). On the piece from \(x_1 = 0{,}5\) m to \(x_2 = 1{,}5\) m \(V(1{,}5) - V(0{,}5) = 500 - (-500) = 1000\) N and \(-\int q\,\mathrm{d}t = q_0\cdot 1 = 1000\) N. The moment: \(M(1{,}5) - M(0{,}5) = 375 - 375 = 0\) and \(-\int_{0{,}5}^{1{,}5} q_0\,(t - 1)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), since the areas to the left and to the right of \(t = 1\) cancel. On the piece from \(x_1 = 0\) to \(x_2 = 1\) m \(M(1) - M(0) = 500\) N m and \(-\int_0^1 1000\,(t - 1)\,\mathrm{d}t = 500\) N m.

We do not need the piece to be arbitrarily long. When we shrink it, we get the derivative.

Corollary 5.11 Differential relations between the load, the shear force and the moment (diferencialne zveze)

Let \(\vek q\) and \(\vek m\) be continuous on an open interval \((a, b)\), without point forces and couples on it. Then \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) are differentiable on \((a, b)\) and

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}\vek F}{\mathrm{d}x} + \vek q = \vek 0 , \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\vek M}{\mathrm{d}x} + \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m = \vek 0 . \tag{5.12}\]

In a plane problem

\[N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad V' = -q , \qquad M' = -V - m . \tag{5.13}\]
Proof

We get the derivative from the lemma on a piece that we shrink.

  1. Let \(x \in (a, b)\) and \([x_1, x_2] \subset (a, b)\) a piece that contains \(x\). By lemma 5.10 \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 2, \(\vek F\) is differentiable with the derivative \(-\vek q\) (continuous).
  2. The function \(t \mapsto \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(t) + \vek m(t)\) is continuous, and by the lemma \(\vek M(x) = \vek M(x_1) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x}\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\). By the fundamental theorem \(\vek M' = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F - \vek m\).
  3. In a plane problem \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, 0, V)\) (step 9 of the lemma), which gives (5.13).

Source: The proof is not from a source. EP (2.5.3)-(2.5.5) (p. 74) and CM (7.3.25)-(7.3.27) (PDF p. 309) have \(N' = -f\), \(V' = -q\) and \(V = M'\) with their \(M = -M_{\mathrm{z}}\), which for us is \(M_{\mathrm{z}}' = -V_{\mathrm{y}}\). SADD (PDF p. 576) has \(V = M'\) with \(V_{\mathrm{SADD}} = -V_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Why does the relation \(M' = -V\) have a minus? Let us look at a short piece \([x, x + \Delta x]\) and a positive \(V\) on the right face (figure 5.15). The force \(V\) on the right face points up and has, about the centre of the left face, the lever arm \(\Delta x\) in the direction \(+x\). Its moment is \(\Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = V\,\Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so it rotates the piece counterclockwise. The piece is in equilibrium, so this rotation is balanced by the difference of the internal moments: \(M(x + \Delta x) - M(x) + V\,\Delta x \approx 0\). This is the reason for the minus. The moment is not constant by itself, but changes exactly by the moment of the shear force of the right face about the left one. For the cantilever: \(V = -500\) N, the moment grows from \(-500\) N m at the clamp to \(0\) at the end (figure 5.12), so \(M' = +500\) N \(= -V\).

For \(m = 0\) from (5.13) follows also \(M'' = -V' = q\). For a uniform load downward, \(q = -q_0\), we therefore have \(M'' = -q_0 < 0\) and \(M\) is a parabola with the peak up, as we got it. The moment is, up to a linear term, the double integral of the load. Later we shall link the moment also with how strongly the axis of the beam curves.

Where is the moment largest? For the beam with a uniform load \(M\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), so by theorem 1.73 it attains its largest value. This is not at the end points, where \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\), since \(M(0{,}5\ \text{m}) = 375\) N m. The largest value is therefore at an interior point \(x^*\), where \(M\) is differentiable, and by lemma 1.82 \(M'(x^*) = 0\). By (5.13) \(V(x^*) = 0\) there. The shear force \(V = q_0\,(x - L/2)\) is zero only at \(x^* = L/2 = 1\) m, where \(M = q_0 L^2/8 = 500\) N m. The largest moment is therefore where the shear force is zero. For the beam with a force at the middle \(V\) is nowhere zero and \(M\) has a peak at the force, where it is not differentiable.

What about a point force? The lemma on a piece requires that there be no point forces on the piece. For the beam with a force at the middle we saw that the shear force jumps at the force and that the moment is continuous. Why is shown by lemma 5.12.

Lemma 5.12 Jump of the shear force and the moment (skok prečne sile in momenta)

Let a point force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} = (F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, F_{\mathrm{z}})\) and a point couple with the moment \(\vek M_{\mathrm{p}}\) act at an interior point \(x_0\) of the axis, and let continuous \(\vek q\) and \(\vek m\) act on the intervals \([x_1, x_0)\) and \((x_0, x_2]\), which extend to continuous functions on \([x_1, x_0]\) and \([x_0, x_2]\), without other point loads. Then the left and right limits \(\vek F(x_0^{\pm})\) and \(\vek M(x_0^{\pm})\) exist and

\[\vek F(x_0^+) - \vek F(x_0^-) = -\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} , \qquad \vek M(x_0^+) - \vek M(x_0^-) = -\vek M_{\mathrm{p}} . \tag{5.14}\]

In a plane problem with a point force \(F_{\mathrm{p}}\) in the direction \(y\) and a couple with the moment \(M_0\) in the direction \(z\): \(V\) jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(M\) jumps by \(-M_0\). For a point force alone, without a couple, \(M\) is continuous.

Proof

On a shorter piece around \(x_0\) we write equilibrium with the point loads and let the piece shrink to the point.

  1. Existence of the limits. By lemma 5.10, applied to the piece \([x, x_2]\), for \(x_0 < x < x_2\) we have \(\vek F(x) = \vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). As \(x\) approaches \(x_0\) from the right, the right-hand side approaches, by the continuity of the integral (fundamental theorem, item 2, for \(\vek q\) extended by a continuous function on \([x_0, x_2]\)), \(\vek F(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_0}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t\). So \(\vek F(x_0^+)\) exists. In the same way, with the lemma on the piece \([x_1, x]\) for \(x_1 < x < x_0\), \(\vek F(x_0^-)\) exists. The function \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m\) therefore extends on each side to a continuous function on the closed interval. By (5.10) \(\vek M(x) = \vek M(x_2) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x_2}(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m)\,\mathrm{d}t\), so \(\vek M(x_0^{\pm})\) exist too.
  2. Let \(x_1 < x_0 < x_2\). Equilibrium of forces on the piece \([x_1, x_2]\), which contains the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\): \(\vek F(x_2) - \vek F(x_1) + \vek F_{\mathrm{p}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\vek q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). We split the integral at \(x_0\) into the integrals of the continuous extensions over \([x_1, x_0]\) and \([x_0, x_2]\) (item 2 of lemma 3.39). Each component of each is by item 3 of the same lemma, with the bounds \(-c\) and \(c\), at most \(c\,(x_2 - x_1)\) in absolute value, where \(c\) is the largest absolute value of the components of the extensions, and tends to zero. When \(x_1 \to x_0^-\) and \(x_2 \to x_0^+\), we get the first equation (5.14).
  3. Moments about \(C(x_1)\): as in step 5 of lemma 5.10 the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{p}}\) has the lever arm \((x_0 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the couple the moment \(\vek M_{\mathrm{p}}\) (the moment of a couple is the same about every point, lemma 5.4), so \(\vek M(x_2) - \vek M(x_1) + (x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2) + (x_0 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} + \vek M_{\mathrm{p}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2}\big((t - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek q + \vek m\big)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
  4. When \(x_1 \to x_0^-\) and \(x_2 \to x_0^+\): \(\vek F(x_2)\) is bounded by step 1, so \((x_2 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F(x_2)\) tends to zero, so does \((x_0 - x_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{p}} \to \vek 0\), and the integral tends to zero by item 3 of the lemma on the properties of the integral. What remains is \(\vek M(x_0^+) - \vek M(x_0^-) + \vek M_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek 0\), the second equation of (5.14).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The jump of the shear force at a point force is seen in SADD (D.5) (PDF p. 576), where \(V_{\mathrm{SADD}}\) for a force \(P\) jumps from \(P\,b/L\) to \(-P\,a/L\), that is by \(-P\), and \(V = -V_{\mathrm{SADD}}\) jumps by \(+P\), which for \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -P\) is exactly \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Let us test it on the beam with a force at the middle (figure 5.16). The force is \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N at \(x_0 = 1\) m, so \(V\) jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}} = +1000\) N, from \(-500\) N to \(+500\) N, as we computed. The jump is exactly such that a short piece around \(x_0\) is in equilibrium: the neighbouring parts act on it with \(-V(x_0^-) = +500\) N and \(V(x_0^+) = +500\) N, both upward, and the force \(1000\) N points downward. The moment is continuous, \(M(1^-) = M(1^+) = 500\) N m. The piece is short, so the lever arm of the forces at \(x_0\) about its centre is small, and the moment of the forces cannot jump if there is no couple.

What about a couple? The same simply supported beam, at the middle we load it with a couple with the moment \(M_0 = 100\) N m in the direction \(z\), counterclockwise, with no other loads. From equilibrium of the beam: \(R_{\mathrm{B}}\,L + M_0 = 0\) (component \(z\) about \(x = 0\)), so \(R_{\mathrm{B}} = -50\) N and \(R_{\mathrm{A}} = +50\) N. By the procedure with equilibrium of a part: \(V = -50\) N along the whole length. On the right half \(M = R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = -50\,(2 - x)\) N m, on the left, where the part contains the couple, \(M = M_0 + R_{\mathrm{B}}\,(L - x) = 50\,x\) N m. At \(x = 1\) m \(M(1^-) = 50\) N m and \(M(1^+) = -50\) N m. The jump is \(-100\) N m \(= -M_0\), as the lemma claims, and \(V\) is continuous. A couple therefore has a different effect from a force: a force shifts \(V\), a couple shifts \(M\).

The same reasoning gives the conditions at the ends of the beam. At the free end \(x = L\) act a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple with the moment \(M_0\). On the piece from \(x\) to \(L\) act, besides the force of the cross section \(-\vek F(x)\), these two as well, so \(V(x) = F_{\mathrm{y}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\,\mathrm{d}t\) and, when \(x \to L\), \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\). In the same way from the moments about \(C(x)\): \(M(x) = M_0 + (L - x)\,F_{\mathrm{y}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L (t - x)\,q\,\mathrm{d}t\), which tends to \(M_0\) as \(x \to L\). For the cantilever with a force \(500\) N downward \(V(L) = -500\) N \(= F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M(L) = 0 = M_0\), as we computed. At the clamped end \(x = 0\) the wall acts on the beam with the force \(-\vek F(0)\) and the moment \(-\vek M(0)\), since the outward normal of the beam there is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For the cantilever this is a force \(500\) N upward and the moment \(+500\) N m, counterclockwise, which holds the beam against the load at the end.
