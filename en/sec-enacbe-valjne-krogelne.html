Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.6 Stresses, strains and equilibrium in cylindrical and spherical coordinates (napetosti, deformacije in ravnotežje v valjnih in krogelnih koordinatah)

The tube of section 11.1 has an inner radius of 40 mm and an outer radius of 60 mm, and it holds oil at a pressure of 30 MPa. What are the stresses in its wall? The Cartesian components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are a poor tool for this. Take two points of the wall, \(A = (50, 0)\) and \(B = (0, 50)\) mm (figure 11.35). The tube is round, so the state is the same at both points: a stress that pulls along the wall in the circumferential direction and a stress that squeezes the wall in the radial direction. But at the first point the radius points along the axis \(x\), at the second along the axis \(y\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the radial stress at the first point and the circumferential stress at the second. The Cartesian components change from point to point, although the state is the same at all points on the same radius. So we need the components in directions that follow the body: in the radial direction, in the circumferential direction and along the axis.

The coordinates of section 11.5 give us such directions. Each point has there its own triple of perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\), the local frame (lokalna baza), which turns from point to point. In this section we answer three questions. What are the components of stress and strain in this frame? How do we compute strains from displacements in cylindrical and spherical coordinates, and why does one strain contain a term \(u_{\mathrm{r}}/r\) without a derivative? And what are the equilibrium equations, in which the term \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r\) appears? We work in two systems: cylindrical coordinates (valjne koordinate) \((r, \theta, z)\) and spherical coordinates (krogelne koordinate) \((R, \varphi, \theta)\) with the local frames \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek e_\theta, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}, \vek e_\varphi, \vek e_\theta\) (lemma 11.15).

11.6.1 Components in the local frame (komponente v lokalni bazi)

How do we measure the stress in the radial direction? By theorem 2.53 we know how the stress components change when the axes are rotated. The local system at a given point is such a rotated system, so its components need no new theory. They need only an agreement on what the indices mean.

Definition 11.19 Local components in orthogonal coordinates (krajevne komponente v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Let \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) be orthogonal coordinates (definition 11.13) and \(P\) a point of their domain, and let \(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3\) be the local frame at \(P\), that is, a right-handed perpendicular system with the same origin and the same unit of length as the Cartesian one. Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stress matrix, \(\ten\varepsilon\) the matrix of the small strain tensor and \(\vek u\) the displacement at the point \(P\) in Cartesian axes. The local components (local components) at \(P\) are the numbers

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\ten\varepsilon\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek u\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} , \tag{11.25}\]

where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(1, 2, 3\). Instead of numbers we write the names of the coordinates, \(r, \theta, z\) for cylindrical and \(R, \varphi, \theta\) for spherical ones, so for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\).

Source: SADD §2.7 (PDF p. 55) introduces the components of displacement and strain in cylindrical axes with (2.7.1), CM §3.5.4 (PDF p. 145) expands the tensors in dyadic form in the basis vectors of the coordinate system. The connection with the rotation of axes is ours.

What does this mean in terms of the earlier chapters? By definition 2.44 the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) from the Cartesian system to the local frame is the matrix whose row \(i\) holds the components of the vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\). By theorem 2.53 and lemma 2.55 the stress matrix in the local frame is \(\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\), and its element in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is exactly \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\). So the components (11.25) are the components in rotated Cartesian axes \(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3\), rotated as the point \(P\) requires. By definition 2.27, and because stress is symmetric (theorem 2.41), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is component \(j\) of the stress vector on the face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\). Hence \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) is the component \(\theta\) of the stress vector on the face that the coordinate surface \(r = \text{const.}\) has at the point \(P\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are the normal stresses on the faces with the three normals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek e_\theta\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

For the tube the components really do not change from point to point. The stresses that we will compute for the tube later in this chapter are, at the radius \(r = 50\) mm, the radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -10{,}56\) MPa, the circumferential stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 58{,}56\) MPa, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0\). Now we take these two numbers and look at what they give in Cartesian components (figure 11.36). The point with \(\theta = 0\) is \((50, 0)\) mm, there \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -10{,}56\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 58{,}56\) MPa. At the point with \(\theta = 90^\circ\), that is \((0, 50)\) mm, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 58{,}56\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -10{,}56\) MPa. At the point with \(\theta = 45^\circ\) we have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\) and \(\vek e_\theta = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)\). The stress matrix is \(\ten\sigma = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}^\T + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta\vek e_\theta^\T\). Because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) are perpendicular unit vectors, namely \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\vek e_\theta\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = 0\), and because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is perpendicular to both, these are by (11.25) the local components of this expression, and the matrix is determined by them, since the triple \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek e_\theta, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) of perpendicular unit vectors determines a rotation \(\mat Q\) and \(\ten\sigma = \mat Q^\T(\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T)\mat Q\). In components we therefore have

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \tfrac12(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}) = 24\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}) = -34{,}56\ \text{MPa} .\]

The same state is described at three points by three different triples of Cartesian components, but in the local frame by the same triple \((-10{,}56,\ 58{,}56,\ 0)\) MPa.

Why is this useful? For the tube, moving along the circumference or along the axis does not change the components in the local frame, while the Cartesian ones do change. For the sphere, they do not change when we move along a circle centred at the centre of the sphere. So in the next sections a single function of the radius, for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\), will describe the stress in the whole wall.

11.6.2 Strains in orthogonal coordinates (deformacije v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

What strains does a displacement in the radial direction cause? Take a ring of radius \(r\) and the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = c\), equal everywhere, so a ring that expands uniformly (figure 11.37). Both ends of a short fibre along the radius move by the same number \(c\), so the fibre keeps its length and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r = 0\). The arc of a fibre along the circumference between two radii whose angles differ by \(\Delta\theta\) was \(r\,\Delta\theta\) long and is now \((r + c)\,\Delta\theta\) long. It has lengthened by \(c\,\Delta\theta\), so relatively by \(c/r\). The growing ring is therefore stretched in the circumferential direction, although nothing stretches along the radius. The derivative \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r\) does not contain this strain. It arises because the length of an arc between two radii is proportional to \(r\).

What if the displacement is not the same everywhere? Before we compute this for arbitrary coordinates, let us do the computation on a figure, in the plane \(r\)-\(\theta\) and on two short fibres from the same point \(A\) (figure 11.38). The fibre \(AB\) lies along the radius, the fibre \(AD\) along an arc of the circle about the axis. Before loading they are grey dashed, after it orange, the differences are blue dimensions with end bars, and the displacements are greatly enlarged. We compute with numbers next to the figure. Let the point \(A\) lie on the radius \(r = 50\) mm, let the fibre \(AB\) be \(\Delta r = 2\) mm long, and let the arc \(AD\) belong to the angle \(\Delta\theta = 0{,}04\), so it is \(r\,\Delta\theta = 2\) mm long. Let the displacements of the three points in millimetres be

point \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) \(u_\theta\)

\(B\) \(0{,}1080\) \(0{,}0560\)
\(D\) \(0{,}1024\) \(0{,}0516\)

The fibre along the radius. Panel (a) shows the ray through \(A\), drawn horizontally, so that \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) points to the right and \(\vek e_\theta\) upwards. Both ends of the fibre lie on the same ray, so they have the same local frame and we may compare their displacements component by component, as in Cartesian axes. The fibre \(AB\) before loading is \(\Delta r = 2\) mm long. The point \(A\) moves along the radius by \(u_{\mathrm{r}}^A = 0{,}1000\) mm, the point \(B\) by \(u_{\mathrm{r}}^B = 0{,}1080\) mm. Across the radius they move by \(u_\theta^A = 0{,}0500\) mm and \(u_\theta^B = 0{,}0560\) mm. The fibre after loading \(A'B'\) therefore measures \(\Delta r + u_{\mathrm{r}}^B - u_{\mathrm{r}}^A = 2{,}0080\) mm along the radius. It has lengthened by the difference of the radial displacements of its ends, \(0{,}0080\) mm, so relatively by

\[\frac{u_{\mathrm{r}}^B - u_{\mathrm{r}}^A}{\Delta r} = \frac{0{,}0080}{2} = 0{,}0040 , \qquad \text{and in the limit } \Delta r \to 0 \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} .\]

Here the assumption of small rotations enters. The transverse displacements of the ends differ by \(\Delta u_\theta = u_\theta^B - u_\theta^A = 0{,}0060\) mm, measured from the parallel to the ray through \(A'\), so the fibre after loading is oblique and its true length is \(\sqrt{2{,}0080^2 + 0{,}0060^2} = 2{,}008009\) mm. The strain is \(0{,}0040045\) instead of \(0{,}0040\). The difference \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is half the square of the slope \(0{,}0060/2 = 0{,}003\), and for slopes below one hundredth it is at least twenty thousand times smaller than one. We neglect it for the same reason as the square of the shift in the sheared cube of section 1.6.2. At a slope of \(0{,}1\) it would be \(0{,}005\), more than the strain itself, and there the formulas of this section no longer hold. The same slope is the angle by which the fibre turns counterclockwise, since for a small angle the slope equals the angle in radians (section 1.17.4):

\[\alpha = \frac{\Delta u_\theta}{\Delta r} = \frac{0{,}0060}{2} = 0{,}0030 , \qquad \text{and in the limit} \qquad \alpha = \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r} .\]

In the figure this is the angle \(\alpha\) between the parallel to the ray and the fibre \(A'B'\). We will need it for the shear.

The fibre along the arc. Panel (b) shows the axis \(O\), the radii through \(A\) and \(D\), which enclose the angle \(\Delta\theta\), and the circle of radius \(r\). The fibre \(AD\) before loading is an arc of this circle, \(r\,\Delta\theta = 2\) mm long. We reach the position after loading in three steps. In the first we move both ends along the radius by \(u_{\mathrm{r}}^A = 0{,}1000\) mm, onto the circle of radius \(r + u_{\mathrm{r}}^A\). The arc between the same two radii is there \((r + u_{\mathrm{r}}^A)\,\Delta\theta = 50{,}1 \cdot 0{,}04 = 2{,}0040\) mm long. This is the expanded ring of figure 11.37. In the second step the ends slide along this circle, the point \(A\) by \(u_\theta^A = 0{,}0500\) mm and the point \(D\) by \(u_\theta^D = 0{,}0516\) mm, so \(D\) by \(\Delta u_\theta = 0{,}0016\) mm further. The common slide does not lengthen the fibre, but the difference of the slides lengthens it by this piece. In the third step the point \(D\) moves away from the circle by a further \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{r}} = u_{\mathrm{r}}^D - u_{\mathrm{r}}^A = 0{,}0024\) mm. This tilts the fibre but, as for the radial fibre, does not change its length to first approximation. The fibre after loading \(A'D'\) is therefore \(2{,}0040 + 0{,}0016 = 2{,}0056\) mm long and its strain is

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac{(r + u_{\mathrm{r}}^A)\,\Delta\theta + \Delta u_\theta - r\,\Delta\theta}{r\,\Delta\theta} = \frac{u_{\mathrm{r}}^A}{r} + \frac1r\,\frac{\Delta u_\theta}{\Delta\theta} \\ &\quad = 0{,}0020 + 0{,}0008 = 0{,}0028 , \\ &\text{and in the limit } \Delta\theta \to 0 \qquad \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \frac1r\Big(u_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta}\Big) . \end{aligned}\]

The first term is the stretch of a ring that grows, the second is the stretch we know from the straight bar: the ends move differently along the fibre. Here too we neglected something. The displacement \(u_\theta\) is a step straight along \(\vek e_\theta\), but we drew it along the circle. The difference is \(u_\theta^2/(2r) = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) mm, which is \(0{,}05\,\%\) of the step. The exact computation of the arc length (NumPy) gives \(0{,}002824\) for our element and \(0{,}002802\) for an element ten times smaller. The remainder is proportional to \(\Delta\theta\), because \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) grows along the arc, and it vanishes in the limit.

The right angle between the fibres. Local components are components in rotated Cartesian axes (definition 11.19), so what we know from section 1.17.4 holds for them: \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) is the angle by which the right angle between the fibres along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) decreases (figure 1.416). Panel (c) shows the fibres \(AB\) and \(AD\) with the right angle at \(A\) and the same fibres after loading, drawn from the same corner. Three rotations change the angle.

  1. The radial fibre turns by \(\alpha = \partial u_\theta/\partial r = 0{,}0030\) towards the fibre \(AD\), as we computed on panel (a). The right angle decreases by \(\alpha\).
  2. The arc fibre slides along the circle and turns with it. The radius through \(A'\) encloses with the radius through \(A\) the angle \(\beta = u_\theta^A/r = 0{,}0500/50 = 0{,}0010\), since an angle in radians is the arc divided by the radius. The tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at every point, so it turns by the same angle: the grey dashed line is the tangent at \(A'\), turned by \(\beta\) counterclockwise, so away from the fibre \(AB\). The right angle increases by \(\beta\).
  3. The end \(D\) moves away from the axis by \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}0024\) mm more than \(A\), so the fibre tilts from the tangent back towards \(AB\) by \(\gamma = \Delta u_{\mathrm{r}}/(r\,\Delta\theta) = 0{,}0024/2 = 0{,}0012\), in the limit by \(\tfrac1r\,\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta\). The right angle decreases by \(\gamma\).

The angle between the fibres after loading is \(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - (\alpha - \beta + \gamma)\), written in the figure as \(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\), so

\[2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \alpha - \beta + \gamma = \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r} - \frac{u_\theta}{r} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} = 0{,}0030 - 0{,}0010 + 0{,}0012 = 0{,}0032 .\]

The exact angle between the fibres \(A'B'\) and \(A'D'\) (NumPy) decreases by \(0{,}00317\) for our element and by \(0{,}003187\) for an element a hundred times smaller. The remaining \(0{,}4\,\%\) are products of strains, which small strain neglects. What is the term \(-u_\theta/r\) for? Without it the formula would ascribe a shear to a rigid rotation too. In a rotation of the body about the axis by the angle \(\omega\) we have \(u_\theta = \omega r\), the radial fibre turns by \(\alpha = \omega\) and the arc fibre with the circle by \(\beta = \omega r/r = \omega\), both in the same sense. The right angle stays right only if we subtract \(\beta\).

On the figure we have thus obtained all three strains in the plane: the stretch of the radial fibre, the stretch of the arc fibre and the decrease of the right angle. In the lectures the angle is denoted by \(\vartheta\) and the positions after loading by \(\tilde A\), \(\tilde B\), \(\tilde D\), but the computations are the same. The figure does not cover the direction \(z\) and does not say what happens in other orthogonal coordinates. For that we need a computation that holds in general.

We make the same computation for an arbitrary displacement field in arbitrary orthogonal coordinates. The derivatives of the displacement components with respect to the coordinates are not enough, because the local frame also changes when we move along the coordinates: in figure 11.38 these are the turn of the radius by \(\beta\) and the longer arc at the larger radius. The derivation will keep to this.

Figure 11.39 shows how the frame changes when we move along the circumference. The centre \(O\) and two radii at the angles \(\theta\) and \(\theta + \Delta\theta\) bound an arc of the circle of length \(r\,\Delta\theta\): for a step \(\Delta\theta\) we move by \(h_\theta\,\Delta\theta\) with \(h_\theta = r\). The frame at the first point has \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) along the radius and \(\vek e_\theta\) perpendicular to it. The frame at the second point is turned by the angle \(\Delta\theta\), because the radius turns by that much. So \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) changes by \(\Delta\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), a vector of length \(\Delta\theta\) along \(\vek e_\theta\), that is \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = \vek e_\theta\), and \(\vek e_\theta\) by \(\Delta\vek e_\theta\), a vector of length \(\Delta\theta\) along \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Theorem 11.20 Strains in orthogonal coordinates (deformacije v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Let \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) be orthogonal coordinates (definition 11.13) with scale factors \(h_1, h_2, h_3\) and local frame \(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3\). Let \(\vek u\) be a displacement field that is continuously differentiable at the points of the domain, and \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek u\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) its local components, written as functions of the coordinates. Then the local components of the small strain tensor are

\[\begin{aligned} &\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\Big[\frac{1}{h_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{h_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big] , \\ &\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\quad + u_{\mathrm{k}}\,\frac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.26}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\ten\varepsilon\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) equals the expression in (11.26). We write the strain matrix with the displacement gradient, and the columns of the gradient with derivatives with respect to the coordinates.

  1. By definition 1.121, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\). By definition 11.19, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat H^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\). By equation (1.42) (point 4 of lemma 1.102), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat H^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{l}}(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\mat H^\T)_{\mathrm{kl}}\,(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{l}}\), and since \((\mat H^\T)_{\mathrm{kl}} = H_{\mathrm{lk}}\), this is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{l}}(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{l}}\,H_{\mathrm{lk}}\,(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\). Hence \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\cdot\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\).
  2. By point 2 of lemma 11.16, \(\mat H\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \dfrac{1}{h_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\), and likewise for the index \(i\). Inserting into step 1 gives the first equality in (11.26).
  3. We expand the displacement in the local frame, \(\vek u = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} u_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) are functions of the coordinates. The partial derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the ordinary derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\) with the other coordinates held constant (definition 1.26), so by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, points 1 and 3), applied to each Cartesian component of the sum, \(\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}} + u_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dfrac{\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big)\). This is the second equality, and its second term is the change of the frame when we move along \(q_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Source: SADD (2.7.1)-(2.7.3) (PDF p. 55), CM §3.5.4 (PDF p. 145-146). SADD expresses the gradient with the operator \(\nabla\) in coordinates and takes the derivatives of the basis vectors from (1.9.10) and (1.9.17). Our proof uses only lemma 11.16 and the product rule.

The theorem gives the strains if we know the derivatives of the local frame. For cylindrical and spherical coordinates the table of lemma 11.15 gives them, so we derive the formulas by the same procedure: first the derivative of the displacement with respect to each coordinate in the local frame, then an element of the gradient, then the symmetric part.

Corollary 11.21 Strains in cylindrical coordinates (deformacije v valjnih koordinatah)

Let \(\vek u\) be a continuously differentiable displacement field in the domain of cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\). Then the local components of the small strain tensor (in the plane \(r\)-\(\theta\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the stretch of the fibre along the radius, \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) the stretch of the fibre along the arc and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) half the decrease of the right angle) are

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac1r\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r} - \frac{u_\theta}{r}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} &= \frac1r\Big(u_{\mathrm{r}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\theta \mathrm{z}} &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial z} \\ &\quad + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rz}} &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial z} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial r}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.27}\]
Proof

We must show that (11.27) follows from theorem 11.20 for the table of lemma 11.15.

  1. By lemma 11.15, \(h_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\), \(h_\theta = r\), \(h_{\mathrm{z}} = 1\), \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = \vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and all other derivatives of the local frame are zero.
  2. By the second equality of (11.26), inserting the derivatives of step 1,
    \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial r} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r}\,\vek e_\theta \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial r}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\theta} &= \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad - u_\theta\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta} + u_{\mathrm{r}}\Big)\vek e_\theta \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial z} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial z}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial z}\,\vek e_\theta \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned}\]
    In the derivative with respect to \(\theta\), the term \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = u_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek e_\theta\) contributes to the component \(\theta\) and \(u_\theta\,\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -u_\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) to the component \(r\). The first is the longer arc at the larger radius, the second the turn of the arc fibre with the circle, which we saw in figure 11.38.
  3. Let \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = \dfrac{1}{h_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\), the local components of the displacement gradient. From step 2 and \(h_\theta = r\), in the columns \(r\), \(\theta\), \(z\),
    \[\begin{bmatrix} A_{\mathrm{rr}} & A_{\mathrm{r}\theta} & A_{\mathrm{rz}} \\ A_{\theta \mathrm{r}} & A_{\theta\theta} & A_{\theta \mathrm{z}} \\ A_{\mathrm{zr}} & A_{\mathrm{z}\theta} & A_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} & \dfrac1r\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} - u_\theta\Big) & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r} & \dfrac1r\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta} + u_{\mathrm{r}}\Big) & \dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial r} & \dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} \end{bmatrix} .\]
  4. By theorem 11.20, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + A_{\mathrm{ji}})\). The diagonal gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and off the diagonal \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{r}\theta} + A_{\theta \mathrm{r}})\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = \tfrac12(A_{\theta \mathrm{z}} + A_{\mathrm{z}\theta})\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rz}} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{rz}} + A_{\mathrm{zr}})\), which is (11.27).

Source: SADD (2.7.3) (PDF p. 55), App. A (A.2) (PDF p. 546), CM (3.5.26) (PDF p. 146). The formulas of the three sources agree. SADD writes the strains as \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the last shear component as \(e_{\mathrm{zr}}\), CM writes \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{z}\theta}\). The derivation from theorem 11.20 is ours, and the formulas are also verified with SymPy: the derivation from the Cartesian expressions by the change of variables gives zero difference for all six.

The angle \(\varphi\) is measured from the axis \(z\) to the radius, the angle \(\theta\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) from the axis \(x\) to the projection of the radius. When we increase \(R\), the point moves along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\). When we increase \(\varphi\), it moves along \(\vek e_\varphi\) on the meridian, of length \(R\,\Delta\varphi\), so \(h_\varphi = R\). When we increase \(\theta\), it moves along \(\vek e_\theta\) on the parallel, which has the radius \(R\sin\varphi\), so \(h_\theta = R\sin\varphi\). Along the parallel the frame turns about the axis \(z\), so \(\vek e_\theta\) changes towards the axis: its derivative \(-\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} - \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi\) is a horizontal arrow towards the axis, resolved into the components along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and \(\vek e_\varphi\).

Corollary 11.22 Strains in spherical coordinates (deformacije v krogelnih koordinatah)

Let \(\vek u\) be a continuously differentiable displacement field in the domain of spherical coordinates \((R, \varphi, \theta)\). Then the local components of the small strain tensor (for spherical coordinates, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}}\) is the stretch of the fibre along the radius, \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi}\) the stretch of the fibre along the meridian and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) the stretch of the fibre along the parallel) are

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial R} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac1R\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\varphi} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial R} - \frac{u_\varphi}{R}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} &= \frac1R\Big(u_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\varphi}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{R}\theta} &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial R} - \frac{u_\theta}{R}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} &= \frac1R\Big(u_{\mathrm{R}} + u_\varphi\cot\varphi \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\varphi\theta} &= \frac{1}{2R}\Big(\frac{1}{\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\varphi} - u_\theta\cot\varphi\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.28}\]
Proof

We must show that (11.28) follows from theorem 11.20 for the table of lemma 11.15. The procedure is the same as in corollary 11.21, only there are more derivatives of the local frame.

  1. By lemma 11.15, \(h_{\mathrm{R}} = 1\), \(h_\varphi = R\), \(h_\theta = R\sin\varphi\) and \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\varphi = \vek e_\varphi\), \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta = \sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\varphi = -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\theta = \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} - \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi\). The other derivatives are zero.
  2. By the second equality of (11.26),
    \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial R} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial R}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial R}\,\vek e_\varphi \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial R}\,\vek e_\theta , \\ \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\varphi} &= \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\varphi} \\ &\quad - u_\varphi\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} + u_{\mathrm{R}}\Big)\vek e_\varphi \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\varphi}\,\vek e_\theta , \\ \frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\theta} &= \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad - \sin\varphi\,u_\theta\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad - \cos\varphi\,u_\theta\Big)\vek e_\varphi \\ &\quad + \Big(\frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \sin\varphi\,u_{\mathrm{R}} \\ &\quad + \cos\varphi\,u_\varphi\Big)\vek e_\theta . \end{aligned}\]
    In the derivative with respect to \(\theta\), where the frame turns about the axis \(z\), the term \(u_{\mathrm{R}}\sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\) comes from \(u_{\mathrm{R}}\,\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta\), the term \(u_\varphi\cos\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\) from \(u_\varphi\,\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\theta\), and the terms \(-u_\theta\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and \(-u_\theta\cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi\) from \(u_\theta\,\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta\).
  3. The elements \(A_{\mathrm{ij}} = \dfrac{1}{h_{\mathrm{j}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\) are, in the columns \(R\), \(\varphi\), \(\theta\) (with \(h_\varphi = R\) and \(h_\theta = R\sin\varphi\)),
    \[\begin{bmatrix} A_{\mathrm{RR}} & A_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} & A_{\mathrm{R}\theta} \\ A_{\varphi \mathrm{R}} & A_{\varphi\varphi} & A_{\varphi\theta} \\ A_{\theta \mathrm{R}} & A_{\theta\varphi} & A_{\theta\theta} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial R} & \dfrac{1}{R}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\varphi} - u_\varphi\Big) & \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial\theta} - \sin\varphi\,u_\theta\Big) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial R} & \dfrac{1}{R}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} + u_{\mathrm{R}}\Big) & \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\theta} - \cos\varphi\,u_\theta\Big) \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial R} & \dfrac{1}{R}\,\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\varphi} & \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \sin\varphi\,u_{\mathrm{R}} + \cos\varphi\,u_\varphi\Big) \end{bmatrix} .\]
  4. By theorem 11.20, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{ij}} + A_{\mathrm{ji}})\). The diagonal gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) (in the last one \(\sin\varphi\,u_{\mathrm{R}}/(R\sin\varphi) = u_{\mathrm{R}}/R\) and \(\cos\varphi\,u_\varphi/(R\sin\varphi) = u_\varphi\cot\varphi/R\)). Off the diagonal, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} + A_{\varphi \mathrm{R}})\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{R}\theta} = \tfrac12(A_{\mathrm{R}\theta} + A_{\theta \mathrm{R}})\) and \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\theta} = \tfrac12(A_{\varphi\theta} + A_{\theta\varphi})\), where the last one contains \(\dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_\varphi}{\partial\theta} - \cos\varphi\,u_\theta\Big) + \dfrac1R\,\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\varphi}\), and after factoring out \(1/R\) this is (11.28).

Source: SADD (2.7.5) (PDF p. 56), App. A (A.3) (PDF p. 546). Both agree with our formulas, SADD writes the last two shear components as \(e_{\varphi\theta}\) and \(e_{\theta \mathrm{R}}\). The table of derivatives of the local frame is not from the source (lemma 11.15), so the derivation is not from the source. The formulas are also verified with SymPy: the derivation from the Cartesian expressions by the change of variables gives zero difference for a general displacement field for all six.

How do we test this? In both corollaries there appear terms without derivatives of the displacement components, which do not exist in Cartesian coordinates. SADD points out one of them next to (2.7.5): in spherical coordinates a uniform displacement \(u_{\mathrm{R}}\) causes the transverse strains \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u_{\mathrm{R}}/R\), which we can picture as blowing up a balloon, where the points on its surface move apart (SADD, PDF p. 56), just as the arc of the ring lengthens when the ring grows. So the term without a derivative appears because of the curvature of the coordinate system. The stretch that it measures, however, is a real stretch of the material, since the points of the balloon really move apart. Let us look at three examples with the ring of chapter 1.

The expanded ring. Take \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = c\) and \(u_\theta = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) with \(c = 10\) µm. By (11.27), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial c/\partial r = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = c/r\) and the other components are zero. At \(r = 40\) mm, \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = 2{,}500 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and at \(r = 60\) mm it is \(1{,}667 \cdot 10^{-4}\). This is the same as the geometric computation at the start of the section: the circumference \(2\pi r\) lengthens by \(2\pi c\), relatively by \(c/r\). We check also with a Cartesian computation for the field \(\vek u = c\,(x, y)/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) (SymPy): at the point \((40, 0)\) mm the only nonzero strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = c/40\) and at the point \((0, 60)\) mm it is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c/60\). The circumferential direction is \(y\) at the first point and \(x\) at the second, as it must be.

The rigid rotation. Take \(u_\theta = \omega\,r\) with a small \(\omega\) and \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so we move each point along the circumference in proportion to its distance from the axis, like a rigid rotation about the axis \(z\) by the angle \(\omega\). By (11.27), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac1r\,\partial(\omega r)/\partial\theta = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\big(0 + \partial(\omega r)/\partial r - \omega r/r\big) = \tfrac12(\omega - \omega) = 0\) and all the other strains are zero. The rotation really does not deform the body, as theorem 1.140 says, since in Cartesian axes this field is \(\vek u = \omega\,(-y, x, 0)\) with a constant antisymmetric matrix. The terms \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r\) and \(-u_\theta/r\) cancel: they are the turn of the radial fibre \(\alpha\) and the turn of the arc fibre with the circle \(\beta\), in a rigid rotation both equal to \(\omega\). So the formula for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) correctly subtracts the term due to the change of direction of the local frame.

The ring of the chapter on strain. Section 1.18.5 prescribed the strain of the ring with radii 40 and 60 mm in words: the fibres along the ring shorten by one thousandth, the fibres towards the centre keep their length, and the right angles between the two kinds of fibres stay right. In local components this is \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = -k\) with \(k = 10^{-3}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\), so in one line. A field that gives it is \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and \(u_\theta = -k\,r\,\theta\) (we have cut the ring, so that the angle \(\theta\) runs over \(-\pi < \theta < \pi\), like a fan that we close): by (11.27), indeed \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac1r\,\partial(-k r\theta)/\partial\theta = -k\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\big(\partial(-k r\theta)/\partial r - (-k r\theta)/r\big) = \tfrac12(-k\theta + k\theta) = 0\). But the displacement \(u_\theta\) has a jump at the cut: \(u_\theta(\pi) - u_\theta(-\pi) = -2\pi k\,r\), which is \(-0{,}2513\) mm at \(r = 40\) mm and \(-0{,}3770\) mm at \(r = 60\) mm. So the ring of chapter 1 has strains that satisfy the compatibility equations, but the displacement is not a continuous function on the whole ring. We will meet this jump again later in this chapter, in the stress function in polar coordinates, and already in the next part in the compatibility equation.

11.6.3 Compatibility in polar coordinates (kompatibilnost v polarnih koordinatah)

In the plane we have two displacements, \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\), and from them three strains, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\). So we cannot choose the three functions freely: a relation holds between them, the compatibility equation, which we met in Cartesian coordinates in chapter 1 (theorem 1.133). What is it in polar coordinates? Let us first look at the case in which we see it on a figure: axial symmetry, that is the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u(r)\) and \(u_\theta = 0\), the same all around the circumference (figure 11.41).

The ring of radius \(r\) expands into the orange ring, by the displacement \(u(r)\). Its circumferential strain is, by the start of the section, \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\), and we can read this the other way too: whoever knows the circumferential strain knows the displacement, \(u = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\). The same holds for the neighbouring ring of radius \(r + \Delta r\), which expands into its own orange ring by the displacement \(u(r + \Delta r)\). The radial fibre between the rings, \(\Delta r\) long before loading, must after loading fit exactly into the space between the orange rings, so it is \(\Delta r + u(r + \Delta r) - u(r)\) long. Its strain is \(\big(u(r + \Delta r) - u(r)\big)/\Delta r\), so in the limit \(\Delta r \to 0\) the derivative \(u'\). Because \(u = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\), the radial strain is no longer free:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\big(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\big) . \tag{11.29}\]

Let us test with numbers. Take \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = a + b/r^2\) with \(a = 1 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(b = 0{,}25\) mm\(^2\) on the wall of the tube between the radii 40 and 60 mm. Then the displacement is \(u = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = a\,r + b/r\) and equation (11.29) requires \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = a - b/r^2\).

\(r\) (mm) \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) \(u = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) (mm) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\)
\(40\) \(2{,}563 \cdot 10^{-4}\) \(0{,}01025\) \(-0{,}563 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
\(50\) \(2{,}000 \cdot 10^{-4}\) \(0{,}01000\) \(0\)
\(60\) \(1{,}694 \cdot 10^{-4}\) \(0{,}01017\) \(0{,}306 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

At \(r = 50\) mm the radial strain is zero although the circumferential one is \(2 \cdot 10^{-4}\): there the displacement \(u\) has its smallest value, the neighbouring rings move equally and the fibre between them keeps its length. And what if, with the same circumferential strain, we prescribed an equal radial one, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = a + b/r^2\), as in a uniform inflation? Between the rings of radii 50 and 52 mm the space grows by \(u(52) - u(50) = 0{,}0100077 - 0{,}0100000 = 7{,}7 \cdot 10^{-6}\) mm. A fibre 2 mm long, however, with a strain of about \(1{,}96 \cdot 10^{-4}\) would want to lengthen by \(3{,}9 \cdot 10^{-4}\) mm, fifty-one times as much. Such a fibre reaches beyond the outer ring, and no axisymmetric displacement field gives such a pair of strains.

Without symmetry the relation is longer, but the idea is the same: from the three equations for the strains we eliminate both displacements.

Theorem 11.23 Compatibility equation in polar coordinates (enačba kompatibilnosti v polarnih koordinatah)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\) be functions of the coordinates \(r > 0\) and \(\theta\) with continuous partial derivatives up to the third order on an open set \(D\), and let \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) (a plane displacement field). Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) be the strains of corollary 11.21. Then at every point of the set \(D\)

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial\theta^2} + \frac2r\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r} - \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r} \\ &\quad = 2\,\Big(\frac1r\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta}\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{11.30}\]

If in addition \(u_\theta = 0\) at all points of the set \(D\), then also \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})/\partial r\), which for strains that depend only on \(r\) is equation (11.29).

Proof

We must show that from the three equations (11.27) for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) we eliminate \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\) by differentiation. The procedure is the same as in Cartesian coordinates: we differentiate so that the displacements appear only in mixed derivatives, which we may interchange.

  1. Starting point: the three strains of the plane. By (11.27) with \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r}\), \(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u_{\mathrm{r}} + \dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta}\) and \(2r\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} + r\,\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial r} - u_\theta\), where we multiplied the second equation by \(r\) and the third by \(2r\).
  2. Interchanging derivatives. The functions \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\) and their first derivatives have continuous mixed derivatives, so by theorem 1.131, applied to the variables \(r\) and \(\theta\), the order of differentiation with respect to \(r\) and \(\theta\) does not matter.
  3. We differentiate the second equation of step 1 with respect to \(r\): \(\dfrac{\partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{\partial r} = \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} + \dfrac{\partial^2 u_\theta}{\partial r\,\partial\theta}\). The first term on the right is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), so
    \[\frac{\partial^2 u_\theta}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} = \frac{\partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{\partial r} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} .\]
    For \(u_\theta = 0\) the left side is zero and we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})/\partial r\), the last claim of the theorem.
  4. We differentiate the third equation of step 1 with respect to \(\theta\): \(\dfrac{\partial(2r\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta})}{\partial\theta} = \dfrac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta^2} + r\,\dfrac{\partial^2 u_\theta}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} - \dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta}\). Into it we insert the mixed derivative of step 3 and \(\dfrac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial\theta} = r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} - u_{\mathrm{r}}\) of step 1, and collect the displacements on the left:
    \[\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta^2} + u_{\mathrm{r}} = 2r\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - r\,\frac{\partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{\partial r} + r\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} .\]
    The displacement \(u_\theta\) is eliminated.
  5. We differentiate the equation of step 4 with respect to \(r\). On the left, by step 2, \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\dfrac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta^2} + \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} = \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} + \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} = \dfrac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial\theta^2} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\). The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) is eliminated too.
  6. We differentiate the right side with respect to \(r\) term by term with the product rule (lemma 1.82, point 3). The first term gives \(2\,\dfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} + 2r\,\dfrac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r\,\partial\theta}\). Because \(\dfrac{\partial(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{\partial r} = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + r\,\dfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}\), the second term gives \(-\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} - 3r\,\dfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r} - r^2\,\dfrac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r^2}\). The third gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + r\,\dfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r}\) and the fourth \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + r\,\dfrac{\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}\).
  7. We equate the left side of step 5 with the sum of step 6. The term \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is on both sides, the terms \(-\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(+\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) cancel, and the terms with \(\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}/\partial r\) give \(-2r\,\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}/\partial r\):
    \[\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial\theta^2} = 2\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} + 2r\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} - 2r\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r} - r^2\,\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r^2} + r\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r} .\]
  8. We move the terms with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) to the left and divide by \(r^2 > 0\). We get (11.30).

Source: SADD Exercise 7.17 (PDF p. 165) gives the equation without derivation in the form \(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(2r\,\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - r^2\,\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}\big) + r\,\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial\theta^2} = 0\), which is the equation of step 7 of our proof. The axisymmetric form (11.29) is SADD Exercise 7.18 (PDF p. 165) and SADD (8.4.76) (PDF p. 220), where SADD turns it into an equation for the stresses (8.4.78) with Hooke's law. BARBER §7.4.1 (PDF p. 115) refers the reader to another book, SADD (7.6.6) (PDF p. 161) is the form in stresses. The derivation is ours. Checked with SymPy: for general functions \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(u_\theta\) the difference of the left and right sides is zero, and with the second derivative \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/\partial\theta^2\) in the last term it is not zero. For any three functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) the difference of the left and right sides equals the difference of the left and right sides of equation (1) in (1.76), into which we insert the Cartesian components of the same tensor.

The proof showed that the equation is a consequence of the existence of displacements. The last sentence of the source says more: equation (11.30) is the same requirement as the first equation in (1.76), only written with local components. So everything we know about sufficiency holds for it too. In a body without holes, strains that satisfy it belong to some displacement field (theorem 4.120), but in a body with a hole not necessarily.

Axial symmetry shows what this difference means. Let the strains depend only on \(r\) and let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). Then in (11.30) all derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) drop out and there remains \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^2\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\mathrm{d}r^2} + \dfrac2r\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\mathrm{d}r} - \dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\mathrm{d}r} = 0\), which is

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\Big(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})}{\mathrm{d}r}\Big) = 0 ,\]

since the derivative of the bracket equals \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\mathrm{d}r} - 2\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\mathrm{d}r} - r\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^2\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}}{\mathrm{d}r^2}\), that is the left side multiplied by \(-r\). So the general equation requires only that the bracket be constant, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} - \mathrm{d}(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta})/\mathrm{d}r = C\), and not that it be zero. What is an example with \(C \neq 0\)? The ring of the chapter on strain, the third example above: for \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = -k\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) we have \(C = 0 - (-k) = k = 10^{-3}\), and its displacement \(u_\theta = -k\,r\,\theta\) has the jump \(-2\pi k\,r\) at the cut. The constant \(C\) measures exactly this jump. In a whole ring, where there must be no jump, and for an axisymmetric displacement, \(C = 0\), and this is the requirement that the fibre fit into the space between the rings, equation (11.29). This short form is the bridge to solving in stresses (SADD (8.4.76), PDF p. 220): we insert Hooke's law into it and get an equation for the stresses, which we solve together with the equilibrium equation of the next section without displacements. In the lectures it is written as \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = (r\,\varepsilon_{\vartheta\vartheta})_{,\mathrm{r}}\), with a comma for the derivative with respect to \(r\).

11.6.4 Equilibrium equations in orthogonal coordinates (ravnotežne enačbe v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Why does the equilibrium equation in the radial direction have the term \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r\)? In Cartesian coordinates it is absent, because an element of the body has parallel faces, but in cylindrical coordinates the element is curved. Let us look at the simplest case: an element of the tube between the radii \(r\) and \(r + \Delta r\), between the angles \(\theta - \Delta\theta/2\) and \(\theta + \Delta\theta/2\) and with the height \(\Delta z\) (figure 11.42). Let the stress be axisymmetric for now: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) depend only on \(r\) and there are no shear components, and let the body force be \(b_{\mathrm{r}}\). This holds for a tube and a rotating disk, where everything is the same around the circumference. When it is not, two terms with the shear stress join the equation, and we add them on panel (b) after this computation. Here panel (a) is drawn. We add up the radial components of the forces on the six faces.

  1. On the outer face with radius \(r + \Delta r\) the area is \((r + \Delta r)\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\) and the force in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is approximately \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r + \Delta r)\,(r + \Delta r)\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). Approximately, because at each point of the curved face the stress vector points along the radius through that point, and this radius deviates from the central radius by at most \(\Delta\theta/2\). On the inner face the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), the area is \(r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\) and the force in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is approximately \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\,r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). Together this is the difference of the products \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) on the two faces, so \(\big[(r\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})(r + \Delta r) - (r\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})(r)\big]\Delta\theta\,\Delta z \approx \dfrac{\mathrm{d}(r\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})}{\mathrm{d}r}\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). The outer face is larger than the inner one, so the contribution differs from zero even for a constant stress: \(\mathrm{d}(r\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/\mathrm{d}r = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + r\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}/\mathrm{d}r\), and the term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the one that comes from the larger area.
  2. On the side face with the angle \(\theta + \Delta\theta/2\) the area is \(\Delta r\,\Delta z\), the outward normal is \(\vek e_\theta(\theta + \Delta\theta/2)\) and the force is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\vek e_\theta(\theta + \Delta\theta/2)\). The force is turned by \(\Delta\theta/2\) from the direction perpendicular to the central radius. Its component in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta)\), that is the central direction of the radius, is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta)\cdot\vek e_\theta(\theta + \Delta\theta/2)\big) = -\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\), since for every real number \(\delta\), by the addition formulas (corollary 8.16, applied to \(\sin(\theta + \delta)\) and \(\cos(\theta + \delta)\)), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta)\cdot\vek e_\theta(\theta + \delta) = -\cos\theta\sin(\theta + \delta) + \sin\theta\cos(\theta + \delta) = -\sin\delta\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = -\sin\delta\) (lemma 8.11, point 1). There are no shear components, so the stress vector on this face is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta\).
  3. On the face with the angle \(\theta - \Delta\theta/2\) the normal is \(-\vek e_\theta(\theta - \Delta\theta/2)\), so the component of the force in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta)\) is \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\big(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta)\cdot\vek e_\theta(\theta - \Delta\theta/2)\big) = -\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\), because by step 2 with \(\delta = -\Delta\theta/2\) and \(\sin(-x) = -\sin x\) (lemma 8.11, point 4) the scalar product is \(+\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\). Both contributions point towards the axis, because the side faces are not parallel: their normals differ by the angle \(\Delta\theta\). Together this is \(-2\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(\Delta\theta/2) \approx -\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\).
  4. The body force contributes \(b_{\mathrm{r}}\,r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\), because the volume of the element is approximately \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). The element is in equilibrium, so the sum of the forces is exactly zero, while the sum of our approximations is zero only approximately. We divide by \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). As \(\Delta r\), \(\Delta\theta\) and \(\Delta z\) tend to zero, the approximations approach the exact values and what remains is \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\mathrm{d}r} + \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{r} + b_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). This is an idea, not a proof: we have not shown here that the errors of the approximations vanish in the limit. Theorem 11.24 below proves the equation without the element.

In this we used two approximations, \(\Delta r \to 0\) and \(\Delta\theta \to 0\). Are they good? Let us try on the field \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - B/r^2\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) with \(A = 24\) MPa and \(B = 24 \cdot 3600\) MPa mm\(^2\), for which we will show later in this chapter that it is the stress in the wall of a 40/60 mm tube under an internal pressure of 30 MPa. Here we take it only as a trial field. At \(r = 50\) mm, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -10{,}56\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 58{,}56\) MPa, the derivative is \(\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}/\mathrm{d}r = 2B/r^3 = 1{,}3824\) MPa/mm and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = -1{,}3824\) MPa/mm, the sum is zero, as the equation requires for \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). Let us test also a finite element between \(49\) and \(51\) mm with the angle \(\Delta\theta = 0{,}1\) and the height \(\Delta z = 1\) mm. There \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(49) = -11{,}985\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(51) = -9{,}218\) MPa. The force on the outer face in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is, by the approximation of step 1, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(51) \cdot 51 \cdot 0{,}1 = -47{,}01\) N, and on the inner face the force in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(49) \cdot 49 \cdot 0{,}1 = +58{,}73\) N, so the sum of the forces on the faces with a radius is \(+11{,}715\) N. The radial components of the circumferential forces, computed with the integral of \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) over the wall, give \(-11{,}710\) N. The remainder is \(0{,}005\) N, that is \(0{,}04\,\%\) of each term. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is constant on each of the faces with a radius and we integrated \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), so the remainder comes only from the approximation of step 1, where we counted the force on the curved face as if all of it pointed along the central radius. The exact component has, instead of \(\Delta\theta = 0{,}1\), the factor \(2\sin(\Delta\theta/2) = 0{,}099958\), the integral of \(\cos(\theta' - \theta)\) over the angles \(\theta'\) of the face from \(\theta - \Delta\theta/2\) to \(\theta + \Delta\theta/2\), just as for the circumferential forces in step 2, and with it the forces on the faces with a radius are \(-46{,}99\) N and \(+58{,}70\) N, and the sum of all forces is exactly zero. So the term \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r\) really comes from the inclination of the side faces, and the term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}/r\) from the larger outer area.

What changes if the stress also has the shear component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) and also depends on the angle \(\theta\)? Panel (b) shows the same element with the shear forces, all drawn in the positive sense. By definition 11.19, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) is the component \(\theta\) of the stress vector on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). On the outer face it therefore points along \(\vek e_\theta\), and on the inner one, where the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), in the opposite direction. On the side faces the component \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{r}}\) acts, which by the symmetry of stress equals \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) and points along the radius of that face: on the face with the angle \(\theta + \Delta\theta/2\) away from the axis, on the face with the angle \(\theta - \Delta\theta/2\), where the outward normal is \(-\vek e_\theta\), towards the axis. We resolve all forces along the directions \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) at the middle of the element. The radii of the side faces are turned from the central one by the half angle \(\Delta\theta/2\), and the same half angle is on panel (a) between a circumferential force and the direction perpendicular to the central radius. This is where \(\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\) in steps 2 and 3 above comes from.

In the radial equation the shear adds one term. The shear forces on the side faces are almost parallel to the central radius, each has the share \(\cos(\Delta\theta/2)\) along it, and they point in opposite directions. What remains is their difference,

\[\big[\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(\theta + \tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(\theta - \tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2})\big]\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\cos\tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2} \approx \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta}\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta r\,\Delta z ,\]

and after division by the volume \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\) the term \(\dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta}\). The divisor \(r\) is here because the side faces are apart by the arc \(r\,\Delta\theta\), not by \(\Delta\theta\). The shear forces on the outer and inner faces are perpendicular to the central radius and in the limit contribute nothing to the radial equation.

In the circumferential equation, the sum of the forces along \(\vek e_\theta\) at the middle of the element, there are three contributions.

  1. The shear forces on the faces with a radius. On the outer one the force is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(r + \Delta r)\,(r + \Delta r)\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\), on the inner one \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(r)\,r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). The difference is, as for the radial forces on these two faces above, approximately \(\dfrac{\partial(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta})}{\partial r}\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\), which after division by the volume gives \(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r} + \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{r}\). The term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\) comes from the outer arc being longer than the inner one.
  2. The shear forces on the side faces. Because the faces are not parallel, each of the forces has a component along \(\vek e_\theta\) of the middle. By the addition formulas, as for the circumferential forces above, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta + \delta)\cdot\vek e_\theta(\theta) = \sin\delta\). The force on the face with the angle \(\theta + \Delta\theta/2\) points along \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta + \Delta\theta/2)\) and gives \(+\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\). The force on the face with the angle \(\theta - \Delta\theta/2\) points along \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(\theta - \Delta\theta/2)\) and gives \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(-\Delta\theta/2)\), so the same. Both blue arrows point in the direction \(+\vek e_\theta\), together \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta z\,\sin(\Delta\theta/2) \approx \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\), after division by the volume a second term \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\).
  3. The circumferential forces of panel (a) are almost parallel to \(\vek e_\theta\) of the middle and oppositely directed. Their difference gives, like the shear forces in the radial equation, the term \(\dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta}\).

The sum is \(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r} + \dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \dfrac{2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{r} + b_\theta = 0\). So the two in front of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\) counts two different causes: the longer outer arc and the tilted side faces.

Let us test this on numbers too. Take the uniform shear \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tau = 10\) MPa in Cartesian axes, which is in equilibrium without body forces. By (11.25) with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and \(\vek e_\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\) its local components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \tau\sin 2\theta\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -\tau\sin 2\theta\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tau\cos 2\theta\). The state is the same everywhere, but the local components change with the angle, because the local frame turns. On the element between the radii 49 and 51 mm with the angle \(\Delta\theta = 0{,}1\) about \(\theta = 0\) and the height \(\Delta z = 1\) mm, the forces along \(\vek e_\theta\) of the middle are, by the approximations above,

The remainder \(0{,}001\) N comes from the same approximation as before, that the force on a curved face points wholly across the central radius. With the integral over the curved faces the forces on them are \(+50{,}979\) N and \(-48{,}980\) N and the sum is zero (NumPy). The general equation in all three directions has the same structure as the computation on the element. The force across a coordinate surface is the product of the area of the surface and the stress vector on it, and the areas of the faces of a local element are, by lemma 11.17, proportional to \(J/h_{\mathrm{q}}\). The curvature that we saw in the tube in the term \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r\) comes from the derivatives of the local frame.

Theorem 11.24 Equilibrium in orthogonal coordinates (ravnotežje v ortogonalnih koordinatah)

Let \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) be orthogonal coordinates (definition 11.13) with scale factors \(h_{\mathrm{q}}\), Jacobian determinant \(J = h_1h_2h_3\) and local frame \(\vek e_{\mathrm{q}}\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be a symmetric stress field with continuous first derivatives and \(\vek b\) the body force, written as a function of the coordinates. Denote \(\vek t_{\mathrm{q}} = \ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{q}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sigma_{\mathrm{kq}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\), the stress vector on the coordinate surface \(q = \text{const.}\) (with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{q}}\)). Then at every point of the domain the equilibrium \(\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma + \vek b = \vek 0\) (theorem 2.31) is equivalent to

\[\sum_{\mathrm{q}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\Big(\frac{J}{h_{\mathrm{q}}}\,\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\Big) + J\,\vek b = \vek 0 . \tag{11.31}\]
Proof

We must show that \(J\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma = \sum_{\mathrm{q}}\partial\big((J/h_{\mathrm{q}})\,\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\big)/\partial q\), because then (11.31) follows from the vector equilibrium equation multiplied by \(J > 0\).

  1. Let \(\vek c\) be a constant vector. The vector \(\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma\) has component \(i\) equal to \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) ((2.38)). Hence \(\vek c\cdot\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\,c_{\mathrm{i}}\Big)\), where we took the constant numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) under the derivative (lemma 1.82, point 2). The inner sum is component \(j\) of the vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek c\), so \(\vek c\cdot\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma = \operatorname{div}(\ten\sigma\,\vek c)\) (definition 4.13).
  2. The field \(\ten\sigma\,\vek c\) is continuously differentiable. By lemma 11.17, \(J\operatorname{div}(\ten\sigma\,\vek c) = \sum_{\mathrm{q}}\partial\big((J/h_{\mathrm{q}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek c\big)/\partial q\).
  3. Because \(\ten\sigma\) is symmetric (theorem 2.41), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek c = \vek c\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{q}} = \vek c\cdot\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\). The vector \(\vek c\) is constant, so by lemma 1.82 (points 1 and 2) it leaves the derivative with respect to \(q\), and \(J\,\vek c\cdot\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma = \vek c\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{q}}\partial\big((J/h_{\mathrm{q}})\,\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\big)/\partial q\).
  4. The equality holds for every \(\vek c\), so also for \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which gives all three components of the vector equality \(J\operatorname{div}\ten\sigma = \sum_{\mathrm{q}}\partial\big((J/h_{\mathrm{q}})\,\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\big)/\partial q\).

Source: SADD (3.8.6) (PDF p. 80), CM §5.3.2 (PDF p. 230-231). Both books obtain the equations with the operator \(\nabla\) in coordinates. The proof with lemma 11.17 is not from the source.

The theorem contains the derivative of the vector \(\vek t_{\mathrm{q}}\), which is expanded in the local frame, which turns, so we must differentiate it by the product rule, as for the strains.

Corollary 11.25 Equilibrium in cylindrical coordinates (ravnotežje v valjnih koordinatah)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be a symmetric stress field with continuous first derivatives in the domain of cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\) and \(\vek b\) the body force. Then the equilibrium is equivalent to the three equations, in which the term \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r\) comes from the inclined side faces of the element and the term \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\) from the longer outer arc and the inclined faces

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{r} + b_{\mathrm{r}} &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{r} + b_\theta &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{\partial r} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{r} + b_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.32}\]
\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr},\mathrm{r}} + \frac1r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta,\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{rz},\mathrm{z}} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{r} + b_{\mathrm{r}} &= 0 , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta,\mathrm{r}} + \frac1r\,\sigma_{\theta\theta,\theta} + \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z},\mathrm{z}} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{r} + b_\theta &= 0 , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{rz},\mathrm{r}} + \frac1r\,\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z},\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz},\mathrm{z}} + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{r} + b_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.32}\]
Proof

We must show that (11.32) follows from theorem 11.24 for the table of lemma 11.15.

  1. By lemma 11.15, \(J = r\), \(h = (1, r, 1)\), so \(J/h = (r, 1, r)\) for \(q = r, \theta, z\) (these are the areas of the faces of the element: the outer face is proportional to \(r\), the side face to \(1\)), and \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = \vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), the other derivatives are zero. Equation (11.31) is \(\dfrac{\partial(r\,\vek t_{\mathrm{r}})}{\partial r} + \dfrac{\partial\vek t_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \dfrac{\partial(r\,\vek t_{\mathrm{z}})}{\partial z} + r\,\vek b = \vek 0\), and after dividing by \(r\) it is \(\vek t_{\mathrm{r}}/r + \dfrac{\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} + \dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \dfrac{\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} + \vek b = \vek 0\).
  2. Because of the symmetry of stress we write the components of the vectors \(\vek t_{\mathrm{q}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sigma_{\mathrm{kq}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) with indices in the order \(r, \theta, z\), so \(\vek t_{\mathrm{r}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\vek e_\theta + \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\vek t_\theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\vek e_\theta + \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\vek e_\theta + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The local frame depends only on \(\theta\), so \(\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r\) is the sum of the derivatives of the components, and likewise \(\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z\). In the derivative with respect to \(\theta\), by the product rule, \(\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\theta}{\partial\theta} = \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\Big)\vek e_\theta + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta = \vek e_\theta\) and \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. The component \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the equation of step 1 is \(\dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}}{r} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}}{\partial r} + \dfrac1r\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big) + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), which is the first equation. The component \(\vek e_\theta\) is \(\dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{r} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}}{\partial r} + \dfrac1r\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\Big) + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} + b_\theta = 0\), where the terms \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\) add to \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\), and this is the second equation. The component \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(\dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{r} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}}{\partial r} + \dfrac1r\,\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}}{\partial\theta} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), which is the third.

Source: SADD (3.8.6) (PDF p. 80), App. A (A.5) (PDF p. 547), CM (5.3.24) (PDF p. 231). CM writes the first term of the second equation as \(\partial\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{r}}/\partial r\) and \((\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta})/r\), which by the symmetry of stress is the same equation, and SADD writes \(\tau\) instead of \(\sigma\) for the shear components.

Corollary 11.26 Equilibrium in spherical coordinates (ravnotežje v krogelnih koordinatah)

Let \(\ten\sigma\) be a symmetric stress field with continuous first derivatives in the domain of spherical coordinates \((R, \varphi, \theta)\) and \(\vek b\) the body force. Then the equilibrium is equivalent to the three equations, in which the terms with \(\cot\varphi\) and \(\sin\varphi\) come from the parallel, along which the frame turns

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}}{\partial R} + \frac1R\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} + \frac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{2\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \sigma_{\theta\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\cot\varphi}{R} + b_{\mathrm{R}} = 0 , \\ &\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{\partial R} + \frac1R\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} + \frac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})\cot\varphi + 3\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{R} + b_\varphi = 0 , \\ &\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{\partial R} + \frac1R\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\varphi} + \frac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\,\frac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} \\ &\quad + \frac{2\sigma_{\varphi\theta}\cot\varphi + 3\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{R} + b_\theta = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.33}\]
Proof

We must show that (11.33) follows from theorem 11.24 for the table of lemma 11.15. The procedure is the same as in corollary 11.25.

  1. By lemma 11.15, \(J = R^2\sin\varphi\), \(h = (1, R, R\sin\varphi)\), so \(J/h = (R^2\sin\varphi,\ R\sin\varphi,\ R)\) for \(q = R, \varphi, \theta\). The factor \(\sin\varphi\) comes from the radius of the parallel \(R\sin\varphi\). Because of the symmetry of stress we write the components of the vectors \(\vek t_{\mathrm{q}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sigma_{\mathrm{kq}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) with indices in the order \(R, \varphi, \theta\). We divide equation (11.31) by \(R^2\sin\varphi\) and use the product rule for the factors \(R^2\sin\varphi\) and \(R\sin\varphi\) (in the derivative with respect to \(R\), \(\partial(R^2)/\partial R = 2R\), in the derivative with respect to \(\varphi\), \(\partial(\sin\varphi)/\partial\varphi = \cos\varphi\)): \(\dfrac{2}{R}\,\vek t_{\mathrm{R}} + \dfrac{\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{R}}}{\partial R} + \dfrac{\cot\varphi}{R}\,\vek t_\varphi + \dfrac1R\,\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} + \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\,\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\theta}{\partial\theta} + \vek b = \vek 0\).
  2. By lemma 11.15 the local frame depends only on \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\), so \(\partial\vek t_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial R\) is the sum of the derivatives of the components. In the derivative with respect to \(\varphi\), by the product rule, \(\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\varphi}{\partial\varphi} = \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} - \sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} + \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} + \sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\Big)\vek e_\varphi + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\varphi}\,\vek e_\theta\) (from \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\varphi = \vek e_\varphi\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\varphi = -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\varphi = \vek 0\)). In the derivative with respect to \(\theta\), \(\dfrac{\partial\vek t_\theta}{\partial\theta} = \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \sin\varphi\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} + \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \cos\varphi\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big)\vek e_\varphi + \Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \sin\varphi\,\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta} + \cos\varphi\,\sigma_{\varphi\theta}\Big)\vek e_\theta\) (from \(\partial\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}/\partial\theta = \sin\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\varphi/\partial\theta = \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\theta\), \(\partial\vek e_\theta/\partial\theta = -\sin\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} - \cos\varphi\,\vek e_\varphi\)).
  3. The component \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) of the equation of step 1 is
    \[\begin{gathered} \dfrac{2\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}}{R} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}}{\partial R} + \dfrac{\cot\varphi\,\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{R} + \dfrac1R\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} - \sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\Big) \\ {}+ \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \sin\varphi\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big) \\ {}+ b_{\mathrm{R}} = 0 \end{gathered}\]
    The term \(\sin\varphi\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}/(R\sin\varphi)\) is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}/R\), and this is the first equation.
  4. The component \(\vek e_\varphi\) is
    \[\begin{gathered} \dfrac{2\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{R} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}}{\partial R} + \dfrac{\cot\varphi\,\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}}{R} + \dfrac1R\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}}{\partial\varphi} + \sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\Big) \\ {}+ \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\theta} - \cos\varphi\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\Big) \\ {}+ b_\varphi = 0 \end{gathered}\]
    The terms with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\) give \(3\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}/R\), and the terms with \(\cot\varphi\) give \((\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})\cot\varphi/R\), so the second equation.
  5. The component \(\vek e_\theta\) is
    \[\begin{gathered} \dfrac{2\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{R} + \dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}}{\partial R} + \dfrac{\cot\varphi\,\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{R} + \dfrac1R\,\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\varphi\theta}}{\partial\varphi} \\ {}+ \dfrac{1}{R\sin\varphi}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \sin\varphi\,\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta} + \cos\varphi\,\sigma_{\varphi\theta}\Big) \\ {}+ b_\theta = 0 \end{gathered}\]
    The terms with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}\) give \(3\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta}/R\), and the terms with \(\sigma_{\varphi\theta}\) give \(2\cot\varphi\,\sigma_{\varphi\theta}/R\), so the third equation.

In (3.8.8) the first terms of the second and third rows are printed as \(\partial\tau_{\mathrm{r}\varphi}/\partial R\) and \(\partial\tau_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/\partial R\) with a small \(r\), which is \(\tau_{\mathrm{R}\varphi}\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{R}\theta}\) as in (A.6) and as in the other terms of these rows (a printing error of SADD, PDF p. 81, confirmed on the image of the equation). The equations are also verified with SymPy: the derivation from the Cartesian equations by the change of variables gives zero difference for a general stress field for all three. The derivation from theorem 11.24 is not from the source.

What have we shown? The theorem gave, from the single equation (11.31), the equilibrium in all orthogonal coordinates. Both corollaries developed it with the derivatives of the local frame, and every term without derivatives, for example \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r\) or \(\cot\varphi\), is a derivative of the local frame that does not exist in Cartesian coordinates. In the tube of figure 11.42 we saw where the first comes from: the side faces of the element are not parallel. The same cause together with the longer outer arc gives the term \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r\) of the second equation.

11.6.5 Hooke's law in local components (Hookov zakon v krajevnih komponentah)

We wrote Hooke's law in chapter 3 in Cartesian axes. Does it also hold for components in the local frame, that is on an element with edges along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) instead of on an element with edges along the axes \(x\) and \(y\)? Isotropy means exactly this: the rule that gives strains from stresses is the same in every perpendicular Cartesian system.

Corollary 11.27 Hooke's law in local components (Hookov zakon v krajevnih komponentah)

Let the material be isotropic and linearly elastic with modulus of elasticity \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (\(\nu \neq -1, \tfrac12\)) and Lamé constants \(\lambda\), \(\mu\) (theorem 3.19). Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the local components at a point of orthogonal coordinates (definition 11.19). Then at this point the equations (3.27) and (3.28) hold with the indices \(x, y, z\) replaced by \(1, 2, 3\). For example in cylindrical coordinates

\[\begin{aligned} &\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) \\ &\quad + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} . \end{aligned} \tag{11.34}\]
Proof

We must show that theorem 3.19 is applied in the local frame.

  1. The triple \(\vek e_1, \vek e_2, \vek e_3\) is at the point a right-handed perpendicular system with the same unit of length as the Cartesian one (definition 11.19), so a perpendicular Cartesian coordinate system at the point.
  2. By theorem 3.19, the equations (3.27) and (3.28) hold at every point of the body and in every perpendicular Cartesian coordinate system, so also in the system of step 1.
  3. The components of stress and strain in this system are, by theorem 2.53 and lemma 2.55, the elements of the matrices \(\mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\) and \(\mat Q\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Q^\T\), and by definition 11.19 these are the local components. The trace \(\tr\ten\varepsilon\) in this system is the sum of the diagonal local components, for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\).
  4. The equations (3.27) and (3.28), written in the system of step 1 with the components of step 3, are therefore equations with local components. For cylindrical coordinates all three equations of (11.34) follow from them (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\) from the first row of point 1, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) from point 2 of the theorem).

Source: SADD (A.8) (PDF p. 548) for cylindrical and (A.9) for spherical coordinates. For the shear components SADD writes \(\tau\) and \(e\) instead of \(\sigma\) and \(\varepsilon\).

The same holds for Hooke's law with temperature (theorem 3.23). The thermal strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) is, by the hypothesis of the theorem, the same in all directions, its matrix is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), and a rotation does not change it, since \(\mat Q\,\mat I\,\mat Q^\T = \mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\). On an element with edges along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) the heated material therefore expands just as on an element with edges along \(x\) and \(y\), and in local components the thermal term is added only to the normal strains: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), likewise \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), while the shear equations, for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\), stay without it.

Isotropy is essential here. For a material with different properties in different directions we would have to rotate Hooke's law as well, and the form of the equations in local axes would change: in a tube wound from fibres around the circumference the material is stiffer in the direction \(\vek e_\theta\) than in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the same strain would require different stresses in the two directions. So in the next two sections we can insert the strains of corollaries 11.21 and 11.22 into Hooke's law and then into the equilibrium equation, without returning to Cartesian components.

The general Navier-Lamé equations of section 11.2 could be obtained in these coordinates by the same procedure (SADD (A.11), (A.12), PDF p. 549), but we do not need them. The tube and the sphere will need only the radial equation with the displacement \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), which follows from the corollaries of this section. The same equations in cylindrical coordinates will be a tool also for circular shafts in chapter 16.

With this we have answered the questions from the start of the section. The local components (definition 11.19) are components in rotated Cartesian axes that follow the body, so for the tube and the sphere they are the same all around the circumference. The strains and equilibrium in them follow from the general theorems (theorems 11.20 and 11.24) with the derivatives of the local frame that lemma 11.15 gives, and every term without derivatives (\(u_{\mathrm{r}}/r\), \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r\)) is a consequence of the curvature of the coordinates. The same curvature gives the compatibility equation in polar coordinates (theorem 11.23). Hooke's law does not change in these components if the material is isotropic (corollary 11.27).
