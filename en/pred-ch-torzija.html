Chapter 16 Torsion

Chapter 16Lecture: Torsion

1 Why the circle is easy and the rectangle is not

We start with the wall. The hatched line is the wall that holds the left end. From it, along the axis \(x\), runs a steel shaft of diameter \(40\) mm. Its length is \(L = 1\) m. At the free end we turn it with a spanner, with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, like a screw. We have three questions. How far does the end turn? Where is the steel loaded most? And why is a shaft of twice the diameter sixteen times stiffer?

Before we turn, we draw a straight thread on the surface along the shaft. Now we turn. What does the thread do? It does not stay straight, it becomes a helix. At the wall it does not move, at the end it moves most. This picture is the whole chapter: every section turns a little more than its left neighbour.

The claim of this segment is one: every section turns as a whole, by \(\alpha\) radians per metre, so a small square at radius \(r\) is sheared by \(\gamma = r\alpha\), the stress is \(\tau = G\alpha r\) around the axis, and the sum of stress times lever arm gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}} \alpha\). The claim uses five things, and for each we ask why.

1.1 Why is the twist per metre the same everywhere?

Why would every metre of the shaft turn by the same angle? What can change along the shaft at all? In our minds we cut the shaft anywhere. We keep only the right part, from the cut at \(x\) to the end. What acts on it? At the end, our moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\). At the cut, the left part we removed holds it with a moment across the cut. The side surface is free, nothing touches it. The right part is at rest. A body at rest that does not start to turn has zero total moment: this is Newton's law for turning. So the moment at the cut is as large as \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) and opposite.

This holds for every \(x\). Every cut carries the same moment. On the right we draw a graph: across, the position \(x\) along the shaft, up, the rotation of the section \(\varphi\). Now we think of the shaft as sliced into pieces of equal length \(h\). Each piece has the same section, the same steel and the same moment. Why would one piece turn more than another? There is no reason, so each turns by the same angle, which we write \(\alpha h\). The steps add up from the wall, where the rotation is zero, and give a straight line

\[\varphi(x) = \alpha\,x .\]

The number \(\alpha\) is the twist per metre of length, unit rad/m. In the book: 16.1 and (16.2).

Here hides the first assumption. We took the moment at the end as if it were spread over the section exactly as the shaft needs it. Why may it be so? A real end is a screw or a gear, which grips differently. Saint-Venant's principle says that another distribution with the same moment changes the stresses only near the end (11.11, quoted without proof). When does it fail? On the piece right next to the screw or the gear. What does it do to the result? Everything that follows holds away from the ends.

1.2 Why does twist mean shear?

The sections turn, each a little more than its left neighbour. What does the steel between two neighbouring sections feel? On the surface of the shaft we choose a small window and enlarge it. In it is a square of side \(h\). Before turning, its two edges are at a right angle. One runs along the axis, the other around the circumference.

Now we turn. The left edge of the square lies on the section at \(x\), which turns by \(\alpha x\). The right edge lies on the section at \(x + h\), which turns by \(\alpha(x + h)\). The difference is \(\alpha h\). How much is that along the circumference? A point at radius \(R\), turned by an angle, travels an arc that is the radius times the angle. That is the meaning of a radian. So the right edge slides past the left one by the shift \(R\alpha h\). The square becomes slanted.

By how much did it tilt? The shift \(R\alpha h\) over the distance \(h\) between the edges is the angle \(\gamma\):

\[\gamma \approx \frac{R\alpha h}{h} = R\alpha .\]

The right angle is no longer right. A change of a right angle is shear, that is how the word is defined in chapter 1. In the book: (16.3).

Here is the second assumption, a small rotation. We look at the section from the front and at a point \(P\) at radius \(R\) on it. The section turns by the angle \(\varphi\). The true rotation carries the point along the arc to \(P'\). The book's model, \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\varphi z\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varphi y\), carries it straight along the tangent by \(R\varphi\). Why may we? The difference is the motion towards the axis \(R(1 - \cos\varphi)\). At \(500\) N m, \(\varphi(L) = 0{,}02639\) rad, the motion along the circumference is \(0{,}528\) mm, the motion towards the axis \(0{,}00696\) mm. The drawing is exaggerated, the two numbers beside it are the true ones. The ratio is \(\tan(\varphi/2) = 1{,}32\,\%\), at our \(50\) N m only \(0{,}13\,\%\). When does it fail? For a large twist, for example a rubber rod twisted by a quarter turn, where the ratio is \(\tan 45^\circ = 100\,\%\). What does it do to the result? It leaves out the motion towards the axis, the error is this percentage. In the book: 16.5.

The third assumption enters at the same place. The section turns like a rigid plate and stays plane, the points do not move along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Why is this right for the circle? Because such a field satisfies all the equations and the free side surface, and the solution is unique (16.3, points 3 and 5). When does it fail? For every other shape, as we will see at the end. What does it do to the result if we use it anyway? We will measure that on the rectangle, with a number.

1.3 Why does the stress grow from the centre to the edge?

Where is the steel loaded more, near the axis or at the surface? We look at the shaft from its end. We draw the section and its axes. The square from the window need not be on the surface. We put it at radius \(r\). The same reasoning gives the shift \(r\alpha h\) and the shear \(\gamma = r\alpha\). On the axis \(r = 0\) and there is no shear.

How do we get stress from shear? By measuring. If we shear a cube of steel and measure, the shear stress is proportional to the shear, \(\tau = G\gamma\). This is Hooke's law for shear, \(G\) is the shear modulus (strižni modul) (3.17), for our steel \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa. So

\[\tau = G\alpha r .\]

We draw it. Along the radius the arrows grow from zero at the centre, linearly, to \(G\alpha R = 3{,}98\) MPa at the edge. On the circumference they are all equally long and point around the axis, because that is where the square shifted. In the book: 16.3, point 2, and 16.4.

Hooke's law is the fourth assumption. Why is it right? Because the steel is far from yielding. By the largest shear criterion it starts to yield at \(\tau = 110\) MPa, that is at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 1382\) N m (2.93). Our \(3{,}98\) MPa at the edge is about twenty-eight times less, \(110 / 3{,}98 \approx 28\). When does it fail? Above the yield limit the stress no longer grows linearly along the radius. What does it do to the result? Without it there is no \(\tau = G\gamma\) and nothing that follows.

1.4 Why the fourth power of the diameter?

Why the fourth power, and not the second or the third? We add up the moment and count how many times the radius enters. We draw the section of radius \(R\). From it we cut a thin ring of width \(h\) at radius \(r\). On it acts the stress \(G\alpha r\) around the axis. A moment is force times lever arm. How long is the lever arm? Exactly \(r\).

Now we count powers. The stress has one \(r\). The lever arm has a second \(r\). The area of the ring is its circumference times its width, \(2\pi r h\), the third \(r\). We add the rings from the centre to \(R\). As the rings get thinner, the sum of \(r^3 h\) tends to the area under the curve \(r^3\) from \(0\) to \(R\), that is \(R^4/4\), and with the factor \(2\pi\) it is \(\pi R^4/2\). The result is

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\alpha , \qquad I_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{\pi R^4}{2} = 251\,327\ \text{mm}^4 .\]

The idea of the proof is exactly this: we slice the disc into thin rings. In the book: 16.2, (16.5) and (16.8).

So where does the stiffness live? We halve the radius. The inner disc up to \(R/2\) gives \((1/2)^4 = 1/16\) of the polar moment. The outer half of the radius gives \(15/16\). The steel near the axis does almost no work. And twice the diameter? Every \(r\) doubles, four of them give \(2^4 = 16\). That is the sixteen times we were looking for at the start.

1.5 Why does the same reasoning fail for a rectangle?

Everything so far was easy. Where is the trap? At the edge of the section. We take a small square of the bar at the edge and look at it from the side. Its top side lies on the side surface of the bar. Nothing touches that surface. Its right side is a piece of the section.

Suppose the stress on the section points towards the edge. That is the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) on the right side and the opposite one on the left side. Together they turn the square anticlockwise. But the square is at rest. What holds it? Only the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) on the top and bottom sides. So \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), this is the symmetry of stress (simetrija napetosti) (2.41). On the top side, however, there is nothing to push or pull. So \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\), and therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) too. The stress on the section must not point across the free edge. At the edge it must run along the edge.

Why can the circle do it? The stress of the circle points around the axis, and the edge of the circle goes around the axis. It runs along the edge by itself. Now we draw the rectangle \(20 \times 40\) mm with its axes and try the same field. Through the corner we draw a circle about the centre. The stress of the circle is tangent to such a circle. At the edge it therefore points at a slant. Its component across the edge is not zero, but it must be. At the corner it is worst: a stress of \(8{,}39\) MPa where two free faces meet and it must be zero. In the book: 16.14.

Now we measure the cost of the third assumption. For this rectangle plane sections give \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\). The true torsion constant is \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\). Plane sections therefore overestimate the stiffness by the factor \(133\,333 / 73\,178 = 1{,}82\), and the twist comes out \(45\,\%\) too small. In the middle of the long face they give \(3{,}75\) MPa, the true stress is \(12{,}71\) MPa. The error is not a small inaccuracy, the field does not satisfy the boundary condition.

1.6 Back up

Now we build the answer from the ground. A part at rest has no total moment, so every cut carries the same moment and the rotation grows linearly. The rotation between neighbouring sections tilts the square by \(\gamma = r\alpha\). Hooke turns the shear into the stress \(G\alpha r\) around the axis. Force times lever arm, added over the rings, gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}} \alpha\) with \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) proportional to \(R^4\).

The answers to the questions from the start are three numbers. From \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) we get

\[\alpha = 0{,}00264\ \text{rad/m} , \qquad \varphi(L) = 0{,}151^\circ , \qquad \tau_{\max} = 3{,}98\ \text{MPa} .\]

The twist per metre is the slope of the line. The end turns by less than a fifth of a degree. The largest stress is on the circumference. And the helix of the first picture? Its slope against the straight thread is \(\gamma_{\max} = R\alpha = 5{,}28 \cdot 10^{-5}\). In the picture it is strongly exaggerated, otherwise we would not see it. In the book: 16.5.

A question remains for next time. What does the stress do in a section where circling around the axis does not follow the edge? In the book: 16.2.

1.7 By hand: a cloth and a thread

Who has not wrung out a wet cloth? We hold one end and turn the other. The cloth does not turn as a whole, it twists. That is what the word torsion (torzija) means, from the Latin torquere, to twist. On the shaft we see it on the thread: the straight thread becomes a helix. What does this view add? The name tells what happens: not a rotation of the body, but a twisting of its slices against each other.

1.8 By numbers: a tube

If the steel near the axis does almost no work, let us take it out. We draw the tube \(40/32\) mm. We removed the disc of radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}8\,R\). That is \(0{,}8^2 = 0{,}64\) of the area and the mass, but only \(0{,}8^4 = 0{,}41\) of the polar moment. So the tube keeps \(0{,}590\) of the stiffness with \(0{,}360\) of the mass. The stress on the inner and outer circle is \(5{,}39\) and \(6{,}74\) MPa. What does this view add? Stiffness lives where the lever arm is large. This will be the seed of the third segment.

1.9 Historically: Coulomb and Duleau

The fourth power was first a measurement. Coulomb twisted wires of the same metal with different lengths and diameters and found

\[M = \frac{\mu\,d^4}{l}\,\varphi\]

((16.1)). We counted the same \(d^4\) on the rings. The failure on the rectangle, too, was first an experiment. On rectangular iron bars Duleau found that the stress is not proportional to the distance from the axis. We saw this in the stress across the edge. What does this view add? Both conclusions came first from experiment, the calculation explained them later. In the book: 16.1.

2 The hill over the section: Prandtl's function and the membrane

For the circle we guessed the stress. But what about a section of any shape? We want three things: where the stress is large, which way it points and how stiff the bar is. Without solving equations, with one look.

Take a wire frame in the shape of the section, here an ellipse with semi-axes \(a = 10\) mm and \(b = 20\) mm. Over it we stretch a soap film (milnica). From below we blow gently, with a uniform pressure \(p\). The film bulges. We measure it as the height above the frame, the top has height \(h\).

The claim of this segment is one: the stress runs along the contour lines of this film, its size is the steepness of the film, and the moment is twice the volume under it. The claim needs six things: the edge, the flow, the hill, its curvature, the soap film and the volume. For each we ask why.

First the view from above, like a map. The axes, the edge, where the film has height zero, and the contour lines (plastnice), lines of equal height. The contour lines are ellipses similar to the edge. Where is the stress largest? At the end of the short axis. At the end of the long axis it is half of that. Where the contour lines crowd at the edge, the film is steeper. That is everything we will show. Now we go down.

2.1 Why must the stress run along the edge?

Which way may the stress point at a point right at the edge? We saw this on the circle, now it holds for every edge. Draw the axes and the edge of the section. On the edge pick a point \(P\). From it the normal \(\vek n\) points outwards, the tangent touches the edge. What touches the side surface of the bar at \(P\)? Nothing. Air. So the surface carries no force. A stress along the tangent is allowed. An oblique stress would have a part along the normal, and somebody outside would have to carry that part. There is nobody. So the edge is one of the lines along which the stress runs. In the book: (16.27).

2.2 Why a flow? What flows in must flow out

Why do we speak of a flow? Draw the edge and in the section a small rectangle with sides \(\Delta y\) and \(\Delta z\). Imagine a short column cut out of the bar over this rectangle. Through two sides shear enters, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\). They act on the column in the direction of the axis \(x\), along the bar. Is the column at rest? Yes. Newton's law says that then the sum of forces is zero. The stresses do not change along the bar, so the forces on the front and back faces are equal and cancel. What remains is the shear: as much as enters must leave through the other two sides. In the book: (16.26).

This is the rule of water in a pipe with no tap and no leak. Nothing is added anywhere, nothing is lost anywhere. Such a flow runs in closed streamlines (tokovnice). The edge is one of them, as we saw above. All circle in the same sense, as the moment drives them.

When does this fail? We assumed that nothing changes along \(x\). That is right for a long straight bar loaded only at its ends, far from the ends (Saint-Venant torsion). It fails where the warping of the section is held back, for example at the built-in end of an open profile. The chapter does not treat that case. Without this assumption equilibrium would have a third term, and there would be no hill.

2.3 Why a hill? Streamlines are contour lines

How do we draw a flow without sources? As a map. Think of a hiker with a map with contour lines. Walking along a contour line, he neither climbs nor descends, the word itself says so. Where the contour lines are close, the slope is steep.

Now we turn this around. Over the section we set up a hill of height \(\Phi\) such that the streamlines of the stress are its contour lines. Between two neighbouring contour lines the same flow runs everywhere. Where the two lines are close, the same flow must pass through a narrower strip, so the stress there is large. The stress is therefore along the contour line, and its size is the steepness of the hill. This is the Prandtl function (Prandtlova funkcija), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\). In the book: 16.22.

Look at the ellipse. The profile along \(y\) falls to the edge over the short distance \(a\), the profile along \(z\) over the long distance \(b\). Both start at the same top, \(c = 79{,}58\) N/mm. Which one is steeper at the edge? The one that must lose the same height over a shorter path. So the stress is largest at the end of the short axis, and we are back on the map. At one point the gradient is drawn, the direction of steepest ascent. It points to the top, at a right angle to the stress.

The edge is a contour line because it is a streamline. We give it height zero. We may, because the stress contains only derivatives of \(\Phi\), and a constant does not change them. Here is the second assumption: the section has no hole. The edge is one single closed line, so it has one height. A tube has two edges, each at its own height, and that is the subject of the next segment (16.8).

2.4 Why does the hill have the same curvature everywhere?

Equilibrium allows any hill that is zero at the edge. Which one is right? What decides is that the stresses come from one single motion of the body. Draw the section with its axes and its corner \(P\). Every point rotates about the axis, the corner by the arrow drawn. At the same time it moves along the axis. We see this from the side, in the axes \(x\) and \(z\): the grey line is the section before loading, the arrow the displacement of one point along the axis. This second displacement is the warping of the section. The points of the opposite face go the opposite way, so the section bulges.

Now go round a small loop in the section and add up the shear along it. The rotation always contributes the same, \(2G\alpha\) per unit area, wherever the loop is. The warping contributes nothing, because it is one single function, and what one function adds on the way up it takes away on the way down. In the language of the hill this is

\[\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha .\]

The left side is the sum of the bends of the hill in two directions. In the book: 16.24, (16.29).

Does this mean that the hill bends equally in every direction? No. Go back to the profile along \(y\): it bends strongly. The profile along \(z\) bends weakly. But the sum of the two bends is the same at every point, \(-2G\alpha\). That is why the hill over the ellipse is what it is, and no other (16.25).

2.5 Why a soap film?

Why does a soap film take exactly this shape? Draw the axis \(y\) and the section of the membrane. We cut out a piece. The rest of the membrane pulls it at both ends with the tension \(N\), along the tangent. The tangents make the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) with the horizontal. The vertical parts are \(N\sin\theta_1\) and \(N\sin\theta_2\). From below the pressure \(p\) pushes.

The piece is at rest. What carries the pressure? The difference of the two vertical pulls. That difference exists only because the slope changes from one end to the other. A change of slope is curvature. So the curvature is the same everywhere, \(-p/N\). We repeat the same story in plan view. The membrane lies over the section \(S\), and the section we looked at is a cut across it. Now we take the piece over the rectangle \(D\), with normals on all four sides. We get \(N\nabla^2\zeta = -p\). In the book: 16.33, (16.36).

Compare with the hill. The same curvature everywhere, zero at the edge. The same boundary problem has only one solution, so the soap film is exactly the hill, only at another scale. We choose the scale with the pressure, \(p/N = 2G\alpha/\kappa\). In the book: 16.34, (16.37).

Three assumptions enter here. First: the tension \(N\) is the same at every point and in every direction. The model asks for such a membrane (16.32, point 2). For an unevenly stretched rubber sheet this does not hold, and then the curvature is not \(p/N\) and there is no analogy. Second: the slope is small, we replace \(\sin\theta\) by the slope. What this costs we see below, with the soap film by hand. Third: the material is linear elastic and the twist is small, as in the first segment (@pred-ch-torzija-01).

2.6 Why is the moment twice the volume?

How do we read the moment off the hill? We slice it. Draw the axis \(y\) and the section along the short axis, over it the hill. We slice the hill into thin horizontal layers. One layer has the thickness \(\Delta\Phi\), and its rim in plan view encloses the area \(A(\Phi)\).

The rim of the layer is a contour line, so a streamline. Between its upper and lower rim runs the flow \(\Delta\Phi\), in a closed loop around the area \(A\). A flow that goes round a loop gives the moment \(2 \times\) flow \(\times\) enclosed area. We will show this on a tube in the third segment, here we use it. One layer gives \(2A\,\Delta\Phi\). We add all layers. Thickness times area, added from the ground to the top, tends to the volume of the hill as the layers get thinner:

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} \Phi\,\mathrm dA .\]

In the book: (16.32). And the triangle at the edge tells once more what we saw on the map: the slope of the hill is the stress \(\tau\).

2.7 Back up

Now we assemble it in a few steps. The free edge makes the edge a streamline. Equilibrium makes the stress a flow without sources, so the streamlines are contour lines of a hill that is zero at the edge. The one twist of the bar fixes the curvature of the hill. A soap film under pressure has exactly this curvature and this zero at the edge, so the soap film is the hill.

Let us read from it. The profile along \(y\) reaches the edge with the slope \(0{,}10\). The profile along \(z\) with the slope \(0{,}05\). The ratio is \(b/a = 2\). In plan view we have the edge and the contour lines. At the end of the short axis, at point \(A\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{A}} = 15{,}92\) MPa. At the end of the long axis, at point \(B\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{B}} = 7{,}96\) MPa. At the intermediate point \(C\) it is \(8{,}90\) MPa.

Did you notice? The largest stress is at the edge point nearest to the centre. The circle formula \(\tau = G\alpha r\) would say exactly the opposite, the most at the farthest point. In a rectangle the stress at the corner must be zero, so the soap film lies flat there (16.5). That is the corner which the circle could not explain in the first segment. And third: material in the corners and at the far ends lifts the hill only a little, so it adds little to the volume and to the stiffness.

2.8 By eye: the map

Say somebody has five minutes. We show them the map. Along the contour line is the direction of the stress. The density of the contour lines is its size: at the short axis they are dense at the edge, at the long one sparse. At the top the hill is level, there is no stress there. And the volume of the hill is the moment. What does this view add? Direction, size and stiffness in one drawing, without an equation.

2.9 By hand: the soap film

The membrane is not only a thought. Prandtl showed in 1903 that a soap film carries all the information about the stresses in torsion. Griffith and Taylor used it in the years 1917 and 1918 to determine stresses. For most sections the solution is not easy to write down, but the film shows it by itself.

How accurate is it? Here is the price of the small-slope assumption. Draw the axes at the same scale and the free piece from the middle to the edge. At the edge the tension pulls it at the angle \(\theta\), the slope at the edge is \(0{,}5\). The true membrane is a circular arc of height \(2{,}361\) mm. The model is a parabola of height \(2{,}236\) mm. The factor that the model leaves out is \(0{,}8944\) at slope \(0{,}5\), an error of \(10{,}56\) %. Our ellipse has a slope of at most \(0{,}10\) and the factor \(0{,}9950\), an error of \(0{,}5\) %. In the book: 16.36.

Important: this is an error of the real soap film as a measuring device, not of the theory. The torsion equation is exactly the equation of the model. That is why the soap film is a guide that shows the shape, and every reading is confirmed by computation, because measuring the slope on a real film is not accurate enough. What does this view add? We can make the hill with our hands, and we know how far to trust it.

2.10 By numbers: six shapes of the same area

The same piece of steel, \(800\) mm\(^2\), six shapes. Which is the stiffest? Draw the circle, the square, the ellipse 1 : 2, the triangle, the strip 1 : 2 and the strip 1 : 5. Next to each is a bar for \(J\). The circle has \(101\,859\) mm\(^4\), the square \(89\,969\), the ellipse \(81\,487\), the triangle \(73\,901\), the strip 1 : 2 \(73\,178\) and the strip 1 : 5 only \(37\,288\) mm\(^4\). The ratios to the circle are \(1\), \(0{,}883\), \(0{,}800\), \(0{,}726\), \(0{,}718\) and \(0{,}366\).

Why is the strip 1 : 5 so soft? The hill must be zero at the edge, and in a narrow strip the edge is close everywhere. The hill stays low, the volume small. Among these six the circle is the stiffest. That it is the stiffest among all shapes we do not claim here. What does this view add? The amount of metal is the same everywhere, the shape decides the stiffness, and we have a number for each. And what if we bend the strip into a tube? That is the third segment.

2.11 Algebraically: two functions, one problem

The same torsion can be described in two ways. The warping function \(\psi\) tells how the section bulges along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\psi\). At the edge it has a condition on its slope. The Prandtl function \(\Phi\) gives the stresses, and at the edge it has a condition on its value, zero. A condition on the value is simpler, and that is exactly why \(\Phi\) has its soap film, stretched over a frame. Both ways give the same \(J\). What does this view add? It says why we compute with \(\Phi\) and not with \(\psi\). In the book: 16.2, 16.24.

3 Why a slit ruins a tube

Let us draw a steel tube in section: outer diameter \(40\) mm, inner \(32\) mm, the wall is \(t = 4\) mm thick. On the right we draw the same tube, only we have cut it along its whole length with a thin saw. We removed almost no metal. We twist both with the same moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m. How much softer do you think the slit tube is? By a tenth? By half?

The answer is: about sixty times. The closed tube has the torsion constant \(J = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\), the slit one only \(2413\) mm\(^4\). Division gives \(61{,}5\). The same metal, one single slit, and the stiffness has almost vanished. Why?

Let us look at where the stress flows. In the closed tube the stress goes once around the hole, everywhere in the same sense. In the slit tube it cannot cross the slit, because the face of the slit is free. So it runs along the outer face one way, turns at the slit and comes back along the inner face. Below we see what this means for the size. In the closed wall the stress is small and of one sign: \(6{,}74\) MPa outside and \(5{,}39\) MPa inside. In the slit wall it goes from \(-82{,}9\) to \(82{,}9\) MPa and is zero in the middle.

The claim of this segment is one. In a closed wall the flow circles the hole and has the whole tube as its lever arm, in an open wall it must turn back inside the wall and has only the wall thickness as its lever arm: \(J_{\text{closed}} = 4A_0^2t/\ell\) against \(J_{\text{open}} = \ell t^3/3\). The claim uses six things, and for each we ask why.

3.1 Why does the flow turn back in an open wall?

Why does the flow turn back at all, and find no other way? In our minds we flatten the wall of the slit tube. We get a narrow rectangle, a strip (trak). We write down its thickness \(t = 2a\) and length \(h = 2b\), since the ratio \(h/t\) will decide. We place the axes: \(y\) across the thickness, \(z\) along the length.

From the previous segment we know that the stress is the slope of a film stretched over the section, and that the film is pinned to zero on every free edge. The strip has a free edge on both faces. What does a film do when it is pinned on two edges only \(t\) apart? It cannot rise high. Across the thickness it makes a low arch, the parabola \(\Phi = G\alpha(a^2 - y^2)\) with a top of only \(G\alpha t^2/4\).

Now look at the two slopes. On the left side of the arch the film rises, on the right it falls. The slopes are opposite, so the stress on the two faces is opposite too: on one face in \(+z\), on the other in \(-z\), in the middle, at the top of the arch, zero. On the faces it is \(\tau = G\alpha t\). This is the turn from the top figure: the flow goes along one face and back along the other.

And along the length? The film drops to zero at the ends of the strip, but in the middle it does not notice: there it is \(0{,}9992\) of the top. The drop is confined to a piece about one thickness long at each end. The ground of this step: a film over a narrow slot cannot rise, because its two edges hold it close together. In the book: 16.6, (16.43).

3.2 Why the third power of the thickness?

Why does the thickness decide, and in the third power? The moment is twice the volume under the film. The volume under the arch is the length \(h\) times the area of a parabola. The area of a parabola is two thirds of the rectangle around it, so \(\tfrac23 \cdot t \cdot G\alpha t^2/4\). Twice this times \(h\) gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\alpha\,ht^3/3\) and \(J = ht^3/3\). In the book: (16.44).

Where are the three powers? Let us count on the strip. The stress on a face is proportional to \(t\). The lever arm between the two opposite streams is proportional to \(t\). The area each stream flows through is proportional to \(t\). Three times \(t\) gives \(t^3\). What happens if we make the wall half as thick? The strip becomes eight times softer.

Care with the counting. The flow along the faces alone gives only half of the moment, \(G\alpha\,ht^3/6\). Where is the other half? It is carried by the flow that turns at the ends of the strip, where the parabola does not hold. The volume under the film contains both, so we compute with it.

How far does this formula hold? Let us draw the axis \(h/t\) and on it the true ratio \(J/(ht^3/3)\). It is always below \(1\), and the bound \(1 - 0{,}631\,t/h\) says by how much: at \(h/t = 10\) the ratio is at least \(0{,}936\), at \(h/t = 20\) at least \(0{,}968\). The points show where the formula fails: the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm has \(h/t = 2\) and \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\), while the formula gives \(106\,667\) mm\(^4\).

3.3 Why does an open profile add up from strips?

And a profile that is not a straight strip, but a bent one? Take a U-profile of sheet metal \(t = 2\) mm thick: web \(40\) mm, flanges \(20\) mm each. In our minds we cut it into three strips. Does the film across the thickness notice that the strip is bent into a corner? No, the low arch across \(2\) mm is the same everywhere. The volumes under the arches add up: \(J \approx (40 + 20 + 20) \cdot 2^3/3 = 213{,}3\) mm\(^4\). A computation on finer and finer grids gives the estimate \(212{,}3\) mm\(^4\), the difference is \(0{,}5\,\%\).

The same for the slit tube. We unroll it into one straight strip of length \(\ell = 2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}\) and thickness \(t = 4\) mm. The formula gives \(\ell t^3/3 = 2413\) mm\(^4\). This is the number from the start. In the book: 16.42, 16.43.

3.4 Why does the hole lift the film?

Why does the closed tube not end up with the same low arch? What does the hole change? We stretch the film over the outer edge of the tube and pin it at height zero at \(\pm 20\) mm. Over the hole there is no metal, so there is no film there. What is at the edge of the hole? It is a free edge too, so it is a contour line of the film, a line of equal height. But nothing says that this height is zero.

So over the hole we lay a thin flat plate without weight and glue the film over the wall to it. The plate is flat because the whole edge of the hole is one contour line with one height. The pressure pushes it up. The film at its rim pulls it down. The plate is at rest, so the two forces are equal. This balance sets the height of the plate \(\Phi_1 = 72\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\). The ground: a body at rest has zero sum of forces (Newton), as for the piece of film in the previous segment.

What does this do to the wall? The film across the wall is no longer a low arch with both ends at zero. It is a ramp from zero outside to \(\Phi_1\) inside. Its slope has one sign, so the stress through the whole wall has one sense, of size about \(\Phi_1/t\). This is the flow once around the hole from the top figure.

And the moment? The moment is twice the volume under the film, and under the plate there is a cylinder with the area of the hole \(A_1 = 804{,}2\) mm\(^2\) as its base and \(\Phi_1\) as its height. This cylinder gives \(78{,}0\,\%\) of the whole stiffness of the tube, although there is no metal in the hole. In the book: 16.53, (16.55), 16.54.

3.5 Why is the flow the same all along the wall?

And if the wall is not equally thick everywhere? Is the stress at a thin place larger or smaller? Take a tube of any shape. We draw the wall and along its middle the centre line of length \(\ell\). Across the thickness we take the simplest film, a straight line from \(0\) to \(\Phi_1\).

From the wall we cut a piece between two cross cuts. What acts on it along the axis of the tube? The two side faces are free. Only the shear on the two cuts is left, a force \(q\) per unit length, where \(q = \tau t\). The piece is at rest, so the two forces are equal. We took the piece anywhere, so the flow \(q\) is the same everywhere along the wall.

Like water in a closed pipe: the same amount of water passes every cross section, so it runs faster where the pipe is narrower. For us the flow \(q\) is the same everywhere, so \(\tau = q/t\) is largest where the wall is thinnest. The ground: Newton on the piece of wall.

3.6 Why the area and not the perimeter?

Why does the formula have the area and not the perimeter? Let us draw the tube once more, to scale. The wall with the point \(O\) on the axis. Along the centre line the flow \(q\) runs in one sense. Take a short piece of the centre line of length \(\Delta s\). The force on it is \(q\,\Delta s\). Its lever arm about \(O\) is the perpendicular distance to the centre line, for a round tube \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 18\) mm.

Force times lever arm is \(q\,\Delta s \cdot r_{\mathrm{m}}\), and that is \(q\) times twice the area of the thin triangle from \(O\) to the piece. The area of a triangle is half the base times the height, this is the ground. All the triangles together fill the area \(A_0\) inside the centre line. For a tube of any shape the lever arm of a piece is the perpendicular distance from \(O\) to its tangent, and the triangles are the same. So \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2qA_0\) and \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(2A_0t)\). The area, not the perimeter, because every piece contributes its own triangle. In the book: 16.56, (16.58).

Now the slit wall. The flow runs out along the outer face and back along the inner one, at the slit it turns, because it cannot cross the slit. How big is the lever arm of this pair? The stress across the thickness grows linearly from \(-\tau\) to \(\tau\). Each half is a triangle, its resultant lies at two thirds of the way from the middle to the face. The distance between the two resultants is \(\tfrac23 t\). That is only \(2{,}7\) mm against \(18\) mm.

3.7 Back up

Let us now build both results from the ground. Open wall: the film is pinned to zero on both faces, the arch has a height proportional to \(t^2\), the flow goes out and back with a lever arm of the order of \(t\), so \(J = \ell t^3/3 = 2413\) mm\(^4\). Closed wall: the plate over the hole lifts the inner edge to \(\Phi_1\), across the wall there is a ramp, the flow has one sense and the same \(q\) everywhere, the lever arm is of the order of \(r_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2qA_0\) and \(J = 4A_0^2t/\ell\). For a round tube this is \(2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^3t\).

Let us compare the two bars. The bar of the slit tube is barely visible. The quotient of the two formulas is \(3r_{\mathrm{m}}^2/t^2 = 3 \cdot 20{,}25 = 60{,}75\). The exact closed tube is stiffer by a further factor \(1{,}0123\), so the exact values give \(61{,}5\). Back to the first figure: the slit takes no metal, but it takes the plate, which it drops to zero. At \(50\) N m the closed tube has at most \(6{,}74\) MPa, the slit one \(82{,}9\) MPa on the faces.

3.8 On the film: a plate or nothing

This is the explanation in a few minutes. Let us put the two films side by side. Over the closed tube a plate hangs at the height \(\Phi_1\) over the whole hole, and the volume under it is large. Over the slit tube there is no plate, the film is a low ridge across the thickness of the wall. The stiffness is the volume: a plateau over the whole hole against a thin ridge. What does this view add? Both sections in one drawing, without a formula.

3.9 In numbers: the error of a forgotten hole

What if we forget that the plate can rise, and solve the closed tube with \(\Phi = 0\) on both circles? The film rises only to \(4{,}006\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\), that is \(5{,}6\,\%\) of the true height of the plate. We get \(J = 2415\) mm\(^4\), only \(1{,}6\,\%\) of the true value and \(0{,}1\,\%\) above the slit tube. Forgetting the plate is the same as cutting the tube. What does this view add? A number for what the plate is worth.

3.10 By hand: a tube and a slit tube

Take a cardboard tube and twist it between your fingers. It hardly twists. Now cut it along its length. It twists easily. The same holds for sheet metal: a tube with a slit twists much more than the whole tube of the same sheet, though both hold the same metal. What does this view add? We feel the difference in our fingers before we compute anything. The reason is in the figure: in the whole wall the flow goes around the hole, in the slit one it must turn back.

3.11 Water flow: a loop and a dead end

A closed wall is a channel that closes on itself: the water runs around, the same amount through every cross section. An open wall is a dead end: the stream must come back beside itself. What does this view add? A rule for the largest stress. In a closed wall \(\tau = q/t\), so the thinnest place is the most loaded. In an open profile \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}} = G\alpha t_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the thickest strip is the most loaded.

3.12 Assumptions

Thin strip, film independent of the length. It enters at the turn of the flow. It holds because the film forgets the ends within one thickness: there it is off by only \(4{,}5\,\%\). It does not hold for stocky rectangles, at \(h/t = 2\) the formula overestimates \(J\) by a factor \(1/0{,}69\). Effect: the true \(J\) is at most \(6{,}4\,\%\) below the formula at \(h/t = 10\) and \(3{,}2\,\%\) at \(h/t = 20\) (bound).

Each strip of a profile carries the arch of a lone strip. It enters at the adding up. It holds away from joints and free ends. Effect on the U-profile: \(213{,}3\) against \(212{,}3\) mm\(^4\), \(0{,}5\,\%\). What it cannot give: the stress in a sharp inner corner of a joint, which is unbounded, so real profiles have a fillet.

The ramp across the wall is a straight line, the area is \(A_0\). It enters at the flow and at the area, where we took a straight line across the thickness. It holds when \(t\) is small against \(r_{\mathrm{m}}\). For a round tube exactly \(J_{\text{exact}}/J = 1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2)\), for our tube \(1{,}012\), an error of \(1{,}2\,\%\). For \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} \le 0{,}28\) the formula is within \(2\,\%\), at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}67\) it is of no use. For sections that are not round this scale is only an estimate. The \(2413\) mm\(^4\) of the slit tube at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}22\) is also an estimate we do not trust to three digits.

Free warping. The whole computation holds if the section may warp freely, that is bulge along the axis, and nothing changes along the bar. When does it fail? Where warping is held back, for example at the built-in end of the slit tube. What does it do to the result? There our \(J\) does not hold. The chapter names this case, restrained warping, but does not treat it.

The edge of the hole is one contour line. That is why the plate is flat. With two holes there are two plates at two heights, each with its own condition. Further on: the shear centre (16.7) and the bounds on the torsion constant (16.9).
