Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.4 Mathematics: centroid and second moment of a cross section

The stress in a beam changes over the cross section. In tension it is the same everywhere, in bending it is tensile on one side of the cross section and compressive on the opposite side, and in between it passes through zero. The forces and moments of the cross section are therefore integrals of the stress over the cross section, and since the stress will be a constant or proportional to the distance \(y\) from some axis, we will compute three kinds of integrals over the area of the cross section: \(\int \mathrm{d}A\), \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). For each cross section these are three numbers that we compute once and afterwards only read. The question of this section is what these numbers mean and how to compute them for a given figure.

The cross section (prerez) is a figure in a plane perpendicular to the axis of the beam, that is, in the plane \(x = \text{const.}\). We look at the cross section along the axis \(x\), from the left end of the beam toward the right, so that the axis \(x\) points away from us, the axis \(y\) points up and the axis \(z\) points to the right. This system is right-handed, because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times \vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\): the thumb of the right hand points away from us, the index finger up and the middle finger to the right. We describe the points of the figure by the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\), and for now we choose the origin anywhere. As an example we will use the cross section of a steel flat bar, \(b = 20\) mm wide and \(h = 40\) mm high, standing on its edge (the width along \(z\), the height along \(y\)), and next to it three composite figures that often appear in beams, the letter T, the letter L and the double T, which we call the I-section.

5.4.1 Integral over a figure

In chapter 4 we defined the integral over a box as an iterated integral and extended it to bodies assembled from the cells of a grid. For a rectangle in the plane \(x = c\) the two iterated integrals run over the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) (definition 4.8). All the cross sections we will compute are made of rectangles whose sides are parallel to the coordinate axes. For such composite figures we can take the same step in the plane: we cut the figure into the cells of a grid and add up the integrals over the cells.

Definition 5.17 Figure made of rectangles (lik iz pravokotnikov)

Let \(R = [y_{\mathrm{a}}, y_{\mathrm{b}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{a}}, z_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a rectangle in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\). The partitions \(y_{\mathrm{a}} = y_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_{\mathrm{m}} = y_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{a}} = z_0 < z_1 < \dots < z_{\mathrm{n}} = z_{\mathrm{b}}\) cut \(R\) into the cells \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = [y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{j}-1}, z_{\mathrm{j}}]\), and this is the grid. A set of points \(L\) is a figure made of rectangles (lik iz pravokotnikov) if it is the union of some cells of this grid, at least one.

  1. The integral of a continuous function \(f(y, z)\) over the figure \(L\) is the sum of the integrals over its cells,
    \[\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{ij}} \subseteq \mathrm{L}} \int_{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{ij}}} f\,\mathrm{d}A ,\]
    where the integral over a cell is the iterated integral of definition 4.8, first over \(z\), then over \(y\). The integral is a number.
  2. The area (ploščina) of the figure \(L\) is the number \(A = \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A\).

Source: An analogue of definition 4.11 of chapter 4, in the plane. The definition is not from a source.

By item 2 of lemma 4.10 the area of a rectangle is \(A = (y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})(z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\), which is the familiar area of a rectangle. Our bar therefore has \(A = 20 \cdot 40 = 800\) mm\(^2\).

Not every figure is a figure made of rectangles. Take the right triangle with the three vertices \((z, y) = (0, 0)\), \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), whose hypotenuse is the segment \(y + z = 1\). The definition demands a single grid with finitely many partition points whose cells fill the triangle. The triangle is therefore not a figure made of rectangles if for no grid it is the union of its cells. We show this as follows.

  1. Suppose the triangle is the union of the cells of some grid that has finitely many dividing lines \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(z = z_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. Take a point \(P\) of the hypotenuse that lies on no dividing line. It lies in some cell, because the triangle is the union of cells, and not on its sides, so in the interior of the cell. Hence \(P\) has a neighbourhood that lies entirely in the cell and thus in the triangle.
  3. But in every neighbourhood of the point \(P = (z_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\) there is the point \((z_{\mathrm{P}} + \varepsilon, y_{\mathrm{P}} + \varepsilon)\) with small \(\varepsilon > 0\), which has \(y + z = 1 + 2\varepsilon > 1\) and is outside the triangle. This is a contradiction, so every point of the hypotenuse lies on a dividing line.
  4. A line \(y = c\) or \(z = c\) meets the hypotenuse in a single point, in \((1 - c, c)\) or \((c, 1 - c)\) respectively. Finitely many dividing lines therefore cover finitely many points of the hypotenuse, but the hypotenuse has infinitely many, since \((t, 1 - t)\) is its point for every \(t \in [0, 1]\). This is a contradiction, so the triangle is not a figure made of rectangles.

We can approach the triangle as closely as we like (figure 5.25). A stepped figure with \(n\) equal steps, which has columns of width \(1/n\) and heights \(1 - i/n\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\), is a figure made of rectangles with area \(\tfrac1n \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} (1 - i/n) = (n - 1)/(2n)\). For \(n = 4\) this is \(3/8\), for \(n = 10\) it is \(9/20\). The area is smaller than \(1/2\) for every \(n\), because the orange triangles along the hypotenuse remain uncovered. The number \(n\) can be arbitrarily large, but every grid has finitely many partition points, so by item 4 it is the condition of a finite grid that rejects the triangle, not the area.

A figure can be cut into cells in several ways, and also a figure made of a few larger rectangles is cut into the cells of a grid that contains all their sides (figure 5.26). The integral must always be the same, and the following lemma guarantees this. The first part is the same argument as for bodies made of boxes (lemma 4.12), and the second part will let us add up the integral over a composite figure part by part.

Lemma 5.18 The integral over a figure does not depend on the grid (integral po liku ni odvisen od mreže)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles and \(f\) a continuous function on \(L\).

  1. The integral \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\) and the area of the figure \(L\) do not depend on the grid by which we cut \(L\) into cells.
  2. Let \(L\) be the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with sides parallel to the coordinate axes, whose interiors do not overlap. Then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}=1}^{k} \int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} f\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
  3. The integral over a figure is linear: \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} (\alpha f + \beta g)\,\mathrm{d}A = \alpha \int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A + \beta \int_{\mathrm{L}} g\,\mathrm{d}A\) for continuous functions \(f\), \(g\) and numbers \(\alpha\), \(\beta\).
Proof

We must show that the sum over the cells does not depend on the grid, that it equals the sum over the rectangles of which the figure is made, and that it is linear. We prove the first by adding lines to the grid one at a time, as in lemma 4.12.

1. Independence of the grid.

  1. Let the second grid have one more line, for example \(y = c\) with \(y_{\mathrm{i}-1} < c < y_{\mathrm{i}}\). The line cuts each cell between \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}}\) into two halves, the other cells remain. A half of a cell that is not in \(L\) is not in \(L\) either, because its points that lie on no line of the grid lie in no other cell of the old grid. So \(L\) is the union of the uncut cells and the halves of the cut cells.
  2. By item 3 of lemma 4.10, applied to a rectangle, the integral over a cut cell is the sum of the integrals over the halves. The sum over the cells is therefore the same in both grids. We proceed in the same way for a line \(z = c\).
  3. Let two arbitrary grids for \(L\) be given. We extend both to a common rectangle that contains both original ones, by adding end points to the partitions. The new cells lie outside \(L\), so the sum does not change. The grid that has all the lines of both grids is obtained from each of them by adding lines one at a time. By steps 1 and 2 each of the two grids gives the same sum as this common grid. With \(f = 1\) this holds for the area.

2. Additivity over rectangles.

  1. We take a grid whose lines are all the coordinates of the sides of the rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\). Each \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) is a union of cells of this grid, because none of its sides is inside a cell. By item 3 of lemma 4.10 the integral over \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the sum of the integrals over the cells in \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  2. Each cell of the figure \(L\) lies in some \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), because it is in \(L\) and so its interior points lie in some \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), which is a union of cells. It does not lie in two rectangles, because their interiors would overlap. The sum over the rectangles is therefore the sum over the cells of the figure \(L\) in this grid, which by step 1 of the first part is the sum that defines \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\).

3. Linearity.

  1. By item 1 of lemma 4.10, applied to a rectangle, the integral over each cell is linear. A sum of linear expressions over the cells is linear.

Source: The proof is not from a source, it is the two-dimensional proof of lemma 4.12.

The lemma says two things. The integral over a figure made of rectangles is a well-defined number, even if we take another cutting for the grid. In addition, we can always compute it over the rectangles of which the figure is made, which we will do for all cross sections. The T-section of figure 5.26 has the area \(A = 1200 + 1600 = 2800\) mm\(^2\) if the web is 20 mm wide and 60 mm high and the flange is 80 mm wide and 20 mm high (we compute both numbers by item 2 of lemma 4.10 as the area of a rectangle).

For the forces and moments of the stress in a cross section we still need a corollary that extends lemma 5.9 from a rectangle to every figure made of rectangles. The moment is computed about a point \(C\) in the plane of the cross section that is not necessarily the centre of a rectangle, so we say so in the statement.

Corollary 5.19 Internal forces from stresses for a composite cross section (notranje sile iz napetosti za sestavljen prerez)

Let the cross section of a beam at \(x\) be a figure \(L\) made of rectangles (definition 5.17), let \(C = (x, 0, 0)\) be a point of its plane from which the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) are measured, and let the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be continuous functions on \(L\). Then for \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) about \(C\) equations (5.4) and (5.5) hold with integrals over \(L\).

Proof

We must show that the forces and moments of the rectangles of which \(L\) is made, added up, give the same as the integrals over \(L\).

  1. Let \(L\) be the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with non-overlapping interiors. The force on \(L\) is the sum of the forces on the individual \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), the moment about \(C\) is the sum of the moments, because the forces and moments of pieces of a surface add.
  2. Steps 1 to 4 of the proof of lemma 5.9 use only that the surface is a rectangle and that the vector from \(C\) to a point of the surface is \(\vek r = (0, y, z)\). They nowhere use the centre of the rectangle. So they hold for every \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) and give the force \(\int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\) and the moment \(\int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} \vek r \times \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  3. The components of \(\vek t\) and \(\vek r \times \vek t\) are continuous functions on \(L\). By item 2 of lemma 5.18 the sum of the integrals over the \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) equals the integral over \(L\). Adding over \(m\) gives for \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) the same expressions as in the lemma, with integrals over \(L\).

Source: The proof is not from a source, it extends the proof of lemma 5.9.

5.4.2 Centroid

Why do we need the second number, \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\)? Take a uniform stress \(\sigma\) pulling the cross section, that is, a force \(\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) on each piece of area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) around the point \((0, y_{\mathrm{j}}, z_{\mathrm{j}})\). The force acts at this point in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}} = (\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}, 0, 0)\), so its moment about the origin (definition 2.32) is \((0, y_{\mathrm{j}}, z_{\mathrm{j}}) \times (\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}, 0, 0) = (0,\ z_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}},\ -y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}})\). All the pieces together give the component \(z\) of the moment \(-\sigma \sum y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the component \(y\) of the moment \(\sigma \sum z_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\). For a finer partition the sums approach the integrals \(-\sigma \int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\sigma \int z\,\mathrm{d}A\). A uniform tension should be only a force without a moment, so both integrals must be zero. This holds only if the axes pass through the right point of the figure. How do we find it?

Definition 5.20 First moment of area (statični moment)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles (definition 5.17) in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) and the origin \(O\). The first moments of the figure \(L\) about the axes \(z\) and \(y\) are the numbers

\[S_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad S_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} z\,\mathrm{d}A .\]

The unit is length cubed, for example mm\(^3\).

Source: The definition is not from a source.

The index tells the axis about which we count: \(S_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the first moment about the axis \(z\), where \(y\) is the distance from this axis, and \(S_{\mathrm{y}}\) about the axis \(y\), where the distance is \(z\). The first moment is the area weighted by the distance from the axis, so it can be positive, zero or negative, depending on which side of the axis the figure mostly lies.

Definition 5.21 Centroid (težišče)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the area \(A > 0\). The centroid (težišče) of the figure \(L\) is the point \(T\) with the coordinates

\[y_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{z}}}{A} , \qquad z_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{y}}}{A} .\]

Source: ONATE2 (Fig. 1.3, PDF p. 35) gives the centre of gravity G of cross sections as known. The definition is not from a source.

The next lemma says that the first moments are zero if we put the origin in the centroid. A plate of equal thickness and density in the shape of the figure therefore stays in balance when supported at the centroid: the weight of a piece of area \(\Delta A\) is proportional to \(\Delta A\), and the moment of the weight about an axis through the centroid is proportional to the sum \(\sum y'_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) or \(\sum z'_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\), which approach the integrals \(\int y'\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int z'\,\mathrm{d}A\), and both are zero.

Lemma 5.22 First moments about axes through the centroid (statična momenta glede na osi skozi težišče)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the centroid \(T\) with the coordinates \((y_{\mathrm{T}}, z_{\mathrm{T}})\), and let \(y' = y - y_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(z' = z - z_{\mathrm{T}}\) be the coordinates about axes through \(T\), parallel to the original ones. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{L}} y'\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \qquad \int_{\mathrm{L}} z'\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]
Proof

We must show that subtracting \(y_{\mathrm{T}} A\) from \(S_{\mathrm{z}}\) leaves exactly zero. The computation is one line.

  1. By linearity (item 3 of lemma 5.18) \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} (y - y_{\mathrm{T}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A - y_{\mathrm{T}} \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A = S_{\mathrm{z}} - y_{\mathrm{T}} A\).
  2. By definition 5.21 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = y_{\mathrm{T}} A\), so the difference is zero. The same holds for \(z'\) and \(S_{\mathrm{y}} = z_{\mathrm{T}} A\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

From now on, except where we say otherwise, we will use the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) with the origin in the centroid, as is usual for beams. For a rectangle and for a composite figure we compute the centroid as follows.

Lemma 5.23 Centroid of a rectangle and of a union (težišče pravokotnika in sestavljenega lika)
  1. The centroid of the rectangle \([y_{\mathrm{a}}, y_{\mathrm{b}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{a}}, z_{\mathrm{b}}]\) is its centre, \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = (y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}})/2\) and \(z_{\mathrm{T}} = (z_{\mathrm{a}} + z_{\mathrm{b}})/2\).
  2. Let \(L\) be the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with areas \(A_{\mathrm{m}}\) and centroids \((y_{\mathrm{m}}, z_{\mathrm{m}})\), whose interiors do not overlap. Then
    \[y_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\,y_{\mathrm{m}}}{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}} , \qquad z_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\,z_{\mathrm{m}}}{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}} .\]
Proof

We must show that the first moment of the figure is the sum of the first moments of the parts, and then that the first moment of a rectangle is the product of the area and the coordinate of the centre.

1. Rectangle.

  1. By definition 4.8 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{y}_\mathrm{a}}^{y_{\mathrm{b}}} \big( \int_{\mathrm{z}_\mathrm{a}}^{z_{\mathrm{b}}} y\,\mathrm{d}z \big)\,\mathrm{d}y\). The inner integral is \(y\,(z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\), because \(y\) is a constant in the inner integral.
  2. The outer integral is \((z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}}) \int_{\mathrm{y}_\mathrm{a}}^{y_{\mathrm{b}}} y\,\mathrm{d}y = (z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\,\dfrac{y_{\mathrm{b}}^2 - y_{\mathrm{a}}^2}{2} = A\,\dfrac{y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}\), because \(y_{\mathrm{b}}^2 - y_{\mathrm{a}}^2 = (y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})(y_{\mathrm{b}} + y_{\mathrm{a}})\) and \(A = (z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})(y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})\). So \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = S_{\mathrm{z}}/A = (y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}})/2\). For \(z_{\mathrm{T}}\) the computation is the same, with the roles of the coordinates exchanged by theorem 4.9 on the order of integration.

2. Composite figure.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 5.18 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}}\) and \(A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\), where \(S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}}\) is the first moment of the rectangle \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), because \(f = y\) and \(f = 1\) are continuous functions.
  2. By step 2 of the first part \(S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}} = A_{\mathrm{m}}\,y_{\mathrm{m}}\). So \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = S_{\mathrm{z}}/A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}} y_{\mathrm{m}} / \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(z_{\mathrm{T}}\) the computation is the same.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The lemma says that the centroid of a composite figure is a weighted mean of the centroids of the parts, where the weights are the areas. If the figure has a symmetry, the computation becomes even shorter.

Lemma 5.24 Centroid of a symmetric figure (težišče simetričnega lika)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles that is symmetric about the axis \(y\): the point \((y, z)\) is in \(L\) exactly when the point \((y, -z)\) is in \(L\). Let \(g\) be a continuous function of the variable \(y\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{L}} z\,g(y)\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]

In particular \(S_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) (with \(g = 1\)), so the centroid lies on the axis of symmetry.

Proof

We must show that the integrals over cells that are mirror images of each other cancel. We choose a symmetric grid.

  1. To the grid that cuts \(L\) we add the mirror lines \(z = -z_{\mathrm{j}}\). By item 1 of lemma 5.18 the integral does not change, and the grid is now symmetric. The mirror image of a cell of the figure \(L\) is therefore a cell of the grid, and it lies in \(L\), because \(L\) is symmetric.
  2. By definition 4.8 the cell \([y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{j}-1}, z_{\mathrm{j}}]\) has the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{y}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^{y_{\mathrm{i}}} g(y) \big( \int_{\mathrm{z}_{\mathrm{j}-1}}^{z_{\mathrm{j}}} z\,\mathrm{d}z \big)\,\mathrm{d}y\), because \(g(y)\) is a constant in the inner integral. The inner integral is \((z_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}-1}^2)/2\).
  3. The mirror cell is \([y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [-z_{\mathrm{j}}, -z_{\mathrm{j}-1}]\) and has the inner integral \((z_{\mathrm{j}-1}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}}^2)/2\), the opposite number. The integrals of the two cells therefore cancel. A cell that lies on the axis, \(z_{\mathrm{j}-1} = -z_{\mathrm{j}}\), is its own mirror image, and its inner integral is \((z_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}}^2)/2 = 0\).
  4. The sum over the cells of the figure \(L\) is a sum of pairs and cells on the axis, so it is zero.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

For our bar, lemma 5.24 gives that the centroid is on the axis of symmetry, \(z_{\mathrm{T}} = 0\), and since the figure is symmetric also about the axis \(z\) (exchanging the roles of the coordinates), also \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = 0\) (lemma 5.24 with the roles of the coordinates exchanged), so the centroid is at the centre of the rectangle, as lemma 5.23 also says. Figure 5.27 shows why: the strip at height \(y\) and the strip at height \(-y\) have equal areas, and their contributions \(y\,\Delta A\) are opposite.

For the letter T the centroid is not at half the height, because the upper part is wider than the lower one. Let us see where it is.

Example 5.25 Centroid of a T-section (težišče prereza T)

The T-section has a web \(20 \times 60\) mm and a flange \(80 \times 20\) mm lying on top of the web, all symmetric about the axis \(y\) (figure 5.28). The total height is 80 mm. We measure heights from the lower edge, \(y = 0\).

  1. The web is the rectangle \([0, 60] \times [-10, 10]\) with the area \(A_1 = 20 \cdot 60 = 1200\) mm\(^2\) and the centroid at the height \(y_1 = 30\) mm (lemma 5.23, item 1). The flange is the rectangle \([60, 80] \times [-40, 40]\) with the area \(A_2 = 80 \cdot 20 = 1600\) mm\(^2\) and the centroid at the height \(y_2 = 70\) mm.
  2. By item 2 of the same lemma the total area is \(A = 1200 + 1600 = 2800\) mm\(^2\) and
    \[y_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{1200 \cdot 30 + 1600 \cdot 70}{2800}\ \text{mm} = \frac{36\,000 + 112\,000}{2800}\ \text{mm} = \frac{370}{7}\ \text{mm} = 52{,}86\ \text{mm} .\]
  3. The centroid is therefore 52,86 mm above the lower edge and \(80 - 52{,}86 = 27{,}14\) mm below the upper one. It lies in the web, because \(52{,}86 < 60\) mm. If the two parts had equal areas, the centroid would be midway between their centroids, at 50 mm. The flange has more area (1600 against 1200 mm\(^2\)), so the centroid is shifted toward it.

5.4.3 Second moment of area of a cross section

In bending the stress grows in proportion to the distance from some axis, \(\sigma = k\,y\), and the component \(z\) of the moment of the stress is, by the computation above, \(-\int y \cdot k y\,\mathrm{d}A = -k \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). A third integral appears at once. First we name it, then we compute it for a rectangle and for composite figures.

Definition 5.26 Second moment of area (vztrajnostni moment prereza)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) with the origin in the centroid (lemma 5.22). The numbers

\[I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad I_{\mathrm{yz}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A\]

are the second moments of area of the cross section about the axes \(z\) and \(y\) and the product of inertia (deviacijski moment) of the cross section. The unit is length to the fourth, for example mm\(^4\) or m\(^4\).

Source: SADD (9.8.18) (PDF p. 276) writes \(I_{\mathrm{x}} = \iint y^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = -\iint x^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\) (with a minus) and \(I_{\mathrm{xy}} = \iint x y\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\). The minus at \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a misprint: from (9.8.15) follows \(B\iint x^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y + C\iint xy\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y = -P_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is (9.8.16) only if \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = +\iint x^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\), and in (9.8.19) the quantity \(I_{\mathrm{x}} I_{\mathrm{y}} - I_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\) uses exactly this positive \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) (Cauchy-Schwarz). So we do not adopt the minus, and our \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) is not negative by item 4 of lemma 4.10 anyway. SADD nowhere uses \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) with a sign as a convention of its flexure formula (PDF pp. 276-277). His axes of the cross section are \(x\) and \(y\), ours are \(y\) and \(z\). ONATE2 (1.5) and Fig. 1.3 (PDF pp. 34 and 35) has \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) with the axis \(z\) up. We have \(y\) up, so for bending in the plane \(x\)–\(y\) it is \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) that matters.

The name is similar to the name from the mechanics of a rigid body, where the moment of inertia (vztrajnostni moment) describes how a body resists rotation. Here it describes how a cross section resists bending, and it has nothing to do with inertia. The name is historical, and we will meet the mechanics of rotating bodies in chapter 8. Here \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) is a property of the figure. Since the integrand \(y^2\) is non-negative, by item 4 of lemma 4.10 the integral over each cell is non-negative, so \(I_{\mathrm{z}} \ge 0\).

Lemma 5.27 Second moment of a rectangle (vztrajnostni moment pravokotnika)

A rectangle of width \(b\) (along \(z\)) and height \(h\) (along \(y\)) with the origin at the centre, \([-h/2, h/2] \times [-b/2, b/2]\), has

\[A = b\,h , \qquad I_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{b\,h^3}{12} , \qquad I_{\mathrm{y}} = \frac{h\,b^3}{12} , \qquad I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 . \tag{5.18}\]
Proof

We must show four integrals, and we compute them as the iterated integrals of definition 4.8, the inner over \(z\), the outer over \(y\). For \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) we use the same computation with the roles exchanged, by theorem 4.9 on the order of integration.

  1. Area: \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \big( \int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2} \mathrm{d}z \big) \mathrm{d}y = \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} b\,\mathrm{d}y = b\,h\).
  2. For \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) the inner integral is \(\int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}z = b\,y^2\), because \(y^2\) is a constant in the inner integral.
  3. The outer integral is \(b \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = b \cdot \dfrac{1}{3}\Big[ y^3 \Big]_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2}\), which by the fundamental theorem of calculus equals \(\dfrac{b}{3} \Big( \dfrac{h^3}{8} + \dfrac{h^3}{8} \Big) = \dfrac{b\,h^3}{12}\).
  4. \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = h\,b^3/12\) after exchanging the roles \(y \leftrightarrow z\), \(b \leftrightarrow h\).
  5. For \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) the integrand is \(y\,z\) and by lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\) the integral is zero, since the rectangle is symmetric about the axis \(y\).

Source: ONATE2 Fig. 1.3 (PDF p. 35) states \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = b h^3/12\) for a rectangle with the axis \(z\) up, which is our \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\). The proof is not from a source.

For our bar \(b = 20\) mm and \(h = 40\) mm,

\[\begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{z}} &= \frac{20 \cdot 40^3}{12}\ \text{mm}^4 = 106\,667\ \text{mm}^4 = 1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4 , \\ I_{\mathrm{y}} &= \frac{40 \cdot 20^3}{12}\ \text{mm}^4 = 26\,667\ \text{mm}^4 . \end{aligned}\]

The bar standing on its edge therefore has a second moment four times that of the same bar lying flat, because the ratio \((h/b)^2 = 4\) enters as the square of the ratio of the sides.

Why does \(h^3\) appear in \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), and not \(h\)? We cut the cross section into strips of height \(\Delta y\). The strip at height \(y\) has the area \(b\,\Delta y\) and contributes \(y^2\,b\,\Delta y\) (figure 5.29). The contribution grows with the square of the distance from the axis, so the strips at the edge count much more than the strips near the axis. For our bar the outer half of the height, \(10\ \text{mm} \le |y| \le 20\ \text{mm}\), contributes

\[2 \cdot 20 \int_{10}^{20} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 2 \cdot 20 \cdot \frac{20^3 - 10^3}{3}\ \text{mm}^4 = \frac{280\,000}{3}\ \text{mm}^4 ,\]

and the inner half \(20 \int_{-10}^{10} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 40\,000/3\ \text{mm}^4\). The outer half of the height contributes \(87{,}5\ \%\) of the whole \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 320\,000/3\ \text{mm}^4\), the inner only \(12{,}5\ \%\). This is why we give cross sections a shape in which the material is as far from the axis as possible.

The integral is the limit of sums over strips. If we replace it by the sum over \(n\) strips, with the contribution computed at the middle of each strip, \(\sum y_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,b\,\Delta y\), we obtain for our cross section for \(n = 2, 4, 8\) and \(1000\) strips \(80\,000\), \(100\,000\), \(105\,000\) and \(106\,667\ \text{mm}^4\). The sum is always smaller, because the mean of \(y^2\) over a strip with the centre \(y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and half-height \(\delta\) is \(y_{\mathrm{k}}^2 + \delta^2/3\), which is more than the value at the middle. With more strips the error shrinks, since \(\delta\) gets smaller.

We computed the second moment of the rectangle about axes through its centre. In a composite figure each part has its own centroid and its own axes. We compute them by knowing how to transfer the second moment to a parallel axis, and the following theorem makes this possible.

Theorem 5.28 Parallel axis theorem (Steinerjev izrek)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the area \(A\), the centroid \(T\) and the second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) about axes through \(T\). Let \(y'\) and \(z'\) be the coordinates about axes parallel to the original ones, with the origin at a point \(O'\) in which the centroid \(T\) has the coordinates \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(d_{\mathrm{z}}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{z}}' &= \int_{\mathrm{L}} y'^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{z}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 , \\ I_{\mathrm{y}}' &= I_{\mathrm{y}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{z}}^2 , \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}}' &= I_{\mathrm{yz}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}\,d_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.19}\]
Proof

We must show that the difference is \(A\,d^2\), and that the term with \(d\) vanishes. The coordinates in the new system are \(y' = y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(z' = z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\), where \(y\) and \(z\) are the coordinates about \(T\).

  1. We expand the square, \(y'^2 = (y + d_{\mathrm{y}})^2 = y^2 + 2 d_{\mathrm{y}}\,y + d_{\mathrm{y}}^2\).
  2. By linearity of the integral (item 3 of lemma 5.18) \(I_{\mathrm{z}}' = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A + 2 d_{\mathrm{y}} \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A\).
  3. The second integral is zero by lemma 5.22, because the \(y\) are coordinates about the centroid. The third is \(A\). So \(I_{\mathrm{z}}' = I_{\mathrm{z}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2\).
  4. For \(I_{\mathrm{y}}'\) the computation is the same with \(z\) in place of \(y\). For \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}'\) we have \((y + d_{\mathrm{y}})(z + d_{\mathrm{z}}) = y z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\,y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\,z + d_{\mathrm{y}} d_{\mathrm{z}}\), and both middle integrals are zero by lemma 5.22, so \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}' = I_{\mathrm{yz}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}} d_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source, it is standard.

Let us test it on the bar (figure 5.30). The second moment about the lower edge, that is, about an axis that is \(d_{\mathrm{y}} = 20\) mm below the centroid, is by the theorem

\[I_{\mathrm{z}}' = 106\,667 + 800 \cdot 20^2 = 106\,667 + 320\,000 = 426\,667\ \text{mm}^4 .\]

Directly, with the integral from \(y = 0\) to \(y = 40\) mm, \(20 \int_0^{40} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 20 \cdot 40^3/3 = 426\,667\ \text{mm}^4\). The results agree. An axis that does not pass through the centroid gives a moment four times larger, because for \(d_{\mathrm{y}} = 20\) mm the term \(A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = 320\,000\ \text{mm}^4\) is three times larger than \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) itself.

The theorem holds only if one of the axes is centroidal. Let us try to use it without the centroid: from the axis through the bottom edge (\(I = 426\,667\) mm\(^4\)) we pass to the parallel axis through the top edge, which is at distance \(d = 40\) mm from it. We get \(426\,667 + 800 \cdot 40^2 = 426\,667 + 1\,280\,000 = 1\,706\,667\) mm\(^4\). The true value is \(426\,667\) mm\(^4\), because the bar is symmetric and the axis through the top edge is equal to the axis through the bottom edge, so the error is a factor of \(4\). In this passage the condition about the centroid is not met. In step 3 of the proof the term \(2 d_{\mathrm{y}} \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A\) dropped out, because \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) only for \(y\) measured from the centroid. With respect to the bottom edge the first moment is \(800 \cdot 20 = 16\,000\) mm\(^3\), and the dropped term at \(d = -40\) mm equals \(2 \cdot (-40) \cdot 16\,000 = -1\,280\,000\) mm\(^4\): exactly this cancels \(1\,280\,000\). The right way goes through the centroid, since the theorem has \(I_{\mathrm{z}}'\) only for an axis at distance \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\) from the centroidal one: first from the bottom edge to the centroidal axis, \(426\,667 - 800 \cdot 20^2 = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\), then from it to the top edge, \(106\,667 + 800 \cdot 20^2 = 426\,667\) mm\(^4\).

The parallel axis theorem allows us to compute the second moment of a composite figure from its parts. For an I-section 200 mm high, with flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm and a web \(6 \times 180\) mm, the centroid is at the middle, \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = 100\) mm from the lower edge, because the figure is symmetric about the horizontal axis (lemma 5.24, with the roles of the coordinates exchanged).

Example 5.29 Second moment of an I-section (vztrajnostni moment I-profila)

The cross section has three rectangles: the upper flange \(100 \times 10\) mm, the web \(6 \times 180\) mm and the lower flange \(100 \times 10\) mm (figure 5.31). We count the coordinate \(y\) from the centroid (the middle of the height).

  1. The area is \(A = 1000 + 1080 + 1000 = 3080\) mm\(^2\).
  2. A flange has, about its own centroid, by lemma 5.27 \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 100 \cdot 10^3/12 = 8333\) mm\(^4\). The centroid of the flange is at the distance \(d_{\mathrm{y}} = 95\) mm from the centroid of the cross section, so by theorem 5.28, equation (5.19), it contributes \(8333 + 1000 \cdot 95^2 = 8333 + 9\,025\,000 = 9\,033\,333\) mm\(^4\). The two flanges together give \(18\,066\,667\) mm\(^4\).
  3. The web has its centroid at the centroid of the cross section, \(d_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so its contribution is just \(6 \cdot 180^3/12 = 2\,916\,000\) mm\(^4\).
  4. By item 3 of lemma 5.18 the integral is the sum over the rectangles, so
    \[I_{\mathrm{z}} = 18\,066\,667 + 2\,916\,000 = 20\,982\,667\ \text{mm}^4 = 2{,}098 \cdot 10^{7}\ \text{mm}^4 .\]
  5. Let us compare with a rectangle of the same area (3080 mm\(^2\)), 20 mm wide and \(3080/20 = 154\) mm high, which has \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 20 \cdot 154^3/12 = 6\,087\,107\) mm\(^4\). The I-section has a second moment \(20\,982\,667 / 6\,087\,107 = 3{,}45\) times larger for the same mass. The reason is that the flanges, which have almost all the area at the edge, lie \(95\) mm from the axis and each contributes \(9{,}03 \cdot 10^6\) mm\(^4\), while the web, which has \(35\ \%\) of the area, contributes only \(14\ \%\) of the total.

For a circle the computation over rectangles is not possible, because it is not a figure made of rectangles, and its integral has a form that we know how to compute only in polar coordinates. We therefore take the value from a source and postpone the proof to chapter 16. A circle of radius \(R\) has, by ONATE2 (Fig. 1.3, PDF p. 35), the area \(A = \pi R^2\) and \(I = \pi R^4/4\), which for the diameter \(d = 2R\) equals \(I = \pi d^4/64\). A circle of diameter \(d = 32\) mm has the area \(804\) mm\(^2\), almost the same as our bar, and \(I = \pi \cdot 32^4/64 = 51\,472\) mm\(^4\). This is less than half of \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\) of the bar standing on its edge, because the bar has more material far from the axis.

5.4.4 Principal axes of a cross section

Cross sections with an axis of symmetry \(y\) or \(z\) have \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\), as in item 5 of the proof of lemma 5.27). For a cross section without an axis of symmetry, for example the letter L, we do not know in advance how the second moments change if we turn the axes. We will need this for a beam that bends in the planes \(x\)–\(y\) and \(x\)–\(z\) at the same time (later in this chapter). What happens to the three numbers \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) if we turn the axes around the centroid by an angle \(\varphi\)?

We leave the axis \(x\) and turn the axes \(y\) and \(z\) around it by the angle \(\varphi\) in the direction from \(y\) toward \(z\), which is a turn in the positive direction about the axis \(x\). Let \(\bar y\) and \(\bar z\) be the coordinates about the turned axes with unit vectors that have in the old axes the components \((c, s)\) and \((-s, c)\), where \(c = \cos\varphi\) and \(s = \sin\varphi\). As in definition 2.37, these are the rows of the rotation matrix, except that the axes \(y\) and \(z\) take the roles of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) of the definition. A point with the coordinates \((y, z)\) has in the new axes the coordinates

\[\bar y = c\,y + s\,z , \qquad \bar z = -s\,y + c\,z .\]

By theorem 2.42 the components of stress in the plane transform in this way. The second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are integrals of the squares and the product of the coordinates, and in the next theorem we show by computation that they change by the same rule as the stresses.

Theorem 5.30 Rotation of the axes of a cross section (vrtenje osi prereza)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles with the origin in the centroid have the second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\). About the axes turned by the angle \(\varphi\) the second moments are

\[\begin{aligned} \bar I_{\mathrm{z}} &= c^2\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + s^2\,I_{\mathrm{y}} + 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} , \\ \bar I_{\mathrm{y}} &= s^2\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + c^2\,I_{\mathrm{y}} - 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} , \\ \bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} &= -cs\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + cs\,I_{\mathrm{y}} + (c^2 - s^2)\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.20}\]

The sum \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} + \bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on the angle.

Proof

We must show three integrals and a sum. We obtain each by writing the coordinates with the new ones and splitting the integral by linearity (item 3 of lemma 5.18).

  1. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (c\,y + s\,z)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = c^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A + 2cs \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A + s^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) equals \(c^2 I_{\mathrm{z}} + 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + s^2 I_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  2. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (-s\,y + c\,z)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = s^2 I_{\mathrm{z}} - 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + c^2 I_{\mathrm{y}}\), the same computation with the sign \(-\) at the middle term.
  3. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (c\,y + s\,z)(-s\,y + c\,z)\,\mathrm{d}A\). The expansion of the product is \(-cs\,y^2 + c^2\,y z - s^2\,y z + cs\,z^2\), so \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = -cs\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + (c^2 - s^2)\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + cs\,I_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. The sum of the first two equations is \((c^2 + s^2)(I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}) = I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}\), because \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\) and the terms with \(2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) cancel.

Source: Our sources have no formula for the transformation of second moments. The computation is the same as for the transformation of stress in chapter 2, the proof is ours.

Equations (5.20) have the same form as the first three equations of (2.45) for stresses, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \to I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \to I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \to I_{\mathrm{yz}}\). The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} I_{\mathrm{z}} & I_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}} & I_{\mathrm{y}} \end{pmatrix}\) is therefore here what the matrix of stress in the plane is. It is symmetric and transforms like the matrix of stress, so the angle we look for is the one at which this matrix is diagonal, that is, \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). Theorem 2.61 on principal directions holds for matrices with three rows, so we show the existence of the angle here directly with the intermediate value theorem, which is shorter than embedding the \(2 \times 2\) matrix in a \(3 \times 3\) matrix and showing that both principal directions lie in the plane of the cross section.

Corollary 5.31 Principal axes of a cross section (glavne osi prereza)

For every figure made of rectangles with the origin in the centroid there is an angle \(\varphi\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) at which \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The axes that belong to this angle are called the principal axes (glavni osi) of the cross section, and the numbers \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}}\) the principal second moments. If the figure is symmetric about the axis \(y\) or about the axis \(z\), the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are principal axes.

Proof

We must show that the function \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}}(\varphi)\) has a zero. We use the intermediate value theorem.

  1. By equation (5.20) with double angles, \(2cs = \sin 2\varphi\) and \(c^2 - s^2 = \cos 2\varphi\), \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}}(\varphi) = \tfrac12 (I_{\mathrm{y}} - I_{\mathrm{z}})\sin 2\varphi + I_{\mathrm{yz}}\cos 2\varphi\). This is a continuous function of the angle.
  2. At \(\varphi = 0\) the value is \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\), at \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) it is \(-I_{\mathrm{yz}}\). If \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), the axes are already principal. Otherwise the values have opposite signs, and by the intermediate value theorem 2.57 there is an angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) at which \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\).
  3. If the figure is symmetric about the axis \(y\), then \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\), and for symmetry about the axis \(z\) with the roles of the coordinates exchanged. The axes \(y\) and \(z\) are therefore principal, as for \(\varphi = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Theorem 2.61 holds for \(3 \times 3\) matrices, so this proof replaces it for a \(2 \times 2\) matrix by the intermediate value theorem.

Let us look at what the principal axes look like for a cross section without symmetry, on the letter L.

Example 5.32 Principal axes of an L-section (glavne osi prereza L)

The L-section has a vertical leg \(10 \times 60\) mm (\(z\) from 0 to 10, \(y\) from 0 to 60) and a horizontal leg \(30 \times 10\) mm (\(z\) from 10 to 40, \(y\) from 0 to 10), together the area \(A = 600 + 300 = 900\) mm\(^2\) (figure 5.32).

  1. By lemma 5.23 \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = (600 \cdot 30 + 300 \cdot 5)/900 = 65/3 = 21{,}67\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{T}} = (600 \cdot 5 + 300 \cdot 25)/900 = 35/3 = 11{,}67\) mm.
  2. The centroid of the vertical leg has, with respect to the centroid of the cross section, the coordinates \((d_{\mathrm{y}}, d_{\mathrm{z}}) = (25/3, -20/3)\) mm, and the centroid of the horizontal leg \((-50/3, 40/3)\) mm (the differences \(30 - 65/3\), \(5 - 35/3\) and \(5 - 65/3\), \(25 - 35/3\)). The own second moments by lemma 5.27 are \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 10 \cdot 60^3/12 = 180\,000\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = 60 \cdot 10^3/12 = 5000\) mm\(^4\) for the vertical one, and \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 30 \cdot 10^3/12 = 2500\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = 10 \cdot 30^3/12 = 22\,500\) mm\(^4\) for the horizontal one, the own products of inertia are zero.
  3. By theorem 5.28 \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = (180\,000 + 600 \cdot (25/3)^2) + (2500 + 300 \cdot (50/3)^2) = 221\,667 + 85\,833 = 307\,500\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = (5000 + 600 \cdot (20/3)^2) + (22\,500 + 300 \cdot (40/3)^2) = 31\,667 + 75\,833 = 107\,500\) mm\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 600 \cdot \tfrac{25}{3} \cdot (-\tfrac{20}{3}) + 300 \cdot (-\tfrac{50}{3}) \cdot \tfrac{40}{3} = -33\,333 - 66\,667 = -100\,000\) mm\(^4\).
  4. The product of inertia is not zero, because the figure has no axis of symmetry. The condition \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) is by step 1 of the proof of corollary 5.31 equivalent to \(\tan 2\varphi = 2 I_{\mathrm{yz}}/(I_{\mathrm{z}} - I_{\mathrm{y}})\) for \(\cos 2\varphi \ne 0\), which is \(-200\,000/200\,000 = -1\), so \(2\varphi = 135^\circ\) and \(\varphi = 67{,}5^\circ\).
  5. At this angle \(c^2 = 0{,}1464\), \(s^2 = 0{,}8536\) and \(2cs = \sin 135^\circ = 0{,}7071\), \(c^2 - s^2 = -0{,}7071\). By theorem 5.30 \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} = 0{,}1464 \cdot 307\,500 + 0{,}8536 \cdot 107\,500 + 0{,}7071 \cdot (-100\,000) = 66\,079\) mm\(^4\), \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = 348\,921\) mm\(^4\) and \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (up to rounding). The sum \(66\,079 + 348\,921 = 415\,000 = 307\,500 + 107\,500\) mm\(^4\), as the theorem requires.
  6. The principal second moments are also the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} I_{\mathrm{z}} & I_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}} & I_{\mathrm{y}} \end{pmatrix}\) (definition 2.59): the zeros of the determinant \((I_{\mathrm{z}} - \lambda)(I_{\mathrm{y}} - \lambda) - I_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 = (307\,500 - \lambda)(107\,500 - \lambda) - 10^{10}\) are \(\lambda = 207\,500 \pm 100\,000\sqrt2\ \text{mm}^4\), which is \(66\,079\) and \(348\,921\) mm\(^4\), the same as in step 5.

We will need the principal axes of a cross section for bending in two planes. For cross sections with an axis of symmetry, such as the rectangle and the I-section, nothing needs to be turned, because this axis is already principal.
