Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.4 Mathematics: centroid and second moment of a cross section

The stress in a beam changes over the cross section. In tension it is the same everywhere, in bending it is tensile on one side of the cross section and compressive on the opposite side, and in between it passes through zero (figure 5.59). The forces and moments of the cross section are therefore integrals of the stress over the cross section, and since the stress will be a constant or proportional to the distance \(y\) from some axis, we will compute three kinds of integrals over the area of the cross section: \(\int \mathrm{d}A\), \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). Why these three? The force on a small piece of the cross section is the stress times the area of the piece. A constant stress \(c\) therefore gives the force \(c \int \mathrm{d}A\). The stress \(k\,y\) gives the force \(k \int y\,\mathrm{d}A\). The force of a piece at the height \(y\) has the lever arm \(y\) about the axis \(z\), so the moment of the stress \(k\,y\) is \(k \int y \cdot y\,\mathrm{d}A = k \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). For each cross section these are three numbers that we compute once and afterwards only read. The question of this section is what these numbers mean and how to compute them for a given figure.

The cross section (prerez) is a figure in a plane perpendicular to the axis of the beam, that is, in the plane \(x = \text{const.}\), in figure 5.59 the front face of the beam. We look at the cross section along the axis \(x\), from the left end of the beam toward the right, in the direction of the blue arrow, so that the axis \(x\) points away from us, the axis \(y\) points up and the axis \(z\) points to the right. In figure 5.13 we looked at the same cross section from the opposite side, so there the axis \(z\) pointed to the left. This system is right-handed, because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times \vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\): the thumb of the right hand points away from us, the index finger up and the middle finger to the right. We describe the points of the figure by the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\), and for now we choose the origin anywhere. As an example we will use the cross section of a steel flat bar, \(b = 20\) mm wide and \(h = 40\) mm high, standing on its edge (the width along \(z\), the height along \(y\)), and next to it three composite figures that often appear in beams, the letter T, the letter L and the double T, which we call the I-section.

5.4.1 Integral over a figure

In chapter 4 we defined the integral over a box as an iterated integral and extended it to bodies assembled from the cells of a grid. For a rectangle in the plane \(x = c\) the two iterated integrals run over the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) (definition 4.8). All the cross sections we will compute are made of rectangles whose sides are parallel to the coordinate axes. For such composite figures we can take the same step in the plane: we cut the figure into the cells of a grid, as the T section is cut in figure 5.61, and add up the integrals over the cells.

Definition 5.17 Figure made of rectangles (lik iz pravokotnikov)

Let \(R = [y_{\mathrm{a}}, y_{\mathrm{b}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{a}}, z_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a rectangle in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\). The partitions \(y_{\mathrm{a}} = y_0 < y_1 < \dots < y_{\mathrm{m}} = y_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{a}} = z_0 < z_1 < \dots < z_{\mathrm{n}} = z_{\mathrm{b}}\) cut \(R\) into the cells \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = [y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{j}-1}, z_{\mathrm{j}}]\), and this is the grid. A set of points \(L\) is a figure made of rectangles (lik iz pravokotnikov) if it is the union of some cells of this grid, at least one.

  1. The integral of a continuous function \(f(y, z)\) over the figure \(L\) is the sum of the integrals over its cells,
    \[\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{ij}} \subseteq \mathrm{L}} \int_{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{ij}}} f\,\mathrm{d}A ,\]
    where the integral over a cell is the iterated integral of definition 4.8, first over \(z\), then over \(y\). The integral is a number.
  2. The area (ploščina) of the figure \(L\) is the number \(A = \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A\).

Source: An analogue of definition 4.11 of chapter 4, in the plane. The definition is not from a source.

By item 2 of lemma 4.10 the area of a rectangle is \(A = (y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})(z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\), which is the familiar area of a rectangle. Our bar therefore has \(A = 20 \cdot 40 = 800\) mm\(^2\).

Not every figure is a figure made of rectangles. Take the right triangle with the three vertices \((z, y) = (0, 0)\), \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), whose hypotenuse is the segment \(y + z = 1\). The definition demands a single grid with finitely many partition points whose cells fill the triangle. The triangle is therefore not a figure made of rectangles if for no grid it is the union of its cells. We show this as follows, on figure 5.60, in which the axis \(z\) points horizontally and the axis \(y\) vertically.

  1. Suppose the triangle is the union of the cells of some grid that has finitely many dividing lines \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(z = z_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. Suppose that some point \(P\) of the hypotenuse lies on no dividing line. It lies in some cell, because the triangle is the union of cells, and not on its sides, so in the interior of the cell. Hence \(P\) has a neighbourhood that lies entirely in the cell and thus in the triangle.
  3. But in every neighbourhood of the point \(P = (z_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}})\) there is the point \((z_{\mathrm{P}} + \varepsilon, y_{\mathrm{P}} + \varepsilon)\) with small \(\varepsilon > 0\), which has \(y + z = 1 + 2\varepsilon > 1\) and is outside the triangle. This is a contradiction, so every point of the hypotenuse lies on a dividing line.
  4. A line \(y = c\) or \(z = c\) meets the hypotenuse in at most one point, in \((1 - c, c)\) or \((c, 1 - c)\) respectively when \(0 \le c \le 1\), and otherwise not at all. Finitely many dividing lines therefore cover finitely many points of the hypotenuse, but the hypotenuse has infinitely many, since \((t, 1 - t)\) is its point for every \(t \in [0, 1]\). This is a contradiction, so the triangle is not a figure made of rectangles.

We can approach the triangle as closely as we like (figure 5.60). A stepped figure with \(n\) equal steps, which has columns of width \(1/n\) and heights \(1 - i/n\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\), is a figure made of rectangles with area \(\tfrac1n \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} (1 - i/n) = (n - 1)/(2n)\). For \(n = 4\) this is \(3/8\), for \(n = 10\) it is \(9/20\). The area is smaller than \(1/2\) for every \(n\), because the orange triangles along the hypotenuse remain uncovered. There are \(n\) of them, each with legs \(1/n\), together with area \(n \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{n^2} = \tfrac{1}{2n}\), which for \(n = 10\) is only \(1/20\) and goes to zero as \(n\) grows. The number \(n\) can be arbitrarily large, but every grid has finitely many partition points. So by item 4 it is not the area that rejects the triangle but the condition of a finite grid.

A figure can be cut into cells in several ways, and also a figure made of a few larger rectangles is cut into the cells of a grid that contains all their sides (figure 5.61). The T-section is the union of the web \(P_1\) and the flange \(P_2\), and the double T is the union of the flanges \(P_1\) and \(P_3\) and the web \(P_2\). The axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie in the plane of the cross section, the numbers next to the T-section are its measures in millimetres. The blue dashed line \(y = c\) is an extra line of the grid that we will need in the proof: it cuts the cell of the web into two halves, and likewise the grey cell that is not part of the figure. The integral must always be the same, and the following lemma guarantees this. The first part is the same argument as for bodies made of boxes (lemma 4.12), and the second part will let us add up the integral over a composite figure part by part.

We have the same figure with two grids. The first grid has lines through all sides of the rectangles. The second grid has one more line, which cuts a cell of the web into two halves, and the other cells remain. Since the second grid has all the lines of the first, it is the common grid of both.

The two grids can also be such that neither has all the lines of the other, as in figure 5.63. Grid \(A\) has the line \(z = c_1\) through the flange, and grid \(B\) has the line \(y = c_2\) through the web and reaches further to the right. The common grid has all the lines of both, on a rectangle that contains both original ones. Grid \(A\) thereby gains a column of new cells, all of which lie outside the figure. From each of the two grids we reach the common one by adding lines one at a time, each time as in figure 5.62.

Lemma 5.18 The integral over a figure does not depend on the grid (integral po liku ni odvisen od mreže)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles and \(f\) a continuous function on \(L\).

  1. The integral \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\) and the area of the figure \(L\) do not depend on the grid by which we cut \(L\) into cells.
  2. Let \(L\) be the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with sides parallel to the coordinate axes, whose interiors do not overlap. Then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}=1}^{k} \int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} f\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
  3. The integral over a figure is linear: \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} (\alpha f + \beta g)\,\mathrm{d}A = \alpha \int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A + \beta \int_{\mathrm{L}} g\,\mathrm{d}A\) for continuous functions \(f\), \(g\) and numbers \(\alpha\), \(\beta\).
Proof

We must show that the sum over the cells does not depend on the grid, that it equals the sum over the rectangles of which the figure is made, and that it is linear. We prove the first by adding lines to the grid one at a time, as in lemma 4.12.

1. Independence of the grid.

  1. Let the second grid have one more line, for example \(y = c\) with \(y_{\mathrm{i}-1} < c < y_{\mathrm{i}}\). The line cuts each cell between \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(y = y_{\mathrm{i}}\) into two halves, the other cells remain. A half of a cell that is not in \(L\) is not in \(L\) either, because its points that lie on no line of the grid lie in no other cell of the old grid. So \(L\) is the union of the uncut cells and the halves of the cut cells.
  2. By item 3 of lemma 4.10, applied to a rectangle, the integral over a cut cell is the sum of the integrals over the halves. The sum over the cells is therefore the same in both grids. We proceed in the same way for a line \(z = c\).
  3. Let two arbitrary grids for \(L\) be given. We extend both to a common rectangle that contains both original ones, by adding end points to the partitions. The new cells lie outside the original rectangle, which contains \(L\), so they are not cells of the figure and the sum does not change. The grid that has all the lines of both grids is obtained from each of them by adding lines one at a time. By steps 1 and 2 each of the two grids gives the same sum as this common grid. With \(f = 1\) this holds for the area.

2. Additivity over rectangles.

  1. We take a grid whose lines are all the coordinates of the sides of the rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\). Each \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) is a union of cells of this grid, because none of its sides is inside a cell, so \(L\) too is a union of cells of this grid and this grid cuts \(L\) into cells. By item 3 of lemma 4.10 the integral over \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the sum of the integrals over the cells in \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  2. Each cell of the figure \(L\) lies in some \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), because it is in \(L\) and so its interior points lie in some \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), which is a union of cells. It does not lie in two rectangles, because their interiors would overlap. The sum over the rectangles is therefore the sum over the cells of the figure \(L\) in this grid, which by the first part is the sum that defines \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\).

3. Linearity.

  1. By item 1 of lemma 4.10, applied to a rectangle, the integral over each cell is linear. A sum of linear expressions over the cells is linear.

Source: The proof is not from a source, it is the two-dimensional proof of lemma 4.12.

The lemma says two things. The integral over a figure made of rectangles is a well-defined number, even if we take another cutting for the grid. In addition, we can always compute it over the rectangles of which the figure is made, which we will do for all cross sections. The T-section of figure 5.61 has the area \(A = 1200 + 1600 = 2800\) mm\(^2\) if the web is 20 mm wide and 60 mm high and the flange is 80 mm wide and 20 mm high, as the measures in the figure show (we compute both numbers by item 2 of lemma 4.10 as the area of a rectangle).

For the forces and moments of the stress in a cross section we still need a corollary that extends lemma 5.9 from a rectangle to every figure made of rectangles. The moment is computed about a point \(C\) in the plane of the cross section that is not necessarily the centre of a rectangle, so we say so in the statement.

On the left of figure 5.64 is the T cross section of figure 5.61, made of the web \(P_1\) and the flange \(P_2\). The point \(C\) is at the bottom of the web, from it we measure the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\), and it is the centre of neither the web nor the flange. On the pieces of the web act stresses, which we draw in blue, on the pieces of the flange stresses, which we draw in orange. Only the two components in the plane of the cross section are drawn, the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) points perpendicular to the plane of the drawing. The moment of a piece at the point \(Q\) about \(C\) is computed with the vector \(\vek r\) from \(C\) to \(Q\). On the right is the flange once more on its own, with the same point \(C\) outside it, and in it we will repeat the proof of the lemma.

Corollary 5.19 Internal forces from stresses for a composite cross section (notranje sile iz napetosti za sestavljen prerez)

Let the cross section of a beam at \(x\) be a figure \(L\) made of rectangles (definition 5.17), so a union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with sides parallel to the coordinate axes and non-overlapping interiors (for example its cells), let \(C = (x, 0, 0)\) be a point of its plane from which the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) are measured, and let the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) be continuous functions on \(L\). Let the hypotheses (H1), (H2) and the additivity of forces and moments of lemma 5.9 hold on each \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) in place of \(A\), and let the force and the moment on \(L\) be the sums of the forces and moments on the \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\). Then for \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) about \(C\) equations (5.4) and (5.5) hold with integrals over \(L\).

Proof

We must show that the forces and moments of the rectangles of which \(L\) is made, added up, give the same as the integrals over \(L\).

  1. \(L\) is the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with non-overlapping interiors, like the web \(P_1\) and the flange \(P_2\) of the T-section. By hypothesis the force on \(L\) is the sum of the forces on the individual \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), and the moment about \(C\) is the sum of the moments. This is the same hypothesis as the additivity in the lemma, since these are the resultant and the moment of the forces on pieces of the surface (definitions 2.2 and 2.37).
  2. Steps 1 to 6 of the proof of lemma 5.9 use only the hypotheses (H1), (H2) and the additivity, that the surface is a rectangle and that the vector from \(C\) to a point of the surface is \(\vek r = (0, y, z)\). Steps 1 to 4 give the force, steps 5 and 6 the moment. They nowhere use the centre of the rectangle, step 3 only that the coordinates of the points of the rectangle are bounded as the rectangles \(R_{\mathrm{n}}\) shrink to the point \(P\). So they hold for every \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) and give the force \(\int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\) and the moment \(\int_{\mathrm{P}_\mathrm{m}} \vek r \times \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  3. The components of \(\vek t\) and \(\vek r \times \vek t\) are continuous functions on \(L\). By item 2 of lemma 5.18 the sum of the integrals over the \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\) equals the integral over \(L\). Adding over \(m\) gives for \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) the same expressions as in the lemma, with integrals over \(L\).

5.4.2 Centroid

Why do we need the second number, \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\)? Let us look at the bar from the side (left part of figure 5.65), so that we see the cross section as a vertical line. The axis \(x\) points out of the cross section, the axis \(y\) upward, and the origin is the point \(O\). Take a uniform stress \(\sigma\) pulling the cross section, that is, a force \(\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) on each piece of area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) around the point \((0, y_{\mathrm{j}}, z_{\mathrm{j}})\). The force acts at this point in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}} = (\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}, 0, 0)\), so its moment about the origin (definition 2.37) is \((0, y_{\mathrm{j}}, z_{\mathrm{j}}) \times (\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}, 0, 0) = (0,\ z_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}},\ -y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}})\). The component \(z\) is seen in the figure: the force acts \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) above the point \(O\), this is its lever arm, and turns clockwise, hence the minus. The component \(y\) would be seen in the same way in the view from above, with the lever arm \(z_{\mathrm{j}}\). All the pieces together give the component \(z\) of the moment \(-\sigma \sum y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the component \(y\) of the moment \(\sigma \sum z_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\). For a finer partition the sums approach the integrals \(-\sigma \int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\sigma \int z\,\mathrm{d}A\).

A uniform tension should be only a force without a moment, so both integrals must be zero. When are they? The piece at height \(-y_{\mathrm{j}}\) carries the same force and turns in the opposite sense, so the moments of this pair cancel. In the bar every piece above \(O\) has such a partner below \(O\) if \(O\) is at mid height, and the sum is zero. If we moved \(O\) to the lower edge, all the pieces would turn in the same sense. The integral would be the area \(20 \cdot 40 = 800\) mm\(^2\) times the mean height of the pieces above the lower edge, which is 20 mm, \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 800 \cdot 20 = 16\,000\) mm\(^3\), and the tension would bend the bar as well. So the integrals are zero only if the axes pass through the right point of the figure. How do we find it for a figure without such a symmetry?

Definition 5.20 First moment of area (statični moment)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles (definition 5.17) in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) and the origin \(O\). The first moments of the figure \(L\) about the axes \(z\) and \(y\) are the numbers, in which each piece of area \(\mathrm{d}A\) counts with its distance from the axis

\[S_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad S_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} z\,\mathrm{d}A .\]

The unit is length cubed, for example mm\(^3\).

Source: The definition is not from a source.

The index tells the axis about which we count: \(S_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the first moment about the axis \(z\), where \(y\) is the distance from this axis, and \(S_{\mathrm{y}}\) about the axis \(y\), where the distance is \(z\). The first moment is the area weighted by the distance from the axis. It is called a moment because it is built like the moment of a force: in place of the force stands the area of a piece, multiplied by the lever arm \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\). A piece above the axis counts as positive, a piece below it as negative. So it can be positive, zero or negative, depending on which side of the axis the figure mostly lies.

Definition 5.21 Centroid (težišče)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the area \(A > 0\). The centroid (težišče) of the figure \(L\) is the point \(T\) with the coordinates

\[y_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{z}}}{A} , \qquad z_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{y}}}{A} .\]

Source: ONATE2 (Fig. 1.3, PDF p. 35) gives the centre of gravity G of cross sections as known. The definition is not from a source.

The next lemma says that the first moments are zero if we put the origin in the centroid. We know this from experience: a ruler rests on a finger when we support it at its middle. A plate of equal thickness and density in the shape of the figure therefore stays in balance when supported at the centroid, like the rod with two weights on the support in the right part of figure 5.68. Hence the Slovenian name težišče, from teža, weight: it is the point at which we may think of the whole weight of the plate as gathered. The reason is the same computation as above, with the weight pulling in place of the stress: the weight of a piece of area \(\Delta A\) is proportional to \(\Delta A\), and the moment of the weight about an axis through the centroid is proportional to the sum \(\sum y'_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\) or \(\sum z'_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(y'_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(z'_{\mathrm{j}}\) are the offsets of the piece from the axes through the centroid. The sums approach the integrals \(\int y'\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int z'\,\mathrm{d}A\), and both are zero. The condition of equal thickness and density matters here, because only then is the weight of a piece proportional to its area. A plate that is thicker on one side has its point of balance shifted toward the thicker side, just as the heavier weight draws the support toward itself, while the centroid of the figure stays where it is, since it depends only on the shape.

Lemma 5.22 First moments about axes through the centroid (statična momenta glede na osi skozi težišče)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the centroid \(T\) with the coordinates \((y_{\mathrm{T}}, z_{\mathrm{T}})\), and let \(y' = y - y_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(z' = z - z_{\mathrm{T}}\) be the coordinates about axes through \(T\), parallel to the original ones. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{L}} y'\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \qquad \int_{\mathrm{L}} z'\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]
Proof

We must show that subtracting \(y_{\mathrm{T}} A\) from \(S_{\mathrm{z}}\) leaves exactly zero. On the balance this means that we no longer measure the distances from the left end but from the support: each of them decreases by the same \(y_{\mathrm{T}}\). The computation is one line.

  1. Each distance from the support is the distance from the origin minus \(y_{\mathrm{T}}\). By linearity (item 3 of lemma 5.18) \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} (y - y_{\mathrm{T}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A - y_{\mathrm{T}} \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A = S_{\mathrm{z}} - y_{\mathrm{T}} A\).
  2. By definition 5.21 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = y_{\mathrm{T}} A\), so the difference is zero, which on the balance means equilibrium about the support at the centroid. The same holds for \(z'\) and \(S_{\mathrm{y}} = z_{\mathrm{T}} A\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

From now on, except where we say otherwise, we will use the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) with the origin in the centroid, as is usual for beams. The reason is the start of this section: only with this origin does a uniform tension have no moment (the pairs cancel), so we will be able to treat tension and bending separately. With another origin every axial force \(N\) would bring a moment \(N\) times the distance to the centroid. For a rectangle and for a composite figure we compute the centroid as follows.

Lemma 5.23 Centroid of a rectangle and of a union (težišče pravokotnika in sestavljenega lika)
  1. The centroid of the rectangle \([y_{\mathrm{a}}, y_{\mathrm{b}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{a}}, z_{\mathrm{b}}]\) is its centre, as for the bar, \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = (y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}})/2\) and \(z_{\mathrm{T}} = (z_{\mathrm{a}} + z_{\mathrm{b}})/2\).
  2. Let \(L\) be the union of rectangles \(P_1, \dots, P_{\mathrm{k}}\) with areas \(A_{\mathrm{m}}\) and centroids \((y_{\mathrm{m}}, z_{\mathrm{m}})\), whose interiors do not overlap. Then
    \[y_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\,y_{\mathrm{m}}}{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}} , \qquad z_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\,z_{\mathrm{m}}}{\sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}} .\]
Proof

We must show that the first moment of the figure is the sum of the first moments of the parts, and then that the first moment of a rectangle is the product of the area and the coordinate of the centre.

1. Rectangle.

  1. By definition 4.8 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{y}_\mathrm{a}}^{y_{\mathrm{b}}} \big( \int_{\mathrm{z}_\mathrm{a}}^{z_{\mathrm{b}}} y\,\mathrm{d}z \big)\,\mathrm{d}y\). The inner integral is \(y\,(z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\), because \(y\) is a constant in the inner integral: this is a strip across the whole width, all of it at the same distance \(y\) from the axis.
  2. The outer integral adds up the strips along the height \(y\) and is \((z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}}) \int_{\mathrm{y}_\mathrm{a}}^{y_{\mathrm{b}}} y\,\mathrm{d}y = (z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})\,\dfrac{y_{\mathrm{b}}^2 - y_{\mathrm{a}}^2}{2} = A\,\dfrac{y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}\), because \(y_{\mathrm{b}}^2 - y_{\mathrm{a}}^2 = (y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})(y_{\mathrm{b}} + y_{\mathrm{a}})\) and \(A = (z_{\mathrm{b}} - z_{\mathrm{a}})(y_{\mathrm{b}} - y_{\mathrm{a}})\). So \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = S_{\mathrm{z}}/A = (y_{\mathrm{a}} + y_{\mathrm{b}})/2\). For \(z_{\mathrm{T}}\) the computation is the same, with the roles of the coordinates exchanged by theorem 4.9 on the order of integration.

2. Composite figure.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 5.18 \(S_{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}}\) and \(A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\), where \(S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}}\) is the first moment of the rectangle \(P_{\mathrm{m}}\), because \(f = y\) and \(f = 1\) are continuous functions.
  2. By step 2 of the first part \(S_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{m}} = A_{\mathrm{m}}\,y_{\mathrm{m}}\), for the T-section these are the web with its centroid at \(y_1\) and the flange with its centroid at \(y_2\). So \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = S_{\mathrm{z}}/A = \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}} y_{\mathrm{m}} / \sum_{\mathrm{m}} A_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(z_{\mathrm{T}}\) the computation is the same.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The lemma says that the centroid of a composite figure is a weighted mean of the centroids of the parts, where the weights are the areas: the support of the balance stands between the two weights, closer to the heavier one. If the figure has a symmetry, the computation becomes even shorter. Take a figure that the axis \(y\) divides into two mirror halves (figure 5.66, the axis \(z\) is horizontal).

Lemma 5.24 Centroid of a symmetric figure (težišče simetričnega lika)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles that is symmetric about the axis \(y\): the point \((y, z)\) is in \(L\) exactly when the point \((y, -z)\) is in \(L\). Let \(g\) be a continuous function of the variable \(y\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{L}} z\,g(y)\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]

In particular \(S_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) (with \(g = 1\)), so the centroid lies on the axis of symmetry.

Proof

We must show that the integrals over cells that are mirror images of each other cancel. The reason is in the pair of mirror points: both are at the same height \(y\), so \(g(y)\) has the same value at both, while \(z\) has opposite signs. This is why the lemma requires \(g\) to depend on \(y\) only. A function that differed on the left and on the right of the axis, for example \(g = z\), would not cancel: \(\int_{\mathrm{L}} z \cdot z\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{y}} > 0\). We choose a symmetric grid.

  1. To the grid that cuts \(L\) we add the mirror lines \(z = -z_{\mathrm{j}}\). By item 1 of lemma 5.18 the integral does not change, and the grid is now symmetric. The mirror image of a cell of the figure \(L\) is therefore a cell of the grid, and it lies in \(L\), because \(L\) is symmetric.
  2. By definition 4.8 the cell \([y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [z_{\mathrm{j}-1}, z_{\mathrm{j}}]\) has the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{y}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^{y_{\mathrm{i}}} g(y) \big( \int_{\mathrm{z}_{\mathrm{j}-1}}^{z_{\mathrm{j}}} z\,\mathrm{d}z \big)\,\mathrm{d}y\), because \(g(y)\) is a constant in the inner integral. The inner integral is \((z_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}-1}^2)/2\).
  3. The mirror cell is \([y_{\mathrm{i}-1}, y_{\mathrm{i}}] \times [-z_{\mathrm{j}}, -z_{\mathrm{j}-1}]\) and has the inner integral \((z_{\mathrm{j}-1}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}}^2)/2\), the opposite number. The integrals of the two cells therefore cancel. A cell that lies on the axis, \(z_{\mathrm{j}-1} = -z_{\mathrm{j}}\), is its own mirror image, and its inner integral is \((z_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - z_{\mathrm{j}}^2)/2 = 0\).
  4. The sum over the cells of the figure \(L\) is a sum of pairs and cells on the axis, so it is zero.

5.4.3 Second moment of area of a cross section

In bending the stress grows in proportion to the distance from some axis, \(\sigma = k\,y\): it pulls above the axis and pushes below it (right part of figure 5.65, where the same bar has the same axes). We derive the relation in 5.5.1, and it holds as long as the cross sections stay plane and the material is linearly elastic below the yield stress. Once the material yields, the stress is no longer proportional to \(y\) and the computation below no longer holds. The distance \(y\) now enters twice. First in the size of the force: the piece at height \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) carries the force \(k\,y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}}\), twice as large twice as far from the axis. Second as the lever arm \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) with which this force turns about the point \(O\). The moment of the piece is therefore

\[-y_{\mathrm{j}} \cdot k\,y_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}} = -k\,y_{\mathrm{j}}^2\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{j}} .\]

The mirror piece below the axis pushes instead of pulling, and it acts below \(O\). Both signs flip, so it turns in the same sense as the upper one: \((-y_{\mathrm{j}})^2 = y_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). In tension the moments of the pair cancelled, here they add. The component \(z\) of the moment of the stress is, by the computation above, \(-\int y \cdot k y\,\mathrm{d}A = -k \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). A third integral appears. First we name it, then we compute it for a rectangle and for composite figures.

In figure 5.69 we first draw the figure \(L\), then through its centroid the axis \(y\) upward and the axis \(z\) horizontally. In the figure we choose a piece of area \(\mathrm{d}A\). Its distance from the axis \(z\) is \(y\) and its distance from the axis \(y\) is \(z\).

Definition 5.26 Second moment of area (vztrajnostni moment prereza)

Let \(L\) be a figure made of rectangles in the plane with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) with the origin in the centroid (lemma 5.22). The numbers

\[I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad I_{\mathrm{yz}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A\]

are the second moments of area of the cross section about the axes \(z\) and \(y\) and the product of inertia (deviacijski moment) of the cross section. For \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) every piece \(\mathrm{d}A\) is weighted by the square of its distance \(y\), for \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) by the square of its distance \(z\), for \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) by the product of both. The unit is length to the fourth, for example mm\(^4\) or m\(^4\).

Lemma 5.27 Second moment of a rectangle (vztrajnostni moment pravokotnika)

A rectangle of width \(b\) (along the axis \(z\)) and height \(h\) (along the axis \(y\)) with the origin at the centre, \([-h/2, h/2] \times [-b/2, b/2]\), has

\[A = b\,h , \qquad I_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{b\,h^3}{12} , \qquad I_{\mathrm{y}} = \frac{h\,b^3}{12} , \qquad I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 . \tag{5.18}\]
Proof

We must show four integrals, and we compute them as the iterated integrals of definition 4.8, the inner over \(z\), the outer over \(y\). The inner integral runs along one strip across the width \(b\), the outer one adds the strips over the height \(h\). For \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) we use the same computation with the roles exchanged, by theorem 4.9 on the order of integration.

  1. Area, a strip of width \(b\) and strips over the height \(h\): \(\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \big( \int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2} \mathrm{d}z \big) \mathrm{d}y = \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} b\,\mathrm{d}y = b\,h\).
  2. For \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\) the inner integral is \(\int_{-\mathrm{b}/2}^{b/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}z = b\,y^2\), because \(y^2\) is a constant in the inner integral: the whole strip is at the same distance \(y\) from the axis \(z\).
  3. The outer integral adds the contributions of all the strips: \(b \int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = b \cdot \dfrac{1}{3}\Big[ y^3 \Big]_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2}\), which by the fundamental theorem of calculus equals \(\dfrac{b}{3} \Big( \dfrac{h^3}{8} + \dfrac{h^3}{8} \Big) = \dfrac{b\,h^3}{12}\).
  4. \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = h\,b^3/12\): the vertical strips are \(h\) long and added over the width \(b\), which is the same computation after exchanging the roles \(y \leftrightarrow z\), \(b \leftrightarrow h\).
  5. For \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) the integrand is \(y\,z\) and by lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\) the integral is zero, since the rectangle is symmetric about the axis \(y\): a piece and its mirror partner have the products \(y\,z\) and \(-y\,z\).

What does \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) mean? We turn the bar by a quarter turn, so that it lies flat. Its width is now 40 mm and its height 20 mm, so its \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 40 \cdot 20^3/12\), which is exactly \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the upright bar. The bar standing on its edge has \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\), the same bar lying flat has \(26\,667\) mm\(^4\), four times less. The lengths of the blue bars below the two cross sections are in this ratio. The ratio is \((h/b)^2 = 4\), the square of the ratio of the sides. Everyone who has bent a ruler knows this: laid flat it gives easily, on its edge hardly at all. The area and the material are the same, only the distance of the material from the axis differs.

Why does \(h^3\) appear in \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), and not \(h\)? We cut the cross section into strips of height \(\Delta y\). The strip at height \(y\) has the area \(b\,\Delta y\) and contributes \(y^2\,b\,\Delta y\) (figure 5.70). The contribution grows with the square of the distance from the axis \(z\), so the strips at the edge count much more than the strips near the axis. For our bar the outer half of the height, \(10\ \text{mm} \le |y| \le 20\ \text{mm}\), contributes

\[2 \cdot 20 \int_{10}^{20} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 2 \cdot 20 \cdot \frac{20^3 - 10^3}{3}\ \text{mm}^4 = \frac{280\,000}{3}\ \text{mm}^4 ,\]

and the inner half \(20 \int_{-10}^{10} y^2\,\mathrm{d}y = 40\,000/3\ \text{mm}^4\). The outer half of the height contributes \(7/8 = 87{,}5\ \%\) of the whole \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 320\,000/3\ \text{mm}^4\), the inner only \(1/8 = 12{,}5\ \%\). This is why we give cross sections a shape in which the material is as far from the axis as possible.

The integral is the limit of sums over strips. If we replace it by the sum over \(n\) strips, with the contribution computed at the middle of each strip, \(\sum y_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,b\,\Delta y\), we obtain for our cross section for \(n = 2, 4, 8\) and \(1000\) strips \(80\,000\), \(100\,000\), \(105\,000\) and \(106\,667\ \text{mm}^4\). Eight strips are the strips and the blue bars in the figure. The sum is always smaller, because the mean of \(y^2\) over a strip with the centre \(y_{\mathrm{k}}\) and half-height \(\delta\) is \(y_{\mathrm{k}}^2 + \delta^2/3\), which is more than the value at the middle. With more strips the error shrinks, since \(\delta\) gets smaller.

We computed the second moment of the rectangle about axes through its centre. In a composite figure each part has its own centroid and its own axes. We compute them by knowing how to transfer the second moment to a parallel axis, and the following theorem makes this possible. For \(I_{\mathrm{y}}'\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}'\) the origin moves also in the direction \(z\) (figure 5.74), but the shape stays where it is. The centroidal axes cross at \(T\), the new axes at \(O'\). The point \(T\) is \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\) away from \(O'\) in the direction \(y\) and \(d_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the direction \(z\). A piece of area has the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) about \(T\) and \(y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\) about \(O'\).

Theorem 5.28 Parallel axis theorem (Steinerjev izrek)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles have the area \(A\), the centroid \(T\) and the second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) about axes through \(T\). Let \(y'\) and \(z'\) be the coordinates about axes parallel to the original ones, with the origin at a point \(O'\) in which the centroid \(T\) has the coordinates \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(d_{\mathrm{z}}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{z}}' &= \int_{\mathrm{L}} y'^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{z}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 , \\ I_{\mathrm{y}}' &= I_{\mathrm{y}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{z}}^2 , \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}}' &= I_{\mathrm{yz}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}\,d_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.19}\]
Proof

We must show that the difference is \(A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) and that the term proportional to \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\) vanishes. The coordinates in the new system are \(y' = y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(z' = z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\), where \(y\) and \(z\) are the coordinates about \(T\): the strip that is \(y\) above the centroidal axis is \(y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) above the axis \(z'\).

  1. We expand the square of the distance \(y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) from the axis \(z'\), \(y'^2 = (y + d_{\mathrm{y}})^2 = y^2 + 2 d_{\mathrm{y}}\,y + d_{\mathrm{y}}^2\).
  2. By linearity of the integral (item 3 of lemma 5.18) \(I_{\mathrm{z}}' = \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A + 2 d_{\mathrm{y}} \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} \mathrm{d}A\). The first term contains the distance \(y\) from the centroidal axis, the second the shift \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\), the third squares it and multiplies by the area.
  3. The second integral is zero by lemma 5.22, because the \(y\) are coordinates about the centroid: the strips above it and below it balance like weights on a balance. The third is \(A\), because every piece of area is shifted by the same \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\). So \(I_{\mathrm{z}}' = I_{\mathrm{z}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}}^2\).
  4. For \(I_{\mathrm{y}}'\) the computation is the same, only the distance \(z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\) replaces \(y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\) and the shift \(d_{\mathrm{z}}\) replaces \(d_{\mathrm{y}}\). For \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}'\) the product of the distances is \((y + d_{\mathrm{y}})(z + d_{\mathrm{z}}) = y z + d_{\mathrm{z}}\,y + d_{\mathrm{y}}\,z + d_{\mathrm{y}} d_{\mathrm{z}}\), and both middle integrals are zero by lemma 5.22, so \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}' = I_{\mathrm{yz}} + A\,d_{\mathrm{y}} d_{\mathrm{z}}\).

5.4.4 Principal axes of a cross section

Cross sections with an axis of symmetry \(y\) or \(z\) have \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\), as in item 5 of the proof of lemma 5.27), because every small piece on one side of the axis has a mirror piece on the other side, with a product \(y\,z\) of opposite sign. For a cross section without an axis of symmetry, for example the letter L (figure 5.77), we do not know in advance how the second moments change if we turn the axes. We will need this for a beam that bends in the planes \(x\)–\(y\) and \(x\)–\(z\) at the same time (later in this chapter). What happens to the three numbers \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) if we turn the axes around the centroid by an angle \(\varphi\)?

We leave the axis \(x\) and turn the axes \(y\) and \(z\) around it by the angle \(\varphi\) in the direction from \(y\) toward \(z\), which is a turn in the positive direction about the axis \(x\). Let \(\bar y\) and \(\bar z\) be the coordinates about the turned axes with unit vectors that have in the old axes the components \((c, s)\) and \((-s, c)\), where \(c = \cos\varphi\) and \(s = \sin\varphi\). As in definition 2.44, these are the rows of the rotation matrix, except that the axes \(y\) and \(z\) take the roles of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) of the definition. A point with the coordinates \((y, z)\) has in the new axes the coordinates

\[\bar y = c\,y + s\,z , \qquad \bar z = -s\,y + c\,z .\]

Why so, the figure shows. Take a small piece of the cross section at the point \(P\) with the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\). We reach it from \(O\) by going first a distance \(y\) along the axis \(y\) to the point \(Q\) and then a distance \(z\) parallel to the axis \(z\). The coordinate \(\bar y\) is the length of the shadow of the point \(P\) on the axis \(\bar y\), which we get with the perpendicular from \(P\) to that axis. The shadow of the first step is \(c\,y\), because the axis \(y\) makes the angle \(\varphi\) with the axis \(\bar y\). The shadow of the second step is \(s\,z\), because the axis \(z\) makes the angle \(90^\circ - \varphi\) with the axis \(\bar y\) and \(\cos(90^\circ - \varphi) = \sin\varphi\). The two shadows add up to \(\bar y = c\,y + s\,z\). We get \(\bar z\) in the same way on the axis \(\bar z\), except that the step along the axis \(y\) casts its shadow backwards there, \(-s\,y\).

By theorem 2.53 the components of stress in the plane transform by the same rule. The second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are integrals of the squares and the product of the coordinates, and in the next theorem we show by computation that they change by the same rule as the stresses when the axes are turned.

Theorem 5.30 Rotation of the axes of a cross section (vrtenje osi prereza)

Let the figure \(L\) made of rectangles with the origin in the centroid have the second moments \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\). About the axes turned by the angle \(\varphi\) the second moments are

\[\begin{aligned} \bar I_{\mathrm{z}} &= c^2\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + s^2\,I_{\mathrm{y}} + 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} , \\ \bar I_{\mathrm{y}} &= s^2\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + c^2\,I_{\mathrm{y}} - 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} , \\ \bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} &= -cs\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + cs\,I_{\mathrm{y}} + (c^2 - s^2)\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.20}\]

The sum \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} + \bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}\) does not depend on the angle.

Proof

We must show three integrals and a sum. Every small piece at \(P\) contributes to \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}}\) its area times the square of its new coordinate \(\bar y\). We obtain each integral by writing the coordinates with the new ones and splitting the integral by linearity (item 3 of lemma 5.18).

  1. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (c\,y + s\,z)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = c^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A + 2cs \int_{\mathrm{L}} y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A + s^2 \int_{\mathrm{L}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) equals \(c^2 I_{\mathrm{z}} + 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + s^2 I_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  2. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (-s\,y + c\,z)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = s^2 I_{\mathrm{z}} - 2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + c^2 I_{\mathrm{y}}\), the same computation with the sign \(-\) at the middle term.
  3. \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} (c\,y + s\,z)(-s\,y + c\,z)\,\mathrm{d}A\). The expansion of the product is \(-cs\,y^2 + c^2\,y z - s^2\,y z + cs\,z^2\), so \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = -cs\,I_{\mathrm{z}} + (c^2 - s^2)\,I_{\mathrm{yz}} + cs\,I_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. The sum of the first two equations is \((c^2 + s^2)(I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}) = I_{\mathrm{z}} + I_{\mathrm{y}}\), because \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\) and the terms with \(2cs\,I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) cancel.

Source: Our sources have no formula for the transformation of second moments. The computation is the same as for the transformation of stress in chapter 2, the proof is ours.

Equations (5.20) have the same form as the first three equations of (2.60) for stresses, with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} \to I_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} \to I_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} \to I_{\mathrm{yz}}\). The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} I_{\mathrm{z}} & I_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}} & I_{\mathrm{y}} \end{pmatrix}\) is therefore here what the matrix of stress in the plane is. It is symmetric and transforms like the matrix of stress, so the angle we look for is the one at which this matrix is diagonal, that is, \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). Theorem 2.83 on principal directions holds for matrices with three rows, so we show the existence of the angle here directly with the intermediate value theorem on the curve, which is shorter than embedding the \(2 \times 2\) matrix in a \(3 \times 3\) matrix and showing that both principal directions lie in the plane of the cross section.

Corollary 5.31 Principal axes of a cross section (glavne osi prereza)

For every figure made of rectangles with the origin in the centroid there is an angle \(\varphi\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) at which \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). The axes that belong to this angle are called the principal axes (glavni osi) of the cross section, and the numbers \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}}\) the principal second moments. If the figure is symmetric about the axis \(y\) or about the axis \(z\), the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are principal axes.

Proof

We must show that the function \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}}(\varphi)\) has a zero. We use the intermediate value theorem.

  1. By equation (5.20) with double angles, \(2cs = \sin 2\varphi\) and \(c^2 - s^2 = \cos 2\varphi\), \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}}(\varphi) = \tfrac12 (I_{\mathrm{y}} - I_{\mathrm{z}})\sin 2\varphi + I_{\mathrm{yz}}\cos 2\varphi\). This is a continuous function of the angle, for the L-section of example 5.32 drawn on the right of figure 5.76.
  2. At \(\varphi = 0\) the value is \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) (left end of the curve), at \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) it is \(-I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) (right end), because then \(c = 0\) and \(s = 1\), so \(\bar y = z\) and \(\bar z = -y\), and the product \(\bar y\,\bar z = -y\,z\) has the opposite sign. If \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), the axes are already principal. Otherwise the values have opposite signs, the curve must cross the axis \(\varphi\) in between, and by the intermediate value theorem 2.79 there is an angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) at which \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (for the L-section \(67{,}5^\circ\)).
  3. If the figure is symmetric about the axis \(y\), then \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) by lemma 5.24 with \(g(y) = y\), and for symmetry about the axis \(z\) with the roles of the coordinates exchanged. The axes \(y\) and \(z\) are therefore principal, as for \(\varphi = 0\), where for a symmetric figure the curve starts on the axis \(\varphi\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Theorem 2.83 holds for \(3 \times 3\) matrices, so this proof replaces it for a \(2 \times 2\) matrix by the intermediate value theorem.

Let us look at what the principal axes look like for a cross section without symmetry, on the letter L.

Example 5.32 Principal axes of an L-section (glavne osi prereza L)

The L-section has a vertical leg \(10 \times 60\) mm (\(z\) from 0 to 10, \(y\) from 0 to 60) and a horizontal leg \(30 \times 10\) mm (\(z\) from 10 to 40, \(y\) from 0 to 10), together the area \(A = 600 + 300 = 900\) mm\(^2\). Through the centroid \(T\) we draw the blackwhite axes, axis \(y\) and axis \(z\), and then the blue axes, axis \(\bar y\) and axis \(\bar z\), turned by the angle \(\varphi\). The L-section is drawn with a full line.

  1. By lemma 5.23 the centroid \(T\) is at \(y_{\mathrm{T}} = (600 \cdot 30 + 300 \cdot 5)/900 = 65/3 = 21{,}67\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{T}} = (600 \cdot 5 + 300 \cdot 25)/900 = 35/3 = 11{,}67\) mm.
  2. The centroid of the vertical leg \(T_1\) has, with respect to the centroid of the cross section, the coordinates \((d_{\mathrm{y}}, d_{\mathrm{z}}) = (25/3, -20/3)\) mm, and the centroid of the horizontal leg \(T_2\) \((-50/3, 40/3)\) mm (the differences \(30 - 65/3\), \(5 - 35/3\) and \(5 - 65/3\), \(25 - 35/3\)). The grey dashed lines from each centroid to the blackwhite axes show these distances. The own second moments by lemma 5.27 are \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 10 \cdot 60^3/12 = 180\,000\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = 60 \cdot 10^3/12 = 5000\) mm\(^4\) for the vertical one, and \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 30 \cdot 10^3/12 = 2500\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = 10 \cdot 30^3/12 = 22\,500\) mm\(^4\) for the horizontal one, the own products of inertia are zero.
  3. By theorem 5.28 \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = (180\,000 + 600 \cdot (25/3)^2) + (2500 + 300 \cdot (50/3)^2) = 221\,667 + 85\,833 = 307\,500\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = (5000 + 600 \cdot (20/3)^2) + (22\,500 + 300 \cdot (40/3)^2) = 31\,667 + 75\,833 = 107\,500\) mm\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 600 \cdot \tfrac{25}{3} \cdot (-\tfrac{20}{3}) + 300 \cdot (-\tfrac{50}{3}) \cdot \tfrac{40}{3} = -33\,333 - 66\,667 = -100\,000\) mm\(^4\). Both contributions are negative, because \(T_1\) lies above the axis \(z\) and to the left of the axis \(y\), and \(T_2\) below the axis \(z\) and to the right of the axis \(y\): for both, the two coordinates have opposite signs.
  4. The product of inertia is not zero, because the figure has no axis of symmetry. The condition \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) is by step 1 of the proof of corollary 5.31 equivalent to \(\tan 2\varphi = 2 I_{\mathrm{yz}}/(I_{\mathrm{z}} - I_{\mathrm{y}})\) for \(\cos 2\varphi \ne 0\), which is \(-200\,000/200\,000 = -1\), so \(2\varphi = 135^\circ\) and \(\varphi = 67{,}5^\circ\), where the curve of figure 5.76 crosses the axis.
  5. At this angle \(c^2 = 0{,}1464\), \(s^2 = 0{,}8536\) and \(2cs = \sin 135^\circ = 0{,}7071\), \(c^2 - s^2 = -0{,}7071\). By theorem 5.30 \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} = 0{,}1464 \cdot 307\,500 + 0{,}8536 \cdot 107\,500 + 0{,}7071 \cdot (-100\,000) = 66\,079\) mm\(^4\), \(\bar I_{\mathrm{y}} = 348\,921\) mm\(^4\) and \(\bar I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (up to rounding). The sum \(66\,079 + 348\,921 = 415\,000 = 307\,500 + 107\,500\) mm\(^4\), as the theorem requires. The smaller one is \(\bar I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{L}} \bar y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), the moment about the axis \(\bar z\), which is tilted from the vertical by only \(22{,}5^\circ\) and runs almost along the long leg, so the pieces of the cross section lie close to it and their distances \(\bar y\) are small.
  6. The principal second moments about the principal axes are also the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} I_{\mathrm{z}} & I_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ I_{\mathrm{yz}} & I_{\mathrm{y}} \end{pmatrix}\) (definition 2.81): the zeros of the determinant \((I_{\mathrm{z}} - \lambda)(I_{\mathrm{y}} - \lambda) - I_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 = (307\,500 - \lambda)(107\,500 - \lambda) - 10^{10}\) are \(\lambda = 207\,500 \pm 100\,000\sqrt2\ \text{mm}^4\), which is \(66\,079\) and \(348\,921\) mm\(^4\), the same as in step 5.

We will need the principal axes of a cross section for bending in two planes. For cross sections with an axis of symmetry, such as the rectangle and the I-section, nothing needs to be turned, because this axis is already principal.
