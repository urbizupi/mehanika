Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.15 A body on several separate supports

In chapter 4 we found that the equation of virtual forces, if it holds for every statically admissible virtual stress field, gives a displacement field with the given strains (theorem 4.124). We also proved that this field agrees with the prescribed displacement on the support when the support is one single piece (theorem 4.130) and when the body is a bar welded at both ends (corollary 4.131). We did not treat a body on several separate supports, for example a straight strip glued to the ground at three places. We wrote that we need statically admissible fields that carry a force and a moment from one support to another, and that in general we get them only by solving boundary value problems. So we moved this question to this section. Does the equation of virtual forces ensure the support condition here too?

The answer has two parts, which we separate from the start. For the plane case, where we write the fields with an Airy function, we prove the answer for the rectangular strip, and for another plane region given a function that still has to be found. For a body in space we prove a theorem that holds if such fields exist, but we do not prove that they exist. At the end we say which is which.

The route is this. First we say what can happen with several pieces of support, and from that we write down what field of stress we need, namely one that carries a force and a moment from one support to another. Then we prove the theorem that concludes the support condition from the existence of such fields. Then we look at the plane case, where the mismatch of the supports is a plane rigid displacement and where we write such fields with one function, and then the strip on three supports with numbers. At the end we say what remains for a body in space.

11.15.1 What can go wrong

Take a steel strip, 300 mm long, 20 mm high and 10 mm thick. On its bottom face it is glued to the ground at three places, under the left end, under the middle and under the right end, each time over a length of 20 mm (figure 11.43). Let the origin \(O\) be at the centre of the left end of the strip, the \(x\) axis along the strip, the \(y\) axis upwards and the \(z\) axis towards the viewer. The supports are three pieces (kosi) \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) of the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) where the displacement is prescribed. Let the prescribed displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) be the same on all three pieces: the ground settles as a whole by 0,1 mm. Let the strains of the strip be zero, so the displacement field of the strip is a small rigid displacement \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) (theorem 1.134). Is this possible? Yes: the strip settles as a whole, by 0,1 mm at each support. But if only the middle support settles, by 0,1 mm, while the end supports stay, this is not possible. A rigid displacement that is zero at two points is a rotation about the line through them (subsection 4.7.11), and this rotation moves every point off the line. A rigid displacement that is zero on both end supports is therefore zero everywhere, while at the middle support it would have to be 0,1 mm downwards. The equation of virtual forces must detect such a difference.

How does this relate to theorem 4.130? There we proved for every rectangle of the support that the mismatch is a rigid displacement (lemma 4.129), and for neighbouring rectangles that it is the same, because otherwise it would differ by a rotation about the common side, which the field from a bump around this side detects. The pieces \(S_1\), \(S_2\) and \(S_3\) have no common side, so this field does not help. The mismatch has its own rigid displacement \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{m}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{m}}\,\vek r\) on each piece, and the difference of two pieces is a rigid displacement. Which virtual fields detect it? The work of a statically admissible field on a rigid displacement that is the same on all pieces is zero: the surface integral \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} (\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\cdot\vek R\,\mathrm{d}A\) is zero for a rigid displacement \(\vek R\) (lemma 4.81, as in step 8 of the proof of theorem 4.130), and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\). The work is therefore nonzero only for a field whose forces on the pieces of the support are not in equilibrium each on its own. Such fields carry a force and a moment through the body from one piece to another, like the six fields of the bar between two walls (figure 4.105). EP (p. 198) derives the support condition from the statement that the virtual stresses in the body and on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) are independently arbitrary. For statically admissible fields this is not true, because equilibrium ties the forces on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) together. So we must either assume or prove the existence of fields that carry force.

11.15.2 Transmitting fields and the theorem

Let us write what it means to "carry a force". Let \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the union of the pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}\), where every piece is a union of rectangles of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the pieces have no common rectangles. For a statically admissible virtual stress field \(\delta\ten\sigma\) (definition 4.96) the force and the moment of the virtual support forces on the piece \(S_{\mathrm{j}}\) about the origin \(O\) are

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{j}} \delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad \vek M_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{j}} \vek r\times(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{11.78}\]

where \(\vek r\) is the position vector from \(O\) to the point of the face. The integrals are sums of surface integrals over the rectangles of the piece.

Definition 11.58 Transmitting field (prenosno polje)

Let the body \(V\) with the parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) be as in theorem 4.130, and let \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the union of the pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(k \ge 2\), as above. Let \(2 \le m \le k\) and let \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) be vectors with three components each. A statically admissible virtual stress field \(\delta\ten\sigma\) is a transmitting field from the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the piece \(S_1\) with force \(\vek F\) and moment \(\vek M\) if \(\vek F_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek F\), \(\vek M_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek M\), \(\vek F_1 = -\vek F\), \(\vek M_1 = -\vek M\), and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) for every other piece \(S_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{j}}\) are as in (11.78).

Source: The definition is not from the sources.

A transmitting field is thus a matrix of functions that is in equilibrium in the body, free on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and whose forces on the support are "force \(\vek F\) and moment \(\vek M\) on the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\), the opposite force and moment on the piece \(S_1\), zero elsewhere". With the pieces \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) and \(m = 2\) the field has an upward resultant on the middle support, an equally large downward one on the left, and zero on the right. We state the theorem. For \(k = 1\) there is nothing to carry, and the theorem is theorem 4.130.

Theorem 11.59 Converse of the principle of virtual forces at supports in several pieces (obrat načela virtualnih sil na podporah v več kosih)

Let the body \(V\), the parts of the surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and the functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be as in theorem 4.124, and let the equation of virtual forces

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{u}} (\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\cdot\bar{\vek u}\,\mathrm{d}A \tag{11.79}\]

hold for every statically admissible virtual stress field. Assume further:

  1. the set of interior points of the body is connected and simply connected,
  2. the prescribed displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) equals a field \(\bar{\vek u}\) whose components have continuous partial derivatives of first and second order on an open set containing the body,
  3. \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the union of the pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(k \ge 2\), without common rectangles, and every piece on its own satisfies condition 3 of theorem 4.130: any two of its rectangles are joined by a chain of its rectangles in which neighbouring rectangles have a common side and the cells of the body containing this side are not exactly two cells without a common face,
  4. for every \(m = 2, \dots, k\) and every pair of vectors \(\vek F\), \(\vek M\) there is a transmitting field from the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the piece \(S_1\) with force \(\vek F\) and moment \(\vek M\) (definition 11.58).

Then there is a displacement field \(\tilde{\vek u}\), defined at the interior points of the body, with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order, whose small strain tensor components equal \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at every interior point and whose extension to the cell of every rectangle of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) (lemma 4.127) equals \(\bar{\vek u}\) on this rectangle.

Proof

We have to show that the mismatch is the same rigid displacement on all pieces. On each piece there is one rigid displacement by theorem 4.130, and the transmitting fields detect the differences between the pieces.

1. The field and the mismatch on a piece.

  1. By theorem 4.124, point 2, which we may apply because of assumption 1, there is a displacement field \(\vek u\) at the interior points of the body with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order and strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). By lemma 4.127 it has an extension \(\vek U_{\mathrm{C}}\) to every cell \(C\) of the body. For a rectangle \(F\) of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) with cell \(C\), the mismatch \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}} = \vek U_{\mathrm{C}} - \bar{\vek u}\) is, by lemma 4.129, which we may apply because of assumption 2, a rigid displacement \(\vek R_{\mathrm{F}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{F}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{F}}\,\vek r\).
  2. Let \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) be neighbouring rectangles of the chain of assumption 3 for the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\), with common side \(E\). Steps 3 to 11 of the proof of theorem 4.130 use only the rectangles \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), the cells of the mesh at the side \(E\), for which the condition of assumption 3 holds, the equality (4.80) and the field from bumps around \(E\), which by step 7 of that proof is zero on the whole surface except on \(F_1\) and \(F_2\). This field is therefore statically admissible regardless of the other pieces, and the steps need nothing about the other rectangles of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). Steps 3 to 11 therefore hold here too and give \(\vek R_{\mathrm{F}_1} = \vek R_{\mathrm{F}_2}\).
  3. Any two rectangles of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) are joined by the chain of assumption 3, in which neighbours have the same rigid displacement by step 2. As in step 12 of the proof of theorem 4.130, all rectangles of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) therefore have the same rigid displacement \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{m}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{m}}\,\vek r\).

2. The work on the mismatch.

  1. By lemma 4.128, which we may apply because of equation (11.79), we have \(\sum_{\mathrm{F}} \int_{\mathrm{F}} (\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{F}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) for every statically admissible virtual stress field. By step 3, \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}} = \vek R_{\mathrm{m}}\) on every rectangle of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\), so
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{m}=1}^{k} \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{m}} (\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\cdot\vek R_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 . \tag{11.80}\]
  2. Let \(\boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} = (W_{\mathrm{zy}}, W_{\mathrm{xz}}, W_{\mathrm{yx}})\) be the triple of entries of the matrix \(\mat W_{\mathrm{m}}\). Since \(\mat W_{\mathrm{m}}\) is antisymmetric, \((\mat W_{\mathrm{m}}\,\vek r)\cdot\vek t = \boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\cdot(\vek r\times\vek t)\) for every vector \(\vek t\), as we saw for the bar between two walls (equation (4.81)): we multiply the components \((\mat W\,\vek r)_{\mathrm{x}} = -W_{\mathrm{yx}}\,y + W_{\mathrm{xz}}\,z\), \((\mat W\,\vek r)_{\mathrm{y}} = W_{\mathrm{yx}}\,x - W_{\mathrm{zy}}\,z\) and \((\mat W\,\vek r)_{\mathrm{z}} = -W_{\mathrm{xz}}\,x + W_{\mathrm{zy}}\,y\) by the components of the vector \(\vek t\) and compare with the components of the vector product \(\vek r\times\vek t\) (definition 2.30). Therefore, by (11.78),
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{m}} (\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n)\cdot\vek R_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \vek a_{\mathrm{m}}\cdot\vek F_{\mathrm{m}} + \boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\cdot\vek M_{\mathrm{m}} . \tag{11.81}\]
  3. Let \(2 \le m \le k\), let \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) be arbitrary vectors and \(\delta\ten\sigma\) a transmitting field from \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to \(S_1\) with force \(\vek F\) and moment \(\vek M\), which exists by assumption 4. The sum (11.80) has, by (11.81), only the terms of the pieces \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(S_1\), the others are zero, so
    \[\vek F\cdot(\vek a_{\mathrm{m}} - \vek a_1) + \vek M\cdot(\boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} - \boldsymbol\omega_1) = 0 . \tag{11.82}\]

3. One rigid displacement.

  1. Equation (11.82) holds for every pair \(\vek F\), \(\vek M\). With \(\vek F = \vek a_{\mathrm{m}} - \vek a_1\) and \(\vek M = \vek 0\) we get \(|\vek a_{\mathrm{m}} - \vek a_1|^2 = 0\), and with \(\vek F = \vek 0\) and \(\vek M = \boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} - \boldsymbol\omega_1\) we get \(|\boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} - \boldsymbol\omega_1|^2 = 0\). Therefore \(\vek a_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek a_1\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} = \boldsymbol\omega_1\). The entries \(W_{\mathrm{zy}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{yx}}\) determine an antisymmetric matrix, so \(\mat W_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat W_1\) and \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek R_1 =: \vek R\) for every \(m\).
  2. Let \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u - \vek R\) at the interior points of the body. The field \(\vek R\) has constant first partial derivatives and a zero small strain tensor (theorem 1.134, point 2), so \(\tilde{\vek u}\) has continuous partial derivatives of first and second order and strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For a cell \(C\), \(\vek U_{\mathrm{C}} - \vek R\) is continuous on \(C\) and equals \(\tilde{\vek u}\) at the interior points, so it is the extension of the field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) to \(C\).
  3. On a rectangle \(F\) of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) with cell \(C\), by steps 3 and 7, \(\vek U_{\mathrm{C}} - \vek R = \bar{\vek u} + \vek w_{\mathrm{F}} - \vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = \bar{\vek u}\).

Source: Lemma 4.129, theorem 4.130 and lemma 4.128 of chapter 4. EP (4.4.10) (p. 198) writes the support condition without an analysis of the supports. The theorem and the proof are not from the sources.

The proof reduced the mismatch on each piece to one rigid displacement with the fields around common sides, as for one piece, and the differences between pieces to six numbers, which the transmitting fields detect: three components of the displacement and three of the rotation. The opposite direction is the principle of virtual forces: if there is a displacement field with the given strains that is continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body with its surface and equals \(\bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the equation holds for every statically admissible field (theorem 4.97). When transmitting fields exist, the equation of virtual forces thus ensures a field that agrees with the supports, and conversely such a field, if it is continuously differentiable up to the surface, ensures the equation.

For the bar between two walls we wrote the transmitting fields with formulas (figure 4.105). For a body of arbitrary shape their existence is not self-evident. Where can we write them?

11.15.3 The plane case

Take a prism \(V = A \times [-c, c]\), where \(A\) is a region in the \(xy\) plane made of finitely many cells \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the mesh of some rectangle, and let the body have the cells \(R_{\mathrm{i}} \times [-c, c]\). We put the pieces of the support on the lateral boundary, from face to face over the whole height: a rectangle of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is \(e \times [-c, c]\) for a side \(e\) of some rectangle \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) on the boundary of the region \(A\). The strains are plane if \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. The prescribed displacement is plane if \(\bar{\vek u} = (\bar u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y), \bar u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y), 0)\). The strip of the introduction is such a case with \(A = [0, 300] \times [-10, 10]\) mm and \(c = 5\) mm, with the mesh that cuts it at \(x = 20\), \(140\), \(160\) and \(280\) mm into five cells, and with the pieces \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) on the bottom face.

In the plane case the mismatch is simpler than the general lemma gives: a plane rigid displacement has only three numbers, \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and one rotation about the \(z\) axis. So we need only three fields instead of six. We prove this before we write the fields.

Lemma 11.60 The mismatch for plane data is a plane rigid displacement (neujemanje pri ravninskih podatkih je ravninski togi premik)

Let \(V = A \times [-c, c]\), \(c > 0\), be a prism over a region \(A\) of the \(xy\) plane made of finitely many cells \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the mesh of some rectangle, with the cells \(R_{\mathrm{i}} \times [-c, c]\), let the interior of the region \(A\) be connected and simply connected, and let \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be a union of rectangles of the form \(e \times [-c, c]\), where \(e\) is a side of a rectangle \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) on the boundary of the region \(A\). Let the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be as in theorem 4.124 and plane, the prescribed displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) plane with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order on an open set containing the body, and let equation (11.79) hold for every statically admissible virtual stress field. Then:

  1. there is a displacement field \(\vek u\) at the interior points of the body with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order and strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) which is plane: \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) independent of \(z\),
  2. for such a field \(\vek u\) the mismatch \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}}\) on every rectangle \(F\) of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is a plane rigid displacement, \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}} = (a_{\mathrm{x}} - \omega\,y,\ a_{\mathrm{y}} + \omega\,x,\ 0)\) with constant \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\omega\).
Proof

We have to show two things. The field from theorem 4.124 differs from a plane field by a rigid displacement, which we subtract, and the plane mismatch follows from the fact that a plane function does not change over the height.

1. A plane field.

  1. The interior of the body is connected and simply connected because the interior of the region \(A\) is (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 11.37 for a prism over a simply connected region). By theorem 4.124, point 2, there is a displacement field \(\vek u\) with continuous partial derivatives of first and second order and strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  2. The field \(\vek v = \partial\vek u/\partial z\) is continuously differentiable. By corollary 1.126 on the independence of the order of differentiation, \(\tfrac12\,(\partial v_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial v_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}) = \partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial z\), and this derivative is zero because the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are either zero or independent of \(z\). By theorem 1.134 therefore \(\vek v = \vek b + \mat\Omega\,\vek r\) with a constant \(\vek b\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat\Omega\).
  3. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = v_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), we have \(b_{\mathrm{z}} + \Omega_{\mathrm{zx}}\,x + \Omega_{\mathrm{zy}}\,y = 0\) at every interior point. The interior contains three points with different pairs \((x, y)\) that do not lie on one line, so \(b_{\mathrm{z}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{zx}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{zy}} = 0\), and by antisymmetry also \(\Omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). What remains is \(v_{\mathrm{x}} = b_{\mathrm{x}} + \Omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y\) and \(v_{\mathrm{y}} = b_{\mathrm{y}} - \Omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\,x\).
  4. Since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(v_{\mathrm{x}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12\,(v_{\mathrm{y}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y) = 0\), we have \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x = -b_{\mathrm{x}} - \Omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y = -b_{\mathrm{y}} + \Omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\,x\). By theorem 1.125 the second mixed derivative of the function \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) does not depend on the order: \(-\Omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\Omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and \(\vek v = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\).
  5. Let \(\vek r_0 = (b_{\mathrm{x}}\,z,\ b_{\mathrm{y}}\,z,\ -b_{\mathrm{x}}\,x - b_{\mathrm{y}}\,y)\). This is a field of the form \(\mat W\,\vek r\) with an antisymmetric matrix (\(W_{\mathrm{xz}} = b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{zx}} = -b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{yz}} = b_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{zy}} = -b_{\mathrm{y}}\), the others zero), so it has zero strains (theorem 1.134, point 2). The field \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u - \vek r_0\) therefore has continuous derivatives up to second order and strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Its derivatives \(\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = v_{\mathrm{x}} - b_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z = v_{\mathrm{y}} - b_{\mathrm{y}}\) are zero by step 4. The component \(\tilde u_{\mathrm{z}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\,x + b_{\mathrm{y}}\,y\) has zero derivatives in \(x\) and \(y\) by step 4, and its derivative in \(z\) is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\).
  6. The field \((0, 0, \tilde u_{\mathrm{z}})\) has a zero gradient by step 5, so zero strains. By theorem 1.134, point 1, it is \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\), and by point 2 its gradient is \(\mat W\), so \(\mat W = \mat 0\) and the field is constant, \((0, 0, \zeta_0)\). The field \(\vek u - \vek r_0 - (0, 0, \zeta_0)\) therefore has a zero third component. Its \(x\) and \(y\) components have zero derivative in \(z\) by step 5, so by corollary 1.85 they do not change along any vertical segment in the body and are functions of \(x\) and \(y\) only. The field has strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and continuous derivatives up to second order (the fields \((0, 0, \zeta_0)\) and \(\vek r_0\) have zero strains), so it is the plane field we look for.

2. The mismatch is plane.

  1. Let \(\vek u\) be the plane field of point 1, \(C\) the cell of the rectangle \(F\) and \(M\) the centre of the cell. The points \(P = (x_0, y_0, z)\) and \(P' = (x_0, y_0, z')\) of the cell have the sequences of interior points \(P + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{PM}\) and \(P' + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{P'M}\) with the same \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates. On both sequences the field \(\vek u\) is the same in \(x\) and \(y\) and has a zero third component. The extension \(\vek U_{\mathrm{C}}\) (lemma 4.127) is continuous on \(C\) and equals \(\vek u\) at the interior points, so by lemma 1.68 \(\vek U_{\mathrm{C}}(P) = \vek U_{\mathrm{C}}(P')\) and \(U_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The field \(\bar{\vek u}\) is plane too, so the mismatch \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}}\) on \(F\) is plane: \(w_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{y}}\) independent of \(z\).
  2. By lemma 4.129, \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}} = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) on \(F\), which we take in the plane \(x = c_0\), \(F = \{c_0\} \times [y_1, y_2] \times [-c, c]\) with \(y_1 < y_2\). The \(x\) component, \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + W_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y + W_{\mathrm{xz}}\,z\), does not depend on \(z\) at two points with the same \(y\) and different \(z\), so \(W_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\). In the same way \(W_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) from the \(y\) component, \(a_{\mathrm{y}} + W_{\mathrm{yx}}\,c_0 + W_{\mathrm{yz}}\,z\). The \(z\) component, \(a_{\mathrm{z}} + W_{\mathrm{zx}}\,c_0 + W_{\mathrm{zy}}\,y\), is zero at \(y_1\) and \(y_2\), so \(W_{\mathrm{zy}} = 0\), by antisymmetry also \(W_{\mathrm{zx}} = -W_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), and therefore \(a_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). With \(\omega = W_{\mathrm{yx}} = -W_{\mathrm{xy}}\) what remains is \(\vek w_{\mathrm{F}} = (a_{\mathrm{x}} - \omega\,y,\ a_{\mathrm{y}} + \omega\,x,\ 0)\). For a rectangle in the plane \(y = d_0\) the computation is the same with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchanged.

Source: The lemma and the proof are not from the sources.

The proof showed that for plane data it suffices to look for plane mismatches: three numbers per piece. Detecting the difference of the pieces \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(S_1\) therefore means detecting the difference of \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, \omega)\), which needs only transmitting fields with the components \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\). We now build such fields from one function.

The field (4.77) of chapter 4, which is independent of \(z\), is statically admissible if it gives zero force on the free faces: on a free face we need \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\). For an Airy function the forces on the boundary are differences of first derivatives (lemma 11.38). The free arcs of the boundary therefore only require that the two derivatives of the function have a constant value along the arc. The plane forces and the moment of a piece are differences of values between the neighbouring arcs. Let us write this precisely.

Lemma 11.61 Transmitting fields from an Airy function (prenosna polja iz Airyjeve funkcije)

Let \(V = A \times [-c, c]\) be as in lemma 11.60, and let the boundary of the region \(A\) be a closed curve of segments without self-contact, which, walked in the positive direction (region \(A\) on the left), we divide into the arcs \(S_1, A_1, S_2, A_2, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}, A_{\mathrm{k}}\) in this order, each of positive length and each made of sides of the cells \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\). Let the pieces of the support be \(S_{\mathrm{j}} \times [-c, c]\) and let the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface consist of the faces \(A_{\mathrm{j}} \times [-c, c]\) and the faces \(z = \pm c\). Let \(\varphi(x, y)\) be a function with continuous partial derivatives of first, second and third order in the plane whose two first derivatives are constant on every arc \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}\), and let \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\) be the value of the function \(\varphi - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\,x - \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}\,y\) at the starting point of the arc \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\). Write \((\alpha_0, \beta_0, \gamma_0) = (\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}, \beta_{\mathrm{k}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{k}})\). Then:

  1. the field (4.77) with the function \(\varphi\) is a statically admissible virtual stress field,
  2. for \(j = 1, \dots, k\) the forces and moments (11.78) of the pieces of the support are
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek F_{\mathrm{j}} &= 2c\,\big(\gamma_{\mathrm{j}} - \gamma_{\mathrm{j}-1},\ -(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}-1}) \\ &\quad ,\ 0\big) , \\ \vek M_{\mathrm{j}} &= 2c\,\big(0,\ 0,\ \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}-1}\big) , \end{aligned} \tag{11.83}\]
  3. if \((\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}, \beta_{\mathrm{j}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}) = (0, 0, 0)\) for \(j < m\) and \((\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}, \beta_{\mathrm{j}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}) = (\alpha, \beta, \gamma)\) for \(j \ge m\), where \(2 \le m \le k\), the field is a transmitting field from the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the piece \(S_1\) with force \(2c\,(\gamma, -\beta, 0)\) and moment \(2c\,(0, 0, \alpha)\). For arbitrary \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) we may choose \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{z}}/(2c)\), \(\beta = -F_{\mathrm{y}}/(2c)\), \(\gamma = F_{\mathrm{x}}/(2c)\).
Proof

We have to show that the field is in equilibrium and free on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and that the lemma on the boundary forces gives the differences. The boundary is made of segments, so we apply lemma 11.38 to each segment separately and add the differences.

  1. We take the function \(\varphi\) as a function of three variables that does not depend on \(z\). Its partial derivatives up to third order are derivatives in \(x\) and \(y\) or zero, and they are continuous in space, because the projections of two points onto the \(xy\) plane are at most as far apart as the points. By lemma 4.121, point 1, the field (4.77) is therefore symmetric, continuously differentiable in the whole space and in equilibrium without body force. The field does not depend on \(z\), and the components \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero.
  2. On the faces \(z = \pm c\) we have \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \pm(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) = \vek 0\). On the face \(A_{\mathrm{j}} \times [-c, c]\) the normal is \(\vek n = (y', -x', 0)\) for an arc \((x(s), y(s))\) with the body on the left, and by lemma 11.38, point 1, applied to each segment of the arc, \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is the derivative along the segment of the vector \((\partial\varphi/\partial y, -\partial\varphi/\partial x)\), which is constant on \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\), \((\gamma_{\mathrm{j}}, -\beta_{\mathrm{j}})\). Therefore \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\) on the whole surface \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and the field is statically admissible (definition 4.96). This is point 1.
  3. On every segment of the arc \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\) the function \(\varphi - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\,x - \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}\,y\) has, by the chain rule, the derivative along the segment \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} x' + \varphi_{\mathrm{y}} y' - \beta_{\mathrm{j}} x' - \gamma_{\mathrm{j}} y' = 0\), where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}}\) are the first derivatives, so it is constant on the segment (corollary 1.85), as in step 5 of the proof of lemma 11.38. The function is continuous, so it is constant on the whole arc, and \(\varphi = \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} + \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\,x + \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}\,y\) on \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\). Let \(S_{\mathrm{j}}\) run from the point \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\), which is the end of the arc \(A_{\mathrm{j}-1}\), to the point \(Q_{\mathrm{j}}\), which is the start of the arc \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\). The points lie on the closed arcs \(A_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the derivatives of the function \(\varphi\) are continuous, so the values of \(\varphi\), \(\partial\varphi/\partial x\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial y\) at \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\) are the values of the linear function \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \beta_{\mathrm{j}-1}\,x + \gamma_{\mathrm{j}-1}\,y\) and its derivatives, and at \(Q_{\mathrm{j}}\) the values of the linear function \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} + \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\,x + \gamma_{\mathrm{j}}\,y\).
  4. By lemma 11.38, point 2, the force per unit height on \(S_{\mathrm{j}}\) is, on each segment, the difference of the values of the vector \((\partial\varphi/\partial y, -\partial\varphi/\partial x)\) between the ends, and the sum over the segments gives the difference between \(Q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\), because the intermediate values are continuous: \((\gamma_{\mathrm{j}} - \gamma_{\mathrm{j}-1},\ -(\beta_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}-1}))\). The same holds for the moment, \(\varphi - x\,\partial\varphi/\partial x - y\,\partial\varphi/\partial y\), which for a linear function \(\alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) equals \(\alpha\). The moment about \(O\) around the \(z\) axis per unit height is therefore \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}-1}\).
  5. The virtual force \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) does not depend on \(z\) and has no \(z\) component. The integral over the height \(-c \le z \le c\) gives a factor \(2c\). The moment components \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -\int z\,t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z\,t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) are zero because the integral of an odd function of \(z\) over a symmetric interval is zero. This is point 2.
  6. With the choice of point 3, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\vek M_{\mathrm{j}}\) by (11.83) for \(j = m\) equal \(2c\,(\gamma, -\beta, 0)\) and \(2c\,(0, 0, \alpha)\), because \((\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}, \beta_{\mathrm{m}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{m}}) - (\alpha_{\mathrm{m}-1}, \beta_{\mathrm{m}-1}, \gamma_{\mathrm{m}-1}) = (\alpha, \beta, \gamma) - (0, 0, 0)\). For \(j = 1\) we have \((\alpha_1, \beta_1, \gamma_1) - (\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}, \beta_{\mathrm{k}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{k}}) = -(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)\). For \(1 < j < m\) and for \(j > m\) the difference is zero. This is point 3.

Source: The lemma and the proof are not from the sources. SADD §7.5 (PDF p. 159) only mentions that the traction condition can be written with first-order derivatives of the stress function, without derivation. Lemma 11.38 is ours.

The lemma turned transmitting fields into the search for a function \(\varphi\) with given constant derivatives on the free arcs. The function need not satisfy the equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\), because the virtual field has only to be in equilibrium, not also compatible. Do such functions exist? For a general region we need a function that is smooth, zero on one part of the boundary and equal to a linear function on another. Such a function would come from a construction of analysis called mollification (zglajevanje), which the book does not have, so we do not use it. For the strip we write the function directly from the steps of chapter 4.

For \(\alpha < \beta\) let \(q_{\alpha\beta}(t) = (t - \alpha)^4\,(\beta - t)^4\) on the interval \([\alpha, \beta]\) and zero elsewhere (lemma 4.122), and

\[s_{\alpha\beta}(t) = \frac{\int_\alpha^t q_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau}{\int_\alpha^\beta q_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau} \ \ (\alpha \le t \le \beta) , \qquad s_{\alpha\beta} = 0 \ \text{for}\ t < \alpha , \qquad s_{\alpha\beta} = 1 \ \text{for}\ t > \beta .\]

The function \(s_{\alpha\beta}\) is a "smooth step" from zero to one. Since \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) is zero outside the interval \([\alpha, \beta]\), we have \(s_{\alpha\beta}(t) = \int_\alpha^t q_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau \big/ \int_\alpha^\beta q_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\) for every \(t\), and by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, point 2, its first derivative is \(q_{\alpha\beta}/\int q_{\alpha\beta}\), which by lemma 4.122, point 1, has continuous derivatives up to third order, equal to zero at \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Therefore \(s_{\alpha\beta}\) has continuous derivatives up to fourth order on the whole line, and these derivatives are zero at \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and outside the interval.

Corollary 11.62 Converse at supports in several pieces for a plane body (obrat na podporah v več kosih za ravninsko telo)

Let \(V = A \times [-c, c]\), the parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(k \ge 2\) and the arcs \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\) be as in lemma 11.61, the functions \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the field \(\bar{\vek u}\) plane as in lemma 11.60, let equation (11.79) hold for every statically admissible virtual stress field and let every piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) satisfy condition 3 of theorem 11.59. Let there be, for every \(m = 2, \dots, k\) and every linear function \(L(x, y) = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\), a function \(\varphi\) as in lemma 11.61 which on the arcs \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{m}-1}\) has the value \(0\) and derivatives \(0\), and on the arcs \(A_{\mathrm{m}}, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\) the value \(L\) and derivatives \(\partial L/\partial x\), \(\partial L/\partial y\). Then the conclusion of theorem 11.59 holds.

The assumption on the function \(\varphi\) and condition 3 hold for the rectangular strip \(A = [0, X] \times [-b, b]\) with the pieces \(S_{\mathrm{j}}\) on the bottom face \(y = -b\) over the intervals \([x_{\mathrm{j}}^-, x_{\mathrm{j}}^+]\), where \(0 \le x_1^- < x_1^+ < x_2^- < \dots < x_{\mathrm{k}}^+ \le X\), and with the mesh that cuts the strip at all \(x_{\mathrm{j}}^\pm\) between \(0\) and \(X\). For the strip the conclusion therefore holds without the assumption on the function \(\varphi\).

Proof

We have to show that the theorem need not be used in full. We need only steps 1 to 3 of its proof and transmitting fields for three of the six numbers, so we repeat the proof with the fields of lemma 11.61. For the strip we show that the function of the assumption exists.

1. The converse from plane transmitting fields.

  1. By lemma 11.60 there is a plane field \(\vek u\) with strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) whose mismatch on every rectangle is a plane rigid displacement. Steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 11.59 (the same rigid displacement on all rectangles of one piece) hold for this field in place of the field of step 1, because they use only that the field has strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and they give on the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) a plane rigid displacement \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = (a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{x}} - \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,y,\ a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{y}} + \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,x,\ 0)\), in the notation of the theorem \(\vek a_{\mathrm{m}} = (a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega_{\mathrm{m}} = (0, 0, \omega_{\mathrm{m}})\).
  2. Steps 4 to 6 of the proof of theorem 11.59 (equations (11.80), (11.81) and (11.82)) hold for every transmitting field, whatever its origin. For a transmitting field of lemma 11.61 we have \(F_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so equation (11.82) becomes
    \[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,(a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{x}} - a_{1,\mathrm{x}}) + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,(a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{y}} - a_{1,\mathrm{y}}) + M_{\mathrm{z}}\,(\omega_{\mathrm{m}} - \omega_1) = 0 .\]
  3. For \(m \ge 2\) take the functions \(\varphi\) of the assumption with \(L = 1\), \(L = x\) and \(L = y\), that is with \((\alpha, \beta, \gamma) = (1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0)\) and \((0, 0, 1)\). By point 3 of lemma 11.61 these are transmitting fields from \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to \(S_1\) with \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}}) = (0, 0, 2c)\), \((0, -2c, 0)\) and \((2c, 0, 0)\). The equation of step 2 gives in turn \(\omega_{\mathrm{m}} = \omega_1\), \(a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{y}} = a_{1,\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{x}} = a_{1,\mathrm{x}}\).
  4. Therefore all \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}}\) are equal, \(\vek R_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek R_1 = \vek R\), and \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u - \vek R\) is the field we look for, as in steps 8 and 9 of the proof of theorem 11.59.

2. The function for the strip.

  1. Let \(g(x)\) and \(k(y)\) be the functions \(g = s_{\mathrm{x}_1^- \mathrm{x}_1^+} - s_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{m}^- \mathrm{x}_\mathrm{m}^+}\) and \(k = 1 - s_{-\mathrm{b}\,\mathrm{b}}\). The function \(g\) is zero for \(x \le x_1^-\) and for \(x \ge x_{\mathrm{m}}^+\), one on the interval \([x_1^+, x_{\mathrm{m}}^-]\), and the function \(k\) is one at \(y = -b\) and zero at \(y = b\). Both have continuous derivatives up to fourth order, which are zero at the points where they change (at \(x_1^\pm\), \(x_{\mathrm{m}}^\pm\), \(\pm b\)) and zero at the points outside the interval of change.
  2. Let \(\varphi = L\,(1 - g(x)\,k(y))\). The functions \(g(x)\), \(k(y)\) and \(L\) have continuous derivatives up to fourth order, so by the product rule so does \(\varphi\), like the product of bumps in point 2 of lemma 4.122, in particular up to third order. On the bottom edge \(y = -b\) we have \(k = 1\), \(k' = 0\), so \(\varphi = L\,(1 - g)\), \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = (\partial L/\partial y)(1 - g)\) and \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = (\partial L/\partial x)(1 - g) - L\,g'\). On the arcs \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{m}-1}\) of the bottom edge \(g = 1\), \(g' = 0\), so \(\varphi\) and its two derivatives are zero. On the parts of the bottom edge that belong to \(A_{\mathrm{m}}, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\) we have \(g = 0\), \(g' = 0\), so \(\varphi = L\) with the derivatives of the function \(L\).
  3. On the top edge \(y = b\) we have \(k = 0\), \(k' = 0\), so \(\varphi = L\), \(\partial\varphi/\partial y = \partial L/\partial y\), \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = \partial L/\partial x\). On the left edge \(x = 0\) and the right edge \(x = X\) we have \(g = 0\) and \(g' = 0\), because \(0 \le x_1^-\) and \(x_{\mathrm{m}}^+ \le X\), so again \(\varphi = L\) with the derivatives of the function \(L\). All these parts of the boundary belong to the arcs \(A_{\mathrm{m}}, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\), which make up the part of the boundary from \(x_{\mathrm{m}}^+\) around the strip to \(x_1^-\). The function \(\varphi\) therefore satisfies the assumption. Every piece of the strip is one rectangle of the mesh, so condition 3 holds for it with a chain of one single rectangle, and the conclusion holds.

The proof showed that for the strip and plane data the converse holds without any hypothesis on the existence of solutions. The transmitting fields are plane and we write them directly. The support condition in several pieces is proved for the strip. For another plane region the condition remains that the function \(\varphi\) exists.

11.15.4 The strip on three supports with numbers

Let us return to the strip of the introduction, \(A = [0, 300] \times [-10, 10]\) mm, of thickness \(2c = 10\) mm, with the pieces \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\) on the bottom face over \([0, 20]\), \([140, 160]\) and \([280, 300]\) mm. The mesh has five cells, cut at \(x = 20\), \(140\), \(160\) and \(280\) mm. Let \(m = 2\): we look for a field that carries a force from the middle support to the left one. The function of corollary 11.62 is \(\varphi = L\,(1 - g\,k)\) with

\[g = s_{0,20} - s_{140,160} , \qquad k = 1 - s_{-10,10} , \qquad L = \alpha + \beta\,x + \gamma\,y\]

(figure 11.44). In the middle of the strip, where \(g = 1\), we have \(\varphi = L\,(1 - k(y))\). On the arc \(A_1\) between \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) we have \(g = 1\) and \(k = 1\), so \(\varphi = 0\). On the arcs \(A_2\) between \(S_2\) and \(S_3\) and \(A_3\) around the strip we have \(g = 0\) or \(k = 0\), so \(\varphi = L\).

The forces and moments of the pieces are given by (11.83). We checked them with SymPy also by direct integration of the virtual forces on the bottom face, together with the two equilibrium equations and with zero force on the top, left and right faces. The field is drawn in figure 11.45. The piece \(S_2\) receives \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}}) = (10\gamma,\ -10\beta,\ 10\alpha)\), the piece \(S_1\) the opposite, the piece \(S_3\) nothing, because there \(\varphi = L\) and the second derivatives are zero. Units: \(\alpha\) in newtons, \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) in N/mm, forces in newtons and moments in N mm. Choose the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) N on the piece \(S_2\) along the line \(x = 150\) mm, without \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\). The moment about \(O\) is then \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = 150\) N mm. This gives \(\alpha = 15\) N, \(\beta = -0{,}1\) N/mm and \(\gamma = 0\). The piece \(S_2\) receives \((0,\ 1\ \text{N},\ 150\ \text{N mm})\), the piece \(S_1\) receives \((0,\ -1\ \text{N},\ -150\ \text{N mm})\) and the piece \(S_3\) nothing. The moment \(-150\) N mm about \(O\) is, about the centre of the piece \(S_1\), \((10, -10)\) mm, equal to \(-150 - 10\cdot(-1) = -140\) N mm. On the piece \(S_1\) therefore act a force \(-1\) N and a couple \(-140\) N mm, and on the piece \(S_2\) a force \(1\) N with no couple about its centre, because the line \(x = 150\) mm passes through this centre.

What is the stress in the strip? In the middle, at \(x = 80\) mm, we have \(L = 15 - 0{,}1\cdot 80 = 7\) N and \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2 = -7\,k''(y)\) N/mm². The axial force is \(N = 10\int \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y = -70\,[k']_{-10}^{10} = 0\), because \(k' = 0\) at both ends. The moment in the convention of chapter 5 is

\[M = -10\int_{-10}^{10} y\,\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}y = 70\,\big[y\,k' - k\big]_{-10}^{10} = 70\,\big(0 - (0 - 1)\big) = 70\ \text{N mm} ,\]

since \(k(-10) = 1\) and \(k(10) = 0\). Does this agree with equilibrium? The left part of the strip, \(x < 80\) mm, is loaded only by the piece \(S_1\), with the force \(-1\) N and the moment \(-150\) N mm about \(O\). About the centre of the section, \((80, 0)\) mm, the moment of this load is \(-150 - 80\cdot(-1) = -70\) N mm. The section must therefore give the moment \(+70\) N mm, which is the computed \(M\).

The beam formula \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I\) (theorem 5.35) would give, at the same \(M\), the largest stress \(6M/(t\,h^2) = 6\cdot 70/(10\cdot 20^2) = 0{,}105\) MPa at the edge. The transmitting field has \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at the edge, because \(k''(\pm 10) = 0\), and its largest stress \(\pm 0{,}164\) MPa at \(y = \mp 3{,}8\) mm. A stress with the same moment and zero axial force that is steeper than linear in the middle and zero at the edge must therefore reach a larger peak value, 1,56 times the beam value. A virtual field has only to be in equilibrium, it need not resemble a beam, so such a shape does no harm. In absolute value the stress along the strip is largest at \(x = 17\) mm near the left support, where the compression is \(0{,}31\) MPa and \(L\) is still \(13\) N, and it passes through zero at \(x = 150\) mm, where \(L = 0\), because the line of the force runs there (figure 11.45).

Now we apply the transmitting field to the mismatch. Let the strain be zero and the prescribed displacement \((0,\ -0{,}1\ \text{mm},\ 0)\) on all three pieces. The field \(\vek u = \vek 0\) has zero strains, and its mismatch is the same rigid displacement \(\vek R = -\bar{\vek u} = (0,\ 0{,}1\ \text{mm},\ 0)\) on every piece. The work of the field by (11.80) is \(1\ \text{N}\cdot 0{,}1\ \text{mm} + (-1\ \text{N})\cdot 0{,}1\ \text{mm} = 0\), as it must be for a common rigid displacement. Let only the middle support settle, that is let \(\bar{\vek u} = (0,\ -0{,}1\ \text{mm},\ 0)\) on \(S_2\) and \(\vek 0\) on \(S_1\) and \(S_3\). The left side of equation (11.79) is zero at zero strain, and the right side is the work of the support force on \(\bar{\vek u}\), that is \(1\ \text{N}\cdot(-0{,}1\ \text{mm}) = -0{,}1\ \text{N mm} = -10^{-4}\ \text{J}\). The equation does not hold. By the principle of virtual forces (theorem 4.97) there is therefore no displacement field with zero strains that is continuously differentiable up to the surface and equal to the prescribed displacement on the supports, because the equation would hold for it. We knew this in advance: a settlement of the middle support by 0,1 mm with a rigid strip does not exist, and the field detects it with the work \(-0{,}1\) N mm. The same field does not detect a rotation of the piece \(S_2\) about its centre, because the couple on it about the centre is zero. For a rotation we need a field with \(\alpha \ne 0\), which gives a couple \(10\alpha\) N mm. For three pieces we need three fields for \(m = 2\) and three for \(m = 3\), which detect the three numbers \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, \omega)\) of each difference of pieces.

11.15.5 A body in space

What about a body of arbitrary shape in space? We need fields for all six numbers of the difference of two pieces. For plane data we wrote them with an Airy function, for a general body we do not have them written. Where would we find them? In the traction problem. If we prescribe smooth forces on a rectangle of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) and on a rectangle of the piece \(S_1\) whose resultants and moments cancel, the stress of the solution of the traction problem is a statically admissible virtual field: it satisfies equilibrium and symmetry and is free everywhere except on these two rectangles. Its force on the support is exactly the chosen force.

But we do not prove that a solution exists, as we said with uniqueness (section 11.1): a proof of existence in general is beyond this book. So we state existence as a hypothesis (domneva) that the theorem needs, not as a result.

Hypothesis (H). A body \(V\) made of boxes with surface \(S\) satisfies hypothesis (H) if there is a linearly elastic material (as in definition 11.1, for example the steel of chapter 3) for which the following holds: if a field of forces per unit area \(\vek p\) on \(S\) is zero outside two external faces \(P\) and \(P'\), if in the plane of each of them, where we take it to be zero outside the face, it has continuous partial derivatives of first, second and third order in the coordinates of the plane, and if its resultant and its moment about \(O\) are zero, then the traction problem with data \(\vek b = \vek 0\) and \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek p\) has a solution in the sense of definition 11.1.

A solution in the sense of the definition has stresses that are continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body together with its surface. The resultant and the moment are conditions of equilibrium (lemma 11.2), without which there is no solution. The smoothness of the field \(\vek p\) is needed too: if the stresses are continuously differentiable up to the surface, \(\vek p = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is continuously differentiable on every face, so for a field \(\vek p\) that is continuous but not continuously differentiable no solution in this sense can exist. We do not prove hypothesis (H) for any body.

A warning. BARBER (§11.2.3, PDF p. 174-175) writes the solution near an edge where two free faces meet with terms in which the stress is proportional to a power \(r^{\lambda - 1}\) of the distance \(r\) from the edge, with the exponents \(\lambda\) determined by the conditions on the two free faces. For an angle between the faces above \(\pi\), at a re-entrant edge, the smallest such \(\lambda - 1\) is negative, so the stress at the edge grows without bound: at the angle \(2\pi\), at the tip of a crack, like \(r^{-1/2}\), and as the angle decreases towards \(\pi\) this growth weakens to nothing. In such a body the stresses at the edge are not necessarily continuously differentiable up to the surface, and hypothesis (H) may fail, for example for a body in the shape of the letter L (figure 4.15). At convex edges, with an angle below \(\pi\), all exponents have a positive real part, so according to BARBER (PDF p. 175-176) the stress is bounded and tends to zero at the edge. This says nothing about continuous differentiability up to the surface. So we never use (H) for a particular body, only as an assumption of the corollary below.

Corollary 11.63 Converse at supports in several pieces under hypothesis (H) (obrat na podporah v več kosih pri domnevi (H))

Let \(V\), \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(\bar{\vek u}\) and the pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{k}}\) be as in theorem 11.59, points 1 to 3, let equation (11.79) hold for every statically admissible virtual stress field and let the body \(V\) satisfy hypothesis (H). Then the conclusion of theorem 11.59 holds.

Proof

We have to show that (H) gives transmitting fields for every \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\). We choose smooth forces on two rectangles with the given resultants and take the stresses of the solution.

  1. Let \(P\) be a rectangle of the piece \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) with centre \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\). We name the axes so that \(P = \{c\} \times [a_2, b_2] \times [a_3, b_3]\), and let \(\vek s = \vek r - \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) be the vector from the centre to a point of the rectangle. Let \(w(y, z) = q_{\mathrm{a}_2 \mathrm{b}_2}(y)\,q_{\mathrm{a}_3 \mathrm{b}_3}(z)\) be the product of bumps of lemma 4.122, which is positive in the interior of the rectangle and zero outside it. The bump \(q_{\mathrm{a}_2 \mathrm{b}_2}\) has equal values at points equally far from the midpoint of the interval, while the component \(s_{\mathrm{y}}\) has opposite values there, so \(\int w\,s_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), likewise \(\int w\,s_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), and the component \(s_{\mathrm{x}}\) is zero on \(P\). Hence \(\int w\,\vek s\,\mathrm{d}A = \vek 0\). Let \(W = \int w\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\). For given vectors \(\vek f\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega\) let \(\vek p = w\,(\vek f + \boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s)\) on \(P\) and zero elsewhere on \(S\). In the plane \(x = c\), where we take it to be zero outside \(P\), \(\vek p\) has continuous partial derivatives up to third order: \(w\) has them as a product of bumps (lemma 4.122, points 1 and 2, for two variables), and \(\vek f + \boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s\) is a polynomial of first degree in \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. The resultant of the force \(\vek p\) is \(W\,\vek f\), because \(\int w\,(\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s)\,\mathrm{d}A = \boldsymbol\omega\times\int w\,\vek s\,\mathrm{d}A = \vek 0\). The moment about \(O\) is \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times W\vek f + \mat J\boldsymbol\omega\) with \(\mat J\boldsymbol\omega = \int w\,\vek s\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s)\,\mathrm{d}A\), because the other terms contain \(\int w\,\vek s = \vek 0\).
  3. The map \(\boldsymbol\omega \mapsto \mat J\boldsymbol\omega\) is linear, so we write it with a matrix \(\mat J\) of order 3 with the columns \(\mat J\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\mat J\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\mat J\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). By a computation with components according to (2.32), checked with SymPy, \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot(\vek s\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s)) = |\boldsymbol\omega|^2\,|\vek s|^2 - (\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\vek s)^2\), and by lemma 2.31, point 5, this equals \(|\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s|^2\). Hence \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mat J\boldsymbol\omega = \int w\,|\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). For \(\boldsymbol\omega \ne \vek 0\) this is positive. The vector \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\vek s\) is zero only for \(\vek s\) parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\), which on the rectangle is a segment or a point, so the continuous nonnegative integrand has a positive value at some interior point, and the integral is positive as in step 7 of the proof of corollary 4.131. Therefore \(\mat J\boldsymbol\omega = \vek 0\) implies \(\boldsymbol\omega = \vek 0\), and by lemma 8.21 the matrix \(\mat J\) has an inverse \(\mat J^{-1}\).
  4. For given \(\vek F\) and \(\vek M\) take \(\vek f = \vek F/W\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega = \mat J^{-1}(\vek M - \vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\times\vek F)\). The force \(\vek p\) then has the resultant \(\vek F\) and the moment \(\vek M\) on \(P\). On a rectangle \(P'\) of the piece \(S_1\) we build in the same way a force with resultant \(-\vek F\) and moment \(-\vek M\). The sum of both is zero outside \(P\) and \(P'\), has on them the smoothness of step 1, and has zero resultant and moment, as hypothesis (H) requires.
  5. By (H) there is a solution of the traction problem with \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek p\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\). Its stresses \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are symmetric (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 11.2), continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body together with its surface, and in equilibrium without body force at every interior point (requirement 2 of definition 11.1), and by continuity at every point of the body. On the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the original problem \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek p = \vek 0\), because the rectangles \(P\) and \(P'\) lie in \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). The field is therefore statically admissible (definition 4.96).
  6. On \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\delta\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek p\), so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek F\), \(\vek M_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek M\), \(\vek F_1 = -\vek F\), \(\vek M_1 = -\vek M\), and the other pieces have zero. The field is a transmitting field from \(S_{\mathrm{m}}\) to \(S_1\) with force \(\vek F\) and moment \(\vek M\). Assumption 4 of theorem 11.59 holds, and the theorem gives the conclusion.

Source: The corollary and the proof are not from the sources. BARBER §11.2.3 (PDF p. 174-176) for the edges.

The proof reduced assumption 4 of the theorem to hypothesis (H), which is not proved. For the strip with plane data we do not need the hypothesis.

11.15.6 What we proved

Let us return to the question of the beginning: does the equation of virtual forces ensure the support condition also for several separate pieces of support? The answer has two parts, a proved one and a conditional one, and at the end we also say what we do not prove.

Proved, without a hypothesis, is the following. Theorem 11.59 concludes the support condition from the existence of transmitting fields. For plane data lemma 11.60 shows that the mismatch is a plane rigid displacement, lemma 11.61 writes transmitting fields with an Airy function, and corollary 11.62 shows for the rectangular strip that such fields exist and that the support condition holds. The strip on three supports is the example with numbers.

Dependent on hypothesis (H) is corollary 11.63 for a body in space: it holds if the traction problem with smooth forces on two rectangles has a solution with stresses continuously differentiable up to the surface. We do not prove this hypothesis, and for bodies with re-entrant edges it may be false.

The rest is not proved. For a body in space without hypothesis (H), for example a plate glued at several places and loaded out of its plane, we have not built transmitting fields. For plane regions other than the rectangular strip we would have to build the function \(\varphi\) by mollification, which the book does not have. The promise of chapter 4 is thereby kept up to the limit that this section proves.
