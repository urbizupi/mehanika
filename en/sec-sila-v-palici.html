Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.1 Force in a bar

What is a force? In everyday language it is what we pull or push something with. A rope pulls a bar, the ceiling holds a hanging bar, the Earth pulls every bit of matter down. Two things count for each such force: how strongly it pulls and in which direction. A bar pulled along its axis gets longer. If we hold it firmly at one end and pull equally strongly across it at the other, it bends. So a force is a quantity with a size and a direction, and in mechanics exactly such quantities are described by vectors (section 1.5.5).

What force holds a weight on an inclined plane so that it does not slide? Until 1586 statics (statika), the science of bodies at rest under forces, rested on the theory of the lever of Archimedes (BALL, PDF pp. 102, 274). A weight on an inclined plane (klanec) is not a lever (vzvod), however, since it has no support about which it could turn. What force holds it had long been in dispute (BALL, PDF p. 274).

The answer was found in 1586 by Simon Stevin in a book on statics and hydrostatics published in Flemish (BALL, PDF p. 273). Over a wedge whose base is horizontal and whose two faces are in the ratio of 2 to 1, he placed a thread of equal weights at equal distances, joined into a loop whose lower part hangs freely below the wedge (BALL, PDF p. 274, figure 2.1). There were twice as many weights on the long face as on the short one, four to two, since the face is twice as long and the weights are equally spaced (BALL, PDF p. 274). The chain of weights stays at rest, he said, and experience shows it: if it did not stay at rest, it would turn forever and we would have a machine that moves perpetually without a drive (BALL, PDF p. 274). The lower part hanging below the wedge is the same on both sides, so it pulls both faces equally and does not affect which side prevails. If the chain is at rest, the effect of the weights on the long face along it must equal the effect of the weights on the short face along it (BALL, PDF p. 274). Let the short face be vertical. The two weights on it hang straight down and pull with the full force with which the Earth pulls each of them. They are held by the four weights on the long face, each of them therefore with a force half as large as the Earth pulls on it, since \(2 : 4 = 1 : 2\). This is also the ratio of the height of the wedge to the length of the long face. A weight on an inclined plane that is twice as long as its height therefore pulls along the plane with half the force with which the Earth pulls it. On an inclined plane at an angle \(\alpha\) to the horizontal plane this fraction is \(\sin\alpha\) (BALL, PDF p. 275).

In the same book Stevin stated the triangle of forces (trikotnik sil), which is equivalent to the parallelogram of forces (paralelogram sil), since a diagonal cuts a parallelogram into two triangles (BALL, PDF pp. 273-274, figure 1.24). He regarded it as the fundamental proposition of the whole of statics (BALL, PDF pp. 273-274). After the publication of his book it became usual to begin statics by proving the possibility of representing forces by straight lines, because statements about forces are in this way reduced to geometrical propositions, among them the proof of the parallelogram of forces (BALL, PDF p. 274). As we said in chapter 1, the same principle was stated by Galileo as well, but he did not fully see how far it reaches (CAJORI, PDF p. 188). He too found the force that supports a given weight on an inclined plane (BALL, PDF p. 277). BALL writes that Stevin is not clear in the arrangement of his propositions and that the new treatment was not definitely established before 1687, when the work of Varignon on mechanics appeared (BALL, PDF p. 274). In that year Varignon recast the treatment of mechanics by building it on the addition of forces (BALL, PDF p. 398).

In the same year 1687 Newton's book Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica appeared (BALL, PDF p. 379). In the introduction Newton wrote that he does not know the nature or origin of force, but that many of its effects can be measured, and that these form the subject-matter of the work (BALL, PDF p. 363). In the same introduction he put dynamics (dinamika), the science of the motion of bodies under forces, on a satisfactory basis and deduced statics from it (BALL, PDF p. 379). Galileo nowhere stated the laws of motion exactly and rather prepared the way for Newton (BALL, PDF p. 277). Among the laws that bear Newton's name is one that says: when one body acts on another with a force, the second acts on the first with a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). Why the two forces of a pair are really like this, we will see for a bar already in this section.

Definition 2.1 Force as a vector (sila kot vektor)

A force (sila) is a vector that describes how one body pulls or pushes another. The length of this vector, the magnitude of the force, tells how strongly, and its direction tells in which direction. The magnitude of a force is measured in newtons (newton), N. A force acts on a definite part of a body: on a part of its surface or on all of its material.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (PDF p. 381), NORTON §1.10 (PDF pp. 44-45), SADD §3.1 (PDF pp. 63-64).

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system we write a force vector with three components, like every vector, only the components have the unit newton instead of metre. When a force acts on a very small part of a surface, like the tip of a pencil on paper, we often imagine this part as a point and say that the force acts at a point. A force distributed over a larger part of the surface or over all the material, like the weight, is the sum of many small forces, one on each bit of that part.

When several forces act at the same point of a body, they have the same effect as a single force, their vector sum (KREYSZIG §9.1, PDF p. 383, where the sum of two forces is drawn as the diagonal of a parallelogram). In figure 2.2 on the left two ropes pull a ring, and a single rope pulling it with the force \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2\) would have the same effect. For a force, though, not only the arrow matters but also where it acts. We open a door if we push it near the handle, but the same force near the hinges does not move it (figure 2.2, right).

How large is a newton? The Earth pulls every body down with a force called the weight (teža) of the body. Its magnitude is proportional to the mass (masa) of the body, that is, to how many kilograms of matter it contains:

\[W = m\,g , \qquad g = 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 . \tag{2.1}\]

The number \(g\) is the gravitational acceleration (težni pospešek) at the surface of the Earth. It tells by how many metres per second the speed of a body falling without air resistance grows every second (NORTON (1.2), PDF p. 45, where the same equation is written as \(m = W/g\)). A body with a mass of 1 kg weighs \(9{,}81\) N, an apple with a mass of 0,1 kg about 1 N, and a person with a mass of 70 kg \(686{,}7\) N. So a newton is a kilogram times a metre per second squared, \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}/\text{s}^2\) (NORTON §1.10, PDF pp. 44-45). A thousand newtons is a kilonewton, kN, which is the weight of about 102 kg.

Take the hanging steel bar from chapter 1, of length \(L = 10\) m. Let it be round, with diameter \(d = 10\) mm. The area (ploščina) of its cross section is, by the school formula for a circle,

\[A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi\,(10\ \text{mm})^2}{4} = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2 .\]

Its volume is the area of the cross section times the length, \(A L = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\). We get the mass by multiplying the volume by the density (gostota) \(\rho\), the mass of one cubic metre of the material. For mild steel (navadno konstrukcijsko jeklo) \(\rho = 7{,}8\) Mg/m³ (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). A megagram, Mg, is 1000 kg (BOWER, PDF p. 100), so this is 7800 kg/m³. So the bar has the mass and, by (2.1), the weight

\[m = \rho A L = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3 = 6{,}126\ \text{kg} , \qquad W = m\,g = 60{,}10\ \text{N} .\]

Several forces usually act on a body at once. On the hanging bar the weight and the ceiling act, on a bar that we pull two ropes, one at each end. For the question whether a body stays at rest the sum of all the forces acting on it will matter, so it gets its own name.

Definition 2.2 Resultant (rezultanta)

The resultant (rezultanta) of the forces \(\vek F_1, \vek F_2, \dots, \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) acting on a body is their vector sum \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (PDF p. 383), SADD §3.7 (PDF p. 77).

When does a body stay at rest under such forces? The answer comes from Newton's law of motion. For a body that moves as a whole, without turning and without changing its shape, it reads: the resultant of the forces on the body equals the mass of the body times its acceleration, that is, times how fast its velocity changes (EP §2.2.1, (2.2.1) and (2.2.2), p. 50, where the law is written for a material particle or a collection of material particles with constant mass, with the resultant of all forces on the collection). A body that is at rest and stays at rest does not accelerate, so the resultant of the forces on it is the zero vector. How the law reads for a body that turns or changes its shape we will see later in this chapter.

That is not yet enough. Push a ruler lying on a table along the axis \(x\) with the force \((0, 5, 0)\) N at one end and with the force \((0, -5, 0)\) N at the other. The resultant is \((0, 0, 0)\) N, and yet the ruler turns (figure 2.3, top). So for rest one more condition must hold: the forces must not turn the body (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77). The two conditions together have a name.

Definition 2.3 Equilibrium of a body (ravnovesje telesa)

A body is in equilibrium (ravnovesje or ravnotežje) when the resultant of the forces acting on it is the zero vector,

\[\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0 , \tag{2.2}\]

and the forces also do not tend to turn the body.

Source: SADD §3.7 (PDF p. 77), BOWER §2.3.1, §2.3.2 (PDF pp. 77-78).

So equilibrium is a state of a body that we check on the forces acting on it. A body at rest is in equilibrium (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77). But a body in equilibrium need not be at rest. A body that moves with constant velocity and does not turn also has no acceleration, so the resultant of the forces on it is the zero vector (EP (2.2.2), p. 50). Equation (2.2) is a vector equation, so it means three equations for numbers, one for each component (definition 1.13). The second part of the condition, that the forces do not tend to turn the body, we will write with numbers in section 2.8.3. Unless we say otherwise, in this chapter the forces on a bar will act along its axis, as on a ruler pulled at both ends along its length (figure 2.3, bottom), and such forces do not turn the bar.

Pull a bar lying along the axis \(x\) at its right end with a force of 10 kN in the direction of the axis \(x\), and at its left end with an equally large force in the opposite direction. The forces are \((10, 0, 0)\) kN and \((-10, 0, 0)\) kN, their resultant is \((0, 0, 0)\) kN, and both act along the axis of the bar. The bar is in equilibrium. For the hanging bar we point the axis \(x\) vertically down, as in figure 1.30. The weight points in the direction of the axis \(x\), so its vector is \(W\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(W = 60{,}10\) N. The ceiling holds the bar with the force \(-W\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the resultant is again the zero vector.

Now we look inside the bar. What holds the left and the right part of the bar together? Cut the bar, pulled with the force \(F\) at both ends, in thought with a cross section at the distance \(x\) from the left end (figure 2.4). The cut is only imagined, the bar stays whole. But we ask which forces act on each of the two parts. On the left part acts the force at the left end, which pulls it to the left. If this were the only force, the left part would not stay at rest. Since it is at rest, the right part must pull it to the right across the face of the cut. We made this argument already for the piece of the bar in figure 1.12. Books use it everywhere: if the whole body is in equilibrium, every part of it is in equilibrium too, and across the cut the forces of the rest of the body act on each part (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77, BOWER §2.2.2, PDF p. 67).

Let us compute these forces. Let the axis \(x\) lie along the axis of the bar, pointing from the left end to the right end. We call the force with which the right part acts on the left part across the cut \(\vek R\), and the force with which the left part acts on the right part \(\vek R'\). We neglect the weight of the bar. For a steel bar with diameter 10 mm and length 10 m it is 60 N, which is 0,6 % of the force 10 kN.

  1. On the left part act the force \(-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the left end and the force \(\vek R\) across the cut.
  2. The left part is in equilibrium, so by (2.2)
    \[-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R = \vek 0 .\]
  3. We add \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) to both sides. By the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15) we may reorder the terms of a sum, and the sum \(-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the zero vector. What remains is
    \[\vek R = F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]
  4. On the right part act the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the right end and the force \(\vek R'\) across the cut. From the equilibrium of the right part, \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R' = \vek 0\), we get in the same way \(\vek R' = -F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  5. So \(\vek R' = -\vek R\).

The right part pulls the left one to the right, the left part pulls the right one to the left, and both forces are equally large. That they are opposite is no accident. When one body acts on another with a force, the other acts on the first with an equal and opposite force. This is Newton's law of action and reaction (akcija in reakcija) (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). For the bar we obtained it from the equilibrium of the two parts.

We chose the distance \(x\) freely, so in every cross section one part of the bar pulls the other with the same force of magnitude \(F\). We do not see this force until we cut the body in thought. So it gets its own name.

Definition 2.4 Internal force (notranja sila)

The internal force (notranja sila) in a cut of a body is the resultant of the forces with which the part of the body on one side of the cut acts on the part on the other side.

Source: EP §2.2.1 (p. 50), SADD §3.1 (PDF p. 63), BOWER §2.2.2 (PDF p. 67).

The force at the end of the bar and the weight, by contrast, are external forces (zunanje sile), because they act on the body from outside, from the rope and from the Earth (EP §2.2.1, p. 50). In general the forces across a cut are distributed over the whole face of the cut and need not all pull in the same direction. The internal force is only their sum. Take a bar that is built into a wall at one end and pushed down at its free end (figure 2.5). The bar bends. In the cut the upper part of the material pulls the neighbouring part towards itself, the lower part pushes it, and in addition the part at the wall holds the rest of the bar up. So the forces across the cut also tend to turn the neighbouring part. Their sum, the internal force, does not tell how strongly they tend to turn it. When everything happens in one plane, books describe them by three quantities: a force along the axis of the bar, a force across it, and a number that measures how strongly the forces tend to turn the neighbouring part (EP (2.5.6), p. 74). We will define them precisely in chapter 5. In a bar pulled along its axis the internal force points along the axis, and one number is enough for it.

Definition 2.5 Axial force (osna sila)

Let the axis \(x\) lie along the axis of the bar. The axial force (osna sila) \(N\) in a cross section of the bar is the component \(x\) of the internal force with which the part of the bar on the side of larger \(x\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(x\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), ONATE1 (PDF p. 38).

The axial force is a number. A positive axial force means that the part on the side of larger \(x\) pulls the other one towards itself, so the two parts pull each other. This is called tension (nateg). A negative axial force means that the parts push each other, as in the compression of chapter 1 (figure 2.6). The part on the side of smaller \(x\) acts on the other one with the opposite force, so it does not matter which part we look at. In the bar pulled with the force \(F\) at both ends, by step 3 above, \(N = F\) in every cross section. For \(F = 10\) kN, \(N = 10\) kN.

In the hanging bar the axial force is not the same everywhere. We point the axis \(x\) vertically down, with the origin at the upper end, as in figure 1.30. Cut the bar in thought at the distance \(x\) from the ceiling and look at the lower part, from the cut to the lower end at \(L\) (figure 2.7).

  1. On the lower part act its weight and the force \(\vek R\) with which the upper part pulls it across the cut. The lower end is free, so there are no other forces.
  2. The lower part has the length \(L - x\), its volume is \(A\,(L - x)\), its mass \(\rho A\,(L - x)\) and its weight, by (2.1), \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\). The weight points in the direction of the axis \(x\), so its vector is \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The weight acts on every bit of the lower part separately, but for equilibrium only the sum of all these forces counts, the weight of the whole part.
  3. The lower part is in equilibrium, so \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R = \vek 0\) and \(\vek R = -\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The upper part pulls the lower one up.
  4. By the law of action and reaction the lower part pulls the upper one with the opposite force \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), downwards. The lower part is on the side of larger \(x\), so the component \(x\) of this force is the axial force:
    \[N(x) = \rho g A\,(L - x) . \tag{2.3}\]

So the axial force in the hanging bar is the weight of the part of the bar below the cut. It is positive, which means tension, as we expect for a bar that hangs. For our bar it is \(N(0) = 60{,}10\) N at the top, the weight of the whole bar, \(N(5\ \text{m}) = 30{,}05\) N at the middle, and zero at the lower end. Exactly this argument explained in section 1.2 why the material at the middle of the bar is stretched half as much as at the top.

We get the same if we look at the upper part, from the ceiling to the cut. On it act the ceiling, its weight \(\rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the force of the lower part, which we call \(\vek Q\). The ceiling holds the whole bar, so by the equilibrium of the whole bar it acts with the force \(-\rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The equilibrium of the upper part, \(-\rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek Q = \vek 0\), gives

\[\vek Q = \rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - \rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

the same as in step 4, this time without the law of action and reaction.

The axial force in our hanging bar falls from 60 N at the top to zero at the bottom, and in the bar that we pull it is 10 kN everywhere. Is 10 kN a lot for this bar? That depends on how much material carries the force, and this is the subject of the next section.
