Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.7 Multicylinder engines (večvaljni motorji)

Engine \(E\) of the previous sections (our model, not data) has one cylinder. At \(1500\) 1/min the primary shaking force of its piston is, by section 23.3, \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a \Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N at the frequency of rotation, and the secondary one is \(564{,}1\) N at twice that frequency. Section 23.6 showed that a counterweight on the crank does not remove these forces: at least half of the primary force remains. What if we put several such cylinders on the same shaft? The piston of one cylinder accelerates upwards while another accelerates downwards, and the forces could cancel. The question is at what angles the cranks must stand for them to cancel, and which forces and moments remain. There is also a second requirement, which pulls the other way: the cylinders must fire evenly, otherwise the engine runs roughly.

The name multicylinder says that there are several cylinders. A cylinder (valj) is a cylindrical space in which the piston slides, and it got its name from this shape. An inline engine (vrstni motor) is an engine whose cylinders stand in a row, one behind the other along the shaft. In the figure the cylinders stand in a row along the shaft.

Model. The engine has \(N\) equal cylinders: equal cranks (radius \(a\)), conrods (length \(l\)) and pistons (reciprocating mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) from section 23.3), as in engine \(E\). The axes of all cylinders are parallel and lie in the same plane, the axis \(x\) is their direction. The crankshaft (kolenasta gred) lies perpendicular to them, on the axis \(z\), and cylinder \(i\) stands in the plane \(z = z_{\mathrm{i}}\). The shaft is rigid (its torsion is treated in section 23.9), it turns with constant angular speed \(\Omega\), the pins are frictionless and all links rigid. The rotating masses (the crank pin and the part of the conrod, \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) by lemma 23.15) are balanced on each crank by a counterweight (lemma 23.32). So only the forces of the reciprocating masses act on the frame, each along its own axis \(x\). By Newton's second law the frame is what accelerates the pistons, and by the third law the pistons act on the frame with the opposite force, \(-m_{\mathrm{B}} a_{\mathrm{B}}\) (definition 23.17). The gas force and the weight do not appear in this force. Figure 23.56 shows this model: the shaft with the cylinders in the planes \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the cranks.

We build figure 23.56 as on a whiteboard. First we draw the shaft vertically, because this is the axis \(z\). On it we place four cylinders: each has a dashed axis along \(x\) and a number, from the bottom up \(1\) to \(4\). To each we add a crank, which goes from the shaft to the crank pin (the dot). At \(\theta = 0\) crank \(1\) points outwards along its axis, in the direction \(+x\), which is top dead centre (definition 23.7), and so does crank \(4\). Cranks \(2\) and \(3\) point in the direction \(-x\), which is their bottom dead centre. In the middle between the outer cylinders we mark the plane \(C\) and from it we measure the distances \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) to the cylinder planes, positive upwards and negative downwards, so \(z_4 = -z_1\) and \(z_3 = -z_2\).

The right part of the figure looks along the shaft: the axis \(x\) is horizontal, the axis \(y\) vertical, and the circle is the path of the crank pins. Crank \(1\) is at angle \(\theta\) from the axis \(x\), crank \(4\) is in the same direction, and cranks \(2\) and \(3\) are turned by half a turn: they lag behind crank \(1\) by the angle \(\varphi_2 = \pi\). Such a lag is called the phase angle.

Definition 23.38 Crank phase angle and crank phase diagram (fazni kot ročice in fazni diagram)

An inline engine with \(N\) cylinders has a crankshaft with cranks \(1, \dots, N\), counted along the axis \(z\). At time \(t\) crank \(1\) is at angle \(\theta = \Omega t\) from the axis \(x\) that cylinder \(1\) holds (the angle of definition 23.7). The crank phase angle of crank \(i\) is the number \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} \in [0, 2\pi)\) for which crank \(i\) is at the same time at angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the axis of its cylinder, so it lags behind crank \(1\) by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\varphi_1 = 0\). The plane of cylinder \(i\) is at \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\). The crank phase diagram is the list \((\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{N}})\) together with the positions \(z_1, \dots, z_{\mathrm{N}}\), drawn as the views of figure 23.56. Crank \(i\) in the plane \((x, y)\) is written by the complex number \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})}\) (definition 9.33).

Source: NORTON section 14.2 (PDF pp. 749-750). NORTON counts a lag as a negative angle and writes positive values without a sign in his lists. We keep the lag \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\), so his lists are \(0\), \(180\), \(180\), \(0\) degrees. The notation with the number \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\) is not from the source.

The word phase says at which point of a repeating motion a body is. The phase angle of crank \(i\) says by what part of a turn along the circle crank \(i\) is behind crank \(1\). In the figure this is the lag of cranks \(2\) and \(3\) behind crank \(1\) in the view along the shaft.

Crank \(i\) is at angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), so by theorem 23.19 the force of cylinder \(i\) is the force of cylinder \(1\) at angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The part of this force given by the reciprocating mass is \(-m_{\mathrm{B}}\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\), that is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\) times the series in the brackets of equation (23.12). We denote the coefficients of this series by \(A_{\mathrm{n}}\): \(A_1 = 1\) and \(A_{\mathrm{n}} = c_{\mathrm{n}}\) for \(n \ge 2\). By theorem 23.12 the coefficients \(A_{\mathrm{n}}\) at odd \(n \ge 3\) are zero, and for our engine (\(\lambda = a/l = 0{,}25\)) we have (computed, not proved in closed form) \(A_1 = 1\), \(A_2 = 0{,}254025\) (close to \(\lambda\), difference \(1{,}6\) %), \(A_4 = -0{,}004098\) and \(A_6 = 0{,}000074\) (num-07.out). The force on the frame along \(x\) is For crank \(4\) this is the same angle as for crank \(1\), for cranks \(2\) and \(3\) it is \(\theta - \pi\).

\[F_{\mathrm{i}}(t) = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 \sum_{\mathrm{n} \ge 1} A_{\mathrm{n}} \cos n(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}) . \tag{23.35}\]

The term with \(\cos n(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\) is the component of order \(n\) (harmonic of order \(n\)): at \(n = 1\) the primary force, at \(n = 2\) the secondary force of section 23.3.

The sum of the forces of all cylinders is one quantity, the sum of their moments another. The force of each cylinder acts in its own plane, and the moment also depends on the distance \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) of that plane from plane \(C\).

Definition 23.39 Shaking moment of the engine (tresilni moment motorja)

Let \(F_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) be the forces of the reciprocating masses of the cylinders on the frame, each along the axis \(x\) of cylinder \(i\), which meets the shaft axis at the point \((0, 0, z_{\mathrm{i}})\). The shaking moment of the engine about the axis \(y\) through the origin of the shaft is the sum of the moments of these forces about this point, \(M(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\). The moment of the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the point \((0, 0, z)\) is namely the cross product \((0, 0, z) \times (F, 0, 0) = (0, zF, 0)\): it has only the component \(y\), equal to \(zF\). The force \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) acts along the axis of cylinder \(i\), and its arm to the point on the shaft is the distance \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: NORTON section 14.5 (PDF pp. 755-757, equation (14.6) on p. 756). NORTON expressly separates the shaking moment, which rocks the engine forwards and backwards in the plane of the cylinders, from the torque about the shaft axis (PDF p. 757).

The shaking moment is not the torque about the shaft axis of section 23.4, which the crank delivers to the drive: the shaking moment rocks the frame about the axis \(y\), the torque turns it about the axis \(z\). The torque of the crank turns about the axis of the shaft, while the shaking moment tilts the frame in the plane of the cylinders.

Before we state the general theorem, take two cylinders with cranks \(0\) and \(\pi\). At \(n = 1\) the forces by equation (23.35) are opposite, \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\theta\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos(\theta - \pi) = -m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\theta\), because \(\cos(x - \pi) = -\cos x\), and they cancel. At \(n = 2\) we have \(\cos 2(\theta - \pi) = \cos 2\theta\), the forces are equal and add. A lag of \(\pi\) reverses the crank, and at double frequency it means a turn by \(2\pi\), that is the same state. The physical reason: the first order comes from the motion of the crank pin, which for opposite cranks really is opposite, while the second order comes from the tilt of the rod, which at the positions \(\theta\) and \(\theta + \pi\) has the same size and is only mirrored, so both pistons push the frame the same way at the same moment. For a general arrangement of the cranks we must add cosines with shifts \(n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), and this is a sum of unit vectors in the complex plane. In figure 23.57 this is the twin: at \(n = 1\) the two steps cancel, at \(n = 2\) they add.

Theorem 23.40 Balance of an inline engine (uravnoteženost vrstnega motorja)

Let an inline engine be as in definition 23.38 with \(N\) cylinders, equal masses \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) and positions \(z_1, \dots, z_{\mathrm{N}}\), all rotating masses balanced. For an integer \(n \ge 1\) denote the complex numbers Figure 23.57 shows the adding of the vectors \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\), the two rows for \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\).

\[S_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} , \qquad T_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{N} z_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} . \tag{23.36}\]

Let \(n\) be such that \(A_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\) (by theorem 23.12 this can only be \(n = 1\) or an even \(n\)). Then:

  1. The component of order \(n\) of the sum of the forces of all cylinders is \(F^{(n)}(\theta) = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}} |S_{\mathrm{n}}| \cos(n\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}})\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \arg S_{\mathrm{n}}\) (zero when \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\)). It is zero for every \(\theta\) exactly when \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). For the three-cylinder engine the chain closes, the sum is zero.
  2. The component of order \(n\) of the shaking moment (definition 23.39) is \(M^{(n)}(\theta) = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}} |T_{\mathrm{n}}| \cos(n\theta - \tau_{\mathrm{n}})\) with \(\tau_{\mathrm{n}} = \arg T_{\mathrm{n}}\). It is zero for every \(\theta\) exactly when \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). The weights \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the distances of the cylinder planes from plane \(C\).
  3. If \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), then \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not depend on the choice of the origin of the axis \(z\). If \(S_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\), a shift of the origin by \(z_0\) changes \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) into \(T_{\mathrm{n}} - z_0 S_{\mathrm{n}}\). The origin of the \(z\) axis is plane \(C\) in the figure, moving it changes all distances \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\).
Proof

We must show three points. We write the sum of cosines as the real part of a sum of exponential functions, and this is a complex number with a modulus and an argument. The sum of the vectors is shown, for example, by the chain of the twin.

  1. By expression (23.35) the component of order \(n\) of the force of cylinder \(i\) is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(n\theta - n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\). For real \(x\) we have \(\cos x = \mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}\) (definition 9.33), and \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n\theta - n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}})} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\) (theorem 9.34, point 1). The sum over the cylinders is therefore \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\big)\).
  2. The number \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}y} = \cos y - \mathrm{i}\sin y\) is the conjugate of \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}y}\), and a sum is conjugate to the sum, so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} = \overline{S_{\mathrm{n}}} = |S_{\mathrm{n}}|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\) (polar form, definition 9.29).
  3. Hence the sum of the forces is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}} |S_{\mathrm{n}}|\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}})} = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}} |S_{\mathrm{n}}|\cos(n\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  4. If \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), the expression is zero for every \(\theta\). If \(S_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\), at \(\theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}/n\) it equals \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 A_{\mathrm{n}} |S_{\mathrm{n}}| \ne 0\), because \(A_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\). This proves point 1. For \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) the chain closes, otherwise it stays open.
  5. By definition 23.39 we have \(M^{(n)} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{i}}^{(n)}\). Steps 1 to 4 hold with the real weights \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) at each term: \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}}\cos(n\theta - n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\overline{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\big) = |T_{\mathrm{n}}|\cos(n\theta - \tau_{\mathrm{n}})\). This is point 2.
  6. When the origin is shifted by \(z_0\), the new positions are \(z_{\mathrm{i}} - z_0\) and the new \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} (z_{\mathrm{i}} - z_0)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} = T_{\mathrm{n}} - z_0 S_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not change. This is point 3.

Source: NORTON section 14.3 (PDF pp. 750-754, equations (14.3a, b) on p. 753) writes the same condition for \(n = 1, 2\) as \(\sum\cos n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and \(\sum\sin n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), which are the real and imaginary part of the number \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\). Section 14.5 (PDF pp. 755-757, equation (14.7) on p. 756) does the same for the moment. The proof with complex numbers, all orders \(n\) and point 3 are not from the source.

The theorem brings three things that we will need. The condition for the balance of order \(n\) is a condition on the addition of the unit vectors \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\) and not on the force itself. The conditions for the force (\(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\)) and for the moment (\(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\)) are independent, because they have different weights. And each order has its own condition: the primary force (\(n = 1\)) and the secondary one (\(n = 2\)) are not linked. Figure 23.57 shows the addition.

In the three-cylinder engine at \(n = 1\) the three steps are equally long and each is turned by \(120^\circ\) against the previous one: the chain closes into a triangle, the sum is zero. At \(n = 2\) the turn between the steps is \(240^\circ\) and the chain closes again. In the four-cylinder engine with cranks \(0\), \(\pi\), \(\pi\), \(0\) the first step is \(+1\), the second and third \(-1\) and the fourth again \(+1\): the chain returns to the origin. At \(n = 2\) the turn \(\pi\) doubles into \(2\pi\), all four steps are equal to \(+1\) and the sum is \(4\). For the two-cylinder engine with \(0\) and \(\pi\) the sum is zero at \(n = 1\) and \(2\) at \(n = 2\). The labels of the rows on the left give the order: \(n = 1\) at the top, \(n = 2\) at the bottom. We shifted the steps that lie on the same line upwards so that they can be seen, and we measure the sum without the shift.

Example 23.41 Engine E with several cylinders (motor E z več valji)

Each cylinder is a cylinder of engine \(E\) (our model): \(a = 50\) mm, \(l = 200\) mm, \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}8\) kg, \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s, so \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N and \(A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 564{,}1\) N. The spacing of neighbouring cylinders is \(b = 0{,}1\) m, \(z_{\mathrm{i}} = (i - (N+1)/2)\,b\), the plane \(C\) is in the middle. The three-cylinder engine cancels the forces of both orders, but not the moment: the primary moment \(\sqrt3\,b\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 384{,}6\) N m is considerably larger than the secondary one. The reason is in the weights. The sum \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a sum of three unit vectors spread around a circle and it closes (\(3\cdot 120^\circ = 360^\circ\)), but the sum \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) has vectors with the weights \(z_{\mathrm{i}} = -b, 0, b\), and the weighted vectors do not close. For the four-cylinder engine \(0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2\) it is the same, only with a square instead of a triangle. The zeros in the columns \(|S_{\mathrm{n}}|\) are the closed chains of figure 23.57, and the values \(2\) and \(4\) at \(n = 2\) are chains whose steps are all equal, so they add.

Of the arrangements in the table only the six-cylinder engine satisfies all four conditions (\(S_1 = S_2 = 0\), \(T_1 = T_2 = 0\)). Both mirror-symmetric shafts, the four-cylinder engine with \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) and the six-cylinder engine, have zero moments at all orders. This is no accident. A crankshaft is mirror symmetric when cylinders \(1\) and \(4\) and cylinders \(2\) and \(3\) are symmetric about plane \(C\), as for the cylinders of the figure.

Lemma 23.42 Mirror symmetry of the crankshaft (zrcalna simetrija gredi)

Let an inline engine be as in theorem 23.40, the positions \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) measured from the plane \(C\) in the middle, and let \(z_{\mathrm{N}+1-\mathrm{i}} = -z_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{N}+1-\mathrm{i}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) hold for every \(i\) (the shaft is mirror-symmetric about the plane \(C\)). Then \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) for every \(n\), so the shaking moment of all orders about the axis \(y\) through the plane \(C\) vanishes. The symmetry is about plane \(C\) in the figure, with respect to which cylinders \(1\) and \(4\) have opposite distances.

Proof

We must show that the terms of the sum \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) of theorem 23.40 cancel in pairs. The pairs are cylinders \(1\) and \(4\) and cylinders \(2\) and \(3\) of the figure of cylinders.

  1. Cylinders \(i\) and \(N + 1 - i\) have by assumption the same \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi}\) and opposite \(z\), so the sum of their terms is \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} - z_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} = 0\). In the figure these are the distances \(z_1\) and \(z_4 = -z_1\).
  2. For odd \(N\) the middle cylinder is \(i = (N+1)/2\) and \(z_{\mathrm{i}} = -z_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \(z_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and its term is zero. The middle cylinder lies in plane \(C\).
  3. The sum \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the sum of these pairs and of a possible middle term, so it is zero. The pairs are the cylinders of the figure.

Source: NORTON section 14.6 (PDF pp. 764-765, figure 14-17) writes the same for the primary moment: a shaft mirror-symmetric about the transverse plane in the middle, with equal spacing and equal forces, balances the primary moment. The proof for all orders \(n\) is not from the source.

For the four-cylinder engine with \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) NORTON (PDF p. 765-766, table 14-4) writes that the secondary moment is nonzero, while our lemma says that about the plane \(C\) it is zero. There is no contradiction: NORTON computes the sum of moments about an axis through the first cylinder (PDF p. 755), and because the secondary force is nonzero (\(S_2 = 4\)), the moment about a point is not independent of the point (point 3 of the theorem). About the first cylinder \(|T_2| = 6b = 0{,}6\) m and the moment is \(0{,}6\cdot 564{,}1 = 338{,}5\) N m. This is the moment of the force \(2256{,}4\) N, which acts at the plane \(C\), on the arm \(1{,}5\,b = 0{,}15\) m. The moment of a force that is not zero depends on the point about which we measure it. For a zero force it would be the same everywhere. The first cylinder is the lowest in the figure, and plane \(C\) is \(1{.}5\,b\) above it.

What if one of the conditions of the theorem does not hold?

Example 23.43 Cases where a condition fails (near misses)

Three assumptions dropped, each on the shaft of engine \(E\).

  1. Equal masses. For the shaft \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) let piston \(4\) have a \(10\) % larger mass, \(\mu_4 = 1{,}1\). Instead of \(S_1\) the sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}} = 1 - 1 - 1 + 1{,}1 = 0{,}1\), so a primary force \(0{,}1\cdot 2220{,}7 = 222{,}1\) N remains, which was not there before. The theorem holds only for equal cylinders. The chain of the four-cylinder engine then does not return to the origin, a step of \(0{.}1\) remains.
  2. Mirror symmetry. The shaft \(0, \pi, 0, \pi\) has \(S_1 = 0\) (the force of the first order is balanced), but it is not mirror-symmetric and \(|T_1| = 0{,}2\) m, so the primary moment is \(0{,}2\cdot 2220{,}7 = 444{,}1\) N m. The condition \(S_1 = 0\) therefore says nothing about the moment. Here crank \(3\) would point the way of crank \(1\) and crank \(4\) the way of crank \(2\) (compare the cranks), so the shaft is not symmetric about plane \(C\).
  3. Higher orders. The shaft \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) has \(S_4 = 4\), so the force of order \(4\) has amplitude \(4\,|A_4|\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 36{,}4\) N at frequency \(4\Omega\) (num-07.out): the neglected component is not zero, but it is \(62\) times smaller than the secondary one, \(36{,}4/2256{,}4 = 1{,}6\) %. As at \(n = 2\), all steps are equal at \(n = 4\) too.

So we are interested only in orders \(1\) and \(2\). In the six-cylinder engine only the force components of orders \(6, 12, 18, \dots\) are nonzero, because at the other orders \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) or \(A_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). The component of order \(6\) has amplitude \(6A_6\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 0{,}99\) N, the component of order \(12\) only \(5{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\) N (NumPy). Orders \(1\) and \(2\) are the two rows of the sum figure.

The same numbers \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) also determine the torque about the shaft axis.

Corollary 23.44 Inertia torque of several cylinders (vztrajnostni moment več valjev)

Let the engine be as in theorem 23.40. The inertia torque of one crank, \(\tfrac12 J'(\theta)\Omega^2\) (lemma 23.23), is a \(2\pi\)-periodic odd function of the crank angle with the series \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}}\sin n\theta\). The coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\) for engine \(E\) are in section 23.4: \(b_1 = -7{,}05\), \(b_2 = 57{,}46\), \(b_3 = 21{,}32\), \(b_4 = 1{,}73\) N m. The component of order \(n\) of the total inertia torque of all cranks is Each crank gives the same torque, only at the angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

\[T_{\mathrm{vzt}}^{(n)}(\theta) = b_{\mathrm{n}}\,|S_{\mathrm{n}}|\,\sin(n\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \arg S_{\mathrm{n}} , \tag{23.37}\]

and it is zero for every \(\theta\) exactly when \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) (for \(b_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\)).

Proof

We must show that the sum over the cranks gives the same number \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) as for the force. In the view along the shaft the angle of the crank is \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. Crank \(i\) is at angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and is the same crank as crank \(1\), so its torque is \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}}\sin n(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  2. For real \(x\) we have \(\sin x = \mathrm{Im}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}\). The sum over \(i\) is therefore \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\big) = b_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}\overline{S_{\mathrm{n}}}\big)\).
  3. By the polar form this is \(b_{\mathrm{n}}|S_{\mathrm{n}}|\sin(n\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}})\). The cancellation at every \(\theta\) is as in step 4 of the proof of theorem 23.40. The zero comes from a closed chain as for the force.

Source: NORTON section 14.4 (PDF pp. 754-755, equations (14.4) and (14.5)) the same condition. The notation with the number \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not from the source. Oddness: \(J\) is an even function of the angle, because the mechanism is mirror-symmetric about the axis \(x\), so \(J'\) is odd.

For engine \(E\) with cranks \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) the inertia torque of order \(2\) equals \(4\cdot 57{,}46 = 229{,}8\) N m, that of order \(4\) is \(4\cdot 1{,}73 = 6{,}9\) N m, and orders \(1\) and \(3\) vanish (num-07.out). For the six-cylinder engine the inertia torque of order \(3\) equals \(6\cdot 21{,}32 = 127{,}9\) N m and all others vanish. NORTON (PDF p. 779) says the same: the third order of the inertia torque remains in the inline six. The condition \(S_2 = 0\) is thus at the same time the condition for the secondary force and for the inertia torque of order \(2\). At \(n = 2\) the chain is open, at \(n = 1\) it is closed.

The four-cylinder engine with \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) therefore has a secondary force \(2256{,}4\) N, which is almost as large as the primary force of one cylinder (\(2220{,}7\) N) and has double frequency. Why would anyone choose this shaft, if the four-cylinder engine with \(0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2\) cancels the forces of both orders? The answer is in the firing. The sum \(S_2 = 4\) is the chain of four equal steps.

23.7.1 Even firing (enakomerno vžiganje)

An engine is not only a machine that shakes, but also a machine that delivers torque, and the gas force in a cylinder acts only during the power stroke. Our model of the gas force (not data, section 23.5) is \(F_{\mathrm{g}} = F_0(1 - \theta/\pi)\) on \([0, \pi]\) every \(4\pi\) with \(F_0 = 20\) kN. In a four-stroke engine the cycle of a cylinder ends after two turns of the crank, that is at the angle \(4\pi\): one power stroke per two turns. With four cylinders we would like the power strokes to follow one another evenly. In figure 23.58 a power stroke is a blue line of length \(\pi\) in the row of the cylinder.

In each part of figure 23.58 the horizontal axis is the angle \(\theta\) of the first cylinder from \(0\) to \(720^\circ\) and each row is the power stroke of one cylinder. In the upper part cylinder \(1\) is first, cylinder \(3\) follows after \(180^\circ\), cylinder \(4\) after \(360^\circ\) and cylinder \(2\) after \(540^\circ\): the strokes fill the whole cycle. In the lower part the strokes are spread unevenly, as we shall see.

Definition 23.45 Firing phase and even firing (vžigni fazni kot in enakomerno vžiganje)

Let a four-stroke inline engine with \(N\) cylinders be as in definition 23.38 and \(T_{\mathrm{g}}(t)\) the gas torque of cylinder \(1\), a \(4\pi/\Omega\)-periodic function of time (our model). The firing phase of cylinder \(k\) is the number \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} \in [0, 4\pi)\) at which its power stroke starts, if the power stroke of cylinder \(1\) starts at \(\psi_1 = 0\), measured by the angle \(\theta = \Omega t\) of crank \(1\). The gas torque of cylinder \(k\) is \(T_{\mathrm{g}}(t - \psi_{\mathrm{k}}/\Omega)\). Because crank \(k\) is at angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\), it reaches the origin of the cycle at \(\theta = \varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) or \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} + 2\pi\), so \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{k}} + 2\pi m_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(m_{\mathrm{k}} \in \{0, 1\}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} \equiv \varphi_{\mathrm{k}} \pmod{2\pi}\). Which of the two turns is the right one is decided by the firing order. Firing is even if the \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) in some order equal \(0, \tfrac{4\pi}{N}, \tfrac{8\pi}{N}, \dots, \tfrac{4\pi(N-1)}{N}\), that is, the power strokes follow one another at equal time intervals at constant speed. In the figure the firing phase is the start of the line of the cylinder.

Source: NORTON section 14.6 (PDF pp. 757-760, equation (14.8b) on p. 758: \(\Delta\varphi = 720^\circ/n\) for a four-stroke engine). The relation \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} \equiv \varphi_{\mathrm{k}} \pmod{2\pi}\) and its proof are ours.

What does even firing say about the orders of the force, of the inertia torque and of the gas torque? First a lemma on the sum of roots of unity.

Lemma 23.46 Sum of roots of unity (vsota enotskih korenov)

Let \(N \ge 1\) and \(q\) be integers. Then \(\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi qj/N} = N\) if \(N\) divides \(q\), and \(0\) if \(N\) does not divide \(q\). For \(N = 3\) the terms are the steps of the three-cylinder engine, which close.

Proof

We must show both cases: in the first all terms are \(1\), in the second the terms form a geometric sequence with ratio \(\rho \ne 1\). In the first case all terms are equal, in the second the chain closes.

  1. Let \(\rho = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi q/N}\). The terms are \(\rho^j\) (theorem 9.34, point 1).
  2. If \(N\) divides \(q\), then \(k = q/N\) is an integer and \(\rho = \cos 2\pi k + \mathrm{i}\sin 2\pi k = 1\) (lemma 8.11, point 4). All terms are \(1\), the sum is \(N\). All terms are equal.
  3. If \(N\) does not divide \(q\), then \(2\pi q/N\) is not an integer multiple of \(2\pi\), so \(\rho \ne 1\). The equation \(\cos x = 1\) namely holds only at \(x \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}\): the cosine is even and \(2\pi\)-periodic (lemma 8.11, point 4), so it is enough to look at \(x \in [0, \pi]\), where it decreases strictly from \(\cos 0 = 1\) (points 2 and 3). We multiply the sum by \(1 - \rho\): \((1 - \rho)\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1}\rho^j = 1 - \rho^N\), because the middle terms cancel. Closure is shown by the chain at \(N = 3\).
  4. Because \(\rho^N = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi q} = 1\), the right side is zero, and because \(1 - \rho \ne 0\), the sum is zero.

Source: The proof is not from the source.

Theorem 23.47 Orders under even firing (redi pri enakomernem vžiganju)

Let a four-stroke inline engine with \(N\) equal cylinders be as in theorem 23.40, with even firing (definition 23.45). Let the gas torque \(T_{\mathrm{g}}\) of cylinder \(1\) have complex coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{m}}\) (lemma 9.122) at the frequencies \(m\Omega/2\). Then: Under even firing the firing phases are equally spaced.

  1. For an integer \(n \ge 1\) we have \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = N\) if \(N\) divides \(2n\), otherwise \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). The same holds for the inertia torque of order \(n\) (corollary 23.44). At \(N = 4\) and \(n = 2\) the chain has equal steps, the sum is \(4\).
  2. The coefficient of the total gas torque of all cylinders at the frequency \(m\Omega/2\) is \(N c_{\mathrm{m}}\) if \(N\) divides \(m\), otherwise zero. The total gas torque therefore has only components of order \(m/2\) with \(m\) divisible by \(N\), that is of orders \(N/2, N, 3N/2, \dots\), and its mean is \(N\) times the mean of one cylinder. The gas torques of the cylinders are shifted by equal angles \(4\pi/N\).
Proof

We must show both points. In the first, \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}}\) and the sum is the root sum of the lemma, in the second the coefficient of a shifted function is the coefficient of the original multiplied by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\psi_{\mathrm{k}}/2}\). The firing phases are equally spaced.

  1. Because \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{k}} + 2\pi m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(n\) are integers, \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\psi_{\mathrm{k}}} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi nm_{\mathrm{k}}} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}}\).
  2. Under even firing \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}} = 4\pi j_{\mathrm{k}}/N\), where \(j_{\mathrm{k}}\) runs through the numbers \(0, \dots, N-1\) exactly once each. So \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi(2n)j/N}\), which by lemma 23.46 (\(q = 2n\)) equals \(N\) or \(0\). The same \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) appears in the inertia torque by corollary 23.44. The strokes follow at spacings of \(4\pi/N\).
  3. The gas torque of cylinder \(k\) is \(T_{\mathrm{g}}(t - t_{\mathrm{k}})\) with \(t_{\mathrm{k}} = \psi_{\mathrm{k}}/\Omega\). Its coefficient is \(\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}T_{\mathrm{g}}(t - t_{\mathrm{k}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\Omega t/2}\,\mathrm{d}t\) with \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 4\pi/\Omega\). By lemma 9.108 (points 1 and 3, shift and period) this is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\Omega t_{\mathrm{k}}/2}c_{\mathrm{m}} = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\psi_{\mathrm{k}}/2}c_{\mathrm{m}}\). Shifting the stroke by the time \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\) only multiplies the coefficient by a phase factor.
  4. The coefficient of the sum is the sum of the coefficients: \(c_{\mathrm{m}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\psi_{\mathrm{k}}/2} = c_{\mathrm{m}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi mj/N}\). By lemma 23.46 with \(q = -m\) this is \(N c_{\mathrm{m}}\) if \(N\) divides \(m\), otherwise zero. At \(m = 0\) \(N\) always divides \(m\), so the mean is \(N\) times larger.

Source: NORTON section 14.9 (PDF pp. 778-779) states the same for the forces: in a four-stroke inline engine with even firing all orders of the force are balanced except those whose number is a multiple of \(n/2\) (with his \(n\) for the number of cylinders). The proof and the notation for the gas and inertia torque are not from the source.

The theorem gives two consequences at once. Under even firing the orders of the force and of the inertia torque are limited to multiples of \(N/2\) (for even \(N\)) or \(N\) (for odd \(N\)), and the orders of the gas torque to multiples of \(N/2\) for every \(N\). For four cylinders these are the orders \(2, 4, \dots\) for all three quantities, for six \(3, 6, \dots\). For three cylinders the force has the orders \(3, 6, \dots\), and the gas torque the orders \(\tfrac32, 3, \tfrac92, \dots\). For four cylinders this is decided by the shaft. With four cylinders the strokes are equally spaced.

Corollary 23.48 Four cylinders under even firing (štirivaljnik pri enakomernem vžiganju)

In a four-stroke inline four-cylinder engine with equal cylinders and even firing, \(S_1 = 0\) and \(S_2 = 4\) for every arrangement of the cranks and every firing order. The secondary force has amplitude \(4A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\), for engine \(E\) \(2256{,}4\) N, and it cannot be removed by the angle between the cranks. With four cylinders the chain at \(n = 2\) has four equal steps.

Proof

We must show that for \(N = 4\) theorem 23.47 determines \(S_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every shaft.

  1. For \(N = 4\), \(N\) divides \(2n\) exactly when \(n\) is even.
  2. So \(S_1 = 0\) and \(S_2 = 4\) by point 1 of theorem 23.47. The firing order does not appear in this point. At \(n = 1\) the chain is closed, at \(n = 2\) open.
  3. By point 1 of theorem 23.40 the secondary force is \(4A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos(2\theta - \sigma_2)\) with \(\sigma_2 = \arg 4 = 0\), the amplitude is \(4\cdot 0{,}254025\cdot 2220{,}7 = 2256{,}4\) N.

Source: NORTON section 14.6 (PDF pp. 758, 763-766): in a four-stroke engine even firing and the balance of forces are in conflict, so the shaft \(0, 180, 180, 0\) degrees is the best compromise and is used in four-cylinder inline four-stroke engines in series production. The proof from the theorem is not from the source.

The shaft \(0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2\) therefore cancels the forces of both orders, but cannot make the firing even. In the lower part of the figure the strokes of cylinders \(3\) and \(4\) overlap.

Example 23.49 Uneven firing (near miss of even firing)

The four-cylinder engine \(E\) with cranks \(0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2\) and firing order \(1, 3, 4, 2\) has the firing angles \(\psi = (0, 450^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ)\) for cylinders \(1\) to \(4\): \(450^\circ \equiv 90^\circ\), as it must be. The intervals between the power strokes are \(180^\circ\), \(90^\circ\), \(180^\circ\) and \(270^\circ\), not all equal to \(180^\circ\) (figure 23.58, lower part). By the model the gas torque of one cylinder has at order \(1/2\) the amplitude \(161{,}8\) N m (section 23.5). For this shaft \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\psi_{\mathrm{k}}/2}\big| = 1{,}0824\), so the total gas torque has at order \(1/2\) the amplitude \(1{,}0824\cdot 161{,}8 = 175{,}1\) N m (num-07.out), and at orders \(3/2\) and \(5/2\) it has \(291{,}6\) and \(130{,}4\) N m. For the shaft \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) (upper part of the figure) the components of orders \(1/2\), \(1\), \(3/2\), \(5/2\) and \(3\) are zero, and of the orders up to \(4\) order \(2\) (\(316{,}7\) N m) and order \(4\) (\(57{,}7\) N m) remain. The mean is \(338{,}4\) N m for both, which is four times the mean of one cylinder (\(84{,}61\) N m).

We build figure 23.59 from the axis. The horizontal axis of the angle \(\theta\) shows one cycle from \(0\) to \(4\pi\). Above it we draw the order-\(1/2\) torque, a sinusoid of amplitude \(T_{\mathrm{m}} = 175.1\) N m that makes one oscillation per cycle. The flywheel turns it by the law of rotation into a speed fluctuation of amplitude \(T_{\mathrm{m}}/(Jq\Omega)\), which lags the torque by a quarter of an oscillation. At \(1500\) 1/min the relative amplitude is \(0.0085\), at idle \(0.0298\), so the curve is \(3.5\) times higher.

A torque of order \(q = 1/2\) with amplitude \(T_{\mathrm{m}}\) and a flywheel with moment of inertia \(J\) give by the law of rotation \(J\dot\omega = T_{\mathrm{m}}\cos(q\Omega t)\) a speed oscillation with amplitude \(T_{\mathrm{m}}/(Jq\Omega)\), so relatively \(T_{\mathrm{m}}/(Jq\Omega^2)\). For the flywheel \(J = 1{,}6721\) kg m² of section 23.5 (chosen for one cylinder, here only for comparison) and the same gas torque at both speeds the relative amplitude at \(1500\) 1/min equals \(175{,}1/(1{,}6721\cdot 0{,}5\cdot 157{,}08^2) = 0{,}0085\), and at idle \(800\) 1/min (\(\Omega = 83{,}78\) 1/s) \(0{,}0298\), which is \(3{,}5\) times more. The frequency of order \(1/2\) at idle is \(6{,}7\) Hz. Figure 23.59 shows both cases.

With four cylinders and even firing the gas torque therefore contains only the orders \(2, 4, \dots\), and under uneven firing also the orders \(1/2\) and \(3/2\) with low frequencies, which the flywheel damps poorly at low speed (the relative oscillation grows with \(1/\Omega^2\)). NORTON (PDF p. 760) says the same: at high speed the flywheel hides the unevenness, at low speed it does not, so designers prefer even firing to the cancellation of inertia forces. The uneven strokes are the lower part of figure 23.58.

From history

Frederick Lanchester in 1897 designed a horizontally opposed two-cylinder engine which, according to NORTON (PDF pp. 785-786), cancelled all harmonics of the inertia forces and moments. It had two counter-rotating crankshafts and six conrods, three to a crank pin. The reason by the same source: collinear opposed pistons cancel each other's accelerations, the weights on the cranks cancel the cranks, and the scissors action of several conrods cancels the higher harmonics of the motion. Around 1913 the same inventor developed a balancer with two counter-rotating shafts with weights (NORTON, PDF pp. 779, 783), which section 23.6 described and which turns at double the speed of the crankshaft. H. Nakamura improved this design, according to NORTON (PDF p. 783), so that the shafts also removed the second order of the inertia torque, but not the secondary shaking moment. The reason is in corollary 23.44: in the four-cylinder engine \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) the inertia torque of order \(2\) has the same condition \(S_2 \ne 0\) as the secondary force and for engine \(E\) equals \(229{,}8\) N m. NORTON (PDF p. 783) adds that the property that each balancer shaft receives no net moment about the transverse axis, which reduces the bending moments in the shafts and thus the bearing load and friction losses, is the main requirement of Nakamura's patent. A balancer makes sense in engines with a displacement above about \(2{,}0\) litres, where at speeds at which the frequency of the second harmonic meets a natural frequency of the body noise and vibration appear (NORTON, PDF p. 783).

Let us return to the four-cylinder engine with cranks \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\): the secondary force that follows from corollary 23.48 is not removed by the shaft, it is removed by a balancer at double speed. The same theorem on the addition of forces also holds for an engine in which the cylinders do not stand one behind the other along the shaft, but are inclined at an angle.

23.7.2 The V twin (dvovaljni V motor)

A V engine has two rows of cylinders, banks, at an angle \(\nu\). The letter V says the shape: the axes of the cylinders form the letter V. Here we treat the smallest case, the V twin with one crank pin (dvovaljni V motor z enim ročičnim čepom): both pistons are connected through conrods to the same crank pin, the axes of the cylinders lie in the plane \((x, y)\), each at angle \(\gamma = \nu/2\) from the bisector of the V. Idealization: both conrods are in the same plane \(z\), which a real engine achieves with the fork-and-blade arrangement (vilice in nož). More often the conrods stand side by side on a longer crank pin, so one bank is shifted along the shaft by the thickness of a conrod (NORTON, PDF p. 773). This offset causes a small moment about an axis perpendicular to the shaft, which we do not treat here. For the V twin with both cylinders on a common crank throw NORTON (PDF p. 769) too only adds the forces of the two cylinders as vectors. Multicylinder V engines (V6, V8, V12), made of two inline engines on the same shaft, we only mention, the source is NORTON section 14.7 (PDF pp. 766-782). In figure 23.60 the axes of the cylinders form the letter V.

We start figure 23.60 with the axes of the cylinders: axis \(1\) is above the bisector \(x\) by the angle \(\gamma\), axis \(2\) below it. On the pin comes one crank at angle \(\alpha\) from the bisector. From the axis of cylinder \(1\) it is away by \(\alpha - \gamma\), from the axis of cylinder \(2\) by \(\alpha + \gamma\) (the second stands on the other side of the bisector). Cylinder \(i\) thus acts by equation (23.35) with the angle \(\alpha \mp \gamma\) instead of \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\). We draw both forces from the bearing, where they act, along the axes of the cylinders, and the sum is their vector sum.

Corollary 23.50 V twin (dvovaljni V motor)

Let the V engine be as described, \(\gamma \in (0, \pi/2)\), equal cylinders with mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\), rotating masses balanced. Let \(\vek F^{(1)}\) be the sum of the primary and \(\vek F^{(2)}\) the sum of the secondary forces of both cylinders (vectors in the plane \((x, y)\)), as functions of the angle \(\alpha\) of the crank from the bisector: The cylinder forces are the forces along the axes, the sum of the primary forces is the curve in the middle of the figure.

\[\vek F^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\,\big(2\cos^2\gamma\cos\alpha,\; 2\sin^2\gamma\sin\alpha\big) , \tag{23.38}\]
\[\vek F^{(2)} = A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\,\big(2\cos\gamma\cos 2\gamma\cos 2\alpha,\; 2\sin\gamma\sin 2\gamma\sin 2\alpha\big) . \tag{23.39}\]
  1. At \(\nu = 90^\circ\) we have \(\vek F^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\) with constant magnitude \(2220{,}7\) N, along the crank. A counterweight of mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) at radius \(a\) opposite the crank removes it. The secondary force is \(A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(0, \sqrt2\sin 2\alpha)\): a force along the line perpendicular to the bisector, with amplitude \(797{,}8\) N. At \(90^\circ\) the primary curve is a circle, the secondary a line segment.
  2. At \(\nu = 60^\circ\) we have \(\vek F^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(1{,}5\cos\alpha, 0{,}5\sin\alpha)\), its magnitude is between \(0{,}5\) and \(1{,}5\) units of \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\) (at most \(3331{,}0\) N). No counterweight removes this force: the best one leaves \(0{,}5\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 1110{,}3\) N. The secondary force is a circle of radius \(0{,}866\,A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 488{,}5\) N, which turns at double speed \(2\Omega\). At \(60^\circ\) the primary curve is an ellipse, the secondary a circle.
Proof

We must show the equations and both points. We get the equations from the addition theorem, the points from the form of the equations, the best counterweight from an estimate of the magnitude. The directions of the cylinder axes enter the sum.

  1. The axes of the cylinders are in the directions \(\vek u_1 = (\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma)\) and \(\vek u_2 = (\cos\gamma, -\sin\gamma)\). The force of cylinder \(i\) is by expression (23.35) with the crank at angle \(\alpha - \gamma\) or \(\alpha + \gamma\) along \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\), the primary one is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos(\alpha \mp \gamma)\,\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\). The cylinder axes are tilted by \(\pm\gamma\) from the bisector.
  2. The component \(x\) of the sum of the primary forces is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\gamma\,[\cos(\alpha - \gamma) + \cos(\alpha + \gamma)] = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\gamma\cdot 2\cos\alpha\cos\gamma\) (addition theorem, corollary 8.16). The component \(y\) is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\sin\gamma\,[\cos(\alpha - \gamma) - \cos(\alpha + \gamma)] = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\sin\gamma\cdot 2\sin\alpha\sin\gamma\). This is equation (23.38).
  3. The secondary force: the same with \(A_2\) and the angles \(2\alpha \mp 2\gamma\). The component \(x\) is \(A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\gamma\,[\cos(2\alpha - 2\gamma) + \cos(2\alpha + 2\gamma)] = A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\cos\gamma\cdot 2\cos 2\alpha\cos 2\gamma\), the component \(y\) is \(A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\sin\gamma\cdot 2\sin 2\alpha\sin 2\gamma\). This is equation (23.39).
  4. At \(\gamma = 45^\circ\) we have \(2\cos^2\gamma = 2\sin^2\gamma = 1\), so \(\vek F^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\) with constant magnitude. The force of the mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) on the crank pin (radius \(a\), angle \(\alpha\), angular speed \(\Omega\)) on the frame is exactly \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\) (lemma 23.32), so a counterweight with the same mass opposite the crank removes it. At \(\gamma = 45^\circ\) we have \(\cos 2\gamma = 0\) and \(\sin 2\gamma = 1\), so \(\vek F^{(2)}\) has the component \(x\) equal to zero and the component \(y\) equal to \(A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\sqrt2\sin 2\alpha\). At \(90^\circ\) the sum is a circle of constant radius, along the crank.
  5. At \(\gamma = 30^\circ\) we have \(2\cos^2\gamma = 1{,}5\) and \(2\sin^2\gamma = 0{,}5\). A counterweight with mass \(c\,m_{\mathrm{B}}\) at radius \(a\) opposite the crank adds the force \(-c\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\), the remainder is \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\big((1{,}5 - c)\cos\alpha,\, (0{,}5 - c)\sin\alpha\big)\) with squared magnitude \((1{,}5 - c)^2\cos^2\alpha + (0{,}5 - c)^2\sin^2\alpha\). At every \(\alpha\) this is at most the larger of the two squares and it reaches it at \(\alpha = 0\) or \(\pi/2\), so the largest magnitude over a turn is \(\max(|1{,}5 - c|, |0{,}5 - c|)\). The difference \((1{,}5 - c) - (0{,}5 - c) = 1\), so at least one of the absolute values is at least \(0{,}5\). At \(c = 1\) both are \(0{,}5\), which is the least. Without a counterweight (\(c = 0\)) the largest magnitude is \(1{,}5\). A counterweight at another angle does not help. The force \((1{,}5\cos\alpha, 0{,}5\sin\alpha)\) is the sum of \((\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\), which turns with the crank, and \(0{,}5\,(\cos\alpha, -\sin\alpha)\), which turns against it (as in equation (23.30)). Every counterweight turns with the crank and changes only the first part. At the angle \(\alpha\) where both parts are parallel and point the same way, the magnitude of the sum is the sum of the magnitudes, so at least \(0{,}5\). At \(60^\circ\) the sum is an ellipse with semi-axes \(1{.}5\) and \(0{.}5\).
  6. At \(\gamma = 30^\circ\) we have \(2\cos\gamma\cos 2\gamma = 2\cdot 0{,}866\cdot 0{,}5 = 0{,}866\) and \(2\sin\gamma\sin 2\gamma = 2\cdot 0{,}5\cdot 0{,}866 = 0{,}866\), so \(\vek F^{(2)} = 0{,}866\,A_2 m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2(\cos 2\alpha, \sin 2\alpha)\): a circle turning with \(2\Omega\). At \(60^\circ\) the sum is a circle turning at \(2\Omega\).

Source: NORTON section 14.9 (PDF p. 781) confirms point 1: in a V engine with \(90^\circ\) and one crank pin the primary force has constant magnitude and rotates, the weights on the shaft remove it, and the secondary one is planar and requires two balancer shafts at double speed. A remark on the same page says that all V twins except \(180^\circ\) have an unbalanced secondary force and most an unbalanced primary one. The equations, point 2 and the estimate in step 5 are not from the source.

For \(\nu = 60^\circ\) the primary force in figure 23.60, in the middle part, is an ellipse with semi-axes \(1{,}5\) and \(0{,}5\), and a counterweight, which turns on a circle, cannot cancel the ellipse: at \(c = 1\) the difference \(0{,}5\) remains at the ends of the ellipse. At \(90^\circ\) the curve is a circle and the counterweight cancels it. The secondary force is at \(90^\circ\) a segment on the axis \(y\), and at \(60^\circ\) a circle. In both cases it remains, and a balancer at double speed from section 23.6 removes it: for the segment two counter-rotating masses, for the circle a single mass.

The section has shown that the conditions for the balance of an inline engine are determined by a sum of unit vectors, and that for a four-stroke engine with even firing this sum is already determined by the number of cylinders. In section 23.8 we look at how much of the remaining forces reaches the foundation, and in section 23.9 at how the crankshaft vibrates in torsion because of the remaining torque. A number of engines remain open, which the section only mentions: opposed engines (NORTON section 14.8, PDF p. 778), V engines with more cylinders (section 14.7, PDF pp. 766-782) and a balancer for the secondary force (PDF pp. 779-785).
