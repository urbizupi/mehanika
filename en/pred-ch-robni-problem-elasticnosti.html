Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

Chapter 11Lecture: The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

1 One problem, one solution

Take a ruler and press one end of it firmly against the edge of a table. We draw the ruler as a long rectangle. We draw the place where we hold it as a hatched wall. With a finger of the other hand we push the free end down. What do we choose here, and what does the ruler choose?

At the wall we choose the position: the ruler does not move there. How much force we feel in the hand we do not choose, the ruler tells us. At the finger it is the other way round. We choose how hard we push, how far the end sags the ruler tells us. This pair, the position at one place and the force at another, is the key to this whole part.

Now a real body. We draw the wall of a steel tube, cut along its length. We look at it also from the front, as a ring. Through the middle runs the axis of the tube with a point \(O\) on it. The inner radius is \(a = 40\) mm, the outer \(b = 60\) mm. We put oil at a pressure of 30 MPa into the bore. The oil presses on the bore wall everywhere perpendicular to it and everywhere equally. The steel has

\[E = 196\ \text{GPa}, \qquad \nu = 0{,}3 .\]

Every point of the wall therefore moves a little, the steel stretches, and stresses arise in it. We say the wall has a field (polje): each of its points has its own displacement, its own strain and its own stress. The question is twofold. What must hold in the wall for the field to be the right one? And could the same data give two different fields?

The answer in one sentence, on the tube:

The field in the wall is fixed by three kinds of equations inside and by what we prescribe on the surface. Such a field is unique, so every field that passes all the checks is the right one.

This sentence uses three claims. That the equations are exactly these, that we also need the surface, and that the solution is unique. Each is the next "why".

1.1 Why exactly these equations?

How many numbers must we know at one point of the wall to know everything about it, and how many equations do we have for them? Let us count. Three things happen at every point of the wall, and the top row of the scheme draws them one after another. First, the point moves. The displacement has three numbers, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\), on the left of the scheme. Second, two neighbouring points move differently, so the steel between them stretches or shears. Six numbers \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) say this, the stretches in three directions and the changes of the three right angles between them. The first arrow computes them from the displacement, six equations. Third, stretched steel resists. How strongly has been measured: the stress is proportional to the strain, this is Hooke's law on the second arrow, again six equations for six stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

What is still missing is why the wall does not keep moving. Cut out, in the mind, a small piece of the wall. It is at rest, so by Newton's law the sum of the forces on it is zero, in each of the three directions. These are the three equilibrium equations. Let us count:

\[\text{unknowns: } 3 + 6 + 6 = 15 , \qquad \text{equations: } 6 + 6 + 3 = 15 .\]

What does the count tell us? Only that there are not, at first sight, too few or too many equations. That the solution is unique does not follow from counting, that needs the next two steps. If we put all three steps of the top row into equilibrium, three equations for the three displacements are left. The bottom row is a second route to the same problem, with the stresses as unknowns, which comes in a later part.

On the first arrow we assumed something: that displacements and strains are small. Why may we here? The stresses in the wall are of the same order as the pressure 30 MPa, so the strain is of the order

\[\frac{30\ \text{MPa}}{196\,000\ \text{MPa}} \approx 1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

A product of two such numbers is of the order \(10^{-8}\), and we drop these products next to terms of the order \(10^{-4}\). The error is therefore of the order of one ten-thousandth of the result. So every equation on the scheme is a sum of first powers of the unknowns, linear, and solutions may be added and subtracted. Uniqueness needs this as its first step. When displacements are not small, for example in rubber or in a thin ruler bent far, the equations are nonlinear and we may not take this step.

1.2 Why are the equations alone not enough?

The same equations hold for every steel tube, every steel beam, every steel hook. What makes this tube this tube? How it is held and loaded, and that happens on the surface. That is why we call it a boundary value problem (robni problem): the equations hold inside, the conditions are given on the boundary of the body, its surface.

We draw a body with surface \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). If on the whole surface we prescribe forces, this is a traction problem. Our tube is such a problem, it is held nowhere and the oil presses. We can clamp the same body over its whole surface, this is a displacement problem. Or we clamp it only in part: on the left face \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we prescribe the displacement, on the rest \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the forces. This is a mixed problem, and our ruler is exactly one.

Why never both at the same place? Recall the ruler. At the wall we prescribed the position, the force and moment we feel are the answer. If we also prescribed the force there, we would be choosing something the ruler chooses, and in general the two data would contradict each other.

A field is therefore a solution when it passes four checks:

  1. the displacements give the strains (first arrow),
  2. the strains give the stresses by Hooke's law (second arrow),
  3. the stresses are in equilibrium at every interior point,
  4. on the surface what is prescribed holds: the displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the force per unit area on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\).

The first three are inside, the fourth on the boundary. In the book: 11.1.

1.3 May we prescribe the forces freely?

Let us try. We push the tube at one end and nothing holds it at the other. Will it stay at rest? No, it starts to move, and there is no field at rest. For every solution the sum of all external forces must be zero, and so must the sum of their moments. Why does this follow from the equations? Cut the body into small pieces. Each is at rest, so the sum of the forces on it is zero. Add these zeros over all pieces. On every inner face the left piece pushes the right one and the right one the left, equally hard in the opposite direction, and the pair cancels. Only the forces on the surface are left, and their sum is zero. In the book: 11.2.

Let us check this on the tube. The pressure presses on the bore equally from all sides, the forces on opposite sides cancel, the sum is zero. With open ends that is all. Now we close the same wall at both ends with plugs. The oil pushes each plug outwards with the force

\[p\,\pi a^2 = 30\ \text{MPa} \cdot \pi \cdot (40\ \text{mm})^2 = 150{,}8\ \text{kN} ,\]

which the plug hands over to the tube. The plug pulls the end ring of the tube of area \(\pi(b^2 - a^2) = 6283\ \text{mm}^2\), which gives 24 MPa. The lower ring pulls down, the upper up, along the axis \(z\), equally hard, and the sum is zero again.

Here we assumed something: that the force of the plug spreads evenly over the ring. Why may we? Only the resultant 150,8 kN is certain, because the plug is at rest. The distribution is an idealisation. When does it not hold? Right at the end, where the plug is screwed or welded on. What does it cost? The field changes only near the ends, and how near the third part of this chapter tells.

With the ruler the data on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) balance nothing by themselves. The force 500 N at the end on the arm 1 m stays unbalanced. So the wall must give a force of 500 N upwards and a moment \(500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 500\) N m. We did not prescribe these, the solution gives them itself.

1.4 Why is the solution unique?

Could two engineers compute two different fields for the same tube and the same pressure, both passing all the checks? Suppose they could. We draw the ring before loading, grey dashed, and the pressure in the bore. The first field widens the ring, we draw it orange. The second field, for the same ring and the same pressure, is to give a different displacement. We subtract them. Because every equation is a sum of first powers, the data subtract too: the pressure minus the same pressure is zero. The difference is therefore a field of the same body on which nothing acts, no body force and no force on the surface. In the book: 11.3.

Second step. The energy a body stores equals the work of the external forces that stretched it (chapter 4). On the difference no force acts, no force has done work, so the stored energy is zero.

Third step. A spring stores energy when we stretch it and also when we compress it. The energy of strained material is positive however we strain it. If the energy is zero, nothing is strained, all strains are zero. Here too is an assumption. Why does it hold for steel? For an isotropic material positive energy is equivalent to the bulk modulus and the shear modulus being positive (3.64). For our steel

\[K = \frac{E}{3(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{196\ \text{GPa}}{3 \cdot 0{,}4} \approx 163\ \text{GPa} > 0 , \qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} = \frac{196\ \text{GPa}}{2{,}6} \approx 75\ \text{GPa} > 0 .\]

When does it not hold? When the steel passes the yield stress and does not return after unloading. Then the equations of this chapter no longer hold and the step from zero energy to zero strain falls, and uniqueness with it.

What is left if there is no strain? Only a motion of the body as a whole. In the plane \(xy\) we draw the first solution with centre \(O\) and the second with centre \(O'\), shifted and turned. The shape is the same, the point \(P\) goes to the point \(P'\), by the difference of the displacements \(\vek w(P)\). The arrows are not equally long, because the ring has also turned. Our tube has no supports, so its stresses and strains are unique, its displacements only up to such a shift and turn. With the ruler the wall prevents the shift and the turn, and the difference is zero. In the book: 11.4.

The theorem says there cannot be two solutions, not that there is one at all. The book does not prove existence. The sources say that a solution exists when the forces are in equilibrium or when the supports prevent the motion of the whole body, like the wall of the ruler.

1.5 What does this give us?

Because the solution is unique, it does not matter how we find the field. We may guess it. We prescribe the form of the stresses, then we check: equilibrium, the conditions on the surface and compatibility, that is, whether the strains come from any displacement at all. This last check replaces our first arrow, because we started from stresses, not from displacements. Then we find the displacements and remove the motion of the whole body. If a check fails, we go back and guess differently. A field that passes everything is the right one, because there is no other. This is how we will solve the hanging bar, the tube and the plate with a hole. In the book: 11.4.

1.6 From the ground back to the top

At the bottom stood statements that need no explanation:

  1. counting: fifteen unknowns, fifteen equations. It adds that the problem is neither under- nor overdetermined,
  2. a piece at rest has zero net force (Newton),
  3. the hand that holds chooses the position and feels the force, the hand that pushes chooses the force and sees the motion. It adds why at every place we prescribe exactly one of the two,
  4. strained material stores energy that is never negative, like a spring,
  5. equations of first powers add, so two fields may be subtracted.

From them we build back up. Moving, stretching, resisting and balance give fifteen equations. The surface says which body it is. When nothing holds the body, the data must be balanced. Linearity and positive energy leave room for one field only. So the tube with open ends and the tube with plugs are two different problems, each with exactly one stress field.

1.7 By energy

This is the few-minute explanation. We subtract two fields, the difference has no load, so it has no energy and no strain. This view adds the reason for uniqueness in one line.

1.8 Historically

The proof that the equations of equilibrium of an elastic body have no more than one solution was first published by Kirchhoff in 1858. Neumann repeated it in his lectures just as we have drawn it: he took two solutions and found that the difference of their displacements satisfies the equations with no body force and no surface force. This view adds that our picture of subtraction is as old as the theorem.

2 The thick tube: equilibrium gives the mean, deformation gives the distribution

Think of a wooden barrel. The staves, the narrow boards it is made of, are held together by a few iron hoops. The liquid in the barrel pushes the staves outward, and the hoop is what it would have to tear, along its circumference. And a tube, which has no hoops? Its wall is its own hoop.

We draw the wall of a steel tube from the front, around the axis \(O\). The inner radius is 40 mm, the outer 60 mm, so the wall is 20 mm thick, half the inner radius. In the bore there is oil at a pressure of 30 MPa, which pushes the wall outward. The stress that pulls the wall along the circumference, as in a hoop, is therefore called the hoop stress (obodna napetost). Is the circumference equally stressed everywhere across the wall? And if not, where is it stressed most, and by how much?

First we draw what we will measure. The horizontal axis runs from the bore at 40 mm to the outer surface at 60 mm, the vertical one is in MPa. Each point on the horizontal axis is one ring of the wall. What would you guess the hoop stress is? If it were the same everywhere, it would be the horizontal line at 60 MPa, which we draw dashed. The true hoop stress is this blackwhite curve. At the bore it is 78 MPa, in the middle of the wall 58,56 MPa and at the outer surface only 48 MPa. Below it we draw the radial stress (radialna napetost), which acts along the radius, the blue curve. At the bore it is \(-30\) MPa, because the oil presses on the wall there, in the middle \(-10{,}56\) MPa and at the free outer surface zero.

This is the claim of today's hour: the half tube in equilibrium tells us only that the mean hoop stress is 60 MPa. The true distribution, from 78 MPa at the bore down to 48 MPa outside, is fixed by the fact that all rings of the wall must widen together.

2.1 Why is the mean 60 MPa?

What can we learn about the hoop stress without knowing anything about the steel? Let us try statics alone. We cut the tube with a plane through its axis.

We keep the upper half of the tube, one millimetre long. We draw the axis of the tube \(O\), and from it the inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and the outer radius \(b = 60\) mm. Everything we cut away we now replace by forces, as in every free-body diagram. First the oil. The oil pushes the wall of the bore outward, at every point perpendicular to the wall. What happens to these forces when we add them up? The horizontal part of the force on the left side of the bore and the horizontal part on the right side are equal and opposite, so they cancel. Only the vertical part is left. Added up it gives the resultant \(2pa\) upward, the pressure times the width of the bore. On the board:

\[2\,p\,a = 2 \cdot 30\ \text{MPa} \cdot 40\ \text{mm} = 2400\ \text{N per mm of length} .\]

What holds the half so that it does not fly upward? The lower half. On both cuts it pulls it down with the hoop stress. Each cut is \(b - a = 20\) mm wide. The half is at rest, so the forces add up to zero. That is Newton's law for a body at rest, and that is the ground we stand on. Each cut therefore carries half, \(p\,a = 1200\) N per mm, spread over 20 mm of wall:

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = p\,a , \qquad \text{mean} = \frac{p\,a}{b - a} = \frac{30 \cdot 40}{20} = 60\ \text{MPa} .\]

In the book: 11.7.1, equation (11.35).

We assumed something here, so let us say it out loud. On the front and back cut of the piece we took the forces as equal and opposite, because we assumed that the stresses do not change along the axis. Why is that right here? The tube is the same along its whole length and the oil presses equally everywhere, and the solution itself will confirm it. When does it fail? Near the end of the tube, where there is a plug or a flange. How far the influence of the end reaches is what Saint-Venant's principle tells us in the next part. What does it give us? The axial forces of the piece cancel, and only the pressure and the hoop stresses are left in the equilibrium.

2.2 Why does equilibrium not give the distribution?

Statics gave us a single number, the area under the curve. Is that enough to know the curve? Let us draw.

Again we draw the axis of the radius from 40 to 60 mm and the axis of the hoop stress. Statics asks for one thing only: the area under the curve must be \(p\,a = 1200\). The first curve that does this is the horizontal line at 60 MPa. The second is a straight line from 78 MPa at the bore to 42 MPa outside. It has the same area under it, because what it gains on the left it loses on the right. The third is the true curve, from 78 to 48 MPa. It too crosses the 60 MPa line somewhere inside the wall. The surplus above 60 MPa near the bore is exactly as large as the deficit below 60 MPa outside. Statics cannot choose among these three, nor among infinitely many others. It is one equation for a whole function. In the book: 11.7.1, last paragraph.

Here hides an assumption that we use in every thin-tube calculation. The formula \(p\,a/t\) assumes that the stress is the same everywhere across the wall, that is the horizontal line. Why is that right for a thin wall? When the thickness \(t\) is small compared with the radius \(a\), all rings are almost equally far from the axis and there is no room to draw a difference between them. When does it fail? For our tube \(t/a = 0{,}5\). What does it cost? The horizontal line at 60 MPa misses the peak of 78 MPa by 18 MPa, that is by 23 %, and on the unsafe side.

2.3 What then chooses the true curve?

What does statics not see? That the wall is one piece, which must widen in one piece, without gaps. Let us picture the wall as thin rings, one inside the other, like the growth rings of a tree trunk.

We draw one ring of radius \(r\). The oil pushes it outward and the ring widens. Each of its points moves outward by \(u\). How much does it stretch along the circumference? Take two radii at a small angle \(\Delta\theta\). Between them the arc was \(r\,\Delta\theta\) long. After widening the arc is \((r + u)\,\Delta\theta\) long. The arc got longer by \(u\,\Delta\theta\), so relatively by

\[\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \frac{u\,\Delta\theta}{r\,\Delta\theta} = \frac{u}{r} .\]

What does this stand on? On the circumference of a circle being \(2\pi r\), and an arc being a part of it. Nothing more. In the book: 11.6.2.

What does this mean for a thick wall? The same displacement \(u\) on a ring with a larger radius \(r\) means a smaller hoop strain. Let us see it on our tube. On the first figure we add the two surfaces after loading, in orange and greatly enlarged. The book computes the displacements with open ends. The bore moves outward by 17,755 µm, the outer surface only by 14,694 µm. We divide each by its radius. At the bore the hoop strain is \(4{,}44\cdot10^{-4}\), outside \(2{,}45\cdot10^{-4}\). The ring at the bore stretches around the axis 1,8 times as much as the outer one, so a larger stress holds it. In the book: 11.7.2.

But why are the rings not independent? Because neighbouring rings touch and may neither separate nor overlap. By as much as the inner edge of a ring moves more or less than its outer edge, the ring gets thinner or thicker. This is the radial strain, the change of the displacement from ring to ring, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}r\). In our tube the bore moves a good 3 µm more than the outer surface, so the wall gets thinner. Both strains, \(u/r\) and \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}r\), come from one single function \(u(r)\). So they are not free, and so the stresses are not free either.

Here is the second assumption. We assumed that each point moves only radially and that \(u\) depends only on \(r\), not on the angle and not on the height. Why is that right? The tube and the pressure are the same at every angle, and once a field meets all the requirements, by the uniqueness theorem of the previous part it is the field, there is no other (11.4). When does it fail? For a bent or eccentric tube, for a point load and near the ends. What does it give us? There are no shear stresses, and only two unknown constants are left.

2.4 Why does the inner ring carry more?

Deformation ties the two strains. What ties the stresses? The equilibrium of a small piece of a ring.

From a ring we cut out a small piece. Through its middle we draw the middle radius. The piece lies between the radius \(r\) and the radius \(r + \Delta r\), between two radii at the angle \(\Delta\theta\). Now we replace the surroundings by forces. On the inner face the radial stress acts, on the outer face too, only the outer face is larger. On both side faces the hoop stress pulls. Because the side faces are not parallel, each hoop force has a small component toward the axis. It is the same as the hoop of a barrel squeezing the staves inward. The piece is at rest, so the difference of the radial forces on the outer and inner face must balance these two components. That is how \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are tied. In the book: 11.6.4.

Now we have all three: the equilibrium of the piece ties the stresses, one function \(u(r)\) ties the strains, Hooke's law ties stresses to strains. What is left? Only two shapes, a constant and \(1/r^2\):

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - \frac{B}{r^2} , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{r^2} .\]

The idea of the proof is the guess and check of the first part: we guess a radial displacement \(u(r)\) and a uniform stretch along the axis, \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and check all requirements of the boundary value problem. In the book: 11.28.

The third assumption is Hooke's law. Why is it right? Steel below its yield stress behaves linearly elastically. When does it fail? When the equivalent stress somewhere reaches the yield stress, and that happens first at the bore, where the hoop stress is largest. What does it give us? The stresses are proportional to the pressure. In the book: 11.7.5.

2.5 Where do 24 and 86 400 come from?

There are two constants, \(A\) and \(B\). Where do two data for them come from? From the two surfaces of the wall. Let us draw the graph once more, and this time start from the edge.

We draw the axis of the radius and the axis of the stress. First the edge. On the outer surface there is nothing, so the radial stress there is zero. In the bore the oil pushes the wall and the wall pushes the oil back with the same pressure, so the radial stress there is \(-30\) MPa. The sign is minus because the wall there is not pulled but squeezed. Two equations for two unknowns on the board:

\[A - \frac{B}{40^2} = -30 , \qquad A - \frac{B}{60^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = 24\ \text{MPa} , \quad B = 86\,400\ \text{MPa mm}^2 .\]

Between them runs the blue radial curve. The hoop curve lies \(B/r^2\) above the line \(A = 24\) MPa, the radial one just as much below it. Let us read it: 78 MPa at 40 mm, 58,56 MPa at 50 mm, 48 MPa at 60 mm, and on the radial one \(-10{,}56\) MPa at 50 mm. In the book: equations (11.37) and (11.39).

One assumption is left, the ends of the tube. The book prescribes a uniform axial stress \(S\) on the ends and looks at three cases: open ends, zero, plane strain, 14,4 MPa, closed ends, 24 MPa. What does the choice cost? \(S\) changes only the axial stress and the displacements. It does not touch the hoop and the radial curves.

2.6 Back up

Now we go from the ground back to the top, in four steps:

  1. The half tube is at rest, so the area under the hoop curve is given and the mean is 60 MPa.
  2. The circumference of a circle is \(2\pi r\), so the same displacement stretches the inner ring more, and neighbours touch, so both strains come from one \(u(r)\).
  3. The piece of a ring is at rest, so the two stresses are tied, and with Hooke's law \(A \pm B/r^2\) is left.
  4. The edge gives \(A\) and \(B\), and with them the curve from 78 MPa down to 48 MPa, which has the same area under it as the dashed mean.

2.7 By numbers

One more view of the same curve, this time by the book's numbers:

\(r\) (mm) 40 45 50 55 60
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) (MPa) \(-30\) \(-18{,}67\) \(-10{,}56\) \(-4{,}56\) 0
\(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (MPa) 78 66,67 58,56 52,56 48
sum (MPa) 48 48 48 48 48

What does the bottom row tell us? The sum of the two stresses is the same on every radius, \(2A = 48\) MPa. That is why the two curves are mirror images about the line at 24 MPa. What the hoop stress gains toward the bore, the radial stress loses. This view adds the mirror symmetry, which one curve alone would not let us guess. In the book: 11.7.2, the table.

2.8 How good is the thin-wall formula?

An engineer would first compute the tube with the thin-wall formula. How good would that be? The formula with the inner radius gives 60 MPa, and that is exactly the mean for any thickness, because it comes from the equilibrium of the half. The formula with the mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\) mm gives 75 MPa, only 3,8 % below the peak of 78 MPa. The ratio is

\[\frac{p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/t}{\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)} = 1 - \frac{(b - a)^2}{2\,(a^2 + b^2)} = 1 - \frac{400}{10\,400} = 0{,}9615 .\]

The error is quadratic in \(t/a\). At \(t/a = 0{,}1\) it is only 0,23 %, which is enough for design. For our tube, \(t/a = 0{,}5\), we need the true curve, all the more because the thin-wall formula neglects the radial stress, which here reaches \(-30\) MPa. This view adds the limit up to which an engineer may forget the curve. In the book: 11.7.3.

What follows from all this? Because the hoop and the radial stress are largest in size at the bore, the steel starts to yield there. In the book: 11.7.5, and how a shrink fit of two tubes makes use of it, 11.7.6.

3 A hole triples the stress, but only near itself

Think of a stream flowing past a pillar. The water that would flow through the pillar has to go around it, so it flows faster next to the pillar. Far behind the pillar the stream is as before. This is only an image, not the physics of the plate. Does something similar happen to the force in a plate?

Let us draw a piece of a steel plate and the axes, the \(x\) axis along the pull. In the middle we drill a hole of radius \(a = 10\) mm. We clamp the plate in the jaws of a machine and pull it with the stress \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa. Without the hole the stress would be 50 MPa everywhere. What does the edge of the hole feel now? The hoop stress (obodna napetost) is the stress along the edge, along the circumference of the hole. The orange curve shows it as an offset from the edge. At the sides of the hole it is 150 MPa, three times as much as far away. At the front and back it is \(-50\) MPa, compression.

The claim in one sentence, on the orange curve:

The hole adds to the uniform field a disturbance that is large at the edge, \(3\sigma_0\) at the sides and \(-\sigma_0\) at the front, and falls off like \((a/r)^2\). The same holds for every change of the distribution of a load with the same resultant: it is felt only nearby.

This is the thread of the whole chapter, this time on a plate. A cut and equilibrium give the sum. Deformation gives the distribution. A distribution with the same sum matters only near the place where it acts. Each part of this claim is the next "why".

3.1 Why does the stress grow next to the hole?

What happens to the force that the material we drilled out used to carry? We do the same as with the tube. We cut the plate in our minds and look at one half. Let us draw the right half of the plate. The cut runs along the \(y\) axis, through the centre of the hole. On the cut is half of the hole of radius \(a\). On the right edge the machine pulls with \(\sigma_0\).

What did we remove? The left half. We have to replace it by the forces with which it pulled on the right half. Without the hole this would be the uniform stress \(\sigma_0\) along the whole cut. The half is at rest, so the forces on it sum to zero, and the left half pulls exactly as hard as the machine.

Now the hole. On the part of the cut where the hole is there is no material, and there is no force. Without the hole the force

\[2a\,\sigma_0 = 20\ \text{mm} \cdot 50\ \text{MPa} = 1000\ \text{N}\]

per millimetre of thickness would go through this part. But the machine still pulls the same. Where does this force go? Equilibrium of the half demands that the material next to the hole carries it. So the true stress next to the hole is larger than \(\sigma_0\). The shaded excess on each side is exactly \(a\sigma_0\), together \(2a\sigma_0\), as much as is missing in the hole.

What does the cut tell us and what not? It tells us the sum, the area of the shaded part. It does not tell us the shape of the orange curve. The same area could be low and wide or high and narrow. With the tube it was the same: the cut gave the mean, not the curve. The numbers on the curve, \(3\sigma_0\) at the edge, \(1{,}0741\,\sigma_0\) at \(3a\) and \(1{,}0224\,\sigma_0\) at \(5a\), come only from the solution. In the book: 11.13.4.

3.2 Why exactly 3 and not 2?

The cut says that there is more than \(\sigma_0\) next to the hole. How much more, twice, three times? The shape is set by deformation, as with the tube. We build the solution from two pieces. The first is the plate without a hole, which we know. Let us draw the circle where the hole will be. The material inside the circle pulls its surroundings towards itself, most strongly at the front and back, not at all at the sides. When we drill the circle out, these forces are gone. The second piece is the disturbance. It puts exactly the opposite forces on the edge of the hole, so that the sum on the edge is zero, since the edge of the hole is free. Far away the disturbance vanishes, since there the plate is pulled as without the hole. The orange arrows show how fast: \(2\sigma_0\) at the edge, half a radius further only \(0{,}52\,\sigma_0\), a whole radius further \(0{,}22\,\sigma_0\). The sum of the two is the plate with the hole. We may add, because the equations are linear. Among the solutions of the equations that fall off with distance, three fit, each with its own constant. The free edge gives three conditions, and the three constants are fixed. In the book: 11.13.2, 11.57.

From the solution we read the hoop stress on the edge,

\[\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a, \theta) = \sigma_0\,(1 - 2\cos 2\theta) .\]

Let us read it on the orange curve. At \(\theta = 90^\circ\) we have \(\cos 180^\circ = -1\) and we get \(3\sigma_0 = 150\) MPa. At \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\cos 0 = 1\) and we get \(-\sigma_0 = -50\) MPa. In between, at \(\theta = 30^\circ\), the curve crosses the edge of the hole, and the stress is zero there. That it is exactly 3 comes only from the material around the hole having to stretch so that it fits together, without gaps or overlaps.

Here we assumed three things. Let us state them here, where they enter.

  • The plate is thin and plane. The stresses are averages through the thickness. Why we may: the plate is thin, free on both faces and loaded only in its plane. When not: when the plate is not thin, the formulas still give only the average and do not tell how the stress varies through the thickness. Effect: 150 MPa is the average through the thickness.
  • The material is linear elastic. Why we may: at \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa the edge has 150 MPa, below the yield stress 220 MPa. When not: above \(\sigma_0 = 73{,}33\) MPa the edge starts to yield and the factor 3 no longer holds.
  • Uniqueness is not proved. The uniqueness theorem holds for bounded bodies, and the plate in the calculation is infinite. So we build the field and check every condition, but we do not claim that it is the only one. In the book: 11.13.1.

3.3 Why is there compression at the front, although nobody presses the plate?

Nobody presses the plate, and yet the edge of the hole is squeezed at the front. Who squeezes it? Let us look at how the edge of the hole moves. From the displacements of the solution, (11.89), at \(r = a\) we get the radial displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the displacement along the edge \(u_\theta\), where for a thin plate \(E'\) is just the modulus \(E\):

\[u_{\mathrm{r}}(a, \theta) = \frac{\sigma_0\,a}{E'}\,(1 + 2\cos 2\theta) , \qquad u_\theta(a, \theta) = -\frac{2\,\sigma_0\,a}{E'}\,\sin 2\theta .\]

The first expression says where the edge goes. At the front and back, at \(\theta = 0\), it moves outward by \(3\sigma_0 a/E'\). At the sides, at \(\theta = 90^\circ\), it moves inward by \(\sigma_0 a/E'\). The circle gets longer along the pull and narrower across it, into an oval, which we draw orange and greatly enlarged. The second expression says that the points of the edge meanwhile slide along the edge, from the sides towards the front and the back point. At the sides they therefore move apart, the arc there is stretched and we have tension. At the front and back they crowd together. Let us count at the front, per unit length of arc. The outward displacement lengthens the arc by \(3\sigma_0/E'\), as for a ring of the tube, where the hoop strain was \(u/r\). The crowding shortens it by \(4\sigma_0/E'\). What is left is \(-\sigma_0/E'\): the arc shortens, and by Hooke's law there is the compression \(-\sigma_0\) there. So nobody presses from outside. The front edge is squeezed by the parts of the edge that slide towards it from both sides. In the book: (11.89) and the hoop strain in 11.6.2.

3.4 How far does the disturbance reach?

Up to where does the hole still disturb the plate? Let us draw the diagram. Horizontally the distance from the centre divided by the radius, vertically the stress divided by \(\sigma_0\). The dashed line at 1 is the plate without a hole. The upper curve is the stress on the \(y\) axis, at the side of the hole. It starts at 3 and falls quickly. The two dots on it are \(1{,}0741\) at \(3a\) and \(1{,}0224\) at \(5a\). At five radii the stress is therefore only \(2{,}2\) % above \(\sigma_0\). The orange curve is the stress along the pull on the \(x\) axis, in front of the hole. It starts at 0, because the edge of the hole is not loaded in the \(x\) direction there. The blue one is the transverse stress on the same axis, it starts at \(-1\), the compression of the previous figure.

How fast does it fall? Like \((a/r)^2\). Twice as far from the centre means a disturbance four times smaller. On the \(y\) axis the deviation is below 5 % from \(3{,}53\,a\) on, on the \(x\) axis from \(7{,}03\,a\) on, because there the leading term is five times larger.

Another assumption hides here. We calculated with an infinite plate. The real plate is a \(200 \times 200\) mm square with the hole in the middle, its edge is at \(10a\). How large an error do we make? The Kirsch field requires slightly different forces on the edge than the true ones. On the left and right edges they deviate from 50 MPa, on the top and bottom from zero, both magnified twenty times against the scale. The largest deviation is at the middle of the right edge, \(1{,}242\) MPa, that is \(2{,}5\) % of \(\sigma_0\). When does the infinite plate not hold? When the hole is near an edge or large compared with the plate. In the book: 11.13.6.

3.5 Why does this hold in general?

Is the hole a special case, or does every load behave like this? Let us look at what the hole brought in the first place. The disturbance had to put on the edge of the hole forces opposite to those of the uniform field. Let us add them around the circle. What pulls on one side pulls equally on the opposite side in the opposite direction, so the resultant is zero. The moment is zero too. Such a load is called self-equilibrated (samouravnotežena): it is in equilibrium by itself. And we saw that its field falls off with distance. In the book: 11.13.4.

The same happens at the end of a long strip of height \(2b\). On the end we put a load with zero resultant and zero moment. At the end it is large. Half a height further its largest value is only \(0{,}0547\) of that at the end, another half height further \(0{,}0205\). In the strip the disturbance falls off exponentially, at the hole like \((a/r)^2\). Why more slowly? Because at the hole the slowest decaying solutions are powers of \(r\), in the strip exponential functions.

This is Saint-Venant's principle. Two loads with the same resultant and the same moment are called statically equivalent (statično enakovredni). Their difference is self-equilibrated, like the load on the end of the strip. The principle says: far from the place of loading the fields of the two loads are approximately equal, the difference is felt only over a distance of the order of the size of the loaded part, in the strip within the first height of the section. Why the resultant and the moment? Because the equilibrium of the whole body sees only these two: a body at rest has zero sum of forces and zero sum of moments, and how the force is distributed it does not see. Careful: the principle is not a theorem. The book calculates it for the strip and checks it on numbers in other cases. Where we use it to justify an approximation, we say so. In the book: 11.48, 11.11.1.

Let us check on the cantilever. We draw the cantilever to scale, 1 m long. On the left it is clamped in a wall, at the free end a force of 500 N pushes it. The real wall prescribes displacements, and what stresses arise at the contact we do not know. But we know that the cantilever is at rest. So the true load of the wall has the same resultant and moment as the solution we calculated, 500 N and 500 N m. The difference is self-equilibrated.

Where is the difference felt? Take the slab at the wall, one section height long, and magnify it. On it are two distances \(b\). We choose an example, not a measurement: let the difference at the wall be as large as the largest bending stress, 93,75 MPa. At the distance \(b\) \(1{,}87\) MPa is left, at the distance \(2b\) \(0{,}056\) MPa. The difference lives in the first one to two heights, that is in the first 4 to 8 % of the length.

What did we assume? That the decaying solutions of the strip add up to every self-equilibrated load, that the end of the cantilever is like the end of an infinitely long strip, and that the cantilever and the wall are symmetric. Why we may: the cantilever is 25 heights long, its ends are far apart. When not: the book does not prove these three conditions, they are an assumption. Effect: for the least favourable shape of the difference \(7{,}5\) MPa instead of \(1{,}87\) MPa is left at the distance \(b\), but at \(2b\) only \(0{,}10\) MPa. In the book: 11.11.3.

3.6 From the ground back to the hole

Now we go up the same path. The half plate is at rest, the hole carries nothing, so the force \(2a\sigma_0\) that would go through the hole flows past it. Compatibility of the deformation gives it its shape, the peak \(3\sigma_0\). The disturbance is the response to a self-equilibrated load on the edge of the hole, so it falls off, at five radii it exceeds \(\sigma_0\) by only \(2{,}2\) %. And every self-equilibrated change of a load is felt only nearby. That is why with the tube we could forget how the plug holds the end of the tube, and why beam theory calculates with resultants. Let us look once more at the orange curve. It hugs the hole, and far from it the plate does not know about the hole.

3.7 The designer's view

A designer wants one number. The ratio of the largest stress to the stress without the hole is called the stress concentration factor (faktor koncentracije napetosti), \(K_{\mathrm{t}}\). For a pull in one direction the arrows pull only horizontally and the orange curve has \(3\sigma_0\) at the ends of the vertical diameter, so \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 3\). What does this mean for steel with the yield stress 220 MPa? The edge of the hole starts to yield when \(3\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa, that is already at \(\sigma_0 = 73{,}33\) MPa, a third of what the plate without the hole carries.

Is a pull in the other direction as well worse? No. For a pull in both directions the arrows pull horizontally and vertically, and the orange curve is a circle, \(2\sigma_0\) everywhere. The pull in the \(y\) direction lowers the stress at the sides of the hole, because there it gives compression. In shear, however, the pull in the \(x\) direction and the compression in the \(y\) direction act in the same sense at the sides, and the orange curve reaches \(4\sigma_0\). We get all three from the Kirsch solution by adding, because the equations are linear. This view adds one number for design and the warning that it depends on the kind of loading. In the book: 11.13.5.
