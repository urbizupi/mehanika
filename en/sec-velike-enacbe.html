Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.6 Large systems of equations and numerical error (veliki sistemi enačb in številčna napaka)

The mesh is chosen, the elements are good, and one computation remains: the system \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\) whose matrix has a thousand or a million rows. This section treats the third of the three errors of definition 17.2, the numerical error (številčna napaka). The computer stores every number with about \(16\) significant digits (double precision, as in chapter 6) and every operation rounds, by a relative amount of about \(10^{-16}\). Reducing the modelling and the discretization error does not reduce this error, and may even increase it. A finer mesh reduces the discretization error (section 17.3) but raises the order \(n\) of the system and with it the cost of the computation. COOK (PDF p. 318, read) says that great mesh refinement, meant to decrease the discretization error, may increase the numerical error to such an extent that the results become less accurate rather than more. We ask three questions. What does one system cost, and what does each further system with the same matrix cost? How many digits do we lose for the bar of chapter 7 as the mesh is refined? And when does a large condition number really spoil the solution, and when does it not?

17.6.1 One factorization, many right-hand sides

Chapter 7 (section 7.8) showed that a banded matrix with half-bandwidth \(b\) costs about \(n b^2/2\) multiplications in elimination instead of \(n^3/6\) (lemma 7.44), and that renumbering the unknowns changes the band but not the solution (lemma 7.46). We do not repeat this, figure 7.32 shows the difference. Take the same example: a mesh of \(50 \times 100\) nodes with two unknowns per node, so \(n = 10^4\) and \(b = 100\), and the cost of the factorization is \(n b^2/2 = 5 \cdot 10^7\) multiplications. The question that practice asks is not answered there. We load the same structure in several ways (own weight, wind from the left, wind from the right), and in a vibration computation by the trapezoidal rule (definition 10.63) we solve a system at every time step, and for a given time step its matrix does not depend on the step (chapter 10). Does every new right-hand side cost another \(5 \cdot 10^7\) multiplications?

Lemma 17.28 Cost of one further right-hand side (cena ene nadaljnje desne strani)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\) with half-bandwidth \(b\) (definition 7.40) and \(\mat K = \mat L\,\mat D\,\mat L^\T\) its factorization from lemma 7.47. Then:

  1. \(L_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for \(i - j > b\).
  2. For every column \(\vek f\) with \(n\) entries we solve \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\) by a forward pass, a division by the diagonal and a backward pass, and this costs at most \(2nb + n\) multiplications and divisions once the factorization is computed.
Proof

We must show that the factor \(\mat L\) has a band and that the solution takes three simple passes. Write \(\vek y = \mat D\,\mat L^\T\vek d\) and \(\vek z = \mat L^\T\vek d\).

  1. Column \(k\) of \(\mat L\) below the diagonal is the column of multipliers \(\vek g/K_{11}^{(k)}\) of the \(k\)-th elimination step (lemma 7.47). By lemma 7.42 the matrix \(\mat K^{(k)}\) has half-bandwidth at most \(b\), so in its first column \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(i > b + 1\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 7.42). Column \(k\) of \(\mat L\) therefore has nonzero entries only in the first \(b\) places below the diagonal, which is item 1.
  2. We split the equation \(\mat L\,\mat D\,\mat L^\T\vek d = \vek f\) into three: \(\mat L\,\vek y = \vek f\), \(\mat D\,\vek z = \vek y\) and \(\mat L^\T\vek d = \vek z\).
  3. The first equation has a lower triangular matrix with ones on the diagonal: \(y_{\mathrm{i}} = f_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j} < \mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{ij}}\,y_{\mathrm{j}}\). By step 1 the sum has at most \(b\) terms, so at most \(b\) multiplications per row and \(n b\) in all, and no divisions.
  4. The second equation is \(n\) divisions, \(z_{\mathrm{i}} = y_{\mathrm{i}}/D_{\mathrm{ii}}\), with pivots \(D_{\mathrm{ii}} > 0\) (theorem 6.29).
  5. The third has an upper triangular matrix with ones: \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = z_{\mathrm{i}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j} > \mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{ji}}\,d_{\mathrm{j}}\), again at most \(b\) terms per row and \(n b\) multiplications.
  6. The sum of steps 3, 4 and 5 is at most \(2nb + n\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources.

On the example: \(n = 10^4\) and \(b = 100\) give \(2nb + n = 2{,}01 \cdot 10^6\) multiplications for one right-hand side, while the factorization costs \(5 \cdot 10^7\). The ratio is \(24{,}9\), in general \((n b^2/2)/(2nb) = b/4\). The factorization dominates until there are about \(b/4 = 25\) right-hand sides. For \(100\) right-hand sides, repeating the factorization costs \(5{,}2 \cdot 10^9\) multiplications, one factorization and \(100\) solutions \(2{,}5 \cdot 10^8\), which is \(20{,}7\) times less, and for \(1000\) time steps \(25\) times less (\(5{,}2 \cdot 10^{10}\) against \(2{,}06 \cdot 10^9\)). The numbers are in num-06.out. This is what chapter 10 promises when it says that the factorization is made once and only applied at every step.

This saving has a limit that we see on the same example. With a single right-hand side the factorization saves nothing, the ratio is \(1{,}00\). The matrix also changes when we change the time step \(\Delta t\), because it contains it, and then the factorization must be computed again. In a nonlinear computation the matrix changes at every correction, as we shall see in later sections of this chapter, so there the factorization is not free. Lemma 17.28 therefore holds for the same matrix and many right-hand sides, not for many matrices.

Band storage is not the only one. COOK (PDF pp. 57-58, read) stores, for a symmetric matrix, in one array all entries from the topmost nonzero entry of each column down to the diagonal (the skyline, obris) and all the zeros between them, because a direct solver turns the zeros under the skyline into nonzero entries (fill, zapolnitev). The number of stored entries is the profile of the matrix. For the same mesh one numbering gives a profile of \(21\), another \(39\), and programs renumber by themselves. The frontal method (čelni postopek) alternates assembly and solution, so that only the part of the matrix still needed by the front that travels across the structure is in memory at one time. Iterative solvers produce no fill, but converge more slowly as the condition number grows, and use preconditioning (predpogojevanje). The book does not treat these methods and names them only because a program offers a choice between them. For the bar we compute the condition number now.

17.6.2 How many digits we lose for the bar

The condition number \(\kappa(\mat K) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) (definition 6.31) tells by how much a relative error of the right-hand side can grow in the solution, and chapter 6 estimated this as \(|\delta\vek x|/|\vek x| \le \kappa\,|\delta\vek b|/|\vek b|\). For hat functions on a bar with \(n = 4\), \(8\) and \(16\) equal elements it gave \(\kappa = 29\), \(113\) and \(438\) (matrix (6.15)), about \(1{,}7\,n^2\). Why a square, and does it still hold at \(n = 1000\)?

Take the same bar: constant cross-section \(EA\), length \(L\), clamped at \(x = 0\), free at \(x = L\), divided into \(n\) equal elements of length \(h = L/n\). The unknowns are the nodal displacements \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the stiffness matrix is \(\mat K = (EA/h)\,\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) with the matrix

\[\mat T_{\mathrm{n}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & & & \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ & & & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{17.21}\]

in which the empty places are zeros. The number \(EA/h\) only multiplies all eigenvalues, so \(\kappa(\mat K) = \kappa(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}})\). Let us look at the condition number of \(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) in figure 17.19, on logarithmic scales, and next to it the shapes that the matrix at \(n = 10\) moves most and least.

First the axes: horizontally the number of elements \(n\), vertically the condition number \(\kappa\), both on logarithmic scales, because we care how many times it grows and not by how much. On the dashed straight line lies \((4n/\pi)^2\) and its slope is \(2\), which means that \(\kappa\) grows a hundredfold for ten times more elements. The points are the exact values of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) and lie on the line. Why slope \(2\)? On the right the left edge is the wall in which the bar is clamped. Above is the softest shape of the matrix \(\mat T_{10}\), a smooth curve: the differences \(v_{\mathrm{k}} - v_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) between neighbouring displacements are small, so the ratio \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} (v_{\mathrm{k}} - v_{\mathrm{k}-1})^2/\sum_{\mathrm{k}} v_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) of element stretches to displacements is small, and this ratio is the eigenvalue (since \(\vek v^\T\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} (v_{\mathrm{k}} - v_{\mathrm{k}-1})^2\) with \(v_0 = 0\)). Below is the stiffest shape, in which neighbouring nodes move oppositely and the stretch of every element is the largest possible for the given displacements. The smallest eigenvalue falls with \(n\), the largest stays below \(4\), and their ratio is the condition number. Let us look at it exactly.

Lemma 17.29 Condition number of the bar (število občutljivosti palice)

Let \(n \ge 1\), let \(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the matrix (17.21) of order \(n\) and \(\theta = \pi/\big(2(2n + 1)\big)\). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = 4\sin^2\!\big((2j - 1)\theta\big)\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n\) and an eigenvector \(\vek v^{(j)}\) with entries \(v^{(j)}_{\mathrm{k}} = \sin\!\big((2j - 1)\,k\,\pi/(2n + 1)\big)\).
  2. The condition number is
    \[\kappa(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}) = \frac{\sin^2\!\big((2n - 1)\theta\big)}{\sin^2\theta} = \frac{\cos^2\!\big(\pi/(2n + 1)\big)}{\sin^2\theta} . \tag{17.22}\]
  3. For every \(n\)
    \[\Big(\frac{2(2n + 1)}{\pi}\Big)^2 \cos^2\frac{\pi}{2n + 1} \le \kappa(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}) \le \Big(\frac{2(2n + 1)}{\pi}\Big)^2 \frac{1}{(1 - \theta/2)^2} . \tag{17.23}\]
Proof

We must show that the listed vectors solve the eigenvalue problem, that these are all the eigenvalues, and that equation (17.22) and the estimate (17.23) follow. Write \(\varphi = (2j - 1)\pi/(2n + 1)\), so that \(v_{\mathrm{k}} = \sin k\varphi\), and also \(v_0 = 0\) and \(v_{\mathrm{n}+1} = v_{\mathrm{n}}\).

  1. Row \(k\) of \(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}}\) for \(1 \le k \le n - 1\) gives \(-v_{\mathrm{k}-1} + 2v_{\mathrm{k}} - v_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) (at \(k = 1\) we have \(v_0 = 0\), which is the clamp). Row \(n\) gives \(-v_{\mathrm{n}-1} + v_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is the same form with \(v_{\mathrm{n}+1} = v_{\mathrm{n}}\) (free end: the last element has no right neighbour).
  2. By the addition formulas (corollary 8.15) \(\sin(k - 1)\varphi + \sin(k + 1)\varphi = 2\sin k\varphi\,\cos\varphi\), so \(-v_{\mathrm{k}-1} + 2v_{\mathrm{k}} - v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = (2 - 2\cos\varphi)\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\). Since by the same theorem \(\cos\varphi = 1 - 2\sin^2(\varphi/2)\), we have \(2 - 2\cos\varphi = 4\sin^2(\varphi/2)\).
  3. The condition \(v_{\mathrm{n}+1} = v_{\mathrm{n}}\) holds, because by the addition formulas \(\sin(n + 1)\varphi - \sin n\varphi = 2\cos\!\big((n + \tfrac12)\varphi\big)\sin\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\) and \((n + \tfrac12)\varphi = (2j - 1)\pi/2\), where the cosine is zero.
  4. The vector \(\vek v^{(j)}\) is not zero, since \(v_1 = \sin\varphi \ne 0\) for \(0 < \varphi < \pi\). So \(4\sin^2(\varphi/2) = 4\sin^2\!\big((2j - 1)\theta\big)\) is an eigenvalue. The angle \((2j - 1)\theta\) lies in \((0, \pi/2)\), where \(\sin^2\) is strictly increasing, so these \(n\) values are pairwise different. A matrix of order \(n\) has at most \(n\) eigenvalues, so these are all. This is item 1.
  5. By the increase the smallest eigenvalue is at \(j = 1\) and the largest at \(j = n\), and their ratio is the first part of equation (17.22). Since \((2n - 1)\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \tfrac{\pi}{2n + 1}\), we have \(\sin\!\big((2n - 1)\theta\big) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2n + 1}\) (addition formula), which is the second part.
  6. The estimate: by theorem 8.60 for \(\sin\) with the second derivative \(\sin\theta = \theta - \tfrac12\,\theta^2\sin\xi\) with \(0 < \xi < \theta\), so \(\theta - \theta^2/2 \le \sin\theta \le \theta\). The upper bound gives the lower bound in (17.23), since \(1/\theta^2 = (2(2n + 1)/\pi)^2\), the lower bound gives the upper one, since \(\cos^2 \le 1\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. That the condition number of an axially loaded bar grows with the square of the number of elements is stated by COOK (PDF p. 322, read, exponent \(2m/n = 2/1\)), and the lemma proves it for this bar.

The lemma says what the figure shows. The largest eigenvalue is below \(4\) and approaches it, the smallest is \(4\sin^2\theta\), about \(\pi^2/(2n + 1)^2\), so it falls as \(1/n^2\). The ratio is therefore about \((4n/\pi)^2\), and the slope \(2\) is the slope of this expression. For \(n = 4\), \(8\) and \(16\) the lemma gives \(29{,}28\), \(113{,}50\) and \(437{,}70\), exactly the \(29\), \(113\) and \(438\) of chapter 6. For larger \(n\):

\(n\) \(\kappa(\mat T_{\mathrm{n}})\) lower bound (17.23) upper bound \(\kappa/(4n/\pi)^2\) \(\log_{10}\kappa\)
\(10\) \(175{,}09\) \(174{,}76\) \(192{,}89\) \(1{,}080\) \(2{,}2\)
\(100\) \(16\,370{,}2\) \(16\,369{,}9\) \(16\,502{,}6\) \(1{,}010\) \(4{,}2\)

The last column is the estimate of the lost digits. COOK (PDF p. 321, read) says that with \(d\) digits in a computer number at least \(d_{\text{acc}} \ge d - \log_{10}\kappa\) digits are accurate, and that the sign \(\ge\) means that \(\log_{10}\kappa\) may overestimate the loss, which we shall see in the next section. For a bar of \(1000\) elements this is \(6{,}2\) of \(16\) digits, so at least \(16 - 6{,}2 = 9{,}8\), almost ten, remain reliable. For a bar, refining the mesh does no harm. For a beam, however, COOK (PDF p. 322) gives the exponent \(2m/n = 4/1\): \(\kappa\) grows with the fourth power of the number of elements, and doubling them increases it \(16\) times. That is \(\log_{10}16 = 1{,}2\) digits more at every doubling, for the bar only \(\log_{10}4 = 0{,}6\) digits. For a beam, refining a mesh that reduces the discretization error therefore quickly raises the numerical one.

17.6.3 When a large condition number is harmful

The condition number is an upper estimate, not a prediction. COOK (PDF p. 316, read) says that we cannot call a matrix ill-conditioned (slabo pogojena) until we state what is done with it: a matrix that is good for solving equations can be bad for eigenvalues and vice versa. The simplest example where this shows is two springs in a chain.

We set up the model. A spring of stiffness \(k_1\) is fixed to the wall, at its other end is node 1, then follows a spring of stiffness \(k_2\) and node 2, where the force \(F\) pulls. The springs are linear, massless and frictionless, and the displacements are small, so that the nodes stay on one straight line (an idealization that chapter 7 also uses). We draw the soft spring \(k_1\) as a long coil and the stiff spring \(k_2\) as a short, thick coil, because that is exactly the difference we care about. The displacement \(u_1\) is the distance between the original position of node 1 and its position after loading, the displacement \(u_2\) the same for node 2.

We write the equilibrium conditions as always, on free bodies. On node 1 the left spring pulls back with the force \(k_1 u_1\) and the right one forward with the force \(k_2(u_2 - u_1)\), because the right spring is stretched by \(u_2 - u_1\). With no external force \(k_1 u_1 = k_2(u_2 - u_1)\). On node 2 the force \(F\) pulls forward and the right spring back with the force \(k_2(u_2 - u_1)\), so \(k_2(u_2 - u_1) = F\). In matrix form

\[\begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 & -k_2 \\ -k_2 & k_2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ F \end{bmatrix} . \tag{17.24}\]

The solution is \(u_1 = F/k_1\) and \(u_2 = F/k_1 + F/k_2\), which we check by substituting into both equations. Let \(k_1 = 1\) and \(F = 1\) (displacements in the unit \(F/k_1\)), \(s = k_2/k_1\) the stiffness ratio, so the true displacement is \(u_2 = 1 + 1/s\). In elimination without row exchange the pivot is \(k_1 + k_2\) and the second row after the first step has the coefficient \(k_2 - k_2^2/(k_1 + k_2) = k_1 k_2/(k_1 + k_2)\). The information about \(k_1\) is hidden in this difference in the last digits of the number \(k_2\): at \(s = 10^{15}\) and \(16\) digits only one of them remains.

Lemma 17.30 Stiff spring on a soft one (toga vzmet na mehki)

Let \(k_1, k_2 > 0\), \(s = k_2/k_1\) and let \(\mat K\) be the matrix of equation (17.24). Then:

  1. The condition number \(\kappa\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) is the larger solution of the equation \(\kappa + 1/\kappa = 4s + 2 + 1/s\) and
    \[4s + 1 + \frac1s < \kappa < 4s + 2 + \frac1s . \tag{17.25}\]
  2. For the unknowns \(u_1\) and \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u_2 - u_1\) the matrix of the system is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathrm{diag}(k_1, k_2)\) and the right-hand side \((F, F)^\T\), so \(\kappa(\mat K_{\mathrm{r}}) = s\) and the solution is \(u_1 = F/k_1\), \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = F/k_2\), each with one division.
Proof

We must show the equation for \(\kappa\) and the form of the second matrix. The first item needs the trace and the determinant, the second the energy.

  1. The eigenvalues are the roots of the equation \(\lambda^2 - (k_1 + 2k_2)\lambda + k_1 k_2 = 0\), since the trace of \(\mat K\) is \(k_1 + 2k_2\) and the determinant is \((k_1 + k_2)k_2 - k_2^2 = k_1 k_2\). The discriminant \((k_1 + 2k_2)^2 - 4k_1 k_2 = k_1^2 + 4k_2^2\) is positive, so the roots are real and distinct. Their sum \(k_1 + 2k_2\) and product \(k_1 k_2\) are positive, so both are positive. We call the larger \(\lambda_{\max}\) and the smaller \(\lambda_{\min}\).
  2. From \((\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min})^2/(\lambda_{\max}\lambda_{\min}) = \kappa + 2 + 1/\kappa\) and step 1 we get \(\kappa + 2 + 1/\kappa = (k_1 + 2k_2)^2/(k_1 k_2) = 4s + 4 + 1/s\). Hence \(\kappa + 1/\kappa = 4s + 2 + 1/s\).
  3. Since the eigenvalues are distinct, \(\kappa > 1\) and \(0 < 1/\kappa < 1\). From step 2 it follows that \(\kappa < 4s + 2 + 1/s\) and \(\kappa > 4s + 1 + 1/s\).
  4. The second item. The potential energy is \(\Pi = \tfrac12 k_1 u_1^2 + \tfrac12 k_2 u_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - F u_2\) and \(u_2 = u_1 + u_{\mathrm{r}}\). By stationarity (corollary 8.58) the equations are \(\partial\Pi/\partial u_1 = k_1 u_1 - F = 0\) and \(\partial\Pi/\partial u_{\mathrm{r}} = k_2 u_{\mathrm{r}} - F = 0\), which is a diagonal system with the matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(k_1, k_2)\) and the right-hand side \((F, F)^\T\). The eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix are its diagonal entries, the ratio is \(k_2/k_1 = s\).

The same numbers are on the right of figure 17.20. On the axes are the stiffness ratio \(s\) horizontally and the relative error \(\varepsilon\) of the displacement \(u_2\) vertically, both on logarithmic scales, because both change by many orders of magnitude. The column \(\kappa\) says that \(\kappa \approx 4k_2/k_1\). At \(s = 10^{15}\) NumPy gives \(4{,}571 \cdot 10^{15}\) from the computed eigenvalues instead of the \(4{,}000 \cdot 10^{15}\) of the lemma (num-06.out). The smallest eigenvalue there is about \(0{,}5\), while the entries of the matrix are about \(10^{15}\), so rounding spoils it already in the computation of the eigenvalues. The column with the error for the unknowns \((u_1, u_2)\) is the displacement error measured on the computer (with the NumPy library), in the figure the orange line. It stays below the estimate \(4s \cdot 10^{-16} \approx \kappa \cdot 10^{-16}\) of the column after it, in the figure the dashed straight line, and follows it: from \(s = 10^3\) to \(s = 10^{15}\) both grow by about \(12\) orders of magnitude. At \(s = 10^{15}\) the error is \(14\) % and the result is useless, while the same system in other unknowns stays accurate. The last column, in the figure the empty blue circles, is a case where a large condition number does not mean a loss: the matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(1, s)\) has \(\kappa = s\), a quarter of the number of the matrix \(\mat K\), but loses no digit, because it contains no subtraction of nearly equal numbers. The condition number is therefore an upper estimate of the loss, not a prediction.

Two more cases show what a large condition number says and what it does not.

  • Multiply the whole matrix by \(1000\), for example by changing from N/mm to N/m. All eigenvalues are multiplied by \(1000\) and the ratio is the same: for \(\mat T_{10}\) we have \(\kappa = 175{,}0866\) before and after. A change of units in all equations at once therefore causes no bad conditioning and cures none. It is different with a change of unknowns, such as from \((u_1, u_2)\) to \((u_1, u_{\mathrm{r}})\). COOK (PDF p. 320, read) therefore proposes to compute \(\kappa\) from the matrix multiplied by a diagonal matrix so that it has ones on the diagonal, because it is then large only when the matrix is really nearly singular. For \(\mat K\) of equation (17.24) this is the matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -r \\ -r & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) with \(r = \sqrt{s/(1 + s)}\), eigenvalues \(1 \pm r\) and ratio \((1 + r)/(1 - r) \approx 4s\), as large as before. For \(\mathrm{diag}(1, s)\) the same procedure gives the unit matrix and \(\kappa = 1\).
  • Load the springs differently: the force \(F\) pulls node 1 back and node 2 forward, so \(\vek f = (-F, F)^\T\), two equal and opposite forces at the ends of the stiff spring, in equilibrium together. At \(s = 10^{12}\) and \(10^{15}\) the computed stretch \(u_2 - u_1\) equals \(10^{-12}\) and \(10^{-15}\) (num-06.out), although the matrix is the same and \(\kappa\) equally large. COOK (PDF p. 317) says that under an equilibrium load the relative displacement is computed accurately even if the rigid displacement carries an error. The error is in the displacement of the whole stiff mass, not in the stretch of the spring.

When, then, do we expect trouble? When a stiff mass sits on a soft support and we care about a displacement or a force that the soft support determines (COOK, PDF p. 317, read): the information about the soft support is in the last digits of the stiff terms. A similar phenomenon is in chapter 12, where the penalty stiffness of a constraint makes \(\kappa\) grow at least as the penalty number (lemma 12.29), and in shear locking, where the stiffness of the element itself does this. Lemma 12.29 is not used here, because the matrix without the stiff spring would have to be positive definite, and the soft spring at the wall does not hold \(u_2\) at all. The kinship is therefore in the mechanism and not in the theorem. The remedy is the same as there. COOK (PDF pp. 319-320, read) names it: we choose the unknowns so that the large number does not appear off the diagonal, as \(u_1\) and \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) above, or with local axes at an inclined support.

17.6.4 What we showed

This section treated only the numerical error, the third of the three, and it does not reduce the modelling or the discretization error. The factorization is computed once and every further right-hand side costs \(2nb + n\) instead of \(n b^2/2\) (lemma 17.28), but only for the same matrix. The condition number of the bar grows as \((4n/\pi)^2\) (lemma 17.29), for a beam by COOK as \(n^4\), so a refinement that reduces the discretization error raises the numerical one. A large condition number is an upper estimate of the loss of digits: a stiff spring on a soft one has it and also suffers the loss (lemma 17.30, \(\kappa \approx 4k_2/k_1\)), the same system in relative unknowns has the number \(s\), a quarter of it, and no loss. For every result we therefore ask three questions: how much modelling error, how much discretization error and how many digits we have lost.
