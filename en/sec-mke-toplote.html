Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.10 Finite elements for heat conduction (končni elementi za prevajanje toplote)

Section 14.9 showed that steady heat conduction has a weak form and a functional, both known from chapter 6. So for the temperature the same two methods apply as for the displacement of a bar, the Ritz and the Galerkin method, and by theorem 6.44 they coincide. How does a computation come from this, which matrices do we get, and what do we know about the result in advance? First we read when finite elements first turned to heat. Then we show that the matrix of a conduction element is the matrix of a bar element from chapter 7 with the letters given a new meaning. We add the loss through a lateral surface, which is new, and put everything together on the fin that section 14.4 solved exactly. This shows where the result is exact, where it is not, and on which side of the true value the heat at the root lies.

From history

The name "finite element" was coined in 1960, and new elements for stress analysis were soon developed largely by intuition and physical argument. In 1963 the method was recognized as a form of the Rayleigh-Ritz method, a classical approximation technique, and so it was no longer only a special trick for stress analysis but a widely applicable method with a sound mathematical basis (COOK, PDF p. 24). Two years later, in 1965, papers appeared on heat conduction and on seepage flow through a porous medium, done with finite elements (COOK, PDF p. 24). The reason why heat conduction came so early is the content of section 14.9: steady conduction has a functional, so the Ritz route of chapter 6 applies unchanged. We drew this link ourselves, the source does not state it.

Back to what we were looking at: steady conduction has a functional, and now we use it.

14.10.1 The model of the fin (model rebra)

We compute on a body that we have solved exactly, so that we can compare. It is the fin of section 14.4 (theorem 14.22, figure 14.36): a straight bar of length \(L\) with a constant cross-section of area \(A\) and perimeter \(P\), of a material with conductivity \(k\) (definition 14.3). The bar is attached at \(x = 0\) to a wall at temperature \(T_0\) above the surroundings, it gives off heat to the air through its lateral surface with heat transfer coefficient \(h\) (definition 14.13), and the end at \(x = L\) is insulated. We measure the temperature \(T(x)\) from the temperature of the surroundings, so the heat flux through the lateral surface is \(h\,T\). We write the element length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), because the letter \(h\) is taken by the heat transfer coefficient (chapter 7 used it for the element length).

We take the idealizations of section 14.4 (the temperature the same over the whole cross-section, \(k\) and \(h\) constant, no radiation) as given here, because the exact solution and the finite element model share them. The comparison of the two therefore measures only the error of the finite elements. A real fin also has the error of the model, which section 14.4 estimates, for instance at most \(5\,\%\) of the temperature excess from the uneven temperature over the cross-section when \(hR/k \le 0{,}1\) for a round bar of radius \(R\).

By theorem 14.22 the temperature solves \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\) with \(T(0) = T_0\) and \(T'(L) = 0\), namely

\[T(x) = T_0\,\frac{\cosh m(L - x)}{\cosh mL} , \qquad m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA} . \tag{14.56}\]

The number \(m\) has the unit 1/m, and \(mL\) has no unit: it says how many times the distance \(L\) is longer than the length \(1/m\) over which the temperature of a long fin falls by the factor \(e\). The heat that the wall supplies to the fin is \(Q = -kA\,T'(0) = \dfrac{kA\,T_0}{L}\,mL\tanh mL\). By equation (14.56) the temperature in units of \(T_0\) depends only on \(x/L\) and on the number \(mL\), and the heat in units of \(kA\,T_0/L\) only on \(mL\). So we compute with two values, \(mL = 1\) and \(mL = 2\), and each computation holds for all fins with that \(mL\), whatever the material and the dimensions. At \(mL = 1\) the temperature of the tip is \(T(L)/T_0 = 1/\cosh 1 = 0{,}64805\) and \(Q = 0{,}76159\), at \(mL = 2\) it is \(T(L)/T_0 = 0{,}26580\) and \(Q = 1{,}92806\) (computation in num-10.py).

We get the weak form as follows: multiply the equation \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\) by a test function \(w\) with \(w(0) = 0\) and integrate over \([0, L]\). A test function is zero wherever the temperature is prescribed (definition 14.49), here at the wall. By integration by parts (lemma 6.13) \(\int_0^L kA\,T''\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = kA\,T'(L)\,w(L) - kA\,T'(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\). Both boundary terms are zero: the first because the end is insulated and no heat flows through it, \(T'(L) = 0\), the second because \(w(0) = 0\) at the wall. The weak form is therefore

\[B(T, w) = \int_0^L \big(kA\,T'\,w' + hP\,T\,w\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \qquad \text{for every } w,\ w(0) = 0 . \tag{14.57}\]

The form \(B\) is bilinear and symmetric, since both terms under the integral are symmetric in \(T\) and \(w\). It is positive, \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every \(w \ne 0\): both terms under the integral are non-negative, and \(hP\,w^2\), the loss through the lateral surface at temperature \(w\), is positive on some interval around a point where \(w \ne 0\), because \(w\) is continuous. So the theorems of chapter 6 on the Ritz method hold, and the functional is \(\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v)\), since the right-hand side is zero (definition 6.32).

14.10.2 The matrix of a conduction element (prevodnostna matrika elementa)

The mesh divides the interval \([0, L]\) into elements as in definition 7.1: on an element \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) the field is the straight line through the nodal temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), given by \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \psi_1^e\,T_1 + \psi_2^e\,T_2\) with the shape functions of definition 7.2. What does this element do when we fix the temperatures of its nodes? We draw it together with the bar element, to see which quantity corresponds to which (figure 14.95).

First we draw the bar as an element with two nodes. If the nodes are displaced from the grey dashed position by \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), the surroundings must hold them with the forces \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), which by lemma 7.5 are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Below we do the same with the conductor element: instead of node displacements we have temperatures, shown by the profile \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) with the heights \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), and instead of forces the heat flows that the surroundings must supply or remove so that the nodes stay at these temperatures. The height of the profile is not a length but the temperature above the zero level. In the steady state the heat must flow through the element from the warm end to the cold end, and the arrow of the heat flux \(q\) shows the direction of this flow.

Before we state the claim, let us say what we expect. Steady state means that the temperature of the element does not change in time, so with no loss through the lateral surface and no source, all the heat that enters at the left end must leave at the right end (corollary 14.10 of section 14.2). By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) the heat flux is \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{h}}'\), and since the profile \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a straight line with one slope, the heat flux \(q\) is constant along the element.

Lemma 14.54 Conductivity matrix of an element (prevodnostna matrika elementa)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element with two nodes and length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definition 7.1), \(kA\) constant on the element, and let there be no heat source on it and no loss through the lateral surface. Let \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (T_1, T_2)^\T\) be the column of nodal temperatures and \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac1{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(-1,\ 1)\). Then:

  1. The temperature gradient on the element is constant, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The element contributes to the functional \(\tfrac12\int kA\,(T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) the part \(\tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,kA\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.58}\]
  3. The heat flows that the surroundings supply to the element at nodes \(1\) and \(2\) (positive when heat enters the element) are \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{e}} = (Q_1, Q_2)^\T = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). So \(Q_1 = -Q_2 = \dfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2)\).
  4. For equal heights \(T_1 = T_2\) both flows are zero: \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1,\ 1)^\T = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show four claims. The first and second are computations from lemma 7.5, the third is the balance of flow, the fourth a consequence of lemma 7.3.

  1. The shape functions are the same as for the bar, so by step 1 of the proof of lemma 7.5 \(T_{\mathrm{h}}' = (-T_1 + T_2)/h_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\), a number independent of \(x\): the profile is a straight line between the heights \(T_1\) and \(T_2\).
  2. The product \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the matrix \(\frac1{h_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 7.5). The integrand \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,kA\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), because the profile has the same slope everywhere, so the integral equals the integrand times \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 3.54). This is equation (14.58). Since \((T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2 = \vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a number, the contribution of the element is \(\tfrac12\,kA\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,(T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2 = \tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. By definition 14.3 the heat flux in the direction \(+x\) is \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{h}}' = k\,(T_1 - T_2)/h_{\mathrm{e}}\), constant along the element. Through the cross-section \(A\) the flow \(A\,q\) goes in the direction \(+x\). At the left end this flow enters the element, so \(Q_1 = A\,q = \dfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2)\). At the right end the same flow leaves, so the element receives \(Q_2 = -A\,q\) from the surroundings. The sum \(Q_1 + Q_2 = 0\) is just the balance of the element in the steady state without source and loss. Comparison with \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2,\ T_2 - T_1)^\T\) gives the claim.
  4. For \(T_1 = T_2\) the heights are equal and the profile is horizontal, so the gradient is zero by claim 1 and both flows are zero by claim 3.

Source: COOK §12.2, equations (12.2-4) (PDF pp. 473-474), where [k] is called the "conductivity matrix". The notation and the signs of claim 3 are ours.

The lemma is the same computation as for the bar, with \(EA\) replaced by \(kA\), as the upper and the lower element of figure 14.95 show. So everything we know from chapters 6 and 7 carries over word for word. The table shows what corresponds to what (after COOK, PDF p. 474).

bar (chapter 7) heat conduction
displacement of a node \(d\) temperature of a node \(T\)
stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(EA\) conductivity matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(kA\)
force on a node \(F\) heat flow into a node \(Q\)
support with a prescribed displacement prescribed temperature
reaction of a support heat supplied by the support

The matrix is the same, but the sign means something different for each quantity. The axial force in a bar is \(N = EA\,u'\) and is positive when the bar stretches. The flow in the direction \(+x\) is \(-kA\,T'\) and is positive when the temperature falls along \(x\), because heat flows from warm to cold. In figure 14.95 the bar is stretched, \(d_2 > d_1\), and the temperature falls, \(T_1 > T_2\). So the force \(F_1\) equals \(\tfrac{EA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(d_1 - d_2) < 0\) and points out of the element, while the flow \(Q_1\) is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2) > 0\) and points into it. At the right end both arrows point out of the element: the force \(F_2\), because we pull the bar, and the arrow \(-Q_2\), because heat leaves there. The directions at node \(1\) are opposite because of the values drawn, not because of the matrix: for \(T_2 > T_1\), \(Q_1\) would be negative too, and heat would leave the element at node \(1\).

A test. A fin with two elements has \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/2\) and conductivity matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = (2kA/L)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Its rows sum to zero, as claim 4 says, so the matrix is singular by itself, like a bar without a support (section 7.3.1), which we can move as a whole by \(c\) without stretching any element. The physical reason is the same: if we add the same number to all nodes, no slope and so no flow changes, as the three solutions shifted upwards of section 14.9.5 show. In conduction without lateral loss, only the prescribed temperature of at least one node fixes the temperature, here \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\). In the fin, the surroundings, which take heat away through the lateral surface, also act as a support (COOK, PDF p. 481). If we add \(c\) to all nodes of the fin, the loss through the lateral surface grows by \(hP\,L\,c\), and the surroundings notice it. This is exactly what the next lemma says: the matrix this loss adds is positive definite, so the matrix of the fin is not singular even without a prescribed temperature. A body without lateral loss and with only prescribed flows has a solution by lemma 14.18 only if the sum of the flows is zero, and then any constant may be added.

Lemma 14.55 Matrix of lateral heat loss (matrika izgube skozi stransko ploskev)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element with two nodes and length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), let the cross-section have perimeter \(P\), let the heat transfer coefficient on the lateral surface be \(h\) and constant (definition 14.13), and let the temperature be measured from the surroundings. The part of the functional \(\tfrac12\int hP\,T_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) that the element contributes is \(\tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat s_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,hP\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.59}\]

The matrix is symmetric and positive definite, and the sum of its entries \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the heat transfer coefficient times the lateral surface of the element: at equal temperature \(T\) of both nodes the loss of heat through the lateral surface is \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,T\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the product of shape functions is the matrix of lemma 10.47, only with another constant.

  1. For \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) we have \(T_{\mathrm{h}}^2 = \vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.102), and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral we take it out.
  2. The integrals \(\int\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) are areas under products of the shape functions: under \(\psi_1^2\) and under \(\psi_2^2\) \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/3\) each, under \(\psi_1\psi_2\) \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/6\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 10.47). The factor \(hP\) is constant and goes in front of the integral, which gives equation (14.59).
  3. Positive definiteness. For \((a, b) \ne (0, 0)\), by step 1, \((a, b)\,\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,(a, b)^\T = hP\int T_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the line from \(a\) to \(b\) on the element. Its square is nowhere below the axis. It reaches the axis at one point at most, because a line that is not zero everywhere crosses the axis at most once. Hence the area under the square is positive. With the integrals of step 2 it equals \(\tfrac{h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\big(a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2\big)\) (step 5 of the same lemma), so \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/4\) for \(a = 1\) and \(b = -0{,}5\).
  4. The sum of the entries is \(\tfrac{hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\,(2 + 1 + 1 + 2) = hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\). Step 1 gives the same for \(T_1 = T_2 = T\): by lemma 7.3 \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\) on the whole element, the square is horizontal and the area under it is a rectangle, so \(\int hP\,T^2\,\mathrm{d}x = hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,T^2\). The heat that the lateral surface of the element, of area \(P\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\), gives off at temperature \(T\) is \(h\,T\) per unit area times \(P\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\), that is \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,T\).

Source: COOK §12.2, (12.2-4) and (12.2-6) (PDF p. 474), where the term with lateral convection is added to the matrix \([k]\) and where COOK compares it with the matrix of an elastic foundation. The proof in steps is ours.

We called the matrix the side matrix, because it comes from the loss through the lateral surface of the bar. Its integral is the same as for the consistent mass matrix of lemma 10.47, with \(\rho A\) replaced by \(hP\). There the matrix multiplies the acceleration, here it multiplies the temperature, so it plays the role of a stiffness, like an elastic foundation. In section 14.11 the same integral appears once more, there with the density and the specific heat, and it will multiply the time derivative.

What does the assumption of an insulated tip do? If the end of the fin gave off heat with heat transfer coefficient \(h\), we would add a term \(hA\) to the diagonal of node \(n\), as for a spring at a node (COOK, PDF p. 474). In units of \(kA/L\) this term is \(hL/k\), and since \((mL)^2 = hPL^2/(kA)\), we have \(hL/k = (mL)^2\,A/(PL)\), where \(A/(PL)\) is the ratio of the tip area \(A\) to the lateral area \(PL\). With two elements at \(mL = 1\) we get the nodal temperatures in units of \(T_0\):

tip \(A/(PL)\) \(T_1\) \(T_2\)
insulated \(0\) \(0{,}7266\) \(0{,}6428\)
giving off heat, slender fin \(1/20\) \(0{,}7162\) \(0{,}6193\)
giving off heat, disc \(1\) \(0{,}6040\) \(0{,}3656\)

For the slender fin the assumption changes the temperature of the tip by \(0{,}023\,T_0\), that is by \(4\,\%\), for the disc by \(43\,\%\). For a round bar of diameter \(d\) we have \(A/(PL) = d/(4L)\) (section 14.4), so \(1/20\) is a bar of length \(5\,d\), and \(A = PL\) a disc of length \(d/4\), which has as much area at the tip as on the lateral surface. The insulated tip is therefore a good model of a slender fin and a poor model of a short one.

14.10.3 Assembly and comparison with the exact solution (sestavljanje in primerjava s točno rešitvijo)

Now we have everything. We divide the fin at \(mL = 1\) into two elements and draw the computation (figure 14.97).

The fin is attached to the wall, the tip is insulated. We divide it into the first element, which we draw blue, and the second, which we draw orange, with the nodes \(0\), \(1\) and \(2\), so \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/2\). The matrix of one element is the sum of the conductivity matrix of lemma 14.54 and the side matrix of lemma 14.55. In units of \(kA/L\) we have \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac{(mL)^2}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), because \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}/6 = hP\,L/12 = (mL)^2\,\tfrac{kA}{12\,L}\). The sum has \(2 + \tfrac{2}{12} = \tfrac{13}{6}\) on the diagonal and \(-2 + \tfrac{1}{12} = -\tfrac{23}{12}\) off it. By theorem 7.13 the matrix of the first element falls into rows and columns \(0\) and \(1\) (blue block), that of the second into \(1\) and \(2\) (orange block), as for the bar in figure 7.22. The two elements share node \(1\), so in the entry \((1, 1)\) their contributions add up, \(\tfrac{13}{6} + \tfrac{13}{6} = \tfrac{13}{3}\). By SymPy the matrix of the structure is

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 13/6 & -23/12 & 0 \\ -23/12 & 13/3 & -23/12 \\ 0 & -23/12 & 13/6 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.60}\]

Then we prescribe \(T_0\) at node \(0\) and solve the reduced system (theorem 7.18): we leave out row \(0\) and move column \(0\), multiplied by \(T_0\), to the right-hand side, as for the bar in figure 7.32. The reduced system for \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) has the right-hand side \(-K_{10}\,T_0 = \tfrac{23}{12}\,T_0\) and \(0\) and the solution

The difference is less than \(0{,}006\,T_0\), so it does not show in the drawing. 14.10.4 When the result is exact (kdaj je rezultat točen)

For a bar, chapter 6 showed that nodal displacements are exact if \(EA\) is constant on every element (theorem 6.38), like the nodes of the hanging bar, which lie on the parabola. We expect the same claim for temperature. The fin does not satisfy it, because it has a lateral loss, so first we look at a body without it. We will test the claim on the three examples of figure 14.99.

Corollary 14.56 Exact nodal temperatures (točne vozliščne temperature)

Let a bar of length \(L\) have a temperature prescribed at \(x = 0\), no loss through the lateral surface, a product \(kA > 0\) that is piecewise constant, a given heat source per unit length and a given flow at the end \(x = L\). Let the nodes of a mesh of linear elements also lie at every jump of \(kA\). Then the temperature at every node equals the true temperature.

Proof

We must show that the weak form of this bar is the weak form of the bar of theorem 6.38 and that its assumptions hold.

  1. By theorem 14.50 the weak form is \(B(T, w) = \ell(w)\) with \(B(T, w) = \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and a linear form \(\ell\) from the source and the end flow. By form this is the weak form of a bar in which \(kA\) stands for \(EA\) and the loads are the source and the end flow (chapter 6).
  2. The field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) of theorem 6.38 has \(kA\,G_{\mathrm{P}}' = 1\) on \((0, x_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(0\) after, and is continuous. For heat it is the temperature of the bar into which heat \(1\) flows at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), while the wall has temperature \(0\): all the heat flows from \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) to the wall, so the flow on \((0, x_{\mathrm{P}})\) is \(1\) and beyond \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) zero. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be a node. On every element \(kA\) is constant, so \(G_{\mathrm{P}}'\) is constant, hence \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is a straight line on every element, like the field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) in figure 6.95.
  3. A field that is continuous and a straight line on every element is a combination of tent functions (definition 6.25), so it lies in the trial family. In figure 6.95 this is the sum of tent functions with the heights \(G_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  4. By theorem 6.38, therefore, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = T(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) at every node \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), like the nodes on the true temperature in figure 14.99.

Source: The proof is not from a source. Exact nodal values for a bar with two-node elements are stated by ONATE1 (PDF p. 363), see the source of theorem 6.38.

We test the corollary on two examples and then at its limit (figure 14.99). In all three parts of the figure the temperature is drawn above the axis \(x\), and the bar below it.

A layer with a jump of conductivity. A bar has \(kA = 1\) on \([0, 0{,}4]\) and \(kA = 4\) on \([0{,}4, 1]\) (in arbitrary units, \(L = 1\)), \(T(0) = 0\) at the wall and a flow \(1\) entering at the end \(x = L\). This flow must pass through the whole bar to the wall, so the flow is \(1\) everywhere and the temperature rises with slope \(1/kA\), steeply in the poorer conducting layer and gently in the better one. So \(T(0{,}4) = 0{,}4\), \(T(0{,}5) = 0{,}4 + 0{,}1/4 = 0{,}425\) and \(T(1) = 0{,}55\). This temperature is just the field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) for \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 1\) of step 2, with a kink at the jump. The mesh with nodes \(0\), \(0{,}4\) and \(1\) has a node at the jump and gives \(0{,}4\) and \(0{,}55\), exactly as the corollary predicts. The mesh with nodes \(0\), \(0{,}5\) and \(1\) has the jump inside the first element, whose mean \(kA\) is \((0{,}4\cdot 1 + 0{,}1\cdot 4)/0{,}5 = 1{,}6\) (as for the bar, section 7.1.2), and gives \(T(0{,}5) = 0{,}3125\) instead of \(0{,}425\) (error \(-26{,}5\,\%\)) and \(T(1) = 0{,}4375\) instead of \(0{,}55\) (\(-20{,}5\,\%\)). Why too low? The straight line on the first element has a single slope, so the element conducts like a material with \(kA = 1{,}6\) over the whole length \(0{,}5\), with a temperature drop \(0{,}5/1{,}6 = 0{,}3125\) at flow \(1\). The true bar has first \(0{,}4\) of the poorly conducting layer with a drop \(0{,}4\) and only then \(0{,}1\) of the good one with a drop \(0{,}025\). Heat crosses the two layers one after the other and the drops add up, so the poorer layer counts more than the mean \(kA\) says, and the element conducts better than the true bar. The error \(0{,}1125\) is the same at both places, because the second element lies in one layer and there the straight line follows the true temperature with slope \(1/4\). The condition that the node lies at the jump is therefore not a refinement: where it holds the result is exact, where it fails the error is a fifth or more. The reason is in step 2: the field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) has a kink at the jump and is not a straight line inside the element.

A heat source. A bar has \(kA = 1\), \(L = 1\), temperature \(0\) at \(x = 0\), an insulated end and a uniform source \(1\) per unit length. The exact temperature is \(T = x - x^2/2\). With two elements we get \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(0{,}5) = 0{,}375\) and \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(1) = 0{,}5\), which is exact. In the middle of an element, at \(x = 0{,}25\), we get \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}1875\) instead of \(0{,}21875\), a difference \(0{,}03125\) \(= g\,h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/(8\,kA)\) with source \(g = 1\) (computation in num-10.py). The parabola is curved downwards, so it lies above the straight line between its two nodal points. So the corollary concerns the nodes and no other points.

The fin, where the corollary does not hold. With the loss through the lateral surface the table above showed that the tip temperature is not exact (\(0{,}64277\) against \(0{,}64805\)). This does not contradict the corollary, because one assumption is missing. The field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) with \(B(G_{\mathrm{P}}, w) = w(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) for every \(w\) is again the temperature of a fin into which heat \(1\) flows at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), while the wall has the temperature of the surroundings and the tip is insulated. Now the heat does not only flow to the wall, part of it is given off by the lateral surface on the way, so the flow is not constant and the field bends. To the right of \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) it even falls, because part of the heat flows there too and leaves through the lateral surface. On both sides of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) it satisfies \(kA\,G_{\mathrm{P}}'' = hP\,G_{\mathrm{P}}\) and is there a combination of \(\cosh\) and \(\sinh\), not a straight line. The broken line through its nodal values misses it inside both elements, so \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) does not lie in the family of piecewise linear fields and step 3 fails.

14.10.5 The heat at the root is above the exact value (toplota ob korenu je nad točno)

So the nodal temperatures of the fin are not exact. The heat \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) that the support must supply, however, has by the table an important property: in all eight computations it is above the true value, as the arrow \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) above the exact value \(Q\). We see why if we write it with energy.

Lemma 14.57 Heat at the root of a fin (toplota ob korenu rebra)

Let \(T\) be the exact temperature of the fin of section 14.10.1, \(Q = -kA\,T'(0)\) the heat supplied by the wall, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) the finite element solution with linear elements on an arbitrary mesh with a node at \(x = 0\), and \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = (\mat K\,\vek T)_0\) row \(0\) of the system, that is, the heat that the support must supply. Then

\[Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q = \frac{B(T - T_{\mathrm{h}},\ T - T_{\mathrm{h}})}{T_0} \ge 0 , \tag{14.61}\]

with equality only for \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\). The heat at the root is thus never below the exact value.

Proof

We must show that \(B(T, T)\) and \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}})\) are \(T_0\) times the heat, and then use theorem 6.34.

  1. By theorem 14.22 \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\). Hence \(B(T, T) = \int_0^L \big(kA\,T'\,T' + kA\,T''\,T\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (kA\,T'\,T)'\,\mathrm{d}x\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55) this is \(kA\,T'(L)\,T(L) - kA\,T'(0)\,T(0) = 0 + Q\,T_0\), because the end is insulated. The right side is the heat entering at the wall times the temperature of the wall.
  2. By theorem 7.13 (claim 3) \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\). The theorem is stated for a bar without the side term, but the proof stays the same, because the side term is also an integral over \([0, L]\), the sum of the integrals over the elements that lemma 14.55 gives. For the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} T_{\mathrm{I}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\), therefore, \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{J}} K_{0\mathrm{J}}\,T_{\mathrm{J}} = B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_0)\), that is, row \(0\) times the temperatures. The reduced system (theorem 7.18) says \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) for every node \(i \ne 0\), since the right-hand side is zero.
  3. Hence \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} T_{\mathrm{I}}\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\), a sum over the nodes in which each term is the temperature of the node times \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\), the heat that the surroundings supply to the node. On the fin with two elements only node \(0\) gets heat, from the wall, the other nodes get nothing by step 2. Of the three bars only the first remains, so \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}}) = T_0\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_0) = T_0\,Q_{\mathrm{h}}\), here \(1 \cdot 0{,}7740\).
  4. The functional is \(\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v)\), and \(T\) and \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) are admissible fields with the same temperature \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\). By claim 1 of theorem 6.34, \(\Pi(T_{\mathrm{h}}) - \Pi(T) = \tfrac12\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}} - T,\ T_{\mathrm{h}} - T)\): \(T\) lies at the bottom of the energy parabola, and \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is higher by half the square of the energy norm of the error.
  5. By steps 1 and 3 the left side equals \(\tfrac12\,(T_0\,Q_{\mathrm{h}} - T_0\,Q) = \tfrac12\,T_0\,(Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q)\), half the difference of the lengths \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}})\) and \(B(T, T)\) on the common axis. Dividing by \(\tfrac12\,T_0 > 0\) gives equation (14.61): the excess is \(B(T - T_{\mathrm{h}},\ T - T_{\mathrm{h}})/T_0\), here \(0{,}0124\), as the magnification shows. The right side is non-negative, because \(B\) is positive (section 14.10.1), and zero only for \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. For the general theorem on the energy of the Ritz solution see the source of theorem 6.34.

The proof showed that the excess heat is exactly the square of the energy norm of the error (definition 6.18), divided by \(T_0\). This explains the numbers in the table: at \(mL = 1\), \(n = 2\) we have \(B(T - T_{\mathrm{h}}, T - T_{\mathrm{h}})/T_0 = 0{,}01240\), the same as \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q\), and the same holds in all eight rows to the fifth decimal (num-10.py). Why the excess decreases by a factor of \(4\) when the elements are halved, the lemma does not say. Theorem 6.41 gives an upper bound for a bar without the side term: the square of the energy norm of the error is at most proportional to \(h^2\), which the slope \(2\) of figure 6.100 shows. The theorem does not cover the side term, and we do not prove a bound for it here. The table, however, shows ratios from \(3{,}98\) to \(4{,}02\), as for a decrease with \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\), and so the dots of figure 14.98 approach the grey line.

Let us also read the lemma physically, without energy. The finite element field has only a straight line on each element, so it does not let the temperature arrange itself as it would on its own. The model behaves as if the fin conducted and gave off heat more easily than it really does, and the support must supply more heat for the same temperature \(T_0\), \(0{,}7740\) instead of \(0{,}7616\). We saw the same with the layer, where the element with the mean conductivity gave too low a temperature for the same flow, so it conducted better than the true bar. For a bar on an elastic foundation, where the stiffness of the foundation replaces \(hP\), with a prescribed displacement of one end and no other loads, the same proof gives that the support must supply at least the true force: the finite element model is too stiff.

When does the lemma hold? The proof needs the right-hand side of the system to be zero, so a fin without a source, with the temperature measured from the surroundings, and with the only prescribed temperature at the wall. If the tip gives off heat, the proof stays the same, because the term \(hA\,T(L)^2\) is part of the form \(B\) and step 1 again gives \(Q\,T_0\). With a heat source, however, step 3 gets an extra term from the source, and the proof in this form does not hold.

What does the lemma not say? It says nothing about the temperature of the tip. There \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is below the exact value in all eight computations, but this is an observation, not a theorem: the temperature at one point is not an energy, and the Ritz method bounds the energy (theorem 6.34), not every value (as for the hanging bar in chapter 6, where the work of the weight is too small and the displacement of the end too large). For design this means: the finite element model never underestimates the heat that the fin gives off, and in these computations it underestimated the temperature of the tip. An overestimated heat is not on the safe side when the fin must remove at least a given heat, since the model promises more than the fin can do. It is on the safe side when we look for an upper bound of a heat loss. An underestimated tip temperature is not on the safe side when we ask about overheating of the tip. For these two values of \(mL\) the error of the heat is below \(5\,\%\) already at two elements and below \(1{,}2\,\%\) at four. The error of the tip temperature, divided by the exact tip temperature, is only \(0{,}8\,\%\) and \(0{,}2\,\%\) at \(mL = 1\), but \(8{,}7\,\%\) at two and \(2{,}0\,\%\) at four elements at \(mL = 2\).

What have we gained and what is left? The conduction element is the bar element with the letters given a new meaning, so assembly, supports and the reduced system carry over without a new proof. The side matrix \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) is new. We added two results: the nodal temperatures are exact when the field of a unit inflow \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is a straight line on every element, which we proved with theorem 6.38 and tested in figure 14.99, and the heat at the root is an upper bound of the true one, which we proved with the energy of the Ritz solution (theorem 6.34). Three steps remain. Temperature changing in time brings one more matrix, which section 14.11 will multiply by the time derivative. Plane and spatial elements for heat are in chapter 15, where temperature is the first field on triangles and tetrahedra. Radiation and temperature-dependent conductivity we did not treat, because then the problem is nonlinear and the functional is no longer quadratic (COOK, PDF pp. 476-478).
