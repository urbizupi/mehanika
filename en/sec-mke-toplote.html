Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.10 Finite elements for heat conduction (končni elementi za prevajanje toplote)

Section 14.9 showed that steady heat conduction has a weak form and a functional, both known from chapter 6. So for the temperature the same two methods apply as for the displacement of a bar, the Ritz and the Galerkin method, and by theorem 6.44 they coincide. How does a computation come from this, which matrices do we get, and what do we know about the result in advance? First we read when finite elements first turned to heat. Then we show that the matrix of a conduction element is the matrix of a bar element from chapter 7 with the letters given a new meaning. We add the loss through a lateral surface, which is new, and put everything together on the fin that section 14.4 solved exactly. This shows where the result is exact, where it is not, and on which side of the true value the heat at the root lies.

From history

The name "finite element" was coined in 1960, and new elements for stress analysis were soon developed largely by intuition and physical argument. In 1963 the method was recognized as a form of the Rayleigh-Ritz method, a classical approximation technique, and so it was no longer only a special trick for stress analysis but a widely applicable method with a sound mathematical basis (COOK, PDF p. 24). Two years later, in 1965, papers appeared on heat conduction and on seepage flow through a porous medium, done with finite elements (COOK, PDF p. 24). The reason why heat conduction came so early is the content of section 14.9: steady conduction has a functional, so the Ritz route of chapter 6 applies unchanged. We drew this link ourselves, the source does not state it.

Back to what we were looking at: steady conduction has a functional, and now we use it.

14.10.1 The model of the fin (model rebra)

We compute on a body that we have solved exactly, so that we can compare. It is the fin of section 14.4 (theorem 14.22): a straight bar of length \(L\) with a constant cross-section of area \(A\) and perimeter \(P\), of a material with conductivity \(k\) (chapter 5 uses \(k\) for the shear factor, here it is the material quantity of definition 14.3). The bar is attached at \(x = 0\) to a wall at temperature \(T_0\) above the surroundings, it gives off heat to the air through its lateral surface with heat transfer coefficient \(h\) (definition 14.13), and the end at \(x = L\) is insulated. We measure the temperature \(T(x)\) from the temperature of the surroundings, so the heat flux through the lateral surface is \(h\,T\). We take the idealizations of section 14.4 as given: the temperature is the same over the whole cross-section, \(k\) and \(h\) are constant, radiation is neglected. We write the element length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), because the letter \(h\) is taken by the heat transfer coefficient (chapter 7 used it for the element length).

By theorem 14.22 the temperature solves \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\) with \(T(0) = T_0\) and \(T'(L) = 0\), namely

\[T(x) = T_0\,\frac{\cosh m(L - x)}{\cosh mL} , \qquad m^2 = \frac{hP}{kA} . \tag{14.51}\]

The number \(m\) has the unit 1/m, and \(mL\) has no unit: it says how many times the distance \(L\) is longer than the length \(1/m\) over which the temperature of a long fin falls by the factor \(e\). The heat that the wall supplies to the fin is \(Q = -kA\,T'(0) = \dfrac{kA\,T_0}{L}\,mL\tanh mL\). The book has no source for the \(k\) of steel, so we compute with the dimensionless numbers \(mL = 1\) and \(mL = 2\) and measure heat in units of \(kA\,T_0/L\). At \(mL = 1\) the temperature of the tip is \(T(L)/T_0 = 1/\cosh 1 = 0{,}64805\) and \(Q = 0{,}76159\), at \(mL = 2\) it is \(T(L)/T_0 = 0{,}26580\) and \(Q = 1{,}92806\) (computation in num-10.py).

We get the weak form as follows: multiply the equation \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\) by a test function \(w\) with \(w(0) = 0\) and integrate over \([0, L]\). By integration by parts (lemma 6.13) \(\int_0^L kA\,T''\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = kA\,T'(L)\,w(L) - kA\,T'(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\). Both boundary terms are zero: the first because the end is insulated, \(T'(L) = 0\), the second because \(w(0) = 0\). The weak form is therefore

\[B(T, w) = \int_0^L \big(kA\,T'\,w' + hP\,T\,w\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \qquad \text{for every } w,\ w(0) = 0 . \tag{14.52}\]

The form \(B\) is bilinear and symmetric, since both terms under the integral are symmetric in \(T\) and \(w\). It is positive, \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every \(w \ne 0\): both terms under the integral are non-negative, and \(hP\,w^2\) is positive on some interval around a point where \(w \ne 0\), because \(w\) is continuous. So the theorems of chapter 6 on the Ritz method hold, and the functional is \(\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v)\), since the right-hand side is zero (definition 6.32).

14.10.2 The matrix of a conduction element (prevodnostna matrika elementa)

The mesh divides the interval \([0, L]\) into elements as in definition 7.1: on an element \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) the field is the straight line through the nodal temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), given by \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \psi_1^e\,T_1 + \psi_2^e\,T_2\) with the shape functions of definition 7.2. What does this element do when we fix the temperatures of its nodes? We draw it together with the bar element, to see which quantity corresponds to which (figure 14.38).

First we draw the bar as an element with two nodes. If the nodes are displaced from the grey dashed position by \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), the surroundings must hold them with the forces \(F_1\) and \(F_2\), which by lemma 7.5 are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Below we do the same with the conductor element: instead of node displacements we have temperatures, shown by the profile \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) with the heights \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), and instead of forces the heat flows that the surroundings must supply or remove so that the nodes stay at these temperatures. The height of the profile is not a length but the temperature above the zero level. In the steady state the heat must flow through the element from the warm end to the cold end, and the arrow of the heat flux \(q\) shows the direction of this flow.

Before we state the claim, let us say what we expect. Steady state means that the temperature of the element does not change in time, so with no loss through the lateral surface and no source, all the heat that enters at the left end must leave at the right end (corollary 14.10 of section 14.2). By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) the heat flux is \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{h}}'\), and since \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a straight line, \(q\) is constant along the element.

Lemma 14.53 Conductivity matrix of an element (prevodnostna matrika elementa)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element with two nodes and length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definition 7.1), \(kA\) constant on the element, and let there be no heat source on it and no loss through the lateral surface. Let \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = (T_1, T_2)^\T\) be the column of nodal temperatures and \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac1{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(-1,\ 1)\). Then:

  1. The temperature gradient on the element is constant, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The part of the functional \(\tfrac12\int kA\,(T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) that the element contributes is \(\tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,kA\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.53}\]
  3. The heat flows that the surroundings supply to the element at nodes \(1\) and \(2\) (positive when heat enters the element) are \(\vek Q_{\mathrm{e}} = (Q_1, Q_2)^\T = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\). So \(Q_1 = -Q_2 = \dfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2)\).
  4. For \(T_1 = T_2\) both flows are zero: \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1,\ 1)^\T = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show four claims. The first and second are computations from lemma 7.5, the third is the balance of flow, the fourth a consequence of lemma 7.3.

  1. The shape functions are the same as for the bar, so by step 1 of the proof of lemma 7.5 \(T_{\mathrm{h}}' = (-T_1 + T_2)/h_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\), a number independent of \(x\).
  2. The product \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the matrix \(\frac1{h_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 7.5). The integrand \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,kA\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so the integral equals the integrand times \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 3.39). This is equation (14.53). Since \((T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2 = \vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a number, the contribution of the element is \(\tfrac12\,kA\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,(T_{\mathrm{h}}')^2 = \tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. By definition 14.3 the heat flux in the direction \(+x\) is \(q = -k\,T_{\mathrm{h}}' = k\,(T_1 - T_2)/h_{\mathrm{e}}\), constant along the element. Through the cross-section \(A\) the flow \(A\,q\) goes in the direction \(+x\). At the left end this flow enters the element, so \(Q_1 = A\,q = \dfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2)\). At the right end the same flow leaves, so the element receives \(Q_2 = -A\,q\) from the surroundings. The sum \(Q_1 + Q_2 = 0\) is just the balance of the element in the steady state without source and loss. Comparison with \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2,\ T_2 - T_1)^\T\) gives the claim.
  4. For \(T_1 = T_2\) the gradient is zero by claim 1 and both flows are zero by claim 3.

Source: COOK §12.2, equations (12.2-4) (PDF pp. 473-474), where [k] is called the "conductivity matrix". The notation and the signs of claim 3 are ours.

The lemma is the same computation as for the bar, with \(EA\) replaced by \(kA\). So everything we know from chapters 6 and 7 carries over word for word. The table shows what corresponds to what (after COOK, PDF p. 474).

bar (chapter 7) heat conduction
displacement of a node \(d\) temperature of a node \(T\)
stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(EA\) conductivity matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(kA\)
force on a node \(F\) heat flow into a node \(Q\)
support with a prescribed displacement prescribed temperature
reaction of a support heat supplied by the support

The matrix is the same, but the sign means something different for each quantity. The axial force in a bar is \(N = EA\,u'\) and is positive when the bar stretches. The flow in the direction \(+x\) is \(-kA\,T'\) and is positive when the temperature falls along \(x\), because heat flows from warm to cold. In figure 14.38 the bar is stretched, \(d_2 > d_1\), and the temperature falls, \(T_1 > T_2\). So the force \(F_1\) equals \(\tfrac{EA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(d_1 - d_2) < 0\) and points out of the element, while the flow \(Q_1\) is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\,(T_1 - T_2) > 0\) and points into it. At the right end both arrows point out of the element: the force \(F_2\), because we pull the bar, and the arrow \(-Q_2\), because heat leaves there. The directions at node \(1\) are opposite because of the values drawn, not because of the matrix: for \(T_2 > T_1\), \(Q_1\) would be negative too, and heat would leave the element at node \(1\).

A test. A fin with two elements has \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/2\) and conductivity matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = (2kA/L)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Its rows sum to zero, as claim 4 says, so the matrix is singular by itself, like a bar without a support (section 7.3.1). The physical reason is the same: if all nodes are at the same temperature, heat flows nowhere, and we can change this common temperature by any number without anything happening. In conduction without lateral loss, only the prescribed temperature of at least one node fixes it, here \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\). In the fin, the surroundings, which take heat away through the lateral surface, also act as a support (COOK, PDF p. 481): the matrix this loss adds is positive definite by the next lemma, so the matrix of the fin is not singular even without a prescribed temperature. A body without lateral loss and with only prescribed flows has a solution by lemma 14.18 only if the sum of the flows is zero, and then any constant may be added.

Lemma 14.54 Matrix of lateral heat loss (matrika izgube skozi stransko ploskev)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element with two nodes and length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), let the cross-section have perimeter \(P\), let the heat transfer coefficient on the lateral surface be \(h\) and constant (definition 14.13), and let the temperature be measured from the surroundings. The part of the functional \(\tfrac12\int hP\,T_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) that the element contributes is \(\tfrac12\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat s_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,hP\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.54}\]

The matrix is symmetric and positive definite, and the sum of its entries \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the heat transfer coefficient times the lateral surface of the element: at equal temperature \(T\) of both nodes the loss of heat through the lateral surface is \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,T\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the product of shape functions is the matrix of lemma 10.31, only with another constant.

  1. For \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) we have \(T_{\mathrm{h}}^2 = \vek T_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.101), and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral we take it out.
  2. The integrals \(\int\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) are \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/3\), \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/6\), \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/6\), \(h_{\mathrm{e}}/3\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 10.31). The factor \(hP\) is constant and goes in front of the integral, which gives equation (14.54).
  3. Positive definiteness: for \((a, b) \ne (0, 0)\) we have \((a, b)\,\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,(a, b)^\T = \tfrac{hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\big(a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2\big) > 0\) (step 5 of the same lemma).
  4. The sum of the entries is \(\tfrac{hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\,(2 + 1 + 1 + 2) = hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\). Step 1 gives the same for \(T_1 = T_2 = T\): by lemma 7.3 \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\), so \(\int hP\,T^2\,\mathrm{d}x = hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,T^2\).

Source: COOK §12.2, (12.2-4) and (12.2-6) (PDF p. 474), where the term with lateral convection is added to the matrix \([k]\) and where COOK compares it with the matrix of an elastic foundation. The proof in steps is ours.

We called the matrix the side matrix, because it comes from the loss through the lateral surface of the bar. Its integral is the same as for the consistent mass matrix of lemma 10.31, with \(\rho A\) replaced by \(hP\). There the matrix multiplies the acceleration, here it multiplies the temperature, so it plays the role of a stiffness, like an elastic foundation. In section 14.11 the same integral appears once more, there with the density and the specific heat, and it will multiply the time derivative. If the end of the fin were not insulated but gave off heat with heat transfer coefficient \(h\), we would add a term \(hA\) to the diagonal of node \(n\), as for a spring at a node (COOK, PDF p. 474). At \(mL = 1\) and \(hA\,L/(kA) = 1\) with two elements we get the temperatures \(0{,}6040\) and \(0{,}3656\) (in units of \(T_0\)) instead of \(0{,}7266\) and \(0{,}6428\) with the insulated tip. The difference is large because at \(mL = 1\) the number \(hL/k = 1\) means \(A = PL\) (divide \(hL/k\) by \((mL)^2 = hPL^2/(kA)\)): the tip has as much area as the whole lateral surface. For a slender fin with \(A = PL/20\), so \(hL/k = 0{,}05\), we get \(0{,}7162\) and \(0{,}6193\), close to the values with the insulated tip.

14.10.3 Assembly and comparison with the exact solution (sestavljanje in primerjava s točno rešitvijo)

Now we have everything. By theorem 7.13 the matrix of the structure is the sum of the expanded matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} + \mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\), then we prescribe \(T_0\) at node \(0\) and solve the reduced system (theorem 7.18). With \(n = 2\) elements and \(mL = 1\) we have \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/2\). In units of \(kA/L\) we have \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac{(mL)^2}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), because \(hP\,h_{\mathrm{e}}/6 = hP\,L/12 = (mL)^2\,\tfrac{kA}{12\,L}\). By SymPy the matrix of the structure is

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 13/6 & -23/12 & 0 \\ -23/12 & 13/3 & -23/12 \\ 0 & -23/12 & 13/6 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{14.55}\]

where for instance the middle entry is \(2 + 2 + \tfrac{2}{12} + \tfrac{2}{12} = \tfrac{13}{3}\), since both elements contribute to it, each with its term from \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and from \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\). The reduced system for \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) has the right-hand side \(-K_{10}\,T_0 = \tfrac{23}{12}\,T_0\) and \(0\) and the solution

\[T_1 = \tfrac{598}{823}\,T_0 = 0{,}72661\,T_0 , \qquad T_2 = \tfrac{529}{823}\,T_0 = 0{,}64277\,T_0 .\]

The heat that the support must supply is row \(0\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) times the vector of temperatures: \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = K_{00}\,T_0 + K_{01}\,T_1 = \tfrac{637}{823}\,\tfrac{kA\,T_0}{L} = 0{,}77400\,\tfrac{kA\,T_0}{L}\). The exact value is \(0{,}76159\). The exact temperature of the tip is \(0{,}64805\,T_0\), and the approximation gives \(0{,}64277\,T_0\).

We build the figure with two coordinate axes: \(x\) along the fin and the temperature vertically, starting at \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\). First we draw the exact curve of equation (14.51) at \(mL = 2\). It falls ever more slowly, because by Fourier's law the slope is proportional to the heat flowing through the cross-section, and there is less and less of it towards the tip: part has already left through the lateral surface, and nothing flows through the insulated end. Over it we lay the piecewise linear field with two elements and the field with four elements. The segments are straight lines through the nodes, each with its own constant gradient by lemma 14.53. At the nodes both fields lie slightly below the exact curve, with two elements by \(0{,}023\,T_0\) at the tip. In the middle of the elements, however, they lie above it, with two elements by at most \(0{,}071\,T_0\) at \(x = 0{,}215\,L\). The exact curve bends upwards, so the straight line between two of its points lies above it, and this outweighs the small deficit at the nodes (NumPy). On the right is the heat at the root: the dots are the values \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) for more and more elements, the grey line is the exact value \(Q\). The dots lie above the line and approach it with every doubling of the number of elements.

14.10.4 When the result is exact (kdaj je rezultat točen)

For a bar, chapter 6 showed that nodal displacements are exact if \(EA\) is constant on every element (theorem 6.38). We expect the same claim for temperature. The fin does not satisfy it, because it has a lateral loss, so first we look at a body without it.

Corollary 14.55 Exact nodal temperatures (točne vozliščne temperature)

Let a bar of length \(L\) have a temperature prescribed at \(x = 0\), no loss through the lateral surface, a product \(kA > 0\) that is piecewise constant, a given heat source per unit length and a given flow at the end \(x = L\). Let the nodes of a mesh of linear elements also lie at every jump of \(kA\). Then the temperature at every node equals the true temperature.

Proof

We must show that the weak form of this bar is the weak form of the bar of theorem 6.38 and that its assumptions hold.

  1. By theorem 14.49 the weak form is \(B(T, w) = \ell(w)\) with \(B(T, w) = \int_0^L kA\,T'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and a linear form \(\ell\) from the source and the end flow. By form this is the weak form of a bar in which \(kA\) stands for \(EA\) and the loads are the source and the end flow (chapter 6).
  2. The field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) of theorem 6.38 has \(kA\,G_{\mathrm{P}}' = 1\) on \((0, x_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(0\) after, and is continuous. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be a node. On every element \(kA\) is constant, so \(G_{\mathrm{P}}'\) is constant, hence \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is a straight line on every element.
  3. A field that is continuous and a straight line on every element is a combination of tent functions (definition 6.25), so it lies in the trial family.
  4. By theorem 6.38, therefore, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = T(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) at every node \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Exact nodal values for a bar with two-node elements are stated by ONATE1 (PDF p. 363), see the source of theorem 6.38.

We test the corollary on two examples and then at its limit.

A layer with a jump of conductivity. A bar has \(kA = 1\) on \([0, 0{,}4]\) and \(kA = 4\) on \([0{,}4, 1]\) (in arbitrary units, \(L = 1\)), \(T(0) = 0\) and a flow \(1\) entering at the end \(x = L\). The temperature rises with slope \(1/kA\), so \(T(0{,}4) = 0{,}4\), \(T(0{,}5) = 0{,}4 + 0{,}1/4 = 0{,}425\) and \(T(1) = 0{,}55\). The mesh with nodes \(0\), \(0{,}4\) and \(1\) has a node at the jump and gives \(0{,}4\) and \(0{,}55\), exactly as the corollary predicts. The mesh with nodes \(0\), \(0{,}5\) and \(1\) has the jump inside the first element, whose mean \(kA\) is \((0{,}4\cdot 1 + 0{,}1\cdot 4)/0{,}5 = 1{,}6\) (as for the bar, section 7.1.2), and gives \(T(0{,}5) = 0{,}3125\) instead of \(0{,}425\) (error \(-26{,}5\,\%\)) and \(T(1) = 0{,}4375\) instead of \(0{,}55\) (\(-20{,}5\,\%\)). The condition that the node lies at the jump is therefore not a refinement: where it holds the result is exact, where it fails the error is a fifth or more. The reason is in step 2: the field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) has a kink at the jump and is not a straight line inside the element.

A heat source. A bar has \(kA = 1\), \(L = 1\), temperature \(0\) at \(x = 0\), an insulated end and a uniform source \(1\) per unit length. The exact temperature is \(T = x - x^2/2\). With two elements we get \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(0{,}5) = 0{,}375\) and \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(1) = 0{,}5\), which is exact. In the middle of an element, at \(x = 0{,}25\), we get \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}1875\) instead of \(0{,}21875\), a difference \(0{,}03125 = g\,h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/(8\,kA)\) with source \(g = 1\) (computation in num-10.py). So the corollary concerns the nodes and no other points.

The fin, where the corollary does not hold. With the loss through the lateral surface the table above showed that the tip temperature is not exact (\(0{,}64277\) against \(0{,}64805\)). This does not contradict the corollary, because one assumption is missing. The field \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) with \(B(G_{\mathrm{P}}, w) = w(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) for every \(w\) now satisfies \(kA\,G_{\mathrm{P}}'' = hP\,G_{\mathrm{P}}\) on both sides of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and is there a combination of \(\cosh\) and \(\sinh\), not a straight line, so it does not lie in the family of piecewise linear fields and step 3 fails.

14.10.5 The heat at the root is above the exact value (toplota ob korenu je nad točno)

So the nodal temperatures of the fin are not exact. The heat \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) that the support must supply, however, has by the table an important property: it is always above the true value. We see why if we write it with energy.

Lemma 14.56 Heat at the root of a fin (toplota ob korenu rebra)

Let \(T\) be the exact temperature of the fin of section 14.10.1, \(Q = -kA\,T'(0)\) the heat supplied by the wall, \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) the finite element solution with linear elements on an arbitrary mesh with a node at \(x = 0\), and \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = (\mat K\,\vek T)_0\) row \(0\) of the system, that is, the heat that the support must supply. Then

\[Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q = \frac{B(T - T_{\mathrm{h}},\ T - T_{\mathrm{h}})}{T_0} \ge 0 , \tag{14.56}\]

with equality only for \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\). The heat at the root is thus never below the exact value.

Proof

We must show that \(B(T, T)\) and \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}})\) are \(T_0\) times the heat, and then use theorem 6.34.

  1. By theorem 14.22 \(kA\,T'' = hP\,T\). Hence \(B(T, T) = \int_0^L \big(kA\,T'\,T' + kA\,T''\,T\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (kA\,T'\,T)'\,\mathrm{d}x\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40) this is \(kA\,T'(L)\,T(L) - kA\,T'(0)\,T(0) = 0 + Q\,T_0\), because the end is insulated.
  2. By theorem 7.13 (claim 3) \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\). The theorem is stated for a bar without the side term, but the proof stays the same, because the side term is also an integral over \([0, L]\), the sum of the integrals over the elements that lemma 14.54 gives. For the field \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} T_{\mathrm{I}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\), therefore, \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{J}} K_{0\mathrm{J}}\,T_{\mathrm{J}} = B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_0)\). The reduced system (theorem 7.18) says \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) for every node \(i \ne 0\), since the right-hand side is zero.
  3. Hence \(B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, T_{\mathrm{h}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} T_{\mathrm{I}}\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = T_0\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}}, \psi_0) = T_0\,Q_{\mathrm{h}}\), because all other terms are zero by step 2 and \(T_0\) is the temperature at node \(0\).
  4. The functional is \(\Pi(v) = \tfrac12\,B(v, v)\), and \(T\) and \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) are admissible fields with the same temperature \(T_0\) at \(x = 0\). By claim 1 of theorem 6.34, \(\Pi(T_{\mathrm{h}}) - \Pi(T) = \tfrac12\,B(T_{\mathrm{h}} - T,\ T_{\mathrm{h}} - T)\).
  5. By steps 1 and 3 the left side equals \(\tfrac12\,T_0\,(Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q)\). Dividing by \(\tfrac12\,T_0 > 0\) gives equation (14.56). The right side is non-negative, because \(B\) is positive (section 14.10.1), and zero only for \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = T\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. For the general theorem on the energy of the Ritz solution see the source of theorem 6.34.

The proof showed that the excess heat is exactly the square of the energy norm of the error (definition 6.18), divided by \(T_0\). This explains the numbers in the table: at \(mL = 1\), \(n = 2\) we have \(B(T - T_{\mathrm{h}}, T - T_{\mathrm{h}})/T_0 = 0{,}01240\), the same as \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q\), and the same holds in all eight rows to the fifth decimal (num-10.py). Why the excess decreases by a factor of \(4\) when the elements are halved, the lemma does not say. Theorem 6.41 gives an upper bound for a bar without the side term: the square of the energy norm of the error is at most proportional to \(h^2\). The theorem does not cover the side term, and we do not prove a bound for it here. The table, however, shows ratios from \(3{,}98\) to \(4{,}02\), as for a decrease with \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\).

What does the lemma not say? It says nothing about the temperature of the tip. There \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) is below the exact value in all eight computations, but this is an observation, not a theorem: the temperature at one point is not an energy, and the Ritz method bounds the energy (theorem 6.34), not every value (as for the hanging bar in chapter 6, where the work of the weight is too small and the displacement of the end too large). For design this means: the finite element model overestimates the heat that the fin gives off and underestimates the temperature of the tip. The first is on the safe side if we ask whether the fin removes enough heat, the second is not on the safe side if we ask about overheating of the tip. For these two values of \(mL\) the error of the heat is below \(5\,\%\) already at two elements and below \(1{,}2\,\%\) at four. The error of the tip temperature, divided by the exact tip temperature, is only \(0{,}8\,\%\) and \(0{,}2\,\%\) at \(mL = 1\), but \(8{,}7\,\%\) at two and \(2{,}0\,\%\) at four elements at \(mL = 2\).

What have we gained and what is left? The conduction element is the bar element with the letters given a new meaning, so assembly, supports and the reduced system carry over without a new proof. The side matrix \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) is new, and with it two results: exactness of the nodal temperatures where \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is a straight line, and an upper bound for the heat at the root, where there is a functional. Three steps remain. Temperature changing in time brings one more matrix, which section 14.11 will multiply by the time derivative. Plane and spatial elements for heat are in chapter 15, where temperature is the first field on triangles and tetrahedra. Radiation and temperature-dependent conductivity we did not treat, because then the problem is nonlinear and the functional is no longer quadratic (COOK, PDF pp. 476-478).
