Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.8 Isolating the vibration of the engine

An engine that we have balanced as far as possible still shakes its block. Section 23.3 showed that the shaking force of one cylinder has harmonics at \(\Omega\) and \(2\Omega\), section 23.6 that a counterweight does not remove it, and section 23.7 that some inline engines still leave a shaking couple. The remainder must not reach the ground, that is the body or the foundation on which the engine is fixed. We therefore put mounts (podložke) between the engine and the ground. In chapter 9 we did this for a machine with one harmonic of the exciting force. The engine has several harmonics in the force, a couple besides the force, and in a four-stroke engine the couple even has half orders. So we ask two questions. Which force goes through the mounts into the ground when the exciting force is periodic with several harmonics? And what do the mounts do with the couple that the gas gives to the block? The first question concerns the force on the ground, which goes through the mounts, the second the block, which the gas turns.

The section has a limit. The mounts are linear springs with damping, the engine is a rigid body, and its motion in three directions and three rotations is a six-degree-of-freedom system. The sources of this book do not have it: CK section 4.4 (PDF pp. 114-118) treats one degree of freedom and NORTON only mentions engine mounts (PDF p. 783). We therefore treat two cases in which the system splits into one degree of freedom: bouncing along the cylinder axis and rocking about an axis through the centre of mass. The six-degree-of-freedom engine is a named gap (subsection 23.8.4). One degree of freedom is the displacement \(q\) of the block on the mounts.

23.8.1 The engine on mounts

We set up the model. Engine \(E\) of sections 23.2 and 23.3 is our model, its numbers are not from a source: \(a = 50\) mm, \(l = 200\) mm, \(\lambda = a/l = 0{,}25\), reciprocating mass \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}8\) kg, 1500 1/min, \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s. Its block together with all moving parts has the mass \(M = 250\) kg like machine \(B\) of example 9.79 and stands on the same mount with \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz, \(k = 246\,740\) N/m, \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) and \(c = 785{,}4\) N s/m. The cylinder axis is vertical, the ground is fixed, the angular speed \(\Omega\) is constant and the crankshaft (kolenasta gred) is balanced for its rotating mass (lemma 23.32), but there is no counterweight for the reciprocating mass (\(p = 0\)). The letters \(k\) and \(c\) mean here the stiffness and damping of the mounts, as in chapter 9, and \(\lambda\) is only the ratio \(a/l\), not an eigenvalue. The figure below shows the block on the mounts and the displacement \(q\).

We build figure 23.66 from below. First the ground, on which everything stands. On it we fix the mounts, the spring \(k\) on the left and the damper \(c\) on the right, because this is how chapter 9 describes a mount. The mounts carry the engine block of mass \(M\). At equilibrium they are compressed by the sag \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = Mg/k = 9{,}94\) mm, and since we measure displacements from the equilibrium position, we do not write the weight (as in example 9.76). In the figure the block is raised by the displacement \(q\) from the dashed equilibrium position. In the block is the cylinder, in it the piston with the piston pin \(B\) at the distance \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) from the shaft axis, and below it the crank \(OA\) of length \(a\) and the angle \(\theta\) from the cylinder axis. The conrod of length \(l\) joins the piston and the crank. We know all these parts from section 23.2. Finally we draw the forces. The force \(F\) is the force with which the moving parts (piston, conrod, crank) act on the block, that is the shaking force of definition 23.17, and it acts at the bearing \(O\) along the cylinder axis. It is positive in the direction from \(O\) toward the piston, as the definition says, so upward, in the direction of the displacement \(q\). At \(\theta = 50^\circ\) we have \(F = 1338\) N, so the arrow points upward. The force \(f_{\mathrm{t}}\) is the force that the mount transmits to the ground, measured from equilibrium. It is positive upward like \(q\): when the block rises by \(q\), the mount presses on the ground with a force \(k\,q\) smaller than at equilibrium, which is the same as an additional force \(k\,q\) upward. So we draw it upward from the ground. The gas in the cylinder is hidden in this figure: it acts on the piston and on the cylinder head with equal and opposite forces, so only inside the engine.

How does the displacement \(q\) of the block connect to the force \(F\)?

Lemma 23.51 Bounce of the engine on its mounts (odskakovanje motorja na podložkah)

Let the engine together with all its moving parts have the mass \(M\), let \(q\) be the vertical displacement of its block from the equilibrium position, which is along the cylinder axis, and let the crank turn with a prescribed constant angular speed \(\Omega\). The mounts have stiffness \(k\) and damping \(c\), the pins are frictionless and the cylinder walls smooth. Let

\[F(t) = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\ddot y_{\mathrm{i}} , \tag{23.40}\]

where \(y_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) is the position of the \(i\)-th moving part relative to the block along the cylinder axis. This is the shaking force (definition 23.17). Then

\[M\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = F(t) . \tag{23.41}\]
Proof

We must show that the total momentum of the engine changes only because of the mounts and that the relative motion of the parts does not depend on \(q\).

  1. Let \(M_{\mathrm{f}}\) be the mass of the block and \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) the mass of the \(i\)-th moving part, \(M = M_{\mathrm{f}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\). The absolute vertical position of part \(i\) is \(q + y_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\), that of the block is \(q\), as the dashed equilibrium position of the block shows.
  2. The constraint \(\theta = \Omega t\) is prescribed (a rheonomic constraint, definition 8.43), so the \(y_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) are known functions of time and the \(\ddot y_{\mathrm{i}}\) do not depend on \(q\). The crank turns by the prescribed angle and the piston with the rod follow it whatever the displacement \(q\) of the block.
  3. By d'Alembert's principle for a system (corollary 8.80) the sum of the external forces on the engine along the cylinder axis equals the sum of \(m_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\) over all parts, that is \(M\ddot q + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\ddot y_{\mathrm{i}}\), because the internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs.
  4. The external forces are only the mounts, \(-k\,q - c\,\dot q\), and the weight. The weight and the static force of the mounts cancel at equilibrium, as in step 3 of example 9.76. The forces of the gas, the bearings and the walls are internal, they appear as equal and opposite forces at the same point or along the same line (the gas on the piston and on the head along the axis), so they do not contribute to the sum.
  5. From steps 3 and 4 it follows that \(M\ddot q + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\ddot y_{\mathrm{i}} = -k\,q - c\,\dot q\), so with the definition (23.40) equation (23.41).

Source: Not from a source. The same argument is example 9.76 for an unbalanced mass, here with arbitrary moving parts. NORTON section 13.5 (PDF pp. 718-720) defines the shaking force as the force of the moving parts on the frame.

Equation (23.41) has the same form as the equation of chapter 9, only the force \(F\) is no longer harmonic. By theorems 23.19 and 23.12, for \(p = 0\) and a balanced rotating mass,

\[F(t) = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\sum_{\mathrm{n} \ge 1} A_{\mathrm{n}}\cos n\Omega t , \qquad A_1 = 1,\ A_2 = 0{,}254025,\ A_3 = 0,\ A_4 = -0{,}004098,\ A_6 = 0{,}000074 . \tag{23.42}\]

The coefficients \(A_{\mathrm{n}}\) are computed (NumPy, Fourier series of the exact piston acceleration) and not proved in closed form, the odd ones with \(n \ge 3\) are zero to \(10^{-16}\), which is theorem 23.12. The amplitudes are therefore \(F_1 = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N, \(F_2 = 564{,}1\) N, \(F_4 = 9{,}10\) N and \(F_6 = 0{,}165\) N. The second harmonic differs from \(\lambda\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 555{,}2\) N of section 23.3 by \(1{,}6\) %, because the two-term approximation by lemma 23.14 is only an approximation. The largest value of the force over one turn is \(2775{,}8\) N at top dead centre.

23.8.2 Transmission of the harmonics to the ground

The mount is linear and the force has several harmonics. The natural idea is to treat each harmonic as in chapter 9 and to add the responses. The idea needs a theorem, because transmissibility is defined there for a force \(Q_0\cos\Omega t\) without phase and with one harmonic. There is also the question of phase: the mount shifts each harmonic differently, so the peak of the sum is not necessarily the sum of the peaks. Figure 23.67 shows the harmonics and their sum.

We build figure 23.67 from the axis: horizontally the angle \(\Omega t\) over one turn of the shaft, above the force of the engine, below the force on the ground. Above we draw the harmonics \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\) of equation (23.42), each with its amplitude, and their sum, the shaking force \(F(t)\). Its peak \(2775{,}8\) N is at top dead centre, where both harmonics are at their peak. Below, each harmonic goes through the mount alone and is reduced by its own \(\mathrm{TR}(r_{\mathrm{n}})\), so the lower harmonics have only \(103{,}43\) N and \(8{,}06\) N. The mount also shifts each harmonic, each by a different time, which the arrow at the first one shows. Therefore their sum has no peak where the harmonics have their peaks together. The dashed lines are the sum of the amplitudes, \(\pm 111{,}54\) N, and the true peak of the sum, \(111{,}36\) N, is a little below it.

Theorem 23.52 Transmission of the harmonics through the mount (prenos harmonikov skozi podložko)

Let \(M > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\) be real numbers, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/M}\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{kM})\). Let \(\Omega > 0\) and

\[F(t) = F_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{N} F_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(n\Omega t + \alpha_{\mathrm{n}})\]

with numbers \(F_0\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{n}} \ge 0\). Let \(r_{\mathrm{n}} = n\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and for \(c = 0\) let \(r_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 1\) for every \(n \le N\). Then:

  1. The function \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = F_0/k + \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^N q_{\mathrm{n}}\) with the steady states \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\) for the harmonics is a solution of the equation \(M\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = F(t)\), and every solution is \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) plus a free motion.
  2. The force on the ground \(f_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,q_{\mathrm{p}} + c\,\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is
    \[f_{\mathrm{t}}(t) = F_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^N F_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{TR}(r_{\mathrm{n}})\cos(n\Omega t + \alpha_{\mathrm{n}} + \beta_{\mathrm{n}}) , \tag{23.43}\]
    where \(\mathrm{TR}(r)\) is the expression (9.77) at the given \(\zeta\) and \(\beta_{\mathrm{n}}\) is some number (a shift).
  3. The sum of the amplitudes is an upper bound: \(\max_{\mathrm{t}} |f_{\mathrm{t}}| \le |F_0| + \sum_{\mathrm{n}} F_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{TR}(r_{\mathrm{n}})\). The harmonic \(n\) is reduced exactly when \(r_{\mathrm{n}} > \sqrt2\).
Proof

We must show that each harmonic goes through the mount independently and that the amplitude of the force for each is the same as for a single harmonic.

  1. The equation is linear, so by lemma 9.6 the sum of the solutions for the forces \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) solves the equation for \(Q_1 + Q_2\). It is therefore enough to treat each term of the force separately.
  2. The constant \(F_0/k\) solves the equation with the force \(F_0\), because \(\dot q = \ddot q = 0\) and \(k\,(F_0/k) = F_0\). The corresponding force on the ground is \(k\,F_0/k + c\cdot 0 = F_0\), so without reduction (\(\mathrm{TR} = 1\) at \(r = 0\)).
  3. For the harmonic \(n\) we have \(n\Omega > 0\) and \(P(\mathrm{i}\,n\Omega) \ne 0\) (\(c > 0\) or \(r_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 1\)). By theorem 9.62, \(u_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(F_{\mathrm{n}} H(n\Omega)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\big)\) is the solution for the force \(F_{\mathrm{n}}\cos n\Omega t\).
  4. With the shift \(\tau_{\mathrm{n}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{n}}/(n\Omega)\) the function \(q_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = u_{\mathrm{n}}(t + \tau_{\mathrm{n}})\) is the solution for the force \(F_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(n\Omega t + \alpha_{\mathrm{n}})\), because by the chain rule (lemma 4.43) \(\dot q_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \dot u_{\mathrm{n}}(t + \tau_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(\ddot q_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \ddot u_{\mathrm{n}}(t + \tau_{\mathrm{n}})\), and the coefficients of the equation are constant. This is point 1 together with the free motion from point 2 of theorem 9.62.
  5. The force on the ground is linear in \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\), so it is the sum of the ground forces of the harmonics. For the harmonic \(n\), by definition 9.77 and step 2 of theorem 9.78, the force is \(f_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big((k + \mathrm{i}\,c\,n\Omega)\,F_{\mathrm{n}} H(n\Omega)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega(t + \tau_{\mathrm{n}})}\big)\), which has the amplitude \(F_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{TR}(r_{\mathrm{n}})\) and the shift \(\beta_{\mathrm{n}} = \arg\big((k + \mathrm{i}\,c\,n\Omega)\,H(n\Omega)\big)\). This is equation (23.43).
  6. For every \(t\) the sum lies below the bound, so \(|f_{\mathrm{t}}(t)| \le |F_0| + \sum_{\mathrm{n}} F_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{TR}(r_{\mathrm{n}})\), because \(|\cos| \le 1\) and \(\big|\sum a_{\mathrm{n}}\big| \le \sum |a_{\mathrm{n}}|\). The last statement is theorem 9.78 for \(r = r_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Source: CK (4.39)-(4.44), PDF p. 115, for the transmissibility of one harmonic. The addition of the harmonics and the upper bound are not from the source.

The theorem is for a finite sum, it does not need a Fourier series. The force of the engine has infinitely many harmonics, so we cut it at \(n = 8\). Theorem 9.126 claims the same for the coefficients of the response to a periodic force, but for the force on the ground the series is not treated, so the remainder of the harmonics above 8 is only computed (the coefficient \(A_8\) is \(10^{-6}\)), not proved.

We build figure 23.68 from the axes. Both are logarithmic, because \(\mathrm{TR}\) spans four orders of magnitude. Then we draw the transmissibility from theorem 9.78, blackwhite without damping and blue for \(\zeta = 0{,}05\). The dashed lines are \(r = \sqrt2\) and \(\mathrm{TR} = 1\): a harmonic to the left of the first is amplified and to the right it is reduced. Finally we put the orange dots on the blue curve, the harmonics \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4\) at \(r_{\mathrm{n}} = 5n\). The dot \(n = 3\) only shows the transmissibility at \(r = 15\), because the force has no third harmonic (\(A_3 = 0\) in equation (23.42)). Each higher harmonic lies lower, but not as fast as without damping. The reason is the approximation \(\mathrm{TR} \approx \sqrt{1/r^4 + 4\zeta^2/r^2}\) of chapter 9: without damping it falls as \(1/r^2\), and at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) above \(r = 1/(2\zeta) = 10\) the term \(2\zeta/r\) dominates, which falls only as \(1/r\). At \(r = 20\) the blue curve is therefore \(0{,}0056\) and the blackwhite one \(0{,}0025\).

Example 23.53 Ground force of engine \(E\) (sila motorja \(E\) v tla)

Engine \(E\) at 1500 1/min on mounts with \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz, so \(r_1 = 5\), \(r_2 = 10\) (computed with Python, NumPy, file num-08.out).

  1. By equation (23.42) the harmonics of the force are \(F_1 = 2220{,}7\) N, \(F_2 = 564{,}1\) N, \(F_4 = 9{,}10\) N, \(F_6 = 0{,}165\) N, \(F_3 = F_5 = 0\).
  2. By expression (9.77) the transmissibilities at \(\zeta = 0\) are \(0{,}04167\) (\(r = 5\)) and \(0{,}01010\) (\(r = 10\)), at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) they are \(0{,}04657\) and \(0{,}01428\). The ground forces are therefore \(92{,}53\) N and \(5{,}70\) N at \(\zeta = 0\), and \(103{,}43\) N and \(8{,}06\) N at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\).
  3. The harmonics \(n = 3\) to \(8\) add only \(0{,}052\) N to the sum of the amplitudes at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\): \(111{,}536\) N for \(n \le 8\) against \(111{,}484\) N for \(n \le 2\).
  4. The true peak of the ground force over one turn is \(111{,}36\) N, and the sum of the amplitudes \(111{,}54\) N is larger than it by \(0{,}2\) %, because the peaks of the harmonics do not happen at the same time (the shifts \(\beta_{\mathrm{n}}\) differ). Independently we solved the same equation (23.41) with the force for \(n \le 8\) step by step (Runge-Kutta of fourth order, step 0,2 ms, 5 s) and obtained the peak \(111{,}42\) N. The difference of \(0{,}06\) N is the remainder of the free motion, which has not quite died out after \(5\) s: after \(20\) s the same computation gives \(111{,}35\) N. Without mounts the ground would receive the peak \(2775{,}8\) N, so the mount reduced the force to \(4{,}0\) %.
  5. The amplitude of the block displacement at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) is \(X = F_1/|k - M\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c\,\Omega| = 0{,}3749\) mm, \(4\) % more than \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a/M = 0{,}360\) mm, which is the displacement of the block if the centre of mass of the engine stood still (as in example 9.79).

Isolation therefore works on each harmonic separately and mostly the first remains in the ground. Whether mounts with \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz are a wise choice is shown by the table. For several natural frequencies \(f_{\mathrm{n}}\) the peak of the ground force at 1500 1/min is computed (the rest as in example 23.53, harmonics up to \(n = 8\), \(\zeta = 0{,}05\)).

\(f_{\mathrm{n}}\) [Hz] 2,5 5 10 17,68 25 50
\(r_1 = 25/f_{\mathrm{n}}\) 10 5 2,5 1,414 1 0,5
ground force peak [N] 34,6 111,4 461,6 2303 22 509 7968
sag \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = g/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) [mm] 39,8 9,94 2,48 0,79 0,40 0,10

The table shows that the choice is limited from both sides. At \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 17{,}68\) Hz we have \(r_1 = \sqrt2\) (the dashed limit), so \(\mathrm{TR} = 1\) for the first harmonic, which goes into the ground unreduced (\(2220{,}7\) N), and the second at \(r_2 = 2{,}83\) is reduced only to \(15\) % (\(\mathrm{TR} = 0{,}148\)). The ground receives \(2303\) N, which is \(83\) % of the force without mounts (\(2775{,}8\) N). At \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 25\) Hz we have \(r_1 = 1\), the first harmonic is amplified \(10{,}05\) times and the peak is \(22\,509\) N, \(8{,}1\) times more than without mounts. At \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 50\) Hz we have \(r_2 = 1\), the second harmonic (\(564{,}1\) N) is amplified \(10{,}05\) times, the first has \(r_1 = 0{,}5\) and \(\mathrm{TR} = 1{,}33\), and the peak \(7968\) N is \(2{,}9\) times larger than the force without mounts. These are cases in which the condition \(r_{\mathrm{n}} > \sqrt2\) of theorems 9.78 and 23.52 does not hold for at least one harmonic. Softer mounts are not free, however: by equation (9.78) the sag is \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = g/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), at \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 2{,}5\) Hz it is \(39{,}8\) mm instead of \(9{,}94\) mm.

The engine finds the same fault at start-up. At 1500 1/min all is well, but on the way there the speed passes through \(r_1 = 1\) at \(300\) 1/min (turning frequency \(5\) Hz) and through \(r_2 = 1\) at \(150\) 1/min. At \(300\) 1/min we have \(F_1 = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 88{,}8\) N, which is \(25\) times less than at 1500 1/min, because \(F_1 \propto \Omega^2\). But \(\mathrm{TR} = 10{,}05\) amplifies it to \(892{,}7\) N on the ground, eight times more than at the working speed (\(111{,}4\) N). This is the steady state, which sets in if the engine runs at this speed for longer than a few time constants \(1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}64\) s, in which the free motion dies out. The limit \(r_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt2\) is at \(424{,}3\) 1/min for the first harmonic and at \(212{,}1\) 1/min for the second. For a four-cylinder inline engine NORTON (PDF p. 783) says that the second harmonic is heard at speeds where its frequency coincides with a natural frequency of the body. This is a phenomenon similar to ours: the force at each order finds its own \(r_{\mathrm{n}}\), and every \(r_{\mathrm{n}}\) near \(1\) is dangerous.

23.8.3 Rocking and the reaction torque

So far the block moved only up and down. The inline three-cylinder engine (section 23.7) has a primary force of zero, but a primary couple \(\sqrt3\,b\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 384{,}6\) N m, if its cylinders are like those of engine \(E\) with the pitch \(b = 0{,}1\) m. This couple turns the block. We model the block as machine \(F\) of example 10.8: mass \(250\) kg, moment of inertia \(J = 37{,}5\) kg m\(^2\) about an axis through the centre of mass and mounts at \(\pm 0{,}5\) m from the centre of mass, the rocking has the natural frequency \(6{,}455\) Hz. The letter \(J\) is here the moment of inertia of the block, not the reduced inertia of the mechanism \(J(\theta)\) of section 23.4.

We build figure 23.69 like this. Again the ground and on it the two mounts with equal stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{p}}\). On them we place the block with the mass \(M\), its centre of mass \(G\) and the moment of inertia \(J\) about the axis through \(G\). The dashed position is the equilibrium with the vertical axis through \(G\). The distances \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are horizontal from this axis to the mounts. The couple \(M_{\mathrm{m}}\) acts on the block and turns it by the angle \(\varphi\) between the dashed and the turned vertical. Because \(\varphi\) is small, each mount is compressed or stretched by \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi\), as in step 4 of example 10.8, and the springs give the block the moment \(-k_{\mathrm{p}}(x_1^2 + x_2^2)\,\varphi\) with zero vertical force if \(x_1 = -x_2\).

Example 23.54 Rocking of machine \(F\) under the primary couple of the three-cylinder engine (zibanje stroja \(F\) pod primarnim momentom trivaljnika)

With mounts at \(\pm 0{,}5\) m the matrices of machine \(F\) of example 10.8 are diagonal: \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(250, 37{,}5)\), \(\mat K = \mathrm{diag}(246\,740, 61\,685)\) in SI units. By theorem 10.3 the coordinates decouple, and the rocking \(\varphi\) is governed by an equation with one coordinate

\[J\ddot\varphi + K_\varphi\,\varphi = M_0\cos\Omega t , \qquad K_\varphi = 61\,685\ \text{N m} . \tag{23.44}\]

By theorem 23.40 the primary couple of the three-cylinder engine with the phases \(0°\), \(120°\), \(240°\) and pitch \(b\) is \(M_0 = \sqrt3\,b\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 384{,}6\) N m with the frequency \(25\) Hz, so \(r = 25/6{,}455 = 3{,}873\) (computed with Python, NumPy).

  1. By theorem 9.62 (with \(J\) in place of the mass) the amplitude of the rocking is \(\varphi_0 = M_0/|K_\varphi - J\Omega^2| = 384{,}6/|61\,685 - 925\,275| = 0{,}4454\) mrad. The term \(J\Omega^2\varphi_0 = 412{,}1\) N m is therefore larger than \(M_0\), because the block is above resonance and moves with the sign opposite to the couple.
  2. Each mount is compressed or stretched by \(0{,}5\,\varphi_0 = 0{,}223\) mm and gives the force \(k_{\mathrm{p}}\cdot 0{,}5\,\varphi_0 = 27{,}47\) N (\(k_{\mathrm{p}} = 123\,370\) N/m). The forces of the two mounts have opposite signs and together form the moment \(K_\varphi\varphi_0\), the sum of their magnitudes is \(54{,}9\) N.
  3. The static rotation under the same couple would be \(M_0/K_\varphi = 6{,}235\) mrad. The mount reduces the rocking to \(7\) % of this value, because \(r^2 - 1 = 14\), so \(\varphi_0/\varphi_{\mathrm{st}} = 1/14 = 0{,}071\).
  4. The rotation \(0{,}4454\) mrad is so small that \(\sin\varphi_0\) differs from \(\varphi_0\) by the relative amount \(\varphi_0^2/6 = 3\cdot 10^{-8}\), so the linearization is good enough, as in step 7 of example 10.8.

The force on the block is not the only source of vibration. The gas in the cylinder gives the shaft a torque, and by the law of action and reaction the block receives an opposite torque. We call it the reaction torque (reakcijski moment), because it is the reaction to the torque that the engine gives to the load. NORTON (PDF p. 713) says that the torque of the gas on the shaft is opposite to the torque with which the gas tries to turn the ground, and points to an engine that rocks on its mounts when the throttle is opened quickly. Let us show what this means and how large the torque is. We assume that the axis of the crankshaft passes through the centre of mass of the engine, because otherwise the rocking about this axis would be accompanied by bouncing (as with the non-diagonal \(\mat K\) in example 10.8). For this rotation we take the same \(J\) and mounts as for machine \(F\), which is our assumption.

We build figure 23.70 from the block with its centre of mass \(G\), standing on the mounts. Through the centre of mass passes the axis of the crankshaft \(z\), perpendicular to the figure. The moving parts, crank, rod and piston, turn about it. The gas drives the shaft with a torque, the load on the output shaft opposes it with the torque \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\). By the law of action and reaction a reaction torque acts on the block, opposite to the driving one, and the block turns by the angle \(\varphi\).

Lemma 23.55 Reaction torque of the engine (reakcijski moment motorja)

Let \(z\) be the axis of the crankshaft through the centre of mass of the engine, \(\varphi\) a small rotation of the block about it, \(J\) the moment of inertia of the engine (the block with all moving parts) about this axis, \(K_\varphi\) and \(C_\varphi\) the stiffness and damping of the mounts for this rotation, \(L(t)\) the angular momentum of the moving parts relative to the block about the axis and \(T_{\mathrm{L}}(t)\) the torque that the engine gives to the load on the output shaft. Let the pins be frictionless, the cylinder walls smooth, the weight negligible (\(m_{\mathrm{B}} g\) is \(0{,}8\) % of \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2\)) and \(J\) constant. In engine \(E\) the reciprocating mass moves between the distances \(l - a\) and \(l + a\) from the axis, so it changes \(J\) by at most \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\big((l + a)^2 - (l - a)^2\big) = 4\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a l = 0{,}072\) kg m\(^2\), that is \(0{,}19\) % of \(37{,}5\) kg m\(^2\). Then

\[J\ddot\varphi + C_\varphi\dot\varphi + K_\varphi\,\varphi = -\big(\dot L + T_{\mathrm{L}}\big) . \tag{23.45}\]
Proof

We must show that the right-hand side is the total moment of all moving parts and of the load on the block. We use Lagrange's equation for the coordinate \(\varphi\).

  1. The coordinates are \(\theta\) (the crank relative to the block) and \(\varphi\) (the block about \(z\)). The position of every point is \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}} = R(\varphi)\,\vek\rho_{\mathrm{i}}(\theta)\) with the rotation \(R(\varphi)\) about \(z\) and the position \(\vek\rho_{\mathrm{i}}\) relative to the block.
  2. The kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}|\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{i}}|^2\) (theorem 8.97) does not depend on \(\varphi\), because turning all points by the same angle does not change the length of any velocity, and \(\partial\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial\varphi = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. Therefore \(\partial T/\partial\dot\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{i}}\), which is the total angular momentum about \(z\). Since \(\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{i}} = R(\varphi)\big(\dot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek\rho_{\mathrm{i}} + \dot{\vek\rho}_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\), it is exactly \(J\dot\varphi + L\), with \(J\) at the current position of the parts.
  4. A virtual rotation of the whole by \(\delta\varphi\) moves each particle by \(\delta\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\). The internal forces act in pairs at the same point (pins, walls) or along the same line (gas, along the cylinder axis), so their virtual work is zero. The work of the mounts is \(-(K_\varphi\varphi + C_\varphi\dot\varphi)\,\delta\varphi\) and the work of the load on the output shaft, which turns with the block, is \(-T_{\mathrm{L}}\,\delta\varphi\). The generalized force for \(\varphi\) is the sum (definition 8.51, lemma 8.52).
  5. Lagrange's equation for \(\varphi\) (theorem 8.99) is \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\partial T/\partial\dot\varphi - \partial T/\partial\varphi = Q_\varphi\), with \(\partial T/\partial\varphi = 0\), steps 3 and 4 and a constant \(J\) it gives \(J\ddot\varphi + \dot L = -K_\varphi\varphi - C_\varphi\dot\varphi - T_{\mathrm{L}}\). This is equation (23.45).

Source: Not from a source. NORTON (PDF p. 713) says the same for the gas: the torque of the gas on the ground is opposite to the driving torque on the shaft.

Equation (23.45) has three consequences. The first is static. Since \(L\) is periodic in steady running (with the period of one or two turns), the mean of \(\dot L\) is zero, so the mean torque on the block opposes the mean torque of the load \(\overline{T_{\mathrm{L}}}\) and the rotation of the block is \(-\overline{T_{\mathrm{L}}}/K_\varphi\). By corollary 23.26 the mean torque of engine \(E\) by the gas model is \(84{,}61\) N m, the static rotation \(-1{,}372\) mrad (opposite to the direction of turning of the shaft) and each mount carries an additional force of \(84{,}61\) N.

The second consequence tells which orders appear. The gas repeats its course after two turns (four-stroke engine, section 23.4), so the right-hand side has half orders besides the whole ones. The moving parts of the mechanism give only whole orders at constant speed, because \(J(\theta)\) is periodic with the turn. The half order of \(\dot L\) therefore comes from the flywheel: at constant load \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\) and smooth running (\(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} \le 0{,}02\), section 23.5) the half order in \(\dot L \approx I\ddot\theta\) equals the half order of the gas torque by the engine equation of section 23.4. The share of the mechanism in \(I\) is at most \(J_{\mathrm{m}}/I = 0{,}007024/1{,}6721 = 0{,}42\) %, which is the error of this estimate. The half orders of the gas torque (Fourier analysis, our model) are \(161{,}8\) N m at order \(0{,}5\), \(111{,}6\) N m at \(1{,}5\) and \(49{,}9\) N m at \(2{,}5\), at every speed. The flywheel smooths the turning of the shaft but not of the block: the torque \(I\ddot\theta\) that evens out the speed is in equation (23.45) exactly what rocks the block.

We build figure 23.71 from the axis: horizontally the angle of the shaft over two turns, because the gas repeats only after \(720^\circ\). Above we draw the gas torque of the gas model, through it the dashed mean \(84{,}61\) N m, which carries the static consequence. Below we split the torque into two parts about zero. The half orders have the period of two turns and at most \(161{,}8\) N m at order \(0{,}5\), the whole orders repeat every turn.

The third consequence is the choice of speed. At the working speed 1500 1/min (\(25\) Hz) the half order is at \(12{,}5\) Hz and \(r = 12{,}5/6{,}455 = 1{,}94\). At the low speed \(n_{\mathrm{p}} = 800\) 1/min, which is near the idle speed (idle, prazni tek: the engine runs almost without load), the half order is at \(6{,}667\) Hz. Our gas model has no partial load, so here we compute with full gas: this is an engine under full load at low speed, not idling in the true sense, and the numbers are an upper estimate if the gas is weaker.

We build figure 23.72 with the angle as a function of speed. The curve is the amplitude \(\varphi_0 = 161{,}8/|K_\varphi - J\Omega_{1/2}^2 + \mathrm{i}\,C_\varphi\Omega_{1/2}|\) with \(\Omega_{1/2} = 2\pi\cdot 0{,}5\,n/60\) and \(C_\varphi\) from \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) for the rocking (our assumption). It has its peak just below the speed \(n_{\mathrm{r}} = 2\cdot 387{,}3 = 774{,}6\) 1/min, at which the half order equals the natural frequency of the rocking \(6{,}455\) Hz. By theorem 9.66 the largest amplitude is at \(r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} = 0{,}9975\), that is at \(772{,}7\) 1/min. The low speed \(800\) 1/min is only \(3{,}3\) % above \(n_{\mathrm{r}}\) (\(r = 1{,}033\)) and the amplitude there is \(21{,}3\) mrad, while the working speed has \(0{,}951\) mrad, \(22\) times less. The dashed limit \(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the rotation at which one mount stretches by the sag, \(0{,}5\,\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 9{,}94\) mm.

This is not a coincidence of one engine. The condition \(r_{\mathrm{n}} > \sqrt2\) of theorem 23.52 holds for every order, not only for the working speed, and the half order is always present in the gas of a four-stroke engine. The working speed saved it, the low one did not. For \(r_{1/2} > \sqrt2\) at \(800\) 1/min the frequency of the rocking would have to be lower than \(6{,}667/\sqrt2 = 4{,}714\) Hz, that is \(K_\varphi = 32\,899\) N m (\(k_{\mathrm{p}} = 65\,797\) N/m), with a bounce frequency of \(3{,}651\) Hz. The amplitude at \(800\) 1/min would be \(4{,}87\) mrad instead of \(21{,}3\) mrad, the price is a sag of \(18{,}6\) mm instead of \(9{,}94\) mm. The choice between the two is the decision of the designer, not the result of a computation.

At \(800\) 1/min the linear model itself shows its limit. Each mount would move by \(10{,}67\) mm, more than the sag \(9{,}94\) mm, and its force \(1316\) N exceeds the static load \(Mg/2 = 1226\) N. In part of the oscillation the mount would therefore stretch beyond its unloaded length and pull the block down. A mount that only presses on the block cannot do this, so the model with a linear spring no longer holds there. We therefore do not trust the number \(21{,}3\) mrad, we trust only that the response there is much larger than at the working speed. The angle approximation itself holds: \(\sin\varphi_0/\varphi_0 - 1 = -7{,}6\cdot 10^{-5}\) at \(\varphi_0 = 21{,}3\) mrad, but the linearity of the mount does not.

23.8.4 The gap

We treated two decoupled cases, bouncing and rocking. A real engine has six degrees of freedom (three displacements and three rotations) and mounts in several directions, the matrix \(\mat K\) is then \(6 \times 6\) and not diagonal. Theorem 10.72 for the response to a harmonic force still holds, but we have no source that would tell how to choose the mounts so that no order falls near any of the six natural frequencies. CK section 4.4 (PDF pp. 114-118) is single-degree-of-freedom and NORTON only mentions engine mounts (PDF p. 783), so the design of mounts and the six-degree-of-freedom system are a gap of this book. The models of the damping of the mounts, which we replaced here with a viscous damper, should be treated by chapter 20.
