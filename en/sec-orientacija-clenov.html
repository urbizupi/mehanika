Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.2 Orientation of links (orientacija členov)

A robot arm is a chain of links, and at the end sits a link with a tool, for example a welding torch or a screwdriver. The chosen point of this link we called the gripper (prijemalo, definition 27.1). The torch must stand at a right angle to the seam, the screwdriver must point straight into the screw. So for every link we ask where it is and which way it faces. In this section we treat the second, the facing, which is called the orientation (orientacija): the word says which way a body is turned, not where it is. The position we add in the next section.

The model is the one the chapter on the rigid body sets up: the links are rigid (definition 1.11), so the points of a link keep their mutual distances. Because of the rigidity it is enough to follow one coordinate system, which we fix to the link and which moves with it. We call it the frame of a link (okvir člena), because in it, as in the frame of a picture, we measure all the points of the link, and the frame moves with the link like a frame with its picture. In this chapter frames are right-handed orthogonal Cartesian coordinate systems (definition 2.32) with the same unit of length. Frame 0 is fixed to the base of the robot and stands still, frame 1 is fixed to the link we watch.

Rigidity is an assumption, so let us say what it costs. It is right as long as a link bends under its load by less than the accuracy we ask of the robot. It fails for long slender arms and heavy loads, where the end of a link visibly sags. Its effect on the result is a turn of the frame that the model does not see: if the end of a link tilts by \(0{,}001\) rad because of bending, the tip of a tool \(0{,}1\) m further on moves by the arc \(0{,}1 \cdot 0{,}001 = 0{,}0001\) m, a tenth of a millimetre (an arc is the radius times the angle in radians). As long as this is below the required accuracy, a link has one orientation and one frame is enough.

We begin with a rotation in the plane, where we know what a rotation is (lemma 8.41). Figure 27.14 looks along the axis \(z\), which points toward us.

First we draw the axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) of frame 0, which is the base of the robot. The link has the axes \(x_1\) and \(y_1\) of frame 1, turned from the axis \(y_0\) by the angle \(\theta\). Where the axis \(x_1\) points relative to frame 0 is told by two numbers, how far it points in the direction \(x_0\) and how far in the direction \(y_0\). So we drop the perpendiculars from the tip of the axis \(x_1\) onto the axes of frame 0 and read off \(\cos 30^\circ = 0{,}866\) and \(\sin 30^\circ = 0{,}5\). These are the components of the unit vector of the axis \(x_1\) in frame 0 (the scalar product with the unit vectors of the axes, definition 2.8). In the same way the perpendiculars from the axis \(y_1\) give the components \(-0{,}5\) and \(0{,}866\). We complete both triples with the axis \(z\) and put them into a matrix, one column for each axis of the link.

Definition 27.8 Orientation matrix (matrika orientacije)

Let \(i\) and \(j\) be frames with the same unit of length and \(\vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{z}}\) the unit vectors of the axes of frame \(j\). The orientation matrix of frame \(j\) relative to frame \(i\) is the \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat R^{\ix{i}}_{\mathrm{j}}\) whose columns hold the components of these three vectors in frame \(i\):

\[\mat R^{\ix{i}}_{\mathrm{j}} = \begin{bmatrix} \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{x}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{x}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{x}} \\ \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{y}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{y}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{y}} \\ \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{z}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{z}} & \vek e^{j}_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\vek e^{i}_{\mathrm{z}} \end{bmatrix} . \tag{27.4}\]

The upper index tells the frame in which the components are written, the lower index the frame whose axes stand in the columns. The matrix is an object of size \(3 \times 3\) that describes the orientation, not the position.

Source: SPONG (2.1), (2.2) (PDF p. 61-63), where the matrix for the plane and for space is written with \(\mat R^0_1\) and columns with the components of the axes of frame 1 in frame 0.

This matrix is the transpose of the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\) of definition 2.44, which has the new axes in its rows (lemma 27.9 proves it). From now on in this chapter we write \(\mat R\) for the orientation matrix, always with both indices. The letter \(\mat R\) is used in the book also for the rotation in the decomposition of the deformation gradient (section 2.13.4) and as \(\mat R(\varphi)\) for the planar \(2 \times 2\) rotation in equation (8.28). We shall not meet them here, and the matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) of definition 8.39 is the same matrix as \(\mat R^0_1(t)\) of a link that lay on frame 0 at time \(0\) and turns about its origin (see the end of the proof of lemma 27.9).

What does the matrix say about the vectors of a link, for example about the vector from one point of the link to another? Let such a vector have the components \(\vek p^{1}\) in frame 1, a column of three numbers, and the components \(\vek p^{0}\) in frame 0. The orientation matrix converts the first into the second. This and the properties of the matrix are stated by the next lemma.

Lemma 27.9 Orientation matrix and turning the axes (matrika orientacije in zasuk osi)

Let 0 and 1 be frames as above and \(\mat Q\) the rotation matrix from frame 0 to frame 1 (definition 2.44). Then:

  1. \(\mat R^0_1 = \mat Q^\T\) (the columns are the axes of frame 1).
  2. \(\mat R^0_1\) is an orthogonal matrix with determinant \(1\) (the axes of both frames are unit, orthogonal and right-handed).
  3. Let a vector have the components \(\vek p^{1}\) in frame 1 and \(\vek p^{0}\) in frame 0. Then \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_1\,\vek p^{1}\) and \(\vek p^{1} = (\mat R^0_1)^\T\,\vek p^{0}\).
  4. \(\mat R^1_0 = (\mat R^0_1)^\T = (\mat R^0_1)^{-1}\) (frame 0 is turned relative to frame 1 by the same angle in the other direction).
Proof

We must show four points. The first is a comparison of columns with rows, the second a restatement of the properties of \(\mat Q\), the third the rule for components in turned axes, the fourth an exchange of frames.

  1. The element in row \(k\) and column \(l\) of \(\mat R^0_1\) is by definition 27.8 the scalar product \(\vek e^{1}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\vek e^{0}_{\mathrm{k}}\). The element in row \(l\) and column \(k\) of \(\mat Q\) is by definition 2.44 \(Q_{\mathrm{lk}} = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{l}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the old frame 0 and the new frame 1, so the same number. The elements \((k, l)\) of \(\mat R^0_1\) and \((l, k)\) of \(\mat Q\) are equal, hence \(\mat R^0_1 = \mat Q^\T\) (definition 1.101).
  2. The matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (lemma 2.47), so \(\mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\). For \(\mat R = \mat Q^\T\) we have \(\mat R^\T = \mat Q\), so \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat Q\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) and \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\). This is orthogonality (definition 2.46).
  3. Determinant: \(\det\mat R = \det\mat Q^\T = \det\mat Q\) (lemma 2.73, point 6). By section 2.10.3, the paragraph after theorem 2.74, \(\det\mat Q = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}}\times\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}})\), which is \(\pm 1\), and \(1\) when the new frame is right-handed. Frame 1 is right-handed by convention, so \(\det\mat R^0_1 = 1\).
  4. By lemma 2.45, point 2, for the old component \(\vek a = \vek p^{0}\) and the new \(\bar{\vek a} = \vek p^{1}\) we have \(\vek p^{0} = \mat Q^\T\vek p^{1} = \mat R^0_1\vek p^{1}\). Point 1 of the same lemma gives \(\vek p^{1} = \mat Q\,\vek p^{0} = (\mat R^0_1)^\T\vek p^{0}\).
  5. We exchange the frames. Since \(\vek p^{1} = \mat R^1_0\,\vek p^{0}\) for every vector by step 4 applied with the roles exchanged, and also \(\vek p^{1} = (\mat R^0_1)^\T\vek p^{0}\), the matrices \(\mat R^1_0\) and \((\mat R^0_1)^\T\) are equal on the columns \(\vek p^{0} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), hence equal. Since \(\mat R^0_1\) is orthogonal, \((\mat R^0_1)^\T\) is its inverse matrix (step 2 of this proof and definition 1.110).
  6. Let the link lie on frame 0 at time \(0\) and turn about the origin of frame 0, so that the two frames always have a common origin. The vector from this origin to a point of the link has the components \(\vek p^{1} = \vek X\) in frame 1, which are constant because frame 1 moves with the link. The position of the point at time \(t\) is \(\vek p^{0}(t) = \mat R^0_1(t)\,\vek X\). This is the rigid motion \(\vek x(t) = \vek c(t) + \mat Q(t)\vek X\) of definition 8.39 with \(\vek c = \vek 0\), so in this case \(\mat Q(t) = \mat R^0_1(t)\).

Source: SPONG (2.9) (PDF p. 67) for the components, orthogonality and determinant 1 after SPONG p. 62-63. The proof from the rotation matrix of the book is not from the source.

In the figure above \(\mat R^0_1\) is the matrix

\[\mat R^0_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}866 & -0{,}5 & 0 \\ 0{,}5 & 0{,}866 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

the first column holds the components of the axis \(x_1\), the second of the axis \(y_1\). The frames of the figure have a common origin, so the vector from it to a point of the link is a vector of point 3 of the lemma. If it has the components \(\vek p^{1} = (0{,}4;\ 0{,}3;\ 0{,}2)\) m in frame 1, it has by point 3 in frame 0 the components \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_1\vek p^{1} = (0{,}1964;\ 0{,}4598;\ 0{,}2000)\) m. The vector is equally long in both frames, \(|\vek p^{1}| = |\vek p^{0}| = 0{,}5385\) m, as it must be for a rigid link: \(|\mat R\vek p|^2 = \vek p\cdot(\mat R^\T\mat R\,\vek p) = |\vek p|^2\) by equation (2.137) and orthogonality.

Why multiplying by the matrix gives the right components is shown in figure 27.15. It draws again frame 0 and frame 1, turned by \(30^\circ\). The vector \(\vek p\) is an arrow drawn on the link. In frame 1 we reach its tip by going \(0{,}4\) m along the axis \(x_1\) and then \(0{,}3\) m along the axis \(y_1\), the third \(0{,}2\) m goes along the axis \(z\) towards us. So the vector is the sum of \(0{,}4\) of the axis \(x_1\), \(0{,}3\) of the axis \(y_1\) and \(0{,}2\) of the axis \(z_1\). The axes of frame 1 are in frame 0 the columns of the matrix \(\mat R^0_1\), so \(\vek p^{0} = 0{,}4\,(0{,}866;\ 0{,}5;\ 0) + 0{,}3\,(-0{,}5;\ 0{,}866;\ 0) + 0{,}2\,(0;\ 0;\ 1)\) m. The product \(\mat R^0_1\vek p^{1}\) computes exactly this sum of the columns multiplied by the components. The perpendiculars from the tip onto the axes of frame 0 read it off: \(0{,}4 \cdot 0{,}866 - 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}5 = 0{,}196\) m and \(0{,}4 \cdot 0{,}5 + 0{,}3 \cdot 0{,}866 = 0{,}460\) m. The arrow did not move in this, we only changed the frame in which we measure it.

A robot is built from links, so we shall compose rotations many times, and each is a rotation about one axis. We give these rotations names.

Definition 27.10 Basic rotations (osnovni zasuki)

For an angle \(\theta\) in radians the basic rotations about the axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are the matrices

\[\mat R_{\mathrm{x},\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} , \quad \mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} , \quad \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{27.5}\]

The matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\) is the orientation matrix of the frame that we get from frame 0 by turning by the angle \(\theta\) about its axis \(a\) in the direction of a right-handed screw: seen from the tip of the axis \(a\) toward the origin, the rotation at \(\theta > 0\) is counterclockwise. At \(\theta = 90^\circ\) the axis that follows \(a\) in the order \(x \to y \to z \to x\) goes to the place of the axis after it (at \(a = z\), \(x\) goes to \(y\)).

Source: SPONG (2.3) (PDF p. 64) for \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) and p. 65 for \(\mat R_{\mathrm{x},\theta}\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\). The matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) is the same rotation as in lemma 8.41.

The columns are read off figure 27.14: for \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\) they are precisely the components of the axes \(x_1\) and \(y_1\), \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) and \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\), and the axis \(z\) stays. In a rotation about \(x\) the axis \(x\) stays and the axis \(y\) goes toward the axis \(z\), in a rotation about \(y\) the axis \(y\) stays and the axis \(z\) goes toward the axis \(x\). This is the same picture with cyclically renamed axes, so the columns are the same with the places exchanged accordingly. The sign of the direction of rotation, into which axis the first axis in the order goes, is a convention, and the right-handed screw is the same convention as for the right-handed system (definition 2.32).

Lemma 27.11 Properties of the basic rotations (lastnosti osnovnih zasukov)

For every axis \(a \in \{x, y, z\}\) and all angles \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\):

  1. \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\varphi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta+\varphi}\) (two rotations about the same axis add up).
  2. \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},0} = \mat I\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},-\theta} = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}^\T = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}^{-1}\) (a rotation by \(-\theta\) returns the axes).
  3. \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\) is orthogonal and has determinant \(1\) (the axes stay unit, orthogonal and right-handed).
Proof

We must show three points. One computation for \(z\) is enough, since \(x\) and \(y\) have the same form with cyclically exchanged indices.

  1. By definition 1.99 the upper left \(2 \times 2\) block of the product \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\) is
    \[\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\cos\varphi - \sin\theta\sin\varphi & -\cos\theta\sin\varphi - \sin\theta\cos\varphi \\ \sin\theta\cos\varphi + \cos\theta\sin\varphi & -\sin\theta\sin\varphi + \cos\theta\cos\varphi \end{bmatrix} ,\]
    and the third row and third column are \((0, 0, 1)\). By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16) the elements are \(\cos(\theta + \varphi)\), \(-\sin(\theta + \varphi)\), \(\sin(\theta + \varphi)\) and \(\cos(\theta + \varphi)\), so the product is \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta+\varphi}\).
  2. At \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\sin 0 = 0\) (lemma 8.11, point 2), so \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},0} = \mat I\). By point 1, \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\theta} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},0} = \mat I\), and the same in the opposite order, so \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\theta}\) is the inverse matrix. Since \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\) (lemma 8.11, point 4), \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\theta}\) is element by element the transpose \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}^\T\).
  3. From point 2, \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}^\T\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}^\T = \mat I\), which is orthogonality. By definition 2.72, \(\det\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta} = \cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta\cdot 1 - (-\sin\theta)\sin\theta\cdot 1 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), since the other four terms are zero (lemma 8.11, point 1).

Source: The proof is not from the source. SPONG (2.4)-(2.6) (PDF p. 64) states the properties for \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta}\).

An arm has several links, and each turns relative to the previous one. Frame 2 we fix to the next link, for example to the gripper. What is its orientation relative to the base if we know the orientation of frame 1 relative to frame 0 and of frame 2 relative to frame 1? And what if we turn about an axis of frame 0 instead of an axis of the link that has already turned? Let us first look at both possibilities in pictures, then compute them.

In the upper row the initial frame first turns about its axis \(z\) by \(90^\circ\). The axis \(x\) of the gripper now lies along the axis \(y_0\), the axis \(y\) along \(-x_0\), the axis \(z\) stays. Then we turn it about its own axis \(x\), which now lies along \(y_0\). After this rotation the final frame is: the axis \(x\) of the gripper is still along \(y_0\), the axis \(y\) has gone along \(z_0\), the axis \(z\) along \(x_0\). In the lower row we take the same start and the rotations in the other order. First about its own axis \(x\): the axis \(x\) stays, the axis \(y\) goes along \(z_0\) and the axis \(z\) along \(-y_0\). Then about its own axis \(z\), which now points along \(-y_0\). The final frame below has the axis \(x\) along \(z_0\), the axis \(y\) along \(-x_0\) and the axis \(z\) along \(-y_0\). The point on the axis \(x\) ends in two different places: the rotations cannot be exchanged without consequences. The computation confirms this and tells how we compose rotations.

Lemma 27.12 Composition of orientations (sestavljanje orientacij)

Let 0, 1 and 2 be frames as above. Then:

  1. \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R^0_1\,\mat R^1_2\) and \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_1\mat R^1_2\,\vek p^{2}\) (the matrix from frame 0 to 1, then from 1 to 2).
  2. If frame 2 is obtained from frame 1 by a basic rotation about its own axis \(a\) by the angle \(\theta\), then \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R^0_1\,\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\) (a rotation about a current axis multiplies from the right).
  3. If frame 2 is obtained by turning the link with frame 1 about the axis \(a\) of frame 0 by the angle \(\theta\), then \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\,\mat R^0_1\) (a rotation about a fixed axis multiplies from the left).
Proof

We must show three points. The first two are rules for components, the third uses that in a rotation of a rigid link all the vectors of the link are mapped by the same matrix.

  1. By point 3 of lemma 27.9, \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_1\vek p^{1}\), \(\vek p^{1} = \mat R^1_2\vek p^{2}\) and \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_2\vek p^{2}\) for every vector. Substituting, \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_1(\mat R^1_2\vek p^{2})\) and by the associativity of the matrix product \(= (\mat R^0_1\mat R^1_2)\vek p^{2}\). This and \(\mat R^0_2\vek p^{2}\) are equal for every \(\vek p^{2}\), so also for \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which select the columns. The matrices are equal.
  2. The axes of frame 2 have in frame 1 the components of the columns of the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\) by definition 27.10, applied with frame 1 instead of 0, so \(\mat R^1_2 = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\). Point 1 gives \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R^0_1\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\).
  3. A rotation of the link about the axis \(a\) of frame 0 is a rigid motion of definition 8.39 with \(\vek c = \vek 0\) and \(\mat Q = \mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\), since for a frame 0 that lay on the link the columns of the basic rotation are the new positions of the axes by definition 27.10, (27.5) (step 6 of the proof of lemma 27.9). So every vector of the link with components \(\vek a\) in frame 0 goes into the vector with components \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\vek a\), because the motion maps the difference of two points of a rigid body with the same matrix \(\mat Q\) (the difference of the points \(\vek x_1 - \vek x_2 = \mat Q(\vek X_1 - \vek X_2)\)).
  4. The axes of frame 1 are before the rotation vectors of the link with components in the columns of the matrix \(\mat R^0_1\). After the rotation these are the columns \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}\) is column \(k\) of \(\mat R^0_1\). These are the columns of the product \(\mat R_{\mathrm{a},\theta}\mat R^0_1\), and these are the axes of frame 2 by definition 27.8.

Source: SPONG (2.17) (PDF p. 71) for point 1 and point 2, (2.20), (2.22), (2.23) (PDF p. 73-74) for point 3. The proof of point 3 is not from the source. SPONG derives it with the similarity transformation (2.12), (2.20).

Why does a rotation about a current axis multiply from the right and a rotation about a fixed axis from the left? In point 2 the new rotation says where the axes of frame 2 are in frame 1. So the column \(\vek p^{2}\) must first go into frame 1 and only then into frame 0, and the new matrix stands on the right, next to the column. In point 3 the new rotation takes hold of a link that is already described in frame 0: the matrix \(\mat R^0_1\) first carries the column into frame 0, the basic rotation then turns it there, and the new matrix stands on the left. The matrix nearer to the column acts first. Now we can compute figure 27.16. This is also an example in which the rule for the order fails if we forget it.

Example 27.13 Order of rotations (vrstni red zasukov)

A gripper has the frame 0 at the start. We turn it first about its own axis \(z\) by \(90^\circ\) and then about its own axis \(x\) by \(90^\circ\) (upper row of figure 27.16). Then the same in the reverse order (lower row).

  1. By point 2 of lemma 27.12, above \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\). From equation (27.5) at \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\) and \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\) we have \(\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\), so
    \[\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]
    The columns are \((0, 1, 0)\), \((0, 0, 1)\) and \((1, 0, 0)\): the axis \(x\) of the gripper is along \(y_0\), the axis \(y\) along \(z_0\), the axis \(z\) along \(x_0\), as in the upper figure.
  2. Below, \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\),
    \[\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\]
    with the columns \((0, 0, 1)\), \((-1, 0, 0)\) and \((0, -1, 0)\), as in the lower figure. The matrices are different.
  3. A point on the axis \(x\) of the gripper at the distance \(0{,}1\) m has in the frame of the gripper the components \(\vek p^{2} = (0{,}1;\ 0;\ 0)\) m, a tenth of the first column. Above, \(\vek p^{0} = \mat R^0_2\vek p^{2} = (0;\ 0{,}1;\ 0)\) m, below \((0;\ 0;\ 0{,}1)\) m. The distance between the ends is \(\sqrt{0{,}1^2 + 0{,}1^2} = 0{,}1414\) m, more than the \(0{,}1\) m the point has from the origin. The two positions differ in gripping, and a matrix with the factors exchanged would turn the gripper the wrong way.
  4. The same two rotations made about the axes of frame 0, in the same order (first \(z_0\), then \(x_0\)), give by point 3 of lemma 27.12 \(\mat R^0_2 = \mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\), which is the matrix of step 2. In the figure the frame after the first rotation is the one in the middle of the upper row, with the axis \(x\) of the gripper on \(y_0\). The second rotation now goes about \(x_0\) and not about the axis \(x\) of the gripper, so it lifts this axis from \(y_0\) to \(z_0\), and the final frame is the one of the lower row. So the same rotations in the same order give a different orientation depending on whether the axes are current or fixed.

A rotation matrix has nine numbers, and the orientation of a body in the plane is fixed by one angle. How many numbers do we need in space? The orthogonality \(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\) gives six equations: three for the lengths of the columns and three for the perpendicularity of pairs of columns. So the nine numbers are not free, and SPONG (PDF p. 75) counts three. The next theorem confirms this by another way: three numbers really suffice, and it tells how to choose them. We take three successive rotations as with the gripper.

We start with frame 0. We turn the frame by the angle \(\varphi\) about the axis \(z\) and get frame \(a\), which has the same axis \(z\). We turn frame \(a\) by the angle \(\theta\) about its axis \(y\) and get frame \(b\), then frame \(b\) by the angle \(\psi\) about its axis \(z\) and get frame 1. All three rotations are about current axes, so by point 2 of lemma 27.12 the matrix product is in the same order: \(\mat R^0_{\mathrm{a}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\), \(\mat R^a_{\mathrm{b}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\), \(\mat R^b_1 = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi}\) and \(\mat R^0_1 = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi}\). The three numbers are called the Euler angles of the frame (Eulerjevi koti) after the sequence of axes \(z\), \(y\), \(z\) (ZYZ).

Theorem 27.14 ZYZ Euler angles (Eulerjevi koti ZYZ)

Let \(\mat R\) be an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix with determinant \(1\) and elements \(R_{\mathrm{kl}}\).

  1. There exist numbers \(\varphi\), \(\psi\) and \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) such that \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi}\). The product has the elements
    \[\begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi\cos\theta\cos\psi - \sin\varphi\sin\psi & -\cos\varphi\cos\theta\sin\psi - \sin\varphi\cos\psi & \cos\varphi\sin\theta \\ \sin\varphi\cos\theta\cos\psi + \cos\varphi\sin\psi & -\sin\varphi\cos\theta\sin\psi + \cos\varphi\cos\psi & \sin\varphi\sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta\cos\psi & \sin\theta\sin\psi & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} . \tag{27.6}\]
  2. If \(R_{33} \ne \pm 1\), then \(\theta \in (0, \pi)\) is uniquely determined by the condition \(\cos\theta = R_{33}\), and the numbers \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) up to multiples of \(2\pi\) by the conditions \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (R_{13}, R_{23})/\sin\theta\) and \((\cos\psi, \sin\psi) = (-R_{31}, R_{32})/\sin\theta\).
  3. If \(R_{33} = 1\), then \(\theta = 0\) and \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi+\psi}\), so every pair \(\varphi\), \(\psi\) with the same sum gives the same matrix. If \(R_{33} = -1\), then \(\theta = \pi\) and \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi-\psi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\), so every pair with the same difference gives the same matrix.
Proof

First we compute the product, then for \(R_{33} \ne \pm 1\) we find the angles from the matrix and check them, finally we treat the cases \(R_{33} = \pm 1\).

  1. From definition 27.10,
    \[\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\cos\psi & -\cos\theta\sin\psi & \sin\theta \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 \\ -\sin\theta\cos\psi & \sin\theta\sin\psi & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} .\]
    We multiply rows 1 and 2 by \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\) from the left: the new row 1 is \(\cos\varphi\cdot(\text{row 1}) - \sin\varphi\cdot(\text{row 2})\), the new row 2 is \(\sin\varphi\cdot(\text{row 1}) + \cos\varphi\cdot(\text{row 2})\), row 3 does not change. This is equation (27.6).
  2. Let \(R_{33} \ne \pm 1\). The third column is a unit vector (orthogonality, (2.52)), so \(R_{13}^2 + R_{23}^2 + R_{33}^2 = 1\) and \(|R_{33}| < 1\). The cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) with \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\) (lemma 8.11, points 2 and 3), so there is exactly one \(\theta \in (0, \pi)\) with \(\cos\theta = R_{33}\), and \(\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - R_{33}^2} > 0\).
  3. From step 2, \(R_{13}^2 + R_{23}^2 = \sin^2\theta\), so the vector \((R_{13}, R_{23})/\sin\theta\) has length \(1\). For every unit vector \((u_1, u_2)\) there is an angle with \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (u_1, u_2)\), as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.41. So we get \(\varphi\), up to multiples of \(2\pi\). Since the third row is a unit vector, \(R_{31}^2 + R_{32}^2 = \sin^2\theta\), and in the same way we get \(\psi\) from \((-R_{31}, R_{32})/\sin\theta\).
  4. Let \(\mat S = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\varphi}\,\mat R\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\psi}\). The matrices \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\pm\varphi}\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\pm\psi}\) are orthogonal with determinant \(1\) (lemma 27.11, point 3). A product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, \((\mat A\mat B)^\T(\mat A\mat B) = \mat B^\T\mat A^\T\mat A\mat B = \mat I\) (lemma 1.102), and determinants multiply (theorem 2.74), so \(\mat S\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\).
  5. The third column of \(\mat S\): \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\psi}\) keeps \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(\mat S\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\varphi}\,(R_{13}, R_{23}, R_{33})^\T\). By step 3 this is \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\varphi}(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta)^\T = (\sin\theta(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi),\ \sin\theta(-\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + \cos\varphi\sin\varphi),\ \cos\theta)^\T = (\sin\theta,\ 0,\ \cos\theta)^\T\).
  6. The third row of \(\mat S\): \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\varphi}\) keeps the third row, so it equals \((R_{31}, R_{32}, R_{33})\,\mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\psi}\), and by step 3 this is \((-\sin\theta\cos\psi\cdot\cos\psi + \sin\theta\sin\psi\cdot(-\sin\psi),\ -\sin\theta\cos\psi\sin\psi + \sin\theta\sin\psi\cos\psi,\ \cos\theta) = (-\sin\theta,\ 0,\ \cos\theta)\).
  7. So the matrix \(\mat S\) has the form \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} a & b & \sin\theta \\ d & e & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{smallmatrix}\right]\). From orthogonality: columns 2 and 3 are perpendicular, \(b\sin\theta = 0\), so \(b = 0\) (since \(\sin\theta > 0\)). Rows 2 and 3 are perpendicular, \(-d\sin\theta = 0\), so \(d = 0\). Rows 1 and 3 are perpendicular, \(-a\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\), so \(a = \cos\theta\). Column 2 is a unit vector, so \(e = \pm 1\).
  8. By definition 2.72, \(\det\mat S = a\,e\cos\theta + e\sin^2\theta = e(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = e\), since the other terms are zero. Since \(\det\mat S = 1\), \(e = 1\), and \(\mat S = \mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\).
  9. From the definition of \(\mat S\) and lemma 27.11, points 1 and 2, \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat S\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi}\). This is point 1 at \(R_{33} \ne \pm 1\).
  10. Uniqueness (point 2). Let \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi'}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta'}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi'}\) with \(\theta' \in (0, \pi)\). By equation (27.6), \(R_{33} = \cos\theta'\), so \(\theta' = \theta\), because the cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\). Then \((R_{13}, R_{23}) = \sin\theta\,(\cos\varphi', \sin\varphi')\) and \(\sin\theta \ne 0\), so \((\cos\varphi', \sin\varphi') = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\) and \(\varphi'\) differs from \(\varphi\) by a multiple of \(2\pi\). The same for \(\psi\) from \((R_{31}, R_{32}) = \sin\theta\,(-\cos\psi', \sin\psi')\).
  11. Let \(R_{33} = 1\). By unit length (\(R_{13}^2 + R_{23}^2 = 0\) and \(R_{31}^2 + R_{32}^2 = 0\)) the third column and third row have the elements \((0, 0, 1)\). The remaining \(2 \times 2\) block is orthogonal with determinant \(1\). As in steps 2 to 4 of the proof of lemma 8.41, which need only orthogonality, determinant \(1\) and \(\mat R\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\) for some \(\alpha\). By lemma 27.11, \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},0}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi+\psi}\), so \(\varphi\), \(\psi\) with \(\varphi + \psi = \alpha\) are a solution.
  12. Let \(R_{33} = -1\). The matrix \(\mat R\,\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\) is orthogonal with determinant \(1\) and the \((3, 3)\) element equal to \(R_{33}\cos\pi = 1\), since \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, -1)\). By step 11 it equals \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\), so \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}^{-1} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\), since \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\) is its own inverse matrix (lemma 27.11, point 2). Since \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},-\psi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\) (both products are \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} -\cos\psi & \sin\psi & 0 \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\)), \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi-\psi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\pi}\), which is point 3.

Source: SPONG (2.27)-(2.38) (PDF p. 75-77), where the derivation goes from the element equations with two solutions \(\theta\) and two cases \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\). The proof with the auxiliary matrix \(\mat S\) and point 3 are not from the source.

An example with definite angles. For \(\varphi = 40^\circ\), \(\theta = 50^\circ\) and \(\psi = 30^\circ\) equation (27.6) gives the matrix

\[\mat R = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}1050 & -0{,}8029 & 0{,}5868 \\ 0{,}7408 & 0{,}4568 & 0{,}4924 \\ -0{,}6634 & 0{,}3830 & 0{,}6428 \end{bmatrix} .\]

We get the angles from it by steps 2 and 3 of the proof of the theorem: \(\cos\theta = R_{33} = 0{,}6428\) gives \(\theta = 50^\circ\) and \(\sin\theta = 0{,}7660\). Then \((R_{13}, R_{23})/\sin\theta = (0{,}7660;\ 0{,}6428) = (\cos 40^\circ, \sin 40^\circ)\) and \((-R_{31}, R_{32})/\sin\theta = (0{,}8660;\ 0{,}5000) = (\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ)\). We get the right angles. Without the condition \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) another triple would give the same matrix: the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\pi}\mat R_{\mathrm{y},-\theta}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\pi}\) is \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},\theta}\) (a rotation by \(\pi\) about \(z\) reverses the sign of the \(x\) and \(y\) elements that the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},-\theta}\) mixes with \(z\)), so the triple \((\varphi + \pi, -\theta, \psi + \pi)\) gives the same matrix. It has \(\theta < 0\) and is excluded by the condition \(\theta \in (0, \pi)\). SPONG counts it as the second solution. For the gripper of example 27.13 we have \(R_{33} = 0\), so \(\theta = 90^\circ\), and \((R_{13}, R_{23}) = (1, 0)\), \((-R_{31}, R_{32}) = (0, 1)\) give \(\varphi = 0^\circ\), \(\psi = 90^\circ\).

Point 3 of the theorem is close to something that demands attention in a robot. What if \(\theta = 0\)? Then the axes \(z_0\), \(z_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(z_1\) coincide, and the angles \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) turn about the same axis.

Example 27.15 Euler angles at \(\theta = 0\) are not unique (Eulerjevi koti pri \(\theta = 0\) niso enolični)

Take \(\theta = 0\) and two pairs of angles, \((\varphi, \psi) = (10^\circ, 50^\circ)\) and \((45^\circ, 15^\circ)\) (figure 27.18).

  1. By lemma 27.11, \(\mat R_{\mathrm{y},0} = \mat I\), so \(\mat R = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\psi} = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\varphi+\psi}\).
  2. Both pairs have the sum \(60^\circ\), so they give the same matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},60^\circ} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0{,}5 & -0{,}866 & 0 \\ 0{,}866 & 0{,}5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\).
  3. In the left figure the axis \(x\) goes first by \(10^\circ\) and then by \(50^\circ\), in the right one first by \(45^\circ\) and then by \(15^\circ\). Both times the final position of the axis \(x_1\) is at \(60^\circ\) and of \(y_1\) at \(150^\circ\) from the axis \(x_0\). From the matrix \(\mat R\) the angles \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) cannot be separated, only their sum is determined (point 3 of theorem 27.14).

Source: The numbers are from NumPy (outlines/ch-roboti/num-02.py, N8). SPONG (2.36) (PDF p. 77).

At \(\theta = \pi\) only the difference \(\varphi - \psi\) remains, which is the mirror picture. In robotics \(\theta = 0\) or \(\pi\) means a wrist position where two axes lie on each other. The unique computation of angles described above does not exist there, and later we shall see that in this position the arm loses a direction in which the gripper can move. For now we shall use the matrix for orientation and not the angles.

Euler angles use three rotations about three axes. Would one rotation about one axis suffice? Figure 27.19 shows the answer on the example of the gripper that we have already turned.

The axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) carry the labels of both frames, because after two quarter circles they exchange cyclically: \(x_1\) is on \(y_0\), \(y_1\) on \(z_0\) and \(z_1\) on \(x_0\). The blue arrows show where the tip of each axis goes. By its shape this is a rotation that carries the triple of axes into itself so that each goes to the next. One vector does not move in it: the cube diagonal, which is equally far from all three axes. The rotation goes about it.

The same rotation becomes simple when we look at it from another side, from the tip of the diagonal towards the origin (figure 27.20). The diagonal \(\vek v\) is now a dot that points towards us. The axes \(x_0\), \(y_0\) and \(z_0\) lean towards us equally steeply, so we see them as three equally long spokes, \(120^\circ\) apart. The blue arcs are the arrows of the previous figure: each spoke goes to the next, and this is an ordinary plane rotation by a third of a circle, \(360^\circ/3 = 120^\circ\). A vector that lies in the plane of the page is perpendicular to the axis and we see it in its true length. Such is the vector \(\vek w\) of length \(1\), which the rotation carries along the circle of radius \(1\) into the vector \(\mat R\vek w\), through the same angle \(120^\circ\). What this view adds to the previous figure is that we read the angle of the rotation with our eyes: a rotation in space is a plane rotation when we look along its axis. The next theorem shows that every rotation has such an axis.

Theorem 27.16 Axis of a rotation (os zasuka)

Let \(\mat R\) be an orthogonal \(3 \times 3\) matrix with determinant \(1\). Then there is a vector \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(\mat R\vek v = \vek v\), and \(\mat R\) maps every vector \(\vek w\) perpendicular to \(\vek v\) into a vector perpendicular to \(\vek v\). The number \(1\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mat R\) (definition 2.81), and the vectors \(k\vek v\) are the axis of the rotation.

Let \(\vek v\) be a unit vector and \(\vek w\) a unit vector perpendicular to it. Then there is an angle \(\alpha\) such that \(\mat R\) has the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\) in the axes \(\vek w\), \(\vek v\times\vek w\), \(\vek v\), and for every vector \(\vek u\) perpendicular to \(\vek v\) we have \(\vek u\cdot\mat R\vek u = |\vek u|^2\cos\alpha\). So \(\mat R\) turns all vectors perpendicular to the axis by the same angle \(\alpha\), the angle of the rotation (kot zasuka).

Proof

We must show that \(\mat R - \mat I\) has zero determinant, and then conclude \(\vek v\) from it. We use that the determinant does not change on transposition, but changes sign when we reverse all three columns. For the angle we write \(\mat R\) in axes that include the axis of the rotation and use the planar case.

  1. Since \(\mat R\mat R^\T = \mat I\), we have \(\mat R\,(\mat I - \mat R^\T) = \mat R - \mat R\mat R^\T = \mat R - \mat I\).
  2. By theorem 2.74, \(\det(\mat R - \mat I) = \det\mat R\,\det(\mat I - \mat R^\T) = \det(\mat I - \mat R^\T)\), since \(\det\mat R = 1\).
  3. The matrix \(\mat I - \mat R^\T\) is the transpose of \(\mat I - \mat R\) (definition 1.101), so \(\det(\mat I - \mat R^\T) = \det(\mat I - \mat R)\) (lemma 2.73, point 6).
  4. The matrix \(\mat I - \mat R\) is \(-(\mat R - \mat I)\), each of the three columns is multiplied by \(-1\), so by linearity in each column (lemma 2.73, point 2) \(\det(\mat I - \mat R) = (-1)^3\det(\mat R - \mat I) = -\det(\mat R - \mat I)\).
  5. Steps 2 to 4 give \(\det(\mat R - \mat I) = -\det(\mat R - \mat I)\), so \(\det(\mat R - \mat I) = 0\).
  6. By lemma 2.75 there is \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \((\mat R - \mat I)\vek v = \vek 0\), that is \(\mat R\vek v = \vek v\). By definition 2.81 this is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(1\). In the example it is the cube diagonal, which from its tip we see as a dot.
  7. Let \(\vek w\cdot\vek v = 0\). By equation (2.137), \((\mat R\vek w)\cdot\vek v = (\mat R\vek w)\cdot(\mat R\vek v) = \vek w\cdot(\mat R^\T\mat R\vek v) = \vek w\cdot\vek v = 0\). So a vector perpendicular to the axis stays perpendicular to it after the rotation. In the example the plane of such vectors is the plane of the page, in which \(\vek w\) and \(\mat R\vek w\) lie.
  8. Let \(\vek v\) be a unit vector, \(\vek w\) a unit vector perpendicular to it, and \(\mat Q\) the matrix with the rows \(\vek w\), \(\vek v\times\vek w\), \(\vek v\). The vector \(\vek v\times\vek w\) is perpendicular to \(\vek v\) and \(\vek w\) (lemma 2.34, point 4) and has the length \(\sqrt{1\cdot 1 - 0^2} = 1\) (point 5). So the rows are unit vectors, pairwise perpendicular, and \(\mat Q\) is the rotation matrix into these axes (definition 2.44), orthogonal (lemma 2.47) with determinant \(\pm 1\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 27.9). A vector with the components \(\vek u\) has the components \(\mat Q\vek u\) in the new axes (lemma 2.45, point 1).
  9. Let \(\mat M = \mat Q\mat R\mat Q^\T\). Since \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\), the vector \(\mat R\vek u\) has the components \(\mat Q\mat R\vek u = \mat M\,(\mat Q\vek u)\) in the new axes, so \(\mat M\) is the matrix of \(\mat R\) in the new axes. As in step 4 of the proof of theorem 27.14, \(\mat M\) is orthogonal, and \(\det\mat M = \det\mat Q\,\det\mat R\,\det\mat Q^\T = (\det\mat Q)^2 = 1\) (theorem 2.74, lemma 2.73, point 6). Its third column is \(\mat M\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \mat Q\mat R\vek v = \mat Q\vek v = (\vek w\cdot\vek v,\ (\vek v\times\vek w)\cdot\vek v,\ \vek v\cdot\vek v) = (0, 0, 1)\), because \(\mat Q^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek v\) is the third row of \(\mat Q\).
  10. As in step 11 of the proof of theorem 27.14 (steps 2 to 4 of the proof of lemma 8.41 use only orthogonality, determinant \(1\) and \(\mat M\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\)), \(\mat M = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\) for some angle \(\alpha\).
  11. Let \(\vek u\cdot\vek v = 0\). Then \(\bar{\vek u} = \mat Q\vek u = (a, b, 0)\), because the third component is \(\vek v\cdot\vek u = 0\). By equation (2.137) and \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) we have \((\mat Q\vek a)\cdot(\mat Q\vek b) = \vek a\cdot\vek b\) for any two vectors, so \(|\bar{\vek u}| = |\vek u|\) and \(\vek u\cdot\mat R\vek u = (\mat Q\vek u)\cdot(\mat Q\mat R\vek u) = \bar{\vek u}\cdot\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\alpha}\bar{\vek u} = a\,(a\cos\alpha - b\sin\alpha) + b\,(a\sin\alpha + b\cos\alpha) = (a^2 + b^2)\cos\alpha = |\vek u|^2\cos\alpha\). In the example \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(\vek w\) and \(\mat R\vek w\), the same for every vector on the circle.

Source: SPONG (2.45), (2.46) (PDF p. 80) states that every matrix \(\mat R \in SO(3)\) is a rotation by some angle about some axis, without proof. The proof with the determinant and the proof of the angle in steps 8 to 11 are not from the source.

The theorem says that an axis exists and that the rotation turns all vectors perpendicular to it by the same angle \(\alpha\), but not how large this angle is. We compute it on an example directly, with a single vector perpendicular to the axis.

Example 27.17 Axis and angle of the rotation for the two orders (os in kot zasuka za dva vrstna reda)

The rotations of example 27.13 have the matrices \(\mat A = \mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\) and \(\mat B = \mat R_{\mathrm{x},90^\circ}\mat R_{\mathrm{z},90^\circ}\).

  1. For \(\mat A\), \(\vek v = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}\): the columns of \(\mat A\) are \((0, 1, 0)\), \((0, 0, 1)\), \((1, 0, 0)\), so \(\mat A\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{z}}, v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek v\). For \(\mat B\), \(\vek v' = (1, -1, 1)/\sqrt{3}\), since \(\mat B\vek v' = (-v'_{\mathrm{y}},\ -v'_{\mathrm{z}},\ v'_{\mathrm{x}}) = (1, -1, 1)/\sqrt{3}\). The axes are different (figures 27.19 and 27.16).
  2. For \(\mat A\) we take the vector \(\vek w\) \(= (1, -1, 0)/\sqrt{2}\). It is perpendicular to \(\vek v\) (\(1 - 1 + 0 = 0\)), so it lies in the plane that the rotation maps into itself (theorem 27.16). The rotated vector \(\mat A\vek w\) is \((0;\ 0{,}7071;\ -0{,}7071)\) and \(\vek w\cdot\mat A\vek w = -0{,}5\). By the theorem this is \(|\vek w|^2\cos\alpha = \cos\alpha\), so the cosine of the angle of the rotation is \(-0{,}5\), and in \([0, \pi]\) this is the angle \(120^\circ\), which the figure shows between the two arrows (the cosine is strictly decreasing there).
  3. The same holds for \(\mat B\) with \(\vek w' = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt{2}\), perpendicular to \(\vek v'\), and \(\vek w'\cdot\mat B\vek w' = -0{,}5\), so \(120^\circ\). Both orders give a rotation by \(120^\circ\), but about different axes.
  4. The general formula for the angle with the trace, \(\cos\theta = (\mathrm{tr}\,\mat R - 1)/2\), is stated by SPONG (2.46) (PDF p. 80) without proof. It agrees: \(\mathrm{tr}\,\mat A = 0\) and \((0 - 1)/2 = -0{,}5\).

The theorem has the condition \(\det\mat R = 1\). Is it really necessary? Let us look at an orthogonal matrix that is not a rotation.

Example 27.18 The point reflection \(-\mat I\) has no axis (središčno zrcaljenje \(-\mat I\) nima osi)

The matrix \(-\mat I\) is orthogonal, \((-\mat I)^\T(-\mat I) = \mat I\), and has determinant \(-1\) (figure 27.21).

  1. The columns of \(\mat I\) are the axes \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) of the right-handed frame. The columns of \(-\mat I\) are \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), which is a frame with reversed axes. The mixed product \((-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot((-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\times(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})) = -1\), so the frame is left-handed (definition 2.32) and is not the frame of a link.
  2. The determinant \(\det(-\mat I - \mat I) = \det(-2\,\mat I) = -8 \ne 0\). By lemma 2.75 there is no vector \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(-\vek v = \vek v\). The matrix has no axis.
  3. In the proof of theorem 27.16 step 2 fails: at \(\det\mat R = -1\) step 2 gives \(\det(\mat R - \mat I) = -\det(\mat I - \mat R^\T)\), and step 4 again the sign \(-1\), so the two steps together give \(\det(\mat R - \mat I) = \det(\mat R - \mat I)\), which is a truth without content. The determinant \(1\) is really used in the proof.
  4. At determinant \(-1\) the theorem says nothing, an axis may exist or not. The reflection in the plane \(xy\), the matrix \(\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\), is also orthogonal with determinant \(-1\), but it leaves every vector of the plane \(xy\) in place: \(\det(\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1) - \mat I) = 0 \cdot 0 \cdot (-2) = 0\). This reflection is not a rotation either, because it turns a right-handed frame into a left-handed one.

Figure 27.22 shows what this matrix does. The plane \(xy\) is the mirror: every point of it stays in place. The axis \(z\) turns into \(-z\), the axes \(x\) and \(y\) stay. The frame we get this way is left-handed, so the reflection is not a rotation.

A rotation is thus an orientation with three numbers, which we get in two ways: as three angles (theorem 27.14) or as an axis and an angle (theorem 27.16). Other notations we state without proof. Roll, pitch and yaw (nagib, kotaljenje in zasuk) are three successive rotations about the axes of frame 0 in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) (SPONG (2.39) (PDF p. 78)), and the axis-angle formula \(\mat R_{\vek{k},\theta}\) is SPONG (2.44) (PDF p. 80). The same axis-turning matrix we already had in the book in the elements of constructions: the rotation matrix \(\mat Q = (\mat R^0_1)^\T\) from the global axes to the axes of the element for the bar (lemma 7.29) and for the beam (lemma 12.23).
