Chapter 19 Symmetry, periodicity and constraints

19.11 Connections

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19.

  • Mirror symmetry and its conditions: that a symmetric structure under a symmetric load gives a symmetric response is shown by theorem 19.4 for a body and by theorem 19.6 for a frame model. The second generalizes lemmas 7.36 and 7.38 of chapter 7, each of which held only for its own structure, to every frame model. Theorem 19.7 says what must hold on the plane of symmetry. This closes the promises of chapters 5 (symmetry of frames) and 11 (symmetry conditions of a body). A plane of symmetry that carries stiffness or load takes over only half of each, as in section 19.2 (lemma 19.9, which generalizes the half model of the bridge of lemma 7.39). A quarter of a plate with two symmetry edges was already used in section 18.9 (MK, AK), and a quarter of a plate with a hole with mirror conditions on the axis planes in section 17.9 (AK).
  • Symmetry in free vibration and buckling: theorem 19.18 splits the modes of a symmetric system into symmetric and antisymmetric ones, and its corollary 19.19 shows that the half model with symmetric conditions has exactly the symmetric modes, and the half model with antisymmetric conditions exactly the antisymmetric ones. The portal frame of section 19.4 has its lowest frequency, \(29{,}511\) Hz, in an antisymmetric mode and its lowest critical force, \(154{,}2\) kN, in the antisymmetric sway. The half model with symmetric conditions would give a lowest frequency of \(116{,}20\) Hz and a buckling force that is \(3{,}42\) times too large. For cyclic symmetry the same split is theorem 19.49, and its corollary 19.51 says that every frequency of harmonic \(k\) with \(k \ne 0\) and \(k \ne N/2\) has multiplicity at least \(2\) (section 19.10). A disk with \(N\) equal blades is cyclically symmetric, so the same split holds for it. Chapter 24 (DSK) does not use it, because there the disk is a rigid body or an axisymmetric rotating disk without blades.
  • Elimination of constraints, multipoint constraints and coupling of different elements: theorem 19.26 shows that the reduced matrix \(\mat T^\T\mat K\,\mat T\) gives the same solution as the multipliers of theorem 12.26 in chapter 12 and that the constraint forces \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) follow from \(\mat K\vek d - \vek f\). The inclined support (lemma 19.27, figure 19.16) is the simplest case. Lemma 19.35 ties the rotation of a beam at a node of a mesh without rotations to two displacements, as figure 19.21 shows. When the mesh is made of plane or solid elements, which likewise have no rotations at a node, the same constraint is written for those elements. Their stiffness matrices were already derived in chapter 15 (AK). Guyan reduction, also called static condensation (statična kondenzacija), which eliminates part of the unknowns by static equilibrium, and substructures are treated in chapter 21 (DSK). There the reduced system has the same form \(\mat T^\T\mat K\,\mat T\), except that the columns of \(\mat T\) are not fixed by a support constraint but by a choice of shapes, for example static shapes in Guyan reduction.
  • Cyclic symmetry and loads without it: theorem 19.46 splits any load into harmonics by the discrete Fourier transform (definition 9.102) and solves each harmonic on one sector with the phase factor \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm e^{2\pi\mathrm i k/N}\). This is our derivation, not from a source (the gap is described in section 19.9). COOK §14.4 (PDF p. 530-531) does the same for bodies of revolution under a load without axial symmetry: it expands the load in a Fourier series in the circumferential angle with sine and cosine terms, solves each term separately and adds the results. We do not prove that series here. The axisymmetric elements were already introduced in section 15.9 (AK), which only named loads without axial symmetry. The split into harmonics is the same idea for a structure of \(N\) equal sectors, with a finite sum of \(N\) harmonics instead of a series.
  • Periodicity: theorem 19.38 (section 19.8) allows a single span with equal boundary values for an infinite row of equal spans. For a finite row this holds only in the interior. The moment at the middle support is \(0{,}125\,ql^2\) for two spans, which is \(50\) % more than the cell value \(ql^2/12 = 0{,}08333\,ql^2\), for five spans the deviation is \(5{,}3\) %, and the cell value is reached to six digits only at 20 spans. From the end towards the interior the deviation decreases at every support by the factor \(2 - \sqrt3 \approx 0{,}268\) (lemma 19.40). Unit cells as a procedure for composite materials (homogenization) are only named, because the books we have contain no account of them.
  • Limits of the chapter: what is proved holds for a linear model in which the stiffness matrix and the load do not depend on the displacement. In nonlinear static problems, for example with large displacements or contact, a symmetry that is present when loading begins may later disappear (COOK PDF p. 71). The proof of theorem 19.4 fails there because it rests on the solution being unique, and a nonlinear problem can have several solutions. The chapter does not treat this. In buckling the two idealizations are a linear elastic model with axial forces that do not change up to buckling and beams with a small slope (section 19.4). At the sway load of \(154{,}2\) kN the stress in the column is \(192{,}7\) MPa, which is \(88\) % of the yield stress of \(220\) MPa, so the linear elastic model is at the edge of its validity. The cyclic split into harmonics and cyclic vibration are in none of the books we have, so they are proved in the chapter, not cited.
