Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.4 Guyan reduction (Guyanova redukcija)

So far in this chapter we have shortened the response: we kept the first few modes and, where needed, added a static correction to them (section 21.1). For that we must know the modes, that is, we must already have solved the eigenvalue problem of the whole structure, and for a large model this is exactly what we would like to save. This section starts at the other end. First we shrink the structure to a few coordinates, only then do we solve the eigenvalue problem, and that problem is small. How do we choose which coordinates to keep, and where do the others go? The simplest answer is equilibrium: the coordinates we do not keep are left to reach equilibrium by themselves, as if no force acted on them. After R. J. Guyan, one of the two authors who published it independently in \(1965\), the procedure is called Guyan reduction (Guyanova redukcija), in the literature also static condensation (statična kondenzacija): the coordinates we eliminate are, as it were, condensed into the kept ones, and it is static because only the equilibrium of forces in the stiffness matrix is taken into account, not inertia. The section proves three things. Static condensation is exact for loads on the kept coordinates (lemma 21.18). The Guyan approximation is a Ritz approximation (definition 10.41), so the upper bound of theorem 10.42 holds, and it is exact when the dependent coordinates have no mass (theorem 21.19). And the exact eigenvalue problem, of which Guyan's is only the first approximation, tells when it is good and when it is not (theorem 21.20). We test everything on two examples, a frame and a cantilever, and on both we also show a choice of coordinates for which the reduction fails.

21.4.1 A frame with one kept floor

Let us start with the smallest example where everything can be seen. The frame with three storeys from section 10.8 has floor displacements \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) (from the bottom up), each storey has stiffness \(k\) and each floor mass \(\bar m\). We measure the eigenvalues in the unit \(k/\bar m\), and they are \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\). The matrices are

\[\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \bar m\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ,\]

and from here on we take \(k = \bar m = 1\). Only the lowest eigenvalue interests us, so of the three coordinates we keep one, the top floor \(q_3\), and ask where floors \(1\) and \(2\) should go.

We build the figure part by part. First we draw the ground, the fixed base. On it we set up the columns, drawn dashed in the undeformed position, and above them the three floors, numbered from the bottom. Each piece of column between two floors (or between the ground and floor \(1\)) is a storey with horizontal stiffness \(k\). Now we move floor \(3\) by \(1\) to the right and hold it there with the force \(F\), which starts at the floor and points in the direction of the displacement. No external force acts on floors \(1\) and \(2\), and this is the requirement that the floors reach equilibrium by themselves. How do they move? Only the storey below and the storey above act on floor \(1\), and since no external force acts on it, the forces of the two storeys are equal by the law of equilibrium: \(k\,q_1 = k\,(q_2 - q_1)\) in magnitude. The same holds for floor \(2\): \(k\,(q_2 - q_1) = k\,(q_3 - q_2)\). The shear force is therefore the same in all three storeys, so the interstorey drifts (section 10.2.3), that is the displacements of a floor relative to the floor below it, are the same in all three storeys. Three equal drifts together give \(q_3 = 1\), so each is \(1/3\) and the floor displacements are \(q_1 = \tfrac13\), \(q_2 = \tfrac23\), \(q_3 = 1\), as the displacements in the figure show. The column \(\vek\psi = (\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ 1)^\T\) is the static shape of the frame for a unit displacement of the top floor.

The force that holds the top floor is the shear force in the top storey, \(F = k\,(q_3 - q_2) = k/3\). At the same time this is the stiffness of the reduced system with one coordinate: a unit displacement needs the force \(k/3\). By a second route, from energy, \(\tilde K\) is twice the elastic energy at a unit displacement of the top floor: \(\tilde K = \vek\psi^\T\mat K\vek\psi = k\,(3 \cdot (1/3)^2) = k/3\), since each of the three storeys with drift \(1/3\) contributes \(k\,(1/3)^2\). The mass of the reduced system is twice the kinetic energy at unit velocity of the top floor, when all floors move according to \(\vek\psi\): \(\tilde M = \vek\psi^\T\mat M\vek\psi = \bar m\,(\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + 1) = \tfrac{14}{9}\bar m\). The reduced eigenvalue is therefore

\[\tilde\lambda = \frac{\tilde K}{\tilde M} = \frac{k/3}{14\bar m/9} = \frac{3}{14}\,\frac{k}{\bar m} = 0{,}214286\,\frac{k}{\bar m} , \tag{21.30}\]

which is \(8{,}19\) % above the true value \(0{,}198062\) (SymPy and NumPy), and \(4{,}01\) % in frequency (\(2{,}3339\) Hz instead of \(2{,}2438\) Hz for \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(\bar m = 500\) kg). That it is higher also follows from theorem 10.42: with one column \(\vek\psi\), \(\tilde\lambda\) is the Rayleigh quotient \(R(\vek\psi)\) of definition 10.41 and lies above \(\lambda_1\).

Why above, and by how much? The true first mode of the frame, normalized to displacement \(1\) of the top floor, is \((0{,}445,\ 0{,}802,\ 1)^\T\) (NumPy), which at floors \(1\) and \(2\) is more than the static shape \((\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ 1)^\T\). In vibration each floor carries the inertia force \(-\bar m\ddot q_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda\bar m\,q_{\mathrm{i}}\), which acts in the same sense as the displacement, so floors \(1\) and \(2\) are loaded additionally and move more than they would without this force. The static shape forgets this force. That is exactly the price of Guyan reduction, and theorem 21.20 measures it later.

21.4.2 Kept and dependent coordinates

We do the same for a system with many coordinates. Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\) (theorem 10.13) and \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) their eigenvalues. We split the coordinates into two sets. The kept coordinates are labelled \(\mathrm{a}\), their number \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\), and we call them active (aktivne), because the shortened model keeps them as true unknowns. The others are labelled \(\text{d}\), their number is \(n_{\mathrm{d}} = n - n_{\mathrm{a}}\), and we call them dependent (odvisne), because equilibrium will determine their displacements from the displacements of the active ones. The labels \(\mathrm{a}\) and \(\text{d}\) are from CK (section 14.6, PDF p. 469), where the active ones are also called master and the dependent ones slave. We number the coordinates so that the active ones come first. Renumbering is a permutation of rows and columns, which changes neither symmetry nor positive definiteness, since \(\vek x^\T\mat P^\T\mat K\mat P\,\vek x = (\mat P\vek x)^\T\mat K\,(\mat P\vek x) > 0\) for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\). In blocks the matrices are then

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} & \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}} \\ \mat K_{\mathrm{da}} & \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \begin{pmatrix} \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} & \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}} \\ \mat M_{\mathrm{da}} & \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} \end{pmatrix} , \tag{21.31}\]

with \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}^\T\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{da}} = \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}^\T\). For the frame \(\mathrm{a} = \{3\}\) and \(\text{d} = \{1, 2\}\). In chapter 12 we labelled the displacements and rotations of a beam \(\mathrm{t}\) and \(\mathrm{r}\) (lemma 12.45), which is a special case of this partition: \(\mathrm{a}\) are the displacements, \(\text{d}\) the rotations. In this chapter the bold \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a mode of vibration, and the rotation of a node is written out in words.

The block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is positive definite: for a column \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\) the column \(\vek z = (\vek 0, \vek y)\) is nonzero and \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z = \vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek y > 0\) (as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 12.45). By theorem 6.29 it is therefore invertible, and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is positive definite as well. What does invertibility say in words? If the active coordinates are held, the structure has exactly one equilibrium position for every load on the dependent coordinates.

From history

In the years from \(1960\) to \(1970\) computer technology grew fast, but the main obstacle in structural dynamics was the limit on the number of degrees of freedom a computer could still handle (GR, PDF p. 434). At the same time it was found that for accurate internal stresses the model must be divided finely enough, but that for the distribution of the inertia forces a large number of degrees of freedom is superfluous (GR, PDF p. 434). This led to the idea of dynamic reduction: we partition the coordinates and transfer the distribution of the inertia forces statically to a smaller set of coordinates (GR, PDF p. 434). The principle of static condensation for dynamic problems was published by Irons and Guyan in the same year \(1965\), independently of each other (GR, PDF p. 434). After both of them CK calls the method the Guyan-Irons reduction, often just Guyan reduction, and cites R. J. Guyan's article »Reduction of Stiffness and Mass Matrices« in the AIAA Journal of \(1965\) (CK, PDF pp. 469 and 481). At first the reduction was seen as a saver of degrees of freedom, but later it proved useful elsewhere too, for example in the exchange of large models between teams (GR, PDF p. 434). In \(1968\) Anderson, Irons and Zienkiewicz showed how the choice and the number of kept coordinates affect the accuracy of eigenvalues and modes in the vibration and stability of plates (CK, PDF pp. 469 and 481). Exactly this question, which coordinates we keep, will be our test on the cantilever.

Let us return to the partition (21.31): what should the columns of the Ritz basis be if we want the active coordinates to remain the coordinates of the shortened model? For the frame we found them from equilibrium, and now we write the same in blocks.

Definition 21.17 Guyan basis (Guyanova baza)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) and the partition \(\mathrm{a}\), \(\text{d}\) be as in equation (21.31). The matrix \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\) has \(n_{\mathrm{d}}\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\) columns, and its column \(s\) holds the displacements of the dependent coordinates when the active coordinate \(s\) is displaced by \(1\), the other active ones are \(0\) and no external forces act on the dependent ones. The Guyan basis (Guyanova baza) is the matrix with \(n\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\) columns

\[\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat I \\ \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat I \\ -\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{21.32}\]

The Guyan approximation (Guyanov približek) is \(\vek q \approx \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\), the reduced matrices \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) are of order \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\), and the Guyan values \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{a}}}\) are the eigenvalues of the reduced problem \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek x = \tilde\lambda\,\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek x\). Guyan reduction is the Ritz approximation (definition 10.41) with the basis \(\mat\Psi = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\), which has independent columns, because from \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek x = \vek 0\) the first block gives \(\vek x = \vek 0\) (definition 6.22).

Source: CK sections 14.6.2 and 14.6.3 (PDF pp. 466-467 and 469, equations 14.63 and 14.64): static condensation with the row \(\text{d}\) free of forces, and the Ritz matrix »the displacement patterns associated with unit displacement of the respective a-coordinates, while the d-coordinates are released«. GR section 6.9.1 (PDF pp. 499-500, equations 6.148 and 6.151): each column is the »static deformation of the structure when the jth retained degree of freedom is set to one, the others being set to zero«. The block notation and the labels \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\), \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\) are ours.

For the frame \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1} = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}} = (\tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ 1)^\T\), which is exactly the column of the figure. The row \(\text{d}\) of the product \(\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat 0\), so there are no forces on floors \(1\) and \(2\), and the row \(\mathrm{a}\) is exactly the force \(F\).

21.4.3 Static condensation

What does the reduced system \(\tilde{\mat K}\) say about statics? If no forces act on the dependent coordinates, it should give all displacements exactly, since the Guyan shape is then truly the equilibrium position. Let us show this and also look at what happens when a force acts on a dependent coordinate.

Lemma 21.18 Static condensation (statična kondenzacija)

Let \(\mat K\) be symmetric positive definite with the partition (21.31) and \(\vek F = (\vek F_{\mathrm{a}}, \vek F_{\mathrm{d}})\) a column of forces. Then:

  1. The matrix \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\) is symmetric and positive definite.
  2. The system \(\mat K\vek q = \vek F\) has exactly one solution. Its active coordinates solve \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{a}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\), and the dependent ones are \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  3. If \(\vek F_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\), then \(\vek q = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{a}}\): the reduced system is exact for all coordinates.
Proof

We must show that the reduced matrix has the properties of a true stiffness matrix, that all displacements follow from the reduced system, and that with force-free dependent coordinates what we saw for the frame happens.

  1. By the rule for the product of matrices in blocks (definition 1.98), \(\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}} = (\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}},\ \mat K_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}})\) by rows, and the second block is \(\mat 0\) by definition 21.17, because \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\). The first block is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\). With \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T = (\mat I,\ \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T)\) the product \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) is therefore the first block plus \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\cdot\mat 0\), that is \(\tilde{\mat K}\).
  2. Symmetry: \((\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}})^\T = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T\mat K^\T\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) (lemma 1.101) and \(\mat K^\T = \mat K\). Positivity: for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x \ne \vek 0\) (the first block is \(\vek x\)), so \(\vek x^\T\tilde{\mat K}\vek x = (\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x)^\T\mat K\,(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x) > 0\). This is point 1.
  3. The rows \(\text{d}\) of the system \(\mat K\vek q = \vek F\) are \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\). Since \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is invertible, \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\) follows.
  4. The rows \(\mathrm{a}\) are \(\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}}\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{a}}\). We insert \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) from step 3: \((\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}})\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{a}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\), which is \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\) on the left side (step 1).
  5. Uniqueness: \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is positive definite by step 2, hence invertible (theorem 6.29), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\) is determined, and then \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) by step 3. Conversely: pairs \((\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}, \vek q_{\mathrm{d}})\) from these equations satisfy both block rows, since they were derived by equivalent transformations. This is point 2.
  6. For \(\vek F_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\), so \(\vek q = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\), and the right side of step 4 is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{a}}\). This is point 3.

Source: CK section 14.6.2 (PDF pp. 466-467): equations 14.62 and 14.63 with the lower partition as the static constraint equation. GR section 6.9.1 (PDF p. 499, equations 6.147 and 6.148): separation of the static part. The general load in point 2 and the proof in steps are not from the sources.

Point 3 is the exact answer to the question we started the section with: for loads on the active coordinates the reduced system is exact, because equilibrium truly fixes the dependent coordinates. For the frame this holds for a force on the top floor: for the force \(F\) we have \(\tilde K\,q_3 = F\) with \(\tilde K = k/3\) and \(q_3 = 3F/k\), which is the true displacement of the top of the frame under this force.

Let us also test a case where the force is not only on the active coordinates. On the same frame with the floors as before, the force \(1\) acts on floor \(2\), and floor \(3\) is still active. The true response (from \(\mat K\vek q = (0, 1, 0)^\T\)): the equation of floor \(3\) is \(q_3 = q_2\), that of floor \(1\) is \(q_2 = 2q_1\), and that of floor \(2\) is \(-q_1 + 2q_2 - q_3 = -q_1 + q_2 = 1\), so \(\vek q = (1,\ 2,\ 2)^\T\). If we wrongly hold on to point 3, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\), and \(\tilde K\,q_3 = 0\) gives \(q_3 = 0\), a displacement that is not there. Point 2, however, correctly accounts for the force on the dependent coordinate: the right side is \(F_{\mathrm{a}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}} = 0 - (0,\ -1)\cdot\tfrac13(1,\ 2)^\T = \tfrac23\), so \(q_3 = (2/3)/(1/3) = 2\), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \tfrac13(1,\ 2)^\T\cdot 2 + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}(0,\ 1)^\T = (\tfrac23 + \tfrac13,\ \tfrac43 + \tfrac23)^\T = (1,\ 2)^\T\), which is the true answer. The static shape \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\,q_3\) alone would give \((\tfrac23,\ \tfrac43,\ 2)^\T\) for a force on the dependent coordinate, which misses \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) by the term \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\). In dynamics the inertia forces \(-\mat M\ddot{\vek q}\) are exactly such a load on the dependent coordinates. The right side of point 2 is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{a}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{d}}\), because \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T = -\mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\): the inertia force of the dependent coordinates is transferred statically to the active ones by the same matrix we used for the displacements. This transfer is the Guyan approximation: it takes the accelerations of the dependent coordinates from the static shape, \(\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} \approx \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{a}}\), and the result is the mass matrix \(\tilde{\mat M}\) below. The error lies in this approximation and not in the statics.

21.4.4 The Guyan approximation and the upper bound

Now dynamics. The theorem says what the Guyan approximation is and in which case it is exact.

Theorem 21.19 Guyan approximation (Guyanov približek)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite with the partition (21.31) and \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). Then:

  1. The reduced mass matrix is \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\), symmetric and positive definite, and the Guyan values are Ritz values: \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  2. Let \(\mat K\) be as above, but let \(\mat M\), instead of positive definite, be only symmetric with \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \mat 0\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{da}} = \mat 0\) and a positive definite block \(\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\) (the dependent coordinates have no inertia). Then \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\), and \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek x = \lambda\,\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\,\vek x\) holds with \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) exactly when \(\vek v = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek x\) solves \(\mat K\,\vek v = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek v\), and every solution \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) of this equation has this form. The Guyan values are therefore exactly all the eigenvalues of the full problem.
Proof

We obtain the first point by multiplication in blocks and Ritz's theorem, the second by the equilibrium of the rows \(\text{d}\), which for massless coordinates is nothing but statics.

  1. By the rule for the product of matrices in blocks (definition 1.98), \(\mat M\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}} = (\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}},\ \mat M_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}})\) by rows, and multiplication by \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}^\T = (\mat I,\ \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T)\) gives the formula. Symmetry and positive definiteness as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 21.18, since \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x \ne \vek 0\) for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\).
  2. The columns of \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) are independent (definition 21.17), so theorem 10.42, point 1, gives \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the first point.
  3. For the second point the formula of step 1 still holds, because it does not use the positive definiteness of \(\mat M\), and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\) is invertible because \(\mat K\) is positive definite. The terms \(\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{da}}\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\) are zero (\(\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}} = \mat M_{\mathrm{da}}^\T = \mat 0\)), so \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\).
  4. Let \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda\mat M\vek v\) with \(\vek v = (\vek x, \vek y) \ne \vek 0\). Since the rows \(\text{d}\) of the mass matrix are zero, the rows \(\text{d}\) of the equation are \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\vek x + \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\vek y = \vek 0\), so \(\vek y = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\vek x\) and \(\vek v = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x\). If \(\vek x = \vek 0\), then also \(\vek y = \vek 0\), so \(\vek v = \vek 0\), which is excluded, hence \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\). The rows \(\mathrm{a}\) are \(\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}}\vek x + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}\vek x = \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\vek x\), which by step 1 of the proof of lemma 21.18 equals \(\tilde{\mat K}\vek x = \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\vek x\).
  5. Conversely: if \(\tilde{\mat K}\vek x = \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\vek x\) and \(\vek v = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\vek x\), the rows \(\text{d}\) of the equation \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda\mat M\vek v\) have both sides zero (\(\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{G}}\) has a zero block \(\text{d}\) by step 1 of the proof of lemma 21.18, and \(\mat M\) has zero rows \(\text{d}\)), and the rows \(\mathrm{a}\) are the equation of the reduced problem. This is the second point.

Source: CK section 14.8.2 (PDF pp. 477-479): Guyan reduction by \(\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} = \mat T^\T\mat M\mat T\) (equations 14.72 and 14.73) and the upper bound by Rayleigh-Ritz. CK section 14.6.2 (PDF p. 467, equation 14.65): with lumped masses the reduced mass matrix is just \(\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\). Point 2 in this form and the proof in steps are not from the sources, the upper bound is by theorem 10.42.

The second point contains lemma 12.45 of chapter 12: for a beam with lumped masses (rotations without inertia) the condensation of the rotations is exact. There we showed this for beams, here it is a special case of the general theorem. The first point says that for no index is the Guyan value below the true one, so Guyan never underestimates the eigenvalues, as we saw for the frame (\(0{,}214286 > 0{,}198062\)). How far above, the theorem does not say, and it cannot say, because that depends on the choice of coordinates. We measure it on the cantilever.

21.4.5 The cantilever: displacements or rotations

We take the cantilever of chapter 12 with two elements of \(0{,}5\) m each (steel, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(\omega_0 = 57{,}883\) 1/s, frequencies measured in units of \(\omega_0\)). The unknowns are, node by node, the displacement and the rotation, \(\vek q = (v_1,\ \varphi_1,\ v_2,\ \varphi_2)^\T\), with the nodes at \(x = L/2\) and \(x = L\). Theorem 21.19, point 2, has its test in the cantilever with lumped masses. On the same cantilever (left, displacements kept) we split the mass of each element of length \(h = 0{,}5\) m in half between its nodes (CK, section 14.2): node \(1\) has \(\rho A\,h\), the end \(\rho A\,h/2\), and the rotations are massless. Then \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \mat 0\), and the Guyan values with kept displacements are \(3{,}15623\) and \(16{,}2580\ \omega_0\), just as in table 14.3 of CK (PDF p. 480) and as the exact condensation of the rotations gives them by lemma 12.45. These are exactly the eigenvalues of the lumped-mass model, and they lie below the true values of the cantilever (\(3{,}15623 < 3{,}51602\), CK notes that lumped masses converge from below, PDF p. 479). The upper bound of theorem 10.42 does not hold here, because \(\mat M\) is singular owing to the massless rotations. What if the rotations have a small inertia \(\varepsilon\,\rho A\,h\)? The true (full) eigenvalues of the model with \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\), \(10^{-4}\) and \(10^{-2}\) differ from the Guyan ones (\(\varepsilon = 0\)) by \(1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-5}\), \(1{,}1 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(0{,}105\) (the largest relative difference of the two frequencies, NumPy). The error is proportional to \(\varepsilon\) (a factor of \(100\) for a factor of \(100\)), so the condition \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \mat 0\) really is a condition: whoever gives the rotations mass should use \(\tilde{\mat M}\) from point 1, otherwise the loss of accuracy is proportional to the neglected mass.

Guyan reduction has one more price that the figure does not show. The matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) are banded, but \(\tilde{\mat K}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M}\) are full (CK, section 14.8.2, caution (2), PDF p. 479). For a cantilever with \(16\) elements, \(\mat K\) of order \(32\) has only \(154\) nonzero entries, the reduced matrix of order \(16\) all \(256\). With \(50\) elements this is \(494\) against \(1688\) of \(2500\) (NumPy, threshold \(10^{-12}\) of the largest entry). The cost of one elimination by lemma 7.44: \(\mat K\) of order \(100\) with half-bandwidth \(3\) (each element spans four consecutive coordinates) needs by equation (7.44) at most \(3 \cdot 4 \cdot (300 - 7)/6 = 586\) multiplications, the reduced full system of order \(50\) by equation (7.43) \(49 \cdot 50 \cdot 51/6 = 20\,825\), which is about \(36\) times more, even though the order is half as large. This does not mean that Guyan reduction is bad, but that it pays off when the order of the shortened model is really small, and not when we use it to halve the order.

21.4.6 Exact condensation and where Guyan is good

Why does the Guyan approximation depend on the ratio \(\lambda/\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1}\)? The exact eigenvalue problem gives the answer. In Guyan's method we eliminated the dependent coordinates by equilibrium without inertia. If we eliminate them with inertia, at a chosen \(\lambda\), we obtain an exact equation for the active coordinates alone. Let us first look at this on the frame (figure 21.10, floors) with the top floor active and \(k = \bar m = 1\). The eigenvalue equation \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\vek v = \vek 0\) has in the rows \(\text{d}\) (floors \(1\) and \(2\)) \(\vek 0 = (\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}})q_3 + (\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\), where \(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} = (0,\ -1)^\T\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{da}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - \lambda & -1 \\ -1 & 2 - \lambda \end{pmatrix}\) with determinant \((2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)\). Solving for \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and inserting into the row \(\mathrm{a}\), \((1 - \lambda)q_3 + (0,\ -1)\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\), gives an equation with one unknown:

\[D(\lambda)\,q_3 = 0 , \qquad D(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda - \frac{2 - \lambda}{(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)} . \tag{21.35}\]

This is one scalar equation, which requires \(D(\lambda) = 0\). The numerator \((1 - \lambda)^2(3 - \lambda) - (2 - \lambda) = -\lambda^3 + 5\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 1\) is exactly the determinant \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\) of the whole frame, and its zeros are \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\), all three true eigenvalues, although we kept a single coordinate. Let us draw \(D\) (figure 21.13).

We build the figure part by part. First we draw the axis \(\lambda\) and the axis \(D\). Along them we place the poles at \(\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1} = 1\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{d}2} = 3\), which are the eigenvalues of floors \(1\) and \(2\) when floor \(3\) is held still (the determinant \((1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)\) is zero): at them \(D\) is not defined, and near them \(|D|\) grows beyond all bounds. The partial fraction decomposition (SymPy) is

\[D(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda - \frac{1/2}{1 - \lambda} - \frac{1/2}{3 - \lambda} , \qquad D'(\lambda) = -1 - \frac{1/2}{(1 - \lambda)^2} - \frac{1/2}{(3 - \lambda)^2} < 0 .\]

The curve \(D\) is therefore continuous and decreasing on each of the three pieces (before the first pole, between the poles and after the second), and the first piece starts at \(\lambda = 0\) with the value \(1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac13\). Its zeros are exactly the eigenvalues of the frame: \(\lambda_1\) before the first pole (its label is in the zoom below), \(\lambda_2\) between the poles and \(\lambda_3\) after the second. The Guyan line is the tangent of the curve at \(\lambda = 0\), \(\tilde K - \lambda\tilde M = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{14}{9}\lambda\), and its zero is the Guyan approximation \(\tilde\lambda_1 = 3/14\). The zoom below shows that it lies to the right of the true zero \(\lambda_1\). The curve \(D\) lies below the tangent, so it has a lower zero. For \(0 \le \lambda < 1\) we expand both fractions in geometric series with positive terms, \(\tfrac{1}{1 - \lambda} = 1 + \lambda + \lambda^2 + \dots\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3 - \lambda} = \tfrac13 + \tfrac{\lambda}{9} + \tfrac{\lambda^2}{27} + \dots\), and obtain \(D(\lambda) = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{14}{9}\lambda - \tfrac{14}{27}\lambda^2 - \tfrac{41}{81}\lambda^3 - \dots\). The term with \(\lambda^k\) for \(k \ge 2\) is \(-\tfrac12(1 + 3^{-k-1})\lambda^k\), so all terms beyond the linear one are negative, and the difference \(D - (\tilde K - \lambda\tilde M)\) is \(-0{,}0014\) at \(\lambda = 0{,}05\), \(-0{,}0057\) at \(0{,}1\), and \(-0{,}0252\) at the true \(\lambda_1 = 0{,}198\) (\(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1} = 0{,}198\)), which shifts the zero by \(0{,}0252/(14/9) = 0{,}0162\), and this is the difference \(0{,}2143 - 0{,}1981\).

The same holds for any system. We write the exact equation in blocks and see that Guyan is its tangent at zero.

Theorem 21.20 Exact dynamic condensation (natančna kondenzacija)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite with the partition (21.31) and let \(\lambda\) not be an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})\). We write

\[\mat D(\lambda) = \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} - (\mat K_{\mathrm{ad}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}})\,(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})^{-1}\,(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}}) . \tag{21.36}\]

Then:

  1. The number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) exactly when \(\mat D(\lambda)\,\vek x = \vek 0\) has a solution \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), and the eigenvector is \(\vek v = (\vek x,\ -(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}})^{-1}(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}})\,\vek x)\).
  2. \(\mat D(0) = \tilde{\mat K}\) and the derivative with respect to \(\lambda\) is \(\mat D'(0) = -\tilde{\mat M}\), so \(\mat D(\lambda) = \tilde{\mat K} - \lambda\tilde{\mat M} + (\text{terms of order } \lambda^2)\): the Guyan reduced problem is the tangent of the exact equation at \(\lambda = 0\).
Proof

We obtain the first point by eliminating the dependent coordinates from the rows \(\text{d}\) at a fixed \(\lambda\), the second by differentiating \(\mat D\) at \(\lambda = 0\).

  1. Let \(\mat N(\lambda) = \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\), invertible by assumption. The two block rows of \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\vek v = \vek 0\) are: the rows \(\mathrm{a}\), \((\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}})\vek x + (\mat K_{\mathrm{ad}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}})\vek y = \vek 0\), and the rows \(\text{d}\), \((\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}})\vek x + \mat N\vek y = \vek 0\), for \(\vek v = (\vek x, \vek y)\).
  2. From the rows \(\text{d}\), \(\vek y = -\mat N^{-1}(\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}})\vek x\). Inserted into the rows \(\mathrm{a}\), this gives \(\mat D(\lambda)\,\vek x = \vek 0\). If \(\vek x = \vek 0\), then by this equation for \(\vek y\) also \(\vek y = \vek 0\) and \(\vek v = \vek 0\), so an eigenvector \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) is possible only with \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\).
  3. Conversely: if \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) and \(\mat D(\lambda)\vek x = \vek 0\), we define \(\vek y\) by step 2. Then the rows \(\text{d}\) hold by construction and the rows \(\mathrm{a}\) by step 2, so \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\vek v = \vek 0\) with \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\). This is the first point.
  4. At \(\lambda = 0\) we have \(\mat N(0) = \mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\), so \(\mat D(0) = \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} = \tilde{\mat K}\) by lemma 21.18, point 1.
  5. Derivative of the inverse: from \(\mat N\,\mat N^{-1} = \mat I\) we get \(\mat N'\mat N^{-1} + \mat N(\mat N^{-1})' = \mat 0\), and since \(\mat N' = -\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\), \((\mat N^{-1})' = \mat N^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat N^{-1}\). The derivative of the triple product \(\mat A\,\mat N^{-1}\mat B\) with \(\mat A = \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\), \(\mat B = \mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \lambda\mat M_{\mathrm{da}}\) at \(\lambda = 0\) is the sum \(-\mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{da}}\). Therefore \(\mat D'(0) = -\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{da}}\).
  6. By theorem 21.19, point 1, with \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{d}}^\T = -\mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\) (symmetry of \(\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}\)), \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} - \mat M_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{da}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{ad}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat M_{\mathrm{dd}}\mat K_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{da}}\), and comparison with step 5 gives \(\mat D'(0) = -\tilde{\mat M}\). The function \(\mat D\) is smooth at \(\lambda = 0\) (it is rational and \(\mat N(0)\) is invertible), so the difference \(\mat D(\lambda) - \tilde{\mat K} + \lambda\tilde{\mat M}\) is of order \(\lambda^2\). This is the second point.

Source: GR section 6.9.1 (PDF p. 499, note 10): eliminating the dependent coordinates from (6.146b) leads to »an intricate eigenvalue problem nonlinear in \(\omega^2\)«. The theorem and the proof in steps are ours, tied to the Guyan approximation as in GR and CK.

What does the theorem say in words? The exact eigenvalue problem has only \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\) unknowns, but it is nonlinear in \(\lambda\) and has poles at the eigenvalues of the dependent coordinates with the active ones fixed. Guyan replaces it by its tangent, which is linear and has the familiar form \(\tilde{\mat K} - \lambda\tilde{\mat M}\). The tangent knows nothing about the poles, so it is good as long as \(\lambda\) is far below the nearest pole \(\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1}\), and makes no sense for \(\lambda\) beyond it. The numbers confirm this. For the frame \(\lambda_1/\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1} = 0{,}198\) and the error is \(8{,}19\) % in the eigenvalue. For the cantilever with \(n = 5\) and kept displacements the ratios \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1}\) for the first four eigenvalues are \(0{,}00015\), \(0{,}0060\), \(0{,}048\) and \(0{,}19\), and the errors of the frequencies relative to the full model with \(n = 5\) are \(0{,}0015\) %, \(0{,}054\) %, \(0{,}48\) % and \(1{,}97\) % (NumPy). With kept rotations the ratios are \(0{,}017\), \(0{,}65\), \(5{,}2\) and \(20\), and the errors \(0{,}78\) %, \(5{,}7\) %, \(14{,}5\) % and \(29{,}4\) %. So the error grows with the ratio: for a ratio below \(0{,}01\) it is below \(0{,}06\) %, for a ratio around \(0{,}2\) it is a few percent, and for a ratio above \(1\) the Guyan value is useless. The ratio \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{d}1}\) is therefore a measure of how far we can trust the Guyan value, and theorem 21.19 alone does not say this, since it guarantees only the upper bound.

The theorem also has a limit: for \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{d}}\) the matrix \(\mat N\) is not invertible and the function \(\mat D\) is not defined. For the frame these are \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = 3\), which are not eigenvalues of the frame (those are \(0{,}198\), \(1{,}555\) and \(3{,}247\)) but poles, in the figure the vertical dashed lines. In general, however, a pole can also be an eigenvalue of the whole structure. If an eigenvector \(\vek v = (\vek 0, \vek y)\) has all active coordinates equal to zero, the rows \(\text{d}\) give the equation \(\mat N(\lambda)\,\vek y = \vek 0\) with \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\), so \(\lambda\) is a pole. The theorem does not capture such eigenvalues, because it says nothing about \(\lambda\) at a pole, and equation (21.36) holds only away from the poles.

The section has shown Guyan reduction from several sides. Static condensation is exact for loads on the kept coordinates (lemma 21.18), and the Guyan approximation for dynamics is a Ritz approximation with an upper bound, exact for dependent coordinates without mass (theorem 21.19). The exact eigenvalue problem (theorem 21.20) shows that Guyan is its tangent at zero, good for \(\lambda\) far below the lowest eigenvalue of the dependent coordinates, and the cantilever showed that the choice of coordinates decides: the active ones should be those with inertia, the dependent ones stiff and light. But what if the dependent coordinates must by no means be static, because there is some vibration inside a part of the structure? Then we add to the Guyan basis the natural vibrations of the interior as well, which section 21.5 does. How we solve the eigenvalue problem of order \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\) when it is still too large is shown by the sections after it, starting with the simplest procedure, which finds one eigenvalue, in section 21.6.

We do not treat the quadratic correction of Guyan reduction (CK, PDF p. 469, reference [14.8]), other methods for test-analysis models with matched coordinates (CK, PDF p. 479, chapter 17), or reduction for moving flexible members (chapter 28). They are not proved in this book.
