Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.11 Transient response with finite elements (prehodni pojav s končnimi elementi)

Section 14.10 assembled the conductivity matrix of a bar from elements and used it to compute the steady (stacionarna) temperature, that is, the temperature that does not change in time. Most heat problems, however, depend on time. A bar that we clamp between two sources at time zero warms up over seconds or hours, and the question of when it reaches a given temperature is not a question about the steady state. Section 14.5 solved this for the bar with an exact series. Now we solve the same question with finite elements, which also exist for bodies that have no closed-form series. There are three questions: what time adds to the node equations, how a computer takes a step in time, and when that step fails.

The model is the bar of corollary 14.10 (figure 14.101 below): length \(L\), constant cross section \(A\), constant \(k\), \(\rho\) and \(c\) (section 14.2), lateral surface insulated and no source, so \(\rho c A\,\partial T/\partial t = \partial(kA\,\partial T/\partial x)/\partial x\). We hold both ends at temperature \(0\). Temperature is measured from this value, as the change \(\Delta T\) in chapter 3. The idealizations of section 14.2 stay: a rigid body at rest, constant \(k\) and \(c\), no coupling with deformation. We add two new ones: in space the temperature is linear within each element (section 14.10), and in time we compute it only at the times \(t_{\mathrm{s}} = s\,\Delta t\).

The letter \(k\) is the conductivity in this chapter, so we count the steps in time with \(s\) and not with \(k\) as in section 10.13 of chapter 10. We write the element length as \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) because \(h\) is the film coefficient (section 14.3), \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an angle, and the parameter of the scheme will be \(\beta\). We measure time in the unit \(L^2/\kappa\) with \(\kappa = k/(\rho c)\) (section 14.2), which is a unit of seconds because \(\kappa\) has the unit m\(^2\)/s. Time \(t\) is then the Fourier number \(\mathrm{Fo} = \kappa t/L^2\), and we compute with \(L = 1\) and \(\kappa = 1\). The book has no sourced values of \(k\) and \(c\) for steel yet, so we do not use steel here. The results in \(\mathrm{Fo}\) hold for every material.

We draw the bar as in section 14.10. The straight bar with its nodes is cut into \(n = 8\) equal elements of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/n\), the nodes are \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = j\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\). We hold the two ends at temperature \(0\), like a clamp in mechanics, where a displacement is prescribed: here the temperature is prescribed and the flow through the end is not. At the start only node \(x_4\) is hot, the initial field has \(1\) K there and \(0\) at the other nodes. Between the nodes the field is linear, so the initial field is exactly the hat function \(\psi_4\) (definition 6.25) multiplied by \(1\) K. Such a sharp start is chosen on purpose: it contains all spatial shapes that are symmetric about the middle of the bar, from the longest to the shortest that eight elements can still describe. The scheme has to cope with all of them, so the start will show what the scheme does not cope with.

What does time add to the node equations? The element stores heat. By definition 14.6 a mass \(m\) needs the heat \(m\,c\,\Delta T\) to raise its temperature by \(\Delta T\). An element of mass \(\rho A h_{\mathrm{e}}\) therefore needs \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\) joules per kelvin if we raise it uniformly. The question of how this capacity is divided among the nodes is the same as for mass in section 10.8.1 of chapter 10. There the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot u^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) gave the mass matrix of an element, here the heat stored in the element will give a matrix with \(\rho c\) in place of \(\rho\). Look at the element of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the local coordinate \(\xi\) from its left end (we do not use the letter \(s\), which counts the steps). Its shape functions are \(\psi_1^e = 1 - \xi/h_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\psi_2^e = \xi/h_{\mathrm{e}}\). A simpler idea is to divide the capacity of the element into two lumped halves \(\tfrac12\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\), one at each end.

Definition 14.58 Capacity matrix (kapacitivna matrika)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an element of a bar of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definition 7.1, figure 14.101), constant area \(A\), density \(\rho\) and specific heat \(c\), and let \(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the row of its shape functions (definition 7.2). The heat capacity (toplotna kapaciteta) of the element is \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\), the heat that raises its temperature by \(1\) K. The consistent capacity matrix (konsistentna kapacitivna matrika) of the element is the matrix of order \(2\)

\[\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\rho c A\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{14.62}\]

and the lumped capacity matrix (zbrana kapacitivna matrika) is \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac12\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat I\). The matrices of a bar with \(n\) elements, \(\mat C\) and \(\mat C^{\mathrm{z}}\), are obtained like the matrix \(\mat K\) in theorem 7.13: we add \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definition 7.11) and keep the rows and columns of the free nodes (theorem 7.18). Before the rows and columns of the prescribed nodes are removed, the sum of the entries of the assembled matrix is \(\rho c A L\) for both, the heat capacity of the whole bar.

Source: COOK (12.2-4) and the comment under (12.2-5), PDF p. 474: "specific heat (or capacity) matrix", analogous to the mass matrix, full when obtained from the integral and diagonal ("lumped") when the capacity of the element is divided among the nodes. The notation and the values for the bar are ours: the matrices are those of lemma 10.47 and definition 10.48 with \(\rho c\) in place of \(\rho\), since \(\rho\) is only a constant factor in the integral there.

The matrix is called capacity because its sum tells how much heat the bar takes in per kelvin, and it is called consistent because it uses the same shape functions as the conductivity matrix (lemma 14.54), like the consistent mass matrix (section 10.8.1 of chapter 10). A test on the element with \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}} = 1\) gives the numbers \(\tfrac26\) and \(\tfrac16\) in \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the sum of all four entries \((2 + 1 + 1 + 2)/6 = 1\). The lumped matrix gives the same sum, so lumped and consistent differ only in how they divide the same heat.

Figure 14.102 shows where the numbers \(\tfrac26\) and \(\tfrac16\) come from. On the axes are the local coordinate \(\xi\) and the rate of warming \(\dot T\). We heat the left node \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) at \(1\) K/s and hold the right one, so the element warms at the rate \(\psi_1^e\), which is the sloping line from \(1\) to \(0\). The heat the element takes in per second is \(\rho c A\) times the area of the triangle under the line, \(\tfrac12 h_{\mathrm{e}}\). The consistent matrix divides every small piece of this heat between the nodes in the ratio \(\psi_1^e : \psi_2^e\) at its place, as a load on a plank is divided between two supports: the closer it is to one, the more of it that one carries. The piece at \(\xi = 0{,}27\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\) warms at \(0{,}73\) K/s and gives \(0{,}73\) of its heat to the left node and \(0{,}27\) to the right one. The left node thus receives the area under the parabola \((\psi_1^e)^2\), which is \(\tfrac13 h_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac26 h_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the right node the rest of the triangle, \(\tfrac12 h_{\mathrm{e}} - \tfrac13 h_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac16 h_{\mathrm{e}}\), because \(\psi_1^e - (\psi_1^e)^2 = \psi_1^e\psi_2^e\). This is the first column of the matrix \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\). The lumped matrix gives the whole triangle to the left node, \(\tfrac12 h_{\mathrm{e}}\), and nothing to the right one. The figure adds to the definition the meaning of the number \(\tfrac16\) off the diagonal: warming one node demands heat in the equation of its neighbour too. The lumped matrix has no such tie, so with it the node equations are solved by division, and the price is eigenvalues that are too low (lemma 14.61). The following lemma shows how the equation of the bar appears in matrices. Its path is the same as for theorem 7.13: we multiply the equation by a test function, transform it by parts in the integral and insert a field made of shape functions. The weight \(\psi_1^e : \psi_2^e\) of the figure is exactly this test function.

Lemma 14.59 Node equations of the transient problem (enačbe vozlišč prehodnega pojava)

Let the bar be as above with \(n\) equal elements, \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) the hat functions of the nodes \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\) (definition 6.25), \(T_{\mathrm{h}}(x, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{n-1}T_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x)\) and \(\vek T = (T_1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{n}-1})^\T\). Let \(T(x, t)\) be a solution of \(\rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = (kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(T(0, t) = T(L, t) = 0\) that is continuously differentiable in \(t\) and twice continuously differentiable in \(x\). Then for every \(i = 1, \dots, n - 1\)

\[\int_0^L\rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 , \tag{14.63}\]

and if we replace \(T\) by \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\) in this equation (Galerkin method, definition 6.42, trial and test functions are \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}-1}\)) we obtain the system

\[\mat C\,\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\,\vek T = \vek 0 , \tag{14.64}\]

where \(\mat C\) is the capacity matrix (definition 14.58) and \(\mat K\) the conductivity matrix of the bar with \(kA\) in place of \(EA\) (lemma 14.54). Both are symmetric and \(\mat C\) is positive definite.

Proof

We have to show that the equation turns into (14.63) by multiplying and integrating by parts, and that the Galerkin replacement gives the matrices we have already computed.

  1. We multiply the equation \(\rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = (kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\) by \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and integrate over \([0, L]\): \(\int\rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int (kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. The function \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is continuous and piecewise linear, \(kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuously differentiable in \(x\), so both are piecewise continuously differentiable on a common partition. By lemma 6.13 with \(N = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(w = kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\int (kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = [kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}]_0^L - \int kA\,T_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\). The term in the square bracket is zero because \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(0) = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}(L) = 0\) for a free node \(i\): the hat function of a free node reaches only to the neighbouring nodes and is zero at the ends of the bar. This proves equation (14.63).
  3. In equation (14.63) we replace \(T\) by \(T_{\mathrm{h}}\). Since \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) does not depend on time, \(\partial T_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial t = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dot T_{\mathrm{j}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), and by linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10) row \(i\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\int\rho c A\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot T_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\int kA\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\big)T_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\).
  4. The first integral is a sum over elements of the integrals \(\int\rho c A\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), which by equation (14.62) give the entries of the matrix \(\mat C\) (on one element these are the areas under the products \(\psi_1^e\psi_1^e\) and \(\psi_1^e\psi_2^e\), blue and orange on figure 14.102, the same is done in the proof of lemma 10.50 with \(\rho\) in place of \(\rho c\)). The second integral is an entry of the matrix \(\mat K\) by theorem 7.13 and lemma 14.54. Row \(i\) is therefore row \(i\) of equation (14.64).
  5. The symmetry is visible in the integrals, since \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\) are symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\). The positive definiteness of \(\mat C\) is lemma 10.50 with \(\rho c\) in place of \(\rho\) (for \(\vek T \ne \vek 0\), \(\vek T^\T\mat C\vek T = \int\rho c A\,T_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\)).

Source: COOK (12.4-1), PDF p. 478: the same equation \([\mat C]\{\dot{\mat T}\} + [\mat K_{\mathrm{T}}]\{\mat T\} = \{\mat R_{\mathrm{T}}\}\) from the functional (12.2-1), PDF p. 473. Our proof from the strong form with test functions is not from the source.

Equation (14.64) for the bar is a system of ordinary differential equations of first order for \(n - 1\) functions of time: space is already discretized, time is not. We call it semi-discrete (polidiskretna), because it is discrete in space and continuous in time. The form of its solution is given by the same theorem as for vibration, because \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) are symmetric and \(\mat C\) is positive definite.

Theorem 14.60 Transient response by modes (prehodni pojav po načinih)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric and \(\mat C\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(m\), and let \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the eigenvalues and \(\mat C\)-orthonormal eigencolumns of the pair \((\mat K, \mat C)\) of theorem 10.21. For every initial column \(\vek T^0\) the system \(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T = \vek 0\) with \(\vek T(0) = \vek T^0\) has exactly one solution

\[\vek T(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} t}\,\big(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat C\,\vek T^0\big)\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} . \tag{14.65}\]

If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, then \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) for every \(r\), every mode is a decaying exponential function and \(\vek T(t) \to \vek 0\) as \(t \to \infty\). Higher modes decay faster: mode \(r\) is reduced by the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} t}\) up to time \(t\) (after \(t = 0{,}1\)), and the ratio of this factor to the factor of mode \(1\) is \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_1)t}\).

Proof

We use the decoupling of theorem 10.28, but for an equation of first order.

  1. Let \(\Phi\) be the modal matrix (definition 10.23) and \(\boldsymbol\eta = \Phi^\T\mat C\,\vek T\). By lemma 10.24, \(\Phi^\T\mat C\Phi = \mat I\), \(\Phi^\T\mat K\Phi = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}})\) and \(\vek T = \Phi\boldsymbol\eta\). The matrix \(\Phi\) does not depend on time, so \(\dot{\vek T} = \Phi\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}\).
  2. We multiply the equation \(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T = \vek 0\) from the left by \(\Phi^\T\): \(\Phi^\T\mat C\Phi\,\dot{\boldsymbol\eta} + \Phi^\T\mat K\Phi\,\boldsymbol\eta = \vek 0\), hence \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(r\).
  3. Conversely, if \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for all \(r\), then \(\Phi^\T(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T) = \vek 0\). The matrix \(\Phi^\T\) is invertible (step 3 of the proof of theorem 10.28), so \(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T = \vek 0\). The system and the decoupled equations are equivalent.
  4. By lemma 9.19 (item 1, \(a = -\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(t_0 = 0\)) the equation \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = -\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat C\vek T^0\) has exactly one solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} t}\). By step 3 the solution of the system is therefore unique, and \(\vek T = \Phi\boldsymbol\eta = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is equation (14.65).
  5. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, then by theorem 10.21 \(\lambda_1 = \min\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v > 0\) among the \(\vek v\) with \(\vek v^\T\mat C\vek v = 1\) (the minimum is positive because \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\)), and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_1 > 0\). Hence \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} t} \to 0\) and \(\vek T(t) \to \vek 0\). The ratio is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} t}/\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_1 t}\).

Source: COOK §12.4 (PDF p. 478 to 479), (12.4-2) to (12.4-5): the modal method, the eigenproblem, normalization with respect to \([\mat C]\) and \(n\) uncoupled equations. The proof with the lemma on the exponential function, the uniqueness and the converse (steps 3 and 4) are not from the source.

The proof showed that a transient problem of the bar behaves like \(m\) independent equations \(\dot\eta + \lambda\eta = 0\), and that the same objects are at work as in vibration, \(\mat C\)-orthogonal eigencolumns, but in time \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\) appears in place of \(\cos\omega t\). In vibration \(\lambda = \omega^2\) and the modes oscillate, here they decay. We saw the same for the bar with the exact series (section 14.5): the modes \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) decay with the factors \(\mathrm{e}^{-n^2\pi^2\kappa t/L^2}\), the higher ones first, unlike the string (chapter 13), where the modes did not decay. Now we have finitely many such modes and can compute them exactly.

The hypothesis of positive definiteness of \(\mat K\) needs a case where it is missing. Let the bar with two elements (\(h_{\mathrm{e}} = 1/2\), \(\rho c A L = 1\), consistent capacity) be insulated at both ends, so all three nodes are free. The matrix \(\mat K = \tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is not positive definite, because \(\mat K\vek 1 = \vek 0\): a constant temperature causes no flow. Theorem 10.21 still holds for \(\mat K\) without positive definiteness and gives \(\lambda_1 = 0\), \(\lambda_2 = 12\), \(\lambda_3 = 48\) (NumPy, in the unit \(\kappa/L^2\)). The expansion (14.65) remains correct, because the proof did not use the positive definiteness of \(\mat K\) up to step 4. The last part of the theorem does not hold, because the mode with \(\lambda_1 = 0\) does not decay: \(\mathrm{e}^{0} = 1\). Below the bar are the axes \(x\) and \(T\). For the start \((1, 0, 0)\) K the temperature of the nodes at \(t = 0{,}1\) is \(0{,}403\), \(0{,}248\) and \(0{,}101\) K, and at \(t = 1\) it is \(0{,}25\) K everywhere and stays so. The heat of the bar has been conserved because the insulation does not let it out of the bar, and it has spread evenly: the thick orange line is horizontal and does not lie on zero. The final temperature is the average weighted by the capacity of the nodes, \(\vek 1^\T\mat C\vek T^0/\vek 1^\T\mat C\vek 1 = 0{,}25\) K. This is the same phenomenon as in the steady problem with only prescribed flows in section 14.3 (lemma 14.18): the matrix is singular and the solution is determined up to a constant.

For a bar with prescribed temperatures at both ends we know the eigenvalues in closed form, as in vibration (theorem 10.52). We compute them for both kinds of capacity, and figure 14.104 after the lemma shows the numbers.

Lemma 14.61 Eigenvalues of a bar with both temperatures prescribed (lastne vrednosti palice s predpisanima temperaturama na koncih)

Let a uniform bar of length \(L\) with \(kA\) and \(\rho c A\) constant have both end sections at temperature \(0\), divided into \(n \ge 2\) equal elements with \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = L/n\), and let \(\kappa = k/(\rho c)\). For \(r = 1, \dots, n - 1\) let \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = r\pi/n\) and \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}} = (\sin\theta_{\mathrm{r}}, \sin 2\theta_{\mathrm{r}}, \dots, \sin(n-1)\theta_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\). Then \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigencolumn of the pair \((\mat K, \mat C)\) and of the pair \((\mat K, \mat C^{\mathrm{z}})\) with the eigenvalues

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{6\kappa}{h_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\,\frac{1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}}{2 + \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}} , \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{2\kappa}{h_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\,\big(1 - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}\big) . \tag{14.66}\]

Both sequences increase, \(0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < \lambda_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), and these are all the eigenvalues of each pair. We have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} \le r^2\pi^2\kappa/L^2\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}-1}^{\mathrm{k}} < 12\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) (upper bounds) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}-1}^{\mathrm{z}} < 4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\). The consistent matrix does not give eigenvalues below the exact ones, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{k}} \ge r^2\pi^2\kappa/L^2\).

Proof

We have to show that every row of the equation \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat C\vek\phi\) at \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is satisfied by the same \(\lambda\), and then the inequalities. Let \(\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \sin j\theta\) for every integer \(j\). At \(j = 0\) and \(j = n\), \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\), because \(\sin n\theta = \sin r\pi = 0\).

  1. Row \(j\) (\(1 \le j \le n - 1\)) of \(\mat K\) is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(-\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 2\phi_{\mathrm{j}} - \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1})\), where \(\phi_0 = \phi_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) are the removed end sections. By the addition theorem (corollary 8.16, applied to the angles \(j\theta\) and \(\pm\theta\)), \(\sin(j\theta + \theta) + \sin(j\theta - \theta) = 2\sin j\theta\,\cos\theta\), so \(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 2\cos\theta\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The row is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. Row \(j\) of \(\mat C\) is \(\tfrac{\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}(\phi_{\mathrm{j}-1} + 4\phi_{\mathrm{j}} + \phi_{\mathrm{j}+1}) = \tfrac{\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}(4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), and of \(\mat C^{\mathrm{z}}\) it is \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) (an interior node has two elements, each with \(\tfrac12\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\)).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, row \(j\) of the equation \(\mat K\vek\phi = \lambda\mat C\vek\phi\) is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\,\tfrac{\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}}{6}(4 + 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), which \(\lambda^{\mathrm{k}}\) of equation (14.66) satisfies for every \(j\), because \(\tfrac{k}{\rho c} = \kappa\). For \(\mat C^{\mathrm{z}}\) the equation is \(\tfrac{kA}{h_{\mathrm{e}}}(2 - 2\cos\theta)\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\,\phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}}\) satisfies it. The vector \(\vek\phi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is not zero, because \(\phi_1 = \sin\theta_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\).
  4. Increase. The function \(1 - \cos\theta\) has the derivative \(\sin\theta > 0\) on \((0, \pi)\), and the quotient \((1 - \cos\theta)/(2 + \cos\theta)\) has by the quotient rule the derivative \(3\sin\theta/(2 + \cos\theta)^2 > 0\) (as in step 7 of the proof of theorem 10.52). The angles \(\theta_1 < \dots < \theta_{\mathrm{n}-1} = \pi - \pi/n\) lie in \((0, \pi)\), so both sequences increase.
  5. All of them. By theorem 10.21 the pair has \(n - 1\) eigenvalues, and by corollary 10.22 every eigenvalue is one of them. We have \(n - 1\) different ones, so these are all.
  6. Upper bounds. At \(\theta_{\mathrm{n}-1} < \pi\) we have \(\cos\theta > -1\), hence \((1 - \cos\theta)/(2 + \cos\theta) < 2\) (the inequality \(1 - \cos\theta < 4 + 2\cos\theta\) is \(\cos\theta > -1\)) and \(\lambda^{\mathrm{k}} < 12\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\). Likewise \(2(1 - \cos\theta) < 4\) and \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}} < 4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\).
  7. The lumped one is below the exact one. By lemma 10.53, \(1 - \cos\theta \le \theta^2/2\), so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}^{\mathrm{z}} \le \tfrac{2\kappa}{h_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\cdot\tfrac{\theta_{\mathrm{r}}^2}{2} = \kappa\,(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}/h_{\mathrm{e}})^2 = r^2\pi^2\kappa/L^2\), because \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}}/h_{\mathrm{e}} = r\pi/L\).
  8. The consistent one is above the exact one. The consistent pair is a Ritz approximation of the eigenproblem of a bar with both ends clamped (trial space \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), matrix \(\mat K\) from the form \(B\), matrix \(\mat C\) from the form with \(\rho c A\)), so by theorem 13.82 every value lies above the exact one with the same ordinal number. Everything that follows stands on these numbers (upper bounds): the largest eigenvalue is the phenomenon that the time step must still see. The question is how to compute the solution (14.65) for a body where we do not know the eigenvalues or there are too many to compute. Then we solve equation (14.64) step by step. If the temperature \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) is known at time \(t_{\mathrm{s}} = s\,\Delta t\), we look for the temperature \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1}\) at time \(t_{\mathrm{s}+1}\). The equation says that the rate of heat storage \(\mat C\dot{\vek T}\) equals the flow \(-\mat K\vek T\). Over a step we compare them by replacing the rate with the difference \((\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} - \vek T_{\mathrm{s}})/\Delta t\) and computing the flow at a weighted average of the temperatures at the start and the end of the step.

We show the same rule on a single mode \(\dot\eta = -\lambda\eta\), which is the easiest to understand. We draw the axes of time and \(\eta\). Let \(\lambda\,\Delta t = z = 3\) and \(\eta = 1\) at time \(t_{\mathrm{s}}\). The exact solution is the curve \(\mathrm{e}^{-z\tau}\) (\(\tau = (t - t_{\mathrm{s}})/\Delta t\)), at the end of the step \(\mathrm{e}^{-3} = 0{,}0498\). The rule that takes the flow at the start of the step, forward, draws a line with the slope of the curve at the start, \(-\lambda\eta = -3\), and lands at \(1 - 3 = -2\), below zero. The rule that takes the flow at the end of the step, backward, draws a line with the slope that the equation \(\dot\eta = -\lambda\eta\) prescribes at its end point: this slope is \(-\lambda\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1}\), so \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1}\) has to be computed at the same time, from \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1} = \eta_{\mathrm{s}} - z\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1}\). We get \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1} = 1/(1 + z) = 0{,}25\), above the exact value. The average of the two, the trapezoidal rule, gives the slope \(-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\eta_{\mathrm{s}} + \eta_{\mathrm{s}+1})\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}+1} = (1 - z/2)/(1 + z/2) = -0{,}2\).

All three rules are members of one family. We write it for a system.

Definition 14.62 The β-scheme (shema s parametrom β)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric and \(\mat C\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(m\), \(\vek f(t)\) a column of heat loads, \(\Delta t > 0\) a time step, \(t_{\mathrm{s}} = s\,\Delta t\) and \(\vek T_0\) the initial temperature. The scheme with parameter \(\beta \in [0, 1]\) (figure 14.105) for the system \(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T = \vek f\) is the sequence of columns \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) that satisfies

\[\mat C\,\frac{\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} - \vek T_{\mathrm{s}}}{\Delta t} + \mat K\big((1 - \beta)\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} + \beta\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1}\big) = (1 - \beta)\,\vek f_{\mathrm{s}} + \beta\,\vek f_{\mathrm{s}+1} , \qquad \vek f_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek f(t_{\mathrm{s}}) . \tag{14.67}\]

At \(\beta = 0\) the scheme is called the forward scheme (shema naprej, forward Euler), at \(\beta = \tfrac12\) the trapezoidal rule or Crank-Nicolson scheme, and at \(\beta = 1\) the backward scheme (shema nazaj, backward Euler).

Source: COOK (12.4-6) to (12.4-8), PDF p. 479: the same scheme with parameter \(\beta\) ("β = 0 forward difference or Euler, β = 1/2 Crank-Nicolson or trapezoidal rule, β = 1 backward difference", PDF p. 480). COOK writes \((\tfrac1{\Delta t}[\mat C] + \beta[\mat K_{\mathrm{T}}])\{\mat T\}_{\mathrm{n}+1} = \dots\), which is equation (14.67) rearranged.

The names have a reason in where we take the flow. At \(\beta = 0\) we take it in the known state and look forward from it, at \(\beta = 1\) in the state we are still looking for, so we look backward from the new time. At \(\beta = \tfrac12\) the flow is the average of the two ends, which is the same average as in the trapezoidal rule of chapter 10 (definition 10.92), here for an equation of first order, and hence the name trapezoidal rule (blackwhite line). At \(\beta = 0\) and a diagonal lumped capacity \(\mat C^{\mathrm{z}}\) the step reduces to a division, \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} = \vek T_{\mathrm{s}} - \Delta t\,(\mat C^{\mathrm{z}})^{-1}(\mat K\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} - \vek f_{\mathrm{s}})\), so lumped capacity is usually combined with the forward scheme (COOK PDF p. 480: little work per step, but \(\Delta t\) is small too). At \(\beta > 0\) every step requires solving a system with the matrix \(\mat C + \beta\Delta t\,\mat K\), which does not depend on \(s\) and which we factor once (COOK PDF p. 479).

What the scheme does with a single mode is told by the following lemma.

Lemma 14.63 Amplification factor of the scheme (ojačitveni faktor sheme)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric and \(\mat C\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(m\) with eigenpairs \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) of theorem 10.21, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\), \(\beta \in [0, 1]\) and \(\Delta t > 0\). The matrix \(\mat C + \beta\Delta t\,\mat K\) is positive definite, hence invertible, and the scheme (14.67) with \(\vek f = \vek 0\) gives exactly one sequence \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) for every \(\vek T_0\). The factor \(g_\beta\) is one step of a single mode of figure 14.105: forward at \(\beta = 0\), backward at \(\beta = 1\). The modal coordinates \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat C\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) satisfy

\[\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}+1} = g_\beta(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}} , \qquad g_\beta(z) = \frac{1 - (1 - \beta)\,z}{1 + \beta\,z} , \tag{14.68}\]

and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} g_\beta(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)^s\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}^2\).

Proof

We have to show invertibility, the splitting into modes and the sum of squares.

  1. For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), \(\vek x^\T(\mat C + \beta\Delta t\,\mat K)\vek x \ge \vek x^\T\mat C\vek x > 0\), because \(\beta\Delta t\,\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x \ge 0\). As in step 5 of the proof of lemma 10.93 this gives invertibility. Equation (14.67) is then \((\mat C + \beta\Delta t\,\mat K)\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} = (\mat C - (1 - \beta)\Delta t\,\mat K)\,\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) with exactly one solution \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1}\).
  2. We multiply the equation at \(\vek f = \vek 0\) from the left by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\). Since \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat C\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\mat K\), \(\mat C\) are symmetric, \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat C\). Hence \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. From step 2: \((\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}+1} - \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}})/\Delta t + \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\big((1 - \beta)\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}} + \beta\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}+1}\big) = 0\). With \(z = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\) it follows that \((1 + \beta z)\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}+1} = (1 - (1 - \beta)z)\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}\), which is equation (14.68).
  4. By corollary 10.22, \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by induction from step 3, \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}} = g^s\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}\). Since \(\Phi^\T\mat C\Phi = \mat I\) (lemma 10.24), \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \boldsymbol\eta_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\boldsymbol\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\).

The exact solution of a single mode multiplies \(\eta\) by \(\mathrm{e}^{-z}\) over a step (grey curve on the graph of the step). Every scheme multiplies it by a different number. The difference is visible in the expansion (SymPy), \(\mathrm{e}^{-z} - g_\beta(z) = (\tfrac12 - \beta)\,z^2 + O(z^3)\), and at \(\beta = \tfrac12\) it is \(z^3/12 + O(z^4)\). For a small step, forward and backward are therefore accurate to first order (the error per step is of order \(z^2\), over \(1/\Delta t\) steps of order \(\Delta t\)), and the trapezoidal rule to second order (COOK PDF p. 480: "second-order accurate", and only first order for \(\beta \ne \tfrac12\)). A test on the first mode of the lumped bar with eight elements, \(\lambda_1 = 9{,}7434\), at \(\Delta t = 0{,}002\) (\(z = 0{,}0195\)) and time \(t = 0{,}1\) after \(50\) steps: the exact value of the semi-discrete solution is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_1 t} = 0{,}37744\). Forward gives \(0{,}37383\) (\(-0{,}96\) %), backward \(0{,}38099\) (\(+0{,}94\) %) and the trapezoidal rule \(0{,}37743\) (\(-0{,}003\) %). All three differ from the true solution of the bar, \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^2 t} = 0{,}37271\), by another \(1{,}3\) %, but that is an error in space, not in time, and it comes from the elements. The errors of forward and backward have opposite signs and about the same size, because the expansion has the terms \(\pm z^2/2\).

Accuracy is not the only question. At a large step the graph of the step shows that the forward scheme lands below zero, so there must be a condition on the size of the step. Lemma 14.63 gives it exactly: the sequence \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) is bounded exactly when all \(g^s\) are bounded, that is, when \(|g_\beta(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)| \le 1\). In chapter 10 we wrote the definition of boundedness for vibration (definition 10.94), here we have it for first order: a scheme is stable (stabilna) at the step \(\Delta t\) if for every start \(\vek T_0\) there is a \(B\) such that \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \le B\) for every \(s\).

Theorem 14.64 Stability of the forward scheme (stabilnost sheme naprej)

Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite and \(\mat C\) symmetric positive definite, \(\lambda_{\max}\) the largest eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat C)\) and \(\vek f = \vek 0\). The scheme (14.67) at \(\beta = 0\) is stable exactly when

\[\Delta t \le \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} . \tag{14.69}\]

On figure 14.105 at \(z = 3 > 2\) the forward step landed at \(g_0 = -2\), above \(1\) in absolute value. At \(\Delta t < \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) every mode decays in absolute value but changes sign if \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t > 1\). At \(\Delta t > \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) the sequence is unbounded for every start that has a component \(\eta_{\mathrm{m},0} \ne 0\) in the mode \(m\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \lambda_{\max}\).

Proof

We use lemma 14.63 with \(\beta = 0\) and \(g_0(z) = 1 - z\).

  1. By the lemma, \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} g_0(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)^{2s}\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}^2\). If \(|g_0(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)| \le 1\) for every \(r\), the sum is at most \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},0}^2 = \vek T_0^\T\mat C\vek T_0\) and the sequence is bounded.
  2. For \(z > 0\), \(|1 - z| \le 1\) exactly when \(0 \le z \le 2\) (for \(z \le 1\), \(1 - z \ge 0\) and less than \(1\), for \(z > 1\), \(|1 - z| = z - 1 \le 1\) at \(z \le 2\)). Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\max}\), the condition for all \(r\) is equivalent to \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t \le 2\), which is equation (14.69).
  3. If \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t > 2\), then \(|1 - \lambda_{\max}\Delta t| > 1\), and for a start with \(\eta_{\mathrm{m},0} \ne 0\), \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \ge |1 - \lambda_{\max}\Delta t|^{2s}\,\eta_{\mathrm{m},0}^2 \to \infty\) as \(s \to \infty\). The scheme is not stable. Such a case is the orange line on the graph of the step: at \(z = 3\) it ends at \(-2\), and the size of the mode doubles in one step.
  4. At \(\Delta t < \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\), \(|g_0| < 1\), so every mode decays. The factor \(1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\) is negative exactly when \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t > 1\), so the mode changes sign at every step.

Source: COOK (12.4-9), PDF p. 479 to 480: "if β < 0,5 the algorithm is conditionally stable", the largest step \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) with the largest eigenvalue (12.4-2) (at \(\beta = 0\)). The proof with lemma 14.63 is not from the source.

The proof showed where the condition comes from: the forward scheme takes \(z\,\eta\) away from the mode \(\eta\) and \((1 - z)\,\eta\) remains. At \(z > 1\) it takes away more than there is of the mode and crosses zero. At \(z > 2\) the overshoot is larger than the original value and the size grows. This is shown by the graph of the step at \(z = 3\) (the orange line ends at \(-2\)) and by the graph of the factor that follows.

On the graph the axes are \(z = \lambda\Delta t\) and \(g\), the exact factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-z}\) is the grey curve that falls from \(1\) towards \(0\) and never becomes negative. The bounds \(\pm 1\) are dashed: inside them the mode is smaller at the end of the step than at the start. The forward scheme is the line \(1 - z\), which crosses the lower bound at \(z = 2\), where \(\Delta t = \Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) for the mode with \(\lambda = \lambda_{\max}\). The number of steps up to a given time is therefore set by the largest eigenvalue, although we are interested in the slow modes.

How much this costs is shown by the numbers for a bar with eight elements (\(L = \kappa = 1\)). Lumped capacity gives \(\lambda_{\max} = 246{,}26\) and \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 0{,}00812\), consistent \(686{,}51\) and \(0{,}00291\) (lemma 14.61, the upper bounds on the graph of eigenvalues). The lemma also gives an estimate without computing eigenvalues: since \(\lambda_{\max} < 4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) for lumped and \(< 12\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) for consistent capacity, \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} > h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/(2\kappa)\) and \(> h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/(6\kappa)\) respectively, and every step up to these values is stable. The critical step is therefore proportional to \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\). With \(16\) elements \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) of lumped capacity is \(0{,}00197\), which is \(4{,}12\) times less than with eight. A half-size element means a four times smaller step, so four times more steps, and twice as many nodes, so eight times more work in one dimension. The fastest mode contributes nothing meanwhile: in the lumped bar with eight elements it falls to \(1\) % in the time \(\ln 100/\lambda_{\max} = 0{,}0187\), which is \(2{,}3\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\), while the first mode needs \(0{,}473\) to reach \(1\) %. The step of the forward scheme is therefore set by a phenomenon that has long been dead.

We get the same limit without eigenvalues, from the flow between the nodes (figure 14.107). This is a second view of theorem 14.64: the factor \(1 - z\) says that the sequence grows, and the flow says what happens to the heat meanwhile. Take a piece of a bar far from its ends with lumped capacity, and above it the axis of temperature. At the start the nodes have \(+1\) K and \(-1\) K in turn (the grey dashed line). This is the shape that varies fastest from node to node, like the fastest mode. The node \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(2\) K warmer than both neighbours at the distance \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\), so by Fourier's law (section 14.1) a flow of density \(2k/h_{\mathrm{e}}\) runs to each side, together \(4kA/h_{\mathrm{e}}\) watts. The lumped capacity of the node is \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\), so the node cools at the rate \(4kA/h_{\mathrm{e}}\) divided by \(\rho c A h_{\mathrm{e}}\), which is \(4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) kelvins per second. The forward scheme keeps this rate for the whole step, and the node drops by \(4\kappa\Delta t/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\). Here the assumption of the scheme enters: the flow is the same during the whole step as at its start. For a small step this is almost true, because the temperatures change little within the step. In truth the flow weakens as the temperatures of the neighbours approach each other, and dies at zero, where all the nodes are equally warm. With a drop of \(1\) K the node ends exactly there. With a drop between \(1\) K and \(2\) K it ends below zero but still between the lines \(+1\) and \(-1\): the zigzag turns over but does not grow. On the figure the drop is \(3\) K: the node ends at \(-2\) K, its neighbour at \(+2\) K, and the new zigzag (orange) is twice as large as the one before. The node has given away more heat than it had above its neighbours, and the scheme does not notice, because it does not correct the flow during the step. The condition that the zigzag does not grow is \(4\kappa\Delta t/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2 \le 2\), that is \(\Delta t \le h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/(2\kappa)\), the same estimate as above. The rate \(4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) is the bound \(\lambda^{\mathrm{z}}_{\mathrm{n}-1} < 4\kappa/h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) of lemma 14.61. On a bar with its ends at \(0\) the zigzag shrinks towards the ends, so its fastest mode is a little slower, \(246{,}26\) instead of \(256\) with eight elements.

The limit is sharp, which we show just before it and just after it on the fastest mode of the lumped bar with eight elements. On the left graph the axes are the number of steps \(s\) and the size of the mode, the latter on a logarithmic scale, so a constant factor per step is a straight line. At \(\Delta t = 0{,}99\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) the factor is \(-0{,}98\): the size after \(100\) steps is \(0{,}133\) of the original and after \(1000\) steps \(1{,}7\cdot 10^{-9}\), the mode decays with sign changes (the blackwhite line). At \(\Delta t = 1{,}01\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) the factor is \(-1{,}02\): the size after \(100\) steps is \(7{,}2\) times the original and after \(1000\) steps \(4{,}0\cdot 10^{8}\) times (the orange line). One percent in the step separates a solution from nonsense (the dashed vertical line at \(z = 2\) on the graph of the factor). A start that does not contain the fastest mode hides the limit (the right graph, the largest temperature on the bar after \(s\) steps). Take as the start the values \(\sin(\pi x_{\mathrm{j}})\) at the nodes, which is the first eigencolumn and is \(\mat C\)-orthogonal to the last one up to \(9\cdot 10^{-17}\), and the step \(\Delta t = 10\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\). The first mode is multiplied by \(1 - \lambda_1\Delta t = 0{,}209\) over a step (the grey dashed line). The largest temperature is \(4{,}0\cdot 10^{-4}\) after five steps, which is the correct value \(0{,}209^5\), so the computation still works then: the orange line lies on the grey one. After ten steps it is \(1{,}0\cdot 10^{-4}\) instead of the correct \(1{,}6\cdot 10^{-7}\), after fifteen \(2{,}6\cdot 10^{2}\) and after twenty \(6{,}6\cdot 10^{8}\), because the rounding error contains the fastest mode, and that grows with the factor \(-19\) per step (the blue dashed line, which starts at about \(2\cdot 10^{-17}\) and passes the grey one after nine steps). That is why the definition of stability demands boundedness for every start: a computation that works with a given start is not yet a stable computation.

Theorem 14.65 Stability of the β-scheme for β ≥ 1/2 (stabilnost sheme s parametrom β pri β ≥ 1/2)

Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite and \(\mat C\) a symmetric positive definite matrix, \(\vek f = \vek 0\) and \(\tfrac12 \le \beta \le 1\). For every \(\Delta t > 0\) and every start \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} < \vek T_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}}\) if \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \ne \vek 0\), and \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \to \vek 0\). The scheme is unconditionally stable (brezpogojno stabilna). The factor \(g_\beta(z)\) is positive for every \(z > 0\) exactly at \(\beta = 1\). At \(\tfrac12 \le \beta < 1\), \(g_\beta(z) < 0\) for \(z > 1/(1 - \beta)\), so for the trapezoidal rule at \(z > 2\), as the average shows (\(g_{1/2} = -0{,}2\) at \(z = 3\)), while backward stays positive (\(g_1 = 0{,}25\)).

Proof

We use lemma 14.63 and estimate \(g_\beta(z)\) for \(z > 0\).

  1. The denominator \(1 + \beta z\) is positive, so \(g_\beta(z) < 1\) exactly when \(1 - (1 - \beta)z < 1 + \beta z\), that is \(-z < 0\), which holds. On the graph of the factor the blue and the blackwhite curve lie below the upper dashed bound \(+1\).
  2. Because of the positive denominator, the inequality \(g_\beta(z) > -1\) is equivalent to \(1 - (1 - \beta)z > -1 - \beta z\), that is \(2 + (2\beta - 1)z > 0\). At \(\beta \ge \tfrac12\), \(2\beta - 1 \ge 0\), so the left side is at least \(2 > 0\) and the inequality holds for every \(z > 0\). Both curves stay above the lower bound \(-1\), which the line of the forward scheme crosses.
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(|g_\beta(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t)| < 1\) for every \(r\), and by the lemma \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1}^\T\mat C\vek T_{\mathrm{s}+1} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} g^2\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}^2 < \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}}^2\) if some \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{s}} \ne 0\), that is \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \ne \vek 0\). Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) (theorem 14.60), \(g^s \to 0\) as \(s \to \infty\) (a geometric sequence with \(|g| < 1\)), so \(\vek T_{\mathrm{s}} \to \vek 0\). The sequence is bounded by \(\vek T_0^\T\mat C\vek T_0\) at every \(\Delta t\).
  4. Sign. At \(\beta = 1\), \(g_1 = 1/(1 + z) > 0\). At \(\beta < 1\) the numerator \(1 - (1 - \beta)z\) is negative exactly when \(z > 1/(1 - \beta)\) and the denominator is positive, so \(g_\beta < 0\). At \(\beta = \tfrac12\) this is \(z > 2\): the blackwhite curve crosses the axis at \(z = 2\), the blue one nowhere.

At a large step the backward scheme and the trapezoidal rule have a factor between the bounds, as the blue and blackwhite curve on the graph of the factor show, and the forward scheme only up to the critical point.

A test of theorem 14.65 is the trapezoidal rule at a large step. The theorem says it is stable, and its last point says that this is less than we expect from a computation: the factor is negative at \(z > 2\), and the temperature of a node changes sign, although it should only fall (blackwhite curve below the axis). Let us look at this with numbers at \(z = \lambda_{\max}\Delta t = 20\) (lumped bar with eight elements, \(\Delta t = 0{,}0812\)).

Example 14.66 Crank-Nicolson stable but not sensible (Crank-Nicolson stabilen, a ne smiseln)

A bar with eight elements and lumped capacity (\(L = \kappa = 1\)) has at time \(0\) the temperature \(1\) K at node \(x_4\) and \(0\) elsewhere (start). The step is \(\Delta t = 10\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 0{,}08122\), \(z_{\max} = 20{,}00\). The backward scheme is positive and decreases at \(x_4\), but at the first step it is \(0{,}2014\) instead of \(0{,}1137\), because at such a large step it cannot follow the fast modes, which are in reality already dead within one step, and it smooths them out too slowly. At \(s = 2\) and \(s = 3\) the dip and the hill in the upper row of figure 14.109 swap places, because the sign of these modes turns at every step and their size falls only with factors between \(0{,}52\) and \(0{,}82\), while the exact solution (grey dashed) has almost nothing of them left, because \(\mathrm{e}^{-z} \le \mathrm{e}^{-6{,}4} = 0{,}0016\). The lower row is smooth and positive. The difference lies in the behaviour of the factors at large \(z\) (figure 14.106): \(g_{1/2} \to -1\) and \(g_1 \to 0\). The backward scheme answers a mode that is already dead within the step with zero, and the trapezoidal rule keeps it at almost the same size. COOK (PDF p. 480) therefore suggests an improved scheme for suddenly imposed temperature changes, which we do not treat here.

The theorem therefore does not say that the computation at a large step is accurate or sensible, only that it is bounded. We choose the scheme by what we demand from the computation (figure 14.106). If we are interested in the slow modes and the temperature must remain sensible, we take the backward scheme, even with a large step, but with an error of first order. If we need second order, we take the trapezoidal rule with a step for which \(\lambda_{\max}\Delta t \le 2\) and \(g_{1/2} \ge 0\). For very short phenomena and lumped capacity the forward scheme is the cheapest per step, but tied to \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\).

What we showed. The transient response of a bar with finite elements is the system \(\mat C\dot{\vek T} + \mat K\vek T = \vek 0\) (lemma 14.59), whose solution is a sum of decaying modes (theorem 14.60) with eigenvalues that we know for a bar with prescribed temperatures (lemma 14.61). Every scheme with parameter \(\beta\) multiplies a mode over a step by \(g_\beta(\lambda\Delta t)\) (lemma 14.63), and stability, sign and accuracy depend on this factor. The forward scheme is stable only at \(\Delta t \le 2/\lambda_{\max}\) (theorem 14.64), the backward scheme and the trapezoidal rule at every step, and the trapezoidal rule changes the sign of modes with \(\lambda\Delta t > 2\) (theorem 14.65). For two- and three-dimensional bodies the matrices are larger, but all three properties remain, because the proofs use only the symmetry of the matrices \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) and their positive definiteness, not the shape of the bar (figure 14.106 holds for every mode of every body). The elements for the plane and for space are in chapter 15.
