Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.10 Geometric stiffness and buckling

Chapter 10 found for bracket B (section 10.1.4) that a bar with axial force \(N\) resists a transverse displacement like a taut string with stiffness \(N/L\), and promised that chapter 12 would treat this term. Chapter 8 computed the critical loads of columns in section 8.14. For pinned ends, for clamped ends and for the cantilever it did so with the sine, which it knew in advance, for the clamp-and-pin column only by computing a determinant. How do the elements we know take in the axial force, and what does the same matrix give for the critical load of a column?

We look for the answer along this path. First we look at the bar, which as a string is the simplest case, and compute the matrix that the axial force adds to the bar. We do the same computation for the beam element with Hermite functions. We test both matrices on bracket B of chapter 10, where the answer is known. Then we pose the buckling question as an eigenproblem, and since we want to know how close the finite element computation comes to the critical load of chapter 8 and on which side, we prove estimates for the derivatives of fields that have kinks. At the end we compare numbers.

The model is the same as in section 8.14. The column is the steel flat bar \(20 \times 40\) mm of chapter 5, perfectly straight, loaded by a force that acts exactly along the axis, and the displacements are small. Bending is about the weak axis, so \(EI = E I_{\mathrm{y}} = 5226{,}67\) N m². The supports are those of definition 5.5. The two idealizations, a perfectly straight column and a perfectly axial force, are ours as in chapter 8. A real column does not satisfy them, which section 8.14.4 treats with another model.

12.10.1 The bar as a string

Take a bar of length \(L\) between the pins \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{C}\) with axial force \(N\), tension positive (definition 2.5), and move the node \(\mathrm{C}\) across the bar by \(\Delta\) (figure 12.31). The bar stays straight and tilts by an angle with slope \(\Delta/L\). The axial force acts along the tilted bar, so it has a transverse component \(N\,\Delta/L\). In tension this component pulls the node back towards the original axis, in compression it pushes the node further. The bar therefore resists a transverse displacement with stiffness \(N/L\) like a taut string, and in compression this stiffness is negative.

For the matrix we need the energy, because the matrix, as for every element, is the matrix of a quadratic form in the displacements. Chapter 8 computed the energy of the axial force for the column: a constant axial force \(N\) adds the term \(\tfrac N2\int v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) to the energy (lemma 8.133). That lemma, however, assumes that the deflection of the axis is zero at both ends, since it describes a column between supports. The ends of an element also move across the axis, so we first state the result without conditions at the ends. We describe the axis by the displacements of its points: the point with coordinate \(x\) along the original axis moves by \(u(x)\) along the axis and by \(v(x)\) across it. As in section 8.14 we take the axis as neither lengthening nor shortening. A transverse displacement therefore pulls the ends towards each other, and the axial forces do work on this approach.

Lemma 12.48 Shortening of an element with an inextensible axis (skrček elementa z neraztegljivo osjo)

Let the element be straight before the displacement and of length \(h > 0\), with its axis along the unit vector \(\vek n\) and with the coordinate \(x \in [0, h]\) along the axis. Let the point of the axis with coordinate \(x\) move by \(u(x)\) in the direction \(\vek n\) and by \(v(x)\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek m\) perpendicular to \(\vek n\), where \(u\) and \(v\) are continuously differentiable on \([0, h]\) and not prescribed at the ends. Let the axis be inextensible: every piece \([0, x]\) of the axis has length \(x\) after the displacement (definition 8.4). Let \(1 + u' > 0\) on \([0, h]\) (the axis does not turn back) and \(q = \max|v'|\) with \(q^2 \le 10^{-2}\). Then:

  1. The ends approach each other in the direction \(\vek n\) by
    \[\begin{aligned} \Delta &= u(0) - u(h) = \int_0^h \big(1 - \sqrt{1 - v'^2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ &\qquad \frac12\int_0^h v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ \le\ \Delta\ \le\ \frac{2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - q^2}}\cdot\frac12\int_0^h v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x . \end{aligned} \tag{12.53}\]
    The factor on the right is at most \(1{,}0026\), so the relative error of the approximation \(\Delta \approx \tfrac12\int_0^h v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is at most \(0{,}26\) %.
  2. Let the surroundings act on the element with the force \(-N\vek n\) at the end \(x = 0\) and with the force \(N\vek n\) at the end \(x = h\), where \(N\) is a constant axial force (tension positive, definition 2.5), and let both forces keep their direction during the displacement. The potential energy of these two forces is \(N\,\Delta\), approximately \(\tfrac N2\int_0^h v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) %.
Proof

We have to show the relation between \(u'\) and \(v'\), the shortening that follows from it, the bound on the integrand and finally the energy of the forces.

  1. In axes along \(\vek n\) and \(\vek m\) the displaced axis is the curve \(\vek r(x) = \big(x + u(x),\ v(x)\big)\), and \(|\vek r'| = \sqrt{(1 + u')^2 + v'^2}\) is continuous. By definition 8.4 the piece \([0, x]\) has length \(\int_0^x |\vek r'|\,\mathrm{d}t\), by assumption it has length \(x = \int_0^x 1\,\mathrm{d}t\). For the continuous function \(g = |\vek r'| - 1\) we therefore have \(\int_0^x g\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\) for every \(x \in [0, h]\) (lemma 3.39, point 1).
  2. By theorem 3.40, point 2, the derivative of the function \(x \mapsto \int_0^x g\,\mathrm{d}t\) is \(g\). By step 1 this function is identically zero, so \(g = 0\), that is \((1 + u')^2 + v'^2 = 1\) on \([0, h]\).
  3. Since \(1 + u' > 0\), we have \(1 + u' = \sqrt{1 - v'^2}\). By theorem 3.40, point 1, \(u(h) - u(0) = \int_0^h u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^h \big(\sqrt{1 - v'^2} - 1\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), which is the equation for \(\Delta\). The ends were at \(0\) and \(h\) in the direction \(\vek n\), after the displacement they are at \(u(0)\) and \(h + u(h)\), so the distance between them in this direction is \(h - \Delta\).
  4. For \(0 \le t \le q^2\) we have \(\big(1 - \sqrt{1 - t}\big)\big(1 + \sqrt{1 - t}\big) = t\), hence \(1 - \sqrt{1 - t} = t/\big(1 + \sqrt{1 - t}\big)\). The denominator is at most \(2\), so \(1 - \sqrt{1 - t} \ge t/2\). The square root is increasing, so the denominator is at least \(1 + \sqrt{1 - q^2}\) and \(1 - \sqrt{1 - t} \le \dfrac{2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - q^2}}\cdot\dfrac t2\).
  5. Step 4 with \(t = v'(x)^2\) and the monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.39, point 4) give the bound (12.53). The factor \(2/(1 + \sqrt{1 - q^2})\) grows with \(q\), at \(q^2 = 10^{-2}\) it is \(2/(1 + \sqrt{0{,}99}) = 1{,}0025\) (NumPy).
  6. The force \(N\vek n\) acts at the end \(x = h\), which moves by \(u(h)\) in the direction \(\vek n\), while the transverse part of its displacement \(v(h)\,\vek m\) is perpendicular to the force. A force that keeps its direction has potential energy equal to minus the dot product of the force and the displacement of its point of application, as the compressive force in lemma 8.133, that is \(-N\,u(h)\). The force \(-N\vek n\) at the end \(x = 0\) has energy \(N\,u(0)\). The sum is \(N\,\big(u(0) - u(h)\big) = N\,\Delta\), and by point 1 this is \(\tfrac N2\int_0^h v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) %.

Source: The proof is not from a source. Lemma 8.133 is a similar statement for a column with zero deflection at both ends, described by a graph over the straight line between the supports. COOK §18.1 (PDF p. 654) obtains the same term from the work of the axial force on the lengthening \(\tfrac12 v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) of the element \(\mathrm{d}x\).

The lemma showed that the shortening of an element, and with it the energy of the axial forces, does not depend on the transverse displacements of the ends themselves but only on the slope \(v'\) along the element. A common transverse translation of the element therefore gives nothing, a tilt does.

In a bar between two pins without transverse load the axis stays straight, just as a string between two points is straight. The transverse displacement is therefore linear and the slope \(v'\) constant.

Lemma 12.49 Geometric stiffness matrix of a bar (geometrijska togostna matrika palice)

Let a straight bar with ends \(i\) and \(j\) have length \(L > 0\) and let \(\vek n\) be the unit vector from \(i\) towards \(j\) in global axes (with two components in the plane, with three in space). Let the axial force \(N\) be constant along the bar (tension positive) and known from a computation that ignores geometric stiffness. Let the displacements of the ends \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) be small, their column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}, \vek u_{\mathrm{j}})\), and let the bar stay straight between the pins and free of transverse load. Let \(\mat P = \mat I - \vek n\,\vek n^\T\) and \(|\mat P\,(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})| \le 0{,}1\,L\). For this term the axis is inextensible as in lemma 12.48, while the elongation of the bar \(\vek n\cdot(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\) is carried by the matrix of lemma 7.28. Then \(\mat P\) is symmetric, \(\vek n^\T\mat P = \vek 0\) and \(\mat P^2 = \mat P\), the vector \(\mat P\,\vek u\) is the part of the displacement \(\vek u\) across the bar, and the potential energy of the axial forces of the surroundings at the ends (lemma 12.48, point 2) is \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) %, with the matrix

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g = \frac{N}{L}\begin{pmatrix} \mat P & -\mat P \\ -\mat P & \mat P \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{N}{2L}\,\big|\mat P\,(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}})\big|^2 . \tag{12.54}\]

The forces of the surroundings on the ends of the bar (in the convention of chapter 7) that this term adds are \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), the gradient of the energy.

Proof

We have to show the properties of the matrix \(\mat P\), then the transverse displacement of the bar and its contribution to the energy, and finally the form with the column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).

  1. The matrix \(\mat P\) is symmetric because \(\mat I\) and \(\vek n\,\vek n^\T\) are symmetric (lemma 1.101). From \(\vek n^\T\vek n = 1\) we get \(\vek n^\T\mat P = \vek n^\T - (\vek n^\T\vek n)\,\vek n^\T = \vek 0\) and \(\mat P^2 = \mat I - 2\,\vek n\,\vek n^\T + \vek n\,(\vek n^\T\vek n)\,\vek n^\T = \mat P\). The vector \((\vek n\cdot\vek u)\,\vek n\) is the projection of the displacement onto the direction of the bar (definition 2.13), so \(\mat P\,\vek u = \vek u - (\vek n\cdot\vek u)\,\vek n\) is the remainder, perpendicular to the bar by \(\vek n^\T\mat P = \vek 0\).
  2. Let \(\vek a = \vek u_{\mathrm{j}} - \vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\). A common translation of both ends by \(\vek u_{\mathrm{i}}\) does not tilt the bar, and the potential energy of the forces \(-N\vek n\) and \(N\vek n\) changes in it by \(N\,\vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} - N\,\vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). We may therefore take the end \(i\) at rest and the end \(j\) displaced by \(\vek a\).
  3. If \(\mat P\,\vek a = \vek 0\), the bar does not tilt, there is no transverse displacement and both sides of equation (12.54) are zero. Otherwise let \(\vek m = \mat P\,\vek a/|\mat P\,\vek a|\), a unit vector perpendicular to \(\vek n\) by step 1. The transverse part of the displacement of the end \(j\) is \(|\mat P\,\vek a|\,\vek m\). The bar stays straight, so the transverse displacement of its point with coordinate \(x\) is \(v(x)\,\vek m\) with the linear function \(v(x) = |\mat P\,\vek a|\,x/L\), which is zero at the end \(i\) at rest. The slope \(v' = |\mat P\,\vek a|/L\) is constant and \(v'^2 \le 10^{-2}\) by assumption.
  4. By lemma 12.48 with \(h = L\) the potential energy of the forces of the surroundings is \(N\,\Delta\), with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) % equal to \(\tfrac N2\int_0^L v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{N}{2L}\,|\mat P\,\vek a|^2\).
  5. We have \(\vek a = (-\mat I,\ \mat I)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). By step 1, \(|\mat P\,\vek a|^2 = \vek a^\T\mat P^\T\mat P\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\mat P\,\vek a\), and by lemma 1.101 \(\vek a^\T\mat P\,\vek a = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\begin{pmatrix} -\mat I \\ \mat I \end{pmatrix}\mat P\,(-\mat I,\ \mat I)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\begin{pmatrix} \mat P & -\mat P \\ -\mat P & \mat P \end{pmatrix}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), which is equation (12.54). The matrix is symmetric by step 1, so the gradient of the quadratic form \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: COOK (18.2-4) (PDF p. 657): a matrix with entries \(\pm P/L\) for the transverse displacements, there with axial force \(P\), tension positive, in the plane. The proof is not from a source.

The lemma showed that the geometric stiffness matrix of a bar is the stiffness \(N/L\) times the matrix \(\mat P\), which keeps only the transverse part of the displacement. The matrix has the same form with the same signs as the bar matrix of lemma 7.28, \(\tfrac{EA}{L}\,\vek n\,\vek n^\T\) in the same four places. There the matrix \(\vek n\,\vek n^\T\) kept only the part of the displacement along the bar, here \(\mat P\) keeps only the part across it.

Let us try it on bracket B (chapter 10, section 10.1.4) under the weight of the mass \(1000\) kg at the node \(\mathrm{C}\). By chapter 10 the bar \(BC\) (\(L = 5\) m, \(\vek n = (0{,}8;\ -0{,}6)\)) has the axial force \(N = 16\,350\) N, tension, so the string stiffness is \(N/L = 3270\) N/m. Let \(\mathrm{C}\) move down by \(1\) mm, \(\vek u = (0;\ -1)\) mm. The part of the displacement along the bar is \(\vek n\cdot\vek u = 0{,}6\) mm, the transverse part \(\mat P\,\vek u = \vek u - 0{,}6\,\vek n = (-0{,}48;\ -0{,}64)\) mm, of length \(0{,}8\) mm, since \(\sqrt{1 - 0{,}6^2} = 0{,}8\). The force of the surroundings on the end \(\mathrm{C}\) of the bar that holds the bar in this state is \(3270\cdot(-0{,}48;\ -0{,}64)\cdot10^{-3} = (-1{,}570;\ -2{,}093)\) N. Its length is \(2{,}616\) N and it is perpendicular to the bar (\(0{,}8\cdot(-1{,}570) - 0{,}6\cdot(-2{,}093) = 0\)), in the direction of the transverse displacement. The bar itself acts on the node with the opposite force, which pulls \(\mathrm{C}\) back, as in tension (figure 12.31, left). By lemma 7.28 the elongation \(0{,}6\) mm gives the force \(3{,}92\cdot10^6\ \text{N/m}\cdot 0{,}6\cdot10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 2352\) N along the bar, so the string force is \(0{,}11\) % of this force. The ratio is \(N/EA = 16\,350/(19{,}6\cdot10^6) = 8{,}3\cdot10^{-4}\) (the axial strain of the bar), because the stiffnesses \(N/L\) and \(EA/L\) differ only in \(N\) and \(EA\). That is why the computation of chapter 10 without this term is so good.

Let us also check whether the matrices give the change of frequencies that chapter 10 estimated. By the same chapter the bar \(AC\) (\(L = 4\) m, \(\vek n = (1;\ 0)\)) has the axial force \(N = -13\,080\) N, compression, and \(\mat P = \mathrm{diag}(0,\ 1)\), so its block at the node \(\mathrm{C}\) is \(\tfrac{N}{L}\mat P = \mathrm{diag}(0,\ -3270)\) N/m. The bar \(BC\) has \(\vek n\,\vek n^\T = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}64 & -0{,}48 \\ -0{,}48 & 0{,}36 \end{pmatrix}\), hence \(\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}36 & 0{,}48 \\ 0{,}48 & 0{,}64 \end{pmatrix}\) and the block \(3270\,\mat P\). The pins \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\) are at rest, so the geometric matrix of the structure in the coordinates \((u, v)\) of the node \(\mathrm{C}\) is the sum of both blocks

\[\mat G = \begin{pmatrix} 1177{,}2 & 1569{,}6 \\ 1569{,}6 & -1177{,}2 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{N/m} .\]

With the mass \(m = 1000\) kg and the matrix \(\mat K\) of chapter 10 the natural frequencies of the pair \((\mat K + \mat G,\ m\,\mat I)\) (theorem 10.13) change from \(29{,}4919\) and \(89{,}1641\) 1/s to \(29{,}4891\) and \(89{,}1650\) 1/s, that is by \(-0{,}0094\) % and \(+0{,}00103\) % (NumPy), as chapter 10 estimated. We can estimate the size of the change. The Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.25) of the pair \((\mat K + \mat G,\ m\,\mat I)\) for the column \(\vek v\) of the first mode (of unit length) is \(\lambda_1\,(1 + \vek v^\T\mat G\,\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v)\) with \(\vek v^\T\mat G\,\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = -162{,}9/869\,771 = -1{,}87\cdot10^{-4}\). By theorem 10.26 the first eigenvalue is at most this much, so the frequency drops by at least half of this, \(0{,}0094\) %, and the computation gives \(-0{,}0094\) %. The change is this small because the largest entry of the matrix \(\mat G\), \(1569{,}6\) N/m, is \(899\) times smaller than the smallest entry of the matrix \(\mat K\), \(1{,}4112\cdot10^6\) N/m.

12.10.2 The beam element with axial force

In a beam the transverse displacement is not a straight line but a cubic polynomial of Hermite functions (definition 12.1). Otherwise the computation is the same as for the bar.

Lemma 12.50 Geometric stiffness matrix of a beam element (geometrijska togostna matrika elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a beam with length \(h\) (definition 12.3) with constant axial force \(N\) along the element (tension positive, definition 2.5), field \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \le 10^{-2}\) and an inextensible axis as in lemma 12.48. Then:

  1. The potential energy of the axial forces of the surroundings at the ends of the element is, with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) %, equal to \(\tfrac N2\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the geometric stiffness matrix (geometrijska togostna matrika) \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g = N\,\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\), where
    \[\mat s_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} (\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}')^\T\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{30\,h}\begin{pmatrix} 36 & 3h & -36 & 3h \\ 3h & 4h^2 & -3h & -h^2 \\ -36 & -3h & 36 & -3h \\ 3h & -h^2 & -3h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{12.55}\]
  2. The matrix \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), with equality only for \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (a,\ 0,\ a,\ 0)^\T\), a translation without rotation.
  3. For a rigid rotation by \(b\) about node 1, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (0,\ b,\ b\,h,\ b)^\T\), we have \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = N\,b\,(-1,\ 0,\ 1,\ 0)^\T\): the transverse forces of the surroundings \(\mp N b\) at the nodes are the transverse components of the axial force, which has tilted with the element by \(b\).
Proof

We have to show the energy of the axial force, its form with a matrix, ten integrals and the properties.

  1. Lemma 12.48 with \(v = v_{\mathrm{h}}\) and the coordinate \(x - x_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives the potential energy of the axial forces of the surroundings \(N\,\Delta_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\tfrac12\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \le \Delta_{\mathrm{e}} \le 1{,}0026\cdot\tfrac12\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\), hence \(\tfrac N2\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) with a relative error of at most \(0{,}26\) %. In tension (\(N > 0\)) the term is nonnegative and increases the stiffness, in compression it decreases it.
  2. The derivative \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a number, so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.101), and with \(N\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) constant, \(\tfrac N2\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 = \tfrac12\,N\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. With \(\mathrm{d}\xi/\mathrm{d}x = 1/h\) the derivatives of the functions of equation (12.1) are \(H_1' = 6\,(\xi^2 - \xi)/h\), \(H_2' = 1 - 4\xi + 3\xi^2\), \(H_3' = 6\,(\xi - \xi^2)/h\), \(H_4' = 3\xi^2 - 2\xi\). The entry \((i, j)\) is \(h\int_0^1 H_{\mathrm{i}}'H_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\mathrm{d}\xi\). For example \(s_{11} = \dfrac{36}{h}\int_0^1(\xi - \xi^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \dfrac{36}{h}\Big(\dfrac13 - \dfrac12 + \dfrac15\Big) = \dfrac{36}{30\,h}\), \(s_{12} = 6\int_0^1(\xi^2 - \xi)(1 - 4\xi + 3\xi^2)\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 6\Big(-\dfrac12 + \dfrac53 - \dfrac74 + \dfrac35\Big) = \dfrac{6}{60} = \dfrac{3h}{30\,h}\) and \(s_{22} = h\int_0^1(1 - 4\xi + 3\xi^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi = h\Big(1 - 4 + \dfrac{22}{3} - 6 + \dfrac95\Big) = \dfrac{2h}{15} = \dfrac{4h^2}{30\,h}\). All ten distinct entries (SymPy) give the matrix of equation (12.55), the others are \(s_{13} = -s_{11}\), \(s_{33} = s_{11}\) and so on by the relation \(H_3' = -H_1'\).
  4. The matrix is symmetric because \((\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\) is symmetric. By step 2, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \ge 0\). If this is zero, then \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = 0\) on the whole element (a continuous nonnegative function with zero integral, lemma 6.10, point 3, with a single piece), so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a constant, \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = a\), and by lemma 12.2, point 1, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (a, 0, a, 0)\). Conversely, for this column \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = 0\).
  5. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (0, b, bh, b)\) we have \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = b\,(x - x_{\mathrm{a}})\) by lemma 12.2, point 2, and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = b^2h\). Multiplying the matrix by \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) gives the first row \(\dfrac{3h\,b - 36\,b\,h + 3h\,b}{30\,h} = -b\), the second \(\dfrac{4h^2 b - 3h\cdot bh - h^2 b}{30\,h} = 0\), the third \(+b\) and the fourth \(\dfrac{-h^2 b - 3h\cdot bh + 4h^2 b}{30\,h} = 0\). Hence \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = b\,(-1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\). The axial force \(N\) along the axis tilted by \(b\) has the transverse component \(N\sin b \approx N b\) (lemma 8.19). In the convention of chapter 7 the force of the surroundings on the element at node 2 points along the axis, at node 1 the opposite way, which gives the transverse forces \(N b\,(-1, 0, 1, 0)\).

Source: COOK (18.2-6) (PDF p. 657, there axial force \(P\), tension positive), GR (5.141) and (5.142) (PDF p. 402, there \(N_0\)). The sign and points 3 and 4 are derived here.

Point 3 is the same statement as for strings. An element with tension \(N\), rigidly rotated by \(b\), is in equilibrium only if a pair of transverse forces \(N b\) acts at its ends, and the matrix \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) contains it. The bar matrix of lemma 12.49 gives the same forces for the same transverse difference of displacements \(b\,h\), \((N/L)\cdot b\,h = N b\). By point 2, \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) is, like \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 12.5), semidefinite and has the translation in its kernel. The difference is the rigid rotation: for \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) it has zero energy, for \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) the energy \(\tfrac12 N b^2 h\) by point 3, so the rigid rotation of an element with axial force is not free of energy.

12.10.3 Buckling as an eigenproblem

Chapter 8 found the critical load of a column as the limit at which the second variation of the energy stops being positive. For finite elements the second variation is a quadratic form in the unknowns. For a column with axial force \(N = -P\) (compression, \(P > 0\)) the energy at a small displacement is, by lemma 12.50, \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d - \tfrac P2\,\vek d^\T\mat S\,\vek d\), where \(\mat K\) and \(\mat S\) are assembled from \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\) as in theorem 12.9. This form is positive for every \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\) exactly when the quotient \(\vek d^\T\mat K\vek d/\vek d^\T\mat S\vek d\) is larger than \(P\) for every \(\vek d\). The smallest value of such a quotient is described by theorem 10.26. The same computation holds for any structure, once the forces in the elements are known.

Definition 12.51 Linear buckling analysis (linearna analiza uklona)

Let a structure of elements be assembled into the matrix \(\mat K\) (theorem 12.9, definition 7.11), and let the supports prescribe zero values of unknowns such that \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite. Let a reference load \(\vek F_{\mathrm{ref}}\) give the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{ref}}\) in the elements, tension positive, computed by a linear analysis (for a truss from the solution of \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{ref}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{ref}}\), for a column from equilibrium). The geometric matrix of the reference state is \(\mat G_{\mathrm{ref}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g(N_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{ref}})\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\) of lemmas 12.49 and 12.50, reduced to the free unknowns, \(\mat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\). Under the load \(\mu\,\vek F_{\mathrm{ref}}\) with a number \(\mu > 0\) the axial forces are \(\mu\,N_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{ref}}\) and the geometric matrix is \(\mu\,\mat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\), because \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^g\) is linear in \(N\). Linear buckling analysis (linearna analiza uklona) finds the numbers \(\mu > 0\) at which \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} + \mu\,\mat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is singular, that is

\[\big(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} + \mu\,\mat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\big)\,\vek d = \vek 0 \quad\text{for some } \vek d \ne \vek 0 . \tag{12.56}\]

If such a \(\mu\) exists, the smallest such \(\mu = \mu_{\mathrm{cr}}\) is the linear buckling factor (uklonski faktor), \(\mu_{\mathrm{cr}}\,\vek F_{\mathrm{ref}}\) the critical load and the corresponding \(\vek d\) the buckling mode (uklonska oblika). The computation assumes that the displacements up to buckling do not change the geometry, so the axial forces grow in proportion to the load. For a column with the reference force \(1\) N along the axis (compression) we have \(N = -1\) N in every element and equation (12.56) is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d = P\,\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d\), where \(\mat S = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: COOK (18.5-1)-(18.5-4) (PDF p. 662), which calls the matrix \(\mat G_{\mathrm{ref}}\) the "stress stiffness matrix" \([K_\sigma]_{\mathrm{ref}}\), and COOK §18.5 (PDF pp. 662-663) for a column with a compressive force. The notation is ours.

Let us try it on the pinned column of length \(L = 2\) m (flat bar, weak axis, \(EI = 5226{,}67\) N m², critical load from chapter 8 \(12\,896\) N) with a single element, \(h = L\). The supports prescribe only \(v_1 = v_2 = 0\), the rotations \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) are free. From equations (12.3) and (12.55)

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{EI}{h}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat S_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{h}{30}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The equation \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d = P\,\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d\) has the determinant \(\big(4 - \tfrac{4p}{30}\big)^2 - \big(2 + \tfrac{p}{30}\big)^2\) with \(p = P h^2/EI\), that is \(\tfrac{p^2}{60} - \tfrac{6p}{5} + 12\) with the zeros \(p = 12\) and \(p = 60\). The smallest is \(P_{\mathrm{h}} = 12\,EI/h^2 = 15\,680\) N, the buckling mode \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1\), the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \varphi_1\,h\,(\xi - \xi^2)\), a parabola (by lemma 12.2). This is the same parabola that in section 8.14.3 gave the Rayleigh quotient \(12/L^2\), \(21{,}6\) % above \(\pi^2/L^2\). Here \(P_{\mathrm{h}}/P_{\mathrm{cr}} = 12/\pi^2 = 1{,}2159\), and that the element cannot give less we prove below. The second zero \(p = 60\), \(78\,400\) N, has the mode \(\varphi_2 = \varphi_1\) and is \(1{,}52\) times the second critical load \(4\pi^2 EI/L^2 = 51\,585\) N.

A number near the smallest eigenvalue is not yet a buckling factor. For a compressive force \(P\) we have \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} + \mu\,\mat G_{\mathrm{ff}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} - P\,\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\), and \(P\) is not a buckling factor if the equation (12.56) has only the solution \(\vek d = \vek 0\), that is if the matrix is invertible. Take \(P = 0{,}9\,P_{\mathrm{h}} = 14\,112\) N, so \(p = 10{,}8\). The determinant from the computation above is then \(p^2/60 - 6p/5 + 12 = 0{,}984 \ne 0\), the matrix is invertible, and since the diagonal entry \(4 - 4p/30 = 2{,}56\) and the determinant are positive, it is even positive definite. At \(P = 0{,}9\,P_{\mathrm{h}}\) no shape is a solution and there is no buckling at this force, although \(P\) is only \(10\) % below \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\). \(P\) differs from \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) in one thing: the determinant is not zero. At \(p = 12\) it is zero, but at \(p = 36\), between the zeros \(12\) and \(60\), it is \(-9{,}6\), the matrix is invertible but not positive definite, and \(p = 36\) is not a buckling factor either. A factor is only a zero of the determinant.

12.10.4 Inequalities between integrals of derivatives of functions with kinks

The smallest quotient \(P_{\mathrm{h}} = \min\vek d^\T\mat K\vek d/\vek d^\T\mat S\vek d\) is the smallest value of the Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.25), which for the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) equals \(EI\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2/\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\). For similar quotients chapter 8 proved lower bounds with the inequalities of section 8.14.1, but for functions with a continuous derivative or a continuous second derivative. The field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is continuously differentiable, but its second derivative jumps at the nodes. For example the function \(H_1^\varphi\) of the middle node of a beam with two elements (figure 12.5) has the second derivative \(4\) 1/m from the left and \(-4\) 1/m from the right. We therefore need the inequalities for fields with kinks of the second kind (lemma 12.10): \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) continuous, \(\eta'\) piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11), so \(\eta''\) is piecewise continuous. We repeat the proofs of chapter 8 for such functions, except that every computation with a derivative is done on a piece.

Lemma 12.52 Inequalities between integrals of derivatives of functions with kinks (neenakosti za integrale odvodov funkcij s pregibi)

All integrals are integrals of piecewise continuous functions (definition 6.9).

  1. Let \(\eta\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([0, \ell]\) (definition 6.11) with \(\eta(0) = 0\). Let one of two cases hold: (a) \(\eta(\ell) = 0\) and \(k = \pi/\ell\), or (b) there is no condition at the end \(\ell\) and \(k = \pi/(2\ell)\). Then \(\int_0^\ell \eta'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge k^2\int_0^\ell \eta^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and equality holds for \(\eta = c\,\sin kx\).
  2. Let \(\eta\) be a field with kinks of the second kind on \([0, L]\) (lemma 12.10: \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) continuous, \(\eta'\) piecewise continuously differentiable) with \(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^L \eta'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \le (L/\pi)^2\int_0^L \eta''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and equality holds for \(\eta = c\,\sin(\pi x/L)\).
  3. Let \(\eta\) be a field with kinks of the second kind on \([0, L]\) with \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = \eta(L) = \eta'(L) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^L \eta'^2\,\mathrm{d}x \le (L/2\pi)^2\int_0^L \eta''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
Proof

We have to show point 1 by dividing by the sine as in lemma 8.127, except that the computation with derivatives is done on pieces, point 2 with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as in corollary 8.128 and point 3 by splitting into an even and an odd part as in theorem 8.135. We write out all steps.

Point 1. Let \(s(x) = \sin kx\). Let the break points of the function \(\eta\) be \(x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}-1}\), on the open piece \((x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}})\) we have \(\eta' = g_{\mathrm{j}}\) with a function \(g_{\mathrm{j}}\) continuous on the closed piece (definition 6.11). Every \(g_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuous on the closed piece, so \(|g_{\mathrm{j}}|\) is bounded there and attains a largest value (section 1.13.1). Let \(M\) be the largest of these values over all pieces.

  1. The function \(s\) is twice differentiable, \(s' = k\cos kx\), \(s'' = -k^2 s\), and \(s(0) = 0\) (theorem 8.12 and the chain rule, lemma 8.29, point 3). By lemma 8.10, points 2 and 3, in case (a) \(s > 0\) on \((0, \ell)\), because \(kx\) lies there between \(0\) and \(\pi\), and \(s(\ell) = \sin\pi = 0\). In case (b) \(kx \in (0, \pi/2]\), so \(s > 0\) on \((0, \ell]\), \(s(\ell) = 1\) and \(s'(\ell) = k\cos(\pi/2) = 0\).
  2. On \((0, \ell)\) let \(w = \eta/s\). Since \(\eta\) and \(s\) are continuous and \(s > 0\), \(w\) is continuous. On every open piece \((x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}})\), \(w\) is differentiable with \(w' = (g_{\mathrm{j}}\,s - \eta\,s')/s^2\) (lemma 4.108, point 3), and the right side is continuous on every closed interval inside \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}] \cap (0, \ell)\), because \(g_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\eta\), \(s\) and \(s'\) are continuous and \(s > 0\). On the open piece \(\eta = w\,s\) and \(\eta' = w' s + w\,s'\).
  3. On the open piece we expand \(\eta'^2 - k^2\eta^2 = w'^2 s^2 + 2\,w\,w'\,s\,s' + w^2 s'^2 - k^2 w^2 s^2\). The function \(B = w^2 s\,s' = \eta^2 s'/s\) has there the derivative \(2\,w\,w'\,s\,s' + w^2 s'^2 + w^2 s\,s'' = 2\,w\,w'\,s\,s' + w^2 s'^2 - k^2 w^2 s^2\) by \(s'' = -k^2 s\). Hence on every open piece \(\eta'^2 - k^2\eta^2 = w'^2 s^2 + B'\). The function \(B\) is continuous on \((0, \ell)\), on the open piece its derivative is \(B' = 2\eta\,g_{\mathrm{j}}\,s'/s + \eta^2 s''/s - \eta^2 s'^2/s^2\), which is continuous on every closed interval inside \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}] \cap (0, \ell)\). For \(0 < \varepsilon < \beta < \ell\), \(B\) is therefore piecewise continuously differentiable on \([\varepsilon, \beta]\) with the partition made of the points \(\varepsilon\), \(\beta\) and the break points \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) between them.
  4. For \(0 < \varepsilon < \beta < \ell\), by lemma 6.12 for \(B\) and by linearity (lemma 6.10, point 2), \(\int_\varepsilon^\beta(\eta'^2 - k^2\eta^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_\varepsilon^\beta w'^2 s^2\,\mathrm{d}x + B(\beta) - B(\varepsilon)\).
  5. As \(\varepsilon \to 0\), \(B(\varepsilon) \to 0\). By lemma 6.12, \(\eta(\varepsilon) = \eta(\varepsilon) - \eta(0) = \int_0^\varepsilon \eta'\,\mathrm{d}x\). The functions \(M - \eta'\) and \(M + \eta'\) are nonnegative on every piece, so their integrals are nonnegative (lemma 6.10, point 3), that is \(-M\varepsilon \le \int_0^\varepsilon\eta' \le M\varepsilon\) and \(|\eta(\varepsilon)| \le M\varepsilon\). By lemma 8.19, \(\sin t \ge t - t^3/6 \ge \tfrac56\,t\) for \(0 \le t \le 1\), hence \(s(\varepsilon) \ge \tfrac56\,k\varepsilon\) for \(k\varepsilon \le 1\). Since \(|s'| = k\,|\cos k\varepsilon| \le k\), we get \(|B(\varepsilon)| \le M^2\varepsilon^2 k/(\tfrac56\,k\varepsilon) = \tfrac65 M^2\varepsilon \to 0\).
  6. As \(\beta \to \ell\), \(B(\beta) \to 0\). In case (a), \(\eta(\ell) = 0\), hence \(\eta(\beta) = -\int_\beta^\ell\eta'\) and \(|\eta(\beta)| \le M(\ell - \beta)\) as in step 5. By the addition theorem (corollary 8.15), \(\sin(\pi - t) = \sin t\), so \(s(\beta) = \sin\big(k(\ell - \beta)\big) \ge \tfrac56\,k(\ell - \beta)\) for \(k(\ell - \beta) \le 1\) and as in step 5 \(|B(\beta)| \le \tfrac65 M^2(\ell - \beta) \to 0\). In case (b), \(\eta\) is bounded (continuous on a closed interval), \(s(\beta) \to 1\) and \(s'(\beta) \to 0\), so \(B(\beta) \to 0\).
  7. The function \(f = \eta'^2 - k^2\eta^2\) is piecewise continuous on \([0, \ell]\), its functions on the closed pieces are continuous and therefore bounded, so \(|f| \le C\) with some number \(C\). The integral does not depend on the partition (lemma 6.10, point 1), so we may add \(\varepsilon\) and \(\beta\) as break points, and the integral over \([0, \ell]\) is the sum of the integrals over \([0, \varepsilon]\), \([\varepsilon, \beta]\) and \([\beta, \ell]\). The functions \(C - f\) and \(C + f\) are nonnegative, so by points 2 and 3 of the same lemma the integrals over \([0, \varepsilon]\) and \([\beta, \ell]\) are in absolute value at most \(C\varepsilon\) and \(C(\ell - \beta)\), which tends to zero. By step 4 the integral \(\int_\varepsilon^\beta w'^2 s^2\) therefore also has a limit, and it equals \(\int_0^\ell(\eta'^2 - k^2\eta^2)\), because \(B(\varepsilon)\) and \(B(\beta)\) tend to zero. The function \(w'^2 s^2\) is nonnegative on every piece, so the integrals are nonnegative (point 3 of lemma 6.10) and so is the limit. This is the inequality of point 1.
  8. The function \(\eta = c\,\sin kx\) is continuously differentiable and satisfies the conditions of case (a) or (b), so by lemma 8.127 equality holds for it.

Point 2. Let \(\|f\|^2 = \int_0^L f^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) for a piecewise continuous \(f\).

  1. The functions \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) are piecewise continuously differentiable on a common partition (\(\eta\) is continuously differentiable, \(\eta'\) is piecewise continuously differentiable). Lemma 6.13 with the functions \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) in the roles of \(N\) and \(w\) of the lemma gives \(\int_0^L\eta\,\eta''\,\mathrm{d}x = [\eta\,\eta']_0^L - \|\eta'\|^2 = -\|\eta'\|^2\), because \(\eta\) is zero at the ends. Hence \(\|\eta'\|^2 = -\int_0^L\eta\,\eta''\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. The functions \(\eta\) and \(\eta''\) are piecewise continuous, so by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (lemma 6.19) \(\|\eta'\|^2 \le \|\eta\|\,\|\eta''\|\).
  3. The function \(\eta\) is continuously differentiable with \(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\), so lemma 8.127, case (a) with \(\ell = L\), gives \(\|\eta\| \le (L/\pi)\,\|\eta'\|\). We insert this into step 10. If \(\|\eta'\| = 0\), the inequality holds, otherwise we divide by \(\|\eta'\|\) and square: \(\|\eta'\|^2 \le (L/\pi)^2\|\eta''\|^2\).
  4. The function \(\eta = c\,\sin(\pi x/L)\) is twice continuously differentiable and zero at \(0\) and \(L\), so by corollary 8.128 equality holds for it.

Point 3. All integrals are over \([0, L]\).

  1. Let \(\tilde\eta(x) = \eta(L - x)\), \(a = (\eta + \tilde\eta)/2\) and \(b = (\eta - \tilde\eta)/2\). By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, point 3), \(\tilde\eta'(x) = -\eta'(L - x)\). The functions \(a\) and \(b\) are continuously differentiable, their derivatives \(a'\) and \(b'\) are piecewise continuously differentiable (break points of \(\eta'\) and their mirror images), \(\eta = a + b\), and \(a\), \(b\) have value zero and derivative zero at \(0\) and \(L\), because \(\eta(0) = \eta(L) = 0\) and \(\eta'(0) = \eta'(L) = 0\), and \(\tilde\eta(0) = \eta(L)\), \(\tilde\eta'(0) = -\eta'(L)\).
  2. We have \(a(L - x) = a(x)\) and \(b(L - x) = -b(x)\). Differentiation gives \(a'(L - x) = -a'(x)\) and \(b'(L - x) = b'(x)\), so at \(x = L/2\), \(a'(L/2) = 0\) and \(b(L/2) = 0\). Differentiating once more on the pieces where the second derivative exists gives \(a''(L - x) = a''(x)\) and \(b''(L - x) = -b''(x)\).
  3. Let \(f\) be piecewise continuous with \(f(L - x) = -f(x)\). On the piece \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\) the function \(F(x) = \int_0^x f\) is the sum of the integrals over the earlier pieces and the integral of the function \(f_{\mathrm{j}}\) from \(x_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) to \(x\), so by theorem 3.40, point 2, it is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable with \(F' = f\) on the open pieces. The function \(G(x) = F(L - x)\) is piecewise continuously differentiable with \(G'(x) = -f(L - x) = f(x)\). Lemma 6.12 gives \(G(L) - G(0) = \int_0^L f = F(L)\), while at the same time \(G(L) - G(0) = F(0) - F(L) = -F(L)\). Hence \(F(L) = -F(L)\), that is \(\int_0^L f = 0\). By step 14, \(a'b'\) and \(a''b''\) have this property, so \(\int\eta'^2 = \int a'^2 + \int b'^2\) and \(\int\eta''^2 = \int a''^2 + \int b''^2\) (the products \(2a'b'\) and \(2a''b''\) have integral zero).
  4. The function \(a'\) is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, and zero at \(0\), \(L/2\) and \(L\) (steps 13 and 14). Point 1 (a) on \([0, L/2]\) and for the function \(t \mapsto a'(L/2 + t)\) on \([0, L/2]\), both times with \(\ell = L/2\) and \(k = 2\pi/L\), gives after adding \(\int a''^2 \ge (2\pi/L)^2\int a'^2\). The shift \(x = L/2 + t\) does not change the integral: for a piecewise continuous \(f\) and \(F(x) = \int_{\mathrm{L}/2}^x f\) the function \(G(t) = F(L/2 + t)\) has the derivative \(f(L/2 + t)\) on the pieces, so by lemma 6.12 \(\int_0^{L/2} f(L/2 + t)\,\mathrm{d}t = G(L/2) - G(0) = \int_{\mathrm{L}/2}^L f\), as in step 15.
  5. The function \(b\) is a field with kinks of the second kind and zero at \(0\), \(L/2\) and \(L\). Point 2 on \([0, L/2]\) and for the function \(t \mapsto b(L/2 + t)\) on \([0, L/2]\), both times with length \(L/2\), and the shift as in step 16 give \(\int b'^2 \le (L/2\pi)^2\int b''^2\).
  6. By steps 15 to 17, \(\int\eta'^2 = \int a'^2 + \int b'^2 \le (L/2\pi)^2\big(\int a''^2 + \int b''^2\big) = (L/2\pi)^2\int\eta''^2\).

The lemma showed that the inequalities of chapter 8 also hold for fields whose second derivative jumps at the nodes. The proofs followed the proofs of chapter 8, except that every computation with a derivative was done on a piece, and the terms at the break points cancel because \(\eta\) or \(\eta'\) is continuous (lemmas 6.12 and 6.13). The continuity of the slope, which the Hermite functions guarantee, is therefore essential, while continuity of the second derivative is not needed.

12.10.5 The critical load of the model is not below the true one

Can the finite element computation predict a smaller critical load than the one chapter 8 computed? We expect not. The model looks for the smallest value of the quotient only among the fields of the Hermite family, and the smallest value on a smaller set is not below the smallest value on a larger one. The fields of the Hermite family have no continuous second derivative, so the theorems of chapter 8 do not cover them. We extended their inequalities to such fields above, so we can prove the theorem.

Theorem 12.53 The finite element buckling load is not below the true one (končni elementi dajo kritično silo, ki ni manjša od prave)

Let a column of length \(L\) with constant bending stiffness \(EI > 0\) be divided into an arbitrary mesh of beam elements (definition 12.3), and let it be supported in one of four ways: (i) a fixed pin support and a roller support, (ii) clamped at both ends, (iii) clamped at \(x = 0\) and free end, (iv) clamped at \(x = 0\) and pin support at \(x = L\). Then:

  1. The matrices \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) and \(\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\) are positive definite, the equation \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d = P\,\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d\) has only positive eigenvalues, and the smallest is \(P_{\mathrm{h}} = \min\,\vek d^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d\,/\,\vek d^\T\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d\) over \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\). This is the linear buckling factor of the column (definition 12.51).
  2. \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge P_{\mathrm{cr}}\) with \(P_{\mathrm{cr}} = \pi^2 EI/L^2\) for (i), \(4\pi^2 EI/L^2\) for (ii) and \(\pi^2 EI/(4L^2)\) for (iii), the critical loads of theorems 8.134, 8.135 and 8.131. For (iv), \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge \pi^2 EI/L^2\).
  3. If every element of the mesh is divided into any number of elements, \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) does not increase.
Proof

We have to show positive definiteness, then the bound on the quotient with lemma 12.52, and finally that the families of fields are nested.

  1. Let \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}} H_{\mathrm{I}}\) be the field with the free unknowns \(\vek d\) and zeros at the supports. By lemma 12.10, point 1, it is a field with kinks of the second kind, \(v_{\mathrm{h}}''\) is a polynomial on every element and therefore piecewise continuous. By theorem 12.9, point 3, \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = EI\int_0^L v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). With \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d\), lemma 1.101 gives \(\vek d^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(\vek d^\T\mat S\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). On the element \(e\), \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 12.8, point 1), so by lemma 12.50, point 2, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat s_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{e}} v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The sum over the elements is \(\int_0^L v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (definition 6.9).
  2. The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite by corollary 12.11: the only straight line \(a + b\,x\) that is zero wherever a support prescribes the displacement and has zero slope where it prescribes the rotation is the zero function in all four ways (two distinct zeros for (i), the clamp for (ii), (iii) and (iv)).
  3. The matrix \(\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is symmetric as a sum of symmetric matrices (lemma 1.101) and \(\vek d^\T\mat S\vek d = \int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \ge 0\). If this is zero, then \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = 0\) on every element (lemma 6.10, point 3, \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is continuous), so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is constant on each piece and, being continuous, constant on \([0, L]\), and since every support prescribes zero displacement, \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\). By lemma 12.8, \(\vek d = \vek 0\). Hence \(\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite.
  4. By theorem 10.13 the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}, \mat S_{\mathrm{ff}})\) has real eigenvalues \(P_1 \le P_2 \le \dots\) with eigenvectors \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\). All of them are positive, because \(P_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite, and by theorem 10.26 \(R(\vek d) = \vek d^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d/\vek d^\T\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d \ge P_1\) for every \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\), with equality at the eigenvector. Hence \(P_1 = \min R = P_{\mathrm{h}}\). The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} - \mu\,\mat S_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is singular exactly at an eigenvalue, so \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the smallest \(\mu > 0\) of definition 12.51.
  5. By step 1, \(R(\vek d) = EI\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\big/\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2\), which depends only on the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\). For (i), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a field with kinks of the second kind with \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}(L) = 0\), and by lemma 12.52, point 2, \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \le (L/\pi)^2\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\). For (ii), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}'(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}(L) = v_{\mathrm{h}}'(L) = 0\) and point 3 gives \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \le (L/2\pi)^2\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\). For (iii), \(\theta = v_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable with \(\theta(0) = 0\), so point 1 (b) with \(\ell = L\) and \(k = \pi/(2L)\) gives \(\int\theta'^2 \ge (\pi/2L)^2\int\theta^2\), that is \(\int v_{\mathrm{h}}'^2 \le (2L/\pi)^2\int v_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\). For (iv), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}(L) = 0\) and point 2 gives the same bound as for (i).
  6. From step 5, \(R(\vek d) \ge EI\,\pi^2/L^2\) for (i) and (iv), \(4\pi^2 EI/L^2\) for (ii) and \(\pi^2 EI/(4L^2)\) for (iii) for every \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\), so by step 4 point 2 holds (equations (8.124), (8.125) and (8.119)).
  7. Let \(V_{\mathrm{H}}\) be the family of fields of the coarse mesh and \(V_{\mathrm{h}}\) that of the fine mesh, both with the same supports. A field of the coarse mesh is continuously differentiable and on every fine element, which lies inside one coarse element, a polynomial of degree at most three, so by lemma 12.8, point 2, applied to the fine mesh, it is a field of the fine mesh with the nodal values and slopes of the field. Hence \(V_{\mathrm{H}} \subset V_{\mathrm{h}}\). By step 1, \(R\) is a function of the field and not of the mesh, so \(P_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{fine}} = \min_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{h}}R \le \min_{\mathrm{V}_\mathrm{H}}R = P_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{coarse}}\).

Source: GR Remark 5.6 (PDF p. 403): "such an analysis results in overestimating the critical load", COOK §18.5 (PDF p. 663) for one element: both results are upper bounds to the correct value, and §18.6 (PDF p. 664): for compatible elements not softened by reduced integration and a consistent \(\mat G\) from the same shape functions, linear buckling analysis gives an upper bound on the critical load of the mathematical model. The proof with the inequality is not from a source.

The theorem showed that the critical loads of the column in the finite element model are, for the first three ways of support, upper bounds of the loads of chapter 8, and that for all four they do not increase when the elements are divided. The proof had three parts: positive definiteness of the matrix \(\mat S\) from the energy, the bound on the Rayleigh quotient with the inequalities for fields with kinks, and nested families of fields.

For the clamp-and-pin column the theorem gives only the weaker bound \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge \pi^2 EI/L^2 = 12{,}896\) kN. Chapter 8 computed the threshold \(kL = 4{,}4934\) for this column with a determinant and did not prove that the second variation stays positive below the threshold (section 8.14.3). We therefore do not prove the bound \(P_{\mathrm{h}} \ge 26{,}383\) kN for this column, and the numbers below lie above it only by computation. Linear buckling analysis moreover takes a perfectly straight column and small displacements. Imperfections are treated in section 8.14.4 with another model, nonlinearity and buckling beyond the linear analysis by COOK in chapters 17 and 18.

The reason we expect is this: the error of the field moment on an element is of order \(h^2\) (corollary 12.16, for a uniform load, halving reduces it \(4\) times), and the error of a Ritz eigenvalue should be proportional to the square of the error of the field in the energy norm, which contains the moment. The error of the eigenvalue would then be of order \(h^4\). We have not proved this relation with the square in the book, so the \(h^4\) law remains an observation.

For the cantilever the agreement with the pinned column follows from the mirroring of chapter 8. Let a column of length \(2L\) with \(2n\) elements be pinned at both ends, and let \(v\) be a field of its family that is even about the middle, \(v(2L - x) = v(x)\). Then \(v'(L) = 0\). With \(w(s) = v(L) - v(L + s)\) for \(0 \le s \le L\) we have \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\) and \(w(L) = v(L)\), because \(v(2L) = 0\). The field \(w\) is a field of the cantilever of length \(L\) with \(n\) elements, and conversely every such field gives an even field of the column. By evenness \(\int_0^{2L} v''^2 = 2\int_0^L w''^2\) and \(\int_0^{2L} v'^2 = 2\int_0^L w'^2\) (\(w' = -v'(L + s)\), \(w'' = -v''(L + s)\)), so the Rayleigh quotients of both fields are equal. Hence \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the cantilever with \(n\) elements is at least \(P_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the column with \(2n\) elements, which has odd fields besides the even ones. The numbers (\(12{,}9933\), \(12{,}9029\), \(12{,}8967\) kN in both cases) show that they are equal, so the first mode is an even field. This is observed, not proved. The agreement of the clamped column with this curve is not explained here: for \(n = 4\), \(P_{\mathrm{h}} = 51{,}973\) kN is exactly \(4\cdot 12{,}9933\) kN.

The geometric stiffness matrix is thus, next to the stiffness matrix, all the column needs for its critical load. With it the finite element model gives, for pinned ends, clamped ends and the cantilever, a critical load that is not below the true one and that with eight elements of the pinned column is \(3{,}3\cdot10^{-5}\) of its value away from it, while the same matrix gives for the bars of bracket B the change of frequencies that chapter 10 estimated.
