Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.16 Random excitation and signal processing

A road does not shake a car with a sine. The shaking that a product meets during a drive has no formula: if we repeat the measurement on the same road, we get a different record. Such a record is called random (naključen). The shaker of section 9.15 can shake like this too, and test standards prescribe random shaking by a curve in the unusual units \((\text{m/s}^2)^2/\text{Hz}\). In this section we say what this curve means, how we compute the response of a product from it and how we get a frequency response from two noisy records. Along the way we meet three limitations of every digital measurement: a record of finite length, which causes leakage, the need for filters, and the finite number of levels into which a converter divides the signal.

The book has no probability theory. So we do not describe a random record by probabilities but by averages that we can compute from the samples, and we check the claims about averages over many records by computer experiments and say so each time.

9.16.1 Mean square and power spectral density

On the top part of figure 9.183 is a random record of acceleration in time. We made it in the computer: each sample is an independent random number with the normal distribution and a typical size of \(3\ \text{m/s}^2\) (NumPy). What can we say about such a record at all? A single value says nothing, because next time it is different. The mean is close to zero, because positive and negative values are equally frequent. The size of the shaking is told only by the mean of the squares, in which the signs do not cancel.

Definition 9.155 Mean, mean square and RMS value of a record (srednja vrednost, srednji kvadrat in efektivna vrednost zapisa)

Let \(N \ge 1\) and let \(x_0, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) be real numbers (the samples of a record). The mean (srednja vrednost), the mean square (srednji kvadrat) and the root mean square or RMS value (efektivna vrednost) of the record are the numbers

\[\bar x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1} x_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \overline{x^2} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1} x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 , \qquad x_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{x^2}} . \tag{9.153}\]

The RMS value has the unit of the record, the mean square its square.

Source: The definition is the usual one and is not from the books of the project. CK has the mean square only in its frequency form as the power spectrum (equation (18.41), PDF p. 619).

For the record in the figure \(x_{\mathrm{rms}} = 3\ \text{m/s}^2\), and the grey band between \(\pm x_{\mathrm{rms}}\) covers most of the values, not all. For a pure cosine \(x = A\cos\Omega t\), sampled over a whole number of periods with more than two samples per period, the mean square is \(A^2/2\) and the RMS value \(A/\sqrt2 = 0{,}707\,A\) (lemma 9.72 for the integral, the theorem below for the samples). The mean square is called power because the power that a damper turns into heat, \(c\dot q^2\), is proportional to the square of the velocity (lemma 9.71), and similarly for an electric current.

How is the mean square divided among the frequencies? The record has a discrete Fourier transform, the numbers \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) of definition 9.135 (there the samples were called \(f_{\mathrm{j}}\), here they are \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), because the letter \(f\) now means frequency). Let the record last \(T\) seconds. The number \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) belongs to the harmonic with \(k\) oscillations in the record, that is, to the frequency

\[f_{\mathrm{k}} = k\,\Delta f , \qquad \Delta f = \frac{1}{T} . \tag{9.154}\]

The spacing of the lines \(\Delta f\) is therefore the reciprocal of the length of the record (CK (18.39), PDF p. 618): a record of length \(1\) s gives lines every \(1\) Hz, a record of length \(4\) s every \(0{,}25\) Hz.

Theorem 9.156 The mean square is the sum of the squared lines (srednji kvadrat je vsota kvadratov črt)

Let \(N \ge 1\), let \(x_0, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) be complex numbers and \(C_0, \dots, C_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) their discrete Fourier transform (definition 9.135). Then the mean square equals the sum of the squared lines:

\[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1} |x_{\mathrm{j}}|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{N-1} |C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 . \tag{9.155}\]

If the samples are real, \(|C_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}}| = |C_{\mathrm{k}}|\) for \(k = 1, \dots, N - 1\).

Proof

We must show that two sums are equal. We write out the right side with the definition and exchange the order of the finite sums, then we use discrete orthogonality.

  1. By definition 9.135, \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N}\), and the conjugate is \(\overline{C_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \overline{x_{\mathrm{l}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}lk/N}\) (definition 9.26). Since \(|C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = C_{\mathrm{k}}\overline{C_{\mathrm{k}}}\), we have \(|C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\overline{x_{\mathrm{l}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(l - j)k/N}\).
  2. We sum over \(k\) and put the sum over \(k\) innermost, which is allowed for finite sums: \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} |C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\overline{x_{\mathrm{l}}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k(l - j)/N}\).
  3. By lemma 9.136 the inner sum is \(N\) if \(N\) divides \(l - j\), and \(0\) otherwise. Since \(j\) and \(l\) are between \(0\) and \(N - 1\), \(|l - j| < N\), so \(N\) divides \(l - j\) only for \(l = j\).
  4. The terms with \(l = j\) remain: \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} |C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\overline{x_{\mathrm{j}}}\,N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} |x_{\mathrm{j}}|^2\).
  5. For real samples \(\overline{C_{\mathrm{k}}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} = C_{-\mathrm{k}} = C_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}}\) (the period \(N\) of definition 9.135), and a conjugate has the same modulus.

Source: The equality is the discrete form of Parseval's equality, which we only named for series in section 9.11.9 (LEBL2, theorem 11.8.13, PDF p. 204). The proof from lemma 9.136 is ours.

The proof showed that when squares are summed, the mixed terms of different harmonics cancel, so each line contributes its own part to the mean square and nothing else. For a real record the lines \(k\) and \(N - k\) are equally high and belong to the same frequency \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) (one is the \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\cdot}\), the other the \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\cdot}\) of the same cosine), so we count them together: the line with frequency \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(0 < k < N/2\), has the power \(P_{\mathrm{k}} = 2|C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\). A cosine of amplitude \(A\) with a whole number of oscillations in the record has \(|C_{\mathrm{k}}| = A/2\) and \(P_{\mathrm{k}} = A^2/2\), as it must.

Now the experiment on the bottom part of figure 9.183. We made \(400\) records of length \(1\) s and computed the mean of \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) over all records for each line. We get lines at every hertz, from \(1\) to \(31\) Hz on the frequency axis, all about equally high, \(0{,}281\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\). Then the same with records of length \(4\) s: the lines are four times denser on the same axis and four times lower, \(0{,}070\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\). Why? The mean square is the same both times, \(3^2 = 9\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\), and by theorem 9.156 it is divided among the lines. At \(64\) samples per second the lines reach up to \(32\) Hz. At a spacing of \(1\) Hz there are \(32\) of them and each gets \(9/32 = 0{,}281\), at a spacing of \(0{,}25\) Hz there are \(128\) and each gets \(9/128 = 0{,}070\). So the height of a line is not a property of the shaking alone, but also of the length of the record. The property of the shaking is the height divided by the spacing: \(0{,}281/1 = 0{,}070/0{,}25 = 0{,}281\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2/\text{Hz}\).

Definition 9.157 Power spectral density (spektralna gostota moči)

Let there be \(n_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 1\) records of the same quantity, each with \(N\) real samples in the time \(T\), and let \(C_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\) be the discrete Fourier transform of the \(i\)-th record. Let \(\Delta f = 1/T\) and \(0 < k < N/2\). The power spectral density (spektralna gostota moči, PSD, also močnostni spekter) at the frequency \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = k\,\Delta f\) is the real number

\[G_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{\Delta f}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{d}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n_{\mathrm{d}}} 2\,\bigl|C_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\bigr|^2 . \tag{9.156}\]

Its unit is the square of the unit of the record per hertz, for an acceleration \((\text{m/s}^2)^2/\text{Hz}\). A record for which \(G_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the same at all lines is called whiteblack noise (beličrni šum).

Source: CK (18.41) and (18.44), PDF p. 619 to 620: the power spectrum \(G_{\mathrm{AA}} = A A^*\) and its average over segments. The division by \(\Delta f\) and the factor \(2\) for the one-sided spectrum are not from the source. The name whiteblack noise follows whiteblack light, in which all colours, that is all frequencies, are present equally.

From the definition and theorem 9.156 it follows how we get the RMS value from the density. A record with mean zero has \(C_0 = 0\). If \(N\) is even and we neglect the last line \(k = N/2\), the average of the mean squares over the records is

\[\overline{x^2} = \sum_{0 < \mathrm{k} < \mathrm{N}/2} G_{\mathrm{k}}\,\Delta f , \tag{9.157}\]

which is the area under the stepped curve of the density. The RMS value is the square root of the area. On the left graph these are \(31\) columns of height \(0{,}281\) and width \(1\) Hz, together \(8{,}7\) of the \(9\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\), the rest are the lines at \(0\) and \(32\) Hz.

9.16.2 The response of a product to random base motion

A standard prescribes the density of the acceleration of the table as a curve that we call the profile. What is the response of product P with \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 80\) Hz and \(\zeta = 0{,}03\) of section 9.15.1?

Theorem 9.158 Response density under base motion (gostota odziva pri gibanju podlage)

Let \(m, c, k > 0\) and let the mass \(m\) be on the spring \(k\) and the damper \(c\) on a base as in theorem 9.80. Let the acceleration of the base be a trigonometric polynomial of period \(T\) without a constant term, \(a_0(t) = \sum_{0 < |\mathrm{n}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}nt/T}\) with \(c_{-\mathrm{n}} = \overline{c_{\mathrm{n}}}\) (the acceleration is real), sampled with \(N > 2M\) samples per period. Let \(a(t)\) be the acceleration of the mass in the periodic response (theorem 9.126), and \(P_{\mathrm{k}}^{(0)}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) the powers of the lines \(2|C_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\) of the samples of \(a_0\) and \(a\). Then for \(0 < k \le M\)

\[P_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{TR}(f_{\mathrm{k}})^2\,P_{\mathrm{k}}^{(0)} , \qquad \mathrm{TR}(f)^2 = \frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2} , \quad r = \frac{2\pi f}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}} , \tag{9.158}\]

where \(N > 2M\) is enough for the response too, which has the same harmonics. The same holds for the densities: \(G_{\mathrm{a}}(f_{\mathrm{k}}) = \mathrm{TR}(f_{\mathrm{k}})^2\,G_{\mathrm{a}_0}(f_{\mathrm{k}})\).

Proof

We must show that each harmonic of the acceleration is passed on with its own factor of transmissibility. We go from the acceleration of the base to its displacement, from there by the equation of motion to the displacement of the mass and back to the acceleration.

  1. Let \(\Omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi n/T\). The displacement of the base \(y\) with \(\ddot y = a_0\) has the coefficients \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = -c_{\mathrm{n}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) for \(n \ne 0\), because the second derivative of the function \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is \(-\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) (theorem 9.34). The acceleration has no constant term, otherwise the base would run away.
  2. By step 4 of the proof of theorem 9.80 the equation of the absolute motion of the mass is \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = k\,y + c\,\dot y\). The right side is a periodic force with the coefficients \((k + \mathrm{i}c\Omega_{\mathrm{n}})\,y_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. By claim 3 of theorem 9.126 the periodic response has the coefficients \(q_{\mathrm{n}} = H(\Omega_{\mathrm{n}})(k + \mathrm{i}c\Omega_{\mathrm{n}})\,y_{\mathrm{n}}\). By step 5 of the proof of theorem 9.80, \(|H(\Omega)(k + \mathrm{i}c\Omega)| = \mathrm{TR}\) at \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  4. The acceleration of the mass has the coefficients \(-\Omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 q_{\mathrm{n}} = H(\Omega_{\mathrm{n}})(k + \mathrm{i}c\Omega_{\mathrm{n}})\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is, \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) multiplied by a number of modulus \(\mathrm{TR}(|n|/T)\). This is the multiplication of the top curve by the middle one in the figure.
  5. Since \(M < N/2\), by corollary 9.137 the lines of the samples equal the coefficients, \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(0 < k \le M\), for \(a_0\) and for \(a\). The square of the modulus and the multiplication by \(2\) give equation (9.158). Averaging over records and dividing by \(\Delta f\) do not change the factor, and we get the density of the response.

Source: The theorem and the proof are ours, from theorems 9.80 and 9.126. A source for the general form (for a random process the density of the response is \(|H|^2\) times the density of the excitation) is missing in the books of the project.

The theorem has an assumption that a random record meets only approximately: the record is one period of a periodic motion, and the response is steady. In true random shaking the record does not repeat. The difference lies in what happens at the start of the record, when the response still carries the trace of the earlier shaking, and this trace dies out in the time of the memory \(1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). So the assumption is good when the record is much longer than the memory. For product P the memory is \(0{,}066\) s and a record of \(2\) s is \(30\) times longer. For cantilever A with a memory of \(2{,}8\) s a record of \(2\) s would give a wrong density of the response.

Example 9.159 Product P on a random profile (izdelek P na naključnem profilu)

Let the profile on the top graph of figure 9.184 be \(G_{\mathrm{a}_0}\): from \(10\) to \(50\) Hz it grows linearly, \(0{,}05 f\), from \(50\) to \(200\) Hz it is flat, \(2{,}5\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2/\text{Hz}\), from \(200\) to \(500\) Hz it falls like \(2{,}5\,(200/f)^2\).

  1. The RMS value of the excitation is the square root of the area under the profile (equation (9.157)). The trapezoid has the area \(\tfrac12(0{,}5 + 2{,}5)\cdot 40 = 60\), the rectangle \(2{,}5\cdot 150 = 375\), the falling part \(2{,}5\cdot 200^2\,(1/200 - 1/500) = 300\). Together \(735\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\), so \(a_{0,\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{735} = 27{,}1\ \text{m/s}^2\), which is \(2{,}76\) times the acceleration of gravity.
  2. The square of the transmissibility on the middle graph has at \(80\) Hz the peak \(16{,}70^2 = 279\), elsewhere it is small: \(1{,}78\) at \(40\) Hz and \(0{,}11\) at \(160\) Hz.
  3. By theorem 9.158 the density of the response on the bottom graph is the product of the two, with the peak \(279\cdot 2{,}5 = 697\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2/\text{Hz}\).
  4. The area under it is \(5177\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\) (the trapezoidal rule in NumPy), so \(a_{\mathrm{rms}} = 71{,}9\ \text{m/s}^2\), which is \(7{,}33\) times the acceleration of gravity and \(2{,}65\) times the excitation.
  5. Of this area \(51\) % is in the half-power band between \(77{,}6\) and \(82{,}4\) Hz and \(94\) % between \(60\) and \(100\) Hz. The product picks its own frequency out of the wide shaking, as the narrow peak shows: the response is almost a sine at \(80\) Hz whose amplitude changes slowly and at random.

So the response is set almost only by the height of the profile at the natural frequency. That gives a simple estimate. If the profile were flat everywhere, \(G_{\mathrm{a}_0} = G_0\) (whiteblack noise), the area under the density of the response would be

\[\overline{a^2} = G_0\int_0^\infty \mathrm{TR}(f)^2\,\mathrm{d}f = G_0\,\frac{\pi f_{\mathrm{n}}\,(1 + 4\zeta^2)}{4\zeta} . \tag{9.159}\]

We do not derive the integral here. We checked it numerically: for \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 80\) Hz and \(\zeta = 0{,}03\) the trapezoidal rule up to \(20\) kHz with an estimated tail and the formula give the same value \(2101{,}93\) Hz to nine digits. The density of the response has at \(r = 1\) the value \(G_0\,(1 + 4\zeta^2)/(4\zeta^2)\), which is up to \(0{,}05\) % below the true peak, so the area equals the peak multiplied by the width \(\pi\zeta f_{\mathrm{n}}\): in the figure this is the orange rectangle of width \(7{,}5\) Hz. For product P equation (9.159) with \(G_0 = 2{,}5\) gives \(5255\ (\text{m/s}^2)^2\), \(1{,}5\) % more than \(5177\), because the true profile falls below \(50\) Hz and above \(200\) Hz. The formula also says what damping does: the RMS response grows like \(1/\sqrt{\zeta}\), not like \(1/\zeta\) as the peak does for a sine, because with smaller damping the peak is higher but also narrower.

9.16.3 Averaging, the estimators \(H_1\) and \(H_2\), coherence

At the end of section 9.12 we got the frequency response as the quotient of a line of the response and a line of the force. That works for a clean periodic record. But a real measurement of force and response has noise mixed in that has nothing to do with the force. What does the quotient of such a record do, and how do we remove the noise?

Definition 9.160 The estimators \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) and the coherence (cenilki \(H_1\) in \(H_2\) ter koherenca)

Let there be \(n_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 1\) pairs of records of force and response of the same length, and let \(F_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\) and \(X_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\) be the lines \(k\) of the discrete Fourier transform of the \(i\)-th pair. The averaged spectra at the line \(k\) are

\[\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{d}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \bigl|F_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\bigr|^2 , \qquad \hat G_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{d}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \bigl|X_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\bigr|^2 , \qquad \hat G_{\mathrm{fx}} = \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{d}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \overline{F_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}}\,X_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)} . \tag{9.160}\]

The first two are real numbers, the third, the cross spectrum, is a complex number. If the denominators are not zero, the estimators (cenilki) of the frequency response and the coherence (koherenca) are the numbers

\[H_1 = \frac{\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}}}{\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}} , \qquad H_2 = \frac{\hat G_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\overline{\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}}}} , \qquad \gamma^2 = \frac{|\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}}|^2}{\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\hat G_{\mathrm{xx}}} = \frac{H_1}{H_2} . \tag{9.161}\]

Source: CK (18.42) to (18.50), PDF p. 619 to 622, where \(G_{\mathrm{UP}} = U P^*\) is the cross spectrum of the response \(U\) and the force \(P\). Our \(\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}}\) is its \(\tilde G_{\hat \mathrm{U}\hat \mathrm{P}}\).

In figure 9.185 each pair of records is one row: the force record and the response record in time. For each pair we take the same line \(k\), one for the force and one for the response. Over all rows the squares and products of the lines are averaged, and the two estimators are made from these averages.

Why two estimators and why an average? Let us first see what they give without noise and what a single record gives.

  • Without noise \(X_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)} = H\,F_{\mathrm{k}}^{(i)}\) in every record (claim 3 of theorem 9.126). Then \(\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}} = H\,\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\) and \(\hat G_{\mathrm{xx}} = |H|^2\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\), so \(H_1 = H\), \(H_2 = |H|^2\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}/(\overline{H}\,\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}) = H\) and \(\gamma^2 = 1\). In figure 9.186 this is the blackwhite curve.
  • For a single record, \(n_{\mathrm{d}} = 1\), \(\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}} = \overline{F_{\mathrm{k}}}X_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(H_1 = \overline{F_{\mathrm{k}}}X_{\mathrm{k}}/|F_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = X_{\mathrm{k}}/F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(H_2 = |X_{\mathrm{k}}|^2/(F_{\mathrm{k}}\overline{X_{\mathrm{k}}}) = X_{\mathrm{k}}/F_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the coherence is \(|F_{\mathrm{k}}|^2|X_{\mathrm{k}}|^2/(|F_{\mathrm{k}}|^2|X_{\mathrm{k}}|^2) = 1\), whatever the noise. One record cannot tell how much of the response is from the force and how much from the noise. Only several records tell, which is why \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) in the figure are drawn from sixteen: the part of the response caused by the force is in the same ratio to the force every time, the noise is different every time.

Two things happen in averaging. In the cross spectrum \(\hat G_{\mathrm{fx}}\) the contribution of the noise has a different direction in the complex plane in each record, so it cancels in the mean. In the spectra \(\hat G_{\mathrm{ff}}\) and \(\hat G_{\mathrm{xx}}\) the noise enters with the square of its modulus, which is always positive and does not cancel. Denote by \(G_{\mathrm{mm}}\) the mean power of the noise on the force and by \(G_{\mathrm{nn}}\) on the response. If the two noises are not related to each other nor to the true signals, the estimators tend, for a large number of averages, to

\[H_1 \to \frac{H}{1 + G_{\mathrm{mm}}/G_{\mathrm{ff}}} , \qquad H_2 \to H\Bigl(1 + \frac{G_{\mathrm{nn}}}{G_{\mathrm{xx}}}\Bigr) \tag{9.162}\]

(CK (18.46) to (18.48), PDF p. 620 to 622). The claim needs probability theory and we do not prove it, we check it by an experiment. In the limit of many averages \(H_1\) is therefore too small and is spoiled only by the noise on the force, \(H_2\) is too large and is spoiled only by the noise on the response (CK, PDF p. 622). The coherence \(\gamma^2 = H_1/H_2\) says what part of the response is from the force: according to CK it is always at most \(1\) and equals \(1\) when there is no noise (PDF p. 622).

The experiment (NumPy, seed 7): we excite product P with a force that has all lines between \(20\) and \(200\) Hz equally high with random phases, \(26{,}9\) N RMS. The records are \(2\) s long with \(512\) samples per second. To the measured force we add noise with an RMS value of \(0{,}3\) of the force, to the measured displacement noise with an RMS value of \(0{,}02\) mm. On the top graph of figure 9.186, \(|H_1|\) lies mostly a little below the true curve, and \(|H_2|\) mostly above it, most of all far from the resonance, where the response is small and the noise on it relatively large. With \(16\) records the scatter is still larger than the constant error, so single lines fall on the wrong side (table). The ratios to the true \(|H|\) at three frequencies:

\(\lvert H_1\rvert/\lvert H\rvert\), \(n_{\mathrm{d}} = 16\) \(0{,}891\) \(0{,}903\) \(1{,}063\)

The limit for \(H_1\) is \(1/(1 + 0{,}0635) = 0{,}940\) at all frequencies, because the noise on the force is in the same ratio to it everywhere. Averaging does not remove this constant error, it only reduces the scatter about it: the root mean square deviation of the ratio \(|H_1|/|H|\) from the limit (a number without a unit) is \(0{,}82\) for one record, \(0{,}34\) for \(4\), \(0{,}18\) for \(16\), \(0{,}085\) for \(64\), \(0{,}044\) for \(256\) and \(0{,}022\) for \(1024\). When the number of records is quadrupled, the scatter is halved, so it falls like \(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{d}}}\). The coherence on the bottom graph wanders about its limit and falls to a half at \(160\) Hz (limit \(0{,}495\)) and to a quarter at \(200\) Hz: at \(160\) Hz half of the power of the measured response is noise. So the coherence is the first thing to look at in every measurement: where it is close to \(1\), we may trust the estimators, where it falls, the datum is not worth much. Which estimator do we take? Near resonance the response is large and the noise on it small, while the force is small there, because at resonance the structure needs little force for a large motion, so \(H_2\) is better there. Far from resonance it is the other way round and \(H_1\) is better (CK, PDF p. 622).

9.16.4 Leakage and windows

The discrete Fourier transform has lines only at the frequencies \(k/T\), that is, at the harmonics that have a whole number of oscillations in the record. What happens to a cosine that has four and a half?

On the top part of figure 9.187 is a cosine of amplitude \(1\) and its \(32\) samples on the axis of samples. If the cosine has exactly \(4\) oscillations in the record, then by corollary 9.137 \(C_4 = C_{28} = 1/2\) and all other lines are zero: on the bottom axis there is a single line of height \(2|C_4| = 1\). At \(4{,}5\) oscillations the true frequency is not among the lines. The amplitude spreads over all of them: \(0{,}611\) and \(0{,}662\) at the neighbouring lines \(4\) and \(5\), \(0{,}187\) and \(0{,}238\) at \(3\) and \(6\), and still \(0{,}097\) at line \(9\). The phenomenon is called leakage (odtekanje), because the amplitude leaks from the true frequency into the neighbouring lines (CK, PDF p. 618, with the same example of \(4\) and \(4{,}5\) Hz). No line shows the true amplitude \(1\), and the distant lines hold something that is not in the signal.

The cause can be seen at the top. The numbers \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the coefficients of a periodic function that has the same samples in every period, so the transform understands the record as a repetition of itself. A record with \(4{,}5\) oscillations ends at \(-1\) and the repetition starts at \(+1\): at the seam there is a jump by \(2\). And a jump needs many harmonics whose coefficients fall only like \(1/n\), as for the square wave in example 9.111. The lemma says this exactly.

Lemma 9.161 The lines of a harmonic that is not on a line (črte harmonika, ki ni na črti)

Let \(N \ge 2\), let \(\nu\) be a real number and \(x_{\mathrm{j}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\nu j/N}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, N - 1\), that is, a harmonic with \(\nu\) oscillations in the record. Let \(k\) be an integer. If \(\nu - k\) is an integer multiple of \(N\), then \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\). Otherwise

\[|C_{\mathrm{k}}| = \frac{|\sin\pi(\nu - k)|}{N\,|\sin(\pi(\nu - k)/N)|} . \tag{9.163}\]

For an integer \(\nu\) therefore \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\) for \(k = \nu\) (up to a multiple of \(N\)) and \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for the other \(k\).

Proof

We must show the value of a geometric sum and its modulus.

  1. By definition 9.135, \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1} w^j\) with \(w = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\nu - k)/N}\) (theorem 9.34, item 1).
  2. If \(\nu - k\) is a multiple of \(N\), then \(w = 1\) as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 9.136, each term is \(1\) and \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\). This is the single line in the figure.
  3. Otherwise \(w \ne 1\) (step 2 of the same proof, which uses only that \((\nu - k)/N\) is not an integer) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1} w^j = (w^N - 1)/(w - 1)\), so \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = \dfrac{1}{N}\cdot\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\nu - k)} - 1}{\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\nu - k)/N} - 1}\).
  4. For real \(\theta\), \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} - 1 = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta/2}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta/2} - \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta/2}\bigr) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta/2}\cdot 2\mathrm{i}\sin(\theta/2)\), so \(|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} - 1| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\). With \(\theta = 2\pi(\nu - k)\) in the numerator and \(\theta = 2\pi(\nu - k)/N\) in the denominator we get equation (9.163), these are the spread lines.
  5. For an integer \(\nu\) that does not differ from \(k\) by a multiple of \(N\), \(\sin\pi(\nu - k) = 0\) and the denominator is not zero, so \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\).

Source: The lemma and the proof are ours. CK shows the phenomenon on an example (figure 18.14, PDF p. 619), without a formula.

The lemma says why the distant lines fall slowly. When the harmonic has half an oscillation too many, the numerator is \(|\sin\pi(\nu - k)| = 1\) at all \(k\), and the denominator is about \(\pi|\nu - k|\) for \(|\nu - k|\) small against \(N\). So the lines fall like \(1/(\pi d)\) with the distance \(d = |\nu - k|\) from the true frequency: \(2/\pi = 0{,}637\) at the nearest ones (\(d = \tfrac12\)), \(0{,}212\) at \(d = \tfrac32\), \(0{,}127\) at \(d = \tfrac52\). At \(N = 32\) equation (9.163) gives \(0{,}6369\), \(0{,}2130\) and \(0{,}1286\). The numbers in the figure are a little different (\(0{,}611\) and \(0{,}662\)), because a cosine is the sum of two such harmonics, \(\nu\) and \(-\nu\), and their lines add.

The lemma also gives the rule of resolution. Two harmonics that are closer than one spacing of the lines \(\Delta f = 1/T\) fall into the same lines and we cannot tell them apart. Whoever wants to separate peaks that are \(0{,}1\) Hz apart needs a record longer than \(10\) s, however densely it is sampled.

What can we do? The best is to have no jump: we choose the excitation so that it is periodic in the record or that it starts and dies out within it (CK, PDF p. 618). When that is not possible, we multiply the record before the transform by a window (okno), a function that lowers it smoothly towards zero at both ends, so that there is no jump at the seam.

On the top part of figure 9.188 are the windows on the time axis from \(0\) to \(T\). Without a window we in fact multiply the record by \(1\), which is the rectangular window (pravokotno okno). The Hann window (Hannovo okno, in the lectures Hanning) is one oscillation of a raised cosine,

\[w_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12\Bigl(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi j}{N}\Bigr) , \qquad j = 0, \dots, N - 1 , \tag{9.164}\]

which is zero at both ends and \(1\) in the middle.

Theorem 9.162 The Hann window in frequency (Hannovo okno v frekvenci)

Let \(N \ge 2\), let \(x_0, \dots, x_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) be complex numbers with the lines \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{j}}\) the Hann window (9.164). The lines \(C_{\mathrm{k}}^w\) of the record \(w_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\) are, for every integer \(k\),

\[C_{\mathrm{k}}^w = \tfrac12 C_{\mathrm{k}} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}-1} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}+1} . \tag{9.165}\]
Proof

We must show that multiplication by a cosine in time shifts the lines by one place in both directions.

  1. By theorem 9.34, \(\cos\theta = \tfrac12(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} + \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta})\), so \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac14\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/N} - \tfrac14\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/N}\). This is the raised cosine in the figure.
  2. The line of the record \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/N}\) at \(k\) is \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/N}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j(k - 1)/N} = C_{\mathrm{k}-1}\). In the same way the line of the record \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j/N}\) equals \(C_{\mathrm{k}+1}\).
  3. The transform is a sum, so the line of a sum of three records is the sum of the lines: \(C_{\mathrm{k}}^w = \tfrac12 C_{\mathrm{k}} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}-1} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}+1}\).

Source: The theorem and the proof are ours. A source for the Hann and Hamming windows is missing in the books of the project, CK only names windows (PDF p. 618).

The proof showed that the Hann window in frequency is an averaging of each line with its two neighbours with the weights \(-\tfrac14, \tfrac12, -\tfrac14\). What does that do to leakage? By lemma 9.161 the distant lines are \(C_{\mathrm{k}} \approx \text{const}/(\nu - k)\), neighbouring ones are almost equal. The expression \(\tfrac12 C_{\mathrm{k}} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}-1} - \tfrac14 C_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is the difference between a line and the mean of its neighbours, and for slowly changing lines it is small: instead of like \(1/d\) they fall like \(1/d^3\). In figure 9.187 the blue lines at \(4{,}5\) oscillations are \(0{,}849\) and \(0{,}849\) at lines \(4\) and \(5\), \(0{,}169\) and \(0{,}170\) at \(3\) and \(6\), and then \(0{,}024\), \(0{,}008\) and \(0{,}004\) at lines \(7\), \(8\) and \(9\), where the rectangular window gave \(0{,}153\), \(0{,}117\) and \(0{,}097\). The factor \(2\) in the figure corrects for the fact that a window with the mean value \(\tfrac12\) halves the amplitude.

The price is twofold. A cosine that is exactly on a line had one line before, now it has three, \(\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14\) of the amplitude: the peak is wider and the resolution worse. And the amplitude still depends on where between two lines the frequency falls: at \(4{,}5\) oscillations the highest lines show \(0{,}849\) instead of \(1\) (the rectangular window \(0{,}637\)). So a window does not repair the measurement, it exchanges one error for a smaller one.

The Hamming window (Hammingovo okno) has the same cosine with two other numbers, \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 0{,}54 - 0{,}46\cos(2\pi j/N)\), and at the ends it stays at \(0{,}08\). The numbers are chosen so that the largest side peak is the lowest: at \(N = 64\) the largest side line is \(0{,}0075\) of the main one, for the Hann window \(0{,}027\), for the rectangular one \(0{,}217\) (a computation in NumPy with a step of \(0{,}01\) in the first number: the minimum is at \(0{,}54\)). But because a small jump stays at the ends, the distant lines again fall only like \(1/d\). A comparison at \(N = 1024\) and a harmonic half a line away from a line, the height of a line relative to the highest one:

distance \(d\) rectangular Hann Hamming
\(1\) \(0{,}33\) \(0{,}20\) \(0{,}14\)
\(2\) \(0{,}20\) \(0{,}029\) \(0{,}0022\)
\(10\) \(0{,}048\) \(3{,}3\cdot 10^{-4}\) \(5{,}2\cdot 10^{-3}\)
\(100\) \(5{,}1\cdot 10^{-3}\) \(3{,}7\cdot 10^{-7}\) \(5{,}8\cdot 10^{-4}\)

So the Hamming window is better right next to the peak, the Hann window far from it.

For a response after a blow, which decays, the exponential window (eksponentno okno) \(w = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{w}}}\) is suitable (CK names it for the response in a hammer test, PDF p. 618). On the bottom part of figure 9.188 is the decay record of cantilever A on the time axis up to \(4\) s. Because of the small damping the amplitude at the end of the record is still \(0{,}49\) mm, \(24\) % of the initial one, and there are \(22{,}5\) oscillations in the record. The jump at the seam is large. If we read the width of the peak at the height \(1/\sqrt2\) from the spectrum of this record (we compute the sum (9.130) also at frequencies between the lines), we get \(\zeta = 0{,}0208\), twice too much. With a window of \(\tau_{\mathrm{w}} = 1\) s the record falls to \(0{,}009\) mm and there is no jump. But the window adds damping that is not there, and by an exactly known amount: the product \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{w}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = \mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta + 1/(\tau_{\mathrm{w}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}))\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) decays like a system with a damping ratio larger by \(1/(\tau_{\mathrm{w}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}02823\). From the spectrum we get \(0{,}03832\), subtract \(0{,}02823\) and are left with \(\zeta = 0{,}01009\), \(0{,}9\) % too much. With \(\tau_{\mathrm{w}} = 0{,}5\) s we get \(0{,}06635 - 0{,}05647 = 0{,}00988\). The correction is a difference of large numbers, so we do not choose the window shorter than necessary. The assumption of the correction is that \(\zeta\) is small, so that \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} \approx \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). For cantilever A the difference is \(0{,}005\) %.

9.16.5 Filters

A window multiplies the record in time, sample by sample. A filter (filter) is a system that multiplies each harmonic by a number that depends on its frequency: it passes some and suppresses others. We already know such systems, since every frequency response does this.

Definition 9.163 Filter, pass band and cut-off frequency (filter, prepustni pas in mejna frekvenca)

A filter is a linear system that in the steady state turns a harmonic input of amplitude \(1\) and frequency \(f\) into a harmonic output of the same frequency with the amplitude \(g(f) \ge 0\). Let \(g_{\max}\) be the least upper bound of the values of \(g\) (the largest value when \(g\) attains it), and let it be finite. The pass band (prepustni pas) is the set of frequencies with \(g(f) \ge g_{\max}/\sqrt2\), a cut-off frequency (mejna frekvenca) is a frequency at the edge of the pass band. A filter is low-pass (nizkoprepusten) if the pass band is \(0 \le f \le f_{\mathrm{c}}\), high-pass (visokoprepusten) if it is \(f \ge f_{\mathrm{c}}\), band-pass (pasoven) if it is \(f_1 \le f \le f_2\) with \(f_1 > 0\), and band-stop (zaporen) if everything passes except a band \(f_1 < f < f_2\).

Source: The definition is the usual one and is not from the books of the project. CK names the low-pass filter before sampling (PDF p. 617). In the lectures the filters are called nizkopasovni, visokopasovni, pasovni and zaporni.

The level \(1/\sqrt2\) is the same as at the half-power points (theorem 9.74): power is proportional to the square of the amplitude, and \((1/\sqrt2)^2 = \tfrac12\). All four kinds come from one system with one degree of freedom. The equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\) has four terms, and the amplitude of each, divided by \(Q_0\), can be seen as the output of a filter whose input is the force. With \(X = Q_0 D/k\) (definition 9.64) and \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the amplitudes of the terms are

\[\frac{kX}{Q_0} = D , \qquad \frac{m\Omega^2 X}{Q_0} = r^2 D , \qquad \frac{c\,\Omega X}{Q_0} = 2\zeta r D , \qquad \frac{|k - m\Omega^2|\,X}{Q_0} = |1 - r^2|\,D . \tag{9.166}\]

In figure 9.189 each term is on its own axes, top left the force of the spring. At \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt2\), \(D = 1/\sqrt{1 + r^4}\) (section 9.8.5): the curve is flat at \(1\) for small \(r\), at \(r = 1\) it falls exactly to \(1/\sqrt2\), which is the cut-off frequency, and above it it falls like \(1/r^2\). The spring passes slow forces and suppresses fast ones: a low-pass filter. This is the isolation of section 9.8, read as a filter. Top right is the inertia term \(r^2 D = r^2/\sqrt{1 + r^4}\), which is the mirror image: zero for slow forces, \(1\) for fast ones, the cut-off again at \(r = 1\). This is a high-pass filter, and this is how the vibrometer of section 9.8.5 works. Bottom left is the force of the damper at \(\zeta = 0{,}1\): \(2\zeta r D\) is \(1\) exactly at \(r = 1\) and falls to \(1/\sqrt2\) at \(r = 0{,}905\) and \(r = 1{,}105\). These are the half-power points \(\Omega_1\) and \(\Omega_2\) of theorem 9.74, since the power of the damper is proportional to the square of its force. The damper feels only forces near the natural frequency: a band-pass filter of width \(2\zeta\). Bottom right are the spring and the inertia together, \(|1 - r^2|D\): at \(r = 1\) they cancel and nothing is left, far from there it is \(1\). This is a band-stop filter with the same cut-offs, because

\[(2\zeta r D)^2 + \bigl((1 - r^2)D\bigr)^2 = \frac{(2\zeta r)^2 + (1 - r^2)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2} = 1 .\]

What the band-pass filter passes, the band-stop filter holds back. The equality says that the force \(Q_0\) is divided between the damper and the pair of spring and inertia like the two legs of a right triangle, which we saw at \(r = 1\) in figure 9.172.

One filter is compulsory in a measurement. By corollary 9.137 the harmonics above half the sampling frequency show as lower frequencies, and after sampling they can no longer be told from the true ones. So before sampling the signal must go through a low-pass filter that removes them (CK, PDF p. 617). Because no filter cuts sharply, as the slow fall above the cut-off shows, analysers use only the lower half to \(0{,}625\) of the band up to half the sampling frequency (CK, PDF p. 617).

So the difference between a window and a filter is this. A window is a multiplication in time: with it we choose which part of the record counts, and in frequency it mixes neighbouring lines (theorem 9.162). A filter is a multiplication in frequency: it does not mix lines, it only raises or lowers each, and in time it has a memory, because its output is the Duhamel integral of the input (theorem 9.96).

9.16.6 Dynamic range

The last limitation is in the converter that turns the voltage of a sensor into a number. A number has finitely many digits, so the converter knows only finitely many levels.

A converter with \(b\) bits divides its range from \(-U\) to \(U\) into \(2^b\) equal steps of size \(\Delta = 2U/2^b\) and rounds every value to the nearest level. In figure 9.190 \(b = 3\), so \(8\) steps, and on the time axis there are two signals. The large sine of amplitude \(0{,}9\,U\) is stored as a staircase, the error is at most half a step. The small sine of amplitude \(0{,}1\,U\) never exceeds half a step, \(0{,}125\,U\), and is rounded to zero everywhere. The converter does not know about it.

The ratio of the whole range to the step, \(2U/\Delta = 2^b\), is the dynamic range (dinamično območje, in the lectures dinamična globina) of the converter. Ratios of amplitudes that run over several orders of magnitude are written in decibels: the ratio \(R\) corresponds to \(20\log_{10} R\) decibels (dB), where \(\log_{10} R = \ln R/\ln 10\). A factor of \(10\) is \(20\) dB, a factor of \(2\) is \(6{,}02\) dB, so each bit adds \(6{,}02\) dB.

bits \(b\) number of steps \(2^b\) dynamic range step for a range of \(\pm 10\) mm
\(8\) \(256\) \(48{,}2\) dB \(0{,}078\) mm
\(12\) \(4096\) \(72{,}2\) dB \(0{,}0049\) mm
\(16\) \(65\,536\) \(96{,}3\) dB \(0{,}00031\) mm
\(24\) \(16\,777\,216\) \(144{,}5\) dB \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-6}\) mm

Why does this matter in vibration? Because the response itself runs over a large span. Cantilever A has a response \(50\) times larger at resonance than under a slow force (\(D = 1/(2\zeta) = 50\), which is \(34\) dB), and a much smaller one far above resonance. We spoiled the decay record of figure 9.170 with a noise of \(0{,}01\) mm. A converter with \(8\) bits and a range of \(\pm 10\) mm would have a step of \(0{,}078\) mm, eight times larger than this noise, and would itself be the main source of error. With \(16\) bits the step is \(0{,}0003\) mm and need not be considered. For the same reason we set the range of the converter to the signal before the measurement: too large a range wastes levels, because the signal occupies only a few middle ones, and too small a range cuts off the peaks.

So the answer to the questions from the start of the section is this. We describe random shaking by the power spectral density, the height of the lines divided by their spacing, and the area under it is the mean square. The density of the response is the density of the excitation multiplied by the square of the transmissibility, so a product picks the neighbourhood of its natural frequency out of a wide shaking. We get a frequency response from noisy records by averaging, with enough averages \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) bracket it from below and from above, and the coherence says where we may trust them. The finite length of the record gives the line spacing \(1/T\) and leakage, which a window reduces but does not remove. A low-pass filter before sampling prevents aliasing, and the number of bits sets the smallest signal that the measurement still sees.
