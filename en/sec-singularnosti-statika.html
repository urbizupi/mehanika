Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.7 Singular configurations and joint forces

The Jacobian matrix \(\mat J(\vek q)\) of section 27.6 connects the velocity of the arm's tip (figure 27.64) with the velocities of the joints, \(\vek\xi = \mat J\dot{\vek q}\) (definition 27.41). Now we use it for two questions that everyone who steers an arm asks. First: the tip is to move with a given velocity, how fast must the joints turn? Second: a load acts on the tip, what torques must the motors supply so that the arm stays at rest? Both questions end at the same matrix. In the first we must invert it, in the second transpose it (swap its rows and columns). We shall see that both have trouble in the same configurations of the arm.

Anyone who has carried a heavy bucket knows the answer to the second question from their muscles. With the arm stretched downward the bucket hardly tires us, with the arm stretched out horizontally it does quickly. The weight is the same, what changes is the distance between the line of the weight and the shoulder (figure 27.66). In the subsection 27.7.3 we shall write this simple fact down for every joint of the arm.

The model of the arm of figure 27.64 is the same as in the previous sections (section 27.1): rigid links (definition 1.11), ideal joints with one degree of freedom, an open chain, motors that put torques and forces \(\boldsymbol\tau\) into the joints, no friction and no inertia of motors and gears. A real joint has friction, which helps at rest, and a real motor has a gearbox with its own inertia. We model neither here. For the velocities and forces of the tip it is enough that the joints are ideal.

27.7.1 When the tip cannot be moved in every direction

Take arm A of section 27.1, the planar arm with lengths \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, and put it in the configuration \(\vek q = (30°, 60°)\). Figure 27.64 (left) draws it step by step. First the axis \(x_0\), from which we measure the angle \(q_1\). Then the two links: the first goes from the base \(O\), the origin \(o_0\) of frame 0, to the first joint \(o_1\), the second from \(o_1\) to the tip \(o_2\). The angle \(q_1 = 30°\) lies between the axis and the first link, the angle \(q_2 = 60°\) between the extension of the first link (dashed) and the second link. The tip lies at \((0{,}866;\ 1{,}000)\) m. Now we ask where the tip moves if we turn a single joint. The two columns of the matrix \(\mat J\) are exactly that: \(\vek j_1\) is the velocity of the tip at \(\dot{\vek q} = (1, 0)\) rad/s, \(\vek j_2\) at \(\dot{\vek q} = (0, 1)\) rad/s. By theorem 27.42 column \(i\) of \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) for a revolute joint is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_2 - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\), for arm A with \(\vek z = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and positions \(\vek o_0 = \vek 0\), \(\vek o_1 = l_1(\cos q_1, \sin q_1)\) therefore

\[\mat J = \begin{pmatrix} -l_1\sin q_1 - l_2\sin(q_1+q_2) & -l_2\sin(q_1+q_2) \\ l_1\cos q_1 + l_2\cos(q_1+q_2) & l_2\cos(q_1+q_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{27.26}\]

For arm A we are interested only in the components of the tip velocity in the plane, so from the matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) (three rows, the third is zero) we take the upper two rows and in this section write them just as \(\mat J\). At \(\vek q = (30°, 60°)\) we have \(\sin(q_1+q_2) = \sin 90° = 1\) and \(\cos(q_1+q_2) = \cos 90° = 0\), so

\[\vek j_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0{,}866 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m} , \qquad \vek j_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m} .\]

By the definition of the product of a matrix and a vector (definition 1.97) the tip velocity is the sum \(\mat J\dot{\vek q} = \dot q_1\,\vek j_1 + \dot q_2\,\vek j_2\). The two columns point in two different directions of the plane, so we can build every planar velocity from them. Let the tip move at \(0{,}1\) m/s in the direction \(x_0\), \(\vek v = (0{,}1;\ 0)\) m/s. The second row \(0{,}866\,\dot q_1 = 0\) gives \(\dot q_1 = 0\), the first \(-0{,}5\,\dot q_2 = 0{,}1\) gives \(\dot q_2 = -0{,}2\) rad/s. Indeed: only the second joint turns, and the end of the second link, which is \(0{,}5\) m long and points vertically, then moves horizontally at \(0{,}5 \cdot 0{,}2 = 0{,}1\) m/s, by the law for the velocity of a point of a rigid body (lemma 8.87).

Now stretch the arm and keep the first angle, \(\vek q = (30°, 0)\) (figure 27.64, right). Both links lie on the same line through \(O\). The columns are now \(\vek j_1 = (-0{,}75;\ 1{,}299)\) m and \(\vek j_2 = (-0{,}25;\ 0{,}433)\) m, and \(\vek j_1 = 3\,\vek j_2\). They are drawn side by side so that they do not overlap, and they point in the same direction, perpendicular to the arm. The reason is the law for the velocity of a point of a rigid body (lemma 8.87). The first link turns about \(O\) with angular velocity \(\dot q_1\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so its contribution to the tip velocity is \(\dot q_1\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times(\vek o_2 - \vek o_0)\), and a cross product is perpendicular to both factors (lemma 2.34). The second link turns relative to the first about \(o_1\) and contributes \(\dot q_2\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times(\vek o_2 - \vek o_1)\), perpendicular to \(\vek o_2 - \vek o_1\). Since \(\vek o_2 - \vek o_0\) and \(\vek o_2 - \vek o_1\) lie on the same line, both contributions are perpendicular to the same line. Every tip velocity that we get from joint velocities therefore has component zero along the arm. The radial direction (dashed, crossed) cannot be reached, however fast we turn the joints.

We name the configuration in which this happens (right in figure 27.64). The word comes from the Latin singularis, single, exceptional: such configurations are exceptions among all configurations of the arm. In matrices the same word names those that have no inverse. The Jacobian matrix in such configurations is not invertible, so we call the configuration singular. Which rows of the matrix we count is decided by the task. For arm A we care about the planar position of the tip, so two rows. For arm B of this section, a three-joint arm in space, we care about the position of the tip, so the matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) with three rows. We count the independent rows with the pivots of the section on elimination (definition 8.21).

Definition 27.45 Singular configuration (singularna lega)

Let \(\mat J_*(\vek q)\) be a matrix of size \(m \times n\) with \(m \le n\) whose rows are the components of the tip velocity that the task requires (for arm A the two planar components, for arm B the three components of \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\), for a six-joint arm all six rows of \(\mat J\)) and whose columns are one per joint. The configuration \(\vek q\) is singular if \(\mat J_*(\vek q)\) does not have a pivot in every row (definition 8.21), like the parallel columns of the stretched arm A. Otherwise the configuration is regular (regularna). For a square matrix \(m = n\) this means that it does not have \(n\) pivots.

Source: SPONG §4.9 (PDF pp. 144-145) defines singular configurations by the number of independent rows being smaller than its maximum. We use pivots instead of this number, because the book introduces them in the section on elimination.

The word configuration (lega) is the one of definition 8.42: the set of all joint variables. Singularity is a property of the configuration, not of the arm: the same arm is regular in most configurations (left in figure 27.64).

For square matrices of order 2 and 3 we have a simple test, the determinant. Determinants in the book are defined for order 3 (definition 2.72), for order 2 we write \(\det\mat A = A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21}\).

Lemma 27.46 Determinant and pivots (determinanta in pivoti)

A square matrix \(\mat A\) of order 2 or 3 has a full set of pivots if and only if \(\det\mat A \neq 0\).

Proof

We must show that \(\det\mat A = 0\) implies missing pivots and the converse. Order 3 is a direct consequence of two known lemmas, order 2 we show separately.

  1. Order 3. By lemma 8.22 \(\mat A\) has three pivots if and only if \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek w = \vek 0\). By lemma 2.75 \(\det\mat A = 0\) if and only if there is \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\). The two statements are opposite, so \(\det\mat A \neq 0\) if and only if the pivots are full.
  2. Order 2, \(\det\mat A \neq 0\). The matrix \(\mat B = \begin{pmatrix} A_{22} & -A_{12} \\ -A_{21} & A_{11} \end{pmatrix}\) gives by multiplication \(\mat A\mat B = (\det\mat A)\,\mat I\). If \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\), then \((\det\mat A)\,\vek w = \mat B\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\) (also \(\mat B\mat A = (\det\mat A)\,\mat I\)), so \(\vek w = \vek 0\). By lemma 8.22 \(\mat A\) has two pivots.
  3. Order 2, \(\det\mat A = 0\). The column \(\vek w = (A_{22}, -A_{12})\) gives \(\mat A\vek w = (\det\mat A,\ 0) = \vek 0\). If \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\), by lemma 8.22 \(\mat A\) does not have two pivots. If \(\vek w = \vek 0\), the second column of the matrix is zero, so \(\mat A(0,1) = \vek 0\) with a nonzero vector, and \(\mat A\) again does not have two pivots.

Source: The proof is not from the sources.

Now we show what a singular configuration means for velocities and for forces. Both statements follow from the same deficiency of the matrix, so we write them together.

Theorem 27.47 Velocities at a singular configuration (hitrosti pri singularni legi)

Let \(\mat J_*\) be a square matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek\xi = \mat J_*\dot{\vek q}\) the relation between the joint velocities and the tip velocity.

  1. If the configuration is regular, \(\mat J_*\dot{\vek q} = \vek\xi\) has for every \(\vek\xi\) exactly one solution \(\dot{\vek q} = \mat J_*^{-1}\vek\xi\).
  2. If the configuration is singular, there is \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*\vek w = \vek 0\) (the joints turn, the tip is at rest) and there is \(\vek u \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*^\T\vek u = \vek 0\). Every velocity \(\vek\xi = \mat J_*\dot{\vek q}\) satisfies \(\vek u\cdot\vek\xi = 0\), and the velocity \(\vek\xi = \vek u\) belongs to no \(\dot{\vek q}\).
Proof

We must show both points. The first is the lemma on pivots, the second applies the same lemma to \(\mat J_*\) and to its transpose.

  1. A regular configuration means \(n\) pivots (definition 27.45). By lemma 8.22 the system \(\mat J_*\dot{\vek q} = \vek\xi\) has exactly one solution for every right-hand side and \(\mat J_*\) has an inverse matrix.
  2. A singular configuration means fewer than \(n\) pivots. By lemma 8.22 (\(n\) pivots if and only if \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek w = \vek 0\)) there is \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*\vek w = \vek 0\).
  3. We apply the same lemma to \(\mat J_*^\T\). If \(\mat J_*^\T\) had \(n\) pivots, then by this lemma so would \((\mat J_*^\T)^\T = \mat J_*\). It does not, so there is \(\vek u \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*^\T\vek u = \vek 0\).
  4. For every \(\dot{\vek q}\) we have \(\vek u\cdot(\mat J_*\dot{\vek q}) = (\mat J_*^\T\vek u)\cdot\dot{\vek q} = 0\) (lemma 1.102). If \(\mat J_*\dot{\vek q} = \vek u\) held, we would have \(\vek u\cdot\vek u = 0\), which is false for \(\vek u \neq \vek 0\).
Explanation

The vector \(\vek u\) is the dashed unreachable direction in the figures of this section, the vector \(\vek w\) the motion of the joints that does not move the tip.

Source: SPONG §4.9 (PDF pp. 144-145) says the same with the number of independent rows, the proof is not from the source.

The right part of figure 27.64 shows point 2 on an example: at \(\vek q = (30°, 0)\) we have \(\vek u = (\cos 30°, \sin 30°) = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}5)\), the unit vector along the arm, and \(\vek u\cdot\vek j_1 = \vek u\cdot\vek j_2 = 0\). The vector \(\vek w\) is here \((1, -3)\), since \(\vek j_1 - 3\vek j_2 = \vek 0\). A singular configuration is not just a point where something does not work: already near it the joint velocities grow beyond every bound when the tip velocity has a component in the direction \(\vek u\). How fast, the determinant shows.

Lemma 27.48 Singular configurations of arm A (singularne lege roke A)

Arm A has \(\det\mat J = l_1l_2\sin q_2\). The configuration is singular if and only if \(q_2 = 0\) or \(q_2 = \pi\) (up to full turns), that is, when the arm is stretched or folded. The angle \(q_1\) does not matter.

Proof

We must show the expression for the determinant and its zeros. The matrix is (27.26), its determinant is \(J_{11}J_{22} - J_{12}J_{21}\) (notation before lemma 27.46). We write \(s_1 = \sin q_1\), \(c_1 = \cos q_1\), \(s_{12} = \sin(q_1+q_2)\), \(c_{12} = \cos(q_1+q_2)\).

  1. \(\det\mat J = (-l_1s_1 - l_2s_{12})\,l_2c_{12} - (-l_2s_{12})(l_1c_1 + l_2c_{12})\).
  2. The terms \(-l_2^2s_{12}c_{12}\) and \(+l_2^2s_{12}c_{12}\) cancel, what remains is \(\det\mat J = l_1l_2\,(s_{12}c_1 - s_1c_{12})\).
  3. By the addition formula (corollary 8.16) \(s_{12}c_1 - s_1c_{12} = \sin((q_1 + q_2) - q_1) = \sin q_2\). So \(\det\mat J = l_1l_2\sin q_2\).
  4. Since \(l_1l_2 = 0{,}5\) m² \(\neq 0\), \(\det\mat J = 0\) exactly when \(\sin q_2 = 0\), that is \(q_2 = k\pi\). By lemma 27.46 this is exactly the singular configuration. The figure says the same: \(|\det\mat J|\) is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns \(\vek j_1\) and \(\vek j_2\). On the left of figure 27.64 the area is \(0{,}433\) m² (at \(q_2 = 60°\)), on the right, where the columns are parallel, the parallelogram is flat and the area is zero.

Source: SPONG, equation (4.62) and PDF page 151 (the same determinant, zeros at \(\theta_2 = 0, \pi\)). The step-by-step writing and the proof are not from the source.

The stretched arm (\(q_2 = 0\), right in figure 27.64) is on the outer edge of the workspace (lemma 27.7), the folded one (\(q_2 = \pi\)) on its inner edge. For arm A the singular configurations are therefore on the edges. Arm B will show that this is not necessary.

We also look at what "near" means (configurations between the left and the right part of figure 27.64). Let the tip velocity be \(\vek v = (0{,}1;\ 0)\) m/s, \(q_1 = 30°\). The inverse of a matrix of order 2 is \(\mat J^{-1} = \mat B/\det\mat J\) with \(\mat B\) from step 2 of the proof of lemma 27.46, so

\[\dot q_1 = \frac{0{,}1\,\cos(q_1+q_2)}{l_1\sin q_2} \ \text{m/s} . \tag{27.27}\]

The denominator is \(\sin q_2\), from lemma 27.48. The table shows what happens as \(q_2\) falls toward zero.

\(q_2\) \(\dot q_1\) (rad/s) \(\dot q_2\) (rad/s) \(\det\mat J\) (m²)
\(60°\) \(0\) \(-0{,}2\) \(0{,}43301\)
\(10°\) \(0{,}4411\) \(-1{,}4386\) \(0{,}08682\)
\(5°\) \(0{,}9399\) \(-2{,}9272\) \(0{,}04358\)
\(1°\) \(4{,}9115\) \(-14{,}8359\) \(0{,}00873\)
\(0{,}1°\) \(49{,}5696\) \(-148{,}8088\) \(0{,}00087\)

The tip velocity is the same in all rows, \(0{,}1\) m/s (configurations between the left and the right part of figure 27.64). When the angle drops from \(10°\) to \(1°\), \(\sin q_2\) falls by the factor \(\sin 10°/\sin 1° = 9{,}95\), and \(\cos(q_1+q_2)\) rises from \(\cos 40° = 0{,}766\) to \(\cos 31° = 0{,}857\), that is by the factor \(1{,}119\). By (27.27) \(\dot q_1\) grows by \(9{,}95 \cdot 1{,}119 = 11{,}1\), and indeed \(4{,}9115/0{,}4411 = 11{,}1\). A motor does not have unbounded speed, so a real arm cannot keep this tip velocity any more as it approaches the stretched configuration, before it reaches the configuration. At \(q_2 = 0\) there is no solution if the velocity has a component along the arm (theorem 27.47, point 2), and ours has one, \(\vek u\cdot\vek v = 0{,}866 \cdot 0{,}1 = 0{,}0866\) m/s. If the component along the arm is zero, there are infinitely many solutions: by theorem 27.47 we add to every solution a multiple of the vector \(\vek w = (1, -3)\), for which the tip is at rest.

A measure of how far a configuration is from a singular one is the manipulability (manipulability) \(\mu = |\det\mat J|\) for a square matrix. SPONG names it in §4.12 (equations (4.121) and (4.123), PDF p. 156) and extends it to general matrices with the root of \(\det\mat J\mat J^\T\) and the manipulability ellipsoid. We only cite this and do not prove it. For arm A (figure 27.64) we have \(\mu = l_1l_2|\sin q_2|\): \(0{,}5\) m² at \(q_2 = 90°\), \(0{,}433\) m² at \(60°\), \(0{,}087\) m² at \(10°\) and \(0\) at \(0°\).

27.7.2 An arm in space: elbow and shoulder singularities

In arm A all singular configurations are on the edges of the workspace. An arm with three revolute joints in space has one more kind. Take arm B, an elbow arm (komolčna roka), after the layout of the book SPONG (Example 4.9, PDF pp. 147-148, figure 4.5) with our dimensions \(d_1 = 0{,}4\) m, \(a_2 = 0{,}5\) m, \(a_3 = 0{,}4\) m. We repeat its DH table from section 27.4: the rows \((a_{\mathrm{i}}, \alpha_{\mathrm{i}}, d_{\mathrm{i}}, \theta_{\mathrm{i}})\) are \((0,\ 90°,\ d_1,\ q_1)\), \((a_2,\ 0,\ 0,\ q_2)\), \((a_3,\ 0,\ 0,\ q_3)\). The first joint turns the base about the vertical axis \(z_0\), the second and third joints have parallel, horizontal axes and raise the upper arm and the forearm (figure 27.65).

How do we get to the columns of \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\)? By theorem 27.42 they are \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_3 - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\). We need the axes and origins of frames 0, 1, 2. By theorem 27.30 \(\vek o_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1} + d_{\mathrm{i}}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1} + a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and by lemma 27.28 the columns of \(\mat R^{i-1}_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the axes of frame \(i\). From the first row of the table, frame 1 has \(\vek x_1 = (c_1, s_1, 0)\), \(\vek y_1 = (0, 0, 1)\) and \(\vek z_1 = (s_1, -c_1, 0)\) (because of \(\alpha_1 = 90°\) the axis \(z\) is horizontal) and origin \(\vek o_1 = (0, 0, d_1)\). In the second and third rows \(\alpha = 0\), the axes \(z\) do not change, \(\vek z_2 = \vek z_1\), and the frame turns about it: \(\vek x_2 = c_2\vek x_1 + s_2\vek y_1 = (c_1c_2,\ s_1c_2,\ s_2)\) and by the same computation \(\vek x_3 = (c_1c_{23},\ s_1c_{23},\ s_{23})\) with \(c_{23} = \cos(q_2+q_3)\) and \(s_{23} = \sin(q_2+q_3)\). The origins are \(\vek o_2 = \vek o_1 + a_2\vek x_2\) and \(\vek o_3 = \vek o_2 + a_3\vek x_3\). With the notation \(r = a_2c_2 + a_3c_{23}\) and \(w = a_2s_2 + a_3s_{23}\) the tip is

\[\vek o_3 = (c_1 r,\ s_1 r,\ d_1 + w) . \tag{27.28}\]

At \(\vek q = (30°, 45°, -60°)\) we have \(r = 0{,}7399\) m, \(w = 0{,}25\) m and \(\vek o_3 = (0{,}6408;\ 0{,}37;\ 0{,}65)\) m. The rotation axis of the base \(z_0\) is drawn dashed in figure 27.65, the links as thick lines from the joint \(o_1\) to \(o_2\) and from \(o_2\) to \(o_3\), the angle \(q_2\) lies between the horizontal in the direction of the axis \(\vek x_1\) (dashed) and the upper arm.

Lemma 27.49 Singular configurations of arm B (singularne lege roke B)

For arm B

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = \begin{pmatrix} -s_1 r & -c_1 w & -a_3c_1s_{23} \\ c_1 r & -s_1 w & -a_3s_1s_{23} \\ 0 & r & a_3c_{23} \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = -a_2a_3\sin q_3\ (a_2\cos q_2 + a_3\cos(q_2+q_3)) . \tag{27.29}\]

The configuration is singular if and only if \(\sin q_3 = 0\) (elbow stretched or folded) or \(a_2\cos q_2 + a_3\cos(q_2+q_3) = 0\), that is \(r = 0\) (the tip on the axis \(z_0\)). In the first case \(\vek u = \vek x_2\) is an unreachable direction, in the second the first column is zero, so turning the base does not move the tip.

Proof

We must show the columns, the determinant and the zeros. We compute the columns from theorem 27.42 and the expressions before the lemma, the determinant by expansion along the first column.

  1. Column 1. \(\vek z_0 = (0,0,1)\) and \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_0 = (c_1r,\ s_1r,\ d_1 + w)\). The cross product \((0,0,1)\times(a,b,c) = (-b,\ a,\ 0)\) gives \((-s_1r,\ c_1r,\ 0)\).
  2. Column 2. \(\vek z_1 = (s_1, -c_1, 0)\) and \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_1 = (c_1r,\ s_1r,\ w)\). By the formula for the cross product \((a_1,a_2,a_3)\times(b_1,b_2,b_3) = (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1 - a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)\) the components are \((-c_1w,\ -s_1w,\ s_1\cdot s_1r + c_1\cdot c_1r) = (-c_1w,\ -s_1w,\ r)\).
  3. Column 3. \(\vek z_2 = \vek z_1\) and \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_2 = a_3\vek x_3 = a_3(c_1c_{23},\ s_1c_{23},\ s_{23})\). The same product gives \(a_3(-c_1s_{23},\ -s_1s_{23},\ s_1^2c_{23} + c_1^2c_{23}) = a_3(-c_1s_{23},\ -s_1s_{23},\ c_{23})\).
  4. Expansion along the first column: \(\det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = (-s_1r)\,M_{11} - (c_1r)\,M_{21}\) with the minors \(M_{11} = (-s_1w)(a_3c_{23}) - (-a_3s_1s_{23})\,r = a_3s_1K\) and \(M_{21} = (-c_1w)(a_3c_{23}) - (-a_3c_1s_{23})\,r = a_3c_1K\), where \(K = s_{23}r - c_{23}w\).
  5. The sum: \(\det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = -a_3rK\,(s_1^2 + c_1^2) = -a_3rK\).
  6. \(K = s_{23}(a_2c_2 + a_3c_{23}) - c_{23}(a_2s_2 + a_3s_{23}) = a_2(s_{23}c_2 - c_{23}s_2) = a_2\sin q_3\) by the addition formula (corollary 8.16). So \(\det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = -a_2a_3\sin q_3\,r\).
  7. By lemma 27.46 the configuration is singular exactly when \(\det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\). Since \(a_2a_3 \neq 0\), this means \(\sin q_3 = 0\) or \(r = 0\).
  8. The unreachable direction. If \(\sin q_3 = 0\), then \(\vek x_3 = \pm\vek x_2\), so \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_1\) and \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_2\) are multiples of \(\vek x_2\). Columns 2 and 3 are cross products with \(\vek x_2\) and by lemma 2.34 are perpendicular to it. For column 1 we have \(\vek x_2\cdot(-s_1r,\ c_1r,\ 0) = r\,c_2(-c_1s_1 + s_1c_1) = 0\). So \(\vek x_2\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = \vek 0\), that is \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\vek x_2 = \vek 0\).
  9. If \(r = 0\), all three components \(-s_1r\), \(c_1r\), \(0\) of column 1 are zero.

Source: SPONG, Example 4.9 (PDF pp. 147-148), equations (4.97) and (4.98). The book states the determinant and leaves the computation to the reader (Problem 4-12), so the proof by steps is ours.

Let us look at both cases in figure 27.65. At \(\vek q = (30°, 45°, 0)\) (left) the elbow is stretched: \(\vek o_1\), \(\vek o_2\), \(\vek o_3\) lie on one line with the unit vector \(\vek x_2 = (0{,}6124;\ 0{,}3536;\ 0{,}7071)\). The column \(\vek j_2\) points perpendicular to this line (column 3 is parallel to it), and the line is unreachable, as in arm A. Column 1 is still nonzero, the base can turn and move the tip horizontally, perpendicular to the plane of the arm. The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) therefore still has two pivots: one direction is unreachable, the other two the tip reaches. In arm A at \(q_2 = 0\) there is only one pivot, because the columns are parallel, \(\vek j_1 = 3\,\vek j_2\). At \(q_3 = 180°\) (folded elbow) the same holds with the same direction \(\vek x_2\).

The other case is more interesting. At \(\vek q = (30°, 45°, 107{,}1144°)\) (figure 27.65, right) we have \(r = a_2\cos 45° + a_3\cos(152{,}1144°) = 0\): the tip is on the axis \(z_0\), at the height \(0{,}9406\) m, and \(\det\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\). Why is it singular exactly here? The base turns about \(z_0\) with angular velocity \(\dot q_1\vek z_0\), and the velocity of a point on the axis is by the law for the velocity of a rigid body \(\dot q_1\vek z_0\times(\vek o_3 - \vek o_0)\) (lemma 8.87), and this is \(\vek 0\), since \(\vek o_3 - \vek o_0 = (0, 0, 0{,}9406)\) m is a multiple of \(\vek z_0\) (lemma 2.34, points 1 and 3). Turning the base does not move the tip, as the zero first column shows. The unreachable direction is the horizontal tangent \(\vek u = (-s_1,\ c_1,\ 0) = (-0{,}5;\ 0{,}866;\ 0)\), since \(\vek u^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} = (r,\ 0,\ 0)\) (columns 2 and 3 have in the first two rows the ratio \(c_1 : s_1\), so they are perpendicular to \(\vek u\)). The tip is \(0{,}9406 - 0{,}4 = 0{,}5406\) m from the shoulder \(o_1\), more than \(a_2 - a_3 = 0{,}1\) m and less than \(a_2 + a_3 = 0{,}9\) m, so it lies inside the workspace (lemma 27.7 for the two links in the plane of the arm), not on its edge, and yet in this configuration it cannot move in the direction \(\vek u\). So arm B has two kinds of singular configurations: the elbow ones on the edges of the workspace and the shoulder one in its interior. SPONG (PDF p. 148) says that in an arm with an offset in the elbow or the shoulder the wrist cannot hit the axis \(z_0\) (Figure 4.8, PDF p. 149), and that points reachable in a singular configuration may no longer be reachable after a small change of the dimensions.

As in the stretched elbow of arm B (figure 27.65), singularity also arises when the axes of two revolute joints lie on one line. In a spherical wrist (kroglasto zapestje), which arms with six joints have, this happens when the axes \(\vek z_3\) and \(\vek z_5\) are collinear, that is at \(q_5 = 0\) or \(\pi\) (SPONG §4.9.2, PDF p. 147). We see the reason in the angular velocity. Let the first three joints be at rest. By lemma 27.39 then \(\vek\omega = \vek z_3\dot q_4 + \vek z_4\dot q_5 + \vek z_5\dot q_6\), and at \(\vek z_5 = \vek z_3\) the choice \((\dot q_4, \dot q_5, \dot q_6) = (1, 0, -1)\) rad/s gives angular velocity zero: the joints turn, the wrist is at rest. SPONG separates the singularities of the arm and of the wrist by splitting the matrix \(\mat J\) into blocks (§4.9.1, PDF pp. 145-147). We do not prove this splitting.

27.7.3 Joint forces

Back to the bucket. Let arm A be in the configuration \(\vek q = (0°, 45°)\), the first link horizontal, the second pointing up at \(45°\) to the first (figure 27.66). From the tip hangs a load of \(5\) kg, which pulls with the vertical downward force \(\vek F\) of magnitude \(5 \cdot 9{,}81 = 49{,}05\) N. The force acts along its line, downward through the tip, and it pulls: the arrow starts at the tip. What must the motors do? The magnitude of the moment of a force about a point (definition 2.37) is the force times the lever arm, the perpendicular distance of the point from the line of the force (lemma 2.38). The line of the force is vertical, so the lever arm about the joint \(O\) is the horizontal distance \(1{,}354\) m (the horizontal coordinate of the tip), and about the joint \(o_1\) it is \(0{,}354\) m. The moment of the force about \(O\) is \(1{,}354 \cdot 49{,}05 = 66{,}39\) N m, about \(o_1\) it is \(0{,}354 \cdot 49{,}05 = 17{,}34\) N m. If we do not count the weight of the links for now, then for rest the motor in each joint must supply a torque of equal magnitude and opposite sense. Why this is exactly what the matrix \(\mat J\) says, we show below. First we write down what a load on the tip means.

In general a load on the tip (figure 27.66) is a force \(\vek f\) at the point \(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}\) and a moment \(\vek m\) acting on the last link. We write them as a column \(\vek F = (\vek f,\ \vek m)\) of six numbers, arranged like \(\vek\xi = (\vek v,\ \vek\omega)\) in section 27.6. The load is the force with which the surroundings (the load, the workpiece, the ground) act on the arm, not the other way round. The motor torques \(\boldsymbol\tau\) are by the notation of the chapter (notation in section 27.6) the generalized forces of the drives, as in definition 8.51. The weight of the links we write as a column \(\vek g(\vek q)\) (bold, to tell it from the gravitational acceleration \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s²): the torques that the motors need merely to hold the links. Let link \(k\) have mass \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and centre of mass \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) with velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.97), and let the unit vector \(\vek e\) point vertically up. Then

\[\vek g(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\,g\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek e . \tag{27.30}\]

Why this form will be shown by the proof of the theorem. For arm A with the centres at the middle of the links, \(m_1 = 10\) kg and \(m_2 = 5\) kg (both masses are our choice, not a source's), \(g_1 = m_1gl_{\mathrm{c}1}\cos q_1 + m_2g(l_1\cos q_1 + l_{\mathrm{c}2}\cos(q_1+q_2))\) and \(g_2 = m_2gl_{\mathrm{c}2}\cos(q_1+q_2)\) with \(l_{\mathrm{c}1} = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}25\) m. At \(\vek q = (0°, 45°)\) we have \(\vek g = (106{,}771;\ 8{,}671)\) N m.

Theorem 27.50 Joint torques of a static load (sile v zglobih)

Let an open chain of \(n\) joints with rigid links and ideal joints (section 27.1), whose joint variables are generalized coordinates (for a planar chain with revolute joints lemma 27.4 says so), have the Jacobian matrix \(\mat J(\vek q) = \begin{pmatrix}\mat J_{\mathrm{v}} \\ \mat J_\omega\end{pmatrix}\) and a load \(\vek F = (\vek f,\ \vek m)\) on the last link. The generalized force of the load is \(\mat J^\T\vek F\), so its \(s\)-th component is \(\vek f\cdot\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + \vek m\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{s}}\) with the \(s\)-th column \(\vek w_{\mathrm{s}}\) of \(\mat J_\omega\). The arm is at rest in the configuration \(\vek q\) if and only if the motors supply

\[\boldsymbol\tau = \vek g(\vek q) - \mat J(\vek q)^\T\vek F . \tag{27.31}\]

Component \(i\) of \(\mat J^\T\vek F\) is for a revolute joint the moment of the load about the axis of the joint, \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot\big((\vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\times\vek f + \vek m\big)\), for a prismatic joint the component of the force along the axis, \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot\vek f\).

Proof

We must show that the load has the generalized force \(\mat J^\T\vek F\) and that equilibrium is the sum of all generalized forces. We use equilibrium in generalized coordinates from chapter 8.

  1. The joint variables are generalized coordinates of the chain (hypothesis of the theorem, for a planar chain with revolute joints lemma 27.4), the joints are ideal constraints (definition 8.3). So theorem 8.53 holds: the configuration \(\vek q\) is an equilibrium if and only if the sum of the generalized forces of all external forces and moments is zero (in figure 27.66 the load and the motor torques).
  2. The generalized force of the load: by definition 8.51 its component \(s\) is \(\vek f\cdot\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + \vek m\cdot\vek w_{\mathrm{s}}\), where \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\vek w_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the virtual rotation of the last link.
  3. With \(\dot{\vek q} = \vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\) in \(\vek v = \mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 27.42) and \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 8.30) the \(s\)-th column of \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\) equals \(\partial\vek o_{\mathrm{n}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The same holds for \(\mat J_\omega\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  4. From steps 2 and 3 the \(s\)-th component of the generalized force is \((\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}^\T\vek f + \mat J_\omega^\T\vek m)_{\mathrm{s}} = (\mat J^\T\vek F)_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  5. The generalized force of gravity: the force on the centre \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(-m_{\mathrm{k}}g\,\vek e\), by definition 8.51 its component \(s\) is \(-m_{\mathrm{k}}g\,\vek e\cdot\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), which is \(-m_{\mathrm{k}}g\,(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek e)_{\mathrm{s}}\) by the same argument as in step 3. The sum over the links is \(-\vek g(\vek q)\).
  6. The motors supply the generalized forces \(\boldsymbol\tau\) (notation of the chapter). By step 1 the equilibrium is \(\boldsymbol\tau - \vek g(\vek q) + \mat J^\T\vek F = \vek 0\), so (27.31).
  7. Component \(i\). For a revolute joint column \(i\) of \(\mat J\) is by theorem 27.42 \((\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1});\ \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1})\). Its scalar product with \(\vek F\) is \((\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times\vek r)\cdot\vek f + \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot\vek m\) with \(\vek r = \vek o_{\mathrm{n}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1}\). By the cyclic exchange in the mixed product (lemma 2.73, point 1) \((\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times\vek r)\cdot\vek f = \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot(\vek r\times\vek f)\). For a prismatic joint the column is \((\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1};\ \vek 0)\) and the component is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot\vek f\). In figure 27.66, for arm A, \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot(\vek r\times\vek f)\) is the force times the lever arm: \(49{,}05 \cdot 1{,}354 = 66{,}39\) N m about \(O\) and \(49{,}05 \cdot 0{,}354 = 17{,}34\) N m about \(o_1\).
Explanation

For \(\vek F = \vek 0\) and links without weight the theorem says only \(\boldsymbol\tau = \vek 0\). With a load and without the weight of the links the torques needed are \(-\mat J^\T\vek F\): opposite to the generalized force of the load \(\mat J^\T\vek F\).

Source: SPONG §4.10 (PDF pp. 151-152), equation (4.104) \(\boldsymbol\tau = \mat J^\T\vek F\), with an informal virtual work and "infinitesimal displacements" \(\delta X = J\,\delta q\) (PDF p. 152). We derive it from equilibrium in generalized coordinates and virtual displacements of chapter 8, without infinitesimal numbers. In equation (4.106) SPONG writes the work as \(F^T\delta X - \tau^T\delta q\), so his \(F\) is the force of the tip on the surroundings, with the opposite sign to our load. The proof by steps is ours.

For arm A the components of the theorem are exactly those that we computed in figure 27.66 as moments. At \(\vek q = (0°, 45°)\) we have by theorem 27.42 \(\mat J = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}3536 & -0{,}3536 \\ 1{,}3536 & 0{,}3536\end{pmatrix}\) m and

\[\mat J^\T\vek F = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}3536 & 1{,}3536 \\ -0{,}3536 & 0{,}3536\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 \\ -49{,}05\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-66{,}392 \\ -17{,}342\end{pmatrix}\ \text{N m} . \tag{27.32}\]

These are exactly \(x_{\mathrm{o}_2}F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1{,}3536 \cdot (-49{,}05)\) and \((x_{\mathrm{o}_2} - x_{\mathrm{o}_1})F_{\mathrm{y}} = 0{,}3536 \cdot (-49{,}05)\), the moments about the joints \(O\) and \(o_1\), as step 7 says. To hold the load alone the motors must supply \(-\mat J^\T\vek F = (66{,}392;\ 17{,}342)\) N m, including the weight of the links by equation (27.31) \(\boldsymbol\tau = \vek g - \mat J^\T\vek F = (106{,}771 + 66{,}392;\ 8{,}671 + 17{,}342) = (173{,}163;\ 26{,}013)\) N m.

At \(\vek q = (30°, 60°)\) and the same load \(\mat J^\T\vek F = (-42{,}479;\ 0)\) N m. The second link points vertically (left in figure 27.64), the line of the force passes through \(o_1\) and the lever arm about \(o_1\) is zero, the first torque is \(0{,}866 \cdot (-49{,}05) = -42{,}479\) N m. A force whose line passes through a joint needs no torque in that joint, since \(\vek r\) and \(\vek f\) of step 7 of theorem 27.50 are parallel and \(\vek r\times\vek f = \vek 0\).

The same matrices also serve the power (figure 27.66): a load \(\vek F\) at the tip, which moves with velocity \(\vek v = \mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\dot{\vek q}\), has power \(\vek F^\T\mat J\dot{\vek q} = (\mat J^\T\vek F)^\T\dot{\vek q}\) (lemma 1.102). At \(\dot{\vek q} = (1, 0)\) rad/s and \(\vek q = (0°, 45°)\) this is \(-66{,}392\) W, the same on both sides. That is why the transposed matrix appears: \(\mat J\) carries joint velocities into tip velocity, and \(\mat J^\T\) carries the force of the tip into joint torques, so that the power stays the same.

27.7.4 Statics at a singular configuration

The inverse of the matrix \(\mat J^\T\) in theorem 27.50 exists in a regular configuration and there the load is determined exactly by the torques: \(\vek F = -\mat J_*^{-\T}\,\boldsymbol\tau\) (theorem 27.47, point 1, for \(\mat J_*^\T\), which by lemma 8.22 also has \(n\) pivots). In a singular configuration it is not so. Why, the stretched arm from the second part of figure 27.64 shows, this time loaded.

Theorem 27.51 Statics at a singular configuration (statika pri singularni legi)

Let \(\mat J_*\) be a square matrix of order \(n\) that belongs to the load \(\vek F_*\) (for arm A a force \(\vek f\) without a moment, for arm B a force without a moment and \(\mat J_{\mathrm{v}}\)). For \(\boldsymbol\tau = -\mat J_*^\T\vek F_*\) (without the weight of the links):

  1. If the configuration is regular, for every \(\boldsymbol\tau\) there is exactly one load \(\vek F_* = -\mat J_*^{-\T}\boldsymbol\tau\).
  2. If the configuration is singular, there is a load \(\vek F_* \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*^\T\vek F_* = \vek 0\), so the torques are zero: the structure holds the arm, not the motors. And there is \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*\vek w = \vek 0\), so \(\vek w\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = 0\) for every balanced load: torques with \(\vek w\cdot\boldsymbol\tau \neq 0\) are balanced by no load and the arm moves.
Proof

We must show both points of theorem 27.47, translated into forces.

  1. Regular configuration: by lemma 8.22 \(\mat J_*^\T\) has \(n\) pivots and an inverse matrix, \(\mat J_*^\T\vek F_* = -\boldsymbol\tau\) has exactly one solution.
  2. Singular configuration: by theorem 27.47, point 2, there is \(\vek u \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*^\T\vek u = \vek 0\). We take \(\vek F_* = \vek u\). For arm A this is the radial force along the stretched arm, which passes through both joints (figure 27.67).
  3. By the same theorem there is \(\vek w \neq \vek 0\) with \(\mat J_*\vek w = \vek 0\). For every \(\vek F_*\) we have \(\vek w\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = -\vek w\cdot(\mat J_*^\T\vek F_*) = -(\mat J_*\vek w)\cdot\vek F_* = 0\) (lemma 1.102).

Source: SPONG §4.9 (PDF p. 145) states that at singular configurations "bounded joint torques may correspond to unbounded end-effector forces and torques". The proof and both directions are ours.

Take arm A at \(\vek q = (30°, 0)\) with a force of \(100\) N along the arm (figure 27.67, top, the arm is turned so that it is horizontal). The line of the force passes through both joints, the lever arm about both is zero, so \(\tau_1 = \tau_2 = 0\) by step 7 of theorem 27.50. Directly: \(\mat J^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{r}} = (0;\ 0)\) N m. The structure carries the force along the link (like the stretched arm with a bucket hanging down), the motors have no work. A force of \(100\) N perpendicular to the arm (bottom) has lever arms \(1{,}5\) m about \(O\) and \(0{,}5\) m about \(o_1\) (measured along the arm), so \(\mat J^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{t}} = (150;\ 50)\) N m. The ratio is \(\tau_1 : \tau_2 = 3 : 1\). The vector \(\vek w = (1, -3)\) of theorem 27.47 therefore indeed gives \(\vek w\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = 150 - 150 = 0\). Conversely, torques with \(\tau_1 \neq 3\tau_2\), for example \((150;\ 0)\) N m, are balanced by no load: by theorem 8.53 the arm is not in equilibrium and starts to move.

Why is it different in a regular configuration (figure 27.64, left)? At \(q_1 = 30°\) and \(q_2 = 60°\) the joint torques \(\boldsymbol\tau = (10;\ 0)\) N m are balanced by exactly one force \(\vek F = -\mat J^{-\T}\boldsymbol\tau = (0;\ -11{,}547)\) N, vertically downward. The closer the configuration is to the stretched one, the larger the force that the same torque still balances: at \(q_2 = 10°\) it is \(57{,}588\) N, at \(1°\) it is \(572{,}987\) N and at \(0{,}1°\) it is \(5729{,}581\) N. The reason is the same as for (27.27): \(\mat J^{-\T} = \mat B^\T/\det\mat J\) with \(\mat B\) of step 2 of the proof of lemma 27.46, and the determinant in the denominator goes to zero. Bounded torques therefore correspond to ever larger forces of the tip as the configuration approaches a singular one (SPONG, PDF p. 145).

Back to the two opening questions. We can move the tip in every direction and every set of motor torques is balanced by exactly one load exactly when the configuration is not singular (theorem 27.47 and theorem 27.51), for arm A exactly when \(\sin q_2 \neq 0\) (lemma 27.48), for arm B when \(\sin q_3\,r \neq 0\) (lemma 27.49). Velocities and forces belong to the same matrix \(\mat J\), one to its columns, the other to its rows. We need the weight of the links \(\vek g(\vek q)\) and \(\mat J^\T\) also in section 27.8: there \(\vek g(\vek q) = \partial V/\partial\vek q\) with the gravitational potential energy \(V\), and equation (27.30) is an expression for the same thing link by link.
