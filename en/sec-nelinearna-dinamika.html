Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.8 Nonlinear dynamics and numerical damping

A pendulum of length \(l = 1\) m is released from the position in which the rod is horizontal. For small deflections the period would be \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g} = 2{,}006\) s, but at a deflection of \(90°\) it is \(2{,}368\) s by theorem 8.123, which is \(18\) % longer. The linear model does not suffice here, because the force on the pendulum is not a linear function of the deflection. In section 17.7 we solved such equations in statics with Newton's method. Now the question is about time: how do we step the equation of motion when the force is nonlinear, and how do we prevent the scheme of chapter 10 from exciting oscillations in the mesh that the model does not have at all?

After section 17.1 (definition 17.2) this section treats the other two kinds of error: the error of discretizing time with a step \(\Delta t\) and the numerical error of the stopped iterations of Newton's method. The modelling error is not reduced: the pendulum is a model of a rigid rod and a point mass, without friction in the pivot and without air. Reducing the step does not reduce the mesh error of section 17.3 either, because the two errors add.

17.8.1 The Newmark family (Newmarkova družina)

In chapter 10 we met two schemes. The trapezoidal rule (definition 10.63) is implicit and unconditionally stable without damping. Central differences (definition 10.68) are explicit and stable only below the critical step. We write both with the same idea: the velocity and the displacement at the end of the step are known from the start of the step and from the acceleration, and the acceleration is fixed by the equation of motion at the end of the step. They differ in how the acceleration is averaged over the step. The velocity changes over a step by \(\Delta t\) times the mean acceleration, so we replace it by the weighted sum \((1 - \gamma)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \gamma\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) with two weights that add to \(1\). For the displacement we take, for each component, the expansion with a Taylor remainder (theorem 8.60): \(q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = q_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\,a(\xi)\) with a point \(\xi\) in the step that may differ from component to component. We replace the term \(\tfrac12 a(\xi)\) by a weighted sum of \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) with the weights \(\tfrac12 - \beta\) and \(\beta\), which add to \(\tfrac12\). Two numbers, \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\), are therefore free, and each pair gives its own scheme. That is why we call it a family. It is named after N. M. Newmark, who proposed it (COOK §11.13, PDF p. 432, BATHE §9.2.3, PDF p. 794). In this section the letters \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) mean only these two parameters, not the shear strain of chapter 1 or anything else.

Definition 17.36 Newmark family (Newmarkova družina)

Let \(\mat M\) be a positive definite and \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) symmetric matrices of order \(n\), \(\vek Q(t)\) a column of forces, \(\Delta t > 0\) a step, \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek v_0\) the initial columns, and \(\gamma\) and \(\beta \ge 0\) real numbers. The Newmark scheme with parameters \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) is the sequence of columns \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\) determined by the initial columns, by equation (10.73) at \(k = 0\) (which gives \(\vek a_0\)) and, for every \(k \ge 0\), by the two equations

\[\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t^2\Big(\big(\tfrac12 - \beta\big)\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \beta\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\Big) , \tag{17.33}\]
\[\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,\Big((1 - \gamma)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \gamma\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\Big) , \tag{17.34}\]

together with equation (10.73) at \(k + 1\). At \(\beta = 0\) the scheme is explicit (equation (17.33) does not contain \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\)), at \(\beta > 0\) it is implicit.

Source: COOK (11.13-3), PDF p. 432, with parameters \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\). BATHE (9.18) and (9.19), PDF p. 794, with parameters \(\delta = \gamma\) and \(\alpha = \beta\). The reason with weights above is ours.

Both schemes of chapter 10 are in the family.

Lemma 17.37 The trapezoidal rule and central differences in the Newmark family (Trapezno pravilo in centralne diference v Newmarkovi družini)
  1. At \(\gamma = \tfrac12\) and \(\beta = \tfrac14\) the Newmark scheme is the trapezoidal rule of definition 10.63.
  2. At \(\gamma = \tfrac12\), \(\beta = 0\) and \(\mat C = \mat 0\) the sequence \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) satisfies equation (10.82) at \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and coincides with the central differences of definition 10.68.
Proof

We show that equations (17.33) and (17.34) turn into the equations of chapter 10.

  1. At \(\gamma = \tfrac12\) equation (17.34) is equation (10.75). At \(\beta = \tfrac14\) the right side of equation (17.33) equals \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\). From equation (10.75) we have \(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\), so this is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1})\), which is equation (10.74). This is point 1.
  2. At \(\beta = 0\) we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\). We insert equation (17.34) with \(\gamma = \tfrac12\) into the same expression for step \(k + 1\): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+2} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\).
  3. We subtract the first equality from the second: \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+2} - 2\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \Delta t^2\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). Since \(\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = -\mat K\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) at \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) and \(\mat C = \mat 0\), this is equation (10.82) at index \(k + 1\). The start \(\vek q_1 = \vek q_0 + \Delta t\,\vek v_0 + \tfrac12\Delta t^2\vek a_0\) is the start of definition 10.68. This is point 2.

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 432): "average acceleration" is \(\gamma = \tfrac12\), \(\beta = \tfrac14\). BATHE §9.2.3 (PDF p. 794): the same as the "constant-average-acceleration method (also called trapezoidal rule)". The sources do not state point 2 in this form. The proof is not from a source.

Figure 17.23 shows the family in the plane of the parameters. We draw a horizontal axis \(\gamma\) and a vertical axis \(\beta\). Both parameters lie on them, and every point of the plane is one scheme. The first point is the trapezoidal rule at \(\gamma = \tfrac12\), \(\beta = \tfrac14\), which lemma 17.37 places in the family. Below it on the same vertical are \(\beta = \tfrac16\), the linear acceleration scheme that COOK and BATHE name (COOK PDF p. 432, BATHE PDF p. 794), and \(\beta = 0\), central differences by point 2 of the lemma. Both are only conditionally stable (COOK (11.13-8), PDF p. 433), and for central differences theorem 10.70 proves it. The blue region is the region of unconditional stability for linear equations: \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\) (COOK (11.13-7), PDF p. 433, not proved here). We shall prove less, but enough for our use, namely for the line of the family \(\beta = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4\), which lies inside the region at \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\), because \(\beta - \gamma/2 = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4 - \gamma/2 = (\gamma - \tfrac12)^2/4 \ge 0\), so \(2\beta \ge \gamma\). On it also lie \(\gamma = 0{,}6\) with \(\beta = 0{,}3025\) and \(\gamma = 0{,}7\) with \(\beta = 0{,}36\), which we shall compare with the trapezoidal rule. The reason for this line is in the step that we compute now.

17.8.2 The step for one mode (Korak za en način)

As with the trapezoidal rule we test the scheme on one mode, since at \(\mat C = \mat 0\) and \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) it splits by modes. Mode \(r\) has the eigenpair \((\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) of theorem 10.13, the coordinate \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v_{\mathrm{k}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). The quantity \(p\) has the same unit as \(\eta\), so we can build one column \((\eta, p)\) from the two. The scheme takes a step with this column as with a matrix \(\mat A\) that has two rows and two columns. The reader knows the matrix, its trace (sled, the sum of the diagonal elements) and its determinant. An eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat A\) is a number \(\lambda\) for which the equation \(\mat A\vek x = \lambda\vek x\) has a nonzero solution. For two rows these two numbers are the roots of the equation \(\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}\mat A)\,\lambda + \det\mat A = 0\). The spectral radius (spektralni polmer) \(\rho(\mat A)\) is the largest absolute value among the eigenvalues. It is called a radius because all eigenvalues lie in the complex plane in the disc of this radius around the origin. Writing it with the matrix, \(\rho(\mat A)\), tells it apart from the density \(\rho\) of other chapters.

Lemma 17.38 One step of the Newmark scheme in one mode (Korak Newmarkove sheme v enem načinu)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C = \mat 0\), \(\vek Q = \vek 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\), \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\) and \(D = 1 + \beta\Omega^2\). For the Newmark scheme (definition 17.36) we have

\[\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1} \\ p_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}+1}\end{pmatrix} = \mat A\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} \\ p_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat A = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{1 - (\frac12 - \beta)\Omega^2}{D} & \dfrac{\Omega}{D} \\[2mm] -\Omega(1 - \gamma) - \gamma\Omega A_{11} & 1 - \dfrac{\gamma\Omega^2}{D}\end{pmatrix} , \tag{17.35}\]

where \(A_{11}\) is the element at the top left, and

\[\operatorname{tr}\mat A = 2 - \frac{(\gamma + \frac12)\,\Omega^2}{D} , \qquad \det\mat A = 1 - \frac{(\gamma - \frac12)\,\Omega^2}{D} . \tag{17.36}\]
Proof

We show that equations (17.33) and (17.34) are written by modes with the matrix \(\mat A\), and then we compute the trace and the determinant.

  1. We multiply equations (17.33) and (17.34) by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\). As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 10.67, \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek a_{\mathrm{k}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}\). The first equation gives \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \Omega\,p_{\mathrm{k}} - \Omega^2\big((\tfrac12 - \beta)\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \beta\,\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\big)\), the second after division by \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives \(p_{\mathrm{k}+1} = p_{\mathrm{k}} - \Omega\big((1 - \gamma)\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \gamma\,\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\big)\) (we drop the index \(r\)).
  2. We solve the first equation for \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\): \(D\,\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \big(1 - (\tfrac12 - \beta)\Omega^2\big)\eta_{\mathrm{k}} + \Omega\,p_{\mathrm{k}}\). This is the first row of matrix (17.35).
  3. We insert \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) into the second equation. The coefficient of \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(-\Omega(1 - \gamma) - \gamma\Omega A_{11}\), and that of \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(1 - \gamma\Omega\cdot\Omega/D\). This is the second row.
  4. Trace: \(A_{11} + A_{22} = \dfrac{1 - (\frac12 - \beta)\Omega^2 + D - \gamma\Omega^2}{D} = \dfrac{2 + (2\beta - \frac12 - \gamma)\Omega^2}{D}\). Since \(2D = 2 + 2\beta\Omega^2\), this is \(2 - (\gamma + \frac12)\Omega^2/D\).
  5. Determinant: \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21}\). The product \(A_{12}A_{21} = -\dfrac{\Omega^2(1 - \gamma)}{D} - \dfrac{\gamma\Omega^2}{D}A_{11}\), and the product \(A_{11}A_{22} = A_{11} - \dfrac{\gamma\Omega^2}{D}A_{11}\). The terms with \(\gamma\Omega^2A_{11}/D\) cancel in the subtraction and what remains is \(\det\mat A = A_{11} + \dfrac{\Omega^2(1 - \gamma)}{D} = \dfrac{1 + (\frac12 + \beta - \gamma)\Omega^2}{D}\). Since \(D + (\frac12 - \gamma)\Omega^2 = 1 + (\beta + \frac12 - \gamma)\Omega^2\), this is \(1 - (\gamma - \frac12)\Omega^2/D\).

Source: COOK §11.14 (PDF pp. 437 to 439) analyses the same with one mode and the roots of the characteristic equation. Equations (17.35) and (17.36) are ours (SymPy, outlines/ch-modeliranje/num-08.py, part S1, which checks both differences against zero).

The trace and the determinant fix the eigenvalues. For the trapezoidal rule (\(\gamma = \tfrac12\), \(\beta = \tfrac14\)) the determinant is \(1\) and the eigenvalues are \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\,2\arctan(\Omega/2)}\) by theorem 10.67. What does a determinant that is not \(1\) do? The eigenvalues are conjugate numbers \(\lambda = \rho\,\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\varphi}\) and their product is \(\rho^2 = \det\mat A\). As point 2 of the theorem below shows, every step therefore multiplies the size of the oscillation by \(\rho\) and shifts its phase by \(\varphi\). At \(\det\mat A < 1\) the oscillation decays, at \(\det\mat A > 1\) it grows. The eigenvalues, however, are not necessarily conjugate: for a positive discriminant \((\operatorname{tr}\mat A)^2 - 4\det\mat A\) they are real, and then one of them can exceed \(1\) in absolute value even when \(\det\mat A < 1\). At \(\beta = (\gamma + \frac12)^2/4\) this cannot happen, as the theorem shows.

Theorem 17.39 Stability of the Newmark scheme at \(\beta = (\gamma + \frac12)^2/4\) (Stabilnost Newmarkove sheme)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be positive definite, \(\mat C = \mat 0\), \(\vek Q = \vek 0\), \(\gamma\) a real number and \(\beta = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4\). For every mode \(r\) and every \(\Delta t > 0\) with \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\):

  1. The discriminant of the matrix \(\mat A\) of lemma 17.38 is \((\operatorname{tr}\mat A)^2 - 4\det\mat A = -4\Omega^2/D^2 < 0\), so the eigenvalues are conjugate numbers \(\rho\,\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\varphi}\) with \(\varphi \in (0, \pi)\) and
    \[\rho(\mat A)^2 = \det\mat A = 1 - \frac{(\gamma - \frac12)\,\Omega^2}{1 + \beta\,\Omega^2} . \tag{17.37}\]
  2. For every start there are numbers \(R\) and \(\delta\) such that \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}} = R\,\rho^k\cos(k\varphi - \delta)\) for every \(k \ge 0\). At \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\) we therefore have \(|\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}| \le R\) for every \(k\), and the scheme is unconditionally stable in the sense of definition 10.65.
  3. At \(\gamma < \tfrac12\) we have \(\rho > 1\) at every \(\Omega > 0\), and the scheme is not stable at any step.
  4. The point \((\gamma, \beta)\) lies in the region \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\) of figure 17.23 exactly when \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\).
Proof

We show that the conjugate eigenvalues have the product \(\rho^2\) and that the recursion of lemma 10.69 then describes every mode.

  1. With \(g = \gamma + \tfrac12\) we have \(\beta = g^2/4\), so \(4\beta\Omega^2 = g^2\Omega^2 = 4(D - 1)\). By equation (17.36), \(\operatorname{tr}\mat A = 2 - g\Omega^2/D\) and \(\det\mat A = 1 - (g - 1)\Omega^2/D\). The discriminant is \(\big(2 - \tfrac{g\Omega^2}{D}\big)^2 - 4\big(1 - \tfrac{(g - 1)\Omega^2}{D}\big) = -\tfrac{4\Omega^2}{D} + \tfrac{g^2\Omega^4}{D^2} = \tfrac{\Omega^2}{D^2}\big(g^2\Omega^2 - 4D\big)\). Since \(g^2\Omega^2 = 4D - 4\), this is \(-4\Omega^2/D^2 < 0\). Hence \((\operatorname{tr}\mat A)^2 < 4\det\mat A\), so \(\det\mat A > 0\) and the eigenvalues are conjugate numbers with nonzero imaginary part. Their product is \(|\lambda|^2 = \rho^2 = \det\mat A\), which gives equation (17.37). The angle \(\varphi \in (0, \pi)\) is the angle of the eigenvalue with positive imaginary part, \(\cos\varphi = \operatorname{tr}\mat A/(2\rho)\).
  2. For a matrix with two rows, \(\mat A^2 - (\operatorname{tr}\mat A)\,\mat A + (\det\mat A)\,\mat I = \mat 0\) (direct multiplication of the elements). We apply this identity to the column \((\eta_{\mathrm{k}-1}, p_{\mathrm{k}-1})^\T\): for \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and also for \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) we get \(u_{\mathrm{k}+1} - (\operatorname{tr}\mat A)\,u_{\mathrm{k}} + \rho^2\,u_{\mathrm{k}-1} = 0\) at \(k \ge 1\). With \(u_{\mathrm{k}} = \rho^k w_{\mathrm{k}}\) and division by \(\rho^{k+1}\) we get \(w_{\mathrm{k}+1} - 2b\,w_{\mathrm{k}} + w_{\mathrm{k}-1} = 0\) with \(b = \operatorname{tr}\mat A/(2\rho)\) and \(|b| < 1\), because \((\operatorname{tr}\mat A)^2 < 4\rho^2\). By point 1 of lemma 10.69, \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = w_0\cos k\psi + c\sin k\psi\) with \(\cos\psi = b\) and \(\psi \in (0, \pi)\), so \(\psi = \varphi\). With \(w_0\cos k\varphi + c\sin k\varphi = R\cos(k\varphi - \delta)\) and \(R = \sqrt{w_0^2 + c^2}\) we get \(\eta_{\mathrm{k}} = R\rho^k\cos(k\varphi - \delta)\).
  3. At \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\) equation (17.37) gives \(\rho \le 1\), so \(|\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}| \le R_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\) and every \(k\). By corollary 10.14, \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), and by \(\mat M\)-orthonormality \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\eta_{\mathrm{s},\mathrm{k}}^2 \le \sum_{\mathrm{s}} R_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). This is the bound \(B\) of definition 10.65 and it does not depend on \(k\). At \(\gamma = \tfrac12\) we have \(\rho = 1\), the amplitude is constant (as after theorem 10.66).
  4. Point 3. At \(\gamma < \tfrac12\) we have \(\det\mat A > 1\), so \(\rho > 1\). Start with \(\vek q_0 = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek v_0 = \vek 0\), so \(\eta_{\mathrm{r},0} = 1\), \(p_{\mathrm{r},0} = 0\), and \(R \ge 1\). If \(\cos(k\varphi - \delta) \to 0\), then \(|\sin(k\varphi - \delta)| \to 1\) and \(\cos((k+1)\varphi - \delta) = \cos(k\varphi - \delta)\cos\varphi - \sin(k\varphi - \delta)\sin\varphi\) would have an absolute value tending to \(\sin\varphi \ne 0\), which is a contradiction. Hence \(|\cos(k\varphi - \delta)| \ge \varepsilon > 0\) for infinitely many \(k\), and \(|\eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}| \ge R\rho^k\varepsilon\) is not bounded. Since \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} \ge \eta_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{k}}^2\), the scheme is not stable by definition 10.65.
  5. Point 4. From \(\beta - \gamma/2 = (\gamma - \tfrac12)^2/4 \ge 0\) we have \(2\beta \ge \gamma\) at every \(\gamma\), so the condition \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\) is the same as \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\).

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 433), (11.13-7): unconditional stability at \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\), stated there without proof, with a reference to the literature. The choice \(\beta = \tfrac14(\gamma + \tfrac12)^2\) is COOK (11.13-10), PDF p. 434: the best damping of high frequencies while unconditional stability is kept. The proof for this line, the splitting by modes and the recursion are ours. The general region of (11.13-7) is not proved here.

Point 2 of the theorem is what we obtained for the trapezoidal rule from one complex number: the size of the oscillation in mode \(r\) is multiplied by \(\rho\) after every step and the phase is turned by \(\varphi\). At \(\gamma = \tfrac12\) we have \(\rho = 1\), at \(\gamma > \tfrac12\) we have \(\rho < 1\), at \(\gamma < \tfrac12\) we have \(\rho > 1\). This is not conditional stability as with central differences. The scheme at \(\gamma < \tfrac12\) inflates every oscillation at every step.

Definition 17.40 Numerical damping (Numerično dušenje)

Let the Newmark scheme at \(\mat C = \mat 0\) satisfy theorem 17.39 and let \(\rho_{\mathrm{r}} = \rho(\mat A)\) be the spectral radius of the step in mode \(r\) at \(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\Delta t\). The scheme has numerical damping (numerično dušenje, in COOK algorithmic damping) in mode \(r\) if \(\rho_{\mathrm{r}} < 1\). The number

\[\zeta_{\mathrm{num},\mathrm{r}} = -\frac{\ln\rho_{\mathrm{r}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{r}}} \tag{17.38}\]

is the numerical damping ratio of mode \(r\).

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 433 to 434): "algorithmic damping", at \(\gamma > \tfrac12\). The ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{num}}\) is ours: the reason is that viscous damping with ratio \(\zeta\) reduces the size of a mode after time \(\Delta t\) by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega\Delta t}\) (section 10.9.2), and the scheme after one step by \(\rho_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is the same at \(\zeta = \zeta_{\mathrm{num},\mathrm{r}}\).

The name says that the damping does not come from the structure but from the computing procedure: it is not in the matrix \(\mat C\) and we cannot assign it to any mechanism. The damping that comes from the structure is treated in chapter 20.

Corollary 17.41 Numerical damping of high and low modes (Numerično dušenje visokih in nizkih načinov)

Let \(\beta = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4\) and \(\tfrac12 < \gamma \le \tfrac32\). The spectral radius \(\rho(\mat A)\) of equation (17.37) is a decreasing function of the number \(\Omega\) with the limits

\[\rho(\mat A) \to 1 \ \ (\Omega \to 0) , \qquad \rho(\mat A) \to \frac{\tfrac32 - \gamma}{\gamma + \tfrac12} \ \ (\Omega \to \infty) . \tag{17.39}\]

At small \(\Omega\) we have \(\rho(\mat A)^2 = 1 - (\gamma - \tfrac12)\,\Omega^2 + O(\Omega^4)\).

Proof

We show that the fraction \(h(\Omega) = \Omega^2/(1 + \beta\Omega^2)\) in equation (17.37) is increasing and has the limit \(1/\beta\).

  1. We have \(h = \tfrac1\beta\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{1 + \beta\Omega^2}\big)\). The fraction \(1/(1 + \beta\Omega^2)\) decreases with \(\Omega\) towards \(0\), so \(h\) increases from \(0\) towards \(1/\beta\).
  2. Hence \(\rho^2 = 1 - (\gamma - \tfrac12)\,h\) decreases from \(1\) towards \(1 - (\gamma - \tfrac12)/\beta = 1 - \dfrac{4(\gamma - \frac12)}{(\gamma + \frac12)^2}\). Since \((\gamma + \tfrac12)^2 - 4\gamma + 2 = \gamma^2 - 3\gamma + \tfrac94 = (\gamma - \tfrac32)^2\), this limit is \(\dfrac{(\gamma - \frac32)^2}{(\gamma + \frac12)^2}\), so the limit of \(\rho\) is \((\tfrac32 - \gamma)/(\gamma + \tfrac12)\) at \(\gamma \le \tfrac32\).
  3. Expansion: \(h = \Omega^2 - \beta\Omega^4 + \dots\) (the series for \(1/(1 + s)\) with remainder \(s^2/(1+s)\), \(s = \beta\Omega^2\)), from which \(\rho^2 = 1 - (\gamma - \tfrac12)\Omega^2 + O(\Omega^4)\).

Source: COOK §11.13 (PDF p. 433 to 434): "when \(\gamma > \tfrac12\), Newmark methods display algorithmic damping, at the cost of reducing guaranteed accuracy from second-order to first-order", and the choice (11.13-10). The limit at \(\Omega \to \infty\) and the expansion are not in the source. The proof is ours (SymPy, num-08.py, parts S1 and S2).

The expansion also tells why COOK says that damping costs one order of accuracy. The size is multiplied in every step by \(\rho \approx 1 - (\gamma - \tfrac12)\Omega^2/2\) (from \(\sqrt{1 - x} \approx 1 - x/2\) at small \(x\)), and there are about \(2\pi/\Omega\) steps per oscillation, so the size after one oscillation falls by about \(\pi(\gamma - \tfrac12)\,\Omega\). This loss is proportional to \(\Omega\), that is to the step \(\Delta t\) in the first power, and is therefore of first order. At the trapezoidal rule (\(\gamma = \tfrac12\)) the loss is zero, and the second order stays in the phase (theorem 10.67).

Example 17.42 How much the amplitude is reduced (Koliko se zmanjša obseg)

The numerical damping ratio by equation (17.38) is \(0{,}01533\). The same mode with the same step loses nothing at the trapezoidal rule. The vertical line at \(2\pi/20\) is the case of the example above, where the curves cut the line in the dots at \(0{,}995\) and \(0{,}990\).

Why damp at all? The highest modes of a mesh have frequencies that are poorly determined: the order of the error from corollary 10.38 holds at fixed \(r\) and falling \(h\), not for the highest modes of the mesh. These modes are often not part of what we care about, and boundary conditions or a sudden load can excite them. COOK gives this as the reason for numerical damping: to damp high modes that are "of little interest or constitute only undesirable nonphysical oscillations associated with discretization" (COOK PDF p. 434). Adding viscous damping raises \(\zeta\), but according to Hughes, whom COOK cites, with the trapezoidal rule it may damp mostly the middle modes, while the highest and the lowest stay almost untouched (COOK PDF p. 434). Numerical damping with the curves of figure 17.24 is therefore the better choice: it damps the more the higher the frequency.

The figure also shows the price. At \(\gamma = 0{,}7\) a mode with \(20\) steps per oscillation is reduced to \(82{,}5\) % in one oscillation, which is about a damping ratio of \(3\) %, and this is not in the model. Whoever compares the measured damping of a structure with the response of a computation with \(\gamma > \tfrac12\) compares the measured value with only a part of the computed damping. For this reason \(\gamma\) is kept near \(\tfrac12\), and at \(\gamma = 0{,}6\) the damping is \(1{,}5\) %. COOK also names the \(\alpha\)-method (Hilber, Hughes and Taylor), which keeps the damping in high modes and reduces it in low ones, without losing the second order (COOK PDF p. 433 to 434). That scheme is only named here. The opposite direction is more dangerous still: at \(\gamma = 0{,}4\) (\(\beta = 0{,}2025\)) we have \(\rho > 1\) by theorem 17.39, point 3, and a mode with \(\Omega = 2\pi/20\) grows after \(20\) steps to \(1{,}1011\) of its size, and at \(\Omega = 10\) by a factor \(1{,}2127\) per step (num-08.py, part S5). A scheme with \(\gamma < \tfrac12\) is therefore not inaccurate but wrong.

17.8.3 The nonlinear step (Nelinearni korak)

So far the force was linear, \(\mat K\vek q\). A nonlinear system has an internal force \(\vek f(\vek q)\) that is no longer linear, and the equation of motion \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \vek f(\vek q) = \vek Q(t)\) (a conservative system without damping). For \(\vek f = \mat K\vek q\) we get the previous equation. The Newmark equations (17.33) and (17.34) stay, because they speak only of displacement, velocity and acceleration. At \(\beta > 0\) we express from the first the acceleration at the end of the step through the unknown \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\),

\[\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \frac{\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} - \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} - \Delta t^2(\frac12 - \beta)\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}}{\beta\,\Delta t^2} , \tag{17.40}\]

and insert it in the equation of motion at the end of the step. An equation for one unknown remains, \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\),

\[\vek G(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = \mat M\,\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1}(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}) + \vek f(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - \vek Q_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek 0 . \tag{17.41}\]

For a linear \(\vek f = \mat K\vek q\) this is a linear equation with the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}} = \mat K + \mat M/(\beta\Delta t^2)\), and at \(\beta = \tfrac14\) and \(\mat C = \mat 0\) it is exactly equation (10.77). In the nonlinear case we solve it with Newton's method from section 17.7 (definition 17.32): from an approximation \(\vek q^{(i)}\) we get the next one with

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}^{(i)}\,\Delta\vek q = -\vek G\big(\vek q^{(i)}\big) , \qquad \mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}^{(i)} = \mat K_{\mathrm{t}}\big(\vek q^{(i)}\big) + \frac{\mat M}{\beta\,\Delta t^2} , \tag{17.42}\]

where \(\mat K_{\mathrm{t}}\) is the tangent stiffness (definition 17.31) and \(\Delta\vek q\) the correction of the approximation. We start with the approximation from the start of the step, \(\vek q^{(0)} = \vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,\vek v_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t^2(\tfrac12 - \beta)\vek a_{\mathrm{k}}\), that is with the displacement that equation (17.33) would give at \(\vek a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek 0\). A warning about the cost: the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ef}}^{(i)}\) changes with \(\vek q^{(i)}\), so the factorization of the matrix (lemma 7.47), which we did once for all steps in the linear system (chapter 10), is repeated here at every iteration of every step. This is the cost of nonlinearity (section 17.6 treats large systems). We stop the iterations when the correction is smaller than \(\delta\) (section 17.7), and this stopping is the numerical error of definition 17.2.

Does equation (17.41) have a solution at all, and one only? We show this on the pendulum, where the unknown is one angle.

Lemma 17.43 The step equation for the pendulum (Enačba koraka za nihalo)

Let the pendulum (\(\ddot\theta = -\sin\theta\), time in units of \(\sqrt{l/g}\)) make a step \(\Delta t\) with the trapezoidal rule (\(\gamma = \tfrac12\), \(\beta = \tfrac14\)) from the state \((\theta_{\mathrm{k}}, v_{\mathrm{k}}, a_{\mathrm{k}})\). The equation for \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is \(g(\theta) = \theta - c + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\sin\theta = 0\) with \(c = \theta_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}a_{\mathrm{k}}\). At \(\Delta t < 2\) it has exactly one solution. At \(\Delta t > 2\) there may be more solutions.

Proof

We show that at \(\Delta t < 2\) the function \(g\) is strictly increasing and unbounded in both directions, and that this does not hold for a larger step.

  1. From equation (17.40) at \(\beta = \tfrac14\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = -\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) it follows that \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \theta_{\mathrm{k}} + \Delta t\,v_{\mathrm{k}} + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\big(a_{\mathrm{k}} - \sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\big)\), which is \(g(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = 0\).
  2. The derivative is \(g'(\theta) = 1 + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\cos\theta \ge 1 - \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\). At \(\Delta t < 2\) this is a positive number, so \(g\) is strictly increasing (mean value theorem).
  3. Since \(|\sin\theta| \le 1\), we have \(g(\theta) \ge \theta - c - \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\) and \(g(\theta) \le \theta - c + \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{4}\). So \(g < 0\) at small enough and \(g > 0\) at large enough \(\theta\). A continuous, strictly increasing function that changes sign has by the intermediate value theorem exactly one zero.
  4. At \(\Delta t > 2\) we have \(g'(\theta) < 0\) where \(\cos\theta < -4/\Delta t^2\), so \(g\) is not monotonic and may have several zeros. An example is in the example below.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

An example with numbers. The pendulum from the position \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}} = \pi/2\) with \(v_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} = -1\) has \(c = \pi/2 - \Delta t^2/4\). Figure 17.25 shows the function \(g\). We draw the axis \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and the axis \(g\), where a zero means a solution. At \(\Delta t = 2\pi/20 = 0{,}31416\) we have \(g' \ge 0{,}9753\), the blue curve is almost a straight line and has one zero at \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1} = 1{,}5215\). At \(\Delta t = 4\) it is no longer monotonic: the orange curve has three zeros at \(-5{,}4335\), \(-3{,}3821\) and \(-0{,}5026\) (num-08.py, part S5). The step \(\Delta t = 4\) is useless for computing the oscillation, since the period at small deflections is \(2\pi \approx 6{,}28\), fewer than two steps per oscillation, but it shows that unconditional stability in the linear case is not a property of the scheme on the nonlinear equation: there the step is not even uniquely determined. What matters most to us is that at this step Newton's method finds one of the three zeros, depending on the initial approximation, and not necessarily the one we want. Similarly, in section 17.7 at a load above the limit force it found an equilibrium that is not stable.

17.8.4 The pendulum (Nihalo)

The model is a point mass on a rigid weightless rod of length \(l\) in the gravity field \(g\) with the angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, without friction in the axis and without air drag. The equation of motion is \(\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\sin\theta\) (theorem 8.123). For time we take the unit \(\sqrt{l/g}\), and the equation is \(\theta'' + \sin\theta = 0\) (the prime is the derivative with respect to dimensionless time). This is equation (17.41) with \(M = 1\), \(f(\theta) = \sin\theta\), \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = \cos\theta\) and \(Q = 0\). We start from \(\theta_0 = \pi/2\), \(v_0 = 0\) and use the trapezoidal rule with Newton at every step. By theorem 8.123 the exact period at amplitude \(90°\) is \(T = 4\int_0^{\pi/2}\big(1 - \tfrac12\sin^2u\big)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}u = 7{,}41630\) (NumPy, arithmetic-geometric mean), at small amplitude \(2\pi = 6{,}28319\). At \(l = 1\) m and \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\) the unit of time is \(0{,}3193\) s, and \(T = 2{,}368\) s.

Figure 17.26 shows three oscillations. We draw the time axis and the axis of deflection \(\theta\) with the limits \(\pm\pi/2\). The grey curve is the exact solution, computed with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method with the step \(3T/20000 \approx 0{,}0011\), about \(565\) times smaller than the step of the scheme (fig-08.py), so the difference is zero to the accuracy of drawing. The blue dots are the values of the trapezoidal rule with step \(2\pi/10\), which is deliberately coarse here so that the error is visible. The dots lag behind the curve: the scheme slows the pendulum down. The cause is already known from theorem 10.67: the trapezoidal rule turns the phase by \(2\arctan(\Omega/2)\) instead of \(\Omega\), and the period in the scheme is therefore longer. The pendulum at amplitude \(90°\) has a frequency lower than \(1\), namely \(2\pi/T = 0{,}84721\). With it \(x = 0{,}84721\cdot 2\pi/20/2 = 0{,}13308\) and \(x/\arctan x - 1 = 0{,}588\) %, which is close to the measured \(0{,}57\) % at step \(2\pi/20\). Why is shown by a computation that repeats the proof of theorem 10.66 and stops where the force is no longer linear. We do it for a general force \(f\) and one degree of freedom.

Theorem 17.44 Energy of the trapezoidal rule for a nonlinear force (Energija trapeznega pravila pri nelinearni sili)

Let \(f\) be a twice continuously differentiable function, \(V\) its antiderivative (\(V' = f\)), \(\ddot q + f(q) = 0\) the equation of motion of unit mass, and let the trapezoidal rule (definition 10.63) with step \(\Delta t\) give the sequence \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{k}} = -f(q_{\mathrm{k}})\). With \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12v_{\mathrm{k}}^2 + V(q_{\mathrm{k}})\) and \(h_{\mathrm{k}} = q_{\mathrm{k}+1} - q_{\mathrm{k}}\) we have

\[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} = \int_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{k}}^{q_{\mathrm{k}+1}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s - \frac{h_{\mathrm{k}}}{2}\big(f(q_{\mathrm{k}}) + f(q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\big) , \tag{17.43}\]

and this difference is in absolute value at most \(\dfrac{\lvert h_{\mathrm{k}}\rvert^3}{12}\max\lvert f''\rvert\), where the maximum is over the interval between \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\). For a linear \(f\) the difference is zero.

Proof

We show that the increment of energy is the error of the trapezoidal formula for the integral of the force.

  1. Kinetic part: \(\tfrac12(v_{\mathrm{k}+1}^2 - v_{\mathrm{k}}^2) = \tfrac12(v_{\mathrm{k}+1} + v_{\mathrm{k}})(v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - v_{\mathrm{k}})\). By equation (10.75) we have \(v_{\mathrm{k}+1} - v_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(a_{\mathrm{k}} + a_{\mathrm{k}+1})\), and by equation (10.74) \(\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(v_{\mathrm{k}} + v_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = h_{\mathrm{k}}\). So the kinetic part is \(\tfrac12h_{\mathrm{k}}(a_{\mathrm{k}} + a_{\mathrm{k}+1}) = -\tfrac{h_{\mathrm{k}}}{2}\big(f(q_{\mathrm{k}}) + f(q_{\mathrm{k}+1})\big)\).
  2. Potential part: \(V(q_{\mathrm{k}+1}) - V(q_{\mathrm{k}}) = \int_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{k}}^{q_{\mathrm{k}+1}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) by the fundamental theorem of calculus. The sum of steps 1 and 2 is equation (17.43).
  3. Let \(a < b\) be the end points of the interval. Integration by parts twice gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b(s - a)(b - s)f''(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = (b - a)\big(f(a) + f(b)\big) - 2\int_{\mathrm{a}}^bf(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\): the first boundary term \((s - a)(b - s)f'\) is zero at the end points, and the second part gives \(-\big[(a + b - 2s)f\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - 2\int_{\mathrm{a}}^bf\), which is \((b - a)(f(a) + f(b)) - 2\int_{\mathrm{a}}^bf\).
  4. Hence \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^bf - \tfrac{b - a}{2}(f(a) + f(b)) = -\tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b(s - a)(b - s)f''(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). The kernel \((s - a)(b - s)\) is non-negative with integral \((b - a)^3/6\), so the absolute value of the right side is at most \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{(b - a)^3}{6}\max|f''| = \tfrac{(b - a)^3}{12}\max|f''|\). At \(h_{\mathrm{k}} < 0\) we swap the meaning of the end points and the expression for the difference gets the opposite sign, the absolute value stays. For a linear \(f\) we have \(f'' = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. For a linear force it condenses theorem 10.66. The error of the trapezoidal formula is mentioned in COOK (PDF p. 619) only as second order (parameter \(0{,}5\) in the generalized trapezoidal formula (17.3-8)).

For the pendulum \(f = \sin\), \(|f''| \le 1\) and every step changes the energy by at most \(|h_{\mathrm{k}}|^3/12\). This is an upper bound for a single step. The sum of these bounds over three oscillations is \(0{,}708\), \(0{,}192\), \(0{,}049\) and \(0{,}0123\) at the four steps of the table, so it indeed falls four times with every halved step, because \(|h_{\mathrm{k}}| \approx \Delta t\,|v_{\mathrm{k}}|\) and \(|h_{\mathrm{k}}|^3\approx\Delta t^3|v_{\mathrm{k}}|^3\) at \(\sim\!1/\Delta t\) steps. But the bound is too loose, since the actual deviation is \(8{,}06\cdot 10^{-3}\) and not \(0{,}192\). The signs cancel, as figure 17.27 shows. We draw the time axis and the axis of the difference \(\mathcal{E} - \mathcal{E}_0\). At step \(2\pi/20\) (blue) the difference is everywhere negative or near zero, reaches \(-0{,}0081\) at the passage through the lowest position and returns to zero at every turning point: the energy therefore does not drift away but oscillates around the true value at the bottom. The steps \(2\pi/40\) (orange) and \(2\pi/80\) (grey) give the same shape four and sixteen times smaller.

Why does it return to zero? By theorem 17.44 the increment is \(-\tfrac{h_{\mathrm{k}}^3}{12}f''(\xi_{\mathrm{k}})\) with \(f'' = -\sin\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{k}}\) between \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), so approximately \(+\tfrac{h_{\mathrm{k}}^3}{12}\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\) (this is an estimate, not a theorem: we replace \(\xi_{\mathrm{k}}\) by \(\theta_{\mathrm{k}}\)). On the way from \(\theta = \pi/2\) towards \(0\) we have \(h_{\mathrm{k}} < 0\) and \(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\), so the increment is negative. On the way from \(0\) towards \(-\pi/2\) we have \(h_{\mathrm{k}} < 0\) and \(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}} < 0\), so the increment is positive. We add the first part as a Riemann sum with step \(\Delta t\) and \(h_{\mathrm{k}} \approx \Delta t\,v_{\mathrm{k}}\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} h_{\mathrm{k}}^3\sin\theta_{\mathrm{k}}/12 \approx \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{12}\int_0^{T/4}v^3\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}t\). For the exact solution, conservation of energy gives \(v^2 = 2\cos\theta\), and with the substitution \(\theta = \theta(t)\), for which \(\theta'(t) = v(t)\), the integral is \(\int_{\pi/2}^{0}2\cos\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = -1\) (NumPy, \(40000\) steps: \(-1{,}00000\)). The deviation at the bottom is therefore about \(-\Delta t^2/12 = -0{,}0833\,\Delta t^2\), and the measured values are \(0{,}0817\), \(0{,}0829\) and \(0{,}0832\) times \(\Delta t^2\). The second part of the oscillation brings the same value with the opposite sign, so the energy returns at the turning points.

Such arguments are estimates and not proofs, and the book claims no more than the computation shows: we proved theorem 17.44 (the energy error per step), the quadratic order of the total error we estimated and checked at four steps. For the pendulum \(\max|\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{k}} - \mathcal{E}_0|\) is small, but the pendulum is a single mode. In a system with many modes the energy error of every mode is different, and nonlinearity couples them, which this section does not treat.

Example 17.45 An explicit step and a growing stiffness (Eksplicitni korak in rastoča togost)

The unconditional stability of the trapezoidal rule is a property of the linear mode (theorem 17.39). Explicit central differences have the critical step \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2/\omega_{\max}\) (theorem 10.70), where \(\omega_{\max}\) is the highest frequency of the system. For a nonlinear force the leading stiffness \(K_{\mathrm{t}}\) depends on the displacement, so \(\omega_{\max}\) depends on it too. Take the hardening spring \(\ddot q + q + 100\,q^3 = 0\) with \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 1 + 300\,q^2\). At \(q = 0\) we have \(\omega_{\max} = 1\) and \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\). At \(q = 1\) we have \(\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{301} = 17{,}35\) and \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}} = 0{,}1153\). We start from \(q_0 = 0\) with velocity \(v_0 = 7{,}14\), which has an amplitude around \(1\), and make \(400\) steps of central differences (num-08.py, part S4). At \(\Delta t = 0{,}02\), \(0{,}05\) and \(0{,}08\) the solution stays bounded with the largest deflection \(1{,}0016\), \(1{,}0120\) and \(1{,}0397\). At \(\Delta t = 0{,}1\) and \(0{,}13\) it exceeds \(10^6\) after \(17\) and \(23\) steps. The step \(0{,}1\) is below the critical step at amplitude \(1\), yet the solution escapes: the critical step holds for a given \(\omega_{\max}\), but the deflection in an explicit scheme grows because of inaccuracy to a value where \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{kr}}\) is smaller than the step. This is not a proof for every system, but it is an example of why explicit schemes for nonlinear systems need a reserve below the critical step computed with the largest expected deflection.

Figure 17.25 connected the step, the equation and Newton, and figure 17.27 the price we pay for nonlinearity. The section showed three things. In a linear mode the family \(\beta = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4\) keeps stability for every \(\gamma \ge \tfrac12\) (theorem 17.39), and the damping of high modes costs one order of accuracy, since the guaranteed accuracy falls from second to first order (corollary 17.41). In the nonlinear step we solve the equation for the unknown displacement with Newton's method, and for the pendulum the step is certainly unique at \(\Delta t < 2\) (lemma 17.43). The trapezoidal rule does not conserve energy for a nonlinear force, and the error per step is given by theorem 17.44. Three proofs (stability of the family, uniqueness of the step and the energy error) we made ourselves, three statements we gave without proof: unconditional stability in the whole region \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\), conditional stability of the linear acceleration scheme and the second order of the \(\alpha\)-method (COOK PDF p. 430 to 434).
