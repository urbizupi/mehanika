Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.9 Mass matrices of beams and frames (masna matrika nosilca in okvirja)

Chapter 10 estimated the mass of the columns of frame A by dividing all of it between the storeys, \(12{,}48\) kg to each, and obtained a decrease of the frequencies by \(1{,}225\) %. It added that this is only an estimate, because the mass of a column is spread along it, and that the corresponding matrices are in chapter 12. What does the mass of a beam, spread along its length, contribute, and how good are the frequencies we get for a cantilever of one, two or sixteen elements?

The path is the same as for the bar (section 10.7). The kinetic energy of an element is a quadratic form of the velocities of the nodes, its matrix is the element mass matrix, and the matrices of the elements are assembled like the stiffness ones. What is new is the field on the element, which has a displacement and a rotation at a node, and the question of what inertia belongs to the rotation.

The model is this. Every point of the axis of the beam moves only transversely with the velocity \(\dot v\), and the kinetic energy of a beam with a straight axis is \(T = \tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot v^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (definition 8.79), with constant density \(\rho\) and cross-section area \(A\). The rotation of the cross-sections about their centroid has a further energy \(\tfrac12\int\rho I\,\dot\varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (rotary inertia, vztrajnost vrtenja), and we leave this term out. Why we may is told by the ratio of the two energies, \(I\dot\varphi^2/(A\dot v^2)\). For our beam with a cross-section of height \(d = 40\) mm we have \(I/A = d^2/12 = 133{,}3\) mm². The rotation \(\varphi = v'\) is of the order of the velocity \(\dot v\) divided by the length over which the deflection changes. For an estimate take the static shape of the cantilever, \(v \propto x^2(3L - x)\) (chapter 5), for which \(\int_0^L v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\big/\!\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 56/(11\,L^2) = 5{,}09/L^2\) (SymPy). The ratio of the energies is therefore, at \(L = 1\) m, about \(133{,}3\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 5{,}09 = 6{,}8\cdot 10^{-4}\). This is an estimate for the lowest mode of the cantilever, not a proof. The neglected kinetic energy is therefore about \(0{,}07\) % of the sum, and the frequency would drop by half of that share, by about \(0{,}03\) %. This is much less than the error of one element below. For higher modes the distance over which the deflection changes is shorter and the effect larger, which chapter 13 treats.

12.9.1 Consistent mass matrix of a beam element (konsistentna masna matrika elementa nosilca)

From history

Before 1963 the stiffness matrices of beams were usually derived from differential equations, and the derivation from energy was popularized by Archer and Martin (FELIPPA, PDF p. 196, the years of the cited works on p. 197). The mass of a structure was then usually lumped physically at the points where the coordinates were (CK, PDF pp. 479-480). In 1963 Archer, in a paper on a consistent mass matrix for distributed mass systems, coined the name consistent mass matrix (konsistentna masna matrika) (CK, PDF pp. 479-481). He compared the frequencies of models with lumped and with consistent mass for uniform beams, free at both ends and supported at both ends, with rotations as degrees of freedom and without them. He concluded that the analysis of modes of vibration and of the response of beams with uniform stiffness and mass is significantly improved if the mass matrix is built with equations of the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure instead of by lumping the mass at points (CK, PDF pp. 479-480). What stands behind this is shown by this section: the consistent mass matrix is the kinetic energy of the same field that gives the stiffness matrix, so for the frequencies of nested meshes the Ritz theorem holds (corollary 12.46), and for lumped mass it does not. At the same time Leckie and Lindberg published error estimates for beam frequencies (CK, PDF pp. 480-481). In 1971 Tong, Pian and Bucciarelli established the rates of convergence of frequencies and modes for both models. They showed that lumping the mass is appropriate for systems described by differential equations of second order, while for systems of higher order, such as beams and plates, the consistent mass must be used (CK, PDF pp. 480-481). The reason for the beam is told by the numbers below: the error of the consistent model falls by a factor of \(16\) when the number of elements is doubled, that of the lumped one by a factor of \(4\).

We have seen how the mass of a beam, spread along its length, entered the kinetic energy of an element: through the field of the element or through mass at points. First we write it with the field, for the beam element of section 12.1.2.

The deflection field on the element is \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(x, t) = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}(x)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\) (definition 12.3), except that the displacements and rotations of the nodes \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) are now functions of time, while the Hermite functions are functions of position. The velocity of a point of the axis is therefore \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (\dot v_1, \dot\varphi_1, \dot v_2, \dot\varphi_2)^\T\) is the column of the velocities of the transverse displacements and of the angular velocities of the nodes. Which matrix describes the kinetic energy?

Lemma 12.43 Consistent mass matrix of a beam element (konsistentna masna matrika elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a beam of length \(h\) (definition 12.3) with constant area \(A\) and density \(\rho\), and let its nodal quantities \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\) be functions of time. The kinetic energy of the element \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\rho A\,\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is

\[\begin{aligned} T_{\mathrm{e}} &= \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} , \\ \mat m_{\mathrm{e}} &= \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\rho A\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &= \frac{\rho A h}{420}\begin{pmatrix} 156 & 22h & 54 & -13h \\ 22h & 4h^2 & 13h & -3h^2 \\ 54 & 13h & 156 & -22h \\ -13h & -3h^2 & -22h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix} . \end{aligned} \tag{12.47}\]

The matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive definite. The motion of the element as a whole, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\), has kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\rho A h\), and the rotation of a rigid line about the middle of the element with angular velocity \(1\), \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (-h/2,\ 1,\ h/2,\ 1)^\T\), has \(\tfrac12\cdot\rho A h^3/12\): both are exactly the kinetic energies of a rigid bar with mass \(\rho A h\) and moment of inertia \(\rho A h^3/12\) about the centre.

Proof

We have to show that the kinetic energy is a quadratic form with this matrix, compute its ten different entries, check positive definiteness and compute the energies of the rigid motions.

  1. The Hermite functions are functions of position, so \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\). The number \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}\) equals its transpose, so by lemma 1.101 \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2 = \dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The column \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10) we take it out of the integral and obtain \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\int\rho A\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\). The entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix is \(\rho A\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} H_{\mathrm{i}} H_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and equals the entry \((j, i)\), because the product of functions does not depend on the order. The matrix is symmetric.
  3. With \(s = x - x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\xi = s/h\) the first entry is \(\int_0^h H_1^2\,\mathrm{d}s = h\int_0^1(1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi\). After multiplying out, the square is \(1 - 6\xi^2 + 4\xi^3 + 9\xi^4 - 12\xi^5 + 4\xi^6\), and the integral term by term is \(1 - 2 + 1 + \tfrac95 - 2 + \tfrac47 = \tfrac{13}{35}\). So \(\int H_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{13}{35}\,h = \tfrac{156}{420}\,h\).
  4. The remaining nine different integrals were computed by SymPy (the same procedure, integrals of polynomials): \(\int H_1H_2 = \tfrac{11}{210}h^2\), \(\int H_1H_3 = \tfrac{9}{70}h\), \(\int H_1H_4 = -\tfrac{13}{420}h^2\), \(\int H_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{105}h^3\), \(\int H_2H_3 = \tfrac{13}{420}h^2\), \(\int H_2H_4 = -\tfrac{1}{140}h^3\), \(\int H_3^2 = \tfrac{13}{35}h\), \(\int H_3H_4 = -\tfrac{11}{210}h^2\), \(\int H_4^2 = \tfrac{1}{105}h^3\). With the fraction \(\tfrac{1}{420}\) in front of the matrix this is \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) of equation (12.47).
  5. Positive definiteness: by step 2 we have \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \int\rho A\,\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\). If it is zero, the integrand, a continuous nonnegative function, is zero everywhere (lemma 6.10, point 3), so the polynomial \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is zero. Its value and slope at the ends are by lemma 12.2 (point 1) the components of \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\).
  6. For \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\) we have by lemma 12.2 (point 2) with \(a = 1\), \(b = 0\) the velocity \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = H_1 + H_3 = 1\) everywhere, so \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\rho A h\). This is also \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{420}(156 + 54 + 54 + 156)\), as it must be by step 1.
  7. For \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (-h/2,\ 1,\ h/2,\ 1)^\T\) it is by the same lemma with \(a = -h/2\), \(b = 1\) the line \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = s - h/2\). Its kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\rho A\int_0^h(s - h/2)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\rho A\,\big[(s - h/2)^3/3\big]_0^h = \tfrac12\cdot\rho A h^3/12\).

Source: COOK (11.3-5) (PDF p. 393), CK (14.15) (PDF p. 441), GR (5.120) (PDF p. 397), where GR says that the quadratic forms for the displacement and for the rotation about the centre equal the translational and rotational inertia \(m\ell\) and \(m\ell^3/12\). The proof in steps is not from the sources, it follows lemma 10.31.

The proof showed that the consistent matrix really is the kinetic energy of the field of the element, and that it keeps the field of rigid motion, because the Hermite functions represent a line exactly (lemma 12.2). The matrix is, as for the bar, full: the acceleration of one node also drags the mass at the other. The entry at the rotation, \(m_{22} = \rho A h^3/105\), has the unit kg m², a moment of inertia. The rotation \(\varphi\) is a number without a unit, and the field \(\varphi H_2\) has a height proportional to \(h\), so the mass moved by the rotation of the node must be multiplied by the length squared.

We test it on the cantilever of chapter 5: steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², length \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\). As the unit of frequency we take \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)} = 57{,}883\) 1/s. For a cantilever of one element only node \(2\) is free, with the unknowns \((v_2, \varphi_2)\), the stiffness matrix (lemma 12.4) is \(\frac{EI}{h^3}\begin{pmatrix}12 & -6h\\ -6h & 4h^2\end{pmatrix}\) and the mass matrix is the corresponding block of the matrix (12.47), \(\frac{\rho A h}{420}\begin{pmatrix}156 & -22h\\ -22h & 4h^2\end{pmatrix}\). The eigenproblem (definition 10.7) has with \(h = L\) and \(\mu = \omega^2\rho A h^4/(420\,EI)\) the determinant

\[\begin{aligned} (12 - 156\mu)(4 - 4\mu) - (-6 + 22\mu)^2 &= 12 - 408\mu + 140\mu^2 = 0 \\ &\Longleftrightarrow\ 35\mu^2 - 102\mu + 3 = 0 . \end{aligned}\]

Its solutions are \(\mu = (51 \mp 8\sqrt{39})/35 = 0{,}029715\) and \(2{,}88457\), so \(\omega^2 = 420\mu\,\omega_0^2 = 12{,}4802\,\omega_0^2\) and \(1211{,}5\,\omega_0^2\), that is \(\omega_1 = 3{,}5327\,\omega_0\) and \(\omega_2 = 34{,}807\,\omega_0\). The true values are \(3{,}51602\,\omega_0\) and \(22{,}0345\,\omega_0\) (CK, Table 14.1, PDF p. 476, the unit is defined on p. 473 (Eq. 4 of Example 14.9), the derivation of the values is in chapter 13, here we take them as a yardstick). The lowest frequency is therefore \(0{,}475\) % too high, and the second \(58\) %. One element is already good for the lowest mode, not for the second.

12.9.2 Lumped mass of a beam and nodes without inertia (zbrana masa nosilca in vozlišča brez vztrajnosti)

The lumped mass of a bar (definition 10.32) divided the mass of the element in halves at the ends. In a beam the node also has a rotation. A point mass does not rotate, so a point mass at a node brings no inertia to the rotation, and the inertia of rotation we have already neglected in the model. The lumped model therefore gives no mass at the rotation.

Definition 12.44 Lumped mass of a beam element (zbrana masa elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an element of a beam with length \(h\), area \(A\) and density \(\rho\) (definition 12.3). The lumped mass matrix (zbrana masna matrika) of a beam element is the diagonal matrix of order \(4\)

\[\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{\rho A h}{2}\,\mathrm{diag}(1,\ 0,\ 1,\ 0) , \tag{12.48}\]

that is, a point mass \(\rho A h/2\) at each end for the transverse displacement and no inertia for the rotation. The total mass is \(\tfrac12\rho A h + \tfrac12\rho A h = \rho A h\).

Source: COOK (11.3-3) (PDF p. 392), the left form "beam without rotary inertia", \([\mathbf m] = m\lceil 1/2\ \ 0\ \ 1/2\ \ 0\rfloor\), and the text before it, saying that the inertia of the rotation must be added separately. CK (14.22) (PDF p. 448, figure 14.8 on p. 447): the lumped mass matrix of a beam supported at both ends of four equal elements is \(\tfrac{\rho A L}{4}\mat I\) for three displacements, without rotations.

The lumped mass is not the kinetic energy of the field of lemma 12.43, but of two point masses. Figure 12.27 shows the simplest motion: node 1 moves with the velocity \(\dot v_1 = 1\), all other data are zero. The consistent model gives the velocity field \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = H_1\), cubic, falling from \(1\) to \(0\) with zero slope at both ends, and \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12 m_{11} = \tfrac{156}{840}\rho A h = \tfrac{13}{70}\rho A h = 0{,}186\,\rho A h\). The lumped model gives \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\cdot 1^2 = 0{,}25\,\rho A h\), so \(35\) % more.

For rotation the difference is still larger, threefold. For rotation about the middle, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (-h/2, 1, h/2, 1)^\T\), the lumped matrix gives \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm z}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = 2\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\cdot\tfrac{h^2}{4} = \rho A h^3/4\), three times the true \(\rho A h^3/12\) of lemma 12.43, because the lumped model computes the rotation as if all the mass were at the ends, where the velocities are largest. For the motion as a whole, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\), it gives the true \(\rho A h\).

For the bar, lemma 10.33 showed that the difference \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm z} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite, so the lumped kinetic energy is never smaller than the consistent one. For the beam there is no such order. Take only the rotation of node \(1\), \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (0, 1, 0, 0)^\T\): the lumped matrix gives \(0\), the consistent one \(\tfrac12 m_{22} = \tfrac{1}{210}\rho A h^3 > 0\), so \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm z} - \mat m_{\mathrm{e}})\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = -\rho A h^3/105 < 0\). For \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 0, 0)^\T\) the difference is \(\rho A h\,(\tfrac12 - \tfrac{156}{420}) > 0\). The quadratic form of the difference therefore has both signs. The eigenvalues of the difference at \(\rho A = h = 1\) are \(-0{,}0921\), \(-0{,}0041\), \(0{,}0754\) and \(0{,}2589\) (NumPy). Lemma 10.33 has no analogue for the beam.

The lumped matrix has zeros on the diagonal, so it is not positive definite and does not belong in the eigenproblem of chapter 10, which requires a positive definite mass matrix (theorem 10.13). What does the equation of motion do at unknowns without mass?

Lemma 12.45 Unknowns without inertia (neznanke brez vztrajnosti)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\) of a supported beam or frame (for example \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) by corollary 12.11), and let the unknowns be split into displacements \(\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) (\(n_{\mathrm{t}}\) unknowns) and rotations \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}\), so that

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix}\mat K_{\mathrm{tt}} & \mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\\ \mat K_{\mathrm{rt}} & \mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \begin{pmatrix}\mat M_{\mathrm{tt}} & \mat 0\\ \mat 0 & \mat 0\end{pmatrix} ,\]

with \(\mat M_{\mathrm{tt}}\) symmetric and positive definite. Then the block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is invertible and the equations of free vibration \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) are equivalent to the pair

\[\begin{aligned} \mat S\,\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{t}} &= \vek 0 , \\ &\qquad \vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\,\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\quad\text{at every time} , \\ &\qquad \mat S = \mat K_{\mathrm{tt}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}} . \end{aligned} \tag{12.49}\]

The matrix \(\mat S\) is symmetric and positive definite, so everything from chapter 10 holds for the pair \((\mat S, \mat M_{\mathrm{tt}})\): the eigenpair (definition 10.7), the spectral theorem (theorem 10.13) and the Rayleigh quotient (theorem 10.26).

Proof

We have to show that the rows of the rotations are not equations of motion but static ones, solve them, and show that the matrix that remains is symmetric and positive definite.

  1. The kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) and the potential \(\tfrac12\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) give by Lagrange's equations (step 6 of the proof of theorem 10.2, which does not use the positive definiteness of \(\mat M\)) \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\). By the rule for the product of matrices by blocks (definition 1.98) these are the equations \(\mat K_{\mathrm{tt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{tt}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{t}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\).
  2. Let \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\) be a column of rotations and \(\vek z = (\vek 0, \vek y)\). By the rule for blocks \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z = \vek y^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek y\), which is positive because \(\mat K\) is positive definite (as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 7.18). The block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is therefore positive definite, and by theorem 6.29 the system \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek y = \vek c\) is uniquely solvable for every \(\vek c\), so \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is invertible.
  3. The second equation of step 1 contains no second derivatives, so by step 2 it is equivalent to \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) at every time. Substituted into the first it gives \((\mat K_{\mathrm{tt}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}})\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{tt}}\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{t}} = \vek 0\). Conversely, if \(\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) solves this equation and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}\) is given by the formula, both equations of step 1 are satisfied. This is the first claim.
  4. Symmetry: \(\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}^\T\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\) is symmetric (the transpose of the inverse is the inverse of the transpose, lemma 1.101), so \(\mat S^\T = \mat S\).
  5. Positivity: let \(\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} \ne \vek 0\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\). Then \(\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = \vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{tt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} + 2\,\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\vek q_{\mathrm{r}} + \vek q_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek q_{\mathrm{r}}\). The last two terms are \(-2\,\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(+\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) (in the second \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1} = \mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\)), together \(-\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}}\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{rt}}\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\). So \(\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}^\T\mat S\,\vek q_{\mathrm{t}} = \vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\), and this is positive because \(\vek q \ne \vek 0\) (its part \(\vek q_{\mathrm{t}}\) is not zero).

Source: COOK, the text after (11.3-3) (PDF p. 392) and p. 393: with lumped masses a beam has only four active degrees of freedom with mass, and higher modes have infinite frequency. CK §14.8.3 (PDF p. 479): with lumped masses the reduced stiffness matrix is again the same as in the elimination of rotations in the Guyan-Irons reduction (equation 14.71, p. 477, the names after COOK §11.6, PDF p. 405). The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma says that unknowns without mass add no dynamics to the vibration but follow the displacements statically. The same formula is used by Guyan reduction (chapter 21) for unknowns that do have mass, and then it is approximate. Here it is exact, because the rotation really has no inertia in the model.

We test it on the cantilever of one element. The free unknowns are \((v_2, \varphi_2)\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{tt}} = 12EI/h^3\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{tr}} = -6EI/h^2\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{rr}} = 4EI/h\), so \(\mat S = \tfrac{EI}{h^3}\big(12 - \tfrac{36h^2}{4h^2}\big) = 3EI/h^3\) (this is the known stiffness \(3EI/L^3\) of a cantilever for a force at the end, chapter 5). The mass \(\mat M_{\mathrm{tt}} = \rho A h/2\) gives \(\omega^2 = \tfrac{3EI}{h^3}\cdot\tfrac{2}{\rho A h} = 6\,\omega_0^2\), that is \(\omega_1 = \sqrt6\,\omega_0 = 2{,}4495\,\omega_0\). Against \(3{,}51602\,\omega_0\) this is \(30{,}3\) % too low, \(f_1 = 22{,}57\) Hz instead of \(32{,}39\) Hz. For lumped masses one element is therefore poor, for consistent ones good.

12.9.3 A beam of several elements (nosilec iz več elementov)

The mass matrices of the elements are assembled like the stiffness ones. The mesh \(0 = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\) has the unknowns \(d_0, \dots, d_{2\mathrm{n}+1}\) (the displacement and the rotation of every node) and the selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) as in theorem 12.9. Let

\[\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat M^{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{z}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{12.50}\]

Steps 1 to 4 of the proof of lemma 10.34 hold with lemma 12.43 in place of lemma 10.31. For the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}(t)\,H_{\mathrm{I}}\) of lemma 12.8 we have \(\int_0^L\rho A\,\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek d}\), the matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric, and positive definite: from \(\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek d} = 0\) it follows that \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) (as in step 4 of that lemma), and by point 2 of lemma 12.8 \(\dot{\vek d}\) is the column of values and slopes of the field \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}\), so zero. The matrix \(\mat M^{\mathrm{z}}\) is diagonal, the contributions of neighbouring elements at a common node add up. For a uniform beam we have at an interior node \(M^{\mathrm z}_{2\mathrm{N},2\mathrm{N}} = \rho A h\) for the displacement and zero for the rotation.

A supported beam has only the free unknowns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\), and by theorem 10.2 and definition 10.7 the vibration and the eigenproblem are the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{ff}})\) for consistent mass and the pair \((\mat S, \mat M^{\mathrm z}_{\mathrm{tt}})\) for lumped, where \(\mat M^{\mathrm z}_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is the block of displacements of the lumped matrix and \(\mat S\) is from lemma 12.45. The block \(\mat M_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite, because its quadratic form is the quadratic form of the matrix \(\mat M\) at zero velocities of the prescribed unknowns.

12.9.5 Nested meshes (gnezdene mreže)

The consistent values fall with \(n\). Is this a rule? If the finer mesh arose by dividing the elements of the coarse one, the field of the coarse mesh is at the same time a field of the fine mesh, as for the bar in corollary 10.43. For the beam the slope must be checked as well: the field of the coarse mesh is continuously differentiable and on an element of the coarse mesh it is one polynomial of degree three, so on every part of the element it is a cubic polynomial with continuous slope, that is, a field of the fine mesh.

Corollary 12.46 Nested meshes of a beam (gnezdene mreže nosilca)

Let a beam with constant \(EI\) and \(\rho A\) be supported so that \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite (corollary 12.11), divided into a coarse mesh of \(n_{\mathrm{H}}\) elements and into a fine mesh of \(n_{\mathrm{h}}\) elements, in which every element of the coarse one is divided into the same number of equal parts. Let \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the matrices of the free unknowns of the coarse and the fine mesh with consistent masses (theorem 12.9 and equation (12.50)), and let \(\mat P\) be the matrix with the rows of the fine free unknowns and the columns of the coarse free unknowns, whose column \(I\) has in the row of the fine node \(j\) the value of the node function \(H_{\mathrm{I}}^H\) of the coarse mesh at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (displacement row) or its slope at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (rotation row). Then \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\), and every consistent eigenvalue of the fine mesh is at most as large as that of the coarse one, \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm f}\), where \(n_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm f}\) is the number of free unknowns of the coarse mesh.

Proof

We have to show that the field of the coarse mesh is represented exactly by the fine mesh, that the quadratic forms of both meshes are then equal, and to apply theorem 10.42 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\).

  1. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) be the column of free unknowns of the coarse mesh (with the prescribed unknowns zero) and \(u_{\mathrm{H}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,H_{\mathrm{I}}^H\) the field of lemma 12.8. It is continuously differentiable and on every element of the coarse mesh a polynomial of degree at most three, so this holds also on every element of the fine mesh that lies in it. By point 2 of the same lemma, applied to the fine mesh, \(u_{\mathrm{H}} = \sum_{\mathrm{J}} (\vek d_{\mathrm{h}})_{\mathrm{J}}\,H_{\mathrm{J}}^h\) with the values and slopes of the field \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) at the fine nodes. These are exactly \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\). The prescribed unknowns of the fine mesh are at the same nodes as those of the coarse one, where \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) has the values zero, so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}\), completed by these zeros, really is the column of the field \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) and satisfies the prescriptions of the fine mesh.
  2. By point 3 of theorem 12.9 \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \tfrac12\int_0^L EI\,(u_{\mathrm{H}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the same field, written in the coarse mesh, gives \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\). The integral is the same, because the field is the same, and does not depend on how many parts we divide the interval into. So \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  3. By equation (12.50) (with \(\vek d\) in place of \(\dot{\vek d}\)) \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \int_0^L\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{H}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  4. A symmetric matrix \(\mat S\) with \(\vek d^\T\mat S\,\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek d\) is zero: at \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) we have \(S_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\), and at \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(2S_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\). The differences \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) are symmetric, so by steps 2 and 3 both equalities hold.
  5. The columns of \(\mat P\) are independent. Let \(\mat P\,\vek a = \vek 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{H}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} a_{\mathrm{I}} H_{\mathrm{I}}^H\) has zero values and slopes at the fine nodes, among them at all the coarse ones. By point 2 of lemma 12.8 for the coarse mesh, \(a_{\mathrm{I}}\) is the value or the slope of the field \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) at the coarse node, so \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
  6. The matrix \(\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\) is symmetric and positive definite (section 12.9.3), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\) is symmetric. By theorem 10.42 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\) and \(m = n_{\mathrm{H}}^{\mathrm f}\) the Ritz problem is by step 4 exactly \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a\), so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Source: COOK (PDF p. 396): "if ... elements are compatible ... and mass matrices are consistent, then computed natural frequencies are upper bounds to exact frequencies", GR (PDF p. 402), Table 5.6 and the text under it: the eigenvalues for a beam with two clamped ends are upper bounds of the true ones, "a property resulting from the conformity of the displacement field". The proof in steps is not from the sources, it follows steps 1 to 6 of corollary 10.43.

The proof showed the same as for the bar: conformity of the fields is already the Ritz method, because the coarse mesh is a subspace of the fine one. A test: a cantilever with \(4\) elements and with \(16\) elements, \(\mat P\) has \(32\) rows and \(8\) columns. NumPy gives for the differences \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{16}\mat P - \mat K_4\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{16}\mat P - \mat M_4\) at most \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-8}\) for entries of the stiffness matrix of size \(3\cdot 10^{7}\) and \(3\cdot 10^{-17}\) for the mass one, where the entries are up to \(1{,}2\): zero up to rounding. The first four eigenvalues, in the unit \(\omega_0\) for frequencies:

Every value of the four-element model lies above the sixteen-element one, and that above the true one, as corollary 12.46 and COOK require. 12.9.6 Mass matrix of a frame element (masna matrika elementa okvirja)

A frame element (definition 12.17) has an axial displacement with a linear field and a transverse one with a cubic field. What is its mass matrix, and must we also transform it at a rotation of the axis of the element, like the stiffness one?

Lemma 12.47 Mass matrix of a frame element (masna matrika elementa okvirja)

Let a frame element of length \(h\), angle \(\alpha\) (definition 12.17) and the matrix \(\mat R = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Q, 1, \mat Q, 1)\) of lemma 12.18 be given, with constant area \(A\) and density \(\rho\), and let the nodal quantities be functions of time. The kinetic energy of the element is in local axes \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with a \(6 \times 6\) matrix that has in the rows and columns \(1\) and \(4\) (the axial displacements \(\bar u_1\), \(\bar u_2\)) the block \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\) of lemma 10.31, in the rows and columns \(2, 3, 5, 6\) (\(\bar v_1, \varphi_1, \bar v_2, \varphi_2\)) the matrix of equation (12.47), and the other entries are zero. In global axes \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R\). Both matrices are symmetric and positive definite. For \(\alpha \ne 0\) the matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is not equal to \(\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\): its entry at the axial displacement of node \(1\) in the direction of the global axis \(x\) is

\[(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}})_{11} = \rho A h\left(\frac{\cos^2\alpha}{3} + \frac{13\sin^2\alpha}{35}\right) , \tag{12.51}\]

and \(\tfrac13 \ne \tfrac{13}{35}\).

Proof

We have to show that the kinetic energy splits into an axial and a transverse part, that each part gives the known block, that the passage to global axes gives \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\), and that the first entry depends on the angle.

  1. The velocity of a point of the axis has in local axes the components \(\dot{\bar u}_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(\dot{\bar v}_{\mathrm{h}}\), which are perpendicular components of the same vector, so the square of its length is \(\dot{\bar u}_{\mathrm{h}}^2 + \dot{\bar v}_{\mathrm{h}}^2\) (\(\bar{\vek a}^\T\bar{\vek a} = \vek a^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek a = \vek a^\T\vek a\) by lemma 2.38 and definition 2.39). So \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot{\bar u}_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}\bar x + \tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot{\bar v}_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}\bar x\).
  2. The first integral is by lemma 10.31 \(\tfrac12\,\tfrac{\rho A h}{6}\,(2\dot{\bar u}_1^2 + 2\dot{\bar u}_1\dot{\bar u}_2 + 2\dot{\bar u}_2^2)\), because \(\bar u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a linear field with the nodal values \(\bar u_1\), \(\bar u_2\). The second is by lemma 12.43 \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{b}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{b}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{b}}\) with \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{b}} = (\dot{\bar v}_1, \dot\varphi_1, \dot{\bar v}_2, \dot\varphi_2)^\T\) and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{b}}\) the matrix of equation (12.47).
  3. The first term contains only \(\dot{\bar u}_1\), \(\dot{\bar u}_2\), the second only the remaining four velocities, so the sum has no mixed terms and is a quadratic form with the matrix \(\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the claim. Both matrices are symmetric and positive definite (lemmas 10.31 and 12.43), so \(\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\) is too: its quadratic form is the sum of both, each is positive if its part of the velocities has some nonzero component.
  4. By lemma 12.18 \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat R\) does not depend on time, so \(\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat R\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) and by lemma 1.101 \(T_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\). The matrix \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\) is symmetric. It is positive definite, because \(\mat R\) is invertible (\(\mat R^\T\mat R = \mat I\), definition 2.39) and \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\dot{\bar{\vek d}}_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) for \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} \ne \vek 0\).
  5. The first column of the matrix \(\mat R\) is \((\cos\alpha,\ -\sin\alpha,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0)^\T\) (the first row of the block \(\mat Q\) is \((\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\), the second \((-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)\) and the first column is the first entry of each). So \((\mat m_{\mathrm{e}})_{11} = \cos^2\alpha\,\bar m_{11} + \sin^2\alpha\,\bar m_{22} + 2\cos\alpha\,(-\sin\alpha)\,\bar m_{12}\), where \(\bar m_{12} = 0\) by step 3, \(\bar m_{11} = \rho A h/3\) by step 2 and \(\bar m_{22} = \tfrac{156}{420}\rho A h = \tfrac{13}{35}\rho A h\) by lemma 12.43. This is equation (12.51), and \(\tfrac13 = \tfrac{35}{105} \ne \tfrac{39}{105} = \tfrac{13}{35}\).

Source: COOK §2.4 (PDF pp. 45-46) for the coordinates of the element, CK §14.3 (PDF pp. 454-455): the mass matrix of an element in global axes is \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof showed why the mass of a frame is not independent of the axes. For a bar in the plane (lemma 10.35) both components have the same linear fields and \(\mat R\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat R^\T = \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\). Here the axial component has a linear field with \(\tfrac13\) at the diagonal entry, the transverse a cubic one with \(\tfrac{13}{35}\), and the rotation of the axis exchanges the mixtures of the components with different weights. At the angle \(\alpha = 0{,}7\) rad we have \((\mat m_{\mathrm{e}})_{11} = 0{,}34914\,\rho A h\), at \(\alpha = 0\) it is \(0{,}33333\,\rho A h\), and at a right angle \(\tfrac{13}{35} = 0{,}37143\). The largest deviation of any entry of \(\mat R^\T\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R\) from \(\bar{\mat m}_{\mathrm{e}}\) at \(0{,}7\) rad is \(0{,}034\) in units where \(\rho A = h = 1\) (NumPy). The lumped matrix (four equal masses \(\rho A h/2\) at the displacements and zero at the rotations) has no such dependence: \(\mat R^\T\,\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0)\,\mat R\) is the same matrix as before the rotation, because the masses are equal in both components and \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\).

We assemble the frame from elements as for stiffness (theorem 12.20): \(\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the global matrices of the elements and lemma 12.45 for the unknowns of rotations without mass in the lumped model.

12.9.7 Frame A and an L-frame (okvir A in L-okvir)

Two questions have remained open. The first is whether the lumped model for a beam has a definite relation to the consistent one. The difference of the matrices is indefinite (section 12.9.2), so lemma 10.33, proved in chapter 10, has no analogue and does not imply that the lumped frequencies are below the consistent ones. That for the cantilever they are below the true ones is a property of this example: CK (PDF p. 479) says that the lumped masses here "converge quite slowly from below". COOK (PDF pp. 393 and 397) says that the lumped matrix usually, but not always, underestimates the frequencies. The second is whether the consistent frequencies are an upper bound of the true ones. Corollary 12.46 proves only that a fine mesh does not raise the frequencies of the coarse one. That every consistent frequency lies above the frequency of the continuous beam, as COOK (PDF pp. 396-397) says under the conditions of compatible fields, an exact representation of the geometry, consistent masses and a stiffness not softened by incomplete integration, we do not prove here, as chapter 10 did not prove it either. We shall show it in chapter 13. The numbers above confirm the bound, they do not prove it.

Now we fulfil the promise of chapter 10 and then test the frame elements on the frame that we know from chapter 5.

Frame A with columns that have mass. Frame A of chapter 10 has two rigid ceilings with mass \(500\) kg and four steel columns of length \(h = 1\) m with a cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, clamped in the ground and in the ceilings, so the nodes of the ceilings do not rotate. The storey stiffness \(k = 2\cdot 12EI/h^3 = 501\,760\) N/m is by lemma 12.4 the stiffness of two columns. Which field does a column have at the displacement of the ceiling \(q_1\)? The node at the ground has \(v = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\), the node at the ceiling has \(v = q_1\) and \(\varphi = 0\), so the field is \(q_1H_3\) with one element per column. This is exactly the S-shape that a column takes in statics (section 12.3.2, theorem 12.13: an element without load and with loads at the nodes has the true field). The field of a column of the second storey is \(q_1H_1 + q_2H_3\). The kinetic energy of the columns of the first storey is \(\tfrac12\dot q_1^2\cdot 2\cdot\rho A\!\int H_3^2 = \tfrac12\dot q_1^2\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac{156}{420}\rho A h\) by lemma 12.43, and of the columns of the second storey \(\tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac{\rho A h}{420}\,(156\,\dot q_1^2 + 2\cdot 54\,\dot q_1\dot q_2 + 156\,\dot q_2^2)\). The rotation has velocity zero, so the terms with rotations do not appear. With \(\rho A h = 6{,}24\) kg the added mass matrix, which contains both contributions, is

\[\mat M_{\mathrm{dod}} = \frac{2\rho A h}{420}\begin{pmatrix}156 + 156 & 54\\ 54 & 156\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}9{,}2709 & 1{,}6046\\ 1{,}6046 & 4{,}6354\end{pmatrix}\ \text{kg} , \tag{12.52}\]

and the mass matrix of the frame is \(\mat M = 500\,\mat I + \mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\), the stiffness one stays \(\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix}2 & -1\\-1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) (figure 12.29). If both ceilings move by \(1\) m, the sum of the entries is \(17{,}12\) kg and not \(24{,}96\) kg, the mass of the four columns: the columns of the first storey are at rest at the ground and contribute only \(\tfrac{156}{420}\) of their mass, that is \(4{,}64\) kg of \(12{,}48\) kg, and the columns of the second storey contribute all of it, \(12{,}48\) kg. The eigenfrequencies are \(\omega = 19{,}4359\) and \(50{,}9249\) 1/s, without the mass of the columns \(19{,}5783\) and \(51{,}2567\) 1/s (chapter 10), so \(-0{,}727\) % and \(-0{,}647\) %. The estimate of chapter 10, \(-1{,}225\) %, was therefore too large. That the estimate is too large at both frequencies follows from corollary 10.30 (point 2). Its hypothesis is satisfied: \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive definite, \(\mat M = 500\,\mat I + \mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\) and \(\mat M' = 512{,}48\,\mat I\) are symmetric and positive definite, and the difference \(\mat M' - \mat M = 12{,}48\,\mat I - \mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\) has the eigenvalues \(2{,}708\) and \(8{,}346\) (NumPy), so it is positive semidefinite. So \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\): no eigenvalue of the model with \(12{,}48\) kg per storey is larger than an eigenvalue of the model with the cubic mass of the columns. The frequencies of the estimate are therefore \(\omega'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), and since \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is below the frequency without the mass of the columns (the same corollary, \(\mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\) is positive semidefinite), the decrease by \(1{,}225\) % is at both frequencies larger than the true one. The upper bound is given by theorem 10.26. Let \(\vek\varphi_1 = (1,\ 1{,}618)^\T\) be the first mode of the frame without the mass of the columns with \(\lambda_1 = \omega_1^2\). The lowest eigenvalue \(\lambda'_1\) of the frame with the mass \(\mat M + \mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\) is at most as large as the Rayleigh quotient of this mass at the shape \(\vek\varphi_1\), and this is \(\lambda_1/(1 + \zeta)\) with \(\zeta = \vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\vek\varphi_1/\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_1 = 26{,}599/1809{,}0 = 0{,}014704\), so \(\omega_1 \le 19{,}5783/\sqrt{1{,}014704} = 19{,}4360\) 1/s, the difference to the true \(19{,}4359\) is only \(2{,}6\cdot 10^{-6}\) relative. The first mode has columns between the ceilings that move less than the ceilings, and so the estimate with \(12{,}48\) kg per ceiling overestimates the mass that the mode moves: \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{dod}}\vek\varphi_1/\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek\varphi_1 = 7{,}35\) kg per unit, not \(12{,}48\) kg.

Is one element per column enough? With \(2\), \(4\) and \(8\) elements for the columns of one storey, with free rotations inside the columns, the lowest frequencies are \(19{,}435881\), \(19{,}435879\) and \(19{,}435879\) 1/s, with one element \(19{,}435900\) 1/s, and the second ones \(50{,}924911\), \(50{,}924771\), \(50{,}924752\) and \(50{,}924751\) 1/s. They fall because the meshes are nested and the model with one element is a Ritz model (the field of a column in it is only the static shape, at the displacement of the ceilings), and each finer one contains this field and others. The static shape differs from the true shape of the column in the mode only because of the inertia of the column itself, and this is small: the mass of the columns is \(24{,}96\) kg, which is \(2{,}5\) % of the mass of the ceilings. The Ritz quotient is stationary at the true shape (theorem 10.27), so the error of the frequency is of the order of the square of the error of the shape, and the difference of one element from the limit value is only \(1{,}1\cdot 10^{-6}\) relative.

This is the same conclusion as in corollary 12.46, since steps 1 to 6 carry over to the frame: the field of the coarse mesh (on each element a linear axial and a cubic transverse field, at the rigid joint \(B\) both members with the same displacement and rotation) is also a field of the fine mesh, and the energy and the kinetic energy of the field do not depend on the mesh (lemma 12.47, theorem 12.20). The other steps stay the same. With one element per member the lowest frequency is already only \(0{,}04\) % above the value at \(16\) elements, the second \(0{,}29\) %, and the third \(20\) %: a higher mode needs more elements, as we saw for the cantilever. Figure 12.30 shows the first two modes, computed from the fields of the elements.

Let us return to the question from the beginning. The mass of a beam, spread along its length, gives with the field of the element the consistent matrix \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{420}(\dots)\), and with mass at the ends the lumped matrix \(\tfrac{\rho A h}{2}\mathrm{diag}(1, 0, 1, 0)\). The consistent one is a Ritz matrix and the nesting of meshes lowers it towards the true one: for the cantilever the error of the lowest frequency for one, two and sixteen elements is \(0{,}475\) %, \(0{,}048\) % and \(10^{-5}\) %. The lumped one is at these numbers \(-30{,}3\) %, \(-10{,}2\) % and \(-0{,}18\) %.
