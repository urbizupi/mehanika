Chapter 22 Shells

22.8 The axisymmetric cylinder element (osnosimetrični element valja)

In the section on bending of the cylinder we worked out the edge of a tube by hand: the equation \(D\,w'''' + (Eh/R^2)\,w = q\) has a closed solution because the radius and the thickness are constant and the load is simple (theorem 22.42, theorem 22.43). A vessel in practice is not like that. The thickness changes at a junction, the shell turns into a cone or a bottom, the pressure changes with height. Programs do not solve such vessels with a closed formula, they divide them into elements, as we divided the beam in chapter 12. This section shows that the element for a cylinder is almost in the book already: it is the beam element of chapter 12 with one more matrix. We then test it on the same tube as before, at the edge where the solution is not smooth, and see how long an element may be so that the moment at the clamp is not wrong. At the end we say what the conical element changes and how the results of an element give the stresses that a program prints.

The tube is the same as in the previous sections: mid-surface radius \(R = 50\) mm, thickness \(h = 2\) mm, steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), internal pressure \(p = 3\) MPa, closed ends. Data: \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2)) = 143\,590\) N mm, \(k = Eh/R^2 = 156{,}80\) N/mm\(^3\), \(\beta = (k/4D)^{1/4} = 0{,}12854\) mm\(^{-1}\), so \(1/\beta = 7{,}780\) mm. Notation: \(h\) is the wall thickness here (as in the whole chapter), so for the element length we write \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) where chapter 12 wrote \(h\). The letter \(K\) is the structure stiffness matrix as in chapter 12, not the Gaussian curvature. The number \(\beta\) is the decay constant of the edge, not \(\beta_{\mathrm{h}}\) of chapter 12, and \(D\) is the bending stiffness of the shell, not the dynamic factor of definition 9.53.

22.8.1 Energy of a cylinder strip (energija traku valja)

First we say what we compute. The body is a thin cylindrical shell (definition 22.1) with mid-surface radius \(R\), constant thickness \(h\) and length \(L\). There are three idealizations. The first is Kirchhoff-Love (definition 22.24): the normal of the middle surface stays straight and normal to the deformed surface, the transverse stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is neglected, displacements are small. The second is that the pressure acts on the middle surface, not on the inner wall. The error is at most \(h/(2R) = 0{,}02\) relative to the radius, as in dropping the factor \(1 + z/R\) in the section on the kinematics of the shell (theorem 22.26). The third is axial symmetry: load, supports and material are the same on every circle around the axis (parallel circle), so the radial displacement \(w\) (positive outward) and the axial displacement \(u\) are functions of \(x\) only, where \(x\) is the distance along the axis. The solution is thus one-dimensional, which is the reason that the element can be a beam.

We count the energy per unit circumference: for the part of the shell over an arc of the middle circle of length 1 mm. The unit is N mm/mm. In this way \(R\) does not enter as a factor and the comparison with the beam is direct. The axial force per unit circumference \(N_0\) is known from equilibrium: in a closed cylinder the pressure on the end plate of area \(\pi R^2\) (the plate counted up to the mid-surface radius) is balanced by the force \(N_0\cdot 2\pi R\) of the wall (Newton's law for the free body of the cut cylinder, lemma 22.36), so \(N_0 = pR/2 = 75\) N/mm.

Lemma 22.51 Energy of a cylinder strip (energija traku valja)

Let the cylinder be as above, loaded axisymmetrically by the internal pressure \(p\) and the axial force \(N_0\) per unit circumference, and let at least one end be free in the axial direction. Let \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\), \(D = Eh^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\), \(k = Eh/R^2\). Then:

  1. The potential energy per unit circumference is
    \[\Pi[u, w] = \int_0^L \Big[ \tfrac12 C\Big(u'^2 + 2\nu\,u'\,\frac{w}{R} + \frac{w^2}{R^2}\Big) + \tfrac12 D\,w''^2 - p\,w - N_0\,u' \Big]\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{22.52}\]
  2. The axial force \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\) equals \(N_0\) everywhere. When \(u\) is eliminated from the energy, what remains, up to a constant, is
    \[\tilde\Pi[w] = \int_0^L \Big[ \tfrac12 D\,w''^2 + \tfrac12 k\,w^2 - q\,w \Big]\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad q = p - \frac{\nu N_0}{R} . \tag{22.53}\]
  3. A field \(w\) for which \(\tilde\Pi\) is stationary satisfies \(D\,w'''' + k\,w = q\) with the natural conditions \(w'' = 0\) and \(w''' = 0\) at an end where \(w\) and \(w'\) are not prescribed. This is the equation of theorem 22.42, derived from the energy.
Proof

We must show three claims: the expression for the energy from the strains, the elimination of \(u\), and stationarity.

  1. Kinematics (theorem 22.26, for a cylinder with axis \(x\) and hoop direction \(\theta\)): the axial strain of the middle surface is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = u'\). A ring of radius \(R\) becomes a ring of radius \(R + w\) after the displacement \(w\), its length \(2\pi R\) becomes \(2\pi(R + w)\), so \(\varepsilon_\theta = w/R\). The normal turns in the plane of the meridian by \(w'\), so the change of curvature in the direction \(x\) is \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = -w''\) (positive when the outer face is stretched, because \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon^0 + z\kappa\) with \(z\) outward). A parallel circle stays a circle, so \(\kappa_\theta = 0\). There are no shear terms because of the axial symmetry.
  2. By theorem 22.28, \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = C(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} + \nu\varepsilon_\theta)\), \(N_\theta = C(\varepsilon_\theta + \nu\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = D(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} + \nu\kappa_\theta) = -D\,w''\). By theorem 22.29 the energy per unit area is \(\tfrac12(N_{\mathrm{x}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} + N_\theta\varepsilon_\theta + M_{\mathrm{x}}\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} + M_\theta\kappa_\theta)\). With \(\kappa_\theta = 0\) this is \(\tfrac12 C(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 2\nu\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}}\varepsilon_\theta + \varepsilon_\theta^2) + \tfrac12 D\,w''^2\).
  3. The pressure \(p\) per unit area does the work \(p\,w\). The force \(N_0\) on the free end does the work \(N_0[u]_0^L = \int_0^L N_0\,u'\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(N_0\) is constant. The potential energy is the strain energy minus the work. Steps 1 and 2 give equation (22.52). This proves point 1.
  4. Variation in \(u\): the integrand depends on \(u\) only through \(u'\), and \(\partial(\cdot)/\partial u' = C(u' + \nu w/R) - N_0 = N_{\mathrm{x}} - N_0\). Stationarity requires \((N_{\mathrm{x}} - N_0)' = 0\), so \(N_{\mathrm{x}} - N_0\) is constant. At the free end \(u\) is not prescribed, so the natural condition there is \(N_{\mathrm{x}} - N_0 = 0\). Hence \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = N_0\) everywhere.
  5. For a given \(\varepsilon_\theta\), \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = N_0\) is the condition of stationarity of the integrand in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}}\). The value of the integrand at \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = N_0/C - \nu\varepsilon_\theta\) is \(\tfrac12 C(1 - \nu^2)\varepsilon_\theta^2 + \nu N_0\varepsilon_\theta - N_0^2/(2C)\) (checked with SymPy). With \(C(1 - \nu^2) = Eh\) and \(\varepsilon_\theta = w/R\) this is \(\tfrac12 k\,w^2 + \nu N_0 w/R\) up to a constant.
  6. Together with \(\tfrac12 D\,w''^2 - p\,w\) we get \(\tfrac12 D\,w''^2 + \tfrac12 k\,w^2 - (p - \nu N_0/R)\,w\). This is equation (22.53). This proves point 2.
  7. For a variation \(\delta w\), \(\delta\tilde\Pi = \int_0^L (D\,w''\,\delta w'' + k\,w\,\delta w - q\,\delta w)\,\mathrm{d}x\). Integration by parts twice (with \([D\,w''\,\delta w' - D\,w'''\,\delta w]_0^L\)) gives \(\int_0^L (D\,w'''' + k\,w - q)\,\delta w\,\mathrm{d}x + [D\,w''\,\delta w' - D\,w'''\,\delta w]_0^L\). Since \(\delta w\) is arbitrary inside, the integrand is zero. At an end where \(\delta w\) and \(\delta w'\) are not prescribed, \(D\,w'' = 0\) and \(D\,w''' = 0\). This proves point 3.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Strains and energy are COOK (16.3-3) and (16.3-5) (PDF p. 586) at meridian angle \(0\) to the axis, where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}\theta} = w/r\). The cylinder equation is from theorem 22.42.

The proof showed that the energy of the cylinder is the energy of a beam with bending stiffness \(D\) on an elastic foundation of stiffness \(k\). The foundation here is the hoop: whoever wants to widen it must stretch it. The axial force is not an unknown, because equilibrium fixes it, and so \(u\) drops out. For the given tube, \(N_0 = 75\) N/mm, \(q = p - \nu N_0/R = 2{,}55\) MPa, and this is \(p(1 - \nu/2)\), as it must be. Without edges the solution is \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = q/k = 16{,}263\) \(\mu\)m (the ring widens uniformly), as in the equation of theorem 22.42.

The lemma has the condition that at least one end is axially free. What if none is? Let both ends be axially fixed. Then \(\int_0^L u'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) and \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\) is constant by step 4, but no longer equal to \(N_0\). In a tube much longer than \(1/\beta\) the edges affect only a small part of the length. If we neglect that part, the axial strain is zero everywhere, so \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = \nu N_\theta = \nu pR = 45\) N/mm instead of 75 N/mm. Then \(q = p(1 - \nu^2) = 2{,}73\) MPa and \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 17{,}411\) \(\mu\)m instead of \(16{,}263\) \(\mu\)m, a difference of 7 %. The lemma is therefore not self-evident: the elimination of \(u\) at known \(N_0\) works because the axial direction is free.

22.8.2 The cylinder element and its stiffness matrix (element valja in njegova togostna matrika)

Since the energy equals that of a beam on a foundation, we use the Hermite functions (definition 12.1) as in chapter 12. First we say what the element is, then we draw, then we compute.

Definition 22.52 Axisymmetric cylinder element (osnosimetrični element valja)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an interval of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = x_{\mathrm{b}} - x_{\mathrm{a}}\) on the axis \(x\) of the cylinder of lemma 22.51. The axisymmetric cylinder element (osnosimetrični element valja) is the ring of width \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) bounded at both ends by a parallel circle, over \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\). A node (vozlišče) is a whole parallel circle. The unknowns at each node are the radial displacement \(w\) and the rotation of the normal \(\varphi = w'\), so four in the column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (w_1, \varphi_1, w_2, \varphi_2)^\T\). The field of the element is \(w_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the functions \(H_1, \dots, H_4\) of equation (12.1). \(R\), \(h\), \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(q\) are known, all constant on the element.

Explanation

Figure 22.26 shows the element on a meridian. The axis of the cylinder is the dash-dotted line. The middle line is at distance \(R\) from the axis, which the distance \(R\) on the left shows. The element is the part of this line bounded by nodes \(1\) and \(2\), each a parallel circle that we see from the side as a point. After loading, the middle line of the element bends into a cubic curve made of Hermite functions, drawn with a strongly enlarged displacement. The displacement of a node is the distance \(w_1\) or \(w_2\) between the position before loading and after it, since it is radial. The slope of the curve at a node is the rotation \(\varphi_1\) or \(\varphi_2\), the tangent to the curve. The length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the distance between the nodes along the axis. On the right is the section by a plane normal to the axis: a circle of radius \(R\) and, after the displacement \(w\), a circle of radius \(R + w\). That is why the hoop strain is \(w/R\) and why the foundation is elastic.

Figure 22.26 shows that a node is not a point but a ring: the displacement \(w_1\) moves the whole parallel circle by the same distance, so the ring widens uniformly and the hoop forces are the same everywhere. Everything else stays as in chapter 12, except that every radial displacement is now also a stretching of the ring.

Lemma 22.53 Stiffness matrix of a cylinder element (togostna matrika elementa valja)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an axisymmetric cylinder element (definition 22.52). Then:

  1. The energy of the element is \(\Pi_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}} + \mat k_{\mathrm{f}})\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the stiffness matrix of the beam element of lemma 12.4 with \(EI\) replaced by \(D\), \(\mat k_{\mathrm{f}}\) is the mass matrix of lemma 12.43 with \(\rho A\) replaced by \(k\), and
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{f}} = \frac{k\,h_{\mathrm{e}}}{420}\begin{pmatrix} 156 & 22h_{\mathrm{e}} & 54 & -13h_{\mathrm{e}} \\ 22h_{\mathrm{e}} & 4h_{\mathrm{e}}^2 & 13h_{\mathrm{e}} & -3h_{\mathrm{e}}^2 \\ 54 & 13h_{\mathrm{e}} & 156 & -22h_{\mathrm{e}} \\ -13h_{\mathrm{e}} & -3h_{\mathrm{e}}^2 & -22h_{\mathrm{e}} & 4h_{\mathrm{e}}^2 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = q\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \\ h_{\mathrm{e}}/12 \\ 1/2 \\ -h_{\mathrm{e}}/12 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{22.54}\]
  2. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{b}} + \mat k_{\mathrm{f}}\) is symmetric and positive definite. Unlike the beam element, the cylinder needs no support against radial displacement as a rigid body.
  3. The uniform widening \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}} = q/k\), that is \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (w_{\mathrm{p}}, 0, w_{\mathrm{p}}, 0)^\T\), satisfies \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\).
Proof

We must show that the energy of the element has this form, that the matrix is positive definite and that the uniform widening is a solution.

  1. By lemma 22.51, equation (22.53), the energy of the element is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}[\tfrac12 D\,w_{\mathrm{h}}''^2 + \tfrac12 k\,w_{\mathrm{h}}^2 - q\,w_{\mathrm{h}}]\,\mathrm{d}x\). With \(w_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{h}}'' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (the numbers equal their transposes, lemma 1.101) this is \(\tfrac12\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\int D\,(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\,\mathrm{d}x + \int k\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \big(\int q\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The first integral is in lemma 12.4 (steps 2 to 5), with \(D\) in place of \(EI\), so \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}} = (D/h_{\mathrm{e}}^3)(\dots)\). The second is in lemma 12.43 (steps 2 to 4), with \(k\) in place of \(\rho A\), and it is \(\mat k_{\mathrm{f}}\) of equation (22.54).
  3. The third: \(\int H_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x = h_{\mathrm{e}}\int_0^1 H_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\). For \(H_1 = 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3\) this is \(h_{\mathrm{e}}(1 - 1 + \tfrac12) = h_{\mathrm{e}}/2\), for \(H_2 = h_{\mathrm{e}}(\xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3)\) it is \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2(\tfrac12 - \tfrac23 + \tfrac14) = h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/12\), for \(H_3\) it is \(h_{\mathrm{e}}(1 - \tfrac12) = h_{\mathrm{e}}/2\) and for \(H_4 = h_{\mathrm{e}}(\xi^3 - \xi^2)\) it is \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2(\tfrac14 - \tfrac13) = -h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/12\). This is \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\) of equation (22.54). This proves point 1.
  4. Symmetry: both matrices are symmetric by lemmas 12.4 and 12.43. For every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int D\,w_{\mathrm{h}}''^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{f}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \int k\,w_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\). The sum is zero only if both are zero, that is if \(w_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a polynomial that is zero everywhere (as in step 5 of lemma 12.43), so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\). This proves point 2.
  5. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (w_{\mathrm{p}}, 0, w_{\mathrm{p}}, 0)^\T\) we have \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) (rigid translation: the first and third columns of the matrix of equation (12.3) add up to zero). The first row of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{f}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is \(k h_{\mathrm{e}}(156 + 54)w_{\mathrm{p}}/420 = k\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\,w_{\mathrm{p}}/2 = q\,h_{\mathrm{e}}/2\), the second is \(k h_{\mathrm{e}}(22h_{\mathrm{e}} + 13h_{\mathrm{e}})w_{\mathrm{p}}/420 = k\,h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\,w_{\mathrm{p}}/12 = q\,h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/12\), the third \(k h_{\mathrm{e}}(54 + 156)w_{\mathrm{p}}/420 = q\,h_{\mathrm{e}}/2\) and the fourth \(k h_{\mathrm{e}}(-13h_{\mathrm{e}} - 22h_{\mathrm{e}})w_{\mathrm{p}}/420 = -q\,h_{\mathrm{e}}^2/12\). So \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). This proves point 3.

Source: ONATE2 §9.9.3.1 (PDF pp. 597-598): the two-node Kirchhoff conical element has a cubic \(w\) of Hermite functions and a linear \(u\), COOK (16.3-4b) (PDF p. 586) the same cubic \(w\). The proof is not from the sources, it uses lemmas 12.4 and 12.43.

The lemma says what is new: besides the bending stiffness of the beam element there is the matrix of the foundation. Its unit is the same as that of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{b}}\): \(12D/h_{\mathrm{e}}^3\) has the unit N mm/mm\(^3\) = N/mm\(^2\) and \(k\,h_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot 156/420\) has N/mm\(^3\cdot\) mm = N/mm\(^2\). Point 2 has a physical reason: a displacement of the cylinder as a whole in the radial direction stretches all the rings, and so it does not have zero energy. The solution of the system \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f\) by theorem 12.9 (the energy of the structure is the sum of the element energies, quadratic in the common unknowns, stationarity gives the system) exists for arbitrary supports. Point 3 is a test: the element represents exactly the state that the solution has away from the edges. So the elements away from the edge add almost no error of their own: what remains there is the error that comes from the edge and decreases with the distance. For the tube we test below with \(n = 8\) equal elements, the error of the nodal displacement at \(x = 12{,}5\) mm is \(7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) of \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\), and at \(x \ge 50\) mm at most \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\).

22.8.3 Accuracy: how long may an element be (natančnost)

We test the element on the tube with the left end clamped (\(w = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\) at \(x = 0\)) and the right end free at \(x = L = 100\) mm. The right end is far from the edge: \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta L} = 2{,}6\cdot 10^{-6}\), so there we already know the solution. The exact solution (theorem 22.43) has the moment at the clamp \(|M_0| = p(1 - \nu/2)/(2\beta^2) = 77{,}167\) N mm/mm. The moment of the element is \(M = -D\,w_{\mathrm{h}}''\) and it is a linear function on the element (step 1 of lemma 12.4). The sign comes from step 2 of the proof of lemma 22.51, where \(z\) points outward. The section on bending of the cylinder chose the sign as for a beam, \(M = D\,w''\), so we compare absolute values. The values are computed in the script num-08.py (NumPy, SymPy). The exact solution for \(L = 100\) mm has zero moment and shear force at the right end and agrees with the semi-infinite one at \(M_0\) to four digits.

On the left is the ratio \(M/M_0\) of the moment of the element to the exact one, the dashed line is 1. For a coarse mesh, \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} > 4{,}3\), the moment is less than a quarter of the true one, at \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} \approx 1\) it is still 16 % less. On the right is the relative error \(\delta\) on a logarithmic scale, the dashed lines are 5 % and 1 %, the blue line shows the slope that corresponds to \(\delta \propto h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\).

Three conclusions follow from the data. First: the error of \(M(0)\) falls by the factor 3,70, 3,91, 3,98 when the element is halved, so it is proportional to \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\), and the displacement at the nodes by 15,6, 15,9 and 15,95, so as \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^4\). The reason: the field of the element is a cubic polynomial, which represents polynomials up to degree three exactly. By the sources the error of the field is proportional to \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^{p+1} = h_{\mathrm{e}}^4\) and the error of the second derivative, which is the moment, to \(h_{\mathrm{e}}^{p+1-2} = h_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) for \(p = 3\) (COOK §9.6, PDF pp. 327-328). We do not prove this, the proof is in the source. Second: for a coarse mesh the two ratios do not hold yet (1,39 and 2,10 for \(n = 2 \to 4 \to 8\)), because an element with \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} > 1\) does not resolve the edge: the edge disturbance \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\) (theorem 22.43) falls to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}043\) in \(\pi/\beta = 24{,}4\) mm, so an element with \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = 25\) mm covers all of it with one cubic polynomial. Third: an error of 5 % requires \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} \le 0{,}56\), so \(h_{\mathrm{e}} \le 4{,}35\) mm \(= 0{,}435\sqrt{Rh}\), and an error of 1 % requires \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} \le 0{,}24\), so \(h_{\mathrm{e}} \le 1{,}89\) mm \(= 0{,}19\sqrt{Rh}\) (\(\sqrt{Rh} = 10\) mm). Cook tests his element on a mesh with \(h_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}625\sqrt{Rh}\) (he writes \(t\) for the thickness), which he calls very coarse near the end (COOK PDF p. 588, figure 16.3-2). There the end is simply supported, the element also includes the shear deformation of the wall and the moment is read at the element centres, where the accuracy is good. At our clamped edge and for the moment at the edge itself, the same length gives \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}} = 0{,}80\) and an error of 9,6 %.

The edge is a local disturbance: its influence falls to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}043\) at the distance \(\pi/\beta = 24{,}44\) mm (section on bending of the cylinder). A fine mesh is needed only there. With 12 elements of 2,00 mm in the first 24 mm and 3 elements of 25,33 mm in the remaining 76 mm (15 elements) we get an error of \(M(0)\) of 1,03 %, while a uniform mesh with the same element of 2,00 mm (50 elements) gives 1,09 %. The mesh is refined at the edge, where the solution is not smooth, and is coarse where \(w\) is almost \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\).

Why does the theorem on accuracy at the nodes not help? Chapter 12 showed that for a beam \(w\) and \(w'\) at the nodes are exact for every load (theorem 12.14). Here the errors in the table are non-zero. The last column of the table says that at \(n = 8\) the error is \(7{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\) of \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) and not about \(10^{-14}\). We test the difference on the same elements: for \(k = 0\) (a beam under uniform load, a cantilever with \(L = 100\) mm) the largest error of the nodal displacements and rotations is \(5\cdot 10^{-15}\), \(9\cdot 10^{-15}\) and \(1\cdot 10^{-13}\) for \(n = 2, 4, 8\) relative to the largest displacement, which is rounding.

Lemma 22.54 Nodal exactness fails for the cylinder element (izrek o točnosti v vozliščih ne velja za valj)

Let \(k > 0\), let the cylinder be supported only at its ends, \(B(v, w) = \int (D\,v''w'' + k\,v\,w)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and let \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) be the deflection under a unit force at a point \(P\) where no support prevents the displacement, with the same supports, so that \(B(G_{\mathrm{P}}, w) = w(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) for every admissible \(w\) (as in step 5 of theorem 12.14). Then \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is not a cubic polynomial on any interval that does not have \(P\) in its interior, so on no element when \(P\) is a node. Therefore \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) does not lie in the family of finite elements and step 4 of the proof of theorem 12.14 does not hold.

Proof

We must show that a cubic polynomial on an interval without a force is not a solution, except zero, and that zero is excluded by the energy.

  1. Let \(I\) be an interval that does not have \(P\) in its interior. There is no force on it, so \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) solves \(D\,G_{\mathrm{P}}'''' + k\,G_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) (lemma 22.51, point 3, with \(q = 0\)).
  2. If \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is cubic on \(I\), then \(G_{\mathrm{P}}'''' = 0\), so \(k\,G_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) and because \(k > 0\), \(G_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\) on \(I\).
  3. On the part of the axis between \(P\) and the end of the cylinder on which \(I\) lies there is neither a force nor a support, so there \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is a solution of a linear equation with constant coefficients. A solution that is zero on \(I\) together with its derivatives is, by uniqueness of the solution of the initial value problem, zero on the whole part up to \(P\). Since \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is continuous, \(G_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\).
  4. By step 5 of theorem 12.14 with \(w = G_{\mathrm{P}}\), \(G_{\mathrm{P}}(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = B(G_{\mathrm{P}}, G_{\mathrm{P}}) = \int(D\,G_{\mathrm{P}}''^2 + k\,G_{\mathrm{P}}^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is positive because \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) is not zero everywhere: otherwise \(B(G_{\mathrm{P}}, w) = 0\) for every admissible \(w\), but no support prevents the displacement at \(P\), so there is an admissible \(w\) with \(w(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 1\). A contradiction with step 3.

Source: The proof is not from the sources, it follows step 4 of theorem 12.14. The non-zero errors in the table are a computation in the script num-08.py.

The lemma says the reason is the term \(k\,w\): because of it the deflection \(G_{\mathrm{P}}\) under a unit force is not piecewise cubic, so the orthogonality of the error to the family (step 3 of theorem 12.14) no longer gives \(e(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\). At \(k = 0\) the theorem is valid again, which the errors of \(10^{-13}\) above show. The difference is therefore in the foundation, not in the element.

22.8.4 The conical element and the results (stožčasti element)

The closed formula for the cylinder is suitable where the wall is a cylinder. The other parts of the vessel, cone and sphere, are covered by the conical element: a piece of a cone with two nodes, which is in the sources the basic element for shells of revolution (COOK PDF pp. 584-585, ONATE2 §9.1, PDF p. 556). The meridian is a straight line for the element, and a curved shell is represented by a polygonal line. A node has three unknowns: the displacement \(u\) along the meridian, the normal displacement \(w\) and the rotation (COOK figure 16.3-1b, PDF p. 585). The strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ms}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}\theta} = (u\sin\alpha + w\cos\alpha)/r\), where \(\alpha\) is the angle between the meridian and the axis (COOK (16.3-3), PDF p. 586, their symbol is \(\phi\), our \(\varphi\) has another meaning in this chapter) and \(r\) is the radius of the parallel circle, which changes along the element. So \(1/r\) appears in the integrals and the source recommends two-point Gauss integration (PDF p. 586). At \(\alpha = 0\) we get the cylinder of this section, at \(\alpha = \pi/2\) a circular plate (PDF p. 587). The matrix \(\mat k\) is \(6 \times 6\) in the local axes of the meridian, for assembly it is rotated into the common radial and axial axes: \(\mat T^\T\mat k\,\mat T\) (COOK PDF p. 586), which is the same step as for the frame in chapter 12 (lemma 12.18). We do not derive the matrix \(\mat k\), we quote it from the sources. This is a simplification: the full derivation with strains from (16.3-3) and integration by (16.3-5) is in COOK (PDF p. 586). For the cylinder of this section the axial unknown \(u\) is eliminated by lemma 22.51, because \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = N_0\) is known. For a cone this is not possible, because \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\) changes along the meridian.

What remains is what a program prints. From \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) follow \(w_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{h}}'\), \(w_{\mathrm{h}}''\) by the Hermite functions. The moment is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -D\,w_{\mathrm{h}}''\), the hoop force \(N_\theta = Eh\,w_{\mathrm{h}}/R + \nu N_0\) (from \(N_\theta = C(\varepsilon_\theta + \nu\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}})\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = N_0/C - \nu\varepsilon_\theta\) from step 5 of lemma 22.51). The stresses on the faces follow because the stress is linear through the thickness: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}(z) = N_{\mathrm{x}}/h + 12M_{\mathrm{x}}\,z/h^3\) (the integral \(\int\sigma_{\mathrm{x}} z\,\mathrm{d}z = M_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(\int z^2\,\mathrm{d}z = h^3/12\) and \(\int\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}z = N_{\mathrm{x}}\)). On the outer face (\(z = h/2\)), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}} = N_{\mathrm{x}}/h + 6M_{\mathrm{x}}/h^2\), on the inner face \(N_{\mathrm{x}}/h - 6M_{\mathrm{x}}/h^2\).

For the tube at the clamp: with the exact \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -77{,}167\) N mm/mm, \(N_{\mathrm{x}}/h = 37{,}50\) MPa, \(6M_{\mathrm{x}}/h^2 = -115{,}75\) MPa, so the inner face is \(153{,}25\) MPa and the outer \(-78{,}25\) MPa. The mean hoop stress there is \(N_\theta/h = \nu N_0/h = 11{,}25\) MPa, because \(w = 0\). On the outer face we add \(6M_\theta/h^2\) to it, on the inner face we subtract it, with \(M_\theta = \nu M_{\mathrm{x}}\) (theorem 22.28 with \(\kappa_\theta = 0\)). The hoop stress is therefore \(45{,}97\) MPa on the inner and \(-23{,}47\) MPa on the outer face. With \(n = 16\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -69{,}724\) N mm/mm, so \(6M_{\mathrm{x}}/h^2 = -104{,}59\) MPa, the inner face is \(142{,}09\) MPa: 7,3 % below the true value, on the unsafe side. With \(n = 8\) it is \(118{,}46\) MPa, 22,7 % less. The moment is linear in the element and jumps at a node (only \(w\) and \(w'\) are continuous). The jump of the moment at the first node is \(17{,}74\) N mm/mm at \(n = 8\), \(3{,}243\) at \(n = 16\), \(0{,}336\) at \(n = 32\) and \(0{,}027\) at \(n = 64\). The jump is here a measure of the error: at \(n = 16\) it is 4,7 % of the moment at the clamp, and when it falls under 1 % (from \(n = 26\) on), the error of \(M(0)\) is under 4 % (3,9 % at \(n = 26\)). This is an observation for this example, not a theorem.

The section showed three things. The energy of a cylinder strip is the energy of a beam on a foundation (lemma 22.51), so the element is the beam of chapter 12 with an added matrix of the foundation (lemma 22.53). The accuracy depends on \(\beta h_{\mathrm{e}}\), not on the number of elements: the reason is that the edge is a local disturbance of length \(\pi/\beta\) (section on bending of the cylinder), and the element must be much shorter than \(1/\beta\). And the exactness at the nodes of the chapter on beams does not hold (lemma 22.54). Chapter 18 gives the elements for the plate, chapter 15 the plane elements, from which we assemble the flat shell elements in the next section.
