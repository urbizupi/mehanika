Chapter 1 Strain

1.6 Displacement in space

Let us return to mechanics, where we left it at the end of section 1.4: to the bar and to bodies with three dimensions. When we load a body, every point of it moves from its old position to a new one. For the bar we described this move by a single number, because the points moved only along the bar. In a body a point can move in any direction, so we describe its move by a vector.

Definition 1.22 Displacement vector (vektor pomika)

If a point of a body moves under loading from the position \(P\) to a new position, its displacement vector (vektor pomika) \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the vector from the old position to the new one. So the new position is \(P + \vek u\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) say how far the point moves in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Source: CM (3.2.7) (PDF p. 117), BARBER (1.32) (PDF pp. 31-32), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

How does the bar of the first sections fit into this? There we labelled a point of the bar by its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, measured before loading, and its displacement by a single number \(u(x)\), how far the point moved down. To write this displacement as a vector, we choose a coordinate system that fits the bar. We put the origin at the top end of the bar and point the axis \(x\) down along the bar. Then the coordinate \(x\) of a point on the axis of the bar is exactly its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, and a move down by \(u(x)\) is a move in the direction of the axis \(x\). So the displacement vector of this point is

\[\vek u = \big(u(x),\ 0,\ 0\big) .\]

Its component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the number \(u(x)\) of the previous sections. The components \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero, because this point moves only along the axis \(x\).

This holds for the points on the axis of the bar, that is, for the line we have looked at so far. A bar that we stretch also gets a little thinner. When a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about \(0{,}3\) thousandths of the thickness (BOWER §3.2, PDF p. 102, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). So the points off the axis of the bar also move a little sideways, toward the axis, and for them \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are not zero. We have not taken this into account so far. In a body all three components of the displacement are in general different from zero.

Can we explain the thinning by the volume? Suppose the material kept its volume exactly. A cube of the material with edge \(1\) becomes a block with the edge \(1{,}001\) along the axis and the edges \(1 - d\) across it, and its volume is \(1{,}001\,(1 - d)^2\) (BARBER §1.3.2, PDF p. 40). This equals \(1\) only for \(d = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1{,}001} \approx 0{,}0005\): the thickness would decrease by half a thousandth. Steel gets thinner by less, \(0{,}0003\), so its volume grows a little, \(1{,}001 \cdot 0{,}9997^2 \approx 1{,}0004\), that is, by about \(0{,}04\) %. So steel does not keep its volume exactly. Keeping the volume is a limiting case. With the measure \(\Delta L / L\), a material that keeps its volume exactly has the ratio of the relative decrease of thickness to the relative increase of length close to \(0{,}5\): at the strain \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) it is \(0{,}49963\), and in general \(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{3}{8}\varepsilon + \dots\), so for a smaller strain it comes still closer to \(0{,}5\). With the logarithmic strain the ratio is exactly \(0{,}5\) for every elongation, because the volume is the product of the ratios of the three edges and the logarithm turns a product into a sum: \(\ln 1{,}001 + 2 \ln (1 - d) = \ln 1 = 0\), so the lateral logarithmic strain is exactly \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) of the axial one. The value \(0{,}5\) that BOWER gives is the small-strain value (BOWER §3.2, PDF p. 102). Rubber, with \(0{,}49\), comes very close to it (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). If the ratio were \(0\), the bar would not get thinner at all (BOWER p. 102). For most materials it lies between these two cases, typically between \(0{,}2\) and \(0{,}49\), and around \(0{,}3\) for metals (BOWER p. 102). How much a material gets thinner is therefore a property of the material, which we measure in a test. Books call this ratio Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število), and we will meet it in chapter 3.

Books also write the components of the displacement vector as \(u_1\), \(u_2\), \(u_3\) (CM) or \(u\), \(v\), \(w\) (SADD §2.1, PDF p. 39). In this book the index says the direction, as with the components of every vector.

Every point of the body has its own displacement vector, and different points move differently. For the hanging bar the displacement depended on the position \(x\). In a body it depends on the position of the point, that is, on three coordinates.

Definition 1.23 Displacement field (polje pomikov)

The displacement field (polje pomikov) of a body is the rule that assigns to every point \((x, y, z)\) of the body, taken in its position before loading, its displacement vector

\[\vek u(x, y, z) = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z)\big) . \tag{1.14}\]

Source: CM (3.2.8) (PDF p. 117), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

The displacement field is a rule of the same kind as a function of definition 1.4, except that it has three numbers at the input and three at the output. At the input are the coordinates of the point before loading, at the output the components of its displacement. Each component on its own, for example \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\), is a rule whose input is three numbers and whose output is one number. The numbers at the input are called variables (spremenljivke), so such a rule is called a function of three variables (funkcija treh spremenljivk). The word field comes from a picture: if we draw at every point of the body the arrow of its displacement, the body is covered with arrows like a field with blades of grass.

How do we tell from the displacement field whether the body is deformed? If the displacement is the same vector at all points, all points move equally, and the body only moves as a whole. This is the rigid-body displacement of section 1.4, now in space. But the body can also be turned as a whole without being deformed. Then the points move differently, while the distances between them stay the same, so this too is a rigid-body displacement. Every rigid-body displacement is a move of all points by the same vector, a turn of the body, or both one after the other (CM (3.8.12), PDF p. 156, for small turns SADD (2.2.10), PDF p. 45). So displacements that differ from point to point do not yet mean deformation. Deformation is a change of the distances between points, and that is why we will measure it with lengths.

1.6.1 Fibres

For the bar we looked at a small piece between two points. In a body we do the same, except that the piece can lie in any direction.

Definition 1.24 Material fibre (vlakno)

A material fibre (vlakno) is a straight segment between two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body, that is, a straight line that starts at \(P\), ends at \(Q\) and lies wholly in the body, which we imagine drawn into the material so that its points move together with the material. Before loading the fibre is described by the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\).

Source: CM §3.3.1 (PDF pp. 118-119, material line element), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42, fibers).

A fibre is like a very thin bar inside the body. So we calculate its strain as the strain of a bar (1.1): the elongation divided by the length before loading. The length before loading is \(L_0 = |\overrightarrow{PQ}|\), the length after loading \(L_1\) is the distance between the new positions of the ends, and the elongation is \(L_1 - L_0\). After loading a fibre is in general no longer perfectly straight. A short fibre bends only a little, so we take the distance between the new positions of the ends as the new length. How accurate this is we will see when we shorten the fibre towards zero, as we did with the piece of the bar. For the cube below the fibres stay straight, and there it is exact.

Where does the fibre go? The end \(P\) moves by \(\vek u(P)\), which means the displacement vector at the point \(P\), and the end \(Q\) by \(\vek u(Q)\). The new positions of the ends are \(P_1 = P + \vek u(P)\) and \(Q_1 = Q + \vek u(Q)\). The index \(1\), as in \(L_1\), means the state after loading. We calculate the vector of the fibre after loading, \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\), component by component, the way we checked the rules of vector arithmetic in the proof of lemma 1.15. Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\).

  1. The coordinate \(x\) of the point \(Q_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)\), and that of the point \(P_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\), because a vector is added to a point in the same way as in the sum of vectors, by adding to each coordinate the corresponding component (definition 1.14).
  2. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\) is the difference of these two coordinates ((1.8)), \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)) - (x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\).
  3. We remove the brackets and rearrange the terms by the rules for numbers: \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) + (u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\). The first bracket is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) ((1.8)), the second the component \(x\) of the difference \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\) ((1.9)).
  4. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same. So the two vectors agree in all three components, and by definition 1.13 that is what equality of vectors means, so
    \[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) . \tag{1.15}\]

So a fibre changes only by the difference of the displacements of its ends (figure 1.32). This is the same difference as \(\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) for the piece of the bar, except that it is now a vector. If the displacement is the same at all points, the difference is the zero vector, and every fibre stays as it was.

1.6.2 A sheared cube

Let us try all this on one simple body. We glue a steel cube with an edge of 10 mm to a table by its bottom face and push its top face sideways, so that it moves by 10 µm (figure 1.33). A micrometre, µm, is a thousandth of a millimetre, so the shift is a thousandth of the edge. The cube leans over like the deck of cards of section 1.1: every horizontal layer slides a little relative to the layer below it. Books call such a deformation simple shear (enostavni strig) (CM (3.3.14), PDF p. 123). The word shear describes exactly this, layers sliding past each other like the blades of scissors.

We place the coordinate system at the bottom front left corner of the cube: the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(y\) along the other bottom edge, and the axis \(z\) vertically up. We measure lengths in micrometres, so the edge of the cube is \(10\,000\) µm. How far does each point move? The bottom layer is glued and does not move at all. The top layer, at the height \(10\,000\) µm, moves by \(10\) µm. A layer in between moves in proportion to its height, by a thousandth of it: the layer at the height \(5\,000\) µm by \(5\) µm, the layer at the height \(z\) by \(0{,}001\,z\) (figure 1.34). All points move only in the direction \(x\). So the displacement field is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = 0{,}001\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) = 0 . \tag{1.16}\]

At the bottom face, where \(z = 0\), the displacement is zero, as it must be, since the face is glued down. The field does not depend on \(x\) and \(y\): all points of one layer move equally.

This field is an idealization, a simple model of the sheared cube. We get it exactly only if, besides the push on the top face and the glue on the bottom one, we also pull along the two side faces that are perpendicular to the axis \(x\): the one we push towards upward, the opposite one downward. The push at the top and the glue at the bottom pull in opposite directions at different heights, and on their own they would tend to turn the cube. If we push only the top face, the field does not hold near these two side faces. Which forces the field needs we compute in chapter 3.

Is it reasonable that the layers move only sideways and not also up or down? One reason is that this way the cube keeps its volume. The front face \(ABCD\) becomes a parallelogram with the same base \(AB\) and the same height, \(10\,000\) µm, and the area of a parallelogram is its base times its height, so the area of the face does not change. The depth of the cube stays the same as well, so its volume does not change either. This holds although the slanted edges get a little longer, as we will compute for the edge \(AD\) below. A deformation that keeps the volume of every piece of a body is called an isochoric deformation (izohorna deformacija), and simple shear is one (CM §3.3.2, PDF pp. 121-122). For small deformations this holds in general: the change of volume of a small piece depends only on its normal strains, while shear changes only the shape, not the volume (BOWER §2.1.9, PDF pp. 51-52).

To see how the material is stretched, we follow four fibres on the front face of the cube, where \(y = 0\) (figure 1.35). Its corners are \(A = (0, 0, 0)\), \(B = (10\,000, 0, 0)\), \(C = (10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) and \(D = (0, 0, 10\,000)\). The fibres are the bottom edge \(AB\), the vertical edge \(AD\), and the two diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\). The bottom corners do not move, \(\vek u(A) = \vek u(B) = \vek 0\), and the top ones move by \(\vek u(C) = \vek u(D) = (10, 0, 0)\). For each fibre we calculate its vector after loading by (1.15), its two lengths by (1.10) and its strain by (1.1).

Let us show the calculation for the vertical edge \(AD\). Before loading \(\overrightarrow{AD} = (0, 0, 10\,000)\), with length \(L_0 = 10\,000\) µm. The difference of the displacements of the ends is \(\vek u(D) - \vek u(A) = (10, 0, 0)\), so the vector after loading is \((0, 0, 10\,000) + (10, 0, 0) = (10, 0, 10\,000)\). Its length is

\[L_1 = \sqrt{10^2 + 0^2 + 10\,000^2} = \sqrt{100\,000\,100} = 10\,000{,}005\ \text{µm} ,\]

and the strain is \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0 = 0{,}005/10\,000 = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The other fibres are calculated in the same way. Before loading both diagonals are \(\sqrt{2 \cdot 10\,000^2} = 14\,142{,}136\) µm long, after loading \(AC\) is \(14\,149{,}208\) µm long and \(BD\) is \(14\,135{,}066\) µm long.

fibre vector before loading vector after loading strain
bottom edge \(AB\) \((10\,000, 0, 0)\) \((10\,000, 0, 0)\) \(0\)
vertical edge \(AD\) \((0, 0, 10\,000)\) \((10, 0, 10\,000)\) \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\)
diagonal \(AC\) \((10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) \((10\,010, 0, 10\,000)\) \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
diagonal \(BD\) \((-10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) \((-9\,990, 0, 10\,000)\) \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

The table shows three things that we could not see with the bar.

  1. The diagonal \(AC\) has become longer by five ten-thousandths, and the diagonal \(BD\) shorter by almost exactly as much (figure 1.37). The same material and the same loading, and yet the material is stretched in one direction and shortened in another. How much the material is stretched depends on the direction in which we measure it.
  2. The edges have hardly changed. The bottom edge has stayed the same, and the vertical one has become longer by only \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), a thousand times less than the diagonals.
  3. The vertical edge has tilted, since its top has moved sideways and its bottom has not. So the right angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) at the corner \(A\) has become a little smaller. This is the change of a right angle that a shear strain describes (definition 1.3), here between the directions \(x\) and \(z\). The front face, which was a square, has become a parallelogram, a leaning four-sided figure with parallel opposite sides.

What happens if we push the top face twice as far, by 20 µm? The displacement field is then \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}002\,z\), and when we repeat the calculation, the diagonals get the strains \(+1{,}0005 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(-0{,}9995 \cdot 10^{-3}\), twice as much as before. The vertical edge, however, gets \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\), four times as much as before. The strain of the diagonals grows in proportion to the shift of the top face, and the strain of the vertical edge with the square of the shift.

These numbers show two patterns that need an explanation. The two diagonals change by almost the same amount with opposite signs, namely by half the ratio between the shift of the top face and the height of the cube: \(0{,}0005\) for the ratio \(10/10\,000 = 0{,}001\). The vertical edge changes a thousand times less, and when the shift doubles, its strain grows four times, so it grows with the square of the shift. Where does this come from? We call the height of the cube \(h\) and the shift of the top face \(s\), so \(h = 10\,000\) µm and \(s = 10\) µm. The ratio \(s/h\) is a ratio of two lengths, a number without a unit.

What gets in the way of the calculation is the square root in the lengths. We get rid of it with one move that we will need again.

  1. We multiply and divide the strain \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) by \(L_1 + L_0\). We may, because \(L_1 + L_0 > 0\), and the value does not change.
  2. The numerator becomes \((L_1 - L_0)(L_1 + L_0)\). When we multiply out the brackets, the terms \(L_1 L_0\) and \(-L_0 L_1\) cancel, and \(L_1^2 - L_0^2\) remains. So
    \[\varepsilon = \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)} . \tag{1.17}\]
    This equality is exact. The squares of the lengths are sums of squares of components ((1.10)), so there are no roots in the numerator.
  3. The denominator of (1.17), \(L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)\), still contains the new length \(L_1\), which we compute with a square root. We would like to get rid of this root too. If the length of the fibre did not change, we would have \(L_1 = L_0\) and the denominator \(L_0\,(L_0 + L_0) = 2L_0^2\). We check how far the true denominator is from this value. From \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) we get \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\), so \(L_1 + L_0 = L_0\,(2 + \varepsilon)\), and the true denominator is \(L_0 \cdot L_0\,(2 + \varepsilon) = 2L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon/2)\). So it differs from \(2L_0^2\) only by the factor \(1 + \varepsilon/2\). For strains smaller than a thousandth in size, \(|\varepsilon| < 0{,}001\), this factor differs from \(1\) by less than half a thousandth. So if we replace the denominator \(L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)\) by \(2L_0^2\), we are wrong by less than half a thousandth of the calculated value:
    \[\varepsilon \approx \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_0^2} .\]
    So the strain is about half the relative change of the square of the length. Why half? The new length is \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\), so its square is \(L_1^2 = L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2\). The square \((1 + \varepsilon)^2\) is just the product \((1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \varepsilon)\) written out: multiplying every term of the first bracket by every term of the second gives \(1 + \varepsilon + \varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\). So the relative change of the square of the length is \((L_1^2 - L_0^2)/L_0^2 = 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\). The last term is the product \(\varepsilon \cdot \varepsilon\), and for \(|\varepsilon| < 0{,}001\) it is smaller than a thousandth of \(\varepsilon\) in size, for example \(\varepsilon^2 = 0{,}000001\) for \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\). Without it, the relative change of the square is \(2\varepsilon\), twice the strain, and the strain is half of it.
  4. The bottom end of each of our three fibres does not move, and the top end moves sideways by \(s\). So the vertical part of each fibre stays \(h\), and its horizontal part changes by \(s\).
  5. The vertical edge \(AD\) has no horizontal part before loading and gets the horizontal part \(s\). The square of its length grows from \(h^2\) to \(h^2 + s^2\), and step 3 gives \(\varepsilon \approx s^2/(2h^2)\). For our cube this is \(10^2/(2 \cdot 10\,000^2) = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\).
  6. The diagonal \(AC\) has the horizontal part \(h\), which grows to \(h + s\). The square of its length grows from \(h^2 + h^2 = 2h^2\) to \((h + s)^2 + h^2 = 2h^2 + 2hs + s^2\), so by \(2hs + s^2\). The part \(s^2\) is only \(s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\) times the part \(2hs\), so we leave it out, and \(\varepsilon \approx 2hs/(2 \cdot 2h^2) = s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\). The diagonal \(BD\) points the other way, its horizontal part shrinks from \(h\) to \(h - s\), the square of its length shrinks by about \(2hs\), and \(\varepsilon \approx -s/(2h) = -0{,}0005\).

Let us put the three fibres side by side.

fibre change of the square of the length strain
edge \(AD\) \(s^2\) \(\approx s^2/(2h^2) = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\)
diagonal \(AC\) \(2hs + s^2\) \(\approx s/(2h) = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
diagonal \(BD\) \(-2hs + s^2\) \(\approx -s/(2h) = -5 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

The changes are made of two kinds of terms: terms with the shift \(s\) itself, namely \(\pm 2hs\), and terms with its square, \(s^2\). The term \(s^2\) is \(s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\) times the term \(2hs\), so it is 2000 times smaller. Where there is a term with \(s\), as for the diagonals, we may leave out the term \(s^2\), and we are wrong by less than a thousandth. Where there is no term with \(s\), as for the vertical edge, which only tilts, the term \(s^2\) is all that is left. That is why the change of the edge is a thousand times smaller than that of the diagonals and grows with the square of the shift: twice the shift, four times the strain. The term \(s^2\) that we left out and the small error of step 3 cause the small difference between \(5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in the table of the exactly computed strains.

We will use this rule all the time with small deformations. Next to a term with a small number itself, a term with its square is smaller roughly by the factor of that small number, as long as the numbers in front of the two terms are of similar size, as here. So we may leave it out.

Is the front face special? It is not. The difference of the displacements of two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of the cube is \(\big(0{,}001\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}),\ 0,\ 0\big)\). It depends only on how much higher \(Q\) lies than \(P\), not on where in the cube the two points are. By (1.15), two fibres with the same vector before loading therefore also have the same vector after loading, and the same strain, wherever in the cube they lie (figure 1.39). The whole cube is deformed in the same way at every point, and what we saw on the front face holds everywhere in it. Such a deformation is called a homogeneous deformation (homogena deformacija) (CM §3.3.2, PDF p. 122).

The cube has taught us two things. First, how the material is stretched close to a point is not described by a single number. We saw this by following four fibres of the front face and computing the strain of each from its lengths before and after loading (the table with the vectors of the fibres). The diagonal \(AC\) has the strain \(+5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the diagonal \(BD\) the strain \(-5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the bottom edge \(AB\) zero and the vertical edge \(AD\) only \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The edge \(AD\) also tilted, so the right angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) became smaller. These four fibres do not all pass through one point. The diagonals cross at the centre of the front face, and through the centre also pass a horizontal fibre with the same vector as \(AB\) and a vertical fibre with the same vector as \(AD\). The cube is deformed in the same way everywhere, so these two fibres get the same vectors after loading as the edges, and with them the same strains and the same change of the right angle between them. So the fibres through the centre of the face are stretched in one direction, shortened in another, almost unchanged in a third, and the angles between them change too. Second, we can calculate all of this from the displacement field. For every fibre we used only the field (1.16). It gave the displacements of the ends, the equation (1.15) turned their difference into the vector of the fibre after loading, and from the vectors before and after loading we got the two lengths and the strain. We needed nothing else.

For the cube the field was very clear, since the displacement was only horizontal and proportional to the height. So the difference of the displacements of the ends of a fibre depended only on the vector of the fibre, and the strain of a fibre only on its direction, not on where it lies or how long it is. A fibre in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\) that is half as long has half the difference of displacements, a vector after loading half as long, and therefore the same strain. That is why the whole edges and diagonals of the cube were enough. In a general body, however, the displacement changes from point to point in a complicated way, as for the hanging bar, where the piece of 1 m at the top had the strain \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) and the piece of 0,001 m the strain \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Now the displacement changes in three directions and depends on three coordinates. For the bar we helped ourselves with a piece that we shortened towards zero, and with the derivative. In space we will do the same. For this we will need derivatives of functions of three variables, and a way of writing that gives the strain of a fibre for every direction. We continue with them in the next section.
