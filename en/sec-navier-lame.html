Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.2 The Navier-Lamé equations (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe)

In the previous section we wrote down the boundary value problem of elasticity (definition 11.1). Its unknowns are the three components of the displacement, the six components of the strain and the six components of the stress, fifteen functions in all. For them we have fifteen equations: six relations between strains and displacements (chapter 1), six equations of Hooke's law (chapter 3) and three equilibrium equations (chapter 2). Can the fifteen equations be replaced by three, for the three unknown components of the displacement?

The idea is simple. The strains are already written with displacements, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\) (definition 1.115). Hooke's law (equation (3.13)) gives the stresses from the strains, so the stresses are written with displacements as well. The three equilibrium equations are equations for the stresses, so they become three equations for the displacements, and the six strains and six stresses drop out of the calculation. The resulting system has the advantage that we mentioned with the compatibility equations (section 1.18.3): the strains are computed from the displacements, so they fit together by themselves and we need no compatibility equations.

The equilibrium equations will contain the second derivatives of a displacement component with respect to all three coordinates, in the sum \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial z^2\). This sum appears so often in mechanics and physics that it gets a name.

Definition 11.5 Laplace operator (Laplaceov operator)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points of space and \(f\) a function of three variables that has continuous second partial derivatives at every point of \(D\). The Laplace operator (Laplaceov operator) assigns to \(f\) the function whose value at every point of \(D\) is the sum of the three second partial derivatives at that point,

\[\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} . \tag{11.2}\]

For a vector field \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components have continuous second partial derivatives, \(\nabla^2\vek u\) is the vector field with components \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the operator is applied to each component separately. If \(f\) also has continuous fourth partial derivatives, \(\nabla^4 f = \nabla^2(\nabla^2 f)\).

Source: CM (2.4.36) (PDF p. 76), where \(\nabla^2 = \nabla\cdot\nabla\) is called the Laplace operator, SADD (5.4.5) (PDF p. 111), KREYSZIG §9.7 (PDF p. 427).

The operator has two views. The gradient \(\nabla f\) is the column of partial derivatives (chapter 8), and its divergence (definition 4.13) is exactly \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 f/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 f/\partial z^2\), so \(\nabla^2 f = \operatorname{div}(\nabla f)\). The symbol \(\Delta\), which KREYSZIG gives as another name for the same operator (§9.7, PDF p. 427), is not used in this book, because \(\Delta\) means a difference (\(\Delta L\), \(\Delta T\)). We write \(\nabla^2\). As an example take \(f = x^2 + 3yz\). Its second derivatives are \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 = 2\), \(\partial^2 f/\partial y^2 = 0\) and \(\partial^2 f/\partial z^2 = 0\), so \(\nabla^2 f = 2\), the same at every point.

Now we can write the equations. Let the material be the same everywhere, so homogeneous (definition 3.5), and let it behave the same in all directions at every point, so isotropic (definition 3.4). The constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) of definition 3.10 are then the same numbers everywhere, so we may leave them in front of the derivative when we differentiate the stresses. Because \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), Hooke's law holds in the form (3.13). The definition of the boundary value problem asks only for continuously differentiable displacements and stresses, while the theorem asks for continuous second derivatives of the displacements, because the stresses will be differentiated once more. Whether the second follows from the first we do not prove. The solutions that we build in this chapter have continuous second derivatives, because they are written with smooth functions, such as polynomials and powers of the distance from the axis, which we check for every solution.

Theorem 11.6 Navier-Lamé equations (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe)

Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) (theorem 3.11, \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)). Let \(D\) be an open set of points of the body and \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) a displacement field with continuous second partial derivatives on \(D\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses that we obtain from the displacements \(\vek u\) by definition 1.115 and Hooke's law (3.13), and \(\vek b\) a vector at a point \(P\) of \(D\). Then the stresses \(\ten\sigma\) at \(P\) satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) with body force \(\vek b\) exactly when at \(P\)

\[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b = \vek 0 , \tag{11.3}\]

in components, for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\),

\[\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}\Big) + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 .\]
Proof

We must show that the left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equilibrium equation, when we insert the stresses of the field \(\vek u\), equals the left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equation of the theorem. We write \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\) (definition 1.109).

  1. By definition 1.115, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and the trace is \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = \operatorname{div}\vek u\) (definition 4.13). The identity matrix \(\mat I\) has the element \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which is \(1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(0\) otherwise. So Hooke's law (3.13), \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\), reads in components
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\right) . \tag{11.4}\]
    Since \(\vek u\) has continuous second derivatives on the open set \(D\), the stresses have continuous first derivatives at every point of \(D\).
  2. The left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equilibrium equation (2.31) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) we insert (11.4) with the indices swapped, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}} = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}} + \mu\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})\), differentiate with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and sum over \(\ix{j}\). The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives and the constants \(\lambda\), \(\mu\) stay in front of the derivative, so the three terms of the sum are those of steps 3 to 5.
  3. First term: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\big(\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\big)/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\) differs from zero only for \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\).
  4. Second term: \(\mu\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\) the mixed derivative is just a second derivative with respect to one variable. For \(\ix{j} \neq \ix{i}\) the two mixed second derivatives of the component \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\) are continuous, so by theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed derivatives we may swap them, \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). In both cases the sum over \(\ix{j}\) equals \(\mu\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\dfrac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\).
  5. Third term: \(\mu\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}\) by definition 11.5.
  6. We add steps 3 to 5:
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  7. The equilibrium equations (2.31) at the point \(P\) are \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\). By step 6 the left side equals the left side of the equation of the theorem. All steps are equalities, so one equation holds exactly when the other does. The three equations together are the vector equation (11.3), because \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) is a vector with components \(\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: SADD (5.4.2)-(5.4.5) (PDF p. 111), CM (7.2.16)-(7.2.17) (PDF p. 299), where CM keeps the acceleration term, zero in equilibrium. SADD writes the body force \(F\) and CM writes \(\rho_0 f_{\mathrm{i}}\), which is our \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\). SADD summarizes the derivation in one sentence, we wrote out the steps.

The proof showed that the equilibrium equations for the stresses of a displacement field are three second-order equations for the displacement itself: we removed the stresses with Hooke's law and the strains with their definition by displacements.

From history

For a long time it did not seem that anyone had tried to obtain general equations of equilibrium and motion of elastic bodies (LOVE, PDF p. 23). At the end of 1820, according to Love, all the effort spent on elastic problems could be summed up as an inadequate theory of flexure, an erroneous theory of torsion, an unproved theory of the vibrations of bars and plates and the definition of Young's modulus, and Love at once adds that such an estimate would give a wrong impression of the value of the older researches (LOVE, PDF p. 23). Navier was the first to investigate the general equations of equilibrium and vibration of elastic bodies. His memoir was read in May 1821 and published in 1827 (LOVE, PDF p. 23). He set out from Newton's conception of bodies: he regarded the molecules as material points and assumed that the force between two molecules whose distance is slightly increased is proportional to the product of the increase of the distance and some function of the initial distance. The elastic reactions arose, in his view, from variations of the forces between molecules when the arrangement of the molecules changes (LOVE, PDF pp. 23-24). He expressed all the forces on a displaced molecule and from them obtained the equations of motion of the molecule, and with them the equations for the displacements of the molecule. He assumed the material to be isotropic, and the equations of equilibrium and vibration contained a single constant of the same kind as Young's modulus (LOVE, PDF p. 24).

Navier's memoir was not generally accepted. The objections were aimed at his expression for the force between two molecules and at his replacing triple sums over molecules by integrals, since the validity of this replacement is disputed (LOVE, PDF p. 24). By the autumn of 1822 Cauchy had discovered most of the elements of the pure theory of elasticity: stress at a point, strain at a point, the equilibrium equations and, from the relation between stresses and strains, the equations for the displacements (LOVE, PDF p. 25). In a later published version he obtained the relation for an isotropic material from two assumptions, that it is linear and that the principal planes of stress are normal to the principal axes of strain. He used no hypothesis of material points and central forces. His equations contain two constants, Navier's only one (LOVE, PDF pp. 25-26). Later Cauchy also used the molecular picture with central forces. In the first memoir the equations for an isotropic body contained two constants. In the second memoir he expressed the stresses across any plane, and for an isotropic body without initial stress the stress was expressed through the strain with a single constant, so one of the constants of the first memoir had to vanish. The equations then agreed with Navier's (LOVE, PDF p. 26). Cauchy's derivation from linearity and isotropy thus gave two constants, and the molecular picture with central forces gave one (LOVE, PDF pp. 25-26).

Lamé and Clapeyron wrote a memoir on the internal equilibrium of homogeneous solid bodies in St. Petersburg. Poinsot and Navier reviewed it in 1828 and it was published in 1833 (TIMO, PDF p. 123). The memoir is important because it contains not only a derivation of the equilibrium equations, which Navier and Cauchy already knew, but also their application to practical problems (TIMO, PDF p. 123). In the first part they derived the equations with Navier's molecular forces and showed that Cauchy's stresses give the same ones. A single elastic constant appeared in their equations (TIMO, PDF pp. 123-124). In 1852 Lamé's book on the theory of elasticity appeared, the first book on this subject. The results of the memoir are in it, but the form of the equations is somewhat changed, because Lamé had concluded that two elastic constants determine the properties of an isotropic material. Cauchy had reached this conclusion earlier (TIMO, PDF pp. 124-125).

Let us return to the three equations for the displacements in the form of theorem 11.6, with the two Lamé constants (definition 3.10). What do the terms \(\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) mean? Together they are the force per unit volume that the stresses give to a piece of the body because they vary from place to place. The term \(\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u\) is nonzero when the sum of the second derivatives of a displacement component is nonzero, and the term \((\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) when the volumetric strain \(\operatorname{div}\vek u\) varies from place to place. Let us look at two simple displacements of a small cube with edge \(h\) and centre at height \(z > 0\) (figure 11.4).

In the first, \(\vek u = (c\,z^2, 0, 0)\) with a constant \(c\). The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) depends only on \(z\), so \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) and the cube only deforms in shear. The strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = c\,z\) and zero elsewhere, so by Hooke's law the only nonzero stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu c\,z\). On the top face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) a force per unit area \(2\mu c\,(z + h/2)\) acts in the direction of the \(x\) axis, on the bottom face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the force is \(-2\mu c\,(z - h/2)\). The total force divided by the volume \(h^3\) is

\[\frac{h^2\,\big[\,2\mu c\,(z + h/2) - 2\mu c\,(z - h/2)\,\big]}{h^3} = 2\mu c = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

because \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial^2(c\,z^2)/\partial z^2 = 2c\). The term with the divergence is zero, because \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\). On the faces perpendicular to \(x\) and \(y\) there is no force in the direction of the \(x\) axis, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The force comes from the shear stresses, which are larger at the top than at the bottom.

In the second, \(\vek u = (c\,x^2, 0, 0)\). The cube stretches more along the \(x\) axis on the right than on the left, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2c\,x\) and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 2c\,x\). By Hooke's law \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\cdot 2c\,x + 2\mu\cdot 2c\,x = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,2c\,x\). The stress on the right face is larger than on the left, so the force per unit volume is \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,2c\). The theorem splits it into two parts, \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\mu c\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x = (\lambda + \mu)\,2c\), which together give the same, \(2c\,(\lambda + 2\mu)\). Here both terms are nonzero at once. There are no shear stresses in this case, yet the term \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is nonzero. The split of the force into the two terms of the theorem is therefore not a split into a shear and a volumetric contribution, but a consequence of writing the equations with displacements.

The theorem holds in the interior of the body. On the surface either a displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) is prescribed on a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), which is already a condition on the displacement, or a traction vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) on a part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) (definition 11.1). The second condition is written with stresses. We can write it with displacements too, from the same step 1 as the equations.

Corollary 11.7 Traction condition in terms of displacements (robni pogoj z napetostnim vektorjem v pomikih)

Let the material be as in theorem 11.6. Let \(P\) be a point of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface of the body with unit outward normal \(\vek n\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\) the prescribed traction vector at \(P\). Let the displacement field \(\vek u\) be continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(P\), with displacement gradient \(\mat H\) (definition 1.109). Then the condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) at the point \(P\) holds exactly when

\[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} , \tag{11.5}\]

in components \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \big[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \mu\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\big]\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = \bar t_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Proof

We must show that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) equals the left side of (11.5).

  1. By definition 1.115, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) and \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = \operatorname{div}\vek u\). Hooke's law (3.13) gives \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\mat I + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\), which is (11.4) written with matrices. We multiply the matrix by the vector \(\vek n\) and use \(\mat I\vek n = \vek n\).

Source: CM (7.2.18) (PDF p. 299), SADD (5.2.1), (5.2.2) (PDF pp. 108-109) for the boundary conditions. The matrix form is ours.

Let us test the theorem and the corollary on three fields. The first is the field of the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter 10 mm, with the \(x\) axis pointing down from the centre \(O\) of the top face, is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\) is fixed and a uniform force acts upward on the top face. The body force is the weight, \(\vek b = (\rho g, 0, 0)\) with \(\rho g = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\ \text{N/m}^3\). The field (1.18) of chapter 1 has \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (chapter 1 rounded it to \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\)) and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). Let us examine the terms of equation (11.3) for the component \(x\).

  1. The second derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are \(-2a\), \(-0{,}6\,a\) and \(-0{,}6\,a\), so \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = -3{,}2\,a\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are linear in each coordinate, so \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} = \nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  2. The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 2a\,(L - x) - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) = 0{,}8\,a\,(L - x)\). It depends only on \(x\), so \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = (-0{,}8\,a, 0, 0)\).
  3. With \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa and \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa (section 3.2.6) we have \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = -3{,}2 \cdot 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 75{,}385 \cdot 10^9 = -47\,088\ \text{N/m}^3\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x = -0{,}8 \cdot 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 188{,}46 \cdot 10^9 = -29\,430\ \text{N/m}^3\).
  4. The sum is \(-47\,088 - 29\,430 + 76\,518 = 0\). The components \(y\) and \(z\) are \(0 + 0 + 0 = 0\), because \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial y = 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). The Navier-Lamé equations hold (checked with SymPy, the residual is zero).

Why exactly \(0{,}3\) in the field? If the number \(k\) stood in the field instead of \(0{,}3\), that is \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -2k\,a\,(L - x)\,y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - k\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), the residual of the component \(x\) by the theorem would be \(2\nu\rho g\,(k - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), which is zero only for \(k = \nu\) (SymPy). For \(k = 0\), that is for a bar that does not get thinner, the residual would be \(-26\,487\ \text{N/m}^3\), which is 35 % of the weight. Equilibrium itself requires the thinning by the Poisson's ratio of the material.

Let us also examine the boundary condition. For the field \(\mat H + \mat H^\T = \operatorname{diag}(4s, -1{,}2\,s, -1{,}2\,s)\) with \(s = a\,(L - x)\), because \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = -0{,}6\,a\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y = 0\) and the same for \(z\), and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0{,}8\,s\). On the mantle \(\vek n = (0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) and by (11.5)

\[\lambda \cdot 0{,}8\,s\,\vek n + \mu\,(-1{,}2\,s)\,\vek n = s\,(0{,}8 \cdot 113{,}08 - 1{,}2 \cdot 75{,}385)\ \text{GPa}\;\vek n = s\,(90{,}46 - 90{,}46)\ \text{GPa}\;\vek n = \vek 0 .\]

So the mantle is free, as it must be. On the bottom face \(s = 0\) and the traction vector is zero. On the top face \(\vek n = (-1, 0, 0)\) and \(s = aL\), so the traction vector is \(-aL\,(0{,}8\lambda + 4\mu)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Here \(0{,}8\lambda + 4\mu = 392\) GPa \(= 2E\), so the vector equals

\[-1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 392\ \text{GPa}\;\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (-0{,}7652\ \text{MPa},\,0,\,0) = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

Since the \(x\) axis points down, this means a force upward, uniform over the top face, as we described it in chapter 1 (figure 11.5). The area of the top face is \(\pi\,(5\ \text{mm})^2 = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), so the force is \(0{,}7652\ \text{MPa} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 60{,}1\) N, which is the weight of the bar, \(\rho g\,L\,A\) with \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). The field is therefore a solution of the problem with the force prescribed on the whole surface (the traction vector is prescribed everywhere on the surface). Here we only checked the field. We will derive it in section 11.4, where it is also shown that, up to a rigid displacement, it is the only solution of this problem.

The second field is the ball in oil of section 4.3.7 in chapter 4, checked there with the divergence and here with the equations of this chapter. A steel ball of radius \(R = 10\) mm lies in oil at pressure \(p = 100\) MPa. In a fluid at rest the stress is \(-p\,\mat I\) (section 3.2.4), so the traction vector of the fluid on the surface of the ball is \(\bar{\vek t} = -p\,\vek n\). The weight of the ball is not important at this pressure, \(\rho g R = 0{,}77\) kPa, which is \(8 \cdot 10^{-6}\) of the pressure, so we take \(\vek b = \vek 0\). For the cube in water Hooke's law gave \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -(1 - 2\nu)\,p/E\) (section 3.2.4). Let us try the same for the ball, that is the displacement \(\vek u = \varepsilon_0\,(x, y, z)\) with the origin at the centre and \(\varepsilon_0 = -(1 - 2\nu)\,p/E = -0{,}4 \cdot 100/196\,000 = -2{,}0408 \cdot 10^{-4}\).

All components are linear, so \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 3\varepsilon_0\) is constant and \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\). The Navier-Lamé equations hold with \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The boundary condition: \(\mat H = \varepsilon_0\,\mat I\), so the left side of (11.5) equals \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_0\,\vek n = 490\ \text{GPa} \cdot (-2{,}0408 \cdot 10^{-4})\,\vek n = -100\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n = -p\,\vek n\). The field is a solution, the ball shrinks, and its edge moves by \(\varepsilon_0 R = -2{,}041\ \mu\text{m}\) towards the centre. The solution is unique up to a rigid displacement (theorem 11.4), since this is a traction problem.

The third field is uniform. The shifted cube of section 1.6.2 has \(\vek u = (0{,}001\,z, 0, 0)\). The components are linear, so \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) and \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\), and the equations hold with \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The stress is the same everywhere, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \mu \cdot 0{,}001 = 75{,}38\) MPa, as we computed in chapter 3. The same holds for every field \(\vek u = \vek c + \mat A\,\vek x\) with a constant vector \(\vek c\) and a constant matrix \(\mat A\): the second derivatives are zero and the divergence is constant, so such a field is in equilibrium without body force.

Let us collect what we have. The unknowns are only the three components of the displacement. The three equations (11.3) are coupled, because the term \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) contains all three components in every equation. Together with the boundary conditions, the displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and (11.5) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), they form the displacement form of the boundary value problem. Let the field \(\vek u\) have continuous second derivatives on an open set that contains the body, let it satisfy (11.3) at every interior point, let \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\) hold on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the condition (11.5) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). By theorem 11.6 and corollary 11.7 the stresses of the field are in equilibrium and satisfy the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), so \(\vek u\) is a solution of the boundary value problem by definition 11.1. By theorem 11.4 (for steel the condition of the theorem on positive energy holds, theorem 3.48) it is the only solution if \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) prevents every small rigid displacement, and otherwise unique up to a rigid displacement. Since the strains are computed from the displacements, we need no compatibility equations, so the displacement form is the natural one when displacements are prescribed (SADD, PDF p. 110). Solving three coupled second-order equations is difficult, though. According to SADD, direct integration of the field equations meets great mathematical difficulties, so it is useful only for simple shapes of the body (SADD, PDF p. 115). Later in this chapter we therefore mostly find solutions by partly assuming the form of the field and computing the rest from the equations, and then checking all equations and conditions, as we did above for the hanging bar.
