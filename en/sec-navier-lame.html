Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.2 The Navier-Lamé equations (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe)

In the previous section we wrote down the boundary value problem of elasticity (definition 11.1). Its unknowns are the three components of the displacement, the six components of the strain and the six components of the stress, fifteen functions in all. For them we have fifteen equations: six relations between strains and displacements (chapter 1), six equations of Hooke's law (chapter 3) and three equilibrium equations (chapter 2). Can the fifteen equations be replaced by three, for the three unknown components of the displacement?

The idea is simple. The strains are already written with displacements, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\) (definition 1.121). Hooke's law (equation (3.28)) gives the stresses from the strains, so the stresses are written with displacements as well. The three equilibrium equations are equations for the stresses, so they become three equations for the displacements, and the six strains and six stresses drop out of the calculation. The resulting system has the advantage that we mentioned with the compatibility equations (section 1.18.3): the strains are computed from the displacements, so they fit together by themselves and we need no compatibility equations.

Why do they fit together by themselves? Six strains prescribed arbitrarily may demand something impossible of the fibres. For the strip of section 1.18.3, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) demands that two neighbouring longitudinal fibres bend into the same shape, while \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) demand that the lower fibre is straight and the upper one curved. Both cannot hold at once. Strains computed from one displacement field, however, describe fibres that this displacement really moved, each point to a single place, so they cannot contradict one another. The way from displacements to strains is therefore always possible, while the way back demands that the strains satisfy the compatibility equations.

In the equilibrium equations we differentiate the stresses, and the stresses already contain the first derivatives of the displacements. So the second derivatives of a displacement component with respect to all three coordinates will appear, in the sum \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial z^2\). This sum appears so often in mechanics and physics that it gets a name.

Definition 11.5 Laplace operator (Laplaceov operator)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points of space and \(f\) a function of three variables that has continuous second partial derivatives at every point of \(D\). The Laplace operator (Laplaceov operator) assigns to \(f\) the function whose value at every point of \(D\) is the sum of the three second partial derivatives at that point,

\[\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} . \tag{11.2}\]

For a vector field \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) whose components have continuous second partial derivatives, \(\nabla^2\vek u\) is the vector field with components \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the operator is applied to each component separately. If \(f\) also has continuous fourth partial derivatives, \(\nabla^4 f = \nabla^2(\nabla^2 f)\).

Source: CM (2.4.36) (PDF p. 76), where \(\nabla^2 = \nabla\cdot\nabla\) is called the Laplace operator, SADD (5.4.5) (PDF p. 111), KREYSZIG §9.7 (PDF p. 427).

The operator has several views. The gradient \(\nabla f\) is the column of partial derivatives (chapter 8), and its divergence (definition 4.13) is exactly \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 + \partial^2 f/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 f/\partial z^2\), so \(\nabla^2 f = \operatorname{div}(\nabla f)\). The symbol \(\Delta\), which KREYSZIG gives as another name for the same operator (§9.7, PDF p. 427), is not used in this book, because \(\Delta\) means a difference (\(\Delta L\), \(\Delta T\)). We write \(\nabla^2\). As an example take \(f = x^2 + 3yz\). Along the \(x\) axis, at constant \(y\) and \(z\), this is the parabola \(x^2\), raised by the constant \(3yz\). The second derivatives of \(f\) are \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 = 2\), \(\partial^2 f/\partial y^2 = 0\) and \(\partial^2 f/\partial z^2 = 0\), so \(\nabla^2 f = 2\), the same at every point.

What does the number \(\nabla^2 f\) measure? Let us stay with the parabola \(x^2\). Choose the point \(x\) on the \(x\)-axis and the neighbouring points \(x - h\) and \(x + h\), equally far on both sides. The average of the values at the neighbouring points is the midpoint of the segment between the points of the graph above them. The graph curves upward, so the midpoint lies above the value \(f(x)\), namely by \(h^2\):

\[\frac{(x - h)^2 + (x + h)^2}{2} - x^2 = \frac{2x^2 + 2h^2}{2} - x^2 = h^2 = \frac{h^2}{2} \cdot 2 .\]

So the difference is \(h^2/2\) times the second derivative \(2\). This holds for every function \(g\) of one variable with a continuous second derivative, but only approximately. By Taylor's theorem 8.68, \(g(x + h) = g(x) + g'(x)\,h + \tfrac12 g''(c_+)\,h^2\) with a point \(c_+\) between \(x\) and \(x + h\), and \(g(x - h) = g(x) - g'(x)\,h + \tfrac12 g''(c_-)\,h^2\) with a point \(c_-\) between \(x - h\) and \(x\). The terms with the first derivative cancel in the sum:

\[\frac{g(x - h) + g(x + h)}{2} - g(x) = \frac{h^2}{2} \cdot \frac{g''(c_-) + g''(c_+)}{2} .\]

As \(h\) tends to zero, \(c_-\) and \(c_+\) tend to \(x\), and by continuity the fraction on the right tends to \(g''(x)\). So the second derivative measures by how much the value at a point lags behind the average of its two neighbours, in the unit \(h^2/2\).

In space the point \(P\) has six neighbours at distance \(h\), two on each axis. We write the last equality for each axis separately, add them and divide by \(3\). For small \(h\) the average of the six neighbours is therefore larger than the value at \(P\) by approximately \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}\,\nabla^2 f\). For \(f = x^2 + 3yz\) this holds exactly. The neighbours on the \(x\) axis give the values \((x \pm h)^2 + 3yz\), together \(2h^2\) more than twice the value at \(P\). The neighbours on the \(y\) axis give \(x^2 + 3(y \pm h)\,z\), where \(\pm 3hz\) cancel, and likewise on the \(z\) axis \(\pm 3hy\) cancel. So the average of the six lies \(2h^2/6 = h^2/3 = \tfrac{h^2}{6} \cdot 2\) above the value at \(P\) (SymPy). A positive \(\nabla^2 f\) therefore means that, for small enough \(h\), the value at the point is below the average of its neighbours, a negative one that it is above it.

In the Navier-Lamé equations \(f\) will be a displacement component. When \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) in the whole neighbourhood of a point, only \(\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u\) remains of the force of the stresses per unit volume (theorem 11.6 below). A point that has moved less than the average of its neighbours then gets a force in the direction in which the neighbours moved more, as if they pulled it along. We will see this on the cube in figure 11.22.

Now we can write the equations. The theorem will have four assumptions. For each let us say where it enters, why it is right for our bodies, when it does not hold and what that would change.

  1. The material is the same everywhere, so homogeneous (definition 3.11). The constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) of definition 3.18 are then the same numbers everywhere, so we may leave them in front of the derivative when we differentiate the stresses (step 2 of the proof). A steel piece has the same \(E\) and \(\nu\) everywhere. For a body of two materials, for example a steel bolt in an aluminium plate in figure 11.16, \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) jump at the contact. The shear modulus \(\mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) along the axis \(x\) across the plate and the bolt is \(75{,}4\) GPa in steel, and in aluminium with \(E = 70\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}35\) (BOWER, table 3.1, PDF p. 100, \(E\) from 69 to 79 GPa) it is \(25{,}9\) GPa, almost three times less (NumPy). At the contact \(\mu\) has no derivative, so the equations hold only in each material separately. If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) changed continuously from place to place, differentiating the product would give two more terms in the equation for the component \(\ix{i}\), with the derivatives of \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) with respect to the coordinates, that is, with the slope of such a curve,
    \[\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\operatorname{div}\vek u + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\mu}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\right) ,\]
    which the theorem does not have (SymPy).
  2. The material behaves the same in all directions at every point, so it is isotropic (definition 3.10). Only then do two constants, \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), determine Hooke's law (theorem 3.19). Steel is isotropic because its crystal grains are oriented randomly (SADD, PDF p. 92). A composite of carbon fibres in epoxy, however, behaves like the wood with fibres in the same figure. Along the fibres it has a modulus of elasticity of 205 GPa, across them only 10 GPa (SADD table 11.2, PDF p. 341, section 3.4). For it Hooke's law with two constants, and with it the theorem, does not hold.
  3. \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), so Hooke's law holds in the form (3.28) (step 1 of the proof). In \(\mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)\big)\) we divide by \(1 + \nu\), in \(\lambda = \nu E/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) also by \(1 - 2\nu\). For a material with positive strain energy \(\nu\) lies in the band \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64), so the condition excludes only the edges of the band. How close a material is to the edge \(\tfrac12\) is told by the ratio of the factors in front of the two terms of the equation, \((\lambda + \mu)/\mu = 1/(1 - 2\nu)\) as a function of \(\nu\), since \(\lambda + \mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) (SymPy). For steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(1 - 2\nu = 0{,}4\) and the ratio is \(2{,}5\). For rubber with \(\nu = 0{,}49\) (BOWER, table 3.1, PDF p. 100) \(1 - 2\nu = 0{,}02\), and the factor \(\lambda + \mu\) in front of the term with the divergence is \(50\) times larger than \(\mu\). A very small change of volume decides the stresses there. As \(\nu\) approaches \(\tfrac12\), the ratio grows without bound. For \(\nu = \tfrac12\) the constant \(\lambda\) is not defined and the theorem cannot be applied.
  4. The displacement has continuous second derivatives. The definition of the boundary value problem asks only for continuously differentiable displacements and stresses, while the theorem asks for continuous second derivatives of the displacements, because the stresses, which already contain first derivatives of the displacements, will be differentiated once more (step 1 of the proof). Whether the second follows from the first we do not prove. The solutions that we build in this chapter have continuous second derivatives, because they are written with smooth functions, such as polynomials and powers of the distance from the axis, which we check for every solution. Continuous second derivatives do not exist under a force \(F\) that acts at a single point \(Q\) of the surface, for example under a tip. Take a half-ball of radius \(r\) around \(Q\). The force \(F\) acts on it at \(Q\), and otherwise the flat part of its boundary on the surface is free. For the half-ball to be in equilibrium, the rest of the body must give it an equal and opposite force across the curved face of area \(2\pi r^2\). The average stress vector on this face therefore has magnitude \(F/(2\pi r^2)\), and somewhere on it the stress vector is at least that large. For \(F = 1\) kN this is \(9{,}95\) MPa at \(r = 4\) mm, \(39{,}8\) MPa at \(2\) mm and \(159\) MPa at \(1\) mm (NumPy). Half the radius gives four times the stress, so the stress near \(Q\) does not stay bounded. The weight of a steel half-ball of radius \(4\) mm is only \(0{,}010\) N, \(10^{-5}\) of the force \(F\), and does not change this. The stresses come from the first derivatives of the displacement, so these are not bounded near \(Q\) either, and the displacement has no continuous first derivatives, let alone second ones, on any open set that contains \(Q\). The theorem is then applied on the set \(D\) without the point \(Q\).
Theorem 11.6 Navier-Lamé equations (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe)

Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) (theorem 3.19, \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)). Let \(D\) be an open set of points of the body and \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) a displacement field with continuous second partial derivatives on \(D\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses that we obtain from the displacements \(\vek u\) by definition 1.121 and Hooke's law (3.28), and \(\vek b\) a vector at a point \(P\) of \(D\). Then the stresses \(\ten\sigma\) at \(P\) satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.38) with body force \(\vek b\) exactly when at \(P\) the equation for the displacement alone holds,

\[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b = \vek 0 , \tag{11.3}\]

in components, for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\),

\[\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}\Big) + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 .\]
Proof

We must show that the left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equilibrium equation, when we insert the stresses of the field \(\vek u\), equals the left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equation of the theorem. We write \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\) (definition 1.112).

  1. By definition 1.121, which ties the strains to the displacements, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and the trace is \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial u_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = \operatorname{div}\vek u\) (definition 4.13). The identity matrix \(\mat I\) has the element \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which is \(1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(0\) otherwise. So Hooke's law (3.28), \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\), reads in components
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\right) . \tag{11.4}\]
    Since \(\vek u\) has continuous second derivatives on the open set \(D\), the stresses have continuous first derivatives at every point of \(D\).
  2. The left side of the \(\ix{i}\)-th equilibrium equation (2.38) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) \(+ b_{\mathrm{i}}\). The stress matrix has three rows and three columns. We take column \(\ix{i}\) of the stress matrix, differentiate each of its elements with respect to the coordinate of its row and add the derivatives. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) we insert (11.4) with the indices swapped, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}} = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}} + \mu\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}})\), differentiate with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and sum over \(\ix{j}\). The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives and the constants \(\lambda\), \(\mu\) stay in front of the derivative, so the three terms of the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), differentiated and summed over \(\ix{j}\), give the three terms of the sum in steps 3 to 5.
  3. First term: \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\big(\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\big)/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \lambda\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). In column \(\ix{i}\) the term with \(\lambda\) sits only in the diagonal element, because \(\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\) is zero for \(\ix{j} \neq \ix{i}\). Of the three derivatives only the derivative of the diagonal element with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) remains.
  4. Second term: \(\mu\,\)\(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\) the mixed derivative is just a second derivative with respect to one variable. For \(\ix{j} \neq \ix{i}\) the two mixed second derivatives of the component \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\) are continuous, so by theorem 1.131 on the symmetry of mixed derivatives we may swap them, \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). In both cases the sum over \(\ix{j}\) equals \(\mu\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\dfrac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\).
  5. Third term: \(\mu\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}\) by definition 11.5.
  6. We add steps 3 to 5. The first two results have the same derivative of the divergence, so they merge into one term with the factor \(\lambda + \mu\), and the third stays as it is. We get the left side of the equilibrium equation in displacements:
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\frac{\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  7. The equilibrium equations (2.38) at the point \(P\) are \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\). By step 6 the left side equals the left side of the equation of the theorem. All steps are equalities, so one equation holds exactly when the other does. The three equations together are the vector equation (11.3), because \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) is a vector with components \(\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: SADD (5.4.2)-(5.4.5) (PDF p. 111), CM (7.2.16)-(7.2.17) (PDF p. 299), where CM keeps the acceleration term, zero in equilibrium. SADD writes the body force \(F\) and CM writes \(\rho_0 f_{\mathrm{i}}\), which is our \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\). SADD summarizes the derivation in one sentence, we wrote out the steps.

The proof showed that the equilibrium equations for the stresses of a displacement field are three second-order equations for the displacement itself: we removed the stresses with Hooke's law and the strains with their definition by displacements.

From history

For a long time it did not seem that anyone had tried to obtain general equations of equilibrium and motion of elastic bodies (LOVE, PDF p. 23). At the end of 1820, according to Love, the fruit of all the effort spent on elastic problems could be summed up as an inadequate theory of flexure, an erroneous theory of torsion, an unproved theory of the vibrations of bars and plates and the definition of Young's modulus, and Love at once adds that such an estimate would give a wrong impression of the value of the older researches (LOVE, PDF p. 23). Navier was the first to investigate the general equations of equilibrium and vibration of elastic bodies. His memoir was read in May 1821 and published in 1827 (LOVE, PDF p. 23). He set out from Newton's conception of bodies: he regarded the molecules as material points and assumed that the force between two molecules whose distance is slightly increased is proportional to the product of the increase of the distance and some function of the initial distance. The elastic reactions arose, in his view, from variations of the forces between molecules when the arrangement of the molecules changes (LOVE, PDF pp. 23-24). He expressed all the forces on a displaced molecule and from them obtained the equations of motion of the molecule, and with them the equations for the displacements of the molecule. He assumed the material to be isotropic, and the equations of equilibrium and vibration contained a single constant of the same kind as Young's modulus (LOVE, PDF p. 24).

Navier's memoir was not generally accepted. The objections were aimed at his expression for the force between two molecules and at his replacing triple sums over molecules by integrals, since the validity of this replacement is disputed (LOVE, PDF p. 24). By the autumn of 1822 Cauchy had discovered most of the elements of the pure theory of elasticity: stress at a point, strain at a point, the equilibrium equations and, from the relation between stresses and strains, the equations for the displacements (LOVE, PDF p. 25). In a later published version he obtained the relation for an isotropic material from two assumptions, that it is linear and that the principal planes of stress are normal to the principal axes of strain. He used no hypothesis of material points and central forces. His equations contain two constants, Navier's only one (LOVE, PDF pp. 25-26). Later Cauchy also used the molecular picture with central forces. In the first memoir the equations for an isotropic body contained two constants. In the second memoir he expressed the stresses across any plane, and for an isotropic body without initial stress the stress was expressed through the strain with a single constant, so one of the constants of the first memoir had to vanish. The equations then agreed with Navier's (LOVE, PDF p. 26). Cauchy's derivation from linearity and isotropy thus gave two constants, and the molecular picture with central forces gave one (LOVE, PDF pp. 25-26).

Lamé and Clapeyron wrote a memoir on the internal equilibrium of homogeneous solid bodies in St. Petersburg. Poinsot and Navier reviewed it in 1828 and it was published in 1833 (TIMO, PDF p. 123). The memoir is important because it contains not only a derivation of the equilibrium equations, which Navier and Cauchy already knew, but also their application to practical problems (TIMO, PDF p. 123). In the first part they derived the equations with Navier's molecular forces and showed that Cauchy's stresses give the same ones. A single elastic constant appeared in their equations (TIMO, PDF pp. 123-124). In 1852 Lamé's book on the theory of elasticity appeared, the first book on this subject. The results of the memoir are in it, but the form of the equations is somewhat changed, because Lamé had concluded that two elastic constants determine the properties of an isotropic material. Cauchy had reached this conclusion earlier (TIMO, PDF pp. 124-125).

Let us return to the three equations for the displacements in the form of theorem 11.6, with the two Lamé constants (definition 3.18). What do the terms \(\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) mean? Together they are the force per unit volume that the stresses give to a piece of the body because they vary from place to place. The term \(\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u\) is nonzero when the sum of the second derivatives of a displacement component is nonzero, and the term \((\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) when the volumetric strain \(\operatorname{div}\vek u\) varies from place to place. Let us look at two simple displacements, with a positive constant \(c\), of a small cube with edge \(h\) whose centre has the coordinates \(x > h/2\) and \(z > h/2\), so that the whole cube lies in the region \(x > 0\), \(z > 0\) (figure 11.22).

In the first, the displacement is \(\vek u = (c\,z^2, 0, 0)\). The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) depends only on \(z\), so \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) and the cube only deforms in shear. The strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = c\,z\) and zero elsewhere, so by Hooke's law the only nonzero stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu c\,z\). On the top face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) a force per unit area \(2\mu c\,(z + h/2)\) acts in the direction of the \(x\) axis, on the bottom face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the force is \(-2\mu c\,(z - h/2)\). The total force divided by the volume \(h^3\) is

\[\frac{h^2\,\big[\,2\mu c\,(z + h/2) - 2\mu c\,(z - h/2)\,\big]}{h^3} = 2\mu c = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

because \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial^2(c\,z^2)/\partial z^2 = 2c\). The term with the divergence is zero, because \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\). On the faces perpendicular to \(x\) and \(y\) there is no force in the direction of the \(x\) axis, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The force comes from the shear stresses, which are larger at the top than at the bottom.

In the second, the displacement is \(\vek u = (c\,x^2, 0, 0)\). The cube stretches more on the right than on the left along the \(x\) axis, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2c\,x\) and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 2c\,x\). By Hooke's law \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\cdot 2c\,x + 2\mu\cdot 2c\,x = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,2c\,x\). The stress on the right face is larger than on the left, so the force per unit volume is \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,2c\). The theorem splits it into two parts, \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\mu c\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x = (\lambda + \mu)\,2c\), which together give the same, \(2c\,(\lambda + 2\mu)\). Here both terms are nonzero at once. There are no shear stresses in this case, yet the term \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is nonzero. The split of the force into the two terms of the theorem is therefore not a split into a shear and a volumetric contribution, but a consequence of writing the equations with displacements.

The theorem holds in the interior of the body. On the surface either a displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) is prescribed on a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), which is already a condition on the displacement, or a traction vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) on a part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) (definition 11.1). The second condition is written with stresses. We can write it with displacements too, from the same step 1 as the equations.

Corollary 11.7 Traction condition in terms of displacements (robni pogoj z napetostnim vektorjem v pomikih)

Let the material be as in theorem 11.6. Let \(P\) be a point of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface of the body with unit outward normal \(\vek n\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\) the prescribed traction vector at \(P\). Let the displacement field \(\vek u\) be continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(P\), with displacement gradient \(\mat H\) (definition 1.112). Then the condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) at the point \(P\) holds exactly when

\[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} , \tag{11.5}\]

in components \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \big[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \mu\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\big]\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = \bar t_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Proof

We must show that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) equals the left side of (11.5).

  1. By definition 1.121, \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) and \(\tr\ten\varepsilon = \operatorname{div}\vek u\). Hooke's law (3.28) gives \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\mat I + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\), which is (11.4) written with matrices.
  2. The point \(P\) lies on the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface, the body lies below it, and the unit normal \(\vek n\) points out of the body. We multiply the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) by \(\vek n\) and use \(\mat I\vek n = \vek n\). The part with \(\lambda\) is therefore the vector \(\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n\), parallel to the normal, and the part with \(\mu\) is the vector \(\mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n\). Their sum is \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\), which is the left side of (11.5).
  3. The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) therefore holds exactly when the left side of (11.5) equals \(\bar{\vek t}\). The component \(\ix{i}\) of the vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\), and with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) from (11.4) we obtain the form by components.

Source: CM (7.2.18) (PDF p. 299), SADD (5.2.1), (5.2.2) (PDF pp. 108-109) for the boundary conditions. The matrix form is ours.

Let us test the theorem and the corollary on three fields. The first is the field of the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter 10 mm, with the \(x\) axis pointing down from the centre \(O\) of the top face, is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\) is fixed and a uniform force acts upward on the top face. The body force is the weight, \(\vek b = (\rho g, 0, 0)\) with \(\rho g = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\ \text{N/m}^3\). The field (1.19) of chapter 1 has \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (chapter 1 rounded it to \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\)) and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). Let us examine the terms of equation (11.3) for the component \(x\).

  1. The second derivatives of \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are \(-2a\), \(-0{,}6\,a\) and \(-0{,}6\,a\), the last two being the lateral contraction, so \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = -3{,}2\,a\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are linear in each coordinate, so \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} = \nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).
  2. The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 2a\,(L - x) - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) - 0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) = 0{,}8\,a\,(L - x)\). It depends only on the axis \(x\), so \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = (-0{,}8\,a, 0, 0)\).
  3. With \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa and \(\lambda + \mu = 188{,}46\) GPa (section 3.2.9) the two terms, which come from the stretching and thinning of the bar, are \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = -3{,}2 \cdot 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 75{,}385 \cdot 10^9 = -47\,088\ \text{N/m}^3\) and \((\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x = -0{,}8 \cdot 1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 188{,}46 \cdot 10^9 = -29\,430\ \text{N/m}^3\).
  4. The sum with the weight is \(-47\,088 - 29\,430 + 76\,518 = 0\). The components \(y\) and \(z\) are \(0 + 0 + 0 = 0\), because \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial y = 0\) and \(b_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). The Navier-Lamé equations hold (checked with SymPy, the residual is zero).

Why exactly \(0{,}3\) in the field? If the number \(k\) stood in the field instead of \(0{,}3\), that is \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -2k\,a\,(L - x)\,y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - k\,a\,(y^2 + z^2)\), the residual of the component \(x\) by the theorem would be \(2\nu\rho g\,(k - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), which is zero only for \(k = \nu\) (SymPy). For \(k = 0\), that is for a bar that does not get thinner, the residual would be \(-26\,487\ \text{N/m}^3\), which is 35 % of the weight. Equilibrium itself requires the thinning by the Poisson's ratio of the material.

Let us also examine the boundary condition. For the field \(\mat H + \mat H^\T = \operatorname{diag}(4s, -1{,}2\,s, -1{,}2\,s)\) with \(s = a\,(L - x)\), because \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = -0{,}6\,a\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y = 0\) and the same for \(z\), and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0{,}8\,s\). On the mantle \(\vek n = (0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) and by (11.5)

\[\lambda \cdot 0{,}8\,s\,\vek n + \mu\,(-1{,}2\,s)\,\vek n = s\,(0{,}8 \cdot 113{,}08 - 1{,}2 \cdot 75{,}385)\ \text{GPa}\;\vek n = s\,(90{,}46 - 90{,}46)\ \text{GPa}\;\vek n = \vek 0 .\]

So the mantle is free, as it must be. On the bottom face \(s = 0\) and the traction vector is zero. On the top face \(\vek n = (-1, 0, 0)\) and \(s = aL\), so the traction vector is \(-aL\,(0{,}8\lambda + 4\mu)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Here \(0{,}8\lambda + 4\mu = 392\) GPa \(= 2E\), so the vector equals

\[-1{,}95199 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 392\ \text{GPa}\;\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (-0{,}7652\ \text{MPa},\,0,\,0) = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

Since the \(x\) axis points down, this means a force upward, uniform over the top face, as we described it in chapter 1 (figure 11.25). The area of the top face is \(\pi\,(5\ \text{mm})^2 = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), so the force is \(0{,}7652\ \text{MPa} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 60{,}1\) N, which is the weight of the bar, \(\rho g\,L\,A\) with \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). The field is therefore a solution of the problem with the force prescribed on the whole surface (the traction vector is prescribed everywhere on the surface). Here we only checked the field. We will derive it in section 11.4, where it is also shown that, up to a rigid displacement, it is the only solution of this problem.

The second field is the ball in oil of section 4.3.7 in chapter 4, checked there with the divergence and here with the equations of this chapter. A steel ball of radius \(R = 10\) mm lies in oil at pressure \(p = 100\) MPa. In a fluid at rest the stress is \(-p\,\mat I\) (section 3.2.5), so the traction vector of the fluid on the surface of the ball is \(\bar{\vek t} = -p\,\vek n\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward normal. The weight of the ball is not important at this pressure, \(\rho g R = 0{,}77\) kPa, which is \(8 \cdot 10^{-6}\) of the pressure, so we take \(\vek b = \vek 0\). For the cube in water Hooke's law gave \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -(1 - 2\nu)\,p/E\) (section 3.2.5). Let us try the same for the ball, that is the displacement \(\vek u = \varepsilon_0\,(x, y, z)\) with the origin at the centre and \(\varepsilon_0 = -(1 - 2\nu)\,p/E = -0{,}4 \cdot 100/196\,000 = -2{,}0408 \cdot 10^{-4}\).

All components are linear, so \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 3\varepsilon_0\) is constant and \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\). The Navier-Lamé equations hold with \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The boundary condition: \(\mat H = \varepsilon_0\,\mat I\), so the left side of (11.5) equals \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_0\,\vek n = 490\ \text{GPa} \cdot (-2{,}0408 \cdot 10^{-4})\,\vek n = -100\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n = -p\,\vek n\). The field is a solution, the ball shrinks. The radial displacement of its edge is \(\varepsilon_0 R = -2{,}041\ \mu\text{m}\), so the edge moves \(2{,}041\ \mu\text{m}\) towards the centre. The solution is unique up to a rigid displacement (theorem 11.4), since this is a traction problem.

The third field is uniform. The shifted cube of section 1.6.2 has \(\vek u = (0{,}001\,z, 0, 0)\). The \(x\) and \(z\) axes lie in the plane of the picture, the displacement runs along the \(x\)-axis and grows with the height \(z\). The components are linear, so \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) and \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\), and the equations hold with \(\vek b = \vek 0\). The stress is the same everywhere, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \mu \cdot 0{,}001 = 75{,}38\) MPa, as we computed in chapter 3. The same holds for every field \(\vek u = \vek c + \mat A\,\vek x\) with a constant vector \(\vek c\) and a constant matrix \(\mat A\): the second derivatives are zero and the divergence is constant, so such a field is in equilibrium without body force.

Let us collect what we have. The unknowns are only the three components of the displacement. The three equations (11.3) are coupled, because the term \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\) contains all three components in every equation. Together with the boundary conditions, the displacement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and (11.5) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), they form the displacement form of the boundary value problem. Let the field \(\vek u\) have continuous second derivatives on an open set that contains the body, let it satisfy (11.3) at every interior point, let \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\) hold on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the condition (11.5) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). By theorem 11.6 and corollary 11.7 the stresses of the field are in equilibrium and satisfy the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), so \(\vek u\) is a solution of the boundary value problem by definition 11.1. By theorem 11.4 (for steel the condition of the theorem on positive energy holds, theorem 3.64) it is the only solution if \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) prevents every small rigid displacement, and otherwise unique up to a rigid displacement. Since the strains are computed from the displacements, we need no compatibility equations. The condition on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is written directly in the unknowns, while the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) uses their derivatives (equation (11.5)). The displacement form is therefore most useful when only displacements are prescribed (SADD, PDF p. 110). Solving three coupled second-order equations is difficult, though. According to SADD, direct integration of the field equations meets great mathematical difficulties, so it is useful only for simple shapes of the body (SADD, PDF p. 115). Later in this chapter we therefore mostly find solutions by partly assuming the form of the field and computing the rest from the equations, and then checking all equations and conditions, as we did above for the hanging bar.
