Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.1 Heat flow and Fourier's law

A spoon left in hot soup cannot be held by its handle after a few minutes, although the soup never reaches the handle. The end in the soup is hot, the end in the hand cool, and in time the hand warms too. Something has moved along the metal. By which path, how fast and how much? In chapters 3 and 5 we took the temperature of a body as known, for instance a cantilever whose upper edge is 20 K warmer than its lower edge. Here we shall compute it from how the body is heated at its boundary. The first step is the question about the spoon: what flows, and what decides how much.

From history

For fourteen years Fourier was prefect of Grenoble, and in that time he carried out elaborate investigations on the propagation of heat in solid bodies, published in 1822 in La Théorie analytique de la chaleur (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). Cajori calls this work the source of all modern methods of mathematical physics in which partial differential equations are integrated for fixed boundary values (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). That is why we return to him in this chapter: we too shall have a body, on its surface a temperature or a heat flow will be given, and in the interior we shall look for the temperature field. The law that connects the flow of heat with the temperature difference carries his name in the books (CM §6.7.2, PDF p. 281, SADD §12.1, PDF p. 378). The story of Fourier series and of the dispute over them was told in section 9.11.

Back to the spoon. To compute, we first have to name three things: the temperature, the heat, and the heat that flows through a surface.

Definition 14.1 Temperature field and heat (temperaturno polje in toplota)

The temperature (temperatura) of a point of a body is the number a thermometer shows when it comes into equilibrium with the piece of the body at that point. It is measured in kelvins (K). In degrees Celsius (°C) a temperature difference is the same as in kelvins, so differences may be measured in either unit (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469). The temperature field (temperaturno polje) of a body is the function \(T\) that assigns to a point \(P\) of the body at time \(t\) the temperature \(T(P, t)\), a number. When time does not change we do not write it. Heat (toplota) is energy that, because of a temperature difference, passes from one part of a body to another or from one body to another (CM §6.7.1, PDF p. 280). The unit of heat is the joule (J), the unit of power, that is heat per second, is the watt, \(1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}\) (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469).

Source: COOK §12.1 (PDF p. 469) for the units, CM §6.7.1 (PDF p. 280) for heat.

The Slovenian word for heat, toplota, comes from topel, warm. A field is a rule that assigns a value to every point (definition 1.23 for displacements), so the temperature field is the temperature at every point. As every quantity in a continuum, the temperature of a point means the temperature of a piece around the point that is large compared with the grains of the material and small compared with the body (definition 1.1). The beam of chapter 5 also has a temperature field, there written \(\theta\) as a change of temperature. Here we write \(T\), as in chapter 3. In chapter 13 \(T\) is the tension in a string, in this chapter it is always the temperature.

Heat is transferred in three ways: by conduction, by the motion of a fluid that carries it, and by radiation (CM §6.7.1, PDF pp. 280-281). Conduction (prevajanje) is the transfer of heat inside a material because of temperature differences between neighbouring points (CM §6.7.1, PDF p. 280). The name says what happens: heat is led through the material from point to point. The transfer by the motion of a fluid is called convection (konvekcija) and we shall meet it in this chapter only at the boundary of the body, where a solid touches a fluid. Radiation (sevanje) is the transfer by electromagnetic waves, which needs no material, and it follows a law in which the fourth power of the temperature appears (CM §6.7.1, PDF p. 281). Because of the fourth power the problem is no longer linear (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469), so we only name radiation in this chapter and do not compute it. The chapter treats conduction in a solid body at rest.

14.1.1 A slab between two temperatures

First let us see what an experiment shows on the simplest body. We use only what the reader knows from mechanics: that energy is conserved and that for two equal parts side by side the effects add.

We take a slab of one material. One of its faces is held at the temperature \(T_1\), for instance by a flame, the other at a lower temperature \(T_2\), for instance by ice. The thickness of the slab is \(\ell\) and the area of each face is \(A\). The slab is wide, so that we neglect its side edges: at \(\ell = 40\) mm and an area of \(1\) m² the ratio is \(\ell/\sqrt{A} = 0{,}04\), an estimate of the order of magnitude, not a calculation. We wait until the temperatures in the slab stop changing. This is the steady state (ustaljeno stanje). In it the slab stores no energy: if more heat entered it than left it, its energy would grow by the conservation of energy (CM §5.4.2, PDF p. 236) and the temperature would not stay still. So in the steady state the same heat per second flows through every cross section of the slab. We call it \(\dot Q\), its unit is W.

Measurements show that for a given slab \(\dot Q\) is proportional to the difference \(T_1 - T_2\). Most materials satisfy this approximately, and the nonlinearity shows only for a large range of temperatures (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 235). From this and from the conservation of energy it follows how \(\dot Q\) depends on \(A\) and on \(\ell\).

  1. Area. Two equal slabs of area \(A\), laid one above the other between the same faces, are a slab of area \(2A\). \(\dot Q\) flows through each, \(2\dot Q\) through both together, because heats per second add like energies. For the same temperature difference \(\dot Q\) is therefore proportional to \(A\). We write \(\dot Q = A\,c(\ell)\,(T_1 - T_2)\), where \(c(\ell)\) is a number that for a given material depends only on the thickness.
  2. Thickness. A slab of thickness \(2\ell\) is two equal slabs of thickness \(\ell\), one behind the other. Let the temperature of the joint be \(T_{\mathrm{m}}\) and the total thickness \(2\ell\). In the steady state the same \(\dot Q\) flows through both slabs, so \(A\,c(\ell)\,(T_1 - T_{\mathrm{m}}) = A\,c(\ell)\,(T_{\mathrm{m}} - T_2)\) and \(T_{\mathrm{m}} = (T_1 + T_2)/2\). Each slab therefore has half of the temperature difference. For the whole slab \(A\,c(2\ell)\,(T_1 - T_2) = A\,c(\ell)\,(T_1 - T_{\mathrm{m}}) = A\,c(\ell)\,(T_1 - T_2)/2\), so \(c(2\ell) = c(\ell)/2\).
  3. The same argument for \(n\) equal slabs, one behind the other, gives \(c(n\ell) = c(\ell)/n\). The product \(\ell\,c(\ell)\) is therefore the same for all thicknesses that are whole multiples of one thickness, and we denote it by \(k\). For other thicknesses we assume it from measurements.

The result for the slab of figure 14.1 is

\[\dot Q = k\,A\,\frac{T_1 - T_2}{\ell} , \qquad k = \frac{\dot Q\,\ell}{A\,(T_1 - T_2)} . \tag{14.1}\]

The number \(k\) is a property of the material and is called the thermal conductivity (toplotna prevodnost), because it tells how well the material conducts: the larger it is, the more heat flows for the same temperature difference. Its unit is W/(m K). For real substances it lies between \(0{,}01\) and \(300\) W/(m K) (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469). For the cantilever of chapter 5 (\(\ell = 40\) mm, \(T_1 - T_2 = 20\) K) we get \(\dot Q/A = k \cdot 20/0{,}040 = 500\,k\) W/m², if \(k\) is in W/(m K). For a slab of the same faces and twice the thickness it is \(250\,k\) W/m², and each of two 40 mm slabs in series has \(20/2 = 10\) K and \(10/0{,}040 = 250\) K/m, so also \(250\,k\) W/m². The books of the project give no value of \(k\) for steel, so we leave the computations dependent on \(k\).

The slab has one direction, the flow is perpendicular to its faces. In a body of any shape heat flows in different directions and through surfaces of any position, so a single \(\dot Q\) is not enough.

14.1.2 Heat flux

Take again the cantilever of chapter 5, now in the steady state.

We draw the block of its longitudinal section, with the axes \(x\) along and \(y\) up. The upper edge is 20 K warmer than the lower one, and in chapter 5 we took the temperature in the section linear, \(T = T_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) with \(g = 20/0{,}040 = 500\) K/m. The surfaces of equal temperature are here horizontal planes, in the section lines, drawn at \(0\), \(10\) and \(20\) K above the lower edge. A surface of equal temperature is called an isotherm (izoterma), from the Greek words for equal and heat. The temperature grows upwards, so the temperature gradient, the column of partial derivatives (definition 8.22, item 3), has the components \(\nabla T = (0,\ 500,\ 0)\) K/m. Heat flows toward the cooler side, that is downwards, and the arrows \(\vek q\) point down. We define the vector \(\vek q\) as follows.

Definition 14.2 Heat flux and heat flow (gostota toplotnega toka in toplotni tok)

Let \(T\) be the temperature field of a body, such as figure 14.2 shows. The heat flux (gostota toplotnega toka) is a field of vectors \(\vek q(P, t)\) with unit W/m² such that: through every surface \(S\) the book knows (a piece of the surface of a body between two graphs by definition 4.36, or a surface described by two parameters, definition 4.61), with a chosen unit normal \(\vek n\), heat flows at the rate

\[\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek q\cdot\vek n\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{14.2}\]

that is the flux of the field \(\vek q\) through \(S\). The number \(\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}\) is the heat flow through the surface (toplotni tok skozi ploskev), unit W. It is positive when heat flows in the direction of the normal \(\vek n\). Through the surface of a body with the outward normal \(\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}\) is the heat that leaves the body per second, and \(-\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}\) enters it.

Source: CM §5.4.2 (PDF p. 237): \(\vek q\) is the heat flux vector, the heat entering through a surface with the outward normal \(\hat{\vek n}\) is \(-\vek q\cdot\hat{\vek n}\). COOK §12.1 (PDF p. 470): the flux is a vector quantity that transforms like a displacement. SADD §12.1 (PDF p. 378).

The name density is as in the density of mass: a quantity per unit, here heat per second and per unit area, which each arrow \(\vek q\) shows. The letters \(q\) and \(k\) already have other meanings in the book: \(q\) is the load per length in chapter 5, \(k\) is the shear factor there and the spring stiffness in chapter 9. Since they never meet in the same calculation, we tell them apart by context: in this chapter \(\vek q\) is always the heat flux and \(k\) the thermal conductivity. That a vector field describes heat is an assumption of the model, introduced in the same way by CM (§5.4.2, PDF p. 237). As the stress vector (theorem 2.24) tells what the surroundings transmit through a surface, \(\vek q\) tells how much heat flows through it.

We test the definition on the cantilever, where \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T = (0,\ -500\,k,\ 0)\) W/m². On three patches of area \(1\ \text{cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\) the vector \(\vek q\) is the same everywhere, so \(\Phi_{\mathrm{S}} = \vek q\cdot\vek n\,A\) exactly. The horizontal patch with the normal \(\vek n_1 = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) has \(\vek q\cdot\vek n_1 = -500\,k\) and \(\Phi = -0{,}05\,k\) W. The sign is minus because heat flows against the normal, downwards. The vertical patch with \(\vek n_2 = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) has \(\vek q\cdot\vek n_2 = 0\) and \(\Phi = 0\): heat flows parallel to it and does not cross it. The inclined patch with the normal \(\vek n_3 = (\sin 60^\circ,\ \cos 60^\circ,\ 0)\) has \(\vek q\cdot\vek n_3 = -250\,k\) and \(\Phi = -0{,}025\,k\) W. Through a horizontal section of the beam, 1 m long and 20 mm wide, so of area \(0{,}02\ \text{m}^2\), \(10\,k\) W flows downwards. For a piece of the cantilever 0,1 m long, bounded above and below by faces of area \(0{,}1 \cdot 0{,}02 = 0{,}002\ \text{m}^2\), we get \(\Phi = -1{,}0\,k\) W through the upper face (normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), heat enters) and \(+1{,}0\,k\) W through the lower face (normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), heat leaves). The side faces contribute nothing, the sum is zero: as much heat enters the piece as leaves it, which the steady state requires.

14.1.3 Fourier's law

How is \(\vek q\) connected with the temperature field? The slab of figure 14.1 and equation (14.1) is the answer in one case. Let the axis \(x\) be perpendicular to the faces, \(T(0) = T_1\) and \(T(\ell) = T_2\). The flux per unit area in the direction \(x\) is \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = \dot Q/A = k\,(T_1 - T_2)/\ell = -k\,(T_2 - T_1)/\ell\). The number \((T_2 - T_1)/\ell\) is the mean derivative of \(T\) with respect to \(x\) across the slab. That the derivative is the same everywhere follows from the law (14.1) applied to parts of the slab, as for the two slabs one behind the other. Since the slab is wide, every plane parallel to the faces has a single temperature, at the depth \(x\) the temperature \(T(x)\). Take two depths \(0 \le x_1 < x_2 \le \ell\). The part of the slab between them is a slab of thickness \(x_2 - x_1\) with faces at the temperatures \(T(x_1)\) and \(T(x_2)\). In the steady state the same \(\dot Q\) flows through it as through every cross section. By equation (14.1), \(\dot Q = k\,A\,\big(T(x_1) - T(x_2)\big)/(x_2 - x_1)\), so

\[\frac{T(x_2) - T(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = -\frac{\dot Q}{k\,A} \qquad \text{for any depths } x_1 < x_2 .\]

The difference quotient is the same number for every pair of depths, so \(T\) is a linear function of \(x\) and its derivative is everywhere \(\partial T/\partial x = -\dot Q/(k\,A) = (T_2 - T_1)/\ell\). So \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = \dot Q/A = -k\,\partial T/\partial x\).

For a body of any shape, for instance the cantilever in figure 14.2, we need three more answers: in which direction \(\vek q\) points, how large it is and which sign it has. The answer for the direction is an argument that is not from a source. Let the material be isotropic also for conduction: slabs cut from the neighbourhood of a point in any direction give the same \(k\) (as in definition 3.4, only that we measure conductivity). We also assume that \(\vek q(P)\) depends only on the material and on the gradient \(\nabla T(P)\) at the same point, not on the temperatures elsewhere. This is how CM (§6.7.2, PDF p. 281) writes the law, and SADD calls it the constitutive relation between \(\vek q\) and \(\nabla T\) (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 378). The slab agrees with this, since its \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) depends only on \(\partial T/\partial x\). Take a point \(P\) with \(\nabla T(P) \ne \vek 0\). The only direction that is distinguished at \(P\) is the direction of \(\nabla T(P)\), because the material is the same in all directions. Turn the body together with its temperature field about the line through \(P\) in the direction of \(\nabla T(P)\). The material stays the same, because it is isotropic. The gradient at \(P\) stays the same, because it lies on the axis of the rotation. The temperatures at other points do move, but by the assumption they have no influence on \(\vek q(P)\). So the turned body has the same flux at \(P\) as the original one. This flux is also the original \(\vek q(P)\) turned together with the body, so \(\vek q(P)\) does not change under the rotation. A vector that stays the same under every rotation about a line lies on that line. So \(\vek q(P)\) is parallel to \(\nabla T(P)\). For \(\nabla T(P) = \vek 0\) the same argument holds for the rotation about every line through \(P\), so \(\vek q(P)\) lies on each of them and \(\vek q(P) = \vek 0\). The size: a slab with \(\nabla T = (T_2 - T_1)/\ell\ \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) gives \(|\vek q| = k\,|\nabla T|\), and since the material is the same in all directions, \(k\) is the same for every direction. The sign: heat flows from warm to cold (CM §6.7.2, PDF p. 281, SADD §12.1, PDF p. 378), \(\vek q\) points toward falling \(T\), that is toward \(-\nabla T\).

Definition 14.3 Fourier's law and thermal conductivity (Fourierjev zakon in toplotna prevodnost)

Let the body be of an isotropic material (by the argument above, also for conduction, figure 14.2) and let the temperature field \(T\) be continuously differentiable (definition 1.90). Fourier's law (Fourierjev zakon) says that the heat flux is

\[\vek q = -k\,\nabla T , \tag{14.3}\]

where the thermal conductivity (toplotna prevodnost) \(k > 0\) is a number with unit W/(m K), a property of the material. In this chapter \(k\) is constant over the body and independent of the temperature, which is an idealization: for a large range of temperatures the problem becomes nonlinear (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 235, COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469).

Source: CM (6.7.1) (PDF p. 281), where \(k\) is a tensor of second order, SADD (12.1.1), (12.1.2) (PDF p. 378), BARBER (14.14) (PDF p. 235), COOK §12.1 (PDF p. 470).

The law is not a theorem. It is a model that measurements support, like Hooke's law in chapter 3. It contains three statements that we have explained: direction, size and sign. On the slab it agrees with equation (14.1). On the cantilever \(\vek q = -k\,(0,\ 500,\ 0) = (0,\ -500\,k,\ 0)\), as the gradient pointing up and the flux pointing down show. If \(k\) depended on the temperature the problem would be nonlinear, which the chapter does not treat (COOK §12.1, PDF p. 469). What actually carries heat in a metal or a crystal, at the level of atoms, is in no book of the project, so we do not justify the law by a mechanism and take it from measurements (BARBER §14.2, PDF p. 235).

14.1.4 Direction of the flux against the isotherms

What does the law say about the path of heat? For the cantilever the answer is in figure 14.2: the isotherm is horizontal, \(\vek q\) points vertically down, so perpendicular to it. We check this for a general field. First numbers. The rate of change of \(T\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek d\) that makes the angle \(\varphi\) with \(\nabla T\) is \(\nabla T\cdot\vek d\). For \(\nabla T = (0,\ 500,\ 0)\) K/m this is \(500\cos\varphi\) K/m: \(500\) at \(\varphi = 0^\circ\) (\(\vek d\) along the gradient, upwards), \(433\) at \(30^\circ\), \(250\) at \(60^\circ\), \(0\) at \(90^\circ\) (horizontal, along the isotherm) and \(-500\) at \(180^\circ\) (downwards, along \(\vek q\)). The temperature falls fastest along \(\vek q\), along the isotherm it does not change at all.

Lemma 14.4 Heat flows from warm to cold and perpendicular to the isotherms (tok teče od toplega k hladnemu in pravokotno na izoterme)

Let (figure 14.2) \(D\) be an open set of points of the body, \(T\) continuously differentiable on \(D\) (definition 1.90), \(k > 0\), \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\), and \(P\) a point of \(D\) with \(\nabla T(P) \ne \vek 0\). The isotherm (izoterma) through \(P\) is the set of points of \(D\) at which the temperature equals \(T(P)\).

  1. For a unit vector \(\vek d\) the rate of change of \(T\) in the direction \(\vek d\) is the number \(\nabla T(P)\cdot\vek d\) (figure 14.2: largest along the gradient). It is largest at \(\vek d = \nabla T/|\nabla T|\), smallest at \(\vek d = -\nabla T/|\nabla T| = \vek q/|\vek q|\), and these are the only vectors with this property.
  2. \(\vek q(P)\cdot\nabla T(P) = -k\,|\nabla T(P)|^2 < 0\), the flux and the gradient point to opposite sides.
  3. For every continuously differentiable curve \(\vek c\), defined on an open interval that contains \(0\), with \(\vek c(0) = P\) that lies in the isotherm through \(P\), \(\vek q(P)\cdot\vek c'(0) = 0\). The isotherm has such curves in two independent directions.
Proof

On the cantilever (figure 14.2) we see all three items. The first two items we prove with the derivative along a segment and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the third with the chain rule.

  1. Let \(\vek d\) be a unit vector, in figure 14.2 for instance the horizontal normal, and \(g(t) = T(P + t\,\vek d)\). By lemma 1.132 on the derivative along a segment \(g\) is differentiable at \(t = 0\) and \(g'(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}(P)\,d_{\mathrm{j}} = \nabla T(P)\cdot\vek d\). This is the rate of change of \(T\) in the direction \(\vek d\).
  2. By lemma 6.19 (in figure 14.2: no direction gives more than along the gradient) for the scalar product (definition 2.8), \(|\nabla T\cdot\vek d| \le |\nabla T|\,|\vek d| = |\nabla T|\), so the rate of change is at most \(|\nabla T|\) in absolute value. Equality holds only when \(\vek d\) is a multiple of \(\nabla T\), that is at \(\vek d = \pm\nabla T/|\nabla T|\).
  3. At \(\vek d = \nabla T/|\nabla T|\), in figure 14.2 upwards, \(\nabla T\cdot\vek d = |\nabla T|^2/|\nabla T| = |\nabla T|\), the upper bound, and at \(\vek d = -\nabla T/|\nabla T|\) it is \(-|\nabla T|\), the lower bound. Since \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\) and \(k > 0\), \(\vek q/|\vek q| = -\nabla T/|\nabla T|\). This proves item 1.
  4. The gradient and the flux in figure 14.2: \(\vek q\cdot\nabla T = -k\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla T = -k\,|\nabla T|^2\) by definition 2.8. The sum of squares of the components is positive because \(\nabla T(P) \ne \vek 0\). This proves item 2.
  5. Let \(\vek c\) be a curve in the isotherm (in figure 14.2 this is the horizontal line) and \(h(s) = T(\vek c(s))\). Since \(T(\vek c(s)) = T(P)\) for every \(s\), \(h\) is constant and \(h'(0) = 0\).
  6. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43, with an interval in place of \(D\)), for the curve in figure 14.2, \(h'(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\,c_{\mathrm{i}}'(0) = \nabla T(P)\cdot\vek c'(0)\). So \(\nabla T(P)\cdot\vek c'(0) = 0\), and \(\vek q(P)\cdot\vek c'(0) = -k\cdot 0 = 0\).
  7. Curves in the isotherm (in figure 14.2 the horizontal line in the section and the one in the direction \(z\)): since \(\nabla T(P) \ne \vek 0\), one partial component is nonzero, after renaming the coordinates \(\partial T/\partial z(P) \ne 0\). By theorem 8.34 (\(N = 3\), \(m = 1\)) the isotherm near \(P\) is the graph of a continuously differentiable function \(z = \gamma(x, y)\), and the curves \(s \mapsto (x_0 + s,\ y_0,\ \gamma(x_0 + s, y_0))\) and \(s \mapsto (x_0,\ y_0 + s,\ \gamma(x_0, y_0 + s))\) lie in the isotherm and have independent tangents. This proves item 3.

Source: The lemma is not from a source. The direction against the gradient is stated by CM §6.7.2 (PDF p. 281) and SADD §12.1 (PDF p. 378).

The lemma has shown why the direction of the flux makes sense at all: heat does not flow in an arbitrary direction but where the temperature falls fastest, and an isotherm does not conduct heat, because along it the temperature does not change. On the cantilever this agrees with the figure, \(\vek q\) is perpendicular to the isotherm and points opposite to the gradient.

14.1.5 A layered material where this fails

The lemma holds for Fourier's law in the form (14.3), that is for an isotropic material. What happens when the material is not isotropic? An example is a layered material (figure 14.3), for instance wood with fibres or a plate of glued layers, which has another conductivity along the layers than across them. It has two perpendicular principal directions, \(r\) along the layers and \(s\) across them, so COOK calls it orthotropic (ortotropen). For it COOK (§12.1, equation (12.1-2), PDF p. 470) writes \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial T/\partial r\) and \(q_{\mathrm{s}} = -k_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial T/\partial s\) with two conductivities \(k_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\). We compute both conclusions of the lemma as before, but with \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 3\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\) (the ratio is chosen for illustration, not from a source) and a gradient of size \(100\) K/m at \(45^\circ\) to the layers.

Example 14.5 Layered material, the flux is not perpendicular to the isotherms (plastni material, tok ni pravokoten na izoterme)

Goal: for the layered material of figure 14.3 with \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 3\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\), compute \(\vek q\) at a point where the gradient is at \(45^\circ\) to the layers, and check items 2 and 3 of lemma 14.4.

  1. We draw the layers with the axes \(r\) and \(s\) and the point \(P\). The gradient has the components \(\partial T/\partial r = \partial T/\partial s = 100\cos 45^\circ = 70{,}71\) K/m, it points at \(45^\circ\). The isotherm is perpendicular to it, at \(135^\circ\), since by step 6 of the proof of lemma 14.4 \(\nabla T\cdot\vek c' = 0\) for the tangent of every curve in an isotherm, for every function \(T\).
  2. By COOK (components of the flux) \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k_{\mathrm{r}} \cdot 70{,}71 = -212{,}13\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\) W/m² and \(q_{\mathrm{s}} = -k_{\mathrm{s}} \cdot 70{,}71 = -70{,}71\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\) W/m².
  3. Direction of the flux: \(\arctan(70{,}71/212{,}13) = 18{,}43^\circ\) below the layers toward \(-r\), so at \(198{,}43^\circ\) from the axis \(r\). The direction of \(-\nabla T\), which the flux would have in an isotropic material, is \(225^\circ\). The angle between them is \(225^\circ - 198{,}43^\circ = 26{,}57^\circ\). The flux is turned toward the layers, because the layers conduct three times better.
  4. Item 2 of the lemma, the gradient and the flux: \(\vek q\cdot\nabla T = q_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial T/\partial r + q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial T/\partial s = (-212{,}13 - 70{,}71)\cdot 70{,}71\,k_{\mathrm{s}} = -20\,000\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\) (W/m²)(K/m). The sum is negative because \(k_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) are positive, so heat still flows toward the cooler side. The form \(-k\,|\nabla T|^2\) does not hold here: with \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) it would give \(-10\,000\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\), with \(k_{\mathrm{r}}\) it would give \(-30\,000\,k_{\mathrm{s}}\), the true value lies between.
  5. Item 3: the unit tangent vector of the isotherm is \(\vek t = (-0{,}7071,\ 0{,}7071)\). Then \(\vek q\cdot\vek t = (-212{,}13)(-0{,}7071) + (-70{,}71)(0{,}7071) = 150 - 50 = 100\,k_{\mathrm{s}} \ne 0\). The flux is not perpendicular to the isotherm.
  6. Why (the layers are another direction): the argument about the direction, by which \(\vek q\) is parallel to \(\nabla T\), used that the material is the same in all directions. The layers give another direction in which the material differs, so a rotation about \(\nabla T\) does not leave the material unchanged. At \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = k_{\mathrm{s}}\) the angle is \(\varphi = 0\) and both conclusions hold again.

Source: COOK §12.1, equation (12.1-2) and figure 12.1-2 (PDF p. 470). The numbers are our computation.

The layered material of figure 14.3 shows which part of the lemma is firm and which is not. The sign of the flux stays: heat flows toward the cooler side here too. The direction of the flux as the direction of fastest fall, and the perpendicularity to the isotherms, however, need isotropy. We wrote Fourier's law for an isotropic material with the number \(k\) (definition 14.3). For a general material CM writes a tensor \(k_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of order two (CM §6.7.2, PDF p. 281), and SADD states that this tensor is symmetric (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 378). For the layered material this is the matrix with \(k_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) on the diagonal in the axes \(r\), \(s\).

So we have the answer to the question about the spoon (figure 14.2). Heat flows through the metal, its density is \(\vek q = -k\,\nabla T\), it points from warm to cold and perpendicular to the surfaces of equal temperature, and how fast is decided by \(k\). What remains is what happens to the temperature when heat enters a piece of the body differently from how it leaves: the piece warms or cools, and how much is told by a quantity called the specific heat. That is the topic of the next section.
