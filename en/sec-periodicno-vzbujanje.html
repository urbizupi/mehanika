Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.12 Periodic excitation

The cantilever with the weight (system A of section 9.1) has mass \(m = 50\) kg, stiffness \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, natural circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, natural period \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}17740\) s and damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}01\). A press acts on the coordinate \(q\) with a force that is on for half of the period and off for the other half (square wave, kvadratna sila):

\[Q(t) = \begin{cases} F_0 & 0 < t < T_{\mathrm{p}}/2 \\ 0 & T_{\mathrm{p}}/2 < t < T_{\mathrm{p}} \end{cases} , \qquad Q(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}) = Q(t) , \qquad F_0 = 20\ \text{N} .\]

We chose the force independently of the motion of the cantilever, as an idealization. A real press would respond somewhat to the motion of the cantilever. The period of the force is three times the natural period, \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}53221\) s, and its circular frequency is \(\Omega = 2\pi/T_{\mathrm{p}} = 11{,}8058\) 1/s, that is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/3\) or \(1{,}879\) Hz. The question is twofold. To which harmonics of the force does system A respond, and to which hardly at all? And is the response to the force really the sum of the responses to its harmonics?

We already have part of the answer. By section 9.11 the square wave force is the sum of a constant value and sines of the odd harmonics (harmonike), that is, of terms that oscillate \(n\) times faster than the force,

\[Q(t) = \frac{F_0}{2} + \sum_{\mathrm{n} = 1,3,5,\dots} \frac{2F_0}{n\pi}\,\sin n\Omega t .\]

The equality holds everywhere except at the jumps, where the sum is \(F_0/2\), the mean of the one-sided limits (theorem 9.94). There are no even harmonics because \(Q(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}/2) = F_0 - Q(t)\). By theorem 9.52 on the response to a harmonic force each term with circular frequency \(n\Omega\) gives a steady state that is larger than the term by the factor \(|H(n\Omega)|\) and lags behind it by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\). The equation is linear (lemma 9.6), so the response to a finite sum is the sum of the responses. The series, however, has infinitely many terms. For the sum of responses to really solve the equation, we would have to differentiate the sum twice term by term. This is doubtful precisely for the square wave. The \(n\)-th term of the series for the acceleration has the coefficient \(-n^2\Omega^2 H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\), which for large \(n\) is approximately \(c_{\mathrm{n}}/m \sim 1/n\), and such a series does not converge absolutely. The true acceleration is not continuous either, because \(\ddot q\) jumps by \(F_0/m = 0{,}4\) m/s\(^2\) when the force jumps. So we will build the response differently, without differentiating the series, and only then compute its series.

The idea is simple. The force has been acting forever. We split the age \(u \ge 0\) of the force into pieces of length \(\Delta u\) with left endpoints \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\). The force that acted in the piece of age near \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), that is at a time near \(t - u_{\mathrm{i}}\), gives approximately the impulse \(Q(t - u_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta u\) (definition 9.71). Its response at time \(t\), after the time \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), is approximately \(h(u_{\mathrm{i}})\,Q(t - u_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta u\), where \(h\) is the impulse response (definition 9.74, section 9.10). The sum of the responses is a Riemann sum of the function \(h(u)\,Q(t - u)\), and its limit is the integral. Theorem 9.79 proves this precisely, and the response at time \(t\) is

\[q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \int_0^\infty h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u . \tag{9.84}\]

The age \(u\) of the force goes to infinity, so the integral is an integral to infinity (definition 9.16) and exists only if \(h\) decays fast enough. For system A it decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\), so after \(13{,}00\) s (that is, \(24{,}4\) periods of the force) its envelope is below one percent and an older force hardly counts. The theorem says that this really is the steady state, and gives everything we need.

Theorem 9.98 Periodic response (periodični odziv)

Let \(m > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\) be real numbers, \(h\) the impulse response (definition 9.74) and \(Q\) a piecewise continuous function on \(\mathbb{R}\) (on every closed interval, definition 6.8) with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\), that is \(Q(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}) = Q(t)\). Let \(\Omega = 2\pi/T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and let \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(n\) an integer) be the complex Fourier coefficients of the force \(Q\) (lemma 9.97). The function \(H(\Omega) = 1/P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)\) is the one from definition 9.51, written with the same formula for every real number \(\Omega\), also negative. Then:

  1. The integral (9.84) exists for every \(t\). The function \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is periodic with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\), continuously differentiable and solves the equation (\(\ddot q_{\mathrm{p}}\) exists everywhere except at the points where the force jumps). For every \(t_0\), \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) on \([t_0, \infty)\) is the solution from theorem 9.79 with the initial values \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\) and \(\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\).
  2. Every solution of the equation differs from \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) by a free motion that tends to zero, for \(\zeta < 1\) like \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\).
  3. The complex Fourier coefficients of the function \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) are exactly \(H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  4. The Fourier series of the function \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) converges absolutely and uniformly to \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\), so
    \[q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} = -\infty}^{\infty} H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t} . \tag{9.85}\]
Proof

We must show four points. First we estimate \(h\) so that the integral exists. Then, with the theorem on the Duhamel integral, we show that \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is a solution. We obtain the coefficients by integrating the equation over one period.

1. Existence and solution.

  1. Let \(0 < \zeta < 1\). Then \(h(u) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} u/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) and \(\dot h(u) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} u - \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} u)/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\). Since \(|\sin| \le 1\) and \(|\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\cos x - \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sin x| \le \sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2 + \zeta^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2}\,\sqrt{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) by corollary 2.15 for the vectors \((\omega_{\mathrm{d}}, -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((\cos x, \sin x)\) in the plane, we have \(|h(u)| \le M_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-au}\) and \(|\dot h(u)| \le M_1\,\mathrm{e}^{-au}\) with \(a = \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\), \(M_0 = 1/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) and \(M_1 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\). For \(\zeta \ge 1\), by theorem 9.43 with \(q_0 = 0\) and \(\dot q_0 = 1/m\), \(h(u) = (\mathrm{e}^{s_1 u} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 u})/(m(s_1 - s_2))\) with real roots \(s_1 \ne s_2\) for \(\zeta > 1\), and \(h(u) = u\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}/m\) for \(\zeta = 1\). For \(\zeta > 1\), \(h\) and \(\dot h\) are combinations of the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 u}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 u}\) with negative \(s_1, s_2\), which are bounded by a constant multiple of \(\mathrm{e}^{-au}\) with \(a = \min(|s_1|, |s_2|)\). For \(\zeta = 1\), \(\dot h = (1 - \omega_{\mathrm{n}} u)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}/m\), and since \(u\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u/2}\) is below \(1\) for large \(u\) by lemma 9.15 (with \(n = 1\)), and on a finite interval it is continuous and hence bounded, \(|h|\) and \(|\dot h|\) are bounded by a constant multiple of \(\mathrm{e}^{-au}\) with \(a = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/2\). In all cases there are therefore \(a > 0\) and \(M_0\), \(M_1\) with \(|h(u)| \le M_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-au}\) and \(|\dot h(u)| \le M_1\,\mathrm{e}^{-au}\). The remaining steps are the same for all cases.
  2. The function \(Q\) is bounded: on each of the finitely many pieces of one period it is continuous on a closed piece, hence bounded there (theorem 1.73 on the maximum value), and by periodicity the bound carries over to the whole axis. Let \(|Q| \le Q_{\max}\). For every \(t\) the function \(u \mapsto h(u)\,Q(t - u)\) is piecewise continuous on every \([0, T]\) and \(|h(u)\,Q(t - u)| \le Q_{\max}M_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-au}\), where \(\int_0^\infty \mathrm{e}^{-au}\,\mathrm{d}u = 1/a\) (the fundamental theorem, \(\mathrm{e}^{-aT} \to 0\) by lemma 9.15 with \(n = 0\)). By lemma 9.17 the integral (9.84) exists. Likewise \(p(t) = \int_0^\infty \dot h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) exists, with \(M_1\) instead of \(M_0\).
  3. Period: since \(Q(t + T_{\mathrm{p}} - u) = Q(t - u)\) for every \(u\), the integrands for \(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and for \(t\) are equal, so \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}) = q_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\).
  4. Let \(t_0\) be arbitrary and \(\tau = t - t_0 \ge 0\). We split the integral up to \(T\), \(\int_0^T h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \int_0^{\tau} h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u + \int_\tau^T h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) (additivity, lemma 6.10, point 1). We denote the first term by \(D(t)\). With the function \(G(s) = h(t - s)\,Q(s)\) we have \(h(u)\,Q(t - u) = G(t - u)\), so by lemma 9.86 (point 2, reflection) \(D(t) = \int_0^\tau G(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \int_{\mathrm{t} - \tau}^{t} G(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^{t} h(t - s)\,Q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), which is the Duhamel integral from time \(t_0\).
  5. In the second term we shift the variable (lemma 9.86, point 1, with \(c = \tau\) and the function \(u \mapsto h(u)\,Q(t - u)\)): \(\int_\tau^T h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \int_0^{T - \tau} h(\tau + w)\,Q(t_0 - w)\,\mathrm{d}w\), because \(t - \tau - w = t_0 - w\).
  6. Let \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) be the free solutions of theorem 9.79. For every \(w \ge 0\) the function \(\tau \mapsto h(\tau + w)\) is a free solution: its derivative with respect to \(\tau\) is \(\dot h(\tau + w)\) (chain rule, lemma 4.43, the shift has derivative \(1\)), and the equation has no explicit time. Its initial values are \(h(w)\) and \(\dot h(w)\) at \(\tau = 0\). By uniqueness (theorem 9.36), \(h(\tau + w) = h(w)\,\varphi_1(\tau) + \dot h(w)\,\varphi_2(\tau)\).
  7. We insert this into step 5 and use the linearity of the integral:
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_\tau^T h(u)\,Q(t - u)\,\mathrm{d}u &= \varphi_1(\tau)\int_0^{T - \tau} h(w)\,Q(t_0 - w)\,\mathrm{d}w \\ &\quad + \varphi_2(\tau)\int_0^{T - \tau} \dot h(w)\,Q(t_0 - w)\,\mathrm{d}w . \end{aligned}\]
    As \(T \to \infty\) the two integrals tend to \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\) and \(p(t_0)\) from step 2 (definition 9.16). So the left integral converges too, and from step 4, \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = D(t) + q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\,\varphi_1(t - t_0) + p(t_0)\,\varphi_2(t - t_0)\) for \(t \ge t_0\).
  8. The function \(w_{\mathrm{t}_0}(t) = D(t) + q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\,\varphi_1(t - t_0) + p(t_0)\,\varphi_2(t - t_0)\) is, by theorem 9.79, a solution on \([t_0, t_0 + T]\) with the values \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\) and \(p(t_0)\) at \(t_0\), for every \(T\). The theorem holds for every starting time \(t_0\) and has the derivative (9.73). By step 7, \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = w_{\mathrm{t}_0}\) on \([t_0, \infty)\). Hence \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) has the right derivative \(p(t_0)\) at every \(t_0\). For \(t_1 < t_0\) we have \(q_{\mathrm{p}} = w_{\mathrm{t}_1}\) on \([t_1, \infty)\), and the function \(w_{\mathrm{t}_1}\) is continuously differentiable by theorem 9.79, so \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) has at \(t_0\) a derivative equal to the right derivative \(p(t_0)\). Thus \(\dot q_{\mathrm{p}} = p\) everywhere, the derivative is continuous (as the derivative of the solution \(w_{\mathrm{t}_1}\) for every \(t_1\) to the left of the point) and the equation holds everywhere except at the points where the force \(Q\) jumps. This is point 1.

2. Other solutions.

  1. Let \(q\) be a solution of the same equation on some \([t_0, \infty)\). By lemma 9.6 (point 2) the difference \(q - q_{\mathrm{p}}\) solves the equation without force, so it is the free motion with the initial values \(q(t_0) - q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\) and \(\dot q(t_0) - \dot q_{\mathrm{p}}(t_0)\) (theorem 9.43). It tends to zero, for \(\zeta < 1\) like \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) times a bounded function (theorem 9.43, point 1). This is point 2.

3. Coefficients.

  1. For a piecewise continuous function \(y\) with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) we write \(Y_{\mathrm{n}}[y] = \dfrac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} y(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t\), that is, the complex coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) of the function \(y\) (equation (9.82)).
  2. Let \(y\) be real, continuous, periodic and piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). By the product rule for complex functions (lemma 9.30) and theorem 9.28 (point 3), on every piece \((y\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t})' = (y' - \mathrm{i}n\Omega\,y)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\). The real and imaginary parts of the function \(y\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\) are piecewise continuously differentiable, so by lemma 6.12, applied to each, the integral of the derivative over the period equals \(y(T_{\mathrm{p}})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}n} - y(0) = 0\), because \(y(T_{\mathrm{p}}) = y(0)\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}n} = \cos 2\pi n - \mathrm{i}\sin 2\pi n = 1\) (lemma 8.10, point 4). By linearity, \(Y_{\mathrm{n}}[y'] = \mathrm{i}n\Omega\,Y_{\mathrm{n}}[y]\). There are no boundary terms because \(y\) is continuous.
  3. The function \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is continuous and continuously differentiable (point 1), so \(Y_{\mathrm{n}}[\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}] = \mathrm{i}n\Omega\,Y_{\mathrm{n}}[q_{\mathrm{p}}]\). The function \(\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is continuous, periodic (the derivative of a periodic function) and piecewise continuously differentiable, because \(\ddot q_{\mathrm{p}} = (Q - c\dot q_{\mathrm{p}} - k q_{\mathrm{p}})/m\) is continuous between the jumps of the force and has one-sided limits on a closed piece. Hence \(Y_{\mathrm{n}}[\ddot q_{\mathrm{p}}] = (\mathrm{i}n\Omega)^2\,Y_{\mathrm{n}}[q_{\mathrm{p}}]\). The jump of the force does not show up in this, because \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\dot q_{\mathrm{p}}\) are continuous.
  4. We multiply the equation by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}/T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and integrate over the period. It must hold everywhere except at finitely many points, which do not change the integral (definition 6.9). By linearity, \(\big(m(\mathrm{i}n\Omega)^2 + c\,\mathrm{i}n\Omega + k\big)\,Y_{\mathrm{n}}[q_{\mathrm{p}}] = c_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(P(\mathrm{i}n\Omega)\,Y_{\mathrm{n}}[q_{\mathrm{p}}] = c_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(n \ne 0\) the polynomial \(P(\mathrm{i}n\Omega) = k - mn^2\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c\,n\Omega\) has the imaginary part \(c\,n\Omega \ne 0\), and for \(n = 0\) the real part \(k > 0\), so \(P(\mathrm{i}n\Omega) \ne 0\) and \(Y_{\mathrm{n}}[q_{\mathrm{p}}] = H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is point 3.

4. Series.

  1. The function \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is real, periodic, continuous and continuously differentiable, hence piecewise continuously differentiable (point 1). By theorem 9.95 the sum \(\sum(|a_{\mathrm{n}}| + |b_{\mathrm{n}}|)\) of its real coefficients is finite and the partial sums \(S_{\mathrm{N}} q_{\mathrm{p}}\) converge uniformly to \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\). By lemma 9.97, \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}(q_{\mathrm{p}})| \le (|a_{\mathrm{n}}| + |b_{\mathrm{n}}|)/2\) and \(S_{\mathrm{N}} q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{n} = -\mathrm{N}}^{N} c_{\mathrm{n}}(q_{\mathrm{p}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\), so the series \(\sum_{\mathrm{n} = -\infty}^{\infty} c_{\mathrm{n}}(q_{\mathrm{p}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\) converges absolutely and (the symmetric partial sums) uniformly to \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\). By point 3, \(c_{\mathrm{n}}(q_{\mathrm{p}}) = H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is equation (9.85).

Source: CK, equations (7.12) to (7.16) (PDF p. 211): the response to a periodic force has the coefficients \(\overline U_{\mathrm{n}} = \overline H_{\mathrm{n}}\,\overline P_{\mathrm{n}}\) (7.15), obtained by letting each term of the complex series of the force respond by itself. KREYSZIG §11.3, example 1 (PDF p. 519) builds the response from the responses to the terms of the series and remarks that this holds "provided that termwise differentiation of (7) is permissible", leaving the proof to a reader familiar with uniform convergence. The proof above (the response as an integral over the past, the coefficients by integration by parts) is not from a source.

The proof showed three things. The periodic response \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\) is a single periodic function that every other motion of system A comes closer to than one percent after \(24{,}4\) periods of the force. Its Fourier coefficients are products of the coefficients of the force and the frequency response, so each harmonic responds according to theorem 9.52. And the sum of these responses converges to \(q_{\mathrm{p}}\), without ever differentiating the series. Differentiation occurs only in step 11, where we use it over one period on a continuous function, so there are no boundary terms. For the acceleration the series indeed does not converge absolutely, as we predicted. The coefficients of the \(n\)-th harmonic of the force, of the response and of the acceleration of the response are

\(n\) \(\lvert c_{\mathrm{n}}\rvert\) (N) \(\lvert H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\rvert\) (mm) \(n^2\Omega^2\lvert H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\rvert\) (m/s\(^2\)) \(\lvert c_{\mathrm{n}}\rvert/m\) (m/s\(^2\))
\(31\) \(0{,}2054\) \(3{,}10\cdot 10^{-5}\) \(4{,}15\cdot 10^{-3}\) \(4{,}11\cdot 10^{-3}\)
\(101\) \(0{,}0630\) \(8{,}87\cdot 10^{-7}\) \(1{,}26\cdot 10^{-3}\) \(1{,}26\cdot 10^{-3}\)
\(301\) \(0{,}0212\) \(3{,}35\cdot 10^{-8}\) \(4{,}23\cdot 10^{-4}\) \(4{,}23\cdot 10^{-4}\)

The coefficients of the response fall as \(1/n^3\) (one factor \(1/n\) comes from the force, two from \(H\)) and the series of the response converges quickly. The coefficients of the acceleration fall only as \(1/n\) and their series does not converge absolutely, because \(\sum 1/n\) grows beyond every bound.

Example 9.99 A square-wave force on system A (kvadratna sila na sistem A)

The force of the press has \(F_0 = 20\) N and \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3T_{\mathrm{n}}\). Its coefficients are \(c_0 = F_0/2 = 10\) N and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = 2F_0/(n\pi)\) for odd \(n\), that is \(12{,}7324\), \(4{,}2441\), \(2{,}5465\), \(1{,}8189\) and \(1{,}4147\) N for \(n = 1, 3, 5, 7, 9\) (figure 9.50, top). The frequency ratio of the \(n\)-th harmonic is \(r_{\mathrm{n}} = n\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n/3\). By theorem 9.52 the amplitude of the response is \(X_{\mathrm{n}} = b_{\mathrm{n}}\,|H(n\Omega)| = b_{\mathrm{n}}\,D(r_{\mathrm{n}})/k\) and the lag is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}} = -\arg H(n\Omega)\) (definition 9.53):

\(1\) \(12{,}7324\) \(1/3\) \(1{,}1250\) \(0{,}22837\) \(0{,}430^\circ\)

  1. The constant value of the force, \(10\) N, gives the displacement \(10/k = 0{,}15944\) mm, because \(H(0) = 1/k\).
  2. The third harmonic has exactly \(3\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3T_{\mathrm{n}}\). So \(r_3 = 1\), \(D = 1/(2\zeta) = 50\) and the lag is exactly \(90^\circ\) (theorem 9.54). The amplitude is \(X_3 = 4{,}2441\cdot 50/62\,720 = 3{,}3834\) mm.
  3. The first harmonic has \(r_1 = 1/3\) and \(D = 1/\sqrt{(1 - 1/9)^2 + (2\cdot 0{,}01/3)^2} = 1{,}1250\), so \(X_1 = 12{,}7324\cdot 1{,}125/62\,720 = 0{,}22837\) mm. The fifth harmonic is at \(r_5 = 5/3\) and has \(D = 0{,}5624\).
  4. The third harmonic has a third of the force of the first, yet its response is \(3{,}3834/0{,}22837 = 14{,}8\) times larger. The reason is the ratio \(D(1)/D(1/3) = 50/1{,}125 = 44{,}4\), which is larger than the force ratio \(3\). The remaining responses are small, because \(D\) is near or below \(1\) at all other \(r_{\mathrm{n}}\) and because the force falls as \(1/n\).

The answer to the first question is therefore: system A responds to the third harmonic, which falls on its resonance, and to all the others hardly at all. The response is the sum of the series (9.85) and figure 9.49 shows it. The tip oscillates almost sinusoidally with a third of the period of the force, that is \(9\) oscillations in three periods of the force, distorted only by small contributions of the constant value and of the first harmonic.

The displacement moves between a smallest and a largest value. The partial sums of the series (9.85) (with terms up to \(n\)) give:

The maximum is at \(t = 0{,}1669\,T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the minimum at \(t = 0{,}6669\,T_{\mathrm{p}}\). By point 4 of theorem 9.98 (absolute convergence), the remainder after \(n = 7\) is smaller than the sum of the amplitudes \(\sum_{\mathrm{n} > 7}|b_{\mathrm{n}}|\,|H(n\Omega)| = 0{,}0076\) mm. Anyone who looked only at the first harmonic, which is the largest in the force, would predict a motion between \(-0{,}07\) and \(0{,}39\) mm, a range of \(0{,}46\) mm instead of the true \(7{,}19\) mm.

We checked the result in two independent ways. First, we computed the integral (9.84) exactly piece by piece (the force is piecewise constant, and the antiderivative of the function \(h\) is the step response of section 9.9). At \(t = 0\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/4\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\) and \(3T_{\mathrm{p}}/4\) we get \(-3{,}22621\), \(0{,}36962\), \(3{,}54509\) and \(-0{,}05075\) mm, which agrees with the series to five places. Second, we integrated the equation exactly from rest over half periods (on each the force is constant and \(q - F/k\) is a free motion, section 9.5) and after \(3000\) periods read off the same range, \(-3{,}4355\) to \(3{,}7544\) mm. From rest, however, the steady state does not appear at once, because the free motion decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). At \(t = 0\) after \(1\), \(12\) and \(24\) periods the displacement is \(-0{,}554\), \(-2{,}890\) and \(-3{,}191\) mm, and the steady value is \(-3{,}226\) mm, so after \(24\) periods (that is \(13\) s) the difference is \(0{,}035\) mm or \(1{,}1\ \%\).

The model of system A holds as long as the cantilever is linearly elastic. At the largest displacement \(3{,}754\) mm the stress at the clamp is \(91{,}97 + 3{,}754\cdot 11{,}760 = 136{,}1\) MPa, which is below \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (example 9.58).

Theorem 9.98 uses the impulse response \(h\) (time), while the previous sections on the harmonic force used the frequency response \(H\) (frequency). It is the same system and two descriptions. How are they related?

If in theorem 9.98 we take the force \(Q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega' t}\) with a complex value, the integral (9.84) equals \(\int_0^\infty h(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega'(t - u)}\,\mathrm{d}u = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega' t}\int_0^\infty h(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega' u}\,\mathrm{d}u\), and by section 9.6 the response to this force is \(H(\Omega')\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega' t}\). So the integral must equal \(H(\Omega')\). We prove this directly.

Theorem 9.100 Frequency response as the transform of the impulse response (frekvenčni odziv kot transform impulznega odziva)

Let \(m > 0\), \(c > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(h\) the impulse response (definition 9.74) and \(H(\Omega) = 1/P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)\) (definition 9.51, written with the same formula for every real \(\Omega\), because for \(c > 0\) the denominator is nonzero everywhere). The integral of a complex function is understood by real and imaginary part (definition 9.29). For every real circular frequency \(\Omega\) the integral \(\int_0^\infty h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t\) exists and

\[H(\Omega) = \int_0^\infty h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{9.86}\]
Proof

We must show an equality. We write the function \(h\) as a combination of complex exponential functions, integrate each, and then recognize the result as the reciprocal of the characteristic polynomial.

  1. Let \(\zeta \ne 1\) and \(s_{1,2}\) the roots of the polynomial \(P\) (theorem 9.43, proof, steps 2 and 4). By equation (9.67), \(h(t) = \dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}}{m\,(s_1 - s_2)}\).
  2. Let \(s\) be a complex number with \(\mathrm{Re}\,s < 0\) and \(w = s - \mathrm{i}\Omega\). By theorem 9.28 (point 3) the function \(\mathrm{e}^{wt}/w\) has the derivative \(\mathrm{e}^{wt}\), which is continuous, so by lemma 9.30 (point 3) \(\int_0^T \mathrm{e}^{wt}\,\mathrm{d}t = (\mathrm{e}^{wT} - 1)/w\). Since \(|\mathrm{e}^{wT}| = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{Re}\,s\,T} \to 0\) (point 2 of the theorem and lemma 9.15), the real and imaginary parts tend to their limits, and \(\int_0^\infty \mathrm{e}^{wt}\,\mathrm{d}t = -1/w = 1/(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s)\). The denominator is not zero, because \(\mathrm{Re}\,s \ne 0\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2 the integral equals
    \[\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{1}{m(s_1 - s_2)}\Big(\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_1} - \dfrac{1}{\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_2}\Big) \\ &= \dfrac{1}{m(s_1 - s_2)}\cdot\dfrac{s_1 - s_2}{(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_1)(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_2)} \\ &= \dfrac{1}{m(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_1)(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s_2)} . \end{aligned}\]
  4. Since \(P(s) = m(s - s_1)(s - s_2)\), the denominator is \(P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)\) and the result is \(1/P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) = H(\Omega)\).
  5. Steps 2 to 4 hold unchanged for \(\zeta > 1\) (real \(s_{1,2}\)). For \(\zeta = 1\), \(h(t) = t\,\mathrm{e}^{st}/m\) with \(s = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) = m(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s)^2\). The function \((t/w - 1/w^2)\,\mathrm{e}^{wt}\) has the derivative \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{wt}\), so \(\int_0^T t\,\mathrm{e}^{wt}\,\mathrm{d}t = (T/w - 1/w^2)\,\mathrm{e}^{wT} + 1/w^2\). Since \(|T\,\mathrm{e}^{wT}| = T\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T} \to 0\) (lemma 9.15 with \(n = 1\)), \(\int_0^\infty t\,\mathrm{e}^{wt}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1/w^2 = 1/(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s)^2\). The integral of \(h\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) is therefore \(1/(m(\mathrm{i}\Omega - s)^2) = 1/P(\mathrm{i}\Omega)\).

Source: CK, equation (7.33) (PDF p. 217): "the system frequency-response function \(H(f)\) is the Fourier transform of the unit impulse response function \(h(t)\)", there from the Fourier integral and its inverse. GR p. 199 (PDF), note 5: the dynamic influence coefficients are Fourier transforms of the impulse responses. The proof by a direct integral is not from a source.

For system A at \(r = 0{,}5\), \(1\) and \(2\) the integral, computed numerically, equals \(2{,}12547\cdot 10^{-5} - 2{,}8340\cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{i}\), \(-7{,}971939\cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-5{,}31368\cdot 10^{-6} - 7{,}085\cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{i}\) m/N, and the values of \(H\) are \(2{,}12547\cdot 10^{-5} - 2{,}8340\cdot 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{i}\), \(-7{,}971939\cdot 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-5{,}31368\cdot 10^{-6} - 7{,}085\cdot 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{i}\) m/N. The values agree to all digits written (mpmath, recomputed).

So the function \(H\) is the Fourier transform (Fourierjeva transformacija) of the impulse response, since \(h(t) = 0\) before the blow. The inverse theorem (from \(H\) we get \(h\) again) and the Fourier integral we mention and do not use. The meaning can be seen in figure 9.51. The impulse response of system A decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), so its memory is \(1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 2{,}823\) s long, while the peak of the function \(|H|\) is \(2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}7084\) 1/s wide (the half-power points of section 9.7). The product of the two is \(2\), whatever \(\zeta\) is: a lightly damped system has a long memory and a narrow peak, a heavily damped one a short memory and a wide peak. For a periodic force the peak tells which harmonics respond strongly. It is \(0{,}02\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) wide and the harmonics are spaced by \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/3\), so only the third is hit: between \(r = 0{,}99\) and \(1{,}01\), \(D\) is above \(35{,}0\), while at the neighbouring odd harmonics (\(r = 1/3\) and \(5/3\)) it is only \(1{,}125\) and \(0{,}562\).

Example 9.101 Periodic hammer blows (zaporedni udarci kladiva)

A hammer strikes system A every \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}53221\) s with the impulse \(I = 2\) N s. Each blow lasts \(\varepsilon = 1\) ms and has the force \(I/\varepsilon = 2000\) N (a model that is sufficient for an impulse \(I\), section 9.9). The force \(Q\) consists of rectangular pulses repeated with the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\).

  1. Coefficient: \(c_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{\varepsilon}\dfrac{I}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \dfrac{I}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega\varepsilon/2}\,\dfrac{\sin(n\Omega\varepsilon/2)}{n\Omega\varepsilon/2}\), because \(\int_0^\varepsilon \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha t}\mathrm{d}t = (1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\varepsilon})/(\mathrm{i}\alpha) = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\varepsilon/2}\,2\sin(\alpha\varepsilon/2)/\alpha\).
  2. The factor \(\sin x/x\) with \(x = n\Omega\varepsilon/2\) at \(n = 1\), \(10\), \(50\), \(100\), \(200\) equals \(0{,}99999\), \(0{,}99942\), \(0{,}98554\), \(0{,}94293\), \(0{,}78337\) (\(x = 0{,}006\), \(0{,}059\), \(0{,}295\), \(0{,}590\), \(1{,}181\)). As long as \(x\) is below \(0{,}3\), that is up to \(n\) around \(50\) (error \(1{,}4\ \%\)), \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}| \approx I/T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3{,}7579\) N. The spectrum of the force is flat, all harmonics have the same amplitude \(2I/T_{\mathrm{p}} = 7{,}5158\) N, whereas for the square wave the amplitude falls as \(1/n\).
  3. The amplitude of the third harmonic of the response is \(2\,(I/T_{\mathrm{p}})\,|H(\omega_{\mathrm{n}})| = 7{,}5158\ \text{N}\cdot 7{,}9719\cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m/N} = 5{,}99\cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 5{,}99\) mm, and the other harmonics are small because \(D \sim 1/n^2\).
  4. Directly: by section 9.9, after one blow the response is \(I\,h(t)\) with the amplitude \(I/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}}) = 1{,}1294\) mm. The next blow comes after \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 3T_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is at almost the same phase of the oscillation (the damped period \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) is only \(0{,}005\ \%\) longer than \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(3T_{\mathrm{d}}\) is longer than \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) by \(0{,}027\) ms). By theorem 9.98, point 1, the response is the sum of the responses of all previous blows, \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = I\sum_{\mathrm{j} \ge 0} h(t + jT_{\mathrm{p}})\) for \(0 \le t < T_{\mathrm{p}}\) (we replace the short pulses by the response to their impulse, the error is estimated in corollary 9.80). Since \(h(t) = \mathrm{Im}\,\mathrm{e}^{st}/(m\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) for \(s = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} + \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), by the sum of a geometric series (lemma 9.83, point 2, complex ratio, with ratio \(|\mathrm{e}^{sT_{\mathrm{p}}}| = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{p}}} = 0{,}8282 < 1\)) \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = \dfrac{I}{m\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{st}}{1 - \mathrm{e}^{sT_{\mathrm{p}}}}\), where \(|1 - \mathrm{e}^{sT_{\mathrm{p}}}| = 0{,}1718\). The amplitude grows to at most \(1/0{,}1718 = 5{,}82\) times a single one.

Both ways give the same response. The series (9.85) with \(40\,001\) terms (\(n = -20\,000, \dots, 20\,000\), rectangular pulses of length \(1\) ms) gives a maximum of \(6{,}4715\) mm and a minimum of \(-6{,}2713\) mm. The closed formula (the blow at the middle of the pulse, shifted by \(\varepsilon/2 = 0{,}5\) ms) gives \(6{,}4718\) and \(-6{,}2716\) mm. The largest difference over the period is \(0{,}004\) mm. The largest displacement is \(5{,}73\) times a single amplitude. Just before the blow the tip is almost at the equilibrium position (\(-0{,}03\) mm) and moves in the direction of the blow with the velocity \(0{,}193\) m/s, and the blow adds \(I/m = 0{,}04\) m/s (section 9.9). So each blow adds to the remainder of the previous ones, and the amplitude \(v/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) reaches \(0{,}2328/35{,}416 = 6{,}57\) mm. The third harmonic contributes \(5{,}99\) mm of the \(6{,}47\) mm.

The same blows with the impulse \(5\) N s, which we used for a single blow in section 9.9, would in such a train give \(16{,}18\) mm, beyond the limit \(10{,}887\) mm of example 9.58. The linear model of system A holds for an impulse of up to \(3{,}36\) N s (the estimate \(2\cdot 10{,}887/6{,}4715\) N s, because the response is proportional to \(I\)).

So far we have had the force as a formula. In a measurement the force or the response is recorded as values at equal time spacings, and we cannot compute the integral for the coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\). We replace the integral by a sum over samples (vzorci). Take the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and divide it into \(N\) parts, and let the samples be \(f_{\mathrm{j}} = f(jT_{\mathrm{p}}/N)\) for \(j = 0, \dots, N - 1\). In \(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\) at the time \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = jT_{\mathrm{p}}/N\) the angle is \(n\Omega t_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\pi jn/N\), so the sum \((1/N)\sum_{\mathrm{j}} f_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jn/N}\) is an approximation for \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) (the rectangle sum of the integral over \([0, T_{\mathrm{p}}]\), divided by \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\)).

Definition 9.102 Discrete Fourier transform (diskretna Fourierjeva transformacija)

Let \(N \ge 1\) be a natural number and \(f_0, \dots, f_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) complex numbers (the samples). Their discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is the \(N\) complex numbers

\[C_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j} = 0}^{N-1} f_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} , \qquad k = 0, \dots, N - 1 . \tag{9.87}\]

We extend the number \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) to all integers \(k\) by the same formula. Since \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j(k + N)/N} = \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N}\), we have \(C_{\mathrm{k}+\mathrm{N}} = C_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG (PDF p. 556), equation (18): \(\hat f_{\mathrm{n}} = N c_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum f_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}nx_{\mathrm{k}}}\). CK, equation (7.36) (PDF p. 219) has the step \(\Delta t = T_{\mathrm{p}}/N\) instead of \(1/N\), so its result is \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\,C_{\mathrm{k}}\).

What this sum reads from the samples is shown by two results. The first is orthogonality: the sum over the samples of the function \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi jk/N}\) is zero unless \(k\) is a multiple of \(N\).

Lemma 9.103 Discrete orthogonality (diskretna ortogonalnost)

For a natural number \(N\) and an integer \(k\),

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j} = 0}^{N-1} \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} = \begin{cases} N & \text{if } N \text{ divides } k \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\]
Proof

We must show both cases. The first is immediate, the second is a geometric sum.

  1. Let \(w = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N}\). By theorem 9.28 (point 1, by induction on \(j\)), \(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} = w^j\). If \(N\) divides \(k\), then \(k/N\) is an integer \(l\) and \(w = \cos 2\pi l + \mathrm{i}\sin 2\pi l = 1\), because the cosine and the sine are periodic with period \(2\pi\) (lemma 8.10, point 4) and \(\cos 0 = 1\), \(\sin 0 = 0\) (point 2). Every term is \(1\) and the sum is \(N\).
  2. If \(N\) does not divide \(k\), then \(w \ne 1\): from \(w = 1\) it would follow that \(\cos\theta = 1\) for \(\theta = 2\pi k/N\), and by point 3 of definition 8.9, \(\cos\theta = \cos r\) for \(r = \theta - 2\pi k'\) from \((-\pi, \pi]\). The cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) and even, so \(\cos r = 1\) only at \(r = 0\), so \(\theta\) is a multiple of \(2\pi\) and \(N\) divides \(k\).
  3. By multiplication, \((w - 1)(1 + w + \dots + w^{N-1}) = w^N - 1\) and \(w^N = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k} = \cos 2\pi k + \mathrm{i}\sin 2\pi k = 1\), because \(k\) is an integer (periodicity as in step 1). Since \(w - 1 \ne 0\), the sum equals zero.

Source: KREYSZIG (PDF pp. 555-557): the proof of the relation \(F_{\mathrm{N}}\overline{F_{\mathrm{N}}} = N I\) with a "geometric sum". The writing in two steps and the justification of \(w \ne 1\) are not from the source.

The second result tells what the samples see. We sample a function \(f\) with finitely many harmonics, that is \(f(t) = \sum_{|\mathrm{n}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\) (a trigonometric polynomial of degree \(M\)), with \(N\) samples.

Corollary 9.104 Aliasing (prekrivanje)

Let \(f(t) = \sum_{|\mathrm{n}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t}\) with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) and let \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the DFT of its \(N\) samples \(f_{\mathrm{j}} = f(jT_{\mathrm{p}}/N)\). For every integer \(k\),

\[C_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{m} \in \mathbb{Z},\ |\mathrm{k} + \mathrm{mN}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{k} + \mathrm{mN}} .\]

If \(M < N/2\), then \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(0 \le k \le M\) and \(C_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = c_{-\mathrm{k}}\), that is, every harmonic is read correctly. If \(M \ge N/2\), the harmonics that differ by a multiple of \(N\) are combined.

Proof

We must show that of the sum only the part where \(N\) divides \(n - k\) remains. We insert the samples into the definition and exchange the order of two finite sums.

  1. At the time \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = jT_{\mathrm{p}}/N\), \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\Omega t_{\mathrm{j}}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jn/N}\), so \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = \dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sum_{|\mathrm{n}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j(n - k)/N} = \sum_{|\mathrm{n}| \le \mathrm{M}} c_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j} = 0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j(n - k)/N}\).
  2. By lemma 9.103 the inner sum is \(1\) if \(N\) divides \(n - k\), that is \(n = k + mN\), and \(0\) otherwise. The terms \(c_{\mathrm{k} + \mathrm{mN}}\) with \(|k + mN| \le M\) remain.
  3. Let \(M < N/2\) and \(|k| \le M\). For \(m \ne 0\), \(|k + mN| \ge |m|N - |k| \ge N - M > N/2 > M\), so only \(m = 0\) remains and \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\). For \(0 \le k \le M\) we therefore have \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}}\), and since \(C_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = C_{-\mathrm{k}}\) (period \(N\)), \(C_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = c_{-\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG (PDF p. 557): "one should use only \(n\)'s that are much smaller than \(N\), to avoid aliasing", with the example of a rotating wheel in a film. CK does not have the word (PDF pp. 202-222). The relation \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum c_{\mathrm{k} + \mathrm{mN}}\) and its proof are not from the source.

The simplest example is shown in figure 9.52. Let the period be \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 1\) s and \(N = 8\). The functions \(\cos 2\pi\cdot 5t\) and \(\cos 2\pi\cdot 3t\) have the same values at the eight times \(t = j/8\), with a difference of at most \(3\cdot 10^{-15}\) (rounding). By corollary 9.104, \(c_5 = c_{-5} = 1/2\) for the first and \(c_3 = c_{-3} = 1/2\) for the second, and both give \(C_3 = C_5 = 1/2\): for \(k = 3\) this is \(c_3 + c_{-5}\) and for \(k = 5\) it is \(c_5 + c_{-3}\). Eight samples do not distinguish the fifth harmonic from the third. So in a measurement we must sample with more than two samples per period of the fastest harmonic present in the signal.

Example 9.105 The DFT of the square wave and of the response of system A (DFT kvadratne sile in odziva sistema A)

The square wave force has a jump at \(t = 0\) and \(t = T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). We take the sample at a jump as the mean of the two sides, \(F_0/2\). Let \(N\) be even, \(z = \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N}\) and \(k\) odd.

  1. The samples are \(f_0 = F_0/2\), \(f_{\mathrm{j}} = F_0\) for \(j = 1, \dots, N/2 - 1\), \(f_{\mathrm{N}/2} = F_0/2\) and \(f_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for the others. So \(N C_{\mathrm{k}} = F_0\big(\tfrac12 + \sum_{\mathrm{j} = 1}^{N/2 - 1} z^j + \tfrac12 z^{N/2}\big)\).
  2. For odd \(k\), \(z^{N/2} = \mathrm{e}^{-\pi\mathrm{i}k} = -1\). The terms \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac12 z^{N/2}\) cancel and the geometric sum remains, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} = 1}^{N/2-1} z^j = (z - z^{N/2})/(1 - z) = (z + 1)/(1 - z)\).
  3. With \(\theta = 2\pi k/N\), \((1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta})/(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}) = \cos(\theta/2)/(\mathrm{i}\sin(\theta/2)) = -\mathrm{i}\cot(\theta/2)\). So \(C_{\mathrm{k}} = -\mathrm{i}\,\dfrac{F_0}{N}\cot\dfrac{\pi k}{N}\) for odd \(k\). For even \(k \ne 0\), \(z^{N/2} = 1\) and the sum is \(\tfrac12(1 + z^{N/2}) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{N/2-1} z^j = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N/2-1} z^j = (1 - z^{N/2})/(1 - z) = 0\).

The true coefficient is \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = -\mathrm{i}F_0/(k\pi)\). For \(k = 1\), \(C_1/c_1 = (\pi/N)\cot(\pi/N) = 1 - (\pi/N)^2/3 - (\pi/N)^4/45 - \dots\) (SymPy), so the error is about \((\pi/N)^2/3\). At \(N = 8\), \(16\), \(64\), \(512\) the error is \(5{,}19\ \%\), \(1{,}29\ \%\), \(0{,}080\ \%\), \(0{,}0013\ \%\). At \(k = 3\) the error is \(51{,}2\ \%\) (\(N = 8\)), \(11{,}8\ \%\) (\(16\)), \(0{,}72\ \%\) (\(64\)) and \(0{,}011\ \%\) (\(512\)), much larger: at \(N = 8\) the fifth harmonic, which has the opposite sign, is not distinguished from the third and partly cancels it. Corollary 9.104 holds for a finite sum, while the series of the square wave has infinitely many terms, so this tells only the cause and does not compute the error.

The response of system A is much smoother than the force. Its coefficients are \(H(n\Omega)\,c_{\mathrm{n}}\), so they fall as \(1/n^3\), and the aliasing is negligible. The DFT of \(N\) samples of the response has errors \(1{,}73\ \%\) (\(k = 1\)) and \(0{,}654\ \%\) (\(k = 3\)) at \(N = 8\), \(0{,}085\ \%\) and \(0{,}019\ \%\) at \(N = 16\), and \(0{,}005\ \%\) and \(0{,}001\ \%\) at \(N = 32\). At \(N = 8\) and \(k = 3\) this is exactly the contribution of the harmonic \(-5\), which is \(0{,}0114\) mm against \(1{,}692\) mm, that is \(0{,}67\ \%\). The system filters, so sampling is easier for the response than for the force.

Computing the sum (9.87) for all \(N\) values of \(k\) requires \(N^2\) products. The fast Fourier transform (FFT, hitra Fourierjeva transformacija) is a procedure that computes the same numbers with \((N/2)\log_2 N\) products. At \(N = 512\) this is, by CK (PDF p. 220), less than \(1\ \%\) of the products of the DFT, indeed \(256\cdot 9 = 2304\) against \(262\,144\), that is \(0{,}88\ \%\). We do not explain the algorithm here (CK, equation (7.41), PDF p. 220, KREYSZIG PDF pp. 557-558). We use it through NumPy: numpy.fft.fft(f)/N gives the numbers \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) of equation (9.87).

So from a measured record of the force and the response over one period we can obtain the frequency response without knowing the equation of the system. By theorem 9.98, point 3, the coefficient of the response at \(k\Omega\) equals \(H(k\Omega)\) times the coefficient of the force, so the quotient of the \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the response and the \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the force is an estimate of \(H(k\Omega)\) whenever the aliasing is small. GR (PDF p. 199) takes the same route: the frequency response is obtained from a simultaneously measured force and response.
