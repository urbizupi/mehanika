Chapter 29 Structural optimization

Chapter 29Lecture: Structural optimization

1 An optimization problem is a picture in the plane of areas

Let us start with a truss we already know.

We draw the wall. On it we pin two steel bars: \(AC\) is horizontal and 4 m long, \(BC\) is inclined and 5 m long. At the joint \(C\) we hang a load \(F = 10\) kN. For each bar we choose a cross-section area, say \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 100\ \text{mm}^2\), because it is a round number. The truss answers. The bars act on the joint with the forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\), the bars carry the stresses \(-133.3\) MPa and \(166.7\) MPa, the joint moves to \(C'\).

The allowed stress of the steel is \(\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa. Both stresses are below it, so both bars have spare capacity. How much steel in the truss is too much? And how would a computer find out, a computer with no experience at all? A designer would guess, check and guess again. A computer needs something else: the requirements written as inequalities and "better" written as a number. Once we write that, the whole problem turns into a picture.

Let us draw two axes. On the horizontal one we put \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\), on the vertical one \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). Every point of this plane is one truss. Our first choice is the point \((100, 100)\). Now we draw the vertical limit: left of it the bar \(AC\) fails. Then the horizontal limit: below it the bar \(BC\) fails. Where both survive is the shaded corner. Through our choice we draw the line of all trusses with 900 cm³ of steel. We move the line toward the origin, which means less steel. The line of 400 cm³ no longer reaches the corner. The last one that still touches it is the line of 621.2 cm³, and it touches it at the corner point \(\vek A^*\).

This is the claim of this part. A design is a point, every requirement is a limit, "better" is a family of parallel lines, and the best design is the point where the lowest line still touches the feasible set (dopustna množica), the shaded corner region. Here that is the corner \((60.61,\ 75.76)\) mm² with 621.2 cm³ of steel instead of 900 cm³, which is 31 % less.

The claim uses four things we have not justified. Let us go through them in turn.

1.1 Why is a design a point?

What is there left to choose in this truss? The wall stands, the load is given, the lengths of the bars are given. What remains are the two cross-sections. The two areas are all the designer still chooses. So we call them design variables (projektni spremenljivki): they are part of the design. Everything else, the forces, the stresses and the displacement, the truss answers by itself. This answer we call the state (stanje).

Two chosen numbers are one point in a plane, just like two coordinates on a map. So our choice is one dot between the two axes. The general form of the problem with an objective function, constraints and equilibrium is in the book: 29.1.

1.2 Why is a requirement a limit?

How does the requirement "the bar survives" become a line in the plane? The requirement is that the stress does not exceed \(\sigma_0\). Stress is force per area, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{e}} = N_{\mathrm{e}}/A_{\mathrm{e}}\). So the requirement is \(|N_{\mathrm{e}}|/A_{\mathrm{e}} \le \sigma_0\). The area is positive, so we may multiply the inequality by \(A_{\mathrm{e}}/\sigma_0\) and its direction stays:

\[A_{\mathrm{e}} \ge \frac{|N_{\mathrm{e}}|}{\sigma_0} .\]

For the bar \(AC\) this is \(13\,333\ \text{N} / 220\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 60.61\ \text{mm}^2\). That is the vertical limit, and we read the number on the axis. For the bar \(BC\) it is \(16\,667/220 = 75.76\ \text{mm}^2\), the horizontal limit. On a limit the stress in that bar is exactly \(\sigma_0\). For \(\sigma_0\) we took the yield stress of the steel, with no safety factor. What a factor would change we draw at the end.

And why are the limits straight and parallel to the axes? Each concerns one bar only, since it contains one area only. The thickness of bar \(BC\) says nothing about whether bar \(AC\) survives. But that holds only if the force \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) does not depend on \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). That is the next "why".

Here we made an assumption: the only requirement is the stress. Why may we? To start simple. When does it fail? The bar \(AC\) is compressed, and a compressed bar can buckle.

Take the thin bar from the corner, 4 m long. Its cross-section has a diameter of 8.78 mm. We compress it with the force \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\). Does it hold? No, it bows out, because its critical force of 35.34 N is 377 times smaller than 13 333 N. Only the thick bar with a cross-section of diameter 38.72 mm holds, that is \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} \ge 1177\ \text{mm}^2\). Effect on the plane: the vertical limit moves far to the right, from 60.61 to 1177 mm², the corner with it, and the volume grows to 5087.7 cm³, 8.2 times as much. In the book: 29.2.4 and 29.14.

1.3 Why do the forces not depend on the areas?

Would a thicker bar take more of the force? Our feeling says perhaps. Let us check at the joint.

Let us draw the truss once more: the wall, the pins \(A\) and \(B\), the bar \(AC\) and the bar \(BC\), the joint \(C\) at a distance \(a = 4\) m from the wall, and \(B\) at \(h = 3\) m above \(A\). At the joint the load \(F\) pulls. Now we cut the joint out in our minds and replace the two bars by the forces they exert on it. The load pulls down. The compressed bar \(AC\) pushes the joint along its axis. The stretched bar \(BC\) pulls it toward \(B\).

The joint is at rest. What does that tell us? By Newton, the sum of the forces on a body at rest is zero. This is our ground: a thing that does not move has no net force. The sum is zero horizontally and vertically, so two equations for two unknown forces. Only the force of bar \(BC\) carries vertically, and from the triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 we get \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F\cdot 5/3 = 16.67\) kN. Horizontally the force of bar \(AC\) balances it, \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -F\cdot 4/3 = -13.33\) kN. There is no area in these two equations. The bars may be thicker or thinner, the equilibrium stays the same. In the book: (29.4).

A truss whose equilibrium alone fixes all forces is called statically determinate (statično določeno): statics, the study of equilibrium, fixes the forces without the stiffness of the bars. This is an assumption. Why does it hold here? Two bars, two equations at the joint \(C\). When does it fail? If we bring a third bar into \(C\), we have three unknowns and only two equations. The forces then depend on the stiffness, so on the areas, the limits are no longer straight and each concerns several areas at once. Effect: the rule "every bar at its limit" is no longer guaranteed to be the best. Such a truss comes in the last part.

Two more assumptions entered here. We write the equilibrium on the undisplaced truss, although the joint moves. Why may we? At the stress \(\sigma_0\) the strain is only \(220\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 1.12\cdot 10^{-3}\). If we compute the equilibrium on the displaced truss, the forces change by 0.11 %. For bars of rubber, which stretch a lot, this would not hold. The second assumption: only the load acts on the joint, not the weight of the bars. The two bars at the corner weigh 47.5 N together against 10 kN, that is 0.48 % of the load, and by that much the forces can be off.

1.4 Why is "better" a family of parallel lines?

The computer has to compare two trusses. By what? It needs one number. We take the volume of steel, because at the same density of steel the mass and the price are proportional to it. A bar is a prism: its volume is the cross-section area times the length. That is the ground. The bar \(AC\) is 4 m long, the bar \(BC\) is 5 m by Pythagoras. So

\[V = 4\,A_{\mathrm{AC}} + 5\,A_{\mathrm{BC}} ,\]

and since \(1\ \text{mm}^2 \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{cm}^3\), we get cm³ directly. Our choice has \(4\cdot 100 + 5\cdot 100 = 900\) cm³.

Which other trusses also have 900 cm³? If we increase \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) by 5 mm², \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) must drop by 4 mm². So the points of equal volume lie on a straight line that goes 4 down for every 5 to the right. That is the line 900 through our choice. A smaller volume gives the same slope, only closer to the origin: the line moves parallel to itself. The line 400 lies entirely below the shaded corner: no truss with 400 cm³ of steel survives. In the book: (29.5).

1.5 Why is the best point exactly the corner?

Where does the line touch the corner region for the last time: somewhere on a side or at the corner? First by eye. We move the line toward the origin as long as it still has a point in the corner region. The line falls from left to right, while the shaded region spreads up and to the right. So the last point the line keeps is not on a side but at the corner.

Now the same by counting, on the same plane.

Take our choice and the corner. How much thicker is our choice? The bar \(AC\) by \(100 - 60.61 = 39.39\) mm², the bar \(BC\) by \(100 - 75.76 = 24.24\) mm². Each mm² of bar \(AC\) costs 4 cm³, each mm² of bar \(BC\) costs 5 cm³. So we write the difference of volumes on the board:

\[900 - 621.2 = 4\cdot 39.39 + 5\cdot 24.24 = 157.6 + 121.2 = 278.8\ \text{cm}^3 .\]

The same holds for every point of the corner region: the difference is 4 times the excess of \(AC\) plus 5 times the excess of \(BC\). Inside the region no excess is negative. A sum of non-negative numbers is zero only when each of them is zero. That is the second ground, counting. So only the corner has the smallest volume. In the book this is the proof of lemma 29.10 for \(n\) bars.

1.6 Back up

Now we build the result from the ground:

  1. the joint is at rest, so the forces are 13.33 kN and 16.67 kN, whatever the areas,
  2. force divided by \(\sigma_0\) gives the thinnest bar that survives, 60.61 and 75.76 mm²: the limits,
  3. area times length gives the parallel lines,
  4. we push the line as long as it still touches the corner region: the corner, 621.2 cm³.

At the corner the stress in both bars is exactly 220 MPa, compressive in \(AC\), tensile in \(BC\). Before they were \(-133.3\) and \(166.7\) MPa. Such a truss is called fully stressed (polno napeto paličje), because every bar is stressed to its limit and none carries spare steel. The mass drops to 4.845 kg. In the book: 29.11.

1.7 Bar by bar

Do we need the plane at all for such a truss? No. We take the bar \(AC\) and make it thinner until its stress reaches 220 MPa. Then we do the same with the bar \(BC\). The order does not matter, because thinning one does not change the force in the other. It is the same thought as the two straight limits, only said with our hands. This view adds speed: two divisions give the optimum. But it works only as long as the limits do not affect each other. The picture in the plane works then too.

1.8 By numbers

So how much did we gain? The first choice lies on the line of 900 cm³, the corner on the line of 621.2 cm³. The saving is \(278.8/900 = 31\) %, and that is exactly the difference on the board. This view adds the size: the spare capacity in the stresses, 133.3 and 166.7 MPa instead of 220 MPa, was almost a third of the steel.

1.9 What else may we change?

So far we kept the geometry fixed. That is one more assumption. What could we change besides the areas?

On the left is our problem: the geometry is given, we change the thicknesses of the bars. This is called sizing optimization (optimizacija dimenzij). In the middle we move the pin \(B\) along the wall. This is shape optimization (optimizacija oblike), and since the directions of the bars change, the two forces change too. The effect is large: at the height \(h = 4\sqrt2 = 5.657\) m the volume drops from 621.2 to 514.3 cm³ (in the book: 29.2.3). On the right we allow a bar zero area, and the bar disappears. This is topology optimization (topološka optimizacija): what changes is which parts are connected to each other. When does our assumption hold? When the geometry is already decided, for instance because the pins are already built into the wall. In the book: 29.4.

1.10 What if we add a safety factor or a catalogue?

We took \(\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa, the yield stress, with no safety factor. Does a factor break the picture?

Let us draw the axes and the old limits at 60.61 and 75.76 mm², grey and dashed, because this is the state before. At their corner is the point \(\vek A^*\) on the line of 621.2 cm³. A factor of 1.5 lowers the allowed stress to \(\sigma_0/1.5\) in both bars. So each bar must be 1.5 times thicker, and both limits move away, to 90.91 and 113.64 mm². Where does the corner go? Both coordinates were multiplied by the same number, so the corner stays on the straight line from the origin and moves along it by the factor 1.5 to the new corner. The volume grows by the same factor, to 931.8 cm³. The picture is the same, only larger, and the solution stays fully stressed. That is why we may leave the factor out and add it at the end.

A second idealization: the area may be any positive number. Real bars have cross-sections from a catalogue. Effect: we choose the nearest catalogue point inside the shaded corner, and the volume is a little above 621.2 cm³.

What do we take away? The computer does not guess. It writes the problem as a point, limits and lines, and looks for the last touch. In the next part we ask what holds at that point when the limits are no longer straight.

2 At the best point the walls hold the balance: KKT and convexity

With two variables we see the best point. With a thousand bars there is no picture. How does a computer recognize that it stands at the best point? And when may we believe it that there is no better point anywhere else? We read the answer off one picture, the plane of the two cross-section areas of our wall truss.

We draw the axes. Horizontally the area of bar \(AC\), vertically the area of bar \(BC\), so every point of the plane is one choice of both areas. From the first part we know that a bar must not be too thin. Its stress must not exceed \(\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa, so we draw two walls: a vertical one at \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 60.61\) mm² and a horizontal one at \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 75.76\) mm². Only points to the right of and above them are allowed. Now lines of equal volume, like contour lines on a map: \(V_1 = 621.2\) cm³ and \(V_2 = 1000\) cm³. Towards the lower left corner the volume falls. Where does the descent stop? At the corner \(P\), where the two walls meet.

The claim of this part we say on this picture. At the best point, here the corner \(P\), the pull downhill is cancelled exactly by the pushes of the walls the point leans on. A wall only pushes and never pulls, and it pushes only if we touch it. If the problem is convex, a point where this holds is the best of all. Each part of this claim is now one "why?".

2.1 Why is there no improvement at \(P\)?

We stand at the corner \(P\). Where may we step at all, and does any step save steel? We may step only along the walls or into the inside. We draw two directions along the walls: \(\vek d_1\) to the right, where \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) grows, and \(\vek d_2\) upwards, where \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) grows. How much steel does a step along \(\vek d_1\) cost? Bar \(AC\) is 4 m long. If we thicken it by 1 mm², it gets \(4000\ \text{mm} \cdot 1\ \text{mm}^2 = 4\) cm³ more steel. This is the ground of this question, counting pieces of steel. Along \(\vek d_2\) the bar is 5 m long, a step costs 5 cm³ per mm². So the bar lengths are simply the components of the gradient, \(\nabla f = (4, 5)\) m. Every allowed step from \(P\) costs steel, and a step into the inside is a mix of both and costs too. So \(P\) cannot be improved.

Here we used an assumption for the first time. We computed the change of \(f\) from the derivative, as a first order change. Why may we? The volume is linear here, the level lines are straight, so the first order is the exact change. When may we not? If we could only pick sections from a catalogue, or only insert or remove a bar, there is no derivative. What does it cost for curved functions? The derivative only tells what happens in a small step. We measure the error of a larger step below, at the curved wall.

2.2 Why do we write it as a sum of arrows?

With a thousand bars we cannot try every direction. How do we say the same with one equation? We take the picture of a ball rolling downhill in a room with walls. We draw the blue arrow \(-\nabla f\). It points where we save steel fastest, its components are \((-4, -5)\) m. The ball would like to go there, but the walls stop it. In which direction does a wall push? Perpendicular to itself, because the gradient \(\nabla g_{\mathrm{i}}\) is perpendicular to the line \(g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). We draw two blackwhite arrows, \(\mu_1\nabla g_1\) to the left and \(\mu_2\nabla g_2\) downwards. They point into the wall, the push of the wall is exactly opposite. We draw them so because now we see it: the two blackwhite ones together, as the sides of a parallelogram, give exactly the blue one. The ball is at rest when the pushes of the walls cancel the pull downhill:

\[-\nabla f = \mu_1\nabla g_1 + \mu_2\nabla g_2 .\]

This is the first equation of the KKT conditions (pogoji KKT), (29.19). At \(P\) we have \(\nabla g_1 = (-\sigma_0, 0)\) and \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -\sigma_0)\), so from \((-4, -5) = \mu_1(-\sigma_0, 0) + \mu_2(0, -\sigma_0)\) we read

\[\mu_1 = \frac{4\ \text{m}}{\sigma_0} = 1.818\cdot 10^{-8}\ \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{N}} , \qquad \mu_2 = \frac{5\ \text{m}}{\sigma_0} = 2.273\cdot 10^{-8}\ \frac{\text{m}^3}{\text{N}} .\]

The numbers \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the multipliers (množitelji). They have the name because we multiply the constraint by them before adding it to the objective. In the book: 29.28 and (29.20).

2.3 Why does a wall only push, \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\)?

Have you ever leaned on a wall and been pulled towards it? No. A wall we lean on pushes back and never pulls. This is the ground. Now on the picture. Suppose \(\mu_1\) were negative. Then the wall \(g_1\) would help the ball away from itself, and a step along \(\vek d_1\), away from that wall, would lower the volume. So at \(P\) we would not be at the best point. The idea of the proof is exactly this: from the corner we go along a curve that releases one wall and stays on the others, and along it \(f\) must not fall. In the book: 29.29, 29.4.2.

The proof needs an assumption, regularity. The arrows of the active walls must be independent, none is a sum of the others. Why does it hold here? At \(P\) the blackwhite arrows are \((-\sigma_0, 0)\) and \((0, -\sigma_0)\), perpendicular to each other. When it fails we show at the end, at the cusp. What does it cost if it fails? The best point may be real, but there are no multipliers, and a computer that looks for the KKT conditions does not recognize it.

2.4 Why does a wall we do not touch not push?

Our problem has two more walls that we have not mentioned so far. Do they push too? An area must not be negative, so these walls are simply the axes, \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 0\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\). The point \(P\) is 60.61 mm² and 75.76 mm² away from them, since that is where the stress walls stand. Does it touch them? No. A wall we do not touch does not push, so their multipliers are \(\mu_3 = \mu_4 = 0\). This is the third part of the KKT conditions, complementarity (komplementarnost), \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\): for every wall its push or its distance is zero, at least one of the two. The walls we touch are called active (aktivni), because they work, they hold the point at the boundary. In the book: 29.27.

2.5 What does the size of the push tell?

The multipliers are \(1.818\cdot 10^{-8}\) and \(2.273\cdot 10^{-8}\) m³/N. What do these two numbers tell the designer? Let us move one wall and see.

We draw the axes once more, enlarged around the corner. We draw the horizontal wall \(g_2 = 0\), which we will not move. Then the vertical wall at 60.61 mm², grey dashed because it is the state before the change, and on it the corner \(P\). Now bar \(AC\) gets 1 kN more force. What happens to the wall? The area must be at least \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|/\sigma_0\), so the wall moves to the right, to \(14\,333/220 = 65.15\) mm², and we draw it orange. The new corner is \(P'\). By how much did the wall move? By \(1000\ \text{N}/220\ \text{MPa} = 4.545\) mm². In the picture the shift is enlarged, otherwise we would not see it. The level line through \(P\) has 621.2 cm³, the level line through \(P'\) 639.4 cm³. The difference is \(4\ \text{m}\cdot 4.545\ \text{mm}^2 = 18.18\) cm³. And how much is \(\mu_1 \cdot 1000\) N? Exactly \(18.18\) cm³. So the multiplier is a price. The push of a wall measures what this requirement costs: a kilonewton more in bar \(AC\) costs 18.18 cm³ of steel, in bar \(BC\) 22.73 cm³. In the book: 29.4.3.

2.6 What if the wall is curved?

So far the walls were straight. Does the balance of arrows survive a curved wall? Let us add a requirement of the designer: the joint \(C\) may sink by at most 2 mm.

We draw the wall and the truss with the areas of the corner \(P\). We hang the load. The bar \(BC\) stretches, the bar \(AC\) shortens, and the joint moves to \(C'\). How much lower is it? The sag is 15.34 mm. Allowed are 2 mm. So the corner \(P\) is no longer allowed, although the bars survive. The sag is the sum \(c_{\mathrm{AC}}/A_{\mathrm{AC}} + c_{\mathrm{BC}}/A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) ((29.21)): the elongation of each bar is inversely proportional to its area, and the sag involves both areas at once.

What does this requirement look like in the plane of areas? We draw new axes at a smaller scale, 1 cm is 250 mm². The old stress walls are now right next to the axes, far away. The new wall is a curve, because the sag is a sum of quotients. The volume level line has the same slope \(-0.8\) as before. We lower it until it touches the curve at the point \(Q\), \(Q = (464.85,\ 581.07)\) mm², with volume 4764.7 cm³. There the picture is the same as at the corner: the blue arrow \(-\nabla f\) \(= (-4, -5)\) m and the blackwhite arrow of the wall \(\mu\nabla g_{\mathrm{v}}\) are equal. One wall, one arrow, balance. This is the geometric view. It adds that we see the balance of arrows as a touch: the level line touches the boundary, so their perpendiculars are parallel. In the book: 29.4.6, (29.23).

Does a curved wall have a price too? It does, but with a catch. We draw the axes: horizontally the allowed sag \(\Delta_0\), vertically the least volume \(V^*\). First our choice, 2 mm and 4764.7 cm³. If we allow a larger sag, the volume falls along the curve \(S^2/\Delta_0\) ((29.23)). The multiplier is \(\mu = 2.382\) m³/m, that is 2382 cm³ saved per millimetre. This is the slope of the line that touches the curve at our point. So the estimate with the price is this line, and the curve is the true value. At 2.1 mm they still overlap, the estimate misses by 0.25 %. At 3 mm the true value is 3176.5 cm³, the estimate 2382.4 cm³, the difference 794.1 cm³, that is 25 %. The assumption that the price stays constant holds for small shifts of the wall. For the straight wall above it was exact, because there the line and the curve coincide.

One more assumption. How did we know that the stress walls do not work at \(Q\)? We guessed and checked: \(464.85 > 60.61\) and \(581.07 > 75.76\), the point does not touch them, the stresses are only 28.68 MPa. When does the guess fail? If a multiplier came out negative or some wall were crossed. What then? We guess again, with another set of active walls.

2.7 When is a KKT point really the best everywhere?

The KKT conditions look only at the neighbourhood of a point. Is that enough to believe the computer? Let us look at a counterexample.

We draw the axes, \(x\) horizontally, \(f\) vertically. We draw the parabola \(f = -x^2\) only between the walls \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\). Where do the KKT conditions hold? At three points. At the ends the pull downhill and the push of the wall cancel, with \(\mu = 2\). In the middle the derivative is zero and no wall works. The values are \(-1\), \(0\) and \(-1\). The middle is even the worst point, a peak. So the KKT conditions give candidates, not the winner. In the book: 29.4.5.

What does this cost in a computation? On the interval we draw a graph with two bottoms and a peak between them. At the point \(x_3 = 0.9456\) the value is 0.3894, lower than all values in its neighbourhood. A procedure that goes downhill stops here. But at the point \(x_1 = -1.0467\) the value is \(-0.4095\). The procedure misses by 0.7989 and does not notice. For a structure this is a heavier structure than needed.

What saves us? Convexity (konveksnost). We draw the axes and a bowl-shaped graph. Between two points of the graph we draw the chord \(PQ\). For a bowl it lies above the graph: at the middle the point \(R\) on the chord is at 4.3542, the point \(T\) on the graph lower, at 2.8717. This is the ground: in a bowl the chord is above the bottom. Now the proof in one sentence. Suppose we are stuck in a local bottom and somewhere else there is a lower point. The chord to it goes downhill from the start, the graph is below the chord, so it is lower also right next to our bottom. But a local bottom forbids that. So in a bowl every local bottom is global. In the book: 29.18, 29.17.

The same holds for the KKT conditions: for a convex problem a KKT point is the best of all, 29.30. The proof in the book uses another property of a bowl. On the same graph we draw two tangents: both lie below the graph. The proof adds up such tangent planes, multiplied by the multipliers, and finds that no feasible point is lower. So convexity is the assumption that carries the whole conclusion. When does it fail? For \(-x^2\) and for the two bottoms above. What does it cost? The KKT conditions give candidates only, and the procedure may get stuck in the local bottom, at 0.3894 instead of \(-0.4095\).

2.8 Why is our problem convex?

Is our problem a bowl over a region without dents? Let us check both parts. The volume is linear, flat. A plane is the limiting case of a bowl, a chord lies in it. The sag is a sum of \(c_{\mathrm{k}}/A_{\mathrm{k}}\). The function \(1/A\) curves upwards for \(A > 0\), its second derivative is \(2/A^3 > 0\). So the region where the sag is at most 2 mm lies on the bulging side of the curve and has no dents. The problem is convex, so \(Q\) is the best choice of all: no choice of areas with a sag up to 2 mm uses less steel. Two bars with equal areas use 1.2 % more. In the book: 29.4.6.

2.9 Back up

Now we build everything again from the ground. The bar lengths give the direction downhill, \((-4, -5)\) m. The walls push perpendicular to themselves. At rest the pushes cancel the pull, this is the first equation. Walls only push, \(\mu_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\). Only the walls we touch push, this is complementarity. A bowl over a region without dents has a single bottom, so the resting point is the best globally. The left half of the picture is the corner of the first part, seen as a balance of three arrows. The right half is the same balance on a curved wall. A computer with a thousand bars checks exactly these three lines and needs no picture.

What should someone with only two minutes keep? The ball in the room. The pull downhill is the bar lengths, the walls are the requirements, the ball stops where the pushes of the walls cancel the pull, and the strength of a push is the price of the requirement. The mechanical view adds the experience with a wall, the geometric one the touch of level line and boundary, and the view by price the meaning of the number \(\mu\).

2.10 Where the picture fails: the cusp

Does the ball always stop so that the walls cancel the pull? Not if two walls meet in a cusp. We draw the axes and the grey set between the axis \(x_1\) and the curve \(x_2 = (1 - x_1)^3\). At the point \(S\) it narrows to a tip. We minimize \(f = -x_1\), so \(S\) is the best point. The gradient \(\nabla f\) points to the left. The arrows of both walls point up and down, along the same vertical line. The pull points horizontally. Can we build it from vertical arrows? No, no sum of vertical arrows is horizontal. There are no multipliers, although \(S\) is the best. That is why the theorem asks for regularity, independent wall arrows. In our problem they were perpendicular and there was no trouble. In the book: 29.4.4.

3 The stiffest structure: steel goes where the energy is densest

We have \(300\ \text{cm}^3\) of steel and three places for it. Where do we put it so that the node under the force gives way as little as possible? And how do the shapes with holes that a computer draws come out of this?

We draw a wall with three points \(S_1\), \(S_2\), \(S_3\), one metre apart. One metre below the middle one is the node \(C\) (node). Each point is joined to the node by a steel bar: bar \(1\), bar \(2\) and bar \(3\). At \(C\) we pull with a force \(F = 10\) kN, tilted from the vertical by an angle of \(30^\circ\). To start, we share the steel equally: each bar has the area \(78.36\ \text{mm}^2\), so all three lines are equally thick.

Which bar works hardest? Below the truss we draw an axis and on it, for each bar, a column as high as the energy the bar stores per cubic metre (energy density, gostota energije). Bar \(1\) has \(30.6\) kJ/m³, bar \(2\) has \(21.4\), bar \(3\) barely \(0.8\). The first is overworked, the third hardly works.

What if we take one cubic centimetre of steel from the third bar and give it to the first? On the right the first bar is thicker, the third thinner, the second stays as it was. The node now gives way less, by as much as the difference of the columns \(30.6 - 0.8\) times the volume moved says: about \(0.03\) J less compliance. That is the whole story in small. We move steel from a low column to a high one as long as the move pays.

When does moving stop paying? We draw an axis and on it the columns for three designs. On the left is our start: the columns are uneven and the compliance is \(5.1625\) J. In the middle is the end of the moving: bars \(1\) and \(2\) have columns of equal height, both at the dashed level \(\lambda = 10.6\) kJ/m³, the third bar has no steel and no column. The compliance is \(3.1735\) J, \(38.5\ \%\) less than at the start.

The claim we now support: a structure is stiffest when all bars with steel have the same energy density, \(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2 = \lambda\), and no empty place would have a higher one. As long as the columns are uneven, moving steel from a low column to a high one makes the structure stiffer.

3.1 What does "stiff" mean as one single number?

How do we say with one number that a structure gives way little? The node moves sideways and down, those are two numbers. Which one should be small?

Take the wall and the truss we will find at the end, with the node \(C\). We pull with the force \(\vek F\). The node moves to \(C'\), by \(0.2323\) mm to the right and \(0.2323\) mm down. Now from \(C'\) we drop a perpendicular onto the line of the force. It cuts off the part of the displacement along the force, \(0.3173\) mm. This is the path the force travels. Force times this path is the work of the force, \(10\ \text{kN}\cdot 0.3173\ \text{mm} = 3.1735\) J. This number is the compliance (podajnost), \(C = \vek F^\T\vek u\). The name says what it means: the more the structure complies with the force, the larger it is. The smaller \(C\), the less the loaded point gives way in the direction in which we pull it.

An assumption entered here: the force does not depend on where we put the steel, so we do not count the weight of the bars. Why may we? \(300\ \text{cm}^3\) of steel weighs \(22.96\) N, that is \(0.23\ \%\) of the force \(F\). When not? When the self-weight is comparable to the load. There added steel also adds load, and the conclusion about the columns no longer holds in this form.

How does the compliance depend on the thickness? Take one bar alone, the middle one, \(1\) m long, with a force of \(10\) kN. On the horizontal axis is the area \(A\), on the vertical one the compliance. Under the force the bar stretches by \(FL/(EA)\), force times this path is \(C = F^2L/(EA)\), in numbers \(510.2/A\) joules when \(A\) is in mm² (blue curve). A thicker bar is stiffer, but each extra square millimetre brings less.

The ground below this: work is force times path, and a stiffer spring moves less under the same force, as we saw on the path of the force. The compliance is also twice the energy the force stores in the truss, \(C = 2U\). That is why we may speak of it as energy. In the book: 29.5.1, 29.32 and 29.33.

3.2 Why does a thicker bar lower the compliance by exactly its energy density?

How much do we gain if we add a little steel to a bar? Stay with the curve and ask how fast it falls. The slope of the touching line says it. At \(A = 100\ \text{mm}^2\) the bar is thin and stretches by \(0.5102\) mm, the line is steep. At \(300\ \text{mm}^2\) the displacement is \(0.1701\) mm and the line is flatter. At \(600\ \text{mm}^2\) the displacement is \(0.0850\) mm and the line is nearly level. Where the bar stretches a lot, added steel does the most.

How much exactly? For one bar we differentiate \(C = F^2L/(EA)\) and get \(-F^2L/(EA^2)\). Since the stretch per length is \(\varepsilon = F/(EA)\), this is \(-E\varepsilon^2 L\). The volume of the bar is \(V = LA\), so the derivative with respect to the volume is the derivative with respect to the area divided by \(L\): \(-E\varepsilon^2\). The gain per cubic metre of added steel is \(E\varepsilon^2\), exactly the height of the column in the first figure. For a truss the same holds for each bar, (29.30): \(\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2 L_{\mathrm{e}}\).

The idea of the proof for the truss is on the lines: the compliance is the largest value of a family of expressions linear in the areas, so its slope is simply the slope of the line touching it, and the displacement need not be differentiated. In the book: 29.36.

3.3 Why must the columns be equal?

Why does the moving stop exactly at equal columns and not earlier? There is exactly \(300\ \text{cm}^3\) of steel. We get no more, we can only move it. Back to the move. What we take from the third bar costs us \(0.8\) kJ/m³ times \(10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3\). What we give to the first brings \(30.6\) kJ/m³ times the same volume. The difference is about \(-0.03\) J, the computation with the whole truss gives \(-0.0296\) J. The move pays.

When does it not? Only when both columns are equally high. As long as any two columns of bars with steel differ, moving from the lower to the higher is a gain. So at the end all columns of bars with steel are at the same height, and we call this height \(\lambda\). The ground below this is counting: if moving a unit from a poorer place to a richer one gains, we are not at the best. At the best a unit gains the same everywhere.

This is the balance of the previous part. The number \(\lambda\) is the multiplier (množitelj) of the volume constraint, the price of steel, and \(\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} + \lambda L_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) is the first line of the KKT conditions. In the book: 29.37, (29.31).

3.4 Why may an empty bar have a lower column but not a higher one?

In the middle plot the third bar has no column, so it is below the line \(\lambda\). Does that not break the rule of equal columns? No. We cannot take steel from an empty bar, because it has none. The area does not go below zero. So a low column at an empty place is fine, we could not move anything away from there anyway. A high column at an empty place, though, means that steel there would do more than anywhere else.

Look at the right plot. Bars \(2\) and \(3\) carry the same stress \(78.87\) MPa and have equal columns, \(31.7\) kJ/m³. The empty bar \(1\) would have a column of \(126.9\), high above the dashed line. The compliance is \(9.5205\) J, three times the optimum. Equal columns are therefore not enough, empty places must stay below the line. This is the complementarity of the previous part.

3.5 Why does the third bar vanish?

Why does the computation take all the steel from the third bar, of all bars? Let us see where the node moves.

We draw the wall and bars \(1\) and \(2\) before loading. Under the force the node moves to \(C'\), the displacement \(\vek u = (0.2323;\ -0.2323)\) mm is drawn three thousand times larger. Now we draw the third bar. The displacement is perpendicular to it.

What does that mean for its length? Swing a stone on a string. When the stone moves across the string, the string does not stretch. The node moves across the third bar, along the tangent of the circle about \(S_3\), so the length of the third bar does not change to first order. A bar that does not stretch carries nothing, and gets no steel. The ground structure (izhodiščno paličje) had three bars, the optimum has two. Which bars exist came out of sizing. In the book: 29.5.3.

Two assumptions are at work here. The first: small displacements and linear elastic steel. Why does it hold? Each component of the displacement is \(0.23\) mm against lengths of \(1\) m, a ratio of \(2.3\cdot 10^{-4}\), and the stress \(45.5\) MPa is far below \(220\) MPa. When not? For large rotations and for yielding. Effect: "across does not stretch" holds only to first order, the neglected stretch is of second order in \(2.3\cdot 10^{-4}\). The second: the ground structure, three points and three bars, was chosen in advance. Effect: the optimum is the best only among these three bars.

3.6 How does a computer find it?

With three bars we guessed the optimum. And with a thousand? We read the rule of the columns as a recipe, the optimality criteria update (posodobitev po merilu optimalnosti). A bar with a column above \(\lambda\) is thickened, one below is thinned, by \(A_{\mathrm{e}} \leftarrow A_{\mathrm{e}}\,(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2/\lambda)^{1/2}\), and \(\lambda\) is chosen so that the volume stays \(300\ \text{cm}^3\).

On the left axes is the area against the iteration. We start at \(78.36\ \text{mm}^2\) for all. The first bar thickens, the second stays near the start, the third thins towards zero. On the right axes are the columns over the iterations. The first and the second come together and settle at the dashed \(\lambda \approx 10.6\) kJ/m³, the third stays below it. After \(30\) iterations the areas are \(155.2\), \(80.3\) and \(0.10\ \text{mm}^2\). In the book: 29.7.5, 29.54, (29.46).

3.7 And the plate with holes?

So where do the shapes with holes come from? The same recipe, only instead of three bars we take \(300\) small squares, each with a density between \(0\) and \(1\). The computation happily leaves grey squares, half full, and such a beam cannot be made of material and holes. So we make grey a bad deal, by penalization (kaznovanje). On the axes is density against stiffness. At \(p = 1\) half the steel gives half the stiffness. At \(p = 3\) half the steel gives only an eighth of the stiffness, but we pay half. Grey does not pay, and the squares choose blackwhite or whiteblack.

Without a filter the result is nearly blackwhite and whiteblack, but it has a checkerboard, \(51\) checkerboard windows. With a filter there is no checkerboard, but the compliance is \(225.9\) against \(190.9\), that is \(18.4\ \%\) more. At \(p = 1\) grey remains. The price of penalization: the problem is no longer convex, and from the previous part we know what that means. After \(60\) iterations we have no proven minimum, the last change is still \(0.0288\). In the book: 29.9.2, 29.67, 29.9.5.

3.8 Back up

We put it all together from the ground up. Force times path is the compliance. One bar: \(C = F^2L/(EA)\), thicker is stiffer, the gain per added cubic metre is the energy density (curve and lines). Three bars with a given volume: we move from a low column to a high one, each move lowers \(C\). The moving ends when the columns of the bars with steel are level at \(\lambda\) and no empty place stands above it. For our truss the areas are \(155.29\), \(80.38\) and \(0\ \text{mm}^2\), the stress in both bars \(45.53\) MPa, the compliance from \(5.1625\) J to \(3.1735\) J.

And the link to the first part. With one material, equal energy density \(E\varepsilon^2\) means equal \(|\sigma|\) in all bars with steel (29.38). The stiffest structure is again fully stressed, only we reached it from energy and nobody prescribed the stress.

What should someone with only two minutes keep? The move of one cubic centimetre. Steel is money, the column is the return per unit, and we move money to where it earns more until it earns the same everywhere. This view by counting adds that the optimum needs no equation, only a comparison of two columns. The view by eye adds a rule: a column is high where the bar stretches most, and the bar that lies across the displacement gets nothing.

3.9 Perspective: the price of steel

What is one more cubic centimetre of steel worth? Algebraically this is the KKT of the previous part. The number \(\lambda = 10.6\) kJ/m³ is the price of steel in joules of compliance per cubic metre: one more cubic centimetre of steel would lower \(C\) at the optimum by about \(\lambda \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3 = 0.0106\) J. At the same time \(\lambda = C^*/V_0 = 3.1735\ \text{J}/(3\cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3)\). This view adds what it costs when steel runs short or we get more.

3.10 Perspective: the direction of the load

What happens to the best truss if we turn the force? At \(\theta = 0^\circ\) only the middle bar remains, with \(300\ \text{mm}^2\) and \(C = 1.7007\) J. At \(30^\circ\) bars \(1\) and \(2\) work. At \(60^\circ\) bars \(1\) and \(3\) work, with \(167.30\) and \(44.83\ \text{mm}^2\), \(C = 5.1020\) J. This view adds that the best structure follows the load. As a test we also took \(20\,000\) random designs at \(30^\circ\): the best has \(3.1783\) J, none is below \(3.1735\) J.

This view also shows two assumptions. The first: one load case. The task gives one force, while a real structure gets forces from several directions. Effect: at \(\theta = 0^\circ\) the optimum is a single bar that does not hold the node sideways. An optimum for one load can be a mechanism for another. The second: no buckling. At \(0^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\) it holds, because all bars are in tension. At \(60^\circ\), though, the third bar is compressed by \(2588\) N, its Euler load is \(154.7\) N, and it would buckle at a force seventeen times smaller. Effect: the optimum at \(60^\circ\) is not usable without a buckling constraint.
