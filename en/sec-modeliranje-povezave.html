Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.10 Connections

The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19. Each section of this chapter treats one or two of the three errors that definition 17.2 in section 17.1 separates: the modelling error, the discretization error and the numerical error. In figure 17.8 these are three steps on the axis of the wanted quantity, from the physical system to the number from the program. For each section we say where its result returns, and for each connection also which of the three steps it shortens and which ones it leaves.

  • Hierarchy of models and the modelling error (section 17.1): lemma 17.3 says that the ratio of the shear part to the bending part of the tip deflection of a cantilever is \(E/(4kG)\,(H/L)^2\). For a steel cantilever with a rectangular section the Euler-Bernoulli model is below the Timoshenko model by \(16{,}32\) % at \(L/H = 2\), the first dot of the blue curve. That is the share of the shear piece \(0{,}796\ \mu\)m in the tip deflection, the piece that the assumption of a plane section without a shear angle leaves out. At \(L/H = 20\) the difference is only \(0{,}19\) %, the last dot of the same curve, because the shear piece grows with \(L\) and the bending piece with \(L^3\). The same question, when a one-dimensional model still suffices, returns for surface models in plates (MK, AK, chapter 18) and shells (MK, AK, chapter 22), and for flexible robot links, where the beam is the model of the link (DSK, chapter 28). This is the modelling error, and the mesh does not reduce it: a single Euler-Bernoulli beam element already gives the deflection of that model exactly (three points on the lower axis coincide), while the whole gap to the plane model stays.
  • Body to model, point force and submodel (section 17.2): theorem 17.9 proves that the total potential energy of a point force on a plane body is not bounded below. The reason is in figure 17.20: a field that equals \(1\) under the force and narrows around it has less and less energy, while the work of the force on it stays the same. So by corollary 17.10 the displacement under the force grows without bound on nested meshes (in the ring model every doubling adds about \(\ln 2\) in the unit \(F/(2\pi T)\)), while in a bar or a beam it does not (chapters 7 and 12). The load on a small area, which the point force replaces, is treated in chapter 25 (DSK). The submodel of lemma 17.5, loaded on the cut by the displacement of the full model, rests on the uniqueness of the boundary value problem (theorem 11.4) and on Saint-Venant's principle of section 11.11. The half model of lemma 7.39 is extended by chapter 19 to mirror planes in several dimensions and to constraints between nodes (AK). The point force, the submodel and the half model are decisions about the modelling error, which a finer mesh does not correct.
  • Mesh convergence (section 17.3): the order of convergence from three nested meshes and Richardson's theorem 17.14 give for the first eigenvalue of a bar with both ends fixed, on three successive triples of meshes, the orders \(2{,}05\), \(2{,}02\) and \(2{,}01\). The first is \(\log_2(d_1/d_2) = \log_2(1{,}613/0{,}390)\), the binary logarithm of the ratio of the two distances between the points of three meshes. The extrapolation steps on from the value of the finer mesh by a third of the difference \(d_1\): at order \(2\) the error of the finer mesh is a quarter of the error of the coarse one, so the difference between the meshes is three times the error that still remains. The meshes with \(n = 4\) and \(8\) elements give \(9{,}867227\), \(0{,}0024\) below \(\pi^2\), and the meshes with \(n = 16\) and \(32\) a value that differs from \(\pi^2\) by only \(10^{-5}\). The same procedure holds for every quantity computed on a sequence of meshes, so also for temperatures (VT, AK, chapter 14) and for displacements of plane elements (AK, chapter 15). The discretization error falls as the mesh is refined, the modelling error and the numerical error stay.
  • Stresses from FE results (section 17.4): corollary 17.18 states the error estimate for finite elements promised by chapter 1 (the mean value theorem), and the stresses are one order worse than the displacements, because we compute them from the derivative of the displacement: in figure 17.73 the error of the displacement falls to a quarter at every doubling of the elements, the error of the stress only to a half. The equivalent stress of definition 2.107 and the largest shear stress of theorem 2.93 are computed at the inner edge of the tube of chapter 11 from element stresses, which satisfy the boundary condition only in the limit (the circles at the edge: with four elements \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -19{,}32\) instead of \(-30\) MPa). For continuum elements (AK, chapter 15) the Gauss points are the stress points, which is the same idea as the exact strain at one point of an element (the dots in the middles of the elements lie almost on the Lamé curves). A finer mesh reduces only the discretization error of the stresses, the modelling error and the numerical error stay.
  • Mesh quality (section 17.5): lemma 17.22 proves that the bar element with three nodes, its middle node at the fraction \(c\) of the length, has \(J > 0\) on the whole element exactly for \(1/4 < c < 3/4\), the grey band in figure 17.39. Outside the band the map folds over and two values of \(\xi\) mean the same point of the bar. The letter \(J\) here is the one-dimensional \(\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}\xi\), in chapter 2 it is \(J = \det\mat F\), the ratio of volumes (lemma 2.77), and \(J_2\) is the second invariant of the deviator (equation (2.105)). For plane and solid elements the quality measures are only named, their Jacobian belongs to chapter 15 (AK). The quarter-point element (\(c = 1/4\), on the edge of the band) captures a smooth field worse than the element with its middle node at the midpoint, and a field that behaves like \(\sqrt{x}\) exactly. The shape and the arrangement of elements change only the discretization error, they do not reduce the modelling error.
  • Large systems of equations (section 17.6): lemma 17.29 gives the condition number \(\kappa\) of definition 6.31 for a bar fixed at one end and free at the other, \(175{,}1\), \(16\,370\) and \(1\,622\,757\) for \(10\), \(100\) and \(1000\) elements. Ten times more elements raise it almost a hundredfold (slope \(2\)), because the softest shape is cut into more and shorter steps and its eigenvalue falls like \(1/n^2\), while the largest one stays below \(4\). The band of the matrix from lemma 7.42 and one factorization for several load cases from lemma 7.47 remain valid for large models too. Constraints that chapter 19 eliminates from the system reduce the number of unknowns, while the penalty method keeps them and, by lemma 12.29, worsens \(\kappa\). The reduced model of chapter 21 shrinks the system to a few modes (DSK). The locking of continuum elements for a nearly incompressible material is a penalty system like the shear locking of the beam (AK, chapter 15). The section treats the numerical error: a finer mesh reduces the discretization error but raises \(\kappa\) and with it the upper bound of the numerical error: with \(1000\) elements about six of the sixteen digits are lost.
  • Nonlinear statics (section 17.7): Newton's method, which converges quadratically when the tangent stiffness at the root is not zero, and theorem 17.35, by which a column made of a rigid bar with an imperfection and a horizontal spring has a limit load of \(0{,}931\) of the critical one at \(\theta_0 = 0{,}01\), complete the linear buckling analysis of definition 12.51 and the upper bound of theorem 12.53. The linear analysis gives the same critical load for both column models and cannot tell the stable model from the unstable one. Theorems 13.96 and 8.148 are about the perfect column, imperfections require a nonlinear model. The modelling error here is the linear model, which does not know the limit load: its straight line passes the top and goes on upwards, while the true path turns down beyond the top. At \(p = 0{,}9\) the linear model gives the rotation \(0{,}0090\) rad instead of \(0{,}0955\) rad. The numerical error is the stopped iteration, and a model with one degree of freedom has no discretization error. Plasticity and nonlinear contact are not treated in the book.
  • Nonlinear dynamics (section 17.8): the Newmark family of definition 17.36 contains the trapezoidal rule of chapter 10 (theorem 10.95) and central differences (theorem 10.99). Stability for one mode follows definition 10.94. For \(\gamma = 0{,}6\) and \(\beta = 0{,}3025\) the spectral radius on the vertical line \(\omega\Delta t = 2\pi/20\) is \(0{,}995\), a little below \(1\). After twenty steps, that is after one period, the amplitude is \(0{,}99520^{20} = 0{,}9082\) of the initial one, which means that numerical damping damps the low modes too. Physical damping with a damping matrix (DSK, chapter 20) is a different notion and must not be confused with the damping of a scheme. For the pendulum of theorem 8.139, released from the horizontal position, the trapezoidal rule with the step \(2\pi/20\) (in units of \(\sqrt{l/g}\)) lengthens the period by \(0{,}57\) %: the dots of the scheme lag behind the accurate curve (the figure shows the twice longer step \(2\pi/10\) with a lag of \(2{,}23\) %, so that it is visible). Unlike for a linear system it does not conserve the energy exactly (theorem 17.44): in three swings it deviates by at most \(8{,}06 \cdot 10^{-3}\) from the initial value \(1\). The step limit through the ratio \(h/c\) of chapter 13 holds for explicit steps. A smaller step reduces the discretization error in time, a more accurate Newton iteration the numerical error, and the modelling error (a point mass on a rigid rod, without friction and air) stays.
  • Plan of an analysis (section 17.9): the steps and checks of sections 7.10.1 and 7.10.3 and the split of errors of section 7.10.4 are the procedure, and section 17.9 applies it to the tube of theorem 11.28: the discretization error of the von Mises stress at the bore at 16 elements (\(1{,}43\) % on the blue line) is larger than the difference between plane strain and closed ends (the dashed line at \(0{,}53\) %), while the difference between open and closed ends (\(3{,}2\) %, \(96{,}56\) against \(93{,}53\) MPa) is a modelling error that the mesh does not reduce, because the axial stress of the ends enters neither of the stresses that the mesh computes. Chapter 29 repeats the analysis in a loop, so there it matters which error each step reduces.
  • Planned in other chapters: plane and solid elements with the isoparametric map and the Jacobian matrix in chapter 15, symmetry and constraints in chapter 19, damping matrices in chapter 20, reduction and the eigenproblem of large systems in chapter 21, contact and Flamant's solution in chapter 25. Plasticity, large strains, the arc-length method, iterative solvers and mesh generation algorithms are only named.
  • Limits of the chapter: the error expansion \(e = C h^q + \dots\) for a general FE quantity, the number of lost digits of about \(\log_{10}\kappa\), the quality limits of plane and solid elements, the ZZ error estimate and adaptive meshing are cited from sources, not proved. Newmark stability is proved for a linear system mode by mode and only on the line \(\beta = (\gamma + \tfrac12)^2/4\), not for the general region \(2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \tfrac12\) and not for a nonlinear system. In figure 17.61 the proved part is thus one curve, and the rest of the region is cited from COOK (11.13-7).
