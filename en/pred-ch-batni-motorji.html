Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

Chapter 23Lecture: Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

1 Why the piston does not move like a sine

The shaft of the engine turns evenly. We would expect the piston to swing nicely, like a sine, the same at the top and at the bottom. It does not. At the top its acceleration is \(1{,}67\) times larger than at the bottom, and its motion contains a part that repeats twice per turn. Where does that come from? We look at everything on engine \(E\). It is the book's own model, not data of an existing engine: crank \(a = 50\) mm, conrod \(l = 200\) mm, \(1500\) revolutions per minute.

Let us draw the mechanism four times, at quarter turns. First the cylinder axis with the point \(O\), about which the shaft turns. To the shaft we fix the crank (ročica), a blue rod of length \(a\), which we turn by the angle \(\theta\): \(0\), \(90°\), \(180°\) and \(270°\). On its end, the crank pin (čep), we hang the conrod (ojnica), a blackwhite rod of length \(l\). The other end of the conrod holds the piston (bat), which the cylinder lets slide only along the axis.

Where is the piston? We measure from \(O\) along the axis. First we look at how much of the axis the crank covers: that is its shadow if we shone a light from above. The crank's shadow is \(+50\), \(0\), \(-50\), \(0\) mm. Then we look at how much of the axis the conrod covers. The conrod's shadow is \(200\), \(193{,}65\), \(200\), \(193{,}65\) mm. The conrod is \(200\) mm long, but when it is tilted it covers less. We draw the difference in orange, the shortfall (primanjkljaj) \(l - s\): \(0\), \(6{,}35\), \(0\), \(6{,}35\) mm.

Here is the whole claim of this part. The position of the piston is the crank's shadow and the conrod's shadow laid end to end. The crank's shadow goes from plus to minus and back once per turn. The conrod's shadow shortens whenever the conrod tilts, and it tilts the same way when the pin is above the axis and when it is below, so it shortens twice per turn. That is the whole second harmonic (drugi harmonik).

1.1 Why is the position of the piston a sum of two shadows?

How far from \(O\) is the piston? We reach it along two rods laid one after the other: along the crank from \(O\) to the pin and along the conrod from the pin to the piston. Along the axis we get as far as the first rod covers, and as far again as the second one covers. In the panel at \(\theta = 0\) we see it right on the shadows: \(50 + 200 = 250\) mm.

Here we assumed something: that the crank and the conrod are rigid. Why may we? We compute the shadows from the lengths of the crank \(a\) and the conrod \(l\), and these do not change for a rigid rod. When does it fail? A real conrod stretches and shortens a little under force. The book does not estimate this change, so we give no number here either. In the book: 23.7 and 23.8.

1.2 Why does the conrod's shadow get shorter?

The conrod is \(200\) mm long all the time. How can it then cover less than \(200\) mm of the axis? Look at the panel at \(90°\). The pin is \(50\) mm above the axis, the conrod from it to the piston is \(200\) mm long, and at \(O\) there is a right angle. That is a right triangle. By Pythagoras the conrod's shadow is

\[s = \sqrt{200^2 - 50^2}\ \text{mm} = 193{,}65\ \text{mm} .\]

We know the same from a ladder: a ladder leaned higher against a wall reaches less far along the floor.

How do we see it as a motion? Lay the conrod flat from the pin, grey dashed. Its end would be \(200\) mm right of the pin. Now let it drop until the end lands on the axis: the end draws an arc and moves back, by \(6{,}35\) mm. The conrod is then tilted by the angle \(\varphi = 14{,}48°\), while at \(0\) and \(180°\) it lies on the axis, \(\varphi = 0\).

One more assumption hides here, the conrod is longer than the crank, \(\lambda = a/l < 1\). Why does it hold? For engine \(E\), \(\lambda = 0{,}25\). When not? If the conrod is shorter than the crank, the pin cannot go round, because at \(90°\) the conrod does not reach the axis, since the hypotenuse would be shorter than a leg (23.9).

1.3 Why twice per turn?

What does the shortfall depend on? Only on how far from the axis the pin is. Whether the pin is above the axis or below it, the conrod does not care: in the panels at \(90°\) and at \(270°\) the tilt is the same, \(14{,}48°\), and the shortfall is the same, \(6{,}35\) mm. At \(0\) and \(180°\) the conrod lies on the axis and there is no shortfall.

Now let us draw one turn. On the horizontal axis we put the angle \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(360°\). The first curve is the crank's shadow: \(+50\), \(0\), \(-50\), \(0\), \(+50\). One peak and one valley, once per turn. The second is the shortfall, on an enlarged scale: \(0\), \(6{,}35\), \(0\), \(6{,}35\), \(0\). Two peaks per turn. That is the second harmonic, seen without a single formula.

The idea of the proof in the book lies in two symmetries. If we mirror the crank across the axis, as from \(90°\) to \(270°\), the shortfall does not change, and if we turn it by half a turn, as from \(0\) to \(180°\), it does not change either. The mirror tells us that the conrod's shadow has no sine terms. The half turn tells us that it has no odd harmonics, since a function that repeats already after half a turn cannot have a part that repeats once or three times per turn. So the first harmonic comes only from the crank, and there are no odd higher ones. In the book: 23.12.

This conclusion stands on the assumption that the cylinder axis passes through \(O\). Why does it hold? For engine \(E\) we chose it so. When not? If we move the cylinder \(e = 10\) mm above \(O\), the pin at \(90°\) is no longer as far from the cylinder axis as at \(270°\), the symmetry breaks and sine terms and odd harmonics appear. The sine coefficient of the first order is then \(b_1 = -0{,}051291\) in units of \(a\Omega^2\) (23.13).

1.4 Why is it worse at the top than at the bottom?

The shortfall is the same at \(90°\) and at \(270°\). Where does the difference between top and bottom come from, then? For the answer we go from the position to the acceleration, which tells how fast the speed changes. Here we need the shaft to turn at a constant angular speed \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s, so that the angle is proportional to time. Why may we? The position as a function of the angle does not need it at all, only the step to time does. What does it give us? The acceleration is then \(\Omega^2\) times the second derivative of the position with respect to the angle, nothing else. When does it fail? A real shaft speeds up and slows down a little, which 23.5 treats.

We draw the axis of the angle \(\theta\) and the axis of acceleration, measured in units of \(a\Omega^2 = 1233{,}70\) m/s\(^2\). The crank's shadow gives the first harmonic, \(-\cos\theta\). At the top, at \(\theta = 0\), its shadow is largest. There the motion turns, the speed away from the shaft becomes a speed toward the shaft, so the acceleration points toward the shaft. At the bottom, at \(\pi\), the shadow is smallest, the motion turns the other way and the acceleration points away from the shaft.

And the conrod? Its shadow is longest in both dead centres (mrtvi legi), at \(0\) and at \(\pi\), since there it lies on the axis. Every move of the crank shortens it. So its part of the acceleration points toward the shaft in both positions. At the top the parts of the crank and of the conrod add, at the bottom they oppose. The sum of the first two harmonics shows it with the values \(-1{,}25\) and \(+0{,}75\). Let us write it on the board:

\[\text{top: } -(1 + \lambda)\,a\Omega^2 = -1542{,}1\ \text{m/s}^2 , \qquad \text{bottom: } (1 - \lambda)\,a\Omega^2 = 925{,}3\ \text{m/s}^2 , \qquad \frac{1{,}25}{0{,}75} = 1{,}67 .\]

That is \(157{,}2\) times the acceleration of gravity at the top and \(94{,}3\) times at the bottom. The exact acceleration lies almost exactly on the sum of the first two. In the book: 23.11, points 3 and 4.

1.5 Back up

Now we build everything again from the ground. We write three lines on the board.

  1. The crank's shadow is \(50\cos\theta\) mm, once per turn.
  2. The conrod's shadow is \(200\) mm minus a shortfall that swings between \(0\) and \(6{,}35\) mm twice per turn. Around its middle this is about \(3{,}2\cos 2\theta\) mm (exactly \(a_2 = 3{,}17531\) mm from the table in the book).
  3. We differentiate twice, as in figure L23.2. Each \(\cos n\theta\) picks up a factor \(n^2\), so the small \(3{,}2\) mm term grows in the acceleration to \(4 \cdot 3{,}17531/50 = 0{,}254\) of the big one. That is the second blue bar next to the first.

Let us draw the mechanism once more, now at any angle. First the cylinder with its axis and on it the shaft \(O\). About \(O\) turns the crank, its pin \(A\) runs on a circle. The conrod joins the pin to the point \(B\) on the piston. At the angle \(\theta\) the pin is \(a\sin\theta\) above the axis, the conrod is tilted by the angle \(\varphi\), and the position of the piston \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is the sum of both shadows, with the conrod's shadow by Pythagoras:

\[x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta} .\]

This is (23.5). Below are the two dead centres: the top one, where the conrod lies in line with the crank and \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = 250\) mm, and the bottom one, where they overlap and \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = 150\) mm. The difference is the stroke (hod), \(100\) mm, twice the crank. The two rows of the strip below in figure L23.1, the crank's shadow and the shortfall, are exactly the first and the second harmonic.

In the third line we kept only two harmonics. Why may we? The bars of the amplitudes are drawn on a logarithmic scale, each labelled tick means a hundred times less. The fourth harmonic is \(0{,}004098\) of the first in the acceleration, the sixth \(0{,}000074\). The second is exactly \(0{,}254025\) instead of \(\lambda = 0{,}25\), a difference of \(1{,}6\) %. What does it cost? The acceleration from two terms differs from the exact one by at most \(0{,}66\) % of the largest acceleration (23.14 and the table below 23.12). When would it be worse? For a shorter conrod, a larger \(\lambda\). The orange bars are the cylinder moved by \(e = 10\) mm, which we met above: there the third and the fifth bar stand too.

1.6 By hand

Whoever has only a few minutes should keep the four panels of figure L23.1. The conrod lies flat, tilts, lies flat, tilts. When it tilts, it covers less of the axis, by \(6{,}35\) mm. Four quarters, two tilted positions, twice per turn. This view adds that we see the word twice without a single formula.

1.7 By numbers

Is the piston at mid-stroke after a quarter turn? Let us draw the axis of the angle \(\theta\) and the axis of the position \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\). The piston moves between \(250\) mm at the top and \(150\) mm at the bottom, so the middle of the stroke is at \(200\) mm. If the piston swung as a pure cosine, it would be exactly in the middle after a quarter turn. Now we draw the true position. After a quarter turn the piston is at \(193{,}65\) mm, so already \(6{,}35\) mm past the middle toward the shaft. In the first quarter of the turn it has done more than half of its way down, and the curve turns more sharply at the top than at the bottom. This view adds that the asymmetry is a length we can measure.

1.8 Algebraically

If we expand the root up to the term with \(\lambda^2\), we get the two-term approximation

\[x_{\mathrm{B}} \approx l\Big(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{4}\Big) + a\Big(\cos\theta + \frac{\lambda}{4}\cos 2\theta\Big) .\]

What do we assume here? That \(\lambda\) is small, since we drop the terms with higher powers of \(\lambda\). For engine \(E\) this costs at most \(107{,}58\) µm of position, that is \(0{,}108\) % of the stroke, and more for a shorter conrod. In the acceleration the second term, after two differentiations, has the size \(\lambda = 0{,}25\) of the first, which is, up to a difference of \(1{,}6\) %, the second blue bar. This view adds that the size of the second harmonic is set by a single number, \(\lambda\). In the book: 23.14.

1.9 The limit: an infinitely long conrod

What would happen if the conrod stopped tilting? An effect is easiest to understand when we switch it off. First the short conrod of engine \(E\), \(l = 4a\), tilted by \(14{,}48°\). Then one twice as long, \(l = 8a\), which with the same pin tilts only by \(7{,}18°\). We read the shortfalls on the enlarged strip: for the short one \(6{,}35\) mm, for the long one only \(3{,}14\) mm, about half. An infinitely long conrod would not tilt at all, there would be no shortfall and the piston would swing as a pure sine. So the second harmonic comes entirely from the tilt of the conrod. This view adds where the effect comes from: shorter conrod, larger \(\lambda\), larger second harmonic.

2 What shakes the frame, and what a counterweight cannot do

We stay with engine \(E\). It is our model from the book, not data from a source: one cylinder, crank \(a = 50\) mm, conrod \(l = 200\) mm, \(1500\) revolutions per minute. In the previous part we saw that the conrod tilts twice in one turn. Today we ask what this means for the frame (ogrodje), the block that holds the cylinder and the bearings of the shaft. What shakes it? And why can a weight on the crank calm it only in part?

Let us draw the mechanism at the crank angle \(\theta = 60^\circ\): the shaft axis \(O\), the crank to the pin \(A\), the conrod to the piston and the dashed cylinder axis. The frame gets two arrows from the moving parts. The first starts at the pin \(A\) and points outward along the crank, \(1110{,}3\) N long. The second starts at the piston and lies on the cylinder axis, \(833{,}0\) N long in this position. We put the arrows tip to tail. The sum is the shaking force (tresilna sila) \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\), the force that shakes the frame. Its components are \(1388{,}2\) N along the cylinder and \(961{,}6\) N across it.

What happens as the crank runs on? The first arrow turns with the crank and keeps its length. The second stays on the cylinder axis, it only grows, shrinks and reverses. This is the claim of this part: the frame feels minus mass times acceleration of everything that moves inside it, and that is one arrow that turns with the crank and one that only swings along the cylinder. A weight on the crank is itself a turning arrow. So it cancels the turning arrow completely and the swinging one never.

The claim uses six things that are not obvious by themselves. Each is the next question why.

2.1 Why does the frame feel a force at all?

A force does not come from nothing. So who pushes the frame, when nothing touches it from outside? First we draw the frame: the bearing below the shaft axis \(O\), the cylinder wall above and below, the cylinder head on the right. This is the block that bolts hold on the base. We lay the axis \(x\) along the cylinder axis. Into the frame we put the moving parts: the crank, the conrod and the piston, again at \(60^\circ\). Between the piston and the head is the gas, which pushes the piston toward the shaft. We come back to it in a moment. During a turn these parts keep speeding up and slowing down. Who pushes them?

Around the moving parts and the gas we draw a dashed boundary and look at what crosses it. What crosses it are the forces of the frame: the reactions \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) in the bearing, the force \(N\) of the wall on the piston and the force of the cylinder head. Nothing else touches the moving parts. By Newton's second law the sum of these forces equals the sum of masses times accelerations. By the third law the moving parts push the frame back with equally large forces in the opposite direction. Their sum is the shaking force:

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}} .\]

It is the same experience as a jump on a bathroom scale. As we push off, the needle jumps past our weight, because the scale pushes us up so that we accelerate, and we push it down. The frame is the scale, the moving parts inside the boundary are us. In the book: 23.17 and 23.18.

Assumption: we do not count weight. Why may we? The piston and the conrod weigh \(2{,}7 \cdot 9{,}81 = 26{,}5\) N, that is \(0{,}7\) % of the largest shaking force \(3886\) N, and weight is a constant force that only loads the frame, it does not shake it. When does it fail? For a slow, heavy machine, where the acceleration of the moving parts is small next to the acceleration of gravity \(g\). Effect: the forces of the frame would get a constant part, the shaking part stays the same.

2.2 Why does the gas not shake?

The gas pushes harder than anything else in the engine. Why does the frame not feel it as shaking? Let us take only the cylinder with its head, which is part of the frame, and put the piston into it. Between the piston and the head is the gas. The gas pushes the piston toward the shaft, with up to \(20\) kN in our gas model. The same gas pushes the cylinder head away from the shaft with the same force. The head is part of the frame. The piston passes its force through the conrod and the crank to the bearing, which is also part of the frame. So the frame gets two forces, equally large and opposite, and their sum is zero. To the same scale we also draw the largest shaking force, \(3886\) N. It is more than five times shorter than the gas force, and yet it is the one that shakes.

The gas lies inside the boundary: it is an internal force, as if we pulled ourselves up by our hair. We do not rise. If we held the engine in our palms, we would not feel the explosion: we would only feel how we throw the moving parts back and forth. The gas is not without effect, though. The reaction in the bearing contains the gas force, and the gas turns the shaft. It just cannot shake. That is why the shaking force is not the force in the bearing.

Assumption: the gas has no mass. It holds because the mass of the gas is small next to the \(1{,}5\) kg of the piston. Effect: the sum of forces on the gas is zero, so the head takes exactly the force with which the piston compresses the gas, and the gas force drops out of the shaking force exactly, not approximately. A gas with mass would add its own mass times acceleration to the sum. In the book: step 3 of the proof of 23.18.

2.3 Why two masses instead of the conrod?

The pin \(A\) runs on a circle, the piston on a line. The conrod rocks between them and its centre of mass draws a complicated curve. Do we need to know it? For the sum of forces only the mass and the acceleration of the centre of mass count, not the shape.

Let us draw the conrod with mass \(1{,}2\) kg, the pin \(A\) on the left and the piston pin \(B\) on the right. Into it we draw the centre of mass \(G\), which is \(50\) mm from \(A\) and \(150\) mm from \(B\). Now we take the mass away from the bar and put two point masses at its ends. Together they shall have the mass of the conrod and the centre of mass at \(G\). How do we split it? Like a seesaw with two children: the heavier one sits closer to the pivot. At \(G\) we need \(m_{\mathrm{A}}' \cdot 50 = m_{\mathrm{B}}' \cdot 150\). The mass at \(A\) is three times larger: \(0{,}9\) kg at \(A\), \(0{,}3\) kg at \(B\). The seesaw balances:

\[0{,}9 \cdot 50 = 0{,}3 \cdot 150 = 45\ \mathrm{kg\,mm} .\]

Why not simply halves at each end? Let us also draw two halves of \(0{,}6\) kg. The mass is the same, but the centre of mass falls in the middle, \(50\) mm away from the true one. Because of this shift the force on the frame is off by up to \(382\) N, almost a tenth of the largest. So the centre of mass counts, not only the mass.

Now we have two places with mass. At \(A\), on the circle, is the rotating mass (vrteča masa) \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}9\) kg. At \(B\), on the line, is the reciprocating mass (nihajoča masa): the piston and part of the conrod, together

\[m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}5 + 0{,}3 = 1{,}8\ \mathrm{kg} .\]

In the book: 23.15 and 23.16.

Assumption: two masses replace the conrod. For the sum of forces this is not an approximation but exact, because the pair has the same mass and the same centre of mass. It does not hold for the turning of the conrod. The pair has the moment of inertia \(m\,l_{\mathrm{A}}\,l_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}2 \cdot 0{,}05 \cdot 0{,}15 = 0{,}0090\) kg m\(^2\) (the product of the two distances), the conrod \(0{,}0065\) kg m\(^2\). The pair resists turning \(38\) % more strongly. This difference goes into the torque of the engine, not into the shaking force. In the book: 23.24, (23.20).

Assumption: the crank itself is balanced, its centre of mass lies on the axis \(O\). If not, its share \(m_2 s/a\) is added to the mass at \(A\) ((23.9)). The picture stays the same, the turning arrow is only longer.

2.4 Why does one arrow turn and the other swing?

What does a mass running on a circle do, and what a mass running on a line? Back to the first figure. The rotating mass runs at constant speed on a circle of radius \(a\). A mass on a circle needs a pull toward the centre. The bearing gives it this pull through the crank, so the mass pulls the frame outward, along the crank, like a washing machine with unbalanced laundry that wants to fly off its axis. The size is the same at every angle:

\[m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a\Omega^2 = 0{,}9 \cdot 1233{,}70 = 1110{,}3\ \mathrm{N} .\]

The direction turns with the crank.

The piston, however, runs on a line. So its arrow cannot leave the cylinder axis. It only changes its length and its direction back and forth. From the first part we know how:

\[2220{,}7\cos\theta + 564{,}1\cos 2\theta\ \ \mathrm{N} ,\]

plus higher terms of at most \(9{,}1\) N. The second term is there because the conrod tilts twice in one turn.

Assumption: the angular speed \(\Omega\) is constant. It holds in steady running with a flywheel. Effect when it fails: if the speed changes by \(1\) % in a turn, \(\Omega^2\) and with it both arrows change by about \(2\) %, the pin also gets an acceleration along the circle, and the turning arrow no longer points exactly along the crank. How large the speed swing is, 23.5 tells.

What does a whole turn look like? Let us draw the axes: the angle \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(360^\circ\) across, the force in newtons up. Along the cylinder we first draw the primary part, \(3331\cos\theta\): both masses contribute to it, the rotating one \(1110{,}3\) N and the reciprocating one \(2220{,}7\) N. Then the secondary part \(564\cos 2\theta\), which swings twice in one turn. Their sum is \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\). At the top, at \(\theta = 0\), everything adds up:

\[1110{,}3 + 2220{,}7 + 564{,}1 - 9{,}1 + 0{,}2 = 3886{,}2\ \mathrm{N} .\]

The last two terms are the fourth and the sixth harmonic, too small to see. The cross component \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) comes from the rotating mass only: a pure sine wave with peak \(1110{,}3\) N. In the book: 23.19 and 23.20.

2.5 Why does a counterweight cancel the turning arrow?

How do we stop an arrow that turns? With a second one that turns with it and points the opposite way. Let us draw the frame with the bearing \(O\) and the cylinder, the axis \(x\) along the cylinder, the crank to the pin \(A\), the conrod to the piston. This time at \(\theta = 50^\circ\). The rotating mass pulls outward, \(1110{,}3\) N along the crank. On the opposite side of the shaft, at the angle \(\theta + 180^\circ\), we bolt on a counterweight (protiutež) with the same mass \(0{,}9\) kg at the same radius \(50\) mm. It also runs on a circle and pulls outward, but in the opposite direction, again with \(1110{,}3\) N.

The two arrows lie on the same line through \(O\), equally long and opposite. When the crank turns on, both turn together and stay opposite. So their sum is zero at every angle, not only at this one. What counts is the product of mass and radius: a counterweight at half the radius must be twice as heavy. In the book: 23.32.

What is left? The arrow of the piston, \(1338{,}1\) N long at \(50^\circ\), along the cylinder.

Assumption: the pin and the counterweight lie in the same plane, perpendicular to the shaft. What if we fix the counterweight to the shaft \(30\) mm beside the plane of the pin?

Let us draw the shaft from the side and its two bearings, \(0{,}2\) m apart. In the plane of the pin the rotating mass pulls with \(1110{,}3\) N. \(30\) mm beside it the counterweight pulls with the same force in the opposite direction. The sum of the forces is zero, the frame does not move. But the two forces do not lie on the same line, so they are a couple and try to turn the shaft:

\[1110{,}3\ \mathrm{N} \cdot 0{,}03\ \mathrm{m} = 33{,}3\ \mathrm{N\,m} , \qquad \frac{33{,}3\ \mathrm{N\,m}}{0{,}2\ \mathrm{m}} = 166{,}5\ \mathrm{N} .\]

The bearings take this couple, each with \(166{,}5\) N, and both forces turn with the shaft. That is why real shafts have counterweights on both sides of the pin, symmetric, and then the calculation in one plane holds. In the book: 23.33.

2.6 Why can it not cancel the swinging arrow?

Can a weight that turns cancel an arrow that only swings along a line? To answer, we build the swing from two turnings.

Let us draw a circle and on it two equal masses at the same point on the axis. The blue one will turn counterclockwise, like the crank. The orange one will turn clockwise, against the crank. At \(\omega t = 0\) both pull outward in the same direction and the sum is double. Let us turn by \(60^\circ\): the blue one is above the axis, the orange one just as far below it. The vertical parts of the forces cancel, the horizontal parts add, the sum is half as long but still on the axis. At \(120^\circ\) the sum points back, at \(180^\circ\) it is double again, in the opposite direction. The sum never leaves the axis. Below we draw the path of the tip of the sum: it swings along a segment. This is the parallelogram of forces: two equal arrows, mirrored across a line, give a sum on that line.

The first harmonic of the piston is exactly such a sum. With \(F_0 = m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N,

\[\tfrac12 F_0\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) + \tfrac12 F_0\,(\cos\theta, -\sin\theta) = F_0\,(\cos\theta, 0) .\]

The first term is the blue half, \(1110{,}3\) N, which turns with the crank. The second is the orange half, also \(1110{,}3\) N, which turns against it. The counterweight is bolted to the crank, so it turns like the blue one. It can cancel the blue half, the orange one never: that one is at a different place from the counterweight at every angle. Of the first harmonic at least \(1110{,}3\) N remain, whatever we bolt to the crank. In the book: (23.30) and 23.34.

2.7 Back up: what the designer really chooses

Now we go from the ground back to the top, in five steps.

First, the conrod is two masses, one on the circle and one on the line. Second, the first gives the turning arrow, the other the swinging one. Third, the counterweight \(m_{\mathrm{c}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\) takes away the turning arrow and the peak of the curve drops:

\[3886{,}2\ \mathrm{N} \ \to\ 2775{,}8\ \mathrm{N} ,\]

which is \(1110{,}3\) N less, up to rounding. And what if we add a fraction \(p\) of the reciprocating mass to the counterweight? That is the fourth step.

Let us draw the axes: \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the cylinder, \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) across, in units of \(F_0 = 2220{,}7\) N. At \(p = 0\) the tip of the first harmonic swings along a segment from \(-1\) to \(1\). At \(p = \tfrac14\) the segment opens into an ellipse, \(\tfrac34\) long along and \(\tfrac14\) across. At \(p = \tfrac12\) it is a circle of radius \(\tfrac12\). At \(p = \tfrac34\) the ellipse lies across, at \(p = 1\) it is again a segment of length \(1\), but across. At \(p = \tfrac12\) we did not remove the force. We turned half of it sideways.

On the right we draw the largest force against \(p\). For the first harmonic these are two straight lines, \(1 - p\) and \(p\), with the lowest point \(\tfrac12\) at \(p = \tfrac12\). The whole force with all harmonics has its lowest point elsewhere, at \(p = 0{,}59\), and there it is \(1465{,}9\) N. Why not at \(\tfrac12\)? The secondary part adds along the cylinder at \(\theta = 0\), so it pays to reduce the force along the cylinder a little more and pay with the cross force. In the book: 23.35.

Fifth step: what would take away the rest? Something that turns against the crank, like the orange mass. That is a shaft turning in the opposite direction. With a counterweight at \(p = \tfrac12\) one is enough, with the unbalance \(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{B}} a = 0{,}045\) kg m. With a counterweight at \(p = 0\) we take a pair of counter-rotating shafts, each with \(0{,}045\) kg m, and this pair is called a Lanchester balancer (Lanchesterjev uravnoteževalnik).

Let us draw the axes: the angle \(\theta\) and the force along the cylinder in units of \(F_0\). With the counterweight only the force has the peak \(2775{,}8\) N. The primary pair at the speed \(\Omega\) takes away the first harmonic. What remains is the secondary part with two swings per turn, at most \(573{,}4\) N. The secondary pair, turning at \(2\Omega\), takes that away too. What remains is \(9{,}3\) N, that is \(0{,}3\) % of the original force. The price is extra shafts with a drive. In the book: 23.36 and 23.37.

Back to the top figure. Of the two arrows the counterweight crosses out the turning one. The swinging one remains, and of its first harmonic at least the half that turns against the crank.

2.8 Geometrically, in a few minutes

Whoever has only a few minutes should keep two pictures. A swing along a line is two equal turnings in opposite directions, and a counterweight is a single turning, with the crank. Two arrows that turn in the same direction give a circle, never a segment. Unequal opposite masses give an ellipse: that is a counterweight that carries a fraction of the reciprocating mass. This view adds the impossibility without a single formula.

2.10 The scale of speed

Every term of the shaking force carries the factor \(\Omega^2\). The numbers on the force axis are multiplied by four at twice the speed, \(3000\) revolutions per minute, while the shape of the curve stays the same. The primary part grows from \(3331\) N to \(13\,324\) N. This view adds why the same engine shakes much harder at high revs than at low ones.

3 Several cylinders: when the arrows close

One cylinder of engine E, our model from the book and not a measurement, always keeps at least half of its primary force, whatever the counterweight. What if we put several cylinders on the same shaft? Put four in a row with cranks \(0\), \(\pi\), \(\pi\), \(0\). The primary force vanishes. Yet the engine still shakes, twice per turn, with a force of \(2256{,}4\) N, four times the secondary force of one cylinder. Why does one force cancel while the other piles up?

One drawing gives the answer. The top row is order \(1\), the primary force, the bottom row order \(2\), the secondary. Take two cylinders first, with cranks \(0\) and \(\pi\). Each cylinder adds one arrow of length \(1\). At order \(1\) the first points forward and the second back: the chain returns to the start \(0\). At order \(2\) both point forward: the chain is \(2\) long. Four cylinders work the same way. At order \(1\) we go \(+1\), \(-1\), \(-1\), \(+1\) and are back at the start. At order \(2\) we go \(+1\) four times, the sum is \(4\) and the force \(4 \cdot 564{,}1 = 2256{,}4\) N.

The claim on this drawing: for each order \(n\), each cylinder adds one arrow turned by \(n\) times the angle of its crank. The force of order \(n\) is the chain of these arrows laid end to end. A closed chain means no force. Order \(2\) doubles every angle, so cranks half a turn apart (\(\pi\)) become a full turn apart (\(2\pi\)) and point the same way.

This claim uses four things. That the force of each cylinder is the same force, only delayed. That the primary forces cancel. That the secondary forces add. And that we may add forces as arrows. Let us take them in turn.

3.1 Why is the force of cylinder \(i\) the same force, only delayed?

Four cylinders give four forces. Must we compute each one separately? We draw the shaft vertically, it is the axis \(z\). On it we put four cylinders, numbered \(1\) to \(4\) from the bottom, each with a dashed axis along \(x\). Each cylinder gets its crank. At \(\theta = 0\) cranks \(1\) and \(4\) point outward, the top dead centre. Cranks \(2\) and \(3\) point the other way, to the bottom dead centre.

Now we look along the shaft. Crank \(1\) is at the angle \(\theta\) from the axis \(x\), crank \(4\) points the same way. Cranks \(2\) and \(3\) lag behind crank \(1\) by half a turn, the crank phase angle (fazni kot ročice) \(\varphi = \pi\). So what is cylinder \(2\) doing now? Exactly what cylinder \(1\) did half a turn earlier. The cylinders are identical and on one shaft, so the force of cylinder \(i\) is the force of cylinder \(1\) at the angle \(\theta - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The ground is simple: two identical machines do the same thing, one with a delay.

Here we assumed two things. First, that the cylinders are identical. Why may we? The parts are made the same. When does it fail and what does it cost? If one piston is \(10\) % heavier, its arrow is a tenth longer and \(222{,}1\) N of primary force is left that should be zero. Second, that the cylinders stand parallel in one plane, an inline engine (vrstni motor). So all forces point along the same axis \(x\) and we add them as numbers with signs. In a V engine this does not hold (23.7.2).

In the book: 23.38, (23.35).

3.2 Why do the primary forces cancel?

What does the frame get from two pistons that move exactly opposite? Let us look at them without formulas. First the angle: left we are at \(\theta = 0\), right a quarter turn later. We draw the shaft and above it two cylinders. The crank of cylinder \(1\) points up, the crank of cylinder \(2\) down, half a turn apart. From the crank pins the connecting rods go up to the pistons. Piston \(1\) is at the top, piston \(2\) at the bottom.

What is piston \(1\) doing at this moment? At the top it stops and turns back, so the frame must pull it down. By Newton's third law the piston pulls the frame up, with the primary force \(2220{,}7\) N. Piston \(2\) also turns back at the bottom, but the other way: it pulls the frame down with \(-2220{,}7\) N. The primary force is the part the crank gives, and the two cranks point opposite ways. The sum is zero. On the right, at \(90^\circ\), both cranks are horizontal and both primary forces are zero. It is like this all through the turn: \(2220{,}7\cos\theta\) and \(-2220{,}7\cos\theta\), equal and opposite at every moment.

Here we assumed that the rotating masses are balanced on every crank by a counterweight, as in the second part. So only the forces of the pistons along the cylinder axes act on the frame. Without the counterweights each crank would add the turning arrow of \(1110{,}3\) N from the second part.

3.3 Why do the secondary forces add?

If the primary forces cancel, why do the secondary ones not cancel too? The secondary force is the part the connecting rod gives. We know from the first part of the lecture that it comes from the tilt of the rod, which depends on \(\theta\) only through \(\sin^2\theta\), and that repeats after half a turn. The tilt does not care whether the crank pin is above or below.

Look at the left. At \(\theta = 0\) both rods are straight, one over the top and one over the bottom dead centre. Both secondary forces point up, \(564{,}1\) N each. On the right both rods are tilted by the same angle \(\beta\), only to opposite sides. Both secondary forces now point down, \(-564{,}1\) N each. The rods are straight at the same moment and tilted at the same moment, so the secondary forces agree at every instant. The sum is \(2 \cdot 564{,}1 = 1128{,}2\) N. In the formula: \(\cos 2(\theta - \pi) = \cos 2\theta\).

This is also the view for someone with only a few minutes: the primary force comes from the crank, which is reversed between the two cylinders, the secondary from the rod, which tilts the same in both. This view adds that the adding at order \(2\) needs no formula at all.

3.4 Why may we add the forces as arrows?

With two cylinders we counted with signs. With three cylinders \(120^\circ\) apart that is no longer so simple. What do we do?

We draw the axis \(x\), the axis of the cylinder. From the origin we draw an arrow of length \(1\) at the angle \(\theta\), the crank of cylinder \(1\). We shine a light on it from above. Its shadow on the axis is \(\cos\theta\) long, and that is just how the force of this cylinder varies. At the tip of the first we lay the second arrow, the crank of cylinder \(2\), turned by its delay. Its shadow is \(\cos(\theta - \varphi_2)\), the force of cylinder \(2\). Now we draw the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second. Its shadow is exactly the two shadows together. So adding the shadows is the shadow of the arrows laid end to end. What happens when the shaft turns? All arrows of order \(n\) turn together, at \(n\Omega\), so the chain keeps its shape and only turns. If it is closed once, it is closed always, and the shadow of a closed chain is always zero.

Here we assumed a rigid shaft and a constant \(\Omega\): only then do all arrows of order \(n\) turn together. When does it fail? Near critical speeds the shaft twists, the arrows shift against each other and the chain does not keep its shape (23.9).

Back to the drawing of the chains. For the three-cylinder at order \(1\) the three steps have length \(1\), each turned by \(120^\circ\) from the one before. The chain closes into a triangle. At order \(2\) the turn doubles to \(240^\circ\) and the chain closes again. So the three-cylinder cancels both forces. Recall too that steps on one line, in the two- and four-cylinder, are drawn shifted up a little so we can see them, the sum is measured without the shift.

The idea of the proof in one sentence: a cosine is the real part of a turning unit arrow, and the sum of the real parts is the real part of the sum. In the book: 23.40, (23.36).

3.5 Why does the three-cylinder still rock when there is no force?

If the sum of the forces is zero, is the engine really at rest? Not necessarily. We draw the shaft horizontally, along \(z\). On it we put three cylinders, mark the plane \(C\) in the middle, and the outer cylinders are \(b = 0{,}1\) m away from it. At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) we draw the primary forces. Cylinder \(1\) pulls the frame up with \(1923{,}1\) N, the middle one with zero, cylinder \(3\) down with \(-1923{,}1\) N. The sum is zero.

But what does a force up at one end and down at the other do? It tips the engine, like a seesaw. This is the shaking moment (tresilni moment): each force times its distance \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\) from the plane \(C\). Here that is \(-0{,}1 \cdot 1923{,}1 + 0 + 0{,}1 \cdot (-1923{,}1)\), in size \(384{,}6\) N m. At this moment it is exactly the largest value.

The chain says the same. We now weight the arrows by \(z_{\mathrm{i}}\): the first is \(-b\) long, the middle one has weight zero, the third is \(b\) long and turned by \(4\pi/3\). This chain does not close, its sum is \(\sqrt3\,b\) long. The moment is \(\sqrt3 \cdot 0{,}1 \cdot 2220{,}7 = 384{,}6\) N m. So the conditions for the force and for the moment are two different conditions, because they have different weights.

In the book: 23.39, 23.41.

3.6 Why does a mirrored shaft have no moment?

The three-cylinder rocks. Why does the four-cylinder \(0\), \(\pi\), \(\pi\), \(0\) not? Cranks \(1\) and \(4\) point the same way, but they are on opposite sides of the plane \(C\), at equal distances \(z_4 = -z_1\). The two cylinders do the same thing, one pushes the seesaw on the left, the other equally on the right. Their moments cancel. So do cylinders \(2\) and \(3\). This holds for every order, not only the primary one. The ground is the seesaw: equal pushes at equal distances on opposite sides balance.

But mind the point we measure about. The secondary force of the four-cylinder is not zero, it is \(2256{,}4\) N. About the plane \(C\) its moment is zero, about cylinder \(1\) it has the lever arm \(1{,}5\,b = 0{,}15\) m and the moment \(2256{,}4 \cdot 0{,}15 = 338{,}5\) N m. The moment of a force that is not zero depends on the point. In the book: 23.42.

3.7 Back up: the table on the board

From the ground we now build back up. Identical machines do the same thing with a delay. The primary force follows the crank, the secondary the tilt of the rod. The force of order \(n\) is a chain of arrows, the moment a chain of weighted arrows. We will read each row of the table off its own chain, so let us also draw the two chains that the first drawing lacks.

Again order \(1\) is above and order \(2\) below. For the four-cylinder with cranks a quarter turn apart the steps of order \(1\) close into a square. At order \(2\) the quarter turn doubles to half a turn, the steps go \(+1\), \(-1\), \(+1\), \(-1\) and we are back at the start. For the six-cylinder three steps close into a triangle and the next three into another, at order \(1\) and at order \(2\). So there are no forces in either of these two rows. The moment is decided by the weighted chain, as for the three-cylinder. Now the table for engine E with spacing \(b = 0{,}1\) m:

two-cylinder \(0, \pi\) 0 1128,2 222,1 0
three-cylinder \(0, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\) 0 0 384,6 97,7
four-cylinder \(0, \pi, \pi, 0\) 0 2256,4 0 0
four-cylinder \(0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) 0 0 628,1 112,8

Now the question from the start is answered. The common four-cylinder trades a large primary problem for a secondary one at twice the frequency, because the chains of order \(2\) do not close. The six-cylinder closes every chain and is mirrored, so all four values are zero.

The table has only the orders \(1\) and \(2\). Why may we drop the higher ones? The coefficient of order \(4\) is only \(A_4 = -0{,}004098\) against \(A_2 = 0{,}254025\). What does it cost? At order \(4\) the shaft \(0\), \(\pi\), \(\pi\), \(0\) has all four steps pointing the same way, as at order \(2\), because \(4\pi\) is two full turns. The force is \(36{,}4\) N at \(4\Omega\), that is \(1{,}6\) % of the secondary.

3.8 The same by computation

For those who prefer to compute: the sum of the arrows is the number \(S_{\mathrm{n}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}\). For the two-cylinder \(S_1 = 1 - 1 = 0\), for order \(2\) \(S_2 = 1 + 1 = 2\). This view adds a rule we can repeat for any shaft, without drawing.

3.9 How sharp is the cancellation?

The cancellation is exact only under exact conditions. Let piston \(4\) of the four-cylinder be \(10\) % heavier. Then the fourth step of the chain is \(1{,}1\) long and does not return to \(0\): \(0{,}1\) is left, a primary force of \(0{,}1 \cdot 2220{,}7 = 222{,}1\) N that was not there before. And the shaft \(0\), \(\pi\), \(0\), \(\pi\)? Its chain of order \(1\) closes, but the shaft is not mirrored about the plane \(C\). The primary moment is \(0{,}2 \cdot 2220{,}7 = 444{,}1\) N m. A closed chain of forces says nothing about the moment. This view adds how sensitive the cancellation is: a tenth of the mass of one piston brings back a tenth of the primary force of one cylinder.

In the book: 23.43.

The same chain of arrows also decides the even firing (23.7.1) and comes back for the twisting of the shaft (23.9), what is left goes into the engine mounts (23.8), and the flywheel smooths the gas torque (23.5).
