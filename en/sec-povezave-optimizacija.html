Chapter 29 Structural optimization

29.10 Connections and limits of the chapter (povezave in meje poglavja)

This is the last chapter of the book, so there is no list of chapters that are still to come. We say what optimization is built on, and then two questions that the chapter did not treat. The first: what changes if the design variable is not a cross-section area but the shape of a boundary (shape optimization). The second: what changes if one goal, for example weight, is not enough and we want several at once (multi-criteria optimization). For each we say what we derive and what we cite from the source. At the end is a list of what the book does not treat. The abbreviations of the courses are the same as in section 1.19.

29.10.1 What optimization stands on

  • Energy principles (VT, MK, chapter 4): the compliance (podajnost) \(C = \vek F\cdot\vek u\) equals \(2U\) by theorem 4.86, and by theorem 4.91 it is \(-2\Pi_{\min}\). From this theorem 29.34 gets the convexity of the compliance as a maximum of functions linear in the areas, theorem 29.37 gets the condition of equal energy density, and theorem 29.43 gets the sensitivity with one extra solve. The promise of chapter 4 that the least compliance follows from the least potential energy is kept by section 29.5.
  • Trusses and assembly (AK, chapter 7): the stiffness matrix \(K(A) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}} K_{\mathrm{e}}\) is linear in the areas by theorem 7.13. This is why the compliance is a maximum of linear functions (theorem 29.34). The promise about optimizing bar cross-sections from the same chapter is kept by sections 29.2, 29.5 and 29.7.
  • Lagrange multipliers and Taylor's formula (VT, chapter 8): theorem 8.36 is the basis of the necessary conditions 29.29, theorem 8.26 of the existence of an optimum (lemma 29.8) and theorem 8.60 of the error of finite differences (section 29.6).
  • Functionals and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (MK, chapters 5 and 6): a rod of variable cross-section is a function \(A(x)\), its compliance is a functional, and its least value is obtained by theorem 29.60 from lemma 6.19. The route through the variational condition, used by the source (CHK, PDF pp. 168-174), uses the same stationarity of a functional as theorem 5.69.
  • The Rayleigh quotient (VD, DSK, chapter 10): by theorem 10.27 the quotient is stationary at an eigenvector. If \(\lambda(p) = R(\vek\varphi(p); p)\) is an eigenvalue that depends on a parameter \(p\), and \(\lambda\) and the eigenvector \(\vek\varphi\) are differentiable with respect to \(p\), then by the chain rule \(\lambda'(p) = (\partial R/\partial\vek\varphi)\,\vek\varphi' + \partial R/\partial p\) and the first term is zero. The derivative of an eigenvalue therefore needs only the derivatives of the stiffness and mass matrices with respect to \(p\), not the derivative of the eigenvector. We do not derive the expression for \(\lambda'\) here and have not checked it on a page of the source, so the book does not use it (open point O5).
  • When the chosen model is good enough: the optimum uses everything the model allows. In the truss of three bars of section 29.5 the optimum for a vertical force (\(\theta = 0\)) is a single middle bar with the area \(300\ \mathrm{mm}^2\). Its stiffness matrix is singular (\(\det = 0\)) and it carries no horizontal force. The model did not notice this, because it had no horizontal force. Checking a model and estimating its error are the subject of chapter 17 (AK).

29.10.2 Shape optimization (oblikovna optimizacija)

In section 29.1 we only named shape optimization (oblikovna optimizacija): the design variable here is the shape of the boundary of a body, not the area of a cross-section. Only what its first step needs remains here. For a surface (a plate in the plane) a change of shape changes the domain of every element, so the derivatives of the element stiffness matrix require the derivatives of the node positions with respect to the design variables (CHK, section 6.3.2.2, PDF p. 113). The first question is therefore how to describe the boundary with a few variables so that the nodes move consistently.

The simplest idea is to take the node coordinates as variables, as for bars. The source says that the boundary then usually becomes ragged and that the stresses are inaccurate because of this (CHK, section 7.1, PDF p. 122). The reason is that no condition ties neighbouring nodes to follow a smooth line, so the optimizer moves each of them on its own. So we describe the boundary with a few control vertices (nadzorna oglišča) and the mesh follows them. The smooth curve that the control vertices determine is a spline (zlepek), because it is composed of polynomial pieces of low degree (CHK, section 7.1, PDF p. 122). Its simplest case is the quadratic Bézier spline (CHK, equation (7.7), PDF p. 124), which we draw in figure 29.34.

First we draw the edge of the plate over the length \(100\) mm and its end points \(\vek V_0\) and \(\vek V_2\), which do not move in the optimization (this is their role, not a property of the curve). Then we take the control vertex \(\vek V_1\), which pulls the edge upward, and let it move along the span (razpon), the segment between \(\vek V_1^{\min}\) and \(\vek V_1^{\max}\). The span gets a variable: \(\vek V_1 = \vek V_1^{\min} + \alpha\,\vek L\) with \(0 \le \alpha \le 1\) and the vector \(\vek L = \vek V_1^{\max} - \vek V_1^{\min}\) (CHK, equation (7.1), PDF p. 122). Here \(\vek L = (0, 40)\) mm. One variable \(\alpha\) thus determines a whole curve and not only one point. The curve is

\[\vek r(u, \alpha) = (1-u)^2\,\vek V_0 + 2u(1-u)\,\vek V_1 + u^2\,\vek V_2 , \qquad 0 \le u \le 1 , \tag{29.60}\]

and the parameter \(u\) runs along it from \(\vek V_0\) to \(\vek V_2\). A mesh node that lies at the value \(u\) moves when \(\alpha\) changes. The lemma says by how much.

Lemma 29.75 Displacement of a point on a quadratic spline (premik točke na kvadratnem zlepku)

Let \(\vek V_0\), \(\vek V_2\) and \(\vek L\) be fixed vectors of the plane, \(\vek V_1 = \vek V_1^{\min} + \alpha\,\vek L\) and \(\vek r(u, \alpha)\) the curve (29.60).

  1. \(\vek r(0, \alpha) = \vek V_0\) and \(\vek r(1, \alpha) = \vek V_2\) for every \(\alpha\).
  2. For every \(u \in [0, 1]\), every \(\alpha\) and every \(h \ne 0\), \(\big(\vek r(u, \alpha + h) - \vek r(u, \alpha)\big)/h = 2u(1-u)\,\vek L\). The derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) is therefore \(\partial\vek r/\partial\alpha = 2u(1-u)\,\vek L\), zero at the end points and equal to \(\vek L/2\) at \(u = 1/2\).
  3. \(\partial\vek r/\partial u\,(0, \alpha) = 2(\vek V_1 - \vek V_0)\) and \(\partial\vek r/\partial u\,(1, \alpha) = 2(\vek V_2 - \vek V_1)\).
Proof

We must show three points, all from equation (29.60). In the second the curve is linear in \(\alpha\), so the difference quotient is exact for every \(h\) and not only in the limit.

  1. At \(u = 0\), \((1-u)^2 = 1\) and \(u(1-u) = u^2 = 0\), at \(u = 1\), \((1-u)^2 = u(1-u) = 0\) and \(u^2 = 1\). What remains is \(\vek V_0\) and \(\vek V_2\) respectively.
  2. The variable \(\alpha\) appears only in \(\vek V_1\). The difference \(\vek r(u, \alpha + h) - \vek r(u, \alpha)\) therefore contains only the term \(2u(1-u)\big(\vek V_1(\alpha + h) - \vek V_1(\alpha)\big) = 2u(1-u)\,h\,\vek L\), since \(\vek V_1(\alpha + h) - \vek V_1(\alpha) = h\,\vek L\). Dividing by \(h\) gives the claim, and the limit \(h \to 0\) gives the derivative. The function \(2u(1-u)\) is zero at \(u = 0\) and \(u = 1\) and \(1/2\) at \(u = 1/2\).
  3. The derivative with respect to \(u\) is \(-2(1-u)\,\vek V_0 + 2(1-2u)\,\vek V_1 + 2u\,\vek V_2\) (the rule for the derivative of a product and of a polynomial). At \(u = 0\) this is \(-2\vek V_0 + 2\vek V_1\), at \(u = 1\) it is \(-2\vek V_1 + 2\vek V_2\).

Source: The spline is CHK equation (7.7) (PDF p. 124), the span variable equation (7.1) (PDF p. 122), the tangent at the end points equation (7.6) (PDF p. 124, there in general for degree \(n\)). The proof for degree \(n = 2\) is not from the source.

In figure 29.34 we see what the lemma says. The displacement of the point at \(u = 1/4\) is \(2\cdot\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac34\cdot 40 = 15\) mm per unit of \(\alpha\), so \(7{,}5\) mm when \(\alpha\) changes from \(0{,}5\) to \(1\) (heights \(11{,}25\) and \(18{,}75\) mm, num-10.out, part B). At \(u = 1/2\) it is \(20\) mm per unit of \(\alpha\), which is half of \(\vek L\). A mesh whose nodes are assigned fixed values of \(u\) therefore moves consistently and the edge stays smooth. The derivatives \(\partial\vek r/\partial\alpha\) are exactly what the derivatives of the stiffness matrix, cited by the source, need.

A case where the lemma says nothing: by point 2 the end points at \(u = 0\) and \(u = 1\) stay where they are, whatever \(\alpha\) is. If we wanted the edge to move there as well, \(\vek V_0\) or \(\vek V_2\) must also get its own span and variable. The move of an end point is not a consequence of the move of \(\vek V_1\). The source extends the same idea to splines of higher degree, to B-splines and to the computation of the derivatives of node positions for an arbitrary mesh (CHK, chapter 7, PDF pp. 122-151). We do not derive this and do not use it. The idealization that remains: the edge is a smooth curve with a few variables, and the optimum is the best among these curves, not among all edges.

29.10.3 Several goals at once (več ciljev hkrati)

In the chapter we always had one goal and the others were constraints: the least volume at a limited stress or displacement (sections 29.2 and 29.6), the least compliance at a given volume (section 29.5). A designer, however, often wants both, a light and a stiff structure, and cannot fix the volume in advance. The source calls such problems multi-criteria (večciljne, vector optimization) and leaves them entirely to other sources (CHK, section 1.3, PDF pp. 13-14). We say what we derive about them with the tools of this chapter.

Definition 29.76 Pareto optimality (Paretova optimalnost)

Let \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{l}}\) be objective functions (ciljne funkcije) on the feasible set \(S\) (definition 29.5), each a real function on \(S\) that we want to make as small as possible. A design \(\vek x^* \in S\) is Pareto optimal if there is no design \(\vek x\) in \(S\) for which \(f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \le f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\) for all \(i = 1, \dots, l\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) < f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\) for at least one \(i\).

Source: CHK section 1.3 (PDF pp. 13-14, the inequalities on p. 14). The name "optimal" is there because no other feasible design can be improved in all goals at once.

Take the wall of chapters 4 and 7 with the force \(F = 10\) kN, two bars (called AC and BC below) and the goals volume \(V\) and compliance \(C\) (section 29.5). Is the design of chapter 7 with \(100\ \mathrm{mm}^2\) per bar Pareto optimal? We get the answer if we know all the designs that can be made. The wall is statically determinate, the bar forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}33\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16{,}67\) kN do not depend on the areas, so by theorem 4.86 and definition 4.2 \(C = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}}^2 L_{\mathrm{i}}/(E A_{\mathrm{i}})\) with lengths \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m and \(L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5\) m. By lemma 6.19 for the vectors \(\big(|N_{\mathrm{i}}|\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{i}}/A_{\mathrm{i}}}\big)_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\big(\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}}\big)_{\mathrm{i}}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\),

\[\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} |N_{\mathrm{i}}|\,L_{\mathrm{i}}\Big)^2 \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{N_{\mathrm{i}}^2 L_{\mathrm{i}}}{A_{\mathrm{i}}}\ \sum_{\mathrm{i}} A_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad\text{so}\qquad C \ge \frac{K}{V} , \quad K = \frac{(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} |N_{\mathrm{i}}|\,L_{\mathrm{i}})^2}{E} , \tag{29.61}\]

with equality exactly for areas proportional to \(|N_{\mathrm{i}}|\) (equality in the lemma). The same conclusion for a rod of variable cross-section is theorem 29.60. Here \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} |N_{\mathrm{i}}| L_{\mathrm{i}} = 136\,667\) N m and \(K = 0{,}0953\) J m\(^3\) (num-10.out, part P). All designs therefore lie on the curve \(C = K/V\) or above it, and on the curve are those with stresses of equal magnitude in both bars (AC is in compression, BC in tension). At \(V = 900\ \mathrm{cm}^3\), (29.61) gives the lower bound \(105{,}88\) J, and the design with \(100\ \mathrm{mm}^2\) has \(107{,}14\) J. It is not Pareto optimal: the same compliance can be reached with \(889{,}4\ \mathrm{cm}^3\), which is \(1{,}18\) % less (num-10.out, part Q). The curve is shown by figure 29.35 on the left.

We draw the axes \(v\) and \(c\) and the curve \(c = 1/v\) that (29.61) requires. We divide both quantities by those of the fully stressed design (section 29.2, \(V_0 = 621{,}2\ \mathrm{cm}^3\), \(C_0 = 153{,}4\) J), so that they are free of units. The design with \(100\ \mathrm{mm}^2\) is the orange dot at \(v = 1{,}449\), \(c = 0{,}698\), just above the curve: at the same \(v\) the curve has a \(c\) smaller by \(1{,}18\) %. How do we choose a point on the curve? The most common way is the weighted sum: with weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) we solve the problem with the single goal \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}} f_{\mathrm{i}}\) (CHK, equation (1.2), PDF p. 14). The lemma proves that the solution is Pareto optimal.

Lemma 29.77 A weighted sum gives a Pareto optimal design (utežena vsota da Paretovo optimalno zasnovo)

Let \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{l}}\) be objective functions on the set \(S\), \(w_1, \dots, w_{\mathrm{l}} > 0\) and \(\vek x^*\) a minimum of \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}} f_{\mathrm{i}}\) on \(S\). Then \(\vek x^*\) is Pareto optimal (definition 29.76).

Proof

We argue by contradiction: if \(\vek x^*\) is not Pareto optimal, there is a design with a smaller weighted sum.

  1. Let \(\vek x \in S\) be such that \(f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) \le f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\) for all \(i\) and \(f_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek x) < f_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek x^*)\) for some \(j\) (definition 29.76).
  2. Multiply every inequality by \(w_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\), which keeps its direction, and add. Since \(w_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\), the inequality at \(j\) is strict, so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}} f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x) < \sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}} f_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek x^*)\).
  3. This contradicts that \(\vek x^*\) is a minimum of the weighted sum. Therefore no such \(\vek x\) exists.

Source: The claim is CHK section 1.3 (PDF p. 14: "the solution of which is a Pareto optimum"). The proof is not from the source.

The condition \(w_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) is needed. The source also allows weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\) (CHK, PDF p. 14), but with a zero weight the claim fails. Take the designs \((f_1, f_2) = (1, 4)\) and \((1, 5)\) and the weights \(w_1 = 1\), \(w_2 = 0\). Both designs have the weighted sum \(1\), so both are a minimum. The second is not Pareto optimal, because the first is equally good in \(f_1\) and better in \(f_2\). In the proof step 2 fails: at \(j = 2\), \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) and the sum is no longer strict.

On the wall we carry this through. Let \(v = V/V_0\) and \(c = C/C_0\), then by (29.61) \(c = 1/v\) on the curve. At a given \(v\) the least weighted sum \(w\,v + (1-w)\,c\) belongs to the design with the least \(c\), which is the design on the curve, so we seek the minimum only on the curve. There the weighted sum is \(f_{\mathrm{w}}(v) = w\,v + (1-w)/v\). Its derivative \(w - (1-w)/v^2\) is zero at \(v = \sqrt{(1-w)/w}\), and the second derivative \(2(1-w)/v^3\) is positive, so this is a minimum. The weights \(w = 0{,}8\), \(0{,}5\) and \(0{,}2\) give \(v = 0{,}5\), \(1\) and \(2\), so \(V = 310{,}6\), \(621{,}2\) and \(1242{,}4\ \mathrm{cm}^3\) and \(C = 306{,}8\), \(153{,}4\) and \(76{,}7\) J. Conversely, for every \(v > 0\) the weight \(w = 1/(1 + v^2)\) is a proper one. On the wall we thus reach every point of the curve with the weighted sum: the grey dashed line \(v + c = 2\) is the line of equal weighted value at \(w = 0{,}5\) and it touches the curve, because the curve lies above it and is convex. Caution: the weight \(0{,}5\) has this meaning only at these scales \(V_0\) and \(C_0\). At other units the same \(w\) gives another point.

The conclusion that the weighted sum reaches every Pareto point is not general, and the source warns about it (CHK, PDF p. 14: "in general not every Pareto optimal point may be obtained"). A small example suffices, three designs \((f_1, f_2) = (1, 4)\), \((3, 3)\) and \((4, 1)\). None improves another in both goals, so all three are Pareto optimal. For every weight \(w \in [0, 1]\) the value \(w f_1 + (1-w) f_2\) at \((3, 3)\) is \(3\), and at the better of the two outer ones it is smaller than \(3\) by at least \(0{,}5\) (num-10.out): \(\min_{\mathrm{w}}\big(3 - \min(w + 4(1-w),\ 4w + (1-w))\big) = 0{,}5\) at \(w = 1/2\). The middle design wins at no weight, because it lies above the line through the outer two, so the set of attainable values is not convex. For the wall with continuous areas there are no such holes, since above we reached every point of the curve with the weighted sum. For the choice "the bar is there or it is not" (the nonconvex set of section 29.3), however, there can be.

29.10.4 What the book does not treat (kar knjiga ne obravnava)

  • Optimization without derivatives: design of experiments, response surfaces, surrogate models, genetic algorithms and neural networks. The chapter uses only first-order methods, which the sensitivities of section 29.6 make possible, and the source says that these methods (zero order methods, metode ničtega reda) solve other kinds of problems and that another source must present them (CHK, preface, PDF p. 6). None of the books we use treats them. Computationally the difference is this: in the nested form every value of the objective is one whole solve of the model (CHK, section 1.3, PDF p. 15). With \(n\) variables the finite differences of section 29.6 require \(n + 1\) solves, and the adjoint method one extra per constraint.
  • Cited, not proved (this is said beside the theorems that use them): Lagrangian duality (CHK, section 3.4, PDF pp. 55-64), the convergence of sequential linear and convex approximations and MMA (CHK, chapter 4, PDF pp. 65-83), the limit \(\varepsilon \to 0\) for bars of zero area (CHK, PDF p. 92) and the relaxation of the topology problem (CHK, section 9.5, PDF pp. 202-204).
  • Idealizations of the chapter: a linearly elastic truss with small displacements, an allowable stress without a safety factor and a single load case. If the load is not single, the optimum for one can be useless for another, like the middle bar that carries no horizontal force at a vertical load (above).
