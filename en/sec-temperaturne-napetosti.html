Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.7 Thermal stresses (temperaturne napetosti)

The sections of this chapter so far have given the temperature: from Fourier's law and the energy balance of a finite part we obtained the heat equation, solved it for a bar and for a rectangle, and computed how the field \(T\) changes in time. Chapter 3 also showed that a freely heated body expands without stress, while a restrained one acquires the stress \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) (theorem 3.15, equation (3.18)), and chapter 5 computed the curvature for a temperature that is linear over the height of a beam (theorem 5.37). In both chapters the temperature was a datum taken from nowhere. Now we have it from a computation. What stresses does a temperature that varies over the body cause?

An everyday case: we pour hot water into a thick glass. The inner layer of the glass warms first and would like to expand, the outer layer is still cold and does not let it. The layers oppose each other as they go, and how large the force between them is cannot be found without a computation. The stress therefore does not depend only on how hot the glass is, but on how the temperature changes from place to place.

Before we write the computation we must say in which direction the influence runs. Temperature determines stresses, because a heated body expands. Conversely, a deformation also changes the temperature of the body slightly, as SADD states (PDF p. 379): the influence is small for most materials under static or slow loading, so we neglect it here and in chapter 3. Let us draw this on the board (figure 14.26). On the left is the box with the heat equation that we solved in the previous sections. From it comes the field \(T\), the blue arrow: this we pass on as a datum. On the right is the box of the elastic problem, whose equation we derive right below, and from it come the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The path back, from the displacements \(\vek u\) to the temperature, is dashed and crossed out: this is the influence we neglect. When a problem has a path in one direction only, we call it uncoupled (nesklopljen). The name comes from the word to couple (sklopiti), to join two things so that each acts on the other. A coupled problem would have both paths and would solve them together, an uncoupled one solves them one after the other.

Definition 14.38 Uncoupled thermoelastic problem (nesklopljeni termoelastični problem)

Let the body be an open connected set of points \(D\) with a boundary split into \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) as in definition 11.1. Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with modulus of elasticity \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (\(\nu \neq -1\), \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)) and coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\), all constant, and stress-free at the reference temperature \(T_0\) (definition 3.13). Let \(T(\vek x, t)\) be the solution of the boundary value problem of conduction (definition 14.14), a field we know before we ask about stresses, and \(\Delta T = T - T_0\). Let the body force \(\vek b\), the displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the traction \(\bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) be given. Time \(t\) is only a parameter: for each instant separately we look for a displacement field \(\vek u\) with continuous second derivatives such that

  1. the strains \(\ten\varepsilon\) are the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek u\) (definition 1.115),
  2. the stresses follow from \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\Delta T\) by theorem 3.15, \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\),
  3. the equilibrium equations (2.31) with the force \(\vek b\) hold in the interior, without acceleration terms,
  4. on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\) and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\).

Source: SADD §12.2 (PDF p. 380): uncoupled thermoelasticity, the temperature from the energy equation, then Hooke's law with temperature and the field equations, the mechanical boundary conditions as before and the thermal ones with prescribed temperature or flux. The definition in this form is ours.

We must assess two idealizations of the definition. The first is uncoupling, which we take from SADD (PDF p. 379) and do not prove. We cannot estimate the size of the coupling term for steel, because we have no source for the conductivity and specific heat of steel (an open question of the chapter). The second is the absence of acceleration, quasi-static (kvazistatičen) behaviour: each instant is solved as an equilibrium. A number justifies it. A disturbance crosses the beam height \(h = 40\) mm at the wave speed of a bar \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho} = 5012{,}80\) m/s (chapter 13, equation (13.13)) in \(h/c = 8{,}0\ \mu\text{s}\). The temperature changes appreciably over the same height in the time \(\mathrm{Fo}\,h^2/\kappa\) with the dimensionless number \(\mathrm{Fo}\) that we introduce below. At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}002\) the stress in the example below is still \(81\) % of its largest value (\(47{,}6\) of \(58{,}8\) MPa), and this time is, for the thermal diffusivity of copper from KREYSZIG (PDF p. 587), \(\kappa = 1{,}1576\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}\), equal to \(27{,}6\) ms, which is \(3460\) times more than \(8{,}0\ \mu\text{s}\). We have no source for \(\kappa\) of steel, so we take copper as an example of a metal. For the passage of a disturbance to be at least \(100\) times faster than the change of temperature, we need \(\kappa \le 0{,}002\,h\,c/100 = 0{,}0040\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}\), which is up to \(35\) times the value for copper. Why does this matter? If a disturbance evens out over the body much faster than the temperature changes, the body is nearly in equilibrium at every instant and we need no acceleration. This comparison covers the stress over the height of the cross-section. The beam also bends at the same time (the curvature \(v''\) in lemma 14.41 below), and this is a slower motion of the whole beam. Its lowest period of vibration grows with the square of the length, as equation (13.39) shows for a simply supported beam. For a simply supported beam with the same cross-section and \(L = 1\) m this period is \(1/(90{,}92\ \text{Hz}) = 11{,}0\) ms (section 13.6.3), only \(2{,}5\) times less than \(27{,}6\) ms. For bending the condition therefore holds only for a short beam or slower heating. Otherwise the beam vibrates about the curvature of the lemma, and the stress differs from the lemma by a term that is linear in \(y\). We do not compute this in the example below.

14.7.1 Temperature as a force in the body and on its boundary

From the definition the unknown is the displacement, as in the boundary value problem of chapter 11. There the Navier-Lamé equations (theorem 11.6) gave three equations for the displacements. Does temperature change them? The stresses now have one more term with \(\Delta T\), so the equation will be different. We must look at where this term goes. We expect two places, because we know two parts of the problem: the equilibrium equation in the interior and the boundary condition for the traction.

Theorem 14.39 Navier-Lamé equations with temperature (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe s temperaturo)

Let the material and the constants \(E\), \(\nu\), \(\alpha\), \(\lambda\), \(\mu\) be as in definition 14.38, \(T_0\) constant, \(T\) with continuous first derivatives and \(\vek u\) with continuous second derivatives on an open set \(D\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses from \(\vek u\) and \(\Delta T\) by step 2 of the definition, \(\mat H\) the displacement gradient (definition 1.109) and \(\vek b\) a vector at a point \(P\) of \(D\).

(a) The stresses \(\ten\sigma\) satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) at \(P\) with body force \(\vek b\) if and only if at \(P\)

\[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -\frac{E\,\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\nabla T . \tag{14.33}\]

(b) Let \(P\) be a point of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface with outward normal \(\vek n\) and prescribed \(\bar{\vek t}\). The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) holds at \(P\) if and only if

\[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} , \qquad \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{E\,\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\Delta T\,\vek n . \tag{14.34}\]
Idea

The stresses of theorem 3.15 are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) without temperature, from which we subtract \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\). This term is the same in all directions at each point, but varies from point to point with \(\Delta T\). The first part we know from chapter 11, and the subtracted term is a number times the identity matrix, so its divergence is only the derivative of the temperature and its product with the normal is only a number times the normal.

Proof

We must show that the left side of the equilibrium equation, with the stresses from \(\vek u\) and \(\Delta T\) inserted, equals the left side of (14.33), and that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) equals the left side of (14.34) minus \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\). Write \(K = 3\lambda + 2\mu\).

  1. The matrix form of theorem 3.15 is \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\). Since \(\tr(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I) = 3\alpha\,\Delta T\), this is \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) with \(\ten\sigma^{e} = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\). These are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) without temperature, their components are (11.4). By steps 3 to 6 of part 2 of the proof of theorem 3.15, \(K = E/(1 - 2\nu)\).
  2. By step 6 of the proof of theorem 11.6, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma^{e}_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). This holds because \(\vek u\) has continuous second derivatives.
  3. The \(ji\) component of the term \(-K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is \(-K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\), which is nonzero only for \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\). Hence \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\big(-K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\big)/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = -K\,\alpha\,\partial(\Delta T)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), the constants \(K\) and \(\alpha\) stay in front of the derivative. Since \(T_0\) is constant, \(\partial(\Delta T)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  4. From steps 1 to 3, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} - K\,\alpha\,\partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\). The equilibrium equation \(\ix{i}\) holds if and only if this sum is zero. For \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\) this is the vector equation (14.33) with \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -K\,\alpha\,\nabla T\). This is part (a).
  5. By step 1, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \ten\sigma^{e}\,\vek n - K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n\), since \(\mat I\,\vek n = \vek n\). By step 1 of corollary 11.7, \(\ten\sigma^{e}\,\vek n = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n\). The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) is therefore the same as \(\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} + K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n\), and this is part (b).

Source: SADD (PDF p. 381): for the plane case Navier's equations contain additional temperature terms, which SADD calls thermal body forces (SADD §12.3, text read on PDF p. 381). The theorem in space and the proof are ours, checked with SymPy (residual zero).

What did the theorem say? Temperature enters the equilibrium in two places. In the interior of the body it acts as a body force (prostorninska sila) \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) that points toward lower temperature, since \(-\nabla T\) is the direction in which the temperature falls (figure 14.27). On the surface it acts as an additional traction \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) that is normal to the surface and proportional to \(\Delta T\) at that point. Let us build the figure from the start. The body is a rectangle, hot on the left and cold on the right. The isotherms, lines of equal temperature \(T_1 > T_2 > T_3\), are vertical. The gradient is the vector that points in the direction of fastest increase of temperature, so \(\nabla T\) points left, toward the hot part. The force \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -(E\alpha/(1 - 2\nu))\,\nabla T\) points the opposite way, to the right, and is the same at every point, because for a linear \(T\) the gradient is constant. Each force arrow starts at the point it acts on. The surface part \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) points outward where \(\Delta T > 0\), and in the figure the whole body is warmer than the reference temperature \(T_0\). It is larger at the hot edge, because \(\Delta T\) is larger there.

The theorem says that forces replace the temperature, but these forces (figure 14.27) are not real. No external force acts, the body can be entirely free. It is a matter of computation: the displacement field satisfies the same equations as the field in a body that is not heated but is loaded with \(\vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) in the interior and with \(\bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the surface. So from temperature onward we need no new tool: what we learned about solutions in chapter 11 holds, only the loading, which we obtain from \(T\), differs. The force \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) is therefore an apparent body force: it has this name because it enters the equilibrium in the same place as weight.

Let us test the theorem on a case we know. A free steel body, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, in equilibrium without external forces, \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\), is heated uniformly by \(\Delta T = 30\) K. Try the field \(\vek u = \alpha\,\Delta T\,(x, y, z)\). The constants: \(\lambda = 113{,}077\) GPa, \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa and \(3\lambda + 2\mu = 490{,}000\) GPa, which is indeed \(E/(1 - 2\nu) = 196/0{,}4\) GPa. Equation (14.33) (a): \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 3\alpha\,\Delta T\) is constant and \(\nabla T = \vek 0\), so \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) and all three terms are satisfied. Boundary condition (b): \(\mat H = \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), the left side is \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n = 490\ \text{GPa}\cdot 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,\vek n = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n\), and the right side is \(0 + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n\), as the orange arrows of figure 14.27 illustrate, which are equally long in this case. The field is a solution, and the stress is \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\mat I\) with \(\ten\sigma^{e} = (\lambda \cdot 3 + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\mat I\), that is, zero. A freely heated body has no stress, as chapter 3 showed. This is also the only stress. By theorem 14.39 every solution \(\vek u\) is also a solution of the boundary value problem without temperature (definition 11.1) for the loading \(\vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) in the interior and \(\bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the surface, and its stresses in that problem are \(\ten\sigma^{e}\). For steel \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), so by theorem 3.48 the strain energy density is positive, and by theorem 11.4, part 1, all solutions have the same \(\ten\sigma^{e}\). With the same \(\Delta T\) they therefore also have the same \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\).

Now a case in which this fails. Suppose every piece of the body expands freely, as if alone, so the strain is \(\ten\varepsilon = \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) at every point, and there is no stress. For the linear variation \(\Delta T = g\,y\) such a field exists: \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha g\,xy\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha g\,(y^2 - x^2 - z^2)/2\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha g\,yz\) gives the strain \(\alpha g\,y\,\mat I\) and all three shear components zero (checked with SymPy). A free body with a linear temperature variation is therefore indeed stress-free and bends, as theorem 5.37 described it. For the quadratic variation \(\Delta T = a\,y^2\) with a constant \(a \neq 0\), however, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \alpha a\,y^2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), and equation (1) of theorem 1.127 gives \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 = 2\alpha a\), while the right side \(2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) is \(0\). The requirement is not satisfied, so no displacement field has this strain. The pieces cannot all grow as they would like at once, so stresses appear between them. This is exactly the answer to the question about the glass.

14.7.2 A bar between walls with an uneven temperature

Take the bar of chapter 3 (diameter \(10\) mm, \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), steel) between two rigid smooth walls, as in section 3.3.3, with the same three idealizations (rigid walls, smooth contacts, the bar stays straight). There the temperature was the same everywhere. What changes if on the left wall it is \(30\) K above the reference and on the right wall \(0\) K, and varies linearly in between, as in the steady state of a bar attached to a wall (lemma 14.19)? The bar is thermally insulated at its sides, so \(\Delta T\) is constant over the cross-section and is a function of \(x\) only (corollary 14.10). Let us draw (figure 14.28). At the top is the profile of \(\Delta T\), a straight line from \(30\) K to \(0\) K. Halfway between them is the mean \(15\) K, dashed. Below is the bar between the walls.

Lemma 14.40 Bar between walls with uneven temperature (palica med stenama z neenakomerno temperaturo)

Let a bar have constant cross-sectional area \(A\) and constant \(E\) and \(\alpha\) on the interval \(0 \le x \le L\), with the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross-sections. Both ends are restrained, \(u(0) = u(L) = 0\), the lateral surface is free and weight is neglected. Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and let the state be the same over each cross-section as in section 3.3.3, and let the temperature change \(\Delta T(x)\) be a continuous function on \([0, L]\) with \(\Delta T = T - T_0\). Then the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the same at all points of the bar,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T} , \qquad N = -E\,A\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T} , \qquad \overline{\Delta T} = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L \Delta T(x)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{14.35}\]
Proof

We must show that the stress is constant and compute its value.

  1. Take a piece of the bar \(a \le x \le b\). At its cut at \(b\) the rest of the bar acts with the force \(N(b) = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(b)\), at the cut at \(a\) with the force \(-N(a)\) (the axis \(x\) points right, the outward normal of the cut at \(a\) points left, and by lemma 5.9 \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\)). The lateral surface is free and weight is neglected, so by Newton's law the piece is at rest only if \(N(b) - N(a) = 0\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) are arbitrary, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is constant in \(x\).
  2. The first row of equation (3.16) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T(x)\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u'\).
  3. We integrate over \([0, L]\). By theorem 3.40, \(\int_0^L u'\,\mathrm{d}x = u(L) - u(0) = 0\) because of the walls. By lemma 3.39 the integral of a sum is the sum of integrals and the constant \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) comes out of the integral, so \(0 = \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E}\,L + \alpha\int_0^L \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. From step 3, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T}\), and \(N = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Uniaxial Hooke's law with temperature: FELIPPA (4.18) (PDF p. 58), SADD (4.4.1) (PDF p. 98), as in section 3.3.3.

The lemma said more than we expected. The stress is the same everywhere although the temperature is not, and it does not depend on the profile of the temperature, only on its mean. The reason is the first step: the equilibrium of every piece requires the same force in all cross-sections, so uneven expansion cannot create a stress that varies. What remains is the condition that the total elongation is zero, and this determines the single number \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Example B. For \(\Delta T(x) = 30\,(1 - x/L)\) we have \(\overline{\Delta T} = 15\) K, so

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196\cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}\cdot 15\cdot 10^{-6}\ \tfrac1{\text{K}}\cdot 15\ \text{K} = -44{,}10\ \text{MPa} , \qquad N = -44{,}10\ \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2}\cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = -3464\ \text{N}\]

(SymPy: \(-3463{,}6\) N). Heated uniformly by \(30\) K it gave \(-88{,}2\) MPa and \(-6927\) N (equation (3.18)): half, because the mean is half. The stress arrow of figure 14.28 is the same along the whole length. The displacement of a point, however, is not zero: from step 2, \(u'(x) = \alpha\,(\Delta T(x) - \overline{\Delta T})\), which is positive on the hot half and negative on the cold half, so \(u(x) = \alpha\int_0^x (\Delta T - \overline{\Delta T})\,\mathrm{d}\xi\), largest at \(x = L/2\) with the value \(3{,}75\ \text{K}\cdot\alpha L\). For \(L = 10\) m (as in section 3.3.3) this is \(0{,}5625\) mm. The points move toward the cold wall, because the hot half stretches more than the mean.

A case the lemma does not cover: remove the right wall. Now the right free end carries no force, so \(N = 0\) by step 1 as before, but step 3 no longer holds, because \(u(L)\) is not zero. Step 1 and \(N(L) = 0\) give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) for the same temperature profile, step 2 gives \(u' = \alpha\,\Delta T\), and the bar lengthens by \(u(L) = \alpha\int_0^L \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}x = \alpha L\,\overline{\Delta T} = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 10\ \text{m}\cdot 15 = 2{,}25\) mm, which is half the lengthening of \(4{,}50\) mm under a uniform \(30\) K. The stress is therefore not a property of the temperature, but of the temperature and the supports together. The same \(\Delta T(x)\) gives \(-44{,}10\) MPa between walls and \(0\) with one wall.

14.7.3 A beam with an arbitrary temperature profile

Now back to the beam (figure 14.29). Theorem 5.37 treated only \(\Delta T = \theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\). But when we heat a beam from one side, the temperature over the height is not a straight line: it is higher near the surface and falls toward the interior along a curve determined by the heat equation. How large is the stress then? We saw that the linear part gives no stress, so we expect the stress to be determined by the part that is not linear.

Lemma 14.41 Free beam with an arbitrary temperature profile over the height (prosti nosilec s poljubnim potekom temperature po višini)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold for a beam, let \(E\) and \(\alpha\) be constant and let the beam be free, that is, without external loading and without supports that would hinder expansion or bending, so that \(N = M = 0\) along its whole length. Let the axis \(x\) run through the centroids of the cross-sections, \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). Let the temperature change \(\Delta T(y)\) be a continuous function of the height \(y\) in the cross-section and the same in every cross-section. With

\[\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1}{A}\int_{\mathrm{A}} \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad g = \frac{1}{I}\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A \tag{14.36}\]

we have \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\) and

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\big(\Delta T(y) - \theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\,y\big) . \tag{14.37}\]

The straight line \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) is therefore the one with the same mean and the same first moment as \(\Delta T\), and the stress is \(-E\alpha\) times the difference between \(\Delta T\) and this line.

Proof

The procedure is as in theorem 5.37, except that \(\Delta T\) is an arbitrary function.

  1. In every fibre \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (assumption 6 of chapter 5), so from the first row of equation (3.16) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T)\), and by theorem 5.15 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\).
  2. By lemma 5.9, \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,u'\,A - E\,v''\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A - E\,\alpha\int_{\mathrm{A}} \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A\). The second term is zero (lemma 5.22), the third is \(E\,\alpha\,A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) by (14.36). Hence \(N = EA\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}})\), and \(N = 0\) gives \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  3. By the same lemma, \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -E\,u'\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,v''\,I + E\,\alpha\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A\). The first term is zero, the last is \(E\,\alpha\,g\,I\) by (14.36). Hence \(M = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\), and \(M = 0\) gives \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\).
  4. Insert \(u'\) and \(v''\) in step 1: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + \alpha\,g\,y - \alpha\,\Delta T)\), which is (14.37).
  5. Check: \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -E\alpha\,(A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\cdot 0) = 0\) and \(-\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\alpha\,(g\,I - \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A) = 0\) by (14.36), so the field indeed gives \(N = M = 0\).

Source: The result is not from the sources, it is derived from theorem 3.15 and the assumptions of chapter 5, checked with SymPy (\(\Delta T = y^2\) on a rectangle: \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = h^2/12\), \(g = 0\), \(N = M = 0\)).

For a linear \(\Delta T = \theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) (the straight line in the left part of figure 14.29) the difference is zero and we get theorem 5.37: the lemma extends it to an arbitrary profile. For a uniform \(\Delta T\), \(g = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), as in a freely heated piece. The stress is therefore self-equilibrated: no force or moment remains, but it can be large. Example H gives a case with numbers.

We must say what the lemma assumes. It is a result of the beam model, not a solution of the three-dimensional problem. For a linear \(\Delta T\) the model is exact, as we saw above. For a nonlinear \(\Delta T\) the strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is not compatible, so plane sections and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) together cannot satisfy all the equations of elasticity. The additional stresses in the cross-section remain unestimated, because the beam model does not contain them (assumption 6 of chapter 5). For a wide plate the difference is marked and we can compute it: because the plate is wide, plane sections are also kept in the direction of the width \(z\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is linear in \(y\) and by symmetry \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). The first row of equation (3.16) gives \(\varepsilon = \sigma\,(1 - \nu)/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), so \(\sigma = E\,(\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T)/(1 - \nu)\), and the same steps 2 and 3 of the proof give \(\sigma = -E\,\alpha\,(\Delta T - \theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\,y)/(1 - \nu)\), which is the stress of the beam divided by \(1 - \nu = 0{,}7\) (the derivation is not from the sources). For steel the stresses of example H, shown in the right part of figure 14.29, are increased for a wide plate by \(1/0{,}7 = 1{,}4286\).

14.7.4 Example H: a suddenly heated surface

Take a beam with a rectangular cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm (width \(20\) mm, height \(h = 40\) mm) of the same steel, everything at the reference temperature. At the instant \(t = 0\) we raise the top edge by \(20\) K and keep it so, and we keep the bottom edge at the reference temperature, for example against a massive support. The sides and ends are thermally insulated, and the top and bottom edges have the same temperature over the whole face, so \(T\) does not depend on \(x\) and \(z\) and heat is conducted only along the height. Insulated sides are an idealization: in reality heat also escapes through the sides. The beam is free, so lemma 14.41 holds.

We measure the height from the bottom edge, \(y' = \eta\,h\) with \(0 \le \eta \le 1\), and time with the dimensionless number \(\mathrm{Fo} = \kappa\,t/h^2\) (Fourier number, Fourierjevo število), which bears Fourier's name like the law of section 14.1. In the series of theorem 14.26, written for a bar of length \(h\), the exponents are \(-\kappa\,n^2\pi^2 t/h^2 = -n^2\pi^2\,\mathrm{Fo}\), so \(\mathrm{Fo}\) is time in the scale in which the temperature evens out over the height. To the temperature profile of section 14.5 we add the steady state: \(20\,\eta\) is a solution of the heat equation with the prescribed values at the edges, so it is the steady state (lemma 14.19). The remainder \(w = T - 20\,\eta\) is zero at both edges and has the initial value \(-20\,\eta\), so by theorem 14.26 \(w = \sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}} \sin(n\pi\eta)\,e^{-n^2\pi^2\mathrm{Fo}}\) with \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\int_0^1 (-20\,\eta)\sin(n\pi\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta = 40\,(-1)^n/(n\pi)\) (integration by parts, corollary 3.41). Hence

\[\Delta T(\eta, \mathrm{Fo}) = 20\,\eta + \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty} \frac{40\,(-1)^n}{n\pi}\,\sin(n\pi\eta)\,e^{-n^2\pi^2\,\mathrm{Fo}} . \tag{14.38}\]

We checked the series against an independent computation, the implicit finite difference method: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}005\) they differ by at most \(0{,}006\) K and at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}05\) by \(0{,}0006\) K. The left part of figure 14.29 shows \(\Delta T\) over the height at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}005\): the lower \(60\) % or so of the height is still at zero, and just below the top edge the temperature rises quickly to \(20\) K. The dashed line is \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) with \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 1{,}60\) K and \(g\,h = 8{,}38\) K, computed from equation (14.36).

The stress from lemma 14.41 is in the right part of the figure. The table shows what happens at the top and bottom edges and where the tension is largest (computed with NumPy, equations (14.38) and (14.37), \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K).

Let us read the curves and the table. The top fibre is in compression. The reason: it would like to expand by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), but the rest of the beam, which is colder, does not let it. By the lemma the stress is minus \(E\alpha\) times the difference between \(\Delta T\) and the line: at the top \(\Delta T = 20\) K is above the line, which has \(5{,}8\) K there, and \(-E\alpha\,(20 - 5{,}78) = -41{,}8\) MPa. The middle is in tension, because there \(\Delta T\) is below the line, and the sum \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) must be zero (step 5 of the lemma), so the tension must balance the compression at the top edge. At the top face the stress is largest right after the step. For \(\mathrm{Fo} \to 0\) the hot layer is thin and its contribution to \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(g\) is negligible, so the top stress is \(-E\,\alpha\cdot 20 = -58{,}8\) MPa, which is the stress of a fully restrained bar from chapter 3 (equation (3.18) with \(\Delta T = 20\) K). We checked: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 10^{-4}\) it is \(-56{,}18\) MPa, and by computation this is also the largest \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|\) in this example for all \(\mathrm{Fo} \ge 10^{-4}\). For another temperature profile between \(0\) and \(20\) K this is no bound: the difference between \(\Delta T\) and its line can exceed \(20\) K. The value \(58{,}8\) MPa is \(26{,}7\) % of the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (definition 2.7). As time goes on, \(\Delta T\) approaches the line \(20\,\eta\) (last row \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}2\): \(-3{,}3\) MPa), for which the lemma, like theorem 5.37, gives zero stress: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}5\) the largest stress is \(0{,}17\) MPa. The stress at the top face is therefore largest at the start. The tension in the middle and the compression at the bottom edge, however, grow later (in the table the largest tension is \(11{,}09\) MPa at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}02\), the bottom \(-12{,}10\) MPa at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}05\)). At the bottom \(\Delta T = 0\) all the time, while the line \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) drops below zero there as \(g\) grows, so the stress of the bottom edge (\(y = -h/2\)) is \(E\alpha\,(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\,h/2) < 0\), a compression. All stresses disappear when the temperature settles. This is contrary to the naive expectation that the stress would be largest when the beam holds the most heat, that is in the steady state with \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 10\) K.

14.7.5 Why the model has no residual stresses

Example H ends in zero. Does this always hold? A body can heat unevenly and then equalize its temperature. Does the model have stresses after the equalization? The answer is given by the next corollary, and from it follows that the stresses that LOVE describes in cooled castings cannot arise in the elastic model.

Corollary 14.42 No residual stresses in the model (brez zaostalih napetosti)

Let the body of definition 14.38 have a material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), the traction \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\) prescribed on its whole boundary (\(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty), no body force, \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and let \(T\) equal a constant value over the whole body at some instant. Then the stresses at that instant are zero, whatever the temperature was before.

Proof
  1. At that instant \(\nabla T = \vek 0\) and \(\Delta T\) is constant, \(\Delta T = \Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\). In theorem 14.39 \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{E\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\vek n\).
  2. The field \(\vek u = \alpha\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,(x, y, z)\) satisfies equation (14.33) (a) as in the test above and the boundary condition (14.34) (b) with \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\), so it is a solution with stress \(\ten\sigma = \vek 0\).
  3. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be any solution of the same problem at that instant. By theorem 14.39, \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) are solutions of the boundary value problem without temperature (definition 11.1) for the same loading, \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) in the interior and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the boundary, and their stresses in that problem are \(\ten\sigma^{e}\) and \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e}\) from step 1 of the proof of the theorem.
  4. Since \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), by theorem 3.48 the strain energy density is positive, so by theorem 11.4, part 1, \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e} = \ten\sigma^{e}\).
  5. Since \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\) is the same for both solutions, by step 1 of the proof of theorem 14.39 also \(\tilde{\ten\sigma} = \tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mat I = \ten\sigma\), which is zero by step 2. The stress at that instant is zero everywhere.

Source: The proof is not from the sources.

The meaning: the elastic model has no memory. There are two reasons. In definition 14.38 time is only a parameter, and by uniqueness as in steps 3 to 5 of the proof the stresses at an instant are determined by the temperature at that instant, not by its history. The corollary says this for a uniform temperature: if after the heating we cool the beam of example H to a uniform temperature, there is no stress in the model, however fast we heated it before. Example H itself ends at the line \(20\,\eta\), and there the stress is zero by lemma 14.41. A stress that remains in a cooled body must therefore have another cause, which the model does not contain.

What this cause is, another example shows: instead of \(20\) K we raise the top edge by \(100\) K. The model is linear in \(\Delta T\) (theorem 14.39), so the stress is five times larger: at the top \(-294{,}0\) MPa for \(\mathrm{Fo} \to 0\) (figure 14.30, orange curve). This is above the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa of the steel of chapter 3, and above it the model does not hold: Hooke's law is valid only below the yield stress. The stress reaches \(220\) MPa when the top stress of the \(20\) K case equals \(44\) MPa, which is at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) (from the same computed series as the table). On the horizontal axis of the figure is \(\mathrm{Fo}\), on the vertical one \(-\sigma\), which grows downward, so a larger compression is lower. Up to \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) the orange curve reaches beyond the dashed limit, and there the model does not hold. The blue curve for \(20\) K never reaches the limit, and for it the model holds to the end. What happens in the layer that has yielded is shown by figure 3.6 of chapter 3: after unloading the strain does not return to zero, a permanent strain remains. So for the \(100\) K step the model does not hold after \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) either, when the compression along the orange curve is already smaller than the limit: the layer that has yielded keeps a permanent strain. Permanent strains in a thin layer at the edge that the surroundings cannot even out are again incompatible, like the quadratic temperature above, and so stresses remain even when the temperature is the same everywhere. LOVE (PDF p. 128) mentions this for cast iron: the outer parts cool more rapidly than the inner ones, and the unequal contractions that accompany the unequally rapid rates of cooling give rise to considerable initial stress in the iron when cold. The elastic model cannot give these stresses: by corollary 14.42 there are none in it, however fast the body cooled. Only a model that also knows yielding captures them.

The model therefore does not contain yielding, coupled thermoelasticity, radiation and the dependence of \(E\), \(\alpha\) and conductivity on temperature, and so it does not know residual stresses (section 14.12). Theorem 14.39 remains the basis also for the plane and cylindrical cases treated in section 14.8: we turn temperature into a force in the same way as here.

Let us state the notation. Chapter 5 uses \(\kappa\) for curvature, in this chapter \(\kappa\) is the thermal diffusivity, so the curvature is written \(v''\) here. The letter \(h\) in this section is the height of the beam from chapter 5 and not the film coefficient (section 14.3). The letter \(k\) is the thermal conductivity, not the shear factor. The letter \(T\) is temperature, not the string force or a stress, and \(c\) is the wave speed in a bar in this section and the specific heat elsewhere in the chapter.
