Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.7 Thermal stresses (temperaturne napetosti)

The sections of this chapter so far have given the temperature: from Fourier's law and the energy balance of a finite part we obtained the heat equation, solved it for a bar and for a rectangle, and computed how the field \(T\) changes in time. Chapter 3 also showed that a freely heated body expands without stress, while a restrained one acquires the stress \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) (theorem 3.23, equation (3.36)), and chapter 5 computed the curvature for a temperature that is linear over the height of a beam (theorem 5.37). In both chapters the temperature was a datum taken from nowhere. Now we have it from a computation. What stresses does a temperature that varies over the body cause?

An everyday case: we pour hot water into a thick glass. The inner layer of the glass warms first and would like to expand, the outer layer is still cold and does not let it. The layers oppose each other as they go, and how large the force between them is cannot be found without a computation. The stress therefore does not depend only on how hot the glass is, but on how the temperature changes from place to place.

Before we write the computation we must say in which direction the influence runs. Temperature determines stresses, because a heated body expands. Conversely, a deformation also changes the temperature of the body slightly, as SADD states (PDF p. 379): the influence is small for most materials under static or slow loading, so we neglect it here and in chapter 3. Let us draw this on the board (figure 14.70). On the left is the box with the heat equation that we solved in the previous sections. From it comes the field \(T\), the blue arrow: this we pass on as a datum. On the right is the box of the elastic problem, whose equation we derive right below, and from it come the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The path back, from the displacements \(\vek u\) to the temperature, is dashed and crossed out: this is the influence we neglect. Next to it is written the term that carries this influence, we read it right after the definition, together with the lower part of the figure. When a problem has a path in one direction only, we call it uncoupled (nesklopljen, in the lectures nevezan). The name comes from the word to couple (sklopiti), to join two things so that each acts on the other. A coupled problem would have both paths and would solve them together, an uncoupled one solves them one after the other.

Definition 14.38 Uncoupled thermoelastic problem (nesklopljeni termoelastični problem)

Let the body be an open connected set of points \(D\) with a boundary split into \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) as in definition 11.1. Let the material be homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with modulus of elasticity \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (\(\nu \neq -1\), \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\)) and coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\), all constant, and stress-free at the reference temperature \(T_0\) (definition 3.21). Let \(T(\vek x, t)\) be the solution of the boundary value problem of conduction (definition 14.14), a field we know before we ask about stresses, and \(\Delta T = T - T_0\). Let the body force \(\vek b\), the displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and the traction \(\bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) be given. Time \(t\) is only a parameter: for each instant separately we look for a displacement field \(\vek u\) with continuous second derivatives such that

  1. the strains \(\ten\varepsilon\) are the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek u\) (definition 1.121),
  2. the stresses follow from \(\ten\varepsilon\) and \(\Delta T\) by theorem 3.23, \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\),
  3. the equilibrium equations (2.38) with the force \(\vek b\) hold in the interior, without acceleration terms,
  4. on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\) and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we have \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\).

Source: SADD §12.2 (PDF p. 380): uncoupled thermoelasticity, the temperature from the energy equation, then Hooke's law with temperature and the field equations, the mechanical boundary conditions as before and the thermal ones with prescribed temperature or flux. The definition in this form is ours.

14.7.1 The coupled heat equation and the way to the uncoupled problem

What exactly did we leave out with the crossed-out path? The heat equation (14.7) is the heat balance of a piece that is at rest and does not change its volume (idealization 1 of section 14.2). A piece that deforms has one more term in its balance. SADD (12.1.7) (PDF p. 379) writes the energy equation of a linearly elastic isotropic body that is stress-free at the reference temperature \(T_0\). In our notation it is

\[k\,\nabla^2 T + Q = \rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,T_0\,\frac{\partial \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial t} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} . \tag{14.33}\]
\[k\,T_{,\mathrm{ii}} + Q = \rho c\,\dot T + (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,T_0\,\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}} \tag{14.33}\]

We do not derive the equation: SADD states it as a result of thermodynamics and refers elsewhere for the derivation (PDF p. 379), and the other books of the project do not have it. Let us read it on the lower part of figure 14.70, term by term. The first box, \(k\,\nabla^2 T\), is the heat that conduction brings to the piece per unit time and volume. The second, \(Q\), is the source in the piece. On the right is the storage \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\), the heat that raises the temperature of the piece. Up to here this is equation (14.7). New is the last box, the coupling term (sklopitveni člen). In it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) is the volumetric strain (definition 2.114), that is the relative change of volume of the piece, the number \(3\lambda + 2\mu = E/(1 - 2\nu)\) is three times the bulk modulus \(K\) (definition 3.15), and \(T_0\) is here the absolute temperature in kelvins and not a difference. The equation is linearized (SADD, PDF p. 379), so it holds for small strains and for changes of temperature that are small compared with \(T_0\): in example H below the change is at most \(20\) K, that is \(6{,}8\) % of \(T_0 = 293\) K. What does the term say? For \(\alpha > 0\), as for the steel of this chapter, a piece whose volume grows, \(\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial t > 0\), spends heat on it, and if no heat flows in, it cools. A piece that we compress warms up under the same conditions. With a gas we know this from experience: air that we compress quickly in a bicycle pump warms the pump. In a solid the effect is of the same kind, only much weaker, as the estimate below will show. This very term is the path back in the upper part of the figure: the displacements determine \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) appears in the equation for the temperature.

In the lectures the equation is written in index notation, with \(c_{\mathrm{v}}\) for the specific heat \(c\) (at constant volume), with a dot for the time derivative and with the factor \(E/(1 - 2\nu)\) in place of \(3\lambda + 2\mu\). SADD writes the source as \(\rho h\) with \(h\) per unit mass, here \(Q = \rho h\) is the source per unit volume.

Let us count the unknowns and the equations, the numbers are above the boxes of figure 14.70. The elastic problem has \(15\) unknowns: three displacements \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), six strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and six stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). There are six strains and six stresses and not nine, because the two tensors are symmetric. For them we have \(15\) equations: six relations between strains and displacements (definition 1.121), six equations of Hooke's law with temperature (theorem 3.23) and three equilibrium equations (2.38). The temperature is the sixteenth unknown and equation (14.33) the sixteenth equation. Together this is \(16\) equations for \(16\) unknowns, and SADD counts the same for the uncoupled set (§12.2, PDF p. 380), in the coupled one only the sixteenth equation changes. We do not count the compatibility equations: once the strains are computed from displacements, they hold by themselves (theorem 1.133). In the coupled problem all \(16\) equations must be solved together, because the temperature appears in Hooke's law and the strain in the equation for the temperature.

Three conditions simplify equation (14.33), each crosses out one box in figure 14.70.

  1. The volume of the pieces does not change in time, \(\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial t = 0\). The coupling term drops out and equation (14.7) remains, in which there are no displacements. We compute the temperature first and by itself, this is \(1\) equation for \(1\) unknown, and then with the known \(T\) we solve the \(15\) mechanical equations. The problem is uncoupled (definition 14.38).
  2. There are no sources, \(Q = 0\). What remains is \(k\,\nabla^2 T = \rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\), which for a temperature that depends on one coordinate only is the equation of the cooling bar of section 14.5.
  3. The temperature no longer changes, \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), the steady state. What remains is the Laplace equation \(\nabla^2 T = 0\) of sections 14.4 and 14.6 (SADD (12.1.9), PDF p. 379).

Condition 1 suffices for uncoupling. Conditions 2 and 3 simplify only the thermal part, and we take them when they hold. Condition 1 does not hold exactly during heating. Let us draw four pieces on the right part of figure 14.71, each grey dashed before heating and orange after it. The first piece warms up and nothing holds it, so it expands, and its volume changes. The condition holds exactly only when nothing changes any more, that is in the steady state under static loading, and for a piece whose expansion we prevent completely, the third in the figure, which walls hold from all sides. Otherwise we adopt it as an approximation, so we must say what it costs.

How large is the term we crossed out? Let us compare it with the storage \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\) for a piece that expands freely, because for it we compute the term without the elastic problem. For it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 3\alpha\,\Delta T\) (definition 3.21), so \(\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial t = 3\alpha\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the coupling term is \(3\,(3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha^2\,T_0\,T_{\mathrm{t}}\). It has the same \(T_{\mathrm{t}}\) as the storage, so we can add them: the piece behaves as if it had the storage \(\rho c\,(1 + \delta)\) with

\[\delta = \frac{3\,(3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha^2\,T_0}{\rho c} . \tag{14.34}\]

For the steel of this chapter \(3\lambda + 2\mu = 490\) GPa and \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, and at the room temperature \(T_0 = 293\) K the numerator is \(3\cdot 490\cdot 10^{9}\cdot (15\cdot 10^{-6})^2\cdot 293 = 96{,}91\ \text{kJ/(m}^3\,\text{K)}\). We have no source for \(\rho c\) of steel, so as an example of a metal we take the copper of example 14.11, \(\rho c = 0{,}82064\) cal/(cm³ °C), which with \(1\ \text{cal} = 4{,}1868\) J equals \(3{,}436\ \text{MJ/(m}^3\,\text{K)}\). We draw both numbers on the left part of figure 14.71: the tall bar is the storage \(3{,}436\) MJ/(m³ K), the thin orange layer on it is the coupling term \(96{,}91\) kJ/(m³ K). The layer is so thin that we see it only in the magnified view, where everything is ten times taller. The ratio is \(\delta = 0{,}028\). A freely heated piece would therefore warm up just under \(3\) % more slowly than equation (14.7) predicts, and this is the cost of condition 1. For a piece whose neighbours hinder its expansion, the second on the right part of the figure, \(\partial\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial t\) is smaller, and zero when the expansion is fully prevented. A piece that its neighbours stretch while its own temperature has not yet changed (the core in example H) has a coupling term with no storage to compare with, so \(\delta\) is a measure of size and not an upper bound. The number is an order-of-magnitude estimate, because it combines the elastic constants of steel with the heat storage of copper. SADD (PDF p. 379) says the same in words: for most materials the term is small under static or slow loading. This estimate does not cover fast loading, in which the volume changes quickly and the temperature hardly at all, and there we do not use the uncoupled model.

The second idealization of the definition is the absence of acceleration, quasi-static (kvazistatičen) behaviour: each instant is solved as an equilibrium. Why may we do this? If a disturbance evens out over the body much faster than the temperature changes, the body is nearly in equilibrium at every instant and we need no acceleration. So we must compare two times. Let us draw them on the time axis of figure 14.72, which is logarithmic: each division means a ten times longer time. A disturbance crosses the beam height \(h = 40\) mm at the wave speed of a bar \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho} = 5012{,}80\) m/s (chapter 13, equation (13.19)) in the time \(h/c = 8{,}0\ \mu\text{s}\), the blue line on the left. The temperature changes appreciably over the same height in the time \(\mathrm{Fo}\,h^2/\kappa\) with the dimensionless number \(\mathrm{Fo}\) that we introduce below. At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}002\) the stress in the example below is still \(81\) % of its largest value (\(47{,}6\) of \(58{,}8\) MPa), and this time is, for the thermal diffusivity of copper from KREYSZIG (PDF p. 587), \(\kappa = 1{,}1576\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}\), equal to \(27{,}6\) ms, the orange line on the right. This is \(3460\) times more than \(8{,}0\ \mu\text{s}\), the blue distance between the lines spans three and a half divisions. We have no source for \(\kappa\) of steel, so we take copper as an example of a metal. When does the idealization fail? For the passage of a disturbance to be at least \(100\) times faster than the change of temperature, we need \(\kappa \le 0{,}002\,h\,c/100 = 0{,}0040\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}\), which is up to \(35\) times the value for copper. For a substance that conducts heat even faster, or for a thinner piece, because the time of the temperature falls with \(h^2\) and the time of the disturbance only with \(h\), we would have to compute with acceleration. Also for the beam of example H below the idealization fails right after the jump of temperature: within a time of the order of \(h/c\) the temperature at the edge changes faster than a disturbance crosses the height. This is up to \(\mathrm{Fo} = \kappa/(h\,c) = 5{,}8\cdot 10^{-7}\), far below the smallest \(\mathrm{Fo} = 10^{-4}\) that the example computes, so the example does not describe these first microseconds.

This comparison covers the stress over the height of the cross-section. The beam also bends at the same time (the curvature \(v''\) in lemma 14.42 below), and this is a slower motion of the whole beam. Its lowest period of vibration grows with the square of the length, as equation (13.51) shows for a simply supported beam. For a simply supported beam with the same cross-section and \(L = 1\) m this period is \(1/(90{,}92\ \text{Hz}) = 11{,}0\) ms (section 13.6.4), the grey line of figure 14.72, only \(2{,}5\) times less than \(27{,}6\) ms. For bending the condition therefore holds only for a short beam or slower heating. Otherwise the beam vibrates about the curvature of the lemma, and the stress differs from the lemma by a term that is linear in \(y\). We do not compute this in the example below.

14.7.2 Temperature as a force in the body and on its boundary

From the definition the unknown is the displacement, as in the boundary value problem of chapter 11. There the Navier-Lamé equations (theorem 11.6) gave three equations for the displacements. Does temperature change them? The stresses now have one more term with \(\Delta T\), so the equation will be different. We must look at where this term goes. We expect two places, because we know two parts of the problem: the equilibrium equation in the interior and the boundary condition for the traction.

Theorem 14.39 Navier-Lamé equations with temperature (Navier-Laméjeve enačbe s temperaturo)

Let the material and the constants \(E\), \(\nu\), \(\alpha\), \(\lambda\), \(\mu\) be as in definition 14.38, \(T_0\) constant, \(T\) with continuous first derivatives and \(\vek u\) with continuous second derivatives on an open set \(D\). Let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stresses from \(\vek u\) and \(\Delta T\) by step 2 of the definition, \(\mat H\) the displacement gradient (definition 1.112) and \(\vek b\) a vector at a point \(P\) of \(D\).

(a) The stresses \(\ten\sigma\) satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.38) at \(P\) with body force \(\vek b\) if and only if at \(P\)

\[\mu\,\nabla^2\vek u + (\lambda + \mu)\,\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) + \vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -\frac{E\,\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\nabla T . \tag{14.35}\]
\[\mu\,u_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{jj}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,u_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{ji}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,T_{,\mathrm{i}} = 0 \tag{14.35}\]

(b) Let \(P\) be a point of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the surface with outward normal \(\vek n\) and prescribed \(\bar{\vek t}\). The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) holds at \(P\) if and only if

\[\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} , \qquad \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{E\,\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\Delta T\,\vek n . \tag{14.36}\]
Idea

The stresses of theorem 3.23 are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) without temperature, from which we subtract \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\). This term is the same in all directions at each point, but varies from point to point with \(\Delta T\). The first part we know from chapter 11, and the subtracted term is a number times the identity matrix, so its divergence is only the derivative of the temperature and its product with the normal is only a number times the normal.

Proof

We must show that the left side of the equilibrium equation, with the stresses from \(\vek u\) and \(\Delta T\) inserted, equals the left side of (14.35), and that \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) equals the left side of (14.36) minus \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\). Write \(\beta = 3\lambda + 2\mu\), which is three times the bulk modulus (definition 3.15). Steps 2 to 4 take place in the interior of the body of figure 14.73 below, step 5 on its surface.

  1. At each point of the body the matrix form of theorem 3.23 is \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\). Since \(\tr(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I) = 3\alpha\,\Delta T\), this is \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - \beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) with \(\ten\sigma^{e} = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon\). These are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) without temperature, their components are (11.4). By steps 3 to 6 of part 2 of the proof of theorem 3.23, \(\beta = E/(1 - 2\nu)\).
  2. In the interior of the body, by step 6 of the proof of theorem 11.6, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma^{e}_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). This holds because \(\vek u\) has continuous second derivatives.
  3. The \(ji\) component of the term \(-\beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is \(-\beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\), which is nonzero only for \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\). Hence \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\big(-\beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ji}}\big)/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = -\beta\,\alpha\,\partial(\Delta T)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), the constants \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\) stay in front of the derivative. Here homogeneity enters: if \(E\), \(\nu\) or \(\alpha\) varied from point to point, the derivative would give one more term with their gradient. Since \(T_0\) is constant, \(\partial(\Delta T)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), a component of the gradient of the temperature.
  4. From steps 1 to 3, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} - \beta\,\alpha\,\partial T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\). The equilibrium equation \(\ix{i}\) holds if and only if this sum is zero. For \(\ix{i} = x, y, z\) this is the vector equation (14.35) with \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -\beta\,\alpha\,\nabla T\), the blue arrows in the interior. This is part (a).
  5. By step 1, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \ten\sigma^{e}\,\vek n - \beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n\), since \(\mat I\,\vek n = \vek n\). By step 1 of corollary 11.7, \(\ten\sigma^{e}\,\vek n = \lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n\). The condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) is therefore the same as \(\lambda\,(\operatorname{div}\vek u)\,\vek n + \mu\,(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} + \beta\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n\), with an addition along the normal that the orange arrows on the surface show. This is part (b).

Source: SADD (PDF p. 381): for the plane case Navier's equations contain additional temperature terms, which SADD calls thermal body forces (SADD §12.3, text read on PDF p. 381). The theorem in space and the proof are ours, checked with SymPy (residual zero).

What did the theorem say? Temperature enters the equilibrium in two places. In the interior of the body it acts as a body force (prostorninska sila) \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) that points toward lower temperature, since \(-\nabla T\) is the direction in which the temperature falls (figure 14.73). On the surface it acts as an additional traction \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) that is normal to the surface and proportional to \(\Delta T\) at that point. Let us build the figure from the start. The body is a rectangle, hot on the left and cold on the right. The isotherms, lines of equal temperature \(T_1 > T_2 > T_3\), are vertical. The gradient is the vector that points in the direction of fastest increase of temperature, so \(\nabla T\) points left, toward the hot part. The force \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = -(E\alpha/(1 - 2\nu))\,\nabla T\) points the opposite way, to the right, and is the same at every point, because for a linear \(T\) the gradient is constant. Each force arrow starts at the point it acts on. The surface part \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) points outward where \(\Delta T > 0\), and in the figure the whole body is warmer than the reference temperature \(T_0\). It is larger at the hot edge, because \(\Delta T\) is larger there. Why does the force point toward the cold side? Look at a piece in the middle of the body. Its left neighbour is warmer and would like to expand more than the right one, so it pushes the piece from the left harder than the right neighbour pushes from the right. The difference of the two pushes is a force to the right, toward the cold side. Where the neighbours are equally warm, the pushes cancel: this is why the gradient \(\nabla T\) appears in the force and not the temperature itself.

The theorem says that forces replace the temperature, but these forces (figure 14.73) are not real. No external force acts, the body can be entirely free. It is a matter of computation: the displacement field satisfies the same equations as the field in a body that is not heated but is loaded with \(\vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) in the interior and with \(\bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the surface. So from temperature onward we need no new tool: what we learned about solutions in chapter 11 holds, only the loading, which we obtain from \(T\), differs. The force \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) is therefore an apparent body force: it has this name because it enters the equilibrium in the same place as weight.

Let us test the theorem on a case we know. A free steel body, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, in equilibrium without external forces, \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek b = \vek 0\), is heated uniformly by \(\Delta T = 30\) K. Try the field \(\vek u = \alpha\,\Delta T\,(x, y, z)\). The constants: \(\lambda = 113{,}077\) GPa, \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa and \(3\lambda + 2\mu = 490{,}000\) GPa, which is indeed \(E/(1 - 2\nu) = 196/0{,}4\) GPa. Equation (14.35) (a): \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 3\alpha\,\Delta T\) is constant and \(\nabla T = \vek 0\), so \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) and all three terms are satisfied. Boundary condition (b): \(\mat H = \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), the left side is \((3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\vek n = 490\ \text{GPa}\cdot 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,\vek n = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n\), and the right side is \(0 + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\vek n\), as the orange arrows of figure 14.73 illustrate, which are equally long in this case. The field is a solution, and the stress is \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\mat I\) with \(\ten\sigma^{e} = (\lambda \cdot 3 + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}\,\mat I\), that is, zero. A freely heated body has no stress, as chapter 3 showed. This is also the only stress. By theorem 14.39 every solution \(\vek u\) is also a solution of the boundary value problem without temperature (definition 11.1) for the loading \(\vek b + \vek b_{\mathrm{T}}\) in the interior and \(\bar{\vek t} + \vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the surface, and its stresses in that problem are \(\ten\sigma^{e}\). For steel \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), so by theorem 3.64 the strain energy density is positive, and by theorem 11.4, part 1, all solutions have the same \(\ten\sigma^{e}\). With the same \(\Delta T\) they therefore also have the same \(\ten\sigma = \ten\sigma^{e} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\).

Now a case in which this fails. Let us draw what each part of the body would like to do if it were alone (figure 14.74). We cut the body into five horizontal layers and align their left ends on one line. Grey dashed is the body before heating, all layers are equally long. Each layer then lengthens freely by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\) with its own \(\Delta T\), these are the orange layers, and the elongations are greatly exaggerated. For a uniform \(\Delta T\) on the left all lengthen equally and the right ends stay on one vertical line. The layers can be glued back without force. For the linear \(\Delta T = g\,y\) in the middle the top layer is the longest and the ends lie on one inclined straight line. The plane cross-section therefore only rotates, the layers again glue together without force, and the body bends in the process. For the quadratic \(\Delta T = a\,y^2\) on the right, where \(y\) is measured from the middle layer, the ends lie on a parabola. No straight line passes through all of them. When we glue the layers, the cross-section must stay plane, so the layers that reach beyond the blackwhite line must be shortened and those that do not reach it must be stretched, each by its blue distance. This takes forces between the layers, that is stresses.

The figure shows one direction, and the computation says the same for a body in space. Suppose every piece of the body expands freely, as if alone, so the strain is \(\ten\varepsilon = \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) at every point, and there is no stress. For the linear variation \(\Delta T = g\,y\) a displacement field exists: \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha g\,xy\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha g\,(y^2 - x^2 - z^2)/2\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha g\,yz\) gives the strain \(\alpha g\,y\,\mat I\) and all three shear components zero (checked with SymPy). A free body with a linear temperature variation is therefore indeed stress-free and bends, as theorem 5.37 described it. For the quadratic variation \(\Delta T = a\,y^2\) with a constant \(a \neq 0\), however, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \alpha a\,y^2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), and equation (1) of theorem 1.133 gives \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 = 2\alpha a\), while the right side \(2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\) is \(0\). The requirement is not satisfied, so no displacement field has this strain. The pieces cannot all grow as they would like at once, so stresses appear between them. This is exactly the answer to the question about the glass: the inner layer of the glass is the layer that reaches beyond the line, the outer one is the layer that does not reach it.

14.7.3 Equations for the stresses with temperature

Theorem 14.39 built the temperature into the equations for the displacements. Chapter 11 knows a second way, in which the unknowns are the stresses: the six equations (11.7) (theorem 11.9) tell when stresses that are in equilibrium belong to some displacement field. There the temperature was constant. What changes when it is not? The strain now has two parts, and figure 14.74 shows both. The thermal part \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is the free elongation of the orange layers, the temperature determines it. The elastic part is determined by the stresses, and it must fill the blue distances exactly, so that the layers stack into a body without gaps. So the sum of both parts must be compatible and not each part by itself. We expect the condition for the stresses to get terms with the temperature on the right side, namely with its second derivatives, because a linear variation makes no gaps.

Theorem 14.40 Beltrami-Michell equations with temperature (Beltrami-Michellove enačbe s temperaturo)

Let the material be isotropic and homogeneous with Young's modulus \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) and coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\), all constant, and let the reference temperature \(T_0\) be constant. Let \(D\) be an open set of points, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\) functions with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\), \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and \(T\) a function with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(D\). Let the equilibrium equations (2.38) hold at every point of \(D\) with a body force \(\vek b\) whose components have continuous first partial derivatives. Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the strains that the stresses and \(\Delta T = T - T_0\) determine by Hooke's law with temperature (3.31). Then the compatibility equations (1.76) hold at a point \(P\) of \(D\) if and only if at \(P\), for all nine choices of the indices \(i\), \(j\),

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = {} & -\frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - \Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) \\ & - \frac{\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T - \frac{\alpha E}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} . \end{aligned} \tag{14.37}\]
\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{kk}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kk},\mathrm{ij}} = {} & -\frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,b_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{k}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} - b_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} - b_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{i}} \\ & - \frac{\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\,T_{,\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} - \frac{\alpha E}{1 + \nu}\,T_{,\mathrm{ij}} \end{aligned} \tag{14.37}\]

Here \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equals \(1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(0\) otherwise. From these equations it follows, by summing over \(i = j\),

\[\nabla^2\Theta = -\frac{1 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b - \frac{2\,\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\,\nabla^2 T . \tag{14.38}\]

For a constant \(T\) the two terms with temperature are zero and the equations are (11.7) and (11.8).

Idea

We repeat the proof of theorem 11.9 with one addition. Hooke's law with temperature adds the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) to every normal strain (the orange layers of figure 14.74). The expression \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\), which measures the incompatibility, is a sum of second derivatives of the strains, so the addition passes through it separately and leaves behind only second derivatives of the temperature.

Proof

We must show that the number \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of lemma 11.8 for these strains equals, up to a constant factor, the difference of the left and right sides of (14.37). By the lemma, compatibility at \(P\) is the same as \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\).

  1. Hooke's law with temperature (3.31) can be written for all six components as \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \nu\,\Theta\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + E\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The first two terms are step 1 of the proof of theorem 11.9, this is the elastic part. The third is the thermal part: every normal strain gets \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), the shear strains nothing. In figure 14.74 the third term is the free elongation of a layer, and the first two are what shortens or stretches the layer to the plane cross-section.
  2. The expression \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) in (11.6) is a sum of second derivatives of the strains, so \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of a sum of two strains is the sum of their \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For the elastic part steps 2 to 4 of the proof of theorem 11.9 hold unchanged, because they use only homogeneity, the symmetry of the stresses, the equilibrium equations and the interchange of mixed derivatives. Their result is the right side of (11.9).
  3. Let us compute \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the thermal part \(\tau\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) with \(\tau = E\alpha\,\Delta T\). The four terms of (11.6) are in turn: \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\tau\), then \(3\,\partial^2\tau/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\), because the sum of the diagonal components is \(3\tau\), then \(-\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \partial^2(\tau\,\delta_{\mathrm{ik}})/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{k}} = -\partial^2\tau/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), because only the term \(\ix{k} = \ix{i}\) remains, and likewise the fourth with \(i\) and \(j\) interchanged. The mixed derivatives are equal, because the second derivatives of \(T\) are continuous (theorem 1.131). Together this is \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\tau + (3 - 1 - 1)\,\partial^2\tau/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). Since \(E\), \(\alpha\) and \(T_0\) are constant, the derivatives of \(\tau\) are \(E\alpha\) times the derivatives of \(T\).
  4. From steps 2 and 3 (the elastic and thermal parts),
    \[\begin{aligned} E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = {} & (1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} - \nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta + (1 + \nu)\Big(\frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) \\ & + E\alpha\,\Big(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T + \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big) . \end{aligned}\]
  5. Sum over \(i = j\). The first row gives \(2\,(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta + 2\,(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\) as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 11.9. The second (thermal part) gives \(E\alpha\,(3 + 1)\,\nabla^2 T\), because \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \delta_{\mathrm{ii}} = 3\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial^2 T/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = \nabla^2 T\). If all \(R_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are zero, then \(2\,(1 - \nu)\,\nabla^2\Theta + 2\,(1 + \nu)\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b + 4E\alpha\,\nabla^2 T = 0\), and since \(1 - \nu > 0\), (14.38) follows.
  6. If equations (14.37) hold for all \(i\), \(j\), then (14.38) follows from them as well. Their sum over \(i = j\) has \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu}\,\nabla^2\Theta\) on the left, and on the right \(-\frac{2 + \nu}{1 - \nu}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b\) (step 6 of the proof of theorem 11.9) and \(-\alpha E\,\big(\frac{3}{1 - \nu} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\big)\,\nabla^2 T\). Since \(3\,(1 + \nu) + (1 - \nu) = 2\,(2 + \nu)\), the last term is \(-\frac{2\,(2 + \nu)\,\alpha E}{(1 - \nu)(1 + \nu)}\,\nabla^2 T\). We divide by \(\frac{2 + \nu}{1 + \nu} > 0\) and get (14.38).
  7. So let (14.38) hold, from step 5 or from step 6. The term \(-\nu\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2\Theta\) in step 4 is then \(\frac{\nu\,(1 + \nu)}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \frac{2\nu\,\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T\). The terms with \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T\) add up to
    \[\frac{2\nu\,\alpha E}{1 - \nu} + \alpha E = \frac{\alpha E\,(2\nu + 1 - \nu)}{1 - \nu} = \frac{\alpha E\,(1 + \nu)}{1 - \nu} ,\]
    so
    \[\begin{aligned} E\,R_{\mathrm{ij}} = (1 + \nu)\Big[ & \nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek b + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial b_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} \\ & + \frac{\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T + \frac{\alpha E}{1 + \nu}\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big] . \end{aligned}\]
    In the bracket is the left side of (14.37) minus the right side. Since \(1 + \nu > 0\), \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) if and only if (14.37) holds.
  8. Let the compatibility equations hold. By lemma 11.8 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), by step 5 (14.38) holds, and by step 7 so does (14.37). Conversely, let equations (14.37) hold. By step 6 (14.38) holds, by step 7 \(R_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for all \(i\), \(j\), and by the lemma the compatibility equations hold.

Source: The derivation is ours, along the steps of the proof of theorem 11.9 (SADD (5.3.3) (PDF p. 109), CM (7.2.26) (PDF p. 300), both without temperature). We did not find the three-dimensional form with temperature in the books of the project, SADD §12.3 (PDF p. 381) treats only the plane case. Checked with SymPy: for stresses from arbitrary fields \(\vek u\) and \(T\) by theorem 3.23 and with the force \(\vek b\) from equilibrium, the residual of all equations (14.37) and of equation (14.38) is zero.

In the lectures the equation is written in index notation with the indices \(1, 2, 3\), with \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the body force and with \(T\) for the temperature change \(\Delta T\). There the coefficient in front of \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\nabla^2 T\) is at first a sum of two fractions, which simplifies as in step 7: \(\frac{2\nu\,\alpha E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - \nu)} + \frac{\alpha E}{1 + \nu} = \frac{\alpha E}{1 - \nu}\) (checked with SymPy). The equations have three levels. With temperature and body force they hold in the form (14.37). For a constant temperature (11.7) remains. If the body force is constant as well, the right side is zero, and after multiplying by \(1 + \nu\) there remains

\[(1 + \nu)\,\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} + \frac{\partial^2\Theta}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} = 0 . \tag{14.39}\]

These are the Beltrami equations (Beltramijeve enačbe, CM (7.2.27), PDF p. 300). So they hold for a constant body force, for example weight, and not only for zero force, as the hanging bar in section 11.3 shows.

What does the theorem say? Temperature acts on the stresses only through its second derivatives. Let us test this on both profiles of figure 14.74, for a free body without body force and with the trial \(\ten\sigma = \mat 0\), for which the left side of (14.37) is zero. For the linear variation \(\Delta T = g\,y\) all second derivatives of the temperature are zero, the right side is zero and the equations are satisfied: a stress-free body is possible, as we saw. For the quadratic variation \(\Delta T = a\,y^2\) we have \(\nabla^2 T = \partial^2 T/\partial y^2 = 2a\). Take steel, \(E\alpha = 2{,}94\) MPa/K and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), so \(\alpha E/(1 - \nu) = 4{,}200\) MPa/K and \(\alpha E/(1 + \nu) = 2{,}262\) MPa/K, and \(a = 0{,}05\ \text{K/mm}^2\), which gives a change of \(20\) K at the edges of a beam of height \(40\) mm, at \(y = \pm 20\) mm. The right side of equation \(xx\) is \(-4{,}200\cdot 2a = -0{,}420\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\), and of equation \(yy\) it is \(-(4{,}200 + 2{,}262)\cdot 2a = -0{,}646\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\). The left side is zero for \(\ten\sigma = \mat 0\), so the equations are not satisfied: a stress-free body is not possible, and the theorem says so with a number. A stress whose second derivative in \(y\) is \(0{,}420\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\) changes from the middle layer to the edge, over \(20\) mm, by \(0{,}420\cdot 20^2/2 = 84\) MPa. This is the size of the blue distances, translated into stress. We find the field that satisfies the equations in section 14.7.5.

14.7.4 A bar between walls with an uneven temperature

Take the bar of chapter 3 (diameter \(10\) mm, \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), steel) between two rigid smooth walls, as in section 3.3.3, with the same three idealizations (rigid walls, smooth contacts, the bar stays straight). There the temperature was the same everywhere. What changes if on the left wall it is \(30\) K above the reference and on the right wall \(0\) K, and varies linearly in between, as in the steady state of a bar attached to a wall (lemma 14.19)? The bar is thermally insulated at its sides, so \(\Delta T\) is constant over the cross-section and is a function of \(x\) only (corollary 14.10). Let us draw (figure 14.75). At the top is the profile of \(\Delta T\), a straight line from \(30\) K to \(0\) K. Halfway between them is the mean \(15\) K, dashed. Below it is the piece of the bar between the cuts \(a\) and \(b\) that we will cut out in the proof, and below the piece the bar between the walls.

Lemma 14.41 Bar between walls with uneven temperature (palica med stenama z neenakomerno temperaturo)

Let a bar have constant cross-sectional area \(A\) and constant \(E\) and \(\alpha\) on the interval \(0 \le x \le L\), with the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross-sections. Both ends are restrained, \(u(0) = u(L) = 0\), the lateral surface is free and weight is neglected. Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and let the state be the same over each cross-section as in section 3.3.3, and let the temperature change \(\Delta T(x)\) be a continuous function on \([0, L]\) with \(\Delta T = T - T_0\). Then the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the same at all points of the bar,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T} , \qquad N = -E\,A\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T} , \qquad \overline{\Delta T} = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L \Delta T(x)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{14.40}\]
Proof

We must show that the stress is constant and compute its value.

  1. Take a piece of the bar \(a \le x \le b\), in figure 14.75 it is cut out and drawn with the forces on both cuts. At its cut at \(b\) the rest of the bar acts with the force \(N(b) = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(b)\), at the cut at \(a\) with the force \(-N(a)\) (the axis \(x\) points right, the outward normal of the cut at \(a\) points left, and by lemma 5.9 \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\)). The lateral surface is free and weight is neglected, so by Newton's law the piece is at rest only if \(N(b) - N(a) = 0\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) are arbitrary, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is constant in \(x\).
  2. The first row of equation (3.31) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T(x)\) (the profile), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u'\).
  3. We integrate over \([0, L]\). By theorem 3.55, \(\int_0^L u'\,\mathrm{d}x = u(L) - u(0) = 0\) because of the walls, which do not let the ends move. By lemma 3.54 the integral of a sum is the sum of integrals and the constant \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) comes out of the integral, so \(0 = \dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E}\,L + \alpha\int_0^L \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. From step 3, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) \(= -E\,\alpha\,\overline{\Delta T}\), and \(N = A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Uniaxial Hooke's law with temperature: FELIPPA (4.18) (PDF p. 58), SADD (4.4.1) (PDF p. 98), as in section 3.3.3.

The lemma said more than we expected. The stress is the same everywhere although the temperature is not, and it does not depend on the profile of the temperature, only on its mean. The reason is the first step: the equilibrium of every piece requires the same force in all cross-sections, so uneven expansion cannot create a stress that varies. What remains is the condition that the total elongation is zero, and this determines the single number \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Example B. For \(\Delta T(x) = 30\,(1 - x/L)\) we have \(\overline{\Delta T} = 15\) K, so

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196\cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}\cdot 15\cdot 10^{-6}\ \tfrac1{\text{K}}\cdot 15\ \text{K} = -44{,}10\ \text{MPa} , \qquad N = -44{,}10\ \tfrac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2}\cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = -3464\ \text{N}\]

(SymPy: \(-3463{,}6\) N). Heated uniformly by \(30\) K it gave \(-88{,}2\) MPa and \(-6927\) N (equation (3.36)): half, because the mean is half. The stress in figure 14.75 is the same along the whole length, the two arrows show how the bar presses on the walls. The displacement of a point, however, is not zero: from step 2, \(u'(x) = \alpha\,(\Delta T(x) - \overline{\Delta T})\), which is positive on the hot half, where the line is above the dashed mean, and negative on the cold half, so \(u(x) = \alpha\int_0^x (\Delta T - \overline{\Delta T})\,\mathrm{d}\xi\), largest at \(x = L/2\) with the value \(3{,}75\ \text{K}\cdot\alpha L\). For \(L = 10\) m (as in section 3.3.3) this is \(0{,}5625\) mm: the middle of the bar moves from the dashed grey position to the orange one, the blue distance is \(0{,}56\) mm (greatly exaggerated in the figure). The points move toward the cold wall, because the hot half stretches more than the mean.

A case the lemma does not cover: remove the right wall. Now the right free end carries no force (the bottom bar of figure 14.75). Step 1 holds as before, the force is the same in all cross-sections, but step 3 no longer holds, because \(u(L)\) is not zero. Step 1 and \(N(L) = 0\) give \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) for the same temperature profile, step 2 gives \(u' = \alpha\,\Delta T\), and the bar lengthens by \(u(L) = \alpha\int_0^L \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}x = \alpha L\,\overline{\Delta T} = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\cdot 10\ \text{m}\cdot 15 = 2{,}25\) mm, this is the blue distance between the dashed previous position of the end and the orange end. It is half the lengthening of \(4{,}50\) mm under a uniform \(30\) K. The stress is therefore not a property of the temperature, but of the temperature and the supports together. The same \(\Delta T(x)\) gives \(-44{,}10\) MPa between walls and \(0\) with one wall.

14.7.5 A beam with an arbitrary temperature profile

Now back to the beam (figure 14.76). Theorem 5.37 treated only \(\Delta T = \theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\). But when we heat a beam from one side, the temperature over the height is not a straight line: it is higher near the surface and falls toward the interior along a curve determined by the heat equation. How large is the stress then? We saw that the linear part gives no stress (figure 14.74), so we expect the stress to be determined by the part that is not linear, by the distances between the free ends of the layers and the plane cross-section.

Lemma 14.42 Free beam with an arbitrary temperature profile over the height (prosti nosilec s poljubnim potekom temperature po višini)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold for a beam, let \(E\) and \(\alpha\) be constant and let the beam be free, that is, without external loading and without supports that would hinder expansion or bending, so that \(N = M = 0\) along its whole length. Let the axis \(x\) run through the centroids of the cross-sections, \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). Let the temperature change \(\Delta T(y)\) be a continuous function of the height \(y\) in the cross-section and the same in every cross-section. With

\[\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1}{A}\int_{\mathrm{A}} \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad g = \frac{1}{I}\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A \tag{14.41}\]

we have \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\) and

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\big(\Delta T(y) - \theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\,y\big) . \tag{14.42}\]

The straight line \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) is therefore the one with the same mean and the same first moment as \(\Delta T\), and the stress is \(-E\alpha\) times the difference between \(\Delta T\) and this line.

Proof

The procedure is as in theorem 5.37, except that \(\Delta T\) is an arbitrary function. We read the proof on the right part of figure 14.74: the fibres of the beam are its layers.

  1. In every fibre \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (assumption 6 of chapter 5), so from the first row of equation (3.31) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T)\), and by theorem 5.15 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\). This is the plane cross-section: the strain of the fibres is linear in \(y\), whatever the temperature. The difference \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) is the blue distance between the line and the free end of the layer, divided by the length, and the stress is \(E\) times this difference.
  2. We add up the forces of the layers. By lemma 5.9, \(N = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,u'\,A - E\,v''\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A - E\,\alpha\int_{\mathrm{A}} \Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A\). The second term is zero (lemma 5.22), the third is \(E\,\alpha\,A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) by (14.41). Hence \(N = EA\,(u' - \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}})\), and \(N = 0\) gives \(u' = \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  3. We add up the moments of the layers. By the same lemma, \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -E\,u'\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,v''\,I + E\,\alpha\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A\). The first term is zero, the last is \(E\,\alpha\,g\,I\) by (14.41). Hence \(M = EI\,(v'' + \alpha\,g)\), and \(M = 0\) gives \(v'' = -\alpha\,g\).
  4. Insert \(u'\) and \(v''\) in step 1: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(\alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + \alpha\,g\,y - \alpha\,\Delta T)\), which is (14.42). Steps 2 and 3 therefore chose the position and the slope of the line: among all plane cross-sections the right one is that for which the forces of the layers and their moments cancel.
  5. Check of the straight cross-section: \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -E\alpha\,(A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - A\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\cdot 0) = 0\) and \(-\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\alpha\,(g\,I - \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}A) = 0\) by (14.41), so the field indeed gives \(N = M = 0\).

Source: The result is not from the sources, it is derived from theorem 3.23 and the assumptions of chapter 5, checked with SymPy (\(\Delta T = y^2\) on a rectangle: \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = h^2/12\), \(g = 0\), \(N = M = 0\)).

For a linear \(\Delta T = \theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\) the difference is zero, the ends of the layers lie on the line, and we get theorem 5.37: the lemma extends it to an arbitrary profile. For a uniform \(\Delta T\), \(g = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), as in a freely heated piece. The stress is therefore self-equilibrated: no force or moment remains, but it can be large. Example H gives a case with numbers.

We must say what the lemma assumes. It is a result of the beam model, not a solution of the three-dimensional problem. For a linear \(\Delta T\) the model is exact, as we saw above. For a nonlinear \(\Delta T\) the strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is not compatible, so plane sections and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) together cannot satisfy all the equations of elasticity. The additional stresses in the cross-section remain unestimated, because the beam model does not contain them (assumption 6 of chapter 5). For a wide plate the difference is marked and we can compute it: because the plate is wide, plane sections are also kept in the direction of the width \(z\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is linear in \(y\) and by symmetry \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). The first row of equation (3.31) gives \(\varepsilon = \sigma\,(1 - \nu)/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), so \(\sigma = E\,(\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T)/(1 - \nu)\), and the same steps 2 and 3 of the proof give \(\sigma = -E\,\alpha\,(\Delta T - \theta_{\mathrm{m}} - g\,y)/(1 - \nu)\), which is the stress of the beam divided by \(1 - \nu = 0{,}7\) (the derivation is not from the sources). For steel the stresses of example H, shown in the right part of figure 14.76, are increased for a wide plate by \(1/0{,}7 = 1{,}4286\).

Which of the two fields is a solution of the three-dimensional problem is told by theorem 14.40. Take again the quadratic variation \(\Delta T = a\,y^2\) with \(a = 0{,}05\ \text{K/mm}^2\) on the height \(h = 40\) mm, where \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = a\,h^2/12 = 6{,}667\) K and \(g = 0\) (equation (14.41), rectangular cross-section). We computed the right sides of equations (14.37) above: \(-0{,}420\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\) for \(xx\) and \(zz\) and \(-0{,}646\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\) for \(yy\). The beam field has only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\alpha\,(a\,y^2 - \theta_{\mathrm{m}})\), so \(\Theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The left side of equation \(xx\) is \(\partial^2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 = -2E\alpha\,a = -0{,}294\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\), which is not \(-0{,}420\), and the left side of equation \(zz\) is \(0\). The beam field does not satisfy the equations, it lacks exactly the factor \(1 - \nu = 0{,}7\). The plate field has \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\alpha\,(a\,y^2 - \theta_{\mathrm{m}})/(1 - \nu)\) and \(\Theta = 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The left side of equations \(xx\) and \(zz\) is \(-2E\alpha\,a/(1 - \nu) = -0{,}420\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\), and the left side of equation \(yy\) is \(\frac{1}{1 + \nu}\,\partial^2\Theta/\partial y^2 = -2\cdot 0{,}420/1{,}3 = -0{,}646\ \text{MPa/mm}^2\). The shear equations are \(0 = 0\), because the field depends only on \(y\). All six equations are satisfied (SymPy, residual zero), and so are the equilibrium equations, because the only derivative that is not zero, \(\partial/\partial y\), acts only on \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), which are zero. The strains of the plate field are therefore compatible. The top and bottom faces are stress-free, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} = 0\). On the side edges of the plate only the condition for the resultant holds, \(N = M = 0\), and not the condition at every point, so the field is the solution away from the edges (Saint-Venant's principle, section 11.11). Numbers: at the edge \(y = \pm 20\) mm the plate stress is \(-2{,}94\cdot(20 - 6{,}667)/0{,}7 = -56{,}0\) MPa, in the middle layer \(+2{,}94\cdot 6{,}667/0{,}7 = +28{,}0\) MPa. The difference is \(84\) MPa, as we predicted above from the second derivative. The beam field gives \(-39{,}2\) and \(+19{,}6\) MPa.

14.7.6 Example H: a suddenly heated surface

Take a beam with a rectangular cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm (width \(20\) mm, height \(h = 40\) mm) of the same steel, everything at the reference temperature. At the instant \(t = 0\) we raise the top edge by \(20\) K and keep it so, and we keep the bottom edge at the reference temperature, for example against a massive support. The sides and ends are thermally insulated, and the top and bottom edges have the same temperature over the whole face, so \(T\) does not depend on \(x\) and \(z\) and heat is conducted only along the height. Insulated sides are an idealization: in reality heat also escapes through the sides. The beam is free, so lemma 14.42 holds.

We measure the height from the bottom edge, \(y' = \eta\,h\) with \(0 \le \eta \le 1\), and time with the dimensionless number \(\mathrm{Fo} = \kappa\,t/h^2\) (Fourier number, Fourierjevo število), which bears Fourier's name like the law of section 14.1. In the series of theorem 14.26, written for a bar of length \(h\), the exponents are \(-\kappa\,n^2\pi^2 t/h^2 = -n^2\pi^2\,\mathrm{Fo}\), so \(\mathrm{Fo}\) is time in the scale in which the temperature evens out over the height. To the temperature profile of section 14.5 we add the steady state: \(20\,\eta\) is a solution of the heat equation with the prescribed values at the edges, so it is the steady state (lemma 14.19). The remainder \(w = T - 20\,\eta\) is zero at both edges and has the initial value \(-20\,\eta\), so by theorem 14.26 \(w = \sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}} \sin(n\pi\eta)\,e^{-n^2\pi^2\mathrm{Fo}}\) with \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\int_0^1 (-20\,\eta)\sin(n\pi\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta = 40\,(-1)^n/(n\pi)\) (integration by parts, corollary 3.56). Hence

\[\Delta T(\eta, \mathrm{Fo}) = 20\,\eta + \sum_{\mathrm{n}=1}^{\infty} \frac{40\,(-1)^n}{n\pi}\,\sin(n\pi\eta)\,e^{-n^2\pi^2\,\mathrm{Fo}} . \tag{14.43}\]

We checked the series against an independent computation, the implicit finite difference method: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}005\) they differ by at most \(0{,}006\) K and at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}05\) by \(0{,}0006\) K. The left part of figure 14.76 shows \(\Delta T\) over the height at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}005\): the lower \(60\) % or so of the height is still at zero, and just below the top edge the temperature rises quickly to \(20\) K. The dashed line is \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} + g\,y\), computed from equation (14.41). At half height it has the value \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 1{,}60\) K, and between the bottom and the top edge it rises by \(g\,h = 8{,}38\) K, from \(-2{,}59\) K to \(5{,}78\) K. This is the plane cross-section of figure 14.74, now for the real temperature profile.

The stress from lemma 14.42 is in the right part of the figure. The table shows what happens at the top and bottom edges and where the tension is largest (computed with NumPy, equations (14.43) and (14.42), \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K).

By the lemma the stress is minus \(E\alpha\) times the difference between \(\Delta T\) and the line: at the top \(\Delta T = 20\) K is above the line, which has \(5{,}78\) K there, the difference is \(14{,}22\) K, and \(-E\alpha\cdot 14{,}22 = -41{,}8\) MPa, the value at the top of the blue curve. All stresses disappear when the temperature settles. This is contrary to the naive expectation that the stress would be largest when the beam holds the most heat, that is in the steady state with \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 10\) K.

14.7.7 Why the model has no residual stresses

Example H ends in zero. Does this always hold? A body can heat unevenly and then equalize its temperature. Does the model have stresses after the equalization? The answer is given by the next corollary, and from it follows that the stresses that LOVE describes in cooled castings cannot arise in the elastic model.

Corollary 14.43 No residual stresses in the model (brez zaostalih napetosti)

Let the body of definition 14.38 have a material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), the traction \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\) prescribed on its whole boundary (\(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty), no body force, \(\vek b = \vek 0\), and let \(T\) equal a constant value over the whole body at some instant. Then the stresses at that instant are zero, whatever the temperature was before.

Proof
  1. At that instant \(\nabla T = \vek 0\) and \(\Delta T\) is constant, \(\Delta T = \Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\). In theorem 14.39 \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{E\alpha}{1 - 2\nu}\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\vek n\).
  2. The field \(\vek u = \alpha\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,(x, y, z)\) satisfies equation (14.35) (a) as in the test above and the boundary condition (14.36) (b) with \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\), so it is a solution with stress \(\ten\sigma = \vek 0\).
  3. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be any solution of the same problem at that instant. By theorem 14.39, \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) are solutions of the boundary value problem without temperature (definition 11.1) for the same loading, \(\vek b_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek 0\) in the interior and \(\vek t_{\mathrm{T}}\) on the boundary, and their stresses in that problem are \(\ten\sigma^{e}\) and \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e}\) from step 1 of the proof of the theorem.
  4. Since \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), by theorem 3.64 the strain energy density is positive, so by theorem 11.4, part 1, \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e} = \ten\sigma^{e}\).
  5. Since \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\) is the same for both solutions, by step 1 of the proof of theorem 14.39 also \(\tilde{\ten\sigma} = \tilde{\ten\sigma}^{e} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mat I = \ten\sigma\), which is zero by step 2. The stress at that instant is zero everywhere.

Source: The proof is not from the sources.

The meaning: the elastic model has no memory. There are two reasons. In definition 14.38 time is only a parameter, and by uniqueness as in steps 3 to 5 of the proof the stresses at an instant are determined by the temperature at that instant, not by its history. The corollary says this for a uniform temperature: if after the heating we cool the beam of example H to a uniform temperature, there is no stress in the model, however fast we heated it before. Example H itself ends at the line \(20\,\eta\), and there the stress is zero by lemma 14.42. A stress that remains in a cooled body must therefore have another cause, which the model does not contain.

What this cause is, another example shows: instead of \(20\) K we raise the top edge by \(100\) K. The model is linear in \(\Delta T\) (theorem 14.39), so the stress is five times larger: at the top \(-294{,}0\) MPa for \(\mathrm{Fo} \to 0\) (figure 14.77, orange curve). This is above the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa of the steel of chapter 3, and above it the model does not hold: Hooke's law is valid only below the yield stress. The stress reaches \(220\) MPa when the top stress of the \(20\) K case equals \(44\) MPa, which is at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) (from the same computed series as the table). On the horizontal axis of the figure is \(\mathrm{Fo}\), on the vertical one \(-\sigma\), which grows downward, so a larger compression is lower. Up to \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) the orange curve reaches beyond the dashed limit, and there the model does not hold. The blue curve for \(20\) K never reaches the limit, and for it the model holds to the end. What happens in the layer that has yielded is shown by figure 3.34 of chapter 3: after unloading the strain does not return to zero, a permanent strain remains. So for the \(100\) K step the model does not hold after \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0{,}0037\) either, when the compression along the orange curve is already smaller than the limit: the layer that has yielded keeps a permanent strain. Permanent strains in a thin layer at the edge that the surroundings cannot even out are again incompatible, like the quadratic temperature above, and so stresses remain even when the temperature is the same everywhere. LOVE (PDF p. 128) mentions this for cast iron: the outer parts cool more rapidly than the inner ones, and the unequal contractions that accompany the unequally rapid rates of cooling give rise to considerable initial stress in the iron when cold. The elastic model cannot give these stresses: by corollary 14.43 there are none in it, however fast the body cooled. Only a model that also knows yielding captures them.

The model therefore does not contain yielding, coupled thermoelasticity, radiation and the dependence of \(E\), \(\alpha\) and conductivity on temperature, and so it does not know residual stresses (section 14.12). Theorem 14.39 remains the basis also for the plane and cylindrical cases treated in section 14.8: we turn temperature into a force in the same way as here.

Let us state the notation. Chapter 5 uses \(\kappa\) for curvature, in this chapter \(\kappa\) is the thermal diffusivity, so the curvature is written \(v''\) here. The letter \(h\) in this section is the height of the beam from chapter 5 and not the film coefficient (section 14.3). The letter \(k\) is the thermal conductivity, not the shear factor. The letter \(T\) is temperature, not the string force or a stress, and \(c\) is the wave speed in a bar in this section and the specific heat elsewhere in the chapter.
