Chapter 16 Torsion

16.10 Connections

The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19.

Earlier chapters took three things as a model, because we did not yet have torsion: chapter 12 took the equation \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G J\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\) (equation (12.16)) with the number \(J\) as data (there \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the rotation of the section about the axis \(x\), in this section the index \(x\) means the derivative, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = \partial\varphi/\partial x\)), chapter 13 took \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) (equation (13.20)) for the round shaft, and chapter 9 took the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) of a shaft under a disc. This chapter derived all three for a shaft in uniform torsion (section 16.1), and for the vibrating round shaft we check equation (13.20) below. Now we put them on one shaft and test them on numbers: what happens to the vibration of a shaft when the circle is replaced by the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip of chapter 12. The question is concrete: by how much does the first natural frequency change, which part of the shaft model must we replace and which may we keep.

Take the shaft of section 13.3. It is fixed in a wall at \(x = 0\), has length \(L = 1\) m and a free end at \(x = L\), and is made of steel (\(G = 75{,}385\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\)). In the figure, in the first mode of vibration given by theorem 13.24 (b), each cross section is turned by \(\varphi(x) = \varphi_0 \sin(\pi x/2L)\), and as the curve below the shaft shows, the rotation grows from zero at the wall to its largest value at the free end. The line on a section is the rotation we see, so the rotation \(\varphi_0\) in the figure is greatly enlarged.

Chapter 13 found for the round shaft the speed \(c_{\mathrm{T}} = \sqrt{G/\rho} = 3108{,}8\) m/s and the frequency \(f_1 = c_{\mathrm{T}}/(4L) = 777{,}2\) Hz (theorem 13.24). What changes for the strip? The proof of theorem 13.22 has two steps that we must repeat, and each has its reason. The first is the angular momentum of a piece of the shaft. The section still turns about the axis as a rigid body (besides that it warps, see below). So, as in step 1 of the proof, a point of the section at distance \(r\) from the axis has the tangential velocity \(r\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) with lever arm \(r\) about the axis, and the sum over the section gives per unit length the angular momentum \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) with the same polar second moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) of the \(20 \times 40\) mm section. Warping does not change this: the axial displacement of warping points along the axis, so it has no lever arm about the axis. Angular momentum therefore depends only on how the mass is spread around the axis.

The second is the moment with which the neighbouring part of the shaft acts on the piece. That is no longer \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\). By theorem 16.18 it is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G J\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) with the torsion constant \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) of theorem 16.38, and \(J = 0{,}549\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\) by lemma 16.20. Why smaller? By point 2 of the lemma \(J\) is the integral of the squares of the shear strains per unit twist. For a section that stayed plane this integral would be \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\). The strip, however, warps, and by point 1 of the lemma the integral is therefore smaller by \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), so the same twist per unit length needs a smaller moment. The law of angular momentum for a piece \([a, b]\) gives, with the same steps 3 to 6 as in theorem 13.22 (in step 4 with \(G J\) in place of \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\), so in step 5 \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) does not cancel),

\[\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}} = G J\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{G J}{\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}}} = c_{\mathrm{T}}\sqrt{\frac{J}{I_{\mathrm{p}}}} = 0{,}7408\,c_{\mathrm{T}} . \tag{16.71}\]

Hence \(c = 2303{,}1\) m/s and \(f_1 = c/(4L) = 575{,}8\) Hz. In the figure the arrow from the circle to the strip shows this: the frequency is multiplied by \(\sqrt{J/I_{\mathrm{p}}}\). Below each section stand its \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\), its \(J\) and its frequency. The circle is the fixed term of the comparison: for it \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\), because a circle does not warp (corollary 16.21).

What would we get if for the strip we stayed with the model of chapter 13, that is replaced \(J\) by \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\)? This is the near miss, which fails the test. Equation (16.71) would have \(c = c_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(f_1 = 777{,}2\) Hz, that is \(201{,}4\) Hz or \(35{,}0\) % too much. The reason is the same as for the frame in chapter 12, where \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) in place of \(J\) gave a displacement from the twisting of the column of \(4{,}97\) mm instead of \(9{,}06\) mm (\(45\) % too little) and a total displacement of point \(C\) of \(11{,}35\) mm instead of \(15{,}44\) mm (\(26\) % too little): the strip is softer in torsion than \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) says, because the section warps, while the mass stays. In vibration \(\omega^2\) is proportional to the ratio of stiffness to inertia, as for a mass on a spring (chapter 9). Let us draw both as bars below the sections. The stiffness \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) that a plane section would give we draw grey dashed, the true stiffness \(G J\) orange. It is \(45\) % shorter, the stiffness falls to \(0{,}549\) of its former value. The inertia \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the same in both models, so the orange bar fills the whole dashed one. The frequency falls less, to \(\sqrt{0{,}549} = 0{,}741\) of its former value, because \(\omega\) is the square root of the ratio.

Equation (16.71) neglects two things and both must be named. First: theorem 16.18 gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G J\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) for a twist per unit length that is constant along the shaft (uniform torsion, enakomerna torzija, definition 16.1). In vibration \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) varies with \(x\), in the first mode it falls from its largest value at the wall to zero at the free end, where the rotation curve is horizontal, so for the strip the model is an approximation. We estimate its size below, with the same computation as for the second neglect. For the round shaft there is no approximation, which we show in six steps.

  1. Each section of the round shaft turns as a rigid body by \(\varphi(x, t)\), so the displacement field is \(\vek u = \varphi(x, t)\,(0, -z, y)\): the point \((y, z)\) of the section moves perpendicular to its radius.
  2. The field has \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\) and \(\nabla^2\vek u = \varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(0, -z, y)\), because \(-z\varphi\) and \(y\varphi\) are linear in \(y\) and \(z\) and their second derivatives in \(y\) and \(z\) are zero.
  3. By theorem 13.33 without body force, where the Lamé constants are \(\lambda\) and \(G\) (the second is the shear modulus), this is a motion of the body exactly when \(G\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho\,\varphi_{\mathrm{tt}}\), which is equation (13.21).
  4. The only nonzero stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G z\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G y\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\). At a point of the circumference the shear vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) therefore points along the circumference, perpendicular to the radius, as in uniform torsion.
  5. The lateral surface is free. Its normal \(\vek n = (0, y, z)/R\) points along the radius, perpendicular to the shear vector on the circumference, so the traction is \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = G\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\,(-z y + y z)/R = 0\), and \(t_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) are zero.
  6. The ends. At the wall \(\varphi(0, t) = 0\), so \(\vek u = 0\) and the section is indeed fixed. At the free end the end face has the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the traction \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) = G\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,(0, -z, y) = 0\), because by theorem 13.22 \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\). The field therefore satisfies the equations of the body and all boundary conditions.

The model of chapter 13 is therefore exact for the circle also in vibration. For the strip the same computation fails, because its section warps. The warping \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\psi\) with \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\) gives in the direction \(x\) the left side \((\lambda + 2G)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xxx}}\,\psi\), while equation (16.71) gives the right side \(\rho\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xtt}}\,\psi = G\,(J/I_{\mathrm{p}})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xxx}}\,\psi\), and since for steel (\(\nu = 0{,}3\)) \(\lambda + 2G = 3{,}5\,G\) while \(G J/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is only \(0{,}549\,G\), they do not agree.

Second: the warping of the section moves during vibration. With the warping function (funkcija zvitja) \(\psi\) (definition 16.15) the axial displacement is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\,\psi\), so the axial displacements of warping change in time together with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\). Their velocity has kinetic energy per unit length \(\tfrac12\rho\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\int \psi^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), which the equation did not have. How large is it? Since \(\psi\) for the strip is the series of section 16.5, we computed the integral: \(\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 1{,}30\cdot10^{6}\) mm\(^6\) and \(\frac{1}{I_{\mathrm{p}}}\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 9{,}75\) mm\(^2\) (num-10.out, as a check the same \(\psi\) gave the corner displacement \(0{,}832\) \(\mu\)m at \(50\) N m, as section 16.5 states). With the first mode \(\varphi = \varphi_0\sin(\pi x/2L)\) the ratio of the kinetic energies of warping and of rotation is

\[r = \frac{\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}A}{I_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\Big(\frac{\pi}{2L}\Big)^2 = 9{,}75\ \mathrm{mm}^2\cdot\Big(\frac{\pi}{2000\ \mathrm{mm}}\Big)^2 = 2{,}4\cdot10^{-5} ,\]

because by theorem 4.9 the kinetic energy splits into an integral over the section and an integral over \(x\), and the integrals over \(x\) are \(\int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = (\pi/2L)^2\,\varphi_0^2 L/2\) and \(\int_0^L \varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \varphi_0^2 L/2\). The Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik) of the shaft (definition 13.79, with numerator \(\int_0^L G J\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and denominator \(\int_0^L \rho I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)) with the same mode has, with the added energy, a denominator larger by the factor \(1 + r\). Hence \(\omega^2\) is lowered by the factor \(1/(1 + r)\) and the frequency by about \(r/2 = 0{,}0012\) %. For a shaft of length \(L = 1\) m the neglect of this energy is therefore justified. The ratio \(r\) grows with \(1/L^2\) and reaches \(0{,}38\) % at \(L = 80\) mm, where the frequency is lower by about \(0{,}19\) %, but there the length is only twice the longer side of the section (\(40\) mm), so uniform torsion is a poor model near the ends (section 11.11). The neglect therefore depends on the shape of the section and on the length, it does not hold for every shaft.

We estimate the first neglect with the same computation. Where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) varies along the shaft, the warping also varies from section to section, so the fibres of the shaft stretch or shorten by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\psi\). The other strains are the same as in uniform torsion, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), so by equation (3.71) the energy density gains the term \(\tfrac12(\lambda + 2G)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\psi^2\), which the numerator of the Rayleigh quotient does not have. With the same mode the ratio of this energy to the energy of twisting is

\[s = \frac{\lambda + 2G}{G}\,\frac{\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}A}{J}\,\Big(\frac{\pi}{2L}\Big)^2 = 3{,}5\cdot\frac{1{,}30\cdot10^{6}\ \mathrm{mm}^6}{73\,178\ \mathrm{mm}^4}\cdot\Big(\frac{\pi}{2000\ \mathrm{mm}}\Big)^2 = 1{,}5\cdot10^{-4} ,\]

because \(\int_0^L \varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = (\pi/2L)^4\,\varphi_0^2 L/2\). The factor \(\lambda + 2G\) comes from the section staying rigid in its own plane (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)). If the fibres were allowed to contract sideways as in a bar under tension, the factor would be \(E = 2{,}6\,G\) instead of \(3{,}5\,G\) and \(s = 1{,}1\cdot10^{-4}\), so the estimate does not change much. The numerator grows by the factor \(1 + s\), so the frequency rises by about \(s/2 = 0{,}008\) %, and the two neglects together raise it by \((s - r)/2 = 0{,}006\) %. This computation assumes that the section warps freely also at the wall. Restrained warping, which the wall may impose, it does not cover (list below). At \(L = 80\) mm we would have \(s = 2{,}4\) % and the two neglects together would raise the frequency by about \(1\) %, one more reason why so short a shaft does not belong in this model.

Figure 16.94 shows both ratios as functions of the shaft length. On the axes the length \(L\) is horizontal and the energy ratio vertical, both on logarithmic scales, so that numbers from \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-1}\) fit in one picture. The blue line is \(r\), the kinetic energy of warping to the kinetic energy of rotation, the orange one is \(s\), the energy of stretching the fibres to the energy of twisting. The lines are parallel because both ratios are proportional to \((\pi/2L)^2\): a ten times longer shaft has a hundred times smaller ratios. For our shaft with \(L = 1\) m we have \(r = 2{,}4\cdot10^{-5}\) and \(s = 1{,}5\cdot10^{-4}\), and they change the frequency by \(r/2\) and \(s/2\), both less than one ten-thousandth. At \(L = 80\) mm they are \(0{,}38\) % and \(2{,}4\) %, and there the grey band begins, of shafts shorter than two longer sides of the section, where uniform torsion is not a good model near the ends.

Example 16.70 Disc on the strip instead of the round shaft (disk na traku namesto na okrogli gredi)

A shaft fixed at \(x = 0\) carries at \(x = L = 1\) m a disc with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}05\) kg m\(^2\), as in example 13.29, but its section is \(20 \times 40\) mm. As in the proof of theorem 13.27 the boundary condition at the disc, which the shaft turns with a moment, is \(G J\,U'(L) = J_{\mathrm{d}}\,\omega^2\,U(L)\), so \(U'(L) = \ell_{\mathrm{d}}\,(\omega/c)^2\,U(L)\) with the length \(\ell_{\mathrm{d}} = J_{\mathrm{d}}/(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}})\) of step 2 of the proof and with \(c\) from equation (16.71). Equation (13.26) therefore holds unchanged with \(\mu = \rho I_{\mathrm{p}} L/J_{\mathrm{d}}\) and \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = c\,s_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\pi L)\), where now \(c = 2303{,}1\) m/s.

  1. The stiffness of the shaft is \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = G J/L = 5516{,}5\) N m/rad (by lemma 13.23 with \(J\) in place of \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\)), \(3{,}43\) times less than \(18\,946{,}3\) N m/rad for the circle. The factor \(18\,946{,}3/5516{,}5 = 3{,}434\) has two parts. The strip has a smaller polar moment, \(133\,333\) mm\(^4\) against \(251\,327\) mm\(^4\) for the circle, which gives \(1{,}885\), and it warps, which gives another \(1/0{,}549 = 1{,}822\). Together \(1{,}885\cdot1{,}822 = 3{,}434\).
  2. The one degree of freedom model gives \(\omega_{\mathrm{1PS}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{t}}/J_{\mathrm{d}}} = 332{,}16\) 1/s, so \(f = 52{,}87\) Hz (for the circle \(97{,}97\) Hz).
  3. The inertia of the strip is \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}} L = 7800\cdot1{,}3333\cdot10^{-7}\cdot 1 = 1{,}040\cdot10^{-3}\) kg m\(^2\), hence \(\mu = 0{,}02080\). The first root of \(s\tan s = \mu\) is \(s_1 = 0{,}14372\) (bisection in num-10.py), so \(f_1 = c\,s_1/(2\pi L) = 52{,}68\) Hz.
  4. By corollary 13.28 (with \(c\) in place of \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\)) the one degree of freedom model lies above the exact value: \(52{,}87/52{,}68 - 1 = 0{,}35\) %. For the circle the error was \(0{,}65\) %, because \(\mu\) was larger (\(0{,}0392\)). A smaller moment of inertia of the shaft relative to the disc, that is a smaller \(\mu\), means a better one degree of freedom model.

Source: The example and its numbers are ours (num-10.out). The value of \(J\) is from theorem 16.38, the equations are from chapter 13.

  • Space frame (AK, MK, chapter 12): equation (12.16), which the element took as an assumption, is proved by theorem 16.18. The number \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) for the strip, which the element took from a source, is the sum of the series of theorem 16.38. The ratio \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}549\) is the reason why the frame of chapter 12 with \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) in place of \(J\) underestimates the displacement from twisting by \(45\) % and the total displacement of point \(C\) by \(26\) %. The assumption that the centroid is the shear centre (strižno središče) holds for the strip by lemma 16.48, because it has two axes of symmetry. For a channel it is not true: the shear centre lies \(12{,}5\) mm from the centroid (section 16.7), and a force of \(500\) N through the centroid twists the channel in uniform torsion, far from a clamp, by \(22{,}3\) \(^\circ\)/m (corollary 16.49), so the element must place such sections on an axis through the shear centre or add an offset.
  • Vibration of shafts and the disc (VD, DSK, chapters 9 and 13): the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}/L\) of lemma 13.23 is now proved by theorem 16.3 and corollary 16.4. Example 16.70 shows what changes for a non-circular section: \(J\) in place of \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) in the stiffness, \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) stays in the inertia. Limit (figure 16.93): equation (16.71) is an approximation for a non-circular section. The kinetic energy of warping is estimated by \(r\), nonuniform torsion with free warping by \(s\), warping restrained at the wall we do not estimate. For the circle the equation is exact.
  • Hollow shafts and rotors (DSK, chapters 24 and 23): for a hollow round shaft \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi(R^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4)/2\) (corollary 16.54). A \(40/32\) mm tube has \(J = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\) and at equal mass is \(4{,}56\) times stiffer than a solid shaft of diameter \(24\) mm (\(J = 32\,572\) mm\(^4\)). The reason is the lever arm. Since \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int r^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(J\) is the area times the mean square distance from the axis, and this is \((R^2 + R_{\mathrm{i}}^2)/2 = 328\) mm\(^2\) for the tube and \(R^2/2 = 72\) mm\(^2\) for the solid shaft (grey circles). The ratio \(328/72 = 4{,}56\) is exactly the ratio of the stiffnesses. In the crankshaft of an engine the journal piece is round and \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}/\ell\) holds for it. For crank webs and cheeks, which are not round and are also loaded in bending, this chapter has no result and we state them as not covered.
  • Restrained warping (zadržan zvitek) and nonuniform torsion (AK, chapter 12): everything we derived assumes that the section can warp freely at the end. When a support or a joint holds it, the twist per unit length varies along the shaft and axial stresses appear in the section as well. COOK (PDF p. 42 to 43) describes an I beam five times as long as it is deep, twisted by a torque \(T\) at end \(B\). If end \(A\) is supported only against rotation, the section there warps freely. If a wall holds it plane, the rotation at end \(B\) is reduced by about half, and axial normal stresses much larger than the torsional shear stress appear in the section. The element of chapter 12 assumes free warping (section 12.5.1) and so has no extra degrees of freedom. Restrained warping needs them (the rate of twist at a node, a \(14 \times 14\) matrix), and programs often do not offer them (COOK, PDF p. 43). ONATE2 (section 4.10, PDF p. 227) states for thin open sections that warping matters precisely in nonuniform and in restrained torsion, where the twist per unit length varies along the axis, and (PDF p. 231) that restrained warping introduces axial stresses. The proof of this is not in the book: the derivation is in the sources named.
  • Multi-cell sections (več celic) and variable diameter (MK): in a section with several cells each cell has its own constant shear flow, the moment gives only one equation for them, and the others come from the condition that all cells have the same twist per unit length (ONATE2 section 4.3.5.1, PDF p. 206), and for a round shaft of variable diameter the field \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), \(u_\theta = u_\theta(r, z)\) is again a solution of all the field equations and the stress function is constant on the boundary (SADD section 9.7, PDF p. 270 to 271). We derive neither.
  • Heat conduction and membranes (MK, AK, chapters 14, 18 and 22): the equation \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) with zero on the boundary (theorem 16.24) is the equation of steady heat conduction with a uniform source and zero temperature on the boundary, so the uniqueness of the solution and the element matrix of chapter 14 hold for it, and section 16.9 uses them for torsion. The reason is the same operator, not the same phenomenon. The membrane of section 16.4, a thin film without bending stiffness that carries a pressure \(p\) with tension \(N\) per unit length (corollary 16.34), has the same equation. The word membrane in shells (chapter 22) means something else, a shell that carries the load only by forces in the plane of its middle surface, without bending, and plates (chapter 18) have a bending stiffness that the film does not.
  • Limits of the chapter: the existence of the functions \(\psi\) and \(\Phi\) for a general section and Saint-Venant's principle at the ends are stated from sources. Open sections made of several strips (junctions), multi-cell sections, restrained warping and nonuniform torsion, variable diameter and plastic torsion are named or left out. The example of this section neglects the kinetic energy of warping and nonuniform torsion, which \(r = 2{,}4\cdot10^{-5}\) and \(s = 1{,}5\cdot10^{-4}\) justify at \(L = 1\) m (both grow with \(1/L^2\), at \(L = 80\) mm together they change the frequency by \(1\) %), and does not estimate warping restrained at the wall.
