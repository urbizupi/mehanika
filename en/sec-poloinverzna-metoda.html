Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.4 The inverse and semi-inverse methods (Inverzna in poloinverzna metoda)

In chapter 1 we took the displacement field of the hanging bar from the books (equation (1.19)) and left its derivation owing. Now we have the equations for displacements (section 11.2), the equations for stresses (section 11.3) and the uniqueness theorem (theorem 11.4). What we do not have is a recipe for finding a field that satisfies the equations and the boundary conditions in the first place. How did the people who wrote it into the books find it?

The question is old. For an elastic body one direction of the computation is simple: if we know the displacements of the points, we get the stresses by differentiation. The converse is not true. We show both directions in figure 11.24.

In each row on the left is the problem: a body that we hold on the hatched part of the surface, while on the other part given forces \(\bar{\vek t}\) pull it, and in the interior the body force \(\vek b\) acts on it. On the right is the field: a piece of material before loading, drawn grey dashed, and after it, orange, with displacements \(\vek u\) and stresses \(\sigma\). The arrow between them tells in which direction we go. From the field to the problem we go by differentiation: from the displacements we get the strains, from them by Hooke's law the stresses, and from the stresses on the surface the forces, every step by a rule that always works. From the problem to the field we go by integration, and there is no general rule here. Why not? In differentiation we already know the function and only compute its slope at each point separately. In integration we know only the relation between the derivatives in the interior and at the same time the conditions on the whole surface, and the field that satisfies both at once we must first find.

From history

Saint-Venant read a long memoir on the torsion of prisms to the Academy on 13 June 1853, and it was printed in 1855 (TP2A, PDF p. 25). Todhunter and Pearson summarize its introductory chapter as follows: if we know the displacements of the points of an elastic body, we get the stresses by differentiation, but the task in the opposite direction, to find the displacements from the stresses, has not been solved in general, because we do not know how to integrate the differential equations that appear (TP2A, PDF p. 26). Saint-Venant therefore proposes a mixed method (méthode mixte ou semi-inverse, the bottom row of figure @fig-pim-metode): we prescribe a part of the displacements and a part of the stresses, and determine the remaining displacements and stresses by an exact analysis (TP2A, PDF p. 26). The committee of the Academy, consisting of Cauchy, Poncelet, Piobert and Lamé, praised the memoir very highly in a report written by Lamé (Comptes rendus, 26 December 1853). Among its reasons it states that by using the mixed method the memoir shows engineers who want to rely on the theory how to use all the procedures of mathematical analysis that are known (TP2A, PDF pp. 25-26). The memoir deals mainly with the torsion of prisms with a great variety of cross-sections (TP2A, PDF p. 26).

We were looking for a way to find the displacement field of the hanging bar. Saint-Venant's idea is to prescribe a part of the field and compute the rest, and this is how we will find the field of the hanging bar as well. Let us see how today's books write this idea down, and separate it from other ways.

The first way is the most natural. We integrate the field equations, that is the relation of strains to displacements (chapter 1), Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) and the equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31), and satisfy the boundary conditions exactly. This succeeds only for bodies of simple shape, because considerable mathematical difficulties appear (SADD, PDF p. 115). The other two ways take a detour and use the fact that the solution is unique.

Definition 11.11 Direct, inverse and semi-inverse method (neposredna, inverzna in poloinverzna metoda)

Let a boundary value problem of elasticity be given (definition 11.1), in which we look for the displacement field \(\vek u\) or the stress field \(\ten\sigma\) from the field equations, that is from the relation of strains to displacements, Hooke's law and the equilibrium equations. The procedures for finding a solution differ in what is known at the start.

  1. In the direct method (neposredna metoda) we integrate the field equations and satisfy the boundary conditions exactly.
  2. In the inverse method (inverzna metoda) we choose a displacement or stress field that satisfies the field equations, and read from it for which body, which body forces and which boundary conditions it is the solution.
  3. In the semi-inverse method (poloinverzna metoda) we prescribe a part of the field, for example some components or the form of the dependence on the coordinates, and determine the rest from the field equations and the boundary conditions.

Source: SADD §5.7.1, §5.7.2, §5.7.3 (PDF pp. 115-117). For the semi-inverse method SADD adds that the choice of the prescribed part is often guided by an approximate strength of materials theory, and that the method is strengthened by Saint-Venant's principle, which replaces a complicated boundary condition by a simpler, statically equivalent one (PDF p. 117).

When is a guessed field really a solution of our problem? Definition 11.1 asks for only three things: the field is continuously differentiable and satisfies the conditions on displacements, its stresses are continuously differentiable and satisfy the equilibrium equations, and on the part of the surface where forces are prescribed they satisfy Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26). A field that satisfies all three is a solution by definition, no matter how we arrived at it. Theorem 11.4 says even more: there is no other solution, except in a problem with surface forces only, where two solutions may differ by a small rigid displacement (lemma 4.72). Without uniqueness the guessed field would be only one of the possible solutions. With it, it is the solution. This is why we may guess a field, provided we then check it exactly. We know the same inference from an equation with numbers. We solve the equation \(x^3 + x = 10\) by guessing: we try \(x = 2\) and check, \(8 + 2 = 10\). There is no other solution, because the graph keeps rising and meets the height \(10\) only once. In a boundary value problem the uniqueness theorem plays the role of the rising.

We repeat the setting, because we must know it in detail. Let us draw it in figure 11.25.

A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m has a round cross-section of diameter \(d = 10\) mm. The steel has the modulus of elasticity \(E = 196\) GPa, Poisson's ratio \(\nu = 0{,}30\) and the density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) (chapter 3). Axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre \(O\) of the top face, axes \(y\) and \(z\) are horizontal. The body force is the weight, \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the gravitational acceleration \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\), so \(\rho g = 76\,518\) N/m\(^3\). The bar is held in an idealized way, as in section 1.7.1 (figure 1.103, left): a uniformly distributed pulling force upward acts on the top face, equal to \(\rho g L\) per unit area, and the mantle and the bottom face are free. The point \(O\) is the only one we constrain, and only so that the bar as a whole does not fly away. A real ceiling holds differently, and what that changes we say at the end of the section.

We do not choose this force on the top face arbitrarily. The bar is in equilibrium only if the forces on its surface balance the weight (lemma 11.2). The weight is \(\rho g A L = 60{,}10\) N, where \(A = \pi d^2/4 = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) is the area of the cross-section. We draw it as one arrow from the middle of the bar, where the centre of gravity is. The force on the top face is \(\rho g L \cdot A = 0{,}7652\ \text{MPa} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 60{,}10\) N upward. The sum is zero.

How do we guess the stress? From chapter 2 we know that the cross-section at depth \(x\) carries the piece of the bar below it. We remove the upper part of the bar in our mind (grey dashed) and replace it by the forces with which it pulled the piece. The piece is at rest, so these forces carry its weight \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\). If the cross-section carries it uniformly over its whole area, the stress on it is perpendicular and equals the weight divided by the area, that is \(\rho g\,(L - x)\).

Why uniformly and why only perpendicular? We cut the piece in our mind into equal vertical columns with the cross-section \(\Delta A\). Each is \(L - x\) long and weighs \(\rho g\,\Delta A\,(L - x)\), so each pulls its part of the cross-section with the same force per unit area, \(\rho g\,(L - x)\). Neighbouring columns are under the same stress and stretch equally, so they do not slide along each other and do not pull each other, there are no shear stresses. Nothing presses on the mantle, so there are no normal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) from the side either. For our idealized load this holds over the whole bar. For a bar welded to a ceiling, the weld would hold the upper ends of the columns from the side as well, and near the ceiling other components would appear (end of the section). The inference with the columns is an idea, not a proof. For the semi-inverse method we write it as a prescription of form: no other component of stress exists, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) depends only on \(x\). The size of the function will be given by the field equations and the boundary conditions, not by guessing. The steps are in figure 11.26.

Figure 11.27 shows where this leads: a stress that falls along a straight line from the top to zero at the bottom, and the displacements computed from it.

On the left is the bar with the axis \(x\), which points down from the point \(O\). The arrows on the top face are the force that pulls the bar upward. Beside the bar the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is drawn as the horizontal distance from the grey dashed line: at the top it is \(\rho g L\), at the lower end zero. On the right is the grid before loading, drawn grey dashed, and the same grid after it, orange. The top edge narrows the most. The lower edge moves down the most, but does not narrow, because the stress there is zero. The lower blue distance measures by how much the lower edge moves, and the upper one by how much the point on the axis at half the length moves. We compute both numbers after the proof. The point \(O\) stays in place, and the arrows are the same force as on the left.

Theorem 11.12 Exact solution for the hanging bar (natančna rešitev za visečo palico)

Let the body be a prism of length \(L\) with a round cross-section, whose axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre \(O\) of the top face \(x = 0\), and whose bottom face is \(x = L\). Let the material of density \(\rho\) be ideal elastic (definition 3.58), isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic with the modulus of elasticity \(E > 0\) and Poisson's ratio \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), so that the strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero (theorem 3.64, item 3). The body force is \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). On the top face the stress vector \(-\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is prescribed, the mantle and the bottom face are free. There are no supports, so this is a problem with surface forces only (definition 11.1). Let \(a = \rho g/(2E)\). Then:

  1. the displacement field
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - \nu a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -2\nu a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -2\nu a\,(L - x)\,z \end{aligned} \tag{11.11}\]
    is a solution of this problem, and its stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) and all other components zero,
  2. every solution of this problem has the same stresses and strains and differs from this field by a small rigid displacement, and the field (11.11) is the only solution in which the displacement of the point \(O\) is zero and the small rotation tensor at the point \(O\) is zero (definition 1.122).

For \(\nu = 0{,}30\) this is the field (1.19) of chapter 1, since \(2\nu a = 0{,}6\,a\) and \(\nu a = 0{,}3\,a\).

Proof

We must show that the guessed stress and the displacement field computed from it satisfy the three requirements of definition 11.1, and then that there is no other solution. The requirement on displacements on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) drops out, because \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty.

1. Stress.

  1. We prescribe the form of the stress: only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is different from zero and it depends only on \(x\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = S(x)\), and the other five components are zero. This is a guess, which the next steps will check. CM justifies it by saying that the body can deform freely, so other stresses do not develop (CM, PDF p. 305). This is the inference with the columns and the free mantle before figure 11.26.
  2. The equilibrium equations (theorem 2.31) with the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} = b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) become for this form \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \rho g = 0\) and \(0 = 0\) for \(y\) and \(z\), because all other derivatives of the stress components are zero. Hence \(S'(x) = -\rho g\) and \(S(x) = -\rho g\,x + c\) with a constant \(c\).
  3. On the bottom face the outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) gives \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = S(L)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which must be zero, since the face is free. Hence \(S(L) = 0\), \(c = \rho g L\) and
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x) .\]
    This is the stress we guessed from the weight below the cross-section. Equilibrium and the condition at the bottom confirmed the guess, because the \(x\)-component of the force on the piece of the bar below a cross-section, \(A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g A\,(L - x)\), equals its weight.
  4. On the mantle the normal is \(\vek n = (0, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\), perpendicular to the axis, so \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0) = \vek 0\). The mantle is free, the condition holds.
  5. On the top face the normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(0)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is the prescribed stress vector. The conditions on the whole surface are satisfied.

2. Strains and a check that the stress suits a field.

  1. Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, equation (3.27)) gives the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = 2a\,(L - x)\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = -2\nu a\,(L - x)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), because the shear stresses are zero. In figure 11.27 these are the grid lines, stretched more at the top than at the bottom, and the top edge, which narrows the most.
  2. The strains depend only on \(x\) and are linear in \(x\), so all their second derivatives are zero. Every term of the compatibility equations (1.76) is a second derivative of a strain, so the equations hold as \(0 = 0\). The same holds in the stress form: the stresses are linear in \(x\), so \(\nabla^2\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\partial^2(\operatorname{tr}\ten\sigma)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\) are zero, the body force is constant, so the right-hand sides of the Beltrami-Michell equations are also zero (theorem 11.9). The guessed stress could therefore be the field of an elastic body. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = k y^2\) the same check would fail (section 11.3).

3. Displacements.

  1. We find the displacements with the given strains. We integrate the normal strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z\) with respect to \(y\) and \(z\). The integration constants are functions of the remaining variables, we choose the simplest, zero, and get \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -2\nu a\,(L - x)\,y\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = -2\nu a\,(L - x)\,z\).
  2. The condition \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) requires \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = -2\nu a\,y\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) requires \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = -2\nu a\,z\). The integral of both is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -\nu a\,(y^2 + z^2) + h(x)\) with a function \(h\) of \(x\) only. The condition \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = h'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\) gives \(h(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), with the constant zero. This is the field (11.11). The function \(h\) is the displacement of the points of the axis downward, and in figure 11.27 the blue distances measure it. The term \(-\nu a\,(y^2 + z^2)\) says that a point farther from the axis moves down a little less than a point on the axis at the same depth. The cross-section therefore does not stay flat but bends slightly, like the orange arc at the top of figure @fig-drzanje-palice.
  3. We check that this field really has the strains of step 6. The field is a polynomial, so it is continuously differentiable on the whole open set that contains the bar. The derivatives are \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 2a\,(L - x)\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = -2\nu a\,(L - x)\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = -2\nu a\,y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = -2\nu a\,z = -\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y = 0\). The shear strains are therefore zero, the normal ones are those of step 6.
  4. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, equation (3.28), which holds for \(\nu \ne -1\), \(\nu \ne \tfrac12\)) the stresses of the field are uniquely determined by its strains. Since the field has the strains of step 6, which we computed from the guessed stress, its stresses are exactly the guessed stress. This is a polynomial, so it is continuously differentiable, as item 2 of the definition requires. By steps 3 to 5 it satisfies the equilibrium equations and the boundary conditions. The field (11.11) is therefore a solution. This is item 1.

4. Rigid displacement and uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be any solution of the same problem. By theorem 11.4, item 2, it has the same stresses and strains, and by the theorem on fields with equal strains (corollary 1.141) \(\tilde{\vek u}(P) = \vek u(P) + \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) with a constant vector \(\vek a\), a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) and the position vector \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) of the point \(P\) from \(O\). This is a small rigid displacement, the translation \(\vek a\) and the rotation \(\mat W\), as for the ring in figure 11.10. The interior of the prism is a connected open set, as this corollary requires.
  2. The field \(\vek u\) of (11.11) has displacement zero at the point \(O = (0, 0, 0)\), because all terms are zero, and a rotation tensor with the elements \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y - \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = -2\nu a\,y\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = -2\nu a\,z\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), which is zero at the point \(O\).
  3. If \(\tilde{\vek u}\) also has displacement zero at \(O\), then \(\vek a = \vek 0\). The gradient of the field \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\) is \(\mat W\), an antisymmetric matrix, so its rotation tensor is \(\tfrac12(\mat W - \mat W^\T) = \mat W\) (definition 1.122). The rotation tensor of the field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) at \(O\) is therefore the sum of zero and \(\mat W\). If this is also zero, then \(\mat W = \mat 0\) and \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\). This is item 2.

Source: SADD Example 5.1, (5.7.1)-(5.7.4) (PDF p. 115) and CM Example 7.3.2, (7.3.16)-(7.3.20) (PDF pp. 305-306). Both assume the form of the stress, equal to step 1, and compute the displacements from it. CM explicitly calls this example semi-inverse, SADD lists it under the direct method. The check of compatibility before integration is in CM only as one sentence, that the stress compatibility equations are satisfied (PDF p. 305). Both books remove the rigid displacement with conditions at one point, SADD with zero displacement and rotation at the point A (PDF p. 115), CM with the constants \(c_1, \dots, c_6\) (PDF p. 306). The proof of uniqueness in the fourth part is ours, it is not in the books.

The proof showed that the field (11.11) is a solution, and the only one once the rigid displacement is removed. The idea was in the first step, to guess the form of the stress, and the field equations did the rest. Step 7 separates the semi-inverse method from blind guessing: before we integrated anything, we checked that the guessed stress does not contradict the compatibility equations. If it did, we would return to the first step with a new form.

SADD puts the same example among the direct methods and CM among the semi-inverse ones. We wrote it as a semi-inverse method, because we prescribe a part of the field, the form of the stress, and compute the rest. The border between them is not sharp.

Now we can explain the constants in CM. If in the integration in steps 8 and 9 we had kept all the integration constants, we would have got the field (11.11) and in addition six constants, which are the components of the vector \(\vek a\) and the matrix \(\mat W\) of step 12. CM writes them \(c_1, \dots, c_6\) in (7.3.19) (PDF p. 306). CM measures \(z\) upward from the lower end, so the top end is at \(z = L\), and the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \gamma z\) with \(\gamma = \rho g\) (CM, PDF p. 305). The condition that the point \(O\) does not move is for the component along the bar \(\gamma L^2/(2E) + c_6 = 0\), so \(c_6 = -\gamma L^2/(2E)\) (PDF p. 306). In our axes, where \(x = L - z\), this constant ensures that \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(0, 0, 0) = 0\), which in our case is satisfied by the choice \(h(0) = 0\) in step 9. CM then writes the final field (7.3.20) without it, which is a misprint of the book (PDF p. 306), since at \(z = L\) it would give the displacement \(\gamma L^2/(2E)\) and not zero. SADD (5.7.4) (PDF p. 115) has the term \(-l^2\) and is correct. The condition that the material at \(O\) does not rotate CM adds separately, and with it removes the rotations as well (PDF p. 306). In our case this is step 13.

How large are the numbers? The stress is largest at the top, \(\rho g L = 0{,}7652\) MPa, which is only \(0{,}35\,\%\) of the yield stress of steel, 220 MPa, so the assumption of linear elasticity is justified for this load. The strain along the bar at the top is \(2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and the strains across the bar are \(-2\nu a L = -1{,}171 \cdot 10^{-6}\). Small strain is therefore justified too: the terms that the linear theory neglects are in relation to the retained ones of the order of the derivatives of the displacements, here less than \(4 \cdot 10^{-6}\). With \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) a point on the axis at depth \(x = 5\) m, where the stress is \(0{,}3826\) MPa, moves down by \(a\,(2Lx - x^2) = 14{,}64\ \mu\text{m}\), and the lower end by \(aL^2 = 19{,}52\ \mu\text{m}\). The point at half the length moves by three quarters of the displacement of the end, because \(a\,(2L \cdot L/2 - L^2/4) = \tfrac34\,aL^2\): the upper half of the bar carries more weight and stretches more than the lower. A point on the edge of the top face, 5 mm from the axis, moves towards the axis by \(2\nu a L \cdot 5\ \text{mm} = 5{,}856\) nm, and the rotation tensor there is \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-10}\). These are the numbers of chapter 1, now computed from the field equations and not taken from a book (SymPy).

Is the field valid for a real bar? Only as far as a uniform pulling force on the top face is a good picture of the ceiling. A real ceiling holds the bar differently, for example by a weld over the whole top face, as figure 1.103 shows, right. There the top face cannot narrow, and the field changes near the ceiling. Far from the ceiling the field is almost the same, if the forces with which we hold the bar have the same sum. This is what Saint-Venant's principle states, which we treat later in this chapter. By the rule of thumb of section 11.11.3 this distance is about one diameter, \(10\) mm, which is \(0{,}1\,\%\) of the length of the bar (CM, PDF p. 333), elsewhere the field (11.11) holds almost exactly. Here we say only that the semi-inverse method solves the problem with the load we prescribed, not the one that would interest us in practice.

The semi-inverse method is not limited to bars. Its best known use is Saint-Venant's torsion of prisms, where we prescribe the displacements in the plane of the cross-section and leave the displacement along the axis as an unknown function, and the field equations shrink to a single equation for it (SADD Example 5.3, PDF p. 117, chapter 16). With it we will later in this chapter solve the thick-walled tube and the spherical shell, where we prescribe that the displacement in the direction of the radius depends only on the distance from the axis or from the centre, and for the tube also that it stretches or shortens uniformly along the axis.

Let us also turn the way around. In the inverse method we do not look for the field for a given body and load, but choose the field and ask which problem has it as a solution. Take the simplest field, a linear one, \(\vek u = k\,(x, y, z)\) with a constant number \(k\), and draw it in figure 11.28.

The body is arbitrary, the point \(O\) in it is the origin. The point \(P\) has the position vector \(\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\), and the field moves it by \(k\,\vek r_{\mathrm{P}}\). At \(k < 0\) all displacements point towards \(O\), each the longer, the farther from \(O\) the point is. If this is a solution, it must satisfy the field equations (theorem 11.6 on the Navier-Lamé equations), and from it we read what the problem is.

  1. The displacements grow uniformly with the distance from \(O\), so the second derivatives of a linear field are zero and \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\). The divergence \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 3k\) is constant, so \(\nabla(\operatorname{div}\vek u) = \vek 0\). Equation (11.3) is satisfied with \(\vek b = \vek 0\). Without a body force the field is therefore a possible field of an elastic body.
  2. By theorem 3.19, equation (3.28), the stress is \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\cdot 3k\,\mat I + 2\mu k\,\mat I = (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,k\,\mat I = 3Kk\,\mat I\), because \(3K = 3\lambda + 2\mu\) (bulk modulus, chapter 3). For steel \(3\lambda + 2\mu = 490\) GPa. The stress is equal in all directions, so every piece shrinks equally in all directions and the body gets a similar, smaller shape.
  3. On the surface of any body with outward normal \(\vek n\) the stress vector is \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = 3Kk\,\vek n\). We multiply the normal by a number, so the stress is perpendicular to the surface, equal everywhere, and the pressure is \(p = -3Kk\).

The field \(\vek u = k\,(x, y, z)\) is therefore a solution for any body that is under uniform pressure \(p = -3Kk\) over its whole surface, without body force and without supports. The choice \(k = -p/(3K)\) gives the ball in oil of section 4.3.7, which we checked with the equations in section 11.2: at \(p = 100\) MPa and \(K = 163{,}3\) GPa we get \(k = -2{,}041 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and the radius of a ball of \(10\) mm shrinks by \(2{,}041\ \mu\text{m}\). The same field also solves a thick spherical shell with inner radius 40 mm and outer radius 60 mm that has oil at the same pressure \(p\) on the inside and on the outside, and shrinks it by \(8{,}163\ \mu\text{m}\) on the inner and \(12{,}245\ \mu\text{m}\) on the outer surface (SymPy). The hole shrinks, although the oil in it presses outward. Why? Imagine the hole filled with steel. Then we have a full ball under pressure \(p\), which is everywhere under stress \(-p\,\mat I\), so the steel core presses on the shell with the same pressure \(p\) as the oil. The oil in the hole acts on the shell like the core, and the shell shrinks like the outer part of the full ball. At \(k = +10^{-4}\) we get \(p = -49\) MPa, which means a uniform tension over the whole surface, the arrows would point away from the body. Since this is a problem with surface forces only, the solution is unique up to a small rigid displacement (theorem 11.4). The condition \(\vek u(O) = \vek 0\) at the centre removes only the translation \(\vek a\), and the rotation \(\mat W\) is removed by the condition that the rotation tensor at \(O\) is zero, as in step 14 of the proof of theorem 11.12. The field \(k\,(x, y, z)\) satisfies both, since its gradient \(k\,\mat I\) is symmetric, so its rotation tensor is zero everywhere.

The advantage of the inverse method is that we find the field with a single check. Its weakness is that we cannot choose the problem, since the arrow goes from the field to the problem. SADD warns that with it it is sometimes difficult to construct a solution for a concrete problem of interest (PDF p. 116). So we use it when we look for solutions of simple problems, such as pressure on all faces, or when we check whether some formula from engineering books really is a solution of elasticity.
