Chapter 1 Strain

1.3 Mathematics: limit and derivative

In this section we forget the bar for now. We have a rule that assigns a number \(u(x)\) to every number \(x\), and we ask how fast \(u(x)\) changes as we change \(x\). The answer will be a new number. Once we can compute it, we return to the bar.

When computing we use the school rules for real numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero number, and working with inequalities. The absolute value \(|a|\) of a number \(a\) is the distance of the number from zero: \(|3| = 3\), \(|-3| = 3\). Precisely, \(|a| = a\) if \(a \ge 0\), and \(|a| = -a\) if \(a < 0\) (LEBL1 §1.3, PDF p. 36). So the distance between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(|a - b|\).

Let us draw this. The upper number line has the zero from which we measure. The number \(-3\) lies three units to the left of it, the blue distance with end stops shows \(|{-3}| = 3\). The number \(3\) lies three units to the right, and \(|3| = 3\). The distance is thus the same in both cases, because we measure the length of the path and not its direction. The lower line shows the numbers \(a = -1\) and \(b = 2\) with the distance \(|a - b| = 3\) between them.

1.3.1 Function

The notion of a function grew out of physical problems. The story in the box says why our definition is so general. To continue, its conclusion in the paragraph after the box is enough.

From history

In 1718 Johann Bernoulli defined a quantity that depends on a variable as a quantity composed in any manner of that variable and of constants (CAJORI, PDF p. 227). Such a quantity is an expression that we write with a formula, for example \(x^2 + 5\).

This definition became too narrow in problems of physics. In 1747 Jean le Rond d'Alembert was led, by the vibrating taut string, to an equation containing rates of change in two variables, in place along the string and in time (CAJORI, PDF p. 257-258). In solving it he restricted himself to quantities that can be expanded in an infinite sum of powers (a sum of terms \(a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots\) without end), while Leonhard Euler held that the restriction was not necessary and that they could be arbitrary, even with jumps (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). A quantity that can be expanded in a sum of powers has no jumps, so the restriction would exclude every quantity with a jump. The dispute was judged by Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who decided for Euler (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). It was ended by Joseph Fourier. He first announced his discovery before the Paris Academy in 1807, and in his treatise on the conduction of heat in solid bodies, published in 1822, he wrote that an arbitrary quantity, given by a graph, can be written as an infinite sum of sines and cosines (terms of the form \(b \sin nx\) and \(c \cos nx\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)), and gave formulas for the numbers in the sum (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). A sum of sines and cosines can have a jump, while a sum of powers cannot, so Fourier's form also covered the quantities that d'Alembert's restriction excluded. A general proof that the sum really gives the value of the quantity it came from he did not have (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). In problems on the conduction of heat the values are given at the boundary of a body. Such problems lead to the definition that Euler called the second: the relation between two numbers cannot necessarily be written with a formula. This idea strongly influenced Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet (CAJORI, PDF p. 286).

Let us draw the story. The left picture is Bernoulli's quantity, the graph of the expression \(x^2 + 5\): a smooth curve without jumps, written with a formula. The middle picture is a quantity that Euler allowed: it has a jump where the empty circles are, and it cannot be written as a sum of powers. In the right picture is the same step, the value \(1\) on the first half and \(-1\) on the second, and the blue sum of three sines, \(\frac{4}{\pi}\big(\sin x + \frac{1}{3}\sin 3x + \frac{1}{5}\sin 5x\big)\). Even three sines come close to the step, and with more sines they would follow it still better.

The story showed that the relation between two numbers need not be written with a formula, so the definition that we need asks only for a rule that assigns a number to a number.

A set (množica) is a collection of objects, called its elements (elementi), for example the set of all numbers between \(0\) and \(L\) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.1, PDF p. 8).

We show the rule as arrows between two sets (figure 1.27). The left grey oval is the set \(D\) on which the rule is given, the domain (definicijsko območje). The right grey oval is the set in which the values lie. Two elements are chosen in the left oval, \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), each drawn as a dot. The blue arrow from \(x_1\) points to the value \(f(x_1)\) in the right oval, because the rule assigns this value to the element \(x_1\). The letter \(f\) above the arrows is the name of the rule. In the same way the second arrow leads from \(x_2\) to the value \(f(x_2)\). Exactly one arrow leaves each dot on the left. This is what the definition that follows will require: every element has exactly one value. In the right oval not every point need be hit, an arrow points only to the values that the rule really assigns.

Definition 1.4 Function (funkcija)

A function (funkcija) \(f\) is a rule that assigns to every element \(x\) of some set exactly one element \(f(x)\) of another set. The set whose elements the rule assigns something to is the domain (definicijsko območje) of the function, and \(f(x)\) is the value of the function at \(x\). For now both sets will be sets of numbers: the function assigns to every number \(x\) from some set of numbers exactly one number \(f(x)\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 0.3.11 (PDF p. 14), EP §6.2.4 (p. 281).

The displacement \(u(x)\) is a function. For the hanging bar its domain is the set of all \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\), that is, of all points of the bar, and the value \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the point \(x\). Why does the definition ask for exactly one number? So that the symbol \(f(x)\) means one definite number that we can calculate with. In the next section we will calculate the difference \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). These are the displacements of two different points, \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), and the difference makes sense only because each of the two symbols is one number. Let us look at three rules that test this requirement (figure 1.28).

Let us draw three rules. In each, the circle on the left is an input, the circle on the right an output, and the arrow shows which output the rule assigns to the input. In the first rule, \(y^2 = x\), the input \(4\) is on the left. Two orange arrows lead from it to the outputs \(2\) and \(-2\), because \(2^2 = 4\) and \((-2)^2 = 4\). They are orange because this is not allowed: the input has two outputs. The blue arrow from \(0\) to \(0\) is a correct assignment, since \(0\) has only one output, but the input \(4\) already suffices for the rule not to be a function. In the second rule, \(x \mapsto 1/x\) on \(0 \le x \le 1\), the input \(0\) has no output. The dashed orange arrow ends at a question mark, because we cannot divide by \(0\). The other two inputs each have exactly one output, \(1 \mapsto 1\) and \(1/2 \mapsto 2\). In the third rule the input \(0\) is excluded. Every input, for example \(1\), \(1/2\) and \(1/4\), has exactly one output, so this is a function. The blue arrows are correct assignments.

The first rule would assign to every \(x \ge 0\) the numbers \(y\) whose square is \(x\). To the number \(4\) it would assign both numbers, \(2\) and \(-2\), so it would not be a function: the symbol \(f(4)\) would not say which number is meant, and we could not calculate with it. The second rule, \(x \mapsto 1/x\) on the set of numbers \(0 \le x \le 1\), assigns no number to \(0\), because we cannot divide by \(0\). The requirement "exactly one" is then not met by any number, so this rule is not a function on this set. The third rule, the same \(x \mapsto 1/x\) on the set \(0 < x \le 1\), is a function, since every \(x\) has exactly one value \(1/x\). For the displacement the requirement holds by itself, because after loading every point of the bar is in one place only.

From history

The word function began to be used in 1694 by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, but not in the modern sense (CAJORI, PDF p. 227).

Functions whose input is one number and whose output is one number, like the displacement \(u(x)\), are only the simplest kind. The elements of the two sets can be anything: points, triples of numbers or even other functions (LEBL1 Example 0.3.12). Soon we will describe the position of every point of a body by three numbers, and section 1.5.1 explains how (figure 1.51). The displacement of a point in space will also be given by three numbers, which say how far the point moves in each of three directions. The rule whose input is the three numbers that give the position of a point and whose output is the three numbers that give its displacement will be a function too, only both of its sets will be sets of triples of numbers. The general definition asks only that every element of the first set gets exactly one element of the second.

1.3.2 Ratio of changes

The ratio we computed in the table has a general form. In mathematics the numbers at the input of a function are often called points, because we picture them as points on a straight line. For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a small change \(\Delta x \neq 0\),

\[\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \tag{1.3}\]

is the change of the value divided by the change of the input \(x\). It tells us by how much \(f\) changed on average per unit change of \(x\). We call it the difference quotient (diferenčni količnik). For the bar it is the strain of the piece. For a car journey, where \(f(t)\) is the distance covered up to the time \(t\), it is the average speed over the time \(\Delta t\).

We can also draw the quotient. We draw a function by placing, above every point \(x\) of a horizontal line, a point at the height \(f(x)\). These points form a curve, called the graph (graf) of the function. Let us draw it (figure 1.29). There are two axes: the horizontal one carries the inputs, the vertical one the values of the function. The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(f\). The first point of the graph is above \(x\) at the height \(f(x)\). Grey dashed lines lead from it to both axes, so that we read \(x\) and \(f(x)\) off the axes. The second point is above \(x + \Delta x\) at the height \(f(x + \Delta x)\), with the same guide lines. The points of the graph above \(x\) and above \(x + \Delta x\) are joined by a straight line, blue.

On the way from the first point to the second we move \(\Delta x\) horizontally and \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\) vertically. The difference quotient is the ratio of these two paths:

\[\frac{\text{vertical path}}{\text{horizontal path}} = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} .\]

So it says by how much the line rises per unit of horizontal path. This number is called the slope (strmina) of the line. If the line falls, the slope is negative.

For \(\Delta x = 0\), when the horizontal path is zero, the quotient is not defined, because we would divide by zero. So we treat it as a function of the change \(\Delta x\), defined for all small \(\Delta x\) except \(\Delta x = 0\), when \(f\) is defined on both sides of the point \(x\), that is, also a little before it and a little after it. We ask what it approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero.

1.3.3 Limit

From history

Pierre de Fermat looked for the \(x\) at which a quantity that depends on \(x\) is largest or smallest. He relied on Johannes Kepler's observation that a quantity hardly changes near its largest or smallest value (CAJORI, PDF pp. 179-180). So in the expression for the quantity he replaced \(x\) by \(x + e\) and set the two values equal. The points \(x\) and \(x + e\) then lie on the graph at the same height, on both sides of the top, and as \(e\) shrinks they approach each other and meet at the top (figure 1.30). Then he divided the equation by \(e\) and took \(e = 0\).

The axes: horizontally \(x\), vertically the quantity. The blackwhite curve is the graph of a quantity with one top. The blue top is the place of the largest value, a grey dashed line drops the top to the axis. The first point is at \(x\), the second point at \(x + e\), both are on the graph. The grey dashed horizontal line shows that the points are at the same height, this is Fermat's condition, because we make the two values equal. The gap \(e\) is marked below the axis with end stops. It is drawn too large so that it can be seen. The blue arrows beside the graph show how the points climb toward the top as \(e\) shrinks, always at the same height, until they meet in it.

The solutions of the equation obtained in this way are the sought \(x\), the places of the top. Let us look at the procedure on the example \(f(x) = x\,(3 - x)\), which is not Fermat's:

\[\begin{aligned} (x + e)(3 - x - e) &= x\,(3 - x) && \text{(we set the two values equal)},\\ 3e - 2x\,e - e^2 &= 0 && \text{(we subtract } x\,(3 - x) \text{)},\\ 3 - 2x - e &= 0 && \text{(we divide by } e \neq 0 \text{)},\\ 3 - 2x &= 0 && \text{(we take } e = 0 \text{)}. \end{aligned}\]

This gives \(x = 3/2\), indeed the top of the graph of the function \(f\). Fermat had this rule in 1629 (CAJORI, PDF p. 180). He also based on it a way of drawing the line that touches a curve at a given point (CAJORI, PDF p. 180). We may divide by \(e\) only while \(e\) is not zero, but in the expression he got by dividing, Fermat then set \(e = 0\). This is exactly the difficulty of our quotient (1.3): at \(\Delta x = 0\) it is not defined, because we would divide by zero.

The story has four steps: Newton and Leibniz used the calculus, Berkeley showed the difficulty, Lagrange tried to get around it, Cauchy resolved it. The names and terms (fluxion, increment) need not be remembered, only the difficulty, which is the same as with \(\Delta x\). By his own account Newton conceived the method of fluxions in 1665 and 1666 and used it to find areas under curves (CAJORI, PDF p. 208). He did not regard quantities as made of very small parts but as described by continuous motion, just as a line is drawn by a moving point (CAJORI, PDF p. 213). He called the rate at which such a quantity grows a fluxion (fluksija). In his words, the ratio of two fluxions equals, as exactly as we like, the ratio of the increments of the two quantities over the same time, taken as short as possible (CAJORI, PDF p. 213). As an example he took the line through two points of a curve. When the second point moves into the first, the line turns into the line that touches the curve (CAJORI, PDF p. 213-214). In 1684 Leibniz published his first paper on the differential calculus. Its rules of calculation are stated without proof, and it does not make clear what \(\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\mathrm{d}y\) are (CAJORI, PDF p. 226).

Bishop George Berkeley pointed out the difficulty in 1734 in his Analyst. He took an increment \(i\) of the variable \(x\) that is expressly supposed to be something and so not zero, and found that the quotient of the increment of the power \(x^n\) and the increment \(i\) equals \(n x^{n-1} + \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} x^{n-2} i + \dots\) For \(n = 2\) and \(n = 3\) this quotient is

\[\frac{(x + i)^2 - x^2}{i} = 2x + i , \qquad \frac{(x + i)^3 - x^3}{i} = 3x^2 + 3x\,i + i^2 :\]

the first term is \(n x^{n-1}\), and all the following ones have the factor \(i\). If we now take \(i = 0\), the hypothesis is shifted, and keeping a result obtained on the supposition that \(i\) is not zero is a sophism (CAJORI, PDF p. 235). He called the vanishing quantities the ghosts of departed quantities (CAJORI, PDF p. 235). We met the same difficulty with \(\Delta x\): the difference quotient (1.3) is defined only for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). We need not look for the value of the quotient at \(\Delta x = 0\), which does not exist, but for the number that the quotient approaches.

Lagrange wanted to found the calculus without this kind of approaching. He defined the rate of change of a function, that is, the slope of the line that we reach when the step \(h\) goes to zero, as the coefficient of \(h\) in the expansion of \(f(x + h)\) in powers of \(h\), but he used infinite sums without checking that these sums have a finite value at all, and his proof of the expansion has serious defects (CAJORI, PDF p. 273). The solution came from Cauchy, who in 1821, in his textbook Cours d'analyse, improved the foundations of the differential calculus by a precise treatment of what the quantities approach and by his new theory of functions without jumps (section 1.12) (CAJORI, PDF p. 385).

We look for the number that the difference quotient (1.3) approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero, and for this we must say precisely what approaching means. What does it mean that some function \(g(h)\) approaches a number \(d\) as \(h\) approaches zero? It means that \(g(h)\) is as close to \(d\) as we wish, provided \(h\) is close enough to zero. Let us take the simplest case, \(g(h) = 5 - h\), and the expected number \(d = 5\) (figure 1.31). Set an allowed error, say \(0{,}001\). Is there a band around zero so narrow that for every \(h\) in the band, except \(h = 0\), the value \(g(h)\) differs from \(d\) by less than \(0{,}001\)? Here it is not hard to find: \(g(h) - 5 = -h\), so the difference is smaller than \(0{,}001\) for every \(h\) with \(|h| < 0{,}001\). The band \(|h| < 0{,}001\) therefore works. If there is one for the error \(0{,}001\), for the error \(0{,}000001\), and for every other error we can think of, then \(g(h)\) really approaches \(d\). For a smaller error the band may be narrower, but it must exist. We call the allowed error \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta) and the distance the band reaches on each side of zero \(\delta\) (the small Greek letter delta). Unlike the capital letter \(\Delta\), the small letter \(\delta\) does not mean a change.

The axes: horizontally \(h\), vertically \(g\). The vertical axis is broken, because \(g = 0\) is far below, and so only the part around \(5\) is shown. The grey dotted line is the height \(5\), the number that the values approach. The blue band around it, from \(5 - \eta\) to \(5 + \eta\), is the allowed error, the label \(\eta\) on the right shows its width. The grey band around zero, from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\), is drawn at \(\delta = \eta\). The distance \(\delta\) at the bottom shows half of the band, from zero to the edge. The blackwhite line is the graph of \(5 - h\), the thick blue part is above the band. The line leaves the blue band exactly at the corners of the grey band, so a band as wide as the error is enough. The empty circle at \(h = 0\) says that the value at \(h = 0\) does not matter: there \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient.

We write this exactly, for an arbitrary function \(g\) and an arbitrary number \(d\) (figure 1.32). In mathematics books the allowed error is usually written \(\varepsilon\). For the allowed error, Karl Weierstrass began to use the letter \(\varepsilon\) in his early papers, as Cauchy had already done in 1821 (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 280). We keep \(\varepsilon\) for strain.

Let us draw the general situation. The axes: horizontally \(h\), vertically \(g\). The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(g\). The grey dotted line is the height \(d\), the number that \(g\) should approach. The blue band around it, from \(d - \eta\) to \(d + \eta\), is the allowed error. The grey vertical band around zero, from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\), is the band that we are looking for. The thick blue part of the graph is the graph above this band. It lies within the blue band, and this is the requirement of the definition that follows. The empty circle at \(h = 0\) says that the value at \(h = 0\) does not matter.

We need one more number: \(g\) need not be defined everywhere, and the number \(r\) tells where \(g\) is defined by assumption: on the grey band of figure 1.33, at \(0 < |h| < r\).

The figure has two equal lines \(h\), one above the other. The empty circle at \(0\) says that \(g\) cannot be defined there. The grey band from \(-r\) to \(r\) is the region in which \(g\) is defined by assumption, except at \(0\). On the upper line the blue band from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\) lies inside the grey one, because \(\delta \le r\). On the lower line the band from \(-\delta'\) to \(\delta'\) reaches beyond \(-r\) and \(r\). The blue part is still in the grey band, but the orange dashed parts reach where \(g\) is not defined.

Definition 1.5 Limit of a function (limita funkcije)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit (limita) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d , \tag{1.4}\]

if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3 (PDF p. 114), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < |h|\) leaves out the point \(h = 0\), where \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient. The limit tells what \(g\) approaches, not what it is at the point itself.

We read the definition as a game with two players. The opponent tries to show that \(g(h)\) does not approach \(d\), we try to show that it does:

  1. The opponent chooses an error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. We answer with a band \(\delta > 0\).
  3. The opponent chooses any point \(h\) from the band, \(0 < |h| < \delta\).
  4. We check that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\).

The limit is \(d\) if we have an answer to every choice of the opponent.

The number \(r\) ensures two things.

  1. The points the condition talks about really are in the domain. The condition talks about the points \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). If \(\delta \le r\), every such point is also among the points with \(0 < |h| < r\), where \(g\) is defined by assumption, so the value \(g(h)\) exists. For \(\delta' > r\) the condition would also talk about points where \(g\) is not defined. But we can always take the band that narrow: if the condition holds for some \(\delta\), it also holds for every smaller \(\delta\), because a narrower band only checks some of the same points. So we may always take \(\delta \le r\).
  2. In every band around \(0\), however narrow, there are points where \(g\) is defined. We will need this in step 4 of the proof below that the limit is unique (lemma 1.7), where we must actually pick such a point.

We have defined the limit only at the point \(0\), because the difference quotient needs it only there. This is a narrowing of a more general notion. The limit of a function \(f\) at an arbitrary point \(c\) is defined in the same way, with the distance \(|x - c|\) between \(x\) and \(c\) in place of \(|h|\) (LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3). The definition uses only distances, \(|g(h) - d|\) and \(|h|\), so it works wherever we can measure distances, for example between points of space.

Let us draw this. The upper line has the point \(c\), a grey band from \(c - \delta\) to \(c + \delta\) around it, a blue point \(x\) and a blue distance \(|x - c|\) from \(c\) to \(x\). The grey arrow means shifting the line down by \(c\), so the point \(x\) gets a new label \(h = x - c\). The lower line is the same band and the same distance, only now \(c\) is the zero: the band is from \(-\delta\) to \(\delta\), and the distance is \(|h|\).

Let us also try the definition step by step on the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\), which we have already drawn. The function \(g\) is defined for every \(h\), so all the points the condition talks about are in the domain, however wide the band is. In figure 1.31 we see: the line \(g(h) = 5 - h\), the allowed error \(\eta\) around \(5\) and the band \(\delta\) around zero.

First we write the definition out for this case, with \(g(h) = 5 - h\) and \(d = 5\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|(5 - h) - 5| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\). The grey band with the labels \(-\delta\) and \(\delta\) is drawn in the figure already now only to look ahead, we will find it in step 4.
  2. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds. The fives cancel, \((5 - h) - 5 = -h\), so \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |{-h}|\).
  3. The numbers \(-h\) and \(h\) are equally far from zero, so \(|{-h}| = |h|\). So the condition we must reach is \(|h| < \eta\).
  4. We choose the band so that this condition holds: we take \(\delta = \eta\). It is a positive number, as the definition requires. In the figure the band is as wide as the error.
  5. Check. For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(|h| < \delta = \eta\), so by steps 2 and 3, \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |h| < \eta\).
  6. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of \(g\) at the point \(0\) is \(5\), \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} (5 - h) = 5\).

For the error \(0{,}001\) the band is \(0{,}001\) wide on each side, for the error \(0{,}000001\) only \(0{,}000001\). A smaller allowed error needs a narrower band, as we expected.

The definition must also tell which number is not the limit. Let us take the number \(5{,}1\), which differs from \(5\) by only \(0{,}1\), and show that it is not the limit of the function \(g(h) = 5 - h\) at the point \(0\). The limit needs a band for every error. So \(5{,}1\) is not the limit if there is at least one error \(\eta\) for which no band works: every band around zero, however narrow, contains a point \(h \neq 0\) where \(g(h)\) differs from \(5{,}1\) by \(\eta\) or more. This is the negation of the definition: instead of "for every error there is a band" we have "there is an error for which every band contains a point \(h\) that does not satisfy the condition". In words: there is \(\eta > 0\) such that for every \(\delta > 0\) there is an \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) and \(|g(h) - 5{,}1| \ge \eta\).

We choose the error as follows. For small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) is close to \(5\), and this is \(0{,}1\) away from \(5{,}1\). We take half of this distance, \(\eta = 0{,}05\). Values that differ from \(5\) by less than \(0{,}05\) then differ from \(5{,}1\) by more than \(0{,}05\) and lie outside the allowed error (figure 1.35). Half is not the only possibility. For every error \(\eta < 0{,}1\)

\[|h| < 0{,}1 - \eta \quad\Rightarrow\quad h > \eta - 0{,}1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 0{,}1 + h > \eta ,\]

only the band is narrower for a larger error, for example \(|h| < 0{,}02\) for \(\eta = 0{,}08\). Half is convenient, because then \(0{,}1 - \eta = \eta = 0{,}05\), and only one number appears in the steps below.

The axes: horizontally \(h\), vertically \(g\). The vertical axis is broken, because \(g = 0\) lies far below. The blackwhite line is the graph of \(5 - h\). The empty circle at \(h = 0\) means the same as in the first figure of the limit. The grey dotted line is the height \(5\), which the graph approaches. The blue band around \(5{,}1\), from \(5{,}05\) to \(5{,}15\), is the allowed error \(\eta = 0{,}05\) around the number \(5{,}1\) that we are testing. The blue band lies above \(5\), because \(5{,}1\) is above \(5\). The label with end stops shows the distance \(0{,}1\) from \(5\) to \(5{,}1\). The grey band around zero is an arbitrarily chosen band, here \(\delta = 0{,}1\). In the band a part of the graph is orange, where the graph is outside the blue band. The graph there is near \(5\) and farther than \(0{,}05\) from \(5{,}1\). The orange part is in every band around zero, however narrow, because the values near \(h = 0\) are near \(5\). The dashed vertical line at \(-0{,}05\) shows where the orange part begins. The wider light band from \(4{,}9\) to \(5{,}3\), with dashed edges, is the allowed error \(0{,}2\) around \(5{,}1\) that we need in the paragraph below the steps.

  1. Take \(\eta = 0{,}05\) and any band \(\delta > 0\). We must show that the band contains a point \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) for which \(|g(h) - 5{,}1| \ge 0{,}05\).
  2. Take any \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) that also has \(|h| < 0{,}05\). Such \(h\) exist, for example half of the smaller of the numbers \(\delta\) and \(0{,}05\).
  3. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds: \((5 - h) - 5{,}1 = -(0{,}1 + h)\). The numbers \(-(0{,}1 + h)\) and \(0{,}1 + h\) are equally far from zero, so \(|g(h) - 5{,}1| = |0{,}1 + h|\).
  4. From \(|h| < 0{,}05\) it follows that \(h > -0{,}05\) (dashed line in the figure), so \(0{,}1 + h > 0{,}1 - 0{,}05 = 0{,}05\). So the number \(0{,}1 + h\) is positive and equal to its absolute value, \(|g(h) - 5{,}1| = 0{,}1 + h > 0{,}05 = \eta\).
  5. We picked the band \(\delta\) freely and found points in it where the condition fails. For the error \(0{,}05\) no band works, so \(5{,}1\) is not the limit of \(g\) at the point \(0\).

For a large error the number \(5{,}1\) would pass the test. For the error \(0{,}2\) the band \(\delta = 0{,}1\) works: for \(0 < |h| < 0{,}1\) the number \(0{,}1 + h\) lies between \(0\) and \(0{,}2\), so \(|g(h) - 5{,}1| < 0{,}2\). Only a small enough error rejects the number \(5{,}1\), for example any error smaller than its distance \(0{,}1\) from \(5\). This is why the definition asks for a band for every error and not just for some. The proof below, which shows that no function has two limits, uses the same idea: the values cannot be close to two different numbers at once.

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Otherwise the word "limit" could mean two different numbers. Let us check that this is so.

The idea is simple (figure 1.36). If \(g(h)\) approached the two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) at once, then for small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) would be close to both: \(g(h)\) would lie in the part of the line around \(d_1\) and in the part of the line around \(d_2\). Two such parts of the line overlap only if the numbers are close to each other. But only equal numbers can be closer to each other than every error.

The line is the number line. The blue dot \(d_1\) and the orange dot \(d_2\) are the candidates for the limit. The thick blue line is the segment of the line around \(d_1\), the orange one around \(d_2\). The radius of each is \(\eta/2\), the same for both. We take half of the error because we will add two such distances (the paragraph under the figure says why) and together they should give \(\eta\). The dot \(g(h)\) lies in both segments at once, in the intersection. Below is the distance \(|d_1 - d_2|\) between the numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\). Since both numbers lie near \(g(h)\), they are near each other. The blue measure shows the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\), the orange one the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). Each is smaller than \(\eta/2\), because \(g(h)\) lies in both segments.

The step "so the two numbers would be close to each other" needs a rule about distances between numbers. The distance between the numbers \(p\) and \(q\) is \(|p - q|\) (introduction of section 1.3). If we go from the number \(p\) to the number \(s\) by way of a third number \(q\), we cover the distance \(|p - q| + |q - s|\), and this is not smaller than the distance \(|p - s|\) that we cover going straight. As an equation:

\[|p - s| \le |p - q| + |q - s| .\]

Let us test it on the numbers of figure 1.37. For \(p = 1\), \(q = 3\) and \(s = 4\) the number \(q\) lies between \(p\) and \(s\), and the two sides are equal: \(|1 - 4| = 3\) and \(|1 - 3| + |3 - 4| = 2 + 1 = 3\). For \(q = 6\) the number \(q\) lies outside, the path through \(q\) covers the stretch from \(4\) to \(6\) twice, and it is longer: \(|1 - 6| + |6 - 4| = 5 + 2 = 7 > 3\). Since \(p - s = (p - q) + (q - s)\), the inequality is a special case of the inequality \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) for the numbers \(a = p - q\) and \(b = q - s\). We prove that inequality in the next lemma. Along with it we prove the rule for how we get the absolute value when two numbers are multiplied. The first part of the proof follows three views on the number line (figure 1.38). We will need the second part already for the hanging bar: the difference between the quotient and its limit will be \(-a\,\Delta x\) with a positive number \(a\), and its absolute value \(a\,|\Delta x|\) (section 1.3.4). For example \(|0{,}2 \cdot (-3)| = 0{,}6 = 0{,}2 \cdot |-3|\).

The figure has two equal number lines with marks \(0\) to \(7\), the upper one and the lower one. On the upper line are the dots \(p = 1\), \(q = 3\) and \(s = 4\), \(q\) lies between \(p\) and \(s\). The blue distances \(|p - q| = 2\) and \(|q - s| = 1\) are the path from \(p\) to \(s\) through \(q\). The grey distance \(|p - s| = 3\) under the line is the straight path. Both paths are equally long. On the lower line are the dots \(p = 1\), \(s = 4\) and \(q = 6\), \(q\) lies outside. The blue distances \(|p - q| = 5\) and \(|q - s| = 2\) are the path through \(q\). The second lies above the first, because they would otherwise overlap. The grey distance \(|p - s| = 3\) is again the straight path, shorter.

Let us show the rule about the product on the line too. The product \(a\,b\) is \(|b|\) copies of the number \(a\), laid one after another from zero, in the opposite direction if \(b\) is negative. For a non-integer \(|b|\), for example \(|b| = \tfrac12\), we take the corresponding part of a copy, half of the distance \(|a|\). The figure shows whole numbers \(b\), but the rule holds for every \(b\), as the steps of the proof below show by signs. The first row has \(a = 2\) and \(b = 3\): the product \(6\) lies six units to the right of zero, like three copies of the distance \(|a| = 2\). The second row has \(a = -2\) and \(b = -3\): the product is again \(6\), because three copies of the number \(-2\) are laid in the opposite direction. The third row has \(a = -2\) and \(b = 3\): the product \(-6\) lies six units to the left. The fourth row has \(a = 2\) and \(b = -3\): the product is again \(-6\). In all four cases the distance from zero to \(a\,b\) equals \(|b| \cdot |a|\).

Lemma 1.6 Triangle inequality and the absolute value of a product (trikotniška neenakost in absolutna vrednost zmnožka)

For any real numbers \(a\) and \(b\):

  1. \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\), the triangle inequality for numbers (trikotniška neenakost za števila),
  2. \(|a\,b| = |a|\,|b|\), the absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values.
Proof

We have to show both items. We use only the definition of the absolute value, \(|t| = t\) for \(t \ge 0\) and \(|t| = -t\) for \(t < 0\) (introduction of section 1.3), and the school rules for inequalities.

1. Triangle inequality.

  1. Let \(t \ge 0\). Then \(|t| = t\), so \(t \le |t|\) and \(-|t| = -t \le 0 \le t\). So the number \(t\) lies between \(-|t|\) and \(|t|\): \(-|t| \le t \le |t|\).
  2. Let \(t < 0\). Then \(|t| = -t\), so \(-|t| = t\) and \(t < 0 < -t = |t|\). Here too \(-|t| \le t \le |t|\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, used for \(t = a\) and for \(t = b\), we have \(-|a| \le a \le |a|\) and \(-|b| \le b \le |b|\).
  4. We add the inequalities of step 3, the left sides together and the right sides together. The sum of two non-strict inequalities in the same direction is again such an inequality, so \(-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|\).
  5. Let \(c = |a| + |b|\). If \(a + b \ge 0\), then \(|a + b| = a + b\), and by the right inequality of step 4, \(|a + b| \le c\).
  6. If \(a + b < 0\), then \(|a + b| = -(a + b)\). We multiply the left inequality of step 4, \(-c \le a + b\), by \(-1\), which reverses it: \(-(a + b) \le c\). So here too \(|a + b| \le c\).
  7. The sum \(a + b\) is either nonnegative or negative, so by steps 5 and 6 we always have \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\).

2. Absolute value of a product. Cases by signs, each is one step below:

\(a\) \(b\) \(a\,b\) step
\(\ge 0\) \(\ge 0\) \(\ge 0\) 2
\(< 0\) \(< 0\) \(> 0\) 3
\(< 0\) \(\ge 0\) \(\le 0\) 4
\(\ge 0\) \(< 0\) \(\le 0\) 5
  1. For every number \(t \le 0\) we have \(|t| = -t\). For \(t < 0\) the definition says so, for \(t = 0\) both sides are zero.
  2. Let \(a \ge 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). A product of nonnegative numbers is nonnegative, so \(|a\,b| = a\,b = |a|\,|b|\).
  3. Let \(a < 0\) and \(b < 0\). Then \(-a\) and \(-b\) are positive, and \(a\,b = (-a)(-b) > 0\). So \(|a\,b| = (-a)(-b) = |a|\,|b|\).
  4. Let \(a < 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). Then \((-a)\,b \ge 0\), so \(a\,b = -\big((-a)\,b\big) \le 0\). By step 1, \(|a\,b| = -a\,b = (-a)\,b = |a|\,|b|\).
  5. Let \(a \ge 0\) and \(b < 0\). As in step 4, with the roles of the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) exchanged, \(a\,b \le 0\) and \(|a\,b| = a\,(-b) = |a|\,|b|\).
  6. Every pair of numbers \(a\), \(b\) falls under one of the cases of steps 2 to 5, so always \(|a\,b| = |a|\,|b|\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 1.3.1, items (iii), (v) and (vi), and Prop. 1.3.2 (PDF pp. 36-37). The proof follows the source.

Both rules are simple, but we will use them in almost every estimate in the book. The first says that a sum of two errors is, in size, at most the sum of their sizes. The second says that we may take a constant factor out of the absolute value, as in \(|a\,\Delta x| = a\,|\Delta x|\) with a positive number \(a\). The name triangle inequality comes from geometry: in a triangle no side is longer than the sum of the other two. We will later prove the same inequality for distances between points of space (chapter 2, section 2.3). Now we can prove that the limit is unique.

Lemma 1.7 Uniqueness of the limit (enoličnost limite)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). Then \(g\) has at most one limit at the point \(0\).

Proof

We have to show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) that both satisfy the definition of the limit are equal.

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \(d_1\) is a limit, the definition gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a band \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We take half the error because in step 6 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \(d_2\) is a limit, for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a band \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\).
  4. Take an \(h\) that is nonzero and smaller than both bands, for example half of the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\). For this \(h\) both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold: \(g(h)\) lies in both parts of the line in figure 1.36.
  5. Write the difference \(d_1 - d_2\) through the value \(g(h)\), which we know: \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - g(h)) + (g(h) - d_2)\).
  6. The distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) is not larger than the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\) plus the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). This is the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1), for \(a = d_1 - g(h)\) and \(b = g(h) - d_2\):
    \[|d_1 - d_2| \le |d_1 - g(h)| + |g(h) - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  7. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2|\) is smaller than every positive number.
  8. A nonnegative number that is smaller than every positive number is zero: if it were positive, it would be smaller than itself. So \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\) and \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.1.4 (PDF pp. 114-115).

The proof showed a pattern that we will meet many times: when we want a sum of two errors to be small, we allow each of them half.

So far the function \(g\) was defined on both sides of the point \(0\). For the bar this does not hold everywhere. The displacement of the bar is defined only for \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\). At the top, at \(x = 0\), the difference quotient of the displacement is therefore defined only for \(\Delta x > 0\), since the point \(x + \Delta x\) must lie on the bar. At the bottom end, at \(x = L\), it is defined only for \(\Delta x < 0\). Such a function does not satisfy the condition \(0 < |h| < r\) of the definition of the limit 1.5. So we also write the definition with a band that reaches to one side of zero only (figure 1.40): on the left \(g\) is not defined, and the band \(\delta\) reaches only to the right.

The axes: horizontally \(h\), vertically \(g\). To the left of zero is a hatched region: \(g\) is not defined there. The blackwhite line is the graph, it starts at \(h = 0\) and runs only to the right. The label \(d\) on the left shows the height that the graph should approach. The blue band around it, from \(d - \eta\) to \(d + \eta\), is the allowed error. The grey band from \(0\) to \(\delta\) reaches only to the right, because \(g\) is not defined on the left. The thick blue part of the graph is the graph above this band, and it is inside the blue band. The empty circle at \(h = 0\) means the same as in the first figure of the limit.

Definition 1.8 One-sided limit (enostranska limita)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit from the right (limita z desne) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} g(h) = d\), if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\). If \(g\) is defined for all \(h\) with \(-r < h < 0\), the limit from the left (limita z leve) is defined in the same way, only the condition holds for every \(h\) with \(-\delta < h < 0\), and we write \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^-} g(h) = d\). Both are called one-sided limits (enostranski limiti).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.16 (PDF p. 119), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The sign \(^+\) says that \(h\) approaches zero from the positive side, the sign \(^-\) from the negative side. A limit from the right is unique too, because the proof of lemma 1.7 on the uniqueness of the limit works without change: the \(h\) of step 4, half of the smallest of the positive numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\), is positive. For a limit from the left we take \(-h\). If a function is defined on both sides of zero and has the limit \(d\) there, it also has both one-sided limits, equal to \(d\), since the condition for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) contains both one-sided conditions.

1.3.4 Derivative

Now we can say what the number is that the strain of the piece approached in the table. What we are looking for is shown by figure 1.41: the lines through a point of the graph and the point above \(x + \Delta x\) approach one line as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller.

The axes are as before. The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(f\). The blackwhite dot on it is the point above \(x\), the grey dashed line drops it to the axis so that we see \(x\). Three grey lines pass through this point, each through a different point of the graph. The first passes through the point above \(x + \Delta x_1\), the second through the point above \(x + \Delta x_2\), which is closer, because \(\Delta x_2 < \Delta x_1\). The third passes through a still closer point. As \(\Delta x\) gets smaller, the lines come closer and closer to the blue line.

Definition 1.9 Derivative (odvod)

Let the function \(f\) be defined for all numbers whose distance from \(x\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). The function \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(x\) if the difference quotient (1.3) has a limit as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. This limit is the derivative (odvod) of the function \(f\) at the point \(x\):

\[f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.5}\]

If \(x\) is the smallest number in the domain of \(f\) and \(f\) is defined for all numbers \(t\) with \(x \le t < x + r\), the derivative at the point \(x\) is the one-sided limit of the difference quotient from the right as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero (definition 1.8). If \(x\) is the largest number in the domain and \(f\) is defined for all numbers \(t\) with \(x - r < t \le x\), the derivative is the limit from the left. Such a derivative is called a one-sided derivative (enostranski odvod).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 4.1.1 (PDF p. 155), which also allows a point at the end of the domain. The notation with \(\Delta x\) follows BARBER (1.35)-(1.36), where it is \(\delta x\).

So the derivative is a rate of change: by how much \(f\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when the change is very small. We read \(f'(x)\) as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)". In books you will also find the derivative written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}\). This notation recalls the quotient \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) from which the derivative comes. The notation is Leibniz's (CAJORI, PDF p. 227). For the hanging bar the derivative at the top, at \(x = 0\), is one-sided, from the right, because there is no bar above the top, and at the bottom, at \(x = L\), from the left.

This is the derivative of a function whose input is one number, \(x\), and whose output is one number, \(f(x)\), like the displacement \(u(x)\) of the bar. For now this is the only kind we need. In a body, however, a point is given by three numbers, which say where it lies, and its displacement is given by three numbers too, which say how far it moves in each of three directions. Figure 1.51 shows such a point and displacement. There we will look at how one of the output numbers changes when only one of the three input numbers changes and the other two stay fixed. We will define such a derivative in section 1.7.2 (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8). A more general derivative, however, is no longer a single number: for a function with \(n\) input and \(m\) output numbers it tells approximately how much each output number changes for a small change of the input numbers (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.1). For the displacement in a body \(n = m = 3\), and we will need such a derivative when we describe strain in space.

On the graph the derivative has a clear picture. As \(\Delta x\) gets smaller, the point of the graph above \(x + \Delta x\) moves along the graph towards the point above \(x\), and the lines through the two points turn towards the line that has the slope \(f'(x)\) and passes through the point of the graph above \(x\) (figure 1.41). This line is called the tangent (tangenta) of the graph at the point \(x\). The slopes of the lines through two points are difference quotients, and their limit, the derivative, is the slope of the tangent.

Let us compute the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a > 0\) and \(L\) are given numbers (for the steel bar \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\)). The way has two parts. First we simplify the difference quotient so that \(\Delta x\), which we must not set to zero, disappears from the denominator. Then we find the limit of the simplified expression from the definition. For the simplification we need the square of a sum \((x + \Delta x)^2\), shown in figure 1.42: the square with side \(x + \Delta x\) is made of the square \(x^2\), two rectangles \(x\,\Delta x\) and a small square \(\Delta x^2\).

The blackwhite outline is the square with side \(x + \Delta x\), two inner lines cut it into four parts. The bottom side is divided into the lengths \(x\) and \(\Delta x\), the left side likewise, because it is a square. The large blue square has side \(x\), so its area is \(x^2\). The two orange rectangles have sides \(x\) and \(\Delta x\), so each has area \(x\,\Delta x\), and they are equal. The grey square has side \(\Delta x\). Together the area is \(x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\). The gap \(\Delta x\) is exaggerated so that all the parts can be seen.

  1. The value at \(x + \Delta x\): \(u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2)\).
  2. Expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = \) \(x^2\) \(+\) \(2x\,\Delta x\) \(+\) \(\Delta x^2\) (figure 1.42), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). So
    \[u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x - x^2 - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  3. Subtract \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) = a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  4. All three terms in the bracket have the factor \(\Delta x\), so we take it out: \(a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  5. Divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
    \[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]

The first part of the way is done. Before we take the limit, let us see what the equation of step 5 tells us. It holds for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), but its two sides behave differently at \(\Delta x = 0\).

The quotient of the numerator \(N\) and the denominator \(\Delta x\) is the same as before for the difference quotient: the vertical path \(N\) divided by the horizontal path \(\Delta x\), that is, the slope of the line. We draw it as the slope of the line from the crossing of the axes to the point \((\Delta x, N)\). In the upper left picture the points above \(\Delta x = 2\) lie ever lower, the dashed lines from the crossing of the axes lie ever flatter towards the horizontal axis, and the slope goes to zero. In the upper right the points lie at the height \(2\) above an ever smaller \(\Delta x\), the lines are ever steeper, and the slope exceeds every bound. In the lower left lie the points \((\Delta x,\ \Delta x)\), all on the same line with slope \(1\). The numbers of all three cases are in the table below. In the lower right the numerator and the denominator are zero, the point is the crossing of the axes itself, and every line passes through it, not just one.

The left side of the equation from step 5 would at \(\Delta x = 0\) have, as in panel (d), the numerator \(u(x + 0) - u(x) = u(x) - u(x) = 0\) and the denominator \(0\):

\[\frac{u(x + 0) - u(x)}{0} = \frac{0}{0} .\]

This is not a number. The quotient \(c/b\) is the number that gives \(c\) when multiplied by \(b\). For \(0/0\) it would have to be a number that gives \(0\) when multiplied by \(0\). Every number does that, so there is no single number we could call \(0/0\). The left side has no value at \(\Delta x = 0\), and that is why we could not shrink the piece to nothing in section 1.2.

How can the quotient approach a number at all, then? As \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, both the numerator \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) and the denominator \(\Delta x\) go to zero. If only one of them did, the outcome would be clear. A numerator going to zero over a fixed denominator gives quotients that go to zero, for example \(0{,}1/2\), \(0{,}01/2\), \(0{,}001/2\). A fixed nonzero numerator over a denominator going to zero gives quotients whose size grows beyond every bound, for example \(2/0{,}1 = 20\), \(2/0{,}01 = 200\), \(2/0{,}001 = 2000\). When both go to zero together, for example \(0{,}1/0{,}1\), \(0{,}01/0{,}01\), \(0{,}001/0{,}001\), the quotient can approach an ordinary number (here \(1\)), and which number depends on how fast the numerator shrinks compared with the denominator. Step 4 tells how fast. The numerator is the denominator \(\Delta x\) times the factor \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\), and this factor is exactly the ratio of the numerator to the denominator. The numerator and the denominator go to zero, their ratio does not. At the top of the bar, at \(x = 0\) and \(L = 10\) m, the ratio for \(\Delta x = 1\) m, \(0{,}1\) m and \(0{,}01\) m is \(a\,(20\ \text{m} - \Delta x)\), that is \(3{,}709\), \(3{,}884\) and \(3{,}902\), all times \(10^{-6}\), as in the table of section 1.2.

Instead of \(0{,}1\) m and \(0{,}01\) m, \(0{,}5\) m and \(0{,}25\) m are drawn, because at smaller \(\Delta x\) the points would merge with the point \((0, 0)\) in the figure. The grey dashed lines of each point drop it to both axes, so that we can read the denominator and the numerator from them. From point to point both get smaller, while the quotient hardly changes. The quotient of a point is the slope of the line from the point \((0, 0)\) to it. These lines are not drawn, because they would hardly be told apart from the curve. The blue line from the point \((0, 0)\) is the tangent (tangenta), the limit of the lines through \((0, 0)\) and a point of the curve as that point approaches \((0, 0)\) (section on the derivative above). Its slope is \(3{,}904\), the number the quotient approaches. Why exactly this number, the computation below shows.

Step 5 changed no value of the quotient: for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\) both sides are equal. The limit uses only these values, since \(0 < |h|\) in definition 1.5 leaves out \(0\). So both sides have the same limit if one has one.

There is no division on the right side any more. It disappeared in step 5, when the factor \(\Delta x\) cancelled. So we may put \(\Delta x = 0\) into the right side:

\[a\,(2L - 2x - 0) = a\,(2L - 2x) .\]

This is an ordinary number, which we can compute for every point \(x\).

Now we split the right side of step 5 into this number and the rest. We multiply the bracket by \(a\) and group the first two terms again: \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\). So the strain of the piece is

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x . \tag{1.6}\]

The first term does not depend on the length \(\Delta x\) of the piece. It is the same for long and short pieces. The second term, \(a\,\Delta x\), is all that separates the quotient from the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and it is proportional to the length of the piece: a piece ten times shorter is ten times closer to the number. Because \(a\,\Delta x\) is subtracted, the strains of pieces with a positive \(\Delta x\) are smaller than the number and grow towards it as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller.

This explains the table of section 1.2, where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(L = 10\) m. At the top, at \(x = 0\), the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) is \(a \cdot 20\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(a \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). These are the two numbers at which the columns of the table settled, and the slopes of the tangents on the graph of the displacement (figure 1.23). For a piece 1 m long the difference is \(a\,\Delta x = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and indeed \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} - 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6} = 3{,}7088 \cdot 10^{-6}\), rounded to four digits \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the first number in the table. In every following row the piece is ten times shorter, so the difference is ten times smaller: \(0{,}1952\), \(0{,}01952\), \(0{,}001952\) and \(0{,}0001952\), each times \(10^{-6}\). The table shows four digits, and the last one stands for \(0{,}001 \cdot 10^{-6}\). In the last row the difference is too small to show in the rounded last digit, so the table shows \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) itself. So the numbers settle when the difference falls below the last digit shown.

The difference \(a\,\Delta x\) does not contain \(x\), so it is the same at the top and at the middle. The table shows this too: in each row both numbers lie equally far below their final values, for example \(1{,}952 - 1{,}757 = 0{,}195\) at the middle and \(3{,}904 - 3{,}709 = 0{,}195\) at the top. The difference comes from the term \(-a\,\Delta x^2\) of step 2, the part of the square \((x + \Delta x)^2\) that does not contain \(x\). After the division by \(\Delta x\) in step 5 it becomes \(-a\,\Delta x\).

Let us draw this. The axes: horizontally \(\Delta x\) in metres, vertically the quotient. The upper line is the quotient at the top, it starts at \(3{,}904\), where the empty circle is, because at \(\Delta x = 0\) the quotient is not defined. The middle line is the quotient at the middle, it starts at \(1{,}952\). The lines are parallel, because each has the slope \(-a\). The dashed lines show the starting heights. At \(\Delta x = 1\) m the upper one drops by \(0{,}195\) and the middle one drops by \(0{,}195\) too.

So the difference \(a\,\Delta x\) goes to zero as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, and we expect the limit to be the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). But a table, as we said in section 1.2, shows only a few rows. The equation (1.6) has exactly the form of the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\) of section 1.3.3: the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) stands in place of \(5\), the change \(\Delta x\) in place of \(h\), and the subtracted term has an extra factor \(a\). So we show the limit with the same steps as there. Because of the factor \(a\), the band will be \(\delta = \eta / a\) instead of \(\delta = \eta\) (figure 1.46).

  1. The quotient of step 5 is \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\). Let us show from the definition of the limit 1.5 that its limit is \(d = a\,(2L - 2x)\). The definition, written out for the quotient of step 5 as a function of the change \(\Delta x\) (which plays the role of \(h\)) and for this number \(d\), demands: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) - a\,(2L - 2x)| < \eta\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\). The formula \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) is defined for every number \(x\), not only for the points of the bar between \(0\) and \(L\), so the quotient of step 5 is defined for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and all the points the condition talks about are in the domain. What this means for the displacement of the bar, which is defined only for the points of the bar, we say in section 1.4.
  2. The axes: horizontally the change \(\Delta x\), vertically the quotient. The blackwhite line is the quotient \(d - a\,\Delta x\). It falls, because \(a > 0\) and \(a\,\Delta x\) is subtracted. The empty circle at \(\Delta x = 0\) says that the quotient is not defined there. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\), which is the blue band around \(d\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  3. Simplify the difference between the quotient and \(d\). By the equation (1.6), \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\), so the difference is \(a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x - a\,(2L - 2x) = -a\,\Delta x\).
  4. The numbers \(-a\,\Delta x\) and \(a\,\Delta x\) are equally far from zero, and since \(a > 0\), \(|a\,\Delta x| = a\,|\Delta x|\). So \(|{-a\,\Delta x}| = a\,|\Delta x|\), and the condition we must reach is \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\).
  5. We divide the inequality by the positive number \(a\), so it does not turn around: the condition holds exactly when \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\). So we take the band \(\delta = \eta / a\), which is positive.
  6. Check. For every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\) we have \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\), so \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\), and by steps 8 and 9 this is the difference between the quotient and \(d\).
  7. We picked the error freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of the quotient is \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and
    \[u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

Step 5 was the heart of the computation (the two quotient lines). Together with step 4 it showed how fast the numerator shrinks compared with the denominator: the numerator is \(\Delta x\) times \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\), and the factor that remains approaches \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). When this number is not zero, the numerator therefore shrinks as fast as \(\Delta x\) itself. At the bottom end, at \(x = L\), however, \(a\,(2L - 2x) = 0\), the factor that remains is \(-a\,\Delta x\) and the numerator \(-a\,\Delta x^2\). There the numerator shrinks faster than the denominator, and the quotient \(-a\,\Delta x\) goes to zero.

Let us take pairs of pieces at the top whose lengths differ by a factor of 10, like neighbouring panels of figure 1.24. The elongation is the length times the strain, so the ratio of the elongations of two pieces is the ratio of the lengths times the ratio of the strains from the table:

\[\frac{\Delta u_{1\,\text{m}}}{\Delta u_{0{,}1\,\text{m}}} = 10 \cdot \frac{3{,}709}{3{,}884} = 9{,}55 , \qquad \frac{\Delta u_{0{,}01\,\text{m}}}{\Delta u_{0{,}001\,\text{m}}} = 10 \cdot \frac{3{,}902}{3{,}904} = 9{,}995 .\]

For the shorter pair the ratio of the elongations is almost exactly the ratio of the lengths, 10. The elongation and the length vanish together, and their ratio, the strain, approaches a definite number.

With patient algebra we cancelled the common factor \(\Delta x\) in step 5 and turned the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and the limit was then easy.
