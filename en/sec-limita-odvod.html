Chapter 1 Strain

1.3 Mathematics: limit and derivative

In this section we forget the bar for a moment. We have a rule that assigns a number \(u(x)\) to every number \(x\), and we ask how fast \(u(x)\) changes as we change \(x\). The answer will be a new number. Once we can compute it, we return to the bar.

When computing we use the school rules for real numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero number, and working with inequalities. The absolute value \(|a|\) of a number \(a\) is the distance of the number from zero: \(|3| = 3\), \(|-3| = 3\). Precisely, \(|a| = a\) if \(a \ge 0\), and \(|a| = -a\) if \(a < 0\) (LEBL1 §1.3, PDF p. 36). So the distance between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(|a - b|\).

1.3.1 Function

From history

In 1718 Johann Bernoulli defined a quantity that depends on a variable as a quantity composed in any manner of that variable and of constants (CAJORI, PDF p. 227). Such a quantity is an expression that we write with a formula, for example \(x^2 + 5\).

This definition became too narrow in problems of physics. In 1747 Jean le Rond d'Alembert was led, by the vibrating taut string, to an equation containing rates of change in two variables, in place along the string and in time (CAJORI, PDF p. 257-258). In solving it he restricted himself to quantities that can be expanded in an infinite sum of powers, while Leonhard Euler held that the restriction was not necessary and that they could be arbitrary, even with jumps (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). A quantity that can be expanded in a sum of powers has no jumps, so the restriction would exclude every quantity with a jump. The dispute was judged by Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who decided for Euler (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). It was ended by Joseph Fourier. He first announced his discovery before the Paris Academy in 1807, and in his treatise on the conduction of heat in solid bodies, published in 1822, he wrote that an arbitrary quantity, given by a graph, can be written as an infinite sum of sines and cosines, and gave formulas for the numbers in the sum (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). A sum of sines and cosines can have a jump, while a sum of powers cannot, so Fourier's form also covered the quantities that d'Alembert's restriction excluded. A general proof that the sum really gives the value of the quantity it came from he did not have (CAJORI, PDF p. 286). In problems on the conduction of heat the values are given at the boundary of a body. Such problems lead to the definition that Euler called the second: the relation between two numbers cannot necessarily be written with a formula. This idea strongly influenced Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet (CAJORI, PDF p. 286).

The story showed that the relation between two numbers need not be written with a formula, so the definition that we need asks only for a rule that assigns a number to a number.

A set (množica) is a collection of objects, called its elements (elementi), for example the set of all numbers between \(0\) and \(L\) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.1, PDF p. 8).

Definition 1.4 Function (funkcija)

A function (funkcija) \(f\) is a rule that assigns to every element \(x\) of some set exactly one element \(f(x)\) of another set. The set whose elements the rule assigns something to is the domain (definicijsko območje) of the function, and \(f(x)\) is the value of the function at \(x\). For now both sets will be sets of numbers: the function assigns to every number \(x\) from some set of numbers exactly one number \(f(x)\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 0.3.11 (PDF p. 14), EP §6.2.4 (p. 281).

The displacement \(u(x)\) is a function. For the hanging bar its domain is the set of all \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\), that is, of all points of the bar, and the value \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the point \(x\). Why does the definition ask for exactly one number? So that the symbol \(f(x)\) means one definite number that we can calculate with. In the next section we will calculate the difference \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). These are the displacements of two different points, \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), and the difference makes sense only because each of the two symbols is one number. A rule that assigned to the number \(4\) both numbers whose square is \(4\), that is, \(2\) and \(-2\), would not be a function: the symbol \(f(4)\) would not say which number is meant, and we could not calculate with it. For the displacement the requirement holds by itself, because after loading every point of the bar is in one place only.

From history

The word function began to be used in 1694 by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, but not in the modern sense (CAJORI, PDF p. 227).

Functions whose input is one number and whose output is one number, like the displacement \(u(x)\), are only the simplest kind. The elements of the two sets can be anything: points, triples of numbers or even other functions (LEBL1 Example 0.3.12). Soon we will describe the position of every point of a body by three numbers, and section 1.5.1 explains how. The displacement of a point in space will also be given by three numbers, which say how far the point moves in each of three directions. The rule whose input is the three numbers that give the position of a point and whose output is the three numbers that give its displacement will be a function too, only both of its sets will be sets of triples of numbers. The general definition asks only that every element of the first set gets exactly one element of the second. Mathematicians even write a function as a set of pairs (input, value) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.11), which we will not need.

1.3.2 Ratio of changes

The ratio we computed in the table has a general form. In mathematics the numbers at the input of a function are often called points, because we picture them as points on a straight line. For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a small change \(\Delta x \neq 0\),

\[\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \tag{1.3}\]

is the change of the value divided by the change of the input \(x\). It tells us by how much \(f\) changed on average per unit change of \(x\). We call it the difference quotient (diferenčni količnik). For the bar it is the strain of the piece. For a car journey, where \(f(t)\) is the distance covered up to the time \(t\), it is the average speed over the time \(\Delta t\).

We can also draw the quotient. We draw a function by placing, above every point \(x\) of a horizontal line, a point at the height \(f(x)\). These points form a curve, called the graph (graf) of the function. In figure 1.14 the points of the graph above \(x\) and above \(x + \Delta x\) are joined by a straight line. On the way from the first to the second we move \(\Delta x\) horizontally and \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\) vertically. The difference quotient is the ratio of these two paths, so it says how much the line rises per unit of horizontal path. This number is called the slope (strmina) of the line. If the line falls, the slope is negative.

For \(\Delta x = 0\) the quotient is not defined, because we would divide by zero. So we treat it as a function of the change \(\Delta x\), defined for all small \(\Delta x\) except \(\Delta x = 0\), when \(f\) is defined on both sides of the point \(x\). At an end of the domain, as for the displacement at the ends of the bar, the quotient is defined only for small \(\Delta x\) of one sign, and we deal with this case at the close of section 1.3.3. We ask what it approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero.

1.3.3 Limit

From history

Pierre de Fermat looked for the \(x\) at which a quantity that depends on \(x\) is largest or smallest. He relied on Johannes Kepler's observation that a quantity hardly changes near its largest or smallest value (CAJORI, PDF p. 179-180). So in the expression for the quantity he replaced \(x\) by \(x + e\), set the two values equal (figure 1.15), divided the equation by \(e\) and then took \(e = 0\). The solutions of the equation obtained in this way are the sought \(x\). He had this rule in 1629 (CAJORI, PDF p. 180). He also based on it a way of drawing the line that touches a curve at a given point (CAJORI, PDF p. 180). We may divide by \(e\) only while \(e\) is not zero, but in the expression he got by dividing, Fermat then set \(e = 0\). This is exactly the difficulty of our quotient (1.3): at \(\Delta x = 0\) it is not defined, because we would divide by zero.

By his own account Newton conceived the method of fluxions in 1665 and 1666 and used it to find areas under curves (CAJORI, PDF p. 208). He did not regard quantities as made of very small parts but as described by continuous motion, just as a line is drawn by a moving point (CAJORI, PDF p. 213). He called the rate at which such a quantity grows a fluxion (fluksija). In his words, the ratio of two fluxions equals, as exactly as we like, the ratio of the increments of the two quantities over the same time, taken as short as possible (CAJORI, PDF p. 213). As an example he took the line through two points of a curve. When the second point moves into the first, the line turns into the line that touches the curve (CAJORI, PDF p. 213-214). In 1684 Leibniz published his first paper on the differential calculus. Its rules of calculation are stated without proof, and it does not make clear what \(\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\mathrm{d}y\) are (CAJORI, PDF p. 226).

Bishop George Berkeley pointed out the difficulty in 1734 in his Analyst. He took an increment \(i\) of the variable \(x\) that is expressly supposed to be something and so not zero, and found that the quotient of the increment of the power \(x^n\) and the increment \(i\) equals \(n x^{n-1} + \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} x^{n-2} i + \dots\) If we now take \(i = 0\), the hypothesis is shifted, and keeping a result obtained on the supposition that \(i\) is not zero is a sophism (CAJORI, PDF p. 235). He called the vanishing quantities the ghosts of departed quantities (CAJORI, PDF p. 235). We met the same difficulty with \(\Delta x\): the difference quotient (1.3) is defined only for \(\Delta x \neq 0\). We need not look for the value of the quotient at \(\Delta x = 0\), which does not exist, but for the number that the quotient approaches.

Lagrange wanted to found the calculus without this kind of approaching. He defined the rate of change of a function as the coefficient of \(h\) in the expansion of \(f(x + h)\) in powers of \(h\), but he used infinite sums without checking that these sums have a finite value at all, and his proof of the expansion has serious defects (CAJORI, PDF p. 273). The solution came from Cauchy, who in 1821, in his textbook Cours d'analyse, improved the foundations of the differential calculus by a precise treatment of what the quantities approach and by his new theory of functions without jumps (section 1.12) (CAJORI, PDF p. 385).

We look for the number that the difference quotient (1.3) approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero, and for this we must say precisely what approaching means. What does it mean that some function \(g(h)\) approaches a number \(d\) as \(h\) approaches zero? It means that \(g(h)\) is as close to \(d\) as we wish, provided \(h\) is close enough to zero. Set an allowed error, say \(0{,}001\). Is there a band around zero so narrow that for every \(h\) in the band, except \(h = 0\), the value \(g(h)\) differs from \(d\) by less than \(0{,}001\)? If there is one for the error \(0{,}001\), for the error \(0{,}000001\), and for every other error we can think of, then \(g(h)\) really approaches \(d\). For a smaller error the band may be narrower, but it must exist.

We write this exactly. We call the allowed error \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta) and the distance the band reaches on each side of zero \(\delta\) (the small Greek letter delta). Unlike the capital letter \(\Delta\), the small letter \(\delta\) does not mean a change. In mathematics books the allowed error is usually written \(\varepsilon\). For the permitted error, Karl Weierstrass began to use the letter \(\varepsilon\) in his early papers, as Cauchy had already done in 1821 (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 280). The definition 1.5 of the limit, with a permitted error and a strip around the point, comes from this tradition. We keep \(\varepsilon\) for strain.

Definition 1.5 Limit of a function (limita funkcije)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit (limita) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d , \tag{1.4}\]

if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3 (PDF p. 114), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < |h|\) leaves out the point \(h = 0\), where \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient. The limit tells what \(g\) approaches, not what it is at the point itself. The number \(r\) makes sure that the points the condition talks about really are in the domain. How? The condition talks about the points \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). If \(\delta \le r\), every such point is also among the points with \(0 < |h| < r\), where \(g\) is defined by assumption, so the value \(g(h)\) exists. And we can always take the band that narrow. If the condition holds for some \(\delta\), it also holds for every smaller \(\delta\), because a narrower band only checks some of the same points. So we may always take \(\delta \le r\). The number \(r\) ensures one more thing: every band around \(0\), however narrow, contains points where \(g\) is defined. We will need this in step 4 of the proof below that the limit is unique, where we must actually pick such a point.

We have defined the limit only at the point \(0\), because the difference quotient needs it only there. This is a narrowing of a more general notion. The limit of a function \(f\) at any point \(c\) is defined in the same way, with the distance \(|x - c|\) between \(x\) and \(c\) in place of \(|h|\) (LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3). LEBL1 also allows functions that are not defined everywhere near \(c\), only at some points that come arbitrarily close to \(c\). The definition uses only distances, \(|g(h) - d|\) and \(|h|\), so it works wherever we can measure distances, for example between points of space. A more general form is the limit of a function between sets in which a distance between the elements is given, called metric spaces (LEBL1 Def. 7.5.15).

Let us try the definition on the simplest example. Let \(g(h) = 5 - h\). We look for its limit at the point \(0\), that is, the number that \(g(h)\) approaches as \(h\) approaches zero. We expect it to be \(5\), since for small \(h\) the value \(5 - h\) is close to \(5\). The function \(g\) is defined for every \(h\), so all the points the condition talks about are in the domain, however wide the band is.

First we write the definition out for this case, with \(g(h) = 5 - h\) and \(d = 5\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|(5 - h) - 5| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds. The fives cancel, \((5 - h) - 5 = -h\), so \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |{-h}|\).
  3. The numbers \(-h\) and \(h\) are equally far from zero, so \(|{-h}| = |h|\). So the condition we must reach is \(|h| < \eta\).
  4. We choose the band so that this condition holds: we take \(\delta = \eta\). It is a positive number, as the definition requires.
  5. Check. For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(|h| < \delta = \eta\), so by steps 2 and 3, \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |h| < \eta\).
  6. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of \(g\) at the point \(0\) is \(5\), \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} (5 - h) = 5\).

For the error \(0{,}001\) the band is \(0{,}001\) wide on each side, for the error \(0{,}000001\) only \(0{,}000001\). A smaller allowed error needs a narrower band, as we expected.

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Otherwise the word "limit" could mean two different numbers. Let us check that this is so.

The idea is simple. If \(g(h)\) approached two numbers at once, then for small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) would be close to both, and so the two numbers would be close to each other. But only equal numbers can be closer to each other than every error.

The step "so the two numbers would be close to each other" needs a rule about distances between numbers. The distance between the numbers \(p\) and \(q\) is \(|p - q|\) (introduction of section 1.3). If we go from the number \(p\) to the number \(s\) by way of a third number \(q\), we cover the distance \(|p - q| + |q - s|\), and this is not smaller than the distance \(|p - s|\) that we cover going straight. As an equation:

\[|p - s| \le |p - q| + |q - s| .\]

Let us test it on the numbers of figure 1.17. For \(p = 1\), \(q = 3\) and \(s = 4\) the number \(q\) lies between \(p\) and \(s\), and the two sides are equal: \(|1 - 4| = 3\) and \(|1 - 3| + |3 - 4| = 2 + 1 = 3\). For \(q = 6\) the number \(q\) lies outside, the path through \(q\) covers the stretch from \(4\) to \(6\) twice, and it is longer: \(|1 - 6| + |6 - 4| = 5 + 2 = 7 > 3\). Since \(p - s = (p - q) + (q - s)\), the inequality is a special case of the inequality \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) for the numbers \(a = p - q\) and \(b = q - s\). We prove that inequality in the next lemma. Along with it we prove the rule for how the absolute value behaves for a product of two numbers. We will need it whenever we compare a distance multiplied by a number with an allowed error.

Lemma 1.6 Triangle inequality and the absolute value of a product (trikotniška neenakost in absolutna vrednost zmnožka)

For any real numbers \(a\) and \(b\):

  1. \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\), the triangle inequality for numbers (trikotniška neenakost za števila),
  2. \(|a\,b| = |a|\,|b|\), the absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values.
Proof

We have to show both items. We use only the definition of the absolute value, \(|t| = t\) for \(t \ge 0\) and \(|t| = -t\) for \(t < 0\) (introduction of section 1.3), and the school rules for inequalities.

1. Triangle inequality.

  1. Let \(t \ge 0\). Then \(|t| = t\), so \(t \le |t|\) and \(-|t| = -t \le 0 \le t\). So the number \(t\) lies between \(-|t|\) and \(|t|\): \(-|t| \le t \le |t|\).
  2. Let \(t < 0\). Then \(|t| = -t\), so \(-|t| = t\) and \(t < 0 < -t = |t|\). Here too \(-|t| \le t \le |t|\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, used for \(t = a\) and for \(t = b\), we have \(-|a| \le a \le |a|\) and \(-|b| \le b \le |b|\).
  4. We add the inequalities of step 3, the left sides together and the right sides together. The sum of two non-strict inequalities in the same direction is again such an inequality, so \(-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|\).
  5. Let \(c = |a| + |b|\). If \(a + b \ge 0\), then \(|a + b| = a + b\), and by the right inequality of step 4, \(|a + b| \le c\).
  6. If \(a + b < 0\), then \(|a + b| = -(a + b)\). We multiply the left inequality of step 4, \(-c \le a + b\), by \(-1\), which reverses it: \(-(a + b) \le c\). So here too \(|a + b| \le c\).
  7. The sum \(a + b\) is either nonnegative or negative, so by steps 5 and 6 we always have \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\).

2. Absolute value of a product.

  1. For every number \(t \le 0\) we have \(|t| = -t\). For \(t < 0\) the definition says so, for \(t = 0\) both sides are zero.
  2. Let \(a \ge 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). A product of nonnegative numbers is nonnegative, so \(|a\,b| = a\,b = |a|\,|b|\).
  3. Let \(a < 0\) and \(b < 0\). Then \(-a\) and \(-b\) are positive, and \(a\,b = (-a)(-b) > 0\). So \(|a\,b| = (-a)(-b) = |a|\,|b|\).
  4. Let \(a < 0\) and \(b \ge 0\). Then \((-a)\,b \ge 0\), so \(a\,b = -\big((-a)\,b\big) \le 0\). By step 1, \(|a\,b| = -a\,b = (-a)\,b = |a|\,|b|\).
  5. Let \(a \ge 0\) and \(b < 0\). As in step 4, with the roles of the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) exchanged, \(a\,b \le 0\) and \(|a\,b| = a\,(-b) = |a|\,|b|\).
  6. Every pair of numbers \(a\), \(b\) falls under one of the cases of steps 2 to 5, so always \(|a\,b| = |a|\,|b|\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 1.3.1, items (iii), (v) and (vi), and Prop. 1.3.2 (PDF pp. 36-37). The proof follows the source.

Both rules are simple, but we will use them in almost every estimate in the book. The first says that a sum of two errors is, in size, at most the sum of their sizes. The second says that we may take a constant factor out of the absolute value, as in \(|a\,\Delta x| = a\,|\Delta x|\) with a positive number \(a\). The name triangle inequality comes from geometry: in a triangle no side is longer than the sum of the other two. We prove the same inequality for points of space in chapter 2 (section 2.3). Now we can prove that the limit is unique.

Lemma 1.7 Uniqueness of the limit (enoličnost limite)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). Then \(g\) has at most one limit at the point \(0\).

Proof

We have to show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) that both satisfy the definition of the limit are equal.

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \(d_1\) is a limit, the definition gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a band \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We take half the error because in step 6 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \(d_2\) is a limit, for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a band \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\).
  4. Take an \(h\) that is nonzero and smaller than both bands, for example half of the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\). For this \(h\) both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold.
  5. Write the difference \(d_1 - d_2\) through the value \(g(h)\), which we know: \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - g(h)) + (g(h) - d_2)\).
  6. The distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) is not larger than the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\) plus the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). This is the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1), for \(a = d_1 - g(h)\) and \(b = g(h) - d_2\):
    \[|d_1 - d_2| \le |d_1 - g(h)| + |g(h) - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  7. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2|\) is smaller than every positive number.
  8. A nonnegative number that is smaller than every positive number is zero: if it were positive, it would be smaller than itself. So \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\) and \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.1.4 (PDF pp. 114-115).

The proof showed a pattern that we will meet many times: when we want a sum of two errors to be small, we allow each of them half.

So far the function \(g\) was defined on both sides of the point \(0\). For the bar this does not hold everywhere. The displacement of the bar is defined only for \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\). At the top, at \(x = 0\), the difference quotient of the displacement is therefore defined only for \(\Delta x > 0\), since the point \(x + \Delta x\) must lie on the bar. At the bottom end, at \(x = L\), it is defined only for \(\Delta x < 0\). Such a function does not satisfy the condition \(0 < |h| < r\) of the definition of the limit 1.5. So we also write the definition with a band that reaches to one side of zero only.

Definition 1.8 One-sided limit (enostranska limita)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit from the right (limita z desne) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} g(h) = d\), if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\). If \(g\) is defined for all \(h\) with \(-r < h < 0\), the limit from the left (limita z leve) is defined in the same way, only the condition holds for every \(h\) with \(-\delta < h < 0\), and we write \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^-} g(h) = d\). Both are called one-sided limits (enostranski limiti).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.16 (PDF p. 119), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The sign \(^+\) says that \(h\) approaches zero from the positive side, the sign \(^-\) from the negative side. A limit from the right is unique too, because the proof of lemma 1.7 on the uniqueness of the limit works without change: the \(h\) of step 4, half of the smallest of the positive numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\), is positive. For a limit from the left we take \(-h\). If a function is defined on both sides of zero and has the limit \(d\) there, it also has both one-sided limits, equal to \(d\), since the condition for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) contains both one-sided conditions.

1.3.4 Derivative

Now we can say what the number is that the strain of the piece approached in the table.

Definition 1.9 Derivative (odvod)

Let the function \(f\) be defined for all numbers whose distance from \(x\) is less than some number \(r > 0\). The function \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(x\) if the difference quotient (1.3) has a limit as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. This limit is the derivative (odvod) of the function \(f\) at the point \(x\):

\[f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.5}\]

If \(x\) is the smallest number in the domain of \(f\) and \(f\) is defined for all numbers \(t\) with \(x \le t < x + r\), the derivative at the point \(x\) is the one-sided limit of the difference quotient from the right as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero (definition 1.8). If \(x\) is the largest number in the domain and \(f\) is defined for all numbers \(t\) with \(x - r < t \le x\), the derivative is the limit from the left. Such a derivative is called a one-sided derivative (enostranski odvod).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 4.1.1 (PDF p. 155), which also allows a point at the end of the domain. The notation with \(\Delta x\) follows BARBER (1.35)-(1.36), where it is \(\delta x\).

So the derivative is a rate of change: by how much \(f\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when the change is very small. We read \(f'(x)\) as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)". In books you will also find the derivative written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}\). This notation recalls the quotient \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) from which the derivative comes. The notation is Leibniz's (CAJORI, PDF p. 227).

This is the derivative of a function whose input is one number, \(x\), and whose output is one number, \(f(x)\), like the displacement \(u(x)\) of the bar. For now this is the only kind we need. In a body, however, a point is given by three numbers, which say where it lies, and its displacement is given by three numbers too, which say how far it moves in each of three directions. There we will look at how one of the output numbers changes when only one of the three input numbers changes and the other two stay fixed. We will define such a derivative in section 1.7.2 (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8). A more general derivative, however, is no longer a single number. Take a function whose input is a group of \(n\) numbers and whose output is a group of \(m\) numbers. For the displacement in a body \(n = 3\) and \(m = 3\): the input is three numbers that give the position of the point, the output three numbers that give its displacement. The derivative of such a function at a point is a rule that tells, for every small change of the \(n\) input numbers, approximately how much each of the \(m\) output numbers changes (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.1). We will need such a derivative of the displacement when we describe strain in space.

On the graph the derivative has a clear picture. As \(\Delta x\) gets smaller, the point of the graph above \(x + \Delta x\) moves along the graph towards the point above \(x\), and the lines through the two points turn towards the line that has the slope \(f'(x)\) and passes through the point of the graph above \(x\) (figure 1.18). This line is called the tangent (tangenta) of the graph at the point \(x\). The slopes of the lines through two points are difference quotients, and their limit, the derivative, is the slope of the tangent.

Let us compute the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a > 0\) and \(L\) are given numbers (for the steel bar \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\)). The way has two parts. First we simplify the difference quotient so that \(\Delta x\), which we must not set to zero, disappears from the denominator. Then we find the limit of the simplified expression from the definition.

  1. The value at \(x + \Delta x\): \(u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2)\).
  2. Expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). So
    \[u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x - x^2 - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  3. Subtract \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) = a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  4. All three terms in the bracket have the factor \(\Delta x\), so we take it out: \(a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  5. Divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
    \[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]

The first part of the way is done. Before we take the limit, let us see what the equation of step 5 tells us. It holds for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), but its two sides behave differently at \(\Delta x = 0\).

On the left side the numerator would be \(u(x + 0) - u(x) = u(x) - u(x) = 0\), and the denominator \(0\):

\[\frac{u(x + 0) - u(x)}{0} = \frac{0}{0} .\]

This is not a number. The quotient \(c/b\) is the number that gives \(c\) when multiplied by \(b\). For \(0/0\) it would have to be a number that gives \(0\) when multiplied by \(0\). Every number does that, so there is no single number we could call \(0/0\). The left side has no value at \(\Delta x = 0\), and that is why we could not shrink the piece to nothing in section 1.2.

On the right side there is no division any more. It disappeared in step 5, when the factor \(\Delta x\) cancelled. So we may put \(\Delta x = 0\) into the right side:

\[a\,(2L - 2x - 0) = a\,(2L - 2x) .\]

This is an ordinary number, which we can compute for every point \(x\).

Now we split the right side of step 5 into this number and the rest. We multiply the bracket by \(a\) and group the first two terms again: \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\). So the strain of the piece is

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x . \tag{1.6}\]

The first term does not depend on the length \(\Delta x\) of the piece. It is the same for long and short pieces. The second term, \(a\,\Delta x\), is all that separates the quotient from the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and it is proportional to the length of the piece: a piece ten times shorter is ten times closer to the number. Because \(a\,\Delta x\) is subtracted, the strains of pieces with a positive \(\Delta x\) are smaller than the number and grow towards it as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller.

This explains the table of section 1.2, where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(L = 10\) m. At the top, at \(x = 0\), the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) is \(a \cdot 20\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(a \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). These are the two numbers at which the columns of the table settled. For a piece 1 m long the difference is \(a\,\Delta x = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and indeed \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} - 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6} = 3{,}7088 \cdot 10^{-6}\), rounded to four digits \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the first number in the table. In every following row the piece is ten times shorter, so the difference is ten times smaller: \(0{,}1952\), \(0{,}01952\), \(0{,}001952\) and \(0{,}0001952\), each times \(10^{-6}\). The table shows four digits, and the last one stands for \(0{,}001 \cdot 10^{-6}\). In the last row the difference is too small to show in the rounded last digit, so the table shows \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) itself. So the numbers settle when the difference falls below the last digit shown.

The difference \(a\,\Delta x\) does not contain \(x\), so it is the same at the top and at the middle. The table shows this too: in each row both numbers lie equally far below their final values, for example \(1{,}952 - 1{,}757 = 0{,}195\) at the middle and \(3{,}904 - 3{,}709 = 0{,}195\) at the top. The difference comes from the term \(-a\,\Delta x^2\) of step 2, the part of the square \((x + \Delta x)^2\) that does not contain \(x\). After the division by \(\Delta x\) in step 5 it becomes \(-a\,\Delta x\).

So the difference \(a\,\Delta x\) goes to zero as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, and we expect the limit to be the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). But a table, as we said in section 1.2, shows only a few rows. The equation (1.6) has exactly the form of the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\) of section 1.3.3: the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) stands in place of \(5\), the change \(\Delta x\) in place of \(h\), and the subtracted term has an extra factor \(a\). So we show the limit with the same steps as there. Because of the factor \(a\), the band will be \(\delta = \eta / a\) instead of \(\delta = \eta\).

  1. Let us show from the definition of the limit 1.5 that the limit of the quotient is \(d = a\,(2L - 2x)\). The definition, written out for the quotient of step 5 as a function of the change \(\Delta x\) (which plays the role of \(h\)) and for this number \(d\), demands: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) - a\,(2L - 2x)| < \eta\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\). The formula \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) is defined for every number \(x\), not only for the points of the bar between \(0\) and \(L\), so the quotient of step 5 is defined for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), and all the points the condition talks about are in the domain. What this means for the displacement of the bar, which is defined only for the points of the bar, we say in section 1.4.
  2. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  3. Simplify the difference. By the equation (1.6), \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\), so the difference is \(a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x - a\,(2L - 2x) = -a\,\Delta x\).
  4. The numbers \(-a\,\Delta x\) and \(a\,\Delta x\) are equally far from zero, and since \(a > 0\), \(|a\,\Delta x| = a\,|\Delta x|\). So \(|{-a\,\Delta x}| = a\,|\Delta x|\), and the condition we must reach is \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\).
  5. We divide the inequality by the positive number \(a\), so it does not turn around: the condition holds exactly when \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\). So we take the band \(\delta = \eta / a\), which is positive.
  6. Check. For every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\) we have \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\), so \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\), and by steps 8 and 9 this is the difference between the quotient and \(d\).
  7. We picked the error freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of the quotient is \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and
    \[u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

Step 5 was the heart of the computation. As \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, both the numerator \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) and the denominator \(\Delta x\) go to zero. If only one of them did, the outcome would be clear. A numerator going to zero over a fixed denominator gives quotients that go to zero. A fixed nonzero numerator over a denominator going to zero gives quotients whose size grows beyond every bound. When both go to zero together, the quotient can approach an ordinary number, and which number depends on how fast the numerator shrinks compared with the denominator. Step 4 showed how fast: the numerator is \(\Delta x\) times \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\), and the factor that remains approaches \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). When this number is not zero, the numerator therefore shrinks as fast as \(\Delta x\) itself. At the bottom end, at \(x = L\), however, \(a\,(2L - 2x) = 0\), the factor that remains is \(-a\,\Delta x\) and the numerator \(-a\,\Delta x^2\). There the numerator shrinks faster than the denominator, and the quotient \(-a\,\Delta x\) goes to zero. In the hanging bar the numerator and the denominator are the elongation and the length of the piece (figure 1.19). At the top of the bar the piece of 1 m stretches by \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m, the piece of 0,001 m only by \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m. The elongation of a piece is its length times its strain from the table:

\[3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{m} \cdot 3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6} , \qquad 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 0{,}001\ \text{m} \cdot 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

The lengths differ by a factor of 1000, the strains only a little, so the elongation of the long piece is \(1000 \cdot 3{,}709 / 3{,}904 = 950\) times larger, almost a thousand times. The strains differ a little because the long piece reaches from the top down to \(x = 1\) m, and lower down the material is stretched less. By step 12, very short pieces at \(x = 1\) m have strains close to \(a\,(2L - 2x) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 18\ \text{m} = 3{,}514 \cdot 10^{-6}\), less than \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the top. The strain of the long piece, \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), lies between the two, exactly halfway, \((3{,}904 + 3{,}514)/2 = 3{,}709\), because the strain falls evenly along the piece. The shorter the piece, the less its strain differs from the one at the top, and the closer the ratio of the elongations comes to the ratio of the lengths. The elongation and the length vanish together, and their ratio, the strain, approaches a definite number. With patient algebra we cancelled the common factor \(\Delta x\) in step 5 and turned the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and after that the limit was easy. We will compute derivatives this way later as well, until we derive rules in section 1.14.2 that do this work once and for all.

On the graph of the displacement the same limit is the turning of the lines through two points of the graph into the tangent (figure 1.18).
