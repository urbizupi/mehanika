Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.3 Strains caused by temperature

Hooke's law of section 3.2 tells us what strains the stresses cause in an isotropic linear elastic material. One of its assumptions was that the temperature of the material does not change. But at the beginning of chapter 2 we wrote that a body also deforms when it is heated (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). In this section we drop that assumption.

The goal of the section is the relation between strains, stresses and the change of temperature. Two facts will follow from it that everyone who builds structures must take into account. A body that nothing holds back and that we heat evenly deforms, but there is no stress in it. A body that cannot expand freely, on the other hand, gets stresses, although no force acts on it apart from its supports. Throughout the section the material is isotropic, linear elastic and homogeneous (definitions 3.10, 3.9 and 3.11), and the strains are small, below \(0{,}001\) in size as before. What we assume about the temperature we will say before we write the relation down.

The route is as follows. First we heat a bar that nothing holds back, and from its elongation we get a number that tells how much the material expands when heated. We will show that an isotropic material changes only its size when heated, not its shape. Then we add the strain caused by temperature to the strain caused by stresses. At the end we use the relation on a bar between two walls, on a bar with a gap, on a cube in a rigid box and on a plate that is warmer in the middle than at the rim.

3.3.1 Free heating

Take a steel bar with length \(L = 10\) m and diameter \(d = 10\) mm, like the hanging bar of chapter 1, only this time lying horizontally on rollers (figure 3.72). The rollers stand close together and carry the weight of the bar, while along its axis the bar can move freely, because the rollers turn. We neglect the friction in the rollers. We may, because its effect would be small: even if the friction force along the bar were as large as its whole weight, \(60{,}10\) N from section 1.12.2, in the cross section of area \(78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) it would cause a stress of \(0{,}765\) MPa and a strain of \(3{,}9 \cdot 10^{-6}\), less than 1 % of the strain we will get from heating. What we must not neglect is anything that holds the bar by its ends, and that case comes in section 3.3.3. Let the axis \(x\) point along the bar. The weight and the forces of the rollers act perpendicular to the axis, so they do not lengthen the bar. At 20 °C there is no stress in the bar. We heat it to 50 °C, so slowly that at every moment it is equally warm at all its points. If it were warmer outside than in the middle, the warmer parts would want to expand more than the cooler ones, the parts would hold each other back and stresses would appear in the bar. Such a case is the plate at the end of this section.

We measure changes of temperature in kelvins, K. A change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1 °C, so we have heated the bar by \(\Delta T = 30\) K. The Greek letter \(\Delta\) again means a change, and \(T\) is the temperature. For computing strains only the change of temperature matters, not the temperature itself (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

The bar gets longer. By how much? For mild steel, the same steel for which we took Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa and the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa, BOWER gives the number \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). It says that steel heated by 1 K gets longer by 15 millionths of its length. For changes of temperature that are not too large the elongation is proportional to the change of temperature (FELIPPA (4.16), PDF p. 58), so heated by 30 K the bar gets longer by thirty times that fraction. Every centimetre of the bar is equally warm and nothing holds it back, so every centimetre gets longer by the same fraction of its length, just as every centimetre of the evenly pulled bar in section 1.1. The strain of the bar is therefore

\[\varepsilon = 30 \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6} = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

and its elongation, by (1.1),

\[\Delta L = \varepsilon\,L = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 4{,}5\ \text{mm} .\]

The diameter grows too, by the same fraction, by \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 4{,}5\) µm. When heated, the material expands in all directions, not only along the bar (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). Why by exactly the same fraction as the length, we will explain a little further down.

What stress is there in the heated bar (figure 3.72)? None. Let us cut the bar in thought with a cross section. We think the left part away and in the bottom row of the figure draw only the part to the right of the cut with all the forces that act on it. These are its weight \(W_1\), the forces of the rollers \(R\) and the force \(N\) with which the left part acts on it across the cut. The weight points down and the forces of the rollers up, both perpendicular to the axis \(x\), so they have no component \(x\). The component \(x\) of the sum of all forces on the right part is therefore just the component \(x\) of the force across the cut, and since the part is in equilibrium (definition 2.3), it is zero. This component is the axial force (definition 2.5), so \(N = 0\) and the stress is \(\sigma = N/A = 0\). This is something new. Until now every strain had its cause in forces, and this one does not.

How large is this strain compared with the strains caused by forces? If we pulled the same bar at constant temperature with the force of 10 kN as in chapter 2, the stress in it would be 127,3 MPa, and the strain, by definition 3.1,

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{N}{EA} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

We got the same number in section 3.1.2. Heating by 30 K gives \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}/(6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4}) = 0{,}69\), that is 69 % of this strain. So strains caused by temperature are as large as the strains caused by ordinary loads, and we must not neglect them.

But the heated bar is not just a pulled bar. A pulled bar gets longer and at the same time thinner. Its diameter decreases by \(\nu\) times the strain along the axis, where \(\nu = 0{,}3\) is Poisson's ratio of steel (definition 3.8). Let us look at this on a small square piece of the bar with side \(a\), seen from the side (figure 3.73). We draw the piece after loading from the same lower left corner as the piece before it, so that the whole change of a side shows at one end. If we lengthened the bar by 4,5 mm by pulling along the axis \(x\), that is by the strain \(\varepsilon = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the side of the piece along the axis would get longer by \(\varepsilon a\) and the side across it shorter by \(\nu\,\varepsilon a\). The diameter of the bar would therefore decrease by \(0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 1{,}35\) µm. In the heated piece the side along the axis gets equally longer, and the side across it also by \(\varepsilon a\), so the diameter of the heated bar grows by 4,5 µm. Pulling changes the shape of the piece, it turns the square into a rectangle. Heating, as we will show, changes only its size.

Now we can say exactly what we have measured. We measure the change of temperature from a temperature at which there is no stress in the material (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379), for our bar from 20 °C. We measure the strain on a piece that nothing holds back, such as the heated piece of figure 3.73.

Definition 3.21 Thermal strain (temperaturna deformacija)

Let a small piece of material around the point \(P\) be free of stress at the temperature \(T_0\). This temperature is called the reference temperature (referenčna temperatura). We heat the piece to the temperature \(T\), that is by \(\Delta T = T - T_0\), while no force acts on it and nothing holds it back. When it is cooled, \(\Delta T < 0\). The thermal strain (temperaturna deformacija) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) is the strain that a short fibre of the piece with direction \(\vek n\) gets in this process. When the thermal strain is the same in all directions, the coefficient of thermal expansion (temperaturni razteznostni koeficient) of the material is the number

\[\alpha = \frac{\text{thermal strain}}{\Delta T} . \tag{3.30}\]

Source: SADD (4.4.2), (4.4.3) (PDF p. 98), FELIPPA (4.16), (4.17) (PDF p. 58), BOWER §3.2.1-3.2.2 (PDF pp. 96-98).

So the coefficient of thermal expansion is the thermal strain per kelvin of temperature change. A strain is a number without a unit, so \(\alpha\) has the unit 1/K. From (3.30) the thermal strain equals

\[\alpha\,\Delta T ,\]

for our bar \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 30\ \text{K} = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), as above, which gives the elongation \(\Delta L\) \(= 4{,}5\) mm. On cooling \(\Delta T\) is negative, so is the thermal strain, and the material shrinks: the orange square of figure 3.73 would then be smaller than the dashed one.

The definition does not claim that \(\alpha\) is the same number for all changes of temperature (figure 3.74, the horizontal axis carries \(\Delta T\), the vertical one the thermal strain). FELIPPA assumes that the thermal strain is proportional to \(\Delta T\) and says that this is justified as long as the changes of temperature are so small that \(\alpha\) is nearly constant over the range of temperatures considered, and as long as the material undergoes no phase change (fazni prehod), that is no change of its structure such as melting (FELIPPA, footnote 3, PDF p. 58). We will assume the same: for a given material \(\alpha\) is a constant number. For large changes of temperature, however, the thermal strain has to be added up over small steps of temperature, each with its own value of \(\alpha\) (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

Some values from BOWER (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100): for aluminium and its alloys \(22 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, for concrete \(10 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, for silica glass \(0{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K and for natural rubber \(200 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K. For different steels its table gives values from \(11 \cdot 10^{-6}\) to \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, while its text gives only \(6 \cdot 10^{-6}\) to \(10 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K for steel (BOWER §3.2.6, PDF p. 102). SADD gives \(13{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/°C for steel (Table 4.2, PDF p. 97), which is the same unit as 1/K. So the values depend on the kind of material and are only approximate. To give the numbers a meaning, let us compare them on our bar, 10 m long and heated by 30 K. The steel one gets longer by \(4{,}5\) mm, an aluminium one would by \(6{,}6\) mm, a concrete one by \(3\) mm, a bar of silica glass by only \(0{,}15\) mm, and a rubber one by \(60\) mm. In this section we compute with the value for mild steel, \(\alpha = 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K.

Why did the diameter of the bar grow by the same fraction as its length? The material is isotropic, so it behaves the same in all directions (definition 3.10). That it also behaves the same in all directions when heated is part of this assumption (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96, SADD §4.4, PDF p. 98). The temperature at a point is a single number and has no direction of its own. In an isotropic material nothing therefore distinguishes one fibre from another, and all fibres through the point get the same thermal strain. If one fibre got longer by a larger fraction than another, the material would have a preferred direction, and that is exactly what isotropy rules out. In the left part of figure 3.76 three such fibres are drawn from the centre of the piece, along the axis \(x\), along the axis \(y\) and along the diagonal between them. Each is drawn blackwhite before heating, and blue is the bit by which it got longer. All three blue bits are equally long. BOWER describes this by saying that an isotropic and homogeneous specimen heated evenly simply increases in size, without changing its shape (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). What this means for the strain components is said by the next lemma. In it \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) is the strain of a short fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\), as given for small strains by (1.61) of section 1.17.

Lemma 3.22 Equal strain in all directions (enaka deformacija v vseh smereh)

Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) be the components of the small strain tensor at the point \(P\), and for every unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) (direction of a fibre) let

\[\varepsilon(\vek n) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

Let \(c\) be a number. Then \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\) for every unit vector \(\vek n\) if and only if

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c \quad\text{and}\quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]
Proof

We have to prove both directions of the statement.

1. Such components give the same strain in all directions.

  1. Into the formula for \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) we insert \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c\) and zero components with different indices. What remains is \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\,(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\).
  2. By (1.10) the sum in the brackets is the square of the length of the vector \(\vek n\), and this is \(1\), because \(\vek n\) is a unit vector. So \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\). Every fibre, the fibres in the left part of the figure too, gets longer by the same fraction \(c\).

2. The same strain in all directions gives such components.

  1. Take \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\), the fibre along the axis \(x\). All terms with \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero, and the formula gives \(\varepsilon(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). By assumption \(\varepsilon(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = c\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c\). In the same way \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), the fibre along the axis \(y\), and \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) give \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = c\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c\).
  2. Take \(\vek n = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\), the fibre along the diagonal between the axes \(x\) and \(y\). It is a unit vector, because \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + 0 = 1\). Its components are \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{y}} = 1/\sqrt2\), so \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} = \tfrac12\), and \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The formula gives
    \[\varepsilon(\vek n) = \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
  3. By step 1, \(\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \tfrac12\,c + \tfrac12\,c = c\), and by assumption \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\). The equality of step 2 therefore gives \(c = c + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The equality of step 2 says what the shear component measures: the excess of the strain of the diagonal fibre over the average of the strains of the fibres along the axes. When all three blue bits are equally long, there is no excess.
  4. We think of the same picture in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(z\) and the plane of the axes \(y\) and \(z\) (figure 3.75). In the same way \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\), the diagonal fibre in the plane \(xz\), gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) and \(\vek n = (0, 1, 1)/\sqrt2\), the diagonal fibre in the plane \(yz\), gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\).

In the plane \(xy\) there is the diagonal fibre of step 2, and in the planes \(xz\) and \(yz\) there are the diagonal fibres that we take in this step. In all of them the extension equals the extension of the fibres along the axes, so there are no shear components.

Source: The lemma and its proof are not from the sources, they follow from (1.61). The consequence for the thermal strain is written by SADD (4.4.3) (PDF p. 98) and BOWER (PDF p. 97).

The proof has shown why the same strain in all directions rules out shear. When the fibre along the diagonal between the axes \(x\) and \(y\) is stretched as much as the fibres along the axes, nothing is left for the shear component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). By lemma 3.22 on equal strain in all directions, the thermal strain of an isotropic material has the components

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 ,\]
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} ,\]

so its matrix is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), where \(\mat I\) is the identity matrix (definition 1.108). Since there are no shear components, the right angles between fibres do not change on heating, and the piece keeps its shape (figure 3.76, left). SADD stresses exactly this, that in an isotropic material a change of temperature causes no shear strains (SADD (4.4.3), PDF p. 98). In the lectures the change of temperature is written simply \(T\), and the thermal strain \(\varepsilon^T\).

In a material that is not isotropic this is not so. Take a material with fibres that expands more along its fibres than across them. A square whose sides are not parallel to the fibres becomes a rhombus on heating, and the right angle at its corner decreases (figure 3.76, right). The circle drawn in it becomes an ellipse, elongated along the fibres. Let us look at the same three short fibres of the piece as on the left. The diagonal one lies along the fibres of the material, and its blue bit is longer than the blue bits of the fibres along the axes \(x\) and \(y\). By the equality of step 2 in the second part of the proof of lemma 3.22, which holds for every strain, this excess is the shear component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). We also see what it means: the fibres along the axes turn towards the diagonal, they are drawn orange after heating, and the angle between them is no longer a right angle. So heating changes its shape too, and in the axes \(x\) and \(y\) a shear strain appears, although no force acts on it. For such a material the thermal strain has different values in different directions. For a material with different properties in three perpendicular directions EP writes three different coefficients (EP §2.4.4, p. 70), and in general the thermal strain is described by a table of numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (SADD (4.4.2), PDF p. 98, CM (6.8.21), PDF p. 285). Materials that are not the same in all directions will be treated in section 3.4, thermal strains in this book only for isotropic ones.

How much does the volume grow on heating? Take a cube with edge \(h = 10\) mm \(= 10\,000\) µm of the same steel and heat it by 30 K with nothing holding it back (figure 3.77). The axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) point along its edges. On the right of the figure is the cube after heating, and we leave the corner \(A\) in place. Every edge gets longer by the fraction \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), that is by \(h\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5\) µm to \(h\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)\) \(= 10\,004{,}5\) µm. The right angles stay right angles, so the heated cube is again a cube. On its front face the front face of the cube before heating is drawn in blackwhite.

Let us cut what the cube has gained where the cube before heating ends. We get three kinds of pieces. On its right face lies a plate with a face \(h\) by \(h\) and the thickness \(h\,\alpha\,\Delta T\). An equal plate lies on the top face, and a third on the back face, which we do not see from this side. Along the edges where two plates meet lie three thin sticks of length \(h\) with a square cross section of side \(h\,\alpha\,\Delta T\). At the corner opposite \(A\) a tiny cube with edge \(h\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) is left. We will recognise these pieces in the computation.

  1. The volume of the heated cube is the third power of its edge, \(V_1 = h^3\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^3\).
  2. We expand the third power. The square \((1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^2\) is \(1 + 2\alpha\,\Delta T + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^2\), as in the expansion of a square in section 1.6.2. When we multiply it by \(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T\), we multiply every term of the first bracket by every term of the second and get
    \[(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^3 = 1 + 3\alpha\,\Delta T + 3(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^3 .\]
    Every term, multiplied by \(h^3\), is the volume of one kind of pieces in figure 3.77. The term \(h^3\) is the cube before heating. The term \(3h^3\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) is the three plates, each of volume \(h \cdot h \cdot h\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 0{,}45\ \text{mm}^3\). The term \(3h^3\,(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2\) is the three sticks, each of volume \(h \cdot (h\,\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 = 2{,}025 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{mm}^3\). The term \(h^3\,(\alpha\,\Delta T)^3\) is the tiny cube of volume \(9{,}1 \cdot 10^{-8}\ \text{mm}^3\).
  3. The relative change of volume is therefore the sum of the contributions of the plates, the sticks and the tiny cube, divided by the volume of the cube before heating,
    \[\begin{aligned} \frac{V_1 - h^3}{h^3} &= 3\alpha\,\Delta T + 3(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^3 \\ &= 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} + 6{,}075 \cdot 10^{-7} + 9{,}1 \cdot 10^{-11} . \end{aligned}\]
  4. The second term is the first term multiplied by \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so it is more than two thousand times smaller than the first: a stick is as many times thinner than a plate as the thickness of the plate is smaller than the edge \(h\). The third term, the tiny cube, is smaller still. We leave both out. The error this makes is \(0{,}045\) % of the computed change of volume, far below the accuracy to which we know \(\alpha\). We may leave them out only while \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) is small. In the figure, where for clarity the edge is drawn \(1{,}2\) times longer, the three plates would give \(0{,}6\), while the true value \(1{,}2^3 - 1 = 0{,}728\) is larger by a fifth. For our heating we get
    \[\frac{V_1 - h^3}{h^3} \approx 3\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} .\]

The volume grows three times as much as the length of an edge, because the cube expands in three directions and each direction contributes its own plate. The rule of section 1.17 gives the same: for small strains the relative change of volume of a small piece is the sum \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), here \(\alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T\).

3.3.2 Hooke's law with temperature

So far we have heated material that nothing held back, and there was no stress in it. But what if we heat a small piece and load it at the same time? Let us picture this in two steps, which we draw in figure 3.78.

Take a small square piece of the steel of the previous section with the side \(h = 10\) mm at the reference temperature \(T_0\). We look at it from the front, the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(y\) upwards. In the first step we heat it freely by \(\Delta T = 30\) K. It gets the thermal strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in every direction, so every side gets longer by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,h = 4{,}5\) µm, but there is no stress. We draw the piece before heating grey dashed, so that the elongation can be seen. In the second step we pull it at the new temperature along the axis \(x\) with the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 88{,}2\) MPa. By Hooke's law the stress adds a further strain, which we call the elastic part of the strain (elastični del deformacije). Along the axis \(x\) the piece gets longer by a further \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/E = 4{,}5\) µm, and across it gets thinner by \(\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/E = 1{,}35\) µm. The final piece is longer by the sum of the two parts along the axis \(x\), \(4{,}5 + 4{,}5 = 9\) µm, and across by \(4{,}5 - 1{,}35 = 3{,}15\) µm. The total strain is the sum of the two parts (SADD (4.4.1), PDF p. 98, FELIPPA (4.18), PDF p. 58).

Why may we add the two parts? Look at the bottom side of the final piece and draw it as a segment, in three rows one below the other (figure 3.79).

Before heating the side has length \(h\), and we draw its material grey. The first step adds the extension \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,h\), which we draw blue, and so multiplies its length by \(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T\). The second step stretches the whole side that is already longer, the grey and the blue part, by the same fraction \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), so it multiplies the length by a further \(1 + \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\). At the end the side has length

\[h\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)\Big(1 + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E}\Big) = h\,\Big(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T + \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E}\Big) .\]

The second and third terms in the brackets are the thermal and the elastic part, which figure 3.78 also shows, together \(9 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The last term is their product, the extension of the blue part in the second step. It arises because the second step stretches the side that is already longer, not the original one. In the figure the fractions are drawn as \(0{,}25\), so the product is \(0{,}25 \cdot 0{,}25 = 0{,}0625\), an eighth of the sum \(0{,}5\). In steel its size is \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = 2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\), which is \(0{,}02\ \%\) of the sum, so on 9 µm about \(0{,}002\) µm. When we drop it, the two parts add.

The same holds for all components at once. The components of small strain are sums of partial derivatives of the displacements, for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\) (section 1.17). Let us draw the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the points on one line in the direction of the axis \(x\) as a graph (figure 3.80).

The horizontal axis carries the position \(x\) of a point on the line, the vertical axis its displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\). We draw the displacement in the first step grey dashed and the displacement in the second step blue. The displacement after both steps is their sum: at every point \(x\) we add the two heights. A partial derivative is the derivative of a function of one variable (definition 1.26), in the graph the slope of the tangent at the point \(x_0\), that is its rise divided by the horizontal distance \(\ell\). The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives (lemma 1.82 on the rules of differentiation): the rise of the orange tangent is the rise of the grey and the rise of the blue one, stacked on each other. So every strain component is the sum of the corresponding components of the two steps. Strictly, the displacements of the second step should be measured from the position after the first step, but this differs from the position before heating only by strains of order \(10^{-4}\). The error is the product we have just computed, and for small strains we neglect it, as throughout chapter 1. EP sums this up in one sentence: when the strains, the changes of geometry and the changes of temperature are small enough, all equations are linear, so the effects of forces and of temperature may be added (EP §2.4.4, p. 70).

From history

Jean Marie Constant Duhamel (1797 to 1872, CAJORI, PDF p. 399) wrote in a work published in 1837 that Poisson knew that the constants connecting stress and strain change with temperature. Poisson thought it better to neglect this, except for very large temperature differences and where extreme accuracy is needed, because he did not have enough experiments to determine the law of the change. Duhamel agreed that within wide enough limits all such quotients may be taken as constant (TP1, PDF p. 484). In 1838, in a memoir, he took Poisson's theory of elasticity and investigated how the equations change when the temperature changes (TP1, PDF p. 480). Like Franz Neumann (TIMO, PDF p. 194) later, he limited the equations to the range in which the extension is proportional to the rise of temperature (TP2B, PDF p. 16). Both treated a cylinder and a sphere in which the temperature depends only on the distance from the axis or from the centre (TIMO, PDF p. 286). Neumann in 1841 claimed priority for the same equations, but Todhunter and Pearson in their history of elasticity judge that in a doubt about priority publication decides, and this is Duhamel's (TP2B, PDF p. 16).

For a heated and loaded piece we also accept Poisson's decision that the constants do not depend on temperature, as the second of the assumptions below says.

We will derive the relation under the following assumptions. For each we say why we may make it, when it fails and what it does to the result.

  1. The material is isotropic, linear elastic and homogeneous. So Hooke's law, equations (3.27), holds for the elastic part of the strain, and by lemma 3.22 on equal strain in all directions the thermal strain has no shear components: the heated piece stays a square. For a material with fibres this does not hold (figure 3.76, right). There a table of numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{ij}}\) would stand in place of the one number \(\alpha\), and the temperature would cause shear strains as well.
  2. Young's modulus \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) and the coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\) do not depend on the temperature over the range of temperatures considered. So in the second step the same \(E\) and \(\nu\) hold as at the reference temperature. EP assumes the same (EP §2.4.4, p. 70). What does this do to the result? For a given stress the elastic part of the strain is inversely proportional to \(E\). The modulus of soft rolled steel falls by \(0{,}028\ \%\) per kelvin on heating, as measurements between about \(-19\) °C and \(19\) °C show (TP2A, PDF p. 547, there \(0{,}000348\) per degree of the Réaumur scale, which is \(1{,}25\) K). For our heating by \(30\) K the modulus is thus smaller by \(0{,}84\ \%\), and the same stress \(88{,}2\) MPa elongates the piece by \(4{,}54\) µm instead of \(4{,}5\) µm. For heatings much larger than those of the measurements this number must not be extended. When the temperature changes very much, these quantities have to be taken as functions of the temperature (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66, for \(\alpha\)).
  3. At the reference temperature \(T_0\) there is no stress in the body (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). Real bodies may already have stresses from their manufacture, which are called residual stresses (zaostale napetosti). These have to be added to the stresses we compute (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).
  4. The temperature is known at every point of the body. The change of temperature \(\Delta T\) may differ from point to point, and the relation holds at each point separately with its own value of \(\Delta T\), for a small piece around that point. The strain also changes the temperature slightly, but SADD says that for most materials under static or slow loading this effect is small and may be neglected (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). So the temperature can be computed first, independently of the stresses, and then inserted into the relation. How large this effect is SADD does not say with a number, it only refers to other literature, and we do not give the error of this assumption as a number either. How the temperature is distributed through a body is treated in chapter 14.
  5. The strains are small, so the strains of the two steps add, as we have just shown. The error is the product of the two parts, with our numbers \(0{,}02\ \%\) of the sum. For parts of size \(0{,}1\), as in a strongly stretched rubber band, the product would already be \(5\ \%\) of the sum and could not be dropped.
Theorem 3.23 Hooke's law with temperature (Hookov zakon s temperaturo)

Let the elastic part of the strain (below) satisfy assumptions 1 to 3 of section 3.2.1 (elasticity, linearity and isotropy), let the strains be small as in assumption 4 of the same section, let the material have Young's modulus \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) and coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\) that do not depend on the temperature, and let its thermal strain be the same in all directions (isotropy also under heating). Let the material be free of stress at the reference temperature \(T_0\), and let \(\Delta T = T - T_0\) be the change of temperature at the point \(P\). Assume that every strain component at \(P\) is the sum of the corresponding components of two parts: the elastic part, which the stresses at \(P\) cause by equations (3.27) of Hooke's law with these \(E\) and \(\nu\), and the thermal strain, which the material around \(P\) would get under the change of temperature \(\Delta T\) if nothing held it back. Then the strain components and the stress components at \(P\) are related by the equations

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned} \tag{3.31}\]
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \frac{\nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \tag{3.31}\]

and, when \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), by the equations, in which the stress follows from the elastic part, the difference of the total and the thermal strain,

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned} \tag{3.32}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \tag{3.32}\]

where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants (definition 3.18). With the matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\) of the small strain tensor, the stress matrix \(\ten\sigma\), the identity matrix \(\mat I\) and the trace \(\tr\), the sum of the diagonal entries, the equations (3.32), in which the difference \(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) is the elastic part, read

\[\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I) .\]
Proof

First we write both parts of the strain and add them, then we express the stresses through the strains. Figure 3.78 shows both parts for the case in which only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) acts.

1. Strains from stresses.

  1. By the hypothesis of the theorem the thermal strain is the same in all directions, and by (3.30) it equals \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), in the figure the elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,h\) of every side. By lemma 3.22 on equal strain in all directions its components with equal indices are \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), and its components with different indices are zero.
  2. By the hypothesis of the theorem, equations (3.27), the elastic part has the components \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E\), \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E\), \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\big)/E\) and \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\), \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E\), \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/E\). Of these the figure shows the elongation \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/E\) along the axis \(x\) and the shortening \(\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,h/E\) across. The last three are the same as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/(2G)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/(2G)\) with the shear modulus \(G = E/\big(2(1 + \nu)\big)\) (definition 3.17).
  3. By the assumption of the theorem every strain component is the sum of the corresponding components from steps 1 and 2. Adding them, like the two elongations in the figure, gives (3.31).

2. Stresses from strains.

  1. By the assumption, the elastic part is the difference between the strain and the thermal strain: from the whole elongation we subtract the thermal one and the elastic one remains. By step 1 of item 1 its components are therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  2. Equations (3.27) relate the elastic part and the stresses, inverted to (3.28) in the proof of theorem 3.19: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(\lambda\) times the sum of the three diagonal components of the elastic part, plus \(2\mu\) times its component \(xx\). Insert step 1:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,\big((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) + (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) + (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T)\big) + 2\mu\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) .\]
  3. We collect the terms with \(\alpha\,\Delta T\). Three come from the first bracket, \(-3\lambda\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), and one from the second, \(-2\mu\,\alpha\,\Delta T\):
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We insert \(\lambda = E\nu/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) and \(\mu = E/\big(2(1 + \nu)\big)\) (definition 3.18):
    \[3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{3E\nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \frac{E}{1 + \nu} .\]
  5. We multiply the numerator and the denominator of the second fraction by \(1 - 2\nu\), so that the two fractions have the same denominator, and add the numerators:
    \[3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{E\,(3\nu + 1 - 2\nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{E\,(1 + \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} .\]
  6. We cancel \(1 + \nu\). This is allowed because \(\nu \neq -1\) by the hypothesis of the theorem. We get \(3\lambda + 2\mu = E/(1 - 2\nu)\), and with step 3 the first equation of the left column of (3.32). The condition \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\) is needed because we divide by \(1 - 2\nu\).
  7. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) we repeat steps 2 to 6, only in the second term we take the component \(yy\) or \(zz\) instead of \(xx\).
  8. By step 1 the shear components of the elastic part equal the shear components of the strain, because heating does not change angles: the heated piece stays a square. By (3.28) therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  9. The matrix form. The matrix \(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), the elastic part, has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) on the diagonal, and off the diagonal the same entries as \(\ten\varepsilon\), because \(\mat I\) has zeros off the diagonal. Its trace is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T\). At the place \(xx\) of the right side there is therefore the right side of the equation of step 2, and at the place \(xy\) there is \(0 + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), as in step 8. The same holds at the other places.

Source: SADD (4.4.1), (4.4.4), (4.4.6) (PDF p. 98), BOWER §3.2.2 (PDF pp. 97-98), EP §2.4.4 (p. 70), for the bar FELIPPA (4.18) (PDF p. 58). For a material that is not isotropic, CM (6.8.21) (PDF p. 285). The sources write the relation as a model, the proof from Hooke's law is not from the sources.

What does the theorem say? In the equations (3.32) the strain appears only in the difference \(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), that is in the elastic part, which figure 3.78 shows as the elongation and the shortening of the second step. The stresses do not depend on how much the material is deformed, but on how much its strain differs from the thermal strain it would get if nothing held it back. Where the strain equals the thermal strain, as in the freely heated piece, there is no stress. Where the material cannot reach its thermal strain, stresses appear. The shear equations are the same as at constant temperature, because the thermal strain of an isotropic material has no shear components.

Where does the number \(E/(1 - 2\nu)\) in the last term come from? It equals \(3K\), where \(K = E/\big(3(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) is the bulk modulus (definition 3.15). The term \(-3K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) has a simple meaning, which we draw in figure 3.81.

A freely heated piece increases its volume by the fraction \(3\alpha\,\Delta T\) compared with the piece before heating, as we saw with the cube (figure 3.77). The bulk modulus tells what equal pressure from all sides changes the volume by a given fraction: to squeeze the piece back to its original volume we need the pressure \(p = K \cdot 3\alpha\,\Delta T\). We will check this with numbers on the cube in a rigid box. For steel with \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(E/(1 - 2\nu) = 2{,}5\,E\). For a material with \(\nu\) just below \(\tfrac12\) this number is very large, at \(\nu = 0{,}499\) already \(500\,E\): a material that can hardly be compressed resists hindered heating with a very large pressure.

The equations of the theorem are written component by component. In books we meet the same law in three forms: strains from stresses, stresses from strains, and the law split into the change of volume and the change of shape, as we split it at constant temperature in (3.29). Let us collect all three and see how to get from one to the next. So that every step has numbers, take a piece of the same steel, with \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa and \(K = 163{,}3\) GPa, heated by \(\Delta T = 30\) K, so with \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). Its surroundings hold it back so that it has the normal strains

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 11{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 8{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\]

and no shear strains. What are the stresses in it? Let us draw it (figure 3.82).

We look at the piece from the front, the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(z\) upwards, and the axis \(y\) along the line of sight. On the left is the whole strain: grey dashed is the piece before heating, orange the piece after it. Under it are its three normal components in units of \(10^{-4}\), in the rows \(xx\), \(yy\) and \(zz\). We split this strain into three parts. The first is the thermal strain, which the piece would get if nothing held it back: \(4{,}5\) in every direction. The square stays a square, and this part causes no stress. What remains is the elastic part, and we split it further as in section 3.2.11. The sum of its normal components is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T = 22{,}5 - 13{,}5 = 9\), so the second part is the same change of size in all directions, \(\tfrac13 \cdot 9 = 3\). The third part is the deviatoric strain \(\ten e\) with the components \(4\), \(1\) and \(-5\), which add up to zero. The piece gets longer along \(x\) and shorter along \(z\), while its volume stays. In every row the sum of the three parts is the whole strain, in the first \(4{,}5 + 3 + 4 = 11{,}5\).

The figure already tells why the temperature enters the split law at one place only. The thermal strain is the same in all directions, so it changes only the size of the piece and joins the change of size entirely. There is none of it in the change of shape. At constant temperature the mean stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\) causes the whole change of volume, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\). Now it causes only the part that the temperature did not give for free, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T\).

How do we get the stresses from the strains? In the first form of the law, written below, the right side holds the stress matrix \(\ten\sigma\) and its trace \(\tr\ten\sigma\), so the stresses cannot be expressed by a single division. The following route helps. We add the three diagonal equations, in the figure the three rows of the table. We get one single equation in which only traces appear, that is ordinary numbers, since the trace of the identity matrix is the number \(3\). From it we express the unknown trace and insert it back into the original equation. We did the same in the proof of theorem 3.19. The route works for every law whose right side has a term with the matrix itself, a term with its trace times the identity matrix and possibly a known term times the identity matrix, like our \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\). The number in front of the matrix must not be zero, because we divide by it at the end. The number in front of the trace in the summed equation must not be zero either, because we divide by it when we express the trace. In our law these are the numbers \(1/(2G) = (1 + \nu)/E\) and \((1 - 2\nu)/E\), hence the conditions \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\). Every law of an isotropic material in this chapter is of this form.

Corollary 3.24 Three forms of Hooke's law with temperature (tri oblike Hookovega zakona s temperaturo)

Let the hypotheses of theorem 3.23 on Hooke's law with temperature hold, with \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\). At the point \(P\) let \(\ten\sigma\) be the stress matrix, \(\ten\varepsilon\) the matrix of the small strain tensor, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = \tfrac13\,\tr\ten\sigma\) the mean stress and \(\ten s = \ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I\) the stress deviator (definition 2.98), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \tr\ten\varepsilon\) the volumetric strain and \(\ten e = \ten\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I\) the deviatoric strain (definition 2.114). Let \(G\) be the shear modulus, \(K\) the bulk modulus, and \(\lambda\) and \(\mu = G\) the Lamé constants. Then the law holds at \(P\) in three forms. The first gives the strains from the stresses,

\[\ten\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2G}\,\ten\sigma - \frac{\nu}{E}\,(\tr\ten\sigma)\,\mat I + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I , \tag{3.33}\]
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac{1}{2G}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} - \frac{\nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \tag{3.33}\]

the second the stresses from the strains,

\[\ten\sigma = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon + \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I , \qquad 3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{E}{1 - 2\nu} = 3K , \tag{3.34}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \qquad 3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{E}{1 - 2\nu} = 3K , \tag{3.34}\]

and the third the change of volume and the change of shape separately,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T) , \qquad \ten s = 2G\,\ten e . \tag{3.35}\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{kk}} = \frac{E}{1 - 2\nu}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kk}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T) , \qquad s_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2G\,e_{\mathrm{ij}} . \tag{3.35}\]
Proof

We read the first form off the equations of the theorem. From it we get the trace of the stress, from the trace the second form, and from the second the third.

1. Strains from stresses.

  1. At the place \(xx\) of (3.33) there is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/(2G) - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), because \(\mat I\) has ones on the diagonal. By definition 3.17, \(G = E/\big(2(1 + \nu)\big)\), so \(1/(2G) = (1 + \nu)/E\). Insert:
    \[\frac{(1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]
    This is the first equation of (3.31). The same holds at the places \(yy\) and \(zz\).
  2. At the place \(xy\) there is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G) = (1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\), because \(\mat I\) has zeros off the diagonal. This is the shear equation of (3.31). The same holds at the places \(xz\) and \(yz\). There is no temperature here, because the heated piece stays a square.

2. The trace of the stress.

  1. We add the three diagonal places of equation (3.33), in the figure the rows \(xx\), \(yy\) and \(zz\). On the left we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\). On the right the sum of the diagonal places of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\) is its trace, and the sum of the diagonal places of the matrix \(\mat I\) is \(3\):
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \frac{1}{2G}\,\tr\ten\sigma - \frac{3\nu}{E}\,\tr\ten\sigma + 3\alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  2. We insert \(1/(2G) = (1 + \nu)/E\). The factor of \(\tr\ten\sigma\) is \((1 + \nu - 3\nu)/E = (1 - 2\nu)/E\):
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \frac{1 - 2\nu}{E}\,\tr\ten\sigma + 3\alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  3. We express the trace. We subtract \(3\alpha\,\Delta T\) on both sides and divide by \((1 - 2\nu)/E\). The division is allowed because \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\):
    \[\tr\ten\sigma = \frac{E}{1 - 2\nu}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T) .\]
  4. The mean stress is a third of the trace, and \(E/\big(3(1 - 2\nu)\big) = K\) by (3.20). So \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T)\), the first equation of (3.35). In the figure this is the column of the mean stress under the elastic change of size.

3. Stresses from strains.

  1. We multiply equation (3.33) by \(2G\) and express \(\ten\sigma\):
    \[\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon + \frac{2G\,\nu}{E}\,(\tr\ten\sigma)\,\mat I - 2G\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I .\]
  2. We insert the trace from step 3 of item 2. Since \(2G/E = 1/(1 + \nu)\), we get
    \[\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon + \frac{\nu\,E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T)\,\mat I - 2G\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I .\]
  3. The fraction is \(\lambda\) by (3.26), \(G\) is \(\mu\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) is \(\tr\ten\varepsilon\). We collect the terms with \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), \(-3\lambda\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) from the bracket and \(-2\mu\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) from the last term:
    \[\ten\sigma = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon + \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I .\]
  4. By steps 4 to 6 of item 2 of the proof of theorem 3.23, \(3\lambda + 2\mu = E/(1 - 2\nu)\), and this is \(3K\) by (3.20).

4. The change of shape.

  1. We add the diagonal places of the equation of step 3 of item 3. We get \(\tr\ten\sigma = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 3\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\,(3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), and a third of this is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = (\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\).
  2. The deviator is \(\ten s = \ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I\). We insert \(\ten\sigma\) from step 3 of item 3 and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\) from step 1:
    \[\ten s = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon + \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I - (\lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I + (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I .\]
  3. The two terms with \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) are equal with opposite signs and cancel. This is where the temperature disappears: in \(\ten\sigma\) it appears only multiplied by \(\mat I\), the same at all three diagonal places, and exactly such a part is subtracted with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I\). In the figure this is the column of the thermal strain with three equal numbers. The terms with \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) cancel as well. What remains is
    \[\ten s = 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon - \tfrac23\,\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I = 2\mu\,\big(\ten\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I\big) = 2G\,\ten e ,\]
    the second equation of (3.35), in the figure the column of the stress deviator under the change of shape.

Source: The first form SADD (4.4.4), the second SADD (4.4.6) (PDF p. 98). SADD does not write the third form in that section, we derived it from the second with the splits of definitions 2.98 and 2.114. The equations are checked with SymPy.

The proof used the same move twice, adding along the diagonal, and showed that \(\ten s = 2G\,\ten e\) is exactly the same equation as at constant temperature in (3.29). The temperature changes only the equation for the mean stress.

Let us now compute the stresses in our piece with the third form, column by column in figure 3.82.

  1. The mean stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T) = 163{,}3 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{Pa} \cdot 9 \cdot 10^{-4} = 147\) MPa, the same in all three rows.
  2. The stress deviator is \(2G = 150{,}77\) GPa times the deviatoric strain: \(s_{\mathrm{xx}} = 150{,}77 \cdot 10^{9}\ \text{Pa} \cdot 4 \cdot 10^{-4} = 60{,}3\) MPa, \(s_{\mathrm{yy}} = 15{,}1\) MPa and \(s_{\mathrm{zz}} = -75{,}4\) MPa (column).
  3. The stress is the sum of the two, the thermal part contributes nothing:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 147 + 60{,}3 = 207{,}3\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 147 + 15{,}1 = 162{,}1\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 147 - 75{,}4 = 71{,}6\ \text{MPa} .\]
  4. A check with the first form (3.33). The trace of the stress is \(3 \cdot 147 = 441\) MPa, so
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{207{,}3\ \text{MPa}}{150{,}77\ \text{GPa}} - \frac{0{,}3 \cdot 441\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} + 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = (13{,}75 - 6{,}75 + 4{,}5) \cdot 10^{-4} = 11{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]
    which is the strain we started with.

What would we get if we forgot the temperature and ascribed the whole volumetric strain to the stress? The mean stress would be \(K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) instead of \(K\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T)\), that is \(147\) MPa and another \(3K\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 490\ \text{GPa} \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = 220{,}5\) MPa, together \(367{,}5\) MPa. The difference \(220{,}5\) MPa is the term of the theorem, and it is larger than every stress in the piece. The deviator would stay correct.

In the lectures the change of temperature is written with the letter \(T\) where the book writes \(\Delta T = T - T_0\), the deviators are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}^{D}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^{D}\) where the book writes \(\ten s\) and \(\ten e\), and the equations are in index notation, which the button Index notation shows. There \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is an entry of the identity matrix, \(1\) for \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise, and the repeated index in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{kk}}\) means the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), that is the trace. Adding along the diagonal is there called multiplying by \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

When does the theorem fail? First, when the temperature changes so much that \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(\alpha\) are no longer constant, or when a phase change occurs. Second, when the stresses exceed the yield stress, because then Hooke's law does not hold. Third, when the body is loaded so fast that the effect of the strain on the temperature may no longer be neglected, since SADD states that this effect is small only for static and slow loading (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). Fourth, when the material is not isotropic and the heated piece does not stay a square.

3.3.3 Prevented expansion

A body that is heated uniformly and is not held back by anything has no stress. But what if something holds it back as it expands? Take the same steel bar as above, \(L = 10\) m long, with diameter \(d = 10\) mm and cross-section area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and place it between two walls (figure 3.84). At 20 °C its ends just touch them, without force. The axis \(x\) points along the bar. The model has three idealizations, which we have to name.

  • The walls are rigid: they do not move, whatever the force with which the bar pushes on them. A real wall gives a little, and then the stress is smaller, as we will see right after the bar with a gap.
  • The walls are smooth, so the ends of the bar can widen freely against them, just as everywhere else along the bar (figure 3.83, top). If the ends were glued on, the glue would hold the rim of each end back from widening, with forces towards the axis that cancel each other. By Saint-Venant's principle the state would therefore change only near the ends (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). How near, BOWER says: the effect of two force distributions with the same resultant and the same moment fades within a distance of about three times the size of the region they act on (BOWER §5.1.5, PDF p. 280), here the diameter \(d = 10\) mm. Only a piece about 3 cm long at each end would be different, 0,3 % of the length of the bar.
  • The bar stays straight. A long thin bar under compression can bend sideways. This is called buckling (uklon) (EP §6.4.7.1, p. 361), and FELIPPA names it as a danger in heated bars that cannot expand (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). We do not treat buckling here. We assume that the bar is guided along its whole length so that it cannot bend, and that the guides exert no force on it as long as it is straight. Without guides such a long thin bar would buckle when heated by a fraction of a kelvin. If its ends are free to turn, by GR (4.266) (PDF p. 293) it buckles already under a compressive force of about 9,5 N (computed with NumPy), while heating by 30 K will cause, as we compute below, a compressive force of 6927 N in it. The guides are therefore a strong idealization.

We heat the bar evenly by \(\Delta T = 30\) K.

  1. The walls do not let the bar get longer: the heated bar is as long as before heating, so its strain is \(\Delta L/L = 0\). All parts of the bar are equally warm and equally loaded, so, as for a bar pulled by an axial force, we assume that the state is the same at all points of the bar. Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at every point. For a bar between the walls that is warmer at one end this assumption would not hold. In figure 3.85 the temperature rise grows from 0 K at the left to 60 K at the right end. Because every part is in equilibrium, the stress would still be the same everywhere, but the strain would not: the warm part would get longer, the cool part shorter, and only the average of the strain along the length would be zero. In (3.36) the average of \(\Delta T\) along the bar, 30 K in the figure, would then stand in place of \(\Delta T\), and the stress would be the same as for uniform heating by 30 K.
  2. No force acts on the lateral surface of the bar, since the walls press only on its ends and the guides do not push a straight bar, so the lateral surface carries no load. As in a bar pulled by an axial force (section 2.6.6), the only nonzero stress component is therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all shear components are zero.
  3. With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the first equation of (3.31) reads
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We insert \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) from step 1 and express the stress:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T . \tag{3.36}\]
  5. In numbers:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \frac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 30\ \text{K} = -88{,}2 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} = -88{,}2\ \text{MPa} .\]
    The kelvins cancel, as they must, since the stress is in pascals.
  6. The axial force is
    \[N = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,A = -88{,}2\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = -6927\ \text{N} .\]
    Each wall pushes on the bar with a force of size 6,93 kN, and by the law of action and reaction the bar pushes each wall outward with a force of the same size. If we cut the bar in thought, each part pushes the other across the cut with the same force of size \(|N|\), because each part is in equilibrium.

The bar is compressed, although nothing compresses it except two walls that do not move. Stresses that arise because the thermal strain is prevented are called thermal stresses (temperaturne napetosti) (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). FELIPPA gives exactly this stress for a bar that cannot get longer (FELIPPA, PDF p. 58). COOK calls it an initial stress (začetna napetost) and describes it as the stress caused by a change of temperature when all displacements are prevented (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66). The stress of 88,2 MPa is 40 % of the yield stress of steel, 220 MPa. For ductile metals (duktilne kovine), metals that deform permanently a great deal before they fail, such as our steel, BOWER states that the stress-strain diagram in compression is a mirror image of the diagram in tension (BOWER §3.7.1, PDF p. 142). In compression steel therefore begins to yield at the stress \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\). The bar would begin to yield when heated by \(\Delta T = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}/(E\alpha) = 220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}/(196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K}) = 74{,}8\) K. And what if we cool the bar? Smooth walls can only push on the bar, so a cooled bar would shrink freely and move away from one wall, leaving a gap between itself and the wall like the one in figure 3.87 top left, and it would stay without stress. But if its ends are fastened to the walls and we cool it by 30 K, then \(\Delta T = -30\) K, and a tensile stress of \(+88{,}2\) MPa appears in it. FELIPPA warns that thermal stresses in members that cannot expand or contract can cause buckling or cracking (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59).

We get the same result in two steps, which show where the stress comes from (figure 3.86). At the reference temperature the bar, of length \(L\), just touches both walls. The left wall holds the left end in all the steps. First we remove the right wall in thought. The bar heats freely and gets longer by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L = 4{,}5\) mm, with no stress in it. Then we push its right end back until the bar is \(L\) long again. We have to shorten it by 4,5 mm, that is by the strain \(-4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). Whether we divide this length by 10 m or by the length of the heated bar, 10,0045 m, makes a difference of only 0,045 %, which for small strains we neglect. Since by assumption 2 of section 3.3.2 \(E\) does not depend on the temperature, by definition 3.1 we need the stress \(E \cdot (-4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}) = -88{,}2\) MPa, the same as above. A force of size \(|N|\) causes the stress \(|N|/A\), the strain \(|N|/(EA)\) and the shortening \(|N|\,L/(EA)\), which must equal the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\). From this equality \(|N| = E A\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 6927\) N, as in step 6.

The numbers contain a pattern that needs explaining. The stress \(-E\alpha\,\Delta T\) depends neither on the length of the bar nor on the area of its cross section. Why not on the length? A longer bar gets longer by more when free, but to shorten it back we need the strain \(-\alpha\,\Delta T\), however long it is. The walls prevent the strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), and the stress that cancels this strain is \(E\alpha\,\Delta T\). So a bar 1 m long between walls has, when heated by 30 K, the same stress as a bar 100 m long. Why not on the area? A thicker bar needs a larger force for the same shortening, but it also has a larger cross section to share the force, just as with the two thin bars glued into a thick one in section 2.2. So the force on the walls is proportional to the cross-section area, while the stress is not.

What about the thickness of the bar between the walls? With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the second equation of (3.31) reads

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\frac{\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]

We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\alpha\,\Delta T\) from (3.36):

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}3 \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = 5{,}85 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

The bottom row of figure 3.84 shows the round cross section of the bar, seen along its axis, as if the bar lay on a table. Before heating it has the diameter \(d = 10\) mm, which we draw grey dashed, after heating it is orange. Between the walls the diameter grows by \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,d = 5{,}85 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 5{,}85\) µm. In the freely heated bar it grows only by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,d = 4{,}5\) µm, so the growth between the walls is 30 % larger. Where does the extra come from? The walls compress the bar along its axis, and compressed material widens sideways, just as pulled material gets thinner. So the bar widens once because it is heated, by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), and once more because it is compressed, by \(\nu\,\alpha\,\Delta T\).

That is why expansion is often not prevented but given room. FELIPPA says that for this reason gaps are left in pavements, buildings and rails, called expansion joints (dilatacijske reže), and long bridges are supported on roller bearings that can move (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). Take the same bar, resting against the left wall, and let there be a gap \(g = 3\) mm between its right end and the right wall at 20 °C (figure 3.87, left). The walls are again rigid and smooth, and the bar is guided.

  1. As long as the gap is open, the bar expands freely and there is no stress in it.
  2. The gap closes when the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\) equals \(g\), that is at the heating \(\Delta T_1\) for which
    \[\Delta T_1 = \frac{g}{\alpha L} = \frac{3 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}}{15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 10\ \text{m}} = 20\ \text{K} .\]
  3. When it is heated further, the walls hold the bar at the length \(L + g\). Its strain is therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = g/L\), and by the first equation of (3.31) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)
    \[\frac{g}{L} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We express the stress and use \(g/L = \alpha\,\Delta T_1\) from step 2:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\Big(\alpha\,\Delta T - \frac{g}{L}\Big) = -E\alpha\,(\Delta T - \Delta T_1) .\]
  5. At \(\Delta T = 30\) K, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 10\ \text{K} = -29{,}4\) MPa, a third of the stress without a gap.

Figure 3.87, right, compares the stress with and without the gap. The gap delays the start of the compression by 20 K, after which the stress grows as fast as without the gap. The stress is determined not by the whole thermal strain, but only by the part of it that the obstacles really prevent, here \(\alpha\,\Delta T - g/L\).

A wall that gives a little also lowers the stress, but differently from a gap (figure 3.88). We put the bar without a gap against the rigid left wall again. The right wall, however, is now a plate held from behind by a spring fixed to a support that does not move. The stiffness \(k\) of the spring is the force per unit displacement: under a force of size \(|N|\) the wall moves back by \(|N|/k\), just as \(EA/L\) is the spring constant of the bar in section 3.1.2. We heat the bar by \(\Delta T\). The heated bar pushes the wall to the right and compresses the spring. Where does it stop?

  1. If nothing held the bar, its end would move from the original end to the orange dashed line, by the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\).
  2. The wall does not let the end go that far, it moves back only by \(|N|/k\). The rest up to the free end, \(|N|\,L/(EA)\), is the shortening that the force \(|N|\) causes in the bar, just as in the bottom row of figure 3.86. The retreat and the shortening together make the free elongation:
    \[\frac{|N|}{k} + \frac{|N|\,L}{EA} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,L .\]
  3. We express the force and with it the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -|N|/A\) (checked with SymPy):
    \[|N| = \frac{EA\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 + EA/(kL)} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 + EA/(kL)} .\]

The fraction \(EA/(kL)\) is the ratio of the stiffness of the bar \(EA/L\) to the stiffness of the wall \(k\). For a very stiff wall it is almost zero, and the stress approaches the stress with a rigid wall. For a very soft wall it is large, the wall moves back by almost the whole free elongation, and there is almost no stress. So the stress grows from the first kelvin on, only more slowly than with a rigid wall, while with the gap there is none at all up to 20 K. Take a wall as stiff as the bar, \(k = EA/L = 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/10\ \text{m} = 1{,}54 \cdot 10^6\) N/m. Then \(EA/(kL) = 1\) and the stress is half the stress with a rigid wall, the dashed line of the graph, so at 30 K it is \(-44{,}1\) MPa. Why exactly half? The bar and the wall are equally stiff springs that share the free elongation of 4,5 mm half and half: the wall moves back by 2,25 mm, and the bar is 2,25 mm shorter than it would be if free. Figure 3.88 shows exactly this case.

Between the walls the bar could at least widen sideways. What if we close off every direction for the material? We put a cube with edge 10 mm of the same steel into a rigid box with smooth walls that just touches it on all six sides at 20 °C, and heat it by 30 K (figure 3.90). Without the walls the cube would expand to the orange dashed square, but the walls prevent this. The axes \(x\) and \(z\) lie in the plane of the picture.

  1. The walls do not allow any edge to change, the cube stays as it was. All parts of the cube are equally warm, so as for the bar we assume that the state is the same at every point, and then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) at every point. In a cube heated unevenly only the mean strain would be zero, as in the bar that is warmer at one end. The faces of the cube stay against the flat walls and the right angles between them do not change, so the shear components of the strain are zero too.
  2. We insert the zero strains into (3.32). What remains is
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]
  3. In numbers \(E/(1 - 2\nu) = 196\ \text{GPa}/0{,}4 = 490\) GPa and
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -490 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = -220{,}5\ \text{MPa} .\]

So the walls press on all faces with the same pressure \(p = 220{,}5\) MPa (figure 3.90, right), 2,5 times more than the walls on the bar. Where does the factor \(1/(1 - 2\nu) = 1/0{,}4 = 2{,}5\) come from? We get the same from the first equation of (3.31). Since all three directions are alike, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) gives

\[0 = \frac{1}{E}\,(\sigma - 2\nu\,\sigma) + \alpha\,\Delta T = \frac{(1 - 2\nu)\,\sigma}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad \sigma = -\frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} .\]

The term \(-2\nu\,\sigma/E\) shows what has changed. With the bar, the walls compressed only along the axis \(x\). In the box the walls compress also in the directions \(y\) and \(z\). What such a pressure does by itself is shown in the lower part of figure 3.90. We press the cube only in the direction \(z\) with the pressure \(p\). It gets shorter in that direction and wider in the direction \(x\) by the strain \(\nu\,p/E\), just as a compressed bar widens sideways. The pressure in the direction \(y\) does the same. The walls perpendicular to the axis \(x\) have to prevent this widening too, so the stress in the direction \(x\) is larger. In numbers, with \(p/E = 220{,}5\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 11{,}25 \cdot 10^{-4}\), the three contributions to \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are (figure 3.89)

\[\underbrace{4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}}_{\alpha\,\Delta \mathrm{T}} + \underbrace{2 \cdot 0{,}3 \cdot 11{,}25 \cdot 10^{-4}}_{2\nu\,\mathrm{p}/\mathrm{E}} - \underbrace{11{,}25 \cdot 10^{-4}}_{\mathrm{p}/\mathrm{E}} = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} + 6{,}75 \cdot 10^{-4} - 11{,}25 \cdot 10^{-4} = 0 .\]

So the pressure in the direction \(x\) has to cancel the elongation from the heating and the elongation from the side pressures, which is half as large again, together 2,5 times as much as for the bar. The result agrees with the meaning of the term \(-3K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) from the previous subsection. The bulk modulus of steel is \(K = 196\ \text{GPa}/(3 \cdot 0{,}4) = 163{,}3\) GPa, the box prevents the relative change of volume \(3\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3}\), and \(K \cdot 3\alpha\,\Delta T = 163{,}3 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} = 220{,}5\) MPa. Does the cube yield under such a pressure? On every face through any point of the cube the stress vector is \(-p\,\vek n\), which lies on the normal of the face (figure 2.253), so it has no shear part. All three principal stresses are equal, \(-220{,}5\) MPa, so the maximum shear stress is \((\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)/2 = 0\) (theorem 2.93), and by the formula with the differences of the principal stresses also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 0\). By neither yield condition of section 2.12.6 does the steel yield, although the pressure is about equal to the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa. Experiments with metals agree: an equal pressure from all sides does not cause yielding (BOWER §3.7.3, PDF p. 146).

So far the expansion was prevented by something outside the body. But the obstacle can also be inside the body itself. Take a thin round steel plate that nothing holds and on which no force acts, and suppose that at some moment it is warmer in the middle than at the rim, for example because we are heating it in the middle (figure 3.91). We divide the plate in thought into an inner disc and an outer ring. If they were separate and nothing held them back, the warmer disc would expand more than the hole of the cooler ring and would no longer fit into it. The band between them would be occupied twice. In the plate, however, the disc and the ring are one piece. The ring holds the disc back and compresses it, and the disc pushes the ring outward and stretches it along its circumference. Stresses arise in the plate, although no force acts on it. SADD says that an uneven temperature in a body generally causes stresses, because the expansion is held back by the supports or by the neighbouring parts of the body (SADD, introduction to chapter 12, PDF p. 378).

A calculation on the same plate confirms that stresses must arise in such a body (figure 3.91). In section 1.18 we saw that strains coming from a displacement field satisfy six equations, otherwise the small pieces of the body do not fit together. There we derived them for displacement fields with continuous third partial derivatives, and we assume such smooth displacements for the plate too. The assumption holds as long as the plate stays whole and does not fold anywhere. At a crack the displacement would jump, and there the equation could not be used. One of them is

\[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} = 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} .\]
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx},\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy},\mathrm{xx}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}} .\]

We put the origin at the centre of the plate, the axes \(x\) and \(y\) in its plane, and let the change of temperature at that moment be \(\Delta T = \Delta T_0\,\big(1 - (x^2 + y^2)/R^2\big)\), where \(R\) is the radius of the plate and \(\Delta T_0\) the heating at the centre. The graph above the plate shows this heating along a diameter: in the middle \(\Delta T = \Delta T_0\), at the rim, at \(x = \pm R\), it is zero, and in between it falls along a parabola.

  1. If there were no stress in the plate, each small piece of it would expand as if it were alone, like the disc and the ring in the figure. By (3.31) the strain at every point would equal the thermal strain: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  2. The right side of the equation is then zero, and the left side is \(\alpha\,\big(\partial^2 \Delta T/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 \Delta T/\partial x^2\big)\).
  3. We differentiate. The first partial derivative of \(\Delta T\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-2\Delta T_0\,x/R^2\), the second \(-2\Delta T_0/R^2\), the same everywhere and negative, because the parabola curves downward equally everywhere. The same with respect to \(y\). The left side is therefore \(\alpha\,(-2\Delta T_0/R^2 - 2\Delta T_0/R^2) = -4\alpha\,\Delta T_0/R^2\), which is not zero.
  4. The thermal strains do not satisfy this equation, so they do not come from any such displacement field: the small pieces of the plate, each heated freely, would not fit together, just as the disc and the hole of the ring do not fit. The assumption of step 1 was therefore wrong, and there are stresses in the plate.

When do the pieces fit, then? If \(\Delta T\) is a linear function of the coordinates, of the form \(c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 y + c_3 z\) with constant numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_3\), all second derivatives of \(\Delta T\) are zero, and the equation holds. The other five hold too, since each contains only second derivatives of the strains, and with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\) and zero shear strains these are all zero. COOK states that in this case there are no thermal stresses in a body whose supports do not hold it back, if the material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic and \(\alpha\) does not depend on temperature. If \(\alpha\) changed with temperature, the thermal strain would no longer be a linear function of the coordinates (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66), and stresses could arise. A straight bar whose temperature increases linearly from its lower to its upper face, for example, bends into a circular arc, but there is no stress in it (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

Figure 3.92 shows why the pieces then fit. We heat a straight bar that nothing holds so that the heating increases evenly from the lower face to the upper, from zero to \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}}\), and cut it in thought into slices. We let every slice heat up by itself. At the bottom it keeps the width \(b\), at the top it widens to \(b\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{h}})\). In between the width increases evenly with the height, because the heating does, so the two sides stay straight and the slice becomes a trapezoid. The straight sides of neighbouring trapezoids fit along their whole height. So we can put the slices together into an arc without gaps and without overlaps, and no slice has to be compressed or stretched. The bar that was straight therefore bends and stays without stress. In the plate it was different. There the heating changed along a parabola, and the disc did not fit into the hole of the ring.

Let us sum up how we reached these conclusions. We got the thermal strain from a freely heated bar, which got longer without stress, and described it by the coefficient \(\alpha\). From isotropy and the formula for the strain of a fibre in any direction we showed with lemma 3.22 that the thermal strain has no shear components. We obtained Hooke's law with temperature by splitting the strain into the elastic part, for which Hooke's law holds, and the thermal strain. The examples then showed that stress arises only where the thermal strain is prevented: by the walls for the bar, by the walls of the box for the cube, or by the neighbouring material in the plate. For the bar the stress is \(E\) times the prevented strain, for the cube in the box the pressure is \(K\) times the prevented relative change of volume. All of this held for isotropic materials. Wood and materials with fibres behave differently in different directions, and the next section is devoted to such materials.
