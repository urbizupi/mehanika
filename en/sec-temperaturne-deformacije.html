Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.3 Strains caused by temperature

Hooke's law of section 3.2 tells us what strains the stresses cause in an isotropic linear elastic material. One of its assumptions was that the temperature of the material does not change. But at the beginning of chapter 2 we wrote that a body also deforms when it is heated (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). In this section we drop that assumption.

The goal of the section is the relation between strains, stresses and the change of temperature. Two facts will follow from it that everyone who builds structures must take into account. A body that nothing holds back and that we heat evenly deforms, but there is no stress in it. A body that cannot expand freely, on the other hand, gets stresses, although no force acts on it apart from its supports. Throughout the section the material is isotropic, linear elastic and homogeneous (definitions 3.4, 3.3 and 3.5), and the strains are small, below \(0{,}001\) in size as before. What we assume about the temperature we will say before we write the relation down.

The route is as follows. First we heat a bar that nothing holds back, and from its elongation we get a number that tells how much the material expands when heated. We will show that an isotropic material changes only its size when heated, not its shape. Then we add the strain caused by temperature to the strain caused by stresses. At the end we use the relation on a bar between two walls, on a bar with a gap, on a cube in a rigid box and on a plate that is warmer in the middle than at the rim.

3.3.1 Free heating

Take a steel bar with length \(L = 10\) m and diameter \(d = 10\) mm, like the hanging bar of chapter 1, only this time lying horizontally on rollers (figure 3.20). The rollers stand close together and carry the weight of the bar, while along its axis the bar can move freely, because the rollers turn. We neglect the friction in the rollers. Let the axis \(x\) point along the bar. The weight and the forces of the rollers act perpendicular to the axis, so they do not lengthen the bar. At 20 °C there is no stress in the bar. We heat it to 50 °C, so slowly that at every moment it is equally warm at all its points.

We measure changes of temperature in kelvins, K. A change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1 °C, so we have heated the bar by \(\Delta T = 30\) K. The Greek letter \(\Delta\) again means a change, and \(T\) is the temperature. For computing strains only the change of temperature matters, not the temperature itself (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

The bar gets longer. By how much? For mild steel, the same steel for which we took Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa and the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa, BOWER gives the number \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). It says that steel heated by 1 K gets longer by 15 millionths of its length. For changes of temperature that are not too large the elongation is proportional to the change of temperature (FELIPPA (4.16), PDF p. 58), so heated by 30 K the bar gets longer by thirty times that fraction. Every centimetre of the bar is equally warm and nothing holds it back, so every centimetre gets longer by the same fraction of its length, just as every centimetre of the evenly pulled bar in section 1.1. The strain of the bar is therefore

\[\varepsilon = 30 \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6} = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

and its elongation, by (1.1),

\[\Delta L = \varepsilon\,L = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 4{,}5\ \text{mm} .\]

The diameter grows too, by the same fraction, by \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 4{,}5\) µm. When heated, the material expands in all directions, not only along the bar (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). Why by exactly the same fraction as the length, we will explain a little further down.

What stress is there in the heated bar (figure 3.20)? None. Let us cut the bar in thought with a cross section. On the part to the right of the cut act its weight, the forces of the rollers and the force with which the left part acts on it across the cut. The weight and the forces of the rollers have no component \(x\). The component \(x\) of the sum of all forces on the right part is therefore just the component \(x\) of the force across the cut, and since the part is in equilibrium (definition 2.3), it is zero. This component is the axial force (definition 2.5), so \(N = 0\) and the stress is \(\sigma = N/A = 0\). This is something new. Until now every strain had its cause in forces, and this one does not.

How large is this strain compared with the strains caused by forces? If we pulled the same bar at constant temperature with the force of 10 kN as in chapter 2, the stress in it would be 127,3 MPa, and the strain, by definition 3.1,

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{N}{EA} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

We got the same number in section 3.1.2. Heating by 30 K gives \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}/(6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4}) = 0{,}69\), that is 69 % of this strain. So strains caused by temperature are as large as the strains caused by ordinary loads, and we must not neglect them.

But the heated bar is not just a pulled bar. A pulled bar gets longer and at the same time thinner. Its diameter decreases by \(\nu\) times the strain along the axis, where \(\nu = 0{,}3\) is Poisson's ratio of steel (definition 3.2). If we lengthened the bar by 4,5 mm by pulling, that is by the strain \(4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), its diameter would decrease by \(0{,}3 \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = 1{,}35\) µm. The heated bar gets equally longer, but its diameter grows by 4,5 µm (figure 3.21). Pulling changes the shape of a small piece of material, while heating, as we will show, changes only its size.

Now we can say exactly what we have measured. We measure the change of temperature from a temperature at which there is no stress in the material (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). We measure the strain on a piece that nothing holds back.

Definition 3.13 Thermal strain (temperaturna deformacija)

Let a small piece of material around the point \(P\) be free of stress at the temperature \(T_0\). This temperature is called the reference temperature (referenčna temperatura). We heat the piece to the temperature \(T\), that is by \(\Delta T = T - T_0\), while no force acts on it and nothing holds it back. When it is cooled, \(\Delta T < 0\). The thermal strain (temperaturna deformacija) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) is the strain that a short fibre of the piece with direction \(\vek n\) gets in this process. When the thermal strain is the same in all directions, the coefficient of thermal expansion (temperaturni razteznostni koeficient) of the material is the number

\[\alpha = \frac{\text{thermal strain}}{\Delta T} . \tag{3.15}\]

Source: SADD (4.4.2), (4.4.3) (PDF p. 98), FELIPPA (4.16), (4.17) (PDF p. 58), BOWER §3.2.1-3.2.2 (PDF pp. 96-98).

So the coefficient of thermal expansion is the thermal strain per kelvin of temperature change. A strain is a number without a unit, so \(\alpha\) has the unit 1/K. From (3.15) the thermal strain equals

\[\alpha\,\Delta T ,\]

for our bar \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 30\ \text{K} = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), as above. On cooling \(\Delta T\) is negative, so is the thermal strain, and the material shrinks.

The definition does not claim that \(\alpha\) is the same number for all changes of temperature. FELIPPA assumes that the thermal strain is proportional to \(\Delta T\) and says that this is justified as long as the changes of temperature are so small that \(\alpha\) is nearly constant over the range of temperatures considered, and as long as the material undergoes no phase change (fazni prehod), that is no change of its structure such as melting (FELIPPA, footnote 3, PDF p. 58). We will assume the same: for a given material \(\alpha\) is a constant number. For large changes of temperature, however, the thermal strain has to be added up over small steps of temperature, each with its own value of \(\alpha\) (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

Some values from BOWER (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100): for aluminium and its alloys \(22 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, for concrete \(10 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, for silica glass \(0{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K and for natural rubber \(200 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K. For different steels its table gives values from \(11 \cdot 10^{-6}\) to \(15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, while its text gives only \(6 \cdot 10^{-6}\) to \(10 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K for steel (BOWER §3.2.6, PDF p. 102). SADD gives \(13{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/°C for steel (Table 4.2, PDF p. 97), which is the same unit as 1/K. So the values depend on the kind of material and are only approximate. In this section we compute with the value for mild steel, \(\alpha = 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K.

Why did the diameter of the bar grow by the same fraction as its length? The material is isotropic, so it behaves the same in all directions (definition 3.4). That it also behaves the same in all directions when heated is part of this assumption (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96, SADD §4.4, PDF p. 98). The temperature at a point is a single number and has no direction of its own. In an isotropic material nothing therefore distinguishes one fibre from another, and all fibres through the point get the same thermal strain. BOWER describes this by saying that an isotropic and homogeneous specimen heated evenly simply increases in size, without changing its shape (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96). What this means for the strain components is said by the next lemma. In it \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) is the strain of a short fibre in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\), as given for small strains by (1.53) of section 1.17.

Lemma 3.14 Equal strain in all directions (enaka deformacija v vseh smereh)

Let \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) be the components of the small strain tensor at the point \(P\), and for every unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) let

\[\varepsilon(\vek n) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

Let \(c\) be a number. Then \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\) for every unit vector \(\vek n\) if and only if

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c \quad\text{and}\quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]
Proof

We have to prove both directions of the statement.

1. Such components give the same strain in all directions.

  1. Into the formula for \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) we insert \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c\) and zero components with different indices. What remains is \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\,(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\).
  2. By (1.10) the sum in the brackets is the square of the length of the vector \(\vek n\), and this is \(1\), because \(\vek n\) is a unit vector. So \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\).

2. The same strain in all directions gives such components.

  1. Take \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\). All terms with \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero, and the formula gives \(\varepsilon(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). By assumption \(\varepsilon(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = c\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c\). In the same way \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) give \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = c\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = c\).
  2. Take \(\vek n = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\). It is a unit vector, because \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + 0 = 1\). Its components are \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{y}} = 1/\sqrt2\), so \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} = \tfrac12\), and \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The formula gives
    \[\varepsilon(\vek n) = \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
  3. By step 1, \(\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \tfrac12\,c + \tfrac12\,c = c\), and by assumption \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = c\). The equality of step 2 therefore gives \(c = c + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  4. In the same way \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\) and \(\vek n = (0, 1, 1)/\sqrt2\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\).

Source: The lemma and its proof are not from the sources, they follow from (1.53). The consequence for the thermal strain is written by SADD (4.4.3) (PDF p. 98) and BOWER (PDF p. 97).

The proof has shown why the same strain in all directions rules out shear. When the fibre along the diagonal between the axes \(x\) and \(y\) is stretched as much as the fibres along the axes, nothing is left for the shear component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). By lemma 3.14 on equal strain in all directions, the thermal strain of an isotropic material has the components

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 ,\]

so its matrix is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), where \(\mat I\) is the identity matrix (definition 1.105). Since there are no shear components, the right angles between fibres do not change on heating, and the piece keeps its shape (figure 3.22, left). SADD stresses exactly this, that in an isotropic material a change of temperature causes no shear strains (SADD (4.4.3), PDF p. 98).

In a material that is not isotropic this is not so. Take a material with fibres that expands more along its fibres than across them. A square whose sides are not parallel to the fibres becomes a rhombus on heating, and the right angle at its corner decreases (figure 3.22, right). So heating changes its shape too, and in the axes \(x\) and \(y\) a shear strain appears, although no force acts on it. For such a material the thermal strain has different values in different directions. For a material with different properties in three perpendicular directions EP writes three different coefficients (EP §2.4.4, p. 70), and in general the thermal strain is described by a table of numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (SADD (4.4.2), PDF p. 98, CM (6.8.21), PDF p. 285). Materials that are not the same in all directions will be treated in section 3.4, thermal strains in this book only for isotropic ones.

How much does the volume grow on heating? Take a cube with edge \(h = 10\) mm \(= 10\,000\) µm of the same steel and heat it by 30 K with nothing holding it back (figure 3.23). Every edge gets longer by the fraction \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), that is to \(h\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T) = 10\,004{,}5\) µm. The right angles stay right angles, so the heated cube is again a cube.

  1. The volume of the heated cube is the third power of its edge, \(V_1 = h^3\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^3\).
  2. We expand the third power. The square \((1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^2\) is \(1 + 2\alpha\,\Delta T + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^2\), as in the expansion of a square in section 1.6.2. When we multiply it by \(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T\), we multiply every term of the first bracket by every term of the second and get
    \[(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^3 = 1 + 3\alpha\,\Delta T + 3(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^3 .\]
  3. The relative change of volume is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} \frac{V_1 - h^3}{h^3} &= 3\alpha\,\Delta T + 3(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^3 \\ &= 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} + 6{,}075 \cdot 10^{-7} + 9{,}1 \cdot 10^{-11} . \end{aligned}\]
  4. The second term is the first term multiplied by \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), so it is more than two thousand times smaller than the first. The third is smaller still. We leave both out and get
    \[\frac{V_1 - h^3}{h^3} \approx 3\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} .\]

The volume grows three times as much as the length of an edge, because the cube expands in three directions. The rule of section 1.17 gives the same: for small strains the relative change of volume of a small piece is the sum \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), here \(\alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T\).

3.3.2 Hooke's law with temperature

So far we have heated material that nothing held back, and there was no stress in it. But what if we heat a small piece and load it at the same time? Let us picture this in two steps. In the first step we heat the piece freely. It gets the thermal strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) in every direction, but no stress. In the second step we load it at the new temperature. By Hooke's law the stresses add a further strain, which we call the elastic part of the strain (elastični del deformacije). The total strain is the sum of the two parts (SADD (4.4.1), PDF p. 98, FELIPPA (4.18), PDF p. 58).

Why may we add the two parts? The components of small strain are sums of partial derivatives of the displacements, for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x)\) (section 1.17). The displacement after both steps is the sum of the displacement in the first step and the displacement in the second. A partial derivative is the derivative of a function of one variable (definition 1.25), and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives (lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation). So every strain component is the sum of the corresponding components of the two steps. Strictly, the displacements of the second step should be measured from the position after the first step, but this differs from the position before heating only by strains of order \(10^{-4}\), which for small strains we neglect, as throughout chapter 1. EP sums this up in one sentence: when the strains, the changes of geometry and the changes of temperature are small enough, all equations are linear, so the effects of forces and of temperature may be added (EP §2.4.4, p. 70).

From history

Jean Marie Constant Duhamel (1797 to 1872, CAJORI, PDF p. 399) wrote in a work published in 1837 that Poisson knew that the constants connecting stress and strain change with temperature. Poisson thought it better to neglect this, except for very large temperature differences and where extreme accuracy is needed, because he did not have enough experiments to determine the law of the change. Duhamel agreed that within wide enough limits all such quotients may be taken as constant (TP1, PDF p. 484). In 1838, in a memoir, he took Poisson's theory of elasticity and investigated how the equations change when the temperature changes (TP1, PDF p. 480). Like Franz Neumann (TIMO, PDF p. 194) later, he limited the equations to the range in which the extension is proportional to the rise of temperature (TP2B, PDF p. 16). Both treated a cylinder and a sphere in which the temperature depends only on the distance from the axis or from the centre (TIMO, PDF p. 286). Neumann in 1841 claimed priority for the same equations, but Todhunter and Pearson in their history of elasticity judge that in a doubt about priority publication decides, and this is Duhamel's (TP2B, PDF p. 16).

For a heated and loaded piece we also accept Poisson's decision that the constants do not depend on temperature, as the second of the assumptions below says.

We will derive the relation under the following assumptions.

  1. The material is isotropic, linear elastic and homogeneous. So Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) holds for the elastic part of the strain, and by lemma 3.14 on equal strain in all directions the thermal strain has no shear components.
  2. Young's modulus \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) and the coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\) do not depend on the temperature over the range of temperatures considered. So in the second step the same \(E\) and \(\nu\) hold as at the reference temperature. EP assumes the same (EP §2.4.4, p. 70). When the temperature changes very much, these quantities have to be taken as functions of the temperature (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66, for \(\alpha\)).
  3. At the reference temperature \(T_0\) there is no stress in the body (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). Real bodies may already have stresses from their manufacture, which are called residual stresses (zaostale napetosti). These have to be added to the stresses we compute (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).
  4. The temperature is known at every point of the body. The change of temperature \(\Delta T\) may differ from point to point, and the relation holds at each point separately with its own value of \(\Delta T\). The strain also changes the temperature slightly, but SADD says that for most materials under static or slow loading this effect is small and may be neglected (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). So the temperature can be computed first, independently of the stresses, and then inserted into the relation. How the temperature is distributed through a body is treated in chapter 14.
  5. The strains are small, so the strains of the two steps add, as we have just shown.
Theorem 3.15 Hooke's law with temperature (Hookov zakon s temperaturo)

Let the material satisfy assumptions 1 to 4 of section 3.2.1 (elasticity, linearity, isotropy and small strains), let it have Young's modulus \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) and coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\) that do not depend on the temperature, and let its thermal strain be the same in all directions (isotropy also under heating). Let the material be free of stress at the reference temperature \(T_0\), and let \(\Delta T = T - T_0\) be the change of temperature at the point \(P\). Assume that every strain component at \(P\) is the sum of the corresponding components of two parts: the elastic part, which the stresses at \(P\) cause by Hooke's law (theorem 3.11), and the thermal strain, which the material around \(P\) would get under the change of temperature \(\Delta T\) if nothing held it back. Then the strain components and the stress components at \(P\) are related by the equations

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{1}{E}\,\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\big) + \alpha\,\Delta T , & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned} \tag{3.16}\]

and, when \(\nu \neq -1\) and \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\), by the equations

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} &= 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned} \tag{3.17}\]

where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants (definition 3.10). With the matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\) of the small strain tensor, the stress matrix \(\ten\sigma\), the identity matrix \(\mat I\) and the trace \(\tr\), the sum of the diagonal entries, the equations (3.17) read

\[\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I) .\]
Proof

First we write both parts of the strain and add them, then we express the stresses through the strains.

1. Strains from stresses.

  1. By the hypothesis of the theorem the thermal strain is the same in all directions, and by (3.15) it equals \(\alpha\,\Delta T\). By lemma 3.14 on equal strain in all directions its components with equal indices are \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), and its components with different indices are zero.
  2. By theorem 3.11, equations (3.12), the elastic part has the components \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E\), \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\big)/E\), \(\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})\big)/E\) and \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/E\), \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/E\), \((1 + \nu)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/E\). The last three are the same as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/(2G)\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/(2G)\) with the shear modulus \(G = E/\big(2(1 + \nu)\big)\) (definition 3.9).
  3. By the assumption of the theorem every strain component is the sum of the corresponding components from steps 1 and 2. Adding them gives (3.16).

2. Stresses from strains.

  1. By the assumption, the elastic part is the difference between the strain and the thermal strain. By step 1 of item 1 its components are therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  2. The stresses are related to the elastic part by theorem 3.11, in the form (3.13): the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(\lambda\) times the sum of the three components of the elastic part with equal indices, plus \(2\mu\) times its component \(xx\). We insert the components of step 1:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,\big((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) + (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) + (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T)\big) + 2\mu\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T) .\]
  3. We collect the terms with \(\alpha\,\Delta T\). Three come from the first bracket, \(-3\lambda\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), and one from the second, \(-2\mu\,\alpha\,\Delta T\):
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We insert \(\lambda = E\nu/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) and \(\mu = E/\big(2(1 + \nu)\big)\) (definition 3.10):
    \[3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{3E\nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} + \frac{E}{1 + \nu} .\]
  5. We multiply the numerator and the denominator of the second fraction by \(1 - 2\nu\), so that the two fractions have the same denominator, and add the numerators:
    \[3\lambda + 2\mu = \frac{E\,(3\nu + 1 - 2\nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} = \frac{E\,(1 + \nu)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} .\]
  6. We cancel \(1 + \nu\). This is allowed because \(\nu \neq -1\) by the hypothesis of the theorem. We get \(3\lambda + 2\mu = E/(1 - 2\nu)\), and with step 3 the first equation of the left column of (3.17).
  7. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) we repeat steps 2 to 6, only in the second term we take the component \(yy\) or \(zz\) instead of \(xx\).
  8. By step 1 the shear components of the elastic part equal the shear components of the strain. By (3.13) therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).
  9. The matrix form. The matrix \(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\) has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) on the diagonal, and off the diagonal the same entries as \(\ten\varepsilon\), because \(\mat I\) has zeros off the diagonal. Its trace is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} - 3\alpha\,\Delta T\). At the place \(xx\) of the right side there is therefore the right side of the equation of step 2, and at the place \(xy\) there is \(0 + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), as in step 8. The same holds at the other places.

Source: SADD (4.4.1), (4.4.4), (4.4.6) (PDF p. 98), BOWER §3.2.2 (PDF pp. 97-98), EP §2.4.4 (p. 70), for the bar FELIPPA (4.18) (PDF p. 58). For a material that is not isotropic, CM (6.8.21) (PDF p. 285). The sources write the relation as a model, the proof from Hooke's law is not from the sources.

What does the theorem say? In the equations (3.17) the strain appears only in the difference \(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\mat I\), that is in the elastic part. The stresses do not depend on how much the material is deformed, but on how much its strain differs from the thermal strain it would get if nothing held it back. Where the strain equals the thermal strain, as in the freely heated bar, there is no stress. Where the material cannot reach its thermal strain, stresses appear. The shear equations are the same as at constant temperature, because the thermal strain of an isotropic material has no shear components.

Where does the number \(E/(1 - 2\nu)\) in the last term come from? It equals \(3K\), where \(K = E/\big(3(1 - 2\nu)\big)\) is the bulk modulus (definition 3.7). The term \(-3K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) has a simple meaning. A freely heated piece increases its volume by the fraction \(3\alpha\,\Delta T\), as we saw with the cube. The bulk modulus tells what equal pressure from all sides changes the volume by a given fraction: to squeeze the piece back to its original volume we need the pressure \(p = K \cdot 3\alpha\,\Delta T\). We will check this with numbers on the cube in a rigid box.

When does the theorem fail? First, when the temperature changes so much that \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(\alpha\) are no longer constant, or when a phase change occurs. Second, when the stresses exceed the yield stress, because then Hooke's law does not hold. Third, when the body is loaded so fast that the effect of the strain on the temperature may no longer be neglected, since SADD states that this effect is small only for static and slow loading (SADD §12.1, PDF p. 379). Fourth, when the material is not isotropic.

3.3.3 Prevented expansion

A freely heated body has no stress. But what if something holds it back as it expands? Take the same steel bar as above, \(L = 10\) m long, with diameter 10 mm and cross-section area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and place it between two walls (figure 3.24). At 20 °C its ends just touch them, without force. The axis \(x\) points along the bar. The model has three idealizations, which we have to name.

  • The walls are rigid: they do not move, whatever the force with which the bar pushes on them. A real wall gives a little, and then the stress is smaller, as we will see right after the bar with a gap.
  • The walls are smooth, so the ends of the bar can widen freely against them. If the ends were glued on, the state would by Saint-Venant's principle change only near the ends (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114).
  • The bar stays straight. A long thin bar under compression can bend sideways. This is called buckling (uklon) (EP §6.4.7.1, p. 361), and FELIPPA names it as a danger in heated bars that cannot expand (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). We do not treat buckling here. We assume that the bar is guided along its whole length so that it cannot bend, and that the guides exert no force on it as long as it is straight. Without guides such a long thin bar would buckle when heated by a fraction of a kelvin. If its ends are free to turn, by GR (4.266) (PDF p. 293) it buckles already under a compressive force of about 9,5 N (computed with NumPy), while heating by 30 K will cause, as we compute below, a compressive force of 6927 N in it. The guides are therefore a strong idealization.

We heat the bar evenly by \(\Delta T = 30\) K.

  1. The walls do not let the bar get longer, so its strain is \(\Delta L/L = 0\). All parts of the bar are equally warm and equally loaded, so, as for a bar pulled by an axial force, we assume that the state is the same at all points of the bar. Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at every point.
  2. The lateral surface of the bar carries no load. As in a bar pulled by an axial force (section 2.6.6), the only nonzero stress component is therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all shear components are zero.
  3. With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the first equation of (3.16) reads
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We insert \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) from step 1 and express the stress:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T . \tag{3.18}\]
  5. In numbers:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \frac{1}{\text{K}} \cdot 30\ \text{K} = -88{,}2 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} = -88{,}2\ \text{MPa} .\]
    The kelvins cancel, as they must, since the stress is in pascals.
  6. The axial force is
    \[N = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,A = -88{,}2\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = -6927\ \text{N} .\]
    Each wall pushes on the bar with a force of size 6,93 kN, and by the law of action and reaction the bar pushes each wall outward with a force of the same size.

The bar is compressed, although nothing compresses it except two walls that do not move. Stresses that arise because the thermal strain is prevented are called thermal stresses (temperaturne napetosti) (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). FELIPPA gives exactly this stress for a bar that cannot get longer (FELIPPA, PDF p. 58). COOK calls it an initial stress (začetna napetost) and describes it as the stress caused by a change of temperature when all displacements are prevented (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66). The stress of 88,2 MPa is 40 % of the yield stress of steel, 220 MPa. For ductile metals (duktilne kovine), metals that deform permanently a great deal before they fail, such as our steel, BOWER states that the stress-strain diagram in compression is a mirror image of the diagram in tension (BOWER §3.7.1, PDF p. 142). In compression steel therefore begins to yield at the stress \(-\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\). The bar would begin to yield when heated by \(\Delta T = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}/(E\alpha) = 220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}/(196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K}) = 74{,}8\) K. If we cool the bar between the walls by 30 K, then \(\Delta T = -30\) K, and a tensile stress of \(+88{,}2\) MPa appears in it. FELIPPA warns that thermal stresses in members that cannot expand or contract can cause buckling or cracking (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59).

We get the same result in two steps, which show where the stress comes from (figure 3.25). First we remove the right wall in thought. The bar heats freely and gets longer by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L = 4{,}5\) mm, with no stress in it. Then we push its right end back until the bar is \(L\) long again. We have to shorten it by 4,5 mm, that is by the strain \(-4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). Whether we divide this length by 10 m or by the length of the heated bar, 10,0045 m, makes a difference of only 0,045 %, which for small strains we neglect. Since \(E\) does not depend on the temperature, by definition 3.1 we need the stress \(E \cdot (-4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}) = -88{,}2\) MPa, the same as above. A force of size \(|N|\) causes the stress \(|N|/A\), the strain \(|N|/(EA)\) and the shortening \(|N|\,L/(EA)\), which must equal the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\). From this equality \(|N| = E A\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 6927\) N, as in step 6.

The numbers contain a pattern that needs explaining. The stress \(-E\alpha\,\Delta T\) depends neither on the length of the bar nor on the area of its cross section. Why not on the length? A longer bar gets longer by more when free, but to shorten it back we need the strain \(-\alpha\,\Delta T\), however long it is. The walls prevent the strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\), and the stress that cancels this strain is \(E\alpha\,\Delta T\). So a bar 1 m long between walls has, when heated by 30 K, the same stress as a bar 100 m long. Why not on the area? A thicker bar needs a larger force for the same shortening, but it also has a larger cross section to share the force, just as with the two thin bars glued into a thick one in section 2.2. So the force on the walls is proportional to the cross-section area, while the stress is not.

What about the thickness of the bar between the walls? With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the second equation of (3.16) reads

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\frac{\nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]

We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\alpha\,\Delta T\) from (3.18):

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \nu\,\alpha\,\Delta T + \alpha\,\Delta T = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}3 \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = 5{,}85 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

The diameter grows by 5,85 µm, 30 % more than in the freely heated bar. Where does the extra come from? The walls compress the bar along its axis, and compressed material widens sideways, just as pulled material gets thinner. So the bar widens once because it is heated and once more because it is compressed.

That is why expansion is often not prevented but given room. FELIPPA says that for this reason gaps are left in pavements, buildings and rails, called expansion joints (dilatacijske reže), and long bridges are supported on roller bearings that can move (FELIPPA, PDF p. 59). Take the same bar, resting against the left wall, and let there be a gap \(g = 3\) mm between its right end and the right wall at 20 °C (figure 3.26, left). The walls are again rigid and smooth, and the bar is guided.

  1. As long as the gap is open, the bar expands freely and there is no stress in it.
  2. The gap closes when the free elongation \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,L\) equals \(g\), that is at
    \[\Delta T_1 = \frac{g}{\alpha L} = \frac{3 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}}{15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 10\ \text{m}} = 20\ \text{K} .\]
  3. When heated further, the walls hold the bar at the length \(L + g\). Its strain is therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = g/L\), and by the first equation of (3.16) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)
    \[\frac{g}{L} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]
  4. We express the stress and use \(g/L = \alpha\,\Delta T_1\) from step 2:
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\Big(\alpha\,\Delta T - \frac{g}{L}\Big) = -E\alpha\,(\Delta T - \Delta T_1) .\]
  5. At \(\Delta T = 30\) K, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{1/K} \cdot 10\ \text{K} = -29{,}4\) MPa, a third of the stress without a gap.

Figure 3.26, right, compares the stress with and without the gap. The gap delays the start of the compression by 20 K, after which the stress grows as fast as without the gap. The stress is determined not by the whole thermal strain, but only by the part of it that the obstacles really prevent, here \(\alpha\,\Delta T - g/L\). A wall that gives a little also lowers the stress, but differently from a gap. Suppose the right wall moves back by \(|N|/k\) under a force of size \(|N|\), where \(k\) is its stiffness, the force per unit displacement, just as \(EA/L\) is the spring constant of the bar in section 3.1.2. By the first equation of (3.16) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the bar lengthens by \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E + \alpha\,\Delta T)\,L\), and this must equal the retreat of the wall, \(|N|/k = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} A/k\). From this equality \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\alpha\,\Delta T/\big(1 + EA/(kL)\big)\) (checked with SymPy). So the stress grows from the first kelvin on, only more slowly than with a rigid wall, which it approaches when \(k\) is very large.

Between the walls the bar could at least widen sideways. What if we close off every direction for the material? We put a cube with edge 10 mm of the same steel into a rigid box with smooth walls that just touches it on all six sides at 20 °C, and heat it by 30 K (figure 3.27).

  1. The walls do not allow any edge to change, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The faces of the cube stay against the flat walls and the right angles between them do not change, so the shear components of the strain are zero too.
  2. We insert the zero strains into (3.17). What remains is
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]
  3. In numbers \(E/(1 - 2\nu) = 196\ \text{GPa}/0{,}4 = 490\) GPa and
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -490 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4} = -220{,}5\ \text{MPa} .\]

So the walls press on all faces with the same pressure \(p = 220{,}5\) MPa (figure 3.27, right), 2,5 times more than the walls on the bar. Where does the factor \(1/(1 - 2\nu) = 1/0{,}4 = 2{,}5\) come from? We get the same from the first equation of (3.16). Since all three directions are alike, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) gives

\[0 = \frac{1}{E}\,(\sigma - 2\nu\,\sigma) + \alpha\,\Delta T = \frac{(1 - 2\nu)\,\sigma}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad \sigma = -\frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} .\]

The term \(-2\nu\,\sigma/E\) shows what has changed. With the bar, the walls compressed only along the axis \(x\). In the box the walls compress also in the directions \(y\) and \(z\), and these two pressures by themselves would lengthen the cube in the direction \(x\), just as a compressed bar widens sideways. The walls perpendicular to the axis \(x\) have to prevent this lengthening too, so the stress in the direction \(x\) is larger. The result agrees with the meaning of the term \(-3K\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) from the previous subsection. The bulk modulus of steel is \(K = 196\ \text{GPa}/(3 \cdot 0{,}4) = 163{,}3\) GPa, the box prevents the relative change of volume \(3\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3}\), and \(K \cdot 3\alpha\,\Delta T = 163{,}3 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-3} = 220{,}5\) MPa. Does the cube yield under such a pressure? All three principal stresses are equal, \(-220{,}5\) MPa, so \(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 0\), and by the formula with the differences of the principal stresses also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 0\). By neither yield condition of section 2.12.5 does the steel yield, although the pressure is about equal to the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa. Experiments with metals agree: an equal pressure from all sides does not cause yielding (BOWER §3.7.3, PDF p. 146).

So far the expansion was prevented by something outside the body. But the obstacle can also be inside the body itself. Take a thin round steel plate that nothing holds and on which no force acts, and suppose that at some moment it is warmer in the middle than at the rim, for example because we are heating it in the middle (figure 3.28). We divide the plate in thought into an inner disc and an outer ring. If they were separate and nothing held them back, the warmer disc would expand more than the hole of the cooler ring and would no longer fit into it. In the plate, however, the disc and the ring are one piece. The ring holds the disc back and compresses it, and the disc pushes the ring outward and stretches it along its circumference. Stresses arise in the plate, although no force acts on it. SADD says that an uneven temperature in a body generally causes stresses, because the expansion is held back by the supports or by the neighbouring parts of the body (SADD, introduction to chapter 12, PDF p. 378).

A calculation confirms that stresses must arise in such a body. In section 1.18 we saw that strains coming from a displacement field satisfy six equations, otherwise the small pieces of the body do not fit together. There we derived them for displacement fields with continuous third partial derivatives, and we assume such smooth displacements for the plate too. One of them is

\[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} = 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} .\]

We put the origin at the centre of the plate, the axes \(x\) and \(y\) in its plane, and let the change of temperature at that moment be \(\Delta T = \Delta T_0\,\big(1 - (x^2 + y^2)/R^2\big)\), where \(R\) is the radius of the plate and \(\Delta T_0\) the heating at the centre. In the middle \(\Delta T = \Delta T_0\), at the rim zero.

  1. If there were no stress in the plate, by (3.16) the strain at every point would equal the thermal strain: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  2. The right side of the equation is then zero, and the left side is \(\alpha\,\big(\partial^2 \Delta T/\partial y^2 + \partial^2 \Delta T/\partial x^2\big)\).
  3. We differentiate. The first partial derivative of \(\Delta T\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-2\Delta T_0\,x/R^2\), the second \(-2\Delta T_0/R^2\). The same with respect to \(y\). The left side is therefore \(\alpha\,(-2\Delta T_0/R^2 - 2\Delta T_0/R^2) = -4\alpha\,\Delta T_0/R^2\), which is not zero.
  4. The thermal strains do not satisfy this equation, so they do not come from any such displacement field: the small pieces of the plate, each heated freely, would not fit together. The assumption of step 1 was therefore wrong, and there are stresses in the plate.

When do the pieces fit, then? If \(\Delta T\) is a linear function of the coordinates, of the form \(c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 y + c_3 z\) with constant numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_3\), all second derivatives of \(\Delta T\) are zero, and the equation holds. COOK states that in this case there are no thermal stresses in a body whose supports do not hold it back. A straight bar whose temperature increases linearly from its lower to its upper face, for example, bends into a circular arc, but there is no stress in it (COOK §2.10, PDF p. 66).

Let us sum up how we reached these conclusions. We got the thermal strain from a freely heated bar, which got longer without stress, and described it by the coefficient \(\alpha\). From isotropy and the formula for the strain of a fibre in any direction we showed with lemma 3.14 that the thermal strain has no shear components. We obtained Hooke's law with temperature by splitting the strain into the elastic part, for which Hooke's law holds, and the thermal strain. The examples then showed that stress arises only where the thermal strain is prevented: by the walls for the bar, by the walls of the box for the cube, or by the neighbouring material in the plate. For the bar the stress is \(E\) times the prevented strain, for the cube in the box the pressure is \(K\) times the prevented relative change of volume. All of this held for isotropic materials. Wood and materials with fibres behave differently in different directions, and the next section is devoted to such materials.
