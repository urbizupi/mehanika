Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.1 Strong and weak form (močna in šibka oblika)

In chapter 4 we wrote the equilibrium of a bar in two ways. The first is an equation at every point of the bar, \(N' + q = 0\) (lemma 4.75). The second is an equality of integrals for every virtual displacement (theorem 4.76). Which of the two asks less of the unknown displacement? And what do we gain by asking less? We need the answer before we look for approximations: an approximate field is usually less smooth than the true one, and if the equation at every point does not admit it, we must write the problem differently. First we describe both forms for a bar and compare them on a bar with a step, then we do the same for a beam and for a body.

6.1.1 Boundary value problem of a bar (robni problem palice)

Take the bar from section 4.4.4. It lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and is fixed at \(x = 0\). Its modulus of elasticity and cross-sectional area determine the axial stiffness \(EA\), a positive continuous function of position, which is constant for a bar with a constant cross section. A distributed axial load \(q\) (definition 4.74) and a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) at the end \(x = L\) act on it. The axial force is \(N = EA\,u'\), because the stress is \(E\,u'\) and the cross section is \(A\) (section 4.6.1). Equilibrium of parts of the bar gave \(N' + q = 0\) and \(N(L) = F\), and the fixing gave \(u(0) = 0\). Let us write all three requirements as a task about the unknown function \(u\).

Definition 6.1 Strong form of a boundary value problem of a bar (močna oblika robnega problema palice)

The bar lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and is fixed at \(x = 0\). Let the axial stiffness \(EA\) be a positive continuous function on \([0, L]\), the load \(q\) a continuous function on \([0, L]\) (definition 4.74) and \(F\) a number, the force at the end \(x = L\). The strong form (močna oblika) of the boundary value problem of a bar is the task: find a function \(u\) on \([0, L]\) that has a continuous derivative \(u'\) (one-sided at the ends), whose axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and which satisfies

\[\begin{aligned} &\big(EA\,u'\big)'(x) + q(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \in (0, L) , \\ &u(0) = 0 , \qquad EA\,u'(L) = F . \end{aligned} \tag{6.1}\]

Such a statement, an equation at every interior point and conditions at the ends, is a boundary value problem (robni problem).

Source: EP §6.3.6.2 (p. 323, 326-327), COOK §4.7, (4.7-2) (PDF p. 166).

What does the definition say in words? At every point of the bar the derivative of the axial force must equal the negative of the load, so the axial force must change along the bar exactly as the load demands at every metre. For the axial force to have a derivative, the function \(u\) must have a second derivative if \(EA\) is constant. Besides the equation we have two conditions at the ends. The condition \(u(0) = 0\) prescribes a displacement and is an essential condition (bistveni pogoj), the condition \(EA\,u'(L) = N(L) = F\) prescribes a force and is a natural condition (naravni pogoj), as we distinguished them in section 4.4.7 and for the beam in section 5.11.4.

Let us test the definition on the hanging bar (section 4.6.8): \(L = 10\) m, \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N, \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, \(F = 0\). These and all further numbers of the hanging bar are computed from unrounded data (\(E\), \(\rho\), \(g\) and \(A = \pi\,(5\ \text{mm})^2\)), as in chapter 4. With the rounded \(EA\) and \(q\) the last digit can differ: the energy of the true field is \(-391{,}03\) µJ, while the rounded data would give \(-391{,}16\) µJ. The true field \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) has the axial force \(N = EA\,u' = 2a\,EA\,(L - x) = 6{,}010\ \text{N/m} \cdot (L - x)\), whose derivative is \(-6{,}010\) N/m \(= -q\), and \(N(L) = 0 = F\). It satisfies the strong form. The line \(u = c\,x\) from section 4.6.8, however, has the constant axial force \(N = EA\,c\), so the left side of equation (6.1) is \(0 + q = 6{,}010\) N/m at every point, whatever the number \(c\) is. The strong form of the line therefore does not distinguish better from worse, because the number \(c\) does not appear in it. No line satisfies it, and the measure of how much it fails tells us nothing about the number \(c\).

6.1.2 Weak form of a bar (šibka oblika palice)

Theorem 4.76 showed that the true displacement satisfies an equation with integrals over the bar in which only the first derivative of the displacement appears. This equation is equilibrium multiplied by a virtual displacement and integrated over the bar. Now let us take it as the definition of the task. We keep in mind that we shall assemble trial fields from pieces, so we allow functions with kinks (pregib), such as the field with a roof in section 1.15.5. The displacement of such a field is continuous, but its derivative may jump at finitely many points. The next section treats these functions exactly, here we say only what we need.

Definition 6.2 Weak form of a boundary value problem of a bar (šibka oblika robnega problema palice)

The bar lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and is fixed at \(x = 0\). Let the axial stiffness \(EA\) (positive) and the load \(q\) be functions continuous piece by piece: there is a partition of the interval \([0, L]\) for which both are continuous on every closed piece, with one-sided values at the ends. Let the force at the end \(x = L\) be \(F\). An admissible function of a bar (dopustna funkcija palice) is a function \(w\) on \([0, L]\) that is continuous, satisfies \(w(0) = 0\), and for which there is a partition \(0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = L\) for which \(w\) is differentiable on every closed piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with a continuous derivative, one-sided at the ends. The derivative \(w'\) is thus determined everywhere except at the points of the partition, and the integral of a product with a function continuous on pieces means the sum of the integrals over the pieces. For several functions we take a partition that is common to all, adding to it the points of the partitions of the other functions. The weak form (šibka oblika) of the boundary value problem of a bar is the task: find an admissible function \(u\) that for every admissible function \(w\) satisfies

\[\int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,w(L) . \tag{6.2}\]

The admissible function \(w\) is here called a test function (preizkusna funkcija).

Source: EP §6.3.6.2, (6.3.40) to (6.3.43) (p. 327), COOK §4.1 (PDF p. 150) and §4.7 (PDF p. 166), BOWER §8.1.2 (PDF p. 496). Summarized from theorem 4.76, the notation with pieces is not from the sources.

The test function is the virtual displacement from section 4.4: an imagined change of displacement that does not move the support (definition 4.70). The condition \(w(0) = 0\) is this constraint, just as for the bar. The function \(u\) in the task is an admissible function, so it satisfies the same condition and the same continuity as the test functions, but not the other equation. The weak form asks for a single derivative of the displacement, \(u'\), whereas the strong form also asks for the derivative of the axial force, that is \(u''\) for a constant stiffness. The natural condition \(EA\,u'(L) = F\) is built into the weak form: the term \(F\,w(L)\) stands right on the right side of equation (6.2). The essential condition \(u(0) = 0\) is not in the equation, because it is part of the set of admissible functions. Figure 6.1 compares the requirements: the strong form asks for equilibrium at every point, and the weak form for the equality of two areas formed by the test function. Figure 6.2 shows two test functions, a smooth one and one with a kink.

How are the forms related? If \(u\) is a solution of the strong form, it is a solution of the weak form. The converse holds under a condition that we state in the theorem, and from the weak form follow all the conditions of the strong one, even those that are not in its statement.

Theorem 6.3 Strong and weak form of a bar (močna in šibka oblika palice)

Let the bar, \(EA\), \(q\) and \(F\) be as in definition 6.2.

  1. Let \(EA\) and \(q\) be continuous on \([0, L]\). If \(u\) is a solution of the strong form (definition 6.1), then \(u\) is a solution of the weak form.
  2. Let \(u\) be a solution of the weak form and let there be a partition \(0 = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = L\) for which \(EA\) and \(q\) are continuous on every closed piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\), \(u\) is differentiable on every piece with a continuous derivative, and on every piece the axial force \(EA\,u'\) equals a function \(N_{\mathrm{k}}\) that is continuously differentiable on the closed piece. Then:
    • (a) \(N_{\mathrm{k}}'(x) + q(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in [x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) and every \(k = 1, \dots, m\),
    • (b) \(N_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = N_{\mathrm{k}+1}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) for \(k = 1, \dots, m-1\), so the axial force is continuous across the points of the partition,
    • (c) \(N_{\mathrm{m}}(L) = F\).
Proof

We must show two directions. The first is integration by parts on the pieces, the second reverses this step and from the equation for every test function chooses in turn functions that are nonzero only on one piece, at one junction and at the end.

1. The strong form implies the weak form.

  1. The derivative \(u'\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), so \(u\) is differentiable on \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the ends, and therefore continuous (lemma 1.79). Together with \(u(0) = 0\) this is an admissible function with a partition of one piece.
  2. Let \(w\) be an admissible function with the partition \(0 = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = L\). By definition 6.1 the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), and the field \(w\) is differentiable on every closed piece with a continuous derivative. By corollary 3.41 on integration by parts, on the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) we have
    \[\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = N(x_{\mathrm{k}})\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - N(x_{\mathrm{k}-1})\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) - \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  3. We add over \(k\). By the convention of definition 6.2, \(\int_0^L N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) is the sum of the integrals over the pieces, so it is the sum of the left sides. The function \(N'\,w = -q\,w\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), so the sum of the integrals \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\) equals \(\int_0^L N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\) by additivity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 2). At the point \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(1 \le k \le m-1\) there appear the terms \(N(x_{\mathrm{k}})\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) from piece \(k\) and \(-N(x_{\mathrm{k}})\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) from piece \(k+1\). They are the same number, because \(N\) and \(w\) are continuous, so they cancel. What remains is
    \[\int_0^L N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = N(L)\,w(L) - N(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^L N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  4. Since \(w(0) = 0\), \(N(L) = F\) and \(N' = -q\) by equation (6.1), this is \(\int_0^L N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = F\,w(L) + \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\). Because \(N = EA\,u'\), this is equation (6.2).

2. The weak form implies the conditions.

  1. Let \(w\) be an admissible function. We refine the partition with the partition of the function \(w\). At the added points \(N\) and \(w\) are continuous, so their terms cancel in the summation, as in step 3 of the first direction. By corollary 3.41 on every piece and after summation we have
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x &= N_{\mathrm{m}}(L)\,w(L) \\ &\quad + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{m-1} \big(N_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - N_{\mathrm{k}+1}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\big)\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \int_0^L N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x , \end{aligned}\]
    where \(N'\) on piece \(k\) is the derivative of the function \(N_{\mathrm{k}}\), and the term at \(x = 0\) vanishes because of \(w(0) = 0\).
  2. Let us introduce \(J_{\mathrm{k}} = N_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - N_{\mathrm{k}+1}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\), the jump of the axial force at the point \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\). We insert this into equation (6.2) and move all terms to the left side:
    \[\begin{aligned} &\big(N_{\mathrm{m}}(L) - F\big)\,w(L) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{m-1} J_{\mathrm{k}}\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - \int_0^L \big(N' + q\big)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \\ &\text{for every admissible function } w . \quad (*) \end{aligned}\]
  3. Let us choose a piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) and a function \(\psi\) that is differentiable on it with a continuous derivative and satisfies \(\psi(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) = \psi(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = 0\). The function \(w\) that equals \(\psi\) on this piece and zero elsewhere is continuous, zero at \(x = 0\) and differentiable with a continuous derivative on every closed piece of the partition, so it is admissible. It is also zero at all points of the partition. Equation \((*)\) therefore says \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} (N_{\mathrm{k}}' + q)\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every such \(\psi\).
  4. The function \(N_{\mathrm{k}}' + q\) is continuous on \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\), because \(N_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuously differentiable and \(q\) continuous on the piece. By the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations (lemma 4.77) we therefore have \(N_{\mathrm{k}}' + q = 0\) on the whole piece. This is item (a).
  5. Now the integral in \((*)\) is zero for every admissible function \(w\), and what remains is \((N_{\mathrm{m}}(L) - F)\,w(L) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} J_{\mathrm{k}}\,w(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = 0\).
  6. Let \(1 \le k \le m-1\) and \(0 < h < \min(x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{k}-1},\, x_{\mathrm{k}+1} - x_{\mathrm{k}})\). The roof \(t_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is zero to the left of \(x_{\mathrm{k}} - h\) and to the right of \(x_{\mathrm{k}} + h\), rises linearly to \(1\) at \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) and falls linearly, is continuous, zero at \(0\) and \(L\), and differentiable with a continuous derivative on every piece of the partition refined by the points \(x_{\mathrm{k}} \pm h\). It is therefore admissible. Its value is \(1\) at \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(0\) at all other points of the partition, including \(L\). Step 5 gives \(J_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\). This is item (b).
  7. The function \(w(x) = x\) with \(w(L) = L\) is also admissible. Since all \(J_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) by step 6, step 5 gives \((N_{\mathrm{m}}(L) - F)\,L = 0\) and therefore \(N_{\mathrm{m}}(L) = F\). This is item (c).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The connection between the two forms for a smooth case is mentioned by EP §6.3.6.2 (pp. 323-328) and COOK §4.7 (PDF pp. 166-167), theorem 4.78 is the same conclusion for a continuously differentiable axial force.

What did the theorem show? The first direction is integration by parts, which moves the derivative from the axial force to the test function, as in theorem 4.76, except that we run it piece by piece also across the points where the test function has a kink. The second direction says more than it seems at first sight. From the single equation (6.2) for all admissible functions \(w\) we get the equation of equilibrium on every piece, the natural condition \(N(L) = F\) and the continuity of the axial force across every point where a piece ends. This continuity is not written in the equation, yet the equation demands it.

For the hanging bar with the line the difference now shows. The line \(u = c\,x\) is an admissible function. Into equation (6.2) we insert the test function \(w = x\) from figure 6.2. The left side is \(\int_0^L EA\,c\,\mathrm{d}x = EA\,c\,L\), the right side \(\int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = q\,L^2/2\), and the equality gives \(c = q\,L/(2EA) = a L = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). This is exactly the equation from step 4 of the first trial field in section 4.6.8. The strong form gave nothing, because the number \(c\) dropped out, and the weak form determined it, because it asked of the true field only an average, equilibrium with the weight \(w\). The line does not satisfy the equation for every test function, because it is not the true field. With \(w = x^2/(1\ \text{m})\), that is \(w' = 2x/(1\ \text{m})\) with \(x\) in metres, the left side is \(\int_0^L EA\,c\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x = EA\,c\,L^2/(1\ \text{m}) = 3005\) N m, the right side \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = q\,L^3/3\,(1\ \text{m})^{-1} = 2003\) N m. The true field \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) gives on the left the same as on the right, \(2003\) N m. The line therefore satisfies the equation only for the chosen test function, not for all, and this is what we expect of it: a trial field with one free number satisfies one requirement. The idea that a trial field with \(n\) free numbers satisfies \(n\) requirements we develop in the following sections.

6.1.3 A bar with a step (palica s stopnico)

What happens if the cross section of a bar changes suddenly along its length? Take a steel hanging bar with an upper half, \(5\) m long, of diameter \(10\) mm and cross-sectional area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and a lower half, \(5\) m long, of diameter \(5\) mm and area \(A/4 = 19{,}63\ \text{mm}^2\) (figure 6.3). The upper end is fixed, the lower end free, \(F = 0\), the only load is the weight. The material is the steel of the book (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\), \(g = 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2\)). The load per unit length is \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m above and \(\rho g A/4 = 1{,}503\) N/m below. The step is a simplification: a real step would have a rounded transition, and the one-dimensional model does not capture the stress concentration next to it. We deal with it in chapter 11.

First let us find the true displacement by the method of chapter 4, piece by piece. Equilibrium of the part of the bar below a cross section (lemma 4.75) gives \(N(x) = \rho g\,(A/4)\,(10 - x)\) in the lower half, with \(x\) in metres, and \(N(x) = \rho g\,(A/4) \cdot 5 + \rho g A\,(5 - x)\) in the upper one. At \(x = 5\) m both expressions equal \(7{,}512\) N, so the axial force is continuous, and at \(x = 0\) we have \(N = 37{,}56\) N, the weight of the whole bar, \(5 \cdot 6{,}010 + 5 \cdot 1{,}503\) N. The strain \(u' = N/(EA)\) has the value \(4{,}880 \cdot 10^{-7}\) above the step and \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) below. They meet at the same point, but the derivative of the displacement jumps by a factor of \(4\), the ratio of the areas: the same force in a cross section four times thinner gives four times the elongation. We get the displacement from the integral of the strain: \(u(5\ \text{m}) = 7{,}320\) µm and \(u(10\ \text{m}) = 12{,}20\) µm. The graph of the displacement therefore has a kink at the point \(x = 5\) m and there is no second derivative there.

What does the strong form say about such a field? Its equation \((EA\,u')' + q = 0\) requires \(EA\) to be continuous (definition 6.1), but the axial stiffness jumps by a factor of \(4\) at the step. The load \(q\) also jumps, from \(6{,}010\) to \(1{,}503\) N/m, and so the axial force \(N' = -q\) does not have a continuous derivative across the step. This definition does not capture the true field. We would have to split the strong form into two parts and add two more conditions at the step: the displacement is continuous, the axial force is continuous. These two conditions are not written in the equation.

The weak form needs nothing extra. Let us test it. With \(w = x\) the left side of equation (6.2) is \(\int_0^L N\,\mathrm{d}x = 131{,}46\) N m, and the right side is \(\int_0^5 6{,}010\,x\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_5^{10} 1{,}503\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = 131{,}46\) N m. The equality also holds for the test function with a kink from figure 6.2, here with the kink at \(x = 5\) m, \(w = x\) up to \(5\) m and \(w = 5\) m afterwards: the left side is \(\int_0^5 N\,\mathrm{d}x = 112{,}68\) N m, the right side \(\int_0^5 6{,}010\,x\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_5^{10} 1{,}503 \cdot 5\,\mathrm{d}x = 112{,}68\) N m. The displacement of the bar with a step is therefore an admissible function and a solution of the weak form, even though its derivative is discontinuous. For every test function this follows from steps 2 to 4 of the first direction of the proof of theorem 6.3: we take the partition common with the point \(x = 5\) m, the axial force is continuous and \(N' = -q\) on every piece, and the terms at the step cancel. Theorem 6.3, item 2, says the converse: every solution of the weak form with piecewise smooth fields \(u\) and \(N\) has a continuous axial force and a continuous displacement at the step, even though we wrote this nowhere. The continuity of the displacement is in the definition of an admissible function, and the continuity of the axial force came out of the equation itself. This is the reason why the weak form also admits bars whose stiffness or load jumps.

What if the test function is not continuous? Take \(w = x\) up to \(5\) m and \(w = 5{,}5\) m afterwards. It differs from the function with a kink only in that at \(x = 5\) m it jumps by \(0{,}5\) m (figure 6.4). It satisfies \(w(0) = 0\) and is differentiable with a continuous derivative on every closed piece, but it is not continuous, so it is not an admissible function. We test the requirement of continuity on the true displacement of the bar with a step. If we did not demand it, equation (6.2) would have to hold for this function too.

  1. On both pieces the derivative is the same as for the function with a kink, \(1\) and \(0\), because the derivative does not register the jump. The left side is therefore still \(\int_0^5 N\,\mathrm{d}x = 112{,}68\) N m.
  2. The right side, \(\int_0^5 6{,}010\,x\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_5^{10} 1{,}503 \cdot 5{,}5\,\mathrm{d}x\), is larger than for the function with a kink by \(\int_5^{10} 1{,}503 \cdot 0{,}5\,\mathrm{d}x = 3{,}76\) N m, so it is \(116{,}44\) N m. The load \(1{,}503\) N/m on the lower \(5\) m acts at a higher value of \(w\), and the left side does not see this height.
  3. The two sides do not agree, \(112{,}68 \ne 116{,}44\). The true displacement would violate the equation for this function, even though it is a solution.

The excess \(3{,}76\) N m is the axial force at the step \(N(5) = 7{,}512\) N multiplied by the jump \(0{,}5\) m. For a jump \(s\) the excess is \(7{,}512\,s\) N m, zero only for \(s = 0\). The continuity of the function \(w\) in the definition of an admissible function is therefore not an add-on: only with it do the left side, which sees only the derivative, and the right side, which sees the value, measure the same function.

6.1.4 Weak form of a beam (šibka oblika nosilca)

We put the same question to the beam from chapter 5. Let it lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) with bending stiffness \(EI\) and load \(q\), positive upward. The strong form is the equation of the deflection curve (theorem 5.42)

\[\big(EI\,v''\big)'' = q\]

with four conditions at the ends from section 5.7.2. At every end there is one condition from the pair \((v, V)\) and one from the pair \((v', M)\): essential if the support prescribes the deflection or the slope, natural if it prescribes the shear force or the moment (the table in section 5.11.4). For a constant stiffness \(v\) must have four derivatives.

We know the weak form from theorem 5.62: equilibrium multiplied by a virtual displacement and integrated by parts twice. For \(N = 0\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) what remains is

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L EI\,v''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x &+ \Big[F_{\mathrm{y}}\,w + M_0\,w'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}} \\ &+ \Big[F_{\mathrm{y}}\,w + M_0\,w'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = 0} . \end{aligned} \tag{6.3}\]

Here \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_0\) are the force across the axis and the moment of a couple at the ends (each end has its own values, the loads are positive upward and counterclockwise), and at every end we omit the terms of the components that the support forbids. The test functions \(w\) are the virtual displacements of a beam (definition 5.60): \(w\) and \(w'\) continuous, \(w''\) continuous (the next section allows \(w''\) to have jumps), and \(w = 0\) where \(v\) is prescribed and \(w' = 0\) where \(v'\) is prescribed. We proved the equivalence of the two forms in chapter 5: the weak one follows from the strong one by theorem 5.62, and the converse by theorem 5.65, which gives the equation \((EI\,v'')'' = q\) in the interior and the natural conditions \(V = F_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M = M_0\) at a free end. A point force or a couple in the interior adds a term by corollary 5.64. The natural conditions and the point forces are therefore in the weak form only terms on the right side.

Why is the continuity of the slope \(w'\) important here? The equation contains \(w''\), and if \(w'\) had a jump, such as a kink in the deflection, then \(w''\) at that point would not be a function. The term \(M_0\,w'\) at a free end is the work of the couple on the rotation of the end, which must be well defined. Later in this chapter we show with numbers that the energy of a rounded kink in the deflection grows beyond all bounds when we shorten the rounding.

Let us test equation (6.3) on the cantilever from section 5.1.3: \(L = 1\) m, force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N at the free end, \(M_0 = 0\), clamped at \(x = 0\). The moment is \(M = EI\,v'' = -500\ \text{N} \cdot (L - x)\). The test function must have \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\), so \(w = x^2/(1\ \text{m})\) is one of the admissible ones, with \(x\) in metres, \(w'' = 2/(1\ \text{m})\) and \(w(L) = 1\) m. The left side is \(\int_0^L M\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x = -500\ \text{N m}\), the right side \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,w(L) + M_0\,w'(L) = -500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m} = -500\ \text{N m}\). The equation holds. We do the same on the simply supported beam from section 5.1.3, \(L = 2\) m, \(q = -q_0\) with \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, where \(M = \tfrac12\,q_0\,x\,(L - x)\). The test function \(w = x\,(L - x)/(1\ \text{m})\) is zero at both supports, and \(w'' = -2/(1\ \text{m})\). The left side is \(\int_0^L M\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x = -1333{,}3\) N m, the right side \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = -1333{,}3\) N m, there are no terms at the ends, because \(w = 0\) everywhere there and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = M_0 = 0\).

The support says what the trial field must satisfy. A beam clamped at \(x = 0\) requires \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\). A simply supported beam requires \(v = 0\) at both supports, but the trial field does not require the moment \(M = 0\), because it is a natural condition and equation (6.3) satisfies it by itself when the field is the true one, as we saw in section 5.11.4.

6.1.5 Weak form of a body (šibka oblika telesa)

The last answer is for a body. Take the body from section 4.5.1: a set of points \(V\), the surface divided into a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) with prescribed displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) and a part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) with prescribed force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\), and the body force \(\vek b\). The strong form gathers what we established in chapters 1 to 3: the equilibrium equations \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) in the interior, the condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the condition \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the stress from the strain by the general Hooke's law (definition 3.16) and the strain from the displacement (definition 1.115). The weak form is the equality of internal and external virtual work, equation (4.51), in which the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is expressed by the displacement \(\vek u\). The equivalence of the two forms is already proved. From the equilibrium equations and the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the equation of virtual work follows by theorem 4.82, and from the equation of virtual work for every admissible virtual displacement the equilibrium equations and the condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) follow by theorem 4.84. The condition on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is built into the set of fields in both. The theorems hold under the assumptions of section 4.5.1: a body made of boxes, a continuously differentiable and symmetric stress. Plane states, in which two coordinates suffice, have the same form and we treat them in chapter 11.

The test function is here a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (figure 6.5). Let us test this on the cube of section 4.5.1, glued to the table, \(h = 10\) mm, \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa, the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\) everywhere. The virtual displacement \(\vek w = (\gamma\,z, 0, 0)\) with \(\gamma = 0{,}001\) is admissible, because it is zero at \(z = 0\) (section 4.5.2), and has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}(\vek w) = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}}(\vek w) = \gamma/2\), the other virtual strains are zero. The internal virtual work is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} (\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}})\,\gamma/2\,\mathrm{d}V = \tau_0\,\gamma\,h^3 = 0{,}0500\) N m. We neglect the weight, \(\vek b = \vek 0\), as in section 4.5.1. The external virtual work comes only from the top face with \(\bar{\vek t} = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), where \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = \gamma h\): \(\tau_0\,\gamma\,h \cdot h^2 = 0{,}0500\) N m. The front and the back face are free, \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\). On the left and right faces the forces are vertical, \(w_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and their work is zero, and the bottom face is \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), where \(\vek w = \vek 0\). The equality holds.

All three statements have the same form. On the left is an expression that is linear in the unknown field and in the test function at the same time, and on the right an expression that is linear only in the test function and contains the loads. Let us name them.

Definition 6.4 Bilinear form (bilinearna forma)

Let \(W\) be a vector space of functions or fields (definition 1.16). A bilinear form (bilinearna forma) on \(W\) is a function \(B\) that takes two elements \(u\) and \(w\) of the space \(W\) as input and gives one number \(B(u, w)\) as output, and is linear in each argument separately: \(B(\alpha u_1 + \beta u_2, w) = \alpha\,B(u_1, w) + \beta\,B(u_2, w)\) and \(B(u, \alpha w_1 + \beta w_2) = \alpha\,B(u, w_1) + \beta\,B(u, w_2)\) for all \(u, u_1, u_2, w, w_1, w_2 \in W\) and numbers \(\alpha, \beta\). It is symmetric (simetrična) if \(B(u, w) = B(w, u)\) for all \(u, w \in W\).

Source: EP §6.2.4 (pp. 281-282), (6.2.31).

Definition 6.5 Linear form (linearna forma)

Let \(W\) be a vector space of functions or fields. A linear form (linearna forma) on \(W\) is a function \(\ell\) that takes an element \(w\) of the space \(W\) as input and gives a number \(\ell(w)\) as output, and satisfies \(\ell(\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2) = \alpha\,\ell(w_1) + \beta\,\ell(w_2)\) for all \(w_1, w_2 \in W\) and numbers \(\alpha, \beta\).

Source: EP §6.2.4 (p. 281), §6.3.6.2, (6.3.42) (p. 327).

The notation \(\ell(w)\) with a function in brackets is a linear form. The letter \(\ell\) without an argument is in chapter 1 the arm of a lever and is something else. The three tasks that we have collected are, in these notations:

task \(B(u, w)\) \(\ell(w)\)
bar \(\int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,w(L)\)
beam \(\int_0^L EI\,u''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x\) \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + [F_{\mathrm{y}} w + M_0 w']_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}} + [F_{\mathrm{y}} w + M_0 w']_{\mathrm{x} = 0}\)
body \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k},\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}(\vek u)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\,\mathrm{d}V\) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A\)

For a body \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}(\vek u)\) is the strain of the field \(\vek u\) (equation (1.51)), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek w)\) the strain of the test field \(\vek w\), which is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.79), and \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) the constants of the generalized Hooke's law (definition 3.16), so that \(\sum_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}(\vek u) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Each of the three has the first argument linear because in every case the stress is linear in the displacement (section 3.2.5), and the second because the virtual strain and the virtual displacement appear linearly. Symmetry: for the bar and the beam the product of functions is symmetric, for the body \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\) by theorem 3.47, item 1, and the change of the order of the arguments is an exchange of the indices of a pair. All three tasks therefore have the same form.

Symmetry is not self-evident. On functions on \([0, 1]\) take \(B(u, w) = \int_0^1 (u'\,w' + u'\,w)\,\mathrm{d}x\). It is bilinear, because \(u'\,w'\) and \(u'\,w\) are linear in \(u\) and in \(w\) separately. For \(u = x\) and \(w = x^2\) we have \(B(x, x^2) = \int_0^1 (2x + x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac43\). If we swap the arguments, \(B(x^2, x) = \int_0^1 (2x + 2x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). The numbers differ, so \(B\) is not symmetric. The cause is the term \(u'\,w\): the derivative stands at the first function, the value at the second, and after the swap this is no longer the same product. In the table, in each task the same quantities stand in both places, the derivatives \(u'\) and \(w'\) for the bar, \(u''\) and \(w''\) for the beam, or the strain with the constants \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\) for the body, so these forms are symmetric. This property will be a condition of theorem 6.44.

Linearity has a condition too. On the hanging bar, \(q = 6{,}010\) N/m and \(L = 10\) m, take \(\tilde\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), which differs from \(\ell\) only in that \(w\) is squared. For \(w = x\) (\(x\) in metres) we have \(\tilde\ell(w) = q\,L^3/3 = 2003\) N m\(^2\). The first requirement of the definition with \(\alpha = 2\) and \(\beta = 0\) would give \(\tilde\ell(2w) = 2\,\tilde\ell(w) = 4007\) N m\(^2\). In fact \(\tilde\ell(2w) = \int_0^L q\,(2w)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 4 \cdot 2003 = 8013\) N m\(^2\), because the factor \(2\) is multiplied by itself in the square. The first requirement therefore fails, \(\tilde\ell\) is not linear. For \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\) the function \(w\) stands to the first power and the factor \(2\) comes out in front of the integral.

The weak form of all three is thus one sentence. Let us write it for every task with such forms, since further on we shall also use it for the bar fixed at both ends, for beams and for the body.

Definition 6.6 Weak form with a bilinear and a linear form (šibka oblika z bilinearno in linearno formo)

Let \(X\) be the vector space of fields among which the task seeks the solution, \(B\) a bilinear form and \(\ell\) a linear form on \(X\). For the bar \(X\) is the space of the continuous functions of definition 6.2 without the condition at the support, for the beam the space of functions with continuous \(w\), \(w'\) and \(w''\), for the body the space of continuously differentiable fields (definition 1.90). The essential conditions of the task prescribe the values of the field on the supported part of the boundary, for the beam possibly also of the slope. An admissible field (dopustno polje) is a field from \(X\) that satisfies the essential conditions. A test function is a field from \(X\) that is zero wherever the essential conditions prescribe something, for the beam together with the slope where this is prescribed. The sum and a multiple of test functions are again test functions, so they form a vector space \(W\). The weak form (šibka oblika) of the task is the task: find an admissible field \(u\) for which

\[B(u, w) = \ell(w) \qquad \text{for every } w \in W . \tag{6.4}\]

For the bar fixed at \(x = 0\) this is definition 6.2, for the beam equation (6.3) and for the body equation (4.51).

Source: EP (6.3.43) (p. 327), where the test function is the first argument of the form \(B\), which for a symmetric form is the same.

We shall need one more property all the time: that \(B(w, w)\) is not negative and is zero only for \(w = 0\). Why? \(B(w, w)\) is twice the strain energy of the field \(w\) (bar and beam: because the energy is \(\tfrac12\,EA\,w'^2\) or \(\tfrac12\,EI\,w''^2\) per unit length, body: equation (3.41)). The energy is positive if the bar really deforms. Zero energy, however, means that the bar or the body deforms nowhere, so it only moves as a rigid body, and the supports do not allow this.

Lemma 6.7 Positivity of the energy (pozitivnost energije)

Let \(B\) be the bilinear form of one of the three tasks in the table and \(W\) the space of its test functions (definition 6.6), and let \(B(w, w) = 2U(w)\) be twice the strain energy of the field \(w\). Then \(B(w, w) \ge 0\) for every \(w \in W\), and \(B(w, w) = 0\) only for \(w = 0\), if the assumptions of the task hold:

  1. bar: \(EA > 0\) continuous on pieces, the bar fixed at least at one end,
  2. beam: \(EI > 0\) continuous, \(w\) and \(w'\) continuous, \(w''\) continuous, and the only straight line \(w = \alpha + \beta x\) in \(W\) is \(w = 0\) (for example for the clamp \(w(0) = w'(0) = 0\) or for the supports \(w(0) = w(L) = 0\)),
  3. body of isotropic material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), a connected interior of the body, and the only rigid motion \(\vek w = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) (constant vector \(\vek a\), antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\)) that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is \(\vek w = \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show non-negativity, and then in each case that zero energy implies the zero field.

  1. Bar. The integrand \(EA\,w'^2\) is not negative, so the integral over every piece is not negative either (lemma 3.39, item 4). Let the sum of the integrals over the pieces be zero. Since none is negative, each is zero. Suppose that \(w'(x_0) \ne 0\) at some point of a piece. Then the function \(EA\,w'^2\) is continuous on the piece and positive at \(x_0\), and as in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of corollary 4.93 the integral over a neighbourhood of \(x_0\) has a positive lower bound, a contradiction. Therefore \(w' = 0\) on every piece, by corollary 1.85 \(w\) is constant on every piece, and since it is continuous, it is constant on the whole \([0, L]\). A test function is zero at the fixed end, so \(w = 0\).
  2. Beam. The same argument with \(EI\,w''^2\) gives \(w'' = 0\) on \([0, L]\). By corollary 1.85 \(w'\) is constant, \(w' = \beta\), and \(w - \beta x\) has derivative zero, so \(w = \alpha + \beta x\). This line is in \(W\), so by assumption \(w = 0\).
  3. Body. By theorem 3.48, item 1, the strain energy density is positive for every strain that is not zero, and by theorem 3.47, item 2, \(B(w, w) = 2\int_{\mathrm{V}} \Phi_{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(\Phi_{\mathrm{w}}\) is the strain energy density (definition 3.44) for the strain of the field \(\vek w\), so \(B(w, w) \ge 0\). Let \(B(w, w) = 0\). The density \(\Phi_{\mathrm{w}}\) is continuous and non-negative, so by the same argument as for the bar it is zero at every point of the interior, so the strain is zero at every point. By theorem 1.134 we have \(\vek w = \vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) on the interior, and by continuity on the whole body. The field is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so by assumption \(\vek w = \vek 0\).

Source: EP §6.2.5 (pp. 283, 287). The proof is not from a source, the bar we treated in steps 6 to 8 of the proof of corollary 4.93.

The assumption about the supports is not empty. The cube of section 4.5.1 is glued with its bottom face to the table, and there \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the face \(z = 0\). If \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r = \vek 0\) at the points \((0, 0, 0)\), \((h, 0, 0)\) and \((0, h, 0)\), then \(\vek a = \vek 0\), \(\mat W\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek 0\). An antisymmetric matrix with zero first two columns has, because of antisymmetry, zero first two rows as well, and its element \(W_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero, because the diagonal of an antisymmetric matrix is zero. So \(\mat W = \mat 0\) and the cube is held. The hanging bar as a body in section 4.5.1, however, has \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) empty, and the energy is zero for a rigid motion. The bar in this section has a fixed end \(u(0) = 0\) and the lemma holds for it.

The existence of a solution of the weak form for a bar and a beam is guaranteed by integration, as we did it in chapters 4 and 5. For a body the proof of existence is outside our computation. It needs the continuity of the bilinear form and the positivity that the lemma gave, and we leave it to chapter 11 (BATHE §4.3.4, PDF pp. 254-255). The statements about the body in this chapter are therefore written with the words "if a solution exists".

The weak form has a close connection with the total potential energy. The energy of the field \(u\) is \(\Pi(u) = \tfrac12\,B(u, u) - \ell(u)\), for the bar equation (4.62). Its difference between the fields \(u + \alpha w\) and \(u\) is \(\alpha\,\big(B(u, w) - \ell(w)\big) + \tfrac12\,\alpha^2\,B(w, w)\), which follows from bilinearity and symmetry. The term with \(\alpha\) is zero exactly when the weak form holds for \(w\). The weak form is therefore the condition that the energy changes, under a displacement in the direction of every test function, only by the term with \(\alpha^2\), and since the lemma on positivity says that this term is positive, the field is then the lowest among all admissible ones. We use this connection in the section where we find the free numbers from the smallest energy.
