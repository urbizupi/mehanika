Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.7 Energy and power in vibration

In section 9.6, for the force \(Q_0 = 20\) N and the frequency \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) we found that the cantilever with weight \(A\) vibrates with the amplitude \(15{,}9\) mm, which is \(50\) times more than the same force causes when it acts constantly (\(0{,}319\) mm). The force drives the vibration in every period, yet the amplitude does not grow without bound. Where does the energy go that the force supplies in each period? And why does the amplitude settle at exactly \(15{,}9\) mm? The answer comes from an account of energy: we compute how much energy the force supplies, how much the damper loses and how much is stored in the spring and the mass, and from this we read how wide the peak of the resonance curve is and why it is so high at small damping.

We assume the same as in sections 9.1 and 9.6: a system with one degree of freedom with a mass \(m\), a spring of stiffness \(k\), a viscous damper with coefficient \(c\) and a force \(Q(t)\) on the coordinate \(q\). The spring and the damper are idealizations, as said in section 9.1.

9.7.1 Power and the energy balance

The power of a force (moč sile) is the work of the force per unit time. When the coordinate moves by \(\mathrm{d}q\), the generalized force \(Q\) does the work \(Q\,\mathrm{d}q\) (section 8.5), so its power at the moment \(t\) is \(Q\,\dot q\). The unit is the watt, \(1\) W \(= 1\) N m/s. Power can be negative: a force that opposes the motion takes energy away. The damper force \(-c\,\dot q\) has the power \(-c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\) and always takes energy away. We call this energy dissipated energy (izgubljena energija): for the vibration it is lost, in a real joint it turns into heat.

The stored energy (shranjena energija) is the sum of the kinetic energy of the mass and the elastic energy of the spring,

\[\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12\,m\,\dot q^2 + \tfrac12\,k\,q^2 . \tag{9.50}\]

We ask how it changes with time.

Lemma 9.59 Energy balance of the single-degree-of-freedom system (energijska bilanca nihala z eno prostostno stopnjo)

Let \(q\) be a twice continuously differentiable solution of the equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t)\) with a continuous function \(Q\). Then

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q\,\dot q - c\,\dot q^2 . \tag{9.51}\]

The derivative of the stored energy is therefore the supplied power of the force minus the dissipated power of the damper.

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the expression (9.50) equals \(Q\,\dot q - c\,\dot q^2\).

  1. At every moment we multiply the equation of motion by \(\dot q\): \(m\,\dot q\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q^2 + k\,q\,\dot q = Q\,\dot q\).
  2. By the product rule and the chain rule (lemmas 1.81 and 4.43) \(\big(\tfrac12 m\,\dot q^2\big)' = m\,\dot q\,\ddot q\) and \(\big(\tfrac12 k\,q^2\big)' = k\,q\,\dot q\), so \(\mathcal{E}' = m\,\dot q\,\ddot q + k\,q\,\dot q\).
  3. From step 1, \(m\,\dot q\,\ddot q + k\,q\,\dot q = Q\,\dot q - c\,\dot q^2\). Together with step 2 this is (9.51).

Source: GR (1.58) (PDF p. 54) writes the power for a system with several degrees of freedom and arbitrary non-conservative forces. Our case is a special case of theorem 8.104 (equation (8.98)) with \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = Q - c\,\dot q\). The notation and steps 1 to 3 are ours.

The lemma showed that every term of the equation of motion, multiplied by the velocity, means a power. If we integrate it between the moments \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) (theorem 3.40),

\[\mathcal{E}(t_2) - \mathcal{E}(t_1) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} Q\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} c\,\dot q^2\,\mathrm{d}t ,\]

the change of the stored energy equals the work of the force minus the loss in the damper. In free vibration (\(Q = 0\)) the energy does not grow, as we saw in section 9.5.

In the steady state of section 9.6 everything repeats with the same period as the force, so we ask how much power the force supplies on average and how much the damper loses on average. The average of a function \(f\) over the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(\tfrac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} f\,\mathrm{d}t\). For this we need the integrals of the squares of sine and cosine over a whole period.

Lemma 9.60 Integrals of sine and cosine over a period of the force (integrali sinusa in kosinusa čez periodo sile)

Let \(\Omega > 0\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) and \(a\) a real number. Then

  1. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\cos(2\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\) and \(\displaystyle\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sin(2\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\),
  2. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\cos^2(\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sin^2(\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{T_{\mathrm{p}}}{2}\),
  3. \(\displaystyle\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sin(\Omega t + a)\cos(\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\).
Proof

We must show the first item with the fundamental theorem and the period, and reduce the second and the third to it with the double angle.

  1. By theorem 8.12 (equation (8.7) with \(\omega = 2\Omega\)), \(\big(\sin(2\Omega t + a)/(2\Omega)\big)' = \cos(2\Omega t + a)\) and \(\big({-\cos(2\Omega t + a)}/(2\Omega)\big)' = \sin(2\Omega t + a)\). By theorem 3.40 the first integral equals \(\big(\sin(4\pi + a) - \sin a\big)/(2\Omega)\), since \(2\Omega T_{\mathrm{p}} = 4\pi\). Because \(\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta\) (lemma 8.10, item 4), used twice, the difference is zero. The same holds for the second integral with the cosine.
  2. For \(\beta = \alpha\), corollary 8.15 gives \(\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1\), so \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)\) with \(\theta = \Omega t + a\). By linearity (lemma 3.39) and by item 1 with \(2a\) in place of \(a\), \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\cos^2(\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = T_{\mathrm{p}}/2 + 0\). Because \(\sin^2 = 1 - \cos^2\) (lemma 8.10, item 1), \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\sin^2(\Omega t + a)\,\mathrm{d}t = T_{\mathrm{p}} - T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\).
  3. By corollary 8.15, \(\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\), so \(\sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac12\sin 2\theta\) and the integral is zero by item 1.

Source: Proof is not from a source.

In words: over a whole period the sine and the cosine of the double angle spend equally long above the axis and below it, so they cancel, while the squares have the average \(\tfrac12\).

9.7.2 Mean power

Now everything is ready for the balance in the steady state (ustaljeno stanje). By theorem 9.52 on the response to a harmonic force, the steady motion under the force \(Q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\) is \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) with the amplitude \(X = Q_0\,|H(\Omega)|\) and the phase lag \(\varphi = \arg P(\mathrm{i}\Omega) \in [0, \pi]\).

Theorem 9.61 Mean power under harmonic excitation (povprečna moč pri harmoničnem vzbujanju)

Let \(m, k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\), \(Q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\) with \(\Omega > 0\) and \(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c\,\Omega \neq 0\). Let \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) be the steady motion from theorem 9.52 and \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\). Then the supplied power \(p_{\mathrm{dov}} = Q\,\dot q\) and the dissipated power \(p_{\mathrm{izg}} = c\,\dot q^2\) have the same average over a period,

\[\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} p_{\mathrm{dov}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12\,Q_0\,\Omega\,X\sin\varphi = \tfrac12\,c\,\Omega^2 X^2 = \frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} p_{\mathrm{izg}}\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{9.52}\]

the stored energy returns after the period, \(\mathcal{E}(t + T_{\mathrm{p}}) = \mathcal{E}(t)\), and in one period the energy \(\pi\,c\,\Omega\,X^2\) is dissipated.

Proof

We must show three things: the average of the supplied power, the average of the dissipated power and that they are equal. We compute the first two with lemma 9.60, and the periodicity of the energy gives the equality.

  1. From \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\), theorem 8.12 gives \(\dot q = -\Omega X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\).
  2. By corollary 8.15, \(\sin(\Omega t - \varphi) = \sin\Omega t\cos\varphi - \cos\Omega t\sin\varphi\), so \(p_{\mathrm{dov}} = Q_0\cos\Omega t\,\dot q = -Q_0\Omega X\cos\varphi\,\sin\Omega t\cos\Omega t + Q_0\Omega X\sin\varphi\,\cos^2\Omega t\).
  3. By lemma 9.60 (items 3 and 2 with \(a = 0\)), \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} p_{\mathrm{dov}}\,\mathrm{d}t = Q_0\Omega X\sin\varphi\,T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). Division by \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) gives the average \(\tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X\sin\varphi\).
  4. \(p_{\mathrm{izg}} = c\,\Omega^2X^2\sin^2(\Omega t - \varphi)\). By lemma 9.60 (item 2 with \(a = -\varphi\)) the integral is \(c\,\Omega^2X^2\,T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\), the average is \(\tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2\) and the energy in one period is \(T_{\mathrm{p}} \cdot \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2 = \tfrac{2\pi}{\Omega}\cdot\tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2 = \pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\).
  5. The functions \(q\) and \(\dot q\) have the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) (lemma 8.10, item 4), so \(\mathcal{E}(T_{\mathrm{p}}) = \mathcal{E}(0)\). By lemma 9.59 and theorem 3.40, \(0 = \mathcal{E}(T_{\mathrm{p}}) - \mathcal{E}(0) = \int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(p_{\mathrm{dov}} - p_{\mathrm{izg}})\,\mathrm{d}t\). The averages are therefore equal, which is (9.52).
  6. We check once more with a second computation. From \(H = 1/(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\,c\Omega)\), \(\mathrm{Im}\,H = -c\Omega\,|H|^2\), so \(\sin\varphi = -\mathrm{Im}\,H/|H| = c\,\Omega\,|H|\). Then \(\tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X\sin\varphi = \tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X\,c\,\Omega\,|H| = \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2\), because \(Q_0|H| = X\). This is the equality of step 5, obtained without periodicity.

Source: CK (4.74) (PDF p. 126) and the text on p. 127 give the energy per period \(\pi\,c\,\Omega\,U^2\) (\(U\) is our \(X\)), and p. 125 says that in steady vibration the loss of energy is balanced by the energy of the excitation. The mean power and the proof with the energy balance are not from the source.

The proof showed that in steady vibration the damper loses exactly as much as the force supplies, and that the stored energy returns to the same value after every period. How much the force supplies is determined by the product of the amplitudes of force and velocity, \(\tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X\), multiplied by \(\sin\varphi\). If the force is in phase with the velocity (\(\varphi = 90^\circ\)), the factor is \(1\) and all the power is lost. If \(\varphi = 0\) or \(180^\circ\), the force is shifted by a quarter period relative to the velocity, \(\sin\varphi = 0\) and on average no power is supplied: in one quarter of the period the force adds energy, in the next it takes it away.

We test it on the cantilever \(A\) of section 9.1 with \(m = 50\) kg, \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, \(\zeta = 0{,}01\), so \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km} = 35{,}418\) N s/m, and with the force \(Q_0 = 20\) N. \(0{,}9\) \(1{,}6708\) \(5{,}412^\circ\) \(0{,}0943\) \(0{,}0502\) \(0{,}00990\)

In every row the mean supplied and dissipated power are equal, as the theorem says (the computations \(\tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X\sin\varphi\) and \(\tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2\) agree in all written digits). At \(r = 0{,}9\) the lag is only \(5{,}4^\circ\), so the force is almost in phase with the displacement and shifted by almost a quarter period relative to the velocity, and of the product of amplitudes, \(\tfrac12 Q_0\Omega X = 0{,}533\) W, only \(\sin\varphi = 9{,}4\ \%\) is supplied on average, \(0{,}0502\) W. At \(r = 1\) the lag is exactly \(90^\circ\), the force is in phase with the velocity at all times, and the damper loses the whole supplied power \(5{,}647\) W: \(112\) times more than at \(r = 0{,}9\), although the force is the same. In one period \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}1774\) s at resonance \(1{,}0018\) J is lost. This energy is the answer to the first question: in every period the force supplies \(1{,}0018\) J, the damper loses it in the same period and nothing is left for the vibration.

Figure 9.26 shows how this happens through a period.

Why does everything agree so exactly at resonance? At \(r = 1\) we have \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) (theorem 9.54), so \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and \(\dot q = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). From \(X = Q_0|H(\omega_{\mathrm{n}})| = Q_0/(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) we get \(Q_0 = c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\) and therefore \(p_{\mathrm{dov}} = Q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2X^2\cos^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = c\,\dot q^2 = p_{\mathrm{izg}}\) at every moment. Hence by lemma 9.59 \(\mathcal{E}' = 0\): the energy is constant, \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2X^2\cos^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + \tfrac12 k\,X^2\sin^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = \tfrac12 k\,X^2\), because \(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\). For \(A\) this is \(7{,}972\) J. All the energy at resonance circulates between the mass and the spring, and the force only replaces what the damper takes.

Now we can answer the second question: why does the amplitude not grow further? Suppose that at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the mass vibrates approximately sinusoidally with the amplitude \(X'\) and is in phase with the force. In a period the force supplies \(\pi\,Q_0\,X'\) (theorem 9.61, \(\sin\varphi = 1\)), and the damper loses \(\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X'^2\). The supplied energy grows in proportion to \(X'\), the lost energy with the square of \(X'\). At \(X' = 8\) mm the force supplies \(0{,}5027\) J, the damper loses \(0{,}2522\) J and \(0{,}2504\) J is left to the vibration, so the amplitude grows. At \(X' = 15{,}944\) mm the supplied and lost energy are equal, \(1{,}0018\) J. At \(X' = 20\) mm the force supplies \(1{,}2566\) J and the damper loses \(1{,}5763\) J, the vibration loses \(0{,}3197\) J per period and the amplitude falls. The balance is at \(X = Q_0/(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), which is exactly the amplitude from theorem 9.52, and it is stable: below it the amplitude grows, above it it falls. This is an approximate argument, because the amplitude is not constant during a period.

The exact computation from rest is as follows. The motion \(X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) is a particular solution (theorem 9.52, \(\varphi = 90^\circ\)), and the free part is by theorem 9.43 equal to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + B\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\). The conditions \(q(0) = 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0\) give \(A = 0\) and \(B = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), so \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t - \tfrac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}X\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\). Up to the factor \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 1{,}00005\) the envelope equals \(X(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t})\) with the time constant \(1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 2{,}823\) s, the largest displacement reaches \(99\ \%\) of \(X\) after \(13{,}0\) s, that is after \(73\) oscillations (numerical computation).

We must be honest here. The amplitude \(15{,}9\) mm is too much for a steel cantilever: at \(10{,}887\) mm the stress at the clamp together with the static one is \(220\) MPa, as computed for the case \(A\) at the yield stress in section 9.6. The vibration from rest reaches \(10{,}887\) mm for the first time after \(3{,}3\) s, that is after \(19\) oscillations (numerical computation), long before it settles. The computation for the steady state holds as long as the model is linearly elastic (an assumption from chapter 5). Above the limit plastic yielding or fracture happens before the damper balances the force.

The loss in a period is also shown by the loop of the damper force. The damper acts on the mass with the force \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c\,\dot q = c\,\Omega X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\), and the displacement is \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\). Therefore \((q/X)^2 + (f_{\mathrm{D}}/(c\,\Omega X))^2 = 1\) (\(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\)): the point \((q, f_{\mathrm{D}})\) runs through an ellipse with semi-axes \(X\) and \(c\,\Omega X\) in every period (figure 9.27). The work of the damper in a period is \(\oint f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\mathrm{d}q = \int f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int c\,\dot q^2\,\mathrm{d}t\), so minus the area the point encloses (when it turns counterclockwise). By theorem 9.61 this is \(-\pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\), so the area is \(\pi\,c\,\Omega X^2 = \pi\,X\cdot c\,\Omega X\), which is \(\pi a b\) for the semi-axes \(a = X\) and \(b = c\,\Omega X\), as the school formula for the area of an ellipse says. The loss from the theorem is thus read from the picture. For \(A\) and \(r = 1\) the semi-axis is \(15{,}944\) mm and \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X = 20\) N, which is exactly \(Q_0\).

9.7.3 Width of the resonance peak

The mean power as a function of frequency is \(\bar P(\Omega) = \tfrac12\,c\,\Omega^2\,|H(\Omega)|^2\,Q_0^2\), because \(X = Q_0|H|\). This function has a peak at resonance. How high and how wide is it? The answer is more precise than the one from section 9.6, where we looked at the amplitude of the displacement. We are interested in the frequencies at which the power is half of the largest. The theorem below gives their position and spacing.

Theorem 9.62 Half-power points (točki polovične moči)

Let \(m, k, c > 0\), \(Q_0 > 0\) and \(\bar P(\Omega) = \tfrac12\,c\,\Omega^2\,|H(\Omega)|^2\,Q_0^2\) for \(\Omega > 0\). Then:

  1. \(\bar P(\Omega) \le Q_0^2/(2c)\) and equality holds exactly at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. \(\bar P(\Omega) = \tfrac12\,\bar P(\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) exactly at \(\Omega_1 = \dfrac{-c + \sqrt{c^2 + 4mk}}{2m}\) and \(\Omega_2 = \dfrac{c + \sqrt{c^2 + 4mk}}{2m}\), and \(\bar P(\Omega) \ge \tfrac12\,\bar P(\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) exactly for \(\Omega_1 \le \Omega \le \Omega_2\).
  3. \(\Omega_2 - \Omega_1 = c/m = 2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\Omega_1\,\Omega_2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), so \(r_2 - r_1 = 2\zeta\) and \(r_1\,r_2 = 1\) for \(r_{\mathrm{i}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{i}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), at every \(\zeta > 0\).
Proof

We must show first a form of the power that is easy to read, then the peak, then half of the peak and finally the difference of the roots.

  1. By definition 9.51, \(|H|^{-2} = (k - m\Omega^2)^2 + c^2\Omega^2\). Division of numerator and denominator by \(\Omega^2\) (\(\Omega > 0\)) gives \(\bar P = \dfrac{Q_0^2}{2}\,\dfrac{c}{f(\Omega)^2 + c^2}\) with the function \(f(\Omega) = \dfrac{k - m\Omega^2}{\Omega} = \dfrac{k}{\Omega} - m\,\Omega\).
  2. Because \(f^2 \ge 0\), \(\bar P \le Q_0^2/(2c)\), and equality holds exactly at \(f = 0\), that is \(\Omega^2 = k/m\), so \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is item 1.
  3. \(\bar P \ge \tfrac12\bar P(\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) means \(f^2 + c^2 \le 2c^2\), that is \(|f(\Omega)| \le c\), with equality exactly at \(|f| = c\).
  4. \(f\) is differentiable with \(f'(\Omega) = -k/\Omega^2 - m < 0\), so by theorem 1.84 it is strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\). At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(f = 0\). The equation \(f = c\) therefore has at most one solution and so does \(f = -c\), and \(|f| \le c\) holds exactly between them.
  5. \(f(\Omega) = c\) is \(k - m\Omega^2 = c\,\Omega\), so \(m\,\Omega^2 + c\,\Omega - k = 0\), which has one positive root \(\Omega_1\) (the product of the roots \(-k/m\) is negative, so one root is positive and the other negative). \(f(\Omega) = -c\) is \(m\,\Omega^2 - c\,\Omega - k = 0\) with one positive root \(\Omega_2\). Both roots are given by the quadratic formula in the theorem. Because \(f\) is decreasing and \(f(\Omega_1) = c > -c = f(\Omega_2)\), \(\Omega_1 < \Omega_2\). Then \(f \le c\) exactly for \(\Omega \ge \Omega_1\) and \(f \ge -c\) exactly for \(\Omega \le \Omega_2\), so \(|f| \le c\) exactly on \([\Omega_1, \Omega_2]\). This is item 2.
  6. The difference is \(\Omega_2 - \Omega_1 = 2c/(2m) = c/m\). The product is \(\Omega_1\Omega_2 = \big((c^2 + 4mk) - c^2\big)/(4m^2) = k/m = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). Because \(c = 2\zeta\sqrt{km} = 2\zeta\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(c/m = 2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Division by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) gives item 3.

Source: Proof is not from a source. CK (4.62) to (4.68) (PDF pp. 124 to 125) defines the half-power points on the amplitude of the displacement, not on the power (see below), FPGL (PDF p. 46) defines them by the power level \(\tfrac12\) of the peak and the corresponding response level \(1/\sqrt2\) of the peak. The exact form of the theorem is not found in the project books (KREYSZIG and GR do not contain the expression "half-power"), SymPy confirms the roots and the difference.

The proof showed that the half-power band is exactly \(2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) wide at every damping and that the geometric mean of the frequencies is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). The reason for the exactness is the factor \(\Omega^2\): the power is \(\tfrac12 c\,(\Omega X)^2\), so the square of the amplitude of the velocity \(\Omega X\) and not of the displacement \(X\). The half-power band is the band in which the amplitude of the velocity is larger than \(1/\sqrt2\) of its peak.

For \(A\) (\(\zeta = 0{,}01\)), \(\Omega_1 = 35{,}065\) 1/s and \(\Omega_2 = 35{,}773\) 1/s, that is \(5{,}5808\) Hz and \(5{,}6935\) Hz, a difference of \(0{,}7084\) 1/s \(= 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(\zeta = 0{,}05\), \(r_1 = 0{,}95125\) and \(r_2 = 1{,}05125\) (figure 9.28).

CK defines the half-power points on the amplitude of the displacement: it looks for the frequencies at which \(X\) equals \(\sqrt2/2\) of the peak and obtains the approximation \(\zeta \approx (r_2 - r_1)/2\) (CK (4.62) to (4.68), PDF pp. 124 to 125). Why is it not exact? The amplitude of the displacement is proportional to \(|H|\), the power to \(\Omega^2|H|^2\). The factor \(\Omega^2\) is not constant across the band, so the bands differ. For \(A\) the error of the approximation \((r_2 - r_1)/2\) relative to \(\zeta\) is \(0{,}010\ \%\) at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\), \(0{,}25\ \%\) at \(0{,}05\), \(1{,}04\ \%\) at \(0{,}1\) and \(4{,}67\ \%\) at \(0{,}2\). For small damping this is more than enough, CK states that with good data the frequency can be determined to about \(\pm 5\ \%\) and the damping to about \(\pm 20\ \%\) (PDF p. 125). For the power, however, the derivation is exact at every \(\zeta\).

9.7.4 Energy ratio and equivalent damping

One question remains: why is the response so strong at small damping? We compare how much energy is stored and how much is lost in one period.

Corollary 9.63 Ratio of stored and dissipated energy at resonance (razmerje shranjene in izgubljene energije pri resonanci)

Let \(m, k, c > 0\) and \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then the stored energy is constant, \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 k\,X^2\), and

\[\frac{2\pi\,\mathcal{E}}{\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2} = \frac{1}{2\zeta} = D(1) . \tag{9.53}\]
Proof

We must show the constancy of the energy and then the ratio.

  1. We computed the constancy above: at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) (theorem 9.54) and \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2X^2\cos^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + \tfrac12 k\,X^2\sin^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = \tfrac12 k\,X^2\).
  2. The loss in a period is by theorem 9.61 \(\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2\), so the ratio is \(\dfrac{2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 k X^2}{\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2} = \dfrac{k}{c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\).
  3. Because \(c = 2\zeta\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k\), \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\zeta\,k\) and the ratio is \(1/(2\zeta)\).
  4. By definition 9.53, \(D(1) = 1/\sqrt{(1 - 1)^2 + (2\zeta)^2} = 1/(2\zeta)\).

For \(A\), \(2\pi\cdot 7{,}972\ \text{J}/1{,}0018\ \text{J} = 50{,}000\), which is \(1/(2\cdot 0{,}01)\) and \(D(1)\) from section 9.6. The meaning is this: in one period the damper takes only \(2\pi/50 = 12{,}6\ \%\) of the stored energy (\(1{,}0018\) J of \(7{,}972\) J), so the force keeps it high with a small contribution per period. At small damping a large stored energy, that is a large amplitude, is thus maintained by a small force: the ratio of the energies and the ratio of the amplitude to the static displacement are the same number \(1/(2\zeta)\).

What about damping that is not viscous? In real joints the loss is often caused by friction or internal losses of the material, and the force is not proportional to the velocity. CK (§4.7, PDF pp. 125 to 128) introduces equivalent viscous damping (ekvivalentno viskozno dušenje): a coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) at which the energy loss in a period \(\pi\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\Omega X^2\) equals the loss of the real mechanism (CK (4.75), PDF p. 127). This keeps the model linear. When this replacement is allowed and what the individual mechanisms give, we treat in chapter 20.
