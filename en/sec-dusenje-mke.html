Chapter 20 Damping models

20.7 Damping in the finite element model

Chapter 10 took the damping matrix \(\mat C\) as given: Rayleigh damping (definition 10.62) or modal damping (definition 10.64), where we choose the ratios of the modes. With finite elements a second question arises: can we assemble \(\mat C\) from elements, as we assembled the stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) (theorem 7.13) and the mass matrix \(\mat M\) (section 10.8.3)? The sources are cautious. CK (PDF p. 321) says that the damping matrix could in principle be derived by finite element procedures, but the material properties are not known well enough and most of the damping comes from joints and non-structural parts, so the damping is usually determined for the whole system. BATHE (PDF p. 813) says that the matrix \(\mat C\) cannot as a rule be assembled from element matrices as the mass and stiffness matrices are, and that it is introduced to capture approximately the total loss of energy.

Still, we know two mechanisms that can be written for one element: a material that resists the rate of strain (the Kelvin-Voigt term, section 20.4), and a dashpot that is attached along the body to a resting surroundings, such as air or oil (section 20.1). There are three questions. What matrix does each of them give for one element? When is the assembled matrix Rayleigh damping, that is classical, and what happens if it is not? And what do we do with damping that we know only for harmonic motion, hysteretic (section 20.3) and frequency-dependent material (section 20.6)? We are guided by frame A from section 10.1: two floors with mass \(m = 500\) kg, stiffness of each storey \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, natural frequencies \(\omega_1 = 19{,}578\) and \(\omega_2 = 51{,}257\) 1/s, and the modal matrix \(\mat\Phi\) from section 10.3.4 (in kg\(^{-1/2}\)).

20.7.1 Damping matrix of one element

Take a bar element with two nodes and length \(h\) (definition 7.1), with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\), and a beam element (section 12.1). In both the field on the element is \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (for the beam \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\)), where the nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\) are functions of time and the shape functions are functions of position. How would we get the damping force on the nodes from this?

We draw the element as a line between two nodes that move by \(d_1\) and \(d_2\). Between them is the length of the element \(h\). On the nodes the surroundings act with the forces \(F_1\) and \(F_2\). They start at the nodes and point outward, because they stretch the element if we pull (lemma 7.9). The first mechanism is the material of the element. For the Kelvin-Voigt element (definition 20.24) the stress is the sum of two parts, \(\sigma = E\varepsilon + \eta\dot\varepsilon = E\,(\varepsilon + \tau_\varepsilon\dot\varepsilon)\), where \(\varepsilon\) is the strain, \(\eta\) the viscosity and \(\tau_\varepsilon = \eta/E\) the retardation time (čas zakasnitve) in seconds. The first part is the spring, as in chapter 7, the second is the dashpot in parallel with it, because both deform by the same strain and their stresses add. A dashpot feels the rate of stretching, so we also draw the velocities of the nodes \(\dot d_1\) and \(\dot d_2\) as blue arrows. The right one is longer than the left one: the right node runs away from the left one, the element stretches at the rate \(\dot d_2 - \dot d_1\), and since strain is elongation divided by length, the strain rate is \(\dot\varepsilon = (\dot d_2 - \dot d_1)/h\). The second mechanism is the external dashpot. We draw it below the bar and attach it to the resting surroundings, because such a dashpot (air, oil, definition 9.1) acts on every piece of the bar with the force \(-c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) per unit length. The constant \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) has the unit N s/m\(^2\). Above the bar we draw the velocities of its pieces \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\). They grow evenly from \(\dot d_1\) on the left to \(\dot d_2\) on the right, because the field between the nodes is linear. Every dashpot therefore pulls its piece backward, against the velocity, and the right one pulls hardest, because its piece is the fastest.

Why the matrices of both mechanisms are so simple is told by the next lemma. For the beam the same conclusions hold, because we only replace the energy of stretching by the energy of curvature.

Lemma 20.39 Damping matrix of an element (matrika dušenja elementa)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a bar (definition 7.1) or of a beam (definition 12.3) with stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 7.5, lemma 12.4) and mass matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 10.47, lemma 12.43) and with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\) (for the beam \(EI\) and \(\rho A\)). Let \(\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the row of shape functions (for the beam \(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\)), \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) the field on the element and \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) the column of nodal velocities. Then:

  1. If the material is Kelvin-Voigt, \(\sigma = E\,(\varepsilon + \tau_\varepsilon\dot\varepsilon)\) with constant \(\tau_\varepsilon > 0\), the virtual work of the viscous part of the stress at a virtual displacement \(\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) equals \(-\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{20.32}\]
  2. If an external dashpot with force \(-c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) per unit length with constant \(c_{\mathrm{z}} \ge 0\) acts on the element, the virtual work of this force equals \(-\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = c_{\mathrm{z}}\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}}\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{c_{\mathrm{z}}}{\rho A}\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{20.33}\]
  3. Both matrices \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\) (material, external dashpot) are symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), for \(\tau_\varepsilon > 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z}} > 0\) the matrix \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\) of point 2 is even positive definite, and the matrix of point 1 has the same kernel as \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\).
Proof

We must show that in each case the virtual work of the viscous force (dashpot in the material and external dashpots) is a bilinear form in \(\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the stated matrix. For the bar the quadratic form of the stiffness is \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} EA\,(u_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) for every column \(\vek d\) (lemma 7.5, \(u_{\mathrm{h}}' = (d_2 - d_1)/h\) is constant), for the beam \(\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (lemma 12.4, step 2 of the proof of lemma 12.5).

  1. The strain of the field is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = u_{\mathrm{h}}'\) (for the beam the strain of the fibre at distance \(y\) from the axis is \(\varepsilon = -y\,v_{\mathrm{h}}''\), and with \(I = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) the moment of the section is \(EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'' + \tau_\varepsilon\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}'')\), and the bending energy is \(\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) instead of \(\int EA\,(u_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)). The shape functions do not depend on time (they are functions of the rest coordinate \(x\), all the time is in \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\)), so \(\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = \dot u_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\). For the bar this is the difference of the velocities of the nodes divided by the length, \((\dot d_2 - \dot d_1)/h\). The viscous part of the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{v}} = E\tau_\varepsilon\dot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\).
  2. The virtual work of the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}\) at the virtual field \(\delta u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is \(-\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} A\,\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{h}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = -\tau_\varepsilon\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} EA\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}'\,\delta u_{\mathrm{h}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\). The sign is minus, because the stress acts on the element against the stretching: the dashpot pulls the nodes together when they move apart.
  3. The integral \(\int EA\,u'w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) is a symmetric bilinear form of the fields \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{h}}\), and its quadratic form is \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d\). By polarization, \(4B(a, b) = Q(a + b) - Q(a - b)\), the integral equals \(\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\). We insert it in step 2 and get point 1.
  4. The force \(-c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\) per unit length, opposite to the velocity of the piece, does at \(\delta u_{\mathrm{h}}\) the work \(-\int c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\dot u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Since \(\dot u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\delta u_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), the work is \(-\delta\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(c_{\mathrm{z}}\int\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  5. By steps 1 to 3 of the proof of lemma 10.47 (for the beam of lemma 12.43) we have \(\int\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\rho A\,\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\int\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat\psi_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mathrm{d}x = (c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is point 2.
  6. The matrices are multiples of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the factors \(\tau_\varepsilon > 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A \ge 0\). By lemma 7.6 (for the beam 12.5) \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d \ge 0\), by lemma 10.47 (12.43) \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive definite. So \((c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite for \(c_{\mathrm{z}} \ge 0\) and positive definite for \(c_{\mathrm{z}} > 0\). Since \(\tau_\varepsilon > 0\), we have \(\tau_\varepsilon\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\), so \(\tau_\varepsilon\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) have the same kernel. This agrees with physics: the power of the forces of the dashpot in the material and of the external dashpots is \(-\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} \le 0\) for every velocity, so they only take energy away and never supply it.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. COOK (PDF p. 402) says that viscous damping is supplied by a surrounding gas or liquid or by viscous dashpots added to the structure, and CK (PDF p. 321) says that the damping matrix could be derived by the procedures for stiffness and mass. BATHE (PDF p. 813) does not as a rule assemble damping matrices from elements. The lemma shows when it is possible.

The lemma showed that for both mechanisms we can use the matrices that we already know for the damping of an element: the dashpot in the material takes the form of the stiffness, the external dashpot the form of the mass. This is the promise from section 12.11. There are two assumptions here.

The first assumption: the material responds to strain by the Kelvin-Voigt law with a constant \(\tau_\varepsilon\). We took it because it is the simplest law with a loss that we can write in time. We see its effect on the graph, where on the horizontal axis we put the frequency \(\Omega\), on the vertical one the loss factor \(\gamma\). A dashpot \(c\) beside a spring \(k\) has at the frequency \(\Omega\) the loss factor \(\gamma = c\,\Omega/k\) (equation (20.3)), so for \(c = \tau_\varepsilon k\) it is \(\gamma = \tau_\varepsilon\Omega\), a straight line through the origin that grows with frequency. On frame A below we will take \(\tau_\varepsilon = 2{,}82346\) ms. At the first natural frequency \(\omega_1\) we then have \(\gamma = 0{,}0553\), at \(\omega_2\) \(0{,}1447\), \(2{,}618\) times as much, which is \(\omega_2/\omega_1\). The second mode will therefore be damped \(2{,}618\) times more than the first. A hysteretic material with the same \(\gamma\) at all frequencies, however, would give both modes the same ratio, at \(\omega_2\) with a loss factor smaller by \(0{,}0894\). The assumption is therefore good for oscillation in a narrow band of frequencies, where the straight line parts little from the horizontal one: in a band of \(\pm 10\,\%\) around \(\omega_1\), \(\gamma\) changes by only \(\pm 10\,\%\). It does not hold for polymers, which section 20.4 describes with several terms, nor for materials whose loss per cycle does not depend on frequency (hysteretic damping, section 20.3).

The second assumption: the external dashpot is linear and the same everywhere, \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) depends neither on the place nor on the velocity (equal dashpots). It is good for a body that moves slowly in a viscous fluid (definition 9.1). It does not hold when the resistance is not proportional to the velocity, as with dry friction (section 20.2), or when the fluid touches only a part of the body. Its effect is the equality with the mass in equation (20.33): for a constant \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) the matrix is \((c_{\mathrm{z}} h/6)\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), which is \((c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} = \rho A h\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/6\\1/6&1/3\end{pmatrix}\). For a \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) that grows evenly from zero at the left node to \(c\) at the right one, however, the matrix is \(c\,h\begin{pmatrix}1/12&1/12\\1/12&1/4\end{pmatrix}\), which is not a multiple of the matrix \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\), since its right diagonal entry is three times the left one. The matrix of the external dashpot does not depend on the stiffness, only on the mass of the element, so we can lump it in the nodes as we lump the mass (section 10.8.2): each node gets a dashpot \(c_{\mathrm{z}} h/2\) and the matrix is \((c_{\mathrm{z}} h/2)\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1)\). This is the same approximation as the lumped mass. When the nodes move together with the velocity \(v\), both matrices give the same total force \(c_{\mathrm{z}} h\,v\). When they move apart with the velocities \(-v\) and \(v\), the pieces between them are slower and the middle is at rest. The distributed dashpots therefore consume the power \(c_{\mathrm{z}} h\,v^2/3\), the lumped ones \(c_{\mathrm{z}} h\,v^2\), three times as much, because they sit at the nodes, where the velocity is largest.

20.7.2 The assembled matrix and when it is classical

We assemble the elements as we assembled the stiffness. For motion, by virtual work, the element matrix \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\) is equivalent to the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) for displacements, so the assembly is the same.

Theorem 20.40 Assembled damping matrix (sestavljena matrika dušenja)

Let the structure have \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) unknowns and \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) elements (for frame A these are the two storeys) with the connection table and selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definitions 7.10 and 7.11). Let element \(e\) have by lemma 20.39 the matrix \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = \tau_{\varepsilon \mathrm{e}}\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} + (c_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{e}}/\rho_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}})\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\tau_{\varepsilon \mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\). Then:

  1. The damping matrix of the structure (of frame A from its two storeys) is \(\mat C = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15).
  2. If all elements (both storeys) have the same \(\tau_{\varepsilon \mathrm{e}} = \tau_\varepsilon\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{e}} = a_0\,\rho_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the same \(a_0 \ge 0\), then \(\mat C = a_0\,\mat M + \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat K\), Rayleigh damping (definition 10.62) with \(a_1 = \tau_\varepsilon\). For positive definite \(\mat K\) it is classical and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}) + \tau_\varepsilon\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2\) (lemma 10.63).
Proof

We must show that the damping forces of the elements (on frame A of the lower and the upper storey) add up like stiffness forces, and that the matrix in the second point splits into \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\).

  1. The force that the damping of the element exerts on the unknown \(I\) is by lemma 20.39 row \(i\) of the matrix \(-\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(g_{\mathrm{ei}} = I\). The selection matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the same as for displacements, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\dot{\vek d}\) (lemma 7.12), so by the first point of theorem 7.13 (which uses only selection matrices and linearity) the total force is \(-\mat C\,\dot{\vek d}\) with the matrix \(\mat C = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\). On frame A the damping of the lower and of the upper storey both act on floor 1, and the two forces add up.
  2. Every \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat c_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) (point 3 of lemma 20.39), so by corollary 7.14 the symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of the matrix \(\mat C\) follow. Physically neither of the storeys supplies energy, so neither does their sum.
  3. For equal \(\tau_\varepsilon\) and \(a_0\) we have \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} + a_0\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) in every element, so \(\mat C = \tau_\varepsilon\sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} + a_0\sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat K + a_0\,\mat M\) by theorem 7.13 and section 10.8.3. What remains is the same statement as in lemma 10.63.

Source: COOK (PDF p. 403-404) and CK (10.76) (PDF p. 321) give Rayleigh damping as a linear combination of \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\), without a link to elements. The link with dashpots in the material and beside the body is ours, the proof is not from the sources.

Rayleigh damping now has a physical meaning: \(a_1\mat K\) is the viscosity of the material in the storeys, which is the same everywhere, and \(a_0\mat M\) are dashpots that tie every mass to the resting surroundings, with the same constant \(a_0\) per unit mass. This is why CK (PDF p. 321) remarks at the formula for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) (CK (10.78)) that the mass term falls with frequency and the stiffness term grows, but does not say why. Now we know. The ratio measures what part of the stored energy the dashpot uses up in one cycle: at resonance \(\zeta = \gamma/2\) and \(\gamma = c\,\Omega/k\) (equation (20.3)). The force of every dashpot grows with the velocity, and for the same amplitude the velocity grows with the frequency. For the material \(c = \tau_\varepsilon k\) and \(\gamma = \tau_\varepsilon\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\): a faster oscillation uses up a larger part. The external dashpot \(c = a_0 m\) acts beside a mass whose mode has the stiffness \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). This grows with the square of the frequency, the force of the dashpot only with the first power, so the part falls: \(\gamma = a_0 m\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2) = a_0/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). The halves of the two are the terms of the formula for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\).

We test on frame A. We draw the frame, two floors with mass \(m\) above the ground, each on two columns. In the left frame the lower storey has the dashpot \(\tau_\varepsilon k\), parallel to its two columns, and the upper storey has one too. We choose \(\tau_\varepsilon = 2{,}82346\) ms, so that the dashpot of the lower storey, \(\tau_\varepsilon k = 1416{,}70\) N s/m, is the same as in section 10.10.4. We will need this for the middle frame. The matrix is \(\mat C = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat K\) and by theorem 20.40 it is classical with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = \tau_\varepsilon\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2 = 0{,}02764\) and \(0{,}07236\). The matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\,\mat\Phi\) is diagonal, \(\mathrm{diag}(1{,}0823,\ 7{,}4179)\) 1/s, as it must be (\(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), SymPy and NumPy in outlines/ch-dusenje/num-07.py). The higher mode is damped \(2{,}618\) times more, \(0{,}07236/0{,}02764 = \omega_2/\omega_1\), because the viscous stress is proportional to the rate of strain, and for the same amplitude this is proportional to the frequency.

In the right frame we tie each floor to the resting wall with the dashpot \(a_0 m\) and get \(\mat C = a_0\mat M\). For \(a_0 = 0{,}56668\) 1/s, the Rayleigh constant of frame A from section 10.10, we have \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}) = 0{,}01447\) and \(0{,}00553\): the higher mode is damped less, exactly the opposite of the material. If we add the two mechanisms with \(\tau_\varepsilon = a_1 = 5{,}6469\cdot10^{-4}\) s, we get the Rayleigh damping of section 10.10 with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}01447 + 0{,}00553 = 0{,}0200\) and \(0{,}00553 + 0{,}01447 = 0{,}0200\) for both modes. The Rayleigh damping of frame A is thus assembled from two mechanisms that we know from physics.

Now the hypothesis that the theorem demands: the same \(\tau_\varepsilon\) in all elements. Take the middle frame of figure 20.59, in which only the lower storey is viscous and the upper has no dashpot. Then \(\mat C = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat K_1\) with \(\mat K_1 = k\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) (the lower storey is between the ground and floor 1, so it has only one unknown). This is the dashpot \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = \tau_\varepsilon k = 1416{,}70\) N s/m under floor 1, so the frame of section 10.10.4. The computation confirms \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\,\mat\Phi = \begin{pmatrix}0{,}7831 & 1{,}2671\\ 1{,}2671 & 2{,}0503\end{pmatrix}\) 1/s. The diagonal gives the ratios \(0{,}0200\) and \(0{,}0200\), and the off-diagonal term is \(1{,}2671 = \sqrt{0{,}7831\cdot 2{,}0503}\): the modes are coupled to the largest possible extent, the matrix is the product of a column and a row, \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\,\vek b\vek b^\T\) with \(\vek b = (\varphi_{11}, \varphi_{12})^\T\). The true roots are \(-0{,}39177\pm19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02493\pm51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s with ratios \(0{,}0200\) and \(0{,}0200\). The classical replacement with the same diagonal is therefore good here (section 10.10.4 computed the difference of amplitudes \(-0{,}029\,\%\) at \(\omega_1\) and \(-1{,}5\,\%\) at \(\omega_2\)), but the shape of the motion is different, since the ratio of the floors is complex, \(u_2/u_1 = 1{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\).

Why does a single dashpot couple the modes, while all the dashpots together do not? We draw both modes. The grey dashed frame on the ground is the rest position, the orange one is the frame in the first mode and in the second mode. The dashpot of a storey feels the storey drift (zamik etaže), the difference of the displacements of its upper and lower end. The drift of the lower storey is the displacement of floor 1, in both modes \(1\). The drift of the upper storey is in the first mode \(1{,}618 - 1 = 0{,}618\) in the same direction, and in the second \(-0{,}618 - 1 = -1{,}618\), in the opposite direction. So both modes stretch the dashpot of the lower storey. The force that the motion in the first mode raises in it acts on floor 1, and this floor moves in the second mode too, so this force does work on the second mode as well. This is the off-diagonal term \(1{,}2671\). If the same dashpot is also in the upper storey, it does work there with the opposite sign, because the drifts of the upper storey in the two modes are opposite, and the sum is \(1\cdot1 + 0{,}618\cdot(-1{,}618) = 0\). This is why the same \(\tau_\varepsilon\) in both storeys does not couple the modes.

This example has two lessons. The first: the same viscosity of the material in every storey gives classical damping, viscosity in a single storey does not (middle frame). The condition of the theorem is therefore no decoration. The second: the difference between the true roots and the classical replacement is small for light damping, but the shape of the motion changes. The general theory of such systems (first-order form and complex modes) is in section 20.8.

The condition \(\tau_{\varepsilon \mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z},\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) of the theorem also has its meaning. If the damping matrix is not built from physics but from two prescribed ratios, the constant \(a_0\) can get a negative value. BATHE (PDF p. 814, example 9.9) prescribes \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}02\) at \(\omega_1 = 2\) and \(\zeta_2 = 0{,}10\) at \(\omega_2 = 3\) and gets \(a_0 = -0{,}336\) and \(a_1 = 0{,}104\) (the same by the formula of lemma 10.63). We draw \(\zeta(\omega) = a_0/(2\omega) + a_1\omega/2\) by lemma 10.63: on the horizontal axis we put \(\omega\), on the vertical one \(\zeta\). The prescribed points, \((2;\ 0{,}02)\) and \((3;\ 0{,}10)\), lie on the curve. Since \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\), we have \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\,\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\), and each diagonal entry is \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). The matrix \(\mat C = a_0\mat M + a_1\mat K\) is therefore positive semidefinite exactly when \(a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 \ge 0\) for every mode, that is when the structure has no mode below \(\omega = \sqrt{-a_0/a_1} = 1{,}797\) 1/s, where the curve crosses zero. BATHE orders the eigenvalues by magnitude (PDF p. 855), so \(\omega_1 = 2\) is the lowest frequency, all modes lie to the right of the zero, and there \(\mat C\) is still positive definite. The same constants on a structure with a lower mode, however, give \(\zeta < 0\) (region below the axis), for example \(-0{,}034\) at \(\omega = 1{,}5\) 1/s, and the amplitude of such a mode would grow in oscillation. A free structure has such a mode, for example, since its rigid-body mode has \(\omega = 0\) (next section). Such a \(\mat C\) does not come from the mechanisms of theorem 20.40, because it would need a negative \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\).

20.7.3 Rigid-body motion and damping

Does the mechanism \(a_0\mat M\) have a further consequence that the formula for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) hides? In the formula there is \(a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\), which grows beyond all bounds as \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} \to 0\). For a structure that is not fixed (aircraft, vehicle, railway cars) \(\mat K\) has a zero eigenvalue with a rigid mode (theorem 10.34), and lemma 10.63 demands a positive definite \(\mat K\). What happens to the rigid mode?

Take the two cars of section 10.5, without friction on the rails, connected by a buffer. We use the damping \(a_0\mat M\), which ties each car to the resting surroundings. On each car there acts then the force \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = -a_0 m_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), which starts at the centre of mass and points against the velocity \(v\) (lemma 9.2). The buffer acts on the cars with equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), so in adding the equations of motion it drops out: \(m_1\ddot q_1 + m_2\ddot q_2 = -a_0\,(m_1\dot q_1 + m_2\dot q_2)\). The momentum \(P = m_1\dot q_1 + m_2\dot q_2\) satisfies \(\dot P = -a_0 P\). If the cars move together at \(10\) m/s, then \(P = 5{,}0\cdot10^5\) kg m/s and after the time \(\ln 2/a_0 = 1{,}223\) s it is one half. The graph shows the ratio \(v/v_0\) against the time \(t\), which we put on the axes. One half is the point on the curve \(\mathrm{e}^{-a_0 t}\) at the height \(1/2\) (one half), after \(1\) s there is still \(0{,}5674\) of the initial velocity. The velocity never falls exactly to zero, and the distance travelled by the cars approaches \(v_0/a_0 = 17{,}65\) m with time (\(16{,}6\) m after \(5\) s). On a level track without friction the cars would travel forever, so this is not a property of the cars but a defect of the model.

Lemma 20.41 Damping of rigid-body motion (dušenje togega gibanja)

Let \(\vek s \ne \vek 0\) be a column with \(\mat K\vek s = \vek 0\) (rigid mode, definition 10.33, for the cars sliding together) of a structure with positive definite mass matrix \(\mat M\), and let there be no external forces. Then for the motion \(\vek q = \vek s\,w(t)\):

  1. For \(\mat C = a_0\mat M + a_1\mat K\) (damping force on every mass) we have \(\ddot w + a_0\,\dot w = 0\), so \(\dot w(t) = \dot w(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_0 t}\) (graph), and for \(a_0 > 0\) the distance \(w(t) - w(0)\) approaches \(\dot w(0)/a_0\) as \(t \to \infty\).
  2. If \(\mat C\vek s = \vek 0\), for example for dashpots between two points of the structure (like the buffer between the cars) and for \(\mat C = a_1\mat K\), then \(\ddot w = 0\): the rigid motion is not damped.
Proof

We must show that the equation of motion for such a \(\vek q\) turns into an equation for \(w\), and solve it: the solution is the falling velocity of the cars.

  1. The equation of motion \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek 0\) (theorem 10.3) gives for \(\vek q = \vek s\,w\) the equation \(\mat M\vek s\,\ddot w + \mat C\vek s\,\dot w + \mat K\vek s\,w = \vek 0\). The last term is zero.
  2. For \(\mat C = a_0\mat M + a_1\mat K\) we have \(\mat C\vek s = a_0\mat M\vek s\), so we multiply the equation from the left by \(\vek s^\T\) and divide by the positive \(\vek s^\T\mat M\vek s\): \(\ddot w + a_0\,\dot w = 0\).
  3. For the velocity \(v = \dot w\) this is a first-order equation \(\dot v = -a_0\,v\) and by lemma 9.39 we have \(v(t) = v(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_0 t}\), that is \(\dot w(t) = \dot w(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-a_0 t}\). The motion \(w(t) = w(0) + \dot w(0)\,(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-a_0 t})/a_0\) approaches \(w(0) + \dot w(0)/a_0\) as \(t \to \infty\).
  4. For \(\mat C\vek s = \vek 0\) the second term of step 1 drops out, \(\mat M\vek s\,\ddot w = \vek 0\) remains and \(\ddot w = 0\). A dashpot between the points \(A\) and \(B\) gives by lemma 9.2 a force proportional to \(\dot\ell\), that is to the rate of the distance, and the distance does not change in a rigid motion. For the cars this would be a dashpot beside the buffer: when the cars roll together, the buffer does not shorten and the dashpot beside it gives no force.

Source: CK (PDF p. 334) says that for a damped system with rigid modes special procedures are needed, examples with dashpots between bodies are on PDF p. 338-339. COOK (PDF p. 404) says that proportional damping can be imagined as immersing the structure in a non-physical fluid whose viscosity becomes infinite for rigid motion (\(\omega = 0\)), which corresponds to \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\to\infty\). The proof and the numbers are ours (outlines/ch-dusenje/num-07.py).

The lemma showed two things. For \(a_0 > 0\) the model \(a_0\mat M\) damps rigid motion, which a real system does not have. This is the defect of an external dashpot on a resting wall, which physically does not exist if the structure floats freely. With dashpots between parts of the structure (like the buffer) and with \(a_1\mat K\) rigid motion cannot be damped. So for free structures we must take \(a_0 = 0\) or dashpots between the parts. For the cars with \(a_1\mat K\) and \(a_1 = 5{,}6469\cdot10^{-4}\) s the roots are \(-0{,}0471\pm12{,}9099\,\mathrm{i}\) and a double zero: the elastic mode is damped (\(\zeta = a_1\omega/2 = 0{,}00365\)), and the rigid one has a double root zero, so \(\vek q = \vek s\,(c_1 + c_2 t)\) (theorem 10.34).

20.7.4 Hysteretic damping in elements

So far we had two viscous mechanisms in the time domain. Hysteretic damping (definition 20.12) is written only for harmonic motion, where the stiffness \(k\) is replaced by \(k\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) and \(\gamma\) is the loss factor (definition 20.3). How do we build it into a structure with several elements? Each element can have its own material and its own \(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\). In a Kelvin-Voigt element the loss comes from the dashpot beside the spring, in a hysteretic one the factor \(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\) in the complex stiffness records it. The matrix that we get is called complex, because its real part is the stiffness, which stores energy (storage), and its imaginary part sets the loss of energy in a cycle (section 20.1), as with the complex stiffness \(k\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\).

Definition 20.42 Complex stiffness matrix (kompleksna togostna matrika)

Let the structure have elements (storeys in frame A) with stiffness matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (theorem 7.13) and loss factors \(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) (definition 20.3). The complex stiffness matrix (kompleksna togostna matrika) of the structure is the complex \(n_{\mathrm{u}} \times n_{\mathrm{u}}\) matrix

\[\hat{\mat K} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}})\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat K' + \mathrm{i}\,\mat K'' , \qquad \mat K' = \mat K , \quad \mat K'' = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{20.34}\]

The matrices \(\mat K'\) and \(\mat K''\) are real, symmetric and positive semidefinite (corollary 7.14). The equation of motion for the harmonic force \(\vek Q = \mathrm{Re}(\vek Q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) is

\[\big(-\Omega^2\mat M + \mat K' + \mathrm{i}\,\mat K''\big)\,\vek x = \vek Q_0 , \tag{20.35}\]

where \(\vek x\) is the column of complex amplitudes, \(\vek q = \mathrm{Re}(\vek x\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) (as in equation (10.101)).

Source: GR (3.31)-(3.33) (PDF p. 180-181), where \(E'\) and \(E''\) in (3.32) are called the "storage" and "loss" modulus. The symbols \(\mat K'\) and \(\mat K''\) are from GR, where the prime does not mean a derivative. For the moduli of a material we use \(E_{\mathrm{shr}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) (section 20.6), because the prime in this book means a derivative.

The matrix \(\hat{\mat K}\) is complex symmetric, not Hermitian. Equation (20.35) has the same form as equation (10.101), with \(\mathrm{i}\,\mat K''\) in place of \(\mathrm{i}\Omega\,\mat C\): at \(\Omega\) viscous damping with \(\mat C = \mat K''/\Omega\) equals the hysteretic one (section 20.2). Again: when is it decoupled (section 10.10.1)?

Lemma 20.43 One material: hysteretic damping has real modes (en material: histerezno dušenje ima realne načine)

Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite, \(\mat\Phi\) the modal matrix of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) (definition 10.23) and \(\mat K'' = \gamma\,\mat K\) with the same \(\gamma \ge 0\) in all elements (blue bars). Then:

  1. The eigenproblem \(\big(\mat K' + \mathrm{i}\mat K''\big)\vek u = \lambda\,\mat M\vek u\) has exactly the eigenvalues \(\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = (1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) with real eigencolumns \(\vek u = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the modes of the undamped structure), and \(\mathrm{Im}\,\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\mathrm{Re}\,\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \gamma\) for every mode (the bars are equally high).
  2. For \(\vek x = \mat\Phi\hat{\boldsymbol\eta}\) (definition 10.27) equation (20.35) splits into \(n\) equations \(\big((1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \Omega^2\big)\,\hat\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q_0\), so every mode oscillates as the hysteretic oscillator of section 20.3 with the same loss factor \(\gamma\) (the bars of equal height).
Proof

We must show that the modal matrix decouples the equation and that there are no other eigenvalues, so that the two modes are indeed equally damped.

  1. By lemma 10.24 \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\). The matrix \(\mat\Phi\) is real, so \(\mat\Phi^\T\hat{\mat K}\,\mat\Phi = (1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\).
  2. We multiply equation (20.35) with \(\vek x = \mat\Phi\hat{\boldsymbol\eta}\) from the left by \(\mat\Phi^\T\) and by step 1 get \(\big((1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2) - \Omega^2\mat I\big)\hat{\boldsymbol\eta} = \mat\Phi^\T\vek Q_0\). This is point 2, since the matrix is diagonal.
  3. From \(\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) it follows that \((1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\,\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = (1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \((\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) is an eigenpair.
  4. For an arbitrary number \(\mu\) we have \(\det(\hat{\mat K} - \mu\mat M) = (1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)^n\det\big(\mat K - \tfrac{\mu}{1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma}\mat M\big)\) (definition 2.72, we factor \(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma\) out of every row). This is zero exactly at \(\mu/(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), so the only eigenvalues are \(\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and there are no others.

Source: GR (3.39) (PDF p. 182) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} = \gamma/2\) for \(\mat K'' = \gamma\mat K'\) ("the damping remains proportional"). The proof is ours.

The lemma showed that one material with one loss factor does not change the shapes of the modes, which stay real, and that every mode loses the same share of energy. At resonance the ratio is by theorem 20.14 \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = \gamma/2\). How far does this hold when the materials are not the same? The example is frame A with \(\gamma = 0{,}04\).

On the horizontal axis we put the number \(r\) of the mode, on the vertical one its loss factor \(\gamma_{\mathrm{r}}\). The first pair of bars is the frame with the same \(\gamma = 0{,}04\) in both storeys: the eigenvalues are \(383{,}311 + 15{,}332\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(2627{,}249 + 105{,}090\,\mathrm{i}\) and the ratios of the imaginary and real part are by point 1 of the lemma \(0{,}04\) and \(0{,}04\) (blue bars). The second pair is the frame in which \(\gamma = 0{,}04\) is only in the lower storey, where there is for example a rubber bearing (the upper storey has \(\gamma = 0\)). Then \(\mat K'' = 0{,}04\,\mat K_1 \ne \gamma\mat K\) and the eigenvalues are \(383{,}454 + 11{,}093\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(2627{,}106 + 29{,}047\,\mathrm{i}\) with the ratios \(0{,}02893\) and \(0{,}01106\), so different from \(\gamma\) and from each other. The reason is the distribution of strain energy. The strain energy of the columns of a storey grows with the square of its drift. In the first mode the drifts of the storeys are in the ratio \(1 : 0{,}618\) (the blue distances of the left frame), so the lower storey holds \(1/(1 + 0{,}618^2) = 72{,}36\,\%\) of the energy and \(0{,}04\cdot 0{,}7236 = 0{,}02894\). In the second the ratio is \(1 : 1{,}618\) (the drift of the upper storey of the right frame) and the lower storey holds \(1/(1 + 1{,}618^2) = 27{,}64\,\%\) of the energy and \(0{,}04\cdot0{,}2764 = 0{,}01106\). The eigencolumns are no longer real, \(u_2/u_1 = 1{,}61789 + 0{,}02895\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-0{,}61789 + 0{,}01105\,\mathrm{i}\), a complex ratio as in section 10.10.4. The lemma does not hold, because its condition \(\mat K'' = \gamma\mat K\) is missing.

GR (PDF p. 181-182) proposes an estimate: if the damping is light and the natural frequencies are well separated, we take only the diagonal terms of the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat K''\,\mat\Phi\), and every mode gets the loss factor

\[\gamma_{\mathrm{r}} \approx \frac{\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K''\,\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} , \qquad \zeta_{\mathrm{r}} \approx \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{r}}}{2} . \tag{20.36}\]

For frame A the estimate gives \(0{,}02894\) and \(0{,}01106\) (grey ticks). For the first mode this is \(0{,}05\,\%\) above the true value \(0{,}028930\), for the second \(0{,}01\,\%\) below the true value \(0{,}011057\), which does not show to four decimal places. The damping is light here: \(\mat K''\) is at most \(4\,\%\) of the matrix \(\mat K\), and the error of the estimate is of the order of the square of this ratio, \(0{,}04^2 = 0{,}16\,\%\), as with the coupling of modes in section 10.10.4, and the measured one is \(0{,}05\,\%\). This is an estimate, not a theorem: a complete treatment requires the eigenproblem of a complex symmetric matrix, which section 20.8 does for viscous damping, and for hysteretic damping GR only states it. A warning: in GR the equations (3.35), (3.37) and (3.38) have \(\mat K'\) printed and not \(\mat K''\) (PDF p. 181-182). This is a misprint, since for \(\mat K'' = \gamma\mat K'\) equation (3.38) would give \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/2\), while equation (3.39) says \(\gamma/2\). Correct is equation (20.36) with the matrix \(\mat K''\).

20.7.5 Material that depends on frequency

The loss factor of a real material is not constant (section 20.6): for the standard linear solid with \(\gamma(\Omega)\) from theorem 20.33 it is small at small and at large \(\Omega\tau\). A finite element with a material that has the complex modulus \(\hat E(\Omega)\) (definition 20.32) gets the stiffness matrix \(\big(\hat E(\Omega)/E\big)\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), if \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is computed with the modulus \(E\). This is our step, not a result from a source: GR (3.32) (PDF p. 181) has \(E'\) and \(E''\) as constants, and we replace them by functions of frequency. No source in the book builds frequency-dependent viscoelastic damping into element matrices (outlines/ch-dusenje/outline.md, O4). The matrix of the structure is therefore \(\hat{\mat K}(\Omega) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\big(\hat E_{\mathrm{e}}(\Omega)/E_{\mathrm{e}}\big)\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\).

We test on frame A, which in both storeys is made of the same standard linear solid with \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\) (section 20.4, a chosen model, not a material), and the stiffness \(k\) is computed with \(E_\infty\). The complex modulus is then \(\hat E/E_\infty = 1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau/(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau) =: c(\Omega)\) (at \(\Omega\tau = 1\) it is \(1{,}5 + 0{,}5\,\mathrm{i}\), as in section 20.6), and \(\hat{\mat K} = c(\Omega)\,\mat K\). On the horizontal axis of the left graph we put \(\Omega\tau\) on a logarithmic scale, on the vertical one the real part \(\mathrm{Re}\,c = 1 + \Omega^2\tau^2/(1 + \Omega^2\tau^2)\). The curve rises from the lower dashed bound \(1\), the modulus \(E_\infty\), to the upper bound \(2\), the modulus \(E_0\). On the right, over the same axis, is the loss factor \(\gamma = \mathrm{Im}\,c/\mathrm{Re}\,c = \Omega\tau/(1 + 2\,\Omega^2\tau^2)\), which has the peak \(0{,}354\) at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}707\) and falls on both sides. The same conclusion as in lemma 20.43 holds for the complex scalar \(c\): the modes are real, every mode has the equation \(\big(c(\Omega)\,\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2 - \Omega^2\big)\hat\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q_0\). The amplitude is largest approximately where \(\Omega^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\mathrm{Re}\,c(\Omega)\), since \(c\) changes slowly with frequency (the curve is almost level to the right of \(\Omega\tau = 10\)). Take \(\tau = 10/\omega_1 = 0{,}5108\) s. For the first mode we get resonance at \(\Omega = 27{,}65\) 1/s (\(\Omega\tau = 14{,}1\)) and for the second at \(72{,}48\) 1/s (\(\Omega\tau = 37{,}0\)), the orange dots on the left graph, loss factors \(\gamma = 0{,}0353\) and \(0{,}0135\) (the dots on the right graph), so \(\zeta \approx 0{,}0177\) and \(0{,}0068\). The approximation of the resonance costs little: the true peak of \(|\hat\eta_1|\) is at \(27{,}66\) 1/s, only \(0{,}03\,\%\) higher, for the second mode only \(0{,}005\,\%\) higher. Two lessons. First: the resonance of the first mode moved up by the factor \(1{,}412 = \sqrt{1{,}995}\) (of the second by \(1{,}414 = \sqrt{1{,}999}\)), because at large \(\Omega\tau\) the material is already almost at its instantaneous modulus \(E_0 = 2E_\infty\) (\(\mathrm{Re}\,c = 1{,}995\) and \(1{,}999\), the dots lie just below the bound \(2\)). The frame is therefore almost twice as stiff, and its natural frequencies are almost \(\sqrt 2\) times higher. Second: the two modes do not have the same loss factor, because \(\gamma(\Omega)\) is a function of frequency: the dot of the second mode lies on the falling side of the bell, lower than the dot of the first. A model with a single \(\gamma\) would prescribe the same value. The damping is light at both resonances (\(\gamma < 0{,}04\)), so the estimate \(\zeta \approx \gamma/2\) is reasonable, but it holds only for oscillation near each resonance.

20.7.6 What we have obtained

In finite elements the damping matrix has a precisely determined origin when we know it: the material (Kelvin-Voigt) gives \(\tau_\varepsilon\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), the external dashpot \((c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 20.39), and the assembly is the same as for stiffness (theorem 20.40). With the same constants in all elements this is Rayleigh damping and classical. Its constant \(a_0\) gets the meaning of dashpots that tie each mass to the resting wall, and \(a_1\) the meaning of the viscosity of the material. With different constants it is not classical, which we saw on frame A. In the frequency domain: one material with one loss factor gives real modes with the same \(\gamma\) (lemma 20.43), several materials do not, and we estimate their factors by equation (20.36). In a free structure the dashpots \(a_0\mat M\) on the resting wall damp the rigid motion, which the real system does not have, while dashpots between parts of the structure and \(a_1\mat K\) do not damp it (lemma 20.41). We reach the first-order form and the complex modes, which treat all non-classical cases, in section 20.8.
