Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.1 The tension test

How strongly a material resists stretching cannot be computed from equilibrium. We have to measure it. The simplest test is the one we have been doing in thought since the beginning of the book: we pull a bar along its axis and measure the force and the elongation. It is called the tension test (natezni preizkus). The goal of this section is to get from the tension test the relation between stress and strain for a given material, to write it with two numbers, and to use it to answer two questions we left open: why in section 2.2 the quotient of the stress in the hanging bar and the number 196 GPa gave its strain, and what its displacement \(u(x)\) is.

Why a bar? Because in its middle part the stress and the strain are the same at all points. In a bar pulled by a force \(F\) at both ends, the axial force in every cross section is \(N = F\) (section 2.1). Far from the ends this force is spread evenly over the cross section (section 2.2). If the material is the same along the whole bar, all pieces between marks on the bar therefore get longer by the same amount (figure 1.12, middle bar). So we get the stress \(N/A\) and the strain \(\Delta L/L\) from one measured force and one measured elongation, and both hold at every point between the two marks. In a body of a more complicated shape the stress and the strain would change from point to point, and from one force and one displacement we would not learn which stress belongs to which strain. This is why we measure the relation between stress and strain on a specimen in which both are uniform (BOWER, PDF p. 93).

From history

Hooke, Mariotte and Coulomb, whom we met in section 1.1, did their experiments mainly to check a particular theory. In the eighteenth century, however, builders needed data on the strength of the materials they used (TIMO, PDF p. 62). Petrus van Musschenbroek, professor of physics at Utrecht and later at Leiden, therefore carried out an extensive series of tests. In a work of 1729 he described a machine that pulled the bar through levers that magnified the force. With it he could break wood and steel only when the cross section of the bar was small. The force that breaks a bar is proportional to the area of the cross section, as Galileo found, and the machine could not produce the force needed for a large cross section. So from the results on small bars he showed how to compute the ultimate load of large beams (TIMO, PDF p. 62-63).

Earlier experimenters usually measured only the largest force the bar carries. When iron and steel entered building and machine construction, this was no longer enough, because the properties of these metals depend on how they are made, and the largest force alone does not tell whether a metal suits a given purpose (TIMO, PDF p. 215). David Kirkaldy, for the firm Robert Napier and Sons, which had ordered high-pressure boilers and marine engines where lightness and strength were most important, tested from April 1858 to September 1861 two new kinds of metal meant to replace wrought iron. The tests grew far beyond what was first intended, and in 1865 he opened a materials testing laboratory in Southwark, London (TIMO, PDF p. 215-216).

Kirkaldy sometimes drew marks on the bars and measured the distances between them after the test. In this way he showed that along most of its length the bar stretches uniformly and stays cylindrical, and only near the fracture is the strain larger and the cross section locally narrower. Small elongations, after which a bar returns to its original length, he could not measure with short bars and crude instruments (TIMO, PDF p. 216). To measure such elongations accurately Eaton Hodgkinson used rods 50 feet long, about 15 m (TIMO, PDF p. 216).

Let us return to measuring the strain \(\Delta L/L\) on a bar. At the same strain \(\Delta L/L\) the elongation \(\Delta L\) is proportional to the length \(L\), and a longer elongation is easier to measure. Both difficulties, the uniformity of the strain and the smallness of the elongations, are answered by the marks on the bar and a device that measures their distance, as we describe below (section 3.1.1).

3.1.1 The specimen and the measurement

The bar we test is called the specimen (preizkušanec). Usually it is a round or flat bar with two thicker ends, the heads, between which there is a long thinner middle part of constant cross section (SADD §4.1, PDF p. 90). The heads are clamped in the grips of a testing machine (preizkuševalni stroj), which slowly moves them apart and so pulls the specimen (figure 3.1). On the middle part we draw two marks at a distance \(L\), which is called the gauge length (merilna dolžina). A load cell (merilnik sile) between the upper crosshead of the machine and the upper grips measures the force \(F\) with which the machine pulls. An extensometer (merilnik raztezka) touches the marks with two knife edges and measures by how much the distance between them grows (SADD, PDF p. 90). The books we build on describe the test only briefly. The exact shapes of specimens and the procedures are prescribed by standards, which we do not treat here.

Why does the specimen have this shape (figure 3.1, right)? The grips squeeze the heads and pull them only over their surface, so near the heads the force is not spread evenly over the cross section, just as near the pins in figure 2.10. We therefore put the marks on the middle part, far from the heads. The middle part is thinner than the heads, so the stress \(F/A\) is largest there, since the same force is carried by a smaller area. If we load the specimen beyond the yield stress, it therefore gets permanently longer in the middle part, between the marks, and not in the grips.

From the measured force and elongation we compute the stress and the strain. In doing so we assume three things, each for a reason.

  1. The axial force in every cross section between the marks equals the force \(F\) of the machine. This is so when the specimen is in equilibrium and its weight is negligible (section 2.1). That is why the machine pulls slowly: in this way the specimen is practically in equilibrium at every moment. And the weight really is negligible. A steel middle part with diameter 10 mm, that is, with the cross-sectional area \(78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and length 50 mm weighs \(\rho g A L = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2 \cdot 0{,}05\ \text{m} = 0{,}30\ \text{N}\), while the machine pulls with a force of several thousand newtons.
  2. Far from the heads the force is spread evenly over the cross section (section 2.2).
  3. The material is the same everywhere between the marks, so all pieces between them get longer by the same amount.

Then at every point between the marks the stress is \(\sigma = F/A\), where \(A\) is the area of the cross section of the middle part before loading (definition 2.6), and the strain is \(\varepsilon = \Delta L/L\) (definition 1.2). So the stress is nominal. For steel the area of the cross section decreases by \(0{,}06\) % at the strain \(0{,}001\) (section 2.2), so by less than a tenth of a percent up to the yield stress, and the difference from the force per unit area after loading is negligible. For large strains it is not, and these we treated in section 2.13.

We put axis \(x\) along the axis of the specimen and axes \(y\) and \(z\) across it (figure 3.1, right). Then the stress \(\sigma\) is the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the only nonzero stress component in a bar pulled by an axial force (section 2.6.6). The strain \(\Delta L/L\) is the normal strain in the direction of axis \(x\), which since section 1.17 we write \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). From here on we therefore write

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{F}{A} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\Delta L}{L} .\]

3.1.2 The straight line and its slope

The machine slowly increases the force, and at each force we write down the stress and the strain. Each pair of numbers is a point in the graph of stress against strain (figure 3.2). For steel and most other materials the following shows up, as long as the stress is not too large (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96, SADD §4.1, PDF pp. 90-91).

  1. The points lie on a straight line through the origin. The stress is proportional to the strain: at twice the stress the strain is twice as large.
  2. When we decrease the force, the points go back along the same line. At zero force the strain is zero too, and the specimen returns to its original length.
  3. The strain depends only on the stress, not on how fast we pull and not on how the specimen was loaded before.

Above some stress the graph bends. If we unload the specimen then, it no longer returns to its original length. For steel this happens at the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) (definition 2.7, figure 2.11). SADD distinguishes three limits: the proportional limit (meja sorazmernosti), up to which the graph is a straight line, the elastic limit (meja elastičnosti), up to which the specimen returns to its original length after unloading, and the yield point, where large permanent strains begin. For many materials all three are taken to be about the same (SADD, PDF p. 90). BOWER adds that the limit is hard to determine accurately, because the graph bends gradually (BOWER, PDF p. 142).

On the line the quotient \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the same for all points. It is the slope of the line (section 1.3.2), that is, how much the line rises per unit of horizontal run. We denote it by \(E\). Figure 3.2 shows it with a triangle: over a horizontal run \(\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) the line rises by \(E\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). This slope is the number that tells how strongly the material resists stretching.

From history

In his lectures of 1807 Young was the first to write down a number that describes how a substance resists pulling and compression, and he gave it by a column of the same substance, as we saw in chapter 2 (figure 2.9), but differently from us (TIMO, PDF p. 100). The weight of the column is to the weight that compresses the substance by a given amount as the length of the substance is to its shortening.

Let us return to the slope \(E\) of our graph and express it by Young's rule. If some weight \(W\) compresses the substance by the fraction \(\varepsilon\) of its length, the weight of the column is \(W/\varepsilon\), that is, \(1/\varepsilon\) times larger. This weight is the product of the slope \(E\) and the cross-sectional area \(A\). This is our calculation on Young's basis. If \(W = \sigma A\), then from \(E A = \sigma A/\varepsilon\) it follows that \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon\), which is our line. For \(\varepsilon = 1\) we get \(\sigma = E\), the stress at which the substance would shorten by its whole length (TIMO, PDF p. 100).

From history

In describing experiments of tension and compression of bars, Young found that the proportionality of force and strain, which Hooke had written down, holds only up to a limit, beyond which a part of the strain is inelastic and remains as permanent set, as our graph shows (TIMO, PDF p. 100). For steel he estimated the stress at which the substance would shorten by its whole length from the frequency of vibration of a tuning fork and obtained \(29 \cdot 10^6\) pounds per square inch, which is about 200 GPa (TIMO, PDF p. 100).

TIMO does not say why the frequency of vibration of a tuning fork gives the slope \(E\) of the graph for steel, so we give the reason ourselves. A bar vibrating along its axis has by GR (4.134), PDF p. 264, the natural frequencies \(\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = (2k-1)\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{EA/(m\ell^2)}\), where \(m = \rho A\) is the mass per unit length (GR, PDF p. 262). Since \(A\) cancels, \(\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = (2k-1)\,\tfrac{\pi}{2\ell}\sqrt{E/\rho}\). A fork bends instead of lengthening, and by GR (4.247), PDF p. 287, its frequency is likewise a product of \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\) and a quantity fixed by the shape alone. A higher pitch means a larger \(E\) or a smaller density \(\rho\). For a known shape and the density of steel, the measured pitch therefore gives the modulus. The slope of our graph is thus the number that Young described with the weight of a column. So we give it a name.

Definition 3.1 Young's modulus (modul elastičnosti)

A bar of some material is pulled along its axis, which lies in the direction of axis \(x\), so that only the stress component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A\) is nonzero. Its strain along the axis is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \Delta L/L\). Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) be proportional for all stresses up to some limit. Young's modulus (modul elastičnosti) of the material in the direction of the bar's axis is the number \(E\) for which, for all these stresses,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} . \tag{3.1}\]

Source: BOWER §3.2.6 (PDF p. 102), SADD §4.1 and §4.3.1 (PDF pp. 91 and 95), CM (6.3.40) (PDF p. 267).

The modulus of elasticity is a number with the unit of stress, since strain has no unit. Books also call it Young's modulus, after Young, who in his lectures of 1807 was the first to call this number the modulus of elasticity (TIMO, PDF p. 100). It is a property of the material and not of the bar. For a material that behaves differently in different directions, however, it depends on the direction in which the bar is cut from the material (section 3.4). A bar of the same steel that is twice as long or twice as thick gives the same line, because the stress and the strain are already taken per unit area and per unit length. The number 196 GPa, which BOWER gives for mild steel and which we used without a name in section 2.2, is the modulus of elasticity of this steel (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Now we see why the quotient of the stress and this number gave the strain there. We divide equation (3.1) by \(E\) and get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\).

How can we picture such a large number? If we put \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 1\) into (3.1), we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\). So the modulus of elasticity is the stress at which the bar would get longer by its whole length, as with Young's column, if the line reached that far. It does not. Mild steel starts to yield already at the strain

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}}{E} = \frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = 0{,}00112 ,\]

which is the thousandth from section 1.1 above which steel no longer returns to its original length. A larger modulus means a smaller strain at the same stress, so Young's modulus is a measure of the stiffness of the material (BOWER, PDF p. 102).

With Young's modulus we compute the elongation of a bar pulled by an axial force. Let the bar have length \(L\), let the area of its cross section be \(A\), let the axial force \(N\) be the same in all cross sections, and let the stress be in the range in which the graph of the tension test is a straight line.

  1. By definition 2.6 the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A\).
  2. The stress is in the range of the line, so (3.1) holds. We divide it by \(E\) and insert step 1: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E = N/(EA)\).
  3. By definition 1.2, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \Delta L/L\), so \(\Delta L = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,L\). We insert step 2:
    \[\Delta L = \frac{N L}{E A} . \tag{3.2}\]

CM writes the elongation of a bar in the same way (PDF p. 316). The formula holds only when the axial force is the same in all cross sections of the bar. In the hanging bar it is not, and there in section 3.1.5 we will compute differently, point by point. The elongation is proportional to the force and to the length and inversely proportional to Young's modulus and to the area of the cross section. We have met each of these patterns already. A bar twice as long gets longer by twice as much under the same force, as we found in section 1.1 for the bars of length 1 m and 2 m (figure 1.4), which is why \(L\) is in the numerator. A bar with twice the area of the cross section gets longer by half as much, because the stress in it is half as large, as with the two thin bars glued into one thick bar (figure 2.8), which is why \(A\) is in the denominator. We can also write the equation as \(N = (EA/L)\,\Delta L\). Under an axial force the bar behaves like a spring whose force is proportional to its elongation, with the spring constant \(EA/L\) (EP, p. 191).

Let us try this on the steel bar of chapter 2 with diameter 10 mm, pulled with the force 10 kN. The area of its cross section is \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\). The stress \(127{,}3\) MPa is 58 % of the yield stress, so we may use (3.1). By step 2 the strain is

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{N}{EA} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} .\]

The units cancel because \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2\). If we make a specimen with gauge length 50 mm from this bar, the extensometer shows \(\Delta L = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 50\ \text{mm} = 0{,}03248\) mm, a little over three hundredths of a millimetre. A bar 10 m long, like the hanging bar, gets longer under the same force by

\[\Delta L = \frac{N L}{E A} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 10\ \text{m}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 6{,}496\ \text{mm} .\]

3.1.3 The bar gets thinner

When the specimen gets longer, it gets thinner at the same time. We described this for steel already in section 1.6: when a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about \(0{,}3\) thousandths. In the test we also measure this, the lateral strain, from the change of the diameter \(d\) of the middle part (SADD §4.3.1, PDF p. 95). The diameter lies in the direction of axis \(y\). Its change \(\Delta d\) divided by \(d\) is therefore the normal strain in the direction of axis \(y\) (figure 3.4, left):

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\Delta d}{d} .\]

The diameter decreases, so \(\Delta d\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) are negative.

In the range in which the stress graph is a straight line, the lateral strain is also proportional to the axial strain. The points \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\) lie on a falling straight line through the origin (figure 3.4, right), as BOWER also draws them (Figure 3.5, PDF p. 97). The quotient of the two strains is therefore a constant number for a given material.

From history

Already Young, in experiments of tension and compression of bars, drew attention to the fact that a longitudinal strain is always accompanied by a change of the lateral dimensions (TIMO, PDF p. 100). In 1821, as we saw in chapter 1, Navier described the body with molecules, between which forces act along the lines joining them (figure 1.1). For a body that behaves the same in all directions he obtained with them a relation between forces and strains with a single constant (TIMO, PDF p. 129 and 189). How many constants are needed without molecules we say in section 3.2, and why molecules give only one, in section 3.4.

If a material is described by a single constant, for example the modulus of elasticity \(E\), materials differ only in it. The ratio of the lateral thinning to the longitudinal elongation must therefore be the same number for all such materials. Poisson derived that in a bar of a material that behaves the same in all directions this ratio is always \(1/4\) (TP1, PDF p. 253). The ratio was measured by Wertheim, who was born in Vienna and moved to Paris in 1840. In 1848 he pulled glass and metal tubes along their axis and measured how their internal volume changes (figure 3.3). This is the product of the area of the inner cross section and the length, so from the measured elongation and the change of volume it follows how much the cross section decreased, that is, the lateral thinning. The results did not agree with Poisson's \(1/4\), and the thinning could be explained only with two constants (TIMO, PDF p. 192).

Love in the fourth edition of his book of 1922 writes that the best experiments support the conclusion that the ratio can differ sensibly from \(1/4\) even in materials that may without cavil be treated as alike in all directions and at all points (LOVE, PDF p. 3 and 31).

So we do not derive the ratio of transverse narrowing to longitudinal extension from theory but measure it for each material. For mild steel it is \(0{,}3\) (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). The modulus of elasticity and this ratio are therefore independent numbers of a material in this book.

Definition 3.2 Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število)

A bar of some material is pulled along its axis, which lies in the direction of axis \(x\), and let the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) be proportional to the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) along the axis. Let the normal strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) in the direction of axis \(y\), perpendicular to the axis of the bar, also be proportional to \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) in this range. Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število) of the material for the bar direction \(x\) and the lateral direction \(y\) is the number

\[\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}} . \tag{3.3}\]

Source: BOWER §3.2.6 (PDF p. 102), SADD §4.3.1 (PDF p. 95), CM §6.3.4 (PDF pp. 264-265).

The minus in the definition makes \(\nu\) a positive number for materials that get thinner when stretched. For them \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is positive, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) negative, their quotient negative, and the minus turns it around. Poisson's ratio has no unit, because it is a quotient of two strains. The Greek letter \(\nu\) is called nu. For mild steel \(\nu = 0{,}30\) (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). The number \(0{,}3\), which in section 1.6 we met as the ratio of thinning to stretching for steel and which stood in the displacement field of the hanging bar (1.18), is therefore Poisson's ratio of steel.

What other values tell us, we saw already in section 1.6. A material that keeps its volume exactly when stretched has \(\nu = 0{,}5\), and a material that does not get thinner at all when stretched has \(\nu = 0\). For most materials \(\nu\) lies between \(0{,}2\) and \(0{,}49\), for metals around \(0{,}3\). There are also unusual materials with negative \(\nu\), which get thicker when stretched (BOWER, PDF p. 102). BOWER states that Poisson's ratio of every stable material lies between \(-1\) and \(0{,}5\), and that Young's modulus is positive (BOWER, PDF p. 102). What stable means we will say in section 3.6: to deform such a material in any way we must do work. From this we will prove both bounds there.

Why do we need \(\nu\) besides \(E\)? Young's modulus tells how the bar gets longer, Poisson's ratio how it gets thinner at the same time. For a material that behaves the same in all directions these two numbers will be enough to write the relation between all components of stress and strain (section 3.2).

For the steel bar with diameter 10 mm pulled with 10 kN we computed \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4}\). By (3.3), therefore,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}3 \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-4} = -1{,}949 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

and the diameter changes by

\[\Delta d = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,d = -1{,}949 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 10\ \text{mm} = -1{,}949 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{mm} ,\]

so it decreases by almost two micrometres, from 10 mm to \(9{,}998\) mm. In a round steel specimen the contraction is the same in all lateral directions, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), because steel behaves the same in all directions, and the cross section stays a circle, as for the hanging bar in figure 1.42. For a material that behaves differently in different directions, like wood, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) can differ, and such a material needs several Poisson's ratios (CM §6.3.4, PDF pp. 263-265). We will turn to such materials in section 3.4.

The table gives Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio for a few materials (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). Where a range is given, the values for different kinds of the material differ. Wood is built of thin fibres that run along the trunk, and the row for wood holds for tension along these fibres, which is called along the grain. These fibres are real threads of matter, not the imagined segments of definition 1.24.

The values in tables are typical, not exact. SADD gives \(E = 207\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}29\) for steel (SADD, Table 4.2, PDF p. 97), because these are average values and individual materials differ somewhat (SADD, PDF p. 96). In this book we stay with 196 GPa and \(0{,}3\). Young's moduli differ between materials much more than Poisson's ratios. The bounds from BOWER allow this: Poisson's ratio of a stable material must lie between \(-1\) and \(0{,}5\), as given above, while Young's modulus only has to be positive and has no upper bound. Figure 3.5 shows the lines of four materials on the same scale. At the same strain \(0{,}001\) steel carries a stress of 196 MPa, wood along the grain only about 12 MPa.

Two materials in the table need a remark. Rubber has a Young's modulus in the table, but at the large strains at which it is usually used, for example in the rubber band of section 1.1 with strain \(0{,}5\), its stress is no longer proportional to its strain. Books therefore describe it with models in which the relation is not a straight line (BOWER §3.5, PDF p. 120). Its Poisson's ratio \(0{,}49\) has a simple cause. Rubber resists a change of volume very strongly but changes its shape very easily (BOWER, PDF p. 121). When we stretch it, it therefore almost keeps its volume, and a material that keeps its volume exactly has \(\nu = 0{,}5\). Wood, on the other hand, is much stiffer along the grain than across it (BOWER §3.2.8, PDF p. 103), which is why the table gives the modulus only for the direction along the grain. For wood a single number \(E\) is not enough.

3.1.4 The idealized material

What we observed in the test we now write down as a model of the material. The model is an idealization: it tells how the material behaves, but only within the limits in which we tested it. It assumes two of the three observations from the beginning of section 3.1.2: that the stress depends only on the current strain (observation 3) and that it depends on it proportionally (observation 1). The remaining observation follows from these two, as we will see right after the definition.

Definition 3.3 Linear elastic material (linearno elastičen material)

A material is elastic (elastičen) when the stress in a bar of this material in the tension test depends at every moment only on the strain at that moment, not on the rate of loading and not on how the bar was loaded before. An elastic material is linear elastic (linearno elastičen) when this dependence is the proportionality

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} ,\]

where \(E\) is Young's modulus of the material.

Source: CM §6.2.1 (PDF p. 255), BOWER §3.2.1 (PDF p. 96), SADD §4.1 (PDF pp. 89-91).

What we saw in the test as observation 2 follows from the definition. As we unload the bar, the stress decreases, and at each stress the strain is again \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\), the same as during loading. So the point goes back along the same line. At zero stress the strain is \(0/E = 0\), and the bar returns to its original length. CM calls a material whose stress depends only on the current strain Cauchy-elastic (Cauchyjevo elastičen) (CM §6.2.1, PDF p. 255). In section 3.6 we will meet a stricter requirement, which concerns the work needed for a deformation.

The definition speaks of the tension test, in which only one stress component, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), is nonzero. This is a special case. In a body there are six different components of stress and six of strain, and there a linear elastic material means that each stress component is a sum of the six strain components, each multiplied by a constant number (SADD (4.2.1), PDF p. 91). How we build this relation from the tension test we will see in sections 3.2 and 3.4.

The second question is whether the material is the same in all directions. Figure 3.6 shows how we check this. From a block of steel we cut specimens in different directions. If they give the same line, the steel is the same in these directions. With wood it is not so: a specimen cut along the grain is much stiffer than a specimen cut across it (BOWER, PDF p. 103).

Definition 3.4 Isotropic material (izotropen material)

A material is isotropic (izotropen) when its properties at each point are the same in all directions. For the relation between stress and strain this means: bars cut from the neighbourhood of the same point in different directions give the same graph of stress against strain in the tension test and the same Poisson's ratio. A material that is not isotropic is anisotropic (anizotropen).

Source: CM §6.1.1 (PDF p. 251), BOWER §3.2.1 (PDF p. 96), SADD §4.2 (PDF p. 92).

Why is steel isotropic, although it is made of tiny crystal grains, each of which behaves differently in different directions (BOWER, PDF p. 103)? The grains are oriented at random, in all directions equally often, so in a piece containing many grains the differences average out (SADD, PDF p. 92). This is the same reasoning as for the continuum in definition 1.1: the model describes the average over a piece that is large compared with the grains and small compared with the body (BOWER, PDF p. 93). When the grains line up in a preferred direction during the processing of a metal, the metal is anisotropic too (BOWER, PDF p. 103).

The third question is whether the material is the same at all points of the body. We check this too with specimens cut from different parts of the body in the same direction (figure 3.7).

Definition 3.5 Homogeneous material (homogen material)

The material of a body is homogeneous (homogen) when its properties are the same at all points of the body, that is, they do not depend on the position of the point. For the relation between stress and strain this means: bars cut from different parts of the body in the same direction give the same graph of stress against strain in the tension test and the same Poisson's ratio.

Source: CM §6.1.1 (PDF p. 251), SADD §4.2 (PDF p. 92).

Isotropy concerns the directions at one point, homogeneity different points. The two properties are independent: a material can be homogeneous and anisotropic, or isotropic and inhomogeneous (CM, PDF p. 251). Soil is often stiffer deeper down than near the surface, so it is not homogeneous (SADD, PDF p. 92, figure 3.7, bottom). For a homogeneous material \(E\) and \(\nu\) are two numbers for the whole body, for an inhomogeneous one they would depend on the position of the point.

The model we will use in this chapter and in most of the book is a linear elastic, isotropic and homogeneous material. It is described by two numbers, \(E\) and \(\nu\). For steel structures and machine parts it is a good model, but only under the conditions in which we measured it. Let us list them, each with the limit where it fails.

  1. Small strains. We computed the stress with the area of the cross section before loading and the strain as \(\Delta L/L\). When the strains are small, it does not matter which area and which strain measure we use (BOWER, PDF p. 96). For steel below the yield stress the area of the cross section changes by less than a tenth of a percent, and the strain measures of section 1.1 differ by half a permille at the strain \(0{,}001\). For the rubber band with strain \(0{,}5\) this is not so, and then we need the stresses of section 2.13.
  2. Stresses in the range of the line. Above the yield stress a permanent strain remains after unloading (figure 2.11), and the material is no longer elastic. Such behaviour is described by other models (BOWER §3.7, PDF pp. 142-156).
  3. Slow loading. We did the test slowly, so that the specimen was in equilibrium at every moment. BOWER states that at sufficiently small stresses the strain of almost any material does not depend on the rate of loading (BOWER, PDF p. 96). For polymers such as nylon, however, the behaviour often depends strongly on the rate of loading, on the temperature and on earlier loadings (BOWER §3.5, PDF p. 120). Such materials are called viscoelastic (viskoelastični), and we will meet them in chapter 20.
  4. Constant temperature. A heated bar gets longer even without a force (BOWER, PDF p. 96). We will add this part of the strain in section 3.3. The modulus itself can also depend on the temperature. Rubber gets stiffer when heated, which is just the opposite of metals (BOWER, PDF p. 121).
  5. Isotropy and homogeneity. They do not hold for wood, for fibre-reinforced composites, for single crystals (BOWER, PDF p. 103) and for soil, whose stiffness changes with depth (SADD, PDF p. 92). We will treat the first three in section 3.4.

3.1.5 The hanging bar to the end

Now we can finish the computation we have owed since chapter 1. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a round cross section of diameter 10 mm hangs under its own weight. The density of steel is \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, the gravitational acceleration \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s². The bar is held in the idealized way described in section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\) at the centre of the top face is fixed, and the top face is pulled up by a force spread evenly over it. Axis \(x\) points vertically down from \(O\), and the coordinate \(x\) is the depth of a point below the top face before loading. In section 2.2 we computed from equilibrium the stress in the cross section at the depth \(x\),

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x) = \rho g\,(L - x) .\]

From it we want to compute the strain and the displacement of the points on the axis of the bar, which in section 1.2 we took from the books.

Which model do we use? Let steel be a linear elastic, isotropic and homogeneous material with modulus \(E = 196\) GPa. The stress at the top, \(0{,}765\) MPa, is 288 times smaller than the yield stress, so we are far inside the range of the line. But we need one more assumption, because the stress in the hanging bar changes with depth, while in the specimen it was the same everywhere. We assume that the stress at a point depends only on the strain of the material close to that point (BOWER, PDF p. 93). The relation (3.1), measured on a specimen in which stress and strain are the same everywhere, then holds at each point separately.

Is this assumption sensible for the hanging bar? A short piece of the bar between the depths \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is loaded like a small specimen (figure 3.8). The part of the bar above pulls it up with the force \(N(x)\), the part below pulls it down with the force \(N(x + \Delta x)\). Nothing pulls it or squeezes it sideways, since the only nonzero stress component in the bar is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). The only difference from a specimen is the weight of the piece, \(\rho g A\,\Delta x\), by which \(N(x)\) is larger than \(N(x + \Delta x)\). This difference goes to zero as the piece gets shorter.

The computation goes in steps.

  1. The stress at the point on the axis at the depth \(x\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\).
  2. By (3.1), applied at this point, the strain there is
    \[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(x) = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x)}{E} = \frac{\rho g\,(L - x)}{E} . \tag{3.4}\]
    At the top, at \(x = 0\), this is \(\rho g L/E = 765\,180\ \text{Pa}/(196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}) = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the number from section 2.2.
  3. The strain at a point is the derivative of the displacement, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(x) = u'(x)\) (definition 1.10). At the top, at \(x = 0\), and at the bottom, at \(x = L\), this is a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), since we know the displacement only on the bar, for \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). When the displacement is a polynomial, which has an ordinary derivative at the end point, this derivative is also the one-sided one, because a limit on both sides of zero is also a limit from one side (section 1.3.3). So we look for a function \(u\) whose derivative is (3.4) and whose value at the top is \(u(0) = 0\), because the point \(O\) is fixed.
  4. By lemma 1.81 on the rules of differentiation, the derivative of the function \(x\) is \(1\), the derivative of the function \(x^2\) is \(2x\), the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives and the derivative of a multiple is the multiple of the derivative. The function
    \[u(x) = \frac{\rho g L}{E}\,x - \frac{\rho g}{2E}\,x^2\]
    therefore has the derivative \(\rho g L/E - (\rho g/(2E)) \cdot 2x = \rho g\,(L - x)/E\), which is (3.4). We chose the factor \(\tfrac12\) in front of \(x^2\) precisely so that it cancels the \(2\) from the derivative \(2x\).
  5. At \(x = 0\) both terms are zero, so \(u(0) = 0\), as step 3 requires. We take out \(\rho g/(2E)\) and get
    \[u(x) = \frac{\rho g}{2E}\,(2Lx - x^2) . \tag{3.5}\]
  6. There is no other such function. Let \(v\) be any function that is continuous on \([0, L]\), has the derivative (3.4) on \((0, L)\) and satisfies \(v(0) = 0\). The function \(u\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous, and \(v\) is continuous by assumption. The difference \(w = u - v\) is therefore continuous on \([0, L]\) by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, and by the lemma on the rules of differentiation it has the derivative \(u' - v' = 0\) on \((0, L)\).
  7. Take any \(x\) in \((0, L]\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.84), applied to \(w\) on \([0, x]\), there is a \(\xi\) between \(0\) and \(x\) with \(w(x) - w(0) = w'(\xi)\,(x - 0)\). Since \(w'(\xi) = 0\), we get \(w(x) = w(0) = u(0) - v(0) = 0\), so \(v(x) = u(x)\). Steps 6 and 7 repeat the proof of corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative for the function \(w\).

Let us compare with section 1.2, where we took \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The two formulas agree if

\[a = \frac{\rho g}{2E} = \frac{7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2}{2 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} ,\]

which is exactly the number from chapter 1 (BARBER (1.33), (1.34), (1.38), (1.39), PDF pp. 32-34). The unit is right. The numerator is in \(\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^2)\), the denominator in \(\text{Pa} = \text{kg}/(\text{m}\,\text{s}^2)\), and the quotient is \(1/\text{m}\). The constant \(a\), of which we said there that it depends only on how heavy steel is and how strongly it resists stretching, is therefore the weight per unit volume of steel, \(\rho g\), divided by twice Young's modulus. The two in the denominator comes from step 4: the derivative of the function \(2Lx - x^2\) is \(2\,(L - x)\), while the derivative of the displacement must be \(\rho g\,(L - x)/E\).

The result holds under the assumptions of the model and for the idealized holding of the bar. Near a real ceiling to which the bar is welded the field is somewhat different (section 1.7.1). The displacement is proportional to \(\rho g/E\). A bar of a material twice as stiff would get longer by half as much, a bar of a material twice as heavy by twice as much. The thickness of the bar does not appear in the displacement, just as it did not appear in the stress (2.5). Figure 3.9 shows the stress, the strain and the displacement along the bar. The lower end moves by \(u(L) = \rho g L^2/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, as in section 1.4. At the depth \(L/2 = 5\) m the displacement is

\[u(L/2) = a\,\Big(2L \cdot \frac{L}{2} - \frac{L^2}{4}\Big) = \frac34\,a L^2 = 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m} ,\]

three quarters of the displacement of the lower end. We got the same number in section 1.7.1 for the downward displacement of the point \(P\) at this depth. Why three quarters? The upper half gets longer by \(u(L/2) - u(0) = \tfrac34\,a L^2\), the lower half by \(u(L) - u(L/2) = \tfrac14\,a L^2\), three times less. The upper half is stretched more. Its average strain is \(\tfrac34\,a L^2/(L/2) = 1{,}5\,aL\), that of the lower half \(\tfrac14\,a L^2/(L/2) = 0{,}5\,aL\). These are the strains (3.4) at the middles of the two halves, at the depths \(L/4\) and \(3L/4\), since \(2a\,(L - L/4) = 1{,}5\,aL\) and \(2a\,(L - 3L/4) = 0{,}5\,aL\).

One more claim from chapter 1 is waiting to be confirmed. In section 1.2 we wrote that the hanging bar gets longer by the same amount as an equal bar whose weight is negligible and which is pulled at its lower end by a force equal to half the weight of the hanging bar (figure 1.12). The weight of the hanging bar is \(\rho g A L\). A bar pulled by the axial force \(N = \rho g A L/2\) gets longer by (3.2) by

\[\Delta L = \frac{N L}{E A} = \frac{\rho g A L \cdot L}{2 E A} = \frac{\rho g L^2}{2E} ,\]

which is exactly \(u(L)\), the elongation of the hanging bar.

In the displacement field (1.18), too, we now recognize both numbers of the material. Where \(a\) stood, there stands \(\rho g/(2E)\), where \(0{,}3\) stood, there stands \(\nu\), and \(0{,}6\,a = 2\nu\,\rho g/(2E) = \nu\,\rho g/E\):

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= \frac{\rho g}{2E}\,(2Lx - x^2) - \frac{\nu\,\rho g}{2E}\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -\frac{\nu\,\rho g}{E}\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -\frac{\nu\,\rho g}{E}\,(L - x)\,z . \end{aligned}\]

This is how CM (7.3.20) (PDF p. 306) and SADD (5.7.4) (PDF p. 115) write the field, in axes placed differently. The component \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \(-\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(x)\,y\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}(x)\) is the strain (3.4). So a point at the distance \(y\) from the axis moves towards the axis by \(\nu\) times the strain along the bar, multiplied by \(y\), and the lateral strain is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), exactly as in a specimen by definition 3.2. Whether the field agrees with the relation between stress and strain in all other components too, we will check in section 3.2, when we write this relation in space.

The tension test gave us two numbers, \(E\) and \(\nu\). The first tells by how much a bar gets longer at a given stress, the second by how much it gets thinner at the same time. With them we computed in two steps the displacement of the hanging bar, which in chapter 1 was left without a derivation. The stress divided by \(E\) gave the strain at each point, and the displacement was the only function that has this strain as its derivative and is zero at the top. In a body, however, stresses act on the material in several directions at once, shear stresses too. How the material deforms then is the question of the next section.
