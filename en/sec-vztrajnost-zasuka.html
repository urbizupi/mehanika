Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.10 Rotary inertia and shear in beam vibration

In chapter 12 we wrote the kinetic energy of a beam as \(\tfrac12\int\rho A\,\dot v^2\,\mathrm{d}x\): every cross section moves up and down, nothing else. But in bending a cross section also turns, and a point at height \(y\) above the axis therefore also moves horizontally. The turning has a kinetic energy that section 13.5 computed (lemma 13.36) and then neglected (section 12.9.2 called it the neglected inertia of turning, and chapter 5 promised this continuation for vibration). We ask how much this energy changes the frequencies and when it matters. The same question is put for the shear strain of the Timoshenko beam (definition 5.87), which also lowers the frequencies. Why both corrections lower the frequency is told by a weight on a spring, whose circular frequency is \(\sqrt{k/m}\) (definition 9.4): the turning of the sections acts as added mass, shear as a second spring in series that makes the whole softer, and both make \(k/m\) smaller. We derive this view in section 13.10.4.

Figure 13.138 shows a piece of the beam from the side, with greatly exaggerated angles. On the left is the axis of the beam: the grey dashed line is its position before bending, the blue line \(w\) is the axis after bending. The orange line is the cross section, which stays perpendicular to the axis, as the right-angle mark shows. Before bending the cross section was vertical, like the grey dashed vertical line. It therefore turns by the angle \(\theta\), which equals the slope of the axis \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\): axis and cross section turn together, like the two legs of a right angle. On the right the axis is tilted more than the cross section, and the grey vertical dashed line is the position of the cross section before bending. The cross section has turned only by the angle \(\theta\), the axis by the angle \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\). The difference \(\gamma = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta\) is the shear strain: the cross section is no longer perpendicular to the axis.

From history

In the early version of the Euler-Bernoulli model only the kinetic energy of the small pieces of the beam moving up and down was counted for vibration. The cross sections do turn in this model, together with the axis, but the kinetic energy of this turning was neglected (GR, PDF p. 281). For lateral vibration of prismatic bars, Bresse was the first to take into account the rotary inertia of the elements of the bar (TIMO, PDF p. 159). The reason is that in bending an element turns, and the turning has a kinetic energy that motion up and down alone does not count. In his treatise on sound Rayleigh corrected the simple theory of lateral vibration of rods to allow for rotary inertia (TIMO, PDF p. 278, footnote). The treatise appeared in 1877 (GR, PDF p. 19). The model with rotary inertia is therefore named after Rayleigh (GR, PDF p. 281). The Euler-Bernoulli model, even with it, shows a limitation, because it has no shear deformation linked to the shear force (GR, PDF p. 295). A more accurate model that takes this deformation into account was introduced by Timoshenko in 1921 and 1922 (GR, PDF p. 295).

The story showed two corrections of the Euler-Bernoulli model, the rotary inertia of Bresse and Rayleigh and the shear of Timoshenko, and that at each we must ask when it matters. This section answers that question for the beam of the introduction.

The answer for a simply supported steel beam of section \(20 \times 40\) mm (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, chapter 5) is short. At length \(L = 1\) m the first natural frequency of the Euler-Bernoulli beam is \(90{,}92\) Hz, the turning of the sections lowers it by \(0{,}066\) %, and the turning together with shear by \(0{,}27\) %. At length \(L = 80\) mm, that is at \(L/h = 2\), the lowering is \(8{,}9\) % and \(24{,}9\) %. The section shows what it depends on and proves it with exact frequencies that we compute with sine modes.

The model has named idealizations. For each we say why it holds here, when it does not hold and what it does to the result.

  1. Displacements and slopes are small (the angles in the figures are greatly exaggerated). So we write the angle instead of its sine and \(1\) instead of the cosine, and the equations are linear. A beam with \(L = 1\) m vibrating in the first mode with deflection \(W = 1\) mm has the largest slope \(\pi W/L = 0{,}0031\) rad, and at this angle the sine differs from the angle by only \(1{,}6 \cdot 10^{-6}\) of its value. It does not hold for deflections comparable with the height of the section \(h\). Then the axis of a beam with two fixed supports lengthens, because the arc is longer than the chord, and the stretch of the axis compared with the bending strain at the edge of the section is \(W/(2h)\): at \(W = 1\) mm and \(h = 40\) mm only \(1/80\), at \(W = h\) already one half.
  2. The axis does not move along itself (\(u = 0\)), every cross section only rises, sinks and turns, there is no axial force. This holds in free vibration without axial load, when one support can move along the axis or the deflection is small as in item 1. It does not hold for a compressed or stretched beam. Then the equation lacks a term with the axial force, like the term with the tension \(T\) of the string (section 13.1).
  3. The cross section stays plane and turns as a whole, as in the Timoshenko model. In a slender beam shear is only a small correction (at \(L = 1\) m and \(n = 1\) about \(0{,}2\) % of the frequency, table in example 13.90), so the error from its unevenness over the section is small too. It holds poorly for a short beam, \(L/h = 2\), where the uneven shear is replaced only by an average with the factor \(k\) from statics (warning in section 13.10.5).
  4. The properties \(E\), \(G\), \(\rho\), \(A\), \(I\) and \(k\) are constant along the beam: the beam is of one steel and has the same section everywhere. It does not hold for a stepped shaft or a beam with holes. Then the coefficients of the equations depend on \(x\), sines are no longer modes and the exact frequencies of section 13.10.4 do not exist, the finite element method of section 13.10.6 remains.
  5. A simple support means that the transverse displacement at the end is zero and that the end can turn freely (the moment is zero). In a real support the stiffness of the bearing restrains the rotation a little. It acts as a spring that adds strain energy for the same motion, so we expect somewhat higher frequencies, but we do not prove this here.
  6. The vibration is free, without load and without damping. Friction in the bearing and in the material dissipates energy and damps the vibration. If every mode were damped like a system with one degree of freedom with damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) (equation (9.11)), its frequency would drop by the factor \(\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) (theorem 9.48), that is by \(0{,}005\) %, thirteen times less than the smallest correction in this section, \(0{,}066\) %.

13.10.1 Kinetic energy of turning cross sections

Let the displacement field of the point \((x, y)\) of a cross section be as in the Timoshenko beam (equation (5.81)), but depending on time: \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -y\,\theta(x, t)\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = w(x, t)\). The function \(w\) is the transverse displacement of the axis (\(v\) in chapter 5, now also depending on time), \(\theta\) is the tilt of the cross section, in radians. Figure 13.140 shows what follows. On the left is the cross section, drawn as a vertical row of dots, and the axis of the beam, a horizontal line. In a translation every point of the cross section moves with the same vertical velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), because the section does not turn. On the right are the axis and the same cross section, now turned. The point at height \(y\) above the axis has in addition the horizontal velocity \(-y\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\), which is proportional to the height \(y\) because the cross section turns as a whole. Above the axis it points left, below it right.

Lemma 13.84 Kinetic energy of a beam with turning sections (kinetična energija nosilca z vrtečimi se prerezi)

A beam of length \(L\) has constant density \(\rho\), cross-section area \(A\) and second moment of area \(I\) (definition 5.26). The functions \(w\) and \(\theta\) have continuous partial derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -y\,\theta(x, t)\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = w(x, t)\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The kinetic energy of the beam at time \(t\) (definition 8.88, the letter \(T\) here is not the tension of section 13.1) is

\[T = \int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + \tfrac12\,\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{13.89}\]

The second term is called rotary inertia (vztrajnost zasuka), and \(\rho I\) is its "mass" for the tilt rate \(\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\), in kg m.

Proof

We must show that the steps of the proof of lemma 13.36 also hold when \(\theta\) is not equal to \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\).

  1. The proof of lemma 13.36 never used the relation \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\), only the form of the displacement field. The velocity of a point is again \((-y\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}},\ w_{\mathrm{t}},\ 0)\), its square is \(y^2\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\) with no product term, and by theorem 4.9 and with \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I\) and \(\int\mathrm{d}A = A\) we get \(T = \tfrac12\int_0^L \rho\,(I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2)\,\mathrm{d}x\), which is (13.89). The only difference is that \(\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\) stands in place of \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. CK (12.50) (PDF p. 401): the kinetic energy of the beam has a translation term and a rotary-inertia term ("rotatory inertia term"). GR (4.280) (PDF p. 296) the same with mass per unit length and radius of gyration.

In the Euler-Bernoulli beam the cross section stays perpendicular to the axis, so \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the turning of the cross section is the turning of the axis. Then \(\theta_{\mathrm{t}} = w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and the second term is \(\tfrac12\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\). The ratio of the two terms is computed in section 13.10.4.

13.10.2 The Rayleigh beam

The simplest correction of the Euler-Bernoulli model is to keep \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and add only the rotary inertia. The deformation energy stays as in theorem 13.37.

Definition 13.85 Rayleigh beam model (Rayleighov model nosilca)

A beam of length \(L\) with constant \(E\), \(\rho\), \(A\) and \(I\) has the transverse displacement of the axis \(w(x, t)\) on \(\Omega = [0, L] \times [t_1, t_2]\). The Rayleigh model is the Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14), \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the kinetic energy of lemma 13.84 and the deformation energy \(\tfrac12\int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The action of the model is the number

\[\mathcal S(w) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + \tfrac12\,\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{13.90}\]

Source: FPGL (PDF p. 181): adding rotary inertia gives a "Rayleigh beam model", adding shear as well a "Timoshenko beam model". GR (PDF p. 416) names it the same way.

By the principle that theorem 13.37 writes for the beam (and theorem 8.118 for the bar), the motion is a stationary point of the action. For the model we take this principle as given, as for the Euler-Bernoulli beam. There it gave the same equation as Newton's two laws for a piece of the beam (lemma 13.38), and for the Rayleigh beam we check this below, with the same piece. The admissible functions are \(w\) on \(\Omega\) with all continuous partial derivatives up to the fourth order, satisfying \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) for every \(t\) (simple support) and with prescribed displacements \(w(x, t_1)\) and \(w(x, t_2)\). An admissible variation is a function \(\eta\) on \(\Omega\) with continuous \(\eta\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\) that is zero at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t = t_1\) and \(t = t_2\). The first variation \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \eta)\) is the derivative of \(\mathcal S(w + \alpha\,\eta)\) with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\), as in theorem 8.118. The last condition allows \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} \neq 0\) at the ends, because a simple support does not prescribe the slope.

Figure 13.141 shows where the variations are zero. On the left is the rectangle \(\Omega\) with position \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and time \(t\) from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\). The thick boundary is the four edges where \(\eta = 0\). Inside we can take the blue bump, which is nonzero only in the rectangle between \(\alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1\) and between \(\alpha_2\) and \(\beta_2\). At the top right, at a fixed time, is the \(x\) axis and on it the function \(\eta = \chi\,x^2(x - L)/L^2\), drawn for \(\chi = 1\). It is zero at both ends, but at \(x = L\) the orange tangent has the slope \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = \chi\), which is not zero: a zero value does not mean a zero slope. At the bottom right are the \(x\) axis and on it the function \(\zeta = \chi\,x/L\), which is zero at \(x = 0\) and has the value \(\chi\) at \(x = L\). We use both examples at the end of the proofs.

Figure 13.142 shows what the first variation is. For a chosen path \(w\) and variation \(\eta\), each number \(\alpha\) is one neighbouring path \(w + \alpha\,\eta\), and its action is one number. On the left are the axes with the number \(\alpha\) horizontal and the action \(\mathcal S(w + \alpha\,\eta)\) vertical. The blue curve is this action. It is the parabola \(\mathcal S(w) + \alpha\,B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2\), because each term under the integral is a product of two derivatives that are both linear in \(\alpha\), for example \((w_{\mathrm{t}} + \alpha\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}})^2\). The blackwhite dot at \(\alpha = 0\) is the action of the path itself, \(\mathcal S(w)\). The dashed line through it is the line \(\mathcal S(w) + \alpha\,B_1\), which at step \(1\) in the direction of \(\alpha\) rises by \(B_1\). The orange segment between it and the parabola is the remainder \(\alpha^2 B_2\). The remainder has the slope \(2\alpha\,B_2 = 0\) at \(\alpha = 0\), so the parabola touches the line there and has the same slope \(B_1\). This slope is the first variation \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \eta)\). On the right is the same picture for a stationary path, on axes with the number \(\alpha\) and the action. The parabola has at the point \(\mathcal S(w)\) a horizontal tangent, \(B_1 = 0\), and this holds for every admissible variation \(\eta\).

Theorem 13.86 Equation of motion of the Rayleigh beam (gibalna enačba Rayleighovega nosilca)

Let \(w\) be an admissible function. Then \(w\) is a stationary point of the action (13.90) if and only if

\[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = 0 \quad \text{on } \Omega , \tag{13.91}\]

and at both ends for every \(t\)

\[w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0 , \qquad w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, t) = 0 . \tag{13.92}\]

Condition (13.92) says that the bending moment \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is zero at the ends.

Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral over \(\Omega\) and terms at the ends. Then smooth bumps isolate the equation, and a variation with a slope at the end isolates the conditions.

  1. At \(w + \alpha\,\eta\) the integrand is a polynomial of degree two in \(\alpha\) with continuous coefficients, so \(\mathcal S(w + \alpha\,\eta) = \mathcal S(w) + \alpha\,B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2\) and \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \eta) = B_1\), where
    \[B_1 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \big(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}} + \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  2. First term: integration by parts in \(t\) (corollary 3.56) and \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\) give \(\int\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. Second term: because \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\) for every \(x\), also \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) there. Integration by parts in \(t\) gives \(\int\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xtt}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}t\). Then in \(x\) (corollary 3.56): \(-\int_0^L \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xtt}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\big[\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xtt}}\,\eta\big]_0^L + \int_0^L \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the bracket is zero because \(\eta(0, t) = \eta(L, t) = 0\).
  4. Third term: integration by parts in \(x\) twice: \(-\int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\big[EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\big]_0^L + \int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\,\eta\big]_0^L - \int_0^L EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\), where the second bracket is zero for the same reason as in step 3.
  5. The order of integration in \(x\) and \(t\) may be exchanged (theorem 4.9). Inserting steps 2 to 4 into \(B_1\):
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\mathcal S(w, \eta) &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \Big(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, t)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t)\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned}\]
    where \(R = -\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) is a continuous function on \(\Omega\).
  6. If (13.91) and (13.92) hold, then \(R = 0\) and both terms at the ends are zero, so \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) for every admissible variation.
  7. Conversely, let \(w\) be stationary. For \(t_1 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < t_2\) and \(0 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < L\) let \(\eta = q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\) (lemma 4.123, item 1). It has all the needed continuous derivatives and is zero together with \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t_1\), \(t_2\), so it is admissible and the terms at the ends vanish. By step 5, \(\int\!\!\int R\,\eta = 0\). The function \(g(x, t, z) = R(x, t)\) is continuous on the open set \(D = (0, L) \times (t_1, t_2) \times (0, 1)\), and for every box \(K = [\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\) in \(D\), with the function \(\varphi(x, t, z) = q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\,q_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z) = \eta(x, t)\,q_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\) of item 2 of lemma 4.123, we have \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} g\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}V = \int\!\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \cdot \int q_{\alpha_3\beta_3}\,\mathrm{d}z = 0\) (theorem 4.9). By item 3 of lemma 4.123, \(R = 0\) on \(D\), so in the interior of \(\Omega\) and by continuity on \(\Omega\). This is (13.91).
  8. Let \(\chi\) be a function with continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\), \(\chi(t_1) = \chi(t_2) = 0\), and \(\eta(x, t) = \chi(t)\,x^2\,(x - L)/L^2\). It has the required continuous derivatives, is zero at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t_1\), \(t_2\), so it is admissible, and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = \chi(t)\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) = 0\). By step 5 and \(R = 0\), \(-\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, t)\,\chi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), and since \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, \cdot)\) is continuous, by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, t) = 0\). In the same way, with \(\eta = \chi(t)\,x\,(x - L)^2/L^2\), which has \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) = \chi(t)\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\), we get \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(0, t) = 0\). This is (13.92).

Source: The proof is not from the source. CK (12.57) (PDF p. 403) separates in the equation of the Timoshenko beam the terms of Euler-Bernoulli theory from the corrections, among them the "principal rotatory inertia term", and this is our term \(-\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}}\).

What does equation (13.91) say? Without the term \(-\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}}\) it is the equation of the Euler-Bernoulli beam \(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = 0\) (equation (13.42)). The new term is the rotary inertia. We check this independently, with Newton's two laws for a piece of the beam as in lemma 13.38, except that this time we do not neglect the turning of the cross sections.

Figure 13.143 shows a piece of the beam from the side. It lies between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\), its length is \(b - a\). On the left face the left neighbour pulls it down with the shear force \(V(a)\), on the right face the right neighbour pulls it up with the force \(V(b)\). On the left face the moment \(M(a)\) acts clockwise, on the right face the moment \(M(b)\) counterclockwise. They point this way because these are the directions in which we count \(V\) and \(M\) as positive (lemma 13.38). Orange is what the forces and moments change: the straight arrow upward is the rate of change of the momentum of the piece, the curved arrow the rate of change of the angular momentum that the cross sections have because they turn.

The sum of the forces on the piece is the rate of change of its momentum (as in equation (13.45), without load):

\[V(b) - V(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

We take the angular momentum of the piece about the point of the axis at \(x = a\). The point \((x, y)\) of a cross section has the velocity \((-y\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}},\ w_{\mathrm{t}})\) (figure 13.140), the lever arm \((x - a,\ y)\) and therefore the angular momentum \(\rho\,\big((x - a)\,w_{\mathrm{t}} + y^2\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\big)\) per unit volume. We measure the arm in the position before the vibration. In the position during the vibration it would change by the horizontal displacement \(-y\,\theta\) and the vertical displacement \(w\), which adds \(\rho\,y\,(w\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}} - \theta\,w_{\mathrm{t}})\) to the angular momentum. This addition is proportional to \(y\), so its integral over the cross section is zero, since the axis passes through the centroid and \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) (lemma 5.22). In the moments of the forces the shifted points of application would add only products of two small quantities (a displacement times a force), which a linear equation drops as in idealization 1. The integral over the cross section gives \(\rho A\,(x - a)\,w_{\mathrm{t}} + \rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\), since \(\int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I\). The sum of the moments on the piece is the rate of change of the angular momentum:

\[M(b) - M(a) + (b - a)\,V(b) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (x - a)\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

This is equation (13.46) with the last term added. We divide it by \(b - a\) and let \(b \to a\) as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 13.38. Let \(C\) be the largest value of \(|\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}|\) on \(\Omega\). The first integral on the right is at most \(C\,(b - a)^2/2\) in absolute value, because \(0 \le x - a \le b - a\), so after the division it tends to zero. The second integral divided by \(b - a\) tends to \(\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}(a, t)\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.55). What remains is

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} + V = \rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}} .\]

In statics the right side is zero and the equation is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = -V\) (corollary 5.11). With \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) we have \(V = \rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xtt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\), a continuously differentiable function, and the first equation gives in the same way \(V_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\). Hence \(\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} - EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), which is equation (13.91). Newton's two laws thus give the same equation as the action, and this is an independent check.

Figure 13.144 shows both limits. On the left are the axes with the position \(x\) from \(a\) on and the points \(b'\) and \(b\), where \(b'\) is halfway between \(a\) and \(b\). The blue curve is the function under the first integral, \((x - a)\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\). Since \(|\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}| \le C\), it lies below the dashed line \(C\,(x - a)\). The integral up to \(b\) is therefore at most the area of the grey triangle with the sides \(b - a\) and \(C\,(b - a)\), that is \(C\,(b - a)^2/2\). Divided by \(b - a\) it gives \(C\,(b - a)/2\), half the height of the triangle. Up to \(b'\) the orange triangle is half as tall, so the divided bound is also half as large. The height of the triangle is proportional to \(b - a\), so the divided bound tends to zero as \(b \to a\). On the right are the axes with the same points \(a\), \(b'\) and \(b\). The blue curve is \(\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}\). The integral divided by \(b - a\) is its mean height over \([a, b]\), that is the height of the grey rectangle with the same area as under the curve. Over the shorter \([a, b']\) the orange rectangle is lower and closer to the value at the point \(a\), because a continuous curve departs less from it on a shorter interval. As \(b \to a\) the mean tends to \(\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}(a, t)\).

13.10.3 The Timoshenko beam in vibration

The Rayleigh model still assumes that the cross section stays perpendicular to the axis. The Timoshenko model (definition 5.87) drops this: the cross section has its own tilt \(\theta\), and the shear strain \(\gamma = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta\) gives, through the shear correction factor \(k\) (definition 5.89), the shear force \(V = k\,G\,A\,\gamma\) and with it energy. The dynamics thus has two unknown functions, \(w\) and \(\theta\), and an action with the kinetic energy of lemma 13.84 and the deformation energy of equations (5.84):

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal S(w, \theta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \Big(&\tfrac12\,\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + \tfrac12\,\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 \\ &- \tfrac12\,EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - \tfrac12\,k\,G\,A\,(w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta)^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t . \end{aligned} \tag{13.93}\]

The first two terms are the kinetic energy by lemma 13.84, the other two the deformation energy of bending, \(\tfrac12 M\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac12 EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}^2\), and the shear energy \(\tfrac12 V\gamma = \tfrac12 kGA\,\gamma^2\) (as in section 5.13.2). Here too we take the principle as given. The check with statics after the theorem shows that it gives the known equations of the Timoshenko beam, and GR also obtains the same equations from the equilibrium of an element (source of the theorem).

An admissible pair is \((w, \theta)\) with continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \(\Omega\), \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) and with prescribed \(w\) and \(\theta\) at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). An admissible variation is a pair \((\eta, \zeta)\) with continuous first partial derivatives on \(\Omega\), \(\eta = 0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) and \(\eta = \zeta = 0\) at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). The rotation \(\theta\) is not prescribed at the ends, so \(\zeta\) need not be zero there.

Theorem 13.87 Equations of motion of the Timoshenko beam (enačbe Timošenkovega nosilca pri nihanju)

Let \((w, \theta)\) be an admissible pair and \(\gamma = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta\). Then \((w, \theta)\) is a stationary point of the action (13.93) if and only if

\[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \big(k\,G\,A\,\gamma\big)_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad \rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}} = \big(E I\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\big)_{\mathrm{x}} + k\,G\,A\,\gamma \quad \text{on } \Omega , \tag{13.94}\]

and at both ends for every \(t\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) = 0\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\) (the moment \(M = EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\) is zero).

Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral over \(\Omega\) and terms at the ends. The argument is the same as for theorem 13.86, only with two functions.

  1. As in step 1 of theorem 13.86, \(\delta\mathcal S(w, \theta; \eta, \zeta) = \int\!\!\int \big(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}} + \rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{t}} - EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{x}} - kGA\,\gamma\,(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} - \zeta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\), because at \((w + \alpha\eta, \theta + \alpha\zeta)\) the shear strain \(\gamma\) becomes \(\gamma + \alpha\,(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} - \zeta)\).
  2. By parts in \(t\) (corollary 3.56), with \(\eta = \zeta = 0\) at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\): \(\int\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\) and \(\int\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. By parts in \(x\) (corollary 3.56): \(-\int_0^L kGA\,\gamma\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\big[kGA\,\gamma\,\eta\big]_0^L + \int_0^L (kGA\,\gamma)_{\mathrm{x}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\), where the bracket is zero (\(\eta = 0\) at the ends), and \(-\int_0^L EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\big[EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\zeta\big]_0^L + \int_0^L (EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x\). The derivatives \((kGA\,\gamma)_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \((EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\) are continuous because the derivatives of \(w\) and \(\theta\) up to second order are continuous.
  4. Inserting (order of integration by theorem 4.9):
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\mathcal S &= \int\!\!\int \big(R_1\,\eta + R_2\,\zeta\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \Big(EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,\zeta(L, t) - EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)\,\zeta(0, t)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned}\]
    \(R_1 = -\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + (kGA\,\gamma)_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(R_2 = -\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}} + (EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}} + kGA\,\gamma\) are continuous.
  5. If (13.94) and the conditions at the ends hold, then \(R_1 = R_2 = 0\) and the terms at the ends are zero, so \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) for every admissible pair.
  6. Conversely, let \((w, \theta)\) be stationary. With \(\zeta = 0\) and \(\eta = q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\) (lemma 4.123), step 4 gives \(\int\!\!\int R_1\,\eta = 0\). As in step 7 of theorem 13.86 (the function \(g(x, t, z) = R_1(x, t)\) and item 3 of lemma 4.123), \(R_1 = 0\). With \(\eta = 0\) and \(\zeta = q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\) in the same way \(R_2 = 0\). This is (13.94).
  7. Let \(\chi\) be continuously differentiable on \([t_1, t_2]\) with \(\chi(t_1) = \chi(t_2) = 0\), \(\eta = 0\) and \(\zeta = \chi(t)\,x/L\). The pair is admissible, \(\zeta(L, t) = \chi\), \(\zeta(0, t) = 0\). By step 4, \(EI\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,\chi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), and by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\). With \(\zeta = \chi(t)\,(L - x)/L\) we get \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t) = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. CK (12.52) to (12.55) (PDF p. 402): the same derivation with the extended Hamilton's principle, four boundary conditions, each of them geometric or natural. GR (4.283) to (4.286) (PDF pp. 297 to 298) and (4.287) to (4.292) (PDF pp. 298 to 299), where GR obtains the same equations also from the balance of an element. The signs are recomputed into ours.

A check against statics. For \(w\) and \(\theta\) constant in time the left sides of equations (13.94) are zero, and what remains is \((kGA\,\gamma)_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \((EI\,\theta_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}} + kGA\,\gamma = 0\), which are equations (5.85) of theorem 5.91 without load. The dynamic part is inertia: \(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is the inertia of translation (\(V_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) from the force balance of a piece of the beam) and \(\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}\) the rotary inertia (the moment balance of a piece, as for the Rayleigh beam). Without the term \(\rho I\,\theta_{\mathrm{tt}}\) the second equation gives \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + V = 0\) as in statics.

13.10.4 Exact frequencies of a simply supported beam

We compute the frequencies of all three models for a simply supported beam (separation of variables and sine modes as in section 13.6). We still need a length that measures the thickness of the cross section against the length of the vibration.

Definition 13.88 Radius of gyration of the section (polmer vrtenja prereza)

A cross section of area \(A\) and second moment of area \(I\) (definition 5.26) has the radius of gyration

\[r = \sqrt{\frac{I}{A}} , \tag{13.95}\]

a number with the unit of length, depending only on the shape of the section (a rectangle of height \(h\) in the plane of bending: \(r = h/\sqrt{12}\)).

For our section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(r = 40/\sqrt{12}\) mm \(= 11{,}547\) mm. This is not the radius of any point of the section, but the length at which a point mass \(\rho A\) on the arm \(r\) would have the inertia \(\rho I\).

Figure 13.145 shows this section from the side. The rectangle is the section, the grey dashed line its axis, the dimension \(h\) the height \(40\) mm. The blue point lies above the axis at the distance \(r\), where the point mass of the previous paragraph would be. Why is \(r = 11{,}547\) mm smaller than half the height, \(h/2 = 20\) mm? The number \(r^2 = I/A = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\,/\int\mathrm{d}A\) is the average of the squared height \(y^2\) over the section. Points at the edge contribute \((h/2)^2\), points near the axis almost nothing, so the average is smaller, for a rectangle exactly one third: \(r^2 = h^2/12 = (h/2)^2/3\).

Theorem 13.89 Frequencies of a simply supported beam with rotary inertia and shear (frekvence prosto podprtega nosilca z vztrajnostjo zasuka in strigom)

A simply supported beam of length \(L\) has constant \(E\), \(G\), \(\rho\), \(A\), \(I\) and \(k\). For a natural number \(n\) let \(\beta = n\pi/L\), \(\varepsilon = r^2\beta^2 = (n\pi r/L)^2\), \(\kappa = E/(kG)\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = \beta^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\).

  1. (Euler-Bernoulli) The function \(w = \sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\) solves equation (13.42) without load and the conditions \(w = w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at the ends if and only if \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\).
  2. (Rayleigh) The same \(w\) solves equation (13.91) and conditions (13.92) if and only if \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{R}} = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}\).
  3. (Timoshenko) The pair \(w = W\sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\), \(\theta = \Theta\cos(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\) with \((W, \Theta) \neq (0, 0)\) solves equations (13.94) and the conditions at the ends if and only if \(\lambda = \omega^2\) is a zero of the polynomial
    \[p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \Big(\frac{E\beta^2}{\rho} + \frac{kG\beta^2}{\rho} + \frac{kG}{\rho\,r^2}\Big)\lambda + \frac{kGE\beta^4}{\rho^2} . \tag{13.96}\]
    The polynomial has two positive zeros \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2\), and for \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\)
    \[\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}}{\sqrt{1 + (1 + \kappa)\,\varepsilon}} < \omega_{\mathrm{T}} < \omega_{\mathrm{R}} < \omega_{\mathrm{EB}} . \tag{13.97}\]
Proof

We must show that substitution gives the conditions for \(\omega\), and that the polynomial \(p\) has a zero in the open interval between \(0\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}^2\), and the lower bound.

  1. For all three functions \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) because \(\sin(n\pi) = 0\). For \(w = \sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\beta^2 w\), so \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) at the ends, and \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}} = -\beta\,\Theta\sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t) = 0\) there.
  2. Euler-Bernoulli: \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \beta^4 w\). The equation gives \((-\rho A\,\omega^2 + EI\,\beta^4)\,w = 0\), so \(\omega^2 = EI\beta^4/(\rho A) = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2\).
  3. Rayleigh: in addition \(w_{\mathrm{xxtt}} = (-\beta^2)(-\omega^2)\,w = \beta^2\omega^2 w\). Equation (13.91) gives \(\big(-\rho A\,\omega^2 - \rho I\,\beta^2\omega^2 + EI\,\beta^4\big)\,w = 0\), so \(\omega^2 = EI\beta^4/\big(\rho A\,(1 + \beta^2 I/A)\big) = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(1 + \varepsilon)\) by the definition \(r^2 = I/A\).
  4. Timoshenko: from \(\gamma = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta = (\beta W - \Theta)\cos(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\) follows \((kGA\,\gamma)_{\mathrm{x}} = -kGA\,\beta\,(\beta W - \Theta)\sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\). The first equation (13.94) gives \(\rho A\,\omega^2 W = kGA\,\beta\,(\beta W - \Theta)\), the second (\(\theta_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2\theta\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\beta^2\theta\)) gives \(\rho I\,\omega^2\Theta = EI\,\beta^2\Theta - kGA\,(\beta W - \Theta)\). Rearranged:
    \[\begin{aligned} (\rho A\,\omega^2 - kGA\,\beta^2)\,W + kGA\,\beta\,\Theta &= 0 , \\ kGA\,\beta\,W + (\rho I\,\omega^2 - EI\,\beta^2 - kGA)\,\Theta &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]
    The coefficient \(kGA\,\beta\) is not zero. From the first equation \(\Theta = -(\rho A\,\omega^2 - kGA\,\beta^2)\,W/(kGA\,\beta)\), and for \(W = 0\) also \(\Theta = 0\). A solution \((W, \Theta) \neq (0, 0)\) therefore has \(W \neq 0\), and substitution into the second equation shows that the determinant of the system, the product of the diagonal coefficients minus the square of the off-diagonal one, must be zero. Conversely, for a zero determinant take \(W = 1\) and \(\Theta\) from the first equation, and the second is satisfied.
  5. The determinant is \((\rho A\lambda - kGA\beta^2)(\rho I\lambda - EI\beta^2 - kGA) - (kGA\beta)^2\). Multiplied out it is \(\rho^2AI\,\lambda^2 - \rho\lambda\,(AEI\beta^2 + kGA^2 + kGAI\beta^2) + kGAEI\beta^4\), because \(k^2G^2A^2\beta^2\) cancels. Division by \(\rho^2 A I\) and \(I/A = r^2\) give \(p(\lambda)\) of (13.96).
  6. Write \(a = E\beta^2/\rho\), \(s = kG\beta^2/\rho\). Then \(kG/(\rho r^2) = s/(\beta^2 r^2) = s/\varepsilon\), \(p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - B\lambda + C\) with \(B = a + s\,(1 + \varepsilon)/\varepsilon\) and \(C = a s\), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 = (E/\rho)\,r^2\beta^4 = a\varepsilon\), so \(\lambda_0 = \omega_{\mathrm{R}}^2 = a\varepsilon/(1 + \varepsilon)\).
  7. Computing \(p(\lambda_0)\): \(B\lambda_0 = a\lambda_0 + s\,(1 + \varepsilon)/\varepsilon\cdot a\varepsilon/(1 + \varepsilon) = a\lambda_0 + a s\), so \(p(\lambda_0) = \lambda_0^2 - a\lambda_0 = \lambda_0\,(\lambda_0 - a) = -\lambda_0\,a/(1 + \varepsilon) < 0\), because \(\lambda_0 - a = a\varepsilon/(1 + \varepsilon) - a = -a/(1 + \varepsilon)\).
  8. The polynomial \(p\) has a positive leading coefficient and \(p(0) = C > 0\), and \(p(\lambda_0) < 0\). By the intermediate value theorem (theorem 2.79) it has a zero in \((0, \lambda_0)\) and in \((\lambda_0, \infty)\). These are all the zeros of a quadratic polynomial, \(0 < \lambda_1 < \lambda_0 < \lambda_2\). Hence \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} < \omega_{\mathrm{R}}\), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}} < \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\) by step 3 because \(\varepsilon > 0\).
  9. Vieta's relations: \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = C\) and \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = B\). Because \(\lambda_1 > 0\), \(\lambda_2 < B\), so \(\lambda_1 = C/\lambda_2 > C/B\) \(= as/\big(a + s(1 + \varepsilon)/\varepsilon\big) = a\varepsilon/\big(1 + \varepsilon + \varepsilon\,a/s\big)\) and \(a/s = E/(kG) = \kappa\). Hence \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2 > \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(1 + (1 + \kappa)\varepsilon)\), which is the left inequality of (13.97).

What does "exactly" mean in theorem 13.89? We insert the Euler-Bernoulli frequency into the Rayleigh equation (13.91), \(w = \sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} t)\). As in step 3 of the proof, the left side is \(\big(-\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 - \rho I\,\beta^2\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 + EI\,\beta^4\big)\,w = -\rho I\,\beta^2\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2\,w\), because \(\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2 = EI\,\beta^4\). What remains is the rotary inertia. Compared with the term \(EI\,\beta^4\,w\) its coefficient is \(\rho I\,\beta^2\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(EI\,\beta^4) = I\beta^2/A = \varepsilon\), at \(L = 1\) m and \(n = 1\) only \(1{,}316 \cdot 10^{-3}\), but not zero. So \(\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\) is not a frequency of the Rayleigh beam: the added inertia requires a smaller \(\omega^2\), and this is \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}^2\). Step 7 says the same for the Timoshenko beam: \(p(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}^2) < 0\), so \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}\) is not its frequency.

The condition (13.92) in theorem 13.86 is not superfluous either. The polynomial \(p\) has two roots for each \(n\), so the Timoshenko beam has two frequencies for each \(n\), each with its own mode. The lower frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}1}\) belongs to the bending mode: the axis bends from its straight position into the blue curve, and the cross sections turn almost exactly with it, by the angle \(\theta \approx w_{\mathrm{x}}\). At \(L = 1\) m, \(n = 1\) the amplitude ratio from the first equation of step 4 is \(W/\Theta = 319{,}6\) mm, close to the \(1/\beta = 318{,}3\) mm that \(\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) would give. The higher frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}2}\) belongs to the shear mode: there \(W/\Theta = -0{,}42\) mm, so the axis stays almost straight. But the cross sections turn relative to their position before the vibration as \(\Theta\cos(\beta x)\), most at the ends and in opposite directions, not at all in the middle. The deformation is therefore almost pure shear \(\gamma = w_{\mathrm{x}} - \theta\), the angle between the cross section and the perpendicular to the axis. The frequency of this mode is \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}2}/2\pi = \sqrt{\lambda_2}/2\pi = 39\,222\) Hz, close to \(\sqrt{kG/\rho}/(2\pi r) = 39\,116\) Hz, far above the range that interests us.

What does the number \(\varepsilon = (n\pi r/L)^2\) say? Why rotary inertia counts more in higher modes and shorter beams we first see in a figure, then compute.

At the top is the axis of the beam before the vibration and the axis in the first mode, which rises in the middle by the deflection \(W\) and has one half wave of length \(L\). The cross sections stay perpendicular to the axis, so at the end they turn by the slope of the axis \(\pi W/L\). At the bottom is the same beam in the third mode with the same deflection \(W\), but with half waves of length \(L/3\). The axis rises equally high over a three times shorter distance, so it is three times steeper, and the sections turn at the nodes by \(3\pi W/L\). If both modes vibrate equally fast, the velocity of the displacement is the same in both, while the horizontal velocity of a point at height \(y\) is three times larger at the bottom. Kinetic energy is proportional to the square of the velocity, so the ratio of the energy of turning to the energy of displacement is nine times larger at \(n = 3\) (whatever the speed of the vibration, since both energies change with it alike). A shorter beam at the same \(n\) does the same: the half wave \(L/n\) is shorter, the axis steeper at the same deflection. The sections have the same height in both rows, but at the bottom they are three times taller compared with the half wave. So what decides the correction is the ratio of the half wave to the section, \(L/(n r)\), not \(L/r\).

We compute the meaning of the number \(\varepsilon\) from the mode \(w = \sin(\beta x)\cos(\omega t)\). Here \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -\omega\sin(\beta x)\sin(\omega t)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -\omega\beta\cos(\beta x)\sin(\omega t)\), so \(\int_0^L\rho I\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\,/\int_0^L\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = I\beta^2\int_0^L\cos^2(\beta x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,/\big(A\int_0^L\sin^2(\beta x)\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\). Both integrals equal \(L/2\) (for a whole number \(n\) of half waves), so the ratio is \(I\beta^2/A = \varepsilon\). The number \(\varepsilon\) is thus the ratio of the kinetic energy of turning to the kinetic energy of translation in this mode. The shear deformation energy (\(V = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\), as in statics) against the bending one gives \(\int V^2/(kGA)\,\mathrm{d}x\,/\int EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\beta^2/(kGA) = \kappa\varepsilon\), because \(V^2 = (EI)^2\beta^6\cos^2(\beta x)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 = \beta^4\sin^2(\beta x)\), both integrals of squares are \(L/2\) and the time factor \(\cos^2(\omega t)\) is the same in both. Shear thus has a \(\kappa\) times larger share than turning. For our section and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) and \(\kappa = 2(1 + \nu)/k = 2{,}6 \cdot 1{,}2 = 3{,}12\) (at \(k = 5/6\)), which is close to the value \(E/(kG) \approx 3\) that CK gives for a rectangular section (CK writes the shear correction factor as \(\kappa\), PDF p. 419).

The ratios \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\varepsilon\) give a second view of theorem 13.89, with an oscillator of one weight and one spring. The circular frequency of such an oscillator is \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) (definition 9.4).

The Euler-Bernoulli beam in one mode is such an oscillator: the spring \(k\) is its bending stiffness, the weight \(m\) the mass of the displacement. We draw rotary inertia as a spinning disc on the weight. For the same motion it adds a kinetic energy \(\varepsilon\) times that of the displacement, as if the mass were \((1 + \varepsilon)\) times larger. Hence \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/(1 + \varepsilon)\), exactly as in item 2 of the theorem, because the Rayleigh mode stays the same sine. We draw shear as a second spring in series. Both springs carry the same force, and at the same force the shear spring holds \(\kappa\varepsilon\) times the energy of the bending one, so it is \(\kappa\varepsilon\) times more compliant. The whole is therefore \((1 + \kappa\varepsilon)\) times softer and \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}^2 \approx \omega_{\mathrm{EB}}^2/\big((1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \kappa\varepsilon)\big)\). The denominator is \(1 + (1 + \kappa)\varepsilon + \kappa\varepsilon^2\). The last term at \(L = 1\) m, \(n = 1\) is only \(5{,}4 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and without it the lower bound of (13.97) remains. That is why the computed ratio in example 13.90 lies just above the lower bound: the oscillator gives \(0{,}997297\), the bound \(0{,}997300\), the exact computation \(0{,}997303\). At \(L = 80\) mm, however, the oscillator gives \(0{,}711\) and the exact ratio is \(0{,}751\). Here the oscillator is no longer an exact picture. One of the reasons is that in the Timoshenko mode the sections no longer turn with the axis but only by \(0{,}64\) of its slope (first equation of step 4), so the added inertia is smaller than \(\varepsilon\) and the frequency higher than the oscillator predicts.

13.10.5 When rotary inertia and shear matter

The question of the section is when the correction is worth the effort. The inequalities (13.97) give an answer that does not need a computer.

Corollary 13.91 Rule for rotary inertia and shear (pravilo za vztrajnost zasuka in strig)

Let the beam be that of theorem 13.89 with \(\kappa = 3{,}12\) (rectangular section, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(k = 5/6\)). For the \(n\)-th mode:

  1. if \(n r/L \le 0{,}022\), then \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} > 0{,}99\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\), so both corrections together lower the frequency by less than \(1\) %,
  2. if \(n r/L \ge 0{,}046\), then \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}} < \omega_{\mathrm{R}} < 0{,}99\,\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}\), so rotary inertia alone already lowers the frequency by more than \(1\) %.
Proof

We must show that at these values of \(\varepsilon\) one of the bounds in (13.97) lies on the right side of the number \(0{,}99\).

  1. At \(n r/L = 0{,}022\), \(\varepsilon = (0{,}022\,\pi)^2 = 4{,}777 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \((1 + \kappa)\varepsilon = 4{,}12 \cdot 4{,}777 \cdot 10^{-3} = 0{,}01968\). Because \(\varepsilon\) grows with \(n r/L\), the number \((1 + \kappa)\varepsilon\) is even smaller for smaller \(n r/L\). By the left inequality of (13.97), therefore, \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}/\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} > (1 + 0{,}01968)^{-1/2} = 0{,}99030 > 0{,}99\).
  2. At \(n r/L = 0{,}046\), \(\varepsilon = (0{,}046\,\pi)^2 = 0{,}02088 \ge 0{,}0204\), and this holds for larger values too. By the right inequalities of (13.97), \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}/\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} < \omega_{\mathrm{R}}/\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = (1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2} \le 1{,}0204^{-1/2} = 0{,}98995 < 0{,}99\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. CK (PDF p. 419): the correction grows with the mode index and falls with slenderness, and what decides is the slenderness measured by the wavelength, not by the length of the beam.

At length \(1\) m, \(r/L = 0{,}0115\): the first rule holds for \(n = 1\) (\(0{,}0115 \le 0{,}022\), correction \(0{,}27\) %), at \(n = 2\) the number \(n r/L = 0{,}0231\) is just above the limit and the correction is \(1{,}06\) %, at \(n = 3\) it is \(0{,}0346\) and the correction \(2{,}3\) %. At length \(80\) mm already the first mode is in the range of the second rule (\(0{,}144 > 0{,}046\)). What decides is thus \(n r/L\), not \(L/r\). The physical explanation: the \(n\)-th mode has half waves of length \(L/n\), which we compare with the radius of gyration of the section. A shorter half wave at the same deflection means a steeper axis and more turned sections, and the correction matters when the wavelength is no longer large compared with \(r\) (CK, PDF p. 419).

The second rule is sharp in the sense that it does not hold even a little below the limit. For \(1\) % we need \((1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2} < 0{,}99\), that is \(\varepsilon > 0{,}99^{-2} - 1 = 0{,}0203\). At \(n r/L = 0{,}045\), \(\varepsilon = (0{,}045\,\pi)^2 = 0{,}019986 < 0{,}0203\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{R}}/\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = (1 + 0{,}019986)^{-1/2} = 0{,}99015\), so rotary inertia alone lowers the frequency by \(0{,}985\) %, less than \(1\) %, and the second rule does not hold. The row \(n = 3\) in the table of example 13.90 shows the same: at \(n r/L = 0{,}0346\) the Rayleigh correction is \(0{,}587\) %, below \(1\) %. The correction that the second rule overlooks is shear. At \(n r/L = 0{,}045\) the Timoshenko ratio is \(0{,}9617\) (computed from the zero of polynomial (13.96)), so the total correction is \(3{,}8\) %, about four times the Rayleigh one. That is why the second rule speaks of rotary inertia alone, and it is a sufficient condition for a large correction, not a necessary one.

A warning. For another section or material \(r\) and \(\kappa = 2(1 + \nu)/k\) are different. The rule is written with the number \(n r/L\), so \(r\) does not change the thresholds. The number \(0{,}046\) holds for every section and material, because it comes only from the Rayleigh bound \((1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2}\). The number \(0{,}022\), however, is computed with \(\kappa = 3{,}12\) and may fail for a larger \(\kappa\), as the example with \(\kappa = 10\) below shows. At \(L = 80\) mm, \(L/h = 2\), even the Timoshenko model is no longer accurate: the shear strain over the cross section is not constant and \(k\) was chosen from statics (section 5.13.2), not from dynamics. The numbers in the table say what the model predicts, not what we would measure. A more accurate answer is given by elastodynamics (section 13.4).

Let us show what this means on the same number \(n r/L = 0{,}022\) at which the first rule holds for our section. Let \(\kappa = 10\), so shear is softer than in our section, where \(\kappa = 3{,}12\). The number \(\varepsilon = (0{,}022\,\pi)^2 = 4{,}777 \cdot 10^{-3}\) is the same, because it does not depend on \(\kappa\). What changes is \((1 + \kappa)\varepsilon = 11 \cdot 4{,}777 \cdot 10^{-3} = 0{,}05255\) instead of \(0{,}01968\). The lower bound of theorem 13.89 is \((1 + 0{,}05255)^{-1/2} = 0{,}9747\), and the computation from the zero of polynomial (13.96) gives \(\omega_{\mathrm{T}}/\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = 0{,}9748\) (NumPy). The correction is \(2{,}5\) %, the first rule does not hold, although \(n r/L\) is the same. At \(\kappa = 3{,}12\) the ratio is \(0{,}99034\), the correction \(0{,}97\) %. The Rayleigh part is the same in both cases, \((1 + \varepsilon)^{-1/2} = 0{,}9976\), so the difference between \(0{,}97\) % and \(2{,}5\) % was caused by shear alone, which has a share \(\kappa\) times that of rotation.

13.10.6 Mass matrix of an element with rotary inertia

Chapter 12 built the mass matrix of a beam element (lemma 12.43) from the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\rho A\int\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and left out the rotary inertia. Now we show what it would add.

Lemma 13.92 Rotary-inertia mass matrix of a beam element (masna matrika vztrajnosti zasuka elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a beam element of length \(h\), area \(A\), second moment of area \(I\) and density \(\rho\), with the fields \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definitions 12.3 and 12.1). The kinetic energy of rotary inertia \(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} = \tfrac12\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \rho I\,\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}'^{\,2}\,\mathrm{d}x\) is \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} \rho I\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'^{\,\T}\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho I}{30\,h}\begin{pmatrix} 36 & 3h & -36 & 3h \\ 3h & 4h^2 & -3h & -h^2 \\ -36 & -3h & 36 & -3h \\ 3h & -h^2 & -3h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{13.98}\]

where \(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\) is the row of derivatives of the Hermite functions with respect to \(x\). The consistent mass matrix of the element with rotary inertia is \(\mat m_{\mathrm{e}} + \mat m_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}}\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the velocity is linear in the nodal quantities and that the integrals give the matrix.

  1. The Hermite functions are functions of position, so \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) and, as in step 1 of lemma 12.43, \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}'^{\,2} = \dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'^{\,\T}\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The column \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on \(x\), so by linearity of the integral (lemma 6.10) \(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\int\rho I\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'^{\,\T}\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. We map the element from \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) to \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) to \([0, 1]\) by \(x = x_{\mathrm{a}} + h\xi\), so \(x'(\xi) = h\), and by the rule for a change of variable (with the same proof as equation (7.28), from the fundamental theorem and the chain rule) the integral over \(x\) is \(h\) times the integral over \(\xi\). There \(H_1 = 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3\), \(H_2 = h\,(\xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3)\), \(H_3 = 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3\), \(H_4 = h\,(-\xi^2 + \xi^3)\) and \(H_{\mathrm{i}}' = \mathrm{d}H_{\mathrm{i}}/\mathrm{d}\xi\,/h\). The integrals of products of polynomials are computed term by term (SymPy), for example \(\int H_1'^{\,2}\,\mathrm{d}x = h\int_0^1 \tfrac{1}{h^2}\,36\,(\xi - \xi^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \tfrac{1}{h}\int_0^1 36\,(\xi - \xi^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \tfrac{36}{30\,h}\), because \(\int_0^1(\xi - \xi^2)^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \tfrac13 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac15 = \tfrac{1}{30}\). The other entries give (13.98).

Source: The proof is not from the source. COOK (11.3-3) (PDF p. 392) and FPGL (PDF p. 185, equations 5.42 to 5.45, the second matrix is the rotary-inertia effect) give the rotary-inertia addition to the mass matrix of a beam.

The matrix is exactly "what was missing": in translation of the element as a whole, \(\dot{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}} = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\), \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}' = 0\) and \(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), and in turning about the middle \(\dot v_{\mathrm{h}}' = 1\) and \(T_{\mathrm{e}}^{\mathrm{r}} = \tfrac12\rho I h\), so the mass of translation does not change and the turning gets an addition.

On the left the grey dashed line is the element before the motion, the orange line is the element after both nodes moved up by \(1\), the arrows show the move. The line stays horizontal, so the slope is zero. On the right the element before the motion and the orange line, turned about the middle, which stays at rest. The slope \(v'\) is \(1\) here, drawn as a small angle.

How much does this change the cantilever of chapters 5 and 12 (\(L = 1\) m, \(20 \times 40\) mm, steel)? With eight elements (SymPy and NumPy) the first frequencies without rotary inertia are \(32{,}391\) Hz and \(203{,}006\) Hz (the coefficients \(3{,}5160\) and \(22{,}0363\) of example 13.67 times \(\omega_0/2\pi = 9{,}2123\) Hz), with it \(32{,}381\) Hz and \(202{,}568\) Hz, that is \(-0{,}031\) % and \(-0{,}215\) %. Leaving out the rotary inertia in chapter 12 was therefore justified for the first mode, for the second \(0{,}2\) % is not negligible if we want the frequency to four digits (\(203{,}0\) against \(202{,}6\) Hz). The shear part is treated separately in the Timoshenko element (section 12.7).

On the right are the axes with the mode numbers \(1\) and \(2\), vertically the lowering of the frequency in percent. The blue bar is the lowering of the first frequency, \(0{,}031\), the orange one of the second, \(0{,}215\). The second mode is lowered seven times more, because it has shorter waves, at which the sections turn more (figure 13.150).

The promise of chapter 12 is thereby kept: the rotary inertia is derived from the kinetic energy (lemma 13.84), inserted into Hamilton's principle for the Rayleigh and Timoshenko beams (theorems 13.86 and 13.87), its effect on the exact frequencies is proved with bounds (theorem 13.89, corollary 13.91) and its contribution to the mass matrix is computed (lemma 13.92). To remember the rule: the correction matters when \(n r/L\) is larger than about \(0{,}02\).
