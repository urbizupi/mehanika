Chapter 19 Symmetry, periodicity and constraints

19.9 Cyclic symmetry and the split into harmonics

A wheel with six spokes looks exactly as before after a turn by a sixth of a circle: the same spokes, the same steel, the same clamping. We press on the rim with a single radial force, say from hitting a kerb. The problem has 18 unknowns, since the rim has six nodes with three unknowns each. The turn by a sixth of a circle is a coincidence that mirror symmetry from section 19.1 does not know: a mirror swaps left and right, a turn does not. Does it help us reduce the problem to one sector of the wheel, and when? For a wheel with thirty-six spokes and a thousand elements per sector the problem would be thirty-six times smaller. The answer has two parts. When the load repeats on every sector, one sector is enough, which is how COOK proceeds (section 10.9, PDF p. 368-370). When the load is arbitrary, like our single force, one sector is not enough, but problems of the size of one sector still are, only there are more of them. The second part is not in the sources. We derive it from the discrete Fourier transform of chapter 9 and it is our derivation.

The model is the same wheel as in section 19.10. All members are straight steel beams with the cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm as in chapter 12, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N, \(EI = 20\,907\) N m\(^2\), and each member is one frame element (definition 12.17). We make three idealizations. First: the hub is the point \(O\), rigidly clamped, while a real hub is elastic and the axle turns, so the rim displacements would change by the hub compliance, which we do not compute: the numbers of this section hold for a rigid hub. Second: the spokes are bending beams and not cables, as on a bicycle wheel. For our radial force the bending stiffness of a spoke does not matter: with cables instead of beams the radial displacement of node \(0\) changes by only \(0{,}09\) %. Under a tangential force, as in braking, spoke bending alone keeps the rim from turning about the hub, and with radial cables the stiffness matrix would be singular. This is why real wheels lace their spokes at an angle. Third: the rim is six straight beams with chord \(1\) m, while a real rim is a circular arc of length \(\pi/3 = 1{,}047\) m per sector, \(4{,}7\) % longer. We chose straight beams because frame elements (definition 12.17) are straight, a circular arc would need a curved element. Its effect on the displacements is not computed. The load acts only at nodes and there is none on the members, so by theorem 12.20, point 3, the displacements at the nodes of the frame model are exact. We compute linearly: the largest displacement is below \(3\) micrometres on a length of \(1\) m, a ratio of \(3\cdot 10^{-6}\), the largest rotation is \(0{,}94\) millionths of a radian and its square, the order of the neglected correction, is \(10^{-12}\).

We build figure 19.47 step by step. First the hub \(O\), clamped at the centre. Six spokes leave it and each ends in one of the six rim nodes, numbered \(0\) to \(5\) counterclockwise. Neighbouring nodes are joined by a rim beam. Every node has three unknowns, 18 in all. We divide the nodes into sectors. Sector \(0\) consists of node \(0\), the spoke that holds it and the rim beam up to node \(1\). Sectors \(1, \dots, 5\) are built the same way. A sector is thus a slice of the circle, like a piece of pie, and for our purposes it holds one piece of rim, one beam and one spoke. We chose the sectors so that each node lies in exactly one. The rim beam of sector \(0\) joins node \(0\) to node \(1\) of sector \(1\), so sectors \(0\) and \(1\) are neighbours, as are sectors \(5\) and \(0\).

We express the unknowns of a node in its own axes: the radial displacement \(r\) along the spoke outwards, the tangential displacement \(t\) perpendicular to it, and the rotation \(\varphi\). The axes of node \(j\) are turned by the angle \(j\alpha\) relative to the global axes, and at node \(1\) the axes \(r_1\), \(t_1\) are turned by the angle \(\alpha = 2\pi/N\) relative to the axes of node \(0\). Why local axes? Because then every sector looks the same: a spoke that runs radially has the same meaning and the same stiffness in local axes everywhere, and comparing sectors becomes comparing numbers.

Definition 19.42 Cyclic symmetry (ciklična simetrija)

Let \(N \ge 3\) be a natural number and \(O\) a point of the plane. A plane frame (definition 5.93), divided into frame elements (definition 12.17), is cyclically symmetric with \(N\) sectors about the point \(O\) (ciklično simetričen z \(N\) odseki) if its nodes, apart from a possible node at \(O\) with all three components clamped, are divided into \(N\) sectors (odseki) \(0, \dots, N-1\) of \(s\) nodes each, and under the rotation \(\rho\) about \(O\) by the angle \(\alpha = 2\pi/N\) counterclockwise three conditions hold.

  1. The rotation \(\rho\) takes the \(s\) nodes of sector \(j\) in order to the \(s\) nodes of sector \(j+1\) (sector numbers are counted modulo \(N\), so sector \(N-1\) goes to sector \(0\)).
  2. The rotation \(\rho\) takes elements to elements: if an element joins nodes \(a\) and \(b\), there is exactly one element that joins \(\rho a\) and \(\rho b\), with the same length, the same \(EA\) and the same \(EI\). Every element joins two nodes of the same sector, nodes of two neighbouring sectors, or a node of a sector with the clamped node at \(O\).
  3. If a component is clamped at node \(a\) of sector \(j\), it is a component in the axes of sector \(j\), turned by \(j\alpha\) (below), and the same component in the axes turned by \((j+1)\alpha\) is clamped at node \(\rho a\).

The unknowns of sector \(j\) are the components of the displacements and rotations of its nodes in the axes that are the global axes turned by \(j\alpha\) counterclockwise (the axis rotation matrix \(\mat Q(j\alpha)\) of definition 2.44). They are collected in a column \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(n\) free unknowns. The axes of sector \(0\) are the global axes.

Source: COOK section 10.9 (PDF p. 368-369) and ONATE1 section 9.6.2 (PDF p. 348-349) describe identical sectors that fit together around a circle, and the displacements on the sector boundaries in their local axes. The definition in this form, with \(N\) sectors and a rotation \(\rho\), is ours, because the sources have no general definition.

The word cyclic comes from the Greek word for a circle: after \(N\) turns by \(2\pi/N\) we are back at the start, so the steps form a circle. A sector is a slice of a circle, like a piece of pie, hence the name. In our wheel \(N = 6\), \(s = 1\) (one node per sector), the clamping is the hub \(O\), which belongs to no sector and is not an unknown. Every rim node has all three unknowns free in local axes, so \(n = 3\). We assume further that the structure satisfies the hypotheses of theorem 12.20, so by that theorem \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite. For the wheel this holds because the hub \(O\) clamps all components.

How is the stiffness matrix arranged in these axes? Let \(\hat{\mat K}\) be the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) in the local axes of the sectors, with blocks \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) of order \(n\) (rows of sector \(j\), columns of sector \(l\)). Figure 19.48 shows the pattern for six sectors. The rows are the sectors \(j\), the columns the sectors \(l\), and every cell of the grid is one block of order \(n\). The block \((j, l)\) is non-zero only if sectors \(j\) and \(l\) are joined by an element, that is for \(l = j\) and for the neighbours \(l = j \pm 1\). The pattern is thus the diagonal, accompanied by one diagonal above it and one below it. The blocks along the diagonals are equal from row to row, a shift by one sector, in the figure the arrow, brings the same block. In the corners the pattern wraps around the circle: row \(5\) has the neighbour \(0\) to the right of the diagonal, and row \(0\) has the neighbour \(5\) on the left.

Lemma 19.43 Block circulant matrix (krožna bločna matrika)

Let a frame be cyclically symmetric with \(N \ge 3\) sectors (definition 19.42) and satisfy the hypotheses of theorem 12.20 (the only small rigid motion with the clamped components zero is zero), let \(\hat{\mat K}\) be the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) in the local axes of the sectors (an \(Nn \times Nn\) matrix with blocks \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) of order \(n\)) and let the blocks \(\mat K_0 = \hat{\mat K}_{00}\) and \(\mat K_1 = \hat{\mat K}_{01}\) be blocks. Then:

  1. Shifting by one sector does not change the block, \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1, \mathrm{l}+1} = \hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) for all \(j\), \(l\) (indices modulo \(N\)).
  2. On the diagonal \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jj}} = \mat K_0\) with \(\mat K_0\) symmetric, \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}, \mathrm{j}+1} = \mat K_1\), \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1, \mathrm{j}} = \mat K_1^\T\), and all other blocks are zero.
  3. The matrix \(\hat{\mat K}\) is symmetric and positive definite.
Proof

We must show that turning a sector by \(\alpha\) changes a stiffness block exactly as it changes the local axes, and that the two changes cancel. For an element we use lemma 12.18 on its stiffness matrix.

  1. Let \(\mat Q(\beta) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta & \sin\beta \\ -\sin\beta & \cos\beta\end{bmatrix}\) be the rotation matrix of the axes by the angle \(\beta\) (equation (12.11), definition 2.44), \(\mat Z = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Q(\alpha), 1)\) a \(3 \times 3\) matrix and \(\tilde{\mat Z} = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Z, \mat Z)\). The matrix \(\mat Z\) is orthogonal, because \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (definition 2.46).
  2. Let the element \(e\) be at the angle \(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(e' = \rho e\) its image. By definition 19.42, item 2, \(e'\) has the same \(h\), \(EA\), \(EI\), hence the same local matrix \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\) (equation (12.12)), and the angle \(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}} + \alpha\). Since \(\mat Q(\beta_1 + \beta_2) = \mat Q(\beta_1)\mat Q(\beta_2)\) (the addition theorems for cosine and sine), \(\mat R_{\mathrm{e}'} = \mat R_{\mathrm{e}}\tilde{\mat Z}\) with \(\mat R\) from lemma 12.18. By item 2 of that lemma \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}'} = \mat R_{\mathrm{e}'}^\T\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\mat R_{\mathrm{e}'} = \tilde{\mat Z}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\tilde{\mat Z}\). For the node blocks \(a\), \(b\) of the element this means \([\mat k_{\mathrm{e}'}]_{\rho \mathrm{a},\rho \mathrm{b}} = \mat Z^\T[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}]_{\mathrm{ab}}\mat Z\).
  3. The block \((a, b)\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) is by theorem 7.13 the sum of the blocks \([\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}]_{\mathrm{ab}}\) over all elements that have both nodes \(a\) and \(b\). By definition 19.42, item 2, the map \(e \mapsto \rho e\) is a bijection between the elements with nodes \(a\), \(b\) and the elements with nodes \(\rho a\), \(\rho b\). Hence \(\mat K_{\rho \mathrm{a}, \rho \mathrm{b}} = \mat Z^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ab}}\mat Z\) (in global axes, before removing the clamped components). By item 3 of the definition clamped components map to clamped components, so from every sector we drop the rows and columns of the same components in local axes, and the blocks \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) keep the same shape for all sectors.
  4. Let \(\mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\) be the matrix that converts the global components of the nodes of sector \(j\) into local ones, \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\vek d_{\mathrm{j}}\), and let \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}} = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Z, \dots, \mat Z)\) with \(s\) blocks. The axes of sector \(j + 1\) are the axes of sector \(j\) turned by \(\alpha\), so \(\mat R_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\mat R_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\) (rotations in the plane commute, step 4 of the proof of lemma 12.18) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{N}} = \mat R_0\), since \(\alpha N = 2\pi\) and cosine and sine are periodic (lemma 8.11, item 4). The local block is \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}}\mat R_{\mathrm{l}}^\T\), because energy is a scalar, \(\hat{\vek d}^\T\hat{\mat K}\hat{\vek d} = \vek d^\T\mat K\vek d\).
  5. From steps 3 and 4, \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{l}+1} = \mat R_{\mathrm{j}+1}\mat K_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{l}+1}\mat R_{\mathrm{l}+1}^\T = \mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mat R_{\mathrm{l}}^\T\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\). Since \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\) commutes with \(\mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{l}}\) and \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}^\T = \mat I\), the \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{s}}\) cancel and what remains is \(\mat R_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}}\mat R_{\mathrm{l}}^\T = \hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\). This is item 1.
  6. Item 2 of the definition says that an element joins nodes of the same sector or of neighbouring sectors, so \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) if \(l\) differs from \(j\) by more than \(1\) modulo \(N\) (for \(N \ge 3\), \(j+1\) and \(j-1\) are different). By item 1, \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jj}} = \hat{\mat K}_{00} = \mat K_0\) and \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}+1} = \hat{\mat K}_{01} = \mat K_1\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric (theorem 7.13), so \(\hat{\mat K} = \mat R\mat K\mat R^\T\) with \(\mat R = \mathrm{diag}(\mat R_0, \dots, \mat R_{\mathrm{N}-1})\) is symmetric (lemma 1.102), hence \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{j}} = \hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{j}+1}^\T = \mat K_1^\T\) and \(\mat K_0 = \mat K_0^\T\).
  7. Since \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite and \(\mat R\) is orthogonal, for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) also \(\vek x^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek x = (\mat R^\T\vek x)^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}(\mat R^\T\vek x) > 0\), because \(\mat R^\T\vek x \ne \vek 0\). This is item 3.

Source: COOK section 10.9 (PDF p. 369, equations 10.9-2 and 10.9-3) says that all sectors have the same stiffness matrix in their own axes and that pairs of nodes on the boundaries have the same displacements in local axes, ONATE1 (9.37) (PDF p. 349) the same. The proof that the block matrix is circulant is ours.

The lemma says where the regularity comes from: the turn rearranges the sectors and also the axes, and the two changes cancel. The block circulant matrix has its name because each block row arises from the previous one by a shift of one block, and at the end it wraps around the circle back to the start, like the numbers on a clock.

Let us look at the blocks of the wheel. Spoke \(0\) is the element from the clamped hub to node \(0\), rim element \(0\) joins node \(0\) to node \(1\), rim element \(5\) joins node \(5\) to node \(0\). At node \(0\) the local axes are the global ones. The spoke lies along the axis \(r\), so it contributes the axial stiffness \(EA/h = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m in the direction \(r\) and the bending stiffness \(12EI/h^3 = 250\,880\) N/m in the direction \(t\). Both rim beams make an angle of \(60\) degrees with the direction \(r\), because the triangle of the hub and two neighbouring rim nodes is equilateral (both spokes and the chord are \(1\) m long). So each contributes \(\cos^2 60° = 0{,}25\) of its axial stiffness in the direction \(r\) and \(\sin^2 60° = 0{,}75\) in the direction \(t\) (equation (12.14)). For the block \(\mat K_0\) this gives \((\mat K_0)_{\mathrm{rr}} = EA/h + 2\,(0{,}25\,EA/h + 0{,}75\cdot 12EI/h^3) = 2{,}3558\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m. For \((\mat K_0)_{\mathrm{tt}} = 12EI/h^3 + 2\,(0{,}75\,EA/h + 0{,}25\cdot 12EI/h^3) = 2{,}3558\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m, equal to \((\mat K_0)_{\mathrm{rr}}\), because both sums are \(\tfrac32\,EA/h + 18\,EI/h^3\). For the rotation \((\mat K_0)_{\varphi\varphi} = 3\cdot 4EI/h = 250\,880\) N m, since each of the three beams at the node has stiffness \(4EI/h\) (matrix (12.12)). The remaining entries of the blocks we get the same way (NumPy). The block \(\mat K_1\) has all entries non-zero and is not symmetric, since the rim beam joins nodes \(0\) and \(1\), which have different axes. The blocks \(\hat{\mat K}_{02}\), \(\hat{\mat K}_{03}\), \(\hat{\mat K}_{04}\) are zero, because no element joins sector \(0\) to sectors \(2\), \(3\) or \(4\), as the empty cells of figure 19.48 show. Numerically the difference \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{l}+1} - \hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) is at most \(1{,}8\cdot 10^{-7}\) N/m in all blocks, for the largest entry \(2{,}4\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m (rounding).

When is the solution the same on all sectors? The answer is as for mirror symmetry: if the turn preserves the structure and the load, it preserves the solution too, since the problem has only one solution.

Theorem 19.44 Cyclic load, one sector suffices (ciklična obremenitev, en odsek zadošča)

Let a frame be cyclically symmetric with \(N \ge 3\) sectors (definition 19.42), with blocks \(\mat K_0\), \(\mat K_1\) from lemma 19.43, and let the load be cyclic, that is the column of sector loads in local axes is the same for all sectors, \(\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}} = \hat{\vek f}_0\) for \(j = 0, \dots, N-1\). Then the system \(\hat{\mat K}\hat{\vek d} = \hat{\vek f}\) has exactly one solution, the solution is cyclic, \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek u\) for every \(j\), and \(\vek u\) is the only solution of the system of order \(n\)

\[(\mat K_0 + \mat K_1 + \mat K_1^\T)\,\vek u = \hat{\vek f}_0 . \tag{19.16}\]

The matrix \(\mat K_0 + \mat K_1 + \mat K_1^\T\) is symmetric and positive definite.

Proof

We must show that the column with equal blocks solves the system if \(\vek u\) solves equation (19.16), and that this equation has a solution. Uniqueness then follows from lemma 19.43.

  1. The matrix \(\mat S = \mat K_0 + \mat K_1 + \mat K_1^\T\) is symmetric, because \(\mat K_0\) is symmetric and \((\mat K_1 + \mat K_1^\T)^\T = \mat K_1^\T + \mat K_1\) (lemma 1.102).
  2. Let \(\vek T = (\mat I, \dots, \mat I)^\T\) be the \(Nn \times n\) matrix with one identity block per sector (this \(\vek T\) is the transformation matrix \(\hat{\vek d} = \vek T\vek u\) of section 19.5 and is neither the matrix \(\mat T\) of lemma 12.22 nor the kinetic energy). By lemma 19.43, item 2, row \(j\) of the matrix \(\hat{\mat K}\) times \(\vek T\vek u\) is \(\mat K_1^\T\vek u + \mat K_0\vek u + \mat K_1\vek u = \mat S\vek u\) for every \(j\).
  3. For \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\), \((\vek T\vek u)^\T\hat{\mat K}(\vek T\vek u) = N\,\vek u^\T\mat S\vek u\), because the sum over all \(N\) rows gives by step 2 \(N\) times \(\vek u^\T\mat S\vek u\). The left side is positive, because \(\hat{\mat K}\) is positive definite and \(\vek T\vek u \ne \vek 0\) (lemma 19.43, item 3). So \(\mat S\) is positive definite, and the system \(\mat S\vek u = \hat{\vek f}_0\) has by theorem 6.29 exactly one solution.
  4. For this solution \(\vek u\), by step 2 row \(j\) of the system \(\hat{\mat K}(\vek T\vek u) = \hat{\vek f}\) is \(\mat S\vek u = \hat{\vek f}_0 = \hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(\vek T\vek u\) solves the system. Since \(\hat{\mat K}\) is positive definite, the system has by theorem 6.29 exactly one solution, and it is \(\vek T\vek u\).

Source: COOK section 10.9 (PDF p. 369, equations 10.9-1 to 10.9-4): pairs of nodes on the boundaries \(AA'\) and \(BB'\) are tied by equal displacements in local axes and \(\mat T^\T\mat K\mat T\) is assembled. Our \(\mat S\vek u = \hat{\vek f}_0\) is the same equation, since \(\vek T^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek T = N\mat S\) and \(\vek T^\T\hat{\vek f} = N\hat{\vek f}_0\). Uniqueness as a consequence of positive definiteness is not from the source.

COOK requires equal displacements of the node pairs on the boundaries (PDF p. 369) and does not show that the solution obtained is really a solution of the whole structure. The proof above shows it: the solution with equal blocks is a solution and the only one. COOK also warns that the load of the nodes on a sector boundary must be listed on only one of the two boundaries \(AA'\) and \(BB'\), since otherwise the sector would receive twice the load (PDF p. 369). With us this cannot happen, because every node belongs to exactly one sector, and the load of a node is in the column \(\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\) of that sector.

What about our single force? The load \(\hat{\vek f}_0 = (-500, 0, 0)\) N at node \(0\) and nothing at the other nodes is not cyclic, since \(\hat{\vek f}_0 \ne \hat{\vek f}_1\). Theorem 19.44 does not apply here and the solution is not cyclic. But the load can be split into cyclic parts by the discrete Fourier transform (definition 9.135), which we know from chapter 9: there we split a periodic function into harmonics, here the column of sector loads, which is "periodic" in \(j\) with period \(N\). We expand the load of sector \(j\) by the number \(k\) and \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N}\), which has \(|w_{\mathrm{k}}| = 1\) and \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^N = 1\) (theorem 9.34). The harmonic \(k\) is a load that is multiplied by the number \(w_{\mathrm{k}}\) from one sector to the next, so in one trip around the wheel it turns \(k\) times in the complex plane, which plays the same role as \(k\) oscillations per period in chapter 9. At \(k = 0\) the load is the same in all sectors, which is the cyclic load of theorem 19.44.

Figure 19.49 shows how our force splits. The single force \(F = 500\) N at node \(0\) equals the sum of four cyclically arranged parts. The harmonic \(k = 0\) is the force \(F/6 = 83{,}3\) N inwards at every node: the average of the load. The harmonics \(k = 1\) and \(5\) together give a radial load that varies as \(\tfrac{2F}{6}\cos(2\pi j/6)\) (\(167\) N inwards at node \(0\), \(83\) N inwards at nodes \(1\) and \(5\), \(83\) N outwards at nodes \(2\) and \(4\), \(167\) N outwards at node \(3\)). The harmonics \(2\) and \(4\) give \(\tfrac{2F}{6}\cos(2\pi\cdot 2j/6)\) and the harmonic \(3\) gives \(\tfrac{F}{6}(-1)^j\). At node \(0\) all parts add up to \(83{,}3 + 166{,}7 + 166{,}7 + 83{,}3 = 500\) N, and at node \(1\) to \(83{,}3 + 83{,}3 - 83{,}3 - 83{,}3 = 0\), since there is no force there.

For every harmonic \(k\) we must solve a smaller problem. Which one? Why such a solution? The structure is the same at every sector, so every row of equations sees the same picture, only turned, and a load that shifts by the phase \(w_{\mathrm{k}}\) from one sector to the next (harmonic in the figure) expects a response with the same phase, as any linear problem with identical terms does. Imagine a solution that is multiplied by \(w_{\mathrm{k}}\) from one sector to the next, \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\vek U\) with a complex column \(\vek U\) of order \(n\). By lemma 19.43 row \(j\) of the system \(\hat{\mat K}\hat{\vek d} = \hat{\vek f}\) is \(\mat K_1^\T\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \mat K_0\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} + \mat K_1\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}+1} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,(\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1^\T + \mat K_0 + w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1)\vek U\), since \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j-1} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\) (from \(w_{\mathrm{k}}\bar w_{\mathrm{k}} = |w_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 = 1\)). That is the phase \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\) times the matrix

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat K_0 + w_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat K_1 + \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat K_1^\T , \qquad w_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N} \tag{19.17}\]

of order \(n\) and the column \(\vek U\). At \(k = 0\) this is the matrix \(\mat K_0 + \mat K_1 + \mat K_1^\T\) of theorem 19.44. Beware of the notation: in this section \(k\) is not a stiffness but the number of the harmonic, and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) at \(k = 0\) is not the block \(\mat K_0\) of the lemma but the sum of three blocks. The problem for one sector is thus \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek U = \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the load of the harmonic \(k\). Does it have a solution, and is the true answer real? We need complex columns for this, so first the properties of the matrix (19.17).

Lemma 19.45 The sector matrix is Hermitian and positive definite (odsekovna matrika je hermitska in pozitivno definitna)

In the setting of lemma 19.43 let \(k\) be an integer, \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) the matrix (19.17). For a complex matrix \(\mat M\) the conjugate transpose \(\mat M^*\) is the matrix with entries \(\overline{M_{\mathrm{lj}}}\), and \(\mat M\) is Hermitian (hermitska) if \(\mat M^* = \mat M\). For a complex column \(\vek z\), \(\vek z^*\) is the row of conjugate entries. Then:

  1. \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) is Hermitian.
  2. For every non-zero complex column \(\vek z\) of order \(n\) the number \(\vek z^*\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek z\) is real and positive, so \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) is invertible.
  3. \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}}\) (conjugate entries) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}+\mathrm{N}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\).
Proof

We must show three items. For the second we use a complex column built from all the sectors with the phase \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\), and the positive definiteness of the matrix \(\hat{\mat K}\).

  1. The matrices \(\mat K_0\) and \(\mat K_1\) are real, so \((w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1)^* = \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1^\T\) with the conjugate number \(\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\) (definition 9.26) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}^* = \mat K_0^\T + \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1^\T + w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1\) (the neighbouring terms swap roles). Since \(\mat K_0 = \mat K_0^\T\) (lemma 19.43, item 2), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}^* = \mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  2. Let \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\) and \(\vek Z\) a column of order \(Nn\) with blocks \(\vek Z_{\mathrm{j}} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek z\) for \(j = 0, \dots, N-1\). Since \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^N = 1\) (theorem 9.34, \(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k} = 1\)), \(\vek Z_{\mathrm{N}} = \vek Z_0\), so at \(j = 0\) the block \(\vek Z_{-1} = \vek Z_{\mathrm{N}-1} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{-1}\vek z\) and at \(j = N-1\) the block \(\vek Z_{\mathrm{N}} = \vek z\), and the formula \(\vek Z_{\mathrm{j}\pm 1} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j\pm 1}\vek z\) holds for all \(j\). By lemma 19.43, \(\vek Z^*\hat{\mat K}\vek Z = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \vek Z_{\mathrm{j}}^*\big(\mat K_1^\T\vek Z_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \mat K_0\vek Z_{\mathrm{j}} + \mat K_1\vek Z_{\mathrm{j}+1}\big)\). The term \(j\) is \(\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\vek z^*\big(\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1^\T + \mat K_0 + w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1\big)\vek z\), and \(\bar w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j} = |w_{\mathrm{k}}|^{2j} = 1\) (theorem 9.34, item 2). Hence \(\vek Z^*\hat{\mat K}\vek Z = N\,\vek z^*\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek z\).
  3. Write \(\vek Z\) \(= \vek x + \mathrm{i}\vek y\) with real columns. Then \(\vek Z^*\hat{\mat K}\vek Z = \vek x^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek x + \vek y^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek y + \mathrm{i}\,(\vek x^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek y - \vek y^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek x)\) (lemma 9.25). The two numbers in the bracket are equal, because \(\vek y^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek x = (\vek y^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek x)^\T = \vek x^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek y\) (a number equals its transpose, \(\hat{\mat K}\) is symmetric), so the imaginary part is zero. At least one of \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\) is non-zero, because \(\vek Z \ne \vek 0\) (\(\vek Z_0 = \vek z\)), and \(\hat{\mat K}\) is positive definite, so the sum is positive. By step 2 \(\vek z^*\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek z\) is real and positive. If \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek z = \vek 0\), then \(\vek z^*\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek z = 0\), hence \(\vek z = \vek 0\), so \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) is invertible.
  4. Since \(w_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}k/N} = \cos(2\pi k/N) - \mathrm{i}\sin(2\pi k/N) = \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\) (theorem 9.34, item 1) and \(\mat K_0\), \(\mat K_1\) are real, \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \mat K_0 + \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1 + w_{\mathrm{k}}\mat K_1^\T = \overline{\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}}\). Likewise \(w_{\mathrm{k}+\mathrm{N}} = w_{\mathrm{k}}\) gives \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}+\mathrm{N}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG section 8.5 (PDF p. 373) defines a Hermitian matrix by \(\bar{\mat A}^\T = \mat A\), says that it is named after Hermite, and that a real Hermitian matrix is symmetric. The lemma and its proof are ours.

The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) thus has the properties of a stiffness matrix that we need: it is Hermitian, which is symmetry for a complex matrix, and positive definite, so the problem \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek U = \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) has exactly one solution. Now we can state the split in full.

Theorem 19.46 Split into harmonics (razcep po harmonikih)

In the setting of lemma 19.43 let \(\hat{\vek f}_0, \dots, \hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{N}-1}\) be arbitrary real columns of order \(n\) (the sector loads in local axes) and

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{N-1}\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N} , \qquad k = 0, \dots, N-1 \tag{19.18}\]

their discrete Fourier transform in \(j\) (definition 9.135, for every component). Then:

  1. The load is the sum of the harmonics, \(\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{N-1} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) for every \(j\).
  2. For every \(k\) the system of order \(n\) \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) has exactly one solution \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. The solution of the system \(\hat{\mat K}\hat{\vek d} = \hat{\vek f}\) is \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{N-1} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\), and it is real.
  4. \(\vek F_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}}\) and \(\vek U_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}}\) for \(0 < k < N\). So it suffices to solve the harmonics \(k = 0, \dots, \lfloor N/2\rfloor\), the harmonics \(0\) and (for even \(N\)) \(N/2\) are real, and every pair \(k\), \(N-k\) gives the real contribution \(2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\).
Proof

We must show four items. The first follows from discrete orthogonality, the third from substitution into the system together with uniqueness, the fourth from conjugation.

  1. Substitute (19.18): \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac1N\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}k(j-i)/N}\) (swapping the order of two finite sums). By lemma 9.136 the inner sum is \(N\) if \(N\) divides \(j - i\) and \(0\) otherwise. Since \(|j - i| < N\), this happens only at \(i = j\), and the sum is \(\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. Item 2 is lemma 19.45, item 2: the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) is invertible, so the system has the one solution \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{k}}^{-1}\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. Let \(\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\). Row \(j\) of the system (lemma 19.43, item 2) is \(\mat K_1^\T\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \mat K_0\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}} + \mat K_1\hat{\vek d}_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\) (term by term as before the lemma, with \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j\pm1} = w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\pm1}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{-1} = \bar w_{\mathrm{k}}\)). By item 2 this equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\) by item 1. So \(\hat{\vek d}\) solves the system. Let \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) be the real and imaginary parts of the column \(\hat{\vek d}\). Since \(\hat{\mat K}\) and \(\hat{\vek f}\) are real, \(\hat{\mat K}\vek a = \hat{\vek f}\) and \(\hat{\mat K}\vek b = \vek 0\). The second equation gives \(\vek b^\T\hat{\mat K}\vek b = 0\), and since \(\hat{\mat K}\) is positive definite, \(\vek b = \vek 0\). So \(\hat{\vek d} = \vek a\) is real, and by theorem 6.29 it is the only solution.
  4. Since \(\hat{\vek f}_{\mathrm{j}}\) is real and \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j(N-k)/N} = \mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jk/N}\) (theorem 9.34, item 1, and \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}j} = 1\)), \(\vek F_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}}\). Conjugating the system \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) gives \(\overline{\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}}\,\overline{\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}} = \overline{\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}}\), that is by lemma 19.45, item 3, \(\mat K_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}}\overline{\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}}\). By item 2 this system has one solution, so \(\vek U_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}}\). At \(k = 0\) and \(k = N/2\), \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = \pm 1\), so \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) are real and \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\) is real. The terms \(k\) and \(N-k\) in the sum of item 3 at sector \(j\) are written \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}} + \overline{w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}} = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,j}\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\).

Source: The split of the load and of the solution into harmonics on a cyclic structure is our derivation from the discrete Fourier transform (definition 9.135, chapter 9), because the sources of this section do not contain it. COOK section 14.4 (PDF p. 530-531) uses the same idea for bodies of revolution under arbitrary load: the load is written as a series of cosines and sines in the circumferential angle \(\theta\), each harmonic \(n\) is solved separately in the plane and the results are added.

The theorem says four things we shall need. The problem with \(18\) unknowns is replaced by six problems with three unknowns, of which by item 4 only four are needed (\(k = 0, 1, 2, 3\)), because \(k\) and \(6 - k\) are conjugates of each other. The solution is a sum of waves that turn by \(2\pi k/N\) from sector to sector, which at \(k = 1\) means one trip around and at \(k = 3\) a half turn per step. Since \(N\) sectors have only \(N\) different harmonics, the series does not reach arbitrarily high numbers: the harmonics \(k\) and \(k + N\) have the same values on the sectors, which is aliasing (corollary 9.137). For a continuous body, which COOK treats with a Fourier series in the angle (section 14.4), there is no aliasing, because the angle is continuous.

Example 19.47 A single force on the rim of the wheel (enojna sila na platišču kolesa)

The wheel of figure 19.47 has a radial force \(500\) N inwards at node \(0\), \(\hat{\vek f}_0 = (-500, 0, 0)\) (the component \(r\) is positive outwards, so inwards it is negative), and nothing at the other nodes (figure 19.49, top left). By equation (19.18) \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac16\,\hat{\vek f}_0 = (-83{,}33, 0, 0)\) N for every \(k = 0, \dots, 5\), since the only non-zero term has the factor \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\). The matrices \(\mat K_{\mathrm{k}}\) are Hermitian and have positive eigenvalues, as lemma 19.45 requires. The smallest are \(3{,}26\cdot 10^{4}\), \(2{,}92\cdot 10^{5}\), \(2{,}09\cdot 10^{5}\) and \(1{,}67\cdot 10^{5}\) at \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3\) (numbers in SI units). The solutions \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\) have a real radial displacement and by item 4 of theorem 19.46 are conjugate for \(k\) and \(6-k\), for example \(\vek U_1 = (-0{,}531, -0{,}530\,\mathrm{i}, -0{,}227\,\mathrm{i})\) and \(\vek U_5 = \overline{\vek U_1}\) (\(\mu\)m, \(\mu\)m, \(\mu\)rad):

\(k\) \(0\) \(1\) and \(5\) \(2\) and \(4\) \(3\)
\((\vek U_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\mu\)m \(-0{,}266\) \(-0{,}531\) \(-0{,}530\) \(-0{,}529\)
contribution to \(r\) of node \(0\), \(\mu\)m \(-0{,}266\) \(-1{,}062\) \(-1{,}060\) \(-0{,}529\)

The contribution of a pair is twice the real part of \((\vek U_{\mathrm{k}})_{\mathrm{r}}\), because at node \(0\) the phase is \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,0} = 1\), and at \(k = 0\), \(3\) once. The sum is \(-2{,}916\ \mu\)m (before rounding the terms). The direct solution of the whole system with \(18\) unknowns gives the same: the difference at all six nodes and three components is at most \(1{,}4\cdot 10^{-19}\) m and the imaginary part of the sum is \(2\cdot 10^{-21}\) m (rounding, NumPy). At node \(1\) the same parts are \(-0{,}266\), \(-0{,}531\), \(+0{,}530\), \(+0{,}529\) with the phase factors \(w_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the sum is \(+0{,}263\ \mu\)m, as the whole system gives. Figure 19.50 draws the radial displacements of the whole system as blue bars above the node numbers, on a horizontal axis with the scale one micrometre per centimetre. The bar of node \(0\) reaches \(2{,}92\ \mu\)m inwards, hence downwards, the other five are short and outwards.

Why is \(\vek U_0\) half of \(\vek U_3\) although the load \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the same? Because at \(k = 0\) the sector is about twice as stiff in the direction \(r\). Take the free body of node \(0\) in figure 19.47. At \(k = 0\) all the nodes move inwards by \(u\), the radius shrinks to \(R - u\) and the chord \(2(R - u)\sin 30° = R - u\) of the rim shrinks by \(u\). Each of the two rim beams is compressed by the force \(EA\,u/h\) along the chord and gives the radial component \(\cos 60°\cdot EA\,u/h\), so both together \(EA\,u/h\), and the spoke gives another \(EA\,u/h\). The radial stiffness is \(2\,EA/h = 3{,}136\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m, which is \((\mat K_{\mathrm{k}=0})_{\mathrm{rr}}\). At \(k = 3\) the nodes alternately move by \(-u\) and \(+u\): by the cosine rule the chord between a point at the radius \(R - u\) and the neighbouring one at the radius \(R + u\) at an angle of \(60°\) has length \(\sqrt{R^2 + 3u^2}\), so to first order it does not change, the rim does not resist and the stiffness is only that of the spoke, \(EA/h = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m (\(1{,}576\cdot 10^{8}\) with the bending of the rim). The ratio of the stiffnesses is \(2{,}0\), as the table shows.

The split exists in the computation too, not only in the formulas: the sum of six problems with three unknowns gives the same as one problem with eighteen. But we also know what would happen if we proceeded like COOK and took only one sector with equal displacements on the boundaries.

Example 19.48 Two cases where the cyclic solution fails (dva primera, kjer ciklična rešitev ne drži)

Theorem 19.44 has two hypotheses, the cyclicity of the structure and the cyclicity of the load. Let us show what happens when each is missing.

  1. The load is not cyclic. The same wheel, the same force \(500\) N at node \(0\). The solution of one sector with equal displacements, equation (19.16) with \(\hat{\vek f}_0 = (-500, 0, 0)/6\), which is the harmonic \(k = 0\) alone, gives the radial displacement \(-0{,}266\ \mu\)m at all nodes, which is the orange dashed line in figure 19.50. The true displacement of node \(0\) is \(-2{,}916\ \mu\)m, which is \(10{,}98\) times larger, and elsewhere it is \(+0{,}26\ \mu\)m instead of \(-0{,}27\ \mu\)m, so of the opposite sign. The reason is in the table of example 19.47: at node \(0\) all six harmonics have the phase \(w_{\mathrm{k}}^{\,0} = 1\) and add up, and the harmonic \(k = 0\) gives only \(9\) % of the sum. Elsewhere harmonics with different phases mostly cancel. The sector with equal displacements answers the question about the average load and not about a force at one place.
  2. The structure is not cyclic. Spoke \(3\) has half the width (\(10 \times 40\) mm), so \(EA/2\) and \(EI/2\), the rest is the same. At every node \(500/6 = 83{,}33\) N acts inwards, a cyclic load. The block \(\hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{l}+1} - \hat{\mat K}_{\mathrm{jl}}\) has an entry \(7{,}8\cdot 10^{7}\) N/m, while the largest entry is \(2{,}4\cdot 10^{8}\) N/m, so the pattern is not circulant. The solution of one sector of a wheel with six equal spokes, \(-0{,}266\ \mu\)m, is still approximately right at five nodes (\(-0{,}245\ \mu\)m), but at node \(3\) the displacement is \(-0{,}49\ \mu\)m, which is \(1{,}8\) times larger, because the spoke there has half the axial stiffness. One spoke has spoiled the symmetry, and the theorem then claims nothing.

Two remarks complete the theorem. The first: COOK describes a trick (PDF p. 370). The sum of blocks in equation (19.16) arises by itself during assembly (theorem 7.13) if the homologous nodes \(0\) and \(1\) on the boundaries of the sector get the same number, and ONATE1 (section 9.6.2, PDF p. 348) recommends the same. The coordinates of the nodes in the computation of the element matrices are then still the true ones, and the displacements of the boundaries must be in local axes, as we did. The second: skew symmetry in COOK, figure 2.11-4 (PDF p. 70-71), where a half turn maps the structure onto itself, is the case \(N = 2\). Our definition requires \(N \ge 3\), because for \(N = 2\) the neighbours \(j - 1\) and \(j + 1\) are the same sector. Then all elements between the two sectors are collected in a single block \(\mat B = \hat{\mat K}_{01}\). By item 1 of lemma 19.43, which does not need \(N \ge 3\), \(\hat{\mat K}_{10} = \hat{\mat K}_{01}\), and by symmetry \(\hat{\mat K}_{10} = \hat{\mat K}_{01}^\T\), so \(\mat B\) is symmetric. The only harmonics are \(w_0 = 1\) and \(w_1 = -1\), and the sector matrices are \(\mat K_0 + \mat B\) and \(\mat K_0 - \mat B\) (not \(\mat K_0 \pm 2\mat B\), which equation (19.17) with \(\mat K_1 = \mat B\) would give). The harmonic \(0\) is a cyclic load, the second sector is a turned copy of the first, which is skew symmetry. The harmonic \(1\) has \(\hat{\vek d}_1 = -\hat{\vek d}_0\), which is skew antisymmetry: after the turn the loads reverse, as COOK describes next to figure 2.11-4b (PDF p. 71).

The wheel has, besides the turns, the mirror across spoke \(0\), which takes sector \(j\) to sector \(-j\) (section 19.1). In our result this is visible: \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\) at node \(1\) is \(+1{,}22\ \mu\)m and at node \(5\) it is \(-1{,}22\ \mu\)m, because the mirror reverses the direction \(t\) (theorem 19.6). In the harmonics the mirror shows in example 19.47: the radial component of every \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\) is real, the tangential component and the rotation are imaginary, for instance \(\vek U_1 = (-0{,}531, -0{,}530\,\mathrm{i}, -0{,}227\,\mathrm{i})\). Item 4 of theorem 19.46, \(\vek U_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}} = \overline{\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}}\), holds for every real load, also an unsymmetric one. Only together with the mirror symmetry of the load does it say that \(\vek U_{\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{k}}\) has the same radial component as \(\vek U_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the opposite tangential component and rotation, which is the mirror \(t \to -t\) at the level of the harmonics. The next section (19.10) uses the same split for the natural frequencies of the wheel. What we have not proved: a wheel with a circular rim instead of a polygon and the loading of bodies of revolution by a series of cosines and sines (COOK sections 14.4 and 14.5, PDF p. 530-531) are only named. Plane and solid elements for such structures are in chapter 15.
