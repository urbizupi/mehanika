Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.12 Energy of a beam and virtual forces (energija nosilca in virtualne sile)

Chapter 4 found displacements from forces without geometry. We multiplied an imagined force in equilibrium by the true displacement and got an equality of work. For bars and trusses this gave the displacement of a joint, and the energy written with forces gave the forces in a truss that equilibrium alone does not determine. A beam has three quantities where a bar has one: the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\). Do the same three steps work here too? How do we get the deflection at any point of a beam without solving the deflection equation? And how do we find the forces in a statically indeterminate beam, such as the propped cantilever of section 5.9.4, where equilibrium is not enough?

The answer has four steps. First we compute the energy that a beam stores. Then we carry the principle of virtual forces over to the beam and derive from it the unit dummy load method. With it we get displacements and rotations, and with Castigliano's second theorem also the forces in a statically indeterminate beam. At the end we show that the displacements caused by forces at two points are reciprocal. Throughout the section we use the steel rectangular cross section of section 5.1.3, 20 mm wide and 40 mm high, with bending stiffness \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), and three beams: the cantilever of length 1 m with a force of 500 N downward at the free end and the simply supported beam of length 2 m with a uniform load of 1 kN/m from section 5.1.3, and the propped cantilever of length 2 m with the same load from section 5.9.4. The supports have the names of definition 5.5: the clamp, the fixed pin support and the roller support.

5.12.1 Strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca)

In a bar the strain was the same at all points of the cross section, so the energy density was the same too. In a beam this is not so. By theorem 5.15 the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) depends on the height \(y\) in the cross section: the fibres at the top and at the bottom stretch or shorten by different amounts, and the fibres on the centroidal axis only by \(u'\). We therefore get the energy of the whole beam by adding the energy density first over the cross section and then along the axis. We compute with the model of definition 5.14 and with the assumption of the model that every fibre is in uniaxial stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (section 5.5.1). The strain energy density is then \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\) (definition 3.44, as for the bar in section 4.1).

Definition 5.75 Strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca)

Let a beam of length \(L\) be as in definition 5.14, with constant axial stiffness \(EA\) and constant bending stiffness \(EI\) (a prismatic beam, for a slowly varying section the same formula holds with \(EA(x)\) and \(EI(x)\), EP (2.5.18), p. 75, but we do not need it here), and let \(u\) and \(v\) be the functions of the axial and the transverse displacement of the axis, \(u\) continuously differentiable and \(v\) twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \([0, L]\). The strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca) is the number

\[U(u, v) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( EA\,(u')^2 + EI\,(v'')^2 \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.67}\]

Source: EP (3.2.37)-(3.2.39) (p. 117), ONATE2 (1.5) and the following lines (PDF pp. 34-36).

The function \(U\) takes two functions as input and gives one number with the unit joule as output. Does this number really mean the energy of the body of section 3.6? The answer is in the next lemma.

Lemma 5.76 Energy of a beam from the energy density (energija nosilca iz gostote energije)

Let a beam be made of a homogeneous material with Young's modulus \(E\) and cross section \(A\) with its centroid on the axis, in the displacement field of theorem 5.15 with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Then the strain energy of the body \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V\) with \(w = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\) equals the number (5.67) with \(EA = E A\) and \(EI = E I\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the density over the cross section gives two terms in which there is no mixed product \(u'\,v''\).

  1. We expand the square of the strain: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 = (u')^2 - 2y\,u'\,v'' + y^2\,(v'')^2\).
  2. The body is made of cross sections and the axis is \(x\), so by the theorem on the order of integration (theorem 4.9) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L \big( \int_{\mathrm{A}} w\,\mathrm{d}A \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. The functions \(u'\) and \(v''\) depend only on \(x\), so in the inner integral we take them out (linearity of the integral): \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}A = (u')^2 \int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A - 2\,u'\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + (v'')^2 \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  4. The axis passes through the centroids of the cross sections, so the first moment of area is \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) (lemma 5.22), and the mixed term drops out. The remaining integrals are the area \(A\) and the second moment of area \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) (definition 5.26).
  5. When we multiply by \(\tfrac12 E\) and integrate over \(x\), we get (5.67).

Source: EP (3.2.37)-(3.2.39) (p. 117). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The lemma also answers the question why there is no mixed term in the energy: the centroidal axis is chosen so that stretching of the axis and bending do not mix. Let us mention what the energy does not capture. In the model \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so shear stresses do not appear in this energy. How much is missing we estimate later in this section, when we can compute the shear energy.

Let us test it on the cantilever. A transverse load causes no axial force, so \(u' = 0\) and only the bending term remains. By theorem 5.35 \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and by equilibrium \(M = -F\,(L - x)\), so the energy per unit length is

\[\frac12\,EI\,(v'')^2 = \frac{M^2}{2EI} = \frac{F^2\,(L - x)^2}{2EI} .\]

At the clamp this is \(500^2 \cdot 1^2/(2 \cdot 20\,906{,}7) = 5{,}979\) J/m, at the free end zero (figure 5.66). The energy is thus very unevenly distributed. The reason is the square: the moment falls linearly towards the free end, and the energy with its square. In the first half of the length, next to the clamp, \(7/8\) of all the energy is stored, \(1{,}744\) J. The total energy is

\[U = \int_0^L \frac{F^2\,(L - x)^2}{2EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{F^2 L^3}{6EI} = \frac{500^2 \cdot 1}{6 \cdot 20\,906{,}7}\ \text{J} = 1{,}993\ \text{J} .\]

This agrees with Clapeyron's theorem (theorem 4.86): \(\tfrac12\,F\,|v(L)| = \tfrac12 \cdot 500\ \text{N} \cdot 7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1{,}993\) J, where \(v(L) = -7{,}972\) mm is the deflection of the free end from section 5.9.2. In a bar the energy is spread evenly along the length, in a beam it is gathered where the moment is largest.

5.12.2 Principle of virtual forces for a beam (načelo virtualnih sil za nosilec)

In section 5.10.2 we wrote the principle of virtual displacements for a beam: true internal forces in equilibrium and an imagined displacement. Chapter 4 showed that the roles can be exchanged. The forces can be imagined if they are in equilibrium, and the displacement is true. Such a displacement does not need equilibrium, only that it is smooth (theorem 4.97). Here we write the same for a beam.

Let the imagined load of the beam consist of a distributed axial load \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and a transverse load \(\delta q\) (force per length, \(\delta q\) positive upward), of point forces \(\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\) and couples \(\delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the interior points \(x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{k}}\), and of forces and couples at the ends. The imagined internal forces \(\delta N\), \(\delta V\), \(\delta M\) have the same meaning as for the true load, that is the forces and the moment with which the part at larger \(x\) acts on the part at smaller \(x\) (section 5.2). The equilibrium conditions of a piece of the beam hold for them, \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta V' = -\delta q\) and \(\delta M' = -\delta V\), and so do the jumps at the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), by lemma 5.12: \(\delta V\) increases by \(-\delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\delta N\) by \(-\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\delta M\) by \(-\delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Theorem 5.77 Principle of virtual forces for a beam (načelo virtualnih sil za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in definition 5.14 of length \(L\), and let \(u\) be a continuously differentiable and \(v\) a twice continuously differentiable function on an open set that contains \([0, L]\) (the true displacements of the axis, defined on the whole beam). Let \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\delta q\) be continuous functions on \([0, L]\) and \(0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{k}} < L\). Let \(\delta N\), \(\delta V\), \(\delta M\) be functions on \([0, L]\) that are continuously differentiable on every interval between consecutive points \(0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{k}}, L\), with \(\delta M\) twice continuously differentiable there, and let \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta V' = -\delta q\), \(\delta M' = -\delta V\) hold on these intervals, and at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\)

\[\delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}} , \quad \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta F_{\mathrm{i}} , \quad \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta M_{\mathrm{i}} .\]

Then

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L \big( \delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'' \big)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L \big( \delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u + \delta q\,v \big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^k \big( \delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\,v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\,v'(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \big) \\ &\quad + \Big[ \delta N\,u + \delta V\,v + \delta M\,v' \Big]_0^L . \end{aligned} \tag{5.68}\]

The right-hand side is the work of the imagined loads on the true displacements, where the last term consists of the imagined forces at the ends: at \(x = L\) these are \(\delta N(L)\), \(\delta V(L)\), \(\delta M(L)\), and at \(x = 0\) they are \(-\delta N(0)\), \(-\delta V(0)\), \(-\delta M(0)\).

Proof

We must show that integration by parts on every interval gives the formula, and that the boundary terms at the interior points combine into the work of the point forces and couples. The displacements \(u\) and \(v\) are not restricted by equilibrium, so the proof does not need the law of the material.

  1. Let \([a, b]\) be one of the intervals. Integration by parts (corollary 3.41) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta N\,u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta N\,u \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta N'\,u\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta N\,u \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\), where we inserted \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The same for \(\delta M\,v''\): \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta M\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta M'\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta M' = -\delta V\).
  3. Once more by parts: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta V\,v \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V'\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta V\,v \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta V' = -\delta q\).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 together give \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (\delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u + \delta q\,v)\,\mathrm{d}x + \big[ \delta N\,u + \delta V\,v + \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b\).
  5. We add over all intervals. The integrals combine into integrals over \([0, L]\). The boundary terms at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the term of the upper limit of the left interval, taken at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-\), and the opposite term of the lower limit of the right interval, taken at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+\). The functions \(u\), \(v\) and \(v'\) are continuous at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the sum of these terms is \(\big( \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \big( \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \big( \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) v'(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and by the jump conditions this is \(\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\,v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\,v'(x_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  6. What remains are the boundary terms at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), which is the last term of equation (5.68).

Source: EP (4.4.5) and (4.4.11) (pp. 197-198), EP example 4.4.2 (p. 200). The proof in steps is not from the sources: it is the same computation as the proof of theorem 5.62, with the roles of the fields exchanged.

The proof showed that the equality is not a demand on the equilibrium of the true beam, but a purely mathematical consequence of the equilibrium of the imagined system and the smoothness of the displacements. The true beam enters only when we insert the law of the material, \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35).

5.12.3 Unit dummy load method for beams (metoda enotske sile za nosilce)

How do we use the theorem for the displacement we look for? As for the truss (theorem 4.95): we choose the imagined load so that on the right-hand side of equation (5.68) only the displacement we look for remains. If we look for the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), the imagined load is one force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at this point, together with the reactions of the supports that balance it. If we look for a rotation, the imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m. When the supports do not yield under the true load, the reactions of the imagined system do no work.

Theorem 5.78 Unit dummy load method for a beam (metoda enotske sile za nosilec)

Let a beam be made of a linearly elastic material as in theorem 5.35, with the true internal forces \(N\) and \(M\) for the given load and the true displacements \(u\), \(v\), which satisfy the conditions of theorem 5.77 (so there are no point couples at interior points, where the moment, that is \(v''\), would jump). Let the supports be such that at every end every displacement or rotation they prevent equals zero under the true load. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}} \in [0, L]\) be a point.

  1. Let \(n(x)\) and \(m(x)\) be the axial force and the moment, divided by \(1\) N, in the imagined system that consists of a force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports (with the same signs as for the true forces). Then the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is
    \[v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = \int_0^L \Big( \frac{n\,N}{EA} + \frac{m\,M}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.69}\]
  2. If the imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) with the reactions, the formula is the same for the rotation \(v'(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), where \(n\) and \(m\) are the axial force and the moment of this system, divided by \(1\) N m.

The same holds for the axial displacement \(u(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) with an imagined force \(1\) N in the direction \(x\).

Proof

We must show that equation (5.68) with the chosen imagined load gives (5.69). We prove item 1, item 2 is the same with a couple.

  1. Let \(\delta F = 1\) N, and the imagined load the force \(\delta F\) in the direction \(y\) at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports. The imagined internal forces are \(\delta N = n\cdot 1\) N and \(\delta M = m \cdot 1\) N, with no distributed imagined load, \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta q = 0\).
  2. By theorem 5.77 the right-hand side of equation (5.68) is the sum of the work of the force at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), which is \(\delta F\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) (if \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is an interior point this is a term of the sum, if it is at an end, it is part of the boundary term), and the work of the reactions at the ends.
  3. A reaction of a support acts in the direction of the displacement or rotation that the support prevents, and by assumption this is zero under the true load. The work of the reactions is therefore zero. At a free end, where there is no support, there is no imagined force (unless \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is there).
  4. Hence \(\int_0^L (\delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \delta F\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\).
  5. In the true beam \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35). We insert, divide by \(\delta F = 1\) N and get (5.69).

Source: EP (4.4.12) (p. 198), examples 4.4.2 to 4.4.4 (pp. 200-203). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The formula contains no term for shear deformation, which would be multiplied by the imagined shear force. We leave it out on purpose. Theorem 5.77 is proved for a model in which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (theorem 5.15), so in the left-hand side of equation (5.68) there is no shear strain that the imagined shear force could multiply. The term would need a model with shear strain, and this section does not have one. That model is the subject of the section on the beam with shear strain, where we complete the unit load method with a shear term. How much the term would add for our cantilever we estimate already later in this section, when we can compute the shear energy.

We test the theorem on three examples from section 5.9.2 and the following ones, which we have already solved with the deflection equation. In each the result is known, so we test the new route on known numbers.

  1. The cantilever, the deflection of the free end (figure 5.67). The true moment is \(M = -F\,(L - x)\) for the force \(F = 500\) N downward. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at the free end has, by the conditions at a free end (section 5.2), the shear force \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) N and the moment \(M(L) = 0\). Hence, divided by \(1\) N, \(m' = -1\) (because \(M' = -V\)) and \(m(L) = 0\), so \(m(x) = L - x\). The axial force is zero in both. By (5.69)
    \[v(L) = \int_0^L \frac{(L - x)\cdot\big( -F\,(L - x) \big)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{F L^3}{3EI} = -7{,}972\ \text{mm} .\]
    This is the same value as in section 5.9.2, and negative because the end goes down. The same computation with a couple \(1\) N m at the free end (\(m = 1\) along the whole length, because \(M(L)\) equals the couple, \(m' = 0\)) gives the rotation \(v'(L) = \int_0^L 1\cdot(-F(L - x))/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x = -F L^2/(2EI) = -0{,}01196\), as in section 5.9.2.
  2. The simply supported beam, the deflection at mid-span (figure 5.68). The true moment is \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\) for \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, \(L = 2\) m. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at mid-span requires reactions of \(\tfrac12\) N downward each, so \(m = -x/2\) for \(x \le L/2\) and \(m = -(L - x)/2\) for \(x \ge L/2\). By the symmetry of the two halves
    \[v\big( \tfrac L2 \big) = 2\int_0^{L/2} \frac{(-x/2)\cdot\tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{5\,q_0 L^4}{384\,EI} = -9{,}965\ \text{mm} .\]
  3. The simply supported beam, the rotation over the support (figure 5.69). The imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m at the left end, in the counterclockwise sense (the positive direction of the axis \(z\)). The reactions are \(1/L\) each and balance it. The imagined couple at the left end is \(-m(0)\), so \(m(0) = -1\), and \(m(L) = 0\), so \(m(x) = -(1 - x/L)\). The rotation is
    \[v'(0) = \int_0^L \frac{-(1 - x/L)\cdot\tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{q_0 L^3}{24\,EI} = -0{,}01594 .\]
    The rotation is negative: the axis of the beam next to the left support falls towards the right.

We checked all three results with SymPy, and the values \(-7{,}972\) mm, \(-9{,}965\) mm and \(-0{,}01594\) agree with the solutions of the deflection equation. A central force \(1\) kN on the simply supported beam gives by the same route \(-FL^3/(48EI) = -7{,}972\) mm, as for the cantilever, because the halves of the beam are clamped cantilevers of length \(1\) m with \(500\) N at the end.

5.12.4 Complementary energy and statically indeterminate beams (komplementarna energija in statično nedoločeni nosilci)

So far we have computed displacements from internal forces that were already known. For a statically indeterminate beam (definition 5.6) equilibrium of forces does not determine them, because there are more unknown reactions than equations of equilibrium. For the propped cantilever of section 5.9.4 there are three reactions under a transverse load (the force and the moment of the clamp and the force of the roller support), and two equations of equilibrium (the sum of forces and the sum of moments). In section 5.9.4 we solved the problem with the deflection equation and four boundary conditions. We can do the same with energy, as for the truss of chapter 4. For this we need the energy written with forces instead of displacements.

In chapter 4 the complementary energy is \(U^* = \int w^*\,\mathrm{d}V\) with the complementary energy density \(w^*\) (definitions 4.98 and 3.49), which we express with stresses. For uniaxial stress in a linearly elastic material \(w^* = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2/(2E)\). By theorem 5.35 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\). The computation of lemma 5.76 with the stress instead of the strain gives

\[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2}{2E}\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{1}{2E} \Big( \frac{N^2}{A^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A - \frac{2NM}{AI} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + \frac{M^2}{I^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A \Big) = \frac12 \Big( \frac{N^2}{EA} + \frac{M^2}{EI} \Big) ,\]

because \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The functions \(N\) and \(M\) depend only on \(x\), so we get the following definition.

Definition 5.79 Complementary energy of a beam (komplementarna energija nosilca)

Let a beam be as in definition 5.75 made of a linearly elastic material, and let \(N\) and \(M\) be continuous (or continuous on each of finitely many pieces) functions on \([0, L]\) (the axial force and the moment). The complementary energy of a beam (komplementarna energija nosilca) in the Euler-Bernoulli model is the number

\[U^*(N, M) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( \frac{N^2}{EA} + \frac{M^2}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.70}\]

Source: EP (3.2.40)-(3.2.44) (p. 118), with the term \(f_{\mathrm{s}} V^2/(GA)\) from equation (3.2.44), which we add in lemma 5.80.

For the true \(N\) and \(M\) we have \(U^* = U\) in value, because the material is linearly elastic (\(w^* = w\) at every point, definitions 3.44 and 3.49). The difference is that \(U\) is a function of displacements, and \(U^*\) of forces.

What about shear stresses? By theorem 5.39 in the cross section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V\,S(y)/(I\,b(y))\), where \(S(y)\) is the first moment of the part of the cross section above the height \(y\) and \(b(y)\) is the width of the cross section. Shear stresses exist, although the model did not include them in the energy, and they also store energy. How much?

Lemma 5.80 Shear factor of a cross section (strižni faktor prereza)

Let a cross section of area \(A\) have the second moment of area \(I\) and the width \(b(y)\), and let the shear stress be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V\,S/(I\,b)\) by theorem 5.39 in a linearly elastic material with shear modulus \(G\), with the other shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) equal to zero. Then the complementary energy of the shear stresses per unit length is

\[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2}{2G}\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac12\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{s}}\,V^2}{G\,A} , \qquad f_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{A}{I^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} \Big( \frac{S}{b} \Big)^2 \mathrm{d}A . \tag{5.71}\]

For a rectangular cross section of width \(b\) and height \(h\), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 6/5\).

Proof

We must show that the complementary energy density of the shear stress gives the first part, and then compute the integral for a rectangle.

  1. The shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) are equal by the symmetry of stress, and the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G)\) (definition 3.9). The complementary energy density in a linear material is half the sum of the products \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) over all indices (definition 3.49), and the terms \(xy\) and \(yx\) together give \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\).
  2. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V S/(I b)\). The shear force \(V\) is constant in the cross section and we take it out: \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\,\mathrm{d}A = V^2/(2G I^2) \int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  3. We multiply and divide by \(A\) and write \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = (A/I^2)\int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). We get (5.71).
  4. The rectangle, \(A = b h\), \(I = b h^3/12\), \(\mathrm{d}A = b\,\mathrm{d}y\). The first moment of the part above the height \(y\) is \(S(y) = \int_{\mathrm{y}}^{h/2} b\,y'\,\mathrm{d}y' = \tfrac b2 (h^2/4 - y^2)\), so \(S/b = \tfrac12 (h^2/4 - y^2)\).
  5. We expand the square: \((h^2/4 - y^2)^2 = h^4/16 - h^2 y^2/2 + y^4\). The integral over \(y\) from \(-h/2\) to \(h/2\) is \(h^5/16 - h^5/24 + h^5/80 = h^5/30\). Therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = b \cdot \tfrac14 \cdot h^5/30 = b h^5/120\).
  6. \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = \dfrac{b h \cdot 144}{b^2 h^6} \cdot \dfrac{b h^5}{120} = \dfrac{144}{120} = \dfrac65\).

Source: EP (3.2.43) and the example for the rectangle (p. 118, equation p118-b2), checked with SymPy.

For our cantilever the shear complementary energy is \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac65 \cdot F^2 L/(GA) = 2{,}487 \cdot 10^{-3}\) J, which is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending energy \(1{,}993\) J. The shear term is thus small, in agreement with the shear stresses, which are about one hundredth of the bending stresses for this cantilever (section 5.6): at \(L/h = 25\) the shear energy is only about a thousandth of the bending energy. The ratio of the energies is proportional to \((h/L)^2\), so for shorter beams it grows. How shear enters the beam equations is shown by the section on the beam with shear strain.

This also tells us how much the omitted shear term would add to the deflection in the unit dummy load method. For a single force, by Clapeyron's theorem (theorem 4.86) the deflection under the force equals twice the energy divided by the force, \(\Delta = 2U/F\), so the ratio of the shear part and the bending part of the deflection equals the ratio of the energies. The shear part of the deflection of the free end of the cantilever is thus \(2 \cdot 2{,}487 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{J}/500\ \text{N} = f_{\mathrm{s}} F L/(GA) = 0{,}00995\) mm (at \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa), which is \(0{,}125\) % of \(7{,}972\) mm. The term \(\int f_{\mathrm{s}}\,\hat V\,V/(GA)\,\mathrm{d}x\) with the imagined shear force \(\hat V\), divided by \(1\) N, would give the same number with a negative sign, because \(\hat V = +1\) and \(V = -500\) N, and the end goes down. The estimate uses the shear stresses of theorem 5.39, which hold only approximately in the model, so it shows the order of magnitude: for a slender beam the omission is negligible.

Now we carry Castigliano's second theorem (theorem 4.102) over to the beam. The idea is the same: the energy written with a force has, with respect to this force, a derivative that is a displacement. The force can be a true one, or an imagined one that we add to get the displacement at a point where there is no force.

Theorem 5.81 Castigliano's second theorem for a beam (drugi Castiglianov izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in theorem 5.78. In addition to the given loads let a force \(F_1\) in the direction \(y\) (or a couple \(F_1\)) act at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\). Let the axial force and the moment be written as the functions \(N(x, F_1) = N_0(x) + F_1\,\hat n(x)\) and \(M(x, F_1) = M_0(x) + F_1\,\hat m(x)\), where \(N_0\), \(M_0\), \(\hat n\), \(\hat m\) are functions of \(x\) only, each continuous on every one of finitely many pieces, and for every value of \(F_1\) let them be in equilibrium with the load and the reactions of the supports, which do not yield under the true load. At the actual value of \(F_1\) let \(N\) and \(M\) be the true ones. Let

\[U^*(F_1) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( \frac{N(x, F_1)^2}{EA} + \frac{M(x, F_1)^2}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

Then

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}U^*}{\mathrm{d}F_1} = v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) \qquad \text{(or } v'(x_{\mathrm{P}}) \text{ for a couple)} . \tag{5.72}\]

Let moreover the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) in the actual beam be supported so that \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\), let \(F_1\) mean the reaction of this support, and let the beam without this support still be supported so that rigid motion is excluded (the remaining supports exclude every deflection of the form \(a + b\,x\) except \(a = b = 0\)). Then \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the energy with respect to \(F_1\) is the right-hand side of equation (5.69) for suitably chosen functions \(n\) and \(m\), and that we get these as the derivatives of the forces with respect to \(F_1\), that is \(\hat n\) and \(\hat m\). For the reaction we show in addition that the deflection at the supported point is zero also without the support.

  1. By assumption \(N = N_0 + F_1\,\hat n\) and \(M = M_0 + F_1\,\hat m\), so \(\partial N/\partial F_1 = \hat n\) and \(\partial M/\partial F_1 = \hat m\) (rules of differentiation, lemma 1.81). These functions do not depend on \(F_1\).
  2. The equilibrium conditions of a piece (\(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\), \(M' = -V\)), the jumps at points and the conditions at the ends are linear in \(N\), \(V\), \(M\), in the given loads and in the force \(F_1\), and they hold for every value of \(F_1\) (the shear force is \(V = -M'\), so it too has the form \(V_0 + F_1\,\hat V\) with \(\hat V = -\hat m'\)). We subtract the conditions at \(F_1 + h\) and at \(F_1\) (\(h \ne 0\)) and divide by \(h\). The given loads cancel, and the force \(F_1\) gives \(h/h = 1\) (in units of newton). The functions \(\hat n\), \(\hat V\), \(\hat m\) are therefore the internal forces of the imagined system of theorem 5.78: a force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports, which are the differences of the reactions divided by \(h\) and do no work, because under the true load the supports do not yield.
  3. By theorem 5.78 therefore \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = \int_0^L \big( \hat n\,N/(EA) + \hat m\,M/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(N\) and \(M\) are the true ones.
  4. In \(U^*\) we insert \(N\) and \(M\) from step 1 and expand the squares: \(N^2 = N_0^2 + 2F_1\,N_0\,\hat n + F_1^2\,\hat n^2\), and the same for \(M^2\). By the linearity of the integral \(U^*(F_1) = a + b\,F_1 + c\,F_1^2\) with \(a = \tfrac12 \int \big( N_0^2/(EA) + M_0^2/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(b = \int \big( N_0\,\hat n/(EA) + M_0\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(c = \tfrac12 \int \big( \hat n^2/(EA) + \hat m^2/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\). These are numbers, because the integrated functions are continuous on each of finitely many pieces of \([0, L]\).
  5. The derivative of a quadratic polynomial is \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = b + 2c\,F_1\), and by the linearity of the integral this is \(\int \big( (N_0 + F_1\hat n)\,\hat n/(EA) + (M_0 + F_1\hat m)\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int \big( N\,\hat n/(EA) + M\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  6. At the actual value of \(F_1\) the \(N\), \(M\) are the true ones, so the right-hand side of step 5 equals the right-hand side of step 3. This proves (5.72). For a couple \(F_1\) the computation is the same with an imagined couple \(1\) N m.
  7. Now let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be supported with the reaction \(F_1\) and \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\). The beam without this support, which has in addition to the given loads a force \(F_1\) equal to the reaction at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), has the same conditions of equilibrium as the actual beam (the support is replaced in it by a force of the same value), so it has the same \(N\), \(V\), \(M\). By theorem 5.35 \(v'' = M/(EI)\) in both beams, so the difference of the two deflections has second derivative zero and is a straight line \(a + b\,x\). The remaining supports, which exclude rigid motion, set it to zero, so the deflections are equal and in the beam without the support \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\) as well. By step 6, applied to this beam, \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\).

Source: EP (4.5.13)-(4.5.16) (pp. 206-207), examples 4.4.3 (pp. 201-202) and 4.5.2 (p. 208). The proof in steps is not from the sources: it is the proof of theorem 4.102 with integrals instead of sums.

Let us see how this solves the propped cantilever (figure 5.70). A cantilever of length \(L = 2\) m is clamped at \(x = 0\), at \(x = L\) there is a roller support (definition 5.5) that prevents the deflection, and the beam carries the uniform load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. There are more reactions than equations of equilibrium: we take the reaction of the roller support \(R\) (a force upward) as the unknown, which we call the redundant unknown (odvečna neznanka). The force method (metoda sil) is then a sequence of steps.

  1. We remove the support at \(x = L\) and replace it by a force \(R\) upward. We get a cantilever that is statically determinate.
  2. The moment of this cantilever is the sum of the contribution of the load and the contribution of the force \(R\). The load \(q_0\) on the length \(L - x\) has the force \(q_0 (L - x)\) downward acting at the midpoint, so by the same reasoning as for the cantilever with a force at the end its contribution is \(-q_0 (L - x)^2/2\). The force \(R\) upward at the free end gives \(R\,(L - x)\), like the imagined unit force in example 1 above. Hence \(M(x, R) = R\,(L - x) - \tfrac12 q_0 (L - x)^2\). It is linear in \(R\), as the theorem requires.
  3. The complementary energy is \(U^*(R) = \tfrac{1}{2EI} \int_0^L M^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), where there is no axial force.
  4. The roller support does not yield, so by theorem 5.81 \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}R = 0\), hence
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}U^*}{\mathrm{d}R} = \frac1{EI} \int_0^L M\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L^3}{EI} \Big( \frac R3 - \frac{q_0 L}{8} \Big) = 0 ,\]
    where we used \(\partial M/\partial R = L - x\).
  5. The solution is \(R = 3 q_0 L/8 = 750\) N. This is the reaction from section 5.9.4.
  6. The moment of the clamp is \(M(0, R) = R L - \tfrac12 q_0 L^2 = 1500 - 2000 = -500\) N m, and the vertical reaction of the clamp is \(q_0 L - R = 1250\) N, both as in section 5.9.4.

A test without the theorem: the cantilever with the load \(q_0\) has its free end lower by \(q_0 L^4/(8EI) = 95{,}66\) mm, and the force \(R = 750\) N lifts it by \(R L^3/(3EI) = 95{,}66\) mm. Together the deflection is zero, as it must be, because the roller support does not yield.

The complementary energy has a meaning here. As a function of the unknown \(R\) it is a parabola: \(U^*(0) = 38{,}27\) J, \(U^*(750) = 2{,}392\) J, \(U^*(1500) = 38{,}27\) J. The true value \(R = 750\) N is the minimum of this parabola, as the principle of minimum complementary energy claims (theorem 4.100): among all moments that are in equilibrium with the load, the true one is the smallest in complementary energy. The theorem does not cover the shear term. If we added the term \(\tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{s}} V^2/(GA)\) of lemma 5.80 to \(U^*\) nevertheless and looked for the minimum over \(R\), we would get \(R = 750{,}08\) N, that is \(0{,}010\) % more. This is an estimate of the size, not a theorem.

5.12.5 Reciprocity for beams (vzajemnost pri nosilcih)

Chapter 4 showed that the work of load 1 on the displacements of load 2 equals the work of load 2 on the displacements of load 1, and promised an example on a beam. Here we prove it for a beam directly from theorem 5.77, and not from theorem 4.137, because that one is written for a body, and a beam is a model. The work of load \(k\) on the displacements of load \(l\) we denote \(W_{\mathrm{kl}}\):

\[W_{\mathrm{kl}} = \int_0^L q^{(k)}\,v^{(l)}\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \Big( F^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\,v^{(l)}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\,{v^{(l)}}'(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \Big) ,\]

where \(q^{(k)}\) is the distributed transverse load, \(F^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the point forces and \(M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\) the couples of load \(k\) at the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), among which are the ends where point loads act.

Theorem 5.82 Betti's theorem for a beam (Bettijev izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be made of a linearly elastic material as in theorem 5.78, with supports that do not yield under either load. Let loads \(1\) and \(2\) be transverse (a distributed load and point forces, without couples at interior points, couples at the ends are allowed, and without axial loads), with the true deflections \(v^{(1)}\) and \(v^{(2)}\), which are twice continuously differentiable. Then

\[W_{12} = W_{21} = \int_0^L \frac{M^{(1)}\,M^{(2)}}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{5.73}\]

where \(M^{(1)}\) and \(M^{(2)}\) are the true moments of the two loads.

Proof

We must show that \(W_{12}\) equals the integral of the product of the moments by theorem 5.77, and that this is symmetric.

  1. As the imagined system we take load \(1\) with its reactions and the internal forces \(\delta V = V^{(1)}\), \(\delta M = M^{(1)}\), which are in equilibrium with it. As the displacement we take the true displacement of load \(2\), \(v = v^{(2)}\) (with \(u = 0\), because there are no axial loads).
  2. By theorem 5.77 the integral \(\int_0^L M^{(1)}\,{v^{(2)}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\) equals the work of load \(1\) on the displacements \(v^{(2)}\) and the work of the reactions. The work of the reactions is zero, because the supports do not yield (as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 5.78). Therefore this integral equals \(W_{12}\).
  3. In load \(2\) \({v^{(2)}}'' = M^{(2)}/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35). Hence \(W_{12} = \int_0^L M^{(1)} M^{(2)}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. The same computation with the loads exchanged gives \(W_{21} = \int_0^L M^{(2)} M^{(1)}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\). The product is commutative, so \(W_{12} = W_{21}\).

Source: EP examples 4.6.4 and 4.6.5 (pp. 228-229) use the same statement for a cantilever. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

Let us see what this means on the cantilever (figure 5.71). Let load 1 be the force \(F = 500\) N downward at the free end, and load 2 the self weight, \(q = -\rho g A = -61{,}21\) N/m. The deflection of the free end due to the weight we get by theorem 5.82, without solving the deflection equation, from the field of the force. We know this field, \(v^{(1)}(x) = -F\,x^2 (3L - x)/(6EI)\). The work of the weight on this field is

\[W_{21} = \int_0^L q\,v^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g A\,F}{6EI} \int_0^L x^2\,(3L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g A\,F L^4}{8EI} .\]

The work of the force on the displacement of the free end due to the weight is \(W_{12} = (-F)\,v^{(2)}(L)\). From \(W_{12} = W_{21}\) follows \(v^{(2)}(L) = -\rho g A\,L^4/(8EI) = -0{,}366\) mm and \(W_{12} = W_{21} = 0{,}1830\) J (SymPy). This is the deflection from section 5.9.2, obtained in a different way. The advantage shows when the load is harder to solve: if the weight acts only on the outer half of the beam, from \(x = L/2\) to \(L\), the deflection of the free end is \(-(1/F)\int_{\mathrm{L}/2}^L \rho g A\,F\,x^2 (3L - x)/(6EI)\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}3126\) mm, and there is no new equation to solve.

A special case of the theorem is two point forces of 1 N each. The displacement of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) due to a force \(1\) N at the point \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) is by theorem 5.78 \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^L m_{\mathrm{i}}\,m_{\mathrm{j}}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the moment of the imagined system with a force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (here the true moment of the load is \(M_{\mathrm{j}} = m_{\mathrm{j}} \cdot 1\) N). These numbers are the flexibility coefficients (podajnostni koeficienti) of the beam, and the displacement at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is measured in the direction of the force \(1\) N, as for the flexibility matrix of definition 4.138: here the weights of the point and the small faces are superfluous, because we treat the beam as a line.

Corollary 5.83 Maxwell's theorem for a beam (Maxwellov izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in theorem 5.82 and \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{m}}\) points on it. Let \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) due to a force \(1\) N upward at the point \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), divided by \(1\) N. Then

\[D_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^L \frac{m_{\mathrm{i}}\,m_{\mathrm{j}}}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = D_{\mathrm{ji}} ,\]

so the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) is symmetric.

Proof

We must show that this is theorem 5.82 with two forces of \(1\) N.

  1. Let load 1 be a force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and load 2 a force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\). The work of load 1 on the displacements of load 2 is \(1\ \text{N} \cdot v^{(2)}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\ \text{N}\cdot D_{\mathrm{ji}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\), and the work of load 2 on the displacements of load 1 is \(1\ \text{N}\cdot D_{\mathrm{ij}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\).
  2. By theorem 5.82 they are equal. Therefore \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = D_{\mathrm{ji}}\), and the value is \(\int_0^L m_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{j}}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\) by equation (5.73), because \(M^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{j}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\) and \(M^{(2)} = m_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\).

Source: EP examples 4.6.6 and 4.6.7 (pp. 230-231), BARBER (1.18) (PDF p. 27) lists the influence coefficient matrix of a linear system as an example of a second-order tensor and does not prove its symmetry there. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

Let us test it on a simply supported beam of length \(L = 2\) m with the steel cross section (figure 5.72). Let the points be \(x_1 = L/4 = 0{,}5\) m and \(x_2 = L/2 = 1\) m. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) gives by theorem 5.78 with SymPy

\[\mat D = \begin{pmatrix} 4{,}484 & 5{,}481 \\ 5{,}481 & 7{,}972 \end{pmatrix} \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m/N} .\]

An independent test: we solve the equation \(EI\,v'''' = 0\) piece by piece with each of the two forces separately (eight constants, four boundary conditions and four matching conditions at the point of the force, section 5.8.4), and get \(D_{12} = D_{21} = 5{,}481 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N. A force \(1\) N at \(x_2\) gives \(D_{22} = L^3/(48EI) = 7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N, the same value as for the central force and for the cantilever.

The matrix gives the displacements for any two forces. For \(F_1 = 400\) N at \(x_1\) and \(F_2 = 1000\) N at \(x_2\) (both upward) the deflection is \(v(x_1) = D_{11}F_1 + D_{12}F_2 = 7{,}274\) mm and \(v(x_2) = D_{21}F_1 + D_{22}F_2 = 10{,}164\) mm. The complementary energy is \(U^* = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{ij}} D_{\mathrm{ij}} F_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{j}} = 6{,}537\) J, and the same number comes from the integral \(\tfrac{1}{2EI}\int (F_1 m_1 + F_2 m_2)^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (SymPy). Castigliano's second theorem shows the same symmetry once more, and does not need the formula for \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\). By superposition (lemma 4.134) the deflection is \(v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} D_{\mathrm{ij}} F_{\mathrm{j}}\), because the deflection is linear in the forces. By theorem 5.81 \(\partial U^*/\partial F_1 = D_{11}F_1 + D_{12}F_2 = v(x_1)\) and \(\partial U^*/\partial F_2 = D_{21}F_1 + D_{22}F_2 = v(x_2)\). The mixed derivatives of the same function are \(\partial^2 U^*/\partial F_2 \partial F_1 = D_{12}\) and \(\partial^2 U^*/\partial F_1 \partial F_2 = D_{21}\), and these are equal, because \(U^*\) is a quadratic polynomial, for which the order of differentiation does not matter (theorem 1.125).
