Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.12 Energy of a beam and virtual forces (energija nosilca in virtualne sile)

Chapter 4 found displacements from forces without geometry. We multiplied an imagined force in equilibrium by the true displacement and got an equality of work. For bars and trusses this gave the displacement of a joint, and the energy written with forces gave the forces in a truss that equilibrium alone does not determine. A beam has three quantities where a bar has one: the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\). Do the same three steps work here too? How do we get the deflection at any point of a beam without solving the deflection equation? And how do we find the forces in a statically indeterminate beam, such as the propped cantilever of section 5.9.4, where equilibrium is not enough?

The answer has four steps. First we compute the energy that a beam stores. Then we carry the principle of virtual forces over to the beam and derive from it the unit dummy load method. With it we get displacements and rotations, and with Castigliano's second theorem also the forces in a statically indeterminate beam. At the end we show that the displacements caused by forces at two points are reciprocal. Throughout the section we use the steel rectangular cross section of section 5.1.3, 20 mm wide and 40 mm high, with bending stiffness \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), and three beams: the cantilever of length 1 m with a force of 500 N downward at the free end and the simply supported beam of length 2 m with a uniform load of 1 kN/m from section 5.1.3, and the propped cantilever of length 2 m with the same load from section 5.9.4. The supports have the names of definition 5.5: the clamp, the fixed pin support and the roller support.

5.12.1 Strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca)

In a bar the strain was the same at all points of the cross section, so the energy density was the same too. In a beam this is not so. By theorem 5.15 the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) depends on the height \(y\) in the cross section: the fibres at the top and at the bottom stretch or shorten by different amounts, and the fibres on the centroidal axis only by \(u'\) (figure 5.164 below). We therefore get the energy of the whole beam by adding the energy density first over the cross section and then along the axis. We compute with the model of definition 5.14 and with the assumption of the model that every fibre is in uniaxial stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (section 5.5.1). The strain energy density is then \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\) (definition 3.59, as for the bar in section 4.1).

Definition 5.76 Strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca)

Let a beam of length \(L\) be as in definition 5.14, with constant axial stiffness \(EA\) and constant bending stiffness \(EI\) (a prismatic beam, for a slowly varying section the same formula holds with \(EA(x)\) and \(EI(x)\), EP (2.5.18), p. 75, but we do not need it here), and let \(u\) and \(v\) be the functions of the axial and the transverse displacement of the axis, \(u\) continuously differentiable and \(v\) twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \([0, L]\). The strain energy of a beam (deformacijska energija nosilca) is the number

\[U(u, v) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( EA\,(u')^2 + EI\,(v'')^2 \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.72}\]

Source: EP (3.2.37)-(3.2.39) (p. 117), ONATE2 (1.5) and the following lines (PDF pp. 34-36).

The function \(U\) takes two functions as input and gives one number with the unit joule as output. Does this number really mean the energy of the body of section 3.6? The answer is in the next lemma.

We follow the proof in figure 5.164. At the top left is the beam from the side with the \(x\) axis, and in it the cross section at \(x\). At the top right we look at this cross section of area \(A\) along the axis of the beam: the \(y\) axis points upward and the centroid lies on the axis of the beam. The strips at the heights \(y\) and \(-y\) are fibres above the centroid and below it. At the bottom are the three terms of the square of the strain, each drawn over the height of the cross section: the constant one, the mixed one and the quadratic one. The mixed term is drawn for \(u'\,v'' < 0\): above the centroid it is positive, below it equally large and negative. Under each graph is its integral over the cross section.

Lemma 5.77 Energy of a beam from the energy density (energija nosilca iz gostote energije)

Let a beam be made of a homogeneous material with Young's modulus \(E\) and cross section \(A\) with its centroid on the axis, in the displacement field of theorem 5.15 with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v''\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and let \(u\) and \(v\) be as in definition 5.76. Then the strain energy of the body \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V\) with \(w = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\) equals the number (5.72) with \(EA = E A\) and \(EI = E I\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the density over the cross section gives two terms in which there is no mixed product \(u'\,v''\).

  1. We expand the square of the strain: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 = (u')^2 - 2y\,u'\,v'' + y^2\,(v'')^2\). These are the first, second and third graph in the figure.
  2. The body is made of cross sections and the axis is \(x\), so by the theorem on the order of integration (theorem 4.9) \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L \big( \int_{\mathrm{A}} w\,\mathrm{d}A \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. The functions \(u'\) and \(v''\) depend only on \(x\), so in the inner integral we take them out (linearity of the integral): \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\,\mathrm{d}A = (u')^2 \int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A - 2\,u'\,v'' \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + (v'')^2 \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  4. The axis passes through the centroids of the cross sections, so the first moment of area is \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) (lemma 5.22), and the mixed term drops out. For our rectangle we see this directly: the strip above the centroid adds as much as the strip below it takes away. For an unsymmetric cross section the strips do not cancel in pairs, but by the lemma the sum is still zero. The remaining integrals are the area \(A\) and the second moment of area \(I = \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) (definition 5.26).
  5. When we multiply by \(\tfrac12 E\) and integrate over \(x\), we get (5.72).

Source: EP (3.2.37)-(3.2.39) (p. 117). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The lemma also answers the question why there is no mixed term in the energy: the centroidal axis is chosen so that stretching of the axis and bending do not mix. If we put the axis on the bottom edge of our \(20 \times 40\) mm cross section, the first moment of area would be \(S = 800\ \text{mm}^2 \cdot 20\ \text{mm} = 16\,000\ \text{mm}^3\) instead of zero, the two areas of the mixed term would not cancel, and the energy per unit length would keep a term \(-E\,S\,u'\,v''\), which couples stretching with bending. Let us mention what the energy does not capture. In the model \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so shear stresses do not appear in this energy. How much is missing we estimate later in this section, when we can compute the shear energy.

Let us test it on the cantilever. A transverse load causes no axial force, so \(u' = 0\) and only the bending term remains. By theorem 5.35 \(v'' = M/(EI)\) and by equilibrium \(M = -F\,(L - x)\), so the energy per unit length is

\[\frac12\,EI\,(v'')^2 = \frac{M^2}{2EI} = \frac{F^2\,(L - x)^2}{2EI} .\]

At the clamp of the cantilever this is \(500^2 \cdot 1^2/(2 \cdot 20\,906{,}7) = 5{,}979\) J/m, which is the highest point of the energy curve, at the free end zero (figure 5.165). The force \(F\) pulls the free end, the axis \(x\) runs from the clamp, and the axes of the graph show \(x\) and the energy per unit length. The energy is thus very unevenly distributed. The reason is the square: the moment falls linearly towards the free end, and the energy with its square. In the first half of the length, next to the clamp, \(7/8\) of all the energy is stored, \(1{,}744\) J. The area under the curve is the total energy

\[U = \int_0^L \frac{F^2\,(L - x)^2}{2EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{F^2 L^3}{6EI} = \frac{500^2 \cdot 1}{6 \cdot 20\,906{,}7}\ \text{J} = 1{,}993\ \text{J} .\]

This agrees with Clapeyron's theorem (theorem 4.86): \(\tfrac12\,F\,|v(L)| = \tfrac12 \cdot 500\ \text{N} \cdot 7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1{,}993\) J, where \(v(L) = -7{,}972\) mm is the deflection of the free end from section 5.9.2. In a bar with constant axial force the energy is spread evenly along the length, in a beam it is gathered where the moment is largest.

5.12.2 Principle of virtual forces for a beam (načelo virtualnih sil za nosilec)

In section 5.10.2 we wrote the principle of virtual displacements for a beam: true internal forces in equilibrium and an imagined displacement. Chapter 4 showed that the roles can be exchanged. The forces can be imagined if they are in equilibrium, and the displacement is true. Such a displacement does not need equilibrium, only that it is smooth (theorem 4.98). Here we write the same for a beam.

Let the imagined load of the beam consist of a distributed axial load \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and a transverse load \(\delta q\) (force per length, \(\delta q\) positive upward), of point forces \(\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\) and couples \(\delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the interior points \(x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{k}}\), and of forces and couples at the ends (the left and the right). Figure 5.166 shows a single interior point \(x_1\) and only transverse forces, axial forces and couples follow the same pattern. The imagined internal forces \(\delta N\), \(\delta V\), \(\delta M\) have the same meaning as for the true load, that is the forces and the moment with which the part at larger \(x\) acts on the part at smaller \(x\) (section 5.2). The equilibrium conditions of a piece of the beam hold for them, \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta V' = -\delta q\) and \(\delta M' = -\delta V\), and so do the jumps at the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), by lemma 5.12: \(\delta V\) increases by \(-\delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\delta N\) by \(-\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\delta M\) by \(-\delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\). In the middle graph the imagined shear force therefore falls with the slope \(-\delta q\) and at \(x_1\) jumps down by \(\delta F_1\), from the value just before the point to the value just after it. At the left end \(\delta V(0) > 0\), so the force \(-\delta V(0)\) is negative and points downward, at the right end \(\delta V(L) < 0\) and this force points downward too. The two arrows in the figure therefore carry the magnitudes \(\delta V(0)\) and \(-\delta V(L)\). Together they balance the two loads that point upward. Below we draw the true state as well: the undeformed axis grey and dashed and the deflection \(v\) in orange. The deflection is smooth through \(x_1\) too, where it has the value \(v(x_1)\).

Theorem 5.78 Principle of virtual forces for a beam (načelo virtualnih sil za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in definition 5.14 of length \(L\), and let \(u\) be a continuously differentiable and \(v\) a twice continuously differentiable function on an open set that contains \([0, L]\) (the true displacements of the axis, defined on the whole beam). Let \(0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{k}} < L\) and let \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\delta q\) be continuous functions on every interval between consecutive points \(0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{k}}, L\) (at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) they may jump, and the point force there may also be zero). Let \(\delta N\), \(\delta V\), \(\delta M\) be functions on \([0, L]\) that are continuously differentiable on every open interval between consecutive points \(0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{k}}, L\) and extend there, together with their derivatives, to continuously differentiable functions on the closed interval (the values of the extensions at the ends are \(\delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^\pm)\), \(\delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^\pm)\), \(\delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^\pm)\)), \(\delta M\) to a twice continuously differentiable one, and let \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta V' = -\delta q\), \(\delta M' = -\delta V\) hold on these intervals, and at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\)

\[\delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}} , \quad \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta F_{\mathrm{i}} , \quad \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) - \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) = -\delta M_{\mathrm{i}} .\]

Then

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L \big( \delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'' \big)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L \big( \delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u + \delta q\,v \big)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^k \big( \delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\,v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\,v'(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \big) \\ &\quad + \Big[ \delta N\,u + \delta V\,v + \delta M\,v' \Big]_0^L . \end{aligned} \tag{5.73}\]

The right-hand side is the work of the imagined loads on the true displacements, where the last term consists of the imagined forces at the ends: at \(x = L\) these are \(\delta N(L)\), \(\delta V(L)\), \(\delta M(L)\), and at \(x = 0\) they are \(-\delta N(0)\), \(-\delta V(0)\), \(-\delta M(0)\).

Proof

We must show that integration by parts on every interval gives the formula, and that the boundary terms at the interior points combine into the work of the point forces and couples. The displacements \(u\) and \(v\) are not restricted by equilibrium, so the proof does not need the law of the material.

  1. Let \([a, b]\) be one of the intervals, in the figure the left or the right band. Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta N\,u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta N\,u \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta N'\,u\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta N\,u \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\), where we inserted \(\delta N' = -\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The same for \(\delta M\,v''\): \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta M\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta M'\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta M' = -\delta V\).
  3. Once more by parts: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta V\,v \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta V'\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[ \delta V\,v \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \delta q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta V' = -\delta q\).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 together give \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (\delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (\delta q_{\mathrm{x}}\,u + \delta q\,v)\,\mathrm{d}x + \big[ \delta N\,u + \delta V\,v + \delta M\,v' \big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b\).
  5. We add over all intervals. The integrals combine into integrals over \([0, L]\). The boundary terms at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the term of the upper limit of the left interval, taken at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-\), and the opposite term of the lower limit of the right interval, taken at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+\). The functions \(u\), \(v\) and \(v'\) are continuous at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) (the deflection has a single value there), so the sum of these terms is \(\big( \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta N(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \big( \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta V(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \big( \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^-) - \delta M(x_{\mathrm{i}}^+) \big) v'(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and by the jump conditions this is \(\delta F_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{i}}\,u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta F_{\mathrm{i}}\,v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + \delta M_{\mathrm{i}}\,v'(x_{\mathrm{i}})\): the jump of the shear force, multiplied by the displacement at the same point, is the work of the point force.
  6. What remains are the boundary terms at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), which is the last term of equation (5.73).

Source: EP (4.4.5) and (4.4.11) (pp. 197-198), EP example 4.4.2 (p. 200). The proof in steps is not from the sources: it is the same computation as the proof of theorem 5.63, with the roles of the fields exchanged.

The proof showed that the equality is not a demand on the equilibrium of the true beam, but a purely mathematical consequence of the equilibrium of the imagined system and the smoothness of the displacements. The true beam enters only when we insert the law of the material, \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35). Smoothness enters in step 5, where we combined the terms from the two sides of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) because \(u\), \(v\) and \(v'\) have a single value there. For a beam in one piece this holds. It does not hold for a beam of two pieces joined at \(x_1\) by a hinge, a joint that lets one piece turn relative to the other: there the axis has a kink compared with the undeformed axis and \(v'\) has one value from the left and another from the right. Then the terms with \(\delta M\) do not combine, and the right-hand side of equation (5.73) keeps an additional term \(-\delta M(x_1)\,\big(v'(x_1^+) - v'(x_1^-)\big)\), in which the difference in brackets is the kink of the slope. For a kink of the slope by \(0{,}01\) and \(\delta M(x_1) = 100\) N m this is \(-1\) N m, that is \(-1\) J.

5.12.3 Unit dummy load method for beams (metoda enotske sile za nosilce)

How do we use the theorem for the displacement we look for? As for the truss (theorem 4.96): we choose the imagined load so that on the right-hand side of equation (5.73) only the displacement we look for remains. If we look for the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), the imagined load is one force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at this point, together with the reactions of the supports that balance it. If we look for a rotation, the imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m. When the supports do not yield under the true load, the reactions of the imagined system do no work.

What does the integral on the left-hand side of equation (5.73) mean? In thought we cut the beam into short pieces of length \(\Delta s\) (figure 5.167). Under the true load every piece deforms in two ways. It stretches by \(\Delta u\), which is about \(N\,\Delta s/(EA)\), because \(u' = N/(EA)\). And it bends, so that its right face turns relative to the left one by the angle \(\Delta\varphi\), which is about \(M\,\Delta s/(EI)\), because \(v'' = M/(EI)\). On the faces of the same piece we imagine the two imagined internal forces, the axial force \(\delta N\) and the moment \(\delta M\). A force does work on a displacement in its direction, a couple on a rotation, so on the true deformation of the piece the imagined forces do the work of about

\[\delta N\,\Delta u + \delta M\,\Delta\varphi = \Big( \frac{\delta N\,N}{EA} + \frac{\delta M\,M}{EI} \Big)\,\Delta s .\]

The sum over all pieces is a Riemann sum of the integral. The unit dummy load method thus adds up the deformations of all pieces, each multiplied by the imagined internal force that this piece carries. A piece that does not feel the imagined force (\(n = m = 0\)) adds nothing to the displacement we look for, however much it deforms. The third way of deforming, the slide of the face under the shear force, comes in the next section.

The first panel shows the stretch, the second the bending. The theorem says the same precisely.

Theorem 5.79 Unit dummy load method for a beam (metoda enotske sile za nosilec)

Let a beam (in figure 5.169 it is the cantilever) be made of a linearly elastic material as in theorem 5.35, with the true internal forces \(N\) and \(M\) for the given load and the true displacements \(u\), \(v\), which satisfy the conditions of theorem 5.78 (so there are no point couples and no axial point forces at interior points, where the moment, that is \(v''\), or the axial force, that is \(u'\), would jump). Let the supports be such that at every end every displacement or rotation they prevent equals zero under the true load. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}} \in [0, L]\) be a point.

  1. Let \(n(x)\) and \(m(x)\) be the axial force and the moment, divided by \(1\) N, in the imagined system that consists of a force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports (with the same signs as for the true forces). Then the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is
    \[v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = \int_0^L \Big( \frac{n\,N}{EA} + \frac{m\,M}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.74}\]
  2. If the imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) with the reactions, the formula is the same for the rotation \(v'(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), where \(n\) and \(m\) are the axial force and the moment of this system, divided by \(1\) N m.

The same holds for the axial displacement \(u(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) with an imagined force \(1\) N in the direction \(x\) (figure 5.168).

Proof

We must show that equation (5.73) with the chosen imagined load gives (5.74). We prove item 1, item 2 is the same with a couple.

  1. Let \(\delta F = 1\) N, and the imagined load the force \(\delta F\) in the direction \(y\) at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports (in figure 5.169 this is the force \(1\) at the free end of the cantilever). The imagined internal forces are \(\delta N = n\cdot 1\) N and \(\delta M = m \cdot 1\) N, with no distributed imagined load, \(\delta q_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta q = 0\).
  2. By theorem 5.78 the right-hand side of equation (5.73) is the sum of the work of the force at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), which is \(\delta F\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) (if \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is an interior point this is a term of the sum, if it is at an end, it is part of the boundary term), and the work of the reactions at the ends.
  3. A reaction of a support acts in the direction of the displacement or rotation that the support prevents, and by assumption this is zero under the true load (at the clamp of the cantilever the bent axis does not move and does not turn). The work of the reactions is therefore zero. At a free end, where there is no support, there is no imagined force (unless \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is there).
  4. Hence \(\int_0^L (\delta N\,u' + \delta M\,v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \delta F\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\): the work of one single force on the deflection of its point of application.
  5. In the true beam \(u' = N/(EA)\) and \(v'' = M/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35). We insert, divide by \(\delta F = 1\) N and get (5.74): the integral of the product of the imagined moment \(m\) and the true moment \(M\), divided by \(EI\), and the same for the axial forces.

Source: EP (4.4.12) (p. 198), examples 4.4.2 to 4.4.4 (pp. 200-203). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

For the axial displacement the picture is simpler than for the deflection. The bar is clamped on the left, and at the free end the imagined force \(1\) N acts in the direction of the axis \(x\). The end of the bar therefore moves along the axis by the sought \(u(x_{\mathrm{P}})\). Below it is the axial force \(n\) of the imagined system, equal to \(1\), divided by \(1\) N, along the whole length. So the formula for \(u(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) contains only the term with the axial force.

The formula contains no term for shear deformation, which would be multiplied by the imagined shear force. We leave it out on purpose. Theorem 5.78 is proved for a model in which \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (theorem 5.15), so in the left-hand side of equation (5.73) there is no shear strain that the imagined shear force could multiply. The term would need a model with shear strain, and this section does not have one. That model is the subject of the section on the beam with shear strain, where we complete the unit load method with a shear term. How much the term would add for our cantilever we estimate in the next section, when we can compute the shear energy, and there we also write the formula with all three terms.

We test the theorem on three examples from section 5.9.2 and the following ones, which we have already solved with the deflection equation. In each the result is known, so we test the new route on known numbers.

  1. The cantilever, the deflection of the free end (figure 5.169). The true moment is \(M = -F\,(L - x)\) for the force \(F = 500\) N downward, \(-500\) N m at the clamp. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at the free end has, by the conditions at a free end (section 5.2), the shear force \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) N and the moment \(M(L) = 0\). Hence, divided by \(1\) N, \(m' = -1\) (because \(M' = -V\)) and \(m(L) = 0\), so \(m(x) = L - x\). The axial force is zero in both. By (5.74)
    \[v(L) = \int_0^L \frac{(L - x)\cdot\big( -F\,(L - x) \big)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{F L^3}{3EI} = -7{,}972\ \text{mm} .\]
    This is the same value as in section 5.9.2, and negative because the axis bends downward and the end goes down by \(7{,}972\) mm. The same computation with a couple \(1\) N m at the free end (\(m = 1\) along the whole length, because \(M(L)\) equals the couple, \(m' = 0\)) gives the rotation \(v'(L) = \int_0^L 1\cdot(-F(L - x))/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x = -F L^2/(2EI) = -0{,}01196\), as in section 5.9.2. This is the slope of the tangent at the free end relative to the original axis.
  2. The simply supported beam, the deflection at mid-span (figure 5.170). The true moment is \(M = \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\) for the load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m, \(L = 2\) m, at mid-span \(q_0L^2/8\). An imagined force \(1\) N upward at mid-span requires reactions of \(\tfrac12\) N downward each, so \(m = -x/2\) for \(x \le L/2\) and \(m = -(L - x)/2\) for \(x \ge L/2\). By the symmetry of the two halves
    \[v\big( \tfrac L2 \big) = 2\int_0^{L/2} \frac{(-x/2)\cdot\tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{5\,q_0 L^4}{384\,EI} = -9{,}965\ \text{mm} .\]
    The axis bends downward and the middle goes down by \(9{,}965\) mm.
  3. The simply supported beam, the rotation over the support (figure 5.171). The imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m at the left end, in the counterclockwise sense (the positive direction of the axis \(z\)). The reactions are \(1/L\) each and balance it. The imagined couple at the left end is \(-m(0)\), so \(m(0) = -1\), and \(m(L) = 0\), so \(m(x) = -(1 - x/L)\). The rotation is
    \[v'(0) = \int_0^L \frac{-(1 - x/L)\cdot\tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{q_0 L^3}{24\,EI} = -0{,}01594 .\]
    The rotation is negative: the tangent to the axis at the left support falls towards the right, by \(0{,}01594\) per unit length (figure 5.170).

We checked all three results with SymPy, and the values \(-7{,}972\) mm, \(-9{,}965\) mm and \(-0{,}01594\) agree with the solutions of the deflection equation. A central force \(1\) kN on the simply supported beam gives by the same route \(-FL^3/(48EI) = -7{,}972\) mm, as for the cantilever, because the halves of the beam are clamped cantilevers of length \(1\) m with \(500\) N at the end.

5.12.4 Complementary energy and statically indeterminate beams (komplementarna energija in statično nedoločeni nosilci)

So far we have computed displacements from internal forces that were already known. For a statically indeterminate beam (definition 5.6) equilibrium of forces does not determine them, because there are more unknown reactions than equations of equilibrium. For the propped cantilever of section 5.9.4 there are three reactions under a transverse load (the force and the moment of the clamp and the force of the roller support), and two equations of equilibrium (the sum of forces and the sum of moments). In section 5.9.4 we solved the problem with the deflection equation and four boundary conditions. We can do the same with energy, as for the truss of chapter 4. For this we need the energy written with forces instead of displacements.

In chapter 4 the complementary energy is \(U^* = \int w^*\,\mathrm{d}V\) with the complementary energy density \(w^*\) (definitions 4.99 and 3.65), which we express with stresses. For uniaxial stress in a linearly elastic material \(w^* = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2/(2E)\). By theorem 5.35 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\), the sum of a uniform and a linear part. The computation of lemma 5.77 with the stress instead of the strain gives

\[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2}{2E}\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{1}{2E} \Big( \frac{N^2}{A^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A - \frac{2NM}{AI} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + \frac{M^2}{I^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A \Big) = \frac12 \Big( \frac{N^2}{EA} + \frac{M^2}{EI} \Big) ,\]

because \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).

Figure 5.172 shows why the terms are independent. The cross section is a rectangle with the axis \(y\) through the centroid. The axial force gives the uniform stress \(N/A\), equal at every height. The moment gives the linear stress \(-M\,y/I\), negative above the axis and positive below it. Their sum is the true stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). When the sum is squared, the mixed term contains \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A\), because the two halves of the linear part above and below the axis cancel. The functions \(N\) and \(M\) depend only on \(x\), so we get the following definition.

Definition 5.80 Complementary energy of a beam (komplementarna energija nosilca)

Let a beam be as in definition 5.76 made of a linearly elastic material, and let \(N\) and \(M\) be continuous (or continuous on each of finitely many pieces) functions on \([0, L]\) (the axial force and the moment). The complementary energy of a beam (komplementarna energija nosilca) in the Euler-Bernoulli model is the number

\[U^*(N, M) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( \frac{N^2}{EA} + \frac{M^2}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.75}\]

Source: EP (3.2.40)-(3.2.44) (p. 118), with the term \(f_{\mathrm{s}} V^2/(GA)\) from equation (3.2.44), which we add in lemma 5.81.

Figure 5.173 shows what the integral counts. The beam axis lies horizontally from \(0\) to \(L\). Below it the squares of the forces are drawn. The area under \(N^2/(2EA)\) is the axial part of the energy, the area under \(M^2/(2EI)\) the bending part. Each piece of length \(\mathrm{d}x\) contributes a narrow strip of both areas, and \(U^*\) is their sum over all pieces.

For the true \(N\) and \(M\) we have \(U^* = U\) in value, because the material is linearly elastic (the two areas are equal, \(w^* = w\) at every point, definitions 3.59 and 3.65). The difference is that \(U\) is a function of displacements, and \(U^*\) of forces.

Figure 5.174 shows why the two energies are equal. The line \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\) is the linearly elastic material, the point is the present state. The area under the line down to the \(\varepsilon\) axis is the energy density \(w = \int \sigma\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\), the area between the line and the \(\sigma\) axis is \(w^* = \int \varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\). Together they make the rectangle \(\sigma\varepsilon\), the line cuts it along the diagonal, so the areas are equal, each \(\sigma\varepsilon/2\).

What about shear stresses? By theorem 5.39 in the cross section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V\,S(y)/(I\,b(y))\). Figure 5.175 shows what \(S\) and \(b\) are. We cut the rectangular cross section with a horizontal cut at the height \(y\). The part of the cross section above the cut has the first moment \(S(y)\) (definition 5.38), the width of the cross section at the cut is \(b(y)\). For the rectangle the quotient \(S/b\) is a parabola over the height. It is zero at the top, because the part cut off is empty, zero at the bottom, because the part cut off is the whole cross section, largest at the axis, and at the height of the cut it has the value that the blue segment shows. The shear stress is this parabola multiplied by \(V/I\). Shear stresses thus exist, although the model did not include them in the energy, and they also store energy. How much?

Figure 5.176 shows what the stress and the strain mean in step 1 of the proof below. The element is a small square in the cross section. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) acts on the vertical edges, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) on the horizontal ones, and by the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41) they are equal. The element turns by the shear \(\gamma = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\), which we split into two equal halves \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = \gamma/2\).

Lemma 5.81 Shear factor of a cross section (strižni faktor prereza)

Let a cross section of area \(A\) have the second moment of area \(I\) and the width \(b(y)\), and let the shear stress be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V\,S/(I\,b)\) (\(S\) of the part cut off, \(b\) at the cut) by theorem 5.39 in a linearly elastic material with shear modulus \(G\), with the other shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) equal to zero. Then the complementary energy of the shear stresses per unit length is

\[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2}{2G}\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac12\,\frac{f_{\mathrm{s}}\,V^2}{G\,A} , \qquad f_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{A}{I^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} \Big( \frac{S}{b} \Big)^2 \mathrm{d}A . \tag{5.76}\]

For a rectangular cross section of width \(b\) and height \(h\), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 6/5\).

Proof

We must show that the complementary energy density of the shear stress gives the first part, and then compute the integral for a rectangle.

  1. The shear stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) are equal by the symmetry of stress, and the strain is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G)\) (definition 3.17). The complementary energy density in a linear material is half the sum of the products \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) over all indices (definition 3.65), and the terms \(xy\) and \(yx\) together give \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\).
  2. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V S/(I b)\). The shear force \(V\) is constant in the cross section and we take it out: \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\,\mathrm{d}A = V^2/(2G I^2) \int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  3. We multiply and divide by \(A\) and write \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = (A/I^2)\int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A\). We get (5.76).
  4. The rectangle, \(A = b h\), \(I = b h^3/12\). By theorem 4.9 the integral over the section is an integral over \(y\) with the factor \(b\). The first moment of the part above the height \(y\) is \(S(y) = \int_{\mathrm{y}}^{h/2} b\,y'\,\mathrm{d}y' = \tfrac b2 (h^2/4 - y^2)\), so \(S/b = \tfrac12 (h^2/4 - y^2)\). This is the parabola in figure 5.175.
  5. We expand the square: \((h^2/4 - y^2)^2 = h^4/16 - h^2 y^2/2 + y^4\). The integral over \(y\) from \(-h/2\) to \(h/2\) is \(h^5/16 - h^5/24 + h^5/80 = h^5/30\). Therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = b \cdot \tfrac14 \cdot h^5/30 = b h^5/120\).
  6. \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = \dfrac{b h \cdot 144}{b^2 h^6} \cdot \dfrac{b h^5}{120} = \dfrac{144}{120} = \dfrac65\).

Source: EP (3.2.43) and the example for the rectangle (p. 118, equation p118-b2), checked with SymPy.

What does the number \(6/5\) say? If the shear stress were uniform over the cross section, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V/A\), the energy per unit length would be \(\tfrac12 V^2/(GA)\) and the factor would be \(1\). The true stress is a parabola, which at the axis is \(1{,}5\) times the mean \(V/A\) (equation (5.30)) and zero at the top and the bottom. Energy grows with the square of the stress, so at the same shear force an uneven distribution stores more energy than a uniform one, for the rectangle one fifth more.

The lemma has two assumptions about the shear stress, both from theorem 5.39. The first: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is uniform across the width along the cut. The second: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\). For a narrow rectangle they are justified, because the side edges are vertical and the width is small against the height. The circular cross section of radius \(R\) on the right of figure 5.175 shows where they fail. The cut is a chord of length \(b(y) = 2\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}\), the part above it is a circular segment. The lemma assumes a vertical stress, the same everywhere along the chord. But there is no load on the outer surface of the beam. If the shear stress at a point on the edge of the cross section had a component perpendicular to the edge, then by the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41) an equal shear stress would act on the outer surface, and there is none there. At the ends of the chord the stress therefore points along the circle and not vertically, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \ne 0\) there.

If we use formula (5.76) for the circle all the same, we get an estimate. The first moment of the circular segment is \(S(y) = \int_{\mathrm{y}}^R 2\sqrt{R^2 - y'^2}\;y'\,\mathrm{d}y' = \tfrac23 (R^2 - y^2)^{3/2}\), so \(S/b = \tfrac13 (R^2 - y^2)\), a parabola again. With the area of the circle \(A = \pi R^2\) and the moment of inertia \(I = \int_{-\mathrm{R}}^{R} y^2\,b(y)\,\mathrm{d}y = \pi R^4/4\),

\[f_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{A}{I^2} \int_{-\mathrm{R}}^{R} \frac{(R^2 - y^2)^2}{9} \cdot 2\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{16}{\pi R^6} \cdot \frac{5\pi R^6}{72} = \frac{10}{9} .\]

We checked all three integrals with SymPy. A computation by the theory of elasticity, which also takes \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and the true distribution along the chord into account, gives for the solid circular cross section the shear coefficient \(6(1 + \nu)/(7 + 6\nu)\), where \(\nu\) is Poisson's ratio (FPGL, the text after equation (5.27), PDF p. 182, after Cowper, 1966). This coefficient stands in the denominator, like \(1/f_{\mathrm{s}}\) (EP, p. 123). Its reciprocal \((7 + 6\nu)/(6(1 + \nu))\) equals \(1{,}128\) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\), which is \(1{,}5\) % more than \(10/9 = 1{,}111\). Note: the formula with \(\nu\) does not follow from the integral in equation (5.76). The integral contains only the shape of the cross section and does not depend on \(\nu\). Formulas with \(\nu\) come from another definition, from a comparison with the solution of the theory of elasticity. The difference has no noticeable effect on the deflection of a slender beam: at a ratio of length to height of \(25\) the shear part is about \(0{,}1\) % of the deflection, and \(1{,}5\) % of that is \(0{,}002\) % of the deflection.

In the lectures the shear factor \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) is written with the letter \(\kappa\), so \(\kappa = f_{\mathrm{s}}\), for the rectangle \(6/5\). The shear force is \(T\) there, the first moment of the part cut off \(S_{\mathrm{ye}}\) and the moment of inertia \(I_{\mathrm{z}}\). The axis \(y\) points downward there, so the bending stress is written as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = M\,y/I_{\mathrm{z}}\), while here, with the axis \(y\) upward, it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I\). The reciprocal \(k = 1/f_{\mathrm{s}}\), for the rectangle \(5/6\), is the number that the section on the beam with shear strain introduces as the shear correction factor. So \(\kappa = f_{\mathrm{s}} = 1/k\). Where two formulas with \(\nu\) stand beside the integral for \(\kappa\), the warning above holds for them.

For our cantilever the shear complementary energy is \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac65 \cdot F^2 L/(GA) = 2{,}487 \cdot 10^{-3}\) J, which is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending energy \(1{,}993\) J. The shear term is thus small, as the shear slip is small compared with the deflection of the cantilever, in agreement with the shear stresses, which are about one hundredth of the bending stresses for this cantilever (section 5.6): at \(L/h = 25\) the shear energy is only about a thousandth of the bending energy. The ratio of the energies is proportional to \((h/L)^2\), so for shorter beams it grows. How shear enters the beam equations is shown by the section on the beam with shear strain.

This also tells us how much the omitted shear term would add to the deflection in the unit dummy load method. For a single force \(F\) both energies, bending and shear, are quadratic functions of the force, \(U_{\mathrm{k}} = c_{\mathrm{k}} F^2\), because \(M\) and \(V\) are proportional to \(F\). By Castigliano's second theorem (theorem 4.103) the corresponding part of the deflection under the force is \(\mathrm{d}U_{\mathrm{k}}/\mathrm{d}F = 2U_{\mathrm{k}}/F\), so the ratio of the shear part and the bending part of the deflection equals the ratio of the energies. This is an estimate, because the two energies come from different stress fields (the Euler-Bernoulli model and Jourawski's shear stresses). The shear part of the deflection of the free end of the cantilever is thus \(2 \cdot 2{,}487 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{J}/500\ \text{N} = f_{\mathrm{s}} F L/(GA) = 0{,}00995\) mm (at \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa), which is \(0{,}125\) % of \(7{,}972\) mm. This is the sum of the slides \(\Delta v_{\mathrm{s}}\) of all pieces of the cantilever (figure 5.167, third panel). The term \(\int f_{\mathrm{s}}\,\hat V\,V/(GA)\,\mathrm{d}x\) with the imagined shear force \(\hat V\), divided by \(1\) N, would give the same number with a negative sign, because \(\hat V = +1\) and \(V = -500\) N, and the end goes down. The estimate uses the shear stresses of theorem 5.39, which hold only approximately in the model, so it shows the order of magnitude: for a slender beam the omission is negligible.

Now we can treat all three ways in which the piece of figure 5.167 deforms by one pattern. The true stress gives the true strain, the imagined stress does work on it, and we add this work first over the cross section, then along the length. The computation over the cross section is each time like the one in lemma 5.81, so we write it once for all three.

Lemma 5.82 Work of the imagined stresses over a cross section (delo zamišljenih napetosti v prerezu)

Let the cross section be as in lemma 5.81, with the axis \(z\) through the centroid, of a linear elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\) and shear modulus \(G\). Let \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) and \(\delta N\), \(\delta V\), \(\delta M\) be numbers (the true and the imagined internal forces in this cross section). Let the true stresses be \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = V\,S/(I\,b)\), the imagined ones \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \delta N/A - \delta M\,y/I\) and \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \delta V\,S/(I\,b)\), and all other components of both stresses zero. Let the true strains be \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(2G)\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \big( \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + 2\,\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} \big)\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{\delta N\,N}{EA} + \frac{\delta M\,M}{EI} + \frac{f_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta V\,V}{GA} , \tag{5.77}\]

where \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the shear factor of equation (5.76). The left-hand side is the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the principle of virtual forces (equation (4.71)), integrated over the cross section, and each of the three terms on the right belongs to one of the three ways of deforming.

Proof

We must show that the integral splits into three terms, one for each way of deforming.

  1. In the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) only the terms \(xx\) (axial and bending ways) and \(xy\) and \(yx\) (shear way) differ from zero, because the other components of the imagined stress are zero. The terms \(xy\) and \(yx\) are equal, because the stress and the strain are symmetric. The sum is therefore \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + 2\,\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
  2. The longitudinal part (axial and bending way). We insert the stresses: \(\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac1E \big( \delta N/A - \delta M\,y/I \big)\big( N/A - M\,y/I \big)\). We multiply the brackets and integrate over the cross section. The numbers \(N\), \(M\), \(\delta N\), \(\delta M\) go in front:
    \[\int_{\mathrm{A}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac1E \Big( \frac{\delta N\,N}{A^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} \mathrm{d}A - \frac{\delta N\,M + \delta M\,N}{A\,I} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\mathrm{d}A + \frac{\delta M\,M}{I^2} \int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A \Big) .\]
  3. The first integral is \(A\), the third is \(I\) (definition 5.26). The middle one is zero, because the axis \(z\) passes through the centroid. The longitudinal part is therefore \(\delta N\,N/(EA) + \delta M\,M/(EI)\). There is no mixed term: the imagined axial force does no work on the rotation of the face, because in a rotation about the centroidal axis the mean stretch of the fibres over the cross section is zero: the fibres above the axis shorten, those below it lengthen.
  4. The shear part (slip). By assumption \(2\,\delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G = \delta V\,V\,S^2/(G\,I^2 b^2)\). The numbers \(\delta V\) and \(V\) go in front. By the definition of \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) in equation (5.76), \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} (S/b)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = f_{\mathrm{s}}\,I^2/A\), so the shear part is \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta V\,V/(GA)\).
  5. We add steps 3 and 4 and get (5.77).

Source: EP (3.2.40)-(3.2.44) (p. 118) for the energy with three terms, EP (4.4.12) (p. 198) for the work of the imagined stresses. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The lemma says what the displacement is in each way of deforming and which imagined force does work on it. For a piece of length \(\Delta s\) the work is about the right-hand side of equation (5.77) multiplied by \(\Delta s\).

  1. The axial way. The piece stretches by \(\Delta u = N\,\Delta s/(EA)\), the work is done by \(\delta N\).
  2. The bending way. The face turns by \(\Delta\varphi = M\,\Delta s/(EI)\), the work is done by \(\delta M\).
  3. The shear way. The face slides by \(\Delta v_{\mathrm{s}} = f_{\mathrm{s}}\,V\,\Delta s/(GA)\), the work is done by \(\delta V\). The factor \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) says that the slide on which the imagined shear force does work is, for the rectangle, one fifth larger than the slide \(V\,\Delta s/(GA)\) at a uniform stress.

The cross section may change along the beam. The lemma holds in every cross section separately, so in the integral along the length we write \(A(s)\), \(I(s)\) and \(f_{\mathrm{s}}(s)\), where \(s\) is measured along the axis. The assumption in this is that the stresses in every cross section have the form of the lemma, although theorems 5.35 and 5.39 are proved for a beam with a constant cross section. It is justified when the cross section changes slowly, as in the tapered bar below, whose edges are inclined to the axis by \(0{,}01\). It does not hold at a step or a notch, where the cross section changes at once. How large an error it makes, the theorems of this chapter cannot tell.

Corollary 5.83 Unit load method with the axial, bending and shear terms (enotska sila z osnim, upogibnim in strižnim členom)

Let a plane beam have an axis of length \(L\), along which we measure \(s\), with cross sections as in lemma 5.82, where \(A\), \(I\) and \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) are continuous positive functions of \(s\). Let \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) be the true internal forces and \(n\), \(v_1\), \(m\) the internal forces of the imagined system divided by \(1\) N, all piecewise continuous functions of \(s\). The imagined system consists of a force \(1\) N at the point and in the direction of the displacement \(\Delta\) we look for, and of the reactions of the supports, which do not move under the true load. In every cross section let the true and the imagined stresses and the true strains be as in lemma 5.82, with \(\delta N = n \cdot 1\) N, \(\delta V = v_1 \cdot 1\) N, \(\delta M = m \cdot 1\) N. Let further the work of the imagined force on the true displacement, \(1\ \text{N} \cdot \Delta\), equal the integral \(\int \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V\) over the body of the beam (this is what the principle of virtual forces, theorem 4.98, states for the true strains of a body). Then

\[\Delta = \int_0^L \Big( \frac{n\,N}{EA} + \frac{m\,M}{EI} + \frac{f_{\mathrm{s}}\,v_1\,V}{GA} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}s . \tag{5.78}\]

For a frame of several straight members \(\Delta\) is the sum of such integrals over the members. For a rotation the imagined load is a couple \(1\) N m.

Proof

We must show that the integral over the body becomes an integral along the length with three terms.

  1. We compute the integral over the body first over the cross section and then along the length (a piece of length \(\mathrm{d}s\), theorem 4.9): \(\int \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L \big( \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \delta\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}A \big)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. By lemma 5.82 the inner integral (one term for each way of deforming) equals \(\big( n\,N/(EA) + m\,M/(EI) + f_{\mathrm{s}}\,v_1\,V/(GA) \big) \cdot 1\) N, with the values \(A\), \(I\), \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\) of this cross section.
  3. By assumption the integral over the body equals \(1\ \text{N} \cdot \Delta\). We divide by \(1\) N and get (5.78).
  4. For a frame the body is the union of the members, so the integral over the body is the sum of the integrals over the members.

Source: EP (4.4.12) (p. 198) and (3.2.44) (p. 118). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

In the frame of figure 5.177 each of the three members contributes its own integral along its own axis \(s\). The force \(1\) N lies at the point and in the direction of the displacement \(\Delta\) we look for. The displacement \(\Delta\) is the sum of three integrals.

How firm is this corollary? The first two terms are theorem 5.79, which we proved in the Euler-Bernoulli model with no further assumption. The third term rests on the assumption of the corollary that the shear stresses of theorem 5.39 are the true ones and that the principle of virtual forces holds for them. In the Euler-Bernoulli model this is not so, because there \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\): in this model the piece does not slide. The third term is therefore an estimate here. It becomes a theorem in the model of the beam with shear strain in the next section, where the piece slides and where \(1/k\) stands in place of \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\).

We test the pattern on a tapered flat bar (figure 5.178). A steel bar of thickness \(t = 20\) mm and length \(L = 1\) m is clamped at \(s = 0\). Its height falls linearly from \(h_0 = 40\) mm at the clamp to \(h_1 = 20\) mm at the free end, \(h(s) = h_0\,\big( 1 - s/(2L) \big)\). The material is the same as for our cantilever, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa, and at the clamp the cross section is the same too, \(20 \times 40\) mm.

  1. The axial term (first panel). A force \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 10\) kN pulls the free end along the axis. The axial force is \(N = F_{\mathrm{x}}\) everywhere, there is no shear force and no moment. An imagined force \(1\) N in the same direction gives \(n = 1\). The area is \(A(s) = t\,h(s)\), so
    \[u(L) = \int_0^L \frac{1 \cdot F_{\mathrm{x}}}{E\,t\,h_0\,\big( 1 - s/(2L) \big)}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{2 F_{\mathrm{x}} L}{E\,t\,h_0}\,\ln 2 = 0{,}0884\ \text{mm} .\]
    The end moves by \(0{,}0884\) mm. A bar of constant height \(40\) mm would stretch by \(F_{\mathrm{x}} L/(E\,t\,h_0) = 0{,}0638\) mm, the tapered one by \(2\ln 2 = 1{,}39\) times as much, because the thinner pieces towards the end are softer.
  2. The bending term (second panel). A force \(F = 500\) N acts downward at the free end. As for the cantilever in section 5.12.3, \(M = -F\,(L - s)\) and \(m = L - s\) for an imagined force \(1\) N upward. The moment of inertia is \(I(s) = t\,h(s)^3/12\), at the clamp \(I_0 = t\,h_0^3/12\), so
    \[\int_0^L \frac{(L - s) \cdot \big( -F\,(L - s) \big)}{E\,I(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s = -(24 \ln 2 - 15)\,\frac{F L^3}{3 E I_0} = -1{,}636 \cdot 7{,}972\ \text{mm} = -13{,}04\ \text{mm} .\]
    The axis bends and the end goes down by \(13{,}04\) mm, \(1{,}64\) times as much as at the constant height \(40\) mm. Why so much more, when at the clamp, where the moment is largest, the cross section is the same? The bending stiffness falls with the third power of the height. At the middle the height is \(30\) mm and the stiffness only \((3/4)^3 = 0{,}42\) of the stiffness at the clamp.
  3. The shear term, the same load. The shear force is \(V = -F\), the imagined one \(v_1 = 1\). Every cross section is a rectangle, so \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 6/5\) everywhere, and the area is \(A(s) = t\,h(s)\):
    \[\int_0^L \frac{f_{\mathrm{s}} \cdot 1 \cdot (-F)}{G\,A(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\frac65 \cdot \frac{2 F L}{G\,t\,h_0}\,\ln 2 = -0{,}0138\ \text{mm} .\]
    This is \(0{,}11\) % of the bending part, so the total deflection is \(-13{,}04 - 0{,}01 = -13{,}05\) mm. For a bar of constant height \(40\) mm the same term gives \(-f_{\mathrm{s}} F L/(GA) = -0{,}00995\) mm, the number we got above from the ratio of the energies.

We computed all three integrals with SymPy.

When may we leave out which term? Take a cantilever of constant rectangular cross section of height \(h\) with a force \(F\) at the end and compare each term with the bending one, which gives the deflection \(F L^3/(3EI)\). The shear term is \(f_{\mathrm{s}} F L/(GA)\). The ratio is \(3 f_{\mathrm{s}} E I/(G A L^2)\), and with \(I/A = h^2/12\), \(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 6/5\) we get \(\tfrac35 (1 + \nu)\,(h/L)^2 = 0{,}78\,(h/L)^2\) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\). For the axial term take the same force along the axis of a member of the same length, as in the column of a frame that carries the force of the girder. The term is \(F L/(EA)\) and the ratio is \(3I/(A L^2) = \tfrac14 (h/L)^2\).

Now we carry Castigliano's second theorem (theorem 4.103) over to the beam. The idea is the same: the energy written with a force has, with respect to this force, a derivative that is a displacement. The force can be a true one (the state with the force \(F_1\)), or an imagined one (the unit force) that we add to get the displacement at a point where there is no force.

Figure 5.180 shows the idea of the proof. The beam has two states, one with the force \(F_1 + h\) at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the other with the force \(F_1\). The difference of the internal forces, divided by \(h\), is \(\hat n\), \(\hat m\). In it the given loads cancel, a force \(1\) N remains at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), and the differences of the support reactions do no work. This is the imagined system of theorem 5.79.

Theorem 5.84 Castigliano's second theorem for a beam (drugi Castiglianov izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in theorem 5.79. In addition to the given loads let a force \(F_1\) in the direction \(y\) (or a couple \(F_1\), at an interior point only a fictitious one with actual value \(0\), since theorem 5.79 allows no point couples at interior points) act at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\). Let the axial force and the moment be written as the functions \(N(x, F_1) = N_0(x) + F_1\,\hat n(x)\) and \(M(x, F_1) = M_0(x) + F_1\,\hat m(x)\), where \(N_0\), \(M_0\), \(\hat n\), \(\hat m\) are functions of \(x\) only, each continuous on every one of finitely many pieces, and for every value of \(F_1\) let them be in equilibrium with the load and the reactions of the supports, which do not yield under the true load. At the actual value of \(F_1\) let \(N\) and \(M\) be the true ones. Let

\[U^*(F_1) = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( \frac{N(x, F_1)^2}{EA} + \frac{M(x, F_1)^2}{EI} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

Then at the actual value of \(F_1\)

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}U^*}{\mathrm{d}F_1} = v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) \qquad \text{(or } v'(x_{\mathrm{P}}) \text{ for a couple)} . \tag{5.79}\]

Let moreover the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) in the actual beam be held by a support that does not yield, \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\), and let \(F_1\) mean the reaction of this support. Let \(N(x, F_1)\) and \(M(x, F_1)\) describe the beam in which this support is replaced by the force \(F_1\), the remaining supports not moving under the true load. Then \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = 0\) at the actual value of the reaction \(F_1\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the energy with respect to \(F_1\) is the right-hand side of equation (5.74) for suitably chosen functions \(n\) and \(m\), and that we get these as the derivatives of the forces with respect to \(F_1\), that is \(\hat n\) and \(\hat m\). For the reaction we show in addition that the deflection at the supported point is zero also without the support.

  1. By assumption \(N = N_0 + F_1\,\hat n\) and \(M = M_0 + F_1\,\hat m\), so \(\partial N/\partial F_1 = \hat n\) and \(\partial M/\partial F_1 = \hat m\) (rules of differentiation, lemma 1.82). These functions do not depend on \(F_1\).
  2. The equilibrium conditions of a piece (\(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\), \(M' = -V\)), the jumps at points and the conditions at the ends are linear in \(N\), \(V\), \(M\), in the given loads and in the force \(F_1\), and they hold for every value of \(F_1\) (the shear force is \(V = -M'\), so it too has the form \(V_0 + F_1\,\hat V\) with \(\hat V = -\hat m'\)). We subtract the conditions at \(F_1 + h\) and at \(F_1\) (\(h \ne 0\)) and divide by \(h\). The given loads cancel, and the force \(F_1\) gives \(h/h = 1\) (in units of newton). The functions \(\hat n\), \(\hat V\), \(\hat m\) are therefore the internal forces of the imagined system of theorem 5.79: a force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports, which are the differences of the reactions divided by \(h\) and do no work, because under the true load the supports do not yield.
  3. By theorem 5.79 therefore \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = \int_0^L \big( \hat n\,N/(EA) + \hat m\,M/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(N\) and \(M\) are the true ones.
  4. In \(U^*\) we insert \(N\) and \(M\) from step 1 and expand the squares: \(N^2 = N_0^2 + 2F_1\,N_0\,\hat n + F_1^2\,\hat n^2\), and the same for \(M^2\). By the linearity of the integral \(U^*(F_1) = a + b\,F_1 + c\,F_1^2\) with \(a = \tfrac12 \int \big( N_0^2/(EA) + M_0^2/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(b = \int \big( N_0\,\hat n/(EA) + M_0\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(c = \tfrac12 \int \big( \hat n^2/(EA) + \hat m^2/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\). These are numbers, as the functions are piecewise continuous.
  5. The derivative of a quadratic polynomial is \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = b + 2c\,F_1\), and by the linearity of the integral this is \(\int \big( (N_0 + F_1\hat n)\,\hat n/(EA) + (M_0 + F_1\hat m)\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int \big( N\,\hat n/(EA) + M\,\hat m/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  6. At the actual \(F_1\) the \(N\), \(M\) are the true ones, so step 5 equals step 3. This proves (5.79). For a couple \(F_1\) use an imagined couple \(1\) N m.
  7. Now let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be supported with the reaction \(F_1\) (in figure 5.181 this is the roller support, replaced by the force \(R\)). At the actual value of \(F_1\) the actual beam has the true internal forces \(N\), \(M\), so steps 1 to 5 hold for it directly. The imagined system of step 2 (a force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the remaining supports) has the internal forces \(\hat n\), \(\hat m\). By theorem 5.78 the integral \(\int_0^L \big( \hat n\,N/(EA) + \hat m\,M/(EI) \big)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is the work of the imagined system on the true displacements: the force \(1\) N does the work \(1\ \text{N} \cdot v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\), because \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = 0\), and the reactions of the remaining supports do none, because the supports do not move. By step 5 this integral is \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1\), so \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}F_1 = 0\). We do not need the beam without the support at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) to be statically determinate.

Source: EP (4.5.13)-(4.5.16) (pp. 206-207), examples 4.4.3 (pp. 201-202) and 4.5.2 (p. 208). The proof in steps is not from the sources: it is the proof of theorem 4.103 with integrals instead of sums.

Let us see how this solves the propped cantilever (figure 5.181). A cantilever of length \(L = 2\) m is clamped at \(x = 0\), at \(x = L\) there is a roller support (definition 5.5) that prevents the deflection, and the beam carries the uniform load \(q = -q_0\), \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. There are more reactions than equations of equilibrium: we take the reaction of the roller support \(R\) (a force upward) as the unknown, which we call the redundant unknown (odvečna neznanka). The force method (metoda sil) is then a sequence of steps.

  1. We remove the support at \(x = L\) and replace it by a force \(R\) upward. We get a cantilever that is statically determinate.
  2. The moment of this cantilever is the sum of the contribution of the load and the contribution of the force \(R\). The load \(q_0\) on the length \(L - x\) has the force \(q_0 (L - x)\) downward acting at the midpoint, so by the same reasoning as for the cantilever with a force at the end its contribution is \(-q_0 (L - x)^2/2\). The force \(R\) upward at the free end gives \(R\,(L - x)\), like the imagined unit force in example 1 above. Hence \(M(x, R) = R\,(L - x) - \tfrac12 q_0 (L - x)^2\). It is linear in \(R\), as the theorem requires.
  3. The complementary energy is \(U^*(R) = \tfrac{1}{2EI} \int_0^L M^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), where there is no axial force.
  4. The roller support does not yield, so the tangent to the parabola is horizontal and by theorem 5.84 \(\mathrm{d}U^*/\mathrm{d}R = 0\), hence
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}U^*}{\mathrm{d}R} = \frac1{EI} \int_0^L M\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{L^3}{EI} \Big( \frac R3 - \frac{q_0 L}{8} \Big) = 0 ,\]
    where we used \(\partial M/\partial R = L - x\).
  5. The solution is \(R = 3 q_0 L/8 = 750\) N. This is the reaction from section 5.9.4.
  6. The moment of the clamp is \(M(0, R) = R L - \tfrac12 q_0 L^2 = 1500 - 2000 = -500\) N m, and the vertical reaction of the clamp is \(q_0 L - R = 1250\) N, both as in section 5.9.4.

A test without the theorem: the cantilever with the load \(q_0\) has its free end lower by \(q_0 L^4/(8EI) = 95{,}66\) mm, and the force \(R = 750\) N lifts it by \(R L^3/(3EI) = 95{,}66\) mm. Together the deflection is zero, as it must be, because the roller support does not yield.

The complementary energy has a meaning here. As a function of the unknown \(R\) it is a parabola (figure 5.182): \(U^*(0) = 38{,}27\) J, \(U^*(750) = 2{,}392\) J, \(U^*(1500) = 38{,}27\) J. The true value \(R = 750\) N is the lowest point of this parabola, where the tangent is horizontal: by step 4 of the proof of theorem 5.84 the coefficient of \(R^2\) is \(c = \frac{1}{2EI}\int_0^L (L - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = L^3/(6EI) > 0\), and the derivative is zero at \(750\) N. Since here all moments in equilibrium with the load have the form \(M(x, R)\), the true one is the smallest in complementary energy among all moments in equilibrium. For a body the principle of minimum complementary energy (theorem 4.101) says the same, here is its form for a beam. It does not cover the shear term. If we added the term \(\tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{s}} V^2/(GA)\) of lemma 5.81 to \(U^*\) nevertheless and looked for the minimum over \(R\), we would get \(R = 750{,}08\) N, that is \(0{,}010\) % more. This is an estimate of the size, not a theorem.

5.12.5 Reciprocity for beams (vzajemnost pri nosilcih)

Chapter 4 showed that the work of load 1 on the displacements of load 2 equals the work of load 2 on the displacements of load 1, and promised an example on a beam. Here we prove it for a beam directly from theorem 5.78, and not from theorem 4.138, because that one is written for a body, and a beam is a model. The work of load \(k\) on the displacements of load \(l\), for example of the weight on the deflection due to the force in figure 5.183 below, we denote \(W_{\mathrm{kl}}\):

\[W_{\mathrm{kl}} = \int_0^L q^{(k)}\,v^{(l)}\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \Big( F^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\,v^{(l)}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) + M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\,{v^{(l)}}'(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \Big) ,\]

where \(q^{(k)}\) is the distributed transverse load, \(F^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the point forces and \(M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{i}}\) the couples of load \(k\) at the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), among which are the ends where point loads act.

Take our cantilever of length \(L = 1\) m, clamped in a wall (section 5.9.2), and load it in two ways (figure 5.183). Load 1 is the force \(F = 500\) N downward at the free end. The beam bends into the deflection \(v^{(1)}\), the free end comes down by \(7{,}972\) mm. Load 2 is the self weight, \(q = -\rho g A = -61{,}21\) N/m along the whole length. It bends the beam into the deflection \(v^{(2)}\) and lowers the free end by \(0{,}366\) mm. The work \(W_{21}\) is the work of the weight on the deflection \(v^{(1)}\) made by the force: every small piece of the beam comes down with its weight along the left dashed curve. The work \(W_{12}\) is the work of the force on the lowering of the free end made by the weight. At first sight these two numbers have nothing in common, since one load is a point load and the other is distributed.

The theorem below claims that they are equal, and tells why: both equal the integral of the product of the two moments. Figure 5.184 shows this integral for our two loads, and we follow the proof on it. Each of the three horizontal axes is the beam from the clamp at \(x = 0\) to the free end at \(x = L\), and above or below it one function of \(x\) is drawn.

Theorem 5.85 Betti's theorem for a beam (Bettijev izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be made of a linearly elastic material as in theorem 5.79, with supports that do not yield under either load. Let loads \(1\) and \(2\) be transverse (a piecewise continuous distributed load and point forces, without couples at interior points, couples at the ends are allowed, and without axial loads), with the true deflections \(v^{(1)}\) and \(v^{(2)}\), which are twice continuously differentiable. Then

\[W_{12} = W_{21} = \int_0^L \frac{M^{(1)}\,M^{(2)}}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{5.80}\]

where \(M^{(1)}\) and \(M^{(2)}\) are the true moments of the two loads.

Proof

We must show that \(W_{12}\) equals the integral of the product of the moments by theorem 5.78, and that this is symmetric.

  1. As the imagined system we take load \(1\) with its reactions and the internal forces \(\delta V = V^{(1)}\), \(\delta M = M^{(1)}\), which are in equilibrium with it, in figure 5.184 the moment \(M^{(1)}\) of the force at the end. As the displacement we take the true displacement of load \(2\), \(v = v^{(2)}\) (with \(u = 0\), because there are no axial loads).
  2. By theorem 5.78 the integral \(\int_0^L M^{(1)}\,{v^{(2)}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\) equals the work of load \(1\) on the displacements \(v^{(2)}\) and the work of the reactions. The work of the reactions is zero, because the supports do not yield: the clamp neither moves nor turns, so the reaction and the reaction moment act at a point that does not move (as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 5.79). Therefore this integral equals \(W_{12}\).
  3. In load \(2\) \({v^{(2)}}'' = M^{(2)}/(EI)\) (theorem 5.35), the curvature due to the weight. Hence \(W_{12} = \int_0^L M^{(1)} M^{(2)}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\), the area under the product of the two diagrams.
  4. The same computation with the loads exchanged gives \(W_{21} = \int_0^L M^{(2)} M^{(1)}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\). The product of two numbers is commutative, so at every point \(x\) the height of the lower diagram is the same, whichever load we take as the imagined one. The area under it is the same, and \(W_{12} = W_{21}\).

Source: EP examples 4.6.4 and 4.6.5 (pp. 228-229) use the same statement for a cantilever. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The assumption that the supports do not yield enters in step 2. For a rigid wall it is right, because the reaction acts at a point that does not move and so does no work. It fails when a support moves or turns under one load and not under the other, for example because the foundation settles under the weight. If the clamp turned by an angle \(\varphi_0\) under the weight, the reaction moment of the force, \(F L = 500\) N m, would do the work \(500\ \text{N m} \cdot \varphi_0\) on this turn. This work would be in \(W_{12}\) and not in \(W_{21}\), since under the force the clamp does not turn. Already at \(\varphi_0 = 10^{-4}\) rad this is \(0{,}05\) J, 27 % of \(W_{12} = 0{,}1830\) J, and the equality no longer holds.

We see the equality from a second point of view as well, the order of loading, which figure 4.298 in chapter 4 shows for a truss. We first slowly put the force \(F\) on the cantilever, then the weight. While the weight grows, the whole force \(F\) already stands on the free end and comes down with it by \(0{,}366\) mm, so it does the work of a constant force, \(W_{12}\), as the blue rectangle above for the truss. In the reverse order the weight, which already stands in full, does \(W_{21}\) on the deflection due to the force, the blue rectangle below. The work of each load while it grows by itself is the same in both orders, as the triangles of one force and the triangles of the other. The final shape of the beam is the same in both orders, so the total work done is the same too, which theorem 4.137 states for a body. Since the triangles are equal, the rectangles must be equal as well. For a beam this is not a proof, because the theorem is written for a body, but it shows the physical reason: the energy in the beam does not know in which order we loaded it. The proof above says the same without an order, both rectangles are the area under the same product of moments.

Now we use the theorem. The deflection of the free end due to the weight we get by theorem 5.85, without solving the deflection equation, from the field of the force. We know this field, \(v^{(1)}(x) = -F\,x^2 (3L - x)/(6EI)\). The work of the weight on this field is

\[W_{21} = \int_0^L q\,v^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g A\,F}{6EI} \int_0^L x^2\,(3L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g A\,F L^4}{8EI} .\]

The work of the force on the displacement of the free end due to the weight is \(W_{12} = (-F)\,v^{(2)}(L)\). From \(W_{12} = W_{21}\) follows \(v^{(2)}(L) = -\rho g A\,L^4/(8EI) = -0{,}366\) mm and \(W_{12} = W_{21} = 0{,}1830\) J (SymPy), the area under the product of the moments. This is the deflection from section 5.9.2, obtained in a different way.

The advantage shows when the load is harder to solve. Let the weight act only on the outer half of the beam, from \(x = L/2\) to \(L\). The weight jumps at \(x = L/2\), which theorem 5.78 allows, because \(L/2\) is one of the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) without a point force. The work of this weight on the known deflection of the force is the integral of the same \(v^{(1)}\), only over the outer half, hence

\[v^{(3)}(L) = -\frac{1}{F}\int_{\mathrm{L}/2}^{L} \frac{\rho g A\,F\,x^2\,(3L - x)}{6EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}3126\ \text{mm} ,\]

the lowering of the free end, and there is no new equation to solve. The half of the weight that is farther from the clamp gives 85 % of the lowering of the whole weight, because the weight of each small piece counts with the deflection \(v^{(1)}\) at its place, and this is largest on the outer half.

A special case of the theorem is two point forces of 1 N each. Take a simply supported beam of length \(L = 2\) m with the steel cross section and on it the points \(x_1 = L/4 = 0{,}5\) m and \(x_2 = L/2 = 1\) m (figure 5.185). The displacement of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) due to a force \(1\) N at the point \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), divided by \(1\) N, we denote \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\). A force \(1\) N upward at \(x_1\) bends the beam into a deflection that has the height \(D_{11}\) at \(x_1\) and the height \(D_{21}\) at \(x_2\). A force at \(x_2\) gives a deflection with the heights \(D_{12}\) and \(D_{22}\). By theorem 5.79 \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^L m_{\mathrm{i}}\,m_{\mathrm{j}}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the moment of the imagined system with a force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (here the true moment of the load is \(M_{\mathrm{j}} = m_{\mathrm{j}} \cdot 1\) N). These numbers are the flexibility coefficients (podajnostni koeficienti) of the beam, and the displacement at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is measured in the direction of the force \(1\) N, as for the flexibility matrix of definition 4.139: here the weights of the point and the small faces are superfluous, because we treat the beam as a line.

Corollary 5.86 Maxwell's theorem for a beam (Maxwellov izrek za nosilec)

Let a beam be as in theorem 5.85 and \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{m}}\) points on it. Let \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the deflection of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) due to a force \(1\) N upward at the point \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), divided by \(1\) N. Then

\[D_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^L \frac{m_{\mathrm{i}}\,m_{\mathrm{j}}}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x = D_{\mathrm{ji}} ,\]

so the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) is symmetric.

Proof

We must show that this is theorem 5.85 with two forces of \(1\) N.

  1. Let load 1 be a force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and load 2 a force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\). The work of load 1 on the displacements of load 2 is \(1\ \text{N} \cdot v^{(2)}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\ \text{N}\cdot D_{\mathrm{ji}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\), and the work of load 2 on the displacements of load 1 is \(1\ \text{N}\cdot D_{\mathrm{ij}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\). In figure 5.185, for \(j = 1\) and \(i = 2\), the first work is the force at \(x_1\) on the deflection \(D_{12}\) made by the force at \(x_2\), and the second is the force at \(x_2\) on the deflection \(D_{21}\).
  2. By theorem 5.85 they are equal. Therefore \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = D_{\mathrm{ji}}\), and the value is \(\int_0^L m_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{j}}/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\) by equation (5.80), because \(M^{(1)} = m_{\mathrm{j}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\) and \(M^{(2)} = m_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\).

Source: EP examples 4.6.6 and 4.6.7 (pp. 230-231), BARBER (1.18) (PDF p. 27) lists the influence coefficient matrix of a linear system as an example of a second-order tensor and does not prove its symmetry there. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

Let us test it on the beam of figure 5.185. With the imagined force \(1\) N at \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) we get by theorem 5.79 with SymPy

\[\mat D = \begin{pmatrix} 4{,}484 & 5{,}481 \\ 5{,}481 & 7{,}972 \end{pmatrix} \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m/N} .\]

The first column is the two heights of the top deflection, the second those of the middle one. An independent test: we solve the equation \(EI\,v'''' = 0\) piece by piece with each of the two forces separately (eight constants, four boundary conditions and four matching conditions at the point of the force, section 5.8.4), and get \(D_{12} = D_{21} = 5{,}481 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N. A force \(1\) N at \(x_2\) gives \(D_{22} = L^3/(48EI) = 7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N. This is the deflection of the central force from section 5.9.3 per newton: the force \(1000\) N gave \(7{,}972\) mm there, as much as the force \(500\) N at the end of the cantilever of length \(1\) m. The reason is that the middle section does not turn, so each half of the beam is a cantilever of length \(L/2\) with the force \(F/2\).

The matrix gives the displacements for any two forces. Put \(F_1 = 400\) N at \(x_1\) and \(F_2 = 1000\) N at \(x_2\), both upward (figure 5.185, bottom). By the addition of deflection curves (lemma 5.60) the deflection is the sum of the deflections of the two forces separately, since the deflection is linear in the forces, \(v(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} D_{\mathrm{ij}} F_{\mathrm{j}}\). At the point \(x_1\) \(D_{11}F_1 = 1{,}79\) mm from the first force and \(D_{12}F_2 = 5{,}48\) mm from the second stack up, at the point \(x_2\) \(D_{21}F_1 = 2{,}19\) mm and \(D_{22}F_2 = 7{,}97\) mm:

\[v(x_1) = D_{11}F_1 + D_{12}F_2 = 7{,}27\ \text{mm} , \qquad v(x_2) = D_{21}F_1 + D_{22}F_2 = 10{,}16\ \text{mm} ,\]

the total deflections. The complementary energy is \(U^* = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{ij}} D_{\mathrm{ij}} F_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{j}} = 6{,}537\) J, and the same number comes from the integral \(\tfrac{1}{2EI}\int (F_1 m_1 + F_2 m_2)^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (SymPy).

Castigliano's second theorem shows the same symmetry once more, from the algebraic point of view, and does not need the formula for \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\). By theorem 5.84 \(\partial U^*/\partial F_1 = v(x_1) = D_{11}F_1 + D_{12}F_2\), the deflection at \(x_1\), and \(\partial U^*/\partial F_2 = v(x_2) = D_{21}F_1 + D_{22}F_2\), the deflection at \(x_2\). The derivative of the first with respect to \(F_2\) is \(D_{12}\), that much deflection at \(x_1\) comes with every newton of the force \(F_2\). The derivative of the second with respect to \(F_1\) is \(D_{21}\), that much deflection at \(x_2\) comes with every newton of the force \(F_1\). These are the mixed derivatives of the same function, \(\partial^2 U^*/\partial F_2 \partial F_1 = D_{12}\) and \(\partial^2 U^*/\partial F_1 \partial F_2 = D_{21}\), and they are equal, because \(U^*\) is a quadratic polynomial, for which the order of differentiation does not matter (theorem 1.131).
