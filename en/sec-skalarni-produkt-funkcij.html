Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.3 Mathematics: inner product of functions

In section 4.6.8 we computed the hanging bar with the trial field \(c\,x\) (poskusno polje) with one free number \(c\). The best line gave the right displacement at the lower end and underestimated it in the middle of the bar. With one more free number we can do better. Take \(u = c_1\,x + c_2\,x^2\) with free numbers \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), which we call coefficients (koeficienta). The bar has length \(L = 10\) m and the same data as in chapter 4, \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N, \(q = 6{,}010\) N/m. Both fields \(x\) and \(x^2\) respect the fixing at \(x = 0\).

  1. The derivative of the field is \(u' = c_1 + 2 c_2\,x\). By the linearity of the integral and the fundamental theorem of calculus, the potential energy from corollary 4.93 (there is no force at the lower end) is
    \[\begin{aligned} \Pi(c_1, c_2) &= \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,(c_1 + 2 c_2 x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,(c_1 x + c_2 x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &= \tfrac12\,EA\Big(L\,c_1^2 + 2 L^2 c_1 c_2 + \tfrac43 L^3 c_2^2\Big) - q\Big(\tfrac12 L^2 c_1 + \tfrac13 L^3 c_2\Big) . \end{aligned}\]
  2. By section 4.6.6 both partial derivatives are zero at the lowest point. The derivative with respect to \(c_1\) and with respect to \(c_2\) gives two equations, linear in the unknowns \(c_1\) and \(c_2\):
    \[\begin{aligned} EA\,L\,c_1 + EA\,L^2\,c_2 &= \tfrac12\,q L^2 , \\ EA\,L^2\,c_1 + \tfrac43\,EA\,L^3\,c_2 &= \tfrac13\,q L^3 . \end{aligned} \tag{6.9}\]
  3. From the first equation \(c_1 = qL/(2EA) - L\,c_2\). We insert it into the second: \(\tfrac12\,qL^3 + \tfrac13\,EA\,L^3 c_2 = \tfrac13\,qL^3\), so \(c_2 = -q/(2EA) = -a\) and \(c_1 = qL/EA = 2aL\), where \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) as in section 3.1. The field is \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), which is the true field of the bar.

The true field is a quadratic polynomial, so the fields \(c_1 x + c_2 x^2\) contain it and we obtain it exactly. Now we ask what kind of equations we have obtained. In the system (6.9) every coefficient is an integral of a product of derivatives of two trial functions, for example \(EA\,L^2 = \int_0^L EA \cdot 1 \cdot 2x\,\mathrm{d}x\) is the product of the derivatives of the functions \(x\) and \(x^2\). The coefficient \(EA\,L^2\) stands in both equations because the product of the derivatives of the same two functions does not depend on their order. With \(n\) trial functions we shall get \(n\) such equations with \(n \times n\) coefficients. When does the system have exactly one solution, and why is it hard to solve in some cases? The answer comes from ideas we know from vectors in space. In this section we carry them over to functions, then show that the coefficients of the system are scalar products of the trial functions, and from this we derive when the system has a single solution.

6.3.1 Functions as vectors

In section 1.5.3 we already saw that functions defined for all numbers between two numbers are vectors in the sense of definition 1.16, since we add them point by point (figure 1.25). The trial fields of the hanging bar are not all such functions. They must be continuous, piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11) and equal to zero at \(x = 0\), because the bar is fixed there. Do they form a vector space? For that we must check that the sum of two such functions and a multiple of such a function again belong to them.

Lemma 6.15 Fields of a bar form a vector space (polja palice so vektorski prostor)

Let \(L > 0\) and let \(X\) be the set of all functions \(w : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) that are continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). With addition point by point and multiplication by a number point by point, \(X\) is a vector space. The set \(W\) of those functions of \(X\) that are zero at the fixed ends of the bar is a vector space too. For a bar fixed at \(x = 0\) these are the test functions of the weak form (definition 6.2), the functions with \(w(0) = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the sum and the multiple stay in \(X\) and in \(W\) and that the rules of definition 1.16 hold.

  1. Let \(w_1, w_2 \in X\) with the partitions of definition 6.11. We take the common partition, which has the break points of both. Each of its pieces lies in a piece of the first and in a piece of the second partition, so \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) have on an open piece a derivative that equals a function continuous on the closed piece.
  2. The sum \(w_1 + w_2\) is continuous (corollary 1.64) and by lemma 1.81, item 1, it has on every open piece of the common partition the derivative \(w_1' + w_2'\), which equals a sum of two functions continuous on the closed piece (corollary 1.64). So the sum is in \(X\). If \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) are zero at a fixed end \(x_{\mathrm{p}}\), then \((w_1 + w_2)(x_{\mathrm{p}}) = 0 + 0 = 0\), and the sum is in \(W\).
  3. The multiple \(k\,w_1\) is continuous and by lemma 1.81, item 2, it has on every open piece the derivative \(k\,w_1'\), which equals a function continuous on the closed piece, so it is in \(X\). If \(w_1(x_{\mathrm{p}}) = 0\), then \((k\,w_1)(x_{\mathrm{p}}) = k \cdot 0 = 0\), and the multiple is in \(W\).
  4. The zero function is continuous, by lemma 1.81, item 4, it has derivative zero everywhere and it is zero at every point, so it is in \(X\) and in \(W\) and is the zero element of both. The opposite element of a function \(w\) is \((-1)\,w\), which by step 3 is in the same space as \(w\).
  5. Rules 1, 2, 4 and 5 of definition 1.16 hold for all functions defined on \([0, L]\), because they hold at every value \(x\) for numbers. Rule 3 is satisfied by steps 4 and 3.

Source: The proof is not from a source. Functions as vectors: LEBL2 example 8.1.4 (PDF p. 9), KREYSZIG §7.9 (PDF p. 335).

For vectors in space we defined the scalar product in section 2.3 by the formula with components. We summarized its properties in lemma 2.12: symmetry, splitting of a sum, taking out a number and \(\vek a\cdot\vek a > 0\) for \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\). For functions there is no formula with components, but we have the properties, and from them we shall derive what we need. So we take them as the definition, just as we took the rules for addition as the definition of a vector space.

Definition 6.16 Inner product of functions (skalarni produkt funkcij)

Let \(X\) be a vector space, for example a space of functions. An inner product (skalarni produkt) on \(X\) is a rule that assigns to every two elements \(f\) and \(g\) of \(X\) a real number \((f, g)\) and has for all \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) in \(X\) and all numbers \(k\) the properties:

  1. \((f, g) = (g, f)\),
  2. \((f + g, h) = (f, h) + (g, h)\),
  3. \((k\,f, g) = k\,(f, g)\),
  4. \((f, f) > 0\) for every \(f \ne 0\).

The number \(\|f\| = \sqrt{(f, f)}\) is the norm (norma) of the element \(f\) induced by this inner product. A vector space with a chosen inner product is an inner product space (prostor s skalarnim produktom).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.9, definition of a real inner product space (PDF p. 338), EP (6.2.21) and the sentence "A linear vector space on which an inner product can be defined is called an inner product space" (p. 279).

From properties 1 and 2 it follows that \((h, f + g) = (f + g, h) = (f, h) + (g, h) = (h, f) + (h, g)\), and from 1 and 3 that \((f, k\,g) = k\,(f, g)\). From property 3 with \(k = 0\) it follows that \((0, g) = 0\). So the inner product is a symmetric bilinear form (definition 6.4) that is in addition positive. The examples for functions need a proof, while for vectors in space properties 1 to 4 are the items of lemma 2.12 (definition 2.8).

Lemma 6.17 Two inner products of functions (dva skalarna produkta funkcij)
  1. On the vector space of continuous functions on \([a, b]\), \(a < b\), \((f, g) = \displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is an inner product.
  2. Let \(EA\) be positive and piecewise continuous (constant on pieces as in the stepped bar). On the space \(W\) of lemma 6.15, for a bar fixed at least at one end, the energy inner product \(B(u, w) = \displaystyle\int_0^L EA\,u'(x)\,w'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) is an inner product.
Proof

For each example we check properties 1 to 4 of definition 6.16.

1. Integral of a product.

  1. The product of continuous functions is continuous (corollary 1.64), so the integral exists.
  2. Property 1: \(f g = g f\) at every value \(x\).
  3. Properties 2 and 3: \((f + g)\,h = f h + g h\) and \((k f)\,g = k\,(f g)\) at every value \(x\). By the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1) the properties hold.
  4. Property 4: let \(f \ne 0\), so \(f(x_0) \ne 0\) at some point \(x_0\). The function \(f^2\) is continuous and \(f(x_0)^2 > 0\). As in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of corollary 4.93, there is a closed interval \([c, d] \subseteq [a, b]\) with \(c < d\) that contains \(x_0\) and on which \(f^2 > f(x_0)^2/2\). By additivity and boundedness (lemma 3.39, items 2 and 3), and because \(f^2\) is nowhere negative, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac12 f(x_0)^2\,(d - c) > 0\).

2. Energy inner product.

  1. On the common partition \(EA\), \(u'\) and \(w'\) equal, on open pieces, functions continuous on the closed pieces, so \(EA\,u'w'\) is piecewise continuous and the integral is the sum of the integrals over the pieces (definition 6.9). Properties 1 to 3 follow from the equalities \(EA\,u'w' = EA\,w'u'\), \(EA\,(u_1' + u_2')\,w' = EA\,u_1'w' + EA\,u_2'w'\) and \(EA\,(k u')\,w' = k\,EA\,u'w'\) on the pieces, since the derivative of a sum and of a multiple holds (lemma 1.81, items 1 and 2), and from the linearity of the integral of piecewise continuous functions (lemma 6.10, item 2).
  2. Property 4 is the claim of lemma 6.7: \(B(w, w) = \int_0^L EA\,w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\) for every \(w \in W\), \(w \ne 0\).

Source: Integral of a product: KREYSZIG §7.9, example 4 (PDF p. 339), LEBL2 (PDF p. 197) for integrable functions, EP (6.2.21) and (6.2.22) (p. 279). Energy product: EP (6.2.31) (p. 282). The proof is not from the sources.

Why must the function \(w\) be zero at \(x = 0\)? Without this condition property 4 of the energy product does not hold. The constant function \(w = 1\) has \(w' = 0\), so \(B(w, w) = 0\) although \(w \ne 0\). Such a function is a rigid displacement of the bar, which stretches no cross section, and the fixing excludes it. For the inner product it is therefore the fixing that provides positivity.

The norm induced by the energy product has a physical meaning: the number \(B(w, w)\) is twice the strain energy of the bar, \(B(w, w) = 2U(w)\) (definition 4.2).

Definition 6.18 Energy norm (energijska norma)

Let \(B\) be the symmetric bilinear form of a task and \(W\) the space of its test functions (definition 6.6), such that \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every \(w \ne 0\) from \(W\). The energy norm (energijska norma) of a field \(w\) from \(W\) is the number

\[\|w\|_{\mathrm{E}} = \sqrt{B(w, w)} = \sqrt{2U(w)} , \tag{6.10}\]

where \(U(w)\) is the strain energy of the field \(w\) and \(B(w, w) = 2U(w)\) as in lemma 6.7. For the bar \(\|w\|_{\mathrm{E}} = \sqrt{\int_0^L EA\,w'(x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x}\).

Source: EP (6.2.42) and (6.2.43) (p. 287), BATHE (4.74) (PDF p. 254).

For the hanging bar the strain energy of the true field is \(U(u) = 391{,}03\) µJ, so \(\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = 782{,}06\) µJ, which is also the work of the weight on the true displacement (section 4.6.8). For the best line \(a L\,x\) we have \(\|a L\,x\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = 2 \cdot 293{,}27 = 586{,}55\) µJ. The unit of the norm is the square root of a joule, so we write the squares in microjoules.

For vectors in space the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality told us that the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) lies between \(-1\) and \(1\) and is the cosine of the angle between them. We shall use the same number for functions, so the inequality must be proved for every inner product. The proof of corollary 2.15 used the splitting of a vector in space (lemma 2.14). For functions we go another way: the square of a norm is not negative, so for every number \(t\) the value \((f + t\,g,\ f + t\,g) \ge 0\) and this is a quadratic polynomial in \(t\). We write the proof so that it uses only symmetry, linearity in both arguments and \((f, f) \ge 0\). Then it also holds for the integral of a product of piecewise continuous functions (lemma 6.10), where \((f, f)\) can be zero also for a function that is not zero everywhere, for example for one that differs from zero at a single point, so property 4 is missing there.

Lemma 6.19 Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for an inner product (Cauchy-Schwarzeva neenakost za skalarni produkt)

Let \((\cdot,\cdot)\) be a rule on a vector space that assigns a real number to every two elements and satisfies properties 1 to 3 of definition 6.16, and let \((f, f) \ge 0\) for every element \(f\). This holds for every inner product (by property 4) and for the integral of a product of piecewise continuous functions. Then for all elements \(f\) and \(g\)

\[|(f, g)| \le \sqrt{(f, f)}\,\sqrt{(g, g)} = \|f\|\,\|g\| . \tag{6.11}\]

If \((\cdot,\cdot)\) is an inner product and \(g \ne 0\), equality holds only when \(f\) is a multiple of the element \(g\).

Proof

We must show the inequality for every pair. We look at a quadratic polynomial in \(t\) and separate the cases \((g, g) = 0\) and \((g, g) > 0\).

  1. For every number \(t\) we have \((f + t\,g,\ f + t\,g) \ge 0\) by assumption. By linearity in both arguments and symmetry
    \[(f + t\,g,\ f + t\,g) = (f, f) + 2t\,(f, g) + t^2\,(g, g) \ge 0 \qquad \text{for every } t .\]
  2. Let \((g, g) = 0\). Then \((f, f) + 2t\,(f, g) \ge 0\) for every \(t\). If \((f, g) \ne 0\) held, then at \(t = -\big((f, f) + 1\big)/\big(2\,(f, g)\big)\) we would get \((f, f) + 2t\,(f, g) = -1 < 0\), which contradicts this. So \((f, g) = 0\) and the inequality holds, because the left side is zero and the right side is not negative. This includes \(g = 0\), since \((0, 0) = 0\) by property 3 with \(k = 0\).
  3. Let \((g, g) > 0\). We insert \(t = -(f, g)/(g, g)\) and obtain \((f, f) - 2\,(f, g)^2/(g, g) + (f, g)^2/(g, g) = (f, f) - (f, g)^2/(g, g) \ge 0\).
  4. We multiply the inequality by the positive number \((g, g)\): \((f, g)^2 \le (f, f)\,(g, g)\).
  5. For non-negative numbers the larger number has the larger square, so \(|(f, g)| \le \sqrt{(f, f)}\sqrt{(g, g)}\).
  6. Let \((\cdot,\cdot)\) be an inner product, \(g \ne 0\) and let equality hold. Then in step 3 the value \((f + t\,g, f + t\,g)\) at the chosen \(t\) is zero, so by property 4 \(f + t\,g = 0\), that is \(f = -t\,g\), a multiple of the element \(g\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.9, inequality (3) without proof (PDF p. 339), problem 24 (PDF p. 344) only checks it on examples. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

We test it on the functions \(f = 1\) and \(g = x\) on \([0, 1]\) with the inner product \(\int_0^1 f g\,\mathrm{d}x\). The product is \((f, g) = \int_0^1 x\,\mathrm{d}x = 1/2\), the squares of the norms are \(\|f\|^2 = \int_0^1 1\,\mathrm{d}x = 1\) and \(\|g\|^2 = \int_0^1 x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1/3\). The inequality (6.11) in squared form says \((1/2)^2 = 1/4 \le 1 \cdot 1/3\). The inequality is strict because \(1\) and \(x\) are not multiples of each other (step 6 of the proof, since \(\int_0^1 f g\,\mathrm{d}x\) is an inner product).

By this lemma the number \((f, g)/(\|f\|\,\|g\|)\) for nonzero \(f\) and \(g\) lies between \(-1\) and \(1\). For vectors in space it is the cosine of the angle between them. For functions we shall use it as a measure of how similar two functions are with respect to the chosen inner product, and call it the normalized inner product. For \(1\) and \(x\) it equals \(\tfrac12/\sqrt{1/3} = 0{,}866\).

6.3.2 Orthogonality

For vectors in space the scalar product is zero exactly when the angle is a right angle (theorem 2.10). For functions this is the definition.

Definition 6.20 Orthogonal functions (pravokotni funkciji)

Elements \(f\) and \(g\) of an inner product space \((\cdot, \cdot)\) are orthogonal (pravokotna) with respect to this inner product if \((f, g) = 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.9 (PDF p. 338), EP (6.2.27) (p. 280).

The notion depends on the chosen inner product. The functions \(1\) and \(x\) on \([0, 1]\) are not orthogonal with respect to \(\int_0^1 f g\,\mathrm{d}x\), since \((1, x) = 1/2\). But \(1\) and \(x - \tfrac12\) are orthogonal, since \(\int_0^1 (x - \tfrac12)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0\). For the energy product \(B\) of the bar orthogonality means \(\int_0^L EA\,f'g'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The product of the derivatives can be nonzero everywhere if it is positive on one part of the bar and negative on another so that the contributions cancel, as we shall see right below.

The Pythagorean theorem for orthogonal vectors in space followed from \(|\vek a + \vek b|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 + 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\) (lemma 2.9). The same computation holds for every inner product.

Lemma 6.21 Pythagorean theorem for orthogonal functions (Pitagorov izrek za pravokotni funkciji)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be orthogonal elements of an inner product space. Then \(\|f + g\|^2 = \|f\|^2 + \|g\|^2\).

Proof

We expand the square of the norm of the sum.

  1. By linearity in both arguments \(\|f + g\|^2 = (f + g, f + g) = (f, f) + (f, g) + (g, f) + (g, g)\).
  2. By symmetry \((g, f) = (f, g)\), and by orthogonality \((f, g) = 0\). What remains is \((f, f) + (g, g) = \|f\|^2 + \|g\|^2\).

Source: EP, Pythagorean theorem in function spaces (p. 280). The proof in steps is not from the source.

We have already met orthogonality in this sense for the hanging bar, without giving it a name. The best line is \(u_1 = a L\,x\) and its error is \(e = u - u_1 = a\,(2Lx - x^2) - a L\,x = a\,(Lx - x^2)\). Let us compute the energy product of the error with the function \(x\), which determines all trial fields \(c\,x\):

  1. The derivative of the error is \(e' = a\,(L - 2x)\), the derivative of the function \(x\) is \(1\).
  2. \(B(e, x) = \int_0^L EA\,a\,(L - 2x)\,\mathrm{d}x = EA\,a\,\big[L x - x^2\big]_0^L = EA\,a\,(L^2 - L^2) = 0\) by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

So the error is orthogonal to \(x\) and therefore to every field \(c\,x\), also to \(u_1\). By lemma 6.21 for \(u = u_1 + e\)

\[\|u\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 = \|u_1\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 + \|e\|_{\mathrm{E}}^2 , \qquad 782{,}06\ \text{µJ} = 586{,}55\ \text{µJ} + 195{,}52\ \text{µJ} ,\]

and dividing by 2, \(U(u) = U(u_1) + U(e)\): \(391{,}03 = 293{,}27 + 97{,}76\) µJ (computation in SymPy). The strain energy of the true field is the sum of the strain energy of the best line and the strain energy of the error. The difference \(97{,}76\) µJ between \(-293{,}27\) and \(-391{,}03\) µJ, which section 4.6.8 found as a difference of potential energies, is therefore the energy of the error. In figure 6.7 the three energies are proportional to the squares of the sides of a right triangle, with the factor \(\tfrac12\).

Why is the error orthogonal? The true field \(u\) satisfies the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.76) with the virtual displacement \(x\). With \(N = EA\,u'\) this says \(B(u, x) = \int_0^L N\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,q L^2\), since \(F = 0\). For the trial field \(c\,x\) the potential energy is \(\tfrac12\,c^2 B(x, x) - c\cdot\tfrac12\,q L^2\) and its derivative with respect to \(c\) is zero at \(c\,B(x, x) = \tfrac12\,q L^2\). So \(B(c\,x, x) = B(u, x)\) and \(B(u - c\,x, x) = 0\). The best line is the one for which the error is orthogonal to the field that we vary. With \(B(x, x) = EA\,L\) we obtain \(c = qL/(2EA) = aL\), as before.

6.3.3 Linear independence

For the hanging bar the functions \(x\) and \(x^2\) were different, and we obtained exactly one solution. What if we took \(x\) and \(2x\)? The field \(c_1 x + c_2 \cdot 2x = (c_1 + 2c_2)\,x\) would again be a line, and the system could not determine \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) separately, since every pair with the same sum \(c_1 + 2c_2\) would give the same field. The functions are too similar. We say it precisely as follows.

Definition 6.22 Linearly independent functions (linearno neodvisne funkcije)

Let \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be elements of a vector space, for example functions on an interval. The expression \(c_1\psi_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}}\psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\) is their linear combination (linearna kombinacija). The elements are linearly independent (linearno neodvisni) if a linear combination equal to the zero element, for functions equal to zero at every point of the interval, holds only for \(c_1 = \dots = c_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). Otherwise they are linearly dependent (linearno odvisni): there are numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\), not all zero, with the combination zero.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.4 (PDF p. 308), EP (6.2.8) and (6.2.9) (p. 275).

For \(x\) and \(2x\) the combination \(2 \cdot x - 1 \cdot 2x = 0\) at every \(x\) with the nonzero numbers \(2\) and \(-1\), so they are dependent. For \(x\) and \(x^2\) the combination \(c_1 x + c_2 x^2\) equals \(c_1 + c_2\) at \(x = 1\) and \(2 c_1 + 4 c_2\) at \(x = 2\). If both are zero, then \(c_2 = -c_1\) and \(2 c_1 - 4 c_1 = -2 c_1 = 0\), so \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\). This computation was special, so we prove a general claim.

Lemma 6.23 Independence of powers (neodvisnost potenc)

Let \(b > 0\) and \(n\) a natural number. The functions \(1, x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) on the interval \([0, b]\) are linearly independent, and so are \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\).

Proof

We must show that \(p(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}} x^n = 0\) at every \(x \in [0, b]\) implies \(c_0 = \dots = c_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). We compute the \(j\)-th derivative at \(x = 0\).

  1. The polynomial \(p\) is defined for all numbers and on the interval \([0, b]\) it is the constant function zero, so by lemma 1.81, item 4, it has derivative zero at every point of the interval, at the point \(x = 0\) one-sided. Since the polynomial is differentiable at every point, its derivative at \(0\) equals the one-sided one, so \(p'(0) = 0\), and \(p' = 0\) on \([0, b]\). The derivative of a polynomial is again a polynomial (items 1, 2 and 5 of the same lemma), so the same conclusion holds for \(p'\), then for \(p''\) and so on: \(p^{(j)}(0) = 0\) for every \(j = 0, 1, \dots, n\).
  2. By lemma 1.81, items 2 and 5, the \(j\)-th derivative of the function \(x^k\) for \(j \le k\) is \(k\,(k - 1)\cdots(k - j + 1)\,x^{k - j}\) and for \(j > k\) it is zero. At \(x = 0\) the value is zero, except when \(k = j\), where it is \(j(j - 1)\cdots 1 = j!\).
  3. The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives (lemma 1.81, item 1), so \(p^{(j)}(0) = j!\,c_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  4. From steps 1 and 3, \(j!\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\), so \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for every \(j = 0, \dots, n\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. A similar argument for particular polynomials: EP example 6.2.2 (p. 275-276), where the coefficients are compared without proof.

The trial fields \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) of the hanging bar are therefore independent. The second family that we shall use is made of straight lines on pieces: we cut the bar into pieces and take a straight line on each. First we name the pieces and the points where they meet.

Definition 6.24 Mesh of an interval (mreža intervala)

Let \(n\) be a natural number and \(L > 0\). A mesh (mreža) of the interval \([0, L]\) is a set of \(n + 1\) points \(0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\), which we call the nodes of the mesh (vozlišča mreže). The intervals \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\), which we have so far called pieces, are the elements of the mesh (elementi mreže), the number \(h_{\mathrm{k}} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) is the length of the \(k\)-th element, and the number \(h = \max_{\mathrm{k}} h_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the length of the largest element. A mesh with equal elements has \(h_{\mathrm{k}} = h = L/n\).

Source: ONATE1 §2.3.1 (PDF p. 69) and §2.4 (PDF p. 76), COOK §4.8 (PDF p. 170): the notions of node and element, piecewise linear interpolation. The definition of the mesh is ours.

On each element we need a straight line, which is determined by two values, one at each end. The values at the nodes therefore determine the whole function, provided the function is continuous. For each node we build a function that equals 1 there and 0 at all other nodes. Its graph has the shape of a tent.

Definition 6.25 Hat function (šotorska funkcija)

Let \(x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a mesh of the interval \([0, L]\) and \(i\) one of the numbers \(0, 1, \dots, n\). The hat function (šotorska funkcija) of the node \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the function \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}} : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) that is continuous, runs along a straight line on each element, and satisfies \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\) for \(j = i\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for \(j \ne i\). Written element by element it is

\[\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x - x_{\mathrm{i}-1}}{x_{\mathrm{i}} - x_{\mathrm{i}-1}} & x_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{i}} , \\[2mm] \dfrac{x_{\mathrm{i}+1} - x}{x_{\mathrm{i}+1} - x_{\mathrm{i}}} & x_{\mathrm{i}} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{i}+1} , \\[2mm] 0 & \text{elsewhere.} \end{cases} \tag{6.12}\]

For \(i = 0\) the first case does not occur, for \(i = n\) the second does not.

Source: ONATE1 §2.4 (PDF p. 76, global interpolation from local ones), COOK §4.8 (PDF p. 170). The form of the writing is ours.

The graph of the function \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a triangle with the apex above \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) (figure 6.9). The function \(\psi_0\) has \(\psi_0(0) = 1\) and does not respect the fixing at \(x = 0\). For \(j = 1, \dots, n\), however, the hat functions are in the space \(W\) of lemma 6.15, since they are continuous, linear on each element and \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(0) = 0\).

Lemma 6.26 Independence of hat functions (neodvisnost šotorskih funkcij)

The hat functions \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) of the mesh \(0 = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\) are linearly independent.

Proof

We must show that \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) implies \(c = 0\). We evaluate the combination at the nodes.

  1. Let \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{j}}\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x) = 0\) at every \(x \in [0, L]\). We insert the node \(x = x_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\).
  2. By definition \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = 1\) for \(j = i\) and \(0\) for \(j \ne i\). The sum is therefore \(c_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot 1 = 0\), so \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  3. Since \(i\) is arbitrary, \(c_1 = \dots = c_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

What does dependence do to our system of equations? Take the simply supported beam from chapter 5 with length \(L = 2\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and the uniform load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m downwards. The trial function \(\psi_1 = x\,(L - x)\) satisfies the conditions \(\psi_1(0) = \psi_1(L) = 0\) of the simply supported beam. If we multiply it by \(L\), we obtain \(\psi_2 = L\,x\,(L - x) = 2\psi_1\), which satisfies the same conditions too. The functions are dependent, \(2\psi_1 - \psi_2 = 0\). The coefficients of the system are the energy products \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \int_0^L EI\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}''\psi_{\mathrm{i}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\) (as for the bar, now with the second derivative, because the energy of the beam is \(\int \tfrac12 EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\)), and the right sides are the work of the load \(\int_0^L(-q_0)\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). With \(\psi_1'' = -2\) and \(\psi_2'' = -2L = -4\) m we get

\[\begin{bmatrix} 8\,EI & 16\,EI \\ 16\,EI & 32\,EI \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1333{,}3 \\ -2666{,}7 \end{bmatrix} \ \text{N m}^2 .\]

The second row is twice the first, on both sides. One equation remains, \(c_1 + 2c_2 = -1333{,}3/(8 \cdot 20\,906{,}7) = -7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-3}\) m\(^{-1}\), for two unknowns. Infinitely many pairs \((c_1, c_2)\) solve it. Each gives the same field \((c_1 + 2c_2)\,\psi_1\), at the middle \(-7{,}972\) mm, which is exactly the result of one term. So the field is determined, but the coefficients are not. The system has infinitely many solutions because the functions are dependent, and the choice among them has no physical meaning. The determinant \(8\,EI \cdot 32\,EI - (16\,EI)^2\) is zero.

For a true answer to when the system has exactly one solution, the coefficients of the system must be connected with the inner product.

6.3.4 Matrix of inner products

The coefficients in the system (6.9) are energy products of the trial functions, \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). With \(n\) trial functions and any inner product \((\cdot, \cdot)\) we obtain a table of \(n \times n\) numbers \((\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}})\), and for the energy product it is the same table, because \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}})\) by symmetry. We need a property of the matrix that says that the system loses no equation. We write it with the number \(\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}} K_{\mathrm{ij}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\), which for a column \(\vek c\) and a square matrix \(\mat K\) of order \(n\) we obtain as a product of matrices (definition 1.93, lemma 1.99) of sizes \(1 \times n\), \(n \times n\) and \(n \times 1\).

Definition 6.27 Positive definite matrix (pozitivno definitna matrika)

A square matrix \(\mat K\) of order \(n\) is positive definite (pozitivno definitna) if it is symmetric (definition 1.102) and for every nonzero column \(\vek c\) with \(n\) elements

\[\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{i}}\,K_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} > 0 .\]

Source: KREYSZIG §8.4, problem 24 (PDF p. 372): positive definiteness of a quadratic form and of its symmetric matrix.

The matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) is symmetric but not positive definite, since for \(\vek c = (0, 1)\) the value is \(-1\). The matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) is symmetric, but for \(\vek c = (2, -1)\) we have \(\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c = 1 \cdot 2^2 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot (-1)^2 = 4 - 8 + 4 = 0\): its rows are multiples of each other, as for the beam above. A lemma follows that connects everything.

Lemma 6.28 Matrix of inner products (matrika skalarnih produktov)

Let \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be elements of an inner product space \((\cdot, \cdot)\) and \(G_{\mathrm{ij}} = (\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}})\) the elements of a matrix \(\mat G\) of order \(n\). Then:

  1. \(\mat G\) is symmetric,
  2. for every column \(\vek c\), \(\vek c^\T\mat G\,\vek c = \Big\|\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\Big\|^2 \ge 0\),
  3. \(\mat G\) is positive definite exactly when \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) are linearly independent.
Proof

We must show three claims from bilinearity. For the second we expand the inner product of the combination.

  1. By property 1 of the inner product \(G_{\mathrm{ij}} = (\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) = (\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = G_{\mathrm{ji}}\), so \(\mat G\) is symmetric.
  2. Let \(\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) in the space. By linearity in both arguments \((\varphi, \varphi) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,G_{\mathrm{ij}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek c^\T\mat G\,\vek c\).
  3. So \(\vek c^\T\mat G\,\vek c = \|\varphi\|^2 \ge 0\) by property 4 (the square of a norm is positive for \(\varphi \ne 0\) and zero for \(\varphi = 0\)).
  4. Let the \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) be independent and \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\). By definition 6.22 then \(\varphi \ne 0\), so \(\vek c^\T\mat G\,\vek c = \|\varphi\|^2 > 0\) and \(\mat G\) is positive definite. Let them be dependent. Then there is \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) with \(\varphi = 0\), so \(\vek c^\T\mat G\,\vek c = \|0\|^2 = 0\) and \(\mat G\) is not positive definite.

Source: The proof is not from a source. Square of the norm of a linear combination: EP (6.2.21), (6.2.26) (p. 279).

For the hanging bar the functions were \(\psi_1 = x\) and \(\psi_2 = x^2\), the product is the energy product \(B\), and the matrix \(\mat K = [B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})]\) is the matrix in the system (6.9), \(K_{11} = EA\,L\), \(K_{12} = EA\,L^2\), \(K_{22} = \tfrac43\,EA\,L^3\). By lemmas 6.23, 6.17 and 6.28 it is positive definite. Another case with numbers are the functions \(1, x, x^2\) on \([0, 1]\) with the product \(\int_0^1 f g\,\mathrm{d}x\), which are independent. Their matrix of inner products has elements \(G_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^1 x^{i + j - 2}\,\mathrm{d}x = 1/(i + j - 1)\):

\[\mat G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac13 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac14 \\ \tfrac13 & \tfrac14 & \tfrac15 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{6.13}\]

Its determinant is \(1/2160\) (computation in SymPy). In this book we have determinants only up to order \(3\), so we shall not solve systems of order \(n\) with them.

How do we solve the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\) for \(n\) unknowns? The known way is Gauss elimination. In the first row we choose the element on the diagonal, the pivot (pivot), and by subtracting multiples of the first row from the others we cancel the first column below it. Then we repeat on the remaining matrix of order \(n - 1\), and so on up to the triangular form, which we solve from the last row upwards (back substitution). Elimination fails if a pivot comes out zero, since we cannot divide by it. For the matrix (6.13) the pivots are \(1\), \(1/12\) and \(1/180\). The first is \(G_{11} = 1\). We subtract \(\tfrac12\) of the first row from the second and \(\tfrac13\) of the first from the third, and obtain the remaining matrix \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1/12 & 1/12 \\ 1/12 & 4/45 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Its first element is the second pivot \(1/12\). We subtract the first row of this matrix from the second and obtain \(4/45 - 1/12 = 1/180\), the third pivot. All pivots are positive, and their product is \(1 \cdot \tfrac1{12} \cdot \tfrac1{180} = 1/2160\), which is also the determinant of the matrix (computation in SymPy).

This is no coincidence. The next theorem says that for a positive definite matrix elimination always succeeds, and without exchanging rows.

Theorem 6.29 Linear system with a positive definite matrix (sistem s pozitivno definitno matriko)

Let \(\mat K\) be a positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\vek f\) a column with \(n\) elements. Then the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\) has exactly one solution, and Gauss elimination without exchanging rows reaches it with all \(n\) pivots positive.

Proof

We prove the existence of a solution and the positive pivots by induction on \(n\) (theorem 1.44), and uniqueness directly. The claim \(P(n)\) reads: for every positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and every column \(\vek f\) elimination without exchanging rows gives \(n\) positive pivots and the system has a solution.

  1. Basis, \(n = 1\). The matrix is the number \(K_{11}\) and \(c\,K_{11}\,c > 0\) for \(c = 1\) says \(K_{11} > 0\). The pivot is \(K_{11}\) and the solution is \(c_1 = f_1/K_{11}\).
  2. Induction step. Let \(P(n - 1)\) hold and let \(\mat K\) be of order \(n \ge 2\). We write \(\mat K = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} K_{11} & \vek b^\T \\ \vek b & \mat K' \end{smallmatrix}\right]\), where \(\vek b\) is the column of elements \(b_{\mathrm{i}} = K_{\mathrm{i}1}\) for \(i = 2, \dots, n\) (by symmetry the first row to the right of the diagonal is \(\vek b^\T\)) and \(\mat K'\) is the matrix of elements \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for \(i, j = 2, \dots, n\), symmetric of order \(n - 1\) as a part of a symmetric matrix.
  3. The first pivot is positive: for \(\vek c = \vek e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\) we have \(\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c = K_{11} > 0\).
  4. The first step of elimination subtracts from row \(i \ge 2\) the multiple \(K_{\mathrm{i}1}/K_{11}\) of the first row. The element \((i, j)\) of the remaining matrix becomes \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} - K_{\mathrm{i}1}K_{1\mathrm{j}}/K_{11} = K_{\mathrm{ij}} - b_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{j}}/K_{11}\), the right side \(f_{\mathrm{i}} - b_{\mathrm{i}} f_1/K_{11}\). The remaining matrix is \(\mat K'' = \mat K' - \vek b\,\vek b^\T/K_{11}\) of order \(n - 1\) and the right side \(\vek f''\). Elimination on rows \(2, \dots, n\) is exactly the elimination of the system \(\mat K''\,\vek c'' = \vek f''\) for the unknowns \(c_2, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\), since row \(1\) stays.
  5. The matrix \(\mat K''\) is symmetric, because \(\mat K'\) and \(\vek b\vek b^\T\) are symmetric.
  6. The matrix \(\mat K''\) is positive definite. Let \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\) be a column with \(n - 1\) elements, \(s = -\vek b^\T\vek y/K_{11}\) and \(\vek z = (s, \vek y)\), a column with \(n\) elements, which is not zero because \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\). Since \(\vek y^\T\vek b = \vek b^\T\vek y\) (a number equals its transpose) and \(\mat K\) is symmetric,
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek z^\T\mat K\,\vek z &= K_{11}\,s^2 + 2 s\,\vek b^\T\vek y + \vek y^\T\mat K'\vek y \\ &= \frac{(\vek b^\T\vek y)^2}{K_{11}} - \frac{2\,(\vek b^\T\vek y)^2}{K_{11}} + \vek y^\T\mat K'\vek y \\ &= \vek y^\T\mat K'\vek y - \frac{(\vek b^\T\vek y)^2}{K_{11}} = \vek y^\T\mat K''\vek y , \end{aligned}\]
    because \(\vek y^\T\vek b\,\vek b^\T\vek y = (\vek b^\T\vek y)^2\). The left side is positive, \(\vek z^\T\mat K\,\vek z > 0\), because \(\mat K\) is positive definite and \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\).
  7. By \(P(n - 1)\) for \(\mat K''\) elimination on rows \(2, \dots, n\) has positive pivots and \(\mat K''\vek c'' = \vek f''\) has a solution \(c_2, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\). The first equation gives \(c_1 = (f_1 - \vek b^\T\vek c'')/K_{11}\) (back substitution). Since every row of the original system is the sum of the transformed row and a multiple of the first row, this \(\vek c\) also solves \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\). The pivots of the matrix \(\mat K\) are \(K_{11}\) and the pivots of the matrix \(\mat K''\), all positive. The claim \(P(n)\) is proved.
  8. Uniqueness. Let \(\vek c\) and \(\vek d\) be solutions, \(\mat K\vek c = \mat K\vek d = \vek f\). For \(\vek w = \vek c - \vek d\) we have \(\mat K\vek w = \vek 0\), so \(\vek w^\T\mat K\,\vek w = 0\). Since \(\mat K\) is positive definite, this means \(\vek w = \vek 0\), so \(\vek c = \vek d\).

Source: KREYSZIG §7.3 (PDF p. 300-302) for Gauss elimination, the pivot and back substitution (with a choice of pivot by exchanging rows, which our matrix does not need), KREYSZIG §20.2 (PDF p. 881): "For a symmetric, positive definite matrix A" for Cholesky's method. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The theorem is proved by induction on the same reasoning as Gauss elimination, only that step 6 says why the pivots do not become zero: after every step a matrix remains that is again symmetric and positive definite. By this theorem and lemma 6.28 a system made of independent trial functions always has a single solution. The same elimination will be used by the finite element method in chapter 7.

One more consequence of positive definiteness is that the solution of the system is the lowest point of a function of the coefficients. For the hanging bar \(\Pi(c_1, c_2)\) from the beginning of the section is the potential energy of the field \(c_1 x + c_2 x^2\). As a quadratic function of both coefficients it equals \(\tfrac12\,\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c - \vek f^\T\vek c\) with the matrix \(\mat K\) from the system (6.9) and \(\vek f = (\tfrac12\,qL^2,\ \tfrac13\,qL^3)\), and the system is the condition that its partial derivatives are zero. We generalize to any order (the same sign \(\Pi\) as for the energy of a field, because it will be the energy of the field \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\)).

Lemma 6.30 Minimum of a quadratic function (najmanjša vrednost kvadratne funkcije)

Let \(\mat K\) be a positive definite matrix of order \(n\), \(\vek f\) a column and \(\Pi(\vek c) = \tfrac12\,\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c - \vek f^\T\vek c\) for columns \(\vek c\). Let \(\vek c^*\) be the solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek f\) (theorem 6.29). Then for every column \(\vek d\)

\[\Pi(\vek d) - \Pi(\vek c^*) = \tfrac12\,(\vek d - \vek c^*)^\T\mat K\,(\vek d - \vek c^*) \ge 0 , \tag{6.14}\]

and equality only at \(\vek d = \vek c^*\). So the function \(\Pi\) has exactly one smallest value, at the point \(\vek c^*\).

Proof

We must show the decomposition as for the bar in the proof of corollary 4.93. We write \(\vek d = \vek c^* + \vek v\).

  1. We expand: \(\tfrac12(\vek c^* + \vek v)^\T\mat K(\vek c^* + \vek v) = \tfrac12\,\vek c^{*\T}\mat K\vek c^* + \tfrac12\,\vek c^{*\T}\mat K\vek v + \tfrac12\,\vek v^\T\mat K\vek c^* + \tfrac12\,\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v\).
  2. By the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat K\) and exchanging the order of summation, \(\vek c^{*\T}\mat K\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c^*_{\mathrm{i}} K_{\mathrm{ij}} v_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} v_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ji}} c^*_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek v^\T\mat K\vek c^*\). The two middle terms combine into \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek c^*\).
  3. So \(\Pi(\vek d) = \Pi(\vek c^*) + \vek v^\T\mat K\vek c^* - \vek f^\T\vek v + \tfrac12\,\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v\).
  4. Since \(\mat K\vek c^* = \vek f\), we have \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek c^* - \vek f^\T\vek v = \vek v^\T\vek f - \vek f^\T\vek v = 0\). What remains is (6.14).
  5. By definition 6.27 the right side is positive for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), so \(\Pi(\vek d) > \Pi(\vek c^*)\) for every \(\vek d \ne \vek c^*\).

Source: EP theorem 6.2 (p. 283) for the minimum of a quadratic functional in general form. The proof in steps is not from the source.

For the hanging bar we know this. For \(\vek c^* = (2aL, -a)\) and \(\vek d = (aL, 0)\), that is the line \(a L\,x\) without the term \(x^2\), we have \(\vek d - \vek c^* = (-aL, a)\) and

\[\tfrac12\,(\vek d - \vek c^*)^\T\mat K\,(\vek d - \vek c^*) = \tfrac12\,EA\int_0^L \big(-aL + 2a x\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 97{,}76\ \text{µJ} ,\]

exactly the difference \(-293{,}27 - (-391{,}03)\) µJ. This is also the energy of the error \(U(e)\) from the previous section, since \(\vek d - \vek c^*\) are the coefficients of the error. In other words: for \(n = 2\) lemma 6.30 is the same thing that section 4.6.6 showed for a quadratic function of two variables, only that now it holds for any order.

All the properties we were looking for hold for independent trial functions. Why then does the choice of functions still matter? A computer writes every number with about 16 digits. For the system (6.9) this suffices. For the functions \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) and larger \(n\) it no longer does. We define a measure for this with the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat K\), and for it we need a statement that the book does not yet have for matrices of order \(n\): a symmetric matrix of order \(n\) has \(n\) real eigenvalues and \(n\) mutually orthogonal unit eigenvectors with real components (CM §2.5.6.2 and §2.5.6.3, PDF p. 92-93). For order 3 this is theorem 2.61, for any order we prove it in chapter 10, where modal analysis needs it. Only the measure we define now and the bound after it rest on this cited statement, and no proof of this chapter does. The eigenvalues of a positive definite matrix \(\mat K\) are positive. From \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda\vek v\) with a real \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) it follows that \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v = \lambda\,\vek v^\T\vek v\). The left side is positive by definition 6.27 and \(\vek v^\T\vek v\) is a sum of squares of components, so it is positive, hence \(\lambda > 0\).

Definition 6.31 Condition number (število občutljivosti)

Let \(\mat K\) be a positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and let \(\lambda_{\max}\) and \(\lambda_{\min}\) be its largest and smallest eigenvalue (definition 2.59), which by the above are real and positive. The condition number (število občutljivosti) of the matrix \(\mat K\) is their ratio,

\[\kappa(\mat K) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \ge 1 .\]

Source: KREYSZIG §20.4, definition (13) and theorem 1 (PDF p. 894): the number is defined by matrix norms, \(\kappa(\mat A) = \|\mat A\|\,\|\mat A^{-1}\|\). The form as a ratio of eigenvalues is ours and for the Euclidean norm of vectors it equals Kreyszig's (see below). CM §2.5.6.2 and §2.5.6.3 (PDF p. 92-93): the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real and the eigenvectors mutually orthogonal, for order 3 theorem 2.61.

Why is this the same measure as Kreyszig's? Let \(\vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{n}}\) be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}}\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then \(\mat K\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \lambda_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}}\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(|\mat K\vek x|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}^2 x_{\mathrm{i}}^2 \le \lambda_{\max}^2 |\vek x|^2\), with equality at \(\vek x = \vek v_{\max}\). The largest ratio \(|\mat K\vek x|/|\vek x|\), which is the norm of the matrix for the Euclidean length of vectors (KREYSZIG §20.4, (9), PDF p. 893), is therefore \(\lambda_{\max}\). The matrix \(\mat K^{-1}\) has the same eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\), so its norm is \(1/\lambda_{\min}\) and \(\|\mat K\|\,\|\mat K^{-1}\| = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\).

The condition number says by how much the relative error can grow in passing from the right side to the solution. If we change the right side \(\vek b\) by \(\delta\vek b\), the solution changes by \(\delta\vek x = \mat K^{-1}\delta\vek b\). By the above \(|\delta\vek x| \le |\delta\vek b|/\lambda_{\min}\), and from \(\vek b = \mat K\vek x\) we have \(|\vek b| \le \lambda_{\max}|\vek x|\), so \(|\delta\vek x|/|\vek x| \le \kappa\,|\delta\vek b|/|\vek b|\), as in theorem 1 in Kreyszig. Let us try it on an example. A bar with \(EA = 1\) and \(L = 1\) is loaded by the force \(1\) at its end. The trial fields \(x, x^2, \dots, x^n\) give the matrix with elements \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = ij/(i + j - 1)\) and the right side \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) (the value \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}(1)\)), the solution is \(c = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\). For \(n = 8\) the matrix has condition number \(5{,}1 \cdot 10^9\). We change the right side of all ones by a relative \(10^{-10}\) (we alternately add and subtract \(10^{-10}\) to the elements, the computation is exact in SymPy). The solution changes by \(5{,}5\,\%\). For \(n = 4\) the matrix has \(\kappa = 7{,}0 \cdot 10^3\) and the solution changes by a relative \(2 \cdot 10^{-7}\). For hat functions with a uniform mesh and \(n = 8\) we have \(\kappa = 113\). The right side of the same load is there \((0, \dots, 0, 1)\), since at the point \(x = 1\) only the function \(\psi_8\) is nonzero. If we change it by a relative \(10^{-10}\) with the same alternating pattern, the solution changes by a relative \(2 \cdot 10^{-11}\), so by less than we changed the right side.

A computer with 16 digits has rounding errors of relative size \(10^{-16}\) during the computation. At \(\kappa = 5 \cdot 10^9\) the error of the solution can therefore be of the order of \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), which means that about ten of the sixteen digits are lost. With larger \(n\) it gets worse: \(\kappa = 5{,}6 \cdot 10^6\) at \(n = 6\) and \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{12}\) at \(n = 10\), so by a factor of about \(30\) with every added term.

Why are powers so sensitive? The functions \(x^8\), \(x^9\) and \(x^{10}\) on \([0, 1]\) (figure 6.8) are almost the same curve. The energy normalized inner product between \(x^8\) and \(x^9\) is \(0{,}998\), and between \(x^9\) and \(x^{10}\) also \(0{,}998\) (with the derivatives \(8 x^7\) and \(9 x^8\) and the product \(\int_0^1 f' g'\,\mathrm{d}x\)). Two almost parallel functions give two almost equal rows in the system. For the dependent functions in the section on linear independence the rows were exactly multiples of each other. So almost dependent functions give an almost singular matrix. For \(x^8\), \(x^9\) and \(x^{10}\) the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix \(\left[B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\right]\) is \(3{,}8 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and the largest \(14{,}3\), the ratio is \(3{,}7 \cdot 10^5\) already with three functions.

Hat functions differ from powers in that each reaches only its neighbours. The function \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is equal to zero outside the pieces \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) and \([x_{\mathrm{i}}, x_{\mathrm{i}+1}]\), because it is zero at all nodes except \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and linear on a piece. The product of derivatives \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\psi_{\mathrm{j}}'\) is therefore zero on every piece, unless the piece is next to both nodes \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), which is possible only for \(|i - j| \le 1\). So \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for \(|i - j| \ge 2\) and the matrix is tridiagonal (figure 6.9). For \(n = 4\) equal pieces of the hanging bar (\(h = 2{,}5\) m)

\[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \tag{6.15}\]

since \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}' = \pm 1/h\) on the pieces, and \(\int \psi_{\mathrm{i}}'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 2/h\) for the inner functions, \(1/h\) for the last one, \(\int\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\psi_{\mathrm{i}+1}'\,\mathrm{d}x = -1/h\). The normalized inner product of two neighbouring inner hat functions is \(-\tfrac1h/\tfrac2h = -\tfrac12\), far from \(\pm 1\). The matrix is therefore not close to singular, as the condition numbers show: \(\kappa = 29\) at \(n = 4\), \(113\) at \(n = 8\) and \(438\) at \(n = 16\), so when \(n\) is doubled it grows by a factor of about \(4\) (\(\kappa \approx 1{,}7\,n^2\)), while for powers it grows by a factor of about \(30\) with every term.

The choice of functions therefore matters, although both families give exactly one solution by theorem 6.29. Independence guarantees existence and uniqueness, but not a good computation. Powers bring simple formulas and sensitivity. Hat functions bring a sparse matrix and small sensitivity, but for a beam they do not qualify, because they have kinks, while the slope of the deflection of a beam must not jump, which we shall show later in this chapter. We shall use both families.
