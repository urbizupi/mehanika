Chapter 20 Damping models

20.9 Damping of continuous systems

We strike the \(20 \times 40\) mm cantilever (konzola) of example 13.38 at its free end. Without damping it would vibrate forever, in reality it comes to rest, and the higher tones fall silent sooner than the first. We hear the same on a guitar: a plucked string falls silent after a few seconds, the higher tones sooner. In chapter 13 we proved the modes and the expansion in them for the string, the bar and the beam, but without damping. At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the beam there has no steady response, except under a load that does not excite the \(n\)-th mode (theorem 13.46). This is a property of the undamped model and not of the real beam. This section answers three questions. Where does the damping that we met for finitely many degrees of freedom in sections 20.4 and 20.7 enter a beam? When do the modes stay as they were, and how strongly is each one damped then? And what does a dashpot do that is not distributed along the beam but attached to the end of a string? (figure 20.25)

The answer to the first question is short. Damping is an additional force or an additional stress, proportional to the velocity. The balance of a piece from section 13.5.3 stays the same, only what stands in the force and in the moment changes. If the additional term is proportional to the mass or to the stiffness, the modes are kept, as with Rayleigh damping (Rayleighovo dušenje, definition 10.45). If it is not, as with the dashpot at the end of the string, there are no modes in the usual sense any more. (figure 20.25)

20.9.1 Where damping enters a beam

We consider the beam of sections 13.5 and 13.6: the Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14) with length \(L\), constant \(EI\) and \(\rho A\) and the displacement \(w(x, t)\) perpendicular to the axis. The three idealizations are the same as there. First: the rotary inertia of the cross-sections and shear are neglected, which for the simply supported \(20 \times 40\) mm beam with \(L = 1\) m lowers the first frequency by \(0{,}27\) % and the third by \(2{,}3\) % (section 13.10). Second: the displacements are small. Third: the supports are ideal. We add two damping mechanisms, each with one number: external dashpots (zunanji dušilniki) with the coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) and internal damping of the material (notranje dušenje) with the retardation time \(\tau_\varepsilon\). (figure 20.25)

We begin with external damping. We draw the cantilever, clamped in a wall, in the position it has at some instant, and we measure the deflection \(w\) upwards from the rest position. Under the beam we put dashpots that connect it to the ground at four points. Each is a viscous dashpot (definition 9.1): the force is proportional to the velocity of its point and points against the velocity. When we install very many such dashpots, we replace them by a density \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) in N s/m\(^2\): on a piece of length \(h\) acts the force \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,h\) against the velocity, that is \(-c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\) per unit length. (figure 20.25)

We obtain the equation of motion from the balance of a piece, as in section 13.5.3. We take the piece between \(a\) and \(b\), of length \(b - a\). The rest of the beam acts on it with the forces \(V(a)\) and \(V(b)\) and the couples \(M(a)\) and \(M(b)\). To these we add the dashpot forces: they are distributed along the whole length of the piece and point downwards because the beam moves upwards, so they start on the beam. (figure 20.25)

Internal damping enters the moment. In section 20.4 we described a viscoelastic material by springs and dashpots. For the beam we take the simplest one, the Kelvin-Voigt element (definition 20.24): the spring and the dashpot are in parallel, so they have the same strain. A fibre of the beam between two cross-sections is thus a spring with modulus \(E\) and a parallel dashpot with viscosity \(E\tau_\varepsilon\), and the axial force \(\sigma A\) pulls both plates. The stress is the sum of the stresses of the spring and the dashpot, \(\sigma = E\,(\varepsilon + \tau_\varepsilon\,\dot\varepsilon)\). (figure 20.25)

Let us see what this does to the moment and to the equation of motion.

Lemma 20.50 Equation of motion of a beam with internal and external damping (gibalna enačba nosilca z notranjim in zunanjim dušenjem)

Let \(EI > 0\), \(\rho A > 0\), \(\tau_\varepsilon \ge 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{z}} \ge 0\) be constant. Let the material of a fibre obey \(\sigma = E\,(\varepsilon + \tau_\varepsilon\,\dot\varepsilon)\), let the cross-sections remain plane (theorem 5.15) and let \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}A\). Let \(w\) have continuous derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) and their derivatives in time, and let \(q\) be a continuous load. Let \(V\) be a continuous function. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

  1. For every pair \(0 \le a < b \le L\) and every \(t\) the balance of the piece holds with the dashpot force \(-c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\) added to the load \(q\), that is equations (13.34) and (13.35) with \(q - c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\) in place of \(q\).
  2. \(V = -M_{\mathrm{x}}\) holds with
    \[M = EI\,\big(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}}\big) \tag{20.43}\]
    and
    \[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,\big(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\big) + c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}} = q . \tag{20.44}\]
Proof

We must show the moment from the material, then follow the same procedure as in lemma 13.29 with another moment and another load. The time \(t\) is fixed throughout, \(\tilde q = q - c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\).

  1. By theorem 5.15, with plane cross-sections and \(u = 0\) the strain of the fibre at height \(y\) is \(\varepsilon = -y\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (equation (5.17)), and its rate is \(\dot\varepsilon = -y\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}}\), because \(y\) is constant for a fibre.
  2. The stress is \(\sigma = -E\,y\,(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}})\). Hence \(M = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}})\int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), and \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I\) (definition 5.26). This is (20.43).
  3. Let \(C\) be the largest value of the function \(|\tilde q - \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}|\) on the closed rectangle. The integral in the moment equation is at most \(C\,(b - a)^2/2\) in absolute value. We divide the moment equation by \(b - a\) and let \(b \to a\): \((M(b) - M(a))/(b - a) \to M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\), \(V(b) \to V(a)\), and the integral term tends to \(0\). Hence \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + V = 0\) for every \(a < L\), and by continuity also at \(L\).
  4. By step 3, \(V' = -M_{\mathrm{xx}}\), a continuous function. The fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40) gives \(V(b) - V(a) = -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b M_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). We insert this into the force equation: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + M_{\mathrm{xx}} - \tilde q\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every piece. The integrand is continuous, so it is zero everywhere (step 4 of the proof of lemma 13.29). Since \(M_{\mathrm{xx}} = EI\,(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}})\), this is (20.44).
  5. Conversely: from \(V = -M_{\mathrm{x}}\) we have \(V' = -M_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and by (20.44) \(V' = \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - \tilde q\). The fundamental theorem gives the force equation for every piece. We check the moment equation by integration by parts as in step 6 of the proof of lemma 13.29, in which we only take \(M' = -V\) as the starting point instead of \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\).

Source: The step-by-step proof is not from a source. The term \(EI\,\tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\) belongs to a beam of Kelvin-Voigt material, the term \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\) to a beam with external damping, both as a generalization of equation (13.31). Kelvin-Voigt term: CM §9.2.3 (PDF p. 429), BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF pp. 137-138).

Equation (20.44) is equation (13.31) with two additional terms. The first, \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\), is a force per unit length proportional to the velocity (the dashpot forces of the figure). The second, \(EI\,\tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\), is the one the moment gives: the elastic part \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\) is multiplied by \((1 + \tau_\varepsilon\,\partial_{\mathrm{t}})\), where \(\partial_{\mathrm{t}}\) denotes the derivative in time. We made the same substitution \(E \to E\,(1 + \tau_\varepsilon\,\partial_{\mathrm{t}})\) for finite elements in section 20.7, where it gave the matrix \(\mat c_{\mathrm{e}} = \tau_\varepsilon\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 20.39). Here the same term appears in continuous form, so it is no surprise that it will lead to the same result as Rayleigh damping. (figure 20.25)

20.9.2 The modes stay and each has its own ratio

What remains of the modes of section 13.6 when we damp the beam? For finitely many degrees of freedom we knew the answer: if the damping matrix is \(\mat C = a_0\mat M + a_1\mat K\), the modes are the same as without damping and each is an oscillator with the ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}) + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2\) (definition 10.45, lemma 10.46). In equation (20.44) the term \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}} = (c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\cdot\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\) is mass times velocity, and the term \(EI\,\tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\) is \(\tau_\varepsilon\) times the time derivative of the stiffness term \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\). These are the continuous forms of \(a_0\mat M\) and \(a_1\mat K\) with \(a_0 = c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A\) and \(a_1 = \tau_\varepsilon\). So we expect the same result. The proof must not depend on the expansion in a series, which for the cantilever is only stated in chapter 13: we use only the orthogonality of the modes and integration by parts. (figure 20.25)

Theorem 20.51 A beam with damping keeps its modes (nosilec z dušenjem ohrani načine)

Let the beam be that of lemma 20.50, with self-adjoint boundary conditions at both ends (definition 13.41), and let \((\omega_{\mathrm{n}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a mode of the undamped beam (definition 13.6), that is \(EI\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}'''' = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\rho A\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\). Let \(w\) be a solution of equation (20.44) that satisfies the same boundary conditions for every \(t \ge 0\), with continuous derivatives up to the fourth order in \(x\), the second in \(t\) and mixed ones up to \(w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\). With

\[\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{\tau_\varepsilon\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}{2} + \frac{c_{\mathrm{z}}}{2\,\rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}} \tag{20.45}\]

the following holds:

  1. The coordinate \(a_{\mathrm{n}}(t) = \int_0^L \rho A\,w(x, t)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) solves the oscillator equation
    \[\ddot a_{\mathrm{n}} + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}} + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,a_{\mathrm{n}} = F_{\mathrm{n}}(t) , \qquad F_{\mathrm{n}} = \int_0^L q\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{20.46}\]
  2. For \(q = 0\) the function \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x)\,a(t)\) is a solution of equation (20.44) with the same boundary conditions if and only if \(a\) solves equation (20.46) with \(F_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). So the mode shape \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not change.
  3. For \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} < 1\) this mode has the damped circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta_{\mathrm{n}}^2}\) and the envelope \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} \ge 1\) it does not oscillate (theorem 9.43).
Proof

We must show that the integral of the equation multiplied by the mode gives the oscillator equation, for which we need only the integration by parts of lemma 13.42. All steps are for a fixed time \(t\), except the derivatives in time.

  1. The derivative in time goes under the integral (lemma 4.19, the integrand and its derivative in \(t\) are continuous on the closed rectangle): \(\dot a_{\mathrm{n}} = \int_0^L \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ddot a_{\mathrm{n}} = \int_0^L \rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. We multiply equation (20.44) by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) and integrate over \([0, L]\): \(\ddot a_{\mathrm{n}} + EI\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{xxxx}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x + EI\,\tau_\varepsilon\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x + c_{\mathrm{z}}\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = F_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. Let \(u\) be either of the functions \(w(\cdot, t)\) or \(w_{\mathrm{t}}(\cdot, t)\). Both satisfy the boundary conditions (the conditions are linear and homogeneous, they hold for every \(t\), and the derivative in \(t\) is continuous). By lemma 13.42, \(\int_0^L EI\,u''''\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = B(u, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}) + b(u, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\), and \(b(u, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0\) because both satisfy self-adjoint conditions. The same lemma with the functions exchanged gives \(\int_0^L (EI\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}'''')\,u\,\mathrm{d}x = B(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}, u) + 0\). Since \(B\) is symmetric, \(\int_0^L EI\,u''''\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L EI\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}''''\,u\,\mathrm{d}x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\int_0^L \rho A\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. For \(u = w\) the last integral is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,a_{\mathrm{n}}\), for \(u = w_{\mathrm{t}}\) it is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}}\) (step 1). So the third term of step 2 is \(\tau_\varepsilon\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}}\), the second \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,a_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the fourth \((c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  5. We add up: \(\ddot a_{\mathrm{n}} + \big(\tau_\varepsilon\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A\big)\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}} + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,a_{\mathrm{n}} = F_{\mathrm{n}}\). We denote the coefficient of \(\dot a_{\mathrm{n}}\) by \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), which gives (20.45) and (20.46). This is point 1.
  6. Point 2: \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) satisfies the boundary conditions, so \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,a\) satisfies them too. For \(q = 0\) we insert \(w = \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,a\) and use \(EI\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}'''' = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\rho A\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\): the left-hand side of equation (20.44) is \(\rho A\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\big(\ddot a + (\tau_\varepsilon\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\dot a + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,a\big)\). Since \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not zero everywhere, this is zero if and only if equation (20.46) holds.
  7. Point 3 is theorem 9.43 with \(m = 1\), \(c = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(k = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).

Source: Not from a source, the theorem is our derivation. The same result for Rayleigh damping of finitely many degrees of freedom: lemma 10.46. The continuous system, which chapter 13 only mentions, has the same form with \(a_0 = c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A\) and \(a_1 = \tau_\varepsilon\).

We proved the theorem for any pair of self-adjoint boundary conditions, so also for the cantilever, for which chapter 13 does not prove the expansion in modes. Points 1 and 2 do not depend on the completeness of the modes. We need completeness only for the claim that every motion is a sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}} a_{\mathrm{n}}\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is proved for the simply supported beam (lemma 13.53) and for the cantilever stated after GR and CK (chapter 13). (figure 20.26)

The boundary conditions need attention. At a free and at a simply supported end the condition is \(M = 0\), that is \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tau_\varepsilon\,w_{\mathrm{xxt}} = 0\). In time this equation gives \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, t) = w_{\mathrm{xx}}(L, 0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\), which is zero only if it is zero already at \(t = 0\). The theorem therefore assumes that the initial deflection satisfies the condition \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\). If it does not, the remainder vanishes in a time of the order of \(\tau_\varepsilon\), for the cantilever of the example below \(9{,}8 \cdot 10^{-5}\) s, which is about one three-hundredth of the period of the first mode (\(2\pi/\omega_1 = 30{,}9\) ms).

Let us see what the theorem gives for the cantilever of example 13.38. Let the first mode have \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}01\) and the frequency ratios of chapter 13. The question is how strongly the other modes are damped under each of the mechanisms. (figure 20.26)

Example 20.52 Cantilever with internal or external damping (konzola z notranjim ali zunanjim dušenjem)

Cantilever \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(\omega_1 = 203{,}52\) 1/s, the ratios \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_1 = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2/x_1^2\) are \(1\), \(6{,}2669\), \(17{,}5475\), \(34{,}3861\), \(56{,}8426\), \(84{,}9130\), \(118{,}5975\), \(157{,}8961\) (roots from theorem 13.37, computed with SymPy and NumPy). We choose \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}01\).

  • External damping only (\(\tau_\varepsilon = 0\)): \(\zeta_1 = c_{\mathrm{z}}/(2\rho A\omega_1)\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{z}} = 2\zeta_1\rho A\omega_1 = 25{,}399\) N s/m\(^2\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = \zeta_1\,\omega_1/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\).

The same data as in the table are plotted. The horizontal axis carries the mode number \(r\) from \(1\) to \(8\). The vertical one is logarithmic, because \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) changes by four orders of magnitude, and shows the ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) (theorem 20.51, equation (20.45)). First internal damping: the points grow in proportion to \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) grows by a factor of \(6{,}27\) at \(r = 2\) and of \(17{,}5\) at \(r = 3\). Then the external dashpots: the points fall as \(1/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), so the higher modes are hardly damped at all, at \(r = 3\) only \(0{,}00057\). Finally the sum of both at \(\zeta_1 = \zeta_3 = 0{,}01\): it has its smallest value between \(r = 1\) and \(r = 3\) (\(0{,}0049\) at \(r = 2\)), because the external term \(c_{\mathrm{z}}/(2\rho A\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\) is already falling there, while the internal one \(\tau_\varepsilon\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2\) has not yet grown (lemma 10.46). The line at \(\zeta = 1\) separates oscillation from no oscillation. (figure 20.26)

Two conclusions. First: the choice of the mechanism, not only \(\zeta_1\), decides how strongly the higher tones are damped. With internal damping the third mode is damped \(17{,}5\) times more strongly than the first (\(\zeta_3 = 0{,}1755\) against \(0{,}01\)), with external damping \(17{,}5\) times more weakly (\(0{,}00057\)). The second conclusion is a reason for caution. With internal damping \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) reaches \(1\) at \(r = 7\) (\(\zeta_7 = 1{,}186\), \(\zeta_6 = 0{,}849\)). By theorem 9.43 a mode with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} > 1\) is overdamped, it no longer oscillates, and there are infinitely many such modes. (figure 20.26)

This is not an error of the proof but a property of the model. By lemma 20.37 the Kelvin-Voigt material has the loss factor \(\gamma = \Omega\tau_\varepsilon\), which grows without bound with \(\Omega\), while a metal has an almost constant \(\gamma\) over a wide band (section 20.6). We therefore use the model only for modes with a small \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\), for example \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} \le 0{,}1\), for the cantilever for \(r = 1\) and \(r = 2\) (\(\zeta_2 = 0{,}0627\) is below \(0{,}1\), \(\zeta_3 = 0{,}1755\) already above). For the simply supported beam \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n^2\omega_1\), so \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = n^2\zeta_1\), and \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}01\) gives \(\zeta_{10} = 1\): the modes with \(n \ge 10\) do not oscillate. External damping does not have this difficulty, but it has the opposite one: the higher modes are almost undamped, with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = \zeta_1/n^2\) we get \(\zeta_{10} = 10^{-4}\). (figure 20.26)

The theorem requires one thing that is hidden in the proof: the dashpot must be the same everywhere. Let us check what happens if it is not.

Example 20.53 External damper on the right half of the cantilever only (zunanji dušilnik le na desni polovici konzole)

We equip the same cantilever with dashpots \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) only on \([L/2, L]\) and leave the left half free. Steps 3 to 5 of the proof of theorem 20.51 still hold for the internal term, but the external term gives, in place of \((c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\,\dot a_{\mathrm{n}}\), a sum over all modes if we expand the motion in modes. The term \(\int c_{\mathrm{z}}(x)\,w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) is then \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dot a_{\mathrm{j}} \int c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). For a constant \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) all terms with \(j \ne n\) are zero by the orthogonality of the modes (theorem 13.43). For \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) only on the right half they are not. With normalized modes, \(\int_0^L\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 1\), the integrals \(G_{\mathrm{jn}} = \frac{1}{c_{\mathrm{z}}}\int c_{\mathrm{z}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) over \([L/2, L]\) are (NumPy, trapezoidal rule with \(2 \cdot 10^5\) points)

\[G = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}9492 & -0{,}1461 & -0{,}1142 \\ -0{,}1461 & 0{,}5587 & -0{,}4005 \\ -0{,}1142 & -0{,}4005 & 0{,}4929 \end{pmatrix} \tag{20.47}\]

for the modes \(1\) to \(3\), and the identity matrix for dashpots along the whole length. The coupling ratios \(G_{\mathrm{jn}}/\sqrt{G_{\mathrm{jj}}G_{\mathrm{nn}}}\) are \(-0{,}2006\) for the modes \(1\) and \(2\), \(-0{,}1670\) for \(1\) and \(3\), and \(-0{,}7632\) for \(2\) and \(3\).

Matrix (20.47) tells three things. The diagonal is the share that the dashpot on the right half receives from each mode: the first mode has \(0{,}95\) of its kinetic energy in the right half, near the end of the beam, the third only \(0{,}49\). Off the diagonal are numbers that do not exist for a constant \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\): the modes \(2\) and \(3\) are strongly coupled, with the coupling ratio \(-0{,}76\). This is damping that is not classical (section 10.9.3): the undamped modes can no longer be separated, the damped modes are complex and are given by the first-order form (theorem 20.49), and with finite elements the same happens if the elements have different \(\tau_\varepsilon\) or \(c_{\mathrm{z}}\) (theorem 20.40, point 2 holds only for equal coefficients). So theorem 20.51 is not "every damping keeps the modes" but "damping proportional to the mass or to the stiffness keeps the modes". (figure 20.25)

20.9.3 A dashpot at the end of a string: no modes

The third question of the opening leads to an extreme case of damping that is not distributed. We take the string of example 13.8: tension \(T = 70\) N, mass \(\mu = 9{,}802 \cdot 10^{-4}\) kg/m per unit length, length \(L = 0{,}65\) m and wave speed \(c = 267{,}24\) m/s (definition 13.4). The left end is fixed as before. The right end we attach to a massless ring (obroček) that slides without friction on a vertical guide, and a viscous dashpot \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) (definition 9.1) connects the ring to the ground. The massless ring is an idealization: a real ring of mass \(m\) would add the force \(m\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) to the balance. (figure 20.27)

We obtain the boundary condition at the right end from the balance of the ring. The string pulls it with the tension \(T\) along its own direction, and for a small slope the vertical component of this force is \(-T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\), as in theorem 13.3. The dashpot acts with the force \(-c_{\mathrm{d}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t)\), against the velocity. The ring has no mass, so the sum of the forces is zero:

\[T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) + c_{\mathrm{d}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t) = 0 . \tag{20.48}\]

For \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) this is the free end, \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). For a very large \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) the ring hardly moves, as a fixed end. In between there is one special value. A wave \(F(x - ct)\) travelling towards the right end (lemma 13.5) has \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = F'\) and \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -c\,F'\), so \(T\,w_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{d}}\,w_{\mathrm{t}} = (T - c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c)\,F'\). For \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c\) this is zero, the wave satisfies condition (20.48) by itself and the end returns nothing to it. (figure 20.27)

Lemma 20.54 Dashpot at the end of a string (dušilnik na koncu strune)

Let \(T > 0\), \(\mu > 0\), \(L > 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 0\), \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) and \(r = (T - c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c)/(T + c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c)\). Let \(F\) and \(G\) be twice continuously differentiable functions on \(\mathbb{R}\) and let \(w(x, t) = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)\) satisfy \(w(0, t) = 0\) and condition (20.48) for every \(t \ge 0\). Then:

  1. For every \(t \ge 0\), \(G(L + ct) - G(L) = r\,\big(F(L - ct) - F(L)\big)\): the wave that leaves the right end is \(r\) times the wave that arrives at it.
  2. For \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c\), \(r = 0\) and \(w(x, t) = 0\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every \(t \ge 2L/c\).
  3. For \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c\) the string has no mode: there is no complex number \(s\) and no twice continuously differentiable complex function \(\varphi\) on \([0, L]\) that is not zero everywhere such that \(w = \varphi(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) solves the equation \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (theorem 13.3) with \(w(0, t) = 0\) and condition (20.48).
Proof

We must show what the boundary condition does to the waves \(F\) and \(G\), and then look for modes as solutions of an ordinary differential equation.

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 4.43, as in the proof of lemma 13.5), \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = F'(x - ct) + G'(x + ct)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -c\,F'(x - ct) + c\,G'(x + ct)\). Condition (20.48) at \(x = L\) is therefore \((T - c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c)\,F'(L - ct) + (T + c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c)\,G'(L + ct) = 0\), that is \(G'(L + ct) = -r\,F'(L - ct)\) for every \(t \ge 0\).
  2. The function \(h(t) = G(L + ct) - r\,F(L - ct)\) has, by step 1, the derivative \(c\,G'(L + ct) + r\,c\,F'(L - ct) = 0\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40), \(h(t) - h(0) = \int_0^t h'\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), which is point 1.
  3. Point 2. For \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c\) we have \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\,c = T\) and \(r = 0\), so by step 2 \(G(u) = G(L)\) for every \(u \ge L\).
  4. The left end: \(w(0, t) = F(-ct) + G(ct) = 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\), so \(F(v) = -G(-v)\) for every \(v \le 0\).
  5. Let \(t \ge 2L/c\) and \(x \in [0, L]\). Then \(x + ct \ge 2L\), so \(G(x + ct) = G(L)\) by step 3. Since \(x - ct \le L - 2L < 0\), step 4 gives \(F(x - ct) = -G(ct - x)\), and since \(ct - x \ge 2L - L = L\), this is \(-G(L)\). The sum is \(w(x, t) = 0\).
  6. Point 3. We insert \(w = \varphi(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) into the wave equation: \(s^2\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = c^2\varphi''\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\). Since \(\mathrm{e}^{st} \ne 0\), \(\varphi'' - (s/c)^2\varphi = 0\) holds, \(w(0, t) = 0\) gives \(\varphi(0) = 0\), and condition (20.48) gives \(T\,\varphi'(L) + c_{\mathrm{d}}\,s\,\varphi(L) = 0\).
  7. For \(s = 0\) the characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2\) has the double root \(0\), so by theorem 9.39 \(\varphi(x) = A x + B\). From \(\varphi(0) = 0\) we get \(B = 0\), and the condition at the right end gives \(T A = 0\), so \(\varphi = 0\).
  8. For \(s \ne 0\) the characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2 - (s/c)^2\) has the distinct roots \(\pm s/c\), so by theorem 9.39 \(\varphi(x) = A\,\mathrm{e}^{sx/c} + B\,\mathrm{e}^{-sx/c}\) with complex \(A\) and \(B\). From \(\varphi(0) = A + B = 0\) we get \(\varphi(x) = A\,\big(\mathrm{e}^{sx/c} - \mathrm{e}^{-sx/c}\big)\).
  9. With \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c\) the left-hand side of the condition at the right end is \(\frac{Ts}{c}\,A\,\big(\mathrm{e}^{sL/c} + \mathrm{e}^{-sL/c}\big) + \frac{Ts}{c}\,A\,\big(\mathrm{e}^{sL/c} - \mathrm{e}^{-sL/c}\big) = \frac{2Ts}{c}\,A\,\mathrm{e}^{sL/c}\). Since \(T\), \(s\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{sL/c}\) are not zero, \(A = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\).

Source: Not from a source, the lemma is our derivation from lemma 13.5 and theorem 9.39.

For the string of example 13.8 the matched dashpot (uglašeni dušilnik) is \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/c = \sqrt{T\mu} = 0{,}26194\) N s/m. The string is then at rest after \(2L/c = 4{,}8645\) ms, no matter how we plucked it. The wave reaches the end and vanishes there, as if the string ran on to infinity (the first case below it). There are no modes, because no shape returns into itself by reflection. Two near cases show that this is a peculiarity of a single value. At \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = 2T/c\), \(r = (1 - 2)/(1 + 2) = -1/3\), and the reflected wave is inverted and three times lower. At \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = T/(2c)\), \(r = (1 - 1/2)/(1 + 1/2) = 1/3\), and the reflected wave is upright and three times lower. At the left end the wave is reflected with the factor \(-1\) (step 4 of the proof), so after every trip there and back, which takes \(2L/c\), the height of the wave is multiplied by \(-r\). After ten such trips, that is after \(48{,}6\) ms, \((1/3)^{10} = 1{,}7 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of the initial height remains. So with the matched dashpot there are no modes of the kind that theorem 20.51 gives for distributed damping, and the motion is described by reflections. (figure 20.27)

20.9.4 Forced vibration: the response at resonance is finite

Let us return to the opening question. In section 13.8.2, for the simply supported beam under the load \(Q(x)\cos\Omega t\), we looked for the steady response \(W(x)\cos\Omega t\) and obtained \(\rho A\,(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2)\,C_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{n}}\). At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) the left-hand side vanishes, so a solution is possible only for \(Q_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\). With damping, by theorem 20.51 (point 3), the free motion decreases with the envelope \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). For the first mode of the cantilever the time constant is \(1/(\zeta_1\omega_1) = 0{,}49\) s, for the simply supported beam of the example below \(0{,}175\) s. After a few such times only the steady state remains, so it makes sense to ask whether some load still excludes a steady response. For one degree of freedom a steady response exists for every harmonic force (theorem 9.52), for the beam we must show this for every mode. This time we write the steady response as the real part of a complex amplitude, because damping brings a phase lag that \(\cos\Omega t\) alone does not capture. (figure 20.26)

Corollary 20.55 Forced vibration of a simply supported beam with damping (prisiljeno nihanje prosto podprtega nosilca z dušenjem)

Let the simply supported beam of length \(L\) be that of lemma 20.50 with \(\tau_\varepsilon > 0\) or \(c_{\mathrm{z}} > 0\), so \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\) for every \(n\) (equation (20.45) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) from equation (13.50)). Let \(Q\) be a continuous real function, \(\Omega > 0\), and \(W\) a complex, four times continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) with \(W(0) = W(L) = W''(0) = W''(L) = 0\) that solves

\[-\rho A\,\Omega^2\,W + EI\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\tau_\varepsilon)\,W'''' + \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,c_{\mathrm{z}}\,W = Q . \tag{20.49}\]

Let \(C_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac2L\int_0^L W\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac2L\int_0^L Q\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x\). Then:

  1. The function \(w(x, t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(W(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big)\) solves equation (20.44) with \(q = Q\cos\Omega t\) and satisfies the boundary conditions.
  2. For every \(n \ge 1\),
    \[\rho A\,\big(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega\big)\,C_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{n}} . \tag{20.50}\]
    The factor in brackets is never zero, so \(C_{\mathrm{n}}\) is determined, the solution \(W\) is unique and is the sum \(W = \sum_{\mathrm{n}} C_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(n\pi x/L)\), uniformly on \([0, L]\).
  3. \(|C_{\mathrm{n}}| = |Q_{\mathrm{n}}|\,\big/\,\big(\rho A\sqrt{(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega)^2}\big)\), and at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(|C_{\mathrm{n}}| = |Q_{\mathrm{n}}|/(2\rho A\,\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2)\), which is finite.
Proof

We must show that \(w\) is a solution, then follow the same procedure as in the proof of theorem 13.46 with a complex factor. We split the function \(W\) into its real and imaginary parts, \(W = U + \mathrm{i}\,V\), because lemma 13.42 is for real functions, while all relations are linear.

  1. Point 1. By theorem 9.29, \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\Omega\,W\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \mathrm{Re}(-\Omega^2\,W\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xxxxt}} = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\Omega\,W''''\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\). The left-hand side of equation (20.44) is therefore \(\mathrm{Re}\big((\text{left-hand side of equation @eq-c20-prisiljeni-dusen})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\big) = \mathrm{Re}(Q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}) = Q\cos\Omega t\), because \(Q\) is real. The boundary conditions hold because they are linear and homogeneous.
  2. Let \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}} = \sin(n\pi x/L)\). This mode of the simply supported beam satisfies self-adjoint conditions and \(EI\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}'''' = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\rho A\,\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(U\) and \(V\), by step 3 of the proof of theorem 20.51, \(\int_0^L EI\,U''''\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\rho A\int_0^L U\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the same for \(V\), so \(\int_0^L EI\,W''''\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\rho A\,\tfrac L2\,C_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. We multiply equation (20.49) by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) and integrate, where \(\int_0^L W\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac L2\,C_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\int_0^L Q\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac L2\,Q_{\mathrm{n}}\): \(\tfrac L2\,\big[-\rho A\,\Omega^2 + \rho A\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_\varepsilon) + \mathrm{i}\Omega\,c_{\mathrm{z}}\big]\,C_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac L2\,Q_{\mathrm{n}}\). The expression in square brackets is \(\rho A\,\big[\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\,(\tau_\varepsilon\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + c_{\mathrm{z}}/\rho A)\big] = \rho A\,[\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega]\) by equation (20.45). This is (20.50).
  4. The imaginary part of the factor is \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega > 0\), so the factor is not zero and \(C_{\mathrm{n}} = Q_{\mathrm{n}}/\big(\rho A\,(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega)\big)\). Two different \(W\) would have the same coefficients, hence the same expansion (theorem 13.45, applied to \(U\) and \(V\), as in theorem 13.46), so the solution is unique.
  5. Point 3: the absolute value of a quotient is the quotient of the absolute values, \(|a + \mathrm{i}b| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\) for real \(a\), \(b\). At \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(a = 0\) and \(b = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).

Source: Not from a source, the corollary is our derivation. Equation (20.50) is equation (13.53) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2\) replaced by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega\), as for the oscillator with one degree of freedom (theorem 9.52).

The corollary closes the opening question: with damping, equation (20.50) determines every coefficient \(C_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every load \(Q\), also at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) with \(Q_{\mathrm{m}} \ne 0\), and every one is finite. The corollary assumes that the solution \(W\) itself exists, and for the uniform load the closed solution in the example below shows it. Without damping (\(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\)) the factor vanishes at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) and we get the condition \(Q_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) of theorem 13.46: this is the test that the undamped model does not pass. For one mode, \(|C_{\mathrm{n}}|\) at resonance is \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}}|/(\rho A\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2)\cdot 1/(2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}})\), that is the static coefficient times \(1/(2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}})\), which is the dynamic factor at \(r = 1\) (theorem 9.54, point 3). (figure 20.26)

Example 20.56 Simply supported beam under an oscillating uniform load (prosto podprt nosilec pod enakomerno silo, ki niha)

A steel beam \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, the load \(q_0\cos\Omega t\) with \(q_0 = 1000\) N/m. The frequencies are \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n^2\omega_1\) with \(\omega_1 = 571{,}28\) 1/s. The static deflection of the midpoint is \(5q_0L^4/(384EI) = 0{,}6228\) mm. Let \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}01\) and the damping either internal, \(\tau_\varepsilon = 2\zeta_1/\omega_1 = 3{,}5009 \cdot 10^{-5}\) s (\(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}01\,n^2\)), or external, \(c_{\mathrm{z}} = 71{,}296\) N s/m\(^2\) (\(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}01/n^2\)). The load coefficients are \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = 4q_0/(n\pi)\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even \(n\).

\(0{,}5\,\omega_1\) \(0{,}8311\) mm \(0{,}8311\) mm \(1{,}334\)
\(\omega_1\) \(31{,}2604\) mm \(31{,}2605\) mm \(50{,}19\)
\(\omega_3 = 9\,\omega_1\) \(0{,}0161\) mm \(1{,}1578\) mm \(0{,}026\) and \(1{,}859\) respectively

The row at \(\omega_1\) is as expected: the first mode has \(c_1 = Q_1/(\rho A\omega_1^2) = 0{,}6252\) mm and at resonance the factor \(1/(2\zeta_1) = 50\), which gives \(31{,}26\) mm. Both kinds of damping give the same response, because they have the same \(\zeta_1\). The row at \(0{,}5\,\omega_1\) is equal for the same reason: \(2\zeta_1 r = 0{,}01\) is negligible there against \(1 - r^2 = 0{,}75\). At \(\omega_3\), however, the responses differ by a factor of \(72\). The third mode has \(\zeta_3 = 0{,}09\) with internal and \(\zeta_3 = 0{,}00111\) with external damping, a ratio of \(81\). At resonance its contribution is \(c_3/(2\zeta_3)\) with \(c_3 = 0{,}00257\) mm, that is \(0{,}0143\) mm and \(1{,}1578\) mm respectively. The first mode contributes the background \(c_1/(1 - 81) = -0{,}00782\) mm, which is real, while the contribution of the third mode at resonance is imaginary (phase lag \(\pi/2\), theorem 9.54). With internal damping they therefore add like the legs of a right triangle, approximately \(\sqrt{0{,}0143^2 + 0{,}00782^2} = 0{,}0163\) mm, while the sum of the series with all modes is \(0{,}0161\) mm. The closed solution of the beam with the complex wave number \(\beta = \big((\rho A\,\Omega^2 - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,c_{\mathrm{z}})/(EI\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\Omega\tau_\varepsilon))\big)^{1/4}\) and the series with \(4001\) terms agree to six digits (NumPy). The example shows the same as the figure of the ratios: damping with the same \(\zeta_1\) can decide, at a higher mode, between \(0{,}026\) times and \(1{,}86\) times the static deflection.
