Chapter 20 Damping models

20.1 Where the energy of a vibration goes and how we measure it

Cantilever A does come to rest after a blow, and in chapter 9 we described this with a viscous damper, chosen so that \(\zeta = 0{,}01\). No real damper hangs on it. So what does take the energy, and how do we compare causes as different as friction in the clamp, the air around the mass and the inside of the steel? This section looks for one common measure, with which we shall judge every model in the following sections.

Let us draw cantilever A (example 9.9). The bar is clamped into the wall, which is tied to the ground. At its end is the mass \(m\), which after a blow moves up and down with velocity \(\dot q\). Three things can take energy. The first is the joint at the wall: two plates and two bolts hold the bar, and at every swing it slips a little against the plates. The force of dry friction has constant size and points against the slip (section 9.14 of chapter 9, theorem 9.150), so during slip it takes energy. We draw the force \(f_{\mathrm{t}}\) as a double arrow in the gap between plate and bar, because the slip changes direction as the bar swings. The second is the air: the mass pushes the air in front of it, and by the law of action and reaction (Newton's third law) the air pushes the mass back with an equal force. When the mass moves up, the air acts on its top face downwards, so the force \(f_{\mathrm{z}}\) is drawn downwards and ends on that face. The third is the material of the bar. The bar bends as it swings, every piece of steel is stretched and compressed, and this loss is there without joints and without air. TIMO (PDF p. 297) says that there are systems in which the damping of vibration depends mainly on the internal friction (notranje trenje) of the material.

Which of the three prevails in A, these words do not tell. We only know that all three have the same consequence: in every period the vibration loses some energy. That consequence can be measured whatever the cause, and it will be our common measure. First, though, let us see what happens in the material, where no layer slides.

20.1.1 Loss in the material: heat between grains

What loses energy in a piece of steel that we stretch and release? TIMO (PDF pp. 295, 296) describes an experiment with a crystal that is stretched with a pulling force while its temperature is observed.

On the axes we measure the elongation \(q\) horizontally and the pulling force \(P\) vertically. When we pull very slowly, the crystal keeps the temperature of its surroundings all the time, and the force grows along the dashed line \(0\) to \(2\), whose slope is proportional to the modulus of elasticity at constant temperature (the isothermal modulus \(E_{\mathrm{iz}}\), definition 3.1). When we pull quickly, no heat escapes and the crystal cools. The quick pull is the segment \(0\) to \(1\), with a larger slope, the adiabatic modulus \(E_{\mathrm{ad}} > E_{\mathrm{iz}}\) (adiabatic means without exchange of heat). Why the larger slope? The cooled bar wants to contract, as a steel rail contracts in winter, so under the same force it is shorter than the warm one: at the corner \(1\) the elongation is smaller than at \(2\) at the same height, and the difference is the segment \(1\) to \(2\). So only a larger force brings it to the same elongation. At \(1\) we stop and hold the force constant. The cooled bar slowly warms to the temperature of its surroundings and therefore stretches a little more, which is the horizontal line \(1\) to \(2\). Now we release the load suddenly. The bar contracts along the line \(2\) to \(3\), parallel to \(0\) to \(1\), because this contraction again has no exchange of heat. It warms up in doing so, so after the release it cools to the temperature of its surroundings and contracts a further segment \(3\) to \(0\). The cycle returns to its start and is a parallelogram. At the corner \(3\) the force is zero but the elongation still positive, so the parallelogram leans to the right.

Why is the area of the parallelogram a loss of energy? The work we do on the bar at elongation \(q\) and force \(P\) is the time integral of \(P\,\dot q\), and this is the area under the graph of \(P\) against \(q\) (fundamental theorem of calculus, theorem 3.55). On loading the graph lies above the graph on unloading, so the difference of the two areas is the area of the parallelogram. We prove this in detail in the next section (lemma 20.2). The bar acts with force \(-P\) on whatever pulls it (Newton's third law), in vibration on the mass. So in these axes the loop of the force on the mass is turned over compared with figure 20.5, but the area is the same. This energy does not go into the bar as elastic energy, since after the cycle the bar is back in the same state. It ends as heat that the bar gives to its surroundings. TIMO (PDF p. 296) writes that the difference between the adiabatic and the isothermal modulus is usually small, for steel about a third of one per cent (footnote 1 on the same page), so the loss of energy per cycle is very small too, but in vibration many cycles follow one another, and such losses are what is called internal friction and damp the motion. In reality heat is exchanged all the time, and instead of the parallelogram we get a rounded loop.

How small is "very small" for steel? Let us count on figure 20.4. The parallelogram has the base \(1\) to \(2\) and the height \(P\). The stiffness of the bar is proportional to the modulus, so for \(E_{\mathrm{ad}} = (1 + 1/300)\,E_{\mathrm{iz}}\) the elongation at \(1\) is \(q_2/(1 + 1/300)\), where \(q_2\) is the elongation at \(2\), and the base is \(q_2/301\). The area of the parallelogram is therefore \(P\,q_2/301\), and the elastic energy under the dashed line up to \(2\) is \(\tfrac12 P\,q_2\). In this cycle \(2/301 = 0{,}66\ \%\) of the stored energy is lost (the calculation is ours, from the number in the footnote). The figure therefore exaggerates the difference of the slopes strongly: to scale, the parallelogram would be narrower than the line it is drawn with.

Steel is not a single crystal but many grains with differently oriented crystal lattices. TIMO (PDF pp. 296, 297) says that the cooling during stretching depends on the orientation of the crystal, so temperatures differ from grain to grain. The figure shows two grains with temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\). The grain with the lower temperature \(T_2\) is shaded more strongly, the grain \(T_1\) is cooled less. Heat flows from the warmer to the colder grain across their common boundary, the arrow, and the temperatures even out. This is an additional flow of heat, which a single crystal would not have, and it increases the loss of energy.

How can the loss be increased? By TIMO (PDF p. 297) the heat generated in a grain is proportional to its volume and the exchange of heat to its surface, so the evening out is easier and the loss larger when the grains are made smaller. Let us count on a cube of side \(2d\) and on the same cube cut into eight cubes of side \(d\), drawn in the plane as a square and four squares. The volume is \(8d^3\) in both cases. The surface of the whole cube is \(6\,(2d)^2 = 24\,d^2\), the surface of the eight cubes \(8 \cdot 6d^2 = 48\,d^2\). The surface across which heat flows is thus twice as large when the grains are twice as small. In the plane of the figure the perimeter \(8d\) grows to \(16d\) in the same way. That is why, in systems whose damping depends mainly on the internal friction of the material, we increase the damping with a material of small grains (TIMO, PDF p. 297).

From history

Losses of energy in metals were measured before their cause was known. TIMO (PDF p. 297, footnote 1) reports that the damping capacity (zmožnost dušenja) of various materials was measured by Rowett (1913), and later by Otto Föppl and his collaborators at the Wöhler Institute in Brunswick (1926 to 1929). A measurement tells how much energy a material loses, not where. The importance of the thermoelastic cause of internal friction was shown by the investigations of C. Zener and his associates (1937 to 1941), that is, the flow of heat of figure 20.4. Zener collected the results in the book "Elasticity and Anelasticity of Metals" (1948).

Let us return to the grains and the loop in the material of figure 20.4. All three causes of figure 20.3 have the same consequence: the force that the member exerts on the mass is not a function of the displacement alone, but differs on loading and on unloading. We now write this down exactly.

20.1.2 Work in a period is the area of the loop

What should we measure? The loss of energy in one period of the motion, because this is a number that can be read off a graph of force against displacement whatever the cause. Take one degree of freedom with mass \(m\), coordinate \(q\) and a member that acts on the mass with force \(f\) in the direction of \(q\) (a spring, a damper, a joint or the material, or all at once). Chapter 9 did this for the damper.

Definition 20.1 Energy lost per period (izguba energije v periodi)

Let \(q(t)\) be a continuously differentiable motion with period \(T > 0\), that is \(q(t + T) = q(t)\), and \(f(t)\) a piecewise continuous force (definition 6.8), with which the member acts on the mass in the direction of \(q\), and which also has period \(T\). The energy lost per period (izguba energije v periodi) is the number

\[W_{\mathrm{izg}} = -\int_0^T f(t)\,\dot q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{20.1}\]

that is, minus the work of the force \(f\) in one period (definition 8.59, the power is \(f\,\dot q\)). The unit is the joule. In the figure of loops it is the shaded area in (b).

Source: Loss per period as the work of the damping force: CK section 4.7 (PDF p. 126), equations (4.70) and (4.72). The definition with a sign that makes the loss of a damper positive is ours.

Why this name? When the mass is in the same state after a period, it has the same velocity, so the total work of all forces on it in the period is zero. From \(m\,\ddot q = f + Q\), where \(Q\) is the external force, we get after multiplying by \(\dot q\) and integrating over the period \(\big[\tfrac12 m\,\dot q^2\big]_0^T = 0 = \int_0^T f\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_0^T Q\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.55). The external force thus supplies exactly \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) in a period, as the loop of the driving force shows. This is the energy that a force must keep supplying for the motion to stay periodic, as at resonance in chapter 9 (theorem 9.73).

Now let us draw \(f\) against \(q\) over one period. For the damper this is the ellipse of figure 9.75. We ask the same question of every member we know.

We draw the axes: the displacement \(q\) horizontally and the force \(f\) vertically, in each of the four panels. First the spring. Its force is \(-kq\) and it is a single line, since on rising and on falling at the same \(q\) it acts the same. The loop has no area, so a spring loses no energy at any amplitude. In (b) we add a viscous damper: the total force \(-kq - c\,\dot q\) runs along an ellipse, tilted by the spring (the dashed line \(-kq\) is its axis), and the shaded area is \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\). The ellipse is run through counter-clockwise. In (c) is dry friction: on rising the force is \(-F_{\mathrm{t}}\), on falling \(+F_{\mathrm{t}}\), so the loop is a rectangle with sides \(2X\) and \(2F_{\mathrm{t}}\). In (d) is the driving force \(Q_0\cos\Omega t\) at resonance against the displacement \(X\sin\Omega t\). This too is an ellipse, but it is run through clockwise, while the damping force runs the other way. Why does the direction matter? Because the area with its sign is the work, as the lemma that follows proves.

We prove the statement for every motion in which the displacement rises once and falls once in a period. Harmonic motion is such, since the point goes round one loop. For others, for instance with two peaks in a period, the curve of force against displacement can make several loops, definition 20.1 still holds, only reading off a single area is lost.

Let us draw the idea of the proof before we write it. Take the axes \(q\) and \(f\) as before and a loop that is neither an ellipse nor a rectangle, so that we use nothing special. In (a) the displacement goes from \(-X\) to \(X\) and the point \((q, f)\) runs to the right along the lower branch \(F_\uparrow\). The work of the force on this way is the area under the branch, because the displacement grows and the force is positive (we drew the branches above the axis so that the areas can be seen, the lemma does not require it). The upper branch is only dashed here, because we do not walk along it yet. In (b) the displacement returns from \(X\) to \(-X\) along the upper branch \(F_\downarrow\), and the lower one is dashed. The force points the same way as before but the displacement the opposite way, so the work is negative: minus the area under the upper branch. In (c) we add the two works. The part under the lower branch enters once with a plus and once with a minus and cancels. What remains is minus the area between the branches: the work of the force in a period is minus the area of the loop, and the loss \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the area of the loop. The point went round the loop counter-clockwise. A narrow upright strip at one \(q\) has the height \(F_\downarrow - F_\uparrow\), and the area of the loop is made of such strips. The lemma says this precisely, and the proof justifies every step by the fundamental theorem.

Lemma 20.2 Work in a period and the area of the loop (delo v periodi in ploščina zanke)

Let \(q\) be a continuously differentiable function with period \(T\) that strictly increases on \([0, t_1]\) from \(-X\) to \(X\) and strictly decreases on \([t_1, T]\) from \(X\) to \(-X\), with \(X > 0\). Let \(f\) be piecewise continuous and let there be continuous functions \(F_\uparrow\), \(F_\downarrow\) on \([-X, X]\) with \(f(t) = F_\uparrow(q(t))\) for \(0 \le t < t_1\) and \(f(t) = F_\downarrow(q(t))\) for \(t_1 < t < T\). Then:

  1. \(\displaystyle\int_0^T f\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X}\big(F_\uparrow(q) - F_\downarrow(q)\big)\,\mathrm{d}q\). This is the difference of the area under the lower branch and the area under the upper branch.
  2. If \(F_\downarrow \ge F_\uparrow\) on \([-X, X]\), then \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the area between the graphs of \(F_\downarrow\) and \(F_\uparrow\), that is the area of the loop that the point \((q(t), f(t))\) goes round counter-clockwise. If \(F_\downarrow \le F_\uparrow\), then \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is minus the area of the loop that the point goes round clockwise, and the member supplies energy.
  3. If we add the same continuous function \(G(q)\) to both branches, the integral in item 1 does not change. In particular, a force \(f = G(q)\) that depends on the displacement alone (a spring) has \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 0\).
Proof

We must show three items. The first we get by recognizing on each branch the integral as a difference of values of an antiderivative, the second from the meaning of the difference of graphs, the third from item 1.

  1. Let \(\Phi_\uparrow(y) = \int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{y} F_\uparrow(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), which is the area under the lower branch from \(-X\) to \(y\). By the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 2) \(\Phi_\uparrow\) is differentiable with derivative \(F_\uparrow\), because \(F_\uparrow\) is continuous.
  2. On the open interval \((0, t_1)\), that is on the way up, the values \(q(t)\) lie in the open interval \((-X, X)\), where \(\Phi_\uparrow\) has the continuous derivative \(F_\uparrow\). By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 3) the function \(t \mapsto \Phi_\uparrow(q(t))\) is therefore differentiable on \((0, t_1)\) with derivative \(F_\uparrow(q(t))\,\dot q(t)\). The function is continuous on \([0, t_1]\), being a composition of continuous functions, and its derivative extends continuously to \([0, t_1]\), being a product of continuous functions.
  3. By the fundamental theorem (item 1) \(\int_0^{t_1} F_\uparrow(q)\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = \Phi_\uparrow(q(t_1)) - \Phi_\uparrow(q(0)) = \Phi_\uparrow(X) - \Phi_\uparrow(-X) = \int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X} F_\uparrow(q)\,\mathrm{d}q\), because \(\Phi_\uparrow(-X) = 0\). This is the whole area in (a).
  4. On the falling branch, with \(\Phi_\downarrow(y) = \int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{y} F_\downarrow\), we get in the same way \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{T} F_\downarrow(q)\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = \Phi_\downarrow(q(T)) - \Phi_\downarrow(q(t_1)) = \Phi_\downarrow(-X) - \Phi_\downarrow(X) = -\int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X} F_\downarrow\,\mathrm{d}q\). The minus comes from going from \(X\) back to \(-X\): this is the area in (b) with a minus.
  5. The points \(t_1\) and \(T\) do not change the integral (definition 6.9), so the sum of steps 3 and 4 is item 1, picture (c).
  6. For item 2, \(F_\downarrow - F_\uparrow \ge 0\) is the height of the region between the graphs at \(q\), so \(\int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X}(F_\downarrow - F_\uparrow)\,\mathrm{d}q\) is its area. From item 1 and definition 20.1, \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = -\int_0^T f\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t\) is this area. As \(q\) increases the point \((q, f)\) runs along the lower graph \(F_\uparrow\) from left to right, and as \(q\) decreases along the upper graph \(F_\downarrow\) from right to left, that is counter-clockwise. For \(F_\downarrow \le F_\uparrow\) the graphs are swapped and the direction reversed.
  7. For item 3, after adding \(G\) both branches are raised by the same number at every \(q\), and the difference \(F_\uparrow - F_\downarrow\) is the same. In figure 20.5 (b), \(G = -kq\) is the dashed line that tilts the ellipse. For \(F_\uparrow = F_\downarrow = G\) the difference is zero.

Source: Work of the damping force as the area of a loop: CK section 4.7 (PDF p. 127, figure 4.17). The proof is not from the sources.

Let us see what the proof says, and test it on what we know. For the ellipse of chapter 9 take \(q = -X\cos\Omega t\), which increases from \(-X\) to \(X\) on \([0, T/2]\). The damper force \(-c\,\dot q = -c\,\Omega X\sin\Omega t\) equals \(F_\uparrow(q) = -c\,\Omega\sqrt{X^2 - q^2}\), because \(\sin\Omega t \ge 0\) on this branch, and on the falling branch \(F_\downarrow(q) = +c\,\Omega\sqrt{X^2 - q^2}\). The difference is \(F_\downarrow - F_\uparrow = 2c\,\Omega\sqrt{X^2 - q^2}\), which by the lemma has area \(2c\,\Omega\cdot\tfrac12\pi X^2 = \pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\), since \(\int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X}\sqrt{X^2 - q^2}\,\mathrm{d}q\) is the area of a half disc of radius \(X\) (the area of a disc is \(\pi X^2\)). This is exactly the loss of theorem 9.73.

The first loop of figure 20.5, the spring alone, is the case that the lemma tells apart from the damper, although \(-kq\) is also a force in the direction of \(q\). For \(-kq\) we have \(F_\uparrow = F_\downarrow = -kq\), area zero and \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 0\) by item 3. That is why the loop of the total force \(-kq - c\,\dot q\) in (b) has the same area as the loop of the damping force alone, the ellipse, as item 3 says: the spring only tilts the ellipse and does not change its area.

The opposite case is the driving force in (d). At resonance \(q = X\sin\Omega t\) and \(Q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\), a loop run clockwise, \(F_\downarrow \le F_\uparrow\). By item 2, \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is minus the area, that is \(-\pi\,Q_0 X = -1{,}0018\) J. A member that goes round its loop in this way is not a damper but a source of energy. At resonance the driving force has exactly the same ellipse as the damping force, only in the opposite direction, which is the balance \(Q_0 = c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\) of chapter 9. The sign of \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) separates a damper from a source.

For a motion with two peaks in a period the hypothesis of the lemma fails already at the branches: at the same \(q\) the force can differ on the two rises, so there is no single function \(F_\uparrow\) such as the lower branch. The loss is then computed directly from equation (20.1). We need the lemma only where we want to read the number off a picture.

20.1.3 Loss factor

How do we compare the loss between systems that have different stiffnesses and amplitudes? By dividing by the energy stored in the system.

Let us draw both energies in one picture, for cantilever A at resonance and this time to scale. On the axes are the displacement \(q\) and the force \(f\) on the mass. The spring line \(-kq\) goes from \(-X\) to \(X\) and at \(X\) reaches \(1000\) N. At the largest displacement \(X\) the mass has zero velocity, all the energy is in the spring and equals \(U = \tfrac12 kX^2\), the stored energy (shranjena energija). In the picture this is the triangle between the spring line and the \(q\) axis with legs \(X\) and \(kX\), as CK shows it in figure 4.17 (PDF p. 127): the work we do when we slowly stretch the spring from \(0\) to \(X\) is the area under its force. The loop of the total force of spring and damper is hardly thicker than the line at this scale, so we magnify the middle ten times. In (b) we see that the branches of the loop run \(20\) N below the spring line and \(20\) N above it, which is the largest damper force \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\).

Why does the definition that follows divide by \(2\pi U\) and not just by \(U\)? Let us compare the two areas in figure 20.7. The loop is the ellipse of the damper with semi-axes \(X\) and \(c\,\Omega X\), which the spring only tilted without changing its area (lemma 20.2, item 3), so \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \pi \cdot X \cdot c\,\Omega X\). The triangle has \(U = \tfrac12 \cdot X \cdot kX\). The ratio is

\[\frac{W_{\mathrm{izg}}}{U} = 2\pi\,\frac{c\,\Omega X}{kX} .\]

The number \(2\pi\) comes from the shapes, \(\pi\) from the ellipse and \(2\) from the triangle. When we remove it, what remains is the ratio of two forces that we read off the picture: the largest damper force to the largest spring force, for A \(20/1000 = 0{,}02\). Counting phase says the same: \(2\pi\) is the number of radians in one period, so \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}/2\pi\) is the loss per radian. For a viscous damper at resonance \(2\pi U/W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 1/(2\zeta)\) (corollary 9.75), a number we already know.

Definition 20.3 Loss factor (faktor izgub)

Let the motion be periodic with amplitude \(X > 0\), let the loss per period have the value \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) (definition 20.1), and let \(k > 0\) be the stiffness of the spring that holds the mass. The loss factor (faktor izgub) is the dimensionless number

\[\gamma = \frac{W_{\mathrm{izg}}}{2\pi\,U} , \qquad U = \tfrac12 k X^2 . \tag{20.2}\]

It can depend on the mechanism, on the amplitude \(X\) and on the frequency \(\Omega\) of the motion, while the mass does not appear in the definition. In the figure of stored energy, \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the loop and \(U\) the triangle.

Source: CK (4.79) (PDF p. 129) introduces \(\gamma\) as the structural damping factor and compares it with the number \(2\zeta r\) of viscous damping (PDF p. 129, (4.82), (4.83)), but does not relate it to the loss of energy. Here we derive it from the energy, and the ratio \(2\pi U/W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is that of chapter 9 (corollary 9.75).

The name says what share of the stored energy is lost: the loop is \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 2\pi\gamma\,U\), where \(U\) is the triangle. In chapter 1 \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the engineering shear strain, but in this chapter \(\gamma\) is always the loss factor.

Lemma 20.4 Loss factor of a viscous damper (faktor izgub viskoznega dušilnika)

Let a system with mass \(m\), stiffness \(k\) and viscous damper \(c\) (definition 9.1) move steadily as \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\), let \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and let \(\zeta\) be the damping ratio (definition 9.5). Then

\[\gamma = \frac{c\,\Omega}{k} = 2\zeta\,r . \tag{20.3}\]

At A, \(c\,\Omega/k\) is the ratio of the largest damper force to the largest spring force. At resonance (\(r = 1\)) we have \(\gamma = 2\zeta = 1/D(1)\) with the dynamic factor \(D\) (definition 9.64), so \(2\pi U/W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 1/\gamma\) (corollary 9.75).

Proof

We must show equation (20.3) and its special case.

  1. By theorem 9.73 the loss per period is \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\), because the mean power lost is \(\tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2X^2\) and \(T = 2\pi/\Omega\).
  2. By definition 20.3 (loop over triangle), \(\gamma = \pi\,c\,\Omega X^2/(2\pi\cdot\tfrac12 kX^2) = c\,\Omega/k\).
  3. From \(c = 2\zeta m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) (definition 9.5) we get \(c\,\Omega/k = 2\zeta\,\Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\zeta\,r\).
  4. At \(r = 1\), \(\gamma = 2\zeta\), and \(D(1) = 1/(2\zeta)\) by corollary 9.75.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. CK (4.77), (4.78) (PDF p. 128) for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is the same calculation.

Let us try it on A, in figure 20.7. For viscous \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) and resonance, \(X = 15{,}944\) mm, the loop has \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 1{,}0018\) J and the triangle \(U = \tfrac12\cdot 62\,720\cdot 0{,}015944^2 = 7{,}9719\) J. So \(\gamma = 1{,}0018/(2\pi\cdot 7{,}9719) = 0{,}0200 = 2\zeta\), as the lemma says. The loss per period is \(2\pi\gamma = 12{,}6\ \%\) of the stored energy: the area of the thin loop is an eighth of the area of the triangle. Since \(\gamma = c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/k = c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X/(kX)\), \(1/\gamma = 50\) is also the ratio of the largest spring force \(kX = 1000\) N to the largest damper force \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X = 20\) N. The loop is thin and shows that the damping is small (on this rests the replacement by an equivalent viscous damper, treated in the next section). In figure 20.5 we drew it exaggeratedly wide in (b), so that the area can be seen.

Example 20.5 Loss factor of viscous damping and of friction on cantilever A (faktor izgub viskoznega dušenja in trenja pri konzoli A)

Cantilever A (\(k = 62\,720\) N/m, \(m = 50\) kg, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s) vibrates at resonance with amplitude \(X\). Its loops are the ellipse of the viscous damper and the rectangle of friction.

  1. Viscous, \(\zeta = 0{,}01\): \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2 = 1{,}0018\) J at \(X = 15{,}944\) mm. The loss factor is \(\gamma = 0{,}0200\). At \(r = 0{,}5\) we get \(\gamma = 0{,}0100\), at \(r = 2\) we get \(0{,}0400\), at any amplitude the same (by lemma 20.4 it depends on \(r\) alone). The loop is a thin ellipse.
  2. Friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N: on rising \(F_\uparrow = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\), on falling \(F_\downarrow = +F_{\mathrm{t}}\), so by lemma 20.2 \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \int_{-\mathrm{X}}^{X}\big(F_\downarrow - F_\uparrow\big)\,\mathrm{d}q = 2F_{\mathrm{t}}\cdot 2X = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\), the rectangle with sides \(2X\) and \(2F_{\mathrm{t}}\). At \(X = 15{,}944\) mm this is \(0{,}1276\) J, and \(\gamma = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X/(\pi k X^2) = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi k X) = 0{,}00255\). That is \(12{,}7\ \%\) of the loss of the viscous damping.
  3. The same friction at smaller amplitudes: \(X = 5\) mm gives \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 0{,}0400\) J, \(U = 0{,}784\) J, \(\gamma = 0{,}00812\). \(X = 1\) mm gives \(0{,}00800\) J, \(0{,}03136\) J, \(\gamma = 0{,}0406\). The friction rectangle at smaller \(X\) is lower and narrower, the triangle much smaller.

The third item shows the difference, so let us draw the numbers of the example. On the axes the amplitude \(X\) in millimetres is horizontal and the loss factor vertical. The viscous damper gives the horizontal line at \(0{,}02\), because \(\gamma\) does not depend on the amplitude. Friction gives three points: \(0{,}00255\) at \(15{,}944\) mm, \(0{,}00812\) at \(5\) mm and \(0{,}0406\) at \(1\) mm. They lie on the curve \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi kX)\), proportional to \(1/X\): the smaller the vibration, the larger the share of energy lost. Why? The rectangle of friction has the constant height \(2F_{\mathrm{t}}\), so its area grows with \(X\) (picture (c)), while the triangle \(U\) has both legs proportional to \(X\) and grows with \(X^2\) (the triangle). At the amplitude of resonance friction is almost eight times weaker than the viscous damper, at \(X = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi k\cdot 2\zeta) = 2{,}03\) mm they are equal, and below this amplitude friction prevails. So for friction there is no single number \(\gamma\), only a number at a given amplitude. The two mechanisms also differ in what they depend on: for the viscous one the loss per period is proportional to \(\Omega X^2\), for friction to \(X\) and independent of the frequency. What this means for the equation of motion and when we may replace the loop by an ellipse is told by the next section (section 20.2).

For steel itself TIMO gives no loss factor on these pages (PDF pp. 296, 297). From the difference of the moduli we got only the loss of \(0{,}66\ \%\) in the cycle with the parallelogram, which by definition 20.3 would give \(\gamma = 0{,}0066/2\pi = 0{,}0011\), almost twenty times less than \(0{,}02\) for A. This is an order of magnitude for one slow cycle, not a measurement of vibration. We shall use the loss factor to judge all the models of this chapter: the viscous one (section 20.2), the hysteretic one (section 20.3) and the viscoelastic models of the material (section 20.4).
