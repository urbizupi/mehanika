Chapter 22 Shells

22.5 Applications of membrane theory (uporabe membranske teorije)

In section 22.4 we proved membrane theory for shells of revolution: under an axisymmetric load the forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) are fixed by equilibrium alone, without displacements. Now we apply it to four examples that everyone knows from daily life: a gas cylinder or a pipe with oil under pressure, a water tank, a dome and the conical head of a vessel. For each we ask how much stress there is in the wall and test the answer on numbers. Then we prove how good the answer is by comparing it with the Lamé solution from chapter 11, and we show two cases where membrane theory fails: at a clamped edge and at a ring squeezed by two forces.

Let us recall what theorem 22.32 gives us, with figure 22.42 from that section. The shell is a surface of revolution with the axis \(z\) pointing up. Its wall has thickness \(h\), and its middle surface has the principal radii of curvature \(R_1\) (along the meridian) and \(R_2\) (along the parallel). A point of the middle surface is given by the angle \(\varphi\) between the axis and the normal and by the distance \(r = R_2 \sin\varphi\) from the axis (in the figure these are \(\varphi_0\) and \(r_0\)). The membrane state has only forces per unit length in the plane of the surface: \(N_\varphi\) along the meridian and \(N_\theta\) along the parallel, both in N/mm, positive as tension. The stress is \(N/h\), uniform across the thickness. The theorem gives two equations. The first is the equilibrium of a cap, the part of the shell above a parallel of radius \(r\), in the direction of the axis:

\[2\pi\,r\,\sin\varphi\;N_\varphi = Z(\varphi) , \tag{22.36}\]

where \(Z(\varphi)\) is the resultant of the external loads on the cap in the direction of the axis \(z\) (in the figure \(P_{\mathrm{z}}\)), and the left side is the sum of the axial components of the force \(N_\varphi\) around the circumference \(2\pi r\). The second is equilibrium in the direction of the normal,

\[\frac{N_\varphi}{R_1} + \frac{N_\theta}{R_2} = p_{\mathrm{n}} , \tag{22.37}\]

where \(p_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the load per unit area in the direction of the outward normal. For a cylinder \(\varphi = \pi/2\) and \(1/R_1 = 0\). The force \(N_\varphi\) of a cylinder is called the axial force \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\). The signs are these: a pressure \(p\) inside pushes the wall outward, so \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = p\) and its axial resultant on the cap is positive. Weight points down, so its axial resultant \(Z\) is negative.

22.5.1 The pressure vessel (tlačna posoda)

Take a steel tube (figure 22.52) with mean radius \(R = 50\) mm and thickness \(h = 2\) mm, closed at both ends, with oil at pressure \(p = 3\) MPa inside. The steel is the steel of the book (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)). The question: what forces do the walls carry along the tube and around it?

The force that the wall transmits must be in equilibrium with the pressure, so we cut the tube and draw the free body, as in section 4.2.1. First we cut it with a plane perpendicular to the axis (figure 22.52, left). The left half has a wall with a flat end on which the pressure \(p\) pushes, and a cut where the right half pulls it with the axial force \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\) per unit of circumference. The radius \(R\) is the distance from the axis to the wall. The force of the pressure on the flat end equals the pressure times the area of the circle, \(p\,\pi R^2\). The pressure also pushes on the cylindrical wall, but there it is perpendicular to the axis and contributes nothing along the axis. The cut has circumference \(2\pi R\), so equilibrium along the axis is \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\,2\pi R = p\,\pi R^2\). Then we cut the tube along the axis (figure 22.52, right). The upper half of the wall has on its inner side the pressure, perpendicular to the wall, and the cut along the axis. The horizontal components on the left and on the right cancel, and the vertical ones add up to \(p\cdot 2R\) per unit length of the tube, since the vertical component of the pressure on an arc at position \(\theta\) is \(p\sin\theta\) and the integral is \(\int_0^\pi p\sin\theta\,R\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2pR\) (the same computation as for the Lamé tube in chapter 11). Downward the half is pulled by the hoop force \(N_\theta\) at both ends of the cut, so \(2N_\theta = 2pR\).

For the sphere the computation is the same, only simpler. We cut a sphere of mean radius \(R\) through its centre (figure 22.53). The half has a flat opening of radius \(R\), on which the pressure \(p\) pushes, and a cut along a great circle, where the other half pulls it with the force \(N_\varphi\) per unit length of circumference. Equilibrium along the axis is \(N_\varphi\,2\pi R = p\,\pi R^2\). A cut through any other diameter gives the same, so the stress is the same in every direction along the wall.

This is the same thought as in the theorem, which we show on all the examples at once. We write it as a lemma that also covers the sphere.

Lemma 22.36 Stresses in a pressure vessel (napetosti v tlačni posodi)

Let a cylinder with mean radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\), closed at the ends, or a sphere with mean radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\) have a pressure \(p\) inside. Let \(h \ll R\) and let the state be membrane (definition 22.30). Then:

  1. for the cylinder \(N_\theta = pR\) and \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = pR/2\), so the hoop stress is \(pR/h\) and the axial stress is \(pR/(2h)\),
  2. for the sphere \(N_\varphi = N_\theta = pR/2\), so the stress is \(pR/(2h)\) in all directions,
  3. the radial displacement of the cylinder is \(w = \dfrac{pR^2}{Eh}\Big(1 - \dfrac{\nu}{2}\Big)\) and that of the sphere is \(w = \dfrac{pR^2}{2Eh}(1 - \nu)\).
Proof

We must show three claims. First we compute the resultant \(Z\) of the pressure on the cap, then the force \(N_\varphi\) from equation (22.36), the force \(N_\theta\) from equation (22.37), and at the end the displacements from Hooke's law for the shell.

  1. The cap above the parallel of radius \(r\) and the disc of radius \(r\) that closes it at the parallel together bound a body. For the constant vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), by theorem 4.14, \(\oint \vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}S = \int \operatorname{div}\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\), because the divergence of a constant field is zero. The pressure \(p\) pushes the wall in the direction of the outward normal, so its axial resultant on the cap is \(Z = p\int_{\text{cap}}\vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}S\). Since this integral over the whole closed surface is zero, it equals minus the integral over the disc, where the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). Hence \(Z = p\,\pi r^2\).
  2. From equation (22.36), for the cap \(N_\varphi = p\,\pi r^2/(2\pi r\sin\varphi) = p\,r/(2\sin\varphi)\). For the cylinder \(r = R\) and \(\sin\varphi = 1\), so \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = pR/2\). For the sphere \(r = R\sin\varphi\) (in section 22.1 \(r = R_2\sin\varphi\) with \(R_2 = R\)), so \(N_\varphi = pR/2\).
  3. For the cylinder \(1/R_1 = 0\) and \(R_2 = R\), so equation (22.37) gives \(N_\theta/R = p\), that is \(N_\theta = pR\). For the sphere the force \(N_\varphi\) is already known, \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) and \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = p\), so \(N_\theta = pR - N_\varphi = pR/2\).
  4. By theorem 22.28, \(N_1 = C(\varepsilon_1 + \nu\varepsilon_2)\) and \(N_2 = C(\varepsilon_2 + \nu\varepsilon_1)\) with \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\). Multiply the second equation by \(\nu\) and subtract it from the first:
    \[N_1 - \nu N_2 = C(1 - \nu^2)\,\varepsilon_1 = Eh\,\varepsilon_1 .\]
    so
    \[\varepsilon_1 = \frac{N_1 - \nu N_2}{Eh} .\]
    Swapping the indices gives \(\varepsilon_2\).
  5. For the cylinder the hoop strain is \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = w/R\) (corollary 11.21 for the field \(\vek u = w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\)), for the sphere \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = w/R\) (corollary 11.22 for the field \(\vek u = w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\)). By step 4, for the cylinder \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (N_\theta - \nu N_{\mathrm{x}})/(Eh) = pR(1 - \nu/2)/(Eh)\), and for the sphere \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (pR/2)(1 - \nu)/(Eh)\). Multiply by \(R\).

Source: VK §14.3 (PDF p. 407-408) for the forces \(N_\theta = pR\) and \(N_1 = pR/2\) for the cylinder and \(pR/2\) for the sphere. The proof of the pressure resultant with the divergence theorem is not from the source.

Let us insert the numbers: for our tube the hoop stress is \(N_\theta/h = pR/h = 3\cdot 50/2 = 75{,}00\) MPa and the axial stress is \(N_{\mathrm{x}}/h = 37{,}50\) MPa. If the vessel were a sphere with the same dimensions, the stress would be \(37{,}50\) MPa everywhere. So the hoop stress is twice the axial one. The reason is in figure 22.52: the section perpendicular to the axis carries the force \(p\pi R^2\) over the circumference \(2\pi R\), that is \(pR/2\) per unit of circumference, while the section along the axis carries the force \(2pR\) per unit length over two cuts, that is \(pR\) per cut. This is why, at too high a pressure, the wall bursts along the axis, where the twice larger force \(N_\theta\) pulls, and not across.

The displacements of item 3 of the lemma give something we need later. Let us show them already now in the upper part of figure 22.70 from section 22.7, where the cylinder and the hemisphere are drawn each on its own and the displacements are enlarged 600 times. The cylinder widens by \(\dfrac{pR^2}{Eh}(1 - \nu/2) = 16{,}263\ \mu\)m and the sphere by \(\dfrac{pR^2}{2Eh}(1 - \nu) = 6{,}696\ \mu\)m. If we closed the cylinder with a hemisphere of the same thickness, the two membrane solutions would part at the joint by the gap \(\dfrac{pR^2}{2Eh} = 9{,}566\ \mu\)m. This difference is only \(0{,}5\ \%\) of the thickness \(h = 2\) mm, but the shell is a single continuous surface: the middle surface cannot tear or overlap at the joint. So the membrane solution cannot be the true solution at the joint. What happens there we show in section 22.7. The same holds at an edge with a prescribed displacement \(w\), for example a clamp. In membrane theory we cannot prescribe it, because that would need a transverse force and a moment, which the theory does not have (VK §13.2, PDF p. 356).

22.5.2 How good is the membrane answer (kako dober je membranski odgovor)

We treated the tube as a thin shell. Did we make a big error by doing so? The true solution of the three-dimensional problem is theorem 11.28 for the tube and theorem 11.30 for the sphere. By comparing, we will see by how much the membrane stress differs from the true one and when the difference is small. First let us look at a picture.

Figure 22.54 shows the stresses across the wall of a tube with pressure on the inner surface only. The stresses are divided by the membrane value \(pR/h\), so the membrane solution is the dashed line at \(1\). On the horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from the inner surface \(a\) to the outer one \(b\). On the left is our tube with \(h/R = 0{,}04\). The hoop stress falls from \(1{,}0004\) at the inner surface to \(0{,}960\) at the outer one, as the numbers at the ends show, and the radial stress rises from \(-0{,}04\) to zero. On the right is the thick tube from chapter 11 with the axis from \(a = 40\) mm to \(b = 60\) mm and \(h/R = 0{,}4\). There the hoop stress falls from \(1{,}04\) to \(0{,}64\) (numbers), and the radial stress at the inner surface is \(-0{,}40\). In a thin wall the hoop stress is almost constant across the thickness and close to the membrane one, in a thick one it is far from it. What exactly holds?

So far we looked at the hoop stress. Figure 22.55 shows also the axial stress of a closed tube and the stress of the sphere, both divided by the membrane value \(pR/(2h)\), which is the dashed line at \(1\). On the horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from the inner surface \(a\) to the outer one \(b\), as before. On the left is the closed tube: the axial stress is constant across the wall, a horizontal line below the membrane one, at \(0{.}960\) for the thin wall (\(h/R = 0{.}04\)) and at \(0{.}640\) for the thick one (\(h/R = 0{.}4\)). On the right is the sphere: the stress of the sphere falls from the inner surface to the outer one, from \(0{.}981\) to \(0{.}941\) for the thin wall and from \(0{.}905\) to \(0{.}505\) for the thick one.

Theorem 22.37 Membrane theory against the Lamé solution (membranska teorija in Laméjeva rešitev)

Let \(a < b\), \(h = b - a\), \(R = (a + b)/2\) and \(h < 2R\), let the pressure \(p\) act on the inner surface and let it be zero on the outer one. Let \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\) be the stresses of the Lamé solution for the tube (theorem 11.28) and for the sphere (theorem 11.30), and \(e = h/R\). Then:

  1. for the tube the hoop stress at radius \(r\) between \(a\) and \(b\) is a decreasing function with the values \(\dfrac{pR}{h} + \dfrac{p\,h}{4R}\) at \(r = a\) and \(\dfrac{pR}{h} - p\Big(1 - \dfrac{h}{4R}\Big)\) at \(r = b\), so \(\big|\sigma_{\theta\theta} - pR/h\big| < p\) everywhere in the wall,
  2. the average of the hoop stress across the wall is exactly \(p\,a/h = (pR/h)\big(1 - h/(2R)\big)\),
  3. for a closed tube the axial stress is exactly \(\dfrac{pR}{2h}\Big(1 - \dfrac{h}{2R}\Big)^2\),
  4. for the sphere the stress \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\) is a decreasing function of the radius with \(-\dfrac{3p}{4} \le \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \dfrac{pR}{2h} \le \dfrac{p}{4}\) everywhere in the wall.
Proof

We must show four points: the hoop stress of the tube, its average, the axial stress and the stress of the sphere. All follow from the stress equations of the Lamé solutions with \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = p\) and \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\). We compute with \(a = R - h/2\), \(b = R + h/2\), so \(b^2 - a^2 = 2Rh\).

  1. By theorem 11.28, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/r^2\) with \(A = pa^2/(2Rh)\) and \(B = pa^2b^2/(2Rh) > 0\). Since \(B > 0\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is decreasing in \(r\), like the blue curves in figure 22.54, so it lies between the values at \(r = b\) and at \(r = a\).
  2. At \(r = a\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = p(a^2 + b^2)/(2Rh)\). Since \(a^2 + b^2 = 2R^2 + h^2/2\), this is \(pR/h + p\,h/(4R)\).
  3. At \(r = b\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 2pa^2/(2Rh) = p\,a^2/(Rh)\). Since \(a^2 = R^2(1 - h/(2R))^2\), this is \((pR/h)(1 - e/2)^2 = pR/h - p + p\,h/(4R)\).
  4. Hence the blue curve lies relative to the dashed membrane value so that \(-p(1 - h/(4R)) \le \sigma_{\theta\theta} - pR/h \le p\,h/(4R)\), and since \(h < 2R\) gives \(h/(4R) < 1/2\), the absolute value is everywhere less than \(p\). This proves point 1.
  5. The average is \(\frac1h\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (A + B/r^2)\,\mathrm{d}r = A + B/(ab) = \dfrac{p\,a(a + b)}{2Rh} = \dfrac{p\,a}{h}\), since \(a + b = 2R\). This is point 2. The same result follows from the equilibrium of half a tube with inner radius \(a\), where the pressure acts on \(2a\) per unit length and is carried by two sections of total thickness \(2h\).
  6. For a closed tube (left in the figure) the pressure \(p\) pushes on each end with the force \(p\,\pi a^2\) (step 1 of lemma 22.36 with \(r = a\), since the pressure acts on the inner surface). This force is carried by the annular section of area \(\pi(b^2 - a^2)\). The end load \(S\) of theorem 11.28 is therefore this force divided by the area, and by the theorem \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = S = p\,a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = p\,a^2/(2Rh) = (pR/(2h))(a/R)^2\). This is point 3.
  7. For the sphere, by theorem 11.30, \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = A + B/r^3\) with \(A = pa^3/(b^3 - a^3)\) and \(B = pa^3b^3/(2(b^3 - a^3)) > 0\), so it is decreasing. We subtract \(pR/(2h)\) from the values at \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) and divide by \(p\): with \(e = h/R\), a SymPy computation gives \(-\dfrac{e^2 - 16e + 12}{4(e^2 + 12)}\) at \(r = a\) and \(-\dfrac{3e^2 - 16e + 36}{4(e^2 + 12)}\) at \(r = b\).
  8. For \(0 < e < 2\) (the curves of the sphere) the first fraction is at least \(-1/4\), because \(e^2 - 16e + 12 \le e^2 + 12\), and at most \(1/4\), because \(2e^2 - 16e + 24 = 2(e - 2)(e - 6) \ge 0\). The second fraction is negative, because \(3e^2 - 16e + 36\) has the discriminant \(256 - 432 < 0\), and at least \(-3/4\), because \(3e^2 - 16e + 36 \le 3(e^2 + 12)\). Since \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\) is decreasing, it lies entirely between these two values, and point 4 is proved.

Source: The Lamé solutions are from chapter 11 (CM example 7.3.1, BOWER §4.1.4). The comparison with the membrane stress is not from the source.

The theorem gives two things. First, the error of the membrane stress is never larger than the pressure \(p\) (for the sphere than \(3p/4\)), which answers the question of what we neglect: the radial stress, which goes from \(-p\) at the inner surface to zero at the outer one (this is the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{nn}}\) along the normal, which the assumptions of the shell neglect, section 22.3), and the difference between the radii \(a\) and \(R\), which gives the factor \(1 - h/(2R)\) in the average. Second, whether this error is small is decided by the ratio of \(p\) to the membrane stress \(pR/h\), that is \(h/R\). Let us collect the numbers for a thin and a thick tube and sphere with mean radius \(R = 50\) mm and pressure on the inner surface in a table, all stresses in MPa:

wall membrane Lamé, inner surface Lamé, outer surface largest difference
tube, \(h = 2\) mm, \(p = 3\) MPa \(75{,}00\) \(75{,}03\) \(72{,}03\) \(2{,}97\) (\(4{,}0\ \%\))
sphere, \(h = 2\) mm, \(p = 3\) MPa \(37{,}50\) \(36{,}79\) \(35{,}29\) \(2{,}21\) (\(5{,}9\ \%\))
tube, \(h = 20\) mm, \(p = 30\) MPa \(75{,}00\) \(78{,}00\) \(48{,}00\) \(27{,}00\) (\(36\ \%\))
sphere, \(h = 20\) mm, \(p = 30\) MPa \(37{,}50\) \(33{,}95\) \(18{,}95\) \(18{,}55\) (\(49\ \%\))

The first row is the numbers at the ends of the left picture, \(1{,}0004\) and \(0{,}960\), multiplied by \(75{,}00\) MPa. For our tube with \(h/R = 0{,}04\) the error of the hoop stress is at most \(4\ \%\), and the average \(73{,}50\) MPa is exactly \(pa/h\), which is \(2\ \%\) below \(75{,}00\), that is \(h/(2R)\). For a sphere with the same dimensions the largest difference is \(2{,}21\) MPa, below the bound \(3p/4 = 2{,}25\) MPa, but this is \(5{,}9\ \%\) of the membrane stress, because for the sphere that stress is half of the one in the tube. For the axial stress of the closed tube the Lamé value is \(36{,}02\) MPa against the membrane \(37{,}50\) MPa, a difference of the factor \((a/R)^2 = 0{,}9604\).

A case where the theorem is still true but does not help us is the thick tube from chapter 11 in the third row: \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm and pressure \(p = 30\) MPa, so \(h/R = 0{,}4\). Its Lamé stresses \(78{,}00\) and \(48{,}00\) MPa are the two numbers of the right picture, \(1{,}04\) and \(0{,}64\), multiplied by \(75{,}00\) MPa, and the average is \(60{,}00\) MPa. The error at the outer surface is \(27{,}00\) MPa, that is \(90\ \%\) of the pressure and \(36\ \%\) of the membrane stress, and the bound \(p(1 - h/(4R)) = 27{,}00\) MPa is reached. For the thick sphere in the last row the error is \(49\ \%\) of the membrane stress. The stress varies strongly across the wall (right half of figure), while membrane theory has a single value across the wall, so at \(h/R = 0{,}4\) it is too crude. For our thin wall with \(h/R = 0{,}04\) the error is under \(4\ \%\) for the tube and under \(6\ \%\) for the sphere. We proved both bounds only for the tube and the sphere under internal pressure, for other shapes and loads the bound must be computed separately.

22.5.3 The water tank (rezervoar za vodo)

In the vessel the pressure was the same everywhere. In a water tank it is not: water presses harder the deeper we go. Take an open cylindrical tank with radius \(R = 2\) m, height \(H = 6\) m and wall thickness \(h = 6\) mm, filled with water to the rim, with a bottom to which the wall is clamped. The specific weight (specifična teža) of water is the weight of a unit volume, \(\gamma = \rho g = 9810\) N/m³. What force is in the wall at height \(x\) above the bottom?

The hydrostatic pressure (hidrostatični tlak) at depth \(H - x\) is the weight of a column of water of unit area, that is \(p(x) = \gamma(H - x)\), as in section 4.2.1. In figure 22.57 on the left is the wall of the tank, clamped at the bottom and free at the top. The pressure of the water is drawn on it with blue arrows that are longer the deeper they are and end on the wall, because the pressure pushes the wall. Their edge is a line from zero at the top to \(\gamma H\) at the bottom. The hoop force at each height is the pressure times the radius, as for the vessel, so its diagram is an orange triangle of the same shape with \(\gamma HR\) at the bottom. The height \(H\) is the distance from the bottom to the water surface.

Lemma 22.38 An open cylindrical tank (odprt valjast rezervoar)

Let a cylindrical shell of radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\), clamped at the bottom and free at the top, be filled to the height \(H\) with a liquid of specific weight \(\gamma\). We neglect the weight of the wall. Let \(x\) be the height above the bottom. In the membrane state

\[N_\theta(x) = \gamma\,(H - x)\,R , \qquad N_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0 . \tag{22.38}\]
Proof

We must show both equations of theorem 22.32, equation (22.37) for \(N_\theta\) and (22.36) for \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\).

  1. For a cylinder \(1/R_1 = 0\), \(R_2 = R\) and \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = p(x) = \gamma(H - x)\), so equation (22.37) gives \(N_\theta/R = \gamma(H - x)\).
  2. The cap above the section at height \(x\) is the upper part of the cylinder. The pressure is everywhere perpendicular to the wall, so it contributes no force along the axis. We dropped the weight of the wall, and the liquid lies on the bottom and does not pull the wall down. So \(Z = 0\) and equation (22.36) gives \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\).

Source: VK §14.2.1 (PDF p. 395-397) for the hydrostatic pressure and the membrane analysis of tanks. The proof is from theorem 22.32.

Numbers in figure 22.57 on the left: at the bottom the pressure is \(\gamma H = 9810\cdot 6 = 0{,}0589\) MPa and the hoop stress is \(\gamma HR/h = 19{,}620\) MPa, the widest part of the orange triangle. At half height it is \(9{,}810\) MPa, at the upper quarter \(4{,}905\) MPa. The stress grows with depth along a line, like the edge of the triangle, so for a constant thickness the wall is highly used at the bottom and not at the top. If the wall does not have the same thickness everywhere, the force stays the same, since \(N_\theta\) does not contain \(h\). For a stress that should be everywhere equal to \(\sigma_{\text{dop}}\) the thickness must be \(h(x) = \gamma(H - x)R/\sigma_{\text{dop}}\). This is why the walls of tanks are thicker at the bottom.

But the clamp has no place in membrane theory. A free wall would widen at the bottom by \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}R = (19{,}620/196\,000)\cdot 2000\ \text{mm} = 0{,}2002\) mm (by step 4 of lemma 22.36). The clamped bottom does not allow this. So the wall bends in a band about \(0{,}27\) m high above the bottom, as we shall compute in section 22.6 (the band has the length \(\pi\sqrt{Rh}/(3(1 - \nu^2))^{1/4}\)). The shape of this bending is shown by figure 22.66 I for the tube: at the clamped edge the deflection is zero, far from the edge it reaches the membrane value, for the tank \(0{,}2002\) mm, and the transition is about \(\pi/\beta\) long, for the tank \(0{,}27\) m. In membrane theory this band does not exist.

22.5.4 A dome under its own weight (kupola pod lastno težo)

Now the load is weight, which points down, no longer pressure. Take a dome, a spherical shell of radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\), closed at the apex (\(\varphi = 0\)), with weight \(q\) per unit area of the middle surface, that is \(q = \rho g h\). What forces are in it at the angle \(\varphi\) from the axis?

In figure 22.56 on the left is the profile of half of the dome with the axis, from the apex to the foot, where \(\varphi = 90^\circ\). We choose a point on the profile. The angle \(\varphi\) is measured from the axis to the radius that goes to this point. Through it passes the parallel, a horizontal circle, and the cap above it is the shell above this circle. The cap is carried by the force \(N_\varphi\) along the profile. Its weight is \(q\) times the area of the cap, and this is \(2\pi R^2(1 - \cos\varphi)\), which we show in the first step of the proof.

Lemma 22.39 A dome under its own weight (kupola pod lastno težo)

Let a spherical shell of radius \(R\) and weight \(q\) per unit area be closed at the apex and in the membrane state. Then

\[N_\varphi = -\frac{qR}{1 + \cos\varphi} , \qquad N_\theta = qR\Big(\frac{1}{1 + \cos\varphi} - \cos\varphi\Big) . \tag{22.39}\]

The force \(N_\varphi\) is compressive everywhere. The force \(N_\theta\) is compressive for \(\varphi < \varphi^*\) and tensile for \(\varphi > \varphi^*\), where \(\cos\varphi^* = (\sqrt5 - 1)/2\), that is \(\varphi^* = 51{,}827^\circ\).

Proof

We must show both equations of theorem 22.32 and the zero of the force \(N_\theta\).

  1. The area of the cap is the surface integral of the function \(1\) (definition 4.61). We describe the cap without a small neighbourhood of the apex and without a narrow strip along one meridian by the angles \(\varphi'\) from \(\varepsilon\) to \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(2\pi - \eta\), \(\vek r(\varphi', \theta) = R\,(\sin\varphi'\cos\theta,\ \sin\varphi'\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi')\). This description is injective, and its normal vector \(\partial\vek r/\partial\varphi' \times \partial\vek r/\partial\theta\) has the length \(R^2\sin\varphi' > 0\). The area of this part is \(\int_0^{2\pi - \eta}\!\int_\varepsilon^\varphi R^2\sin\varphi'\,\mathrm{d}\varphi'\,\mathrm{d}\theta = (2\pi - \eta)\,R^2(\cos\varepsilon - \cos\varphi)\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) and \(\eta \to 0\) it tends to the area of the cap \(2\pi R^2(1 - \cos\varphi)\). The weight of the cap points down, so \(Z(\varphi) = -q\cdot 2\pi R^2(1 - \cos\varphi)\). For a sphere \(r = R\sin\varphi\), so equation (22.36) gives \(N_\varphi = Z/(2\pi R\sin^2\varphi) = -qR(1 - \cos\varphi)/\sin^2\varphi\). Since \(\sin^2\varphi = (1 - \cos\varphi)(1 + \cos\varphi)\), \(N_\varphi = -qR/(1 + \cos\varphi)\).
  2. The outward normal has the component \(\cos\varphi\) in the direction of the axis \(z\), and the weight per unit area points down, so its component in the direction of the outward normal is \(p_{\mathrm{n}} = -q\cos\varphi\). For \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) equation (22.37) gives \(N_\theta = p_{\mathrm{n}}R - N_\varphi = -qR\cos\varphi + qR/(1 + \cos\varphi)\).
  3. \(N_\theta = 0\) means \(1 = \cos\varphi(1 + \cos\varphi)\), that is \(\cos^2\varphi + \cos\varphi - 1 = 0\). The only solution between \(0\) and \(1\) is \(\cos\varphi^* = (\sqrt5 - 1)/2 = 0{,}61803\). For \(\varphi < \varphi^*\) we have \(\cos\varphi > \cos\varphi^*\) and \(N_\theta < 0\), and for a larger angle \(N_\theta > 0\).

Source: VK §14.1.1 (PDF p. 378-379), where it is \(51^\circ 49'\). The proof is from theorem 22.32.

On the right of figure 22.56 the forces are shown as functions of the angle. On the axis is \(\varphi\) from \(0\) to \(90^\circ\), and vertically the forces divided by \(qR\). The meridian force (blue) goes from \(-1/2\) at the apex to \(-1\) at the foot, always compressive, since the cap carries an ever larger weight. The hoop force (orange) goes from \(-1/2\) to \(+1\) and crosses zero at \(\varphi^*\). Why does the hoop force change sign? By equation (22.37), \(N_\theta = p_{\mathrm{n}}R - N_\varphi\), a sum of two parts. The first, \(-qR\cos\varphi\), is the normal component of the weight, which presses the shell toward the centre and requires a compressed hoop. At \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) it is zero, because the weight there lies in the surface. The second, \(-N_\varphi = qR/(1 + \cos\varphi)\), comes from the compressed meridians, which because of the curvature push outward and stretch the hoop. At small \(\varphi\) the first part prevails, at large \(\varphi\) the second.

Numbers for a steel dome of radius \(R = 5\) m: for \(q = \rho g h\) the stress \(N/h\) is \(\rho g R\) times a dimensionless function of the angle \(\varphi\), so it does not depend on \(h\), and \(\rho g R = 7800\cdot 9{,}81\cdot 5 = 0{,}3826\) MPa. So we multiply every value in the right picture of figure 22.56 by \(0{,}3826\) MPa. At the apex, where both curves start at \(-1/2\), it is \(-0{,}1913\) MPa in both directions. At the foot of the hemisphere the meridian one ends at \(-1\), that is \(-0{,}3826\) MPa, and the hoop one at \(+1\), that is \(+0{,}3826\) MPa. At \(\varphi = 60^\circ\), \(N_\theta = qR\,(1/(1 + 0{,}5) - 0{,}5) = qR/6\), tensile. The stresses are very small, because the shell is light compared with its size.

A hemisphere (\(\varphi_1 = 90^\circ\)) has a tensile hoop at the foot. A dome with the edge at \(\varphi_1 < \varphi^*\), however, has a compressed hoop over the whole height and no tension (VK §14.1.1, PDF p. 379). Such is the cap with \(\varphi_1 = 30^\circ\) in the left picture of figure 22.47 from section 22.4. At the edge it is not a hemisphere, so the force \(N_\varphi\) along the tangent of the meridian is not vertical. Its horizontal component \(N_\varphi\cos\varphi_1\) pushes the support outward. This component is taken by a supporting ring around the edge. The ring is in tension and the dome is compressed in the hoop, so their deformations do not match at the contact (VK §14.1.1, PDF p. 380). This is again a reason for bending at the edge, as for the vessel.

22.5.5 The conical head (stožčasta glava)

A vessel often has a conical head instead of a hemisphere. Take a closed cone on a cylinder of radius \(R\), with pressure \(p\) inside. What forces are in the cone, and what force remains at the joint with the cylinder?

In figure 22.57 on the right is the profile of the vessel: the cylinder, the cone on it and the axis. The half angle \(\alpha\) is measured between the axis and the generator. The cone is a surface of revolution whose normal makes a constant angle \(\varphi = \pi/2 - \alpha\) with the axis, so \(\sin\varphi = \cos\alpha\). The meridian is a straight segment, so \(1/R_1 = 0\). The radius \(R_2\) is the length of the normal from the surface to the axis, and since \(r = R_2\sin\varphi\), \(R_2 = r/\cos\alpha\).

Lemma 22.40 A cone under internal pressure (stožec pod notranjim tlakom)

Let a cone with half angle \(\alpha\) be under internal pressure \(p\) and in the membrane state, closed at the apex. At distance \(r\) from the axis

\[N_\varphi = \frac{p\,r}{2\cos\alpha} , \qquad N_\theta = \frac{p\,r}{\cos\alpha} . \tag{22.40}\]

If the cone is attached at radius \(R\) to a cylinder of the same thickness, which has \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = pR/2\), then at the joint along the parallel an unbalanced radial line load per unit length remains,

\[H = N_\varphi\sin\alpha = \frac{pR\tan\alpha}{2} , \tag{22.41}\]

directed toward the axis.

Proof

We must show both forces and \(H\).

  1. For the cap above the parallel of radius \(r\), \(Z = p\,\pi r^2\) as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 22.36. Equation (22.36) with \(\sin\varphi = \cos\alpha\) gives for the force along the generator \(N_\varphi = p\,\pi r^2/(2\pi r\cos\alpha)\).
  2. Equation (22.37) with \(1/R_1 = 0\) and \(R_2 = r/\cos\alpha\) (because of the angle \(\alpha\)) gives \(N_\theta\cos\alpha/r = p\).
  3. At the joint \(r = R\). The force \(N_\varphi\) goes along the generator, which makes the angle \(\alpha\) with the axis. Its axial component is \(N_\varphi\cos\alpha = pR/2\) and equals the force \(N_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the cylinder, so it is balanced. Its radial component is \(N_\varphi\sin\alpha = pR\tan\alpha/2\). The cylinder has no radial force, so this component remains unbalanced.

Source: The proof is not from the source.

Let us look at the joint in figure 22.57 on the right. At the joint \(N_\varphi\) pulls along the generator toward the apex. Its axial component is \(pR/2\) and is balanced by the axial force of the cylinder, which pulls down. What remains is the horizontal component \(H\), pointing toward the axis. For \(R = 50\) mm and \(p = 3\) MPa, \(H = 43{,}30\) N/mm at \(\alpha = 30^\circ\), \(75{,}00\) N/mm at \(45^\circ\) and \(129{,}90\) N/mm at \(60^\circ\). At \(45^\circ\) the radial force thus equals the axial force of the cylinder, \(pR/2 = 75{,}00\) N/mm, and at a larger angle it is larger. The force \(H\) pulls the joint toward the axis along the whole circumference, as if a pressure \(H\) pressed on a ring along the joint. Such a ring carries the compressive hoop force \(HR\), which the membrane wall at the joint cannot take over, so the wall bends there (section 22.7).

22.5.6 The ring: when membrane theory fails (obroč: ko membranska teorija odpove)

So far pressure or weight stretched the wall like a rope. But what if the wall cannot carry the load by tension? The simplest example is a thin ring squeezed by two opposite forces, like a tin can gripped in the middle.

Let us compare it with a ring under pressure. Figure 22.58 on the left shows the original ring of radius \(R\), dashed. In it is the pressure \(p\), which pushes the wall outward and ends on the wall. The ring carries it with the hoop tension \(pR\), as in the vessel, so it widens uniformly. Here \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = pR/(Eh)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = w/R\) (corollary 11.21), so the ring widens by \(w = pR^2/(Eh)\).

Now the same ring, of width one (per unit length along the axis), loaded by two forces \(F\) per unit width (figure 22.58, right). They act along a vertical line toward each other and each ends at its own point on the deformed ring. Here too the original ring is dashed, with the radius \(R\). Does a membrane answer exist?

We get the answer if we cut out of the ring a short piece around the top, between sections at the angle \(\alpha\) to the left and to the right of the vertical. We remove the rest of the ring and replace it by the forces in both sections. First the ring under pressure (figure 22.59, left). In each section the axial force \(N\) pulls along the tangent to the ring. In the section the tangent is tilted by the angle \(\alpha\), so \(N\) has the vertical component \(N\sin\alpha\) downward. The pressure pushes the piece upward with the vertical force \(2pR\sin\alpha\), which is the same integral as for the vessel, only from \(-\alpha\) to \(\alpha\) instead of from \(0\) to \(\pi\). Both vertical forces are proportional to \(\sin\alpha\), so \(N = pR\) balances them at every size of the piece, even a very small one: the pressure shrinks together with the piece and tension carries it.

Under the force \(F\) (figure 22.59, right) it is different. The piece is acted on by the whole force \(F\), however short the piece is, because the force is applied at a single point. The vertical components of the axial forces, together \(2N\sin\alpha\), go to zero as the angle \(\alpha\) shrinks, but \(F\) stays. Vertical equilibrium therefore needs a force perpendicular to the middle line, the shear force \(Q\), which for a very short piece carries \(F/2\) in each section. The shear force has a lever arm to the point of application of \(F\), so it brings a moment that varies along the ring (item 1 of the lemma below), and the ring bends. A membrane force alone cannot carry a force at a point. How much does the ring deform?

The lemma gives the answer. For its proof we cut the ring as figure 22.60 shows on the left, and we explain its result on the right picture.

Lemma 22.41 A ring under two forces (obroč pod dvema silama)

Let a thin ring with mean radius \(R\), thickness \(h\) and width \(1\), with bending stiffness \(EI\), \(I = h^3/12\), be loaded by two opposite forces \(F\) along a diameter. We take into account only the bending energy (the reason and the size of the error are in point 4 and in step 6 of the proof). Let \(\theta\) be the angle from the point where the force is applied. Then:

  1. the moment is \(M(\theta) = M_0 - \dfrac{FR}{2}(1 - \sin\theta)\) for \(0 \le \theta \le \pi/2\) with \(M_0 = \dfrac{FR}{2}\Big(1 - \dfrac{2}{\pi}\Big)\), and its largest absolute value is \(FR/\pi\) at the point of application,
  2. the distance between the points of application decreases by \(\delta = \Big(\dfrac{\pi}{4} - \dfrac{2}{\pi}\Big)\dfrac{FR^3}{EI} = 0{,}14878\,\dfrac{FR^3}{EI} = 1{,}7853\,\dfrac{FR^3}{Eh^3}\),
  3. for \(F = pR\) the ratio of the displacement \(\delta/2\) of a point of application to the radial displacement \(pR^2/(Eh)\) under pressure is \(0{,}8927\,(R/h)^2\), so at \(R/h = 25\) it is \(558\),
  4. the ratio of the bending and the stretching energy is \(U_{\mathrm{b}}/U_{\mathrm{m}} = 12(\pi^2 - 8)/\pi^2\cdot (R/h)^2 = 2{,}273\,(R/h)^2\), so at \(R/h = 25\) it is \(1421\).
Proof

We must show four claims. The moment follows from equilibrium and from the condition of minimum energy, the displacement from Clapeyron's theorem, and the ratios from the computation with the energies.

  1. We cut the ring along the horizontal diameter and look at the upper half. In the section the tangent to the ring is vertical, so the axial force is vertical and the shear force horizontal. The shear force is zero: the mirror image in the horizontal diameter takes the ring and both forces \(F\) into themselves, so the horizontal force with which the lower half acts on the upper one would equal the force with which the upper half acts on the lower one, while by Newton's third law they are opposite. Because of the mirror image in the vertical diameter the two sections share the force \(F\) equally, so in each there is the axial force \(F/2\), compressive, because it pushes the upper half upward. Only the moment \(M_0\) is unknown.
  2. For the arc from the angle \(\theta\) to \(\pi/2\) the vertical force \(F/2\) in the section at \(\pi/2\) is at the horizontal distance \(R(1 - \sin\theta)\) from the section at \(\theta\), because the section at \(\theta\) is \(R\sin\theta\) from the vertical diameter and the section at \(\pi/2\) is the radius \(R\) from it. The equilibrium of moments on the arc about the section at \(\theta\) therefore gives \(M(\theta) = M_0 - (F/2)\,R(1 - \sin\theta)\).
  3. We do not get the moment \(M_0\) from equilibrium, the problem is statically indeterminate. By theorem 4.101, among all statically admissible fields we take the one with the least energy. For a thin ring the energy is \(U = \dfrac{R}{2EI}\int_0^{2\pi} M^2\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \dfrac{2R}{EI}\int_0^{\pi/2} M^2\,\mathrm{d}\theta\) (the expression of definition 5.76 with \(EI\,v'' = M\) holds for a straight beam, for an arc with curvature \(M/(EI)\) we assume it. The fibres of the arc at a distance up to \(h/2\) from the middle line are from \(1 - h/(2R)\) to \(1 + h/(2R)\) times as long as the middle line, so the error is at most of order \(h/(2R)\), that is \(2\ \%\) at \(R/h = 25\)). The condition \(\partial U/\partial M_0 = 0\) gives \(\int_0^{\pi/2} M\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\), so \(M_0\,\pi/2 = (FR/2)(\pi/2 - 1)\).
  4. With the inserted \(M_0\), \(U = F^2R^3(\pi^2 - 8)/(8\pi EI)\) (SymPy). By Clapeyron's theorem 4.86, the work of the forces \(F\) on the squeeze equals the energy, \(U = \tfrac12 F\delta\), so \(\delta = 2U/F = (\pi^2 - 8)FR^3/(4\pi EI)\), which is \((\pi/4 - 2/\pi)\,FR^3/(EI)\). With \(I = h^3/12\), \(\delta = 12\cdot 0{,}14878\,FR^3/(Eh^3)\).
  5. Under pressure \(w = pR^2/(Eh)\). By symmetry each point of application moves by half the decrease of the distance, \(\delta/2 = 0{,}89267\,FR^3/(Eh^3)\). We compare the displacement of a point of the wall with the displacement of a point of the wall, so we take \(\delta/2\) and not \(\delta\). For \(F = pR\) the ratio is \(0{,}89267\,pR^4/(Eh^3)\,/\,\big(pR^2/(Eh)\big) = 0{,}89267\,(R/h)^2\).
  6. The axial force in the section \(\theta\) is \(N = (F/2)\sin\theta\). The stretching energy is \(U_{\mathrm{m}} = \dfrac{R}{2Eh}\int_0^{2\pi}N^2\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \dfrac{\pi F^2R}{8Eh}\). The ratio is \(U_{\mathrm{b}}/U_{\mathrm{m}} = 12(\pi^2 - 8)(R/h)^2/\pi^2\). The shear force \((F/2)\cos\theta\) gives an energy of the same order as \(U_{\mathrm{m}}\): the same integral with \(\cos^2\theta\) instead of \(\sin^2\theta\) and with the shear modulus \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) instead of \(E\), so about \(2{,}6\) times \(U_{\mathrm{m}}\), multiplied by a factor of order one for the non-uniform shear stress across the thickness. At \(R/h = 25\) the stretching and the shear energy together are under half a percent of the bending energy, so neglecting them changes the displacement by less than half a percent. For a thick ring with \(R/h = 3\), however, the stretching energy alone is already \(1/(2{,}273\cdot 9) = 4{,}9\ \%\) of the bending energy and neglecting it is no longer good.

Source: The proof is not from the source. The same displacement is given by an independent computation with the equation \(w'' + w = (FR^3/(2EI))(2/\pi - |\sin\theta|)\) in SymPy (displacement of the point of application \(-0{,}07439\,FR^3/(EI)\) for each).

The lemma says why the ring has no membrane answer. Let us compare the two loads in figure 22.58. Under pressure the ring stands by the hoop tension \(pR\) and widens by \(w = pR^2/(Eh)\). Under the force \(F = pR\) at two points, however, each point of application moves by \(\delta/2\), which is \(0{,}8927\,(R/h)^2\) times as far, for our thin wall with \(R/h = 25\) that is \(558\) times as far. In the figure both deformations are drawn about equally large: the widening on the left measures \(0{,}18R\), the indentation of the top on the right \(0{,}20R\). Since the displacement on the right is in reality \(558\) times larger, the left picture is enlarged \(558\cdot 0{,}18/0{,}20 = 502\) times, so about \(500\) times more than the right one. The shape the ring takes is an oval: the top and the bottom are pressed in, the sides widen by \(0{,}18R\). The middle line of the ring hardly lengthens in this, the ring bends.

Why is the difference so large, and why does it grow with the square of the ratio \(R/h\)? The answer has two factors, both in figure 22.60. The first is the lever arm. The force \(F/2\) in the section acts on the section at the angle \(\theta\) with the lever arm \(R(1 - \sin\theta)\), which is of order \(R\), so the moment is of order \(FR\), while the axial force is only of order \(F\). The second is the thickness. On the section of the wall the axial force \(N\) is carried by the whole thickness equally, with the stress \(N/h\). The moment \(M\), however, is carried by fibres with a stress that grows with the distance from the middle line and is \(6M/h^2\) at the face (from \(\sigma = My/I\) at \(y = h/2\) and \(I = h^3/12\), lemma 5.27). As the lever arm of its inner forces the wall has only its own thickness. The ratio of the stresses is therefore of order \((6FR/h^2)/(F/h) = 6R/h\). The density of strain energy is \(\sigma^2/(2E)\), proportional to the square of the stress, so the ratio of the energies is of order \((R/h)^2\), exactly \(U_{\mathrm{b}}/U_{\mathrm{m}} = 2{,}273\,(R/h)^2\) by point 4 of the lemma, so \(1421\) at \(R/h = 25\). At the same force \(F\) the ring stores \(1421\) times more energy in bending than in stretching. Since by Clapeyron's theorem 4.86 the energy is half the force times the displacement, bending contributes \(1421\) times more to the displacement of the point of application than stretching. So the ring does not carry by tension but bends. We know the same from our hands: a thin metal sheet bends easily between the fingers, but we cannot stretch it noticeably (VK §13.3, PDF p. 356).

From history

The same difference between stretching and bending was noticed by Rayleigh in the vibration of thin shells. He distinguished two kinds of vibration: the extensional one, in which the middle surface stretches and the strain energy is proportional to the thickness, and the bending or inextensional one, in which the energy is proportional to the third power of the thickness (TIMO, PDF p. 278). Since for given displacements the strain energy must be as small as possible, Rayleigh concluded that as the thickness decreases without bound, the actual displacement will be purely bending, if one exists under the given conditions (TIMO, PDF p. 278). This is the same ratio of energies as we computed for the ring, where \(U_{\mathrm{b}}/U_{\mathrm{m}} \propto (R/h)^2\) at the same force: the thinner the wall, the more the ring avoids stretching.

For the ring under two forces we saw that it bends and does not stretch, because stretching costs more energy by a factor of order \((R/h)^2\). A shell that can bend without stretching will therefore bend. This is why VK (§13.3, PDF p. 356) says that membrane theory is permitted for shells that cannot bend without stretching of the middle surface, or where the bending moments are very small. Which shells these are is shown by an attempt to flatten a shell.

We cut the cylinder along a generator and unroll it into a flat sheet. The circumference \(2\pi R\) becomes the edge of the sheet with the same length \(2\pi R\), and the lines along the axis stay straight and equally long. No line on the middle surface lengthens, we have flattened the cylinder without stretching. In the same way a sheet of metal can be rolled into a cylinder or a cone without stretching (inextensional bending, neraztezno upogibanje). With a spherical cap around the axis, however, this does not work. If the meridians keep their length \(R\varphi\), the rim of the cap moves from the radius \(R\sin\varphi\) to the radius \(R\varphi\), so its circumference must stretch from \(2\pi R\sin\varphi\) to \(2\pi R\varphi\). In the figure \(\varphi = 60^\circ\) and the radius of the rim grows from \(0{,}866R\) to \(1{,}047R\), by \(21\ \%\). If the rim kept its length, the meridians would have to shrink. So the cap cannot be flattened without stretching. Closed convex surfaces, for example table tennis balls, cannot be deformed at all without stretching of the middle surface, by Lagrange's theorem, which VK states without proof. Cylinders, cones and convex domes also cannot, according to VK, bend without stretching if their edges are supported by rigid rings. The ring is a section of a cylinder without such supports and can bend inextensionally, so the forces \(F\) turn it into an oval. Vessels and domes carry the membrane state, except where we impose a displacement, at edges and joints.

Summary of the section. For the vessel, the tank, the dome and the cone we obtained the forces \(N_\varphi\) and \(N_\theta\) from the equilibrium of the cap and from the normal equation alone. For the tube and the sphere we proved that the membrane stress differs from the Lamé one by at most the pressure \(p\) (for the sphere \(3p/4\)), which at \(h/R = 0{,}04\) is under \(4\ \%\) for the tube and under \(6\ \%\) for the sphere, while at \(h/R = 0{,}4\) it is not usable. An edge or a joint with incompatible displacements (cylinder and hemisphere \(9{,}566\ \mu\)m, the clamped bottom of the tank \(0{,}2002\) mm) and the ring under two forces require bending, which we treat in section 22.6.
