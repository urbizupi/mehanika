Chapter 1 Strain

1.10 Mathematics: when a sequence converges

In section 1.9 we always knew the limit in advance. For the strains of the pieces of the hanging bar it was \(2aL\), for the sequence \((1/n)\) the number \(0\), and each time we checked the definition of the limit with a computation of its own. On the road ahead it will be different. The statement that we need from the end of section 1.7.3 on is the mean value theorem (izrek o srednji vrednosti), which we define and prove in section 1.14 (theorem 1.84). We write it on the board (figure 1.113):

\[f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a) \quad \text{for some number } \xi \text{ between } a \text{ and } b .\]

The change of the function between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(f(b) - f(a)\), the difference of the two numbers is \(b - a\). The equation says that the slope of the chord, \(\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\), equals the slope of the tangent, \(f'(\xi)\), at some point \(\xi\) between them on the graph. Its proof will find such a point as the limit of a sequence that we build step by step, as segments that shrink around the sought point, and it will have no formula for the limit. So we need two things: a way to get new limits from known ones without a new computation with \(\eta\) and \(N\), and a guarantee that a sequence converges even when we cannot name its limit.

We develop both on one example, the search for the number \(\sqrt 3\) by bisection, which computes only with fractions. Along the way we meet a way of proving statements for all natural numbers at once, a rule that gets the limit of a sequence trapped between two sequences from their limits, the rule that a non-strict inequality between the terms carries over to the limits, and a theorem about sequences that only increase or only decrease. In this theorem the completeness axiom appears again. About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.8.1: the school rules of arithmetic, division of integers with a remainder, the fact that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element, and the completeness axiom 1.32.

1.10.1 Search by bisection

In section 1.8.6 we proved that the number \(\sqrt 3\) exists: it is the supremum of the set of nonnegative numbers with the square below \(3\) (theorem 1.36 on the existence of square roots). But the proof does not tell how large \(\sqrt 3\) is. How would we compute it if we can compute exactly only with fractions? Take the numbers \(1\) and \(2\) and all the numbers between them, which we write \([1, 2]\) (we define them precisely below). We have \(1^2 = 1 < 3\) and \(2^2 = 4 > 3\), so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(\sqrt 3\) lies between them. Then each time we look at the midpoint, the number halfway between the endpoints, and its square. Let us write the first steps as we would on the board:

\[\begin{aligned} [1,\ 2]: &\quad \text{midpoint } 1{,}5, \quad 1{,}5^2 = 2{,}25 < 3 \ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt 3 \in [1{,}5,\ 2] , \\ [1{,}5,\ 2]: &\quad \text{midpoint } 1{,}75, \quad 1{,}75^2 = 3{,}0625 \ge 3 \ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt 3 \in [1{,}5,\ 1{,}75] . \end{aligned}\]

The reason in both rows is the same: a larger number has a larger square. Since \(1{,}5^2 < 3\), we have \(1{,}5 < \sqrt 3\) and the root lies to the right of the midpoint. Since \(1{,}75^2 \ge 3\), we have \(1{,}75 \ge \sqrt 3\) and the root lies to the left of the midpoint. We go on like this. Each step halves the distance over which we search, so we call the procedure bisection (razpolavljanje).

The number line shows the same. The first row is the segment \([1, 2]\), a thick blackwhite segment with small ticks at the endpoints, because it holds all numbers between \(1\) and \(2\). The blue tick is its midpoint \(1{,}5\), to the right of the row the square of the midpoint is written, \(1{,}5^2 = 2{,}25\). The second row is the segment \([1{,}5, 2]\) that the first step left, and it is half of the first. Its midpoint \(1{,}75\) is again blue, the square \(3{,}0625\) is again written to the right. The third row is the segment \([1{,}5, 1{,}75]\) that the second step left. The grey dashed vertical is at \(\sqrt 3 = 1{,}7320\dots\) and passes through all three rows, because \(\sqrt 3\) stays in each of the segments. Each row is half of the previous one, so the third is twice as short as the second.

We need a name for all the numbers between two numbers.

Definition 1.43 Interval (interval)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers. The closed interval (zaprti interval) \([a, b]\) is the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : a \le x \le b\}\), the open interval (odprti interval) \((a, b)\) is the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : a < x < b\}\), and the half-open intervals (polodprta intervala) are the sets \([a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb R : a \le x < b\}\) and \((a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb R : a < x \le b\}\). The numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are the endpoints (krajišči) of the interval, the number \(b - a\) is its length (dolžina), and the number \(\tfrac12(a + b)\) is its midpoint (središče).

Source: LEBL1 §1.4 (PDF p. 41). The length and the midpoint are not from the source.

A square bracket says that the endpoint belongs to the interval, and a round one that it does not (figure 1.172). So the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : 0 \le x < 1\}\) from section 1.8.2 is the half-open interval \([0, 1)\). An interval contains all real numbers between its endpoints, not only the fractions. The requirement \(a < b\) makes the length positive and keeps an interval from being a single point, as the closed interval \([a, a] = \{a\}\) would be for \(a = b\). The notation \((a, b)\) for the open interval is the same as the notation for a pair of numbers, for example a point in the plane, and the text tells which is meant. The midpoint lies strictly between the endpoints, because it is half the length away from each of them:

\[\tfrac12(a + b) - a = \tfrac12(b - a) > 0 , \qquad b - \tfrac12(a + b) = \tfrac12(b - a) > 0 .\]

So every interval is nonempty. The intervals of the definition are bounded, because \(a\) and \(b\) are numbers. We will need unbounded intervals later, for example the set of all numbers \(x \ge a\), which we write \([a, \infty)\) (LEBL1 §1.4, PDF p. 41).

  • If \(m_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3\), the next interval is the right half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  • If \(m_{\mathrm{k}}^2 \ge 3\), the next interval is the left half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\).

From the table we read four properties that hold for every \(k\), and we list them below.

  1. The left endpoint has a square below \(3\), and the right one a square of at least \(3\): \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\). At \(k = 1\) we have \(1 < 3 \le 4\). The rule replaces the left endpoint only by a midpoint whose square is below \(3\), and the right one only by a midpoint whose square is at least \(3\).
  2. The left endpoints do not decrease, and the right ones do not increase: \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). This is because a new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint, which lies between the old endpoints, since the left endpoint is smaller than the right one (by the third property \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1} > 0\)). We prove the third property in section 1.10.2, and it does not need the second property, so there is no circle.
  3. The length of the interval is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) the length is \(1 = 1/2^0\), and each step halves it, because the midpoint is half the length away from both endpoints.

From the first property it follows that \(\sqrt 3\) is in every bisection interval. By the fourth property the endpoints are positive, so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 = (\sqrt 3)^2\) gives \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < \sqrt 3\), and \((\sqrt 3)^2 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) gives \(\sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The table shows even more. The left endpoints increase and crowd together below \(1{,}732\), and the right ones decrease toward the same value. Do they really approach some number, and is it \(\sqrt 3\)? If we were allowed to use \(\sqrt 3\), the answer would be short. In the proofs ahead of us, though, there will be no such number. There bisection will search for a point about which we know nothing in advance. So we will first show that the left endpoints converge without using \(\sqrt 3\), and only then that their limit is \(\sqrt 3\). For this we need four tools, which we build in the next sections, each with its own figure:

  1. induction (section 1.10.2): the statement holds at every \(k\) if it holds at \(k = 1\) and every step from \(k\) to \(k+1\) preserves it,
  2. the rule on a sequence trapped between two sequences with the same limit (section 1.10.3): the trapped sequence also has this limit,
  3. the rule that the limit preserves the inequality \(\le\) (section 1.10.4),
  4. the theorem that a sequence that only increases and does not exceed some bound has a limit (section 1.10.5).

We are already using the first tool: we showed each of the four properties of bisection by the argument that it holds at \(k = 1\) and that every step preserves it. Does such an argument really prove the statement for every \(k\)?

1.10.2 Induction

The argument for the properties of bisection was the same each time. Let \(P(k)\) denote a claim that depends on a natural number \(k\), for example "the length of the \(k\)-th interval is \(1/2^{k-1}\)". The argument was: \(P(1)\) holds, and if \(P(k)\) holds at some \(k\), then \(P(k + 1)\) holds too. Let us draw it as a row of dominoes (figure 1.176).

Let us draw a row of dominoes. The grey horizontal line is the ground. On it stands a row of dominoes, under each is a number \(n\). The blue domino is the first, for which we check the start \(P(1)\). The first three dominoes are drawn tilted so that the falling can be seen: the first, the second and the third are already falling, the others are still standing. The three dots at the end mean that the row goes on without end. The arrows above the row are the step from one domino to the next: \(P(n + 1)\) follows from \(P(n)\). Each goes from domino \(n\) to domino \(n + 1\), because the fallen domino \(n\) knocks over the next one. The last arrow points toward the dots, because the step holds there too.

Why does it follow that it holds at every \(k\)? Since it holds at \(1\), it holds at \(2\). Since it holds at \(2\), it holds at \(3\). We can go on like this, but in finitely many steps we do not reach all natural numbers. We need an argument that covers all natural numbers at once. On the board we would write the scheme that we will prove as a principle:

\[\Big( P(1) \ \text{holds} \Big) \ \text{and} \ \Big( P(k) \Rightarrow P(k + 1) \ \text{for every } k \Big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(k) \ \text{holds for every } k .\]

From history

Mathematicians before us also asked when the step from \(k\) to \(k + 1\) is allowed. In his edition of Euclid of 1260 Giovanni Campano of Novara (in Latin Campanus) proved that the ratio in which the whole segment is to the greater part as the greater part to the lesser (the golden section), that is \(\frac{a + b}{a} = \frac{a}{b}\) for the greater part \(a\) and the lesser part \(b\), cannot be written as a fraction. The argument led to a contradiction with ever smaller whole numbers, that is with a sequence of ever smaller natural numbers that cannot go on without end (what these numbers mean on the segment, Cajori does not say on these pages, so those in the figure are invented). It did not go in order from \(n\) to \(n - 1\) and \(n - 2\), rather by leaps over several whole numbers (CAJORI, PDF p. 158). The discovery of such an argument by a sequence of steps is attributed to Francesco Maurolico (in Latin Franciscus Maurolycus, 1494 to 1575) of Messina. It appears in the introduction to his Opuscula mathematica (Venice, 1575) (CAJORI, PDF p. 157-158). In 1656 John Wallis, in his Arithmetica infinitorum, inferred from several cases to all, as is the custom in natural science (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). Blaise Pascal used it in his Traité du triangle arithmétique in 1662 (CAJORI, PDF p. 158). In 1686 Jakob Bernoulli rejected Wallis's procedure because it is not logically binding (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). A statement may hold for the first few numbers and fail at the next.

Let us draw the history in order. Campanus's question is in the top row: the whole segment \(a + b\) is divided into the greater part \(a\) and the lesser part \(b\), the ratio of the whole segment to the greater part equals the ratio of the greater to the lesser. Campanus showed that it cannot be written as a fraction. Below is his argument on the number line: each arrow leads from a number to a smaller one, but not to the neighbouring one, rather over several numbers, in the figure from \(13\) to \(8\), \(5\) and \(3\). Then there is a number line again, on which each arrow leads to the neighbouring number, \(n\), \(n - 1\), \(n - 2\) and on: such an argument by a sequence of steps is attributed to Maurolico. Wallis's procedure is in the bottom row: four blue dots are checked cases, empty circles are the numbers about which he infers after the checked cases, and the dashed arrow shows the inference to all. Bernoulli rejected this and proposed a start at \(1\) and a step from \(n\) to \(n + 1\). All the numbers are examples only, for illustration.

An inference from a few cases to all natural numbers does not always hold. Let us look at an example that we add ourselves: divide a circle by all possible segments between \(n\) points on the circle, so that no three of them meet in the same point. For \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) we get \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16\) parts, which suggests \(2^{n-1}\), but for \(n = 6\) there are 31 and not 32 (figure 1.178). We do not need the general number of parts for \(n\) points: we have counted all six cases, \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31\). Instead of Wallis's procedure Bernoulli proposed a proof from \(n\) to \(n + 1\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). One must show that the statement holds at \(n = 1\) and that from its validity at some \(n\) its validity at \(n + 1\) follows. From these two facts we get the statement for all natural numbers at once.

Let us draw the circles in order, each with one more point, all the points are the same and stand in the same places. The first circle has one point and no segments, so it is one part. In the second there is one segment between the two points, which cuts the circle into two parts, one of them coloured grey. In the third three segments make a triangle, and there are four parts. The fourth and the fifth circle have ever more segments. Some parts are coloured grey only so that we can see where one part ends and another begins. The colour has no meaning. The points are spread unevenly, so that no three segments meet in the same point, otherwise the number of parts would be smaller. The parts need not be counted, the number under each circle is already written: \(1\), \(2\), \(4\), \(8\) and \(16\), which suggest doubling. The sixth circle is in a blue frame, because it is special: the number under it is \(31\) and not \(32\).

That such an argument is allowed is shown by the property of the natural numbers that we assumed in section 1.8.1: every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element. The idea: if the statement failed somewhere, there would be a smallest such \(n\), right before it the statement would still hold, and the step would carry it forward, which is a contradiction. We write a claim that depends on a natural number as \(P(n)\). It is a statement that for every natural number \(n\) is either true or not, for example the statement \(b_{\mathrm{n}} - a_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/2^{n-1}\) about the length of the \(n\)-th bisection interval.

We read the proof of the principle on the number line (figure 1.179).

Let us draw an example. The line shows the natural numbers \(1, \dots, 9\). Each dot is one \(n\) and says whether \(P(n)\) is true: a blue dot means that it is, an orange one that it is not. The first dot, \(P(1)\), is blue, because the start requires it. The orange dots are the set \(S\) of numbers at which \(P\) is not true. For the example we took \(S = \{5, 6, 8\}\), which is an invented example that shows how a counterexample would look. The dot at \(7\) is blue, because \(7\) is not in \(S\). The set \(S\) can be arbitrary and has gaps, so there can be blue dots after an orange one too. The smallest element of \(S\) is \(m = 5\), the arrow points to it. The arrow at \(m - 1 = 4\) points to the number right before it. This number is not in \(S\), because \(m\) is the smallest element of \(S\), so the dot is blue. Since \(P(4)\) holds, the step from \(n\) to \(n + 1\) would give \(P(5)\), which contradicts the fact that \(5\) is in \(S\). In this example, then, only the step from \(4\) to \(5\) is violated. At \(7\) the statement holds although it does not hold at \(6\), and this is allowed: a step about a false statement requires nothing.

Theorem 1.44 Principle of induction (načelo indukcije)

Let \(P(n)\) be a statement that depends on the natural number \(n\). Suppose that:

  1. basis (začetek): the statement \(P(1)\) is true,
  2. induction step (indukcijski korak): for every natural number \(n\) the truth of the statement \(P(n)\) implies the truth of the statement \(P(n + 1)\).

Then the statement \(P(n)\) is true for every natural number \(n\).

Proof

We must show that there is no natural number for which the statement \(P(n)\) is false.

  1. Let the set \(S\) be the set of all natural numbers \(n\) for which the statement \(P(n)\) is not true. We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(S\) is not empty.
  2. Every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element (section 1.8.1). Let \(m\) be the smallest element of the set \(S\).
  3. By the basis \(P(1)\) is true, so \(1\) is not in \(S\), and \(m \neq 1\). Since \(m\) is a natural number different from \(1\), we have \(m \ge 2\), and \(m - 1\) is a natural number.
  4. The number \(m - 1\) is smaller than \(m\). Since \(m\) is the smallest element of the set \(S\), the number \(m - 1\) is not in \(S\), and the statement \(P(m - 1)\) is true.
  5. So by the induction step with \(n = m - 1\) the statement \(P(m - 1 + 1)\), that is, \(P(m)\), is true as well. Then \(m\) is not in \(S\), which contradicts step 2.
  6. The assumption of step 1 is false. The set \(S\) is empty, and \(P(n)\) is true for every natural number \(n\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 0.3.6 (PDF p. 12). The source accepts the property of the smallest element as an axiom (the well ordering property) and says in a remark that the argument also needs \(m - 1\) to be a natural number for every natural number \(m > 1\), which we accept with the school properties of the integers.

From history

The name mathematical induction first appears, as far as is known, in an article by Augustus De Morgan of 1838, and it was popularized by Todhunter in his Algebra (CAJORI, PDF p. 347).

Induction works like the row of dominoes in figure 1.176, where the first three are already tilted. The proof tells why none of them can stay standing: among the standing dominoes there would be a first one, \(m\), and right before it the fallen domino \(m - 1\), which would have had to knock it over.

Let us write the proof of the third property of bisection by induction, because we will need it in a computation. The statement \(P(k)\) is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\).

  1. Basis. We have \(b_1 - a_1 = 2 - 1 = 1\) and \(1/2^0 = 1\), so \(P(1)\) is true.
  2. Induction step. Let \(P(k)\) be true, that is, \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\). The midpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}}) - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\) away from the left endpoint, which is the left half, and \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\) away from the right one, which is the right half.
  3. The next interval is \([m_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) or \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, m_{\mathrm{k}}]\). In both cases, by step 2, its length is
    \[\tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}) = \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = \frac{1}{2^k} ,\]
    which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction 1.44, \(P(k)\) is true for every natural number \(k\).

The first and the fourth property have the same structure: a start at \(k = 1\) and a step that preserves the property. In the list of properties these two parts are already written for each of them, but here we have written out in full only the third, because the second relies on it. The second property speaks only about one step of the rule, but it needs \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) at every \(k\). The third property guarantees this, and it is proved by induction. So the chain is:

\[\text{second property} \ \Leftarrow\ \text{third property } (b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} > 0) \ \Leftarrow\ \text{induction} .\]

Induction also tells why the bisection rule determines an interval for every \(k\): the first interval is given, and from every determined interval the rule determines the next one. We will build sequences in the same way later too (LEBL1 Example 2.2.8, PDF p. 66, shows by induction that all terms of such a sequence are defined).

We will also need that the lengths \(1/2^{k-1}\) go to zero. We will compare them with the fractions \(1/k\), which go to zero by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. For this we need the inequality \(2^{k-1} \ge k\). The numbers show that \(2^{k-1}\) is much larger than \(k\). At \(k = 10\), for example, \(2^9 = 512\).

The horizontal axis counts \(k\), the vertical axis shows the values. The grey dots are the numbers \(k\) and grow by one. The blackwhite dots are \(2^{k-1}\), their value is written above each: \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\), each twice as high as the previous one. At \(k = 1\) and \(k = 2\) the dots coincide, because \(2^0 = 1\) and \(2^1 = 2\), but from \(k = 3\) on the blackwhite dots are above the grey ones and escape them more and more.

Let us draw the step for \(k = 4\). The top blackwhite segment is \(2^{k-1} = 8\), the grey one below it is \(k = 4\), shorter than the assumption claims. The third segment is the blackwhite one laid twice, \(2^k = 2 \cdot 2^{k-1} = 16\). The fourth is the grey one laid twice, \(2k = k + k = 8\). The fifth is the grey \(k\) and one more unit, orange, \(k + 1 = 5\). The lengths get shorter going down: \(2^k \ge 2k \ge k + 1\), which the proof computes.

Lemma 1.45 Powers of 2 are at least as large as the natural numbers (potence števila 2 so vsaj tolikšne kot naravna števila)

For every natural number \(k\) we have \(2^{k-1} \ge k\).

Proof

We use the principle of induction 1.44 for the statement \(P(k)\): \(2^{k-1} \ge k\).

  1. Basis. At \(k = 1\) we have \(2^0 = 1 \ge 1\).
  2. Induction step. Let \(2^{k-1} \ge k\) hold. We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(2\), which does not reverse it, and get \(2^k = 2 \cdot 2^{k-1} \ge 2k\).
  3. We have \(2k = k + k \ge k + 1\), because \(k \ge 1\). The inequalities of step 2 and of this step form a chain, so \(2^k \ge k + 1\), which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction, \(2^{k-1} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 0.3.7 (PDF p. 13) proves \(2^{n-1} \le n!\) in the same way. The lemma is not from the source.

The induction step multiplied the inequality for \(k\) by \(2\) and compared the result with \(k + 1\). We will meet such a step, which turns the claim for \(k\) into the claim for \(k + 1\) with one computation, many more times.

1.10.3 A trapped sequence

Do the lengths of the bisection intervals, that is, \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots\), go to zero? We could repeat the computation from the proof of lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n and find a place \(N\) for every error \(\eta\). There is a shorter way, though. The lengths are positive, and by lemma 1.45 on the powers of 2 they are not larger than the fractions \(1/k\). So every length is trapped between \(0\) and \(1/k\), which we write on the board (figure 1.182):

\[0 < \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} \le \frac{1}{k} .\]

From below they are held by the constant number \(0\), from above by the fractions \(1/k\), which go to \(0\). The lengths have no choice but to go to \(0\). The next lemma writes this idea down for any sequences.

Let us draw what we have said. The horizontal axis counts the steps \(k\), the vertical axis shows the values from \(0\) to \(1\). The blackwhite dots are the fractions \(1/k\), the blue dots the lengths \(1/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) and \(k = 2\) the two numbers are equal, so the dots cover each other and only the blue one is seen. From \(k = 3\) on the blue dots are below the blackwhite ones, because by the lemma on the powers of 2, \(1/2^{k-1} \le 1/k\). The blue dots lie between the axis and the blackwhite ones. The blackwhite ones approach \(0\), so the blue ones cannot stay away from \(0\) either.

First let us draw what we will claim (figure 1.183). The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis the values of the terms. The grey dots below are the terms \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\), the grey dots above the terms \(z_{\mathrm{n}}\). Both approach the same number \(d\), the grey dashed line. The blackwhite dots in between are the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), which the two sequences hold between them, so they are always above \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) and below \(z_{\mathrm{n}}\). Around \(d\) we take a blue band, of width \(\eta\) on each side. From the place \(N\) on, the grey dashed vertical line, \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{n}}\) are in the band. Since \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is between them, it is in the band too.

Lemma 1.46 Squeeze lemma (lema o sendviču)

Let \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\), \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) be sequences such that \(y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\). If the sequences \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to the same number \(d\), the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) also converges to \(d\).

Proof

We must show that from some place on all terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) are closer to the number \(d\) than a given error.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N_1\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). The inequality \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) means \(-\eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\), and its left half gives \(d - \eta < y_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. Since \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_2\). The right half of the inequality \(-\eta < z_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\) gives \(z_{\mathrm{n}} < d + \eta\).
  4. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). For \(n \ge N\) steps 2 and 3 hold at once. With the assumption \(y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}}\) the inequalities form a chain:
    \[d - \eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}} < d + \eta .\]
  5. We subtract \(d\) from all sides and get \(-\eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\), that is, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 2.2.1 (PDF p. 61).

From each of the two sequences the proof needed only one side of the band. From the place \(N_1\) on, the lower sequence does not let the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) below the band, and from the place \(N_2\) on, the upper one does not let them above it. From the larger of the two places on, both sides are closed. The name of the lemma gives the same picture: the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is squeezed between two sequences like the filling between two slices of bread.

The lemma needs both bounds to converge to the same number. Let us see what happens otherwise (figure 1.184).

Let us draw this example. The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the places from \(1\) to \(12\) are written below the axis, the vertical axis shows the values. The upper grey dashed line is the constant sequence \(z_{\mathrm{n}} = 1\), the lower the constant sequence \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = -1\). Both converge, but to different numbers, \(1\) and \(-1\). The blackwhite dots are the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = (-1)^n\): the terms at even places lie on the upper line, the terms at odd places on the lower, so every term is between \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{n}}\). They jump between the lines and do not settle at any number.

The terms of the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) are trapped between the constant sequences \(-1, -1, \dots\) and \(1, 1, \dots\), and still they have no limit (lemma 1.40 on a sequence without a limit). Bounds with different limits let the terms jump between them.

Now we apply the lemma to the lengths of the bisection intervals.

Corollary 1.47 Limit of the powers of one half (limita potenc polovice)

The sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\), that is, \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots\), converges to \(0\).

Proof

We must show that the sequence is trapped between two sequences with the limit \(0\), and use the squeeze lemma.

  1. For every \(k\) we have \(1/2^{k-1} > 0\), because \(2^{k-1} > 0\).
  2. For every \(k\) we have \(1/2^{k-1} \le 1/k\). We write the difference over a common denominator,
    \[\frac1k - \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = \frac{2^{k-1} - k}{k \cdot 2^{k-1}} .\]
    By lemma 1.45 on the powers of 2 the numerator is not negative, and the denominator is positive, so the difference is not negative.
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(0 \le 1/2^{k-1} \le 1/k\) for every \(k\).
  4. The constant sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) converges to \(0\) (section 1.9.2), and the sequence \((1/k)\) does so by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. So by the squeeze lemma 1.46 with the lower sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) and the upper sequence \((1/k)\), the sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\) also converges to \(0\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 2.2.2 (PDF pp. 61-62) proves in the same way that \(1/(n\sqrt n)\) goes to \(0\). The corollary is not from the source.

The comparison with \(1/k\) is rough, but it is enough. At \(k = 11\) the length of the interval is \(1/2^{10} = 1/1024 < 0{,}001\), while the fraction \(1/11 = 0{,}0909\dots\) is almost a hundred times larger. The squeeze lemma only needs the upper sequence to lie above the sequence of lengths and to go to the same limit. How tightly it follows the sequence does not matter.

1.10.4 Limit and inequalities

Let us see where the terms and their limit lie (figure 1.185).

Let us draw both cases. At the top the horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis the values. The lower grey dashed line is the constant sequence \(a, a, a, \dots\), its grey dots are the terms, the upper one is the constant sequence \(b, b, b, \dots\). The blackwhite dots are the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), all between \(a\) and \(b\). The blue dashed line is the limit of \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). At the bottom is a number line. The grey band is the interval \([1, 2]\), the blackwhite dots are the left endpoints \(a_1, \dots, a_8\) from the table (\(a_2 = a_3\) and \(a_6 = a_7\) lie on the same dot), the blue dot is their limit, if it exists.

By the fourth property the left endpoints of the bisection all lie in the interval \([1, 2]\). If they converge, does their limit lie in it too? We expect that it does, since the terms come arbitrarily close to it and all of them are in the interval. Let us show that this is so for closed intervals, and let us look at where the expectation breaks down: for open intervals and for strict inequalities.

Lemma 1.48 Limits preserve non-strict inequalities (limita ohrani nestroge neenakosti)

Let the sequences \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge.

  1. If \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le y_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\), then \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} \le \lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} y_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. If \(a\) and \(b\) are numbers such that \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\), then \(a \le \lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\).
Proof

We must show both items. The second follows from the first, applied with constant sequences.

1. Inequality between the limits.

  1. Let \(x = \lim x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(y = \lim y_{\mathrm{n}}\). We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(x > y\).
  2. As the error we take half the distance between the limits, \(\eta = \tfrac12(x - y)\), which is positive. Then
    \[x - \eta = \tfrac12(x + y) = y + \eta ,\]
    so the band of width \(\eta\) around \(x\) and the band of width \(\eta\) around \(y\) touch in the middle, at \(\tfrac12(x + y)\) (figure 1.186).
  3. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N_1\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). The left half of the inequality \(-\eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} - x < \eta\) gives \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > x - \eta = \tfrac12(x + y)\).
  4. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(y\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_2\). The right half of the inequality \(-\eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} - y < \eta\) gives \(y_{\mathrm{n}} < y + \eta = \tfrac12(x + y)\).
  5. Let \(n\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). By steps 3 and 4, \(y_{\mathrm{n}} < \tfrac12(x + y) < x_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > y_{\mathrm{n}}\). This contradicts the assumption \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le y_{\mathrm{n}}\). So \(x > y\) does not hold, and \(x \le y\).

2. Bounds of the terms are bounds of the limit.

  1. The constant sequence \(a, a, a, \dots\) converges to \(a\) (section 1.9.2). Since \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\), we apply item 1 with two sequences: the first sequence is \(a, a, a, \dots\) and the second is \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\). Item 1 gives \(a \le \lim x_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. The constant sequence \(b, b, b, \dots\) converges to \(b\). Since \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\), item 1 gives \(\lim x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 2.2.3 and Cor. 2.2.4 (PDF p. 62). The source proves the first item without contradiction, by showing \(x - y < \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\). The proof by contradiction is not from the source.

The proof used the same picture as the proof of the uniqueness of the limit (figure 1.167). Two bands around two different numbers, each as wide as half the distance between the numbers, do not overlap. There, late terms of the same sequence cannot be in both bands. Here the late terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) would be in the upper band and the late terms \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the lower one, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) would be above \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Let us draw what would happen if \(x > y\) held. The horizontal axis counts the places \(n\), the vertical axis the values. The grey dashed line is the limit \(x\), the lower one the limit \(y\). Around each we draw a band, the upper band around \(x\) and the lower, darker one around \(y\). The width of each is \(\eta = \tfrac12(x - y)\), so the bands touch in a common edge and do not overlap. On the left the height of this edge, the middle \(\tfrac12(x + y)\), is marked. The edges of the bands are labelled on the right: the upper band is between \(x - \eta\) and \(x + \eta\), the lower one between \(y - \eta\) and \(y + \eta\). The grey dots are the terms \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\), the blackwhite ones the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). Before the place \(N\) they can be anything, and indeed the first three blackwhite dots are below the grey ones. The lower panel shows a case that the claim allows: the horizontal axis counts the places, the grey dots are the terms \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the blackwhite ones the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), and at every \(n\) the blackwhite dot is below the grey one. The lower dashed line is the limit \(x\), the upper one the limit \(y\), and \(x\) is below \(y\), as item 1 claims.

For the left endpoints of the bisection, item 2 says: if they converge, their limit is in the interval \([1, 2]\), because all \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) are in it.

A limit, however, does not necessarily preserve a strict inequality. Take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m that hangs under its own weight, and the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL - a/n\) of its top pieces of length \(1/n\) m from section 1.9.1. All of them are smaller than \(2aL\), because \(a/n > 0\). Their limit, though, is \(2aL\) (section 1.9.2). The dots in figure 1.153 approach the line \(2aL\) without reaching it. On the board we would write:

\[\varepsilon_1 < \varepsilon_2 < \varepsilon_3 < \dots < 2aL , \qquad \lim \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL .\]

Of the strict inequalities \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} < 2aL\) only \(2aL \le 2aL\) remains in the limit. That is why the lemma speaks of non-strict inequalities. From the strict inequalities \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < y_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\) we may conclude only \(\lim x_{\mathrm{n}} \le \lim y_{\mathrm{n}}\), because every strict inequality is also a non-strict one.

So it matters whether an interval contains its endpoints (figure 1.187). The terms of the sequence \((1/n)\) lie in the half-open interval \((0, 1]\), but their limit \(0\) does not, because it is not larger than \(0\). With a closed interval this cannot happen: if all terms of a convergent sequence are in \([a, b]\), then by item 2 the limit is there too. We will need this when we search for points as limits. The point we find must lie in the interval in which we search.

Let us draw two number lines with the same terms. The thick light grey band above is the half-open interval \((0, 1]\), the band below the closed interval \([0, 1]\). The notation \((0, 1]\) and the notation \([0, 1]\) are to the right of them. The small blackwhite dots are the terms \(1/n\) for \(n = 2, \dots, 10\), which crowd together toward \(0\), and they are the same in both rows, so below there are the same dots too. The larger blackwhite dot at \(1\) is the term \(1/1\) and at the same time an endpoint that belongs to both intervals. Below there is such a dot too. At \(0\) there is a difference. Above there is an empty circle, because \(0\) does not belong to \((0, 1]\). Below there is a blue dot, because \(0\) is an endpoint that belongs to \([0, 1]\), and it is the limit of the terms. So above the limit is outside the interval, below it is in it.

1.10.5 Monotone sequences

Let us return to the left endpoints of the bisection: \(1\), \(1{,}5\), \(1{,}5\), \(1{,}625\), \(1{,}6875\), \(1{,}71875\), \(1{,}71875\), \(1{,}7265625\) and so on. No term is smaller than the previous one, and none is larger than \(2\). Such terms cannot go back down and cannot get past \(2\). It seems that they must pile up somewhere. Where? At the smallest number that no term exceeds, that is, at the supremum of the set of terms. Here the completeness axiom appears again, and it guarantees that this supremum exists without our knowing it. First let us name the sequences that do not turn back.

Definition 1.49 Monotone sequence (monotono zaporedje)

A sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing (naraščajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\), and decreasing (padajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\). A sequence is monotone (monotono) if it is increasing or decreasing. A sequence is strictly increasing (strogo naraščajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\) (an example is the strains of the pieces), and strictly decreasing (strogo padajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.9 (PDF p. 55), the strictly increasing sequence at Def. 2.1.16 (PDF p. 58).

Let us draw the examples. Panel (1) shows the left endpoints of bisection \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 1, \dots, 8\). The axes count the steps \(k\), the numbers from \(1\) to \(8\) are below the horizontal axis, and the values. The vertical one starts at the mark \(1\), because all endpoints are at least \(1\), and it also has the marks \(1{,}5\) for \(a_2 = a_3\) and about \(1{,}72\) for \(a_6 = a_7\). The blackwhite dots are the terms. No dot is lower than the previous one, so the sequence is increasing. The orange horizontal lines are thicker and join \(a_2\) with \(a_3\) and \(a_6\) with \(a_7\), because these terms are equal, so the sequence is not strictly increasing. In panel (2) there are the terms \(1/n\), the first is at the mark \(1\) on the vertical axis, each is lower than the previous one, so the sequence is strictly decreasing. In panel (3) there are the terms \((-1)^n\), the vertical axis has only the marks \(-1\) and \(1\), and the terms alternately rise and fall, so the sequence is not monotone.

So an increasing sequence allows equal consecutive terms, and a strictly increasing one does not. Some books mean strictly increasing by the word increasing, so when reading the sources it is worth checking the definition. The left endpoints of the bisection are an increasing sequence by the second property from section 1.10.1, but not a strictly increasing one, because \(a_2 = a_3 = 1{,}5\). At \(k = 2\) the rule kept the left half and moved only the right endpoint. The right endpoints are a decreasing sequence, and \(b_1 = b_2 = 2\). The strains of the pieces of the hanging bar are strictly increasing:

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}+1} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} &= \Big(2aL - \frac{a}{n+1}\Big) - \Big(2aL - \frac{a}{n}\Big) \\ &= \frac{a}{n} - \frac{a}{n+1} = \frac{a\,(n+1) - a\,n}{n(n+1)} = \frac{a}{n(n+1)} > 0 . \end{aligned}\]

The sequence \((1/n)\) is strictly decreasing. A constant sequence is increasing and decreasing at once. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) is not monotone, because \(x_1 = -1 < 1 = x_2\), but \(x_2 = 1 > -1 = x_3\).

The definition compares only neighbouring terms. In proofs we will need that in an increasing sequence no later term is smaller than any earlier one, that is \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{m}}\) for all \(n \le m\). This is a claim about infinitely many places, so we prove it by induction.

Let us draw the chain. The horizontal axis counts the places, the vertical axis starts at the value \(1\). The blackwhite dots are the left endpoints of the bisection \(a_1, \dots, a_8\), an increasing sequence. The circle at the place \(n = 2\) is the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), the circle at \(m = 6\) the term \(x_{\mathrm{m}}\). The blue arrows lead from each dot between them to the next, and each is one comparison \(x_{\mathrm{j}} \le x_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). One of them, from the place \(4\) to \(5\), is one induction step, here from \(x_4\) to \(x_5\). Below the axis are the places \(n\), \(n + 1\), \(n + j - 1\), \(n + j\) and \(m\). The place \(n + j - 1\) is the start of the arrow that the proof uses in the induction step, in the figure at \(j = 3\).

Lemma 1.50 Terms of a monotone sequence (členi monotonega zaporedja)

If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing, then \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{m}}\) for any natural numbers \(n \le m\). If it is decreasing, then \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{m}}\) for any natural numbers \(n \le m\).

Proof

We must show that a later term of an increasing sequence is not smaller than an earlier one. We keep the place \(n\) fixed and step forward by induction.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be increasing and \(n\) a natural number. For a natural number \(j\) let \(P(j)\) be the statement \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\). The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\) is \(j - 1\) places after the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), so as \(j\) runs through \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), we cover all terms from \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) on.
  2. Basis. The statement \(P(1)\) is \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is true.
  3. Induction step. Let \(P(j)\) hold, that is, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\). Since the sequence is increasing, \(x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}}\). The inequalities form a chain, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}}\), which is the statement \(P(j + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction 1.44, \(P(j)\) holds for every natural number \(j\). For \(m \ge n\) we take \(j = m - n + 1\), so \(n + j - 1 = m\), and we get \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  5. For a decreasing sequence steps 1 to 4 are the same, only with \(\ge\) in place of \(\le\).

Source: LEBL1 uses the claim in the proof of Thm 2.1.10 (PDF p. 55) and says that it follows by induction. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma builds a comparison of any two terms from comparisons of neighbouring ones. Between \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{m}}\) stands a chain of \(m - n\) comparisons of neighbouring terms, and induction joins them all:

\[x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+1} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+2} \le \dots \le x_{\mathrm{m}} .\]

Now we can say where an increasing sequence goes if it cannot get past some bound (figure 1.190). Let \(s\) be the supremum of the set of its terms, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s\) for every \(n\). The number \(s - \eta\) is not an upper bound, so some term exceeds it, \(x_{\mathrm{N}} > s - \eta\). Since the sequence increases, for every \(n \ge N\) we have

\[s - \eta < x_{\mathrm{N}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s .\]

From the place \(N\) on, all the terms are therefore in the band between \(s - \eta\) and \(s\).

The horizontal axis counts the places, the vertical axis shows the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). The grey dashed horizontal line is the supremum \(s\) and no dot is above it. The blue band is below it, between \(s - \eta\) and \(s\), because the terms we follow are below \(s\). The sequence increases, so the dots rise from left to right. The grey dots are the terms before the place \(N\), which are still below the band. The vertical line \(N\) marks the term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\), which is the first above \(s - \eta\). The blackwhite dots from it on are all in the band, because the sequence cannot fall back.

For a decreasing sequence we draw the mirror image.

The horizontal axis counts the places, the vertical axis shows the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\). The grey dashed horizontal line is the infimum \(i\) and no dot is below it. The blue band is above it, between \(i\) and \(i + \eta\), because the terms cannot go below \(i\). Here the dots fall from left to right. The grey dots are the terms before the place \(N\), which are still above the band. The vertical line \(N\) is at the place \(N = 6\) and marks the term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\), which is the first below \(i + \eta\). The blackwhite dots from it on are all in the band, because the sequence cannot rise back.

Theorem 1.51 Monotone convergence theorem (izrek o monotonih zaporedjih)

A monotone sequence converges if and only if it is bounded. If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing and bounded, then

\[\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = \sup\{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\} ,\]

and if it is decreasing and bounded, then

\[\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = \inf\{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\} .\]
Proof

We must show that a convergent monotone sequence is bounded, and that a bounded monotone sequence converges to the supremum or the infimum of the set of its terms.

A convergent monotone sequence is bounded.

  1. By lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence every convergent sequence is bounded, so every convergent monotone sequence is bounded too.

An increasing bounded sequence converges to the supremum.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be increasing and bounded, and let \(A = \{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\}\) be the set of its terms. The set \(A\) is nonempty, because it contains \(x_1\), and bounded above, because the sequence is bounded (definition 1.37).
  2. By the completeness axiom 1.32, \(A\) has a supremum \(s\). We will show that the sequence converges to \(s\).
  3. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 2, there is an element of the set \(A\) that is larger than \(s - \eta\). The elements of the set \(A\) are the terms of the sequence, so there is a place \(N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{N}} > s - \eta\).
  4. Let \(n \ge N\), that is, a late term. By lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{N}}\), and so by step 3, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > s - \eta\).
  5. The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is an element of the set \(A\), and the supremum \(s\) is an upper bound of this set, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s\).
  6. By steps 4 and 5, \(s - \eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s\). So the difference \(s - x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not negative and is smaller than \(\eta\), that is, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - s| = s - x_{\mathrm{n}} < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the sequence converges to \(s\).

A decreasing bounded sequence converges to the infimum.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be decreasing and bounded, and let \(A = \{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\}\). The set \(A\) is nonempty and bounded below, so by theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 1, it has an infimum \(i\).
  2. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By item 2 of the same theorem there is an element of the set \(A\) that is smaller than \(i + \eta\), that is, a place \(N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{N}} < i + \eta\).
  3. Let \(n \ge N\), that is, a late term. By lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{N}}\), and so by step 2, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < i + \eta\).
  4. The infimum \(i\) is a lower bound of the set \(A\), so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge i\).
  5. By steps 3 and 4, \(i \le x_{\mathrm{n}} < i + \eta\), so \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - i| = x_{\mathrm{n}} - i < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the sequence converges to \(i\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 2.1.10 (PDF pp. 55-56). The source leaves the decreasing case to the reader.

Where is the completeness axiom in the proof? In step 2 of the second part. It guarantees the number \(s\) to which the sequence converges. We did not know the limit in advance, and the proof named it as the supremum of the set of terms. An increasing sequence always has the lower bound \(x_1\), because by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence \(x_1 \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\). So it is enough to check that it is bounded above (LEBL1, PDF p. 56). We will use the theorem every time we must show that some number exists without being able to write it down. So it is one of the results that carry the road to the statement about the change of a function between two numbers.

The strains of the pieces of the hanging bar are strictly increasing and all smaller than \(2aL\), so by the theorem they converge even before we compute the limit. The limit is unique and equal to \(2aL\), so the theorem also tells us that \(2aL\) is the supremum of the set of strains: the smallest number that no strain of a piece exceeds. Boundedness is needed (figure 1.192).

Let us draw the sequence \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) on a number line: the grey dots grow by \(1\) at every step, the dashed line \(B\) is a trial bound, and the orange dots are the terms that exceed it. The bound is made up and no bound will do, so the sequence is not bounded.

The sequence \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) is strictly increasing, but it is not bounded, because by the Archimedean property 1.35 the set of natural numbers is not bounded above. So by the first part of the theorem it does not converge.

Now we can answer the question from section 1.10.1. Let us show that the left endpoints of the bisection converge to \(\sqrt 3\) (figure 1.195). Let us recall the four properties of bisection:

  • (1) \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\),
  • (2) \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\),
  • (3) \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\),
  • (4) all endpoints are in the interval \([1, 2]\).

For step 3 below, let us draw why \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an upper bound of all the left endpoints (figure 1.193).

The upper line is the case \(j \le k\): the term \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) is to the left of \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\), because the left endpoints increase, and \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) is to the left of \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\). The lower line is the case \(j > k\): to the right of the endpoint \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the endpoint \(b_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the right endpoints decrease, so \(b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{j}} < b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). In both cases \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is to the right of \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) or at it.

First we show that the left endpoints converge, without using \(\sqrt 3\).

  1. The sequence \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) is increasing by property (2) and bounded by property (4), because all its terms are in \([1, 2]\).
  2. So by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51 it converges to the number \(c = \sup\{a_{\mathrm{k}} : k \in \mathbb N\}\).
  3. The number \(c\) lies in every bisection interval. Since the supremum is an upper bound, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\). Let us also show \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let \(j\) be any natural number. For \(j \le k\) (upper line) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence and by property (3), which gives \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\). For \(j > k\) (lower line) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} < b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by property (3) and by the same lemma for the decreasing sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the number \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an upper bound of all the left endpoints, and the supremum \(c\) is not larger than any upper bound. So \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Up to here we have used nothing about the number \(\sqrt 3\). Let us now show that \(c = \sqrt 3\). Figure 1.194 shows step 5.

The thick grey segment is the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\), here at \(k = 4\), so \([1{,}625,\ 1{,}75]\). Its endpoints are labelled \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\). The blue dot is the number \(c\) that we have in the interval, the orange one is \(\sqrt 3 = 1{,}7320\dots\) The number \(c\) is drawn at a different place than \(\sqrt 3\) so that the distance between them can be seen, since we do not yet know that they are equal. The upper measure is their distance \(|c - \sqrt 3|\). The measure above it is the length of the interval \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\), and grey dashed lines drop it to the endpoints. The distance is shorter than the length, because both numbers are inside the interval.

  1. The number \(\sqrt 3\) lies in every bisection interval, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < \sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), as we showed in section 1.10.1.
  2. Two numbers in the same interval are at most its length apart. From \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le \sqrt 3\) it follows that \(c - \sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\), and from \(\sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\) that \(\sqrt 3 - c \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\). So by property (3)
    \[|c - \sqrt 3| \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\]
    for every natural number \(k\).
  3. On the left side of the inequality stands a constant sequence, which converges to \(|c - \sqrt 3|\), and on the right side a sequence that converges to \(0\) by corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half. By lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 1, \(|c - \sqrt 3| \le 0\). An absolute value is not negative, so \(|c - \sqrt 3| = 0\) and \(c = \sqrt 3\).

Why was the axiom of completeness needed? Let us draw only the fractions (figure 1.196).

The line runs from \(1\) to \(2\), the two ticks are the endpoints. The dots are the left endpoints \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\), which pile up ever closer to the point \(\sqrt 3\). It is drawn as an open circle: on the line of fractions there is a hole there, because \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction.

All the left endpoints are fractions, but their limit \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction, because by lemma 1.27 no fraction has square 3. If we knew only fractions, \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) would be an increasing bounded sequence of fractions that has no limit among the fractions. The argument has two steps. First, fractions are real numbers, so a limit that is a fraction would also be a limit in the real numbers, and by lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence it would equal \(\sqrt 3\). Second, \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction. So the theorem on monotone sequences does not hold among fractions, and the gap is exactly in the step where its proof uses the axiom of completeness. The hole among the fractions from figure 1.130 shows up here as a sequence that finds no limit among the fractions.

Bisection worked because at every step we knew which half to keep: the one that contains \(\sqrt 3\), which we recognized by the square of the midpoint. The next section applies bisection to an arbitrary bounded sequence, because we look for a convergent sequence made of chosen terms of a given sequence (the claim that we will prove is the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57). We will keep the half that contains infinitely many terms, and in this way choose from the sequence infinitely many terms that converge.
