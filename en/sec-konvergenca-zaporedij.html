Chapter 1 Strain

1.10 Mathematics: when a sequence converges

In section 1.9 we always knew the limit in advance. For the strains of the pieces of the hanging bar it was \(2aL\), for the sequence \((1/n)\) the number \(0\), and each time we checked the definition of the limit with a computation of its own. On the road ahead it will be different. The statement that we have needed since the end of section 1.7.3 says that the change of a function between two numbers equals the derivative at some point between them times the difference of the two numbers. Its proof will find such a point as the limit of a sequence that we build step by step, and it will have no formula for the limit. So we need two things: a way to get new limits from known ones without a new computation with \(\eta\) and \(N\), and a guarantee that a sequence converges even when we cannot name its limit.

We develop both on one example, the search for the number \(\sqrt 3\) by bisection, which computes only with fractions. Along the way we meet a way of proving statements for all natural numbers at once, a rule that gets the limit of a sequence trapped between two sequences from their limits, the rule that a non-strict inequality between the terms carries over to the limits, and a theorem about sequences that only increase or only decrease. In this theorem the completeness axiom appears again. About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.8.1: the school rules of arithmetic, division of integers with a remainder, the fact that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element, and the completeness axiom 1.32.

1.10.1 Search by bisection

In section 1.8.6 we proved that the number \(\sqrt 3\) exists: it is the supremum of the set of nonnegative numbers with the square below \(3\) (theorem 1.36 on the existence of square roots). But the proof does not tell how large \(\sqrt 3\) is. How would we compute it if we can compute exactly only with fractions? Take the numbers \(1\) and \(2\). We have \(1^2 = 1 < 3\) and \(2^2 = 4 > 3\), so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(\sqrt 3\) lies between them. We look at the number in the middle, \(1{,}5\). Its square \(2{,}25\) is below \(3\), so \(\sqrt 3\) lies between \(1{,}5\) and \(2\). We look at the new middle, \(1{,}75\). Its square \(3{,}0625\) is not below \(3\), so \(\sqrt 3\) lies between \(1{,}5\) and \(1{,}75\). We go on like this. Each step halves the distance over which we search, so we call the procedure bisection (razpolavljanje).

We need a name for all the numbers between two numbers.

Definition 1.43 Interval (interval)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers. The closed interval (zaprti interval) \([a, b]\) is the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : a \le x \le b\}\), the open interval (odprti interval) \((a, b)\) is the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : a < x < b\}\), and the half-open intervals (polodprta intervala) are the sets \([a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb R : a \le x < b\}\) and \((a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb R : a < x \le b\}\). The numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are the endpoints (krajišči) of the interval, the number \(b - a\) is its length (dolžina), and the number \(\tfrac12(a + b)\) is its midpoint (središče).

Source: LEBL1 §1.4 (PDF p. 41). The length and the midpoint are not from the source.

A square bracket says that the endpoint belongs to the interval, and a round one that it does not (figure 1.65). So the set \(\{x \in \mathbb R : 0 \le x < 1\}\) from section 1.8.2 is the half-open interval \([0, 1)\). An interval contains all real numbers between its endpoints, not only the fractions. The requirement \(a < b\) makes the length positive and keeps an interval from being a single point, as the closed interval \([a, a] = \{a\}\) would be for \(a = b\). The notation \((a, b)\) for the open interval is the same as the notation for a pair of numbers, for example a point in the plane, and the text tells which is meant. The midpoint lies strictly between the endpoints, because it is half the length away from each of them:

\[\tfrac12(a + b) - a = \tfrac12(b - a) > 0 , \qquad b - \tfrac12(a + b) = \tfrac12(b - a) > 0 .\]

So every interval is nonempty. The intervals of the definition are bounded, because \(a\) and \(b\) are numbers. We will need unbounded intervals later, for example the set of all numbers \(x \ge a\), which we write \([a, \infty)\) (LEBL1 §1.4, PDF p. 41).

Let us now write the search as a rule. We start with the interval \([a_1, b_1] = [1, 2]\). Suppose that for some natural number \(k\) the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) is already determined, and let \(m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}})\) be its midpoint.

  • If \(m_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3\), the next interval is the right half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = b_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  • If \(m_{\mathrm{k}}^2 \ge 3\), the next interval is the left half: \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1} = a_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} = m_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The rule determines the first interval, and from each interval the next one. The left endpoints \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\) form a sequence, and so do the right endpoints. The rule uses only addition, division by \(2\), multiplication and comparison of fractions. It does not use the number \(\sqrt 3\) anywhere. The table shows the first eight steps, and figure 1.66 shows the intervals.

The endpoints are fractions with the denominators \(1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\), so they have finite decimal expansions, and the table gives them exactly. The squares of the midpoints that end in three dots are cut off after four decimal places, and the others are exact.

The table shows four properties that hold for every \(k\).

  1. The left endpoint has a square below \(3\), and the right one a square of at least \(3\): \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\). At \(k = 1\) we have \(1 < 3 \le 4\). The rule replaces the left endpoint only by a midpoint whose square is below \(3\), and the right one only by a midpoint whose square is at least \(3\).
  2. The left endpoints do not decrease, and the right ones do not increase: \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). This is because a new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint, which lies between the old endpoints, since the left endpoint is smaller than the right one (by the third property \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1} > 0\)).
  3. The length of the interval is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\). At \(k = 1\) the length is \(1 = 1/2^0\), and each step halves it, because the midpoint is half the length away from both endpoints.
  4. All endpoints are fractions in the interval \([1, 2]\). At \(k = 1\) they are \(1\) and \(2\). A new endpoint is an old endpoint or the midpoint of two fractions from \([1, 2]\), which is again a fraction and lies between them.

From the first property it follows that \(\sqrt 3\) is in every bisection interval. By the fourth property the endpoints are positive, so by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 = (\sqrt 3)^2\) gives \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < \sqrt 3\), and \((\sqrt 3)^2 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) gives \(\sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The table shows even more. The left endpoints increase and crowd together below \(1{,}732\), and the right ones decrease toward the same value. Do they really approach some number, and is it \(\sqrt 3\)? If we were allowed to use \(\sqrt 3\), the answer would be short. In the proofs ahead of us, though, there will be no such number. There bisection will search for a point about which we know nothing in advance. So we will first show that the left endpoints converge without using \(\sqrt 3\), and only then that their limit is \(\sqrt 3\). For this we need four tools. The first one is already hidden in the four properties: we showed each of them by the argument that it holds at \(k = 1\) and that every step preserves it. Does such an argument really prove the statement for every \(k\)?

1.10.2 Induction

The argument for the properties of bisection was the same each time. The property holds at \(k = 1\), and if it holds at some \(k\), it also holds at \(k + 1\). Why does it follow that it holds at every \(k\)? Since it holds at \(1\), it holds at \(2\). Since it holds at \(2\), it holds at \(3\). We can go on like this, but in finitely many steps we do not reach all natural numbers. We need an argument that covers all natural numbers at once.

From history

Mathematicians before us knew the same difficulty. In his edition of Euclid of 1260 Giovanni Campano of Novara (in Latin Campanus) proved that the ratio in which the whole segment is to the greater part as the greater part to the lesser (the golden section) cannot be written as a fraction. The argument went back to smaller numbers and led to a contradiction, but not in order from \(n\) to \(n - 1\) and \(n - 2\), rather by leaps over several whole numbers (CAJORI, PDF p. 158). The discovery of such an argument by a sequence of steps is attributed to Francesco Maurolico (in Latin Franciscus Maurolycus, 1494 to 1575) of Messina. It appears in the introduction to his Opuscula mathematica (Venice, 1575) (CAJORI, PDF p. 157-158). In 1656 John Wallis, in his Arithmetica infinitorum, inferred from several cases to all, as is the custom in natural science (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). Blaise Pascal used the argument by steps in his Traité du triangle arithmétique in 1662 (CAJORI, PDF p. 158). In 1686 Jakob Bernoulli rejected Wallis's procedure because it is not logically binding (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). A statement may hold for the first few numbers and fail at the next.

An inference from a few cases to all natural numbers does not always hold. Let us look at an example that we add ourselves: divide a circle by all possible segments between \(n\) points on the circle, so that no three of them meet in the same point. For \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) we get \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16\) parts, which suggests \(2^{n-1}\), but for \(n = 6\) there are 31 and not 32 (figure 1.67). Instead of Wallis's procedure Bernoulli proposed a proof from \(n\) to \(n + 1\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 347). One must show that the statement holds at \(n = 1\) and that from its validity at some \(n\) its validity at \(n + 1\) follows. From these two facts we get the statement for all natural numbers at once.

That such an argument is allowed is shown by the property of the natural numbers that we assumed in section 1.8.1: every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element. We write a claim that depends on a natural number as \(P(n)\). It is a statement that for every natural number \(n\) is either true or not, for example the statement \(b_{\mathrm{n}} - a_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/2^{n-1}\) about the length of the \(n\)-th bisection interval.

Theorem 1.44 Principle of induction (načelo indukcije)

Let \(P(n)\) be a statement that depends on the natural number \(n\). Suppose that:

  1. basis (začetek): the statement \(P(1)\) is true,
  2. induction step (indukcijski korak): for every natural number \(n\) the truth of the statement \(P(n)\) implies the truth of the statement \(P(n + 1)\).

Then the statement \(P(n)\) is true for every natural number \(n\).

Proof

We must show that there is no natural number for which the statement \(P(n)\) is false.

  1. Let \(S\) be the set of all natural numbers \(n\) for which the statement \(P(n)\) is not true. We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(S\) is not empty.
  2. Every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element (section 1.8.1). Let \(m\) be the smallest element of the set \(S\).
  3. By the basis \(P(1)\) is true, so \(1\) is not in \(S\), and \(m \neq 1\). Since \(m\) is a natural number different from \(1\), we have \(m \ge 2\), and \(m - 1\) is a natural number.
  4. The number \(m - 1\) is smaller than \(m\). Since \(m\) is the smallest element of the set \(S\), the number \(m - 1\) is not in \(S\), and the statement \(P(m - 1)\) is true.
  5. So by the induction step with \(n = m - 1\) the statement \(P(m - 1 + 1)\), that is, \(P(m)\), is true as well. Then \(m\) is not in \(S\), which contradicts step 2.
  6. The assumption of step 1 is false. The set \(S\) is empty, and \(P(n)\) is true for every natural number \(n\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 0.3.6 (PDF p. 12). The source accepts the property of the smallest element as an axiom (the well ordering property) and says in a remark that the argument also needs \(m - 1\) to be a natural number for every natural number \(m > 1\), which we accept with the school properties of the integers.

From history

The name mathematical induction first appears, as far as is known, in an article by Augustus De Morgan of 1838, and it was popularized by Todhunter in his Algebra (CAJORI, PDF p. 347).

Induction works like a row of dominoes (figure 1.68). The first domino falls, and this is the basis. Every domino that falls knocks over the next one, and this is the induction step. The proof tells why none of them can stay standing: among the standing dominoes there would be a first one, \(m\), and right before it the fallen domino \(m - 1\), which would have had to knock it over.

Let us write the proof of the third property of bisection by induction, because we will need it in a computation. The statement \(P(k)\) is \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\).

  1. Basis. We have \(b_1 - a_1 = 2 - 1 = 1\) and \(1/2^0 = 1\), so \(P(1)\) is true.
  2. Induction step. Let \(P(k)\) be true, that is, \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\). The midpoint \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{k}}) - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\) away from the left endpoint and \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}})\) away from the right one.
  3. The next interval is \([m_{\mathrm{k}}, b_{\mathrm{k}}]\) or \([a_{\mathrm{k}}, m_{\mathrm{k}}]\). In both cases, by step 2, its length is
    \[\tfrac12(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}) = \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = \frac{1}{2^k} ,\]
    which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction 1.44, \(P(k)\) is true for every natural number \(k\).

The first and the fourth property have the same structure: a basis at \(k = 1\) and a step that preserves the property. The second property speaks only about one step of the rule. It needs induction only through the third property, which guarantees \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) at every \(k\). Induction also tells why the bisection rule determines an interval for every \(k\): the first interval is given, and from every determined interval the rule determines the next one. We will build sequences in the same way later too (LEBL1 Example 2.2.8, PDF p. 66, shows by induction that all terms of such a sequence are defined).

We will also need that the lengths \(1/2^{k-1}\) go to zero. We will compare them with the fractions \(1/k\), which go to zero by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. For this we need the inequality \(2^{k-1} \ge k\). The numbers show that \(2^{k-1}\) is much larger than \(k\). At \(k = 10\), for example, \(2^9 = 512\).

Lemma 1.45 Powers of 2 are at least as large as the natural numbers (potence števila 2 so vsaj tolikšne kot naravna števila)

For every natural number \(k\) we have \(2^{k-1} \ge k\).

Proof

We use the principle of induction 1.44 for the statement \(P(k)\): \(2^{k-1} \ge k\).

  1. Basis. At \(k = 1\) we have \(2^0 = 1 \ge 1\).
  2. Induction step. Let \(2^{k-1} \ge k\) hold. We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(2\), which does not reverse it, and get \(2^k = 2 \cdot 2^{k-1} \ge 2k\).
  3. We have \(2k = k + k \ge k + 1\), because \(k \ge 1\). The inequalities of step 2 and of this step form a chain, so \(2^k \ge k + 1\), which is the statement \(P(k + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction, \(2^{k-1} \ge k\) for every natural number \(k\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 0.3.7 (PDF p. 13) proves \(2^{n-1} \le n!\) in the same way. The lemma is not from the source.

The induction step multiplied the inequality for \(k\) by \(2\) and compared the result with \(k + 1\). We will meet such a step, which turns the claim for \(k\) into the claim for \(k + 1\) with one computation, many more times.

1.10.3 A trapped sequence

Do the lengths of the bisection intervals, that is, \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots\), go to zero? We could repeat the computation from the proof of lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n and find a place \(N\) for every error \(\eta\). There is a shorter way, though. The lengths are positive, and by lemma 1.45 on the powers of 2 they are not larger than the fractions \(1/k\). So every length is trapped between \(0\) and \(1/k\) (figure 1.69). From below they are held by the constant number \(0\), from above by the fractions \(1/k\), which go to \(0\). The lengths have no choice but to go to \(0\). The next lemma writes this idea down for any sequences.

Lemma 1.46 Squeeze lemma (lema o sendviču)

Let \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\), \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) be sequences such that \(y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\). If the sequences \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge to the same number \(d\), the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) also converges to \(d\).

Proof

We must show that from some place on all terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) are closer to the number \(d\) than a given error.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N_1\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). The inequality \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) means \(-\eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\), and its left half gives \(d - \eta < y_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. Since \((z_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|z_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_2\). The right half of the inequality \(-\eta < z_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\) gives \(z_{\mathrm{n}} < d + \eta\).
  4. Let \(N\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). For \(n \ge N\) steps 2 and 3 hold at once. With the assumption \(y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}}\) the inequalities form a chain:
    \[d - \eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le z_{\mathrm{n}} < d + \eta .\]
  5. We subtract \(d\) from all sides and get \(-\eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} - d < \eta\), that is, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - d| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(d\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 2.2.1 (PDF p. 61).

From each of the two sequences the proof needed only one side of the band (figure 1.70). From the place \(N_1\) on, the lower sequence does not let the terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) below the band, and from the place \(N_2\) on, the upper one does not let them above it. From the larger of the two places on, both sides are closed. The name of the lemma gives the same picture: the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is squeezed between two sequences like the filling between two slices of bread.

The lemma needs both bounds to converge to the same number. The terms of the sequence \(((-1)^n)\) are trapped between the constant sequences \(-1, -1, \dots\) and \(1, 1, \dots\), and still they have no limit (lemma 1.40 on a sequence without a limit). Bounds with different limits let the terms jump between them.

Now we apply the lemma to the lengths of the bisection intervals.

Corollary 1.47 Limit of the powers of one half (limita potenc polovice)

The sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\), that is, \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots\), converges to \(0\).

Proof

We must show that the sequence is trapped between two sequences with the limit \(0\), and use the squeeze lemma.

  1. For every \(k\) we have \(1/2^{k-1} > 0\), because \(2^{k-1} > 0\).
  2. For every \(k\) we have \(1/2^{k-1} \le 1/k\). We write the difference over a common denominator,
    \[\frac1k - \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = \frac{2^{k-1} - k}{k \cdot 2^{k-1}} .\]
    By lemma 1.45 on the powers of 2 the numerator is not negative, and the denominator is positive, so the difference is not negative.
  3. By steps 1 and 2, \(0 \le 1/2^{k-1} \le 1/k\) for every \(k\).
  4. The constant sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) converges to \(0\) (section 1.9.2), and the sequence \((1/k)\) does so by lemma 1.39 on the limit of the sequence 1/n. So by the squeeze lemma 1.46 with the lower sequence \(0, 0, 0, \dots\) and the upper sequence \((1/k)\), the sequence \((1/2^{k-1})\) also converges to \(0\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 2.2.2 (PDF pp. 61-62) proves in the same way that \(1/(n\sqrt n)\) goes to \(0\). The corollary is not from the source.

The comparison with \(1/k\) is rough, but it is enough. At \(k = 11\) the length of the interval is \(1/2^{10} = 1/1024 < 0{,}001\), while the fraction \(1/11 = 0{,}0909\dots\) is almost a hundred times larger. The squeeze lemma only needs the upper sequence to lie above the sequence of lengths and to go to the same limit. How tightly it follows the sequence does not matter.

1.10.4 Limit and inequalities

By the fourth property the left endpoints of the bisection all lie in the interval \([1, 2]\). If they converge, does their limit lie in it too? We expect that it does, since the terms come arbitrarily close to it and all of them are in the interval. Let us show that this is so, and let us look at where the expectation breaks down.

Lemma 1.48 Limits preserve non-strict inequalities (limita ohrani nestroge neenakosti)

Let the sequences \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converge.

  1. If \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le y_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\), then \(\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} \le \lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} y_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. If \(a\) and \(b\) are numbers such that \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\), then \(a \le \lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\).
Proof

We must show both items. The second follows from the first, applied with constant sequences.

1. Inequality between the limits.

  1. Let \(x = \lim x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(y = \lim y_{\mathrm{n}}\). We argue by contradiction: suppose that \(x > y\).
  2. As the error we take half the distance between the limits, \(\eta = \tfrac12(x - y)\), which is positive. Then
    \[x - \eta = \tfrac12(x + y) = y + \eta ,\]
    so the band of width \(\eta\) around \(x\) and the band of width \(\eta\) around \(y\) touch in the middle, at \(\tfrac12(x + y)\) (figure 1.71).
  3. Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(x\), by the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38 there is a place \(N_1\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - x| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_1\). The left half of the inequality \(-\eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} - x < \eta\) gives \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > x - \eta = \tfrac12(x + y)\).
  4. Since \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(y\), there is a place \(N_2\) such that \(|y_{\mathrm{n}} - y| < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N_2\). The right half of the inequality \(-\eta < y_{\mathrm{n}} - y < \eta\) gives \(y_{\mathrm{n}} < y + \eta = \tfrac12(x + y)\).
  5. Let \(n\) be the larger of the numbers \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). By steps 3 and 4, \(y_{\mathrm{n}} < \tfrac12(x + y) < x_{\mathrm{n}}\), so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > y_{\mathrm{n}}\). This contradicts the assumption \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le y_{\mathrm{n}}\). So \(x > y\) does not hold, and \(x \le y\).

2. Bounds of the terms are bounds of the limit.

  1. The constant sequence \(a, a, a, \dots\) converges to \(a\) (section 1.9.2). Since \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\), item 1, applied with this constant sequence in place of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and with the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) in place of the sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\), gives \(a \le \lim x_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. The constant sequence \(b, b, b, \dots\) converges to \(b\). Since \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\), item 1 gives \(\lim x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 2.2.3 and Cor. 2.2.4 (PDF p. 62). The source proves the first item without contradiction, by showing \(x - y < \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\). The proof by contradiction is not from the source.

The proof used the same picture as the proof of the uniqueness of the limit (figure 1.63). Two bands around two different numbers, each as wide as half the distance between the numbers, do not overlap. There, late terms of the same sequence cannot be in both bands. Here the late terms \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) would be in the upper band and the late terms \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the lower one, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) would be above \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\).

For the left endpoints of the bisection, item 2 says: if they converge, their limit is in the interval \([1, 2]\), because all \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) are in it.

A limit, however, does not necessarily preserve a strict inequality. Take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m that hangs under its own weight, and the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = 2aL - a/n\) of its top pieces of length \(1/n\) m from section 1.9.1. All of them are smaller than \(2aL\), because \(a/n > 0\). Their limit, though, is \(2aL\) (section 1.9.2). The dots in figure 1.57 approach the line \(2aL\) without reaching it. Of the strict inequalities \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} < 2aL\) only \(2aL \le 2aL\) remains in the limit. That is why the lemma speaks of non-strict inequalities. From the strict inequalities \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < y_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\) we may conclude only \(\lim x_{\mathrm{n}} \le \lim y_{\mathrm{n}}\), because every strict inequality is also a non-strict one.

So it matters whether an interval contains its endpoints (figure 1.72). The terms of the sequence \((1/n)\) lie in the half-open interval \((0, 1]\), but their limit \(0\) does not, because it is not larger than \(0\). With a closed interval this cannot happen: if all terms of a convergent sequence are in \([a, b]\), then by item 2 the limit is there too. We will need this when we search for points as limits. The point we find must lie in the interval in which we search.

1.10.5 Monotone sequences

Let us return to the left endpoints of the bisection: \(1\), \(1{,}5\), \(1{,}5\), \(1{,}625\), \(1{,}6875\), \(1{,}71875\), \(1{,}71875\), \(1{,}7265625\) and so on. No term is smaller than the previous one, and none is larger than \(2\). Such terms cannot go back down and cannot get past \(2\). It seems that they must pile up somewhere. Where? At the smallest number that no term exceeds, that is, at the supremum of the set of terms. Here the completeness axiom appears again, and it guarantees that this supremum exists without our knowing it. First let us name the sequences that do not turn back.

Definition 1.49 Monotone sequence (monotono zaporedje)

A sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing (naraščajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\), and decreasing (padajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\). A sequence is monotone (monotono) if it is increasing or decreasing. A sequence is strictly increasing (strogo naraščajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\), and strictly decreasing (strogo padajoče) if \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) for every \(n\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 2.1.9 (PDF p. 55), the strictly increasing sequence at Def. 2.1.16 (PDF p. 58).

So an increasing sequence allows equal consecutive terms, and a strictly increasing one does not. Some books mean strictly increasing by the word increasing, so when reading the sources it is worth checking the definition. The left endpoints of the bisection are an increasing sequence by the second property from section 1.10.1, but not a strictly increasing one, because \(a_2 = a_3 = 1{,}5\). At \(k = 2\) the rule kept the left half and moved only the right endpoint. The right endpoints are a decreasing sequence, and \(b_1 = b_2 = 2\). The strains of the pieces of the hanging bar are strictly increasing:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}+1} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{n}} = \Big(2aL - \frac{a}{n+1}\Big) - \Big(2aL - \frac{a}{n}\Big) = \frac{a}{n} - \frac{a}{n+1} = \frac{a}{n(n+1)} > 0 .\]

The sequence \((1/n)\) is strictly decreasing. A constant sequence is increasing and decreasing at once. The sequence \(((-1)^n)\) is not monotone, because \(x_1 = -1 < 1 = x_2\), but \(x_2 = 1 > -1 = x_3\).

The definition compares only neighbouring terms. In proofs we will need that in an increasing sequence no later term is smaller than any earlier one. This is a claim about infinitely many places, so we prove it by induction.

Lemma 1.50 Terms of a monotone sequence (členi monotonega zaporedja)

If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing, then \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{m}}\) for any natural numbers \(n \le m\). If it is decreasing, then \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{m}}\) for any natural numbers \(n \le m\).

Proof

We must show that a later term of an increasing sequence is not smaller than an earlier one. We keep the place \(n\) fixed and step forward by induction.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be increasing and \(n\) a natural number. For a natural number \(j\) let \(P(j)\) be the statement \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\). The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\) is \(j - 1\) places after the term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), so as \(j\) runs through \(1, 2, 3, \dots\), we cover all terms from \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) on.
  2. Basis. The statement \(P(1)\) is \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\), which is true.
  3. Induction step. Let \(P(j)\) hold, that is, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1}\). Since the sequence is increasing, \(x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}-1} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}}\). The inequalities form a chain, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{n}+\mathrm{j}}\), which is the statement \(P(j + 1)\).
  4. By the principle of induction 1.44, \(P(j)\) holds for every natural number \(j\). For \(m \ge n\) we take \(j = m - n + 1\) and get \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  5. For a decreasing sequence steps 1 to 4 are the same, only with \(\ge\) in place of \(\le\).

Source: LEBL1 uses the claim in the proof of Thm 2.1.10 (PDF p. 55) and says that it follows by induction. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma builds a comparison of any two terms from comparisons of neighbouring ones. Between \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{m}}\) stands a chain of \(m - n\) comparisons of neighbouring terms, and induction joins them all.

Now we can say where an increasing sequence goes if it cannot get past some bound (figure 1.73). Let \(s\) be the supremum of the set of its terms. No term is above \(s\). The number \(s - \eta\), which for a given error \(\eta > 0\) is smaller than \(s\), is no longer an upper bound, though, so some term \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\) exceeds it. Since the sequence increases, all later terms are at least as large as \(x_{\mathrm{N}}\), so they are above \(s - \eta\), and at the same time at most \(s\). From the place \(N\) on all of them are in the band between \(s - \eta\) and \(s\).

Theorem 1.51 Monotone convergence theorem (izrek o monotonih zaporedjih)

A monotone sequence converges if and only if it is bounded. If the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is increasing and bounded, then

\[\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = \sup\{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\} ,\]

and if it is decreasing and bounded, then

\[\lim_{\mathrm{n} \to \infty} x_{\mathrm{n}} = \inf\{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\} .\]
Proof

We must show that a convergent monotone sequence is bounded, and that a bounded monotone sequence converges to the supremum or the infimum of the set of its terms.

A convergent monotone sequence is bounded.

  1. By lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence every convergent sequence is bounded, so every convergent monotone sequence is bounded too.

An increasing bounded sequence converges to the supremum.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be increasing and bounded, and let \(A = \{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\}\) be the set of its terms. The set \(A\) is nonempty, because it contains \(x_1\), and bounded above, because the sequence is bounded (definition 1.37).
  2. By the completeness axiom 1.32, \(A\) has a supremum \(s\). We will show that the sequence converges to \(s\).
  3. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 2, there is an element of the set \(A\) that is larger than \(s - \eta\). The elements of the set \(A\) are the terms of the sequence, so there is a place \(N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{N}} > s - \eta\).
  4. Let \(n \ge N\). By lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge x_{\mathrm{N}}\), and so by step 3, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} > s - \eta\).
  5. The term \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is an element of the set \(A\), and the supremum \(s\) is an upper bound of this set, so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s\).
  6. By steps 4 and 5, \(s - \eta < x_{\mathrm{n}} \le s\). So the difference \(s - x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not negative and is smaller than \(\eta\), that is, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - s| = s - x_{\mathrm{n}} < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the sequence converges to \(s\).

A decreasing bounded sequence converges to the infimum.

  1. Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) be decreasing and bounded, and let \(A = \{x_{\mathrm{n}} : n \in \mathbb N\}\). The set \(A\) is nonempty and bounded below, so by theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 1, it has an infimum \(i\).
  2. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By item 2 of the same theorem there is an element of the set \(A\) that is smaller than \(i + \eta\), that is, a place \(N\) such that \(x_{\mathrm{N}} < i + \eta\).
  3. Let \(n \ge N\). By lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \le x_{\mathrm{N}}\), and so by step 2, \(x_{\mathrm{n}} < i + \eta\).
  4. The infimum \(i\) is a lower bound of the set \(A\), so \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \ge i\).
  5. By steps 3 and 4, \(i \le x_{\mathrm{n}} < i + \eta\), so \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - i| = x_{\mathrm{n}} - i < \eta\) for every \(n \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the sequence converges to \(i\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 2.1.10 (PDF pp. 55-56). The source leaves the decreasing case to the reader.

Where is the completeness axiom in the proof? In step 2 of the second part. It guarantees the number \(s\) to which the sequence converges. We did not know the limit in advance, and the proof named it as the supremum of the set of terms. An increasing sequence always has the lower bound \(x_1\), because by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence \(x_1 \le x_{\mathrm{n}}\) for every \(n\). So it is enough to check that it is bounded above (LEBL1, PDF p. 56). We will use the theorem every time we must show that some number exists without being able to write it down. So it is one of the results that carry the road to the statement about the change of a function between two numbers.

The strains of the pieces of the hanging bar are strictly increasing and all smaller than \(2aL\), so by the theorem they converge even before we compute the limit. The limit is unique and equal to \(2aL\), so the theorem also tells us that \(2aL\) is the supremum of the set of strains: the smallest number that no strain of a piece exceeds. Boundedness is needed. The sequence \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) is strictly increasing, but it is not bounded, because by the Archimedean property 1.35 the set of natural numbers is not bounded above. So by the first part of the theorem it does not converge.

Now we can answer the question from section 1.10.1. Let us show that the left endpoints of the bisection converge to \(\sqrt 3\) (figure 1.74). Let us recall what we know about them: \(a_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}^2\), \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = 1/2^{k-1}\), and all endpoints are in the interval \([1, 2]\). First we show that the left endpoints converge, without using \(\sqrt 3\).

  1. The sequence \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) is increasing by the second property and bounded by the fourth, because all its terms are in \([1, 2]\).
  2. So by the monotone convergence theorem 1.51 it converges to the number \(c = \sup\{a_{\mathrm{k}} : k \in \mathbb N\}\).
  3. The number \(c\) lies in every bisection interval. Since the supremum is an upper bound, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\). Let us also show \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\). Let \(j\) be any natural number. For \(j \le k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}} < b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence and by the third property, which gives \(b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\). For \(j > k\) we have \(a_{\mathrm{j}} < b_{\mathrm{j}} \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) by the third property and by the same lemma for the decreasing sequence \((b_{\mathrm{k}})\). So the number \(b_{\mathrm{k}}\) is an upper bound of all the left endpoints, and the supremum \(c\) is not larger than any upper bound. So \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Up to here we have used nothing about the number \(\sqrt 3\). Let us now show that \(c = \sqrt 3\).

  1. The number \(\sqrt 3\) lies in every bisection interval, \(a_{\mathrm{k}} < \sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\), as we showed in section 1.10.1.
  2. Two numbers in the same interval are at most its length apart. From \(c \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le \sqrt 3\) it follows that \(c - \sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\), and from \(\sqrt 3 \le b_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le c\) that \(\sqrt 3 - c \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}}\). So by the third property
    \[|c - \sqrt 3| \le b_{\mathrm{k}} - a_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\]
    for every natural number \(k\).
  3. On the left side of the inequality stands a constant sequence, which converges to \(|c - \sqrt 3|\), and on the right side a sequence that converges to \(0\) by corollary 1.47 on the limit of the powers of one half. By lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 1, \(|c - \sqrt 3| \le 0\). An absolute value is not negative, so \(|c - \sqrt 3| = 0\) and \(c = \sqrt 3\).

So the left endpoints of the bisection converge to \(\sqrt 3\). At \(k = 12\), for example, \(a_{12} = 3547/2048 = 1{,}73193359375\), and this is less than \(1/2^{11} = 0{,}000488\dots\) away from \(\sqrt 3 = 1{,}7320508\dots\), in fact \(0{,}000117\dots\) away.

Why was the completeness axiom needed? All the left endpoints are fractions, but their limit \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction, because by lemma 1.27 no rational number has square 3. If we knew only the fractions, \((a_{\mathrm{k}})\) would be an increasing bounded sequence of fractions that has no limit among the fractions. A limit that was a fraction would be equal to \(\sqrt 3\) by lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence. So the monotone convergence theorem does not hold among the fractions, and the gap is exactly in the step where its proof uses the completeness axiom. The hole among the fractions from figure 1.52 shows up here as a sequence that finds no limit among the fractions.

Bisection worked because at every step we knew which half to keep: the one that contains \(\sqrt 3\), which we recognized by the square of the midpoint. The next section applies bisection to an arbitrary bounded sequence. We will keep the half that contains infinitely many terms, and in this way choose from the sequence infinitely many terms that converge.
