Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.4 Critical speed and a conserved quantity

In section 26.3 we found the frequencies of the modes of the moving string, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \dfrac{n\pi c}{L}\Big(1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}\Big)\) (theorem 26.13). With a higher transport speed \(v\) they all fall, and at \(v = c\) they are all zero. For our band of example A (steel, \(L = 1\) m, \(\sigma = 100\) MPa, \(\mu = 0{,}3510\) kg/m, \(T = 4500\) N, \(c = 113{,}23\) m/s) the first frequency is \(56{,}61\) Hz at \(v = 0\), \(49{,}55\) Hz at \(v = 40\) m/s and \(0\) at \(v = c\). Why exactly at \(c\), and what does it mean for a band that runs so fast? This is the first question of the section. The second is about energy. For the strings of section 13.1 the sum of kinetic and elastic energy stays constant during the motion. Does this hold here too, where the material runs through the eyelets? And if not, does any quantity exist that is conserved and that tells us in the end that the motion stays small?

From history

Archibald and Emslie modelled a belt running from an eccentric pulley to a smooth pulley as a string running through eyelets and excited by the harmonic motion of an eyelet. They found that the natural frequencies decrease with the transport speed and that the moving string, at a sufficiently high speed, becomes unstable by divergence (divergence instability, divergenčna nestabilnost) (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2, a work of 1958 by the reference list on PDF p. 21). Why their belt matters to us: the span between two pulleys is our moving string, and their finding is exactly what this section shows, the frequencies of section 26.3 and the speed at which they fail. Swope and Ames investigated with the method of characteristics how a disturbance spreads along such a string when it is excited at an end. They predicted irregular behaviour of the string near the critical speed, that is the speed of waves in the material (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2, a work of 1963 by the reference list on PDF p. 21), which for us is \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) (definition 13.4).

The story tells two things: at the speed \(c\) something jumps, and this speed is the same number as the wave speed in a string at rest. Let us see why. We stay with the model of section 26.1: the moving string (definition 26.3) of length \(L\), mass \(\mu\) per unit length, tension \(T\) and transport speed \(v > 0\) in the eyelets, which stand at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), with the equation

\[\mu\,\big(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\big) = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} \tag{26.16}\]

from theorem 26.4. By idealization 4 the slope is small, \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}005\). In this section the letter \(T\) is always the tension force, not the kinetic energy of chapters 8 and 9, and \(v\) is the transport speed, not the deflection.

26.4.1 When the tension is just enough

Let us look first at the simplest possible motion: a string whose shape does not change. Like water in a steady channel: the shape of the channel rests and the material flows along it. Can this be a motion of the moving string? The answer is given by the force on a piece of material, so we build figure 26.25.

In the left panel we take the axis \(x\) and on it the string, held in the eyelets at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). We have given it a resting shape \(w(x) = h\sin(\pi x/L)\), where \(h\) is the largest deflection. The string runs through the eyelets with speed \(v\), which in the left panel is half of \(c\), so the arrow is half of the one in the right panel. Take the piece of material that is at this moment over the interval from \(a\) to \(b\), that is the same material points that we follow as they run (section 26.1). What acts on it? The neighbouring parts of the string pull the piece with the force \(T\) along the tangent, each force starting at the point where it acts. The horizontal components of these two forces are opposite and cancel, the vertical ones add up to one force \(T\sigma\) with \(\sigma = w_{\mathrm{x}}(b) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\). Here idealization 4 enters. The vertical component of the force \(T\) along the tangent is \(T\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of the tangent to the axis \(x\), and we write \(T w_{\mathrm{x}} = T\tan\theta\). At a slope of \(0{,}005\) the numbers \(\tan\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) differ by \(0{,}00125\) %, so we may. At a slope of \(0{,}1\) the difference would already be \(0{,}5\) % and the force \(T\sigma\) wrong by that much. The number \(\sigma\) is negative, because the slope decreases along the piece (the string bulges upwards), so this force points down. What is it equal to? By Newton's second law the sum of forces on a body is mass times acceleration, which for a piece of material is written by equation (26.4) of section 26.1. Since the shape does not change, \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), and by lemma 26.1 the acceleration of each material point is only the centripetal one, \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\): the point runs along a curved curve with speed \(v\) and something must pull it towards the centre of curvature. The sum of these accelerations along the piece, multiplied by the mass, is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \mu v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \mu v^2\sigma\) by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55). This is the orange arrow, also pointing down. We draw the two arrows from two different points of the piece, so that they do not overlap.

Each arrow has its role. The blue one is what the piece gets from the neighbouring parts, the orange one what the piece needs to stay on the steady curve. In the left panel the blue is four times the orange. So the piece would get more than it needs: the surplus \(T\sigma - \mu v^2\sigma = (T - \mu v^2)\sigma\) would pull it towards the straight line and the shape would not stay. On the band of example A and a piece from \(0{,}3\) to \(0{,}7\) m with \(h = 1{,}5\) mm the blue arrow is the same at every speed, \(T\sigma = -24{,}93\) N, while the orange one \(\mu v^2\sigma\) grows with the square of the speed:

\(v\) (m/s) \(v/c\) \(T\sigma\) (N) \(\mu v^2\sigma\) (N) surplus \((T - \mu v^2)\sigma\) (N)
\(40\) \(0{,}353\) \(-24{,}93\) \(-3{,}11\) \(-21{,}82\)
\(56{,}61\) (left panel) \(0{,}5\) \(-24{,}93\) \(-6{,}23\) \(-18{,}70\)
\(101{,}90\) \(0{,}9\) \(-24{,}93\) \(-20{,}19\) \(-4{,}74\)
\(113{,}23\) (right panel) \(1\) \(-24{,}93\) \(-24{,}93\) \(0\)

As \(v\) grows towards \(c\) the surplus falls to zero. In the right panel the string runs with speed \(v = c\) (the longer arrow \(v\)), the same piece feels the same tension forces and their sum \(T\sigma\), and the needed force is \(\mu c^2\sigma = T\sigma\), because \(\mu c^2 = T\) (definition 13.4). The two arrows are equal: the tension is exactly enough for the material points to run along the curve, and there is no surplus that would straighten the shape. Is this shape special? It is not: the other shapes below the right panel have the same property. The lemma below states this exactly.

Lemma 26.19 Arbitrary steady shape at the speed \(c\) (poljubna stalna oblika pri hitrosti \(c\))

Let \(v = c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) and let \(f : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) be a twice continuously differentiable function with \(f(0) = f(L) = 0\). Then every such shape \(w(x, t) = f(x)\) is a moving string (definition 26.3). For \(v \ne c\) the only steady shape \(w(x, t) = f(x)\) that is a moving string is \(f = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the function satisfies equation (26.16) exactly when \((T - \mu v^2) f'' = 0\), and that the claim follows from this.

  1. For the steady shape \(w = f(x)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), hence also \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = 0\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = 0\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tx}} = 0\). The derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = f'\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = f''\) are continuous, as definition 26.3 requires, and the boundary conditions \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) are satisfied.
  2. For \(w = f(x)\) equation (26.16) is rewritten as \(\mu v^2 f'' = T f''\), so \((T - \mu v^2)\,f'' = 0\). For the piece over \([a, b]\) this is the comparison of the blue and the orange arrow: the integral of \(T f''\) along the piece is \(T\sigma\), the integral of \(\mu v^2 f''\) is \(\mu v^2\sigma\).
  3. If \(v = c\), then \(T - \mu v^2 = T - \mu c^2 = 0\) by definition 13.4, and the two arrows are equally long on every piece of every shape. The equation holds for every \(f\), because the connection with the equation of the piece (26.4) is an equivalence by theorem 26.4.
  4. If \(v \ne c\), then \(T - \mu v^2 \ne 0\) (in the left panel the arrows are not equal), so \(f'' = 0\) everywhere, hence \(f'\) is constant and \(f\) is a straight line (corollary 1.86 twice). From \(f(0) = f(L) = 0\) it follows that \(f = 0\).

Source: WM1990, appendix, section 1 (PDF p. 6): every natural frequency of the travelling string is zero at \(v = 1\) (dimensionless, that is \(v = c\)), the critical speed is \(v_{\mathrm{c}} = 1\). The claim about an arbitrary steady shape and its proof are ours.

What does the claim say? At \(v = c\) we have infinitely many resting equilibria, each with its own shape. At \(v \neq c\) a string at rest is only straight. So the moving string at \(v = c\) loses a property that the string without flow has: the string of section 13.1 always has, after we displace it, a force that returns it towards the straight line. This restoring is measured by \(T - \mu v^2\), as we saw in the surplus of the blue arrow over the orange one. Let us test it on the band. At \(v = 40\) m/s and \(w(x) = 1{,}5\text{ mm}\cdot\sin(\pi x/L)\) the residual of the equation \((\mu v^2 - T)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) has the largest value \(58{,}31\) N/m, at \(v = 0{,}9c\) only \(12{,}66\) N/m, at \(v = c\) it is zero (recomputed in num-04.out).

Definition 26.20 Critical speed of the moving string (kritična hitrost gibajoče se strune)

The critical speed (kritična hitrost) of a moving string with mass \(\mu\) per unit length and tension \(T\) is the number

\[v_{\mathrm{c}} = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = c , \tag{26.17}\]

in m/s, that is the wave speed of definition 13.4. The transport speed \(v > 0\) is subcritical (podkritična) for \(v < v_{\mathrm{c}}\), critical for \(v = v_{\mathrm{c}}\) and supercritical (nadkritična) for \(v > v_{\mathrm{c}}\).

Source: WM1990, PDF p. 2 and appendix, section 1, PDF p. 6 (string: \(v_{\mathrm{c}} = 1\) in dimensionless form), CHEN2005, PDF p. 2 (Swope and Ames: the critical speed is the characteristic wave speed in the material). The form as a definition is ours.

The word critical comes from the Greek krisis, which means decision or break. The name fits, because at this speed the behaviour of the string jumps: below it every resting shape except the straight line has a surplus of force, at it none, above it \(T - \mu v^2\) is negative. On the piece of the table the orange arrow would at \(v = 1{,}2c\) be \(1{,}44\) times as long as the blue one: the flow needs \(-35{,}90\) N, the tension gives \(-24{,}93\) N, and the shortfall of \(10{,}97\) N points away from the straight line. What follows from this at a supercritical speed we do not claim here, because the model with two fixed eyelets is no longer good for \(v > c\). The reason is the two waves of section 26.2. Every motion is a sum \(F\big(x - (c + v)t\big) + G\big(x + (c - v)t\big)\) (lemma 26.6, which holds for every \(v \ge 0\)). For \(v < c\) the second wave runs against the flow with speed \(c - v\) and carries into the span what the eyelet at \(x = L\) determines. For \(v > c\) the number \(c - v\) is negative: the flow carries this wave forward too, with speed \(v - c\), like a swimmer who swims slower than the river runs. Nothing then returns into the span from the eyelet at \(x = L\). The deflection at \(x = L\) is already determined by what arrives from the left, so the condition \(w(L, t) = 0\) can no longer be prescribed. On the band of example A at \(v = 1{,}2c\) both waves would run forward, with speeds \(249{,}1\) and \(22{,}6\) m/s. WM1990 (PDF p. 2) also requires the speed to be subcritical, as will we in the theorems that require \(v < c\). The critical speed of the band of example A is \(v_{\mathrm{c}} = c = 113{,}23\) m/s. Above it this model does not describe the band.

From history

Archibald and Emslie found the divergence instability at a sufficiently high speed in the story above, and WM1990 (PDF p. 6, appendix) shows that at \(v = c\) every natural frequency of the string vanishes. At a zero frequency the difference between oscillation and static deformation is lost, and this is the lemma above: the string stays in every shape. Hence divergence (divergence): the deflection does not oscillate but stays.

The story showed where the name divergence comes from. Let us return to the model and to the second question: energy.

26.4.2 The conserved quantity

For the strings of section 13.1 the sum \(\tfrac12\mu\int w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 + \tfrac12 T\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) is constant during the motion: the first half is the kinetic, the second the elastic energy. Let us try the same here. The kinetic energy of the material is no longer only \(\tfrac12\mu\int w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\), because the material runs. In figure 26.26 the top shows a piece of string and on it a material point. The string itself moves up with the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the point also slides along it with the speed \(v\) over the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), which lifts it with the velocity \(v w_{\mathrm{x}}\). The transverse velocity of the point is the sum of the two, \(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v w_{\mathrm{x}}\) (lemma 26.1), so the kinetic energy per unit length is \(\tfrac12\mu\,(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v w_{\mathrm{x}})^2\). When we expand the square we get three parts, shown at the bottom: the square with side \(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v w_{\mathrm{x}}\) splits into the square \(w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\), two rectangles \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\,v w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the square \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\). Summed over the span, they are called by chapter 8 (theorem 8.97) \(T_2\), \(T_1\) and \(T_0\):

\[T_2 = \tfrac12\mu\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad T_1 = \mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad T_0 = \tfrac12\mu v^2\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{26.18}\]

where \(T_2\) is the part quadratic in \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(T_1\) the mixed part, linear in \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), and \(T_0\) the part without the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\). The elastic energy is \(V = \tfrac12 T\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). Why this expansion at all? Because the energy integral \(\mathcal{E}^* = T_2 - T_0 + V\) of chapter 8 (theorem 8.124) warns us that with time-dependent constraints it is this combination that is conserved, not \(T + V\), as long as \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\), \(T_0\) and \(V\) do not contain the time. Does this hold here too? Theorem 8.124 is proved for a system with finitely many coordinates, and strings have infinitely many, so we do not use it but check the same idea directly. The test is simple: \(\mathcal{E}^*\) should be a conserved quantity (definition 8.119).

From history

Chubachi was the first to discuss the periodicity of energy transfer in a moving string (CHEN2005, PDF p. 6, a work of 1958 by PDF p. 21). Miranker discussed the energy of the moving string and from the equation without excitation derived an expression for the rate of change of energy, which is usually not zero (CHEN2005, PDF p. 6, year 1960 by PDF p. 21). Wickert and Mote pointed out that this expression describes only the local rate of change, since it does not account for the flow of energy through the supports (CHEN2005, PDF p. 6, a work of 1989 by PDF p. 22). Miranker also showed that there is another functional that is constant for the moving string (CHEN2005, PDF p. 7), while the energy of the span changes. We shall check all this: first the constant functional, then the expression for the energy.

The story showed two things: the energy of the span is not constant, something else is. Now we prove it.

Theorem 26.21 Conserved quantity of the moving string (ohranjena količina gibajoče strune)

Let \(w\) be a moving string (definition 26.3) and

\[\mathcal{E}^*(t) = \tfrac12\int_0^L\Big(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, t)^2 + (T - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(x, t)^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x = T_2 - T_0 + V . \tag{26.19}\]

Then \(\mathcal{E}^*(t)\) is constant for every \(t \ge 0\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime}(t)\) is zero, that is, that the line is horizontal. We differentiate under the integral. The term with \(w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) is transformed by integration by parts so that it can be compared with the equation of motion. What then remains of the Coriolis term is a derivative in \(x\) and vanishes at the eyelets.

  1. Since \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) are continuous, by lemma 4.19 we differentiate under the integral: \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime}(t) = \int_0^L\big(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{tt}} + (T - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\big)\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. Since \(w(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\), we have \(w_{\mathrm{t}}(0, t) = 0\), and likewise \(w_{\mathrm{t}}(L, t) = 0\): the eyelets stand still.
  3. By theorem 1.131 \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = w_{\mathrm{tx}}\), and integration by parts (corollary 3.56) with \(f = w_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(g = w_{\mathrm{t}}\) gives \(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{tx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{t}}\big]_0^L - \int_0^L w_{\mathrm{xx}} w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{xx}} w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), because the eyelets make the square bracket zero by step 2.
  4. Steps 1 and 3 give \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime} = \int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}\,\big(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - (T - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)\mathrm{d}x\).
  5. Equation (26.16) is equivalent to \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - (T - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\). Insert into step 4: \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime} = -2\mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{tx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\mu v\int_0^L \big(w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\big)_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \((w_{\mathrm{t}}^2)_{\mathrm{x}} = 2 w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{tx}}\).
  6. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) the last integral is \(\big[w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\big]_0^L = 0\) by step 2. So \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime} = 0\), and by corollary 1.86 \(\mathcal{E}^*\) is constant. On the band this is the horizontal blue line in figure 26.28.

Source: CHEN2005 (33), PDF p. 7 (Miranker): \(S_{\mathrm{E}}[V] = \int_0^L \tfrac12\big[\rho V_{\mathrm{T}}^2 + (P - \rho c^2)V_{\mathrm{X}}^2\big]\mathrm{d}X\) is constant for the moving string. The proof by steps and the connection with theorem 8.124 are not from the source.

What does the theorem say? The mixed term \(2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) in the equation of motion (the Coriolis one, section 26.1) turns out in step 5 to be the \(x\)-derivative of the square of \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), which is zero at the eyelets. The velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) is zero at the eyelets because they stand still (idealization 6). The power of the Coriolis force on the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(-2\mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{tx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), is therefore zero, and a force without power does not change \(\mathcal{E}^*\). The connection with \(T_2 - T_0 + V\) tells what happened to \(T_0\): the kinetic energy of the flow, \(\tfrac12\mu v^2\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\), reduces the stiffness by \(\mu v^2\). Since \(T_0\) has no velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), it acts as a negative elastic energy. Why? \(V\) and \(T_0\) are multiples of the same integral, \(V = \tfrac12 T\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(T_0 = \tfrac12\mu v^2\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so in \(T_2 - T_0 + V\) they merge into \(\tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the elastic energy of a string with the tension \(T - \mu v^2\) in place of \(T\). On the band this is \(3938{,}4\) N of \(4500\) N at \(v = 40\) m/s (\(12{,}5\) % less), and only \(855{,}0\) N at \(v = 0{,}9c\) (\(81\) % less). This is the surplus of figure 26.25: the tension must spend a part of its force on making the material points run along a curved path, and only \(T - \mu v^2 = \mu(c^2 - v^2)\) is left for the return towards the straight line.

Let us test the conserved quantity on the band. For the first mode, \(w = a\sin(\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_1 t + k_1 x)\) with amplitude \(a = 1{,}5\) mm, \(\omega_1 = 311{,}32\) 1/s and \(k_1 = \pi v/(cL)\), at \(v = 40\) m/s (theorem 26.13) the largest slope \(a\pi/L = 4{,}71\cdot 10^{-3}\) matches the limit of idealization 4. A computation with NumPy gives \(\mathcal{E}^* = 21{,}86\) mJ at each of \(200001\) times spread evenly over one period (period \(\tau = 2\pi/\omega_1 = 20{,}18\) ms), the difference between the largest and the smallest value is below \(10^{-12}\) mJ. The residual of the equation of motion for this mode is below \(3\cdot 10^{-14}\) N/m.

Corollary 26.22 Boundedness of the motion below the critical speed (omejenost gibanja pod kritično hitrostjo)

Let \(w\) be a moving string (definition 26.3) with \(0 < v < c\), initial displacement \(w_0(x) = w(x, 0)\) and initial velocity \(w_1(x) = w_{\mathrm{t}}(x, 0)\). Then for every \(t \ge 0\) the bounds hold

\[\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x \le \int_0^L w_0'^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \frac{\mu}{T - \mu v^2}\int_0^L w_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x \le \frac{T - \mu v^2}{\mu}\int_0^L w_0'^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L w_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{26.20}\]

and for every \(x \in [0, L]\) we have \(|w(x, t)| \le \sqrt{L}\,\Big(\int_0^L w_0'^2\,\mathrm{d}x + \dfrac{\mu}{T - \mu v^2}\int_0^L w_1^2\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{1/2}\).

Proof

We must show that from the constancy of \(\mathcal{E}^*\) and from both of its terms being nonnegative each of the three inequalities follows. The displacement is obtained from the slope by the fundamental theorem and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

  1. By theorem 26.21 \(\mathcal{E}^*\) is constant, so \(\mathcal{E}^*(t) = \mathcal{E}^*(0) = \tfrac12\mu\int w_1^2 + \tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\int w_0'^2\).
  2. Since \(v < c\), we have \(T - \mu v^2 > 0\), so both terms in (26.19) are nonnegative: the curve lies above zero. Hence each of them is at most \(\mathcal{E}^*(0)\): \(\tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le \mathcal{E}^*(0)\) and \(\tfrac12\mu\int w_{\mathrm{t}}^2 \le \mathcal{E}^*(0)\).
  3. Division by \(\tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\) or \(\tfrac12\mu\) and insertion of \(\mathcal{E}^*(0)\) from step 1 give the first two inequalities. The right-hand side of the first is the blackwhite line in figure 26.27, the left-hand side the curve below it.
  4. Since \(w(0, t) = 0\) (the string starts at the eyelet at height zero), by the fundamental theorem the deflection at \(x\) is the sum of the slopes from the eyelet: \(w(x, t) = \int_0^x w_{\mathrm{x}}(s, t)\,\mathrm{d}s\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (lemma 6.19) for the product of the functions \(1\) and \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}|\) we have \(|w(x, t)| \le \sqrt{x}\,\big(\int_0^x w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big)^{1/2} \le \sqrt{L}\,\big(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big)^{1/2}\), and the first inequality gives the third.

Source: The claim is ours, the idea is the same Dirichlet criterion as in chapter 8 (theorem 8.131): a conserved quantity that is positive definite bounds the motion. WM1990 (PDF p. 2) requires a subcritical speed, because then the stiffness operator is positive definite.

What do the inequalities say? The motion stays bounded in energy: the initial data determine a bound that it never exceeds. On the band with \(a = 1{,}5\) mm and \(v = 40\) m/s (\(T - \mu v^2 = 3938{,}4\) N) figure 26.27 shows this for the first mode. The horizontal axis is time up to the period \(\tau = 20{,}18\) ms, the vertical one \(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) in \(10^{-6}\) m. The orange curve is this integral during the motion. It starts at \(\int w_0'^2 = 8{,}24\cdot 10^{-6}\) m, which is the first term of the bound. The second term of the bound is \(\mu\int w_1^2/(T - \mu v^2) = 2{,}87\cdot 10^{-6}\) m, and the sum of the two is the blackwhite line at \(1{,}110\cdot 10^{-5}\) m. The curve comes close to it twice in one period, at most up to \(1{,}072\cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and does not reach it: the difference is \(0{,}38\cdot 10^{-6}\) m, the bound is \(3{,}5\) % too high.

The bound for the displacement is \(3{,}33\) mm at an amplitude of \(1{,}5\) mm. At \(v = 0{,}9c\) we have \(T - \mu v^2 = 855{,}0\) N, so the coefficient of the second term is \(5{,}26\) times larger than at \(v = 0\) (\(1/(1 - 0{,}81)\)), and the sum of the bound is again \(1{,}110\cdot 10^{-5}\) m (for the first mode with amplitude \(a\) the bound is \(a^2\pi^2/(2L)\) at every speed \(v < c\), that is \(\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) of the resting shape \(a\sin(\pi x/L)\), recomputed with SymPy), the real maximum \(1{,}066\cdot 10^{-5}\) m. The bound does not say that the slope is small everywhere, because \(\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) bounds the average and not the largest values, so idealization 4 still has to be checked on the solution.

Where does the bound fail? Look back at lemma 26.19. At \(v = c\) we have \(T - \mu v^2 = 0\), and \(\mathcal{E}^*\) is zero for every resting shape \(w = f(x)\), even if \(f \neq 0\): the case \(f = 1{,}5\text{ mm}\cdot\sin(\pi x/L)\), the shape in the right panel of figure 26.25, has \(\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = 1{,}110\cdot 10^{-5}\) m and \(\mathcal{E}^* = 0\). The conserved quantity then tells nothing about the slope, and the bound (26.20) has a zero in the denominator. For \(v > c\) it is worse. The same shape gives \(\mathcal{E}^* = \tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\int w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 < 0\), at \(v = 1{,}2c\) it is \(-10{,}99\) mJ (the model is here outside the limit of the subcritical speed, the number only shows that \(\mathcal{E}^*\) is no longer nonnegative). The theorem still holds, since its proof does not require \(v < c\), but the corollary does not.

26.4.3 The energy of the span is not conserved

What remains is the second question: what about the sum of kinetic and elastic energy of the span, that is what everyone would expect? Let us call it

\[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = \tfrac12\int_0^L\Big(\mu\,\big(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\big)^2 + T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x = T_2 + T_1 + T_0 + V . \tag{26.21}\]

Here we have left out the constant part \(\tfrac12\mu v^2 L\) of the kinetic energy of the flow along the axis, which does not show in the derivative. Comparison with (26.19) tells that \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}} - \mathcal{E}^* = T_1 + 2T_0\), which is the difference between \(T + V\) and \(T_2 - T_0 + V\) of theorem 8.124. If \(T_1 + 2T_0\) changes during the motion, \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}\) cannot be constant. On the band of example A at \(v = 40\) m/s and \(a = 1{,}5\) mm we draw figure 26.28 as follows: the axis \(t\) runs to the period of the first mode \(\tau\) \(= 20{,}18\) ms and the vertical axis shows energy in mJ. The blue line is \(\mathcal{E}^* = 21{,}9\) mJ, which is constant, and the orange curve is \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}\), which oscillates between \(25{,}6\) and \(30{,}6\) mJ, the ratio of the largest and the smallest value is \(1{,}197\). Two cycles per period \(\tau\) arise because \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}\) contains squares of \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), which repeat after half an oscillation.

Why does \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}\) change? The answer is given by the work of forces, which we draw in figure 26.29. The span is not closed: material runs through it and the neighbouring parts of the string pull it at the eyelets. We take the string of the first mode of the band at \(t = 0\) (\(a = 1{,}5\) mm, \(v = 40\) m/s, slope strongly exaggerated) between the eyelets at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). Let us look first at the piece of material that is at this moment over \([0, L]\). At its ends the neighbours pull it with the force \(T\) along the tangent, forward at \(x = L\) and backward at \(x = 0\), and the power of a force is force times the velocity of the point in its direction. At an eyelet \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\) and the material point has the transverse velocity \(v w_{\mathrm{x}}\) (lemma 26.1 holds for points in \((0, L)\), at the eyelet this is the limit, since \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) are continuous), these are the blue arrows at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\). The transverse component of the force is \(T w_{\mathrm{x}}\), the thin vertical arrow next to each force. In the model of small slopes the lengthwise component of the force, \(T\), and the lengthwise velocity, \(v\), are the same at both eyelets, so the lengthwise parts of the power, \(T v\) at \(x = L\) and \(-T v\) at \(x = 0\), cancel and the transverse ones remain. This is the balance of the model (CHEN2005 (27), (28)), not an exact one: the lengthwise component is \(T\cos\theta\), and the correction \(T v(\cos\theta - 1) \approx -\tfrac12 T v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) is of the same order as the transverse power (on the band at \(x = 0\), \(2{,}00\) W against \(3{,}997\) W). The model contains neither this correction nor second-order lengthwise motion, so the lemma below follows from equation (26.16), and the balance only explains it. At \(x = L\) the transverse force and the transverse velocity point the same way, the power is \(T v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)^2\), on the band \(+0{,}791\) W. At \(x = 0\) the force is reversed, the power is \(-T v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)^2\), on the band \(-3{,}997\) W. The energy of the piece of material changes by the theorem on work and energy with the sum \(T v\,[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2]_0^L\). But the span is not this piece: in a short time \(h\) the piece moves by \(v h\) along \(x\), at the left end of the span material enters with energy per unit length \(\hat e(0, t)\), at the right end it leaves with \(\hat e(L, t)\). At an eyelet \(\hat e = \tfrac12(\mu v^2 + T)\,w_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \tfrac12\mu v^2\), since \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\). The energy of the span therefore changes by the work of forces minus the energy that the material carries away: \(v\,\hat e\) at the right end and brings in at the left. The constant \(\tfrac12\mu v^2\) cancels, and without it the material on the band brings in \(2{,}248\) W and carries away \(0{,}445\) W. What remains is stated by the next lemma.

Lemma 26.23 Rate of change of the energy of the span (sprememba energije razpona)

Let \(w\) be a moving string (definition 26.3). Then \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}\) of (26.21) is differentiable and

\[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}'(t) = \frac{v}{2}\,\big(T - \mu v^2\big)\,\Big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)^2 - w_{\mathrm{x}}(0, t)^2\Big) . \tag{26.22}\]
Proof

We must show that the difference \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}} - \mathcal{E}^*\) differentiates into the right-hand side, because \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime} = 0\) by theorem 26.21.

  1. By expanding the square in (26.21), \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}} - \mathcal{E}^* = \mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x + \mu v^2\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. By lemma 4.19, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 2\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L w_{\mathrm{tt}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. The last integral is \(\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{t}} w_{\mathrm{tx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\big[w_{\mathrm{t}}^2\big]_0^L = 0\) by the fundamental theorem and by step 2 of the proof of theorem 26.21 (the eyelets stand still).
  4. From equation (26.16), \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + (T - \mu v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Hence \(\mu\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{tt}} w_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -2\mu v\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\,\mathrm{d}x + (T - \mu v^2)\int_0^L w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the last integral is \(\tfrac12\big[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big]_0^L\) by the fundamental theorem, the difference of the squared slopes at the eyelets, because \((w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)_{\mathrm{x}} = 2 w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  5. The derivative of step 1 is by steps 2 to 4 equal to \(v\Big(-2\mu v\int w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}} + \tfrac12(T - \mu v^2)\big[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big]_0^L\Big) + 2\mu v^2\int w_{\mathrm{x}} w_{\mathrm{xt}}\). The terms with the integral cancel (\(-2\mu v^2 + 2\mu v^2 = 0\)), what remains is \(\tfrac{v}{2}(T - \mu v^2)\big[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2\big]_0^L\). Since \(\mathcal{E}^{*\prime} = 0\), this is \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}'\). It depends only on the slopes at the eyelets, and on the band at \(t = 0\) it is the blue bar.

Source: CHEN2005 (32), PDF p. 7 (Renshaw and others, a work of 1998 by the reference list on PDF p. 22): \(\mathrm{d}E_{\mathrm{E}}/\mathrm{d}T = \tfrac12 c(P - \rho c^2)\,(V_{\mathrm{X}})^2\big|_0^L\), and (28), PDF p. 6 (Lagrangian piece of material): \(\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}T = P c\,(V_{\mathrm{X}})^2\big|_0^L\). The proof by steps is ours, a similar explanation with the flow of energy through the eyelets is in CHEN2005, PDF p. 6.

Let us check on numbers. If we add the work of forces and the flow of energy, as described before the lemma, we get the same as the right-hand side of (26.22). On the band with \(a = 1{,}5\) mm and \(v = 40\) m/s at time \(t = 0\) the slopes are \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(0) = 4{,}712\cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = -2{,}096\cdot 10^{-3}\). In the lower part of figure 26.29 we measure downwards from the zero line. The power of the tension forces is \(T v\,[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2]_0^L = 0{,}791 - 3{,}997 = -3{,}206\) W, the grey bar. The energy carried out minus carried in is \(v\,\tfrac12(T + \mu v^2)\,[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2]_0^L = 0{,}445 - 2{,}248 = -1{,}803\) W, the orange bar. The difference is \(-1{,}403\) W, the blue bar between the ends of the first two, and \(\tfrac{v}{2}(T - \mu v^2)\,[w_{\mathrm{x}}^2]_0^L = -1{,}403\) W, and the numerical derivative \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{r}}'\) from a central difference \(-1{,}403\) W. At \(t = 2{,}523\) ms we have \(-1{,}592 - (-0{,}896) = -0{,}697\) W and the right-hand side \(-0{,}697\) W. At \(v = 0{,}9c\) at \(t = 0\) we have \(-0{,}972 - (-0{,}880) = -0{,}092\) W, and the right-hand side and the derivative are equal to it. So the energy of the span changes although there is no damping: material runs through the eyelets and the flow of energy is not zero, because the squares of the slopes at the ends differ: the flow would vanish only for \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(0)^2 = w_{\mathrm{x}}(L)^2\), which does not hold for the band in general. CHEN2005 (PDF p. 6) says the same: the moving string is nonconservative in this sense, although the equation has no dissipative term, and Hamilton's principle holds because the forces are potential.

What does this section show and what returns? The critical speed \(v_{\mathrm{c}} = c\) is not an ansatz but the point where we have infinitely many resting shapes, where the conserved quantity loses positive definiteness and where all frequencies vanish (lemma 26.19, theorem 26.21, corollary 26.22, theorem 26.13). Below it the motion is bounded in energy. The energy of the span is not conserved (lemma 26.23), because material carries energy at the eyelets and because the tension force does work on the moving material. The mixed part \(T_1\) of (26.18) is not zero, yet it is absent from the constant functional: the force \(-2\mu v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) it brings does no work over the span, as the Coriolis force in a rotating frame. Its name, the matrix that writes it and the eigenvalues of the discrete system we shall meet in section 26.5. Above \(v_{\mathrm{c}}\) we claim nothing, nor whether the motion dies out in time, because the model has no damping (chapter 20).
