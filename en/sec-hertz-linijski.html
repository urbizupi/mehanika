Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.5 Hertz contact of two cylinders: the contact conditions and line contact

At the end of section 25.4 we found that a point force cannot be a model of contact: the deflection under it is infinite, and the pressure is infinite for every finite force. A contact needs a region of finite size, which we do not know in advance, because the deflection itself determines it. In a roller bearing such a contact is met at every step: a roller, a steel cylinder with a radius of a few millimetres, lies on a raceway, and the raceway pushes it with a force that the bearing has to carry. The question of this section is therefore: how wide is the strip along which roller and raceway touch, how large is the pressure in it, and how does the load connect them? We start with two cylinders, because this is a plane problem, and for the plane we have Flamant's solution (theorem 25.13). The ball, which needs a three-dimensional solution after Boussinesq, is treated in section 25.6.

Let us draw figure 25.34, first the left half. We take the lower body, whose surface bulges upwards with radius \(R_2\). It stands for a piece of the raceway under the roller, and we need its flat lower face only to have something to rest it on. On it we place the upper cylinder with radius \(R_1\), whose axis is perpendicular to the plane of the drawing. It is drawn as a circle because we look at a cross-section, and both bodies have the same length along the axis, which is much larger than everything in the cross-section. Therefore nothing changes along the axis and the plane of the drawing tells everything (plane strain, definition 11.32). The bodies are pressed together by the forces \(F'\): the upper cylinder pushes down, the lower body pushes up, both along the line through the centres. The prime on \(F'\) is not a derivative, it says that this is the force per unit length of the axis (sila na enoto dolžine), in N/m, in numbers in N/mm. The bodies touch at the point of the cross-section on this line, which in space is a line along the whole length of the axis. For this reason we call it a line contact (črtni stik).

Before we compute, let us say when the model is good. For the roller we take a steel cylinder with radius \(R_1 = 5\) mm, for the raceway the radius \(R_2 = 25\) mm and for the force \(F' = 100\) N/mm. Below we will compute that under this force the point of touching widens into a strip \(0{,}14\) mm wide, which is \(2{,}8\ \%\) of the radius of the roller. At the scale of figure 25.34 it would measure \(0{,}22\) mm, less than the thickness of the drawn line, so figure 25.36 below draws it strongly exaggerated. This is what we need: the strip is small compared with the bodies, so in computing the stresses near the contact we can take each body to be a half-plane, as if the surface were infinite. The assumption holds as long as the strip is small against both radii. It fails for a conformal contact, where the contact surface is comparable with the body, and for a body that is not much larger than the strip in every direction, for example a thin layer on a base. What it costs in the shape of the surfaces is told by lemma 25.20: for our numbers the error is of the order \((b/R_1)^2 = 2\cdot 10^{-4}\), where \(b\) is half the width of the strip. We do not estimate the error due to the finite thickness of the bodies.

Definition 25.18 Nonconformal contact (nekonformni stik)

A contact of two bodies is nonconformal if it has three properties:

  1. Before loading the bodies touch at a point or along a line.
  2. Both bodies are smooth near the contact, so that their local shape can be described by convex surfaces (here a cylinder or a sphere).
  3. The contact patch is much smaller than both bodies.

The name says the opposite, as figure 25.35 shows. On the left is a ball in a groove of almost the same radius: in a conformal contact (konformni stik) the bodies fit each other and the contact patch is already large at a small load. On the right is a roller on a raceway, where before loading the bodies touch at a point or a line and the contact patch is small. Such a contact is treated in section 25.6. In addition we take the bodies to be linearly elastic, isotropic and homogeneous, the strains to be small, and the contact to be frictionless, so that only normal forces act on the contact surface (BOWER, section 5.4.11, PDF p. 333).

Source: BOWER §5.4.11 (PDF p. 333): "(1) the two contacting solids initially touch at a point or along a line, (2) both contacting solids are smooth in the neighborhood of the contact, so that their local geometry can be approximated as ellipsoids, and (3) the size of the contact patch between the two solids is much smaller than either solid." Frictionless contact is assumed there too (same place). For surface shear see HK §6.5 (PDF pp. 139-142), which we do not treat here.

Property 3 is an inequality that we must check, and we will: for our numbers \(b/R_1 = 0{,}014\), and at four times the load \(0{,}028\). Property 1 tells where we start: as the load grows, the point of touching, a line in space, widens into a strip.

The contact strip (stična ožina) is the set of points where the surfaces touch, and we do not know which points these are. So we describe it with two functions, which we draw in figure 25.36. Let \(x\) be the coordinate along the common tangent at the point of touching, measured in the plane of the cross-section. The orange lines are the surfaces of bodies 1 and 2 after loading. In the middle they touch along the strip of width \(2b\), outside it they part. The gap (zazor) \(g(x)\) is the distance between the surfaces at the coordinate \(x\), measured in the direction of the force. The pressure (tlak) \(p(x)\) is the normal force per area of the contact surface that the bodies exert on each other, in N/m\(^2\), in numbers in MPa. By Newton's third law body 1 acts on body 2 with the same force as body 2 on body 1, so on both surfaces we have the same \(p\). The force per unit length of the axis is then \(\int p\,\mathrm{d}x\) over the contact strip, in the figure the area under the pressure curve.

Definition 25.19 Contact conditions (stična pogoja)

Let two bodies that are in contact before loading be loaded by a force \(F' > 0\) per unit length. The unknowns are the functions of gap \(g\) and pressure \(p\) on the line of contact, both with values in \(\mathbb{R}\). The contact problem (stični problem) requires that for every \(x\):

  1. \(g(x) \geq 0\): the bodies do not penetrate each other.
  2. \(p(x) \geq 0\): the contact does not pull, the surfaces only push each other.
  3. \(g(x)\,p(x) = 0\): there is pressure only where the gap is zero, and the gap is larger than zero only where there is no pressure.
  4. \(\int p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = F'\): the pressure carries the whole force.

The set \(\Omega = \{x : g(x) = 0\}\) is the contact set (stična množica). Besides \(p\) and \(g\) we must determine it, and for this very reason the contact problem is not linear: for a given \(F'\) we cannot add the solutions for two different sets \(\Omega\).

Source: BARBER §12.5.3 (PDF p. 195), inequalities (12.48) "contact traction should not be tensile" and (12.49) "no interference between the bodies outside the contact area". The complete form with requirement (3) is my formulation of both. Uniqueness of the solution is not proved (see the remark below theorem 25.24).

Let us read the conditions in figure 25.36. Condition 1 says that the orange lines must not pass through each other, because the bodies are solid and cannot be in the same place at once. Condition 2 says that the pressure is everywhere above the axis: the surfaces are not glued, so they can push each other but cannot pull, like a palm laid on a table. Condition 3 connects the two: where there is a gap between the surfaces, they do not touch and there is no force between them, and where they touch, in the strip, there is no gap. At every point at least one of the numbers \(g\) and \(p\) is therefore zero. Condition 4 is the equilibrium of the cylinder: the area under the pressure is the force with which the raceway holds the cylinder, and it must equal the force \(F'\) that presses it.

Let us compare this with the previous example of a contact. In the shrink fit of tubes (section 11.7.6) the contact was on the whole cylindrical surface, so \(\Omega\) was known and only the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) was unknown. Uniqueness of the contact problem itself, where \(p_{\mathrm{c}}\) is not given, was not proved there, and it is not proved here either. For the cylinders we do not know in advance how wide the strip is: the deformation itself decides it.

The gap after loading consists of three parts, which we show in figure 25.36. The first is the gap of the undeformed bodies \(g_0\), the distance between the surfaces before we press them. The second is the movement of the bodies towards each other: if the bodies could pass through each other, their distant parts would come closer by \(\delta\) and the undeformed surfaces would overlap, as the grey dashed lines show. In the middle the overlap is largest, exactly \(\delta\), and the gap would be \(g_0 - \delta\), negative in the middle. The third part are the elastic displacements of the surfaces: the pressure pushes each surface back into its body, from the grey dashed to the orange line, and we denote the sum of the two displacements by \(w\). Together the gap is \(g = g_0 - \delta + w\) (in theorem 25.24 equation (25.19)). In the middle of the strip \(g_0 = 0\) and \(g = 0\), so there the two displacements together make up exactly \(\delta\). First the first part, \(g_0\).

Let us draw the cylinders before loading in figure 25.37. Through the point of touching \(O\) we draw the common tangent, which is the \(x\) axis, and perpendicular to it the normal, on which the centres of both circles lie. The upper circle with radius \(R_1\) rises from the tangent, the lower one with radius \(R_2\) falls away from it. We choose a distance \(x\) from the normal. The point of the upper circle is there \(d_1\) above the tangent, the point of the lower one \(d_2\) below it. The distance between them is the gap \(g_0(x) = d_1 + d_2\), which by symmetry is the same on both sides of the normal, so it is drawn on the left. We get the offset \(d_1\) from the right triangle: its hypotenuse is the radius \(R_1\) from the centre to the point on the circle, the horizontal leg is \(x\), and the vertical leg is \(\sqrt{R_1^2 - x^2}\) and reaches from the centre to the height of the point. The centre is \(R_1\) above the tangent, so \(d_1 = R_1 - \sqrt{R_1^2 - x^2}\). The dashed parabolas \(x^2/(2R_1)\) and \(x^2/(2R_2)\) fit the circles near \(O\), and further from \(O\) they stay between the tangent and the circle. The lemma tells by how much.

Lemma 25.20 Gap of two cylinders (zazor dveh valjev)

Let two cylinders with radii \(R_1, R_2 > 0\) be convex, touching at the point \(O\) of the cross-section where \(x = 0\). The gap of the undeformed cylinders at the distance \(x\) from \(O\), \(|x| \leq \min(R_1, R_2)\), is

\[g_0(x) = \big(R_1 - \sqrt{R_1^2 - x^2}\big) + \big(R_2 - \sqrt{R_2^2 - x^2}\big) , \tag{25.13}\]

and it satisfies

\[\frac{x^2}{2R} \leq g_0(x) \leq \frac{x^2}{2R}\Big(1 + \frac{x^2}{R_{\min}^2}\Big) , \qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} , \tag{25.14}\]

where \(R_{\min} = \min(R_1, R_2)\).

Proof

We must show that \(g_0\) is the sum of the offsets of two circles from the common tangent, and estimate each.

  1. A circle with radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) that touches the tangent at \(O\) has its centre on the normal at \(O\) at the distance \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\). Its point at the distance \(x\) from the normal is at the distance \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = R_{\mathrm{i}} - \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{i}}^2 - x^2}\) from the tangent by Pythagoras' theorem in the triangle. The bodies lie on the two sides of the tangent, so the gap is the sum of the two offsets, which is (25.13).
  2. For each offset we extend the fraction: \(R_{\mathrm{i}} - \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{i}}^2 - x^2} = x^2/(R_{\mathrm{i}} + \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{i}}^2 - x^2})\).
  3. Lower bound. Since \(\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{i}}^2 - x^2} \leq R_{\mathrm{i}}\), the denominator is at most \(2R_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the offset is at least \(x^2/(2R_{\mathrm{i}})\): the parabola nowhere reaches beyond the circle.
  4. Upper bound: the offset does not exceed the parabola multiplied by \(1 + q^2\). For \(q = |x|/R_{\mathrm{i}} \leq 1\) we have \(\sqrt{1 - q^2} \geq 1 - q^2\), because \(1 - q^2 \geq (1 - q^2)^2 = 1 - 2q^2 + q^4\) exactly when \(q^2 \geq q^4\). So the denominator is at least \(R_{\mathrm{i}}(2 - q^2)\) and the offset at most \(\dfrac{x^2}{R_{\mathrm{i}}(2 - q^2)} = \dfrac{x^2}{2R_{\mathrm{i}}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - q^2/2} \leq \dfrac{x^2}{2R_{\mathrm{i}}}(1 + q^2)\), because \(1/(1 - u) \leq 1 + 2u\) for \(0 \leq u \leq \tfrac12\) (here \(u = q^2/2 \leq \tfrac12\)).
  5. We add the two offsets to the gap: the lower bound is \(\tfrac{x^2}{2}(1/R_1 + 1/R_2) = x^2/(2R)\), the upper bound is \(\tfrac{x^2}{2R}(1 + q_{\max}^2)\) with \(q_{\max} = |x|/R_{\min}\), because \(q_{\mathrm{i}} \leq q_{\max}\).

Source: The computation is mine. BARBER §12.6 (PDF p. 197) takes the radii to be large compared with the contact width, \(R_1, R_2 \gg a\), and describes the gap by a known function \(h(x)\). Our estimate tells how large the correction is.

For our roller and raceway at \(x = b = 70{,}19\ \mu\text{m}\) the largest relative error is \(\big(b/R_1\big)^2 = 2{,}0\cdot 10^{-4}\), and the actual error of a single circular offset, for example for \(R_1\), at \(x = b\) is only \(4{,}9\cdot 10^{-5}\) (SymPy, num-05.py). In the contact strip we may therefore write the gap of the undeformed bodies as \(g_0 = x^2/(2R)\), a parabola, and this is the gap of one cylinder with radius \(R\) against a plane. The replacement holds only near the point of touching: in figure 25.37, where \(x = 0{,}77\,R_1\) is drawn, the parabola is \(18\ \%\) too low, while in the strip \(x \leq 0{,}014\,R_1\). For what follows we need one more number: both bodies deform under load, and their elasticity must enter in a single number.

Definition 25.21 Effective modulus and effective radius of contact (efektivni modul in polmer stika)

For two bodies in contact with moduli \(E_1, E_2\), Poisson ratios \(\nu_1, \nu_2\) and radii \(R_1, R_2\) we define the numbers \(E^*\) (half-plane) and \(R\) (rigid cylinder) by

\[\frac{1}{E^*} = \frac{1 - \nu_1^2}{E_1} + \frac{1 - \nu_2^2}{E_2} , \qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} . \tag{25.15}\]

Both are numbers with the units Pa and m. If the surface of a body is convex, its radius is positive, if it is concave, negative (BOWER, PDF p. 333, and the treatment in section 25.6).

Source: BOWER §5.4.11 (PDF pp. 333-334): "effective modulus" and "effective contact radius". HK (6.51) (PDF p. 132): \(b = \big[\tfrac{4Q}{\pi l\,\Sigma\rho}\big(\tfrac{1 - \xi_{\mathrm{I}}^2}{E_{\mathrm{I}}} + \tfrac{1 - \xi_{\mathrm{II}}^2}{E_{\mathrm{II}}}\big)\big]^{1/2}\) contains the same sum \((1 - \xi^2)/E\) and \(\Sigma\rho = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2\).

Three warnings about notation. The letter \(E^*\) is here an italic number, while in the chapter on deformation the bold \(\mat E^*\) is the Euler-Almansi tensor, a different object. In section 25.3, \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) was the modulus of one body in plane strain (corollary 11.38). The effective modulus extends it to two bodies: if the second body is rigid (\(E_2 \to \infty\)), then \(E^* = E_1/(1 - \nu_1^2)\), the same number. For two equal bodies, however, it is half as large, because (25.15) has two equal terms \((1 - \nu^2)/E\) instead of one. The letter \(R\) is the radius of the contact, not to be confused with the distance \(R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\) of section 25.4. For two steel bodies of the book (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) we have \(E^* = E/(2(1 - \nu^2)) = 107{,}692\) GPa, because both sums are equal and each is \((1 - \nu^2)/E\). For the roller and the raceway \(R = 4{,}1667\) mm.

Now we can look at the right half of figure 25.34. The equivalent problem is drawn: a half-plane with modulus \(E^*\) replaces both elastic bodies, and a rigid cylinder with radius \(R\) and the same force \(F'\) replaces the gap \(g_0 = x^2/(2R)\). The horizontal and the vertical coordinate are drawn with the axes, as in sections 25.3 and 25.4. Whether the problem is really equivalent is shown by the computation of displacements below: the displacements of the surfaces of the two bodies add up in the gap, and their sum has the same form as for a half-plane with modulus \(E^*\).

Why such a sum appears we see when we compute the deflection that a pressure causes on the half-plane.

Let us draw the left half of figure 25.38. On the surface of the half-plane the pressure \(p(s)\) acts between \(-b\) and \(b\). We cut out of it a narrow slice at \(s\) with width \(\mathrm{d}s\), which carries the force \(p(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) per unit length. So narrow a slice acts as a single force, and for that we have Flamant's solution (lemma 25.14): a force \(F\) per unit length at the point \(s\) of the surface of the half-plane gives the deflection into the body \(-\dfrac{2(1 - \nu^2)\,F}{\pi E}\ln|x - s| + \text{const.}\), where the constant does not depend on \(x\). This is the orange dimple, deepest at the slice and ever shallower with the distance \(|x - s|\). At the point \(x\) the slice contributes the deflection that the blue distance shows. We cut the pressure \(p\) on the interval \([-b, b]\) into such pieces \([s_{\mathrm{j}}, s_{\mathrm{j}} + h]\) with forces \(F_{\mathrm{j}} = \int p\,\mathrm{d}s\) over the piece and add the contributions by the principle of superposition (corollary 11.3). The limit of the Riemann sums is an integral (as in lemma 25.17, where here the kernel \(\ln|x - s|\) is integrable even at \(s \to x\)). We state it in a lemma.

Lemma 25.22 Surface deflection under a line pressure (poves površja pod črtnim tlakom)

Let the bodies of definition 25.18 be in plane strain with constant \(E_{\mathrm{i}}, \nu_{\mathrm{i}}\), and let \(p \geq 0\) be a continuous function on \((-b, b)\) that is zero outside and has the integral (improper if needed, definition 9.21) \(\int p\,\mathrm{d}s = F'\). The sum of the displacements \(w = w_1 + w_2\) of the surfaces of the bodies into each body, caused by pressure \(p\), is

\[w(x) = -\frac{2}{\pi E^*}\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b} p(s)\,\ln|x - s|\,\mathrm{d}s + C_0 , \tag{25.16}\]

where \(C_0\) does not depend on \(x\) (and is undetermined, see lemma 25.25), and

  1. for \(|x| > b\) we have \(w'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{\pi E^*}\displaystyle\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\dfrac{p(s)}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s\), an ordinary integral,
  2. for \(|x| < b\) we have \(w'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{\pi E^*}\,\mathrm{PV}\displaystyle\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\dfrac{p(s)}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s\), the principal value (glavna vrednost) \(\mathrm{PV}\displaystyle\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b} f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\Big(\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{x - \varepsilon} + \int_{\mathrm{x} + \varepsilon}^{b}\Big) f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
Proof

We must show the form (25.16) of the deflection of the surface and both expressions for the derivative.

  1. Each body is a half-plane. By lemma 25.14 the deflection of the surface under the force \(F\) of a slice at the point \(s\) is the dimple \(-\tfrac{2(1 - \nu_{\mathrm{i}}^2)F}{\pi E_{\mathrm{i}}}\ln|x - s| + \text{const.}\) (checked in SymPy, num-05.py, part A: it satisfies Hooke's law and the relation between strains and displacements, residual zero). Both bodies carry the same force \(F\) at the point \(s\) by Newton's third law.
  2. The sum of the deflections of the two bodies has the factor \(2\big((1 - \nu_1^2)/E_1 + (1 - \nu_2^2)/E_2\big)/\pi = 2/(\pi E^*)\) by definition 25.21. Superposition of the slices and the passage to the limit of the Riemann sums (lemma 25.17 for point forces, the argument is the same) give (25.16).
  3. For \(|x| > b\) the function \(x \mapsto \ln|x - s|\) is smooth for \(s \in [-b, b]\) with the derivative \(1/(x - s)\) in \(s\) and \(x\), so we may differentiate under the integral (lemma 4.19). This is claim 1.
  4. For \(|x| < b\) the slice reaches the point \(x\), the distance \(|x - s|\) tends to zero and the integrand in claim 2 has a pole at \(s = x\), so the expression is an ordinary integral only as a principal value. That this is really the derivative is a result of BARBER (12.27), (12.28) (PDF p. 190), which we quote without proof. For the case we need, we check it in step 3 of the proof of theorem 25.24 by computing the displacement itself (NumPy, num-05.py, part G).

Source: BARBER §12.3.1 (PDF p. 188, (12.12) to (12.20)) and §12.4 (PDF pp. 189-190, (12.26) to (12.28)): the deflection under a distributed pressure as an integral of the Flamant kernel, logarithmically unbounded far from the contact, and therefore the formulation of contact problems with the derivative (12.27), in which, for a point inside the region, the integral is a Cauchy principal value. Claim 2 is therefore quoted and not proved.

The proof showed two things: the displacement depends on the logarithm of the distance, so it is determined only up to a constant (this will matter at the end of the section), while its derivative is determined and we need it. The first is shown by the right half of figure 25.38: the surface is lowest in the strip, and outside it rises along a straight line with the distance on the logarithmic axis and never levels off, so there is no level from which to measure the deflection. The derivative is an integral with a pole, which we must compute for the half-ellipse, and so let us first say what the pole does.

Let us draw figure 25.39. On the axis \(s\) there is the interval \([-b, b]\), bounded by the vertical lines at \(\pm b\), where the pressure vanishes. The point where we compute the slope we mark with \(x\), in the figure \(x = 0{,}4\,b\). There the integrand has a pole, because the denominator \(x - s\) becomes zero. The left branch lies to the left of the pole, where \(x - s > 0\), so the value grows beyond every bound as \(s\) approaches \(x\). The right branch lies to the right, where \(x - s < 0\), and goes to \(-\infty\). Each branch alone has an infinite area, but the sum need not be infinite. So we cut out the interval \((x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon)\) with the half-width \(\varepsilon\) on both sides and take the limit \(\varepsilon \to 0\). With such a symmetric cut-out the infinities cancel each other. With an asymmetric cut-out, for example \((x - \varepsilon, x + 2\varepsilon)\), the value would change by \(\sqrt{b^2 - x^2}\,\ln 2\), which at \(x = 0{,}4\,b\) equals \(0{,}635\,b\), and this is large compared with the true value \(\pi x = 1{,}257\,b\). Without an agreement on the cut-out the integral therefore has no value, and this agreement is the principal value.

Lemma 25.23 Integrals of the half-circle with a pole (integrali polkroga s polom)

Let \(b > 0\). If \(|x| < b\), then

\[\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\frac{1}{(x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0 , \qquad \mathrm{PV}\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s = \pi x . \tag{25.17}\]

If \(x > b\), both integrals are ordinary and

\[\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\frac{1}{(x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}} , \qquad \int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s = \pi\big(x - \sqrt{x^2 - b^2}\big) . \tag{25.18}\]
Proof

We must show four values. The key is that \(1/((x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2})\) has elementary antiderivatives, different for \(x\) inside and outside the interval.

  1. Let \(|x| < b\) and \(c = \sqrt{b^2 - x^2} > 0\). We denote \(N(s) = b^2 - x s + c\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}\) and \(H(s) = \dfrac{1}{c}\ln\dfrac{N(s)}{|x - s|}\). The derivative is \(H'(s) = \dfrac{1}{(x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\) on every interval where \(s \neq x\), that is on the left and on the right branch separately (checked in SymPy, num-05.py, part B, residual \(10^{-16}\) at three points).
  2. At the end points \(N(b) = b(b - x)\) and \(|x - b| = b - x\), so \(N(b)/|x - b| = b\). Similarly \(N(-b) = b(b + x)\) and \(|x + b| = b + x\), so \(N(-b)/|x + b| = b\). Hence \(H(b) = H(-b) = \tfrac1c\ln b\) (num-05.py, part B, values \(1{,}0\)).
  3. The integral over both branches without the cut-out is \(\big[H(x - \varepsilon) - H(-b)\big] + \big[H(b) - H(x + \varepsilon)\big]\) by steps 1 and 2 \(= H(x - \varepsilon) - H(x + \varepsilon) = \dfrac{1}{c}\ln\dfrac{N(x - \varepsilon)}{N(x + \varepsilon)}\), since \(|x - s| = \varepsilon\) cancels on both sides. The function \(N\) is continuous at \(s = x\) with the value \(N(x) = 2c^2 > 0\), so the ratio tends to \(1\) and the value to \(0\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). For \(b = 1\), \(x = 0{,}4\) the value is \(0{,}1047\), \(0{,}01039\), \(0{,}001039\), \(0{,}0001039\) at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-1}, \dots, 10^{-4}\), so proportional to \(\varepsilon\). This is the first value in (25.17).
  4. For the second value we write \(b^2 - s^2 = (b^2 - x^2) + (x - s)(x + s)\). Therefore the integrand \(\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}{x - s} = \dfrac{c^2}{(x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}} + \dfrac{x + s}{\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\).
  5. The principal value of the first term is \(0\) by step 3. The second term, with the substitution \(s = b\sin\varphi\) by theorem 4.59 (the Jacobian factor \(b\cos\varphi\), and \(\sqrt{b^2 - s^2} = b\cos\varphi\), which cancels), equals \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(x + b\sin\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}\varphi = \pi x\), because \(\sin\) is odd. The second term has no pole at \(s = x\), so no cut-out is needed and the integral is ordinary (improper at \(\pm b\), definition 9.21). This is the second value in (25.17).
  6. Let \(x > b\), a point to the right of the vertical line at \(b\), where the integrand has no pole on \([-b, b]\), and \(c' = \sqrt{x^2 - b^2}\). We denote \(K(s) = \dfrac{1}{c'}\arctan\dfrac{b^2 - x s}{c'\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\). The derivative is \(K'(s) = -\dfrac{1}{(x - s)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}\) on \((-b, b)\) (num-05.py, part B). As \(s \to b\) the argument has the value \(b(b - x)/0 \to -\infty\) and as \(s \to -b\) the value \(b(b + x)/0 \to +\infty\), so \(K(b) = -\pi/(2c')\) and \(K(-b) = \pi/(2c')\). The integral is \(K(-b) - K(b) = \pi/c'\) (improper integral, definition 9.21). This is the first value in (25.18).
  7. With the split of step 4, for a point outside the interval the second integral is \(c^2\cdot\pi/c' + \pi x\) with \(c^2 = b^2 - x^2 = -c'^2\), so \(-\pi c' + \pi x\). This is the second value in (25.18).

Source: The computation is mine, the pattern with an antiderivative with a pole is a standard technique (BARBER, §12.5, PDF pp. 191-196, solves the same integral with Fourier series). We check the antiderivatives in SymPy, the numerical principal value agrees to \(10^{-8}\) (num-05.py, part C): \(\pi x\) at \(x/b = 0{,}3, 0{,}6, 0{,}9\) and \(\pi(x - \sqrt{x^2 - b^2})\) at \(x/b = 1{,}2, 2, 5\).

The proof showed that the pressure \(p_0\sqrt{1 - s^2/b^2}\) gives a straight line in \(x\) in the expression for the slope. The gap is zero in the contact if its derivative is zero, and the theorem meets this with the choice of \(p_0\).

Before the theorem derives this exactly, let us estimate the result roughly, with figure 25.36. This is a second view of the same thing: it does not give the exact factors, but it shows where the root in \(b \propto \sqrt{F'}\) comes from.

  1. Geometry. For the surfaces to touch along the whole strip with half-width \(b\), they must close the gap that at its edge was \(g_0(b) = b^2/(2R)\) (lemma 25.20). By that much they must yield together, from the grey dashed to the orange line.
  2. Strain and pressure. The only length near the strip is \(b\), so we take the displacement \(b^2/(2R)\) to be spread over a depth of the order \(b\). The strain is then of the order \(\dfrac{b^2/(2R)}{b} = \dfrac{b}{2R}\) and the pressure by Hooke's law of the order \(p \approx E^*\,\dfrac{b}{2R}\).
  3. Force. The pressure acts on the width \(2b\), so \(F' \approx p\cdot 2b = \dfrac{E^* b^2}{R}\) and \(b \approx \sqrt{F'R/E^*}\).

A wider strip therefore counts twice: the pressure acts on a larger width and is itself larger, so the force grows with the square of \(b\) and \(b\) with the root of the force. For our numbers the estimate gives \(b \approx 62\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(p \approx 804\) MPa, the theorem \(70{,}19\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(907{,}0\) MPa. Both times the estimate misses by the factor \(\sqrt{4/\pi} = 1{,}13\). Step 2 is an assumption and not a proof, since we did not compute the depth over which the strain is spread. That is why we need the theorem, which fixes the factor and also gives the shape of the pressure.

Theorem 25.24 Hertz line contact of cylinders (Hertzov črtni stik valjev)

Let the bodies of definition 25.18 be two convex cylinders with radii \(R_1, R_2\) in plane strain with effective modulus \(E^*\) and radius \(R\) (definition 25.21), pressed together by a force \(F' > 0\) per unit length. Let the gap of the undeformed bodies be \(g_0(x) = x^2/(2R)\) (lemma 25.20) and the gap after loading

\[g(x) = \frac{x^2}{2R} + w(x) - \delta , \tag{25.19}\]

where \(w\) is from lemma 25.22 and \(\delta\) is a number still unknown (the approach, explained below lemma 25.25). Let

\[b = \sqrt{\frac{4F'R}{\pi E^*}} , \qquad p_0 = \frac{2F'}{\pi b} = \frac{E^*\,b}{2R} = \sqrt{\frac{F'E^*}{\pi R}} , \qquad p(x) = \begin{cases} p_0\sqrt{1 - x^2/b^2} & |x| \leq b \\ 0 & |x| > b . \end{cases} \tag{25.20}\]

Then:

  1. The functions \(p\) and \(g\) satisfy the contact conditions of definition 25.19 with \(\Omega = [-b, b]\) for a suitable value of \(\delta\).
  2. Outside the contact \(g(x) = \dfrac{b^2}{R}\,G(|x|/b)\) with \(G(t) = \tfrac12\big(t\sqrt{t^2 - 1} - \ln(t + \sqrt{t^2 - 1})\big)\), which is positive for \(t > 1\), and \(g\) is continuous at \(x = \pm b\).
  3. For \(\kappa \neq 0\) the family \(p_\kappa(x) = p_0\big(\sqrt{1 - x^2/b^2} + \kappa/\sqrt{1 - x^2/b^2}\big)\) on \(|x| < b\) (with the same \(b\) and \(p_0\), zero outside, the force is then \((1 + 2\kappa)\) times larger) satisfies the slope equation \(g'(x) = 0\) on \((-b, b)\) but not the contact conditions: for \(\kappa > 0\) we have \(g(x) = \dfrac{b^2}{R}\big(G(t) - \kappa\ln(t + \sqrt{t^2 - 1})\big) < 0\) for \(t > 1\) close to \(1\), for \(\kappa < 0\) we have \(p_\kappa < 0\) for \(1 - t^2 < -\kappa\).
Idea

We show that the inserted pressure makes the gap constant in the contact strip, then we compute the gap outside with the same lemma. The width \(b\) is determined by equating the force and the slope. The edge separates the solution from the remaining ones through the requirement that the gap does not penetrate and the pressure does not pull.

Proof
  1. Slope. For \(|x| < b\) lemma 25.22 gives \(w'(x) = -\dfrac{2}{\pi E^*}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\dfrac{p(s)}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\dfrac{2p_0}{\pi E^* b}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}}{x - s}\,\mathrm{d}s\), because \(p(s) = (p_0/b)\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}\). By lemma 25.23 this is \(-\dfrac{2p_0}{\pi E^* b}\,\pi x = -\dfrac{2p_0\,x}{E^* b}\).
  2. Gap in the strip. The derivative of (25.19) is \(g'(x) = x/R + w'(x) = x\big(1/R - 2p_0/(E^* b)\big)\). This is zero for every \(|x| < b\) exactly when \(p_0 = E^* b/(2R)\), which is the second equality in (25.20). The function \(g\) is constant on \([-b, b]\), because it is continuous and has zero derivative. With a suitable choice of \(\delta\) (the constant \(C_0\) in (25.16) and \(\delta\) appear only in the sum \(C_0 - \delta\)) this constant is zero, \(g = 0\) on \(\Omega = [-b, b]\).
  3. Check. We obtained the derivative \(w'\) in step 1 from claim 2 of lemma 25.22, which is stated without proof. So we check the integral of the displacement itself under the strip: \(J(x) = \int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}\ln|x - s|\,\mathrm{d}s\) must by step 1 have the difference \(J(x) - J(0) = \pi x^2/2\). For \(b = 1\) we get (NumPy) \(0{,}14137\), \(0{,}56549\), \(1{,}27235\) at \(x = 0{,}3, 0{,}6, 0{,}9\), and \(\pi x^2/2\) is equal to five places (num-05.py, part G). By (25.16) we have therefore \(w(x) - w(0) = -\dfrac{2p_0}{\pi E^* b}\cdot\dfrac{\pi x^2}{2} = -\dfrac{p_0\,x^2}{E^* b}\), which is \(-x^2/(2R)\) for \(p_0 = E^* b/(2R)\).
  4. Force. The force is the area under the half-ellipse. With \(s = b t\) we have \(\int p\,\mathrm{d}s = p_0 b\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1 - t^2}\,\mathrm{d}t = p_0 b\,\pi/2\) (half the area of a circle, numerically also \(1{,}5707963\)). Equating with \(F'\) gives \(p_0 = 2F'/(\pi b)\). By step 2 \(p_0 = E^* b/(2R)\), so \(E^* b/(2R) = 2F'/(\pi b)\) and \(b^2 = 4F'R/(\pi E^*)\). The remaining expression \(p_0 = \sqrt{F'E^*/(\pi R)}\) follows by inserting \(b\) in \(E^*b/(2R)\).
  5. Outside the strip. For \(x > b\), by lemma 25.22, claim 1, and the second value in (25.18), \(w'(x) = -\dfrac{2p_0}{\pi E^* b}\,\pi\big(x - \sqrt{x^2 - b^2}\big) = -\dfrac{x - \sqrt{x^2 - b^2}}{R}\), with the same \(p_0 = E^*b/(2R)\). Therefore \(g'(x) = \dfrac{x}{R} + w'(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}}{R}\).
  6. Since \(w\) is continuous (an integral with an integrable kernel) and \(g(b) = 0\) from step 2, we have \(g(x) = \int_{\mathrm{b}}^x\dfrac{\sqrt{s^2 - b^2}}{R}\,\mathrm{d}s = \dfrac{b^2}{R}\int_1^t\sqrt{\tau^2 - 1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\) with \(s = b\tau\). The derivative \(G'(t) = \tfrac12\big(\sqrt{t^2 - 1} + t^2/\sqrt{t^2 - 1} - 1/\sqrt{t^2 - 1}\big) = \sqrt{t^2 - 1}\), because \(\dfrac{1 + t/\sqrt{t^2 - 1}}{t + \sqrt{t^2 - 1}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{t^2 - 1}}\), and \(G(1) = 0\). So \(g = (b^2/R)\,G(t)\). Since \(G' > 0\) for \(t > 1\), \(G > 0\) there. For \(x < -b\) the same by evenness. This is claim 2, the orange curve outside the strip.
  7. Contact conditions. Condition 1 (gap) is satisfied in the strip (\(g = 0\)) and outside (\(g > 0\)). Condition 2 (\(p \geq 0\)) holds because the root is nonnegative. Condition 3 is satisfied because \(p = 0\) where \(g > 0\), and \(g = 0\) where \(p > 0\). Condition 4 is step 4. This is claim 1.
  8. The family (figure 25.41, the orange and the grey curve). The term \(\kappa p_0/\sqrt{1 - s^2/b^2} = \kappa p_0 b/\sqrt{b^2 - s^2}\) gives zero in the slope by the first value in (25.17), so the same \(b, p_0\) keep \(g' = 0\) on \((-b, b)\). Outside, by the first value in (25.18), it adds \(-\dfrac{2\kappa p_0 b}{E^*}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}} = -\dfrac{\kappa b^2}{R\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}}\) to \(w'\). Therefore \(g'(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}}{R} - \dfrac{\kappa b^2}{R\sqrt{x^2 - b^2}}\), and with \(x = b t\) we get \(g = \dfrac{b^2}{R}\big(G(t) - \kappa\ln(t + \sqrt{t^2 - 1})\big)\) by \(\int_1^t\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\tau^2 - 1}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \ln(t + \sqrt{t^2 - 1})\). As \(t \to 1^+\) we have \(G = O\big((t - 1)^{3/2}\big)\), while the logarithm is \(\sim\sqrt{2(t - 1)}\), so for \(\kappa > 0\) the value of \(g\) near the edge is negative. For \(\kappa < 0\) we have \(\sqrt{1 - t^2} + \kappa/\sqrt{1 - t^2} < 0\) exactly when \(1 - t^2 < -\kappa\).

Source: BARBER §12.5.3 (PDF pp. 195-196): the same \(p(x) = -2F\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}/(\pi a^2)\) (12.60) with the edge from the requirement that the pressure is not singular at \(x = \pm a\) (12.56) (BARBER computes with a pressure of negative sign and a rigid cylinder, and calls the half-width \(a\)). HK (6.50), (6.51) (PDF p. 132): \(\sigma = \frac{2Q}{\pi l b}\big[1 - (y/b)^2\big]^{1/2}\) and \(b\) in the same form (after converting the symbols, \(4Q/(\pi l\,\Sigma\rho)\cdot 1/E^*\)). The proof of claim 3 and of the outside gap is mine. The fact that \(p_\kappa\) covers all solutions of the slope equation is classical (BARBER §12.5, PDF pp. 191-193, Fourier series, and Muskhelishvili, whom BARBER cites in a footnote on p. 192) and we do not prove it, so we do not claim uniqueness of the contact problem.

The meaning of the theorem is easiest to see in a figure.

Let us draw figure 25.40. At the top there is the axis \(x\) and an axis for the pressure. On the contact strip from \(-b\) to \(b\) the pressure is half an ellipse with the largest value \(p_0\) in the middle and zero at the edges. Below it is the second axis, on which we measure the gap downwards. The dashed parabola is the gap of the undeformed bodies \(x^2/(2R)\), which the cylinders would have if they were rigid. The orange curve is the gap under load: in the strip it is zero, outside it is positive, at the edge it grows with the power \(3/2\), as \(G(t) \sim (t - 1)^{3/2}\) shows, and it is everywhere smaller than the parabola. This is the difference between elastic and rigid bodies: under the pressure the bodies are compressed and the gap next to the strip closes.

Let us test the theorem on numbers. For a roller \(R_1 = 5\) mm on a raceway \(R_2 = 25\) mm of the book's steel, \(E^* = 107{,}692\) GPa and \(R = 4{,}1667\) mm. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm the theorem gives the half-width \(b = 70{,}19\ \mu\text{m}\) and the largest pressure \(p_0 = 907{,}0\) MPa, at \(F' = 400\) N/mm \(b = 140{,}37\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(p_0 = 1814{,}1\) MPa (num-05.py, part D). Four times the force gives twice the width and twice the pressure, because \(b\) and \(p_0\) grow as \(\sqrt{F'}\). So the contact is not linear: twice the force does not give twice the pressure. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm we have \(b^2/R = 1{,}182\ \mu\text{m}\). The gap at \(t = 2\), that is \(140\ \mu\text{m}\) from the centre, is \(G(2)\,b^2/R = 1{,}0736\cdot 1{,}182 = 1{,}269\ \mu\text{m}\), while the undeformed gap is \(x^2/(2R) = 2{,}365\ \mu\text{m}\). The two elastic displacements therefore reduce the gap by a little less than half.

Does HK have the same result? His equation (6.51) has the same form \(b = [4Q/(\pi l\,\Sigma\rho)\cdot(\ldots)]^{1/2}\), and for steel his (6.52) gives \(b = 3{,}35\cdot 10^{-3}\sqrt{Q/(l\,\Sigma\rho)}\) (mm, N, 1/mm). For our numbers (\(Q/l = 100\) N/mm, \(\Sigma\rho = 0{,}24\) mm\(^{-1}\)) this is \(68{,}4\ \mu\text{m}\), while we got \(70{,}19\ \mu\text{m}\). The difference of \(2{,}6\ \%\) is a difference of moduli: from (6.51) and (6.52) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) it follows that the constant corresponds to \(E = 2{,}065\cdot 10^5\) MPa, while we took \(E = 196\) GPa. The ratio of the roots \(\sqrt{2{,}065/1{,}96} = 1{,}026\) gives \(68{,}4\cdot 1{,}026 = 70{,}2\ \mu\text{m}\), which agrees.

Our two numbers are a measure for the question whether the model still holds. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm we have \(p_0/E^* = 0{,}0084\), at \(400\) N/mm \(0{,}0168\), which is the order of magnitude of the strain under the strip. Section 25.4 took strains below \(10^{-3}\) for the linear theory, so here we are above this limit and the result holds only as far as the steel still behaves as linearly elastic. This limits the model, it does not refute it. The steel of the book has the yield stress (definition 2.7) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (chapter 3), while our \(p_0 = 907\) MPa is four times larger. For the ball, section 25.6 (lemma 25.29) shows that the steel of the book starts to yield already at \(p_0 = 1{,}613\,\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 355\) MPa. The stresses below the surface of a line contact we do not compute here (HK §6.4, PDF pp. 133-138), so we do not give the yield limit for the cylinders and do not claim that the steel of the book stays elastic at our forces. The numbers of this section hold for a steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) that does not yet yield at such a pressure.

The theorem chose one shape of the pressure, the half-ellipse. Would another shape also give zero gap in the strip? Claim 3 says there is a whole family of them, but all except one violate the contact conditions at the edge. This is the near miss of the theorem: a solution of the slope equation is not yet a solution of the contact problem.

Let us draw figure 25.41. On the left is the pressure from \(t = 0{,}6\) to the edge, which is the vertical dashed line at \(b\). The blue curve is \(\kappa = 0\) and falls to zero at \(b\), as in the theorem. The orange one has \(\kappa = 0{,}3\): at the edge it does not fall to zero but grows beyond every bound, and at \(0{,}99\,b\) it is \(2{,}27\,p_0\), at \(0{,}999\,b\) it is \(6{,}75\,p_0\). The grey one has \(\kappa = -0{,}3\) and goes to \(-\infty\) at the edge: at \(0{,}99\,b\) it is \(-1{,}99\,p_0\) and at \(0{,}999\,b\) it is \(-6{,}67\,p_0\), so the contact pulls, which condition 2 forbids. On the right is the gap outside the strip, drawn above the second axis, which starts at the edge of the strip \(t = 1\). For \(\kappa = 0\) (blue) it is positive. For \(\kappa = 0{,}3\) (orange) the gap near the edge is negative, at least \(-0{,}1065\,b^2/R = -126\) nm at \(t = 1{,}14\), and positive only from \(t = 1{,}41\): the bodies would penetrate each other, which condition 1 forbids. For \(\kappa = -0{,}3\) (grey) the gap is positive, but this is reached with a pressure that pulls. Conditions 1 and 2 together therefore leave only \(\kappa = 0\), and this is what BARBER says in words, that the pressure at the edge is not singular: \(\kappa \neq 0\) has a singular pressure at the edge.

Uniqueness of the contact problem as a whole is not proved by this: we showed that among the family \(p_\kappa\) only the theorem satisfies the contact conditions. That the family covers all solutions of the slope equation is stated by BARBER (see the source of the theorem), we do not prove it. Uniqueness of the contact problem is unproved also in chapter 11 (section 11.7.6), and here too we state it as open.

The proof of the theorem gave the pressure, the width and the gap, but not the approach \(\delta\) of the bodies. This is not a defect of the proof, it is a property of the plane problem.

Lemma 25.25 The approach of a line contact is not defined (približevanje pri črtnem stiku ni določeno)

Let \(p\) be from theorem 25.24 and \(L > b\). Let \(w_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\) be the deflection from lemma 25.22 with the choice \(C_0 = \dfrac{2F'}{\pi E^*}\ln L\), that is \(w_{\mathrm{L}}(x) = \dfrac{2}{\pi E^*}\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}p(s)\,\ln\dfrac{L}{|x - s|}\,\mathrm{d}s\). This is the deflection relative to the point at the distance \(L\) from the middle of the contact, which for \(L \gg b\) approximately stays at rest, since there \(\ln\big(L/|L - s|\big) \approx s/L\) and \(\int p\,s\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) by the evenness of \(p\). Then the deflection under the middle of the strip is

\[w_{\mathrm{L}}(0) = \frac{2F'}{\pi E^*}\Big(\ln\frac{2L}{b} + \frac12\Big) , \tag{25.21}\]

and as \(L \to \infty\) it grows beyond every bound, like \(\ln L\). Therefore the distance between the bodies that the contact requires is not determined by \(F'\), \(E^*\) and \(R\) alone, but also by \(L\).

Proof

We must show the value of the integral \(\int p\,\ln|s|\,\mathrm{d}s\) over the slices of pressure and think about \(L\).

  1. By definition the deflection under the middle \(w_{\mathrm{L}}(0) = \dfrac{2}{\pi E^*}\Big(F'\ln L - \int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}p(s)\ln|s|\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)\), because \(\int p = F'\).
  2. With \(s = b u\) on the strip and \(p = p_0\sqrt{1 - u^2}\) we have \(\int p\ln|s|\,\mathrm{d}s = p_0 b\Big(\ln b\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,\mathrm{d}u + \int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\ln|u|\,\mathrm{d}u\Big)\). The first integral is \(\pi/2\). The second is \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big(\ln\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\big)\) (numerically \(-1{,}8741912\) against \(-1{,}8741912\), num-05.py, part G). Since \(p_0 b\,\pi/2 = F'\), we get \(\int p\ln|s|\,\mathrm{d}s = F'\big(\ln\tfrac{b}{2} - \tfrac12\big)\).
  3. We insert into step 1, for the deflection under the middle we get \(w_{\mathrm{L}}(0) = \dfrac{2F'}{\pi E^*}\big(\ln L - \ln\tfrac b2 + \tfrac12\big)\), which is (25.21).

Source: The computation is mine. BARBER §12.4 (PDF p. 190): the deflection is logarithmically unbounded, and the rigid-body displacement of the loaded region relative to distant parts of the body cannot be found without taking the finite size of the body into account.

Let us look at this on numbers and on the right half of figure 25.38. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm we have \(2F'/(\pi E^*) = 0{,}5911\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(b = 70{,}19\ \mu\text{m}\). For \(L_1 = 0{,}1\) m we get \(w_{\mathrm{L}}(0) = 4{,}998\ \mu\text{m}\), for \(L_2 = 1\) m \(6{,}359\ \mu\text{m}\). In the figure these are the blue distances from the bottom of the dimple to the level at which the surface lies at \(L_1\) and at \(L_2\). The same bodies therefore "approach" by \(5{,}0\) or by \(6{,}4\ \mu\text{m}\), depending on where on the bodies we measure the distance, and the difference of \(1{,}361\ \mu\text{m}\) is larger than \(b^2/R = 1{,}182\ \mu\text{m}\), which is the scale of everything that happens in the strip (figure 25.40). For the ball (section 25.6) this does not happen, because the deflection \(1/r\) of section 25.4 falls with distance and distant points stay at rest. A logarithm in the plane grows and does not allow anything to stay at rest. For a real cylinder the scale of the body is the length \(l\) of the cylinder, and indeed the Lundberg and Sjövall deflection of a line contact (HK (6.53), PDF pp. 132-133) has in front of the logarithm a factor proportional to \(Q/l\) and in the logarithm the length of the cylinder squared, \(\ln\big(\pi E l^2/(Q\cdots)\big)\). We do not copy the symbols that HK does not explain on this page, and we do not claim agreement with the lemma. The fact is that for roller bearings HK uses the force-approach law \(Q \propto \delta^{n}\) with \(n = 10/9\), not \(3/2\) as for balls (HK (7.3), PDF pp. 155-156). This law is empirical: it comes from the equation \(\delta \propto Q^{0{,}9}/l^{0{,}8}\) (HK (6.54), PDF p. 133), which Palmgren obtained from tests of rollers slightly convex along their length (crowned rollers) loaded against raceways, and \(1/0{,}9 = 10/9\). Whether this is because of what was described, HK does not say. We do not derive the law with \(10/9\) in the book, we quote it as HK.

For a line contact we therefore have the pressure, the width of the strip (\(b \propto \sqrt{F'}\)) and the gap, but the model does not give the approach. Section 25.6 does the same for a sphere, and there the approach exists, because the deflection falls with distance.

Notation and what is left out. Notation of the section: \(b\) is the half-width of the contact, not the radius of a tube from chapter 11. The letter \(\delta\) is the approach of the bodies, not a virtual increment. The letter \(p\) is the pressure in N/m\(^2\), and the force per unit length of the axis is \(F'\) in N/m. Outside the chapter: the ends of cylinders and the crowning of rollers that prevents edge loading (HK, PDF pp. 145-148) are not part of this section, and neither is friction in the contact (HK §6.5, PDF pp. 139-142).
