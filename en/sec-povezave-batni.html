Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.10 Connections (povezave)

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19.

  • Analytical dynamics (DSK, chapter 8): the slider-crank mechanism of section 23.2 has one degree of freedom by lemma 23.8 (definition 8.45): five independent holonomic constraints (definition 8.42) tie the six coordinates of the crank and the conrod, and the angle \(\theta\) is a generalized coordinate in every position. In section 23.4 we obtained its equation of motion from Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.88) with the reduced inertia \(J(\theta)\) of theorem 8.86, and the gas force as a generalized force (definition 8.50). By lemma 23.18 the shaking force of section 23.3 is the sum of the inertia forces \(-m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\) of the moving parts, the forces that d'Alembert's principle (corollary 8.72) adds to the applied forces. We proved the lemma with theorem 8.74 for each part and the law of action and reaction, and section 23.8 used d'Alembert's principle for a system for the engine on its mounts. For the equivalent masses of the conrod the centre-of-mass part of theorem 8.74 was decisive: the resultant force on the conrod is \(m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\), so the force on the frame is exact. The moment on the conrod is not exact, because the pair of masses has the moment of inertia \(0{,}0090\) instead of \(0{,}0065\ \text{kg m}^2\) (difference \(0{,}0025\ \text{kg m}^2\)). The comparison of the approximate piston motion with the exact form used theorem 8.60 with the error bound \(107{,}58\ \mu\text{m}\) and the actual error \(100{,}83\ \mu\text{m}\) for our engine E. The slider-crank differs from toggle mechanism \(D\) of example 8.48 in its lengths. In both the slider moves on a line through the pivot, but in \(D\) the two bars are equally long (\(\lambda = 1\)), while in the slider-crank the conrod is longer than the crank (\(l > a\)). So the conrod never folds onto the crank, the piston position is fixed at every \(\theta\) (example 23.9, item 2), and the dead positions of the piston belong to the angles \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\).
  • Unbalance and the machine on mounts (VD, chapter 9): the excitation \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}} \Omega^2 \cos \Omega t\) of section 9.8 was given for machine B. In section 23.1 we treated \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\) as the complex unbalance \(U = m e\), which by theorem 23.2 is fixed by the sums \(\sum U_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\sum U_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}}\). Rotor F (\(0{,}01\ \text{kg m}\) at \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) in the planes \(z = 0{,}1\) and \(0{,}4\) m) is statically balanced and still loads both bearings with \(592{,}18\) N each at \(3000\) 1/min. Two correction masses in two planes remove this (theorem 23.6), one plane does not. A correction in it would have to keep \(\sum U_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and cancel \(\sum U_{\mathrm{i}} z_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the same time, which contradict each other, so either the couple with moment \(296{,}09\) N m remains or a static unbalance appears (with the plane \(z = 0{,}25\) m a force of \(1184{,}4\) N in bearing \(A\)).
  • Isolation (VD, chapter 9): in section 23.8 we used theorem 9.66 for each harmonic of the shaking force separately. This is allowed by theorem 23.52, which that section proved for a finite sum of harmonics with theorem 9.52. Theorem 9.98 states the same for the response to a periodic force, but does not treat the ground force. Engine E on the mounts of machine B (\(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz) transmits \(92{,}53\) N of first order (\(r = 5\)) and \(5{,}70\) N of second order (\(r = 10\)) to the ground without damping, and \(103{,}43\) N and \(8{,}06\) N at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\). At \(r = 5\) and \(r = 10\) the damping increased the transmission, which theorem 9.66 predicts for \(r > \sqrt{2}\). Engine E and the gas force are our model and not data, so these values are an example of a calculation and not a measurement.
  • Several degrees of freedom (VD, chapter 10): the rocking of the engine on its mounts is machine F of example 10.6. With the mounts at \(\pm 0{,}5\) m both matrices of machine F are diagonal, so by theorem 10.2 bounce and rocking decouple and rocking is solved by an equation with one coordinate (theorem 9.52 with the moment of inertia in place of the mass). The primary couple of the inline three-cylinder engine (\(384{,}6\) N m at \(25\) Hz) gives a rotation with amplitude \(0{,}4454\) mrad, and each mount a force of \(27{,}47\) N with the sign opposite to the other (sum of magnitudes \(54{,}9\) N). The natural frequency of rocking is \(6{,}455\) Hz, so the resonance is at \(387{,}3\) 1/min for order \(1\) and at \(774{,}6\) 1/min for order \(0{,}5\) of a four-stroke engine, close to the idle speed. With six degrees of freedom and non-diagonal matrices we would need theorem 10.53. Theorem 10.23 on rigid modes is used in section 23.9: the zero frequency of the chain of disks is the rigid rotation of the whole shaft.
  • The torsional chain of disks (VD, chapter 13): the stiffness of a shaft segment \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}} / L\) from lemma 13.17 became an entry of the stiffness matrix \(K\) in section 23.9, and the mass matrix is diagonal with the moments of inertia of the disks. The frequencies of the chain by theorem 10.13 (check: FPGL, example 9.4.1, \(0\), \(123{,}07\), \(227{,}40\) and \(297{,}11\) Hz) are exact for the model with a massless shaft. The direction of the error of this model is given by corollary 13.22: the model with a massless shaft overestimates the frequency. For the shaft with a disk of example 13.23, where the shaft has \(3{,}9\) % of the moment of inertia of the disk, the error was \(0{,}65\) %. The first positive natural frequency of the chain with engine \(0{,}05\), flywheel \(0{,}5\) and generator \(0{,}3\ \text{kg m}^2\) is \(70{,}190\) Hz, far from \(1500\) 1/min (\(25\) Hz), but order \(3\) at \(1404\) 1/min and order \(2{,}5\) at \(1685\) 1/min are \(6{,}4\) % and \(12{,}3\) % from the running speed.
  • The Fourier series (chapters 9 and 10): the orders of the torque of section 23.5 (\(0{,}5\), \(1\), \(1{,}5\), \(2\), \(2{,}5\), \(3\) with amplitudes \(161{,}8\), \(147{,}7\), \(111{,}6\), \(50{,}7\), \(49{,}9\) and \(31{,}9\) N m) are the coefficients of the series of definition 9.88 with period \(4\pi\), fixed by the orthogonality of lemma 9.87. The half orders come from the four-stroke cycle with period \(4\pi\): a series with period \(2\pi\) does not describe such a torque. The flywheel does not need the series, theorem 23.28 fixes it from the energy of the excess torque. Its Slovenian name, vztrajnik, comes from vztrajnost (inertia), which it uses (\(J = 1{,}6721\ \text{kg m}^2\) for \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}02\) and \(3{,}3442\ \text{kg m}^2\) for \(0{,}01\)): while the torque is above its mean and the shaft speeds up, it stores the energy \(\Delta E = 825{,}14\) J, and while the torque is below the mean, it gives it back.
  • Bending vibration of rotating shafts and critical speeds (DSK, chapter 24): in section 23.1 the rotor is rigid and the bearings do not move. The flexible rotor, where bending couples to rotation, critical speeds, gyroscopic effects and correcting unbalance in place on the running machine are there. That chapter takes two-plane balancing of the rigid rotor as known.
  • Bearings (DSK, chapter 25): we computed the bearing forces without the bearing having a stiffness. The stiffness and load capacity of bearings and their effect on the frequencies of a rotor are in that chapter.
  • Damping models of the mounts (DSK, chapter 20): the damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}05\) of the mounts is a given value, equivalent viscous, hysteretic or other damping is in that chapter. We do not treat torsional dampers, because we have no source for them.
  • Torsional stiffness of non-circular sections (MK, VD, chapter 16): section 23.9 uses only the circular shaft with polar moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\). That chapter derives the stiffness for other sections.
  • Reduced models of large chains (DSK, chapter 21): the chain of three disks is solved directly, and the chain of a multi-cylinder engine with several intermediate shafts is a case where we keep only the first modes.
  • Robots (DSK, chapter 27): the slider-crank is the simplest case of a system whose reduced inertia \(J(\theta)\) depends on the position. The same equation with several coordinates and the constraints given by the links is the motion of a robot by Lagrange.
  • Limits of the chapter: we have no gas forces from real measurements, so the gas model is ours and not data. Only these facts remained without proof. Pin forces and the side thrust of the piston (NORTON, section 13.9), the balancing of linkages (sections 12.3 to 12.6) and measuring and correcting imbalance (section 12.7) are cited from the source. V engines with more cylinders and opposed engines are only named (sections 14.7 and 14.8), only the V twin with one crank pin is treated (corollary 23.50). Rocking with all six degrees of freedom and the design of engine mounts have no source. The coefficients \(A_2 = 0{,}254025\) and \(A_4 = 0{,}004098\) of the piston acceleration are computed and not derived in closed form. The effect of the variable inertia \(J(\theta)\) on torsional vibration is named and not treated.
