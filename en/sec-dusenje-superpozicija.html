Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.9 Damping and modal superposition (dušenje in modalna superpozicija)

So far we let frame A (the two-storey frame of section 10.1) vibrate without damping. A real frame comes to rest, because in the joints, in the floors and in the material mechanical energy turns into heat. Let us therefore add dampers to the frame. The question is what remains of the modal picture. Does every mode still behave as a single oscillator of chapter 9, or do the modes become entangled again? And if they do, how large is the error when we separate them?

The equation of motion with damping is \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K \vek q = \vek Q(t)\) (theorem 10.2). The damping matrix (matrika dušenja) \(\mat C\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15). For \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) we have already done the decoupling. Insert \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\) (definition 10.19) and multiply the equation from the left by \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\). Because of \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}})\) (lemma 10.16) we get

\[\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta} + \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\,\dot{\boldsymbol\eta} + \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{n}})\,\boldsymbol\eta = \mat\Phi^{\T} \vek Q(t) . \tag{10.53}\]

Masses and stiffnesses are decoupled. The damping is decoupled only if the matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\) is diagonal. Otherwise row \(r\) still contains terms \((\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi)_{\mathrm{rs}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\) of other modes, and the modal coordinates influence each other.

10.9.1 Classical damping (klasično dušenje)

Diagonality gets a name.

Definition 10.44 Classical damping (klasično dušenje)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix, \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\), \(\mat\Phi\) the modal matrix of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) (definition 10.15) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} > 0\). A symmetric positive semidefinite matrix \(\mat C\) of order \(n\) is classical damping (klasično dušenje) if \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\) is a diagonal matrix. We write its diagonal elements as \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is,

\[\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \mat C\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}}{2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}} \ge 0 , \qquad r = 1, \dots, n , \tag{10.54}\]

and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the damping ratio of the \(r\)-th mode (razmernik dušenja \(r\)-tega načina). For a repeated eigenvalue we require diagonality for at least one choice of the columns \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) of theorem 10.13.

Source: CK (10.79)-(10.80) (PDF p. 322), where the same is called "modal damping", "proportional damping" or "classical damping". Nonnegativity from definition 7.15: \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \mat C\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\).

The symbol \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) has the same meaning as in definition 9.5. Row \(r\) of equation (10.53) is the equation of an oscillator with mass \(1\), stiffness \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) and damping constant \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), and its damping ratio is \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\sqrt{1\cdot\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2}) = \zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Where does such a matrix \(\mat C\) occur at all? We usually do not know the dampers of a structure one by one, since damping arises in joints and in nonstructural parts. It is measured as the damping ratio of individual modes, which for structures according to Craig and Kurdila lies between \(0{,}01\) and \(0{,}1\) (CK PDF 322). We therefore need matrices \(\mat C\) for which the \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) are chosen or computed without difficulty. The simplest is the sum of the mass matrix and the stiffness matrix, because both are already diagonal in modal coordinates.

Definition 10.45 Rayleigh damping (Rayleighovo dušenje)

Let \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\). Rayleigh damping (Rayleighovo dušenje) is the matrix

\[\mat C = a_0 \mat M + a_1 \mat K , \qquad a_0 \ge 0 , \quad a_1 \ge 0 , \tag{10.55}\]

where \(a_0\) (unit 1/s) and \(a_1\) (unit s) are real constants.

Source: CK (10.76) (PDF p. 321), where it is also called "proportional damping".

This is classical damping, and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is obtained without computing the matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\).

Lemma 10.46 Damping ratios of Rayleigh damping (razmerniki Rayleighovega dušenja)

Let \(\mat C = a_0 \mat M + a_1 \mat K\) be Rayleigh damping and \(\mat K\) positive definite. Then \(\mat C\) is classical damping with

\[\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{a_0}{2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}} + \frac{a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{2} , \qquad r = 1, \dots, n . \tag{10.56}\]

Let \(i < j\) be two modes with \(0 < \omega_{\mathrm{i}} < \omega_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\) the desired damping ratios. The constants for which Rayleigh damping gives exactly these ratios are

\[a_0 = \frac{2\omega_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{j}}(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{j}} - \zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}})}{\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2} , \qquad a_1 = \frac{2(\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\omega_{\mathrm{j}} - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}})}{\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2} , \tag{10.57}\]

and we can use them if both are nonnegative. The damping ratio of the other modes is then given by equation (10.56).

Proof

We must show that \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\) is diagonal with elements \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and that the constants solve the two equations for \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\).

  1. By lemma 10.16, \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_1^2, \dots, \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2)\). Hence \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = a_0 \mat I + a_1\,\mathrm{diag}(\omega_1^2, \dots, \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2)\), a diagonal matrix with elements \(a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric as a sum of symmetric matrices and positive semidefinite, because \(a_0, a_1 \ge 0\) and \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are positive definite (definition 6.27). So it is classical damping, and by equation (10.54), \(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). Division by \(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives equation (10.56).
  3. The requirement on \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\) gives two linear equations for \(a_0\) and \(a_1\): \(a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(a_0 + a_1\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\omega_{\mathrm{j}}\). Subtract them: \(a_1(\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = 2(\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\omega_{\mathrm{j}} - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}})\), from which \(a_1\) is the one in equation (10.57), because \(\omega_{\mathrm{j}}^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2 > 0\). From the first equation \(a_0 = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}} - a_1\omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2\), which after bringing to a common denominator gives \(a_0\) of equation (10.57).

Source: CK (10.77)-(10.78) (PDF p. 321). The expressions for \(a_0\) and \(a_1\) are the solution of a system of two equations (checked with SymPy), they are not written in the source.

Expression (10.56) has two parts. The term \(a_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\) falls with frequency, the term \(a_1\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/2\) rises. The first belongs to \(a_0 \mat M\), which acts on every mass \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the force \(-a_0 m_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), as if every mass were connected to the resting surroundings by a damper. The second belongs to \(a_1 \mat K\), which is like a damper in parallel with every spring. Such dampers do not exist in reality, but the matrix is simple and the computation with it is transparent.

Frame A has \(\omega_1 = 19{,}578\) 1/s and \(\omega_2 = 51{,}257\) 1/s, and its modal matrix (in kg\(^{-1/2}\)) is \(\mat\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}023511 & 0{,}038042 \\ 0{,}038042 & -0{,}023511 \end{pmatrix}\). We want \(\zeta_1 = \zeta_2 = 0{,}02\), a value inside the range from \(0{,}01\) to \(0{,}1\) given by CK. Equation (10.57) gives \(a_0 = 0{,}56668\) 1/s and \(a_1 = 5{,}6469\cdot 10^{-4}\) s. The damping matrix is

\[\mat C = a_0 \mat M + a_1 \mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 850{,}02 & -283{,}34 \\ -283{,}34 & 566{,}68 \end{pmatrix} \text{N s/m} ,\]

and the computation confirms that \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(0{,}7831,\ 2{,}0503)\) 1/s, the off-diagonal elements are below \(10^{-15}\). Check: \(2\cdot 0{,}02\cdot 19{,}578 = 0{,}7831\) and \(2\cdot 0{,}02\cdot 51{,}257 = 2{,}0503\). Between the two modes the damping ratio is smaller than \(0{,}02\). It is smallest at \(\omega = \sqrt{a_0/a_1} = \sqrt{\omega_1\omega_2} = 31{,}678\) 1/s (that is \(5{,}042\) Hz), where it equals \(\sqrt{a_0 a_1} = 0{,}01789\), and outside the interval between \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) it grows (figure 10.27). A mode at \(\omega = 300\) 1/s would already have \(\zeta = 0{,}0856\). Rayleigh damping therefore damps higher modes more strongly. This is not a property of the structure but a consequence of the formula, and we must check it by measuring the damping ratios.

With two constants we can prescribe two damping ratios. For \(n = 3\) this is not enough. Take a frame with three equal storeys (mass \(500\) kg, storey stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, section 10.3), which has \(\omega = 14{,}098\), \(39{,}502\) and \(57{,}082\) 1/s. If we fit Rayleigh damping to \(\zeta_1 = \zeta_3 = 0{,}02\), we get \(a_0 = 0{,}45224\) 1/s, \(a_1 = 5{,}6195\cdot 10^{-4}\) s and \(\zeta_2 = 0{,}01682\) by equation (10.56). The second mode is not free. For all three modes at once we need another form of the matrix.

Definition 10.47 Modal damping (modalno dušenje)

Let \(\mat\Phi\) be the modal matrix of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with positive \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and let \(\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_{\mathrm{n}} \ge 0\) be arbitrary numbers. Modal damping (modalno dušenje) with damping ratios \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the matrix

\[\mat C = \mat M\mat\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_1\omega_1, \dots, 2\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\,\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M . \tag{10.58}\]

Source: CK (10.79)-(10.84) (PDF pp. 322-323), where the same formula is derived from \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{\T}\mat M\).

Lemma 10.48 Modal damping is classical damping (modalno dušenje je klasično dušenje)

The matrix (10.58) is classical damping with damping ratios \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\). Every classical damping with these damping ratios has the form (10.58).

Proof

We must show both directions, each in one step.

  1. If \(\mat C\) is given by equation (10.58), then \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = (\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi)\,\mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\,(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi) = \mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\), because \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) (lemma 10.16). The matrix \(\mat C\) is symmetric, because \(\mat M\) and the diagonal matrix are symmetric, and positive semidefinite: \(\vek v^{\T} \mat C\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \mat M\vek v)^2 \ge 0\) for every column \(\vek v\) (we write \(\mat C = \mat M\mat\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\,(\mat M\mat\Phi)^{\T}\) and the sum is a quadratic form in the column \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\mat M\vek v\)). By definition 10.44 it is classical damping.
  2. If \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = \mat D\) is diagonal, we use \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\) (lemma 10.16) and its transpose \(\mat\Phi^{-\T} = \mat M\mat\Phi\). From \(\mat C = \mat\Phi^{-\T} \mat D\,\mat\Phi^{-1}\) it follows that \(\mat C = \mat M\mat\Phi\,\mat D\,\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. CK (10.81)-(10.84) (PDF pp. 322-323) derives the formula in direction 2.

Classical and modal damping are therefore the same set of matrices, with different starting points: for classical damping we have the matrix \(\mat C\) and read \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) from it, for modal damping we choose \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) and build \(\mat C\) from them. For three storeys with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}02\) in all three modes we get

\[\mat C = \begin{pmatrix} 858{,}20 & -251{,}63 & -54{,}66 \\ -251{,}63 & 803{,}55 & -306{,}29 \\ -54{,}66 & -306{,}29 & 551{,}91 \end{pmatrix} \text{N s/m} .\]

The element \(C_{13} = -54{,}66\) N s/m says that we would have to connect the first and the third floor with a damper that the frame does not have: the force on floor \(1\) from the dampers between the ground and floor \(1\) and between floors \(1\) and \(2\) is determined only by the velocities of floors \(1\) and \(2\). Modal damping is mathematically convenient, but physically we cannot always build it from dampers. It is a computation with prescribed damping ratios, not a prediction of dampers.

10.9.2 Modal superposition (modalna superpozicija)

With classical damping every mode behaves as an oscillator of chapter 9. Let us write this as a theorem that combines existence and uniqueness of the solution, the decoupling and the formula.

Theorem 10.49 Modal superposition (modalna superpozicija)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix, \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\), \(\mat\Phi\) their modal matrix and \(\mat C\) classical damping with damping ratios \(0 \le \zeta_{\mathrm{r}} < 1\). Let \(\vek Q\) be a column of piecewise continuous functions on \([0, T]\). Denote \(\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta_{\mathrm{r}}^2}\) and \(\boldsymbol\eta = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M \vek q\). Solutions are understood as in section 9.4.4, for each component.

  1. The function \(\vek q\) solves the equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K \vek q = \vek Q(t)\) if and only if for every \(r = 1, \dots, n\)
    \[\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \vek Q(t) . \tag{10.59}\]
  2. For given columns \(\vek q(0)\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0)\) a solution exists and is unique, \(\vek q(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^n\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\), with \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \mat M \vek q(0)\) and \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \mat M\dot{\vek q}(0)\).
  3. For \(t \in [0, T]\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t}\Big(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}t \\ &\quad + \frac{\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)}{\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}t\Big) \\ &\quad + \int_0^t h_{\mathrm{r}}(t - s)\,\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \vek Q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s . \end{aligned} \tag{10.60}\]
    where \(h_{\mathrm{r}}(u) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} u}\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}u)/\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}\).
Proof

We must show that the substitution \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\) is invertible, that it decouples the equations, and that every row falls under the theorems of chapter 9.

  1. By lemma 10.16, \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\), so \(\boldsymbol\eta = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M \vek q\) is uniquely determined by the column \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\). The components of \(\boldsymbol\eta\) are linear combinations of the components of \(\vek q\) and conversely, so \(\vek q\) is a solution in the sense of section 9.4.4 if and only if \(\boldsymbol\eta\) is.
  2. Insert \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\) into the equation and multiply it from the left by \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\). The matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\) is invertible, so the new equation is equivalent to the original. We get equation (10.53).
  3. Because of \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\), \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\) (lemma 10.16) and \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}})\) (definition 10.44), row \(r\) of equation (10.53) is exactly equation (10.59). This proves item 1.
  4. The initial values \(\vek q(0)\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0)\) determine \(\boldsymbol\eta(0) = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M \vek q(0)\) and \(\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}(0) = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\dot{\vek q}(0)\) and conversely. Row \(r\) is a second-order equation with constant coefficients, the characteristic polynomial \(s^2 + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} s + \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) has leading coefficient \(1 \ne 0\), and the right-hand side \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \vek Q\) is piecewise continuous. By theorem 9.36 it has exactly one solution for given \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\) and \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\). The function \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\) built from these solutions is, by step 3, a solution of the original equation. If \(\tilde{\vek q}\) is another solution with the same initial values, the rows for \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\eta} = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\tilde{\vek q}\) solve the same equations with the same initial values, so \(\tilde{\boldsymbol\eta} = \boldsymbol\eta\) and \(\tilde{\vek q} = \mat\Phi\tilde{\boldsymbol\eta} = \vek q\). This proves item 2.
  5. Row \(r\) is the equation of chapter 9 with \(m = 1\), \(k = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), \(c = 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), so with natural frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} < 1\) (definition 9.5). Theorem 9.79 for \(t_0 = 0\) and \(m = 1\) gives \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{pr}} + \int_0^t h_{\mathrm{r}}(t - s)\,\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek Q(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), where \(\eta_{\mathrm{pr}}\) is the free motion with the given initial values. By theorem 9.43 (the underdamped case) this is the first term of equation (10.60). This proves item 3.

Source: CK (11.8)-(11.18) (PDF pp. 344-346), (11.34) (PDF p. 360), GR section 2.7 (PDF pp. 109-113, undamped: normal equations (2.105), initial conditions (2.107), Duhamel integral (2.113)). The damped form with \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is from CK. The proof of existence and uniqueness from theorem 9.36 for each row separately is not in the source.

The theorem showed that classical damping splits a problem of order \(n\) into \(n\) oscillators, each of which obeys everything we learned in chapter 9. Free damped vibration, the step response, beating and the harmonic response hold mode by mode. The force \(\vek Q\) enters mode \(r\) as the modal force \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T} \vek Q(t)\) (modalna sila): with weights that are the components of the mode.

Let us test the theorem on frame A with Rayleigh damping at \(\zeta_1 = \zeta_2 = 0{,}02\). On the upper floor a constant force of \(100\) N starts to act at \(t = 0\), and the frame was at rest until then. The static displacement is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{st}} = \mat K^{-1}\vek Q = (0{,}19930,\ 0{,}39860)\) mm (this is \(100\) N times the second column of the flexibility matrix \(\mat D\) of lemma 10.3: a force on floor \(2\) moves floor \(1\) by \(F/k\) and floor \(2\) by \(2F/k\)). The modal forces are \(f_1 = \varphi_{21}\cdot 100 = 3{,}8042\) and \(f_2 = \varphi_{22}\cdot 100 = -2{,}3511\) in units of N kg\(^{-1/2}\) (with \(\mat\Phi\) from the example above). By theorem 9.69 every mode has the response \(f_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) multiplied by

\[g_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t}\Big(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}t + \frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}t\Big) ,\]

which reaches at most \(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta_{\mathrm{r}}^2}} = 1{,}9391\). The static displacement splits into the modal shares \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} f_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is, on floor \(1\) and on floor \(2\):

  • mode 1: \(0{,}23334\) mm on floor \(1\) and \(0{,}37756\) mm on floor \(2\), that is \(1{,}1708\) and \(0{,}9472\) of the static displacement,
  • mode 2: \(-0{,}03404\) mm on floor \(1\) and \(0{,}02104\) mm on floor \(2\), that is \(-0{,}1708\) and \(0{,}0528\) of the static displacement.

The sum is the static displacement. The largest displacement of floor \(2\) is \(0{,}76160\) mm, that is \(1{,}9107\) times the static displacement. Here the shares are positive and add up to \(1\), so \(q_2(t)/q_{2,\mathrm{st}} = 0{,}9472\,g_1(t) + 0{,}0528\,g_2(t)\) is an average of the two \(g_{\mathrm{r}}\) and cannot exceed \(1{,}9391\). Floor \(1\), however, is surprising: its maximum is \(0{,}42100\) mm, that is \(2{,}1124\) times the static displacement, at \(t = 0{,}1443\) s (figure 10.28). The maximum of twice the static displacement from theorem 9.69 holds for one oscillator, here it is exceeded, because floor \(1\) has shares with opposite signs. Mode 1 alone would give floor \(1\) the factor \(1{,}1708\cdot 1{,}9391 = 2{,}2703\) of the static displacement, because its share \(1{,}1708\) is larger than \(1\), the sum of both shares. Mode 2 has the share \(-0{,}1708\) and it reduces the sum. At the moment of the maximum of the sum \(g_1 = 1{,}892\) and \(g_2 = 0{,}601\), so the sum is \(1{,}1708\cdot 1{,}892 - 0{,}1708\cdot 0{,}601 = 2{,}1124\). The reduction is only \(0{,}1027\), not \(1{,}9391\cdot 0{,}1708 = 0{,}33\), because mode 2 is not at its maximum at this time: its period is \(0{,}1226\) s, so by \(0{,}1443\) s it has already fallen once to \(g_2 = 0{,}118\) and is rising again. For a sum with shares of opposite signs, twice the static displacement is therefore not an upper bound. The upper bound is the sum of the absolute values of the shares multiplied by \(1{,}9391\), here \((1{,}1708 + 0{,}1708)\cdot 1{,}9391 = 2{,}60\).

10.9.3 Damping that is not classical (dušenje, ki ni klasično)

What happens if the dampers are such that the matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\) is not diagonal? Take a single damper between the ground and floor \(1\), in parallel with the columns of the lower storey. The force on floor \(1\) is \(-c_{\mathrm{d}}\dot q_1\) and floor \(2\) does not feel it directly, so

\[\mat C = c_{\mathrm{d}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = c_{\mathrm{d}}\begin{pmatrix} \varphi_{11}^2 & \varphi_{11}\varphi_{12} \\ \varphi_{11}\varphi_{12} & \varphi_{12}^2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.61}\]

By \(\varphi_{\mathrm{ij}}\) we denote the element of \(\mat\Phi\) in row \(i\) and column \(j\), so \(\varphi_{11} = 0{,}023511\) and \(\varphi_{12} = 0{,}038042\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). Choose \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) so that the diagonal element of the first mode is \(2\cdot 0{,}02\cdot\omega_1\), that is, \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\cdot 0{,}02\,\omega_1/\varphi_{11}^2 = 1416{,}70\) N s/m. Then

\[\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}7831 & 1{,}2671 \\ 1{,}2671 & 2{,}0503 \end{pmatrix} \text{1/s} .\]

The diagonal is the same as for Rayleigh damping, since \(\varphi_{12}^2/\varphi_{11}^2 = 2{,}618 = \omega_2/\omega_1\) (the same golden number as in the ratio of frequencies in section 10.2), so \(\zeta_2 = 0{,}02\) as well. The off-diagonal element \(1{,}2671\) 1/s, however, is not small. It equals \(\sqrt{0{,}7831\cdot 2{,}0503}\), because the matrix has the form \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\,\vek b\vek b^{\T}\) with the column \(\vek b = (\varphi_{11}, \varphi_{12})^{\T}\), where the product of the diagonal elements \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\varphi_{11}^2\cdot c_{\mathrm{d}}\varphi_{12}^2\) equals the square of the off-diagonal one \((c_{\mathrm{d}}\varphi_{11}\varphi_{12})^2\). The modes are therefore coupled and theorem 10.49 cannot be used for this case, since it requires a diagonal matrix.

What remains of the modes? Let us try in the free equation \(\vek q = \mathrm{Re}(\vek u\,\mathrm{e}^{st})\) with a complex number \(s\) and a complex column \(\vek u \ne 0\) (theorem 9.28, lemma 9.32). The equation \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K \vek q = 0\) becomes

\[(s^2 \mat M + s \mat C + \mat K)\,\vek u = 0 . \tag{10.62}\]

With classical damping the solutions are \(\vek u = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(s = -\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}} \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d},\mathrm{r}}\). Here the columns \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) no longer solve the equation. The numbers \(s\) are the zeros of the determinant of the matrix \(s^2 \mat M + s\mat C + \mat K\) (definition 2.50), a polynomial of fourth degree in \(s\). We compute them with NumPy from the first-order form (zapis prvega reda), in which \(\vek q\) and \(\dot{\vek q}\) are collected into a column of \(2n = 4\) numbers. The matrix of this form is not symmetric, so the spectral theorem of section 10.3 does not apply to it. The roots are \(-0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02493 \pm 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s, and the damping ratios \(-\mathrm{Re}\,s/|s|\) are both \(0{,}0200\). With classical damping and the same damping ratios they would be \(-0{,}39157 \pm 19{,}5744\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02513 \pm 51{,}2464\,\mathrm{i}\), which differs in the fourth digit. Frequencies and decay hardly change. The shape of the motion changes.

The columns \(\vek u\) for the two roots with positive imaginary part have a complex ratio between the floors: \(u_2/u_1 = 1{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\) for the first and \(-0{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\) for the second. With classical damping the ratios are real, \(1{,}61803\) and \(-0{,}61803\). The ratio \(1{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\) has modulus \(1{,}61745\) and argument \(1{,}417^\circ\). The real solution \(\vek q = \mathrm{Re}(\vek u\,\mathrm{e}^{st})\) then has the floors with a phase shift of \(1{,}417^\circ\): floor \(2\) leads floor \(1\) by \(1{,}417/360 = 0{,}39\,\%\) of the period, that is \(1{,}26\) ms at \(T = 0{,}3209\) s, so the floors do not pass through zero together (figure 10.29). Such a solution is called a complex mode (kompleksni način). With classical damping the floors are always in phase or in antiphase, as in section 10.2.

How large is the error if we nevertheless neglect the coupling, that is, use classical damping with the same diagonal of \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\)? This is the matrix \(\mat C\) of the Rayleigh damping from the example above. We compare the response to the force \(100\) N \(\cos\Omega t\) on floor \(2\). Try \(\vek q = \mathrm{Re}(\vek x\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t})\) as in theorem 9.52: differentiation becomes multiplication by \(\mathrm{i}\Omega\) and we get two complex linear equations

\[(\mat K - \Omega^2 \mat M + \mathrm{i}\Omega \mat C)\,\vek x = \vek Q_0 , \tag{10.63}\]

which we solve with NumPy for every \(\Omega\) (the general relation between force and response is treated in the next section). The largest amplitude of the upper floor near \(\omega_1\) is \(9{,}4445\) mm with the coupling and \(9{,}4418\) mm without it, that is, \(-0{,}029\,\%\). Near \(\omega_2\) it is \(0{,}5418\) mm with the coupling and \(0{,}5337\) mm without it, \(-1{,}50\,\%\).

Why is the error at \(\omega_1\) so small and at \(\omega_2\) fifty times larger? At \(\Omega = \omega_1\) the modal coordinate \(\eta_2\) is small and in phase with the force, so the coupling term \(\tilde c_{12}\Omega\eta_2\) in the equation for \(\eta_1\) is only \(2{,}5\,\%\) of the term \(\tilde c_{11}\Omega\eta_1\) (the notation \(\tilde c_{\mathrm{rs}}\) means the element \((\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi)_{\mathrm{rs}}\)). In addition, the coupling term lies in the complex plane perpendicular to the force, so it changes the modulus of the sum only in second order: \(\tfrac12\cdot 0{,}025^2 = 0{,}03\,\%\). At \(\Omega = \omega_2\) it is just the opposite: at resonance mode \(2\) is excited and its velocity drives mode \(1\) with \(38\,\%\) of the modal force \(f_1\). Mode \(1\) is off resonance at this frequency, but it contributes \(0{,}089\) mm of the \(0{,}540\) mm of the upper floor (\(16{,}5\,\%\)), and the coupling changes its amplitude by \(38\,\%\). The computed effect on the floor is nevertheless only \(1{,}5\,\%\): at \(\omega_2\) the contribution of mode \(1\) on floor \(2\) is in quadrature (a phase difference of \(90^\circ\)) with the contribution of mode \(2\), so the modulus changes only in second order, \(\tfrac12\cdot 0{,}165^2 = 1{,}4\,\%\). The coupling is therefore not small in the matrix (\(1{,}2671\) against \(0{,}78\) and \(2{,}05\)), but it is small in its effect, because the modes at different frequencies (\(2{,}6\) times apart) do not meet in resonance. We must be careful where the response itself is small: at \(7{,}130\) Hz the upper floor has \(0{,}0250\) mm with the coupling and \(0{,}0150\) mm without it, a relatively large difference at a small amplitude.

For systems whose damping is not classical and where such errors are not acceptable, we need the first-order form and complex modes in full. This theory requires the eigenproblem of a nonsymmetric matrix of order \(2n\), which the spectral theorem of section 10.3 does not cover, and it is in chapter 20. The other way out is numerical solution in time, which does not need modes and which one of the following sections treats.

10.9.4 Truncation of modes (odrez načinov)

For large systems we do not use all \(n\) modes. Bar C of section 10.7 has \(16\) modes with \(16\) finite elements, and a model of a structure can have more than a million (CK PDF 344). If we keep the \(N\) lowest modes, the sum \(\vek q = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^N\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is no longer exact. How large is the error? Higher modes respond statically to a slow force: the harmonic response of a mode at \(\Omega \ll \omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is close to \(f_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) (theorem 9.52). We therefore need to know how much of the static displacement falls on each mode.

Lemma 10.50 Flexibility matrix by modes (podajnostna matrika po načinih)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive definite matrix, \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\) and \(\mat\Phi\) their modal matrix. Then

\[\mat K^{-1} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^n\frac{\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} . \tag{10.64}\]
Proof

We must show that the right-hand side is a matrix that gives \(\mat I\) when multiplied by \(\mat K\).

  1. From \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) it follows that \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{\T}\mat M\), so \(\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M = \mat I\) and \(\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^{\T} = \mat M^{-1}\).
  2. From \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat K\mat\Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}) =: \Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}\) (the matrix with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) on the diagonal) it follows that \(\mat K = \mat\Phi^{-\T}\Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat M\mat\Phi\Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\), where we used \(\mat\Phi^{-1} = \mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\) and \(\mat\Phi^{-\T} = \mat M\mat\Phi\).
  3. Let \(\mat B = \mat\Phi\Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1}\mat\Phi^{\T}\), the matrix with the sum on the right-hand side of equation (10.64) (it exists, because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) by definition 6.27, since \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \ne \vek 0\)). Then \(\mat K\mat B = \mat M\mat\Phi\Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat M\mat\Phi)\Lambda_{\mathrm{d}}^{-1}\mat\Phi^{\T} = \mat M\mat\Phi\mat\Phi^{\T} = \mat M \mat M^{-1} = \mat I\) by step 1. By definition 1.107, \(\mat B = \mat K^{-1}\).

Source: The proof is not from a source.

For a static force \(\vek F\) the displacement is therefore \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F)/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Mode \(r\) contributes \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F)/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the static share that we computed above for frame A, and it falls as \(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Bar C: steel, \(L = 1\) m, \(A = 100\) mm\(^2\), clamped at \(x = 0\), free at \(x = L\), \(16\) equal elements with the mass matrix of lemma 10.31. A force of \(1\) kN acts on the free end. The static displacement of the end is \(FL/(EA) = 51{,}0204\) µm. The share of mode \(r\) in the displacement of the end is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2F/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)\) is the last component of the column \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and with one, two, three, five and all sixteen modes it is

\[81{,}12\,\%,\quad 90{,}19\,\%,\quad 93{,}50\,\%,\quad 96{,}29\,\%,\quad 100\,\%\]

of the static displacement (figure 10.30). The first mode thus gives \(81{,}12\,\%\), the other fifteen modes together the remaining \(18{,}88\,\%\). Why does the sum approach so slowly? With the normalization \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\mat M\mat\Phi = \mat I\) we have \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2\rho A L = 2{,}003,\ 2{,}029,\ 2{,}082\) for \(r = 1, 2, 3\), so almost constant: all modes have about the same value at the free end. The eigenvalues, however, grow like \((2r-1)^2\), since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}/\lambda_1\) divided by \((2r-1)^2\) equals \(1{,}000\), \(1{,}006\) and \(1{,}019\) (the frequencies of the bar are close to \((2r-1)\,c/(4L)\), section 10.7). So the shares fall like \(1/(2r-1)^2\): \(81{,}12\,\%\), \(9{,}07\,\%\), \(3{,}31\,\%\), \(1{,}72\,\%\) and \(1{,}07\,\%\). The sum of the remaining shares for \(r > N\) is approximately proportional to \(1/N\), which can be seen from \(18{,}88\,\%\) at \(N = 1\) and \(3{,}71\,\%\) at \(N = 5\) (ratio \(5{,}1\), prediction \(5\)). With ten modes the error is still \(1{,}33\,\%\).

The error does not come from damping or from fast motions, but from the fact that higher modes respond statically to every slow force, and truncation loses these shares. Craig and Kurdila call the solution with the kept modes the mode-displacement solution (rešitev z odmiki načinov) and write that it can give an inaccurate result even under static loading, because the series converges slowly (CK §11.4, PDF 367). The improved mode-acceleration solution (rešitev s pospeški načinov) adds to the truncated modes their static part, computed directly (pseudostatic response, CK (11.40), PDF 361). This improvement, the effective modal mass and the additional mass method are only named, they are treated in chapter 21. For bar C the displacement of the end under a static force is known directly (\(FL/(EA)\)), so truncation here is only an example of slow convergence.

The section showed three things. With classical damping (a diagonal matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T} \mat C\mat\Phi\), obtained by Rayleigh's choice or by chosen \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\)) every mode remains an oscillator of chapter 9 (theorem 10.49), which we proved by the substitution \(\vek q = \mat\Phi\boldsymbol\eta\). With damping that is not classical the modes couple and become complex, but for frame A the error of the resonance peaks is \(0{,}03\,\%\) and \(1{,}5\,\%\), because modes with a frequency ratio of \(2{,}6\) do not meet in resonance. With truncation of modes the error decreases only as \(1/N\), because higher modes contribute a static part. Section 10.10 computes the response to harmonic excitation from the modal sum.
