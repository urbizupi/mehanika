Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.6 Modes of a beam

For the string and the bar, the equation of motion has a second derivative in space, so the modes were sines. For the beam it has a fourth derivative. What shape does a beam take when it vibrates in one mode (definition 13.6), and at which frequencies? Chapter 12 gave approximations with the consistent and the lumped mass of a beam, and the values with the consistent mass lay above the true ones (errors in figure 13.56). Now we compute the true value and compare the approximations with it (section 13.6.7).

From history

Jacob Bernoulli wrote that the curvature of a bent bar at each point is proportional to the bending moment at that point. He computed the constant wrongly because of a mistaken assumption about the axis of rotation of the cross section, but the general form of the relation was correct and Euler used it later (TIMO, PDF p. 35). It is the relation \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) of chapter 5. Daniel Bernoulli suggested to Euler in a letter that he should use the calculus of variations to find the shape for which \(\int \mathrm{d}s/r^2\) is a minimum, where \(r\) is the radius of curvature. Up to a constant factor this integral is the strain energy of a bent bar (TIMO, PDF p. 35). Euler worked this out in the appendix on elastic curves to his book of 1744 (TIMO, PDF pp. 37 and 39). Daniel Bernoulli was also the first to derive the differential equation of the lateral vibration of prismatic bars and used it to study particular modes of the motion, and Euler integrated it (TIMO, PDF p. 36). Bernoulli made a series of experiments on the position of the nodes, that is the points that stay at rest during the vibration, and on the pitch of the tone, and wrote to Euler that they agree with the theory (TIMO, PDF p. 36). For vibration Euler took small deflections, so that the second derivative equals the curvature, and clamped the bar vertically at one end so that gravity does not affect the motion (TIMO, PDF p. 42). A small piece of the bar moves like a pendulum, so the force toward the axis is proportional to the displacement of the piece (TIMO, PDF pp. 42-43). Euler concluded that the bar at rest would take the same curvature as during the vibration if a force proportional to the displacement acted on each piece (TIMO, PDF p. 43). The reason is that in one mode all points vibrate with a common time dependence \(\cos\omega t\), so the acceleration of each point is \(-\omega^2\) times its displacement and the force the bar must supply is proportional to the displacement. He obtained the equation of motion by differentiating the relation between curvature and moment twice, because the second derivative of the moment equals the intensity of the lateral load (TIMO, PDF p. 43). The load is here the force of the piece due to its displacement, which gives \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \rho A\,\omega^2 w\), the equation of this section for one mode. TIMO says that Euler thus reached a conclusion that we now reach with d'Alembert's principle, and that he did not know d'Alembert's book of 1743 when he wrote the appendix (TIMO, PDF p. 43, footnote). He integrated the equation and found the frequency equation from the end conditions, for the cantilever, simply supported ends, clamped ends and both ends free, and for all these cases a formula for the frequency with a number that depends on the end conditions and on the mode (TIMO, PDF pp. 43-44). He also proposed to determine from the frequency, by experiment, the stiffness that he called the "absolute elasticity" (TIMO, PDF p. 44).

The story showed that the mode of a beam is fixed by a fourth derivative, driven by the force of the displacement itself, and that the frequency is fixed by the end conditions and the mode. So we now write the mode of the beam and compute the frequencies.

We study a straight prismatic beam of length \(L\) in the Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14). The axis \(x\) lies along the beam, \(w(x, t)\) is the transverse deflection (poves) at time \(t\), and the stiffness \(EI\) and the mass per length \(\rho A\) are constant. The beam carries no load, so by theorem 13.28 (equation (13.31) with \(q = 0\))

\[\rho A\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} + EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = 0 .\]

The model has three named idealizations. The first is the Euler-Bernoulli one: cross-sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis, so there is no shear deformation, and the turning of the cross-sections is neglected. As a measure of smallness we take the radius of gyration \(r = \sqrt{I/A}\), a length that is \(11{,}5\) mm for the \(20 \times 40\) mm section (height \(40\) mm in the plane of bending), so \(r/L = 0{,}0115\) at \(L = 1\) m. Section 13.10 computes that at this slenderness the turning of the cross-sections and then shear lower the first frequency of a simply supported beam by \(0{,}066\) % and \(0{,}270\) %, and for a short beam \(L = 80\) mm (\(r/L = 0{,}144\)) by \(8{,}9\) % and \(24{,}9\) %. The model therefore holds for slender beams. The second idealization is a small slope: the equation is linear because we take \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for the curvature of the axis in place of the exact \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}/(1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{3/2}\) (chapter 5) and neglect the stretching of the axis. Chapter 5 and section 13.5 allow a slope up to \(0{,}1\), at which the factor \((1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\) differs from \(1\) by at most \(1{,}5\) %. Here we take the stricter bound \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\), at which this difference is at most \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\). We take the stricter bound because the bound \(0{,}1\) would allow a tip displacement of \(73\) mm in the first mode of the cantilever, while the steel of the beam of the introduction (\(E = 196\) GPa, yield stress \(220\) MPa) yields at the clamp already at a tip displacement of about \(16\) mm. In the first vibration of the cantilever (figure 13.52) the largest slope, when the tip is displaced by \(1\), is \(1{,}3765/L\), so the slope stays below \(0{,}01\) as long as the tip displacement does not exceed \(0{,}01\,L/1{,}3765 = 7{,}3\) mm at \(L = 1\) m. The third idealization is ideal supports: the clamping is perfectly rigid and the simple support frictionless. A real wall rotates a little, so a real end is not as stiff as a perfect clamp. We expect that a real beam therefore vibrates somewhat more slowly than we compute, but we do not prove it here.

For numbers we take the steel beam of chapters 5 and 12: section \(20 \times 40\) mm, bending in the plane of height \(40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m. The computations are in SymPy, mpmath and NumPy. Figure 13.42 shows these dimensions on the section on the left and on the beam seen from the side on the right. On the left is the rectangular section with the \(20\) mm dimension for the width and the \(40\) mm dimension for the height, and the arrow \(w\) shows the direction of the deflection in the plane of the height. On the right is the side view of the beam with its length \(L\), which is in reality much longer and is shortened here.

13.6.1 End conditions

A beam end is supported in one of three ways that we know from chapter 5, and figure 13.43 shows them one under the other. The blue curve in each row is the deflection of the beam and the grey dashed line is the axis before bending. A clamp has a wall and allows neither displacement nor rotation: the curve leaves the wall on the axis and horizontally. A simple support has a support that allows no displacement but transmits no moment, so the moment there is zero: the curve starts on the axis and its slope is free. A free end has no support and transmits neither force nor moment, so the curve may leave the axis and tilt there. The conditions beside each row are written in the definition that follows. The signs are as in chapter 5: moment \(M = EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), shear force \(V = -M_{\mathrm{x}} = -EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\).

Definition 13.30 Clamped, simply supported and free end of a beam (vpet, prostoležeč in prost konec nosilca)

Let \(e\) be one of the ends \(0\) or \(L\) of a beam with deflection \(w(x, t)\). The end \(e\) is, as in figure 13.43,

  • clamped (vpet) if \(w(e, t) = 0\) and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(e, t) = 0\) at every time \(t\),
  • simply supported (prostoležeč) if \(w(e, t) = 0\) and \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(e, t) = 0\) at every time \(t\),
  • free (prost) if \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(e, t) = 0\) and \(EI\,w_{\mathrm{xxx}}(e, t) = 0\) at every time \(t\).

Source: CK (13.15) to (13.17), PDF p. 411, GR (4.244), PDF p. 286. The conditions on the moment and the shear force are the natural conditions of theorem 13.28 with zero force and moment at the end.

The clamp conditions are essential (displacement and slope are prescribed). The conditions at a free end are natural, the principle gives them by itself (section 13.5). At a simple support the displacement is prescribed and the zero moment is a natural condition.

Figure 13.44 shows what one mode means. The grey dashed line is the undeformed axis of the beam, the blue curve is the deflection at three times, \(t = 0\), \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), written in the labels on the left. An example of the shape is a sine, used here only for drawing. The orange curve is the time factor \(\cos\omega t\) with the horizontal axis \(t\), and the blackwhite dots on it are three instants at which the factor has the values \(1\), \(0{,}5\) and \(-0{,}8\). At each instant the deflection is the same shape multiplied by that value, so at \(t_1\) it is half as high and at \(t_2\) it is turned downward.

For a beam, definition 13.6 means the following: the beam vibrates in the same shape \(\varphi(x)\) at all times, \(w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) with \(\omega > 0\), and satisfies the equation of motion and the conditions at both ends. The theorem says what must then hold for \(\varphi\).

Theorem 13.31 Eigenvalue equation of a beam (lastna enačba nosilca)

Let the beam have at each end the conditions of definition 13.30, let \(\omega > 0\) and let \(\varphi\colon [0, L] \to \mathbb R\) be a four times continuously differentiable function that is not identically zero. The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the beam (definition 13.6, shape times time factor) if and only if

\[\varphi'''' = \beta^4\,\varphi \quad \text{on } [0, L] , \qquad \beta^4 = \frac{\rho A\,\omega^2}{EI} , \quad \beta > 0 , \tag{13.36}\]

and the end conditions hold for \(\varphi\) in place of \(w(\cdot, t)\): clamped end \(\varphi(e) = \varphi'(e) = 0\), simply supported \(\varphi(e) = \varphi''(e) = 0\), free \(\varphi''(e) = \varphi'''(e) = 0\).

Proof

We must show that the time factor \(\cos\omega t\) is a common factor of every equation and is not identically zero.

  1. By theorem 8.12, \((\cos\omega t)'' = -\omega^2\cos\omega t\), and \(\cos\omega t\) is not identically zero because \(\cos 0 = 1\) (the first dot on the curve).
  2. For \(w = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) (blue curve times the factor) we have \(w_{\mathrm{xxxx}} = \varphi''''\cos\omega t\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2\varphi\cos\omega t\), and \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xxx}}\) are \(\varphi'\), \(\varphi''\), \(\varphi'''\) times \(\cos\omega t\). The equation of motion is therefore \(\big(EI\,\varphi'''' - \rho A\,\omega^2\varphi\big)(x)\cos\omega t = 0\) for every \(x\) and \(t\).
  3. If equation (13.36) holds, this product (shape times factor) is zero at every \(x\) and \(t\). Conversely, choose \(t = 0\): then \(EI\,\varphi''''(x) = \rho A\,\omega^2\varphi(x)\) for every \(x\), which is equation (13.36), since \(\beta\) is the positive fourth root of \(\rho A\,\omega^2/EI\).
  4. Every end condition of definition 13.30 is an equation in which the value of \(\varphi\) or of one of its derivatives at \(e\), multiplied by \(\cos\omega t\), equals zero (the ends of the blue curve stay where the support requires at all three times). By step 1 it holds at every time if and only if it holds for \(\varphi\) itself.

Source: CK (13.9) to (13.13), PDF p. 410, GR (4.245) to (4.247), PDF pp. 286 to 287: the harmonic motion is substituted into the equation of motion. The proof by steps is not from the source.

The definition requires \(\omega > 0\), that is, vibration. Could there be a motion in a constant shape whose displacement grows or stands still, which would be \(\lambda = \omega^2 \le 0\) in the equation \(EI\,\varphi'''' = \lambda\,\rho A\,\varphi\)? The lemma shows that for the three supports we compute, there cannot. Figure 13.45 shows what the proof compares. At upper left is the deflection with its ends: the blue curve \(\varphi\) above the grey dashed axis, for a simply supported example, the blackwhite dots are the ends, where at least one factor of each product is zero. Below it are the two areas, the blue and the orange one, above their grey axes, the integrals of \(\varphi^2\) and \(\varphi''^2\) (for a sine of the same shape). Both are nonnegative and not zero, so their quotient \(\lambda\) is positive. On the right are the rigid motions: the horizontal deflection of a beam with both ends free, raised above the axis, and the slanted line of a beam simply supported at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), above its axis. These are rigid motions, for which \(\varphi'' = 0\) and therefore \(\lambda = 0\).

Lemma 13.32 Positive eigenvalue of a beam (pozitivna lastna vrednost nosilca)

Let \(\varphi \not\equiv 0\) be a four times continuously differentiable function on \([0, L]\) (blue deflection), \(\lambda\) a real number and \(EI\,\varphi'''' = \lambda\,\rho A\,\varphi\). Let the beam be clamped at both ends, or simply supported at both ends, or clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), and let \(\varphi\) satisfy the end conditions of theorem 13.31. Then \(\lambda > 0\).

Proof

We must show that \(\lambda\) is the quotient of a nonnegative integral and a positive one, that is, of the orange and the blue area. We multiply the equation by \(\varphi\) and integrate over the whole beam, a statement about the interval \([0, L]\) and not about a slice.

  1. By corollary 3.41 with \(f = \varphi\) and \(g = \varphi'''\), and once more with \(f = \varphi'\) and \(g = \varphi''\),
    \[\int_0^L \varphi''''\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x = \Big[\varphi'''\,\varphi - \varphi''\,\varphi'\Big]_0^L + \int_0^L \varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  2. The bracket is zero at each end. A clamped end has \(\varphi = \varphi' = 0\), a simply supported one \(\varphi = \varphi'' = 0\), a free one \(\varphi'' = \varphi''' = 0\), and in every case both products have at least one zero factor.
  3. From the equation and step 1, \(\lambda\,\rho A \int_0^L \varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x = EI \int_0^L \varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The integrand \(\varphi^2\) is continuous and nonnegative (blue area), and since \(\varphi\) is not identically zero, \(\int_0^L \varphi^2\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\) (lemma 6.10, item 3). The right side is nonnegative (orange area), so \(\lambda \ge 0\).
  4. Let \(\lambda = 0\). Then \(\int_0^L \varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), by lemma 6.10 (item 3) \(\varphi'' = 0\) on \([0, L]\), by corollary 1.85 \(\varphi'\) is constant, and \(\varphi(x) = p + q\,x\). At a clamped end \(e\), \(\varphi'(e) = q = 0\) and \(\varphi(e) = p = 0\), so \(\varphi = 0\). For the beam simply supported at both ends, \(\varphi(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi(L) = 0\) give \(p = 0\) and \(q\,L = 0\), so again \(\varphi = 0\). This is a contradiction, so \(\lambda > 0\). The line \(p + q\,x\) would survive only for supports that allow a rigid motion, and a clamped end and two simple supports do not.

For a beam with both ends free, or with one free and one simply supported end, the lemma does not hold, because such a beam can move or rotate as a rigid body. We do not compute these beams here.

Two cases show where the proof of lemma 13.32 stops. For a beam with both ends free we have \(\varphi'' = \varphi''' = 0\) at both ends. The function \(\varphi = 1\) (horizontal orange line) solves \(\varphi'''' = 0 = 0 \cdot \varphi\) and both conditions at both ends, so \(\lambda = 0\) and not \(\lambda > 0\). For a beam simply supported at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), such a solution is \(\varphi = x\) (slanted orange line): \(\varphi(0) = \varphi''(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi''(L) = \varphi'''(L) = 0\). These are a rigid translation and a rigid rotation about the support. Steps 1 to 3 of the proof hold here, since the square bracket is zero at every end, and in both cases \(\int_0^L \varphi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), which in step 3 is exactly what gives \(\lambda = 0\). The difference is in step 4: from \(\varphi'' = 0\) it follows that \(\varphi = p + q\,x\), and with a clamped end or with simple supports at both ends the conditions give \(p = q = 0\). In these two cases \(p\) and \(q\) are arbitrary. The lemma therefore requires supports that prevent rigid motion.

13.6.2 The general solution

The equation \(\varphi'''' = \beta^4\,\varphi\) is an ordinary differential equation (definition 5.44) of fourth order. We know four solutions (four curves): by theorem 8.12 and lemma 9.42 (item 1) the fourth derivative of each of the functions \(\cos\beta x\), \(\sin\beta x\), \(\operatorname{cosh}\beta x\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\beta x\) equals \(\beta^4\) times the function. The lemma says that the other solutions are already their linear combinations.

Figure 13.46 shows the four functions for \(\beta = 1\). On the left the cosine and the sine are blue, the first solid and the second dashed. They oscillate, so they resemble the modes of the string. The hyperbolic cosine and the hyperbolic sine are orange, solid and dashed, and grow without oscillating. The labels at the ends of the curves say which is which. All four start on the vertical axis, where \(x = 0\), and have four different values of the derivatives there. In the middle, on their own axes, is a pair of curves, the blue function \(\varphi\) and the orange dashed combination, which have the same starting point with the same derivatives and therefore lie one on the other. On the right is the third graph: on the axis lies the blue line, the quantity of step 4 of the proof, which is zero when the two curves are equal. It starts at zero and must stay on the axis because the band above and below it is forbidden.

Lemma 13.33 All solutions of the fourth order equation (vse rešitve enačbe četrtega reda)

Let \(\beta > 0\). Every four times differentiable function \(\varphi\) on \([0, L]\) with \(\varphi'''' = \beta^4\,\varphi\) is a sum of four curves of the form

\[\begin{aligned} \varphi(x) &= C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x \\ &\quad + C_3\operatorname{cosh}\beta x + C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta x \quad \text{on } [0, L] , \end{aligned} \tag{13.37}\]

with numbers \(C_1\) to \(C_4\), and every such function is a solution.

Proof

We must show that for given values \(\varphi(0)\), \(\varphi'(0)\), \(\varphi''(0)\), \(\varphi'''(0)\) there are numbers \(C_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the same values, and that the equation with zero initial values has only the zero solution, that is, two solutions with the same starting point and the same derivatives coincide (middle of the figure).

  1. Every function of the form (13.37) is a solution (a sum of four curves), because the equation is linear and all four terms solve it.
  2. The values \((f, f', f'', f''')\) at \(0\) (vertical axis of the figure) are \((1, 0, -\beta^2, 0)\) for \(\cos\beta x\), \((0, \beta, 0, -\beta^3)\) for \(\sin\beta x\), \((1, 0, \beta^2, 0)\) for \(\operatorname{cosh}\beta x\) and \((0, \beta, 0, \beta^3)\) for \(\operatorname{sinh}\beta x\) (theorem 8.12, lemma 9.42, items 1 and 2). We choose
    \[\begin{aligned} C_1 &= \tfrac12\Big(\varphi(0) - \frac{\varphi''(0)}{\beta^2}\Big), & C_3 &= \tfrac12\Big(\varphi(0) + \frac{\varphi''(0)}{\beta^2}\Big), \\ C_2 &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\varphi'(0)}{\beta} - \frac{\varphi'''(0)}{\beta^3}\Big), & C_4 &= \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\varphi'(0)}{\beta} + \frac{\varphi'''(0)}{\beta^3}\Big) . \end{aligned}\]
    Substitution gives \(C_1 + C_3 = \varphi(0)\), \(\beta^2(C_3 - C_1) = \varphi''(0)\), \(\beta(C_2 + C_4) = \varphi'(0)\) and \(\beta^3(C_4 - C_2) = \varphi'''(0)\), so the function (13.37) has the same initial values as \(\varphi\).
  3. Let \(\psi\) be the difference of \(\varphi\) and this function, that is, of the two curves in the middle of the figure. It is a solution (step 1), so \(\psi'''' = \beta^4\psi\) with zero initial values. Let \(S = \psi^2 + \psi'^2 + \psi''^2 + \psi'''^2\). The function \(S\) is differentiable and \(S' = 2\psi\psi' + 2\psi'\psi'' + 2\psi''\psi''' + 2\beta^4\psi\psi'''\). Since \(2uv \le u^2 + v^2\) (from \((u - v)^2 \ge 0\)), \(S' \le (1 + \beta^8)\,\psi^2 + 2\psi'^2 + 2\psi''^2 + 2\psi'''^2 \le K\,S\) with \(K = 2 + \beta^8\).
  4. The function \(\mathrm{e}^{-Kx}\,S(x)\) has derivative \(\mathrm{e}^{-Kx}(S' - K\,S) \le 0\) (theorem 9.13, item 2), so by theorem 1.84 it is nonincreasing: it cannot enter the upper band. Its value at \(0\) is \(S(0) = 0\), so \(\mathrm{e}^{-Kx} S(x) \le 0\) for every \(x\), and since \(S \ge 0\), it cannot enter the lower band, so it lies on the axis and \(S = 0\). So \(\psi = 0\), which means the blue and the orange curve coincide, and \(\varphi\) is the function (13.37).

The end conditions give a homogeneous system for the numbers \(C_{\mathrm{i}}\). We shall need a system of two equations in two unknowns twice, so we solve it in advance. Figure 13.47 shows it in the \((C_1, C_2)\) plane with the axes \(C_1\) and \(C_2\). Each equation is a line through the origin, because the pair \((0, 0)\) is always a solution. On the left the blue and the orange line are different (the numbers \(A_{11}, A_{12}, A_{21}, A_{22}\) are \(2, 1, 1, 3\)), so they meet only at the origin and there is no nonzero solution. In the middle the second pair of equations (\(2, 1, 4, 2\)) gives the same blue line, covered by the orange dashed one, so the solutions are all points on it, the origin included. The arrow shows the direction of this line, \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\). On the right is the case in which the first equation is empty: one orange line remains, since both \(A\) of the first row are zero.

Lemma 13.34 Homogeneous system of two equations (homogen sistem dveh enačb)

Let \(A_{11}\), \(A_{12}\), \(A_{21}\), \(A_{22}\) be real numbers and \((A_{11}, A_{12}) \ne (0, 0)\), as in the first two cases of the figure. The system \(A_{11}\,C_1 + A_{12}\,C_2 = 0\), \(A_{21}\,C_1 + A_{22}\,C_2 = 0\) has a nonzero solution \((C_1, C_2)\) if and only if \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21} = 0\), and then all solutions are multiples of the pair \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\).

Proof

We must show that the condition is necessary and sufficient and that the solutions are multiples, that is, that they all lie on the line with direction \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\).

  1. Let \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21} = 0\), which is the middle case of the figure. The pair \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\) is not zero because \((A_{11}, A_{12}) \ne (0, 0)\). It solves the first equation, \(A_{11}A_{12} - A_{12}A_{11} = 0\), and the second, \(A_{21}A_{12} - A_{22}A_{11} = -(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21}) = 0\).
  2. Let \((C_1, C_2) \ne (0, 0)\) be a solution, that is, a point other than the origin on both lines. We multiply the first equation by \(A_{22}\), the second by \(A_{12}\) and subtract: \((A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21})\,C_1 = 0\). We multiply the first by \(A_{21}\), the second by \(A_{11}\) and subtract: \((A_{12}A_{21} - A_{11}A_{22})\,C_2 = 0\). One of \(C_1\), \(C_2\) is not zero, so \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21} = 0\).
  3. Let this determinant be zero and \((C_1, C_2)\) a solution, that is, a point on the common line. The first equation says \(A_{11}\,C_1 = -A_{12}\,C_2\). If \(A_{11} \ne 0\), then \((C_1, C_2) = (C_2/A_{11})\,(-A_{12}, A_{11})\), a multiple of the pair \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\). If \(A_{11} = 0\), then \(A_{12} \ne 0\), so \(C_2 = 0\) and \((C_1, C_2) = (C_1/A_{12})\,(A_{12}, 0)\), again a multiple.

In the computations we also need two estimates, shown in figure 13.48. The solid orange curve is \(\cosh t\), the dashed orange one is \(\sinh t\), the grey dashed line is the height \(1\), and the labels beside them say which is which. The first lies above the height \(1\) for \(t > 0\), the second above the horizontal axis \(t\), and the gap between them is \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\), which is not zero. For \(t > 0\), by theorem 9.13 (item 1) \(\mathrm{e}^{2t} > 1\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{t} \ne 1\), so

\[\operatorname{sinh}t = \tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{-t}\big(\mathrm{e}^{2t} - 1\big) > 0 , \qquad \operatorname{cosh}t = 1 + \tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{-t}\big(\mathrm{e}^{t} - 1\big)^2 > 1 . \tag{13.38}\]

Also \(\operatorname{cosh}t > \operatorname{sinh}t\), so the solid curve lies above the dashed one, because \(\operatorname{cosh}t - \operatorname{sinh}t = \mathrm{e}^{-t} > 0\) (theorem 9.13, item 1). The functions \(\operatorname{sinh}\) and \(\operatorname{cosh}\) are increasing on \([0, \infty)\) because their derivatives \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\) are nonnegative (lemma 9.42, items 1 and 3, and theorem 1.84). We write \(\operatorname{sech}t = 1/\operatorname{cosh}t\).

The condition \((A_{11}, A_{12}) \ne (0, 0)\) of lemma 13.34 is not decoration. Take \(A_{11} = A_{12} = 0\) and \(A_{21} = A_{22} = 1\), which is the right case of the figure. The determinant is \(0 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 1 = 0\), but the solutions are all pairs with \(C_1 + C_2 = 0\), that is \(t\,(1, -1)\) with arbitrary \(t\). These are not multiples of \((A_{12}, -A_{11}) = (0, 0)\), because multiples of the zero pair are zero themselves, and the zero pair is only the origin. The first equation is empty here (\(0 = 0\)) and step 1 of the proof fails, since the pair \((A_{12}, -A_{11})\) is not nonzero. This is why we will check, for the cantilever in step 3 of theorem 13.37, that for every \(x > 0\) at least one of the numbers \(A_{11}\), \(A_{12}\) differs from zero.

13.6.3 The simply supported beam

The simplest case is a beam simply supported at both ends. The shape is the same as for the string, the reason is different: the sine is here a solution of the fourth order equation because its second derivative is zero where it is zero itself. Figure 13.49 shows the first three modes. Each row has the grey dashed axis, the triangular supports at the ends and the blue deflection, a sine with \(k\) half waves, and beside the row is the label \(k\). On the right is an orange bar for the frequency, with a length proportional to it.

Theorem 13.35 Modes of a simply supported beam (načini prostoležečega nosilca)

Let a uniform beam be simply supported at both ends. A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) with \(\omega > 0\) is a mode if and only if \(\beta L = k\pi\) with an integer \(k \ge 1\) and \(\varphi\) is a nonzero multiple of the function \(\sin(k\pi x/L)\), the blue curves of the figure. The frequencies are

\[\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \frac{(k\pi)^2}{L^2}\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho A}} , \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \dots \tag{13.39}\]
Proof

We must show that the conditions at \(x = 0\) remove the cosines and the conditions at \(x = L\) leave only the sine. They are the conditions of the supports at the left and the right end.

  1. By theorem 13.31 and lemma 13.33, \(\varphi = C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x + C_3\operatorname{cosh}\beta x + C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta x\), a sum of the four curves.
  2. At the left support the conditions \(\varphi(0) = C_1 + C_3 = 0\) and \(\varphi''(0) = \beta^2(C_3 - C_1) = 0\) give \(C_3 = -C_1\) and \(C_3 = C_1\), so \(C_1 = C_3 = 0\).
  3. The conditions at the right support at \(x = L\) are \(\varphi(L) = C_2\sin\beta L + C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta L = 0\) and \(\varphi''(L) = \beta^2\big({-C_2}\sin\beta L + C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta L\big) = 0\). The sum gives \(2\,\beta^2 C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta L = 0\), and since \(\operatorname{sinh}\beta L > 0\) (equation (13.38)), \(C_4 = 0\).
  4. What remains is \(C_2\sin\beta L = 0\). Since \(\varphi \ne 0\), steps 2 and 3 give \(C_2 \ne 0\), so \(\sin\beta L = 0\), that is, the blue curve has a zero at the right support. By lemma 8.10 (items 3 and 4) the sine is positive on \((0, \pi)\), negative on \((-\pi, 0)\) and periodic with period \(2\pi\), so \(\sin\theta = 0\) only at multiples of \(\pi\). Since \(\beta L > 0\), \(\beta L = k\pi\) with \(k \ge 1\).
  5. Conversely, for \(\beta = k\pi/L\) (the three modes of the figure) the function \(\varphi = \sin\beta x\) satisfies \(\varphi'''' = \beta^4\varphi\) (step 1 of lemma 13.33), \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\), and \(\varphi'' = -\beta^2\varphi\) is zero at both ends. By theorem 13.31 it is a mode.
  6. From \(\beta^4 = \rho A\,\omega^2/EI\) we get \(\omega = \beta^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)}\), which is equation (13.39) and the length of the orange bars.

Source: CK example 13.2, PDF pp. 411 to 413: equation (9) on p. 412, \(\sin\lambda L = 0\), and figure 2 on p. 413. GR table 4.3, PDF p. 289, row bipinned: \(\mu_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi\). The proof by steps is not from the source.

At \(L = 1\) m and the beam of the introduction, \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)} = 57{,}883\) 1/s and \(\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = (k\pi)^2\,\omega_0\). The frequencies \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = \omega_{\mathrm{k}}/(2\pi)\) are \(90{,}92\), \(363{,}69\) and \(818{,}30\) Hz for \(k = 1, 2, 3\). The second is \(4\) times and the third \(9\) times the first (the bars are \(4\) times and \(9\) times as long), because the frequency is proportional to the square of the wave number \(\beta\): a wave half as long gives a frequency four times as high. For the string it was proportional to \(\beta\).

13.6.4 Roots of transcendental equations

For the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends we shall obtain the equations \(\cos x\operatorname{cosh}x = -1\) and \(\cos x\operatorname{cosh}x = 1\) for \(x = \beta L > 0\). There is no closed formula for the roots, CK (p. 414) gives a table and GR (p. 288) an iterative computation. Before computing, we show that the roots exist and place them in intervals. Figure 13.50 shows the idea: we write the equations as \(\cos x = \mp\operatorname{sech}x\). The upper graph is the equation \(\cos x = -\operatorname{sech}x\) of the cantilever, the lower one is the equation \(\cos x = \operatorname{sech}x\) of the beam clamped at both ends. In both, the horizontal axis is the value \(x = \beta L\), marked at multiples of \(\pi/2\), because the zeros and peaks of the cosine are there. The blue curve is \(\cos x\), the upper orange one is \(-\operatorname{sech}x\), the lower orange one is \(\operatorname{sech}x\). Where a blue and an orange curve cross there is a blackwhite dot, a root of the equation. The function \(\operatorname{sech}x\) is smaller than \(0{,}09\) for \(x \ge \pi\), so the cosine meets its graph right beside its own zeros.

The figure shows that in each band where the cosine has the right sign it meets the orange curve twice, once on each side of the middle of the band, in two dots. The lemma proves this. Figure 13.51 enlarges one band for \(n = 1\). The left dot and the right dot are the solutions. At \(y = 0\) there is a gap between the curves, since the cosine is \(1\) and sech is small. In the middle are the blue \(g(s)\) and the orange \(g_+(s)\) for \(s \ge 0\) above the axis \(s\), the differences for the left and the right half. Both decrease and each has one zero. At the bottom, on the axis \(y\), is the case \(n = 0\), where the blue and the orange dashed curve touch at a dot and do not cross.

Lemma 13.36 Roots of the frequency equation of the cantilever (korenine frekvenčne enačbe konzole)

Let \(n \ge 1\) be an integer. The equation \(\cos y = \operatorname{sech}(n\pi + y)\) (the upper band of the figure) has exactly two solutions in the interval \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\), one in \((-\pi/2, 0)\) and one in \((0, \pi/2)\).

Proof

We must show that each half of the interval contains a solution (theorem 2.57, the dots) and that there is only one on it (strictly monotone difference, decreasing curves). At \(y = 0\) there is no solution (the gap in the figure) because \(\cos 0 = 1 > \operatorname{sech}(n\pi)\) by equation (13.38). We use the numerical values \(\operatorname{sech}\pi = 0{,}08627\), \(\sin 0{,}1 = 0{,}09983\) and \(\cos 0{,}1 = 0{,}9950\) (mpmath). The decisive inequality is \(\sin 0{,}1 > \operatorname{sech}\pi\) in step 5. The margin \(0{,}0136\) is more than a thousand times the rounding \(5 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and the bound for \(\operatorname{sech}\pi\) also follows without a computer: from \(\mathrm{e}^\pi > 23\), \(\operatorname{sech}\pi = 2/(\mathrm{e}^\pi + \mathrm{e}^{-\pi}) < 2\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} < 2/23 < 0{,}087\).

  1. Left half: let \(y = -s\) and \(g(s) = \cos s - \operatorname{sech}(n\pi - s)\) on \([0, \pi/2]\), where \(u = n\pi - s \ge n\pi - \pi/2 > 0\). The derivative of \(\operatorname{sech}\) is \(-\operatorname{sinh}/\operatorname{cosh}^2\) (lemma 9.42, item 1, and the quotient rule), so \(g'(s) = -\sin s - \operatorname{sech}u\,\tanh u\) with \(\tanh u = \operatorname{sinh}u/\operatorname{cosh}u > 0\) (equation (13.38)). On \((0, \pi/2]\), \(\sin s > 0\) (lemma 8.10, item 3), so \(g' < 0\) and \(g\) is strictly decreasing by theorem 1.84 (the blue curve).
  2. Values: \(g(0) = 1 - \operatorname{sech}(n\pi) > 0\) and \(g(\pi/2) = -\operatorname{sech}(n\pi - \pi/2) < 0\). By theorem 2.57 there is a zero in \((0, \pi/2)\) (the blue dot), by step 1 only one.
  3. Right half: let \(g_+(s) = \cos s - \operatorname{sech}(n\pi + s)\) on \([0, \pi/2]\). Again \(g_+(0) > 0\) and \(g_+(\pi/2) = -\operatorname{sech}(n\pi + \pi/2) < 0\), so by theorem 2.57 a zero in \((0, \pi/2)\) exists (the orange curve crosses the axis).
  4. On \([0, 0{,}1]\) (the grey segment) there is no zero: the cosine is decreasing there (lemma 8.10, item 3), so \(\cos s \ge \cos 0{,}1 = 0{,}9950\), while \(\operatorname{sech}(n\pi + s) \le \operatorname{sech}\pi = 0{,}08627\) because \(\operatorname{cosh}\) is increasing.
  5. On \([0{,}1, \pi/2)\), \(g_+'(s) = -\sin s + \operatorname{sech}x\,\tanh x\) with \(x = n\pi + s\). The sine is increasing here because its derivative \(\cos > 0\) (theorem 1.84), so \(\sin s \ge \sin 0{,}1 = 0{,}09983\). At the same time \(\operatorname{sech}x\,\tanh x < \operatorname{sech}x \le \operatorname{sech}\pi = 0{,}08627\), because \(\tanh x < 1\) (equation (13.38)) and \(x \ge \pi\). So \(g_+' < 0\), \(g_+\) (the orange curve) is strictly decreasing on \([0{,}1, \pi/2)\) and has at most one zero there. Together with steps 3 and 4 the zero is unique.

13.6.5 The cantilever

The cantilever is clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), as in chapter 5. The end conditions give the frequency equation, and lemma 13.36 locates its roots.

Theorem 13.37 Modes of a cantilever beam (načini konzole)

Let a uniform beam be clamped at \(x = 0\) (the wall) and free at \(x = L\). A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) with \(\omega > 0\) is a mode if and only if \(x = \beta L\) is a positive solution of the equation, the blackwhite dots of the upper graph of figure 13.50,

\[\cos x\,\operatorname{cosh}x + 1 = 0 \tag{13.40}\]

and \(\varphi\) is a nonzero multiple of the function (the blue curves of figure 13.52)

\[\begin{aligned} \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(x) &= \operatorname{cosh}\beta x - \cos\beta x - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\big(\operatorname{sinh}\beta x - \sin\beta x\big) , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac{\operatorname{cosh}x_{\mathrm{r}} + \cos x_{\mathrm{r}}}{\operatorname{sinh}x_{\mathrm{r}} + \sin x_{\mathrm{r}}} , \end{aligned} \tag{13.41}\]

where \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = \beta L\) is that solution. There are infinitely many positive solutions, \(x_1 < x_2 < \dots\). On each interval \(\big((4m - 3)\pi/2,\ (4m - 1)\pi/2\big)\), \(m = 1, 2, \dots\), there are exactly two, one on each side of the point \((2m - 1)\pi\), and there are none elsewhere. The frequencies are \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\omega_0\) with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)}\). The first four roots are \(x_1 = 1{,}8751\), \(x_2 = 4{,}6941\), \(x_3 = 7{,}8548\) and \(x_4 = 10{,}9955\).

Proof

We must show that the end conditions give a system of two equations with a nonzero solution exactly for equation (13.40), and that lemma 13.36 captures all the roots of this equation. The equation is the upper graph of figure 13.50, its roots the blackwhite dots.

  1. By theorem 13.31 and lemma 13.33, \(\varphi = C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x + C_3\operatorname{cosh}\beta x + C_4\operatorname{sinh}\beta x\). The clamp at \(x = 0\) (the wall, the curve leaves it horizontally): \(\varphi(0) = C_1 + C_3 = 0\) and \(\varphi'(0) = \beta(C_2 + C_4) = 0\), so \(C_3 = -C_1\), \(C_4 = -C_2\) and \(\varphi = C_1(\cos\beta x - \operatorname{cosh}\beta x) + C_2(\sin\beta x - \operatorname{sinh}\beta x)\).
  2. The derivatives are \(\varphi'' = -\beta^2\big[C_1(\cos\beta x + \operatorname{cosh}\beta x) + C_2(\sin\beta x + \operatorname{sinh}\beta x)\big]\) and \(\varphi''' = \beta^3\big[C_1(\sin\beta x - \operatorname{sinh}\beta x) - C_2(\cos\beta x + \operatorname{cosh}\beta x)\big]\) (theorem 8.12, lemma 9.42).
  3. The free end (the right end of figure 13.43), \(\varphi''(L) = \varphi'''(L) = 0\), gives with \(c = \cos x\), \(s = \sin x\), \(\mathrm{ch} = \operatorname{cosh}x\), \(\mathrm{sh} = \operatorname{sinh}x\) at \(x = \beta L\) the system
    \[(c + \mathrm{ch})\,C_1 + (s + \mathrm{sh})\,C_2 = 0 , \qquad (s - \mathrm{sh})\,C_1 - (c + \mathrm{ch})\,C_2 = 0 .\]
    This is the system of lemma 13.34 with \(A_{11} = c + \mathrm{ch}\), \(A_{12} = s + \mathrm{sh}\), \(A_{21} = s - \mathrm{sh}\), \(A_{22} = -(c + \mathrm{ch})\). The condition \((A_{11}, A_{12}) \ne (0, 0)\) holds for every \(x > 0\): for \(x \le \pi/2\), \(c \ge 0\) and \(\mathrm{ch} > 1\), so \(A_{11} > 0\), and for \(x > \pi/2\), \(\mathrm{sh} \ge \operatorname{sinh}(\pi/2) = 2{,}3013 > 1 \ge |s|\), so \(A_{12} > 0\).
  4. The determinant is \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21} = -(c + \mathrm{ch})^2 - (s + \mathrm{sh})(s - \mathrm{sh}) = -\big(c^2 + 2c\,\mathrm{ch} + \mathrm{ch}^2 + s^2 - \mathrm{sh}^2\big) = -2\,(1 + c\,\mathrm{ch})\), because \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\) (lemma 8.10) and \(\mathrm{ch}^2 - \mathrm{sh}^2 = \tfrac14\big((\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x})^2 - (\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x})^2\big) = 1\). By lemma 13.34 a nonzero solution exists if and only if equation (13.40) holds.
  5. Positive solutions of equation (13.40): because \(\mathrm{ch} > 0\) we need \(\cos x < 0\), which holds only on the parts of the blue curve below the axis, so \(x\) lies in the interval \(\big((2m - 1)\pi - \pi/2,\ (2m - 1)\pi + \pi/2\big)\) for some \(m \ge 1\) (lemma 8.10, items 3 and 4). We write \(x = n\pi + y\) with \(n = 2m - 1\) and \(|y| < \pi/2\). By corollary 8.15, \(\cos x = \cos n\pi\cos y - \sin n\pi\sin y = -\cos y\), and equation (13.40) becomes \(\cos y = \operatorname{sech}(n\pi + y)\). By lemma 13.36 it has exactly two solutions in each such interval. The intervals follow one another by size, so \(x_{2\mathrm{m}-1}\) is the left and \(x_{2\mathrm{m}}\) the right solution of the \(m\)-th interval, the first and the second blackwhite dot near a zero of the cosine.
  6. At a solution \(x_{\mathrm{r}} > \pi/2\), so \(A_{12} = s + \mathrm{sh} > 0\) (step 3). By lemma 13.34, \((C_1, C_2)\) is a multiple of the pair \((s + \mathrm{sh},\ -(c + \mathrm{ch}))\). Substituting into step 1 and dividing by \(-(s + \mathrm{sh})\) gives \(\varphi \propto \operatorname{cosh}\beta x - \cos\beta x - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}(\operatorname{sinh}\beta x - \sin\beta x)\), which is equation (13.41), the blue curve of the mode. From \(\beta^4 = \rho A\,\omega^2/EI\), \(\omega = \beta^2\sqrt{EI/(\rho A)} = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\omega_0\). Modes with \(\omega^2 \le 0\) do not exist by lemma 13.32.
  7. Numerical values (the blackwhite dots of the upper graph): the function \(f(x) = \cos x\operatorname{cosh}x + 1\) changes sign on short intervals: \(f(1{,}8751) > 0 > f(1{,}8752)\), \(f(4{,}6940) < 0 < f(4{,}6941)\), \(f(7{,}8547) > 0 > f(7{,}8548)\) and \(f(10{,}9955) < 0 < f(10{,}9956)\) (mpmath). By theorem 2.57 each of these intervals contains a root, and by step 5 it is the only root in its half of the interval \((\pi/2, 3\pi/2)\) or \((5\pi/2, 7\pi/2)\).

Source: CK example 13.3, PDF pp. 413 to 415: the frequency equation (6) and the values (7) on p. 414 (\(\lambda_1 L = 1{,}8751\), \(\lambda_2 L = 4{,}6941\), \(\lambda_3 L = 7{,}8548\), \(\lambda_4 L = 10{,}996\)), the shape (14) on p. 415. Example 13.38 Frequencies of the 20 × 40 mm cantilever (frekvence konzole 20 × 40 mm)

The cantilever of the introduction: \(L = 1\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(\omega_0 = 57{,}883\) 1/s. The coefficients are the orange ticks of figure 13.55. By theorem 13.37 the coefficients \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}/\omega_0 = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) are \(3{,}51602\), \(22{,}03449\), \(61{,}69721\) and \(120{,}9019\), and the frequencies \(f_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}/(2\pi)\) are \(32{,}39\), \(202{,}99\), \(568{,}38\) and \(1113{,}79\) Hz (the spacing of the orange ticks on the line). The second is \(6{,}27\) times and the third \(17{,}5\) times the first. For the bar with a free end the ratio is \(1 : 3 : 5\), here it is about \(1 : 6{,}3 : 17{,}5\). The reason is that \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is proportional to \(x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). At higher modes \(x_{\mathrm{r}}\) is close to \((2r - 1)\pi/2\), so there \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is approximately proportional to \((2r - 1)^2\) and not to \((2r - 1)\) as for the bar: \(\omega_3/\omega_2 = 2{,}800\) against \(25/9 = 2{,}778\). The first mode is the exception, because \(x_1 = 1{,}8751\) lies \(19\) % above \(\pi/2\) (the difference \(\delta_1 = 0{,}304\) below), so the ratio is \(1 : 6{,}3 : 17{,}5\) and not \(1 : 9 : 25\).

The difference from the nearest zero of the cosine disappears quickly: at higher roots the blackwhite dots lie almost on the zeros of the blue curve. From the equation \(\cos x_{\mathrm{r}} = -\operatorname{sech}x_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi/2 + \delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) it follows that \(|\sin\delta_{\mathrm{r}}| = \operatorname{sech}x_{\mathrm{r}}\), which gives \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = +0{,}304\), \(-0{,}0183\), \(+7{,}8 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(-3{,}4 \cdot 10^{-5}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, 4\). From the second step on, the difference is each time about \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}043\) times smaller, because \(\operatorname{sech}x \approx 2\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{r}}\) grows by \(\pi\). So CK's \(x_4 = 10{,}996\) already equals \(7\pi/2 = 10{,}9956\) to three decimals.

Why is the differential equation alone not enough? Figure 13.53 shows the hatched wall on the left and the blue sine \(\varphi(x) = \sin(\pi x/L)\) with the undeformed axis and the free end on the right. The clamp requires the curve to leave the wall horizontally (grey line), but the sine has an orange tangent with a slope. Take the same function with \(L = 1\) m, a mode of the simply supported beam with \(\beta = \pi/L\). It solves the equation \(\varphi'''' = \beta^4\varphi\) and satisfies two of the four conditions of the cantilever, \(\varphi(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi''(L) = 0\). It does not satisfy the other two. The slope at the clamp is \(\varphi'(0) = \pi/L = 3{,}1416\) 1/m and not zero, the shear force at the free end is proportional to \(\varphi'''(L) = -(\pi/L)^3\cos\pi = +31{,}006\) 1/m³ and not zero. The equation alone also accepts the sine, and a function becomes a mode of the cantilever only when it satisfies all four end conditions at once. These conditions select \(\beta L\) and the ratio \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\).

13.6.6 The beam clamped at both ends

The beam clamped at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\) has at the right end the conditions \(\varphi = \varphi' = 0\) instead of a free end. Figure 13.54 shows the first three modes. The right half of the figure shows this function on an axis with the labels \(1\) and \(\pi\): the orange curve starts at the height \(1\) and on \((0, \pi]\) falls below it.

Theorem 13.39 Modes of a beam clamped at both ends (načini obojestransko vpetega nosilca)

Let a uniform beam be clamped at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\). A pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) with \(\omega > 0\) is a mode if and only if \(x = \beta L\) is a positive solution of the equation

\[\cos x\,\operatorname{cosh}x - 1 = 0 \tag{13.42}\]

and \(\varphi\) is a nonzero multiple of the function (the blue curves of the figure) \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(x) = \operatorname{cosh}\beta x - \cos\beta x - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\big(\operatorname{sinh}\beta x - \sin\beta x\big)\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}} = (\operatorname{cosh}x_{\mathrm{r}} - \cos x_{\mathrm{r}})/(\operatorname{sinh}x_{\mathrm{r}} - \sin x_{\mathrm{r}})\), where \(x_{\mathrm{r}} = \beta L\) is that solution. There is no solution on \((0, \pi]\), because the orange curve lies there below the height \(1\). On each interval \(\big((4m - 1)\pi/2,\ (4m + 1)\pi/2\big)\), \(m = 1, 2, \dots\), there are exactly two, one on each side of the point \(2m\pi\), and none elsewhere. The frequencies are \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = x_{\mathrm{r}}^2\,\omega_0\). The first three roots are \(x_1 = 4{,}7300\), \(x_2 = 7{,}8532\) and \(x_3 = 10{,}9956\).

Proof

We must show that the conditions at \(x = L\) (the right wall) give a system with determinant \(2 - 2\cos x\operatorname{cosh}x\), that there are no roots on \((0, \pi]\), and that for \(x > \pi\) the same lemma holds as for the cantilever.

  1. The conditions at \(x = 0\) (the left wall) are as for the cantilever (theorem 13.37, step 1): \(\varphi = C_1(\cos\beta x - \operatorname{cosh}\beta x) + C_2(\sin\beta x - \operatorname{sinh}\beta x)\), with derivative \(\varphi' = \beta\big[-C_1(\sin\beta x + \operatorname{sinh}\beta x) + C_2(\cos\beta x - \operatorname{cosh}\beta x)\big]\).
  2. The conditions \(\varphi(L) = \varphi'(L) = 0\) (the right wall, which the deflection reaches horizontally) give, with the notation of step 3 of theorem 13.37, the system \((c - \mathrm{ch})\,C_1 + (s - \mathrm{sh})\,C_2 = 0\), \(-(s + \mathrm{sh})\,C_1 + (c - \mathrm{ch})\,C_2 = 0\). This is the system of lemma 13.34 with \(A_{11} = c - \mathrm{ch}\), \(A_{12} = s - \mathrm{sh}\), \(A_{21} = -(s + \mathrm{sh})\), \(A_{22} = c - \mathrm{ch}\). For \(x > 0\), \(\mathrm{ch} > 1 \ge c\) (equation (13.38)), so \(A_{11} < 0\) and \((A_{11}, A_{12}) \ne (0, 0)\).
  3. The determinant is \(A_{11}A_{22} - A_{12}A_{21} = (c - \mathrm{ch})^2 + (s - \mathrm{sh})(s + \mathrm{sh}) = c^2 - 2c\,\mathrm{ch} + \mathrm{ch}^2 + s^2 - \mathrm{sh}^2 = 2 - 2\,c\,\mathrm{ch}\) (as in step 4 of theorem 13.37). By lemma 13.34 a nonzero solution exists if and only if equation (13.42) holds (the lower graph, the dots), and \((C_1, C_2)\) is a multiple of the pair \((s - \mathrm{sh},\ -(c - \mathrm{ch}))\). For \(x > \pi/2\), \(\mathrm{sh} > 1 \ge |s|\), so \(s - \mathrm{sh} \ne 0\). Substitution into step 1 and division by \(-(s - \mathrm{sh})\) gives the shape \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the theorem.
  4. On \((0, \pi]\) the equation has no solution. Let \(g(x) = \operatorname{sinh}x\cos x - \operatorname{cosh}x\sin x\). Then \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(x) = -2\operatorname{sinh}x\sin x < 0\) on \((0, \pi)\) (product rule, theorem 8.12, lemma 9.42, lemma 8.10), so \(g < 0\) on \((0, \pi]\) by theorem 1.84. The derivative of \(f_1(x) = \cos x\operatorname{cosh}x\) is \(f_1' = -\sin x\operatorname{cosh}x + \cos x\operatorname{sinh}x = g\), so \(f_1\) (the orange curve) is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) with \(f_1(0) = 1\), hence \(f_1(x) < 1\) for \(0 < x \le \pi\), below the height \(1\).
  5. For \(x > \pi\) the equation \(\cos x\operatorname{cosh}x = 1 > 0\) requires \(\cos x > 0\), so \(x \in (2m\pi - \pi/2,\ 2m\pi + \pi/2)\) for some \(m \ge 1\) (the interval for \(m = 0\) is \((0, \pi/2)\), which step 4 excludes). We write \(x = 2m\pi + y\) with \(|y| < \pi/2\). By corollary 8.15, \(\cos x = \cos y\) and the equation is \(\cos y = \operatorname{sech}(2m\pi + y)\). Lemma 13.36 with \(n = 2m\) gives exactly two solutions on each such interval, the two dots of the lower graph beside each peak of the cosine.
  6. Numerically (the dots of the lower graph): \(f(x) = \cos x\operatorname{cosh}x - 1\) has \(f(4{,}7300) < 0 < f(4{,}7301)\), \(f(7{,}8532) > 0 > f(7{,}8533)\) and \(f(10{,}9956) < 0 < f(10{,}9957)\) (mpmath). By theorem 2.57 each of these intervals contains a root, and by step 5 there is no more than one in its half.
