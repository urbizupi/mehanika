Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.14 Frames (okvirji)

Until now the axis of a beam was a single straight line. The structures we meet in buildings, machines and vehicles, however, often consist of several beams, welded or bolted together so that under load they cannot turn with respect to each other. How does such a structure carry a load, and where does it move? Every part is a beam from the earlier sections, only the joint is new. The section therefore revolves around the joint. First we say what the joint demands of forces and moments (section 5.14.1), then we obtain displacements from the unit load (section 5.14.2). Next we return to the two bars of the chapter on energy principles and weld them together (section 5.14.3), look at what happens when there are more supports than equilibrium requires (section 5.14.4), and finally say what is added when the structure no longer lies in a plane (section 5.14.5).

5.14.1 Frame and rigid joints (okvir in toga vozlišča)

In a truss (definition 4.4) the pin does not prevent the bars from turning about it. If two bars that were at a right angle are loaded, the angle between them changes. What if we weld the bars at the contact? Then the angle between them cannot change without the bars bending. Each bar therefore carries not only an axial force but also a shear force and a moment, and in the joint all three quantities must be transmitted. The situation is shown in figure 5.77.

What of what we have built so far remains valid, and what must we add? We treat each part of such a structure as a beam by the Euler-Bernoulli theory, that is with an axial force \(N\), a shear force \(V\) and a moment \(M\) by definitions 5.7 and 5.8. What is new is that each member gets its own coordinate system, and that we must say how the forces and displacements of the members are connected in the joint. Before the definition let us state the model.

  1. The structure and its loads lie in one plane, the plane \(xy\), which is the plane of symmetry of the cross sections of all members (assumption 2 of section 5.1).
  2. The members are straight beams (definition 5.1). The joint has negligible size, and the length of a member is counted from the intersection of axes to the intersection of axes. A real weld has some size and is slightly flexible, which the model neglects.
  3. The joint is a rigid connection. All members meeting in the joint have the same displacement at their end at the joint and turn by the same angle as the joint. The joint can transmit forces and moments, like a plate that does not deform.
  4. Displacements and rotations are small, equilibrium is written on the shape before loading (assumption 3 of section 5.2).
  5. Each member is linearly elastic, has a constant cross section and a length much greater than the height of the cross section. We neglect the shear deformation. For the members of our structure, 1 m long and 40 mm high, the shear part of the displacement is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending part by section 5.13.

The members of the structure have different directions, so we cannot use a single axis \(x\). Each member therefore gets a local axis (lokalna os). The axis \(x\) of the member lies along its axis and points from one end to the other, we write the coordinate along it \(s\), so as not to confuse it with the axis \(x\) of the whole structure. The local axis \(y\) is the axis \(x\) turned in the plane of the structure by a right angle in the counterclockwise direction, and the axis \(z\) points toward the viewer. We call the end of the member at \(s = 0\) the start and at \(s = L\) the end. The internal forces \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) of the member are then the internal forces from the definitions of section 5.2.1 in these local axes: the force and moment with which the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(s\).

Definition 5.90 Frame (okvir)

A plane frame (ravninski okvir) is a model of a structure made of straight beams (definition 5.1) that lie together with the loads in the plane \(xy\) and are connected in rigid joints (togo vozlišče). Each beam, which we call a member of the frame, has local axes \(s\), \(y\), \(z\) as stated above, and a length \(L\) from the intersection of axes at the start to the intersection of axes at the end. One or more members meet in a rigid joint. The displacement of a joint is a vector \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\), the rotation of a joint is a number \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\), which is an angle in radians and therefore without unit (the slope \(v'\) has the same meaning). For all members meeting in the joint \(J\), the displacement and rotation of their end at \(J\) equal \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\). External loads act in joints and on members.

Source: ONATE1 §1.9.2 (PDF pp. 52-56), EP example 3.2.6 (pp. 122-123) for a frame of two members.

What does equilibrium say about the forces in a joint? Let us take the joint as a small piece of the frame around the intersection of axes and treat it as a body in equilibrium. We know the forces on it from the definition of the internal force. If the joint is at the start of a member, the piece is on the side of smaller \(s\), so the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on it with the internal force \(\vek F(0)\). If the joint is at the end of a member, the piece is on the side of larger \(s\), so the part of the member on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on it. By Newton's law of action and reaction this force is opposite to the internal force, that is \(-\vek F(L)\).

Lemma 5.91 Equilibrium of a rigid joint (ravnovesje togega vozlišča)

Let \(J\) be a joint of a plane frame (definition 5.90) in equilibrium. Let an external force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) and an external moment (\(z\) component) \(M_{\mathrm{J}}\) also act on the joint, which may also be zero. For each member \(k\) at the joint let \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) be its internal force in the global components \(x\), \(y\) and \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) its internal moment, namely at that end of the member which is at \(J\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the start}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the end}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\quad + \vek F_{\mathrm{J}} = \vek 0 , \\ &\sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the start}} M_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the end}} M_{\mathrm{k}} + M_{\mathrm{J}} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.81}\]
Proof

We must show that the sum of forces and the sum of moments on the piece of the frame around \(J\) equal zero. We know the forces on the piece, so it suffices to add them.

  1. We cut out a piece of the frame around \(J\) with radius \(\varepsilon\). It is in equilibrium, so by equations (2.2) and (2.35) the sum of forces on it is zero and the sum of moments about \(J\) is zero.
  2. On the cross section of a member that has \(J\) at its start, the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on the piece with the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\), by the definition of the internal force. On the cross section of a member that has \(J\) at its end, the part of the member on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on the piece with the force \(-\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the moment \(-M_{\mathrm{k}}\), by Newton's law of action and reaction. The forces and moments are written in the local axes of the member. The global components of a force are obtained from the local ones with the rotation matrix (lemma 2.38, point 2). The moment (\(z\) component) does not change under a rotation about the axis \(z\).
  3. The sum of forces is \(\sum_{\text{start}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\text{end}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{J}} = \vek 0\), which is the first equation.
  4. The moment of the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the cross section about \(J\) has magnitude at most \(\varepsilon\,|\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}|\), because the cross section is at most \(\varepsilon\) away from \(J\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) this moment tends to zero, because the internal forces are finite, and so does the moment of the external loads on the piece. What remains are the moments \(\pm M_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{J}}\), and their sum is zero. This is the second equation.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The equilibrium of moments at the ends of a member of a rigid frame is written in ONATE1 (1.36) (PDF p. 56).

The lemma says two things, which we remember with two examples. At a joint without external load in which two members meet, one having it at the start and the other at the end, \(\vek F_1 = \vek F_2\) and \(M_1 = M_2\). The internal force and moment are passed from one member to the other, only written in different local axes. The axis turns, so the axial force of one member can appear in the other as a shear force. If the joints are hinges, that is pins, \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for all members and the first equation remains, the equilibrium of a pin from the chapter on trusses.

Let us test the lemma on an L-shaped frame (figure 5.78). The column \(AB\) is vertical, of length \(a = 1\) m, and clamped at \(A = (0, 0)\) (clamp, definition 5.5). The crossbeam \(BC\) is horizontal, of length \(b = 1\) m, and rigidly attached at \(B = (0, a)\) to the top of the column. At the free end \(C = (b, a)\) acts a force \(F = 500\) N downward, \(\vek F = (0, -F)\). Both members are made of the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm as in section 5.1.3, standing on its edge, so \(A = 800\ \text{mm}^2\), \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\), \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^8\) N. The crossbeam is exactly the cantilever of section 5.1.3, only clamped into the column instead of the wall.

The local axes of the column are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) (upward) and the local axis \(y\) is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (to the left), turned by a right angle in the counterclockwise direction. For the crossbeam the local axes equal the global ones. We obtain the internal force and moment from the equilibrium of the part that lies on the side of larger \(s\). The load and the force of the part on the side of smaller \(s\) act on this part. This part is in equilibrium, so the force with which the part on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on it is opposite to the resultant of the load. By Newton's law of action and reaction the internal force, that is the force in the opposite direction, equals the resultant of the load on the part on the side of larger \(s\). The same holds for the moment: the internal moment is the moment of the load on this part about the center of the cross section.

  1. The crossbeam \(BC\). For the cross section at \(s\) (\(0 \le s \le b\)) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is the piece of the crossbeam between the cross section and \(C\) with the force \((0, -F)\) at \(C\). The resultant is \((0, -F)\), so \(N = 0\) and \(V = -F = -500\) N. The moment of the force about the center of the cross section is the \(z\) component of the cross product \(\vek r\times\vek F\) for \(\vek r = (b - s, 0)\), that is \((b-s)\cdot(-F) - 0 = -F\,(b - s)\). We obtain \(M(s) = -F\,(b - s)\), at \(s = 0\) we have \(M = -500\) N m. This is the same result as for the cantilever.
  2. The column \(AB\). For the cross section at \(s\) (\(0 \le s \le a\)) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is the piece of the column above the cross section together with the crossbeam, and the load \((0, -F)\) at \(C\) acts on it. The resultant in local components: \(N = (0, -F)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = -F = -500\) N, \(V = (0, -F)\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = 0\). The vector from the center of the cross section \((0, s)\) to \(C\) is \(\vek r = (b, a - s)\), so the moment is \(b\cdot(-F) - (a - s)\cdot 0 = -F\,b\). So \(M = -F\,b = -500\) N m along the whole length of the column.
  3. Let us test lemma 5.91 at \(B\). The column has \(B\) at its end and the crossbeam at its start. By equation (5.81) (without an external load at \(B\)) we must have \(\vek F_{\mathrm{BC}}(0) = \vek F_{\mathrm{AB}}(a)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{BC}}(0) = M_{\mathrm{AB}}(a)\). The first is the force of the crossbeam in global components, \(V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, -500)\) N, the second is the force of the column, \(N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, -500)\) N. The moments are \(-500\) N m and \(-500\) N m. The equality holds.

The transfer shows what happens in the joint. The shear force of the crossbeam (\(-500\) N) becomes the axial force of the column (\(-500\) N), because the column is turned by a right angle. The moment stays. Therefore the column has a compressive stress \(500\ \text{N}/800\ \text{mm}^2 = 0{,}625\) MPa, and the moment is \(500\) N m along its whole length. The bending stress is thus everywhere in the column the same as at the clamp of the cantilever, \(93{,}75\) MPa, so 150 times greater than the compressive stress. All three quantities are drawn in figure 5.79. The moment is constant in the column, because there \(V = 0\) and by corollary 5.11 \(M' = -V = 0\). In the crossbeam \(M' = -V = F\), so the moment grows from \(-500\) N m at the joint to zero at \(C\).

5.14.2 Displacements of a frame (pomiki okvirja)

Where does the end \(C\) of the frame move? For a truss the way led through the geometry given by the condition that the bars meet at the end. For a frame we would have to integrate the deflection curve over each member and equate displacements and rotations at the joints. A shorter way is the unit load. Theorem 5.78 on the unit load for a beam holds for each member separately. The question is whether the boundary terms in the joints cancel in the sum.

Corollary 5.92 Unit load method for a frame (enotska sila za okvir)

Let a plane frame (definition 5.90) of members \(k = 1, \dots, m\) with lengths \(L_{\mathrm{k}}\) and stiffnesses \(EA_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(EI_{\mathrm{k}}\) be linearly elastic, with supports (definition 5.5) at which the displacement or rotation that the support prevents is zero, in equilibrium with the given load, to which belong internal forces \(N_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) in local axes. Let the point \(P\) be a joint or a free end of a member (a member that has \(P\) in its interior is divided into two at \(P\)). Let another system of forces be in equilibrium with a unit force at \(P\) in the direction \(\vek d\) (or with a unit moment), possible reactions of the supports, and no other loads, with internal forces \(n_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) by the same definitions. Then the component of the displacement of the point \(P\) in the direction \(\vek d\) (or the rotation at \(P\)) equals

\[\Delta = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \int_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}} \left( \frac{n_{\mathrm{k}}\,N_{\mathrm{k}}}{EA_{\mathrm{k}}} + \frac{m_{\mathrm{k}}\,M_{\mathrm{k}}}{EI_{\mathrm{k}}} \right) \mathrm{d}s . \tag{5.82}\]
Proof

We must show that the sum of the virtual force identities over the members equals the displacement \(\Delta\), because the boundary terms in the joints combine into the work of the external forces of the unit system. The unit system is the virtual system of forces, and the actual displacements are the displacements of the members. Since \(P\) is a joint or an end, there is no point force in the interior of any member, and in theorem 5.77 the sum over the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the integrals of loads drop out.

  1. For member \(k\), by theorem 5.77 on the principle of virtual forces in the form with boundary terms, because \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) are in equilibrium without a load on the member (\(n_{\mathrm{k}}' = 0\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}' = 0\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}' = -\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\)): \(\int_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}} (n_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}' + m_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'')\,\mathrm{d}s = \big[\,n_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}} + \bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}} + m_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'\,\big]_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}}\). The actual displacements of the member are smooth, because they are the solution of a beam (theorem 5.35). By the relations \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) the left side is \(\int (n_{\mathrm{k}} N_{\mathrm{k}}/(EA_{\mathrm{k}}) + m_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}}/(EI_{\mathrm{k}}))\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. We sum over the members. The left side is the right side of equation (5.82). The right side is the sum of the boundary terms. We shall collect them by joints.
  3. At the joint \(J\) the displacement of the end of every member at \(J\) equals \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\) and the rotation \(v_{\mathrm{k}}'\) equals \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) (rigid joint in a frame, definition 5.90). The boundary term \(n_{\mathrm{k}} u_{\mathrm{k}} + \bar V_{\mathrm{k}} v_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the scalar product of the force \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the displacement, both in local axes, and the scalar product has the same value in every orthogonal Cartesian system (proof of lemma 2.38, point 2), so we may compute it in global axes. Therefore the sum of the boundary terms at \(J\) is \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\cdot\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{end}} \bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{start}} \bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) + \varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{end}} m_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{start}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\Big)\), because at the upper limit of the integral the sign is plus, at the lower one minus.
  4. By lemma 5.91 for the unit system the two brackets equal the external force \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{J}}\) and the external moment \(\bar M_{\mathrm{J}}\) of the unit system at \(J\). At the joint with the unit force this is the unit force, at a joint with a support the reaction, elsewhere zero. A reaction acts only in the direction of the displacement or rotation that the support prevents, and that is zero under the actual load, so the reaction does no work. What remains is the term of the unit force at \(P\), \(\vek d\cdot(u_{\mathrm{P}}, v_{\mathrm{P}}) = \Delta\) (for a unit moment \(\varphi_{\mathrm{P}}\)).

Source: EP (4.4.12) (p. 198), example 4.4.4 (pp. 202-203) for a frame, where only the moments are taken into account. The proof for a frame is not from a source.

The corollary says that for the computation of displacements a frame needs nothing new: for each member we compute \(N\), \(M\) and \(n\), \(m\) in its local axes and add the integrals.

Let us use it on the L-frame (figure 5.78), with \(F = 500\) N, \(a = b = 1\) m and \(EI\), \(EA\) as above. We are interested in the vertical displacement \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) and the horizontal displacement \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) of the end \(C\).

  1. The vertical displacement. A unit force \(1\) N upward at \(C\) gives, by the same procedure as above: in the crossbeam \(n = 0\), \(m = +(b - s)\), in the column \(n = +1\), \(m = +b\) (the signs are opposite to those of the load, because the force points upward). For the load \(N = -F\) in the column and \(M = -F(b-s)\) in the crossbeam, \(M = -F\,b\) in the column. Equation (5.82) gives
    \[\begin{aligned} v_{\mathrm{C}} &= \underbrace{\int_0^b \frac{-F\,(b-s)\,(b-s)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{crossbeam}} + \underbrace{\int_0^a \frac{-F\,b\cdot b}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{column, bending}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\int_0^a \frac{(-F)\cdot 1}{EA}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{column, axial force}} \\ &= -\frac{F\,b^3}{3EI} - \frac{F\,b^2\,a}{EI} - \frac{F\,a}{EA} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.83}\]
  2. The numbers. The crossbeam alone gives \(F b^3/(3EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, as for the cantilever. The bending of the column gives \(F b^2 a/EI = 23{,}916\) mm, and the axial force of the column \(F a/EA = 0{,}0032\) mm. Together \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -31{,}89\) mm, this displacement is downward. The axial term is \(10^{-4}\) of the bending one, so we drop the axial terms in frames. EP does the same in example 4.4.4 (pp. 202-203).
  3. The horizontal displacement. A unit force \(1\) N in the direction \(+x\) at \(C\) gives in the crossbeam \(n = 1\), \(m = 0\) (its line of action runs along the axis of the crossbeam), in the column \(n = 0\) and \(m = -(a - s)\), because \(\vek r = (b, a - s)\) and \(\vek F = (1, 0)\), so the moment is \(-(a-s)\). Under the load in the column \(M = -F\,b\), in the crossbeam \(N = 0\). Therefore
    \[u_{\mathrm{C}} = \int_0^a \frac{(-F\,b)\,\big(-(a - s)\big)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{F\,b\,a^2}{2EI} = +11{,}96\ \text{mm} . \tag{5.84}\]
    The end moves to the right, because the moment \(M = -F b\) bends the column so that its top leans to the right (the top of the column turns clockwise).
  4. The rotation of the joint \(B\). A unit moment at \(B\) gives in the column \(m = 1\), in the crossbeam zero, so \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = \int_0^a (-F\,b)/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = -F\,b\,a/EI = -0{,}02392\) (radians, clockwise).

The vertical displacement is, up to the axial term, exactly 4 times the displacement of the cantilever of section 5.1.3 (\(7{,}972\) mm), although the crossbeam is exactly that cantilever. Why exactly four times? The crossbeam alone contributes \(F b^3/3EI = 7{,}972\) mm, because its moment and the unit moment decrease from the joint to zero, and their product integrates to \(\int_0^b (b-s)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = b^3/3\). The column, which has a constant moment and a constant unit moment, gives \(b^2 a\) instead of a third, so at \(a = b\) three times as much. We obtain the same number from the rotation of the joint: the crossbeam turns together with the joint as a rigid body by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\), so its end descends by a further \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\,b = F b^2 a/EI = 23{,}92\) mm. Together \(1 + 3 = 4\). The horizontal displacement is exactly half of the displacement that a straight column turned by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) about the clamp would give, \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\,a/2 = 11{,}96\) mm. Under a constant moment the column bends into a circular arc, in which the transverse displacement of the end is half the product of the rotation and the length, as for a cantilever with a moment at the end (\(M a^2/2EI\) against \(M a/EI\) for the rotation). The deformed shape is shown in figure 5.81, the two unit systems in figure 5.80.

5.14.3 A truss with welded joints (paličje z zvarjenimi vozlišči)

In the chapter on energy principles we computed the truss of two bars with pins. We then said that real joints of bars also transmit some bending (FELIPPA §2.3, PDF p. 30, where the bars of real trusses are joined with gusset plates, so they carry some bending), and promised to show how to compute it. Let us look at the same truss from the chapter 4 with welded joints. The bars \(AC\) (\(4\) m) and \(BC\) (\(5\) m) are of steel, \(E = 196\) GPa, with a square cross section \(10 \times 10\) mm, so \(A = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\) as before and \(I = 833{,}3\ \text{mm}^4\), \(EI = 163{,}3\) N m\(^2\). The bars are clamped into the wall at \(A\) and \(B\) (clamp, definition 5.5, in the truss with pins they were attached there with pins), and rigidly welded at \(C\), where a load of \(10\) kN acts downward. What is new? At \(C\) there is, besides two displacements, a rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}\), so there are three unknowns. The bar \(AC\) has the local axis \(s\) from \(A\) toward \(C\), the bar \(BC\) from \(B\) toward \(C\). In both \(C\) is the end.

For the computation we need the relation between the displacement of the end \(C\) of a member and the forces on it. This is the relation we get from the flexibility matrix of a cantilever (section 5.9.5), inverted.

Lemma 5.93 End forces of a clamped beam (sile na koncu vpetega nosilca)

Let a straight beam of length \(L\) and stiffnesses \(EA\), \(EI\), without load along its length, be clamped (clamp, definition 5.5) at \(s = 0\), and let its end \(s = L\) move by \(u_{\mathrm{L}}\) in the direction of the axis and by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}\) transversely and turn by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}} = v'(L)\) (small displacements). Then the internal forces at \(s = L\) are

\[N = \frac{EA}{L}\,u_{\mathrm{L}} , \qquad \begin{pmatrix} V \\ M \end{pmatrix} = EI \begin{pmatrix} 12/L^3 & -6/L^2 \\ -6/L^2 & 4/L \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_{\mathrm{L}} \\ \varphi_{\mathrm{L}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{5.85}\]
Proof

We must show that the matrix in equation (5.85) inverts the flexibility matrix of the cantilever, and that the axial force is obtained separately. Let a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple \(M_0\) act at the end, so by the definitions of the internal force \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M(L) = M_0\).

  1. By section 5.9.5 the displacement of the end of a cantilever is \(v_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} L^3/(3EI) + M_0 L^2/(2EI)\) and the rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} L^2/(2EI) + M_0 L/EI\). In matrix form: \((v_{\mathrm{L}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{L}})^\top = \mat D\,(F_{\mathrm{y}}, M_0)^\top\) with \(\mat D = \frac{1}{EI}\begin{pmatrix} L^3/3 & L^2/2 \\ L^2/2 & L\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. The determinant of the matrix \(\mat D\) is \(\frac{1}{(EI)^2}\big(L^4/3 - L^4/4\big) = \frac{L^4}{12\,(EI)^2}\). It is not zero, so the inverse matrix exists (definition 1.107).
  3. The inverse of a \(2\times 2\) matrix is obtained by swapping the diagonal elements, changing the sign of the off-diagonal ones and dividing everything by the determinant: \(\mat D^{-1} = EI\,\frac{12}{L^4}\begin{pmatrix} L & -L^2/2 \\ -L^2/2 & L^3/3\end{pmatrix} = EI\begin{pmatrix} 12/L^3 & -6/L^2 \\ -6/L^2 & 4/L\end{pmatrix}\). This is the equation for \((V, M)\).
  4. The axial force. Without an axial load \(N' = 0\) (corollary 5.11), so \(N\) is constant and \(u' = N/EA\) is constant. Because of \(u(0) = 0\) we have \(u(L) = N L/EA\), so \(N = EA\,u_{\mathrm{L}}/L\).

Source: EP example 4.2.3 (p. 176), ONATE1 (1.35) and (1.36) (PDF pp. 55-56), COOK §2.3 (PDF pp. 38-42). The proof is not from the sources.

The coefficients \(12EI/L^3\), \(6EI/L^2\) and \(4EI/L\) are stiffnesses: the force that the end demands for a unit of displacement, and the moment that it demands for a unit of rotation. For our bars: \(12EI/L^3 = 30{,}6\) N/m, \(6EI/L^2 = 61{,}3\) N, \(4EI/L = 163{,}3\) N m, \(EA/L = 4{,}9\cdot 10^6\) N/m for \(AC\) (\(L = 4\) m), and \(15{,}7\) N/m, \(39{,}2\) N, \(130{,}7\) N m, \(3{,}92\cdot 10^6\) N/m for \(BC\) (\(L = 5\) m). The axial stiffnesses are a million times greater than the transverse ones. This is the reason for the result that follows.

For each bar we must write the displacement \((u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}})\) of the joint in local axes. The local axis \(s\) has the direction \((1, 0)\) for \(AC\) and \((0{,}8, -0{,}6)\) for \(BC\), because the vector from \(B = (-4, 3)\) m to \(C = (0, 0)\) equals \((4, -3)\) m. The unit vector of the axis is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = (c_{\mathrm{x}}, c_{\mathrm{y}})\) (the components of the vector of the bar divided by its length). The local displacement along the axis \(s\) is then \(u_{\mathrm{s}} = c_{\mathrm{x}}\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}\,v_{\mathrm{C}}\), and the transverse one \(v_{\mathrm{L}} = -c_{\mathrm{y}}\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}\,v_{\mathrm{C}}\) (lemma 2.38, the local axis \(y\) is \((-c_{\mathrm{y}}, c_{\mathrm{x}})\)).

The equations of the joint \(C\) we get from lemma 5.91. Both bars have \(C\) at their end, so the sum of the internal forces at the ends equals the external force, \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{C}} = (0, -10\,000)\) N, and the sum of the moments equals the external moment, \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\). The global components of the force of a member are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = N_{\mathrm{k}}\,(c_{\mathrm{x}}, c_{\mathrm{y}}) + V_{\mathrm{k}}\,(-c_{\mathrm{y}}, c_{\mathrm{x}})\). When we insert the force from lemma 5.93, we obtain three linear equations for \((u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{C}})\), in matrix form

\[\begin{pmatrix} 7{,}409\cdot 10^6 & -1{,}882\cdot 10^6 & -23{,}52 \\ -1{,}882\cdot 10^6 & 1{,}411\cdot 10^6 & -92{,}61 \\ -23{,}52 & -92{,}61 & 294{,}0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathrm{C}} \\ v_{\mathrm{C}} \\ \varphi_{\mathrm{C}} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -10\,000 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{5.86}\]

in units N/m, N, N m for rows and columns in turn. The matrix is symmetric, because the matrix \(\mat D\) from the proof is symmetric (reciprocity, theorem 4.140), and so is its inverse, and because a rotation of axes does not spoil the symmetry. Its upper left \(2\times 2\) part is, up to corrections that are five to six orders of magnitude smaller, the stiffness matrix of the truss with pins. The solution (SymPy):

\[u_{\mathrm{C}} = -2{,}721\ \text{mm} , \quad v_{\mathrm{C}} = -10{,}714\ \text{mm} , \quad \varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = -3{,}593\cdot 10^{-3} .\]

The truss with pins from the chapter 4 gives \(-2{,}7211\) mm and \(-10{,}7143\) mm. The displacements differ by \(0{,}0013\) %. The axial forces are \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}333\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = +16{,}666\) kN, and they differ from the values of the truss (\(-13{,}333\) kN and \(16{,}667\) kN) by less than \(0{,}3\) N. What is new is the bending. The internal moment at \(C\) is \(M_{\mathrm{AC}}(L) = +0{,}069\) N m and \(M_{\mathrm{BC}}(L) = -0{,}069\) N m, their sum is zero, as the joint requires. The moment at the wall we get from \(M' = -V\) at a constant shear force, \(M(0) = M(L) + V\,L\), so \(-0{,}363\) N m for \(AC\) and \(-0{,}165\) N m for \(BC\). The largest bending stress is \(363\ \text{N mm}\cdot 5\ \text{mm}/833{,}3\ \text{mm}^4 = 2{,}2\) MPa, and the axial stress in the bar \(AC\) is \(13{,}33\ \text{kN}/100\ \text{mm}^2 = 133\) MPa and in the bar \(BC\) \(167\) MPa. The bending stress is thus \(1{,}6\) % of the axial stress in \(AC\).

Why is the bending so small, although the joints are welded? The end of a bar at the wall is clamped without rotation, and the end at \(C\) turns by the rotation of the joint \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = -3{,}59\cdot 10^{-3}\). The bar would stay straight if it turned as a whole, that is if the rotation of the joint equalled the rotation of its chord (tetiva), the straight line between its end points. The chord of the bar \(AC\) turns by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}/L = -2{,}68\cdot 10^{-3}\), the chord of the bar \(BC\) by \(-2{,}04\cdot 10^{-3}\). The difference between the rotation of the joint and the rotation of the chord tells how much the bar must bend. By equation (5.85) we have \(M = \frac{4EI}{L}\big(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} - \frac32\,\frac{v_{\mathrm{L}}}{L}\big)\), for \(AC\) therefore \(163{,}3\cdot(-3{,}593 + 4{,}018)\cdot 10^{-3} = 0{,}069\) N m. The rotations are thousandths, the bars are thin (the ratio of length to height is \(400\) and \(500\)), so the required moment is extremely small. A truss with pins is therefore a good approximation of a truss with welded joints if the bars are long and thin. For thick and short bars the bending would be larger.

The general method, in which for an arbitrary number of members and joints we assemble matrices like (5.86), is called the finite element method. We treat it in chapters 7 and 12.

5.14.4 A statically indeterminate frame (statično nedoločen okvir)

Until now the reactions followed from the equilibrium of the whole frame. What if there are more supports than equilibrium requires? Let us take the L-frame of figure 5.78, but now support the free end \(C\) of the crossbeam with a roller support (pomična členkasta podpora, definition 5.5), which prevents the vertical displacement but not the horizontal one. We place the load \(F = 500\) N downward at the middle of the crossbeam. If it stood at \(C\), the support would take all of it and the frame would not bend at all.

There are three reactions at the clamp \(A\) (horizontal force, vertical force, moment) and one at \(C\), four in all, and equilibrium in the plane gives three equations (definition 5.6). The frame is once statically indeterminate (statično nedoločen). If we remove the support at \(C\), a statically determinate L-frame remains with the load \(F\) and an unknown force \(R\) upward at \(C\). The value of \(R\) is determined by the condition that \(C\) does not move vertically. This is stated by Castigliano's second theorem for a beam (theorem 5.81): if \(U^*\) is the complementary energy expressed with the unknown reaction \(R\), then \(\partial U^*/\partial R\) is the vertical displacement of the point \(C\), and this is zero. The theorem is proved for one beam. For a frame we prove it in the same way, steps 1 to 5 of its proof, in which instead of theorem 5.78 we use corollary 5.92. The complementary energy of the frame is the sum over the members.

  1. The internal forces as functions of \(R\). For a cross section of the crossbeam at \(s\) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is loaded with the force \(R\) upward at \(C\) and with the force \(F\) downward at the middle of the crossbeam. For \(s < b/2\) we have \(M = R\,(b - s) - F\,(b/2 - s)\), for \(s > b/2\) we have \(M = R\,(b - s)\). In the column \(M = R\,b - F\,b/2\) along the whole length and \(N = R - F\).
  2. The complementary energy. For now we do not take the axial term \(N^2/(2EA)\), we estimate its size below. Then \(U^* = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \int M_{\mathrm{k}}^2/(2EI)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  3. The derivative with respect to \(R\). By a computation in SymPy we get \(EI\,\partial U^*/\partial R = \big(R\,b^3/3 - 5F\,b^3/48\big) + \big(R\,a\,b^2 - F\,a\,b^2/2\big) = 0\). The first bracket comes from the crossbeam, the second from the column.
  4. The solution: \(R = F\,\dfrac{5b + 24a}{16\,(b + 3a)}\). For \(a = b = 1\) m we have \(R = 0{,}453\,F = 226{,}6\) N. With the axial term \((R - F)^2 a/(2EA)\) we get \(R = 226{,}59\) N, so it changes by \(0{,}01\) %, and we drop it.

From \(R\) the other reactions and moments follow. The vertical force of the clamp is \(F - R = 273{,}4\) N upward, there is no horizontal force. The moment at the clamp, that is the internal moment of the column, is \(M = R\,b - F\,b/2 = -23{,}44\) N m. The moment under the load (\(s = b/2\)) is \(R\,b/2 = +113{,}3\) N m. The largest moment is therefore \(113{,}3\) N m under the load, the bending stress \(21{,}2\) MPa. Without the support at \(C\) the moment at the clamp would be \(F\,b/2 = 250\) N m (and \(C\) would descend by \(14{,}45\) mm). The support took \(45\) % of the load and so reduced the moment at the clamp to about a tenth, and the largest moment to \(45\) % of the value without the support at \(C\).

A check. The condition that the vertical displacement of \(C\) is zero we check with a unit force. The unit system is the frame without the support at \(C\) with a unit force upward at \(C\), so a statically determinate system. Corollary 5.92 holds for any system of forces in equilibrium, so we may choose also one that has fewer supports than the actual frame. The reaction that we leave out would be multiplied by the displacement of the support, which is zero. The sum of integrals \(\sum \int m M/EI\) is the same as \(\partial U^*/\partial R\) of step 3, because \(\partial M/\partial R\) are the moments \(m\) of the unit system, so this is a check of the computation. The sum is \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) up to rounding (\(10^{-16}\) mm). The horizontal displacement of the end \(C\) we get with a unit horizontal force, which has in the column \(m = -(a - s)\) and in the crossbeam zero: \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = \int_0^a (-23{,}44)\,(-(a-s))/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = +0{,}561\) mm. The same frame without the support at \(C\) has under this load \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = F\,b\,a^2/(4EI) = 5{,}98\) mm. The horizontal displacement decreased because of the support \(10{,}7\) times, as much as the moment in the column decreased (\(250/23{,}44\)), because the column is a circular arc with a constant moment.

The same procedure, only with more unknowns, is given by EP in example 4.5.7 (pp. 216-217) for a frame of three members, where the reactions at the clamp are three unknowns and for each the displacement at the clamp is zero.

5.14.5 Spatial frames (prostorski okvirji)

What changes when the frame is no longer in a plane? A member has in space six internal forces, no longer three: the axial force \(N\) and two shear forces \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\) (the force \(\vek F = (N, V_{\mathrm{y}}, V_{\mathrm{z}})\) in local axes) and three components of the moment \(\vek M = (M_{\mathrm{x}}, M_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}})\) about the center of the cross section, as in the definitions of section 5.2.1. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis of the member is the torsional moment (torzijski moment, torque), which this section does not treat, because it needs chapter 16. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the bending moment in the plane \(xy\) from this chapter, and \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\) is the one in the plane \(xz\) (\(w\) is the deflection in the direction \(z\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\)). Bending in two planes is therefore two copies of this chapter, if the local axes \(y\) and \(z\) are the principal axes of the cross section (section 5.4.4). A force and a moment are vectors, so we obtain the local components from the global ones with the rotation matrix (definition 2.37), \(\bar{\vek F} = \mat Q\,\vek F\) and \(\bar{\vek M} = \mat Q\,\vek M\) by lemma 2.38, because both systems are right-handed. The rows of the matrix are the local axes in global components (COOK §2.4, PDF pp. 45-46). The same transformation with the unit vectors \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) of the local axes is written for a finite element by ONATE2 (4.86), §4.4.2, PDF p. 216. The complete computation with matrices that assemble a structure from an arbitrary number of members is in chapter 12.

Let us test on the L-frame that we turn into space. The column is along the global axis \(Z\) (upward), the crossbeam along the axis \(X\), and the force \(F = 500\) N acts in the direction of the axis \(Y\), perpendicular to the plane of the frame. We write the global axes with capital letters. The local axes of the crossbeam equal the global ones, \((\vek e_{\mathrm{X}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{Y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{Z}})\). The local axes of the column are \(x' = \vek e_{\mathrm{Z}}\), \(y' = \vek e_{\mathrm{X}}\), \(z' = \vek e_{\mathrm{Y}}\), and the cross product \(x'\times y' = z'\) confirms that the system is right-handed. We compute the internal forces as before: the resultant of the load on the part on the side of larger \(s\) and its moment about the center of the cross section, then the rotation matrix (computation in NumPy). The axial force is zero everywhere.

member \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\) \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\)
crossbeam \(BC\), \(s = 0\) \(500\) N \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(500\) N m
column \(AB\), \(s = a\) (below the joint) \(0\) \(500\) N \(500\) N m \(0\) \(0\)
column \(AB\), \(s = 0\) (clamp) \(0\) \(500\) N \(500\) N m \(-500\) N m \(0\)

The joint \(B\) transmits the vector of the moment, as lemma 5.91 requires (its proof holds for all three components of the moment, because the equilibrium of moments (2.35) is a vector equation). The vector is the same in both members, in global axes \((0, 0, 500)\) N m, only in local axes it has different components. The bending moment of the crossbeam (\(M_{\mathrm{z}} = 500\) N m at \(B\)) becomes the torsional moment of the column (\(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 500\) N m), and the shear force of the crossbeam (\(V_{\mathrm{y}} = 500\) N) becomes the shear force of the column in the other axis (\(V_{\mathrm{z}} = 500\) N). The column therefore twists under a constant torsional moment \(F\,b\) and bends about the local axis \(y'\) with a moment that falls from \(-500\) N m at the clamp to zero at \(B\). An example of the same kind, a frame with a torsional moment, is EP example 4.5.5 (pp. 213-214).
