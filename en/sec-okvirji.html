Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.14 Frames (okvirji)

Until now the axis of a beam was a single straight line. The structures we meet in buildings, machines and vehicles, however, often consist of several beams, welded or bolted together so that under load they cannot turn with respect to each other: the angle between them stays as it was, and so they must bend. How does such a structure carry a load, and where does it move? Every part is a beam from the earlier sections, only the joint is new. The section therefore revolves around the joint. First we say what the joint demands of forces and moments (section 5.14.1), then we obtain displacements from the unit load (section 5.14.2) and show the longer route as well, on which we solve the frame as a boundary value problem (section 5.14.3). Next we return to the two bars of the chapter on energy principles and weld them together (section 5.14.4), look at what happens when there are more supports than equilibrium requires (section 5.14.5), and finally say what is added when the structure no longer lies in a plane (section 5.14.6).

5.14.1 Frame and rigid joints (okvir in toga vozlišča)

In a truss (definition 4.4) the pin does not prevent the bars from turning about it. On the left of figure 5.196 the two bars before loading are drawn grey dashed, at a right angle. When we load them, we draw them orange: each turns about the pin by its own angle and the angle between them is no longer a right angle. What if we weld the bars at the contact? In the middle of the figure the two bars before loading are again at a right angle. The weld holds the ends of both bars together like one small plate, so the angle between them cannot change. The joint can only turn as a whole, by the angle \(\varphi\), and the bars must bend to follow the load. A bent beam carries a bending moment, since by theorem 5.35 the moment is proportional to the curvature, \(M = EI\,v''\). The bar receives this moment from the weld, which acts on its end with a couple. Each bar therefore carries not only an axial force but also a moment, and where the moment changes along the bar, also a shear force \(V = -M'\) (corollary 5.11). In the joint all three quantities must be transmitted.

What of what we have built so far remains valid, and what must we add? We treat each part of such a structure as a beam by the Euler-Bernoulli theory, that is with an axial force \(N\), a shear force \(V\) and a moment \(M\) by definitions 5.7 and 5.8. What is new is that each member gets its own coordinate system, and that we must say how the forces and displacements of the members are connected in the joint. Before the definition let us state the model.

  1. The structure and its loads lie in one plane, the plane \(xy\), which is the plane of symmetry of the cross sections of all members (assumption 2 of section 5.1). In the L-frame further on the force \(F\) lies in the plane of the frame and acts on the axis of the crossbeam, so there the assumption holds exactly. If a load acted out of the plane or beside the plane of symmetry of the cross sections, the members would also turn about their own axes and bend in the other plane, which this section does not cover.
  2. The members are straight beams (definition 5.1). The joint has negligible size, and the length of a member is counted from the intersection of axes to the intersection of axes. A real corner has a size (on the right of figure 5.196): the axes of the strips meet at the point \(J\), and the strips overlap in a square whose side is the height of the cross section \(h\). The square is almost rigid and reaches \(h/2 = 20\) mm into each member. The model shrinks it to the point \(J\), so it counts the member as flexible in bending also over these 20 mm. If we took the square as fully rigid, the vertical displacement of the end of the L-frame further on would be 3 % smaller (unit load without the integral over the 20 mm rigid end of each member). A real weld, on the other hand, is slightly flexible, which increases the displacement. The model takes neither into account. For short and high members, where \(h/2\) is not small compared with the length, the error is larger.
  3. The joint is a rigid connection (middle of figure @fig-nok-vozlisce). All members meeting in the joint have the same displacement at their end at the joint and turn by the same angle as the joint. The joint can transmit forces and moments, like a plate that does not deform. For a welded joint this is a good model, since the square of the joint is of the same steel as the members but short, so it bends much less than the members. In bolted joints the members turn slightly relative to each other and the model holds only approximately. The extreme case is the pin, which transmits no moment. What the assumption means for the result is shown by the L-frame further on: with a pin in the corner the crossbeam would turn freely about it and would not carry the force at all. Only the rigid connection passes its moment into the column.
  4. Displacements and rotations are small, equilibrium is written on the shape before loading (assumption 3 of section 5.2). In the L-frame further on the end \(C\) moves to the right by \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = 11{,}96\) mm, so the vertical force \(F\) has the lever arm \(b + u_{\mathrm{C}}\) about the clamp instead of \(b\). The moment at the clamp is in fact \(u_{\mathrm{C}}/b = 1{,}2\) % larger than the one we compute on the shape before loading. In a slender column with a large compressive force it is different: a larger displacement gives a larger lever arm, that a larger moment, that a still larger displacement, and the computation on the shape before loading fails there.
  5. Each member is linearly elastic, has a constant cross section and a length much greater than the height of the cross section. Linear elasticity holds because the largest stress in the L-frame, \(93{,}75 + 0{,}625 = 94{,}4\) MPa at the inner edge of the column, is 43 % of the yield stress \(220\) MPa (section 5.1.3). We neglect the shear deformation. For the members of our structure, 1 m long and 40 mm high, the shear part of the displacement is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending part by section 5.13.

The members of the structure have different directions, like member 1 and member 2 in figure 5.197, so we cannot use a single axis \(x\). Each member therefore gets a local axis (lokalna os). The axis \(x\) of the member lies along its axis and points from one end to the other, we write the coordinate along it \(s\), so as not to confuse it with the axis \(x\) of the whole structure. The local axis \(y\) is the axis \(x\) turned in the plane of the structure by a right angle in the counterclockwise direction, and the axis \(z\) points toward the viewer. We call the end of the member at \(s = 0\) the start and at \(s = L\) the end. Member 1 has the joint \(J\) at its end, member 2 at its start. The internal forces \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) of the member are then the internal forces from the definitions of section 5.2.1 in these local axes: the force and moment with which the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(s\).

Definition 5.93 Frame (okvir)

A plane frame (ravninski okvir) is a model of a structure made of straight beams (definition 5.1) that lie together with the loads in the plane \(xy\) and are connected in rigid joints (togo vozlišče). Each beam, which we call a member of the frame, has local axes \(s\), \(y\), \(z\) as stated above, and a length \(L\) from the intersection of axes at the start to the intersection of axes at the end. One or more members meet in a rigid joint. The displacement of a joint is a vector \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\), the rotation of a joint is a number \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\), which is an angle in radians and therefore without unit (the slope \(v'\) has the same meaning). For all members meeting in the joint \(J\), the displacement and rotation of their end at \(J\) equal \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\). External loads act in joints and on members.

Source: ONATE1 §1.9.2 (PDF pp. 52-56), EP example 3.2.6 (pp. 122-123) for a frame of two members.

What does equilibrium say about the forces in a joint? Let us take the joint as a small piece of the frame around the intersection of axes. Let us cut it out with a circle and treat it as a body in equilibrium. On the right of figure 5.197 this piece is enlarged. We know the forces on it from the definition of the internal force. If the joint is at the start of a member, as for member 2, the piece is on the side of smaller \(s\), so the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on it with the internal force \(\vek F(0)\). If the joint is at the end of a member, as for member 1, the piece is on the side of larger \(s\), so the part of the member on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on it. By Newton's law of action and reaction this force is opposite to the internal force, that is \(-\vek F(L)\). Besides, an external load may act on the joint.

Lemma 5.94 Equilibrium of a rigid joint (ravnovesje togega vozlišča)

Let \(J\) be a joint of a plane frame (definition 5.93) in equilibrium. Let an external force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\) and an external moment (\(z\) component) \(M_{\mathrm{J}}\) also act on the joint, which may also be zero. For each member \(k\) at the joint let \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) be its internal force in the global components \(x\), \(y\) and \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) its internal moment, namely at that end of the member which is at \(J\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} &\sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the start}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the end}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} \\ &\quad + \vek F_{\mathrm{J}} = \vek 0 , \\ &\sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the start}} M_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{k}:\ \mathrm{J}\ \text{is the end}} M_{\mathrm{k}} + M_{\mathrm{J}} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.88}\]
Proof

We must show that the sum of forces and the sum of moments on the piece of the frame around \(J\) equal zero. We know the forces on the piece, so it suffices to add them.

  1. We cut out a piece of the frame around \(J\) with radius \(\varepsilon\). It is in equilibrium, so by equations (2.2) and (2.45) the sum of forces on it is zero and the sum of moments about \(J\) is zero.
  2. On the cross section of a member that has \(J\) at its start, the part of the member on the side of larger \(s\) acts on the piece with the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\), by the definition of the internal force. On the cross section of a member that has \(J\) at its end, the part of the member on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on the piece with the force \(-\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the moment \(-M_{\mathrm{k}}\), by Newton's law of action and reaction. The forces and moments are written in the local axes of the member. The global components of a force are obtained from the local ones with the rotation matrix (lemma 2.45, point 2). The moment (\(z\) component) does not change under a rotation about the axis \(z\), because the axis \(z\) stays the same, and the moment says how strongly the force turns about it.
  3. The sum of forces is \(\sum_{\text{start}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\text{end}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{J}} = \vek 0\), which is the first equation. In the figure these are \(\vek F_2\), \(-\vek F_1\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{J}}\).
  4. The moment of the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the cross section about \(J\) has magnitude at most \(\varepsilon\,|\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}|\), because the cross section is at most \(\varepsilon\) away from \(J\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) this moment tends to zero, because the internal forces are finite, and so does the moment of the external loads on the piece. What remains are the moments \(\pm M_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{J}}\), and their sum is zero. This is the second equation.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The equilibrium of moments at the ends of a member of a rigid frame is written in ONATE1 (1.36) (PDF p. 56).

The lemma says two things, which we remember with two examples. At a joint without external load in which two members meet, one having it at the start and the other at the end, like members 1 and 2, \(\vek F_1 = \vek F_2\) and \(M_1 = M_2\). The internal force and moment are passed from one member to the other, only written in different local axes. The axis turns, so the axial force of one member can appear in the other as a shear force. If the joints are hinges, that is pins, \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for all members and the first equation remains, the equilibrium of a pin from the chapter on trusses.

Let us test the lemma on an L-shaped frame (figure 5.199). The column \(AB\) is vertical, of length \(a = 1\) m, and clamped at \(A = (0, 0)\) (clamp, definition 5.5). The crossbeam \(BC\) is horizontal, of length \(b = 1\) m, and rigidly attached at \(B = (0, a)\) to the top of the column. At the free end \(C = (b, a)\) acts a force \(F = 500\) N downward, \(\vek F = (0, -F)\). Both members are made of the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm as in section 5.1.3, standing on its edge (the height \(h = 40\) mm lies in the plane of the frame), so \(A = 800\ \text{mm}^2\), \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4\), \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^8\) N. The crossbeam is exactly the cantilever of section 5.1.3, only clamped into the column instead of the wall.

The local axes of the column are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) (upward) and the local axis \(y\) is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (to the left), turned by a right angle in the counterclockwise direction. For the crossbeam the local axes equal the global ones.

We obtain the internal force and moment from the equilibrium of the part that lies on the side of larger \(s\). Let us cut the crossbeam at \(s\), keep the part between the cut and \(C\) and leave out the part from \(B\) to the cut (left in figure 5.200). On the kept part act the load \(F\) and, at the cut, the force and moment of the part on the side of smaller \(s\). This part is in equilibrium, so the force with which the part on the side of smaller \(s\) acts on it is opposite to the resultant of the load, \(F\) upward. By Newton's law of action and reaction the internal force, that is the force in the opposite direction, equals the resultant of the load on the part on the side of larger \(s\). The same holds for the moment: the internal moment is the moment of the load on this part about the center of the cross section, and the moment at the cut is opposite to it.

  1. The crossbeam \(BC\). For the cross section at \(s\) (\(0 \le s \le b\)) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is the piece of the crossbeam between the cross section and \(C\) with the force \((0, -F)\) at \(C\). The resultant is \((0, -F)\), so \(N = 0\) and \(V = -F = -500\) N. The moment of the force about the center of the cross section is the \(z\) component of the cross product \(\vek r\times\vek F\) for \(\vek r = (b - s, 0)\), where \(b - s\) is the distance from the cut to \(C\), that is \((b-s)\cdot(-F) - 0 = -F\,(b - s)\). We obtain \(M(s) = -F\,(b - s)\), at \(s = 0\) we have \(M = -500\) N m. The part on the side of smaller \(s\) holds the kept part with the opposite moment \(F\,(b - s)\). This is the same result as for the cantilever.
  2. The column \(AB\). For the cross section at \(s\) (\(0 \le s \le a\)) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is the piece of the column above the cross section together with the crossbeam (middle of figure 5.200), and the load \((0, -F)\) at \(C\) acts on it. The resultant in local components: \(N = (0, -F)\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = -F = -500\) N, \(V = (0, -F)\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = 0\). The vector from the center of the cross section \((0, s)\) to \(C\) is \(\vek r = (b, a - s)\), so the moment is \(b\cdot(-F) - (a - s)\cdot 0 = -F\,b\). The vertical force has only the horizontal lever arm \(b\) about the cut, the height \(a - s\) does not contribute to the moment, because the force has no horizontal component. So \(M = -F\,b = -500\) N m along the whole length of the column. The lower part holds the upper one with the force \(F\) upward and the moment \(F\,b\), the same at every height.
  3. Let us test lemma 5.94 at \(B\). The column has \(B\) at its end and the crossbeam at its start. By equation (5.88) (without an external load at \(B\)) we must have \(\vek F_{\mathrm{BC}}(0) = \vek F_{\mathrm{AB}}(a)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{BC}}(0) = M_{\mathrm{AB}}(a)\). The first is the force of the crossbeam in global components, \(V\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, -500)\) N, the second is the force of the column, \(N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, -500)\) N. The moments are \(-500\) N m and \(-500\) N m. The equality holds. On the right of figure 5.200 the joint \(B\) is cut out, with the forces in their actual directions. The crossbeam pushes it down with 500 N and turns it clockwise with 500 N m. The column holds it up with 500 N and turns it back with 500 N m. The sum of forces and the sum of moments are zero, the joint stays at rest.

The transfer shows what happens in the joint. The shear force of the crossbeam (\(-500\) N) becomes the axial force of the column (\(-500\) N), because the column is turned by a right angle. On the cut-out joint this is the same vertical force of 500 N, which is transverse for the crossbeam and axial for the column. The moment stays. Therefore the column has a compressive stress \(500\ \text{N}/800\ \text{mm}^2 = 0{,}625\) MPa (the area of the cross section is \(800\ \text{mm}^2\)), and the moment is \(500\) N m along its whole length. The bending stress is thus everywhere in the column the same as at the clamp of the cantilever, \(93{,}75\) MPa, so 150 times greater than the compressive stress. All three quantities are drawn in figure 5.201. The moment is constant in the column, because there \(V = 0\) and by corollary 5.11 \(M' = -V = 0\). In the crossbeam \(M' = -V = F\), so the moment grows from \(-500\) N m at the joint to zero at \(C\).

5.14.2 Displacements of a frame (pomiki okvirja)

Where does the end \(C\) of the frame move? For a truss the way led through the geometry given by the condition that the bars meet at the end. For a frame we would have to integrate the deflection curve over each member and equate displacements and rotations at the joints. A shorter way is the unit load. Theorem 5.79 on the unit load for a beam holds for each member separately. The question is whether the boundary terms in the joints, where one member ends and another starts, cancel in the sum.

Corollary 5.95 Unit load method for a frame (enotska sila za okvir)

Let a plane frame (definition 5.93) of members \(k = 1, \dots, m\) with lengths \(L_{\mathrm{k}}\) and stiffnesses \(EA_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(EI_{\mathrm{k}}\) be linearly elastic, with supports (definition 5.5) at which the displacement or rotation that the support prevents is zero, in equilibrium with the given load, to which belong internal forces \(N_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) in local axes. Let the point \(P\) be a joint or a free end of a member (a member that has \(P\) in its interior is divided into two at \(P\)). Point couples and axial point forces of the actual load act only at joints (a member under one is split there into two), so that \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuously differentiable and \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\) twice continuously differentiable, as theorem 5.78 requires. Let another system of forces be in equilibrium with a unit force at \(P\) in the direction \(\vek d\) (or with a unit moment), possible reactions of the supports, and no other loads, with internal forces \(n_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\) by the same definitions. Then the component of the displacement of the point \(P\) in the direction \(\vek d\) (or the rotation at \(P\)) equals

\[\Delta = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \int_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}} \left( \frac{n_{\mathrm{k}}\,N_{\mathrm{k}}}{EA_{\mathrm{k}}} + \frac{m_{\mathrm{k}}\,M_{\mathrm{k}}}{EI_{\mathrm{k}}} \right) \mathrm{d}s . \tag{5.89}\]
Proof

We must show that the sum of the virtual force identities over the members equals the displacement \(\Delta\), because the boundary terms in the joints combine into the work of the external forces of the unit system. The unit system is the virtual system of forces, and the actual displacements are the displacements of the members. Since \(P\) is a joint or an end, there is no point force in the interior of any member, and in theorem 5.78 the sum over the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the integrals of loads drop out.

  1. For member \(k\), by theorem 5.78 on the principle of virtual forces in the form with boundary terms, because \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) are in equilibrium without a load on the member (\(n_{\mathrm{k}}' = 0\), \(\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}' = 0\), \(m_{\mathrm{k}}' = -\bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\)): \(\int_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}} (n_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}' + m_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'')\,\mathrm{d}s = \big[\,n_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}} + \bar V_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}} + m_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'\,\big]_0^{L_{\mathrm{k}}}\). The actual displacements of the member are smooth enough, because they are the solution of a beam (theorem 5.35) and inside the member there are no point couples and no axial point forces. By the relations \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) the left side is \(\int (n_{\mathrm{k}} N_{\mathrm{k}}/(EA_{\mathrm{k}}) + m_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}}/(EI_{\mathrm{k}}))\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. We sum over the members. The left side is the right side of equation (5.89). The right side is the sum of the boundary terms. We shall collect them by joints.
  3. At the joint \(J\) the displacement of the end of every member at \(J\) equals \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\) and the rotation \(v_{\mathrm{k}}'\) equals \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) (rigid joint in a frame, definition 5.93). The boundary term \(n_{\mathrm{k}} u_{\mathrm{k}} + \bar V_{\mathrm{k}} v_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the scalar product of the force \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the displacement, both in local axes, and the scalar product has the same value in every orthogonal Cartesian system (proof of lemma 2.45, point 2), so we may compute it in global axes. Therefore the sum of the boundary terms at \(J\) is \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\cdot\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{end}} \bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{start}} \bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) + \varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{end}} m_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{J}\ \text{start}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\Big)\), because at the upper limit of the integral, for a member that has \(J\) at its end, the sign is plus, at the lower one, for a member that has \(J\) at its start, minus.
  4. By lemma 5.94 for the unit system the two brackets equal the external force \(\bar{\vek F}_{\mathrm{J}}\) and the external moment \(\bar M_{\mathrm{J}}\) of the unit system at \(J\). At the joint with the unit force this is the unit force, at a joint with a support the reaction, elsewhere zero. A reaction acts only in the direction of the displacement or rotation that the support prevents, and that is zero under the actual load, so the reaction does no work. In the L-frame this is the clamp, which holds the point \(A\) at rest and unturned. What remains is the term of the unit force at \(P\), \(\vek d\cdot(u_{\mathrm{P}}, v_{\mathrm{P}}) = \Delta\) (for a unit moment \(\varphi_{\mathrm{P}}\)).

Source: EP (4.4.12) (p. 198), example 4.4.4 (pp. 202-203) for a frame, where only the moments are taken into account. The proof for a frame is not from a source.

The corollary says that for the computation of displacements a frame needs nothing new: for each member we compute \(N\), \(M\) and \(n\), \(m\) in its local axes and add the integrals.

Let us use it on the L-frame (figure 5.199), with \(F = 500\) N, \(a = b = 1\) m and \(EI\), \(EA\) as above. We are interested in the vertical displacement \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) and the horizontal displacement \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) of the end \(C\) and in the rotation of the joint \(B\). For each of these quantities we set up its own unit system, side by side in figure 5.202.

  1. The vertical displacement. A unit force \(1\) N upward at \(C\) gives, by the same procedure as above: in the crossbeam \(n = 0\), \(m = +(b - s)\), in the column \(n = +1\), \(m = +b\) (the signs are opposite to those of the load, because the force points upward). For the load \(N = -F\) in the column and \(M = -F(b-s)\) in the crossbeam, \(M = -F\,b\) in the column. Equation (5.89) gives
    \[\begin{aligned} v_{\mathrm{C}} &= \underbrace{\int_0^b \frac{-F\,(b-s)\,(b-s)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{crossbeam}} + \underbrace{\int_0^a \frac{-F\,b\cdot b}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{column, bending}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\int_0^a \frac{(-F)\cdot 1}{EA}\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\text{column, axial force}} \\ &= -\frac{F\,b^3}{3EI} - \frac{F\,b^2\,a}{EI} - \frac{F\,a}{EA} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.90}\]
  2. The numbers. The crossbeam alone gives \(F b^3/(3EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, as for the cantilever. The bending of the column gives \(F b^2 a/EI = 23{,}916\) mm, and the axial force of the column \(F a/EA = 0{,}0032\) mm, too little to be drawn. Together \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -31{,}89\) mm, this displacement is downward. The axial term is \(10^{-4}\) of the bending one, so we drop the axial terms in frames. EP does the same in example 4.4.4 (pp. 202-203). Dropping them is right for frames that carry the load by bending. In a truss with welded joints (section 5.14.4) the bars carry mainly by axial forces, and there the axial terms stay.
  3. The horizontal displacement. A unit force \(1\) N in the direction \(+x\) at \(C\) gives in the crossbeam \(n = 1\), \(m = 0\) (its line of action runs along the axis of the crossbeam), in the column \(n = 0\) and \(m = -(a - s)\), because \(\vek r = (b, a - s)\) and \(\vek F = (1, 0)\), so the moment is \(-(a-s)\). Under the load in the column \(M = -F\,b\), in the crossbeam \(N = 0\). Therefore
    \[u_{\mathrm{C}} = \int_0^a \frac{(-F\,b)\,\big(-(a - s)\big)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{F\,b\,a^2}{2EI} = +11{,}96\ \text{mm} . \tag{5.91}\]
    The end moves to the right, because the moment \(M = -F b\) bends the column so that its top leans to the right (the top of the column turns clockwise).
  4. The rotation of the joint \(B\). A unit moment at \(B\) (right in figure 5.202) gives in the column \(m = 1\), in the crossbeam zero, so \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = \int_0^a (-F\,b)/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = -F\,b\,a/EI = -0{,}02392\) (radians, clockwise).

Let us look at the deformed shape, left in figure 5.203. We draw the original frame, clamped at \(A\), grey dashed and the deformed one orange, with the displacements enlarged 20 times. The column bends to the right, the joint moves to \(B'\) and turns, but the angle between the members stays a right angle. The crossbeam bends downward and its end arrives at \(C'\), lower by \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) and further right by \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\).

The vertical displacement is, up to the axial term, exactly 4 times the displacement of the cantilever of section 5.1.3 (\(7{,}972\) mm), although the crossbeam is exactly that cantilever. Why exactly four times? The crossbeam alone contributes \(F b^3/3EI = 7{,}972\) mm, because its moment and the unit moment decrease from the joint to zero, and their product integrates to \(\int_0^b (b-s)^2\,\mathrm{d}s = b^3/3\). The column, which has a constant moment and a constant unit moment, gives \(b^2 a\) instead of a third, so at \(a = b\) three times as much. We obtain the same number from the rotation of the joint, on the same deformed shape on the right of figure 5.203. The crossbeam turns together with the joint as a rigid body by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\), and without its own bending it would lie on the blue dashed line. Its end therefore descends by \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\,b = F b^2 a/EI = 23{,}92\) mm. The angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) is small, so \(\tan\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) differs from \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) by only 0,02 % and the descent really is \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\,b\). The crossbeam's own bending, as for the cantilever, adds another \(7{,}97\) mm. Together \(1 + 3 = 4\).

The horizontal displacement is shown by the tangent to the column at \(B'\), right in the figure. The column carries a constant moment, so it has the same curvature \(\kappa\) everywhere. For small displacements its axis is the parabola \(\kappa s^2/2\), which differs from a circular arc only by terms of order \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}^2\). The tangent to this parabola at the top crosses the original axis of the column exactly at half the height. The top of the column is therefore away from the axis by the slope of the tangent times \(a/2\), \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = |\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\,a/2 = 11{,}96\) mm. This is half of the displacement that a straight column turned by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) about the clamp would give. Why exactly half the height? The curvature of the column is \(\kappa = F b/EI\). At the clamp the displacement and the slope are zero, so after integrating twice the transverse displacement at the height \(s\) equals \(\kappa s^2/2\) and the slope \(\kappa s\). The tangent at \(s = a\) crosses the original axis at the height \(s_0\) for which

\[\frac{\kappa\,a^2}{2} - \kappa\,a\,(a - s_0) = 0 , \qquad s_0 = \frac{a}{2} .\]

The crossbeam does not change its length, because \(N = 0\) in it, so the end \(C\) moves horizontally by the same distance as the top of the column, \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = u_{\mathrm{B}}\).

5.14.3 The frame as a boundary value problem (okvir kot robni problem)

The unit load gives one displacement of one point per computation. What if we want the whole deformed shape, the deflection curve of every member? Then we take the route that section 5.14.2 named and skipped: for every member we write the two beam equations, and at the supports and joints the conditions. We get a boundary value problem (definition 5.48), only that we now look not for one function but for two per member. Let us show the principle on the L frame of section 5.14.1, where we already know the result.

Start on the left. The column is member 1, clamped at \(A\), the crossbeam is member 2 from \(B\) to \(C\), where the force \(F\) acts downward. Each member has its own local axes, as in section 5.14.1: for the column \(s_1\) points up and \(y_1\) to the left, for the crossbeam \(s_2\) points to the right and \(y_2\) up. A point of a member moves, and we resolve this movement along the member's local axes: \(u\) is the part along the axis, \(v\) the part across it. For the column, then, \(u_1\) is the displacement upward and \(v_1\) the displacement to the left, for the crossbeam \(u_2\) is the displacement to the right and \(v_2\) the displacement upward. The unknowns are four functions: \(u_1(s)\), \(v_1(s)\) on \([0, a]\) and \(u_2(s)\), \(v_2(s)\) on \([0, b]\).

We already have the two equations for each member. By theorem 5.42 on the deflection curve equation, a member \(k\) with constant \(EA_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(EI_{\mathrm{k}}\) obeys

\[EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}'' = -q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{k}} , \qquad EI_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'''' = q_{\mathrm{k}} \qquad \text{on } (0, L_{\mathrm{k}}) , \tag{5.92}\]

where \(q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{k}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the loads per length along the local axis \(s\) and along the local axis \(y\). The internal forces follow from the displacements by \(N_{\mathrm{k}} = EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}'\), \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = EI_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}''\) and \(V_{\mathrm{k}} = -EI_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'''\) (theorem 5.35 and corollary 5.11). The first equation is of second order, so its general solution has two open constants, the second is of fourth order and has four (theorem 5.46). Every member thus brings six constants. For a member without a load along its length, as our two are,

\[u_{\mathrm{k}}(s) = c_1 + c_2\,s , \qquad v_{\mathrm{k}}(s) = c_3 + c_4\,s + c_5\,s^2 + c_6\,s^3 .\]

Why exactly six? At each end of a beam there are three pairs of quantities, \((u, N)\), \((v, V)\) and \((v', M)\), and in each pair we must know one member at the end (section 5.7.2). A member has two ends, so it needs \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\) data. For a single beam the supports give them. For a frame the supports give some, and the joints, in which one member holds another, give the rest. Let us see what a joint tells.

The middle of the figure shows the joint \(B\) before loading and after it. A rigid joint moves as one point, from \(B\) to \(B'\), and this displacement vector is one and the same for both members (definition 5.93). Each member, however, writes it in its own axes. The horizontal part is for the crossbeam the displacement along its axis, \(u_2(0)\). For the column it is a displacement across, in the direction opposite to the axis \(y_1\), which points to the left, so \(-v_1(a)\). The vertical part is for the crossbeam the transverse displacement \(v_2(0)\), and for the column the displacement along the axis, \(u_1(a)\). The weld keeps the right angle, so the tangent of the crossbeam and the tangent of the column turn by the same angle, \(v_2'(0) = v_1'(a)\). These are three matching conditions for the displacements.

On the right is the joint \(B\), cut out of the frame. The piece is in equilibrium, so lemma 5.94 on the equilibrium of a rigid joint holds for it. The crossbeam has \(B\) at its start, so it acts on the piece with its internal force and moment: \(N_2(0)\) to the right, \(V_2(0)\) upward and \(M_2(0)\) counterclockwise. The column has \(B\) at its end, so it acts with the opposite values: \(N_1(a)\) downward, \(V_1(a)\) to the right, because \(y_1\) points to the left, and \(M_1(a)\) clockwise. The sum of the horizontal forces, the sum of the vertical forces and the sum of the moments on the piece are zero:

\[N_2(0) + V_1(a) = 0 , \qquad V_2(0) - N_1(a) = 0 , \qquad M_2(0) - M_1(a) = 0 .\]

These are three equilibrium conditions. The joint \(B\) gives six conditions in all. We can now state the task for any frame.

Definition 5.96 Boundary value problem of a plane frame (robni problem ravninskega okvirja)

Let a plane frame (definition 5.93) be given, made of members \(k = 1, \dots, m\) with lengths \(L_{\mathrm{k}}\), constant stiffnesses \(EA_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(EI_{\mathrm{k}}\) and continuous loads per length \(q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{k}}\), \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the local axes. Let every end of every member lie in exactly one joint, where a free end and a supported end of a single member also count as joints. In every joint \(J\), for each of the three quantities \(u_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{J}}\) (the components of the displacement in the global axes) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) (the rotation), let one of two things be given: a support (definition 5.5) prescribes the quantity, or the external force or the external moment in that direction is given (it may be zero). The boundary value problem of a plane frame (robni problem ravninskega okvirja) is the task of finding functions \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) (twice continuously differentiable) and \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\) (four times continuously differentiable) on \([0, L_{\mathrm{k}}]\), \(k = 1, \dots, m\), that satisfy:

  1. the equations (5.92) on every member,
  2. the matching conditions (pogoji ujemanja) in every joint \(J\): the displacement of the end of every member at \(J\), converted from the local components \((u_{\mathrm{k}}, v_{\mathrm{k}})\) to the global ones by the rotation matrix (lemma 2.45), equals \((u_{\mathrm{J}}, v_{\mathrm{J}})\), and \(v_{\mathrm{k}}'\) at that end equals \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\),
  3. in every joint, for each of the quantities \(u_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\): the prescribed value if a support (definition 5.5) that holds along a global axis prescribes it, otherwise the corresponding component of equation (5.88), in which the internal forces are expressed by \(N_{\mathrm{k}} = EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}'\), \(V_{\mathrm{k}} = -EI_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}'''\) and \(M_{\mathrm{k}} = EI_{\mathrm{k}}\,v_{\mathrm{k}}''\).

The conditions in a joint where two or more members meet are also called transition conditions (prehodni pogoji), the conditions at a supported or free end of a single member boundary conditions.

Source: The task is assembled from the beam equations (CM (7.3.33), (7.3.34), PDF p. 310, for the end conditions) and from the equilibrium of a rigid joint (ONATE1 §1.9.2, (1.36), PDF pp. 52 to 56). In this form it is not from a source.

Are there as many conditions as constants? Let us count them for the L frame and then in general. There are \(6 \cdot 2 = 12\) constants. The clamp \(A\) prescribes all three quantities of the column end, \(u_1(0) = 0\), \(v_1(0) = 0\), \(v_1'(0) = 0\), which are three conditions. The free end \(C\) prescribes none, so we get three equilibrium equations with the external force. The joint \(B\) gives three matching conditions between the column and the crossbeam and three of equilibrium. Together \(3 + 3 + 6 = 12\). Here we have already eliminated the displacement and the rotation of the joint: instead of "the end of the column has the displacement of the joint" and "the start of the crossbeam has the displacement of the joint" we wrote directly "the end of the column and the start of the crossbeam have the same displacement".

Lemma 5.97 Number of conditions of the frame boundary value problem (število pogojev robnega problema okvirja)

The boundary value problem of a plane frame (definition 5.96) with \(m\) members and \(n\) joints has \(6m + 3n\) unknowns, namely \(6m\) open constants and \(3n\) joint quantities, and \(6m + 3n\) conditions. Once the joint quantities are eliminated, \(6m\) conditions remain for \(6m\) constants.

Proof

We must show two things: that there are as many conditions as unknowns, and that the joint quantities can be eliminated. Both are counting.

  1. Constants. The general solution of the first equation (5.92) has two open constants, that of the second four (theorem 5.46 for \(n = 2\) and \(n = 4\)). A member has six constants, the frame \(6m\).
  2. Joint quantities. Every joint \(J\) has three: \(u_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\). There are \(n\) joints and \(3n\) quantities. The unknowns number \(6m + 3n\) in all.
  3. The matching conditions (item 2 of the definition) are three for every end of every member: two components of the displacement and the rotation (in the figure for the end of the crossbeam and the end of the column at \(B\)). A member has two ends, there are \(2m\) ends and \(3 \cdot 2m = 6m\) conditions.
  4. Item 3 of the definition gives one condition for each of the three quantities of every joint: a prescribed value or an equilibrium equation (in the figure the three equilibrium equations at \(B\)). That is \(3n\) conditions. The conditions number \(6m + 3n\) in all, as many as the unknowns.
  5. Elimination. In every joint lies at least one member end. Its three matching conditions express \(u_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{J}}\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{J}}\) by the constants of that member. We insert these expressions into all the other conditions. This uses up \(3n\) conditions and eliminates \(3n\) unknowns, and \(6m\) conditions remain for \(6m\) constants.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

For the L frame \(m = 2\) and \(n = 3\): \(21\) unknowns and \(21\) conditions, after elimination \(12\) and \(12\), as we counted. The lemma counts, it does not guarantee a solution. An equal number of equations and unknowns gives exactly one solution only if the equations are independent. A case where they are not: take the clamp away from the L frame, so that \(A\) is a free end too. There are still \(12\) conditions, but SymPy shows that the rank of their matrix is only \(9\). Three are missing, because an unsupported frame can move as a rigid body in three ways (two displacements and a rotation) without any internal force changing. With the force \(F\) there is no solution at all, since nothing balances it. The supports must therefore prevent rigid motion, as for a beam.

Let us now solve the L frame with numbers: \(F = 500\) N, \(a = b = 1\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\), \(EA = 1{,}568 \cdot 10^8\) N. We write the constants of the column \(c_1, \dots, c_6\) and those of the crossbeam \(d_1, \dots, d_6\):

\[\begin{aligned} u_1 &= c_1 + c_2\,s , & v_1 &= c_3 + c_4\,s + c_5\,s^2 + c_6\,s^3 , \\ u_2 &= d_1 + d_2\,s , & v_2 &= d_3 + d_4\,s + d_5\,s^2 + d_6\,s^3 . \end{aligned}\]
  1. The free end \(C\), \(s = b\) on the crossbeam. The end is an outer section with its normal in the direction \(+s_2\), so the internal forces there equal the external ones (equation (5.39), and the same holds for the axial force): \(N_2(b) = 0\), \(V_2(b) = -F\), \(M_2(b) = 0\). From \(N_2 = EA\,u_2' = EA\,d_2\) we get \(d_2 = 0\). From \(V_2 = -EI\,v_2''' = -6EI\,d_6\) we get \(d_6 = F/(6EI)\). From \(M_2(b) = EI\,(2 d_5 + 6 d_6\,b) = 0\) we get \(d_5 = -F\,b/(2EI)\).
  2. Equilibrium of the joint \(B\). From \(V_2(0) - N_1(a) = 0\) we get \(N_1 = V_2 = -F\), so \(EA\,c_2 = -F\) and \(c_2 = -F/EA\). From \(N_2(0) + V_1(a) = 0\) we get \(V_1 = 0\), so \(c_6 = 0\). From \(M_2(0) - M_1(a) = 0\) we get \(M_1(a) = M_2(0)\) \(= 2EI\,d_5 = -F\,b\), so \(2EI\,c_5 = -F\,b\) and \(c_5 = -F\,b/(2EI)\).
  3. The clamp \(A\), \(s = 0\) on the column: \(u_1(0) = 0\), \(v_1(0) = 0\), \(v_1'(0) = 0\) give \(c_1 = c_3 = c_4 = 0\). The column is thereby determined:
    \[u_1(s) = -\frac{F}{EA}\,s , \qquad v_1(s) = -\frac{F\,b}{2EI}\,s^2 .\]
  4. Matching of the displacements at \(B\). The horizontal part: \(d_1 = u_2(0) = -v_1(a) = F\,b\,a^2/(2EI)\). The vertical part: \(d_3 = v_2(0) = u_1(a) = -F\,a/EA\). The rotation: \(d_4 = v_2'(0) = v_1'(a) = -F\,b\,a/EI\). The crossbeam is
    \[u_2(s) = \frac{F\,b\,a^2}{2EI} , \qquad v_2(s) = -\frac{F\,a}{EA} - \frac{F\,b\,a}{EI}\,s - \frac{F\,b}{2EI}\,s^2 + \frac{F}{6EI}\,s^3 .\]
  5. Numbers. The joint \(B\) moves to the right by \(u_2 = 11{,}96\) mm and down by \(F a/EA = 0{,}0032\) mm, and it turns by \(v_2'(0) = -0{,}02392\). The end \(C\) has \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = u_2(b) = +11{,}96\) mm and
    \[v_{\mathrm{C}} = v_2(b) = -\frac{F\,a}{EA} - \frac{F\,b^2\,a}{EI} - \frac{F\,b^3}{3EI} = -0{,}0032 - 23{,}916 - 7{,}972 = -31{,}89\ \text{mm} .\]

These are exactly the values of equations (5.90) and (5.91), which the unit load gave (corollary 5.95), and the rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = -0{,}02392\) of the same section. SymPy solves all twelve equations at once and gives the same constants. But now we see more than three numbers. Every term of \(v_2\) has its picture: \(-F a/EA\) is the drop of the joint, because the column shortens, \(-(F b a/EI)\,s\) is a straight line, the crossbeam turned together with the joint as a rigid body, and the last two terms are the deflection curve of the cantilever of section 5.9.2, since \(-F b s^2/(2EI) + F s^3/(6EI) = -F s^2 (3b - s)/(6EI)\). The constant \(u_2\) says that the crossbeam as a whole moves to the right as far as the top of the column bends.

Here the conditions untangled one after another: first the forces from the free end toward the clamp, then the displacements from the clamp back. This works because the frame is statically determinate and the internal forces follow from equilibrium alone. For a statically indeterminate frame (section 5.14.5) we do not get the forces in advance and solve all \(6m\) equations together. That is why this route is laborious for larger frames: a frame of ten members has \(60\) constants. The finite element computation (chapter 12) turns the elimination of the lemma around: it keeps the displacements and rotations of the joints as unknowns, three per joint, and eliminates the constants.

Inextensible members (neraztegljivi elementi). The term \(F a/EA = 0{,}0032\) mm is \(10^{-4}\) of the whole displacement \(31{,}89\) mm. In hand computation of frames we therefore often assume that the members neither shorten nor lengthen along their axes, \(EA \to \infty\). Let us say three things about this assumption.

Why is it justified here? Compare how far the end of the column moves under the same force \(F\) when it acts along the axis and when it acts across it: \(F a/EA\) against \(F a^3/(3EI)\). The ratio is

\[\frac{F\,a/EA}{F\,a^3/(3EI)} = \frac{3\,I}{A\,a^2} = \frac{h^2}{4\,a^2} = \frac{(40\ \text{mm})^2}{4\,(1000\ \text{mm})^2} = 4 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

because \(I/A = h^2/12\) for a rectangle. Along the axis the whole section resists at once, while in bending the force acts on the long lever arm \(a\) and the fibres stretch only in proportion to their small distance from the axis. A slender member is therefore much stiffer along its axis than across it, our column \(2500\) times.

When does it fail? When the structure carries the load by axial forces and hardly bends. This is so for the truss with welded joints of section 5.14.4: there the displacements are set by the elongations of the bars, and with \(EA \to \infty\) we would get zero displacement. It also fails for short, thick members, where \(h/a\) is not small.

What does it change? From \(u_{\mathrm{k}}' = N_{\mathrm{k}}/EA_{\mathrm{k}} \to 0\) it follows that \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) is constant: a member has one constant of axial displacement instead of two. The axial force, however, can no longer be computed from \(N_{\mathrm{k}} = EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,u_{\mathrm{k}}'\), because that is a product of an infinitely large and a zero number. \(N_{\mathrm{k}}\) becomes a new unknown, set by the equilibrium of the joints, like the reaction of a support. On a member without axial load it is constant, because \(N_{\mathrm{k}}' = -q_{\mathrm{x},\mathrm{k}} = 0\). The count stays \(6m\) against \(6m\): per member four constants of \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\), one constant \(u_{\mathrm{k}}\) and one constant \(N_{\mathrm{k}}\). For the L frame \(u_1 = 0\) gives the condition \(v_2(0) = 0\), the joint \(B\) does not move vertically, and the result is \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -F b^2 a/EI - F b^3/(3EI) = -31{,}888\) mm instead of \(-31{,}891\) mm. The difference \(0{,}0032\) mm is \(0{,}01\) %, and \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\) do not change (SymPy).

In the lectures the conditions in a joint are called transition conditions, the conditions at the supports boundary conditions. They are of the same kinds as the conditions between two pieces of a beam under a point force (section 5.8.4): matching of the displacements and equilibrium.

5.14.4 A truss with welded joints (paličje z zvarjenimi vozlišči)

In the chapter on energy principles we computed the truss of two bars with pins. We then said that real joints of bars also transmit some bending (FELIPPA §2.3, PDF p. 30, where the bars of real trusses are joined with gusset plates, so they carry some bending), and promised to show how to compute it. Let us look at the same truss from the chapter 4 with welded joints. The bars \(AC\) (\(4\) m) and \(BC\) (\(5\) m) are of steel, \(E = 196\) GPa, with a square cross section \(10 \times 10\) mm, so \(A = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\) as before and \(I = 833{,}3\ \text{mm}^4\), \(EI = 163{,}3\) N m\(^2\). The bars are clamped into the wall at \(A\) and \(B\) (clamp, definition 5.5, in the truss with pins they were attached there with pins), and rigidly welded at \(C\), where a load of \(10\) kN acts downward. The grey line is the truss before loading, the orange curves are the bars after it, with the displacements enlarged \(200\) times. What is new? At \(C\) there is, besides two displacements, a rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}\), so there are three unknowns. The bar \(AC\) has the local axis \(s\) from \(A\) toward \(C\), the bar \(BC\) from \(B\) toward \(C\). In both \(C\) is the end.

For the computation we need the relation between the displacement of the end \(C\) of a member and the forces on it. Let us draw a single member (figure 5.205, left). The wall holds the left end, so that it neither moves nor turns. The grey dashed line is the axis of length \(L\) before loading, the orange curve is the axis after it. The right end has moved along the axis by \(u_{\mathrm{L}}\) and across it by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}\), and its tangent has turned by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}}\) from the original direction. At the end act the axial force \(N\), the shear force \(V\) and the moment \(M\). How large must they be for the end to arrive exactly in this position? The opposite question we have already solved: the flexibility matrix of a cantilever (section 5.9.5) gives the displacement and the rotation from a force and a couple at the end. Now we invert it.

Lemma 5.98 End forces of a clamped beam (sile na koncu vpetega nosilca)

Let a straight beam of length \(L\) and stiffnesses \(EA\), \(EI\), without load along its length, be clamped (clamp, definition 5.5) at \(s = 0\), and let its end \(s = L\) move by \(u_{\mathrm{L}}\) in the direction of the axis and by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}\) transversely and turn by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}} = v'(L)\) (small displacements). Then the internal forces at \(s = L\) are

\[N = \frac{EA}{L}\,u_{\mathrm{L}} , \qquad \begin{pmatrix} V \\ M \end{pmatrix} = EI \begin{pmatrix} 12/L^3 & -6/L^2 \\ -6/L^2 & 4/L \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_{\mathrm{L}} \\ \varphi_{\mathrm{L}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{5.93}\]
Proof

We must show that the matrix in equation (5.93) inverts the flexibility matrix of the cantilever, and that the axial force is obtained separately. Let a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and a couple \(M_0\) act at the end, so by the definitions of the internal force \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M(L) = M_0\).

  1. By section 5.9.5 the displacement of the end of a cantilever is \(v_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} L^3/(3EI) + M_0 L^2/(2EI)\) and the rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}} = F_{\mathrm{y}} L^2/(2EI) + M_0 L/EI\). In matrix form: \((v_{\mathrm{L}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{L}})^\top = \mat D\,(F_{\mathrm{y}}, M_0)^\top\) with \(\mat D = \frac{1}{EI}\begin{pmatrix} L^3/3 & L^2/2 \\ L^2/2 & L\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. The determinant of the matrix \(\mat D\) is \(\frac{1}{(EI)^2}\big(L^4/3 - L^4/4\big) = \frac{L^4}{12\,(EI)^2}\). It is not zero, so the inverse matrix exists (definition 1.110).
  3. The inverse of a \(2\times 2\) matrix is obtained by swapping the diagonal elements, changing the sign of the off-diagonal ones and dividing everything by the determinant: \(\mat D^{-1} = EI\,\frac{12}{L^4}\begin{pmatrix} L & -L^2/2 \\ -L^2/2 & L^3/3\end{pmatrix} = EI\begin{pmatrix} 12/L^3 & -6/L^2 \\ -6/L^2 & 4/L\end{pmatrix}\). This is the equation for \((V, M)\).
  4. The axial force. Without an axial load \(N' = 0\) (corollary 5.11), so \(N\) is constant and \(u' = N/EA\) is constant. Because of \(u(0) = 0\) at the wall, \(u(L)\) equals \(N L/EA\), so \(N = EA\,u_{\mathrm{L}}/L\).

Source: EP example 4.2.3 (p. 176), ONATE1 (1.35) and (1.36) (PDF pp. 55-56), COOK §2.3 (PDF pp. 38-42). The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma stands on three assumptions, and each has its cost. The first is small displacements: this is why we may compute the axial displacement and the transverse displacement each by itself and write equilibrium on the straight axis. For our bars the rotation will be \(3{,}6\cdot 10^{-3}\), and its square \(1{,}3\cdot 10^{-5}\) is the order of the error. For rotations of a few tenths of a radian the lemma no longer holds. The second is that there is no load along the length. The self-weight of the bars would add the forces and moments of a clamped beam under load, which chapter 12 treats. The third is the model without shear from section 5.3.2: the share of shear in the deflection falls with the square of the ratio \(h/L\), and at \(L/h = 400\) it is of the order \(10^{-5}\).

The coefficients \(12EI/L^3\), \(6EI/L^2\) and \(4EI/L\) are stiffnesses: the force that the end demands for a unit of displacement, and the moment that it demands for a unit of rotation. Where does the number \(12\) come from, when a cantilever with a force at its end has the stiffness of only \(3EI/L^3\)? Let us look at the right sketch of figure 5.205: the beam is again clamped into a wall, the grey dashed line is the axis before loading. We move the end by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}\) and do not allow it to turn, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\). By the lemma then \(V = 12EI\,v_{\mathrm{L}}/L^3\) and \(M(L) = -6EI\,v_{\mathrm{L}}/L^2\), and the moment at the middle is \(M(L) + V\,L/2 = 0\). The axis has the shape of the letter S and at the middle an inflection without moment. We may cut the beam there in thought into two cantilevers of length \(L/2\), each with the force \(V\) at its end and with the deflection \(v_{\mathrm{L}}/2\). The formula of the cantilever gives \(v_{\mathrm{L}}/2 = V\,(L/2)^3/(3EI)\), so \(V = 12EI\,v_{\mathrm{L}}/L^3\). This view adds the reason for the number: half the length makes a cantilever eight times stiffer, half the deflection twice more, and two cantilevers in a row change nothing, because they carry the same force. Together \(3\cdot 8/2 = 12\). For our bars: \(12EI/L^3 = 30{,}6\) N/m, \(6EI/L^2 = 61{,}3\) N, \(4EI/L = 163{,}3\) N m, \(EA/L = 4{,}9\cdot 10^6\) N/m for \(AC\) (\(L = 4\) m), and \(15{,}7\) N/m, \(39{,}2\) N, \(130{,}7\) N m, \(3{,}92\cdot 10^6\) N/m for \(BC\) (\(L = 5\) m). The axial stiffnesses are more than a hundred thousand times greater than the transverse ones (\(1{,}6\cdot 10^5\) times for \(AC\) and \(2{,}5\cdot 10^5\) times for \(BC\)). This is the reason for the result that follows.

For each bar we must write the displacement \((u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}})\) of the joint in local axes. The local axis \(s\) has the direction \((1, 0)\) for \(AC\) and \((0{,}8, -0{,}6)\) for \(BC\), because the vector from \(B = (-4, 3)\) m to \(C = (0, 0)\) equals \((4, -3)\) m. The unit vector of the axis is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = (c_{\mathrm{x}}, c_{\mathrm{y}})\) (the components of the vector of the bar divided by its length). The local displacement along the axis \(s\) is then \(u_{\mathrm{s}} = c_{\mathrm{x}}\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}\,v_{\mathrm{C}}\), and the transverse one \(v_{\mathrm{L}} = -c_{\mathrm{y}}\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}\,v_{\mathrm{C}}\) (lemma 2.45, the local axis \(y\) is \((-c_{\mathrm{y}}, c_{\mathrm{x}})\)).

The equations of the joint \(C\) we get from lemma 5.94. Both bars have \(C\) at their end, so the sum of the internal forces at the ends equals the external force, \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{C}} = (0, -10\,000)\) N, and the sum of the moments equals the external moment, \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\). The global components of the force of a member are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = N_{\mathrm{k}}\,(c_{\mathrm{x}}, c_{\mathrm{y}}) + V_{\mathrm{k}}\,(-c_{\mathrm{y}}, c_{\mathrm{x}})\). When we insert the force from lemma 5.98, we obtain three linear equations for \((u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{C}})\), in matrix form

\[\begin{pmatrix} 7{,}409\cdot 10^6 & -1{,}882\cdot 10^6 & -23{,}52 \\ -1{,}882\cdot 10^6 & 1{,}411\cdot 10^6 & -92{,}61 \\ -23{,}52 & -92{,}61 & 294{,}0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathrm{C}} \\ v_{\mathrm{C}} \\ \varphi_{\mathrm{C}} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -10\,000 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{5.94}\]

in units N/m, N, N m for rows and columns in turn. The matrix is symmetric, because the matrix \(\mat D\) from the proof is symmetric (reciprocity, theorem 4.141), and so is its inverse, and because a rotation of axes does not spoil the symmetry. Its upper left \(2\times 2\) part is, up to corrections that are four to six orders of magnitude smaller, the stiffness matrix of the truss with pins. The solution (SymPy):

\[u_{\mathrm{C}} = -2{,}721\ \text{mm} , \quad v_{\mathrm{C}} = -10{,}714\ \text{mm} , \quad \varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = -3{,}593\cdot 10^{-3} .\]

In figure 5.207 the joint \(C\) moves to the left by \(2{,}72\) mm and downward by \(10{,}71\) mm, and the tangent of the bar \(AC\) at \(C\) turns clockwise.

The truss with pins from the chapter 4 gives \(-2{,}7211\) mm and \(-10{,}7143\) mm. The displacements differ by \(0{,}0013\) %. The axial forces are \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}333\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = +16{,}666\) kN, and they differ from the values of the truss (\(-13{,}333\) kN and \(16{,}667\) kN) by less than \(0{,}3\) N. What is new is the bending. The internal moment at \(C\) is \(M_{\mathrm{AC}}(L) = +0{,}069\) N m and \(M_{\mathrm{BC}}(L) = -0{,}069\) N m, their sum is zero, as the joint requires. The moment at the wall we get from \(M' = -V\) at a constant shear force, \(M(0) = M(L) + V\,L\), so \(-0{,}363\) N m for \(AC\) and \(-0{,}165\) N m for \(BC\). The largest bending stress is \(363\ \text{N mm}\cdot 5\ \text{mm}/833{,}3\ \text{mm}^4 = 2{,}2\) MPa, and the axial stress in the bar \(AC\) is \(13{,}33\ \text{kN}/100\ \text{mm}^2 = 133\) MPa and in the bar \(BC\) \(167\) MPa. The bending stress is thus \(1{,}6\) % of the axial stress in \(AC\).

Why is the bending so small, although the joints are welded? A bar bends only as much as the directions of its ends deviate from the direction of its chord (tetiva), the straight line between its end points. A bar that turned as a whole would stay straight and without moment. The chord of the bar \(AC\) turns by \(v_{\mathrm{L}}/L = -2{,}68\cdot 10^{-3}\) (in figure 5.205 this is the angle of the chord), the chord of the bar \(BC\) by \(-2{,}04\cdot 10^{-3}\). The end at the wall may not turn, so it deviates from the chord by \(2{,}68\cdot 10^{-3}\). The end at \(C\) turns with the joint by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = -3{,}59\cdot 10^{-3}\) (the tangent at \(C\)) and deviates from the chord by \(-0{,}91\cdot 10^{-3}\). These deviations, thousandths of a radian, are all that bends the bar, which is why the deformed bars are nearly straight in spite of the \(200\)-fold enlargement. We estimate the size of the moment without the lemma: the moment is \(EI\) times the curvature, and the curvature is the change of direction per length, here of the order of \(2{,}7\cdot 10^{-3}\) over \(4\) m, so \(M \approx 163{,}3\cdot 6{,}7\cdot 10^{-4} = 0{,}11\) N m. Exactly: by equation (5.93) we have \(M = \frac{4EI}{L}\big(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} - \frac32\,\frac{v_{\mathrm{L}}}{L}\big)\), for \(AC\) therefore \(163{,}3\cdot(-3{,}593 + 4{,}018)\cdot 10^{-3} = 0{,}069\) N m. The rotations are thousandths, the bars are thin (the ratio of length to height is \(400\) and \(500\)), so the required moment is extremely small. A truss with pins is therefore a good approximation of a truss with welded joints if the bars are long and thin. For thick and short bars the bending would be larger.

The general method, in which for an arbitrary number of members and joints we assemble matrices like (5.94), is called the finite element method. We treat it in chapters 7 and 12.

5.14.5 A statically indeterminate frame (statično nedoločen okvir)

Until now the reactions followed from the equilibrium of the whole frame. What if there are more supports than equilibrium requires? Let us take the L-frame of figure 5.199 with a column of height \(a\) and a crossbeam of length \(b\), but now support the free end \(C\) of the crossbeam with a roller support (pomična členkasta podpora, definition 5.5), which prevents the vertical displacement but not the horizontal one. We place the load \(F = 500\) N downward at the middle of the crossbeam. If it stood at \(C\), the support would take all of it and the frame would not bend at all.

There are three reactions at the clamp \(A\) (horizontal force, vertical force, moment) and one at \(C\), four in all, and equilibrium in the plane gives three equations (definition 5.6). The frame is once statically indeterminate (statično nedoločen). If we remove the support at \(C\), a statically determinate L-frame remains with the load \(F\) and an unknown force \(R\) upward at \(C\). The value of \(R\) is determined by the condition that \(C\) does not move vertically. This is stated by Castigliano's second theorem for a beam (theorem 5.84): if \(U^*\) is the complementary energy expressed with the unknown reaction \(R\), then \(\partial U^*/\partial R\) is the vertical displacement of the point \(C\), and this is zero. The theorem is proved for one beam. For a frame we prove it in the same way, steps 1 to 5 of its proof, in which instead of theorem 5.79 we use corollary 5.95. The complementary energy of the frame is the sum over the members.

  1. The internal forces as functions of \(R\). For a cross section of the crossbeam at \(s\) the part on the side of larger \(s\) is loaded with the force \(R\) upward at \(C\) and with the force \(F\) downward at the middle of the crossbeam. For \(s < b/2\) we have \(M = R\,(b - s) - F\,(b/2 - s)\), for \(s > b/2\) we have \(M = R\,(b - s)\). In the column \(M = R\,b - F\,b/2\) along the whole length and \(N = R - F\).
  2. The complementary energy. For now we do not take the axial term \(N^2/(2EA)\). We may, because the column shortens under a force of a few hundred newtons by thousandths of a millimetre, while bending moves \(C\) by millimetres. The size of the error is in step 4. Then \(U^* = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \int M_{\mathrm{k}}^2/(2EI)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  3. The derivative with respect to \(R\). By a computation in SymPy we get \(EI\,\partial U^*/\partial R = \big(R\,b^3/3 - 5F\,b^3/48\big) + \big(R\,a\,b^2 - F\,a\,b^2/2\big) = 0\). The first bracket comes from the crossbeam, the second from the column.
  4. The solution: \(R = F\,\dfrac{5b + 24a}{16\,(b + 3a)}\). For \(a = b = 1\) m we have \(R = 0{,}453\,F = 226{,}6\) N. With the axial term \((R - F)^2 a/(2EA)\) we get \(R = 226{,}59\) N, so it changes by \(0{,}01\) %, and we drop it. For a short and thick column the share would be larger and we would keep the term.

The condition of step 3 can also be seen. We remove the support and let only the load \(F\) act (the lower left sketch, the displacements are enlarged \(50\) times, the grey dashed line is the frame before loading, the orange one after it). The column bends into an arc, the joint \(B\) moves to the right by \(5{,}98\) mm and turns, the crossbeam tilts with it and also bends itself, and the end \(C\) descends by \(14{,}45\) mm. Of this the rotation of the joint \(B\) brings \(11{,}96\) mm and the bending of the crossbeam itself \(2{,}49\) mm. Now we push upward at \(C\) with the force \(R\). Every newton of this force lifts \(C\) by \((b^3/3 + a\,b^2)/EI = 0{,}06378\) mm. The support does not allow the descent, so \(R\) must lift the end exactly back: \(R = 14{,}45/0{,}06378 = 226{,}6\) N. These are the brackets of step 3, arranged differently: the terms with \(F\) are the descent due to the load, the terms with \(R\) the lift due to the reaction. This view adds a picture to the derivative of the energy: the reaction is the force that cancels the displacement that the load would make without the support. The lower right sketch shows the frame with both forces: \(C\) stays at its height and moves only by \(0{,}56\) mm to the right.

From \(R\) the other reactions and moments follow. The vertical force of the clamp is \(F - R = 273{,}4\) N upward, there is no horizontal force. The moment at the clamp, that is the internal moment of the column, is \(M = R\,b - F\,b/2 = -23{,}44\) N m. The moment under the load (\(s = b/2\)) is \(R\,b/2 = +113{,}3\) N m. The largest moment is therefore \(113{,}3\) N m under the load, the bending stress \(21{,}2\) MPa. Without the support at \(C\) the moment at the clamp would be \(F\,b/2 = 250\) N m (and \(C\) would descend by \(14{,}45\) mm). The support took \(45\) % of the load and so reduced the moment at the clamp to about a tenth, and the largest moment to \(45\) % of the value without the support at \(C\).

A check. The condition that the vertical displacement of \(C\) is zero we check with a unit force. The unit system is the frame without the support at \(C\) with a unit force upward at \(C\), so a statically determinate system. Corollary 5.95 holds for any system of forces in equilibrium, so we may choose also one that has fewer supports than the actual frame. The reaction that we leave out would be multiplied by the displacement of the support, which is zero. The sum of integrals \(\sum \int m M/EI\) is the same as \(\partial U^*/\partial R\) of step 3, because \(\partial M/\partial R\) are the moments \(m\) of the unit system, so this is a check of the computation. The sum is \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) up to rounding (\(10^{-16}\) mm). The horizontal displacement of the end \(C\) we get with a unit horizontal force, which has in the column \(m = -(a - s)\) and in the crossbeam zero: \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = \int_0^a (-23{,}44)\,(-(a-s))/EI\,\mathrm{d}s = +0{,}561\) mm. The same frame without the support at \(C\) has under this load \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = F\,b\,a^2/(4EI) = 5{,}98\) mm. The horizontal displacement decreased because of the support \(10{,}7\) times, as much as the moment in the column decreased (\(250/23{,}44\)), because the column is a circular arc with a constant moment.

The same procedure, only with more unknowns, is given by EP in example 4.5.7 (pp. 216-217) for a frame of three members, where the reactions at the clamp are three unknowns and for each the displacement at the clamp is zero.

5.14.6 Spatial frames (prostorski okvirji)

What changes when the frame is no longer in a plane? A member has in space six internal forces, no longer three: the axial force \(N\) and two shear forces \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\) (the force \(\vek F = (N, V_{\mathrm{y}}, V_{\mathrm{z}})\) in local axes) and three components of the moment \(\vek M = (M_{\mathrm{x}}, M_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}})\) about the center of the cross section, as in the definitions of section 5.2.1. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis of the member is the torsional moment (torzijski moment, torque), which twists the member. The relation between it and the rotation is derived in chapter 16, here we use it in section 5.14.8. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the bending moment in the plane \(xy\) from this chapter, and \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\) is the one in the plane \(xz\) (\(w\) is the deflection in the direction \(z\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\)). Bending in two planes is therefore two copies of this chapter, if the local axes \(y\) and \(z\) are the principal axes of the cross section (section 5.4.4). Why just then and what happens otherwise, we show in section 5.14.7. A force and a moment are vectors, so we obtain the local components from the global ones with the rotation matrix (definition 2.44), \(\bar{\vek F} = \mat Q\,\vek F\) and \(\bar{\vek M} = \mat Q\,\vek M\) by lemma 2.45, because both systems are right-handed. The rows of the matrix are the local axes in global components (COOK §2.4, PDF pp. 45-46). The same transformation with the unit vectors \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) of the local axes is written for a finite element by ONATE2 (4.86), §4.4.2, PDF p. 216. The complete computation with matrices that assemble a structure from an arbitrary number of members is in chapter 12.

Let us test on the L-frame that we place into space (figure 5.209). The column \(AB\) of height \(a = 1\) m is along the global axis \(Z\) (upward) and clamped at \(A\), the crossbeam \(BC\) of length \(b = 1\) m is along the axis \(X\), but the force \(F = 500\) N acts at \(C\) in the direction of the axis \(Y\), perpendicular to the plane of the frame, in the figure away from the viewer. We write the global axes with capital letters. The local axes of the crossbeam equal the global ones, \((\vek e_{\mathrm{X}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{Y}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{Z}})\). The local axes of the column are \(x' = \vek e_{\mathrm{Z}}\), \(y' = \vek e_{\mathrm{X}}\), \(z' = \vek e_{\mathrm{Y}}\), and the cross product \(x'\times y' = z'\) confirms that the system is right-handed. We compute the internal forces as before: the resultant of the load on the part on the side of larger \(s\) and its moment about the center of the cross section, then the rotation matrix (computation in NumPy). The axial force is zero everywhere.

5.14.7 Bending in two planes (upogib v dveh ravninah)

All through the chapter we kept the load in the plane of symmetry of the cross section (section 5.1, assumption 2 in section 5.15.1). In a spatial frame we can no longer do so: the force in the example above acted perpendicular to the plane of the frame, and in general a force has components in both transverse directions. In this section and the next we lift the restriction. In its place we require two things. The axes \(y\) and \(z\) are to be the principal axes of the cross section through the centroid (corollary 5.31). The line of the force is to pass through the shear centre of the cross section (section 16.7 in chapter 16), which for a section with two axes of symmetry is simply the centroid. The first requirement we need at once, the second in section 5.14.8.

Let us begin with a question. The cantilever from a flat bar \(20 \times 40\) mm we have so far loaded upright, with a force along the longer side of the section. Now we load it at the free end obliquely, along the diagonal of the section. Where does it bend and where is the stress largest? An experiment with a ruler in the hand tells that not in the direction of the force: a ruler that we push obliquely escapes into its soft direction.

Let us draw the cross section as we see it when we look along the axis \(x\) (figure 5.211, left). The rectangle is \(40\) mm high and \(20\) mm wide, the axis \(y\) points upward and the axis \(z\) to the right, the origin is at the centroid. The dashed diagonal is the line of the force. The force \(F = 500\) N points along it downward and to the left. We resolve it into the component along the axis \(y\), \(447\) N downward, and the component along the axis \(z\), \(224\) N to the left. Each component bends the beam in its own plane, and the question is whether we may compute each bending by itself and add them.

First the displacement field. In the plane we assumed that a cross section stays plane and perpendicular to the axis, and obtained \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u - y\,v'\) (equation (5.16)): a cross section that tilts with the axis by \(v'\) pushes the point at the height \(y\) back by \(y\,v'\). The same picture holds in the plane \(x\)-\(z\), which is the plane \(x\)-\(y\) turned by a quarter of a turn about the axis of the beam. If the axis bends also in the direction \(z\) by \(w(x)\), the cross section tilts in addition by \(w'\) and pushes the point with the offset \(z\) back by \(z\,w'\). Together

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x) - z\,w'(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = w(x) . \tag{5.95}\]

The derivative with respect to \(x\) gives the strain of the fiber through the point \((y, z)\) of the cross section,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = u' - y\,v'' - z\,w'' . \tag{5.96}\]

Two assumptions enter here. The first: we may add the tilts because they are small. Two successive rotations by \(v'\) and \(w'\) differ from the sum by terms of the order \(v'\,w'\), for our cantilever of the order \(10^{-4}\) against \(10^{-2}\). For rotations of a few tenths of a radian the sum no longer holds. The second: the cross section stays plane and perpendicular to the axis in both planes at once. This is the assumption of section 5.3.2, used twice, with the same cost as there (the share of shear in the deflection, \(0{,}125\) % at \(L/h = 25\) and a quarter of this, \(0{,}031\) %, for bending about the weak axis, where the governing dimension is \(b\) and \(L/b = 50\)). Chapter 12 obtains the same equation from the vector of rotation of the cross section (equation (12.20)).

Theorem 5.99 Stress in bending in two planes (napetost pri upogibu v dveh ravninah)

Let a beam be of a linearly elastic material with the modulus \(E\), with the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections and with the displacement field (5.95). Let the fibers be in a uniaxial state of stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), as in section 5.5.1. Let the cross section have the area \(A\) and the moments of inertia \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = \int y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A\). Then the axial force and the bending moments (equations (5.4) and (5.5)) are

\[N = EA\,u' , \qquad M_{\mathrm{z}} = E\,\big(I_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'' + I_{\mathrm{yz}}\,w''\big) , \qquad M_{\mathrm{y}} = -E\,\big(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\,v'' + I_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\big) . \tag{5.97}\]

If \(y\) and \(z\) are the principal axes of the cross section, \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), then

\[M_{\mathrm{z}} = EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'' , \qquad M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'' , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{N}{A} - \frac{M_{\mathrm{z}}\,y}{I_{\mathrm{z}}} + \frac{M_{\mathrm{y}}\,z}{I_{\mathrm{y}}} . \tag{5.98}\]
Proof

We must show three integrals over the cross section and then express the stress with them. We compute on the cross section with the axes \(y\) and \(z\) through the centroid.

  1. The stress at the point \((y, z)\) is by equation (5.96) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,v'' - z\,w'')\). The quantities \(u'\), \(v''\) and \(w''\) depend only on \(x\), so in integrating over the cross section we take them out.
  2. The axial force: \(N = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,\big(u' A - v'' \int y\,\mathrm{d}A - w'' \int z\,\mathrm{d}A\big)\). The origin is at the centroid, so the first moments \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int z\,\mathrm{d}A\) are zero and \(N = EA\,u'\) remains.
  3. The moment about the axis \(z\): \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = -\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,\big({-u'} \int y\,\mathrm{d}A + v'' \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A + w'' \int y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A\big) = E\,(I_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'' + I_{\mathrm{yz}}\,w'')\).
  4. The moment about the axis \(y\): \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = E\,\big(u' \int z\,\mathrm{d}A - v'' \int y\,z\,\mathrm{d}A - w'' \int z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\big) = -E\,(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\,v'' + I_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'')\).
  5. For \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) we have \(u' = N/EA\), \(v'' = M_{\mathrm{z}}/EI_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(w'' = -M_{\mathrm{y}}/EI_{\mathrm{y}}\). We insert into step 1: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M_{\mathrm{z}}\,y/I_{\mathrm{z}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,z/I_{\mathrm{y}}\).

Source: GR §5.3.4 (PDF pp. 404-405: the principal axes of bending are chosen so that a moment about one does not cause bending about the other, so the energy (5.147) has separate terms for the two planes), COOK §2.3 (PDF p. 42: the axes \(y\) and \(z\) must be principal centroidal axes, otherwise additional terms appear in the matrix). Equation (5.98) in this form is not in our sources, the derivation from equation (5.5) is ours. The signs are checked in SymPy on a rectangle and agree with \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\) of chapter 12.

Why does the term with \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) have a minus and the term with \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) a plus? A positive \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) is by step 3 a positive curvature \(v''\), which shortens the fibers above the axis (\(y > 0\)), so there is compression there. A positive \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) is by step 4 a negative curvature \(w''\), which lengthens the fibers at \(z > 0\), so there is tension there. The difference comes from the right-hand rule for the vector of the moment: a rotation about the axis \(z\) carries the axis \(x\) toward the axis \(y\), while a rotation about the axis \(y\) carries the axis \(z\) toward the axis \(x\).

What does the requirement of principal axes cost? Let us take the L-section of example 5.32 in the axes \(y\) and \(z\) parallel to its legs, where \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = 307\,500\) mm\(^4\), \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = 107\,500\) mm\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} = -100\,000\) mm\(^4\) (the blackwhite axes in figure 5.69). We load it only with \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\), that is with a force along the axis \(y\). From \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) in equation (5.97) follows \(w'' = -(I_{\mathrm{yz}}/I_{\mathrm{y}})\,v'' = 0{,}93\,v''\): the beam bends sideways by \(93\) % of what it bends in the direction of the force. From the first equation \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = E\,(I_{\mathrm{z}} - I_{\mathrm{yz}}^2/I_{\mathrm{y}})\,v'' = E\cdot 214\,477\ \text{mm}^4\cdot v''\), the stiffness in the direction of the force is only \(70\) % of the value \(EI_{\mathrm{z}}\). Whoever computed here with equation (5.98) would underestimate the deflection in the direction of the force by \(30\) % and would not get the sideways one at all. This is why for a section without symmetry we first find the principal axes and resolve the load along them.

Now we can answer where the stress is zero.

Corollary 5.100 Neutral axis in skew bending (nevtralna os pri poševnem upogibu)

Let the assumptions of theorem 5.99 hold with the principal axes \(y\) and \(z\), let \(N = 0\) and at least one of the moments \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) be different from zero. Skew bending (poševni upogib) is bending in which the plane of the load contains no principal axis of the cross section. For \(I_{\mathrm{y}} \ne I_{\mathrm{z}}\) this is exactly when \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) are both different from zero. For \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}}\) every axis through the centroid is principal and there is no skew bending. Then:

  1. The points of the cross section with zero stress lie on a straight line through the centroid, the neutral axis (definition 5.34),
    \[\frac{M_{\mathrm{z}}}{I_{\mathrm{z}}}\,y = \frac{M_{\mathrm{y}}}{I_{\mathrm{y}}}\,z . \tag{5.99}\]
  2. The neutral axis is parallel to the vector of the moment \((M_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}})\), that is perpendicular to the plane of the load, exactly when \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}}\) or when one of the moments is zero.
  3. The vector of curvature \((v'', w'')\) is perpendicular to the neutral axis: the beam bends in the direction perpendicular to the neutral axis (the curvature in this cross section, the deflection of the end only if the moment has the same direction in all cross sections).
Proof

All three statements we read from equation (5.98) on the cross section.

  1. For \(N = 0\) the condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) equals \(-M_{\mathrm{z}}\,y/I_{\mathrm{z}} + M_{\mathrm{y}}\,z/I_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). This is a linear equation for \((y, z)\) without a constant term and with at least one coefficient different from zero, so a straight line through the origin, which is at the centroid.
  2. The line of step 1 has the direction \((y, z) = (M_{\mathrm{y}}/I_{\mathrm{y}},\ M_{\mathrm{z}}/I_{\mathrm{z}})\), because this pair satisfies the equation. The vector of the moment has in the plane of the cross section the direction \((y, z) = (M_{\mathrm{y}}, M_{\mathrm{z}})\). The two vectors are parallel exactly when \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot M_{\mathrm{z}}/I_{\mathrm{z}} - M_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot M_{\mathrm{y}}/I_{\mathrm{y}} = M_{\mathrm{y}} M_{\mathrm{z}}\,(1/I_{\mathrm{z}} - 1/I_{\mathrm{y}})\) is zero. The plane of the load contains the axis \(x\) and the force, and the vector of the moment of the force is perpendicular to it (definition 2.33).
  3. By equation (5.98) \((v'', w'') = (M_{\mathrm{z}}/EI_{\mathrm{z}},\ -M_{\mathrm{y}}/EI_{\mathrm{y}})\). Its scalar product with the direction of step 2 is \(\frac{1}{E}\big(\frac{M_{\mathrm{z}}}{I_{\mathrm{z}}}\frac{M_{\mathrm{y}}}{I_{\mathrm{y}}} - \frac{M_{\mathrm{y}}}{I_{\mathrm{y}}}\frac{M_{\mathrm{z}}}{I_{\mathrm{z}}}\big) = 0\).

Source: The position of the neutral axis for a load whose plane does not pass through a principal axis of the cross section was first determined by Saint-Venant (TIMO, PDF p. 145: the neutral axis is parallel to the tangent to the ellipse of inertia at its intersection with the plane of the forces). The form with equation (5.99) and the proof are ours.

Point 2 is the answer to the guess from the beginning. In plane bending the neutral axis always lay perpendicular to the force, and this is what we would expect here too. It holds only if the cross section is equally stiff in both directions (a circle, a square) or if the force points along a principal axis. Otherwise the neutral axis turns toward the axis with the smaller moment of inertia. Let us check on numbers.

Example 5.101 A cantilever loaded along the diagonal of its section (konzola, obremenjena vzdolž diagonale prereza)

The cantilever of section 5.1.3 is \(L = 1\) m long, its cross section has \(b = 20\) mm and \(h = 40\) mm, so \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = b\,h^3/12 = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\) and \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = h\,b^3/12 = 26\,667\) mm\(^4\) (lemma 5.27), \(EI_{\mathrm{z}} = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and \(EI_{\mathrm{y}} = 5226{,}7\) N m\(^2\). A rectangle has two axes of symmetry, so \(y\) and \(z\) are principal axes and the centroid is also the shear centre (lemma 16.48). At the free end a force \(F = 500\) N acts through the centroid along the diagonal.

  1. The components of the force. The diagonal has the direction \((y, z) = (h, b)/\sqrt{h^2 + b^2} = (0{,}8944,\ 0{,}4472)\). The force points in the opposite direction: \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -447{,}2\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{z}} = -223{,}6\) N.
  2. The internal moment. At the cross section at \(x\) this is the moment of the load on the part on the side of larger \(x\) about the center of the cross section, \(\vek M = \vek r\times\vek F\) with \(\vek r = (L - x, 0, 0)\), so \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -(L - x)\,F_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = (L - x)\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\). At the clamp (\(x = 0\)) we have \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = +223{,}6\) N m and \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = -447{,}2\) N m. The vector of the moment is perpendicular to the force.
  3. The stress at the clamp by equation (5.98), with \(y\) and \(z\) in millimetres: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \dfrac{447\,200}{106\,667}\,y + \dfrac{223\,600}{26\,667}\,z = 4{,}193\,y + 8{,}385\,z\) MPa.
  4. The corners. At the corner \((y, z) = (20, 10)\) mm, opposite to the direction of the force, it is \(83{,}85 + 83{,}85 = 167{,}7\) MPa. At the corner \((-20, -10)\) mm it is \(-167{,}7\) MPa. At the corners \((20, -10)\) and \((-20, 10)\) mm the contributions subtract, \(83{,}85 - 83{,}85 = 0\).
  5. The neutral axis by equation (5.99): \(4{,}193\,y + 8{,}385\,z = 0\), so \(y = -2z\). This is the other diagonal of the rectangle, because it passes through the corners \((20, -10)\) and \((-20, 10)\) mm. It is not perpendicular to the force.
  6. The deflection of the end. Each plane is a cantilever with a force at its end (theorem 5.57): \(v(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} L^3/(3EI_{\mathrm{z}}) = -7{,}13\) mm and \(w(L) = F_{\mathrm{z}} L^3/(3EI_{\mathrm{y}}) = -14{,}26\) mm. The total deflection is \(\sqrt{7{,}13^2 + 14{,}26^2} = 15{,}94\) mm in the direction \((y, z) = -(1, 2)/\sqrt5\). With the direction of the neutral axis \((-2, 1)\) it has the scalar product \(2 - 2 = 0\), as point 3 of the corollary requires. The force points \(26{,}6^\circ\) from the axis \(-y\), the deflection \(63{,}4^\circ\), the deviation is \(36{,}9^\circ\).
  7. A comparison. The same force along the axis \(y\) gives \(93{,}75\) MPa and \(7{,}97\) mm (section 5.1.3). Along the diagonal the stress is \(1{,}79\) times larger and the deflection twice as large.

Why does the bar escape sideways? About the axis \(y\) it is four times softer, \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}}/4\). The smaller component of the force, half of the larger one, acts on a quarter of the stiffness and gives twice the deflection of the larger one. Step 4 shows one more view of the same equation: skew bending is two plane bendings, each with its own linear distribution of stress, added at the corners. Where both are tensile we get the largest tension, where they are opposite they cancel. That the neutral axis is exactly the other diagonal is a special feature of a load along the diagonal of a rectangle: the moments are in the ratio \(h : b\), and so are the section moduli \(b\,h^2/6\) and \(h\,b^2/6\), so both bendings contribute the same stress at a corner.

5.14.8 A beam in space: four boundary value problems (nosilec v prostoru: štirje robni problemi)

How do we compute the displacements of a beam that the load stretches, twists and bends in two planes at once? A cross section that does not deform can move in six ways: three displacements and three rotations, as many as any rigid body. Along the axis these are the displacements \(u(x)\), \(v(x)\), \(w(x)\) and the rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\) about the axis of the beam. The other two rotations are not new unknowns, because the cross section stays perpendicular to the axis: \(\varphi_{\mathrm{z}} = v'\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} = -w'\) (the minus because a positive rotation about the axis \(y\) carries the axis \(x\) toward \(-z\)). Four unknown functions remain.

Figure 5.212 shows each of them on a cantilever, the grey dashed line is the axis with the end section before loading, the orange one after it. An axial load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) stretches the beam, the end moves by \(u\) along the axis. A moment \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) per unit length twists it, the end section turns by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis and the axis stays straight. A load \(q_{\mathrm{y}}\) bends it in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), the end rises by \(v\). A load \(q_{\mathrm{z}}\) bends it in the plane \(x\)-\(z\), the end moves by \(w\) toward the viewer. Under each sketch are the pairs of quantities that we shall meet in the conditions at the ends: a motion of the cross section and the force that belongs to it.

Theorem 5.102 The four boundary value problems of a beam in space (štirje robni problemi nosilca v prostoru)

Let a straight beam on the interval \((0, L)\) have constant \(EA\), \(EI_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(EI_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(GJ\), the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections, the principal axes \(y\) and \(z\) of the cross section and the displacement field (5.95). In addition let the cross sections turn about the axis of the beam by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\), and let the torsional moment be \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\) (theorem 16.18 with the twist per unit length \(\alpha = \varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\)), measured about the longitudinal axis through the shear centres of the cross sections. Let the cross sections turn about the longitudinal axis through the shear centres, and let \(v\), \(w\) be the displacements of that axis (for a cross section with two axes of symmetry it is the axis of the beam). Let the load be a force \(\vek q = (q_{\mathrm{x}}, q_{\mathrm{y}}, q_{\mathrm{z}})\) per unit length, whose line passes through the shear centre, and a moment \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) per unit length about the axis of the beam, \(m_{\mathrm{y}} = m_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} EA\,u'' &= -q_{\mathrm{x}} , &\qquad GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'' &= -m_{\mathrm{x}} , \\ EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'''' &= q_{\mathrm{y}} , &\qquad EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'''' &= q_{\mathrm{z}} , \end{aligned} \tag{5.100}\]

and the internal forces are

\[\begin{aligned} N &= EA\,u' , &\qquad M_{\mathrm{x}} &= GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}' , \\ M_{\mathrm{z}} &= EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'' , &\qquad V_{\mathrm{y}} &= -EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v''' , \\ M_{\mathrm{y}} &= -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'' , &\qquad V_{\mathrm{z}} &= -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''' . \end{aligned} \tag{5.101}\]

Each equation contains a single unknown function: the problems are not coupled.

Proof

We must show that the equilibrium of a piece of the beam gives each function its own equation. We look at the forces on the cross section of the element with six internal forces.

  1. Equilibrium. By equation (5.12) we have \(\vek F' + \vek q = \vek 0\) and \(\vek M' + \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F + \vek m = \vek 0\). With \(\vek F = (N, V_{\mathrm{y}}, V_{\mathrm{z}})\) we have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F = (0, -V_{\mathrm{z}}, V_{\mathrm{y}})\) (definition 2.33). By components: \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{y}}' = -q_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}}' = -q_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' = -m_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}' = V_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{z}}' = -V_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  2. Stretching. By theorem 5.99 \(N = EA\,u'\). Inserted into \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\): \(EA\,u'' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), equation (5.34).
  3. Twisting. \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\) by assumption. Inserted into \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' = -m_{\mathrm{x}}\): \(GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'' = -m_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  4. Bending in the plane \(x\)-\(y\). With principal axes \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v''\) (equation (5.98)). From \(M_{\mathrm{z}}' = -V_{\mathrm{y}}\) we have \(V_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'''\), and from \(V_{\mathrm{y}}' = -q_{\mathrm{y}}\) follows \(EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'''' = q_{\mathrm{y}}\), the equation of the deflection curve (5.33).
  5. Bending in the plane \(x\)-\(z\). With principal axes \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\). From \(M_{\mathrm{y}}' = V_{\mathrm{z}}\) we have \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'''\), and from \(V_{\mathrm{z}}' = -q_{\mathrm{z}}\) follows \(EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'''' = q_{\mathrm{z}}\). The signs in steps 4 and 5 are equal, although \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\) have opposite signs at the curvature, because the term \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek F\) has opposite signs too.
  6. We needed the principal axes in steps 4 and 5: with \(I_{\mathrm{yz}} \ne 0\), by equation (5.97), \(w''\) would also appear in \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(v''\) in \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the equations for \(v\) and \(w\) would be coupled. We needed the shear centre in step 3: a transverse force whose line passes the shear centre at a distance \(e\) contributes to \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) a term of the size \(e\,q\), and the twisting would depend on the bending load.

Source: GR §5.3.4 (PDF pp. 404-405: a spatial beam with bending in the two principal planes, stretching and torsion, the energy (5.147) as a sum of four separate terms, the torsional stiffness \(GJ_{\mathrm{x}}\) under the assumption of free warping of the cross sections). COOK §2.3 (PDF pp. 41-43: the twelve unknowns of an element, the principal centroidal axes, the torsional stiffness and the warping of noncircular sections). BOWER §10.3.2 (PDF p. 669: a straight beam with the principal axes of inertia along the transverse directions, the equation of bending in one plane). The shear centre: ONATE2 sections 4.2.6 and 4.2.7 (PDF pp. 189-191), as in definition 16.44. The proof from equation (5.12) is ours.

Two assumptions of the theorem are new and deserve their cost. The first is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\). Theorem 16.18 proves it for a twist per unit length that is constant along the beam, and for cross sections that may warp freely out of their plane. At a clamp the cross section cannot warp, so the beam is stiffer there than the equation tells. For a solid section such as our bar this reaches about one dimension of the section from the clamp (Saint-Venant's principle, section 5.15.1), so \(40\) mm of \(1000\) mm, and by this estimate it changes the rotation of the end by about \(4\) % or less. For thin-walled open sections (I-sections and channels) restrained warping may not be neglected and the equation does not suffice (COOK §2.3, PDF pp. 42-43). The second is the force through the shear centre. For the channel of section 16.7.2 a force of \(500\) N through the centroid passes the shear centre at \(12{,}5\) mm and twists the cantilever, besides bending it, by \(22{,}3^\circ\) per metre (corollary 16.49). For a section with two axes of symmetry the shear centre is at the centroid and every force through the axis of the beam satisfies the condition.

Conditions at the ends. The four equations have the orders \(2\), \(2\), \(4\) and \(4\), and their general solutions have twelve constants together. The two ends must therefore give twelve conditions, each six. Where do six come from? The end section has six motions, and for each motion one of two things holds. Either a support prescribes the motion, and the force that holds it is an unknown reaction. Or the motion is free, and then the force is known: zero or the load at the end. We cannot prescribe both at once, just as we cannot hold a bar still and pull it with a chosen force with both being our choice. The pairs are:

motion of the end section internal force that belongs to it problem
displacement \(u\) \(N = EA\,u'\) stretching
displacement \(v\) \(V_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'''\) bending \(x\)-\(y\)
displacement \(w\) \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'''\) bending \(x\)-\(z\)
rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'\) twisting
rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{z}} = v'\) \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v''\) bending \(x\)-\(y\)
rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} = -w'\) \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = -EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w''\) bending \(x\)-\(z\)

In the plane we had three of these pairs, \((u, N)\), \((v, V)\) and \((v', M)\) (section 5.7.2). The supports of definition 5.5 extend into space like this:

end prescribed motions prescribed forces
clamp \(u = v = w = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(v' = 0\), \(w' = 0\) none
ball joint \(u = v = w = 0\) \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\)
hinge with the axis \(z\) \(u = v = w = 0\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(w' = 0\) \(M_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\)
free end none \(N\), \(V_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(V_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(M_{\mathrm{z}}\) equal to the load at the end, zero without a load

A clamp grips all six motions, like the wall in figure 5.212. A ball joint (kroglični členek) holds a point and allows all three rotations. A hinge (tečaj) is the pin of the plane chapter placed into space: it allows turning only about its own axis, so a single moment is prescribed. At a free end with a load equation (5.39) holds for each component. Each of the four problems must be supported so that the beam cannot move in it as a rigid body, otherwise it has no unique solution. In stretching \(u\) must be held at one end at least, in twisting \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) at one end at least. Each bending problem has two rigid motions, a shift and a rotation, so the displacement must be held at both ends, or the displacement at one end and the rotation at either (theorem 5.50). A beam between two ball joints has both bending problems uniquely determined, like the simply supported beam, but \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) at both ends: nothing holds the rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the beam turns freely about its own axis. The tables are ours, an extension of definition 5.5 by the twelve unknowns of an element from COOK §2.3 (PDF pp. 41-42).

Example 5.103 Displacement of the L-frame loaded out of its plane (pomik okvirja L, obremenjenega pravokotno na svojo ravnino)

How far does the end \(C\) of the frame of section 5.14.6 move (figure 5.209)? The strip \(20 \times 40\) mm stands as in the plane frame, with \(40\) mm in the plane of the frame and \(20\) mm perpendicular to it. A force perpendicular to the plane therefore bends it about the weak axis: \(I = 40\cdot 20^3/12 = 26\,667\) mm\(^4\) and \(EI = 5226{,}7\) N m\(^2\), for the crossbeam this is \(EI_{\mathrm{z}}\), for the column \(EI_{\mathrm{y}}\). The torsion constant of the rectangle \(20 \times 40\) mm is \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) (section 16.5), so \(GJ = 5516{,}5\) N m\(^2\). The computation is linear, so we take a tenth of the force of the table, \(F = 50\) N, the reason is in step 6.

  1. The column \(AB\) is clamped at \(A\), and at \(B\) it is loaded, by the table of section 5.14.6, with the shear force \(V_{\mathrm{z}} = F\) and the torsional moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = F\,b = 50\) N m (the vector of the moment at \(B\)). There is no load along the length, so the right sides of equations (5.100) are zero.
  2. Twisting of the column: \(GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'' = 0\) with the conditions \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(0) = 0\) and \(GJ\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}'(a) = F\,b\). The solution is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(s) = F\,b\,s/GJ\), at the top \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(a) = 50/5516{,}5 = 9{,}06\cdot 10^{-3}\), that is \(0{,}52^\circ\).
  3. Bending of the column in the plane \(x'\)-\(z'\): \(EI_{\mathrm{y}}\,w'''' = 0\) with the conditions \(w(0) = 0\), \(w'(0) = 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{y}}(a) = 0\) and \(V_{\mathrm{z}}(a) = F\). This is a cantilever with a force at its end, \(w(s) = F\,s^2\,(3a - s)/(6EI_{\mathrm{y}})\), at the top \(w(a) = F\,a^3/(3EI_{\mathrm{y}}) = 3{,}19\) mm and \(w'(a) = F\,a^2/(2EI_{\mathrm{y}}) = 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-3}\). Stretching and bending in the plane \(x'\)-\(y'\) have no load and zero conditions at \(A\), so \(u = 0\) and \(v = 0\).
  4. The joint \(B\) is rigid, so the start of the crossbeam has the same displacement and the same vector of rotation as the top of the column. The displacement is \(3{,}19\) mm in the direction \(Y\). The rotation about the axis \(Z\) is \(9{,}06\cdot 10^{-3}\), and the rotation about the axis \(X\) is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}'} = -w'(a) = -4{,}78\cdot 10^{-3}\).
  5. The crossbeam \(BC\): bending in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), \(EI_{\mathrm{z}}\,v'''' = 0\) with the conditions \(v(0) = 3{,}19\) mm, \(v'(0) = 9{,}06\cdot 10^{-3}\), \(M_{\mathrm{z}}(b) = 0\) and \(V_{\mathrm{y}}(b) = F\). The solution is \(v(x) = v(0) + v'(0)\,x + F\,x^2\,(3b - x)/(6EI_{\mathrm{z}})\), at the end \(v(b) = 3{,}19 + 9{,}06 + 3{,}19 = 15{,}44\) mm. The rotation of the joint about the axis \(X\) turns the crossbeam about its own axis. There is no torsional moment in the crossbeam, so it turns as a whole and does not move the point \(C\) on the axis.
  6. The assumptions. At \(F = 50\) N the displacement is \(1{,}5\) % of the length of the crossbeam, the largest bending stress at the clamp is \(50\ \text{N m}\cdot 10\ \text{mm}/26\,667\ \text{mm}^4 = 18{,}75\) MPa and the largest shear stress due to torsion \(12{,}7\) MPa (equation (16.41)). At \(F = 500\) N everything would be ten times larger: the displacement \(154\) mm, the rotation \(5{,}2^\circ\), the stresses \(187{,}5\) MPa and \(127\) MPa against the yield stress of \(220\) MPa. Small displacements and an elastic material could no longer be assumed there, so the table of internal forces for \(500\) N holds, but we do not compute the displacements for this force.
  7. A check with energy. The work of a slowly growing force is \(\tfrac12 F\,v(b) = 0{,}386\) J. The bending of the column stores \(F^2 a^3/(6EI_{\mathrm{y}}) = 0{,}0797\) J, the bending of the crossbeam the same, the twisting of the column \(\tfrac12 M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(a) = 0{,}2266\) J. The sum is \(0{,}386\) J.

The orange line in figure 5.209 shows the deformed frame at \(F = 50\) N, with the displacements enlarged \(40\) times. Of the \(15{,}44\) mm by which \(C\) moves, \(9{,}06\) mm or \(59\) % come from the twisting of the column, although the column is straight and the force does not twist it directly, and only \(3{,}19\) mm from the bending of the column. The four problems were solved each by itself, only the conditions at the joint connected them. The general computation for an arbitrary number of members is in section 12.5.
