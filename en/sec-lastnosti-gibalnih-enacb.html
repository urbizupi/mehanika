Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.9 Properties of the equations of motion of a robot

In section 27.8 we obtained for a robot with \(n\) joints the equation of motion (theorem 27.56)

\[\mat M(\vek q)\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek g(\vek q) = \vek\tau ,\]

with the mass matrix \(\mat M\) (theorem 27.52), the matrix \(\mat C\) built from the Christoffel symbols (definition 27.55), the gravity column \(\vek g = \partial V/\partial\vek q\) (lemma 27.54) and the column \(\vek\tau\) of motor torques and forces. Two symbols meet other meanings used elsewhere in the book here: \(\mat C\) is the matrix of the velocity terms and is not the damping matrix of chapter 10, and \(\vek g\) with its argument is a column of \(n\) numbers that depends on the configuration, not the gravitational acceleration \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s². The model is the same as before: rigid links, ideal joints with one degree of freedom, no friction and no inertia of the motors. These are idealizations. A real arm loses energy in its bearings and the motors have inertia of their own, which the equation does not capture.

We know how to write the equation. Now we ask what we know about it before solving it, and what these properties are good for. We begin with an experiment that shows the equation does describe falling. Arm A (the planar two-link arm of section 27.1 with lengths \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, masses \(m_1 = 10\) kg and \(m_2 = 5\) kg, which are our values and not from a source, uniform straight bars with the centres of mass at mid-length) is stretched out horizontally, \(\vek q = (0, 0)^\T\), we switch the motors off, \(\vek\tau = \vek 0\), and release it from rest.

Let us draw what happens. The first link turns about the base \(O\), which stays at rest, and the second is attached to the first at a joint that a small circle shows in every position. At time zero the arm is stretched out in the starting position, dashed, and the tip is \(1{,}5\) m from \(O\). Downwards points the gravitational acceleration \(\vek g\), which pulls on the centres of mass of both links. Why does the arm fall? To hold it in this position the motors would have to give the torques \(\vek g(\vek 0) = (110{,}363,\ 12{,}263)^\T\) N m (statics, section 27.7), and there are no motors. After \(0{,}25\) s the arm is in the position \(\vek q = (-22{,}8^\circ,\ 35{,}3^\circ)^\T\), the first link has turned down and the second has bent against it. After \(0{,}5\) s it is in the blue position \(\vek q = (-69{,}4^\circ,\ 5{,}8^\circ)^\T\), nearly stretched again, now hanging obliquely down. After \(0{,}75\) s it is in the position \((-128{,}8^\circ,\ -5{,}4^\circ)^\T\), already on the other side of the vertical, and after \(1\) s in the position \((-173{,}3^\circ,\ 20{,}6^\circ)^\T\), nearly horizontal on the left. The path of the tip is the thin line below the base and is never farther than \(1{,}5\) m from \(O\), as the workspace of section 27.1 says.

The picture raises three questions, which this section answers.

  1. The arm lost height and gained speed. How much energy is that, and who gives or takes it when the motors work? This is the power of the motors.
  2. The column \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) appears in the equation, but the matrix \(\mat C\) is not determined by it alone. Why did we choose the one from the Christoffel symbols?
  3. How large can the mass matrix be as the arm moves through all configurations? Can we bound it from above and from below with numbers that do not depend on the configuration?

At the end there is also a short remark on how the equation depends on the masses and inertias.

27.9.1 The matrix \(\mat C\) and antisymmetry

We reach the answer to question 2 through energy. The kinetic energy of the robot is \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 8.86). By the product rule (lemma 1.81) and the symmetry of \(\mat M\) its derivative in time is

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q} ,\]

where \(\dot{\mat M}\) is the matrix of the time derivatives \(\dot m_{\mathrm{kj}} = \mathrm{d}m_{\mathrm{kj}}(\vek q(t))/\mathrm{d}t\) along the motion. We multiply the equation of motion by \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\) and get \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek g\). So

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau - \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek g + \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\big(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\big)\dot{\vek q} .\]

The kinetic energy changes with the power of the motors \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\) and the power of the weights \(-\dot{\vek q}^\T\vek g\) (definition 8.54), and with nothing else. The last term must therefore be zero. What must the matrix \(\mat C\) be for this to hold, and for which columns?

Let us compare two matrices that give the same column \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\).

Example 27.59 Two matrices with the same column Cq̇ (dve matriki z istim stolpcem Cq̇)

For arm A, \(\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{35}{4} + \frac52\cos q_2 & \frac{5}{12} + \frac54\cos q_2 \\ \frac{5}{12} + \frac54\cos q_2 & \frac{5}{12} \end{pmatrix}\) kg m² (theorem 27.52). With \(h = -m_2 l_1 l_{\mathrm{c}2}\sin q_2 = -1{,}25\sin q_2\) kg m² the matrix from the Christoffel symbols is \(\mat C = h\begin{pmatrix} \dot q_2 & \dot q_1 + \dot q_2 \\ -\dot q_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and another matrix is \(\mat C' = h\begin{pmatrix} 2\dot q_2 & \dot q_2 \\ -\dot q_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Both give

\[\mat C\dot{\vek q} = \mat C'\dot{\vek q} = h\begin{pmatrix} 2\dot q_1\dot q_2 + \dot q_2^2 \\ -\dot q_1^2 \end{pmatrix} ,\]

so the equation with one or the other describes the same motion. Take the position \(q_2 = 60^\circ\) and the velocity \(\dot{\vek q} = (1,\ 0{,}5)^\T\) rad/s, then \(h = -1{,}0825\) kg m². The matrices are

\[\mat C = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}5413 & -1{,}6238 \\ 1{,}0825 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat C' = \begin{pmatrix} -1{,}0825 & -0{,}5413 \\ 1{,}0825 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat C\dot{\vek q} = \mat C'\dot{\vek q} = \begin{pmatrix} -1{,}3532 \\ 1{,}0825 \end{pmatrix} \text{ N m} .\]

The time derivative of the mass matrix is \(\dot{\mat M} = -1{,}25\,\dot q_2\sin q_2\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and with these numbers \(\dot{\mat M} = \begin{pmatrix} -1{,}0825 & -0{,}5413 \\ -0{,}5413 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m²/s. Hence

\[\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2{,}7063 \\ -2{,}7063 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C' = \begin{pmatrix} 1{,}0825 & 0{,}5413 \\ -2{,}7063 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The first matrix is antisymmetric (definition 1.102), the second is not, since it has \(1{,}0825 \ne 0\) on the diagonal. For the velocity of the motion, \(\dot{\vek q}^\T(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C)\dot{\vek q} = 0\) in both cases (up to a rounding error of \(10^{-16}\)), as expected, because the columns \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) are equal. For the column \(\vek z = (1,\ 0)^\T\), however, \(\vek z^\T(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C')\vek z = 1{,}0825\) and \(\vek z^\T(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C)\vek z = 0\). We tell the two matrices apart only with a column that is not the velocity.

So energy does not tell \(\mat C\) from \(\mat C'\), because we test it only with \(\vek z = \dot{\vek q}\). The matrix from the Christoffel symbols satisfies more: the expression is zero for every column \(\vek z\). SPONG (PDF p. 217) uses this property later in the control of robots, which is outside this book. We prove it because it follows from the formula for \(\mat C\) itself and the symmetry of the mass matrix.

Theorem 27.60 Skew symmetry of Ṁ − 2C (antisimetričnost matrike Ṁ − 2C)

Let \(\mat M(\vek q)\) be the mass matrix of a robot with \(n\) degrees of freedom (theorem 27.52), symmetric with continuously differentiable entries \(m_{\mathrm{kj}}(\vek q)\). Let \(\mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) be the matrix with entries \(c_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\) are the Christoffel symbols (definition 27.55)

\[c_{\mathrm{ijk}} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial m_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} + \frac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ki}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\Big) .\]

For a motion \(\vek q(t)\) let \(\dot{\mat M}\) be the matrix of the time derivatives of the entries \(m_{\mathrm{kj}}(\vek q(t))\). Then the matrix \(\mat N = \dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\) is antisymmetric: \(n_{\mathrm{jk}} = -n_{\mathrm{kj}}\) for all \(j, k\). Therefore \(\vek z^\T\mat N\vek z = 0\) for every column \(\vek z\) of \(n\) numbers.

Proof

We must show that in the entry \(n_{\mathrm{kj}}\) all terms but two cancel, and that swapping \(j \leftrightarrow k\) changes the sign of those two. For this we need the symmetry of the mass matrix.

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1), \(\dot m_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial m_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. By the definition of the matrix \(\mat C\), \(n_{\mathrm{kj}} = \dot m_{\mathrm{kj}} - 2c_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n}\Big[\dfrac{\partial m_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} - \Big(\dfrac{\partial m_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} + \dfrac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ki}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} - \dfrac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\Big)\Big]\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The first and second term in the bracket cancel, what remains is
    \[n_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n}\Big[\frac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}} - \frac{\partial m_{\mathrm{ki}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}}\Big]\dot q_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{27.43}\]
  3. We swap the names \(k\) and \(j\) in equation (27.43): \(n_{\mathrm{jk}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\big[\partial m_{\mathrm{ik}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} - \partial m_{\mathrm{ji}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}\big]\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  4. The mass matrix is symmetric (definition 1.102), so \(m_{\mathrm{ik}} = m_{\mathrm{ki}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{ji}} = m_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Thus \(n_{\mathrm{jk}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\big[\partial m_{\mathrm{ki}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{j}} - \partial m_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}\big]\dot q_{\mathrm{i}} = -n_{\mathrm{kj}}\) by equation (27.43).
  5. For \(j = k\) step 4 gives \(n_{\mathrm{kk}} = -n_{\mathrm{kk}}\), so \(n_{\mathrm{kk}} = 0\). For any column \(\vek z\), \(\vek z^\T\mat N\vek z = \sum_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{j}} z_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{kj}} z_{\mathrm{j}}\). The terms \((k, j)\) and \((j, k)\) with \(j \ne k\) are \(z_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{kj}} z_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{jk}} z_{\mathrm{k}} = -z_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{kj}} z_{\mathrm{j}}\) and cancel, and the diagonal terms are zero.

Source: SPONG, Proposition 6.1 (PDF pp. 216-217) with equations (6.112) to (6.114), in our notation \(\mat M\) for \(D\). The same source stresses that the antisymmetry is guaranteed only by the matrix \(\mat C\) of equation (6.67) (PDF p. 217, which example 27.59 confirms). The proof follows SPONG (PDF pp. 216-217), step 5 is our write-up.

The proof showed that the matrix \(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\) is mirrored across the diagonal and has its sign changed, so no column receives power from it. Example 27.59 is a counterexample here: with the matrix \(\mat C'\) the column \(\mat C'\dot{\vek q}\) is right, but the antisymmetry fails. The matrix \(\mat C\) from here on is therefore always the one from the Christoffel symbols.

27.9.2 Power of the motors and passivity

Back to question 1. In the derivative of the kinetic energy the last term vanishes by theorem 27.60 with \(\vek z = \dot{\vek q}\). We add the potential \(V(\vek q)\) that the weights of the links produce, and the total energy \(E = T + V\). The property we are going to prove is called passivity, and the name comes from the theory of electrical circuits: a system is passive if it can be built from passive elements, resistors, capacitors and inductors, and a passive mechanical system from masses, springs and dampers (SPONG, PDF p. 217). Such a system cannot give out more energy than it had.

Corollary 27.61 Power of the motors and passivity (moč motorjev in pasivnost)

Let \(\vek q(t)\) be a twice continuously differentiable motion of the robot that satisfies the equation of motion of theorem 27.56, and \(E = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\dot{\vek q} + V(\vek q)\) its total energy.

  1. At every time \(t\), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\), the power of the motors (definition 8.54).
  2. If there is a number \(V_{\min}\) with \(V(\vek q) \ge V_{\min}\) for every \(\vek q\), then for every \(T > 0\)
    \[\int_0^T \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\,\mathrm{d}t \ge -\beta , \qquad \beta = E(0) - V_{\min} \ge 0 . \tag{27.44}\]
Proof

We must show that the derivative of the energy equals the power of the motors (item 1), then that the energy is bounded below and that the integral of the power is a difference of energies (item 2).

  1. By the product rule and the symmetry of the mass matrix, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\big) = \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q}\) (lemma 1.81, lemma 1.101). By the chain rule (lemma 8.29), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial\vek q} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek g\) (lemma 27.54).
  2. From the equation of motion, \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \vek\tau - \mat C\dot{\vek q} - \vek g\), so \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau - \dot{\vek q}^\T\mat C\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek g\).
  3. We add steps 1 and 2. The terms with \(\vek g\) cancel and \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau + \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\big(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\big)\dot{\vek q}\) remains.
  4. By theorem 27.60 the last term, \(\vek z^\T\mat N\vek z\) with \(\vek z = \dot{\vek q}\), is zero. This is item 1.
  5. The matrix \(\mat M\) is positive definite (theorem 27.52), so \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q} \ge 0\) and \(E(T) \ge V(\vek q(T)) \ge V_{\min}\).
  6. The function \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\) is continuous, because \(\vek q\) is twice continuously differentiable and \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\), \(\vek g\) are continuous. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40), \(\int_0^T\dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\,\mathrm{d}t = E(T) - E(0) \ge V_{\min} - E(0) = -\beta\), and \(\beta \ge 0\) because \(E(0) \ge V_{\min}\) by step 5.

Source: SPONG equations (6.115) to (6.119) (PDF pp. 217-218). In the proof SPONG says that \(H(T)\) is nonnegative, which holds when the potential is chosen so that \(V \ge 0\). We require this explicitly as the condition \(V \ge V_{\min}\), because \(\beta\) does not change if a constant is added to the potential. The proof by steps is ours.

Item 1 is the balance equation that theorem 8.106 also gives for any forces outside the potential, with \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = \tau_{\mathrm{s}}\). Here we reached it in the matrix form of the robot and saw what makes it possible: the antisymmetry of theorem 27.60. Item 2 says that the motors cannot take from the robot more energy than it has at the start above its lowest possible position, \(\beta\).

Let us test item 1 on a motion with motors. We move joint \(q_1\) of arm A cubically from \(0\) to \(90^\circ\) in \(1\) s, \(q_1(t) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(3t^2 - 2t^3)\), and hold the second joint at \(q_2 = 60^\circ\). The motors must give the torques from the equation of motion for this, and by theorem 27.56 we compute them at every time.

The upper panel has a time axis and a power axis. The curve in it shows the power \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\) of the motors. It is positive while the motors accelerate the arm and negative towards the end, when they brake it. The area between the curve and the axis is the work of the motors, above the axis a gain of the arm, below it a loss. At time \(0{,}5\) s the power is \(155{,}96\) W. The lower panel has the same time axis and an energy axis. In it is the energy \(E - E(0)\), which at the same time has a tangent with the slope \(155{,}96\) J/s, the same value as the power has. This is item 1 of the corollary with a number: the power and the derivative of the energy agree to \(3 \cdot 10^{-4}\) W (central differences with step \(0{,}5\) ms). The work of the motors over the whole motion is \(93{,}6116\) J and equals the difference of energies \(E(1) - E(0) = 104{,}2313 - 10{,}6196\) J, as step 6 of the proof says. At \(t \approx 0{,}29\) s the power is largest, \(262{,}36\) W. At \(t = 0{,}84\) s it is smallest, \(-80{,}35\) W: the motors brake the arm, and energy goes from the arm into the motor, which must give out or store this power.

Example 27.62 The most the motors can take (največ, kar motorji lahko vzamejo)

Take arm A from the start of the section, horizontal and at rest. Choose \(V = 0\) in this position. The potential is \(V = g\big[(m_1 l_{\mathrm{c}1} + m_2 l_1)\sin q_1 + m_2 l_{\mathrm{c}2}\sin(q_1 + q_2)\big]\) with \(l_{\mathrm{c}1} = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}25\) m, so \(V \ge V_{\min} = -g(m_1 l_{\mathrm{c}1} + m_2 l_1 + m_2 l_{\mathrm{c}2}) = -110{,}3625\) J, because a sine is at least \(-1\). This is the position \(\vek q = (-90^\circ, 0)^\T\), the arm hangs vertically. The energy at the start is \(E(0) = 0\), so \(\beta = 110{,}3625\) J. If the motors brake the arm from the horizontal position to the vertical one, where it is at rest, then by item 1 the work of the motors is \(\int \dot{\vek q}^\T\vek\tau\,\mathrm{d}t = E(T) - E(0) = V_{\min} - 0 = -110{,}3625\) J. The bound of equation (27.44) is reached: the motors take from the arm exactly all the energy it has above its lowest position, and no more. For the freely falling arm of figure 27.38, \(E(0) = 0\) and it stays so, to within \(2 \cdot 10^{-7}\) J in \(2\) s, which is one more check of the equation with \(\vek\tau = \vek 0\): at \(0{,}5\) s, \(T = 102{,}822\) J and \(V = -102{,}822\) J.

Example 27.63 No passivity if the potential has no lower bound (pasivnosti ni, če potencial nima spodnje meje)

The condition \(V \ge V_{\min}\) is necessary. Let a robot carry a weight of \(5\) kg on a vertical prismatic joint with coordinate \(z\), which in the model can be arbitrarily low (\(V = m g z\)). A motor lowering the load by a height difference \(s\) must pull upwards with the force \(m g = 49{,}05\) N, the load moves downwards, so its work is \(-49{,}05\,s\) J: \(-49{,}05\) J for \(s = 1\) m and \(-490{,}5\) J for \(s = 10\) m. The number \(\beta\) of equation (27.44) does not exist, because \(V\) is not bounded below. For arm A with revolute joints only, \(V\) is bounded because a sine is bounded, and \(\beta\) exists (example 27.62).

27.9.3 Bounds on the mass matrix

Now question 3. The mass matrix of arm A at \(q_2 = 60^\circ\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 1{,}0417 \\ 1{,}0417 & 0{,}4167 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m², and at \(q_2 = 0\) it is \(\begin{pmatrix} 11{,}25 & 1{,}6667 \\ 1{,}6667 & 0{,}4167 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m². How does it change with the configuration? Its eigenvalues (definition 2.59) are real, because the matrix is symmetric (corollary 10.17), and we compute them for every \(q_2\).

On the horizontal axis the angle \(q_2\) runs from \(0\) to \(360^\circ\), because after \(360^\circ\) the arm is in the same position again. On the vertical axis are the eigenvalues in kg m². The upper curve is the larger eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\), the lower one the smaller \(\lambda_1\). Both lie everywhere between the bounds. The orange dashed lines are the numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\), which the lemma below obtains from the smallest and largest value of a quadratic form. The largest eigenvalue is at \(q_2 = 0\), when the arm is stretched out: then \(M_{11} = 11{,}25\) kg m² is the inertia of both links about joint 1 (bar \(1\): \(m_1 l_1^2/3 = 3{,}3333\), bar \(2\): \(m_2(l_1 + l_{\mathrm{c}2})^2 + J_{\mathrm{G}2} = 7{,}9167\) kg m²), since then the second link is farthest from the axis. At \(q_2 = 180^\circ\), when the arm is folded, \(\lambda_2 = 6{,}3667\) kg m². The smallest eigenvalue is also at \(q_2 = 0\), \(0{,}1661\) kg m², with the eigencolumn \(\vek z = (0{,}149,\ -0{,}989)^\T\): the cheapest motion is mostly a turn of the light second link with a small opposite turn of the first. That \(\lambda_1\) does not exceed \(M_{22} = 0{,}4167\) kg m² is given by theorem 10.13, in which we put the mass matrix of the robot in place of \(\mat K\) and the identity matrix \(\mat I\) in place of \(\mat M\) (the smallest value of the quadratic form on unit columns), since at \(\vek z = (0, 1)^\T\) it equals \(M_{22}\).

The bounds of the figure do not hold for arm A only.

Lemma 27.64 Bounds on the mass matrix of a robot with revolute joints (meje masne matrike robota z vrtljivimi sklepi)

Let a robot have \(n\) revolute joints and let \(\mat M(\vek q)\) be its mass matrix (theorem 27.52). Then there are numbers \(0 < \lambda_{\mathrm{m}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\), independent of \(\vek q\), such that for every \(\vek q\) and every column \(\vek z\) of \(n\) numbers

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\,|\vek z|^2 \le \vek z^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\vek z \le \lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\,|\vek z|^2 . \tag{27.45}\]

Every eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat M(\vek q)\) lies in the interval \([\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{M}}]\).

Proof

We must show that the quadratic form attains its smallest and largest value on a bounded closed set, that because of periodicity this set covers all configurations, and that the eigenvalues follow from the quadratic form.

  1. For a revolute joint \(i\) the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) enters as in the matrix \(\mat R_{\mathrm{z},\theta_\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}} = q_{\mathrm{i}} + \text{const.}\) (section 27.4, lemma 27.28), and appears only in \(\cos q_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\sin q_{\mathrm{i}}\). By theorem 27.52 the mass matrix is built from these matrices and their products, so its entries are continuous functions (corollary 1.64) with period \(2\pi\) in every \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. Let \(S = \{\vek z : |\vek z| = 1\}\) and \(K = [0, 2\pi]^n \times S\). The set \(K\) is bounded. It is closed, because the length is continuous, so the limit of a sequence of points of length \(1\) has length \(1\) (definition 1.74).
  3. The function \(f(\vek q, \vek z) = \vek z^\T\mat M(\vek q)\vek z\) is continuous on \(K\) by step 1. By theorem 8.26 there are points where \(f\) is smallest and largest: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = f(\vek q^*, \vek z^*)\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}} = f(\vek q^{**}, \vek z^{**})\). The matrix \(\mat M(\vek q^*)\) is positive definite and \(\vek z^* \ne \vek 0\), so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\).
  4. For any \(\vek q\) let \(\vek q'\) be the point in \([0, 2\pi]^n\) with \(q_{\mathrm{i}}' = q_{\mathrm{i}} - 2\pi\lfloor q_{\mathrm{i}}/2\pi\rfloor\). By step 1, \(\mat M(\vek q) = \mat M(\vek q')\), so for \(|\vek z| = 1\) the value \(f(\vek q, \vek z) = f(\vek q', \vek z) \in [\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{M}}]\). For \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\), \(\vek z^\T\mat M\vek z = |\vek z|^2 f(\vek q, \vek z/|\vek z|)\), which gives equation (27.45).
  5. The matrix \(\mat M(\vek q)\) is symmetric, so by corollary 10.17 it has an eigenpair \(\mat M\vek\varphi = \lambda\vek\varphi\) with \(|\vek\varphi| = 1\). Then \(\lambda = \vek\varphi^\T\mat M\vek\varphi \in [\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}, \lambda_{\mathrm{M}}]\) by equation (27.45).

Source: SPONG, section 6.5.2, equations (6.120) and (6.121) (PDF p. 218). SPONG says that \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\) can be found because the entries are bounded, since they are sines and cosines. The proof with the extreme value theorem is ours.

For arm A, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}1661\) kg m² and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}} = 11{,}5006\) kg m² (figure 27.40, both at \(q_2 = 0\)). The kinetic energy at a velocity of length \(|\dot{\vek q}| = 1\) rad/s is therefore between \(\tfrac12\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}083\) J and \(\tfrac12\lambda_{\mathrm{M}} = 5{,}750\) J, whatever the configuration. The largest eigenvalue stays finite, the smallest positive: the mass matrix does not approach infinity or a singular matrix as the configuration changes.

Example 27.65 A prismatic joint breaks the bound (prizmatični sklep razbije mejo)

The hypothesis of revolute joints is necessary. Take a planar robot with a revolute joint \(q_1\) and a prismatic joint \(d\) on a rod that turns with the joint \(q_1\). Our choice (not from a source): the inertia of the rod about the axis is \(0{,}5\) kg m² and a point mass of \(2\) kg sits at distance \(d\) from the axis. The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\big(0{,}5 + 2d^2\big)\dot q_1^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 2\,\dot d^2\), so \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}\big(0{,}5 + 2d^2,\ 2\big)\), the first entry in kg m², since it belongs to a turn, the second in kg, since it belongs to a shift. For \(d = 0\), \(0{,}5\), \(1\), \(2\), \(5\), \(10\) m, \(M_{11} = 0{,}5\), \(1\), \(2{,}5\), \(8{,}5\), \(50{,}5\), \(200{,}5\) kg m². For \(\vek z = (1, 0)^\T\), \(\vek z^\T\mat M\vek z = 0{,}5 + 2d^2\), which grows without bound with \(d\). The bound \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\) does not exist, because \(d\) is not an angle: it is not periodic and we cannot confine it to \([0, 2\pi]\). If the joint has a stroke, say \(0 \le d \le 1\) m, then \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}}\) is finite, but it depends on the stroke, not only on the robot.

27.9.4 What depends on the parameters, and what follows

The equation of motion depends on the masses and inertias of the links. For arm A the entries of \(\mat M\) and \(\vek g\) are functions of five numbers, which SPONG works out for his planar arm on PDF pp. 219-220 (equations (6.123) to (6.133), in other notation). Let \(a = m_1 l_{\mathrm{c}1}^2 + m_2(l_1^2 + l_{\mathrm{c}2}^2) + J_{\mathrm{G}1} + J_{\mathrm{G}2} = 8{,}75\) kg m², \(b = m_2 l_1 l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 1{,}25\) kg m², \(d = m_2 l_{\mathrm{c}2}^2 + J_{\mathrm{G}2} = 0{,}4167\) kg m², \(p_1 = m_1 l_{\mathrm{c}1} + m_2 l_1 = 10\) kg m and \(p_2 = m_2 l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 1{,}25\) kg m. Then \(\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} a + 2b\cos q_2 & d + b\cos q_2 \\ d + b\cos q_2 & d \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\vek g = g\,(p_1\cos q_1 + p_2\cos(q_1 + q_2),\ p_2\cos(q_1 + q_2))^\T\). Therefore \(\vek\tau = \mat Y(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, \ddot{\vek q})\,\vek\Theta\) with \(\vek\Theta = (a, b, d, p_1, p_2)^\T\) and

\[\mat Y = \begin{pmatrix} \ddot q_1 & \cos q_2\,(2\ddot q_1 + \ddot q_2) - \sin q_2\,(2\dot q_1\dot q_2 + \dot q_2^2) & \ddot q_2 & g\cos q_1 & g\cos(q_1 + q_2) \\ 0 & \cos q_2\,\ddot q_1 + \sin q_2\,\dot q_1^2 & \ddot q_1 + \ddot q_2 & 0 & g\cos(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix} .\]

For \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)^\T\), \(\dot{\vek q} = (1, 0{,}5)^\T\) rad/s and \(\ddot{\vek q} = (2, -1)^\T\) rad/s², \(\mat Y\vek\Theta\) gives the torques \((102{,}562,\ 2{,}749)^\T\) N m that the direct equation gives. The column \(\vek\Theta\) holds the parameters and the matrix \(\mat Y\) only the motion: the equation is linear in the parameters. Without gravity three parameters, \(a\), \(b\) and \(d\), are enough. SPONG states this property (section 6.5.3, PDF pp. 218-219) for every robot, with the matrix \(\mat Y\) that he calls the regressor, and at most \(10\) parameters per link: the mass, six entries of the inertia tensor and three coordinates of the centre of mass. We do not give the general proof here: for arm A we checked the property by computation, for a general robot we cite it from the source as not proved. A minimal set of independent parameters is hard to find in general (SPONG, PDF p. 219, and the sources on PDF p. 236).

Two more details from the same source, which we only mention. For a planar robot with a parallelogram (SPONG, PDF pp. 215-216), for a chosen ratio of parameters (equation (6.108)) the mass matrix is diagonal and constant, there are no Coriolis and centrifugal terms, and each joint feels gravity separately. The equations of motion are then separate joint by joint, which SPONG gives as the reason for the popularity of the parallelogram in industrial robots. Deriving the equations of motion with the Newton-Euler recursion instead of Lagrange's equations (SPONG, section 6.6, PDF pp. 220-230) is mentioned in section 27.8. That the two ways give the same equation SPONG only states with a reference (PDF pp. 220 and 236) and does not prove.

We have answered the three questions. The matrix \(\mat C\) from the Christoffel symbols is the one for which \(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\) is antisymmetric (theorem 27.60, proved from the symmetry of the mass matrix). Therefore the power of the motors is the derivative of the total energy and the arm cannot give out more energy than it had above its lowest position (corollary 27.61). The mass matrix of an arm with revolute joints is bounded from above and from below by constant numbers (lemma 27.64). What is not proved here is cited: linearity in the parameters for a general robot, the Newton-Euler recursion and its equality with Lagrange's equation. Flexible links, a spinning rotor and closed chains are not part of this section, and the connections to the chapters that treat them are in the last section of the chapter.
