The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

In the earlier chapters we computed stresses and displacements for a given structure. Here we ask the opposite question: what should the structure be, so that it is the best one? The wall truss of chapters 4 and 7 was computed with bars of cross-section 100 mm\(^2\). Its largest stress is 166,7 MPa, and the yield limit of the steel is 220 MPa (chapter 3). The bars are therefore stronger than they need to be. By how much may we thin them, so that as little steel as possible is used, and how do we find it by computing instead of by trying?

The chapter is named after two words. Optimization (optimizacija) comes from the Latin optimus, the best: to optimize means to find the best among the possibilities. Structure (konstrukcija) comes from the Latin construere, to build together: it is a load-carrying assembly of parts, built so that it carries its load. It matters because the weight and the price of a structure are proportional to the amount of material, and because the first idea we try is almost never the best.

The chapter promises three things. First we write the question "what is best" in a form that a computer understands, and say when a best solution exists at all. Then we prove how the best solution is recognized, and show the procedures that find it: for trusses, for a rod whose cross-section varies along its length, and for a plate in which we search for where the material should be at all. For every procedure we say what was neglected and when it fails.
