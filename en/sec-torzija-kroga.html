Chapter 16 Torsion

16.1 Torsion of the circle (torzija kroga)

A steel shaft with a diameter of \(40\) mm and a length of \(1\) m is clamped into a wall at its left end, and at its right end we turn it with a moment of \(50\) N m about its axis, like a bolt with a wrench. How far does the right end turn? (Figure 16.4 is a little further down.) Where in the shaft is the material loaded most? And how would we find the answer for a shaft that has a rectangle instead of a circle? This phenomenon is called torsion (torzija, in Slovenian also zvijanje), from the Latin torquere, to twist. We begin the chapter with the circle, because there the ideas of elementary strength of materials are exact and every step can be tested. Then we change the cross section and see what breaks.

From history

Coulomb measured the twisting of a wire. Chapter 1 said that from the oscillation of a metal cylinder on a wire he determined how the wire resists twisting. Let us now add what he derived from it. For the oscillation he assumed that the moment with which the twisted wire resists is proportional to the angle of rotation \(\varphi\), and he obtained the period of oscillation. The experiment showed that the period does not depend on the angle if the angle is not too large, and from this he concluded that the proportionality of the moment to the angle is correct (TIMO, PDF p. 60). The reason is that a cylinder with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{v}}\) on a wire that gives the moment \(c\,\varphi\) obeys \(J_{\mathrm{v}}\,\ddot\varphi = -c\,\varphi\), and its period does not depend on the amplitude, as we saw for torsional oscillation in chapter 9. Then he took wires of the same metal but of different lengths and diameters, and obtained the equation for the moment

\[M = \frac{\mu\,d^4}{l}\,\varphi , \tag{16.1}\]

where \(l\) is the length of the wire, \(d\) its diameter and \(\mu\) a constant of the material (TIMO, PDF p. 61, equation (i), printed page 53). The diameter thus stands in the fourth power: a wire with twice the diameter is \(2^4 = 16\) times stiffer at the same angle and length, and a wire twice as long is twice as soft.

Equation (16.1) is the law that this section explains: it hides the fourth power of the diameter and the constant \(\mu\), which we shall express by the shear modulus of the material. Before we start, let us say precisely what we compute.

Definition 16.1 Torsion of a prism (torzija prizme)

A prism (prizma) is a body obtained when a cross section \(S\), a figure in a plane with an area (definition 4.27), is moved by a length \(L\) perpendicular to itself. The axis \(x\) runs along the prism, in the cross section we measure the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) as in chapter 5, with the origin \(O\) in the cross section. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with shear modulus \(G\) (definition 3.17), there is no body force, and the lateral surface is free, that is, without forces. On the two end sections only surface forces act (stress vector, definition 2.20) that have zero resultant and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\): the moment about the axis \(x\) is \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), the moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are zero. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the moment of the forces on the section with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), with respect to the direction \(+x\), and on the opposite section its moment is \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}\).

The torsion is uniform (enakomerna torzija) if the displacement in the plane of the section at every \(x\) agrees with a rigid rotation of the section about the point \(O\) by a small angle \(\varphi(x)\),

\[u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\varphi(x)\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varphi(x)\,y , \qquad \varphi(x) = \alpha\,x , \tag{16.2}\]

with a constant number \(\alpha\), which we call the twist per unit length (zasuk na enoto dolžine), unit rad/m. The displacement along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), is not prescribed: in this section we test \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), in the following ones we shall look for it.

Source: SADD section 9.3 (PDF p. 246) prescribes the same displacement in the plane and an unknown function for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the axis \(z\) along the bar, the cross section in the plane \(x\), \(y\) and the twist per unit length \(\alpha\). Here the axes are moved to ours (axis \(x\) along the bar). The definition is not from the source.

The words each have a reason. A prism is a body with a constant cross section \(S\) along its whole length \(L\), as bars and shafts have. The twist per unit length is called so because \(\alpha = \varphi(x)/x\) is the angle of rotation of the section divided by the distance \(x\) of this section from the start, so the angle by which the section turns for every metre of length. The letter \(\varphi\) is here the rotation of the section as in chapter 13 and \(\alpha\) the twist per unit length, and we use no other meaning of these two letters in this chapter. We write the moment about the axis \(x\) as \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) as in chapters 5 and 13, because the letter \(T\) would be taken by temperature.

Why do we expect a constant \(\alpha\)? Let us draw the shaft and think.

The hatched line is the wall into which the shaft is clamped. From it along the axis \(x\) extends the shaft, a cylinder of length \(L\), measured from the wall. At the right end we turn it with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis. Before we load it, we draw a straight thread on the surface along the shaft. Let us cut off in thought the right part of the shaft, from the section at \(x\) to the end. Only the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the end and the moment of the forces of the left part across the section act on this part, the lateral surface is free. The moment about the axis \(x\) must be in equilibrium, therefore the moment across every section equals \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\). Every piece of the shaft with the same length and cross section thus carries the same moment. Two equal pieces of the same material under the same load deform equally, so the right end of every piece turns by the same angle with respect to its left end. The rotation of the section \(\varphi(x)\) therefore grows from \(\varphi(0) = 0\) at the wall with the same rate \(\alpha\) along the shaft: the thread that was straight bends into a helix after loading. At the wall the two lines coincide, towards the right the orange one rises above the grey one, and its height at the right end is \(R\sin\varphi(L)\), which shows the rotation of the end.

The model has five idealizations. For each let us say why we may take it, when it fails and what it does to the result, with numbers for the shaft from the beginning of the section.

  • Linear elasticity. Hooke's law holds as long as the material does not yield. On the surface of the shaft, where the shear is largest, the stress at \(50\) N m will be \(3{,}98\) MPa, while first yielding comes at \(110\) MPa, that is at a moment of \(1382\) N m (example 16.5), almost \(28\) times larger. Above this moment the outer layer yields, the rotation grows faster than our equations say and stays after unloading.
  • Free lateral surface. Nothing touches the lateral surface of the shaft and nothing holds it back, so the moment is the same in every section. It fails for a drill that the wood holds back along its whole length, or for a shaft in a tight bushing: there the surroundings turn the lateral surface back and the moment varies along the shaft. This case is treated in section 16.1.1, where a shaft with the same total moment, spread along the length, gets half the rotation of the end.
  • Loading of the end. The theorem below requires the forces at the right end to be distributed over the section in one exactly given way. The real load is a wrench, a gear or a clamping in a wall. Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) says that another distribution with the same moment differs only near the end, over a length of about one diameter. For our shaft this is \(40\) mm of \(L = 1000\) mm at each end, so \(4\,\%\) of the length. We state the principle here and do not prove it.
  • Uniform twisting. The twist per unit length \(\alpha\) is the same everywhere, so every section deforms like its neighbour. For the circle this holds also at the wall, because the sections, as we shall prove, stay plane and the wall hinders them in nothing. For sections that warp, the clamping hinders the warping and near it the torsion is no longer uniform. This nonuniform torsion (neenakomerna torzija) we do not treat here, see COOK (PDF pp. 42 and 43) and ONATE2 section 4.10 (PDF pp. 227 to 246).

The fifth idealization is the small rotation, and it deserves its own figure.

We look along the axis of the shaft: the point \(O\) is the axis and the thin arc is a part of the circle of the surface. The point \(P\) lies on the surface, \(R\) from the axis. When the section turns by the angle \(\varphi\), the radius \(OP\) rotates and the point goes along the arc to \(P'\). The displacement (16.2), however, is for the point \(P = (R, 0)\) equal to \((u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}}) = (0, \varphi R)\): it moves the point straight up along the tangent by \(R\varphi\) to \(P''\). The difference between \(P'\) and \(P''\) has two parts. The true point rises only by \(R\sin\varphi\), not by \(R\varphi\), and in doing so it comes closer to the axis \(z\) by \(R(1 - \cos\varphi)\), which the displacement along the tangent does not have. For our shaft with \(R = 20\) mm and \(50\) N m we have \(\varphi(L) = 0{,}00264\) rad:

\[R\varphi = 0{,}05278\ \text{mm} , \qquad R\,(\varphi - \sin\varphi) = 6 \cdot 10^{-8}\ \text{mm} , \qquad R\,(1 - \cos\varphi) = 7{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{mm} .\]

The larger of the two errors is \(0{,}13\,\%\) of the displacement, the ratio \((1 - \cos\varphi)/\sin\varphi = \tan(\varphi/2)\). The small rotation is therefore justified as long as \(\tan(\varphi/2)\) is small against \(1\). It fails for a rubber hose that we twist by hand: at \(\varphi = 30^\circ\) of the figure the ratio is \(\tan 15^\circ = 27\,\%\) and the theory of small strains is no longer usable.

Let us enlarge the view. On the surface of the shaft take a window with a square of side \(h\), enlarged on the right. Before loading its edges are perpendicular. One runs along the axis, the other along the circumference. After loading, the section at \(x\) and the section at \(x + h\) are turned with respect to the wall by \(\alpha x\) and \(\alpha(x + h)\), therefore with respect to each other by \(\alpha h\). A point on the surface that is \(R\) from the axis is thereby shifted along the circumference by \(R\alpha h\), this is the shift. The edge in the direction of the axis therefore tilts by the angle \(\gamma\) with respect to the original direction, the sheared square is no longer rectangular, and

\[\tan\gamma = \frac{R\alpha h}{h} = R\alpha , \qquad \gamma \approx R\alpha , \tag{16.3}\]

for a small angle. For our shaft \(R\alpha = 5{,}28 \cdot 10^{-5}\), and \(\tan\gamma\) differs from \(\gamma\) by the fraction \(\gamma^2/3 = 9 \cdot 10^{-10}\), so we do not distinguish them. In the figure the angle \(\gamma\) is drawn \(6600\) times larger. The change of the right angle between two edges is the shear strain (chapter 1, definition 1.121), in the engineering form \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon\) as in definition 3.17. With it the stress on the surface is \(\tau = G\gamma = G R\alpha\). The same reasoning holds for a square in the depth of the shaft, at the distance \(r\) from the axis: there the shift is only \(r\alpha h\), because a point closer to the axis moves less at the same rotation, so \(\gamma = r\alpha\) and \(\tau = G\alpha r\). This is the estimate that the theorem below will prove.

So we arrived at the displacement (16.2) with \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Can this really be a solution? The displacement was obtained by guessing, and this is exactly the semi-inverse method (definition 11.11): we choose a field, check the field equations and the boundary conditions, and then theorem 11.4 guarantees that there is no other solution. Before we do so, let us see how much moment such a stress carries. The answer tells where the fourth power in Coulomb's equation (16.1) comes from.

Let us draw the section of the shaft, a disc of radius \(R\), with the axes \(y\) and \(z\). In it we choose a thin ring at the distance \(r\) from the axis, with width \(h\). By the estimate above the stress on it is \(G\alpha r\), everywhere along the ring. Closer to the axis it is smaller, at half the radius half as large. How much does the ring contribute to the moment about the axis? Three things are multiplied:

  • the stress, force per unit area, is \(G\alpha r\),
  • the area of the ring is circumference times width, \(2\pi r\,h\), exactly for the mean radius of the ring,
  • the lever arm of this force to the axis is \(r\).

The moment of the ring is therefore \(G\alpha r \cdot 2\pi r h \cdot r = 2\pi G\alpha\,r^3 h\). The radius enters three times: farther from the axis the stress is larger, the ring longer and the lever arm longer. We add the moments of the rings from the axis to the edge. The sum of the numbers \(r^3 h\) over the rings approximates the integral \(\int_0^R r^3\,\mathrm{d}r = R^4/4\), so the fourth power comes from three factors \(r\) and the summation. The whole moment is

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\pi G\alpha \cdot \frac{R^4}{4} = G\alpha\,\frac{\pi R^4}{2} .\]

Let us test with numbers for \(R = 20\) mm. The number that multiplies \(G\alpha\) is the sum of \(2\pi r^3 h\) over the rings, with \(r\) at the middle of each:

rings width \(h\) sum of \(2\pi r^3 h\) difference to \(\pi R^4/2 = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\)
\(5\) \(4\) mm \(246\,301\) mm\(^4\) \(2{,}0\,\%\)
\(20\) \(1\) mm \(251\,013\) mm\(^4\) \(0{,}125\,\%\)

Already five rings miss by only \(2\,\%\). Let us draw the five rings of the section and stack their shares in two bars of equal height. The left bar shows the shares of the area, from the axis outward \(4\), \(12\), \(20\), \(28\) and \(36\,\%\), because the area \(2\pi r h\) of a ring grows in proportion to its middle radius \(r\). The right bar shows the shares of the sum \(2\pi r^3 h\), which grow with \(r^3\). The outer ring between \(16\) and \(20\) mm has only \(36\,\%\) of the area, but alone contributes \(59{,}5\,\%\) of the sum. The material at the edge carries most of the moment. The inner four rings, a disc of radius \(16\) mm, have \(64\,\%\) of the area and only \(40{,}5\,\%\) of the sum, because their material lies close to the axis. This view adds what the theorem below does not say: the reason for \(R^4\) and for a hollow shaft being stiffer than a solid one for its mass. The outer ring by itself is a tube with diameters \(40\) and \(32\) mm. With \(36\,\%\) of the mass of the solid shaft it has about \(59\,\%\) of its stiffness, exactly \(0{,}590\) (example 16.5 at the end).

The number \(\pi R^4/2\) is the integral \(\int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\) over the disc, the polar second moment of the circle. Chapter 5 took \(I = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \pi R^4/4\) from a source, half of this number (lemma 16.2 below). The reasoning with rings guessed it, but did not prove it: we assumed that the area of the disc may be summed by radii and angles. The circle is not a figure made of rectangles, so there is no computation by rectangles, and the proof requires polar coordinates.

Let us first draw the coordinate axes of the section and the circle of radius \(R\) about the origin. A point of the circle is written with the distance \(r\) from the centre and the angle \(\theta\) from the axis \(y\), \(y = r\cos\theta\), \(z = r\sin\theta\). The angle \(\theta\) and the distance \(r\) are thus coordinates of the point, and every pair \((r, \theta)\) from the rectangle on the right gives a point of the circle. This assignment, \(\Phi(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\), is shown by the arrows between the two pictures: \(\Phi\) takes the rectangle onto the circle, the inverse map \(\Phi^{-1}\) takes a point of the circle back into the rectangle. If the angle \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), the point comes back to the same place: the side \(\theta = 0\) and the side \(\theta = 2\pi\) of the rectangle are mapped onto the same segment, the radius on the axis \(y\), which we therefore cut out of the circle. Let us choose a piece of the circle between the radii \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) and between the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\). Its preimage in the rectangle has area \((r_2 - r_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\), and the piece itself has the area of the difference of two circular sectors, \(\tfrac12(\theta_2 - \theta_1)(r_2^2 - r_1^2)\). Since \(r_2^2 - r_1^2 = (r_2 - r_1)(r_2 + r_1)\), this is \(\bar r\,(r_2 - r_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\) with the mean radius \(\bar r\). The piece is therefore \(\bar r\) times larger than its preimage: the larger, the farther from the centre it lies. This factor, equal to \(r\) at the point \((r, \theta)\), is the Jacobian determinant of the map \(\Phi\). Its matrix of derivatives and determinant are

\[\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} , \qquad J_\Phi = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r ,\]

as in lemma 11.15, item 1, only without the coordinate \(z\). Theorem 4.59 on the change of variables uses this factor exactly.

Lemma 16.2 Second moments of a disc and an annulus (vztrajnostni momenti kroga in kolobarja)

Let \(K\) be a disc of radius \(R\) with centre at the origin of the plane \(y\), \(z\), and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) a disc of radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}} < R\) with the same centre. Both are sets with an area (definition 4.27), since they are closed, bounded and their boundary is a circle. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{K}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \qquad I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{\pi R^4}{4} , \tag{16.4}\]

the polar second moment of area (polarni vztrajnostni moment) \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}}\) is

\[I_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{\pi R^4}{2} , \tag{16.5}\]

and for the annulus \(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}\) it is

\[I_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\big(R^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4\big) . \tag{16.6}\]
Proof

We must show that the integral of \(r^2\) over the disc can be computed with radii and angles, and the other relations follow by reflection. We cut the disc into a piece with an exact computation and a remainder, a small disc at the axis and a narrow sector, whose area is arbitrarily small. The remainder is needed because the map \(\Phi\) is not invertible at the centre and on the radius \(\theta = 0\).

  1. Let \(0 < \rho < R\) and \(0 < \delta < \pi/2\) be numbers, the radius of the small disc and the half angle of the sector. Let \(\Phi(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\) on the open set \(D = (\rho/2, 2R) \times (0, 2\pi)\). The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable and has, as computed at figure 16.8, the Jacobian determinant \(J_\Phi = r > 0\). The map is injective: from \(r\cos\theta = r'\cos\theta'\) and \(r\sin\theta = r'\sin\theta'\) it follows that \(r^2 = r'^2\), so \(r = r'\), and then \(\cos\theta = \cos\theta'\), \(\sin\theta = \sin\theta'\), which in \((0, 2\pi)\) gives \(\theta = \theta'\).
  2. The rectangle \(W = [\rho, R] \times [\delta, 2\pi - \delta]\) lies in \(D\) and is a set with an area, its image \(\Phi(W)\) is the disc without the small disc of radius \(\rho\) and without the sector with angle \(2\delta\) around the radius \(\theta = 0\). In the rectangle of the variables \(r\) and \(\theta\), \(W\) is a rectangle like the preimage of the piece. By theorem 4.59 on the change of variables, for the function \(f(y, z) = y^2 + z^2 = r^2\),
    \[\int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{W}} r^2\cdot r\,\mathrm{d}A = (2\pi - 2\delta)\int_\rho^R r^3\,\mathrm{d}r = (\pi - \delta)\,\frac{R^4 - \rho^4}{2} ,\]
    since the integral over a rectangle is the product of two integrals (theorem 4.9) and \(r^4/4\) is an indefinite integral of \(r^3\) (fundamental theorem of calculus, 3.55).
  3. Let \(N\) be the closure of the set \(K \setminus \Phi(W)\). The set \(N\) is closed and bounded and its boundary consists of circular arcs and segments, so it is a set with area. A point of \(N\) is at most \(\rho\) from the origin or has the polar angle in \([-\delta, \delta]\). The former lie in the square \(Q = [-\rho, \rho]^2\) with area \(4\rho^2\). The latter have \(0 \le y \le R\) and \(|z| = r|\sin\theta| \le R\sin\delta \le R\delta\) and lie in the rectangle \(P = [0, R] \times [-R\delta, R\delta]\) with area \(2R^2\delta\). The square and the rectangle overlap, so we may not simply add their areas. The closure of the part of the rectangle \(P\) outside the square \(Q\) is a union of at most three rectangles and, as a subset of \(P\), has area at most \(2R^2\delta\) (monotonicity, lemma 4.32, item 2). It has no interior points in common with the square \(Q\), so the union of the two, which contains \(N\), has area at most \(4\rho^2 + 2R^2\delta\) (additivity, item 4). By monotonicity the area of the set \(N\) is at most \(4\rho^2 + 2R^2\delta\).
  4. The sets \(\Phi(W)\) and \(N\) (the grey figures cover it) have union \(K\) and no interior point of one lies in the other. By additivity (lemma 4.32, item 4) and by the bound (item 3, \(|f| \le R^2\) on \(K\)),
    \[\Big|\int_{\mathrm{K}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\Big| = \Big|\int_{\mathrm{N}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\Big| \le R^2\,\big(4\rho^2 + 2R^2\delta\big) .\]
  5. Let \(\rho \to 0\) and \(\delta \to 0\). The right-hand side of step 4 tends to zero, and the integral of step 2 tends to \(\pi R^4/2\). So \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi R^4/2\). For \(R = 20\) mm, \(\rho = 0{,}01\) mm and \(\delta = 0{,}01\) rad the integral over the blue region of step 2 equals \(250\,527\) mm\(^4\). It differs from the exact value \(251\,327\) mm\(^4\) by \(800\) mm\(^4\), the contribution of the sector and the small disc, and the estimate of step 4 for it is \(3200\) mm\(^4\).
  6. The map \((y, z) \mapsto (z, y)\), the reflection across the bisector of the axes \(y\) and \(z\), is linear with \(|J| = 1\) and maps the disc \(K\) onto itself. By theorem 4.59, \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}} = I_{\mathrm{p}}/2 = \pi R^4/4\). The map \((y, z) \mapsto (-y, z)\) has \(|J| = 1\) and maps \(K\) onto itself, therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} (-y)\,\mathrm{d}A\) and this integral is zero. The same holds for \(z\).
  7. The annulus \(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) have union \(K\) and no interior point of one lies in the other. By additivity \(I_{\mathrm{p}}(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}) = I_{\mathrm{p}}(K) - I_{\mathrm{p}}(K_{\mathrm{i}}) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(R^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4)\).

Source: The value \(I = \pi R^4/4\) is in ONATE2 (Fig. 1.3, PDF p. 35), and the polar moment \(J = \pi c^4/2\) with radius \(c\) in SADD (D.2, PDF p. 573). The computation with polar coordinates and with reflection is not from the sources.

Chapter 5 took \(I = \pi R^4/4\) for a circle of diameter \(d = 32\) mm: \(\pi \cdot 16^4/4 = 51\,472\) mm\(^4\). Now it is proved. The polar moment of a shaft with \(d = 40\) mm is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi \cdot 20^4/2 = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\), and of a hollow shaft with \(d_{\mathrm{o}} = 40\) mm and \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 32\) mm it is \(\pi(20^4 - 16^4)/2 = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\).

One requirement remains, the one on which the whole chapter stands: the lateral surface of the shaft is free. What does this mean for the stress on the section?

Let us look at the section along the axis \(x\), with the axes \(y\) and \(z\), and in it at a small cube of material right at the edge, on the axis \(z\). The shear stress on the section is there a vector in the plane of the section. We resolve it into the part along the edge, \(\tau_{\mathrm{t}}\), and the part across the edge, \(\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\), which points towards the free surface. We claim that \(\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\) must be zero.

Why? Let us look at the cube from the side, so that the axis \(x\) runs to the right and the axis \(z\) upwards. Take first a cube in the interior of the shaft. Its right and left faces are pieces of two neighbouring sections. On the right one the stress \(\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\) acts upwards, on the left one, which faces the opposite way, equally large downwards: this is a couple of forces that would turn the cube counterclockwise. The cube is at rest, so an opposite couple must also act on it. Only the upper and lower face can give it, with an equally large shear stress along the axis \(x\). This is the content of the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41): the shear on the face of the section in the direction \(z\) equals the shear on the face with normal \(z\) in the direction \(x\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\).

Now the same cube just under the surface. Its upper face is a piece of the free lateral surface: nothing acts on it, the stress vector is \(\vek t = \vek 0\). The shear along the axis on it is missing, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = 0\). By the symmetry of stress, at the same point then also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\): the couple on the faces of the section has no couple to balance it, so it must not be there. The shear on the lower face also tends to zero as we make the cube smaller, because this face approaches the free one.

The conclusion holds for every section and every free surface: the shear vector at the edge of the section points along the edge, there is none across the edge. For the circle the edge is a circle, so the vector must be tangent there. At a corner where two free faces meet at an angle smaller than \(180^\circ\) (as in a rectangle), it must not point across either of them, so it is zero there. This condition will separate the circle from the rectangle.

Now the theorem. The guess is the displacement (16.2) with \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), we omit nothing. What the theorem will claim about the stress is shown in figure 16.10.

We draw the section of the solid shaft and its axes. The theorem will claim that the stress at a point of the section is a shear vector with magnitude \(G\alpha r\) and direction perpendicular to the radius, as we estimated with the square on the surface. On the radius on the axis \(y\) the arrows grow from zero at the centre to \(G\alpha R = 3{,}98\) MPa at the edge, which is a straight line in \(r\). On the circumference the arrow at every point has the same length and points in the direction of circulation, as the vector \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\) shows: it lies along the edge and has no part across it, exactly what the free surface requires. On the right is the tube with outer radius \(R\) and inner radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\), on the inner and outer circle the stresses are \(5{,}39\) and \(6{,}74\) MPa (at the same moment it is larger because the \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the tube is smaller). The distance \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a cavity where there is no material and no stress.

Theorem 16.3 Torsion of a circle and an annulus (torzija kroga in kolobarja)

Let the prism of definition 16.1 be uniformly twisted, with a cross section that is a disc of radius \(R\) or an annulus with radii \(R_{\mathrm{i}} < R\) and centre at \(O\), of a material as required by theorems 11.6 and 11.4 (ideally elastic with positive strain energy density). Let \(r = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2}\) be the distance from the axis. The displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\alpha\,x\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha\,x\,y \tag{16.7}\]

has the following properties.

  1. The field satisfies the Navier-Lamé equations (11.3) with zero body force.
  2. The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha\,z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha\,y\) and all other components are zero. The shear vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) is perpendicular to the radius, tangential, with magnitude \(\tau = G\alpha\,r\).
  3. The lateral surface is free: the stress vector on the outer and, for the annulus, the inner circle is zero.
  4. On an end section with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the resultant force and the moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are zero, and the moment about the axis \(x\) is
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\alpha . \tag{16.8}\]
  5. This field is, up to a small rigid motion, the only one that has such stresses and such forces on the end sections. At the clamping, where \(u = 0\) at \(x = 0\), it is the only solution and its displacement is exactly (16.7).
Idea

We check everything that the boundary value problem demands: the field equations, the condition on the lateral surface and the distribution of forces at the ends. The lateral surface is the key: the vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) at the edge is perpendicular to the radius, and the normal of the circle is parallel to the radius, so the vector has no part across the edge, as figure 16.9 requires. This is why the circle works.

Proof
  1. By definition 1.121 the strains of the field (16.7) are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y) = \tfrac12(-\alpha x + \alpha x) = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = -\alpha z/2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = \alpha y/2\). The trace of the strain is zero. Only the two shears between the axis \(x\) and the directions of the section differ from zero, which is the shear of the square on the surface, now at every point.
  2. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\)), because of step 1, \(\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\) and the other components are zero. With the coordinates \(y = r\cos\theta\), \(z = r\sin\theta\) we have \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) = G\alpha r\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), a vector of length \(G\alpha r\) perpendicular to the radius, which grows along a straight line from the axis to the edge. This is item 2.
  3. Every component of the field (16.7) is a product of \(x\) and \(y\) or \(z\), so second derivatives are zero, \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\), and \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\). So the left side of (11.3) and the body force are zero (theorem 11.6). This is item 1.
  4. Let \(\rho\) be the radius of one circle of the lateral surface, \(\rho = R\) or \(\rho = R_{\mathrm{i}}\). A point \((y, z)\) of the circle has the normal \(\vek n = \pm(0, y, z)/\rho\) along the radius, so \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and the shear vector at the edge is perpendicular to it. By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26), \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = \pm(-G\alpha z\,y + G\alpha y\,z)/\rho = 0\), \(t_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(t_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), because \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The expression \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the part of the shear vector across the edge, \(\tau_{\mathrm{r}}\), and it is zero. This is item 3.
  5. On the section with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the stress vector is \(\vek t = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}) = (0, -G\alpha z, G\alpha y)\) by step 2 and the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41). The resultant is \(\int \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A = (0, -G\alpha\int z\,\mathrm{d}A, G\alpha\int y\,\mathrm{d}A) = \vek 0\) by lemma 16.2, equation (16.4): the arrows at opposite ends of a diameter are opposite and equal and cancel. For the annulus the integral is a difference of integrals over \(K\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) and is again zero.
  6. The moment about the axis \(x\) of these forces is \(\int (y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\), which is the sum of the stress \(G\alpha r\) times the lever arm \(r\) over the rings. The moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are \(\int (z\,t_{\mathrm{x}} - x\,t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int (x\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). Since \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(x\) is constant on the section, they equal \(-x\int t_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(x\int t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), so by step 5 they are zero. At \(x = 0\) the normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the forces and the moment are opposite. This is item 4.
  7. The forces on the lateral surface and on the end sections are thereby given, the body force is zero. By theorem 11.4, item 1, the stresses and strains of every solution with the same forces equal ours, the difference of the displacements is a small rigid motion (item 2 of the theorem), that is \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) with an antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\). At the clamping, displacement \(u = 0\) at \(x = 0\), our displacement is of this kind, since at \(x = 0\) also \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero. Item 3 of the theorem further requires that the only small rigid motion that is zero on the clamped section \(x = 0\) is zero. We show this in the next step.
  1. Let us draw the clamped section \(x = 0\) in all four parts of the figure, with the axes \(y\) and \(z\) in the plane of the section and the axis \(x\) perpendicular to it. For two points \(\vek r\), \(\vek r'\) of this section, \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r = \vek a + \mat W\vek r' = \vek 0\), so \(\mat W(\vek r - \vek r') = \vek 0\). The points \(A\) and \(A'\) on the axis \(y\), \(R\) from the axis, lie in the disc and in the annulus, so this holds for \(\vek r - \vek r' = 2R\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), and likewise for the points \(B\) and \(B'\) on the axis \(z\) with \(\vek r - \vek r' = 2R\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). So \(\mat W\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (W_{\mathrm{xy}}, 0, W_{\mathrm{zy}}) = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (W_{\mathrm{xz}}, W_{\mathrm{yz}}, 0) = \vek 0\). Each of these components is a rotation that the clamping forbids. The component \(W_{\mathrm{xy}}\) would move the point \(A\) along the axis \(x\), out of the plane of the section, that is the rotation about the axis \(z\). The component \(W_{\mathrm{zy}}\) would move it along the axis \(z\), in the plane of the section, that is the rotation about the axis \(x\). The component \(W_{\mathrm{xz}}\) would move the point \(B\) along the axis \(x\), that is the rotation about the axis \(y\). All are zero, by antisymmetry \(W_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and the diagonal are zero too, so \(\mat W = 0\). Then at the point \(A\) the displacement is \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r = \vek a = \vek 0\), so there is no translation either, in which all points move by the same \(\vek a\). By item 3 of the theorem our solution is therefore the only one. This is item 5.
Explanation

The sections do not warp (\(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\)) and radial lines stay straight and radial: the point \((y, z)\) of the section at \(x\) moves under the field to \((y - \varphi z,\ z + \varphi y)\) with small \(\varphi = \alpha x\), that is along the tangent. Up to terms of order \(\varphi^2\) this is the point \(r(\cos(\theta + \varphi), \sin(\theta + \varphi))\), the original point \(r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) rotated along the arc by \(\varphi\). The map is linear, so it takes a segment from the centre to a segment from the centre, and radial lines stay straight and radial. So the assumptions of elementary strength of materials from chapter 13 (axis straight, sections plane, radial lines straight) are here a result of the computation. The moment (16.8) proves for the circle equation (12.16) of chapter 12, with the torsion constant \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\), and equation (13.20) of chapter 13 for uniform torsion, when the twist per unit length \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = \alpha\) is constant. When \(\partial\varphi/\partial x\) varies along the shaft or in time, as in vibration, equation (13.20) remains a model that this theorem supports but does not prove.

Source: SADD section D.2 (PDF pp. 573 and 574) has the field (16.7) from the assumption that the points of the section move tangentially in proportion to the distance from the centre, and from equilibrium obtains \(\tau = T\rho/J\) and equation (D.4). SADD example 5.3 (PDF p. 117) checks this field by the semi-inverse method. The proof with the Navier-Lamé equations, the boundary condition and uniqueness is ours, not from the sources.

The proof checked every requirement of the boundary value problem, so the guess is a solution and there is no other. Two of its moves will return for every section: the condition on the free surface (step 4) and the moment as an integral over the section (step 6). For the circle they came out without effort, because the edge is a circle.

Corollary 16.4 Angle, stiffness and largest stress of a circular shaft (zasuk, togost in največja napetost krožne gredi)

Let a shaft of length \(L\) with a solid circular or annular cross section, as in theorem 16.3, be loaded by the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the end. Then

\[\alpha = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad \varphi(L) = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}\,L}{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad k_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{\varphi(L)} = \frac{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}}{L} , \tag{16.9}\]

the shear stress at radius \(r\) is \(\tau(r) = M_{\mathrm{x}}\,r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the largest is at the outer edge,

\[\tau_{\max} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}\,R}{I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad \gamma_{\max} = R\,\alpha .\]
Proof

From equation (16.8), \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and from equation (16.2) \(\varphi(L) = \alpha L\), the rotation of the right end. From item 2 of theorem 16.3, \(\tau(r) = G\alpha r = M_{\mathrm{x}} r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\). The function \(r \mapsto r\) is increasing, so the stress is largest at \(r = R\), where the arrow at the edge is longest. The shear strain \(\gamma = \tau/G = \alpha r\) has its largest value \(R\alpha\), the shear of the square on the surface, which agrees with equation (16.3).

Source: SADD (D.3) and (D.4) (PDF pp. 573 and 574), CK (12.10) (PDF p. 389). The equation for \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) is taken in chapters 9 and 13 as a model. Here it is proved for a shaft at rest in uniform torsion. In the vibrating shaft of chapter 13 the twist per unit length varies along the shaft and in time, and there the equation remains a model.

The corollary explains Coulomb's equation (16.1). With \(\varphi = \varphi(L)\), \(l = L\) and \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi d^4/32\) for a wire of diameter \(d = 2R\),

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}}{L}\,\varphi(L) = \frac{\pi G}{32}\cdot\frac{d^4}{l}\,\varphi , \qquad \text{so} \quad \mu = \frac{\pi G}{32} .\]

Coulomb's constant of the material is the shear modulus multiplied by the number \(\pi/32 = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac1{16}\). The factor \(2\pi\) comes from the circumference of the rings, \(\tfrac14\) from summing \(r^3\) from the axis to the edge, \(\int_0^R r^3\,\mathrm{d}r = R^4/4\), and \(\tfrac1{16}\) from \(R^4 = d^4/16\), because Coulomb measures the diameter. For steel with \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa it is \(\mu = 7401\) MPa. The fourth power of the diameter comes from the three factors \(r\), the stress, the circumference of the ring and the lever arm, and from summing over the rings.

The product \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the torsional rigidity (torzijska togost) of a circular shaft. Chapter 12 gave the same role to the product \(GJ\) with the torsion constant \(J\) of the section (equation (12.16)). For the circle and the annulus we have now proved \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\). In the lectures \(J\) is called the torsional moment of inertia (torzijski vztrajnostni moment), with the symbol \(I_{\mathrm{t}}\). The book says constant, because this number is a second moment of the section only for the circle and the annulus. For every other section it is smaller than \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\), as the rectangle at the end of this section and the next section show.

16.1.1 The shaft as a one-dimensional boundary value problem (gred kot enorazsežni robni problem)

So far the moment acted only at the end of the shaft, so it was the same in every section. Real shafts are not like that. A drill boring into wood is held back by friction along its whole length. A gearbox shaft has a larger diameter on one part than on another. A rod welded between two walls is held at both ends and we twist it somewhere in between. In all three cases we do not know in advance how large the moment is in each section and by how much each section turns. We are therefore looking for a function of one variable, the rotation of the section \(\varphi(x)\), and for it an equation with two conditions at the ends, just as for the bar we looked for the displacement \(u(x)\) (definition 6.1). The path is the same as for the bar: first the equilibrium of a piece, then the relation between moment and rotation, then both together.

Let us draw the axis \(x\) and along it a shaft of length \(L\), fixed into a wall at \(x = 0\). The surroundings act on its lateral surface with a moment about the axis, spread along the length: these are the blue arrows around the shaft. How much of it there is at each place is told by the function \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\).

Definition 16.6 Distributed torque (porazdeljeni torzijski moment)

Let the shaft lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). The distributed torque (porazdeljeni torzijski moment) is a continuous function \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the interval \([0, L]\) whose input is the place \(x\) on the shaft and whose output is the moment about the axis \(x\) with which the surroundings act on the shaft, per unit length of the shaft at that place. The unit is N m/m. On a piece of the shaft between the sections \(a < b\) the surroundings act with the moment about the axis \(x\)

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]

Source: CK section 14.2 (PDF p. 444) uses a "distributed torque per unit length" with the symbol \(t_\theta\). The definition follows the pattern of definition 4.74, it is not from the source.

The moment on a piece is an integral for the same reason as the force on a part of a bar in definition 4.74: the moments of short pieces add up. For a constant \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m/m the moment \(50\ \text{N m/m} \cdot (b - a)\) acts on the piece between \(a\) and \(b\), and \(50\) N m on the whole shaft with \(L = 1\) m. Besides the load we need the torque \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\) in a section, as definition 13.21 fixes it: it is the moment about the axis \(x\) with which the part of the shaft to the right of the section \(x\) acts on the part to the left of it.

Now let us cut out the piece and draw it alone. Three moments about the axis act on it. The part of the shaft to the right of \(b\) acts on it with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b)\). The part to the left of \(a\) acts on it with \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\), because the piece acts on the left part with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\) and the reaction is opposite. In the figure it is therefore drawn in the opposite sense to \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b)\). The surroundings act on it with the moment of the load. The piece is at rest, so their sum is zero.

Lemma 16.7 Equilibrium of a shaft under a distributed torque (ravnovesje gredi pod porazdeljenim torzijskim momentom)

Let the shaft lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). Let the distributed torque \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 16.6) act on it, and at the end \(x = L\) an external moment \(M_{\mathrm{L}}\) about the axis \(x\). There are no other moments about the axis \(x\) on the shaft between the sections \(0\) and \(L\). Let \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\) be the torque in the section \(x\) (definition 13.21) and let every piece of the shaft between sections \(a < b\) be in equilibrium. Then:

  1. For every piece \(0 \le a < b \le L\),
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}}(b) - M_{\mathrm{x}}(a) + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = 0 . \tag{16.10}\]
  2. The torque in the section \(x\) is
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = M_{\mathrm{L}} + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{16.11}\]
    where at \(x = L\) the integral is zero.
  3. The function \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}}'(x) + m_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \in (0, L) , \qquad M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = M_{\mathrm{L}} . \tag{16.12}\]
Proof

First we obtain item 1 from the equilibrium of moments of the piece, from it item 2 with \(b = L\), and then the derivative by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

  1. Three moments about the axis \(x\) act on the piece between \(a\) and \(b\). By definition 13.21 the part to the right of \(b\) acts on it with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b)\). The piece acts on the part to the left of \(a\) with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\), so by the law of action and reaction the left part acts on the piece with \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\). The surroundings act on it, by definition 16.6, with \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. The equilibrium of moments (2.45) has one equation in the component \(x\): the sum of the three moments of step 1 is zero. This is (16.10).
  3. At \(b = L\) only the external source is to the right of the piece, acting on the end of the shaft with \(M_{\mathrm{L}}\), so \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = M_{\mathrm{L}}\). Equation (16.10) with \(a = x\) and \(b = L\) is \(M_{\mathrm{L}} - M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), which is (16.11).
  4. Let \(Q(x) = \int_0^x m_{\mathrm{x}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) for \(x \in [0, L]\), with \(Q(0) = 0\). By additivity (lemma 3.54 on the properties of the integral, item 2), \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L m_{\mathrm{x}} = Q(L) - Q(x)\), so \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = M_{\mathrm{L}} + Q(L) - Q(x)\).
  5. The function \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 2, the function \(Q\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\) and \(Q'(x) = m_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\). The number \(M_{\mathrm{L}} + Q(L)\) is constant, so \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}'(x) = -Q'(x) = -m_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\). On the graph of the torque this is the slope of the line.

Source: CK equations (12.11) to (12.13) (PDF p. 390) derive the same equilibrium for a slice of length \(\Delta x\) with the inertia term in place of the external load and with a limit. The equation \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' + m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) is the component \(x\) of equation (5.12) of chapter 5. The proof with a piece of finite length, after the pattern of lemma 4.75, is not from the source.

The lemma says that on every piece of the shaft the torque drops by exactly the load on that piece. In the lower part of figure 16.12 we see this for a constant \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\). On the axes \(x\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) the graph of the torque is the straight line \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = m_{\mathrm{x}}\,(L - x)\) by equation (16.11) with \(M_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\). At the wall the torque is largest, \(m_{\mathrm{x}} L\), because the wall must hold the whole load, and at the free end it is zero. Between \(a\) and \(b\) the graph drops by \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b m_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}s\), which is equation (16.10). The lemma is equilibrium only: it holds for every material and every cross section.

From the torque to the rotation leads a second relation, and that one depends on the material. For uniform torsion theorem 16.3 proved \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is the twist per unit length, the same along the whole shaft. The rotation \(\varphi(x) = \alpha x\) has the derivative \(\varphi' = \alpha\), so \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\). Now the torque varies along the shaft, so \(\varphi'\) must vary too. May we use the same relation in every section separately? Let us try the same guess as before, only with a rotation \(\varphi(x)\) that is no longer a straight line.

Lemma 16.8 Circular shaft with a varying twist per unit length (krožna gred s spremenljivim zasukom na enoto dolžine)

Let the cross section of the prism be a disc of radius \(R\) or an annulus with centre at \(O\), the material homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with shear modulus \(G\), and \(\varphi\) a function on \([0, L]\) with a continuous second derivative. The displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\varphi(x)\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varphi(x)\,y \tag{16.13}\]

has the following properties.

  1. The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\,\varphi'(x)\,z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\,\varphi'(x)\,y\), all other components are zero.
  2. The lateral surface is free.
  3. The moment of the forces on the section \(x\) about the axis \(x\) is
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'(x) . \tag{16.14}\]
  4. The field satisfies the Navier-Lamé equations (11.3) exactly with the body force
    \[\vek b = \frac{m_{\mathrm{x}}}{I_{\mathrm{p}}}\,(0,\ -z,\ y) , \qquad m_{\mathrm{x}} = -G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'' . \tag{16.15}\]
    At every point this force points along the circle about the axis, its magnitude grows in proportion to the distance \(r\) from the axis, its resultant on the section is zero and its moment about the axis \(x\) per unit length of the shaft is \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\).
Proof

We repeat the steps of the proof of theorem 16.3 with \(\varphi(x)\) in place of \(\alpha x\) and see what changes.

  1. The derivatives with respect to \(y\) and \(z\) are the same as before, the derivative with respect to \(x\) gives \(\varphi'(x)\) in place of \(\alpha\). The strains are therefore \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\varphi' z/2\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varphi' y/2\), the others zero, the trace is zero. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) \(\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\). This is item 1: the shear vector is the same as in uniform torsion, only with \(\varphi'(x)\) in the place of \(\alpha\).
  2. Step 4 of the proof of theorem 16.3 uses only that the shear vector at the edge is perpendicular to the radius. This still holds, so the lateral surface is free. This is item 2.
  3. Step 6 of the same proof gives the moment \(G\varphi'(x)\int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'(x)\), the sum over the rings. This is item 3.
  4. Now the field equations. Only the second derivative in \(x\) survives: \(\nabla^2\vek u = (0,\ -\varphi'' z,\ \varphi'' y)\), \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0\). Equation (11.3) is \(G\,\nabla^2\vek u + \vek b = \vek 0\), so \(\vek b = G\varphi''\,(0,\ z,\ -y)\), which is (16.15) with \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = -G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi''\).
  5. The vector \((-z, y)\) at the point \((y, z)\) is perpendicular to the radius and has length \(r\), like the shear vector. The resultant of the force \(\vek b\) on the section is zero, because \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int z\,\mathrm{d}A\) are zero (equation (16.4)). Its moment about the axis \(x\) per unit length is \(\int (y\,b_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,b_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = (m_{\mathrm{x}}/I_{\mathrm{p}})\int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = m_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is item 4.

Source: Not from a source. The computation follows the pattern of theorem 16.3 and is checked with SymPy.

The lemma tells what we assume when we write \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) for a varying torque, and let us say it plainly.

  • Why the relation is right here. If the distributed torque acts on every particle of the section with a force along the circle, in proportion to the distance from the axis, the field (16.13) is an exact solution of the equations of elasticity: the sections stay plane and the relation (16.14) holds in every section without error. Such a load is not invented. This is how the inertia of a shaft acts when its rotation accelerates: a particle at the distance \(r\) has the acceleration \(r\ddot\varphi\) along the circle. By the same computation with equation (13.33) the wave equation of the shaft (13.21) is therefore exact for a field of this form, not only a model.
  • When it does not hold exactly. The wood holds the drill back on its lateral surface, not in the interior. Then the lateral surface is not free and the shear just under it differs from the field of the lemma. The equilibrium (16.12) stays exact, because it follows from the equilibrium of a piece alone. What becomes approximate is the relation \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\). The same holds near a place where a moment enters at one point, and at a step where the diameter changes: by Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) over a length of about one diameter.
  • What it does to the result. The rotation of the end is the integral of \(\varphi'\) over the whole length, so the disturbed region counts with the share of its length: for a shaft with \(d = 40\) mm and \(L = 1\) m one diameter is \(4\,\%\) of the length. The stress in the disturbed region, however, can locally be considerably larger than the value \(M_{\mathrm{x}} R/I_{\mathrm{p}}\), and this model does not tell that. How large the error is for a load through the lateral surface we do not compute here.

Now we have both relations and we join them. The equilibrium \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' + m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) with \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) gives an equation for the rotation. At each end of the shaft we know one of two things: either the end is held and we know its rotation, or a known moment acts on it.

Definition 16.9 Boundary value problem of a shaft (robni problem gredi)

A shaft with a circular or annular cross section lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). Let the torsional rigidity \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) be a positive continuous function on \([0, L]\) and the distributed torque \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) a continuous function on \([0, L]\) (definition 16.6). The boundary value problem of a shaft (robni problem gredi) is the task: find a function \(\varphi\) on \([0, L]\), the rotation of the section, that has a continuous derivative \(\varphi'\), whose torque \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and that satisfies

\[\big(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\big)'(x) + m_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \in (0, L) \tag{16.16}\]

and at each of the two ends one condition: either the rotation \(\varphi\) is prescribed, or the torque \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) is prescribed. At least at one end let the rotation be prescribed. For a shaft fixed at \(x = 0\), with an external moment \(M_{\mathrm{L}}\) at the end \(x = L\), the conditions are

\[\varphi(0) = 0 , \qquad G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'(L) = M_{\mathrm{L}} . \tag{16.17}\]

Source: CK equations (12.14) and (12.15) (PDF p. 390) for the vibrating shaft: the same equation with the inertia term, a fixed end with zero rotation and an end with a given moment. The statement as a static boundary value problem, after the pattern of definition 6.1, is not from the source.

The lemma holds for a constant radius. For a radius that changes along the shaft the lateral surface has a normal with a component along the axis, so the shear on the section gives a force that is not zero on it. The lateral surface of the field (16.13) is therefore no longer free, and the relation \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) is an approximation there, good for a slowly changing diameter (SADD section 9.7, PDF p. 270).

A condition that prescribes the rotation is an essential condition, a condition that prescribes the moment is a natural condition, as for the bar and the beam (section 5.11.4). The task is letter for letter the task of the bar of definition 6.1:

bar shaft
unknown displacement \(u(x)\) rotation \(\varphi(x)\)
internal quantity axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) torque \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\)
load per unit length \(q\) in N/m \(m_{\mathrm{x}}\) in N m/m
equilibrium of a piece \(N' + q = 0\) \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' + m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\)

What we proved for the bar from these equations alone therefore holds for the shaft too, and conversely. The same equation is solved by the torsional degree of freedom of the beam element in chapter 12 (equation (12.16)).

Why do we require the rotation at least at one end? Take the shaft of the figure without the wall, with given moments at both ends. Equation (16.10) for the whole shaft, \(a = 0\) and \(b = L\), requires \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) - M_{\mathrm{x}}(0) + \int_0^L m_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). If the given moments do not satisfy this, there is no solution: the shaft is not in equilibrium and starts to rotate. If they do, then with \(\varphi\) also \(\varphi + c\) is a solution for every number \(c\), because only \(\varphi'\) enters the equation and both conditions. We can turn the shaft as a whole by any angle. For \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m/m, \(L = 1\) m and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = 0\) we must have \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(0) = 50\) N m, otherwise the task has no solution.

For a shaft fixed at one end, however, we write the solution down directly.

Corollary 16.10 Rotation of a shaft fixed at one end (zasuk gredi, pritrjene na enem koncu)

The boundary value problem of a shaft of definition 16.9 with the conditions (16.17) has exactly one solution,

\[\varphi(x) = \int_0^x \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}(s)}{G I_{\mathrm{p}}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s , \qquad M_{\mathrm{x}}(s) = M_{\mathrm{L}} + \int_{\mathrm{s}}^L m_{\mathrm{x}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{16.18}\]
Proof

We must show that the equation and the condition at the end determine the torque, and the torque and the fixing determine the rotation.

  1. Let \(\varphi\) be a solution and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\). The function \(M_{\mathrm{x}} + Q\) with \(Q(x) = \int_0^x m_{\mathrm{x}}\) has, by equation (16.16) and the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55, item 2), zero derivative, so by corollary 1.86 it is constant. From \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = M_{\mathrm{L}}\) it follows that \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = M_{\mathrm{L}} + Q(L) - Q(x)\), which is the torque of lemma 16.7.
  2. Then \(\varphi' = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) is a continuous function, because \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is positive and continuous. The function \(\Psi(x) = \int_0^x M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) has the same derivative by the fundamental theorem, so \(\varphi - \Psi\) is constant, and since \(\varphi(0) = 0\) and \(\Psi(0) = 0\), \(\varphi = \Psi\). There is therefore at most one solution: the rotation grows from the wall with the slope \(M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\).
  3. Conversely the function (16.18) has the derivative \(\varphi' = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\), so \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi' = M_{\mathrm{x}}\), which by step 5 of lemma 16.7 is continuously differentiable with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}' = -m_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = M_{\mathrm{L}}\). Moreover \(\varphi(0) = 0\). The function is a solution.

Source: Not from a source. For constant \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) this is lemma 13.23.

The corollary says: the slope of the graph of the rotation in every section is the torque in that section divided by the rigidity. Where the torque is large or the shaft thin, the rotation accumulates fast. Let us test it on three shafts of steel with \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa.

Example 16.11 Shaft with a uniform distributed torque (gred z enakomerno porazdeljenim momentom)

A shaft with \(d = 40\) mm and \(L = 1\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\), is loaded with \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m/m along the whole length, the end is free, \(M_{\mathrm{L}} = 0\). The total load is \(m_{\mathrm{x}} L = 50\) N m, equal to the moment at the end in example 16.5, the torsional rigidity is \(G I_{\mathrm{p}} = 18\,946\) N m\(^2\).

  1. The torque by equation (16.11) is \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = m_{\mathrm{x}}\,(L - x)\), a straight line from \(50\) N m at the wall to zero at the end.
  2. The rotation by equation (16.18) is
    \[\varphi(x) = \frac{m_{\mathrm{x}}}{G I_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_0^x (L - s)\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{m_{\mathrm{x}}\,x\,(2L - x)}{2\,G I_{\mathrm{p}}} ,\]
    a parabola on the axes \(x\) and \(\varphi\). At the wall it is steep, because the torque is largest there, at the end it has a horizontal tangent, because the torque is zero there: this is the natural condition \(\varphi'(L) = 0\).
  3. The rotation of the end is \(\varphi(L) = m_{\mathrm{x}} L^2/(2\,G I_{\mathrm{p}}) = 1{,}320 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad, that is \(0{,}0756^\circ\). At the middle \(\varphi(L/2) = 0{,}990 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad, three quarters of the final value.
  4. The largest stress is at the wall at the edge of the section, \(\tau_{\max} = M_{\mathrm{x}}(0)\,R/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 3{,}98\) MPa.

The rotation of the end is exactly half of the rotation \(2{,}639 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad of example 16.5, where the same \(50\) N m acted at the end. The reason is shown by the grey dashed line: there the torque is \(50\) N m in every section, while here it drops along a straight line from \(50\) N m to zero and is \(25\) N m on average. The rotation of the end is the area under the graph of the torque divided by \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\), and a triangle has half the area of the rectangle. The largest stress, however, is the same, because the torque at the wall is \(50\) N m in both cases.

The shaft is fixed at \(x = 0\). Over the length \(L_1 = 0{,}6\) m it has the diameter \(40\) mm, over the remaining length \(L_2 = 0{,}4\) m the diameter \(30\) mm. At the end the moment \(M = 50\) N m acts, there is no distributed torque.

part \(d\) \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi d^4/32\) \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\)
thick \(40\) mm \(251\,327\) mm\(^4\) \(18\,946\) N m\(^2\)
thin \(30\) mm \(79\,522\) mm\(^4\) \(5995\) N m\(^2\)

The rigidity \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) has a jump at \(x = L_1\), so definition 16.9 holds on each part separately. Two conditions at the step join the parts. The rotation is continuous, because the shaft is of one piece and the section at the step cannot turn by two angles at once. The torque is continuous by equation (16.10) for a short piece around the step: there is no load on it, so \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b) = M_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\).

  1. Torque: \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{L}} = M\), so by lemma 16.7 \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m in every section, a horizontal line on the axes \(x\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. Thick part: \(\varphi' = M/(G I_{\mathrm{p}}) = 2{,}639 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad/m and \(\varphi(0) = 0\), so the rotation grows along a straight line to \(\varphi(L_1) = 1{,}583 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad.
  3. Thin part: \(\varphi' = 50/5995 = 8{,}341 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad/m, the line is steeper and starts at \(\varphi(L_1)\). On the axes \(x\) and \(\varphi\) the graph has a kink at the step.
  4. The rotation of the end is \(\varphi(L) = 1{,}583 \cdot 10^{-3} + 0{,}4 \cdot 8{,}341 \cdot 10^{-3} = 4{,}920 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad, that is \(0{,}282^\circ\).

The slope in the thin part is \(3{,}16\) times larger, because \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is proportional to \(d^4\) and \((40/30)^4 = 3{,}16\). The thin part therefore has only \(40\,\%\) of the length, but contributes \(3{,}336\) of \(4{,}920\), that is \(68\,\%\) of the rotation. The stress is \((40/30)^3 = 2{,}37\) times larger, because \(R/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is proportional to \(1/d^3\). The parts act like springs in series: they carry the same moment and their rotations add, so \(1/k_{\mathrm{t}} = L_1/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})_1 + L_2/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})_2\) and \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = 10\,163\) N m/rad, against \(18\,946\) N m/rad of a shaft with the diameter \(40\) mm along the whole length.

Where is the model not exact? At the step the field is not the field of lemma 16.8. By Saint-Venant's principle the disturbance reaches about one diameter to each side, here \(40\) mm and \(30\) mm of \(1000\) mm, so the rotation of the end is little affected. The stress in the inner corner of the step, however, is larger than \(9{,}43\) MPa, and how much depends on the rounding of the corner (chapter 17). A shaft with a variable diameter is treated in SADD section 9.7 (PDF pp. 270 to 272).

Example 16.13 Shaft fixed at both ends (gred, pritrjena na obeh koncih)

A shaft with \(d = 40\) mm and \(L = 1\) m is fixed into a wall at both ends. In the section \(x = c = 0{,}3\) m an external moment \(M_0 = 50\) N m acts on it, for example through a gear. How much of the moment does each wall take?

Equilibrium alone does not tell. Three moments act on the shaft: the external \(M_0\), the left wall with \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(0)\) and the right wall with \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(L)\). The equilibrium of the whole shaft is one equation, \(M_0 - M_{\mathrm{x}}(0) + M_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = 0\), for two unknowns. Such a task is called statically indeterminate (statično nedoločena): equilibrium gives fewer equations than there are unknown forces. The missing equation comes from the deformation: both ends are held, \(\varphi(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi(L) = 0\). These are two essential conditions and the boundary value problem accepts them without difficulty.

  1. On each part \(m_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), so by equation (16.10) the torque is constant on each part: \(M_1 = M_{\mathrm{x}}(0)\) to the left of \(c\) and \(M_2 = M_{\mathrm{x}}(L)\) to the right of \(c\), drawn on the axes \(x\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. A short piece around the section \(c\), between \(a < c\) and \(b > c\): on it act \(M_{\mathrm{x}}(b) = M_2\), \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}(a) = -M_1\) and the external \(M_0\). Equilibrium gives \(M_2 - M_1 + M_0 = 0\). The torque has at \(c\) a jump down by \(M_0\).
  3. Left part: \(\varphi' = M_1/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and \(\varphi(0) = 0\), so \(\varphi(x) = M_1\,x/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\). Right part: \(\varphi' = M_2/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and \(\varphi(L) = 0\), so \(\varphi(x) = -M_2\,(L - x)/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\). These are the two sides of the roof on the axes \(x\) and \(\varphi\).
  4. The rotation is continuous at \(c\): \(M_1\,c = -M_2\,(L - c)\). Together with step 2 these are two equations for \(M_1\) and \(M_2\):
    \[M_1 = M_0\,\frac{L - c}{L} = 35\ \text{N m} , \qquad M_2 = -M_0\,\frac{c}{L} = -15\ \text{N m} .\]
  5. The rotation of the loaded section is \(\varphi(c) = M_0\,c\,(L - c)/(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,L) = 0{,}5542 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad, that is \(0{,}0318^\circ\).
  6. The largest stress is in the left part, \(35\,000 \cdot 20/251\,327 = 2{,}79\) MPa, in the right part it is \(1{,}19\) MPa.

The left wall takes \(35\) N m, the right one \(15\) N m, in the ratio \(70 : 30\), the reverse of the ratio of the lengths \(0{,}3 : 0{,}7\). The reason: both parts turn by the same angle at \(c\), and the shorter part is stiffer. The parts act like springs in parallel with the stiffnesses \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}/c = 63\,154\) N m/rad and \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}/(L - c) = 27\,066\) N m/rad. Their sum is \(90\,220\) N m/rad and \(50/90\,220 = 0{,}5542 \cdot 10^{-3}\) rad, as in step 5. The negative \(M_2\) means that the right part is twisted in the opposite sense to the left one: the rotation drops there. When \(c = L/2\), each wall takes half.

All three examples went the same way: the equilibrium of a piece gives the torque or its jumps, the relation \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\varphi'\) gives the slope of the rotation, the conditions at the ends and the continuity of the rotation determine the remaining constants. For the statically indeterminate shaft the only difference was the condition \(\varphi(L) = 0\) in place of a given moment.

16.1.2 Does the guess of the circle hold for a rectangle (ali ugibanje kroga velja za pravokotnik)

Let us return to the shaft with the moment at the end, which twists uniformly. For the circle all points of the section at \(x\) turn through the same angle \(\alpha x\), where \(\alpha\) is the twist per unit length, and the section does not warp. Is this true for every section? A section of another shape is the first test, and the story of how it was tested follows.

From history

Coulomb's law was also tested by experiments on shafts with other cross sections. Duleau, whom chapter 5 has introduced, tested iron bars, first round shafts, for which, with the assumption that the sections stay plane and the radii in them straight, he obtained an equation for the rotation that agreed with Coulomb's, and the same for tubes (TIMO, PDF pp. 90 and 91). For rectangular bars he found that these assumptions no longer hold: until then the stresses had been taken as proportional to the distance from the axis, but Duleau's experiments showed that this is not true (TIMO, PDF p. 91). At the same time, the practical handbooks of the first half of the 19th century used for the torsion of every cross section Coulomb's result with the polar second moment of the section (LOVE, PDF p. 36). Why this result fails for the rectangle is shown by the test below.

Let us return to the section \(20 \times 40\) mm of chapter 12 with half sides \(a = 10\) mm along \(y\) and \(b = 20\) mm along \(z\), loaded with \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m. Let us try the same field (16.7) and the same \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 4ab(a^2 + b^2)/3 = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) (with the expression of lemma 5.27 for \(I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}}\)).

Lemma 16.14 The guess of the circle fails on a rectangle (ugibanje kroga na pravokotniku odpove)

Let the cross section be the rectangle \([-a, a] \times [-b, b]\) with \(a, b > 0\) and let \(\alpha \ne 0\). For the field (16.7) the same field equations and stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\) hold as in items 1 and 2 of theorem 16.3, but on the lateral face \(y = a\) the stress vector has the component

\[t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha\,z ,\]

which is zero only at \(z = 0\), and on the face \(z = b\) the component \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = G\alpha y\), which is zero only at \(y = 0\). The field is therefore not a solution of the problem with a free lateral surface.

Proof

We must show that the requirement of a free lateral surface fails, although the field equations hold by the same computation as in the proof of the theorem.

  1. The field equations and stresses are computed in steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 16.3, which say nothing about the shape of the section.
  2. On the face \(y = a\), the right side of the section, the outward normal is \(\vek n = (0, 1, 0)\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\). This is the part of the shear vector along the normal, across the edge.
  3. On the face \(z = b\), the upper side, we have \(\vek n = (0, 0, 1)\) and by the same formula \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = G\alpha y\), again the part of the vector along the normal.
  4. For \(\alpha \ne 0\) and \(G > 0\) the expressions are zero only at \(z = 0\) and \(y = 0\) respectively, at the middle of the side. On most of the edge the stress vector is therefore different from zero: the shear vector does not lie along the edge there.
Explanation

Numbers for our section: at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, \(G = 75\,385\) MPa and the torsion constant \(J\) of chapter 12 taken as \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) we have \(\alpha = 4{,}97 \cdot 10^{-6}\) rad/mm and \(G\alpha = 0{,}375\) MPa/mm. On the face \(y = a\) the corner \(z = b\) has the component \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = -G\alpha b = -7{,}50\) MPa, on the face \(z = b\) it is there \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = G\alpha a = 3{,}75\) MPa: these are the orange arrows at the corner. The stress vector at the corner has length \(G\alpha\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = 8{,}39\) MPa, although at the corner where two free faces meet all the shear stress must be zero (figure 16.9). At the middle of the long face, at \(y = a\) and \(z = 0\), the field of the circle gives \(G\alpha a = 3{,}75\) MPa along the edge, and the true value is \(12{,}71\) MPa, which we compute in the following sections. The rotation with \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) is also wrong: \(\alpha = 0{,}00497\) rad/m, while the true value with \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) from chapter 12 is \(0{,}00906\) rad/m. The smaller constant gives the larger rotation, because \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ)\). The ratio is \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}549\), so the rotation with \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(45\,\%\) too small. The same difference was found by the frame in chapter 12, where the twisting with \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) gave \(4{,}97\) mm instead of \(9{,}06\) mm. What to do instead is shown by the following sections.

Source: The test is ours. The value \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) is taken from SADD (9.5.12) in chapter 12, in the following sections it is derived.

Figure 16.16 shows what happens. Drawn is the section \(20 \times 40\) mm with the axes. Through the corner runs a circle about the origin: the shear vector of the circle is tangential to it. On the edge we draw the vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) at four points of the right face and two points of the upper one. A free face cannot carry a force, so its component along the normal is a stress on the face. For a round section, on the dashed circle, this component is zero everywhere, because the normal is parallel to the radius and the vector perpendicular to it. For the rectangle the normal of the face \(y = a\) is parallel to the axis \(y\), and the vector \(G\alpha(-z, y)\) has there the component \(-G\alpha z\). The corner with two components is the extreme case: the stress vector has length \(8{,}39\) MPa, at the place where all the shear stress must be zero.

So what Duleau noticed shows up. For the circle the shear vector at the edge is along the edge. For the rectangle it must be just so, because every free surface requires it (figure 16.9), and at the corner, where it must not point across either of the two faces, even zero. The field of the circle cannot do this. The assumptions of elementary strength of materials are therefore not slightly inexact for the rectangle, they fail the boundary condition. The next section therefore extends the displacement: we add to it a displacement along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} \ne 0\), which makes the sections bulge, and look for the \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) that satisfies the boundary condition.
