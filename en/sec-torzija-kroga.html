Chapter 16 Torsion

16.1 Torsion of the circle (torzija kroga)

A steel shaft with a diameter of \(40\) mm and a length of \(1\) m is clamped into a wall at its left end, and at its right end we turn it with a moment of \(50\) N m about its axis, like a bolt with a wrench. How far does the right end turn? Where in the shaft is the material loaded most? And how would we find the answer for a shaft that has a rectangle instead of a circle? This phenomenon is called torsion (torzija, in Slovenian also zvijanje), from the Latin torquere, to twist. We begin the chapter with the circle, because there the ideas of elementary strength of materials are exact and every step can be tested. Then we change the cross section and see what breaks.

From history

Coulomb measured the twisting of a wire. Chapter 1 said that from the oscillation of a metal cylinder on a wire he determined how the wire resists twisting. Let us now add what he derived from it. For the oscillation he assumed that the moment with which the twisted wire resists is proportional to the angle of rotation \(\varphi\), and he obtained the period of oscillation. The experiment showed that the period does not depend on the angle if the angle is not too large, and from this he concluded that the proportionality of the moment to the angle is correct (TIMO, PDF p. 60). The reason is that a cylinder with moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{v}}\) on a wire that gives the moment \(c\,\varphi\) obeys \(J_{\mathrm{v}}\,\ddot\varphi = -c\,\varphi\), and its period does not depend on the amplitude, as we saw for torsional oscillation in chapter 9. Then he took wires of the same metal but of different lengths and diameters, and obtained the equation for the moment

\[M = \frac{\mu\,d^4}{l}\,\varphi , \tag{16.1}\]

where \(l\) is the length of the wire, \(d\) its diameter and \(\mu\) a constant of the material (TIMO, PDF p. 61, equation (i), printed page 53). The diameter thus stands in the fourth power: a wire with twice the diameter is \(2^4 = 16\) times stiffer at the same angle and length, and a wire twice as long is twice as soft.

Equation (16.1) is the law that this section explains: it hides the fourth power of the diameter and the constant \(\mu\), which we shall express by the shear modulus of the material. Before we start, let us say precisely what we compute.

Definition 16.1 Torsion of a prism (torzija prizme)

A prism (prizma) is a body obtained when a cross section \(S\), a figure in a plane with an area (definition 4.27), is moved by a length \(L\) perpendicular to itself. The axis \(x\) runs along the prism, in the cross section we measure the coordinates \(y\) and \(z\) as in chapter 5, with the origin \(O\) in the cross section. The material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with shear modulus \(G\) (definition 3.9), there is no body force, and the lateral surface is free, that is, without forces. On the two end sections only surface forces act (stress vector, definition 2.20) that have zero resultant and the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\): the moment about the axis \(x\) is \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), the moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are zero. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the moment of the forces on the section with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), with respect to the direction \(+x\), and on the opposite section its moment is \(-M_{\mathrm{x}}\).

The torsion is uniform (enakomerna torzija) if the displacement in the plane of the section at every \(x\) agrees with a rigid rotation of the section about the point \(O\) by a small angle \(\varphi(x)\),

\[u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\varphi(x)\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \varphi(x)\,y , \qquad \varphi(x) = \alpha\,x , \tag{16.2}\]

with a constant number \(\alpha\), which we call the twist per unit length (zasuk na enoto dolžine), unit rad/m. The displacement along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), is not prescribed: in this section we test \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), in the following ones we shall look for it.

Source: SADD section 9.3 (PDF p. 246) prescribes the same displacement in the plane and an unknown function for \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the axis \(z\) along the bar, the cross section in the plane \(x\), \(y\) and the twist per unit length \(\alpha\). Here the axes are moved to ours (axis \(x\) along the bar). The definition is not from the source.

The words each have a reason. A prism is a body with a constant cross section, as bars and shafts have. The twist per unit length is called so because \(\alpha = \varphi(x)/x\) is the angle by which the section turns for every metre of length. Let us watch the letters: \(\varphi\) is here the rotation of the section as in chapter 13, not the Airy function of chapter 11. The letter \(\alpha\) in this chapter is not the thermal expansion coefficient of chapter 3, which we shall not use. We write the moment about the axis \(x\) as \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) as in chapters 5 and 13, because the letter \(T\) would be taken by temperature.

The assumptions are idealizations and each has its meaning. Linear elasticity holds as long as the stress does not reach the yield limit, which we shall test. A free lateral surface means that nothing slides along the shaft. Loading the end with an exactly given distribution of forces is an idealization: the real load is a bolt or a gear. Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) says that another distribution with the same moment differs only near the end, which we state here and do not prove. The twisting that develops equally in every section is the one required by the constant twist \(\alpha\). For nonuniform torsion (neenakomerna torzija), which is hindered by the clamping and which we do not treat here, see COOK (PDF pp. 42 and 43) and ONATE2 section 4.10 (PDF pp. 227 to 246).

Why do we expect a constant \(\alpha\)? Let us draw the shaft and think.

The hatched line is the wall into which the shaft is clamped. From it along the axis \(x\) extends the shaft, a cylinder of length \(L\), measured from the wall. At the right end we turn it with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis. Before we load it, we draw a straight thread on the surface along the shaft. Let us cut off in thought the right part of the shaft, from the section at \(x\) to the end. Only the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the end and the moment of the forces of the left part across the section act on this part, the lateral surface is free. The moment about the axis \(x\) must be in equilibrium, therefore the moment across every section equals \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\). Every piece of the shaft with the same length and cross section thus has the same moment and therefore turns by the same angle. If the cross section is the same everywhere, the rotation of the section \(\varphi(x)\) therefore grows from \(\varphi(0) = 0\) at the wall with the same rate \(\alpha\) along the shaft, and after loading the thread bends into a helix. The height of the orange line at the right end is \(R\sin\varphi(L)\), which shows the rotation of the end.

Let us enlarge the view. On the surface of the shaft take a window with a square of side \(h\), enlarged on the right. Before loading its edges are perpendicular. One runs along the axis, the other along the circumference. After loading, the section at \(x\) and the section at \(x + h\) are turned with respect to the wall by \(\alpha x\) and \(\alpha(x + h)\), therefore with respect to each other by \(\alpha h\). A point on the surface that is \(R\) from the axis is thereby shifted along the circumference by \(R\alpha h\), this is the shift. The edge in the direction of the axis therefore tilts by the angle \(\gamma\) with respect to the original direction, the sheared square is no longer rectangular, and

\[\tan\gamma = \frac{R\alpha h}{h} = R\alpha , \qquad \gamma \approx R\alpha , \tag{16.3}\]

for a small angle. The change of the right angle between two edges is the shear strain (chapter 1, definition 1.115), in the engineering form \(\gamma = 2\varepsilon\) as in definition 3.9. With it the stress on the surface is \(\tau = G\gamma = G R\alpha\), which is the first estimate that the theorem below will prove and extend to every radius.

So we arrived at the displacement (16.2) with \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Can this really be a solution? The displacement was obtained by guessing, and this is exactly the semi-inverse method (definition 11.11): we choose a field, check the field equations and the boundary conditions, and then theorem 11.4 guarantees that there is no other solution. For the computation we first need the polar second moment of the circle, which chapter 5 took from a source. The circle is not a figure made of rectangles, so there is no computation by rectangles, and the integral requires polar coordinates.

Let us first draw the coordinate axes of the section and the circle of radius \(R\) about the origin. A point of the circle is written with the distance \(r\) from the centre and the angle \(\theta\) from the axis \(y\), \(y = r\cos\theta\), \(z = r\sin\theta\). The angle \(\theta\) and the distance \(r\) are thus coordinates of the point, and every pair \((r, \theta)\) from the rectangle on the right gives a point of the circle. This assignment, \(\Phi(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\), is shown by the arrows between the two pictures: \(\Phi\) takes the rectangle onto the circle, the inverse map \(\Phi^{-1}\) takes a point of the circle back into the rectangle. If the angle \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), the point comes back to the same place: the side \(\theta = 0\) and the side \(\theta = 2\pi\) of the rectangle are mapped onto the same segment, the radius on the axis \(y\), which we therefore cut out of the circle. Let us choose a piece of the circle between the radii \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) and between the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\). Its preimage in the rectangle has area \((r_2 - r_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\), and the piece itself has the area of the difference of two circular sectors, \(\tfrac12(\theta_2 - \theta_1)(r_2^2 - r_1^2)\). Since \(r_2^2 - r_1^2 = (r_2 - r_1)(r_2 + r_1)\), this is \(\bar r\,(r_2 - r_1)(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\) with the mean radius \(\bar r\). The piece is therefore \(\bar r\) times larger than its preimage: the larger, the farther from the centre it lies. This factor, equal to \(r\) at the point \((r, \theta)\), is the Jacobian determinant of the map \(\Phi\). Its matrix of derivatives and determinant are

\[\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} , \qquad J_\Phi = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r ,\]

as in lemma 11.15, item 1, only without the coordinate \(z\). Theorem 4.59 on the change of variables uses this factor exactly.

Lemma 16.2 Second moments of a disc and an annulus (vztrajnostni momenti kroga in kolobarja)

Let \(K\) be a disc of radius \(R\) with centre at the origin of the plane \(y\), \(z\), and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) a disc of radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}} < R\) with the same centre. Both are sets with an area (definition 4.27), since they are closed, bounded and their boundary is a circle. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{K}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 , \qquad I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{\pi R^4}{4} , \tag{16.4}\]

the polar second moment of area (polarni vztrajnostni moment) \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int_{\mathrm{K}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}}\) is

\[I_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{\pi R^4}{2} , \tag{16.5}\]

and for the annulus \(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}\) it is

\[I_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\big(R^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4\big) . \tag{16.6}\]
Proof

We must show that the integral of \(r^2\) over the disc can be computed with radii and angles, and the other relations follow by reflection. We cut the disc into a piece with an exact computation and a remainder whose area is arbitrarily small.

  1. Let \(0 < \rho < R\) and \(0 < \delta < \pi/2\) be numbers. Let \(\Phi(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\) on the open set \(D = (\rho/2, 2R) \times (0, 2\pi)\). The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable and has, as computed at figure 16.2, the Jacobian determinant \(J_\Phi = r > 0\). The map is injective: from \(r\cos\theta = r'\cos\theta'\) and \(r\sin\theta = r'\sin\theta'\) it follows that \(r^2 = r'^2\), so \(r = r'\), and then \(\cos\theta = \cos\theta'\), \(\sin\theta = \sin\theta'\), which in \((0, 2\pi)\) gives \(\theta = \theta'\).
  2. The rectangle \(W = [\rho, R] \times [\delta, 2\pi - \delta]\) lies in \(D\) and is a set with an area, its image \(\Phi(W)\) is the disc without the small disc of radius \(\rho\) and without the sector with angle \(2\delta\) around the radius \(\theta = 0\). By theorem 4.59 on the change of variables, for the function \(f(y, z) = y^2 + z^2 = r^2\),
    \[\int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{W}} r^2\cdot r\,\mathrm{d}A = (2\pi - 2\delta)\int_\rho^R r^3\,\mathrm{d}r = (\pi - \delta)\,\frac{R^4 - \rho^4}{2} ,\]
    since the integral over a rectangle is the product of two integrals (theorem 4.9) and \(r^4/4\) is an indefinite integral of \(r^3\) (fundamental theorem of calculus, 3.40).
  3. Let \(N\) be the closure of the set \(K \setminus \Phi(W)\). A point of \(N\) is at most \(\rho\) from the origin or has the polar angle in \([-\delta, \delta]\). The former lie in the square \([-\rho, \rho]^2\) with area \(4\rho^2\). The latter have \(0 \le y \le R\) and \(|z| = r|\sin\theta| \le R\sin\delta \le R\delta\) and lie in a rectangle with area \(2R^2\delta\). So the area of the set \(N\) is at most \(4\rho^2 + 2R^2\delta\) (monotonicity and additivity, lemma 4.32, items 2 and 4).
  4. The sets \(\Phi(W)\) and \(N\) have union \(K\) and no interior point of one lies in the other. By additivity (lemma 4.32, item 4) and by the bound (item 3, \(|f| \le R^2\) on \(K\)),
    \[\Big|\int_{\mathrm{K}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\Big| = \Big|\int_{\mathrm{N}} (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\Big| \le R^2\,\big(4\rho^2 + 2R^2\delta\big) .\]
  5. Let \(\rho \to 0\) and \(\delta \to 0\). The right-hand side of step 4 tends to zero, and the integral of step 2 tends to \(\pi R^4/2\). So \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi R^4/2\). For \(R = 20\) mm, \(\rho = 0{,}01\) mm and \(\delta = 0{,}01\) rad the integral of step 2 equals \(250\,527\) mm\(^4\), it differs from the exact value \(251\,327\) mm\(^4\) by \(800\) mm\(^4\), and the estimate of step 4 is \(3200\) mm\(^4\).
  6. The map \((y, z) \mapsto (z, y)\) is linear with \(|J| = 1\) and maps \(K\) onto itself. By theorem 4.59, \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(I_{\mathrm{y}} = I_{\mathrm{z}} = I_{\mathrm{p}}/2 = \pi R^4/4\). The map \((y, z) \mapsto (-y, z)\) has \(|J| = 1\) and maps \(K\) onto itself, therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} y\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{K}} (-y)\,\mathrm{d}A\) and this integral is zero. The same holds for \(z\).
  7. The annulus \(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) have union \(K\) and no interior point of one lies in the other. By additivity \(I_{\mathrm{p}}(K \setminus K_{\mathrm{i}}) = I_{\mathrm{p}}(K) - I_{\mathrm{p}}(K_{\mathrm{i}}) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}(R^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4)\).

Source: The value \(I = \pi R^4/4\) is in ONATE2 (Fig. 1.3, PDF p. 35) and SADD (D.2, PDF p. 573) for the polar moment \(J = \pi c^4/2\) with radius \(c\). The computation with polar coordinates and with reflection is not from the sources.

Chapter 5 took \(I = \pi R^4/4\) for a circle of diameter \(d = 32\) mm: \(\pi \cdot 16^4/4 = 51\,472\) mm\(^4\). Now it is proved. The polar moment of a shaft with \(d = 40\) mm is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi \cdot 20^4/2 = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\), and of a hollow shaft with \(d_{\mathrm{o}} = 40\) mm and \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 32\) mm it is \(\pi(20^4 - 16^4)/2 = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\).

Now the theorem. The guess is the displacement (16.2) with \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), we omit nothing.

Theorem 16.3 Torsion of a circle and an annulus (torzija kroga in kolobarja)

Let the prism of definition 16.1 be uniformly twisted, with a cross section that is a disc of radius \(R\) or an annulus with radii \(R_{\mathrm{i}} < R\) and centre at \(O\), of a material as required by theorems 11.6 and 11.4 (ideally elastic with positive strain energy density). Let \(r = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2}\) be the distance from the axis. The displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\alpha\,x\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \alpha\,x\,y \tag{16.7}\]

has the following properties.

  1. It satisfies the Navier-Lamé equations (11.3) with zero body force.
  2. The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha\,z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha\,y\) and all other components are zero. The shear vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) is perpendicular to the radius, tangential, with magnitude \(\tau = G\alpha\,r\).
  3. The lateral surface is free: the stress vector on the outer and, for the annulus, the inner circle is zero.
  4. On an end section with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the resultant force and the moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are zero, and the moment about the axis \(x\) is
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\,\alpha . \tag{16.8}\]
  5. This field is, up to a small rigid motion, the only one that has such stresses and such forces on the end sections. At the clamping, where \(u = 0\) at \(x = 0\), it is the only solution and its displacement is exactly (16.7).
Idea

We check everything that the boundary value problem demands: the field equations, the condition on the lateral surface and the distribution of forces at the ends. The lateral surface is the key: the vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) is perpendicular to the radius, and the normal of the circle is parallel to the radius, so their product is zero. This is why the circle works.

Proof
  1. By definition 1.115 the strains of the field (16.7) are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial y) = \tfrac12(-\alpha x + \alpha x) = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x) = -\alpha z/2\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x) = \alpha y/2\). The trace of the strain is zero.
  2. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.11, \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\)), because of step 1, \(\ten\sigma = 2G\,\ten\varepsilon\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\) and the other components are zero. With the coordinates \(y = r\cos\theta\), \(z = r\sin\theta\) we have \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) = G\alpha r\,(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), a vector of length \(G\alpha r\) perpendicular to the radius. This is item 2.
  3. Every component of the field (16.7) is a product of \(x\) and \(y\) or \(z\), so the second derivatives \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x^2\), \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y^2\) and \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial z^2\) are zero, so \(\nabla^2\vek u = \vek 0\). At the same time \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = 0 + 0 + 0\). The left-hand side of equation (11.3) is therefore zero, the body force is zero (theorem 11.6). This is item 1.
  4. Let \(\rho\) be the radius of one circle of the lateral surface, \(\rho = R\) or \(\rho = R_{\mathrm{i}}\). A point \((y, z)\) of the circle has the normal \(\vek n = \pm(0, y, z)/\rho\), so \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24), \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = \pm(-G\alpha z\,y + G\alpha y\,z)/\rho = 0\), \(t_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and \(t_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), because \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). This is item 3.
  5. On the section with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the stress vector is \(\vek t = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}) = (0, -G\alpha z, G\alpha y)\) by step 2 and the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.36). The resultant is \(\int \vek t\,\mathrm{d}A = (0, -G\alpha\int z\,\mathrm{d}A, G\alpha\int y\,\mathrm{d}A) = \vek 0\) by lemma 16.2, equation (16.4). For the annulus the integral is a difference of integrals over \(K\) and \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) and is again zero.
  6. The moment about the axis \(x\) of these forces is \(\int (y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\). The moments about the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are \(\int (z\,t_{\mathrm{x}} - x\,t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(\int (x\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). Since \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(x\) is constant on the section, they equal \(-x\int t_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(x\int t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), so by step 5 they are zero. At \(x = 0\) the normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the forces and the moment are opposite. This is item 4.
  7. The forces on the lateral surface and on the end sections are thereby given, the body force is zero. By theorem 11.4, item 1, the stresses and strains of every solution with the same forces equal ours, the difference of the displacements is a small rigid motion (item 2 of the theorem), that is \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r\) with an antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\). At the clamping, displacement \(u = 0\) at \(x = 0\), our displacement is of this kind, since at \(x = 0\) also \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero. Item 3 of the theorem further requires that the only small rigid motion that is zero on the clamped section \(x = 0\) is zero. For two points \(\vek r\), \(\vek r'\) of this section, \(\vek a + \mat W\vek r = \vek a + \mat W\vek r' = \vek 0\), so \(\mat W(\vek r - \vek r') = \vek 0\), and since \(\vek r - \vek r'\) can point in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), \(\mat W\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat W\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek 0\). Then \(W_{\mathrm{xy}} = W_{\mathrm{zy}} = W_{\mathrm{xz}} = W_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and by antisymmetry \(\mat W = 0\), and then \(\vek a = \vek 0\). By item 3 of the theorem our solution is therefore the only one. This is item 5.
Explanation

The sections do not warp (\(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\)) and radial lines stay straight and radial: the point \((y, z)\) of the section at \(x\) moves under the field to \((y - \varphi z,\ z + \varphi y)\) with small \(\varphi = \alpha x\). Up to terms of order \(\varphi^2\) this is the point \(r(\cos(\theta + \varphi), \sin(\theta + \varphi))\), the original point \(r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) rotated by \(\varphi\). The map is linear, so it takes a segment from the centre to a segment from the centre, and radial lines stay straight and radial. So the assumptions of elementary strength of materials from chapter 13 (axis straight, sections plane, radial lines straight) are here a result of the computation. The moment (16.8) proves for the circle equation (12.16) of chapter 12, with the torsion constant \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\), and equation (13.14) of chapter 13 for uniform torsion, when the twist per unit length \(\partial\varphi/\partial x = \alpha\) is constant. When \(\partial\varphi/\partial x\) varies along the shaft or in time, as in vibration, equation (13.14) remains a model that this theorem supports but does not prove.

Source: SADD section D.2 (PDF pp. 573 and 574) has the field (16.7) from the assumption that the points of the section move tangentially in proportion to the distance from the centre, and from equilibrium obtains \(\tau = T\rho/J\) and equation (D.4). SADD example 5.3 (PDF p. 117) checks this field by the semi-inverse method. The proof with the Navier-Lamé equations, the boundary condition and uniqueness is ours, not from the sources.

Let us look at the result on the section.

We draw the section of the solid shaft and its axes. The stress at a point of the section is the shear vector \(G\alpha r\) with direction perpendicular to the radius. On the radius on the axis \(y\) the arrows grow from zero at the centre to \(G\alpha R = 3{,}98\) MPa at the edge, which is a straight line in \(r\). On the circumference the arrow at every point has the same length and points in the direction of circulation, as the vector \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\) shows. On the right is the tube with outer radius \(R\) and inner radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\), on the inner and outer circle the stresses are \(5{,}39\) and \(6{,}74\) MPa (at the same moment it is larger because the \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the tube is smaller). The distance \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a cavity where there is no material and no stress.

Corollary 16.4 Angle, stiffness and largest stress of a circular shaft (zasuk, togost in največja napetost krožne gredi)

Let a shaft of length \(L\) with a solid circular or annular cross section, as in theorem 16.3, be loaded by the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the end. Then

\[\alpha = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad \varphi(L) = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}\,L}{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad k_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{\varphi(L)} = \frac{G\,I_{\mathrm{p}}}{L} , \tag{16.9}\]

the shear stress at radius \(r\) is \(\tau(r) = M_{\mathrm{x}}\,r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the largest is at the outer edge,

\[\tau_{\max} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}\,R}{I_{\mathrm{p}}} , \qquad \gamma_{\max} = R\,\alpha .\]
Proof

From equation (16.8), \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(G I_{\mathrm{p}})\) and from equation (16.2) \(\varphi(L) = \alpha L\). From item 2 of theorem 16.3, \(\tau(r) = G\alpha r = M_{\mathrm{x}} r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\). The function \(r \mapsto r\) is increasing, so the maximum is at \(r = R\). The shear strain \(\gamma = \tau/G = \alpha r\) has its largest value \(R\alpha\), which agrees with equation (16.3).

Source: SADD (D.3) and (D.4) (PDF pp. 573 and 574), CK (12.10) (PDF p. 389). The equation for \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) is taken in chapters 9 and 13 as a model. Here it is proved for a shaft at rest in uniform torsion. In the vibrating shaft of chapter 13 the twist per unit length varies along the shaft and in time, and there the equation remains a model.

Now let us answer the questions from the beginning.

Example 16.5 Steel shaft (jeklena gred)

The shaft has \(d = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, steel \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), so \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu)) = 75{,}38\) GPa. The polar moment is \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\), the torsional stiffness \(G I_{\mathrm{p}} = 18\,946\) N m\(^2\) and \(k_{\mathrm{t}} = 18\,946\) N m/rad. Results by equations (16.9):

\(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) \(\alpha\) \(\varphi(L)\) \(\tau_{\max}\) \(\gamma_{\max}\)
\(50\) N m \(0{,}00264\) rad/m \(0{,}151^\circ\) \(3{,}98\) MPa \(5{,}28 \cdot 10^{-5}\)
\(500\) N m \(0{,}02639\) rad/m \(1{,}512^\circ\) \(39{,}79\) MPa \(5{,}28 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

At \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 500\) N m the stress at radius \(10\) mm is half of that on the surface, \(19{,}89\) MPa.

When does the shaft begin to yield? On the surface the state is pure shear with stress \(\tau\) and principal stresses \(\tau\), \(0\), \(-\tau\). By theorem 2.70 the largest shear stress is \((\tau - (-\tau))/2 = \tau\), and at the yield limit \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa the first yielding by the criterion of the largest shear is at \(\tau = 110\) MPa, that is at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = \tau\,I_{\mathrm{p}}/R = 1382\) N m. By von Mises (definition 2.76) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{3J_2} = \sqrt3\,\tau\), because the deviator of pure shear equals the stress and \(J_2 = \tau^2\), so \(\tau = 220/\sqrt3 = 127{,}0\) MPa and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 1596\) N m. At \(500\) N m the shaft is therefore still elastic.

Is the rotation really small? At \(500\) N m we have \(\varphi(L) = 0{,}02639\) rad and a point on the surface moves by \(R\sin\varphi = 0{,}528\) mm. The field (16.2) moves the point along the circumference by \(R\varphi\) instead of \(R\sin\varphi\), and the difference \(R(\varphi - \sin\varphi)\) is \(6 \cdot 10^{-5}\) mm. The displacement towards the axis that the exact rotation has, \(R(1 - \cos\varphi) = 0{,}00696\) mm, the field does not have. The ratio of this displacement to the displacement along the circumference is \((1 - \cos\varphi)/\sin\varphi = \tan(\varphi/2)\), which is \(1{,}32\,\%\), and at \(50\) N m it is \(0{,}13\,\%\).

The tube \(d_{\mathrm{o}} = 40\) mm, \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 32\) mm has \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\), which is \(0{,}590\) of the solid shaft, but only \(0{,}360\) of its mass. At \(50\) N m, \(\alpha = 0{,}00447\) rad/m, \(\tau(R) = 6{,}74\) MPa and \(\tau(R_{\mathrm{i}}) = 5{,}39\) MPa. The reason for the ratio \(0{,}590\) against \(0{,}360\): in the integral \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = \int (y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}A\) the area at radius \(r\) counts with the weight \(r^2\), because there it has the stress \(G\alpha r\) and the lever arm \(r\). The tube removes the disc of radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}} = 0{,}8\,R\). This is \(0{,}8^2 = 0{,}64\) of the area and the mass, but only \(0{,}8^4 = 0{,}41\) of the polar moment (equation (16.5) with radius \(0{,}8\,R\)), because the removed material lies close to the axis. So \(1 - 0{,}41 = 0{,}59\) of the stiffness remains at \(1 - 0{,}64 = 0{,}36\) of the mass.

For the circle the twist \(\alpha\) of all points of the section is equal and the section does not warp. Is this true for every section? A section of another shape is the first test, and the story of how it was tested follows.

From history

Coulomb's law was also tested by experiments on shafts with other cross sections. Duleau tried iron bars, first round shafts, for which, with the assumption that the sections stay plane and the radii in them straight, he obtained an equation for the rotation that agreed with Coulomb's, and the same for tubes (TIMO, PDF pp. 90 and 91). For rectangular bars he found that these assumptions no longer hold: until then the stresses had been taken as proportional to the distance from the axis, but Duleau's experiments showed that this is not true (TIMO, PDF p. 91). At the same time, the practical handbooks of the first half of the 19th century used for the torsion of every cross section Coulomb's result with the second moment of the section (LOVE, PDF p. 36). Why this result fails for the rectangle is shown by the test below.

Chapter 5 has already introduced Duleau as a person. Let us return to the section \(20 \times 40\) mm of chapter 12 with half sides \(a = 10\) mm along \(y\) and \(b = 20\) mm along \(z\), loaded with \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m. Let us try the same field (16.7) and the same \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 4ab(a^2 + b^2)/3 = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) (with the expression of lemma 5.27 for \(I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}}\)).

Lemma 16.6 The guess of the circle fails on a rectangle (ugibanje kroga na pravokotniku odpove)

Let the cross section be the rectangle \([-a, a] \times [-b, b]\) with \(a, b > 0\) and let \(\alpha \ne 0\). For the field (16.7) the same field equations and stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\) hold as in items 1 and 2 of theorem 16.3, but on the lateral face \(y = a\) the stress vector has the component

\[t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha\,z ,\]

which is zero only at \(z = 0\), and on the face \(z = b\) the component \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = G\alpha y\), which is zero only at \(y = 0\). The field is therefore not a solution of the problem with a free lateral surface.

Proof

We must show that the requirement of a free lateral surface fails, although the field equations hold by the same computation as in the proof of the theorem.

  1. The field equations and stresses are computed in steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 16.3, which say nothing about the shape of the section.
  2. On the face \(y = a\) the outward normal is \(\vek n = (0, 1, 0)\). By Cauchy's formula \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} = -G\alpha z\).
  3. On the face \(z = b\) we have \(\vek n = (0, 0, 1)\) and \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = G\alpha y\).
  4. For \(\alpha \ne 0\) and \(G > 0\) the expressions are zero only at \(z = 0\) and \(y = 0\) respectively. On most of the edge the stress vector is therefore different from zero.
Explanation

Numbers for our section: at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, \(G = 75\,385\) MPa and the torsion constant \(J\) of chapter 12 taken as \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) we have \(\alpha = 4{,}97 \cdot 10^{-6}\) rad/mm and \(G\alpha = 0{,}375\) MPa/mm. On the face \(y = a\) the corner \(z = b\) has the component \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = -G\alpha b = -7{,}50\) MPa, on the face \(z = b\) it is \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = 3{,}75\) MPa. The stress vector at the corner has length \(G\alpha\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = 8{,}39\) MPa, although at the corner where two free faces meet all the shear stress must be zero. At the middle of the long face the given value is \(3{,}75\) MPa, and the true one is \(12{,}71\) MPa, which we compute in the following sections. The rotation with \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is also wrong: \(\alpha = 0{,}00497\) rad/m, while the true value with \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) from chapter 12 is \(0{,}00906\) rad/m. The ratio is \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}549\), so the rotation is \(45\,\%\) too small. The same difference was found by the frame in chapter 12, where the twisting with \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) gave \(4{,}97\) mm instead of \(9{,}06\) mm. What to do instead is shown by the following sections.

Source: The test is ours. The value \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) is taken from SADD (9.5.12) in chapter 12, in the following sections it is derived.

Figure 16.4 shows what happens. Drawn is the section \(20 \times 40\) mm with the axes. Through the corner runs a circle about the origin: the shear vector of the circle is tangential to it. On the edge we draw the vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) at four points of the right face and two points of the upper one. A free face cannot carry a force, so its component along the normal is a stress on the face. For the circle this component is zero everywhere, because the normal is parallel to the radius and the vector perpendicular to it. For the rectangle the normal of the face \(y = a\) is parallel to the axis \(y\), and the vector \(G\alpha(-z, y)\) has there the component \(-G\alpha z\). The corner with two components is the extreme case: the stress vector has length \(8{,}39\) MPa, at the place where all the shear stress must be zero.

So what Duleau noticed shows up. For the circle the shear vector at the edge is along the edge, for the rectangle it must be so too, and at the corner even zero, and the field of the circle cannot do this. The assumptions of elementary strength of materials are therefore not slightly inexact for the rectangle, they fail the boundary condition. The next section therefore extends the displacement: we add to it a displacement along the axis, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} \ne 0\), which makes the sections bulge, and look for the \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) that satisfies the boundary condition.
