Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.5 Stress at a point

So far the force over each cut was distributed evenly, and the force per unit area was the same at all points of the cut. In the hanging bar the stress changed along the bar, from \(\rho g L = 0{,}765\) MPa at the top to zero at the bottom (figure 2.19, right), but over each cross section it was still the same. In a general body it is not the same even over a cut. Near the ends of a bar, where grips hold it, the force is concentrated on a smaller part of the cross section (section 2.2). So we need the force per unit area at a single point of a cut.

The same approach as for the strain at a point in chapter 1 helps. There we looked at ever shorter pieces of the bar around a point. Here we do the same with the face of the cut. Cut the body in thought with a plane face through the point \(P\) (figure 2.38). We draw the parts apart and see the cut from the side: the left part is the one we are looking at. Let \(\vek n\) be the outward normal of the face for this part. Around the point \(P\) on the face of the cut we choose a small piece of the face, a patch (zaplata) with the area \(\Delta A\). The part of the body on the other side of the cut, the one into which \(\vek n\) points, acts on our part across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). The quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\), that is, the vector \(\Delta\vek F\) multiplied by the number \(1/\Delta A\), is the average force per unit area on the patch. As we shrink the patch around the point \(P\), the quotient approaches a vector that describes the force per unit area at the point \(P\) (figure 2.39 below).

Definition 2.20 Traction vector (vektor napetosti)

Let \(P\) be a point of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\). For \(h > 0\) let \(\Delta\vek F\) be the force with which the part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the part on the other side across a square patch of the face with centre \(P\) and side \(h\), which has the area \(\Delta A = h^2\). The traction vector (vektor napetosti), also called the stress vector, \(\vek t(\vek n)\) at the point \(P\) is the limit

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} , \tag{2.15}\]

a limit from the right (definition 1.8), if this limit exists and is the same for every rotation of the square in the face about \(P\). The limit of a vector is taken component by component.

Source: CM (4.2.1) (PDF p. 180), SADD (3.2.1) (PDF p. 65), BOWER §2.2.2 (PDF p. 67). LOVE Art. 42 (PDF pp. 93-94) shrinks the area "by any continuous process", keeping the point in it, and assumes that the quotient of force and area tends to a limit. The source therefore assumes more than we do, since the limit must not depend on the way of shrinking at all, while we require this only for rotations of the square. The square patches are not from the sources.

The traction vector is a vector whose components have the unit of stress, Pa. The notation \(\vek t(\vek n)\) stresses that it depends on the normal of the face. It depends on two things, on the point \(P\) and on the normal (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). In a body that moves or whose load changes it also depends on time. In this chapter the body is at rest, so we do not write the time. In the lectures the traction vector is written as \(\vec t^{(\vec n)}\), the force across the patch as \(\vec R\) and the area of the patch as \(\mathcal A\). We wrote the definition for a plane cut. The force across a curved surface, for example across the surface of a pipe, is described in the same way, with the normal at each point of the surface. What the normal of such a surface is and why we may do so, we show in subsection 2.5.1. The same concept also describes a force acting on the surface of a body from outside, for example the pressure of water on a wall. Then \(\Delta\vek F\) is the force of the surroundings on a patch of the surface (SADD §3.1, PDF pp. 63-64).

What exactly does the limit in (2.15) mean? The definition of the limit 1.5 speaks about a function whose input is one number \(h\) and whose output is one number. Here, though, what shrinks is not a number but a patch, and the output is a vector. We remove the first difficulty by choosing how to shrink the patch. This is why the definition takes squares with side \(h\) that lie in the face of the cut and have their centre at \(P\) (figure 2.39, left). Then \(\Delta A = h^2\), and the quotient is a function of one number, \(h\). On the right, from the same point \(Q\), are drawn the quotients for ever smaller squares and their limit. A square centred at \(P\) can also be turned in the face. The definition therefore requires the limit to be the same for every rotation, otherwise the traction vector would depend on how we placed the squares and not only on the forces in the body. The side of a square is positive, so in the definition of the limit we require \(0 < h < \delta\) instead of \(0 < |h| < \delta\). This is the limit from the right of definition 1.8. We remove the second difficulty with components. That the limit is the vector \(\vek t\) means that each of the three components of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) approaches the corresponding component of the vector \(\vek t\), each by the definition of the limit. That the limit exists and is the same for all rotations is an assumption about how forces are distributed over a continuum (LOVE Art. 42, PDF pp. 93-94). We cannot derive it from the definition of the limit. It holds when the force is spread over a face, as in the bar below, where the quotients do come ever closer to the limit. It does not hold when the force acts along a line, as figure 2.40 below shows, and there no traction vector exists.

Let us test the definition on bars. In a bar pulled by the axial force \(N\) the force across an oblique cut is, by our assumption (far from the ends of the bar), distributed evenly. So a patch with the area \(\Delta A\) receives the share \(\Delta A/A_\theta\) of the force \(\vek R = N\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} = \frac{1}{\Delta A}\,\frac{\Delta A}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \frac{1}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\]

for every patch. The quotient is the same vector for all patches, so its limit is this vector too. So the traction vector at every point of the oblique cut is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force per unit area (2.10). The definition of the normal and shear stress 2.18 therefore carries over to a point: at a point \(P\) of a face with the unit normal \(\vek n\) we take for \(\vek t\) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). It makes no difference whether we split the force \(\Delta\vek F\) into the normal and the shear part before the limit, or the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) after it. The projection of the quotient onto the normal is \((\Delta\vek F/\Delta A)\cdot\vek n\), a sum of the components of the quotient multiplied by the constant numbers \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\), the components of the normal. By lemma 2.25 on the limit of a sum, which we prove in section 2.6.3, it therefore approaches the number \(\vek t(\vek n)\cdot\vek n = \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\). The same holds for every component of the shear part, which is also a sum of the components of the quotient with constant factors.

The definition must also say when there is no stress vector. Let us take a force that does not act over a surface but along a line through \(P\) in the plane of the cut (line load, figure 2.40): every millimetre of the line carries \(5\) N in the direction of the normal \(\vek n\), and the line is parallel to the sides of the squares. The stress vector at \(P\) does not exist if the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) does not tend to any vector (the limit does not exist) or if the limit is different for different rotations of the square.

  1. We measure the side \(h\) in mm. A square with sides parallel to the line captures a piece of the line of length \(h\), so \(\Delta F = 5h\) N. The area is \(\Delta A = h^2\) mm². The quotient is \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A = (5/h)\,\vek n\) N/mm².
  2. For \(h = 1\) mm the quotient is \(5\) N/mm², for \(h = 0{,}1\) mm it is \(50\) N/mm², for \(h = 0{,}01\) mm it is \(500\) N/mm². Each time we reduce the side tenfold, the force falls tenfold, the area a hundredfold, and the quotient grows tenfold.
  3. The limit component by component: the component of the quotient in the direction \(\vek n\) is \(5/h\). Suppose the number \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) is its limit, on the axis of values. We take the error \(\eta = 1\) N/mm², that is the band around \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\), and an arbitrary band \(\delta\) for \(h\). We choose \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\) and \(h < 5/(|t_{\mathrm{n}}| + 1)\), so \(5/h > |t_{\mathrm{n}}| + 1\) and \(5/h\) lies to the right of the band.
  4. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) applied to \(5/h = (5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}) + t_{\mathrm{n}}\), we have \(5/h \le |5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}| + |t_{\mathrm{n}}|\), so \(|5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}|\) \(\ge 5/h - |t_{\mathrm{n}}| > 1 = \eta\). For every band there is therefore some \(h\) at which the component of the quotient lies outside the allowed error.
  5. We chose the number \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) arbitrarily, so no number is the limit. The limit does not exist, and there is no stress vector at a point of the line. A square rotated by \(45^\circ\) has on the line a diagonal of length \(h\sqrt{2}\), so the quotient is \(5\sqrt{2}/h\), different from \(5/h\) at the same \(h\). On the right the face and \(P\) are the same as on the left.

The reason is that the force falls with \(h\), while the area of the square falls with \(h^2\). For a force over a surface, for example \(5\) N/mm², we would have \(\Delta F = 5h^2\) N and the quotient \(5\) N/mm² at every \(h\) and every rotation, as for the bar above.

In the hanging bar with the axis \(x\) pointing down, take the cross section at the distance \(x\) from the ceiling (figure 2.11). For the upper part the outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), since it points down, into the lower part. The lower part pulls the upper one down, the force is distributed evenly over the cross section, and by (2.5)

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

At the top this is \(\rho g L = 0{,}765\) MPa in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For the lower part the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the upper part pulls it up, and \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

In the hanging bar the traction vectors on the two sides of the same cut are opposite. This is not peculiar to bars. The idea is simple: the two parts act on each other across the same patch with opposite forces, and opposite forces give opposite quotients (figure 2.38). For the right part the outward normal is \(-\vek n\), and the point \(P\) is the same on both parts.

Lemma 2.21 Traction on the opposite side of a cut (vektor napetosti na nasprotni strani reza)

If at a point of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\) (the right part has the outward normal \(-\vek n\)) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) exists, then \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) exists too, and

\[\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n) . \tag{2.16}\]
Proof

We must show that the limit that defines \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the opposite vector of the limit that defines \(\vek t(\vek n)\). We take the same patch twice, once as part of the face of the left part and once as part of the face of the right part. For the left part the outward normal is \(\vek n\), for the right part it is \(-\vek n\).

  1. The right part acts on the left one across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). By definition 2.20 of the traction vector, for the left part \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\).
  2. The left part acts on the right one with the opposite force \(-\Delta\vek F\), by the law of action and reaction (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).
  3. So for the right part, whose outward normal is \(-\vek n\), \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \((-\Delta\vek F)/\Delta A\) \(= -(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A)\).
  4. Let \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the component \(x\) of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) and \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). The component \(x\) of the opposite quotient is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\), and its expected limit is \(-t_{\mathrm{x}}\). The distance \(|(-q_{\mathrm{x}}) - (-t_{\mathrm{x}})| = |q_{\mathrm{x}} - t_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is the same. So when \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(-t_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the same bands \(\delta\) for every error \(\eta\). The same holds for the components \(y\) and \(z\).
  5. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7) each component has only one limit, so \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-t_{\mathrm{x}}\) are single points on the axis. The proof of the lemma holds equally for the right-hand limit, since the number \(h\) that the proof chooses in step 4 is positive. So \(\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n)\).

Source: CM §4.2.1 (PDF p. 181), SADD §3.2 (PDF p. 65). The proof by components is not from the sources.

So the traction vector on one side of a cut already determines the traction vector on the other side: the outward normals \(\vek n\) and \(-\vek n\) give, by the lemma, opposite forces across the same patch.

2.5.1 A curved cut and Cauchy's postulate

So far we cut the body with a plane. A cut can also be curved. From a thick pipe we can cut out a thinner pipe in thought, and the cut is then the lateral surface of a cylinder. The surface of most bodies is curved too. We have to answer two questions. What is the normal of a surface that is not plane? And is the force per unit area at the point \(P\) different for a curved cut than for a plane one?

Let us start with the normal. A curved surface is almost plane from close up: the ground under our feet is flat, although we stand on a sphere. Let us draw a cylindrical cut with the radius \(R\), seen along the axis of the cylinder, which passes through the point \(C\), and on it the point \(P\) (figure 2.43, left). Through \(P\) we lay a plane that only touches the cut, and we draw its unit normal \(\vek n\). How well the plane fits the cut we measure at another point \(Q\) of the cut. We compare two lengths: the distance \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\) between the points and the offset \(d\) of the point \(Q\) from the plane. The offset is the length of the projection of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) onto the direction \(\vek n\) (definition 2.13), so \(d = |\vek n\cdot\overrightarrow{PQ}|\). Every plane through \(P\) has a small offset when \(Q\) is close to \(P\). A plane that touches the surface does more: its offset is small even compared with the distance \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).

Definition 2.22 Tangent plane and normal of a curved surface (tangentna ravnina in normala ukrivljene ploskve)

Let \(P\) be a point of a surface in space and \(\vek n\) a unit vector. The plane through \(P\) perpendicular to \(\vek n\) is the tangent plane (tangentna ravnina) of the surface at the point \(P\) if for every number \(\eta > 0\) there is a number \(\delta > 0\) such that for every point \(Q\) of the surface with \(0 < |\overrightarrow{PQ}| < \delta\)

\[|\vek n\cdot\overrightarrow{PQ}| < \eta\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}| . \tag{2.17}\]

When such a plane exists and there is only one, \(\vek n\) and \(-\vek n\) are the unit normals (enotski normali) of the surface at the point \(P\). A surface that has a tangent plane at each of its points is called a smooth surface (gladka ploskev).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.7 (PDF p. 424) defines the tangent plane as the plane of the tangent vectors of all curves on the surface through \(P\), and the normal as the straight line through \(P\) perpendicular to it. The condition with the offset is not from the sources. We chose it because it needs only the scalar product and the distance.

Let us test the definition on a cylindrical cut with the radius \(R\) \(= 20\) mm.

  1. For a plane surface the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) between two of its points is perpendicular to its unit normal (definition 2.17), so the offset is zero and the condition (2.17) holds for every \(\eta\). A plane surface is its own tangent plane, and the new normal agrees with the old one.
  2. On the cylinder the offset of the point \(Q\) from the plane through \(P\) perpendicular to the radius is exactly
    \[d = \frac{|\overrightarrow{PQ}|^2}{2R} .\]
    This comes from the Pythagorean theorem. Take \(Q\) on the same circle as \(P\). Along the plane, \(Q\) is at the distance \(a\) from \(P\), and perpendicular to it at the distance \(d\), so \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|^2 = a^2 + d^2\). The point \(Q\) lies on the circle around \(C\), so \(a^2 + (R - d)^2 = R^2\), that is \(a^2 + d^2 = 2Rd\). A shift along the axis of the cylinder does not change the offset and makes the distance larger, so for the other points of the cylinder \(d\) is even smaller than \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|^2/(2R)\).

The numbers show how fast the offset falls. The lengths in the table are in millimetres, and the last two columns belong to the plane through \(P\) tilted by \(30^\circ\) and to the offset \(d'\) of the point \(Q\) from it.

\(\vert\overrightarrow{PQ}\vert\) \(d\) \(d/\vert\overrightarrow{PQ}\vert\) \(d'\) \(d'/\vert\overrightarrow{PQ}\vert\)
\(1\) \(0{,}025\) \(0{,}025\) \(0{,}52\) \(0{,}52\)
\(0{,}1\) \(0{,}00025\) \(0{,}0025\) \(0{,}050\) \(0{,}50\)
\(0{,}01\) \(0{,}0000025\) \(0{,}00025\) \(0{,}0050\) \(0{,}50\)

The ratio of the offset \(d\) and the distance is at most \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|/(2R)\) by step 2, so it is smaller than \(\eta\) as soon as \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}| < 2R\eta\). So the condition (2.17) holds with \(\delta = 2R\eta\). The plane perpendicular to the radius is the tangent plane of the cylinder, and the normal of the cylinder at \(P\) points along the radius.

The tilted plane does not pass the test. The offset \(d'\) goes to zero too, but the ratio \(d'/|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\) does not: it stays close to \(\sin 30^\circ = 0{,}5\), because close to \(P\) the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) lies almost in the tangent plane, which makes \(30^\circ\) with the tilted one. For \(\eta = 0{,}1\) the condition holds for no \(\delta\). The tilted plane cuts the cylinder at \(P\), it does not touch it.

Now to the force. Through the same point \(P\) of a body let us draw three cuts, seen from the side (figure 2.44): the plane one \(S_1\), the gently curved one \(S_2\) and the strongly curved one \(S_3\). All three have the same tangent plane at \(P\), and so the same normal \(\vek n\). But each divides the body into two different parts. Does the part on the side into which \(\vek n\) points act on the other part at the point \(P\) with the same force per unit area for all three cuts? Continuum mechanics assumes that it does.

Definition 2.23 Cauchy's postulate (Cauchyjev postulat)

Let \(P\) be a point of a body and \(S\) a smooth cut surface through \(P\) that has at \(P\) a tangent plane with the unit normal \(\vek n\) (definition 2.22). For \(h > 0\) let \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the force with which the part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the part on the other side across that piece of the surface \(S\) which the perpendicular projection onto the tangent plane maps into a square with the centre \(P\) and the side \(h\), for any rotation of the square in the tangent plane. Cauchy's postulate (Cauchyjev postulat) is the assumption that the limit from the right

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}}{h^2} = \vek t(\vek n) \tag{2.18}\]

exists and equals the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) of the plane cut through \(P\) with the same normal (definition 2.20). The traction vector at the point \(P\) of a curved surface is therefore \(\vek t(\vek n)\). It depends only on the point \(P\) and on the normal \(\vek n\) of the surface at this point, and in a body that moves or whose load changes also on time. It does not depend on the curvature of the surface.

Source: That the traction vector depends on the point and on the unit normal of the surface is said by SADD §3.2 (PDF p. 65) and CM §4.2.1 (PDF p. 181). The name of the postulate, the statement for curved surfaces and the form with the limit are not from the sources.

The postulate is an assumption, so let us say three things: why it is justified here, when it does not hold, and what it does to the results.

Why it is justified is shown by a thin piece of the body between a curved cut and its tangent plane (figure 2.43, right). In the tangent plane of the cylindrical cut let us take a square with the centre \(P\) and the side \(h\), with two sides parallel to the axis of the cylinder. Under it lies a piece of the curved cut. Between them is a piece of the body in the shape of a lens. The normal \(\vek n\) points away from the axis of the cylinder. Let us show that the quotient of the force across the curved piece and the area \(h^2\) approaches the same vector as the quotient of the force across the plane square.

  1. The lens is bounded by the plane square with the area \(h^2\), the curved piece and four narrow side walls, perpendicular to the tangent plane. The walls are highest at the edge of the square, where the cut is at the offset \(s\) from the plane. By the formula for the offset on a cylinder \(s = c^2/(2R)\), where \(c\) is the distance from \(P\) to the edge of the curved piece, and by the Pythagorean theorem \(c^2 = (h/2)^2 + s^2\). For \(h = 1\) mm and \(R = 20\) mm this gives \(s = 0{,}00625\) mm. All four walls together have an area no larger than \(4hs\), which is \(0{,}025\) mm², \(2{,}5\) % of the area of the square.
  2. Four forces act on the lens. Across the plane square the part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on it with the force \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{r}}\). Across the curved piece the lens, which is on the side of the normal, acts on the part of the body under the cut with the force \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}\), and by the law of action and reaction this part acts on the lens with the opposite force \(-\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}\), as in the proof of lemma 2.21. Across the side walls the surrounding body acts with the force \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\). The fourth force is the weight of the lens \(\Delta\vek G\).
  3. The body is at rest, so the lens is at rest too, and the forces on it add up to zero: \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{r}}\) \(-\) \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}\) \(+\) \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\) \(+ \Delta\vek G = \vek 0\). We solve the equation for \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}\) and divide by \(h^2\):
    \[\frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{u}}}{h^2} = \frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{r}}}{h^2} + \frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}}{h^2} + \frac{\Delta\vek G}{h^2} . \tag{2.19}\]
  4. The first term on the right approaches the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) of the plane cut, by definition 2.20. Here we assume that it exists.
  5. The second term. We assume that the force is spread over the side walls too, which means that there is a number \(M\) such that the force across no wall is larger than \(M\) times the area of the wall. Then \(|\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}|/h^2 \le M\cdot 4hs/h^2 = 4Ms/h\). For \(h < R\) the edge is at an offset smaller than \(h/2\) from the plane, so \(c < h\) and \(s < h^2/(2R)\). So the second term is at most \(2Mh/R\) and goes to zero with \(h\). If \(M\) is as large as the force per unit area on the square, the side force is \(2{,}5\) % of the force on the square at \(h = 1\) mm, and \(0{,}25\) % at \(h = 0{,}1\) mm.
  6. The third term. The volume of the lens is no larger than \(h^2 s\), so the weight per unit area of the square is no larger than \(\rho g s\), where \(s\) is the height of the side walls. For steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ this is \(0{,}48\) Pa at \(h = 1\) mm, next to the stress of \(0{,}765\) MPa at the top of the hanging bar. Since \(s < h^2/(2R)\), this term goes to zero too.
  7. By lemma 2.25 on the limit of a sum, which we prove in section 2.6.3, used on each component separately, the right side of (2.19) approaches the vector \(\vek t(\vek n) + \vek 0 + \vek 0\): the first term approaches \(\vek t(\vek n)\), the second and third approach zero. A component of a vector is no larger in size than its length, so the components of the second and third term go to zero too. The left side is the same function of the number \(h\), so it approaches the same vector, which is what (2.18) claims.

The square with the side \(h\) has its centre at the point \(P\), and the turned square has the same centre and the same side. For a turned square the edge of the piece is at most \(h/\sqrt 2\) from \(P\), so the bound \(s < h^2/(2R)\) and steps 5 to 7 stay.

For the cylindrical cut we have thus derived the postulate from two weaker assumptions: that the traction vector of the plane cut exists at \(P\), and that the force across the side walls does not grow faster than their area. For another smooth surface the argument is similar. If the surface crosses its tangent plane, like a saddle, there are several pieces between them on both sides of the plane. We write the balance for each piece, and the bounds for the walls and the weight stay the same. By (2.17) the offset of the surface over the square is smaller than \(\eta h\) as soon as \(h\) is small enough, the walls together have less than \(4\eta h^2\) of area, and the share \(4\eta\) of the area of the square is as small as we like. So a small plane piece of the tangent plane is a good replacement for the curved cut near the point \(P\): all three cuts depart from the common tangent plane near \(P\) by too little to show in the limit.

When does the postulate not hold? First, where the surface has no tangent plane, for example on an edge where two plane faces meet, or at the apex of a cone (KREYSZIG §9.7, PDF p. 424). There is no single normal there and so no vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). Second, where there is no traction vector even for a plane cut, as on the line along which a line load acts: there the force across a patch does not fall as fast as the area, and the number \(M\) of step 5 does not exist.

What does the postulate do to the results? It shrinks the input of the function that describes the stress at a point to three numbers, the components of the normal \(\vek n\). Without it we would have to know the traction vector separately for every shape of surface through \(P\). With it plane cuts are enough, so in the next section we may cut out of the body a piece with plane faces only, and the result will hold for curved cuts and for the curved surface of a body too, where at every point we take the normal of that point. The error we make when we replace the curved piece by the plane one is, for a piece with the side \(1\) mm on a cut with the radius \(20\) mm, of the order of those \(2{,}5\) % of step 5, ten times smaller for a piece ten times smaller, and absent in the limit.

At the same point of the bar we got a different traction vector for every tilt of the cut, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.46). For every cut the unit normal is also drawn, and for the oblique cuts the angle \(\theta\) between the cross section and the cut is marked. So the stress at a point is not a single vector. It is a function whose input is the unit normal \(\vek n\), three numbers that tell how the cut is tilted, and whose output is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), three numbers that give the force per unit area. For the bar we computed this function from equilibrium. But how does the traction vector depend on the normal in a general body, where the forces are not distributed so simply? Do we have to know \(\vek t(\vek n)\) separately for each of the infinitely many directions \(\vek n\)? This is the question of the next section.
