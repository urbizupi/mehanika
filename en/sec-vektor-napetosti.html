Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.5 Stress at a point

So far the force over each cut was distributed evenly, and the force per unit area was the same at all points of the cut. In the hanging bar the stress changed along the bar, from 0,765 MPa at the top to zero at the bottom, but over each cross section it was still the same. In a general body it is not the same even over a cut. Near the ends of a bar, where grips hold it, the force is concentrated on a smaller part of the cross section (section 2.2). So we need the force per unit area at a single point of a cut.

The same approach as for the strain at a point in chapter 1 helps. There we looked at ever shorter pieces of the bar around a point. Here we do the same with the face of the cut. Cut the body in thought with a plane face through the point \(P\) (figure 2.22). Let \(\vek n\) be the outward normal of the face for the part of the body we are looking at. Around the point \(P\) on the face of the cut we choose a small piece of the face, a patch (zaplata) with the area \(\Delta A\). The part of the body on the other side of the cut, the one into which \(\vek n\) points, acts on our part across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). The quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\), that is, the vector \(\Delta\vek F\) multiplied by the number \(1/\Delta A\), is the average force per unit area on the patch. As we shrink the patch around the point \(P\), the quotient approaches a vector that describes the force per unit area at the point \(P\).

Definition 2.20 Traction vector (vektor napetosti)

Let \(P\) be a point of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\). For \(h > 0\) let \(\Delta\vek F\) be the force with which the part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the part on the other side across a square patch of the face with centre \(P\) and side \(h\), which has the area \(\Delta A = h^2\). The traction vector (vektor napetosti), also called the stress vector, \(\vek t(\vek n)\) at the point \(P\) is the limit

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} , \tag{2.15}\]

a limit from the right (definition 1.8), if this limit exists and is the same for every rotation of the square in the face about \(P\). The limit of a vector is taken component by component.

Source: CM (4.2.1) (PDF p. 180), SADD (3.2.1) (PDF p. 65), BOWER §2.2.2 (PDF p. 67). LOVE Art. 42 (PDF pp. 93-94) shrinks the area "by any continuous process", keeping the point in it, and assumes that the quotient of force and area tends to a limit. The source therefore assumes more than we do, since the limit must not depend on the way of shrinking at all, while we require this only for rotations of the square. The square patches are not from the sources.

The traction vector is a vector whose components have the unit of stress, Pa. The notation \(\vek t(\vek n)\) stresses that it depends on the normal of the face. Of course it also depends on the point \(P\). SADD stresses that it depends on exactly these two things, on the point and on the normal (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). We wrote the definition for a plane cut. The force across a curved surface, for example across the surface of a pipe, is described in the same way, with the normal at each point of the surface, which we will show when we need such a surface. The same concept also describes a force acting on the surface of a body from outside, for example the pressure of water on a wall. Then \(\Delta\vek F\) is the force of the surroundings on a patch of the surface (SADD §3.1, PDF pp. 63-64).

What exactly does the limit in (2.15) mean? The definition of the limit 1.5 speaks about a function whose input is one number \(h\) and whose output is one number. Here, though, what shrinks is not a number but a patch, and the output is a vector. We remove the first difficulty by choosing how to shrink the patch. This is why the definition takes squares with side \(h\) that lie in the face of the cut and have their centre at \(P\) (figure 2.23). Then \(\Delta A = h^2\), and the quotient is a function of one number, \(h\). A square centred at \(P\) can also be turned in the face. The definition therefore requires the limit to be the same for every rotation, otherwise the traction vector would depend on how we placed the squares and not only on the forces in the body. The side of a square is positive, so in the definition of the limit we require \(0 < h < \delta\) instead of \(0 < |h| < \delta\). This is the limit from the right of definition 1.8. We remove the second difficulty with components. That the limit is the vector \(\vek t\) means that each of the three components of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) approaches the corresponding component of the vector \(\vek t\), each by the definition of the limit. CM and SADD only write the limit down (CM (4.2.1), PDF p. 180, SADD (3.2.1), PDF p. 65). They do not say what shape the patches shrinking around \(P\) have, nor whether the limit exists. LOVE does say it: he shrinks the area by any continuous process in which the point stays in it, and assumes that the quotient of force and area tends to a limit (LOVE Art. 42, PDF pp. 93-94). That it exists and is the same for all shapes of patches is an assumption about how forces are distributed over a continuum, and we cannot derive it from our definition of the limit.

Let us test the definition on bars. In a bar pulled by the axial force \(N\) the force across an oblique cut is distributed evenly. So a patch with the area \(\Delta A\) receives the share \(\Delta A/A_\theta\) of the force \(\vek R = N\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} = \frac{1}{\Delta A}\,\frac{\Delta A}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \frac{1}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\]

for every patch. The quotient is the same vector for all patches, so its limit is this vector too. So the traction vector at every point of the oblique cut is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force per unit area (2.10). The definition of the normal and shear stress 2.18 therefore carries over to a point: at a point \(P\) of a face with the unit normal \(\vek n\) we take for \(\vek t\) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\).

The definition must also say when there is no stress vector. Let us take a force that does not act over a surface but along a line through \(P\) in the plane of the cut (line load, figure 2.24): every millimetre of the line carries \(5\) N in the direction of the normal \(\vek n\), and the line is parallel to the sides of the squares. The stress vector at \(P\) does not exist if the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) does not tend to any vector (the limit does not exist) or if the limit is different for different rotations of the square.

  1. We measure the side \(h\) in mm. A square with sides parallel to the line captures a piece of the line of length \(h\), so \(\Delta F = 5h\) N. The area is \(\Delta A = h^2\) mm². The quotient is \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A = (5/h)\,\vek n\) N/mm².
  2. For \(h = 1\) mm the quotient is \(5\) N/mm², for \(h = 0{,}1\) mm it is \(50\) N/mm², for \(h = 0{,}01\) mm it is \(500\) N/mm². Each time we reduce the side tenfold, the force falls tenfold, the area a hundredfold, and the quotient grows tenfold.
  3. The limit component by component: the component of the quotient in the direction \(\vek n\) is \(5/h\). Suppose the number \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) is its limit. We take the error \(\eta = 1\) N/mm² and an arbitrary band \(\delta\). We choose \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\) and \(h < 5/(|t_{\mathrm{n}}| + 1)\), so \(5/h > |t_{\mathrm{n}}| + 1\).
  4. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, item 1) applied to \(5/h = (5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}) + t_{\mathrm{n}}\), we have \(5/h \le |5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}| + |t_{\mathrm{n}}|\), so \(|5/h - t_{\mathrm{n}}| \ge 5/h - |t_{\mathrm{n}}| > 1 = \eta\). For every band there is therefore some \(h\) at which the component of the quotient lies outside the allowed error.
  5. We chose the number \(t_{\mathrm{n}}\) arbitrarily, so no number is the limit. The limit does not exist, and there is no stress vector at a point of the line. A square rotated by \(45^\circ\) has on the line a diagonal of length \(h\sqrt{2}\), so the quotient is \(5\sqrt{2}/h\), different from \(5/h\) at the same \(h\).

The reason is that the force falls with \(h\), while the area falls with \(h^2\). For a force over a surface, for example \(5\) N/mm², we would have \(\Delta F = 5h^2\) N and the quotient \(5\) N/mm² at every \(h\) and every rotation, as for the bar above.

In the hanging bar with the axis \(x\) pointing down, take the cross section at the distance \(x\) from the ceiling. For the upper part the outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), since it points down, into the lower part. The lower part pulls the upper one down, the force is distributed evenly over the cross section, and by (2.5)

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

At the top this is \(0{,}765\) MPa in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For the lower part the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the upper part pulls it up, and \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

In both cases the traction vectors on the two sides of the same cut are opposite. This is not peculiar to bars. The idea is simple: the two parts act on each other across the same patch with opposite forces, and opposite forces give opposite quotients (figure 2.22).

Lemma 2.21 Traction on the opposite side of a cut (vektor napetosti na nasprotni strani reza)

If at a point of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) exists, then \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) exists too, and

\[\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n) . \tag{2.16}\]
Proof

We must show that the limit that defines \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the opposite vector of the limit that defines \(\vek t(\vek n)\). We take the same patch twice, once as part of the face of the left part and once as part of the face of the right part. For the left part the outward normal is \(\vek n\), for the right part \(-\vek n\).

  1. The right part acts on the left one across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). By definition 2.20 of the traction vector, for the left part \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\).
  2. The left part acts on the right one with the opposite force \(-\Delta\vek F\), by the law of action and reaction (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).
  3. So for the right part, whose outward normal is \(-\vek n\), \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \((-\Delta\vek F)/\Delta A = -(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A)\).
  4. Let \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the component \(x\) of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) and \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). The component \(x\) of the opposite quotient is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the distance \(|(-q_{\mathrm{x}}) - (-t_{\mathrm{x}})| = |q_{\mathrm{x}} - t_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is the same. So when \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(-t_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the same bands \(\delta\) for every error \(\eta\). The same holds for the components \(y\) and \(z\).
  5. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7) each component has only one limit. The proof of the lemma holds equally for the right-hand limit, since the number \(h\) that the proof chooses in step 4 is positive. So \(\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n)\).

Source: CM §4.2.1 (PDF p. 181), SADD §3.2 (PDF p. 65). The proof by components is not from the sources.

So the traction vector on one side of a cut already determines the traction vector on the other side. CM and SADD write this as a consequence of the law of action and reaction (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).

At the same point of the bar we got a different traction vector for every tilt of the cut, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.25). So the stress at a point is not a single vector. It is a function whose input is the unit normal \(\vek n\), three numbers that tell how the cut is tilted, and whose output is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), three numbers that give the force per unit area. For the bar we computed this function from equilibrium. But how does the traction vector depend on the normal in a general body, where the forces are not distributed so simply? Do we have to know \(\vek t(\vek n)\) separately for each of the infinitely many directions \(\vek n\)? This is the question of the next section.
