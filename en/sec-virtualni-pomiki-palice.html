Chapter 4 Energy principles and virtual work

4.4 Virtual displacements of a bar

So far we have always written equilibrium with forces. For a whole body these are the conditions that the resultant of the forces is the zero vector and that the sum of the moments is the zero vector (equations (2.2) and (2.45)), and for every small box in the body the equilibrium equations (2.38). This is how in section 4.1 we computed the axial forces in both bars of the bracket fixed to the wall, from the equilibrium of the pin at joint \(C\). In this section we write the same equilibrium in another way, with work. The idea is simple. In our minds we move a body in equilibrium a little, but only in the way its supports allow, and we leave the forces as they are. Then we compute the work the forces would do on this imagined displacement. We will show that the body is in equilibrium exactly when this work is zero for every such displacement.

Why write equilibrium once more when we already know it? For three reasons. First, the support forces, which we often do not know, drop out, because the points held by the supports do not move. Second, for a bar we will get an equation in which an integral stands in place of the derivative of the axial force. The finite element method, which we will meet in chapters 6 and 7, rests on this form of equilibrium. Third, with a cleverly chosen imagined displacement we get one unknown force at a time, without solving a system of equations.

The goal of the section is to state this claim precisely and to prove it for three kinds of bodies, starting with the simplest. First for a rigid body, a lever on a pin, on which the idea is clearest. Then for a straight bar loaded along its axis by forces distributed over its length, as weight loads the hanging bar. There we will need integration by parts from section 3.5.8 and a new lemma from mathematics. At the end for the bracket of two bars from section 4.1. The general body follows in the next section.

What do we assume? In section 4.1 we assumed four things for the whole chapter: a linear elastic material, small displacements, slow loading and loads that keep their direction. The principle we are going to derive needs less, so we list the assumptions separately.

  1. The displacements are small. We therefore write equilibrium on the shape of the body before loading, as in chapter 2.
  2. The supports are ideal, without friction. An ideal support acts only in the directions in which it prevents motion, so its force does no work in a displacement the support allows. A support with friction would act also in the direction in which it allows sliding, its force would do work and would stay in the equations (EP §3.4.1, p. 128, GR §1.1.2, PDF p. 34).
  3. We assume nothing about the material, as with the rigid lever. We will use only equilibrium, not the relation between stress and strain. The principle therefore holds for steel, rubber or any other material. We will need a linear elastic material only at the end of the section, when we compute the displacement of the joint from the changes of the lengths of the bars.

We do not need slow loading, because we will not load the body, we will test its equilibrium position.

4.4.1 The lever

We start with a body whose equilibrium we already know how to write. A rigid lever (vzvod) lies horizontally on a support and can turn about a pin (zatič) at the point \(O\) (figure 4.135). The pin is frictionless. We lay the axis \(x\) along the lever to the right, the axis \(y\) vertically up and the axis \(z\) toward the viewer, so that the coordinate system is right-handed (definition 2.32), with the origin at \(O\). We measure lengths in centimetres, so that the coordinates are whole numbers. The left end of the lever is \(A = (-40, 0, 0)\), the right end \(B = (120, 0, 0)\). They are 40 cm and 120 cm from \(O\). At the left end a load with a weight of 300 N acts, so the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}} = (0, -300, 0)\) N. We push the right end down with the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}} = (0, -P, 0)\) N, where \(P\) is the number we are looking for. The pin acts on the lever at the point \(O\) with a force \(\vek R\) that we do not know. We neglect the weight of the lever. That the lever is rigid is an idealization. A steel lever bends a little under the load, but its points move by much less than its lengths, so the moments of the forces hardly change (assumption 1).

How large must \(P\) be for the lever to be in equilibrium? As in section 2.8.3 we write the sum of the moments about \(O\). The force \(\vek R\) acts at \(O\), the vector from \(O\) to \(O\) is the zero vector, so its moment is zero. By (2.39) the moments of the load on the arm of 40 cm and of the push on the arm of 120 cm are

\[\begin{aligned} (-40, 0, 0)\times(0, -300, 0) &= (0, 0, 12\,000)\ \text{N cm} , \\ (120, 0, 0)\times(0, -P, 0) &= (0, 0, -120\,P)\ \text{N cm} . \end{aligned}\]

The sum of the moments \((0, 0, 12\,000 - 120\,P)\) N cm is the zero vector for \(P = 100\). From the equilibrium of the forces, \(\vek R + \vek F_{\mathrm{A}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}} = \vek 0\), we then get \(\vek R = (0, 400, 0)\) N. The pin carries the load and the push.

The conditions of equilibrium of a lever were already proved by Archimedes (section 2.8.2), and he used his theory in hoisting devices (TIMO, PDF p. 3). EP names him as the first to use work arguments for levers (EP, PDF p. 2). What we gain in force at a lever we lose in path. For our lever we will see that the push moves three times as far as the load, so it can be three times smaller.

From history

The general idea was written down in 1717 in a letter to Varignon by Johann Bernoulli, who called it virtual velocities (virtualne hitrosti), meaning the velocities that the points of a body would have in an imagined motion (EP, PDF p. 2, TIMO, PDF p. 35). Lagrange turned the computation of motion into a computation of equilibrium and used the principle we will state in theorem 4.82 to derive the equations of motion. His book Mécanique analytique of 1788 became, together with Euler's work, the basis of the later theory of motion (EP, PDF p. 3). We will name the imagined displacement in section 4.4.2 (definition 4.70), and we will state and prove the principle for an arbitrary body in section 4.5.

We now solve the same question, how large \(P\) must be for the lever to be in equilibrium, with work. Imagine that we turn the lever about the pin a little and leave the forces as they are.

We will denote imagined displacements and the quantities that describe them by the letter \(\delta\) in front of the symbol of the quantity: \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}}\) will be the imagined displacement of the point \(A\). The notation \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}}\) is a single name of a new quantity, not the product of a number \(\delta\) and a vector \(\vek u_{\mathrm{A}}\). The letter \(\delta\) alone, without a symbol after it, has another meaning in the book. In the definition of the limit 1.5 and of continuity 1.59 it is a positive number that bounds how far the argument is from the chosen point. The notation \(\delta\) for imagined displacements is used by EP (§3.4.1, pp. 126-128) and GR (§1.1.1, PDF pp. 32-33).

The pin allows only turning about the axis \(z\) through \(O\). How do the points of the lever move then? Let the point at a distance of 1 cm from \(O\) move perpendicular to the lever by \(\delta\varphi\) centimetres. The number \(\delta\varphi\) is a ratio of two lengths, a number without a unit, like the ratio \(s/h\) for the sheared cube in section 1.6.2. The point at a distance of 40 cm moves 40 times as much, the point at 120 cm 120 times as much. For positive \(\delta\varphi\) the lever turns counterclockwise, seen from the tip of the axis \(z\): the right end goes up, the left end down. These displacements come from the vector product. Let \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = (0, 0, \delta\varphi)\) be a vector in the direction of the axis of turning. For a point \(\vek r = (x, 0, 0)\) on the lever, by (2.39)

\[\begin{aligned} \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r &= (0, 0, \delta\varphi)\times(x, 0, 0) \\ &= (0 \cdot 0 - \delta\varphi \cdot 0,\ \ \delta\varphi\,x - 0 \cdot 0,\ \ 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot x) = (0,\ x\,\delta\varphi,\ 0) . \end{aligned}\]

The ends therefore move by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}} = (0, -40\,\delta\varphi, 0)\) cm and \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = (0, 120\,\delta\varphi, 0)\) cm, and the point \(O\) does not move (figure 4.135). The work the forces would do on these displacements we compute as if they were constant, with the scalar product of force and displacement:

  1. Load: \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{A}} = (-300)(-40\,\delta\varphi) = 12\,000\,\delta\varphi\) N cm. The load moves down, in the direction of its force, so the work is positive.
  2. Push: \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{B}} = (-P)(120\,\delta\varphi) = -120\,P\,\delta\varphi\) N cm. The end \(B\) moves up, against the force, so the work is negative.
  3. Pin: \(\vek R\cdot\vek 0 = 0\), because \(O\) does not move.
  4. The sum is \((12\,000 - 120\,P)\,\delta\varphi\) N cm. It is zero for every \(\delta\varphi\) exactly when \(P = 100\).

We got the same equation as from the moments, and the force of the pin did not enter the computation at all. The meaning is plain. When the lever turns, the push moves three times as far as the load, 120 against 40. For the two parts of the work to cancel, the push must be three times smaller than the load. This is the law of the lever we know from school, told with work. The number \(12\,000 - 120\,P\) is moreover exactly the \(z\) component of the sum of the moments. This is no coincidence. The work of the load is force times displacement, and the displacement is the arm times \(\delta\varphi\): \(300 \cdot (40\,\delta\varphi) = (300 \cdot 40)\,\delta\varphi\). We only grouped the same three numbers differently, and in the bracket stands the moment, force times arm. We will show this in general in theorem 4.73.

Where did assumption 2, a pin without friction, enter the computation? In item 3: the pin acts only with the force \(\vek R\) at a point that stays in place. A pin with friction would also resist the turn with a moment, which would stay in the sum of the works. If the friction in the pin can supply, say, a moment of up to 600 N cm, the lever stays at rest as long as \(|12\,000 - 120\,P| \le 600\), that is, for every \(P\) between 95 and 105. The single value \(P = 100\) widens into a range, and the principle in the form we are deriving no longer fixes the push. This is why we use it where the friction in the supports is small compared with the moments of the loads.

Is the displacement \((0, x\,\delta\varphi, 0)\) really a turn? In a true turn the point \(B\) would move along a circle about \(O\), while our displacement moves it along the tangent, to the point \(B' = (120, 120\,\delta\varphi, 0)\). Its distance from \(O\) is then \(120\sqrt{1 + \delta\varphi^2}\) cm, a little more than 120 cm: the point \(B'\) lies outside the circle, by the orange piece in the magnified view. For \(\delta\varphi = 0.001\) the point \(B\) moves by \(0.12\) cm, while the distance grows by \(6 \cdot 10^{-5}\) cm, 2000 times less. The figure is drawn with \(\delta\varphi = 0.2\), otherwise the orange piece could not be seen. This is the same effect as with the vertical edge \(AD\) of the sheared cube, which only leaned and became longer by a term with the square of the shift. Our imagined displacement therefore keeps the distances only up to terms with the square of the number \(\delta\varphi\). This is why we think of imagined displacements as small: they must respect rigidity and the supports at least to first order.

4.4.2 Virtual displacement and virtual work

Three things mattered for the lever. The displacement was imagined, the forces did not cause it. Only a displacement allowed by the pin and by the rigidity of the lever was permitted. During the displacement the forces stayed as they are in equilibrium. Conditions that restrict how the points of a body can move are called constraints (vezi) (GR §1.2.2, PDF p. 36). The pin is a constraint that holds the point \(O\) in place. Rigidity is a constraint that keeps the distances between the points of the lever. Now we state all this precisely.

Definition 4.70 Virtual displacement (virtualni pomik)

Let given forces act on a body in a given position. A virtual displacement (virtualni pomik) of the body is an imagined displacement of its points that its supports and other constraints allow, that is, all the conditions that restrict how its points can move. A virtual displacement must satisfy the conditions of the constraints exactly or at least up to terms with the squares of the virtual displacement. During a virtual displacement all forces on the body stay as they are in the given position.

Source: EP §3.4.1 (pp. 126-128), EP §4.2.1 (p. 167), GR §1.1-1.2 (PDF pp. 32-36). EP takes the virtual displacement from the equilibrium position. The book also uses it in converse statements, in which equilibrium is only being checked, so it defines it for every given position.

Definition 4.71 Virtual work (virtualno delo)

Let a force \(\vek F\) act at a point of a body that has the virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\). The virtual work (virtualno delo) of the force \(\vek F\) is the number \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u\). The virtual work of several forces is the sum of the virtual works of the individual forces.

Source: EP §3.4.1 (p. 127), EP (4.2.1) (p. 167), GR (1.5) (PDF p. 33).

Virtual work is therefore the work a constant force would do on a straight displacement (definition 4.1), except that the displacement is not real. Why do we not change the forces during a virtual displacement? Because with a virtual displacement we test the equilibrium position. We ask whether the forces acting in this position are in equilibrium, and so we compute with exactly these forces (EP, p. 126). A virtual displacement has nothing to do with the real displacement that would occur if we changed the loading (EP, p. 126). Virtual work is proportional to the virtual displacement: if we double the displacement, the work doubles as well. Whether the virtual work is zero therefore does not depend on the size of the displacement. Smallness matters only because of the constraints, which, as we saw with the lever, a virtual displacement keeps only to first order.

Quantities determined by a virtual displacement are called virtual quantities. We denote them by the letter \(\delta\) in front of the symbol, as for the lever: \(\delta\vek u\) is the virtual displacement of a point, \(\delta u(x)\) the virtual displacement of the point of a bar at the place \(x\), \(\delta e\) the virtual elongation (virtualni podaljšek) of a bar.

4.4.3 The rigid body

Which virtual displacements are available to a free rigid body, held by no support? Its only constraint is rigidity: the distance between no two points may change (definition 1.11). For the lever the role was played by a turn about the axis \(z\). In general a small displacement of a rigid body has two parts (figure 4.136). The axis \(z\) points, as for the lever, toward the viewer. The first part is a displacement of all points by the same vector \(\delta\vek a\). The second part is a small turn about an axis through a chosen point \(O\), which we describe by a vector \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) in the direction of the axis. The point \(P\) with the vector \(\vek r = \overrightarrow{OP}\) moves in the turn by \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\). By item 4 of lemma 2.34 on the properties of the vector product this displacement is perpendicular to the axis and to \(\vek r\). How long is it? Let \(\vek e = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi/|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\) be the unit vector in the direction of the axis. By item 1 of lemma 2.38 on the lever arm and the magnitude of the moment, applied to the line through \(O\) in the direction \(\vek e\) and to the point \(P\), the distance of the point \(P\) from the axis of the turn is \(\ell = \sqrt{|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2}\). In the lemma the vector from the point to the line appears, here \(-\vek r\), which gives the same squares. By item 5 of the lemma on the properties of the vector product, and because \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\,\vek e\), we have \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r|^2 = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\,|\vek r|^2 - (\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\vek r)^2 = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\,\big(|\vek r|^2 - (\vek r\cdot\vek e)^2\big) = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\,\ell^2\). The point therefore moves by \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\) times its distance from the axis. The number \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\) is a ratio of lengths, like \(\delta\varphi\) for the lever. For the lever \(\delta\vek a = \vek 0\), because the pin holds the point \(O\).

For the lever we saw that such a displacement keeps the distances only approximately. The next lemma says how closely, and its proof is a single right triangle (figure 4.137). Take two points of the body, \(P\) and \(Q\), and watch only how \(Q\) moves relative to \(P\). Before the displacement the vector \(\vek d\) of length \(L\) joins them, grey and dashed. The common shift \(\delta\vek a\) moves both points alike and does not change the vector between them. The turn, however, moves the end \(Q\) by \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d\), and it does so perpendicular to \(\vek d\), just as the displacement along the tangent at the lever is perpendicular to the lever. The new vector between the points, orange, is therefore the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the legs \(\vek d\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d\). A hypotenuse is longer than a leg, so the distance cannot decrease. The circle of radius \(L\) about \(P\) shows by how much it is longer: by the piece \(L_1 - L\) outside the circle.

Lemma 4.72 Small rigid displacement (majhen togi premik)

Let \(O\) be a point and let \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) be vectors. Let every point \(P\) of a body move by

\[\delta\vek u(P) = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\overrightarrow{OP} .\]

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be points of the body with the distance \(L = |\overrightarrow{PQ}| > 0\) before the displacement and the distance \(L_1\) after it. Then

\[L \le L_1 \le L\,\big(1 + \tfrac12\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\big) .\]
Proof

We must show that the distance does not decrease in the displacement and that it grows by at most \(\tfrac12 L\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\). We compute the square of the new distance.

  1. Let \(\vek d = \overrightarrow{PQ}\). The points after the displacement are \(P + \delta\vek u(P)\) and \(Q + \delta\vek u(Q)\), and the vector between them is \(\vek d + \delta\vek u(Q) - \delta\vek u(P)\), because coordinates are subtracted component by component ((1.8)).
  2. The vector \(\delta\vek a\) cancels in the difference \(\delta\vek u(Q) - \delta\vek u(P)\). By item 2 of lemma 2.34 on the properties of the vector product, \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\overrightarrow{OQ} - \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\overrightarrow{OP} = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times(\overrightarrow{OQ} - \overrightarrow{OP}) = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d\), because \(\overrightarrow{OQ} - \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{PQ}\). The vector between the points after the displacement is therefore \(\vek d + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d\).
  3. By the formula for the square of the length of a sum (lemma 2.9), the square of the length of the new vector
    \[L_1^2 = |\vek d + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d|^2 = |\vek d|^2 + 2\,\vek d\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d) + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d|^2 .\]
  4. By item 4 of the lemma on the properties of the vector product, the vector product is perpendicular to its second factor, \(\vek d\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d) = 0\). This is the right angle at \(Q\). Hence \(L_1^2 = L^2 + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d|^2\), which is the Pythagorean theorem for the triangle \(PQQ'\).
  5. By item 5 of the same lemma, \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d|^2 = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\,L^2 - (\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot\vek d)^2\), which lies between \(0\) and \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\,L^2\). The leg \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek d\) is longest when \(\vek d\) is perpendicular to the axis of the turn, as in the figure, and vanishes when \(\vek d\) lies along the axis. Hence \(L^2 \le L_1^2 \le L^2\,(1 + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2)\).
  6. The expanded square \(\big(1 + \tfrac12\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\big)^2 = 1 + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2 + \tfrac14\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^4\) is not smaller than \(1 + |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\). Hence \(L^2 \le L_1^2 \le \big(L\,(1 + \tfrac12\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2)\big)^2\).
  7. The numbers \(L\), \(L_1\) and \(L\,(1 + \tfrac12\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2)\) are nonnegative. By lemma 1.29, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, the inequalities between the squares carry over to the numbers themselves.

Source: CM, PDF p. 143, shows that the displacement \(\delta\vek u = \boldsymbol\omega\times\delta\vek x\) is a small rigid turn, and SADD (2.2.10) (PDF p. 45) writes the displacement \(\delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) in components as the most general small rigid displacement. The bound and its proof are not from the sources.

The lemma says that the distance between two points changes by at most \(\tfrac12 L\,|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|^2\), a term with the square of a small number. For the lever, with \(L = 160\) cm from \(A\) to \(B\) and \(\delta\varphi = 0.001\), this is at most \(8 \cdot 10^{-5}\) cm, while \(B\) moves by \(0.12\) cm. This is where assumption 1 of small displacements enters. For \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi| = 0.2\), as the two figures are drawn, the distance could change by 2 % of the length, and such a displacement would not respect rigidity even approximately. A displacement of the form \(\delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) therefore respects rigidity to first order and is, by definition 4.70, a virtual displacement of a free rigid body. The converse also holds, if we read "to first order" as saying that the displacement has a zero small strain tensor at every point of the body, that is, that no short fibre gets longer by a first-order term (SADD (2.2.10), PDF p. 45). For a body in one piece, whose interior points form a connected open set (definition 1.136), and a continuously differentiable displacement, such a displacement is at the interior points of the body, by theorem 1.140 on a displacement field without strain, of the form \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r\) with a constant vector \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\). By (2.39), \(\mat W\,\vek r = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) for \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = (W_{\mathrm{zy}}, W_{\mathrm{xz}}, W_{\mathrm{yx}})\), because \(W_{\mathrm{ij}} = -W_{\mathrm{ji}}\) and the diagonal entries of the matrix \(\mat W\) are zero (lemma 1.104, item 3).

Now let us show why the virtual work for the lever was the moment of the forces times \(\delta\varphi\). We will read the proof on figure 4.138, which shows both parts of the displacement on forces. The axis \(z\) again points toward the viewer. On the left is a body on which act the force \(\vek F_1\) at the point \(A_1\) and the force \(\vek F_2\) at the point \(A_2\). The displacement \(\delta\vek a\) moves the body to the blue dashed position and moves both points of application by the same vector. Because the displacement is the same for both forces, we may add the forces first: beside the body they are drawn one after the other, and the orange arrow from the start of the first to the end of the second is their resultant. On the right is the same body in a turn about the axis through \(O\). The point of application \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the vector \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\) moves by \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\), perpendicular to \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\). We split the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\) into the part along \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\), grey and dashed, and the part \(F_\perp\) in the direction of the displacement, orange. The part along \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\) is perpendicular to the displacement and does no work. Only \(F_\perp\) does work, on a displacement of length \(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\,|\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}|\):

\[F_\perp \cdot \big(|\delta\boldsymbol\varphi|\,|\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}|\big) = |\delta\boldsymbol\varphi| \cdot \big(|\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}|\,F_\perp\big) .\]

In the right bracket stands lever arm times force, that is the moment of the force about the axis through \(O\), as \((300 \cdot 40)\,\delta\varphi\) was for the lever. In the figure \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\) lie in the plane perpendicular to the axis. The theorem says the same for arbitrary directions, and in the proof the scalar triple product does this regrouping.

Theorem 4.73 Principle of virtual displacements for a rigid body (načelo virtualnih pomikov za togo telo)

Let the forces \(\vek F_1, \dots, \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) act on a rigid body at the points \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{k}}\), the forces of the supports among them. Let \(O\) be a point and \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}} = \overrightarrow{OA_{\mathrm{i}}}\). For arbitrary vectors \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) let the point \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) move by \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \delta\vek a + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then the virtual work of the forces is

\[\begin{aligned} \vek F_1\cdot\delta\vek u_1 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{k}} &= \delta\vek a\cdot(\vek F_1 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}) \\ &\quad + \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek r_1\times\vek F_1 + \dots + \vek r_{\mathrm{k}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{4.44}\]

The body is in equilibrium exactly when the virtual work of the forces is zero for all vectors \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\).

Proof

We first rearrange the virtual work of each force separately, then we check both directions of the claim about equilibrium.

  1. By item 2 of lemma 2.12 on the properties of the scalar product, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\delta\vek a + \vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}})\). The first term is the work of the force on the common displacement, the second the work on the displacement due to the turn.
  2. The second term is a scalar triple product, which in the figure becomes the part of the force in the direction of the displacement times the length of the displacement. By definition 2.72 it is the determinant of the matrix with the columns \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) and \(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\), and by the cyclic exchange of the columns (lemma 2.73 on the properties of the determinant, item 1)
    \[\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}) = \det(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}, \delta\boldsymbol\varphi, \vek r_{\mathrm{i}}) = \det(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi, \vek r_{\mathrm{i}}, \vek F_{\mathrm{i}}) = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\cdot(\vek r_{\mathrm{i}}\times\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}) .\]
  3. We sum over \(i\) from \(1\) to \(k\). The vector \(\delta\vek a\) is the same for all forces, so it multiplies their sum, and in the same way \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) multiplies the sum of the moments. By the symmetry and the distributivity of the scalar product (the lemma on the properties of the scalar product, items 1 and 2) we get (4.44).
  4. Let the body be in equilibrium. By definition 2.3 and by equation (2.45) the resultant \(\vek F_1 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the sum of the moments about \(O\) are zero vectors. If the moment condition was written about some other point, the sum of the moments about \(O\) equals the sum about that point, so it is the zero vector. This is what lemma 2.39 says, that the moment does not depend on the point, and we may use it because the resultant is zero. Both terms on the right side of (4.44) are therefore zero for all \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\).
  5. Conversely, let the virtual work be zero for all \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\). For \(\delta\vek a = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\), when we move all points by one unit in the direction of the axis \(x\), the right side of (4.44) is the \(x\) component of the resultant, which is therefore zero. In the same way, with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the other two components are zero. The resultant is the zero vector.
  6. With \(\delta\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), when we turn the body about one of the coordinate axes through \(O\), we get in the same way that the sum of the moments about \(O\) is the zero vector. By definition 2.3 and equation (2.45) the body is in equilibrium.

Source: EP §4.2.1 (pp. 167-168) for forces acting at one point and with the displacement \(\delta\vek u\) only, GR §1.2.1 (PDF pp. 35-36) for a system of particles. The form with \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) is not from the sources.

The proof showed where the connection between work and moments comes from. The displacement \(\delta\vek a\) multiplies the resultant of the forces, the turn \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) the sum of the moments. Because the vectors \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\) are arbitrary, zero virtual work means six equations, three for the forces and three for the moments, which is exactly equilibrium. Where did rigidity enter the theorem? In the choice of displacements. A rigid body can move only as a whole, with three numbers in \(\delta\vek a\) and three in \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\), so we get six equations and no more. For a body that deforms, these displacements still give the equilibrium of the whole, but they do not say whether each of its parts is in equilibrium. A hanging bar with its weight and the force of its support is in equilibrium as a whole, but how the axial force changes along it these six equations do not tell. For the bar we will therefore need more virtual displacements, ones that also stretch the bar.

And a body held by supports? For the lever the pin does not allow all \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\), only \(\delta\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = (0, 0, \delta\varphi)\). The force of the pin acts at a point that does not move, so its virtual work is zero, and only the load and the push remain in the equation. The right side of (4.44) is then \(\delta\varphi\) times the \(z\) component of the sum of the moments, and zero virtual work gives exactly the equation \(12\,000 - 120\,P = 0\), in which the force of the pin does not appear. In general: when a body is held by ideal supports, the virtual work of the forces other than the support forces is zero for every virtual displacement the supports allow exactly when those equilibrium equations hold in which the support forces do not appear (GR §1.2.1, PDF pp. 35-36). The support forces then follow from the remaining equations.

A support force can also be found with virtual work (figure 4.139). We remove the support in our minds and keep its force as a force on the body. Then we move the body exactly in the direction the support prevented. The whole lever we move vertically up to the blue dashed position, by \(\delta\vek a = (0, \delta v, 0)\) with \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\). The points \(A\), \(O\) and \(B\) move by the same \(\delta v\). The load of 300 N and the push of 100 N point against the displacement, the force of the pin with the component \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) in its direction. The virtual work of all three forces is

\[(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}} + \vek R)\cdot\delta\vek a = (-300 - 100 + R_{\mathrm{y}})\,\delta v ,\]

and because it must be zero for every \(\delta v\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}} = 400\) N. Such a displacement picks out a single unknown force. On this idea rests the unit dummy-displacement method (metoda enotskega virtualnega pomika). In a structure that can deform, we choose a virtual displacement that is a unit vector in the direction of the sought force at the point where we look for the force, and zero at the other points where forces act. For the rigid lever we moved the whole lever, so the known forces \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\) also stayed in the equation. The word dummy is an old word for virtual (EP §4.2.3, p. 172). We will use it for the bracket at the end of the section.

4.4.4 A bar under a distributed load

For the rigid body a virtual displacement was described by six numbers, the components of the vectors \(\delta\vek a\) and \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi\). A bar, however, can stretch, and every piece of it can move differently. A virtual displacement of a bar will therefore be a function. First we set up the model precisely.

A straight bar lies along the axis \(x\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\) and is fixed at the end \(x = 0\), so that this end does not move, \(u(0) = 0\). All forces act along the axis of the bar, so the bar only stretches or shortens, and the internal force in every cross section is the axial force \(N(x)\) (definition 2.5). This is the first assumption of the model: a force across the axis would bend the bar, a shear force and a moment would appear in the cross section, and a single axial force would no longer describe the internal forces. Chapter 5 treats that case. As in chapter 1 we describe the bar by the displacement \(u(x)\): the whole cross section at the place \(x\) moves along the axis by \(u(x)\). This is the second assumption, and for the hanging bar we can say what it costs. By the field (1.19) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) the edge of a cross section, 5 mm from the axis, moves down by \(0{,}3\,a \cdot (5\ \text{mm})^2 = 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-12}\) m less than the axis, while the lower end of the bar moves by \(a\,L^2 = 19{,}52\) µm. The difference across a cross section is less than one ten-millionth of the largest displacement, so a single displacement \(u(x)\) for the whole cross section is enough. The thinning of the bar, which the same field describes (section 1.7.1), is left out of this model. This does not change the results of this section, because the equilibrium of the forces along the axis and their work contain only the force along the axis and the displacement along the axis. At the free end \(x = L\) a force \(F\) acts in the direction of the axis \(x\), positive when it pulls toward increasing \(x\). Along the length of the bar forces are distributed that act on every piece of it, like weight in the hanging bar (figure 4.140). We describe them by the force per unit length. Imagine also the cross section at \(x\), which separates the bar into a left and a right part. On the right part the left part acts across the section with the force \(-N(x)\), drawn in the figure for \(N(x) > 0\) and pointing left.

Definition 4.74 Distributed axial load (porazdeljena osna obtežba)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). A distributed axial load (porazdeljena osna obtežba) is a continuous function \(q\) on the interval \([0, L]\) whose input is the place \(x\) on the bar and whose output is the force per unit length of the bar in the direction of the axis \(x\) at that place. The force in the direction of the axis \(x\) on the part of the bar between the cross sections \(x_1 < x_2\) is

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}_1}^{x_2} q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

Source: ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF pp. 66-67), where it is written \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\), EP example 3.5.3 (p. 148), where it is written \(f\).

Why is the force on a part of the bar an integral? The function \(q\) gives the force per unit length at every place. On a short piece \([t_{\mathrm{i}-1}, t_{\mathrm{i}}]\) a force therefore acts that lies between \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the smallest and the largest value of the function \(q\) on the piece. The forces of the pieces add up, so the force on a part of the bar lies between the lower and the upper sum of the function \(q\) for every partition, and by lemma 3.50, item 2, it is the integral. The same reasoning gave us in section 3.5.6 the weight of the part of the hanging bar below a cross section. For the hanging bar \(q(x) = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m at every place. Why do we require continuity? So that the integral exists (theorem 3.53) and so that we may use the fundamental theorem of calculus. A force acting at a single place inside the bar, for example the weight at the middle of the hanging bar from section 1.12.2, is not of this form. There the axial force jumps, and such loads are excluded in this section.

From the equilibrium of the parts of the bar we now get the axial force, as we got it for the hanging bar in section 2.1. Before we write this as a lemma, let us see in figure 4.141 what to expect.

Above an axis that carries the places \(t\) on the bar we draw the graph of the load \(q\), here for \(q > 0\). Its height above the place \(x\) is the force per unit length \(q(x)\). The cross section at \(x\) splits the area under the graph into two parts. The area to the right of the cross section is, by definition 4.74, the force with which the load acts on the right part of the bar. This part is also pulled by the force \(F\) at the end, and it is held only by the force across the cross section. The axial force in the cross section must therefore be the sum of the two. Below the first graph we draw one more axis with the places \(x\) and above it this sum: above the place \(x\) first a segment of length \(F\), and on top of it a segment that measures the blue area. Their top is the value \(N(x)\). Doing this for every cross section gives the graph of the axial force. At \(x = L\) no area is left to the right of the cross section, so there the graph comes down to the height \(F\). How fast does the graph fall? Move the cross section to the right by \(\Delta x\). The blue area loses a narrow strip of width \(\Delta x\) and height about \(q(x)\), so the axial force decreases by about \(q(x)\,\Delta x\). Per unit length it therefore drops by \(q(x)\), and this is the slope of the graph. The area to the left of the cross section will be needed in the proof. The lemma says all this exactly.

Lemma 4.75 Equilibrium of an axially loaded bar (ravnovesje palice pod osno obtežbo)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). Let a distributed axial load \(q\) act on it, and at the end \(x = L\) a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). Let every part of the bar between the cross section \(x\) and the end \(L\) be in equilibrium. Then the axial force in the cross section \(x\) is

\[N(x) = F + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{4.45}\]

where for \(x = L\) the integral is zero. The function \(N\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and

\[N'(x) + q(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \in (0, L) , \qquad N(L) = F . \tag{4.46}\]
Proof

We first get \(N(x)\) from the equilibrium of a part of the bar, then the derivative from the fundamental theorem of calculus.

  1. Let \(0 \le x < L\). Three forces along the axis \(x\) act on the part of the bar from the cross section \(x\) to the end \(L\). By definition 2.5, \(N(x)\) is the \(x\) component of the force with which the part on the side of larger \(x\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(x\). By the law of action and reaction (section 2.1) the left part acts on our right part with the opposite force, whose \(x\) component is \(-N(x)\). The distributed load acts on it, by definition 4.74, with the force \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), which is the blue area, and on its end acts the force \(F\).
  2. All forces point along the axis \(x\), so the equilibrium of forces (2.2) is a single equation for the \(x\) components: \(-N(x) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t + F = 0\). This gives (4.45).
  3. At \(x = L\) no part of the bar is left, only the end cross section. The force across it is the force at the end, \(N(L) = F\), which is (4.45) with the integral zero.
  4. Let \(G(x) = \int_0^x q(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) for \(x \in [0, L]\), the area to the left of the cross section, with \(G(0) = 0\) by the convention of definition 3.49. For \(0 < x < L\), by additivity (lemma 3.54 on the properties of the integral, item 2), \(\int_0^L q = G(x) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L q\), the area under the whole graph is the sum of the areas to the left and to the right of the cross section, so \(N(x) = F + G(L) - G(x)\). The same equality holds at \(x = 0\), where \(G(0) = 0\), and at \(x = L\), where it gives \(N(L) = F\).
  5. The function \(q\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 2, the function \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and \(G'(x) = q(x)\). The grey area grows as fast as the graph is high at its right edge.
  6. The number \(F + G(L)\) is constant. By step 4, \(N\) is therefore continuous on \([0, L]\), and by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, items 1, 2 and 4) \(N\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\) with the derivative \(N'(x) = 0 - G'(x) = -q(x)\), which is the slope of the graph of the axial force. This is the first equation of (4.46), the second is step 3.

Source: EP example 3.5.3, equations (4) and (5) (pp. 148-149), derived from minimum energy for a linear elastic bar that also has a spring at its end, ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF pp. 66-67). The proof from the equilibrium of parts is not from the sources.

Let us test the lemma on the hanging bar (figure 4.141, the two graphs on the right). There \(q = \rho g A\) and \(F = 0\), because the lower end is free. By (4.45) \(N(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L \rho g A\,\mathrm{d}t = \rho g A\,(L - x)\), which is (2.3), and \(N'(x) = -\rho g A = -q(x)\). In numbers: the area under the whole graph of the load is \(\rho g A\,L = 6{,}010 \cdot 10 = 60{,}10\) N, the weight of the whole bar, and this is the axial force at the top. From there it drops by \(6{,}010\) N over every metre, down to zero at the lower end. The equation \(N' + q = 0\) says that the axial force drops over every metre of the bar by exactly the load on that metre. It is the equation \(\sigma' + \rho g = 0\) from section 2.7.2, multiplied by the cross-section area \(A\).

4.4.5 The principle for a bar

What does a virtual displacement of a bar look like? Let us draw it (figure 4.142).

We draw the bar before the virtual displacement grey dashed, and the bar after it orange below. The fixed end must not move, this is the constraint the support imposes, so \(\delta u(0) = 0\). There are no other constraints. The bar is not rigid, so every piece of it may stretch. The piece at \(x\) therefore moves by its own \(\delta u(x)\), the free end by its own \(\delta u(L)\). A virtual displacement is thus a function of the place \(x\): above every place we draw how far the cross section at that place moves. This function may be almost arbitrary. We will require only that it is differentiable and that its derivative is continuous. Physically this means that there are no cracks in the bar, since at a crack the displacement would change abruptly, and that the stretching changes from piece to piece without jumps. Mathematically we need it for the integrals and for the fundamental theorem of calculus. The requirement does not restrict us, since all polynomials with \(\delta u(0) = 0\) satisfy it, and the theorem below will hold for every such function.

How large is the virtual work of the external forces? On a short piece \([x, x + \Delta x]\) the load acts with a force of about \(q(x)\,\Delta x\), and the piece moves by about \(\delta u(x)\). The virtual work is about \(q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\Delta x\), and by the same reasoning as above the sum over the pieces approaches an integral. The force at the end contributes \(F\,\delta u(L)\), the support nothing, because \(\delta u(0) = 0\). The virtual work of the external forces is therefore

\[\int_0^L q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) .\]

For a rigid body in equilibrium the virtual work was zero. For a bar it is not. Take the hanging bar and the virtual displacement \(\delta u(x) = c\,x\), in which every piece stretches by the same fraction \(c\). The virtual work of the weight is \(\int_0^L \rho g A\,c\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = c\,\rho g A\,L^2/2\), which is not zero for \(c \neq 0\), although the bar is in equilibrium. For \(c = 10^{-6}\), when the lower end moves by \(c\,L = 10\) µm, it is \(10^{-6} \cdot 6{,}010 \cdot 100 / 2 = 3{,}005 \cdot 10^{-4}\) J. Where is the difference? In a bar the internal forces also do work when the pieces stretch.

Look at one piece of the bar between the cross sections \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) (figure 4.143). The left part of the bar acts on it with the force \(-N(x_1)\), the right part with the force \(N(x_2)\), and the load acts on it, and all these forces are in equilibrium. The piece passes from its position before the displacement to its position after it. In this the left face of the piece moves by \(\delta u(x_1)\), the right one by \(\delta u(x_2)\). We split the displacement into two parts. First the whole piece moves by \(\delta u(x_1)\), as a rigid body, both faces by the same distance. In this part the virtual work of the forces on the piece is the resultant of the forces times \(\delta u(x_1)\), and this is zero, because the piece is in equilibrium, just as for the rigid body. Then the piece stretches: the right face moves by a further \(\delta u(x_2) - \delta u(x_1)\), while the left face stays. In this part the work is done mainly by the force \(N(x_2)\) on the right face, \(N(x_2)\,(\delta u(x_2) - \delta u(x_1))\). The load adds a part that we neglect. Why may we? During the stretching the points of the piece move by at most about as much as the right face, and the force of the load on the piece is about \(q(x)\,\Delta x\). Its part of the work is therefore at most about \(q(x)\,\Delta x\) times the stretching, smaller than the work of the force \(N(x_2)\) by a factor of about \(q(x)\,\Delta x / N(x)\). In the hanging bar the axial force at the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, is \(6{,}010 \cdot 5 = 30{,}05\) N, and the weight of a piece of length \(\Delta x = 1\) mm is \(0{,}006010\) N, so the factor is \(0{,}006010 / 30{,}05 = 0{,}0002\). The shorter the pieces, the smaller it is. The sum of the neglected parts over all pieces is small as well: it is at most \(\max|q| \cdot \max|\delta u'| \cdot L\) times the length of the longest piece, and goes to zero with that length. For a short piece of length \(\Delta x\) the stretching is about \(\delta u'(x)\,\Delta x\), so the virtual work of all forces on the piece is about \(N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\,\Delta x\). The number \(\delta u'(x)\) is the virtual strain (virtualna deformacija) close to the place \(x\), the virtual stretching per unit length.

Now we add up the virtual works of all the pieces. At every inner cross section, equal and opposite forces act on the two neighbouring pieces: what is the force \(N(x_2)\) on the right face for our piece is the force \(-N(x_2)\) on the left face for its right neighbour, drawn like the force on the left face of our piece. The faces of both pieces move by the same \(\delta u\), so their parts of the work cancel. Nothing remains of the internal forces, and the sum is the virtual work of the external forces. On the other side are the sums of \(N\,\delta u'\,\Delta x\), which approach an integral. We therefore expect

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) .\]

Books call the left side the internal virtual work (notranje virtualno delo) and the right side the external virtual work (zunanje virtualno delo) (EP §3.4.1, p. 128, ONATE1 (2.4), PDF p. 67). The internal virtual work has the same form as the work of the stresses on a small box in section 3.6.3. There the stress was multiplied by the change of the strain, here the axial force is multiplied by the virtual strain. The reasoning with pieces was only approximate. Now we prove the equality exactly.

Theorem 4.76 Principle of virtual displacements for a bar (načelo virtualnih pomikov za palico)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and be fixed at \(x = 0\). Let a distributed axial load \(q\), continuous on \([0, L]\), act on it, and at the end \(x = L\) a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). Let the function \(N\) be continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and satisfy \(N'(x) + q(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in (0, L)\) and \(N(L) = F\). Let a virtual displacement of the bar be any function \(\delta u\) that is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), has a derivative \(\delta u'\) continuous on \([0, L]\), and satisfies \(\delta u(0) = 0\). Then for every virtual displacement of the bar

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) . \tag{4.47}\]
Proof

We must show that the two integrals differ by exactly \(F\,\delta u(L)\). The proof is an integration by parts. We cannot use corollary 3.56 on integration by parts literally, because it requires the derivative of the function \(N\) also at the end points of the interval, so we repeat its proof with item 1 of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

  1. Let \(G(x) = N(x)\,\delta u(x)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\), the product of the heights of the graph of the axial force and the graph of the virtual displacement above the same place \(x\). The function \(\delta u\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, so it is continuous there (lemma 1.80). The function \(N\) is continuous by assumption. Hence \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) as a product of continuous functions (corollary 1.65).
  2. Let \(0 < x < L\). The functions \(N\) and \(\delta u\) are differentiable at \(x\). By the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3) and by the assumption \(N'(x) = -q(x)\),
    \[G'(x) = N'(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x) = -q(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x) .\]
  3. The function \(f(x) = -q(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), because \(q\), \(\delta u\), \(N\) and \(\delta u'\) are continuous there, and sums and products of continuous functions are continuous (corollary 1.65). By step 2, \(G'(x) = f(x)\) for every \(x \in (0, L)\).
  4. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 1, with the functions \(G\) and \(f\),
    \[\int_0^L \big(-q(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0) = N(L)\,\delta u(L) - N(0)\,\delta u(0) .\]
    At \(x = L\) the terms are \(N(L)\) and \(\delta u(L)\), at \(x = 0\) the term is \(\delta u(0)\).
  5. Because \(\delta u(0) = 0\) and \(N(L) = F\), the right side is \(F\,\delta u(L)\).
  6. By linearity (lemma 3.54 on the properties of the integral, item 1) the left side is \(\int_0^L N\,\delta u' - \int_0^L q\,\delta u\). We add \(\int_0^L q\,\delta u\) to both sides and get (4.47).

Source: EP §3.4.1, example 3.4.2 (pp. 129-131) for a cantilever beam with an axial load, ONATE1 (2.3), (2.4) (PDF p. 67), EP example 3.5.3 (p. 148), where the integration by parts is done in the opposite direction.

What did the proof show? Equation (4.47) is the equilibrium of the bar, multiplied by the virtual displacement and integrated over the length of the bar. Integration by parts moved the derivative from the axial force to the virtual displacement. In doing so two terms at the end points appeared. The term at the fixed end dropped out, because \(\delta u(0) = 0\), and the term at the free end gave \(F\,\delta u(L)\), because \(N(L) = F\). This is the exact form of the reasoning with pieces. The end-point term is the virtual work of the forces acting at the ends, while the internal forces between the pieces cancelled.

Three things are worth remembering. First, the theorem holds for every material, since we used only the equilibrium (4.46). Second, the force of the support at \(x = 0\) does not appear in the equation, because \(\delta u(0) = 0\), just as the force of the pin for the lever. Third, the axial force in the equation is not differentiated, only multiplied by \(\delta u'\). For a linear elastic bar with Young's modulus \(E\) the axial force is \(N = A\,\sigma = E A\,u'\), because the stress is \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\) ((3.1)) and the strain \(\varepsilon = u'\) (definition 1.10). The equation then reads

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L E A\,u'(x)\,\delta u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) \\ &\quad \text{for every virtual displacement of the bar } \delta u , \end{aligned}\]

and only the displacement \(u\) with its first derivative appears in it. The finite element method for bars builds on this form (ONATE1 (2.4), PDF p. 67), and we will meet it in chapter 7.

The equality we checked at the end of section 3.5.8 is exactly this theorem. There we computed for the hanging bar (graph of the axial force and graph of the load) that \(\int_0^L N\,u'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\int_0^L \rho g A\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\) are both \(7{,}821 \cdot 10^{-4}\) J. This is equation (4.47) with \(q = \rho g A\), \(F = 0\) and the real displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) in the role of the virtual displacement. The real displacement is also a virtual displacement of the bar, since it is a polynomial and satisfies \(u(0) = 0\).

4.4.6 Mathematics: the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations

Does the converse hold? If some axial force \(N\) satisfies equation (4.47) for every virtual displacement of the bar, is the bar then in equilibrium, that is, do \(N' + q = 0\) and \(N(L) = F\) hold? Only a yes tells us that the equation with virtual work fully replaces equilibrium.

The idea is this. Take a bar whose left end is fixed to a wall. Along its axis it is loaded by the load \(q\), and at the free end at \(x = L\) by the force \(F\). We do the integration by parts in the opposite direction and get

\[\int_0^L (N' + q)\,\delta u\,\mathrm{d}x + (F - N(L))\,\delta u(L) = 0\]

for every virtual displacement. The integral adds up the values of the function \(N' + q\) along the axis \(x\), each multiplied by the virtual displacement at the same place. A virtual displacement is therefore a probe: it feels the function \(N' + q\) where it is itself nonzero and does not notice it elsewhere. If the function \(N' + q\) were positive somewhere, we would choose a virtual displacement that is nonzero and positive only there. The product would be positive there and zero elsewhere, so the integral would be positive, while the equation demands zero. The function \(N' + q\) therefore may not differ from zero anywhere. Once the integral drops out, \((F - N(L))\,\delta u(L) = 0\) is left. Now we take a virtual displacement that is zero at the fixed end but not at the free end, for example \(\delta u = x\) with the value \(L\) at the end. What is left is \((F - N(L))\,L = 0\), so the force \(N(L)\), with which the interior of the bar pulls the end face, equals the force \(F\).

The conclusion with the bump has to be proved. What if the function is positive at one single point only, which no bump notices? The answer is given by a lemma that books name after the calculus of variations (variacijski račun), the branch of mathematics that looks for the functions for which some integral is smallest (EP §3.5, pp. 134 and 142).

From history

In 1696 Johann Bernoulli challenged the best mathematicians in Europe to find the curve along which a body falls from one point to another in the shortest time (figure 4.145). The second point is 4 m to the right and 4 m lower, the acceleration of gravity \(g\) points down. The figure compares the straight path and the circular arc with the cycloid, along which the time is the shortest (CAJORI, PDF p. 233). Leibniz found it on the very day he received the problem, and it was also solved by Newton, de l'Hôpital and the two Bernoullis (CAJORI, PDF p. 233). Here we do not seek a number that is largest or smallest, but a whole curve. So is the problem we met in section 1.13.3: among closed curves of a given length, find the one that encloses the largest area (EP, PDF p. 2). From problems of this kind Euler, who started from the researches of Johann and Jakob Bernoulli, created a new branch of mathematics. He collected his researches in the book Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas maximi minimive proprietate gaudentes of 1744 (CAJORI, PDF p. 250).

At the age of nineteen Lagrange sent Euler a general method for such problems. Euler recognized its generality and withheld a paper he had written earlier, so that Lagrange could complete his work (BALL, PDF p. 430, CAJORI, PDF p. 267). Euler derived the calculus with geometric arguments and assumed that the two end points of the sought curve are fixed. Lagrange separated the principles of the calculus from geometry by deriving them by calculation, and removed the restriction: he allowed all coordinates of the curve to vary at the same time (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). The name calculus of variations was introduced by Euler in 1766 (CAJORI, PDF p. 267). The virtual displacement of the bar, with which we compare a position with its neighbours in this section, is an idea of the same kind.

The first to calculate with the calculus of variations took the theorems underlying its formulas for axioms, but they are not. Proving them was begun by Todhunter, Mikhail Ostrogradski, Jacobi, Joseph Bertrand, Paul du Bois-Reymond, G. Erdmann and Alfred Clebsch. The turning point came with Weierstrass, who applied modern requirements of rigor to the calculus of variations and gave his results in lectures from 1872, so they spread slowly (CAJORI, PDF p. 387).

Back to the lemma that we need for the conclusion that the bar is in equilibrium. We prove it instead of assuming it. Let us first draw what it claims and how we shall prove it (figure 4.146).

Above the axis \(x\) we draw a continuous function \(g\) on the interval \([a, b]\). The lemma claims that such a function is zero everywhere if its integral with every admissible function \(\psi\) is zero. Suppose the opposite, that it is positive at the point \(c\), with the value \(g(c)\). Because it is continuous, its value cannot change much near \(c\): between \(c - \delta\) and \(c + \delta\) it stays above half of that value, \(g(c)/2\). Inside this interval we choose the points \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Below the graph of \(g\) we draw, on the same interval \([a, b]\), the function \(\psi\), which is a bump between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and zero elsewhere. The bump is narrow, so we enlarge it: at the bottom the interval between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) of length \(h\) is stretched along the axis. The bump \(\psi\) joins the axis gently at both ends. The product \(g\,\psi\) is a bump as well, because \(g\) is positive there. The area under it is the integral, which by the assumption of the lemma should be zero. It is not, since over the middle half of the interval a rectangle of positive area lies below the product. We return to the grey dashed curve, the function without the squares, after the proof.

Lemma 4.77 Fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations (osnovna lema variacijskega računa)

Let \(g\) be a function continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\), and let

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g(x)\,\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\]

for every function \(\psi\) that is differentiable at every point of the interval \([a, b]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), has a derivative continuous on \([a, b]\), and satisfies \(\psi(a) = \psi(b) = 0\). Then \(g(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).

Proof

The proof is by contradiction. If \(g\) were positive at some point, it would be positive on a small interval around it because of continuity, and with a function \(\psi\) that is positive only there the integral would be positive.

  1. Suppose that \(g(c) \neq 0\) for some \(c \in [a, b]\). If \(g(c) < 0\), we take \(-g\) instead of \(g\). The graph of \(-g\) is the graph of \(g\) mirrored across the axis \(x\), so a valley below the axis becomes a peak above it. This function is continuous, it satisfies the same assumption, because \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (-g)\,\psi = -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\psi = 0\) by linearity (lemma 3.54 on the properties of the integral, item 1), and it is positive at \(c\). We may therefore assume \(g(c) > 0\).
  2. The function \(g\) is continuous at \(c\). By definition 1.59 with \(\eta = g(c)/2\) there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(x) - g(c)| < g(c)/2\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\), that is, between \(c - \delta\) and \(c + \delta\). For these \(x\), \(g(x) > g(c) - g(c)/2 = g(c)/2\). This is where continuity enters, the only assumption on \(g\). Without it \(g\) could be positive at one single point, and the proof would stop here (the example after the lemma, figure 4.147). We take one half because we only need \(g\) to stay above some positive number. Any fraction between \(0\) and \(1\) would do equally well, it would only change the number in step 10.
  3. Let \(a'\) be the larger of the numbers \(a\) and \(c - \delta\), and \(b'\) the smaller of the numbers \(b\) and \(c + \delta\). Each of the numbers \(a\) and \(c - \delta\) is smaller than each of the numbers \(b\) and \(c + \delta\), because \(a < b\), \(a \le c < c + \delta\), \(c - \delta < c \le b\) and \(c - \delta < c + \delta\). Hence \(a' < b'\). In the figure \(c - \delta\) and \(c + \delta\) are inside \([a, b]\), so there \(a' = c - \delta\) and \(b' = c + \delta\). When \(c\) is near an end point \(a\) or \(b\), or is one of them, the interval around \(c\) reaches beyond \([a, b]\), and the numbers \(a'\) and \(b'\) trim it. We take \(\alpha = a' + (b' - a')/3\) and \(\beta = b' - (b' - a')/3\). We cut off a third on each side so that \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are strictly inside \([a, b]\) also when \(a' = a\) or \(b' = b\), since \(\psi\) must be zero at \(a\) and \(b\). Then \(a < \alpha < \beta < b\), and every \(x \in [\alpha, \beta]\) satisfies \(c - \delta < x < c + \delta\). By step 2, therefore, \(g(x) > g(c)/2\) for every \(x \in [\alpha, \beta]\).
  4. For every real number \(x\) let \(\psi(x) = (x - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - x)^2\) when \(\alpha \le x \le \beta\), and \(\psi(x) = 0\) otherwise (the blue curve in figure 4.146). The function \(\psi\) is nowhere negative. Because \(a < \alpha\) and \(\beta < b\), \(\psi(a) = \psi(b) = 0\).
  5. The function \(\psi\) is differentiable at every point \(x < \alpha\) and \(x > \beta\) with the derivative \(0\), because it is constant on an open interval around such a point (the horizontal parts of the graph). On the interval \((\alpha, \beta)\) it equals the polynomial \(p(x) = (x - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - x)^2\) (the bump in the enlargement). The expanded squares \((x - \alpha)^2 = x^2 - 2\alpha x + \alpha^2\) and \((\beta - x)^2 = \beta^2 - 2\beta x + x^2\) have, by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82), the derivatives \(2\,(x - \alpha)\) and \(-2\,(\beta - x)\), so by the product rule
    \[p'(x) = 2\,(x - \alpha)\,(\beta - x)^2 - 2\,(x - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - x) = 2\,(x - \alpha)\,(\beta - x)\,(\alpha + \beta - 2x) .\]
  6. At the point \(\alpha\), \(\psi(\alpha) = 0\). For \(0 < h < \beta - \alpha\) the difference quotient is \((\psi(\alpha + h) - \psi(\alpha))/h = h^2\,(\beta - \alpha - h)^2/h = h\,(\beta - \alpha - h)^2\), and for \(h < 0\) it is \(0\), because there \(\psi(\alpha + h) = 0\). In both cases its absolute value is at most \(|h|\,(\beta - \alpha)^2\). For \(\eta > 0\) we take \(\delta\) the smaller of the numbers \(\beta - \alpha\) and \(\eta/(\beta - \alpha)^2\), and for \(0 < |h| < \delta\) the absolute value of the quotient is smaller than \(\eta\). By definitions 1.5 and 1.9, therefore, \(\psi'(\alpha) = 0\). In the same way \(\psi'(\beta) = 0\). In the enlargement the bump touches the axis at \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) with a horizontal tangent.
  7. The derivative \(\psi'\) is continuous at every point. Outside the interval \([\alpha, \beta]\) it is constantly zero on an open interval around the point. On \((\alpha, \beta)\) it equals the polynomial \(p'\), which is continuous (corollary 1.65). At the point \(\alpha\), \(p'(\alpha) = 0 = \psi'(\alpha)\). For a given \(\eta > 0\) the continuity of the polynomial \(p'\) at \(\alpha\) therefore gives \(\delta > 0\), which we take at most \(\beta - \alpha\), such that \(|\psi'(x)| = |p'(x)| < \eta\) for \(\alpha \le x < \alpha + \delta\), while to the left of \(\alpha\), \(\psi'(x) = 0\). The same at the point \(\beta\). The slope of the graph of \(\psi\) therefore does not jump at \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), as it would for the function without the squares.
  8. The function \(\psi\) therefore meets all the requirements of the lemma, since it is differentiable everywhere, also at \(a\) and \(b\), where the limit from both sides also gives the limit from one side, its derivative is continuous and \(\psi(a) = \psi(b) = 0\). By assumption \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\psi = 0\).
  9. The function \(g\,\psi\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) as a product of continuous functions. By additivity (lemma 3.54, item 2), \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\psi = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^\alpha g\,\psi + \int_\alpha^\beta g\,\psi + \int_\beta^b g\,\psi\). On \([a, \alpha]\) and on \([\beta, b]\), \(\psi = 0\), so the first and the third integral are zero by the bound (the same lemma, item 3, with \(m = M = 0\)).
  10. Let \(h = \beta - \alpha\). On the middle part \([\alpha + h/4, \beta - h/4]\) both \(x - \alpha\) and \(\beta - x\) are at least \(h/4\), so \(\psi(x) \ge (h/4)^4 = h^4/256\) and \(g(x)\,\psi(x) \ge g(c)\,h^4/512\), because by step 3, \(g(x) > g(c)/2\) there. This number is the height of the rectangle that lies below the graph of the product. On the remaining two parts of the interval \([\alpha, \beta]\), \(g\,\psi \ge 0\). By additivity and the bound, therefore,
    \[\int_\alpha^\beta g\,\psi \ge 0 + \frac{g(c)\,h^4}{512} \cdot \frac{h}{2} + 0 = \frac{g(c)\,h^5}{1024} > 0 .\]
  11. By steps 9 and 10, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\psi > 0\), which contradicts step 8. Hence \(g(c) = 0\) for every \(c \in [a, b]\): there is no peak above the axis anywhere.

Source: CHK lemma 8.1 (PDF p. 157), for functions \(\psi\) with a continuous second derivative, without proof. EP lemma 3.1 (pp. 140-141) states it for arbitrary continuous functions and proves it with \(\psi = g\). The proof with the function \((x - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - x)^2\) is not from the sources.

Why not simply \(\psi = g\), as EP does (figure 4.146)? Because \(\psi\) must be zero at the end points and differentiable with a continuous derivative, and \(g\) need be neither. The \(g\) we drew, for example, is not zero at \(a\) and \(b\). EP allows any continuous function, which is why \(\psi = g\) works there. A virtual displacement of the bar must be zero at least at the attached end, and its derivative appears in the internal virtual work. The bump (grbina) \((x - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - x)^2\) is zero outside a small interval where \(g\) is positive, and the squares make it join the zero function smoothly, with zero derivative, at the end points of that interval. Without the squares, with the function \((x - \alpha)(\beta - x)\), it would have a corner at \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and would not be differentiable. What does this stricter requirement cost? Nothing. A lemma that may test only with smooth functions \(\psi\) that are zero at the end points says more: it reaches the same conclusion, \(g = 0\), with fewer tests, so we may use it also where only virtual displacements are available.

The rectangle in the figure is only a thin strip below the product, because the estimate in step 10 is crude. The integral of the bump alone is \(\int_\alpha^\beta \psi\,\mathrm{d}x = h^5/30\) (our computation with the fundamental theorem of calculus). For a function \(g\) that is close to \(g(c)\) everywhere on \([\alpha, \beta]\) the integral of the product is therefore close to \(g(c)\,h^5/30\), about 34 times the estimate \(g(c)\,h^5/1024\). This does not harm the proof, which only needs the integral to be positive.

What does the lemma reject if \(g\) is not continuous? Let us look at an example (figure 4.147).

Above the axis \(x\) take \(g(x) = 1\) at \(x = \tfrac12\) and \(g(x) = 0\) at the other \(x \in [0, 1]\). The open circle says that the value \(0\) does not hold at \(\tfrac12\), the full dot above it is the true value. The function is not continuous at \(\tfrac12\) and \(g(\tfrac12) = 1 \neq 0\). Let us show that nevertheless \(\int_0^1 g\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every admissible function \(\psi\). On the right we draw, above the same axis, one admissible function \(\psi\), which is zero at the end points. Let \(p = \psi(\tfrac12)\). The function \(g\,\psi\) is bounded, because \(\psi\) is continuous on a closed interval, and it has the value \(p\) at \(x = \tfrac12\) and \(0\) elsewhere. The case \(p > 0\) is drawn. We understand the integral by definition 3.49, with lower and upper sums.

  1. Every piece of every partition has a point other than \(\tfrac12\) where \(g\,\psi = 0\). So every \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le 0\) and every \(M_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\), hence the lower sum satisfies \(s \le 0\) and the upper sum \(S \ge 0\) for every partition. The lower integral is therefore at most \(0\) and the upper at least \(0\).
  2. For \(0 < w < 1\) we take the partition \(\{0,\ \tfrac12 - \tfrac w2,\ \tfrac12 + \tfrac w2,\ 1\}\). Only the middle piece of width \(w\) contains \(\tfrac12\), and on it the values are \(0\) and \(p\), so \(m = \min(0, p)\) and \(M = \max(0, p)\). On the other two pieces \(g\,\psi = 0\). The lower sum is thus \(\min(0, p)\,w \ge -|p|\,w\) and the upper sum \(\max(0, p)\,w \le |p|\,w\), which is the area of the rectangle over the middle piece.
  3. The lower integral is the supremum of the lower sums, so it is at least \(-|p|\,w\) for every \(w \in (0, 1)\). A number that is not smaller than any negative number \(-|p|\,w\) with arbitrarily small \(w\) is not negative (the narrower rectangle has a smaller area). Together with step 1 the lower integral equals \(0\). Likewise the upper integral is the infimum of the upper sums, which are at least \(0\) by step 1 and by step 2 can be at most \(|p|\,w\), so it equals \(0\).

The lower and upper integrals are equal, so \(\int_0^1 g\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every \(\psi\), yet \(g\) is not zero. The proof of the lemma fails here in its step 2: from \(g(\tfrac12) > 0\) it does not follow that \(g\) is positive on an interval, and a function \(\psi\) that is positive on an interval does not see the value of \(g\) at a single point, since we may narrow the rectangle over it as much as we like. That \(g\) is positive on an interval is guaranteed only by continuity.

4.4.7 Equilibrium from virtual work

With the fundamental lemma we can prove the converse of the principle for the bar of figure 4.144.

Theorem 4.78 Equilibrium of a bar from virtual displacements (ravnovesje palice iz virtualnih pomikov)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and be fixed at \(x = 0\). Let a distributed axial load \(q\), continuous on \([0, L]\), act on it, and at the end \(x = L\) a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). Let the function \(N\) be continuous on \([0, L]\) and differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and let there be a function \(p\), continuous on \([0, L]\), with \(N'(x) = p(x)\) for every \(x \in (0, L)\). If equation (4.47) holds for every function \(\delta u\) that is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative, has a derivative continuous on \([0, L]\), and satisfies \(\delta u(0) = 0\), then

\[N'(x) + q(x) = 0 \quad \text{for every } x \in (0, L) \qquad \text{and} \qquad N(L) = F .\]
Proof

We do the integration by parts in the opposite direction. The fundamental lemma then gives the equation inside the bar, and one chosen virtual displacement the condition at the end.

  1. Let \(\delta u\) be a virtual displacement of the bar as described in the theorem (the bump and the line are two of them), and \(G(x) = N(x)\,\delta u(x)\) on \([0, L]\). As in steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 4.76, \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), at every point \(x \in (0, L)\) we have \(G'(x) = p(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\), and this function is continuous on \([0, L]\).
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 1, and because \(\delta u(0) = 0\),
    \[\int_0^L \big(p(x)\,\delta u(x) + N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = N(L)\,\delta u(L) .\]
  3. By the linearity of the integral, therefore, \(\int_0^L N\,\delta u' = N(L)\,\delta u(L) - \int_0^L p\,\delta u\). We insert this into (4.47), which holds by assumption, and move all terms to the left side. The first term of the resulting equation feels the function \(p + q\) along the bar with the virtual displacement, the second belongs to the free end:
    \[\int_0^L \big(p(x) + q(x)\big)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \big(F - N(L)\big)\,\delta u(L) = 0 .\]
  4. Take a function \(\psi\) that is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, has a derivative continuous on \([0, L]\), and satisfies \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\). Such a function is a virtual displacement of the bar. For \(\delta u = \psi\) the end-point term drops out, and by step 3, \(\int_0^L (p + q)\,\psi = 0\).
  5. The function \(p + q\) is continuous on \([0, L]\). By step 4 it satisfies the assumption of the fundamental lemma 4.77 on the interval \([0, L]\), so \(p(x) + q(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\). For \(x \in (0, L)\), \(p(x) = N'(x)\), so \(N'(x) + q(x) = 0\).
  6. The integral in step 3 is now zero for every virtual displacement, so \((F - N(L))\,\delta u(L) = 0\) for every virtual displacement of the bar. Take \(\delta u(x) = x\). This function is a polynomial, differentiable everywhere with derivative \(1\), and \(\delta u(0) = 0\). With \(\delta u(L) = L\) we get \((F - N(L))\,L = 0\), and because \(L > 0\), \(N(L) = F\): the force of the bar's interior on the end face equals the force \(F\).

Source: EP example 3.5.3, equations (3) to (5) (pp. 148-149), for a bar with a spring at its end, EP §4.2.2 (p. 169) for a body, ONATE1 §1.7 (PDF p. 47), which states that the principle is a necessary and sufficient condition for equilibrium.

Theorems 4.76 and 4.78 together say that equation (4.47) for all virtual displacements of the bar is equivalent to the equilibrium of the bar. Two conditions came out of it. The equation \(N' + q = 0\) holds inside the bar, the condition \(N(L) = F\) at the free end. Conditions at the ends of a body that a solution must satisfy are called boundary conditions (robni pogoji). The condition at the fixed end, \(u(0) = 0\), does not appear in the equation, it is built into the choice of the virtual displacements, which must be zero there. EP calls the first kind of boundary conditions, which prescribe a displacement, essential or geometric boundary conditions (bistveni ali geometrijski robni pogoji), and the second kind, which prescribe a force and which the equation itself delivers, natural or force boundary conditions (naravni ali silni robni pogoji) (EP §3.5.7, pp. 146-147).

The assumptions of the theorem enter the proof at three places. The first is that the equation holds for every virtual displacement. The proof needed a bump over every place of the bar and also the line. With one single virtual displacement the conclusion does not hold, which we show with numbers for the hanging bar in section 4.4.8. The second is that \(q\) and the derivative \(N'\) are continuous functions. The fundamental lemma needs them in step 5, and it does not hold without continuity (figure 4.147). When a point force acts on the bar between its ends, the axial force is not differentiable there, and the theorem in this form must not be used on the whole bar (section 4.5.7). The third is that the left end is fixed. The virtual displacement is therefore zero there and the force of the wall does no virtual work, so the theorem does not contain it. We get it when we remove the constraint in our minds, as at the end of the next section.

4.4.8 The hanging bar

Let us test the principle on the hanging bar and see with numbers what the equation says. Recall the setting. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a round cross section of diameter 10 mm, of area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), and of density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ hangs under its own weight. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre of the top face. Only the centre \(O\) of the top face is fixed, and the top face is pulled up evenly by a force that carries the weight of the bar (section 1.7.1). For the displacement along the axis only \(u(0) = 0\) matters. The load is the weight, \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, so a piece of length 1 cm weighs 0,0601 N. The lower end is free, \(F = 0\), and by lemma 4.75 the axial force is \(N(x) = \rho g A\,(L - x)\) (figure 4.148).

Uniform virtual stretching. Let \(\delta u(x) = c\,x\), where \(c\) is a small number without a unit, for example \(c = 10^{-6}\), for which the lower end moves by \(c\,L = 10\) µm. The virtual strain is \(\delta u' = c\) everywhere, every piece stretches by the same fraction, so the graph is a straight line. The function is a polynomial and \(\delta u(0) = 0\), so it is a virtual displacement of the bar.

  1. Internal virtual work, which contains the axial force and the slope \(c\) of the line. The function \(L x - x^2/2\) has the derivative \(L - x\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus
    \[\int_0^L \rho g A\,(L - x)\,c\,\mathrm{d}x = c\,\rho g A\,\Big(L^2 - \frac{L^2}{2}\Big) = c\,\frac{\rho g A\,L^2}{2} .\]
  2. External virtual work, in which the weight of every piece is multiplied by its displacement \(c\,x\). The function \(x^2/2\) has the derivative \(x\), so
    \[\int_0^L \rho g A\,c\,x\,\mathrm{d}x + 0 \cdot \delta u(L) = c\,\frac{\rho g A\,L^2}{2} .\]
  3. Both sides are equal, \(c \cdot 300{,}5\) N m, so for \(c = 10^{-6}\) they are \(3{,}005 \cdot 10^{-4}\) J. The number \(300{,}5\) N m is the area of the triangle, \(\tfrac12 \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 60{,}10\ \text{N}\).

What do the two sides mean? The left side is \(c\) times the area under the graph of the axial force in figure 4.148, a triangle with base \(L\) and height \(\rho g A L\). The right side adds up the weights of the pieces, each multiplied by the depth of the piece, since the piece at depth \(x\) moves by \(c\,x\). The principle says that these two numbers are equal. This is so because the axial force at depth \(x'\) is the weight of everything below it. The weight of the piece at depth \(x\) is therefore contained in the axial force at every depth \(x'\) between \(0\) and \(x\), and when we integrate the axial forces over depth, this weight contributes over a length \(x\). In the graph of the axial force this piece is a vertical strip, as wide as the weight of the piece and as long as its depth \(x\). The triangle is stacked from such strips, so the integral of the axial forces is the sum of the weights of the pieces, each multiplied by its depth.

Nonuniform virtual stretching. Let \(\delta u(x) = c\,x^2/L\). The virtual strain \(\delta u'(x) = 2c\,x/L\) grows from zero at the top to \(2c\) at the bottom. At the top and at the bottom this is a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), as theorem 4.76 allows.

  1. Internal virtual work, which contains the axial force and the slope \(2c\,x/L\) of the parabola. The function \(L x^2/2 - x^3/3\) has the derivative \(L x - x^2\), so
    \[\int_0^L \rho g A\,(L - x)\,\frac{2c\,x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{2c\,\rho g A}{L}\,\Big(\frac{L^3}{2} - \frac{L^3}{3}\Big) = c\,\frac{\rho g A\,L^2}{3} .\]
  2. External virtual work, in which the weight of every piece is multiplied by its displacement \(c\,x^2/L\). The function \(x^3/3\) has the derivative \(x^2\), so
    \[\int_0^L \rho g A\,\frac{c\,x^2}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{c\,\rho g A}{L} \cdot \frac{L^3}{3} = c\,\frac{\rho g A\,L^2}{3} .\]
  3. Both sides are equal, \(c \cdot 200{,}3\) N m, so for \(c = 10^{-6}\) they are \(2{,}003 \cdot 10^{-4}\) J. This is two thirds of the previous work, because the parabola lies to the left of the line: every piece except the lowest moves less than before, so the weight does less virtual work.

A wrong axial force. Does the principle notice a mistake? Suppose we wrongly thought that every cross section carries the weight of the whole bar, \(N(x) = \rho g A\,L\). With the virtual displacement \(\delta u = c\,x\) the left side is \(\int_0^L \rho g A\,L\,c\,\mathrm{d}x = c\,\rho g A\,L^2 = c \cdot 601{,}0\) N m, which is \(c\) times the area of the rectangle, twice the area of the triangle. The right side stays \(c \cdot 300{,}5\) N m. The equation does not hold, and by theorem 4.76 such an axial force is not in equilibrium. Indeed, for it \(N' + q = \rho g A \neq 0\) and \(N(L) = \rho g A\,L \neq 0 = F\).

One virtual displacement is not enough. Theorem 4.78 requires the equation for every virtual displacement. What happens if we check it with only one? Take another wrong axial force, \(N(x) = \rho g A\,L/2\), half the weight of the bar in every cross section. The rectangle under it has the same area as the triangle, \(\tfrac12\,\rho g A\,L \cdot L\). With the virtual displacement \(\delta u = c\,x\) the left side is therefore \(c \cdot 300{,}5\) N m, equal to the right side. The equation holds, although \(N' + q = \rho g A \neq 0\) and a bar with such an axial force is not in equilibrium. Only another virtual displacement finds the mistake. With \(\delta u = c\,x^2/L\) the left side is

\[\int_0^L \frac{\rho g A\,L}{2} \cdot \frac{2c\,x}{L}\,\mathrm{d}x = c\,\frac{\rho g A\,L^2}{2} = c \cdot 300{,}5\ \text{N m} ,\]

while the right side is \(c \cdot 200{,}3\) N m by the previous computation. This is why the principle demands all virtual displacements: each feels the axial force in a different way, and only all of them together miss it nowhere.

The force that holds the bar. The displacement \(\delta u(x) = c\), in which all points move by the same \(c\), is not a virtual displacement of this bar, because the top must not move. As for the lever, however, we may remove the constraint in our minds and keep the force with which the bar is held at the top as a force on the bar. We denote its \(x\) component by \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\). Step 4 of the proof of theorem 4.76 holds also for such a displacement, only the term \(-N(0)\,\delta u(0)\) no longer drops out. By definition 2.5 and by the law of action and reaction at the top cross section, \(R_{\mathrm{x}} = -N(0)\), so this term is the virtual work of the force that holds the bar, and the equation reads

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,\delta u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q(x)\,\delta u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) + R_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(0) .\]

For \(\delta u = c\) the virtual strain is zero, so the left side is zero, and the right side is \(\int_0^L \rho g A\,c\,\mathrm{d}x + 0 + R_{\mathrm{x}}\,c = (\rho g A\,L + R_{\mathrm{x}})\,c\). Because it must be zero for every \(c\), \(R_{\mathrm{x}} = -\rho g A\,L = -60{,}10\) N. The bar is held at the top by a force of the size of its weight, pointing up, against the axis \(x\).

4.4.9 The bracket of two bars

Finally we apply virtual displacements to the bracket of two bars, a truss (definition 4.4), from section 4.1.4. The setting is the same, so we only show it in the left half of figure 4.149. The axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie in the vertical plane of the bracket, the axis \(z\) points toward the viewer, and the origin is at the joint \(C\). The horizontal bar \(AC\) is 4 m long, and its pin \(A = (-4, 0, 0)\) m is on the wall. The pin \(B = (-4, 3, 0)\) m is on the wall 3 m above it, so the inclined bar \(BC\) has the length \(\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2}\ \text{m} = 5\ \text{m}\). At the joint \(C\) hangs a load that acts on it with the force \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\), \(F = 10\) kN. Each bar carries only an axial force, \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\) respectively (lemma 4.5). As in section 4.1.4 we do not treat buckling of the compressed bar \(AC\). For the principle we will not need Young's modulus or the cross-section areas, since it does not use the relation between stress and strain. We will need them only at the end, for the displacement of the joint.

The unit vectors along the bars, from the pin on the wall toward the joint \(C\), are (figure 4.149)

\[\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = (1, 0, 0) , \qquad \vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0) .\]

A bar acts on the pin \(C\) with the force \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\) or \(-N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\): a bar in tension pulls it toward the wall, a compressed bar pushes it away from the wall. The equilibrium of the pin \(C\) is therefore

\[-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} + \vek F = \vek 0 .\]

From it we got in section 4.1.4 \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac53 F = 16{,}67\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -\tfrac43 F = -13{,}33\) kN. The forces of the bars on the pin are then \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = \tfrac{F}{3}\,(4, 0, 0)\), of size \(\tfrac43 F\), and \(-N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac{F}{3}\,(-4, 3, 0)\), of size \(\tfrac53 F\), and their sum with \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\) is the zero vector.

What does a virtual displacement of the bracket look like? The pins on the wall do not move. The joint \(C\), however, can move by any small vector \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (\delta u, \delta v, 0)\) in the plane of the bracket, by \(\delta u\) horizontally and by \(\delta v\) vertically, and the bars follow it, each turning about its pin and stretching a little. By how much does a bar stretch? Take a bar of length \(L\) from the pin \(P\) on the wall to the joint \(C\), pointing in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) (figure 4.150). Before the displacement the vector of the bar is \(L\,\vek n\), after the displacement of the joint to the point \(C'\) it is \(L\,\vek n + \delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\). The length of this vector is the new length of the bar \(L_1\). We look for the elongation \(L_1 - L\), and we show every step in the figure.

  1. We split the displacement into a part along the bar and a part perpendicular to it (lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, item 1). The part along the bar has the length \(a = \vek n\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and reaches from \(C\) to the point \(D\) on the line of the bar. The perpendicular part leads from \(D\) to \(C'\), and we denote its length by \(b\). In the figure \(a\) is positive. For a negative \(a\) the point \(D\) lies on the bar, to the left of \(C\).
  2. The triangle \(PDC'\) has a right angle at \(D\). Its legs are \(PD = L + a\) and \(DC' = b\), and its hypotenuse is the bar in the new position. By item 3 of the same lemma, which is the Pythagorean theorem for this triangle, \(L_1^2 = (L + a)^2 + b^2\).
  3. The grey arc with centre \(P\) carries the length \(L_1\) over to the line of the bar, to the point \(E\). The exact elongation \(L_1 - L\) is therefore the segment \(CE\). It consists of \(CD = a\) and the part \(DE\), of length \(L_1 - (L + a)\).
  4. How long is the part \(DE\)? As for the sheared cube in equation (1.18) we write the difference of lengths \(L_1 - (L + a)\) with the difference of squares, \(L_1 - (L + a) = \big(L_1^2 - (L + a)^2\big)/(L_1 + L + a)\). The denominator is positive, because the displacement is shorter than the bar. By step 2 the numerator equals \(b^2\). Hence exactly
    \[L_1 - L = a + \frac{b^2}{L_1 + L + a} . \tag{4.48}\]

Here the only assumption of this computation enters: the displacement of the joint is small next to the length of the bar. Then the denominator is almost \(2L\), and the part \(DE\) is almost \(b^2/(2L)\), that is, the perpendicular part \(b\) times the small number \(b/(2L)\). We leave this part out, as we left out the term \(s^2\) next to the term \(2hs\) for the sheared cube. Why may we? We choose the virtual displacement ourselves, and we may choose it as small as we like. If we make it ten times smaller, \(a\) becomes ten times smaller and the part \(DE\) a hundred times smaller. When may we not? When the displacement is not small next to the length of the bar, for example for a rope that sags visibly under a load. How much the part left out amounts to is shown by the numbers below.

The virtual elongation of the bar is therefore

\[\delta e = \vek n\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} , \tag{4.49}\]

the projection of the virtual displacement of the joint onto the direction of the bar. The part of the displacement perpendicular to the bar only turns the bar about \(P\), as the vertical edge \(AD\) of the sheared cube only leaned. Let us check with numbers on the bar \(BC\), of length \(L = 5000\) mm. Let \(C\) move vertically down by 1 mm, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (0, -1, 0)\) mm. The part along the bar is \(a = \tfrac15\,\big(4 \cdot 0 + (-3)(-1\ \text{mm})\big) = 0{,}6\) mm, and the perpendicular part is \(b = \sqrt{1^2 - 0{,}6^2}\ \text{mm} = 0{,}8\) mm. By (4.49) the bar becomes longer by \(0{,}6\) mm, while the exact computation with the lengths gives \(0{,}600064\) mm. The difference \(0{,}000064\) mm is the part \(DE\), \(b^2/(2L) = 0{,}64\ \text{mm}^2/(10\,000\ \text{mm})\), a ten-thousandth of the elongation. For the bar \(AC\), of length \(4000\) mm, the same displacement is perpendicular to the bar, \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\) mm. By (4.49) it does not become longer, while exactly it becomes longer by the part \(DE\), \(1\ \text{mm}^2/(8000\ \text{mm}) = 0{,}000125\) mm.

Now we multiply the equilibrium of the pin \(C\) by the virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) with the scalar product, just as for the rigid body we multiplied forces by displacements.

  1. By the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12, items 2 and 3) the equilibrium of the pin, which contains the force of bar \(AC\), the force of bar \(BC\) and the load, gives \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} + \vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\).
  2. By (4.49) the scalar products with \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\) are the virtual elongations of the bars, each a projection of the displacement onto the bar. We move the first two terms to the right side:
    \[N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = \vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} . \tag{4.50}\]

On the left is the internal virtual work: the axial force of each bar times its virtual elongation. It is the internal virtual work of a bar from theorem 4.76, the integral of the axial force times the derivative of the virtual displacement along the bar. Bar \(i\) has no load along its length, so its axial force \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the same everywhere and we write it in front of the integral. The integral of a derivative is the difference of the values at the ends (the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55). The virtual displacement along the bar is zero at the pin on the wall, and at the joint it equals the virtual elongation \(\delta e_{\mathrm{i}}\), so \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta e_{\mathrm{i}}\) remains. On the right is the external virtual work of the load. The pins on the wall do not move, so the forces of the wall do not appear. The converse also holds. If equation (4.50) holds for every \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) in the plane of the bracket, we read it back as \((\vek F - N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}})\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\), and with \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (1, 0, 0)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\) we get the \(x\) and \(y\) components of the equilibrium of the pin. The \(z\) component is zero by itself for forces in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane.

Let us compute the axial forces. For \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (\delta u, \delta v, 0)\), by (4.49) \(\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} = \delta u\), since the bar \(AC\) is horizontal, and \(\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0{,}8\,\delta u - 0{,}6\,\delta v\). The load pulls down while the joint rises by \(\delta v\), so the work of the load is \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -F\,\delta v\). Equation (4.50) reads

\[N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\delta u + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,(0{,}8\,\delta u - 0{,}6\,\delta v) = -F\,\delta v \quad \text{for all } \delta u, \delta v .\]

The equation holds for every choice, so we may take first a horizontal and then a vertical displacement. For \(\delta v = 0\) we get \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} + 0{,}8\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), and for \(\delta u = 0\), \(-0{,}6\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -F\). Hence \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F/0{,}6 = 16{,}67\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -0{,}8\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -13{,}33\) kN, the same as in section 4.1.4.

It is even better to choose the virtual displacement so that only one bar becomes longer (figure 4.151).

  1. Let \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = \tfrac{c}{5}\,(3, 4, 0)\), where \(c\) is a small length. In the left half of the figure are the bracket on the wall, the load and the position of the bars after the displacement. The displacement is perpendicular to the bar \(BC\), since \((3, 4, 0)\cdot(4, -3, 0) = 12 - 12 = 0\). The bar \(BC\) only turns about \(B\), \(\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), while the bar \(AC\) becomes longer by \(\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} = 0{,}6\,c\), as much of the displacement as lies along it. The load rises by \(0{,}8\,c\), so its work is \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -0{,}8\,F\,c\). Equation (4.50) gives \(0{,}6\,c\,N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -0{,}8\,F\,c\), so \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -\tfrac43 F = -13{,}33\) kN, without knowing \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\).
  2. Let \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (0, c, 0)\), perpendicular to the bar \(AC\). In the right half of the figure are the same bracket on the wall, the load and the new position of the bars. Then \(\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} = 0\). The perpendicular from the new joint to the bar \(BC\) meets the bar \(0{,}6\,c\) before the end \(C\), so the bar becomes shorter, \(\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = -0{,}6\,c\). The load rises by \(c\), and its work is \(-F\,c\). The equation gives \(-0{,}6\,c\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -F\,c\), so \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac53 F = 16{,}67\) kN.

Each choice gave one axial force, because only one bar became longer. This is how the unit dummy-displacement method works (EP §4.2.3, p. 172), which we mentioned for the lever. EP uses it on a similar truss of two bars in example 4.2.2 (pp. 173-174). Take the unit virtual displacement in the direction of the load, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (0, -1, 0)\). This is the displacement of the check with numbers above, only without the unit. Then \(\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} = 0\), \(\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0{,}6\), the part of the displacement along the bar \(BC\), and \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = F\), so equation (4.50) says \(F = 0{,}6\,N_{\mathrm{BC}}\). The load equals the internal virtual work for the unit virtual displacement in its direction.

Now we can also answer the question of section 4.1.4, where the joint \(C\) moves. By section 4.1.4 the bars changed their lengths by \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = -2{,}721\) mm and \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 4{,}252\) mm. The real displacement of the joint \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (u, v, 0)\), written by its components as in section 4.1.4, is small, so the same computation holds for it as for the virtual displacement: the change of the length of a bar is, to first order, the projection of the displacement of the joint onto the direction of the bar, \(\Delta L = \vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\).

Figure 4.152 shows the surroundings of the joint, magnified so much that the millimetres can be seen. The ends of the bars before loading are grey dashed and meet at the joint \(C\), and the axes are the same as before. The bar \(AC\) becomes shorter by \(2{,}721\) mm, and without turning its end would be that much closer to the wall. The bar \(BC\) becomes longer by \(4{,}252\) mm in its own direction, away from \(B\). So the ends are no longer together. Each bar may still turn about its pin. For a small turn its end moves perpendicular to the bar, and to first order this does not change the length (the part \(b\) in figure 4.150). The end of the bar \(AC\) therefore lies somewhere on the perpendicular to \(AC\), and the end of the bar \(BC\) on the perpendicular to \(BC\). The bars are pinned together at the joint, so the new position of the joint is the point \(C_1\) where the perpendiculars cross. The equations of the perpendiculars are two equations for two unknowns:

  1. Bar \(AC\): \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = u\), so \(u = -2{,}721\) mm.
  2. Bar \(BC\): \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = 0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\), so \(v\) equals \((0{,}8\,u - \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}})/0{,}6 = \big(0{,}8 \cdot (-2{,}721\ \text{mm}) - 4{,}252\ \text{mm}\big)/0{,}6 = -10{,}71\ \text{mm}\).

The joint \(C\) therefore moves by \(2{,}721\) mm toward the wall and by \(10{,}71\) mm down, and the bars after loading meet again. What does the first-order computation cost here, where the displacement is no longer arbitrarily small? For the bar \(AC\) the perpendicular part of the displacement is \(b = 10{,}71\) mm, and the part left out of equation (4.48) is \(b^2/(2L) = (10{,}71\ \text{mm})^2/(8000\ \text{mm}) = 0{,}014\) mm. The exact computation finds the crossing of the circles with radii \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} + \Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}\) about \(A\) and \(L_{\mathrm{BC}} + \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}\) about \(B\), and the perpendiculars are tangents of these circles. It gives \(u = -2{,}735\) mm and \(v = -10{,}716\) mm. The first-order computation is thus off by \(0{,}014\) mm in \(u\), which is half a percent, and by \(0{,}002\) mm in \(v\), which is \(0{,}02\) percent. The component \(v = -10{,}71\) mm agrees with the number that we got in section 4.1.4 from the equality of the work of the load and the stored energy, and now we have the horizontal part as well. Here we used that the material is linear elastic (assumption 1 of section 4.1), since the changes of the lengths were computed from the axial forces with Young's modulus. EP does the same in example 4.2.2 (pp. 173-174), except that it first writes the axial forces with the displacement of the joint and inserts them into the virtual work equation. How to get the displacement of the joint from energy without computing the axial forces we will see in section 4.6.

The position of the joint \(C\) in the plane was described by two numbers, the horizontal \(\delta u\) and the vertical \(\delta v\), and two independent choices of the virtual displacement gave two equations. The joint has two degrees of freedom, and the two numbers that describe its position are generalized coordinates, which we mentioned in section 1.5.1. Virtual displacements written with generalized coordinates are the starting point of analytical mechanics in chapter 8 (GR §1.2.3, PDF pp. 38-39).

Let us sum up how we arrived at the principle. For the rigid body we multiplied the forces by the virtual displacements of their points with the scalar product and added. The displacement of all points by the same vector picked out the resultant, the turn the sum of the moments, so zero virtual work was equivalent to equilibrium, and the support forces dropped out. For the bar we multiplied the equilibrium \(N' + q = 0\) by the virtual displacement, integrated over the length and moved the derivative to the virtual displacement with integration by parts. This produced the internal virtual work \(\int N\,\delta u'\,\mathrm{d}x\), which equals the external one. We proved the converse with the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations. For the bracket of two bars we multiplied the equilibrium of the pin at the joint by the virtual displacement of the joint, and the virtual elongations of the bars were the projections of this displacement. The same projection rule also gave the real displacement of the joint. In the next section we do the same for a general body. The stress components will take the place of the axial force, and the divergence theorem the place of integration by parts.
