Chapter 16 Torsion

16.8 Hollow sections and thin-walled tubes (votli prerezi in tankostenske cevi)

The slit tube of the previous section gave \(\alpha = 0{,}2749\) rad/m and a stress of \(82{,}9\) MPa at \(50\) N m. The same tube without the slit is what drive shafts, rotors and frames are built from, because it looks much stiffer. How much stiffer cannot be guessed from the shape of the section. The closed tube differs from the slit one only in that its hole is surrounded by material on all sides, so the calculation with Prandtl's function has to take the hole into account. This section first says what the hole changes in the problem (theorem 16.53), then solves the tube and compares it with the circle of section 16.1, and finally derives from the same problem the simple formula for thin walls that engineers use for profiles of any shape, and measures its error as a number.

Prandtl's function of section 16.3 was made for a section without holes. Its existence was guaranteed by the theorem on a field from partial derivatives (theorem 4.115) because the region is simply connected. The ring of corollary 4.114 is not, and what holds for the ring holds for every section that has a hole. Let us see what this means for our problem.

Definition 16.51 Section with holes (prerez z luknjami)

Let \(\Gamma_0\) be a simple closed curve in the plane \(y, z\) with piecewise continuous tangent and \(D_0\) the region it bounds. Let \(\Gamma_1, \dots, \Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\) be pairwise disjoint simple closed curves in \(D_0\) with the same property and \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\) the region bounded by \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), with area \(A_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\). If the \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\) are pairwise disjoint regions, the section with \(m\) holes (prerez z \(m\) luknjami) is the set

\[S = \overline{D_0} \setminus (H_1 \cup \dots \cup H_{\mathrm{m}}) , \tag{16.53}\]

with boundary \(\partial S = \Gamma_0 \cup \Gamma_1 \cup \dots \cup \Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\). Hole \(k\) is the region \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is not part of the body, and the outer boundary \(\Gamma_0\) is one curve. Because \(m \ge 1\), a section with holes is by corollary 4.114 (proved for the ring, the same for other shapes, without proof) and definition 1.142 multiply connected, that is, not simply connected. The normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the curve \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) points out of the hole, that is, into the material, and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{S}}\) is the outer normal of the section \(S\), which on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) points into the hole, so \(\vek n_{\mathrm{S}} = -\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Explanation

The word is composed: multiply connected means that two points of the section can be joined by several essentially different paths, because a path can go round the hole on the left or on the right. A tube and a hollow shaft have \(m = 1\), a profile with two cells \(m = 2\).

Source: SADD section 9.3.3 (PDF pp. 252-253) for sections with several holes, and BARBER section 16.5 (PDF pp. 260-261). The definition is not from a source.

We draw a section with one hole, because the idea is the same for every number of holes. The grey surface is the material \(S\). Its outer boundary \(\Gamma_0\) is a curve on which the lateral surface is free, so by lemma 16.23 Prandtl's function is constant there, and we may choose the value zero, because the stresses contain only derivatives of the function. The novelty is the hole with its curve \(\Gamma_1\): this surface is free too, so \(\Phi\) is constant on it, but the curve \(\Gamma_1\) is not joined to \(\Gamma_0\) inside the material, so there is no reason for the constant to be the same, and we write \(\Phi = \Phi_1\) with an unknown number \(\Phi_1\) in N/mm. On \(\Gamma_1\) we draw the normal \(\vek n\), pointing out of the hole, and mark the area of the hole \(A_1\). For later we add the loop \(C\), a closed curve in the material that goes once round the hole, in the direction of positive rotation about \(x\) (axes: \(y\) to the right, \(z\) up, \(x\) towards the reader). With it we shall check what happens to the functions \(\Phi\) and \(\psi\) on a path round the hole.

First: does Prandtl's function exist on this section as a single function at all?

Lemma 16.52 Prandtl function of a section with holes (Prandtlova funkcija prereza z luknjami)

Let \(S\) be a section with holes (definition 16.51), let \(\psi\) be the warping function of theorem 16.18, twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\), as the theorem demands, let the lateral surface be free on all curves \(\Gamma_0, \dots, \Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\), and let lemma 16.17 hold for \(S\), for every region \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\) and for every region between \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) and a closed curve in \(S\) that goes round \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) and no other hole. Then:

  1. There is exactly one function \(\Phi\) on the interior of the section, twice continuously differentiable, that satisfies equation (16.28). It is determined up to an added constant.
  2. On every curve \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) the function \(\Phi\) is constant. We choose \(\Phi = 0\) on \(\Gamma_0\) and \(\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k \ge 1\).
  3. \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) in \(S\).
Idea

We cut the section with arcs from the hole to the outer boundary so that it becomes simply connected, and use the theorem on a field from derivatives. The jump of the function \(\Phi\) across a cut is the flux of stress through a loop round the hole, and that is zero.

Proof

We must show that \(\Phi\), made on the cut section, does not break at the cuts. We draw the cut, the loop and the jump.

We start with the section with its outer boundary and the boundary of the hole, which we know. In it we draw the cut \(c_1\), an arc from the hole to the outer boundary, which makes the section simply connected. The cut has two sides, the upper and the lower, and on them \(\Phi\) may have different values. So we draw the loop \(C_1\), which goes round the hole from one side of the cut to the other. Between the loop and the hole lies the region \(R_1\), which we shall use for the flux. The difference of the values on the sides is the jump \([\Phi]_1\).

  1. For every hole \(k\) choose an arc \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) from \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) to \(\Gamma_0\), separate from the other arcs and holes (cut). The set \(S^\circ \setminus (c_1 \cup \dots \cup c_{\mathrm{m}})\) is open, connected and simply connected (a topological fact that we state here without proof, as with the cut ring in lemma 4.121). By step 2 of lemma 16.23, which uses only equilibrium (16.26), there is a function \(\Phi\) on it with derivatives \(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
  2. The jump \([\Phi]_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the function \(\Phi\) across the cut \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the difference of its values on the two sides. We get it by going along a loop \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) round hole \(k\) from one side of the cut to the other, since \(\Phi\) is the integral of its gradient. With the unit tangent \(\vek t = (t_{\mathrm{y}}, t_{\mathrm{z}})\) of the loop, pointing in the direction of positive rotation, and its outer normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{C}} = (t_{\mathrm{z}}, -t_{\mathrm{y}})\) we have \([\Phi]_{\mathrm{k}}\) \(= \oint_{\mathrm{C}_\mathrm{k}}(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} t_{\mathrm{y}} + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = \oint_{\mathrm{C}_\mathrm{k}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}t_{\mathrm{z}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}s = \oint_{\mathrm{C}_\mathrm{k}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s\). This is the flux of the shear vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) through \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. Let \(R_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the part of the section between \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\). By lemma 16.17 with the field \(\vek v = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\) the integral of the divergence over \(R_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is zero by equation (16.26), equals the flux through the boundary of \(R_{\mathrm{k}}\). This is the sum of the flux through \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the flux through \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), where the normal is \(\vek n_{\mathrm{S}}\) and the flux is zero by equation (16.27). So the flux through \(C_{\mathrm{k}}\) is zero, hence \([\Phi]_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\).
  4. Since the jumps are zero, the values of \(\Phi\) on the two sides of the cuts agree and \(\Phi\) is a twice continuously differentiable function on the whole interior, with the same derivatives and so with the same stresses. If \(\tilde\Phi\) is another such function, the difference has both derivatives zero in a connected set and is constant. This is point 1.
  5. Point 2 follows as in step 5 of lemma 16.23 for each of the curves \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\): the derivative of \(\Phi\) along the tangent is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) by equation (16.27), and each \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) is one connected curve. Since the stresses contain only derivatives, we can choose the constant so that \(\Phi = 0\) on \(\Gamma_0\).
  6. Point 3 is the computation of step 1 of theorem 16.24: the left side of equation (16.29) is \(-\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nabla^2\Phi\) and it equals \(2G\alpha\).

Source: SADD section 9.3.3 (PDF pp. 252-253): constants \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) on every inner curve, one chosen zero. BARBER section 16.5 (PDF p. 260): the same, with the remark that \(\Phi_1 = \Phi_0\) does not follow because the curves do not connect. The proof with cuts and flux is not from the sources.

So the function \(\Phi\) is a single one, but on the hole it has an unknown value \(\Phi_1\). What fixes this number? For a section without holes, equation (16.31) with zero on the boundary was enough, and by lemma 16.25 it has exactly one solution. Now the boundary has several curves and \(\Phi_1\) is an additional unknown, so we need an additional equation. We get to it with a question that we did not have to ask for a section without holes: is the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\psi\) a single-valued function of the point? The displacement of a body is one vector at every point. The warping function \(\psi\) must therefore come back to the same value after every closed loop round the hole, just as in lemma 4.121 the displacement on the cut must not have a jump. The function \(\Phi\) guaranteed this only for itself, and for \(\psi\) it is a new requirement.

We write the requirement on the loop \(C\) and move it to the edge of the hole, because there the function \(\Phi\) is best known. The path along the loop is closed, so \(\psi\) does not change along it: the integral of the derivative \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}t_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}t_{\mathrm{z}}\) along \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) is zero. We express the derivatives \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}}\) by the stresses from equation (16.25), and the stresses by \(\Phi\). Two things must be computed: how large the circulation of the shear vector along the edge of the hole is, that is the sum of its component along the edge, as in the circulation around the square of figure 16.31, and what \(\oint(z\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s\) is. There is no flux through the edge, because the edge is free, but the circulation is not zero. The first will be the derivative of \(\Phi\) in the direction of the normal \(\vek n\) with the opposite sign, summed along the edge, the second the area \(A_1\).

Theorem 16.53 Hole condition and torque of a section with holes (pogoj luknje in moment prereza z luknjami)

Let \(S\) be a section with \(m\) holes (definition 16.51) and let \(\Phi\) be the Prandtl function of lemma 16.52 with \(\Phi = 0\) on \(\Gamma_0\) and \(\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\) (as in theorem 16.24, because the proof uses lemma 16.17 with fields made of \(\Phi\) and its derivatives), with the other assumptions of the lemma. Let \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) be the normal of \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) out of hole \(k\). Then:

  1. For every hole \(k = 1, \dots, m\)
    \[\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\,\mathrm{d}s = -2G\alpha\,A_{\mathrm{k}} . \tag{16.54}\]
  2. The same holds for every closed curve \(C\) in the interior of the section that goes once round hole \(k\) and no other, with the area \(A_{\mathrm{C}}\) it encloses instead of \(A_{\mathrm{k}}\). The normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{C}}\) is the outer normal of the curve \(C\).
  3. The resultant force of the section is zero, and its moment about the axis \(x\) is
    \[M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + 2\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\,A_{\mathrm{k}} , \tag{16.55}\]
    so (the term \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the hole) \(J = \dfrac{2}{G\alpha}\Big(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\Big)\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\alpha\).
  4. Let \((\Phi, \Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_{\mathrm{m}})\) and \((\tilde\Phi, \tilde\Phi_1, \dots, \tilde\Phi_{\mathrm{m}})\) be twice continuously differentiable functions on an open set that contains \(S\), with \(\nabla^2\Phi = \nabla^2\tilde\Phi = -2G\alpha\) in \(S\), zero on \(\Gamma_0\), constant values on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the conditions (16.54). Then they are equal.
Idea

The condition from the single-valued warping function is the flux of the gradient of \(\Phi\) through the edge of the hole. The moment is the divergence theorem with the field \((y\Phi, z\Phi)\), where the term on \(\Gamma_0\) cancels because \(\Phi = 0\), and on the holes \(-2\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\) remains. Uniqueness is an energy proof as in lemma 16.25.

Proof

We must show four claims. We turn the tangent \(\vek t\) of the curve \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the direction of positive rotation, so that \(\vek t = (-n_{\mathrm{z}}, n_{\mathrm{y}})\), as in lemma 16.23.

We draw the steps in four panels. Top left we go round the edge of the hole \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) from the point \(P_0\) in the direction of positive rotation. To the right of the edge is the graph of the warping height \(\psi\) along the path, with the arc length \(s\) horizontal: the curve rises and falls, but the end is at the height of the start, because the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\psi\) at the point \(P_0\) is a single value. Top right is a piece of the edge with the point \(P\), at it the normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\) out of the hole into the material and the tangent \(\vek t\) in the direction of positive rotation. Along the edge run the contour lines of the function \(\Phi\), because \(\Phi\) is constant on the edge, and the stress runs along them. Bottom left we draw from the point \(O\) the triangle \(OPP'\) to a short piece \(PP'\) of the edge, its height is the lever arm \(p\), the distance from \(O\) to the tangent, and a fan of such triangles covers the hole. Bottom right is the hole filled with small squares, which we use after the proof.

  1. The function \(\psi\) is a single-valued function of the point of the section, continuously differentiable up to the boundary, so its derivative along the closed curve \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) satisfies, by the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}(\psi_{\mathrm{y}}t_{\mathrm{y}} + \psi_{\mathrm{z}}t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\): the integral of the derivative along the path is the difference of the heights at the end and at the start, and this is zero.
  2. From equation (16.25), \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/(G\alpha) + z\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/(G\alpha) - y\). The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\), so the component of the stress along the tangent is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}t_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}t_{\mathrm{z}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}n_{\mathrm{z}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}n_{\mathrm{y}} = -\partial\Phi/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\). The stress runs along the contour lines, and its size is the slope of \(\Phi\) in the direction of the normal.
  3. The term \(z\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} = -(y\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\) is minus the lever arm \(p\). The triangle \(OPP'\) over a piece of the edge of length \(\delta\) has the area \(\tfrac12\,p\,\delta\), and the triangles together cover the hole, so we expect \(\oint p\,\mathrm{d}s = 2A_{\mathrm{k}}\). Exactly this follows from lemma 16.17 with the field \(\vek v = (y, z)\) on the region \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\) (the divergence is \(2\)): \(\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}(y\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 2A_{\mathrm{k}}\), so \(\oint(z\,t_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,t_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = -2A_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 together give \(0 = -\dfrac{1}{G\alpha}\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}}\,\mathrm{d}s - 2A_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is equation (16.54).
  5. Point 2: by lemma 16.17 with \(\vek v = \nabla\Phi\) on the region between \(C\) and \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\oint_{\mathrm{C}}\partial\Phi/\partial n_{\mathrm{C}}\,\mathrm{d}s - \oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}\partial\Phi/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}s = \int\nabla^2\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = -2G\alpha(A_{\mathrm{C}} - A_{\mathrm{k}})\). With equation (16.54) it follows that \(\oint_{\mathrm{C}}\partial\Phi/\partial n_{\mathrm{C}}\,\mathrm{d}s = -2G\alpha A_{\mathrm{C}}\). The sign in front of the second integral is minus because the outer normal of the region on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(-\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  6. Point 3, force: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}}\Phi\,n_{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}s\) by lemma 16.17 with \((0, \Phi)\). On \(\Gamma_0\) we have \(\Phi = 0\), and on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) we get \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}n_{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot 0\), because \(\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}s\) is zero by the lemma with \(\vek v = (0, 1)\) on \(H_{\mathrm{k}}\). Likewise \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) with \((\Phi, 0)\).
  7. Point 3, moment: by lemma 5.9, \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\,\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\). The field \(\vek v = (y\Phi, z\Phi)\) has divergence \(2\Phi + y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}}\Phi\,(y\,n_{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s - 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\). On \(\Gamma_0\) we have \(\Phi = 0\), on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) we have \(\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{S}} = -\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\), which by step 3 (fan of triangles) gives \(-2\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\). So \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + 2\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  8. Point 4: the difference \(w = \Phi - \tilde\Phi\) is harmonic, zero on \(\Gamma_0\), constant \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), with \(\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}\partial w/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) (the difference of equations (16.54)). By lemma 16.17 with \(\vek v = w\nabla w\) we have \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}}w\,\partial w/\partial n_{\mathrm{S}}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\sum_{\mathrm{k}}c_{\mathrm{k}}\oint_{\Gamma_\mathrm{k}}\partial w/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\). As in lemma 16.25, \(\nabla w = 0\), \(w\) is constant and zero on \(\Gamma_0\), so \(w = 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\).

Source: Condition (16.54): SADD section 9.3.3, equations (9.3.26) to (9.3.29) (PDF p. 253) and BARBER section 16.5, equations (16.53) and (16.54) (PDF p. 261), both with Green's theorem on a loop round the hole. Moment: BARBER equation (16.55) (PDF p. 261) with \(\Phi = \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the hole. The proof with flux and the energy uniqueness are not from the sources. We cite, and do not prove, the existence of a solution for a general section (SADD PDF pp. 246-250 for a section without holes), and for the tube theorem 16.3 gives it with \(\psi = 0\).

Let us read the theorem. The hole condition (16.54) is one number for every hole, and our constant \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) used it up: the flux of the gradient of \(\Phi\) out of the hole is exactly \(-2G\alpha A_{\mathrm{k}}\). By step 2 of the proof, \(-\partial\Phi/\partial n_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the component of the stress along the edge, so the condition also says: the circulation of the stress around the hole is \(2G\alpha A_{\mathrm{k}}\). This is what the small squares would give if we filled the hole with material twisted in the same way. By equation (16.29) (figure 16.31) each has the circulation \(2G\alpha\) times its area, on the shared side of two squares the circulations subtract, because we go along it in opposite directions, and only the circulation along the outline remains. The circulation of the stress around the hole is therefore as if the hole were full. The moment (16.55) has two terms: the first is an integral over the material, the second is the contribution of the hole, which, although there is no material in it, can be large. Let us see this on the tube.

Corollary 16.54 Torsion of a tube (torzija cevi)

Let the section be an annulus with outer radius \(R_{\mathrm{o}}\) and inner radius \(R_{\mathrm{i}} < R_{\mathrm{o}}\) with centre \(O\), \(S = \{R_{\mathrm{i}} \le r \le R_{\mathrm{o}}\}\), \(r = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2}\), with one hole \(H_1 = \{r < R_{\mathrm{i}}\}\) and \(A_1 = \pi R_{\mathrm{i}}^2\). Then

\[\Phi = \frac{G\alpha}{2}\,(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - r^2) , \qquad \Phi_1 = \frac{G\alpha}{2}\,(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2) , \qquad J = \frac{\pi}{2}\,(R_{\mathrm{o}}^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4) = I_{\mathrm{p}} . \tag{16.56}\]
Proof

We must show that the function (16.56) is the Prandtl function of the tube, that it satisfies the conditions of theorem 16.53, and compute \(J\) from equation (16.55).

  1. By theorem 16.3, \(\psi = 0\) is a solution for the annulus with the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\), so by lemma 16.52 its Prandtl function exists, with the derivatives \(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -G\alpha y\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -G\alpha z\). The function (16.56) has the same derivatives. Two functions with the same derivatives on a connected set differ by a constant, and both are zero at \(r = R_{\mathrm{o}}\), so (16.56) is the Prandtl function of the tube. By point 4 of theorem 16.53 it is the only one that satisfies the conditions of the theorem, so every other way of solving gives it too.

  2. \(\nabla^2\Phi = \dfrac{G\alpha}{2}\,\nabla^2(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - y^2 - z^2) = \dfrac{G\alpha}{2}(-2 - 2) = -2G\alpha\) and \(\Phi = 0\) at \(r = R_{\mathrm{o}}\). On \(r = R_{\mathrm{i}}\) we have \(\Phi = \Phi_1\) (the plate over the hole).

  3. The slope of the membrane at the hole edge, the normal out of the hole is radial, \(\partial\Phi/\partial n_1 = \partial\Phi/\partial r = -G\alpha R_{\mathrm{i}}\) on \(r = R_{\mathrm{i}}\), which is constant, so \(\oint_{\Gamma_1}\partial\Phi/\partial n_1\,\mathrm{d}s = -G\alpha R_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot 2\pi R_{\mathrm{i}} = -2G\alpha\,\pi R_{\mathrm{i}}^2 = -2G\alpha A_1\). The condition (16.54) holds.

  4. The integral over \(S\) by theorem 4.59 in polar coordinates: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\pi\int_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{i}}^{R_{\mathrm{o}}}\dfrac{G\alpha}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - r^2)\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = \pi G\alpha\Big[\dfrac{R_{\mathrm{o}}^2r^2}{2} - \dfrac{r^4}{4}\Big]_{\mathrm{R}_\mathrm{i}}^{R_{\mathrm{o}}} = \dfrac{\pi G\alpha}{4}\,(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2)^2\).

  5. So by equation (16.55) \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = \dfrac{\pi G\alpha}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2)^2 + \pi G\alpha\,R_{\mathrm{i}}^2(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = \dfrac{\pi G\alpha}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2)(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = G\alpha\,\dfrac{\pi}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4)\), which is \(GJ\alpha\) with \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) from lemma 16.2.

Source: The result \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) for the tube is theorem 16.3 with \(\psi = 0\): a second way to the same result. BARBER section 16.5 (PDF pp. 260-261) mentions the tube as an example, SADD section 9.6 (PDF pp. 267-269) treats a hollow bar. The representation with the function \(\Phi\) is not from the sources.

The tube agrees with the circular annulus of section 16.1, which is a double check of both theorems: the first used a displacement without warping, the second the function \(\Phi\) with the hole. On the tube \(40/32\) mm of the figure we have \(R_{\mathrm{o}} = 20\) mm, \(R_{\mathrm{i}} = 16\) mm, \(t = 4\) mm, \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 18\) mm, \(A_1 = 804{,}2\) mm\(^2\). The first term of the moment (16.55) is \(2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 32\,572\,G\alpha\) mm\(^4\) (units mm\(^4\), like \(J\)), the second \(2\Phi_1A_1 = 115\,812\,G\alpha\) mm\(^4\), together \(J = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\). The hole therefore contributes \(78{,}0\,\%\) of the whole: leaving it out would lose almost four fifths of the stiffness. At \(50\) N m we have \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ) = 0{,}00447\) rad/m, and the stress is by corollary 16.4 \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\), so \(6{,}74\) MPa on the outer edge and \(5{,}39\) MPa on the inner one, in the section with the same direction everywhere (the blue arrows point round the hole in the same direction, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha y\) is positive at \(y > 0\)).

And what if we forget the hole condition?

Example 16.55 A tube with a wrong boundary condition (cev z napačnim robnim pogojem)

The tube \(40/32\) mm would be solved with \(\Phi = 0\) on both circles instead of \(\Phi = \Phi_1\) on the inner circle, as if the section were simply connected.

  1. The rotationally symmetric solution of the equation \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) is \(\Phi/(G\alpha) = -r^2/2 + c_1\ln r + c_2\) (SymPy, num-08.py). From \(\Phi(20) = \Phi(16) = 0\) we get \(c_1 = 72/\ln 1{,}25 = 322{,}66\) mm\(^2\) and \(c_2 = -766{,}61\) mm\(^2\).
  2. The function has its largest value \(4{,}006\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\) at \(r = 17{,}96\) mm (grey dashed hill on the figure), so only \(5{,}6\,\%\) of the true \(\Phi_1 = 72\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\), shown by the flat plate over the blue membrane.
  3. The flux of the wrong function through the inner circle is \(\oint\partial\Phi/\partial n_1\,\mathrm{d}s = +418{,}9\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\), whereas condition (16.54) demands \(-2G\alpha A_1 = -1608{,}5\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\). The wrong \(\Phi\) even has the wrong sign: it rises from the edge of the hole into the wall, the true function falls.
  4. The moment (16.55) with \(\Phi_1 = 0\) gives for the wrong hill \(J = \frac{2}{G\alpha}\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 2415\) mm\(^4\). This is \(1{,}6\,\%\) of the true value and \(0{,}1\,\%\) more than \(\ell t^3/3 = 2413\) mm\(^4\) for the slit tube of example 16.43.

The reason for the last result: \(\Phi = 0\) on both faces of the wall is the problem of a strip with zero on both sides, as in theorem 16.41, and the wall of the tube is a strip, only bent into a circle. The wrong problem therefore has the solution of a strip bent into a closed ring without ends. This is not exactly the slit tube. The two faces of the slit must be free, but there the wrong \(\Phi\) would give a shear vector with the component \(-\partial\Phi/\partial r\) across the face, which is not zero (its resultant over the face is zero). Moreover, the slit tube has two free ends of the strip at the slit, which lower \(J\): for a straight strip with the same \(\ell/t = 113{,}1/4 = 28{,}3\), by theorem 16.41 by at most \(2{,}2\,\%\). The formula \(\ell t^3/3\) neglects both, so it agrees with the wrong problem to \(0{,}1\,\%\). The hole condition (16.54) is what closes the tube in the problem, and without it the calculation gives almost the stiffness of the slit tube.

The hole can be shown on the membrane.

We stretch the membrane of section 16.4 (corollary 16.34) over the outer edge and fix it on the line \(y\) at \(-R_{\mathrm{o}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{o}}\), where the height is zero. Over the wall the membrane rises, as the height of the function \(\Phi\) rises towards the hole. Over the hole there is no material, so there is no membrane: there we place a thin flat plate without weight, which can only rise and fall, and glue the membrane to its edge. The plate is flat because \(\Phi\) does not depend on the point on the hole, being the constant \(\Phi_1\) on \(\Gamma_1\). The pressure \(p\) acts from below on the membrane and on the plate, in the tube it is the ratio \(p/N = 2G\alpha\) of the corollary, and the plate rises to the height at which it is in equilibrium: the membrane pulls it down along its edge with the force \(N\) per unit length, the vertical component is \(N\,|\partial\Phi/\partial n|\) (for small slopes, at slope \(0{,}2\) the error is \(1{,}94\,\%\), section 16.4), and the sum along the edge must balance the \(p\,A_1\) of the pressure on the plate:

\[N\oint_{\Gamma_1}\Big(-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n_1}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s = p\,A_1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \oint_{\Gamma_1}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n_1}\,\mathrm{d}s = -\frac{p}{N}\,A_1 = -2G\alpha\,A_1 . \tag{16.57}\]

This is the hole condition (16.54): the equilibrium of the plate and the single-valued displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) give the same equation. The height \(\Phi_1\) of the plate, which is unknown, is the one at which the forces are in equilibrium. Likewise the moment (16.55) is twice the whole volume under the membrane and the plate: \(2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\) is twice the volume under the membrane over the material, and \(2\Phi_1A_1\) twice the volume of the cylinder under the plate, with base \(A_1\) and height \(\Phi_1\).

For thin walls we need less than the solution. The figure shows the same tube in three panels, a general section with one hole.

The section is a tube of any shape, and its wall is thin compared with the size of the hole. The mean line is the dashed curve halfway between the two edges of the wall, its length is \(\ell\), and the area it encloses is \(A_0\). How \(\Phi\) varies across the wall we know only for the circular tube, so we take the simplest guess: across the thickness \(t\) it is linear, from \(0\) on the outer edge to \(\Phi_1\) on the inner one. Then the slope across the wall is \(\Phi_1/t\) everywhere, and by lemma 16.28 the stress has the size \(\tau = \Phi_1/t\) and is tangent to the wall.

Why the straight line is a good approximation we check on the circular tube \(40/32\) mm, where we know the exact \(\Phi\) from corollary 16.54. In axes with the radius \(r\) from \(16\) to \(20\) mm horizontal and \(\Phi/(G\alpha)\) vertical we draw the exact function \(\Phi/(G\alpha) = (R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - r^2)/2\) from \(\Phi_1 = 72\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\) at the inner edge to zero at the outer one, and the straight line between the same end points. At this scale they nearly coincide, so we draw their difference magnified below: it is a parabola with zeros at the edges and largest value \(G\alpha\,t^2/8 = 2\,G\alpha\) mm\(^2\) at the mean line, which is \(t/(8r_{\mathrm{m}}) = 2{,}8\,\%\) of \(\Phi_1 = G\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{m}}t\) (SymPy). The guess is therefore good when the wall is thin compared with the radius, and worse for a thick wall: at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}67\) the deviation is already \(8{,}3\,\%\). What the guess costs in \(J\) is measured by point 4 of the theorem below and by example 16.57.

The stress \(\tau = \Phi_1/t\) must satisfy equilibrium and the moment. Equilibrium: take a piece of wall between two transverse cuts \(s_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{b}}\) along the mean line, of length \(L\) along the axis \(x\). The lateral faces of the piece are free, and on the end faces at \(x\) there is no normal stress, so in the direction \(x\) only the shear stress on the cuts acts. Its integral across the thickness is \(q(s_{\mathrm{a}})\,L\) on one cut and \(q(s_{\mathrm{b}})\,L\) on the other, where \(q = \tau\,t\) is the shear force per unit length of the mean line, and they point in opposite directions. Equilibrium in the direction \(x\) demands \(q(s_{\mathrm{a}})L = q(s_{\mathrm{b}})L\), so the flow \(q\) is constant along the whole mean line, and where the wall is thinner the stress is larger. Moment: we put the origin of the axes \(y, z\) at the point \(O\). The flow \(q\) runs at a point \(P\) of the mean line along its tangent \(\vek t\) and has about \(O\) the lever arm \(p\), the distance from \(O\) to the tangent at \(P\). On a straight stretch \(PP'\) of length \(\delta\) the force would be \(q\,\delta\) with the moment \(q\,p\,\delta\), which is \(2q\) times the area \(\tfrac12\,p\,\delta\) of the triangle \(OPP'\) (base times height over two). The triangles over all stretches cover the area \(A_0\), so we expect \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2qA_0\). We get exactly this without stretches. With the outer normal of the mean line \(\vek n = (t_{\mathrm{z}}, -t_{\mathrm{y}})\) the lever arm at the point \((y, z)\) is \(p = y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}} = y\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), so the moment of the flow about the axis \(x\) is

\[M_{\mathrm{x}} = \oint q\,(y\,t_{\mathrm{z}} - z\,t_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}s = q\oint(y\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}s = 2qA_0 ,\]

the last equality by lemma 16.17 with the field \(\vek v = (y, z)\), whose divergence is \(2\), on the region inside the mean line, as in step 3 of theorem 16.53. So \(q = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(2A_0)\) and \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(2A_0t)\), the well-known formula of mechanics of materials. Now the same from \(\Phi\), because that also gives \(J\) and the error.

We write the guess as two hypotheses. (H1) is the straight line across the wall, written as the slope at the mean line. (H2) says that the volume under the membrane and the plate, which by equation (16.55) is half of the moment, equals \(\Phi_1A_0\). The reason: over the hole there is a cylinder with base \(A_1\) and height \(\Phi_1\), and over the wall, with the straight line, a wedge, \(\Phi_1\) high at the hole and zero outside. For a flat wall the wedge has the volume \(\Phi_1\) times half the area of the wall, which is \(\Phi_1\) times the area between the mean line and the hole, and together with the cylinder we get \(\Phi_1\) times the area \(A_0\) inside the mean line. For a curved wall the outer half is larger than the inner one, for the tube \(40/32\) mm \(76\pi\) against \(68\pi\) mm\(^2\), and the exact \(\Phi\) is not a straight line, so (H2) does not hold exactly. What this costs in \(J\) is given by point 4: \(1{,}2\,\%\) for this tube, already \(10\,\%\) at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}67\).

Theorem 16.56 Thin-walled closed section (tankostenski zaprt prerez)

Let \(S\) be a section with one hole (definition 16.51), \(\ell\) the length of the mean line (the curve halfway between \(\Gamma_0\) and \(\Gamma_1\) along the normal), \(A_0\) the area it encloses, \(t(s)\) the wall thickness along the mean line (\(s\) is the arc length). Let the following hold:

(H1) on the mean line \(\partial\Phi/\partial n = -\Phi_1/t(s)\), where \(\vek n\) is the normal of the mean line out of the hole (mean slope across the wall),

(H2) \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + \Phi_1A_1 = \Phi_1A_0\), on the right is \(\Phi_1\) times the area \(A_0\).

Then:

  1. \(\Phi_1\) \(= \dfrac{2G\alpha A_0}{\oint ds/t}\).
  2. The flow \(q\) \(= \Phi_1 = \dfrac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{2A_0}\) and the mean stress across the wall \(\tau = \dfrac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{2A_0\,t(s)}\).
  3. \(J = \dfrac{4A_0^2}{\oint ds/t}\), where \(A_0\) is the area and \(\oint ds/t\) the integral along the mean line, and for constant thickness
    \[J = \frac{4A_0^2\,t}{\ell} . \tag{16.58}\]
  4. For a tube with mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}}\) and thickness \(t\), (H1) holds exactly and \(J_{\text{exact}}/J = 1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2)\).
Idea

The hole condition on the mean line gives \(\Phi_1\), and the moment contains the hypothesis (H2). For the tube we check both on the exact solution.

Proof

We must show four claims, the first two with the hypotheses, the third by combining, the fourth by computation.

  1. By point 2 of theorem 16.53 on the mean line with area \(A_{\mathrm{C}} = A_0\) we have \(\oint\partial\Phi/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s = -2G\alpha A_0\). By (H1) the left side is \(-\Phi_1\oint ds/t\), whence point 1.
  2. By equation (16.55) and (H2), \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\big(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + \Phi_1A_1\big) = 2\Phi_1A_0\), so \(\Phi_1 = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(2A_0)\). The mean slope of \(\Phi\) across the wall is \(\Phi_1/t\) (a height difference \(\Phi_1\) over a length \(t\)), and by lemma 16.28 this is the size of the stress.
  3. From points 1 and 2 (the flow \(q\) is \(\Phi_1\)): \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2A_0\Phi_1 = 4G\alpha A_0^2/\oint ds/t\), which is \(GJ\alpha\). For constant \(t\) we have \(\oint ds/t = \ell/t\).
  4. For the tube \(\Phi = \tfrac{G\alpha}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - r^2)\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial r = -G\alpha r\), at \(r = r_{\mathrm{m}}\) it is \(-G\alpha r_{\mathrm{m}}\). From \(\Phi_1 = \tfrac{G\alpha}{2}(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = G\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{m}}t\) we get \(\Phi_1/t = G\alpha r_{\mathrm{m}}\), so (H1) holds exactly. From \(R_{\mathrm{o},\mathrm{i}} = r_{\mathrm{m}} \pm t/2\) we have \(R_{\mathrm{o}}^4 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^4 = (R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 - R_{\mathrm{i}}^2)(R_{\mathrm{o}}^2 + R_{\mathrm{i}}^2) = 2r_{\mathrm{m}}t\,(2r_{\mathrm{m}}^2 + t^2/2)\), so \(J_{\text{exact}} = \pi r_{\mathrm{m}}t\,(2r_{\mathrm{m}}^2 + t^2/2) = 2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^3t\,(1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2))\). Equation (16.58) with \(A_0 = \pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^2\) and \(\ell = 2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}\) gives \(4\pi^2r_{\mathrm{m}}^4t/(2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}) = 2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^3t\). The quotient is \(1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2)\).

Source: BARBER section 16.5.1, equations (16.56) to (16.59) (PDF pp. 261-262): \(\Phi\) linear across the wall, \(\tau = \Phi_1/t\), \(\Phi_1\) from the hole condition, moment and rigidity. ONATE2 section 4.3.5 (PDF pp. 204-206) the same for thin walls. The route with equilibrium of a piece and the lever arm is not from the sources, we do not name an author of the formula because none is in our sources. Claim 4 is not from the sources, the computation is in num-08.py.

The hypotheses (H1) and (H2) are a guess, so we test the theorem: is the error really what claim 4 says, and when does the formula fail? We let the inner diameter grow while the outer diameter stays \(40\) mm and draw the error.

First the axes: horizontally the ratio \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}}\), downwards the error of the formula in percent. In them we draw the curve that claim 4 predicts, and on it the five points from the rows below, from the thinnest to the thickest wall. Last we add the limit \(-0{,}3\,\%\) and the limit \(-2\,\%\), up to which the rule below uses the formula.

Example 16.57 Error of the thin-wall formula on a tube (napaka tankostenske formule na cevi)

For tubes with \(d_{\mathrm{o}} = 40\) mm and growing wall thickness, by equation (16.58) (num-08.py, part 4), points on the curve:

  1. \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 38\) mm: \(t = 1\) mm, \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}051\), \(J = 46\,620\) mm\(^4\), formula \(46\,589\) mm\(^4\), error \(-0{,}07\,\%\).
  2. \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 36\) mm: \(t = 2\) mm, \(J = 86\,431\) mm\(^4\), formula \(86\,193\) mm\(^4\), \(-0{,}28\,\%\).
  3. \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 32\) mm: \(t = 4\) mm, \(J = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\), formula \(146\,574\) mm\(^4\), \(-1{,}22\,\%\).
  4. \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 30\) mm: \(t = 5\) mm, \(J = 171\,806\) mm\(^4\), formula \(168\,370\) mm\(^4\), \(-2{,}00\,\%\).
  5. \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 20\) mm: \(t = 10\) mm, \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}667\), \(J = 235\,619\) mm\(^4\), formula \(212\,058\) mm\(^4\), \(-10{,}0\,\%\).

The exact value is always larger, the curve is below zero, in agreement with the quotient \(1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2)\), which is \(1{,}0007\), \(1{,}0028\), \(1{,}0123\), \(1{,}0204\) and \(1{,}111\). The error grows with the square of \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}}\). For the tube (H1) is exact (point 4), so the whole error lies in (H2), and the left side of (H2) exceeds the right side by \(\pi G\alpha\,r_{\mathrm{m}}t^3/4\) (SymPy), which is the difference \(J_{\text{exact}} - J = \pi r_{\mathrm{m}}t^3/2\) divided by \(2/(G\alpha)\). The difference has two causes. Two thirds arise because the exact \(\Phi\) is not a straight line: the stress \(\tau = G\alpha r\) is not constant across the wall, it is \(6{,}74\) MPa outside and \(5{,}39\) MPa inside. One third arises from the curvature, because the mean line divides the wall into unequal halves, the outer and the inner one, and the wedge before theorem 16.56 gives (H2) exactly only for equal halves.

The last row is the case where the theorem fails: a wall of thickness \(10\) mm at mean radius \(15\) mm is no longer thin. Rule: at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} \le 0{,}1\) the formula is accurate to \(0{,}3\,\%\), at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} \le 0{,}28\) to \(2\,\%\) (at \(0{,}3\) the error is already \(2{,}2\,\%\)), at \(t/r_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}67\) it is not usable. For a section that is not circular we do not prove the same bound, so we use the same scale only as an estimate.

Now the same tube in both forms.

Example 16.58 Closed and slit tube (zaprta in razrezana cev)

Tube \(40/32\) mm, \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa, \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, figure 16.74, \(t = 4\) mm, \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 18\) mm, \(\ell = 113{,}1\) mm:

  1. Closed: \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} = 148\,384\) mm\(^4\), \(GJ = 11\,186\) N m\(^2\), \(\alpha = 0{,}00447\) rad/m. Stress \(6{,}74\) MPa outside and \(5{,}39\) MPa inside. Thin formula: \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(2A_0t) = 6{,}14\) MPa, between the two values.
  2. Slit: by corollary 16.42 \(J = \ell t^3/3 = 2413\) mm\(^4\), \(\alpha = 0{,}2749\) rad/m, \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}t/J =\) \(82{,}9\) MPa.
  3. The ratio of stiffnesses \(J_{\text{closed}}/J_{\text{slit}} = 61{,}5\) (closed against slit) and the ratio of largest stresses \(82{,}9/6{,}74 = 12{,}3\) (slit against closed).
  4. The reason for \(61{,}5\): from theorem 16.56 (point 4), \(J_{\text{closed}}/J_{\text{slit}} = \dfrac{2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^3t\,(1 + t^2/(4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2))}{2\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}t^3/3} = 3\,\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{m}}^2}{t^2}\Big(1 + \dfrac{t^2}{4r_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\Big) = 3\cdot 20{,}25\cdot 1{,}0123 = 61{,}5\), because \(r_{\mathrm{m}}^2/t^2 = 20{,}25\).

The flow of stress explains where this size comes from. In the closed wall the shear flow goes round the hole in the same direction through the whole thickness, the whole wall contributes to the moment with the same sign and with the lever arm \(r_{\mathrm{m}}\), which is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2qA_0\) of the theorem. In the slit wall there is no flow round the hole, because the slit would interrupt it: the stress runs forward on one face and back on the other, the profile across the wall is antisymmetric with zero in the middle. The forces of the two halves cancel, and what remains is a couple whose arm is of size \(t\) instead of \(r_{\mathrm{m}}\). The ratio \(r_{\mathrm{m}}/t = 4{,}5\) enters twice, hence squared (\(3\,r_{\mathrm{m}}^2/t^2\) in point 4 of the example): once in the lever arm and once in the stress at the same twist \(\alpha\). In the closed wall it is \(\tau = G\alpha r\) (from \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}r/I_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GI_{\mathrm{p}}\alpha\)), at the face of the slit wall it is \(\tau = G\alpha t\) (from \(\tau = M_{\mathrm{x}}t/J\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\alpha\), corollary 16.42), so at the mean line of the closed wall it is \(r_{\mathrm{m}}/t\) times larger than at the face of the slit one. In the closed wall the profile has the same sign but not the same size: \(6{,}74\) outside and \(5{,}39\) inside, because \(\tau = G\alpha r\) is larger at the larger radius.

From this follows advice for design.

We compare three sections with the same outer diameter. First the solid shaft, to which we assign \(1\) for mass and for \(J\). Next to it the tube \(40/32\): by mass it has \(0{,}360\) of the cross-section of the solid shaft \(\varnothing 40\) mm, as the mass bar shows, and \(0{,}590\) of its \(J\) (num-08.py, part 6), as the stiffness bar shows, so it has \(1{,}64\) times more stiffness per unit mass. Last the slit tube with the same mass, but its stiffness bar is \(61{,}5\) times lower than that of the closed one.

With theorem 16.53 we have also covered sections with several holes (cells): for every hole its own condition (16.54) holds with \(\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\) as the unknown, and the moment (16.55) has one term \(2\Phi_{\mathrm{k}}A_{\mathrm{k}}\) for each. For thin walls with several cells we get a system of equations for the flows in the walls, which we state without derivation (SADD section 9.3.3, PDF pp. 252-254, and ONATE2 section 4.3.5.1, PDF p. 206). Effects that we do not treat here: restrained warping and nonuniform torsion of hollow sections (COOK PDF pp. 42-43, ONATE2 section 4.10, PDF pp. 227-246).

A question remains: how well can we bound \(J\) without solving the problem? For a section with a hole this is harder, because a trial function must also satisfy the hole condition (16.54) or take the hole as a constant value. Section 16.9 bounds \(J\) from both sides on a section without a hole, where theorem 16.24 is enough.
