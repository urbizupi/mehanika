Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.1 The triangle: the simplest element of a body

In chapter 7 we cut a bar into elements with two nodes, and the field of displacements on each was a straight line. Now, instead of a bar, we take a body that extends in a plane or in space, and cut it up the same way. The chapter is called Finite elements of the continuum. A continuum (kontinuum) is matter that a field describes at every point, so one without holes, and the word comes from Latin, where it means unbroken. Elements of the continuum are therefore finite pieces of such matter. The chapter makes three promises. We show that the Ritz theorems of chapter 6 hold for a body cut into pieces as well. We compute the tube and the sphere of chapter 11 with finite elements and compare the result with the Lamé solutions. And we say when an element fails and how to test for it. We start with the simplest piece, the triangle, and with a field that has a single number at each node, like a temperature. Displacements have two numbers per node in the plane, so section 15.4 treats them, on the same triangle.

15.1.1 A plate cut into triangles

The model is a thin plate (tanka plošča) lying in the plane \(x, y\) with the same small thickness everywhere, and the temperature in it is a single number at each place, not depending on the thickness. This is an idealization. A real plate can be warmer on one face than on the other. For a plate 2 mm thick and 200 mm wide the thickness is one hundredth of the width, and the temperature difference across the thickness is small against the difference across the plane, so we neglect it. We look for the field \(T(x, y)\), a number for every point of the plate.

We first draw the outline of the plate, a polygon with eight corners. Then we cut it with straight segments into pieces so that the mesh consists of triangles. We choose the triangle because it needs the fewest points to hold it. A segment, the bar element, is held by two points, and the field on it is fixed by two numbers. The plane is spanned by two directions, so the field on a triangle will be fixed by three numbers, as we shall see with the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) of this section. Moreover, a convex polygon (konveksen mnogokotnik), such as the outline in figure 15.1, is cut into triangles by the diagonals from one corner. In a polygon with a re-entrant corner, such as the letter L, the diagonals from some corners lie partly outside it. Such a polygon we first cut into convex pieces, the letter L into two rectangles, and then each piece by diagonals. A plate with a curved boundary is filled by triangles only approximately: along the boundary narrow slivers remain between the straight edge and the arc, and they are smaller for smaller triangles. The corners of the triangles are the nodes of the mesh, as the ends of the elements were for the bar (definition 6.24). Each element, the highlighted blue triangle in the figure, has three nodes. We enlarge it to the right and number its nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) in the counterclockwise direction. Why this direction, we shall see with the area: with this choice the signed area of the triangle is positive.

We have to define the concepts we used first. A triangle in the plane is a set of points and not only three lines.

Definition 15.1 Triangle in the plane (trikotnik v ravnini)

Let \(P_1 = (x_1, y_1)\), \(P_2 = (x_2, y_2)\) and \(P_3 = (x_3, y_3)\) be points of the plane and \(\vek u = P_2 - P_1\), \(\vek v = P_3 - P_1\) vectors with components \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \((v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}})\). The points \(P_1, P_2, P_3\) do not lie on one line exactly when the vectors are not parallel, that is, when \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} \neq 0\). The triangle (trikotnik) with vertices (oglišča) \(P_1\), \(P_2\), \(P_3\) that do not lie on one line is the set of points

\[T = \{\, P_1 + s\,\vek u + t\,\vek v \;:\; s \ge 0,\ t \ge 0,\ s + t \le 1 \,\} . \tag{15.1}\]

The segments between two vertices are the edges (stranice) of the triangle. The signed area (predznačena ploščina) of the vertices used is the number

\[\Delta(P_1, P_2, P_3) = \tfrac12\,(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}}) = \tfrac12\,\big(x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 + x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2 + x_3 y_1 - x_1 y_3\big) . \tag{15.2}\]

The vertices are numbered counterclockwise (v smeri, nasprotni vrtenju kazalcev na uri) when \(\Delta(P_1, P_2, P_3) > 0\).

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.1 (PDF p. 156), COOK §7.3 (PDF p. 278). The notation of the set \(T\) and of the signed area is ours.

The second expression in equation (15.2) follows by inserting \(\vek u\) and \(\vek v\) in the first and multiplying out: \((x_2 - x_1)(y_3 - y_1) - (x_3 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1) = x_2 y_3 - x_2 y_1 - x_1 y_3 + x_1 y_1 - x_3 y_2 + x_3 y_1 + x_1 y_2 - x_1 y_1\), and the two terms \(x_1 y_1\) cancel. Why the order with \(\Delta > 0\) is named after the direction of a tour, the triangle with vertices \((0, 0)\), \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\) shows. The tour \(P_1 \to P_2 \to P_3\) goes from the origin to the right, then up and to the left and back down, so counterclockwise. The vectors \(\vek u = (1, 0)\) and \(\vek v = (0, 1)\) give \(\Delta = \tfrac12 > 0\). If we swap the second and third vertex, the tour runs clockwise, \(\vek u\) and \(\vek v\) swap, the difference in the bracket changes sign and \(\Delta = -\tfrac12\). We shall always number the vertices counterclockwise, so that \(\Delta\) is the area without a sign. The next lemma shows this.

Lemma 15.2 Area of a triangle (ploščina trikotnika)

The triangle \(T\) of definition 15.1 is a set with area and its area is \(A = |\Delta(P_1, P_2, P_3)|\).

Proof

We must show that \(T\) is an affine image of the right triangle of area \(\tfrac12\) and use the theorem on the area under a linear map.

  1. Let \(T_0 = \{(s, t) : s \ge 0,\ t \ge 0,\ s + t \le 1\}\) be the triangle with vertices \((0, 0)\), \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\). It is the figure of the points with \(0 \le t \le 1\) and \(0 \le s \le 1 - t\), so the figure between the graphs of the continuous functions \(g(t) = 0\) and \(h(t) = 1 - t\) with \(g \le h\) on \([0, 1]\), in which the coordinate \(s\) runs between the graphs. By theorem 4.33, the planar case with the roles of the coordinates exchanged, \(T_0\) is a set with area (definition 4.27).
  2. The area of \(T_0\) is the integral of the function 1 (definition 4.30). By the same theorem it equals the iterated integral: \(\int_0^1 \big(\int_0^{1-t} 1\,\mathrm{d}s\big)\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^1 (1 - t)\,\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12\).
  3. The map \(\Lambda(s, t) = P_1 + s\,\vek u + t\,\vek v\) has the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} u_{\mathrm{x}} & v_{\mathrm{x}} \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} & v_{\mathrm{y}} \end{pmatrix}\) with determinant \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} = 2\Delta \neq 0\) (definition 2.50), and \(T = \Lambda(T_0)\) by equation (15.1).
  4. By theorem 4.58 on the area under a linear map, \(T\) is a set with area, and \(A = |2\Delta| \cdot \tfrac12 = |\Delta|\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The area of a triangle as half a determinant is quoted in ONATE1 (4.32) (PDF p. 158), where \(A^{(e)}\) appears.

Take the triangle with vertices \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\), \((10, 30)\) mm. For it \(\vek u = (40, 0)\) mm, \(\vek v = (10, 30)\) mm and \(\Delta = \tfrac12\,(40 \cdot 30 - 10 \cdot 0)\ \text{mm}^2 = 600\ \text{mm}^2\). The same triangle carries all the computations of this section.

15.1.2 Area coordinates

How do we describe the place of a point \(P\) in a triangle with ratios that the choice of axes does not change? For the bar it was the ratio of lengths, \((x_{\mathrm{b}} - x)/h\) and \((x - x_{\mathrm{a}})/h\) (definition 7.2). For the triangle we take ratios of areas.

We draw the triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) and in it the point \(P\). The segments from the point \(P\) to all three vertices cut the triangle into three smaller ones. The blue triangle \(A_1\) lies opposite vertex \(1\), since it is bounded by the point \(P\) and the edge \(2\)–\(3\). Likewise the orange \(A_2\) lies opposite vertex \(2\) and the grey \(A_3\) opposite vertex \(3\). The areas are \(150\), \(250\) and \(200\) mm², together \(600\) mm², the area of the triangle. The ratios \(150/600\), \(250/600\), \(200/600\) are numbers between 0 and 1 with sum 1. They get the name area coordinates (ploskovne koordinate) because they are ratios of areas. Since the sum is 1, two of the ratios already fix the point. The same ratio is also a ratio of distances, as the right part of the figure shows: the area of a triangle with base the edge \(2\)–\(3\) is half the base times the height, so \(A_1/A = s/h\), where \(s\) is the distance of the point \(P\) from the edge \(2\)–\(3\) and \(h\) the distance of vertex \(1\) from the same edge. With \(P\) at vertex \(1\) we have \(s = h\) and the ratio is 1, with \(P\) on the edge \(2\)–\(3\) we have \(s = 0\) and the ratio is 0. This is the same behaviour as that of the shape functions of the bar. For points outside the triangle the areas would have to get a sign, so we define the coordinates with the signed area.

Definition 15.3 Area coordinates (ploskovne koordinate)

Let \(T\) be a triangle with vertices \(P_1, P_2, P_3\) numbered counterclockwise and \(A = \Delta(P_1, P_2, P_3) > 0\) its area (lemma 15.2). The area coordinates (ploskovne koordinate) of a point \(P\) of the plane are the numbers

\[L_1(P) = \frac{\Delta(P, P_2, P_3)}{A} , \qquad L_2(P) = \frac{\Delta(P_1, P, P_3)}{A} , \qquad L_3(P) = \frac{\Delta(P_1, P_2, P)}{A} . \tag{15.3}\]

The numbers \(L_1, L_2, L_3\) are functions of the point \(P\) with values in \(\mathbb{R}\). We always write the index \(\ix{i}\), because \(L\) elsewhere in the book means a length. In the numerator of \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) the vertex \(P_{\mathrm{i}}\) is replaced by the point \(P\).

Source: ONATE1 §5.5.1, (5.25) (PDF p. 196), COOK §7.3, (7.3-1) (PDF p. 278-279). In both, the coordinates are defined for a point in the triangle by ratios of areas. The signed area is our addition, since we shall need the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) also outside the triangle, for example on the edge of a neighbouring triangle.

Lemma 15.4 Properties of area coordinates (lastnosti ploskovnih koordinat)

Let \(L_1, L_2, L_3\) be the area coordinates of a triangle \(T\) with vertices \(P_{\mathrm{i}} = (x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\) (definition 15.3). Let \(a_1 = x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2\), \(b_1 = y_2 - y_3\), \(c_1 = x_3 - x_2\) and let \(a_2, b_2, c_2\), \(a_3, b_3, c_3\) be the numbers obtained by the cyclic exchange of indices \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\). Then:

  1. \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(x, y) = (a_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} x + c_{\mathrm{i}} y)/(2A)\), so every \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is an affine function, of the form \(\alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) with constant \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\).
  2. \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_{\mathrm{j}}) = 1\) for \(i = j\) and \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for \(i \neq j\).
  3. \(L_1 + L_2 + L_3 = 1\), \(\;L_1 x_1 + L_2 x_2 + L_3 x_3 = x\), \(\;L_1 y_1 + L_2 y_2 + L_3 y_3 = y\) at every point \((x, y)\).
  4. A point \(P = P_1 + s\,\vek u + t\,\vek v\) has the coordinates \(L_1 = 1 - s - t\), \(L_2 = s\), \(L_3 = t\). The point lies in \(T\) exactly when all three \(L_{\mathrm{i}} \ge 0\).
Proof

We must show four claims. The first follows by expanding the signed area, the second from it, the third by adding the coefficients, the fourth by introducing the coordinates \(s\) and \(t\).

  1. By equation (15.2), \(2\Delta(P, P_2, P_3) = x y_2 - x_2 y + x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2 + x_3 y - x y_3 = (x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2) + (y_2 - y_3)\,x + (x_3 - x_2)\,y = a_1 + b_1 x + c_1 y\), where we ordered the terms by \(x\) and \(y\). Dividing by \(2A\) gives the first claim for \(i = 1\). For \(i = 2\), \(2\Delta(P_1, P, P_3) = x_1 y - x y_1 + x y_3 - x_3 y + x_3 y_1 - x_1 y_3 = (x_3 y_1 - x_1 y_3) + (y_3 - y_1)\,x + (x_1 - x_3)\,y = a_2 + b_2 x + c_2 y\), and for \(i = 3\) the computation is the same.
  2. At \(P = P_1\), \(L_1 = \Delta(P_1, P_2, P_3)/A = 1\). If two of the three arguments of the signed area coincide, one of the vectors \(\vek u\), \(\vek v\) is zero or the two are equal, and in both cases \(u_{\mathrm{x}} v_{\mathrm{y}} - v_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). So \(L_1(P_2) = \Delta(P_2, P_2, P_3)/A = 0\) and \(L_1(P_3) = \Delta(P_3, P_2, P_3)/A = 0\). For \(L_2\) and \(L_3\) the computation is the same.
  3. The sum \(a_1 + a_2 + a_3 = (x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2) + (x_3 y_1 - x_1 y_3) + (x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1) = 2A\) by equation (15.2). The sum \(b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = (y_2 - y_3) + (y_3 - y_1) + (y_1 - y_2) = 0\), and \(c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 0\) for the same reason. So \(L_1 + L_2 + L_3 = (2A + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot y)/(2A) = 1\). Further, \(\sum a_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} = x_1 (x_2 y_3 - x_3 y_2) + x_2 (x_3 y_1 - x_1 y_3) + x_3 (x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1) = 0\), because the six terms cancel in pairs, \(\sum b_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} = x_1 (y_2 - y_3) + x_2 (y_3 - y_1) + x_3 (y_1 - y_2) = 2A\) and \(\sum c_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} = x_1 (x_3 - x_2) + x_2 (x_1 - x_3) + x_3 (x_2 - x_1) = 0\). So \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} = (0 + 2A\,x + 0)/(2A) = x\). For \(y\) the computation is the same.
  4. By claim 3, \(P = \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} P_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\sum L_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\), so \(P = P_1 + L_2\,(P_2 - P_1) + L_3\,(P_3 - P_1)\), and \(L_1 = 1 - L_2 - L_3\). Comparing with \(P = P_1 + s\,\vek u + t\,\vek v\) gives \(L_2 = s\) and \(L_3 = t\), because \(\vek u\) and \(\vek v\) are not parallel (definition 15.1). By equation (15.1) the point is in \(T\) exactly when \(s \ge 0\), \(t \ge 0\), \(s + t \le 1\), that is \(L_2 \ge 0\), \(L_3 \ge 0\) and \(L_1 \ge 0\).

Source: ONATE1 (5.26)-(5.28) (PDF p. 196-197), COOK (7.3-2), (7.3-3) (PDF p. 279). The notation of the coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{i}}, b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}}\) is as in ONATE1 (4.32b), the proof is not from the sources.

Let us look at the lemma on the triangle of the example. The coefficients are \(a = (1200, 0, 0)\), \(b = (-30, 30, 0)\) mm, \(c = (-30, -10, 40)\) mm and \(2A = 1200\) mm². So

\[L_1 = 1 - \frac{x}{40} - \frac{y}{40} , \qquad L_2 = \frac{x}{40} - \frac{y}{120} , \qquad L_3 = \frac{y}{30} , \tag{15.4}\]

with \(x\) and \(y\) in mm. At the point \(P = (20, 10)\) mm the values are \(0{,}25\), \(0{,}4167\) and \(0{,}3333\), which are exactly the ratios \(150/600\), \(250/600\) and \(200/600\) of figure 15.2. At the centroid \((50/3, 10)\) mm all three equal \(\tfrac13\), since the partial areas are \(200\) mm² each. At \(P = (10, 0)\) mm, on the edge \(1\)–\(2\), \(L_3 = 0\) (the point is on the edge opposite vertex \(3\)), \(L_1 = 0{,}75\) and \(L_2 = 0{,}25\). The point \((40, 30)\) mm is outside the triangle and \(L_1 = -0{,}75\), which by claim 4 means that it lies outside.

15.1.3 The linear triangle

For the bar the field on the element is a straight line through the displacements of the ends, and the shape functions had the properties of definition 7.2 and lemma 7.3: the value 1 at their own node, 0 at the other and the sum 1. For the triangle we take the same idea: the field on the element equals the value of the node at each node, and between them is as simple as possible. We shall show that three numbers fix exactly one plane \(\alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) through three points that are not on one line. That the plane has three coefficients is not enough for this. If the points were on one line, for example \((0, 0)\), \((1, 0)\), \((2, 0)\), the values \(0\), \(1\), \(3\) would give \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = 1\) and at \(x = 2\) the value \(2 \neq 3\), so no plane at all. The values \(0\), \(1\), \(2\) would give \(\beta = 1\) and any \(\gamma\), so infinitely many planes. That is why the definition required that the vertices not lie on one line. By lemma 15.4 the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) have the value 1 at their own node and 0 at the other two, so the sum \(L_1 d_1 + L_2 d_2 + L_3 d_3\) is a plane with the values \(d_1, d_2, d_3\) at the nodes, as for the bar. There is no other such plane. Every affine function \(f = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) equals, by claim 3 of the same lemma, \(\alpha \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} + \beta \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} + \gamma \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} y_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\alpha + \beta x_{\mathrm{i}} + \gamma y_{\mathrm{i}}) = \sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\,f(P_{\mathrm{i}})\), so its values at the three vertices already fix it.

Over the nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) of the base triangle we draw, for each function \(L_1\), \(L_2\) and \(L_3\), a triangle, above its node the height is \(1\) and above the other two nodes \(0\). By the previous paragraph exactly one plane passes through three such points, and it is the graph of the function \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\). If we add the three planes, we get the plane at height \(1\), since the sum at every node is \(1 + 0 + 0 = 1\), and a sum of affine functions is an affine function with value 1 at three vertices (lemma 15.4, claim 3).

Definition 15.5 Linear triangle (linearni trikotnik)

Let \(T\) be a triangle with vertices \(P_1, P_2, P_3\) in the counterclockwise direction (definition 15.1). The linear triangle (linearni trikotnik) is this triangle together with three local nodes (lokalna vozlišča) at the vertices and with a column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (d_1, d_2, d_3)^\T\) of three real numbers, the values of the field at the nodes. The field on the element is

\[u_{\mathrm{h}}(x, y) = L_1(x, y)\,d_1 + L_2(x, y)\,d_2 + L_3(x, y)\,d_3 = \mat N_{\mathrm{e}}(x, y)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} , \tag{15.5}\]

where \(\mat N_{\mathrm{e}} = (N_1, N_2, N_3)\) is the row of shape functions (definition 7.2) \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = L_{\mathrm{i}}\). At \(P_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(P_{\mathrm{j}}) = d_{\mathrm{j}}\) by lemma 15.4, claim 2. The field is linear in the sense of an affine function, a polynomial of degree at most one in \(x\) and \(y\).

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.1, (4.25)-(4.30) (PDF p. 156-157) and §5.5.1, (5.28) (PDF p. 197): the area coordinates are exactly the shape functions of the three-node triangle. COOK §7.3 (PDF p. 279), where \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is written as a conversion.

From history

The linear triangle is also called the Turner element. ONATE1 (PDF p. 155) explains the name: before the finite element era, Courant successfully used linear polynomials over triangular regions to solve differential equations in plane domains, and some years later Turner and co-authors proposed in a classic paper that a plane solid be discretized into simple triangles, because this fits the matrix methods for structures of bars. The same book states that this is the first element ever used for the analysis of structures and that its success lies in its simplicity, which lets the finite element method merge with the matrix method for bar structures that engineers know (ONATE1, PDF p. 155). Why Turner developed the triangle and what Martin, Turner, Clough and Topp said about it is in chapter 7, and Courant's idea is in chapter 6.

Let us return to the element we have: the linear triangle with the field (15.5), which is continuous and has its own formula on each triangle of the mesh. Do two neighbouring elements together give a continuous field over the whole plate? For the bar the answer comes by itself, because neighbouring elements share a point, the node. For the triangle they share a whole edge, which is infinitely many points.

15.1.4 Joining elements across an edge

The body is assembled from elements that do not overlap and touch along nodes and edges, like the pieces of a puzzle.

Definition 15.6 Triangular mesh (mreža trikotnikov)

A triangular mesh (mreža trikotnikov) of a body, which is a set of points of the plane \(\Omega\) with area (definition 4.27), is a finite set of triangles \(T_1, \dots, T_{\mathrm{m}}\) (definition 15.1), the elements (elementi) of the mesh, with the following two properties. The union of the elements is \(\Omega\). The intersection of two different elements is empty, a single common point that is a vertex of both, or a whole common edge that is an edge of both. The vertices of the elements are the nodes (vozlišča) of the mesh, numbered with global numbers \(1, \dots, n\). A field on the mesh is a function \(u_{\mathrm{h}} : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) determined by one value \(d_{\mathrm{k}}\) for each node \(k\), which on each element has the form (15.5) with the values \(d_{\mathrm{k}}\) of its three nodes. Two elements with the same node therefore have the same value for it.

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.1 (PDF p. 155-156), COOK §7.3 (PDF p. 278). The wording of the condition on the intersection is ours.

The definition of the field on the mesh has a hidden requirement. At a common point of two elements their formulas must give the same value, otherwise \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is not a single function. The requirement that the intersection of two elements be a whole edge is a condition on neighbouring elements. What follows from it?

Lemma 15.7 Continuity across an edge (zveznost čez stranico)

Let \(T\) and \(T'\) be two elements of a triangular mesh (definition 15.6) with a common edge between nodes \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\), and let \(u\) and \(u'\) be the fields of the linear triangles \(T\) and \(T'\) (definition 15.5) with values \(d_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(d_{\mathrm{b}}\) at the nodes \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\). Then \(u\) and \(u'\) are equal on the common edge. At a point \(P = P_{\mathrm{a}} + \lambda\,(P_{\mathrm{b}} - P_{\mathrm{a}})\) with \(0 \le \lambda \le 1\) both have the value \((1 - \lambda)\,d_{\mathrm{a}} + \lambda\,d_{\mathrm{b}}\). The field on the mesh is therefore well defined and continuous on \(\Omega\).

Proof

We must show that on the edge the field of a triangle depends only on the values at its ends, and then build from this the continuity on the whole mesh. We treat the triangle \(T\) with vertices \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{c}}\).

  1. The function \(\lambda \mapsto L_{\mathrm{a}}(P_{\mathrm{a}} + \lambda\,(P_{\mathrm{b}} - P_{\mathrm{a}}))\) is an affine function of the number \(\lambda\), because \(L_{\mathrm{a}}\) is an affine function (lemma 15.4, claim 1) and \(x\) and \(y\) are affine functions of \(\lambda\). It has the value 1 at \(\lambda = 0\) (the point \(P_{\mathrm{a}}\)) and 0 at \(\lambda = 1\) (the point \(P_{\mathrm{b}}\)) by claim 2 of the lemma. The affine function of \(\lambda\) with these two values is \(1 - \lambda\).
  2. Likewise \(L_{\mathrm{b}} = \lambda\) on the edge, and \(L_{\mathrm{c}}\) is an affine function of \(\lambda\) with the value \(0\) at \(\lambda = 0\) and \(0\) at \(\lambda = 1\), so it is zero everywhere.
  3. By equation (15.5), therefore, \(u = (1 - \lambda)\,d_{\mathrm{a}} + \lambda\,d_{\mathrm{b}} + 0 \cdot d_{\mathrm{c}}\). The same computation for \(T'\) gives the same expression, because it contains nothing special to \(T\) or \(T'\).
  4. The field is well defined. By definition 15.6 the intersection of two different elements is empty, a common vertex or a common edge. At a common vertex \(P_{\mathrm{k}}\) both elements have the value \(d_{\mathrm{k}}\) by lemma 15.4, claim 2, and on a common edge the same value by step 3. So every point of \(\Omega\) has a single value \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\).
  5. Every element \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a closed set (definition 1.74). Let a sequence of points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} \in T_{\mathrm{j}}\) converge to a point \(Q\). The area coordinates \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the element \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) are affine, hence continuous (corollary 1.69), so \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(Q)\) (lemma 1.68). Since \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge 0\), also \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(Q) \ge 0\) (lemma 1.48), and \(Q \in T_{\mathrm{j}}\) by lemma 15.4, claim 4.
  6. Continuity. Let \(P \in \Omega\) and let \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of points of \(\Omega\) converging to \(P\). We assign the term \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) to the element with the smallest index \(j\) that contains it, which is possible because the union of the elements is \(\Omega\). This splits the sequence into at most \(m\) subsequences. A subsequence of the element \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) with infinitely many terms converges to \(P\), so \(P \in T_{\mathrm{j}}\) by step 5. On \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a polynomial of degree one, hence continuous (corollary 1.69), so \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(P)\) along this subsequence. For \(\eta > 0\) every infinite subsequence has an index from which on \(|u_{\mathrm{h}}(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) - u_{\mathrm{h}}(P)| < \eta\), and every finite one has a last index. Above the largest of these at most \(m\) indices, \(|u_{\mathrm{h}}(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) - u_{\mathrm{h}}(P)| < \eta\) for every \(n\). By lemma 1.68, \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is continuous at \(P\).

Source: ONATE1 §3.8.1 (PDF p. 124) and §3.10.1 (PDF p. 128-129): continuity across element boundaries as the compatibility condition. The proof is not from the sources.

A field that agrees on the common edge is called conforming (skladno), since the elements fit along the edge like pieces. The lemma is proved for a mesh in which every common edge runs from node to node, and the condition in the definition of the mesh is not arbitrary. Let us see what happens if we break it.

Take a figure with the points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\) and the point \(M\) in the middle of the segment \(AB\). Below the segment \(AB\) lies the triangle \(ABC\), above it the triangles \(AMD\) and \(MBD\), which meet along the segment \(MD\). The point \(M\) is a vertex of both upper triangles but not of the lower one: it lies on its edge \(AB\) and is not its vertex, so we call it a hanging node (viseče vozlišče), since it hangs on the edge of a neighbouring element. The intersection of the triangles \(ABC\) and \(AMD\) is the segment \(AM\). It is an edge of the triangle \(AMD\) but only part of the edge \(AB\) of the triangle \(ABC\), so the condition of the mesh definition is not met. Let the value of the field at \(M\) be \(d_{\mathrm{M}} = 1\) and at all other nodes \(0\). Along the edge \(AB\) we draw the axis \(x\) in mm and the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\). The upper triangles give a tent: \(0\) at \(A\), \(1\) at \(M\), \(0\) at \(B\), so \(0{,}5\) at \(x = 10\) mm. The lower triangle does not know the node \(M\), and by step 3 of the proof of lemma 15.7 its field along the edge depends only on the values at \(A\) and \(B\), that is, it is \(0\) along the whole edge. The difference, the jump, is \(0{,}5\) at \(x = 10\) mm and at \(M\) it is \(1\). The field is discontinuous here. A body whose displacements had a jump across a segment would open or overlap there, which matter does not do. So the mesh must satisfy the condition on edges. The consequences of a jump for the energy are shown in section 15.2.

15.1.5 The gradient is constant on an element

The field on a triangle is affine, so its gradient is constant. For the bar the strain \(\varepsilon = u_{\mathrm{h}}'\) was constant on the element (lemma 7.5). Here there are two derivatives, in \(x\) and in \(y\). The gradient (gradient) of a field \(u\) is the vector of partial derivatives \((\partial u/\partial x,\ \partial u/\partial y)\). For temperature the gradient is the direction in which the temperature rises fastest, and the rate of rise.

Lemma 15.8 Constant gradient of the linear triangle (stalen gradient linearnega trikotnika)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the field of a linear triangle with nodal values \(d_1, d_2, d_3\) (definition 15.5) and \(a_{\mathrm{i}}, b_{\mathrm{i}}, c_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(A\) as in lemma 15.4. Then:

  1. The gradient of the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) at every point of the element is
    \[\begin{pmatrix} \partial u_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial x \\ \partial u_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat B = \frac{1}{2A}\begin{pmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{15.6}\]
    the \(2 \times 3\) matrix \(\mat B\) does not depend on \(x\) and \(y\).
  2. If \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = \alpha + \beta x_{\mathrm{i}} + \gamma y_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the values of an affine function at the nodes, then \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\) on the whole element, and the gradient is \((\beta, \gamma)\). The linear triangle therefore reproduces every affine field.
  3. For \(d_1 = d_2 = d_3 = c\), \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = c\) and the gradient is zero.
Proof

We must show three claims, the first by differentiating the formula for \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\), the second and third with the lemma on area coordinates.

  1. By lemma 15.4, claim 1, \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = b_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\). Differentiating equation (15.5) by \(x\) and \(y\), where the \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) are numbers, gives \(\partial u_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial x = \sum b_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial y = \sum c_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\), which is the product \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. By equation (15.5) and lemma 15.4, claim 3, \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum L_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\alpha + \beta x_{\mathrm{i}} + \gamma y_{\mathrm{i}}) = \alpha \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} + \beta \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} x_{\mathrm{i}} + \gamma \sum L_{\mathrm{i}} y_{\mathrm{i}} = \alpha + \beta x + \gamma y\).
  3. It is a special case of claim 2 with \(\beta = \gamma = 0\).

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.1 and (4.32) (PDF p. 158). COOK §7.3 (PDF p. 279). Claim 2 is the condition of "constant strain" (PDF p. 155, 158), written here for a scalar. The proof is not from the sources.

On the same triangle, with temperature values \(T = 20 + 0{,}5\,x - 0{,}2\,y\) in \(^\circ\)C (\(x\), \(y\) in mm) at the nodes, we have \(T_1 = 20\), \(T_2 = 40\), \(T_3 = 19\). By lemma 15.8,

\[\begin{pmatrix} \partial T_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial x \\ \partial T_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial y \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{1200\ \text{mm}} \begin{pmatrix} -30 & 30 & 0 \\ -30 & -10 & 40 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 20 \\ 40 \\ 19 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}5 \\ -0{,}2 \end{pmatrix}\ \tfrac{\text{K}}{\text{mm}} ,\]

exactly the gradient of the function from which we took the values. At the point \((20, 10)\) mm, \(T_{\mathrm{h}} = 28\,^\circ\text{C}\), which is also \(20 + 10 - 2\). The triangle therefore represents every affine field exactly.

And what about a field that is not affine? Take \(u = x^2\) (in mm²), which has the values \(0\), \(1600\), \(100\) at the nodes. From them the triangle builds a field that has at the centroid \((50/3, 10)\) mm the value \((0 + 1600 + 100)/3 = 566{,}7\), while the true field has \((50/3)^2 = 277{,}8\). The gradient of the triangle is \((40, -10)\), the true gradient at the centroid is \((33{,}3, 0)\). On the edge from \((0, 0)\) to \((40, 0)\) mm, \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = 40\,x\) and \(u = x^2\), and at the middle they are \(800\) and \(400\), a difference of \(400 = h^2/4\) with edge length \(h = 40\) mm, as for the hanging bar in chapter 6. The triangle is therefore exact only for affine fields, and for the field \(x^2\) the difference on the edge falls with the square of the edge length.

At \(h = 40\) mm the difference is large because \(x^2\) is far from a straight line over such a long edge. For an edge with \(h = 10\) mm the difference at the middle would be \(h^2/4 = 25\), sixteen times less. This is why we refine meshes.

For temperature and for displacements we shall also need integrals over the element, namely of products of shape functions. We want to compute them without a new integration for each triangle.

Lemma 15.9 Integral of powers of area coordinates (integral potenc ploskovnih koordinat)

Let \(T\) be a triangle of area \(A\) with area coordinates \(L_1, L_2, L_3\) (definition 15.3), and \(a, b, c\) non-negative integers. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1^a\,L_2^b\,L_3^c\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{a!\,b!\,c!}{(a + b + c + 2)!}\;2A . \tag{15.7}\]
Proof

We must show that an affine map carries the integral over the triangle to the reference triangle, where we compute it with two iterated integrals.

  1. Let \(\Lambda(s, t) = P_1 + s\,\vek u + t\,\vek v\) and \(T_0\) as in the proof of lemma 15.2. The map \(\Lambda\) is continuously differentiable and injective, because its matrix is invertible, and its Jacobian determinant is constant, \(2\Delta = 2A \neq 0\). By the change of variables theorem 4.59, for the plane, \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = 2A \int_{\mathrm{T}_0} f(\Lambda(s, t))\,\mathrm{d}A\) for a continuous function \(f\).
  2. Each of the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is affine, so \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(\Lambda(s, t)) = L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_1) + s\,(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_2) - L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_1)) + t\,(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_3) - L_{\mathrm{i}}(P_1))\). By lemma 15.4, claim 2, these are \(L_1 = 1 - s - t\), \(L_2 = s\), \(L_3 = t\).
  3. We compute the integral over \(T_0\) as an iterated integral, first over \(s\) from \(0\) to \(1 - t\) and then over \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\), by theorem 4.33 as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 15.2. The auxiliary formula for \(h > 0\): \(\int_0^h (h - s)^a s^b\,\mathrm{d}s = \dfrac{a!\,b!}{(a + b + 1)!}\,h^{a+b+1}\). For \(b = 0\) the integral is \(h^{a+1}/(a + 1)\), since \(-(h - s)^{a+1}/(a + 1)\) is a primitive function (theorem 3.40). For \(b \ge 1\), by the product rule (lemma 1.81) the derivative of the function \((h - s)^{a+1} s^b\) is \(-(a + 1)(h - s)^a s^b + b\,(h - s)^{a+1} s^{b-1}\). Its integral from \(0\) to \(h\) is zero by theorem 3.40, since the function is zero at both ends. So \((a + 1) \int_0^h (h - s)^a s^b\,\mathrm{d}s = b \int_0^h (h - s)^{a+1} s^{b-1}\,\mathrm{d}s\), and induction on \(b\) gives the formula.
  4. By step 3 with \(h = 1 - t\) the inner integral is \(\int_0^{1-t} (1 - t - s)^a s^b\,\mathrm{d}s = \dfrac{a!\,b!}{(a + b + 1)!}\,(1 - t)^{a+b+1}\). The outer integral is \(\int_0^1 (1 - t)^{a+b+1} t^c\,\mathrm{d}t = \dfrac{(a + b + 1)!\,c!}{(a + b + c + 2)!}\) by step 3 with \(h = 1\) and the replacement \(a \to a + b + 1\), \(b \to c\).
  5. We multiply and get \(\dfrac{a!\,b!\,c!}{(a + b + c + 2)!}\) for the integral over \(T_0\), then by step 1 multiply by \(2A\).

Source: COOK (7.3-7) (PDF p. 280), ONATE1 (5.47) (PDF p. 203). The proof is not from the sources.

For the triangle of the example, \(A = 600\) mm², the lemma gives \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{1}{3!}\,1200 = 200\) mm², \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{2}{4!}\,1200 = 100\) mm², \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1 L_2\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{1}{4!}\,1200 = 50\) mm² and \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1 L_2 L_3\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{1}{5!}\,1200 = 10\) mm². The case \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_1 L_2\,\mathrm{d}A\) we also checked with a direct iterated integral over \(x\) and \(y\) and got the same, \(50\) mm² (num-01.out). The first expression is a third of the area, which makes sense: on a triangle \(L_1\) has the mean \(\tfrac13\), because the formula of the lemma does not distinguish the indices, so all three mean values are equal, and their sum is 1. The products \(L_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{j}}\) give \(A/6\) for \(i = j\) and \(A/12\) for \(i \neq j\), which we shall use for loads and masses of the element.

The lemma needs straight edges, since the Jacobian determinant of the map \(\Lambda\) is constant only for an affine map. For an element with a curved edge the determinant is not constant and the formulas do not hold, which is the subject of section 15.5.

In this section we described the triangle with vertices and area coordinates, gave it a field with three numbers, checked that neighbouring triangles give a continuous field if they meet along edges, and computed the gradient and the integrals. From this we shall derive, in section 15.2, the energy of a body as a sum of energies of elements, and in section 15.3 the conduction matrix of the triangle and the first computation.

Notation. \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) with an index are area coordinates, \(L\) without an index is a length elsewhere. \(\mat B\) is the matrix of derivatives of the shape functions, for the bar the same role was played by the row \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 7.5). \(A\) here is the area of the element, for the bar \(A\) was the area of the cross-section. The signed area \(\Delta\) is the area with a definite sign. In a change of variables \(J\) is the Jacobian determinant (theorem 4.59). We write the thickness of the plate \(t\), except in chapter 14, where \(t\) means time, but time does not appear in this chapter.
