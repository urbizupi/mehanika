Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.6 The Rayleigh quotient

The spectral theorem (theorem 10.13) tells us that every system with \(n\) degrees of freedom has \(n\) modes of vibration and \(n\) natural frequencies. It does not tell us how to find them when \(n\) is large. We solved the frame with two storeys with a \(2 \times 2\) determinant (section 10.2). A bar cut into 16 finite elements (chapter 7) has 16 coordinates, and we do not compute determinants of order 16 by hand. In the next section we will see that the mass matrix of such a system is assembled in the same way as the stiffness matrix, and we leave the solution of the eigenvalue problem to a computer. Often, however, we do not need all modes but only the lowest frequency, for example because we want to compare it with the frequency of a load. So we ask: can a guessed shape of the motion tell us the lowest frequency, and how good is such an estimate?

Take frame A with two storeys, which we met in section 10.1: rigid floors of mass \(m = 500\) kg, each storey has stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, the coordinates are the floor displacements \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and \(\mat K = k\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Let us guess that both floors move equally, \(\vek v = (1, 1)\). Imagine that a constraint imposes this shape, so \(\vek q(t) = a(t)\,\vek v\) with a single coordinate \(a\). By section 10.1 the kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q} = \tfrac12\,(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v)\,\dot a^2\) and the potential energy is \(V = \tfrac12\,\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q = \tfrac12\,(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v)\,a^2\). This is a system with one degree of freedom (theorem 9.3) with mass \(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v = 1000\) kg and stiffness \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v = k\). For \(\vek v = (1, 1)\) only the first storey is stretched, because the second floor does not move relative to the first, so the stiffness equals the stiffness of one storey. By theorem 9.43 its circular frequency is \(\omega^2 = k/1000\ \text{kg} = 501{,}76\ \text{1/s}^2\), so \(\omega = 22{,}40\) 1/s. The true lowest frequency of the frame is \(\omega_1 = 19{,}578\) 1/s, so the estimate is \(14{,}41\) % too high. The high estimate is no accident: by imposing the shape we made the system stiffer, because we took away its freedom to move the second floor differently from the first, and a constraint does not lower the frequencies. Theorem 10.26 will state this for every system and every shape.

We compute the same for any shape \(\vek v\) and any system. The ratio of the energies at the shape \(\vek v\) is the number we need.

Definition 10.25 Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric matrix and \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\) (definition 6.27). For a column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) elements the Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik) is the number

\[R(\vek v) = \frac{\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v}{\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v} . \tag{10.29}\]

The function \(R\) assigns one real number to every nonzero column. The denominator is positive because \(\mat M\) is positive definite.

Source: CK (10.40) (PDF p. 316), GR equation (2.200) (PDF p. 130), where GR introduces it for an eigenmode as the »Rayleigh quotient«. The name as in chapter 6 and in the section on buckling in chapter 8.

In buckling we wrote a quotient with the same name with integrals (equation (8.126)), here it is written with two matrices. The column \(\vek R\) of chapter 8 is the remainder of a linearization, so a different object, while \(R(\vek v)\) is a number. For the column \(c\,\vek v\) with \(c \ne 0\) we have \(R(c\,\vek v) = c^2\,\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v/(c^2\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v) = R(\vek v)\): the quotient does not depend on the scale of the column, only on its shape. This is as it should be, since a mode of vibration is known only up to multiplication by a number. If \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a mode with \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), we multiply the equation by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\) and obtain \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), so

\[R(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} . \tag{10.30}\]

At a mode the quotient equals the eigenvalue. For other shapes we want to know where it lies among the eigenvalues. For \(\vek v = (1, 1)\) in frame A we have \(R = 501{,}76\ \text{1/s}^2\), between \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}31\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\ \text{1/s}^2\). Let us show that it always lies like this.

Theorem 10.26 Bounds of the Rayleigh quotient (meje Rayleighovega količnika)

Let \(\mat K\) be symmetric and \(\mat M\) symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) and \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) modes with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) otherwise (theorem 10.13). For every column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) elements and coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v\)

\[R(\vek v) = \frac{\lambda_1 c_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_{\mathrm{n}} c_{\mathrm{n}}^2}{c_1^2 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}}^2} , \qquad \lambda_1 \le R(\vek v) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}} . \tag{10.31}\]

The equality \(R(\vek v) = \lambda_1\) holds exactly when \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda_1\mat M\vek v\), and the equality \(R(\vek v) = \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) exactly when \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\mat M\vek v\).

Proof

We write the quotient with the expansion in modes, which is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues, and read off both bounds and the cases of equality.

  1. By corollary 10.14, \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v\). Since \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), at least one \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) is nonzero, since otherwise \(\vek v = \vek 0\).
  2. The denominator is \(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), because by theorem 10.13 all terms with \(r \ne s\) are zero and those with \(r = s\) equal \(c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\).
  3. The numerator is \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\). Because \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) we have \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), so the numerator is \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). Steps 2 and 3 prove the first part of (10.31).
  4. Write \(S = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2 > 0\). By step 3, \(R(\vek v) - \lambda_1 = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_1)\,c_{\mathrm{r}}^2/S\). Every term is nonnegative because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_1\). Hence \(R(\vek v) \ge \lambda_1\). In the same way \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} - R(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}})\,c_{\mathrm{r}}^2/S \ge 0\).
  5. The equality \(R(\vek v) = \lambda_1\) means that every term in step 4 is zero, so \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(r\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > \lambda_1\). Then \(\vek v\) is a combination of modes with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_1\) and \(\mat K\vek v = \sum c_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_1\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_1\mat M\vek v\). Conversely, from \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda_1\mat M\vek v\) multiplication by \(\vek v^\T\) gives \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v = \lambda_1\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v\), so \(R(\vek v) = \lambda_1\). The same holds for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Source: CK (10.40)-(10.45) (PDF p. 316): expansion in modes and the bounds, there for \(\omega_1 \ne 0\). The form of the proof with the weighted mean and the cases of equality are ours.

We have proved that \(R(\vek v)\) is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues, with weights \(c_{\mathrm{r}}^2/S\) that are nonnegative and add up to \(1\), so it lies between the smallest and the largest. This keeps the promise of chapter 1: the eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) is the smallest and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the largest value of the quotient, and it takes them at the modes \(\vek\varphi_1\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\). For a system with \(\lambda_1 \ge 0\) we therefore have \(\sqrt{R(\vek v)} \ge \omega_1\): every guessed shape estimates the lowest frequency from above. For \(n = 2\) we already had this: point 3 of theorem 8.125 says that \(K_{11}/M_{11}\) and \(K_{22}/M_{22}\) lie between \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\). Here this is a special case, since the column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) with a one in place \(i\) and zeros elsewhere gives \(R(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}) = K_{\mathrm{ii}}/M_{\mathrm{ii}}\). In frame A these are \(2007{,}04\) and \(1003{,}52\ \text{1/s}^2\).

10.6.1 How good is a guessed shape

Let us try the quotient on frame A with two more shapes (figure 10.17). With \(\vek v = (1, 2)\) the second floor is displaced twice as far as the first. This is the shape the frame takes under a static force at the top: the flexibility matrix is \(\mat D = \mat K^{-1} = \frac1k\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (lemma 10.3), and the force \((0, F)\) gives the displacements \((F/k, 2F/k)\). The computation by (10.29) gives \(R = 401{,}41\ \text{1/s}^2\), so \(\omega = 20{,}035\) 1/s, \(2{,}333\) % above \(\omega_1\). We find the third shape as follows. In vibration in the mode \(\vek v\) with frequency \(\omega\) the floors carry the inertial forces \(\omega^2\mat M\vek v\), their static displacement is \(\omega^2\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek v\), and \(\mat K\vek v = \omega^2\mat M\vek v\) means exactly that this displacement equals \(\vek v\). For a true mode, therefore, \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek v\) is parallel to \(\vek v\). For a guessed shape it is not, but it is the static displacement under the inertial forces of that shape, so we take it as the new shape. For \(\vek v = (1, 1)\) the forces \(\mat M\vek v\) on the two floors are equal, as if the weight of each floor acted horizontally, and \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek v = (m/k)\,(2, 3)\), which is the shape \((1, 1{,}5)\). The quotient of this shape is \(R = 385{,}97\ \text{1/s}^2\), so \(\omega = 19{,}646\) 1/s, only \(0{,}346\) % above \(\omega_1\).

The errors are \(14{,}41\) %, \(2{,}333\) % and \(0{,}346\) % for shapes that come ever closer to the first mode. The error seems to fall with the difference between the shape and the mode, but how fast? Let us compare one more guessed shape with an exactly known error: the first mode with a small addition of the second, \(\vek v = \vek\varphi_1 + \varepsilon\,\vek\varphi_2\). At \(\varepsilon = 0{,}1\) we get \(R - \lambda_1 = 22{,}217\ \text{1/s}^2\), and at the ten times smaller \(\varepsilon = 0{,}01\) we get \(R - \lambda_1 = 0{,}22437\ \text{1/s}^2\). When the shape is ten times closer to the mode, the error of the eigenvalue is a hundred times smaller: \(22{,}217\) is \(5{,}80\) % of \(\lambda_1\) and \(0{,}22437\) only \(0{,}0585\) %, in frequency \(2{,}86\) % and \(0{,}0292\) %. The next theorem says why.

Theorem 10.27 Stationarity of the Rayleigh quotient (stacionarnost Rayleighovega količnika)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as in theorem 10.26.

  1. Let \(\vek v = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}e_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), where the \(e_{\mathrm{s}}\) are numbers. Then
    \[R(\vek v) - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{\sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}})\,e_{\mathrm{s}}^2}{1 + \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2} . \tag{10.32}\]
    With \(\varepsilon^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and \(\Delta = \max_{\mathrm{s}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}|\) we therefore have \(|R(\vek v) - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}| \le \Delta\,\varepsilon^2\). Every column \(\vek v\) with \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\vek v \ne 0\) takes this form with \(e_{\mathrm{s}} = c_{\mathrm{s}}/c_{\mathrm{r}}\) after multiplication by the number \(1/c_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  2. The partial derivatives of the quotient with respect to the elements of the column are
    \[\frac{\partial R}{\partial v_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek v) = \frac{2\,\bigl(\mat K\vek v - R(\vek v)\,\mat M\vek v\bigr)_{\mathrm{i}}}{\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v} . \tag{10.33}\]
    They are all zero exactly when \(\mat K\vek v = R(\vek v)\,\mat M\vek v\). The stationary points of the quotient, the points at which all partial derivatives are zero (as in corollary 8.58), are therefore exactly the eigencolumns, and the value of the quotient at them is the eigenvalue.
  3. If there are \(s\) and \(t\) with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{t}}\), then \(R - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) takes both signs at arbitrarily small deviations from \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(R\) has neither its smallest nor its largest value at \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\).
Proof

We obtain point 1 from the expansion (10.31), point 2 with the quotient rule, and point 3 from the signs of the terms in point 1.

  1. In (10.31), \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) and \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = e_{\mathrm{s}}\) for \(s \ne r\) (the coefficients are \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\vek v\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{t}} = \delta_{\mathrm{st}}\)). Hence \(R(\vek v) = \bigl(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\bigr)/\bigl(1 + \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ne \mathrm{r}}e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\bigr)\). We subtract \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), written as \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\bigl(1 + \sum e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\bigr)/\bigl(1 + \sum e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\bigr)\), and obtain (10.32).
  2. The estimate: the absolute value of each term in the numerator is at most \(\Delta\,e_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and the denominator is \(\ge 1\), so \(|R - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}| \le \Delta\sum e_{\mathrm{s}}^2 = \Delta\,\varepsilon^2\). If \(c_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\), then because \(R(c\,\vek v) = R(\vek v)\) the quotient at \(\vek v/c_{\mathrm{r}}\) equals the quotient at \(\vek v\), and \(\vek v/c_{\mathrm{r}}\) has the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{s}}/c_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. We compute the derivative with respect to \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) as the derivative of a function of one variable, with the other elements held constant (definition 1.25). The numerator \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} K_{\mathrm{jl}}v_{\mathrm{j}}v_{\mathrm{l}}\) contains \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) only in the terms with \(j = i\) or \(l = i\), so its derivative is \(\sum_{\mathrm{l}} K_{\mathrm{il}}v_{\mathrm{l}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ji}}v_{\mathrm{j}} = 2\,(\mat K\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}}\) by the symmetry \(K_{\mathrm{ji}} = K_{\mathrm{ij}}\). In the same way the denominator has the derivative \(2\,(\mat M\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  4. A column that differs from \(\vek v\) only in element \(i\) is zero for at most one value of that element, and this value is not \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\). On an open interval around \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) without this value the denominator is positive, so by point 3 of lemma 4.108 \(\partial R/\partial v_{\mathrm{i}} = \bigl(2(\mat K\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v - \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v\cdot 2(\mat M\vek v)_{\mathrm{i}}\bigr)/(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v)^2\). Cancelling one \(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v\) gives (10.33).
  5. The derivatives are all zero exactly when \(\mat K\vek v - R(\vek v)\mat M\vek v = \vek 0\), since the denominator is positive. This is the eigenvalue problem with the eigenvalue \(R(\vek v)\). Conversely, if \(\mat K\vek v = \lambda\mat M\vek v\), multiplication by \(\vek v^\T\) gives \(R(\vek v) = \lambda\) and the expression is zero.
  6. Let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{t}}\). Take \(e_{\mathrm{s}} = e\) and all other \(e_{\mathrm{s}'} = 0\). By step 1, \(R - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = (\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}})e^2/(1 + e^2) < 0\) for every \(e \ne 0\). With \(e_{\mathrm{t}} = e\) and the others zero, \(R - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = (\lambda_{\mathrm{t}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{r}})e^2/(1 + e^2) > 0\). The deviation \(e\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) or \(e\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) is arbitrarily small for small \(e\).

Source: GR sections 2.10.2 and 2.10.3 (PDF pp. 130-132), (2.204) and (2.206): the quotient is stationary in the vicinity of an eigenmode, the error is of second order, the expansion of the error in modes. The exact form (10.32), the derivative (10.33) and point 3 are not from the source, GR on PDF p. 131 says only that without restricting the comparison vectors the stationary point cannot be characterized as a smallest or largest value, because the matrix \(\mat K - \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\mat M\) is not positive definite.

The theorem says three things. First, the error of the quotient is the square of the error of the shape, not the error itself (figure 10.18). This explains why the estimate of \(0{,}346\) % for the shape \((1, 1{,}5)\) is so good. Relative to the first mode it contains the share \(e_2 = 0{,}0344\) of the second, the shape \((1, 2)\) contains \(e_2 = -0{,}0902\), which is \(2{,}618\) times more. The error of the quotient \(R - \lambda_1\) is \(2{,}66\ \text{1/s}^2\) against \(18{,}10\ \text{1/s}^2\), which is \(6{,}81\) times more, close to \(2{,}618^2 = 6{,}85\). The denominator \(1 + e_2^2\) in (10.32) keeps it from the exact square. For the shape \((1, 1)\), \(e_2 = 0{,}2361\), \(\Delta\,e_2^2 = 125{,}05\ \text{1/s}^2\), and the denominator \(1{,}056\) reduces it to \(118{,}45\ \text{1/s}^2\), which is \(R - \lambda_1\). Second, the quotient has zero partial derivatives at a mode and nowhere else, so the eigencolumns are exactly the columns at which \(R\) does not change to first order. Third, at the lowest and the highest mode the quotient has its smallest and largest value (theorem 10.26), while at intermediate modes with \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) it has neither: a deviation towards a lower mode lowers the value and a deviation towards a higher mode raises it. In the frame with three storeys of section 10.3 the eigenvalues are \(198{,}76\), \(1560{,}43\) and \(3258{,}41\ \text{1/s}^2\). The second mode with an addition \(e_1 = 0{,}1\) of the first gives \(R - \lambda_2 = -13{,}48\ \text{1/s}^2\), with an addition \(e_3 = 0{,}1\) of the third it gives \(+16{,}81\ \text{1/s}^2\), as (10.32) predicts.

We see the same law in bracket B with a mass at joint \(C\) of section 10.1. Its mass matrix is \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) with \(m = 1000\) kg, and by section 10.1 its stiffness matrix is \(\mat K = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 7{,}4088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\cdot 10^6\) N/m. A displacement of the joint in the direction \(\vek v = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), which makes the angle \(\theta\) with the \(x\) axis, has the quotient \(R(\theta) = \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v/m\). The modes are perpendicular, because \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\), in the directions \(\theta_1 = 73{,}947^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 = \theta_1 + 90^\circ = 163{,}947^\circ\) (section 10.2). Normalized with \(\mat M\) they are \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}/\sqrt m\) with the unit vectors \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}} = (\cos\theta_{\mathrm{r}}, \sin\theta_{\mathrm{r}})\). By the addition formula (corollary 8.15), \(c_1 = \vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek v = \sqrt m\,(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta) = \sqrt m\,\cos(\theta - \theta_1)\), and in the same way \(c_2 = \sqrt m\,\cos(\theta - \theta_1 - 90^\circ) = \sqrt m\,\sin(\theta - \theta_1)\). The sum \(c_1^2 + c_2^2 = m\) is the denominator in (10.31) (lemma 8.10, point 1), so

\[\begin{aligned} R(\theta) &= \lambda_1\cos^2(\theta - \theta_1) \\ &\quad + \lambda_2\sin^2(\theta - \theta_1) \\ &= \lambda_1 + (\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)\sin^2(\theta - \theta_1) . \end{aligned} \tag{10.34}\]

With \(\lambda_1 = 869{,}77\) and \(\lambda_2 = 7950{,}23\ \text{1/s}^2\) the curve in figure 10.19 is lowest at \(\theta_1\) and highest at \(\theta_2\), and at both it has a horizontal tangent. If we miss the direction by \(5^\circ\), \(R = 923{,}56\ \text{1/s}^2\), so \(\omega\) is \(3{,}05\) % too high. At \(10^\circ\) the error is \(11{,}6\) %, at \(20^\circ\) it is \(39{,}7\) %. For a small deviation \(\theta - \theta_1\) in radians, \(\sin(\theta - \theta_1) \approx \theta - \theta_1\) (theorem 8.12, \(\sin'0 = \cos 0 = 1\)), so \(R - \lambda_1 \approx (\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)(\theta - \theta_1)^2\), the square of the deviation of the direction. At \(5^\circ = 0{,}08727\) rad this gives \(7080{,}46 \cdot 0{,}08727^2 = 53{,}92\ \text{1/s}^2\), while the exact value by (10.34) is \(R - \lambda_1 = 7080{,}46 \cdot \sin^2 5^\circ = 53{,}78\ \text{1/s}^2\), only \(0{,}25\) % less.

10.6.2 Second and higher modes, additional constraints

The quotient gave us only \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\). What about \(\lambda_2\) and the others? At a mode \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the quotient has no extremum (theorem 10.27, point 3), so we cannot write such \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) as the smallest or largest value over all columns. But if we pay attention to the columns over which we look for the value, we can. The spectral theorem found them in turn, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) as the smallest value over the columns with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek v = 0\) for \(j < r\). This route needs the earlier modes to be known. We shall show that it is enough to restrict ourselves to columns that satisfy any \(r - 1\) linear conditions \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\), and among all choices of conditions to take the one for which the smallest value is largest. Let us see what this means for \(r = 2\) in frame A, where there is a single condition. The condition \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\) with \(\vek b = (1, 0)\) means that the first floor is at rest. What remains is \(\vek v = (0, v_2)\) and \(R = k/m = 1003{,}52\ \text{1/s}^2\), which is less than \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\). The condition with \(\vek b = (0, 1)\) holds the second floor, \(\vek v = (v_1, 0)\) and \(R = 2k/m = 2007{,}04\), again less than \(\lambda_2\). These are two of infinitely many conditions. The theorem below says that for every condition the smallest \(R\) over the columns that satisfy it is no larger than \(\lambda_2\), and that the choice \(\vek b = \mat M\vek\varphi_1\) reaches the value \(\lambda_2\). So \(\lambda_2\) is not the smallest value of the quotient but the largest of the smallest values over all possible conditions.

For the second form of the theorem we need a notation. For linearly independent columns \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) (definition 6.22), \(L(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\) is the set of all linear combinations \(a_1\vek w_1 + \dots + a_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) with numbers \(a_{\mathrm{i}}\) that are not all zero. Since the columns are independent, every such combination is nonzero and the quotient \(R\) is defined on it.

Theorem 10.28 Courant minimax principle (Courantov izrek o najmanjši največji vrednosti)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as in theorem 10.26 and let \(1 \le r \le n\).

  1. For columns \(\vek b_1, \dots, \vek b_{\mathrm{r}-1}\) with \(n\) elements (for \(r = 1\) there are none) let \(\beta(\vek b_1, \dots, \vek b_{\mathrm{r}-1})\) be the smallest value of \(R(\vek v)\) over the columns \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0\) for all \(j\). This smallest value exists, every choice gives \(\beta \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and for \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\beta = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \max\beta\) over all choices of \(\vek b_1, \dots, \vek b_{\mathrm{r}-1}\).
  2. For linearly independent columns \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) let \(\gamma(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\) be the largest value of \(R\) on the set \(L(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\). This largest value exists, every choice gives \(\gamma \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and for \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\gamma = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \min\gamma\) over all choices of \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\).
Proof

First we show that the smallest and the largest value exist, then four estimates. The estimates use the expansion (10.31) and lemma 10.9, by which a homogeneous system with more unknowns than equations has a nonzero solution.

  1. Existence in point 1. Let \(S\) be the set of columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v = 1\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = 0\) for all \(j\). By lemma 10.12 (with the columns \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(r - 1 < n\) conditions, none for \(r = 1\)) \(S\) is nonempty, closed and bounded. On \(S\), \(R(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v\) is a polynomial, hence a continuous function (lemma 8.24, point 3), and by theorem 8.26 it has a smallest value on \(S\). Every column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with the given conditions gives \(\vek v/\sqrt{\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v}\) in \(S\), because the conditions are homogeneous, and since \(R(c\,\vek v) = R(\vek v)\), this is also the smallest value over all such columns.
  2. Existence in point 2. The columns of \(L(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\) are \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\) with a column of numbers \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\). Let \(\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\) be the matrix of order \(r\) with elements \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}\). It is symmetric because \(\mat M\) is symmetric, and \(\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\vek a = \vek v^\T\mat M\vek v\). For \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) by independence, so \(\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\vek a > 0\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\) is positive definite. By lemma 10.12 (with \(\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\) in place of \(\mat M\), without conditions) the set of columns \(\vek a\) with \(\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\vek a = 1\) is nonempty, closed and bounded. On it, \(R(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}) = (\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek w_{\mathrm{i}})^\T\mat K(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek w_{\mathrm{i}})\) is a polynomial in \(a_1, \dots, a_{\mathrm{r}}\) and by theorem 8.26 it has a largest value. Every \(\vek v\) of \(L\) is a nonzero multiple of a column with \(\vek a^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{w}}\vek a = 1\), so this is the largest value of \(R\) on \(L\).
  3. The estimate \(\beta \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). We look for a column \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) that satisfies all conditions. The conditions \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek b_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, r - 1\) are \(r - 1\) equations for \(r\) unknowns \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\), so a nonzero solution exists (lemma 10.9, for \(r = 1\) we take \(a_1 = 1\)). Then \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), because \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek v\) (multiplication by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\) and \(\mat M\)-orthonormality). By (10.31) with \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = a_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j \le r\) and \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(s > r\), \(R(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}^2/\sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}}a_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(j \le r\). Hence \(\beta \le R(\vek v) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  4. Equality. Let \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j < r\). Since \(\mat M\) is symmetric, \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek v = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek v\) (lemma 1.101). A column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek v = 0\) for \(j < r\) has the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(j < r\) in (10.31), so \(R(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2 \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), because \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s \ge r\). The column \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) satisfies the conditions, since \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), and \(R(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) ((10.30)). Hence \(\beta = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Steps 3 and 4 prove point 1.
  5. The estimate \(\gamma \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). For the columns \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) we look for a combination \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} \ne \vek 0\) that satisfies \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek v = 0\) for \(j < r\). These are \(r - 1\) equations for \(r\) unknowns \(a_{\mathrm{i}}\), so a nonzero solution exists (lemma 10.9, for \(r = 1\) we take \(a_1 = 1\)), and \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) by independence. By step 4, \(R(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), so \(\gamma \ge R(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  6. Equality. The columns \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are independent by point 1 of corollary 10.11, so they are an admissible choice. Every column of \(L(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) has the form \(\sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}}a_{\mathrm{j}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) with numbers \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) that are not all zero, so by the computation in step 3, \(R \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and at \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(R = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Hence \(\gamma = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Steps 5 and 6 prove point 2.

Source: GR section 2.10.5, (2.217) (PDF p. 134), where Courant's theorem is stated as point 1, with the proof on PDF pp. 134-135. Point 2 (the smallest largest value over sets) and the form of the proofs with lemma 10.9 are not from the source.

The two points describe the same number \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) from two sides. In the first we ask which conditions to add so that the smallest value is as large as possible, in the second which \(r\) columns to choose so that the largest value on their combinations is as small as possible. The best conditions are \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j < r\), and the best columns are \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). For \(r = 1\) there is no condition and point 1 says \(\lambda_1 = \min R\), as theorem 10.26 does.

What does an additional condition mean physically? A support that holds one coordinate, or a rigid link that makes two coordinates equal. After such a constraint the system has \(n - 1\) coordinates and its own natural frequencies. What do they tell us about the original ones?

Corollary 10.29 Rayleigh's theorem on constraints (Rayleighov izrek o vezeh)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as in theorem 10.26, \(n \ge 2\) and \(\vek b \ne \vek 0\) a column with \(n\) elements. Let \(p\) be an index with \(b_{\mathrm{p}} \ne 0\) and \(\mat G\) the matrix with \(n\) rows and \(n - 1\) columns that assigns to every column \(\vek u\) with \(n - 1\) elements \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(i \ne p\), the column \(\vek v = \mat G\vek u\) with \(v_{\mathrm{i}} = u_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i \ne p\) and \(v_{\mathrm{p}} = -\frac{1}{b_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i} \ne \mathrm{p}}b_{\mathrm{i}}u_{\mathrm{i}}\), hence with \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\). Let \(\mat K' = \mat G^\T\mat K\mat G\) and \(\mat M' = \mat G^\T\mat M\mat G\), and let \(\lambda'_1 \le \dots \le \lambda'_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) be the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\). Then

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}+1} \qquad \text{for } r = 1, \dots, n - 1 . \tag{10.35}\]
Proof

The matrices \(\mat K'\) and \(\mat M'\) are the stiffness and mass matrices of the system with the constraint \(\vek b^\T\vek q = 0\), since the energies \(\tfrac12\vek q^\T\mat K\vek q\) and \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) at \(\vek q = \mat G\vek u\) equal \(\tfrac12\vek u^\T\mat K'\vek u\) and \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek u}^\T\mat M'\dot{\vek u}\). The quotients of the two systems agree, so we bound \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) from both sides with theorem 10.28.

  1. By lemma 1.101, \((\mat G^\T\mat K\mat G)^\T = \mat G^\T\mat K^\T\mat G = \mat G^\T\mat K\mat G\), so \(\mat K'\) is symmetric, and likewise \(\mat M'\). By the same lemma, \(\vek u^\T\mat M'\vek u = (\mat G\vek u)^\T\mat M(\mat G\vek u) > 0\) if \(\mat G\vek u \ne \vek 0\), and this holds for \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\), because the elements of \(\vek u\) are among the elements of \(\mat G\vek u\). The matrix \(\mat M'\) is therefore positive definite. By theorem 10.13 the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda'_1 \le \dots \le \lambda'_{\mathrm{n}-1}\). The quotient of the pair is \(R'(\vek u) = R(\mat G\vek u)\). Independent columns \(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\) go over into independent columns \(\mat G\vek u_1, \dots, \mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\): from \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat G\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) it follows for the same reason that \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\), and hence all \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). The set \(L(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}})\) goes over into \(L(\mat G\vek u_1, \dots, \mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{r}})\), because \(\mat G\sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. The lower bound. By point 2 of theorem 10.28 for the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\), \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of \(\gamma'\) over independent \(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\). By step 1, \(\gamma'(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}) = \gamma(\mat G\vek u_1, \dots, \mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) by point 2 of theorem 10.28 for the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). Hence \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. The upper bound. Since \(r \le n - 1\), we have \(r + 1 \le n\). We look for \(r\) independent columns of \(L(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}+1})\) that satisfy \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\). For \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{i} \le \mathrm{r}+1}a_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) the condition is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}}a_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) with \(d_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek b^\T\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), one equation for \(r + 1\) unknowns. If all \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) are zero, we take for \(j = 1, \dots, r\) the solution \(\vek a^{(j)}\) with \(a^{(j)}_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) and all other elements zero. Otherwise \(d_{\mathrm{i}_0} \ne 0\) for some \(i_0\), and for each of the \(r\) indices \(j \ne i_0\) we take the solution \(\vek a^{(j)}\) with \(a^{(j)}_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\), \(a^{(j)}_{\mathrm{i}_0} = -d_{\mathrm{j}}/d_{\mathrm{i}_0}\) and all other elements zero. The solutions are independent, because \(\vek a^{(j)}\) has a one in place \(j\) and all the others have zero there. The columns \(\vek v^{(j)} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a^{(j)}_{\mathrm{i}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are independent too: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v^{(j)} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\bigl(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}a^{(j)}_{\mathrm{i}}\bigr)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\) it follows by point 1 of corollary 10.11 that \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek a^{(j)} = \vek 0\), and hence all \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\).
  4. The columns \(\vek v^{(j)}\) satisfy \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\), so \(v^{(j)}_{\mathrm{p}} = -\frac{1}{b_{\mathrm{p}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i} \ne \mathrm{p}}b_{\mathrm{i}}v^{(j)}_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek v^{(j)} = \mat G\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the column of the elements of \(\vek v^{(j)}\) with indices \(i \ne p\). The columns \(\vek u_{\mathrm{j}}\) are independent, because from \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) it follows that \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v^{(j)} = \mat G\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek u_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) and by step 3 all \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). The nonzero combinations of the columns \(\vek v^{(j)}\) lie in \(L(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}+1})\), where by step 3 of the proof of theorem 10.28 (with \(r + 1\) in place of \(r\)), \(R \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}+1}\). By step 1, \(\gamma'(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}})\) is the largest value of \(R\) on \(L(\vek v^{(1)}, \dots, \vek v^{(r)})\), so \(\gamma'(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}+1}\), and by point 2 of theorem 10.28 for the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\), \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \gamma'(\vek u_1, \dots, \vek u_{\mathrm{r}}) \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}+1}\).

Source: GR section 2.10.6, (2.230)-(2.240) (PDF pp. 135-136): Rayleigh's theorem on constraints from Courant's theorem, with \(m\) constraints. Here it is proved for one constraint directly from point 2 of theorem 10.28, a different proof from the source's.

The corollary says that the eigenvalues of the constrained system interlace with the original ones, as in figure 10.20. In frame A with the second floor at rest, \(\lambda' = 2k/m = 2007{,}04\ \text{1/s}^2\), between \(\lambda_1 = 383{,}31\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2627{,}25\). With the first floor at rest, \(\lambda' = k/m = 1003{,}52\), also in between. In the frame with three storeys of section 10.3, a constraint on the third floor gives a frame with two storeys, \(\mat K' = k\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\), \(\mat M' = m\,\mat I\), with the eigenvalues \(1\) and \(3\) in units of \(k/m\). The original ones are \(0{,}198\), \(1{,}555\) and \(3{,}247\), so \(0{,}198 \le 1 \le 1{,}555 \le 3 \le 3{,}247\). In bracket B with the vertical displacement of the joint prevented (\(\vek b = (0, 1)\)), what remains is \(\lambda' = K_{11}/m = 7408{,}8\ \text{1/s}^2\), between \(869{,}77\) and \(7950{,}23\).

One more consequence of the same theorem, for a change of stiffness or mass.

Corollary 10.30 Monotonicity of the eigenvalues (monotonost lastnih vrednosti)

Let \(\mat K\) be symmetric, \(\mat M\) symmetric positive definite, and \(\mat K'\) symmetric and \(\mat M'\) symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) and \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\).

  1. If \(\mat M' = \mat M\) and \(\mat K' - \mat K\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), then \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\): added stiffness lowers no eigenvalue.
  2. If \(\mat K' = \mat K\) is positive semidefinite and \(\mat M' - \mat M\) is positive semidefinite, then \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\): added mass raises no eigenvalue.
Proof

We compare the quotients \(R'\) and \(R\) at every column and use point 2 of theorem 10.28.

  1. In the first case \(\vek v^\T\mat K'\vek v = \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v + \vek v^\T(\mat K' - \mat K)\vek v \ge \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v\) and the denominators are equal, so \(R'(\vek v) \ge R(\vek v)\) for every \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\).
  2. In the second case \(\vek v^\T\mat M'\vek v \ge \vek v^\T\mat M\vek v > 0\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat K\vek v \ge 0\), so \(R'(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat M'\vek v \le \vek v^\T\mat K\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v = R(\vek v)\).
  3. By point 2 of theorem 10.28, \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of \(\gamma'\) over the choices of independent columns, where \(\gamma'(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\) is the largest value of \(R'\) on \(L(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}})\), and likewise \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of \(\gamma\) with \(R\) in place of \(R'\). In the first case, by step 1, every choice gives \(\gamma'(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}) \ge \gamma(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), so the smallest value also satisfies \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). In the second case we take the modes \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\), for which \(\gamma = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). By step 2, \(\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \gamma'(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) \le \gamma(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Source: GR section 2.10.4 (PDF pp. 132-133) states the general comparison principle: additional constraints do not lower the smallest value. The corollary for a change of stiffness and mass and its proof are not from the source.

The condition that \(\mat K\) is positive semidefinite is needed in point 2. For \(n = 1\), \(K = -1\) N/m and \(M = 1\) kg we have \(\lambda = -1\ \text{1/s}^2\), and with twice the mass \(\lambda' = -0{,}5\ \text{1/s}^2\), which is larger: the added mass brings the negative eigenvalue closer to zero.

Let us look at frame A. We add a spring of stiffness \(k\) between the first floor and the ground, so \(\mat K' = \mat K + k\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\), where the added matrix is positive semidefinite. The eigenvalues rise from \(383{,}31\) and \(2627{,}25\) to \(587{,}85\) and \(3426{,}23\ \text{1/s}^2\), as point 1 predicts. Each of the four columns of the frame (steel, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, section \(20 \times 40\) mm, length \(1\) m) has the mass \(6{,}24\) kg, which we have neglected so far, so \(12{,}48\) kg per storey. If we assign all of it to the floors, \(\mat M' = 512{,}48\,\mat I\) kg, the difference \(\mat M' - \mat M = 12{,}48\,\mat I\) kg is positive semidefinite, and so is \(\mat K' = \mat K\), so by point 2 no eigenvalue rises. Since \(\mat M' = (512{,}48/500)\,\mat M\), every eigenvalue is multiplied by \(500/512{,}48 = 0{,}9756\) (\(373{,}98\) and \(2563{,}27\ \text{1/s}^2\)), and every natural frequency falls by \(1{,}225\) %. This is only an estimate, because the mass of a column is spread along the column and not at the floors, and the matrices for this are in chapter 12. In bracket B the bars AC and BC have the total mass \(7{,}02\) kg. If each bar adds a third of its mass to joint \(C\) (the next section shows the reason for the third, here it is an estimate), that is \(2{,}34\) kg, the mass at the joint rises to \(1002{,}34\) kg and both natural frequencies fall by \(0{,}117\) %, as point 2 predicts.

This section has shown three things, all from the expansion in modes. The quotient is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues, so it lies between the lowest and the highest. It estimates an eigenvalue with an error of second order, because the shares of the other modes enter the expansion squared. Courant's theorem, proved with the lemma on a system with more unknowns than equations, describes every \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) by a search over sets of columns, and from it follow the interlacing under an additional constraint and the monotonicity under added stiffness or mass. When we guess several shapes at once and build from them an estimate of several eigenvalues together, we obtain the Ritz method in matrix form. In the section after the next we will prove from point 2 of theorem 10.28 that every such estimate is an upper bound of the true value with the same index. The example that will need it is the bar with 16 elements.
