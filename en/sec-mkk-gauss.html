Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.6 Gauss rules for elements and spurious modes (Gaussova pravila za elemente in lažni načini)

For the four-node element of section 15.5 take a concrete example: a steel quadrilateral (figure 15.55) with nodes \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\), \((36, 30)\) and \((4, 24)\) mm, thickness \(t = 10\) mm, with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) in plane stress (theorem 11.37). We have to compute the stiffness matrix of this element as an integral, and here we meet a difficulty that the bar did not have.

The map of the square \([-1, 1]^2\) onto the quadrilateral (definition 15.31) has, by lemma 15.32, a determinant \(J\) that is linear in \(\xi\) and \(\eta\). For our quadrilateral \(J = 243 + 30\,\xi - 27\,\eta\) mm², which gives \(240\), \(300\), \(246\) and \(186\) mm² at the corners (num-06.out, part H), as figure 15.55 shows: the smallest is at corner \(4\) (\(186\)), the largest at corner \(2\) (\(300\)), and the ratio of the largest to the smallest value is about \(1{,}6\). Since \(J > 0\) everywhere, the quadrilateral is a curved box in the plane (theorem 15.33), and by theorem 4.59

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = t \int_{[-1,1]^2} \mat B^\T\,\mat C\,\mat B\,J\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{15.35}\]

Every component of the matrix \(\mat B\) (definition 15.21) is a derivative of a shape function with respect to \(x\) or \(y\). By the chain rule it is a combination of the derivatives with respect to \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) with the inverse of the \(2 \times 2\) matrix of partial derivatives of the map, and this inverse is the adjugate matrix divided by \(J\). The integrand in equation (15.35) therefore has the form \((\text{polynomial})/J\), not a polynomial. A word on notation: \(\mat B\) is here the bold matrix of the element, whereas \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) of chapter 7 is the row of the bar, \(\mat C\) is the material matrix of definition 3.27 (ONATE1 and COOK write it \(\mat D\) and \(\mat E\)), \(t\) is the thickness (not time), \(A\) the area of the element (for the bar a cross-section), and \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is always an area coordinate with an index (a length \(L\) elsewhere in the book has none).

For the bar we replaced such integrals by a weighted sum which, for some points \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\), has the form \(\sum w_{\mathrm{i}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\) (definition 7.24). We obtained the points for \(q = 1, 2, 3\) by demanding exactness for the powers \(1, \xi, \dots, \xi^{2q-1}\), and lemma 7.25 checked it. For larger \(q\) chapter 7 only stated that the rule exists (ONATE1, PDF p. 113). Here we prove it, because we need the statement for every \(q\): the number of points decides how much work every element costs, and whether the element stands firmly at all, as we shall see with spurious modes.

The idea of the proof in words. On the axis \(\xi\) we have \(q\) points and \(q\) weights, so \(2q\) free numbers, and with them we would like to hit the polynomials up to degree \(2q - 1\). We divide a polynomial of degree \(2q - 1\) by a polynomial \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) of degree \(q\) whose roots are exactly the chosen points. The quotient and the remainder have degree at most \(q - 1\). The remainder is integrated exactly by every rule with \(q\) points and the right weights. The product of \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) with the quotient, however, is zero at the chosen points, so the rule gives zero for it, and its integral must be zero too. This is true if \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) is orthogonal to all polynomials of lower degree. So the points are the roots of such a polynomial. What "orthogonal" means we say for functions on \([-1, 1]\) with the inner product \((f, g) = \int_{-1}^{1} f\,g\,\mathrm{d}\xi\) (definition 6.16): \(f\) and \(g\) are orthogonal if \((f, g) = 0\).

Theorem 15.42 Gauss quadrature with \(q\) points (Gaussovo pravilo s \(q\) točkami)

Let \(q\) be a natural number and \(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{n}}\) the set of polynomials of one variable of degree at most \(n\) (definition 1.64).

  1. There is exactly one polynomial \(p_{\mathrm{q}}(\xi) = \xi^q + c_{\mathrm{q}-1}\,\xi^{q-1} + \dots + c_0\) for which \(\int_{-1}^{1} p_{\mathrm{q}}\,s\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0\) for every \(s \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\).
  2. The polynomial \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) on the horizontal axis has \(q\) distinct real roots \(\xi_1 < \dots < \xi_{\mathrm{q}}\) and all of them lie in the open interval \((-1, 1)\).
  3. Let \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi) = \prod_{\mathrm{j} \ne \mathrm{i}} \dfrac{\xi - \xi_{\mathrm{j}}}{\xi_{\mathrm{i}} - \xi_{\mathrm{j}}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = \int_{-1}^{1} \ell_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\). Then \(\int_{-1}^{1} g\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{q} w_{\mathrm{i}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every \(g \in \mathcal{P}_{2\mathrm{q}-1}\), and all weights are positive.
  4. No rule with \(q\) points \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and arbitrary weights \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) is exact for all polynomials of degree \(2q\). For the points \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) and weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\) of item 3 the polynomial \(p_{\mathrm{q}}^{\,2}\) fails.
Proof

We have to show four claims. First we find \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) as the solution of a linear system, then the roots by contradiction, then we divide a polynomial, and last we find a polynomial for which the rule fails.

  1. The inner product \((f, g) = \int_{-1}^{1} f\,g\,\mathrm{d}\xi\) has properties 1 to 4 of definition 6.16. The proof is the same as for the interval \([0, L]\) in chapter 6, only the interval differs. The functions \(1, \xi, \dots, \xi^{q-1}\) are linearly independent by lemma 6.23 (the proof with derivatives at \(0\) holds on \([-1, 1]\) too). The matrix \(G_{\mathrm{kj}} = \int_{-1}^{1} \xi^{k+j}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\) with \(k, j = 0, \dots, q-1\) is therefore positive definite by lemma 6.28, item 3, so it has an inverse.
  2. The condition of item 1 for \(s = \xi^k\) reads \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} G_{\mathrm{kj}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = -\int_{-1}^{1} \xi^{k+q}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\) for \(k = 0, \dots, q-1\). Since the integral is linear (lemma 3.54, item 1), it suffices to check the condition for these powers, because every \(s \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\) is a linear combination of them. A system with an invertible matrix has exactly one solution \(c_0, \dots, c_{\mathrm{q}-1}\), which is item 1.
  3. An auxiliary fact: if \(p(c) = 0\), then \(p(\xi) = (\xi - c)\,h(\xi)\) with a polynomial \(h\) of degree one lower. The reason is \(\xi^k - c^k = (\xi - c)(\xi^{k-1} + c\,\xi^{k-2} + \dots + c^{k-1})\), which we use on every term of \(p(\xi) - p(c)\). A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) therefore has at most \(d\) roots, since every root lowers the degree by one. So \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) has finitely many roots.
  4. Item 2. Let \(c_1 < \dots < c_{\mathrm{m}}\) be those roots of \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) in \((-1, 1)\) at which \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) changes sign (\(m \ge 0\)), and \(\pi(\xi) = (\xi - c_1) \cdots (\xi - c_{\mathrm{m}})\) (\(\pi = 1\) for \(m = 0\)). The product \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\,\pi\) does not change sign on \((-1, 1)\): at every \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) both factors change sign and the changes cancel, elsewhere it is continuous and cannot change sign between two consecutive zeros, because it would have a zero in between (intermediate value theorem). The product is not identically zero and is continuous, so \(\int_{-1}^{1} p_{\mathrm{q}}\,\pi\,\mathrm{d}\xi \neq 0\): at a point where it differs from zero it is, by continuity, at least half of that value in absolute value on a small interval, which by lemma 3.54, items 3 and 4, gives a positive contribution, and elsewhere it has the same sign.
  5. If \(m < q\), then \(\pi \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\) and by item 1 we would have \(\int_{-1}^{1} p_{\mathrm{q}}\,\pi\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0\), a contradiction with step 4. So \(m \ge q\), and since \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) has at most \(q\) roots by step 3, \(m = q\). Hence \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) has exactly \(q\) distinct real roots \(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_{\mathrm{q}}\) in \((-1, 1)\). The difference \(p_{\mathrm{q}} - (\xi - \xi_1) \cdots (\xi - \xi_{\mathrm{q}})\) has degree less than \(q\) and \(q\) roots, so by step 3 it is zero, and \(p_{\mathrm{q}} = \prod_{\mathrm{i}} (\xi - \xi_{\mathrm{i}})\). This is item 2.
  6. Item 3. The denominators in \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\) are not zero, because the roots are distinct. We have \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\) and \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi_{\mathrm{j}}) = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For every \(r \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\) the polynomial \(r - \sum_{\mathrm{i}} r(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\) has degree at most \(q - 1\) and \(q\) roots \(\xi_{\mathrm{j}}\), so by step 3 it is zero. By linearity of the integral, \(\int_{-1}^{1} r\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}}\,r(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every \(r \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\).
  7. Let \(g \in \mathcal{P}_{2\mathrm{q}-1}\) and \(r = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\ell_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\). The polynomial \(g - r\) has the roots \(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_{\mathrm{q}}\). By step 3, \(g - r = (\xi - \xi_1)\,h_1\), and since \(\xi_2 - \xi_1 \neq 0\) we have \(h_1(\xi_2) = 0\) and \(h_1 = (\xi - \xi_2)\,h_2\). Repeating the procedure, \(g - r = p_{\mathrm{q}}\,s\) with a polynomial \(s\) of degree at most \((2q - 1) - q = q - 1\).
  8. Then \(\int_{-1}^{1} g\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \int_{-1}^{1} p_{\mathrm{q}}\,s\,\mathrm{d}\xi + \int_{-1}^{1} r\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \int_{-1}^{1} r\,\mathrm{d}\xi\) by item 1, because \(s \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\). The sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}}\,\big(p_{\mathrm{q}}(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,s(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}) + r(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\big) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} w_{\mathrm{i}}\,r(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\), because \(p_{\mathrm{q}}(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\). By step 6 this is \(\int_{-1}^{1} r\,\mathrm{d}\xi\). The two sides are equal.
  9. Weights. The polynomial \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}^{\,2}\) has degree \(2q - 2 \le 2q - 1\), so by step 8 \(\int_{-1}^{1} \ell_{\mathrm{i}}^{\,2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} w_{\mathrm{j}}\,\ell_{\mathrm{i}}(\xi_{\mathrm{j}})^2 = w_{\mathrm{i}}\). The integral of the nonnegative continuous polynomial \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}^{\,2}\), which equals \(1\) at \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\), is positive (as in step 4), so \(w_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\). This is item 3.
  10. Item 4. Let \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) be arbitrary points, \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) arbitrary weights and \(\omega(\xi) = \prod_{\mathrm{i}} (\xi - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\). The polynomial \(\omega^2\) has degree \(2q\), is nonnegative and has at most \(q\) roots (step 3), so \(\int_{-1}^{1} \omega^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi > 0\) as in step 4. The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} v_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}})^2\) is zero, because \(\omega(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\). For \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}} = \xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) we have \(\omega = p_{\mathrm{q}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG, section 19.5, PDF p. 863: Gauss showed exactness up to degree \(2n - 1\), the nodes are the zeros of the Legendre polynomial \(P_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the coefficients are obtained from Lagrange's interpolation polynomial. COOK, section 6.3, PDF p. 224: the points are roots of Legendre polynomials. ONATE1, PDF p. 113: degree \(2q - 1\). The proof in steps 1 to 10 is not from the sources, they do not contain it. The polynomial \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) is the Legendre polynomial divided by its leading coefficient (SymPy, num-06.out, part A).

The theorem has shown that the rule exists for every \(q\), that the points are the roots of \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\), and that nothing better is possible: with \(q\) points we never hit degree \(2q\), whatever the choice, so \(2q - 1\) is the highest degree that can be reached. What the polynomials \(p_{\mathrm{q}}\) look like is shown in figure 15.68. They are drawn as \(p_{\mathrm{q}}(\xi)/p_{\mathrm{q}}(1)\), so that all have the value \(1\) at \(\xi = 1\) and can be compared. The polynomial \(p_1 = \xi\) has the root \(0\), the only point of the one-point rule with weight \(2\). The polynomial \(p_2 = \xi^2 - \tfrac13\) has the roots \(\pm 1/\sqrt3\), as in definition 7.24. The polynomial \(p_3\) has the roots \(0\) and \(\pm\sqrt{3/5}\), and for \(q = 1, 2, 3\) the computation from the proof (SymPy, num-06.out, part A) gives the weights \(2\), \(1\) and \(1\), \(5/9\) and \(8/9\), exactly those of chapter 7. Only the polynomial \(p_4\) with roots \(\pm 0{,}3400\) and \(\pm 0{,}8611\) (weights \(0{,}6521\) and \(0{,}3479\)) and the polynomial \(p_5\) with roots \(0\), \(\pm 0{,}5385\) and \(\pm 0{,}9062\) (weights \(0{,}5689\), \(0{,}4786\) and \(0{,}2369\)) are new. The roots of each polynomial are the Gauss points, all of them lie inside the end points and crowd more towards them.

A second view is counting. A rule with \(q\) points has \(q\) points and \(q\) weights, so \(2q\) freely choosable numbers. It must integrate the monomials \(1, \xi, \dots, \xi^{2q-1}\) exactly, which is \(2q\) conditions. There are as many numbers as conditions, so we can expect exactly one choice to satisfy them. The theorem gives one, and it is the only one by item 1: if a rule with points \(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and weights \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) is exact on \(\mathcal{P}_{2\mathrm{q}-1}\), then for \(\omega = \prod_{\mathrm{i}} (\xi - \zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\) and every \(s \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{q}-1}\) we have \(\int_{-1}^{1} \omega\,s\,\mathrm{d}\xi = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} v_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}})\,s(\zeta_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\), so \(\omega = p_{\mathrm{q}}\), and exactness for \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\) fixes the weights. For \(q = 2\) these are four equations for \(\xi_1, \xi_2, w_1, w_2\) with the common solution \(\pm 1/\sqrt3\) and \(w_1 = w_2 = 1\). The view adds the reason for the degree \(2q - 1\): one more degree would give \(2q + 1\) conditions for \(2q\) numbers.

For a plane element we have two coordinates. It suffices to use the rule in each, one after the other, as the next corollary says.

Corollary 15.43 Gauss rule on the square and cube (Gaussovo pravilo na kvadratu in kocki)

Let \(g(\xi, \eta)\) be a polynomial of two variables of degree at most \(2n - 1\) in \(\xi\) and at most \(2m - 1\) in \(\eta\). Let \((\xi_{\mathrm{a}}, w_{\mathrm{a}})\) be the points and weights of the rule with \(n\) points and \((\eta_{\mathrm{b}}, v_{\mathrm{b}})\) of the rule with \(m\) points from theorem 15.42. Then

\[\int_{[-1,1]^2} g\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{a}=1}^{n} \sum_{\mathrm{b}=1}^{m} w_{\mathrm{a}}\,v_{\mathrm{b}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{a}}, \eta_{\mathrm{b}}) . \tag{15.36}\]

The same holds for a polynomial of three variables on the cube \([-1, 1]^3\) with a triple sum.

Proof

We have to show that the double integral splits into two one-dimensional ones, and to use theorem 15.42 on each.

  1. By theorem 4.9 (the same in the plane), \(\int_{[-1,1]^2} g\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{-1}^{1} G(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta\) with \(G(\eta) = \int_{-1}^{1} g(\xi, \eta)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\).
  2. For every \(\eta\) the function \(g(\cdot, \eta)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(2n - 1\) in \(\xi\), so by item 3 of theorem 15.42 \(G(\eta) = \sum_{\mathrm{a}} w_{\mathrm{a}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{a}}, \eta)\).
  3. The function \(\eta \mapsto g(\xi_{\mathrm{a}}, \eta)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(2m - 1\), and \(G\) is their linear combination with the weights \(w_{\mathrm{a}}\), so it is a polynomial of the same degree. By theorem 15.42, \(\int_{-1}^{1} G\,\mathrm{d}\eta = \sum_{\mathrm{b}} v_{\mathrm{b}}\,G(\eta_{\mathrm{b}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{b}} \sum_{\mathrm{a}} v_{\mathrm{b}}\,w_{\mathrm{a}}\,g(\xi_{\mathrm{a}}, \eta_{\mathrm{b}})\). For the cube we repeat the step once more.

Source: ONATE1, equation (6.39), PDF p. 221: a double sum with weights \(W_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(W_{\mathrm{q}}\), and the statement that the points and weights per coordinate come from table 3.1. The proof is not from the source.

For a rectangular four-node element with a 2D material this means the following. Let the element be a rectangle \(2a \times 2b\) with nodes at the corners, so \(x = a\,\xi\) and \(y = b\,\eta\) and \(J = ab\) is constant. The derivative of the shape function \(N_{\mathrm{i}} = \tfrac14(1 + \xi_{\mathrm{i}}\xi)(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\eta)\) is \(\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = \tfrac{\xi_{\mathrm{i}}}{4a}\,(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\eta)\), linear in \(\eta\) and independent of \(\xi\), and \(\partial N_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = \tfrac{\eta_{\mathrm{i}}}{4b}\,(1 + \xi_{\mathrm{i}}\xi)\), linear in \(\xi\). Every term of the integrand \(\mat B^\T \mat C \mat B\,J\) is therefore a product of two such derivatives, so in each variable of degree at most \(2 \le 3 = 2 \cdot 2 - 1\). By corollary 15.43 the \(2 \times 2\) rule is exact. In the eight-node element (definition 15.39) the shape functions also have the terms \(\xi^2\), \(\eta^2\), \(\xi^2\eta\) and \(\xi\eta^2\). The derivative with respect to \(\xi\) is therefore a combination of \(1\), \(\xi\), \(\eta\), \(\xi\eta\) and \(\eta^2\), and the derivative with respect to \(\eta\) a combination of \(1\), \(\xi\), \(\eta\), \(\xi\eta\) and \(\xi^2\). A product of two derivatives has degree up to \(4\) in each variable, and since \(2n - 1 \ge 4\) holds only from \(n = 3\) on, the \(3 \times 3\) rule is exact. That the rule with one point fewer in each direction is not exact for either element is shown by the computation below and by the count of spurious modes (num-06.out, parts D and G). We call the smallest exact rule full integration.

Definition 15.44 Full integration (polna integracija)

The rule \(n \times n\) (in 3D \(n \times n \times n\)) for computing \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the full integration of an element if \(n\) is the smallest number of points for which the rule is exact for the integrand of the stiffness matrix in the form where \(J\) is constant (a rectangle, and for elements with side nodes these are at the midpoints of the sides). A rule with fewer points is reduced integration (reducirana integracija). For the four-node element full integration is \(2 \times 2\) and reduced \(1 \times 1\), for the eight-node element \(3 \times 3\) and \(2 \times 2\).

Source: COOK, section 6.8, PDF p. 237: "full integration" is a rule that exactly integrates all stiffness coefficients of an undistorted element, and "underintegration" is a rule of lower order. ONATE1, section 6.8, PDF p. 232: full or exact integration is the rule that exactly integrates the same expression for a rectangle or a straight-sided triangle.

Let us try it on an element \(40 \times 30\) mm with \(t = 10\) mm (SymPy, num-06.out, part D). The entry \(k_{11}\) is \(873\,504{,}27\) N/mm with the exact integral. The \(2 \times 2\) rule gives this value, the \(3 \times 3\) rule too (the difference in the largest entry of the matrix is below \(10^{-9}\) N/mm), since by corollary 15.43 both have enough points. The \(1 \times 1\) rule gives \(655\,128{,}21\) N/mm, exactly \(3/4\) of the true value. The reason is in the terms: \(k_{11}\) is the sum of two terms, each containing one of \((1 - \eta)^2\) and \((1 - \xi)^2\) (squares of the derivatives of \(N_1\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\)) multiplied by constants. The exact integral is \(\int_{[-1,1]^2} (1 - \eta)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 2 \cdot \tfrac83 = \tfrac{16}{3}\), the one-point rule at the centre gives \(4 \cdot 1 = 4\), a ratio of \(3/4\) for both terms. This is the case of corollary 15.43 with degree \(2\) against \(2n - 1 = 1\): the rule fails exactly at the term \(\eta^2\), which the full integration still captures.

For a general quadrilateral the situation changes. The square \([-1, 1]^2\) with the points of the rules \(1 \times 1\), \(2 \times 2\) and \(3 \times 3\) shows where the values of the integrand are taken, the coordinates \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) are as before. For our quadrilateral the integrand is \((\text{polynomial})/J\) and not a polynomial, so corollary 15.43 says nothing about the accuracy. A computation with the rules \(n \times n\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 5\) and a comparison with a very accurate computation (the \(12 \times 12\) rule, value \(k_{11} = 799\,129{,}02\) N/mm) gives the relative errors of the entry \(k_{11}\)

(num-06.out, part E). Every extra point in each direction reduces the error by a factor of about \(270\). The cause is not proved in this section: the integrand is not a polynomial, because the denominator \(J\) is not constant. The zero of the denominator is not one point: at \(\eta = -1\) it lies at \(\xi = -9\), at \(\eta = 0\) at \(\xi = -243/30 = -8{,}1\) and at \(\eta = 1\) at \(\xi = -7{,}2\), all outside the interval, and the polynomials of the rule imitate it badly only near it. For comparison take an element in which \(J\) changes along \(\xi\) in the ratio \(3 : 1\), so that the integrand is \(1/(1 + \xi/2)\) with the zero of the denominator at \(\xi = -2\). For \(\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{1 + \xi/2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 2\ln 3 = 2{,}19722\) the errors at \(q = 1, \dots, 5\) are \(-1{,}97 \cdot 10^{-1}\), \(-1{,}54 \cdot 10^{-2}\), \(-1{,}15 \cdot 10^{-3}\), \(-8{,}39 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(-6{,}09 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (file mkk.out, part A, not num-06.out), so it falls only by a factor of about \(13\) per point. A more distorted element, that is a nearer zero of the denominator, thus needs more points. The reason for the number is easy to state, although we do not prove it here: for an integrand \(1/(\xi - s)\) with the zero of the denominator at \(s\) outside the interval, \(|s| > 1\), the error of the rule with \(q\) points drops by a factor of about \(\rho^2\) with each extra point, where \(\rho = |s| + \sqrt{s^2 - 1}\). For \(s = 9\), \(8{,}1\) and \(7{,}2\) we get \(\rho^2 = 322\), \(260\) and \(205\). The observed factor of about \(270\) lies in this range and slowly falls with more points (\(257\) at \(n = 5 \to 6\)). For \(s = 2\) we get \(\rho^2 = 13{,}9\) and above it is \(13\) (SymPy and NumPy, agreement checked for \(q = 1, \dots, 5\)). A zero at \(s = \pm 1\) would give \(\rho = 1\), so the error would no longer fall by a fixed factor per point, only slowly (for \(1/(\xi - s)\) the integral does not even exist), and this is the limit that a distorted element must not reach. The formula for \(\rho\) is not in the project sources (source needed), so we state it as checked on examples and not as a theorem. The folded quadrilateral of section 15.5, where \(J\) is negative at one corner, is not covered by theorem 4.59 at all, so no rule computes an area integral there.

For a triangle we do not use the square but area coordinates \(L_1, L_2, L_3\) (definition 15.3), and the integral of a monomial is in lemma 15.9. The rule has points given by area coordinates and weights that are shares of the area \(A\).

Lemma 15.45 Quadrature rules for a triangle (pravila za trikotnik)

Let a triangle have area \(A\) and area coordinates \(L_1, L_2, L_3\) (definition 15.3). The rule \(R_1\) has one point \((\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)\) with weight \(A\). The rule \(R_2\) has three points \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)\) and the two others by cyclic exchange, each with weight \(A/3\) (midpoints of the sides). The rule \(R_3\) has three points \((\tfrac23, \tfrac16, \tfrac16)\) and the two others by cyclic exchange, each with weight \(A/3\). Then:

  1. All three rules integrate polynomials of degree at most \(1\) in \(x\) and \(y\) exactly. The rules \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) also integrate polynomials of degree \(2\) exactly, the rule \(R_1\) does not.
  2. The rules \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) fail at \(L_1^3\), the rule \(R_1\) at \(L_1^2\).
Proof

We have to show that the integrals of the monomials \(L_1^a L_2^b L_3^c\) are computed correctly, and then that this suffices for all polynomials.

  1. By lemma 15.9, \(\int L_1^a L_2^b L_3^c\,\mathrm{d}A = a!\,b!\,c!\,2A/(a + b + c + 2)!\). For degree \(1\) this is \(A/3\), for degree \(2\) \(\int L_1^2\,\mathrm{d}A = A/6\) and \(\int L_1 L_2\,\mathrm{d}A = A/12\).
  2. Degree 1. For \(L_1\) the rule \(R_1\) gives \(A \cdot \tfrac13\). The rules \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) give \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + 0) = \tfrac{A}{3}\) and \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac23 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac16) = \tfrac{A}{3}\), and by symmetry the same holds for \(L_2\) and \(L_3\).
  3. Degree 2. For \(R_2\): \(L_1^2\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + 0) = \tfrac{A}{6}\) and \(L_1 L_2\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac14 + 0 + 0) = \tfrac{A}{12}\). For \(R_3\): \(L_1^2\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac49 + \tfrac1{36} + \tfrac1{36}) = \tfrac{A}{6}\), and \(L_1 L_2\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac16 + \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac23 + \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac16) = \tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac19 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{36}) = \tfrac{A}{12}\). For \(R_1\): \(L_1^2\) gives \(A/9 \neq A/6\).
  4. Degree 3. \(\int L_1^3\,\mathrm{d}A = A/10\). The rule \(R_2\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac18 + \tfrac18) = \tfrac{A}{12}\), and the rule \(R_3\) gives \(\tfrac{A}{3}(\tfrac8{27} + \tfrac1{216} + \tfrac1{216}) = \tfrac{11A}{108}\). Both differ from \(A/10\).
  5. A general polynomial. The coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) are affine functions of the \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) (lemma 15.4), so a polynomial of degree \(d\) in \(x\) and \(y\) is a linear combination of products of at most \(d\) coordinates \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\). A product with fewer than \(d\) factors we multiply by \((L_1 + L_2 + L_3)^{d - k} = 1\) and get a linear combination of monomials \(L_1^a L_2^b L_3^c\) with \(a + b + c = d\). All rules are linear in the function and the integral is linear, so it suffices to check these monomials. The monomials of degree \(2\) are \(L_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) and \(L_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{j}}\), of degree \(1\) they are \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\), and \(L_2\), \(L_3\) give the same computations as \(L_1\) by the cyclic symmetry of the rules.

Source: ONATE1, section 6.4.2 and figure 6.5, PDF p. 222: the rule for a triangle in area coordinates, weights with sum \(1/2\) for the reference triangle, accuracy is the degree of the highest polynomial integrated exactly. The values are ours, checked in num-06.out, part B.

The points of the three rules are shown in the lower row of figure 15.71: the centroid, the midpoints of the sides and the interior points, each of which lies at the middle of the segment between one corner and the centroid, and the corners have \(L_1\), \(L_2\) and \(L_3\) equal to \(1\) respectively. The area coordinates are affine functions of \(x\) and \(y\) (lemma 15.4), so the midpoint of a segment has as its coordinates the average of the coordinates of the end points: the average of the corner \((1, 0, 0)\) and the centroid \((\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)\) is exactly \((\tfrac23, \tfrac16, \tfrac16)\).

In all this we did not ask what happens if the rule is reduced. If so, the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) contains only the information that the points of the rule see. Let us look at what this means for the motions of the element that they do not see.

Let the element have \(n\) degrees of freedom (the four-node plane element \(8\), the eight-node \(16\)) and let the rule have \(P\) points with weights \(W_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\) (products of one-dimensional weights, positive by item 3 of theorem 15.42). The strain matrix at the point \(p\) is \(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\) of size \(3 \times n\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\) is the determinant of the map in it, and

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \sum_{\mathrm{p}=1}^{P} W_{\mathrm{p}}\,t\,J_{\mathrm{p}}\,\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\,\mat C\,\mat B_{\mathrm{p}} . \tag{15.37}\]

The rigid motions in the plane, two translations and one rotation, three in all, have zero strain. For the triangle lemma 15.26 showed that there are no other motions without strain.

Definition 15.46 Spurious mode (lažni način)

Let \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the stiffness matrix of an element of order \(n\) from equation (15.37) and \(\vek d\) the column of nodal displacements of the element. The column \(\vek d\) is a spurious mode if \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) but the displacement field of the element determined by \(\vek d\) is not a rigid motion (a translation and a rotation of a rigid body). The name is "spurious" (false) because the element deforms under \(\vek d\) but needs no force for it and stores no energy, so it behaves as if the motion met no resistance. When \(\mat C\) is symmetric positive definite, \(t > 0\), \(W_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\), \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite (lemma 15.47, step 1), and then by lemma 7.16 \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = 0\).

Source: COOK, section 6.8, PDF pp. 237 and 238: "spurious mode" (also "zero-energy deformation mode", "hourglass mode", "mechanism"): an element with it has no resistance to nodal forces that excite it. The proof and the expressions are not from the source, the name in Slovenian is ours.

Lemma 15.47 Number of spurious modes under reduced integration (število lažnih načinov pri reducirani integraciji)

Let \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the matrix of order \(n\) from equation (15.37) with \(\mat C\) symmetric positive definite, \(t > 0\), \(W_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\) and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > 0\). Then:

  1. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) at every point \(p\).
  2. The dimension of the kernel of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is at least \(n - 3P\). The kernel contains three independent rigid motions, so the number of independent spurious modes, that is the dimension of the kernel minus \(3\) (the number of directions the kernel has besides the rigid motions), is at least \(n - 3P - 3\).
Proof

We have to show that the energy splits into contributions of the points and that the number of equations bounds the kernel.

  1. From equation (15.37), \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} W_{\mathrm{p}}\,t\,J_{\mathrm{p}}\,(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\vek d)^\T\,\mat C\,(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\vek d)\). Every term is nonnegative, because \(\mat C\) is positive definite, and zero exactly for \(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\vek d = \vek 0\), because \(W_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(t\), \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) are positive. The sum is zero exactly when every term is zero. By lemma 7.16 this is equivalent to \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) (the matrix is symmetric and, by this computation, positive semidefinite).
  2. By step 1 the kernel is the set of solutions of the system \(\mat B_{\mathrm{p}}\,\vek d = \vek 0\) for \(p = 1, \dots, P\), which is \(3P\) homogeneous equations in \(n\) unknowns. Such a set of solutions has dimension at least \(n - 3P\) (every equation lowers the dimension by at most one).
  3. The displacements of a rigid motion correspond to the field \(\vek u = (a - \omega y,\ b + \omega x)\), which is linear and is therefore reproduced exactly by the element, by lemma 15.36. Its strain is zero everywhere, so it lies in the kernel. The three columns of the rigid motions (translation in the direction \(x\), translation in the direction \(y\), rotation) are independent. Indeed, if \(a - \omega y_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and \(b + \omega x_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) at all nodes \(i\), we subtract the equations of two distinct nodes \(i\) and \(j\) and get \(\omega\,(y_{\mathrm{i}} - y_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) and \(\omega\,(x_{\mathrm{i}} - x_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\), so \(\omega = 0\) and then \(a = b = 0\). So we subtract the rigid motions from the \(\ge n - 3P\) kernel directions, and at least \(n - 3P - 3\) spurious ones remain.

Source: COOK, section 6.8, PDF p. 240: the counting, in which an \(n\)-point rule gives \(3n\) (plane element) or \(6n\) (solid) items of independent information, and the number of spurious modes equals the order of the matrix minus \(3n + 3\) or \(6n + 6\). The proof is not from the source.

Let us see what the lemma gives for our elements (num-06.out, part G, unit square). The condition that \(\mat C\) is positive definite holds for our steel: its eigenvalues are \(75{,}38\), \(150{,}77\) and \(280{,}00\) GPa (num-06.out, part C). For the four-node element \(n = 8\). The \(2 \times 2\) rule has \(P = 4\) and the bound \(8 - 12 - 3\) is negative, so nothing forces a spurious mode. The matrix indeed has exactly three zero eigenvalues (rigid motions). The \(1 \times 1\) rule has \(P = 1\) and the lemma demands at least \(8 - 3 - 3 = 2\) spurious modes. The matrix indeed has five zero eigenvalues, so besides the three rigid motions two spurious modes. For the eight-node element \(n = 16\). The \(3 \times 3\) rule (\(P = 9\)) demands nothing and indeed has only three zero eigenvalues, no spurious modes. The \(2 \times 2\) rule (\(P = 4\)) demands at least \(16 - 12 - 3 = 1\) and the matrix has four zero eigenvalues, so one spurious mode. The lemma holds for plane elements with three strain components and three rigid motions. For the eight-node solid element with six strain components and six rigid motions the same count with one point and \(n = 24\) gives at least \(24 - 6 - 6 = 12\) spurious modes, and COOK (PDF p. 240, table 6.8-1) states exactly \(12\).

What does such a spurious mode look like under \(1 \times 1\)? Take the square \([-1, 1]^2\) with nodes \(1\) to \(4\) in order at \((-1, -1)\), \((1, -1)\), \((1, 1)\), \((-1, 1)\) (side \(2\), so that \(x = \xi\) and \(y = \eta\)) and the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, -1, 1, -1)\,a\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). From the shape functions the displacement field in the element is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \tfrac{a}{4}\big[(1-\xi)(1-\eta) - (1+\xi)(1-\eta) + (1+\xi)(1+\eta) - (1-\xi)(1+\eta)\big] = a\,\xi\eta , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0 . \tag{15.38}\]

The strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = a\,\eta\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y = a\,\xi\). At the centre \((\xi, \eta) = (0, 0)\) all three are zero, so by lemma 15.47, item 1, the \(1 \times 1\) rule does not detect this mode and \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d = 0\). With the \(2 \times 2\) rule the strains at the points \((\pm 1/\sqrt3, \pm 1/\sqrt3)\) are \((\pm 0{,}577, 0, \pm 0{,}577)\,a\) and for a square of side \(2\) mm and thickness \(t = 10\) mm made of our steel, \(\vek d^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d = 3{,}88 \cdot 10^{6}\) N mm at \(a = 1\) mm, different from zero (num-06.out, part F). The strain energy is half of this, \(1{,}94 \cdot 10^{6}\) N mm.

We draw figure 15.73 step by step. First the original element, the square with nodes \(1\) to \(4\), dashed. We move the nodes: \(1\) and \(3\) to the right, \(2\) and \(4\) to the left, each by \(a\), and the quadrilateral becomes a trapezoid, because the lower side shortens and the upper one lengthens. The displacement is exaggerated (\(a = 0{,}3\) of the half side) so that we can see it. Both midlines \(\xi = 0\) and \(\eta = 0\) neither move nor change length here, because \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a\xi\eta\) is zero on both. At the centre, where the midlines cross and which is the only point of the \(1 \times 1\) rule, the element therefore sees no deformation. The four points of the \(2 \times 2\) rule, however, lie off the midlines, where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\eta\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = a\xi\) differ from zero, and so the \(2 \times 2\) rule detects the mode.

The source calls such a mode the hourglass mode (COOK, PDF pp. 237 and 238) and does not explain the name, so we use it as a name and not as a description of the shape: in our mode the upper half of the element stretches in the direction \(x\) and the lower half shortens, since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = a\eta\). The consequence in a mesh is important. In a bar of these elements with the reduced rule, clamped at one end and loaded axially, spurious modes become "near mechanisms": the restraint is not felt at distant elements, and if the ratio of length to height \(L/H\) is large enough, the computed displacement of the loaded node can exceed the length of the bar, although the strains at the element centres look good (COOK, section 6.8, PDF pp. 238 and 239, figure 6.8-2). In the eight-node element with \(2 \times 2\) there is one spurious mode, which cannot spread over several elements (ONATE1, section 6.8, PDF p. 235, figure 6.14b), and in the four-node one with \(1 \times 1\) both spread (ONATE1, PDF p. 234, figure 6.14a). These last two sentences are quoted from the sources and not proved here.

Let us sum up what the section showed and what we shall use later. The rule with \(q\) points is exact up to degree \(2q - 1\) (theorem 15.42) and on the square and the cube as a product (corollary 15.43). For a rectangular element the full integration is therefore \(2 \times 2\) (four-node) and \(3 \times 3\) (eight-node). For a distorted element the integrand is rational and the corollary says nothing about the accuracy. For our quadrilateral the error fell by a factor of about \(270\) per point, which lies in the range of \(\rho^2\) from \(205\) to \(322\) along the zero of the denominator, checked on examples and not proved. For a triangle the rules \(R_1\) to \(R_3\) hold (lemma 15.45). With a rule with fewer points (definition 15.44) the matrix has at least \(n - 3P - 3\) spurious modes (lemma 15.47), which for \(1 \times 1\) on the four-node element is the hourglass mode. This counting returns in the patch test of the next section and in locking, where we shall deliberately reduce the rule for only one of the terms.
