Appendix AWeekly tests

This page holds short answers to the questions of the weekly tests. Each answer is what one would write in a test in about five minutes: the definitions and equations to be written down, with a short note on what they mean and where they stop holding, without derivations. Derivations, calculations and examples are in the sections of the book named in the line "In the book" under each answer. The concepts in the text are linked to their definitions, so a definition opens with one click. Each week we add a new section.

A.1 Test 1: general equations of elastostatics

A.1.1 Traction vector, stress and strain tensor, the infinitesimal element, equivalent stress

The traction vector at a point of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\) is the force per unit area with which the part of the body into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the part for which \(\vek n\) is the outward normal:

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{A} \to 0} \frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n , \qquad \tau = \big|\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\big| .\]

It depends on the point and on the normal. The normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) is positive in tension. The shear part \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\) lies in the face, and the shear stress \(\tau\) is its length (figure A.1).

The stress tensor at a point consists of the nine components of the traction vectors on the faces perpendicular to the axes. The first index tells the face (its normal), the second the direction of the component:

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) .\]

Equal indices mean a normal stress, different ones a shear stress (figure A.2). On the opposite face, with the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), so there positive components point in the negative directions of the axes, and a positive normal stress pulls outward on both faces. The equilibrium of moments gives the symmetry, and the equilibrium of a small tetrahedron gives Cauchy's formula \(t_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek n) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\) (a sum over the first index), which gives the traction vector on every face. In matrices this is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma^\T\vek n\), which by the symmetry equals \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\):

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n^\T\ten\sigma\,\vek n .\]

Six components are independent. It is a tensor because its components change by the rule \(\bar{\ten\sigma} = \mat Q\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Q^\T\) when the axes turn with the rotation matrix \(\mat Q\), whose row \(k\) holds the components of the unit vector of the new axis \(k\) in the old axes, \(Q_{\mathrm{kj}} = \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\). CM writes the indices the other way round (the first index is the direction of the component, the second the face), so it writes Cauchy's formula as \(t_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\).

The strain tensor (small strain tensor) describes how the material close to a point is stretched and sheared:

\[\ten\varepsilon = \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]

The normal components are the relative elongations of fibres along the axes, the shear components half the decrease of the right angle between two fibres. The engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the whole decrease of the angle (figures A.3 and A.4).

The infinitesimal element (elementarni delec) is a small cube with faces parallel to the axes, cut out of the body in our minds. We draw the stress components on its faces and write its equilibrium. When we shrink it to a point, we get the equations at the point (the continuum idealization).

The equivalent stress is a single number from all the components that we compare with the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) from a tension test. Yielding starts when the equivalent stress equals \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\). The principal stresses \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3\), ordered by size, are the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\ten\sigma\), the solutions of \(\det(\ten\sigma - \lambda\,\mat I) = 0\). On a face perpendicular to a principal direction there is no shear stress. The von Mises and the Tresca equivalent stresses are

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{3J_2} = \sqrt{\tfrac12\big[(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2 + (\sigma_2 - \sigma_3)^2 + (\sigma_3 - \sigma_1)^2\big]} ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{Tr}} = \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 2\,\tau_{\max}\]

(BOWER, PDF p. 146), and the von Mises stress in components (SADD (3.5.5), PDF p. 73)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + 3\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}^2\big)} .\]

Here \(J_2 = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} s_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\) is the second invariant of the deviator \(\ten s = \ten\sigma - \sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\,\mat I\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})/3\) the mean stress. In uniaxial tension \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma\), in pure shear with the shear stress \(\tau_0 > 0\) (section A.1.6) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt3\,\tau_0\). Both measure only the deviator (the change of shape), so they do not depend on a hydrostatic pressure.

A.1.2 Equilibrium equations

From the equilibrium of forces on an infinitesimal element shrunk to a point we get three first-order partial differential equations (figure A.5):

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{y}} &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \end{aligned}\]

in short

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} + b_{\mathrm{j}} = 0 , \qquad j = x, y, z .\]

Here \(\vek b\) is the body force per unit volume (weight: \(\rho\,\vek g\)). The equilibrium of moments gives the symmetry \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\). On the surface a boundary condition holds, where the load \(\bar{\vek t}\) or the displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) is prescribed:

\[\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t} \quad \text{on } S_{\mathrm{t}} , \qquad \vek u = \bar{\vek u} \quad \text{on } S_{\mathrm{u}} .\]

In motion the right side is \(\rho\,a_{\mathrm{j}}\) instead of zero. For a finite part of the body, by the divergence theorem,

\[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t\,\mathrm dA + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\,\mathrm dV = \vek 0 .\]

Meaning: three equations for six unknown stress components, so in general equilibrium alone does not give the stresses, we also need kinematics and Hooke's law. The equations hold for every material. For small deformations we write them on the undeformed shape. A test: in the hanging bar, with the axis \(x\) pointing vertically down from the support, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) and \(b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g\), so \(-\rho g + \rho g = 0\).

In the book: sections 2.7, 2.7.6, 2.8 and 4.2.13.

A.1.3 Relations between the displacement field and the strain components

The displacement field \(\vek u(x, y, z) = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) assigns to every point its displacement. Strain tells how the displacement changes from point to point, so we get it from the displacement gradient \(\mat H\) with the entries \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\). For small strains:

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}\Big) . \end{aligned}\]

A normal component is the relative elongation of a fibre along an axis, a shear component half the decrease of the right angle between two fibres: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y\) is the tilt of the fibre along \(y\) towards the axis \(x\), \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\) the tilt of the fibre along \(x\) towards the axis \(y\), and their sum is \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). A tilt is a ratio of lengths, the displacement in the direction \(x\) per unit length in the direction \(y\), so it has no unit. For the angle \(\varphi\) by which a short fibre along \(y\) tilts towards the axis \(x\), measured in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), exactly \(\tan\varphi = (\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y)/(1 + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y)\). An angle in radians is the length of the arc that the angle cuts from a circle of radius \(1\) (LEBL2, PDF p. 167). For small strains the denominator is almost \(1\) and \(\tan\varphi \approx \varphi\), so the tilt is the angle \(\varphi\) itself in radians, and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the decrease of the right angle in radians. For the sheared cube with the edge 10 mm, whose top face we shift by 10 µm in the direction of the axis \(x\) (the axis \(z\) points up, section 1.6.2), the tilt of a vertical edge is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = 0{,}001\), which is \(0{,}001\) rad or \(0{,}057^\circ\).

The displacement gradient splits into the strain and a small rotation, which to first order does not lengthen the fibres (figure A.6):

\[\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega , \qquad \ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\big(\mat H + \mat H^\T\big) , \qquad \ten\omega = \tfrac12\big(\mat H - \mat H^\T\big) .\]

Validity: only for \(|\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}| \ll 1\), that is, for small strains and small rotations. For large rotations we use the Green-Lagrange strain tensor, which has quadratic terms as well, for example

\[\begin{aligned} \mat E &= \tfrac12\big(\mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\big) , \\ E_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac12\bigg[\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2\bigg] . \end{aligned}\]

For a rigid rotation \(\mat E = \mat 0\), while the small strain tensor is not zero (figure A.7).

A.1.4 The role of the compatibility equations

The six strain components are computed from only three displacement components, so they cannot be arbitrary. If we cut the body into infinitesimal elements and deformed each by arbitrarily prescribed strains, the elements would no longer fit (gaps, overlaps). The compatibility equations are the conditions under which a continuous displacement field exists from which the strains come. We get them by eliminating the displacements from the relations between displacements and strains. In the plane there is one:

\[\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} = 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} ,\]

in space there are six (the Saint-Venant equations):

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial y^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial z^2} &= 2\,\frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial y^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} , \\ \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial x\,\partial y} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial z^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial x\,\partial z} + \frac{\partial^2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial y\,\partial z} . \end{aligned}\]

An example of strains that are not compatible: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = k\,y^2\), all others zero. The left side of the plane equation is \(2k\), the right side \(0\) (figure A.8).

When we need them: when the unknowns are stresses or strains (solving with stresses), we must require them separately. When we solve with displacements, they hold by themselves. In a body without holes (simply connected) they are also sufficient, in a body with holes (a ring) further conditions are needed so that the displacements match after going around the hole (figure A.9). Strains that are not compatible, for example from uneven heating, cause stresses without any external load.

In the book: sections 1.18.1, 1.18.3, 1.18.5 and 3.3.3.

A.1.5 Hooke's law and the meaning of the material constants

For a linear elastic material (small strains, stresses below the yield stress, unloading along the same path) every stress component is a linear combination of the strain components:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}} .\]

Of the 81 constants, 36 remain because \(\ten\sigma\) and \(\ten\varepsilon\) are symmetric, and 21 because a strain energy exists (\(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\)). An orthotropic material has 9 constants, a transversely isotropic one 5, an isotropic one 2.

For an isotropic material:

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{2G} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}})}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} &= \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}})}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T , \qquad & \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac{1 + \nu}{E}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} , \end{aligned}\]

where \(\Delta T = T - T_0\) is the change of temperature from the reference temperature \(T_0\) at which the material is free of stress, and the other way round (at constant temperature)

\[\ten\sigma = \lambda\,(\tr\ten\varepsilon)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,\ten\varepsilon , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{m}} = K\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} , \qquad \ten s = 2G\,\ten e .\]

Meaning of the constants: Young's modulus \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon\) is the slope of the line in uniaxial tension (196 GPa for steel). Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) is the ratio of the lateral contraction to the axial elongation (about 0,3 for metals) (figures A.10 and A.11). The shear modulus \(G\), the bulk modulus \(K\) and the Lamé constants are

\[G = \frac{E}{2\,(1 + \nu)} , \qquad K = \frac{E}{3\,(1 - 2\nu)} , \qquad \lambda = \frac{\nu\,E}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} , \qquad \mu = G .\]

\(G\) is the ratio of the shear stress to \(\gamma\) (figure A.12), \(K\) the ratio of the pressure to the relative decrease of volume, \(\alpha\) the coefficient of thermal expansion. The mean stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{m}}\) changes only the volume (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \tr\ten\varepsilon\)), the deviator \(\ten s\) only the shape, which the deviatoric strain \(\ten e = \ten\varepsilon - \tfrac13\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\mat I\) describes. Because the strain energy \(w = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) must be positive, \(E, G, K > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). At \(\nu = \tfrac12\) the material is incompressible (\(K \to \infty\)), which rubber comes close to.

In the book: sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.2.8, 3.3, 3.4 and 3.6.6.

A.1.6 Special states of stress

Uniaxial (a bar in tension or compression):

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\sigma}{E} , \quad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\frac{\sigma}{E} , \qquad \tau_{\max} = \frac{|\sigma|}{2} \ \text{at } 45^\circ .\]

The stress is uniaxial, the strain is not: the bar gets thinner (figure A.13).

Hydrostatic (a fluid at rest, a body under water):

\[\ten\sigma = -p\,\mat I , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = -\frac{p}{K} , \qquad \ten s = \mat 0 , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 0 .\]

There is no shear on any plane, and every direction is principal. Only the volume changes, not the shape, and the material does not yield (figure A.14).

Pure shear (for example a thin-walled tube in torsion), written in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(z\) as in figures A.12 and A.15:

\[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \tau_0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \tau_0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \sigma_1 = \tau_0 , \quad \sigma_2 = 0 , \quad \sigma_3 = -\tau_0 \quad (\tau_0 > 0) ,\]
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \frac{\tau_0}{2G} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0 , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt3\,\tau_0 .\]

The principal directions \((1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) and \((-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\) are the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\), the third is the axis \(y\), so pure shear is the same as a tension \(\tau_0\) and a compression \(\tau_0\) at \(45^\circ\). The volume does not change (figure A.15).

The formulas of the plane states below hold at constant temperature, \(\Delta T = 0\). The terms for \(\Delta T \neq 0\) are in the paragraph Temperature before generalized plane strain.

Plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje, RNS), a thin plate loaded in its own plane, with free faces (figure A.16):

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{\nu}{E}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]

The strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is in general not zero, so the thickness of the plate changes (BOWER §3.2.3, PDF p. 99, SADD (7.2.2), PDF p. 153). Plane stress is only an approximation, the better the thinner the plate (BARBER §3.2, PDF p. 56).

Plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje, RDS), an infinitely long prismatic body, or a finite one whose plane ends rest without friction on two rigid walls, loaded on its lateral surface by a load that does not depend on \(z\) and has no component \(z\), or a cylinder that cannot stretch axially (SADD, PDF pp. 149 and 151, BARBER §3.1, PDF p. 54):

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) , \quad u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) , \quad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{E\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 .\]

The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) prevents the axial elongation (SADD §7.1, (7.1.1), (7.1.3), PDF pp. 149-150, BOWER §3.2.3, PDF pp. 99 and 229). Plane strain is an exact solution of the equations of elasticity (BOWER, PDF p. 229, BARBER §3.2, PDF p. 56).

Temperature in the plane states. Let \(\Delta T\) be the change of temperature defined above and \(\alpha\) the coefficient of thermal expansion. The thermal strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) is added to every normal strain, so the stresses are determined only by the remainder, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) equals 1 for normal components and 0 for shear components. For plane stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - \nu} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}) - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - \nu} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\frac{\nu}{E}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) + \alpha\,\Delta T .\]

For plane strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) means that the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) also prevents the thermal expansion in the \(z\) direction:

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{E\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\big)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{E\,\big((1 - \nu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} ,\]
\[\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T = \frac{E\,\nu\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} - \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1 - 2\nu} .\]

A test of the sign: with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) zero, the body is held in all three directions, like the cube in a rigid box (figure 3.26), and all three formulas give the same stress \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\). It must be so, because none of the three directions is special. On heating (\(\Delta T > 0\)) the stress is therefore compressive in all three directions. The sign of the \(\Delta T\) term in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is the same as in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) (SADD §12.3.1, PDF p. 381, BARBER §14.1, PDF p. 234). The formulas follow from theorem 3.15 and were checked with SymPy. BOWER on PDF p. 99 has a plus sign at \(\sigma_{33}\), \(+E\alpha\Delta T/(1-2\nu)\), which is a misprint (the online edition at solidmechanics.org has the same), and the book does not follow it.

Generalized plane strain (generalizirano ravninsko deformacijsko stanje, GRDS), a long cylinder loaded on its lateral surface, with free ends or with a prescribed axial force:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0 = \text{const.} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) + E\,\varepsilon_0 .\]

The constant \(\varepsilon_0\) follows from the condition that the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm dA\) over the cross section \(A\) equals the prescribed axial force, zero for free ends (BOWER, PDF p. 233). An approximate solution for a cylinder with axially symmetric loading, which holds far from the ends, about three radii away (BOWER, PDF pp. 229-230).

In the book: sections 2.6.6, 2.11, 2.12.2, 2.12.3, 2.12.4 and 3.2.6 (plane stress). The book does not have plane strain yet, it will come in chapter 11.
