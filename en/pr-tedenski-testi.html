Appendix AWeekly tests

This page holds short answers to the questions of the weekly tests. Each answer is what one would write or say in a test in about five minutes: what the concept means, why we need it and where it stops holding, without derivations. Derivations, calculations and examples are in the sections of the book named in the line "In the book" under each answer. The concepts in the text are linked to their definitions, so a definition opens with one click. Each week we add a new section.

A.1 Test 1: general equations of elastostatics

A.1.1 Traction vector, stress and strain tensor, the elementary particle, equivalent stress

The traction vector describes the force with which one part of a body acts on another across an imagined cut. Through a point we place a small patch with a unit normal and shrink it to the point: the limit of the force on the patch divided by its area is the traction vector. Its unit is Pa, and it depends on the point and on the tilt of the patch, that is, on the normal. Its component along the normal is the normal stress (positive in tension), and the part that lies in the patch is the shear stress.

The stress tensor: at a point there are infinitely many traction vectors, one for each normal. Cauchy's formula says that three of them determine all the others, those on the faces perpendicular to the coordinate axes. Their nine components are the stress tensor. The first index tells the face (the axis the face is perpendicular to), the second the direction of the component. Components with equal indices are normal stresses, those with different indices shear stresses. Some books (CM) swap the indices. Because of the equilibrium of moments the tensor is symmetric, so six components are independent. The word tensor says that the components change by an exact rule when the axes turn, while the state of stress at the point stays the same.

The strain tensor describes how the material close to a point is stretched and sheared. Its normal components are the relative elongations of short fibres along the axes, its shear components half the decrease of the right angle between fibres along two axes. The engineering shear strain is twice the tensor one. This tensor too is symmetric and has six components.

The elementary particle is a small cube around a point with its faces parallel to the axes, which we cut out of the body in our minds. We draw the stress components on it and write its equilibrium. When we shrink the cube to the point, we get equations that hold at the point. This is the continuum idealization: we picture matter as filling space without gaps, without molecules and the empty space between them.

The equivalent stress is a single number computed from all the stress components that we compare with the yield stress from a tension test. In this way a state of stress in several directions is compared with a test in one direction. The von Mises equivalent stress measures only the deviatoric part, which changes the shape: in uniaxial tension it equals the stress in the bar, in pure shear it is \(\sqrt 3\) times the shear stress. The Tresca equivalent stress is the difference between the largest and the smallest principal stress, that is, twice the largest shear stress. Both are independent of a hydrostatic pressure, because metals yield by shear.

In the book: sections 2.5, 2.6, 2.6.5, 2.8, 2.9.6, 1.17.4, 2.7 and 2.12.5.

A.1.2 Equilibrium equations

A body is in equilibrium when every part of it is in equilibrium, every elementary particle too. On a particle act the stress forces on its six faces and a body force inside it, for example the weight. The stress is a function of position, so it differs a little on opposite faces of the particle. The equilibrium of forces in each direction requires that the differences of the forces on opposite faces together with the body force add up to zero. When we divide the equation by the volume and shrink the particle to a point, we get three first-order partial differential equations, one for each direction: the sum of the derivatives of the stress components that point in that direction, with respect to the matching coordinates, together with the body force per unit volume, is zero. The equilibrium of moments gives no new differential equation, but the symmetry of the stress tensor.

What they mean: there are three equations for six unknown stress components, so in general the stresses cannot be computed from equilibrium alone. We also need the relations between displacements and strains (or the compatibility conditions) and Hooke's law. On the surface of the body a boundary condition holds: the traction vector from Cauchy's formula must equal the prescribed surface load.

Validity: the equations hold for every material, since the relation between stress and strain was not used. For small deformations we write them on the shape of the body before loading. In motion, the density times the acceleration stands on the right side instead of zero. A test is the hanging bar: its stress falls linearly with depth, and its derivative balances the weight exactly. The equilibrium equations written at every point give, with the divergence theorem, also the equilibrium of every finite part of the body.

In the book: sections 2.7, 2.7.6, 2.8 and 4.2.7.

A.1.3 Relations between the displacement field and the strain components

The displacement field assigns to every point of a body its displacement, three components that are functions of the coordinates of the point. Strain tells how the displacement changes from point to point, so we get it from the partial derivatives of the displacements. The same displacement of all points, a move of the body as a whole, causes no strain.

The normal strain in the direction of the axis \(x\) is the derivative of the displacement component in the direction \(x\) with respect to \(x\). It tells by how much the displacements of two nearby points on a fibre along \(x\) differ, divided by the length of the fibre, that is, the relative elongation of that fibre. It is positive for an elongation and negative for a shortening. The shear strain between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) is half the sum of two derivatives: the derivative of the component \(x\) with respect to \(y\), which tells how much the fibre along \(y\) tilts towards the axis \(x\), and the derivative of the component \(y\) with respect to \(x\), which tells how much the fibre along \(x\) tilts towards the axis \(y\). The sum of the two tilts is the decrease of the right angle between the fibres, half of it is the tensor shear strain, the whole sum the engineering one.

The nine derivatives of the displacements split into a symmetric part, the small strain tensor, and an antisymmetric part, which describes a small rotation. A rotation does not lengthen the fibres, so it does not belong to the strain.

Validity: these linear relations hold only when all derivatives of the displacements are small compared with one, that is, for small strains and small rotations. For a large rotation they would predict elongations that are not there: a rigid rotation would have nonzero strains. The exact measure is the Green-Lagrangeov tensor, which has quadratic terms in the derivatives as well.

In the book: sections 1.17.1, 1.17.2, 1.17.3, 1.17.4, 1.17.5, 1.17.6 and 2.9.5.

A.1.4 The role of the compatibility equations

Why they exist: the six strain components are computed from only three functions, the components of the displacement. So they cannot be arbitrary, there are relations between them. Imagine that we cut the body into elementary particles, deform each by the prescribed strains and try to put them back together. For arbitrarily prescribed strains they would not fit: gaps or overlaps would appear.

What they are: the compatibility equations are the conditions under which the particles fit together into a continuous body without gaps or overlaps, that is, under which a continuous displacement field exists from which the strains come. They are six equations between second partial derivatives of the strain components (the Saint-Venant equations). We get them by eliminating the displacements from the relations between displacements and strains, using that the order of differentiation does not matter. In the plane there is a single equation.

When we need them: when we solve with stresses or strains as the unknowns, we must require compatibility separately, otherwise the stresses we get belong to no displacement field. When we solve with displacements, they hold by themselves. In a body without holes (a simply connected body) they are also sufficient. In a body with holes, a ring for example, further conditions are needed so that the displacements match after going once around the hole. Strains that are not compatible, for example from uneven heating, force the body into different strains and cause stresses without any external load.

In the book: sections 1.18.1, 1.18.3, 1.18.4 and 3.3.3.

A.1.5 Hooke's law and the meaning of the material constants

Hooke's law holds for a linear elastic material: the stresses are linear functions of the strains and the other way round, on unloading the material returns to its original shape, and the work of the load is stored as strain energy. It holds for small strains and for stresses below the yield stress.

In general every stress component is a linear combination of all six strain components. Of the 81 constants, 36 remain because the stresses and strains are symmetric, and 21 because a strain energy exists, which is the count for a fully anisotropic material. Symmetries of the material reduce the number: an orthotropic material has 9 constants, a transversely isotropic one 5, an isotropic one 2.

An isotropic material is described by two independent constants. Young's modulus \(E\) is the slope of the stress-strain line in uniaxial tension and measures the stiffness against stretching (196 GPa for steel). Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) is the ratio of the lateral contraction to the axial elongation in tension (about 0,3 for metals). The shear modulus \(G\) is the ratio of the shear stress to the engineering shear strain, \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\). The bulk modulus \(K\) is the ratio of the pressure to the relative decrease of volume, \(K = E/(3(1 - 2\nu))\). The Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu = G\) are convenient when the stresses are written in terms of the strains.

Physically: in an isotropic material normal stresses cause only normal strains, a shear stress only its own shear strain, and the principal directions of stress and strain coincide. The mean stress changes only the volume (through \(K\)), the deviator only the shape (through \(G\)). Because the strain energy must be positive, \(E\), \(G\) and \(K\) are positive and \(-1 < \nu < 1/2\). The value \(\nu = 1/2\) means an incompressible material, which rubber comes close to. A change of temperature adds a strain that is the same in all directions, the coefficient of thermal expansion times the change of temperature.

In the book: sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.2.8, 3.3, 3.4 and 3.6.6.

A.1.6 Special states of stress

Uniaxial stress: only one normal stress is nonzero, as in a bar that we pull or push. The principal stresses are this stress and two zeros. The strains, however, are not uniaxial: the bar gets longer and thinner, as Poisson's ratio tells. The largest shear stress is half the stress and acts on planes tilted by 45° to the axis.

Hydrostatic stress: the same normal stress acts in all directions, minus the pressure in compression, there is no shear on any plane, and every direction is principal. Such a state changes only the volume, not the shape. The deviator and the von Mises stress are zero, so the material does not yield. Examples are a fluid at rest and a body deep under water.

Pure shear: only shear stresses of equal size act on the faces of the cube. The principal stresses are the shear stress, zero and minus the shear stress, along the diagonals. So pure shear is the same as equal tension and compression in directions tilted by 45°. The volume does not change. An example is a thin-walled tube in torsion.

RNS, plane stress (ravninsko napetostno stanje): the stress components that involve the third direction are zero, that is, the normal stress in the third direction and both shear stresses with that direction. This is so in thin plates loaded in their own plane, because their two faces are free. The strain in the third direction is not zero, though: the thickness of the plate changes.

RDS, plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje): the strain components that involve the third direction are zero, and the displacements do not depend on that direction. This is so in a long prismatic body when the body forces and the loads on the side surface do not depend on the axial coordinate and have no axial component (SADD §7.1, PDF p. 149). An example is a long cylinder that cannot stretch axially at all: there this state is exact, throughout the cylinder (BOWER, PDF p. 229). The normal stress in the axial direction is not zero: for an isotropic material it is Poisson's ratio times the sum of the other two normal stresses, and it is exactly this stress that prevents the axial elongation (SADD (7.1.3), PDF p. 150).

GRDS, generalized plane strain (generalizirano ravninsko deformacijsko stanje): like plane strain, except that the axial strain is not zero but the same throughout the body, that is, constant. This is so in a long cylinder with free ends or with a prescribed axial force at its ends. The solution is approximate and holds far from the ends, about three radii away (BOWER, PDF pp. 229-230).

In the book: sections 2.6.6, 2.11, 2.12.2, 2.12.3, 2.12.4 and 3.2.6 (plane stress). The book does not have plane strain yet, it will come in chapter 11.
