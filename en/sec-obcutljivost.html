Chapter 29 Structural optimization

29.6 Sensitivity (občutljivost): how a function responds to an area

In section 29.5 we obtained the optimal areas from one condition that held for all bars at once. For conditions that such a condition does not cover, for example a limit on the deflection, we cannot guess the areas. The procedure of the next section corrects the areas step by step: at the current design it computes how the objective and each constraint change if the area of one bar is increased a little, and chooses new areas on that basis. So it needs, for the objective and for each constraint, the derivative with respect to each design variable. In this section we first show that these derivatives exist, then compute them in three ways and compare how much computation each needs and when it fails.

The model is the wall truss of the chapters on energy principles and on the finite element method for bars (figure 29.62, left). Bars \(AC\) and \(BC\) are fixed to the wall at points \(A\) and \(B\) and meet in the joint \(C\), on which the load \(F = 10\) kN acts downward. Bar \(AC\) is horizontal and \(4\) m long, bar \(BC\) is oblique and \(5\) m long. The design variables are the cross-section areas \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\), collected in the column \(\vek A = (A_1, A_2)\) with \(A_1 = A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_2 = A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). The design at which we compute is the design of section 7.5.2, \(A_1 = A_2 = 100\) mm², steel with \(E = 196\) GPa. The assumptions are the same as there: small displacements, linearly elastic material, the load \(F\) does not depend on the areas. The last assumption is an idealization: we neglect the self-weight of the bars. At \(100\) mm², a total bar length of \(9\) m and a steel density of \(7800\) kg/m³ the mass of both bars is \(7{,}02\) kg and their weight \(68{,}9\) N, which is \(0{,}69\,\%\) of the force \(F\). If we included it, the right-hand side of the equilibrium equation would depend on the areas. The letter \(C\) has two meanings here: the joint \(C\) and the compliance \(C(\vek A)\) of definition 29.32, which we always write with its argument (in chapter 10, \(\mat C\) is the damping matrix).

In the left half of the figure the load \(F\) pulls the joint \(C\) downward. Under it bar \(AC\) is compressed, \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}33\) kN, and bar \(BC\) is stretched, \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16{,}67\) kN. The right half is the same truss with a different load, which we need in the section on the adjoint method: a unit force \(1\) N downward at the joint \(C\) and the forces in the bars under it.

The function that interests us is the deflection of the joint \(C\) downward, \(g = -v_{\mathrm{C}}\), where \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) is the vertical displacement. At our design it is, by section 7.5.2, \(g = 10{,}714\) mm. The limit on this deflection will be the constraint of our problem in the next section.

The displacements of the free unknowns, the horizontal and vertical displacement \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}})\) of the joint \(C\), are determined by the equilibrium equation of theorem 7.18, in which the structure matrix (theorem 7.13) depends on the areas. By lemma 7.28, written for the free unknowns, bar \(e\) contributes the matrix \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) with

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{E}{L_{\mathrm{e}}}\,\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T , \tag{29.32}\]

where \(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the column for which the elongation of the bar is \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek u\) (for our bar, fixed to the wall with one end, \(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\) at the joint \(C\), with the unit vector \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the bar, directed from the wall towards \(C\)). In figure 29.63 the joint moves by \(\vek u\) from \(C\) to \(C'\), and the bars move from their old position to the new one. For small displacements only the part of the displacement along the bar changes its length, the part cut off by the perpendicular from \(C'\): this is the scalar product \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek u\), for bar \(AC\) \(-2{,}72\) mm, for bar \(BC\) \(4{,}25\) mm. The part of the displacement perpendicular to the bar turns it by an angle \(\theta\) and lengthens it only by about \(L_{\mathrm{e}}\theta^2/2\), for bar \(AC\) (\(\theta = 0{,}0027\) rad) by \(0{,}014\) mm, which is \(0{,}5\,\%\) of the elongation, so we neglect it. The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not depend on the areas, so

\[\mat K(\vek A)\,\vek u = \vek f , \qquad \mat K(\vek A) = A_1\,\mat K_1 + A_2\,\mat K_2 . \tag{29.33}\]

Hence \(\partial\mat K/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\). In the nested form of the problem (definition 29.2) \(\vek u\) is a function of the areas, so every function \(g(\vek A, \vek u)\) of displacements and areas is also a function of the areas alone, \(\hat g(\vek A) = g(\vek A, \vek u(\vek A))\).

Figure 29.64 draws the deflection \(g\) as a function of one area, \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\), with \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 100\) mm². On the axes the area of bar \(AC\) is horizontal and the deflection vertical. The curve falls, because more material means a smaller deflection. The point is our design, \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 100\) mm² and \(g = 10{,}714\) mm. If we increase the area by the step \(h\), the deflection falls, and the secant through the two points has the slope \(\Delta g/h\). The shorter the step, the more the secant turns toward the tangent at the design point, and its slope is the sensitivity. At the end of the step the tangent lies below the curve by a small remainder, which falls faster than the step. The secant across both sides, from \(A - h\) to \(A + h\), is closer still to the tangent. At a small area, where the curve is steep, the tangent is steep too: there the function responds strongly to the area.

Definition 29.40 Sensitivity (občutljivost)

Let \(\vek A = (A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{n}})\) be the column of design variables (definition 29.1), \(\vek u(\vek A)\) the solution of equation (29.33) at \(\vek A\) and \(g(\vek A, \vek u)\) a function whose input is \(n\) areas and \(m\) displacements and whose output is a number, continuously differentiable in all inputs. The sensitivity (občutljivost) of the function \(\hat g(\vek A) = g(\vek A, \vek u(\vek A))\) to the design variable \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\) at the design \(\vek A\) is the partial derivative, in figure 29.64 the slope of the tangent at the design point,

\[\frac{\partial\hat g}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}}(\vek A) = \lim_{\mathrm{h}\to 0}\frac{\hat g(\vek A + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{e}}) - \hat g(\vek A)}{h} , \tag{29.34}\]

where \(\vek e_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the column with a one at place \(e\) and zeros elsewhere, provided that the limit exists. It is a number with the unit of the function \(g\) per m². The sensitivities to all variables form the column \(\nabla\hat g = (\partial\hat g/\partial A_1, \dots, \partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{n}})\).

Source: CHK section 6 (PDF p. 103): calls the derivatives of the objective and constraint functions with respect to the design variables "sensitivities" and the procedure "sensitivity analysis". The notation \(\hat g\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) is ours.

The word says how sensitive the function is to a change of the variable: a large derivative means that a small change of the area changes the function strongly, as at the steep tangent at a small area. The word must not be confused with the condition number \(\kappa(\mat K)\) of definition 6.31. That is a property of a matrix and tells how errors of the right-hand side are amplified when the system is solved. The sensitivity of this definition is the derivative of a function with respect to an area. The words that follow them tell them apart: "condition number of a matrix", "sensitivity of a function to an area".

29.6.1 Finite differences

The simplest way is to really change the design and see what happens. We increase the area \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\) by a small number \(h > 0\), solve equation (29.33) again, compute \(\hat g\) and divide the difference by \(h\). This is the forward difference quotient (količnik naprej)

\[D_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{\hat g(\vek A + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{e}}) - \hat g(\vek A)}{h} . \tag{29.35}\]

By definition 29.40 it approximates the sensitivity for small \(h\). How small is told by the next lemma, and we compare its estimate with a computation on our truss in figure 29.67.

Figure 29.65 shows the second source of error. On the axes the area is horizontal and the deflection vertical, strongly magnified around one value. The computer computes the deflection at the initial design and at the step \(h\), the two points. Each is off from the true value by at most \(\delta\), so the true value lies in a band around it. The difference, which we divide by \(h\), can therefore be wrong by \(2\delta\). At a small \(h\) the true difference is comparable with this error and the quotient is spoiled.

Figure 29.66 puts both sources of error on one line. On the line of slopes, on the left, is the slope of the tangent \(\varphi'(0)\) that we seek. The secant has the slope \(D_{\mathrm{h}}\), the truncation distance to the right of it, because on our truss the deflection curve bends upward. The computed quotient \(\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}}\) lies the round-off distance away from \(D_{\mathrm{h}}\), in either direction. If it goes the same way as the truncation, the total error is the sum of the two distances, if it goes the opposite way, it is shorter. It is never longer than the sum, so \(\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}}\) never falls beyond the bound, the sum of the two largest distances.

Lemma 29.41 Error of the forward difference quotient (napaka količnika naprej)

Let \(\varphi(t) = \hat g(\vek A + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{e}})\) for \(t \in [0, h]\) be a function (the curve of the deflection over the step) with continuous derivative \(\varphi'\) on \([0, h]\) and with a derivative \(\varphi''\) on \((0, h)\), and let \(|\varphi''| \le M\) on \((0, h)\). Let each of the computed values \(\varphi(0)\) and \(\varphi(h)\) be wrong by at most \(\delta > 0\) (the round-off error in solving equation (29.33)) and \(M > 0\). Then for the quotient \(\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the two computed values, with the subtraction and the division by \(h\) taken as exact,

\[\big|\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}} - \varphi'(0)\big| \le \tfrac12\,M\,h + \frac{2\,\delta}{h} . \tag{29.36}\]

For \(h > 0\) the right-hand side is smallest at \(h_* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M}\) and there it equals \(2\sqrt{\delta\,M}\).

Idea

The error has two sources. The difference \(\varphi(h) - \varphi(0)\) replaces the derivative by a secant, which differs from the tangent by the remainder, which is the truncation error, growing with \(h\) (by theorem 8.68). But the computed values are inexact, and their difference is divided by \(h\), so the round-off error grows as \(h\) falls.

Proof

We must show estimate (29.36) and its minimum.

  1. By theorem 8.68 for \(f = \varphi\), \(x_0 = 0\), \(x = h\) there is a point \(c \in (0, h)\) with \(\varphi(h) = \varphi(0) + \varphi'(0)\,h + \tfrac12\varphi''(c)\,h^2\). Divide by \(h\) and compare the secant with the tangent, whose remainder is \(\tfrac12\varphi''(c)h^2\): the exact quotient \(D_{\mathrm{h}} = \big(\varphi(h) - \varphi(0)\big)/h\) satisfies \(D_{\mathrm{h}} - \varphi'(0) = \tfrac12\varphi''(c)\,h\), in absolute value at most \(\tfrac12 M h\), in figure 29.66 the truncation distance.
  2. The computed values differ from the exact ones by at most \(\delta\) each, so the computed difference differs from the exact one by at most \(2\delta\) and the quotient by at most \(2\delta/h\), that is \(|\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}} - D_{\mathrm{h}}| \le 2\delta/h\), the round-off distance.
  3. The triangle inequality for \(\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}} - \varphi'(0) = (\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}} - D_{\mathrm{h}}) + (D_{\mathrm{h}} - \varphi'(0))\) says that the total error is not longer than the sum of the truncation distance and the round-off distance, and equal to it only when both go the same way (otherwise it is shorter). With the bounds of steps 1 and 2 this gives (29.36), the bound on the line of slopes. In figure 29.67 the estimate is the sum of the two terms.
  4. The right-hand side \(r(h) = \tfrac12 M h + 2\delta/h\) has derivative \(r'(h) = \tfrac12 M - 2\delta/h^2\), which is zero at \(h_*^2 = 4\delta/M\), that is \(h_* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M}\), in figure 29.67 the best step. The second derivative \(r''(h) = 4\delta/h^3\) is positive, so this is the minimum, \(r(h_*) = \sqrt{\delta M} + \sqrt{\delta M} = 2\sqrt{\delta M}\).

Source: CHK section 6.1 (PDF pp. 103-104): the truncation error \(O(h)\) and the error from the cancellation of numbers, which for small \(h\) grows "dramatically", equation (6.1). The estimate (29.36) with the sum of both and \(h_*\) is not from the source.

We test the lemma on the truss. The area of bar \(AC\) is the only variable, the rest stays at \(100\) mm². The truss is statically determinate, so the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) are fixed by equilibrium, and by theorem 4.96 the deflection is \(\varphi(t) = c_1/(A_1 + t) + c_2/A_2\) with \(c_{\mathrm{e}} = \hat N_{\mathrm{e}} N_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}/E\), where \(\hat N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -4/3\) and \(\hat N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5/3\) are the forces under a unit force downward at the joint \(C\) (section 4.1.4). So \(c_1 = (4/3)(13\,333)(4)/(196 \cdot 10^9) = 3{,}628 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m³ and \(c_2 = 7{,}086 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m³. Hence \(\varphi'(0) = -c_1/A_1^2 = -36{,}281\) m/m² and \(\varphi''(t) = 2c_1/(A_1 + t)^3\), which falls with \(t\), so \(M = \varphi''(0) = 7{,}256 \cdot 10^{5}\) m⁻³. We take the round-off error \(\delta = 1{,}11 \cdot 10^{-16}\,\varphi(0) = 1{,}19 \cdot 10^{-18}\) m. This is a choice that the computation confirms: at all eight steps we tried, the measured error is below the estimate. We measure the step relatively, \(s = h/A_1\), and the error relatively, \(r = |\tilde D_{\mathrm{h}} - \varphi'(0)|/|\varphi'(0)|\). The truncation term is then \(\tfrac12 M h/|\varphi'(0)| = s\), and the round-off term is \(k/s\) with \(k = 2\delta/(A_1|\varphi'(0)|) = 6{,}56 \cdot 10^{-16}\).

Figure 29.67 draws all eight steps. On the horizontal axis is the step \(s\), on the vertical one the error \(r\), both on a logarithmic scale: equal distances on an axis mean equal factors. On such a scale the functions \(r = s\) and \(r = k/s\) are straight lines. At large steps the measured error agrees with the truncation term, which is \(r = s\). At small steps the round-off term takes over, \(r = k/s\), which grows as the step falls. The estimate is their sum, and the measured points lie below it. The smallest estimate is at the step \(s_* = \sqrt{k} = 2{,}6 \cdot 10^{-8}\), that is \(h_* = 2{,}56 \cdot 10^{-12}\) m², and the estimate is there \(2\sqrt{k} = 5{,}1 \cdot 10^{-8}\). At this step the estimate guarantees about seven correct digits of the sensitivity. The measured error at \(s = 10^{-8}\), \(8{,}6 \cdot 10^{-9}\), is somewhat smaller still, because the estimate is only an upper bound.

The lemma says what the step should be, and the test shows what happens at a wrong one. Take first a step that is too small, \(s = 10^{-14}\), at the far left of figure 29.67, where the round-off term dominates. The truncation term there is only \(10^{-14}\), and by it alone we would get the derivative almost exactly. But the difference \(\varphi(h) - \varphi(0) = -36{,}28 \cdot 10^{-18}\) m is only \(3{,}4 \cdot 10^{-15}\) of the value of the deflection. In absolute value it is only \(30\) times the round-off error \(\delta = 1{,}19 \cdot 10^{-18}\) m of each of the two values, so the error of the difference can be up to \(2\delta/(36{,}28 \cdot 10^{-18}\ \text{m}) = 6{,}6\,\%\) of the difference. This is the round-off term \(k/s\) of the table. The measured error is \(4{,}1 \cdot 10^{-3}\), which is \(4{,}8 \cdot 10^{5}\) times more than the best measured one at \(s = 10^{-8}\). The truncation term alone does not predict this, because it does not include round-off. A step that is too small is therefore an error that no further reduction of the step removes, it only makes it larger.

At a step that is too large, \(s = 10^{-1}\), the error is of another kind and we see its cause at once: we replaced the derivative by a secant across \(10\,\%\) of the area. For the function \(c/A\) we can even write it exactly: \(\big(c/(A(1+s)) - c/A\big)/h = -c/(A^2(1+s))\), so the relative error is \(1 - 1/(1+s) = s/(1+s) = 0{,}0909\). It is smaller than the estimate \(s\) because \(\varphi''\) falls.

The difference across both sides, \(D_{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{c}} = \big(\hat g(\vek A + h\vek e_{\mathrm{e}}) - \hat g(\vek A - h\vek e_{\mathrm{e}})\big)/(2h)\), has a truncation error of order \(h^2\) (CHK, equation (6.2), PDF p. 104, stated there without proof), and on the same truss at \(s = 10^{-4}\) it is exact to \(1{,}0 \cdot 10^{-8}\) instead of \(1{,}0 \cdot 10^{-4}\). But it costs two solutions per variable instead of one. A gradient with \(n\) variables needs \(n\) extra solutions of equation (29.33) with the forward difference, \(2n\) with the difference across both sides, besides the solution at the design itself. CHK (PDF p. 104) says of both that they can be very inexact and expensive, but are easy to write down. The two analytic ways are therefore treated by the statements below.

29.6.2 The direct method

Figure 29.68 shows what we shall differentiate. The two bars have fixed lengths and the joint \(C\) without load is at the origin. Under the load it moves by \(\vek u\), which the equilibrium equation determines at the current areas. If we increase the area of bar \(AC\), the joint moves further by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{AC}}\), and if we increase the area of bar \(BC\), by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{BC}}\). Both arrows are derivatives of the displacement with respect to an area and are much smaller than \(\vek u\). The solution for \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\) is an equilibrium in which a pseudo-load \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\) acts in place of the load.

Instead of changing the design, we differentiate the equilibrium equation and so obtain the derivative of the displacement without changing the design. If both sides are equal for every \(\vek A\), so are their derivatives. For this we must know that \(\vek u\) as a function of the areas has a derivative at all, and that is what the implicit function theorem guarantees.

Figure 29.69 shows why such a derivative exists. The equilibrium equation has two rows, and each is a line in the plane of displacements \((u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}})\). The first row, the balance of forces on the joint \(C\) in the horizontal direction, passes through the origin, because there is no horizontal load. The second row is the balance in the vertical direction. The solution \(\vek u\) is the intersection, and since the lines are not parallel, there is exactly one. When we increase the area of bar \(AC\), the first line turns about the origin, the second stays, and the intersection slides along the second line. At a step of \(100\) mm² it moves by \((1{,}36,\ 1{,}81)\) mm. At a smaller step the shift divided by the step comes ever closer to the derivative \(\vek w_{\mathrm{AC}} = (0{,}0272,\ 0{,}0363)\) mm/mm². On the right we decrease the area of bar \(BC\): the first line becomes steeper, the second moves away parallel to itself, and the intersection slides down. At \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\) the second row has no unknowns left, \(0\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + 0\,v_{\mathrm{C}} = -10\,000\) N, and there is no intersection.

Lemma 29.42 Direct method (neposredna metoda)

Let \(\mat K(\vek A) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) with given symmetric matrices \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) of order \(m\), the right-hand side \(\vek f(\vek A)\) continuously differentiable and \(\vek A^*\) a design at which \(\mat K(\vek A^*)\) is positive definite. Then in a neighbourhood of \(\vek A^*\) the solution \(\vek u(\vek A)\) of the equation \(\mat K(\vek A)\,\vek u = \vek f(\vek A)\) is a continuously differentiable function of the areas, and its derivative \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial\vek u/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}\) at \(\vek A^*\) (the arrow of the shift per area) solves a system with the same matrix,

\[\mat K\,\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek p_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \vek p_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek u . \tag{29.37}\]

For the function \(g(\vek A, \vek u)\) of definition 29.40 it follows that \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} + (\partial g/\partial\vek u)\,\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(\partial g/\partial\vek u\) is the row with entries \(\partial g/\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Idea

Solving \(\vek F(\vek A, \vek u) = \mat K(\vek A)\,\vek u - \vek f(\vek A) = \vek 0\) for the unknowns \(\vek u\) at given \(\vek A\) (the displacement) is exactly a case of the implicit function theorem, whose pivot condition is ensured by positive definiteness.

Proof

We must show that theorem 8.35 applies to \(\vek F\), and that its derivative (8.21) gives (29.37).

  1. The points \((\vek A, \vek u)\) have \(n + m\) coordinates. The term \(\mat K(\vek A)\vek u\) is a polynomial in \(\vek A\) and \(\vek u\), \(\vek f\) is continuously differentiable, so \(\vek F\) is continuously differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\). The point \((\vek A^*, \vek u^*)\) with \(\vek u^* = \mat K(\vek A^*)^{-1}\vek f(\vek A^*)\) satisfies \(\vek F = \vek 0\): \(\vek u^*\) is the equilibrium displacement at \(\vek A^*\).
  2. The matrix \(\partial\vek F/\partial\vek u = \mat K(\vek A)\) has, by theorem 6.29, all \(m\) pivots (definition 8.21) at \(\vek A^*\), because it is positive definite. For \(m = 2\) this means that the lines of the rows are not parallel and meet in one point. The condition of theorem 8.35 is met.
  3. The theorem gives a continuously differentiable \(\vek u(\vek A)\) (the intersection moves continuously when a line turns, its derivative is the arrow of the shift) near \(\vek A^*\) with \(\vek F(\vek A, \vek u(\vek A)) = \vek 0\), and by equation (8.21), \(\partial\vek u/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -\mat K^{-1}\,\partial\vek F/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  4. Column \(e\) of the matrix \(\partial\vek F/\partial\vek A\) is \(\partial\vek F/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek u - \partial\vek f/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\), because \(\partial\mat K/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\). Hence \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat K^{-1}\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\), which is (29.37).
  5. The function \(\hat g(\vek A) = g(\vek A, \vek u(\vek A))\) is composed of continuously differentiable maps. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30) and the shift of the joint per unit area, \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (\partial g/\partial u_{\mathrm{r}})\,\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}\), which is the last claim.

Source: CHK section 6.2.1 (PDF pp. 104-105), equation (6.4) and the chain rule (6.3). CHK obtains equation (29.37) by differentiating the equilibrium equation. The proof that the derivative exists, by the implicit function theorem, is not from the source.

The right-hand side \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\) has the form of a load on the joints, so CHK calls it a pseudo-load (navidezna obremenitev): equation (29.37) is the equilibrium equation, only that instead of the force \(\vek F\) the load \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\) acts on the joint \(C\). When the matrix \(\mat K\) is factorized (chapter 7), each such solution takes only a forward and a back substitution (CHK, PDF pp. 104-105), but it must be done for each of the \(n\) areas.

On our truss \(\vek f = (0, -10\,000)\) N, independent of the areas, so \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek u\) with \(\vek u = (-2{,}7211, -10{,}7143)\) mm. Two solutions of equation (29.37) give the arrows \(\vek w_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{BC}}\)

\[\vek w_{\mathrm{AC}} = (0{,}027211,\ 0{,}036281)\ \text{mm/mm}^2 , \qquad \vek w_{\mathrm{BC}} = (0,\ 0{,}070862)\ \text{mm/mm}^2 .\]

When the area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) is increased a little by \(\Delta A\), the joint \(C\) moves about \(0{,}027\,\Delta A\) mm/mm² to the right and \(0{,}036\,\Delta A\) mm/mm² upward, and the deflection decreases: \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{AC}} = -0{,}036281\) mm/mm² \(= -36{,}281\) m/m². The sensitivity to \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) is \(-70{,}862\) m/m². The zero in \(\vek w_{\mathrm{BC}}\) is no accident: by the unit force method (theorem 4.96, \(\hat N_{\mathrm{AC}} = 1\), \(\hat N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\)) the horizontal displacement of the joint \(C\) equals the elongation of bar \(AC\), which does not depend on \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\).

29.6.3 The adjoint method

The direct method solves one system per variable, so one arrow per area, even when we want the sensitivity of a single function. We turn the question around: instead of "how does \(\vek u\) change with \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\)" we ask "how does one function \(g\) respond to each displacement". The answer is one solution for all \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Theorem 29.43 Adjoint method (prirejena metoda)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\vek f\) and \(\vek A^*\) be as in lemma 29.42 (so \(\mat K\) is positive definite at \(\vek A^*\)) and \(g(\vek A, \vek u)\) as in definition 29.40. Let \(\vek\lambda\) be the solution of the adjoint system (prirejeni sistem, the displacement under the unit force)

\[\mat K\,\vek\lambda = \Big(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\vek u}\Big)^\T . \tag{29.38}\]

Then at \(\vek A^*\), for every \(e\),

\[\frac{\partial\hat g}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}} + \vek\lambda^\T\Big(\frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek u\Big) , \tag{29.39}\]

and for all \(n\) areas only one system (29.38) is needed for each function \(g\).

Idea

The derivative \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\) of lemma 29.42 enters the sensitivity only through the row \(\partial g/\partial\vek u\). If this row already contains \(\mat K\), which multiplies \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\) into \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\), we arrive at \(\vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\) without \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\), with a unit force in place of the arrows.

Proof

We must show that the row \(\partial g/\partial\vek u\) can be written as \(\vek\lambda^\T\mat K\), which eliminates \(\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\).

  1. The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite, so by theorem 6.29 system (29.38) has exactly one solution \(\vek\lambda\) for every right-hand side: a unit force has a single response.
  2. The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric (corollary 7.14), so transposing equation (29.38) gives \(\partial g/\partial\vek u = \vek\lambda^\T\mat K\).
  3. By lemma 29.42, \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} + (\partial g/\partial\vek u)\,\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek\lambda^\T\,\mat K\,\vek w_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  4. By equation (29.37), \(\mat K\,\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek p_{\mathrm{e}} = \partial\vek f/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek u\). Substituting gives (29.39).
  5. The vector \(\vek\lambda\) does not depend on \(e\), so one system (29.38) serves all variables, one unit load for all areas.

Source: CHK section 6.2.2 (PDF pp. 105-106), equations (6.7) and (6.8), where the proof is by substituting (6.4) into (6.6). The comparison of costs (PDF p. 106) we summarize below. The notation with \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) is ours.

The name comes from the matrix of system (29.38) being the adjoint (transposed) matrix of the system of the direct method. For symmetric \(\mat K\) they are the same, as for every truss of ours, but in general it would be the transpose. Let us compare costs. The direct method solves \(n\) systems \(\mat K\vek w_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek p_{\mathrm{e}}\), one for each area (an arrow each). The adjoint method solves one (unit load) for each function that we differentiate, that is for the objective and each of the \(l\) constraints, in all \(l + 1\). For a truss with \(n = 100\) bars and one deflection constraint this is \(100\) against \(2\) solutions with a factorized matrix. CHK (PDF p. 106) summarizes: the adjoint method is better when there are fewer constraints than design variables, otherwise the direct one. On our truss the objective is the volume \(V = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(\partial V/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = L_{\mathrm{e}}\) and no system, so the adjoint method needs one system for the deflection, the direct method two, and at \(n = 2\) the advantage is not yet large.

The theorem holds when the matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite, and for a truss we lose it when the area of a bar drops to zero. Let us test it: we reduce the area of bar \(BC\) and \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 100\) mm² stays. Bar \(AC\) is horizontal and cannot carry a vertical force, so the joint \(C\) moves more and more. The deflection and the sensitivity to \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\), computed by theorem 29.43 and from the closed form \(-c_2/A_{\mathrm{BC}}^2\) (both give the same), are in the table and on the curve of figure 29.70, drawn on logarithmic axes, and the rows of the table are the four points:

\(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) in mm² deflection \(g\) in mm \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) in mm/mm²
\(100\) \(10{,}7\) \(-0{,}0709\)
\(10\) \(74{,}5\) \(-7{,}09\)
\(1\) \(712\) \(-709\)
\(0{,}1\) \(7090\) \(-70\,862\)

Each row (a step along the curve) has a \(10\) times smaller area and a \(100\) times larger sensitivity, as for \(1/A^2\). At \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), \(\mat K = A_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{AC}}\) has a zero second row and column, it is singular (determinant zero, in figure 29.69 the intersection escapes), the adjoint system (29.38) has no solution and the theorem does not apply. The lower two points have deflections of \(0{,}71\) m and \(7{,}1\) m on a truss with a span of \(4\) m, where the small-displacement model of section 7.5.2 no longer holds, the region above the limit. The formula therefore says only that the sensitivity grows beyond all bounds as the bar vanishes, not what the deflection of such a bar would really be. Section 29.5 has the same difficulty for bars whose area falls toward zero.

29.6.4 Sensitivity of the compliance

For the compliance \(C(\vek A) = \vek f^\T\vek u\) (definition 29.32), the work of the load on the displacement of the joint, the function \(g = \vek f^\T\vek u\) is itself part of the equilibrium equation, and the theorem will give a special, simple result.

Corollary 29.44 Sensitivity of the compliance (občutljivost podajnosti)

Let \(\vek f\) be independent of the areas and \(\mat K(\vek A) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) positive definite at \(\vek A\), with \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) from equation (29.32). Let bar \(e\) have the elongation \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}} = \Delta L_{\mathrm{e}}/L_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then

\[\frac{\partial C}{\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}} = -\,\vek u^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek u = -E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\,L_{\mathrm{e}} = -\frac{2\,U_{\mathrm{e}}}{A_{\mathrm{e}}} , \tag{29.40}\]

where \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12 E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\,A_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the strain energy of bar \(e\). The sensitivity is negative for every bar: more material in any bar decreases the compliance.

Idea

For \(g = \vek f^\T\vek u\) the adjoint system is the equilibrium equation, so the adjoint solution is \(\vek u\) itself, since the unit force is replaced by the actual load, and we need no new system.

Proof

We must show that theorem 29.43 gives \(\vek\lambda = \vek u\), and then express \(\vek u^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek u\) by the elongation.

  1. For \(g(\vek A, \vek u) = \vek f^\T\vek u\), \(\partial g/\partial\vek A = \vek 0\) and \(\partial g/\partial\vek u = \vek f^\T\). The adjoint system (29.38) is \(\mat K\vek\lambda = \vek f\), which is the equilibrium equation under the actual load, so \(\vek\lambda = \vek u\).
  2. Since \(\partial\vek f/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), equation (29.39) gives \(\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -\vek u^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek u\).
  3. By equation (29.32), \(\vek u^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek u = (E/L_{\mathrm{e}})\,(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek u)^2 = (E/L_{\mathrm{e}})\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{e}}^2\), because \(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek u\) is the elongation of the bar, the projection of the displacement onto the direction of the bar. With \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{e}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}\) this is \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\,L_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  4. By definition 4.2 for the bar with \(w = \tfrac12 E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) constant along the bar, \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2A_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2L_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}}/A_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: CHK examples 6.1 and 6.2 (PDF pp. 105-106): equation (6.5) at \(\partial\vek f/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) and the conclusion \(\vek\lambda = \vek u\). The expression with the elongation and with the energy of the bar is not from the source.

By theorem 29.37, at minimum compliance \(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\) is the same in all bars with \(A_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\). By equation (29.40) this is \(-\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}\) divided by \(L_{\mathrm{e}}\): every bar with \(A_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) gives, at the optimum, the same decrease of compliance per unit volume, \(-\partial C/\partial(A_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}) = E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}^2\).

Example 29.45 Sensitivities of the wall truss (občutljivosti stenskega paličja)

The wall truss of the introduction of this section has, by section 7.5.2, the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}33\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16{,}67\) kN and the compliance \(C = 107{,}14\) J. Let \(g = -v_{\mathrm{C}}\) be the deflection. The adjoint system (29.38) is \(\mat K\vek\lambda = (0, -1)^\T\), which is the equilibrium system with a unit force \(1\) N downward at the joint \(C\): the solution is \(\vek\lambda = \vek u/(10\,000\ \text{N}) = (-2{,}7211, -10{,}7143)\cdot 10^{-7}\) m/N. The adjoint load is thus the unit force of the unit force method (theorem 4.96, \(\vek d = -\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\)), and the axial forces are, by the right half of the figure, \(\hat N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -4/3\) and \(\hat N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5/3\), per unit force.

  1. The displacements \(\vek\lambda\) are the displacements under the unit force, for which bar \(e\) carries the force \(\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}\). The elongation of a bar is its force times \(L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}})\), so \(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek\lambda = \hat N_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}})\) and \(\vek b_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek u = N_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}})\). By equation (29.32), \(\vek\lambda^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek u = (E/L_{\mathrm{e}})\,(\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}}))\,(N_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}})) = \hat N_{\mathrm{e}}\,N_{\mathrm{e}}\,L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}}^2)\) and by equation (29.39) \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}\,N_{\mathrm{e}}\,L_{\mathrm{e}}/(E\,A_{\mathrm{e}}^2)\).
  2. Bar \(AC\): \(-(-4/3)(-13\,333)(4)/(196\cdot 10^9\cdot (10^{-4})^2) = -36{,}281\) m/m². Bar \(BC\): \(-(5/3)(16\,667)(5)/(196\cdot 10^9\cdot(10^{-4})^2) = -70{,}862\) m/m². The same numbers were given by the direct method and the adjoint equation (29.39), and a third time by the closed form: for a truss whose forces are fixed by equilibrium, \(g = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \hat N_{\mathrm{e}}N_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}})\), and its derivative with respect to \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the one above.
  3. A check. Multiply all areas by \(s\) (for one area this is a move along the curve of the deflection): the matrix \(\mat K\) is multiplied by \(s\), the solution \(\vek u\) is divided by \(s\), so \(g(s\vek A) = g(\vek A)/s\). By the chain rule at \(s = 1\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}\,\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -g\): \(100\cdot(-0{,}036281) + 100\cdot(-0{,}070862) = -10{,}714\) mm, which is \(-g\).
  4. Compliance from the strains of the bars: \(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AC}}^2 = 196 \cdot 10^9 \cdot (-6{,}803 \cdot 10^{-4})^2 = 90{,}70\) kJ/m³ and \(E\varepsilon_{\mathrm{BC}}^2 = 196\cdot 10^9\cdot(8{,}503\cdot 10^{-4})^2 = 141{,}72\) kJ/m³. By equation (29.40), \(\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{AC}} = -362\,812\) J/m² \(= -0{,}3628\) J/mm² and \(\partial C/\partial A_{\mathrm{BC}} = -0{,}7086\) J/mm², which is \(F\,\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}}\) (\(F = 10\) kN). The check with \(s\): \(100\cdot(-0{,}3628 - 0{,}7086) = -107{,}14\) J \(= -C\).

For this truss the expression \(\hat N_{\mathrm{e}} N_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}}^2)\) is computed without solving anything, because \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}\) are fixed by equilibrium. For a statically indeterminate truss, in figure 29.71 with an extra bar, the forces depend on the areas: with a thin extra bar almost all the load is carried by \(AC\) and \(BC\), with a thick one it is taken by \(BC\) and the extra bar, and \(AC\) stays unstressed. The deflection there is no longer a sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}} c_{\mathrm{e}}/A_{\mathrm{e}}\) with constant \(c_{\mathrm{e}}\), and we do not get the forces \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}\) without solving the systems for \(\vek u\) and \(\vek\lambda\). The adjoint equation (29.39) holds there too, since it uses only the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the solutions \(\vek u\) and \(\vek\lambda\). Step 1 of the example uses only the elongations of the bar under these two solutions, so \(\partial\hat g/\partial A_{\mathrm{e}} = -\hat N_{\mathrm{e}}N_{\mathrm{e}}L_{\mathrm{e}}/(EA_{\mathrm{e}}^2)\) holds there as well, with the two forces computed from \(\vek u\) and \(\vek\lambda\).

We now have the derivative of the deflection and of the compliance with respect to each area, and we compute it without repeating the whole computation for each area separately. The next section uses these derivatives to correct the design step by step.
