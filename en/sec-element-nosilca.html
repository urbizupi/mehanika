Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.1 The beam element (element nosilca)

Chapter 6 showed that the deflection of a beam with a kink has no finite energy (section 6.7.4): when the length of the rounding of the kink tends to zero, the energy tends to infinity. A deflection made of straight lines is therefore not usable for a beam. Chapter 5, however, for a beam clamped at one end, by inverting the flexibility matrix of the cantilever, computed the stiffnesses \(12EI/L^3\), \(6EI/L^2\) and \(4EI/L\) (lemma 5.98). Which fields on one piece of beam continue onto the neighbouring piece with a continuous slope, and which matrix ties the forces and couples at the ends of the piece to the displacements and rotations of the ends?

We treat the Euler-Bernoulli beam model (definition 5.14) in the plane \(xy\) with the axis through the centroids of the cross-sections. We do not look at the axial force in this section (the axial part is the bar of chapter 7, and we join it to the bending part for a frame). The shear force \(V\) and the moment \(M\) have the signs of definitions 5.7 and 5.8, so \(V\) and \(M\) in a cross-section mean the force and the couple with which the part of the beam at larger \(x\) acts on the part at smaller \(x\). The model stands on three assumptions. Let us draw them before we use them.

On the straight axis of the beam we mark one cross-section, an upright line across the beam. When we bend the beam into the orange curve, the cross-section goes with it and stays perpendicular to the bent axis. This is the first assumption. At this point the axis has a tangent with slope \(v'\). The cross-section is perpendicular to it, so it rotates from the vertical by the same angle that the tangent makes with the horizontal. The rotation of a cross-section is therefore \(\varphi = v'\) as in chapter 5 and is positive counterclockwise. Why is this right here? The cross-section could lag behind the perpendicular by the shear angle \(\gamma\). For a slender beam this angle is small: for the cantilever of our running example, 1 m long, the shear part of the deflection is by section 5.13.4 only 0,125 % of the bending part. When does the assumption fail? For a short and deep beam: for the same bar, 80 mm long, the shear part is already 19,5 % of the bending part. What does it do to the result? An element that does not know the angle \(\gamma\) computes the deflection without this shear part, so it is too stiff. Section 12.7.5 removes this assumption.

The second assumption is small displacements and rotations: we write the slope \(v'\) in place of the angle, although the slope is the tangent of the angle. It is right because the rotations of steel beams are small: the end of our cantilever rotates by 0,012, and there the tangent differs from the angle by 0,005 %. At a rotation of 0,3, that is 17 degrees, the difference is already 3 % and the linear calculation no longer holds. In the figure the angle is strongly magnified for clarity.

The third assumption: the bending stiffness \(EI\) is constant on each element. It is right when the cross-section and the material do not change between the nodes along the straight axis, as for our bar with the same cross-section everywhere. Where the cross-section changes in a jump, we put a node there. For a beam that is clamped at the left and tapers continuously the assumption fails. What does it do to the result? With constant \(EI\) the stiffness goes in front of the energy integral and the matrix of the element gets a closed form (equation (12.3)). Without it \(EI\) stays under the integral and we compute the matrix of each element numerically.

As the running example we take the cantilever of chapter 5. A steel beam with a rectangular cross-section of width 20 mm and height 40 mm has \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and length \(L = 1\) m. At \(x = 0\) it is clamped in a wall, and at the free end a force of 500 N acts on it downward. We draw the axis before loading grey dashed and the deflection orange. By chapter 5 the free end deflects by 7,972 mm.

12.1.1 Hermite functions (Hermitove funkcije)

The energy of a beam contains the square of the second derivative of the deflection (definition 5.76), so the field on a piece of beam must be twice differentiable. What must hold at a node between two pieces? Let us draw the axis of the beam with three nodes, between which lie the pieces (1) and (2). For the bar one number per node, the displacement, was enough for the field on an element. If neighbouring elements shared only the displacement of a node, nothing would require them to have the same slope there. The deflection would be a tent of two straight lines: at the middle node piece (1) has its own slope, piece (2) another, and the deflection has a kink. Why is a kink not admissible? The direction of the beam turns in it by a finite angle over the length zero. The second derivative \(v''\) says by how much the direction turns per unit length, and a finite angle over the length zero is no number at all. If we round the kink off over a short length and shrink this length, the energy goes to infinity (section 6.7.4). We know this from the hand: we can bend a steel ruler into an arc, but we cannot bring it into a sharp corner without bending it for good. So a beam can only be a smooth curve that has one single tangent at the node, common to both pieces. So the slope too must be a number of the node that the neighbouring elements share. Each node therefore has two numbers, the displacement \(v\) and the rotation \(\varphi = v'\), and an element with two nodes has four. A polynomial of the third degree has exactly four coefficients. So let us try a polynomial of the third degree on each element and find its form as we found the straight line for the bar (equation (7.1)): as a sum of four terms, each of which contains only one of the four numbers.

Definition 12.1 Hermite functions (Hermitove funkcije)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an interval of length \(h = x_{\mathrm{b}} - x_{\mathrm{a}} > 0\) and \(\xi = (x - x_{\mathrm{a}})/h\) the coordinate on it, running from 0 to 1. The Hermite functions (Hermitove funkcije) are four functions \(H_1, H_2, H_3, H_4 : [x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}] \to \mathbb{R}\) that are polynomials of degree at most three (definition 1.64) and for which the quadruple \(\big(H_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{a}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}'(x_{\mathrm{a}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{b}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}'(x_{\mathrm{b}})\big)\) equals \((1, 0, 0, 0)\) for \(H_1\), \((0, 1, 0, 0)\) for \(H_2\), \((0, 0, 1, 0)\) for \(H_3\) and \((0, 0, 0, 1)\) for \(H_4\). Explicitly:

\[\begin{aligned} H_1 &= 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3 , & H_2 &= h\,(\xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3) , \\ H_3 &= 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3 , & H_4 &= h\,(\xi^3 - \xi^2) . \end{aligned} \tag{12.1}\]

The functions \(H_1\) and \(H_3\) have no unit, \(H_2\) and \(H_4\) have the unit of length, because they multiply a rotation, a number without unit, into a displacement. The derivative with respect to \(x\) is the derivative with respect to \(\xi\) divided by \(h\).

Source: ONATE2 (1.8) and (1.11) (PDF pp. 37-38), CK (14.13) (PDF p. 440), GR (5.116)-(5.117) (PDF p. 396). EP calls them Hermite cubic interpolation functions (p. 551), ONATE2 says that they coincide with Hermite polynomials (PDF p. 38). The name was already announced in chapter 6. The notation with \(H_{\mathrm{i}}\) is ours.

Figure 12.6 shows all four functions with their values and slopes at the ends of the element. In each of the four drawings the element \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) is drawn as a dashed line, which is also the value \(H = 0\). The orange curves are \(H_1\), \(H_2\), \(H_3\) and \(H_4\). The dots at the ends of each curve show its values at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\), 0 or 1. The blue line outside the element is the tangent at the end where the quadruple of the definition has its 1: \(H_1\) has the value 1 and the slope 0 at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(H_3\) the same at \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(H_2\) has the slope 1 at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\), \(H_4\) at \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\). At the other end the value and the slope are zero, so there the curve touches the dashed line horizontally.

That we get exactly these functions and no others, and that every polynomial of the third degree can be expressed with them, is stated by the lemma.

Lemma 12.2 Hermite interpolation (Hermitova interpolacija)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an interval of length \(h > 0\) and \(H_1, \dots, H_4\) the Hermite functions (definition 12.1). Then:

  1. For any real numbers \((v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2)\) there is exactly one polynomial \(v\) of degree at most three with \(v(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = v_1\), \(v'(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = \varphi_1\), \(v(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = v_2\), \(v'(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = \varphi_2\), and it is \(v = v_1H_1 + \varphi_1H_2 + v_2H_3 + \varphi_2H_4\).
  2. Every line \(a + b\,(x - x_{\mathrm{a}})\) is obtained with the numbers \((v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2) = (a,\ b,\ a + bh,\ b)\). In particular \(H_1 + H_3 = 1\) and \(H_2 + h\,H_3 + H_4 = x - x_{\mathrm{a}}\).
Proof

We have to show that the four values determine a cubic polynomial, that the functions of equation (12.1) give it, and that a line belongs to it. Let \(s = x - x_{\mathrm{a}}\), so \(s = h\xi\).

  1. A polynomial of degree at most three is \(v = c_0 + c_1 s + c_2 s^2 + c_3 s^3\) and its derivative is \(v' = c_1 + 2c_2 s + 3c_3 s^2\) (lemma 1.82).
  2. The conditions at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) (\(s = 0\)) give \(c_0 = v_1\) and \(c_1 = \varphi_1\).
  3. The conditions at \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) (\(s = h\)) give \(c_0 + c_1 h + c_2 h^2 + c_3 h^3 = v_2\) and \(c_1 + 2c_2 h + 3c_3 h^2 = \varphi_2\). By step 2 these are two linear equations for \(c_2\) and \(c_3\): \(h^2 c_2 + h^3 c_3 = v_2 - v_1 - \varphi_1 h\) and \(2h\,c_2 + 3h^2 c_3 = \varphi_2 - \varphi_1\). The determinant is \(h^2\cdot 3h^2 - h^3\cdot 2h = h^4 \neq 0\), so by lemma 5.49 the system has exactly one solution. There is therefore exactly one polynomial with the given four values.
  4. By the chain rule (\(\mathrm{d}\xi/\mathrm{d}x = 1/h\)) the derivatives of the functions of equation (12.1) are \(H_1' = 6\,(\xi^2 - \xi)/h\), \(H_2' = 1 - 4\xi + 3\xi^2\), \(H_3' = 6\,(\xi - \xi^2)/h\), \(H_4' = 3\xi^2 - 2\xi\). At \(\xi = 0\) and \(\xi = 1\) we get the table of values \(\big(H_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{a}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}'(x_{\mathrm{a}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}(x_{\mathrm{b}}),\ H_{\mathrm{i}}'(x_{\mathrm{b}})\big)\) by rows:
    \[\begin{array}{c|cccc} & H(x_{\mathrm{a}}) & H'(x_{\mathrm{a}}) & H(x_{\mathrm{b}}) & H'(x_{\mathrm{b}}) \\ \hline H_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ H_2 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ H_3 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ H_4 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\]
    The functions of equation (12.1) are therefore indeed the Hermite functions.
  5. The sum \(v_1H_1 + \varphi_1H_2 + v_2H_3 + \varphi_2H_4\) is a polynomial of degree at most three, and by step 4 and the linearity of the derivative (lemma 1.82) its value and derivative at the ends equal \(v_1\), \(\varphi_1\), \(v_2\), \(\varphi_2\). By step 3 it is the only such polynomial. This proves point 1.
  6. The line \(a + b\,s\) is a polynomial of degree at most one and therefore at most three. It has value \(a\) and slope \(b\) at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and value \(a + bh\) and slope \(b\) at \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\). By point 1 we therefore have \(a + b\,s = a\,H_1 + b\,H_2 + (a + bh)\,H_3 + b\,H_4\).
  7. For \(a = 1\), \(b = 0\) we get \(1 = H_1 + H_3\). For \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\) we get \(s = H_2 + h\,H_3 + H_4\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. ONATE2 (1.8)-(1.11) (PDF pp. 37-38), CK (14.12)-(14.13) (PDF p. 440).

Let us check at the middle of the element, \(\xi = 1/2\). By equation (12.1) we have \(H_1 = H_3 = \tfrac12\), \(H_2 = h/8\) and \(H_4 = -h/8\). The sum \(H_1 + H_3\) is indeed 1 and \(H_2 + h H_3 + H_4 = h/8 + h/2 - h/8 = h/2 = s\), which is the distance from \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) to the middle. The first property says that for equal displacements \(v_1 = v_2\) and zero rotations the element stays straight and moves as a whole. The second says that for a rotation \(b\) of both nodes, \(v_1 = 0\) and a displacement \(v_2 = bh\) of the second node, the element rotates as a rigid line.

12.1.2 Finite element of a beam (končni element nosilca)

Now we can define the element as for the bar (definition 7.1), except that each node has two numbers.

Definition 12.3 Finite element of a beam (končni element nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be an interval of length \(h > 0\) on which the bending stiffness \(EI\) is constant. A finite element of a beam (končni element nosilca) with two nodes is this interval together with the local nodes, number 1 at \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and number 2 at \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\), and with a column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (v_1, \varphi_1, v_2, \varphi_2)^\T\) of four real numbers: the transverse displacements \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) (in the direction of the axis \(y\)) and the rotations \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) (counterclockwise, without unit) of both nodes. The deflection field on the element is the polynomial

\[v_{\mathrm{h}}(x) = v_1H_1 + \varphi_1H_2 + v_2H_3 + \varphi_2H_4 = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}(x)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} , \tag{12.2}\]

where \(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}} = (H_1, H_2, H_3, H_4)\) is the row of Hermite functions (definition 12.1) and the product of a row and a column is the number \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(x)\) (definition 1.99). By lemma 12.2, \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a polynomial of degree at most three with \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = v_1\), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = \varphi_1\), \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = v_2\) and \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = \varphi_2\).

Source: ONATE2 §1.3.1 and (1.10)-(1.12) (PDF pp. 37-38), COOK §2.3 (PDF pp. 38-39), EP §8.3.3 (pp. 551-552). ONATE2 (PDF p. 37): »The continuity of the beam slope across adjacent elements requires that dw/dx be a nodal variable.«

Continuity of the slope is not an additional requirement that we would have to check at every node. Two elements that share a node use the same numbers \(v\) and \(\varphi\) in it. By the definition both elements therefore have the same value and the same slope there, the deflection has no kink and the energy is finite. The second derivative at the node is in general not the same for the two elements, which is allowed: the admissible fields of a beam in chapter 6 require continuous \(v\) and \(v'\), and the second derivative may be piecewise continuous (the admissible field of a beam in corollary 6.14, part Beam), and the energy is finite at a jump of the second derivative.

For our cantilever with a single element (\(h = L = 1\) m) the clamp at \(x = 0\) means \(v_1 = 0\) and \(\varphi_1 = 0\). The unknowns are the displacement \(v_2\) and the rotation \(\varphi_2\) of the free end, and the field is \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = v_2 H_3 + \varphi_2 H_4\). We find their values once we know the matrix of the element.

12.1.3 Stiffness matrix of a beam element (togostna matrika elementa nosilca)

What forces and couples does a beam element need to stay in the state \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\)? Two routes lead to the answer, which did not differ for the bar. The first is energy: the element stores the energy \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\int EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (definition 5.76), which is a quadratic form in \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), and its matrix is a candidate for the stiffness matrix. The second is equilibrium: an element without load along it holds only four forces and couples at its ends, and the internal forces give the true forces of the surroundings. The lemma shows that both routes give the same matrix.

We write the forces of the surroundings on the element by the convention of chapter 7 (definition 7.4): \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = (F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4)^\T\), where \(F_1\) and \(F_3\) are the transverse forces in the direction of the axis \(y\) at nodes 1 and 2, and \(F_2\) and \(F_4\) the couples at them, positive counterclockwise. The columns \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) have the same directions in order, as definition 7.4 requires.

Lemma 12.4 Stiffness matrix of a beam element (togostna matrika elementa nosilca)

Let \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) be a finite element of a beam of length \(h\) and constant bending stiffness \(EI\) (definition 12.3), and let no load act along the element. Then:

  1. The strain energy of the element is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), where
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} EI\,(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'')^\T\,\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{EI}{h^3}\begin{pmatrix} 12 & 6h & -12 & 6h \\ 6h & 4h^2 & -6h & 2h^2 \\ -12 & -6h & 12 & -6h \\ 6h & 2h^2 & -6h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{12.3}\]
  2. The forces and couples that the surroundings exert on the element at both ends to hold it in the state \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is therefore the stiffness matrix of the element (definition 7.4).
  3. The lower right \(2 \times 2\) block of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the matrix of lemma 5.98, \(EI\begin{pmatrix} 12/h^3 & -6/h^2 \\ -6/h^2 & 4/h \end{pmatrix}\).
Proof

We have to show three claims. The first we get from the second derivative of the Hermite functions and ten integrals of polynomials, the second from equilibrium, the third by comparing terms.

  1. The second derivatives of the functions of equation (12.1), differentiated once more with respect to \(x\) with \(\mathrm{d}\xi/\mathrm{d}x = 1/h\) (step 4 of lemma 12.2), are \(H_1'' = \ell_1/h^2\), \(H_2'' = \ell_2/h\), \(H_3'' = \ell_3/h^2\), \(H_4'' = \ell_4/h\), where \(\ell_1 = 12\xi - 6\), \(\ell_2 = 6\xi - 4\), \(\ell_3 = 6 - 12\xi\), \(\ell_4 = 6\xi - 2\) are linear functions of \(\xi\).
  2. For \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) the second derivative \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'' = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a number, so \((v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2 = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.102). Because \(EI\) is constant, \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\big(\int EI\,(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\,\mathrm{d}x\big)\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is the first expression for \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  3. The term \((i, j)\) is \(EI\int H_{\mathrm{i}}''H_{\mathrm{j}}''\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\,h\int_0^1 H_{\mathrm{i}}''H_{\mathrm{j}}''\,\mathrm{d}\xi\), because by the substitution rule (as in equation (7.28)) with \(x = x_{\mathrm{a}} + h\xi\) the factor \(h = x'(\xi)\) stands before the integral. By step 1 this is \(EI\,h^{1-p_{\mathrm{i}}-p_{\mathrm{j}}}\,m_{\mathrm{ij}}\) with \(p = (2, 1, 2, 1)\) and \(m_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_0^1 \ell_{\mathrm{i}}\ell_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\).
  4. We compute the integrals with \(\int_0^1\xi^k\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 1/(k+1)\). For example \(m_{11} = \int_0^1(144\xi^2 - 144\xi + 36)\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 48 - 72 + 36 = 12\), \(m_{12} = \int_0^1(72\xi^2 - 84\xi + 24)\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 24 - 42 + 24 = 6\), \(m_{22} = \int_0^1(36\xi^2 - 48\xi + 16)\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 12 - 24 + 16 = 4\). Because \(\ell_3 = -\ell_1\), we have \(m_{3\mathrm{j}} = -m_{1\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j \ne 3\) and \(m_{33} = m_{11}\). Also \(m_{14} = 24 - 30 + 12 = 6\), \(m_{24} = 12 - 18 + 8 = 2\), \(m_{44} = 12 - 12 + 4 = 4\). All ten different terms (SymPy): the matrix \((m_{\mathrm{ij}})\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} 12 & 6 & -12 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 & -6 & 2 \\ -12 & -6 & 12 & -6 \\ 6 & 2 & -6 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
  5. The power of \(h\) is \(h^{-3}\) for terms between \(H_1\) and \(H_3\), \(h^{-2}\) for mixed terms (\(H_1\) or \(H_3\) with \(H_2\) or \(H_4\)), and \(h^{-1}\) for terms between \(H_2\) and \(H_4\). With \(EI/h^3\) factored out we get the matrix of equation (12.3). This proves point 1.
  6. Without load along the element (\(q = 0\), \(m = 0\)) we have \(V' = 0\) and \(M' = -V\) on \((x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}})\) (corollary 5.11). So \(V\) is constant (corollary 1.86), \(M' = -V\) is constant, and the function \(M + V x\) has derivative \(-V + V = 0\) and is constant (corollary 1.86). Hence \(M\) is a linear function of \(x\). In the figure we see this without derivatives. We cut the element anywhere in two places and draw the piece between the cuts separately. The piece is at rest, so the vertical forces on it balance and the shear force \(V\) is the same in both cuts. The moments \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) differ by the moment of the force \(V\) at the right cut, and this grows in proportion to the distance between the cuts. Here the assumption of statics enters: the piece is at rest because we apply the load slowly. In vibration the piece accelerates, its inertia acts as a load along the element and the conclusion does not hold, which is why the element also gets a mass matrix in section 12.9.
  7. By theorem 5.35, \(v'' = M/EI\), so \(v''\) is linear and continuous on \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\). By theorem 5.46 for \(n = 2\), \(v\) is the double integral of a linear function plus a polynomial of degree at most one. The integral of a polynomial of degree \(k\) is a polynomial of degree \(k + 1\) (the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(\int x^k = x^{k+1}/(k+1)\)), so the double integral of a linear function is a cubic polynomial and \(v\) is a polynomial of degree at most three. Its values and slopes at the ends are \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). By lemma 12.2, point 1, the true deflection of the element in the state \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is exactly \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat H_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  8. The second derivative of the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) at the ends (step 1 at \(\xi = 0\) and \(\xi = 1\)) is \(v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = \dfrac{-6v_1 - 4h\varphi_1 + 6v_2 - 2h\varphi_2}{h^2}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = \dfrac{6v_1 + 2h\varphi_1 - 6v_2 + 4h\varphi_2}{h^2}\). Because \(v_{\mathrm{h}}''\) is linear, the third derivative is constant: \(v_{\mathrm{h}}''' = \dfrac{v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{b}}) - v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{a}})}{h} = \dfrac{12\,(v_1 - v_2) + 6h\,(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)}{h^3}\).
  9. The force of the surroundings on the element in the cross-section with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \((V, M)\) by the definition of internal forces (definitions 5.7 and 5.8), and in the cross-section with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it is the opposite, \((-V, -M)\) (Newton's third law). With \(V = -M' = -EI\,v'''\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) we have \(F_1 = -V(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = EI\,v_{\mathrm{h}}'''\), \(F_2 = -M(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = -EI\,v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{a}})\), \(F_3 = V(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = -EI\,v_{\mathrm{h}}'''\), \(F_4 = M(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = EI\,v_{\mathrm{h}}''(x_{\mathrm{b}})\). The cross-section with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the right cut of the piece, where \(V\) is drawn upward, and the cross-section with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the left one, where the opposite force is drawn. In figure 12.8 the four components are the arrows and arcs at the ends of the element: \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) at node 1, \(F_3\) and \(F_4\) at node 2.
  10. We insert step 8. First row: \(F_1 = \dfrac{EI}{h^3}\,\big(12v_1 + 6h\varphi_1 - 12v_2 + 6h\varphi_2\big)\). Second: \(F_2 = \dfrac{EI}{h^3}\,\big(6h\,v_1 + 4h^2\varphi_1 - 6h\,v_2 + 2h^2\varphi_2\big)\). The third is \(-F_1\), the fourth \(F_4 = \dfrac{EI}{h^3}\,\big(6h\,v_1 + 2h^2\varphi_1 - 6h\,v_2 + 4h^2\varphi_2\big)\). The coefficients are the rows of the matrix of equation (12.3), so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). For \(v_1 = 1\) and the other three numbers zero the first terms of the rows remain, and these are the labels of the upper drawing of figure 12.8: \(12EI/h^3\), \(6EI/h^2\), \(-12EI/h^3\) and \(6EI/h^2\). This proves point 2.
  11. For a clamped first end, \(v_1 = \varphi_1 = 0\), we have \(V(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = F_3 = EI\,(12\,v_2/h^3 - 6\varphi_2/h^2)\) and \(M(x_{\mathrm{b}}) = F_4 = EI\,(-6\,v_2/h^2 + 4\varphi_2/h)\). This is equation (5.93), written with \(h\) instead of \(L\). This proves point 3.

Source: ONATE2 (1.19)-(1.20) (PDF pp. 40-41), COOK (2.3-5) (PDF p. 40, obtains the same matrix column by column from the forces of a cantilever and equilibrium), CK (14.15a) (PDF p. 441), GR (5.122) and (5.125) (PDF p. 397), EP (8.3.16) (p. 553). We derived steps 6 to 10 ourselves with the signs of chapter 5, because the sources use the axis \(z\) upward and different signs of rotations. ONATE2 (PDF p. 41) knows this equality: »the cubic deflection field assumed in Eq.(1.10) coincides with that obtained by integrating the differential equations of equilibrium for a beam segment«, CK (PDF p. 440) says that these functions are »exact displacement shapes for uniform beams that are statically loaded only by end shears and moments«.

The lemma shows that the energy route equals the equilibrium route. The reason is in step 7: an element without load has as its true deflection exactly a polynomial of the third degree from the family of Hermite functions. For the bar it was so because the true solution without load is a straight line. The assumption that there is no load along the element is right for our cantilever, which carries only the force at node 2. If there is a load along the element, the equilibrium route no longer gives \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). For the same cantilever under its own weight the true deflection is a polynomial of the fourth degree. The displacement and the rotation of the end stay exact, but the clamp moment computed from the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) of one element is 17 % too small in absolute value, \(-25{,}51\) instead of \(-30{,}61\) N m (section 12.3, figure 12.34).

Figure 12.8 shows the first two columns of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\). Column \(j\) is, by definition 7.4, the column of forces and couples that hold the element in the state with one unit displacement or rotation and all others zero. For the element of our cantilever (\(h = 1\) m, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m²) the coefficients are \(12EI/h^3 = 250\,880\) N/m, \(6EI/h^2 = 125\,440\) N, \(4EI/h = 83\,627\) N m and \(2EI/h = 41\,813\) N m. For \(v_1 = 1\) m the first end rises and the second stays at rest without rotation. The element bends into the orange curve \(H_1\), the grey dashed line is its position before the move. The surroundings must lift node 1 with a force of \(250\,880\) N, push node 2 down with an equal force, and act on both nodes with a couple of \(125\,440\) N m counterclockwise. The forces give a moment about node 1 equal to \(-250\,880\) N m (node 2 is 1 m to the right, the force points down), the couples give \(+250\,880\) N m, and the sum is zero as it must be. For \(\varphi_1 = 1\) rad node 1 rotates and the element bends into the curve \(H_2\). This requires a couple of \(83\,627\) N m at node 1, a couple of \(41\,813\) N m at node 2 and a force of \(125\,440\) N upward at node 1 and downward at node 2.

We can feel the same column with the hands. We hold a steel ruler at both ends and lift one end without rotating it, as the orange curve \(H_1\). The hands feel exactly these two forces, one lifting and the other pushing down, and a twist in both wrists in the same direction. This view adds that a column of the stiffness matrix is something we hold in our hands, not only the result of integrals.

Let us now solve the running example. The cantilever of figure 12.4 has a single element with nodes 1 and 2, node 1 is clamped (\(v_1 = 0\), \(\varphi_1 = 0\)) and the force \(-500\) N (downward) acts on node 2 without a couple. The force of the surroundings on the element at node 2 is exactly this load, because there are no other bodies. By theorem 7.18 of chapter 7 rows 3 and 4 of the system \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) remain, with the unknowns \(v_2\) and \(\varphi_2\):

\[\frac{EI}{h^3}\begin{pmatrix} 12 & -6h \\ -6h & 4h^2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_2 \\ \varphi_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -500\ \text{N} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]

From the second row we get \(\varphi_2 = \tfrac{3}{2h}\,v_2\). Inserting into the first: \(\tfrac{EI}{h^3}\,(12 - 9)\,v_2 = -500\) N, so \(v_2 = -500\,h^3/(3EI) = -7{,}9719\) mm and \(\varphi_2 = -500\,h^2/(2EI) = -1{,}1958\cdot 10^{-2}\). The first number is the blue distance between the axis and the free end, the second the angle between the horizontal and the tangent at the end, negative because the end rotates clockwise. These are exactly the values of chapter 5. The reactions of the clamp come from the first two rows: \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (500\ \text{N},\ 500\ \text{N m},\ -500\ \text{N},\ 0)^\T\). The force \(F_1 = +500\) N upward and the couple \(F_2 = +500\) N m counterclockwise on the wall are exactly the reactions of a cantilever with a downward loaded end, in agreement with \(V(0) = -500\) N and \(M(0) = -500\) N m of chapter 5 (\(F_1 = -V(0)\), \(F_2 = -M(0)\)). The third and fourth components are \(V(L) = -500\) N and \(M(L) = 0\).

That this is no coincidence is shown by step 7 of the lemma. By chapter 5 the true deflection of the cantilever is a polynomial of the third degree (the moment is linear), so by step 7 of the lemma it equals \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\). At the middle \(x = L/2\) we have \(\xi = 1/2\), so

\[\begin{aligned} v_{\mathrm{h}} &= v_2\,H_3 + \varphi_2\,H_4 \\ &= (-7{,}9719\ \text{mm})\cdot\tfrac12 + (-1{,}1958\cdot 10^{-2})\cdot\big(-\tfrac18\cdot 1\ \text{m}\big) \\ &= -3{,}9860\ \text{mm} + 1{,}4947\ \text{mm} = -2{,}4912\ \text{mm}. \end{aligned}\]

The true deflection is \(-F x^2(3L - x)/(6EI)\) with \(x = L/2\) and \(F = 500\) N, which is \(-2{,}4912\) mm, the blue distance at the middle of the cantilever. The two numbers are equal, as follows from step 7. The general reason why the values at the nodes are exact for other loads too is in the following sections.

12.1.4 Properties of the matrix and integration (lastnosti matrike in integriranje)

What does the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) say about the element? As for the bar (lemma 7.6) we expect that only a rigid motion has zero energy, because an element that moves and rotates as a whole does not deform. For a beam there are more rigid motions than for a bar.

Lemma 12.5 Properties of the beam element stiffness matrix (lastnosti togostne matrike elementa nosilca)

Let \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the matrix of equation (12.3) with \(EI > 0\) and \(h > 0\). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric.
  2. For every column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) we have \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\), and equality holds exactly for \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (a,\ b,\ a + bh,\ b)^\T\) with real numbers \(a\) and \(b\) (rigid motions of the element, a displacement by \(a\) and a rotation by \(b\)).
  3. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly for such columns.
  4. For every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) the column \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is in equilibrium: \(F_1 + F_3 = 0\) and \(F_2 + F_4 + h\,F_3 = 0\).
Proof

We have to show four properties. We read off the symmetry, derive the next two from the energy and the lemma on the kernel, and the equilibrium from the third.

  1. The matrix in equation (12.3) has equal terms \((i, j)\) and \((j, i)\), which we see from its form, so it is symmetric (definition 1.103).
  2. By lemma 12.4, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), because the integrand is nonnegative (lemma 3.54, point 4).
  3. Let the integral be zero. The integrand \(EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, so it is zero at every point, otherwise the integral over a neighbourhood of a point where it is positive would have a positive lower bound (the same argument as in steps 1 and 2 of the proof of lemma 6.7). Hence \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'' = 0\) on \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\), by corollary 1.86 \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is constant, \(v_{\mathrm{h}}' = b\), and \(v_{\mathrm{h}} - b\,(x - x_{\mathrm{a}})\) has derivative zero, so \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = a + b\,(x - x_{\mathrm{a}})\). The values and slopes at the ends are \((a,\ b,\ a + bh,\ b)\), so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) has this form.
  4. Conversely: for \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (a,\ b,\ a + bh,\ b)^\T\), by lemma 12.2, point 2, the field is \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = a + b\,(x - x_{\mathrm{a}})\), its second derivative is zero and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\). This proves point 2.
  5. By points 1 and 2 the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15). By lemma 7.16, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) therefore holds exactly when \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\), so by point 2 exactly for these columns. This proves point 3.
  6. Let \(\vek r_1 = (1, 0, 1, 0)^\T\) and \(\vek r_2 = (0, 1, h, 1)^\T\), the columns for \((a, b) = (1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\). By point 3, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek r_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\). By symmetry (point 1) we have \(\vek r_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek r_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek r_{\mathrm{j}})^\T\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) (lemma 1.102).
  7. We write out the products: \(\vek r_1^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = F_1 + F_3\) and \(\vek r_2^\T\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = F_2 + h\,F_3 + F_4\). The first is the sum of the transverse forces, the second the sum of the moments about node 1 (the force \(F_3\) at node 2 with lever arm \(h\) gives the moment \(h\,F_3\) counterclockwise, the couples have the same moment about every point). Both are therefore zero. This proves point 4.

Source: The proof is not from a source. COOK §2.6 (PDF pp. 51-52) states the symmetry of the stiffness matrix and \([K]\{D\} = \{0\}\) for a small rigid motion, GR (PDF p. 397) states for the matrix of a beam element the translational and the rotational rigid motion.

The second part of point 4 is the condition of equilibrium of a piece of beam: the forces of the surroundings on an element without load that is in equilibrium have no resultant and no resulting moment. Figure 12.9 shows both rigid motions. On the left is the element that we moved up by \(a\) from the position before the motion. On the right is the element that we rotated by \(b\) about node 1, so node 2 rises by \(bh\). Neither motion needs any force or couple. With our cantilever: an element that we move by \(a = 1\) cm or rotate by \(b = 0{,}01\) about node 1 needs no force or couple, because \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek r_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\).

For the integrals in equation (12.3) a computer usually uses a Gauss rule (definition 7.24). By step 1 of the proof, the integrand \(EI\,(\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}'')^\T\mat H_{\mathrm{e}}''\) is a product of two linear functions of \(\xi\), hence a polynomial of degree two. By lemma 7.25 (written for the interval \([-1, 1]\), which we obtain from the interval \([0, 1]\) with the map \(\xi \mapsto 2\xi - 1\), and this does not change the degree of a polynomial) the rule with two points is exact for polynomials up to degree three, so it gives the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) exactly. ONATE2 (PDF p. 41) says: »can be computed analytically or using a 2-point Gauss quadrature«. The rule with one point, at the middle of the element (\(\xi = 1/2\)), is the reduced integration (nepopolno integriranje) of chapter 7 and replaces the values \(\ell_{\mathrm{i}}\) by \((0, -1, 0, 1)\) (by step 1 of the proof), so that \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^{(1)} = \frac{EI}{h}\,(0, -1, 0, 1)^\T(0, -1, 0, 1)\). This matrix has a single independent column, so there are three independent columns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with zero energy. Two are the rigid motions, the third is not: \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (0, 1, 0, 1)^\T\), that is the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = H_2 + H_4 = h\,\xi\,(1 - \xi)(1 - 2\xi)\), in figure 12.10 the orange curve with rotation 1 at both nodes above the axis of the element. Its curvature \((12\xi - 6)/h\), drawn below from its zero line, is a straight line that is zero at the middle but not at the ends. Why does this shape have no energy? The rule with one point measures the curvature only at the middle, and there it is exactly zero. The true energy of this shape is not zero: \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 6EI/h\), for the element of our cantilever \(125\,440\) J. The same warning as for the quadratic bar with one point in chapter 7: too few points give a spurious mode without energy (lažni način brez energije).

With this we solved the cantilever with a single matrix \(4 \times 4\). The question remains that the example raises: would we get the true values if the cantilever were loaded by its own weight or if the beam had several elements? How we assemble elements into a beam and how we replace loads along the beam by forces at the nodes is the topic of the next section.
