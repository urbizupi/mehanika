The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

We start this chapter with a question that the earlier chapters left open. In chapter 3 we showed that a body expands when heated and that stresses arise in it if the expansion is not free. There we took the temperature as known, for example for a steel bar between two walls that we heated from 20 °C to 50 °C, that is by 30 K. Here we will compute it: from how the body is heated or cooled at its boundary we will find the temperature at every point and how it changes with time. Then we will compute the stresses from it.

The word conduction says what happens: heat is led through the material from point to point, as a stream of water is led through a pipe, while the material itself stays at rest. Heat is energy that passes from one part of a body to another because of a difference in temperature. Let us see both on a spoon that we leave in hot soup. The bowl holds soup at temperature \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\), and into it we put a metal spoon. We measure length along the spoon, from the place where the spoon leaves the soup, \(s = 0\), to the end of the handle, \(s = \ell\). Heat flows along the spoon from the part in the hot soup towards the cold handle. On the right we draw what the temperature is at every point of the spoon, the distance \(s\) across and the temperature \(T\) up. Before we put the spoon into the soup it has the air temperature \(T_0\) everywhere, so we draw the horizontal axis at the height \(T_0\). The soup temperature \(T_{\mathrm{j}}\) we draw as a grey dashed height. Soon after we put the spoon into the soup, at time \(t_1\), only the part near the soup is warm, while the handle is still almost at \(T_0\). We draw this curve grey dashed. Later, at time \(t_2\), the heat has got further, and we draw this curve orange. The end of the handle is now warmer, and we draw the difference as a blue segment. With the hand we feel only that the handle becomes warm. The chapter shows how to compute both curves, that is along which path and how fast the heat spreads.

A pipe shows why this matters. In a thick-walled pipe carrying a hot fluid the inner wall is warmer than the outer one. The stresses caused by this difference add to the stresses from internal pressure from chapter 11. Without knowing the temperature we cannot predict these stresses.

The chapter promises four things, and figure 14.2 shows each in its own panel. First, we will say what the flow of heat is and what determines it, and from the conservation of energy in a piece of a body derive the equation that the temperature satisfies, with all assumptions. We will also prove that for given data at the boundary and at the start the solution is one single curve, and a second curve with the same data is not a solution. Second, we will solve the equation for a wall, a bar and a rectangle, also with the Fourier series from chapters 9 and 13. Third, from the computed temperature we will determine the stresses in a bar between walls, a beam and a pipe. Fourth, we will carry the same computation over to the finite elements of chapters 7 and 12, also for a process that changes with time.
