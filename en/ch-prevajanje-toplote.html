We start this chapter with a question that the earlier chapters left open. In chapter 3 we showed that a body expands when heated and that stresses arise in it if the expansion is not free. There we took the temperature as known, for example 30 K of difference in a steel bar between two walls. Here we will compute it: from how the body is heated or cooled at its boundary we will find the temperature at every point and how it changes with time. Then we will compute the stresses from it.

The word conduction says what happens: heat is led through the material from point to point, as a stream of water is led through a pipe, while the material itself stays at rest. Heat is energy that passes from one part of a body to another because of a difference in temperature. In everyday experience, such as a spoon left in hot soup, we see only that the handle becomes warm. The chapter shows along which path and how fast.

A pipe and a housing show why this matters. In a thick-walled pipe carrying a hot fluid the inner wall is warmer than the outer one, and the stresses caused by this difference add to the stresses from internal pressure from chapter 11. In a steel bar between rigid walls the temperature alone decides how large a stress arises in it. Without knowing the temperature we cannot predict these stresses.

The chapter promises four things. First, we will say what the flow of heat is and what determines it, and from the conservation of energy derive the equation that the temperature satisfies, with all assumptions and with a proof that the solution is not doubled for given data at the boundary and at the start. Second, we will solve the equation for a wall, a bar and a rectangle, also with the Fourier series from chapters 9 and 13. Third, from the computed temperature we will determine the stresses in a bar, a beam and a pipe. Fourth, we will carry the same computation over to the finite elements of chapters 7 and 12, also for a process that changes with time.
