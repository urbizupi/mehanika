Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

Chapter 17Lecture: FEM modeling in practice

1 Three errors: why a finer mesh does not repair a wrong model

Let us start with one single number. We draw a wall and clamp a steel bar firmly into it. The bar is \(L = 80\) mm long, its cross-section is \(H = 40\) mm high and \(20\) mm wide. On the free end we hang a mass that pulls down with a force \(F = 500\) N. The end sinks by \(\delta\). For steel this is only a few thousandths of a millimetre, so we draw it greatly enlarged. We give the bar to a program and the program says: \(\delta = 4.082\ \mu\)m. We refine the mesh and the program says \(4.082\ \mu\)m again. A question for you: is the number therefore right?

Let us draw the answer on one line. We take an axis on which we mark the number we seek, \(Q\), here the deflection. On the left we put \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\), what we would measure on the real bar. Then comes \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), the exact solution of the equations we chose, and the distance to it is the first step, \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\). Next is \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\), what the mesh would give with perfect arithmetic, and the second step, \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) leads to it. At the end is \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\), the number on the screen, after the third step, \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\). The claim of this part of the lecture is: the way from the real bar to the printed number has three steps, each has its own cause and its own cure, and a finer mesh shortens only the middle one.

Why this is so we will uncover with questions "why?", until we reach something that needs no further explanation.

1.1 What is the right number?

What would we compare the program's number with to call it right? With the one we would measure on the bar. Before we compute anything, let us see what holds the bar at rest. We remove the wall and draw the bar alone. This is called a free-body diagram (diagram prostega telesa), because we free the body from its surroundings. The mass still pulls down with the force \(F\). The bar is at rest, so by Newton the sum of the forces is zero. This is possible only if the wall pushes up with a force \(R\), and \(R = F = 500\) N. The sum of the moments must be zero too. The force \(F\) turns the bar about the clamp with the moment \(F L\), so the wall adds a couple \(M_0\) in the opposite sense, \(M_0 = F L = 500\ \text{N} \cdot 0.08\ \text{m} = 40\) N m. This is the ground everything further stands on: a bar at rest has neither a resultant nor a moment.

The measured deflection is \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\). We have no measurement, so we will replace \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) by a richer model. Why may we? Because we want to know what the simplification leaves out. When is that not enough? When we want to know how close the richer model itself is to the metal: this we do not learn so. What it means for the result: every \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) in this lecture is a difference between two models, not between a model and the metal.

1.2 Why is there not one answer for \(\delta\)?

There is only one bar. Why would it have several deflections? Because a model is not the bar but a list of what we keep of the bar. Let us draw the same bar four times and take something away at each drawing. First the whole bar as a body: every point has three components of displacement. Then we press it into a plane: everything happens in the plane \(xy\), a point has only \(u\) and \(v\). We took away the third direction.

The next step is the Timoshenko beam. We replace the cross-section by a point on the axis and demand that the section stays plane while the axis bends. We took away the section's freedom to warp. But look, the section is not perpendicular to the axis. The angle between them is the shear angle \(\gamma\).

The last step is the Euler-Bernoulli beam. We also demand that the section stays perpendicular to the bent axis, so \(\gamma = 0\). We took away shear. Why may we? In a slender bar the shear part of the deflection is small. When may we not? In a short thick bar, like ours. What do we lose? The whole term of the deflection that shear causes. How much that is we will measure in a few minutes.

Each model gives its own number, and for our bar we have three. Let us draw them on an axis.

We take the axis of the deflection in micrometres. The Euler-Bernoulli model gives \(4.082\ \mu\)m, and so does the plane model with clamp (i). Timoshenko gives \(4.878\ \mu\)m, the plane model with clamp (ii) \(5.077\ \mu\)m. The same bar, the same force, three different numbers. So the answer for \(\delta\) depends on what we kept.

1.3 Why three errors and not one?

Let us go back to the axis and count the numbers on it: \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\). There are four. How many gaps are there between four points on a line? Three. And the gaps add up to the distance from the first point to the last. This is counting, nothing more:

\[Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}} = \underbrace{(Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{r}})}_{\mathrm{e}_{\mathrm{num}}} + \underbrace{(Q_{\mathrm{r}} - Q_{\mathrm{m}})}_{\mathrm{e}_{\mathrm{dis}}} + \underbrace{(Q_{\mathrm{m}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}})}_{\mathrm{e}_{\mathrm{mod}}} .\]

In the book: 17.2, equation (17.1).

Each gap has its own cause. \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) arises when we choose the equations, before the computer even starts. \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) arises when we replace a continuous field by finitely many numbers. \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) arises because the computer works with numbers of finite length.

1.4 Why does the mesh shorten only the middle step?

What does a finer mesh do? It solves the same equations better. Which equations does it change? None. This is the meaning of the words: the mesh says how well we solve, not what we solve. So the mesh moves \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) towards \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), but does not touch the point \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\). The step \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) stays as it is.

In our bar it is even more striking. A single Euler-Bernoulli beam element gives the deflection at the node exactly when the force acts at the node, as theorem 12.14 says. In our bar the force hangs right at the end, at the node. So \(4.082\ \mu\)m comes already with one element, and with two, ten or more elements the point is the same, \(e_{\mathrm{dis}} = 0\). The computation differs from the exact formula \(F L^3/(3EI)\) by \(4 \cdot 10^{-16}\) relative, so \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) too is only rounding. Where is the error then? All of it is in the distance to the plane model (ii), \(0.995\ \mu\)m, that is \(19.6\ \%\) of the deflection. This is the answer to the opening question: the number did not move under refinement because the middle step was already zero, and it is still almost a fifth away.

1.5 Why is the beam model so far off here, and when is it fine?

Where does a difference of almost a fifth come from in such a simple bar? Recall what the Euler-Bernoulli model took away: the shear angle \(\gamma\). The Timoshenko deflection has two parts. The bending part is \(F L^3/(3EI)\), it grows with the third power of the length. The shear part is \(F L/(kGA)\), it grows only with the first power. If we shorten the bar to half, the bending part shrinks eightfold, the shear part only twofold. The ratio of shear to bending is therefore proportional to \((H/L)^2\), for our steel rectangle

\[\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = 0.78 \Big(\frac{H}{L}\Big)^2 .\]

In the book: 17.3 for the steps.

For our bar \(L/H = 2\), so \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = 0.78 \cdot 0.25 = 0.195\). The Euler-Bernoulli deflection is therefore below Timoshenko by \(16.3\ \%\). This is the price of the assumption \(\gamma = 0\) for a short bar.

When is the model fine, then? We draw the slenderness \(L/H\) on the horizontal axis and the percentage error on the vertical. The blue curve is the error against Timoshenko, the orange one against the plane model (ii). Both rise steeply for short bars, because \((H/L)^2\) grows fast there. The dashed lines tell where the error drops below a limit: below \(5\ \%\) from \(L/H = 3.85\) on, below \(1\ \%\) from \(L/H = 8.79\) on. At \(L/H = 25\), at the right end of the blue curve, only \(0.12\ \%\) is left.

These thresholds hold under three conditions: steel with \(\nu = 0.3\), shear factor \(k = 5/6\) and a rectangular section. Why may we take them? Our bar is like that. When do they not hold? For another section or material. What changes then? The coefficient \(0.78\), while the form \((H/L)^2\) stays. We also assume small displacements and linear elastic steel with \(E = 196\) GPa, which holds for a deflection of a few micrometres.

1.6 Why do we not trust even the "better" model at \(L/H = 2\)?

Is the cure not simple, just take Timoshenko? No, the Timoshenko deflection is itself below the plane model (ii) by \(3.9\ \%\). Worse: the plane models (i) and (ii) have the same equations and differ only in how we write the clamp. Yet they are \(0.995\ \mu\)m apart, which is \(24.4\ \%\) of the deflection (i). At \(L/H = 25\) this difference is only \(0.16\ \%\). Why does the clamp matter so much for a short bar?

We draw the wall and clamp a long slender bar in it, \(25\) heights long. We draw it with a break, since the whole of it would not fit on the board. Below it we clamp a short bar with the same height \(H\), only \(2H\) long. Now comes Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo): two loads with the same resultant and the same moment differ only near the place where they act. For us that place is the wall. How far does its influence reach? For a long strip it has been computed: one to two section heights. The computation assumes a semi-infinite strip, so for a short bar it is only a rough estimate, but it is enough for a comparison. In the book: 11.11.

For the slender bar this is only \(4\) to \(8\ \%\) of the length. The rest of the bar does not know exactly how it is clamped. For the short bar the same region covers everything, \(50\) to \(100\ \%\) of the length. The whole bar lies where we do not know the stresses at the wall. So none of the models is reliable here. A real wall would decide through how much the clamp itself deforms, and none of our models contains that.

The assumption is a rigid clamp. Why do we take it? Because the wall is much stiffer than the bar. When does it fail? For short bars, where the region of the clamp reaches over the whole bar. What does it do to the result? At \(L/H = 2\) it decides \(24.4\ \%\), at \(L/H = 25\) only \(0.16\ \%\).

1.7 Back up

Now we build the claim from the ground. First we choose the equations, and this fixes \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\). Then we cut the equations into a mesh, and \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) approaches \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) as the mesh gets finer. At the end we compute, and \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\) approaches \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\). So each step has its own cure: a better model for \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\), a better mesh for \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\), better solving for \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\).

On the lower axis of the same figure this is our bar: three points at the same place, because the second and third steps are zero, and the plane model (ii) \(0.995\ \mu\)m away. So no mesh would help, only a better model helps.

1.8 Perspective: steps with a sign

One last trap, and with it a view that adds the sign: a step can also go backwards. On the axis we start again at \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\). The model takes us far to the right, to \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\). A coarse mesh brings us almost as far back, to \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\), and a small numerical step to \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\). The total error is short. Is the result therefore good? No: both large errors are still there, they only happen to cancel. When we refine the mesh, \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) goes away and the total error grows. A small total error does not prove that the parts are small, so we estimate each one by itself.

1.9 Perspective: by numbers

Whoever has only two minutes should remember this axis. The program gave \(4.082\ \mu\)m and the same with every mesh. A richer model gives \(5.077\ \mu\)m. The difference of \(19.6\ \%\) is not an error of the mesh but an error of the choice. The axis adds the size: the modelling error is \(19.6\ \%\), the error of the arithmetic \(4 \cdot 10^{-16}\).

1.10 Perspective: by the question

Is the Timoshenko model good or bad? Wrong question. BATHE treats a bracket and replaces it by a Timoshenko beam. Such a model gives the bending moment to within \(1\ \%\), the deflection only to within \(20\ \%\), and the largest stress not at all, because it does not see the fillets where the stress rises. The difference in the deflection is again the clamp: the model takes the end as rigidly clamped and does not see the deformation at the bolts. This perspective adds: a model is good for a question, not by itself.

1.11 Perspective: by the word

Why is the middle step called discretization (diskretizacija)? Latin discretus means separate. We replace the continuous deflection along the bar by finitely many separate numbers at the nodes. The name itself says where this error arises and what reduces it: more separate numbers, a finer mesh.

2 How much is still missing: order of convergence and Richardson extrapolation

In the previous part we saw that a finer mesh reduces only one of the three errors, the discretization error (diskretizacijska napaka). Today we ask how much of that error is still left. For a real structure we do not know the exact value. We only have the same quantity, computed on three meshes. Can the three numbers alone tell us how far off the last one still is?

Take the bar from the book. We draw it as a line of length \(L = 1\) and fix it in walls at both ends. We compute its first eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the square of the first natural circular frequency with which the bar vibrates along its axis. First we divide the bar into four equal elements, each of length \(h\). Then we halve every element and get eight elements of length \(h/2\). All the earlier nodes have stayed in place. We halve once more: sixteen elements of length \(h/4\). What does the program give on each of the three meshes?

We draw a number line for \(\lambda\), larger values to the right. On four elements the program gives \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = 10.386642\). On eight it gives \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} = 9.997081\), on sixteen \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4} = 9.901354\). The points move left and crowd together. Somewhere to the left of the last one is the exact value \(Q\), which we do not know. How far to the left?

The claim of this part is short. If every halving of the element removes the same share of the error, three quarters for this bar, then what the last halving removed is three times what is still left. The remaining error is therefore the last difference divided by \(3\). The claim uses three things that are not obvious by themselves: differences instead of errors, the same share at every halving, and the third. Each one is the next question why.

2.1 Why do differences tell us anything when we do not know the exact value?

What of all we have drawn can we really measure? The error of a mesh is the distance from its point to \(Q\), for example \(e(h) = Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q\). We cannot measure that distance, because we do not know where \(Q\) is. The distance between two computed points we can measure. Between \(Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\) it is \(d_1 = 0.389561\), between \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4}\) it is \(d_2 = 0.095727\). This is plain subtraction of two numbers we have.

Why is it worth anything? Because it is the difference of two errors: \(d_1 = (Q_{\mathrm{h}} - Q) - (Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q) = e(h) - e(h/2)\). The unknown \(Q\) cancels in the subtraction. In the figure we see it this way: the blue distance \(d_1\) does not depend on where to the left the exact value lies. Wherever we put it, the distance between the two points stays the same. Differences are therefore all we really know about the error.

2.2 Why does the ratio of differences tell how fast the error falls?

Let us compare the two blue distances. The ratio is \(d_1/d_2 = 0.389561/0.095727 = 4.07\). The second distance is almost exactly a quarter of the first. What does that tell us?

Suppose the error is proportional to a power of the element length: \(e(h) = C h^q\). When we halve \(h\), \(h^q\) becomes \(2^q\) times smaller, and the error shrinks by the same factor. If every error shrinks \(2^q\) times, then every difference of two successive errors also shrinks \(2^q\) times. So the ratio is \(d_1/d_2 = 2^q\). Here \(4.07 \approx 4 = 2^2\), so \(q \approx 2\), more precisely \(\log_2 4.07 = 2.02\). We call the number \(q\) the order of convergence (red konvergence), because it is the "order" of the notation "of order \(h^q\)". We needed neither \(C\) nor \(Q\).

Why three meshes and not two? Two meshes give one difference and no ratio. Only three points tell whether the differences shrink and how fast. COOK puts it this way: at least three meshes are needed to tell a curve from a straight line. The proof in the book shows that in \(d_1 - 2^q d_2\) the term with \(C\) cancels and only the remainder is left, so the ratio is the closer to \(2^q\), the smaller \(h\) is. In the book: 17.12.

2.3 Why does the error behave like \(C h^q\) at all?

Is a quarter at every halving an accident of these three meshes or a rule? We draw the axes: horizontally the number of elements \(n\), every doubling equally long, vertically the error on a logarithmic scale. We plot the errors of our eigenvalue, divided by the error at \(n = 2\), so the line starts at \(1\). At \(n = 4\), \(8\), \(16\), \(32\) the errors are \(0.517\), \(0.127\), \(0.0317\), \(0.0079\), each about a quarter of the one before. The error energy of the linear element from the chapter on bars falls almost the same way. On logarithmic axes this is a straight line, and the grey triangle shows its slope: over one doubling it falls by a factor of \(4\), which is slope \(2\). The order is a slope we read off with a ruler.

Why a power? The element draws piecewise straight pieces. What it misses is the lowest term its polynomial leaves out, and that term is proportional to a power of \(h\). For our bar the book computes it: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}} = \pi^2 + \tfrac{\pi^4}{12} h^2 + \dots\), so \(q = 2\) and \(C = 8.117\). An element of higher degree leaves out a higher term, so the error energy of the quadratic element falls by a factor of \(16\) per doubling. Let us say honestly where we stand. For a general quantity the book proves only upper bounds and takes the equality \(e = C h^q\) from COOK. So it is an assumption, and at the end we say when it holds. In the book: 17.11, 17.13.

2.4 Why is exactly a third of the last difference left?

We know the step we have just taken. How much of the way is still ahead? Now we only count. We draw a line and on it the exact value \(Q\), grey dashed because we do not know it. To the right is \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2}\). The distance between them is the error \(e(h/2)\). We divide it into four equal quarters. When we halve the mesh, only a quarter of the error is left, because the ratio \(4.07\) measured \(2^q \approx 4\). So the new point \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4}\) lands on the first mark. The step we took is the difference \(d_2\), and it covers three quarters. What is still left is one quarter. Three quarters against one: the step is three times as long as the remainder. So the remainder is a third of the step, \(e(h/4) = d_2/3\).

For a general order \(q\) every halving leaves \(1/2^q\) of the error. The step covers \(2^q - 1\) such parts, one part is left, so the remainder is \(d_2/(2^q - 1)\). For \(q = 1\) the remainder would equal the whole step, for \(q = 2\) a third, for \(q = 4\) a fifteenth.

We see the same in another way if we look at all the future steps. Let us go back to the first figure and magnify the region around \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4}\). The next halving would make a step \(d_2/4\), the one after \(d_2/16\), then \(d_2/64\), each a quarter of the one before. The steps crowd together and stop at the exact value. All together they are \(d_2/4 + d_2/16 + \dots = d_2/3\), which is \(0.095727/3 = 0.031909\). Subtracting it we get the estimate \(\hat Q = 9.901354 - 0.031909 = 9.869445\). The exact value is \(\pi^2 = 9.869604\). The estimate is \(0.00016\) from it, the mesh \(n = 16\) is \(0.0317\) from it, two hundred times more. In the book: 17.14.

2.5 From the ground back to the answer

Now we have the ground: subtracting two known numbers and counting quarters. From there we build the answer in four steps, each on the first figure.

  1. Three meshes give two differences, \(0.389561\) and \(0.095727\).
  2. Their ratio \(4.07\) gives \(2^q \approx 4\). We measured the order, we did not guess it.
  3. The last difference divided by \(2^q - 1 = 3\) is the estimate of what is still missing, \(0.0319\).
  4. Subtracting it gives \(\hat Q\).

The answer to the opening question: the mesh \(n = 16\) is about \(0.0319\) too high. The true error is \(0.031749\). Let us also recall the previous part. All of this is only the discretization error, the distance to \(\pi^2\), the exact value of the model. If the model is wrong, the staircase stops just as neatly, only at the wrong value (COOK).

2.6 The ruler view: a straight line against \(h^2\)

We can do the same with a ruler. We draw the axes: vertically \(\lambda_{\mathrm{h}}\), horizontally not \(h\) but \(h^2\). Why \(h^2\)? Because \(Q_{\mathrm{h}} = Q + C h^2\), and that is a straight line in the variable \(h^2\), which meets the axis \(h^2 = 0\) at \(Q\). We plot the points, one for each mesh, \(n = 2\) at the top right and \(n = 32\) right next to the axis. They lie almost on a straight line. We take only two of them, \(n = 4\) and \(n = 8\), and draw the line through them. We extend it to the axis, where the intersection is at \(9.867\). The exact value \(\pi^2\) is only \(0.0024\) higher, while the mesh \(n = 8\) was \(0.127\) too high.

What does this view add? The name. We extend the line outside the region where we have points, so this is extrapolation (ekstrapolacija). And the intersection of the line is exactly the formula of the book,

\[\hat Q = Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} + \frac{Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q_{\mathrm{h}}}{2^q - 1} ,\]

where \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/2} - Q_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the last difference with its sign and \(2^q - 1\) the number of parts from counting quarters. For the last pair of meshes this is \(\hat Q = Q_{\mathrm{h}/4} - d_2/3\), the orange remainder subtracted from \(Q_{\mathrm{h}/4}\). In the book: (17.9).

2.7 The historical view

L. F. Richardson used difference equations on grids of points for plane problems of elasticity and published this in 1910 (TIMO). The formula carries his name (COOK, ONATE1), but the sources do not say that he introduced it at that time. In the book: 17.3.4.

2.8 What if we guess the order wrong?

What if we do not measure the order and take \(q' = 1\)? We draw the axes, now with \(h\) horizontally. We plot the points for \(n = 8\), \(16\) and \(32\). They are no longer on a straight line but bend: the point \(n = 32\) lies above the line through the first two. That is the sign of a wrong order. Still, we draw the line through \(n = 8\) and \(16\) to the axis. It lands at \(9.806\), below the exact value. The mesh \(n = 16\) was \(0.032\) too high, the estimate is \(0.064\) too low. The estimate is twice as bad as the mesh we wanted to improve, and it has the opposite sign.

In the language of the staircase: with \(q' = 1\) we believe that every halving leaves half, so we take the remainder to be the whole step. We subtract all of \(d_2 = 0.0957\) instead of a third and overshoot. The book writes this with the factor \(\gamma = 1 - (2^q - 1)/(2^{q'} - 1) = 1 - 3/1 = -2\) that multiplies the leading error: \(-2 \cdot 0.032 = -0.064\). The opposite mistake is milder: with the true \(q = 1\) and \(q' = 2\) taken, \(\gamma = 2/3\). In the book: (17.10).

2.9 Assumptions: why they hold, when they do not, and what they cost

The error is \(C h^q\) with a small remainder. Precisely: \(e(h) = C h^q + R(h)\) with \(C \ne 0\) and \(|R(h)| \le M h^{q+1}\). Why it holds here: we measured the ratios, and with elements half as long they approach \(4\): \(4.14\), \(4.07\), \(4.02\). When not: on coarse meshes the remainder is not yet small, so the first triple gives \(4.14\) instead of \(4\), and for singular solutions and at points that are not nodes the form does not hold at all (17.3.7). What it costs: the estimate is exact only for \(M = 0\), otherwise its error is at most \(\tfrac{3M}{2(2^q - 1)} h^{q+1}\). For our bar the estimate from \(n = 4\) and \(8\) misses by \(2.4 \cdot 10^{-3}\), and the estimates from finer pairs less and less:

meshes error of the estimate \(\hat Q - \pi^2\)
\(n = 4, 8\) \(-2.38 \cdot 10^{-3}\)
\(n = 8, 16\) \(-1.60 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
\(n = 16, 32\) \(-1.01 \cdot 10^{-5}\)

Nested meshes (gnezdene mreže), the quantity at the same place relative to the element. The meshes come from halving and each keeps the nodes of the one before. Why this is needed: only then does \(h\) shrink by exactly a factor of \(2\) each time and \(C\) stays the same. When not: at a point that is not a node, that point sits at a different place in the element on every mesh. On the right of the error figure the error at the point \(x = 0.4\,L\) therefore does not fall evenly but jumps around the grey line of slope \(2\). What it costs: the ratio of differences no longer measures \(2^q\), and the third is no longer right.

The order is the right one. Why: we measured it from the ratio. When not: when we guess it. What it costs: the leading error is multiplied by \(\gamma\), for \(q' = 1\) instead of \(2\) by \(-2\), as the orange error of the estimate shows.

We estimate only the discretization error. The staircase leads to \(\pi^2\), the exact value of the model of the bar with \(EA = \rho A = 1\). Model errors and round-off errors stay as they are. Neither a finer mesh nor extrapolation reduces them.

3 Stress is a slope: good in the middle of an element, poor at its edge

Take the thick-walled steel tube of the book (section 17.4) and draw its cross-section. The inner radius is \(a = 40\) mm, the outer \(b = 60\) mm. In the bore there is a pressure \(p = 30\) MPa, which pushes the wall outwards. The tube is long, so every point of the wall moves only radially, and the displacement depends only on the radius \(r\). The inner wall moves by \(u(a)\), which we draw greatly enlarged. We divide the wall from \(a\) to \(b\) into four linear elements. The program computes the displacements of the nodes, and from them the stresses. We write on the board what it gives at the inner wall and what is right:

inner wall, \(n = 4\) program true value error
displacement \(u(a)\) (µm) 16.8268 16.8735 0.3 %
stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\) (MPa) -19.32 -30 36 %

The same calculation, the same mesh. The displacement is almost exact, the stress at the wall the pressure pushes on misses by a third. How can that be? Let us draw where in the wall the stress misses.

First the axes: horizontally the radius \(r\), from the inner wall at \(40\) mm to the outer at \(60\) mm, vertically the stress in MPa. The grey dashed verticals are the nodes, the borders of the four elements. Into them we draw the true solution, the Lamé curves that we know for the tube from the book. The upper one is the hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (obodna napetost), which stretches the wall around its circumference. The lower one is the radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\). It equals \(-30\) MPa at \(r = a\), because there the pressure pushes on the wall, and zero at \(r = b\), because nothing pushes on the outer face. Now we draw what the program computes, the element stresses, one blackwhite piece for each element. Did you notice that the pieces do not meet at the nodes? Each element tells its own value. Where is the error largest? At the inner wall: element 1 gives there \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -19.32\) instead of \(-30\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 82.33\) instead of \(78.00\) MPa. We do not draw the midpoints of the elements yet, we come back to them at the end.

The answer in one sentence: a linear element replaces the curve of the displacement by its chord, the stress is the slope of the displacement, and the slope of the chord is right in the middle of the element and misses by about \(|u''|\,h/2\) at its edge. So the element stress is good in the middle, poor at the edge, and one order worse than the displacement. One picture shows all of it.

3.1 Where does the program get the stress from at all?

What does the program actually compute? Not stresses. Its unknowns are the displacements of the nodes. Let us draw the axes: horizontally the position \(x\), for the tube this is the radius, vertically the displacement \(u\). We draw the true displacement \(u\), a curve that we do not know. The program knows only two of its points, the nodes \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) of one element. The distance between them is the length of the element \(h\). The linear element joins them with a straight line, the chord \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) (tetiva). The element knows nothing more about the displacement.

And what is stress? First strain. If two neighbouring points move by the same amount, the piece between them only moves and does not stretch. It stretches by the difference of the displacements. Strain is therefore the difference of displacements per unit length, that is the slope (naklon) of the displacement. For a bar, stress is \(E\) times the strain. A straight line has a single slope, the word straight says that already. So the element has a single strain, the slope of the chord:

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{u(x_{\mathrm{b}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{a}})}{h} ,\]

equation (17.17) in the book. The element stress \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the same for the whole element. Stress is a slope, and the question becomes: how good is the slope of the chord?

One assumption has already come in: we drew the nodes exactly on the curve. Why may we? For a bar with \(EA\) constant on each element the nodal displacements are exact, the book proves this (at (17.17)). When does it fail? For the tube. There the factors \(r\) and \(1/r\) in the equations vary along the element, and at \(n = 16\) the displacement of the inner node is \(0.0030\) µm below the true one. What does it do? For the tube the chord picture is not a proof, only the mechanism, so for the tube we will measure the order and compare it with the prediction.

3.2 Why is the slope of the chord right exactly in the middle?

Is the curve anywhere exactly as steep as the chord? Let us slide the chord parallel to itself until it touches the curve. Somewhere between the nodes it must touch it. Why? Think of a road and a straight ramp that start and end at the same points. The road cannot be flatter than the ramp everywhere, or it would not get as high at the end. It cannot be steeper everywhere either. Somewhere it is exactly as steep as the ramp. This is the mean value theorem, in the book corollary 17.18.

Where is that point? In our picture exactly at the midpoint of the element \(m\). The shifted chord is the tangent there, the line with the slope of the curve at that point, \(u'(m)\). Why exactly the middle? The height the chord gains from \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) to \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the sum of all the small gains of the curve on the way. So the slope of the chord is the average of the slope of the curve over the element. The slope of the curve is larger than \(u'(m)\) on the left half and smaller on the right, and since the halves are equally long, this linear part of the deviation cancels in the average. What remains is only the part caused by a change of the curvature, and it is of order \(h^2\): at the midpoint the slopes differ by at most \(\tfrac{1}{24}\,M_3\,h^2\), where \(M_3\) is the largest value of the third derivative of the displacement. In the book: 17.19, point 1. In the picture the curve is a parabola, its third derivative is zero, so the tangent at the middle is exactly parallel to the chord.

The assumption of this step: the displacement is smooth on the element, with a bounded third derivative. Why is it right? Inside an element the material and the section are the same, so the displacement has no kink. When does it fail? Where the section or the material changes abruptly, the slope has a jump. There must be a node there, otherwise the bound does not hold. What does it do? The error at the midpoint is at most \(\tfrac{1}{24}\,M_3\,h^2\).

3.3 Why is it wrong at the edge, and by how much?

And at the edge of the element, is the chord still as steep as it should be? Let us draw the tangent at the node \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\). It is steeper than the chord. By how much? The slope of the curve changes along the element at the rate \(u''\), the second derivative, the curvature of the displacement. From the edge to the middle is half an element, \(h/2\). On this way the slope changes by about \(|u''|\,h/2\), and by that much the tangent at the edge differs from the chord. The element makes this difference of slopes at its edge, and multiplied by \(E\) it is the error of the stress at the edge. In the book: 17.19, point 2.

What happens when we halve \(h\)? The error at the edge halves, because it is proportional to \(h\): order 1. The error at the middle falls to a quarter, because it is proportional to \(h^2\): order 2. The displacement has an error of order \(h^2\) everywhere. So the stress at the edge is one order worse than the displacement. This is the answer to the question on the board, in kind. Does it hold in numbers too?

Assumption: linear elements. Why? We chose this element, its displacement is the straight chord. When does it fail? For elements of higher degree, whose displacement on an element is a polynomial of degree \(p\). What does it do? The element stresses have the order \(p\), so \(1\) for linear elements (COOK, quoted in 17.4).

3.4 Does it really happen in a calculation?

How do we check a picture drawn freehand? On a case where we can compute everything to the end: the hanging steel bar with a step from the book.

We draw a ceiling and hang the bar from it. The upper half is thick, the lower half has only a quarter of the section. We measure the depth \(x\) from the ceiling down: the step is at \(5\) m, the end of the bar at \(10\) m. The bar is loaded only by its own weight. What is the stress at depth \(x\)? Let us cut the bar there. The section carries everything that hangs below it. The deeper we cut, the less hangs below, so in the upper half the stress falls linearly with depth. We divide the bar into eight elements and look at the first one, from the ceiling to the depth \(1.25\) m.

Let us draw the axes: horizontally the depth \(x\) of the first element, from \(0\) to \(1.25\) m, vertically the stress in MPa. The stress axis does not start at zero, otherwise we would hardly see the difference. We draw the true stress \(\rho g\,(6.25 - x)\), a straight line with \(\rho g = 76\,518\) Pa/m. The mesh has eight elements. The stress of the first element is the horizontal line at \(0.43041\) MPa. Where does it cross the true line? In the middle, at \(x = 0.625\) m, where the true stress is also \(0.43041\) MPa, to all five digits. The stress is linear here, the third derivative of the displacement is zero, so the middle is exact.

At the edge at \(x = 0\), however, the true stress is \(0.47824\) MPa, while the element still shows \(0.43041\) MPa. The difference is \(0.0478\) MPa. Where does this number come from? The true stress falls by \(\rho g\) for every metre, from the edge to the middle is half an element, \(0.625\) m, so the difference is

\[76\,518\ \text{Pa/m} \cdot 0.625\ \text{m} = 0.0478\ \text{MPa} = \rho g\,\frac{h}{2} ,\]

exactly the prediction of the chord picture. That is \(11\) % of the element stress. At \(n = 16\) the element is half as long and the error at the edge is \(0.0239\) MPa, half as large. In the middle it is zero again.

3.5 And in the tube of the question?

So where do we expect the program to hit for the tube? At the midpoints of the elements. Let us return to the tube and draw what we left out before, the midpoints of the elements. The dots lie almost on the Lamé curves, at \(n = 4\) at most \(0.046\) MPa from them. The circles at the wall are far off, as the tangent at the edge predicts. The number agrees too. The radial stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u' + \lambda\,u/r\). The part \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'\) is constant on the element, and it misses by the difference of slopes multiplied by \(\lambda + 2\mu\). On the board:

  • prediction \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,|u''|\,h/2\) at \(n = 16\): \(2.82\) MPa,
  • actual error of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\) at \(n = 16\): \(2.88\) MPa,
  • measured order at the midpoints of the elements: \(1.88\) to \(1.98\) (predicted 2),
  • measured order at the edges: \(0.89\) to \(0.98\) (predicted 1).

The assumption of the tube: plane strain (ravninsko deformacijsko stanje). Why? The tube is long and its ends are held by smooth rigid walls. When does it fail? Near the ends of the tube, where a real wall would hold it differently. What does it do? The Lamé curves are the solution of this model, so all the numbers here are mesh errors within it. What the model itself costs we say at the end.

3.6 Why is the boundary condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) not met?

We gave the program the pressure of \(30\) MPa ourselves. Why does it not return it at the wall? This is the view through the balance of forces. The pressure in the bore enters the calculation as a force at the inner node. The equation of that node balances it with the average of the stress over the first element, weighted over the element, not with the value at the wall. So the method never promised \(-30\) MPa at the wall. It promised the balance of forces at the node. As \(h\) goes to zero, the average over the element approaches the value at the wall, with order 1, like every error at an edge. In the book: 17.17 and the paragraph after lemma 17.19.

3.7 Why may we not simply average at the nodes?

At a node we have two values, one from each element. Which one should the program print? It often prints the average of the two neighbouring elements, nodal averaging (povprečenje v vozliščih). Where the true stress is smooth, this is good. Let us see where it is not.

The same hanging bar, now all of it. We draw the axes, the depth \(x\) to \(10\) m, the dashed vertical at \(5\) m is the step, where the section shrinks to a quarter. The true stress: the axial force is continuous across the step, but the area falls to a quarter. Stress is force per area, so the same force on a four times smaller area gives a four times larger stress: a jump from \(0.0956\) to \(0.3826\) MPa. We draw the stresses of the eight elements. There is a node at the step, so each element lies on one side, and each crosses the true line in its middle, as the first element did before. The average of the two elements at the step, however, is \(0.2391\) MPa, between the two true values. Where in the bar is this stress? Nowhere. And a finer mesh does not remove it: at \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16\) the average is always \(0.2391\) MPa, because each element approaches its own true value while their average stays in the middle. In the thin part this would underestimate the stress by \(37.5\) %.

So the assumption of averaging is that the true stress is smooth across the node. It fails at a step in the section or at the border of two materials, and there the average is wrong independently of \(n\). One does not average across such a border.

3.8 What does the program see without the true solution?

The program does not know the true solution. What can it see at all, then? It sees the jumps between the blackwhite pieces at the nodes of the tube. The largest jump of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is \(16.71\) MPa at \(n = 4\), then \(9.87\), \(5.39\) and \(2.82\) MPa at \(n = 8, 16, 32\). At each doubling of \(n\) it falls to almost half, like an error of order 1. The jump is therefore a gauge of where the mesh is too coarse. The ZZ error estimate, which the book describes in its history box (17.4), stands on this idea. The same trap as with averaging holds: across a step the jump is not an error.

3.9 From the ground back up

Now we build the answer again, from the ground. A straight line has one slope, so an element has one strain, the slope of the chord. The slope of the chord is the average slope of the curve, so it is right in the middle and misses at the edge by \(|u''|\,h/2\). We therefore read stresses at the midpoints of the elements. At the wall, where the engineer needs them most, the order is 1.

What does this mean for the Richardson extrapolation of the previous part? With order 1, halving the element halves the error, so the error of the fine mesh equals the last difference itself (\(2^1 - 1 = 1\)). On the board for \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\), the hoop stress at the wall:

  • \(n = 16\): \(79.218\) MPa, \(n = 32\): \(78.621\) MPa,
  • with order 1: \(2 \cdot 78.621 - 79.218 = 78.024\) MPa, error \(0.024\) MPa,
  • with the wrong order 2: \((4 \cdot 78.621 - 79.218)/3 = 78.422\) MPa, error \(0.42\) MPa,
  • true value: \(78.00\) MPa.

The wrong order gives an error about 18 times larger, and without a warning: the value looks nice. The order must be measured or derived, not guessed (17.14).

Let us end with the quantity the engineer really compares with a limit, the equivalent stress (primerjalna napetost) at the wall.

At the top are the true principal stresses at the wall and the von Mises equivalent stress \(94.02\) MPa. At the bottom is the same calculation with the values of element 1 at \(n = 16\): \(92.68\) MPa, \(1.43\) % too low. Why too low? The larger part of the error comes from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), which is closer to zero than \(-30\) MPa, so the difference \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is too small. That is exactly the error at the edge from the tangent.

One last picture. The axes: horizontally the number of elements \(n\), doubled each time, vertically the error in per cent, both scales logarithmic. The error of the displacement falls steeply, by a factor 4 per doubling. The error of the equivalent stress falls more gently, by a factor 2. This is the chord picture at one glance: the stress is one order worse. The tolerance \(2\) % is met at \(n = 16\), \(1.43\) %. But the error is one-sided and on the unsafe side: the yield pressure, at which the equivalent stress at the wall reaches the yield limit, is \(71.21\) MPa from the mesh against the true \(70.20\) MPa. Richardson with the measured order 1 gives \(2 \cdot 93.3449 - 92.6803 = 94.01\) MPa against the true \(94.02\) MPa.

And what remains is the part no mesh reduces: the difference between plane strain and closed ends is \(0.53\) %, between open and closed ends \(3.2\) %. This is the model error of the first part. So we first decide what ends the tube really has, and only then refine the mesh. In the book: 17.9.4.
