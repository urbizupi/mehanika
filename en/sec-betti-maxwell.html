Chapter 4 Energy principles and virtual work

4.8 Reciprocity: Betti's and Maxwell's theorems

So far we have loaded every body with a single load. The bracket of two bars from the beginning of the chapter carried a load at the joint \(C\), the hanging bar its own weight. We computed the displacement of the joint \(C\) under the load in several ways, from the changes of the lengths of the bars in section 4.4, with the minimum of the total potential energy in section 4.6 and with virtual forces in section 4.7. Structures, however, usually carry several loads at once, and the loads change. Besides the load, the wind pushes on the bracket, and a lorry drives across a bridge, now here, now there. Do we have to compute everything again for every new combination of loads? And is there any connection between what one load causes and what another load causes?

Engineers had the same question when computing statically indeterminate structures for moving loads, such as a truck on a bridge. They helped themselves with influence lines, which show how a chosen quantity, for example the displacement of a chosen point, changes as the load travels over the structure (TIMO, PDF pp. 224 and 259). In drawing these lines a theorem came in handy that Maxwell presented in 1864 for the case of two forces (CM, PDF p. 320, TIMO, PDF p. 259, EP, PDF p. 230). It says that the displacement of the second point in the direction of the second force, caused by the first force, equals the displacement of the first point in the direction of the first force, caused by the second force, if the forces are equally large. Why does this help? The downward displacement of the point \(A\) when the load stands at the point \(B\) equals the downward displacement of the point \(B\) when the same load stands at \(A\). To get the displacement of the point \(A\) for a load at every place \(B\), we therefore do not need to put the load at every place separately. One load at \(A\) and the displacements of all points \(B\) are enough.

The general proof was given in 1872 by Enrico Betti, so one says that Betti generalized Maxwell's work (EP, PDF p. 230, CM, PDF p. 320, TIMO, PDF p. 259-260). John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh, extended it to the vibration of elastic systems (TIMO, PDF p. 259-260, LOVE, PDF p. 192).

The goal of the section is to obtain three results that answer these questions for bodies of a linear elastic material with small displacements. The first says that the effects of loads add up: the displacements and stresses caused by two loads together are the sum of the displacements and stresses that each causes alone. The second compares the work of two loads. The work that the first load would do on the displacements caused by the second equals the work that the second load would do on the displacements caused by the first. The third is a special case of the second for two forces, each of magnitude 1 N. The displacement of the point of application of the first force in its direction, caused by the second force, equals the displacement of the point of application of the second force in its direction, caused by the first force. The second result is named after Betti (1823-1892), the third after Maxwell (1831-1879) (EP §4.6.4 and §4.6.5, pp. 227 and 230). The property that the roles of two loads may be exchanged is called reciprocity (vzajemnost).

The route is as follows. First we compute the displacement of the joint \(C\) of the bracket under an arbitrary force at the joint. In the result we notice both phenomena: the displacement is a sum of contributions of the two components of the force, and the horizontal displacement caused by a vertical force equals the vertical displacement caused by an equally large horizontal force. We prove the first phenomenon for every body. The second we first explain with the work the two forces do when we put them on the bracket in different orders, and then we prove it for every body, from the principle of virtual displacements and the symmetry of the elastic constants. At the end we obtain the third result from it, test it on the bracket and on the hanging bar, and show how it is used in measurement.

The physical model is the same as in section 4.6. Let us list the assumptions and say for each where we use it.

  1. Small displacements. The strains are small. Equilibrium and strains are computed on the shape of the body before loading, the strain with the small strain tensor (definition 1.115). In the bracket the strains of the bars are smaller than one thousandth (section 4.1.4). Therefore all equations that determine the displacements are linear, which we use when adding loads, and the principle of virtual displacements holds (theorem 4.82), on which the proofs of both theorems of the section rest.
  2. Linear and ideal elastic material. The stresses are below the yield stress, as in the steel bars of the bracket (section 4.1.4). The stress components are linear functions of the strain components, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}\) (definition 3.16), and the material stores the work it receives and returns it on unloading (definition 3.43). Therefore \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\) (theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants). We use the linearity when adding loads and the symmetry of the constants in the proof of the relation between the works of two loads.
  3. Stable material. The strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero (section 3.6.6). We use it when we show that a load determines the displacements uniquely.
  4. Loads independent of the deformation. The magnitude and direction of the loads do not change with the deformation (section 4.6.3). Therefore the work of a load on displacements is the work of a constant force, as in Clapeyron's theorem.
  5. Fixed supports. On a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of the surface the body is held, and the displacement there is zero, for all the loads we compare. The supports shall prevent every small rigid displacement of the body, that is, a translation and a small rotation of the body as a whole, in which the distances between its points do not change. We use the first in both theorems, the second for uniqueness.
  6. Slow loading. The forces grow so slowly that the velocities of the points are negligible, as in section 4.1. We need it only in the explanation with the order of loading. The proofs do not need it, because they speak only about final states.

4.8.1 The bracket with two forces

Let us restate the setting of the bracket from section 4.1.4 (figure 4.107). The bracket lies in a vertical plane. The axis \(x\) points horizontally to the right, the axis \(y\) vertically up, and the origin is at the joint \(C\). The horizontal bar \(AC\) has the length \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m, the inclined bar \(BC\) the length \(L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5\) m. The pins \(A = (-4, 0, 0)\) m and \(B = (-4, 3, 0)\) m are fixed to the wall. Both bars are of steel, with \(E = 196\) GPa, and have the same cross section with the area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\). We therefore simply write \(EA = E\,A_{\mathrm{AC}} = E\,A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^7\) N. The truss model (definition 4.4) assumes pins without friction, forces only at the joints and bars without weight, and as in section 4.1.4 we assume that compressed bars stay straight, without buckling. Instead of the load, however, let an arbitrary force in the plane of the bracket act at the joint \(C\), \(\vek F = (F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\). The load from the beginning of the chapter is the case \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -10\) kN.

We denote the displacement of the joint \(C\) by \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (u, v, 0)\), as in sections 4.4.9 and 4.6.5: \(u\) is the horizontal displacement, positive to the right, \(v\) the vertical displacement, positive upwards. Let us compute them in five steps.

  1. Equilibrium of the pin \(C\). A bar with the axial force \(N\) acts on the pin with the force \(-N\,\vek n\), where \(\vek n\) is the unit vector along the bar from the wall towards \(C\), \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0)\) (section 4.1.4). The sum of the three forces on the pin is zero:
    \[(F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) - N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,(1, 0, 0) - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0) = (0, 0, 0) .\]
  2. The forces in the bars. The \(y\) component of the equation gives \(F_{\mathrm{y}} + \tfrac35\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), so \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -\tfrac53\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\). The \(x\) component gives \(F_{\mathrm{x}} - N_{\mathrm{AC}} - \tfrac45\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), so \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = F_{\mathrm{x}} - \tfrac45\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F_{\mathrm{x}} + \tfrac43\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\). For the load, \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -10\) kN, we get \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16{,}67\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -13{,}33\) kN, as in section 4.1.4.
  3. The elongations of the bars. A bar with a constant axial force \(N\) gets longer by \(\Delta L = N L/(EA)\) (section 4.1.4). For our two bars \(L_{\mathrm{AC}}/(EA) = 4\ \text{m}/(EA)\) and \(L_{\mathrm{BC}}/(EA) = 5\ \text{m}/(EA)\), so
    \[\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = \frac{4\ \text{m}}{EA}\,\Big(F_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac43\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\Big) , \qquad \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = \frac{5\ \text{m}}{EA}\,\Big({-\frac53}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\Big) .\]
  4. The elongations from the displacement. For small displacements the change of length of a bar is the projection of the displacement of the joint \(C\) onto the direction of the bar. We showed this for a virtual displacement in equation (4.48), and since the derivation used only that the displacement is small, in section 4.4.9 we used the same for the actual displacement, \(\Delta L = \vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\). Therefore
    \[\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = u , \qquad \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac45\,u - \tfrac35\,v .\]
  5. The displacement. From the first equation of step 4, \(u = \Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}\), from the second \(v = \tfrac43\,u - \tfrac53\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}\). We insert the elongations from step 3:
    \[u = \frac{4\ \text{m}}{EA}\,F_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\text{m}}{EA}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad v = \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\text{m}}{EA}\,F_{\mathrm{x}} + 21\,\frac{\text{m}}{EA}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} . \tag{4.82}\]
    The number \(\tfrac{16}{3}\) in the expression for \(u\) is \(4 \cdot \tfrac43\) from the elongation of the bar \(AC\). In the expression for \(v\), \(\tfrac{16}{3}\) is the number \(\tfrac43 \cdot 4\) from the term \(\tfrac43\,u\). The number 21 at \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) in the expression for \(v\) is the sum of two contributions, \(\tfrac43 \cdot \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{64}{9}\) from the term \(\tfrac43\,u\) and \(\tfrac53 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac53 = \tfrac{125}{9}\) from the term \(-\tfrac53\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}\), together \(\tfrac{189}{9} = 21\).

Let us check with the load. For \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -10^4\) N

\[u = \frac{16}{3} \cdot \frac{1\ \text{m} \cdot (-10^4\ \text{N})}{1{,}96 \cdot 10^7\ \text{N}} = -2{,}721\ \text{mm} , \qquad v = 21 \cdot \frac{1\ \text{m} \cdot (-10^4\ \text{N})}{1{,}96 \cdot 10^7\ \text{N}} = -10{,}71\ \text{mm} .\]

Under the load the joint moves by 2,721 mm towards the wall and by 10,71 mm down, as we computed in sections 4.4, 4.6 and 4.7. The horizontal displacement is exactly the shortening of the compressed bar \(AC\), because this bar is horizontal.

The result (4.82) shows two things. The first is that each component of the displacement is a sum of two terms, one proportional to \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the other proportional to \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\). The force \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) therefore causes the sum of the displacements that the forces \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\) and \((0, F_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) would cause each alone, and a force twice as large causes a displacement twice as large. Why? In each of the five steps we only multiplied numbers by constant numbers and added them. We wrote the equilibrium on the shape before loading, so the directions \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\) do not depend on the force. The elongation of a bar is proportional to its force, and the projection is a sum of products of components with constant numbers.

Let us name the four numbers in front of the forces in (4.82):

\[u = D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}} + D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad v = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}} + D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} .\]

The first index gives the direction of the displacement, the second the direction of the force. Each number is a displacement per newton of force. The number \(D_{\mathrm{yx}}\), for example, is the vertical displacement of the joint caused by a horizontal force of 1 N. For our bracket

\[\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{4\ \text{m}}{EA} = 204{,}1\ \frac{\text{µm}}{\text{kN}} , \\ D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}} &= \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{\text{m}}{EA} = 272{,}1\ \frac{\text{µm}}{\text{kN}} , \\ D_{\mathrm{yy}} &= \frac{21\ \text{m}}{EA} = 1071{,}4\ \frac{\text{µm}}{\text{kN}} , \end{aligned}\]

where we wrote the displacements in micrometres per kilonewton of force. The second thing the result shows is exactly the equality \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\). A horizontal force of 1 kN to the right moves the joint by 272,1 µm up, and a vertical force of 1 kN upwards moves it by 272,1 µm to the right (figure 4.108).

Let us look at how each of the two displacements arises. For the horizontal force \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) kN, step 2 gives the forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = 1\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\). The bar \(AC\) gets longer by \(1\ \text{kN} \cdot D_{\mathrm{xx}} = 204{,}1\) µm, while the bar \(BC\) keeps its length. The joint must therefore stay 5 m from the pin \(B\) and moves along the circle about \(B\), for a small displacement perpendicular to the bar \(BC\) (figure 4.108, left). The second equation of step 4 with \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\) gives \(v = \tfrac43\,u = \tfrac43 \cdot 204{,}1\ \text{µm} = 272{,}1\) µm. Here the factor \(\tfrac43\) comes from geometry: the direction perpendicular to the bar \(BC\) goes 4 units up for every 3 units sideways. We made the same argument in section 4.7 for the displacement under the load (figure 4.84). For the vertical force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) kN upwards, step 2 gives the forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = \tfrac43\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -\tfrac53\) kN. The horizontal displacement is the elongation of the bar \(AC\), \(\tfrac43\ \text{kN} \cdot 4\ \text{m}/(EA) = 272{,}1\) µm (figure 4.108, right). Here the factor \(\tfrac43\) comes from equilibrium: this many kilonewtons must be carried by the horizontal bar when the inclined bar balances the vertical force of 1 kN. The same triangle with the sides 3, 4 and 5, two different routes, the same number. Is this a coincidence of this bracket? It is not. In the next two subsections we will see that the equality holds for every elastic body, and that its reason is the work the two forces do.

4.8.2 Adding loads

The first observation holds for every body that satisfies the assumptions from the beginning of the section. To state it precisely, we must first say which displacement field the body takes under a given load. Let the body be as in theorem 4.82 on the principle of virtual displacements: before loading it occupies the set of points \(V\) with the surface \(S\). On a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of the surface it is held, and on the remaining part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) a prescribed traction vector \(\bar{\vek t}\) acts on it, the force per unit area with which the surroundings act on the body. A body force \(\vek b\) acts in the body (definition 2.26). The displacement field that the body takes in equilibrium must meet three requirements, which combine what we learned in the first three chapters: strains from displacements, the relation between stresses and strains, and equilibrium.

Definition 4.132 Solution for a load (rešitev za obremenitev)

Let the body \(V\) and the division of its surface into the parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) be as in theorem 4.82 on the principle of virtual displacements, and let the displacement \(\vek 0\) be prescribed on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). Let the material be linear elastic, with the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kl}}\) (definition 3.16). A load on the body is a pair \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t})\): a body force \(\vek b\), continuous on \(V\), and a prescribed traction vector \(\bar{\vek t}\), continuous on each outer face of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). A displacement field \(\vek u\) is a solution for the load \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t})\) if:

  1. \(\vek u\) is continuously differentiable (definition 1.90) on an open set that contains the body together with its surface, and is equal to \(\vek 0\) at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\),
  2. the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), computed from the components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek u\), are continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface, and satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) at every interior point of the body,
  3. Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) holds at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal of the surface.

Source: EP §4.6.1, (4.6.1)-(4.6.4) (p. 218), SADD §6.2 (PDF p. 133), BOWER §5.8.1 (PDF p. 379).

The first requirement says that the field respects the supports and has a small strain tensor at every point. The second says that the stresses are as the material requires, and that every small piece of the body is in equilibrium (theorem 2.28). The third says that the stresses at the surface carry the prescribed forces. All three requirements are written on the shape of the body before loading, as assumption 1 requires. The stresses of a solution are symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\), because the elastic constants are symmetric in the first pair of indices, \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{jikl}}\) (lemma 3.17, item 2). A solution therefore satisfies the assumptions that the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) makes about the stresses, and the assumptions that the principle of minimum total potential energy (theorem 4.91) makes about the true displacement field. Can one load have two different solutions? If it could, we could not speak of the displacement that the load causes, because there would be two of them. It cannot.

Lemma 4.133 Uniqueness of the solution (enoličnost rešitve)

Let the body be as in definition 4.132, of an ideal elastic material whose strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero. Let the body be in one piece: its interior points form a connected open set (definition 1.130), and every point of the body is the limit of a sequence of its interior points, so that the body has no parts without an interior, such as a sheet without thickness. Let \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be solutions for the same load \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t})\), both with small strains. Then:

  1. the strains and stresses of the two solutions are equal at every point of the body,
  2. the difference \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is a small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). If the supports prevent every small rigid displacement of the body, then \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\).
Proof

We must show that the difference of the two solutions has no strain, and draw from this the conclusions about the stresses and the displacements. We use the principle of minimum total potential energy, once for each solution.

  1. By requirement 1 of definition 4.132 both fields are continuously differentiable and equal to \(\vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so they are kinematically admissible displacement fields with the prescribed displacement \(\vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) (definition 4.88).
  2. By requirements 2 and 3 the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) are continuously differentiable and satisfy the equilibrium equations and, on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), Cauchy's formula. The load \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t})\) is given and does not change with the deformation, as the theorem requires. By theorem 4.91 on the principle of minimum total potential energy, item 1, applied with the other admissible field \(\tilde{\vek u}\), therefore \(\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) - \Pi(\vek u) \ge 0\).
  3. The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is a solution too, so we may exchange the roles, and the same theorem gives \(\Pi(\vek u) - \Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) \ge 0\).
  4. A number that is not negative and whose opposite is not negative either is zero, so \(\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) = \Pi(\vek u)\). By item 2 of the same theorem the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek v = \tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is zero at every point of the body.
  5. A partial derivative of a sum is the sum of the partial derivatives (lemma 1.81), so the strain of the field \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u + \vek v\) is the sum of the strains of the fields \(\vek u\) and \(\vek v\), and thus equal to the strain of the field \(\vek u\). The stresses are computed from the strains (definition 3.16), so they are equal too. This is item 1.
  6. The field \(\vek v\) is continuously differentiable, since the fields \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) are (lemma 1.81, corollary 1.69, item 1), and by step 4 it has a zero small strain tensor at every point of the body. The interior points of the body form a connected open set. So by theorem 1.134 on a displacement field without strain there are a constant vector \(\vek a\) and a constant antisymmetric matrix \(\mat W\) such that \(\vek v(P) = \vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\) at every interior point \(P\). A continuously differentiable field is continuous (section 1.15.6), and the field \(\vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\) is a polynomial in the coordinates and therefore continuous (corollary 1.69, item 2). Let \(P\) be a point of the surface and \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) a sequence of interior points that converges to \(P\). By lemma 1.68, used for each component, the sequence \(\vek v(P_{\mathrm{n}})\) converges to \(\vek v(P)\), and the sequence \(\vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{n}}}\) to \(\vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\). The two sequences are equal term by term, so by lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence their limits are equal too, and the equality holds at the points of the surface as well. By (2.32), \(\mat W\,\vek r = \delta\boldsymbol\varphi\times\vek r\) for \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi = (W_{\mathrm{zy}}, W_{\mathrm{xz}}, W_{\mathrm{yx}})\), which we check component by component, for the component \(x\) for example \(W_{\mathrm{xx}}\,x + W_{\mathrm{xy}}\,y + W_{\mathrm{xz}}\,z = W_{\mathrm{xz}}\,z - W_{\mathrm{yx}}\,y\), because \(W_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) (lemma 1.103, item 3) and \(W_{\mathrm{xy}} = -W_{\mathrm{yx}}\). So the field \(\vek v\) is a small rigid displacement of lemma 4.72 with \(\delta\vek a = \vek a\). On \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(\vek v = \vek 0 - \vek 0 = \vek 0\).
  7. If the supports prevent every small rigid displacement, the only small rigid displacement that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the field \(\vek 0\). Therefore \(\vek v = \vek 0\) and \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\). This is item 2.

Source: SADD §6.2, (6.2.1)-(6.2.5) (PDF p. 133), EP §4.6.3, (4.6.14)-(4.6.21) (pp. 225-226), CM (7.3.45) (PDF p. 314). The sources insert the difference of the two solutions directly into the strain energy. The proof with the principle of minimum total potential energy is not from the sources. The statement in step 6, that a field without strain is a small rigid displacement, SADD states without proof (PDF p. 133), and in this book it is proved by theorem 1.134 on a displacement field without strain.

The proof showed why there is only one solution. Two solutions would have the same smallest total potential energy, while a difference with a strain that is not zero would increase the energy. The difference therefore has no strain and can only be a rigid displacement, which the supports do not allow. What if they allow it? The hanging bar of chapter 1 is held only at the centre \(O\) of its top face. A fixed point prevents a translation, but not a rotation of the bar about \(O\), so CM adds the condition that the material at the point \(O\) does not rotate (CM, PDF p. 306). Without this condition the stresses would still be determined uniquely, the displacements only up to a small rotation. In the bracket of two bars the pins \(A\) and \(B\) are enough. The two elongations determined the displacement of the joint \(C\) uniquely in step 4, since we computed it from them. Step 6 uses the two assumptions on a body in one piece. Without the first, theorem 1.134 on a displacement field without strain fails, as the two separate cubes of figure 1.149 show, each of which moves rigidly in its own way. The second carries the equality \(\vek v = \vek a + \mat W\,\overrightarrow{OP}\) from the interior points to the surface, where the supports are. Only because of it does the condition \(\vek v = \vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) restrict the rigid displacement. For a body made of boxes the second assumption holds by itself, as we saw in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.82.

Now we can state the first observation for every body.

Lemma 4.134 Principle of superposition (načelo superpozicije)

Let the body be as in definition 4.132, let \(\vek u^{(1)}\) be a solution for the load \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)})\) and \(\vek u^{(2)}\) a solution for the load \((\vek b^{(2)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(2)})\), with the strains \(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the stresses \(\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be numbers. Then the field \(\alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) is a solution for the load

\[\big(\alpha\,\vek b^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek b^{(2)},\ \alpha\,\bar{\vek t}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\big) ,\]

its strains are \(\alpha\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \beta\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and its stresses \(\alpha\,\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \beta\,\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

Proof

For the field \(\vek u = \alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) we must check the three requirements of definition 4.132. Each follows from the requirement being linear.

  1. A partial derivative of a sum is the sum of the partial derivatives, and a partial derivative of a multiple is the multiple of the partial derivative (lemma 1.81). A sum of continuous functions of a point and a multiple of a continuous function are continuous functions (corollary 1.69, item 1). Therefore \(\vek u\) is continuously differentiable. On \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(\vek u = \alpha\,\vek 0 + \beta\,\vek 0 = \vek 0\). This is requirement 1.
  2. By the same rule for derivatives, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \alpha\,\partial u^{(1)}_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \beta\,\partial u^{(2)}_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\) and \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\). The component of the small strain tensor \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\) is therefore \(\alpha\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \beta\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  3. By definition 3.16 the stresses are
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\big(\alpha\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}} + \beta\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\big) = \alpha \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}} + \beta \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{kl}} = \alpha\,\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \beta\,\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} ,\]
    because a finite sum may be split into two sums and the constant numbers \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) taken out. By step 1, applied to the stresses, they are continuously differentiable.
  4. We insert the stresses from step 3 and the body force \(\alpha\,\vek b^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek b^{(2)}\) into the first equilibrium equation (2.31) and use the rule for derivatives from step 1:
    \[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\Big(\frac{\partial\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b^{(1)}_{\mathrm{x}}\Big) + \beta\,\Big(\frac{\partial\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b^{(2)}_{\mathrm{x}}\Big) .\]
    Both brackets are zero, because \(\vek u^{(1)}\) and \(\vek u^{(2)}\) are solutions, so the left side is \(\alpha \cdot 0 + \beta \cdot 0 = 0\). The same holds for the second and third equilibrium equations. This is requirement 2.
  5. On \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the component \(i\) of the column \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is, by (1.38),
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \big(\alpha\,\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \beta\,\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\big)\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = \alpha \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} + \beta \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} ,\]
    where we split the finite sum and took out the numbers as in step 3. On the right are the components \(i\) of the columns \(\ten\sigma^{(1)}\vek n = \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}\) and \(\ten\sigma^{(2)}\vek n = \bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\), so \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \alpha\,\bar{\vek t}^{(1)} + \beta\,\bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\). This is requirement 3.

Source: EP §4.6.1, (4.6.1)-(4.6.4) (pp. 217-218), CM §7.4.1 (PDF p. 314). EP states the principle for all problems that are linear geometrically and in the material, but does not write a proof. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The lemma together with lemma 4.133 on the uniqueness of the solution says what we saw in the computation for the bracket. If the supports prevent rigid displacements, \(\alpha\,\vek u^{(1)} + \beta\,\vek u^{(2)}\) is the only solution for the combined load. We may therefore compute the displacements and stresses of two loads separately and add them, and a load increased by the factor \(\alpha\) causes \(\alpha\) times larger displacements and stresses. It is therefore enough to compute the effect of each load on a structure once, and the combinations follow by adding. The same holds for the bracket, which is not a body in the sense of the definition but a model of bars. There the linearity was shown by the computation (4.82).

When does adding fail? Each of the linearities we used can fail. For large displacements we may no longer write the equilibrium on the shape before loading, and the directions of the bars of the bracket would depend on the displacement, as they would for a rope that sags under a load. A material above the yield stress (definition 2.7) or rubber is not linear elastic. A load that changes with the deformation, like water pressure on a wall that tilts (section 4.6.3), does not allow adding, because the force is no longer determined by the load alone. Adding also fails for a support that can only push the body and cannot pull it, for example for a body lying on a table. Under one load the body touches the table, under another it lifts off, and the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is not the same for both. EP states the principle for problems that are linear geometrically and in the material (EP, p. 218).

Displacements and stresses add up, but energy does not. Take the bracket with a horizontal force \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) kN and a vertical force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) kN upwards at the joint \(C\). By step 2 the force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) alone gives the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = 1\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\), the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) alone \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = \tfrac43\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -\tfrac53\) kN, and both together the sums, \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = \tfrac73\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -\tfrac53\) kN. The strain energy of the bracket is \(U = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}}^2 L_{\mathrm{i}}/(2EA)\) by (4.5), and with SymPy we get

\[U(F_{\mathrm{x}}) = 0{,}1020\ \text{J} , \qquad U(F_{\mathrm{y}}) = 0{,}5357\ \text{J} , \qquad U(F_{\mathrm{x}}\ \text{and}\ F_{\mathrm{y}}) = 0{,}9099\ \text{J} .\]

The sum of the first two is only \(0{,}6378\) J, \(0{,}2721\) J less than the energy with both forces. The reason is the square in the formula for the energy. With both forces the force in a bar is the sum \(N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)} + N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)}\) of the forces caused by each force alone, and

\[\big(N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)} + N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)}\big)^2 = \big(N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)}\big)^2 + 2\,N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)} N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)} + \big(N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)}\big)^2 .\]

The first and last terms give the energies of each force alone, and the middle term adds the mixed energy \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)} N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)} L_{\mathrm{i}}/(EA)\). For the bracket this is \(1\ \text{kN} \cdot \tfrac43\ \text{kN} \cdot 4\ \text{m}/(EA) + 0 \cdot \big({-\tfrac53}\ \text{kN}\big) \cdot 5\ \text{m}/(EA) = 0{,}2721\) J. EP and CM say this explicitly: energy does not add up, because it is a quadratic function of the displacements or forces (EP, p. 220, CM §7.4.1, PDF p. 314). The mixed energy is exactly \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 272{,}1\ \text{µm} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 0{,}2721\) J. The next subsection shows that this is no coincidence.

4.8.3 The order of loading

Let us load the bracket slowly with the forces \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) kN to the right and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1\) kN upwards, first with the first and then with the second, and count the work the two forces do (figure 4.109, top). We need the work of a force that grows in proportion to the displacement of its point of application. Let us compute it in three steps.

  1. Let the point of application move along a straight segment by \(\Delta\vek u = \ell\,\vek d\), where \(\ell\) is the length of the segment and \(\vek d\) the unit vector of its direction, and let the force grow in proportion to the distance travelled \(s\), from zero to \(\vek F\). At the distance \(s\) the force is \((s/\ell)\,\vek F\), and its projection onto the direction of motion is \((s/\ell)\,\vek F\cdot\vek d\).
  2. By item 2 of definition 4.1 the work is \(\int_0^\ell (s/\ell)\,\vek F\cdot\vek d\ \mathrm{d}s\). The function \(s^2/(2\ell)\) has the derivative \(s/\ell\) (lemma 1.81), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 the work equals \(\tfrac12\,\ell\,\vek F\cdot\vek d\).
  3. Since \(\ell\,\vek d = \Delta\vek u\), this is \(\tfrac12\,\vek F\cdot\Delta\vek u\), half the work that the constant force \(\vek F\) would do on the same displacement (item 1 of definition 4.1).

For a bar we got the same in theorem 4.3 on the work and energy of a bar. Now let us count.

  1. The force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) grows slowly from zero to 1 kN, the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) is not there yet. By (4.82) the joint moves along a straight segment, in proportion to the force, and at the end \(u = D_{\mathrm{xx}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 204{,}1\) µm and \(v = D_{\mathrm{yx}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 272{,}1\) µm. The force \((F_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\) counts only the horizontal part of the displacement, since its scalar product with the displacement is \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\,u\). By the computation above it does the work \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1\ \text{kN} \cdot 204{,}1\ \text{µm} = 0{,}1020\) J, the area of the grey triangle at the top left of the figure.
  2. The force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) grows slowly from zero to 1 kN, while the force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) stays 1 kN. The joint moves further by \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 272{,}1\) µm to the right and by \(D_{\mathrm{yy}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 1071{,}4\) µm up. The force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) does \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1\ \text{kN} \cdot 1071{,}4\ \text{µm} = 0{,}5357\) J, the grey triangle at the top right. The force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the same throughout this stage and its point of application moves by 272,1 µm in its direction, so it does the work of a constant force, \(1\ \text{kN} \cdot 272{,}1\ \text{µm} = 0{,}2721\) J, the blue rectangle at the top left. There is no half here, because the force does not grow from zero, it is already fully present.
  3. The total work is \(0{,}1020 + 0{,}5357 + 0{,}2721 = 0{,}9099\) J. This is exactly the strain energy of the bracket with both forces, which we computed in subsection 4.8.2, as it must be for slow loading (section 4.1).

Now we apply the two forces in the opposite order (figure 4.109, bottom). First \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) grows and does \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1\ \text{kN} \cdot 1071{,}4\ \text{µm} = 0{,}5357\) J, and the joint also moves by 272,1 µm to the right. Then \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) grows and does \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1\ \text{kN} \cdot 204{,}1\ \text{µm} = 0{,}1020\) J, while the joint moves further by \(D_{\mathrm{yx}} \cdot 1\ \text{kN} = 272{,}1\) µm up, and on this displacement the constant force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) does \(0{,}2721\) J. Together again \(0{,}9099\) J. We have now recognized the mixed energy of subsection 4.8.2. It is the work done by a force that is already fully present when the other force moves its point of application.

Let us write the same with letters, for forces \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) of any magnitude. With the order first \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), then \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\), the work is

\[W_{\mathrm{I}} = \tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + F_{\mathrm{x}}\,D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} ,\]

and with the opposite order

\[W_{\mathrm{II}} = \tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,D_{\mathrm{yx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

The Roman numerals in the index give the order, they are not forces or directions. In both cases the final state is the same: by adding loads, the final forces in the bars and the final displacement of the joint with both forces are the same, regardless of which force came first. For the bracket we computed that the work in both cases equals the strain energy of the final state. In general we expect this for every ideal elastic body. The work the material receives depends only on the final strain, not on the path (definition 3.43, item 2), and in slow loading the work of the forces is stored entirely in the material (section 4.1). If this is so, \(W_{\mathrm{I}} = W_{\mathrm{II}}\). The first two terms are the same in both expressions, so the last ones must be equal too, \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\,D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,D_{\mathrm{yx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}\), and for forces that are not zero \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\).

What would it mean if \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} \ne D_{\mathrm{yx}}\)? We would load the bracket in the first order and unload it in the reverse of the second order, first reducing \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and then \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\). When unloading along the same path backwards, the forces act in every state as in loading, but the displacements have the opposite sign, so the bracket gives back the work \(W_{\mathrm{II}}\). After such a cycle the bracket would be in the same state as at the start, but the forces would have done on it the work \(W_{\mathrm{I}} - W_{\mathrm{II}} = F_{\mathrm{x}} F_{\mathrm{y}}\,(D_{\mathrm{xy}} - D_{\mathrm{yx}})\), which is not zero. The cycle in the opposite direction would give the work \(W_{\mathrm{II}} - W_{\mathrm{I}}\). One of the two numbers would be negative, and in that cycle the bracket would give out work from nothing. This is forbidden by the same argument that we used for a small piece of material in section 3.6.3. The equality \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\) is therefore a consequence of the fact that an elastic body returns the work it receives.

This explanation is the explanation of EP and CM (EP §4.6.4, (4.6.22)-(4.6.24), pp. 226-227, CM §7.4.2, (7.4.11)-(7.4.13), PDF pp. 317-318). The two sources justify the equality of the works by the fact that at the end the body is loaded by the same pair of forces (EP, p. 227, CM, PDF p. 318). It uses, however, a conclusion that we have not yet proved for a body: that the work of the external forces in slow loading depends only on the final state, also when the ratio between the loads changes during loading, as in our two orders. For a bar we proved it in theorem 4.3, and for a body only for loads that all grow in proportion at the same time (corollary 4.87 on the work of the loads and the strain energy). Let us now prove it for a body.

First let us say what a path is. For the bracket the loads are the forces \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\), which change during loading each in its own way. For a body, take \(m\) chosen loads \((\vek b^{(k)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(k)})\), \(k = 1, \dots, m\), with solutions \(\vek u^{(k)}\), and multiply them during loading by numbers \(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\) that change. We describe the state by a parameter \(\lambda\), as in corollary 4.87, except that the factors are no longer all equal to \(\lambda\). For the bracket the chosen loads are the force of 1 kN in the direction \(x\) and the force of 1 kN in the direction \(y\), and the factors are the magnitudes of the forces in kilonewtons. The order first \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), then \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a path of two stages. On the first, \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\lambda\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) for \(\lambda\) from \(0\) to \(\tfrac12\), on the second \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 2\lambda - 1\) for \(\lambda\) from \(\tfrac12\) to \(1\). But the two forces can also grow at the same time and at different rates, for example \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = \lambda\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = \lambda^2\) (figure 4.110, left). For every path, by the principle of superposition (lemma 4.134), the field \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek u^{(k)}\) is a solution for the load \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\,(\vek b^{(k)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(k)})\), and if the supports prevent every small rigid displacement, it is the only one by lemma 4.133 on the uniqueness of the solution. This is the state the body takes in slow loading, as in corollary 4.87.

What work do the loads do? The points no longer move along straight segments. On the path with \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = \lambda\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = \lambda^2\) the joint \(C\) moves along a curve (figure 4.110, right), while definition 4.1 counted the work only along a straight segment. We proceed as with every integral. On a short step of the parameter from \(\lambda\) to \(\lambda + \Delta\lambda\) the point \(P\) moves approximately by \(\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}(P, \lambda)\,\Delta\lambda\), where \(\partial\vek u/\partial\lambda\) is the derivative of the displacement of the point with respect to the parameter. A small piece of volume \(\Delta V\) at \(P\) carries the force of approximately \(\vek b(P, \lambda)\,\Delta V\), which hardly changes over the short step, so by item 1 of definition 4.1 it does the work of approximately \(\vek b\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}\,\Delta V\,\Delta\lambda\). The same holds for a patch of the surface. When we add the contributions over the pieces and the steps and make the pieces and steps shorter, the sum turns into an integral. Books call such a sum along a curve a line integral, and KREYSZIG says that it is exactly the work of a force along a path (KREYSZIG §10.1, (3), PDF pp. 440-441). In section 4.1.1 we put it off, because proportional loads move the points along straight segments.

Definition 4.135 Loading path and the work along it (pot obremenjevanja in delo na njej)

Let the body be as in definition 4.132, let \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}), \dots, (\vek b^{(m)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(m)})\) be loads of the body with solutions \(\vek u^{(1)}, \dots, \vek u^{(m)}\), and let \(\kappa_1, \dots, \kappa_{\mathrm{m}}\) be functions that are differentiable on an open interval \(J\) containing the closed interval \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\) and have derivatives continuous on \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\).

  1. A loading path (pot obremenjevanja) is the family of loads and displacement fields
    \[\vek b(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\vek b^{(k)} , \qquad \bar{\vek t}(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\bar{\vek t}^{(k)} , \qquad \vek u(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\vek u^{(k)}\]
    for \(\lambda \in [\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\).
  2. The work of the loads (delo obremenitev) along this path is the number
    \[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = \int_{\lambda_0}^{\lambda_1} \bigg( \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b(\lambda)\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}(\lambda)\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}A \bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda , \tag{4.83}\]
    where \(\partial\vek u/\partial\lambda = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}'(\lambda)\,\vek u^{(k)}\) is the derivative of the displacement of every point with respect to the parameter \(\lambda\).

Source: KREYSZIG §10.1, (3) (PDF pp. 440-441), where the work of a force along a path is a line integral, EP (3.1.2) (p. 97). The path as a sum of chosen loads with variable factors is not from the sources.

The integral in (4.83) exists. By the linearity of the integral over the body and over the surface (section 4.2), the expression in brackets equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'(\lambda)\,W_{\mathrm{kl}}\), where

\[W_{\mathrm{kl}} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(k)}\cdot\vek u^{(l)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(k)}\cdot\vek u^{(l)}\,\mathrm{d}A \tag{4.84}\]

is the work that the load \(k\) would do on the displacements of the solution \(l\) if it stayed the same all the time. The letter \(W\) with two indices is a work, not the weight of the hanging bar. The expression in brackets is therefore a sum of products of continuous functions of the parameter with constant numbers, a continuous function on \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\) (corollary 1.64), and by theorem 3.38 it has an integral. The definition agrees with corollary 4.87. For a single load with \(\kappa_1(\lambda) = \lambda\) on \([0, 1]\) the expression in brackets is \(\lambda\,W_{11}\), and the work is \(\int_0^1 \lambda\,W_{11}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda = \tfrac12\,W_{11}\), as in the corollary. Now we can state the theorem.

Theorem 4.136 Work along any loading path (delo pri poljubni poti obremenjevanja)

Let the loading path be as in definition 4.135, the material ideal elastic and linear elastic, and let the strains of the field \(\vek u(\lambda)\) be small at every point of the body for every \(\lambda \in [\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\). Let \(U(\lambda)\) be the strain energy of the body (definition 4.85) at the displacement field \(\vek u(\lambda)\). Then the work of the loads along the path equals the difference of the strain energies in the final and in the initial state,

\[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = U(\lambda_1) - U(\lambda_0) . \tag{4.85}\]

If all factors are zero at the start, \(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda_0) = 0\) for all \(k\), then \(W_{\mathrm{zun}} = U(\lambda_1)\), the strain energy of the final state.

Proof

We have to show that the expression in brackets in (4.83) is the derivative of the function \(U\), and then use the fundamental theorem of calculus. We get the derivative from the principle of virtual displacements with the virtual displacement \(\partial\vek u/\partial\lambda\).

  1. The state at every \(\lambda\). By the principle of superposition (lemma 4.134), applied to every term of the sum, \(\vek u(\lambda)\) is a solution for the load \((\vek b(\lambda), \bar{\vek t}(\lambda))\), with the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\varepsilon^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\sigma^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(\varepsilon^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\sigma^{(k)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the strains and stresses of the solution \(\vek u^{(k)}\).
  2. The virtual displacement. Choose \(\lambda \in [\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\) and take \(\delta\vek u = \partial\vek u/\partial\lambda = \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'(\lambda)\,\vek u^{(l)}\). As in step 1 of the proof of lemma 4.134 on the principle of superposition, this field is continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body with its surface, and it is \(\vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so it is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.79). By step 2 of the same proof its virtual strain is \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'(\lambda)\,\varepsilon^{(l)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  3. The principle of virtual displacements. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda)\) are the stresses of a solution, so they are continuously differentiable, satisfy the equilibrium equations with the body force \(\vek b(\lambda)\) and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the Cauchy formula with \(\bar{\vek t}(\lambda)\). They are symmetric, because \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{jikl}}\) (lemma 3.17, item 2). By theorem 4.82 on the principle of virtual displacements, therefore,
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda)\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b(\lambda)\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}(\lambda)\cdot\frac{\partial\vek u}{\partial\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
    The right side is the expression in brackets in (4.83).
  4. The energy density at a point. At a point \(P\) of the body the strain at \(\lambda\) is small by assumption, so by theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants, item 2, \(w(\lambda) = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda)\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}(\lambda)\), where \(\{\varepsilon\}(\lambda)\) is the Voigt column of the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda)\) (definition 3.18). By step 1, \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\{\varepsilon^{(k)}\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81), therefore, \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) is differentiable on \(J\) with the derivative \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}'(\lambda)\,\{\varepsilon^{(k)}\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), which by step 2 is the component \(\{\delta\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the Voigt column of the virtual strain.
  5. By the product rule the derivative of the density with respect to \(\lambda\) is
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\delta\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\delta\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} .\]
    By theorem 3.47, item 1, \(C_{\mathrm{pq}} = C_{\mathrm{qp}}\), so the second sum, after swapping the indices \(p\) and \(q\), equals the first. Hence \(\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}\lambda = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \big(\sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\big)\,\{\delta\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\delta\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), which by (3.38), applied to products, is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\lambda)\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the integrand on the left in step 3.
  6. The energy as a function of the parameter. We insert \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\{\varepsilon^{(k)}\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) into the density of step 4:
    \[w(\lambda) = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}(\lambda)\,B_{\mathrm{kl}} , \qquad B_{\mathrm{kl}} = \sum_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\mathrm{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon^{(k)}\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\varepsilon^{(l)}\}_{\mathrm{q}} .\]
    The functions \(B_{\mathrm{kl}}\) depend only on the point and are continuous on the body, because the strains of the solutions are continuous (corollary 1.69, item 1). By the linearity of the integral over the body (section 4.2), \(U(\lambda) = \tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}(\lambda)\,U_{\mathrm{kl}}\), where \(U_{\mathrm{kl}} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} B_{\mathrm{kl}}\,\mathrm{d}V\) are constant numbers.
  7. The derivative of the energy. By the rules of differentiation \(U\) is differentiable on \(J\) and
    \[U'(\lambda) = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \big(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}'(\lambda)\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}(\lambda) + \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda)\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'(\lambda)\big)\,U_{\mathrm{kl}} .\]
    By the same rules the derivative of the density of step 6 is \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} (\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}'\kappa_{\mathrm{l}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}')\,B_{\mathrm{kl}}\) at every point, and by the linearity of the integral over the body the integral of this derivative over the body is \(U'(\lambda)\).
  8. Steps 5 and 7 computed the derivative of the same function \(\lambda \mapsto w(\lambda)\) at the same point in two ways. The derivative is the limit of the difference quotient, and a limit is unique (lemma 1.7), so the two expressions are equal. The integral of the left side of step 3 is therefore \(U'(\lambda)\), and by step 3 the expression in brackets in (4.83) equals \(U'(\lambda)\) for every \(\lambda \in [\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\).
  9. The function \(U'\) is a sum of products of the functions \(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'\) with constant numbers. The functions \(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\) are continuous because they are differentiable (lemma 1.79), and the functions \(\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'\) are continuous on \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\) by assumption, so \(U'\) is continuous on \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\) (corollary 1.64). The function \(U\) is differentiable on \(J\), hence continuous on \([\lambda_0, \lambda_1]\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1,
    \[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = \int_{\lambda_0}^{\lambda_1} U'(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda = U(\lambda_1) - U(\lambda_0) .\]
  10. If all \(\kappa_{\mathrm{k}}(\lambda_0) = 0\), then by step 6 \(U(\lambda_0) = 0\), and \(W_{\mathrm{zun}} = U(\lambda_1)\).

Source: EP §4.6.4 (pp. 226-227) and CM §7.4.2 (PDF pp. 317-318) state the equality of the works for two orders of loading, and SADD §6.1 (PDF p. 130) says that the work does not depend on the order of loading. The proof is not from the sources.

The proof showed where the independence of the path comes from. The principle of virtual displacements with the virtual displacement \(\partial\vek u/\partial\lambda\) says that the work of the loads on a short step of the path equals the work of the stresses on the change of the strains. Step 5 says that this work of the stresses is exactly the change of the energy density, \(\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}\lambda = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). This is the relation between the stresses and the derivatives of the energy density of lemma 3.46, written along the path. In step 5 we used the symmetry \(C_{\mathrm{pq}} = C_{\mathrm{qp}}\). Without it the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) would not be the derivative of any function of the state, and the work would depend on the path, as for the imagined material with \(C_{16} \ne C_{61}\) of section 3.6.3. We proved the theorem for a linear elastic material and for loads that are sums of chosen loads with variable factors. This is a simplification. For a nonlinear ideal elastic material the principle of superposition does not hold, so we would not get the states by adding as in step 1. We would have to assume that the body takes an equilibrium state at every \(\lambda\) and that its displacements change continuously differentiably with \(\lambda\), and step 5 would need the chain rule for a function of six variables (LEBL2 theorem 8.3.7, PDF p. 38). For a load that changes its shape during loading, for example a truck driving across a bridge, step 6 does not hold, and the derivative of the energy would need differentiation under the integral over the body. Lemma 4.19 gives it only for an integral over an interval, and LEBL2 also only for such an integral (theorem 9.1.1, PDF pp. 65-66). For a body one would prove it with uniform continuity in the point and the parameter at the same time, which we do not develop in the book.

A path of several stages, like our two orders, is not a path in the sense of definition 4.135, because the derivatives of the factors jump at the change from one stage to the next. The theorem holds, however, on each stage separately. In the order first \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), then \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\), the factors \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\lambda\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) of the first stage are polynomials, differentiable at all numbers, and the theorem on the interval \([0, \tfrac12]\) gives the work \(U(\tfrac12) - U(0)\). On the second stage \(\kappa_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\) and \(\kappa_{\mathrm{y}} = 2\lambda - 1\) are polynomials too, and the theorem on \([\tfrac12, 1]\) gives \(U(1) - U(\tfrac12)\). The sum is \(U(1) - U(0) = U(1)\), and the intermediate state cancels. The same holds for the opposite order and for every path of finitely many stages. Therefore \(W_{\mathrm{I}} = W_{\mathrm{II}}\) for every body of a linear and ideal elastic material, and the explanation by the order of loading has become a proof. The expression in brackets in (4.83) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathrm{l}} \kappa_{\mathrm{k}}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{l}}'\,W_{\mathrm{kl}}\), so for two loads of a body, on the stages of the order first \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)})\), then \((\vek b^{(2)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(2)})\), we get \(W_{\mathrm{I}} = \tfrac12\,W_{11} + \tfrac12\,W_{22} + W_{12}\), and for the opposite order \(W_{\mathrm{II}} = \tfrac12\,W_{22} + \tfrac12\,W_{11} + W_{21}\). From \(W_{\mathrm{I}} = W_{\mathrm{II}}\) it follows that \(W_{12} = W_{21}\): the work of the first load on the displacements of the second equals the work of the second on the displacements of the first.

The bracket is not a body, but the computation is the same. On the path \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = \lambda\) kN and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = \lambda^2\) kN the displacement of the joint is, by (4.82), \(u = 204{,}1\,\lambda + 272{,}1\,\lambda^2\) and \(v = 272{,}1\,\lambda + 1071{,}4\,\lambda^2\) in micrometres (figure 4.110, right). The work of the forces is the sum of the works of the two forces along the path of the joint, \(\int_0^1 (F_{\mathrm{x}}\,u' + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v')\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\), where the prime means the derivative with respect to \(\lambda\). With SymPy we get \(0{,}9099\) J, exactly as for both orders. Why? By (4.82) the integrand is

\[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,u' + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v' = D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}' + D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}' + D_{\mathrm{yx}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}' + D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}' .\]

The first and the last term are the derivatives of the functions \(\tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}^2\) and \(\tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}^2\). The two middle terms together, however, are the derivative of the function \(D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}\), whose derivative is \(D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,(F_{\mathrm{x}}'\,F_{\mathrm{y}} + F_{\mathrm{x}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}')\), only when \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\). For the bracket this is so, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus the work along every path equals \(\tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{xx}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + D_{\mathrm{xy}}\,F_{\mathrm{x}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}} + \tfrac12\,D_{\mathrm{yy}}\,F_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) at the final forces, \(\tfrac12 \cdot 204{,}1 + 272{,}1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1071{,}4 = 909{,}9\) µm kN \(= 0{,}9099\) J. For the bracket the role of the symmetry \(C_{\mathrm{pq}} = C_{\mathrm{qp}}\) of step 5 is played by the equality \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\).

The next subsection proves the relation \(W_{12} = W_{21}\) also directly, without a loading path, from the principle of virtual displacements and the symmetry of the elastic constants.

4.8.4 Betti's theorem

Take a body with supports on a part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of its surface and two loads (figure 4.111). The first, \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)})\), causes the displacement field \(\vek u^{(1)}\), the second, \((\vek b^{(2)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(2)})\), the field \(\vek u^{(2)}\). The work that the forces of the first load would do if they stayed the same while their points of application move by \(\vek u^{(2)}\) is, by definition 4.1 summed over the pieces of the body and of the surface as for the potential energy of the loads in section 4.6.3, equal to

\[W_{12} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A .\]

The first index says which load works, the second on whose displacements. The letter \(W\) with two indices is a work, not the weight of the hanging bar. For the bracket this work was the blue rectangle in figure 4.109, the work of the first force on the displacement caused by the second.

Theorem 4.137 Betti's reciprocal theorem (Bettijev izrek)

Let the body be as in definition 4.132, of an ideal elastic material. Let \(\vek u^{(1)}\) be a solution for the load \((\vek b^{(1)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(1)})\) and \(\vek u^{(2)}\) a solution for the load \((\vek b^{(2)}, \bar{\vek t}^{(2)})\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A \\ &\qquad = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(2)}\cdot\vek u^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\cdot\vek u^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}A . \end{aligned} \tag{4.86}\]
Proof

We must show that both sides equal the same integral of stresses and strains. We get each side from the principle of virtual displacements, taking as the virtual displacement the solution for the other load, and the equality of the two integrals from the symmetry of the elastic constants.

  1. By requirement 1 of definition 4.132 the field \(\vek u^{(2)}\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body with its surface, and equal to \(\vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so it is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.79). The definition does not require the virtual displacement to be small. The components of the small strain tensor of the field \(\delta\vek u = \vek u^{(2)}\) are the strains \(\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) of the solution, because they are computed from the same partial derivatives.
  2. By requirements 2 and 3 the stresses \(\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuously differentiable, satisfy the equilibrium equations with the body force \(\vek b^{(1)}\) and, on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), Cauchy's formula with \(\bar{\vek t}^{(1)}\). They are symmetric, because \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{jikl}}\) (lemma 3.17, item 2). These are the assumptions of the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82), which with the virtual displacement \(\vek u^{(2)}\) gives
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(1)}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
    The right side is the left side of equation (4.86).
  3. In the same way, with the roles of the two loads exchanged, the principle with the virtual displacement \(\vek u^{(1)}\) gives
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b^{(2)}\cdot\vek u^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}^{(2)}\cdot\vek u^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}A ,\]
    and the right side is the right side of equation (4.86). It remains to show that the left sides of steps 2 and 3 are equal.
  4. At every point of the body \(\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\) by definition 3.16, so
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} .\]
  5. The material is ideal elastic and linear elastic, so by theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants, item 1, \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\) for all indices. We insert this:
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} C_{\mathrm{ijkl}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \Big( \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{klij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} \Big)\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}} ,\]
    where we exchanged the order of the finite sums and took \(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\) out of the sum over \(i\) and \(j\).
  6. By definition 3.16, written for the pair of indices \(kl\) instead of \(ij\), the expression in the bracket is the stress \(\sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\) of the second solution. The right side of step 5 is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \sum_{\mathrm{l}} \sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{kl}}\). When we rename the indices, \(k\) to \(i\) and \(l\) to \(j\), this is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  7. The integrands on the left sides of steps 2 and 3 are therefore equal at every point, so the integrals are equal too. The right sides of steps 2 and 3 are therefore equal, which is (4.86).

Source: EP theorem 4.6.2, (4.6.25)-(4.6.27) (pp. 227-228), SADD §6.4.2, (6.4.2), (6.4.3) (PDF pp. 135-136), CM §7.4.2, (7.4.14)-(7.4.18) (PDF pp. 318-319), BOWER §5.8.1 (PDF pp. 379-380). In steps 2 and 3 the sources use the divergence theorem directly, which in our case the principle of virtual displacements has already used.

This is Betti's reciprocal theorem. The work \(W_{12}\) of the first load on the displacements of the second equals the work \(W_{21}\) of the second load on the displacements of the first. The proof showed which properties of the body are needed for this. The principle of virtual displacements knows nothing about the material and holds for every body in equilibrium. The material enters only in steps 4 to 6, and there only with the symmetry \(C_{\mathrm{ijkl}} = C_{\mathrm{klij}}\), which we derived in section 3.6 from the requirement that the material returns the work it receives. This is the precise form of the explanation with the order of loading.

For an isotropic material the symmetry is directly visible. By Hooke's law (3.13), \(\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) when \(i = j\), and \(\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\) when \(i \ne j\), where \(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants (definition 3.10). The sum in step 2 is therefore

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} &= \lambda\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{v}}\,\big(\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{zz}}\big) + 2\mu \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} \\ &= \lambda\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{v}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ij}} , \end{aligned}\]

an expression that does not change if we exchange the labels (1) and (2). And what if, for example, \(C_{\mathrm{xxyy}} \ne C_{\mathrm{yyxx}}\)? Take a first strain whose only nonzero component is \(\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and a second whose only nonzero component is \(\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{yy}}\). In the sum of step 4 only the term with \(i = j = y\) and \(k = l = x\) remains, that is \(C_{\mathrm{yyxx}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and with the roles exchanged only \(C_{\mathrm{xxyy}}\,\varepsilon^{(2)}_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\varepsilon^{(1)}_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The two sums would differ, and the theorem would not hold. Such a material would give out work in a cycle, like the imagined material with \(C_{16} \ne C_{61}\) in section 3.6.3. SADD mentions that Truesdell proved the theorem to be even a necessary and sufficient condition for an elastic material to have a strain energy density (SADD, PDF p. 136).

When does the theorem fail? First, when the supports prescribe displacements that are not zero. Then the support forces on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) do work, and in the proof the field \(\vek u^{(2)}\) is not a kinematically admissible virtual displacement. SADD therefore integrates the traction vector over the whole surface \(S\) in the theorem, where on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) it is the force of the support (SADD (6.4.2), PDF p. 135). For fixed supports these terms are zero, because the displacement there is zero, and we get (4.86). Second, for a material without symmetric elastic constants, as we have just seen. Third, when adding loads fails: for large displacements, for a nonlinear material and for loads that change with the deformation, since there the principle of virtual displacements or Hooke's law in the form we used does not hold.

Let us test the theorem on the bracket. The bracket is not a body but a model of bars, and the same statement holds for it with the same proof. Instead of the principle of virtual displacements for a body we use the principle for the truss (4.49), \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\delta e_{\mathrm{AC}} + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\delta e_{\mathrm{BC}} = \vek F\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\), where \(\delta e = \vek n\cdot\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) is the virtual elongation of a bar (equation (4.48)). For the first load, the force \(\vek F^{(1)}\) at the joint, we take as the virtual displacement of the joint its actual displacement under the second load, \(\delta\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = \vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(2)}\). The virtual elongations are then the actual elongations \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{i}}^{(2)}\) of the bars under the second load (step 4), and by step 3 the axial forces are \(N_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)} = EA\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{i}}^{(1)}/L_{\mathrm{i}}\). Therefore

\[\frac{EA}{L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}^{(1)}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}^{(2)} + \frac{EA}{L_{\mathrm{BC}}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}^{(1)}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}^{(2)} = \vek F^{(1)}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(2)} .\]

The left side does not change if we exchange the labels (1) and (2), so \(\vek F^{(1)}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(2)} = \vek F^{(2)}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(1)}\). The role of the symmetric elastic constants is played here by the numbers \(EA/L_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(EA/L_{\mathrm{BC}}\), by which each force in a bar is connected only with the elongation of the same bar.

Let us check with numbers. For the first load take the load from the beginning of the chapter, \(\vek F^{(1)} = (0, -10, 0)\) kN, for the second a horizontal force \(\vek F^{(2)} = (1, 0, 0)\) kN. By (4.82) the displacement of the joint has under the first load the components \(u^{(1)} = -2721\) µm and \(v^{(1)} = -10\,714\) µm, and under the second \(u^{(2)} = 204{,}1\) µm and \(v^{(2)} = 272{,}1\) µm. The two works are

\[\begin{aligned} W_{12} &= \vek F^{(1)}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(2)} = (-10\ \text{kN}) \cdot 272{,}1\ \text{µm} = -2{,}721\ \text{J} , \\ W_{21} &= \vek F^{(2)}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}^{(1)} = 1\ \text{kN} \cdot (-2721\ \text{µm}) = -2{,}721\ \text{J} . \end{aligned}\]

Both works are negative. The horizontal force lifts the joint, against the load that pulls down. The load in turn pushes the joint towards the wall, against the horizontal force that pulls to the right. The works are equal, although they are products of very different numbers, a large force of 10 kN with a small rise of 272,1 µm and a small force of 1 kN with a large displacement of 2721 µm.

Let us also test it on loads that do not act at a point (figure 4.112). Let the steel bar of chapter 1, of length \(L = 10\) m, with the cross-sectional area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa, be held at its top cross section, and let the axis \(x\) point down from it. We describe it with the bar model of section 4.4, in which the whole cross section at \(x\) moves down by \(u(x)\), as in section 4.6.2. The first load is the weight of the bar, per unit length \(q = \rho g A\), with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ and \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s². We know the displacement it causes from chapter 1, \(u^{(1)}(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (section 3.1.5). The second load is a force \(F\) at the lower end, without the weight. Then every cross section carries the force \(F\), the strain is \(F/(EA)\) everywhere, and the displacement is \(u^{(2)}(x) = F x/(EA)\).

The work of the weight on the displacements of the second load is the sum of the works of the weights of the small pieces, \(q\,\Delta x\), on the displacement \(u^{(2)}(x)\), in the limit an integral. The work of the force \(F\) on the displacements of the first load is \(F\) times the displacement of the lower end, \(u^{(1)}(L)\):

\[\begin{aligned} W_{12} &= \int_0^L \rho g A \cdot \frac{F x}{EA}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g F}{E}\cdot\frac{L^2}{2} , \\ W_{21} &= F\,u^{(1)}(L) = F\,a\,(2L \cdot L - L^2) = F\,\frac{\rho g}{2E}\,L^2 . \end{aligned}\]

We computed the first integral with the fundamental theorem of calculus, since \(x^2/2\) has the derivative \(x\). Both works are \(\rho g F L^2/(2E)\), for \(F = 1\) kN this is \(0{,}01952\) J. Here too the theorem holds with the same proof as for a body. In the principle of virtual displacements for a bar (theorem 4.76), \(\int_0^L N\,\delta u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,\delta u\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L)\), we take \(\delta u = u^{(2)}\) for the first load and \(\delta u = u^{(1)}\) for the second. Both fields are polynomials, so they have a continuous derivative, as the theorem requires, and both are zero at \(x = 0\). The axial force of a linear elastic bar is \(N = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} A = E A\,u'\) (definition 3.1), so the left side for the first load is \(\int_0^L EA\,(u^{(1)})'\,(u^{(2)})'\,\mathrm{d}x\), and for the second \(\int_0^L EA\,(u^{(2)})'\,(u^{(1)})'\,\mathrm{d}x\), the same integral. The role of the elastic constants is played here by the single number \(EA\), and the product does not change if we exchange the labels (1) and (2).

What does the theorem give us here? Suppose we knew only the simple field \(u^{(2)}\) of the end force, but not the displacement due to the weight. The theorem would give us the displacement of the lower end due to the weight without solving the problem with the weight:

\[u^{(1)}(L) = \frac{W_{12}}{F} = \int_0^L \rho g A\,\frac{x}{EA}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho g L^2}{2E} = \frac{7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 10^2}{2 \cdot 196 \cdot 10^9}\ \text{m} = 19{,}52\ \text{µm} ,\]

which is \(a L^2\), as in chapter 1. This is how EP uses the theorem for the bending of beams, which we will meet in chapter 5: the displacement of the end due to a force at the end is known, and the displacement of the end due to a distributed load is sought (EP example 4.6.5, p. 229). EP and CM also check the theorem on the same pair of loads (EP example 4.6.4, pp. 228-229, CM example 7.4.4, PDF pp. 319-320).

4.8.5 Maxwell's theorem

Betti's theorem compares the works of whole loads. The simplest load is a single force, and for the bracket we got \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = D_{\mathrm{yx}}\) for two such loads. How do we state this for a body? A force at a single point of a body is an idealization that does not work well in a body. SADD writes the displacement of an unbounded isotropic body due to a force at one point, and at the distance \(r\) from the point it is proportional to \(1/r\) (SADD (6.4.5), PDF p. 136). As \(r\) approaches zero it grows beyond all bounds, and the displacement of the point itself is not a finite number. We therefore spread the force over a small patch of the surface, and as the displacement we take the average of the displacements of the patch in the direction of the force. For the bracket and for the bar, where the forces act at joints and on cross sections, this is not necessary, and there the displacement of the point of application is a finite number.

How do we spread the force? The simplest way would be evenly, with a traction vector equal to the force divided by the area of the patch on the patch and zero elsewhere. Such a load, however, jumps to zero at the edge of the patch, and a solution with continuously differentiable stresses as in definition 4.132 cannot carry it. The normal of a flat face is the same on both sides of the edge, so a continuous stress gives the same traction vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on both sides, as we said at assumption 3 in section 4.5.1. We therefore spread the force with a weight, a function that is largest in the middle of the patch and falls continuously to zero towards the edge, like the bump of lemma 4.122 (figure 4.113). The traction vector is the force times the weight, divided by the integral of the weight over the surface. In this way the total force is exactly \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{j}}\). With the same weight we compute the average of the displacements of the patch. Why with the same one? Because then the work of the load on the displacements is exactly the force times this average, and this is just what we will need in the proof of the equality \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = D_{\mathrm{ji}}\).

Definition 4.138 Flexibility matrix (podajnostna matrika)

Let the body be as in lemma 4.133 on the uniqueness of the solution, with supports that prevent every small rigid displacement of it, and let patches \(\Delta S_1, \dots, \Delta S_{\mathrm{m}}\) of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), unit vectors \(\vek d_1, \dots, \vek d_{\mathrm{m}}\) and weights \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{m}}\) be chosen. The weight (utež) \(\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\) of the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a function whose input is a point of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and whose output is a number that is not negative, which is continuous on every outer face of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), zero outside \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\), and whose integral \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) is positive. The force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) on the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the load without a body force with the traction vector

\[\bar{\vek t} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{j}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}}{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}A}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{j}} \tag{4.87}\]

on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). Let a solution for a load exist for every such force, with small strains when the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) is small enough. The displacement of the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{i}}\) for a displacement field \(\vek u\) is the number

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = \frac{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A}{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A} . \tag{4.88}\]

The flexibility coefficient \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the displacement \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the solution with small strains for the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) on the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\), divided by \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\). The matrix \(\mat D\) with \(m\) rows and \(m\) columns and the elements \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the flexibility matrix of the chosen patches, weights and directions.

Source: EP §4.6.5 (p. 230) and CM §7.4.3 (PDF p. 320), where the two displacements are due to unit forces at points, BARBER (1.18) (PDF p. 27), where the coefficients are called influence coefficients. Patches with weights instead of points are not from the sources.

Does such a weight exist at all? On a patch that is a rectangle on a flat face of the body we build it from two bumps, as in lemma 4.122 we built a bump in a box from three.

Lemma 4.139 Weight on a rectangular patch (utež na pravokotni ploskvici)

Let an outer face of a body be a rectangle in the plane \(z = c\), as for a body made of boxes (definition 4.11, item 2), and let it contain the rectangle \(\Delta S\) of the points \((x, y, c)\) with \(\alpha_1 \le x \le \beta_1\) and \(\alpha_2 \le y \le \beta_2\), where \(\alpha_1 < \beta_1\) and \(\alpha_2 < \beta_2\). Let \(\ell_1 = \beta_1 - \alpha_1\) and \(\ell_2 = \beta_2 - \alpha_2\), let \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) be the bump of lemma 4.122, and at every point of the face let

\[\varphi(x, y, c) = \frac{q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)}{(\ell_1/2)^8\,(\ell_2/2)^8} . \tag{4.89}\]

Then \(\varphi\) is continuous on the face and nowhere negative, it is zero on the edges of \(\Delta S\) and outside it, positive in the interior of \(\Delta S\), equal to \(1\) at the centre of \(\Delta S\), and its integral over the face is

\[\int \varphi\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{2^{16}}{630^2}\,\ell_1\,\ell_2 = 0{,}1651\,\ell_1\,\ell_2 .\]

The same holds for a face in the plane \(x = c\) or \(y = c\), with the two coordinates that vary on it.

Proof

We have to show the continuity, the sign, the value at the centre and the integral. Everything follows from the properties of a single bump.

  1. Continuity. By lemma 4.122, item 1, \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) is differentiable at every number, so it is continuous (lemma 1.79). The factor \(q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\) is therefore a continuous function of the point of the face: for a point \(P_0\) with the coordinate \(x_0\) and an error \(\eta\), the band that the continuity of \(q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}\) gives at the number \(x_0\) will do, because a difference of coordinates is not larger than the distance between the points, as in step 2 of item 2 of the proof of lemma 4.122. The same holds for the other factor. A product of continuous functions of a point and a multiple of it are continuous (corollary 1.69, item 1).
  2. The sign. The factors \((t - \alpha)^4\) and \((\beta - t)^4\) are powers with an even exponent, so \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) is nowhere negative, and neither is \(\varphi\). If a point lies on the edge of \(\Delta S\) or outside it, then \(x \le \alpha_1\) or \(x \ge \beta_1\) or \(y \le \alpha_2\) or \(y \ge \beta_2\), and the corresponding factor is zero by the definition of the bump. In the interior of \(\Delta S\) both factors are products of positive numbers, so \(\varphi > 0\).
  3. The centre. At \(x = (\alpha_1 + \beta_1)/2\) the numbers \(x - \alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1 - x\) both equal \(\ell_1/2\), so \(q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x) = (\ell_1/2)^8\). The same holds for \(y\), and \(\varphi = 1\).
  4. The integral over the face. By assumption the face is a rectangle that contains \(\Delta S\). The lines \(x = \alpha_1\), \(x = \beta_1\), \(y = \alpha_2\) and \(y = \beta_2\) cut it into at most nine rectangles. On all of them except \(\Delta S\) we have \(\varphi = 0\) by step 2, so by additivity and by the rule for a constant function (lemma 4.10, items 3 and 2, for a rectangle) the integral over the face equals the integral over \(\Delta S\).
  5. By the definition of the integral over a rectangle 4.8 and the linearity of the integral over an interval (lemma 3.39, item 1), with which we take out constant numbers,
    \[\int_{\Delta \mathrm{S}} \varphi\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{1}{(\ell_1/2)^8\,(\ell_2/2)^8} \int_{\alpha_1}^{\beta_1} q_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \int_{\alpha_2}^{\beta_2} q_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y .\]
  6. The integral of one bump is \(\int_\alpha^\beta (t - \alpha)^4\,(\beta - t)^4\,\mathrm{d}t = \ell^9/630\), where \(\ell = \beta - \alpha\). With SymPy we found a polynomial whose derivative is the integrand, and used the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1. We insert this into step 5 and get \(\int \varphi\,\mathrm{d}A = (\ell_1^9/630)(\ell_2^9/630)/\big((\ell_1/2)^8\,(\ell_2/2)^8\big) = 2^{16}\,\ell_1\,\ell_2/630^2\).

Source: The lemma is not from the sources.

For a patch of \(10\ \text{mm} \times 10\ \text{mm}\) the integral of the weight is \(0{,}1651 \cdot 100\ \text{mm}^2 = 16{,}51\ \text{mm}^2\). A force of 1 kN spread with the weight therefore has the traction vector \(1000\ \text{N}/16{,}51\ \text{mm}^2 = 60{,}56\) MPa at the centre of the patch, while spread evenly it has \(10\) MPa everywhere. The weight gathers the force towards the centre, because the force it loses at the edge, where it falls continuously to zero, it must gain in the middle. The definition also allows other weights and patches that are not rectangles, since it requires only continuity on every outer face, no negative values and a positive integral. The existence of a solution for such a load, however, the definition assumes, because we do not prove it in this book. EP remarks that the existence of solutions is difficult to establish, while their uniqueness is easy to prove (EP, p. 225). We proved the uniqueness in lemma 4.133.

The displacement \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the average of the component of the displacement in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{i}}\) over the patch, weighted with the weight \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\). If this component equals the same number \(a\) over the whole patch, then by (4.88) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = a \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A/\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = a\), as it must be for an average. The total force of the load (4.87), the integral of the traction vector over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), is \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{j}} \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}A/\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}A = F_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{j}}\). By lemma 4.133 on the uniqueness of the solution, item 2, the solution with small strains is unique, because the supports prevent every small rigid displacement of the body, so \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is a definite number. The name says that \(D_{\mathrm{ij}}\) measures how flexible the body is: how far the patch \(i\) moves per newton of force acting on the patch \(j\). Its unit is metre per newton. For the bracket we need no patches. Force 1 acts horizontally at the joint \(C\), force 2 vertically at the same joint, displacement 1 is the horizontal displacement of the joint, displacement 2 the vertical one. The matrix

\[\mat D = \begin{bmatrix} D_{\mathrm{xx}} & D_{\mathrm{xy}} \\ D_{\mathrm{yx}} & D_{\mathrm{yy}} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1\ \text{m}}{EA}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 16/3 \\ 16/3 & 21 \end{bmatrix}\]

is the flexibility matrix of the bracket for these two forces. The same patch or the same joint may appear several times, with different directions, as here. The quotient depends only on the patches, weights and directions, not on the magnitude of the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\). By the principle of superposition and the uniqueness of the solution, the solution for the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \((F_{\mathrm{j}}/1\ \text{N})\) times the solution for the force 1 N, and the average \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is linear in the field \(\vek u\), since the integral of a sum splits into the sum of the integrals. For the same reason the displacement under several forces at once is the sum of the contributions,

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^{m} D_{\mathrm{ij}}\,F_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \text{that is} \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \Delta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \Delta_{\mathrm{m}} \end{bmatrix} = \mat D \begin{bmatrix} F_1 \\ \vdots \\ F_{\mathrm{m}} \end{bmatrix} ,\]

where on the right is the product of a matrix and a vector (definition 1.96). For the bracket this is exactly (4.82).

Theorem 4.140 Maxwell's reciprocal theorem (Maxwellov izrek)

Let the body be as in definition 4.132, of an ideal elastic material, and let \(\mat D\) be the flexibility matrix of the chosen patches \(\Delta S_1, \dots, \Delta S_{\mathrm{m}}\), weights \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{m}}\) and directions \(\vek d_1, \dots, \vek d_{\mathrm{m}}\). Then \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = D_{\mathrm{ji}}\) for all \(i\) and \(j\) from \(1\) to \(m\): the flexibility matrix is symmetric.

Proof

We must show that the displacement of the patch \(i\) due to a force of 1 N on the patch \(j\) equals the displacement of the patch \(j\) due to a force of 1 N on the patch \(i\). This is Betti's theorem for two loads that are single forces.

  1. Let \(\vek u^{(1)}\) be the solution with small strains for the force \(F_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) N on the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek u^{(2)}\) the solution with small strains for the force \(F_{\mathrm{j}} = 1\) N on the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\). When a force of 1 N is not small enough for this, we take a small enough force instead of 1 N in all steps, and the proof stays the same. By definition 4.138 the displacement of the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the field \(\vek u^{(2)}\) equals \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} \cdot 1\) N, and the displacement of the patch \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{j}}\) for the field \(\vek u^{(1)}\) equals \(D_{\mathrm{ji}} \cdot 1\) N.
  2. The first load has no body force, and by (4.87) its traction vector on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is \((1\ \text{N})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{i}}/\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). The left side of equation (4.86) is therefore
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \frac{(1\ \text{N})\,\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}}{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A = 1\ \text{N} \cdot \frac{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek u^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}A}{\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A} = 1\ \text{N} \cdot D_{\mathrm{ij}} \cdot 1\ \text{N} ,\]
    where we took the constant number out of the integral by the linearity of the integral over the surface, and then used (4.88) and step 1.
  3. In the same way the right side of equation (4.86) equals \(1\ \text{N} \cdot D_{\mathrm{ji}} \cdot 1\ \text{N}\).
  4. By Betti's theorem 4.137 the two sides are equal, so \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = D_{\mathrm{ji}}\).

Source: EP §4.6.5, (4.6.28) (p. 230), CM §7.4.3, (7.4.19), (7.4.20) (PDF p. 320). The sources take the two forces at two different points. The proof does not need this, so the theorem holds also for the same point and two directions, as for the bracket. Forces spread with weights are not from the sources.

The proof showed why the force and the displacement use the same weight. The work of the load \(i\) on the displacements \(\vek u^{(2)}\) is the integral of the weight times the displacement, and exactly this integral, divided by the integral of the weight, is the displacement \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\). With a different weight for the displacement the work would not be the force times the displacement, and Betti's theorem would not give the equality of the coefficients.

For the bracket we saw the symmetry of the flexibility matrix in the computation and proved it in subsection 4.8.4 with the principle of virtual displacements for the truss. For a truss, however, the symmetry is also visible in a formula. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}\) be the directions of two forces at the joint \(C\). Let \(\hat N_{\mathrm{k}}^{(a)}\) be the axial forces in the bars due to a force of 1 N in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{a}}\), divided by 1 N, as in the unit dummy load method (theorem 4.95), and likewise \(\hat N_{\mathrm{k}}^{(b)}\) for the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}\). Under an actual force of 1 N in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}\) the axial force of the bar \(k\) is \(\hat N_{\mathrm{k}}^{(b)} \cdot 1\) N. The unit dummy load method with a unit force in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{a}}\) gives the component of the displacement in the direction \(\vek d_{\mathrm{a}}\), and dividing it by 1 N we get

\[D_{\mathrm{ab}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{m} \hat N_{\mathrm{k}}^{(a)}\,\hat N_{\mathrm{k}}^{(b)}\,\frac{L_{\mathrm{k}}}{E_{\mathrm{k}} A_{\mathrm{k}}} .\]

Exchanging \(a\) and \(b\) does not change the formula. For our bracket, \(\hat N^{(x)}_{\mathrm{AC}} = 1\) and \(\hat N^{(x)}_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\) for the horizontal force, and \(\hat N^{(y)}_{\mathrm{AC}} = \tfrac43\) and \(\hat N^{(y)}_{\mathrm{BC}} = -\tfrac53\) for the vertical force upwards (step 2), so \(D_{\mathrm{xy}} = 1 \cdot \tfrac43 \cdot 4\ \text{m}/(EA) + 0 \cdot \big({-\tfrac53}\big) \cdot 5\ \text{m}/(EA) = \tfrac{16}{3}\ \text{m}/(EA)\), as in (4.82).

Now we also see why the factor \(\tfrac43\) appeared twice in subsection 4.8.1. The same unit vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\) tell, in the equilibrium of the pin, how the forces in the bars make up the force on the pin (step 1), and, in the elongations, how the displacement of the pin changes the lengths of the bars (step 4). This connection between equilibrium and geometry is expressed by the principle of virtual displacements for the truss, which we obtained precisely by taking the scalar product of the equilibrium of the pin with the displacement of the pin (section 4.4.9).

For the bracket both forces were at the same joint. Let us also look at two forces at different points (figure 4.114). Take again the steel bar of length 10 m with the cross section \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\), held at its top cross section, in the bar model, and this time leave out its weight. By the principle of superposition the displacement due to the weight is simply added to the displacements we compute. The product of Young's modulus and the cross-sectional area is \(EA = 196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2 = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N. Force 1 acts downwards on the cross section at the depth \(x_1 = 5\) m, force 2 downwards at the lower end, at the depth \(x_2 = 10\) m.

  1. A force \(F_1 = 1\) kN at the depth 5 m. The upper half of the bar carries 1 kN and gets longer by \(1\ \text{kN} \cdot 5\ \text{m}/(EA) = 324{,}8\) µm. The lower half carries nothing, does not get longer and only moves together with the middle cross section. The lower end therefore moves by 324,8 µm, and \(D_{21} = 324{,}8\) µm/kN.
  2. A force \(F_2 = 1\) kN at the lower end. The whole bar carries 1 kN. The middle cross section moves by the elongation of the upper half, again \(1\ \text{kN} \cdot 5\ \text{m}/(EA) = 324{,}8\) µm, and \(D_{12} = 324{,}8\) µm/kN.

Here the mechanism is visible. In both cases the displacement of the other point is the elongation of the part of the bar above the higher of the two points, which carries 1 kN in both cases. In general, for forces at the depths \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) the flexibility coefficient is \(D_{\mathrm{ij}} = \min(x_{\mathrm{i}}, x_{\mathrm{j}})/(EA)\), where \(\min(x_{\mathrm{i}}, x_{\mathrm{j}})\) is the smaller of the two numbers, and exchanging \(i\) and \(j\) does not change it. For the same point \(D_{11} = 5\ \text{m}/(EA) = 324{,}8\) µm/kN and \(D_{22} = 10\ \text{m}/(EA) = 649{,}6\) µm/kN, so

\[\mat D = \begin{bmatrix} 324{,}8 & 324{,}8 \\ 324{,}8 & 649{,}6 \end{bmatrix} \frac{\text{µm}}{\text{kN}} .\]

That \(D_{11} = D_{12}\) is a peculiarity of the bar: the middle cross section moves by the elongation of the upper half, which carries 1 kN, no matter where below the middle the force acts.

Let us also test the theorem with two forces spread with weights, on the same bar in the bar model (figure 4.115). The bar has no patches, but a force can be spread over part of its length as a distributed axial load (definition 4.74). Let force 1 be spread between the depths 3 m and 7 m with the weight \((x - 3)^4\,(7 - x)^4\), and force 2 between the depths 5 m and 10 m with the weight \((x - 5)^4\,(10 - x)^4\), depths in metres. Both weights are bumps of lemma 4.122, and we spread the forces as in (4.87), only with an integral over the length instead of over the surface. The displacement \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the average of the displacement \(u(x)\), weighted with the weight of force \(i\). The two loads overlap between 5 m and 7 m. With SymPy we computed for each force of 1 kN the axial force, the displacement and the two averages, and got

\[D_{12} = D_{21} = \frac{4{,}9993\ \text{m}}{EA} = 324{,}76\ \frac{\text{µm}}{\text{kN}} .\]

Why is the number just below \(5\) m? For point forces at the depths \(x\) and \(x'\) the flexibility coefficient is \(\min(x, x')/(EA)\). With SymPy we checked that for the spread forces \(D_{12}\) is the average of this number, weighted with both weights at once, and such an average does not change when the two weights are swapped. The average depth of force 1 is 5 m, that of force 2 is 7,5 m. The average of the number \(\min(x, x')\) is smaller than the average depth of force 1 by the average of the difference \(x - \min(x, x')\). This difference is different from zero only for pairs in which the point of force 2 lies above the point of force 1, \(x' < x\), and this happens only in the overlap between 5 m and 7 m. That is why \(D_{12}\,EA\) is just below 5 m.

What is the theorem good for in measurement? Take a light footbridge with three marked points \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) on the walking surface, and suppose that the bridge behaves linearly elastically (figure 4.116). A point is here a short name for a small patch around it: the load is spread over it with a weight as in definition 4.138, and its displacement is the weighted mean of the displacements of the patch, which a gauge with a small plate on the surface also measures. We load the bridge with a test load of 10 kN at the point \(B\) and measure the vertical displacements downwards: 2,8 mm at \(A\), 5,0 mm at \(B\) and 4,0 mm at \(C\). The numbers are made up for the example. With this we know the column \(B\) of the flexibility matrix, the displacements of all three points per kilonewton of force at \(B\): \(D_{\mathrm{AB}} = 0{,}28\) mm/kN, \(D_{\mathrm{BB}} = 0{,}50\) mm/kN and \(D_{\mathrm{CB}} = 0{,}40\) mm/kN. By Maxwell's theorem we thereby also know the row \(B\): \(D_{\mathrm{BA}} = D_{\mathrm{AB}} = 0{,}28\) mm/kN and \(D_{\mathrm{BC}} = D_{\mathrm{CB}} = 0{,}40\) mm/kN. How far does the point \(B\) go down when 6 kN stand at \(A\) and 4 kN at \(C\)? By adding loads

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\mathrm{B}} &= D_{\mathrm{BA}} \cdot 6\ \text{kN} + D_{\mathrm{BC}} \cdot 4\ \text{kN} = 0{,}28 \cdot 6\ \text{mm} + 0{,}40 \cdot 4\ \text{mm} \\ &= 1{,}68\ \text{mm} + 1{,}60\ \text{mm} = 3{,}28\ \text{mm} . \end{aligned}\]

Without the theorem we would need two tests, with the load at \(A\) and with the load at \(C\), and would measure the displacement at \(B\) each time. With the theorem one test with the load at \(B\) is enough. EP and CM give such an example (EP example 4.6.7, p. 231, CM example 7.4.4, PDF pp. 319-320). If we measured the displacement at many points, one test with the load at \(B\) would tell how far \(B\) goes down when the load stands at any one of them.

The flexibility matrix gives displacements from forces. The reverse, forces from displacements, is given by the stiffness matrix of the bracket, which we obtained in section 4.6.7 from the derivatives of the strain energy with respect to the displacements. It is the inverse matrix of the flexibility matrix (definition 1.107). For the bracket we checked with SymPy that

\[\begin{bmatrix} 7{,}4088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{bmatrix} 10^6\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}} \cdot \frac{1\ \text{m}}{EA}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 16/3 \\ 16/3 & 21 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The stiffness matrix is symmetric too. In section 4.6.7 we saw this from the equality of the mixed derivatives of the energy, and COOK proves the symmetry of the stiffness matrix precisely with Betti's theorem (COOK (2.6-1)-(2.6-3), PDF p. 51). The stiffness matrix is the basis of the finite element method, which we will meet in chapter 7.

With this we have reached the end of the chapter. We started with the work of a force and the energy a bar stores. Then we wrote equilibrium as the equality of the work of the external forces and the work of the stresses for every admissible virtual displacement, derived from this form the principle of minimum total potential energy, and with the roles of forces and displacements exchanged the principle of virtual forces. In this section we obtained from the same principle of virtual displacements, used twice, and from the symmetry of the elastic constants that the roles of two loads may be exchanged. All results of the chapter hold for small displacements and for a linear elastic material that returns the work it receives.
