The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

A bar that we pull at its ends carries the load by stretching. In every cross section one part pulls the other with the same force along the axis, and the stress came out in chapter 2 as the force divided by the area of the cross section. Many structures, however, carry a load that does not point along the axis but pushes across it. The girder of a bridge carries traffic that presses it down, a diving board bends under the diver, a ceiling rests on joists loaded by its weight, a shaft in a machine carries wheels hanging on it. How does a body carry a load that pushes across it?

We have already seen three pictures. A bar clamped in a wall and pushed down at its free end bends (figure 2.5). The forces across a cut do not all pull along the axis: the upper part of the material pulls the neighbouring part toward itself, the lower part pushes it, and the part at the wall holds the rest of the bar up. Chapter 2 only described these forces and promised to compute them. A strip that bends so that the fibres above the middle get longer and below it shorter in proportion to the distance from the middle, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\), bends without gaps between the bands (figure 1.145). Chapter 1 gave the displacement field of such a strip and predicted that a bent slender body bends this way. The truss of chapter 4 (definition 4.4) is a model of a structure with pins. In a real structure the bars are usually welded at the joints, so they also carry some bending, and there we promised to compute it.

The goal of the chapter is the path from the loads to the displacements and stresses of a slender body that carries a load across it. For given loads and supports we shall compute how large the force across the body is and how strongly the forces try to bend the body in every cross section, what the stress in the material is, where every point of the body axis moves to, and how much energy the body stores. We reach this by two paths, the path of forces and the path of energy, just as for bars in chapter 4. The model of such a body is a simplification. So for each of its assumptions we shall measure on numerical examples what it costs us, and so say how far we may trust the model.

The route of the chapter is this. In the first section we say how to describe a slender body that carries a load across it, how it is supported and loaded, and choose two examples that will stay with us to the end. Then we compute the internal forces and moments along the body. In the next section we find how the points of the body move if the cross sections stay plane. For stresses we also need the mean position of the points of the cross section and the integral of the square of the height over the cross section, and then come the sections on stresses in bending and on shear stresses. From forces, deformations and material we build an equation for the unknown deflection of the axis, prepare the mathematics for solving it, and compute the deflection in both examples. The second path starts with virtual work. Since it brings in for the first time the search for a function that is the minimum of an integral, the mathematics that makes this possible follows, then the energy of the body and virtual forces, with which we also solve cases in which equilibrium alone does not give the reactions. The sections on the corrected model that also allows for shear deformation (Timoshenko) and on structures made of several rigidly joined parts at the end of the chapter extend the model, and the last section collects all the assumptions and their cost.
