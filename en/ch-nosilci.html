The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

A bar that we pull at its ends carries the load by stretching. In every cross section one part pulls the other with the same force along the axis, and the stress came out in chapter 2 as the force divided by the area of the cross section. Many structures, however, carry a load that does not point along the axis but pushes across it. The girder of a bridge carries traffic that presses it down, a diving board bends under the diver, a ceiling rests on joists loaded by its weight, a shaft in a machine carries wheels hanging on it. How does a body carry a load that pushes across it?

A beam (nosilec) is a body that carries: the Slovenian word comes from the verb nositi, to carry, because the body passes the load on to its supports. Theory (teorija) here means an explanation that derives from a few assumptions what happens in the body, and the word is Greek (theōría, watching, contemplating). Beam theory is therefore the explanation of how a slender body carries a load across it. It matters because almost every structure contains such bodies, and the calculation tells whether they will hold and how much they will bend.

The chapter promises the path from the loads to the displacements and stresses, which figure 5.1 shows on a cantilever. On the left is what we know: the body in the axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), the wall that holds it, and the force \(F\) that pushes the free end down. In thought we cut the body and draw in the middle the part to the right of the cut, enlarged. This part is at rest, so by Newton's laws the left part must hold it up with a force as large as the one by which \(F\) pushes it down. The force \(F\) also tries to turn it about the cut, so the forces across the cross section turn it back. For every cross section we shall compute how large this force across the body is and how strongly the forces try to bend the body. From this follows the stress in the material: in a cantilever the left part pulls the right one toward itself at the top and pushes it at the bottom, and this pull and push together are what turns the part back. On the right is the last station, where every point of the axis moves to, for example the free end. The arrows between the pictures show the order of the calculation. We shall compute the displacements by the path of forces and by the path of the energy that the body stores when it bends, just as for bars in chapter 4. The model is a simplification, so for each of its assumptions we shall measure on numerical examples what it costs us, and so say how far we may trust the model.

We begin beam theory as the first use of the foundations laid so far. We have already seen three pictures. A bar clamped in a wall and pushed down at its free end bends. Across a cut the forces do not pull along the axis equally everywhere: the upper part of the material pulls the neighbouring part toward itself, the lower part pushes it, and the part at the wall holds the rest of the bar up (figure 2.9). Chapter 2 only described these forces and promised to compute them. A strip that bends so that the fibres above the middle get longer and below it shorter in proportion to the distance from the middle, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = y/R\), bends without gaps between the bands, while fibres that got longer in proportion to the square of the distance would leave gaps (figure 1.446). Chapter 1 gave the displacement field of such a strip and predicted that a bent slender body bends this way. Chapter 3 tied stress to deformation in the material. The truss of chapter @ch-energijski-principi (definition 4.4) is a model of a structure with pins, about which the bars turn freely. In a real structure the bars are usually welded at the joints. A welded joint can move and turn only as a whole, so the angles between the bars stay the same and the bars bend near the joints. So they also carry some bending, and there we promised to compute it.

The route of the chapter is this. In the first section we say how to describe a slender body that carries a load across it, how it is supported and loaded, and choose two examples that will stay with us to the end. Then we compute the internal forces and moments along the body. In the next section we find how the points of the body move if the cross sections stay plane. For stresses we also need the mean position of the points of the cross section and the integral of the square of the height over the cross section, and then come the sections on stresses in bending and on shear stresses. From forces, deformations and material we build an equation for the unknown deflection of the axis, prepare the mathematics for solving it, and compute the deflection in both examples. The second path starts with virtual work. Since we need a general tool for finding a function that is the minimum of an integral, the mathematics that provides it follows, then the energy of the body and virtual forces, with which we also solve cases in which equilibrium alone does not give the reactions. The sections on the corrected model that also allows for shear deformation (Timoshenko) and on structures made of several rigidly joined parts at the end of the chapter extend the model, and the last section collects all the assumptions and their cost.
