Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.3 Mathematics: the scalar product

So far we have cut the bar only perpendicular to its axis. What if we cut it obliquely? The force with which one part of the bar pulls the other still points along the axis, but the face of the cut is tilted. Part of this force pulls the two parts of the bar apart, perpendicular to the face of the cut. The other part tries to shift one part along the other, along the face, as the cards slide in the deck of figure 1.5. We will see that it is exactly this second part that decides when steel yields. So we want to know how large each of them is. The mathematical question is this: how do we split a vector into a part that points in a given direction and a part perpendicular to that direction?

In this section all vectors are written with components in the same chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system.

For the direction of the axis \(x\) the answer is simple. The vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the sum \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) (figure 2.13). The first part points in the direction of the axis \(x\). The second part has the component \(x\) equal to zero, so it lies in the plane of the axes \(y\) and \(z\), which is perpendicular to the axis \(x\). So the part in the direction of the axis is the component times the unit vector of the axis. But for a direction that is not the direction of one of the axes, we need a way to check with components whether two arrows are perpendicular.

The Pythagorean theorem helps. Draw the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) from the same point \(O\), to the points \(A\) and \(B\) (figure 2.14). The third side of the triangle \(OAB\) is the vector from \(A\) to \(B\), that is, \(\vek b - \vek a\). From school we know the Pythagorean theorem and its converse: the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when the square of the length of the side \(AB\) equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the sides \(OA\) and \(OB\). By how much do these two numbers differ? Let us compute with components. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek b - \vek a\) is \(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and its square is

\[(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

The same holds for \(y\) and \(z\). When we add all three squares, we get by the formula for the length (1.10)

\[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

The square of the length of the side \(AB\) differs from the sum of the squares of the other two sides by \(-2\) times the number \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when this number is zero.

The same number arose in history from the search for a product of two directed lines. Hamilton found it in quaternions (chapter 1). From his basic formula \(i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 349) it follows that \(ij = k\) and \(ji = -k\). We multiply the formula \(ijk = -1\) from the right by \(k\) and get \(ij = k\). We multiply it from the left by \(i\) and get \(jk = i\), and this equality multiplied from the left by \(j\) gives \(ji = -k\). The same follows for the other two pairs, \(jk = i\), \(kj = -i\), \(ki = j\) and \(ik = -j\), because the formula is the same under the exchange \(i \to j \to k \to i\). Exchanging the order thus reverses the sign. The product of the vectors \(a_{\mathrm{x}} i + a_{\mathrm{y}} j + a_{\mathrm{z}} k\) and \(b_{\mathrm{x}} i + b_{\mathrm{y}} j + b_{\mathrm{z}} k\) is therefore

\[-(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) + (a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\,i + (a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{z}})\,j + (a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{x}})\,k .\]

Hamilton's product of two vectors thus has two parts, a number and a vector (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158). The number is our number with a minus sign. Grassmann developed several products, among them two, of which the first corresponds to the number and the second to the vector (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158).

Physicists found it unnatural that in quaternions the square of a vector is negative (CAJORI, PDF p. 350). This follows from the expression above. In the product of the vector \(x i + y j + z k\) with itself the terms with \(ij\) and \(ji\) cancel, and the square is \(-(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\), that is minus the square of the length. Josiah Willard Gibbs and Alexander Macfarlane therefore each independently proposed an algebra of vectors in which the square of a vector is positive, and a third system was used in research on electricity by Oliver Heaviside (CAJORI, PDF p. 350). Gibbs wrote the number from Hamilton's product with a dot, \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 158), without the minus, so that \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = |\vek a|^2\) is positive. The other part of Hamilton's product we meet in section 2.8.1.

Definition 2.8 Scalar product (skalarni produkt)

The scalar product (skalarni produkt) of the vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\), written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, is the number

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{2.6}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (2) (PDF p. 387). CM (2.2.6) (PDF p. 41) defines it with the two lengths and the angle.

The dot between the vectors is not a multiplication of numbers. On both sides of the dot stands a vector, and the result is a number. A number that is not a vector is called a scalar (skalar) in physics, hence the name (KREYSZIG §9.2, PDF p. 387). Books also call it the inner product (notranji produkt) or the dot product (pikčasti produkt) (KREYSZIG §9.2, PDF p. 387, CM §2.2.2.1, PDF p. 41).

Let us try it. The flight of the fly from chapter 1 was \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\). For \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\)

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-1) + (-2) \cdot 0 = 0 ,\]

so the angle between them is a right angle. We check with the Pythagorean theorem: \(\vek b - \vek a = (1, -3, 2)\), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = 1 + 9 + 4 = 14\) and \(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 = 9 + 5 = 14\). But for the vector \(\vek c = (2, 0, 0)\), by which the fly crawled along the floor after landing, \(\vek a\cdot\vek c = 2\), and \(|\vek c - \vek a|^2 = |(1, -2, 2)|^2 = 9\) is \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\) less than \(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek c|^2 = 13\). The angle between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek c\) is not a right angle.

The formula (2.6) holds only for components in a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. We derived it from the Pythagorean theorem and from the formula for the length, which holds only there (section 1.5.4). KREYSZIG goes the other way. It defines the scalar product with the lengths of the vectors and the angle between them, \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\cos\gamma\), and derives the formula with components from the axes being perpendicular (KREYSZIG §9.2 (1), (2), PDF pp. 387-389).

First let us write the computation with the triangle as a statement, and add a similar formula for the sum, which we will need.

Lemma 2.9 Square of the length of a difference and of a sum (kvadrat dolžine razlike in vsote)

For any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system

\[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b , \qquad |\vek a + \vek b|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 + 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b . \tag{2.7}\]
Proof

We write both sides with components and compare them.

  1. The difference of vectors \(\vek b - \vek a\) has the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.14).
  2. By the school formula for the square of a difference, \((b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. By (1.10), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2\) is the sum of these three squares. We arrange the terms in three groups:
    \[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = (a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2) + (b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{z}}^2) - 2\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]
  4. By (1.10) the first group is \(|\vek a|^2\) and the second \(|\vek b|^2\), and by (2.6) the bracket in the third is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b\). This proves the first formula.
  5. For the sum we repeat steps 1 to 4 with the components \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{z}}\) and the school formula \((a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The only difference is the sign in front of \(2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Adding the two equalities gives the parallelogram equality in KREYSZIG §9.2 (8) (PDF p. 389).

The lemma also answers a question that soon comes up about the scalar product. The components of a vector depend on how we place the axes (section 1.5.5). Does the scalar product therefore depend on it too? It does not. From the first formula of the lemma we express it:

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = \tfrac12\left(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - |\vek b - \vek a|^2\right) .\]

On the right stand only the lengths of the three sides of the triangle \(OAB\) in figure 2.14. But the distance between two points is one and the same, and the formula for the length (1.10) gives it in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with the same unit of length. So the formula (2.6) also gives the same number in every such system. Let us try it on the fly. In axes turned by a quarter turn about the axis \(z\) (the new axis \(x\) points in the direction of the old axis \(y\), the new axis \(y\) in the direction opposite to the old axis \(x\)), its flight has the components \((2, -1, -2)\), and the vector \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\) the components \((-1, -2, 0)\). The scalar product is \(2 \cdot (-1) + (-1) \cdot (-2) + (-2) \cdot 0 = 0\), as in the old axes. For mechanics this matters. The stresses we will compute with the scalar product will not depend on how we placed the axes, only on the loading of the body and on the surface on which we compute them.

Now we can say exactly what the scalar product means.

Theorem 2.10 Scalar product and the right angle (skalarni produkt in pravi kot)

Nonzero vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and drawn from the same point, form a right angle exactly when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Proof

We must show that a right angle gives \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\) and that \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\) gives a right angle. We get both directions at once from the Pythagorean theorem and its converse.

  1. We draw the arrows from the point \(O\) to the points \(A = O + \vek a\) and \(B = O + \vek b\). Since the vectors are not zero, \(A \neq O\) and \(B \neq O\).
  2. First the case when the points \(O\), \(A\) and \(B\) lie on one line. Then \(\vek b = k\,\vek a\) for some number \(k \neq 0\), the arrows point in the same or in opposite directions, and they do not form a right angle. On the other hand, by (2.6) \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2) = k\,|\vek a|^2\), which is not zero, because \(k \neq 0\) and \(|\vek a| > 0\). Both statements are false, so they are equivalent.
  3. Otherwise \(OAB\) is a triangle. The sides at the vertex \(O\) have the lengths \(|\vek a|\) and \(|\vek b|\), and the third side the length \(|\vek b - \vek a|\), because by (1.8) \(\vek b - \vek a\) is the vector from \(A\) to \(B\).
  4. By the Pythagorean theorem and its converse from school, the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2\).
  5. By the formula for the square of the length of a difference (lemma 2.9), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\). So the condition of step 4 is \(-2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\), which holds exactly when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2, Theorem 1 (PDF p. 388), CM §2.2.2.1, item 2 (PDF p. 42). The route through the Pythagorean theorem is not from the sources.

The proof showed that the scalar product measures by how much a triangle made of two arrows differs from a right triangle. The theorem gives a computational test for a right angle that needs neither a picture nor a protractor. So we take it as the definition.

Definition 2.11 Perpendicular vectors (pravokotna vektorja)

The vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) are perpendicular (pravokotna) or orthogonal (ortogonalna) when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (PDF p. 388), CM §2.2.2.1 (PDF p. 42).

For nonzero vectors the definition, by theorem 2.10, means exactly what it meant at school: the arrows form a right angle. But it also covers the zero vector, which is perpendicular to every vector, since \(\vek 0\cdot\vek b = 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{y}} + 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). We cannot give the zero vector an angle, because it has no direction. But when we split vectors, the part perpendicular to a given direction will sometimes be the zero vector, and even then we will want to say that it is perpendicular. The unit vectors of the axes are pairwise perpendicular, for example \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 0\), which agrees with the axes being perpendicular to each other.

To compute with long expressions, we also need rules that say how the scalar product works together with addition and multiplication by a number.

Lemma 2.12 Properties of the scalar product (lastnosti skalarnega produkta)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and all numbers \(k\):

  1. \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = \vek b\cdot\vek a\),
  2. \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b + \vek c) = \vek a\cdot\vek b + \vek a\cdot\vek c\),
  3. \((k\,\vek a)\cdot\vek b = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b) = \vek a\cdot(k\,\vek b)\),
  4. \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = |\vek a|^2\), so \(\vek a\cdot\vek a \ge 0\), and \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = 0\) only for \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
Proof

We write each rule with components by (2.6) and reduce it to rules for numbers.

1. Symmetry.

  1. The left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\), the right side \(b_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. In multiplying numbers the order does not matter, \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} = b_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). The sums agree term by term.

2. Splitting a sum.

  1. The sum of vectors \(\vek b + \vek c\) has the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.14), so the left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}}(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}) + a_{\mathrm{y}}(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}) + a_{\mathrm{z}}(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  2. By the rule for multiplying a sum by a number, \(a_{\mathrm{x}}(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}) = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. We rearrange the six terms so that the products with \(\vek b\) are together and the products with \(\vek c\) are together: \((a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) + (a_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} c_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} c_{\mathrm{z}})\). This is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b + \vek a\cdot\vek c\).

3. Taking out a number.

  1. The vector \(k\,\vek a\) has the components \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(k\,a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(k\,a_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \((k\,\vek a)\cdot\vek b = (k\,a_{\mathrm{x}})\,b_{\mathrm{x}} + (k\,a_{\mathrm{y}})\,b_{\mathrm{y}} + (k\,a_{\mathrm{z}})\,b_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. In multiplying three numbers it does not matter which two we multiply first, so the terms equal \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  3. We take out the common factor \(k\) and get \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\).
  4. In the same way \(\vek a\cdot(k\,\vek b) = a_{\mathrm{x}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{x}}) + a_{\mathrm{y}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{y}}) + a_{\mathrm{z}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{z}}) = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\).

4. The product of a vector with itself.

  1. By (2.6) \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2\), and by (1.10) this is \(|\vek a|^2\).
  2. Every square of a real number is nonnegative, so their sum is nonnegative too.
  3. A sum of three nonnegative numbers is zero only if each of them is zero. So \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = 0\) gives \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{y}} = a_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), that is, \(\vek a\) is the zero vector \(\vek 0\) (definition 1.13).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (3), (5), (5a*) (PDF pp. 388-389), CM (2.2.7), (2.2.8) (PDF p. 42). The proofs are not from the sources.

The proof again showed the principle from chapter 1: we check a statement about vectors on the components, where it becomes a rule for numbers. With properties 1 to 3 we may compute with the scalar product as with the multiplication of numbers: expand brackets, swap factors and take out numbers. Property 4 says that the scalar product contains the length, \(|\vek a| = \sqrt{\vek a\cdot\vek a}\) (KREYSZIG §9.2 (3), PDF p. 388).

Exactly these properties are what mathematics takes as the definition of a general scalar product. Take a vector space in which elements are multiplied by real numbers (definition 1.16), and a rule whose input is two elements of the space and whose output is one number. The rule is called a scalar product when it splits sums and multiples as our product does (properties 2 and 3), when it is symmetric (property 1), and when the product of every element with itself is positive, except for the zero element, where it is zero (the second part of property 4). A space with such a rule is an inner product space (prostor s skalarnim produktom) (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF pp. 337-338). Such a space gets the length of an element from the scalar product, as property 4 gives it for us. We will need only the scalar product (2.6).

Now we can answer the question from the beginning of the section. Let the unit vector \(\vek e\) give a direction. We want to write the vector \(\vek a\) as \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\), where \(p\,\vek e\) is the part in the direction \(\vek e\) and the vector \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\). How large must \(p\) be? The remainder is \(\vek r = \vek a - p\,\vek e\), and the condition that it is perpendicular to \(\vek e\) is \(\vek e\cdot(\vek a - p\,\vek e) = 0\). By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product the left side equals \(\vek e\cdot\vek a - p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e)\), and by item 4 \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = |\vek e|^2 = 1\). So the condition is \(\vek e\cdot\vek a - p = 0\), and by item 1 \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\). So the number \(p\) is the scalar product of the vector with the unit vector of the direction.

Definition 2.13 Projection of a vector onto a direction (projekcija vektorja na smer)

Let \(\vek e\) be a unit vector. The projection (projekcija) of the vector \(\vek a\) onto the direction \(\vek e\) is the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek e\). It is also called the component in the direction of \(\vek e\) (komponenta v smeri \(\vek e\)). The vector \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is the part of the vector \(\vek a\) in the direction \(\vek e\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (12) (PDF p. 391), CM §2.2.2.1, item 4 (PDF p. 42).

The projection is a number that can also be negative. Then the part in the direction \(\vek e\) points opposite to \(\vek e\). For the direction of the axis \(x\) the projection is just the component, \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 1 + a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which justifies the name component in the direction. For a direction given by a vector \(\vek b\) that is not a unit vector, we take the unit vector in the direction of the vector \(\vek b\), and the projection is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/|\vek b|\) (KREYSZIG §9.2 (11), PDF p. 391). The next lemma says that the split we were looking for really exists, that there is only one, and how lengths behave in it.

Lemma 2.14 Splitting a vector into a part along a direction and a perpendicular part (razstavitev vektorja na del v smeri in pravokotni del)

Let \(\vek e\) be a unit vector and \(\vek a\) any vector, both written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. Then:

  1. The vector \(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\), and
    \[\vek a = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e + \big(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\big) . \tag{2.8}\]
  2. If \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\) for some number \(p\) and a vector \(\vek r\) perpendicular to \(\vek e\), then \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\).
  3. \(|\vek a|^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 + |\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e|^2\).
Proof

We compute all three statements with the properties of the scalar product. The key is that \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = |\vek e|^2 = 1\) by item 4 of the properties, because \(\vek e\) is a unit vector.

1. The perpendicular part.

  1. We write \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\) and \(\vek r = \vek a - p\,\vek e\).
  2. By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek e\cdot\vek r = \vek e\cdot\vek a - p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) = \vek e\cdot\vek a - p\).
  3. By item 1 \(\vek e\cdot\vek a = \vek a\cdot\vek e = p\), so \(\vek e\cdot\vek r = 0\), and \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\).
  4. The equality (2.8) holds by the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15): \(p\,\vek e + (\vek a - p\,\vek e) = \vek a + (p\,\vek e - p\,\vek e) = \vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\).

2. Uniqueness.

  1. We take the scalar product of both sides of the equality \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\) with \(\vek e\). By items 1 to 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) + \vek r\cdot\vek e\).
  2. Here \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = 1\), and \(\vek r\cdot\vek e = 0\), because \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\). What remains is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = p\).

3. The Pythagorean theorem for the split.

  1. Let \(\vek u = p\,\vek e\) with \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\), and \(\vek r = \vek a - \vek u\). By item 1 \(\vek a = \vek u + \vek r\).
  2. By item 3 of the properties and item 1 of this lemma \(\vek u\cdot\vek r = p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek r) = 0\).
  3. By the formula for the square of the length of a sum (lemma 2.9) \(|\vek a|^2 = |\vek u|^2 + |\vek r|^2 + 2\,\vek u\cdot\vek r = |\vek u|^2 + |\vek r|^2\).
  4. By items 3 and 4 of the properties \(|\vek u|^2 = (p\,\vek e)\cdot(p\,\vek e) = p^2\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) = p^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (10)-(12), Fig. 181 (PDF p. 391), CM §2.2.2.1, item 4 (PDF p. 42), CM (4.2.16) (PDF p. 184). The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma says three things. Every vector can be split into a part in the direction \(\vek e\) and a part perpendicular to it. There is only one such split, so the projection is the only number that makes it possible. And the lengths of the two parts add up by the Pythagorean theorem, like the legs of a right triangle with the hypotenuse \(\vek a\). We will use exactly this triangle for the force on an oblique cut.

Let us try it with numbers. Let \(\vek a = (5, 10, 5)\) and \(\vek e = (0, \tfrac35, \tfrac45)\). The vector \(\vek e\) is a unit vector, since \(|\vek e|^2 = 0 + \tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 1\). The projection is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = 0 + 6 + 4 = 10\), the part in the direction \(\vek e\) is \(10\,\vek e = (0, 6, 8)\), and the perpendicular part is \((5, 10, 5) - (0, 6, 8) = (5, 4, -3)\). Indeed \((5, 4, -3)\cdot\vek e = 0 + \tfrac{12}{5} - \tfrac{12}{5} = 0\). Lengths: \(|\vek a|^2 = 25 + 100 + 25 = 150\), and \(10^2 + (25 + 16 + 9) = 100 + 50 = 150\), as item 3 requires.

What does the projection have to do with the angles from school? In figure 2.15 the arrows \(\vek a\), \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) and \(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) form a right triangle. The hypotenuse has the length \(|\vek a|\), and the right angle is between the last two, which are perpendicular by lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector. Let first \(\vek a\cdot\vek e > 0\). Then the leg \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) points in the direction of \(\vek e\), so the angle of the triangle between the hypotenuse \(\vek a\) and this leg is the angle \(\gamma\) between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek e\). It is acute, like every angle of a right triangle that is not the right angle. Then the leg at the angle \(\gamma\) has the length \(\vek a\cdot\vek e\), since the square of the length of the vector \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\), as we computed in step 4 of the proof of item 3. The cosine of an acute angle in a right triangle is, from school, the ratio of the adjacent leg to the hypotenuse, so

\[\vek a\cdot\vek e = |\vek a|\cos\gamma .\]

If \(\vek a\cdot\vek e < 0\), the leg \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) points opposite to \(\vek e\), and the angle of the triangle between the hypotenuse and this leg is \(180^\circ - \gamma\). The leg has the length \(-\vek a\cdot\vek e\), so \(-\vek a\cdot\vek e = |\vek a|\cos(180^\circ - \gamma) = -|\vek a|\cos\gamma\) by the school rule \(\cos(180^\circ - \gamma) = -\cos\gamma\), and the formula holds again. If \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = 0\), the angle \(\gamma\) is a right angle, \(\cos\gamma = 0\), and the formula holds. If \(\vek a\) lies on the line in the direction of \(\vek e\), there is no triangle. Then \(\vek a = \pm|\vek a|\,\vek e\), the angle \(\gamma\) is \(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\), and the formula holds, since \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = \pm|\vek a| = |\vek a|\cos\gamma\). KREYSZIG introduces the projection with this formula (§9.2 (10), PDF p. 391). In our example \(\cos\gamma = 10/\sqrt{150} = 0{,}816\) and \(\gamma = 35{,}3^\circ\).

Item 3 of the lemma gives one more thing. The number \(|\vek a|^2\) is the sum of \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\) and the square of a length, which is not negative, so the projection never exceeds the length of the vector in absolute value. For any two vectors this inequality reads as follows.

Corollary 2.15 Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Cauchy-Schwarzeva neenakost)

For any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b| \le |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).

Proof

We reduce the inequality to the projection onto the direction of the vector \(\vek b\).

  1. If \(\vek b = \vek 0\), then \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = 0\) and \(|\vek b| = 0\). Both sides are zero, and the inequality holds.
  2. Let \(\vek b \neq \vek 0\). Then \(\vek e = \vek b/|\vek b|\) is the unit vector in the direction of a vector, here of \(\vek b\) (lemma 1.19).
  3. By item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, \(|\vek a|^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 + |\vek r|^2\) with \(\vek r = \vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\). The square \(|\vek r|^2\) is not negative, so \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 \le |\vek a|^2\).
  4. The numbers \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e|\) and \(|\vek a|\) are nonnegative. Of two nonnegative numbers the larger one has the larger square. So if \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e|\) were larger than \(|\vek a|\), then \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\) would be larger than \(|\vek a|^2\), which contradicts step 3. So \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e| \le |\vek a|\).
  5. By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = \vek a\cdot\big(\tfrac{1}{|\vek b|}\,\vek b\big) = \tfrac{1}{|\vek b|}\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\), so \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b|/|\vek b| \le |\vek a|\).
  6. We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(|\vek b|\) and get \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b| \le |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (6) (PDF p. 389). The proof is not from the sources.

For two nonzero vectors the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) therefore always lies between \(-1\) and \(1\), and from school we know that exactly one angle \(\gamma\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) has this cosine. KREYSZIG takes it as the angle between the vectors (KREYSZIG §9.2 (4), PDF p. 388):

\[\cos\gamma = \frac{\vek a\cdot\vek b}{|\vek a|\,|\vek b|} .\]

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives one more property of lengths, which we need for distances between points. The fly from chapter 1 flew from the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) down to the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) on the floor and then crawled along the floor by the vector \((2, 0, 0)\) to the point \(R = (5, 5, 0)\) (figure 2.16). The path from \(P\) through \(Q\) to \(R\) is \(|PQ| + |QR| = 3 + 2 = 5\) m long. The straight path from \(P\) to \(R\) is the vector \(\overrightarrow{PR} = (3, 2, -2)\), which has the length \(\sqrt{9 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{17} = 4{,}12\) m and is shorter. From school we know the rule that each side of a triangle is shorter than the sum of the other two. In chapter 1 we needed it for distances in space but did not prove it (section 1.15.6). Now we prove it from the formula for the length, with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Corollary 2.16 Triangle inequality for vectors (trikotniška neenakost za vektorje)

For any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and for any points \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\):

  1. \(|\vek a + \vek b| \le |\vek a| + |\vek b|\),
  2. \(|PR| \le |PQ| + |QR|\), where \(|PQ| = |\overrightarrow{PQ}|\) is the distance between the points \(P\) and \(Q\),
  3. \(\big||\vek a| - |\vek b|\big| \le |\vek a - \vek b|\).
Proof

We must show three inequalities. We prove the first one for the squares of both sides and reduce the other two to it.

1. Length of a sum.

  1. By the formula for the square of the length of a sum (lemma 2.9), \(|\vek a + \vek b|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 + 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\).
  2. Every number is at most its absolute value, so \(\vek a\cdot\vek b \le |\vek a\cdot\vek b|\). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (corollary 2.15), \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b| \le |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).
  3. Steps 1 and 2 give \(|\vek a + \vek b|^2 \le |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 + 2\,|\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).
  4. By the school formula for the square of a sum, the right-hand side equals \(\big(|\vek a| + |\vek b|\big)^2\).
  5. The numbers \(|\vek a + \vek b|\) and \(|\vek a| + |\vek b|\) are nonnegative. By lemma 1.28, that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, steps 3 and 4 give \(|\vek a + \vek b| \le |\vek a| + |\vek b|\).

2. Distances between points.

  1. By (1.8) the \(x\) component of the sum \(\overrightarrow{PQ} + \overrightarrow{QR}\) is \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) + (x_{\mathrm{R}} - x_{\mathrm{Q}}) = x_{\mathrm{R}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}\), and this is the \(x\) component of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PR}\). The same holds for the \(y\) and \(z\) components, so \(\overrightarrow{PR} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \overrightarrow{QR}\).
  2. The distance between two points is the length of the vector between them (definition 1.17). By item 1 with \(\vek a = \overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\vek b = \overrightarrow{QR}\), therefore, \(|PR| = |\overrightarrow{PQ} + \overrightarrow{QR}| \le |PQ| + |QR|\).

3. Difference of lengths.

  1. By the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.15), \(\vek a = (\vek a - \vek b) + \vek b\). So by item 1, \(|\vek a| \le |\vek a - \vek b| + |\vek b|\), that is, \(|\vek a| - |\vek b| \le |\vek a - \vek b|\).
  2. With the roles of the vectors exchanged we get in the same way \(|\vek b| - |\vek a| \le |\vek b - \vek a|\).
  3. The components of the vector \(\vek b - \vek a\) are the opposites of the components of \(\vek a - \vek b\) and have the same squares, so by (1.10) \(|\vek b - \vek a| = |\vek a - \vek b|\).
  4. By steps 1 to 3 both the number \(|\vek a| - |\vek b|\) and its opposite are at most \(|\vek a - \vek b|\). The absolute value of a number is one of these two numbers, so it is at most \(|\vek a - \vek b|\) too.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (7) (PDF p. 389) states inequality 1 and leaves its proof to the reader, with the hint to square and use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (problem 16, PDF p. 393). LEBL2 proves it in \(\mathbb R^n\) (def. 8.2.1 and the text after theorem 8.2.2, PDF pp. 20-21), and LEBL1 proves inequality 3 for numbers (corollary 1.3.2, PDF p. 37). The proof is not from the sources.

The name says what it means geometrically: in the triangle \(PQR\) the side \(PR\) is not longer than the sum of the other two sides (KREYSZIG §9.2, PDF p. 389). The proof showed why. The square of the length of the sum and the square of the sum of the lengths differ only in the doubled term, \(2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\) against \(2\,|\vek a|\,|\vek b|\), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality says that the first is not larger. For the fly \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\cdot\overrightarrow{QR} = (1, 2, -2)\cdot(2, 0, 0) = 2\), which is less than \(|PQ|\,|QR| = 3 \cdot 2 = 6\). So the square of the straight path, \(17\), is smaller by \(2\,(6 - 2) = 8\) than the square of the path through \(Q\), \(25\). Item 1 is also the third of the requirements for a norm that we listed in section 1.5.4, and our length therefore satisfies it. Item 3 says that the lengths of two vectors differ by at most the length of their difference. For \(\vek a = \overrightarrow{PR} = (3, 2, -2)\) and \(\vek b = \overrightarrow{PQ} = (1, 2, -2)\) we have \(|\vek a| - |\vek b| = 4{,}12 - 3 = 1{,}12\), while the difference \(\vek a - \vek b = (2, 0, 0)\) has the length \(2\).

Now we can also prove the claim from section 1.15.6. Let all points closer to a point \(P\) than \(r\) lie in a set \(M\), and let \(S\) be a point with \(|PS| < r\). The number \(r - |PS|\) is positive. For every point \(T\) with \(|ST| < r - |PS|\), item 2 gives

\[|PT| \le |PS| + |ST| < |PS| + (r - |PS|) = r ,\]

so \(T\) lies in \(M\). The point \(S\) is therefore an interior point of the set \(M\), with the bound \(r - |PS|\) instead of the \(r/4\) of lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point (LEBL1 prop. 7.2.9, PDF p. 266). For \(M\) we may take the ball with centre \(P\) and radius \(r\) itself, the set of all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\). Each of its points is therefore an interior point, and the ball is an open set.

We will need the angle between arbitrary vectors in section 2.9. For the oblique cut we will know the angle from school trigonometry already, and the scalar product will split the force.
