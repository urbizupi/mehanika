Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.8 The thick-walled spherical shell

In chapter 4 we cut a thick spherical shell with inner radius 40 mm and outer radius 60 mm into six curved boxes (section 4.3.7) and promised to load it with pressure. Now we do so. The shell holds oil at pressure \(p = 30\) MPa and its outer surface is free, the same as for the tube of section 11.7. What stresses and displacements does the pressure cause in the shell? Where is the material loaded most? And how does the shell differ from a tube with the same two radii?

The steel has, as in the previous chapters, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}30\), so the Lamé constant is \(\lambda = 113{,}08\) GPa, the shear modulus \(\mu = 75{,}38\) GPa and the bulk modulus \(K = 163{,}3\) GPa (definitions 3.10 and 3.7). We write the inner radius as \(a = 40\) mm and the outer one as \(b = 60\) mm, and the spherical coordinates \(R\), \(\varphi\), \(\theta\) are those of section 11.6, with the centre at the point \(O\) of the shell and the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) pointing away from \(O\).

How do we guess the displacement field? The shell, the pressure and the material do not change if we rotate the shell about the centre \(O\). No direction through \(O\) is preferred over the others. We therefore expect that a point at distance \(R\) from the centre moves along the ray from \(O\), and by an amount that depends only on the distance \(R\) and not on the direction. The displacement field then has the form \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\). This is only a guess, a semi-inverse one in the sense of definition 11.11: we prescribe the form of the field and compute the unknown function \(u(R)\). The guess is justified only once we check the field, as we said in section 11.4: if it satisfies the field equations and the boundary conditions, it is a solution by theorem 11.4, otherwise it is not.

Theorem 11.28 Lamé solution for the thick-walled spherical shell (Laméjeva rešitev za debelo krogelno lupino)

Let \(a \le R \le b\) be a thick spherical shell with centre \(O\), made of a homogeneous isotropic linearly elastic material with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), without body force. Let the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) act on the inner surface \(R = a\) and the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) on the outer surface \(R = b\). Denote

\[A = \frac{p_{\mathrm{a}} a^3 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^3}{b^3 - a^3} , \qquad B = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^3 b^3}{2\,(b^3 - a^3)} . \tag{11.38}\]

Then the displacement field

\[\vek u = \Big( \frac{A}{3K}\,R + \frac{B}{2\mu}\,\frac{1}{R^2} \Big)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} , \qquad K = \lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu , \tag{11.39}\]

is a solution of the force problem (definition 11.1), with stress components

\[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = A - \frac{2B}{R^3} , \\ &\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{R^3} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} = \sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta} = \sigma_{\varphi\theta} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.40}\]

There is no other solution, except for fields that differ from this one by a small rigid displacement (lemma 4.72).

Source: CM, Example 7.3.1, (7.3.5) to (7.3.15) (PDF pp. 302-304), BOWER §4.1.4 (PDF pp. 224-225).

The proof has seven steps: we compute the strains, stresses and equilibrium for the field \(u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), integrate the equation twice, determine the constants from the boundary conditions and finally use uniqueness.

Proof

We must show that the field (11.39) satisfies the requirements of definition 11.1 and that there is no other solution. First we write all the equations for the field \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) with a twice continuously differentiable function \(u\) on \([a, b]\), and then we determine \(u\).

  1. Strains. For \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) the components are \(u_\varphi = u_\theta = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{R}} = u(R)\) depends only on \(R\). By corollary 11.22 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/R\), and all shear strains are zero, because they contain derivatives with respect to \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) and the components \(u_\varphi\) and \(u_\theta\). The term \(u/R\) is the stretch of the fibres that surround the centre: a circle with circumference \(2\pi R\) has circumference \(2\pi (R + u)\) after the displacement, so it stretches by \(u/R\).
  2. Stresses. By corollary 11.26 Hooke's law (3.13) holds in local components. The trace of the strains is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}} + \varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u' + 2u/R\), hence
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = \lambda\Big( u' + \frac{2u}{R} \Big) + 2\mu\,u' , \qquad \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \lambda\Big( u' + \frac{2u}{R} \Big) + 2\mu\,\frac{u}{R} ,\]
    and the shear stresses are zero.
  3. Equilibrium. By corollary 11.25 the equilibrium equations without body force in the directions \(\vek e_\varphi\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) are sums of derivatives with respect to \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) and of shear stresses. All of these are zero, so the equations hold. In the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) what remains is
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}}{\mathrm{d}R} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{R} = 0 ,\]
    that is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}' + 2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/R = 0\). We insert the stresses of step 2. The derivative of the first term is \(\lambda\,(u'' + 2u'/R - 2u/R^2) + 2\mu u''\), and the difference \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is \(2\mu\,(u' - u/R)\), so the equation is
    \[(\lambda + 2\mu)\Big( u'' + \frac{2u'}{R} - \frac{2u}{R^2} \Big) = 0 .\]
    We see that \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) have combined into \(\lambda + 2\mu = E\,(1 - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), and this is positive (theorem 3.48), so we may divide.
  4. Integration. The expression in the bracket is the derivative of the function \(R^{-2}\,(R^2 u)'\) (by the product rule \(\big(R^{-2}\,(R^2 u)'\big)' = \big(u' + 2u/R\big)' = u'' + 2u'/R - 2u/R^2\), since \(R^{-2}(R^2u)' = u' + 2u/R\)). Hence \(R^{-2}\,(R^2 u)'\) is a function with zero derivative on the interval \([a, b]\) and is constant by corollary 1.85, say \(3C_1\). So \((R^2 u)' = 3C_1 R^2\), and since this is the derivative of the function \(C_1 R^3\), the function \(R^2 u - C_1 R^3\) again has zero derivative, so it is a constant \(C_2\). It follows that \(u = C_1 R + C_2/R^2\). This function is indeed twice continuously differentiable on \([a, b]\), because \(a > 0\).
  5. Stresses in terms of the constants. The trace of the strains is \(u' + 2u/R = 3C_1\), because the terms \(-2C_2/R^3\) and \(2C_2/R^3\) cancel. From step 2 we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = 3\lambda C_1 + 2\mu\,(C_1 - 2C_2/R^3) = 3K\,C_1 - 4\mu\,C_2/R^3\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 3\lambda C_1 + 2\mu\,(C_1 + C_2/R^3) = 3K\,C_1 + 2\mu\,C_2/R^3\), where \(3\lambda + 2\mu = 3K\).
  6. Boundary conditions. On the inner surface the outward normal of the shell is \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and the pressure gives the stress vector \(\bar{\vek t} = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek n = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24) \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = -\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) (there are no shear stresses), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(a) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\). On the outer surface \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and in the same way \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(b) = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). By step 5 these are two equations for \(C_1\) and \(C_2\):
    \[3K\,C_1 - \frac{4\mu\,C_2}{a^3} = -p_{\mathrm{a}} , \qquad 3K\,C_1 - \frac{4\mu\,C_2}{b^3} = -p_{\mathrm{b}} .\]
    We subtract them: \(4\mu\,C_2\,(1/a^3 - 1/b^3) = p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(C_2 = (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^3 b^3/\big(4\mu\,(b^3 - a^3)\big) = B/(2\mu)\). From the first equation \(3K\,C_1 = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + 4\mu\,C_2/a^3 = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + 2B/a^3\), which by (11.38) equals \(A\), since \(A - 2B/a^3 = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\) (computation: \((p_{\mathrm{a}} a^3 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^3 - (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,b^3)/(b^3 - a^3) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\)). Therefore \(C_1 = A/(3K)\) and we obtain (11.39). The stresses of step 5 are now \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = A - 2B/R^3\), which is the first relation in (11.40), and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/R^3\), since \(2\mu C_2 = B\).
  7. Uniqueness. The field (11.39) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the shell (\(R > 0\)), it satisfies the equilibrium equations (step 3) and the traction condition on both surfaces (step 6), so it is a solution of the force problem. The shell is a body made of pieces between graphs and curved boxes (section 4.3.7), for which we proved the divergence theorem, so we may use uniqueness. By theorem 11.4 this is the only solution, except for a small rigid displacement.

Source: The proof follows CM, Example 7.3.1 (PDF pp. 302-304), where the equilibrium equation is solved by the change of variable \(R = \mathrm{e}^{\xi}\), and BOWER §4.1.4 (PDF p. 225), where the equation is integrated twice, as here. Step 7 is ours.

The proof showed that the guessed displacement field is indeed a solution and that it is the only one. The two constants have a meaning: the term \(C_1 R\) is a uniform stretch in which every small piece is stretched equally in all directions, and the term \(C_2/R^2\) decreases with distance and appears because the shell is hollow (a solid ball does not have it, as we shall see soon).

Let us compute the shell of our problem. For \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 30\) MPa, \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) we get

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{30 \cdot 40^3}{60^3 - 40^3}\ \text{MPa} = 12{,}632\ \text{MPa} , \\ B &= \frac{30 \cdot 40^3 \cdot 60^3}{2\,(60^3 - 40^3)}\ \text{MPa} = 1{,}3642 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{MPa mm}^3 . \end{aligned}\]

The displacement constants are \(C_1 = A/(3K) = 2{,}578 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(C_2 = B/(2\mu) = 9{,}048\ \text{mm}^3\). All numbers of this section were computed with SymPy. The stresses and displacements on the inner surface, in the middle of the wall and on the outer surface are:

\(R\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (MPa) \(u\) (\(\mu\)m) \(u/R\)
40 mm \(-30{,}000\) \(33{,}947\) \(6{,}686\) \(1{,}672 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
50 mm \(-9{,}196\) \(23{,}545\) \(4{,}908\) \(0{,}982 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
60 mm \(0\) \(18{,}947\) \(4{,}060\) \(0{,}677 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

Let us also check that the boundary conditions are indeed satisfied: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(40) = -30\) MPa is the oil pressure and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(60) = 0\) is the free outer surface. The stresses are shown in figure 11.21.

The figure and the table show two things. First, the radial stress is compressive everywhere, from \(-30\) MPa to \(0\), and the hoop stress is tensile everywhere, even on the outer surface, which is free: \(18{,}947\) MPa. No force acts on that surface, yet the hoop rings are stretched, because the inside of the shell pushes them outward. Second, the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} + 2\,\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 3A = 37{,}89\) MPa is the same everywhere. This is no coincidence: the sum is three times the mean stress, and by step 5 the trace of the strains \(3C_1 = 7{,}73 \cdot 10^{-5}\) is the same in every point. The volume of every small piece therefore increases by the same fraction, although the pieces are stretched differently: closer to the inner surface they stretch more along the circumference, closer to the outer one less. The radial and the hoop stretching add up to the same total. The cavity grows by \(4\pi a^2 u(a) = 134{,}4\ \text{mm}^3\).

The load is largest on the inner surface. There the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 33{,}947\) MPa (twice the same, because \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) are equivalent) and \(\sigma_3 = -30\) MPa. The von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.76) is therefore \(\sqrt{\tfrac12\big(0 + 2\,(63{,}947)^2\big)} = 63{,}947\) MPa, that is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\). We get the same from the largest shear stress, \(2\tau_{\max} = \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 63{,}947\) MPa (theorem 2.70). With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa the material first starts to yield (von Mises condition) when \(p = 30 \cdot 220/63{,}947 = 103{,}21\) MPa. The stresses are proportional to the pressure, so this computation is exact.

Why is the tube with the same radii worse? At \(p = 30\) MPa the tube reaches \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 78\) MPa on the inner surface (section 11.7) and starts to yield at \(p = 68{,}35\) MPa. The shell carries a \(103{,}21/68{,}35 = 1{,}51\) times larger pressure. We show the reason with equilibrium. We cut the shell by a plane through the centre \(O\) and consider one half (figure 11.22). Let the axis \(z\) point perpendicular to the cut and let the half lie at \(z \ge 0\). The oil pressure on the inner hemisphere has a component in the direction \(z\) equal to \(p\int \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), and the area element of the sphere is \(a^2\sin\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta\) (section 4.3.6), so the component is \(p\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\varphi\, a^2\sin\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta = p\,\pi a^2\). The components perpendicular to the axis cancel after rotation about the axis. This is the pressure on a disc with radius \(a\), as if the hemisphere were flat. This force is resisted by the stress on the cut annulus with area \(\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\). The normal of the cut there is \(\vek e_\varphi\) (at \(\varphi = \pi/2\)), there are no shear stresses, so the stress on the cut is \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). Equilibrium of the half in the direction \(z\) requires

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R = p\,\pi a^2 , \tag{11.41}\]

so the mean hoop stress over the annulus equals \(p\,a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 24\) MPa. The same computation for the expression of theorem 11.28 gives \(\int_{40}^{60} \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R = 150\,796\ \text{N} = 30\ \text{MPa} \cdot \pi \cdot 40^2\ \text{mm}^2\), as it must. Equilibrium of the half therefore confirms the solution without using Hooke's law.

We cut the tube in the same way by a plane through the axis. The cut surface consists of two strips, each with area \((b - a)\) per unit length, and the pressure gives the force \(2pa\) per unit length. The mean hoop stress is therefore \(p\,a/(b - a) = 60\) MPa, which is \((b + a)/a = 2{,}5\) times more than for the shell (\(24 \cdot 2{,}5 = 60\)). The reason lies in the areas: the force of the pressure on half of the shell is \(p\,\pi a^2\) and the area of the cut is \(\pi\,(b - a)(b + a)\), for the tube the force is \(2pa\) and the area of the cut is \(2\,(b - a)\). The quotient is \(a/(a + b) = 0{,}4\). For a thin wall \(a/(a + b) \to \tfrac12\). In a spherical shell two hoop directions share the load, in a tube only one does, so for the same thickness the shell is much less stressed.

For a thin wall we use, as usual, the formula \(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/(2t)\) with mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\) mm and thickness \(t = 20\) mm: \(30 \cdot 50/40 = 37{,}5\) MPa. The obtained value is \(56\ \%\) larger than the exact mean \(24\) MPa. The reason is a substitution: the mean is \(p\,a^2/\big(t\,(a + b)\big)\), while the formula replaces \(a^2\) by \(r_{\mathrm{m}}^2\), and \((r_{\mathrm{m}}/a)^2 = 1{,}5625\). It agrees better with the largest stress on the inner surface: \(37{,}5\) MPa is \(10{,}5\ \%\) more than \(33{,}947\) MPa, so the formula is on the safe side. The formula holds exactly only in the limit: \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\,t/(p\,a) \to \tfrac12\) as \(t \to 0\) (SymPy), which is exactly \(p\,a/(2t)\).

Two special cases follow from the theorem. The first is a solid ball, that is a shell without a cavity, such as the one we immersed in oil at pressure \(p = 100\) MPa in chapter 4. For a solid ball the displacement equation (11.39) still holds on \(0 < R \le b\), but the term \(C_2/R^2\) tends to infinity as \(R \to 0\) and the field would not be continuously differentiable at the centre. Hence \(C_2 = 0\). What remains is \(u = C_1 R\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = 3K\,C_1 = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(u = -p_{\mathrm{b}} R/(3K)\), that is the displacement field \(\vek u = \varepsilon\,(x, y, z)\) with \(\varepsilon = -p/(3K) = -2{,}041 \cdot 10^{-4}\), which we took there as a solution. Uniqueness says that it is the only one. The same follows from the theorem with \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) and \(a \to 0\): the constants tend to \(A \to -p_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(B \to 0\), and the stress is \(-p_{\mathrm{b}}\) in every point.

The second is a small cavity in a ball under outer pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) (\(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\)). The hoop stress on its wall is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + B/a^3 = -\tfrac32\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\,b^3/(b^3 - a^3)\). For \(b/a = 1{,}5\) this is \(-2{,}13\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\), for \(b/a = 3\) it is \(-1{,}56\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\) and for \(b/a = 10\) it is \(-1{,}50\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\). When the cavity is small compared with the ball, the compressive hoop stress on the wall of the cavity is \(1{,}5\) times larger than the pressure, whatever the size of the cavity. The cavity therefore increases the stress, but its size does not matter.

We showed how the pressure in the interior of a thick spherical shell distributes the stresses, which are largest on the inner surface, and that the shell carries a \(1{,}51\) times larger pressure than a tube with the same radii.
