Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.8 The thick-walled spherical shell

In chapter 4 we cut a thick spherical shell with inner radius 40 mm and outer radius 60 mm into six curved boxes (section 4.3.7) and promised to load it with pressure. Now we do so. The shell holds oil at pressure \(p = 30\) MPa and its outer surface is free, the same as for the tube of section 11.7. What stresses and displacements does the pressure cause in the shell? Where is the material loaded most? And how does the shell differ from a tube with the same two radii?

The steel has, as in the previous chapters, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}30\), so the Lamé constant is \(\lambda = 113{,}08\) GPa, the shear modulus \(\mu = 75{,}38\) GPa and the bulk modulus \(K = 163{,}3\) GPa (definitions 3.18 and 3.15). We write the inner radius as \(a = 40\) mm and the outer one as \(b = 60\) mm, and the spherical coordinates \(R\), \(\varphi\), \(\theta\) are those of section 11.6, with the centre at the point \(O\) of the shell and the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) pointing away from \(O\).

Let us draw the shell in a section by a plane through the centre \(O\) (figure 11.51, part (a)). The grey dashed circles are the inner surface with radius \(a\) and the outer one with radius \(b\), before we let the oil into the shell. The oil pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) pushes on the inner surface at a right angle to it, so everywhere away from the centre. The orange circles are the same surfaces under pressure, as we shall compute them in this section: the inner one moves outward by \(u(a)\), the outer one by \(u(b)\). The displacements are drawn 2500 times enlarged, otherwise the orange lines could not be told from the grey ones.

How do we guess the displacement field? The shell, the pressure and the material do not change if we rotate the shell about the centre \(O\). No direction through \(O\) is preferred over the others. We therefore expect that a point at distance \(R\) from the centre moves along the ray from \(O\), in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), and by an amount that depends only on the distance \(R\) and not on the direction. The displacement field then has the form \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\). This is only a guess, a semi-inverse one in the sense of definition 11.11: we prescribe the form of the field and compute the unknown function \(u(R)\). The guess is justified only once we check the field, as we said in section 11.4: if it satisfies the field equations and the boundary conditions, it is a solution by theorem 11.4, otherwise it is not.

The computation rests on three assumptions. For each one let us say why it holds here, when it does not hold and what it does to the result.

  1. Spherical symmetry of the field, \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\): all points of one surface move equally far, so the orange lines in figure 11.51 remain circles. It holds here because the shell, the pressure and the material are the same in all directions from \(O\), and because the proof checks it at the end by uniqueness, so it is not taken on trust. It does not hold when one of these three things is not symmetric: a shell with a nozzle or a support, the pressure of a liquid that grows with depth, a material with fibres in one direction. Effect: instead of three partial differential equations for three displacement components, one ordinary equation for one function \(u(R)\) remains.
  2. No body force. The weight of steel with density \(7850\ \text{kg/m}^3\) gives over the height of the shell \(2b = 120\) mm a pressure \(\rho\,g\,2b = 9{,}2\) kPa, which is \(0{,}03\ \%\) of the oil pressure \(30\) MPa. It does not hold for large tanks at low pressure, where the weight of the wall and the weight of the liquid are comparable with the pressure. Effect: the right-hand side of the equilibrium equation is zero. The weight would also break the symmetry of item 1, because it points in one direction.
  3. Small strains and Hooke's law. The largest strain in the shell is \(2{,}6 \cdot 10^{-4}\) (we compute it below), so lengths change by less than three hundredths of a percent. We may therefore write the boundary conditions on the surfaces before loading, \(R = a\) and \(R = b\) (the grey circles), instead of on the displaced ones (the orange ones): the radii change by less than two ten-thousandths, and the stresses change by a fraction of that size. It does not hold once the material yields (for our shell from the pressure \(103\) MPa on, as we shall compute), nor for rubber, where lengths change by tens of percent. Effect: the stresses are proportional to the pressure.
Theorem 11.30 Lamé solution for the thick-walled spherical shell (Laméjeva rešitev za debelo krogelno lupino)

Let \(a \le R \le b\) be a thick spherical shell with centre \(O\), made of a homogeneous isotropic linearly elastic material with Lamé constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), without body force. Let the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) act on the inner surface \(R = a\) and the pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) on the outer surface \(R = b\). Denote

\[A = \frac{p_{\mathrm{a}} a^3 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^3}{b^3 - a^3} , \qquad B = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^3 b^3}{2\,(b^3 - a^3)} . \tag{11.45}\]

Then the displacement field

\[\vek u = \Big( \frac{A}{3K}\,R + \frac{B}{2\mu}\,\frac{1}{R^2} \Big)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}} , \qquad K = \lambda + \tfrac23\,\mu , \tag{11.46}\]

is a solution of the force problem (definition 11.1), with stress components

\[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = A - \frac{2B}{R^3} , \\ &\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{R^3} , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{R}\varphi} = \sigma_{\mathrm{R}\theta} = \sigma_{\varphi\theta} = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{11.47}\]

There is no other solution, except for fields that differ from this one by a small rigid displacement (lemma 4.72).

Source: CM, Example 7.3.1, (7.3.5) to (7.3.15) (PDF pp. 302-304), BOWER §4.1.4 (PDF pp. 224-225).

The proof has seven steps: we compute the strains, stresses and equilibrium for the field \(u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), integrate the equation twice, determine the constants from the boundary conditions and finally use uniqueness.

Proof

We must show that the field (11.46) satisfies the requirements of definition 11.1 and that there is no other solution. First we write all the equations for the field \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) with a twice continuously differentiable function \(u\) on \([a, b]\), and then we determine \(u\).

  1. Strains. For \(\vek u = u(R)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) the components are \(u_\varphi = u_\theta = 0\) and \(u_{\mathrm{R}} = u(R)\) depends only on \(R\). By corollary 11.22 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/R\), and all shear strains are zero, because they contain derivatives with respect to \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) and the components \(u_\varphi\) and \(u_\theta\). We read both off figure 11.51 (a). The term \(u/R\) is the stretch of the fibres that surround the centre: a circle with circumference \(2\pi R\) has circumference \(2\pi (R + u)\) after the displacement, so it stretches by \(u/R\). The term \(u'\) tells what happens to the thickness of the wall: if the outer surface moves by less than the inner one, the wall gets thinner and \(u' < 0\).
  2. Stresses. By corollary 11.27 Hooke's law (3.28) holds in local components. The trace of the strains is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}} + \varepsilon_{\varphi\varphi} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u' + 2u/R\), hence
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = \lambda\Big( u' + \frac{2u}{R} \Big) + 2\mu\,u' , \qquad \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \lambda\Big( u' + \frac{2u}{R} \Big) + 2\mu\,\frac{u}{R} ,\]
    and the shear stresses are zero. In figure 11.51, part (b), the stresses are drawn on a piece of the wall between two rays from \(O\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\) acts on the inner and the outer face of the piece, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and its equal \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi}\) on the side faces. They are drawn in the positive sense, as tension.
  3. Equilibrium. By corollary 11.26 the equilibrium equations without body force in the directions \(\vek e_\varphi\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) are sums of derivatives with respect to \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) and of shear stresses. All of these are zero, so the equations hold. In the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) what remains is
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}}{\mathrm{d}R} + \frac{2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\varphi\varphi} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{R} = 0 ,\]
    that is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}' + 2\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/R = 0\). Where does the term with \(1/R\) come from? Look at the piece in figure 11.51 (b). Its outer face is larger than the inner one, because the area between the same rays from \(O\) grows like \(R^2\). The same \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\) therefore pulls with a larger force on the outer face than on the inner one, and this gives the term \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}/R\). The side faces lie on the rays and lean toward each other, so the pull of \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) on them does not point exactly in the hoop direction (the dashed line) but a little below it, toward \(O\). This gives the term \(-(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/R\). In a flat wall the side faces would be parallel, opposite faces equally large, and neither term would be there. We insert the stresses of step 2. The derivative of the first term is \(\lambda\,(u'' + 2u'/R - 2u/R^2) + 2\mu u''\), and the difference \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is \(2\mu\,(u' - u/R)\), so the equation is
    \[(\lambda + 2\mu)\Big( u'' + \frac{2u'}{R} - \frac{2u}{R^2} \Big) = 0 .\]
    We see that \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) have combined into \(\lambda + 2\mu = E\,(1 - \nu)/\big((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), and this is positive (theorem 3.64), so we may divide.
  1. Integration. The bracket is a derivative, as \(R^{-2}(R^2u)' = u' + 2u/R\):
    \[\big(R^{-2}(R^2u)'\big)' = \big(u' + 2u/R\big)' = u'' + \frac{2u'}{R} - \frac{2u}{R^2} = 0 .\]
    Zero derivative means a constant (corollary 1.86), say \(3C_1\):
    \[R^{-2}(R^2u)' = 3C_1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (R^2u)' = 3C_1 R^2 = (C_1 R^3)' .\]
    The difference has zero derivative, so it is a constant \(C_2\):
    \[R^2u - C_1R^3 = C_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad u = C_1 R + \frac{C_2}{R^2} .\]
    This function is twice continuously differentiable on \([a, b]\), because \(a > 0\). The displacement is thus a sum of two parts. Figure 11.52, part (a), shows them for the shell of our problem, whose constants \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) we compute below. The horizontal axis carries the radius \(R\), the vertical one the displacement \(u\) in \(\mu\)m, and the grey band between \(a\) and \(b\) is the wall. The first part \(C_1 R\) is a line which, extended, passes through \(O\), so it grows in proportion to \(R\). The second part \(C_2/R^2\) decreases with distance and towards \(O\) grows beyond all bounds. The displacement \(u\) is their sum: the bars at \(R = a\) and \(R = b\) stack the blue and the orange part on top of each other. In this shell the second part falls faster than the first one grows, so the inner surface moves more than the outer one.
  2. Stresses in terms of the constants. The derivative of the solution of step 4 and the trace of the strains:
    \[u' = C_1 - \frac{2C_2}{R^3} , \qquad u' + \frac{2u}{R} = 3C_1 ,\]
    because the terms \(\mp 2C_2/R^3\) cancel. The trace is the volumetric strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\) (definition 2.114), the relative change of volume of a small piece, and figure 11.52 shows why only the first part gives it. It shows the directions \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), \(\vek e_\theta\) and \(\vek e_\varphi\) and a piece with edge \(h\) before loading. Under the part \(C_1 R\) the piece is stretched by \(C_1 h\) in all three directions, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 3C_1\). Under the part \(C_2/R^2\) the same piece is shortened by \(2C_2 h/R^3\) along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and stretched by \(C_2 h/R^3\) in both hoop directions, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0\): only the shape changes. The first part is therefore the spherical, the second the deviatoric part of the strain. Insert into step 2 (\(3\lambda + 2\mu = 3K\)):
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = 3\lambda C_1 + 2\mu\Big(C_1 - \frac{2C_2}{R^3}\Big) = 3K\,C_1 - \frac{4\mu\,C_2}{R^3} ,\]
    \[\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 3\lambda C_1 + 2\mu\Big(C_1 + \frac{C_2}{R^3}\Big) = 3K\,C_1 + \frac{2\mu\,C_2}{R^3} .\]
    With \(C_1\) stands the bulk modulus \(K\) (definition 3.15), which measures the resistance to a change of volume, with \(C_2\) only the shear modulus \(\mu\), since the second part changes only the shape.
  3. Boundary conditions. On the inner surface the outward normal of the shell is \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\), because it points out of the material into the cavity, toward \(O\), and the pressure gives the stress vector \(\bar{\vek t} = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek n = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\). By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = -\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) (there are no shear stresses), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(a) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\). On the outer surface \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\) and in the same way \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(b) = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\). By step 5 these are two equations for \(C_1\) and \(C_2\):
    \[3K\,C_1 - \frac{4\mu\,C_2}{a^3} = -p_{\mathrm{a}} , \qquad 3K\,C_1 - \frac{4\mu\,C_2}{b^3} = -p_{\mathrm{b}} .\]
    We subtract them: \(4\mu\,C_2\,(1/a^3 - 1/b^3) = p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(C_2 = (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,a^3 b^3/\big(4\mu\,(b^3 - a^3)\big) = B/(2\mu)\). From the first equation \(3K\,C_1 = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + 4\mu\,C_2/a^3 = -p_{\mathrm{a}} + 2B/a^3\), which by (11.45) equals \(A\), since \(A - 2B/a^3 = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\) (computation: \((p_{\mathrm{a}} a^3 - p_{\mathrm{b}} b^3 - (p_{\mathrm{a}} - p_{\mathrm{b}})\,b^3)/(b^3 - a^3) = -p_{\mathrm{a}}\)). Therefore \(C_1 = A/(3K)\) and we obtain (11.46). The stresses of step 5 are now \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = A - 2B/R^3\), which is the first relation in (11.47), and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + B/R^3\), since \(2\mu C_2 = B\).
  4. Uniqueness. The field (11.46) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the shell between the surfaces (\(R > 0\)), it satisfies the equilibrium equations (step 3) and the traction condition on both surfaces (step 6), so it is a solution of the force problem. The shell is a body made of pieces between graphs and curved boxes (section 4.3.7), for which we proved the divergence theorem, so we may use uniqueness. By theorem 11.4 this is the only solution, except for a small rigid displacement.

Source: The proof follows CM, Example 7.3.1 (PDF pp. 302-304), where the equilibrium equation is solved by the change of variable \(R = \mathrm{e}^{\xi}\), and BOWER §4.1.4 (PDF p. 225), where the equation is integrated twice, as here. Step 7 is ours.

The proof showed that the guessed displacement field is indeed a solution and that it is the only one. The two constants have a meaning: the term \(C_1 R\) is a uniform stretch in which every small piece is stretched equally in all directions, and the term \(C_2/R^2\) decreases with distance and appears because the shell is hollow. Towards \(O\) it grows beyond all bounds, so a solid ball does not have it, as we shall see soon. Because of it the outer surface moves by less than the inner one.

Let us compute the shell of our problem. For \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 30\) MPa, \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) we get

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{30 \cdot 40^3}{60^3 - 40^3}\ \text{MPa} = 12{,}632\ \text{MPa} , \\ B &= \frac{30 \cdot 40^3 \cdot 60^3}{2\,(60^3 - 40^3)}\ \text{MPa} = 1{,}3642 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{MPa mm}^3 . \end{aligned}\]

The displacement constants are \(C_1 = A/(3K) = 2{,}578 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(C_2 = B/(2\mu) = 9{,}048\ \text{mm}^3\). All numbers of this section were computed with SymPy. The stresses and displacements on the inner surface, in the middle of the wall and on the outer surface are:

\(R\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\) (MPa) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (MPa) \(u\) (\(\mu\)m) \(u/R\)
40 mm \(-30{,}000\) \(33{,}947\) \(6{,}686\) \(1{,}672 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
50 mm \(-9{,}196\) \(23{,}545\) \(4{,}908\) \(0{,}982 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
60 mm \(0\) \(18{,}947\) \(4{,}060\) \(0{,}677 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

The displacements of the table are drawn in figure 11.51 (a): the inner surface moves outward by \(u(a) = 6{,}686\ \mu\)m, the outer one by \(u(b) = 4{,}060\ \mu\)m. The wall therefore gets thinner by \(6{,}686 - 4{,}060 = 2{,}626\ \mu\)m, which is \(1{,}31 \cdot 10^{-4}\) of its thickness \(20\) mm. The radial strain is largest on the inner surface, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RR}}(a) = u'(a) = C_1 - 2C_2/a^3 = -2{,}570 \cdot 10^{-4}\). In the radial direction the material shrinks, although the whole shell expands.

Let us also check that the boundary conditions are indeed satisfied: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(40) = -30\) MPa is the oil pressure and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}(60) = 0\) is the free outer surface. The stresses are shown in figure 11.53. The horizontal axis carries the radius \(R\) in mm, the vertical one the stress in MPa, and the grey horizontal line is zero stress. The vertical lines at \(R = 40\) mm and \(R = 60\) mm are the inner and the outer surface of the shell. Between them we draw the radial stress and the hoop stress of the shell, and for comparison the hoop stress of the tube with the same radii. The dots at the ends of the curves are the values on the two surfaces: \(33{,}947\) MPa and \(18{,}947\) MPa on the hoop stress, \(-30\) MPa and \(0\) on the radial stress, and \(78\) MPa on the curve of the tube.

The figure and the table show two things. First, the radial stress is compressive everywhere, from \(-30\) MPa to \(0\), and the hoop stress is tensile everywhere, even on the outer surface, which is free: \(18{,}947\) MPa. No force acts on that surface, yet the hoop rings are stretched, because the inside of the shell pushes them outward. Second, the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} + 2\,\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 3A = 37{,}89\) MPa is the same everywhere. This is no coincidence: the sum is three times the mean stress, and by step 5 the trace of the strains \(3C_1 = 7{,}73 \cdot 10^{-5}\) is the same in every point. The volume of every small piece therefore increases by the same fraction, although the pieces are stretched differently: closer to the inner surface they stretch more along the circumference (the column \(u/R\) of the table) and therefore shrink more radially, closer to the outer one less. The radial shrinking and the hoop stretching add up to the same total: on the inner surface it is \((-2{,}570 + 2 \cdot 1{,}672) \cdot 10^{-4} = 0{,}773 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The cavity grows by \(4\pi a^2 u(a) = 134{,}4\ \text{mm}^3\).

The load is largest on the inner surface. There the principal stresses are \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 33{,}947\) MPa (twice the same, because \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\) are equivalent) and \(\sigma_3 = -30\) MPa. The von Mises equivalent stress (definition 2.107) is therefore \(\sqrt{\tfrac12\big(0 + 2\,(63{,}947)^2\big)} = 63{,}947\) MPa, that is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{RR}}\). We get the same from the largest shear stress, \(2\tau_{\max} = \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 63{,}947\) MPa (theorem 2.93). With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa the material first starts to yield (von Mises condition) when \(p = 30 \cdot 220/63{,}947 = 103{,}21\) MPa. The stresses are proportional to the pressure, so this computation is exact.

Why is the tube with the same radii worse? At \(p = 30\) MPa the tube reaches \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 78\) MPa on the inner surface (section 11.7). With open ends it starts to yield at \(p = 68{,}35\) MPa, with closed ends at \(70{,}57\) MPa. The shell carries a \(103{,}21/68{,}35 = 1{,}51\) times larger pressure than the open tube and a \(103{,}21/70{,}57 = 1{,}46\) times larger one than the closed tube. We show the reason with equilibrium. We cut the shell by a plane through the centre \(O\) and consider one half (figure 11.54). Let the axis \(z\) point perpendicular to the cut and let the half lie at \(z \ge 0\). The oil pressure on the inner hemisphere has a component in the direction \(z\) equal to \(p\int \vek e_{\mathrm{R}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). The sphere described by the angles \((\varphi, \theta)\) has the surface vector of length \(|\vek N| = a^2\sin\varphi\) (section 4.3.6, equation (4.34)), so the component is \(p\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\varphi\, a^2\sin\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\theta = p\,\pi a^2\). The components perpendicular to the axis cancel after rotation about the axis. This is the pressure on a disc with radius \(a\), as if the hemisphere were flat. This force is resisted by the stress on the cut annulus between the radii \(a\) and \(b\), with area \(\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\). The normal of the cut there is \(\vek e_\varphi\) (at \(\varphi = \pi/2\)), there are no shear stresses, so the stress on the cut is \(\sigma_{\varphi\varphi} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). Equilibrium of the half in the direction \(z\) requires

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R = p\,\pi a^2 , \tag{11.48}\]

so the mean hoop stress over the annulus equals \(p\,a^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 24\) MPa. The same computation for the expression of theorem 11.30 gives \(\int_{40}^{60} \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,2\pi R\,\mathrm{d}R = 150\,796\ \text{N} = 30\ \text{MPa} \cdot \pi \cdot 40^2\ \text{mm}^2\), as it must. Equilibrium of the half therefore confirms the solution without using Hooke's law.

We cut the tube in the same way by a plane through the axis, as in section 11.7.1. Figure 11.55 shows both cuts face on: part (a) is the cut of the shell with radii \(a\) and \(b\), part (b) the cut of the tube. Light blue is the area on which the pressure pushes, light orange is the cut of the wall that carries the force. For the tube the cut surface consists of two strips, each of width \(b - a\) and so with area \((b - a)\) per unit length, and the pressure pushes on the rectangle of width \(2a\) and gives the force \(2pa\) per unit length. The mean hoop stress is therefore \(p\,a/(b - a) = 60\) MPa, which is \((b + a)/a = 2{,}5\) times more than for the shell (\(24 \cdot 2{,}5 = 60\)). The reason lies in the areas: the force of the pressure on half of the shell is \(p\,\pi a^2\) and the area of the cut is \(\pi\,(b - a)(b + a)\), for the tube the force is \(2pa\) and the area of the cut is \(2\,(b - a)\). The quotient is \(a/(a + b) = 0{,}4\). For a thin wall \(a/(a + b) \to \tfrac12\). In a spherical shell two hoop directions share the load and the cut is a closed annulus around the whole cavity, in a tube only one does and the cut is two strips at the sides. So for the same thickness the shell is much less stressed.

The equilibrium of the half shell (11.48) gives the formula for thin spherical shells. The area of the cut is exactly \(\pi\,(b^2 - a^2) = 2\pi\,r_{\mathrm{m}}\,t\) with the mean radius \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = (a + b)/2\) and the thickness \(t = b - a\). If we replace the force of the pressure \(p\,\pi a^2\) by \(p\,\pi r_{\mathrm{m}}^2\), that is the radius \(a\) by the mean one, the hoop stress is \(p\,r_{\mathrm{m}}/(2t)\). For our shell \(r_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\) mm and \(t = 20\) mm, so the formula gives \(30 \cdot 50/40 = 37{,}5\) MPa. The obtained value is \(56\ \%\) larger than the exact mean \(24\) MPa. The reason is a substitution: the mean is \(p\,a^2/\big(t\,(a + b)\big)\), while the formula replaces \(a^2\) by \(r_{\mathrm{m}}^2\), and \((r_{\mathrm{m}}/a)^2 = 1{,}5625\). It agrees better with the largest stress on the inner surface: \(37{,}5\) MPa is \(10{,}5\ \%\) more than \(33{,}947\) MPa, so for the hoop stress the formula is on the safe side. For yielding it is not: on the inner surface there is also the radial stress \(-30\) MPa, which the formula ignores, and the equivalent stress \(63{,}947\) MPa is \(1{,}71\) times larger than \(37{,}5\) MPa. The formula holds exactly only in the limit: \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\,t/(p\,a) \to \tfrac12\) as \(t \to 0\) (SymPy), which is exactly \(p\,a/(2t)\).

Two special cases follow from the theorem (figure 11.56). The first is a solid ball, that is a shell without a cavity (part (a)), such as the one we immersed in oil at pressure \(p = 100\) MPa in chapter 4. For a solid ball steps 1 to 4 of the proof still hold on \(0 < R \le b\) and give \(u = C_1 R + C_2/R^2\), but the term \(C_2/R^2\) tends to infinity as \(R \to 0\) and the field would not be continuously differentiable at the centre. Hence \(C_2 = 0\). What remains is \(u = C_1 R\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{RR}} = 3K\,C_1 = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\), so \(u = -p_{\mathrm{b}} R/(3K)\), that is the displacement field \(\vek u = \varepsilon\,(x, y, z)\) with \(\varepsilon = -p/(3K) = -2{,}041 \cdot 10^{-4}\), which we took there as a solution. The pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) squeezes the ball equally from all sides: the surface before loading moves toward \(O\) onto the orange circle, by \(u(b) = -p_{\mathrm{b}}\,b/(3K)\). For \(p_{\mathrm{b}} = 100\) MPa and \(b = 60\) mm this is \(12{,}2\ \mu\)m toward the centre. Uniqueness says that it is the only one. The same follows from the theorem with \(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\) and \(a \to 0\): the constants tend to \(A \to -p_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(B \to 0\), and the stress is \(-p_{\mathrm{b}}\) in every point.

The second is a small cavity in a ball under outer pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) (\(p_{\mathrm{a}} = 0\)). The hoop stress on its wall is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + B/a^3 = -\tfrac32\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\,b^3/(b^3 - a^3)\). In figure 11.56, part (b), the horizontal axis carries the ratio \(b/a\) and the vertical one the ratio \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)/p_{\mathrm{b}}\), that is how many times the compressive stress on the wall of the cavity exceeds the pressure. The curve falls: the dots are at \(b/a = 1{,}5\), where the stress is \(-2{,}13\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\), at \(b/a = 3\), where it is \(-1{,}56\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\), and at \(b/a = 10\), where it is \(-1{,}50\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\). The curve approaches the dashed line \(\tfrac32\) and not the line \(1\), which holds for a ball without a cavity. When the cavity is small compared with the ball, the compressive hoop stress on its wall is therefore \(1{,}5\) times larger than the pressure, whatever the size of the cavity.

11.8.1 Circular wall, tube and sphere: the same steps three times

We solved the thin ring of section 11.7.4, the tube of theorem 11.28 and the spherical shell of theorem 11.30 with the same seven steps. In the lectures the ring is called the circular wall (krožna stena), and all three problems ask the same: the assumptions, the equations, the concept of the solution. The picture is the same too. For the ring and the tube the section in figure 11.51 (a) is the section at a right angle to the axis: the circles before loading move under pressure into the orange ones, every point along the ray from the centre. On the piece of the wall in part (b) the radial stress acts on the curved faces and the hoop stress on the side faces. The table puts the steps side by side.

The columns differ in two things. The first is the number of hoop directions. A piece of the wall of a ring or a tube is curved in one direction, \(\theta\), a piece of a spherical shell in two, \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\): besides the drawn side faces, the piece in figure 11.51 (b) has one more pair in front and behind. Hence the factor \(2\) in rows 4 and 5 and the different power in row 6. With \(n\) hoop directions the equation is \(u'' + n\,u'/r - n\,u/r^2 = 0\) and its solutions are \(r\) and \(r^{-n}\): for \(u = r^{-n}\) the left-hand side gives \(\big(n(n+1) - n^2 - n\big)\,r^{-n-2} = 0\). For \(n = 1\) this is \(1/r\), for \(n = 2\) it is \(1/R^2\). The second difference is the condition along the axis, which the sphere does not have. For the circular wall the faces \(z = \pm l\) are free, for the tube the stress \(S\) is prescribed on the ends. The radial and the hoop stress are the same in both cases, because the constants \(A\) and \(B\) in equation (11.37) do not depend on \(S\). Only the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) and the displacement depend on \(S\).

The power also decides by how much a hole raises the stress. A small hole in a circular wall under outer pressure \(p_{\mathrm{b}}\) has \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = A + B/a^2 = -2\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\,b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\) on its edge, which tends to \(-2\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\) when the hole is small. A small cavity in a ball gives only \(-1{,}5\,p_{\mathrm{b}}\) (the dashed line \(\tfrac32\) in figure 11.56). Around a cavity the load spreads into two hoop directions, around a hole into one.

11.8.2 What we have shown

We showed how the pressure in the interior of a thick spherical shell distributes the stresses, which are largest on the inner surface, and that the shell carries a \(1{,}51\) times larger pressure than a tube with the same radii and open ends (\(1{,}46\) times larger than a tube with closed ends).
