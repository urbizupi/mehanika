Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.3 A line force on a half-plane: the Flamant solution

In section 25.1 every point of the foundation acted on its own. A real foundation does not act so: where we press it, the surroundings sink too. We answer how first for the simplest load that the contact of a cylinder with a plane really has, a force spread along a long straight line. A cylinder lying on a plane presses along its line of contact, and if the cylinder is much longer than the depth to which the field matters, the field in a cross-section does not change along the line. The question of this section is therefore: what stresses and displacements does a force \(F'\) per unit length of a line produce in an elastic body that it presses on its flat surface? We need the answer in section 25.5, where we build the contact of two cylinders from it.

The body we take is a prism with axis \(z\) whose cross-section is the half-plane (half-plane (polravnina)) \(y \geq 0\): all points of the \(x\), \(y\) plane on one side of the line \(y = 0\). The name says that we cut the plane in half and kept one half. The axis \(y\) points into the body, the surface is the line \(y = 0\). The material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic with \(E\) and \(\nu\), the strains are small, the surface is smooth without friction. The field does not depend on \(z\) and the displacement in the direction \(z\) is zero, so the state is plane strain (definition 11.30) and by corollary 11.34 it is described by the constants \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) of theorem 11.31. The star is the same as in the plane problem of chapter 11. The same number, \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\), will appear again in section 25.5 as the contact modulus of a body against a rigid opponent, but there \(E^*\) describes a pair of bodies. For the steel of the book (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) it is \(E^* = 215{,}38\) GPa and \(\nu^* = 0{,}4286\). Let \(F' = 100\) N/mm, as if a cylinder of length \(10\) mm pressed with a force of \(1000\) N.

A force per unit length has the unit N/m, not N. Since in the plane problem the whole load lies on a line along \(z\), we write it \(F'\) so as not to confuse it with the force \(F\) in a point of section 25.4. A stress is a force per area, N/m\(^2\). The problem is linear, so the stress is proportional to \(F'\), and the factor multiplying \(F'\) has the unit 1/m. This factor depends on the distance \(r\) from the line, on the angle and on \(E\) and \(\nu\). Of these only \(E\) has newtons in its unit, so \(E\) cannot appear in the factor, and the stress must be \(F'/r\) times a function of the angle (and of \(\nu\)). Equilibrium says the same: the length of an arc of radius \(r\) is proportional to \(r\) and its force must be \(F'\) for every \(r\), so the stress on the arc is proportional to \(1/r\).

Let us draw figure 25.9. First the surface, a horizontal line with hatching above it: the body is below. Through its point \(O\) we draw the axis \(y\) down into the body and the axis \(x\) along the surface. In \(O\) the force \(F'\) presses down, so the arrow ends in \(O\), like every push. A point of the body is described by the distance \(r\) and the angle \(\theta\) that the radius makes with the axis \(y\), that is with the line of the force. The body occupies \(-\pi/2 \leq \theta \leq \pi/2\), the surface is at \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\). Around \(O\) we draw a half-disc of radius \(a\), which is part of the body. The rest of the body acts on it with stresses on the arc, and for the equilibrium of the half-disc they must give zero together with the force \(F'\). So we get a condition for a force in a point where the stress is not defined.

Definition 25.11 Line force on a half-plane (linijska sila na polravnini)

Let the body be the half-plane \(y > 0\) of an isotropic linearly elastic material in plane strain, without body force, and let \(F' > 0\) be a number with the unit N/m. A stress field \(\ten\sigma\) with continuous second derivatives on \(\{y > 0\}\) that extends continuously to the surface \(y = 0\) at \(x \neq 0\) is the stress field of the line force \(F'\) in \(O\) if:

  1. it satisfies the equilibrium equations and the compatibility condition of the plane problem (theorem 11.36),
  2. the surface is free outside \(O\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) at \(y = 0\), \(x \neq 0\),
  3. for every \(a > 0\) the force that the part of the body outside the half-disc \(r < a\) exerts on it through the arc \(r = a\) equals \((0, -F')\) per unit length, which is opposite to the force \(F'\) pressing in \(O\).

The second condition says that outside \(O\) the surface is free. The third condition is the equilibrium of the half-disc: if the stress stayed bounded as \(r \to 0\), the force on the arc, which has length \(\pi a\), would go to zero as \(a \to 0\) and could not balance \(F'\). The stress must therefore go beyond every bound in \(O\), which is a measure of the model, not a fault (see below the theorem).

Where do we look for the function? The stress is \(F'/r\) times a function of the angle, and the Airy function is twice an integral of the stress over length, so it has the form \(\varphi = r f(\theta)\). Let us show what such a form gives.

Lemma 25.12 The Airy function \(r f(\theta)\) (Airyjeva funkcija \(r f(\theta)\))

Let \(f\) be a four times continuously differentiable function of the angle on \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\) and \(\varphi = r f(\theta)\) on the half-plane \(y > 0\). Then:

  1. \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (f + f'')/r\), where the stresses are given by (11.59),
  2. \(\nabla^4\varphi = (f'''' + 2f'' + f)/r^3\), so \(\varphi\) is biharmonic exactly when \(f'''' + 2f'' + f = 0\),
  3. all such \(f\) are \(f = C_1\cos\theta + C_2\sin\theta + C_3\,\theta\cos\theta + C_4\,\theta\sin\theta\), and for them \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (2C_4\cos\theta - 2C_3\sin\theta)/r\).
Proof

We must show three claims. We use lemma 11.46 and corollary 11.18 without derivatives in \(z\).

  1. For \(\varphi = r f(\theta)\) we have \(\partial\varphi/\partial r = f\), \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial r^2 = 0\) and \(\partial^2\varphi/\partial\theta^2 = r f''\). By (11.59), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = f/r + r f''/r^2 = (f + f'')/r\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 0\). Next, \(\frac1r\,\partial\varphi/\partial\theta = f'\), which does not depend on \(r\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\partial f'/\partial r = 0\).
  2. By corollary 11.18, \(\nabla^2\varphi = 0 + f/r + f''/r = (f + f'')/r\). For \(g(\theta)/r\) we have \(\partial^2/\partial r^2 = 2g/r^3\), \(\frac1r\,\partial/\partial r = -g/r^3\) and \(\frac{1}{r^2}\,\partial^2/\partial\theta^2 = g''/r^3\), so \(\nabla^2(g/r) = (g + g'')/r^3\). With \(g = f + f''\) we get \(\nabla^4\varphi = (f + 2f'' + f'''')/r^3\).
  3. The equation \(f'''' + 2f'' + f = 0\) has the characteristic polynomial \((s^2 + 1)^2\) with the double roots \(\pm\mathrm{i}\). By theorem 9.39 every solution has the form \((p + q\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} + (\bar p + \bar q\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\) and by corollary 9.40 the real solutions are the linear combinations of \(\cos\theta\), \(\sin\theta\), \(\theta\cos\theta\), \(\theta\sin\theta\). For \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) we have \(f + f'' = 0\). For \(\theta\cos\theta\), \(f'' = -2\sin\theta - \theta\cos\theta\), so \(f + f'' = -2\sin\theta\), for \(\theta\sin\theta\), \(f'' = 2\cos\theta - \theta\sin\theta\) and \(f + f'' = 2\cos\theta\). With claim 1 the expression for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) follows.

Source: SADD (8.4.31)-(8.4.32) (PDF p. 199-200) and BARBER (12.2)-(12.3) (PDF p. 186) take from the general Michell solution the terms with the stress \(1/r\). The proof is ours.

The lemma says something surprising: with this form \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) are zero for every \(f\). The stress has only a radial component, so the solution is called the simple radial distribution (BARBER PDF p. 187). The surface is the line \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), its normal is \(\pm\boldsymbol e_\theta\), and the stress vector on it is \(\pm(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\boldsymbol e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\boldsymbol e_\theta) = \boldsymbol 0\), so the surface is free by itself. What remains is the force: \(C_3\) gives a stress \(\propto \sin\theta\), which is odd in \(\theta\) and would give the horizontal force \(-\pi C_3\) (03-check.py, C3), while condition 3 of the definition requires a vertical force, so \(C_3 = 0\). The terms with \(C_1\), \(C_2\) give \(\varphi = C_1\,r\cos\theta + C_2\,r\sin\theta = C_1 y + C_2 x\), which is linear and has all stresses zero.

Theorem 25.13 The Flamant solution (Flamantova rešitev)

Let the half-plane \(y > 0\) be as in definition 25.11 and \(F' > 0\). The function \(\varphi = -\dfrac{F'}{\pi}\,r\theta\sin\theta\) (\(r\) and \(\theta\) as in figure 25.9) is biharmonic and its stresses are the stress field of the line force \(F'\). More precisely:

  1. \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -\dfrac{2F'\cos\theta}{\pi r}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). In Cartesian components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\dfrac{2F'\,y^3}{\pi\,(x^2 + y^2)^2}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\dfrac{2F'\,x^2 y}{\pi\,(x^2 + y^2)^2}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\dfrac{2F'\,x y^2}{\pi\,(x^2 + y^2)^2}\).
  2. The surface is free outside \(O\), and on every half circle \(r = a\) the resultant of the stresses equals \((0, -F')\).
  3. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is constant, \(-2F'/(\pi d)\), on the circle \(r = d\cos\theta\) that touches the surface in \(O\), with diameter \(d\).
  4. Under the force (\(x = 0\)), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -2F'/(\pi y)\), and on the line at depth \(a\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}(x)/\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}(0) = (1 + x^2/a^2)^{-2}\).
Proof

We must show that \(\varphi\) satisfies lemma 25.12 with a determined \(C_4\) and that the claims follow.

  1. We take \(f = C_4\,\theta\sin\theta\), so \(f'''' + 2f'' + f = 0\) by lemma 25.12, claim 3, and \(\varphi\) is biharmonic. By claim 1 of the lemma \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) and by claim 3 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 2C_4\cos\theta/r\). By theorem 11.36, claims 1 and 3 (without body force, \(V = 0\), \(\varphi\) has continuous derivatives up to the fourth order on \(y > 0\), where \(r > 0\)), the stresses satisfy equilibrium and the compatibility condition. This is the first condition of definition 25.11.
  2. The arc \(r = a\) has the outer normal \(\boldsymbol e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\) in the components \(x\), \(y\) and the force per unit length of arc \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\boldsymbol e_{\mathrm{r}}\), since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). The arc between the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) has length \(a(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\), so the resultant over the arc is an integral over the angle with the factor \(a\), \(\big(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\sin\theta\,a\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\ \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\cos\theta\,a\,\mathrm{d}\theta\big)\). With \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 2C_4\cos\theta/r\) the first component is zero, since the integrand is odd, and the second is \(2C_4\int\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \pi C_4\). The requirement \(\pi C_4 = -F'\) gives \(C_4 = -F'/\pi\), and with it \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -2F'\cos\theta/(\pi r)\). This is the first part of claim 1 and the third condition of the definition.
  3. The surface at \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\): in Cartesian components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\sin\theta\cos\theta\) (transformation of stress, theorem 2.42, since \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) are zero). At \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) has the factor \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\cos^2\theta = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are zero at \(r > 0\). This is the second condition of the definition and the first part of claim 2.
  4. Since \(x = r\sin\theta\) and \(y = r\cos\theta\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -2F'\cos^3\theta/(\pi r) = -2F'y^3/(\pi r^4)\) and likewise \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\sin^2\theta\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). This is the rest of claim 1 (checked in SymPy, num-03.py, S4).
  5. On the circle \(r = d\cos\theta\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -2F'\cos\theta/(\pi d\cos\theta) = -2F'/(\pi d)\), claim 3. At \(x = 0\), \(r = y\) and \(\theta = 0\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -2F'/(\pi y)\). At \(y = a\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}(x) = -\dfrac{2F'}{\pi a}\,\dfrac{1}{(1 + x^2/a^2)^2}\), claim 4.

Source: SADD §8.4.7, (8.4.34)-(8.4.36) (PDF p. 199-201): the same stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}} = -2Y\sin\theta/(\pi r)\) with the angle measured from the surface (crop 8.4.35 read), and "lines of constant radial stress are circles tangent to the surface" (PDF p. 201). BARBER §12.2-12.3, (12.2)-(12.7), (12.11) (PDF p. 186-188). Neither source discusses uniqueness on the pages cited, see the text below the theorem.

The proof showed that the stress of the theorem satisfies all three conditions of the definition. What does it say? Figure 25.10 shows two panels.

In the left panel there is the surface with the force \(F'\) in \(O\). Through the point \(O\) we draw three circles, all touching the surface in \(O\), with a vertical diameter \(d = 1\), \(2\), \(4\) mm. By claim 3 the stress on each is constant, and the values are \(63{,}66\), \(31{,}83\) and \(15{,}92\) MPa in compression: half for a twice larger diameter. This is the same law \(\sigma \propto 1/r\) as in the motivation. The right panel shows the profile of the stress on the line at depth \(a\): it is largest under the force, at \(x = a\) it is \(\tfrac14\), at \(x = 2a\) only \(0{,}04\) and at \(x = 5a\) only \(0{,}0015\) of the largest value (SymPy and NumPy, num-03.py, N7). The vertical stress on this line is thus gathered under the force, in a strip a few \(a\) wide. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) itself, however, falls only like \(1/r\) (claim 1): at the point \(x = 5a\), \(y = a\) it is still \(1/26 = 0{,}038\) of the value under the force at depth \(a\), only there it points almost horizontally, so its vertical part is small (03-check.py, C5).

Under the force. The compressive stress \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}| = 2F'/(\pi y)\) is \(63{,}7\) MPa at \(y = 1\) mm, \(636{,}6\) MPa at \(0{,}1\) mm and \(6366\) MPa at \(0{,}01\) mm (num-03.py, N2). It goes beyond every bound as \(y \to 0\). This is not a fault of the solution but a measure of the model: a force on a line means a force on zero area. The linear theory assumes that all derivatives of the displacements are below about \(0{,}001\) in size (section 3.2.1, assumption 4). Under the force \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are zero, so by Hooke's law of plane strain (theorem 11.31) the derivative is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/E^*\). The bound \(0{,}001\) at \(E^* = 215{,}4\) GPa thus means a stress of at most \(215{,}4\) MPa, and at \(F' = 100\) N/mm this holds for \(y \geq 2F'/(\pi\cdot 215{,}4\ \mathrm{MPa}) = 0{,}30\) mm. Closer than that the solution does not hold. What a real contact does we see in section 25.5: the force spreads over a strip of finite width.

Is the solution the only one? We proved that the field is a solution, not that it is the only one. By definition 25.11 it is not the only one either. Take the uniform stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = s\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) with any number \(s\). Its Airy function \(s\,y^2/2\) is biharmonic, so it satisfies equilibrium and compatibility. It leaves the surface free, since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are zero. On the half-disc it gives the force \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} s\sin\theta\,a\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\) in the direction \(x\) and zero in the direction \(y\) (03-check.py, C2). Added to Flamant's, it gives another field that satisfies all three conditions of the definition. It is the stress of a horizontal tension or compression at infinity, not a consequence of the force in \(O\). The uniqueness theorem of the boundary value problem (theorem 11.4) holds for bounded bodies and regular data, and a half-plane with a point force is neither, so we cannot use it here, and we do not prove uniqueness under extra conditions at infinity. We proved only this much: among the functions of the form \(r f(\theta)\) only this one gives the stress field of a line force (lemma 25.12 and the paragraph after it).

The displacements follow from the stresses through Hooke's law and the relation between displacements and strains.

Lemma 25.14 Displacements of the Flamant solution (pomiki pri Flamantovi rešitvi)

Let the stress field be that of theorem 25.13 and \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) as above. The displacement field in local components

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{r}} &= -\frac{F'}{\pi E^*}\Big(2\cos\theta\,\ln r + (1 - \nu^*)\,\theta\sin\theta\Big) , \\ u_\theta &= \frac{F'}{\pi E^*}\Big(2\sin\theta\,\ln r - (1 - \nu^*)\,\theta\cos\theta + (1 + \nu^*)\sin\theta\Big) \end{aligned} \tag{25.10}\]

has strains that by Hooke's law of plane strain give the stresses of theorem 25.13. Every other displacement field with the same strains differs from it by a rigid displacement \((A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta,\ -A\sin\theta + B\cos\theta + W r)\) with constants \(A\), \(B\), \(W\). The field (25.10) has \(u_\theta = 0\) on the line of the force \(\theta = 0\). On the surface (\(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), \(r = |x|\)) it has:

  1. the horizontal displacement of size \(\dfrac{(1 - \nu^*)F'}{2E^*}\) toward \(O\), \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = -\dfrac{(1 - \nu^*)F'}{2E^*}\), equal at all \(r\),
  2. the vertical displacement (into the body) \(w(x) = -\dfrac{F'}{\pi E^*}\big(2\ln|x| + 1 + \nu^*\big)\),
  3. the difference of displacements \(w(x_1) - w(x_2) = \dfrac{2F'}{\pi E^*}\ln\dfrac{x_2}{x_1}\) does not depend on the rigid displacement.
Proof

We must show that (25.10) gives the right strains, that it is the only form, and claims 1 to 3. We get the strains in cylindrical coordinates from corollary 11.21 and the stresses from corollary 11.26.

  1. By theorem 11.31 plane strain has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu^*\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E^*\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu^*\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E^*\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = (1 + \nu^*)\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/E^*\). With \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) we get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}/E^* = -2F'\cos\theta/(\pi E^* r)\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = -\nu^*\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}/E^* = 2\nu^* F'\cos\theta/(\pi E^* r)\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\).
  2. From \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}}\) it follows that \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = -\dfrac{2F'\cos\theta}{\pi E^*}\ln r + f(\theta)\) with a so far unknown function \(f\). From \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (u_{\mathrm{r}} + \partial u_\theta/\partial\theta)/r\) it follows that \(\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta = 2\nu^*F'\cos\theta/(\pi E^*) - u_{\mathrm{r}}\). With an integral over \(\theta\), where \(\Phi(\theta)\) is a primitive function of \(f\), \(u_\theta = \dfrac{2F'\sin\theta}{\pi E^*}\big(\ln r + \nu^*\big) - \Phi(\theta) + g(r)\) with an unknown function \(g\).
  3. We multiply the condition \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) by \(2r\) using corollary 11.21 and insert: \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta + r\,\partial u_\theta/\partial r - u_\theta = 0\). The terms with \(\ln r\) cancel and what remains is \(f' + \Phi + c\sin\theta + (r g' - g) = 0\) with \(c = 2(1 - \nu^*)F'/(\pi E^*)\). The first three terms depend only on \(\theta\), the last only on \(r\), so each is constant: \(r g' - g = -K\), \(g = K + W r\), and \(f' + \Phi + c\sin\theta = K\).
  4. The derivative of the last equation with respect to \(\theta\) gives \(f'' + f = -c\cos\theta\). The function \(-\tfrac{c}{2}\,\theta\sin\theta\) solves it, since \((\theta\sin\theta)'' + \theta\sin\theta = 2\cos\theta\). The difference of two solutions solves \(h'' + h = 0\), so by theorem 9.39 it is \(A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta\). Hence \(f = -\tfrac c2\,\theta\sin\theta + A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta\), and from \(f' + \Phi + c\sin\theta = K\) follows \(\Phi\), which agrees with \(f\) (\(\Phi' = f\) is checked by differentiation). Inserted in step 2, \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\) are as (25.10), only with the terms \(A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta\) in \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(-A\sin\theta + B\cos\theta + Wr\) in \(u_\theta\). These terms are a rigid displacement: a shift \((B, A)\) in the directions \(x\), \(y\) and a rotation \(W\) (their strains are zero, SymPy, S9). For (25.10), \(A = B = W = 0\) and \(K\) is already included. Since \(\sin 0 = 0\), \(u_\theta = 0\) at \(\theta = 0\).
  5. On the surface \(\cos\theta = 0\), \(\sin\theta = \pm1\), \(\theta\sin\theta = \pi/2\) and \(\theta\cos\theta = 0\). Hence \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = -\dfrac{(1 - \nu^*)F'}{2E^*}\) on both sides. The basis vector \(\boldsymbol e_{\mathrm{r}}\) points along the surface away from \(O\), so the displacement is toward \(O\), and nothing depends on \(r\). This is claim 1.
  6. At \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\), \(\boldsymbol e_\theta = (0, \mp1)\) in the components \(x\), \(y\) and \(u_\theta = \pm\dfrac{F'}{\pi E^*}(2\ln r + 1 + \nu^*)\), so the vertical displacement is \(-\dfrac{F'}{\pi E^*}(2\ln r + 1 + \nu^*)\) on both sides. This is claim 2, its difference is claim 3, since the rigid displacement cancels in the difference.

Source: SADD (8.4.37)-(8.4.43) (PDF p. 201-203), crop (8.4.43) read: the same expressions with the angle measured from the surface and for plane stress (\(E\), \(\nu\) in place of \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) by corollary 11.34). SADD (8.4.44), (8.4.45) (PDF p. 203): all points of the surface move horizontally the same amount toward the force, "an unexpected result". BARBER (12.12), (12.13) (PDF p. 188) the same expression in other notation. We checked the result in SymPy (num-03.py, S6 to S9).

The proof showed that the displacement field is unique up to a rigid displacement and has a simple form on the surface. Figure 25.11 shows it.

In the left panel there is the surface with the force \(F'\), and below it a grid of points, in each a displacement is drawn (\(0{,}28\) cm per unit). Near the force the displacements point down, and along the surface all are equal horizontally and point toward the force, in agreement with claim 1. The points at \(|x| = 2{,}5\) cm, however, rise. This is not a property of the body but of the choice of the rigid displacement, for which the vertical displacement of the surface is zero at \(|x| = 1{,}96\) cm (03-check.py, C6). The right panel shows the surface after deformation: as \(x \to 0\) the sinking goes beyond every bound, like \(\ln(1/|x|)\). The solid line is \(w\) for the choice of the rigid displacement that gives \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -(1 + \nu^*)F'/(\pi E^*)\) at \(|x| = 4\) cm, the dashed one at \(|x| = 40\) cm. They are the same curve, shifted by \(2\ln 10 = 4{,}605\) units of \(F'/(\pi E^*)\), which is \(0{,}68\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) at \(F' = 100\) N/mm.

The sinking is determined only up to a constant. At \(F' = 100\) N/mm, \(2F'/(\pi E^*) = 0{,}2956\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) (num-03.py, N5). The difference of sinking between \(x_1 = 1\) mm and \(x_2 = 10\), \(100\), \(1000\) mm is \(0{,}681\), \(1{,}361\) and \(2{,}042\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) (N6): each tenfold distance adds the same. As \(x_2 \to \infty\) the difference goes beyond every bound, so the displacement \(w\) is never determined at a point but always only relative to a chosen reference point, and this choice is a decision of the model, not a result. The same cause lies behind the stress \(\propto 1/r\): a displacement is an integral of the strain, and \(\int\frac{1}{r}\,\mathrm{d}r = \ln r\) does not converge at infinity (by SADD PDF p. 203 this follows from the radial stress being \(O(1/r)\) and is a feature of the plane model, the three-dimensional solution of section 25.4 has no logarithm). Some authors remove the logarithm with the condition that the point on the load line at a chosen depth \(r_0\) does not move, in our notation \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(r_0, 0) = 0\) (SADD PDF p. 204 writes \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(r_0, \pi/2) = 0\), since it measures the angle from the surface). Then \(\ln(r/r_0)\) stands everywhere in (25.10) in place of \(\ln r\), which is exactly this choice.

Consequence for contact: we do not know the approach. When in section 25.5 two cylinders are pressed together, we would like to know by how much their centres approach. Each cylinder at a small contact is a half-plane, and its sinking at the contact is by this section determined up to a constant that depends on the size of the body. The approach (approach (pogrezanje)) \(\delta\) of cylinders therefore does not follow from the plane model. No choice repairs it: this is why for rollers the law \(Q \propto \delta^{10/9}\) of section 25.7 is fitted empirically, not derived (HK, PDF p. 156). For a three-dimensional contact the stiffness is well defined, for a two-dimensional one it is not (BOWER, PDF p. 330).

The line is not infinitely long. For a cylinder of length \(\ell\) the line of contact is not infinite, so the plane model holds only if the depth is small against \(\ell\). We can compute the quantity once we know the field of a point force, that is in section 25.4 (theorem 25.15). We state the result in advance. If we spread the force \(F'\ell = F\) over a line of length \(\ell\) and add the fields of the point forces, the stress under the centre at depth \(y\) equals the Flamant stress times \(\dfrac{1 + 6t^2}{(1 + 4t^2)^{3/2}}\), \(t = y/\ell\) (num-03.py, M2, the integral checked by differentiation and numerically). The expansion is \(1 - 6t^4\), so the difference is below \(1\,\%\) for \(t \leq 0{,}2\), and at \(t = 1\) the stress is only \(0{,}626\) of the Flamant one, at \(t = 2\) only \(0{,}357\) (figure 25.12).

Let us draw the strip of length \(\ell\) on the surface, over which the force \(F\) is spread. Under its centre we take the depth \(y\). The blue region reaches to \(0{,}2\,\ell\), and in it the plane solution holds to \(1\,\%\). On the right is the curve of the ratio: at \(y/\ell = 0{,}2\) it is \(0{,}993\) and at \(1{,}0\) it is \(0{,}626\). For a cylinder with \(\ell = 10\) mm the plane model is thus good to a depth of \(2\) mm. HK calls this edge loading (HK PDF p. 145-148): at the ends of the cylinder the pressure is larger than in the middle, which is why the cylinders are crowned, a matter we do not treat here (HK, PDF p. 147-148).

The plane solution is thus accurate only in the band of depths smaller than the length of the line. In it the stress and the displacement are fully determined by \(F'\), \(E^*\) and \(\nu^*\).
