Chapter 22 Shells

22.9 Flat shell finite elements

In section 22.8 we wrote a shell of revolution under axisymmetric load as a chain of elements along the meridian. A shell of general shape, such as a car body, a vessel with nozzles or a folded roof, has no such chain. How do we build its model from what we already have? We replace the middle surface (definition 22.1) by flat pieces, as a circular arc is replaced by straight segments, and we treat each flat piece with two elements that chapters 15 and 18 already provide: a plane element for the force in the plane of the piece and a plate element for bending. No new theory is needed for this. What is new is only the geometry of assembly, and two difficulties hide in it: the force in the plane of one piece loads the next one transversally, and a node in which all pieces lie in the same plane is singular. We prove both.

We start with the tube of the pressure vessel example: mean radius \(R = 50\) mm, thickness \(h = 2\) mm, oil pressure \(p = 3\) MPa. We cut the tube across its axis, as figure 22.84 shows.

The cross-section of the tube is a circle of radius \(R\). We replace it by \(n = 8\) flat facets whose vertices lie on the circle, each a flat strip of width \(b = 2R\sin(\pi/n) = 38{,}268\) mm. Two neighbours meet at a fold \(J\). A fold (pregib) is a line along which two flat surfaces meet at an angle, as a sheet of paper is folded along a line. In the figure we look at the cross-section, so the fold is one point, in the model it is a straight line along the tube.

What does a facet carry? In its own plane the hoop force \(N\) per unit length of the tube in N/mm (definition 22.27), equal over the whole facet. At the fold \(J\) the two facets pull with a force \(N\) each in its own plane, and both pulls point toward the inside of the polygon, because in the direction of the radius through \(J\) the neighbouring vertex is only \(R\cos\alpha < R\) from the axis, so in that direction it is nearer to the axis than \(J\). The radial line load \(P\) on the fold comes from the pressure: the pressure on a facet has the resultant \(p\,b\) perpendicular to the facet at its middle, each half of the facet passes it on to its own fold, and the two halves give at the fold the radial component \(p\,b\cos(\pi/n)\) (the angle between the normal of the facet and the radius is \(\pi/n\)). The directions of the facets differ at \(J\) by the angle \(\alpha\), the exterior angle of the polygon, \(\alpha = 2\pi/n\). The law we know is the equilibrium of forces on a piece of the fold: the sum of the three forces must be zero, which means that the force triangle closes.

What happens to one facet, to a fold close up and to the whole cross-section, we will look at in figure 22.85.

Lemma 22.55 Tube of flat facets under line loads (cev iz ravnih ploskev pod linijskimi silami)

A tube with \(n \ge 3\) flat facets whose vertices in the cross-section lie on a circle of radius \(R\) has thickness \(h\) and elastic constants \(E\), \(\nu\). The facets have the same width \(b = 2R\sin(\pi/n)\), are rigidly joined at the folds and loaded at each fold by a radial line load \(P\) outward per unit length of the tube, with no other forces. The ends of the tube are free, and we consider states that do not vary along the tube (the load is constant along it). Then in every facet the hoop force is

\[N = \frac{P}{2\sin(\pi/n)}\]

(tension, N/mm), all other internal forces of the facet (axial, shear, bending moment, transverse force) are zero, and every vertex moves radially by \(R\,N/(E\,h)\).

Proof

We have to show that the state with the hoop force \(N\) in every facet satisfies equilibrium of the fold and of the facet, Hooke's law and compatibility of the displacements, and that it is the only one.

  1. Equilibrium of the fold. We take a piece of the fold of length 1 mm. Let \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) point from the axis toward \(J\). The facets have the directions \(\vek d_{1,2} = (-\sin(\pi/n),\ \pm\cos(\pi/n))\) with respect to (\(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), tangent), since the directions of the facets differ by \(\alpha = 2\pi/n\) and are symmetric with respect to the radius. The radial component of each force \(N\) is \(-N\sin(\pi/n)\), the tangential ones cancel. Equilibrium of forces on the piece in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the piece is at rest, the sum of forces is zero) gives \(P - 2N\sin(\pi/n) = 0\), which is the closed force triangle.
  2. Equilibrium of the facet. In this state the facet is a strip loaded at its two folds by equal opposite forces \(N\) in its plane and by nothing else. The two forces lie on one line, so the sum of forces and of moments about every point is zero. Every part of the strip, cut off parallel to the fold, is in equilibrium if its section carries the hoop force \(N\) and no other internal force, which is the state of the lemma.
  3. Compatibility. Since the axial force is zero and the ends are free, the hoop force \(N\) causes by Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) the hoop strain \(\varepsilon = N/(Eh)\) and the axial strain \(-\nu\varepsilon\), constant along the tube. Each side lengthens by the factor \(1 + \varepsilon\), the polygon stays similar to its initial shape, so the angles between the facets do not change, the facets stay flat and need not be bent. A vertex that was at distance \(R\) from the axis \(O\) is at distance \(R(1 + \varepsilon)\), which is the radial displacement \(R\varepsilon = R\,N/(Eh)\).
  4. Uniqueness. Since the state does not vary along the tube, we take a piece of the tube of length 1 mm as a plane frame (definition 5.93) of \(n\) elements, strips with constant axial and bending stiffness, loaded only by the forces \(P\) at the nodes. The forces \(P\) are radial, so their moment about the axis is zero, and their sum is zero, since they are equal and evenly spread around the axis. We add to the frame a pin support at node \(J\) and at the neighbouring node a support that prevents the displacement perpendicular to the facet between them. The only rigid motion the two supports allow is zero. Take any solution of the unsupported piece and subtract the rigid motion for which the displacements at the supports are zero. The internal forces do not change and the reactions are zero, so this is a solution of the supported frame, and so is the state of steps 1 to 3. By lemma 12.19 (point 2) the two are equal, so the internal forces of every solution are as the lemma states.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The idea of representing a shell by flat facets joined at folds is in ONATE2 §8.1 (PDF pp. 469-470), where folded plates are the model of a smooth shell.

The lemma assumes rigid folds. In the state of the lemma the facets carry no moment (step 2), so neither does the fold, and the same forces \(N\) would be obtained with hinges at the folds. We need the stiffness of the folds only for uniqueness (step 4). A polygon of \(n\) bars joined by hinges has \(2n\) vertex coordinates in the plane, \(n\) prescribed side lengths and three rigid motions, so \(2n - n - 3 = n - 3\) free ways of motion, five for \(n = 8\). Such a polygon would collapse under an unsymmetric load, only the triangle (\(n = 3\)) would stay rigid.

The table gives the numbers for our tube. The force \(N = P/(2\sin(\pi/n))\) with \(P = p\,b\cos(\pi/n)\) is \(N = pR\cos(\pi/n)\), that is \(p\) times the distance of the facet from the axis, which for \(n \to \infty\) is the familiar \(pR = 150\) N/mm and the hoop stress \(N/h = 75\) MPa. The radial displacement of the vertex is for \(n = 8\) equal to \(17{,}68\) \(\mu\)m, for \(n = 32\) \(19{,}041\) \(\mu\)m and for \(n \to \infty\) \(pR^2/(Eh) = 19{,}133\) \(\mu\)m (\(E = 196\) GPa). Here the ends are free and there is no axial force in the wall, so the displacement is larger than the \(16{,}263\) \(\mu\)m of the closed vessel of lemma 22.36, where by Hooke's law the axial force \(pR/2\) reduces the hoop strain to \((1 - \nu/2)\) times its value.

How far a flat facet is from the true wall, figure 22.86 shows.

The column \(s = R(1 - \cos(\pi/n))\) is the sagitta of the arc, the distance of the middle of the facet between the folds from the true middle surface, in units of the thickness \(h\). The flat model is good if the sagitta is small against the thickness of the shell, since otherwise the facet is no longer near the middle surface: for \(s > h/2\) the middle of the facet even lies outside the true wall. For \(n = 8\), which the figure shows to scale, the sagitta is almost two thicknesses (\(1{,}903\,h\)) and the middle of the facet lies \(3{,}806 - 1 = 2{,}806\) mm nearer to the axis than the inner surface of the wall, for \(n = 16\) it is almost half the thickness (\(0{,}480\,h\)), and for \(n = 32\) it is \(0{,}12\,h\). If we choose the bound \(s \le 0{,}1\,h\), a tenth of the thickness, it requires \(n \ge 36\) (for \(n = 35\), \(s = 0{,}1006\,h\)), which is \(\alpha = 10^\circ\) between neighbouring facets.

The lemma shows what assembly means. Let us look at the fold \(J\) close up, where facets 1 and 2 meet. The force \(N\) of facet 1 lies in its plane. We resolve it into the component along facet 2, \(N\cos\alpha\), and the component perpendicular to facet 2, \(N\sin\alpha\) (for \(n = 8\) this is \(138{,}58\sin 45^\circ = 97{,}99\) N/mm). The angle \(\alpha\) between the force and the extension of facet 2 is the exterior angle of the polygon. Facet 2 as a membrane cannot carry the perpendicular component, since its force lies only in its plane, so at the fold it is balanced by the line load \(P\). For elements this means: the in-plane unknown of one element enters the transverse unknown of another if we write them in the same axes, and the coupling of the in-plane and bending stiffness appears only in assembly (ONATE2 §8.5, PDF p. 487). So we must write each element in its own axes and turn it into the global ones.

The lemma holds only for line loads on the folds. The pressure directly on the interior of a facet is a transverse load that a flat strip (membrane) cannot carry, since the plane element has no transverse unknown. The facet carries it by bending, so with pressure on the facets the state is no longer free of bending. In the elements the pressure is replaced by nodal forces (ONATE2 equation 8.35b, PDF pp. 485-486), but this does not remove the bending of the facet.

How much bending does the pressure bring? Every fold lies on a plane of symmetry of the tube that passes through the axis and the fold, and all facets are loaded equally. If a fold turned, it would turn to one side, but symmetry prefers no side, so the fold does not turn. The facet is therefore a long strip, clamped at both folds, under the constant pressure \(p\). By corollary 18.26 (point 2) the moment at the fold is \(M = p\,b^2/12\), and by equation (18.10) the bending stress at the surface is

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{b}} = \frac{6\,M}{h^2} = \frac{p\,b^2}{2\,h^2} .\]

For \(n = 8\) we have \(M = 366{,}1\) N mm/mm and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{b}} = 549\) MPa, eight times the hoop stress \(N/h = 69{,}3\) MPa. For \(n = 32\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{b}} = 36{,}0\) MPa, almost half the hoop stress \(74{,}6\) MPa, and for \(n = 64\) it is \(9{,}0\) MPa. A round tube under internal pressure has no such bending (lemma 22.36), so this is an error of the model of flat facets. From \(b = 2R\sin(\pi/n)\) and \(s = R(1 - \cos(\pi/n))\) follows \(b^2 = 8\,R\,s\cos^2(\pi/(2n))\), and the ratio to the hoop stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}/(N/h) \approx 4\,s/h\). The sagitta from the table is thus also a measure of the error of the stress: at \(s = 0{,}1\,h\) the bending stress at the fold is still about \(40\) % of the hoop stress.

22.9.1 The element and its unknowns

Now we build the element. Take a triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) in global axes, for instance \(\vek x_1 = (0, 0, 0)\), \(\vek x_2 = (60, 0, 0)\), \(\vek x_3 = (0, 40, 30)\) mm. We build it in figure 22.87.

On the left we draw the nodes \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) and the sides \(\vek x_{21} = \vek x_2 - \vek x_1\) and \(\vek x_{31} = \vek x_3 - \vek x_1\), which we take as the two vectors that span the plane of the element. Together they determine the parallelogram, which we complete in grey dashed, the triangle is its half. The local axes \(x'\), \(y'\), \(z'\) we draw separately, because only the directions count: \(x'\) along \(\vek x_{21}\), \(z'\) perpendicular to the plane and \(y'\) in the plane, so that the axes are right-handed (lemma 22.57). On the right we look at the same triangle in its plane, in which it measures \(60 \times 50\) mm. Every node has six local unknowns, as in the space frame element (definition 12.21), but it does not use them all in the same way. The membrane unknowns \(u'\) and \(v'\) are displacements in the plane, carried by the plane element. The bending unknowns \(w'\) (the displacement perpendicular to the plane, the symbol is a circle with a dot, meaning an arrow toward the viewer), \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\) are carried by the plate element. The last one, the drilling rotation (vrtenje okoli normale) \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'}\), called drilling after a drill turning about its own axis, is a rotation in the plane of the element that neither of the two elements contains. So we draw it dashed.

We connect the rotations \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\) to the displacement similarly as for the beam (equation (12.19)). How the normal turns in bending, the left part of figure 22.88 shows.

On the left we look at a flat strip along the axis \(y'\) pointing into the paper (a circle with a cross), the axis \(z'\) points up. We bend the strip by the displacement \(w'(x')\) and draw the bent strip in orange. At the point \(P\) the normal was vertical before bending (grey dashed), after bending it is perpendicular to the strip (orange). When we move along the strip by \(1\), the strip rises by the slope \(w'_{,\mathrm{x}'}\), and the normal, which stays perpendicular to the strip, tilts toward smaller \(x'\) by the angle with this tangent. In space the normal \(z'\) of the plane after the displacement \(w'(x', y')\) turns, to first order, into \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} - w'_{,\mathrm{x}'}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'} - w'_{,\mathrm{y}'}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\). The exact normal is this vector divided by its length \(\sqrt{1 + w'^2_{,\mathrm{x}'} + w'^2_{,\mathrm{y}'}}\). The first approximation omits the division, because at the slope \(10^{-3}\) the length is \(1 + 5\cdot 10^{-7}\): the error is proportional to the square of the slope and the linear theory neglects it like all terms of second order. At the slope \(0{,}1\) it would already be \(0{,}5\) %, and such a bending would need a nonlinear theory. The rotation vector \(\vek\varphi\) turns the normal by \(\vek\varphi\times\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = (\varphi_{\mathrm{y}'}, -\varphi_{\mathrm{x}'}, 0)\), so

\[\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = \frac{\partial w'}{\partial y'} , \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = -\,\frac{\partial w'}{\partial x'} . \tag{22.56}\]

We see the minus at \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\) in the figure. The positive \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\) is a rotation about the axis \(y'\) pointing into the paper, that is clockwise, while the normal at a positive slope tilts in the opposite direction. The signs are the same as for the beam (\(\varphi_{\mathrm{y}} = -w'\)), but ONATE2 uses different sign rules for local and global rotations (PDF p. 489). We write all rotations as components of the rotation vector.

Definition 22.56 Flat shell element (ploščati element lupine)

A flat shell element (ploščati element lupine) is a flat triangle or quadrilateral with \(m\) nodes (\(m = 3\) or \(4\)) in one plane \(\Pi\), thickness \(h\) and elastic constants \(E\), \(\nu\), together with right-handed local axes \(x'\), \(y'\) (\(y'\) in the plane \(\Pi\)) and \(z'\) (perpendicular to \(\Pi\)) and with a column of \(6m\) real numbers, the local unknowns,

\[\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = (u'_{\mathrm{i}},\ v'_{\mathrm{i}},\ w'_{\mathrm{i}},\ \theta_{\mathrm{x}'\mathrm{i}},\ \theta_{\mathrm{y}'\mathrm{i}},\ \theta_{\mathrm{z}'\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{i} = 1, \dots, \mathrm{m}}^\T ,\]

that is the displacement and the rotation vector of each node in the local axes. We divide the unknowns into a membrane part \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{m}} = (u'_{\mathrm{i}}, v'_{\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) (\(2m\) numbers) and a bending part \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{b}} = (w'_{\mathrm{i}}, \theta_{\mathrm{x}'\mathrm{i}}, \theta_{\mathrm{y}'\mathrm{i}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) (\(3m\) numbers). The energy of the element is the sum

\[U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{m}}'^\T\,\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\,\vek d'_{\mathrm{m}} + \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}'^\T\,\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\,\vek d'_{\mathrm{b}} , \tag{22.57}\]

where \(\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the stiffness matrix of a plane element (chapter 15) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) the stiffness matrix of a plate element (chapter 18), both symmetric. So the matrix of the element \(\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\) is of order \(6m\) with energy \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the following properties:

  1. The unknowns \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{b}}\) are not coupled to each other, and \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'\mathrm{i}}\) does not appear in the energy, so \(\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\) has a zero row and a zero column at every \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. Rigid motions (R), a requirement on the chosen \(\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) that does not follow from equation (22.57). The term \(\vek d_{\mathrm{m}}'^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{m}}\) is zero exactly for a rigid motion of the plane, the term \(\vek d_{\mathrm{b}}'^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{b}}\) exactly for \(w' = a_0 + a_1\,x' + a_2\,y'\) with the rotations \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = a_2\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = -a_1\) (equation (22.56)), at the nodal values \(w'_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the rotations. The matrix \(\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive semidefinite, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\).

Source: ONATE2 §8.4 (PDF pp. 482-487, equations 8.34-8.38: the local matrix is the sum of the matrix of the plane element and of the plate element if the membrane-bending coupling matrix is zero, \(\hat D'_{\mathrm{mb}} = 0\)), COOK §16.4 (PDF pp. 588-589, equation 16.4-1: the division of the unknowns into membrane and bending ones, matrix \([k_{\mathrm{m}}]\) of a plane element and \([k_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of any good plate element). Property (R) is the usual rigid-body test, which we require of every element.

The symbol \(\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\) here is a stiffness matrix, not the bulk modulus of theorem 11.30 and not the Gaussian curvature, and the thickness \(h\) is the thickness of the shell as in section 22.3. Why are the plane and plate parts independent (property 1)? By theorem 22.29 the energy is the sum of a stretching part, proportional to \(h\), and a bending part, proportional to \(h^3\). The normal displacement \(w'\) enters the stretching only through the curvature of the middle surface (section 22.3), and for a flat surface this is zero.

Why only through the curvature, figure 22.88 shows. In the middle there is an arc of radius \(R\), which we push outward by \(w\). The angle that the arc subtends stays the same, and the radius grows to \(R + w\). The length of the arc is the radius times the angle, so the pushed arc is longer by the factor \((R + w)/R\), its strain is \(w/R\). For our tube \(w = 19{,}133\) \(\mu\)m and \(R = 50\) mm, so \(w/R = 3{,}83\cdot 10^{-4}\), which is exactly the hoop strain \(pR/(Eh)\). On the right is a flat strip, which we push by the same \(w\). The strip only moves and its length stays the same. A nonuniform \(w'\) lengthens it only by a term of second order in the slope, at the slope \(10^{-3}\) by \(5\cdot 10^{-7}\), which the linear theory neglects. The stretching of a flat surface therefore contains only displacements in the plane, the bending only \(w'\). In a curved shell curvature would link them, and exactly this we lose in a flat element, so the folds between the elements take over the role of curvature. In figure 22.85 the vertices move outward and the sides lengthen, as the arc lengthens. This is the same phenomenon as the force \(N\sin\alpha\) that the neighbouring facet receives transversally, once seen from displacements, once from forces.

Independence has one more condition, a material symmetric about the middle surface (figure 22.89).

ONATE2 (equation 8.20, PDF p. 480) writes the membrane-bending coupling matrix as a sum over layers of the thickness, in which every layer has the weight \(z_{\mathrm{i}+1}^2 - z_{\mathrm{i}}^2\), the difference of the squares of the distances of its upper and lower limit from the middle surface. For one material through the whole thickness the limits \(h/2\) and \(-h/2\) are equally far from the middle surface, the sum of weights is \((h/2)^2 - (-h/2)^2 = 0\), and the coupling term in the matrix of the element vanishes (ONATE2 PDF p. 486). For two layers of different materials the lower weight is \(0 - h^2/4\) and the upper \(h^2/4 - 0\), and since we multiply them by different stiffnesses, they do not cancel. If we pull such a strip along the middle surface, the softer upper layer stretches more than the lower, and the strip bends. For \(E_1 = 2E_2\) the strain of the top surface is three times that of the bottom one. A force in the plane here causes bending and property 1 does not hold.

22.9.2 Local axes

How do we get the axes \(x'\), \(y'\), \(z'\) from the coordinates of the nodes? The sides \(\vek x_{21}\) and \(\vek x_{31}\) from node 1 span the plane, and their normal is the cross product.

Lemma 22.57 Local axes of a triangle (lokalne osi trikotnika)

Let the nodes \(\vek x_1\), \(\vek x_2\), \(\vek x_3\) of a triangle in global axes not lie on one line, and

\[\vek c = (\vek x_2 - \vek x_1)\times(\vek x_3 - \vek x_1) , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{x}'} = \frac{\vek x_2 - \vek x_1}{|\vek x_2 - \vek x_1|} , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = \frac{\vek c}{|\vek c|} , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'} .\]

Then:

  1. \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\) are a right-handed orthonormal triple, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\) lie in the plane of the triangle, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\) is perpendicular to it, and the matrix \(\mat Q\) with rows \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}^\T\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}^\T\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}^\T\) is a rotation matrix (definition 2.44).
  2. \(|\vek c| = 2A\), where \(A\) is the area of the triangle.
  3. A cyclic exchange of the nodes \(1 \to 2 \to 3\) gives the same \(\vek c\) and the same \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\), and exchanging two nodes reverses the sign of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\).
Proof

We have to show orthogonality, right-handedness and independence of the numbering.

  1. Let \(\vek a = \vek x_2 - \vek x_1\) and \(\vek b = \vek x_3 - \vek x_1\) be nonzero and not parallel (the triangle is not degenerate). The cross product \(\vek a\times\vek b\) is perpendicular to \(\vek a\) and to \(\vek b\) and has length \(|\vek a||\vek b|\sin\gamma > 0\) (angle \(\gamma\) between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), \(0 < \gamma < \pi\) because of nondegeneracy). So \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\) is a unit vector perpendicular to the plane spanned by \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\).
  2. Since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\) is parallel to \(\vek a\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = 0\). The vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\) is perpendicular to both, its length is \(|\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}||\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}|\sin 90^\circ = 1\). So the triple is orthonormal, and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\) lies in the plane because it is perpendicular to the normal.
  3. Right-handedness: by cyclic exchange \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'} = 1 > 0\) (lemma 2.73, point 1, for the mixed product). The matrix \(\mat Q\) has orthonormal rows and determinant 1, as definition 2.44 requires.
  4. Point 2. The area of the parallelogram spanned by \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) is \(|\vek a||\vek b|\sin\gamma = |\vek c|\), the triangle has half of it.
  5. Point 3. Expanding, \(\vek c = \vek x_2\times\vek x_3 + \vek x_3\times\vek x_1 + \vek x_1\times\vek x_2\), since \(\vek x_1\times\vek x_1 = \vek 0\). The sum does not change under a cyclic exchange of the nodes, and reverses sign when two are exchanged, since the cross product is antisymmetric.

Source: ONATE2 §8.8.1 (PDF pp. 494-496, equations 8.55-8.57: the direction \(x'\) along a side, \(z'\) from the cross product of two sides, \(y'\) from the product of \(z'\) and \(x'\), for a triangle \(|\vek c|\) is twice the area), COOK §16.4 (PDF pp. 590-591, equation 16.4-3, the same construction). The proof is ours.

For our triangle \(\vek c = (0, -1800, 2400)\) mm\(^2\), \(|\vek c| = 3000\) mm\(^2\) and \(A = 1500\) mm\(^2\) (the area of a right triangle \(60 \times 50\) mm is indeed \(1500\), half of the parallelogram), and

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'} = (1, 0, 0) , \qquad \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'} = (0, 0{,}8, 0{,}6) , \qquad \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = (0, -0{,}6, 0{,}8) .\]

We see these directions if we look at the triangle along the global axis \(X\), as in figure 22.90.

The axis \(X\) points toward us (a circle with a dot), the axes \(Y\) and \(Z\) lie in the paper. Nodes 1 and 2 lie on the axis \(X\) one behind the other, so we see them as one point, and we see the whole triangle edge-on, as a segment to node 3. Node 3 is \(40\) mm in the direction \(Y\) and \(30\) mm in the direction \(Z\) from the axis \(X\). The segment therefore has length \(\sqrt{40^2 + 30^2} = 50\) mm and direction \((0, 40, 30)/50 = (0, 0{,}8, 0{,}6)\), which is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\). The normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\) is perpendicular to the segment, and \(\vek c\) points the same way, since \((-1800, 2400)/3000 = (-0{,}6, 0{,}8)\).

The coordinates of the nodes in the local axes are \(\mat Q(\vek x_{\mathrm{i}} - \vek x_1)\), that is \((0, 0)\), \((60, 0)\) and \((0, 50)\) mm, which is the triangle with the dimensions \(60\) and \(50\) mm on the right of figure 22.87. Node 3 lies on the axis \(y'\) and \(\vek x_{31}\) has length \(50\) mm, as it must.

Two cases where the construction fails are instructive (figure 22.91).

For a triangle with nodes \((0, 0, 0)\), \((1, 0, 0)\), \((2, 0, 0)\) we get \(\vek c = \vek 0\), since the sides are parallel, and there is no plane. Three points always lie in a plane, four need not. Take a square of side \(100\) mm with vertices \(A = (0, 0, 0)\), \(B = (100, 0, 0)\), \(C = (100, 100, 0)\) and \(D = (0, 100, \delta)\). The normal at each vertex is the cross product of the two sides from that vertex: at \(A\) it is proportional to \((0, -\delta, 100)\), at \(C\) to \((\delta, 0, 100)\), and the normals tilt each to its own side. For every \(\delta \ne 0\) the normals are not equal and the plane of the element is not determined: the largest angle between the normals of the four vertices, for example between \(\vek n_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{C}}\), is \(0{,}81^\circ\) for \(\delta = 1\) mm and \(8{,}07^\circ\) for \(\delta = 10\) mm. An element with such a surface is called warped. Its local axes, \(\mat K_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) need a special treatment that we do not prove: COOK §16.4 (PDF p. 591) warns that without it the results can be wrong by orders of magnitude, and gives a correction with \(z\) forces that balance pairs of membrane forces (PDF pp. 591-592).

22.9.3 Rotating into global axes and assembly

Now we place the element into the structure. A node of the common mesh may have several elements in different planes, like the fold \(J\) of the tube, so it must have common unknowns: the displacement and the rotation vector in global axes, six numbers. We get the local unknowns from the global ones with the matrix \(\mat Q\) of lemma 22.57.

Lemma 22.58 Matrix of a flat shell element in global axes (matrika ploščatega elementa v globalnih oseh)

Let a flat shell element with \(m\) nodes (definition 22.56) have local axes with matrix \(\mat Q\) (lemma 22.57), global unknowns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (the displacement and the rotation vector of each node in global axes) and \(\mat T = \mathrm{diag}(\mat Q, \dots, \mat Q)\) with \(2m\) blocks \(\mat Q\) (two per node). Then:

  1. \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek F'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T\,\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\) for the forces and moments of the surroundings, \(\mat T^\T\mat T = \mat I\).
  2. The matrix of the element in global axes is \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T^\T\,\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat T\), symmetric, with \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\).
  3. The local rotations of a node with rotation vector \(\vek\varphi\) are \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek\varphi\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek\varphi\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\cdot\vek\varphi\). Only the first two appear in the energy.
  4. The matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) enter theorem 7.13 with six unknowns at every node.
Proof

We have to show that the unknowns of the element turn as for a space frame, and carry over the energy.

  1. By lemma 12.23 the displacement and the rotation vector of a node are each mapped by \(\mat Q\), and so are the force and moment of the surroundings. So \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek F'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T\,\vek F_{\mathrm{e}}\). The matrix \(\mat Q\) is orthogonal (lemma 2.47), so \(\mat T^\T\mat T = \mat I\).
  2. The energy of the element is a number independent of the axes: \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,(\mat T\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\,(\mat T\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}) = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T(\mat T^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\mat T)\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 1.102). The matrix \(\mat T^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\mat T\) is symmetric since \(\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric. The force of the surroundings is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T^\T\vek F'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat T^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), as in equation (12.23).
  3. The rows of the matrix \(\mat Q\) are \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\) (lemma 22.57), so \(\mat Q\vek\varphi = (\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek\varphi,\ \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek\varphi,\ \vek e_{\mathrm{z}'}\cdot\vek\varphi)\), these are the orthogonal projections of the rotation vector on the local axes. By property 1 of definition 22.56, \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'}\) does not appear in \(\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  4. Each element has \(6m\) unknowns and theorem 7.13 demands nothing about their meaning, only the connection table and a symmetric matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\).

Source: ONATE2 §8.5 (PDF pp. 487-490, equations 8.39-8.48: local unknowns to global ones with a block matrix, for a flat element the same matrix at all nodes), COOK §16.4 (PDF p. 591: the coordinate transformation as for the beam in section 2.4). The proof is ours and follows lemma 12.23.

Let us see on an example what this means. Take our triangle and node 3, which rotates about the global axis \(Z\) by \(\varphi = 10^{-3}\) rad, so \(\vek\varphi = (0, 0, 10^{-3})\). The local rotations are \(\mat Q\vek\varphi = (0, 0{,}6\cdot 10^{-3}, 0{,}8\cdot 10^{-3})\). The element feels \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = 0{,}6\cdot 10^{-3}\), because this is a rotation about an axis that lies in its plane, and it bends the element. The component \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'} = 0{,}8\cdot 10^{-3}\) is a rotation about the normal, which the element does not resist. Of \(10^{-3}\) rad the element sees only \(0{,}6\cdot 10^{-3}\). What this means for the structure is shown by the next theorem.

22.9.4 Rotation about the normal

At which point of the mesh does the matrix of the structure lose the property we need for a unique solution? Figure 22.92 gives the answer.

On the left stands the node \(J\) in which the surface of four elements lies in one plane. The normal \(\vek n\) of this plane points toward the viewer. The rotation vector of size \(1\) and direction of the normal, the rotation \(\vek\varphi\), is the rotation about the normal. The axes \(x'\) and \(y'\) of every element are perpendicular to \(\vek n\), so by lemma 22.58 the component of this rotation along them is zero: no element resists it. We can also look at it differently. For a plane element the node \(J\) is like a hinge that transmits forces in the plane but knows no rotation. A plate element knows only the rotations about axes in the plane. So neither of the two holds the rotation about the normal.

Theorem 22.59 Drilling rotation at a coplanar node (vrtenje okoli normale ravninskega vozlišča)

Let there be a mesh of flat shell elements (definition 22.56) and \(\mat K\) the matrix of the structure from theorem 7.13 with the matrices \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) of lemma 22.58, with six unknowns at every node. Let \(J\) be a node in which all elements lie in one plane with normal \(\vek n\) (a coplanar node), and let \(\vek d^*\) be the column with the rotation vector \(\vek n\) (\(|\vek n| = 1\)) at node \(J\) and zeros everywhere else. Then:

  1. \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^* = 0\) and \(\mat K\,\vek d^* = \vek 0\), so \(\mat K\) is singular unless supports prevent the rotation at \(J\).
  2. The system \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\) has a solution only if \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek f = 0\) (the moment of the load at \(J\) about \(\vek n\) is zero), and the solution is not unique: with \(\vek d\) also \(\vek d + c\,\vek d^*\) is a solution for every \(c\).
  3. Let \(k_\theta > 0\) be any number and \(\mat K_\theta = \mat K + k_\theta\,\vek d^*\vek d^{*\T}\) (an added artificial stiffness of the rotation about the normal). If \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek f = 0\), every solution of the system \(\mat K_\theta\,\vek d = \vek f\) solves \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\) and has \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d = 0\), independently of \(k_\theta\).
Proof

We have to show that the energy of a rotation about the normal is zero, and from it the singularity.

  1. Let the element \(e\) contain \(J\). Its local axes have \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = \pm\vek n\), because all elements lie in one plane. In its column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) there is only the rotation vector \(\vek n\) at one node, so by lemma 22.58 (point 3) \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek n = 0\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek n = 0\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'} = \pm 1\). All displacements are zero. The energy \(U_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not contain \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'}\) (property 1 of definition 22.56), so \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\). Elements without \(J\) have \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\).
  2. By theorem 7.13 (point 2), \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^* = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^{*\T}\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^* = 0\).
  3. By corollary 7.14, \(\mat K\) is symmetric with \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v \ge 0\) for every \(\vek v\). By theorem 10.21 with \(\mat M = \mat I\) there are orthonormal columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), which are a basis, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\). We write \(\vek d^*\) \(= \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and \(0 = \vek d^{*\T}\mat K\vek d^* = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\). All terms are nonnegative, so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for every \(r\) and \(\mat K\,\vek d^* = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). This is point 1. A matrix with a nonzero column in its kernel is singular.
  4. Point 2. If \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f\), then \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek f = \vek d^{*\T}\mat K\vek d = (\mat K\vek d^*)^\T\vek d = 0\) (symmetry). Moreover \(\mat K(\vek d + c\vek d^*) = \mat K\vek d\).
  5. Point 3. If \(\mat K_\theta\vek d = \vek f\), we multiply by \(\vek d^{*\T}\): \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\vek d + k_\theta(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d^*)(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d) = \vek d^{*\T}\vek f = 0\). The first term is zero by step 3, \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d^* = |\vek n|^2 = 1\), so \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d = 0\). From \(\mat K\vek d + k_\theta(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d)\vek d^* = \vek f\) it follows that \(\mat K\vek d = \vek f\).

Source: COOK §16.4 (PDF p. 589: if a node is surrounded by coplanar elements, the structure matrix is singular, because the rotation about the normal is not resisted), ONATE2 §8.9 (PDF pp. 498-500, the text on singularity, the corrections by local assembly or by an artificial stiffness \(K_{\theta \mathrm{z}'}\) of the order \(E\,h\,A^{(e)}\)). The proof is ours: the sources state the singularity.

The theorem tells us how to repair the system. The first way is to leave out the unknown \(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'}\) at coplanar nodes and to assemble the node in local axes, so it has five unknowns, the second is to add an artificial stiffness \(k_\theta\) (point 3), as if we tied the rotation about the normal to a weak spring. ONATE2 PDF pp. 499-501 reports that the result is little sensitive to \(k_\theta\) and chooses it of the order \(E\,h\,A^{(e)}\) (unit N mm, \(A^{(e)}\) the area of the element). Our proof tells why: as long as the moment about the normal at \(J\) is zero, the solution for the other unknowns does not depend on \(k_\theta\) at all (point 3). If the moment about the normal were nonzero, the rotation would be \(\vek d^{*\T}\vek d = \vek d^{*\T}\vek f/k_\theta\) and would depend on the choice, so a flat surface cannot carry such a load.

What happens if the elements do not lie in one plane? A fold has the angle \(\alpha\) between the normals \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\) (\(\vek n_1\cdot\vek n_2 = \cos\alpha\)). On the right we look along the fold. Drawn are facet 1 and 2, their normals \(\vek n_1\) and \(\vek n_2\) and the angle \(\alpha = 30^\circ\) between the facets. The node \(J\) rotates about the normal of facet 1, this is the rotation \(\vek\varphi\). Facet 1 again feels nothing of it, but facet 2 has a component of the rotation along its plane, the projection of size \(\varphi\sin\alpha\), and this is a rotation about an axis that lies in its plane, so a bending that the facet resists.

Lemma 22.60 A node on a fold (vozlišče na pregibu)

Let the mesh be as in theorem 22.59, \(J\) a node in which the elements \(e\) have normals \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek n_1\) is the normal of one of them. Let \(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the angle between \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek n_1\) (from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\), since a normal may be reversed) and \(\vek d^*\) as in the theorem with \(\vek n = \vek n_1\). Then:

  1. \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^* = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat K_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{e}}\,\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\), where \(\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}} = (\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek n_1,\ \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek n_1)\) and \(\mat K_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{e}}\) is the submatrix of order \(2\) of the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) of element \(e\) for the rotations at \(J\), and \(|\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}| = \sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. If \(\sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}} \ne 0\) for at least one element, then \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^* > 0\).
  3. The smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) of the matrix \(\mat K\) (theorem 10.21 with \(\mat M = \mat I\)) is at most
    \[\lambda_1 \le \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\ \ell_{\mathrm{e}} ,\]
    where \(\ell_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the largest eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat K_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{e}}\). If the rotations at \(J\) are not prescribed, the same bound holds for the matrix of free unknowns.
Proof

We have to show that the energy of a rotation about \(\vek n_1\) is positive and bounded by \(\sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\).

  1. The element \(e\) at \(J\) has local axes with \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}'} = \pm\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\). By lemma 22.58 (point 3) the local rotations are \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek n_1\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek n_1\). The vector \(\vek n_1\) has in the basis of the element the components \((\vek e_{\mathrm{x}'}\cdot\vek n_1,\ \vek e_{\mathrm{y}'}\cdot\vek n_1,\ \pm\cos\alpha_{\mathrm{e}})\) and unit length, so \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'}^2 + \theta_{\mathrm{y}'}^2 = 1 - \cos^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}} = \sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\): the component in the plane of the element has length \(\sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the component along its normal \(\cos\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The displacements of all nodes are zero and only the node \(J\) has the rotations \(\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{b}}\) of element \(e\) contains \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\) at \(J\) and zeros elsewhere, \(\vek d'_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek 0\). By equation (22.57), \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}'^\T\mat k'_{\mathrm{e}}\vek d'_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat K_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{e}}\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}\) (\(\theta_{\mathrm{z}'}\) has no effect). By theorem 7.13 (point 2), \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\vek d^*\) is the sum of these terms. This is point 1.
  3. Point 2. Let \(\sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}} \ne 0\). If the energy of the element were zero, then by property (R) of definition 22.56 \(w'_{\mathrm{i}} = a_0 + a_1\,x'_{\mathrm{i}} + a_2\,y'_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the nodes. Since all \(w'_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) at at least three nodes that are not on a line, \(a_0 = a_1 = a_2 = 0\), so \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'} = a_2 = 0\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'} = -a_1 = 0\), which contradicts \(|\vek\theta_{\mathrm{e}}| = \sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}} \ne 0\). So the energy is positive, and the other elements add nonnegative terms.
  4. Point 3. By theorem 10.21 \(\lambda_1\) is the smallest value of \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v\) among columns with \(\vek v^\T\vek v = 1\), so \(\lambda_1 \le \vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^*\) (\(|\vek d^*| = |\vek n_1| = 1\)). For a symmetric matrix of order \(2\), \(\vek\theta^\T\mat A\,\vek\theta \le \ell\,|\vek\theta|^2\) with the largest eigenvalue \(\ell\) (theorem 10.21 for \(2 \times 2\)), so the term of element \(e\) is at most \(\ell_{\mathrm{e}}\sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\), the square of the projection times the largest stiffness. If the rotations at \(J\) are not prescribed, \(\vek d^*\) is a column of free unknowns only, so \(\vek d^{*\T}\mat K\,\vek d^*\) is the same for the matrix of free unknowns and the same estimate holds for it.

Source: COOK §16.4 (PDF p. 589: if elements are not coplanar, the rotation about the normal in one element has a component resisted by the bending stiffness of the adjacent one, and PDF p. 590: the correction by a small stiffness or by leaving out the unknown), ONATE2 §8.9 (PDF pp. 498-500, "quasi-coplanar" nodes). The proof is ours.

The bound \(\lambda_1 \le \sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\,\ell_{\mathrm{e}}\) has a meaning: \(\sin\alpha_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the length of the projection of the rotation on the plane of the neighbouring facet. At a fold \(\alpha = 30^\circ\) we have \(\sin^2\alpha = 0{,}25\), and at \(\alpha = 5^\circ\) it is \(0{,}0076\): the rotation about the normal is nearly free at a small fold, the matrix is near singular, which worsens the solution of the system. Why does it worsen it? If we expand the solution \(\vek d\) in the eigenvectors of theorem 10.21, its component in the direction \(\vek\varphi_1\) is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek f/\lambda_1\). Every small error of the load or rounding in this direction is therefore magnified by the factor \(1/\lambda_1\), which with one neighbouring element is at least \(1/(\sin^2\alpha\,\ell)\), at \(\alpha = 5^\circ\) thus at least \(132/\ell\) instead of \(1/\ell\) at a right angle. Such nodes are called quasi-coplanar (skoraj ravninska). For a tube of \(n\) facets the fold is \(\alpha = 360^\circ/n\) and the bound from the lemma is \(\sin^2\alpha\):

\(n\) \(\alpha\) \(\sin^2\alpha\) \(s/h\)
8 \(45{,}00^\circ\) 0,5000 1,903
16 \(22{,}50^\circ\) 0,1464 0,480
36 \(10{,}00^\circ\) 0,0302 0,095
64 \(5{,}625^\circ\) 0,0096 0,030
72 \(5{,}00^\circ\) 0,0076 0,024

A finer mesh reduces the sagitta \(s\), the error of the geometry (last column), but it also reduces the angle \(\alpha\), and the matrix becomes worse conditioned. Good geometry (\(s \le 0{,}1\,h\) at \(n = 36\)) requires \(\alpha \le 10^\circ\), so for a smooth shell we cannot avoid quasi-coplanar nodes. ONATE2 (PDF p. 500) writes that a node is marked coplanar when the angle between the normals exceeds a limit (for instance \(5^\circ\)). This is a slip, since a large angle means a fold: the right statement is that the angle does not exceed the limit, as lemma 22.60 and the table show. For quasi-coplanar nodes ONATE2 §8.9.5 (PDF pp. 504-505) assembles the rotations in axes \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\) perpendicular to the mean direction of the normals and keeps five unknowns.

Two matters we name without proving them. The first are warped quadrilaterals, which we met above: the local axes of lemma 22.57 are not determined, and the correction with forces is in COOK §16.4 (PDF pp. 591-592). The second is locking: ONATE2 §8.11 (PDF pp. 507-508) shows that for a flat element the membrane part has the same dependence on the thickness as the shear part, so the solution can "lock" for thin shells, and ONATE2 PDF p. 503 states that elements with the drilling rotation as an extra unknown are prone to membrane locking in problems where bending dominates, because the drilling rotation brings in a membrane stiffness that outweighs the stiffness of the rotations \(\theta_{\mathrm{x}'}\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{y}'}\). The membrane stiffness is proportional to \(h\), the bending one to \(h^3\) (theorem 22.29). Both are further difficulties of the element that this section does not resolve.

What have we shown? A smooth shell is replaced by flat elements, each has six unknowns at a node, but the element resists only five (definition 22.56, lemma 22.58). The sixth, the rotation about the normal, is free at a coplanar node (theorem 22.59), and at a fold it is resisted by the bending of the neighbouring element with a stiffness proportional to the square of the projection \(\sin^2\alpha\) (lemma 22.60). In the tube the force of an element in its plane has, with respect to the neighbouring element, the transverse component \(N\sin\alpha\), which the line load \(P\) balances at the fold (lemma 22.55 and the computation after it), which is the coupling of the in-plane and the bending part. The pressure on the facets, however, bends the facets with a stress at the fold of about \(4\,s/h\) of the hoop stress, which we obtained from a strip clamped at the folds (corollary 18.26). Both things must be taken into account in every mesh of flat elements, including the tube above.
