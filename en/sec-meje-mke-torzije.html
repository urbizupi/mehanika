Chapter 16 Torsion

16.9 Bounds of the torsion constant and finite elements

We took the torsion constant \(J\) of the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip in section 16.5 from a series, because Prandtl's equation on a rectangle is solved by separation of variables. For an L-shaped section, a section with rounded corners or any other outline there is no such series. The engineer still has to know how far the shaft twists, and besides the number needs to know how much it may be off. In this section we show that the torsion constant allows such knowledge. Both boundary problems of sections 16.2 and 16.3 are the search for the smallest value of something. So every trial function, even a poor one, gives a bound for \(J\): the warping function from above, Prandtl's function from below, and the exact value lies between them, as the number line at the end of the section shows for the strip. When we compute these two bounds on a mesh of triangles we obtain finite elements for torsion whose error we know.

16.9.1 Upper bound from the warping function

We draw the way to the upper bound on the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip [figure 16.81].

Take a prism in Saint-Venant torsion (definition 16.1) and try another function \(\tilde\psi\) in place of the true warping function \(\psi\), that is a trial displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\tilde\psi\). By lemma 16.16 the two shear strains of this field are \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \alpha(\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z)\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \alpha(\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\). Write the pair \((\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z,\ \tilde\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\) as the vector \(\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w\), where \(\vek w = (z, -y)\) is the field that every point of the section gets from the rotation of the section as a rigid body. On the section we draw it with arrows: each is perpendicular to the line joining the point to the centre and the longer, the farther the point is from the centre, like the velocity of the points of a turning wheel. The strain energy density is \(\tfrac12(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}) = \tfrac12 G\alpha^2\,|\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w|^2\), and the energy per unit length of the prism is \(\tfrac12 G\alpha^2 F(\tilde\psi)\) with the integral

\[F(\tilde\psi) = \int_{\mathrm{S}} |\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad \vek w = (z, -y) . \tag{16.59}\]

The true warping function has energy \(\tfrac12 G J\alpha^2\) (by theorem 16.18 \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = GJ\alpha\), and the work of the moment over the twist \(\alpha\) is half the product, because during loading the moment grows from zero in proportion to the twist). So we expect \(F(\psi) = J\) and \(F(\tilde\psi) \ge J\) for every other function. This is the principle of minimum total potential energy (theorem 4.91) for the twisting of a prism. We prove it directly, because a section is not a body of that theorem.

Lemma 16.59 Warping functional and an upper bound of \(J\) (funkcional zvitja in zgornja meja \(J\))

Let \(S\) be the section of a prism in Saint-Venant torsion with the warping function \(\psi\) of theorem 16.18, twice continuously differentiable on an open set that contains \(S\) (for the rectangle this fails at the corners, section 16.5). Let \(S\) be cut into finitely many pieces \(S_1, \dots, S_{\mathrm{m}}\) for which lemma 16.17 holds also for fields continuously differentiable up to the boundary of the piece (for a triangle chapter 15 proves it), meeting along whole edges. Let \(\tilde\psi\) be continuous on \(S\) and continuously differentiable up to the boundary on every piece. Then:

  1. \(F(\psi) = J\).
  2. \(J \le F(\tilde\psi)\).
  3. Equality \(J = F(\tilde\psi)\) holds exactly when \(\tilde\psi - \psi\) is a constant.
Idea

The difference \(F(\tilde\psi) - F(\psi)\) is the energy of the difference of the fields, because the mixed term vanishes through equilibrium and the free lateral surface.

Proof
  1. Let \(\vek q = \nabla\psi - \vek w\) and \(g = \tilde\psi - \psi\). The field \(\vek q\) is continuous up to the boundary. Its divergence is \(\nabla^2\psi - (\partial z/\partial y + \partial(-y)/\partial z) = 0 - 0\), because \(\psi\) is harmonic and \(\partial z/\partial y = \partial y/\partial z = 0\). On the boundary \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = \partial\psi/\partial n - (z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\) by the boundary condition of theorem 16.18. At a boundary point the field \(\vek q\) therefore has no component along the normal \(\vek n\) and lies along the boundary.
  2. From \(\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w = \vek q + \nabla g\) it follows that \(F(\tilde\psi) = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\vek q|^2\,\mathrm{d}A + 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla g\cdot\vek q\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla g|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), where the first term is \(F(\psi)\).
  3. On a piece \(S_{\mathrm{k}}\) (the figure has the pieces \(S_1\) and \(S_2\)) the field \(g\,\vek q\) has divergence \(\nabla g\cdot\vek q + g\,\mathrm{div}\,\vek q = \nabla g\cdot\vek q\). By lemma 16.17, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{k}}\nabla g\cdot\vek q\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}_\mathrm{k}}g\,\vek q\cdot\vek n_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  4. Add over the pieces. An edge that two pieces share has opposite normals for the two, \(\vek n_1 = -\vek n_2\), while \(g\) and \(\vek q\) are continuous there, so the contributions cancel. This is where the continuity of \(\tilde\psi\) enters: if \(g\) jumped on the edge, a term with the jump would remain. On the boundary \(\partial S\) we have \(\vek q\cdot\vek n = 0\). So \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla g\cdot\vek q\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).
  5. From steps 2 and 4, \(F(\tilde\psi) = F(\psi) + \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla g|^2\,\mathrm{d}A \ge F(\psi)\). Equality requires \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla g|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), so \(\nabla g = 0\) on every piece (a continuous nonnegative function with zero integral is zero everywhere, as in the proof of lemma 16.25). A continuous function with zero gradient on every piece is constant on a connected section.
  6. The function \(\tilde\psi = 0\) is one of the admissible ones (one piece). From step 5, \(F(0) = F(\psi) + \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), and \(F(0) = \int_{\mathrm{S}}(z^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{p}}\). By lemma 16.20, \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(F(\psi) = J\). This proves points 1 and 2, point 3 is in step 5.

Source: The idea is the principle of minimum energy from chapter 4, recast for twisting. The form of \(F\) and step 6 are ours, with the equations of section 16.2. SADD section 9.3.2 (PDF pp. 251-252) writes the same problem for the warping function with other letters (his \(z\) is the bar axis, the section has \(x\) and \(y\)). Step 3 needs \(\psi\) twice differentiable: lemma 16.17 asks for the field \(g\,\vek q\) continuously differentiable up to the boundary of the piece. For the rectangle the second derivatives of \(\psi\) are not continuous at the corners, so for the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip we checked the identity of step 5 with the series of section 16.5: at \(\tilde\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\), \(F(\tilde\psi) - J\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla g|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) are both \(12\,155\) mm\(^4\).

The function \(\tilde\psi\) does not have to be chosen so that it satisfies the boundary condition \(\partial\tilde\psi/\partial n = z\,n_{\mathrm{y}} - y\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). That condition is natural, as zero force at a free end is for the bar (chapter 6), and the functional finds it itself. This is the whole strength of the lemma: it asks only for a continuous function that is continuously differentiable on every piece. But continuity it does ask for. In the proof it enters in step 4, where the contributions of the shared side cancel only if \(\tilde\psi\) has the same value on both sides. What happens without it is shown by example 16.60.

We test the lemma on a circle of radius \(R = 20\) mm, where we know the answer. For the circle \(\psi = 0\) (theorem 16.3), \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}} = \pi R^4/2 = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\). Take \(\tilde\psi = c\,y z\) with an unknown number \(c\). Then \(\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w = ((c - 1)z,\ (c + 1)y)\) and

\[F(c\,yz) = (c - 1)^2\int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A + (c + 1)^2\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = (c^2 + 1)\,I_{\mathrm{p}} , \tag{16.60}\]

because \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = I_{\mathrm{p}}/2\) (the circle is symmetric under swapping \(y\) and \(z\)) and the terms \(\mp 2c\) cancel. Over the \(c\) axis this is a parabola. Its bottom is at \(c = 0\) and equals \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 251\,327\) mm\(^4\), so it is the exact value. At \(c = 0{,}3\) we get \(F = 1{,}09\,I_{\mathrm{p}} = 273\,947\) mm\(^4\), which is \(9{,}00\) % above \(J\). So the bound holds, and it also says that the warping function of the circle really is zero: every \(c \ne 0\) adds energy.

Example 16.60 A discontinuous trial function is no upper bound (nezvezna poskusna funkcija ni zgornja meja)

In thought, saw the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip along the line \(z = 0\) into two bars \(20 \times 20\) mm, as the shared side in the figure divides it into the pieces \(S_1\) and \(S_2\), only here through the middle. Give each piece its own warping: on the upper one \(\tilde\psi = \psi_\square(y, z - 10) + 10y\), on the lower one \(\tilde\psi = \psi_\square(y, z + 10) - 10y\), where \(\psi_\square\) is the warping function of the \(20 \times 20\) mm square about its own centre (equation (16.42) with \(a = b = 10\) mm). The term \(\pm10y\) moves the axis of rotation from the centre of the piece to the centre of the strip, because \(\vek w = (z, -y) = (z \mp 10, -y) + (\pm 10, 0)\) and \(\nabla(\pm 10y) = (\pm10, 0)\). So on each piece \(\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w\) is exactly the field \(\vek q\) of the square on its own, and \(F(\tilde\psi) = 2J_\square = 2\cdot22\,492 = 44\,985\) mm\(^4\) (\(J_\square\) is the torsion constant of the square from section 16.6). That is \(38{,}5\) % below \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\): the number that should be an upper bound lies below the exact value. The cause is the jump on the cut. We draw the values of \(\tilde\psi\) along the cut over the \(y\) axis. The upper piece gives a curve that rises from \(-100\) to \(+100\) mm\(^2\), the lower one its mirror image, because \(\psi_\square\) is odd in \(z\). At the point \(y = 5\) mm, \(z = 0\) the upper piece has \(\tilde\psi = +63{,}9\) mm\(^2\), the lower one \(-63{,}9\) mm\(^2\), the jump is \(127{,}8\) mm\(^2\), and step 4 of the proof of lemma 16.59 fails. Mechanically we have computed a different body: two bars that slide freely along the axis against each other on the cut are together softer than the whole strip and have only \(61{,}5\) % of its torsional stiffness.

16.9.2 Lower bound from Prandtl's function

With the warping we caught \(J\) from above. Now we need it from below. Divide Prandtl's function by \(G\alpha\) to get \(\hat\Phi = \Phi/(G\alpha)\). By theorem 16.24 we then have \(\nabla^2\hat\Phi = -2\) in \(S\), \(\hat\Phi = 0\) on \(\partial S\) and \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = GJ\alpha\), so

\[J = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{16.61}\]

The function \(\hat\Phi\) has units mm\(^2\), and we see it as Prandtl's function for \(G\alpha = 1\).

What can we say about \(J\) from a function \(f\) that is not the true one? Compare it with the true one by subtracting \(t\) times the true gradient from its gradient and adding up the square of the difference over the section, \(p(t) = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f - t\nabla\hat\Phi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) [figure 16.83]. This is a sum of squares, so it is never negative. As a function of the number \(t\) it is a parabola over the \(t\) axis. Its lowest point cannot go below the axis, and exactly this condition is the lower bound for \(J\).

Lemma 16.61 Prandtl functional and a lower bound of \(J\) (funkcional Prandtlove funkcije in spodnja meja \(J\))

Let \(S\) be a section as in theorem 16.24 with \(\hat\Phi\) twice continuously differentiable up to the boundary, cut into pieces as in lemma 16.59. Let \(f\) be continuous on \(S\), continuously differentiable up to the boundary on every piece, \(f = 0\) on \(\partial S\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\). Then

\[J \ge \frac{4\Big(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\Big)^2}{\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A} , \tag{16.62}\]

and equality holds exactly when \(f = c\,\hat\Phi\) for some constant \(c\).

Idea

The square \(|\nabla f - t\nabla\hat\Phi|^2\) is nonnegative for every \(t\). We choose the best \(t\).

Proof
  1. The field \(f\,\nabla\hat\Phi\) has divergence \(\nabla f\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi + f\,\nabla^2\hat\Phi = \nabla f\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi - 2f\) on a piece. By lemma 16.17 on every piece the integral equals the flux through the boundary of the piece. When we add, the contributions of a shared edge cancel (the fields \(f\) and \(\nabla\hat\Phi\) are continuous, the normals are opposite), and on the boundary of the section \(f = 0\). So \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla f\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  2. For \(f = \hat\Phi\), step 1 and equation (16.61) give \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\hat\Phi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = J\).
  3. For every real number \(t\), \(0 \le \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f - t\nabla\hat\Phi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A - 4t\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A + t^2J\) by steps 1 and 2. This is a parabola in \(t\) that does not reach below the axis.
  4. At the lowest point of the parabola, at \(t = t^* = \frac{2}{J}\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\), this is \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A - \frac{4}{J}\big(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\big)^2 \ge 0\). Multiply by \(J > 0\) and divide by \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  5. Equality requires \(\nabla f = t\nabla\hat\Phi\) everywhere, so \(f - t\hat\Phi\) is a constant that is zero on the boundary. The parabola then touches the axis.

Source: The computation is ours, with the equations of section 16.3. The assumption of two derivatives up to the boundary carries the same reservation as lemma 16.23: for a rectangle the second derivatives jump at the corners and the cut near a corner is not proved, SADD cites it (PDF pp. 246-251). The lower bound for the rectangle in this section therefore rests on that cited step.

In the language of energy the lemma is the principle of minimum complementary energy (theorem 4.101). By section 16.3 the stresses from any \(f\) are in equilibrium and the free lateral surface is unloaded, and the complementary energy per unit length is \(\tfrac{1}{2G}\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) at the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\). The true stress picture has the smallest complementary energy \(M_{\mathrm{x}}^2/(2GJ)\). The same inequality follows. The lemma also says what matters about \(f\): the function must be zero on the whole boundary, and its height plays no role, since it cancels in the quotient. We test this.

On a circle of radius \(R\) take \(f_1 = R^2 - r^2\), which is the exact shape (section 16.1), drawn as a hill over a diameter. The integrals over the circle by the polar change of variables of theorem 4.59 are \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\pi\int_0^R(R^2 - r^2)\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = \pi R^4/2\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\pi\int_0^R 4r^2\cdot r\,\mathrm{d}r = 2\pi R^4\), and the quotient \(4(\pi R^4/2)^2/(2\pi R^4) = \pi R^4/2 = I_{\mathrm{p}}\) equals the exact value. By contrast \(f_2 = (R^2 - r^2)^2\) has a zero on the boundary but the wrong shape, it is too flat near the boundary: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \pi R^6/3\), \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 4\pi R^8/3\), and the lower bound is \(\pi R^4/3 = \tfrac23I_{\mathrm{p}}\), which is \(167\,552\) mm\(^4\) at \(R = 20\) mm, \(33{,}33\) % below \(J\). The bound holds but is poor. The two parabolas on the right of the figure show it: the first touches the axis, the second stays above it by \(\tfrac13\) of its starting height. This remainder is exactly the share of \(J\) that the bound loses, because by step 4 of the proof \(p(t^*)/p(0) = 1 - J_{\mathrm{f}}/J\), where \(J_{\mathrm{f}}\) is the right side of inequality (16.62).

16.9.3 Bounds on the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip

Now take the strip of section 16.5 with half sides \(a = 10\) mm along \(y\) and \(b = 20\) mm along \(z\), where we know the exact value \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) from the series and use it only for comparison. For the lower bound we need a function that is zero on all four sides. Such a function is \(f = (a^2 - y^2)(b^2 - z^2)\), the function \(\Phi_{\mathrm{t}}\) of example 16.29, which was not a solution of the equation there. Here its accuracy does not matter, because we are only looking for the best function of this form.

We draw it together with the true function [figure 16.84]. At the top is three times the same \(20 \times 40\) mm section, at the scale \(0{,}8\) cm per \(10\) mm, with the \(y\) axis across the width and the \(z\) axis along the length. In the left section are the level lines of the function \(f\) at \(0{,}2\), \(0{,}4\), \(0{,}6\) and \(0{,}8\) of the peak, with the peak \(100\) mm\(^2\) at the centre (\(f\) has been multiplied by the constant \(c = 0{,}0025\), which the quotient (16.62) does not feel). In the middle section are the same lines of the true function \(\hat\Phi\), computed with the series of section 16.5, with peak \(91{,}1\) mm\(^2\). Both families are rounded closed curves, but the true lines are flatter near the middle of the longer side, because there \(\hat\Phi\) changes more slowly with \(z\). The two cuts below the sections show it, with each function divided by its peak. Along the \(z\) axis at \(y = 0\) the true function lies above the trial one. From \(z = 0\) to \(z = 5\) mm, \(\hat\Phi\) falls by \(3{,}2\) % (from \(91{,}10\) to \(88{,}21\) mm\(^2\)), the trial function by \(6{,}25\) %, and at \(z = 10\) mm they are at \(0{,}853\) and \(0{,}750\) of the peak. Across the width at \(z = 0\), however, the trial and the true one nearly coincide, at \(y = 5\) mm they are at \(0{,}750\) and \(0{,}754\) of the peak. In a very long strip \(\hat\Phi\) would be nearly independent of \(z\) in the middle (section 16.6), at the ratio \(2\) this is only beginning.

We compute the quotient (16.62) for \(f\), which has units mm\(^4\). The integrals are \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}(a^2 - y^2)\,\mathrm{d}y\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}(b^2 - z^2)\,\mathrm{d}z = \tfrac{4a^3}{3}\cdot\tfrac{4b^3}{3} = 14\,222\,222\) mm\(^6\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{128}{45}a^3b^3(a^2 + b^2) = 1{,}1378\cdot10^{10}\) mm\(^8\) (the term \(4y^2(b^2 - z^2)^2\) and the term \(4z^2(a^2 - y^2)^2\) give \(\tfrac{128}{45}a^3b^5\) and \(\tfrac{128}{45}a^5b^3\)). The quotient is \(\tfrac{40}{9}\,a^3b^3/(a^2 + b^2) = 71\,111\) mm\(^4\), which is \(-2{,}82\) % relative to \(J\). Since the trial function has only the form \(c\,(a^2 - y^2)(b^2 - z^2)\), we add a term in \(y^2\) with a second free constant: \(f = (a^2 - y^2)(b^2 - z^2)(c_1 + c_2y^2/a^2)\). The best choice (\(c_1 = 0{,}002476\), \(c_2 = 0{,}0001268\), from \(\partial/\partial c_1 = \partial/\partial c_2 = 0\) of the functional \(\tfrac12\int|\nabla f|^2 - 2\int f\)) gives \(71\,152\) mm\(^4\), \(-2{,}77\) %. The extra parameter improved the bound by barely \(0{,}06\) percentage points (more precisely from \(-2{,}825\) to \(-2{,}768\) %), because the error is not in the shape across the width (there the true function is nearly a parabola) but along the length, where the trial function has no flat part. For a thinner strip this gets worse: when \(b/a\) goes beyond all limits, \(\tfrac{40}{9}a^3b^3/(a^2 + b^2) \to \tfrac{40}{9}a^3b\), while the thin strip has by section 16.6 \(J \to \tfrac{16}{3}a^3b\), so the ratio is \(5/6\) and the error tends to \(-16{,}7\) %. The error \(-2{,}82\) % holds for the ratio \(2\), not for every strip.

Example 16.62 A function that is not zero on the whole boundary is no lower bound (funkcija, ki ni nič na vsem robu, ni spodnja meja)

Try \(f_2 = b^2 - z^2\) with peak \(b^2 = 400\) mm\(^2\). Its level lines are segments across the width. This function is zero on the shorter sides \(z = \pm b\), but on the long sides \(y = \pm a\) it is not. The computation by equation (16.62) nevertheless gives \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f_2\,\mathrm{d}A = 2a\cdot\tfrac{4b^3}{3} = 213\,333\) mm\(^4\), \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f_2|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 2a\cdot\tfrac{8b^3}{3} = 426\,667\) mm\(^4\) and the quotient \(426\,667\) mm\(^4\), which is \(5{,}83\,J\). That is more than \(J\), so it is not a bound. Step 1 of the proof of lemma 16.61 has failed: the boundary term \(\oint f_2\,\partial\hat\Phi/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s\) on the long sides \(y = \pm a\) is not zero. We draw both factors over the \(z\) axis along the long side. There \(f_2\) is not zero, with its peak \(400\) mm\(^2\) at the middle, and \(\partial\hat\Phi/\partial n\) is not zero either: at the middle of the long side it is largest in absolute value, \(18{,}6\) mm (the shear stress is largest at the middle of the long side, section 16.5). Their product is negative everywhere and the area under it is \(-176\,744\) mm\(^4\) on one side, \(-353\,489\) mm\(^4\) on both. A computation without the series gives the same number: since \(\hat\Phi = 0\) at \(z = \pm b\), integration by parts in \(z\) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla f_2\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}}(-2z)\,\hat\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = J\) instead of \(2\int_{\mathrm{S}} f_2\,\mathrm{d}A = 426\,667\) mm\(^4\), and the difference \(73\,178 - 426\,667 = -353\,489\) mm\(^4\) is exactly the boundary term. The same failure happens for \(f = 1\): the gradient is zero and the quotient has no value, because the denominator vanishes, and at the same time \(f\) is not zero on the boundary. The lemma asks for both, zero on the whole boundary and nonzero energy.

For the upper bound we need trial warping functions. The rectangle is a single piece and lemma 16.17 holds for it. Try \(\tilde\psi = k\,yz\), where \(k\) is a number. As for the circle, \(\nabla\tilde\psi - \vek w = ((k - 1)z,\ (k + 1)y)\) and

\[F(k\,yz) = (k - 1)^2\int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A + (k + 1)^2\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad \int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{4a^3b}{3} = 26\,667 , \quad \int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac{4ab^3}{3} = 106\,667 \ \text{mm}^4 . \tag{16.63}\]

Over the \(k\) axis \(F\) is again a parabola, all of it above the line of the exact value \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\). At \(k = 0\) (no warping, the assumption of section 16.1) \(F = I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\), \(82{,}2\) % above \(J\). At \(k = 0{,}3\), \(F = 97\,333\) (\(+33{,}0\) %). The derivative with respect to \(k\) is zero at \(k^* = (\int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2 - \int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2)/I_{\mathrm{p}} = (b^2 - a^2)/(a^2 + b^2) = 0{,}600\), and then \(F = 4\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 85\,333\) mm\(^4\), \(16{,}6\) % above \(J\). The function \(k^*yz\) is exactly the warping function of the ellipse with semi-axes \(a\) and \(b\) (theorem 16.26, point 4). The reason: \(k^*\) depends only on the ratio \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} z^2\,\mathrm{d}A/\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\), which for the ellipse (\(\pi ab^3/4\) against \(\pi a^3b/4\)) is also \(b^2/a^2\), and for the ellipse the true warping function lies in the family \(k\,yz\), so by point 3 of lemma 16.59 the lowest point of the parabola hits it exactly. At \(k = 1\), \(F = 4\int_{\mathrm{S}} y^2 = \tfrac{16}{3}a^3b = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\). This is exactly the thin-strip value \(h\,t^3/3\) with \(h = 2b = 40\) mm and \(t = 2a = 20\) mm. So the function \(yz\) is the warping of the thin strip, and the upper estimate \(J < ht^3/3\) of theorem 16.41 is, for the rectangle, a consequence of lemma 16.59.

We put all the bounds on a number line [figure 16.86]. We draw the axis for \(J\) in units of \(10^3\) mm\(^4\) from \(60\) to \(140\), with marks every \(20\). On it sits the exact value \(J = 73{,}178\) from the series. To the left of it is the lower bound \(71{,}111\) from the function \(f\), \(2{,}82\) % below \(J\). To the right are the upper bounds: \(85{,}333\) from \(k = 0{,}6\), \(16{,}6\) % above \(J\), and also \(106{,}667\) (\(k = 1\)) and \(133{,}333\) (\(k = 0\)), which are worse. The gap between the two best bounds is \(19{,}4\) % of \(J\).

Why is the lower bound so much closer to the exact value than the best upper one? The difference is in the trial functions. The true warping function of the strip is by equation (16.42) \(yz\) minus a series that corrects \(yz\) near the shorter sides \(z = \pm b\). We draw this along the long side \(y = a = 10\) mm [figure 16.87]. Over the \(z\) axis from \(0\) to the corner at \(20\) mm is the true warping function \(\psi(a, z)\), computed with the series, and next to it the lines \(0{,}6\,yz\) and \(yz\). At \(z = 4\) mm, \(\psi = 34{,}0\) mm\(^2\), which is \(0{,}85\,yz\), while at \(z = 20\) mm it is \(91{,}8\) mm\(^2\), only \(0{,}46\,yz\): the true function bends down towards the shorter side. A line with a single number \(k\) cannot bend, so at \(k = 0{,}6\) it is too low in the middle (\(24\) instead of \(34{,}0\) mm\(^2\)) and too high at the shorter side (\(120\) instead of \(91{,}8\) mm\(^2\)). The trial function \(f\), on the other hand, has almost exactly the right shape across the width and lacks only the flat part along the length.

Theorem 16.63 Bounds of the torsion constant (meji torzijske konstante)

Let \(S\) be a section of lemma 16.61 and lemma 16.59. Then for every trial function \(f\) of lemma 16.61 and every trial function \(\tilde\psi\) of lemma 16.59

\[\frac{4\Big(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\Big)^2}{\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A} \ \le\ J\ \le\ F(\tilde\psi) . \tag{16.64}\]

For the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip (\(a = 10\) mm, \(b = 20\) mm) it therefore holds that

\[71\,111\ \text{mm}^4 \ \le\ J\ \le\ 85\,333\ \text{mm}^4 , \tag{16.65}\]

with \(f = (a^2 - y^2)(b^2 - z^2)\) and \(\tilde\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\). At \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m and \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa the twist per unit length \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ)\) is therefore between \(0{,}00777\) and \(0{,}00933\) rad/m.

Idea

The left inequality is lemma 16.61, the right one lemma 16.59. The numbers only have to be computed.

Proof
  1. The left inequality (16.64) is lemma 16.61, the right one lemma 16.59.
  2. For \(f\) on the strip: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f\,\mathrm{d}A = 14\,222\,222\) mm\(^6\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 1{,}1378\cdot10^{10}\) mm\(^8\) (above), the quotient is \(71\,111\) mm\(^4\).
  3. For \(\tilde\psi = 0{,}6\,yz\), by equation (16.63), \(F = 0{,}16\cdot106\,667 + 2{,}56\cdot26\,667 = 17\,067 + 68\,267 = 85\,333\) mm\(^4\), the upper bound at \(k = 0{,}6\).
  4. Twist: \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ)\) with \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\,000\) N mm and \(G = 75\,385\) N/mm\(^2\) equals \(7{,}77\cdot10^{-6}\) rad/mm \(= 0{,}00777\) rad/m at \(J = 85\,333\) mm\(^4\), and \(0{,}00933\) rad/m at \(J = 71\,111\) mm\(^4\). Both twists are below the axis.

Source: Inequality (16.64) and the numbers are ours (num-09.py). SADD in Example 16.4 (PDF pp. 535-536) solves Prandtl's equation with linear triangular elements, but gives no bounds for the torsion constant.

Below the axis are these twists in \(10^{-3}\) rad/m: the exact value \(9{,}06\) lies between \(7{,}77\) and \(9{,}33\). Since \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ)\) is inversely proportional to \(J\), the lower bound for \(J\) gives the upper bound for the twist and the upper one the lower bound. The gap of \(19{,}4\) % of \(J\) thus becomes a band of twists \(17{,}2\) % of the exact twist wide. For an engineer this is already useful: the shaft has a stiffness of at least \(G\cdot71\,111\ \text{mm}^4 = 5361\) N m\(^2\) and at most \(6433\) N m\(^2\), and for the torsional twist we get a bound that depends on no series.

16.9.4 Mesh of triangles

Two bounds on a section raise a question: how to find better trial functions when we do not know by shape where the true one is flat? The answer is the same as for the bar in chapter 7. We cut the section into pieces and write the function on every piece as a straight line, in the plane as a linear function. The pieces are triangles, because three values at the corners determine a linear function \(c_0 + c_1y + c_2z\) exactly.

For the \(20 \times 40\) mm strip we take a mesh. We cut the section into \(n \times 2n\) squares and each of them with one diagonal into two right isosceles triangles. The figure shows \(n = 2\): squares of side \(10\) mm, \(2\) across the width and \(4\) along the length, \(16\) triangles in all. The corners of the triangles are nodes (vozlišča). In the figure there are \(15\) of them, of which 12 on the boundary (open circles) and 3 free (filled circles), inside the section on the line \(y = 0\) at \(z = -10\), \(0\), \(10\) mm.

Definition 16.64 Triangle mesh and piecewise linear function (mreža trikotnikov in odsekoma linearna funkcija)

A triangle mesh (mreža trikotnikov) of the section \(S\) is a cut of \(S\) into finitely many triangles that meet along whole edges or at corners, and whose edges cover \(\partial S\). A piecewise linear function (odsekoma linearna funkcija) \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a continuous function on \(S\) that is linear on every triangle. It is determined by the values at the nodes, \(f_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}N_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(N_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the piecewise linear function with value \(1\) at node \(i\) and \(0\) at the other nodes (shape function, oblikovna funkcija).

Explanation

It is continuous because the edge shared by two triangles carries on both a linear function of one variable with equal values at the end points, hence the same function. The shape functions are the same as for the bar in chapter 7 (definition 7.2), only we have pieces of a plane instead of pieces of a line.

Source: The definition is ours. The linear triangle in its general form is in chapter 15.

First look at what one of the triangles does [figure 16.88]. The shaded triangle in the upper left corner of the mesh is drawn once more enlarged, turned so that its legs of length \(s\) lie along the axes \(\xi\) and \(\eta\). It has nodes 1, 2 and 3, node 1 at the right angle. Since the integral \(\int|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\) over it depends only on three values, we write it with a matrix that is a special case of the element matrix for heat conduction (chapter 14), because the operator is the same.

Lemma 16.65 Matrix of a right isosceles triangle (matrika enakokrakega pravokotnega trikotnika)

Let a triangle with legs of length \(s\) have node 1 at the right angle and nodes 2 and 3 on the legs. Let \(f_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^3\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}N_{\mathrm{i}}\) be linear on it. Then

\[\int|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\vek\varphi , \qquad \mat K_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{16.66}\]

independent of \(s\), and \(\int N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = s^2/6\) for all three nodes.

Idea

Gradients of linear functions are constant, so the integral is the area times the scalar product of the gradients.

Proof
  1. The scalar product \(\nabla N_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla N_{\mathrm{j}}\) does not change under a rotation of the coordinates (a rotation preserves scalar products). So we may take coordinates \(\xi\), \(\eta\) along the legs with node 1 at the origin, node 2 at \((s, 0)\) and node 3 at \((0, s)\).
  2. The linear functions with value \(1\) at their own node and \(0\) at the others are \(N_1 = 1 - \xi/s - \eta/s\), \(N_2 = \xi/s\), \(N_3 = \eta/s\). Their gradients \((-1, -1)/s\), \((1, 0)/s\) and \((0, 1)/s\) are constant: each points from the side on which its function is zero towards its own node.
  3. The area is \(A = s^2/2\). The entry \((\mat K_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = A\,\nabla N_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\nabla N_{\mathrm{j}}\) is for \(i = j = 1\) equal to \(\tfrac{s^2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{2}{s^2} = 1\), for \((1, 2)\) and \((1, 3)\) it is \(\tfrac{s^2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{-1}{s^2} = -\tfrac12\), for \((2, 2)\) and \((3, 3)\) it is \(\tfrac12\), for \((2, 3)\) it is \(0\). The length \(s\) cancels.
  4. The shape function of a linear triangle is its area coordinate, so by lemma 15.9 on the integral of powers of area coordinates \(\int N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = A/3\). With \(A = s^2/2\) this is \(s^2/6\).

Source: The computation is ours. We checked the matrix with \(s = 20\), \(10\) and \(5\) mm in NumPy (num-09.py): the difference from the matrix in equation (16.66) is zero. COOK (PDF p. 483) states that torsion, with the warping function or with Prandtl's stress function, is described by the same equation as plane steady heat conduction, for the warping function with \(k = 1\).

The row sum \(1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0\) says that a constant function costs nothing, as for a bar without supports (lemma 7.16): this property will matter at once. All triangles of the mesh have legs along \(y\) or \(z\) and differ only in which corner has the right angle. Each contributes its matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{e}}\) at the places of its three nodes in the global matrix \(\mat K\), as when assembling bars (theorem 7.13). The sum over all triangles gives \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi\).

Prandtl's function on the mesh

Take a piecewise linear \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) with value zero at all boundary nodes. Since the boundary of the section consists of triangle edges, \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is zero on the whole boundary too (a linear function with zero values at the ends of an edge is zero there). So \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is admissible in lemma 16.61. With the vector \(\vek b\) whose entry \(b_{\mathrm{i}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} N_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) is twice the integral of the shape function (from lemma 16.65), \(\int_{\mathrm{S}} f_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \tfrac12\vek b^\T\vek\varphi\) and the quotient of the lemma is \((\vek b^\T\vek\varphi)^2/(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K\vek\varphi)\). Its largest value among functions with a zero on the boundary is the solution of the system

\[\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\,\vek\varphi = \vek b_{\mathrm{p}} , \qquad J_{\mathrm{h}}^{\Phi} = \vek b_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\vek\varphi = \vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi , \tag{16.67}\]

where the index \(p\) marks only the free nodes. The matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\) is positive definite: if \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), then \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is constant and hence zero, since it is zero on the boundary. The largest value of the quotient is indeed \(\vek b_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\vek\varphi\), because for every other \(\vek\varphi'\) we have \((\vek b_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\vek\varphi')^2 = (\vek\varphi'^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi)^2 \le (\vek\varphi'^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi')(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi)\) (the same computation with a square as in the proof of lemma 16.61, with \(\int|\nabla\cdot|^2\) replaced by \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi\)). For a node in the interior, the six triangles around it (at the right angle in two of them, at an acute angle in four) add up to \(K_{\mathrm{ii}} = 2\cdot1 + 4\cdot\tfrac12 = 4\), each of the four neighbours on legs gives \(-1\), and the neighbours on the diagonal give \(0\). Let us look at \(n = 2\).

For \(n = 2\) every free node has six triangles of area \(50\) mm\(^2\) (those around the middle one are shaded), so \(b_{\mathrm{i}} = 2\cdot6\cdot50/3 = 200\) mm\(^2\). The neighbours to the left and right lie on the boundary, where the value is zero, so the equations at the three free nodes have the form

\[4\varphi_1 - \varphi_2 = 200 , \qquad -\varphi_1 + 4\varphi_2 - \varphi_3 = 200 , \qquad -\varphi_2 + 4\varphi_3 = 200 . \tag{16.68}\]

From the symmetry \(\varphi_1 = \varphi_3\) and from the first equation \(\varphi_2 = 4\varphi_1 - 200\), into the second: \(-2\varphi_1 + 16\varphi_1 - 800 = 200\), so \(\varphi_1 = 71{,}43\) and \(\varphi_2 = 85{,}71\) mm\(^2\) (these are the numbers beside the free nodes in the figure). The true value at the centre is \(91{,}1\) mm\(^2\). The lower bound is \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi = 200\,(71{,}43 + 85{,}71 + 71{,}43) = 45\,714\) mm\(^4\), \(37{,}5\) % below \(J\).

Warping function on the mesh

For the upper bound take a piecewise linear \(\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{h}}\) with values \(\vek\psi\) at all nodes, since there is no condition on the boundary. By equation (16.59), \(F(\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{h}}) = \vek\psi^\T\mat K\vek\psi - 2\vek\psi^\T\vek b_{\mathrm{w}} + I_{\mathrm{p}}\), where \((b_{\mathrm{w}})_{\mathrm{i}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla N_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A\). The field \(\vek w = (z, -y)\) is linear and \(\nabla N_{\mathrm{i}}\) is constant on a triangle, so the contribution of a triangle is the area times the value at the centroid (a linear function \(g = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} g_{\mathrm{i}}N_{\mathrm{i}}\) has by step 4 of lemma 16.65 the integral \(A\,(g_1 + g_2 + g_3)/3\), which is \(A\) times the value at the centroid). At \(\mat K\vek\psi = \vek b_{\mathrm{w}}\) the smallest value is \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \vek\psi^\T\vek b_{\mathrm{w}} = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \vek\psi^\T\mat K\vek\psi\), because for any other vector \(\vek\psi + \vek d\) the value of \(F\) is larger by \(\vek d^\T\mat K\vek d = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla d_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A \ge 0\), where \(d_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the piecewise linear function with the values \(\vek d\).

Example 16.66 The warping matrix without a fixed node is singular (matrika zvitja brez pritrjenega vozlišča je singularna)

For \(n = 2\) take all \(15\) nodes as free. The row sums of \(\mat K\) are zero (step 3 of lemma 16.65), so \(\mat K\) has the eigenvector \((1, \dots, 1)\) with eigenvalue zero: NumPy gives the smallest eigenvalue \(2{,}2\cdot10^{-17}\) and the second \(0{,}250\). The matrix is singular, so the system \(\mat K\vek\psi = \vek b_{\mathrm{w}}\) has no unique solution. This is not an error of the mesh: the warping function is determined up to a constant, as in lemma 16.19, and \(\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{h}} + c\) has the same energy. At the same time \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}(b_{\mathrm{w}})_{\mathrm{i}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}})\cdot\vek w\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), because \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} N_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) and the gradient of a constant is zero, so the system is solvable. We fix one node, the lower left one (the square in the middle figure), \(\psi_1 = 0\), and the matrix becomes positive definite: from \(\vek\psi^\T\mat K\vek\psi = 0\) it follows that \(\nabla\tilde\psi_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\), so a constant function, zero at the fixed node. The result is \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi = 87\,619\) mm\(^4\). If we fix the node at the centre or at the opposite corner \((a, b)\) instead, \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi\) is the same (\(87\,619\)), and the two fields differ only by a constant (the largest difference after subtracting the mean is below \(10^{-12}\) mm\(^2\), which is the rounding error of the computation). Without fixing, a program reports a singular matrix, with fixing the answer does not depend on the choice of node.

The example answers a question every program must have: which node do we fix? The answer is any, because \(F\) does not feel a constant. For Prandtl's function this question does not arise, because the boundary is already fixed by the zero.

Corollary 16.67 Bounds from a triangle mesh (meje iz mreže trikotnikov)

Let the section \(S\) be a polygon that meets the assumptions of lemmas 16.59 and 16.61, and \(\mathcal{T}\) a triangle mesh (definition 16.64). Then

\[J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\ \le\ J\ \le\ J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi , \tag{16.69}\]

where \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) is the solution of equation (16.67) and \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi = I_{\mathrm{p}} - \vek\psi^\T\mat K\vek\psi\) for \(\mat K\vek\psi = \vek b_{\mathrm{w}}\). If \(\mathcal{T}'\) is obtained from \(\mathcal{T}\) so that every triangle is a union of triangles of \(\mathcal{T}'\), then \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi(\mathcal{T}) \le J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi(\mathcal{T}')\) and \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi(\mathcal{T}') \le J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi(\mathcal{T})\).

Idea

The bounds are lemma 16.61 and lemma 16.59 with piecewise linear functions. A finer mesh has more functions, so the maximum is larger and the minimum smaller.

Proof
  1. A piecewise linear function with zero values at the boundary nodes is zero on the boundary, continuous and continuously differentiable on every triangle, so admissible in lemma 16.61. By equation (16.67), \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) is the largest value of the quotient over all such functions, so by the lemma \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi \le J\).
  2. Every piecewise linear function is admissible in lemma 16.59, and \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi\) is the smallest \(F\) over all these functions, so by the lemma \(J \le J_{\mathrm{h}}^\psi\).
  3. If every triangle of \(\mathcal{T}\) is a union of triangles of \(\mathcal{T}'\), every function linear on the triangles of \(\mathcal{T}\) is also linear on the smaller ones, and continuity is kept. The space of functions for \(\mathcal{T}\) is thus a part of the space for \(\mathcal{T}'\). A maximum over a larger set is not smaller, a minimum over a larger set is not larger.

Stress from the nodal values

The torsion constant says by how much the shaft twists. Whether the shaft holds is said by the largest shear stress, and that the mesh has not given us yet: the system (16.67) returns only the values \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the nodes. How we get from them to the stress we show on the meshes \(n = 2\) and \(n = 4\) of the strip [figure 16.90].

By equation (16.28) the stress is a derivative of Prandtl's function, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial\Phi/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\partial\Phi/\partial y\). By lemma 16.28 on the stress along the level lines this means that the stress flows along the level lines of the hill \(\Phi\) and is as large as the hill is steep. The piecewise linear function \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a flat piece of surface over every triangle of the mesh, like one side of a roof. A flat piece has the same slope everywhere. So every triangle gets one single stress, which we draw as an arrow at its centroid.

Lemma 16.69 Stress on a triangle of the mesh (napetost na trikotniku mreže)

Let \(f_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}N_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the piecewise linear function from the solution of the system (16.67) on a mesh of triangles (definition 16.64) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}} = G\alpha\,f_{\mathrm{h}}\) the trial Prandtl function. Then:

  1. The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\alpha\,\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -G\alpha\,\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial y\) are constant on every triangle. The size of the stress is \(\tau_{\mathrm{h}} = G\alpha\,|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|\), its direction is perpendicular to \(\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}\).
  2. On a right isosceles triangle with legs of length \(s\), node 1 at the right angle, node 2 on the leg along \(\xi\) and node 3 on the leg along \(\eta\), \(\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial\xi = (\varphi_2 - \varphi_1)/s\) and \(\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial\eta = (\varphi_3 - \varphi_1)/s\).
  3. The moment of this stress field is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G\alpha\,J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\). For a given moment therefore \(G\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\).
Idea

The derivative of a linear function is constant. Along a leg it equals the difference of the nodal values divided by the length of the leg.

Proof
  1. On a triangle \(f_{\mathrm{h}} = \varphi_1N_1 + \varphi_2N_2 + \varphi_3N_3\) is linear, so its two derivatives are constant. The stresses follow from equation (16.28) with \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}} = G\alpha f_{\mathrm{h}}\). The size is \(\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2} = G\alpha\,|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|\). The scalar product of the stress with the gradient is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), as in lemma 16.28.
  2. The gradients of the shape functions from step 2 of lemma 16.65 are \(\nabla N_1 = (-1, -1)/s\), \(\nabla N_2 = (1, 0)/s\) and \(\nabla N_3 = (0, 1)/s\). So \(\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}} = \big((\varphi_2 - \varphi_1)/s,\ (\varphi_3 - \varphi_1)/s\big)\) in the coordinates \(\xi\), \(\eta\).
  3. The derivation of the moment (16.32) in theorem 16.24 uses lemma 16.17 for the field \(\Phi\,(y, z)\) and the zero on the boundary. For \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) we use it on every triangle. The field \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\,(y, z)\) is continuous, so in the sum the contributions of a shared side cancel as in step 4 of the proof of lemma 16.59, and on the boundary of the section \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\). So \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\cdot2\int_{\mathrm{S}} f_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}A = G\alpha\,\vek b_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\vek\varphi = G\alpha\,J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) by equation (16.67).

Source: The computation is ours, with the equations of section 16.3. SADD in example 16.4 (PDF p. 535) reads the stress from a mesh of linear triangles in the same way: the slope of the level lines of the stress function gives the shear stress in the direction perpendicular to the slope.

The lemma stands on two assumptions, and both cost something. The first: over a triangle \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) is flat. For the trial function this is true by definition, but the true hill is curved. One slope per triangle is therefore an approximation, the worse the larger the triangle and the more the hill is curved over it. On the mesh \(n = 2\) it costs us more than half of the largest stress (\(8{,}57\) instead of \(18{,}60\) mm, the computation follows). The second: we get \(G\alpha\) from \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) and not from \(J\), because for a section without a series we do not have the exact \(J\). Since \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi \le J\) (corollary 16.67), such a \(G\alpha\) is too large: on the mesh \(n = 2\) by \(60\) % (\(73\,178/45\,714 = 1{,}60\)), on the mesh \(n = 16\) by \(0{,}72\) %. The stress field from \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) is in equilibrium across the sides of the triangles too. The component of the stress perpendicular to a side is namely the derivative of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) along the side, and that is the same for both neighbours because \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) is continuous. But no warping function can be found for such a field, so it is an approximation and not a solution.

Let us compute the stresses on the mesh \(n = 2\). Take the triangle at the middle of the left long side with nodes \((-10, 0)\), \((0, 0)\) and \((-10, -10)\) mm. The right angle is at \((-10, 0)\) on the boundary, where the value is zero. The leg along \(y\) leads to the middle free node \((0, 0)\) with the value \(\varphi_2 = 85{,}71\) mm\(^2\), the leg along \(z\) to the boundary node \((-10, -10)\) with the value zero. By item 2 of the lemma

\[\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}}{\partial y} = \frac{85{,}71 - 0}{10} = 8{,}571\ \text{mm} , \qquad \frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}}{\partial z} = \frac{0 - 0}{-10} = 0 .\]

So the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -8{,}571\,G\alpha\) mm. The arrow in this triangle points along the long side, as it must next to a free boundary, at the scale in which the arrow at the side measures \(10\) mm. The exact largest stress is by equation (16.41) \(k_\tau\,G\alpha\) with \(k_\tau = 18{,}60\) mm, at the middle of the long side. The mesh gives \(8{,}57\) mm, which is \(53{,}9\) % too little. In megapascals at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, item 3 of the lemma gives \(G\alpha = 50\,000/45\,714 = 1{,}094\) N/mm\(^3\) (exact \(0{,}683\)) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{h}} = 1{,}094\cdot8{,}571 = 9{,}37\) MPa against the exact \(12{,}71\) MPa, \(26{,}2\) % too little. The too large \(G\alpha\) hides a part of the error of the slope.

And where does the mesh \(n = 2\) have its own largest stress? Not there. It is largest in the triangles at the shorter sides, for example in the one with nodes \((0, -10)\), \((10, -10)\) and \((0, -20)\) mm. The right angle is at the free node \((0, -10)\) with the value \(\varphi_1 = 71{,}43\) mm\(^2\), the other two nodes are on the boundary. The derivatives are \(\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial y = (0 - 71{,}43)/10 = -7{,}143\) mm and \(\partial f_{\mathrm{h}}/\partial z = (0 - 71{,}43)/(-10) = 7{,}143\) mm, the size is \(7{,}143\sqrt2 = 10{,}10\) mm. The exact value at the centroid of this triangle is \(6{,}19\) mm, so the mesh is \(63\) % too high here. In the corner triangles all three nodes are on the boundary, so \(f_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\) and the stress is zero, while the exact value at the centroid is \(7{,}21\) mm. A mesh with three unknowns therefore tells only the order of magnitude of the stress, and puts the largest one in the wrong place. On the mesh \(n = 4\) the picture is already right: the arrows circle around the centre like level lines, and the largest stress \(13{,}55\) mm is in the elements at the middle of the long sides, where the exact largest one is too. It is still \(27{,}2\) % too small.

Why is the stress so much worse than \(J\)? Cut the hill across the width at \(z = 0\) [figure 16.91].

Over the \(y\) axis we draw the exact hill \(\hat\Phi\) with peak \(91{,}1\) mm\(^2\) and under it the roof of the mesh \(n = 2\): two flat sides from the boundary to the peak \(85{,}7\) mm\(^2\). A side of the roof has the slope \(8{,}57\) mm everywhere, the height \(85{,}7\) mm\(^2\) over the element width \(h = 10\) mm. The hill, however, is curved. At the boundary it is steepest, the tangent at \(y = 10\) mm has the slope \(18{,}60\) mm. Towards the middle it flattens: at the middle of the element, at \(y = 5\) mm, the slope is only \(9{,}01\) mm. The side of the roof joins the values at the two ends of the element. If these two values were exact, its slope would be the mean of the slopes of the hill over the whole element, the height of the top \(91{,}1\) mm\(^2\) divided by the width \(10\) mm, which is \(9{,}11\) mm, close to the slope of the hill at the middle of the element and not at the boundary. The nodal value \(85{,}7\) mm\(^2\) is a little below the exact one, so the side of the roof is a little flatter still. But the stress is largest exactly at the boundary.

How much is missing is told by the curvature of the hill. On the long side \(\hat\Phi = 0\) for every \(z\), so \(\partial^2\hat\Phi/\partial z^2 = 0\) there, and the equation \(\nabla^2\hat\Phi = -2\) leaves \(\partial^2\hat\Phi/\partial y^2 = -2\). So near the boundary the slope of the tangent decreases by \(2\) mm for every millimetre of the way inwards. To the middle of the element the way is \(h/2\), and the slope there is smaller by about \(2\cdot h/2 = h\). The shortfall is about equal to the width of the element, and the table confirms it: \(10{,}03\), \(5{,}06\), \(2{,}52\), \(1{,}26\) and \(0{,}63\) mm for \(h = 10\), \(5\), \(2{,}5\), \(1{,}25\) and \(0{,}625\) mm. When we halve \(h\), the error of the stress halves. This is an observation with an explanation, not a theorem: the nodal values are not exact and the curvature over the element is not constant. The roof of the mesh \(n = 4\) already fits the hill better, its boundary side has the slope \(13{,}54\) mm.

For \(J\) it is different. Look at how much the slope \(\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}\) differs from the true \(\nabla\hat\Phi\), added up over the whole section:

  1. We expand the square of the difference: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\hat\Phi - \nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\hat\Phi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A - 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A + \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  2. The first term is \(J\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 16.61).
  3. In the middle term \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}\cdot\nabla\hat\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}} f_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (step 1 of the same proof with \(f = f_{\mathrm{h}}\)), and that is \(\vek b_{\mathrm{p}}^\T\vek\varphi = J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\). So the middle term is \(-2J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\).
  4. The last term is \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{pp}}\vek\varphi = J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) (equation (16.67)).

The sum \(J - 2J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi + J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) gives

\[J - J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\hat\Phi - \nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}|^2\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{16.70}\]

Let us draw the equation [figure 16.92], as we drew the error of the bar in figure 6.63. On functions with a zero on the boundary, \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}A\) is a scalar product, since \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla u|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) implies that \(u\) is constant and hence zero. Steps 3 and 4 give \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{h}}\cdot(\nabla\hat\Phi - \nabla f_{\mathrm{h}})\,\mathrm{d}A = J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi - J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi = 0\): the roof \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) and its error \(\hat\Phi - f_{\mathrm{h}}\) are orthogonal in this product. The hypotenuse from the origin \(O\) to the point \(\hat\Phi\) has the length \(\sqrt J = 270{,}5\) mm\(^2\). The blue leg from \(O\) to the point \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the mesh \(n = 2\) measures \(\sqrt{J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi} = 213{,}8\) mm\(^2\), the orange leg from \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) to \(\hat\Phi\) measures \(\sqrt{J - J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi} = 165{,}7\) mm\(^2\). The angle between the legs is a right angle. The Pythagorean theorem (lemma 6.21) for this triangle is equation (16.70), and since a leg is shorter than the hypotenuse, \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi \le J\).

Every mesh gives its own point \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) with a right angle, so all of them lie on the Thales semicircle over the hypotenuse. The triangle of each mesh lies in its own plane, only the hypotenuse is common to them. We drew them in one plane, the lengths are true. For \(n = 4\) and \(n = 8\) the point moves towards \(\hat\Phi\) and the orange leg shortens to \(88{,}8\) and \(45{,}4\) mm\(^2\). The bars on the right show the same lengths squared. Each is \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) high, the blue part is \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) (\(45\,714\), \(65\,284\) and \(71\,113\) mm\(^4\)), the orange part is \(J - J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) (\(27\,464\), \(7\,894\) and \(2\,065\) mm\(^4\)).

The error of the torsion constant is the sum of the squares of the error of the slope over the section (with the same reservation at the corners as lemma 16.61). We checked this by adding up over small triangles: for \(n = 2\) the right side gives \(27\,459\) mm\(^4\), the left \(73\,178 - 45\,714 = 27\,464\) mm\(^4\). If the error of the slope halves, its square falls to a quarter. The mean error of the slope over the section of area \(800\) mm\(^2\) is \(\sqrt{(J - J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi)/800}\), which is \(5{,}86\), \(3{,}14\) and \(1{,}61\) mm for \(n = 2\), \(4\) and \(8\), each time about half of the previous one. So the error of \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) falls about four times per doubling, the error of the stress twice. On the right of figure 16.91 both errors stand over the \(n\) axis: the error of the stress falls from \(53{,}9\) to \(3{,}37\) %, the error of \(J_{\mathrm{h}}^\Phi\) along a line twice as steep from \(37{,}5\) to \(0{,}18\) %. For finite elements this is the usual case: a quantity that we get by differentiating the solution is typically less accurate than the solution itself (COOK, PDF p. 382, on stresses from displacement gradients and heat flux from temperatures).

What does an engineer do with this? Three things. He refines the mesh where the stress is largest, not everywhere. He does not read the largest stress from the printout without looking at which element it lies in, since the mesh \(n = 2\) put it in the wrong place. And he knows that the stress from the element at the boundary is too small by about \(h\). The corrected value \(8{,}57 + 10 = 18{,}57\) mm on the mesh \(n = 2\) is only \(0{,}16\) % below the exact \(18{,}60\) mm, on the mesh \(n = 4\) it is \(13{,}54 + 5 = 18{,}54\) mm (\(-0{,}33\) %). This correction holds only next to a straight boundary on which \(\hat\Phi = 0\), because we derived the curvature \(-2\) from that.

From history

Chapter 6 said in section 6.7 that in a paper of 1943, the written form of a lecture of 1941, Courant divided the cross-section of a hollow shaft into triangles and interpolated the stress function on each by straight lines from the values at the nodes, to compute the torsional rigidity of the shaft (COOK, PDF p. 24). The stress function of torsion is in section 16.3 Prandtl's function, so Courant's interpolation by straight lines on triangles is exactly the piecewise linear function \(f_{\mathrm{h}}\) of this section, and its value at the nodes is the unknown of system (16.67). Courant treated a hollow shaft, where on a hole there is one more condition (section 16.8), which this section does not include.

Let us sum up what we have seen, because this section leans on several chapters. The warping function gives an upper bound of \(J\) (lemma 16.59, the principle of minimum potential energy), Prandtl's function a lower one (lemma 16.61, the principle of minimum complementary energy), both from arbitrary continuous functions, with no boundary conditions for \(\tilde\psi\) and with a zero on the boundary for \(f\). On a mesh of triangles both bounds become linear systems with the same matrix \(\mat K\), as for heat conduction in chapter 14 with source \(2\) and zero temperature on the boundary: the operator is the same, the phenomenon is not. The upper bound needs one node fixed, the lower does not. The stress from the mesh is the slope of \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) on a triangle (lemma 16.69), its error falls with the element size \(h\), the error of \(J\) with \(h^2\) (equation (16.70)). The one step that remained unproved is for the rectangle at the corners (lemma 16.61), and the numbers of the series and of the meshes agree with the two bounds.
