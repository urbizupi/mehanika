Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.1 The moving string (gibajoča struna)

A magnetic tape runs past a head, a band saw runs over two wheels, a cable car pulls its cable over two pylons, a fluid flows through a pipe, a chain and a belt run over pulleys. What they share is that matter flows along its own length and at the same time can oscillate sideways (WM1990, PDF p. 1, read). We call such a system an axially moving continuum (aksialno gibajoč se kontinuum): axially means in the direction of the axis, and a continuum is a continuous system from chapter 13. The chapter treats belts, so we take the simplest part of a belt, the span (razpon) between two pulleys, and write it as a string that runs. The question of this section is simple: does the wave equation of section 13.1 hold for such a span, or must it be changed, and how does it change?

From history

The methods for axially moving materials reach back into the nineteenth century, which WM1990 (PDF p. 1) says by naming Skutch. The text on page 1 gives the year 1987 there, which is a printing error: on the image of the page 1987 is indeed printed, but the reference list of the same paper (PDF p. 6) and the reference list of CHEN2005 (PDF p. 21) give 1897 for the same work, published in Annalen der Physik und Chemie on pages 190 to 195. The title of the work is German and in free translation reads the motion of a taut thread that is forced to run at constant speed through two fixed points and between them oscillates sideways with small amplitude (WM1990, PDF p. 6, CHEN2005, PDF p. 21). This is exactly the model of this section. According to CHEN2005 (PDF p. 2) Skutch was the first to study a moving string without excitation.

Let us return to the span between the pulleys. The story told what the model is: a taut thread that runs through two fixed points. Here we build it and derive the equation ourselves, on a steel band that we choose as the running example.

We draw the span in several steps in figure 26.1. First we take the axis \(x\) and on it two eyelets at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). An eyelet is a small ring through which the thread runs, as a thread runs through the eye of a needle, and the word means a small eye. The eyelets stand in space and the string runs through them. In this way we replace the pulleys: the span is clamped at a pulley, so the transverse displacement there is zero, and what happens to the belt on the pulley itself (wrapping, friction) the model does not ask about (idealization 6 below). We draw the string blackwhite: at rest it is a straight line along the axis \(x\), and during oscillation it is a curve \(w(x, t)\), where \(w\) is the transverse displacement of the point at \(x\) at time \(t\), as in section 13.1. The slope in the figure is strongly exaggerated so that the curve can be seen. Outside the eyelets the string runs on along the axis: the arrows \(v\) show that it enters on the left and leaves on the right, and all points of the string move along the axis \(x\) with speed \(v > 0\) in the direction of increasing \(x\). The number \(v\) in m/s we call the transport speed (hitrost transporta). In addition the string moves transversely in \(w\).

Now we can say what it is that moves. On the string we imagine a mark, like a coloured line on a thread: a material particle (točka snovi) is a piece of the string that we follow as it runs. Two dots at \(a\) and \(b\) at time \(t\) are two such particles, and the blue part of the string between them is a material piece (kos snovi): the same pieces of matter all the time, wherever they are. The lower row of the figure shows time \(t + \tau\). The material particles have moved by \(v\tau\) along the axis \(x\), since they run with speed \(v\), and the material piece now lies over the interval from \(a + v\tau\) to \(b + v\tau\). We drew the shape of the curve at \(t + \tau\) the same only to make the shift of matter visible, in real motion the shape changes too. The neighbouring parts pull the piece with force \(T\) along the tangent, each force starting at the point where it acts, as in section 13.1. The forces are drawn at both times.

The model has eight idealizations (idealizacije). We name each and give it a measure. The example is a steel band that we choose ourselves: thickness \(0{,}9\) mm, width \(50\) mm, with span length \(L = 1\) m and stress \(\sigma = 100\) MPa. Bands are found on band saws (WM1990, PDF p. 1), the dimensions, stress and speeds are our choice, we have no data on real belts (the project sources contain none). The cross-section is \(A = 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-5}\) m\(^2\), the density of steel \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) and the elastic modulus \(E = 196\) GPa (BOWER, table 3.1, PDF p. 100), so the mass per unit length is \(\mu = \rho A = 0{,}3510\) kg/m and the tension is \(T = \sigma A = 4500\) N.

  1. The string is homogeneous: \(\mu\) is constant. Since all points of the string move along \(x\) with the same speed, the spacing between them along \(x\) does not change, so the mass per unit length stays \(\mu\) also in the running flow (WM1990, PDF p. 1: the axial speed is approximately \(V\)).
  2. The string has no bending stiffness, so the force in it is along the tangent. For the band \(EI = E\,b h^3/12 = 0{,}595\) N m\(^2\) and the ratio \(EI/(T L^2) = 1{,}3\cdot 10^{-4}\), which is an order-of-magnitude estimate and is small. We include bending stiffness later in this chapter, with the moving beam.
  3. The tension \(T\) is constant along the span and in time. When the string moves it stretches and the tension changes. For our band, \(EA = 8{,}82\) MN, the strain at slope \(5\cdot 10^{-3}\) is at most \(1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the tension changes by \(EA\cdot 1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5} = 110\) N, which is \(2{,}4\) % of \(T\), and the wave speed by \(1{,}2\) %. For the wire of section 13.1 the estimate at slope \(0{,}01\) was \(1{,}8\) %. The tension in a real belt depends also on the speed and on friction at the pulleys, about which the project sources have no data: \(T\) is here a given number (CHEN2005, PDF p. 2, assumption 2).
  4. The slope is small, \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}005\) (stricter than \(0{,}01\) for the wire, because the band is much stiffer under tension). The tangent then makes an angle with the axis \(x\) of less than \(0{,}29^\circ\).
  5. The axial speed of every material particle is \(v\), constant and equal for all (WM1990, PDF p. 1). If a particle ran along the arc with the same speed, the component along \(x\) would be smaller by at most a relative \(1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5}\) at slope \(0{,}005\), which we neglect.
  6. The eyelets are fixed in space, \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\). The connection of the span with the pulley (wrapping, friction, oscillation of the pulley) is neglected (CHEN2005, §2.4.2, PDF p. 4).
  7. Weight is neglected: the weight of the span \(\mu g L = 3{,}4\) N is \(7{,}6\cdot 10^{-4}\) of the tension force.
  8. The transport speed \(v\) is constant in time. We require only \(v > 0\). The ratio between \(v\) and the wave speed \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) (definition 13.4) we shall restrict only where it is needed.

For the example \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{\sigma/\rho} = 113{,}23\) m/s. The ratio \(v/c\) at \(v = 40\) m/s is \(0{,}353\). We write this ratio simply as \(v/c\), with no new letter: WM1990 denotes it by \(\nu\), which in this book means Poisson's ratio, and CHEN2005 uses the letter \(c\) for the transport speed, which with us means the wave speed. So we keep \(v\) for the transport speed, \(c\) for the wave speed, \(T\) for the tension force (WM1990 writes \(P\)) and \(\mu\) for the mass per length (WM1990 writes \(\rho\)). In the chapter on beams (5) \(v\) meant the deflection, here the deflection is \(w\), as in section 13.1. The letter \(T\) is not the kinetic energy of chapter 9, we shall write the kinetic energy differently when we need it.

What remains from section 13.1? There the acceleration of a point of the string was simply \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), because the point stayed at the same \(x\). A material particle of the moving string does not stay at the same \(x\). How large its acceleration is, is shown by figure 26.2, which we build in three panels. In all three the same string at the same time, bulging upward, and on it one material particle, the blackwhite dot at \(x_0\).

In panel (a) we ask: what would be the acceleration of a point that stayed at the same \(x\), as in section 13.1? The string moves in time \(\tau\) and the dashed curve is its position at time \(t + \tau\). The point at constant \(x\) would rise, its acceleration is \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), called the local acceleration (krajevni pospešek), because it is the acceleration at a fixed place. The arrow \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is drawn upward for the case where the curve rises ever faster. That the curve rises says only that the velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\) is positive, the acceleration points upward when this velocity grows. In panel (b) we take the point to run. The tangent to the string at it has slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), and since the shape changes, the slope changes with rate \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), the tangent turns like a rod. The point runs along the tangent with speed \(v\), like a bead on a turning rod. A bead on a rod that turns with angular velocity \(\Omega\) and runs along it with speed \(v\) relative to the rod has, besides other terms, an acceleration \(2\Omega v\) perpendicular to the rod (Coriolis acceleration, Coriolisov pospešek), as school physics knows it. At small slope the angular velocity of the tangent is \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), so the acceleration is \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), drawn upward, because the slope grows. In panel (c) the point runs along a curved curve. The circle that fits the curve best at this point has by definition 5.16 radius \(R = 1/|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\), and whoever runs along a circle with speed \(v\) has acceleration \(v^2/R\) toward the centre of the circle (school physics, uniform circular motion). That is why this term is called the centripetal acceleration (centripetalni pospešek): Latin centrum means centre and petere to seek, so an acceleration that seeks the centre. At small slope the arrow toward the centre is almost vertical and equal to \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The curve bulges upward, \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} < 0\), so it points downward.

The above are explanations from the figure and school physics. The exact computation with the chain rule is given by the lemma that follows.

Lemma 26.1 Velocity and acceleration of a material particle of the moving string (hitrost in pospešek točke snovi gibajoče strune)

Let \(v > 0\) and let \(w\) be a function on \([0, L] \times [0, \infty)\) with continuous partial derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{tx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) (definition 1.25). Let \(s\) be a number and let the material particle that at time \(t\) is at \(x_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = s + vt\) lie in \((0, L)\) during an open interval of times. Then its transverse displacement is \(W(t) = w(s + vt, t)\), its transverse velocity is

\[\dot W = w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}} \tag{26.1}\]

and its transverse acceleration is

\[\ddot W = w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} , \tag{26.2}\]

both at the point \((s + vt, t)\). The terms \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\), \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are in turn the local, the Coriolis and the centripetal acceleration.

Proof

We must show the first and second derivative of the displacement along the path of the material particle \((s + vt, t)\). We get each with the chain rule, the second by applying the same rule to the velocity.

  1. The material particle is at time \(t\) at \(x_{\mathrm{p}}(t) = s + vt\) by idealization 5, so \(x_{\mathrm{p}}' = v\). Its displacement is \(W(t) = w(x_{\mathrm{p}}(t), t)\), which is \(w\) along the path \(\Phi(t) = (x_{\mathrm{p}}(t), t)\), whose derivative is \((v, 1)\).
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, point 1, where \(D\) is an interval) \(\dot W = w_{\mathrm{x}}\,v + w_{\mathrm{t}}\). This is equation (26.1).
  3. The function \(g = w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\) has continuous derivatives \(g_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{tx}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(g_{\mathrm{t}} = w_{\mathrm{tt}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and \(\dot W(t) = g(x_{\mathrm{p}}(t), t)\). The same rule applied to \(g\) gives \(\ddot W = g_{\mathrm{x}}\,v + g_{\mathrm{t}} = v\,w_{\mathrm{tx}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} + w_{\mathrm{tt}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\).
  4. The mixed derivatives are continuous at every point, so by the theorem on the interchange of mixed derivatives (theorem 8.30) \(w_{\mathrm{tx}} = w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and from step 3 equation (26.2) follows.

Source: WM1990, PDF pp. 1 and 2: the transverse velocity is \(U_{\mathrm{T}} + V U_{\mathrm{X}}\) (PDF p. 1), the terms of the acceleration are in turn the local, the Coriolis and the centripetal (PDF p. 2). CHEN2005, PDF p. 2 (the same description). The proof by steps is not from the source.

The acceleration therefore has three terms, not one, as in panel (a) of figure 26.2. The computation has confirmed the explanations of panels (b) and (c): the second term is the Coriolis one, the third the centripetal one.

Let us check the lemma on numbers and on figure 26.2. Let \(v = 40\) m/s, which is \(v/c = 0{,}353\), and let

\[w_*(x, t) = \varepsilon\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,\cos(\omega t + k x) , \qquad \varepsilon = 1\ \text{mm} , \quad \omega = \frac{\pi c}{L}\Big(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Big) = 311{,}32\ \text{1/s} , \quad k = \frac{\pi v}{c L} = 1{,}110\ \text{1/m} . \tag{26.3}\]

We chose the function so that it will be a motion of the span. Why exactly these \(\omega\) and \(k\), the section on the modes of the moving string shows later in the chapter, here it is only a test function. At the point \(x = 0{,}3\) m at \(t = 0\), \(w_* = 0{,}765\) mm and \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = 1{,}452\cdot 10^{-3}\). By the lemma the three terms of the acceleration are \(-74{,}10\), \(-36{,}17\) and \(-15{,}72\) m/s\(^2\), so the transverse acceleration of the material particle there is \(-126{,}0\) m/s\(^2\) (SymPy), and not only \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -74{,}10\) m/s\(^2\). The largest values over time and \(x\) of the terms are \(97{,}0\), \(78{,}2\) and \(17{,}8\) m/s\(^2\), so the Coriolis term reaches \(81\) % of the local one, and the centripetal \(18\) %. At \(v/c = 0{,}353\) we therefore must not neglect them: the Coriolis term is almost equal to the local one (panels (a) and (b) of figure 26.2).

Now we can write the equation of motion of the material piece. The law of motion of a body (definition 8.70), integrated over the points of the piece, says that the resultant of the forces on the piece equals the integral of the mass density times the acceleration. The piece of section 13.1 was over an interval that did not move. Now the material piece is over an interval that moves with speed \(v\), as the lower row of figure 26.1 shows, so in the law we use the acceleration from the lemma.

Derivation 26.2 Equation of motion of a material piece of the moving string (gibalna enačba kosa snovi gibajoče strune)

Goal: for a material piece that at time \(t\) is over the interval \([A, B] \subset (0, L)\), \(A < B\), to write the law of motion in the transverse direction. The assumptions are idealizations 1 to 8 and \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}005\) on \([A, B]\).

1
\[\text{material piece} = \{\text{material particles } s \in [a, b]\} , \qquad a = A - vt , \quad b = B - vt , \qquad m = \mu\,(b - a) .\]
Hint

The material particle with label \(s\) is at time \(t\) at \(x = s + vt\) (idealization 5), so the material piece with labels from \(a\) to \(b\) lies over \([a + vt, b + vt] = [A, B]\) at time \(t\), and over \([a + v t', b + v t']\) at time \(t'\) (upper and lower row of figure 26.1). All particles run with the same speed, so the length of the piece along \(x\) does not change, it stays \(b - a\), and the mass is by idealization 1 constant: \(m = \mu\,(b - a)\).

2
\[F_{\text{navp}} = T\,\big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(B, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(A, t)\big) .\]
Hint

The forces of the neighbouring parts are by idealization 2 along the tangent, by idealization 3 of magnitude \(T\), so the sum of the vertical components, \(T\sin\theta_{\mathrm{B}} - T\sin\theta_{\mathrm{A}}\) with \(\tan\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\), equals this, with a relative error at \(\sin\theta\) of at most \(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + 0{,}005^2} = 1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5}\) (step 3 of derivation 13.1 with the bound \(0{,}005\)). The horizontal resultant, \(T|\cos\theta_{\mathrm{B}} - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{A}}| \le 1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5}\,T = 56\) mN, would accelerate the piece along \(x\), but by idealization 5 this speed does not change, so we neglect it. We do not count the weight (idealization 7). The forces are drawn in the upper row of figure 26.1.

3
\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \mu\,\ddot W_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}s = F_{\text{navp}} , \qquad \ddot W_{\mathrm{s}}(t) = w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} \quad \text{at the point } (s + vt, t) .\]
Hint

The law of motion of a body (definition 8.70) for the material piece: the mass density per label \(s\) is \(\mu\), because the spacing between the particles does not change (step 1), the transverse acceleration of the particle with label \(s\) is by lemma 26.1 equation (26.2). On the right is the resultant from step 2.

4
\[\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B \mu\,\big(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\big)(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}x = T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(B, t) - T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(A, t) . \tag{26.4}\]
Hint

The change of variable \(x = s + vt\) at constant \(t\) (lemma 9.78, point 2, shift) turns the integral over \(s\) from \(a\) to \(b\) into an integral over \(x\) from \(a + vt = A\) to \(b + vt = B\). The function is the same, only the argument is shifted.

Equation (26.4) holds for every material piece that at time \(t\) is over the interval \([A, B]\). For the piece with labels \(a = A - vt\) and \(b = B - vt\) this is a piece that stays in \((0, L)\) for a time around \(t\) if \(A\) and \(B\) are interior points, so the equation is a condition for every interior pair \(A < B\). For the end points we extend it by continuity of both sides.

Source: WM1990 (1), PDF p. 2, and CHEN2005 (1), PDF p. 2, obtain the equation with Newton's second law (Swope and Ames, 1963) or with Hamilton's principle for travelling media (Archibald and Emslie, 1958, Benjamin, 1961, WM1990, PDF p. 2). The derivation with the material piece and with the error bounds is not from the source.

With this the model is fixed. Let us write it as a definition in which it gets its name.

Definition 26.3 Moving string (gibajoča struna)

Let \(L\), \(\mu\), \(T\) and \(v\) be positive numbers: the span length in m, the mass per unit length in kg/m, the tension force in N and the transport speed in m/s. The moving string with length \(L\), mass \(\mu\) per unit length, tension \(T\) and transport speed \(v\) is a motion described by a function \(w : [0, L] \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\) that has continuous partial derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{tx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) (definition 1.25), satisfies \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\) and for every \(A < B\) from \([0, L]\) and every \(t \ge 0\) the equation (26.4). The function \(w(x, t)\) is the transverse displacement of the point at \(x\) at time \(t\) in metres. The model holds with the error from derivation 26.2 as long as \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}005\) and idealizations 1 to 8 hold.

Source: WM1990, PDF p. 2 (first paragraph of section 2.1: "uniform, flexible, axially moving string of linear density, tension and transport speed that travels between two fixed eyelets"), CHEN2005, PDF p. 2 (section 2.1.1, assumptions 1 and 2). The error measures are ours.

Equation (26.4) is one condition for every pair \(A < B\), as for the string of section 13.1. Does the equation at every point follow from it? Yes, and also conversely, as in theorem 13.3.

Theorem 26.4 Equation of the moving string (enačba gibajoče strune)

Let \(w\) be a function with continuous \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) on \([0, L] \times [0, \infty)\). Then for every \(t \ge 0\) equation (26.4) holds for all \(A < B\) from \([0, L]\) if and only if for every \(x \in [0, L]\) and every \(t \ge 0\)

\[\mu\,\big(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\big) = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} , \tag{26.5}\]

which with \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) (definition 13.4) is equal to

\[w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} - (c^2 - v^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0 . \tag{26.6}\]
Proof

We must show that equation (26.4) for all pieces is equivalent to equation (26.5) at all points. We write the difference of the two sides as an integral of one continuous function and use that a continuous function with zero integral over every piece is zero everywhere, as in theorem 13.3.

  1. Fix \(t \ge 0\) and let \(f(x) = \mu\,(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}})(x, t) - T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)\). The function \(f\) is continuous, because the derivatives are continuous.
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40, point 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = w_{\mathrm{x}}(B, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(A, t)\).
  3. By step 2 and linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39) \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B f\,\mathrm{d}x\) is the difference of the left and right side of equation (26.4). The equation holds for the piece \([A, B]\) if and only if \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  4. If (26.5) holds, \(f = 0\) and every integral is zero, so by step 3 (26.4) holds.
  5. Conversely let (26.4) hold for all \(A < B\). By step 3 \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for all \(A < B\), so \(G(x) = \int_0^x f\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The function \(G\) is differentiable with \(G' = f\) (theorem 3.40, point 2), because \(f\) is continuous, and it is zero, so \(f = G' = 0\).
  6. From \(f = 0\) follows (26.5). Division by \(\mu\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), which is (26.6).

Source: WM1990 (1), PDF p. 2, CHEN2005 (1), PDF p. 2. The proof with an arbitrary finite piece is not from the source.

At \(v = 0\) equation (26.6) simplifies to the wave equation (13.2): the string of section 13.1 is a special case of the moving string. At \(v \ne 0\) the mixed derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) is added and the second derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) has a different coefficient, \(c^2 - v^2\) instead of \(c^2\).

Let us test the theorem on numbers. The function \(w_*\) of equation (26.3) at \(v = 40\) m/s satisfied equation (26.5) by SymPy: the residual of the left and right side is at five points at most \(1\cdot 10^{-14}\) N/m in magnitude, so zero up to rounding. For the material piece over \([0{,}2\ \text{m},\ 0{,}5\ \text{m}]\) at \(t = 0\) the left side of equation (26.4) is \(-13{,}142\) N, the right \(-13{,}142\) N. At \(t = 4\) ms the piece is over \([0{,}360,\ 0{,}660]\) and both sides are \(3{,}629\) N (numbers by SymPy). The equality is no accident: we chose a function that satisfies the equation, and the theorem says that then it holds for every piece.

The theorem is tied to the material piece. Would it not be simpler to take an interval that stands in space, as in section 13.1? Let us try, and on the same motion \(w_*\).

A window (okno) is an interval \([a, b]\) that stands in space: such was the piece in section 13.1. In figure 26.3 the blackwhite row is the window and the blue row is the material piece. At time \(t\) they have the same matter, as the upper row of the figure says. At time \(t + \tau\) the window is still over \([a, b]\), but the material piece is over the interval from \(a + v\tau\) to \(b + v\tau\). So the matter in the window is not the same: the left orange area, of width \(v\tau\), is matter that entered through the left edge, the right orange one, also of width \(v\tau\), is matter of the piece that left the window through the right edge. The window is therefore not an unchanging set of material particles, so the law of motion of a body for it does not mean that mass times \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) equals the force: matter enters the window and leaves it and carries momentum with it. If we write the equation of motion as in section 13.1, \(\int_{\mathrm{A}}^B \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x = T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(B) - T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(A)\), then for the motion \(w_*\) and \([A, B] = [0{,}2\ \text{m},\ 0{,}5\ \text{m}]\) at \(t = 0\) we get on the left \(-8{,}027\) N, on the right \(-13{,}142\) N. The difference is \(5{,}115\) N, which is \(39\) % of the force. The wave equation (13.2) itself is not satisfied either: the residual \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} - c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is at the point \(x = 0{,}3\) m \(51{,}9\) m/s\(^2\), at \(x = 0{,}5\) m \(38{,}6\) m/s\(^2\), not zero. The window therefore does not give the law.

What is missing is told by the momentum of the window. Let \(P_{\text{ok}}(t) = \int_{\mathrm{A}}^B \mu\,(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}x\) be the momentum in the window (by equation (26.1) \(\mu(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}})\) is the density of transverse momentum). At \(t = 0\) its derivative in time is \(-9{,}764\) N, and the flux of momentum through the edges of the window, \(\mu v\,(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v\,w_{\mathrm{x}})\) at \(B\) minus the same expression at \(A\), is \(-3{,}377\) N. The sum is \(-9{,}764 - 3{,}377 = -13{,}141\) N (the deviation from \(-13{,}142\) N is rounding), and this is exactly the left side of equation (26.4). The momentum of the window therefore changes because of the force, which is \(T\) at the edges, and because of matter that enters and leaves through the edges. Only the second part is missing when we use the window with \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\).

At \(v = 0\) the flux is zero, the material piece equals the window and equation (26.6) is equation (13.2). For a string at rest the window is therefore a material piece, and so in section 13.1 it was correct to take an interval that stands still.

The section has set up the model of the moving string: a span between eyelets that runs with speed \(v\), a material piece with the same matter, and the acceleration of a material particle with three terms. From this followed equation (26.6) with the mixed derivative \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\), which at \(v = 0\) equals the wave equation. For our band of the example \(c = 113{,}23\) m/s, the speed \(v = 40\) m/s is \(0{,}353\,c\) and \(T - \mu v^2 = 3938\) N instead of \(4500\) N at \(v = 0\). The next section introduces the material coordinate \(x - vt\), which finds solutions of the equation in the form of two waves that travel with different speeds.
