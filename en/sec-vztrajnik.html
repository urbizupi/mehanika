Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.5 Flywheel

In section 23.4 we computed the torque of engine E as if the crank turned at the constant speed \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s. Engine E and the gas force are our model, not data from a source. For a single cylinder a constant speed is a poor assumption. In a four-stroke cycle the gas pushes the piston in only one stroke of four (NORTON, PDF p. 702), and in the other three the piston is moved by the shaft, which is already turning. The shaft can do this if it holds kinetic energy that it can give up without its speed dropping much. So we fit a heavy wheel to it, the flywheel (vztrajnik). Its name says what it does: it persists in the turning it has, since a given torque changes its speed the less, the larger its moment of inertia (definition 8.73, equation (23.23) below). In this section we compute how heavy the flywheel must be for the speed to vary by only a chosen small ratio.

First we draw what we compute (figure 23.15).

We start with the engine: the cylinder, piston and conrod of sections 23.2 and 23.4 sit in the box, and out of it comes the shaft, turned by the crank. The crank has an angle \(\theta\) from the cylinder axis, measured from top dead centre, and turns with angular speed \(\dot\theta\). Both are drawn in the end view, since from the side we would not see them. The gas acts on the shaft with the torque \(Q_{\mathrm{g}}\), the generalized force of the gas for the coordinate \(\theta\) (definition 8.50, lemma 23.22). On the shaft we fit the flywheel with moment of inertia \(J_0\) about the shaft axis, a number with unit kg m\(^2\), and at the other end sits the load, for instance a generator, which brakes the turning with the torque \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\).

This is a model, so let us say what we assume in it. Crank, shaft, flywheel and the input of the load are rigidly joined and turn through the same angle \(\theta\), so the shaft is not a twisting spring. A real shaft twists a little, which a later section of this chapter treats. The load torque \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\) is constant, as for a generator with steady power, and it includes the friction in the bearings. Real loads do not behave so: their torque changes with speed, which we leave out here. The system therefore has one degree of freedom with the coordinate \(\theta\) (definition 8.45). Its kinetic energy is by theorem 8.86 equal to \(\tfrac12\big(J_0 + J(\theta)\big)\dot\theta^2\). Here \(J(\theta)\) is the part of the reduced moment of inertia (23.16) of section 23.4 that belongs to the piston and conrod, that is (23.16) without \(J_0\) (not the \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) of chapter 8, which refers to the centre of mass). In \(J_0\) is everything that has a constant moment of inertia about the shaft axis: the crank, the flywheel and the rotor of the generator. Equation (23.17) of theorem 23.21, with \(J_0\) written separately (a constant does not change \(J'\)) and the load torque \(M_{\mathrm{b}}\) called \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\), reads

\[\big(J_0 + J(\theta)\big)\,\ddot\theta + \tfrac12 J'(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 = Q_{\mathrm{g}}(\theta) - T_{\mathrm{L}} . \tag{23.23}\]

If the shaft turned at the constant speed \(\dot\theta = \Omega\), we would have \(\ddot\theta = 0\) and equation (23.23) would require \(Q_{\mathrm{g}} - \tfrac12 J'\,\Omega^2 = T_{\mathrm{L}}\) at every \(\theta\). The left side changes with the angle and the constant \(T_{\mathrm{L}}\) does not, so a constant speed is impossible. The left side is the torque the engine delivers to the shaft at the speed \(\Omega\): the gas torque reduced by the inertia torque of piston and conrod (lemma 23.23):

\[T(\theta) = Q_{\mathrm{g}}(\theta) - \tfrac12 J'(\theta)\,\Omega^2 . \tag{23.24}\]

Figure 23.16 shows it for engine E with our gas model: in the expansion stroke, between \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\), the gas force falls linearly from \(F_0 = 20\) kN to zero, and in the other three strokes it is zero. The cycle lasts 720° or two turns (NORTON, PDF p. 702), so the torque repeats only after the angle \(4\pi\).

We build the figure from the top. The horizontal axes carry the angle, the vertical ones the torque \(T\) at the top and the energy \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) at the bottom, which we explain below. The first is the torque curve: a large peak of \(611{,}4\) N m in the expansion stroke, then a long valley in which the torque oscillates only because of the inertia term and falls to \(-71{,}9\) N m. The mean over the cycle is the dashed line

\[T_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{4\pi} T(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 84{,}61\ \text{N m} .\]

By corollary 23.26 this is the same number as the mean of the gas torque, because the mean of the inertia term over the cycle is zero. At \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s the power is \(T_{\mathrm{m}}\,\Omega = 13{,}29\) kW. Where the curve is above the mean, the surplus is coloured orange, where it is below, grey. The surplus is positive between \(\theta_1 = 4{,}7^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 = 152{,}7^\circ\), that is between the two crossings of the curve with the dashed line, and negative elsewhere. At \(\theta_2\) we draw a vertical line through both halves of the figure.

The lower half shows what the surplus does. By lemma 8.51 the work of a torque on the coordinate \(\theta\) is the integral of the torque over the angle, so the work of the surplus from \(0\) to \(\theta\) is \(E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta) = \int_0^{\theta}\big(T(u) - T_{\mathrm{m}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}u\), in J. This is the curve below. By theorem 3.40 its derivative is the surplus, so it rises from \(\theta_1\) to \(\theta_2\) and falls elsewhere. The orange dots are its largest and smallest value, \(821{,}7\) J at \(\theta_2\) and \(-3{,}4\) J at \(\theta_1\), and the difference between them is \(\Delta E = 825{,}14\) J. Theorem 23.28 will show that exactly this difference sets the flywheel.

Because the torque repeats after \(4\pi\) and not after \(2\pi\), it contains by definition 9.88 with period \(4\pi\) also harmonics of order \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac32\) (order \(n\) means \(n\) oscillations per turn). Their amplitudes are (NumPy, our model) \(161{,}8\) N m at order \(\tfrac12\), \(147{,}7\) N m at 1, \(111{,}6\) N m at \(\tfrac32\), \(50{,}7\) N m at 2, \(49{,}9\) N m at \(\tfrac52\) and \(31{,}9\) N m at 3. The largest is the half order: the figure shows a single large peak per two turns, which one turn as a period does not describe.

What does such a torque do to the shaft? Think of a flywheel so heavy that we can neglect the variation of \(J(\theta)\) and the speed changes only a little. If in equation (23.23) we neglect \(J(\theta)\) next to \(J_0\) and replace \(\dot\theta^2\) in the inertia term by \(\Omega^2\), there remains \(J_0\,\ddot\theta = T(\theta) - T_{\mathrm{L}}\): the angular acceleration is the difference of the torques on the shaft divided by \(J_0\). In steady running \(T_{\mathrm{L}} = T_{\mathrm{m}}\), which the theorem below proves (point 2). Where in figure 23.16 the surplus is positive, \(\ddot\theta > 0\) and the shaft speeds up, where it is negative, it slows down. The speed oscillates between a smallest value \(\Omega_{\min}\) and a largest value \(\Omega_{\max}\). The difference \(\Omega_{\max} - \Omega_{\min}\) alone does not say much: 3 1/s is a lot at 10 1/s and little at 1000 1/s. So we divide it by a mean speed.

Definition 23.27 Coefficient of speed fluctuation (neenakomernost teka)

Let \(\dot\theta(t)\) be the angular speed of the crank, continuous, positive and periodic with the period of the cycle. By theorem 1.73 (on an interval of one period) it attains a largest value \(\Omega_{\max}\) and a smallest value \(\Omega_{\min}\). The mean speed is the midpoint between them,

\[\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} = \tfrac12\,(\Omega_{\max} + \Omega_{\min}) , \tag{23.25}\]

and the coefficient of speed fluctuation is the dimensionless number

\[\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\Omega_{\max} - \Omega_{\min}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}} . \tag{23.26}\]

It is zero exactly at constant speed.

Source: NORTON, equations (11.19b) and (11.21) (PDF p. 648), where the number is called the coefficient of fluctuation \(k\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{avg}}\) is taken by (11.21) as the midpoint of the extreme speeds. The symbol \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\) is ours, because \(k\) is a stiffness in this book. NORTON advises values from 0,01 to 0,05.

The Slovenian word tek means the running of a machine, so the number says how unevenly it runs. We took the mean speed as the midpoint between the extremes and not as the time average of the speed, because this gives the exact equality in the theorem below. Both numbers lie between \(\Omega_{\min}\) and \(\Omega_{\max}\), so they differ by at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\). For engine E with the flywheel of the example below the midpoint is \(157{,}080\) 1/s and the time average \(157{,}048\) 1/s, a difference of \(0{,}02\) %. What the speed looks like over a cycle is shown in figure 23.17. It is drawn for engine E with the flywheel that we compute only in the example below, and here we care only what the lines mean.

The curve shows the speed over the angle, the extreme values are the orange dots, the limits are dashed. Between them is the midpoint, and the difference of the limits, divided by it, is \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\). The speed has its largest value when it stops growing and its smallest when it stops falling. There \(\ddot\theta = 0\) and the surplus torque is zero. In figure 23.16 this is at the crossings \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\), which the theorem below proves for a constant moment of inertia. This figure is computed with the varying \(J(\theta)\), so its extreme values are at \(152{,}9^\circ\) and \(4{,}6^\circ\), only a little away from \(\theta_2 = 152{,}7^\circ\) and \(\theta_1 = 4{,}7^\circ\).

Now we can turn the question around: for a chosen \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\), how large must the flywheel be? For a model with one angle coordinate, a constant moment of inertia and a surplus torque \(S(\theta)\), the answer follows from energy.

Theorem 23.28 Flywheel theorem (izrek o vztrajniku)

Let \(J > 0\) be a constant moment of inertia, \(P > 0\) the angle of a cycle (\(P = 4\pi\) for a four-stroke engine), \(S\) a continuous function of the angle with period \(P\) (the surplus torque, the difference between the torque the shaft receives and the load torque) and \(\theta(t)\) a twice continuously differentiable motion with

\[J\,\ddot\theta = S(\theta) , \qquad \dot\theta > 0 , \tag{23.27}\]

that repeats: for some period \(t_{\mathrm{c}} > 0\) we have \(\theta(t + t_{\mathrm{c}}) = \theta(t) + P\). Let \(E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta) = \int_0^{\theta} S(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) and let \(\Delta E\) be the difference between the largest and smallest value of \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) on \([0, P]\). Then:

  1. The difference \(\tfrac12 J\,\dot\theta(t)^2 - E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta(t))\) is constant (lower half of figure 23.16).
  2. \(\int_0^{P} S(u)\,\mathrm{d}u = 0\), so the mean of the load torque over a cycle equals the mean of the engine torque (the surplus and the deficit in figure 23.16 balance).
  3. \(\tfrac12 J\,(\Omega_{\max}^2 - \Omega_{\min}^2) = \Delta E\) (figure 23.17), and with \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\) of definition 23.27
    \[J\,\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2 = \Delta E , \qquad J = \frac{\Delta E}{\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2} . \tag{23.28}\]
Proof

We must show that the kinetic energy changes by the integral of the surplus torque, that this requires a zero mean, and that the difference of the extreme energies gives \(J\).

  1. By theorem 3.40, \(E_{\mathrm{S}}'(\theta) = S(\theta)\). By the chain rule (lemma 4.43), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta(t)) = S(\theta(t))\,\dot\theta(t)\).
  2. By the product rule and the chain rule (lemma 1.81), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\tfrac12 J\,\dot\theta^2 = J\,\dot\theta\,\ddot\theta = S(\theta)\,\dot\theta\), where we used (23.27).
  3. The difference of steps 2 and 1 has derivative zero, so by corollary 1.85 it is constant. This is point 1.
  4. Let \(t_0\) be any time and \(\theta_0 = \theta(t_0)\). Differentiating \(\theta(t + t_{\mathrm{c}}) = \theta(t) + P\) gives \(\dot\theta(t_0 + t_{\mathrm{c}}) = \dot\theta(t_0)\), so by point 1 \(E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta_0 + P) = E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta_0)\). Hence \(\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta_0 + P} S(u)\,\mathrm{d}u = 0\), and by lemma 9.86, point 3, this is \(\int_0^P S(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\). This is point 2 (in figure 23.16 the \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) returns to zero at the end of the cycle).
  5. By point 2, \(E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta + P) = E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta)\) for every \(\theta\), so \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) has the same largest and smallest value on every interval of length \(P\). Since \(\dot\theta > 0\), \(\theta(t)\) is an increasing continuous function and on one period \([t_0, t_0 + t_{\mathrm{c}}]\) it takes every value of \([\theta_0, \theta_0 + P]\). By point 1, \(\tfrac12 J\,\dot\theta^2 = K + E_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta)\) with a constant \(K\), so its largest value is \(K + \max E_{\mathrm{S}}\) and its smallest \(K + \min E_{\mathrm{S}}\) (orange dots in figure 23.16).
  6. Since \(\dot\theta > 0\), the largest value of \(\dot\theta^2\) is \(\Omega_{\max}^2\) and the smallest \(\Omega_{\min}^2\). The difference of the values in step 5 gives \(\tfrac12 J\,(\Omega_{\max}^2 - \Omega_{\min}^2) = \Delta E\).
  7. By the difference of squares, \(\Omega_{\max}^2 - \Omega_{\min}^2 = (\Omega_{\max} - \Omega_{\min})(\Omega_{\max} + \Omega_{\min})\). From (23.25) and (23.26), \(\Omega_{\max} + \Omega_{\min} = 2\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\Omega_{\max} - \Omega_{\min} = \delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(\tfrac12 J \cdot 2\,\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2 = J\,\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2 = \Delta E\). This is point 3.

Source: NORTON, equations (11.20b) and (11.22) (PDF p. 648) and section 13.8 (PDF p. 724). NORTON obtains the same formula, \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = E/(k\,\omega_{\mathrm{avg}}^2)\), from the energy \(E = \tfrac12 I\,(\omega_{\max}^2 - \omega_{\min}^2)\), and says of \(\omega_{\mathrm{avg}}\) by (11.21) that it would be exact if the torque varied purely harmonically and is otherwise an approximation. We take this midpoint as the definition, so the equality is exact. The proof by steps is not from the source.

The proof showed three things. The kinetic energy follows the integral of the surplus, so the speed is largest and smallest where \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) is largest and smallest, that is at the crossings \(\theta_1\), \(\theta_2\) of figure 23.16. The load torque cannot be arbitrary, because over a cycle it must carry away exactly as much energy as the gas gives (point 2). And the flywheel is fixed only by \(\Delta E\), which the engine sets, and by the chosen \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\). The factor \(2\) in the proof comes from the difference of squares: \(\Delta E = J\,\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2\) is twice \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\) times the energy \(\tfrac12 J\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2\) stored in the flywheel. For the same \(\Delta E\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(J\) is therefore four times smaller if the speed doubles, which shows why the flywheel is computed at the lowest speed.

The theorem requires a constant \(J\) and a known \(S\), but our engine has \(J_0 + J(\theta)\) and an inertia term that depends on \(\dot\theta^2\). For an estimate of the flywheel we therefore use it with two simplifications: we replace \(J_0 + J(\theta)\) by \(J_0\) and \(\dot\theta^2\) in the term \(\tfrac12 J'\,\dot\theta^2\) by \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2\), so we take \(S = T - T_{\mathrm{m}}\) with the torque \(T\) of (23.24). How close this comes to the true equation (23.23) is shown by the example below.

Example 23.29 Flywheel for engine E (vztrajnik za motor E)

Engine E (our model) runs at \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} = 157{,}0796\) 1/s (1500 1/min) and the gas model is as above. We choose \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}02\), a value from the range 0,01 to 0,05 that NORTON advises (PDF p. 648), and compute \(J_0\) with the script num-05.py.

  1. From figure 23.16 we read \(T_{\mathrm{m}} = 84{,}61\) N m. The surplus \(T - T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is positive between \(\theta_1 = 4{,}7^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 = 152{,}7^\circ\) and negative elsewhere.
  2. As in figure 23.16, the extreme values of \(E_{\mathrm{S}}\) are \(-3{,}4\) J at \(\theta_1\) and \(821{,}7\) J at \(\theta_2\), so \(\Delta E\) \(= 825{,}14\) J.
  3. By (23.28), \(J_0 = \dfrac{825{,}14}{0{,}02\cdot 157{,}0796^2} = \dfrac{825{,}14}{493{,}48} = 1{,}6721\) kg m\(^2\). For \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}01\) it is \(J_0 = 3{,}3442\) kg m\(^2\), twice as much, since \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\) stands in the denominator.
  4. The stored energy is \(\tfrac12 J_0\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2 = 20\,629\) J. The ratio \(\Delta E/(\tfrac12 J_0\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2) = 0{,}0400\) is \(2\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\), as the theorem requires: the kinetic energy of the flywheel oscillates between \(20{,}2\) kJ at \(\Omega_{\min}\) and \(21{,}0\) kJ at \(\Omega_{\max}\).
  5. We make the flywheel a thin ring of radius \(R = 0{,}25\) m, all the mass at radius \(R\), as in NORTON (PDF p. 649, mass at the rim, since \(J = mR^2\) is larger at a larger radius). Then \(J_0 = m\,R^2\), because every particle of the ring is at distance \(R\) from the axis, so \(m = 1{,}6721/0{,}25^2 = 26{,}75\) kg and the rim speed is \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,R = 39{,}3\) m/s. The strength of the ring is not checked here.
  6. We check the two simplifications. The variation of \(J(\theta)\) is \(0{,}00493\) kg m\(^2\), which is \(0{,}30\) % of \(J_0\). The square of the speed differs from \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2\) by at most \((1 + \delta_{\mathrm{s}}/2)^2 - 1 = 2{,}01\) %. We solve the true equation (23.23) from the energy: by theorem 8.106, \(\tfrac12\big(J_0 + J(\theta)\big)\dot\theta^2 = K + \int_0^\theta (Q_{\mathrm{g}} - T_{\mathrm{L}})\,\mathrm{d}u\), from which (NumPy) \(\Omega_{\max} = 158{,}648\) 1/s at \(152{,}9^\circ\) and \(\Omega_{\min} = 155{,}511\) 1/s at \(4{,}6^\circ\), which is 1515,0 and 1485,0 1/min. The resulting coefficient is \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}01997\) instead of \(0{,}02\), an error of \(0{,}14\) %. The extremes are close to \(\theta_2\) and \(\theta_1\), where by the proof the surplus torque is zero (figure 23.17).

The speed is lowest at \(\theta_1\), so at the start of the expansion stroke: from \(\theta_2\) to there \(572^\circ\) have passed. They contain a turn and a half, from \(180^\circ\) to \(720^\circ\), in which there is no gas and the flywheel is the only source of energy. Then the gas in the expansion stroke raises it up to \(\theta_2\).

We computed the flywheel at \(1500\) 1/min. Would it be the same at another speed? For \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0{,}02\) and the same gas model (the gas force does not depend on speed, which is an assumption of the model) we get

speed (1/min) \(\Delta E\) (J) \(J_0\) (kg m\(^2\))
600 832,3 10,541
1500 825,1 1,672
3000 944,8 0,4786

\(\Delta E\) hardly changes. With the gas alone it is \(834{,}0\) J, because the work of the gas in a cycle is fixed by the force and the stroke of the piston, not by the speed (the difference in figure 23.16). At 3000 1/min \(\Delta E\) is larger, because the inertia term \(\tfrac12 J'\,\Omega^2\) is proportional to \(\Omega^2\), four times larger than at 1500 1/min, and it changes the shape of the curve in figure 23.16. So \(J_0\) falls roughly as \(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{-2}\) and the largest flywheel is needed at the lowest speed at which the engine still works, as NORTON also writes (PDF p. 724).

The theorem has conditions that we can violate, and each violation gives a wrong flywheel. We look at two.

The theorem requires a constant \(J\), but the \(J(\theta)\) of piston and conrod varies, so this is not quite true. Take engine E without gas and without load (\(S = 0\)), with \(J_0 = 0{,}5\) kg m\(^2\) on a shaft that turns at \(\Omega = 157{,}0796\) 1/s at \(\theta = 0\). For a constant \(J\) the theorem (point 3 with \(\Delta E = 0\)) would give \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\). Figure 23.18 shows what happens.

The figure is drawn over the angle for one turn. At the top is the moment of inertia, below it the speed. At \(\theta = 0\) the piston is at rest and piston and conrod carry the least kinetic energy per unit of \(\dot\theta^2\), \(J\) is smallest, so the speed is largest. As the crank turns on, the piston and conrod move faster at the same \(\dot\theta\) and carry more energy. Since the whole kinetic energy is constant, the speed must fall, and it is lowest at \(77{,}1^\circ\), where \(J\) is largest. \(J\) is an even function with period \(2\pi\) (theorem 23.21), so besides the peak at \(77{,}1^\circ\) it has an equal peak at \(360^\circ - 77{,}1^\circ = 282{,}9^\circ\), and the speed has its second valley there.

Example 23.30 The constant inertia hypothesis: an engine coasting without torques (hipoteza stalnega vztrajnostnega momenta)
  1. Without external torques the kinetic energy is constant by theorem 8.106: \(\tfrac12\big(J_0 + J(\theta)\big)\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\big(J_0 + J(0)\big)\Omega^2\).
  2. So \(\dot\theta = \Omega\sqrt{(J_0 + J(0))/(J_0 + J(\theta))}\). At \(\theta = 0\), \(J(0) = 0{,}002094\) kg m\(^2\) is the smallest, and the largest, \(0{,}007024\) kg m\(^2\), is at \(77{,}1^\circ\) (figure 23.18).
  3. The speed is thus highest at \(\theta = 0\) and lowest, \(156{,}314\) 1/s, at \(77{,}1^\circ\), so \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 4{,}89\cdot 10^{-3}\) instead of zero. For \(J_0 = 1{,}6721\) kg m\(^2\) it is \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = 1{,}47\cdot 10^{-3}\), which is \(7\) % of \(0{,}02\): a small share, but not zero.
Example 23.31 The zero-mean hypothesis: one turn instead of two (hipoteza ničelnega povprečja)

The theorem requires the surplus over a cycle \(P = 4\pi\) to have mean zero (point 2). What if we compute the flywheel over one turn, \(P = 2\pi\), as if the cycle were a turn? This would be right if the cycle repeated after a turn, but here it does not.

  1. The mean of the surplus \(T - T_{\mathrm{m}}\) over the first turn, which contains the expansion stroke, is \(+84{,}61\) N m, over the second \(-84{,}61\) N m, so \(E_{\mathrm{S}}(2\pi) = 531{,}6\) J, not zero (figure, at \(360^\circ\)). The hypothesis \(\int_0^P S = 0\) is violated for \(P = 2\pi\).
  2. If we take the first turn with its own mean \(169{,}22\) N m, we get \(\Delta E = 621{,}3\) J instead of \(825{,}14\) J and \(J_0 = 621{,}3/493{,}48 = 1{,}2590\) kg m\(^2\), which is \(24{,}7\) % too little.
  3. With the true \(\Delta E\) and the theorem (point 3), the engine with this flywheel would have \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}} = \Delta E/(J_0\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2) = 825{,}14/(1{,}2590\cdot 24\,674) = 0{,}0266\) instead of \(0{,}02\).

The error has a cause. The first turn sees only part of the story: the energy that the gas gave in the expansion stroke must be taken away in the second turn, which the falling part of the curve after \(360^\circ\) shows and which the first turn alone does not capture.

With theorem 23.28 the section answered the question of how large the flywheel must be: \(J_0 = \Delta E/(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^2)\), where we determine \(\Delta E\) over the whole cycle and choose \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\). Every order of the torque contributes to the oscillation of the speed. From \(J_0\,\ddot\theta = S\), order \(n\) with amplitude \(A_{\mathrm{n}}\) has the speed amplitude \(A_{\mathrm{n}}/(J_0\,n\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})\), which for engine E is \(1{,}23\) 1/s for order \(\tfrac12\), \(0{,}56\) for 1, \(0{,}28\) for \(\tfrac32\), \(0{,}10\) for 2 and less for the higher orders (NumPy), compared with half of the range \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/2 = 1{,}57\) 1/s (figure 23.17). So the half order contributes most and the flywheel smooths the higher orders very effectively, because \(n\) is in the denominator. That the shaft does not twist we assumed, which the section on the torsion of the shaft later in the chapter checks, and there we also see when the orders of the torque meet a natural frequency of the shaft. Several cylinders firing evenly over the cycle reduce \(\Delta E\) and with it the flywheel for the same \(\delta_{\mathrm{s}}\), which also follows in a later section.
