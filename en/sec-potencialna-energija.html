Chapter 4 Energy principles and virtual work

4.6 Minimum total potential energy

In section 4.5 we wrote the equilibrium of a body as one single equation, the principle of virtual displacements. In the derivation we used no relation between stress and strain, so it holds for every material. In section 3.6, however, we met materials that store the work needed for a deformation and return all of it on unloading. What does the principle of virtual displacements say for a body made of such a material? It says something that fits into one sentence: among all shapes that the supports allow, the body comes to rest in the one for which a certain number, the sum of the stored energy and the potential energy of the loads, is smallest. So we can also find the equilibrium by looking for a smallest value.

From history

In 1740 Pierre Louis Moreau de Maupertuis, as he is credited, wrote that at an equilibrium to which a body returns after a small displacement the potential energy has its smallest value (EP, PDF p. 3). Euler developed this thought on the elastica (elastika), the curve into which a thin bar bends. He showed that the equations follow from it that tell how a thin bar bends and at what compressive force it suddenly bends sideways (EP, PDF p. 3). The expression for the bending energy was passed on to him by Daniel Bernoulli: the whole force that is in a curved elastic strip can be written with one formula, which he called the potential force, and for the true curve it must be a minimum (TIMO, PDF p. 40). For a strip that is straight in its natural position this is the integral \(\int \mathrm{d}s/R^2\) along the curve, where \(\mathrm{d}s\) is the length of a small arc and \(R\) is the radius of the circle that fits the curve best at the given point. Up to a constant factor this is the bending energy (TIMO, PDF p. 35 and 227). The first systematic treatment of the elastica was brought by the same book of 1744 (TIMO, PDF p. 37).

Back to the statement about the smallest sum of the stored energy and the potential energy of the loads. In this section it does not remain a statement. We prove it from the principle of virtual displacements and use it on an arbitrary body.

The goal of the section is to state this claim precisely, to prove it, and to show what it is good for. It is good for three things. First, it gives a new way of computing displacements. Instead of writing the equilibrium for every part of a structure separately, we look for the smallest value of one single function. For the bracket of two bars from section 4.1 this will be a function of two numbers, the horizontal and the vertical displacement of the joint, and its smallest value will give the displacement of the joint without knowing the bar forces first. Second, it gives approximate solutions. When we cannot compute the true displacement field, we try a family of simple fields and choose the one with the smallest energy. The computation with trial fields and the finite element method, which we will meet in chapters 6 and 7, rest on this idea. Third, it tells how force is related to energy. The force on a structure is the derivative of the strain energy with respect to the displacement of the point it acts on. This relation bears Castigliano's name.

The physical model is the same as in section 4.5, with additions about the material and the loads. Let us list all assumptions and say for each where we use it.

  1. The body is at rest, the strains are small, and we compute all quantities on the shape of the body before loading, the strain with the small strain tensor (definition 1.115). This is how the principle of virtual displacements is written, and all proofs of the section stand on it.
  2. The material is ideal elastic (definition 3.43) and linear elastic. So the stress components are linear functions of the strain components, and the material stiffness matrix is symmetric (theorem 3.47). The strain energy density is therefore half the sum of the products of stresses and strains, \(w = \tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (theorem 3.47, item 2). We use this when we express the energy of a body in equilibrium by the work of the loads, and in the proof of the principle.
  3. The strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero. This is the assumption about a stable material from section 3.6, which for an isotropic material is equivalent to a positive bulk modulus and a positive shear modulus (theorem 3.48). We use it in the proof that the energy of the true field is smallest.
  4. The loads do not change with the deformation, neither in size nor in direction. Weight is such a load. What this means and why it is needed we say in subsection 4.6.3, where we use the assumption.
  5. Supports prescribe the displacements on a part of the surface. In the first theorem of the section we assume that these displacements are zero, as for a face that is glued or welded to an immovable wall.
  6. The displacement fields are continuously differentiable (definition 1.90), and the stresses of the true field have continuous partial derivatives, as the principle of virtual displacements requires.

The route is this. First, on a vertical bar with a load, where everything depends on one number, we show what potential energy is and why it is smallest in equilibrium. Then we derive from the principle of virtual displacements a theorem which says that the energy a body stores in equilibrium is half the work the loads would do if they were at full value all the time. The precise definition of the number we compare and the proof of the principle follow. We test the principle on the bracket of two bars, show how it is related to the principle of virtual displacements, and derive from it that a force is the derivative of the energy with respect to a displacement. At the end we test on the hanging bar what the energy of simple trial fields gives, fields that are not the true field.

4.6.1 A bar with a load

Let us start with the simplest body we know. We stand the steel bar from section 4.1 upright, with length \(L = 10\) m and a round cross section of diameter 10 mm and area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). Its upper end is fixed to the ceiling so that it does not move. On its lower end we hang a load that weighs \(F = 10\) kN (figure 4.72). We neglect the weight of the bar, 60,10 N, because it is only 0,6 % of the weight of the load. The stress in the bar is then \(F/A = 127{,}3\) MPa, below the yield stress 220 MPa, so the force with which the bar pulls the load up is proportional to the elongation \(e\). This force is \(k\,e\), where \(k = EA/L = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, as in section 4.1, and \(E = 196\) GPa is Young's modulus of steel. The upper end is at rest, so the elongation \(e\) is also the downward displacement of the lower end. The bar acts as a spring whose state is described by one single number, \(e\).

Where does the load come to rest? The load is in equilibrium when the bar pulls it up with a force as large as the weight pulls it down, \(k\,e = F\), so at the elongation

\[\Delta L = \frac{F}{k} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m}} = 6{,}496\ \text{mm} .\]

We got there with forces. Now let us answer the same question with energy. Imagine that we hold the lower end with the load by hand at some elongation \(e\), not necessarily \(\Delta L\) (figure 4.72). The bar pulls the load up with the force \(k\,e\), the weight pulls it down with the force \(F\), and the hand must add the difference \(F - k\,e\) upward. At \(e = 0\) the hand carries the whole load, at \(e = \Delta L/2\) half of it, since \(k\,\Delta L/2 = F/2\), and at \(e = \Delta L\) nothing. All these states are possible, since in all of them the bar is fixed to the ceiling, but without the hand only the last one is in equilibrium. In each state the bar is stretched evenly, because only the two forces at its ends act on it. To each state we assign two energies.

The first is the strain energy of the bar. In section 4.1 we showed that the force that stretches the bar slowly to the elongation \(\Delta L\) does the work \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\), and that the bar stores it as strain energy (theorem 4.3). The same statement holds for every final elongation, so at the elongation \(e\) the strain energy of the bar is \(U(e) = \tfrac12 k\,e^2\).

The second is the energy of the load. When the load is lowered by \(e\), the weight does the work \(F\,e\) on it, because it is a constant force pointing along the path, and its work is the product of force and path (definition 4.1). The load could pass this work on, for example by lifting another body over a pulley. When we lift the load back, however, we have to put the same work in ourselves. So the load holds a store of work that lowering uses up and lifting restores. This store is called the potential energy (potencialna energija) of the load. Only its change matters, so we choose it to be zero at \(e = 0\). At the elongation \(e\) the load is lower by \(e\), the weight has done the work \(F\,e\), and the potential energy of the load is \(-F\,e\). This is the school rule that the potential energy of a body equals its weight times its height, except that here the height is measured from the starting position and is therefore negative. EP uses the same example, a spring with a weight, to show that the work of the constant force of the load is not equal to the work of the internal force of the spring (EP §3.1.2, pp. 99-100).

The sum of the two energies is

\[\Pi(e) = \tfrac12\,k\,e^2 - F\,e . \tag{4.52}\]

The table shows three patterns (figure 4.73). The sum \(\Pi\) is smallest at \(e = \Delta L\), exactly at equilibrium. The values at elongations equally far to the left and to the right of \(\Delta L\) are equal, at \(\Delta L/2\) and at \(3\Delta L/2\) it is \(-24{,}36\) J. At \(2\Delta L\) the sum is zero again, as at the start. Where does all this come from? Let us rewrite the sum so that it shows how it depends on the distance from \(\Delta L\).

  1. We expand the square of the difference as the product \((e - \Delta L)(e - \Delta L)\): we multiply every term of the first bracket by every term of the second and get \(e^2 - 2\,e\,\Delta L + \Delta L^2\). Therefore
    \[\tfrac12\,k\,(e - \Delta L)^2 = \tfrac12\,k\,e^2 - k\,\Delta L\,e + \tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2 .\]
  2. The equilibrium elongation satisfies \(k\,\Delta L = F\). So the middle term is \(k\,\Delta L\,e = F\,e\), and the last one is \(\tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2 = \tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L\).
  3. From step 1 we express \(\tfrac12\,k\,e^2 - F\,e\) and insert step 2:
    \[\Pi(e) = \tfrac12\,k\,(e - \Delta L)^2 - \tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L . \tag{4.53}\]

The first term is half the positive number \(k\) times a square, so it is never negative and is zero only at \(e = \Delta L\). The second term does not depend on \(e\). The sum is therefore smallest at \(e = \Delta L\), where \(\Pi(\Delta L) = -\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L = -32{,}48\) J. The first term depends only on the distance \(|e - \Delta L|\), so the values at \(\Delta L/2\) and \(3\Delta L/2\) are equal. At \(e = 2\Delta L\) the distance is again \(\Delta L\), the first term is \(\tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2 = \tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L\) and cancels the second to zero. But the first term is one more thing: the strain energy the bar would have if it were stretched by the difference \(e - \Delta L\). By how much the energy of a state exceeds the energy of equilibrium is thus told by the strain energy of the difference of the two elongations. This will be the core of the proof for every body.

Why is the smallest value exactly at equilibrium? By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81) the derivative of \(\Pi\) with respect to \(e\) is \(k\,e - F\), the force with which the bar pulls the load up, minus the weight of the load. This is the negative of the force \(F - k\,e\) that the hand has to add. For \(e < \Delta L\) the derivative is negative. The weight overcomes the bar, and a load that the hand lets go starts to move down, toward smaller energy. For \(e > \Delta L\) the derivative is positive, the bar overcomes the weight, and the load starts to move up, again toward smaller energy. Without the hand the load is in equilibrium only where the derivative is zero, and that is at \(e = \Delta L\), where \(\Pi\) has its smallest value.

And what does the smallest value itself, \(-\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L\), mean? In equilibrium the load is lower by \(\Delta L\) than at the start, the weight has done the work \(F\,\Delta L = 64{,}96\) J, but the bar stores only \(\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L = 32{,}48\) J (figure 4.74). Where did the other half go? It depends on how we lower the load. If we lower it slowly by hand, the hand pushes up with the force \(F - k\,e\) all the time, while the load moves down. The force of the hand and the displacement point in opposite directions, so the hand does negative work. The function \(F\,e - \tfrac12\,k\,e^2\) has the derivative \(F - k\,e\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 the work of the hand is

\[-\int_0^{\Delta L} (F - k\,e)\,\mathrm{d}e = -\Big(F\,\Delta L - \tfrac12\,k\,\Delta L^2\Big) = -\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L ,\]

where we used \(k\,\Delta L = F\). So the hand receives exactly the other half, the grey triangle in figure 4.74. If instead we let the load go suddenly at \(e = 0\), the hand receives nothing, and the difference goes into motion. In section 4.1 we saw that the load then slides past \(\Delta L\), stops only at \(2\,\Delta L\) when there is no damping, and oscillates about \(\Delta L\) (CK §5.1, PDF p. 137). At \(2\,\Delta L\) the energy \(\Pi\) is zero again, as at the start, so the zero in the last row of the table is no coincidence: there the strain energy of the bar uses up exactly the work of the weight.

The bar with a load has shown the idea that we now carry over to a body. We compare all states that the supports allow, not only the equilibrium one. To each state we assign the sum of the strain energy and the potential energy of the loads. The equilibrium state has the smallest sum, and the difference to every other state is the strain energy of the difference of the displacements, \(\tfrac12\,k\,(e - \Delta L)^2\). We also saw that the strain energy in equilibrium is half the work the weight would do over the whole displacement. Let us start with this observation.

4.6.2 Clapeyron's theorem

Let us return to the body from section 4.5 and repeat the setting. Before loading the body occupies the set of points \(V\) with surface \(S\). On the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of the surface there are supports that prescribe the displacements. On the rest of the surface, \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\) with which the surroundings act on the body is prescribed. On the free parts of the surface \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\). A body force with density \(\vek b\) (definition 2.26) acts on the material, for example the weight. The stresses in the body are continuously differentiable, symmetric, satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) in the interior and Cauchy's formula \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal. The principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) then says that

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A\]

for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\), that is for every continuously differentiable field that is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) (definition 4.79). Here \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the components of its virtual strain, and the indices \(\ix{i}\) and \(\ix{j}\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

In section 4.1 we got the strain energy of a bar by multiplying the energy density \(w\) by the area of the cross section and integrating along the length. In a body that is not a bar the energy density changes in all three directions. A small piece of the body with volume \(\Delta V\) at the point \(P\) stores approximately \(w(P)\,\Delta V\). The energy of the body is the sum of the energies of its pieces, and when we shrink the pieces, the sum becomes an integral over the body (section 4.2).

Definition 4.85 Strain energy of a body (deformacijska energija telesa)

Let a body occupy the set of points \(V\), and let the material be ideal elastic at every point, with a strain energy density \(w\) (definition 3.44) that is a continuous function of the point on \(V\). The strain energy (deformacijska energija) of the body is the number

\[U = \int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V .\]

Source: EP (3.2.8) (p. 106) and (4.3.4) (p. 180), SADD §6.1 (PDF pp. 128-131), CM (7.6.11) (PDF p. 345).

Let us test the definition on the bar pulled at its end with the force 10 kN. The energy density is the same in the whole bar, \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon = 4{,}136 \cdot 10^4\ \text{J/m}^3\) (section 4.1), the volume of the bar is \(A L = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\), and by the rule for the integral of a constant function over the round bar (section 4.2.8) \(U = 4{,}136 \cdot 10^4\ \text{J/m}^3 \cdot 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3 = 32{,}48\) J, as it should be.

For the bar with a load the strain energy in equilibrium was half the product of the weight of the load and the displacement, \(\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L\). Does this hold for every body? It does, and the proof is short, because the principle of virtual displacements does all the work.

From history

B. P. E. Clapeyron and Lamé served as French engineers in Russia. For the reconstruction of Saint Isaac's cathedral in Saint Petersburg they studied the stability of arches (TIMO, PDF p. 123). In 1831 Clapeyron returned to France, where he worked mostly on the building of railways and on the use of thermodynamics in the design of locomotives (TIMO, PDF p. 126). He stated the theorem we will prove long before Lamé's book of 1852 appeared. It says that the sum of the products of the external forces and the corresponding displacements equals twice the strain energy, and this was the first general expression for the strain energy of a deformed isotropic body (TIMO, PDF p. 126). Lamé described it in his book on elasticity and attributed it to Clapeyron (TIMO, PDF p. 126, 227). Clapeyron came to the theorem through springs. Studying various kinds of springs he concluded that the energy an elastic body stores and returns on unloading is proportional to its volume, so he asked how to express this work (TP2A, PDF p. 446). The sources do not agree on the credit. TIMO says that Clapeyron stated the theorem, and EP says that Lamé was the first to prove the work equation (delovna enačba) for deformable bodies, in 1852, that is, the equation that makes the work of the external forces equal to the energy of the body, and named it after Clapeyron (EP, PDF p. 3). TP2A (PDF p. 446-447) remarks that in a paper of 1858 Clapeyron presented the theorem as if it were entirely his own.

Back to the question whether for every body the strain energy is half the product of the external forces and the corresponding displacements. Why twice and not once is shown by the proof and the review after it.

Theorem 4.86 Clapeyron's theorem (Clapeyronov izrek)

Let the body be as in theorem 4.82 on the principle of virtual displacements, made of an ideal elastic and linear elastic material. Let \(\vek u\) be the displacement field of the body in equilibrium, continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface and equal to zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), and let its stresses satisfy assumptions 1 to 4 of theorem 4.82. Then the strain energy of the body is half the work that the body force and the prescribed force on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) would do on the displacements \(\vek u\) if they were at full value all the time:

\[2\,U = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{4.54}\]
Proof

We have to show that the two sides of the principle of virtual displacements become the two sides of equation (4.54) when we take the true displacement field as the virtual displacement.

  1. The field \(\vek u\) is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body with its surface, and it is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so it is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.79). In the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82) we may therefore take \(\delta\vek u = \vek u\).
  2. The virtual strain of the field \(\delta\vek u = \vek u\) is the small strain tensor of the body in equilibrium, because both are computed from the same partial derivatives, \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The left side of the principle is therefore \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  3. The material is ideal elastic and linear elastic, so by theorem 3.47 on the symmetry of the elastic constants, item 2, at every point \(\sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2\,w\).
  4. By the linearity of the integral over a body (section 4.2) the integral of the function \(2\,w\) is twice the integral of \(w\). The left side is therefore \(2\int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V = 2\,U\) (definition 4.85).
  5. The right side of the principle at \(\delta\vek u = \vek u\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A\). The two sides are equal, which is (4.54).

Source: EP Theorem 4.6.1, (4.6.10) (pp. 220-221), SADD (6.4.1) (PDF p. 135), CM §7.4 (PDF pp. 314-321). EP and SADD prove the theorem directly with the divergence theorem. We got it from the principle of virtual displacements, which had already taken that step.

In the proof we took the true displacement as the virtual one. In section 4.4 a virtual displacement meant an imagined displacement with the forces unchanged, while the true displacement is the one the forces really cause. May we do this? We may, because as a mathematical statement the principle of virtual displacements asks only two things of the field \(\delta\vek u\): that it is continuously differentiable and that it is zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). The true field meets both, the first by assumption 6 at the start of the section, the second because the supports do not move. The principle does not ask the displacement to be small, because both sides are multiplied by the same number when the field is multiplied by a number (section 4.5).

Why half? The right side of equation (4.54) is the work the loads would do if they were as large during the whole motion as at its end, like the load on the bar. For the bar with a load this was \(F\,\Delta L = 64{,}96\) J, and the bar stored half of it. In slow loading, however, the loads grow from zero, and each does only half the product of final force and final displacement, like the force at the end of the bar in section 4.1. Clapeyron's theorem says that for every linear elastic body this work is stored completely. In section 4.1 we checked this for the bar and for the hanging bar and promised a proof for every body. Let us write it down.

Corollary 4.87 Work of the loads and strain energy (delo obremenitev in deformacijska energija)

Let the body, its material and the displacement field \(\vek u\) be as in theorem 4.86. Let the loads grow slowly from zero to their final values, all in the same proportion: at a number \(\lambda\) between \(0\) and \(1\) the body force is \(\lambda\,\vek b\) and the prescribed force on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is \(\lambda\,\bar{\vek t}\). Let the body take the state with displacements \(\lambda\,\vek u\) at every \(\lambda\). Then the work of the loads in this loading equals the strain energy of the body in the final state,

\[W_{\mathrm{zun}} = \tfrac12 \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \tfrac12 \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = U .\]
Proof

We have to show that every bit of the load does half the product of final force and final displacement, then write the sum as an integral and use Clapeyron's theorem.

  1. Let \(P\) be a point of the body with \(\vek u(P) \ne \vek 0\). At the number \(\lambda\) its displacement is \(\lambda\,\vek u(P)\), so the point moves along a straight segment from its position before loading to its position after loading, in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek d = \vek u(P)/|\vek u(P)|\). When it has travelled the distance \(s\), \(\lambda = s/|\vek u(P)|\).
  2. A small piece with volume \(\Delta V\) at \(P\) is then acted on by the force approximately \(\lambda\,\vek b(P)\,\Delta V\). Its projection onto the direction of motion is \((s/|\vek u(P)|)\,\vek b(P)\cdot\vek d\,\Delta V\). The function \(s^2/2\) has the derivative \(s\), so by item 2 of definition 4.1 and the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40 the work of this force is
    \[\int_0^{|\vek u(P)|} \frac{s}{|\vek u(P)|}\,\vek b(P)\cdot\vek d\,\Delta V\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,|\vek u(P)|\,\vek b(P)\cdot\vek d\,\Delta V = \tfrac12\,\vek b(P)\cdot\vek u(P)\,\Delta V .\]
    At \(\vek u(P) = \vek 0\) the piece does not move, the work is zero, and the formula gives the same.
  3. In the same way the prescribed force on a small patch with area \(\Delta A\) at a point \(P\) of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) does the work \(\tfrac12\,\bar{\vek t}(P)\cdot\vek u(P)\,\Delta A\). The forces of the supports on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) do no work, because \(\vek u = \vek 0\) there.
  4. We add up the works of the pieces and patches. The sums are Riemann sums of the functions \(\tfrac12\,\vek b\cdot\vek u\) and \(\tfrac12\,\bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\), which are continuous, and when we shrink the pieces they approach the integrals by theorem 4.9, item 1 (section 4.2). The work of the loads is therefore \(\tfrac12 \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \tfrac12 \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  5. By Clapeyron's theorem 4.86 this is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2\,U = U\).

Source: CM (7.4.4) (PDF p. 315), SADD §6.1 (PDF p. 128), EP p. 100. The step-by-step proof is not from the sources.

The corollary assumes that the body takes the state \(\lambda\,\vek u\) at every \(\lambda\). Why is this reasonable? Under the loads \(\lambda\,\vek b\) and \(\lambda\,\bar{\vek t}\) the field \(\lambda\,\vek u\) meets all the requirements of equilibrium. The stresses are linear functions of the strains, so the stresses of the field \(\lambda\,\vek u\) are \(\lambda\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The equilibrium equations and Cauchy's formula are linear, so \(\lambda\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) satisfy them with the loads \(\lambda\,\vek b\) and \(\lambda\,\bar{\vek t}\) when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) satisfy them with \(\vek b\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\). That the body takes exactly this state and not some other follows from item 2 of theorem 4.91, proved below, when the supports prevent every displacement without strain. We used the same argument for the hanging bar in section 4.1.

Let us test the theorem on the hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m and cross-section area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points down from the top cross section. We describe it with the bar model from section 4.1, in which every cross section moves only down, by \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), while the top cross section does not move. The weight per unit length is \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m. In section 4.1 we computed \(U = 391{,}03\) µJ. The work of the weight over the whole displacement, if the weight were at full value all the time, is

\[\int_0^L q\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \rho g A\,a \int_0^L (2Lx - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac23\,\rho g A\,a\,L^3 = 782{,}06\ \text{µJ} ,\]

where we computed the integral by the fundamental theorem of calculus with the function \(Lx^2 - x^3/3\), whose derivative is \(2Lx - x^2\). This is exactly \(2\,U\), as Clapeyron's theorem says. We saw the same equality, \(\int_0^L N\,u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,u\,\mathrm{d}x\), for the hanging bar already in section 4.4, but there we did not connect it with energy. The bar model is not the body of the theorem, since it is described by one single function \(u(x)\). For it we get the theorem from the principle of virtual displacements for a bar (theorem 4.76), taking \(\delta u = u\) and the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\), with the same steps as above.

When does the theorem fail? First, when the supports prescribe displacements that are not zero, for example when we move one end of a bar by a chosen distance. Then the forces of the supports do work too, and the theorem holds if on the right we take, instead of the integral over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the integral of the force per unit area \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) times the displacement over the whole surface \(S\) (EP (4.6.10), p. 221). Our proof does not cover this case, because the true field is then not zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and is not a kinematically admissible virtual displacement. Second, for a material that is not linear elastic, for example rubber, the energy density is not half the sum of the products of stresses and strains (section 3.6), and step 3 of the proof fails. Third, for large deformations, where the principle of virtual displacements does not hold in this form (section 4.5).

4.6.3 Total potential energy

For the bar with a load we compared the states that the supports allow. For a body we need the same: displacement fields that agree with the prescribed displacements at the supports and are arbitrary elsewhere. They need not be true, and the body need not be in equilibrium in them. For the bar such a state was every elongation \(e\) at which the hand held the load (figure 4.72).

Definition 4.88 Kinematically admissible displacement field (kinematično dopustno polje pomikov)

Let a body occupy the set of points \(V\) before loading, and let the displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) be prescribed on the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of its surface. A displacement field \(\vek u\) is kinematically admissible (kinematično dopustno) if it is continuously differentiable on an open set that contains the body together with its surface and equals the prescribed displacement at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(\vek u = \bar{\vek u}\).

Source: EP (4.3.13) (p. 181), where the admissible fields satisfy \(\vek u = \hat{\vek u}\) on \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{u}}\), CM (PDF p. 346), SADD §6.6 (PDF p. 139).

The word kinematically says here too that only displacements are involved, not forces. The requirement on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) means that the field respects the supports. Continuous differentiability makes sure that the field has a small strain tensor at every point and with it a strain energy density. The difference of two kinematically admissible fields is continuously differentiable and equals \(\bar{\vek u} - \bar{\vek u} = \vek 0\) on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so it is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.79). We will need this in the proof.

Now we still need the potential energy of the loads. For the load on the bar we got it from the work of the weight, \(-F\,e\). There we used that the weight is the same at every elongation, so its work was the product of a constant force and the path. The same holds for every load that does not change with the deformation, neither in size nor in direction. Such a load is called a dead load (obremenitev, neodvisna od deformacije), and weight is the most important example. A constant force \(\vek F\) whose point of application moves by \(\vek u\) does the work \(\vek F\cdot\vek u\) on the straight path (definition 4.1), and the potential energy of such a force is \(-\vek F\cdot\vek u\). For the body force and for the prescribed force on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) we add up the contributions of the pieces and patches and shrink them, as for the strain energy. The potential energy of the loads is therefore

\[-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A\]

(EP §3.3.1, p. 124).

Which loads are not like this? Water presses perpendicular to the surface it touches (section 2.6.6). When a wall under water tilts, the force of the water on it tilts too, so the direction of the load changes with the deformation. Similarly the force of a rope fastened to a point of the body and to a fixed point in the surroundings changes when the point of the body moves sideways. For small deformations these changes are small, and we treat the load as independent of the deformation. For large ones, however, such loads have no potential energy of this form. EP defines the potential energy of the loads only for forces that are independent of the deformation (EP §3.3.1, p. 124).

Definition 4.89 Total potential energy (celotna potencialna energija)

Let a body be made of an ideal elastic material with strain energy density \(w\), with the displacement prescribed on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), a body force with density \(\vek b\) and a prescribed force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), where \(\vek b\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\) are independent of the deformation. The total potential energy (celotna potencialna energija) of the body at a kinematically admissible displacement field \(\vek u\) is the number

\[\Pi(\vek u) = U(\vek u) - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A , \qquad U(\vek u) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} w\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{4.55}\]

where \(w\) at every point is computed from the components of the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek u\) at that point.

Source: EP (3.3.1) (p. 124) and (4.3.7) (p. 180), CM (7.6.11)-(7.6.13) (PDF p. 345), SADD §6.6 (PDF p. 139), where \(\Pi = U_{\mathrm{T}} - W\).

What kind of object is \(\Pi\)? A function whose input is a whole displacement field and whose output is one number in joules. The function \(\Pi(e)\) of the bar with a load had one number as its input, because one number described the whole state. Here the input is a field that assigns a displacement to every point of the body. Books call such functions functionals (funkcionali) (CM, PDF p. 343). The first term is the strain energy of the body at the field \(\vek u\). The notation \(U(\vek u)\) says that the energy is computed from this field, which need not be the true one. The other two terms are the potential energy of the loads. For the bar with a load \(U(e) = \tfrac12\,k\,e^2\) and the potential energy of the load is \(-F\,e\), so the definition gives \(\Pi(e)\) from equation (4.52).

4.6.4 The principle of minimum total potential energy

For the bar with a load the difference between the energy of a state and that of equilibrium was the strain energy of the difference, \(\tfrac12\,k\,(e - \Delta L)^2\). For a body we will show the same. First we need a rule for how the strain energy density behaves for a sum of two strains. For the bar it was the expanded square \((e - \Delta L)^2\). For a body \(w\) is a sum of products of strain components, and we get the rule by expanding the products in the same way. We write the strain as the Voigt column of six components, as in section 3.6,

\[\{\varepsilon\} = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{yz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}) ,\]

where \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are the engineering shear strains, and \(\{\varepsilon\}_{\ix{p}}\) is the entry of the column in place \(\ix{p}\), which runs from \(1\) to \(6\). We stack the stresses into the column \(\{\sigma\} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\) without factors of two. For a symmetric stress with components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and another symmetric strain with components \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), whose column \(\{\eta\}\) we stack in the same way as \(\{\varepsilon\}\),

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_{\ix{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}} &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{zz}} \\ &\quad + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{yz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}} , \end{aligned} \tag{4.56}\]

because in the double sum of nine terms every shear pair appears twice, for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{yx}} = 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{xy}}\), while in the column the factor two hides in the engineering shear strain.

Lemma 4.90 Energy density of a sum of strains (gostota energije vsote deformacij)

Let the material at the point \(P\) be ideal elastic and linear elastic, with entries \(C_{\mathrm{pq}}\) of the material stiffness matrix, and let \(\{\varepsilon\}\) and \(\{\eta\}\) be columns of six strain components that need not be small strains. For every such column \(\{\zeta\}\) let \(w(\{\zeta\}) = \tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{p}}\sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\zeta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\zeta\}_{\mathrm{q}}\), and let the stress that belongs to it be the column with the entries \(\sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\zeta\}_{\mathrm{q}}\). For a small strain these are the strain energy density (theorem 3.47, item 2) and the stress given by the material law. Then

\[w(\{\varepsilon\} + \{\eta\}) = w(\{\varepsilon\}) + \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}} + w(\{\eta\}) , \tag{4.57}\]

where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the stress components that belong to the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the components of the strain \(\{\eta\}\).

Proof

We have to show that the expanded energy density of the sum falls into three parts. We use the formula for the energy density and the symmetry of the stiffness matrix.

  1. By the definition of the polynomial \(w\) in the hypothesis of the lemma
    \[w(\{\varepsilon\} + \{\eta\}) = \frac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\big(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}} + \{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\big)\big(\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \{\eta\}_{\mathrm{q}}\big) ,\]
    where \(\ix{p}\) and \(\ix{q}\) run from \(1\) to \(6\).
  2. We expand every term as the product of two brackets and get four double sums:
    \[\frac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \frac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \frac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}} + \frac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{q}} .\]
  3. By the same definition the first sum is \(w(\{\varepsilon\})\) and the last one \(w(\{\eta\})\).
  4. In the third sum we take out \(\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) and get \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}} \sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\). The inner sum is the entry \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the stress that belongs to the strain \(\{\varepsilon\}\), so the third sum is \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  5. In the second sum we rename the indices, \(\ix{p}\) to \(\ix{q}\) and \(\ix{q}\) to \(\ix{p}\). The sum runs over all pairs, so it does not change, and it equals \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{q}} \sum_{\ix{p}} C_{\mathrm{qp}}\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{q}}\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\). By item 1 of the theorem on the symmetry of the elastic constants \(C_{\mathrm{qp}} = C_{\mathrm{pq}}\), so this is the third sum from step 4, \(\tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\).
  6. The second and the third sum together give \(\sum_{\ix{p}} \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}}\), which by equation (4.56) equals \(\sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Together with step 3 this is (4.57).

Source: The squares are expanded in the proof of EP (4.3.19) (p. 183), where this is written for an isotropic material. The lemma as a separate statement and the step-by-step proof are not from the sources.

The lemma is the expansion of the square \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) for a function of six variables. It does not ask for small strains, because it only expands a polynomial. We shall need this, since the difference of two small strains need not be small: the components \(-0{,}0009\) and \(0{,}0009\) are below the bound \(0{,}001\) in absolute value, but their difference \(0{,}0018\) is not. The middle term is expressed here with the stress and has a physical meaning: the energy density of a sum is the sum of the densities, corrected by the work of the stress of the first strain on the second strain. The symmetry of the stiffness matrix is needed in step 5. Without it the second and the third sum would differ, and we could not write the mixed term with the stress. For an isotropic material we check the lemma directly. Its energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \mu \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}^2\) (section 3.6), where \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are the Lamé constants and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is the volumetric strain. Expanding the squares gives the mixed term \(\lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(\eta_{\mathrm{v}} = \eta_{\mathrm{xx}} + \eta_{\mathrm{yy}} + \eta_{\mathrm{zz}}\). Hooke's law (3.13) gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for equal indices and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) for different ones, so \(\sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{v}} + 2\mu \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), the same.

Now we can state the main theorem of the section. Figure 4.75 shows the two fields the theorem speaks about: the true displacement field and any other field that respects the supports.

Theorem 4.91 Principle of minimum total potential energy (načelo najmanjše celotne potencialne energije)

Let the body be as in theorem 4.82 on the principle of virtual displacements, made of an ideal elastic and linear elastic material whose strain energy density is positive for every small strain that is not zero. Let the loads \(\vek b\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\) be independent of the deformation. Let \(\vek u\) be a kinematically admissible displacement field whose stresses satisfy assumptions 1 to 4 of theorem 4.82: they are continuously differentiable, symmetric, satisfy the equilibrium equations (2.31) in the interior, and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). Then for every kinematically admissible displacement field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) with small strains:

  1. the difference of the total potential energies is the strain energy of the difference of the fields,
    \[\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) - \Pi(\vek u) = U(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u) \ge 0 , \tag{4.58}\]
  2. the equality \(\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) = \Pi(\vek u)\) holds only if the small strain tensor of the field \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) is zero at every point of the body.

The strain of the field \(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\) need not be small, so we compute \(U(\tilde{\vek u} - \vek u)\) with the density \(w\) as the polynomial from lemma 4.90.

Proof

We have to show that the difference of the energies falls into an expression from the principle of virtual displacements, which is zero, and the strain energy of the difference of the fields, which is not negative. We follow the computation for the bar with a load.

1. Difference of the energies.

  1. Let \(\vek v = \tilde{\vek u} - \vek u\). The difference of two kinematically admissible fields is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement (definition 4.88 and the paragraph after it).
  2. We denote the components of the small strain tensor of the field \(\vek v\) by \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial v_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial v_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\), where \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\) and \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\). The partial derivative of a sum is the sum of the partial derivatives (lemma 1.81, applied to one variable with the other two fixed), so the field \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u + \vek v\) has at every point the strain with components \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} + \eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the strain components of the field \(\vek u\).
  3. By lemma 4.90 on the energy density of a sum of strains, at every point \(w(\{\varepsilon\} + \{\eta\}) = w(\{\varepsilon\}) + \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}} + w(\{\eta\})\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the stresses of the field \(\vek u\).
  4. We integrate over the body and use the linearity of the integral over a body (section 4.2):
    \[U(\tilde{\vek u}) = U(\vek u) + \int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V + U(\vek v) .\]
  5. The scalar product is distributive (lemma 2.12), so \(\vek b\cdot\tilde{\vek u} = \vek b\cdot\vek u + \vek b\cdot\vek v\) and \(\bar{\vek t}\cdot\tilde{\vek u} = \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u + \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek v\). By the linearity of the integral the integrals of the potential energy of the loads split in the same way.
  6. We insert steps 4 and 5 into equation (4.55), which defines the total potential energy, and arrange the terms:
    \[\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) = \Pi(\vek u) + \Big[ \int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\ix{i}} \sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}A \Big] + U(\vek v) .\]
  7. The stresses of the field \(\vek u\) satisfy the assumptions of the principle of virtual displacements, and by step 1 \(\vek v\) is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement with virtual strain \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). By theorem 4.82 the expression in square brackets is therefore zero, and \(\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) - \Pi(\vek u) = U(\vek v)\).
  8. The polynomial \(w\) is zero at the zero column. At every column \(\{\eta\} \ne 0\) it is positive, also when \(\{\eta\}\) is not a small strain. Indeed, choose a number \(\lambda > 0\) so small that \(\lambda\{\eta\}\) is a small strain. Every term of the polynomial \(w\) has two strain factors, so \(w(\lambda\{\eta\}) = \lambda^2\,w(\{\eta\})\). At the small strain \(\lambda\{\eta\} \ne 0\) the polynomial \(w\) is the strain energy density, which is positive by assumption, so \(w(\{\eta\}) = w(\lambda\{\eta\})/\lambda^2 > 0\). Hence \(w(\{\eta\}) \ge 0\) at every point. The integral over a box is an iterated integral (definition 4.8), and each of the three iterated integrals of a nonnegative function is nonnegative (lemma 3.39, item 4). The integral over the body is the sum of the integrals over its boxes (section 4.2), so \(U(\vek v) \ge 0\).

2. Equality.

  1. Let \(\Pi(\tilde{\vek u}) = \Pi(\vek u)\). By item 1, \(U(\vek v) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} g\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\), where \(g = w(\{\eta\})\) at every point of the body.
  2. The function \(g\) is continuous. The field \(\vek v\) is continuously differentiable, so the components \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are continuous functions of the point, and \(g\) is a sum of products of two of them with constant numbers, which is continuous by corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space. By step 8 of item 1, \(g \ge 0\).
  3. Suppose that \(g(P) > 0\) at some interior point \(P\) of the body. By the definition of continuity 1.67 with the allowed error \(g(P)/2\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(g > g(P)/2\) at all points of the body closer to \(P\) than \(\delta\).
  4. Since \(P\) is an interior point, a small cube \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) with centre \(P\) and edge \(d > 0\) lies in the body around it, with its corners closer to \(P\) than \(\delta\). On it \(g > g(P)/2\).
  5. To the grid that cuts the body into cells we add the six planes in which the faces of the cube \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) lie. The integral over the body does not change, because it does not depend on the grid (section 4.2), and the cube \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) is now a union of cells. The integral over the body is the sum of the integrals over the cells. By step 8 of item 1, applied to each cell, none of them is negative, so the sum is not smaller than the sum of the integrals over the cells of the cube \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\), that is than the integral over \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\).
  6. On \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) we have \(g > g(P)/2\), so by monotonicity and the rule for a constant function, applied to the three iterated integrals (lemma 3.39, items 3 and 4), the integral over \(K_{\mathrm{P}}\) is not smaller than \(\tfrac12\,g(P)\,d^3\). Hence \(U(\vek v) \ge \tfrac12\,g(P)\,d^3 > 0\), which contradicts step 1. So \(g = 0\) at every interior point.
  7. Every point on the surface of the body is the limit of a sequence of interior points, as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.82, and \(g\) is continuous, so by lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points \(g = 0\) on the surface too.
  8. By step 8 of item 1 the polynomial \(w\) is positive at every column that is not zero. Since \(g = w(\{\eta\}) = 0\) at every point, \(\{\eta\}\) is the zero strain at every point.

Source: EP §4.3.1, (4.3.6), (4.3.19)-(4.3.20) (pp. 179-183), CM (7.6.14) (PDF p. 346), SADD §6.6 (PDF p. 139), which states the principle and leaves the proof to other books. EP proves the minimum only for an isotropic material, by expanding the squares in the Navier equations. The proof with lemma 4.90 and item 2 are not from the sources. For a body cut into pieces between graphs, such as the round bar, item 1 holds, because the principle of virtual displacements holds for it (end of section 4.5.4) and so does the monotonicity of the integral (lemma 4.22). Item 2 holds for it and for other bodies with a curved surface as far as the rules of step 5 hold for the integral over such a body, which LEBL2 §10.3-10.5 (PDF pp. 108-127) gives.

What does the theorem say? The true displacement field has the smallest total potential energy among all fields that respect the supports. Every other field has a larger one, larger by exactly the strain energy the body would have if it were deformed by the difference of the two fields. For a body this is the same as the term \(\tfrac12\,k\,(e - \Delta L)^2\) in equation (4.53) was for the bar with a load. The proof has shown where this comes from. The mixed term of the expanded energy, the work of the stress of the true field on the strain of the difference, cancels against the potential energy of the loads on the difference, because the true field is in equilibrium and the difference is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement. Only the energy of the difference remains, which is not negative because the material is stable.

Item 2 tells when another field has the same energy as the true one. The difference must be free of strain. For a body held by its supports so that it can neither move nor turn as a rigid body, such a difference can only be zero, and the true field is the only one with this energy. That a field without strain is a small rigid displacement we will use in section 4.8.

Where does the theorem stop holding? Each of the assumptions was needed. Without loads independent of the deformation, the potential energy of the loads of the form \(-\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A\) does not exist. Without linearity and the symmetry of the stiffness matrix, lemma 4.90 does not hold. Without a positive energy density, step 8 fails, and the true field may only be a point where the derivative of the energy in every direction is zero, not the smallest value, as we will see in subsection 4.6.6. For large displacements, finally, a compressed bar may bend sideways, and the straight shape is no longer the shape with the smallest energy. EP shows that for a bar in which the term due to large rotations is kept, the minimum cannot be guaranteed when the axial force is compressive (EP Example 4.3.1, pp. 184-187). We do not treat buckling in this chapter.

4.6.5 The bracket of two bars

Let us test the principle on the bracket from section 4.1 and repeat its setting (figure 4.76, left). The bracket lies in a vertical plane. The axis \(x\) points horizontally to the right, the axis \(y\) vertically up, and the origin is at the joint \(C = (0, 0, 0)\). The horizontal bar \(AC\) has length \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m, and its end \(A = (-4, 0, 0)\) m is pinned to the wall. The inclined bar \(BC\) has length \(L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 5\) m, and its end \(B = (-4, 3, 0)\) m is also pinned to the wall. At the joint \(C\) a load acts with the force \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\), \(F = 10\) kN, which is independent of the deformation. Both bars are steel, with \(E = 196\) GPa and cross-section area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2\), so \(E A_{\mathrm{AC}} = E A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^7\) N. The truss model (definition 4.4) assumes that the bars carry only axial forces. We do not check the compressed bar \(AC\) for buckling. In section 4.1 we got from the equilibrium of the pin \(C\) the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac53 F = 16{,}67\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -\tfrac43 F = -13{,}33\) kN and the energy \(U = 53{,}57\) J. There we estimated the component \(v\) of the displacement of the joint from the equality of work and energy as \(v = -10{,}71\) mm.

The state of the bracket is described by two numbers. The pins \(A\) and \(B\) do not move and the bars are straight, so everything is fixed by the displacement of the joint \(C\). As in sections 4.1 and 4.4 we write it as \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = (u, v, 0)\). The number \(u\) is the horizontal displacement, positive to the right, the number \(v\) the vertical displacement, positive upward. In section 4.4 we got the displacement of the joint from the elongations of the bars, and these from the axial forces, which we knew from the equilibrium of the pin. Now let us find it with energy, without knowing the axial forces in advance. Every pair of numbers \(u\), \(v\) is an admissible state, since it respects both pins. For every such state let us compute the total potential energy.

  1. For a small displacement of the joint a bar lengthens by the projection of the displacement of the joint onto the direction of the bar, as we saw for virtual displacements in section 4.4. With the unit vectors from the pin toward the joint, \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac15\,(4, -3, 0)\), the elongations are
    \[\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = u , \qquad \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = \vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = 0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v .\]
  2. A bar with elongation \(\Delta L\) carries the axial force \(N = EA\,\Delta L/L\) and stores the energy \(N^2 L/(2EA)\) (theorem 4.3). Inserting the force, the energy is \(EA\,\Delta L^2/(2L)\). The energy of the bracket is the sum of the energies of the two bars:
    \[U(u, v) = \frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,u^2 + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{BC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{BC}}}\,\big(0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\big)^2 .\]
  3. The load is independent of the deformation, so its potential energy is \(-\vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -\big(0 \cdot u + (-F)\,v\big) = F\,v\). A load that goes down has \(v < 0\), and its potential energy is negative, as for the bar with a load.
  4. The total potential energy is the sum. With numbers, \(E A_{\mathrm{AC}}/(2 L_{\mathrm{AC}}) = 2{,}45 \cdot 10^6\) N/m and \(E A_{\mathrm{BC}}/(2 L_{\mathrm{BC}}) = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, and expanding the square,
    \[\begin{aligned} \Pi(u, v) &= 3{,}7044 \cdot 10^6\,u^2 - 1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\,u\,v \\ &\quad + 0{,}7056 \cdot 10^6\,v^2 + 10^4\,v , \end{aligned} \tag{4.59}\]
    with displacements in metres and energy in joules. The numbers in front of the squares and the product are \(3{,}7044 \cdot 10^6 = 2{,}45 \cdot 10^6 + 1{,}96 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 0{,}8^2\), \(0{,}7056 \cdot 10^6 = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 0{,}6^2\) and \(1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6 = 1{,}96 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 2 \cdot 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}6\), the last one with a minus, because the bracket contains \(-0{,}6\,v\). The number \(10^4\) is the load \(F = 10\,000\) N.

Theorem 4.91 is about a body, while we described the bracket with the truss model, in which every bar is only an elongation and an axial force. So we repeat the computation of the proof for the bracket, where it is simpler. Let \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) be the true displacement of the joint from section 4.4, at which the bars carry the axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\) from section 4.1, and let \(\vek w = (w_{\mathrm{x}}, w_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) be any vector.

  1. The elongation of a bar for the displacement \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} + \vek w\) is \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} + \vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w\), by the distributivity of the scalar product, and the same for \(BC\).
  2. We expand the square of the sum, \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\). The energy of the bar \(AC\) is therefore
    \[\frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}})^2 + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}})(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w) + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w)^2 ,\]
    and the same for \(BC\). The first term is the energy of the bar in the true state.
  3. In the middle term \(E A_{\mathrm{AC}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}})/L_{\mathrm{AC}} = E A_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}}/L_{\mathrm{AC}} = N_{\mathrm{AC}}\), the axial force of the true state. The middle term is therefore \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w)\).
  4. The potential energy of the load changes by \(-\vek F\cdot\vek w\). All together:
    \[\begin{aligned} \Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} + \vek w) - \Pi(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}) &= \big[ N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\vek w - \vek F\cdot\vek w \big] \\ &\quad + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w)^2 + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{BC}}}{2 L_{\mathrm{BC}}}\,(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\vek w)^2 . \end{aligned}\]
  5. The expression in square brackets is \((N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} - \vek F)\cdot\vek w\). The equilibrium of the pin \(C\) from section 4.1, \(\vek F - N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = \vek 0\), says that the vector in the bracket is the zero vector. This is also the principle of virtual displacements for the truss from section 4.4 with the virtual displacement \(\vek w\). Let us check with numbers: \(-13\,333\ \text{N} \cdot (1, 0, 0) + \tfrac15 \cdot 16\,667\ \text{N} \cdot (4, -3, 0) = (0, -10\,000, 0)\ \text{N} = \vek F\).
  6. Two squares remain, which are not negative. Both are zero only if \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}}\cdot\vek w = w_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}}\cdot\vek w = 0{,}8\,w_{\mathrm{x}} - 0{,}6\,w_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = w_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). Every other displacement of the joint therefore has a larger energy than the true one, and there is only one true displacement.

Now let us find the true displacement. Where \(\Pi\) has its smallest value, the function of one variable that we get by holding one component fixed has its smallest value too. Its derivative is zero there (lemma 1.82), and this derivative is a partial derivative of \(\Pi\) (definition 1.25). From steps 2 and 3 of the energy computation, with \(E A_{\mathrm{AC}}/L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4{,}9 \cdot 10^6\) N/m and \(E A_{\mathrm{BC}}/L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 3{,}92 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, we get

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial u} &= \frac{E A_{\mathrm{AC}}}{L_{\mathrm{AC}}}\,u + \frac{E A_{\mathrm{BC}}}{L_{\mathrm{BC}}}\,\big(0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\big) \cdot 0{,}8 = 0 , \\ \frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial v} &= \frac{E A_{\mathrm{BC}}}{L_{\mathrm{BC}}}\,\big(0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\big) \cdot (-0{,}6) + F = 0 . \end{aligned}\]

What do the two equations say? The expression \(E A_{\mathrm{AC}}\,u/L_{\mathrm{AC}}\) is the axial force of the bar \(AC\), and the expression \(E A_{\mathrm{BC}}\,(0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v)/L_{\mathrm{BC}}\) the axial force of the bar \(BC\). So the equations are \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} + 0{,}8\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0\) and \(-0{,}6\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} + F = 0\), the \(x\) and \(y\) components of the equilibrium of the pin \(C\) with the sign changed. The derivatives of the total potential energy are exactly the equilibrium equations of the joint. The second gives \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F/0{,}6 = 16{,}67\) kN, the first \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -0{,}8\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -13{,}33\) kN, as in section 4.1. The elongations are \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = N_{\mathrm{BC}} L_{\mathrm{BC}}/(E A_{\mathrm{BC}}) = 4{,}252\) mm and \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = -2{,}721\) mm. From \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} = u\) and \(\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0{,}8\,u - 0{,}6\,v\) it follows that

\[u = -2{,}721\ \text{mm} , \qquad v = \frac{0{,}8\,u - \Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}}{0{,}6} = \frac{-2{,}177 - 4{,}252}{0{,}6}\ \text{mm} = -10{,}71\ \text{mm} .\]

We checked with SymPy that the smallest value of the function (4.59) lies at \(u = -2/735\) m and \(v = -3/280\) m, which is the same, and that \(\Pi = -53{,}57\) J there.

The joint moves 2,721 mm to the left and 10,71 mm down (figure 4.76, right). To the left because the compressed bar \(AC\) shortens and the joint stays at its end. Down almost four times as much. Why? A vertical motion of the joint does not lengthen the bar \(AC\) to first order, since it is perpendicular to it, and it lengthens the bar \(BC\) by only \(0{,}6\) of the motion. The bracket is therefore much more flexible vertically than horizontally. This shows in equation (4.59) too, where the number in front of \(v^2\) is more than five times smaller than the number in front of \(u^2\).

Let us also confirm the observation from section 4.1. There we got the component \(v\) of the displacement of the joint from the equality of the work of the load and the energy as \(v = -2U/F\) and promised a proof. For the bracket Clapeyron's theorem reads \(2U = \vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\). We prove it like theorem 4.86, except that in the principle of virtual displacements for the truss from section 4.4 we take the true displacement of the joint instead of a virtual one. The left side of the principle is then \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{AC}} + N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\Delta L_{\mathrm{BC}}\), and each term is twice the energy of the bar, \(N\,\Delta L = 2 \cdot \tfrac12\,N\,\Delta L\) (theorem 4.3). The right side is \(\vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\). With numbers the left side is \((-13\,333\ \text{N})(-2{,}721\ \text{mm}) + 16\,667\ \text{N} \cdot 4{,}252\ \text{mm} = 36{,}28\ \text{J} + 70{,}86\ \text{J} = 107{,}14\) J, and the right side \((-10\,000\ \text{N})(-10{,}71\ \text{mm}) = 107{,}14\) J. Since \(\vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = -F\,v\), we get \(v = -2U/F = -10{,}71\) mm, as estimated. The smallest value of the energy is \(\Pi = U - \vek F\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} = U - 2U = -U = -53{,}57\) J, as for the bar with a load, where it was \(-\tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L\).

Figure 4.77 shows the function (4.59) with contour lines. A contour line (plastnica) is a line along which the function has a constant value, as a contour line on a map connects points of the same height above sea level. The contour lines are ellipses with a common centre at the lowest point. They are ellipses because \(\Pi\) is a polynomial of degree two in two variables with a smallest value, and the difference from the smallest value is, by step 4 of the computation above, a sum of two squares. The ellipses are strongly elongated and almost vertical, because the energy changes slowly in an almost vertical direction and quickly in the horizontal one. The state before loading, \(u = v = 0\), lies on the contour line \(\Pi = 0\).

Let us also test equation (4.58). Take the state in which the joint goes down by the true 10,71 mm but does not move horizontally, \(\vek u_{\mathrm{C}} + \vek w\) with \(\vek w = (2721, 0, 0)\) µm. The function (4.59) gives \(\Pi = -26{,}14\) J, which is \(27{,}43\) J more than the smallest value. By step 4 of the computation above, the energy of the difference \(\vek w\) is \(2{,}45 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot (2{,}721 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 + 1{,}96 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot (0{,}8 \cdot 2{,}721 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 18{,}14\ \text{J} + 9{,}29\ \text{J} = 27{,}43\) J, exactly that much.

4.6.6 The derivative in every direction is zero

For the bracket the partial derivatives of the total potential energy were zero in the true state. This is not special to the bracket. What happens to the energy of a body if we move from some admissible field \(\vek u\) in the direction of an admissible virtual displacement \(\vek v\), by a multiple \(\alpha\,\vek v\)? This time the field \(\vek u\) need not be the true one.

  1. The field \(\vek u + \alpha\,\vek v\) has the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} + \alpha\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) are the strain components of the field \(\vek v\), because the partial derivative of the field \(\alpha\,\vek v\) is \(\alpha\) times the partial derivative of the field \(\vek v\) (lemma 1.81).
  2. In lemma 4.90 we take \(\alpha\,\{\eta\}\) as the second strain. The mixed term is \(\alpha \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The last term is \(w(\alpha\,\{\eta\}) = \tfrac12 \sum_{\ix{p}}\sum_{\ix{q}} C_{\mathrm{pq}}\,(\alpha\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{p}})(\alpha\,\{\eta\}_{\mathrm{q}}) = \alpha^2\,w(\{\eta\})\), because every term of the energy density has two strain factors. The lemma does not ask \(\alpha\,\{\eta\}\) and \(\{\varepsilon\} + \alpha\,\{\eta\}\) to be small strains, and for a large \(\alpha\) they are not. Then we compute \(w\), and with it \(\Pi(\vek u + \alpha\,\vek v)\), with the polynomial from the lemma.
  3. We integrate and add the potential energy of the loads as in the proof of theorem 4.91:
    \[\Pi(\vek u + \alpha\,\vek v) = \Pi(\vek u) + \alpha\,B(\vek v) + \alpha^2\,U(\vek v) , \tag{4.60}\]
    where \(B(\vek v) = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\ix{i}}\sum_{\ix{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\vek v\,\mathrm{d}A\) is the internal virtual work of the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) on the virtual displacement \(\vek v\) minus the external virtual work (section 4.5).

Along every direction \(\vek v\) the energy is thus a polynomial of degree two in the number \(\alpha\), a parabola (figure 4.78). Its derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is \(B(\vek v)\), by the rules of differentiation. If \(\vek u\) is the true field, then \(B(\vek v) = 0\) for every admissible virtual displacement by the principle of virtual displacements (theorem 4.82), and all the parabolas have a horizontal tangent at \(\alpha = 0\). The converse holds too. If \(B(\vek v) = 0\) for every admissible virtual displacement and the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) are continuously differentiable, the virtual work equation holds for every such displacement, and by the converse of the principle of virtual displacements in section 4.5 the stresses of the field \(\vek u\) satisfy the equilibrium equations and the condition \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). An admissible field is therefore the true one exactly when the derivative of the total potential energy in every admissible direction is zero at it. Such a point is called a stationary point (stacionarna točka) of the energy, and the condition stationarity (SADD §6.6, PDF p. 139). The expression \(\alpha\,B(\vek v)\), the part of the change of the energy proportional to \(\alpha\), is called the first variation (prva variacija) in the books and written \(\delta\Pi\) (EP (4.3.2), p. 180, CM (7.6.1), PDF p. 344).

For the bracket \(U(\vek w)\) is \(3{,}7044\) J for \(\vek w = 1\ \text{mm}\ \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(0{,}7056\) J for \(\vek w = 1\ \text{mm}\ \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), which are the numbers in front of \(u^2\) and \(v^2\) in equation (4.59), converted to millimetres. Along the horizontal line through the lowest point, therefore, \(\Pi = -53{,}57\ \text{J} + 3{,}7044\ \text{J} \cdot \alpha^2\), and along the vertical one \(-53{,}57\ \text{J} + 0{,}7056\ \text{J} \cdot \alpha^2\), with \(\alpha\) in millimetres (figure 4.78). The derivatives along the directions \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the lowest point are the partial derivatives from the previous subsection, and both are zero.

Stationarity is weaker than a smallest value. It says only that the tangent along every line is horizontal, not that the energy is larger elsewhere. For our material the parabola (4.60) opens upward, because \(U(\vek v) \ge 0\), and the stationary point is therefore the lowest point. Stationarity, however, also holds for a nonlinear elastic material, where lemma 4.90 does not hold: EP shows that \(\delta\Pi = 0\) follows from the principle of virtual displacements for every material whose stresses are partial derivatives of an energy density (EP (4.3.2), p. 180). That the stationary point is also the lowest one must then be checked separately, and for a bar that can bend sideways it is not always so (EP Example 4.3.1, pp. 184-187).

4.6.7 Castigliano's first theorem

For the bar with a load the strain energy is \(U(e) = \tfrac12\,k\,e^2\) and its derivative \(k\,e\). At the equilibrium elongation this is \(k\,\Delta L = F\), the weight of the load (figure 4.79). For the bracket the energy \(U(u, v)\) is equation (4.59) without its last term. We differentiate by the rules of differentiation: the derivative of the term \(3{,}7044 \cdot 10^6\,u^2\) with respect to \(u\) is \(7{,}4088 \cdot 10^6\,u\), the derivative of the term \(0{,}7056 \cdot 10^6\,v^2\) with respect to \(v\) is \(1{,}4112 \cdot 10^6\,v\), and the mixed term gives \(-1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\) times the other variable for each variable. With numbers the partial derivatives are

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial U}{\partial u} &= 7{,}4088 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot u - 1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot v , \\ \frac{\partial U}{\partial v} &= -1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot u + 1{,}4112 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot v . \end{aligned}\]

At \(u = -2{,}721\) mm and \(v = -10{,}71\) mm the first derivative is \(0\) and the second \(-10\,000\) N, which we checked with SymPy. These are the \(x\) and \(y\) components of the load \(\vek F = (0, -F, 0)\). The derivative of the strain energy with respect to a displacement is thus the component of the force that acts at the point and in the direction of this displacement.

From history

Alberto Castigliano (1847-1884) was born in Asti and taught for several years, and in 1870 he entered the Turin Polytechnical Institute (TIMO, PDF p. 228). There, as a student, he did outstanding work in the theory of structures. In his thesis of 1873 he stated his theorem with a few applications, and in an extended form the Turin Academy of Sciences published it in 1875. Engineers at first did not see its importance, so in 1879 Castigliano published the work again in French, with a complete proof and many examples (TIMO, PDF p. 228). He began with a truss loaded at the joints. He wrote its strain energy with the displacements of the joints. If the displacements change a little, the energy changes by the work of the external forces on these changes, since the work of the forces is stored in the truss. If only one displacement changes by a small \(\delta\), only the corresponding force does work, \(F\,\delta\). So the derivative of the energy with respect to each displacement equals the corresponding force (TIMO, PDF p. 229). The derivative with respect to a force, which Castigliano wrote, we meet in section 4.7.4.

What we saw for the bar with a load in figure 4.79, where the slope of the energy with respect to the elongation equals the weight of the load, we now state as a theorem for an arbitrary structure.

Theorem 4.92 Castigliano's first theorem (prvi Castiglianov izrek)

Let the state of a structure made of a linear elastic material be fixed by \(n\) numbers \(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{\mathrm{n}}\), components of displacements of chosen points in chosen directions, so that every choice of the numbers gives a state that respects the supports. Let the strain energy be a function \(U(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{\mathrm{n}})\) that has partial derivatives with respect to all variables. Let only forces independent of the deformation act on the structure, such that the component of the force at the point and in the direction of the displacement \(\Delta_{\ix{i}}\) has the value \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the potential energy of the loads is \(-\sum_{\ix{i}} F_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\). If the total potential energy

\[\Pi(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{\mathrm{n}}) = U(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{\mathrm{n}}) - \sum_{\ix{i}} F_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\]

has its smallest value among all choices of the numbers at the equilibrium values of the displacements, then at these values, for every \(\ix{i}\),

\[F_{\mathrm{i}} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}} . \tag{4.61}\]
Proof

We have to show that the partial derivative of the total potential energy with respect to each displacement is zero in equilibrium, and compute this derivative.

  1. We denote the equilibrium values by \(\Delta_1^0, \dots, \Delta_{\mathrm{n}}^0\). Choose \(\ix{i}\), and for every number \(t\) let \(g(t)\) be the value of \(\Pi\) when the \(\ix{i}\)-th displacement equals \(t\) and all others equal their equilibrium values.
  2. Every choice of the number \(t\) gives a state that respects the supports, and \(\Pi\) has its smallest value at the equilibrium values among all states, so also among these. The function \(g\) therefore has its smallest value on the interval \([\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0 - 1, \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0 + 1]\) at the interior point \(t = \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0\).
  3. The derivative of \(g\) at \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0\) is the partial derivative of \(\Pi\) with respect to \(\Delta_{\ix{i}}\) at the equilibrium values, since we vary only one variable and hold the others fixed (definition 1.25, in the same way for \(n\) variables). It exists because \(U\) has it by assumption and the linear term has it by the rules of differentiation.
  4. By lemma 1.82 on the derivative at an interior extremum, \(g'(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0) = 0\).
  5. When only \(\Delta_{\ix{i}}\) varies, the term \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a linear function with derivative \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the other terms of the sum \(\sum F_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) are constant and have derivative zero (lemma 1.81). Therefore \(g'(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}^0) = \partial U/\partial\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} - F_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  6. Steps 4 and 5 give \(\partial U/\partial\Delta_{\mathrm{i}} = F_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: EP §4.3.2, (4.3.22), (4.3.23) (pp. 188-189), where the theorem is derived from stationarity and holds also for nonlinear elastic materials, and EP Example 4.3.2 (pp. 189-190) for a truss of two bars.

When does \(\Pi\) really have its smallest value at the equilibrium values? For the bar with a load we showed it with equation (4.53), for the bracket with the computation in subsection 4.6.5, and for a body, when the true displacement field is among the fields described by the numbers \(\Delta_{\ix{i}}\), by theorem 4.91. In a truss the state is always fixed by the displacements of the joints, because every bar carries a constant axial force and stretches evenly, and the computation of subsection 4.6.5 repeats with more bars and joints. EP derives the theorem from stationarity and therefore states it also for nonlinear elastic materials (EP, p. 188).

What does the theorem say? If we know the strain energy as a function of the displacements, differentiation gives the forces these displacements require. For the bracket the theorem gives two equations that are linear in the displacements:

\[\begin{aligned} 7{,}4088 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot u - 1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot v &= 0 , \\ -1{,}8816 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot u + 1{,}4112 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} \cdot v &= -10\,000\ \text{N} . \end{aligned}\]

Their solution is again \(u = -2{,}721\) mm and \(v = -10{,}71\) mm. The four numbers on the left say how much force is needed for every metre of displacement in each direction. Arranged in a matrix they are the stiffness matrix (togostna matrika) of the bracket (EP Example 4.3.3, p. 191). The matrix is symmetric, because the numbers off the diagonal are the mixed derivatives of the energy, \(\partial^2 U/\partial u\,\partial v\) and \(\partial^2 U/\partial v\,\partial u\), which are equal by theorem 1.125 on the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives. This is how the finite element method, which we will meet in chapter 7, sets up its equations, with the energy written in terms of the displacements of the joints. We did not compute the bracket with the geometry of intersecting bars but with derivatives of one single function, and this is the route a computer takes too.

4.6.8 Trial fields for the hanging bar

For the bracket the state was described by finitely many numbers. For a body, or for a bar with a load along its length, the displacement is a function, and we often cannot compute the true field. The principle of minimum energy offers a way out. We choose a family of simple fields that respect the supports and depend on a few numbers, write the energy as a function of these numbers and look for the smallest value. We get the best field in the family. Let us try it on the hanging bar, where we know the true field and can compare the approximation with it.

The model is the bar from section 4.4: a bar of length \(L\), fixed at \(x = 0\), with Young's modulus \(E\) and cross-section area \(A\), loaded by a distributed axial load \(q\), a force per unit length, and by a force \(F\) at the end \(x = L\). Every cross section moves only along the axis, by \(u(x)\), the axial force is \(N = EA\,u'\), and the energy per unit length is \(\tfrac12\,EA\,u'^2\) (section 4.1). The model is not a special case of a body with \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), since in the bar the cross sections may thin freely. So we state the principle for it separately, with a proof that is the proof of theorem 4.91 carried over to integrals along the length.

Corollary 4.93 Minimum total potential energy of a bar (najmanjša potencialna energija palice)

Let a bar lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) and be fixed at \(x = 0\). Let its Young's modulus \(E\) and its cross-section area \(A\) be positive numbers. Let a distributed axial load \(q\), continuous on \([0, L]\), act on it, and at the end \(x = L\) a force \(F\) in the direction of the axis \(x\), both independent of the deformation. For every function \(u\) that is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), at the endpoints with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), has a derivative continuous on \([0, L]\), and satisfies \(u(0) = 0\), let

\[\Pi(u) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,u'(x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q(x)\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,u(L) . \tag{4.62}\]

Let \(u\) be such a function that the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), \(N'(x) + q(x) = 0\) for every \(x \in (0, L)\) and \(N(L) = F\). Then for every function \(\tilde u\) with the same properties, that is, differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, with a derivative continuous on \([0, L]\), and with \(\tilde u(0) = 0\)

\[\Pi(\tilde u) - \Pi(u) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,\big(\tilde u'(x) - u'(x)\big)^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0 ,\]

with equality only for \(\tilde u = u\).

Proof

We have to show the same splitting as for a body, and then that the integral of a square is zero only for the zero function.

  1. Let \(v = \tilde u - u\). By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81, items 1 and 2), used for one-sided derivatives at the endpoints, \(v\) is differentiable at every point of \([0, L]\) with the derivative \(v' = \tilde u' - u'\), which is continuous as a difference of continuous functions (corollary 1.64), and \(v(0) = 0\).
  2. We expand the square: \(\tilde u'^2 = (u' + v')^2 = u'^2 + 2\,u'\,v' + v'^2\). Therefore \(\tfrac12\,EA\,\tilde u'^2 = \tfrac12\,EA\,u'^2 + N\,v' + \tfrac12\,EA\,v'^2\), because \(EA\,u' = N\).
  3. We integrate and use the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1). In the same way we split \(\int_0^L q\,\tilde u\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,u\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(F\,\tilde u(L) = F\,u(L) + F\,v(L)\). We get
    \[\Pi(\tilde u) = \Pi(u) + \Big[ \int_0^L N\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,v(L) \Big] + \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  4. By the principle of virtual displacements for a bar (theorem 4.76) with the virtual displacement \(\delta u = v\), the expression in square brackets is zero. We may do this because \(N\) is continuous on \([0, L]\), since \(u'\) is continuous, and satisfies the equilibrium of the bar, while \(v\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, with a continuous derivative and \(v(0) = 0\), so it is a virtual displacement of the bar.
  5. The function \(\tfrac12\,EA\,v'^2\) is not negative, so by the monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 4) its integral is not negative either.
  6. Let the integral of step 5 be zero, and suppose that \(v'(x_0) \ne 0\) at some point \(x_0\) of the interval \([0, L]\). The function \(f = \tfrac12\,EA\,v'^2\) is continuous and \(f(x_0) > 0\). By the definition of continuity 1.58 with the allowed error \(f(x_0)/2\) there is a closed interval \([c, d] \subseteq [0, L]\) with \(c < d\) that contains \(x_0\) and on which \(f > f(x_0)/2\).
  7. By additivity (lemma 3.39, item 2) the integral over \([0, L]\) is the sum of the integrals over \([0, c]\), \([c, d]\) and \([d, L]\). The first and the last are not negative by step 5, and the middle one is not smaller than \(\tfrac12\,f(x_0)\,(d - c) > 0\) by item 3 of the same lemma. The integral is therefore positive, which contradicts the assumption. So \(v' = 0\) on \([0, L]\).
  8. By step 1 the function \(v\) is differentiable at every point of \([0, L]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, so by lemma 1.79, that a differentiable function is continuous, it is continuous on \([0, L]\). So by corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative, \(v(x) = v(0) = 0\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\), and \(\tilde u = u\).

Source: EP (3.3.3), (3.3.4) (p. 124) for the total potential energy of bars and beams, EP §4.3.1 (pp. 179-183) for the principle. The step-by-step proof is not from the sources.

The hanging bar is steel, with length \(L = 10\) m, cross-section area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa, so \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N. The top cross section is fixed, \(u(0) = 0\), the axis \(x\) points down, the only load is the weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m per unit length, and there is no force at the lower end, \(F = 0\). The true field is \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (section 3.1). Its axial force is \(N = EA \cdot 2a\,(L - x) = \rho g A\,(L - x)\), because \(2aE = \rho g\), and it satisfies \(N' + q = -\rho g A + \rho g A = 0\) and \(N(L) = 0 = F\). By corollary 4.93 the true field therefore has the smallest energy. By Clapeyron's theorem this energy is \(-U = -391{,}03\) µJ, since the work of the weight over the whole displacement is \(2U\).

First trial field: a straight line. Take the fields \(\tilde u(x) = c\,x\) with a free number \(c\). All of them respect the fixing, \(\tilde u(0) = 0\).

  1. The derivative is \(\tilde u' = c\), so the strain energy is \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,c^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,EA\,c^2 L\) by item 3 of lemma 3.39.
  2. The function \(x^2/2\) has the derivative \(x\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus the potential energy of the weight is \(-\int_0^L q\,c\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = -\tfrac12\,q\,c\,L^2\).
  3. The total potential energy is \(\Pi(c) = \tfrac12\,EA\,L\,c^2 - \tfrac12\,q\,L^2\,c\), a parabola in \(c\).
  4. Its derivative is \(EA\,L\,c - \tfrac12\,q\,L^2\). At the lowest point it is zero by lemma 1.82, so \(c = q\,L/(2EA)\). Since \(q = \rho g A\), this is \(c = \rho g L/(2E) = a\,L = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\).

The best straight line is therefore \(\tilde u(x) = a L\,x\). For it \(\Pi = \tfrac12\,EA\,L\,(aL)^2 - \tfrac12\,\rho g A\,L^2\,aL = \tfrac12\,EA\,a^2 L^3 - EA\,a^2 L^3 = -\tfrac12\,EA\,a^2 L^3 = -293{,}27\) µJ, where we used \(\rho g = 2aE\) again. The true field has \(-\tfrac23\,EA\,a^2 L^3 = -391{,}03\) µJ, less, as the principle requires. The difference is \(97{,}76\) µJ.

How good is the approximation? Figure 4.80 shows two remarkable things. At the lower end the displacement of the line is \(aL \cdot L = aL^2 = 19{,}52\) µm, exactly the true displacement \(u(L) = a\,(2L^2 - L^2) = aL^2\). At the middle, however, the line gives \(9{,}760\) µm instead of \(14{,}64\) µm, a third too little. Why is the displacement of the end exact? The strain of the line is \(aL\) everywhere, while the strain of the true field falls from \(2aL\) at the top to zero at the bottom, and its average is \(aL\), because \(\int_0^L 2a\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = aL^2\). So the best line has the average strain of the true field. The displacement of the end is the integral of the strain along the length, so it agrees. This is no coincidence. Step 4 requires \(\int_0^L EA\,c\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the principle of virtual displacements for a bar with the virtual displacement \(\delta u = x\) says that the right side equals \(\int_0^L N\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(N\) is the true axial force. Hence \(EA\,c\,L = \int_0^L N\,\mathrm{d}x\), and \(c\) is the average of the true strain \(N/(EA)\). The line is the chord of the parabola from figure 1.111.

The difference of energies, \(97{,}76\) µJ, has a precise meaning. By corollary 4.93 it equals the strain energy of the error, the difference between the trial field and the true field. The error is \(a\,(2Lx - x^2) - aL\,x = a\,(Lx - x^2)\), with the derivative \(a\,(L - 2x)\), and its energy is \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,a^2\,(L - 2x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac16\,EA\,a^2 L^3 = 97{,}76\) µJ, which we computed with the function \((2x - L)^3/6\), whose derivative is \((2x - L)^2\). Looking for the smallest energy in a family of fields thus picks the field whose error has the smallest strain energy. The error in energy, \(97{,}76\) µJ out of \(391{,}03\) µJ, is a quarter, the error in the displacement at the middle a third, and the error in the displacement of the end zero. The energy measure is an average over the whole bar, and single points may be better or worse than it.

Second trial field: a parabola. Take the fields \(\tilde u(x) = c_1\,x + c_2\,x^2\) with two free numbers.

  1. The derivative is \(\tilde u' = c_1 + 2c_2\,x\), its square \(c_1^2 + 4c_1 c_2\,x + 4c_2^2\,x^2\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus with the functions \(x\), \(x^2/2\) and \(x^3/3\) the strain energy is \(\tfrac12\,EA\,\big(c_1^2 L + 2c_1 c_2 L^2 + \tfrac43\,c_2^2 L^3\big)\).
  2. The potential energy of the weight is \(-q\,\big(\tfrac12\,c_1 L^2 + \tfrac13\,c_2 L^3\big)\).
  3. The partial derivatives of the total potential energy with respect to \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are zero at the lowest point, as for the bracket:
    \[EA\,(c_1 L + c_2 L^2) - \tfrac12\,q\,L^2 = 0 , \qquad EA\,\big(c_1 L^2 + \tfrac43\,c_2 L^3\big) - \tfrac13\,q\,L^3 = 0 .\]
  4. We divide the first equation by \(EA\,L\) and the second by \(EA\,L^2\), and use \(q/(EA) = \rho g/E = 2a\): \(c_1 + c_2 L = aL\) and \(c_1 + \tfrac43\,c_2 L = \tfrac23\,aL\).
  5. We subtract the first from the second: \(\tfrac13\,c_2 L = -\tfrac13\,aL\), so \(c_2 = -a\). The first then gives \(c_1 = aL + aL = 2aL\).

The best parabola is \(2aL\,x - a\,x^2 = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), exactly the true field, and its energy is \(-391{,}03\) µJ. Why exactly? The family contains the true field, since we get it for \(c_1 = 2aL\) and \(c_2 = -a\). By corollary 4.93 the true field has a smaller energy than all other admissible fields, so also than all other parabolas, and the search for the smallest value in the family finds it. We checked with SymPy that the system of step 3 gives exactly these two numbers.

The example shows the idea that we will develop into a systematic computation with trial fields in chapter 6. Every family of trial fields gives an energy that is not smaller than the true one, so every trial computation is an estimate of the true energy from above (CM, PDF p. 343). A richer family that contains a poorer one gives an estimate at least as good, since it searches among more fields. The finite element method of chapter 7 cuts the bar into pieces and takes a straight line on each piece, which is a family that we can make as rich as we like by a finer division.

With this we have answered the question from the start of the section. For a body made of an ideal elastic and linear elastic material with loads independent of the deformation, the total potential energy of the true displacement field is the smallest among all kinematically admissible fields. We proved this by splitting the energy of any admissible field into the energy of the true field, the work of the stresses on the difference, which the principle of virtual displacements cancels against the potential energy of the loads, and the strain energy of the difference, which is not negative. Clapeyron's theorem, from the principle of virtual displacements with the true field in place of a virtual one, gave \(2U\) as the work of the full loads, and so confirmed that the work of slow loading is stored completely. For the bracket, the search for the smallest value of a function of two displacements gave the displacement of the joint, 2,721 mm to the left and 10,71 mm down, and the derivatives of the energy gave the equilibrium of the joint and Castigliano's first theorem. For the hanging bar the two trial fields showed how the principle picks the best approximation. The principle searches among displacement fields that respect the supports and gets the equilibrium as the condition for the smallest value. In the next section we swap the roles: we will search among stresses that are in equilibrium, and the condition will be that the strains fit the displacements and the supports. For this we will need the complementary strain energy density from section 3.6.
