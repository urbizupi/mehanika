The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

We begin with bodies from everyday life: the floor of a storey, a trench cover, the sheet metal of a car body, the wing of an aircraft. All are flat and have one dimension, the thickness, much smaller than the other two. In 5 we treated slender bodies and described them by a function of one variable, the deflection of the axis. Here only one dimension is small, so we must write equilibrium, energy and vibration for two dimensions at once.

In everyday language the word plate (plošča) means a flat, thin piece. Engineers use it for a flat body that is thin compared with its other two dimensions and is loaded perpendicular to its plane. A plate is therefore distinguished from a shell (lupina), which is just as thin but curved. As a beam is described by its axis, a plate is described by the plane halfway through its thickness.

Why this matters is shown by a steel plate with a square plan of side \(500\) mm and thickness \(10\) mm, which accompanies all the calculations of the chapter. Under a load of \(10\) kPa it bends a little more than a tenth of a millimetre at its centre, and its lowest natural frequency is about \(190\) Hz. The first figure decides whether a floor or a cover carries the load, the second whether a machine turning at a similar frequency excites the plate. If we compress the plate in its own plane it can buckle, and the chapter shows at which load.

The chapter promises three things. First, from the assumptions about a thin plate we derive, following the beam, the displacements, the internal forces, a single equation of equilibrium and the edge conditions of the different supports. Second, we solve the equation on a strip, on a rectangle with the series of 13 and on a circular plate, and then use energy methods to find vibration and buckling. Third, we show where the simplification fails for thick plates and carry the plate over to the finite elements of 12.
