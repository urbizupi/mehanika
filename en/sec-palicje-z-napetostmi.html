Chapter 29 Structural optimization

29.2 Trusses with stress constraints (paličje z napetostnimi omejitvami)

In section 7.5.2 we gave the two bars on the wall of section 4.1.4 the area \(100\ \text{mm}^2\), because it was a round number, not because anyone had chosen it. The stresses were \(-133{,}3\) and \(166{,}7\) MPa, both below the yield stress of steel, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (chapter 3). Both bars therefore have a reserve. The question of optimization: how much steel can we save if we use up the reserve? Material is paid for by volume, so we look for the two bars of least total volume that still hold. This is a problem of definition 29.1, and in this section we solve it by hand, on a truss we know, so that we see what the general methods will later do.

29.2.1 What the bars carry

We put the truss on the board (figure 29.11, left). We draw the wall with hatching and on it the pins \(A\) and \(B\), \(B\) at \(h = 3\) m above \(A\). From \(A\) horizontally to the joint \(C\) runs bar \(AC\) with cross-section area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\), from \(B\) obliquely to \(C\) bar \(BC\) with area \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). At the joint \(C\), at distance \(a = 4\) m from the wall, hangs the load, pulling down with the force \(F = 10\) kN. Notation: \(A\) and \(B\) here are points on the wall, areas are written with an index, \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). Likewise \(C\) here is the joint, later in the chapter \(C\) will also mean compliance, and the joint will then have another name.

What the bars carry is told by the equilibrium of the joint, not by their thickness. We cut the joint \(C\) out in our mind (figure 29.11, right) and draw what acts on it. The load force \(F\) pulls down. Bar \(AC\) is compressed under the load and pushes the joint away, so its force on the joint points toward the joint along the axis of the bar and has magnitude \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\). Bar \(BC\) is stretched and pulls the joint toward \(B\) along its axis, its force has magnitude \(N_{\mathrm{BC}}\). That the forces point along the bars is guaranteed by lemma 4.5 on the force in a bar of a truss. Let \(\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} = (1, 0)\) and \(\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} = (a, -h)/s\) be the unit vectors from the wall toward \(C\), where \(s = \sqrt{a^2 + h^2}\) is the length of bar \(BC\). The force of bar \(e\) on the joint is \(-N_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\), as in section 4.1.4. By the first condition of equilibrium (2.2) for the joint \(C\), \(-N_{\mathrm{AC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{AC}} - N_{\mathrm{BC}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{BC}} + (0, -F) = \vek 0\). These are two equations,

\[N_{\mathrm{AC}} + \frac{a}{s}\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = 0 , \qquad -\frac{h}{s}\,N_{\mathrm{BC}} = -F , \tag{29.4}\]

for two unknowns. The second gives \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F\,s/h\), the first \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -F\,a/h\). At \(h = 3\) m we have \(s = 5\) m, \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac53 F = 16{,}67\) kN and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -\tfrac43 F = -13{,}33\) kN, as in section 4.1.4. No area appears in the equations. We could make the bars thinner or thicker and the equilibrium of the joint would stay the same. This property gets a name.

Definition 29.9 Statically determinate truss (statično določeno paličje)

A truss (definition 4.4) with one load case is statically determinate (statično določeno) if the equilibrium of all its free joints, written as a system of equations for the axial forces \(N_1, \dots, N_{\mathrm{n}}\) of all bars, has exactly one solution for the given forces on the joints. The axial forces are then computed from equilibrium, and contain neither the cross-section areas \(A_{\mathrm{e}}\) nor the modulus of elasticity.

Source: CHK §2.1 (PDF p. 19): when the number of bars equals the number of degrees of freedom of the joint, the forces in the bars follow from the equilibrium equations alone, the truss is statically determinate. The definition is ours, in the form that lemma 29.10 needs.

Our truss is statically determinate: the matrix of system (29.4) has determinant \(1\cdot(-h/s) - (a/s)\cdot 0 = -h/s \ne 0\), so the solution is unique. This will not always be so. A truss with more bars than the joint has degrees of freedom is not statically determinate, and the forces in its bars then depend on the stiffness of the bars, that is, on the areas. We deal with it later in the chapter, in the section on least compliance.

29.2.2 The optimization problem

Let us write the problem as in definition 29.1. First the model and the idealizations, because the result holds only for them.

  • The model is the truss of section 4.1.4 with steel, \(E = 196\) GPa, density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³. The bars have a solid round section, so each is determined by its cross-section area.
  • Idealization 1: small displacements. We compute the forces on the undeformed position. At stress \(\sigma_0\) the strain is \(\sigma_0/E = 220\ \text{MPa}/196\ \text{GPa} = 1{,}12\cdot 10^{-3}\). When we actually move the joint \(C\) (the distances from \(A\) and \(B\) change by this fraction, the joint is found by Newton's method in NumPy) and compute the equilibrium of the joint once more on the deformed position, the forces change by \(0{,}11\) %. The source of this number is the computation in num-02.py.
  • Idealization 2: allowed stress \(\sigma_0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa in tension and compression, with no safety factor. A factor would multiply all areas by the same number, as we shall see from equation (29.7) (a factor of \(1{,}5\) gives \(V = 931{,}8\ \text{cm}^3\)). It does not help with constraints that are not stress constraints, for example buckling in the last section, where the limit is not proportional to the stress.
  • Idealization 3: the weight of the bars is neglected. At the areas we find in this section the two bars weigh \(47{,}5\) N together against \(10\) kN, that is \(0{,}48\) %. Lemma 4.5 would then not hold exactly, since weight acts between the joints.
  • Idealization 4: areas are arbitrary positive numbers. Real bars have sections from a catalogue. The effect is small: rounding each area up to a whole mm² (\(61\) and \(76\ \text{mm}^2\)) gives \(V = 624\ \text{cm}^3\), which is \(0{,}45\) % more than \(621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\).

The design variables (projektni spremenljivki) are the areas \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\). The objective function (ciljna funkcija) is the volume of material,

\[V(A_{\mathrm{AC}}, A_{\mathrm{BC}}) = L_{\mathrm{AC}}\,A_{\mathrm{AC}} + L_{\mathrm{BC}}\,A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 4\,A_{\mathrm{AC}} + 5\,A_{\mathrm{BC}} \quad (\text{m}\cdot\text{mm}^2 = \text{cm}^3) , \tag{29.5}\]

because a straight bar with cross-section area \(A\) and length \(L\) contains \(A\,L\) of material (area times length), and because \(1\ \text{mm}^2\cdot 1\ \text{m} = 1000\ \text{mm}^3 = 1\ \text{cm}^3\). We use volume instead of weight. The weight is \(\rho\,g\,V\) with the same density \(\rho\) in both bars, so it is proportional to the volume, and both are smallest at the same areas. (The letter \(g\) elsewhere in the book means the acceleration of gravity. Here we write it for the acceleration only in this remark, otherwise it denotes constraints, \(g_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(g_{\mathrm{BC}}\).)

The stress constraint (omejitev napetosti) demands that the stress in each bar, by definition 2.6, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{e}} = N_{\mathrm{e}}/A_{\mathrm{e}}\), not exceed the allowed one in absolute value,

\[g_{\mathrm{e}}(A_{\mathrm{e}}) = \frac{|N_{\mathrm{e}}|}{A_{\mathrm{e}}} - \sigma_0 \le 0 , \qquad e \in \{AC, BC\} . \tag{29.6}\]

By static determinacy, \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) are numbers, not functions of the areas. Since \(A_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\), multiplying the inequality by \(A_{\mathrm{e}}/\sigma_0 > 0\) does not reverse its direction and gives \(A_{\mathrm{e}} \ge |N_{\mathrm{e}}|/\sigma_0\). So only lower bounds on the areas remain, not two curves (figure 29.12). On the figure the axes carry the areas \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}}\) in mm². The feasible set (definition 29.5) is a corner, bounded by the vertical line at \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 13\,333/220 = 60{,}61\ \text{mm}^2\) and the horizontal at \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 16\,667/220 = 75{,}76\ \text{mm}^2\), and it extends without limit upward and to the right. There is no bound above, since a thicker bar still holds, and is only dearer.

Where in it is the least volume? Equation (29.5) is linear, so the points of equal volume are straight lines, the level lines of the objective. For \(V = 400\ \text{cm}^3\) (dashed) the line lies entirely below the feasible set, no section of this volume holds. For \(V = 621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\) the line just touches the feasible set at the corner. For \(V = 900\ \text{cm}^3\) it cuts it, and on it lies our first choice, \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 100\ \text{mm}^2\), which is inside the set. Any move from this choice toward smaller areas is a saving, until we hit one of the bounds. The same reasoning holds for every truss of this kind, so we prove it.

Lemma 29.10 The fully stressed truss is optimal (polno napeto paličje je optimalno)

Let the truss be statically determinate (definition 29.9) with \(n\) bars of lengths \(L_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) and axial forces \(N_{\mathrm{e}} \ne 0\), and let \(\sigma_0 > 0\). Among all areas \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\) that satisfy \(|N_{\mathrm{e}}|/A_{\mathrm{e}} \le \sigma_0\) for all \(e\), the volume \(V = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}} A_{\mathrm{e}}\) has exactly one least value, at

\[A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*} = \frac{|N_{\mathrm{e}}|}{\sigma_0} , \quad e = 1, \dots, n , \tag{29.7}\]

and the stress in every bar is then exactly \(\sigma_0\) in absolute value.

Proof

We must show that the volume has its least value on the feasible set at \(A^*\) and nowhere else.

  1. Since \(N_{\mathrm{e}}\) are numbers independent of the areas (definition 29.9), \(|N_{\mathrm{e}}|/A_{\mathrm{e}} \le \sigma_0\) with \(A_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) is equivalent to \(A_{\mathrm{e}} \ge |N_{\mathrm{e}}|/\sigma_0 = A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\), because we multiply by \(A_{\mathrm{e}}/\sigma_0 > 0\). The feasible set is therefore \(\{A : A_{\mathrm{e}} \ge A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\ \forall e\}\), the lower bounds of different bars do not influence each other.
  2. Let \(A\) be feasible. Then \(V(A) - V(A^{*}) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} L_{\mathrm{e}}\,(A_{\mathrm{e}} - A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*})\), and every term is nonnegative, since \(L_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) and \(A_{\mathrm{e}} - A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*} \ge 0\) by step 1. In figure 29.12, \(A_{\mathrm{e}} - A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\) is the distance of the point \(A\) from the wall on which the optimum lies, and every such distance only increases the volume.
  3. A sum of nonnegative terms is zero exactly when every term is zero, so \(A_{\mathrm{e}} = A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\) for every \(e\), since \(L_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\). The point \(A^{*}\) is feasible and is the only least one.
  4. The stress at \(A^{*}\) is \(N_{\mathrm{e}}/A_{\mathrm{e}}^{*} = \sigma_0\,N_{\mathrm{e}}/|N_{\mathrm{e}}| = \pm\sigma_0\).

Source: CHK §2.1 (PDF pp. 18 to 21) solves the same kind of problem for a truss of two stretched bars of equal length graphically as a linear program in two variables (figure 2.3, PDF p. 20) and finds that at the optimum the stress in both bars is at its upper bound (PDF p. 21). The proof is not from the source.

Let us draw steps 2 and 3 on our truss (figure 29.13). We stack the volume of a choice into a column. At the bottom there is always the same piece, the volume of the optimum \(V(A^{*}) = 621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\), reaching up to the dashed line. For the choice of \(100\ \text{mm}^2\) we put on it the excess of bar \(AC\), \(4\cdot(100 - 60{,}61) = 157{,}6\ \text{cm}^3\), and then the excess of bar \(BC\), \(5\cdot(100 - 75{,}76) = 121{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\). The column reaches \(900\ \text{cm}^3\). We cannot take any piece away, since no bar may go below its bound. Let us thin bar \(AC\) down to its bound. Its piece disappears, but the column is still \(121{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\) above the line, at \(742{,}4\ \text{cm}^3\). The column reaches the line only when both pieces are gone, that is, at \(A^{*}\).

The lemma says that the problem is trivial once the forces are known: each bar gets the least area that still holds its stress. CHK notes that such a linear structure is rare and depends on the truss being statically determinate and the constraints being simple (§2.1, PDF p. 20). The requirement \(N_{\mathrm{e}} \ne 0\) is a condition of the lemma: a bar with \(N_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\) would have least area zero, and the requirement \(A_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) does not let a least value be found, since the infimum zero would not be reached. The bar disappears from the structure, which is a topology change (CHK §2.1, PDF p. 21), and with the vanished bar the truss changes. What this does to equilibrium we shall see in the section on least compliance.

Example 29.11 The fully stressed wall truss (polno napeto paličje na steni)

Equation (29.7) gives at \(h = 3\) m, \(F = 10\) kN and \(\sigma_0 = 220\) MPa

\[A_{\mathrm{AC}}^{*} = \frac{13\,333{,}3\ \text{N}}{220\ \text{N/mm}^2} = 60{,}61\ \text{mm}^2 , \qquad A_{\mathrm{BC}}^{*} = \frac{16\,666{,}7\ \text{N}}{220\ \text{N/mm}^2} = 75{,}76\ \text{mm}^2 .\]

By equation (29.5), \(V = 4\cdot 60{,}61 + 5\cdot 75{,}76 = 621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\), mass \(\rho V = 4{,}845\) kg. The first choice with \(100\ \text{mm}^2\) had \(900\ \text{cm}^3\). In figure 29.12 this is the move from the choice with \(100\ \text{mm}^2\) down along the level lines to the optimum. We saved \((900 - 621{,}2)/900 = 31\) % of the material, and the stresses in the bars are now \(-220\) and \(220\) MPa instead of \(-133{,}3\) and \(166{,}7\) MPa (NumPy, num-02.py).

Back to the assumption of static determinacy: without it step 1 of the proof fails, because the forces are functions of the areas and the constraints are no longer an upper or lower bound on one variable. The first example of such a problem is in the section on least compliance, later in the chapter.

29.2.3 The best height of the joint

So far we have held \(B\) at \(h = 3\) m. Nobody prescribes the position of the pin on the wall, and the height is one more design variable. Since it changes the shape of the truss and not only the thicknesses, it is a shape variable (see definition 29.4 on the kinds of optimization). At each height we can use lemma 29.10, so the least volume at that height is a function of one variable, \(V(h)\). Which height reduces it most? We expect two opposing effects. A flat truss (small \(h\)) has by equation (29.4) large forces, \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F\,s/h\) and \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -F\,a/h\), which grow as \(h\) falls, so they need thick bars. A steep truss (large \(h\)) has small forces, but a longer bar \(BC\) with \(s = \sqrt{a^2 + h^2}\). The best height is somewhere in between (figure 29.14).

Lemma 29.12 Best height of the joint (najboljša višina vozlišča)

Let the pin \(A\) be on the wall at \((-a, 0)\), the pin \(B\) at \((-a, h)\) with \(h > 0\), the joint \(C\) at \((0, 0)\) with a vertical load \(F > 0\), and let \(\sigma_0 > 0\). The volume of the fully stressed truss is

\[V(h) = \frac{F\,(2a^2 + h^2)}{h\,\sigma_0} = \frac{F}{\sigma_0}\Big(h + \frac{2a^2}{h}\Big) , \tag{29.8}\]

and on \(h \in (0, \infty)\) it has exactly one least value, at \(h = a\sqrt{2}\), equal to \(V = 2\sqrt{2}\,F\,a/\sigma_0\). The least volume over all areas and heights is this value.

Proof

We must show the form of equation (29.8) and that \(h = a\sqrt2\) attains the least value.

  1. By equation (29.4), \(N_{\mathrm{AC}} = -F\,a/h\) and \(N_{\mathrm{BC}} = F\,s/h\), both nonzero. By lemma 29.10 the least volume at the given height is at \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = F\,a/(h\,\sigma_0)\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = F\,s/(h\,\sigma_0)\).
  2. The bars have lengths \(a\) and \(s\), so \(V(h) = a\cdot\dfrac{F\,a}{h\,\sigma_0} + s\cdot\dfrac{F\,s}{h\,\sigma_0} = \dfrac{F\,(a^2 + s^2)}{h\,\sigma_0}\). With \(s^2 = a^2 + h^2\) this is equation (29.8).
  3. For every \(h > 0\) the difference between the sum \(h + 2a^2/h\) and the height \(2\sqrt2\,a\) equals \(\dfrac{(h - \sqrt2\,a)^2}{h}\). We check this by multiplying out: \((h - \sqrt2\,a)^2 = h^2 - 2\sqrt2\,a h + 2a^2\), and divided by \(h\) this is \(h - 2\sqrt2\,a + 2a^2/h\).
  4. The difference is a square divided by a positive \(h\), so it is \(\ge 0\), and it equals zero exactly at \(h = \sqrt2\,a\). So \(V(h) \ge \dfrac{F}{\sigma_0}\,2\sqrt2\,a\), the sum lies above the line, with equality only at \(h = a\sqrt2\).
  5. Every choice \((h, A_{\mathrm{AC}}, A_{\mathrm{BC}})\) with feasible areas has by lemma 29.10 volume at least \(V(h)\), so by step 4 at least \(2\sqrt2\,F a/\sigma_0\), and this value is attained (figure 29.14, the lowest point).

Source: CHK in the introduction of chapter 2 (PDF p. 18) mentions that an example of combined shape and sizing optimization of a two-bar truss is in exercise 2.5. We did not read the exercise. The variable \(h\), the proof and equation (29.8) are not from the source.

Let us draw steps 3 and 4 (figure 29.15, left). On the horizontal axis we put the height \(h\), on the vertical one the volume in units of \(F/\sigma_0\), that is, \(h + 2a^2/h\) for \(a = 4\) m. First we draw the line \(h\). This is the "price of the length", since bar \(BC\) is long at large \(h\). Then we draw the hyperbola \(2a^2/h\), the "price of the forces", since both forces are large at small \(h\). Adding the two gives the sum. At the height \(2\sqrt2\,a = 11{,}31\) m we draw a horizontal line. At \(h = 2\) m the sum is \(2 + 16 = 18\) m, and the vertical difference to the line is \(6{,}69\) m, exactly \((2 - 4\sqrt2)^2/2\). This difference is a square divided by a positive \(h\), so the sum never falls below the line. It touches the line only where the square is zero, at \(h = a\sqrt2\), and right below this touching point the line and the hyperbola cross, the two terms are equal.

Why the terms are equal at the least sum is shown on the right of the figure. We take the two terms as the sides of a rectangle. Its area is \(h\cdot 2a^2/h = 2a^2 = 32\ \text{m}^2\) at every height. We draw three: a narrow tall one at \(h = 2\) m, the square at \(h = a\sqrt2\) and a wide low one at \(h = 10\) m. Below them we lay the two sides of each one after the other and get three segments, \(18\), \(11{,}31\) and \(13{,}2\) m long. The shortest is the segment of the square. So a sum of two positive numbers with a given product is smallest when the numbers are equal, \(h = 2a^2/h\).

With our numbers \(F a/\sigma_0 = 181{,}8\ \text{cm}^3\) and the best height is \(h = 4\sqrt2 = 5{,}657\) m, which corresponds to the angle \(\arctan\sqrt2 = 54{,}74^\circ\) between \(BC\) and \(AC\). There \(V = 2\sqrt2\cdot 181{,}8 = 514{,}3\ \text{cm}^3\), and the areas are \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 32{,}14\ \text{mm}^2\) and \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 55{,}67\ \text{mm}^2\). Figure 29.14 shows it. The axes carry the height \(h\) and the volume \(V\). The curve \(V(h)\) falls from the left. The point at \(h = 1{,}5\) m is at \(1037{,}9\ \text{cm}^3\), the point at \(3\) m at \(621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\), the lowest point is at \(h = a\sqrt2\), which the dashed guides point to, and the point at \(8\) m is higher again, at \(545{,}5\ \text{cm}^3\). Below the curve are the trusses for three heights, with line widths proportional to the areas. At \(h = 1{,}5\) m both bars are thick, at \(h = 3\) m thinner, at \(h = 5{,}657\) m thinnest, but longer.

29.2.4 When fully stressed is not enough

Lemma 29.10 has a condition that is easy to overlook: the only constraints are stress constraints (figure 29.16 shows what happens when this fails). Let us see what happens if we add one that is always present in mechanics, for a compressed bar. Bar \(AC\) is in compression, and a compressed bar can lose stability: the straight position is still an equilibrium, but no longer stable, and the bar bows out sideways (buckling, uklon). By theorem 8.150 the second variation of the potential energy is positive exactly for \(P < P_{\mathrm{cr}} = \pi^2 EI/L^2\), for a bar with pinned ends, of length \(L\), with bending stiffness \(EI\). A bar with pins at the ends corresponds to the model we have.

Take a solid round section of diameter \(d\) (figure 29.16, section at the top). From \(A = \pi d^2/4\) follows \(d^2 = 4A/\pi\) and \(I = \pi d^4/64 = A^2/(4\pi)\). The critical force is by theorem 8.150

\[P_{\mathrm{cr}}(A) = \frac{\pi^2 E}{L^2}\cdot\frac{A^2}{4\pi} = \frac{\pi E A^2}{4 L^2} . \tag{29.9}\]

The fully stressed bar \(AC\) at \(h = 3\) m has \(A = 60{,}61\ \text{mm}^2\), \(d = 8{,}78\) mm and \(L = 4\) m. Its critical force is \(P_{\mathrm{cr}} = 35{,}34\) N, and it is loaded with \(13\,333\) N, \(377\) times more (figure 29.16). The thin bar with the section of diameter 8,78 mm, at the top of the figure, would bow out under the force \(|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\), and the bow is drawn strongly exaggerated. Both bars in the figure have the same length \(L\). The stress constraint is satisfied here with equality, but the bar does not fail because the material would exceed its limit, it fails because it loses stability, and the stress says nothing about that.

So let us add a second constraint, which the thick bar at the bottom of figure 29.16 satisfies, \(P_{\mathrm{cr}}(A_{\mathrm{AC}}) \ge |N_{\mathrm{AC}}|\) (with no safety factor, as for stress). By equation (29.9) this is \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}^2 \ge 4L^2|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|/(\pi E)\), so

\[A_{\mathrm{AC}} \ge 2L\sqrt{\frac{|N_{\mathrm{AC}}|}{\pi E}} = 8\ \text{m}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{13\,333\ \text{N}}{\pi\cdot 196\cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}}} = 1177\ \text{mm}^2 . \tag{29.10}\]

The constraint is nonlinear in \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\), but still a lower bound on one variable, because \(N_{\mathrm{AC}}\) does not depend on the areas. What this means for the proof of lemma 29.10 we see in figure 29.17.

We take the axis of the area \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\). The stress constraint allows all areas from \(60{,}61\ \text{mm}^2\) on, which we draw as the blue ray. The buckling constraint by equation (29.10) allows all from \(1177\ \text{mm}^2\) on, the orange ray. Both hold where the rays overlap, and that is the ray from the larger bound on. The larger one is the buckling bound. Above the axis we draw the volume at \(A_{\mathrm{BC}} = 75{,}76\ \text{mm}^2\). It grows with \(A_{\mathrm{AC}}\), since \(L_{\mathrm{AC}} = 4\) m is positive, so on the feasible part it is least at the left end, at the point at \(1177\ \text{mm}^2\). This is step 2 of the proof of the lemma with the new lower bound, so the proof still holds. The fully stressed bar with \(60{,}61\ \text{mm}^2\) lies to the left of this bound and is no longer feasible.

Example 29.14 Buckling defeats the fully stressed truss (uklon premaga polno napeto paličje)

With the bound (29.10) we have \(A_{\mathrm{AC}} = 1177{,}2\ \text{mm}^2\), \(d = 38{,}72\) mm (figure 29.16, the thick bar and its section at the bottom, drawn to the same scale as the thin one). The stress is \(13\,333/1177{,}2 = 11{,}33\) MPa, which is about a twentieth of the allowed one. The volume is \(4\cdot 1177{,}2 + 5\cdot 75{,}76 = 5087{,}7\ \text{cm}^3\), \(8{,}2\) times as much as \(621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\), and the mass is \(39{,}7\) kg. Bar \(BC\) is in tension and does not lose stability, it stays at \(75{,}76\ \text{mm}^2\).

The conclusion (figure 29.16, the thick bar has a stress far below the allowed one) we shall need in the following sections: the fully stressed truss is not optimal in general, only when all constraints are stress constraints. When a constraint arrives that is below the allowed stress (buckling, displacement, natural frequency), the optimal bar sits at the bound of that constraint, and its stress is below the allowed one. Lemma 29.10 stays in the form "each constraint is a lower bound on one area, so each bar gets the largest of the bounds". This no longer holds when a constraint involves several areas at once or when the forces are not independent of the areas. Such constraints (displacement of the joint) will come up in the following sections of the chapter.

Buckling also changes the best height of figure 29.14, where the lowest point would no longer hold. With the bound (29.10) at every height, the least volume over the height has its minimum at \(h = 20{,}56\) m with \(V = 2768{,}6\ \text{cm}^3\) (NumPy, grid search from 0,5 to 200 m, num-02.py), and no longer at \(5{,}657\) m. Such a height next to a \(4\) m long horizontal bar is not sensible, so the problem would need a geometry constraint as well (the wall is not higher than a few metres). Nor can the weight be neglected as lightly any more: at \(h = 3\) m with the buckling bound the two bars weigh \(389\) N, that is \(3{,}9\) % of \(F\) instead of \(0{,}48\) %. We also chose a solid round section because of \(I = A^2/(4\pi)\). A tube would have a larger \(I\) at the same area, so it would resist buckling with a smaller area and a smaller volume, but that is a problem with two variables per section (CHK §2.2, PDF pp. 21 to 23 uses a round section as we do, and finds that, depending on the coefficients, the stress or the buckling constraint is active, PDF p. 22).

29.2.5 What we have learned

On the truss of chapters 4 and 7 we derived three things. First, for a statically determinate truss with stress constraints the least volume is at the fully stressed truss (lemma 29.10), \(621{,}2\ \text{cm}^3\) instead of \(900\ \text{cm}^3\). Second, the height of the joint is a shape variable with the best value \(a\sqrt2\) and \(514{,}3\ \text{cm}^3\) (lemma 29.12). Third, when a constraint arrives that is not a stress constraint, fully stressed is no longer optimal, buckling at \(h = 3\) m demands \(8{,}2\) times as much material. On the same truss we shall try, in the following sections of the chapter, general methods that do not require static determinacy, among others for a constraint on the displacement of the joint, which depends on both areas at once and for which the fully stressed truss does not help.
