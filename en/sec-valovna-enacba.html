Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.1 The string and the wave equation

The cord of a violin or a guitar is long, thin and stretched between two rigid points. If we pull it aside and let go, it vibrates, and each of its points moves differently. Which equation determines these motions, and how fast does a disturbance travel along the cord?

From history

In a treatise of 1747 on the famous problem of vibrating chords, d'Alembert was led to partial differential equations (CAJORI, PDF p. 258). The displacement of a cord depends on two variables, the place along the cord and the time, so one had to differentiate with respect to both. For the equation in which the second derivative of the displacement with respect to time equals \(a^2\) times the second derivative with respect to place, he gave the general solution \(y = f(x + at) + \varphi(x - at)\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 258). Each term is a shape that moves: \(f(x + at)\) has the same value wherever \(x + at\) has the same value, that is at \(x = c - at\), so the shape \(f\) moves to the left with speed \(a\) and the shape \(\varphi\) to the right, as figure 13.8 below shows. By this solution \(f\) and \(\varphi\) are arbitrary. He also showed that only one arbitrary function remains if \(y\) is zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = l\) (CAJORI, PDF p. 258). The reason is that the two boundary conditions tie the functions together. At \(x = 0\) we need \(f(at) + \varphi(-at) = 0\) at every time, so \(\varphi(s) = -f(-s)\) and the first function fixes the second. The graph of \(\varphi\) is the graph of \(f\) turned by half a turn about the origin, just as in figure 13.15 of the next section the orange part on \([-L, 0]\) is the blue part on \([0, L]\) turned. At \(x = l\) this then gives \(f(l + at) = f(at - l)\), so \(f\) repeats after a step \(2l\), as in the same figure the pattern repeats on \([L, 2L]\). A cord with fixed ends therefore admits one arbitrary function, not two. The dispute whether this solution and Bernoulli's series of sines cover all shapes of the cord is told in section 9.11 of chapter 9.

The story showed two things: the equation of the cord has a solution made of two shapes travelling in opposite directions, and fixed ends leave only one arbitrary shape. Here we derive the equation from a model of the cord and solve it ourselves. We start with a model: we must say what we assume about the cord, and for each assumption how far a real cord departs from it. We stretch the cord along the axis \(x\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\) and fasten it rigidly at both ends. Its state at time \(t\) is described by the displacement \(w(x, t)\) of the point at \(x\) perpendicular to the axis, in one plane. The running example (example A) is a steel cord of diameter \(d = 0{,}4\) mm, length \(L = 0{,}65\) m and tension \(T = 70\) N. Its cross section is \(A = \pi d^2/4 = 1{,}2566\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^2\), the density of steel is \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\), so the mass per unit length is \(\mu = \rho A = 9{,}802\cdot 10^{-4}\) kg/m.

The model has six idealizations (idealizacije). We name each and give it a measure, so that we know what is neglected. Figure 13.2 has a part for each, marked with its number in a circle.

  1. The cord is homogeneous: \(\mu\) is constant along the cord. We divide the cord into equal pieces of length \(h\), and each has the same mass \(\mu h\). For the cord of example A this means that the diameter \(d\) and the density \(\rho\) are the same everywhere. We have no measure from a source for how much the diameter of a real cord varies, so we say what a variation would do. The mass per unit length is \(\mu = \rho\pi d^2/4\), so a diameter larger by \(1\) % would give a \(\mu\) larger by \(2{,}01\) %, and the speed \(\sqrt{T/\mu}\) with which a disturbance travels along the cord, computed in this section, would be smaller by \(0{,}99\) % on that part. It does not hold for a cord with a knot, a bead or a part of another diameter.
  2. The cord has no bending stiffness. The force in it is therefore directed along the tangent to the cord at every cross section. The bending stiffness of the steel cord with Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa (definition 3.1), the value we take for steel in the whole chapter, is \(EI = E\,\pi d^4/64 = 2{,}46\cdot 10^{-4}\) N m\(^2\) (definition 5.26 for a circle of diameter \(d\)). The force that bending stiffness puts up against the bending of a piece of length \(L\) is of the order \(EI/L^2 = 5{,}8\cdot 10^{-4}\) N, and the tension force is \(T\). Their ratio \(EI/(T L^2) = 8{,}3\cdot 10^{-6}\) is very small, so this idealization is good for the cord of example A. This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a calculation. The ratio grows with the fourth power of the diameter: for a cord ten times thicker with the same tension and length it would be \(10^4\) times larger, \(0{,}083\), and the idealization would no longer be good.
  3. The tension \(T\) is constant in magnitude. The cord stretches a little as it moves and the force changes: at slope \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) (idealization 4) the stretch is at most \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\) of the original length (derivation 13.1, step 3, the estimate of \(\sqrt{1 + 10^{-4}} - 1\)). By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, uniaxial stress \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\)) the force changes by \(EA\cdot 5\cdot 10^{-5} = 24{,}63\ \text{kN}\cdot 5\cdot 10^{-5} = 1{,}23\) N, which is at most \(1{,}8\) % of \(T\). At a force of \(71{,}23\) N instead of \(70\) N the speed \(\sqrt{T/\mu}\) with which a disturbance travels along the cord, computed in this section, would be larger by \(0{,}88\) %. This gives only the size of the neglected effect, not a bound on the error: with the real stretch the force varies along the cord and with time, which a model with a single speed does not describe. GR (PDF p. 276) assumes that the prestress dominates the effect of the stretching of the cord ("cable effect"), and the model neglects it too.
  4. The slope is small: \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\), which means that the tangent makes an angle of at most \(0{,}573^\circ\) with the axis. For a displacement in the shape of one sine arc, \(w = \varepsilon\sin(\pi x/L)\), the largest slope is \(\varepsilon\pi/L\), so \(\varepsilon \le 2{,}07\) mm for the cord of example A. What this bound does to the result is told by steps 3, 5 and 6 of the derivation below, which bound the errors by \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\), \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(1\) %. At a slope ten times larger, \(0{,}1\), the last error would be \(10\) %.
  5. The points of the cord move only transversally, they do not move along the axis. The point at \(x\) stays on the same vertical line at all times. This is a good approximation because at a slope up to \(0{,}01\) the horizontal force on a piece is at most \(1\) % of the same measure with which we measure the vertical one (derivation 13.1, step 6), on the piece of the test below \(0{,}49\) % of the vertical resultant. The model leaves this force out. At a slope of \(0{,}1\) the share would be up to \(10\) %, and the horizontal motion could no longer be neglected.
  6. Weight is neglected. The weight of the cord \(\mu g L = 6{,}3\) mN is \(9\cdot 10^{-5}\) of the tension. What this means for the motion we show below, once we have the equation of motion: in the model with idealizations 1 to 5 weight only lowers the position about which the cord vibrates, below the straight position without weight by at most \(7{,}3\ \mu\)m, and does not change the motion about this position.

Idealizations 2 to 4 are needed so that the force on a piece of the cord is a simple function of the slope, as the derivation that follows shows.

Before we calculate, let us see why the cord moves at all. Figure 13.3 shows the same short piece of cord in three shapes, and at each end the neighbouring part pulls it with the force \(T\) along the tangent (idealizations 2 and 3).

We draw all three pieces above the axis \(x\). A straight piece, even a tilted one, is pulled by the two forces in opposite directions on one line, so they cancel and the resultant is zero. A piece bent into a hill is pulled by both forces slanting downward. Their horizontal parts cancel in the figure, while the vertical parts add up to a resultant \(R\) pointing downward. The slope at the left end is \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(a) > 0\) and at the right end \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b) < 0\), as the slope triangles with horizontal side \(1\) show. A piece in a valley is pulled by both forces slanting upward, and the resultant points upward. So a piece accelerates only when it is bent, and then toward the inner side of the arc. Tilt alone gives no force. The derivation that follows says this exactly, with the error of each step.

Figure 13.4 shows a piece of the cord and the forces on it. On the axis \(x\) the cord is stretched between the points \(0\) and \(L\), where it is rigidly fastened. The blackwhite line is its position at time \(t\), with the slope greatly exaggerated. From it we choose the piece between the end at \(a\) and the end at \(b\), with length \(b - a\). The neighbouring parts of the cord act on the piece: the left one pulls it with the force \(T\) along the tangent to the left, the right one with the force \(T\) along the tangent to the right. The vertical acceleration of the piece is determined only by the vertical components of these forces, so we draw them separately. We derive the equation of motion of the piece by using each idealization in one step and stating the error in it. The equation is for a finite piece, not for a piece of infinitesimal width: the piece is an arbitrary interval \([a, b]\) and the calculation does not require it to be short. How the force \(T\) along the tangent splits into a vertical and a horizontal part is shown in figure 13.5, at which steps 1 to 3 and 6 point.

Derivation 13.1 Equation of motion of a piece of a stretched cord (gibalna enačba kosa napete žice)

Goal: for the piece of the cord above the interval \([a, b] \subset [0, L]\), \(a < b\), to write Newton's second law in the vertical direction at time \(t\). The assumptions are idealizations 1 to 6, \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) on \([a, b]\) and continuous derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\). The piece is a part of the cord with its mass and the forces that its surroundings exert on it: the neighbouring part at \(x = a\) and the neighbouring part at \(x = b\).

1
\[\vek F_{\mathrm{b}} = T\,(\cos\theta_{\mathrm{b}},\ \sin\theta_{\mathrm{b}}) , \qquad \vek F_{\mathrm{a}} = -\,T\,(\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}},\ \sin\theta_{\mathrm{a}}) , \qquad \tan\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}} .\]
Hint

By idealization 2 the force is along the tangent, by idealization 3 it has magnitude \(T\). The tangent to the graph of the function \(x \mapsto w(x, t)\) has slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definitions 1.9 and 1.26): over the horizontal run \(1\) it rises by \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), so for the angle \(\theta\) between the tangent and the axis \(\tan\theta = w_{\mathrm{x}}\). The right neighbour pulls the piece in the direction of increasing \(x\), the left one in the opposite direction.

2
\[\sin\theta = \frac{\tan\theta}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2\theta}} = \frac{w_{\mathrm{x}}}{\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2}} , \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2}} .\]
Hint

The hypotenuse of the slope triangle has length \(\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\) by Pythagoras' theorem. The force \(T\) lies on the same line, so its triangle is similar to the slope triangle: the vertical part is to \(T\) as \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) is to the hypotenuse, the horizontal part as \(1\) is to the hypotenuse. By calculation: for an angle \(\theta\) with \(\cos\theta > 0\) we have \(\cos^2\theta = 1/(1 + \tan^2\theta)\) and \(\sin\theta = \tan\theta\,\cos\theta\).

3
\[T\sin\theta = T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\,(1 - \epsilon) , \qquad 0 \le \epsilon \le 5\cdot 10^{-5} .\]
Hint

At \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) the hypotenuse is at most \(1{,}00005\), so \(1/\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2} \ge 1/\sqrt{1{,}0001} = 0{,}99995\) and \(\epsilon = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2} \le 5\cdot 10^{-5}\). With the same estimate \(\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2} - 1 \le 5\cdot 10^{-5}\) the stretch of the cord (the arc length is \(\int\sqrt{1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\,\mathrm{d}x\), definition 8.5) at this slope is at most \(5\cdot 10^{-5}\), as used in idealization 3.

4
\[F_{\text{vert}} = T\sin\theta_{\mathrm{b}} - T\sin\theta_{\mathrm{a}} = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \frac{T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)}{\big(1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2(x, t)\big)^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
Hint

The first equality adds the vertical components of the forces of step 1. For the second we write \(T\sin\theta = T\,g(w_{\mathrm{x}})\) with \(g(s) = s/\sqrt{1 + s^2}\) (step 2). The derivative is \(g'(s) = (1 + s^2)^{-1/2} - s^2(1 + s^2)^{-3/2} = (1 + s^2)^{-3/2}\), so by the chain rule (lemma 4.43) the derivative of the function \(x \mapsto T\,g(w_{\mathrm{x}}(x, t))\) is the continuous function \(T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\). The fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, part 1) writes the difference of the values at \(b\) and \(a\) as the integral of this derivative. The equality is exact.

5
\[\Big|F_{\text{vert}} - T\,\big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big)\Big| \le 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)|\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
Hint

By the fundamental theorem, \(T\,\big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x\). The difference from step 4 is therefore the integral of the function \(T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\big(1 - (1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\big)\), and the bracket in it lies between \(0\) and \(1 - 1{,}0001^{-3/2} = 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\), so the integrand of the difference never exceeds the bound \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\) at any point. When \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) does not change sign on the piece, the integral on the right equals \(T\,|w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)|\), and the relative error is at most \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\) for every such piece, however short. The model therefore replaces the vertical resultant by \(T\,\big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big)\). On the piece of the test below the exact value is \(-0{,}517561\) N and the replacement is \(-0{,}517583\) N, and their difference of \(0{,}022\) mN is smaller than the bound \(0{,}078\) mN.

6
\[\big|F_{\text{hor}}\big| = T\,|\cos\theta_{\mathrm{b}} - \cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}}| \le 0{,}01\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)|\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
Hint

As in step 4, the horizontal part of the force is \(T\cos\theta = T\,h(w_{\mathrm{x}})\) with \(h(s) = (1 + s^2)^{-1/2}\) and \(h'(s) = -s\,(1 + s^2)^{-3/2}\), so \(T\cos\theta_{\mathrm{b}} - T\cos\theta_{\mathrm{a}} = -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\,\mathrm{d}x\). At \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) the absolute value of this integrand is at most \(0{,}01\,T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\). The horizontal resultant is therefore at most \(1\) % of the same measure with which step 5 measured the error of the vertical one. By idealization 5 the model has no horizontal motion, so it neglects this force. On the piece of the test below \(F_{\text{hor}} = 2{,}5\) mN, less than the bound \(5{,}2\) mN, which is \(0{,}49\) % of the vertical resultant.

7

The mass of the piece times its acceleration equals the vertical resultant:

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}x = T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - T\,w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t) . \tag{13.1}\]
Hint

By idealization 5 the piece is the same matter above \([a, b]\) at all times, with mass \(\mu\) per unit length (idealization 1). A point with constant \(x\) moves along a vertical line, so its acceleration is \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) (definition 8.77). For a group of particles of matter Newton's law says that the resultant of the forces on it is the sum of the masses of the particles times their accelerations (section 2.6.4, CM (5.3.6), (5.3.9), PDF p. 227). For mass spread over \([a, b]\) with density \(\mu\) per unit length this sum is the integral on the left. The right side is the vertical resultant as the model takes it in step 5. Weight is not counted (idealization 6).

Equation (13.1) holds for every piece and every time under the assumptions of the model. Both sides are a force in newtons: \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is a force per unit length, and its integral over the length is a force.

Source: KREYSZIG §12.2 (PDF pp. 569-570, read), GR §4.3.2 (PDF pp. 276-277, read). KREYSZIG takes a small part of the cord of length \(\Delta x\) and at the end lets \(\Delta x\) go to zero, GR derives the equation from Hamilton's principle. The derivation in steps with an integral over a finite piece and with error bounds is not from a source. The law of linear momentum for a given mass: CM (5.3.6), (5.3.9) (PDF p. 227, read).

Figure 13.6 shows the bounds of steps 5 and 6 on the piece of the test below, \(w = \varepsilon\sin(\pi x/L)\) with \(\varepsilon = 2\) mm at time \(0\). The top graph has an axis from \(a = 0{,}1\) m to \(b = 0{,}3\) m. In blue is the difference between the exact integrand of step 4 and the integrand of the model, \(T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\,\big(1 - (1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\big)\), in grey the bound \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\). At the point \(a\) the blue height is \(0{,}167\) mN/m and the grey one \(0{,}228\) mN/m. The blue curve lies under the grey one at every point of the piece, because the bracket is at most \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\), so the blue area, \(0{,}022\) mN, is also smaller than the grey one, \(0{,}078\) mN. This is the inequality of step 5. Towards \(b\) the blue curve drops almost to zero, because there the slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) drops towards zero and the bracket with it. The bottom graph on its own axis shows the same for step 6: in blue the horizontal force per unit length \(T\,|w_{\mathrm{x}}\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\,(1 + w_{\mathrm{x}}^2)^{-3/2}\), in grey the bound \(0{,}01\,T\,|w_{\mathrm{xx}}|\), at the point \(a\) the heights \(13{,}0\) mN/m and \(15{,}2\) mN/m, and the areas \(2{,}5\) mN and \(5{,}2\) mN.

This fixes the model. We write it as a definition, in which it gets its name.

Definition 13.2 String (struna)

Let \(L\), \(\mu\) and \(T\) be positive numbers: the length, the mass per unit length in kg/m and the tension in N. A string with length \(L\), mass \(\mu\) per unit length and tension \(T\) is a stretched cord, fastened at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), for which we assume idealizations 1 to 6. A motion of the string is a function \(w : [0, L] \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\) that has continuous partial derivatives \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) (definitions 1.26 and 1.130), satisfies \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\), and satisfies equation (13.1) for all \(a < b\) in \([0, L]\) and every \(t \ge 0\). The function \(w(x, t)\) is the displacement of the point at \(x\) at time \(t\) in metres, perpendicular to the axis \(x\). The model holds with the error of derivation 13.1 as long as \(|w_{\mathrm{x}}| \le 0{,}01\) and idealizations 1 to 6 hold.

Source: KREYSZIG §12.2, assumptions 1 to 3 (PDF p. 569, read), GR §4.3.2 (PDF p. 276, read), which calls the string an idealized structure without bending stiffness that moves transversally under axial prestress. The error measures are ours.

The equation of motion of a piece is one condition for every pair \(a < b\). Does it imply an equation at every point? It does, because the piece is arbitrary: a continuous function whose integral over every piece is zero is zero everywhere. Why, we see on a function that is not zero everywhere.

The upper graph is the function \(r\) on the interval from \(0\) to \(L\), the residual of a motion we look at after the theorem. The integral over the whole string is the area below the axis, \(-0{,}2842\) N, together with the area above the axis, \(+0{,}2842\) N, and this sum is zero. On a piece on which \(r\) does not change sign, however, the integral is an area on one side of the axis only, \(-0{,}1421\) N, and it is not zero. Wherever a continuous function is not zero, it has such a piece around it. The lower graph on its own axis is \(G(x)\), the integral of \(r\) from \(0\) to \(x\), that is the area from \(0\) to \(x\): at \(L/4\) it equals the grey area, and its slope is \(r\). If the integral over every piece were zero, \(G\) would be zero everywhere, flat, and its slope \(r\) would be zero everywhere. This idea is step 5 of the proof of the next theorem. The theorem holds in the other direction too.

Theorem 13.3 Wave equation of the string (valovna enačba strune)

Let \(L\), \(\mu\) and \(T\) be positive numbers and let \(w\) be a function with continuous \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) on \([0, L] \times [0, \infty)\). Then for every \(t \ge 0\): equation (13.1) holds for all \(a < b\) in \([0, L]\) if and only if for every \(x \in [0, L]\)

\[w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \frac{T}{\mu}\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} . \tag{13.2}\]
Proof

We must show that equation (13.1) for all pieces is the same as equation (13.2) at all points. We write the difference of the two sides as the integral of one continuous function over the piece and then use that a continuous function with zero integral over every piece is zero everywhere.

  1. Fix \(t \ge 0\) and let \(f(x) = \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}(x, t) - T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, t)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The function \(f\) is continuous because \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are continuous.
  2. The function \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(\cdot, t)\) is differentiable with the continuous derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, part 1) \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b w_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}x = w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\) for all \(a < b\).
  3. By step 2 and the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.54), \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x - T\,\big(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b, t) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a, t)\big)\). Equation (13.1) for the piece \([a, b]\) is therefore the same as \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\): the area under the graph of \(f\) over the piece is zero.
  4. If (13.2) holds, then \(f = 0\) everywhere, so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for all \(a < b\), and by step 3 (13.1) holds.
  5. Conversely, let (13.1) hold for all \(a < b\). By step 3 \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for all \(a < b\), so also \(G(x) = \int_0^x f\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\) for every \(x \in (0, L]\) and \(G(0) = 0\). The function \(G\) is differentiable with \(G' = f\) (theorem 3.55, part 2), because \(f\) is continuous, and it is zero everywhere, so \(f = G' = 0\) on \([0, L]\).
  6. From \(f = 0\) it follows that \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and since \(\mu > 0\), equation (13.2).

Source: KREYSZIG (3) (PDF p. 570, read), GR (4.203) and (4.210) (PDF pp. 277 and 278, read). The sources obtain the equation as a limit in which the length of the piece goes to zero, or from Hamilton's principle. The proof with an arbitrary finite piece and the fundamental theorem is not from a source.

We call equation (13.2) the wave equation. It is linear and homogeneous: if \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) are solutions, so is \(\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2\) (derivatives are linear). The second order in time is needed because the equation gives an acceleration, and by definition 8.78 the force must determine it. The equation writes down what figure 13.3 showed. In a hill \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} < 0\), the slope decreases with increasing \(x\), so \(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b) < w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} < 0\): the resultant and the acceleration point downward. In a valley \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} > 0\) and the acceleration points upward, while a straight line, even a tilted one, has \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and no acceleration. The acceleration of a point is therefore proportional to the curvature of the string at it, not to its displacement or its slope. For a longer piece the sum counts. On the drawn piece of figure 13.4 the slope at \(b\) is smaller than at \(a\), so the resultant points downward, although \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is already positive just before \(b\): the resultant is \(T\,(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a))\), which is the integral of \(T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) over the whole piece.

Linearity also tells what we neglected with weight (idealization 6). The axis \(w\) points upward. The weight of the piece adds the force \(-\mu g\,(b - a)\) to the right side of equation (13.1), and the same proof as for theorem 13.3, with the function \(f = \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} + \mu g\), gives the equation \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} - \mu g\). The shape at rest \(w_{\mathrm{s}}(x) = -\mu g\,x\,(L - x)/(2T)\) satisfies it, since \(w_{\mathrm{s}}'' = \mu g/T\), and it is zero at both ends. The difference \(v = w - w_{\mathrm{s}}\) has \(\mu\,v_{\mathrm{tt}} = \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} - \mu g = T\,v_{\mathrm{xx}}\), so it satisfies the wave equation without weight. Weight therefore only lowers the position about which the string vibrates, most in the middle, by \(|w_{\mathrm{s}}(L/2)| = \mu g L^2/(8T) = 7{,}3\ \mu\)m. The motion about this position is exactly the one without weight. At the amplitude of \(2\) mm of the test below the lowering is \(0{,}36\) % of the amplitude.

We test the theorem on the numbers of example A. Let \(w(x, t) = \varepsilon\sin(\pi x/L)\cos(\pi c\,t/L)\) with \(\varepsilon = 2\) mm and \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) (we shall meet this function in the next section as the first mode of the string). Its largest slope is \(\varepsilon\pi/L = 9{,}67\cdot 10^{-3}\), which fits the bound of idealization 4. At \(t = 0\) and the piece \([a, b] = [0{,}1\ \text{m},\ 0{,}3\ \text{m}]\) the left side of equation (13.1) is \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}5176\) N and the right side is \(T\,(w_{\mathrm{x}}(b) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)) = -0{,}5176\) N (SymPy). The equality is no accident, since \(\mu c^2 = T\): \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -(\pi c/L)^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(\pi/L)^2 w\), so \(\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} = T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

The theorem requires equation (13.1) for every piece, not only for one. Let us look at a motion that satisfies it for the whole string but not for the piece \([0, L/4]\). Let \(w = \varepsilon\sin(2\pi x/L)\cos\omega t\) with \(\varepsilon = 1\) mm and \(\omega = 1{,}1\cdot 2\pi c/L = 2841{,}6\) 1/s. The function is smooth and zero at both ends. Why it does not satisfy the equation shows in the residual \(r = \mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}} - T\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), drawn in figure 13.7.

  1. At \(t = 0\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2 w\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(2\pi/L)^2 w\). Since \(T = \mu c^2\), \(r = \mu\,\big(c^2 (2\pi/L)^2 - \omega^2\big)\,w = \mu\,(1 - 1{,}21)\,(2\pi c/L)^2\,w = -1{,}374\ \text{N/m}\cdot\sin(2\pi x/L)\), because \(\mu\cdot 0{,}21\cdot(2\pi c/L)^2\,\varepsilon = 1{,}374\) N/m.
  2. For the piece \([0, L]\) the difference of the two sides of equation (13.1) is by step 3 of the proof of the theorem \(\int_0^L r\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), because the sine with one whole period on \([0, L]\) changes sign and the areas below and above the axis cancel. For the whole string the equation holds.
  3. For the piece \([0, L/4]\) we have \(\int_0^{L/4}\mu\,w_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -0{,}8187\) N and \(T\,(w_{\mathrm{x}}(L/4) - w_{\mathrm{x}}(0)) = -0{,}6767\) N. The difference is \(\int_0^{L/4} r\,\mathrm{d}x = -1{,}374\cdot L/(2\pi) = -0{,}1421\) N, not zero. Also at the point \(x = L/4\) we have \(r = -1{,}374\) N/m, not zero.

So the equation for the whole string alone does not suffice. In the piece \([0, L]\) residuals of opposite signs cancel, in a short piece they do not. This is why definition 13.2 requires the equation for all \(a < b\), and why the theorem can get the equation at every point from it.

The number \(T/\mu\) in equation (13.2) has the unit \(\text{N}/(\text{kg/m}) = \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2\). We name its root.

Definition 13.4 Wave speed (hitrost valovanja)

The wave speed of a string with mass \(\mu\) per unit length and tension \(T\) is the number

\[c = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} , \tag{13.3}\]

in m/s, the speed of a travelling shape. The wave equation (theorem 13.3) is then \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG (3) with \(c^2 = T/\rho\) (PDF p. 570, read), GR (4.211) \(c = \sqrt{N_0/m}\) (PDF p. 278, read). GR calls \(c\) the phase speed and says that the equation describes a transverse wave with propagation velocity \(c\) (PDF p. 278).

Why exactly \(T/\mu\)? On a piece in a hill acts a resultant proportional to the tension \(T\), since both forces have magnitude \(T\). It accelerates the mass of the piece, which is proportional to \(\mu\). By Newton's law the acceleration is the force divided by the mass, so for the same shape four times the tension gives four times the acceleration, and four times the mass per unit length a quarter of it. A tighter string moves faster, a heavier one slower. The speed \(c\), however, grows only with the square root. If we run the same motion \(k\) times faster, every velocity is \(k\) times larger and every acceleration \(k^2\) times larger, since the acceleration is the second derivative with respect to time. Four times the acceleration for the same shape is therefore the same motion running twice as fast.

For the string of example A, \(c = \sqrt{70/(9{,}802\cdot 10^{-4})} = 267{,}24\) m/s, and at the tension \(4 \cdot 70 = 280\) N it would be \(534{,}47\) m/s, twice as large. Why do we call it a speed? We show that a shape travels along an infinite string with this speed.

Lemma 13.5 Travelling wave (potujoči val)

Let \(c > 0\) and let \(F\) and \(G\) be twice continuously differentiable functions on \(\mathbb{R}\). Then \(w(x, t) = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)\) is a solution of \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) for every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). The graph of \(x \mapsto F(x - ct)\) at time \(t + \tau\) is the graph at time \(t\) shifted by \(c\tau\) in the direction of increasing \(x\), and the graph of \(x \mapsto G(x + ct)\) by \(c\tau\) in the opposite direction.

Proof

We must show that the function satisfies the equation and that the shape moves with speed \(c\).

  1. For \(F(x - ct)\) the chain rule (lemma 4.43) gives \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = F'(x - ct)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = -c\,F'(x - ct)\), then \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''(x - ct)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 F''(x - ct)\). So \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  2. For \(G(x + ct)\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = G'(x + ct)\), \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = c\,G'(x + ct)\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = G''(x + ct)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 G''(x + ct)\), so again \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  3. The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, so the same holds for \(F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)\).
  4. For the shift: \(F\big((x + c\tau) - c(t + \tau)\big) = F(x - ct)\). The value \(F(x - ct)\) at the point \(x\) at time \(t\) is therefore equal to the value at time \(t + \tau\) at the point \(x + c\tau\), and the shape has moved by \(c\tau\) toward larger \(x\). For \(G\) we have \(G\big((x - c\tau) + c(t + \tau)\big) = G(x + ct)\), the shift is in the opposite direction.

Source: GR (4.212) (PDF p. 278, read), CAJORI (PDF p. 258, read) for the form of D'Alembert's solution. The proof in steps is not from a source.

Figure 13.8 shows the lemma. Above the axis \(x\) we draw the shape of the string \(w(x, t)\) at time \(t\) grey dashed, and the shape at time \(t + \tau\) in orange. Both have an equally high crest and the same width, only the orange one is shifted by \(c\tau\) toward larger \(x\). The speed \(c\) is therefore the speed with which the shape that the string has moves along it. For the string of example A a shape would move \(0{,}65\) m in \(0{,}65/267{,}24 = 2{,}43\) ms. If a shape travels along the string and back, the time is \(2L/c = 4{,}86\) ms, which is one oscillation with frequency \(c/(2L) = 205{,}57\) Hz. This is not a proof but an announcement of a result of the next section, which we shall prove with a different calculation.

The lemma says that a shape moves with the speed \(c\). Could a shape also move along this string with another speed? Let \(w = F(x - c'\,t)\) with \(c' = 1{,}1\,c = 293{,}96\) m/s and \(F(s) = \varepsilon\sin(\pi s/L)\), \(\varepsilon = 1\) mm. The shape is kept and in the time \(\tau\) it moves by \(c'\tau\), as in step 4 of the proof of the lemma. It does not satisfy the equation, though.

  1. As in step 1 of the proof of the lemma, with \(c'\) instead of \(c\), \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = F''(x - c't)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c'^2 F''(x - c't)\).
  2. So \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} - c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} = (c'^2 - c^2)\,F'' = 0{,}21\,c^2 F''\). Since \(F'' = -(\pi/L)^2 F\), at a point with \(F = \varepsilon\) the left term is \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -2018{,}6\) m/s\(^2\) and the right term is \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -1668{,}3\) m/s\(^2\). The difference \(-350{,}3\) m/s\(^2\) is not zero. A shape moving with speed \(c'\) would need an acceleration of \(2018{,}6\) m/s\(^2\) at its crest, but the tension forces give it only \(1668{,}3\) m/s\(^2\).
  3. The speed \(c'\) would hold at another tension: from \(c'^2 = T'/\mu\) we get \(T' = \mu c'^2 = 1{,}21\,T = 84{,}7\) N.

So the speed of the moving shape is not fixed by the shape but by the string with its tension and mass: \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) is the only speed at which \(F(x - ct)\) is a solution.

The lemma holds for an infinite string. A string of length \(L\) has the condition \(w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0\), which the general \(F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)\) satisfies only for a special relation between \(F\) and \(G\). It is this condition, and not the equation itself, that decides which motions are possible: the next section deals with it. This section has shown that the displacement of a string satisfies the wave equation \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) with wave speed \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = 267{,}24\) m/s, under idealizations 1 to 6 and a small slope, and that this equation allows shapes that travel with this speed.
