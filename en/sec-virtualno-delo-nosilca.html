Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.10 Virtual work of a beam (virtualno delo nosilca)

Chapter 4 wrote the equilibrium of a bar as an equation about virtual displacements. The axial force times the virtual strain gave the internal virtual work, and this was equal to the work of the loads on the virtual displacements (theorem 4.76). The converse (theorem 4.78) showed that one equation for all virtual displacements replaces the equilibrium in every cross section and the conditions at the end. Now we repeat this for a beam. A bar carries only the axial force and has one displacement function. A beam also carries the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\), and its displacement consists of the axial displacement \(u(x)\) and the deflection \(v(x)\). Which displacements of a beam are virtual? What equation about virtual work holds for it, and what do \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) do in it?

We can see the answer on the cantilever of this chapter (a steel flat bar 20 mm × 40 mm, \(L = 1\) m, a force of 500 N downward at the free end, so that by the convention of the chapter \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -500\,(1\ \text{m} - x)\) N, section 5.2.2). In this section we first say which displacements of a beam are virtual and how much work a couple does. Then we state and prove the virtual work equation for a beam, test it on the numbers of the cantilever and of the simply supported beam, and finally show the converse: the equation for all virtual displacements gives the differential equation and the boundary conditions.

5.10.1 Virtual displacement of a beam (virtualni pomik nosilca)

For a bar, a virtual displacement was an imagined displacement allowed by the supports, during which the forces stayed as they are (definition 4.70). For a beam the same two parts hold. The supports are the constraints the beam has at its ends (definition 5.5). Besides them we have constraints that the model of the beam itself imposes. By theorem 5.15 a point of the beam at height \(y\) above the axis moves by \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\) along the axis and by \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x)\) across it. Two functions, \(u\) and \(v\), therefore determine the displacements of all points of the beam. This is the constraint that the model of the beam (definition 5.14) demands: cross sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis. A virtual displacement must satisfy the constraints of the model just as the true displacement does. So a virtual displacement here is also a pair of functions \((\delta u, \delta v)\) that we insert into the same formula, and the virtual strain of the fibre at height \(y\) is, by the equation for the strain in theorem 5.15,

\[\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \delta u' - y\,\delta v'' .\]

The second derivative of the function \(\delta v\) appears in it, so we must be allowed to compute it at every point of the beam, also at the ends. This is the reason for the requirement of a continuous second derivative in the next definition. A continuous slope \(\delta v'\) means that the beam does not acquire a sharp corner or a hinge in our imagination, and a continuous \(\delta v''\) also means that the curvature does not change abruptly. For the same reason, because the virtual work contains the second derivative, ONATE2 requires \(C^1\) continuity, that is continuous \(w\) and \(w'\), and explains it by the slope having to be continuous so that the deflection is smooth (PDF p. 36). Our requirement is stronger, because we need a continuous integrand in the fundamental theorem of calculus. For a bar we required a continuous \(\delta u'\), here also a continuous \(\delta v''\).

At the ends of the beam the supports forbid some components of the virtual displacement. A clamp (vpetje) forbids the displacement of the axis and the rotation of the cross section, a fixed pin support (nepomična členkasta podpora) forbids the displacement of the axis, a roller support (pomična členkasta podpora) only the displacement across the axis. A free end forbids nothing. As for a bar, where a support prescribes a displacement the virtual displacement is zero, because a virtual displacement is an imagined change relative to the true position, and the support does not allow a change.

Definition 5.60 Virtual displacement of a beam (virtualni pomik nosilca)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) with the constraints of the supports at its ends (definition 5.5). A virtual displacement of the beam is a pair of functions \((\delta u, \delta v)\), where \(\delta u\) is a continuously differentiable function of a real variable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), and \(\delta v\) is a function with continuous first and second derivatives on an open interval containing \([0, L]\). At each end the constraints of the support hold: at a clamp \(\delta u = \delta v = \delta v' = 0\), at a fixed pin support \(\delta u = \delta v = 0\), at a roller support \(\delta v = 0\), at a free end there are no constraints. The number \(\delta u(x)\) is the virtual displacement of the point of the axis along the axis, \(\delta v(x)\) across the axis, the number \(\delta v'(x)\) is the virtual slope of the axis, which by theorem 5.15 is also the virtual rotation of the cross section, and \(\delta v''(x)\) is the virtual curvature (virtualna ukrivljenost). During a virtual displacement all forces on the beam and internal forces in it stay as they are.

Source: EP §4.2.2 (p. 168) and example 4.2.1 (pp. 170-171), ONATE2 §1.2.5 (PDF pp. 35-36), where the \(C^1\) requirement (\(w\) and \(w'\) continuous) is explained. The requirement of a continuous second derivative is stronger than that. The definition follows definition 4.70, the requirement on the second derivative and the constraints by type of support are ours.

Why is the slope \(\delta v'\) a number without a unit? It is the ratio of a virtual displacement across the axis to a length along the axis, a ratio of two lengths, like the slope \(v'\) of the cantilever, which for the cantilever of this chapter is \(-0{,}01196\) at the free end.

Figure 5.58 shows, for the cantilever and for the simply supported beam, pairs of functions that are a virtual displacement of a beam and pairs that are not. For the cantilever \(\delta v = c\,x^2\) is a virtual displacement, because at \(x = 0\) it is zero with zero slope. The function \(\delta v = c\,x\) is not, because its slope at the clamp is \(c \neq 0\): the clamp does not allow rotation. A function that is zero up to \(x = L/2\) and linear afterwards is not, because it has a corner at \(x = L/2\), that is a jump of the slope, and has no second derivative. Such a displacement would mean a hinge in the middle of the beam. For the simply supported beam \(\delta v = c\,x\,(L - x)\) is a virtual displacement with slopes \(\pm c\,L\) at the ends, because the supports allow rotation. The function \(\delta v = c\,(x + L/2)\) is not, because at \(x = 0\) it has the value \(cL/2 \neq 0\), and the support does not allow a displacement across the axis there.

At the free end and inside the beam there can be point loads, that is forces and couples. We know the work of a force in a virtual displacement (definition 4.71). But what about a couple (definition 5.3)? The moment of a couple is the same about every point, and its resultant is zero, so it seems that a couple does no work. But if the cross section rotates, the two points of application move differently. Take two opposite forces \(P\) across the axis, one at \(x_0 + d/2\) and the other at \(x_0 - d/2\) (figure 5.59), and let the cross section rotate by the virtual slope \(\delta v'\). The upper point of application rises by about \(\delta v'\,d/2\), the lower one sinks by the same amount, and the work is \(P\,d\,\delta v'\). This is the moment of the couple times the virtual rotation. Precisely:

Lemma 5.61 Virtual work of a couple (virtualno delo dvojice sil)

Let \(x_0 \in [0, L]\) and let \(\delta v\) be differentiable at \(x_0\). Let the couple on the beam be a force \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point of the axis \(x_0 + d/2\) and a force \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point of the axis \(x_0 - d/2\), where \(P > 0\) and \(d > 0\) are numbers. Its moment (component \(z\)) about the point \(x_0\) is \(M_0 = P\,d\). The virtual work of the couple is

\[P\,\delta v\big(x_0 + \tfrac d2\big) - P\,\delta v\big(x_0 - \tfrac d2\big) = M_0\,\frac{\delta v(x_0 + d/2) - \delta v(x_0 - d/2)}{d} ,\]

and when \(d \to 0\) at constant \(M_0\) (a point couple with moment \(M_0\)) it tends to \(M_0\,\delta v'(x_0)\).

Proof

We must show the moment of the couple and the limit. We compute the moment from the definition, the limit from the definition of the derivative.

  1. By the definition of the moment of a force (definition 2.32) the moment of the force \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) about \(x_0\) is \((d/2)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (d/2)\,P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the moment of the force \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) is \((-d/2)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times (-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = (d/2)\,P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The sum is \(P\,d\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(M_0 = P\,d\).
  2. Both forces point across the axis, so by definition 4.71 they do work only with the virtual displacements \(\delta v(x_0 \pm d/2)\) of their points of application, and the virtual work is \(P\,\delta v(x_0 + d/2) - P\,\delta v(x_0 - d/2)\).
  3. With \(P = M_0/d\) we obtain the second form.
  4. Let \(h = d/2\). The quotient is \(\dfrac{\delta v(x_0 + h) - \delta v(x_0 - h)}{2h} = \tfrac12\Big(\dfrac{\delta v(x_0 + h) - \delta v(x_0)}{h} + \dfrac{\delta v(x_0 - h) - \delta v(x_0)}{-h}\Big)\). By the definition of the derivative (definition 1.9) both quotients in the bracket tend to \(\delta v'(x_0)\) as \(h \to 0\), so by the rule for the limit of a sum the quotient tends to \(\delta v'(x_0)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The idea of a couple as a pair of forces that move differently when the section rotates: ONATE2 (1.6) (PDF p. 35), where the concentrated moment \(M^c_{\mathrm{j}}\) multiplies the virtual slope.

The lemma tells where a point couple enters the equation of virtual work: its moment is multiplied by the virtual slope. This is exactly what we need at the free end of the cantilever, where a couple \(M_0\) acts.

5.10.2 Principle of virtual displacements for a beam (načelo virtualnih pomikov za nosilec)

First look at a piece of the beam between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), as for the bar (figure 5.60). Forces and couples act on it at both faces, and a load acts along it, and all is in equilibrium. In a virtual displacement we first move the piece rigidly in our imagination, by \(\delta u(x_1)\) and \(\delta v(x_1)\), and rotate it by \(\delta v'(x_1)\) about its left face. The work of all forces in a rigid displacement is zero, because the resultant of the forces and their moment are zero (equilibrium, section 5.2.3). What remains is the stretching and bending of the piece. The right face moves along the axis relative to the left one by \(\delta u(x_2) - \delta u(x_1) \approx \delta u'\,\Delta x\), where \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\) is the length of the piece, and the force \(N(x_2)\) acts on it, so the work is approximately \(N\,\delta u'\,\Delta x\), as for the bar. Across the axis the right face moves relative to the rotated left one by \(\delta v(x_2) - \delta v(x_1) - \delta v'(x_1)\,\Delta x \approx \tfrac12\,\delta v''\,\Delta x^2\), which is a term with the square of the length of the piece, and the work of the shear force \(V\) on it is therefore negligible for a short piece. The right face rotates relative to the left one by \(\delta v'(x_2) - \delta v'(x_1) \approx \delta v''\,\Delta x\), and by lemma 5.61 the moment \(M(x_2)\) on this face does the work of about \(M\,\delta v''\,\Delta x\). The internal virtual work of the piece is therefore \((N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\Delta x\), and when we sum over the pieces it approaches an integral. The shear force does not appear in the internal virtual work because by the assumption of the model the cross section only rotates and does not slide, as we shall see in lemma 5.63. Two neighbouring pieces cancel each other's work at their common cross section, as for the bar.

So we expect that for a beam

\[\int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x + \text{work of forces and couples at the ends} .\]

Here \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) are the axial and the transverse load per unit length (definition 5.2). Now we prove it precisely. The equilibrium of a piece of the beam, which we shall use, has the form of the differential relations from corollary 5.11: \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\), \(M' = -V\). The minus has the reason we gave there: \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) are the force and the moment that the part of the beam at larger \(x\) transmits to the part at smaller \(x\) (definition 5.7), so the increment of the force across a piece is balanced by the load on it.

Theorem 5.62 Principle of virtual displacements for a beam (načelo virtualnih pomikov za nosilec)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\). Let the load per unit length, \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the axis and \(q\) across it, be continuous on \([0, L]\). Let the functions \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) be continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and let for every \(x \in (0, L)\)

\[N'(x) + q_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0 , \qquad V'(x) + q(x) = 0 , \qquad M'(x) + V(x) = 0 .\]

At each end of the beam let one of the possibilities hold for each of the three pairs \((\delta u, N)\), \((\delta v, V)\) and \((\delta v', M)\): the support forbids the corresponding component of the virtual displacement, or the end load is prescribed and equal to the internal force. At the end \(x = L\) this means \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = N(L)\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = V(L)\), \(M_0 = M(L)\), and at the end \(x = 0\) it means \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = -N(0)\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -V(0)\), \(M_0 = -M(0)\), where \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) are the forces on the end of the beam along the axis and across it and \(M_0\) is the moment of the couple at the end (component \(z\)). Then for every virtual displacement \((\delta u, \delta v)\) of the beam

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \Big[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v + M_0\,\delta v'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}} \\ &\quad + \Big[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v + M_0\,\delta v'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = 0} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.61}\]

where at each end we omit the terms of the components that the support forbids.

Proof

We must show that the left and the right integral differ exactly by the work of the end loads. The proof is integration by parts twice, as for the bar, written with one function \(G\) that we differentiate.

  1. Let \(G(x) = N(x)\,\delta u(x) + V(x)\,\delta v(x) + M(x)\,\delta v'(x)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The functions \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\) are differentiable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), so they are continuous there (lemma 1.79). The functions \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) are continuous by assumption, so \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) as a sum of products of continuous functions (corollary 1.64).
  2. Let \(0 < x < L\). All functions in \(G\) are differentiable at \(x\): \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) by assumption, \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) by definition, \(\delta v'\) because \(\delta v\) has a second derivative. By the product rule (lemma 1.81, item 3)
    \[G' = N'\,\delta u + N\,\delta u' + V'\,\delta v + V\,\delta v' + M'\,\delta v' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
  3. By assumption \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The terms \(V\,\delta v'\) and \(M'\,\delta v' = -V\,\delta v'\) cancel, so
    \[G'(x) = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
  4. The function on the right is continuous on \([0, L]\), because \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(q\), \(N\), \(M\), \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\), \(\delta u'\) and \(\delta v''\) are continuous (corollary 1.64). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1, with the functions \(G\) and \(G'\) we therefore have
    \[\int_0^L \big(N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0) .\]
  5. At the end \(x = L\) each term of the function \(G\) is either zero, because the support forbids the component of the virtual displacement (the constraints of the supports are in definition 5.60), or by assumption \(N(L)\,\delta u(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L)\), \(V(L)\,\delta v(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\), \(M(L)\,\delta v'(L) = M_0\,\delta v'(L)\). At the end \(x = 0\) it is the same, either zero or \(-N(0)\,\delta u(0) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(0)\) and so on. So \(G(L) - G(0)\) is the sum of the expressions in the square brackets of equation (5.61).
  6. By linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 1) and moving the terms \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u\) and \(q\,\delta v\) to the right-hand side, (5.61) follows.

Source: EP example 4.2.1 (pp. 170-171), ONATE2 (1.6a), (1.7) (PDF pp. 35-36). ONATE2 has the deflection \(w\) and the loads positive in the direction of the axis \(z\) (our \(y\)), concentrated moments positive anticlockwise and \(M = EI\,w''\) (1.6b), which is our \(M = EI\,v''\) with the same sign. Its (1.6a) also has a distributed moment \(m(x)\), which we do not have. EP has the axis \(z\) upward and the axis \(y\) into the page, so its bending moment is \(M_{\text{EP}} = -M\) in the convention of this chapter, and the same expression \(-M_{\text{EP}}\,\mathrm{d}^2\delta w/\mathrm{d}x^2\) in EP is our \(M\,\delta v''\). We derived everything from the definitions of this chapter.

What did the proof show? Equation (5.61) is the equilibrium of the beam multiplied by the virtual displacement and integrated along the length. The derivative twice passed from the internal forces to the virtual displacement: \(M\,\delta v''\) stayed under the integral, and \(V\) was only an intermediate term that cancelled in step 3. The terms at the ends remained. Where a support forbids a displacement they drop out, so the support reactions do not appear. Where the end is free they are exactly the work of the prescribed forces and couples, as for the bar. Three things are worth remembering. First, the theorem holds for every material, because we used only equilibrium. Second, the internal force enters as a link between the two integrations by parts, and in the equation \(N\) is multiplied by \(\delta u'\) and \(M\) by \(\delta v''\). For a linearly elastic beam, by theorem 5.35, \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\), and the equation reads \(\int_0^L (EA\,u'\,\delta u' + EI\,v''\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \dots\), and in it only the displacements \(u\) and \(v\) appear with their derivatives. Third, the shear force does no internal virtual work. Why, shows the next lemma, which computes the internal virtual work from the principle of virtual displacements for a body.

The body is a beam whose cross section \(A\) is a rectangle or a figure made of rectangles. For a rectangle let \(-h/2 \le y \le h/2\), \(-b/2 \le z \le b/2\). Let the axis of the beam pass through the centroids of the cross sections, that is through the points \(y = z = 0\). The virtual displacement of a point of the body at height \(y\) is, by the constraint of the model, \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta u - y\,\delta v'\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).

Lemma 5.63 Virtual work of a beam from the principle for a body (virtualno delo nosilca iz načela za telo)

Let the body be a beam with the axis \(x \in [0, L]\) and a cross section \(A\) that is a rectangle or a figure made of rectangles, with the axis through its centroid. Let the stress component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) be continuous on the body, and denote its integrals over the cross section by \(N(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M(x) = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (lemma 5.9 for a rectangle, corollary 5.19 for a figure made of rectangles). Let \((\delta u, \delta v)\) be a virtual displacement of the beam and \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta u - y\,\delta v'\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) the field that it determines. Then:

  1. The virtual strains are \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \delta u' - y\,\delta v''\), all others are zero, in particular \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\).
  2. The internal virtual work of the body (definition 4.80) is \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. Let a force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t} = (\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}, \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) with components continuous on the cross section be prescribed on the cross section at \(x = L\), and let \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(M_0 = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). Its virtual work on the cross section is \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L) + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) + M_0\,\delta v'(L)\).
Proof

We must show three items. The first two are a computation with derivatives and the theorem on the order of integration, the third a computation with the cross section.

  1. By the definition of the virtual strain (definition 4.79) \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \delta u' - y\,\delta v''\), \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = 0\) and \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\).
  2. The shear components: \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\big) = \tfrac12\,(-\delta v' + \delta v') = 0\), \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x\big) = 0\) and \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). This proves item 1.
  3. By item 1 the sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(\delta u' - y\,\delta v'')\).
  4. By theorem 4.9 on the order of integration the integral over the body is \(\int_0^L \big(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(\delta u' - y\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}A\big)\mathrm{d}x\). The functions \(\delta u'\) and \(\delta v''\) do not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), so we take them out, and the inner integral is \(\delta u'\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A - \delta v''\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\). This proves item 2.
  5. On the cross section \(x = L\) the virtual work is \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \big(\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\delta u(L) - y\,\delta v'(L)) + \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\big)\mathrm{d}A\). The values \(\delta u(L)\), \(\delta v'(L)\) and \(\delta v(L)\) are numbers, so this is \(\delta u(L)\,F_{\mathrm{x}} - \delta v'(L)\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \delta v(L)\,F_{\mathrm{y}} = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L) + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) + M_0\,\delta v'(L)\). This proves item 3.

Source: ONATE2 p. 36 (PDF), section 1.2.5 and block p36-b1: the integral of the moment and the virtual curvature. The proof is not from a source.

The lemma tells why the internal virtual work of a beam has the form \(N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\) and why the shear force does not appear in it. In the model the cross section only rotates by the slope of the axis, so the shear virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(-\delta v' + \delta v')\) is zero, and the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which \(V\) summarizes, does no work on it, although \(V \neq 0\). This is the price of the model from section 5.3.4. Whoever needs the shear force in the virtual work must use a model that allows a shear strain \(\gamma\). We treat the model with shear strain later in this chapter, and in its internal virtual work a term \(\int V\,\delta\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x\) appears.

The connection with theorem 4.82 for a body is here only a remark that we do not use. Items 2 and 3 of the lemma compute the internal virtual work of the body and the work of the force on the end cross section for the field \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta u - y\,\delta v'\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\). To obtain equation (5.61) from theorem 4.82 with this, we would have to show three more things: that the beam is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) with stresses that satisfy the assumptions of the theorem (continuously differentiable, symmetric, in equilibrium with the body force), that the work of the surface forces on the lateral surface of the beam is \(\int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x\), which requires an integral of the force along the perimeter of the cross section, and that at \(x = 0\) the virtual displacement of the cross section is zero. The work of the body force of the weight would be \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L (-\rho g A)\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\) does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), so the self weight would enter as the load \(q = -\rho g A\). We do not prove this here, we proved the equation directly with theorem 5.62.

Test on the cantilever.

Let us test equation (5.61) on the cantilever of this chapter: a steel flat bar 20 mm × 40 mm, \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), a force of 500 N downward at the free end, so \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = M_0 = 0\). By the convention of the chapter \(N = 0\), \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -500\,(1\ \text{m} - x)\) N, \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = q = 0\) (we neglect the self weight). The equation reads \(\int_0^L M\,\delta v''\,\mathrm{d}x = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\). Take two virtual displacements across the axis (figure 5.61), both with zero displacement and slope at the clamp, and numbers \(c\) for which the free end moves by 1 mm: \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\) for the first displacement and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}^2\) for the second.

First virtual displacement, \(\delta v = c\,x^2\). The virtual curvature is \(\delta v'' = 2c\) everywhere.

  1. Internal virtual work: \(\int_0^L (-500\,(L - x))\,2c\,\mathrm{d}x = -1000\,c\,\big(L^2 - L^2/2\big) = -500\,c\,L^2\), for \(L = 1\) m and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\) that is \(-0{,}5\) J.
  2. External virtual work: \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = (-500)\,(c\,L^2) = -500\,c\,L^2\), that is \(-0{,}5\) J.

Second virtual displacement, \(\delta v = c\,x^3\). The virtual curvature is \(\delta v'' = 6c\,x\), zero at \(x = 0\) and largest at the free end.

  1. Internal virtual work: \(\int_0^L (-500\,(L - x))\,6c\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = -3000\,c\,\big(L^3/2 - L^3/3\big) = -500\,c\,L^3\), for \(L = 1\) m and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}^2\) that is \(-0{,}5\) J.
  2. External virtual work: \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = -500\,c\,L^3\), that is \(-0{,}5\) J.

Both equations hold. Why are the numbers the same for both displacements? Because both displacements have the same value 1 mm at the free end, and only this value appears on the right-hand side. The left-hand side is by the principle equal, whatever the shape of the displacement. The weight with which the curvature counts is the moment, which is largest at the clamp and zero at the free end. The displacement \(c\,x^3\) has its largest curvature at the free end, where the moment is zero, so this must be compensated by a larger curvature.

For the true displacement, \(\delta v = v\) with \(v(x) = -F\,x^2\,(3L - x)/(6EI)\) and \(F = 500\) N (\(EI\) from section 5.1.3), the internal virtual work is \(\int_0^L M\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x = F^2 L^3/(3EI) = 3{,}986\) J, and the external is \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(L) = (-500)\,(-7{,}972\ \text{mm}) = 3{,}986\) J. This is twice the strain energy, \(2U = 2 \cdot 1{,}993\) J, from Clapeyron's theorem (theorem 4.86), as for the bar.

The principle notices a mistake. If we wrongly thought that the moment is \(M = -F\,L = -500\) N m along the whole length (the moment at the clamp), then for \(\delta v = c\,x^2\) we would obtain the internal work \(-1000\,c\,L^2\) and the external \(-500\,c\,L^2\). The equation does not hold, and by theorem 5.62 such a moment is not in equilibrium.

What about a virtual displacement that is not admissible? Take \(\delta v = c\,x\), which has slope \(c\) at the clamp. The left side is zero, because \(\delta v'' = 0\), and the right side is \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = -500\,c\,L\). The equation does not hold, because the theorem holds only for admissible virtual displacements. Why does it not hold? The clamp acts on the beam with the force \(-V(0) = 500\) N upward and with the couple \(-M(0) = 500\) N m. For an admissible virtual displacement these reactions do no work, because \(\delta v(0) = \delta v'(0) = 0\). For the displacement \(\delta v = c\,x\) we have \(\delta v(0) = 0\), so the force still does no work, but the couple does the work \(-M(0)\,\delta v'(0) = 500\,c\) N m by lemma 5.61. If we add it to the right-hand side, we obtain for \(L = 1\) m \(-500\,c + 500\,c = 0\), which is the left side. The reaction of the clamp therefore does work if we allow rotation at the clamp, and that is why we forbade it in definition 5.60.

Simply supported beam with a uniform load.

The same for the simply supported beam of the chapter, \(L = 2\) m, load \(q = -q_0\) with \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m. By the convention \(V(x) = q_0\,(x - L/2)\) and \(M(x) = \tfrac12\,q_0\,x\,(L - x)\). The supports do not forbid the rotation of the cross section, and \(M(0) = M(L) = 0\), so the terms at the ends are zero, also when \(\delta v' \neq 0\). The equation reads \(\int_0^L M\,\delta v''\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x\).

  1. Let \(\delta v = c\,x\,(L - x)\) with \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\), 1 mm in the middle, \(\delta v'' = -2c\). Left side: \(\int_0^L \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\,(-2c)\,\mathrm{d}x = -c\,q_0\,L^3/6 = -1{,}333\) J.
  2. Right side: \(\int_0^L (-q_0)\,c\,x\,(L - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -c\,q_0\,L^3/6 = -1{,}333\) J.
  3. Let also \(\delta v = c\,x\,(L^2 - x^2)\) with \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}^2\), which is not symmetric, at most \(3{,}08\) mm at \(x = L/\sqrt 3\), and has slopes \(c\,L^2 = 0{,}004\) and \(-2c\,L^2 = -0{,}008\) at the supports, \(\delta v'' = -6c\,x\). Left side: \(\int_0^L \tfrac12 q_0\,x\,(L - x)\,(-6c\,x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -c\,q_0\,L^4/4 = -4\) J. Right side: \(\int_0^L (-q_0)\,c\,x\,(L^2 - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = -c\,q_0\,L^4/4 = -4\) J.

Both equations hold, as the theorem predicts. The third item shows that the equation is satisfied also for a displacement that is not symmetric, and that it does not matter what slope it has at the supports.

A point force inside.

The theorem requires a continuous load. A point force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) at an interior point \(x_0\) is not a continuous load, and by section 5.2.3 the shear force jumps at \(x_0\) while the moment is continuous. We apply the theorem on each of the parts \([0, x_0]\) and \([x_0, L]\) and add.

Corollary 5.64 Point force and couple inside a beam (točkovna sila in dvojica v notranjosti nosilca)

Let the conditions of theorem 5.62 hold on each of the intervals \((a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}})\), \(k = 1, \dots, m\), into which the points \(0 = a_0 < a_1 < \dots < a_{\mathrm{m}} = L\) divide the beam, with the functions \(N\) and \(M\) continuously differentiable on each closed interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with one-sided derivatives and \(M\) continuous on \([0, L]\). At an interior point \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) let a point force \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) act, and let \(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) = -F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) = -M_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^\pm)\) and \(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^\pm)\) denote one-sided limits. Then for every virtual displacement of the beam equation (5.61) holds with the added terms \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} F_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}})\) on the right.

Proof

We must show that the sum over the parts from the theorem differs by the terms at the points \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\). It suffices to look at one interior term.

  1. On each part \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 5.62 hold with the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) instead of \([0, L]\) and with the function \(G\) on that part: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{a_{\mathrm{k}}} (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}-1}^+)\).
  2. By additivity of the integral (lemma 3.39, item 2) the integral over \([0, L]\) is the sum over the parts. The sum of the right-hand sides telescopes: \(G(L) - G(0) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{m-1} \big(G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+)\big)\).
  3. At an interior point \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) both \(N\) and \(M\) are continuous (there is no axial point force in the theorem), and \(V\) jumps. Therefore \(G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) = -\big(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-)\big)\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) - \big(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-)\big)\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}}) = F_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) + M_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  4. We treat the terms \(G(L) - G(0)\) as in step 5 of the proof of the theorem, and the equation follows.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The use on parts: EP example 3.4.2 (pp. 129-131), ONATE2 (1.6) (PDF p. 35), where the sum over point loads stands next to the integral.

A point couple enters the corollary, as lemma 5.61 says, as a moment multiplied by the virtual slope. Test: a simply supported beam, \(L = 2\) m, a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N in the middle \(x_0 = 1\) m. The shear force is \(V = -500\) N on the left and \(+500\) N on the right, a jump of \(+1000\) N \(= -F_{\mathrm{y}}\), the moment is \(M = 500\,x\) on the left and \(500\,(L - x)\) on the right with a peak of 500 N m. For \(\delta v = c\,x\,(L - x)\) the left side is \(\int_0^1 500\,x\,(-2c)\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_1^2 500\,(2 - x)\,(-2c)\,\mathrm{d}x = -500\,c - 500\,c = -1000\,c\), and the right side \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(1) = -1000\,c\), for \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\) both \(-1\) J.

5.10.3 Equilibrium from virtual work (ravnovesje iz virtualnega dela)

Theorem 5.62 says: if the beam is in equilibrium, the equation holds for every virtual displacement. Does the converse hold? Let \(N\) be the axial force and \(M\) the bending moment of some beam and let equation (5.61) hold for all virtual displacements. Are \(N\) and \(M\) then in equilibrium, that is, do \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(M'' = q\) and the conditions at the ends hold? For the bar we obtained the answer with the fundamental lemma (theorem 4.78). Here we do the same, only that we have two displacement functions, and therefore two parts of the equation and two conditions at the end. We prove it for arbitrary supports at both ends: at each end the support forbids an arbitrary set of the components \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\), \(\delta v'\), and the clamp, the pin support, the roller support and the free end are only special cases.

The idea: we do the integration by parts in the reverse direction. We obtain an integral in which \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) are multiplied by functions that should be zero, and terms at the ends. With virtual displacements that are zero at the ends we show that the functions are zero. Then we choose virtual displacements that are different from zero only near one end: a bump multiplied by a polynomial of degree at most one, so that one single free component remains in the equation. For \(\delta v\) we must use a bump with more derivatives, because a virtual displacement of a beam must be twice continuously differentiable. We made such bumps in lemma 4.122, item 1.

Theorem 5.65 Equilibrium of a beam from virtual displacements (ravnovesje nosilca iz virtualnih pomikov)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\). At each end \(\xi \in \{0, L\}\) let the support forbid an arbitrary set of the components \(\delta u(\xi)\), \(\delta v(\xi)\), \(\delta v'(\xi)\) (none, some or all three), and let a virtual displacement of the beam be a pair \((\delta u, \delta v)\) from definition 5.60 that has the forbidden components equal to zero. At the end \(\xi\) let a force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\) along the axis, a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(C^\xi\) (component \(z\)) act. Let \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\) and \(n_0 = -1\), the component of the outward normal of the end along the axis \(x\). Let the load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) be continuous on \([0, L]\). Let the functions \(N\) and \(M\) be continuous on \([0, L]\) and differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and let there exist continuous functions \(p\), \(r\), \(s\) on \([0, L]\) with \(N' = p\), \(M' = r\) and \(r' = s\) on \((0, L)\). Let \(V = -r\). If the equation

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + C^\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.62}\]

holds for every virtual displacement of the beam, then

\[N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad V' + q = 0 , \qquad M' + V = 0 \quad \text{on } (0, L) ,\]

and for every end \(\xi\): if the support does not forbid the component \(\delta u(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,N(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\), if it does not forbid \(\delta v(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\), and if it does not forbid \(\delta v'(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\).

Proof

We do the integration by parts in the reverse direction. From the fundamental lemma we obtain the equations in the interior, and with bumps multiplied by polynomials each condition at an end separately.

  1. Let \(G = N\,\delta u + V\,\delta v + M\,\delta v'\) on \([0, L]\) for a virtual displacement of the beam. As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 5.62, \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\).
  2. For \(0 < x < L\), by the product rule (lemma 1.81, item 3), with \(N' = p\), \(V' = -r' = -s\) and \(M' = r = -V\),
    \[G' = p\,\delta u + N\,\delta u' - s\,\delta v + V\,\delta v' + M'\,\delta v' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
    The terms \(V\,\delta v'\) and \(M'\,\delta v' = -V\,\delta v'\) cancel, so \(G' = p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\), and this is a continuous function on \([0, L]\).
  3. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1, \(\int_0^L (p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0) = \sum_{\xi} n_\xi\,G(\xi)\).
  4. From step 3 we express \(\int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_\xi n_\xi\,G(\xi) - \int_0^L (p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and insert it in the equation of the theorem. After moving the terms, with \(A_\xi = n_\xi\,N(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\), \(B_\xi = n_\xi\,V(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) and \(D_\xi = n_\xi\,M(\xi) - C^\xi\),
    \[\int_0^L \big((p + q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\delta u + (q - s)\,\delta v\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(A_\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + B_\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + D_\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\Big) . \quad (*)\]
  5. Let \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta u = \psi\), where \(\psi\) is differentiable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), with a continuous derivative and \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\). This is a virtual displacement for every set of supports, because all three components at \(\xi\) are zero or forbidden. The right side of equation \((*)\) is zero, and \(\int_0^L (p + q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The function \(p + q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous, so by the fundamental lemma 4.77 it is zero on \([0, L]\), and \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = p + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) on \((0, L)\).
  6. Let \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = q_{\alpha\beta}\) for numbers \(0 < \alpha < \beta < L\), where \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) is the bump from lemma 4.122. By item 1 of the lemma it has continuous first three derivatives and is zero with all derivatives at the ends of the interval \([0, L]\), so this is a virtual displacement of the beam for every set of supports, the right side of \((*)\) is zero, and \(\int_\alpha^\beta (q - s)\,q_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every such pair.
  7. Let \(g = q - s\) be continuous on \([0, L]\), and suppose that \(g(c_0) \neq 0\) for some \(c_0 \in (0, L)\). If \(g(c_0) < 0\) we take \(-g\) (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 4.77). Continuity gives an interval \([\alpha, \beta] \subset (0, L)\) around \(c_0\) on which \(g > g(c_0)/2\) (step 2 of the same proof). The function \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) is nowhere negative, so \(\int_\alpha^\beta g\,q_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac{g(c_0)}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta q_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac{g(c_0)}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\ell}{2}\,(\ell/4)^8 > 0\) for \(\ell = \beta - \alpha\) (item 3 of lemma 4.122, step 2). This contradicts step 6. So \(g = 0\) on \((0, L)\), and \(M'' = s = q\), hence \(V' = -s = -q\), which is \(V' + q = 0\). From \(V = -r = -M'\) we have \(M' + V = 0\).
  8. The functions \(p + q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(g = q - s\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and zero on \((0, L)\), so by continuity they are zero also at the endpoints. The left integral in \((*)\) is therefore zero for every virtual displacement, and what remains is \(\sum_\xi \big(A_\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + B_\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + D_\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\big) = 0\).
  9. Let \(\xi\) be one of the ends. For \(\xi = L\) let \(\chi = q_{\mathrm{L}/2,\,2\mathrm{L}}\), for \(\xi = 0\) let \(\chi = q_{-\mathrm{L},\,\mathrm{L}/2}\), bumps from lemma 4.122. By item 1 of the lemma \(\chi\) has continuous first three derivatives on the whole line. Let \(k = \chi(\xi) > 0\) (each of the two factors is positive at \(\xi\)) and \(m = \chi'(\xi)\). The function \(\chi\) is zero on an open interval around the other end (\(x < L/2\) for \(\xi = L\), \(x > L/2\) for \(\xi = 0\)), so its derivatives are zero there too, and every displacement below has all three components zero at the other end. Below, every \(\delta u\) is equal to \(\chi\) or zero, and every \(\delta v\) is \(\chi\) multiplied by a polynomial of degree at most one. Such a \(\delta u\) has a continuous first derivative and such a \(\delta v\) continuous first three derivatives (product rule, lemma 1.81, item 3, and corollary 1.64), so it satisfies the requirements of definition 5.60 if the forbidden components are zero at \(\xi\).
  10. Let \(\delta u(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = \chi\) and \(\delta v = 0\). At \(\xi\) we have \(\delta u(\xi) = k\) and \(\delta v(\xi) = \delta v'(\xi) = 0\), so this is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(A_\xi\,k = 0\), so \(A_\xi = 0\) and \(n_\xi\,N(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\).
  11. Let \(\delta v'(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = \chi(x)\,(x - \xi)\). By the product rule \(\delta v'(x) = \chi'(x)\,(x - \xi) + \chi(x)\), so at \(\xi\) we have \(\delta v(\xi) = 0\) and \(\delta v'(\xi) = k\). This is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(D_\xi\,k = 0\), so \(D_\xi = 0\) and \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\).
  12. Let \(\delta v(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = \chi(x)\,\big(1 - (m/k)\,(x - \xi)\big)/k\). At \(\xi\) we have \(\delta v(\xi) = 1\) and by the product rule \(\delta v'(\xi) = \big(m - (m/k)\,k\big)/k = 0\). This is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(B_\xi = 0\), so \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\).

Source: EP example 4.2.1 (pp. 170-171), more precisely the natural boundary conditions \(N - P = 0\) and \(M + M_0 = 0\) at \(x = L\) (with EP's sign of the moment), EP §3.5.7 (pp. 146-147). The proof for arbitrary supports, with test displacements that are bumps multiplied by a polynomial, is not from a source.

Theorems 5.62 and 5.65 together say that for any supports equation (5.61) for all virtual displacements is equivalent to equilibrium. For a linearly elastic prismatic beam \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) (theorem 5.35), and from the theorem we obtain the equations \(EA\,u'' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(EI\,v'''' = q\) from theorem 5.42, together with the conditions \(EA\,u'(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-EI\,v'''(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(EI\,v''(L) = M_0\) at the free end \(x = L\). These are exactly the boundary conditions of a free end from section 5.7.2. For our cantilever \(v'' = -F\,(L - x)/(EI)\), so \(EI\,v'' = M = -F\,(L - x)\), \(-EI\,v''' = -F = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(EI\,v''(L) = 0 = M_0\), and all three conditions indeed hold. At the clamp at \(x = 0\) all three components are forbidden and the theorem demands nothing, at a pin support \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) are forbidden, and the theorem demands only \(-M(0) = C^0\), so \(M(0) = 0\) if there is no couple.

Why are exactly these three conditions what the equation "gives by itself"? Because in the terms at the ends stand pairs in which one quantity is a displacement and the other a force or moment that does work on it: \((u, N)\), \((v, V)\) and \((v', M)\). At an end one component of each pair is prescribed. If the support prescribes the displacement, the virtual displacement is zero there and the corresponding force is not in the equation. If it does not, the virtual displacement is free, and the equation demands that the force equals the prescribed load. The displacements \(u\), \(v\) and \(v'\) are essential or geometric boundary conditions (bistveni ali geometrijski robni pogoji), the forces \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) natural or force boundary conditions (naravni ali silni robni pogoji), as for the bar (EP §3.5.7, pp. 146-147, and example 4.2.1 on p. 171, where \(u\), \(w\) and \(-\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}x\) are the basic variables). The slope \(v'\) and the moment \(M\) are a new pair that the bar did not have.
