Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.10 Virtual work of a beam (virtualno delo nosilca)

Chapter 4 wrote the equilibrium of a bar as an equation about virtual displacements. The axial force times the virtual strain gave the internal virtual work, and this was equal to the work of the loads on the virtual displacements (theorem 4.76). The converse (theorem 4.78) showed that one equation for all virtual displacements replaces the equilibrium in every cross section and the conditions at the end. Now we repeat this for a beam. A bar carries only the axial force and has one displacement function. A beam also carries the shear force \(V\) and the bending moment \(M\), and its displacement consists of the axial displacement \(u(x)\) and the deflection \(v(x)\). Which displacements of a beam are virtual? What equation about virtual work holds for it, and what do \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) do in it?

We look at the answer on the cantilever of section 5.9.2 (\(L = 1\) m, a force of 500 N downward at the free end, \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -500\,(1\ \text{m} - x)\) N). In this section we first say which displacements of a beam are virtual and how much work a couple does. Then we state and prove the virtual work equation for a beam, test it on the numbers of the cantilever and of the simply supported beam, and finally show the converse: the equation for all virtual displacements gives the differential equation and the boundary conditions.

5.10.1 Virtual displacement of a beam (virtualni pomik nosilca)

For a bar, a virtual displacement was an imagined displacement allowed by the supports, during which the forces stayed as they are (definition 4.70). For a beam the same two parts hold. The supports are the constraints the beam has at its ends (definition 5.5). Besides them we have constraints that the model of the beam itself imposes. By theorem 5.15 a point of the beam at height \(y\) above the axis moves by \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = u(x) - y\,v'(x)\) along the axis and by \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = v(x)\) across it. The term \(-y\,v'\) is the horizontal displacement of the point, because the cross section tilts together with the axis. Two functions, \(u\) and \(v\), therefore determine the displacements of all points of the beam. This is the constraint that the model of the beam (definition 5.14) demands: cross sections stay plane and perpendicular to the axis. A virtual displacement must satisfy the constraints of the model just as the true displacement does. Why? The constraint of the model acts as an inner support. What keeps the cross section perpendicular to the axis is the shear stress that the shear force \(V\) sums up, and it does no work in a displacement that respects the constraint, just as the reaction of a support does no work in a displacement the support allows. We show this in the next section. The price of the constraint is the neglected shear strain (section 5.3.4). In the model with shear strain, treated later in this chapter, the cross section may rotate differently from the axis, and a virtual displacement has a third function, the rotation of the cross section. Since a virtual displacement respects the constraint of the model, it is also a pair of functions \((\delta u, \delta v)\) that we insert into the same formula, and the virtual strain of the fibre at height \(y\) is, by the equation for the strain in theorem 5.15,

\[\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \delta u' - y\,\delta v'' .\]

The second derivative of the function \(\delta v\) appears in it, so we must be allowed to compute it at every point of the beam, also at the ends. It should be continuous, because in the next section we integrate the product of the moment and \(\delta v''\) and use the fundamental theorem of calculus, which needs a continuous integrand. For a bar we required a continuous \(\delta u'\) for the same reason, here also a continuous \(\delta v''\). A continuous slope \(\delta v'\) means that the beam does not acquire a sharp corner or a hinge in our imagination, and a continuous \(\delta v''\) also means that the curvature does not change abruptly. The requirement is stricter than necessary. Some books require only continuous \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\), because \(\delta v''\) appears only under an integral, and the integral exists also when the curvature jumps at some places. For this chapter the stricter requirement costs nothing, because in the converse of the theorem of virtual work, which we prove last, we insert only functions \(\delta v\) with continuous first three derivatives. We relax it in chapter 12, where a displacement is assembled from polynomials on pieces of the beam and the curvature changes abruptly at the borders of the pieces.

At the ends of the beam the supports forbid some components of the virtual displacement. Let us look at this on both beams of this chapter (figure 5.153).

At the top is the axis of the cantilever from \(0\) to \(L\), clamped into a wall at \(x = 0\). A clamp (vpetje) forbids the displacement of the axis and the rotation of the cross section. The end at \(x = L\) is free and forbids nothing. At the bottom is the axis of the simply supported beam. The fixed pin support (nepomična členkasta podpora) at \(x = 0\) forbids the displacement of the axis but allows rotation, the roller support (pomična členkasta podpora) at \(x = L\) forbids only the displacement across the axis. As for a bar, where a support prescribes a displacement the virtual displacement is zero, because a virtual displacement is an imagined change relative to the true position, and the support does not allow a change.

Definition 5.61 Virtual displacement of a beam (virtualni pomik nosilca)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) with the constraints of the supports at its ends (definition 5.5). A virtual displacement of the beam is a pair of functions \((\delta u, \delta v)\), where \(\delta u\) is a continuously differentiable function of a real variable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), and \(\delta v\) is a function with continuous first and second derivatives on an open interval containing \([0, L]\). At each end the constraints of the support hold: at a clamp \(\delta u = \delta v = \delta v' = 0\), at a fixed pin support \(\delta u = \delta v = 0\), at a roller support \(\delta v = 0\), at a free end there are no constraints. The number \(\delta u(x)\) is the virtual displacement of the point of the axis along the axis, \(\delta v(x)\) across the axis, the number \(\delta v'(x)\) is the virtual slope of the axis, which by theorem 5.15 is also the virtual rotation of the cross section, and \(\delta v''(x)\) is the virtual curvature (virtualna ukrivljenost). During a virtual displacement all forces on the beam and internal forces in it stay as they are.

Source: EP §4.2.2 (pp. 168-169) and example 4.2.1 (pp. 170-171), ONATE2 §1.2.5 (PDF pp. 35-36), where the \(C^1\) requirement (\(w\) and \(w'\) continuous) is explained. The requirement of a continuous second derivative is stronger than that. The definition follows definition 4.70, the requirement on the second derivative and the constraints by type of support are ours.

Above the two axes we draw pairs of functions that are a virtual displacement of a beam in orange, and pairs that are not in grey dashed lines (figure 5.153). For the cantilever \(\delta v = c\,x^2\) is a virtual displacement, because at \(x = 0\) it is zero with zero slope. The function \(\delta v = c\,x\) is not. Its slope is the ratio of the rise \(c\,\Delta x\) to the length \(\Delta x\), equal to \(c \neq 0\) everywhere, also at the clamp, and the clamp does not allow rotation. A function that is zero up to \(x = L/2\) and linear afterwards is not, because it has a corner at \(x = L/2\), that is a jump of the slope, and has no second derivative. For the simply supported beam \(\delta v = c\,x\,(L - x)\) is a virtual displacement with the slopes \(c\,L\) and \(-c\,L\) at the ends, because the supports allow rotation. The function \(\delta v = c\,(x + L/2)\) is not, because at \(x = 0\) it has the value \(cL/2 \neq 0\), and the support does not allow a displacement across the axis there.

At the free end and inside the beam there can be point loads, that is forces and couples. We know the work of a force in a virtual displacement (definition 4.71). But what about a couple (definition 5.3)? Its resultant is zero, and its moment is the same about every point (lemma 5.4). So it seems that a couple does no work. Let us draw a couple on the axis of the beam and move the axis in our imagination in two ways (figure 5.154).

At the top left there are two opposite forces \(P\) across the axis, one upward and the other downward. First we only shift the axis upward by \(\delta v\), without rotating it, into the orange position. Both points of application rise by the same \(\delta v\), and the works of the two forces cancel:

\[P\,\delta v + (-P)\,\delta v = 0 .\]

In a shift without rotation the couple indeed does no work.

At the top right are the same forces, one at \(x_0 + d/2\) and the other at \(x_0 - d/2\), at distance \(d\) around the point \(x_0\). Now let the axis rotate about \(x_0\) with the virtual slope \(\delta v'\). Since the axis is straight between the points of application, the right point of application rises by \(\delta v'\,d/2\) and the left one sinks by the same amount. The work of the two forces is

\[P\cdot\delta v'\,\frac d2 + (-P)\cdot\Big(-\delta v'\,\frac d2\Big) = P\,d\,\delta v' .\]

This is the moment of the couple times the virtual rotation. A general virtual displacement, however, also bends the axis, and then the rises are not exactly \(\delta v'\,d/2\).

We draw the graph of the function \(\delta v\) around the point \(x_0\) and on the axis the points \(x_0 - h\) and \(x_0 + h\), each at the distance \(h\) from \(x_0\). The middle secant passes through the points of the graph above them. Over the length \(2h\) it rises by \(\delta v(x_0 + h) - \delta v(x_0 - h)\), so its slope is the quotient of the next lemma. The left secant runs from \(x_0 - h\) to \(x_0\), the right one from \(x_0\) to \(x_0 + h\). The rise of the middle one is the sum of the rises of the left and the right one, each over the length \(h\), so the slope of the middle one is the average of the slopes of the left and the right one. In the right panel \(h\) is smaller on the same axis, and all three secants approach the tangent with slope \(\delta v'(x_0)\).

Precisely:

Lemma 5.62 Virtual work of a couple (virtualno delo dvojice sil)

Let \(x_0 \in [0, L]\) and let \(\delta v\) be a function on \([0, L]\), differentiable at \(x_0\) (one-sided at an end point). Let the couple on the beam be a force \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point of the axis \(b\) and a force \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point of the axis \(a\), where \(P \neq 0\) and \(d = b - a > 0\) are numbers, and let both points lie on the beam next to \(x_0\): for \(0 < x_0 < L\) let \(a = x_0 - d/2\) and \(b = x_0 + d/2\), where \(d/2\) is not larger than \(x_0\) nor than \(L - x_0\), for \(x_0 = 0\) let \(a = 0\) and \(b = d \le L\), and for \(x_0 = L\) let \(a = L - d \ge 0\) and \(b = L\). Its moment (component \(z\)) about the point \(x_0\) is \(M_0 = P\,d\). The virtual work of the couple is

\[P\,\delta v(b) - P\,\delta v(a) = M_0\,\frac{\delta v(b) - \delta v(a)}{d} ,\]

and when \(d \to 0\) at constant \(M_0\) (a point couple with moment \(M_0\)) it tends to \(M_0\,\delta v'(x_0)\).

Proof

We must show the moment of the couple and the limit in which the chord turns to the tangent. We compute the moment from the definition, the limit from the definition of the derivative, separately for an interior point and for the ends.

  1. By the definition of the moment of a force (definition 2.37) the force \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point \(b\) has the lever arm \((b - x_0)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) about \(x_0\) and the moment \((b - x_0)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (b - x_0)\,P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The force \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at the point \(a\) has the moment \((a - x_0)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} \times (-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = -(a - x_0)\,P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The sum is \((b - a)\,P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = P\,d\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \(M_0 = P\,d\) for all three placements. The point \(x_0\) cancels from the sum, as for every couple (lemma 5.4).
  2. Both forces point across the axis, so by definition 4.71 they do work only with the virtual displacements across the axis of their points of application, \(\delta v(b)\) and \(\delta v(a)\). These are the heights of the curved axis after the virtual displacement above the straight axis before it at the points \(a\) and \(b\). The virtual work is \(P\,\delta v(b) - P\,\delta v(a)\).
  3. With \(P = M_0/d\) we obtain the second form. The quotient in it is the slope of the chord, the straight segment that joins the points of the graph of \(\delta v\) above \(a\) and \(b\).
  4. Interior point, \(0 < x_0 < L\). Let \(h = d/2\). The quotient is the slope of the middle secant, and this is the average of the slopes of the left and the right secant: \(\dfrac{\delta v(x_0 + h) - \delta v(x_0 - h)}{2h} = \tfrac12\Big(\dfrac{\delta v(x_0 + h) - \delta v(x_0)}{h} + \dfrac{\delta v(x_0 - h) - \delta v(x_0)}{-h}\Big)\). By the definition of the derivative (definition 1.9) both quotients in the bracket tend to \(\delta v'(x_0)\) as \(h \to 0\) (the left and the right secant approach the tangent), the first with the increment \(h\), the second with the increment \(-h\). So by the rule for the limit of a sum the quotient tends to \(\delta v'(x_0)\). In the figure the shorter chord turns closer to the tangent with slope \(\delta v'(x_0)\).
  5. The end \(x_0 = 0\). The forces sit at \(0\) and \(d\), mirrored as at the end \(L\). The quotient is \(\dfrac{\delta v(d) - \delta v(0)}{d}\), the quotient from the definition of the derivative at the point \(0\) with the increment \(d\), so it tends to \(\delta v'(0)\).
  6. The end \(x_0 = L\). The forces sit at \(L - d\) and \(L\). The quotient is \(\dfrac{\delta v(L) - \delta v(L - d)}{d} = \dfrac{\delta v(L - d) - \delta v(L)}{-d}\), the quotient from the definition of the derivative at the point \(L\) with the increment \(-d\), so it tends to \(\delta v'(L)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. ONATE2 (1.6a) (PDF p. 35) multiplies the concentrated moment \(M^c_{\mathrm{j}}\) by the virtual slope. The idea of a couple as a pair of forces that move differently when the section rotates is ours, and so are the placements of the forces at the ends of the beam.

The lemma tells where a point couple enters the equation of virtual work: its moment is multiplied by the virtual slope. The units agree. The moment has the unit N m, and the slope is the rise \(\delta v'\,d/2\) divided by the length \(d/2\), a ratio of two lengths without a unit, so the product has the unit N m = J, like a work. This is exactly what we need at the free end of the cantilever, where a couple \(M_0\) acts. There the lemma places the forces differently, because for \(x_0 = L\) the right force at \(L + d/2\) would lie outside the beam, which ends at \(L\). So both forces sit on the beam, at \(L - d\) and \(L\), at distance \(d\). The moment is again \(P\,d\) (step 1 of the proof), and the work is \(M_0\) times the slope of the chord from \(L - d\) to \(L\), which lies on one side of the point \(L\) only.

A point couple is an idealization, like a point force. A real couple is applied for example by a lever welded across the beam, with a finite \(d\), and then the work is \(M_0\) times the slope of the chord, not of the tangent. How much do they differ? At the end nothing cancels and the error is proportional to \(d\). A point couple is therefore a good model when \(d\) is small compared with the length over which the slope changes noticeably.

5.10.2 Principle of virtual displacements for a beam (načelo virtualnih pomikov za nosilec)

First look at a piece of the beam between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), as for the bar (figure 5.156). The shear force \(V(x_2)\) and the couple \(M(x_2)\) act on its right face, \(-V(x_1)\) and \(-M(x_1)\) on its left face, and a load acts along it, and all is in equilibrium. At the bottom, in grey dashed, is the piece before the virtual displacement, its axis and the two end cross sections, of length \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\), and in orange its axis after it. We split the virtual displacement into two parts. We first move the piece rigidly in our imagination, by \(\delta u(x_1)\) and \(\delta v(x_1)\), and rotate it by \(\delta v'(x_1)\) about its left face. This brings it to the position shown by the thin blackwhite line, a straight piece with its right cross section. The work of all forces in a rigid displacement is zero. Why? In a shift without rotation all points of application move by the same amount, so the work equals the resultant of the forces multiplied by the shift, and the resultant is zero. In a rotation each force does work equal to its moment multiplied by the rotation, like the couple in lemma 5.62, and the sum of the moments is zero. Both are the equilibrium of the piece (section 5.2.3). What remains is the second part of the displacement, from the thin blackwhite line to the orange axis: the stretching and bending of the piece.

Along the axis the right face moves relative to the left one by \(\delta u(x_2) - \delta u(x_1) \approx \delta u'\,\Delta x\) (the figure does not show this shift, it is the same as for the bar in theorem 4.76), and the force \(N(x_2)\) acts on it, so the work is approximately \(N\,\delta u'\,\Delta x\). Across the axis the right end rises above the rigidly moved piece by \(\delta v(x_2) - \delta v(x_1) - \delta v'(x_1)\,\Delta x \approx \tfrac12\,\delta v''\,\Delta x^2\). This is the blue measure between the end of the thin blackwhite line and the end of the orange axis, the remainder of the whole rise \(\delta v(x_2)\). It is a term with the square of the length of the piece, so we leave out the work of the shear force \(V(x_2)\) on it. Why may we? A beam of length \(L\) has \(L/\Delta x\) pieces, each contributes a term of size \(\Delta x^2\), so all together a term of size \(\Delta x\), which approaches zero as the pieces get smaller. On the cantilever of this chapter (\(V = -500\) N, \(L = 1\) m) and for the virtual displacement \(\delta v = c\,x^2\), which we test below, the sum of the omitted terms is 1 % of the internal virtual work for pieces of 10 mm, and 0,1 % for pieces of 1 mm. For a piece that is not short the term must not be left out, but the exact proof below does not need this step.

The right face rotates relative to the left one by \(\delta v'(x_2) - \delta v'(x_1)\) \(\approx \delta v''\,\Delta x\). This is the blue angle between the dashed direction that the cross section would have after the rigid displacement and the orange cross section. By lemma 5.62 the moment \(M(x_2)\) on this face does the work of about \(M\,\delta v''\,\Delta x\). The internal virtual work of the piece is therefore approximately \((N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\Delta x\), up to terms with \(\Delta x^2\), and when we sum over the pieces it approaches an integral. The shear force does not appear in the internal virtual work because by the assumption of the model the cross section stays perpendicular to the axis: it only rotates and does not slide along the neighbouring cross section, as we shall see in lemma 5.64. Two neighbouring pieces cancel each other's work at their common cross section, as for the bar, because the right piece acts on the left one with the forces \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) and the left one on the right one with the opposite ones, while the displacement of the common cross section is the same for both.

So we expect that for a beam made of such pieces

\[\int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x + \text{work of forces and couples at the ends} .\]

Here \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) are the axial and the transverse load per unit length (definitions 4.74 and 5.2). Now we prove it precisely. The equilibrium of a piece of the beam, which we shall use, has the form of the differential relations from corollary 5.11, without a distributed couple (\(m = 0\)): \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\), \(M' = -V\). The minus has the reason we gave there: \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) are the force and the moment that the part of the beam at larger \(x\) transmits to the part at smaller \(x\) (definition 5.7), so the increment of the force across a piece is balanced by the load on it.

The ends of the beam are shown in figure 5.157.

It is drawn as on a board. The beam lies along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\). At \(x = 0\) it is clamped, so \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\) are zero there and \(G(0) = 0\). At the free end \(x = L\) the prescribed load \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(M_0\) acts and does work on the virtual displacement of the end, \(\delta u(L)\), \(\delta v(L)\) and \(\delta v'(L)\). Just inside the end the internal forces \(N(L)\), \(V(L)\) and \(M(L)\) hold the load in equilibrium, so they equal it. The sum of the three products force times displacement is \(G(L)\).

We draw the interval \([0, L]\) and above it the function \(G\), continuous up to the end points. The interior \((0, L)\) is where \(G\) is differentiable, the end points are not included. The values at the ends are \(G(0)\) and \(G(L)\), and their difference is what the fundamental theorem of calculus will give us. Below the graph we draw the derivative \(G'\), and the area under it is exactly this difference.

Theorem 5.63 Principle of virtual displacements for a beam (načelo virtualnih pomikov za nosilec)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\). Let the load per unit length, \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the axis and \(q\) across it, be continuous on \([0, L]\), and let there be no distributed couple (\(m = 0\) in corollary 5.11). Let the functions \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) (the shear force and moment on the face of the piece) be continuous on \([0, L]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and let for every \(x \in (0, L)\)

\[N'(x) + q_{\mathrm{x}}(x) = 0 , \qquad V'(x) + q(x) = 0 , \qquad M'(x) + V(x) = 0 .\]

At each end of the beam let one of the possibilities hold for each of the three pairs \((\delta u, N)\), \((\delta v, V)\) and \((\delta v', M)\): the support forbids the corresponding component of the virtual displacement, or the end load is prescribed and equal to the internal force. At the end \(x = L\) this means \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = N(L)\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = V(L)\), \(M_0 = M(L)\), and at the end \(x = 0\) it means \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = -N(0)\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -V(0)\), \(M_0 = -M(0)\), where \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) are the forces on the end of the beam along the axis and across it and \(M_0\) is the moment of the couple at the end (component \(z\)). Then for every virtual displacement \((\delta u, \delta v)\) of the beam

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \Big[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v + M_0\,\delta v'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}} \\ &\quad + \Big[F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v + M_0\,\delta v'\Big]_{\mathrm{x} = 0} . \end{aligned} \tag{5.66}\]

where at each end we omit the terms of the components that the support forbids.

Proof

We must show that the left and the right integral differ exactly by the work of the end loads. The proof is integration by parts twice, as for the bar, written with one function \(G\) that we differentiate. The number \(G(x)\) has a meaning that we see on the piece of figure 5.156: it is the virtual work that the internal forces on the face at \(x\) do on the part of the beam to the left of it. For the right face of the piece this is the work of the force \(N(x_2)\) on \(\delta u(x_2)\), of the force \(V(x_2)\) on the rise \(\delta v(x_2)\) and of the couple \(M(x_2)\) on the rotation \(\delta v'(x_2)\).

  1. Let \(G(x) = N(x)\,\delta u(x) + V(x)\,\delta v(x) + M(x)\,\delta v'(x)\) for \(x \in [0, L]\). The functions \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\) are differentiable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), so they are continuous there (lemma 1.80). The functions \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) are continuous by assumption, so \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) as a sum of products of continuous functions (corollary 1.65). The term \(V\,\delta v\) is the work of the force \(V\) on the rise of the right face, and the term \(M\,\delta v'\) the work of the couple \(M\) on the rotation.
  2. Let \(0 < x < L\). All functions in \(G\) are differentiable at \(x\): \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) by assumption, \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) by definition, \(\delta v'\) because \(\delta v\) has a second derivative. By the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3)
    \[G' = N'\,\delta u + N\,\delta u' + V'\,\delta v + V\,\delta v' + M'\,\delta v' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
  3. By assumption \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The terms \(V\,\delta v'\) and \(M'\,\delta v' = -V\,\delta v'\) cancel, so
    \[G'(x) = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
    What has cancelled is shown by the piece of figure 5.156 in a rigid rotation by \(\delta v'\). The force \(V(x_2)\) rises with the right face by \(\delta v'\,\Delta x\) and does the work \(V\,\delta v'\,\Delta x\). The moments on the faces, \(M(x_2)\) and \(-M(x_1)\), differ by \(M'\,\Delta x = -V\,\Delta x\) and do the work \(-V\,\Delta x\,\delta v'\) on the rotation. The sum is zero, which is the moment equilibrium of the piece. What remains reads as follows: the increase of the work of the forces on the faces per unit length, \(G'\), and the work of the load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v\) together give the internal virtual work \(N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\), which we estimated on the piece before.
  4. The function on the right is continuous on \([0, L]\), because \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(q\), \(N\), \(M\), \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\), \(\delta u'\) and \(\delta v''\) are continuous (corollary 1.65). By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 1, with the functions \(G\) and \(G'\) we therefore have
    \[\int_0^L \big(N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0) .\]
  5. At the end \(x = L\) each term of the function \(G\) is either zero, because the support forbids the component of the virtual displacement (the constraints of the supports are in definition 5.61), or by assumption \(N(L)\,\delta u(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L)\), \(V(L)\,\delta v(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\), \(M(L)\,\delta v'(L) = M_0\,\delta v'(L)\). At the end \(x = 0\) it is the same, either zero or \(-N(0)\,\delta u(0) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(0)\) and so on. So \(G(L) - G(0)\) is the sum of the expressions in the square brackets of equation (5.66). At the clamped end the displacements are zero, and at the free end the internal forces equal the load.
  6. By linearity of the integral (lemma 3.54, item 1) and moving the terms \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u\) and \(q\,\delta v\) to the right-hand side, (5.66) follows.

ONATE2 has the deflection \(w\) and the loads positive in the direction of the axis \(z\) (our \(y\)), concentrated moments positive anticlockwise and \(M = EI\,w''\) (1.6b), which is our \(M = EI\,v''\) with the same sign. What did the proof show? Equation (5.66) is the equilibrium of the beam multiplied by the virtual displacement and integrated along the length. The derivative twice passed from the internal forces to the virtual displacement: \(M\,\delta v''\) stayed under the integral, and \(V\) was only an intermediate term that cancelled in step 3. The terms at the ends remained. Where a support forbids a displacement (like the clamp) they drop out, so the support reactions do not appear. Where the end is free (the right end) they are exactly the work of the prescribed forces and couples, as for the bar. Three things are worth remembering. First, the theorem holds for every material, because we used only equilibrium. Second, the internal force enters as a link between the two integrations by parts, and in the equation \(N\) is multiplied by \(\delta u'\) and \(M\) by \(\delta v''\). For a linearly elastic beam, by theorem 5.35, \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\), and the equation reads \(\int_0^L (EA\,u'\,\delta u' + EI\,v''\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \dots\), and in it only the displacements \(u\) and \(v\) appear with their derivatives. Third, the shear force does no internal virtual work. Why, shows the next lemma, which computes the internal virtual work from the principle of virtual displacements for a body.

Where did the assumptions enter the theorem, and what do they cost (figure 5.159)?

  1. A continuous load. Step 4 needs it, because the fundamental theorem of calculus requires a continuous \(G'\). At a point force inside the beam \(V\) jumps and \(G\) is not continuous, so the theorem in this form does not hold. The right-hand side then lacks the term \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}})\) (corollary 5.65). In the test with a force of 1000 N in the middle below this term is \(-1\) J, which is the whole right-hand side. In the figure this is the force at an interior point, where the shear force jumps, and with it the function \(G\).
  2. No distributed couple, \(m = 0\). It enters in step 3. With \(m \neq 0\), corollary 5.11 gives \(M' = -V - m\), a term \(-m\,\delta v'\) remains in \(G'\), and the right-hand side of the equation gains \(\int_0^L m\,\delta v'\,\mathrm{d}x\): a moment per unit length does work on the slope, like the couple in lemma 5.62. In the figure the moment per unit length is drawn as a row of small couples along the axis, which do work on the slope.
  3. The relations \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(V' = -q\), \(M' = -V\) are equilibrium written on the straight axis, as if the beam had not moved. This is justified as long as the slopes are small: for the cantilever of this chapter the slope of the free end is \(-0{,}01196\), and the cosine of this angle differs from 1 by \(7 \cdot 10^{-5}\). For large rotations the equilibrium must be written on the bent axis, and the theorem in this form does not hold. At a small slope the equilibria on the straight axis and on the bent axis are almost the same, the cosine of the angle is almost 1. The body is a beam whose cross section \(A\) is a rectangle or a figure made of rectangles. For a rectangle let \(-h/2 \le y \le h/2\), \(-b/2 \le z \le b/2\). The constraint is the same as for the true displacement (figure 5.56): the cross line stays straight and perpendicular to the axis, so the point at height \(y\) moves, for a slope \(\delta v'\) of the axis, back along the axis by \(y\,\delta v'\).

Lemma 5.64 Virtual work of a beam from the principle for a body (virtualno delo nosilca iz načela za telo)

Let the body be a beam with the axis \(x \in [0, L]\) and a cross section \(A\) that is a rectangle or a figure made of rectangles, with the axis through its centroid. Let the stress component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) be continuous on the body, and denote its integrals over the cross section by \(N(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M(x) = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (lemma 5.9 for a rectangle, corollary 5.19 for a figure made of rectangles). Let \((\delta u, \delta v)\) be a virtual displacement of the beam and \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta u - y\,\delta v'\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) the field that it determines (the displacement of the point at height \(y\)). Then:

  1. The virtual strains are \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \delta u' - y\,\delta v''\), all others are zero, in particular \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The term \(-y\,\delta v''\) comes from the displacement of the point at height \(y\), which changes along the axis with the curvature.
  2. The internal virtual work of the body (definition 4.80) is \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the work we have already estimated on the piece as \((N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\Delta x\).
  3. Let a force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t} = (\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}, \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) with components continuous on the cross section be prescribed on the cross section at \(x = L\), and let \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(M_0 = -\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\). Its virtual work on the cross section is \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L) + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) + M_0\,\delta v'(L)\). The work is done by the load on the cross section of the free end.
Proof

We must show three items. The first two are a computation with derivatives and the theorem on the order of integration, the third a computation with the cross section.

  1. By the definition of the virtual strain (definition 4.79) \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \delta u' - y\,\delta v''\), \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = 0\) and \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\).
  2. The shear components: \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\big) = \tfrac12\,(-\delta v' + \delta v') = 0\), \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x\big) = 0\) and \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\). This proves item 1. The shear strains are zero because the angle between the cross section and the axis stays a right angle.
  3. By item 1 the sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(\delta u' - y\,\delta v'')\).
  4. By theorem 4.9 on the order of integration, for a figure of rectangles on each box separately and then added, the integral over the body is \(\int_0^L \big(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,(\delta u' - y\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}A\big)\mathrm{d}x\). The functions \(\delta u'\) and \(\delta v''\) do not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), so we take them out, and the inner integral is \(\delta u'\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A - \delta v''\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\). This proves item 2. The inner integral is a sum over the heights \(y\) of the cross section, the outer one over the length of the axis.
  5. On the cross section \(x = L\) the virtual work is \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \big(\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\delta u(L) - y\,\delta v'(L)) + \bar t_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\big)\mathrm{d}A\). The values \(\delta u(L)\), \(\delta v'(L)\) and \(\delta v(L)\) are numbers, so this is \(\delta u(L)\,F_{\mathrm{x}} - \delta v'(L)\int_{\mathrm{A}} y\,\bar t_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A + \delta v(L)\,F_{\mathrm{y}} = F_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u(L) + F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) + M_0\,\delta v'(L)\). This proves item 3. The work is done by the load on the cross section on the displacements of the end.

Source: ONATE2 p. 36 (PDF), section 1.2.5 and block p36-b1: the integral of the moment and the virtual curvature. The proof is not from a source.

The lemma tells why the internal virtual work of a beam has the form \(N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\) and why the shear force does not appear in it. In the model the cross section only rotates by the slope of the axis, so the shear virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\,(-\delta v' + \delta v')\) is zero. We see both terms on the piece of figure 5.156: the first is the tilt of the cross section backward, the second the rise of the axis, and since they are equally large, the angle between the cross section and the axis stays a right angle. The shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), which \(V\) summarizes, therefore does no work, although \(V \neq 0\). This is the price of the model from section 5.3.4. How much does it cost? For the cantilever of this chapter (\(L/h = 25\)) shear adds 0,125 % of the bending part to the deflection of the free end, and the omitted term is negligible. For the same bar shortened to 80 mm it adds 19,5 %, and there the model is no longer good enough. Whoever needs the shear force in the virtual work must use a model that allows a shear strain \(\gamma\). We treat the model with shear strain later in this chapter, and in its internal virtual work a term \(\int V\,\delta\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x\) appears.

The connection with theorem 4.82 for a body is here only a remark that we do not use. Items 2 and 3 of the lemma compute the internal virtual work of the body and the work of the force on the end cross section for the field \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} = \delta u - y\,\delta v'\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\). To obtain equation (5.66) from theorem 4.82 with this, we would have to show three more things: that the beam is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) with stresses that satisfy the assumptions of the theorem (continuously differentiable, symmetric, in equilibrium with the body force), that the work of the surface forces on the lateral surface of the beam is \(\int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x\), which requires an integral of the force along the perimeter of the cross section, and that the virtual displacement is zero on the part of the surface where the supports are (for a clamp at \(x = 0\) that is the whole cross section). The work of the body force of the weight would be \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_0^L (-\rho g A)\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x\), because \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} = \delta v\) does not depend on \(y\) and \(z\), so the self weight would enter as the load \(q = -\rho g A\). We do not prove this here, we proved the equation directly with theorem 5.63. Such a cross section is the clamp, where the displacement is zero.

Test on the cantilever.

Let us test equation (5.66) on the cantilever of section 5.1.3: an axis of length \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), a force of 500 N downward at the free end, so \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = M_0 = 0\). By the convention of the chapter \(N = 0\), \(V = -500\) N and \(M(x) = -500\,(1\ \text{m} - x)\) N, \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = q = 0\) (we neglect the self weight). The equation reads \(\int_0^L M\,\delta v''\,\mathrm{d}x = F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L)\). Take two virtual displacements across the axis (figure 5.160), both with zero displacement and slope at the clamp, and numbers \(c\) for which the free end rises by 1 mm: \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\) for the first displacement and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}^2\) for the second. At the top are drawn the first displacement and the second displacement above the axis of the beam, and in the middle, on the axes, the moment \(M\), which is \(-500\) N m at the clamp and zero at the free end, and the virtual curvatures. At the bottom, on two pairs of axes, are the products \(M\,\delta v''\), whose areas are the left-hand sides of the equation.

First virtual displacement, \(\delta v = c\,x^2\). The virtual curvature of the displacement is \(\delta v'' = 2c\) everywhere.

  1. Internal virtual work: \(\int_0^L (-500\,(L - x))\,2c\,\mathrm{d}x = -1000\,c\,\big(L^2 - L^2/2\big) = -500\,c\,L^2\), for \(L = 1\) m and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}\) that is \(-0{,}5\) J. This is the area of the triangle with the peak \(-1\) J/m at the clamp.
  2. External virtual work: \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = (-500)\,(c\,L^2) = -500\,c\,L^2\), that is \(-0{,}5\) J, the force of 500 N downward on the rise of 1 mm.

Second virtual displacement, \(\delta v = c\,x^3\). The virtual curvature of the displacement is \(\delta v'' = 6c\,x\), zero at \(x = 0\) and largest at the free end.

  1. Internal virtual work: \(\int_0^L (-500\,(L - x))\,6c\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = -3000\,c\,\big(L^3/2 - L^3/3\big) = -500\,c\,L^3\), for \(L = 1\) m and \(c = 10^{-3}\ 1/\text{m}^2\) that is \(-0{,}5\) J. This is the area of the arc with the bottom \(-\tfrac34\) J/m in the middle.
  2. External virtual work: \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = -500\,c\,L^3\), that is \(-0{,}5\) J. This is the force on the displacement of the free end.

Both equations hold. Why are the numbers the same for both displacements? Because both displacements have the same value 1 mm at the free end, and only this value appears on the right-hand side. The left-hand side is by the principle equal, whatever the shape of the displacement. The weight with which the curvature counts is the moment, which is largest at the clamp and zero at the free end. The displacement \(c\,x^3\) has its curvature largest at the free end, where the moment is zero, and zero at the clamp, where the moment is largest. So its curvature in the middle must be larger than the constant curvature of the first displacement, and the arc reaches deeper in the middle than the triangle at the same place, \(-\tfrac34\) J/m against \(-\tfrac12\) J/m, while the areas are equal.

For the true displacement, \(\delta v = v\) with \(v(x) = -F\,x^2\,(3L - x)/(6EI)\) and \(F = 500\) N (\(EI\) from section 5.1.3), the internal virtual work is \(\int_0^L M\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x = F^2 L^3/(3EI) = 3{,}986\) J, and the external is \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,v(L) = (-500)\,(-7{,}972\ \text{mm}) = 3{,}986\) J, the force on the drop of the free end of figure 5.140. Since \(v'' = M/(EI)\), this is \(\int_0^L M^2/(EI)\,\mathrm{d}x\). In section 5.12.1 we shall see that this is twice the strain energy, \(2U = 2 \cdot 1{,}993\) J, as for the bar.

The principle notices a mistake. If we wrongly thought that the moment is \(M = -F\,L = -500\) N m along the whole length (the moment at the clamp), then for \(\delta v = c\,x^2\) we would obtain the internal work \(-1000\,c\,L^2\) and the external \(-500\,c\,L^2\). The equation does not hold, and by theorem 5.63 such a moment is not in equilibrium.

What about a virtual displacement that is not admissible? Take \(\delta v = c\,x\), which has slope \(c\) at the clamp. The left side is zero, because \(\delta v'' = 0\), and the right side is \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta v(L) = -500\,c\,L\). The equation does not hold. The function \(c\,x\) is not a virtual displacement of a beam, because it violates \(\delta v'(0) = 0\), so the theorem does not cover it. What is the reason that the equation really fails? The clamp acts on the beam with the force \(-V(0) = 500\) N upward and with the couple \(-M(0) = 500\) N m. For an admissible virtual displacement these reactions do no work, because \(\delta v(0) = \delta v'(0) = 0\). For the displacement \(\delta v = c\,x\) we have \(\delta v(0) = 0\), so the force still does no work, but the couple does, on the slope \(c\), the work \(-M(0)\,\delta v'(0) = 500\,c\) N m by lemma 5.62. If we add it to the right-hand side, we obtain for \(L = 1\) m \(-500\,c + 500\,c = 0\), which is the left side. The reaction of the clamp therefore does work if we allow rotation at the clamp, and that is why we forbade it in definition 5.61.

Corollary 5.65 Point force and couple inside a beam (točkovna sila in dvojica v notranjosti nosilca)

Let \(0 = a_0 < a_1 < \dots < a_{\mathrm{n}} = L\) be points that divide the beam into the parts \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\), \(k = 1, \dots, n\). On each closed interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) let \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) be continuous and \(N\) and \(M\) continuously differentiable with one-sided derivatives at the ends (the values of \(V\) and \(M\) at the ends are the one-sided limits). Let \(N\) be continuous on \([0, L]\) (there are no axial point forces). On each open interval \((a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}})\) let the differential equations of theorem 5.63 hold, and at the ends \(0\) and \(L\) its conditions on supports and loads. At an interior point \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) let a point force \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(M_{\mathrm{k}}\) act, and let \(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) = -F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) = -M_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^\pm)\) and \(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^\pm)\) denote one-sided limits. Then for every virtual displacement of the beam equation (5.66) holds with the added terms \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} F_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}})\) on the right.

Proof

We must show that the sum over the parts from the theorem differs by the terms at the points \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\). It suffices to look at one interior term.

  1. On each part \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) (the left and the right one in the figure) steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 5.63 hold with the interval \([a_{\mathrm{k}-1}, a_{\mathrm{k}}]\) instead of \([0, L]\) and with the function \(G\) on that part: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{a_{\mathrm{k}}} (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'' - q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u - q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}-1}^+)\).
  2. By additivity of the integral (lemma 3.54, item 2) the integral over \([0, L]\) is the sum over the parts, in figure 5.162 the sum over the left and the right part. The sum of the right-hand sides telescopes: \(G(L) - G(0) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{n-1} \big(G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+)\big)\).
  3. At an interior point \(a_{\mathrm{k}}\) the function \(N\) is continuous (there are no axial point forces), and \(V\) jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(M\) by \(-M_{\mathrm{k}}\), as assumed. The virtual displacement \(\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}})\) and the slope \(\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}})\) are the same on both sides of the point, because \(\delta v\) is continuously differentiable. Therefore \(G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-) - G(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) = -\big(V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - V(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-)\big)\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) - \big(M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^+) - M(a_{\mathrm{k}}^-)\big)\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}}) = F_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v(a_{\mathrm{k}}) + M_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta v'(a_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  4. We treat the terms \(G(L) - G(0)\) at the ends of the beam as in step 5 of the proof of the theorem, and the equation follows.

5.10.3 Equilibrium from virtual work (ravnovesje iz virtualnega dela)

Theorem 5.63 says: if the beam is in equilibrium, the equation holds for every virtual displacement. Does the converse hold? Let \(N\) be the axial force and \(M\) the bending moment of some beam and let equation (5.66) hold for all virtual displacements. Are \(N\) and \(M\) then in equilibrium, that is, do \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), \(M'' = q\) and the conditions at the ends hold? For the bar we obtained the answer with the fundamental lemma (theorem 4.78). Here we do the same, only that we have two displacement functions, and therefore two parts of the equation in the interior and three conditions at each end, for \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\). We prove it for arbitrary supports at both ends: at each end the support forbids an arbitrary set of the components \(\delta u\), \(\delta v\), \(\delta v'\), and the clamp, the pin support, the roller support and the free end are only special cases.

Let us draw what we will work with (figure 5.164). Top left is the axis of the beam from the end \(0\) to the end \(L\). At the right end act a force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}^L\) along the axis, a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^L\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(C^L\), at the left end the same three kinds of load with the mark \(0\). Below the ends are the outward normals: the right one points in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\), the left one against it, \(n_0 = -1\). A tensile axial force pulls the right end to the right and the left end to the left, so the conditions at the ends will contain the product \(n_\xi\,N(\xi)\). Top right we enlarge the end \(x = L\). Before the virtual displacement we draw it grey dashed, the axis and the cross section, after it orange. The end moves along the axis by \(\delta u(L)\) and across the axis by \(\delta v(L)\), and the cross section turns by \(\delta v'(L)\). These are the three components that a support can forbid: a clamp all three, a pin support \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\), a roller support only \(\delta v\), a free end none.

The idea: we do the integration by parts in the reverse direction. We obtain an integral in which \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) are multiplied by functions that should be zero (one of them, the function \(g\), is drawn in the middle row of the figure), and terms at the ends. With virtual displacements that are zero at the ends, such as \(\psi\) and the bump \(\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\), we show that the functions are zero. Then we choose virtual displacements that are different from zero only near one end: a bump multiplied by a polynomial of degree at most one, so that one single free component remains in the equation. For \(\delta v\) we must use a bump with more derivatives, because a virtual displacement of a beam must be twice continuously differentiable. We made such bumps in lemma 4.123, item 1.

Theorem 5.66 Equilibrium of a beam from virtual displacements (ravnovesje nosilca iz virtualnih pomikov)

Let a beam lie along the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\). At each end \(\xi \in \{0, L\}\) let the support forbid an arbitrary set of the components \(\delta u(\xi)\), \(\delta v(\xi)\), \(\delta v'(\xi)\) (none, some or all three), and let a virtual displacement of the beam be a pair \((\delta u, \delta v)\) from definition 5.61 that has the forbidden components equal to zero. At the end \(\xi\) let a force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\) along the axis, a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(C^\xi\) (component \(z\)) act. Let \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\) and \(n_0 = -1\), the component of the outward normal of the end along the axis \(x\). Let the load \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) be continuous on \([0, L]\). Let the functions \(N\) and \(M\) be continuous on \([0, L]\) and differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, L)\), and let there exist continuous functions \(p\), \(r\), \(s\) on \([0, L]\) with \(N' = p\), \(M' = r\) and \(r' = s\) on \((0, L)\). Let \(V = -r\). If the equation

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L (q_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta u + q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + C^\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.67}\]

holds for every virtual displacement of the beam, then

\[N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0 , \qquad V' + q = 0 , \qquad M' + V = 0 \quad \text{on } (0, L) ,\]

and for every end \(\xi\): if the support does not forbid the component \(\delta u(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,N(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\), if it does not forbid \(\delta v(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\), and if it does not forbid \(\delta v'(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\).

Proof

We do the integration by parts in the reverse direction. From the fundamental lemma we obtain the equations in the interior (middle row of the figure), and with bumps multiplied by polynomials each condition at an end separately (bottom row).

  1. Let \(G = N\,\delta u + V\,\delta v + M\,\delta v'\) on \([0, L]\) for a virtual displacement of the beam. \(G(x)\) is the work that the forces \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) at the cross section at \(x\) would do on the displacement of this cross section: each force on its own component, at \(x = L\) these are the components \(\delta u(L)\), \(\delta v(L)\) and \(\delta v'(L)\). As in step 1 of the proof of theorem 5.63, \(G\) is continuous on \([0, L]\).
  2. For \(0 < x < L\), by the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3), with \(N' = p\), \(V' = -r' = -s\) and \(M' = r = -V\),
    \[G' = p\,\delta u + N\,\delta u' - s\,\delta v + V\,\delta v' + M'\,\delta v' + M\,\delta v'' .\]
    The terms \(V\,\delta v'\) and \(M'\,\delta v' = -V\,\delta v'\) cancel, so \(G' = p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v''\), and this is a continuous function on \([0, L]\).
  3. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.55, item 1, \(\int_0^L (p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v + N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0) = \sum_{\xi} n_\xi\,G(\xi)\), where \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\) and \(n_0 = -1\) give the signs of the two ends.
  4. From step 3 we express \(\int_0^L (N\,\delta u' + M\,\delta v'')\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_\xi n_\xi\,G(\xi) - \int_0^L (p\,\delta u - s\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and insert it in the equation of the theorem. After moving the terms, with \(A_\xi = n_\xi\,N(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\), \(B_\xi = n_\xi\,V(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) and \(D_\xi = n_\xi\,M(\xi) - C^\xi\),
    \[\int_0^L \big((p + q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\delta u + (q - s)\,\delta v\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(A_\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + B_\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + D_\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\Big) . \quad (*)\]
    The number \(A_\xi\) is the difference between the axial force at the end and the force \(F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\) acting there, \(B_\xi\) and \(D_\xi\) are the same differences for the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) and the couple \(C^\xi\). The conditions of the theorem at the ends say that these differences are zero.
  5. Let \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta u = \psi\), where \(\psi\) is differentiable on an open interval containing \([0, L]\), with a continuous derivative and \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\). This is a virtual displacement for every set of supports, because all three components at \(\xi\) are zero or forbidden. The right side of equation \((*)\) is zero, and \(\int_0^L (p + q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). Every function \(\psi\) that is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and zero at the endpoints can be extended beyond the ends by its tangent lines, \(\psi(0) + \psi'(0)\,x\) for \(x < 0\) and \(\psi(L) + \psi'(L)\,(x - L)\) for \(x > L\), which gives a function of this kind, and the integral over \([0, L]\) does not change, so \(\int_0^L (p + q_{\mathrm{x}})\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every \(\psi\) that the fundamental lemma requires. The function \(p + q_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous, so by the fundamental lemma 4.77 it is zero on \([0, L]\), and \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = p + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) on \((0, L)\).
  6. Let \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = \varphi_{\alpha\beta}\) for numbers \(0 < \alpha < \beta < L\) on the axis \(x\), where \(\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\) is the bump from lemma 4.123 (called \(q_{\alpha\beta}\) there, \(\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\) here so that it is not confused with the load \(q\)). By item 1 of the lemma it has continuous first three derivatives and is zero with all derivatives at the ends of the interval \([0, L]\), so this is a virtual displacement of the beam for every set of supports, the right side of \((*)\) is zero, and \(\int_\alpha^\beta (q - s)\,\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) for every such pair.
  7. Let \(g = q - s\) be continuous on \([0, L]\), and suppose that \(g(c_0) \neq 0\) for some \(c_0 \in (0, L)\). If \(g(c_0) < 0\) we take \(-g\) (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 4.77). Continuity gives an interval \([\alpha, \beta] \subset (0, L)\) around \(c_0\) on which \(g > g(c_0)/2\) (step 2 of the same proof). The function \(\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\) is nowhere negative, so \(\int_\alpha^\beta g\,\varphi_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac{g(c_0)}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta \varphi_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x \ge \tfrac{g(c_0)}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\ell}{2}\,(\ell/4)^8 > 0\) for \(\ell = \beta - \alpha\) (item 3 of lemma 4.123, step 2). This contradicts step 6. So \(g = 0\) on \((0, L)\), and \(M'' = s = q\), hence \(V' = -s = -q\), which is \(V' + q = 0\). From \(V = -r = -M'\) we have \(M' + V = 0\).
  8. The functions \(p + q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(g = q - s\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and zero on \((0, L)\), so by continuity they are zero also at the endpoints. The left integral in \((*)\) is therefore zero for every virtual displacement, and only the terms at the ends remain, \(\sum_\xi \big(A_\xi\,\delta u(\xi) + B_\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + D_\xi\,\delta v'(\xi)\big) = 0\).
  9. Let \(\xi\) be one of the ends. For \(\xi = L\) let \(\chi = \varphi_{\mathrm{L}/2,\,2\mathrm{L}}\), for \(\xi = 0\) let \(\chi = \varphi_{-\mathrm{L},\,\mathrm{L}/2}\), bumps from lemma 4.123. By item 1 of the lemma \(\chi\) has continuous first three derivatives on the whole line. Let \(k = \chi(\xi) > 0\) (each of the two factors is positive at \(\xi\)) and \(m = \chi'(\xi)\), the slope of the tangent. The function \(\chi\) is zero on an open interval around the other end (\(x < L/2\) for \(\xi = L\), \(x > L/2\) for \(\xi = 0\)), so its derivatives are zero there too, and every displacement below has all three components zero at the other end. Below, every \(\delta u\) is equal to \(\chi\) or zero, and every \(\delta v\) is \(\chi\) multiplied by a polynomial of degree at most one. Such a \(\delta u\) has a continuous first derivative and such a \(\delta v\) continuous first three derivatives (product rule, lemma 1.82, item 3, and corollary 1.65), so it satisfies the requirements of definition 5.61 if the forbidden components are zero at \(\xi\). What the bump does beyond the end \(L\) does not concern the beam, because there is no beam there.
  10. Let \(\delta u(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = \chi\) and \(\delta v = 0\). At \(\xi\) we have \(\delta u(\xi) = k\) and \(\delta v(\xi) = \delta v'(\xi) = 0\), so this is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(A_\xi\,k = 0\), so \(A_\xi = 0\) and \(n_\xi\,N(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{x}}^\xi\).
  11. Let \(\delta v'(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = \chi(x)\,(x - \xi)\), magnified nine times in the figure. By the product rule \(\delta v'(x) = \chi'(x)\,(x - \xi) + \chi(x)\), so at \(\xi\) we have \(\delta v(\xi) = 0\) and \(\delta v'(\xi) = k\). This is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(D_\xi\,k = 0\), so \(D_\xi = 0\) and \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\).
  12. Let \(\delta v(\xi)\) not be forbidden. Take \(\delta u = 0\) and \(\delta v = \chi(x)\,\big(1 - (m/k)\,(x - \xi)\big)/k\). At \(\xi\) we have \(\delta v(\xi) = 1\) and by the product rule \(\delta v'(\xi) = \big(m - (m/k)\,k\big)/k = 0\), the tangent is horizontal. This is a virtual displacement, and step 8 gives \(B_\xi = 0\), so \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\).

Source: EP example 4.2.1 (pp. 170-171), more precisely the natural boundary conditions \(N - P = 0\) and \(M + M_0 = 0\) at \(x = L\) (with EP's sign of the moment), EP §3.5.7 (pp. 146-147). The proof for arbitrary supports, with test displacements that are bumps multiplied by a polynomial, is not from a source.

Where did we need the loads \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(q\) to be continuous and \(N\) and \(M\) to have the continuous derivatives \(p\), \(r\), \(s\)? In step 2, where without derivatives we could not differentiate the product, in step 3, where the fundamental theorem of calculus needs a continuous \(G'\), so continuous \(p\) and \(s\), in step 5, where the fundamental lemma needs a continuous function \(p + q_{\mathrm{x}}\), and in step 7, where only the continuity of the function \(g\) gives the band \([\alpha, \beta]\) around \(c_0\) on which \(g\) is greater than \(g(c_0)/2\). For self-weight and other loads that vary continuously along the axis the assumption holds. It does not hold for a point force or couple inside the beam. For a simply supported beam with a force of \(1\) kN at mid-span the shear force jumps there from \(-500\) N to \(+500\) N (lemma 5.12), so \(M\) has no derivative at this point and the equations \(V' + q = 0\) and \(M' + V = 0\) have no meaning there. The theorem does not cover such a beam. The equations hold on the pieces between the forces (corollary 5.11), and at a force the jump takes their place.

Theorems 5.63 and 5.66 together say that for any supports equation (5.66) for all virtual displacements is equivalent to equilibrium (the proof of theorem 5.63 holds unchanged for an arbitrary set of forbidden components at each end). For a linearly elastic prismatic beam \(N = EA\,u'\) and \(M = EI\,v''\) (theorem 5.35), and from the theorem we obtain the equations \(EA\,u'' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(EI\,v'''' = q\) from theorem 5.42, together with the conditions \(EA\,u'(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-EI\,v'''(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(EI\,v''(L) = M_0\) at the free end \(x = L\), where the forces and the couple act. The last two are exactly the boundary conditions of a free end from section 5.7.2, and the first is the condition of the bar, \(N(L) = F_{\mathrm{x}}\) (theorem 4.78). For our cantilever \(v'' = -F\,(L - x)/(EI)\), so \(EI\,v'' = M = -F\,(L - x)\), \(-EI\,v''' = -F = F_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(EI\,v''(L) = 0 = M_0\), and all three conditions indeed hold. At the clamp at \(x = 0\) all three components are forbidden and the theorem demands nothing, at a pin support \(\delta u\) and \(\delta v\) are forbidden, and the theorem demands only \(-M(0) = C^0\), so \(M(0) = 0\) if there is no couple.

Why are exactly these three conditions what the equation "gives by itself"? Because in the terms at the ends stand pairs in which one quantity is a displacement and the other a force or moment that does work on it: \((u, N)\), \((v, V)\) and \((v', M)\), in the figure each component of displacement next to the load that does work on it. At an end one component of each pair is prescribed. If the support prescribes the displacement, the virtual displacement is zero there and the corresponding force is not in the equation. If it does not, the virtual displacement is free, and the equation demands that the force equals the prescribed load. The displacements \(u\), \(v\) and \(v'\) are essential or geometric boundary conditions (bistveni ali geometrijski robni pogoji), the forces \(N\), \(V\) and \(M\) natural or force boundary conditions (naravni ali silni robni pogoji), as for the bar (EP §3.5.7, pp. 146-147, and example 4.2.1 on p. 171, where \(u\), \(w\) and \(-\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}x\) are the basic variables). The slope \(v'\) and the moment \(M\) are a new pair that the bar did not have.
