Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.8 The Ritz method for natural frequencies (Ritzova metoda za lastne frekvence)

Bar C (palica C) of section 10.7 has 16 coordinates with 16 elements, but we are interested mainly in its lowest natural frequency. The same holds for a building with many storeys (etaže): its motion is often almost only a few simple shapes, while there are as many coordinates as storeys. Can we keep only a few shapes of the motion of a large system and still get its lowest frequencies? And on which side of the true value will we land?

The model is the system of section 10.2 without damping and without external force. The matrix \(\mat K\) of order \(n\) is symmetric, the mass matrix \(\mat M\) of order \(n\) is symmetric and positive definite (definition 6.27). The eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) are \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and its eigencolumns, the modes (nihajni načini), are \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) equal to 1 for \(r = s\) and 0 otherwise (theorem 10.13). The Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik) \(R(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) of definition 10.25 is the number we get from a column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) numbers.

The idea is the same as in section 6.4 of chapter 6. There, instead of all fields we tried only a family of fields with a few free numbers and looked for the best field in it. Now the family is the set of columns \(\vek v = c_1\vek\psi_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{m}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) with given columns \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), which are the numbers of the trial field in the coordinates of the system, just as the \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) were functions. Instead of the lowest point of the energy we look for the points where the Rayleigh quotient is stationary, since theorem 10.27 says that the quotient is stationary exactly at the modes and that an error of order \(\varepsilon\) in the shape gives an error of order \(\varepsilon^2\) in the quotient. First a single column on frame A (okvir A) of section 10.1, with \(\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\mat M = \bar m\,\mat I\), \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(\bar m = 500\) kg.

Take the column \(\vek\psi = (1,\ 1{,}5)^\T\), the static shape of the frame under the weights of the storeys from section 10.6. Every \(\vek v = c\,\vek\psi\) with \(c \ne 0\) gives the same quotient, because \(c^2\) cancels. We compute \(\vek\psi^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi = k\,(2 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1{,}5 + 1{,}5^2) = 1{,}25\,k\) and \(\vek\psi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = \bar m\,(1 + 1{,}5^2) = 3{,}25\,\bar m\) (the floor mass is written \(\bar m\) here because \(m\) is the number of columns in this section). The quotient is \(\tilde\lambda = 1{,}25\,k/(3{,}25\,\bar m) = 385{,}97\ \text{1/s}^2\), the circular frequency is \(19{,}6461\) 1/s, the true one is \(\omega_1 = 19{,}5783\) 1/s. The error of the frequency is \(+0{,}346\) % and the error of \(\omega^2\) is \(+0{,}694\) %, because a relative error roughly doubles when squared.

With several columns the computation turns into a small eigenproblem. We write the columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) as the columns of a matrix \(\mat\Psi\) with \(n\) rows and \(m\) columns, so that \(\vek v = \mat\Psi\,\vek a\) for a column \(\vek a\) with the \(m\) numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(\vek v = \mat\Psi\,\vek a\) we have \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \vek a^\T(\mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi)\,\vek a\) and similarly for \(\mat M\), so the quotient \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a)\) is nothing other than the Rayleigh quotient of a pair of matrices of order \(m\), which we now name.

Lemma 10.40 Reduced pair of matrices (reducirani par matrik)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as above, \(\mat\Psi\) a matrix with \(n\) rows and \(m \le n\) columns whose columns are linearly independent (definition 6.22), and \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi\), \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi\) matrices of order \(m\). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is symmetric, and the matrix \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric and positive definite.
  2. There exist real numbers \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) and columns \(\vek a_1, \dots, \vek a_{\mathrm{m}}\) with \(m\) numbers such that \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) is equal to 1 for \(r = s\) and 0 otherwise. The columns \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) have \(n\) numbers and satisfy \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat K\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. For \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) we have \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a) = \vek a^\T\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a/\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\), which is the Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\). Its partial derivatives with respect to the components of \(\vek a\) are zero exactly at the eigencolumns of this pair, and the value of the quotient there is the corresponding eigenvalue.
Proof

We must show that the reduced pair satisfies the conditions of theorem 10.13, and then read points 2 and 3 from the theorem and from the definition of the quotient.

  1. By lemma 1.101, \(\tilde{\mat K}^\T = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K^\T\mat\Psi = \tilde{\mat K}\), because \(\mat K\) is symmetric. In the same way \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric.
  2. Let \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\). Since the columns of \(\mat\Psi\) are independent, \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a \ne \vek 0\) (definition 6.22). Hence \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a = (\mat\Psi\,\vek a)^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a) > 0\), because \(\mat M\) is positive definite. This is point 1.
  3. By steps 1 and 2, theorem 10.13 applied to the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\) of order \(m\) gives the numbers \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the columns \(\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\). The products in point 2 are only the opening of brackets: \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T(\mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\), and \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) gives the second.
  4. Point 3: by step 3 the numerator and denominator of the quotient \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a)\) are \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a\) and \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\). The partial derivatives are zero exactly at the eigencolumns by theorem 10.27 for the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\), whose conditions are guaranteed by steps 1 and 2.

Source: CK (10.58) to (10.63) (PDF p. 318): »Ritz proposed that the coefficients ... be chosen to make R(v) stationary«. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says that Ritz's problem is indeed an eigenproblem of the same kind as the original, only smaller. Let us name it.

Definition 10.41 Ritz approximation of eigenvalues (Ritzov približek lastnih vrednosti)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as in definition 10.7, \(\mat\Psi\) a matrix with \(n\) rows and \(m\) linearly independent columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(1 \le m \le n\), and \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi\) matrices of order \(m\). The reduced eigenproblem (reducirani lastni problem) is \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) for a column \(\vek a\) with \(m\) numbers. Its eigenvalues \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) are the Ritz values (Ritzove vrednosti), and the columns \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(n\) numbers are the Ritz shapes (Ritzove oblike). Together they are the Ritz approximation (Ritzov približek) of the first \(m\) eigenvalues and shapes of the system. For \(m = 1\) the Ritz value is the Rayleigh quotient \(R(\vek\psi_1)\).

Source: CK (10.59) to (10.63) (PDF p. 318), which writes the matrices with a hat. The notation is ours.

Frame A with two columns shows what happens when \(m = n\). Let \(\mat\Psi\) have \(\vek\psi_1 = (1,\ 1{,}5)^\T\) and \(\vek\psi_2 = (0,\ 1)^\T\). We get \(\tilde{\mat K} = k\begin{pmatrix} 1{,}25 & 0{,}5 \\ 0{,}5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \bar m\begin{pmatrix} 3{,}25 & 1{,}5 \\ 1{,}5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) (SymPy), and the reduced problem has \(\tilde\lambda = 383{,}31\) and \(2627{,}25\) 1/s\(^2\), exactly the eigenvalues of the frame. The reason is that two independent columns in a space with two coordinates form an invertible matrix, so we have only written the system in other coordinates (the proof is point 2 of the theorem below). The real case is \(m < n\), where we have left shapes out. The Rayleigh quotient of section 10.6 always lay above \(\lambda_1\) for frame A. Does this also hold for the other Ritz values and for every choice of \(\mat\Psi\)?

Theorem 10.42 Ritz values are upper bounds (Ritzove vrednosti so zgornje meje)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as at the beginning of the section, and \(\mat\Psi\) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in definition 10.41 with \(m \le n\). Then:

  1. \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r = 1, \dots, m\).
  2. If \(m = n\), then \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\) and \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigencolumn of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
Proof

For point 1 we take the first \(r\) Ritz shapes. We must show that the set of their combinations has nowhere a quotient larger than \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), but that every such set contains a column where the quotient is not smaller than \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is point 2 of theorem 10.28 for the columns \(\vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{r}}\), written out here to the end.

  1. Let \(r \le m\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{j}}\). By lemma 10.40, point 2, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\) is equal to 1 for \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise, and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat K\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. The columns \(\vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{r}}\) are independent. If \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\), we multiply by \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\) and by step 1 we get \(t_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  3. Let \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\). By step 1, \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). Since \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(j \le r\), we have \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), so \(R(\vek v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every such combination.
  4. The conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, r - 1\) are \(r - 1\) homogeneous linear equations for the \(r\) numbers \(t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}\) (for \(r = 1\) there are none). By lemma 10.9 they have a solution \((t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}) \ne \vek 0\). The corresponding \(\vek v\) is not zero, because by step 2 the columns are independent, and it is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\).
  5. By the expansion theorem (corollary 10.14), \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\), and by step 4, \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(s < r\). By equation (10.31), \(R(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s \ge r\), \(R(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  6. The column from step 4 is a combination from step 3, so for the same \(\vek v\) we have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le R(\vek v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is point 1.
  7. For point 2, \(\mat\Psi\) is square with independent columns. From \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\) it follows that \(\vek a = \vek 0\), so \(\mat\Psi\) and also \(\mat\Psi^\T\) have an inverse matrix (lemma 8.21).
  8. The pair \((\tilde\lambda, \vek a)\) solves \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) exactly when \(\mat\Psi^\T(\mat K - \tilde\lambda\mat M)\,\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\), and by step 7 this holds exactly when \((\mat K - \tilde\lambda\mat M)\,\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\). The eigenpairs of the reduced problem and of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) therefore agree through \(\vek a \mapsto \mat\Psi\,\vek a\). This map takes independent columns to independent columns (step 7), so the two eigenspaces of the same eigenvalue have the same dimension in both problems. That dimension is the number of repetitions of the eigenvalue, since by corollary 10.14 (point 2) an eigencolumn is a sum of modes with the same eigenvalue, and these are independent. The ordered lists are therefore equal.

Source: CK, text after (10.71) (PDF p. 319): »each of the \hat N < N eigenvalues produced by a Rayleigh-Ritz approximation to an N-DOF system is an upper bound to the corresponding exact eigenvalue«. CK proves it with the eigenvalue separation theorem (10.69) to (10.71), GR with a conclusion about constraints (2.239), (2.240) (PDF p. 136). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof has shown two things. The Ritz value \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the largest quotient on the combinations of the first \(r\) Ritz shapes, and every set of combinations of \(r\) independent columns contains a column on which the quotient is not smaller than \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). So the Ritz method does not underestimate eigenvalues. This is similar to chapter 6, where the energy of the Ritz field was above the true one (theorem 6.34): a family of fields is an additional restriction, and a restriction does not lower the eigenvalues (corollary 10.29).

Let us test the theorem on a system where \(m < n\) in the true sense, on a frame with \(n = 3\) storeys, each with mass \(\bar m = 500\) kg and stiffness \(k\) (section 10.3). The eigenvalues are \(\lambda/(k/\bar m) = 0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\), the frequencies \(2{,}2438\), \(6{,}2870\) and \(9{,}0850\) Hz. For the columns we take two simple shapes, a displacement of all storeys by the same amount and a displacement that grows with the storey: \(\vek\psi_1 = (1,\ 1,\ 1)^\T\) and \(\vek\psi_2 = (0,\ 1,\ 2)^\T\). The storey drift (etažni zamik, section 10.2.3) is the displacement of a ceiling relative to the ceiling below it (for the first storey relative to the ground). For \(\vek\psi_1\) the first storey has drift 1 and the second and third have 0. For \(\vek\psi_2\) the first storey has drift 0 and the second and third have 1 each. The energy of a storey is \(\tfrac12\,k\) times the square of its drift, so \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_1 = k\), \(\vek\psi_2^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_2 = 2k\) and \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_2 = k\,(1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1) = 0\). The masses are \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_1 = 3\bar m\), \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_2 = (0 + 1 + 2)\,\bar m = 3\bar m\) and \(\vek\psi_2^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_2 = (0 + 1 + 4)\,\bar m = 5\bar m\). The reduced problem with \(x = \tilde\lambda\,\bar m/k\) has the determinant

\[\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 - 3x & -3x \\ -3x & 2 - 5x \end{pmatrix} = (1 - 3x)(2 - 5x) - 9x^2 = 6x^2 - 11x + 2 = 0 ,\]

with solutions \(x = (11 \mp \sqrt{73})/12 = 0{,}204666\) and \(1{,}628667\). The Ritz values are \(\tilde\lambda_1/(k/\bar m) = 0{,}204666\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2/(k/\bar m) = 1{,}628667\), the frequencies \(2{,}2809\) and \(6{,}4343\) Hz. The error of the frequencies is \(+1{,}65\) % and \(+2{,}34\) %, and the errors of the eigenvalues are \(+3{,}33\) % and \(+4{,}74\) %. Both values are above the true ones, \(0{,}204666 \ge 0{,}198062\) and \(1{,}628667 \ge 1{,}554958\), as the theorem predicts. This approximation does not give the third value, because the reduced problem has only order \(m = 2\). The plane of two columns is a single constraint on three coordinates: the cross product \(\vek\psi_1 \times \vek\psi_2 = (1,\ -2,\ 1)^\T =: \vek b\) is perpendicular to both columns, so the plane is the set of columns with \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\). In the notation of corollary 10.29, \(p = 1\) and \(\mat\Psi = \mat G\,\mat T\), where \(\mat T\) is the matrix with the rows of \(\mat\Psi\) with index \(i \ne p\). The matrix \(\mat T\) is invertible, because \(\mat T\vek a = \vek 0\) implies \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \mat G\,\mat T\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\vek a = \vek 0\) (lemma 8.21). The reduced pair is then \((\mat T^\T\mat K'\mat T,\ \mat T^\T\mat M'\mat T)\). By point 2 of theorem 10.42, applied to the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\) of order \(n - 1\) with the matrix \(\mat T\) in place of \(\mat\Psi\), it has the same eigenvalues as \((\mat K', \mat M')\), so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\). The corollary thus also gives an upper bound, \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2 \le \lambda_3\), and indeed \(0{,}204666 \le 1{,}554958\) and \(1{,}628667 \le 3{,}246980\).

Figure 10.25 shows step 4 of the proof for \(r = 2\) and \(n = 3\). In the mode coordinates, \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) (corollary 10.14), we have \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = \sum\eta_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \sum\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\eta_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). The plane of two columns passes through the origin and therefore meets the plane \(\eta_1 = 0\) at least in a line: the two equations \(\eta_1 = 0\) and the equation of the plane have, for three unknowns, a nontrivial solution by lemma 10.9. On this line, in the direction \((0,\ -0{,}978,\ -0{,}208)^\T\) (NumPy), \(R = 1{,}6285\,k/\bar m\), above \(\lambda_2\), because a mixture of \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) cannot fall below the smaller of the two. The largest quotient in the plane is therefore at least as large.

The theorem also says what it does not say. It does not say how large the error is: it can be \(+3\) %, as above, or much more if we choose bad columns. And it says nothing about the shapes: the Ritz shape \(\vek v_1\) is \(2{,}8°\) from the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\), which by theorem 10.27 gives an error of the quotient of the order of the square of the angle. The theorem also does not say how the error changes with the ordinal number \(r\). This is shown by finite element meshes.

Bar C, clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L = 1\) m, consists of \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) equal elements of length \(h = L/n_{\mathrm{e}}\) made of steel with wave speed \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s (section 10.7). With the consistent mass matrix (lemma 10.31) the lowest frequencies, the true one being \(f_1 = 1253{,}20\) Hz, are:

\(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) 1 2 4 8 16 32
\(f_1\) consistent (Hz) 1381,85 1285,61 1261,27 1255,22 1253,70 1253,33
\(f_1\) lumped (Hz) 1128,28 1221,24 1245,16 1251,19 1252,70 1253,08

The consistent values fall with every doubling of \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) and stay above the true one, the lumped ones (definition 10.32) rise and are below it (figure 10.26). Is this a rule or a coincidence? If the finer mesh arose by dividing the elements of the coarse one, the field of the coarse mesh is at the same time a field of the fine mesh, and this is exactly what the Ritz method is made for: \(\mat\Psi\) is the matrix that converts the nodal displacements of the coarse mesh into the nodal displacements of the fine one.

Corollary 10.43 Nested meshes (gnezdene mreže)

Let a bar with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\), clamped at \(x = 0\), be divided into a coarse mesh with \(n_{\mathrm{H}}\) elements and a fine mesh in which every element of the coarse one is divided into the same number of equal parts, with \(n_{\mathrm{h}}\) elements. Let \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the matrices of the coarse and the fine mesh with consistent masses (lemma 10.31), without the clamped node, and let \(\mat P\) be the matrix with \(n_{\mathrm{h}}\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{H}}\) columns whose column \(I\) has in row \(j\) the value of the hat function (šotorska funkcija) of the coarse mesh of node \(I\) at the fine node \(j\) (definition 6.25). Then \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\), and every consistent eigenvalue of the fine mesh is at most as large as that of the coarse one, \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{H}}\).

Proof

We must show that the fine mesh represents the field of the coarse mesh exactly, that the quadratic forms of the two meshes are then equal, and apply theorem 10.42 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\).

  1. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) be the column of free nodal displacements of the coarse mesh and \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) the field (theorem 7.13). On every element of the fine mesh that lies inside one element of the coarse one, \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) is a straight line. A straight line is determined by the values at the end points (definition 7.1), so \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) is a field of the fine mesh with nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\): the value of \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) at the fine node \(j\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{I}} \psi^H_{\mathrm{I}}(x_{\mathrm{j}})\,d_{\mathrm{I}}\).
  2. By point 3 of theorem 7.13, \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \tfrac12\int_0^L EA\,(u_{\mathrm{H}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the same field written in the coarse mesh gives \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\). Hence \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  3. By lemma 10.34 (without time, so with \(\vek d\) in place of \(\dot{\vek d}\)) \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \int_0^L\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{H}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\), that is \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  4. A symmetric matrix \(\mat S\) with \(\vek d^\T\mat S\,\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek d\) is zero: for \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) we get \(S_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\), and for \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) we get \(2S_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\). The matrices \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) are symmetric, so by steps 2 and 3 both equalities hold.
  5. The columns of \(\mat P\) are independent: the node \(I\) of the coarse mesh is also a node of the fine one, and there the hat function \(I\) has the value 1 and all the others 0. The submatrix of the rows of the coarse nodes is therefore the identity matrix, and \(\mat P\,\vek a = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
  6. Theorem 10.42 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\) and \(m = n_{\mathrm{H}}\): by step 4 the Ritz problem is exactly \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a\), so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Source: COOK (PDF p. 396): »if ... elements are compatible ... and mass matrices are consistent, then computed natural frequencies are upper bounds to exact frequencies«. GR (PDF p. 389): finite elements of displacement type are upper bounds in the Rayleigh-Ritz sense. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof has shown that nesting by itself applies the Ritz method, because the coarse mesh is a subspace of the fine one. A test on bar C: the fine mesh has 16 elements, the coarse one 4, so \(\mat P\) is a matrix with 16 rows and 4 columns, each column has the hat function of a coarse node at the fine nodes, for example the first one \((\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac34,\ 1,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ 0, \dots, 0)^\T\). NumPy gives zero matrices for the differences \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{16}\,\mat P - \mat K_4\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{16}\,\mat P - \mat M_4\). The Ritz approximation with 4 columns is therefore exactly the four-element model, and we compare its frequencies with the first four frequencies of the sixteen-element model and with the true ones, all in Hz:

\(r\) 1 2 3 4
4 elements (Ritz) 1261,27 3978,85 7227,70 10 451,89
16 elements 1253,70 3773,21 6329,09 8945,89
true 1253,20 3759,60 6266,01 8772,41

Every value of the four-element model is above the sixteen-element one, and that is above the true one, as corollary 10.43 and theorem 10.42 require. The errors of the coarse model are \(0{,}64\) %, \(5{,}83\) %, \(15{,}35\) % and \(19{,}15\) %, those of the fine one \(0{,}04\) %, \(0{,}36\) %, \(1{,}01\) % and \(1{,}98\) %. The highest Ritz value is the worst. By theorem 10.36 the error depends on \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi h/(2L)\), where \(\theta = \pi\) is one whole half-wave of the sine per element, the shortest one that an element can still show. With four elements \(\theta_1 = \pi/8 = 0{,}39\) and \(\theta_4 = 7\pi/8 = 2{,}75\), almost \(\pi\), so the fourth mode is shown with barely one element per half-wave. This is why we use the Ritz method for a few of the lowest frequencies and not for all \(m\).

For lumped masses the theorem does not hold, and the numbers show this. The lumped matrix of the fine mesh, mapped by \(\mat P\), is not the lumped matrix of the coarse one: \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{16,\mathrm{z}}\,\mat P = \tfrac{\rho A L}{16}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(0{,}625,\ 2{,}75,\ 0{,}625)\) with the last diagonal entry \(1{,}375\), while \(\mat M_{4,\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{\rho A L}{16}\,\mathrm{diag}(4,\ 4,\ 4,\ 2)\). The first diagonal entry is, for example, the sum of the squares of the values of the hat function at the fine nodes, \(2\,(\tfrac1{16} + \tfrac4{16} + \tfrac9{16}) + 1 = 2{,}75\), and not \(4\), as in the coarse lumped matrix, which assigns the whole mass of an element to its two nodes. Corollary 10.43 therefore does not hold for lumped masses: the coarse mesh has \(f_1 = 1245{,}16\) Hz, the fine one \(1252{,}70\) Hz, so the coarse one is below the fine one, and both are below the true one. Theorem 10.42 of course holds for the pair \((\mat K_{16}, \mat M_{16,\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\), but this is a different reduced pair: its lowest frequency is \(1260{,}24\) Hz, above the fine \(1252{,}70\) Hz, and not the \(1245{,}16\) Hz of the coarse lumped mesh. That the lumped values here lie below the true one is a property of this bar (GR, PDF p. 389, »in general mass lumping induces an overestimation of the inertia of the system«) and not a theorem: the theorem is proved only for consistent masses.

What has this section given us? A small eigenproblem of order \(m\) that uses only \(m\) chosen shapes out of \(n\) coordinates, and the guarantee that each of the \(m\) values obtained is above the true one with the same ordinal number. The guarantee is no more than that: it does not say how far above, and not which shapes to choose. A good choice is shapes that are already close to the modes, such as static shapes under weight (section 10.6) or low modes of parts of a structure. Such a selection in a large structure is treated in chapter 21. The same idea, with the energy scalar product \(B(u, w)\) of chapter 6 in place of the matrix \(\mat K\) and with mass in place of \(\mat M\), we use for the natural frequencies of bars and beams with trial functions. Chapter 6 promised this to us, and chapter 13 carries it out.
