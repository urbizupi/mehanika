Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.9 The Ritz method for natural frequencies (Ritzova metoda za lastne frekvence)

Bar C (palica C) of section 10.8 has 16 coordinates with 16 elements, but we are interested mainly in its lowest natural frequency. The same holds for a building with many storeys (etaže): its motion is often almost only a few simple shapes, while there are as many coordinates as storeys. Can we keep only a few shapes of the motion of a large system and still get its lowest frequencies? And on which side of the true value will we land?

The model is the system of section 10.2 without damping and without external force. The matrix \(\mat K\) of order \(n\) is symmetric, the mass matrix \(\mat M\) of order \(n\) is symmetric and positive definite (definition 6.27). The eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) are \(\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and its eigencolumns, the modes (nihajni načini), are \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) equal to 1 for \(r = s\) and 0 otherwise (theorem 10.21). The Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik) \(R(\vek v) = \vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v/\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) of definition 10.41 is the number we get from a column \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\) with \(n\) numbers.

The idea is the same as in section 6.4 of chapter 6. There, instead of all fields we tried only a family of fields with a few free numbers and looked for the best field in it. Now the family is the set of columns \(\vek v = c_1\vek\psi_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{m}}\vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) with given columns \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), which are the numbers of the trial field in the coordinates of the system, just as the \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) were functions. Instead of the lowest point of the energy we look for the points where the Rayleigh quotient is stationary, since theorem 10.43 says that the quotient is stationary exactly at the modes and that an error of order \(\varepsilon\) in the shape gives an error of order \(\varepsilon^2\) in the quotient (figure 10.64: a ten times smaller error of the shape gives a hundred times smaller error of the quotient). First a single column on frame A (okvir A) of section 10.1, with \(\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\mat M = \bar m\,\mat I\), \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and \(\bar m = 500\) kg.

Take the column \(\vek\psi = (1,\ 1{,}5)^\T\), the static shape of the frame under the weights of the storeys from section 10.7 (figure 10.84, left). The frame stands on the ground. We draw it in the equilibrium position grey dashed, then we move the lower floor by 1 and the upper one by 1,5, and draw the displaced frame in orange. Every \(\vek v = c\,\vek\psi\) with \(c \ne 0\) gives the same quotient, because \(c^2\) cancels. The numerator \(\vek\psi^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi\) is twice the energy of the storeys. A storey resists drift (section 10.2.3), the displacement of its floor relative to the floor below it (for the first storey relative to the ground), and has the energy \(\tfrac12\,k\) times the square of the drift. The lower storey drifts by 1, the upper one by \(1{,}5 - 1 = 0{,}5\), so

\[\vek\psi^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi = k\,(2 \cdot 1^2 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1{,}5 + 1{,}5^2) = k\,(1^2 + 0{,}5^2) = 1{,}25\,k .\]

The denominator adds the squares of the floor displacements, \(\vek\psi^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi = \bar m\,(1^2 + 1{,}5^2) = 3{,}25\,\bar m\) (the floor mass is written \(\bar m\) here because \(m\) is the number of columns in this section). The quotient is \(\tilde\lambda = 1{,}25\,k/(3{,}25\,\bar m) = 385{,}97\ \text{1/s}^2\), the circular frequency is \(19{,}6461\) 1/s, the true one is \(\omega_1 = 19{,}5783\) 1/s. The error of the frequency is \(+0{,}346\) % and the error of \(\omega^2\) is \(+0{,}694\) %, because a relative error roughly doubles when squared:

\[(1 + 0{,}003462)^2 = 1 + 2 \cdot 0{,}003462 + 0{,}003462^2 = 1 + 0{,}006924 + 0{,}000012 = 1{,}006936 .\]

The second term is the doubled error, the third is almost 600 times smaller than it.

With several columns we mix the shapes: we take the first shape \(c_1\) times, the second \(c_2\) times and add the floor displacements, \(\vek v = c_1\vek\psi_1 + c_2\vek\psi_2\). The computation then turns into a small eigenproblem. We write the columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) as the columns of a matrix \(\mat\Psi\) with \(n\) rows and \(m\) columns, so that \(\vek v = \mat\Psi\,\vek a\) for a column \(\vek a\) with the \(m\) numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(\vek v = \mat\Psi\,\vek a\) we have \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \vek a^\T(\mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi)\,\vek a\) and similarly for \(\mat M\), so the quotient \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a)\) is nothing other than the Rayleigh quotient of a pair of matrices of order \(m\), which we now name.

Lemma 10.57 Reduced pair of matrices (reducirani par matrik)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as above, \(\mat\Psi\) a matrix with \(n\) rows and \(m \le n\) columns whose columns are linearly independent (definition 6.22), and \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi\), \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi\) matrices of order \(m\). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is symmetric, and the matrix \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric and positive definite. For frame A with \(\mat M = \bar m\,\mat I\), \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) is the mass \(\bar m\) times the sum of the squared floor displacements.
  2. There exist real numbers \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) and columns \(\vek a_1, \dots, \vek a_{\mathrm{m}}\) with \(m\) numbers such that \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) is equal to 1 for \(r = s\) and 0 otherwise. The columns \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) have \(n\) numbers and satisfy \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat K\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. For \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) we have \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a)\) \(= \vek a^\T\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a/\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\), which is the Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\). Its partial derivatives with respect to the components of \(\vek a\) are zero exactly at the eigencolumns of this pair, and the value of the quotient there is the corresponding eigenvalue.
Proof

We must show that the reduced pair satisfies the conditions of theorem 10.21, and then read points 2 and 3 from the theorem and from the definition of the quotient.

  1. By lemma 1.102, \(\tilde{\mat K}^\T = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K^\T\mat\Psi = \tilde{\mat K}\), because \(\mat K\) is symmetric. In the same way \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric.
  2. Let \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\). Since the columns of \(\mat\Psi\) are independent, \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a \ne \vek 0\) (definition 6.22). Hence \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a = (\mat\Psi\,\vek a)^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a) > 0\), because \(\mat M\) is positive definite. This is point 1. On frame A this means that for every nonzero mixture of the two shapes at least one floor moves: only \(\vek\psi_1\) moves the lower one, so for \(c_1 \ne 0\) it moves, and for \(c_1 = 0\) and \(c_2 \ne 0\) the upper one does. Every motion within the family therefore has kinetic energy.
  3. By steps 1 and 2, theorem 10.21 applied to the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\) of order \(m\) gives the numbers \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the columns \(\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\). The products in point 2 are only the opening of brackets: \((\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\mat M\,(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}) = \vek a_{\mathrm{r}}^\T(\mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\), and \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{s}}\) gives the second.
  4. Point 3: by step 3 the numerator and denominator of the quotient \(R(\mat\Psi\,\vek a)\) are \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a\) and \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\). The derivatives vanish exactly at the eigencolumns by theorem 10.43 for the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\), whose conditions are guaranteed by steps 1 and 2. In figure 10.63 this is the horizontal tangent through the mode, here for a pair of order \(m\).

Source: CK (10.58) to (10.63) (PDF p. 318): »Ritz proposed that the coefficients ... be chosen to make R(v) stationary«. The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says that Ritz's problem is indeed an eigenproblem of the same kind as the original, only smaller. Let us name it.

Definition 10.58 Ritz approximation of eigenvalues (Ritzov približek lastnih vrednosti)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be as in definition 10.13, \(\mat\Psi\) a matrix with \(n\) rows and \(m\) linearly independent columns \(\vek\psi_1, \dots, \vek\psi_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(1 \le m \le n\), and \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi\) matrices of order \(m\). The reduced eigenproblem (reducirani lastni problem) is \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) for a column \(\vek a\) with \(m\) numbers. Its eigenvalues \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) are the Ritz values (Ritzove vrednosti), and the columns \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(n\) numbers are the Ritz shapes (Ritzove oblike). Together they are the Ritz approximation (Ritzov približek) of the first \(m\) eigenvalues and shapes of the system. For \(m = 1\) the Ritz value is the Rayleigh quotient \(R(\vek\psi_1)\).

Source: CK (10.59) to (10.63) (PDF p. 318), which writes the matrices with a hat. The notation is ours.

Frame A with two columns shows what happens when \(m = n\). Let \(\mat\Psi\) have \(\vek\psi_1 = (1,\ 1{,}5)^\T\) and \(\vek\psi_2 = (0,\ 1)^\T\). We get \(\tilde{\mat K} = k\begin{pmatrix} 1{,}25 & 0{,}5 \\ 0{,}5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \bar m\begin{pmatrix} 3{,}25 & 1{,}5 \\ 1{,}5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) (SymPy), and the reduced problem has \(\tilde\lambda = 383{,}31\) and \(2627{,}25\) 1/s\(^2\), exactly the eigenvalues of the frame. The reason is seen in figure 10.84: with the two shapes we get every position of the frame. For a position with displacements \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) the first shape alone moves the lower floor, so \(c_1 = q_1\). The second moves only the upper floor and drifts only the upper storey, with it we add what is missing, \(c_2 = q_2 - 1{,}5\,q_1\). The family is therefore all positions, we have left no shape out, we have only written the system in other coordinates (the proof is point 2 of the theorem below). The real case is \(m < n\), where we have left shapes out. The Rayleigh quotient of section 10.7 always lay above \(\lambda_1\) for frame A. Does this also hold for the other Ritz values and for every choice of \(\mat\Psi\)?

Theorem 10.59 Ritz values are upper bounds (Ritzove vrednosti so zgornje meje)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) be as at the beginning of the section, and \(\mat\Psi\) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in definition 10.58 with \(m \le n\). Then:

  1. \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r = 1, \dots, m\).
  2. If \(m = n\), then \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\) and \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigencolumn of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
Proof

For point 1 we take the first \(r\) Ritz shapes. We must show that the set of their combinations has nowhere a quotient larger than \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), but that every such set contains a column where the quotient is not smaller than \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is point 2 of theorem 10.44 for the columns \(\vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{r}}\), written out here to the end. Figure 10.86 shows steps 3 to 6 for \(r = 2\) and \(n = 3\).

  1. Let \(r \le m\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat\Psi\,\vek a_{\mathrm{j}}\). By lemma 10.57, point 2, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\) is equal to 1 for \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise, and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat K\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  2. The columns \(\vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{r}}\) are independent. If \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\), we multiply by \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\) and by step 1 we get \(t_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  3. Let \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\). By step 1, \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\). Since \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(j \le r\), we have \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2\), so \(R(\vek v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every such combination. For \(r = 2\) these combinations are the blue plane, those with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) are the circle, on which \(R\) nowhere exceeds \(\tilde\lambda_2\). On the number line \(R(\vek v)\) is the centroid of weights over the Ritz values, which cannot lie to the right of \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  4. The conditions \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, r - 1\) are \(r - 1\) homogeneous linear equations for the \(r\) numbers \(t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}\) (for \(r = 1\) there are none). By lemma 10.16 they have a solution \((t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}) \ne \vek 0\). The corresponding \(\vek v\) is not zero, because by step 2 the columns are independent, and it is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\). For \(r = 2\) this is one condition \(\eta_1 = 0\), a plane that cuts the sphere in the grey circle, and the solutions are the orange line where the blue plane cuts it.
  5. By the expansion theorem (corollary 10.22), \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\), and by step 4, \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(s < r\). By equation (10.59), \(R(\vek v) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). Since \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s \ge r\), \(R(\vek v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). On the orange line \(R\) is the average of \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) with the weights \(c_2^2\) and \(c_3^2\), so it is not below \(\lambda_2\). On the number line these are the weights below the axis, there is none at \(\lambda_1\), so the centroid is not to the left of \(\lambda_2\).
  6. The column from step 4 is a combination from step 3, so for the same \(\vek v\) we have \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le R(\vek v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is point 1. In the figure: the orange line lies in the blue plane. On the number line both centroids are the same point in the band between \(\lambda_2\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2\).
  7. For point 2, \(\mat\Psi\) is square with independent columns. From \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\) it follows that \(\vek a = \vek 0\), so \(\mat\Psi\) and also \(\mat\Psi^\T\) have an inverse matrix (lemma 8.22). For frame A these are the shapes from which we build every position.
  8. The pair \((\tilde\lambda, \vek a)\) solves \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) exactly when \(\mat\Psi^\T(\mat K - \tilde\lambda\mat M)\,\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\), and by step 7 this holds exactly when \((\mat K - \tilde\lambda\mat M)\,\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \vek 0\). The eigenpairs of the reduced problem and of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) therefore agree through \(\vek a \mapsto \mat\Psi\,\vek a\). For frame A, \(\vek a_1\) is a multiple of the column \((1,\ 0{,}118)^\T\): with \(c_1 = 1\) and \(c_2 = 1{,}618 - 1{,}5 = 0{,}118\) the two shapes give the first mode with displacements \(1\) and \(1{,}618\). This map takes independent columns to independent columns (step 7), so the two eigenspaces of the same eigenvalue have the same dimension in both problems. That dimension is the number of repetitions of the eigenvalue, since by corollary 10.22 (point 2) an eigencolumn is a sum of modes with the same eigenvalue, and these are independent. The ordered lists are therefore equal.

Source: CK, text after (10.71) (PDF p. 319): »each of the \hat N < N eigenvalues produced by a Rayleigh-Ritz approximation to an N-DOF system is an upper bound to the corresponding exact eigenvalue«. CK proves it with the eigenvalue separation theorem (10.69) to (10.71), GR with a conclusion about constraints (2.239), (2.240) (PDF p. 136). The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof has shown two things. The Ritz value \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the largest quotient on the combinations of the first \(r\) Ritz shapes (for \(r = 2\) on the circle), and every set of combinations of \(r\) independent columns contains a column on which the quotient is not smaller than \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). So the Ritz method does not underestimate eigenvalues. This is similar to chapter 6, where the energy of the Ritz field was above the true one (theorem 6.34): a family of fields is an additional restriction, and a restriction does not lower the eigenvalues (corollary 10.45).

The proof needed two assumptions, which are worth seeing in numbers.

  • The columns of \(\mat\Psi\) are independent (step 2 of the proof of lemma 10.57). We choose them ourselves, so we can always guarantee this. But if on frame A we add \(2\vek\psi_1\) to the shape \(\vek\psi_1\), which is the same shape, only twice as large, we get \(\tilde{\mat K} = 1{,}25\,k\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = 3{,}25\,\bar m\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]\). Then \(\det(\tilde{\mat K} - \tilde\lambda\tilde{\mat M}) = (1{,}25\,k - 3{,}25\,\bar m\,\tilde\lambda)^2\,(1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2) = 0\) for every \(\tilde\lambda\) (SymPy), and the reduced problem has no meaning. We leave out the redundant column and the family stays the same.
  • The bound holds with respect to the eigenvalues of the same pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). The proof uses nothing but \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\), so the bound holds for the model, not for the real structure. Whether the model itself lies above the real structure is another question. For bar C with a consistent mass it does (corollary 10.54), with a lumped one it does not: the sixteen-element model with a lumped mass has \(f_1 = 1252{,}70\) Hz, below the true value \(1253{,}20\) Hz.

Let us test the theorem on a system where \(m < n\) in the true sense, on a frame with \(n = 3\) storeys, each with mass \(\bar m = 500\) kg and stiffness \(k\) (section 10.3).

The eigenvalues of the frame are \(\lambda/(k/\bar m) = 0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\), the frequencies \(2{,}2438\), \(6{,}2870\) and \(9{,}0850\) Hz. The frame stands on the ground and we draw its equilibrium position grey dashed. For the columns we take two simple shapes (figure 10.85), a displacement of all floors by the same amount and a displacement that grows with the storey: \(\vek\psi_1 = (1,\ 1,\ 1)^\T\) and \(\vek\psi_2 = (0,\ 1,\ 2)^\T\). We again count the energy by the storey drifts. For \(\vek\psi_1\) the first storey has drift 1, the second and third have none, they only move as a whole, so their columns stay straight. For \(\vek\psi_2\) the first storey has no drift, the second and third have drift 1 each. So \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_1 = k\), \(\vek\psi_2^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_2 = 2k\) and \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek\psi_2 = k\,(1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 1) = 0\). The masses add the products of the floor displacements: \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_1 = 3\bar m\), \(\vek\psi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_2 = (0 + 1 + 2)\,\bar m = 3\bar m\) and \(\vek\psi_2^\T\mat M\,\vek\psi_2 = (0 + 1 + 4)\,\bar m = 5\bar m\). The reduced problem with \(x = \tilde\lambda\,\bar m/k\) has the determinant

\[\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 - 3x & -3x \\ -3x & 2 - 5x \end{pmatrix} = (1 - 3x)(2 - 5x) - 9x^2 = 6x^2 - 11x + 2 = 0 ,\]

with solutions \(x = (11 \mp \sqrt{73})/12 = 0{,}204666\) and \(1{,}628667\). The Ritz values are \(\tilde\lambda_1/(k/\bar m) = 0{,}204666\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2/(k/\bar m) = 1{,}628667\), the frequencies \(2{,}2809\) and \(6{,}4343\) Hz. The error of the frequencies is \(+1{,}65\) % and \(+2{,}34\) %, and the errors of the eigenvalues are \(+3{,}33\) % and \(+4{,}74\) %. Both values are above the true ones, \(0{,}204666 \ge 0{,}198062\) and \(1{,}628667 \ge 1{,}554958\), as the theorem predicts. This approximation does not give the third value, because the reduced problem has only order \(m = 2\).

We can see why they are above the true ones even without the proof. Every combination \(\vek v = c_1\vek\psi_1 + c_2\vek\psi_2 = (c_1,\ c_1 + c_2,\ c_1 + 2c_2)^\T\) has its middle displacement exactly halfway between the lower and the upper, \(v_2 = (v_1 + v_3)/2\), so \(v_1 - 2v_2 + v_3 = 0\). The floors always lie on a line. This is how a massless rigid bar would hold them, fixed to the right ends of all three floors, free to move and rotate but unable to bend. The Ritz shape \(\vek v_1\) is the lowest vibration of the frame with such a bar. The first mode would move the middle floor out to \(1{,}80\), and the bar holds it at \(1{,}63\). A constraint stiffens the structure (COOK, PDF p. 164: »Constraints stiffen a structure«), so the frequency of the frame with the bar, \(2{,}2809\) Hz, is higher than \(2{,}2438\) Hz. Leaving a shape out means adding a constraint (CK, PDF p. 319). This is the physical picture of the theorem: by choosing a family of shapes we add invisible massless constraints to the structure, and these never lower the frequencies.

The plane of two columns is the same single constraint on three coordinates: the cross product \(\vek\psi_1 \times \vek\psi_2 = (1,\ -2,\ 1)^\T =: \vek b\) is perpendicular to both columns, so the plane is the set of columns with \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\), which is exactly \(v_1 - 2v_2 + v_3 = 0\). Rayleigh's theorem on constraints therefore says even more. In the notation of corollary 10.45, \(p = 1\), the matrix \(\mat G\) builds from a column with the elements \(u_2\) and \(u_3\) a column \(\vek v\) with \(\vek b^\T\vek v = 0\), \(\mat K' = \mat G^\T\mat K\,\mat G\) and \(\mat M' = \mat G^\T\mat M\,\mat G\). We have \(\mat\Psi = \mat G\,\mat T\), where \(\mat T\) is the matrix with the rows of \(\mat\Psi\) with index \(i \ne p\). The matrix \(\mat T\) is invertible, because \(\mat T\vek a = \vek 0\) implies \(\mat\Psi\,\vek a = \mat G\,\mat T\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\vek a = \vek 0\) (lemma 8.22). The reduced pair is then \((\mat T^\T\mat K'\mat T,\ \mat T^\T\mat M'\mat T)\). By point 2 of theorem 10.59, applied to the pair \((\mat K', \mat M')\) of order \(n - 1\) with the matrix \(\mat T\) in place of \(\mat\Psi\), it has the same eigenvalues as \((\mat K', \mat M')\), so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\). The corollary thus also gives an upper bound, \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2 \le \lambda_3\), and indeed \(0{,}204666 \le 1{,}554958\) and \(1{,}628667 \le 3{,}246980\).

Figure 10.86 shows steps 3 to 6 of the proof for \(r = 2\) and \(n = 3\). In the mode coordinates, \(\vek v = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek v\) (corollary 10.22), we have \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = \sum\eta_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and \(\vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v = \sum\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\eta_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), so the columns with \(\vek v^\T\mat M\,\vek v = 1\) form the unit sphere. The plane of two columns passes through the origin and therefore meets the plane \(\eta_1 = 0\) at least in a line: the two equations \(\eta_1 = 0\) and the equation of the plane have, for three unknowns, a nontrivial solution by lemma 10.16. On this line, in the direction \((0,\ -0{,}978,\ -0{,}208)^\T\) (NumPy), \(R = 1{,}6285\,k/\bar m\), above \(\lambda_2\), because a mixture of \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) cannot fall below the smaller of the two. The largest quotient in the plane, which is reached on the circle where the plane cuts the sphere, is therefore at least as large.

Steps 3 and 5 use the same rule as figure 10.61: the quotient is the centroid of weights on the number line, so it lies between the smallest and the largest number that carries weight. Figure 10.87 shows this for the frame with three storeys. The horizontal axis has the unit \(k/\bar m\). At the top it carries the Ritz values \(\tilde\lambda_1\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2\), and above them the weights of the column \((\vek v_1 + \vek v_2)/\sqrt 2\), \(t_1^2 = t_2^2 = 0{,}5\). The centroid is at \(R = 0{,}9167\), to the left of \(\tilde\lambda_2\). This holds for every combination of the first two Ritz shapes, because to the right of \(\tilde\lambda_2\) there is no Ritz value that could carry weight. This is step 3. In the middle, the column from the orange line on the unit sphere, \(\eta_1 = 0\), is split in two ways. Above the axis are its weights by Ritz shapes, \(0{,}0001\) and \(0{,}9999\), below the axis its weights by modes, \(c_2^2 = 0{,}957\) and \(c_3^2 = 0{,}043\). At \(\lambda_1\) there is no weight, because \(c_1 = \eta_1 = 0\), so the centroid of the lower weights is not to the left of \(\lambda_2\). This is step 5. Both splits describe the same column, so they have the same centroid, \(R = 1{,}6285\) (NumPy). It lies in the band between \(\lambda_2\) and \(\tilde\lambda_2\), which the bottom axis shows enlarged, \(1{,}5550 \le 1{,}6285 \le 1{,}6287\). This is step 6. Since by step 4 such a column exists for every choice of columns, the band is never empty, so \(\lambda_2 \le \tilde\lambda_2\).

The theorem also says what it does not say. It does not say how large the error is: it can be \(+3{,}33\) % and \(+4{,}74\) %, as above, or much more if we choose bad columns. And it says nothing about the shapes: the Ritz shape \(\vek v_1\) is \(2{,}8°\) from the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\), which by theorem 10.43 gives an error of the quotient of the order of the square of the angle. On the frame the shape and the mode, both with displacement 1 of the lowest floor, differ by \(0{,}17\) at the middle floor. The theorem also does not say how the error changes with the ordinal number \(r\). This is shown by finite element meshes.

Bar C, clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L = 1\) m, consists of \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) equal elements of length \(h = L/n_{\mathrm{e}}\) made of steel with wave speed \(c = 5012{,}80\) m/s (section 10.8). With the consistent mass matrix (lemma 10.47) the lowest frequencies, the true one being \(f_1 = 1253{,}20\) Hz, are:

\(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) 1 2 4 8 16 32
\(f_1\) consistent (Hz) 1381,85 1285,61 1261,27 1255,22 1253,70 1253,33
\(f_1\) lumped (Hz) 1128,28 1221,24 1245,16 1251,19 1252,70 1253,08

The consistent values \(f_1\) fall with every doubling of \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) and stay above the true one, the lumped ones (definition 10.48) rise and are below it (figure 10.91). Is this a rule or a coincidence?

Let us draw the bar, clamped at \(x = 0\), with a mesh of four elements and with a mesh of sixteen, in which every coarse element is divided into four fine ones. The field of the coarse mesh is on every coarse element a straight line through the nodal displacements at its ends. Its values at the fine nodes lie on the same lines, so on every fine element this field is a straight line through the values at its end points, that is a field of the fine mesh. The field of the coarse mesh is at the same time a field of the fine mesh, and this is exactly what the Ritz method is made for: \(\mat\Psi\) is the matrix that converts the nodal displacements of the coarse mesh into the nodal displacements of the fine one. Its column for the coarse node \(L/4\) is the values of the hat function \(\psi^H_1\) of this node at the fine nodes.

Corollary 10.60 Nested meshes (gnezdene mreže)

Let a bar with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\), clamped at \(x = 0\), be divided into a coarse mesh with \(n_{\mathrm{H}}\) elements and a fine mesh in which every element of the coarse one is divided into the same number of equal parts, with \(n_{\mathrm{h}}\) elements. Let \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the matrices of the coarse and the fine mesh with consistent masses (lemma 10.47), without the clamped node, and let \(\mat P\) be the matrix with \(n_{\mathrm{h}}\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{H}}\) columns whose column \(I\) has in row \(j\) the value of the hat function (šotorska funkcija) of the coarse mesh of node \(I\) at the fine node \(j\) (definition 6.25). Then \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P = \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\), and every consistent eigenvalue of the fine mesh is at most as large as that of the coarse one, \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{H}}\).

Proof

We must show that the fine mesh represents the field of the coarse mesh exactly, that the quadratic forms of the two meshes are then equal, and apply theorem 10.59 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\).

  1. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) be the column of free nodal displacements of the coarse mesh and \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) the field (theorem 7.13). On every element of the fine mesh that lies inside one element of the coarse one, \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) is a straight line. A straight line is determined by the values at the end points (definition 7.1), so \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) is a field of the fine mesh with nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\): the value of \(u_{\mathrm{H}}\) at the fine node \(j\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{I}} \psi^H_{\mathrm{I}}(x_{\mathrm{j}})\,d_{\mathrm{I}}\). In figure 10.88 the orange values lie on the lines of the field.
  2. By point 3 of theorem 7.13, \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \tfrac12\int_0^L EA\,(u_{\mathrm{H}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the same field written in the coarse mesh gives \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\). Hence \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  3. By lemma 10.50 (without time, so with \(\vek d\) in place of \(\dot{\vek d}\)), for the same field, \(\vek d_{\mathrm{h}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{h}} = \int_0^L\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{H}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\), that is \(\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{H}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{H}}\).
  4. A symmetric matrix \(\mat S\) with \(\vek d^\T\mat S\,\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek d\) is zero: for \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) we get \(S_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\), and for \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) we get \(2S_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\). The matrices \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mat P - \mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\) are symmetric, so by steps 2 and 3 both equalities hold.
  5. The columns of \(\mat P\) are independent: the node \(I\) of the coarse mesh is also a node of the fine one, and there the hat function \(I\) has the value 1 and all the others 0 (at the node \(L/4\) the function \(\psi^H_1\) has the value 1 and \(\psi^H_2\) the value 0). The submatrix of the rows of the coarse nodes is therefore the identity matrix, and \(\mat P\,\vek a = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
  6. Theorem 10.59 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\) and \(m = n_{\mathrm{H}}\): by step 4 the Ritz problem is exactly \(\mat K_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\mat M_{\mathrm{H}}\,\vek a\), so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}}\). In figure 10.91 this is the blue value of \(f_1\) at \(n_{\mathrm{e}} = 16\), \(1253{,}70\) Hz, below the one at \(n_{\mathrm{e}} = 4\), \(1261{,}27\) Hz.

Source: COOK (PDF p. 396): »if ... elements are compatible ... and mass matrices are consistent, then computed natural frequencies are upper bounds to exact frequencies«. GR (PDF p. 389): finite elements of displacement type are upper bounds in the Rayleigh-Ritz sense. The proof in steps is not from the sources.

The proof has shown that nesting by itself applies the Ritz method, because the coarse mesh is a subspace of the fine one. It needed two things. The meshes must be nested, otherwise a field of the coarse mesh is not a field of the fine one. Figure 10.89 shows why. On the bar we draw a mesh of three elements and a mesh of four. The hat function of the node \(L/3\) is a field of the three-element mesh and has a kink at \(L/3\), where the four-element mesh has no node. If it were a field of the four-element mesh, it would have its values \(\tfrac34\), \(\tfrac12\), \(0\) and \(0\) at that mesh's nodes. A field of a mesh is on each element the straight line through the nodal displacements at its ends, so with these nodal displacements there is only one, the orange broken line. Between \(L/4\) and \(L/2\) it is a straight line that drops by \(\tfrac14\), and the point \(L/3\) lies a third of the way along this element, since \((L/3 - L/4)/(L/4) = \tfrac13\). At \(L/3\) its value is therefore \(\tfrac34 - \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac23\), \(\tfrac13\) below the top of the hat. So the hat is not a field of the four-element mesh.

The masses must be consistent, otherwise step 3 does not hold, which we show in numbers below. A test on bar C: the fine mesh has 16 elements, the coarse one 4, so \(\mat P\) is a matrix with 16 rows and 4 columns, each column has the hat function of a coarse node at the fine nodes, for example the first one \((\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac34,\ 1,\ \tfrac34,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ 0, \dots, 0)^\T\). NumPy gives zero matrices for the differences \(\mat P^\T\mat K_{16}\,\mat P - \mat K_4\) and \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{16}\,\mat P - \mat M_4\). The Ritz approximation with 4 columns is therefore exactly the four-element model, and we compare its frequencies with the first four frequencies of the sixteen-element model and with the true ones, all in Hz:

\(r\) 1 2 3 4
4 elements (Ritz) 1261,27 3978,85 7227,70 10 451,89
16 elements 1253,70 3773,21 6329,09 8945,89
true 1253,20 3759,60 6266,01 8772,41

Every value of the four-element model is above the sixteen-element one, and that is above the true one, as corollary 10.60 and corollary 10.54 require. The errors of the coarse model are \(0{,}64\) %, \(5{,}83\) %, \(15{,}35\) % and \(19{,}15\) %, those of the fine one \(0{,}04\) %, \(0{,}36\) %, \(1{,}01\) % and \(1{,}98\) %. The highest Ritz value is the worst. By theorem 10.52 the error depends on \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = (2r - 1)\pi h/(2L)\), the change of the phase of the sine of mode \(r\) along one element. Figure 10.90 shows two such waves on the bar with four elements. At \(\theta = \pi\) there is one whole half-wave of the sine per element, so the values of the sine at all nodes are zero and the mesh field through them is zero. The mesh can no longer show such a wave. The highest mode of a mesh with \(n\) elements, \(\theta_{\mathrm{n}} = (2n - 1)\pi/(2n)\), approaches this limit. With four elements \(\theta_1 = \pi/8 = 0{,}39\) and \(\theta_4 = 7\pi/8 = 2{,}75\), almost \(\pi\). Its half-wave is \(8h/7\) long, barely one element, so the nodal values change sign from node to node and the mesh field through them is a broken line with a single corner per half-wave (figure 10.78 shows all four modes of this mesh on sines). This is why we use the Ritz method for a few of the lowest frequencies and not for all \(m\).

For lumped masses the proof of corollary 10.60 does not go through, and the numbers show this. The lumped matrix of the fine mesh, mapped by \(\mat P\), is not the lumped matrix of the coarse one: \(\mat P^\T\mat M_{16,\mathrm{z}}\,\mat P = \tfrac{\rho A L}{16}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(0{,}625,\ 2{,}75,\ 0{,}625)\) with the last diagonal entry \(1{,}375\), while \(\mat M_{4,\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac{\rho A L}{16}\,\mathrm{diag}(4,\ 4,\ 4,\ 2)\). We see the difference in the first diagonal entry (figure 10.92). It is twice the kinetic energy when the coarse node \(L/4\) moves with velocity 1 and the other coarse nodes are at rest, since the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\,\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\) and \(\dot{\vek d}\) is then the first unit column. The coarse lumped matrix gives the node \(L/4\) half the mass of both neighbouring elements, that is \(\rho A L/4 = 4 \cdot \rho A L/16\), and this mass moves with velocity 1, so it counts with full weight, \(4\). The fine lumped matrix gives every fine node the mass \(\rho A L/16\). The mapping by \(\mat P\) means that a fine node then moves with a velocity that is the value of the hat function there, from \(1\) at the node \(L/4\) to \(\tfrac14\) at the outermost fine nodes. The kinetic energy of a mass is proportional to the square of its velocity, so each mass counts only with the square of this value. Stacked on top of each other these pieces give \(2\,(\tfrac1{16} + \tfrac4{16} + \tfrac9{16}) + 1 = 2{,}75\), \(1{,}25\) less than \(4\). Corollary 10.60 therefore does not hold for lumped masses: the coarse mesh has \(f_1 = 1245{,}16\) Hz, the fine one \(1252{,}70\) Hz, so the coarse one is below the fine one, and both are below the true one. Theorem 10.59 of course holds for the pair \((\mat K_{16}, \mat M_{16,\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\), but this is a different reduced pair: its lowest frequency is \(1260{,}24\) Hz, above the fine \(1252{,}70\) Hz, and not the \(1245{,}16\) Hz of the coarse lumped mesh. That the lumped values here lie below the true one does not follow from the Ritz method but from the closed form of the frequencies of this bar (corollary 10.55). GR (PDF p. 389) explains it so: »in general mass lumping induces an overestimation of the inertia of the system«.

What has this section given us? A small eigenproblem of order \(m\) that uses only \(m\) chosen shapes out of \(n\) coordinates, and the guarantee that each of the \(m\) values obtained is above the true one with the same ordinal number. We obtained the guarantee with a proof on the sphere of modes and understood it as a constraint that stiffens the structure. The guarantee is no more than that: it does not say how far above, and not which shapes to choose. A good choice is shapes that are already close to the modes, such as static shapes under weight (section 10.7) or low modes of parts of a structure. Such a selection in a large structure is treated in chapter 21. The same idea, with the energy scalar product \(B(u, w)\) of chapter 6 in place of the matrix \(\mat K\) and with mass in place of \(\mat M\), we use for the natural frequencies of bars and beams with trial functions. Chapter 6 promised this to us, and chapter 13 carries it out.
