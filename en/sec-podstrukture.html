Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.5 Substructures and the Craig-Bampton reduction (podstrukture in Craig-Bamptonova redukcija)

The Guyan reduction of section 21.4 kept a few coordinates and tied all the others to them by statics. That statics is its weakness: the tied part has mass, and its own vibration is lost in the reduced model (theorem 21.19). What must be added to get that vibration back? And how to proceed when the structure is assembled from parts that different groups compute? Such is the fuselage of an aircraft made of sections or a bridge made of spans: each part has its own mesh and its own matrices, and only a few coordinates at the joint are shared. The section shows how to reduce each part separately, so that it keeps its joint and a few of its own modes, and how to assemble the reduced parts. A part of a structure that has its own matrices is called a substructure (podstruktura) or a component (komponenta), since it is a structure by itself, only beneath the whole structure. The procedure is a Ritz approximation (definition 10.58) with a new choice of the columns \(\mat\Psi\), so the upper bound of theorem 10.59 holds for it as for the Guyan basis.

21.5.1 The frame cut at the second floor

Let us try the smallest example in which everything can be seen: the frame with three floors from section 21.4.1 (floor displacements \(q_1, q_2, q_3\) from the bottom up, storey stiffness \(k\), floor mass \(\bar m\)). Its eigenvalues in the unit \(k/\bar m\) are \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\). The matrices are

\[\mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = \bar m\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ,\]

and in what follows we take \(k = \bar m = 1\). We cut the frame at the second floor into a lower and an upper substructure, as figure 21.33 shows.

The figure has three floors with displacements \(q_1, q_2, q_3\), joined by columns. The gray dashed vertical is the rest position of the frame, from which the displacements are measured. We cut the frame at floor \(2\). The coordinate \(q_2\) is common to the lower and the upper substructure and we call it the interface coordinate (robna koordinata): it lies at the edge where the substructures touch, and we mark it with the index \(\mathrm{a}\). The lower substructure \(1\) has in addition the internal coordinate \(q_1\), and the upper substructure \(2\) the internal coordinate \(q_3\). For each substructure two kinds of motion matter to us, and each asks what is to happen to the interface.

The first motion is the constraint mode (vezni način) \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}\): we move the interface by \(q_2 = 1\) and let the interior settle by itself. Floors \(1\) and \(3\) have mass, but we add no force to them, and since this is rest, by Newton's law the sum of forces on each of them is zero. On floor \(3\) acts only the column of the upper storey (between floors \(2\) and \(3\)) with force \(k(q_3 - q_2)\), which must be zero, so \(q_3 = q_2 = 1\): the upper column stays straight and floor \(3\) goes with the interface. On floor \(1\) act the column below it with force \(k\,q_1\) and the column above it with force \(k(q_1 - q_2)\), and \(2k\,q_1 - k\,q_2 = 0\) gives \(q_1 = \tfrac12\), halfway between the ground and the interface. The column of displacements is \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}} = (\tfrac12,\ 1,\ 1)^\T\). The force that holds floor \(2\) in place is read off the columns: the lower column pulls it back with \(k(q_2 - q_1) = k/2\) and the upper one with \(k(q_2 - q_3) = 0\), so a force \(k/2\) is needed, drawn in orange on the horizontal of floor \(2\). All three forces together are \(\mat K\,\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}} = (0,\ k/2,\ 0)^\T\): only at the interface. The name comes from this: all other interface displacements are held (constrained), here the interface is a single coordinate, and the interior is free.

The second motion is the own vibration of the interior with the interface at rest. We hold floor \(2\) at \(q_2 = 0\) and substructure \(1\) has a single floor, held by the column below it and the column above it, with total stiffness \(2k\) and mass \(\bar m\). Its eigenpair (definition 10.13) is \(\lambda^{(1)} = 2\) and \(\vek\varphi^{(1)} = (1,\ 0,\ 0)^\T\). Substructure \(2\) has floor \(3\) on one column, \(\lambda^{(2)} = 1\) and \(\vek\varphi^{(2)} = (0,\ 0,\ 1)^\T\). Since we hold the interface at rest here, as if it were clamped, we call these two modes fixed-interface modes (načini z vpetim robom).

Every motion of the frame is the sum of these three: the constraint mode and the fixed-interface modes \(\vek\varphi^{(1)}\) and \(\vek\varphi^{(2)}\). From \(\vek q = \vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}\,q_2 + \eta_1\vek\varphi^{(1)} + \eta_2\vek\varphi^{(2)}\) we get \(q_1 = \tfrac12 q_2 + \eta_1\) and \(q_3 = q_2 + \eta_2\), so for every \(\vek q\) the numbers \(q_2\), \(\eta_1 = q_1 - \tfrac12 q_2\) and \(\eta_2 = q_3 - q_2\). We stack the three columns into a matrix \(\mat\Psi\), its determinant is \(-1\), and \(\mat\Psi\) is invertible. The reduced matrices \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat K\mat\Psi\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi^\T\mat M\mat\Psi\) for the coordinates \((q_2, \eta_1, \eta_2)\) are, by a computation in SymPy,

\[\tilde{\mat K} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \tilde{\mat M} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac94 & \tfrac12 & 1 \\ \tfrac12 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{21.39}\]

Each entry has its meaning. The entry \(\tilde K_{11} = \tfrac12\) is exactly the force \(k/2\) of the figure, which holds a unit of interface displacement. The entries \(2\) and \(1\) on the diagonal are the eigenvalues \(\lambda^{(1)}\) and \(\lambda^{(2)}\), since \(\vek\varphi^{(j)\T}\mat K\vek\varphi^{(j)} = \lambda^{(j)}\,\vek\varphi^{(j)\T}\mat M\vek\varphi^{(j)} = \lambda^{(j)}\). The entry \(\tilde M_{11} = \tfrac94\) is the mass that moves with the constraint mode: \(\bar m\,(\tfrac14 + 1 + 1)\) by the squares of the floor displacements. The entries \(\tilde M_{12} = \tfrac12\) and \(\tilde M_{13} = 1\) are the products \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi^{(1)} = \tfrac12\) and \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\mat M\vek\varphi^{(2)} = 1\). Why are there zeros off the diagonal in the stiffness matrix and none in the mass matrix? The energy \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi^{(j)}\) is the work of the forces \(\mat K\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}} = (0,\ k/2,\ 0)^\T\) on the displacements of the mode \(\vek\varphi^{(j)}\). The force is only at floor \(2\), and there the fixed-interface modes are at rest, so the work is zero. The mass entry is a product of displacements, and these do not cancel.

Let us also test what we get with fewer columns, since that is the whole point of reduction. With \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) alone the reduced equation is \(\tfrac12 = \tilde\lambda\cdot\tfrac94\), \(\tilde\lambda = 2/9 = 0{,}222222\), which is the Guyan approximation of section 21.4 for the interface \(q_2\), \(12{,}20\) % above the true value \(0{,}198062\). With the mode of substructure \(2\) added, the reduced pair is \(\tilde K = \mathrm{diag}(\tfrac12, 1)\), \(\tilde M = \begin{pmatrix}9/4 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) with the values \(0{,}200000\) and \(2{,}0\), the first is only \(0{,}98\) % above the true one. With all three columns \(\mat\Psi\) is square and independent, so \(\det(\tilde{\mat K} - \lambda\tilde{\mat M}) = -\lambda^3 + 5\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 1\) (SymPy), exactly the same polynomial as \(\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\), and the Ritz values are exactly \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\), \(3{,}246980\). The reason why the first value is so good with two columns and the second poor (\(2{,}0\) instead of \(1{,}554958\), \(28{,}6\) % too much): the omitted mode of substructure \(1\) has \(\lambda^{(1)} = 2\), which is ten times larger than the first eigenvalue \(0{,}198062\) (ratio \(0{,}099\)), but only \(1{,}29\) times larger than the second. In the Guyan approximation the lowest omitted value is \(\lambda^{(2)} = 1\) and the ratio for the first is \(0{,}198\), and the error \(12\) %. An omitted mode spoils the eigenvalue that is not far enough below it. This conclusion is the same as for dynamic condensation (theorem 21.20) and for the acceleration correction (theorem 21.4).

21.5.2 General partition: interface and internal coordinates

We do the same for a structure with many coordinates. Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\) (theorem 10.21) and let the structure be cut into \(J\) substructures, as figure 21.34 shows. The coordinates shared by several substructures are the interface ones, we mark them with \(\mathrm{a}\), their number is \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\), these are the active coordinates that the reduced model keeps as physical (notation as for Guyan, CK section 14.6). The internal coordinates of substructure \(j\), which no other substructure knows, we mark with \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\) (dependent), their number with \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\). We write the couplings between the interface and the interior as \(\mat K_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\) and so on. Since substructures \(j\) and \(l \ne j\) have no common elements, an element of one does not reach into the internal coordinates of the other: by theorem 7.13, \(\mat K = \sum_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the term of every element of substructure \(j\) has nonzero rows and columns only in \(\mathrm{a}\) and \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\). Therefore \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) for \(j \ne l\), and for two substructures

\[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} & \mat K_{\mathrm{a}1} & \mat K_{\mathrm{a}2} \\ \mat K_{1\mathrm{a}} & \mat K_{11} & \mat 0 \\ \mat K_{2\mathrm{a}} & \mat 0 & \mat K_{22} \end{pmatrix} , \tag{21.40}\]

in the frame this form is exactly \(\mat K\) with the order \(q_2, q_1, q_3\). The matrix \(\mat M\) has the same form. The labels \(\mathrm{a}\) and \(\text{d}\) are from CK, as for Guyan: the letters \(t\) and \(r\) of lemma 12.45 for displacements and rotations are a special case, where \(\mathrm{a}\) are the displacements and \(\text{d}\) the rotations. For beams a bold \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) means a mode of vibration, and we write the rotation of a node with a word.

We now name in general the two kinds of motion found in the frame. The block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\) is positive definite, because \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z > 0\) for a column \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\) that is nonzero only in \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\) (as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 12.45), and by theorem 6.29 it is invertible.

The rows follow the previous figure: the labels at the left say whether a block belongs to the interface or to which substructure, and the line separates the interface columns from the modal ones. The matrix \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) that we are going to build has as its first block of columns the constraint modes: the identity says that the interface coordinate is displaced by itself, and the blocks \(\mat\Psi_1\) and \(\mat\Psi_2\) say what happens in the interiors meanwhile. The remaining columns are the fixed-interface modes \(\mat\Phi_1\) and \(\mat\Phi_2\), each nonzero only in the interior of its own substructure, elsewhere there are zeros. The definitions below name these blocks.

Definition 21.21 Constraint mode (vezni način)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) and the partition be as in equation (21.40). The matrix \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\) has \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{a}}\) columns, the column of \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) with index \(s\) is the displacement of the interior of substructure \(j\) when the interface coordinate \(s\) is displaced by \(1\), all other interface coordinates are \(0\) and there are no forces on the interior. The constraint modes (vezni načini) are the columns of the \(n \times n_{\mathrm{a}}\) matrix

\[\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat I \\ \mat\Psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{J}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{21.41}\]

The matrix \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) is the matrix of the Guyan basis (definition 21.17) for the kept coordinates \(\mathrm{a}\), that is the interface ones.

Source: CK section 17.2.2 (PDF p. 554-555, equations 17.12 and 17.13): a constraint mode is the "static deformation of a structure when a unit displacement is applied to one coordinate of a specified set", while the other coordinates of this set are held and the rest is free of forces. GR section 6.9.2 (PDF p. 503, equation 6.176): "the same reduction matrix as in the Guyan-Irons method, except that the remaining degrees of freedom are now interface ones". The notation by substructures is ours.

Definition 21.22 Fixed-interface mode (način z vpetim robom)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\) and \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) be as in definition 21.21. The pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{jj}})\) of order \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) is symmetric and positive definite (as above for \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\) and the same for \(\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\)), so by theorem 10.21 it has eigenvalues \(\lambda^{(j)}_1 \le \lambda^{(j)}_2 \le \dots \le \lambda^{(j)}_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\)-orthonormal columns \(\vek\varphi^{(j)}_1, \dots, \vek\varphi^{(j)}_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{j}}\) with \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) numbers each. A fixed-interface mode (način z vpetim robom) of substructure \(j\) is an eigenpair \((\lambda^{(j)}_{\mathrm{s}}, \vek\varphi^{(j)}_{\mathrm{s}})\), extended by zeros to all coordinates: it is an eigenpair of substructure \(j\) in which all interface coordinates are held at zero. We stack the first \(k_{\mathrm{j}} \le n_{\mathrm{j}}\) columns into a matrix \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) rows and \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) columns and the values into \(\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda^{(j)}_1, \dots, \lambda^{(j)}_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}})\), so that \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat I\).

Source: CK section 17.2.1 (PDF p. 553, equations 17.7 to 17.9): "restraining all boundary DOFs and solving the following eigenproblem", normalization with \(\mat M_{\mathrm{ii}}\). The naming and the notation by substructures are from the sources, the extension by zeros is ours.

A constraint mode is named for the interface coordinates being held (constrained): one is displaced, the others are constrained to zero. A fixed-interface mode is named for the interface, which we clamp and thereby switch off, so that the substructure vibrates by itself. Together they cover everything that a substructure can do: a motion in which the interface is not at rest we split into a constraint part, which has the same interface, and a remainder, which has the interface at rest and is therefore a combination of fixed-interface modes (corollary 10.22 for the pair \((\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}, \mat M_{\mathrm{jj}})\)). That this gives really every motion is item 4 of the theorem below.

21.5.3 The Craig-Bampton basis

How did the two kinds of modes end up in the same method? Substructures with modal coordinates were in use before the form that we use.

From history

In the mid-1960s Hurty published several reports and papers on coupling substructures with fixed-interface modes (CK, PDF p. 550). His paper of 1965 was the first complete development of a method oriented to finite elements, which was based on constraint modes and fixed-interface modes, and his method required the interface coordinates to be separated into coordinates of rigid-body motion and redundant coordinates (CK, PDF p. 575). In 1968 Craig and Bampton simplified the method by treating all interface coordinates together (CK, PDF p. 575 and the list of references on p. 589). The simplification paid off, because in this case all the physical interface coordinates are independent generalized coordinates and the assembly of substructures is just assembling the stiffness matrix by elements (CK, PDF p. 576). The method spread because of its accuracy, easy implementation and efficient use of the computer (CK, PDF p. 575).

In this section we use this simplified form of Craig and Bampton. The Craig-Bampton basis stacks the constraint modes and the fixed-interface modes into one matrix.

Definition 21.23 Craig-Bampton basis (Craig-Bamptonova baza)

Let \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) be from definition 21.21 and \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) from definition 21.22. The Craig-Bampton basis (Craig-Bamptonova baza) is the matrix with \(n\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{a}} + k_1 + \dots + k_{\mathrm{J}}\) columns

\[\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat I & \mat 0 & \cdots & \mat 0 \\ \mat\Psi_1 & \mat\Phi_1 & & \mat 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \\ \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{J}} & \mat 0 & & \mat\Phi_{\mathrm{J}} \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek q = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\begin{pmatrix} \vek q_{\mathrm{a}} \\ \vek\eta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \vek\eta_{\mathrm{J}} \end{pmatrix} . \tag{21.42}\]

The coordinates of the reduced model are the interface coordinates \(\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\) and the modal coordinates of the substructures \(\vek\eta_{\mathrm{j}}\) (a column of \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) numbers, how much of each fixed-interface mode there is in the interior of substructure \(j\)). The matrices \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}^\T\mat K\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}^\T\mat M\,\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) are the reduced matrices. For \(k_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for every \(j\) this is the Guyan basis for the interface.

Source: CK section 17.6.1 (PDF p. 575, equations 17.70 and 17.71), GR section 6.9.2 (PDF p. 503, equation 6.141 in the text). The notation by substructures and \(\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) are ours.

The Ritz method of section 10.9 requires independent columns. Does the Craig-Bampton basis have them, are the three kinds of matrices in it really as friendly as the frame suggested, and what do we lose if we do not keep all the modes? The theorem answers.

We read the theorem with two figures. In the left part of the reduced stiffness matrix the interface and the modes do not mix: the block \(\mat S\) belongs to the interface, the block \(\mat\Lambda\) to the eigenvalues of the substructures, and everything between them is zeros. In the mass matrix the modal part is the identity, the interface block is \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{aa}}\) and the mixed blocks are not zero.

The second figure shows what happens with the number of modes. The basis with fewer modes is a subset of the basis with more modes, so the values do not rise as modes are added. When we keep all the modes, the matrix is square and the values are the true ones.

Theorem 21.24 Craig-Bampton approximation (Craig-Bamptonov približek)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite with the partition (21.40) (\(\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\), \(\mat M_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) for \(j \ne l\)), \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) the basis (21.42) and \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). Let \(m = n_{\mathrm{a}} + k_1 + \dots + k_{\mathrm{J}}\). Then:

  1. The columns of \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) are independent, so \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a Ritz approximation of the eigenvalues (definition 10.58) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, m\).
  2. The reduced matrices have the form
    \[\tilde{\mat K} = \begin{pmatrix} \mat S & \mat 0 \\ \mat 0 & \mat\Lambda \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \tilde{\mat M} = \begin{pmatrix} \tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{aa}} & \tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{a}\eta} \\ \tilde{\mat M}_{\eta\mathrm{a}} & \mat I \end{pmatrix} , \tag{21.43}\]
    where \(\mat S = \mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\), \(\mat\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\mat\Lambda_1, \dots, \mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{J}})\), \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{aa}} = \mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}}(\mat M_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}})\) and \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{a}\eta}\) has by blocks \((\mat M_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}})\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. Let \(\mat\Psi'_{\mathrm{CB}}\) be a basis with \(k'_{\mathrm{j}} \ge k_{\mathrm{j}}\) kept substructure modes (the first \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) columns of \(\mat\Phi'_{\mathrm{j}}\) are \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\)). Then \(\tilde\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, m\). In particular, for \(k_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) the \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the Guyan value for the interface coordinates, and every Craig-Bampton value is below it.
  4. If \(k_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{j}}\) for every \(j\), then \(m = n\) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\).
Proof

We must show that the columns are independent (item 1), that the blocks of the reduced matrices are computed by equation (21.43) (item 2), that the basis with more modes is a superset of the basis with fewer modes (item 3) and that the basis with all modes is square (item 4). Items 1, 3 and 4 use the same theorem 10.59.

  1. Let \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\,(\vek x, \vek y_1, \dots, \vek y_{\mathrm{J}}) = \vek 0\). The first block of rows gives \(\vek x = \vek 0\) (figure 21.33, the interface is at rest), and block \(j\) gives \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\vek y_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\). Multiplying by \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\) and using \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat I\) (definition 21.22), we get \(\vek y_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\). The columns are independent (definition 6.22), and theorem 10.59, item 1, gives \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  2. Block \(j\) of rows of the equation \(\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}} + \mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat 0\), since \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) for \(l \ne j\) and \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} = -\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\) (definition 21.21). Block \(\mathrm a\) is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{a} \mathrm{j}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat S\). So \(\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}} = (\mat S, \mat 0, \dots, \mat 0)\) by blocks. On the frame this is the orange force \(k/2\): the constraint mode needs a force only at the interface, floors \(1\) and \(3\) carry none.
  3. We multiply from the left by \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\). The first block of \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) is \(\mat I\), so \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}} = \mat S\). We denote the columns of the modes of substructure \(j\) in \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) by \(\hat{\mat\Phi}_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat 0, \dots, \mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}, \dots, \mat 0)\) by blocks of rows: the block \(\mathrm a\) and the blocks of the other substructures hold zeros. The block \((\mathrm a, \eta_{\mathrm{j}})\) of \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}^\T\mat K\hat{\mat\Phi}_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}})^\T\hat{\mat\Phi}_{\mathrm{j}}\) by lemma 1.102 and \(\mat K^\T = \mat K\). By step 2, \((\mat K\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}})^\T = (\mat S^\T, \mat 0, \dots, \mat 0)\) by blocks of columns, so the product is \(\mat S^\T\cdot\mat 0 + \mat 0\cdot\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat 0\): the only nonzero block \(\mat S^\T\) meets the zeros of \(\hat{\mat\Phi}_{\mathrm{j}}\) in the block \(\mathrm a\). On the frame: the force at the interface does no work on the modes \(\vek\varphi^{(1)}\) and \(\vek\varphi^{(2)}\), because floor \(2\) is at rest in both. The block \((\eta_{\mathrm{j}}, \eta_{\mathrm{j}})\) is \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the block \((\eta_{\mathrm{j}}, \eta_{\mathrm{l}})\) is \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{l}} = \mat 0\) for \(j \ne l\). This is the first part of equation (21.43).
  4. In \(\tilde{\mat M}\) the block \((\eta_{\mathrm{j}}, \eta_{\mathrm{j}})\) equals \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat I\), \((\eta_{\mathrm{j}}, \eta_{\mathrm{l}})\) is \(\mat 0\) because \(\mat M_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\), and \((\mathrm a, \eta_{\mathrm{j}})\) is by the rule for a product of matrices by blocks (definition 1.99) \((\mat M_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}})\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), and \((\mathrm a, \mathrm a)\) is the sum of \(\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}}\) and the three terms of the theorem. The mass blocks are therefore not zero, because \(\mat M\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\) has no zeros in the interior. On the frame floor \(1\) moves by \(\tfrac12\) in the constraint mode and by \(1\) in the mode \(\vek\varphi^{(1)}\), and the product with the mass \(\bar m\) is the entry \(\tilde M_{12} = \tfrac12\) of equation (21.39). This is the second item.
  5. Let \(\mat T\) be the matrix that selects from the columns of the basis \(\mat\Psi'_{\mathrm{CB}}\) the first \(k_{\mathrm{j}}\) modes of each substructure and all constraint modes. Then \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}} = \mat\Psi'_{\mathrm{CB}}\,\mat T\), and \(\mat T\) has independent columns (they are columns of the identity). The reduced matrices are \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat T^\T\tilde{\mat K}'\mat T\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat T^\T\tilde{\mat M}'\mat T\) by lemma 1.102. This is the reduced pair \((\tilde{\mat K}', \tilde{\mat M}')\) with the Ritz matrix \(\mat T\). The pair \((\tilde{\mat K}', \tilde{\mat M}')\) of order \(m' = n_{\mathrm{a}} + k'_1 + \dots + k'_{\mathrm{J}}\) is symmetric and positive definite, because \(\mat\Psi'_{\mathrm{CB}}\) has independent columns by step 1. So theorem 10.59, item 1, for this pair gives \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \tilde\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, m\). For \(k_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) we have \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}} = \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}\), so the Guyan basis.
  6. For \(k_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{j}}\) for every \(j\) we have \(m = n_{\mathrm{a}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{j}} = n\). The matrix \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{CB}}\) is square with independent columns by step 1, so theorem 10.59, item 2, gives \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Source: CK section 17.6 (PDF p. 575-576): the form of the reduced matrices with zeros between constraint modes and fixed-interface modes (orthogonality 17.14 on PDF p. 555) and the mixed mass terms. GR section 6.9.2 (PDF p. 503): a basis with constraint and internal modes. The proof in steps is not from the sources. The upper bound is from theorem 10.59, which we do not prove again here.

The proof showed three things. The columns of the basis are independent (step 1), so its values are upper bounds. Its stiffness matrix splits into an interface part and a modal part, because the forces of the constraint modes act only at the interface (steps 2 and 3), which we saw in the frame. And no added mode raises any value (step 5). The assumption \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}} = \mat 0\) entered in step 2. It holds when the substructures share only interface coordinates, like floors \(1\) and \(3\), which no column joins. An element reaching from the interior of one substructure into the interior of another would give \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jl}} \ne \mat 0\): the constraint mode would then need forces in the interior too and the zeros in \(\tilde{\mat K}\) of equation (21.43) would vanish, while items 1, 3 and 4 would remain. The coordinates of such an element are therefore counted with the interface. But why not drop the constraint modes, since the fixed-interface modes are the true modes of the substructures? Because without them the interface cannot leave its rest, which the cantilever in the last subsection of this section shows.

21.5.4 Assembling the substructures

The figure shows where the sum comes from. Substructure \(1\) contributes a matrix that is nonzero only in the interface block, in its own internal block and in the couplings between them, substructure \(2\) likewise with its internal block. In the sum the two contributions add on the interface, while the internal blocks stay as they were. The plus sign between the contributions means addition, and the equals sign says that the result is the whole \(\mat K\).

Different groups compute the substructures, so each computation must be self-contained. The following lemma says that the reduced blocks are sums of contributions of substructures, each of which needs only its own part of the structure.

Lemma 21.25 Assembly of substructures (sestavljanje podstruktur)

Let \(\mat K = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K^{(j)}\) and \(\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat M^{(j)}\), where \(\mat K^{(j)}\) and \(\mat M^{(j)}\) are the stiffness and mass matrix of substructure \(j\) alone, written in the numbering of the whole structure (rows and columns outside \(\mathrm a\) and \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\) are zero). Then for the bases of definition 21.23:

  1. \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}} = \mat K^{(j)}_{\mathrm{jj}}\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}} = \mat K^{(j)}_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\) and the same for \(\mat M\). Therefore \(\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\mat\Phi_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) are computed from the matrices of substructure \(j\) alone.
  2. \(\mat S = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat S^{(j)}\) with \(\mat S^{(j)} = \mat K^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}} - \mat K_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}^{-1}\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{aa}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\tilde{\mat M}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}}\) with \(\tilde{\mat M}^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}} = \mat M^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}} + \mat M_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}} + \mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M_{\mathrm{jj}}\mat\Psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The blocks \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{a}\eta_\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\mat\Lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\) belong only to substructure \(j\).
Proof

We must show that every part of the expression of theorem 21.24 contains the data of one substructure.

  1. By theorem 7.13, \(\mat K\) is the sum of element terms \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), and an element of substructure \(j\) has nonzero rows and columns only in \(\mathrm a\) and \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\) (the substructures have no common internal coordinates). If we order the elements by substructures, \(\mat K = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K^{(j)}\). The block \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\) receives a contribution only from substructure \(j\), because the others have no rows \(\text{d}_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the same holds for \(\mat K_{\mathrm{j}\mathrm{a}}\). The same for \(\mat M\) by equation (12.50) or lemma 10.50.
  2. The interface block is \(\mat K_{\mathrm{aa}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat K^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}}\), each containing the elements that touch the interface. Inserted in \(\mat S\) of theorem 21.24, item 2, and since the blocks \(\mat K_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{j}}\), \(\mat K_{\mathrm{jj}}\) of step 1 are the contribution of substructure \(j\), \(\mat S\) splits by \(j\). In the same way \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{aa}}\) has the term \(\mat M_{\mathrm{aa}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\mat M^{(j)}_{\mathrm{aa}}\) and three terms with substructure \(j\) only.

Source: CK section 17.6.2 (PDF p. 576): "the component coupling matrix S is just the direct-stiffness assembly matrix". The proof is not from the source.

The lemma says what we mentioned at the end of the history: assembling is just adding on the interface coordinates, as with finite elements (theorem 7.13), only that now the "elements" are reduced substructures. When we change one substructure, only its own contribution changes, and the others stay as they were computed. We never solve the system of all coordinates at once.

21.5.5 The cantilever cut in the middle

Let us test on the cantilever of chapter 12: steel with \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(\rho A = 6{,}24\) kg/m, \(L = 1\) m, \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)} = 57{,}883\) 1/s as the unit of frequency. We take a mesh of \(16\) equal elements with consistent mass (lemma 12.43) and clamping at \(x = 0\), which gives \(n = 32\) free coordinates (displacement and rotation of nodes \(1\) to \(16\)). For comparison, the frequencies of this mesh are \(3{,}51602\), \(22{,}0346\), \(61{,}6997\) and \(120{,}920\ \omega_0\), the lowest values for infinitely fine elements are \(3{,}51602\), \(22{,}0345\), \(61{,}6972\) and \(120{,}902\ \omega_0\) (CK, table 14.1, PDF p. 476), and the difference is the error of the mesh, not of the method. We cut the cantilever in the middle, at node \(8\) (\(x = 0{,}5\) m), as figure 21.39 shows.

We draw the cantilever from the clamping at \(x = 0\) to the free end, substructure \(1\) is blue, substructure \(2\) orange. Between them is the interface at node \(8\) with the coordinates displacement and rotation of the node, so \(n_{\mathrm{a}} = 2\). The internal coordinates are \(n_1 = 14\) (nodes \(1\) to \(7\)) and \(n_2 = 16\) (nodes \(9\) to \(16\)). Both substructures have their own matrices. The first constraint mode is a unit displacement of the interface at zero rotation: substructure \(1\) adapts with a curve that has zero slope at the interface, and substructure \(2\) moves as a rigid body. The second constraint mode is a unit rotation of the interface: substructure \(1\) bends, substructure \(2\) turns like a rigid rod. The rigid motion shows at the end: for \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}1}\) the end has the same displacement \(1\) as the interface, and for \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}2}\) the displacement \(L/2\), which is the rotation \(1\) times the arm \(L/2\) from the interface to the end. The computation confirms it already at the first node past the interface: node \(9\) is \(h = 0{,}0625\) from the interface and has displacement \(1{,}0000\) for \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}1}\) and \(0{,}0625\) for \(\vek\psi_{\mathrm{c}2}\) (NumPy).

Why does substructure \(2\) move rigidly? By definition a constraint mode has no forces on the interior, and substructure \(2\) is clamped at the interface and has a free end, so it is statically determinate: the only thing that holds it is the interface, so it moves with it as if it were rigid. A rigid body has no deformation energy (theorem 10.34), so substructure \(2\) contributes nothing to the stiffness matrix, \(\mat S^{(2)} = \mat 0\) (NumPy: entries below \(10^{-11}\), rounding error). All the stiffness of the interface coordinates comes from substructure \(1\): \(\mat S = \mat S^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 96 & -24 \\ -24 & 8\end{pmatrix}\) in units \(EI = L = 1\). These are exactly the terms \(12EI/(L/2)^3 = 96\), \(-6EI/(L/2)^2 = -24\) and \(4EI/(L/2) = 8\) of lemma 12.4 for a single element of length \(L/2\), which is no coincidence: there is no load in the interior of substructure \(1\), so the static shape is a cubic parabola, which Hermite elements represent exactly (lemma 12.2), and eight elements are the same as one element.

The fixed-interface modes are \(\vek\varphi^{(1)}_1\) with the first shape of substructure \(1\), clamped at both ends (length \(L/2\)), and \(\vek\varphi^{(2)}_1\) with the first shape of substructure \(2\), clamped at the interface and free at the end. The lowest four frequencies of the fixed-interface modes are \(89{,}5007\), \(246{,}847\), \(484{,}760\) and \(804{,}425\ \omega_0\) for substructure \(1\) and \(14{,}0641\), \(88{,}1450\), \(246{,}939\) and \(484{,}691\ \omega_0\) for substructure \(2\) (NumPy). Substructure \(2\) is a cantilever of length \(L/2\) with eight elements, that is the cantilever of length \(L\) with eight elements with its length halved and the same cross-section. The frequency is proportional to \(\sqrt{EI/(\rho A L^4)}\) (unit \(\omega_0\)), so half the length gives a \(2^2 = 4\) times higher frequency: \(4 \cdot 3{,}51602 = 14{,}0641\ \omega_0\), where \(3{,}51602\ \omega_0\) is the first frequency of the cantilever of length \(L\) with eight elements (the same to six digits as with sixteen). Substructure \(1\) is clamped at both ends, so it is stiffer than a cantilever of the same length and has a higher first frequency, \(89{,}5007\ \omega_0\).

The reduced matrices at \(k = 1\) (one mode per substructure, \(m = 4\)) have the stiffness blocks between \(\vek q_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(\vek\eta\) zero by equation (21.43), in practice below \(10^{-10}\) (NumPy, rounding error), and \(\tilde{\mat M}_{\mathrm{a}\eta} = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}2938 & -0{,}5537 \\ -0{,}0316 & -0{,}2011\end{pmatrix}\) in units \(\rho A = L = 1\) is not zero. The sign of a column depends on the direction of the mode: \(\vek\varphi^{(1)}_1\) points upward in the figure, \(\vek\varphi^{(2)}_1\) downward, so the second column is negative. This is the same pattern as in the frame: the interface part and the modal part are separated by stiffness and coupled by mass.

21.5.7 What does not work: fixed-interface modes alone

Theorem 21.24 speaks about a basis with all the constraint modes. What if we omit them and keep only the fixed-interface modes, since these are the true modes of the substructures? Let us try on the cantilever with \(k = 3\) and \(k = 5\) (with \(6\) and \(10\) coordinates).

The axis of the cantilever, clamped at \(x = 0\), is drawn with the interface at node \(8\) (open circle). The true first mode is the gray dashed curve, which is already displaced at the interface by \(0{,}3395\) of the end displacement, and its end has displacement \(1\). The lowest Ritz shape from fixed-interface modes alone is orange: the left half stays on the axis, the right half bends. The frequency is the same at \(k = 3\) and \(k = 5\), \(14{,}0641\ \omega_0\), which is \(300{,}0\) % above the true value \(3{,}51602\ \omega_0\), or \(4{,}0000\) times as much (NumPy), and adding modes does not lower it. Theorem 10.59 guarantees only that the approximation is above the true value, and it is. Why is it so bad? All the columns have both interface coordinates equal to zero, so they describe a structure that is also clamped at node \(8\). A constraint, as described by corollary 10.45, does not lower the eigenvalues: the lowest eigenvalue is the smallest Rayleigh quotient among all allowed shapes, a constraint forbids some shapes, and the smallest value among fewer candidates cannot be lower. Here the constraint forbids exactly the true first mode, which moves the interface, so it raises the lowest value strongly. The structure, clamped also at \(x = L/2\), falls apart into two independent beams of length \(L/2\). The left one is clamped at both ends and has the lowest frequency \(89{,}5007\ \omega_0\) (substructure \(1\)), the right one is a cantilever with frequency \(14{,}0641\ \omega_0\), four times higher than \(3{,}51602\ \omega_0\), as we computed above (substructure \(2\)). The lower of the two, \(14{,}0641\ \omega_0\), is the lowest frequency of the model. The constraint modes are the columns that allow the interface to move.

The section has thus shown substructures with two kinds of modes. The constraint modes are the statics of the interface, the fixed-interface modes the own vibration of the interior, and together they form a Ritz approximation that is computable substructure by substructure and gives upper bounds and accurate frequencies when they are far enough below the lowest omitted frequency (figure 21.40). For the cantilever we reduced \(32\) coordinates to \(8\) with an error below \(0{,}18\) % at four frequencies, because the lowest omitted frequency is far enough away. The reduced eigenproblem of order \(8\) is small, and how to solve a large one that reduction has not shrunk enough is shown by the following sections, starting with the simplest procedure, which finds one eigenvalue. The coupled model also answers the question about the damping of the reduced model, which is in one of the later sections.

We do not treat substructures with attachment modes (načini pripetja), with free-interface modes (načini s prostim robom) and substructures with several levels (multilevel substructuring): these are in CK sections 17.3, 17.7 and 17.8 in this order (PDF p. 557-561, 577-581 and 582-588) and are not proved in this book.
