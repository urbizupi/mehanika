Chapter 18 Plates

18.10 Connections

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19. The chapter closed the promises of earlier chapters about plates and opened questions that later chapters continue. First we say where the chapter took its tools from, then where it goes next.

  • Deformation, stress and Hooke's law: the displacement field of the plate in section 18.1 is the two-dimensional version of the displacement field of the beam (theorem 5.15), the small strains from displacements are equations (1.71), and the stress resultants over the thickness are the moments and shear forces per unit length. Every layer of the plate is in plane stress (definition 11.36), so the plate rigidity contains \(E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and not \(E\) (VT, MK). The bending rigidity \(D\) thereby closed the promise of chapter 3, and the stress resultants over the thickness the promise of chapter 2. The orthotropic material (definition 3.32) and plates of several layers are only named in this chapter.
  • Beam and plate: the plate equation (theorem 18.12) is the two-dimensional version of the deflection equation (theorem 5.42). In cylindrical bending (section 18.4) it becomes the beam equation with \(D\) in place of \(EI\) per unit width. For steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) with \(h = 10\) mm, span \(L = 500\) mm and load \(q = 10\) kPa the deflection of a simply supported strip is \(0{,}4534\) mm, for a beam of the same width and section \(0{,}4982\) mm, the ratio is \(1 - \nu^2 = 0{,}91\). A narrow beam is not a strip of a plate, because its layers contract freely sideways, while a strip of a plate cannot, because the two neighbouring strips hold it, so it has the transverse moment \(M_{\mathrm{y}} = \nu M_{\mathrm{x}}\) (section 18.1).
  • Calculus of variations and boundary conditions: theorem 18.21 is the two-dimensional version of theorem 5.70, except that integration by parts in the plane is replaced by the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14), as chapter 5 promised. The boundary conditions of the plate in section 18.3 are essential and natural as in section 5.11.4, except that each edge has two of them. The replacement of the twisting moment \(M_{\mathrm{xy}}\) by two vertical forces and the corner force are an application of Saint-Venant's principle, promised by chapter 11: the edge load is replaced by a statically equivalent one (definition 11.48), which changes the field only near the edge.
  • Fourier series and polar coordinates: Navier's solution (theorem 18.30) is a double series of sines, each direction by lemma 13.10, and its convergence is guaranteed by Weierstrass's criterion (theorem 9.107). Circular plates (section 18.6) use the Laplace operator in polar coordinates from corollary 11.18 and turned the promise of chapter 11 into theorem 18.35.
  • Energy methods: theorem 18.46 is theorem 6.34 for the plate functional: the Ritz field has the least potential energy \(\Pi\) in the trial space and the work of the load on it is not larger than on the true solution. The frequency parameter of the clamped square with 1, 3, 6 and 10 terms is \(36{,}000\), \(36{,}000\), \(35{,}9888\) and \(35{,}9863\) and does not increase, as an upper bound would require. We do not prove this bound for the clamped plate, so the decrease is only an observation (section 18.7). Hamilton's principle (theorem 18.48) uses the same procedure as theorem 13.37, and the free vibrations of a simply supported square (\(a = 500\) mm, \(h = 10\) mm) have the frequencies \(190{,}62\) Hz (mode \((1, 1)\)), \(476{,}56\) Hz (double, modes \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 1)\)) and \(762{,}50\) Hz (mode \((2, 2)\)) (DSK). A shortened model with fewer modes is the subject of chapter 21, damping of plates of chapter 20 (DSK). Buckling of a compressed plate (theorem 18.53) uses the work of in-plane forces from lemma 8.149 in two directions. Unlike the column (theorem 8.150), a longer plate is not weaker: the coefficient \(k\) in the critical force \(N_{\mathrm{cr}} = k\,\pi^2 D/b^2\), where \(b\) is the width of the compressed edge, is at least 4 and returns to 4 at every integer ratio \(a/b\) (equation (18.60), MK).
  • Timoshenko beam and Mindlin plate: theorem 18.56 is theorem 5.91 in two dimensions with the shear factor \(k = 5/6\) of definition 5.89. As for the beam (section 5.15), the boundary between the two theories is computed and not guessed, so a paragraph below follows (MK, AK).
  • Elements: for plates the idea of chapter 12 has to be continued. The Hermite functions of definition 12.1 give in one dimension a continuous displacement and rotation. In two dimensions the Kirchhoff energy requires a continuous slope in the direction of the normal across an edge, without a jump (lemma 18.62, point 1). The bicubic Hermite rectangle, a product of one-dimensional Hermite functions, has it, while a rectangle with only two values of the slope per edge does not (points 2 and 3). The Mindlin element has only the displacement and two rotations as unknowns, so \(C^0\) is enough and the shear acts as a penalty in two directions, as in lemma 12.35 (lemma 18.66). With a mesh of a quarter of the plate with 16 elements per side and \(a/h = 100\), full integration gives 0,4154 of the true Mindlin deflection and selective integration 0,9997 (theorem 18.67). Curved quadrilaterals and the patch test are explained in chapter 15 (AK). In the quarter of the square the symmetry conditions are already used, how to use them in general and how to join plates to beams is explained in chapter 19 (AK).
  • Shells: a shell is a plate with a curved middle surface, its energy is the sum of a membrane part, proportional to \(h\), and a bending part, proportional to \(h^3\), with the same rigidity \(D\) as the plate (MK, AK, chapter 22). For a plate the membrane part is separate, because the middle surface is flat and the in-plane part is solved by chapter 11.
  • Foundation, heat and nonlinearity: a plate on an elastic foundation gets an extra term of the foundation stiffness in its equation, like a beam on the springs of the foundation (chapter 25). Bending caused by a change of temperature through the thickness is only named, the temperature field in a plate is given by chapter 14. For deflections comparable to \(h\) the middle plane between two edges that cannot move sideways stretches, because the curve is longer than the chord. So a term nonlinear in \(w\) appears in the strain, which EP develops for the beam in section 2.5.4 (p. 80) and calls von Kármán's. This chapter does not compute plates with it. For our plate \(w/h = 0{,}014\), below the bound \(|w| \le h/5\) of idealization 2 of small deflections (section 18.1). How to choose a model when this idealization fails is a question of modelling (AK, chapter 17).

How far below the bound our plate is, figure 18.103 shows to scale. The plate is \(h = 10\) mm thick, the bound of idealization 2 lies \(h/5 = 2\) mm below the middle plane. The deflection at the centre is \(0{,}1415\) mm (section 18.5) and on the left it can hardly be told apart from the middle plane. In the enlargement the two lengths stand side by side: the bound is \(14\) times longer than the deflection. The ratio of the stretch of the middle plane to the bending strain is \(w/(2h)\) (section 18.1), so it is proportional to the deflection. The bars therefore also show this share: at the bound it is \(10\) %, for our plate \(0{,}7\) %.

When does which theory apply? Section 18.8.5 answered this question with figure 18.90, where the ratio of the deflections of the simply supported square is a parabola above 1. Thin plates are squeezed towards 1 there and their excess cannot be seen. So we draw the same excess once more on a logarithmic scale in figure 18.105. This view adds three things: the excess of thin plates, the exponent \(2\) as the slope of a line, and the comparison with the beam.

Why is the excess proportional to \((h/a)^2\)? By theorem 18.60 the Mindlin deflection is the Kirchhoff deflection plus the addition \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(kGh)\). Since in this solution the rotation of the normal is \(\vek\theta = \nabla w_{\mathrm{K}}\), the shear angle is \(\vek\gamma = \nabla w_{\mathrm{M}} - \vek\theta = \nabla\big(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(kGh)\big)\): the addition is the deflection that the shear angle gathers from the edge to the centre. The moment \(M_{\mathrm{K}}\) is Marcus's moment, which along the midline of the square rises from zero at the edge to its largest value at the centre. The excess is the ratio of the addition to the Kirchhoff deflection, and each of the three factors depends on the thickness differently:

  • \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0{,}07367\,q a^2\) at the centre does not depend on \(h\), because it is fixed by the equation \(\nabla^2 M_{\mathrm{K}} = -q\) with \(M_{\mathrm{K}} = 0\) on the edge alone (theorem 18.60, point 1), in which the thickness does not appear,
  • the shear stiffness \(kGh\), which links the shear force to the shear angle, grows like \(h\), because a strip of unit width carries the shear force through a cross-section of area \(h\),
  • the bending rigidity \(D = E h^3/(12(1 - \nu^2))\) (definition 18.6) grows like \(h^3\), so the Kirchhoff deflection at the centre \(w_{\mathrm{K}} = 0{,}004062\,q a^4/D\) is proportional to \(h^{-3}\).

We add the three dependences on figure 18.104. The excess is \(M_{\mathrm{K}}/(kGh\,w_{\mathrm{K}})\), so the exponent of a factor in the numerator is added and that of a factor in the denominator subtracted. For the thickness we start with \(M_{\mathrm{K}} \propto h^0\) at zero, the division by \(kGh \propto h\) takes us down to \(-1\), the division by \(w_{\mathrm{K}} \propto h^{-3}\) lifts us by \(3\), the sum is \(2\). For the side \(M_{\mathrm{K}} \propto a^2\), \(kGh\) does not depend on \(a\), the division by \(w_{\mathrm{K}} \propto a^4\) subtracts \(4\), the sum is \(-2\). So the excess is proportional to \(h^2 a^{-2} = (h/a)^2\). The constant comes from the two Navier coefficients:

\[\frac{w_{\mathrm{M}}}{w_{\mathrm{K}}} - 1 = \frac{M_{\mathrm{K}}}{kGh\,w_{\mathrm{K}}} = \frac{0{,}07367}{0{,}004062}\cdot\frac{D}{kGh\,a^2} = 18{,}135\cdot\frac{D}{kGh\,a^2} .\]

With \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\) we have \(D/(kGh) = h^2/(6k(1 - \nu))\). For steel (\(k = 5/6\), \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) \(6k(1 - \nu) = 3{,}5\) and \(18{,}135/3{,}5 = 5{,}1815\), so

\[\frac{w_{\mathrm{M}}}{w_{\mathrm{K}}} - 1 = 5{,}1815\,\left(\frac{h}{a}\right)^2 \tag{18.80}\]

The number \(5{,}1815\) carries three assumptions, each with a different effect. The factors \(0{,}07367\) and \(0{,}004062\) are Navier's series for the square with a hard simple support and change for another shape or support. Poisson's ratio enters only through \(D/(kGh)\): at \(\nu = 0{,}25\) the number is \(4{,}836\) (section 18.8). The shear factor \(k = 5/6\) (definition 5.89) accounts for the shear stress not being uniform over the thickness but parabolic. Without it (\(k = 1\)) the number would be \(4{,}318\), 17 % smaller, while the 1 % limit would move only from \(a/h = 22{,}8\) to \(20{,}8\), because the limit is proportional to the square root of the number. All three assumptions only move the line up or down, they do not change its slope \(-2\).

Equation (18.80) holds at every \(a/h\). Since the logarithm of the excess is \(\log 5{,}1815 - 2\log(a/h)\), in the logarithmic figure 18.105 it is a single line of slope \(-2\). We build the figure in parts.

First we draw the axes: the ratio \(a/h\) from 3 to 200 horizontally, the excess in percent from 0,01 to 100 vertically, both logarithmic, because both quantities change over several powers of ten. In them equation (18.80) is a line. Its slope is shown by the triangle: from \(a/h = 20\) to \(200\) we go one decade to the right, while the excess falls from 1,30 % to 0,013 %, two decades down. This is the exponent \(2\) in \((h/a)^2\). On the line we mark the points from section 18.8: at \(a/h = 5\) the excess is 20,7 %, at 10 it is 5,18 %, at 20 it is 1,30 % and at 100 it is 0,052 %. Two limits show where the excess reaches 5 % and 1 %, at \(a/h = 10{,}2\) and \(22{,}8\) (from the equation, \(\sqrt{5{,}1815/0{,}05}\) and \(\sqrt{5{,}1815/0{,}01}\)). Our plate has \(a/h = 50\) and an excess of 0,21 %, so Kirchhoff's theory suffices for it. For \(a/h\) below about 10, so for a thicker plate, Mindlin's theory changes the deflection by more than 5 % and is needed. In the linear figure 18.90 the excess of our plate would be only \(0{,}0021\) above 1 on an axis from 1 to 1,8, here we can read it.

For comparison we also draw the cantilever with a force at the free end from section 5.15. There the shear part of the deflection was \((1 + \nu)/(2k)\,(h/L)^2 = 0{,}78\,(h/L)^2\) of the bending part, so the horizontal axis now also counts \(L/h\). The line of the cantilever is parallel to the line of the plate, because the excess has the same cause: the shear stiffness grows like \(h\), the bending stiffness like \(h^3\). It lies lower because it has another constant, and this constant depends on the load, the support and the shape. So the cantilever reaches 1 % already at \(L/h = 8{,}8\), the plate only at \(a/h = 22{,}8\). The slope is therefore general, the constant holds for one case only.

The line of the plate holds only for the simply supported square with a hard support (\(\theta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\)). For a clamped circular plate the hypotheses of theorem 18.60 do not hold (the edge is not a polygon and is not simply supported), \(M_{\mathrm{K}} \ne 0\) on the edge (right curve in figure 18.86) and the relation fails. At \(2R/h = 5\), where \(R\) is the radius of the plate, the true ratio \(w_{\mathrm{M}}/w_{\mathrm{K}}\) at the centre is 1,731, while Marcus's equation gives only 1,366, half the correction (section 18.8.4). The difference between the theories therefore depends on the support, not only on \(a/h\).

Limits of the chapter: all proved results are for an isotropic linearly elastic plate of small deflection. Cited and not proved are Lévy's solutions (EP §7.2.5, p. 449-454), buckling of a circular plate (EP §7.2.2.2, p. 411-414), the conforming and nonconforming Kirchhoff elements, the DKT element, the patch test, von Kármán's theory and the existence of a solution of the boundary problem of the plate. The completeness of the double family of sines for continuously differentiable functions vanishing on the edge is, however, proved: corollary 13.13 of chapter 13, applied in each variable separately with theorem 4.9 (theorem 18.50, step 5). For the clamped square we have only Ritz approximations, not exact values. Shells are in chapter 22, the plate on a foundation in chapter 25, the temperature field in chapter 14 and mesh convergence in chapter 17.
