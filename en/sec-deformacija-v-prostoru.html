Chapter 1 Strain

1.17 Strain in space

In section 1.1 we announced that three normal and three shear strains together describe completely how the material close to a point is deformed, as long as the deformation is small. At the end of section 1.6.2 we found what we need to derive this: derivatives of functions of three variables, and a way of writing that gives the strain of a fibre for every direction. We now have both. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, the difference of the displacements of the ends of a short fibre is almost exactly a sum of products of partial derivatives with the components of the fibre. In section 1.16 we wrote such sums as the product of a matrix and a vector.

The goal of this section is to calculate, from the displacement field, the strain of a short fibre in any direction through any interior point of a body. First we calculate it exactly, without assuming that the strains are small. Then we look at what remains when the partial derivatives of the displacements are small, as in steel structures. This gives the six promised numbers, and we say what each of them measures. At the end we separate the part of the change in which the material only turns, and we show with an example when the simplified measure fails. Finally we meet a measure that compares the change of the length of a fibre with its length after loading.

The physical model is the same as before. A body is a continuum (definition 1.1), its change of shape is described by the displacement field (definition 1.23), and we measure the stretching of the material close to a point with short fibres (definition 1.24), which we shorten towards zero, just as we shortened a piece of the bar. We make two assumptions.

  1. The displacement field is continuously differentiable (definition 1.90). Physically this means that the body has no cracks, that no layer slides along its neighbour and that the strain nowhere jumps, as it would at the joint of two different materials (section 1.15.6). We need the assumption because without it we may not use the theorem on the change of a field over a short step. At a crack, or at a glued joint that gives way, the results of this section do not hold. In a body made of two materials we use them in each material separately.
  2. The point \(P\) at which we calculate is an interior point of the body: there is still material around it in all directions, up to some distance \(r > 0\). In this way every fibre from \(P\) shorter than \(r\) stays in the body.

We do not need the assumption that the strains are small in the first part of the section. We introduce it only in section 1.17.3, and there we say how small they must be.

We will test everything on two bodies we already know. The first is a steel bar 10 m long, with a round cross section 10 mm in diameter, hanging under its own weight. It is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: only the point \(O\), the centre of the top end, is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down, the axes \(y\) and \(z\) lie horizontally, and the displacement field is ((1.18))

\[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{x}} &= a\,(2Lx - x^2) - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + z^2) , \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,y , \\ u_{\mathrm{z}} &= -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x)\,z , \end{aligned}\]

where \(L = 10\) m and \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). We will calculate at the point \(P\) with \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 5\) m, \(y_{\mathrm{P}} = 4\) mm and \(z_{\mathrm{P}} = 0\), which lies 5 m below the point \(O\) and 4 mm from the axis of the bar. The second body is the sheared cube of section 1.6.2: a steel cube with an edge of 10 mm, glued to a table by its bottom face, whose top face we pushed sideways by 10 µm. The axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(z\) up, and the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) ((1.16)).

1.17.1 The displacement gradient and the deformation gradient

We start with a fibre. Let a fibre from the interior point \(P\) have the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) and reach to the point \(Q\) with \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\), as in section 1.7.3. The number \(h\) may be positive or negative, the length of the fibre is \(|h|\), and we take \(0 < |h| < r\), so that the fibre stays in the body. After loading the fibre has, by (1.15), the vector

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = h\,\vek n + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) .\]

In section 1.7.3 we wrote the difference of displacements component by component. For the component \(x\), by (1.24) with the components of the step \(\Delta x = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta y = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta z = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\),

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P) = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}(P)\,h\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y}(P)\,h\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z}(P)\,h\,n_{\mathrm{z}} + R_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

and the same for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and for \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) with the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\). Theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step tells us that for a continuously differentiable field each of the three remainders is small compared with the length of the fibre: for every allowed error \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\), and the same for \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\).

The three sums contain nine partial derivatives at the point \(P\). Each sum is the product of one row of partial derivatives with the components of the vector \(h\,\vek n\), so it is exactly one component of the product of a matrix and a vector ((1.38)). So we arrange the partial derivatives in a matrix in which the row says which component of the displacement we take, and the column the coordinate with respect to which we differentiate. This is the table of section 1.7.2, written as a matrix.

Definition 1.109 Displacement gradient (gradient pomikov)

Let the displacement field \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) have all nine partial derivatives at an interior point \(P\) of a body. The displacement gradient (gradient pomikov) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} \end{bmatrix} , \tag{1.44}\]

in which all partial derivatives are taken at the point \(P\). Its entry in row \(\ix{i}\) and column \(\ix{j}\) is \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(P)\). The indices \(\ix{i}\) and \(\ix{j}\) run over \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) means the coordinate named \(\ix{j}\): \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\).

Source: BOWER §2.1.2 (PDF p. 41), SADD (2.1.5) (PDF p. 40). CM (3.3.8) (PDF p. 120) writes \(\nabla_0\vek u\), which is the transpose of the matrix \(\mat H\).

Let us try the definition on both bodies. For the hanging bar we calculated all nine partial derivatives at the point \(P\) in section 1.7.2. We arrange them in a matrix:

\[\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} & -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & 0 \\ 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} .\]

For the sheared cube \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\) depends only on \(z\), and the components \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero everywhere. So the only partial derivative different from zero is \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = 0{,}001\), in row \(x\) and column \(z\), and this at every point of the cube:

\[\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

With the displacement gradient the three equations for the components of the difference of displacements become one. By (1.38) the component \(\ix{i}\) of the product \(\mat H\,(h\,\vek n)\) is the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,h\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\), and this is exactly the sum in the equation for \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\). So

\[\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) = \mat H\,(h\,\vek n) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}}) ,\]

and the vector of the fibre after loading is

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = h\,\vek n + \mat H\,(h\,\vek n) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

We can combine the first two terms. The identity matrix \(\mat I\) leaves every vector as it is (lemma 1.106 on multiplication by the identity matrix), so \(h\,\vek n = \mat I\,(h\,\vek n)\). By point 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, the sum \(\mat I\,(h\,\vek n) + \mat H\,(h\,\vek n)\) is the product of the matrix \(\mat I + \mat H\) with the vector \(h\,\vek n\). So the matrix \(\mat I + \mat H\) gets its own name.

Definition 1.110 Deformation gradient (gradient deformacije)

Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient at an interior point \(P\) of a body and \(\mat I\) the identity matrix. The deformation gradient (gradient deformacije) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} & 1 + \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y} & 1 + \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} \end{bmatrix} , \tag{1.45}\]

with the entries \(F_{\mathrm{ij}} = I_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ij}}\), where \(I_{\mathrm{ij}} = 1\) for \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) for \(\ix{i} \neq \ix{j}\).

Source: CM (3.3.1) (PDF p. 118), (3.3.4) (PDF p. 119) and (3.3.8) (PDF p. 120), BOWER §2.1.2 (PDF p. 41).

Why the name deformation gradient? In the books the gradient of a function means all its partial derivatives at a point, collected together (CM (2.4.26), PDF p. 74). The matrix \(\mat H\) collects the partial derivatives of the displacement, so it is the displacement gradient. The matrix \(\mat F\) collects the partial derivatives of the position of a point after loading, whose coordinates are \(x + u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(y + u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(z + u_{\mathrm{z}}\). For example \(F_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial(x + u_{\mathrm{x}})/\partial x = 1 + \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), because the coordinate \(x\) changes with \(x\) at the rate \(1\), and \(F_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial(x + u_{\mathrm{x}})/\partial y = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y\), because \(x\) does not change when we change only \(y\) (CM (3.3.8), PDF p. 120). The positions of the points after loading describe the deformed body, hence the name. What \(\mat F\) tells us about a short fibre is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 1.111 A short fibre after loading (kratko vlakno po obremenitvi)

Let the displacement field of a body be continuously differentiable, \(P\) an interior point of the body, \(r > 0\) a number such that all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\) are interior points of the body (such an \(r\) exists by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient at \(P\) and \(\vek n\) a unit vector. For a number \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) let a fibre reach from \(P\) to the point \(Q\) with \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). Then the vector of the fibre after loading is

\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \mat F\,(h\,\vek n) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}}) , \tag{1.46}\]

where \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the remainders of (1.24) for the step \(h\,\vek n\). For every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le \eta\,|h|\), \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) and \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Proof

We must show the equation (1.46) and the bounds for the remainders.

  1. By (1.15), \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) = h\,\vek n + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big)\).
  2. By (1.24), written for the step with the components \(h\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\), the component \(\ix{i}\) of the difference of displacements is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,h\,n_{\mathrm{j}} + R_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(R_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the remainder \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(R_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. By (1.38) the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,h\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the component \(\ix{i}\) of the product \(\mat H\,(h\,\vek n)\). So \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) = \mat H\,(h\,\vek n) + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  4. By lemma 1.106 on multiplication by the identity matrix, \(h\,\vek n = \mat I\,(h\,\vek n)\).
  5. By point 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, used for the column \(h\,\vek n\), \(\mat I\,(h\,\vek n) + \mat H\,(h\,\vek n) = (\mat I + \mat H)(h\,\vek n) = \mat F\,(h\,\vek n)\).
  6. Steps 1, 3, 4 and 5 give (1.46).
  7. The field is continuously differentiable, so each component of the displacement has partial derivatives at all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\), and they are continuous at \(P\). By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step, for a given \(\eta > 0\) there are numbers \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}} > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \eta\,|h|\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta_{\mathrm{z}}\). The step \(h\,\vek n\) has the length \(|h|\), so \(|h|\) here is exactly the length of the step in the theorem.
  8. For \(\delta\) we take the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(r\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) all three bounds hold at once.

Source: CM (3.3.1) (PDF p. 118) and BOWER §2.1.5 (PDF p. 48) write the equation for an infinitesimally short fibre, without a remainder. The remainders and their bounds are not from a source, they follow from the theorem on the change of a field over a short step.

The lemma tells us what the deformation gradient means. The vector of a short fibre after loading is almost exactly \(\mat F\) times the vector of the same fibre before loading (figure 1.131). The difference is the remainders, which for a short fibre are small compared with its length. For the bar it was the same with a single number. A piece of length \(\Delta x\) was, after loading, about \((1 + u'(x))\,\Delta x\) long, because it got longer by \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) \approx u'(x)\,\Delta x\). In space the number \(1 + u'(x)\) is replaced by the matrix \(\mat I + \mat H\), because a fibre can not only get longer but also tilt.

What does a single entry of the matrix \(\mat F\) tell us? Take a fibre in the direction of the axis \(x\), with \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0)\). By (1.38), \(\mat F\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the first column of the matrix \(\mat F\):

\[\mat F\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \Big(1 + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big) .\]

So the end of a fibre of length \(h\) along the axis \(x\) moves relative to \(P\), up to the remainder, by \(h\,\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) in the direction of the fibre, and by \(h\,\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) and \(h\,\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\) in the direction of the axis \(z\), that is, sideways. The first shift makes the fibre longer, the other two tilt it. The second and third columns say the same for the fibres in the directions of the axes \(y\) and \(z\) (figure 1.132). So the entries on the diagonal of the matrix \(\mat H\) describe how the fibres along the axes get longer, and the entries off the diagonal how they tilt. We will need this when we explain what the components of strain mean.

For the sheared cube the deformation gradient is

\[\mat F = \mat I + \mat H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The first column is \((1, 0, 0)\): the fibres along the axis \(x\) do not change, just as the bottom edge \(AB\) did not change. The third column has the entries \(0{,}001\), \(0\) and \(1\): the fibres along the axis \(z\) tilt towards the axis \(x\), their end moves sideways by a thousandth of the length of the fibre, as for the vertical edge \(AD\). The field of the cube has one more special feature. The difference of the displacements of two points of the cube has the components \(0{,}001\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}})\), \(0\) and \(0\), which is exactly \(\mat H\,\overrightarrow{PQ}\). So there are no remainders, and every fibre of the cube, not only a short one, has the vector \(\mat F\,\overrightarrow{PQ}\) after loading. For the vertical edge \(AD\) with \(\overrightarrow{AD} = (0, 0, 10\,000)\) in micrometres,

\[\mat F\,\overrightarrow{AD} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 10\,000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}001 \cdot 10\,000 \\ 0 \\ 10\,000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \\ 10\,000 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

which is the vector after loading in the table of section 1.6.2.

CM also writes the deformation gradient as \(\mat F\), denotes the positions of points before loading by \(\vek X\) and writes \(d\vek x = \mat F\cdot d\vek X\) (CM (3.3.1), PDF p. 118).

1.17.2 The exact strain of a fibre

From the vector of a fibre after loading we want to calculate its length and its strain. For the sheared cube the square root in the length was in the way, and we got rid of it by comparing the squares of the lengths instead of the lengths ((1.17)). We do the same here. The square of the length of a vector is the sum of the squares of its components ((1.10)) and contains no root.

First let us see what we get if we leave out the remainders in (1.46). That we may do so for ever shorter fibres we will show in the theorem below. Without the remainders the fibre has the vector \(\mat F\,(h\,\vek n) = h\,\mat F\,\vek n\) after loading (lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix).

  1. The square of the length after loading is \(L_1^2 \approx |h\,\mat F\,\vek n|^2 = h^2\,|\mat F\,\vek n|^2\). This is because every component of the vector \(h\,\mat F\,\vek n\) is \(h\) times a component of the vector \(\mat F\,\vek n\), so its square is \(h^2\) times the square.
  2. The component \(\ix{i}\) of the vector \(\mat F\,\vek n\) is, by (1.38), the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\). So by (1.10)
    \[|\mat F\,\vek n|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\Big)^2 .\]
  3. We write each square as the product of two equal sums. In the second sum we write the letter \(\ix{k}\) instead of \(\ix{j}\), to tell the terms of the two sums apart. We multiply every term of the first sum by every term of the second, just as in section 1.6.2 we multiplied out the product \((1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \varepsilon)\):
    \[\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\Big)^2 = \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\Big)\Big(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} F_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,F_{\mathrm{ik}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,n_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
  4. We sum over \(\ix{i}\). The sum has finitely many terms, so we may rearrange them as we like. We collect the terms with the same \(\ix{j}\) and \(\ix{k}\) and take the factors \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) out:
    \[|\mat F\,\vek n|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,F_{\mathrm{ik}}\Big) n_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
  5. In the transposed matrix \(\mat F^\T\) the entry in row \(\ix{j}\) and column \(\ix{i}\) is \(F_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 1.100). So the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,F_{\mathrm{ik}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} (\mat F^\T)_{\mathrm{ji}}\,F_{\mathrm{ik}}\) is, by the definition of the matrix product 1.98, the entry in row \(\ix{j}\) and column \(\ix{k}\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\):
    \[|\mat F\,\vek n|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{jk}}\,n_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek n^\T\,\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n .\]
    The last expression is short for the double sum, as in section 1.16: the row \(\vek n^\T\), multiplied by the matrix and then by the column \(\vek n\), gives one number.
  6. The square of the length before loading is \(L_0^2 = h^2\). The number \(1 = n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\), the square of the length of the unit vector, we write in the same way with the identity matrix, \(1 = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{k}} n_{\mathrm{j}}\,I_{\mathrm{jk}}\,n_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek n^\T\mat I\,\vek n\). In the double sum only the terms with \(\ix{j} = \ix{k}\) remain, because \(I_{\mathrm{jk}} = 0\) for \(\ix{j} \neq \ix{k}\).
  7. We divide the relative change of the square of the length by \(2\), as in section 1.6.2, and subtract the two double sums term by term:
    \[\frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_0^2} \approx \frac{h^2\,\vek n^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n - h^2\,\vek n^\T\mat I\,\vek n}{2h^2} = \vek n^\T\,\tfrac12\big(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I\big)\,\vek n .\]

On the right stands a single matrix, which depends only on the displacement field at the point \(P\). The direction of the fibre appears only in the vector \(\vek n\). This matrix has its own name.

Definition 1.112 Green-Lagrange strain tensor (Green-Lagrangeov tenzor deformacij)

Let \(\mat F\) be the deformation gradient at an interior point \(P\) of a body. The Green-Lagrange strain tensor (Green-Lagrangeov tenzor deformacij) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\mat E = \tfrac12\big(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I\big) . \tag{1.47}\]

Source: CM (3.4.10), (3.4.11) (PDF p. 129), BOWER §2.1.5 (PDF p. 47).

What kind of object is \(\mat E\)? It is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix, calculated from the deformation gradient at one point. It is symmetric (definition 1.102). This is because the entry \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{jk}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{ij}}\,F_{\mathrm{ik}}\) equals the entry \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} F_{\mathrm{ik}}\,F_{\mathrm{ij}}\), since the order of the factors in a product does not matter, and the identity matrix is symmetric too. So \(\mat E\) has at most six different entries: three on the diagonal, \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(E_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{zz}}\), and three above it, \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(E_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{yz}}\), which equal the entries below it. Books call such a matrix a tensor (tenzor). What this word means we will say in chapter 2, when we see how the components change when we turn the axes. Until then the Green-Lagrange strain tensor is for us a matrix of six numbers in a chosen coordinate system. CM also calls it the Green strain tensor (CM, PDF p. 129).

In practice we do not know \(\mat F\) directly, but the displacement field, and from it the matrix \(\mat H\). Let us write \(\mat E\) with \(\mat H\).

Lemma 1.113 Green-Lagrange strain from the displacement gradient (Green-Lagrangeov tenzor iz gradienta pomikov)

Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient and \(\mat E\) the Green-Lagrange strain tensor at an interior point \(P\) of a body. Then

\[\mat E = \tfrac12\big(\mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H\big) , \qquad E_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac12\Big(H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\Big) ,\]

written out, with all partial derivatives at the point \(P\),

\[\begin{aligned} E_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac12\bigg[\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big)^2\bigg] , \\ E_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac12\bigg[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}\bigg] . \end{aligned} \tag{1.48}\]

The entries \(E_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{zz}}\) follow from \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and the entries \(E_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{yz}}\) from \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\), when we rename the axes. The remaining three are \(E_{\mathrm{yx}} = E_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(E_{\mathrm{zx}} = E_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{zy}} = E_{\mathrm{yz}}\).

Proof

We must show the form with entries, from which the other two follow.

  1. By the definitions of the transposed matrix and of the matrix product, \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} F_{\mathrm{ki}}\,F_{\mathrm{kj}}\).
  2. We substitute \(F_{\mathrm{ki}} = I_{\mathrm{ki}} + H_{\mathrm{ki}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{kj}} = I_{\mathrm{kj}} + H_{\mathrm{kj}}\) ((1.45)) and multiply out the brackets term by term:
    \[(\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} I_{\mathrm{ki}}\,I_{\mathrm{kj}} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} I_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,I_{\mathrm{kj}} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}} .\]
  3. In the first sum \(I_{\mathrm{ki}}\) is different from zero only for \(\ix{k} = \ix{i}\), so the sum equals \(I_{\mathrm{ii}}\,I_{\mathrm{ij}} = I_{\mathrm{ij}}\). In the second sum only the term with \(\ix{k} = \ix{i}\) remains, which is \(H_{\mathrm{ij}}\). In the third sum only the term with \(\ix{k} = \ix{j}\) remains, which is \(H_{\mathrm{ji}}\).
  4. So \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{ij}} - I_{\mathrm{ij}} = H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\), and by (1.47) \(E_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is half of this. This is the form with entries.
  5. The entry \(H_{\mathrm{ji}}\) is the entry \((\mat H^\T)_{\mathrm{ij}}\), and the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\) is, by step 1 written for \(\mat H\), the entry \((\mat H^\T\mat H)_{\mathrm{ij}}\). Since the matrices agree in all entries, \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H)\).
  6. For \(\ix{i} = \ix{j} = x\) we have \(H_{\mathrm{xx}} + H_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2\,\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\), and the sum is \(H_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + H_{\mathrm{yx}}^2 + H_{\mathrm{zx}}^2\). Substituting \(H_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\), \(H_{\mathrm{yx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) and \(H_{\mathrm{zx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\) gives \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\).
  7. For \(\ix{i} = x\) and \(\ix{j} = y\) the sum is \(H_{\mathrm{xx}}\,H_{\mathrm{xy}} + H_{\mathrm{yx}}\,H_{\mathrm{yy}} + H_{\mathrm{zx}}\,H_{\mathrm{zy}}\). With the partial derivatives substituted we get \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
  8. The formula for \(E_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the same for all pairs of indices, so we get the other entries by renaming the axes. Step 4 shows that \(E_{\mathrm{ij}}\) does not change when \(\ix{i}\) and \(\ix{j}\) are swapped.

Source: CM (3.4.11) (PDF p. 129), (3.4.12) and (3.4.13) (PDF p. 130), BOWER §2.1.5 (PDF p. 47). CM writes the displacement gradient transposed and the coordinates before loading as \(X_1\), \(X_2\), \(X_3\).

So far we have left out the remainders. Let us show that this is justified for ever shorter fibres. The relative change of the square of the length approaches the number \(\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\), and the strain of the fibre approaches a number calculated from it.

Theorem 1.114 Strain of a short fibre (deformacija kratkega vlakna)

Let the displacement field of a body be continuously differentiable, \(P\) an interior point of the body, \(r > 0\) a number such that all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\) are interior points of the body (such an \(r\) exists by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient and \(\mat E\) the Green-Lagrange strain tensor at \(P\), and \(\vek n\) a unit vector. For a number \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) let a fibre reach from \(P\) to the point \(Q\) with \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). Its length before loading is \(L_0 = |h|\), and after loading \(L_1 = |\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}|\). Then:

  1. the relative change of the square of the length, divided by \(2\), approaches the number \(\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\),
    \[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_0^2} = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n , \tag{1.49}\]
  2. if \(\mat F\,\vek n\) is not the zero vector, the strain of the fibre approaches the number
    \[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{L_1 - L_0}{L_0} = \sqrt{1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n} - 1 . \tag{1.50}\]
Proof

We first prove item 1, then item 2 from it.

1. The square of the length. We write the definition of the limit 1.5 for the function \(g(h) = (L_1^2 - L_0^2)/(2L_0^2)\), which is defined for \(0 < |h| < r\), and for the number \(\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. By lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading, \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = h\,\mat F\,\vek n + (R_{\mathrm{x}}, R_{\mathrm{y}}, R_{\mathrm{z}})\), where we wrote \(\mat F\,(h\,\vek n) = h\,\mat F\,\vek n\) by lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix. We write \(\vek a = \mat F\,\vek n\) and \(\vek q = (R_{\mathrm{x}}/h,\ R_{\mathrm{y}}/h,\ R_{\mathrm{z}}/h)\). The vector \(\vek a\) does not depend on \(h\), the vector \(\vek q\) does. Then \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = h\,(\vek a + \vek q)\).
  2. The squares of the lengths are \(L_1^2 = h^2\,|\vek a + \vek q|^2\) and \(L_0^2 = h^2\), so \(L_1^2/L_0^2 = |\vek a + \vek q|^2\).
  3. We write the square of the length component by component ((1.10)), and each square of a sum as \((a_{\mathrm{i}} + q_{\mathrm{i}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{i}}^2 + 2a_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}} + q_{\mathrm{i}}^2\):
    \[|\vek a + \vek q|^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}^2 + 2\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} q_{\mathrm{i}}^2 .\]
  4. The first sum is \(|\mat F\,\vek n|^2 = \vek n^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n\), as we calculated before definition 1.112, and \(1 = \vek n^\T\mat I\,\vek n\). So \(\tfrac12\big(|\vek a|^2 - 1\big) = \vek n^\T\,\tfrac12(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I)\,\vek n = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\).
  5. From steps 2 to 4,
    \[g(h) = \frac12\Big(\frac{L_1^2}{L_0^2} - 1\Big) = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}} + \frac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}} q_{\mathrm{i}}^2 .\]
    So we must bound the difference \(g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}} + \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}} q_{\mathrm{i}}^2\).
  6. Let \(A = |a_{\mathrm{x}}| + |a_{\mathrm{y}}| + |a_{\mathrm{z}}|\). Suppose that every component of the vector \(\vek q\) is at most \(\mu\) in absolute value, where \(0 < \mu \le 1\). The absolute value of a product is the product of the absolute values, and by the triangle inequality for numbers, used twice (lemma 1.6, items 2 and 1), \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\,q_{\mathrm{i}}\big| \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}} |a_{\mathrm{i}}|\,|q_{\mathrm{i}}| \le A\,\mu\). Each \(q_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) is at most \(\mu^2\), and this is at most \(\mu\), because \(\mu \le 1\). So \(\tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i}} q_{\mathrm{i}}^2 \le \tfrac32\,\mu\). Altogether \(|g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n| \le (A + \tfrac32)\,\mu\).
  7. We choose any error \(\eta > 0\). For \(\mu\) we take the smaller of the numbers \(1\) and \(\eta/(A + 2)\). The number \(\mu\) is positive.
  8. By the lemma on a short fibre after loading there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|R_{\mathrm{x}}| \le \mu\,|h|\), \(|R_{\mathrm{y}}| \le \mu\,|h|\) and \(|R_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \mu\,|h|\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). For such \(h\) every component of the vector \(\vek q\) has the absolute value \(|R_{\mathrm{i}}|/|h| \le \mu\).
  9. By step 6, for these \(h\) the difference is \(|g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n| \le (A + \tfrac32)\,\mu < (A + 2)\,\mu \le \eta\). We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily, so the condition of the definition of the limit is satisfied.

2. The strain. By step 4, \(|\vek a|^2 = 1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\), so \(|\vek a| = \sqrt{1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n}\), and by assumption \(|\vek a| > 0\). We write the definition of the limit for the function \((L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) and the number \(|\vek a| - 1\).

  1. We rearrange the difference we must bound: \(\dfrac{L_1 - L_0}{L_0} - \big(|\vek a| - 1\big) = \dfrac{L_1}{L_0} - |\vek a|\).
  2. We multiply and divide by the positive number \(L_1/L_0 + |\vek a|\), as for (1.17). A difference of squares appears in the numerator:
    \[\frac{L_1}{L_0} - |\vek a| = \frac{L_1^2/L_0^2 - |\vek a|^2}{L_1/L_0 + |\vek a|} .\]
  3. The numerator is \((1 + 2g(h)) - (1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n) = 2\big(g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\big)\), because \(L_1^2/L_0^2 = 1 + 2g(h)\). The denominator is not smaller than \(|\vek a|\), because \(L_1/L_0\) is not negative. So
    \[\Big|\frac{L_1}{L_0} - |\vek a|\Big| \le \frac{2\,|g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n|}{|\vek a|} .\]
  4. We choose \(\eta > 0\). By item 1 there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n| < \eta\,|\vek a|/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). For these \(h\), by step 3, the difference is smaller than \(\eta\), which means that the limit equals \(|\vek a| - 1\).

Source: BOWER §2.1.5 (PDF pp. 47-48) and CM (3.4.8), (3.4.10) (PDF p. 129) write item 1 for an infinitesimally short fibre, without a limit. The proof with remainders is not from a source.

This is the main result of the section, so let us remember it. The nine partial derivatives of the displacement at the point \(P\) determine, through the matrix \(\mat E\), the strain of a short fibre in every direction through \(P\). For the direction we need only the unit vector \(\vek n\), and for the state of the material near the point only the six numbers of the matrix \(\mat E\). We assumed nothing about the size of the strains, so the theorem also holds for rubber stretched by half. The proof showed why it holds. After loading the vector of a short fibre differs from \(h\,\mat F\,\vek n\) only by the remainders, which become ever smaller after division by \(h\), and the square of the length of the vector \(h\,\mat F\,\vek n\) is \(h^2\) times a double sum with the matrix \(\mat F^\T\mat F\). The theorem does not hold where the field is not continuously differentiable, for example at a crack, and not on the surface of a body, where in some directions there is no fibre.

The theorem is also the Green-Lagrange strain of section 1.1, written in space. There, for a bar of length \(L\) that gets longer by \(\Delta L\), we wrote the quotient \(\big((L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2\big)/(2L^2)\). The left side of (1.49) is the same quotient for a short fibre. For a fibre along the axis \(x\), with \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), only the term with \(\ix{j} = \ix{k} = x\) remains in the double sum \(\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\), and it is \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\). So the entry \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the Green-Lagrange strain of a short fibre along the axis \(x\) (CM (3.4.15), PDF p. 131). Where does the root in item 2 come from? From \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\) we get \(L_1^2 / L_0^2 = (1 + \varepsilon)^2\), so \(1 + \varepsilon\) is the square root of \(L_1^2 / L_0^2\), which approaches \(1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\). We take the positive root because \(1 + \varepsilon = L_1 / L_0\) is positive.

What does the assumption that \(\mat F\,\vek n\) is not the zero vector mean? By lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading, a short fibre in the direction \(\vek n\) would otherwise be squeezed almost into a point: its length after loading would be small even compared with its original length \(|h|\). Matter cannot be squeezed so that a fibre disappears (CM, PDF pp. 119-120), so the assumption holds for every real body.

Let us test the theorem on the sheared cube. From its deformation gradient we get

\[\mat F^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0{,}001 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat F^\T\mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0{,}001 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0{,}001 & 0 & 1{,}000001 \end{bmatrix} .\]

For example, the entry in row \(z\) and column \(z\) is the product of row \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) with column \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat F\), that is, \(0{,}001 \cdot 0{,}001 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1{,}000001\). So

\[\mat E = \tfrac12\big(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I\big) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}0005 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0{,}0005 & 0 & 0{,}0000005 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The field of the cube is linear, so the remainders are zero, as we saw above, and in the proof \(\vek q\) is the zero vector. So for the cube the equalities (1.49) and (1.50) hold for every fibre, whatever its length, without a limit. Let us compare them with the table of exactly calculated strains in section 1.6.2. For the diagonal \(AC\) we have \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{z}} = 1/\sqrt2\) and \(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), so only the terms with the indices \(x\) and \(z\) remain in the double sum:

\[\begin{aligned} \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n &= E_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + E_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + E_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + E_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 \\ &= 0 + 0{,}0005 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0{,}0005 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0{,}0000005 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0{,}00050025 . \end{aligned}\]

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor is exact but awkward to compute, because each of its entries contains products of partial derivatives. For the hanging bar these products are extremely small. This leads us to a simplification.

1.17.3 Small deformations

Let us look at how large each part of the Green-Lagrange tensor is. By lemma 1.113, \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T) + \tfrac12\,\mat H^\T\mat H\). The first part is linear in the partial derivatives: each of its entries is half the sum of two partial derivatives. The second part is quadratic: each of its entries is half the sum of three products of two partial derivatives. For the hanging bar at the point \(P\), \(E_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the sum of the linear part \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) and the quadratic part

\[\tfrac12\big((1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6})^2 + (4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10})^2 + 0^2\big) = 1{,}905 \cdot 10^{-12} ,\]

which is a million times smaller. The entry \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\) has no linear part, because the sum of the partial derivatives is \(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} + 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} = 0\). Only the quadratic part remains,

\[\begin{aligned} &\tfrac12\big(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot (-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}) + 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} \cdot (-5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}) + 0 \cdot 0\big) \\ &\qquad = -5{,}94 \cdot 10^{-16} . \end{aligned}\]

Since \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y = -\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) and the last product is zero, the sum in the bracket equals \(-\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big(\dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y}\Big)\). This is, with the opposite sign, the product that we mentioned in section 1.7.2 as the difference between the tilts of two fibres, about \(1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-15}\), and \(E_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is half of it. For the sheared cube the linear part of the entry \(E_{\mathrm{xz}}\) is \(0{,}0005\), and the quadratic part of the entry \(E_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}001^2 = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), a thousand times less.

This is the rule we met with the sheared cube: next to a small number, its square is smaller by about a factor of that small number. Let us write it precisely. Suppose that every partial derivative of the displacement at the point \(P\) is at most \(m\) in absolute value. Then each of the three products in the entry \((\mat H^\T\mat H)_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\) is at most \(m \cdot m\) in absolute value, their sum at most \(3m^2\), and the quadratic part of the entry \(E_{\mathrm{ij}}\) at most \(\tfrac32 m^2\). The linear part can be as large as \(m\). For \(m = 0{,}001\) the quadratic part of every entry is at most \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\), one and a half thousandths of the largest size of the linear part. For the hanging bar \(m = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and the calculated quadratic part \(1{,}905 \cdot 10^{-12}\) is indeed below the bound \(\tfrac32 m^2 = 5{,}7 \cdot 10^{-12}\).

In the autumn of 1822 Cauchy showed how to calculate the elongation of every short line through a point from the derivatives of the displacements (LOVE, PDF p. 25). In an article on the condensation and dilatation of solid bodies, published in 1827, he described the change of shape by six numbers and three perpendicular directions, as we saw in section 1.1 (LOVE, PDF p. 25). In 1847 Saint-Venant treated the question of when a body is at rest if the displacements are not very small (LOVE, PDF p. 78). Exact deformation that is not limited to small displacements, such as the Green-Lagrange tensor of section 1.17, was treated by Love only in an appendix to the first chapter of his book. He regarded it as an interesting branch of kinematics and remarked that the rest of his book does not need it (LOVE, PDF p. 78).

From the estimate of the quadratic part follows the assumption that we will use throughout the book unless we say otherwise.

  • Small deformation assumption (predpostavka majhnih deformacij): all partial derivatives of the displacements are small in absolute value compared with \(1\).

In steel structures it is justified for the strains by section 1.1: the strains of steel are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\), because at larger strains steel deforms permanently. The name of the assumption is somewhat misleading, though, because it asks for more than small strains. The partial derivatives also describe how the material turns, and in section 1.17.6 we will see a body that does not deform at all, while its partial derivatives are not small. For the turns there is no such reason. We can bend a thin steel ruler into an arc, and when we let go it straightens again. So its strains stayed below about \(0{,}001\), while its end turned visibly. The smallness of the turns therefore has to be checked separately for every structure. We now use the assumption by leaving out the quadratic part.

Definition 1.115 Small strain tensor (tenzor malih deformacij)

Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient at an interior point \(P\) of a body. The small strain tensor (tenzor malih deformacij) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\big(\mat H + \mat H^\T\big) , \tag{1.51}\]

with the entries \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}})\), written out, with all partial derivatives at the point \(P\),

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial x}\Big) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial y}\Big) , \end{aligned} \tag{1.52}\]

and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\).

Source: CM (3.5.3)-(3.5.5) (PDF p. 141), SADD (2.1.7) (PDF p. 40) and (2.2.5) (PDF p. 43), BOWER §2.1.7 (PDF pp. 49-50).

The small strain tensor is a symmetric matrix, since \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) does not change when the indices are swapped. It is the symmetric part of the matrix \(\mat H\) (lemma 1.103 on the split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts) and has six different entries. The Green-Lagrange tensor differs from it only by the quadratic part, \(\mat E = \ten\varepsilon + \tfrac12\,\mat H^\T\mat H\). CM calls it the infinitesimal strain tensor (tenzor infinitezimalnih deformacij) (CM §3.5.1, PDF pp. 140-141), SADD simply the strain tensor, written \(e_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (SADD (2.1.7), PDF p. 40). For the hanging bar at the point \(P\),

\[\ten\varepsilon = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \end{bmatrix} ,\]

and for the sheared cube

\[\ten\varepsilon = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0{,}0005 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0{,}0005 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

What remains of theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre? We denote the strain of a short fibre in the direction \(\vek n\), that is, the limit (1.50), by \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\). The vector in the brackets reminds us that this is a strain in a direction, not the strain \(\varepsilon(x)\) of a bar at the point \(x\). Let us show in steps how \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) differs from the double sum with the matrix \(\ten\varepsilon\).

  1. From (1.50), \(1 + \varepsilon(\vek n) = \sqrt{1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n}\). We square and multiply out \((1 + \varepsilon(\vek n))^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon(\vek n) + \varepsilon(\vek n)^2\). Subtracting \(1\) and dividing by \(2\) gives exactly
    \[\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n = \varepsilon(\vek n) + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon(\vek n)^2 .\]
  2. We substitute \(\mat E = \ten\varepsilon + \tfrac12\,\mat H^\T\mat H\). A double sum with a sum of matrices is the sum of the double sums, because every term \(n_{\mathrm{j}}\,(A_{\mathrm{jk}} + B_{\mathrm{jk}})\,n_{\mathrm{k}}\) splits into two. So \(\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n = \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n + \tfrac12\,\vek n^\T\mat H^\T\mat H\,\vek n\).
  3. The double sum \(\vek n^\T\mat H^\T\mat H\,\vek n\) is \(|\mat H\,\vek n|^2\). The same steps 2 to 5 as for \(|\mat F\,\vek n|^2\) above show this, only with \(\mat H\) instead of \(\mat F\).
  4. Steps 1 to 3 give the exact equality
    \[\varepsilon(\vek n) = \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n + \tfrac12\,|\mat H\,\vek n|^2 - \tfrac12\,\varepsilon(\vek n)^2 .\]
  5. The two corrections on the right are halves of squares of two numbers. The number \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) is the strain of the fibre. The number \(|\mat H\,\vek n|\) is, by lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading, the length of the vector by which the end of the fibre moves relative to its start, divided by the length of the fibre. For small deformations both numbers are small. If they are smaller than \(0{,}001\) in absolute value, each correction is smaller than \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}001^2 = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The corrections have opposite signs, so \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) differs from \(\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\) by less than \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The condition concerns these two numbers, not the individual partial derivatives, and \(|\mat H\,\vek n|\) can be larger than every partial derivative. If all entries of the matrix \(\mat H\) equal \(0{,}001\) and \(\vek n = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3\), each component of the vector \(\mat H\,\vek n\) is a sum of three equal products, \(3 \cdot 0{,}001/\sqrt3 = \sqrt3 \cdot 0{,}001\), so \(|\mat H\,\vek n| = \sqrt{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 0{,}001^2} = 0{,}003\) and \(\tfrac12|\mat H\,\vek n|^2 = 4{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\). When the material near the point turns more, the correction \(\tfrac12|\mat H\,\vek n|^2\) is larger still, as we will see in section 1.17.6. We leave the corrections out:
    \[\varepsilon(\vek n) \approx \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} .\]
  6. We write out the double sum. The terms with \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) give \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). The terms with \(\ix{i} \neq \ix{j}\) come in pairs, for example \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{x}}\), which are equal because \(\ten\varepsilon\) is symmetric. Each pair gives twice one term:
    \[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon(\vek n) &\approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 \\ &\quad + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{1.53}\]

This is the formula we will use most often. From six numbers at the point \(P\) it gives the strain of a short fibre in every direction through \(P\), with no roots and no products of partial derivatives (CM (3.5.6), PDF p. 142, BOWER, PDF p. 50, SADD (2.3.3), PDF p. 47). Let us test it on our fibres.

  • The diagonal \(AC\) of the sheared cube, \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\): the formula gives \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} = 2 \cdot 0{,}0005 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0{,}0005\), the exact strain is \(5{,}0012 \cdot 10^{-4}\). The corrections of step 4 are \(\tfrac12|\mat H\,\vek n|^2 = 2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and \(-\tfrac12\,\varepsilon(\vek n)^2 = -1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\), and their sum \(1{,}25 \cdot 10^{-7}\) is exactly the difference.
  • The fibre from the point \(P\) of the hanging bar in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\): the formula gives
    \[\begin{aligned} &1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot \tfrac19 - 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \tfrac49 - 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot \tfrac49 \\ &\qquad = 2{,}1689 \cdot 10^{-7} - 5{,}2053 \cdot 10^{-7} = -3{,}0364 \cdot 10^{-7} , \end{aligned}\]
    the exact limit is \(-3{,}036441 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The difference is \(3{,}2 \cdot 10^{-13}\), almost a million times less than the strain.
  • The vertical edge \(AD\) of the sheared cube, \(\vek n = (0, 0, 1)\): the formula gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), but the exact strain is \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\). Here the term we keep vanishes, and only the correction \(\tfrac12|\mat H\,\vek n|^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}001^2\) remains. This is the vertical edge of section 1.6.2, which only tilts and whose strain grows with the square of the shift. The error \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) is small compared with the strains we care about, but it is not small compared with zero. The small strain tensor says that the edge does not get longer, while in fact it gets longer by a thousand times less than the diagonals.

Figure 1.134 shows \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) for all directions in one plane. For the cube take the directions in the plane \(x\)-\(z\), \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, n_{\mathrm{z}})\), where \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = \sqrt{1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\). A fibre and the opposite fibre have the same strain, because in (1.53) all terms are products of two components of the direction, and these do not change when both change sign. So the directions with \(n_{\mathrm{z}} \ge 0\) are enough. By (1.53), \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0{,}001\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Why are the extreme values exactly in the directions of the diagonals? The square of the product is \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,(1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2) = \tfrac14 - \big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - \tfrac12\big)^2\), which is never larger than \(\tfrac14\) and equals \(\tfrac14\) exactly for \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = \tfrac12\). So \(|n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}}| \le \tfrac12\), and the strain is largest, \(0{,}0005\), in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\) and smallest, \(-0{,}0005\), in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\). For the hanging bar at the point \(P\) take the directions in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\). There \(\varepsilon(\vek n) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7}\,(1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2)\): it is largest along the bar and smallest across it.

1.17.4 What the components mean

The formula (1.53) keeps the promise of section 1.1. The strain of a short fibre in every direction through the point \(P\) is determined by six numbers, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\), and we need nothing else for it (SADD §2.2, PDF p. 43). What does each of them measure?

Let us start with the numbers with equal indices. For a fibre along the axis \(x\), with \(\vek n = (1, 0, 0)\), only the first term remains in (1.53), \(\varepsilon(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) \approx \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). So the number \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the relative change of length of a short fibre along the axis \(x\), the normal strain in the direction of the axis \(x\) (definition 1.3). In the same way \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are the normal strains in the directions of the axes \(y\) and \(z\). When there are no shear strains, a small cube of material with edge \(h\) and edges along the axes becomes a box with the edges \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})\,h\), \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})\,h\) and \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,h\) (figure 1.135). For the hanging bar, which hangs under its own weight with the axis \(x\) pointing down, on the axis \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = 2a\,(L - x)\), which is the strain at a point from definition 1.10. So the strain at a point that we defined for the bar in section 1.4 is the component \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) of the small strain tensor.

And the numbers with different indices? A fibre along a diagonal gives the answer. A fibre in the direction \(\vek n = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\) has \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} = \tfrac12\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so by (1.53)

\[\varepsilon\big((1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\big) \approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} ,\]

and for the other diagonal, \(\vek n = (-1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\) with \(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}} = -\tfrac12\),

\[\varepsilon\big((-1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\big) \approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]

So the number \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) tells how much more the first diagonal stretches than the average of the fibres along the axes \(x\) and \(y\), and the second diagonal stretches by as much less. The difference between the two diagonals is \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\). When \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the only component different from zero, the fibres along the axes do not get longer, one diagonal gets longer and the other shorter (figure 1.136). This is the deck of cards of section 1.1.

We also see the same number in the tilt of the fibres. By the columns of the matrix \(\mat F\) (figure 1.132), a short fibre along the axis \(x\) tilts towards the axis \(y\): its end moves in the direction of the axis \(y\) by \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\) times the length of the fibre. A fibre along the axis \(y\) tilts towards the axis \(x\) by \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y\) times its length. Both ratios of the sideways shift of the end to the length of the fibre are numbers without a unit, and their sum is

\[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]

When both ratios are positive, the two fibres tilt towards each other, and the right angle between the positive directions of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) becomes smaller. The sum says by how much. When the ratios are equal in size and opposite in sign, the sum is zero. Then the two fibres tilt in the same sense of turning, and the right angle stays a right angle, as for the hanging bar in section 1.7.2. So the shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) measures the part of the tilting that changes the right angle. We will be able to measure angles between vectors in chapter 2, and there we will show how exactly \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is related to the decrease of the right angle (CM (3.5.9), PDF p. 142, SADD (2.2.3), PDF p. 43). SADD fixes the sign in the same way: the shear strain is positive when the right angle between the positive directions of the axes decreases (SADD, PDF p. 43).

Let us test both on the sheared cube, which is glued to the table by its bottom face and whose top face moves by \(s = 10\) µm at the height \(h = 10\,000\) µm. There \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}0005 = s/(2h)\), and the other components are zero. The vertical edge \(AD\) tilts in the direction of the axis \(x\) by the ratio \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z = 0{,}001 = s/h\), while the bottom edge \(AB\) does not tilt, because \(\partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x = 0\). The sum of the ratios is \(0{,}001 = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\). The diagonals have the strains \(\pm\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \pm 0{,}0005\), because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) are zero. Now we see where the half came from in section 1.6.2, where the diagonals changed by half the ratio \(s/h\). The tilt \(s/h\) of the edge is the sum of both ratios, \(2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\), while a diagonal stretches only by \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\).

This half is a source of frequent mistakes. Many books and programs for structural calculations use, instead of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the sum of both ratios, the engineering shear strain (inženirska strižna deformacija)

\[\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} ,\]

and likewise \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) (SADD (2.2.4), PDF p. 43, CM, PDF p. 141). BOWER warns that this factor of \(2\) is an endless source of confusion, and advises us to check, for every shear strain reported, which of the two is meant (BOWER §2.1.8, PDF p. 51). In this book a shear strain will always be \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), because with it the formula (1.53) is a plain double sum with a symmetric matrix. For the cube \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}001\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0{,}0005\).

Let us return to the hanging bar, which hangs under its own weight, with the axis \(x\) pointing down from the centre \(O\) of the top end. From the table of partial derivatives in section 1.7.2 we get at each of its points

\[\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} &= 2a\,(L - x) , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \tfrac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,y + 0{,}6\,a\,y) = 0 , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} &= \tfrac12\,(-0{,}6\,a\,z + 0{,}6\,a\,z) = 0 , \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} &= \tfrac12\,(0 + 0) = 0 . \end{aligned}\]

There are no shear strains anywhere in the bar, as we predicted in words in section 1.7.2 and as CM also finds (CM Example 7.3.2, equation (3), PDF p. 305). The pairs of partial derivatives that are equal and opposite cancel in the sum. The transverse normal strains are \(-0{,}3\) times the axial one, \(-0{,}6\,a\,(L - x) = -0{,}3 \cdot 2a\,(L - x)\), which is the thinning of section 1.6. Now we also understand the fibre in the direction \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), which got shorter. By (1.53) its strain is

\[\varepsilon(\vek n) \approx 2a\,(L - x)\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 0{,}3\,(n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\big) = 2a\,(L - x)\,\big(\tfrac19 - 0{,}3 \cdot \tfrac89\big) .\]

The fibre lies mostly across the bar, \(n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = \tfrac89\), and across the bar the bar gets thinner. The thinning (\(0{,}3 \cdot \tfrac89 = 0{,}267\)) wins over the stretching along the bar (\(\tfrac19 = 0{,}111\)), so the fibre gets shorter, although its end moves down more than \(P\). The fibres with \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = 0{,}3\,(1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2)\), that is \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = 0{,}3/1{,}3\) and \(|n_{\mathrm{x}}| = 0{,}48\), do not change their length (figure 1.134, right).

The normal strains also tell how the volume of a small piece changes. With normal strains alone, a small cube with edge \(h\) becomes a box with the volume \((1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}})(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}})\,h^3\) (figure 1.135). Multiplying out the brackets gives \(1 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}\) plus products of two or three strains, which we leave out for small deformations. The shear strains change only the right angles and for small deformations do not change the volume (BOWER §2.1.9, PDF p. 52), just as for the sheared cube the area of the front face did not change. So the fraction by which the volume of a small piece changes is about

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \tr\ten\varepsilon ,\]

the sum of the diagonal entries of the matrix, which we call the trace (sled) of the matrix (BOWER §2.1.9, PDF p. 51, SADD §2.4, PDF p. 49). We will derive the exact relation, which also holds for large deformations, in chapter 2. For the sheared cube the trace is zero, which agrees with the finding of section 1.6.2 that simple shear does not change the volume. For the hanging bar at the point \(P\) the trace is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6} - 2 \cdot 5{,}856 \cdot 10^{-7} = 7{,}808 \cdot 10^{-7}\), which is \((1 - 2 \cdot 0{,}3)\) times, that is \(0{,}4\) times, the axial strain. The volume grows because the bar gets thinner across by less than it gets longer. For an axial strain of \(0{,}001\) the relative change would be \(0{,}0004\), which in section 1.6 we got from \(1{,}001 \cdot 0{,}9997^2 \approx 1{,}0004\).

The graph in figure 1.134 shows one more thing. For the cube the strain is largest in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\) and smallest in the direction of the diagonal \(BD\), for the bar largest along the bar and smallest across it. Books show that for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear, only stretching or compression, and that among all fibres through the point the largest and the smallest strain occur in these directions (SADD §2.4, PDF pp. 48-49, and §1.6, PDF p. 22). We already announced this in section 1.1. For the hanging bar these are the directions of the axes, because there are no shear strains. For the cube they are the directions of the two diagonals of the front face and the direction of the axis \(y\). The proof needs calculations with right angles between vectors, so we will do it in chapter 2, together with stresses (section 2.11.4).

1.17.5 The turning of the material near a point

The small strain tensor is the symmetric part of the matrix \(\mat H\). By lemma 1.103 on the split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, \(\mat H\) is the sum of its symmetric and antisymmetric parts, and this split is unique. What does the antisymmetric part describe?

Definition 1.116 Small rotation tensor (tenzor malih zasukov)

Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient at an interior point \(P\) of a body. The small rotation tensor (tenzor malih zasukov) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\ten\omega = \tfrac12\big(\mat H - \mat H^\T\big) , \tag{1.54}\]

with the entries \(\omega_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ij}} - H_{\mathrm{ji}})\), written out

\[\ten\omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \omega_{\mathrm{xy}} & \omega_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ -\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} & 0 & \omega_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ -\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} & -\omega_{\mathrm{yz}} & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\]

where \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial y - \partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x\big)\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial z - \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial x\big)\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{yz}} = \tfrac12\big(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial z - \partial u_{\mathrm{z}} / \partial y\big)\), all at the point \(P\).

Source: CM (3.5.10), (3.5.11) (PDF p. 143), where the symbol is \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), SADD (2.1.7) (PDF p. 40), BOWER §2.1.10 (PDF p. 52).

On the diagonal there are zeros, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ii}} - H_{\mathrm{ii}}) = 0\), and below the diagonal the opposite values of those above it, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{ji}} = -\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}\). So the matrix has only three different numbers. We have \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\), which we check entry by entry: \(\tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ij}} + H_{\mathrm{ji}}) + \tfrac12(H_{\mathrm{ij}} - H_{\mathrm{ji}}) = H_{\mathrm{ij}}\). By lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading, the difference of the displacements of the ends of a short fibre is, up to the remainders, \(\mat H\,(h\,\vek n)\), so

\[\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) \approx h\,\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n + h\,\ten\omega\,\vek n . \tag{1.55}\]

We already know the first part: it gives the strain of the fibre. What does the second part do? Let us show that it changes the length of no fibre, up to terms with squares of partial derivatives.

  1. Without the remainders the fibre has the vector \(h\,(\vek n + \mat H\,\vek n)\) after loading. We write the square of its length component by component as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre:
    \[h^2\,|\vek n + \mat H\,\vek n|^2 = h^2\Big(|\vek n|^2 + 2\sum_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\mat H\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}} + |\mat H\,\vek n|^2\Big) .\]
  2. The first term is \(h^2\), the square of the length before loading. The last is the square of a small number. So the first-order change is the middle term, and in it \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\mat H\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,H_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek n^\T\mat H\,\vek n\).
  3. Since \(\mat H = \ten\varepsilon + \ten\omega\), we have \(\vek n^\T\mat H\,\vek n = \vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n + \vek n^\T\ten\omega\,\vek n\).
  4. In the double sum \(\vek n^\T\ten\omega\,\vek n = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\) the terms with \(\ix{i} = \ix{j}\) are zero, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\). Every term with \(\ix{i} \neq \ix{j}\) has a partner in the sum with the indices swapped, and \(n_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}} + n_{\mathrm{j}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{ji}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = n_{\mathrm{i}}\,n_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\omega_{\mathrm{ij}} + \omega_{\mathrm{ji}}) = 0\). So \(\vek n^\T\ten\omega\,\vek n = 0\).
  5. So the first-order change of the square of the length is \(2h^2\,\vek n^\T\ten\varepsilon\,\vek n\), and the matrix \(\ten\omega\) does not appear in it.

So the part \(h\,\ten\omega\,\vek n\) moves the end of the fibre in such a way that the length of the fibre does not change to first order: the fibre only turns. Since this holds for all directions at once, with the same matrix \(\ten\omega\), all short fibres through \(P\) turn together, as if they were part of a rigid body. CM shows that a displacement given by an antisymmetric matrix that is the same at all points is a rigid turn when its entries are small (CM, PDF p. 143). Hence the name small rotation tensor. To first order, the material close to the point deforms according to \(\ten\varepsilon\) and at the same time turns according to \(\ten\omega\) (figure 1.137). SADD (2.1.8) (PDF p. 40) and BOWER (PDF p. 52) split the change in the same way.

For the hanging bar at the point \(P\)

\[\begin{aligned} \omega_{\mathrm{xy}} &= \tfrac12\big(-4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} - 4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10}\big) = -4{,}685 \cdot 10^{-10} , \\ \omega_{\mathrm{xz}} &= 0 , \qquad \omega_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0 . \end{aligned}\]

Since at the same time \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), the material near \(P\) only turns in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) and is not sheared. We described this in words in section 1.7.2, and figure 1.45 shows it. For the sheared cube \(\omega_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12(0{,}001 - 0) = 0{,}0005\), the same as \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\). So the tilt of the vertical edge by the ratio \(0{,}001\) is half shear strain and half turn, as we saw in the split of the cube's matrix in section 1.16.

1.17.6 When the small strain tensor fails

We obtained the small strain tensor by leaving out products of partial derivatives. For this we assumed that all partial derivatives are small, not only the strains. What happens if a body turns a lot but deforms little? Let us take the most extreme case, a body that does not deform at all.

The model is a thin square plate that lies in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) and that we turn as a whole about the axis \(z\), without deforming it. We measure lengths in centimetres, the side of the plate is \(5\), and its corners are \(O = (0, 0, 0)\), \(A = (5, 0, 0)\), \(B = (5, 5, 0)\) and \(C = (0, 5, 0)\). Let the displacement field be

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = (c - 1)\,x - s\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = s\,x + (c - 1)\,y , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 , \qquad c = 0{,}6 , \quad s = 0{,}8 .\]

The numbers \(c\) and \(s\) are the sides of the right triangle with sides \(3\), \(4\) and \(5\), divided by \(5\), so \(c^2 + s^2 = 0{,}36 + 0{,}64 = 1\). The point \((x, y, z)\) moves to the position \((x + u_{\mathrm{x}},\ y + u_{\mathrm{y}},\ z + u_{\mathrm{z}}) = (c\,x - s\,y,\ s\,x + c\,y,\ z)\). The corner \(A\) moves to \(A_1 = (3, 4, 0)\), the corner \(C\) to \((-4, 3, 0)\), and the points on the axis \(z\) do not move (figure 1.138). Is this really a rigid-body displacement (definition 1.11)? Let us check that the distance between no two points changes.

  1. Let the points \(P\) and \(Q\) differ by \((\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z)\) before loading. We subtract the new positions coordinate by coordinate. Since each new coordinate is a sum of multiples of the old ones, the new positions differ by \((c\,\Delta x - s\,\Delta y,\ s\,\Delta x + c\,\Delta y,\ \Delta z)\).
  2. We multiply out the square of the new distance:
    \[\begin{aligned} (c\,\Delta x - s\,\Delta y)^2 + (s\,\Delta x + c\,\Delta y)^2 + \Delta z^2 &= c^2\Delta x^2 - 2cs\,\Delta x\,\Delta y + s^2\Delta y^2 \\ &\quad + s^2\Delta x^2 + 2sc\,\Delta x\,\Delta y + c^2\Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 . \end{aligned}\]
  3. The mixed terms cancel, and we collect the others: \((c^2 + s^2)(\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2) + \Delta z^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2\), because \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\).

The distances stay the same, the plate does not deform. The fibre \(OA\) of length \(5\) becomes \(OA_1\) with the length \(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\). What do the two measures of strain say? The displacement gradient is, at every point,

\[\mat H = \begin{bmatrix} c - 1 & -s & 0 \\ s & c - 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}4 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 0{,}8 & -0{,}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat F = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}6 & -0{,}8 & 0 \\ 0{,}8 & 0{,}6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The entry \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is \(0{,}6^2 + 0{,}8^2 + 0^2 = 1\), the entry \((\mat F^\T\mat F)_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is \(0{,}6 \cdot (-0{,}8) + 0{,}8 \cdot 0{,}6 + 0 = 0\), and in the same way we find \(\mat F^\T\mat F = \mat I\). So the Green-Lagrange tensor is exactly zero, \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat I - \mat I) = \mat 0\), and it correctly says that no fibre gets longer. The small strain tensor, however, is

\[\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\big(\mat H + \mat H^\T\big) = \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -0{,}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

By it every fibre in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) would get shorter by \(40\) %, which is false. Where does the error come from? The number \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x = c - 1 = -0{,}4\) measures only how the component \(x\) of a fibre along the axis \(x\) changes. The fibre \(OA\) turns into \(OA_1\), its component \(x\) decreases from \(5\) to \(3\), but its length stays \(5\) (figure 1.138). The small strain tensor mistakes the turn for a shortening, because it leaves out the term \(\tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}} / \partial x)^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}8^2 = 0{,}32\) and the term \(\tfrac12\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x)^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}4^2 = 0{,}08\). With them, by (1.48), \(E_{\mathrm{xx}} = -0{,}4 + 0{,}08 + 0{,}32 = 0\).

How large is the error for a small turn? Take \(s = 0{,}001\) and \(c = \sqrt{1 - s^2}\), so that again \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\) and the displacement is rigid. A point 1 m from the axis moves sideways by about 1 mm. Then \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = c - 1 = -5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\). This is about \(-s^2/2\), because \((1 - s^2/2)^2 = 1 - s^2 + s^4/4\), which differs from \(c^2 = 1 - s^2\) only by \(s^4/4\). So the false strain is proportional to the square of the turn. For \(s = 0{,}001\) it is small compared with the strains of steel of about \(0{,}001\). For \(s = 0{,}1\), when a point 1 m from the axis moves sideways by about 10 cm, however, \(c - 1 = -0{,}0050\). This is five times the strain at which steel already gets permanently longer (section 1.1), although the body does not deform at all.

So the small strain tensor is a good approximation only when all partial derivatives of the displacements are small, that is, when the strains and also the turns are small. In steel structures the strains are small, and when the turns of their parts are small too, this is so. When parts of a body turn a lot while the body deforms little, for example a thin ruler bent into an arc, the Green-Lagrange tensor must be used. The same holds for large strains, for example in rubber. CM warns that smallness must be checked separately in every application, and also shows the opposite example: a field for which the small strain tensor is zero but the Green-Lagrange tensor is not (CM (3.5.16)-(3.5.18), PDF pp. 143-144). The example with the turned plate is not from a source.

1.17.7 Strain relative to the shape after loading

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor and the small strain tensor measure fibres that we choose before loading. We take the point \(P\) and the direction \(\vek n\) on the unloaded body, and we compare the change of the square of the length of the fibre with the square of its length before loading, \(L_0^2\). This is how we have described a body ever since section 1.6: we name every point by the position it had before loading and assign to it its displacement. Books call this the Lagrangian or material description (Lagrangeov ali materialni opis).

Sometimes, however, we know a body mainly after loading. We draw a grid of short marks on an unloaded rubber band, stretch the band and take a photograph. On the photograph we see each mark in its position, direction and length after loading, and by these we also choose it. For a liquid flowing in a pipe, we want to know what happens at a given place in the pipe, not where the bit of liquid that is there now was before. A description in which we name points by their position after loading is the Eulerian or spatial description (Eulerjev ali prostorski opis). CM says that for solid bodies the Eulerian description is less useful, because we do not know the shape of the body after loading in advance, while for the motion of liquids it is the one usually used (CM §3.2.1-§3.2.3, PDF pp. 112-114 and 117).

The goal of this section is a measure of stretching that compares the change of the square of the length of a short fibre with the square of its length after loading, \(L_1^2\), and gives the direction of the fibre after loading. We will find a matrix that gives this measure for every direction, and show how it is related to the Green-Lagrange tensor. The physical model and both assumptions are the same as in the whole of section 1.17: the body is a continuum, its displacement field is continuously differentiable, and the point \(P\) is an interior point of the body. We add one more assumption: the deformation gradient \(\mat F\) at the point \(P\) has an inverse matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\). What this means physically we said in section 1.16.5. Matter cannot be squeezed so that a fibre disappears, and CM concludes from this that the matrix \(\mat F\) is invertible (CM §3.3.1, PDF pp. 119-120). That a matrix which sends no nonzero vector to the zero vector really has an inverse matrix, we will show only in chapter 2. Until then the existence of the matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\) is an assumption, which we check by calculation in the examples.

Let us start with the bar, where everything is a single number. A bar of length \(L_0\) gets longer, to the length \(L_1\). In section 1.1 we met the Green-Lagrange strain \((L_1^2 - L_0^2)/(2L_0^2)\). If we divide the change of the square of the length by the square of the new length, we get the quotient

\[\frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_1^2} .\]

Let us express it with the strain of the bar \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\).

  1. From the definition of the strain, \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\). The square is a multiplied-out product, \(L_1^2 = L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \varepsilon) = L_0^2\,(1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2)\), as in section 1.6.2.
  2. So the numerator is \(L_1^2 - L_0^2 = L_0^2\,(2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2)\), and the denominator is \(2L_1^2 = 2L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2\).
  3. The factor \(L_0^2\) cancels:
    \[\frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_1^2} = \frac{2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2}{2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2} . \tag{1.56}\]
  4. The quotient differs from the strain \(\varepsilon\) by
    \[\varepsilon - \frac{2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2}{2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2} = \frac{2\varepsilon\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2 - 2\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2}{2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2} = \frac{\varepsilon^2\,(3 + 2\varepsilon)}{2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2} ,\]
    which for small \(\varepsilon\) is almost \(\tfrac32\,\varepsilon^2\). We got the numerator by multiplying out \((1 + \varepsilon)^2\) as in step 1 and collecting the terms: \(2\varepsilon + 4\varepsilon^2 + 2\varepsilon^3 - 2\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2 = 3\varepsilon^2 + 2\varepsilon^3\). The Green-Lagrange strain \(\varepsilon + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon^2\), on the other hand, differs from \(\varepsilon\) by \(\tfrac12\,\varepsilon^2\).

For a steel bar with \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) the new quotient is \(0{,}0009985\) and the Green-Lagrange strain \(0{,}0010005\). The first differs from \(\varepsilon\) by one and a half per mille of its value, the second by half a per mille. For a rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm, \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\), the new quotient is \((15^2 - 10^2)/(2 \cdot 15^2) = 125/450 = 0{,}278\) and the Green-Lagrange strain \(0{,}625\) (figure 1.139). For large strains the two measures differ a lot.

Why is the new quotient always smaller than the Green-Lagrange one when the length changes? Both have the same numerator \(L_1^2 - L_0^2\). When the bar is stretched, \(L_1 > L_0\), so the new quotient divides a positive numerator by a larger number and is smaller. When the bar gets shorter, \(L_1 < L_0\), and the new quotient divides a negative numerator by a smaller number, so it is larger in size and again smaller. The strain \(\varepsilon\) always lies between the two. The Green-Lagrange strain is larger than it by \(\tfrac12\,\varepsilon^2\), and the new quotient is smaller by step 4, because a bar cannot disappear, so \(L_1 > 0\), \(\varepsilon > -1\) and \(3 + 2\varepsilon > 0\).

The new quotient has one more meaning. We let the stretched band of length 15 cm go back to 10 cm, and we take the length of the stretched band as the initial length. The Green-Lagrange strain of this unloading is \((10^2 - 15^2)/(2 \cdot 15^2) = -0{,}278\), the new quotient with the opposite sign. This is always so: when we swap the roles of the lengths \(L_0\) and \(L_1\), the Green-Lagrange quotient becomes \((L_0^2 - L_1^2)/(2L_1^2)\), which is the new quotient with the opposite sign. So the new quotient measures stretching with the length seen by someone who knows only the loaded body.

In space we proceed as in section 1.17.2 and first leave out the remainders, which we will justify in the theorem below. A fibre from the point \(P\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\) with length \(h > 0\) has after loading, by lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading and without the remainders, the vector \(\mat F\,(h\,\vek n) = h\,\mat F\vek n\) (figure 1.140).

  1. Its length after loading is \(L_1 \approx h\,|\mat F\vek n|\). The vector \(\mat F\vek n\) is not the zero vector, as we will show in the theorem below, so we may divide it by its length. We get the unit vector \(\vek m = \mat F\vek n / |\mat F\vek n|\), the direction of the fibre after loading. The vector of the fibre after loading is thus \(h\,\mat F\vek n \approx L_1\,\vek m\).
  2. We express the vector of the fibre before loading with the vector after loading. By lemma 1.97 on the linearity of the product with a matrix, item 1 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product, (1.43) and lemma 1.106, \(\mat F^{-1}(h\,\mat F\vek n) = h\,(\mat F^{-1}\mat F)\,\vek n = h\,\vek n\). So \(h\,\vek n \approx L_1\,\mat F^{-1}\vek m\). The matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\) takes the vector of the fibre after loading back to the vector before loading, just as for the sheared cube the matrix \(\mat I - \mat K\) took back the edge \(AD\) (section 1.16.5).
  3. So the square of the length before loading is \(L_0^2 = h^2 \approx L_1^2\,|\mat F^{-1}\vek m|^2\). The same steps 2 to 5 as for \(|\mat F\vek n|^2\) in section 1.17.2, only with \(\mat F^{-1}\) instead of \(\mat F\) and \(\vek m\) instead of \(\vek n\), give
    \[|\mat F^{-1}\vek m|^2 = \vek m^\T\,(\mat F^{-1})^\T\mat F^{-1}\,\vek m .\]
  4. The vector \(\vek m\) is a unit vector, so \(1 = \vek m^\T\mat I\,\vek m\) as in step 6 there, and \(L_1^2 = L_1^2\,\vek m^\T\mat I\,\vek m\).
  5. We subtract the squares, divide by \(2L_1^2\) and subtract the two double sums term by term:
    \[\frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_1^2} \approx \frac{L_1^2\,\vek m^\T\mat I\,\vek m - L_1^2\,\vek m^\T(\mat F^{-1})^\T\mat F^{-1}\vek m}{2L_1^2} = \vek m^\T\,\tfrac12\big(\mat I - (\mat F^{-1})^\T\mat F^{-1}\big)\,\vek m .\]

On the right there is again a single matrix, which depends only on the displacement field at the point \(P\), and the direction appears only in the vector \(\vek m\), this time in the direction of the fibre after loading. The matrix contains the transposed matrix of the inverse matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\). Before we give the matrix a name, let us show that this is the same matrix as the inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\), so that we may denote both by one symbol.

Lemma 1.117 Inverse of the transposed matrix (inverzna matrika transponirane matrike)

Let a square matrix \(\mat A\) of order \(n\) have an inverse matrix \(\mat A^{-1}\). Then the transposed matrix \(\mat A^\T\) also has an inverse matrix, and it is the transposed matrix of the matrix \(\mat A^{-1}\):

\[(\mat A^\T)^{-1} = (\mat A^{-1})^\T .\]
Proof

We must show that the matrix \((\mat A^{-1})^\T\) satisfies both equalities of definition 1.107 for the matrix \(\mat A^\T\).

  1. We write \(\mat B = \mat A^{-1}\). By (1.43), \(\mat A\mat B = \mat I\) and \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\).
  2. We transpose the first equality. By item 3 of lemma 1.101, \((\mat A\mat B)^\T = \mat B^\T\mat A^\T\), so \(\mat B^\T\mat A^\T = \mat I^\T\).
  3. The identity matrix equals its transposed matrix. The entry \((\ix{i}, \ix{j})\) of the matrix \(\mat I^\T\) is \(I_{\mathrm{ji}}\) (definition 1.100), which is \(1\) for \(\ix{j} = \ix{i}\) and \(0\) otherwise, the same as \(I_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 1.105). So \(\mat B^\T\mat A^\T = \mat I\).
  4. In the same way we transpose the second equality, \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\), and get \(\mat A^\T\mat B^\T = \mat I\).
  5. The matrices \(\mat A^\T\) and \(\mat B^\T\) are square matrices of order \(n\), and by steps 3 and 4 they satisfy both equalities (1.43) with \(\mat A^\T\) in the place of the matrix \(\mat A\). So \(\mat B^\T\) is an inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat A^\T\). By lemma 1.108 on the uniqueness of the inverse matrix there is no other, so \((\mat A^\T)^{-1} = \mat B^\T = (\mat A^{-1})^\T\).

Source: CM §2.3.6 (PDF p. 65) states the equality without proof, KREYSZIG §7.8, problems 13 and 14 (PDF p. 335), leaves its check and proof to the reader. The proof is not from the sources.

So we write the matrix \((\mat A^{-1})^\T = (\mat A^\T)^{-1}\) more briefly as \(\mat A^{-\T}\), as CM does (CM §2.3.6, PDF p. 65). The proof used only the rule for transposing a product, the fact that the identity matrix equals its transposed matrix, and the uniqueness of the inverse matrix. For the sheared cube \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat K\) (section 1.16.5), so by item 2 of lemma 1.101 and step 3 above, \(\mat F^{-\T} = \mat I^\T - \mat K^\T = \mat I - \mat K^\T\). This is the matrix that has a single nonzero entry off the main diagonal, \(-0{,}001\) in row \(z\) and column \(x\).

Definition 1.118 Euler-Almansi strain tensor (Euler-Almansijev tenzor deformacij)

Let \(\mat F\) be the deformation gradient at an interior point \(P\) of a body, and let \(\mat F\) have an inverse matrix. The Euler-Almansi strain tensor (Euler-Almansijev tenzor deformacij) at the point \(P\) is the matrix

\[\mat E^* = \tfrac12\big(\mat I - \mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1}\big) , \tag{1.57}\]

where \(\mat F^{-\T} = (\mat F^{-1})^\T\) is the inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) (lemma 1.117). We denote its entry in row \(\ix{i}\) and column \(\ix{j}\) by \(E^*_{\mathrm{ij}}\).

Source: CM (3.4.24) (PDF p. 133), BOWER §2.1.6 (PDF p. 49).

What kind of object is \(\mat E^*\)? Like \(\mat E\), it is a \(3 \times 3\) matrix, calculated from the deformation gradient at one point. It is symmetric (definition 1.102). By items 3 and 1 of lemma 1.101, \((\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1})^\T = (\mat F^{-1})^\T(\mat F^{-\T})^\T = \mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1}\), and the identity matrix equals its transposed matrix, so by item 2 of the same lemma \((\mat E^*)^\T = \mat E^*\). The matrix \(\mat E^*\) therefore has at most six different entries. We calculate it from the partial derivatives of the displacements at the point \(P\), but its numbers describe the fibres that pass through the point \(P_1\) after loading, in their directions after loading. The symbol \(\mat E^*\) is used by BOWER, while CM writes the same matrix with a small bold \(\mathbf e\).

CM calls this tensor the Almansi-Hamel or Euler strain tensor (Almansi-Hamelov ali Eulerjev tenzor deformacij), and in its examples the Almansi strain tensor (CM, PDF pp. 133-134). BOWER calls it the Eulerian strain tensor (Eulerjev tenzor deformacij) and denotes it by \(\mat E^*\) (BOWER §2.1.6, PDF p. 49). The name in this book joins the two names. CM also writes it with partial derivatives of the displacements with respect to the coordinates of the points after loading, and there the quadratic part has the opposite sign to that of the Green-Lagrange tensor (CM (3.4.25) and (3.4.27), PDF p. 133). We will not use such derivatives, because we name the points of a body by their position before loading.

Both matrices, \(\mat E\) and \(\mat E^*\), are calculated from the same matrix \(\mat F\). How do we get one from the other?

Lemma 1.119 Relation between the Euler-Almansi and the Green-Lagrange strain tensor (zveza med Euler-Almansijevim in Green-Lagrangeovim tenzorjem)

Let the deformation gradient \(\mat F\) at an interior point \(P\) of a body have an inverse matrix, and let \(\mat E\) be the Green-Lagrange and \(\mat E^*\) the Euler-Almansi strain tensor at \(P\). Then

\[\mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F = \mat E \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mat E^* = \mat F^{-\T}\mat E\,\mat F^{-1} . \tag{1.58}\]
Proof

We must show both equalities. We use the properties of the matrix product (lemma 1.99) and the fact that \(\mat F^{-\T}\) is the inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\) (lemma 1.117).

The first equality.

  1. We substitute (1.57). By item 3 of lemma 1.99, used for multiplication from the left and from the right, \(\mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F = \tfrac12\big(\mat F^\T\mat I\,\mat F - \mat F^\T\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1}\mat F\big)\).
  2. By lemma 1.106, \(\mat F^\T\mat I\,\mat F = \mat F^\T\mat F\).
  3. By item 2 of lemma 1.99, \(\mat F^\T\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1}\mat F = (\mat F^\T\mat F^{-\T})(\mat F^{-1}\mat F)\). By (1.43) both brackets equal \(\mat I\), the first because \(\mat F^{-\T}\) is the inverse matrix of the matrix \(\mat F^\T\). By lemma 1.106, \(\mat I\mat I = \mat I\).
  4. So \(\mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F = \tfrac12(\mat F^\T\mat F - \mat I)\), which by (1.47) is the matrix \(\mat E\).

The second equality.

  1. We multiply the first equality from the left by \(\mat F^{-\T}\) and from the right by \(\mat F^{-1}\): \(\mat F^{-\T}\mat E\,\mat F^{-1} = \mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F\mat F^{-1}\).
  2. By item 2 of lemma 1.99 the right side equals \((\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^\T)\,\mat E^*\,(\mat F\mat F^{-1})\). By (1.43) both brackets equal \(\mat I\), and by lemma 1.106, \(\mat I\,\mat E^*\,\mat I = \mat E^*\).

Source: The definitions of the two tensors are CM (3.4.10) (PDF p. 129) and (3.4.24) (PDF p. 133). The proof is not from the sources.

So we get \(\mat E^*\) from \(\mat E\) by multiplying \(\mat E\) from the left by \(\mat F^{-\T}\) and from the right by \(\mat F^{-1}\), and \(\mat E\) from \(\mat E^*\) with the matrices \(\mat F^\T\) and \(\mat F\). When \(\mat E = \mat 0\), therefore also \(\mat E^* = \mat 0\), because every entry of a product with the zero matrix is a sum of products with zero, and the other way round. The proof used only the grouping of factors and the fact that the product of a matrix with its inverse matrix gives the identity matrix.

So far we have left out the remainders. As for the Green-Lagrange tensor, let us show that this is justified for ever shorter fibres. We will not need a new calculation with remainders, because the new quotient is expressed through the Green-Lagrange one.

Theorem 1.120 Strain of a short fibre relative to its length after loading (deformacija kratkega vlakna glede na dolžino po obremenitvi)

Let the displacement field of a body be continuously differentiable, \(P\) an interior point of the body, \(r > 0\) a number such that all points closer to \(P\) than \(r\) are interior points of the body (such an \(r\) exists by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point), \(\mat F\) the deformation gradient at \(P\), which has an inverse matrix, \(\mat E\) the Green-Lagrange and \(\mat E^*\) the Euler-Almansi strain tensor at \(P\), and \(\vek n\) a unit vector. For a number \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) let a fibre reach from \(P\) to the point \(Q\) with \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = h\,\vek n\). Its length before loading is \(L_0 = |h|\), and after loading \(L_1 = |\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}|\). Then \(\mat F\vek n\) is not the zero vector, and for the unit vector \(\vek m = \mat F\vek n / |\mat F\vek n|\):

  1. the quotient \((L_1^2 - L_0^2)/(2L_1^2)\) is defined for all small enough \(|h|\) and approaches the number \(\vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m\),
    \[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_1^2} = \vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m , \tag{1.59}\]
  2. the two measures are related by the equality
    \[\vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m = \frac{\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n}{1 + 2\,\vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n} . \tag{1.60}\]
Proof

First we show that \(\mat F\vek n\) is not the zero vector, then item 2, and from it item 1.

The vector \(\mat F\vek n\).

  1. Suppose that \(\mat F\vek n = \vek 0\).
  2. Then by item 1 of lemma 1.99, \((\mat F^{-1}\mat F)\,\vek n = \mat F^{-1}(\mat F\vek n) = \mat F^{-1}\vek 0\), and every entry of the product \(\mat F^{-1}\vek 0\) is a sum of products with zero, so it is zero.
  3. By (1.43) and lemma 1.106, however, \((\mat F^{-1}\mat F)\,\vek n = \mat I\vek n = \vek n\), which is not the zero vector, because its length is \(1\). This contradicts step 2.
  4. So \(\mat F\vek n \neq \vek 0\), and its length \(|\mat F\vek n|\) is positive. Every component of the vector \(\vek m\) is a component of the vector \(\mat F\vek n\) divided by \(|\mat F\vek n|\), so by (1.10) \(|\vek m|^2 = |\mat F\vek n|^2/|\mat F\vek n|^2 = 1\), and \(\vek m\) is a unit vector.

2. The relation between the two measures.

  1. We write \(G = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\). By step 4 of the proof of theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre, \(|\mat F\vek n|^2 = \vek n^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\vek n\) and \(G = \tfrac12\big(|\mat F\vek n|^2 - 1\big)\). So \(|\mat F\vek n|^2 = 1 + 2G\), and by step 4 above this number is positive.
  2. By lemma 1.119, \(\mat E = \mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F\), so \(G = \vek n^\T\mat F^\T\mat E^*\,\mat F\,\vek n\).
  3. By item 3 of lemma 1.101, \(\vek n^\T\mat F^\T = (\mat F\vek n)^\T\), and by item 2 of lemma 1.99 we may group the factors as we like. So \(G = (\mat F\vek n)^\T\,\mat E^*\,(\mat F\vek n)\).
  4. We substitute \(\mat F\vek n = |\mat F\vek n|\,\vek m\). By item 2 of lemma 1.101, \((|\mat F\vek n|\,\vek m)^\T = |\mat F\vek n|\,\vek m^\T\), and by item 3 of lemma 1.99 we take the number out of each product. We get \(G = |\mat F\vek n|^2\,\vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m = (1 + 2G)\,\vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m\).
  5. We divide by the positive number \(1 + 2G\) and get (1.60).

1. The limit.

  1. We write \(g(h) = (L_1^2 - L_0^2)/(2L_0^2)\) and \(c = 1 + 2G\), where \(G = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\) as above. By item 1 of theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre, \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = G\), and by step 1 of item 2, \(c = |\mat F\vek n|^2 > 0\).
  2. From the definition of the function \(g\) we get \(L_1^2/L_0^2 = 1 + 2g(h)\), when we multiply the equality \(g(h) = \tfrac12\big(L_1^2/L_0^2 - 1\big)\) by \(2\) and add \(1\).
  3. We use the definition of the limit 1.5 with the error \(c/4\): there is a \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - G| < c/4\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We may always narrow the band, as we said after the definition of the limit, so we take \(\delta_1 \le r\), and \(g(h)\) is defined for all these \(h\). For such \(h\), \(1 + 2g(h) = c + 2\big(g(h) - G\big) > c - c/2 = c/2\). So \(L_1^2 = L_0^2\,\big(1 + 2g(h)\big) > 0\), hence \(L_1 > 0\), and the quotient \(a(h) = (L_1^2 - L_0^2)/(2L_1^2)\) is defined. So we may apply the definition of the limit to the function \(a\), with \(\delta_1\) in the role of the number \(r\).
  4. For these \(h\) we divide the numerator and the denominator of the quotient \(a(h)\) by \(L_0^2\) and use step 2:
    \[a(h) = \frac{(L_1^2 - L_0^2)/L_0^2}{2L_1^2/L_0^2} = \frac{2g(h)}{2\,\big(1 + 2g(h)\big)} = \frac{g(h)}{1 + 2g(h)} .\]
  5. By item 2, \(\vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m = G/(1 + 2G) = G/c\). We bring the two fractions to a common denominator:
    \[a(h) - \frac{G}{c} = \frac{g(h)\,(1 + 2G) - G\,\big(1 + 2g(h)\big)}{\big(1 + 2g(h)\big)\,c} = \frac{g(h) - G}{\big(1 + 2g(h)\big)\,c} ,\]
    because the terms \(2\,g(h)\,G\) in the numerator cancel.
  6. By step 3 the denominator is larger than \((c/2) \cdot c = c^2/2\), so \(|a(h) - G/c| < 2\,|g(h) - G|/c^2\).
  7. We choose any error \(\eta > 0\). By the definition of the limit there is a \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - G| < \eta\,c^2/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\). For \(\delta\) we take the smaller of the numbers \(\delta_1\) and \(\delta_2\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\), by step 6, \(|a(h) - G/c| < \eta\). We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily, so the limit of the quotient \(a(h)\) is \(G/c = \vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m\).

The theorem is the version in space of the quotient for the bar. The Euler-Almansi tensor gives, for every direction \(\vek m\) after loading, how much a short fibre has been stretched, measured with its length after loading. The vector \(\vek m\) is the direction of the fibre after loading up to the remainders, since by lemma 1.111 on a short fibre after loading the vector of the fibre after loading is \(h\,\mat F\vek n\) plus the remainders. For negative \(h\) the fibre points in the direction \(-\vek m\), which does not change the double sum, because each of its terms is a product of two components of the direction. The proof showed why the theorem holds. The new quotient is exactly the Green-Lagrange quotient \(g\) divided by \(1 + 2g = L_1^2/L_0^2\), so we got its limit from theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre, and item 2 is the same relation in the limit.

Item 2 relates the two measures of the same fibre. We write \(G = \vek n^\T\mat E\,\vek n\) and \(A = \vek m^\T\mat E^*\,\vek m\). From (1.60),

\[G - A = G - \frac{G}{1 + 2G} = \frac{G\,(1 + 2G) - G}{1 + 2G} = \frac{2G^2}{1 + 2G} = 2\,G\,A .\]

So the two measures of the same fibre differ by exactly twice their product. When \(|G| \le 0{,}001\), the difference is at most \(0{,}002\,|A|\) in size, two thousandths of the value \(A\). For small strains it therefore does not matter which of the two measures we use. With the strain of the fibre \(\varepsilon(\vek n)\) of section 1.17.3 the relation is even simpler. By item 2 of theorem 1.114 on the strain of a short fibre, \(1 + 2G = \big(1 + \varepsilon(\vek n)\big)^2\), so \(G = \varepsilon(\vek n) + \tfrac12\,\varepsilon(\vek n)^2\) and \(A = G/\big(1 + \varepsilon(\vek n)\big)^2\), which is exactly the formula (1.56) with \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon(\vek n)\). So the curves of figure 1.139 hold for every short fibre, not only for the bar.

How close is \(\mat F^{-1}\) to the matrix \(\mat I - \mat H\)? For the sheared cube \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat K\) exactly, because \(\mat K\mat K = \mat 0\) (section 1.16.5). For the hanging bar the matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\), which we computed with a computer, differs from the matrix \(\mat I - \mat H\) by at most \(3{,}81 \cdot 10^{-12}\), in the entry \(xx\). This is the square of the largest partial derivative, \((1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6})^2 = 3{,}81 \cdot 10^{-12}\). Why? We can express the difference \(\mat B = \mat I - \mat F^{-1}\) from the equation \(\mat F\mat F^{-1} = \mat I\), and we get \(\mat B = \mat H - \mat H\mat B\). If \(\mat B\) is small, then \(\mat H\mat B\) is a product of two small matrices, and \(\mat B\) is almost \(\mat H\). That \(\mat B\) really is small, the same equation tells us when we read it as an inequality for the largest entry of \(\mat B\). As in section 1.17.3, let \(m\) be a number that no partial derivative of the displacement at the point exceeds in absolute value.

Lemma 1.121 Estimate of the inverse matrix (ocena inverzne matrike)

Let \(\mat H\) be a \(3 \times 3\) matrix and \(m\) a number with \(0 < m \le \tfrac16\) such that \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m\) for all indices \(i\) and \(j\). Let the matrix \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) have an inverse matrix \(\mat F^{-1}\), and let \(\mat B = \mat I - \mat F^{-1}\). Then

\[\mat B = \mat H - \mat H\mat B , \qquad \mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat H + \mat H\mat B , \tag{1.61}\]

and \(|B_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 2m\) and \(|(\mat H\mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 6m^2\) for all \(i\) and \(j\).

Proof

We must show both equalities and both estimates. The equalities come from the definition of the inverse matrix, the estimate for \(\mat B\) from the first equality read as an inequality, and the estimate for \(\mat H\mat B\) from the estimate for \(\mat B\).

  1. By definition 1.107 of the inverse matrix, \(\mat F\mat F^{-1} = \mat I\). We insert \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) and \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat B\). By item 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product and by lemma 1.106 on multiplication by the identity matrix, \((\mat I + \mat H)(\mat I - \mat B) = \mat I - \mat B + \mat H - \mat H\mat B\).
  2. This matrix is \(\mat I\), so by the rules of matrix arithmetic (lemma 1.95) \(\mat H - \mat B - \mat H\mat B = \mat 0\), that is, \(\mat B = \mat H - \mat H\mat B\). This is the first equality.
  3. From \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat B\) and the first equality, \(\mat F^{-1} = \mat I - \mat H + \mat H\mat B\), the second equality.
  4. Let \(\mu\) be the largest of the nine numbers \(|B_{\mathrm{ij}}|\). Among finitely many numbers one is the largest.
  5. By the first equality, \(B_{\mathrm{ij}} = H_{\mathrm{ij}} - \sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ik}}\,B_{\mathrm{kj}}\). By the triangle inequality for numbers and the rule for the absolute value of a product (lemma 1.6), \(|B_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le |H_{\mathrm{ij}}| + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} |H_{\mathrm{ik}}|\,|B_{\mathrm{kj}}| \le m + 3m\mu\), since the sum has three terms and each is at most \(m\mu\).
  6. The inequality of step 5 holds for all entries, also for the entry with \(|B_{\mathrm{ij}}| = \mu\). So \(\mu \le m + 3m\mu\), that is, \((1 - 3m)\,\mu \le m\).
  7. Since \(m \le \tfrac16\), we have \(1 - 3m \ge \tfrac12\). Dividing the inequality by the positive number \(1 - 3m\) gives \(\mu \le m/(1 - 3m) \le 2m\). So \(|B_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 2m\) for all \(i\) and \(j\).
  8. The entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat H\mat B\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ik}}\,B_{\mathrm{kj}}\), a sum of three terms, each at most \(m \cdot 2m\) in absolute value. By the triangle inequality for numbers, therefore, \(|(\mat H\mat B)_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 6m^2\).

The lemma says that \(\mat F^{-1}\) equals \(\mat I - \mat H\) up to the matrix \(\mat H\mat B\), whose entries are at most \(6m^2\). The proof used the equation \(\mat B = \mat H - \mat H\mat B\) twice: first as an inequality that bounds the largest entry of \(\mat B\), then to estimate the correction \(\mat H\mat B\). We needed the condition \(m \le \tfrac16\) in step 7, so that the number \(1 - 3m\) is positive and not too small. The lemma does not prove that \(\mat F^{-1}\) exists but assumes it, like this whole section. For the cube \(m = 0{,}001\), \(\mat B = \mat K\) and \(\mat H\mat B = \mat K\mat K = \mat 0\). For the hanging bar \(m = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the largest entry of \(\mat B\) is \(1{,}951996 \cdot 10^{-6}\), less than \(2m\), and the largest entry of \(\mat H\mat B\) is \(3{,}81 \cdot 10^{-12}\), below the bound \(6m^2 = 2{,}29 \cdot 10^{-11}\). Now we can compare all three measures.

Corollary 1.122 Three strain measures for small partial derivatives (tri mere deformacije pri majhnih parcialnih odvodih)

Let \(\mat H\) be the displacement gradient at an interior point of a body and \(m\) a number with \(0 < m \le \tfrac16\) such that \(|H_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le m\) for all \(i\) and \(j\), and let the deformation gradient \(\mat F = \mat I + \mat H\) have an inverse matrix. Let \(\mat E\), \(\mat E^*\) and \(\ten\varepsilon\) be the Green-Lagrange strain tensor, the Euler-Almansi strain tensor and the small strain tensor at this point. Then for all \(i\) and \(j\)

\[\begin{aligned} |E_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| &\le \tfrac32\,m^2 , \\ |E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| &\le 12\,m^2 , \\ |E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - E_{\mathrm{ij}}| &\le \tfrac{27}{2}\,m^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{1.62}\]
Proof

We must show three estimates. We write each difference with products of matrices and bound it with lemma 1.121 on the estimate of the inverse matrix.

  1. By lemma 1.113 on the Green-Lagrange strain from the displacement gradient, \(\mat E = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T + \mat H^\T\mat H)\), and by (1.51), \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\). So \(\mat E - \ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\,\mat H^\T\mat H\).
  2. The entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat H^\T\mat H\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} H_{\mathrm{ki}}\,H_{\mathrm{kj}}\), a sum of three products, each at most \(m^2\) in absolute value. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6), therefore, \(|E_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le \tfrac12 \cdot 3m^2 = \tfrac32\,m^2\).
  3. Let \(\mat B = \mat I - \mat F^{-1}\) as in lemma 1.121. By item 2 of lemma 1.101 on transposing a sum, a multiple and a product, and since the identity matrix equals its transpose, \(\mat F^{-\T} = (\mat I - \mat B)^\T = \mat I - \mat B^\T\).
  4. By item 3 of lemma 1.99 on the properties of the matrix product and lemma 1.106 on multiplication by the identity matrix, \(\mat F^{-\T}\mat F^{-1} = (\mat I - \mat B^\T)(\mat I - \mat B) = \mat I - \mat B - \mat B^\T + \mat B^\T\mat B\). By (1.57), therefore, \(\mat E^* = \tfrac12(\mat B + \mat B^\T - \mat B^\T\mat B)\).
  5. By lemma 1.121, \(\mat B = \mat H - \mat H\mat B\), and by item 2 of lemma 1.101, \(\mat B^\T = \mat H^\T - (\mat H\mat B)^\T\). Inserting this into step 4 and subtracting \(\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\) gives
    \[\mat E^* - \ten\varepsilon = -\tfrac12\big(\mat H\mat B + (\mat H\mat B)^\T + \mat B^\T\mat B\big) .\]
  6. By lemma 1.121 every entry of the matrices \(\mat H\mat B\) and \((\mat H\mat B)^\T\) is at most \(6m^2\) in absolute value. The entry \((i, j)\) of the matrix \(\mat B^\T\mat B\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} B_{\mathrm{ki}}\,B_{\mathrm{kj}}\), a sum of three products, each at most \(2m \cdot 2m = 4m^2\) by the same lemma, so it is at most \(12m^2\). By the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le \tfrac12\,(6m^2 + 6m^2 + 12m^2) = 12m^2\).
  7. We write the difference as \(E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - E_{\mathrm{ij}} = (E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}) + (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} - E_{\mathrm{ij}})\). By the triangle inequality for numbers and steps 6 and 2, \(|E^*_{\mathrm{ij}} - E_{\mathrm{ij}}| \le 12m^2 + \tfrac32\,m^2 = \tfrac{27}{2}\,m^2\).

BOWER §2.1.7 (PDF p. 50) says that the infinitesimal strain approximates the Lagrange strain, and the Eulerian strain with the same accuracy, and leaves the latter to the reader. The numbers \(\tfrac32\), \(12\) and \(\tfrac{27}{2}\) are the bounds that the proof gives, and with the same assumptions they cannot be made smaller. For the matrix \(\mat H\) with all nine entries equal to \(-\tfrac16\) and \(m = \tfrac16\) we computed with SymPy that all three bounds, and both bounds of lemma 1.121 on the estimate of the inverse matrix too, are attained exactly. The first bound is attained for every \(m\): if all nine entries of the matrix \(\mat H\) equal \(m\), every entry of the matrix \(\tfrac12\,\mat H^\T\mat H\) equals \(\tfrac12 \cdot 3m^2 = \tfrac32\,m^2\). In both examples below the actual differences are smaller than the bounds, about three times for the first estimate and at least six times for the other two.

  • Sheared cube, \(m = 0{,}001\): the bounds are \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}\), \(1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and \(1{,}35 \cdot 10^{-5}\). The largest differences are in the entry \(zz\), \(E_{\mathrm{zz}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), \(E^*_{\mathrm{zz}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and \(E^*_{\mathrm{zz}} - E_{\mathrm{zz}} = -10^{-6}\).
  • Hanging bar at the point \(P\), \(m = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\): the bounds are \(5{,}7 \cdot 10^{-12}\), \(4{,}6 \cdot 10^{-11}\) and \(5{,}1 \cdot 10^{-11}\). The largest differences are in the entry \(xx\), \(1{,}905 \cdot 10^{-12}\), \(-5{,}715 \cdot 10^{-12}\) and \(-7{,}6 \cdot 10^{-12}\).

So in steel structures we use the small strain tensor, while for large strains, for example in rubber, we must say which measure we use and whether the direction of the fibre is given before loading or after it.

We can now calculate from the displacement field the strain at every interior point and in every direction. The Green-Lagrange tensor gives it exactly when we give the direction of the fibre before loading, and the Euler-Almansi tensor when we give it after loading, while for small partial derivatives the small strain tensor is enough. The path went from displacements to strains. Often we must go the other way: we know the six components of strain and look for the displacements. Is this always possible? We calculate six components from only three components of the displacement, so they cannot be arbitrary. Which conditions they must satisfy, we look at in the next section.
