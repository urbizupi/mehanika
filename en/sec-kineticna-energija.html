Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.8 Kinetic energy of a rigid body

The equations of motion, which we derive in section 8.9, need the energy of motion (kinetična energija) of the system written with generalized coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and their time derivatives \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\). For a point mass \(m\) with velocity \(\vek v\) we know it, \(\tfrac12 m\,|\vek v|^2\). In a rigid body (definition 1.11), however, each of infinitely many points can have a different velocity. How fast does each point of a turning body move, and what is \(\tfrac12\int\rho\,|\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\)?

As an example we take the bar \(BC\) of mechanism D. The mechanism has bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, each of mass \(2\) kg. Bar \(AB\) is pinned at \(A\), the bars are joined at \(B\) by a pin, and the other end \(C\) of bar \(BC\) is hinged to a slider of mass \(1\) kg that slides along a horizontal guide through \(A\). The angle \(\theta\) measures bar \(AB\) above the horizontal. Point \(B\) moves on a circle about \(A\), point \(C\) on a straight line, and the points of bar \(BC\) in between on still other curves. How a rigid body can move at all, we found in section 8.4.1: the position of a point at time \(t\) is \(\vek x(t) = \vek c(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek X\) ((8.26)) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) of determinant \(1\) (lemma 8.40). The answer to the question therefore has two parts. First we compute from this the velocity of every point, then the energy of motion from the mass of the body, the velocity of its centre of mass, and a quantity that says how the mass is distributed around it.

8.8.1 Velocity of a point and angular velocity

The velocity of a point is the time derivative of its position vector. A matrix is differentiated by differentiating each element. From (8.26) we get

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek c} + \dot{\mat Q}\,\vek X .\]

In this form the velocity is tied to \(\vek X\), which is hard to see in a calculation. Let us write it with the position of the point at time \(t\). From \(\vek x = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\) and \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) it follows that \(\vek X = \mat Q^\T(\vek x - \vek c)\), so

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek c} + \mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c) , \qquad \mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T . \tag{8.85}\]

The matrix \(\mat\Omega\) has nine elements. We shall show that, because of the orthogonality of \(\mat Q\), only three of them are independent.

Lemma 8.85 The matrix of the rotation rate (matrika odvoda zasuka)

In a motion of a rigid body as in definition 8.39, the matrix \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\) is antisymmetric (definition 1.103). Let \(\vek\omega = (\Omega_{32},\ \Omega_{13},\ \Omega_{21})\). Then \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y = \vek\omega\times\vek y\) for every vector \(\vek y\).

Proof

We must show \(\mat\Omega^\T = -\mat\Omega\) and then compare components.

  1. The equality \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) holds for every \(t\), so the derivative of the left side is zero. By the rule for the derivative of a product (lemma 1.82), applied to each element of the product, \(\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T + \mat Q\,\dot{\mat Q}^\T = \mat 0\).
  2. By lemma 1.102, \(\mat\Omega^\T = (\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q\,\dot{\mat Q}^\T\), which by step 1 equals \(-\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T = -\mat\Omega\). The matrix is antisymmetric.
  3. Hence \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\) and \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ij}} = -\Omega_{\mathrm{ji}}\). Component \(1\) of \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y\) is \(\Omega_{12}\,y_2 + \Omega_{13}\,y_3 = -\Omega_{21}\,y_2 + \Omega_{13}\,y_3\). By (2.39) component \(1\) of \(\vek\omega\times\vek y\) equals \(\omega_2\,y_3 - \omega_3\,y_2 = \Omega_{13}\,y_3 - \Omega_{21}\,y_2\). They are equal.
  4. Components \(2\) and \(3\) give the same with the indices exchanged \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\): \(\Omega_{21}\,y_1 + \Omega_{23}\,y_3 = \Omega_{21}\,y_1 - \Omega_{32}\,y_3\) is \(\omega_3\,y_1 - \omega_1\,y_3\), and \(\Omega_{31}\,y_1 + \Omega_{32}\,y_2 = -\Omega_{13}\,y_1 + \Omega_{32}\,y_2\) is \(\omega_1\,y_2 - \omega_2\,y_1\).

Source: SHABANA (2.42)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61): the matrix \(\dot{\mat A}\mat A^\T\) is skew symmetric and defines a vector \(\vek\omega\). Here the vector is written with components and checked.

Definition 8.86 Angular velocity (kotna hitrost)

The angular velocity (kotna hitrost) of a rigid body at time \(t\) is the vector \(\vek\omega(t) = (\Omega_{32},\ \Omega_{13},\ \Omega_{21})\), where \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\) is from lemma 8.85. The unit is \(1/\mathrm{s}\).

Source: SHABANA (2.43)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61).

The angular velocity is one quantity for the whole body, not for an individual point. The connection with the velocities of the points is the following.

Lemma 8.87 Velocity of a point of a rigid body (hitrost točke togega telesa)

In a motion of a rigid body, the velocity of the point with position \(\vek x\) at time \(t\) is

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek c} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek c) . \tag{8.86}\]

For an arbitrary point \(\vek p\) of the frame with position \(\vek p(t)\) and velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{p}}\) we also have

\[\vek v = \vek v_{\mathrm{p}} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek p) . \tag{8.87}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c)\) in (8.85) is a vector product, and the second equality follows by subtraction.

  1. By lemma 8.85 with \(\vek y = \vek x - \vek c\), \(\mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c) = \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek c)\). Inserting into (8.85) gives (8.86).
  2. Equation (8.86) for the point \(\vek p\) gives \(\vek v_{\mathrm{p}} = \dot{\vek c} + \vek\omega\times(\vek p - \vek c)\).
  3. We subtract this equation from (8.86) and use the splitting of a sum in a product (lemma 2.34, points 2 and 3): \(\vek v - \vek v_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek\omega\times\big((\vek x - \vek c) - (\vek p - \vek c)\big) = \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek p)\).

Source: SHABANA (2.43)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61), where \(\dot{\mat A}\,\bar{\vek u} = \tilde{\vek\omega}\,\mat A\,\bar{\vek u} = \vek\omega\times\vek u\) is written. The second equality is not from the source.

Equation (8.87) says that the velocity of any point follows from the velocity of one chosen point and the angular velocity: a point that is displaced by \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek p\) from the chosen one has the additional velocity \(\vek\omega\times\vek r\), which is perpendicular to \(\vek r\) and to \(\vek\omega\) (lemma 2.34, point 4) and proportional to the distance from the axis that passes through \(\vek p\) in the direction of \(\vek\omega\).

Figure 8.87 shows this on bar \(BC\) of mechanism D at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\), with the chosen point \(G\) at the middle of the bar. The numbers are computed below, here we look at the picture. We cut the bar into pieces, five of them are drawn. On the left every piece has its own velocity \(\vek v\), different from piece to piece. This picture equals the sum of two simpler ones. In the middle all the pieces have the velocity of the point \(G\), as if the bar were not turning. On the right comes the addition \(\vek\omega\times\vek r\) due to the turning: at the piece \(K_2\) with the vector \(\vek r\) it points one way, at the piece \(K_1\) with the vector \(-\vek r\) the opposite way, and at the ends, which are twice as far from \(G\), the arrow is twice as long. The numbers under the pictures are the energies of motion of the whole bar, of the motion with the centre of mass and of the turning, which we reach in section 8.8.4.

Why is the addition perpendicular to \(\vek r\)? The distance between two points of a rigid body does not change, so a piece can neither approach the chosen point nor move away from it. Its velocity relative to the chosen point therefore has no part along \(\vek r\), only the part across it is left.

In plane motion in the plane \(xy\), by lemma 8.41 the matrix \(\mat Q\) is a rotation about the axis \(z\) by an angle that we write here as \(\theta(t)\), just as we described the position of a body by a point and an angle in definition 8.42 and in section 8.7 (\(\mat R(\varphi)\) is the upper left part of the matrix \(\mat Q\)). We take the angle \(\theta(t)\) twice continuously differentiable, like the elements of the matrix \(\mat Q\). The derivatives of sine and cosine are \(\sin' = \cos\) and \(\cos' = -\sin\) (theorem 8.13), the angle being measured in radians. Then

\[\mat Q = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T = \dot\theta\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]

We compute the product by rows: the first row of \(\dot{\mat Q}\) is \(\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta,\ 0)\) and with the columns of \(\mat Q^\T\) it gives \(\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos\theta\sin\theta,\ -\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta,\ 0) = \dot\theta\,(0, -1, 0)\), and the second row likewise \(\dot\theta\,(1, 0, 0)\). From \(\Omega_{21} = \dot\theta\) and \(\Omega_{32} = \Omega_{13} = 0\) we get

\[\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

The angular velocity in plane motion is therefore the rate of change of the angle, which is why we write it \(\dot\theta\), not \(\omega\).

This is a second view of the angular velocity, without matrices. We draw any segment on the body, for example bar \(BC\) itself or the vector \(\vek r\) between two of its points, and measure how fast its angle to the axis \(x\) changes. The angles between segments on a rigid body do not change, so all of them turn by the same angle and every one gives the same number. That is why the angular velocity is one quantity for the whole body. The matrix \(\mat\Omega\) adds what we need in space, where one angle is not enough: the direction of the axis about which the body turns at this instant.

Let us test this on bar \(BC\) of mechanism D at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) (figure 8.88). Point \(A\) is the origin. The positions are \(\vek B = a\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) and \(\vek C = (2a\cos\theta,\ 0)\), because the bars are equally long and \(C\) is on the guide. The point \(G\) at the middle of bar \(BC\) is the centre of mass (težišče) of the bar (definition 8.81), which we show for a uniform bar in section 8.8.2. So \(\vek G = \tfrac12(\vek B + \vek C) = a\,(\tfrac32\cos\theta,\ \tfrac12\sin\theta)\). The time derivative gives

\[\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = a\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta) , \qquad \vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-2a\dot\theta\sin\theta,\ 0) , \qquad \vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = a\dot\theta\,\big(-\tfrac32\sin\theta,\ \tfrac12\cos\theta\big) ,\]

numerically \(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = (-0{,}3536,\ 0{,}3536)\) m/s, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-0{,}7071,\ 0)\) m/s and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768)\) m/s. The direction from \(C\) to \(B\) is \((-\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\), which is the direction at the angle \(\pi - \theta\) from the axis \(x\). As \(\theta\) increases this angle decreases, bar \(BC\) turns by \(-\dot\theta\), and the angular velocity is \(\vek\omega = -\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). We check (8.87) with the chosen point \(G\) and the point \(B\). Since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times(r_{\mathrm{x}}, r_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) = (-r_{\mathrm{y}},\ r_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0)\), for \(\vek r = \vek B - \vek G = (-0{,}1768,\ 0{,}1768)\) m

\[\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768) + (0{,}1768,\ 0{,}1768) = (-0{,}3536,\ 0{,}3536)\ \text{m/s} = \vek v_{\mathrm{B}} .\]

The velocity of point \(B\) is indeed the velocity of point \(G\) plus the addition due to the rotation about \(G\). Figure 8.88 shows this sum as the triangle on the right.

The figure also shows the point \(R\), where the velocity of the bar is zero at this instant. The perpendiculars to \(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}}\) (the line \(AB\)) and to \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}}\) (the vertical through \(C\)) meet in \(\vek R = (2a\cos\theta,\ 2a\sin\theta) = (0{,}7071,\ 0{,}7071)\) m. For \(\vek p = \vek R\) in (8.87) we take \(\vek r = \vek R - \vek G = (0{,}1768,\ 0{,}5303)\) m, and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768) + (0{,}5303,\ -0{,}1768) = \vek 0\). At this instant bar \(BC\) turns about the point \(R\). The point \(R\) is the instantaneous centre of rotation (trenutno središče vrtenja) of the bar, in the lectures the velocity pole (pol hitrosti). It is not attached to the body and moves during the motion, but at this instant it will come in handy.

8.8.2 Mass, centre of mass and kinetic energy

We already have the energy of motion of a point mass. We imagine the body cut into small pieces. The piece at the point \(\vek X\) with a small volume \(\Delta V\) has approximately the mass \(\rho(\vek X)\,\Delta V\), the more accurately the smaller the piece, where \(\rho\) is the mass density (masna gostota) at the point, unit kg/m\(^3\). If every piece gets the energy \(\tfrac12 (\rho\,\Delta V)\,|\vek v|^2\) and we add them up, then for ever smaller pieces we get the integral over the body from section 4.2. Why the pieces cannot move each in its own way, we found out above: their velocities are connected by (8.86).

Mass, centre of mass and the moment of inertia about an axis have already been defined (definition 8.81), and we define nothing new here. We take a body \(B\) with a continuous density \(\rho > 0\) that at time \(0\) occupies a set with volume (definition 4.30). The mass is \(m = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V\) and the centre of mass at time \(0\) is the point \(G\) with the position vector \(\vek X_{\mathrm{G}} = \dfrac1m\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\vek X\,\mathrm{d}V\), where we integrate componentwise. The word "centre of mass" (težišče) means here the centre of mass of the body, weighted by density, not the centroid of an area of a figure (definition 5.21). If the body is uniformly dense (\(\rho\) constant), the density cancels in the average and the centre of mass is the centroid of its volume, for example the middle of a uniform bar or the centre of a uniform ball. For a non-uniform density the two points differ.

At time \(t\) the piece that was at \(\vek X\) is at \(\vek x = \Phi(\vek X) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\), and the density at the point \(\Phi(\vek X)\) is \(\rho(\vek X)\). Does the piece have the same mass? The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable, the matrix of its derivatives is \(\mat Q\), the Jacobian determinant is \(\det\mat Q = 1\) (lemma 8.40), and it is injective, because \(\mat Q\,\vek X = \mat Q\,\vek Y\) gives \(\vek X = \mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek X = \mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek Y = \vek Y\). By theorem 4.59 on the change of variables, the integral of a continuous function over the image \(\Phi(W)\) of every piece \(W\) with volume is therefore equal to the integral over \(W\) of the same function evaluated at \(\Phi(\vek X)\), since \(|J_\Phi| = 1\). With the function \(1\) this says that the volume of the piece is kept, and with the density that the mass of the piece is kept. So at time \(t\) the position of the centre of mass is \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \dfrac1m\int_{\mathrm{B}}\rho\,(\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X)\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}}\), because \(\mat Q\) is constant when we integrate over \(\vek X\), and we take it out by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.32, point 1). Let \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \mat Q\,(\vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}})\) be the vector from the centre of mass to the point at time \(t\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \mat Q\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(\vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \mat Q\,(m\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}} - m\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}}) = \vek 0 . \tag{8.88}\]

So at every time the property of the centre of mass from definition 8.81, \(\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\), is fulfilled. For a uniform bar of length \(L\) with the coordinate \(s\) measured from the middle, \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2}s\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), so the centre of mass is the middle of the bar.

Definition 8.88 Kinetic energy of a system (kinetična energija)

The kinetic energy of a body with mass \(m\), density \(\rho\) and point velocity \(\vek v\) is

\[T = \frac12\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}V .\]

A point mass \(m\) with velocity \(\vek v\) has \(T = \tfrac12 m\,|\vek v|^2\). The kinetic energy of a system of bodies and point masses is the sum of the kinetic energies of all the parts. The unit is J.

Source: SHABANA (3.234)-(3.236) (PDF pp. 159-160) writes the kinetic energy of a rigid body as an integral over the body, and GR (1.22) (PDF p. 42) the kinetic energy of a system of particles as the sum of \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v|^2\) over the particles.

For a point mass we took \(\tfrac12 m\,v^2\) as known. How is the kinetic energy of a body written with the mass, the velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity? The answer needs a quantity that says how the mass is distributed around the centre of mass.

8.8.3 Inertia tensor and moment of inertia

At angular velocity \(\vek\omega\) the point with the vector \(\vek r\) from the centre of mass has, by (8.87), the additional velocity \(\vek\omega\times\vek r\). The square of its length is, by point 5 of lemma 2.34, \(|\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r|^2 - (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). All the components are quadratic in \(\vek\omega\) and in \(\vek r\) and we can write them with a matrix that does not depend on \(\vek\omega\). Let \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\) be the matrix with elements \(r_{\mathrm{i}}\,r_{\mathrm{j}}\) (column times row).

Definition 8.89 Inertia tensor (vztrajnostni tenzor)

Let \(B\) be a rigid body with density \(\rho\) and centre of mass \(G\) (definition 8.81), and \(\vek r\) the vector from \(G\) to a point of the body. The inertia tensor (vztrajnostni tenzor) of the body about the centre of mass is the \(3 \times 3\) matrix

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\big(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T\big)\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{8.89}\]

where we integrate elementwise. With \(\vek r = (x, y, z)\) the elements are

\[J_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad J_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,x\,y\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad J_{\mathrm{yy}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(x^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad \text{and so on.}\]

The unit is kg m\(^2\).

Source: SHABANA (3.231), (3.233) (PDF p. 159): \(\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}^\T\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}\,\mathrm{d}V\), written out with the elements \(i_{11}\), \(i_{12}\) and others. The matrix \(\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}^\T\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}\) equals \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T\).

The diagonal elements are sums of \(\int\rho\,(\text{square of the distance from the axis})\,\mathrm{d}V\), for example \(J_{\mathrm{xx}}\) measures the distance from the axis \(x\). The off-diagonal elements have, unlike the product of inertia of a section \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) in section 5.4, the sign minus, because this makes \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) exactly the matrix from (8.89). The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is symmetric, because \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\) is symmetric. During the motion it changes, since \(\vek r\) rotates. Let us see how.

Lemma 8.90 Properties of the inertia tensor (lastnosti vztrajnostnega tenzorja)

Let \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the inertia tensor of a rigid body in a motion as in definition 8.39, and let the body have interior points. For every vector \(\vek\omega\):

  1. \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  2. \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega > 0\) for every \(\vek\omega \neq \vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is therefore positive definite (definition 6.27).
  3. \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(t) = \mat Q(t)\,\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(0)\,\mat Q(t)^\T\).
Proof

We get the first point by a computation from the definition, the second from distances from an axis, the third from the preservation of lengths.

  1. By lemma 2.34 (point 5), \(|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2 = |\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r|^2 - (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). By (8.89), \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \int_{\mathrm{B}}\rho\,\big(|\vek r|^2\,|\vek\omega|^2 - \vek\omega\cdot(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\vek\omega)\big)\,\mathrm{d}V\), and \(\vek\omega\cdot(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\vek\omega) = (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). This is the first point.
  2. Let \(\vek\omega \neq \vek 0\) and \(\vek r' = \vek r - \dfrac{\vek\omega\cdot\vek r}{|\vek\omega|^2}\,\vek\omega\) the part of the vector \(\vek r\) perpendicular to \(\vek\omega\). Then \(|\vek r|^2 = |\vek r'|^2 + (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2/|\vek\omega|^2\) and by point 1, \(|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2 = |\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r'|^2\). The value \(|\vek r'|\) is the distance of the point from the line through \(G\) in the direction of \(\vek\omega\). The integrand \(\rho\,|\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r'|^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, so the integral is nonnegative (lemma 4.32, point 2). The body has an interior point that does not lie on the line, since in every ball there are points outside every line. At it the integrand is positive, so by continuity it is at least \(\varepsilon > 0\) on a smaller ball \(K\) around it that lies in the body. By point 2 of the same lemma, \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} \rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \varepsilon\,V_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \ge \int_{\mathrm{K}}\), so the integral over the body is positive.
  3. At time \(t\), \(\vek r = \mat Q\,\vek R\) with \(\vek R = \vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}}\). An orthogonal matrix keeps lengths, \(|\mat Q\,\vek R|^2 = \vek R^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek R = |\vek R|^2\), and \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T = \mat Q\,\vek R\,\vek R^\T\mat Q^\T\). Hence \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T = \mat Q\,(|\vek R|^2\,\mat I - \vek R\,\vek R^\T)\,\mat Q^\T\), and we take \(\mat Q\) out of the integral.

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §3.8 (PDF p. 159) notes that the elements \(\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta}\) in the axes of the body are constant for a rigid body.

We draw the body as a shape with centre of mass \(G\). Through \(G\) goes the line in the direction \(\vek\omega\). We take a point \(P\) of the body with the vector \(\vek r\) from \(G\). The part \(\vek r'\) perpendicular to the line has length \(d\), the distance of the point from the line, and for it \(|\vek\omega\times\vek r| = |\vek\omega|\,d\). A point off the line has in the body around it a small ball \(K\) on which the integrand is positive, so the integral over the body is positive too.

Point 3 says that \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is constant in the axes that are fixed to the body. Point 2 says that the rotational energy is not zero, unless the body lies on a single line, like a point mass. And what does the tensor say about the moment of inertia about an axis? By definition 8.81 the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek n\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,d^2\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(d\) is the distance of the point from the axis. This distance is \(d = |\vek n\times\vek r|\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.90), so by point 1 of the same lemma

\[J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek n\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek n\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n . \tag{8.90}\]

For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) this is \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) from definition 8.81. The unit is kg m\(^2\).

The name was already met in section 8.7, and in section 5.4.3 we said that we would treat it more generally in this chapter. The form is the same as for \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) of a section: the square of the distance from the axis, multiplied by a small piece, and a sum over the pieces. There the piece is an area of the section and measures the resistance to bending, here the piece is a mass and measures the resistance to a change of rotation. The name is the same because the computation is the same, and the substance is different.

Let us compute the moment of the simplest body that we need, a uniform thin bar of length \(L\) and mass \(m\) (figure 8.90). We idealize the bar as a segment with mass \(m/L\) per unit length, that is, as a body without a section. A point at the distance \(s\) from the axis perpendicular to the bar has \(d = |s|\). The moment is an integral over the bar as in definition 8.81, with the density per volume replaced by the density \(m/L\) per length, and a piece of length \(\Delta s\) has the mass \((m/L)\,\Delta s\). With the axis through the middle of the bar, that is, with \(s\) from \(-L/2\) to \(L/2\),

\[J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2} \frac mL\,s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac mL\cdot\frac{2}{3}\Big(\frac L2\Big)^3 = \frac{m\,L^2}{12} , \tag{8.91}\]

and with the axis through the end, \(s\) from \(0\) to \(L\),

\[J_{\mathrm{O}} = \int_0^L \frac mL\,s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac mL\cdot\frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{m\,L^2}{3} . \tag{8.92}\]

The integrals follow from the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) with \(\int s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = s^3/3\). With the axis through the middle the mass is closer to the axis and the moment is four times smaller, as the figure shows: the area under the parabola is \(mL^2/12\) for the axis through the middle, and four times larger for the axis through the end. The chosen bar \(BC\) (\(m = 2\) kg, \(L = a = 0{,}5\) m) has \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 2 \cdot 0{,}25/12 = 0{,}04167\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = 2 \cdot 0{,}25/3 = 0{,}1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) about the end. A real bar has a section. If it is steel with a square section and mass \(2\) kg at density \(7800\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), its side is \(h = \sqrt{2/(7800 \cdot 0{,}5)} = 22{,}6\) mm. For the axis through the middle, parallel to a side of the section, a point of the cuboid has \(d^2 = s^2 + w^2\), where \(w\) is the coordinate across the section, and \(\int\rho\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,h^2/12\) by the same computation as (8.91). The exact moment is therefore \(m\,(L^2 + h^2)/12\), and the idealization is off by \(h^2/L^2 = 0{,}2\ \%\). For the bars of mechanism D we therefore neglect it. For a short thick piece we may not: at \(h = L/2\) the error would be \(h^2/L^2 = 25\ \%\).

The tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) of a bar that lies in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek u\) follows from the definition with \(\vek r = s\,\vek u\): \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T = s^2\,(\mat I - \vek u\,\vek u^\T)\), so

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \frac{m\,L^2}{12}\,(\mat I - \vek u\,\vek u^\T) . \tag{8.93}\]

Bar \(BC\) at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) lies in the direction \(\vek u = (-1,\ 1,\ 0)/\sqrt2\), and \(\vek u\,\vek u^\T\) has elements \(\tfrac12\) and \(-\tfrac12\). With \(m\,L^2/12 = 1/24\),

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \frac1{24}\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}02083 & 0{,}02083 & 0 \\ 0{,}02083 & 0{,}02083 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0{,}04167 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} .\]

Off the diagonal, \(J_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\int\rho\,x\,y\,\mathrm{d}V = -(m/L)\int s^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}s = +0{,}02083\), because \(x\) and \(y\) along the bar have opposite signs: from \(G\) towards \(B\), \(x\) goes to the left and \(y\) upwards. The rotation about the axis \(z\) is measured by \(J_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0{,}04167 = m\,L^2/12\), as by (8.91).

The bar is not the only body we shall turn. Wheels, pulleys, cylinders and rings turn about their axis of symmetry, and for them we need the moment of inertia about that axis. Let us start with the simplest body, a thin ring (tanek obroč) of mass \(m\) and radius \(R\) (figure 8.91). We idealize it as a circle along which the mass is spread uniformly, just as we idealized the bar as a segment. The axis passes through the centre perpendicular to the plane of the ring. Every piece of the ring with mass \(\Delta m\) is at the same distance \(R\) from the axis. In the sum of the squared distances multiplied by the masses of the pieces we therefore take \(R^2\) out, and the sum of the masses is left:

\[J = m\,R^2 . \tag{8.94}\]

More than this a body of mass \(m\) that lies within the radius \(R\) cannot have, because none of its pieces is farther from the axis than \(R\).

A solid cylinder (poln valj) of the same mass and the same radius has material near the axis too, so we expect less. How much less? We cut the cylinder, seen along its axis, into thin shells between the radius \(s\) and the radius \(s + \Delta s\). Every shell is almost a ring. Its volume is approximately the circumference times the thickness times the height \(H\) of the cylinder, so its mass is approximately \(\rho\,H\,2\pi s\,\Delta s = (2m/R^2)\,s\,\Delta s\), because the mass of the cylinder is \(m = \rho\,H\,\pi R^2\). By (8.94) it contributes approximately \((2m/R^2)\,s\cdot s^2\,\Delta s\). On the right of figure 8.91 this weight is drawn against the radius \(s\), and the column is the contribution of the shell drawn. For ever thinner shells the sum over the shells is the area under the curve, \(\int_0^R (2m/R^2)\,s^3\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,m\,R^2\), half the moment of the ring. Why exactly half? Most of the mass is in the outer shells, because they have the largest circumference, but the inner ones weigh something too, and the average of the squared distance \(s^2\) over the mass of the cylinder is \(R^2/2\).

That is the idea. In it we said "approximately" twice, so we do the computation exactly as well, and for a hollow cylinder (votel valj), a tube, at once, since it contains both cases: with the inner radius zero it is a solid cylinder, with the inner radius just below the outer one it is a ring. We describe a point of the cylinder by its distance \(s\) from the axis, the angle \(\varphi\) around the axis and the height \(z\). In these three numbers the shell is a box, and over a box we know how to integrate.

Lemma 8.91 Moment of inertia of a hollow cylinder about its axis (vztrajnostni moment votlega valja glede na njegovo os)

Let \(0 \le r_1 < r_2\) and \(H > 0\). Let the body \(B\) consist of the points \((x, y, z)\) with \(r_1^2 < x^2 + y^2 < r_2^2\) and \(0 < z < H\), and let the density \(\rho > 0\) be constant. This is a uniform hollow cylinder with inner radius \(r_1\), outer radius \(r_2\) and height \(H\), and for \(r_1 = 0\) a solid cylinder without its axis. Then \(B\) has the mass \(m = \rho\,\pi\,(r_2^2 - r_1^2)\,H\) and the moment of inertia about the axis \(z\) (definition 8.81)

\[J = \tfrac12\,m\,(r_1^2 + r_2^2) . \tag{8.95}\]

A solid cylinder of radius \(R\) and mass \(m\) has \(J = \tfrac12\,m\,R^2\) about its axis.

Proof

We must show the values of the integrals \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V\), since \(x^2 + y^2\) is the squared distance from the axis \(z\). We carry both over to a box with the change of variables theorem.

  1. Let \(\Phi(s, \varphi, z) = (s\cos\varphi,\ s\sin\varphi,\ z)\) on the open set \(D\) of points \((s, \varphi, z)\) with \(s > 0\) and \(0 < \varphi < 2\pi\). The point \(\Phi(s, \varphi, z)\) is at the distance \(s\) from the axis \(z\), because \(s^2\cos^2\varphi + s^2\sin^2\varphi = s^2\) (lemma 8.11, point 1). In figure 8.91 this is the radius \(s\), and the angle \(\varphi\) runs around the axis.
  2. The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable, because sine and cosine are differentiable with continuous derivatives (theorem 8.13). The matrix of its derivatives has the rows \((\cos\varphi,\ -s\sin\varphi,\ 0)\), \((\sin\varphi,\ s\cos\varphi,\ 0)\) and \((0,\ 0,\ 1)\), and its determinant is \(J_\Phi = s\cos^2\varphi + s\sin^2\varphi = s > 0\). The factor \(s\) is what the circumference of the shell was in the idea: a shell at a larger \(s\) is longer and has more volume at the same thickness.
  3. The map \(\Phi\) is injective. If \(\Phi(s, \varphi, z) = \Phi(s', \varphi', z')\), then \(z = z'\), by step 1 \(s = s'\), and so \(\cos\varphi = \cos\varphi'\) and \(\sin\varphi = \sin\varphi'\). The sine is positive on \((0, \pi)\), zero at \(\pi\) and negative on \((\pi, 2\pi)\), because \(\sin\varphi = -\sin(2\pi - \varphi)\) (lemma 8.11, points 3 and 4). The angles \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi'\) therefore lie in the same one of these three pieces. The cosine is strictly decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) (point 3) and strictly increasing on \([\pi, 2\pi]\), because \(\cos\varphi = \cos(2\pi - \varphi)\) (point 4). Hence \(\varphi = \varphi'\).
  4. Let \(W\) be the box of points with \(r_1 < s < r_2\), \(0 < \varphi < 2\pi\) and \(0 < z < H\), which lies in \(D\). Its image \(\Phi(W)\) consists of the points of the body \(B\) except those with \(y = 0\) and \(x > 0\). Indeed, a point \((x, y, z)\) of the body has \(s = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) between \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), and \((x/s,\ y/s)\) is a point of the unit circle. For \(y \ge 0\) it equals \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\) for one \(\varphi \in [0, \pi]\) (definition 8.10, point 1), and for \(y < 0\) we have \((x/s,\ -y/s) = (\cos\psi, \sin\psi)\) for one \(\psi \in (0, \pi)\) and therefore \((x/s,\ y/s) = (\cos(2\pi - \psi),\ \sin(2\pi - \psi))\). The angle \(\varphi = 0\) occurs only for \(y = 0\), \(x > 0\).
  5. The points left out, \(y = 0\) and \(r_1 < x < r_2\), lie in a rectangle in the plane \(y = 0\). It has zero volume (lemma 4.26, points 1 and 3), because it is a part of the graph of a constant function. By lemma 4.32 (points 4 and 5), \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} f\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} f\,\mathrm{d}V\) for every function \(f\) continuous on \(B\): a cut along one radius does not change the integral.
  6. By theorem 4.59 and steps 2 and 3, \(\int_{\Phi(\mathrm{W})} f\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{W}} f(\Phi)\,s\,\mathrm{d}V\). For \(f = \rho\) we integrate over the box first in \(z\), then in \(\varphi\) and last in \(s\) (theorem 4.9, the fundamental theorem 3.55): \(m = \rho\,H\cdot 2\pi\int_{\mathrm{r}_1}^{r_2} s\,\mathrm{d}s = \rho\,\pi\,(r_2^2 - r_1^2)\,H\).
  7. For \(f = \rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\), by step 1, \(f(\Phi) = \rho\,s^2\) and \(J = \rho\,H\cdot 2\pi\int_{\mathrm{r}_1}^{r_2} s^3\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,\rho\,\pi\,H\,(r_2^4 - r_1^4)\). This is the area under the curve \(s^3\), here between \(r_1\) and \(r_2\). Since \(r_2^4 - r_1^4 = (r_2^2 - r_1^2)(r_2^2 + r_1^2)\), by step 6 \(J = \tfrac12\,m\,(r_1^2 + r_2^2)\).
  8. For \(r_1 = 0\) and \(r_2 = R\), \(B\) is the solid cylinder without the points of its axis. The axis has zero volume, so as in step 5 \(J = \tfrac12\,m\,R^2\) for the solid cylinder too.

Source: FPGL, Table A1.1 (PDF p. 515): a hollow cylinder with outer diameter \(D\) and inner diameter \(d\) has the polar moment of inertia \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = M\,(D^2 + d^2)/8\), which is (8.95) written with the diameters. The proof in steps is not from the source.

On the left is the box \(W\) in the space of the numbers \((s, \varphi, z)\), here in the plane \((s, \varphi)\), because \(z\) does not change. The map \(\Phi\) wraps it into the cross-section of the cylinder in the middle, where the angle \(\varphi\) runs around the axis from the omitted radius, \(y = 0\) and \(x > 0\), back to it. On the right is the unit circle with the point \((x/s,\ y/s)\) of the body. The upper half has \(y \ge 0\) and an angle in \([0, \pi]\), the lower half has \(y < 0\) and an angle in \((\pi, 2\pi)\).

The lemma has confirmed the idea with the shells and has given both cases at once. For \(r_1 = 0\) it is the solid cylinder with \(\tfrac12\,m\,R^2\). As \(r_1\) tends to \(r_2 = R\), \(\tfrac12\,m\,(r_1^2 + r_2^2)\) tends to \(m\,R^2\) of the ring. When may a tube be taken for a thin ring? A tube with mean radius \(R\) and wall thickness \(t\) has \(r_1 = R - t/2\) and \(r_2 = R + t/2\), so \(J = m\,(R^2 + t^2/4)\). Formula (8.94) is off by \(t^2/(4R^2)\), for a wall \(t = R/10\) by \(0{,}25\ \%\), and for a thick tube with \(t = R\) already by \(25\ \%\).

Numbers: a cylinder of mass \(m = 2\) kg and radius \(R = 0{,}1\) m has \(J = \tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot 0{,}01 = 0{,}01\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), a thin ring of the same mass and radius \(0{,}02\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), and a tube with \(r_1 = 0{,}09\) m and \(r_2 = 0{,}1\) m has \(\tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot(0{,}0081 + 0{,}01) = 0{,}0181\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). The height \(H\) is not in the formulas: a long cylinder and a thin plate of the same mass and radius have the same moment about their axis, because the distances from the axis do not change along it. In the lectures the radius of the cylinder is written with a small \(r\), so \(J = \tfrac12\,m\,r^2\) for the cylinder and \(J = m\,r^2\) for the ring, and the kinetic energy is written \(E_{\mathrm{k}}\).

8.8.4 Kinetic energy of a rigid body

Now we have everything for the answer to the question at the start.

Theorem 8.92 Kinetic energy of a rigid body (kinetična energija togega telesa)

Let a rigid body with mass \(m\), centre of mass \(G\) and inertia tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) move by definition 8.39. Let \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the velocity of the centre of mass and \(\vek\omega\) the angular velocity. Then the kinetic energy of the body is

\[T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega . \tag{8.96}\]

In plane motion \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\) with \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}} = \int\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the kinetic energy splits into two parts, because the mixed term vanishes owing to the property of the centre of mass. We write the velocity of a point with the velocity of the centre of mass. The steps are drawn in figure 8.87 for bar \(BC\).

  1. Let \(\vek x\) be a point of the body and \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). By (8.87) of lemma 8.87 with \(\vek p = \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\), \(\vek v = \vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r\): the velocity of a piece is the velocity of the centre of mass and the addition due to the turning.
  2. By the formula for the square of a sum, \(|\vek v|^2 = |\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + 2\,\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\vek\omega\times\vek r) + |\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\).
  3. We multiply by \(\tfrac12\,\rho\) and integrate over the body. By the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.32, point 1) the first term is \(\tfrac12\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}V = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\). This is the energy of the middle picture, in which all the pieces have the velocity of the centre of mass.
  4. The map \(\vek r \mapsto \vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\vek\omega\times\vek r)\) is linear (lemma 2.34, points 2 and 3). Hence the second term is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\big(\vek\omega\times\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\), and by (8.88) the integral is \(\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\). The mixed term vanishes. In figure 8.87 we see this on a pair of pieces: at \(K_1\) the addition points partly along the velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\vek\omega\times\vek r) = +0{,}03125\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), at \(K_2\) on the other side of the centre of mass it points against it and the product is \(-0{,}03125\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). As much as one piece is faster than the centre of mass because of the turning, the piece on the opposite vector is slower. In a body without such symmetry the pieces do not cancel in pairs, but the sum is still zero, because \(G\) is the centre of mass.
  5. In the third term, by lemma 8.90 (point 1), \(\tfrac12\int\rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V = \tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega\). This is the energy of the right picture, of the turning about a centre of mass at rest. The sum of steps 3 to 5 is (8.96).
  6. In plane motion \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}} = \int\rho\,(|\vek r|^2 - z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V = \int\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V\), so \(\tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\).

Source: SHABANA (3.234)-(3.236) (PDF pp. 159-160), which writes the kinetic energy of a rigid body as the sum of the translational part \(T_{\mathrm{RR}}\) and the rotational part \(T_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12\,\bar{\vek\omega}^\T\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta}\,\bar{\vek\omega}\) and says that the mixed part \(T_{\mathrm{R}\theta}\) is zero if the origin of the body is at the centre of mass. The proof by steps is not from the source.

The proof has shown that the energy of a rigid body splits into the energy as if all the mass were at the centre of mass and the energy of rotation about it, and that the rotational part depends on the body only through \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\).

The proof used two assumptions. The first is rigidity: without it the velocities of the pieces are not \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r\), and the body also has the energy of its changing shape, which the formula does not see. The second is that the velocities are measured from the centre of mass. If we take another point of the body, the mixed term of step 4 stays. For bar \(BC\) with the point \(C\) in place of \(G\) and with the numbers from below, \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{C}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{C}}\,\dot\theta^2 = 0{,}5 + 0{,}0833 = 0{,}5833\) J, which is \(75\ \%\) more than the true value \(0{,}3333\) J. The mixed term \(m\,\vek v_{\mathrm{C}}\cdot\big(\vek\omega\times(\vek G - \vek C)\big) = -0{,}25\) J is missing. The formula without the mixed term holds for one more point, the one that is at rest, since there the first term is zero and the mixed one with it. We use this below.

Let us test it on bar \(BC\) of mechanism D at \(\theta = 45^\circ\), \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\). We took \(m = 2\) kg, \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 = 0{,}5303^2 + 0{,}1768^2 = 0{,}3125\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\vek\omega = -\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0{,}04167\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). So

\[T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 0{,}3125 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}04167 \cdot 1 = 0{,}3125 + 0{,}02083 = 0{,}3333\ \mathrm{J} .\]

These are the numbers under the pictures of figure 8.87: \(0{,}3125\) J for the motion with the centre of mass, \(0{,}0208\) J for the turning and \(0{,}3333\) J together. The turning contributes only a sixteenth, because for this bar the additions due to the turning are much shorter than the velocity of the centre of mass.

For this bar the rotation about the point \(R\) gives the same result with one expression: the squared distance \(|\vek G - \vek R|^2 = 0{,}1768^2 + 0{,}5303^2 = 0{,}3125\ \mathrm{m^2}\) is numerically equal to \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\), because \(\dot\theta = 1\) and \(|\vek\omega| = 1\). For the computation we need the moment about the axis through \(R\). Figure 8.93 shows what changes when we move the axis. Bar \(BC\) has an axis through the centre of mass \(G\) and a parallel axis through the point \(O\), here the instantaneous centre of rotation \(R\), both perpendicular to the plane of the picture. The vector \(\vek g\) from \(O\) to \(G\) has the length \(d = \sqrt{0{,}3125} = 0{,}559\) m. The piece \(K\) is at \(\vek r\) from the centre of mass and at \(\vek g + \vek r\) from the point \(O\). The piece \(K'\) on the other side of the centre of mass is at \(-\vek r\) from it and at \(\vek g - \vek r\) from \(O\). With \(|\vek r| = 0{,}15\) m the squared distances from \(O\) are \(0{,}3125 + 0{,}075 + 0{,}0225 = 0{,}41\ \mathrm{m^2}\) and \(0{,}3125 - 0{,}075 + 0{,}0225 = 0{,}26\ \mathrm{m^2}\). The piece \(K\) is farther from the new axis, the piece \(K'\) closer, and the mixed terms \(\pm 0{,}075\) cancel in the sum. What is left is twice \(d^2\) and twice \(|\vek r|^2\). That is the whole content of the next lemma.

Lemma 8.93 Parallel axis theorem for mass (Steinerjev izrek za maso)

Let a rigid body with mass \(m\) and centre of mass \(G\) have the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}}\) about the axis through \(G\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\). The moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the point \(O\), which is at the distance \(d\) from the axis through \(G\), is

\[J_{\mathrm{O},\vek{n}} = J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}} + m\,d^2 . \tag{8.97}\]
Proof

We must show that the term with the mixed product vanishes, as in the parallel axis theorem for a section (theorem 5.28). A point of the body is at the distance \(|\vek n\times(\vek r + \vek g)|\) from the axis through \(O\), where \(\vek r\) is the vector from \(G\) to the point and \(\vek g\) the vector from \(O\) to \(G\). In figure 8.93 \(\vek n\) is perpendicular to the plane of the picture, so \(|\vek n\times\vek y|\) is simply the length of a vector \(\vek y\) in the plane.

  1. By lemma 2.34 (point 2), \(|\vek n\times\vek r + \vek n\times\vek g|^2 = |\vek n\times\vek r|^2 + 2\,(\vek n\times\vek r)\cdot(\vek n\times\vek g) + |\vek n\times\vek g|^2\). For the piece \(K\) this is \(0{,}0225 + 0{,}075 + 0{,}3125 = 0{,}41\ \mathrm{m^2}\).
  2. We multiply by \(\rho\) and integrate. The first term gives \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}}\). The second term is linear in \(\vek r\), so it equals \(2\,\big(\vek n\times\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\cdot(\vek n\times\vek g)\) and by (8.88) it is zero: what the mixed term adds at the piece \(K\) it takes away at the piece \(K'\). In the third term \(|\vek n\times\vek g|\) is the distance \(d\) of the point \(G\) from the axis through \(O\), so the third term is \(d^2\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,d^2\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §3.8 (PDF p. 159) mentions the theorem and treats it in chapter 8.

The theorem holds only if one of the two axes passes through the centre of mass. Between two axes neither of which passes through the centre of mass the mixed term does not vanish, and the moment is carried over in two steps, first to the axis through the centre of mass and then from it. The addition \(m\,d^2\) is always positive: among all parallel axes the one through the centre of mass has the smallest moment of inertia.

Bar \(BC\): \(J_{\mathrm{R}} = J_{\mathrm{G}} + m\,d^2 = 0{,}04167 + 2 \cdot 0{,}3125 = 0{,}6667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{R}}\,\dot\theta^2 = 0{,}3333\) J. The same number. This is so because in an instantaneous rotation about \(R\) the velocity of every point is \(\vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek R)\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{R}}\,\dot\theta^2\). This is a second view of the same energy: instead of the motion with the centre of mass and the turning about it (figure 8.87), only a turning about the point that is at rest at this instant. It is quicker to compute, but it holds only for this instant, because \(R\) moves. Bar \(AB\) rotates about \(A\) with \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and by Steiner \(J_{\mathrm{A}} = \tfrac{m\,a^2}{12} + m\,(\tfrac a2)^2 = \tfrac{m\,a^2}{3} = 0{,}1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) as in (8.92).

The parallel axis theorem also tells how a body hanging on a pin swings, and why a metronome ticks more slowly when the weight on its rod is pushed upwards. The pendulum of example A was a point on a massless rod, which is a mathematical idealization. A real body that turns about a horizontal pin and is pulled back towards the vertical by its weight is called a compound pendulum (fizično nihalo), because its material is spread out in space. The metronome in figure 8.94 is such a body. On the pin \(O\) there is a light rod, below it carries the weight \(m_1 = 40\) g at the distance \(l_1 = 30\) mm, above the sliding weight \(m_2 = 10\) g at the distance \(l_2\), which we set. We neglect the mass of the rod, so that the calculation stays with two point masses. What this costs depends on where the centre of mass is: a rod of mass 2 g from \(30\) mm below the pivot to \(110\) mm above it would raise the equivalent length from \(65\) to \(81\) mm, and with the upper mass at \(100\) mm from \(680\) to \(1187\) mm, because there the centre of mass is only \(4\) mm below the pivot and every small mass above it counts. The numbers of the example therefore show the direction and the order of magnitude, not a real metronome. The centre of mass \(G\) of the two weights is below the pin at the distance \(s\). When we deflect the metronome by the angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, the weight \(m\,\vek g\) at the centre of mass acts on the lever arm \(s\sin\theta\) and brings it back.

Example 8.94 Compound pendulum (fizično nihalo)

A rigid body of mass \(m\) can turn about a horizontal pin \(O\) and moves in the vertical plane \(xy\), with the axis \(y\) pointing upwards. The centre of mass \(G\) is at the distance \(s > 0\) from \(O\), and the angle \(\theta\) measures the segment \(OG\) from the downward vertical. We neglect the friction in the pin and the air resistance.

  1. The pin is at rest, so by corollary 8.83 \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\theta = M_{\mathrm{O}}\), where \(M_{\mathrm{O}}\) is the moment of the weight about \(O\) and \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\) the moment of inertia about the axis of the pin.
  2. The weight per volume is \(-\rho\,g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\). Its moment about \(O\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} (\vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}})\times(-\rho\,g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \big(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(\vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}})\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\times(-g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = m\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}})\times(-g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\), by the linearity of the integral and the definition of the centre of mass (definition 8.81). The weight therefore turns the body as if all of it acted at the centre of mass.
  3. With \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}} = s\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta,\ 0)\) the component \(z\) of this moment is \(M_{\mathrm{O}} = -m\,g\,s\sin\theta\): the force \(m\,g\) on the lever arm \(s\sin\theta\), with the sign that reduces the angle.
  4. By lemma 8.93, \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = J_{\mathrm{G}} + m\,s^2\).
  5. From steps 1 and 3, \(\ddot\theta + \dfrac{m\,g\,s}{J_{\mathrm{O}}}\,\sin\theta = 0\). This is the pendulum equation (8.82) with the length \(l\) replaced by
    \[l_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{J_{\mathrm{O}}}{m\,s} = s + \frac{J_{\mathrm{G}}}{m\,s} . \tag{8.98}\]
    The body therefore moves exactly as a point weight on a massless rod of length \(l_{\mathrm{r}}\), for every initial angle. The length \(l_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the equivalent length (reducirana dolžina) of the compound pendulum.
  6. For angles near zero we replace \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) as for the pendulum in section 8.2.4, with the error bounded by lemma 8.20. By theorem 8.18 the motion is a harmonic oscillation with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l_{\mathrm{r}}} = \sqrt{m\,g\,s/J_{\mathrm{O}}}\) and the period \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{l_{\mathrm{r}}/g}\).

Source: NORTON (10.10a) to (10.10g) (PDF pp. 587-588): a body hung on a knife edge, with the equation \(-(W\sin\theta)\,r = I_{\mathrm{ZZ}}\,\mathrm{d}^2\theta/\mathrm{d}t^2\) and the period \(\tau\) from \(\sqrt{W r/I_{\mathrm{ZZ}}}\,\tau = 2\pi\), used there to measure a moment of inertia from a measured period. The transfer to the axis through the centre of mass is NORTON (10.8) (PDF p. 586). The equivalent length and the metronome example are not from the source.

Equation (8.98) has two terms. The first, \(s\), is the length of the pendulum we would get if all the mass were squeezed into the centre of mass. The second, \(J_{\mathrm{G}}/(m\,s)\), is always positive: a body with its material spread around the centre of mass swings more slowly than a point at its centre of mass. As \(s\) tends to zero, the second term grows beyond all bounds. Why? The inertia \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\) does not fall below \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\), while the lever arm \(s\sin\theta\) of the weight tends to zero. A body hung near its centre of mass is hardly brought back by its weight.

The metronome uses this. The total mass is \(m = 50\) g. With the upper weight at \(l_2 = 40\) mm the centre of mass is at \(s = (m_1 l_1 - m_2 l_2)/m = (1200 - 400)/50 = 16\) mm below the pin, the moment is \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2 = 5{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) (two point weights, each with \(m\,d^2\) by definition 8.81), and \(l_{\mathrm{r}} = J_{\mathrm{O}}/(m\,s) = 65\) mm. Now we push the weight \(60\) mm upwards to \(l_2 = 100\) mm. The centre of mass rises to \(s = (1200 - 1000)/50 = 4\) mm, the moment grows to \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = 1{,}36\cdot 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), and \(l_{\mathrm{r}} = 680\) mm. A metronome a little over ten centimetres tall swings like a pendulum more than half a metre long. The period of small swings grows from \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{0{,}065/9{,}81} = 0{,}51\) s to \(1{,}65\) s. A metronome ticks twice per swing, so \(235\) times and \(73\) times a minute. Of the two changes the lever arm counts most: \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\) grew \(2{,}6\) times, while \(s\) became four times smaller, together a \(10{,}5\) times longer pendulum.

And what if we push the weight still higher? At \(l_2 = 120\) mm, \(m_1 l_1 = m_2 l_2\), the centre of mass is at the pin, \(s = 0\) and step 5 is gone: the equation is \(J_{\mathrm{O}}\,\ddot\theta = 0\), the weight does not bring the body back, and the metronome stays in any position or turns uniformly. Still higher, the centre of mass is above the pin and the upright position is unstable, as for the inverted pendulum in section 8.6. So the condition \(s > 0\) in the example is not there only because of the division.

The same formula holds for every hanging body. Bar \(BC\) (\(m = 2\) kg, \(L = 0{,}5\) m), hung by its end, has \(s = L/2 = 0{,}25\) m and \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = 0{,}1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) ((8.92)), so \(l_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}1667/(2\cdot 0{,}25) = 0{,}3333\) m, two thirds of the length of the bar, and \(T_0 = 1{,}16\) s. Hung \(10\) mm from the middle it has \(l_{\mathrm{r}} = s + L^2/(12\,s) = 0{,}01 + 2{,}083 = 2{,}09\) m and \(T_0 = 2{,}90\) s.

By theorem 2.83 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix, the symmetric tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) has three mutually perpendicular directions in which it is diagonal.

Corollary 8.95 Principal axes of inertia (glavne osi vztrajnosti)

For a rigid body with interior points there are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) and numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) such that \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} = J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}_\mathrm{i}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(G\) in the direction \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\vek\omega = \omega_1\,\vek n_1 + \omega_2\,\vek n_2 + \omega_3\,\vek n_3\),

\[\tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \tfrac12\,\big(\lambda_1\,\omega_1^2 + \lambda_2\,\omega_2^2 + \lambda_3\,\omega_3^2\big) .\]
Proof

We must show that theorem 2.83 holds for \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) and that the eigenvalues are moments of inertia.

  1. The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is symmetric (definition 8.89) and by theorem 2.83 it has vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. We multiply by \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the scalar product: \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,|\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}|^2 = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\). By (8.90) this is \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}_\mathrm{i}}\), and by lemma 8.90 (point 2) it is positive.
  3. From \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the orthonormality of \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) it follows that \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2\).

Source: The proof is not from the source, theorem 2.83 is ours.

We draw the body as a box with centre of mass \(G\). Through \(G\) go the mutually perpendicular axis \(\vek n_1\), axis \(\vek n_2\) and axis \(\vek n_3\), each with a moment of inertia \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\). We split the angular velocity \(\vek\omega\) into the components \(\omega_1\,\vek n_1\), \(\omega_2\,\vek n_2\) and \(\omega_3\,\vek n_3\).

The tensor of the bar (8.93) has principal axes in the direction of the bar (\(\lambda = 0\), as for an idealized bar without a section) and two perpendicular to it (\(\lambda = m\,L^2/12\)), one in the plane of the motion and one perpendicular to it. The zero value is a consequence of the idealization, because a bar with a section has a small but positive moment about the axis in the direction of the bar.

8.8.5 Kinetic energy in generalized coordinates

The equations of motion of section 8.9 need \(T\) as a function of the generalized coordinates \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 8.46), their derivatives and time. Let us take a system of rigid bodies \(k = 1, \dots, K\) with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and inertia tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\), and if needed point masses, which we treat as bodies without rotation. The position of body \(k\) is given by \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\), continuously differentiable in \(\vek q\) and \(t\) (the map from coordinates). Which terms do we get?

Independence of the coordinates, the third requirement of definition 8.46, says one more thing that we need for this theorem: when \(\vek q\) changes, the system really moves. Whoever writes the kinetic energy with the rates \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) must know that a nonzero \(\dot{\vek q}\) does not give a resting system.

Lemma 8.96 Nonzero rates of the coordinates move a body (neničelne hitrosti koordinat gibljejo telo)

Let \(\vek q\) be generalized coordinates of a system of mass points and rigid bodies by definition 8.46, \(\mat U = \partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) and \(\vek c \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\vek c \neq \vek 0\). Let \(\dot{\vek x} = \mat U\,\vek c\) be the column of velocities of the numbers of the configuration (for scleronomic constraints this is the velocity of the configuration at \(\dot{\vek q} = \vek c\), for rheonomic ones its part without \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\)). Then there exists a mass point with nonzero velocity or a body that has nonzero angular velocity or whose centre of mass has nonzero velocity.

Proof

We must show that a nonzero column \(\mat U\,\vek c\) means motion of a mass point or of a body, and that for a body this means motion of the centre of mass or rotation.

  1. The matrix \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots by definition, so \(\mat U\,\vek c = \vek 0\) only for \(\vek c = \vek 0\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 8.48). The column \(\dot{\vek x} = \mat U\,\vek c\) is therefore nonzero: some number of the configuration has a nonzero velocity.
  2. The numbers of the configuration are the coordinates of mass points, the coordinates of the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) of each body and its angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (in the plane, definition 8.42) or the elements of the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (in space, as for the free rigid body in section 8.4.5). If the velocity of a coordinate of a mass point is nonzero, this point moves and the lemma is proved.
  3. Let the velocity of the angle \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) or of some element \(\dot{\mat Q}_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the body \(k\) be nonzero. The angular velocity is by definition 8.86 determined by the matrix \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\), which is zero exactly when \(\dot{\mat Q} = \vek 0\), since \(\mat Q\) is invertible. In the plane the angular velocity is \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). So the angular velocity of the body \(k\) is nonzero.
  4. Let the angular velocity of every body be zero and the velocity of some coordinate of the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the body \(k\) nonzero. By (8.87) of lemma 8.87 with the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) the velocity of the centre of mass is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}} + \vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} \times (\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}) = \vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}\), because \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0\). The velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}\) has a nonzero component, so \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) is nonzero too.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

For the kinetic energy we may therefore say: if \(\dot{\vek q} \neq \vek 0\), the centre of mass of some body moves, some body rotates, or some mass point moves.

Theorem 8.97 Kinetic energy in generalized coordinates (kinetična energija v posplošenih koordinatah)

Let \(T\) be the kinetic energy of a system of rigid bodies with interior points and point masses, with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) and inertia tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat 0\) for a point mass), and let it be given with generalized coordinates \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) by definition 8.46. Then

\[T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q, t)\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek b(\vek q, t)\cdot\dot{\vek q} + T_0(\vek q, t) , \tag{8.99}\]

where \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\) and \(T_0\) are continuous functions, \(\mat M\) is a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix, and it is positive definite. If the constraints do not depend on time (scleronomic), \(\vek b = \vek 0\), \(T_0 = 0\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\). The matrix \(\mat M\) is called the mass matrix (masna matrika) of the system.

Proof

We must show that \(T\) is a polynomial of the second degree in \(\dot{\vek q}\), and that the quadratic part is positive. We write the velocities in theorem 8.92 with \(\dot{\vek q}\).

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 1), \(\dot{\vek c}_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \dfrac{\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} + \dfrac{\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial t}\) and likewise for \(\dot{\mat Q}_{\mathrm{k}}\). Hence the velocity of the centre of mass is, by lemma 8.87, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and, by definition 8.86, the angular velocity is \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) are \(3 \times n\) matrices, \(\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) vectors, all depending only on \(\vek q\) and \(t\). For scleronomic constraints \(\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0\).
  2. By theorem 8.92, \(T = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(\tfrac12\,m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}})\big)\). The tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is, by lemma 8.90 (point 3), a function of \(\vek q\) and \(t\).
  3. We expand the squares. Because \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric, \((\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\). We get (8.99) with
    \[\begin{aligned} \mat M &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \\ \vek b &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \\ T_0 &= \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\big) . \end{aligned}\]
  4. The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric, because each \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric (lemma 1.102).
  5. By step 3, \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}|^2 + (\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\big) \ge 0\), because \(m_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\), \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) of a body is positive definite by lemma 8.90, and \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat 0\) for a point mass. Let \(\dot{\vek q} \neq \vek 0\). The columns \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) are the velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity of body \(k\), computed from the velocities \((\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) of the numbers of the configuration without the part \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\). By lemma 8.96 with \(\vek c = \dot{\vek q}\), for at least one body or mass point one of them is nonzero, and the term of this body is positive. Hence \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} > 0\) and \(\mat M\) is positive definite.

Source: GR (1.30)-(1.33) (PDF p. 45): \(T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0\) with terms of degree \(2\), \(1\) and \(0\) in \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The positivity of \(\mat M\) and the proof by steps are not from the source.

GR calls the terms \(T_2\), \(T_1\) and \(T_0\) the relative, mutual and transport kinetic energy. Positive definiteness matters because in section 8.9 we shall solve the equations of motion for \(\ddot{\vek q}\), and for that we need an invertible matrix.

Let us test the theorem on three examples.

Example A, pendulum. A point mass \(m\) on a rigid bar of length \(l\), at the angle \(\theta\) from the vertical. The position is \(l\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta)\), the velocity \(l\,\dot\theta\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) with length \(l\,|\dot\theta|\), so \(T = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2\) and \(M = m\,l^2\) is a \(1 \times 1\) mass matrix. For \(m = 1\) kg and \(l = 1\) m, \(M = 1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). If the support moves horizontally by a prescribed law \(\xi(t)\), the position is \((\xi + l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\) and the velocity \((\dot\xi + l\,\dot\theta\cos\theta,\ l\,\dot\theta\sin\theta)\). After squaring,

\[T = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2 + m\,l\,\dot\xi\cos\theta\,\dot\theta + \tfrac12\,m\,\dot\xi^2 .\]

Here \(M = m\,l^2\), \(b = m\,l\,\dot\xi\cos\theta\) and \(T_0 = \tfrac12\,m\,\dot\xi^2\). The terms \(b\) and \(T_0\) are nonzero because the constraint (the support) now contains time (rheonomic constraint). We checked the computation with SymPy.

Example C, rigid bar on two springs. A uniform thin bar (as above) of length \(L = 2\) m and mass \(m = 30\) kg moves along a guide only vertically and rotates about the axis \(z\). The coordinates are the vertical displacement \(y\) of the centre of mass and the rotation \(\varphi\). The velocity of the centre of mass is \(\dot y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the moment about the middle is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m\,L^2/12 = 10\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) by (8.91). By theorem 8.92, \(T = \tfrac12\cdot 30\,\dot y^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 10\,\dot\varphi^2\) and

\[\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The coordinates do not mix, because \(y\) is the coordinate of the centre of mass (so there is no mixed term in theorem 8.92) and because the constraints are scleronomic (\(\vek b = \vek 0\)). If instead of \((y, \varphi)\) we choose the displacements of the ends \(y_1 = y - \sin\varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + \sin\varphi\) (the ends are \(L/2 = 1\) m from the centre), then \(\dot y = (\dot y_1 + \dot y_2)/2\) and \(\dot\varphi = (\dot y_2 - \dot y_1)/(2\cos\varphi)\). The term with \(\dot\varphi^2\) therefore has the factor \(1/\cos^2\varphi\), which at \(\varphi = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad equals \(1 + 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\). For such a small rotation we replace the factor \(1/\cos^2\varphi\) by \(1\), which changes the second term by less than \(2\cdot 10^{-6}\) of its value. Then

\[T \approx \tfrac12\cdot 30\cdot\tfrac14\,(\dot y_1 + \dot y_2)^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 10\cdot\tfrac14\,(\dot y_2 - \dot y_1)^2 = 5\,\dot y_1^2 + 5\,\dot y_1\,\dot y_2 + 5\,\dot y_2^2 , \qquad \mat M \approx \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 5 & 10 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The mass matrix is no longer diagonal. The same bar with the same motions has a different \(\mat M\) in the new coordinates, because the coefficients of \(\dot q_{\mathrm{r}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are different. We shall need this in vibrations, where the coupling moves between the mass and the stiffness matrix.

Example D, toggle mechanism. Term by term. The bars have masses \(m_{\mathrm{b}} = 2\) kg, the slider \(m_{\mathrm{C}} = 1\) kg, \(a = 0{,}5\) m.

  1. Bar \(AB\) rotates about \(A\) with \(\dot\theta\). By Steiner, \(J_{\mathrm{A}} = \tfrac{m_{\mathrm{b}}\,a^2}{3} = \tfrac16\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{AB}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac16\,\dot\theta^2\).
  2. Bar \(BC\) has \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = a\dot\theta\,(-\tfrac32\sin\theta,\ \tfrac12\cos\theta)\), so \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 = a^2\,\dot\theta^2\,(\tfrac94\sin^2\theta + \tfrac14\cos^2\theta) = a^2\,\dot\theta^2\,(\tfrac14 + 2\sin^2\theta)\). The angular velocity is \(-\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = \tfrac{m_{\mathrm{b}}\,a^2}{12} = \tfrac1{24}\). Hence \(T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12\,\big(2\cdot\tfrac14\,(\tfrac14 + 2\sin^2\theta) + \tfrac1{24}\big)\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\,\big(\tfrac16 + \sin^2\theta\big)\,\dot\theta^2\).
  3. The slider moves with \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-2a\dot\theta\sin\theta,\ 0)\), so \(T_{\mathrm{C}} = \tfrac12\cdot 1\cdot 4a^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\,\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2\).

The sum of the three terms is \(T = \tfrac12\,m(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2\) with

\[m(\theta) = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 + \sin^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = \tfrac13 + 2\sin^2\theta\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} . \tag{8.100}\]

At \(\theta = 0\), \(m = 0{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) (the slider is at rest, because \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = \vek 0\)) and at \(\theta = 45^\circ\), \(m = 1{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), which agrees with the sum from the example above: \(\tfrac16 + 0{,}6667 + 0{,}5 = 1{,}3333\) for bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) and the slider. The mass matrix of the mechanism is therefore not constant: it depends on the position \(\theta\), because with the position the ratios of the velocities of the points change. In section 8.9 this will show up as the term with \(\dot\theta^2\) in the equation of motion.

On the axes are the angle \(\theta\) and the mass matrix \(m(\theta)\). The graph rises from its smallest value at \(\theta = 0\). At \(\theta = 0\) \(m = 0{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and the sketch shows the mechanism stretched out. At \(\theta = 45^\circ\) \(m = 1{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and the sketch shows its pose.
