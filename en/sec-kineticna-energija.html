Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.8 Kinetic energy of a rigid body

The equations of motion, which we derive in section 8.9, need the energy of motion (kinetična energija) of the system written with generalized coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and their time derivatives \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\). For a point mass \(m\) with velocity \(\vek v\) we know it, \(\tfrac12 m\,|\vek v|^2\). In a rigid body (definition 1.11), however, each of infinitely many points has a different velocity. How fast does each point of a turning body move, and what is \(\tfrac12\int\rho\,|\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\)?

As an example we take the bar \(BC\) of mechanism D. The mechanism has bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, each of mass \(2\) kg. Bar \(AB\) is pinned at \(A\), the bars are joined at \(B\) by a pin, and the other end \(C\) of bar \(BC\) is hinged to a slider of mass \(1\) kg that slides along a horizontal guide through \(A\). The angle \(\theta\) measures bar \(AB\) above the horizontal. Point \(B\) moves on a circle about \(A\), point \(C\) on a straight line, and the points of bar \(BC\) in between on still other curves. How a rigid body can move at all, we found in section 8.4.1: the position of a point at time \(t\) is \(\vek x(t) = \vek c(t) + \mat Q(t)\,\vek X\) ((8.24)) with an orthogonal matrix \(\mat Q(t)\) of determinant \(1\) (lemma 8.39). The answer to the question therefore has two parts. First we compute from this the velocity of every point, then the energy of motion from the mass of the body, the velocity of its centre of mass, and a quantity that says how the mass is distributed around it.

8.8.1 Velocity of a point and angular velocity

The velocity of a point is the time derivative of its position vector. A matrix is differentiated by differentiating each element. From (8.24) we get

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek x} = \dot{\vek c} + \dot{\mat Q}\,\vek X .\]

In this form the velocity is tied to \(\vek X\), which is hard to see in a calculation. Let us write it with the position of the point at time \(t\). From \(\vek x = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\) and \(\mat Q^\T\mat Q = \mat I\) it follows that \(\vek X = \mat Q^\T(\vek x - \vek c)\), so

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek c} + \mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c) , \qquad \mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T . \tag{8.66}\]

The matrix \(\mat\Omega\) has nine elements. We shall show that, because of the orthogonality of \(\mat Q\), only three of them are independent.

Lemma 8.76 The matrix of the rotation rate (matrika odvoda zasuka)

In a motion of a rigid body as in definition 8.38, the matrix \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\) is antisymmetric (definition 1.102). Let \(\vek\omega = (\Omega_{32},\ \Omega_{13},\ \Omega_{21})\). Then \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y = \vek\omega\times\vek y\) for every vector \(\vek y\).

Proof

We must show \(\mat\Omega^\T = -\mat\Omega\) and then compare components.

  1. The equality \(\mat Q\,\mat Q^\T = \mat I\) holds for every \(t\), so the derivative of the left side is zero. By the rule for the derivative of a product (lemma 1.81), applied to each element of the product, \(\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T + \mat Q\,\dot{\mat Q}^\T = \mat 0\).
  2. By lemma 1.101, \(\mat\Omega^\T = (\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T)^\T = \mat Q\,\dot{\mat Q}^\T\), which by step 1 equals \(-\dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T = -\mat\Omega\). The matrix is antisymmetric.
  3. Hence \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\) and \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ij}} = -\Omega_{\mathrm{ji}}\). Component \(1\) of \(\mat\Omega\,\vek y\) is \(\Omega_{12}\,y_2 + \Omega_{13}\,y_3 = -\Omega_{21}\,y_2 + \Omega_{13}\,y_3\). By (2.32) component \(1\) of \(\vek\omega\times\vek y\) equals \(\omega_2\,y_3 - \omega_3\,y_2 = \Omega_{13}\,y_3 - \Omega_{21}\,y_2\). They are equal.
  4. Components \(2\) and \(3\) give the same with the indices exchanged \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\): \(\Omega_{21}\,y_1 + \Omega_{23}\,y_3 = \Omega_{21}\,y_1 - \Omega_{32}\,y_3\) is \(\omega_3\,y_1 - \omega_1\,y_3\), and \(\Omega_{31}\,y_1 + \Omega_{32}\,y_2 = -\Omega_{13}\,y_1 + \Omega_{32}\,y_2\) is \(\omega_1\,y_2 - \omega_2\,y_1\).

Source: SHABANA (2.42)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61): the matrix \(\dot{\mat A}\mat A^\T\) is skew symmetric and defines a vector \(\vek\omega\). Here the vector is written with components and checked.

Definition 8.77 Angular velocity (kotna hitrost)

The angular velocity (kotna hitrost) of a rigid body at time \(t\) is the vector \(\vek\omega(t) = (\Omega_{32},\ \Omega_{13},\ \Omega_{21})\), where \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\) is from lemma 8.76. The unit is \(1/\mathrm{s}\).

Source: SHABANA (2.43)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61).

The angular velocity is one quantity for the whole body, not for an individual point. The connection with the velocities of the points is the following.

Lemma 8.78 Velocity of a point of a rigid body (hitrost točke togega telesa)

In a motion of a rigid body, the velocity of the point with position \(\vek x\) at time \(t\) is

\[\vek v = \dot{\vek c} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek c) . \tag{8.67}\]

For an arbitrary point \(\vek p\) of the frame with position \(\vek p(t)\) and velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{p}}\) we also have

\[\vek v = \vek v_{\mathrm{p}} + \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek p) . \tag{8.68}\]
Proof

We must show that \(\mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c)\) in (8.66) is a vector product, and the second equality follows by subtraction.

  1. By lemma 8.76 with \(\vek y = \vek x - \vek c\), \(\mat\Omega\,(\vek x - \vek c) = \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek c)\). Inserting into (8.66) gives (8.67).
  2. Equation (8.67) for the point \(\vek p\) gives \(\vek v_{\mathrm{p}} = \dot{\vek c} + \vek\omega\times(\vek p - \vek c)\).
  3. We subtract this equation from (8.67) and use the splitting of a sum in a product (lemma 2.31, points 2 and 3): \(\vek v - \vek v_{\mathrm{p}} = \vek\omega\times\big((\vek x - \vek c) - (\vek p - \vek c)\big) = \vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek p)\).

Source: SHABANA (2.43)-(2.48) (PDF p. 61), where \(\dot{\mat A}\,\bar{\vek u} = \tilde{\vek\omega}\,\mat A\,\bar{\vek u} = \vek\omega\times\vek u\) is written. The second equality is not from the source.

Equation (8.68) says that the velocity of any point follows from the velocity of one chosen point and the angular velocity: a point that is displaced by \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek p\) from the chosen one has the additional velocity \(\vek\omega\times\vek r\), which is perpendicular to \(\vek r\) and to \(\vek\omega\) (lemma 2.31, point 4) and proportional to the distance from the axis that passes through \(\vek p\) in the direction of \(\vek\omega\).

In plane motion in the plane \(xy\), by lemma 8.40 the matrix \(\mat Q\) is a rotation about the axis \(z\) by an angle that we write here as \(\theta(t)\), just as we described the position of a body by a point and an angle in definition 8.41 and in section 8.7 (\(\mat R(\varphi)\) is the upper left part of the matrix \(\mat Q\)). We take the angle \(\theta(t)\) twice continuously differentiable, like the elements of the matrix \(\mat Q\). The derivatives of sine and cosine are \(\sin' = \cos\) and \(\cos' = -\sin\) (theorem 8.12), the angle being measured in radians. Then

\[\mat Q = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T = \dot\theta\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]

We compute the product by rows: the first row of \(\dot{\mat Q}\) is \(\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta,\ 0)\) and with the columns of \(\mat Q^\T\) it gives \(\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos\theta\sin\theta,\ -\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta,\ 0) = \dot\theta\,(0, -1, 0)\), and the second row likewise \(\dot\theta\,(1, 0, 0)\). From \(\Omega_{21} = \dot\theta\) and \(\Omega_{32} = \Omega_{13} = 0\) we get

\[\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} .\]

The angular velocity in plane motion is therefore the rate of change of the angle, which is why we write it \(\dot\theta\), not \(\omega\).

Let us test this on bar \(BC\) of mechanism D at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) and \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) (figure 8.24). Point \(A\) is the origin. The positions are \(\vek B = a\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) and \(\vek C = (2a\cos\theta,\ 0)\), because the bars are equally long and \(C\) is on the guide. The point \(G\) at the middle of bar \(BC\) is the centre of mass (težišče) of the bar (definition 8.73), which we show for a uniform bar in section 8.8.2. So \(\vek G = \tfrac12(\vek B + \vek C) = a\,(\tfrac32\cos\theta,\ \tfrac12\sin\theta)\). The time derivative gives

\[\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = a\dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta) , \qquad \vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-2a\dot\theta\sin\theta,\ 0) , \qquad \vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = a\dot\theta\,\big(-\tfrac32\sin\theta,\ \tfrac12\cos\theta\big) ,\]

numerically \(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = (-0{,}3536,\ 0{,}3536)\) m/s, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-0{,}7071,\ 0)\) m/s and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768)\) m/s. The direction from \(C\) to \(B\) is \((-\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\), which is the direction at the angle \(\pi - \theta\) from the axis \(x\). As \(\theta\) increases this angle decreases, bar \(BC\) turns by \(-\dot\theta\), and the angular velocity is \(\vek\omega = -\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). We check (8.68) with the chosen point \(G\) and the point \(B\). Since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\times(r_{\mathrm{x}}, r_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) = (-r_{\mathrm{y}},\ r_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0)\), for \(\vek r = \vek B - \vek G = (-0{,}1768,\ 0{,}1768)\) m

\[\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768) + (0{,}1768,\ 0{,}1768) = (-0{,}3536,\ 0{,}3536)\ \text{m/s} = \vek v_{\mathrm{B}} .\]

The velocity of point \(B\) is indeed the velocity of point \(G\) plus the addition due to the rotation about \(G\). Figure 8.24 shows this sum as the triangle on the right.

The figure also shows the point \(R\), where the velocity of the bar is zero at this instant. The perpendiculars to \(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}}\) (the line \(AB\)) and to \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}}\) (the vertical through \(C\)) meet in \(\vek R = (2a\cos\theta,\ 2a\sin\theta) = (0{,}7071,\ 0{,}7071)\) m. For \(\vek p = \vek R\) in (8.68) we take \(\vek r = \vek R - \vek G = (0{,}1768,\ 0{,}5303)\) m, and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r = (-0{,}5303,\ 0{,}1768) + (0{,}5303,\ -0{,}1768) = \vek 0\). At this instant bar \(BC\) turns about the point \(R\). The point \(R\) is the instantaneous centre of rotation (trenutno središče vrtenja) of the bar. It is not attached to the body and moves during the motion, but at this instant it will come in handy.

8.8.2 Mass, centre of mass and kinetic energy

We already have the energy of motion of a point mass. We imagine the body cut into small pieces. The piece at the point \(\vek X\) with a small volume \(\Delta V\) has the mass \(\rho(\vek X)\,\Delta V\), where \(\rho\) is the mass density (masna gostota) at the point, unit kg/m\(^3\). If every piece gets the energy \(\tfrac12 (\rho\,\Delta V)\,|\vek v|^2\) and we add them up, then for ever smaller pieces we get the integral over the body from section 4.2. Why the pieces cannot move each in its own way, we found out above: their velocities are connected by (8.67).

Mass, centre of mass and the moment of inertia about an axis have already been defined (definition 8.73), and we define nothing new here. We take a body \(B\) with a continuous density \(\rho > 0\) that at time \(0\) occupies a set with volume (definition 4.30). The mass is \(m = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\mathrm{d}V\) and the centre of mass at time \(0\) is the point \(G\) with the position vector \(\vek X_{\mathrm{G}} = \dfrac1m\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\vek X\,\mathrm{d}V\), where we integrate componentwise. The word "centre of mass" (težišče) means here the centre of mass of the body, weighted by density, not the centroid of an area of a figure (definition 5.21). If the body is uniformly dense (\(\rho\) constant), the density cancels in the average and the centre of mass is the centroid of its volume, for example the middle of a uniform bar or the centre of a uniform ball. For a non-uniform density the two points differ.

At time \(t\) the piece that was at \(\vek X\) is at \(\vek x = \Phi(\vek X) = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X\), and the density at the point \(\Phi(\vek X)\) is \(\rho(\vek X)\). Does the piece have the same mass? The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable, the matrix of its derivatives is \(\mat Q\), the Jacobian determinant is \(\det\mat Q = 1\) (lemma 8.39), and it is injective, because \(\mat Q\,\vek X = \mat Q\,\vek Y\) gives \(\vek X = \mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek X = \mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek Y = \vek Y\). By theorem 4.59 on the change of variables, the integral of a continuous function over the image \(\Phi(W)\) of every piece \(W\) with volume is therefore equal to the integral over \(W\) of the same function evaluated at \(\Phi(\vek X)\), since \(|J_\Phi| = 1\). With the function \(1\) this says that the volume of the piece is kept, and with the density that the mass of the piece is kept. So at time \(t\) the position of the centre of mass is \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \dfrac1m\int_{\mathrm{B}}\rho\,(\vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X)\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek c + \mat Q\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}}\), because \(\mat Q\) is constant when we integrate over \(\vek X\), and we take it out by the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.32, point 1). Let \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \mat Q\,(\vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}})\) be the vector from the centre of mass to the point at time \(t\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \mat Q\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(\vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}})\,\mathrm{d}V = \mat Q\,(m\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}} - m\,\vek X_{\mathrm{G}}) = \vek 0 . \tag{8.69}\]

So at every time the property of the centre of mass from definition 8.73, \(\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\), is fulfilled. For a uniform bar of length \(L\) with the coordinate \(s\) measured from the middle, \(\int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2}s\,\mathrm{d}s = 0\), so the centre of mass is the middle of the bar.

Definition 8.79 Kinetic energy of a system (kinetična energija)

The kinetic energy of a body with mass \(m\), density \(\rho\) and point velocity \(\vek v\) is

\[T = \frac12\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek v|^2\,\mathrm{d}V .\]

A point mass \(m\) with velocity \(\vek v\) has \(T = \tfrac12 m\,|\vek v|^2\). The kinetic energy of a system of bodies and point masses is the sum of the kinetic energies of all the parts. The unit is J.

Source: SHABANA (3.234)-(3.236) (PDF pp. 159-160) writes the kinetic energy of a rigid body as an integral over the body, and GR (1.22) (PDF p. 42) the kinetic energy of a system of particles as the sum of \(\tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v|^2\) over the particles.

For a point mass we took \(\tfrac12 m\,v^2\) as known. How is the kinetic energy of a body written with the mass, the velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity? The answer needs a quantity that says how the mass is distributed around the centre of mass.

8.8.3 Inertia tensor and moment of inertia

At angular velocity \(\vek\omega\) the point with the vector \(\vek r\) from the centre of mass has, by (8.68), the additional velocity \(\vek\omega\times\vek r\). The square of its length is, by point 5 of lemma 2.31, \(|\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r|^2 - (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). All the components are quadratic in \(\vek\omega\) and in \(\vek r\) and we can write them with a matrix that does not depend on \(\vek\omega\). Let \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\) be the matrix with elements \(r_{\mathrm{i}}\,r_{\mathrm{j}}\) (column times row).

Definition 8.80 Inertia tensor (vztrajnostni tenzor)

Let \(B\) be a rigid body with density \(\rho\) and centre of mass \(G\) (definition 8.73), and \(\vek r\) the vector from \(G\) to a point of the body. The inertia tensor (vztrajnostni tenzor) of the body about the centre of mass is the \(3 \times 3\) matrix

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,\big(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T\big)\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{8.70}\]

where we integrate elementwise. With \(\vek r = (x, y, z)\) the elements are

\[J_{\mathrm{xx}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(y^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad J_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,x\,y\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad J_{\mathrm{yy}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,(x^2 + z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V , \quad \text{and so on.}\]

The unit is kg m\(^2\).

Source: SHABANA (3.231), (3.233) (PDF p. 159): \(\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta} = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}^\T\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}\,\mathrm{d}V\), written out with the elements \(i_{11}\), \(i_{12}\) and others. The matrix \(\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}^\T\tilde{\bar{\vek u}}\) equals \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T\).

The diagonal elements are sums of \(\int\rho\,(\text{square of the distance from the axis})\,\mathrm{d}V\), for example \(J_{\mathrm{xx}}\) measures the distance from the axis \(x\). The off-diagonal elements have, unlike the product of inertia of a section \(I_{\mathrm{yz}}\) in section 5.4, the sign minus, because this makes \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) exactly the matrix from (8.70). The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is symmetric, because \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\) is symmetric. During the motion it changes, since \(\vek r\) rotates. Let us see how.

Lemma 8.81 Properties of the inertia tensor (lastnosti vztrajnostnega tenzorja)

Let \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the inertia tensor of a rigid body in a motion as in definition 8.38, and let the body have interior points. For every vector \(\vek\omega\):

  1. \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  2. \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega > 0\) for every \(\vek\omega \neq \vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is therefore positive definite (definition 6.27).
  3. \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(t) = \mat Q(t)\,\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}(0)\,\mat Q(t)^\T\).
Proof

We get the first point by a computation from the definition, the second from distances from an axis, the third from the preservation of lengths.

  1. By lemma 2.31 (point 5), \(|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2 = |\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r|^2 - (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). By (8.70), \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \int_{\mathrm{B}}\rho\,\big(|\vek r|^2\,|\vek\omega|^2 - \vek\omega\cdot(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\vek\omega)\big)\,\mathrm{d}V\), and \(\vek\omega\cdot(\vek r\,\vek r^\T\vek\omega) = (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2\). This is the first point.
  2. Let \(\vek\omega \neq \vek 0\) and \(\vek r' = \vek r - \dfrac{\vek\omega\cdot\vek r}{|\vek\omega|^2}\,\vek\omega\) the part of the vector \(\vek r\) perpendicular to \(\vek\omega\). Then \(|\vek r|^2 = |\vek r'|^2 + (\vek\omega\cdot\vek r)^2/|\vek\omega|^2\) and by point 1, \(|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2 = |\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r'|^2\). The value \(|\vek r'|\) is the distance of the point from the line through \(G\) in the direction of \(\vek\omega\). The integrand \(\rho\,|\vek\omega|^2\,|\vek r'|^2\) is continuous and nonnegative, so the integral is nonnegative (lemma 4.32, point 2). The body has an interior point that does not lie on the line, since in every ball there are points outside every line. At it the integrand is positive, so by continuity it is at least \(\varepsilon > 0\) on a smaller ball \(K\) around it that lies in the body. By point 2 of the same lemma, \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} \rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \varepsilon\,V_{\mathrm{K}} > 0\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \ge \int_{\mathrm{K}}\), so the integral over the body is positive.
  3. At time \(t\), \(\vek r = \mat Q\,\vek R\) with \(\vek R = \vek X - \vek X_{\mathrm{G}}\). An orthogonal matrix keeps lengths, \(|\mat Q\,\vek R|^2 = \vek R^\T\mat Q^\T\mat Q\,\vek R = |\vek R|^2\), and \(\vek r\,\vek r^\T = \mat Q\,\vek R\,\vek R^\T\mat Q^\T\). Hence \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T = \mat Q\,(|\vek R|^2\,\mat I - \vek R\,\vek R^\T)\,\mat Q^\T\), and we take \(\mat Q\) out of the integral.

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §3.8 (PDF p. 159) notes that the elements \(\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta}\) in the axes of the body are constant for a rigid body.

Point 3 says that \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is constant in the axes that are fixed to the body. Point 2 says that the rotational energy is not zero, unless the body lies on a single line, like a point mass. And what does the tensor say about the moment of inertia about an axis? By definition 8.73 the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass in the direction of a unit vector \(\vek n\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,d^2\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(d\) is the distance of the point from the axis. This distance is \(d = |\vek n\times\vek r|\) (step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.81), so by point 1 of the same lemma

\[J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}} = \int_{\mathrm{B}} \rho\,|\vek n\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek n\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n . \tag{8.71}\]

For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) this is \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\) from definition 8.73. The unit is kg m\(^2\).

The name was already met in section 8.7, and in section 5.4.3 we said that we would treat it more generally in this chapter. The form is the same as for \(I_{\mathrm{z}} = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) of a section: the square of the distance from the axis, multiplied by a small piece, and a sum over the pieces. There the piece is an area of the section and measures the resistance to bending, here the piece is a mass and measures the resistance to a change of rotation. The name is the same because the computation is the same, and the substance is different.

Let us compute the moment of the simplest body that we need, a uniform thin bar of length \(L\) and mass \(m\) (figure 8.25). We idealize the bar as a segment with mass \(m/L\) per unit length, that is, as a body without a section. A point at the distance \(s\) from the axis perpendicular to the bar has \(d = |s|\). The moment is an integral over the bar as in definition 8.73, with the density per volume replaced by the density \(m/L\) per length, and a piece of length \(\Delta s\) has the mass \((m/L)\,\Delta s\). With the axis through the middle of the bar, that is, with \(s\) from \(-L/2\) to \(L/2\),

\[J_{\mathrm{G}} = \int_{-\mathrm{L}/2}^{L/2} \frac mL\,s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac mL\cdot\frac{2}{3}\Big(\frac L2\Big)^3 = \frac{m\,L^2}{12} , \tag{8.72}\]

and with the axis through the end, \(s\) from \(0\) to \(L\),

\[J_{\mathrm{O}} = \int_0^L \frac mL\,s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac mL\cdot\frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{m\,L^2}{3} . \tag{8.73}\]

The integrals follow from the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40) with \(\int s^2\,\mathrm{d}s = s^3/3\). With the axis through the middle the mass is closer to the axis and the moment is four times smaller, as the figure shows: the area under the parabola is \(mL^2/12\) for the axis through the middle, and four times larger for the axis through the end. The chosen bar \(BC\) (\(m = 2\) kg, \(L = a = 0{,}5\) m) has \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 2 \cdot 0{,}25/12 = 0{,}04167\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{O}} = 2 \cdot 0{,}25/3 = 0{,}1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) about the end. A real bar has a section. If it is steel with a square section and mass \(2\) kg at density \(7800\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), its side is \(h = \sqrt{2/(7800 \cdot 0{,}5)} = 22{,}6\) mm. For the axis through the middle, parallel to a side of the section, a point of the cuboid has \(d^2 = s^2 + w^2\), where \(w\) is the coordinate across the section, and \(\int\rho\,w^2\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,h^2/12\) by the same computation as (8.72). The exact moment is therefore \(m\,(L^2 + h^2)/12\), and the idealization is off by \(h^2/L^2 = 0{,}2\ \%\). For the bars of mechanism D we therefore neglect it.

The tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) of a bar that lies in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek u\) follows from the definition with \(\vek r = s\,\vek u\): \(|\vek r|^2\,\mat I - \vek r\,\vek r^\T = s^2\,(\mat I - \vek u\,\vek u^\T)\), so

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \frac{m\,L^2}{12}\,(\mat I - \vek u\,\vek u^\T) . \tag{8.74}\]

Bar \(BC\) at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) lies in the direction \(\vek u = (-1,\ 1,\ 0)/\sqrt2\), and \(\vek u\,\vek u^\T\) has elements \(\tfrac12\) and \(-\tfrac12\). With \(m\,L^2/12 = 1/24\),

\[\mat J_{\mathrm{G}} = \frac1{24}\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}02083 & 0{,}02083 & 0 \\ 0{,}02083 & 0{,}02083 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0{,}04167 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} .\]

Off the diagonal, \(J_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\int\rho\,x\,y\,\mathrm{d}V = -(m/L)\int s^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} u_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}s = +0{,}02083\), because \(x\) and \(y\) along the bar have opposite signs. The rotation about the axis \(z\) is measured by \(J_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0{,}04167 = m\,L^2/12\), as by (8.72).

8.8.4 Kinetic energy of a rigid body

Now we have everything for the answer to the question at the start.

Theorem 8.82 Kinetic energy of a rigid body (kinetična energija togega telesa)

Let a rigid body with mass \(m\), centre of mass \(G\) and inertia tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) move by definition 8.38. Let \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\) be the velocity of the centre of mass and \(\vek\omega\) the angular velocity. Then the kinetic energy of the body is

\[T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega . \tag{8.75}\]

In plane motion \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\) with \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}} = \int\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V\).

Proof

We must show that the integral of the kinetic energy splits into two parts, because the mixed term vanishes owing to the property of the centre of mass. We write the velocity of a point with the velocity of the centre of mass.

  1. Let \(\vek x\) be a point of the body and \(\vek r = \vek x - \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\). By (8.68) of lemma 8.78 with \(\vek p = \vek x_{\mathrm{G}}\), \(\vek v = \vek v_{\mathrm{G}} + \vek\omega\times\vek r\).
  2. By the formula for the square of a sum, \(|\vek v|^2 = |\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 + 2\,\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\vek\omega\times\vek r) + |\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\).
  3. We multiply by \(\tfrac12\,\rho\) and integrate over the body. By the linearity of the integral (lemma 4.32, point 1) the first term is \(\tfrac12\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}V = \tfrac12\,m\,|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\).
  4. The map \(\vek r \mapsto \vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot(\vek\omega\times\vek r)\) is linear (lemma 2.31, points 2 and 3). Hence the second term is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\big(\vek\omega\times\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\), and by (8.69) the integral is \(\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V = \vek 0\). The mixed term vanishes.
  5. In the third term, by lemma 8.81 (point 1), \(\tfrac12\int\rho\,|\vek\omega\times\vek r|^2\,\mathrm{d}V = \tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega\). The sum of steps 3 to 5 is (8.75).
  6. In plane motion \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = J_{\mathrm{zz}} = \int\rho\,(|\vek r|^2 - z^2)\,\mathrm{d}V = \int\rho\,(x^2 + y^2)\,\mathrm{d}V\), so \(\tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\theta^2\).

Source: SHABANA (3.234)-(3.236) (PDF pp. 159-160), which writes the kinetic energy of a rigid body as the sum of the translational part \(T_{\mathrm{RR}}\) and the rotational part \(T_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12\,\bar{\vek\omega}^\T\bar{\mat I}_{\theta\theta}\,\bar{\vek\omega}\) and says that the mixed part \(T_{\mathrm{R}\theta}\) is zero if the origin of the body is at the centre of mass. The proof by steps is not from the source.

The proof has shown that the energy of a rigid body splits into the energy as if all the mass were at the centre of mass and the energy of rotation about it, and that the rotational part depends on the body only through \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\).

Let us test it on bar \(BC\) of mechanism D at \(\theta = 45^\circ\), \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\). We took \(m = 2\) kg, \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 = 0{,}5303^2 + 0{,}1768^2 = 0{,}3125\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\vek\omega = -\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0{,}04167\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). So

\[T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 0{,}3125 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0{,}04167 \cdot 1 = 0{,}3125 + 0{,}02083 = 0{,}3333\ \mathrm{J} .\]

For this bar the rotation about the point \(R\) gives the same result with one expression: the distance \(|\vek G - \vek R|^2 = 0{,}1768^2 + 0{,}5303^2 = 0{,}3125\ \mathrm{m^2}\) equals \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2\), because \(\dot\theta = 1\) and \(|\vek\omega| = 1\). For the computation we need the moment about the axis through \(R\), which is given by the next lemma.

Lemma 8.83 Parallel axis theorem for mass (Steinerjev izrek za maso)

Let a rigid body with mass \(m\) and centre of mass \(G\) have the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}}\) about the axis through \(G\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\vek n\). The moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the point \(O\), which is at the distance \(d\) from the axis through \(G\), is

\[J_{\mathrm{O},\vek{n}} = J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}} + m\,d^2 . \tag{8.76}\]
Proof

We must show that the term with the mixed product vanishes, as in the parallel axis theorem for a section (theorem 5.28). A point of the body is at the distance \(|\vek n\times(\vek r + \vek g)|\) from the axis through \(O\), where \(\vek r\) is the vector from \(G\) to the point and \(\vek g\) the vector from \(O\) to \(G\).

  1. By lemma 2.31 (point 2), \(|\vek n\times\vek r + \vek n\times\vek g|^2 = |\vek n\times\vek r|^2 + 2\,(\vek n\times\vek r)\cdot(\vek n\times\vek g) + |\vek n\times\vek g|^2\).
  2. We multiply by \(\rho\) and integrate. The first term gives \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}}\). The second term is linear in \(\vek r\), so it equals \(2\,\big(\vek n\times\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\big)\cdot(\vek n\times\vek g)\) and by (8.69) it is zero. In the third term \(|\vek n\times\vek g|\) is the distance \(d\) of the point \(G\) from the axis through \(O\), so the third term is \(d^2\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}V = m\,d^2\).

Source: The proof is not from the source. SHABANA §3.8 (PDF p. 159) mentions the theorem and treats it in chapter 8.

Bar \(BC\): \(J_{\mathrm{R}} = J_{\mathrm{G}} + m\,d^2 = 0{,}04167 + 2 \cdot 0{,}3125 = 0{,}6667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{R}}\,\dot\theta^2 = 0{,}3333\) J. The same number. This is so because in an instantaneous rotation about \(R\) the velocity of every point is \(\vek\omega\times(\vek x - \vek R)\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,J_{\mathrm{R}}\,\dot\theta^2\). Bar \(AB\) rotates about \(A\) with \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and by Steiner \(J_{\mathrm{A}} = \tfrac{m\,a^2}{12} + m\,(\tfrac a2)^2 = \tfrac{m\,a^2}{3} = 0{,}1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) as in (8.73).

By theorem 2.61 on the principal directions of a symmetric matrix, the symmetric tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) has three mutually perpendicular directions in which it is diagonal.

Corollary 8.84 Principal axes of inertia (glavne osi vztrajnosti)

For a rigid body with interior points there are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\vek n_1\), \(\vek n_2\), \(\vek n_3\) and numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) such that \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}} = J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}_\mathrm{i}}\) is the moment of inertia about the axis through \(G\) in the direction \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\). For \(\vek\omega = \omega_1\,\vek n_1 + \omega_2\,\vek n_2 + \omega_3\,\vek n_3\),

\[\tfrac12\,\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \tfrac12\,\big(\lambda_1\,\omega_1^2 + \lambda_2\,\omega_2^2 + \lambda_3\,\omega_3^2\big) .\]
Proof

We must show that theorem 2.61 holds for \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) and that the eigenvalues are moments of inertia.

  1. The matrix \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\) is symmetric (definition 8.80) and by theorem 2.61 it has vectors \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. We multiply by \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the scalar product: \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,|\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}|^2 = \lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\). By (8.71) this is \(J_{\mathrm{G},\vek{n}_\mathrm{i}}\), and by lemma 8.81 (point 2) it is positive.
  3. From \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{\mathrm{i}}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the orthonormality of \(\vek n_{\mathrm{i}}\) it follows that \(\vek\omega\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\vek\omega = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\lambda_{\mathrm{i}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{i}}^2\).

Source: The proof is not from the source, theorem 2.61 is ours.

The tensor of the bar (8.74) has principal axes in the direction of the bar (\(\lambda = 0\), as for an idealized bar without a section) and two perpendicular to it (\(\lambda = m\,L^2/12\)). The zero value is a consequence of the idealization, because a bar with a section has a small but positive moment about the axis in the direction of the bar.

8.8.5 Kinetic energy in generalized coordinates

The equations of motion of section 8.9 need \(T\) as a function of the generalized coordinates \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 8.45), their derivatives and time. Let us take a system of rigid bodies \(k = 1, \dots, K\) with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and inertia tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\), and if needed point masses, which we treat as bodies without rotation. The position of body \(k\) is given by \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\), continuously differentiable in \(\vek q\) and \(t\) (the map from coordinates). Which terms do we get?

Independence of the coordinates, the third requirement of definition 8.45, says one more thing that we need for this theorem: when \(\vek q\) changes, the system really moves. Whoever writes the kinetic energy with the rates \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) must know that a nonzero \(\dot{\vek q}\) does not give a resting system.

Lemma 8.85 Nonzero rates of the coordinates move a body (neničelne hitrosti koordinat gibljejo telo)

Let \(\vek q\) be generalized coordinates of a system of mass points and rigid bodies by definition 8.45, \(\mat U = \partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) and \(\vek c \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\vek c \neq \vek 0\). Let \(\dot{\vek x} = \mat U\,\vek c\) be the column of velocities of the numbers of the configuration (for scleronomic constraints this is the velocity of the configuration at \(\dot{\vek q} = \vek c\), for rheonomic ones its part without \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\)). Then there exists a mass point with nonzero velocity or a body that has nonzero angular velocity or whose centre of mass has nonzero velocity.

Proof

We must show that a nonzero column \(\mat U\,\vek c\) means motion of a mass point or of a body, and that for a body this means motion of the centre of mass or rotation.

  1. The matrix \(\mat U\) has \(n\) pivots by definition, so \(\mat U\,\vek c = \vek 0\) only for \(\vek c = \vek 0\) (step 3 of the proof of lemma 8.47). The column \(\dot{\vek x} = \mat U\,\vek c\) is therefore nonzero: some number of the configuration has a nonzero velocity.
  2. The numbers of the configuration are the coordinates of mass points, the coordinates of the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) of each body and its angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (in the plane, definition 8.41) or the elements of the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (in space, as for the free rigid body in section 8.4.5). If the velocity of a coordinate of a mass point is nonzero, this point moves and the lemma is proved.
  3. Let the velocity of the angle \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) or of some element \(\dot{\mat Q}_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the body \(k\) be nonzero. The angular velocity is by definition 8.77 determined by the matrix \(\mat\Omega = \dot{\mat Q}\,\mat Q^\T\), which is zero exactly when \(\dot{\mat Q} = \vek 0\), since \(\mat Q\) is invertible. In the plane the angular velocity is \(\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). So the angular velocity of the body \(k\) is nonzero.
  4. Let the angular velocity of every body be zero and the velocity of some coordinate of the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the body \(k\) nonzero. By (8.68) of lemma 8.78 with the point \(O_{\mathrm{k}}\) the velocity of the centre of mass is \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}} + \vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} \times (\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}) = \vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}\), because \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0\). The velocity \(\vek v_{\mathrm{O}_\mathrm{k}}\) has a nonzero component, so \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) is nonzero too.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

For the kinetic energy we may therefore say: if \(\dot{\vek q} \neq \vek 0\), the centre of mass of some body moves, some body rotates, or some mass point moves.

Theorem 8.86 Kinetic energy in generalized coordinates (kinetična energija v posplošenih koordinatah)

Let \(T\) be the kinetic energy of a system of rigid bodies with interior points, masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) and inertia tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\), and let it be given with generalized coordinates \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) by definition 8.45. Then

\[T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q, t)\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek b(\vek q, t)\cdot\dot{\vek q} + T_0(\vek q, t) , \tag{8.77}\]

where \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\) and \(T_0\) are continuous functions, \(\mat M\) is a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix, and it is positive definite. If the constraints do not depend on time (scleronomic), \(\vek b = \vek 0\), \(T_0 = 0\) and \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\). The matrix \(\mat M\) is called the mass matrix (masna matrika) of the system.

Proof

We must show that \(T\) is a polynomial of the second degree in \(\dot{\vek q}\), and that the quadratic part is positive. We write the velocities in theorem 8.82 with \(\dot{\vek q}\).

  1. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1), \(\dot{\vek c}_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \dfrac{\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} + \dfrac{\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial t}\) and likewise for \(\dot{\mat Q}_{\mathrm{k}}\). Hence the velocity of the centre of mass is, by lemma 8.78, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and, by definition 8.77, the angular velocity is \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) are \(3 \times n\) matrices, \(\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\) vectors, all depending only on \(\vek q\) and \(t\). For scleronomic constraints \(\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0\).
  2. By theorem 8.82, \(T = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(\tfrac12\,m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 + \tfrac12\,(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}})\big)\). The tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is, by lemma 8.81 (point 3), a function of \(\vek q\) and \(t\).
  3. We expand the squares. Because \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric, \((\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\). We get (8.77) with
    \[\begin{aligned} \mat M &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \\ \vek b &= \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\big) , \\ T_0 &= \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\vek\beta_{\mathrm{k}}|^2 + \vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek\gamma_{\mathrm{k}}\big) . \end{aligned}\]
  4. The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric, because each \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) is symmetric (lemma 1.101).
  5. By step 3, \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}|^2 + (\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\cdot\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q})\big) \ge 0\), because \(m_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) and \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is positive definite by lemma 8.81. Let \(\dot{\vek q} \neq \vek 0\). The columns \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\dot{\vek q}\) are the velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity of body \(k\), computed from the velocities \((\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q}\) of the numbers of the configuration without the part \(\partial\vek X/\partial t\). By lemma 8.85 with \(\vek c = \dot{\vek q}\), for at least one body or mass point one of them is nonzero, and the term of this body is positive. Hence \(\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q} > 0\) and \(\mat M\) is positive definite.

Source: GR (1.30)-(1.33) (PDF p. 45): \(T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0\) with terms of degree \(2\), \(1\) and \(0\) in \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The positivity of \(\mat M\) and the proof by steps are not from the source.

GR calls the terms \(T_2\), \(T_1\) and \(T_0\) the relative, mutual and transport kinetic energy. Positive definiteness matters because in section 8.9 we shall solve the equations of motion for \(\ddot{\vek q}\), and for that we need an invertible matrix.

Let us test the theorem on three examples.

Example A, pendulum. A point mass \(m\) on a rigid bar of length \(l\), at the angle \(\theta\) from the vertical. The position is \(l\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta)\), the velocity \(l\,\dot\theta\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) with length \(l\,|\dot\theta|\), so \(T = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2\) and \(M = m\,l^2\) is a \(1 \times 1\) mass matrix. For \(m = 1\) kg and \(l = 1\) m, \(M = 1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). If the support moves horizontally by a prescribed law \(\xi(t)\), the position is \((\xi + l\sin\theta,\ -l\cos\theta)\) and the velocity \((\dot\xi + l\,\dot\theta\cos\theta,\ l\,\dot\theta\sin\theta)\). After squaring,

\[T = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2 + m\,l\,\dot\xi\cos\theta\,\dot\theta + \tfrac12\,m\,\dot\xi^2 .\]

Here \(M = m\,l^2\), \(b = m\,l\,\dot\xi\cos\theta\) and \(T_0 = \tfrac12\,m\,\dot\xi^2\). The terms \(b\) and \(T_0\) are nonzero because the constraint (the support) now contains time (rheonomic constraint). We checked the computation with SymPy.

Example C, rigid bar on two springs. A uniform thin bar (as above) of length \(L = 2\) m and mass \(m = 30\) kg moves along a guide only vertically and rotates about the axis \(z\). The coordinates are the vertical displacement \(y\) of the centre of mass and the rotation \(\varphi\). The velocity of the centre of mass is \(\dot y\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), the moment about the middle is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m\,L^2/12 = 10\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) by (8.72). By theorem 8.82, \(T = \tfrac12\cdot 30\,\dot y^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 10\,\dot\varphi^2\) and

\[\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The coordinates do not mix, because \(y\) is the coordinate of the centre of mass (so there is no mixed term in theorem 8.82) and because the constraints are scleronomic (\(\vek b = \vek 0\)). If instead of \((y, \varphi)\) we choose the displacements of the ends \(y_1 = y - \sin\varphi\) and \(y_2 = y + \sin\varphi\) (the ends are \(L/2 = 1\) m from the centre), then \(\dot y = (\dot y_1 + \dot y_2)/2\) and \(\dot\varphi = (\dot y_2 - \dot y_1)/(2\cos\varphi)\). The term with \(\dot\varphi^2\) therefore has the factor \(1/\cos^2\varphi\), which at \(\varphi = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad equals \(1 + 1{,}5\cdot 10^{-6}\). For such a small rotation, where \(\cos\varphi\) equals \(1\) to six places,

\[T = \tfrac12\cdot 30\cdot\tfrac14\,(\dot y_1 + \dot y_2)^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 10\cdot\tfrac14\,(\dot y_2 - \dot y_1)^2 = 5\,\dot y_1^2 + 5\,\dot y_1\,\dot y_2 + 5\,\dot y_2^2 , \qquad \mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 5 & 10 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The mass matrix is no longer diagonal. The same bar with the same motions has a different \(\mat M\) in the new coordinates, because the coefficients of \(\dot q_{\mathrm{r}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are different. We shall need this in vibrations, where the coupling moves between the mass and the stiffness matrix.

Example D, toggle mechanism. Term by term. The bars have masses \(m_{\mathrm{b}} = 2\) kg, the slider \(m_{\mathrm{C}} = 1\) kg, \(a = 0{,}5\) m.

  1. Bar \(AB\) rotates about \(A\) with \(\dot\theta\). By Steiner, \(J_{\mathrm{A}} = \tfrac{m_{\mathrm{b}}\,a^2}{3} = \tfrac16\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) and \(T_{\mathrm{AB}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac16\,\dot\theta^2\).
  2. Bar \(BC\) has \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}} = a\dot\theta\,(-\tfrac32\sin\theta,\ \tfrac12\cos\theta)\), so \(|\vek v_{\mathrm{G}}|^2 = a^2\,\dot\theta^2\,(\tfrac94\sin^2\theta + \tfrac14\cos^2\theta) = a^2\,\dot\theta^2\,(\tfrac14 + 2\sin^2\theta)\). The angular velocity is \(-\dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = \tfrac{m_{\mathrm{b}}\,a^2}{12} = \tfrac1{24}\). Hence \(T_{\mathrm{BC}} = \tfrac12\,\big(2\cdot\tfrac14\,(\tfrac14 + 2\sin^2\theta) + \tfrac1{24}\big)\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\,\big(\tfrac16 + \sin^2\theta\big)\,\dot\theta^2\).
  3. The slider moves with \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = (-2a\dot\theta\sin\theta,\ 0)\), so \(T_{\mathrm{C}} = \tfrac12\cdot 1\cdot 4a^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12\,\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2\).

The sum of the three terms is \(T = \tfrac12\,m(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2\) with

\[m(\theta) = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 + \sin^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = \tfrac13 + 2\sin^2\theta\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2} . \tag{8.78}\]

At \(\theta = 0\), \(m = 0{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) (the slider is at rest, because \(\vek v_{\mathrm{C}} = \vek 0\)) and at \(\theta = 45^\circ\), \(m = 1{,}3333\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), which agrees with the sum from the example above: \(\tfrac16 + 0{,}6667 + 0{,}5 = 1{,}3333\) for bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) and the slider. The mass matrix of the mechanism is therefore not constant: it depends on the position \(\theta\), because with the position the ratios of the velocities of the points change. In section 8.9 this will show up as the term with \(\dot\theta^2\) in the equation of motion.
