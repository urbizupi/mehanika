Chapter 22 Shells

22.6 Bending of the cylinder and the edge effect

A steel tube (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) with mid-surface radius \(R = 50\) mm and thickness \(h = 2\) mm is closed and filled with oil at pressure \(p = 3\) MPa. The membrane theory of section 22.4 gives the hoop stress \(pR/h = 75{,}00\) MPa and the axial stress \(pR/(2h) = 37{,}50\) MPa (lemma 22.36 on the pressure vessel), and Hooke's law gives that the wall widens by \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = pR^2(1 - \nu/2)/(Eh) = 16{,}263\ \mu\)m. What happens where the tube ends in a thick flat plate welded to it? The plate is very stiff, so the edge of the wall next to it stays in place and does not rotate: there \(w = 0\) instead of \(16{,}263\ \mu\)m. We call such a support of a beam a clamp (vpetje, definition 5.5). This too is an idealization. A real plate bends a little under the forces of the tube, and we make it infinitely stiff. How a real plate acts is the subject of chapter 18.

Why can the wall not stay a plain cylinder next to the clamp? At the plate the ring of the wall does not widen, the ring \(10\) mm away widens to \(0{,}66\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\) (the computation is in lemma 22.45), and far away to \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\). The hoop force is proportional to the widening, so neighbouring rings press on the wall with different forces. By Newton's law of equilibrium of a piece of wall, this difference of radial forces must be balanced by a shear force in the wall, which the membrane does not have. A shear force cannot act without a moment, since the derivative of the moment equals minus the shear force, as we saw for the beam (corollary 5.11): where there is a shear force, the moment changes along the wall. So bending enters the wall. Let us show this exactly on a band of the wall, as we computed on a piece for the beam.

22.6.1 Equilibrium of the band

We start with the outline of the tube, seen along the axis, which is the circle of the middle surface. We cut the tube with two planes through the axis that enclose the angle \(\alpha\), and obtain a separate band of the wall. From inside the oil presses on it and pushes it outward, every arrow ends on the wall. The rest of the wall beyond each section pulls the band with hoop forces \(N_2\). The hoop force is tensile, so it pulls away from the band and its arrow starts at the point of the section, along the tangent to the circle. Each tangent encloses the angle \(\alpha/2\) with the tangent at the middle of the band, so each force has the radial component \(N_2\sin(\alpha/2)\), and both together the radial component \(2N_2\sin(\alpha/2)\) per unit length of \(x\), directed toward the axis of the tube.

Now we look at the same band from the side, between the planes \(x = a\) and \(x = b\). We measure the distance along the axis \(x\), and the radial deflection \(w\) outward. The band is like a beam loaded by a radial load, so we choose signs as in chapter 5. The pressure acts from below. The radial part of the hoop forces is seen from the side as the reaction \(N_2/R\) per unit area, which pushes the band down. It is not an external force but the radial part of the hoop forces from the left view. On the face \(x = b\) there is the shear force \(Q(b)\), the force of the part of the tube on the side of larger \(x\) on the part on the side of smaller \(x\) (definition 5.7, there \(V\)). On the face \(x = a\) it is \(-Q(a)\) by Newton's third law. In the same way on the faces there are the moments \(M(b)\) and \(-M(a)\) (definition 5.8) and the axial forces \(N_1\), per unit length. So the units of \(Q\) and \(N_1\) are N/mm and of \(M\) N mm/mm, all per length of the middle ring.

We write the radial equilibrium of the band, and on a band with a finite angle \(\alpha\), not on a piece that we would shrink. A point on the face \(x = b\) lies on the arc inclined by the angle \(\theta \in [-\alpha/2, \alpha/2]\) toward the middle of the band. The shear force there is radial, so its component in the radial direction of the middle of the band is \(Q(b)\cos\theta\). The sum along the arc is \(\int_{-\alpha/2}^{\alpha/2} Q(b)\cos\theta\,R\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2R\sin(\alpha/2)\,Q(b)\). We denote \(\Lambda = 2R\sin(\alpha/2)\). The same factor \(\Lambda\) appears at \(-Q(a)\) and at the pressure \(p\), since all three are radial. For the hoop forces the factor is \(2\sin(\alpha/2) = \Lambda/R\). So \(\alpha\) cancels in the equilibrium.

Theorem 22.42 Equation of the cylinder under axisymmetric load (enačba valja pri osnosimetrični obtežbi)

Let a cylinder of radius \(R\) and thickness \(h\), both constant, be made of an isotropic, linearly elastic material with \(E\) and \(\nu\) (theorem 3.48). Let the Kirchhoff and Love assumptions hold (definition 22.24), let the pressure \(p(x)\) be a continuous function of the axial coordinate \(x\) only and let there be no axial surface load. Let the deformation be axisymmetric, the slope small, \(|w'| \le 10^{-2}\), and let the equilibrium be written on the undeformed band. Then:

  1. The axial force \(N_1\) is constant on the whole cylinder.
  2. The radial displacement \(w(x)\) of the middle surface, positive outward, solves the equation
    \[D\,w'''' + \frac{Eh}{R^2}\,w = p - \frac{\nu N_1}{R} , \qquad D = \frac{Eh^3}{12(1 - \nu^2)} . \tag{22.41}\]
  3. The moment, the shear force and the hoop force are \(M = D\,w''\), \(Q = -D\,w'''\) and \(N_2 = Eh\,w/R + \nu N_1\).

With \(\beta\) defined by \(4\beta^4 = Eh/(R^2 D)\), that is

\[\beta = \frac{\sqrt[4]{3(1 - \nu^2)}}{\sqrt{R h}} , \tag{22.42}\]

the equation (22.41) is \(w'''' + 4\beta^4 w = (p - \nu N_1/R)/D\).

Proof

We must show four things. The equilibrium of the band gives the relation between \(Q\), \(M\), \(N_2\) and \(p\), the axial equilibrium gives the constancy of \(N_1\), kinematics and Hooke's law express \(N_2\) and \(M\) by \(w\), then we substitute.

  1. Radial equilibrium of the band between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\) with the factors computed above: \(\Lambda\,Q(b) - \Lambda\,Q(a) + \Lambda\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b p\,\mathrm{d}x - \dfrac{\Lambda}{R}\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b N_2\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). After division by \(\Lambda > 0\) we get \(Q(b) - Q(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\big(N_2/R - p\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\). The radial component of the axial force \(N_1\), the product \(N_1\,w'\), we neglected, and we estimate its size in the section after theorem 22.43.
  2. The function \(N_2/R - p\) is continuous, because \(p\) is continuous by assumption and \(N_2\) by step 6. So by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40, item 2) the function \(Q\) is differentiable and \(Q' = N_2/R - p\).
  3. The band is a beam with radial load \(q = p - N_2/R\) per unit width, since the radial part of the hoop forces is just one more radial load. By corollary 5.11 (equation (5.13), \(m = 0\)) we have \(M' = -Q\) and therefore \(M'' = -Q' = p - N_2/R\).
  4. By the same corollary \(N_1' = -q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), because the axial load is zero, so \(N_1\) is constant. This is item 1.
  5. By theorem 22.26 on the deformations of the shell (equations (22.21)), for a cylinder the meridian is a straight line, \(1/R_1 = 0\), the normal is perpendicular to the axis, \(\varphi = \pi/2\), and \(\rho = R\). For an axisymmetric displacement \((u, w)\) of the middle surface the axial strain is therefore \(\varepsilon_1^0 = u'\) and the hoop strain \(\varepsilon_2^0 = w/R\) (corollary 11.21 gives the same with \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = w\) and \(r = R\)). The changes of curvature are \(\kappa_1 = (u/R_1 - w')' = -w''\) and \(\kappa_2 = (u/R_1 - w')\cos\varphi/\rho = 0\), since \(\cos\varphi = 0\).
  6. By theorem 22.28 we have \(N_1 = C(\varepsilon_1^0 + \nu\varepsilon_2^0)\) and \(N_2 = C(\varepsilon_2^0 + \nu\varepsilon_1^0)\) with \(C = Eh/(1 - \nu^2)\). From the first, \(\varepsilon_1^0 = N_1/C - \nu w/R\). We substitute in the second: \(N_2 = C(1 - \nu^2)\,w/R + \nu N_1 = Eh\,w/R + \nu N_1\). The same theorem gives \(M_1 = D(\kappa_1 + \nu\kappa_2) = -D\,w''\). This moment, \(M_1 = \int\sigma_{11}z\,\mathrm{d}z\) (equation (22.23)), is positive when the outer face is in tension. The bending moment of the beam \(M\) (definition 5.8), however, is positive when the lower face, here the inner one, is in tension, since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M\,y/I\) (theorem 5.35), here with \(y = z\) outward. So \(M = -M_1 = D\,w''\), and with \(Q = -M'\) from step 3, \(Q = -D\,w'''\). This is item 3.
  7. We substitute \(M = D\,w''\) and \(N_2\) from step 6 into \(M'' = p - N_2/R\) from step 3. Since \(D\) is constant, \(D\,w'''' = p - Eh\,w/R^2 - \nu N_1/R\). This is the equation (22.41). From \(4\beta^4 = Eh/(R^2 D) = 12(1 - \nu^2)/(R^2h^2)\) we get \(\beta^4 = 3(1 - \nu^2)/(Rh)^2\), which is the equation (22.42).

Source: VK (15.35)-(15.45) (PDF pp. 426-427). VK measures the deflection \(w\) and the pressure \(p_3\) inward, \(\varepsilon_2 = -w/R\) (15.38), and has the shear force with the opposite sign, \(Q_1 = \mathrm{d}M_1/\mathrm{d}x\). Its equation (15.43) becomes ours with \(w \to -w\) and \(p_3 \to -p\). The proof from a band with angle \(\alpha\) and from corollary 5.11 is not literally from the source, VK takes the equilibrium equations from the general theory (12.41), (12.42).

Notation. The quantity \(\beta\) has the unit \(\mathrm{mm}^{-1}\), so \(1/\beta\) is a length. We call it the decay constant (konstanta upadanja), because theorem 22.43 will show that the disturbance at the edge decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\). The symbol \(D\) is the bending stiffness of the shell per unit width from theorem 22.28, as \(EI\) is the bending stiffness of the beam. It is not the amplification factor of chapter 9 or the compliance matrix of chapter 4. The symbol \(N\) here means a force per unit length, for the beam a force through the whole cross-section, and the same holds for \(M\). The symbol \(h\) is the thickness of the shell, not the size of an element (\(h_{\mathrm{e}}\) in section 22.8).

The equation (22.41) has a form that we already know. The beam has \(EI\,v'''' = q\) (theorem 5.42), the band has \(D\,w'''' = p - k\,w - \nu N_1/R\) with \(k = Eh/R^2\). So the band acts like a beam on an elastic foundation, called the Winkler foundation (Winklerjeva podlaga). Winkler proposed the simplest model of a foundation, in which the reaction at every point is proportional to the deflection, \(q = k\,w\) (VK (3.65), PDF p. 81), hence the name. The foundation here is not a new material: it is the hoop forces of the wall itself, which act like springs in the radial direction, since the wall cannot widen without the ring holding it back. For our tube \(k = 156{,}80\ \mathrm{N/mm^3}\), \(D = 143\,590\) N mm and \(\beta = 0{,}12854\ \mathrm{mm^{-1}}\), so \(1/\beta = 7{,}780\) mm. Chapter 25 uses the same equation for a beam on a real foundation. Here we solve it completely, because we need it for the edge effect.

We test the theorem on two extreme cases. If \(R \to \infty\), the band would become a straight beam, \(k \to 0\), and the equation would be the beam equation. A band of length \(100\) mm, clamped at one end, would deflect under the pressure \(3\) MPa by \(pL^4/(8D) = 261{,}2\) mm, which is absurd. For the tube \(\beta L = 12{,}85\) and the deflection is \(16{,}263\ \mu\)m. The curvature of the tube, that is the hoop forces, therefore changes the equation decisively. The second case is the free edge of an open tube without axial force: \(N_1 = 0\), \(M = Q = 0\) and \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}} = pR^2/(Eh) = 19{,}133\ \mu\)m solves the equation and all boundary conditions, so there is no bending in the wall. Bending does not come from the pressure but from the edge not being allowed to do what the wall would do by itself.

22.6.2 Solution and edge effect

The equation (22.41) is an ordinary linear equation of the fourth order with constant coefficients. Its solution is the sum of one particular solution and the general solution of the homogeneous equation. The particular solution for constant \(p\) is the constant function \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = (p - \nu N_1/R)\,R^2/(Eh)\), which solves \(Eh\,w_{\mathrm{p}}/R^2 = p - \nu N_1/R\). This is the membrane solution: by lemma 22.36 \(N_1 = pR/2\), for our tube \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = 16{,}263\ \mu\)m and \(N_2 = Eh\,w_{\mathrm{p}}/R + \nu N_1 = pR = 150\) N/mm, which is the membrane hoop force. The homogeneous equation \(w'''' + 4\beta^4 w = 0\) remains.

What solutions does it have? We try \(\mathrm{e}^{sx}\). Substitution gives \(s^4 = -4\beta^4\). Since \((1 + \mathrm{i})^4 = (2\mathrm{i})^2 = -4\), the four solutions are \(s = \beta(\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})\). So we have the real solutions \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta x}\cos\beta x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta x}\sin\beta x\). The factors \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) decay, \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\) grow. Which belong in a long tube that starts at \(x = 0\)?

Theorem 22.43 Edge effect (robni učinek)

Let \(w\) be a solution of the equation (22.41) on \([0, \infty)\) with constant \(p\) and \(N_1\), \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = (p - \nu N_1/R)R^2/(Eh)\) and \(\beta\) from the equation (22.42). Then:

  1. The difference \(\omega = w - w_{\mathrm{p}}\) is a linear combination of the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\), \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\), \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\cos\beta x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\sin\beta x\), and every such combination solves \(\omega'''' + 4\beta^4\omega = 0\).
  2. If \(w\) is bounded on \([0, \infty)\), the coefficients at \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\) are zero and
    \[w(x) = w_{\mathrm{p}} + \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\big(C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x\big) \tag{22.43}\]
    with constants \(C_1\), \(C_2\) determined by the edge.
  3. For \(c = \sqrt{C_1^2 + C_2^2}\) and \(\omega = w - w_{\mathrm{p}}\) the following holds for \(x \ge 0\): \(|\omega| \le c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\), \(|\omega''| \le 2\beta^2 c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) and \(|\omega'''| \le 2\sqrt2\,\beta^3 c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\). The moment and the shear force, \(M = D\,\omega''\) and \(Q = -D\,\omega'''\), therefore decay as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\).
Proof

We must show three items. The first comes from the roots of the characteristic polynomial and from the uniqueness theorem, the third from writing the solution as the real part of a complex function, the second from the growth of \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\).

  1. The function \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) is constant, so \(w_{\mathrm{p}}'''' = 0\) and \(D\,w_{\mathrm{p}}'''' + (Eh/R^2)w_{\mathrm{p}} = p - \nu N_1/R\) by the choice of \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\). The difference \(\omega\) therefore solves \(D\,\omega'''' + (Eh/R^2)\,\omega = 0\), that is \(\omega'''' + 4\beta^4\omega = 0\).
  2. The characteristic polynomial is \(P(s) = D(s^4 + 4\beta^4)\) with roots \(s = \beta(\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})\), because \((\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})^4 = -4\). By theorem 9.29, item 3, and lemma 9.37 every function \(\mathrm{e}^{sx}\) for these four \(s\) is a solution. Their real and imaginary parts are solutions by linearity and they are exactly the four named functions.
  3. The vectors \((f, f', f'', f''')\) at \(x = 0\) for the four functions are \((1, -\beta, 0, 2\beta^3)\), \((0, \beta, -2\beta^2, 2\beta^3)\), \((1, \beta, 0, -2\beta^3)\), \((0, \beta, 2\beta^2, 2\beta^3)\). The determinant of the matrix made of them is \(32\beta^6 \ne 0\) (SymPy, num-06.out B). So from them we can build every initial vector \((\omega, \omega', \omega'', \omega''')(0)\), and by the uniqueness theorem (theorem 9.36) every solution on \([0, T]\) is such a combination, for every \(T\). This is item 1.
  4. Let \(\omega_- = \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x)\). With \(t = \beta x\) we have \(C_1\cos t + C_2\sin t = \mathrm{Re}\big[(C_1 - \mathrm{i}C_2)(\cos t + \mathrm{i}\sin t)\big]\), so \(\omega_- = \mathrm{Re}\big[(C_1 - \mathrm{i}C_2)\,\mathrm{e}^{(-1 + \mathrm{i})\beta x}\big]\). By theorem 9.29, item 3, its \(j\)-th derivative is \(\mathrm{Re}\big[(C_1 - \mathrm{i}C_2)\,\beta^j(-1 + \mathrm{i})^j\,\mathrm{e}^{(-1 + \mathrm{i})\beta x}\big]\).
  5. The absolute value of the real part is not larger than the absolute value of the complex number, and that is the product of the absolute values. Since \(|\mathrm{e}^{(-1 + \mathrm{i})\beta x}| = \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) (theorem 9.29, item 2), \(|C_1 - \mathrm{i}C_2| = c\) and \(|-1 + \mathrm{i}| = \sqrt2\), we have \(|\omega_-^{(j)}| \le c\,(\sqrt2\beta)^j\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\). With \(j = 0, 2, 3\) these are the estimates of item 3 when \(\omega = \omega_-\).
  6. Let \(\omega = \omega_- + \omega_+\) with \(\omega_+ = \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}(C_3\cos\beta x + C_4\sin\beta x)\) and \(c' = \sqrt{C_3^2 + C_4^2} > 0\). Then \(\omega_+ = c'\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\cos(\beta x - \varphi)\) for some angle \(\varphi\), and at the points \(x_{\mathrm{k}} = (\varphi + 2k\pi)/\beta\) with natural \(k\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}} \ge 0\) we have \(\omega_+(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = c'\mathrm{e}^{\beta x_{\mathrm{k}}} \ge c'(1 + \beta x_{\mathrm{k}})\) (lemma 9.15, item 1), which is unbounded as \(k \to \infty\). Since \(|\omega_-| \le c\) by step 5, \(\omega\) would also be unbounded. A bounded \(w\) therefore requires \(C_3 = C_4 = 0\). This is item 2 and with it item 3.

Source: VK (15.47)-(15.49) and (15.53) (PDF pp. 428, 430). VK finds the solution with an ansatz and characteristic roots and sets \(A_3 = A_4 = 0\), because the deflection and its derivatives must be finite. The estimates of item 3 and the proof of boundedness are ours.

Figure 22.22 shows what the theorem says. In both parts of the figure the distance \(x\) from the edge runs to the right, the deflection \(w\) is at the top, the moment \(M\) at the bottom. On the left side, at I, is the clamped edge, where the wall is forced to stay at \(w = 0\). The dashed line is \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\), where the wall would widen by itself. The deflection grows from zero at the edge toward \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\), exceeds it by 4 % and then approaches it. Why does it exceed it? The deflection at the clamped edge is \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\big)\), which we derive in lemma 22.45. The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) decays, while the cosine and sine oscillate. The derivative of the deflection is \(2\beta w_{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\) and is zero for the first time at \(\beta x = \pi\), where \(\cos\pi + \sin\pi = -1\), so \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}}(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-\pi})\) with \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432\). Below is the moment. It is largest at the edge and then oscillates a few times and decays. The right part of the figure, II, shows the simply supported edge, like the simple support of a beam in definition 5.5: the edge does not move outward, but it may rotate. Its deflection exceeds the membrane value even more and the moment at the edge is zero. Where the disturbance dies out is determined by the following corollary.

Corollary 22.44 Disturbance length (dolžina motnje)

Under the conditions of theorem 22.43 the edge disturbance \(\omega\) decays at the distance \(\ell = \pi/\beta\) from the edge to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432\) of its bound at the edge or less, and

\[\ell = \frac{\pi}{\beta} = \frac{\pi\sqrt{R h}}{\sqrt[4]{3(1 - \nu^2)}} = 2{,}444\,\sqrt{R h}\quad(\nu = 0{,}3) . \tag{22.44}\]

For a tube with \(R = 50\) mm and \(h = 2\) mm, \(\ell = 24{,}44\) mm, that is \(0{,}49\,R\) or \(12{,}2\,h\).

Proof
  1. By item 3 of theorem 22.43 \(|\omega(x)| \le c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\), at \(x = \ell\) we have \(\beta x = \pi\), so \(|\omega(\ell)| \le \mathrm{e}^{-\pi}c\).
  2. From the equation (22.42), \(1/\beta = \sqrt{R h}/\sqrt[4]{3(1 - \nu^2)}\). For \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\sqrt[4]{3\cdot 0{,}91} = 1{,}2854\) and \(\pi/1{,}2854 = 2{,}444\) (SymPy, num-06.out A). For \(R = 50\) and \(h = 2\), \(\sqrt{Rh} = 10\) mm, so \(\ell = 24{,}44\) mm.

Source: VK (15.55) (PDF p. 431) and example 15.1 (PDF p. 433) give \(3/\beta \approx 2{,}3\sqrt{Rh}\). The length \(\pi/\beta\) is our choice, because it is the first point where the deflection at a clamped edge reaches its maximum.

The two bars of length \(\pi/\beta\) in figure 22.22 thus mark where the disturbance has almost died out. The whole region of bending is therefore a band of the wall only twelve thicknesses or half a radius long. The name edge effect comes from here: the bending is confined to the neighbourhood of the edge or of another place where membrane theory does not hold, and decays quickly in the direction perpendicular to it (VK §17.5, PDF p. 512). The edge may be a physical one or a contact with another shell (section 22.7).

What do the assumptions of theorem 22.42 cost? The pressure acts on the inner face with radius \(R - h/2\), and we stated it on the middle one. Per unit area of the middle surface it therefore gives the force \(p\,(1 - h/(2R))\), the difference is \(h/(2R) = 0{,}02\), that is 2 % for our tube. We treated the band on the undeformed shape. The radial component \(N_1\,w'\) of the axial force on the faces of the band (step 1 of the proof) would give the radial load \(N_1\,w'' = N_1\,\omega''\), since \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\) is constant. We compare it with \(D\,\omega''''\). By item 3 of theorem 22.43 \(N_1|\omega''| \le 2\beta^2N_1c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) and because of \(\omega'''' = -4\beta^4\omega\) we have \(D|\omega''''| \le 4\beta^4Dc\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\). The ratio of the estimates is \(N_1/(2D\beta^2) = 0{,}016\) at \(N_1 = 75\) N/mm and \(D\beta^2 = 2372\) N/mm (num-06.out A), that is 1,6 %. For a tensile axial force the sign is such that the tube is slightly stiffened. For a compressive axial force the sign is opposite and the tube is slightly softened. For a large enough compressive force the ratio is no longer small and the tube can buckle, which the equation without this term does not capture. The Kirchhoff-Love assumption means that we neglect the transverse shear deformation, as for the beam (definition 5.14). For the beam this error is small when the depth is small compared with the length over which the bending changes. Here that length is \(1/\beta = 7{,}78\) mm against the thickness \(h = 2\) mm, the ratio is \(h\beta = 0{,}26\). The first two errors are thus at most 2 % for our tube, the third we do not estimate with a number, since that would need a shell theory with shear deformation.

22.6.3 Clamped and simply supported edge

Now we can finish the tube we started with. The pressure and the axial force are known and the edge is clamped, so we determine \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) from the boundary conditions.

Lemma 22.45 Clamped edge of a long pressurised tube (vpeti rob dolge cevi pod tlakom)

Let the tube of theorem 22.43 be closed, \(N_1 = pR/2\), with a clamped edge \(w(0) = 0\), \(w'(0) = 0\) and bounded deflection on \([0, \infty)\). Then

\[w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\Big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\Big) , \qquad w_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{pR^2}{Eh}\Big(1 - \frac{\nu}{2}\Big) , \tag{22.45}\]
\[M(0) = \frac{p}{2\beta^2}\Big(1 - \frac{\nu}{2}\Big) , \qquad Q(0) = \frac{p}{\beta}\Big(1 - \frac{\nu}{2}\Big) . \tag{22.46}\]

The axial stress on the faces at the edge is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N_1/h \pm 6M(0)/h^2 = \dfrac{pR}{2h}\Big(1 \pm \dfrac{2\sqrt3\,(1 - \nu/2)}{\sqrt{1 - \nu^2}}\Big)\), with the plus sign on the inner face. The hoop stress at the edge on the middle surface is \(\nu N_1/h\).

Proof

First we determine \(C_1\), \(C_2\) from the boundary conditions, then \(M\) and \(Q\) with theorem 22.42.

  1. By lemma 22.36 \(N_1 = pR/2\), so \(w_{\mathrm{p}} = (p - \nu p/2)R^2/(Eh) = pR^2(1 - \nu/2)/(Eh)\).
  2. By theorem 22.43 \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}} + \omega\) with \(\omega = \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x)\). Its derivatives at \(0\) are \(\omega(0) = C_1\), \(\omega'(0) = \beta(C_2 - C_1)\), \(\omega''(0) = -2\beta^2C_2\) and \(\omega'''(0) = 2\beta^3(C_1 + C_2)\) (SymPy, num-06.out B).
  3. From \(w(0) = 0\) we get \(C_1 = -w_{\mathrm{p}}\) and from \(w'(0) = 0\) we get \(C_2 = C_1 = -w_{\mathrm{p}}\). This is the equation (22.45).
  4. By item 3 of theorem 22.42 \(M(0) = D\,w''(0) = -2D\beta^2C_2 = 2D\beta^2w_{\mathrm{p}}\). From \(4D\beta^4 = Eh/R^2\) we get \(2D\beta^2 = Eh/(2R^2\beta^2)\), so \(M(0) = Eh\,w_{\mathrm{p}}/(2R^2\beta^2) = p(1 - \nu/2)/(2\beta^2)\).
  5. Similarly \(Q(0) = -D\,w'''(0) = -2D\beta^3(C_1 + C_2) = 4D\beta^3w_{\mathrm{p}} = p(1 - \nu/2)/\beta\).
  6. By theorem 5.35 the stress due to the moment is \(-M\,y/I\) with \(I = h^3/12\) per unit width, on the face \(y = \mp h/2\) so \(\pm 6M/h^2\), and the axial force contributes \(N_1/h\). From the equation (22.42) \(\beta^2 = \sqrt{3(1 - \nu^2)}/(Rh)\), so \(6M(0)/h^2 = 3p(1 - \nu/2)/(h^2\beta^2) = \sqrt3\,(1 - \nu/2)\,pR/(h\sqrt{1 - \nu^2})\), which is in the theorem.
  7. At the edge \(w = 0\), so \(N_2 = \nu N_1\) by item 3 of theorem 22.42 and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \nu N_1/h\) on the middle surface.

Source: VK example 15.2 (PDF pp. 433-434) for \(R = 1{,}2\) m, \(h = 0{,}01\) m has \(M_0 = p(1 - \nu/2)/(2\beta^2)\) and \(Q_0 = -p(1 - \nu/2)/\beta\). The sign of \(Q_0\) is opposite to ours, since VK has \(Q_1 = \mathrm{d}M_1/\mathrm{d}x\) and we have \(Q = -M'\).

The numbers for our tube (num-06.out C): \(M(0) = 77{,}167\) N mm/mm, \(Q(0) = 19{,}838\) N/mm and the bending stress \(6M(0)/h^2 = 115{,}75\) MPa. The axial stress is therefore \(37{,}50 + 115{,}75 = 153{,}25\) MPa on the inner face and \(37{,}50 - 115{,}75 = -78{,}25\) MPa on the outer one. This is \(4{,}09\) times the membrane axial stress and \(2{,}04\) times the membrane hoop stress \(75\) MPa. The ratio of the bending stress to the membrane hoop stress, \(\sqrt3\,(1 - \nu/2)/\sqrt{1 - \nu^2} = 1{,}543\), does not depend on \(R\) and \(h\): a thin tube at a clamp is not safe merely because the membrane stress is small. The hoop stress at the edge is, because of \(w = 0\), only \(\nu N_1/h = 11{,}25\) MPa instead of \(75\) MPa. For a tube with \(R = 1{,}2\) m and \(h = 0{,}01\) m our formula gives \(M(0) = 3{,}087\cdot 10^{-3}\,p\) N m/m, which agrees with VK, who writes \(3{,}08\cdot 10^{-3}\,p\) (PDF p. 434) and cuts off the last digit. Besides the axial moment the wall has by Hooke's law also the hoop moment \(M_2 = D(\kappa_2 + \nu\kappa_1) = \nu M_1\) (theorem 22.28, \(\kappa_2 = 0\) by step 5 of the proof of theorem 22.42). The hoop bending stress is therefore \(\nu\) times the axial bending stress, with the same sign on each face, and at the edge it gives \(\pm 6\nu M(0)/h^2 = \pm 34{,}7\) MPa on the faces, tension on the inner face.

What about a support that does not clamp? For a tube that merely must not move outward but may rotate we have the following lemma.

Lemma 22.46 Simply supported edge of a long pressurised tube (členkasto podprt rob dolge cevi pod tlakom)

Let the tube be as in lemma 22.45 and let the edge have \(w(0) = 0\) and \(M(0) = 0\). Then

\[w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\big) , \qquad M(x) = -2D\beta^2w_{\mathrm{p}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x , \tag{22.47}\]

\(|M|\) reaches its maximum at \(\beta x = \pi/4\) and \(\max|M| = \mathrm{e}^{-\pi/4}\,M_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt2 = 0{,}322\,M_{\mathrm{v}}\), where \(M_{\mathrm{v}}\) is the moment at the clamped edge from lemma 22.45. The deflection is largest at \(\beta x = 3\pi/4\) and equals \(w_{\mathrm{p}}\big(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-3\pi/4}/\sqrt2\big) = 1{,}067\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\).

Proof
  1. With \(\omega\) from theorem 22.43 and the derivatives from step 2 of lemma 22.45, \(w(0) = w_{\mathrm{p}} + C_1 = 0\), so \(C_1 = -w_{\mathrm{p}}\). The condition \(M(0) = D\,\omega''(0) = -2D\beta^2C_2 = 0\) gives \(C_2 = 0\). This is the first equality in (22.47).
  2. The second derivative of \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x\) is \(2\beta^2\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\) (theorem 9.29, item 3, twice), so \(M = D\,w'' = -2D\beta^2w_{\mathrm{p}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x\).
  3. Let \(g(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin t\) for \(t = \beta x\). The derivative \(g' = \mathrm{e}^{-t}(\cos t - \sin t)\) is positive on \([0, \pi/4)\) and negative on \((\pi/4, 5\pi/4)\). So \(g\) on \([0, \pi]\) is largest at \(\pi/4\) with the value \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi/4}/\sqrt2 = 0{,}3224\). For \(t \ge \pi\), \(|g| \le \mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432 < 0{,}3224\). With \(2D\beta^2w_{\mathrm{p}} = M_{\mathrm{v}}\) (step 4 of lemma 22.45) this is the claim about the moment.
  4. Deflection: \(w' = w_{\mathrm{p}}\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}(\cos\beta x + \sin\beta x)\) and \(\cos t + \sin t = \sqrt2\sin(t + \pi/4)\). So the derivative is positive on \([0, 3\pi/4)\), zero at \(t = 3\pi/4\) and negative on \((3\pi/4, 7\pi/4)\). At \(t = 3\pi/4\), \(\cos t = -1/\sqrt2\), so \(w = w_{\mathrm{p}}\big(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-3\pi/4}/\sqrt2\big) = 1{,}067\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\). For \(t \ge 7\pi/4\), \(w - w_{\mathrm{p}} = -w_{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos t \le w_{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{e}^{-7\pi/4} = 0{,}0041\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\), which is less. So this is the largest deflection.

Source: VK (15.57) (PDF p. 431) gives the deflection and the moment for general \(M_0\) and \(Q_0\) at the edge. The special case of the simply supported edge is our derivation from theorem 22.43.

For our tube \(\max|M| = 24{,}878\) N mm/mm at \(\beta x = \pi/4\), that is \(x = 6{,}11\) mm, the bending stress is \(37{,}32\) MPa, the shear force at the edge \(Q(0) = 2D\beta^3w_{\mathrm{p}} = 9{,}919\) N/mm and the deflection has its maximum \(1{,}067\,w_{\mathrm{p}}\) at \(18{,}33\) mm (num-06.out D). A simple support therefore does not remove bending. At the edge the moment is indeed zero, as we required, but a third of the clamped moment is reached already \(6{,}11\) mm away. The bending has moved into the interior of the wall.

22.6.4 The short tube

Theorem 22.43 held for a tube that starts at \(x = 0\) and does not end. How long must a real tube be for this to be good? Take a tube clamped at both ends, of length \(L\). Now we may no longer drop \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\), because \(x\) does not reach infinity. We determine the solution with all four functions from four conditions (\(w\) and \(w'\) at \(0\) and \(L\)) and compare the moment at the edge with the value for the long tube, \(77{,}167\) N mm/mm.

At \(L = 10\) mm the two edges are hardly \(1{,}3/\beta\) apart, the disturbances from both edges overlap and the short tube is so stiff that the moment is only 27 % of the value for the long tube and \(w(L/2) = 0{,}452\ \mu\)m. The formula for the long tube would give \(w(L/2) = 4{,}291\ \mu\)m, so it does not hold there either. At \(L = 30\) mm the error of \(M(0)\) is \(+5{,}7\) %, at \(50\) mm it is \(-0{,}09\) %. The error is not equal to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta L}\) and it oscillates. From the computation for \(\beta L \in [1, 8]\) (num-06.out I) it is at most \(4{,}1\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta L}\), below \(5\) % for \(\beta L \ge 4{,}33\) and below \(1\) % for \(\beta L \ge 5{,}57\). VK (15.54), (15.55) (PDF p. 431) gives \(\beta L \ge 3\), that is \(L \ge 2{,}3\sqrt{Rh} = 23\) mm, for an error of 5 % in stresses. For the moment at the clamped edge, however, we get an error of \(2{,}8\) % at \(\beta L = 3\) and \(5{,}7\) % at \(\beta L = 3{,}86\), so for this quantity we take \(\beta L \ge 4{,}33\), that is \(L \ge 33{,}7\) mm or \(0{,}67\,R\). This is a result of a computation, not a proof. What is proved is only that the correction at the other edge comes from terms of size \(c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta L}\) (theorem 22.43, item 3).

What have we shown? Bending appears in the wall because the clamp or support demands a different deflection than the membrane gives. The equation of the cylinder (theorem 22.42) is the equation of a beam on a Winkler foundation whose foundation is the hoop forces. The solution decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) from the edge (theorem 22.43) and the disturbance almost vanishes over the length \(\pi/\beta = 2{,}44\sqrt{Rh}\) (corollary 22.44). At the clamp the stress is \(153{,}25\) MPa, at the simple support \(37{,}32\) MPa of bending. A long tube has its two edges independent, a short one does not. We use the same edge effect in the next section at the junction of a tube and a hemisphere.
