Chapter 1 Strain

1.13 Mathematics: the largest and the smallest value

In section 1.12 we said precisely what it means that a function has no jumps: it is continuous (definition 1.58). Now we use this for a question that mechanics asks all the time: where is some quantity largest? In which cross section is the bar stretched most, in which direction does a fibre through a given point of a body get longest? For the hanging bar we will read the answer off a formula. The statement we have needed since the end of section 1.7.3, however, says (figure 1.240):

\[f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b - a) \qquad \text{za neko točko } \xi \text{ med } a \text{ in } b .\]

So the change of a function \(f\) between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) equals the derivative at some point \(\xi\) between them, multiplied by the difference \(b - a\). Its proof will find the point \(\xi\) as the place where some function is largest, and we will not know that function through a formula. So we must first know whether such a place exists at all.

Let us draw the theorem. The axes are the input \(t\) and the value \(f\). The blackwhite curve is the graph of the function \(f\). Above the endpoint \(a\) and above the endpoint \(b\) there is a point of the graph, and a vertical line joins it to the axis. The grey dashed chord joins these two points and has the slope \((f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\). At the points \(\xi_1\) and \(\xi_2\) the graph is marked with a blue dot, and through each runs a blue tangent, the other one. Both are parallel to the chord, because at both points the derivative equals its slope. The theorem guarantees at least one such point, here there are two.

It does not always exist. We will show that it exists when the function is continuous on a closed interval, and with counterexamples that each of these requirements is needed. The route is this. First we name what we are looking for. Then we show that the values of a continuous function on a closed interval cannot grow beyond every bound, and that among them the function attains a largest and a smallest one. Both times we use the same move. We find a sequence of points with the property we want, and from it we choose a convergent subsequence with the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57. That the interval is closed keeps the limit of the subsequence in the interval, and continuity carries the limit of the points over to a limit of the values. At the end we do the same for functions of a point in space, for example for functions of a direction. There the interval is replaced by a set of points that has those two properties of a closed interval that the proof needs. About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.10: the school rules of arithmetic, the properties of the integers from section 1.8.1 and the completeness axiom 1.32.

1.13.1 The largest value

Let us start with a question whose answer we already know. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight. As in section 1.7.1 it is held in an idealized way: only the point \(O\) at the centre of its upper end is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly. The axis \(x\) points from \(O\) vertically down along the axis of the bar. In section 1.4 we computed the strain of the bar at the point \(x\) on its axis,

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) , \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} ,\]

for every \(x\) in the interval \([0, L]\). Where is the material stretched most, and where least (figure 1.241)?

The figure has two parts. On the left is the upright rectangle, a bar of length \(L\), and the grey dimension line next to it shows \(L\). The bar is fixed only at the point \(O\) at the centre of the top end. The arrows on the top face are a force that pulls the face up evenly, which is an idealization. The axis \(x\) goes from \(O\) down along the axis of the bar, and its lower end is at \(x = L\). On the right are the axes, the strain \(\varepsilon\) horizontally and \(x\) downward, as in the bar, so the top and bottom of the graph match the top and bottom of the bar. The grey dashed lines join them. The straight line is the graph of \(\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\). At the top it is largest, \(2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (blue dot), and at the lower end it is zero (blackwhite dot).

  1. For every \(x \in [0, L]\) we have \(x \ge 0\), so \(L - x \le L\). We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(2a\), which does not reverse it, and get \(2a\,(L - x) \le 2aL\). The right side is \(\varepsilon(0)\), so \(\varepsilon(x) \le \varepsilon(0)\) for every \(x \in [0, L]\).
  2. For every \(x \in [0, L]\) we have \(x \le L\), so \(L - x \ge 0\) and \(\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) \ge 0 = \varepsilon(L)\).

At the top the strain is \(\varepsilon(0) = 2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and no point of the axis is stretched more. At the lower end the strain is zero, and no point is stretched less. At both ends the strain is a one-sided derivative of the displacement (definition 1.9). Why exactly there? Every cross section carries the weight of the part of the bar below it. The top cross section carries the whole bar, every lower one less, and the lowest one nothing. The number \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) holds for the idealized holding. For a bar welded to the ceiling the strain near the ceiling would be different (section 1.7.1).

So the number \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is a value of the function \(\varepsilon\) that no other of its values on \([0, L]\) exceeds, and the number \(0\) a value below which none falls. Such values get a name.

Definition 1.70 Maximum and minimum of a function (največja in najmanjša vrednost funkcije)

Let \(f\) be a function with real values, defined on a set \(D\), and let \(c \in D\). The elements of the set \(D\) may be numbers or points of space. The function \(f\) attains at the point \(c\) its largest value or maximum (maksimum) on \(D\) if \(f(x) \le f(c)\) for every \(x \in D\), and its smallest value or minimum (minimum) on \(D\) if \(f(x) \ge f(c)\) for every \(x \in D\). The number \(f(c)\) is then the largest or the smallest value of the function \(f\) on \(D\). Maximum and minimum together are called extrema, and each of them an extremum (ekstrem).

Source: LEBL1 §3.3.1 (PDF p. 130), where they are called the absolute maximum and the absolute minimum.

So the largest value is a number, and the point \(c\) is the place where the function attains it. For the strain of the hanging bar the largest value is \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), attained at the point \(0\), and the smallest value \(0\) is attained at the point \(L\). The definition agrees with the maximum of a set from definition 1.30. The values of the function form the set \(\{f(x) : x \in D\}\). If \(f\) attains its largest value at the point \(c\), then \(f(c)\) is an element of this set and at the same time an upper bound of it. No upper bound is smaller than the element \(f(c)\), so \(f(c)\) is a supremum of the set of values that lies in the set, that is, its maximum. The definition requires that the point \(c\) lies in \(D\). It is not enough that the values come arbitrarily close to some number. It does not require, though, that the point \(c\) is the only one.

Nor is the largest value necessarily at an endpoint of the interval. Let us stay with the hanging bar, held in the idealized way, and take its cross section at the depth \(x = 5\) m. On it we look at the points of the diameter that lies in the plane \(z = 0\), that is, the points \((5\ \text{m}, y, 0)\) with \(-0{,}005\ \text{m} \le y \le 0{,}005\ \text{m}\), since the bar has a diameter of 10 mm (figure 1.242). By (1.18) such a point moves down by

\[g(y) = u_{\mathrm{x}}(5\ \text{m}, y, 0) = a\,(2 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 - 5^2)\ \text{m}^2 - 0{,}3\,a\,(y^2 + 0^2) = 75\ \text{m}^2 \cdot a - 0{,}3\,a\,y^2 .\]

Let us draw it. The horizontal axis is the position \(y\) on the diameter of the cross section in millimetres, and the marks \(-5\) and \(5\) are the endpoints of the diameter. The vertical axis is the displacement \(g\) downward. The zigzag on it says that it does not start at zero, since the difference of the displacements is extremely small and is therefore enlarged: the displacement at the centre is larger than the displacement at the rim by \(1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-12}\) m, shown by the grey dimension line, while the displacement itself is \(1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m. The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(g(y)\), an arch with its top in the middle. The graph is an arch, because \(g(y) = 75a - 0{,}3a\,y^2\) falls with \(|y|\). The blue dot is the largest displacement at the centre of the cross section. The blackwhite dots are the smallest displacements at the endpoints. The grey dashed lines carry the displacement values to the vertical axis, and the two vertical ones drop the endpoints to the horizontal axis.

  1. For every \(y\) we have \(y^2 \ge 0\), so \(-0{,}3\,a\,y^2 \le 0\) and \(g(y) \le 75\ \text{m}^2 \cdot a = g(0)\). So the function \(g\) attains its largest value at the point \(y = 0\), the centre of the cross section, and this value is \(75\ \text{m}^2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} = 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m.
  2. For every \(y\) with \(-0{,}005\ \text{m} \le y \le 0{,}005\ \text{m}\) we have \(|y| \le 0{,}005\) m, and by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(y^2 = |y|^2 \le (0{,}005\ \text{m})^2\). So \(-0{,}3\,a\,y^2 \ge -0{,}3\,a\,(0{,}005\ \text{m})^2\) and \(g(y) \ge g(-0{,}005\ \text{m}) = g(0{,}005\ \text{m})\). The function attains its smallest value at two points, the two ends of the diameter on the rim of the cross section.

The difference between the largest and the smallest value is \(0{,}3\,a\,(0{,}005\ \text{m})^2 = 1{,}464 \cdot 10^{-12}\) m, ten million times less than the displacement itself. The cross section is therefore in the U shape from section 1.7.1: the centre moves a little lower than the rim, because the bar gets thinner more at the top than lower down, so the vertical fibres lean and the cross section rises toward the rim (figure 1.242). The example shows two things. A function may attain its largest value inside the interval, and an extremum may be attained at several points. In both examples we found the extrema with an inequality, because the functions were simple. The proof in section 1.14, however, will need to know only that an extremum exists.

Does every function attain a largest value? No. Figure 1.243 shows two ways in which this fails.

The first is the function \(f(x) = x\) on the half-open interval \([0, 1)\). Its values are exactly the numbers in \([0, 1)\). For this set we showed in section 1.8.2 that it has the supremum \(1\) and no maximum: for every \(x \in [0, 1)\) the number \(\tfrac12(x + 1)\) is larger than \(x\) and still in \([0, 1)\). So the function attains no largest value at any point. The values do not run off to infinity, they only approach the number \(1\), which the function would reach only at the point \(1\), and this point does not lie in the domain.

The second is the function \(f(x) = 1/x\) on the half-open interval \((0, 1]\). For every number \(B\) there is by the Archimedean property 1.35 a natural number \(n > B\). The point \(1/n\) lies in \((0, 1]\), and there \(f(1/n) = n > B\). So no number is larger than all the values. The set of values is not bounded above, so it has no supremum, let alone a maximum.

On the left is \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\). The axes are \(x\) and \(f\), with marks \(1\). The thick line is the graph, which starts at the origin. The full dot says that the point \(0\) is in the interval and the function attains the value \(0\) there. The empty circle at \(x = 1\) says that the point \(1\) is not in the interval. The grey dashed line shows the height \(1\): the values approach it but do not reach it. On the right is \(f(x) = 1/x\) on \((0, 1]\). The axes have the marks \(\tfrac12\) and \(1\) for \(x\) and \(1, 2, 3, 4\) for \(f\). The curve starts at \(x = 1\). The dots are the values at the points \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac14\), which are \(1, 2, 3, 4\). The grey dashed arrow indicates that the graph keeps rising as \(x\) approaches the point \(0\), which is not in the interval.

The first way is mild: the values are caught between two numbers, and only the point that would carry the largest value is missing. The second is harsh: the values are not caught. For this second case we need a name. Let us draw it together with the bounded case (figure 1.244).

On the left is the graph. The axes are \(x\) and \(f\). The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(f\). The grey dashed lines are at heights \(B\) and \(-B\), and the graph stays between them. On the right are the values. The number line is marked \(0\). The blue segment is the set of values, from the lower bound \(b_1\) to the upper bound \(b_2\), here \(-1\) and \(1{,}5\) as an example. The grey measure is the interval from \(-|b_1| - |b_2|\) to \(|b_1| + |b_2|\), which contains all the values.

Definition 1.71 Bounded function (omejena funkcija)

A function \(f\) with real values, defined on a set \(D\), is bounded (omejena) on \(D\) if there is a number \(B\) such that \(|f(x)| \le B\) for every \(x \in D\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 1.3.6 (PDF p. 38).

The inequality \(|f(x)| \le B\) means \(-B \le f(x) \le B\). So a bounded function has a bounded set of values \(\{f(x) : x \in D\}\) in the sense of definition 1.29, with the upper bound \(B\) and the lower bound \(-B\). The converse holds too. If the set of values has a lower bound \(b_1\) and an upper bound \(b_2\), then for every \(x \in D\)

\[\begin{aligned} f(x) &\le b_2 \le |b_2| \le |b_1| + |b_2| , \\ f(x) &\ge b_1 \ge -|b_1| \ge -(|b_1| + |b_2|) , \end{aligned}\]

so \(|f(x)| \le |b_1| + |b_2|\) for every \(x \in D\). In the same way we bounded a sequence by a single number in lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence. The function \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) is bounded with \(B = 1\), while the function \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) is not bounded.

A bounded function on a nonempty set \(D\) has a supremum of its values by the completeness axiom 1.32, but, as the function \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) shows, it does not necessarily attain it. So for continuous functions on a closed interval we will prove two things: that they are bounded, and that they attain the supremum of their values.

1.13.2 A continuous function on a closed interval is bounded

Why would a continuous function on a closed interval be bounded? Continuity at a point \(c\) says (definition 1.58, with the error \(\eta = 1\)) that there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(f(x)\) differs from \(f(c)\) by less than \(1\) for every \(x\) in the domain with \(|x - c| < \delta\). So near every point the values are caught in a horizontal band of width \(1\) on each side of \(f(c)\), which belongs to the vertical band between \(c - \delta\) and \(c + \delta\) (figure 1.245). The function \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) gets around this restriction. Its values grow at the points \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\), which approach the point \(0\), and this point does not lie in the domain, so there is no horizontal band there to hold the values. On a closed interval this way out does not exist. If the values were unbounded, we would find points at which they get ever larger. From these, with the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, we would choose points that approach some point \(c\), and \(c\) would lie in the interval, because the interval is closed. In the horizontal band around \(f(c)\) the values would have to be bounded and arbitrarily large at the same time, which is impossible.

Let us draw the proof. The axes are the input \(x\) and the value \(f\). The marks \(a\) and \(b\) are the endpoints of the interval, and the blackwhite dots are the points of the graph above them. The thin curve is the graph of a continuous function. We choose the point \(c\) and on the graph above it the dot, which a dotted line carries to the axis as the value \(f(c)\). The horizontal blue band reaches from \(f(c) - 1\) to \(f(c) + 1\), as shown by the marks on the axis: this is the error \(1\) that we allow. Two grey vertical dashed lines bound the band between \(c - \delta\) and \(c + \delta\), in which the graph stays in the horizontal band. The thick part of the graph belongs to the points of this band. The grey dashed line at the top is an arbitrary bound \(B\) that the values would have to exceed if the function were unbounded, but the points in the band are below \(f(c) + 1\), and its mark is on the axis. The dots are the points of a subsequence that approach \(c\), on the axis and above them on the graph. The first two are grey, because they are still outside the band. The following ones are blackwhite, because they are in the band, so their values are below \(f(c) + 1\) and cannot exceed \(B\).

The proof has eight steps, so let us first look at it on a number line. For every \(n\) we choose a point \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) whose value is larger in size than \(n\), then from these points we choose a convergent subsequence. An example where the chosen indices are \(n_1 = 2\), \(n_2 = 4\), \(n_3 = 6\):

\[\begin{array}{l|cccccc} n & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ \hline |f(x_{\mathrm{n}})| & > 1 & > 2 & > 3 & > 4 & > 5 & > 6 \\ \text{izbrane točke} & & x_{\mathrm{n}_1} & & x_{\mathrm{n}_2} & & x_{\mathrm{n}_3} \end{array}\]

The values of the chosen points grow past every bound, since \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). But since by continuity they approach the number \(f(c)\), they would have to stay bounded. This is the contradiction that the proof writes in steps (figure 1.246).

On the left are the points of the table. The axes are the index \(n\) and the value \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}})|\). The grey dashed line is the height \(n\). The points are the values \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}})|\) for \(n = 1, \dots, 6\), all above the line, because \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}})| > n\). The blue points are the chosen indices \(n_1 = 2\), \(n_2 = 4\), \(n_3 = 6\). On the right are the chosen terms at index \(k\), with the made-up example \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k + 1\). The axes are \(k\) and \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})|\). The grey dashed line is the height \(k\), and the points are above it because \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). The horizontal line is the bound \(B'\) that the values would have to stay within, because they converge. The blackwhite points are below it, the orange ones above it, and these are the contradiction.

Lemma 1.72 A continuous function on a closed interval is bounded (zvezna funkcija na zaprtem intervalu je omejena)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers and \(f\) a function that is defined and continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\). Then \(f\) is bounded on \([a, b]\): there is a number \(B\) such that \(|f(x)| \le B\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).

Proof

We argue by contradiction: we assume that \(f\) is not bounded and derive that the natural numbers would be bounded above.

  1. Suppose that \(f\) is not bounded on \([a, b]\). By definition 1.71 no number \(B\) then works, that is, for every number \(B\) there is an \(x \in [a, b]\) with \(|f(x)| > B\).
  2. For every natural number \(n\) we use step 1 with \(B = n\) and choose a point \(x_{\mathrm{n}} \in [a, b]\) with \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}})| > n\). We take the bound \(B = n\) so that the values \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}})|\) grow beyond every bound.
  3. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is bounded, because \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\). So by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57 it has a convergent subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\). We denote its limit by \(c\).
  4. For every \(k\) we have \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b\). So by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, \(a \le c \le b\), that is, \(c\) \(\in [a, b]\).
  5. The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(c\), because \(c \in [a, b]\). The subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) lies in \([a, b]\) and converges to \(c\), so by lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences the sequence of values \((f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}))\) converges to \(f(c)\).
  6. By lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence there is a number \(B'\) such that \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| \le B'\) for every \(k\).
  7. By step 2, \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| > n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). So \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| > k\) for every \(k\).
  8. By steps 6 and 7, \(k < |f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| \le B'\), so \(k < B'\) for every natural number \(k\). The number \(B'\) would be an upper bound of the set \(\mathbb N\), but by the Archimedean property 1.35 this set is not bounded above. So the assumption of step 1 is false, and \(f\) is bounded on \([a, b]\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 3.3.1 (PDF p. 130).

The proof used three properties, each in its own step. Because the interval is bounded, the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) was bounded and had a convergent subsequence (step 3). Because the interval is closed, the limit \(c\) stayed in it (step 4). Because \(f\) is continuous at \(c\), the values in the horizontal band followed the points in the vertical one (step 5). For the function \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) step 4 fails: the points \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/(n + 1)\), at which \(f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) = n + 1 > n\), approach the point \(0\), which does not lie in the interval. The lemma does not say how large the bound \(B\) is, only that it exists.

1.13.3 The extreme value theorem

Fermat's rule from section 1.3.3 looks for the greatest value, if one exists. We prove the rule that remains from it, that a function has a horizontal tangent at an interior point of the greatest value, in section 1.14.3. Here we first ask whether a greatest value exists at all.

From history

That a greatest value exists was not obvious in itself. Archimedes and Zenodorus already discussed the statement that among all closed plane curves of given length the circle encloses the greatest area (figure 1.247), but nobody proved it rigorously for two millennia, until Weierstrass and Schwarz did (CAJORI, PDF p. 386). Jakob Steiner thought he had proved this statement for the circle. His argument was that a curve that is not a circle can be improved so that the area increases, so the greatest area must belong to the circle (CAJORI, PDF p. 386). Weierstrass gave a simple general existence theorem that applies to the extremes of continuous functions (CAJORI, PDF p. 386). In 1913 Oskar Perron showed by an example what is wrong with Steiner's proof: let us try to "prove" that \(1\) is the largest natural number. No number larger than \(1\) can be the largest, because its square is larger than itself. Hence the largest is \(1\). The argument excludes all the other numbers, but it does not say that a largest number exists at all. In the same way Steiner's proof does not show that among the curves of given length there exists one whose area is greatest (CAJORI, PDF p. 386).

Let us draw the figure part by part. At the top are three closed curves of the same length \(12\), the unit being arbitrary: a \(5 \times 1\) rectangle, a \(3 \times 3\) square and a circle. Under each is its area, \(5\), \(9\) and \(11{,}46\). The blue arrows show Steiner's idea: a curve that is not a circle is improved so that the area increases. At the bottom is Perron's example. The number line has the numbers from \(1\) to \(9\). The blue arcs lead from \(2\) to its square \(4\) and from \(3\) to \(9\), because the square is larger than the number, so \(2\) and \(3\) are not the largest. The grey arrows show the same for \(4\), \(5\) and \(6\), whose squares are already outside the picture. What remains is the number \(1\), in orange, because its square is not larger. This still does not prove that a largest number exists.

We prove Weierstrass's version of the statement that a continuous function on a closed interval attains a greatest and a least value in this section, for functions of one variable.

So a continuous function on a closed interval is bounded, and the set of its values has a supremum \(s\) by the completeness axiom. Does the function attain it? By theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 2, the values come arbitrarily close to the supremum. So for every natural number \(n\) there is a point \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) at which the value is closer to the number \(s\) than \(1/n\). The points \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) themselves, however, need not converge. In figure 1.248 the function has two equally high peaks, and the points \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) may jump from one to the other. So again we choose a convergent subsequence. Its limit lies in the interval, because the interval is closed, and continuity tells us that the value there is exactly \(s\).

We deliberately draw the function with two equally high peaks. The axes are \(x\) and \(f\), and the marks \(a\) and \(b\) are the endpoints of the interval. The blackwhite curve is the graph. The grey dashed line is the supremum \(s\), the height of both peaks, so the function attains it at two points. The points \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) have a value above \(s - 1/n\), so they lie near one peak or the other, and these points jump between the peaks. The sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) therefore does not converge. Each point \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a small dot on the axis \(x\), and above it is a dot on the graph that shows the value \(f(y_{\mathrm{n}})\). The blue dots are these points at odd indices, which form a subsequence, and they lie near the left peak. The blackwhite dots are the points with even indices near the right peak. The blue subsequence converges to the point \(x_{\max}\), which the grey dashed vertical line shows, and there the value equals \(s\).

Let us also draw the values from the proof on a number line (figure 1.250). For the least value we need the function \(-f\) (figure 1.251).

The line is the axis of values \(y\), and the point \(s\) is the supremum. The grey marks show the numbers \(s - 1\), \(s - \tfrac12\) and \(s - \tfrac13\), that is, \(s - 1/k\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3\). The blackwhite dots are the values \(f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\), always closer to \(s\) (a made-up example with \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k + 1\)). The grey measure of width \(\eta\) below \(s\) is the given error, and from some point on all the values are inside it. The blue dot is the limit of the values, \(f(x_{\max})\), which by the uniqueness of the limit is the same point as \(s\). At the bottom is the set of all values \(A\), a thick grey line that ends at \(s\), because \(s\) is its upper bound.

The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(f\), the blue one the graph of \(-f\), its mirror image across the \(x\) axis. The axes are \(x\) and \(y\). The dashed vertical joins the least value \(f(x_{\min})\) of \(f\) and the largest value \(-f(x_{\min})\) of \(-f\), both at the same \(x_{\min}\). At the points \(c\) and \(x\) the difference of values \(d\) is the same for \(f\) and for \(-f\), so the same band \(\delta\) works for both functions.

Theorem 1.73 Extreme value theorem (izrek o največji in najmanjši vrednosti)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers and \(f\) a function that is defined and continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\). Then \(f\) attains a largest and a smallest value on \([a, b]\): there are points \(x_{\max}\) and \(x_{\min}\) in \([a, b]\) such that

\[f(x_{\min}) \le f(x) \le f(x_{\max}) \quad \text{for every } x \in [a, b] .\]
Proof

We must show that there is a point at which the value equals the supremum of the values, and a point at which it equals the infimum.

Largest value.

  1. The set of values \(A = \{f(x) : x \in [a, b]\}\) is nonempty, because it contains \(f(a)\), and bounded above, because by lemma 1.72 the function \(f\) is bounded on \([a, b]\). So by the completeness axiom 1.32 it has a supremum \(s\).
  2. Let \(n\) be a natural number. By theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 2, with the error \(\eta = 1/n\) there is an element of the set \(A\) that is larger than \(s - 1/n\). The elements of the set \(A\) are values of the function, so there is a point \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) in \([a, b]\) with \(s - 1/n < f(y_{\mathrm{n}}) \le s\). For every \(n\) we choose one such point. We take the error \(1/n\) because it goes to zero, so the values \(f(y_{\mathrm{n}})\) will approach the number \(s\).
  3. The sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\) is bounded, because \(a \le y_{\mathrm{n}} \le b\) for every \(n\). By the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57 it has a convergent subsequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\). We denote its limit by \(x_{\max}\).
  4. For every \(k\) we have \(a \le y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b\), so by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, also \(a \le x_{\max} \le b\).
  5. The function \(f\) is continuous at \(x_{\max}\), so by lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences the sequence \((f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}))\) converges to \(f(x_{\max})\).
  6. By step 2, \(s - 1/n_{\mathrm{k}} < f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) \le s\), so \(|f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| = s - f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) < 1/n_{\mathrm{k}}\). By lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(1/n_{\mathrm{k}} \le 1/k\), since for positive numbers a larger denominator gives a smaller fraction. So \(|f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| < 1/k\) for every \(k\).
  7. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is an \(N\) such that \(1/k < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\). By step 6, then, \(|f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\). We picked the error freely, so the sequence \((f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}))\) converges to \(s\) too (definition 1.38).
  8. By lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence, the limits of steps 5 and 7 are equal: \(f(x_{\max}) = s\).
  9. The supremum \(s\) is an upper bound of the set \(A\), so \(f(x) \le s = f(x_{\max})\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\). The function attains its largest value at the point \(x_{\max}\).

Smallest value.

  1. The function \(-f\), which assigns to every \(x \in [a, b]\) the number \(-f(x)\), is continuous on \([a, b]\). For a point \(c \in [a, b]\) and an error \(\eta > 0\) we take the band \(\delta\) that definition 1.58 gives for the function \(f\). For every \(x \in [a, b]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) we then have \(|(-f(x)) - (-f(c))| = |f(x) - f(c)|\) \(< \eta\), so the same band works for \(-f\).
  2. By the part on the largest value, applied to the function \(-f\), there is a point \(x_{\min} \in [a, b]\) with \(-f(x) \le -f(x_{\min})\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\).
  3. Multiply the inequality by \(-1\), which reverses it: \(f(x) \ge f(x_{\min})\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\). The function attains its least value at the point \(x_{\min}\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 3.3.2 (PDF pp. 130-131). The source proves the smallest value with a sequence that approaches the infimum, instead of with the function \(-f\).

The theorem is one of the results that carry the road to the statement about the change of a function between two numbers (figure 1.249). The proof used the same three properties as the proof of lemma 1.72. The boundedness of the interval gave a convergent subsequence (step 3), closedness kept its limit in the interval (step 4), and continuity carried the limit of the points over to a limit of the values (step 5). The theorem says that the extrema exist, not where they are. For the strain of the hanging bar and for the displacement of the points of a cross section we found them with inequalities. Physically the theorem says this. Every quantity that changes along a bar without jumps has a place on the bar where it is largest and a place where it is smallest, if we look at the whole bar including its two ends. If we left out the ends, the largest value could be missing, as for the function \(x\) on \([0, 1)\).

Without any one of the three assumptions the theorem fails. For each let us show where the proof breaks down.

The interval must be closed. The function \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) from figure 1.243, right, is not bounded, as we saw. It is continuous, though. Let us show this at a point \(c \in (0, 1]\). The condition of definition 1.58, written out for this case, is: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|1/x - 1/c| < \eta\) for every \(x \in (0, 1]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) (figure 1.252).

The numbers \(c = \tfrac12\) and \(\eta = \tfrac12\) are an example. The axes are \(x\) and \(y\). The blackwhite curve is the graph of \(1/x\), and the dot is the point \((c, 1/c)\). The horizontal blue band is the error \(\eta\) around \(1/c\). The grey vertical band between \(c/2\) and \(3c/2\) is the window from step 2. The grey lines have the slopes \(\pm 2/c^2\) from step 3, and the graph in the window lies between them. The empty circle at the left edge of the window is the only point where the graph reaches a line, but the edge of the window is not in the window, so inside the window the graph lies strictly between the lines. The blue verticals bound the band \(\delta\) around \(c\), in which the graph stays inside the horizontal blue band.

  1. We pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We bring the fractions to a common denominator: \(1/x - 1/c = (c - x)/(xc)\), so \(|1/x - 1/c| = |x - c|/(xc)\), because \(x\) and \(c\) are positive.
  2. The denominator \(xc\) changes with \(x\), so first we bound it from below. For \(|x - c| < c/2\) we have \(x > c - c/2 = c/2\), so \(xc > c^2/2\) and \(1/(xc) < 2/c^2\).
  3. By steps 1 and 2, for \(|x - c| < c/2\) the difference is \(|1/x - 1/c| \le 2\,|x - c|/c^2\).
  4. As the band \(\delta\) we take the smaller of the positive numbers \(c/2\) and \(\eta c^2/2\). For every \(x \in (0, 1]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) we have \(|x - c| < c/2\) and \(|x - c| < \eta c^2/2\), so by step 3
    \[\Big|\frac1x - \frac1c\Big| \le \frac{2\,|x - c|}{c^2} < \frac{2\delta}{c^2} \le \frac{2}{c^2} \cdot \frac{\eta c^2}{2} = \eta .\]
  5. We picked the error freely and found a band for it, so \(1/x\) is continuous at \(c\). We picked the point \(c \in (0, 1]\) freely, so \(1/x\) is continuous on \((0, 1]\).

So a continuous and unbounded function on \((0, 1]\) exists, and in the proof of lemma 1.72 step 4 fails. The function \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) from figure 1.243, left, is continuous and bounded, but it does not attain its supremum \(1\). In the proof of the theorem the points \(y_{\mathrm{n}}\) would approach the number \(1\), which does not lie in \([0, 1)\), so step 4 fails.

The interval must be bounded. The function \(f(x) = x\) on the unbounded interval \([0, \infty)\), that is, on the set of all \(x \ge 0\), is continuous, since it is a polynomial (corollary 1.64 on sums and products of continuous functions), and it attains its smallest value \(0\) at the point \(0\). It attains no largest value, because for every \(x \ge 0\) the number \(x + 1\) is a larger value (figure 1.253, left). By the Archimedean property it is not even bounded. In the proof of lemma 1.72 step 3 fails. The points \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) = x_{\mathrm{n}} > n\) run away beyond every bound, and so does every subsequence of them, since \(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). So by lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence none of them converges.

The function must be continuous. On the closed interval \([0, 2]\) let us take a function with a jump (figure 1.253, right):

\[f(x) = x \quad \text{for } 0 \le x < 1 , \qquad f(x) = 0 \quad \text{for } 1 \le x \le 2 .\]

The function is bounded, because \(0 \le f(x) < 1\) for every \(x \in [0, 2]\). Its values are the numbers in \([0, 1)\), so the supremum of the values is \(1\), but it is not attained. The function is not continuous at the point \(1\). Let us take the points \(w_{\mathrm{n}} = 1 - 1/(2n)\) for \(n = 1, 2, \dots\) They lie in \([\tfrac12, 1)\), where \(f(w) = w\). The distance of the point \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) from \(1\) is \(1/(2n) < 1/n\), and \(1/n\) goes to zero, so the \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) converge to \(1\). The values \(f(w_{\mathrm{n}}) = w_{\mathrm{n}}\), however, converge to \(1\), not to \(f(1) = 0\), and by lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence they do not converge to \(0\). So by lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences \(f\) is not continuous at the point \(1\). In the proof of the theorem step 5 fails: the points approach the point \(1\), but their values do not approach the value there. This is how, for example, the force carried by a cross section of the bar with the pin and the two blocks from section 1.12.2 jumps as we pass the pin.

On the left is \(f(x) = x\) on the unbounded interval \([0, \infty)\). The axes have the marks \(1\) and \(2\). The thick line is the graph, and the full dot is its start at \(0\). The grey dashed arrow indicates that the graph continues and grows beyond every bound. On the right is a function on \([0, 2]\). The axes: the horizontal one has the marks \(1\) and \(2\), the vertical one the mark \(1\). The thick line is the graph of \(f(x) = x\) up to the point \(1\), which starts at the origin. The empty circle says that the value \(1\) at \(x = 1\) is not reached, and the grey dashed line shows that the values approach it. The full dot at \(x = 1\) is the value at that point, \(0\). The thick line on the axis \(x\) is the graph on \([1, 2]\), where \(f = 0\), with the end dot at \(x = 2\).

The theorem gives sufficient conditions, not necessary ones. A function with a jump can attain a largest value too. The function that equals \(0\) on \([0, 1)\) and equals \(1\) on \([1, 2]\) jumps at the point \(1\), but it attains its largest value \(1\) at every point of the interval \([1, 2]\). The force carried by a cross section of the bar with the pin and the two blocks attains its largest value at the top in spite of the jump, where the top cross section carries the whole bar and both blocks. The theorem only says that for a continuous function on a closed interval the extrema are never missing.

1.13.4 Closed and bounded sets of points

Now let us return to the second question from the start of the section, to fibres. In section 1.6.2 we deformed a steel cube with the edge \(h = 10\) mm, glued with its lower face to a table, by shifting its upper face by \(s = 10\) µm in the direction of the axis \(x\). The axis \(z\) points up, the displacement field is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,z\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) ((1.16)). The four fibres of the front face stretched differently (figure 1.256 shows the directions of the fibres):

\[\begin{array}{l|cccc} \text{vlakno} & AB & AD & AC & BD \\ \hline \text{deformacija} & 0 & 5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7} & +5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4} & -4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4} \end{array}\]

Is there a direction in which a fibre stretches most? The cube is deformed the same way everywhere, so the strain of a fibre depends only on its direction, not on where it lies or how long it is. We describe a direction by a unit vector \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) (definition 1.18). A fibre \(PQ\) of length \(L_0\) in this direction has the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = L_0\,\vek n\). Let us compute how the square of its length changes. Let us first draw the fibre (figure 1.255).

The axes are \(x\) and \(z\). The fibre drawn lies in the front face (\(n_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\)) in the direction of the diagonal \(AC\). The blackwhite segment is the fibre \(PQ\) before loading. The point \(P\) is on the bottom face, where the displacement is zero, so \(P_1 = P\). The orange segment is the fibre after loading. The tip shifts horizontally by \(0{,}001\,L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}}\), magnified in the figure. The vertical measure is \(L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the horizontal measures are \(L_0 n_{\mathrm{x}}\) before loading and \(L_0 n_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}}\) after it.

  1. By (1.16) the difference of the displacements of the ends is \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) = \big(0{,}001\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}),\ 0,\ 0\big)\). The difference of heights \(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}\) is the component \(z\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = L_0\,\vek n\), that is, \(L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}}\). So \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P) = (0{,}001\,L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}},\ 0,\ 0)\).
  2. By (1.15) the vector of the fibre after loading is \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = (L_0 n_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}},\ L_0 n_{\mathrm{y}},\ L_0 n_{\mathrm{z}}) = L_0\,(n_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,n_{\mathrm{z}},\ n_{\mathrm{y}},\ n_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  3. By (1.10) the square of its length is the sum of the squares of the components. We write the square of the first component out as the product \((n_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,n_{\mathrm{z}})(n_{\mathrm{x}} + 0{,}001\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\):
    \[L_1^2 = L_0^2\,\big(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 0{,}002\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 0{,}000001\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big) .\]
  4. The vector \(\vek n\) is a unit vector, so \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = 1\), and \(L_1^2 = L_0^2\,(1 + 0{,}002\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 0{,}000001\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2)\).

So the relative change of the square of the length is

\[q(\vek n) = \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{L_0^2} = 0{,}002\,n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} + 0{,}000001\,n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 .\]

Let us check it on the diagonal \(AC\), which has the direction \(\vek n = (1/\sqrt 2, 0, 1/\sqrt 2)\). Then

\[n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{z}} = \tfrac12 , \quad n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = \tfrac12 , \qquad q = 0{,}002 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0{,}000001 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0{,}0010005 .\]

The length is \(L_1 = L_0 \sqrt{1{,}0010005}\) and the strain

\[\frac{L_1}{L_0} - 1 = \sqrt{1{,}0010005} - 1 = 5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4} ,\]

as in the table of section 1.6.2. The computation is exact, we neglected nothing. Why do we look at \(q\) instead of the strain? Because \(q\) is a polynomial in the three components \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and so by corollary 1.69 it is a continuous function of the point \((n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\). For the strain \(\sqrt{1 + q} - 1\) we would also need the continuity of the square root, which we have not proved. From \(L_1 = L_0\sqrt{1 + q(\vek n)}\), though, it follows that for the same length \(L_0\) the fibre is the longer after loading the larger \(q\) is, since by lemma 1.28 a larger nonnegative number has a larger square. So the direction with the largest \(q\) is the direction with the largest strain.

On which points do we look at the function \(q\)? Let us draw the unit vectors from the origin \(O\). Their tips are exactly the points at distance \(1\) from \(O\), and they form the sphere with radius \(1\) and centre \(O\), that is, the surface of a ball. We call it the unit sphere (enotska sfera) (figure 1.256). Every point of the unit sphere is one direction in space. So the question reads: does a continuous function on the unit sphere attain a largest value? The unit sphere is not an interval, so the extreme value theorem 1.73 is not enough. Which properties of the closed interval did the proof really need? Two. Boundedness: every sequence in it was bounded, so it had a convergent subsequence. Closedness: the limit of a convergent sequence from the interval stayed in the interval. Let us write both properties for sets of points.

Let us draw the figure in parts. The circle is the outline of the sphere with radius \(1\) and centre \(O\), a side view of the ball. The equator is the intersection of the sphere with the horizontal plane, the other circle is the intersection with the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(z\). The front halves are solid grey, the back ones dashed, because the ball hides them. The axes \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are drawn only outside the sphere. Definition 1.74 Closed and bounded set of points (zaprta in omejena množica točk)

Let \(K\) be a set of points of space and \(O\) the origin of the coordinate system. The set \(K\) is bounded (omejena) if there is a number \(R\) such that \(|\overrightarrow{OP}| \le R\) for every point \(P \in K\). The set \(K\) is closed (zaprta) if for every sequence of points \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) from \(K\) that converges to some point \(P\) (definition 1.65) the limit \(P\) also lies in \(K\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 7.1.12 (PDF p. 261) for boundedness, Prop. 7.3.12 and 7.3.13 (PDF p. 277) for closedness.

So a bounded set of points lies in the ball of radius \(R + 1\) around the origin (the ball here does not contain its surface, so we take \(R + 1\) and not \(R\)), since each of its points is at most \(R\) away from the origin, which is less than \(R + 1\). Instead of the origin we could take any other point, which gives the same notion (LEBL1 Def. 7.1.12 and item (i) after it, PDF p. 261). A closed set is a set from which a sequence of its points cannot escape in the limit. LEBL1 defines a closed set differently, with open sets (Def. 7.2.4, PDF p. 264). From its Propositions 7.3.12 and 7.3.13 (PDF p. 277) and 7.2.19 (PDF p. 270) it follows that this is the same property as ours, but we will not need open sets. Both definitions use only distances, so they work in the same way for sets of numbers, where \(|x|\) stands in place of \(|\overrightarrow{OP}|\). The closed interval \([a, b]\) is closed, because by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, the limit of a sequence from \([a, b]\) lies in \([a, b]\). It is also bounded, because by the same argument as for bounded functions \(|x| \le |a| + |b|\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\). The half-open interval \((0, 1]\), however, is not closed, because the sequence \((1/n)\) from it converges to \(0\), which does not lie in it (figure 1.171). It is exactly these two properties that the proof of the extreme value theorem needed.

In space too a set is not necessarily closed (figure 1.258). Let us take all points whose distance from the origin is less than \(1\), that is, the ball of radius \(1\) around the origin. The ball does not contain the points at distance exactly \(1\), which form its surface. The points \(P_{\mathrm{n}} = (1 - 1/n, 0, 0)\) lie in it, because \(|\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{n}}}| = 1 - 1/n < 1\). Their coordinates converge to \(1\), \(0\) and \(0\), so by lemma 1.66 on convergence coordinate by coordinate the points converge to the point \((1, 0, 0)\). Its distance from the origin is exactly \(1\), and it does not lie in the set. The set of all points of space, on the other hand, is closed, because the limit of every convergent sequence of points is again a point of space, but it is not bounded, because the point \((n, 0, 0)\) is at distance \(n\) from the origin, and by the Archimedean property the natural numbers exceed every bound \(R\).

The ray of points \((t, 0, 0)\) with \(t \ge 0\) is also closed but unbounded. It shows that closedness alone is not enough, even when the set is not the whole space. It is closed: if the points \((t_{\mathrm{n}}, 0, 0)\) converge to a point \(P\), then by lemma 1.66 \(P = (t, 0, 0)\) with \(t = \lim t_{\mathrm{n}}\), and by lemma 1.48, item 2, \(t_{\mathrm{n}} \ge 0\) gives \(t \ge 0\). It is not bounded: for every \(R\) the point \((R + 1, 0, 0)\) is in the ray and is at distance \(R + 1 > R\) from the origin, so no number \(R\) meets the definition of boundedness. The ray would be bounded only by a cut-off end, for example the condition \(0 \le t \le 1\), and the set would be closed and bounded (figure 1.257).

The thick arrow starts at the origin \(O\) and runs along the \(x\) axis without end. The grey dashed circle has radius \(R\), which the grey measure shows. The orange dot is the point \((R + 1, 0, 0)\), outside the circle, and the grey measure shows its distance \(R + 1\) from the origin.

How do we show that a set is closed? For the unit sphere, for the cube and for the bar we will use the same argument: the set is given by equations or non-strict inequalities for continuous functions.

Let us draw the argument for \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) and \(c = 1\) in the plane. At the top left the blackwhite circle is the set of points with \(f = c\), and the grey region together with it is the set with \(f \le c\). The blackwhite dots are the points of a sequence in it, and the blue dot is their limit \(P\), which lies on the circle, so in the set. At the top right are the set \(K_1\) and the set \(K_2\), both closed. The blackwhite dots lie in both, and so does their limit. At the bottom are the values of \(f\) on a number line. The first row shows the constant sequence \(c, c, c, \dots\) for points with \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) = c\). The second row shows the values \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) \le c\) of points from the disc, and the third the values \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge c\) of points outside the circle. In all rows the values approach the blue dot, the number \(c\).

Lemma 1.75 Equations and non-strict inequalities give closed sets (enačbe in nestroge neenačbe dajo zaprte množice)

Let \(f\) be a function that is defined and continuous at every point of space (definition 1.67), and \(c\) a number.

  1. The sets of all points \(P\) with \(f(P) = c\), of all points with \(f(P) \le c\) and of all points with \(f(P) \ge c\) are closed.
  2. If the sets \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) are closed, then the set of points that lie in both is closed too.
Proof

We must show that the limit of a convergent sequence from each of these sets lies in it.

1. The equation and the two inequalities.

  1. Let \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a sequence of points with \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) = c\), and let it converge to a point \(P\).
  2. The function \(f\) is continuous at \(P\), so by lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points the sequence \((f(P_{\mathrm{n}}))\) converges to \(f(P)\).
  3. The sequence \((f(P_{\mathrm{n}}))\) is the constant sequence \(c, c, c, \dots\), which converges to \(c\) (section 1.9.2). By lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence, \(f(P) = c\), so \(P\) lies in the set.
  4. If \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) \le c\) for every \(n\), then by step 2 and by lemma 1.48 that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 1, applied to the sequences \((f(P_{\mathrm{n}}))\) and \(c, c, c, \dots\), \(f(P) \le c\). In the same way \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}}) \ge c\) gives \(f(P) \ge c\).

2. Points that lie in both sets.

  1. Let the points \(P_{\mathrm{n}}\) lie in \(K_1\) and in \(K_2\) and converge to \(P\).
  2. The sequence \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) is a sequence from \(K_1\), and \(K_1\) is closed, so \(P\) lies in \(K_1\). In the same way \(P\) lies in \(K_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Example 7.5.9 (PDF p. 291) for item 1 and Prop. 7.2.8 (ii) (PDF p. 266) for item 2, both with open sets. The proof with sequences is not from the source.

The lemma tells us why it matters that the inequalities are non-strict. A limit preserves only non-strict inequalities, as we saw with the strains of the pieces of the hanging bar, which are all smaller than \(2aL\), while their limit equals \(2aL\). The set of points with \(|\overrightarrow{OP}| < 1\) is given by a strict inequality, so a sequence from it could converge to a point on its edge that does not belong to it, as with the disc without its edge and unlike the rectangle with its edge.

Let us apply the lemma to three sets that we will need.

The unit sphere is the set of all points \(P = (x, y, z)\) with \(|\overrightarrow{OP}| = 1\). A length is nonnegative, so by lemma 1.28, used in both directions, it equals \(1\) exactly when its square equals \(1\), that is, when \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\). The function \(f(P) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) is a polynomial in the coordinates, so by corollary 1.69 it is continuous at every point of space. By lemma 1.75, item 1, the unit sphere is closed. It is bounded with \(R = 1\).

A box with edges along the axes is the set of all points \((x, y, z)\) with \(a_1 \le x \le b_1\), \(a_2 \le y \le b_2\) and \(a_3 \le z \le b_3\), where \(a_1 < b_1\), \(a_2 < b_2\) and \(a_3 < b_3\) are numbers. We write it as \([a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2] \times [a_3, b_3]\), where the sign \(\times\) says that each coordinate lies in its own interval. The sheared cube before loading is such a box, \([0, 10\,000] \times [0, 10\,000] \times [0, 10\,000]\) in micrometres. The box is given by six non-strict inequalities (figure 1.260):

\[a_1 \le x \le b_1 , \qquad a_2 \le y \le b_2 , \qquad a_3 \le z \le b_3 .\]

Each contains one coordinate, which is a polynomial and so a continuous function of the point, so by lemma 1.75, item 1, each of the six sets that the inequalities determine is closed. The box is the set of points that lie in all six, so by item 2, used five times, the box is closed too. It is bounded. As for an interval, \(|x| \le |a_1| + |b_1|\), so by lemma 1.28 \(x^2 \le (|a_1| + |b_1|)^2\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). So \(|\overrightarrow{OP}|^2 \le R^2\) with \(R^2 = (|a_1| + |b_1|)^2 + (|a_2| + |b_2|)^2 + (|a_3| + |b_3|)^2\), and by the same lemma \(|\overrightarrow{OP}| \le R\). For the cube \(R = \sqrt{3 \cdot 10\,000^2}\ \text{µm} = 17\,320{,}5\) µm, the length of the space diagonal from the corner \(A\) at the origin to the opposite corner.

The box is drawn obliquely like a cube. The solid edges are visible, the grey dashed ones are hidden behind it. The letters next to the edges show the directions of the axes: \(x\) horizontal, \(z\) vertical and \(y\) into the depth, so the back face is shifted up and to the right. The corner \(A\) is at the origin. The blue segment is the space diagonal to the opposite corner, and its length is \(R\). Every point of the box is at most that far from \(A\).

The hanging bar before loading is the set of all points with \(0 \le x \le L\) and \(y^2 + z^2 \le r^2\), where \(r = 0{,}005\) m is the radius of the cross section (in the figure \(-r\) and \(r\) are the endpoints of the diameter, in millimetres). All three inequalities are non-strict and contain polynomials, so by lemma 1.75, item 1 for each inequality and item 2 used twice, the bar is closed. It is bounded, because by lemma 1.28 \(x^2 \le L^2\), and \(y^2 + z^2 \le r^2\), so \(|\overrightarrow{OP}|^2 \le L^2 + r^2\) and \(|\overrightarrow{OP}| \le \sqrt{L^2 + r^2}\) for every point of it. So both bodies we have computed with in space so far, the sheared cube and the hanging bar, are closed and bounded sets of points together with their surfaces.

1.13.5 The largest value in space

The proof of the extreme value theorem needed the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, which is about sequences of numbers. For sequences of points we get it by using it three times, on each coordinate separately. First we choose a subsequence in which the coordinates \(x\) converge, from it a subsequence in which the coordinates \(y\) converge as well, and from that one a subsequence in which the coordinates \(z\) converge too. Why not all three at once? Because the subsequence that the theorem chooses for the coordinates \(x\) does not take care of the coordinates \(y\) (figure 1.262). Along the way we need that a subsequence chosen from a subsequence is again a subsequence of the original sequence (figure 1.261).

At the top is the row of numbers \(k\), and below it the row of numbers \(n\). The grey arrows lead from \(k\) to \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\) for the example \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12\). The chosen indices are \(k_1 = 2\), \(k_2 = 4\) and so on, so \(m_1 = n_{\mathrm{k}_1} = 3\) and \(m_2 = 6\). The blue arrows and dots show the step for \(j = 1\): from \(k_1 = 2\) to \(m_1 = 3\), from \(k_1 + 1 = 3\) to \(n_{\mathrm{k}_1 + 1} = 5\) and from \(k_2 = 4\) to \(m_2 = 6\), so \(3 < 5 \le 6\). At the bottom is the nesting from the table: the row \(n\) and below it the rows \(n_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\), each a subsequence of the previous one. On the right it is written which coordinates already converge in it.

Lemma 1.76 A subsequence of a subsequence (podzaporedje podzaporedja)

Let \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) be a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\), and \((x_{\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}}})\) a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\), given by a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers \(k_1 < k_2 < \dots\) Then the sequence of numbers \(m_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}}\) is strictly increasing, so \((x_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\). The same holds for sequences of points.

Proof

We must show that \(m_{\mathrm{j}} < m_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) for every \(j\).

  1. Let \(j\) be a natural number. Since the sequence \((k_{\mathrm{j}})\) is strictly increasing, \(k_{\mathrm{j}} < k_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), so \(k_{\mathrm{j}} + 1 \le k_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), since both numbers are natural numbers.
  2. The sequence \((n_{\mathrm{k}})\) is strictly increasing (definition 1.52), so \(n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}} < n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j} + 1}\).
  3. A strictly increasing sequence is also increasing (definition 1.49). So by lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence and step 1, \(n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j} + 1} \le n_{\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{j}+1}}\).
  4. The inequalities of steps 2 and 3 form a chain: \(m_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}} < n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j} + 1} \le n_{\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{j}+1}} = m_{\mathrm{j}+1}\).
  5. For sequences of points the proof is the same, since it is only about the indices.

Source: LEBL1 uses the argument in the proof of Thm 7.4.14 (PDF pp. 284-285). The lemma is not from the source.

The points of the sequence approach three points in turn: top left, bottom left and right. The figure has two parts. On the left are the axes \(x\) and \(y\). The grey dots are the points that the first choice discards, those that approach the right point. The blackwhite dots are chosen. Their coordinates \(x\) are small blackwhite dots on the axis and crowd together at the number \(x\). The blackwhite dots still alternate between top and bottom, so their coordinates \(y\) do not converge. On the right the axes are again \(x\) and \(y\), the grey dots are the same as on the left, and the blackwhite dots are only the lower ones, not chosen, because the upper ones have become blue. From the previous blackwhite ones we choose those that approach the upper point. These are the blue dots, their coordinates \(y\) are small blue dots on the axis \(y\) and crowd together at \(y\). The grey dashed line shows that the blue subsequence converges to the point \((x, y)\).

Three choices give three rows of indices, each a subsequence of the previous one. An example, chosen only to illustrate the nesting:

The last row is a subsequence of the original sequence, and in it all three coordinates converge: \(x\) already from the first choice, because a subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit, \(y\) from the second and \(z\) from the third.

Lemma 1.77 Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem in space (Bolzano-Weierstrassov izrek v prostoru)

Every bounded sequence of points, that is, every sequence of points \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) for which there is a number \(R\) with \(|\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{n}}}| \le R\) for every \(n\), has a convergent subsequence.

Proof

We must show that there is a strictly increasing sequence of indices along which all three coordinates converge. We denote the coordinates of the point \(P_{\mathrm{n}}\) by \((x_{\mathrm{n}}, y_{\mathrm{n}}, z_{\mathrm{n}})\).

  1. The coordinates are bounded. We have \(x_{\mathrm{n}}^2 \le x_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + y_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + z_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = |\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{n}}}|^2 \le R^2\), and since \(x_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = |x_{\mathrm{n}}|^2\), by lemma 1.28 that a larger nonnegative number has a larger square, \(|x_{\mathrm{n}}| \le R\). In the same way \(|y_{\mathrm{n}}| \le R\) and \(|z_{\mathrm{n}}| \le R\) for every \(n\).
  2. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is bounded, so by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57 it has a subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) that converges to some number \(x\).
  3. The sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\), which we index by \(k\), is bounded by step 1, so by the same theorem it has a subsequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}}})\) that converges to some number \(y\). Let \(m_{\mathrm{j}} = n_{\mathrm{k}_\mathrm{j}}\). By lemma 1.76 on a subsequence of a subsequence, \((P_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  4. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\), which converges to \(x\). So by lemma 1.54 on the limit of a subsequence the \(x_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}}\) converge to \(x\). By step 3 the \(y_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}}\) converge to \(y\).
  5. The sequence \((z_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\) is bounded by step 1, so it has a subsequence \((z_{\mathrm{m}_{\mathrm{j}_\mathrm{i}}})\) that converges to some number \(z\). Let \(p_{\mathrm{i}} = m_{\mathrm{j}_\mathrm{i}}\). By lemma 1.76, \((P_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{i}})\) is a subsequence of the sequence \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  6. The sequences \((x_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{i}})\) and \((y_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{i}})\) are subsequences of the sequences \((x_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\) and \((y_{\mathrm{m}_\mathrm{j}})\), so by lemma 1.54 they converge to \(x\) and \(y\).
  7. So all three coordinates of the points \(P_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{i}}\) converge, to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). By lemma 1.66 on convergence coordinate by coordinate the subsequence \((P_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{i}})\) converges to the point \((x, y, z)\).

Source: LEBL1 proof of Thm 7.4.14 (PDF pp. 284-285), where it is written for two coordinates.

The proof repeated the same step three times, and with every choice it kept what it had achieved before: a subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit. In a space with more dimensions we would repeat the step as many times as there are coordinates. Now we can carry the proof of the extreme value theorem over to space. In the transfer we replace three things:

  • the points \(x\) of the interval \([a, b]\) are replaced by the points \(P\) of the set \(K\),
  • the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem is replaced by its version for points (lemma 1.77),
  • the lemma on preserving inequalities is replaced by the closedness of the set \(K\).

Everything else, the supremum, the error \(1/n\) and continuity, stays the same, so the steps below are almost the same as the steps of the proof on the interval. On the unit sphere we will choose in the proof points of a sequence that approach the point \(P\), and it lies on the sphere because the sphere is closed. What the function does to the values is shown in figure 1.264.

Let us draw what the function does. The circle is the set \(K\), the four blackwhite dots are the points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), each closer to the blue point \(P_{\max}\). The thin arrows show that the function \(f\) assigns a number to each point, and the blackwhite dots on the line of values are the values \(f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\). The blue arrow leads from \(P_{\max}\) to the value \(f(P_{\max})\), which is the number \(s\) on the line, where the values of the other points approach, all below it. The values are below \(s\) or at \(s\), because \(s\) is the supremum of all the values.

Theorem 1.78 Extreme value theorem in space (izrek o največji vrednosti v prostoru)

Let \(K\) be a nonempty closed and bounded set of points of space (definition 1.74) and \(f\) a function that is defined and continuous on \(K\) (definition 1.67). Then \(f\) attains a largest and a smallest value on \(K\): there are points \(P_{\max}\) and \(P_{\min}\) in \(K\) such that

\[f(P_{\min}) \le f(P) \le f(P_{\max}) \quad \text{for every } P \in K .\]
Proof

We must show that \(f\) is bounded on \(K\) and that it attains the supremum and the infimum of its values.

Boundedness.

  1. We argue by contradiction. Suppose that \(f\) is not bounded on \(K\) (definition 1.71). Then for every natural number \(n\) there is a point \(P_{\mathrm{n}} \in K\) with \(|f(P_{\mathrm{n}})| > n\). For every \(n\) we choose one such point.
  2. The set \(K\) is bounded, so \(|\overrightarrow{OP_{\mathrm{n}}}| \le R\) for every \(n\). By lemma 1.77, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem in space, the sequence \((P_{\mathrm{n}})\) has a subsequence \((P_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) that converges to some point \(P\).
  3. The set \(K\) is closed, so \(P\) lies in \(K\).
  4. The function \(f\) is continuous at \(P\), so by lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points the values \(f(P_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converge to \(f(P)\).
  5. By lemma 1.42 on the boundedness of a convergent sequence there is a \(B\) with \(|f(P_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| \le B\) for every \(k\).
  6. By step 1 and lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(|f(P_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})| > n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\). So by step 5, \(k < B\) for every natural number \(k\), which contradicts the Archimedean property 1.35. The assumption of step 1 is false, and \(f\) is bounded on \(K\).

Largest value.

  1. The set of values \(A = \{f(P) : P \in K\}\) is nonempty, because \(K\) is nonempty, and bounded above by the first part. By the completeness axiom 1.32 it has a supremum \(s\).
  2. For every natural number \(n\), by theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 2, with the error \(1/n\) there is a point \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} \in K\) with \(s - 1/n < f(Q_{\mathrm{n}}) \le s\). For every \(n\) we choose one such point.
  3. The set \(K\) is bounded, so by lemma 1.77 the sequence \((Q_{\mathrm{n}})\) has a subsequence \((Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) that converges to some point. We denote it by \(P_{\max}\).
  4. The set \(K\) is closed, so \(P_{\max}\) lies in \(K\).
  5. The function \(f\) is continuous at \(P_{\max}\), so by lemma 1.68 the values \(f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converge to \(f(P_{\max})\).
  6. By step 2, \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| = s - f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) < 1/n_{\mathrm{k}}\), and by lemma 1.53, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\) and so \(1/n_{\mathrm{k}} \le 1/k\). So \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| < 1/k\) for every \(k\).
  7. For every error \(\eta > 0\), by the Archimedean property 1.35 there is an \(N\) such that \(1/k < \eta\) for every \(k \ge N\), and by step 6, then, \(|f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - s| < \eta\). So the values \(f(Q_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converge to \(s\) too.
  8. By lemma 1.41 on the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence, the limits of steps 5 and 7 are equal: \(f(P_{\max}) = s\).
  9. The supremum \(s\) is an upper bound of the values, so \(f(P) \le s = f(P_{\max})\) for every \(P \in K\).

Smallest value.

  1. The function \(-f\) is continuous on \(K\): for a point \(P \in K\) and an error \(\eta > 0\), the band \(\delta\) that definition 1.67 gives for the function \(f\) works for \(-f\) too, because \(|(-f(Q)) - (-f(P))| = |f(Q) - f(P)|\).
  2. By the part on the largest value, applied to \(-f\), there is a point \(P_{\min} \in K\) with \(-f(P) \le -f(P_{\min})\) for every \(P \in K\). Multiplying by \(-1\) we get \(f(P) \ge f(P_{\min})\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm 7.5.6 (PDF p. 290) for nonempty compact sets, together with the Heine-Borel theorem 7.4.14 (PDF p. 284), by which closed and bounded sets in space are compact. Our proof is not from the source, it follows the proof of Thm 3.3.2 (PDF pp. 130-131).

The requirement that \(K\) is nonempty is needed, because in the empty set there is no point to carry the largest value. Now we can answer the question about the fibres of the sheared cube. We do not need this notion in this section, but the reader meets it in the literature. The property that the proofs of both theorems needed is that every sequence from the set has a subsequence that converges to a point of the same set. Mathematicians call such a set compact (kompaktna). LEBL1 defines compactness differently (Def. 7.4.7, PDF p. 280) and shows in Thm 7.4.11 (PDF p. 282) that it is the same property. In the space \(\mathbb R^n\) with any number of coordinates the compact sets are exactly the closed and bounded sets. This is the Heine-Borel theorem (LEBL1 Thm 7.4.14, PDF p. 284). The direction from closed and bounded to compact we proved for three coordinates with lemma 1.77 and closedness. In more general sets with a distance, however, closedness and boundedness are not enough (LEBL1 Example 7.4.15, PDF p. 285). The extreme value theorem holds for continuous functions on every nonempty compact set (LEBL1 Thm 7.5.6, PDF p. 290).
