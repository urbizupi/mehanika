Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.3 Equivalent masses of the conrod and the shaking force of one cylinder

An engine stands on a frame (ogrodje): the block with the cylinder, the cylinder head and the bearings of the crankshaft, held by bolts or mounts. The moving parts, the crank, the conrod and the piston, speed up and slow down all the time as they turn. Something has to push them for every acceleration, and that something is the frame. By the law of action and reaction the moving parts push the frame with an equal force in the opposite direction. This force shakes the frame, so it is called the shaking force (tresilna sila). The question of this section is how large this force is and how it changes during one turn of the shaft. We need the answer because it says how much the engine shakes its support, and without it no measure against shaking can be judged.

The model is one cylinder of engine \(E\). Engine \(E\) and its numbers are our model, chosen for this chapter, and are not data from a source. The crank has radius \(a = 50\) mm, the conrod length \(l = 200\) mm, the ratio is \(\lambda = a/l = 0{,}25\), and the cylinder axis passes through the shaft axis \(O\) (section 23.2). The piston with its pin has mass \(m_{\mathrm{p}} = 1{,}5\) kg, the conrod \(m = 1{,}2\) kg. The centre of mass \(G\) of the conrod is at distance \(l_{\mathrm{A}} = 50\) mm from the crank pin \(A\) and at \(l_{\mathrm{B}} = 150\) mm from the piston pin \(B\), and its moment of inertia about the axis through \(G\) is \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0{,}0065\) kg m\(^2\) (definition 8.73). The crank itself is balanced: its centre of mass is on the axis \(O\), so it contributes no force. The engine turns at \(1500\) 1/min, that is at angular speed \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s like machine \(B\) of chapter 9. The axis \(x\) runs along the cylinder axis from \(O\) towards the piston and the axis \(y\) is perpendicular to it in the plane of the mechanism. The crank angle \(\theta = \Omega t\) is measured from the axis \(x\), that is from top dead centre, counterclockwise.

There are five idealizations and each is stated with its error. The links are rigid. The pins and the sliding of the piston on the wall have no friction. The angular speed \(\Omega\) is constant. We do not count the weight of the moving parts: the piston and the conrod weigh \(2{,}7 \cdot 9{,}81 = 26{,}5\) N, which is \(0{,}7\) % of the largest shaking force that we will compute (\(3886\) N), and weight is a constant force, which does not shake the frame. The gas in the cylinder has zero mass. What these idealizations would change is said where we use them.

The acceleration of the crank pin \(A\) is known because it moves on a circle. The acceleration of the piston is known because it moves on a line (section 23.2). The conrod, however, moves in the plane, its centre of mass draws a complicated curve, and its acceleration is hard to write. We ask whether we need it at all. By theorem 8.74 the resultant force on a body equals mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass. For the force it is therefore enough to know how much mass the conrod has and where its centre of mass is, its shape is not needed. So we replace the conrod by two point masses that together have the same mass and the same centre of mass, one at \(A\) and one at \(B\). We already know the motion of both places: \(A\) moves on a circle, \(B\) on a line.

We build the figure as at the board. We draw the conrod as a bar with the crank pin \(A\) at the left end and the piston pin \(B\) at the right end and give it the mass \(m = 1{,}2\) kg. Into it we put the centre of mass \(G\), which by the assumption of the lemma lies on the bar between the pins. We mark the distances \(l_{\mathrm{A}} = 50\) mm from \(A\) to \(G\) and \(l_{\mathrm{B}} = 150\) mm from \(G\) to \(B\), together \(l = l_{\mathrm{A}} + l_{\mathrm{B}}\). Now we leave the bar without mass and put two point masses at \(A\) and \(B\). Where must the centre of mass of the pair be, so that it falls on \(G\)? This is a seesaw. On a seesaw with a child at each end the board balances when the moments about the support are equal, that is when the heavier child sits nearer the support. At \(G\) we must have \(m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,l_{\mathrm{A}} = m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,l_{\mathrm{B}}\) and together \(m_{\mathrm{A}}' + m_{\mathrm{B}}' = m\). This is a system of two equations with two unknowns.

Lemma 23.15 Equivalent masses of the connecting rod (nadomestni masi ojnice)

Let the conrod be a rigid body of mass \(m\) moving in a plane, and let its centre of mass \(G\) lie on the segment between the pins \(A\) and \(B\) with distances \(l_{\mathrm{A}} = |AG|\), \(l_{\mathrm{B}} = |GB|\), \(l = l_{\mathrm{A}} + l_{\mathrm{B}}\). Let \(m_{\mathrm{A}}' = m\,l_{\mathrm{B}}/l\) be the mass at the point \(A\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}' = m\,l_{\mathrm{A}}/l\) the mass at the point \(B\). Then the pair of point masses that move with the points \(A\) and \(B\) has at every time the same mass and the same centre of mass as the conrod, and therefore

\[m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}} , \tag{23.8}\]

where \(\vek a_{\mathrm{A}}\), \(\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) are the accelerations of the points \(A\), \(B\) and \(G\) of the conrod. The resultant force that accelerates the conrod is therefore the same as the resultant force that accelerates the pair of masses. In general the pair does not keep the moment of inertia: it is \(m\,l_{\mathrm{A}}\,l_{\mathrm{B}}\), while the conrod has \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\).

Proof

We have to show that the pair satisfies both conditions, mass and centre of mass, and that (23.8) follows from them.

  1. Masses: \(m_{\mathrm{A}}' + m_{\mathrm{B}}' = m\,(l_{\mathrm{B}} + l_{\mathrm{A}})/l = m\).
  2. The centre of mass of the pair by the definition of the centre of mass (definition 8.73) lies on the line from \(A\) towards \(B\): its distance from \(A\) is \((m_{\mathrm{A}}'\cdot 0 + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\cdot l)/m = l_{\mathrm{A}}\), so the centre of mass of the pair is at \(G\).
  3. Because the conrod is a rigid body and \(G\) lies on the segment \(AB\), at every time \(\vek x_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek x_{\mathrm{A}} + \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{A}}}{l}\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}) = \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{B}}}{l}\,\vek x_{\mathrm{A}} + \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{A}}}{l}\,\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} = (m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,\vek x_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,\vek x_{\mathrm{B}})/m\).
  4. We differentiate twice with respect to time. The ratios \(l_{\mathrm{A}}/l\) and \(l_{\mathrm{B}}/l\) are constant, so \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = (m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}})/m\), which is (23.8).
  5. By theorem 8.74 the resultant is \(\vek F = m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\) for the conrod and \(m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\) for the pair, and by step 4 they are equal.
  6. The moment of inertia of the pair about the axis through \(G\) is \(m_{\mathrm{A}}'\,l_{\mathrm{A}}^2 + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\,l_{\mathrm{B}}^2 = m\,l_{\mathrm{A}} l_{\mathrm{B}}\,(l_{\mathrm{B}} + l_{\mathrm{A}})/l = m\,l_{\mathrm{A}}\,l_{\mathrm{B}}\).

Source: Idea after NORTON §13.4, (13.9) and (13.10), PDF pp. 715-716. The source asks for three conditions for a dynamically equivalent model (mass, centre of mass, moment of inertia), puts the second mass at the centre of percussion, then for a first design moves it to \(A\), where the error is small, and gives a rule of thumb: two thirds of the mass at \(A\), one third at \(B\) (PDF p. 716). The proof for the force is not from the source: it shows that for the force the first two conditions are enough, the approximation comes only with the moment of inertia.

The lemma is exact and we test it on the conrod of engine \(E\). The masses are \(m_{\mathrm{A}}' = 1{,}2\cdot 150/200 = 0{,}900\) kg and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}' = 1{,}2\cdot 50/200 = 0{,}300\) kg (figure 23.8, middle row). The seesaw balances at the centre of mass \(G\) of the pair: \(0{,}9\cdot 50 = 0{,}3\cdot 150 = 45\) kg mm. We also check numerically on the conrod with its real motion: the acceleration of the centre of mass from rigid-body motion (\(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + \tfrac{l_{\mathrm{A}}}{l}\,(\vek a_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek a_{\mathrm{A}})\)) and the sum \(m_{\mathrm{A}}'\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}'\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\) agree in \(8192\) crank positions to \(10^{-12}\) N, which is the rounding error of the computer (script num-03.py).

Why not simply halves, half of the mass at each end? Let us do it. The halves of \(0{,}6\) kg at \(A\) and at \(B\) have the same mass, but the centre of mass \(G'\) of the pair is at the middle of the bar, \(50\) mm from the true centre of mass \(G\). The hypothesis of the lemma, the centre of mass in the same place, does not hold, and the force really changes. The difference of the forces on the frame is largest at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and is \(382\) N, which is \(9{,}8\) % of the largest shaking force (\(3886\) N, computed below). The halves keep the mass but not the centre of mass, and that is the error.

A second example that does not work is the moment of inertia. The pair of the lemma has \(m\,l_{\mathrm{A}} l_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}2\cdot 0{,}05\cdot 0{,}15 = 0{,}0090\) kg m\(^2\), the conrod has \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = 0{,}0065\) kg m\(^2\), so the pair has a moment of inertia \(38\) % larger. For the force this does not matter, because theorem 8.74 contains only \(m\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}}\). For the torque it would matter, since the moment on the conrod in plane motion is \(J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ddot\varphi\) (corollary 8.75). In the computation of the engine torque we will therefore have to account for this difference, while the force is computed exactly with two masses.

We now have the conrod as a pair of masses at \(A\) and \(B\). The crank turns about \(O\), the piston moves along the cylinder axis. We gather all masses into two: the one moved by the crank pin \(A\) on a circle and the one moved by the piston pin \(B\) on a line. The first turns about the shaft axis, so we call it the rotating mass. The second moves back and forth along the cylinder, so we call it the reciprocating mass. The motion of the piston is an oscillation in the same sense as that of a pendulum: it goes back and forth past the middle, although it is not sinusoidal.

A crank of mass \(m_2\) has its centre of mass on the line \(OA\) at distance \(s\) from the axis (for a balanced crank \(s = 0\)). The crank turns about the fixed point \(O\) with constant angular speed, so its centre of mass moves on a circle of radius \(s\), its acceleration is \(s\,\Omega^2\) towards \(O\), and the force that accelerates it is \(m_2\,s\,\Omega^2 = (m_2 s/a)\,(a\,\Omega^2)\). This is the force that would accelerate the mass \(m_2 s/a\) at \(A\) on a circle of radius \(a\). We can therefore replace the crank by the mass \(m_2 s/a\) at \(A\).

Definition 23.16 Rotating and reciprocating mass (vrteča in nihajoča masa)

Let an engine have one cylinder, a crank of mass \(m_2\) with its centre of mass on the line \(OA\) at distance \(s\) from \(O\), a conrod of mass \(m\) and centre of mass \(G\) (distances \(l_{\mathrm{A}}\), \(l_{\mathrm{B}}\) as in lemma 23.15) and a piston with pin of mass \(m_{\mathrm{p}}\). The rotating mass (vrteča masa) is the number

\[m_{\mathrm{A}} = m\,\frac{l_{\mathrm{B}}}{l} + m_2\,\frac{s}{a} , \tag{23.9}\]

and the reciprocating mass (nihajoča masa) is the number

\[m_{\mathrm{B}} = m_{\mathrm{p}} + m\,\frac{l_{\mathrm{A}}}{l} . \tag{23.10}\]

We think of the first at the crank pin \(A\), which runs on a circle of radius \(a\), and of the second at the piston pin \(B\), which runs on the cylinder axis. Both are in kilograms.

Source: NORTON (13.11), PDF pp. 717-718, where the sum of the masses at \(A\) and at \(B\) is denoted \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) and the crank is statically equivalent to a mass at \(A\) (because the crank has constant angular speed, its moment of inertia has no effect, PDF p. 717). The source uses the same symbols. The product \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\) is the unbalance of the rotating part in the sense of definition 23.1, that is the \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\) of machine \(B\) of example 9.64.

For engine \(E\) we have \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 1{,}2\cdot 150/200 + 0 = 0{,}900\) kg (the crank is balanced, \(s = 0\)) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}5 + 1{,}2\cdot 50/200 = 1{,}8\) kg. Together this is \(2{,}7\) kg, which the piston and the conrod have together, as it must be.

Which force do we count as the force on the frame? The moving parts act on the frame in three places: the crank in the bearing, the piston on the cylinder wall and the gas on the cylinder head. If we looked only at the bearing, we would get the force of the bearing, which is not the force on the frame. So we must define the frame and the force on it.

Definition 23.17 Shaking force (tresilna sila)

The frame of the engine (ogrodje) is the body that is at rest relative to the cylinder axis and the shaft axis: the block with the head and the walls of the cylinder, and the bearings of the shaft. The shaking force of the engine \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\) is a vector, the sum of all forces with which the moving parts (crank, conrod, piston) and the gas in the cylinder act on the frame. It has two components in the plane of the mechanism: \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the cylinder axis, pointing from \(O\) towards the piston, and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) perpendicular to it, so that the angle \(\theta\) is measured from the axis \(x\) towards the axis \(y\).

Source: NORTON §11.8, PDF pp. 639-640, where the shaking force is the sum of the forces on the frame, and §13.5, PDF p. 720.

The name says what this force does: it shakes the frame and everything fastened to it. Before the result, let us look at who acts on whom.

We start with the frame: the bearing under \(O\), the cylinder wall above and below and the head on the right. Into the frame we put the moving parts: the crank \(OA\), the conrod \(AB\) and the piston with the point \(B\) at \(\theta = 60^\circ\). Between the piston and the head is the gas, which pushes the piston back towards the shaft with the force \(F_{\mathrm{g}}\) (the arrow in the gas) and the cylinder head outwards. Around the moving parts and the gas we draw a dashed boundary and ask which forces cross it. Of the forces of the frame, the reactions \(R_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{y}}\) in the bearing \(O\), the force of the cylinder wall \(N\) and the force \(F_{\mathrm{h}}\) of the head on the gas cross it. The load driven by the shaft also crosses the boundary, but it is a couple with zero sum, so we do not draw it. We do not count weight. The gas and the force \(F_{\mathrm{g}}\) lie inside the boundary. The axis \(x\) is the cylinder axis.

Lemma 23.18 The shaking force is minus the sum of the inertia forces (tresilna sila je negativna vsota vztrajnostnih sil)

Let an engine have one cylinder, let the crank, conrod and piston be rigid, let the pins and the sliding of the piston be frictionless, let the gas have zero mass, let weight not count, and let the load on the shaft be a couple. Let \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the masses and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\) the accelerations of the centres of mass of the crank, conrod and piston. Then the shaking force (definition 23.17) is

\[\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}} , \tag{23.11}\]

and the gas force does not appear in it.

Proof

We have to show that among the forces that the moving parts and the gas pass to the frame all known forces cancel, except the inertia forces.

  1. For every moving body theorem 8.74 gives sum of forces \(= m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\). We add over the crank, conrod and piston. The forces in the pin \(A\) act on the crank and the conrod with opposite signs, in the pin \(B\) on the conrod and the piston, so they cancel in the sum by the law of action and reaction.
  2. What remains are the external forces on the moving parts: the reaction of the bearing \(\vek R\) on the crank, the wall force \(\vek N\) on the piston and the gas force \(\vek P\) on the piston. Weight does not count. The load is a couple and is not in the sum of forces. So \(\vek R + \vek N + \vek P = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\).
  3. The gas has zero mass, so the sum of forces on it is zero. The piston acts on the gas with the force \(-\vek P\) (law of action and reaction), the head and the wall together with \(\vek F_{\mathrm{h}}\), and \(-\vek P + \vek F_{\mathrm{h}} = \vek 0\), so \(\vek F_{\mathrm{h}} = \vek P\).
  4. The moving parts act on the frame with the forces \(-\vek R\) and \(-\vek N\), the gas with \(-\vek F_{\mathrm{h}} = -\vek P\), again by the law of action and reaction.
  5. The sum is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = -\vek R - \vek N - \vek P = -\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\) by step 2. The gas force \(\vek P\) has cancelled.

Source: NORTON §13.5, PDF p. 720, (13.14e) and (13.14f), and the text: "the gas force is an internal force which is cancelled within the mechanism". The source writes \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = -\vek F_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the inertia force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{i}} = -\sum m\vek a\), which gives the opposite sign to ours. Why, cannot be read from the source: its definition of the shaking force on PDF p. 639 is the sum of forces on the frame, which equals our (23.11), while its expression in (13.14e) has the opposite sign. The magnitude agrees. We use the sign that follows from the law of action and reaction. The proof is not from the source.

The gas force therefore does not shake the frame, although it is much larger than the other forces. In the gas model for engine \(E\) (our choice, not data from a source) the largest gas force is \(20\) kN and the shaking force at most \(3886\) N, five times less. The gas pushes the piston towards the shaft and the head away from it with the same force, and the frame that carries both feels the sum, that is nothing. The gas does load the bearing, though: the reaction of the bearing is \(\vek R = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}} - \vek N - \vek P\) and the gas force \(\vek P\) is in it. So the shaking force is not the force in the bearing. The gas turns the shaft, and we use that in the section on the torque.

We get the acceleration of the piston \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\) from the position of the piston \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(\theta)\) (lemma 23.8). With constant \(\Omega\) we have \(\theta = \Omega t\), \(\dot\theta = \Omega\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\), so by the chain rule \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = x_{\mathrm{B}}''(\theta)\,\Omega^2\), where the prime means the derivative with respect to \(\theta\). By theorem 23.12 the Fourier series of \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) (chapter 9) has the first harmonic only from the term \(a\cos\theta\), and besides the constant only even harmonics, because \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is an even function of the angle \(\theta\) and the square root in it has period \(\pi\). Therefore

\[\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = -a\,\Omega^2\,\bigl(\cos\theta + c_2\cos 2\theta + c_4\cos 4\theta + c_6\cos 6\theta + \dots\bigr) , \tag{23.12}\]

with no sine terms. The coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the Fourier coefficients of the exact expression, and we compute them numerically, we do not prove them in closed form here. For \(\lambda = 0{,}25\) they are \(c_2 = 0{,}254025\), \(c_4 = -0{,}004098\), \(c_6 = 0{,}000074\). The two-term approximation of lemma 23.14 has \(\lambda = 0{,}25\) in place of \(c_2\) and \(c_4 = c_6 = 0\). At \(\theta = 0\) we have \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = -a\,\Omega^2\,(1 + \lambda)\) and at \(\theta = 180^\circ\) we have \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\,\Omega^2\,(1 - \lambda)\), which SymPy confirms by differentiating the exact position: \(x_{\mathrm{B}}''(0) = -a\,(a + l)/l\), \(x_{\mathrm{B}}''(\pi) = a\,(l - a)/l\).

Theorem 23.19 Shaking force of one cylinder (tresilna sila enega valja)

Let an engine have one cylinder with its axis through \(O\), a crankshaft with constant angular speed \(\Omega\) and \(\theta = \Omega t\), and let the assumptions of lemma 23.18 hold. Let \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\) be the rotating and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}\) the reciprocating mass (definition 23.16), \(a\) the crank radius and \(c_2, c_4, \dots\) the coefficients of (23.12). Then the shaking force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = (F_{\mathrm{x}}, F_{\mathrm{y}})\) has the components

\[F_{\mathrm{x}} = (m_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\,\Omega^2\cos\theta + m_{\mathrm{B}}\,a\,\Omega^2\,\bigl(c_2\cos 2\theta + c_4\cos 4\theta + \dots\bigr) , \qquad F_{\mathrm{y}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a\,\Omega^2\sin\theta . \tag{23.13}\]

It therefore has a primary part at frequency \(\Omega\), a secondary part at \(2\Omega\) only along the cylinder axis, and higher even harmonics. The average of each component over a turn is zero. The amplitudes are proportional to \(\Omega^2\).

Idea

By the lemma the shaking force is minus the sum of the inertia forces. With the lemma on equivalent masses and the definition of rotating and reciprocating mass, two point masses remain: one on a circle, one on a line. We know how to write their accelerations, and we substitute.

Proof

We have to show that the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} m_{\mathrm{i}}\vek a_{\mathrm{Gi}}\) of the crank, conrod and piston equals \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\), and that this gives (23.13).

  1. The centre of mass of the crank is on the line \(OA\) at the ratio \(s/a\) from \(O\), so \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}2} = (s/a)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(m_2\,\vek a_{\mathrm{G}2} = (m_2 s/a)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}}\). By lemma 23.15 (equation (23.8)) the conrod is equivalent to the masses \(m_{\mathrm{A}}'\) at \(A\) and \(m_{\mathrm{B}}'\) at \(B\). The piston moves on a line, so \(\vek a_{\mathrm{G}4} = \vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\) and it contributes \(m_{\mathrm{p}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\).
  2. The sum is \((m\,l_{\mathrm{B}}/l + m_2 s/a)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + (m_{\mathrm{p}} + m\,l_{\mathrm{A}}/l)\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}} = m_{\mathrm{A}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}}\,\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\) by definition 23.16.
  3. The point \(A = a\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) with \(\theta = \Omega t\) and constant \(\Omega\), so \(\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} = -a\,\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). The point \(B = (x_{\mathrm{B}}, 0)\) has \(\vek a_{\mathrm{B}} = (\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}, 0)\) from (23.12).
  4. By lemma 23.18, \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}} = -m_{\mathrm{A}}\vek a_{\mathrm{A}} - m_{\mathrm{B}}\vek a_{\mathrm{B}}\). The component \(y\) is \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a\,\Omega^2\sin\theta\), the component \(x\) is \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a\,\Omega^2\cos\theta + m_{\mathrm{B}}\,a\,\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta + c_2\cos 2\theta + \dots)\). We collect the terms with \(\cos\theta\) and get (23.13).
  5. The average over a turn: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos n\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin n\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\) for every \(n \ge 1\) (lemma 9.87). The series for \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\) may be integrated term by term, because it converges uniformly (theorem 23.12, item 4). So each component has average zero. The factor \(\Omega^2\) stands in front of all terms.

Source: NORTON (13.14) and the expression after it, PDF pp. 718-720: the primary and secondary parts of the force are written in the source with the two-term approximation (\(c_2\) replaced by \(\lambda\)), the higher harmonics are only mentioned ("small-magnitude, higher, even harmonics which we truncated", PDF p. 720). The coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) are our computation (num-03.py). The proof is not from the source.

The theorem shows three things. The primary force in the direction \(x\) has amplitude \((m_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\,\Omega^2\), in the direction \(y\) it has \(m_{\mathrm{A}}\,a\,\Omega^2\). If \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 0\) (no reciprocating mass), then \(F_{\mathrm{s}} = m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) is a vector of constant length that turns with the crank, like the centrifugal force of the unbalance \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) of machine \(B\). If \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 0\), the force always points along the cylinder axis. The names primary and secondary part come from the order: the primary is the first harmonic, at the frequency of rotation, the secondary the second, at twice that (NORTON uses the same names, PDF p. 720). The primary is therefore not more important, only first. The amplitude of the secondary part is \(c_2\,m_{\mathrm{B}}\,a\,\Omega^2\) with \(c_2 \approx \lambda\): a shorter conrod means a larger \(\lambda\) and a larger secondary part.

Let us try an example. We draw the mechanism of engine \(E\) at \(\theta = 60^\circ\): the crank \(OA\), the conrod and the piston, the dashed cylinder axis and the angle \(\theta\). On the crank pin \(A\) and on the piston we put the forces: the force of the rotating mass \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 = 0{,}9\cdot 1233{,}70 = 1110{,}3\) N goes from \(A\) along the extension of the crank, the force of the reciprocating mass \(-m_{\mathrm{B}}\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is at this position \(833{,}0\) N, in the direction \(+x\). We put the two arrows head to tail, their sum is the shaking force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\), and by components \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 1110{,}3\cos 60^\circ + 833{,}0 = 1388{,}2\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1110{,}3\sin 60^\circ = 961{,}6\) N. Why \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 = 1110{,}3\) N? Because \(a\Omega^2 = 0{,}05\cdot 157{,}08^2 = 1233{,}70\) m/s\(^2\) is the acceleration that the crank pin has towards the axis.

Example 23.20 Shaking force of engine E (tresilna sila motorja E)

Engine \(E\) (our model) with \(m_{\mathrm{A}} = 0{,}9\) kg, \(m_{\mathrm{B}} = 1{,}8\) kg, \(a\,\Omega^2 = 1233{,}70\) m/s\(^2\). We look for the shaking force in one turn.

  1. Basic forces: \(m_{\mathrm{A}} a\Omega^2 = 1110{,}3\) N, \(m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 2220{,}7\) N, \((m_{\mathrm{A}} + m_{\mathrm{B}})\,a\Omega^2 = 3331{,}0\) N.
  2. By (23.13), \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = 1110{,}3\sin\theta\) and \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 3331{,}0\cos\theta + 2220{,}7\,(c_2\cos 2\theta + c_4\cos 4\theta + \dots)\). With the coefficients of (23.12) the terms with \(\cos 2\theta\), \(\cos 4\theta\), \(\cos 6\theta\) are \(564{,}10\) N, \(-9{,}10\) N, \(0{,}17\) N in turn. The two-term approximation would give \(\lambda\,m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 555{,}2\) N, a difference of \(8{,}9\) N, \(1{,}6\) % of the secondary part, \(0{,}2\) % of the largest force.
  3. At top dead centre \(\theta = 0\) we have \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = 1110{,}3 + 2220{,}7\cdot(1 + \lambda) = 3886{,}2\) N, which is the largest value of the component. At bottom dead centre \(\theta = 180^\circ\) we have \(F_{\mathrm{x}} = -1110{,}3 - 2220{,}7\,(1 - \lambda) = -2775{,}8\) N.
  4. The vector \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\) has its largest magnitude \(3886{,}2\) N at \(\theta = 0\), its smallest \(1094{,}1\) N, and \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) is at most \(1110{,}3\) N.
  5. Comparison: if engine \(E\) weighed as much as machine \(B\) of chapter 9 (\(M = 250\) kg, weight \(2452{,}5\) N), the largest shaking force would be \(1{,}58\) times the weight. If such an engine with a vertical cylinder axis and the piston on top only stood on the floor, not fastened, the upward force would lift it at every top dead centre, because it is larger than its weight. At twice the speed (\(3000\) 1/min) the primary part would be \(13\,324\) N, four times as much.

Figure 23.11 shows these terms over one turn.

First we draw the axes: the angle \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(360^\circ\) and the force in newtons. Then we draw the primary part, \(3331\cos\theta\) with a dashed line, and the secondary part \(564\cos 2\theta\) together with the higher harmonics \(-9\cos 4\theta + 0{,}17\cos 6\theta\) with a dotted line, which makes two oscillations in one turn. The higher harmonics are too small to be seen in the figure. The sum of the two lines is \(F_{\mathrm{x}}\), which has its largest value \(3886\) N at \(\theta = 0\) and its smallest \(-2776\) N at \(180^\circ\). The secondary part has the same sign at both dead centres, \(\cos 2\theta = 1\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(180^\circ\), so at the top the primary and secondary parts add, and at the bottom they subtract in magnitude: \(3331 + 564 - 9 = 3886\) and \(-3331 + 564 - 9 = -2776\) (both with \(0{,}17\) N for \(\cos 6\theta\)). The component \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) is a pure sine, because the rotating mass has only a primary term. The largest magnitude of the vector \(\vek F_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(3886\) N, exactly at \(\theta = 0\), where the component \(F_{\mathrm{y}}\) is zero. That it is not larger elsewhere is shown by the computation in \(8192\) crank positions (num-03.py).

Let us also test when the theorem does not hold. We move the cylinder axis by \(e = 10\) mm sideways from \(O\) (section 23.2), so that it no longer passes through the shaft axis. The position of the piston is then no longer an even function of the angle \(\theta\), and equation (23.12) does not hold. The amplitudes of the harmonics of \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}/(a\Omega^2)\) of order \(n = 1\) to \(4\) are \(1{,}0013\), \(0{,}2550\), \(0{,}0038\) and \(0{,}0042\). The first harmonic gets a sine part \(-0{,}0513\sin\theta\), which the theorem does not allow, and its amplitude changes by \(0{,}13\) %. A third harmonic \(0{,}0038\sin 3\theta\) appears as well, which the theorem does not allow either, with force amplitude \(0{,}0038\cdot m_{\mathrm{B}} a\Omega^2 = 8{,}3\) N. The error is small, but the theorem is no longer exact for such an engine. Lemma 23.18 still holds, because its proof does not use the position of the cylinder, only the acceleration of the piston changes.

This section gave the shaking force of one cylinder as the sum of a primary and a secondary part. In the next sections we use this force: in the torque the same masses and the same accelerations give a moment, and there the difference of the moments of inertia shows (\(38\) % for the pair of masses), and in the balancing of one cylinder we will see how much of the force can be removed. All of this assumes constant angular speed. If the speed changes by \(1\) % in a turn, \(\Omega^2\) changes by about \(2\) % (\(1{,}01^2 = 1{,}0201\)), the amplitudes change by as much, and a tangential acceleration \(a\ddot\theta\) appears as well. The unevenness of running is treated in the section on the flywheel.
