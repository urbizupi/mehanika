Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.4 Stresses from a finite element solution (napetosti iz rešitve s končnimi elementi)

A finite element computation returns the displacements of the nodes. The engineer, however, often wants a stress, for example the largest equivalent stress on the inner wall of a tube, and that is not an unknown of the computation: it is calculated afterwards from the displacements. How good is a stress computed from an approximate displacement, and at which point of the element may we trust it? Of the three errors of definition 17.2 we treat here the discretization error (diskretizacijska napaka) of the stress. A finer mesh reduces only this one.

The answer is in one picture. The stress of a bar is \(E\,u'\), so up to the constant factor \(E\) it is the slope of the displacement, and a linear element has one single slope along its whole length. In figure 17.32 we draw the axes, the position \(x\) horizontally and the displacement \(u\) vertically, and into them the true displacement \(u\) on one element. The element knows only the values at the nodes \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\), which are \(h\) apart, and joins them by a straight line, the chord. Its slope \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) is all the element knows about the strain. The true strain is the slope of the tangent, and that changes from point to point. The tangent at the midpoint \(m\) is almost parallel to the chord, the tangent at the edge \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) is visibly steeper, and somewhere in between lies the point \(\xi\) at which the tangent is exactly parallel to the chord. The stress of an element is therefore good near the middle and poor at the edge. The section first shows this with numbers on the tube, then proves it on a bar and finally uses it for the equivalent stress.

The model is the tube of chapter 11. We draw a quarter of its cross-section (figure 17.33). The grey dashed line is the unloaded wall with the inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and the outer \(b = 60\) mm, and the axis \(r\) measures the radius from the centre. The material is steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)), the bore carries a pressure \(p = 30\) MPa and the outer surface is free. The tube is long and its ends are held by smooth rigid walls, so the state is plane strain (definition 11.32). This is an idealization: a real wall would hold the tube differently near the ends, and the model does not hold there. What it costs we measure later in the chapter, in the plan of an analysis: plane strain and closed ends differ by \(0{,}53\) % in the equivalent stress at the bore, open and closed ends by \(3{,}2\) %. The displacement is radial, \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = u(r)\), depending on the radius only: the wall moves outwards, the inner edge by \(u(a) = 16{,}87\) µm and the outer by \(u(b) = 13{,}37\) µm (the cross-section is drawn full size, the displacements 300 times enlarged). The Lamé solution (theorem 11.28) is known, so we shall see the error: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -30\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 78{,}00\) MPa and \(u(a) = 16{,}8735\) µm.

We divide the interval \([a, b]\) by a mesh of \(n\) equal elements (definition 6.24) of length \(h = (b - a)/n\), four in the figure, and take a piecewise linear displacement \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) built from hat functions (definition 6.25). On an element \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a straight line, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u_{\mathrm{h}}'\) is constant but \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u_{\mathrm{h}}/r\) is not (corollary 11.21). Hooke's law (corollary 11.27) with the Lamé constants (definition 3.18), \(\lambda = 113{,}1\) GPa and \(\mu = 75{,}38\) GPa, and with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives the stresses of an element, the radial and the hoop one on the piece of the wall and the axial one perpendicular to the picture,

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} , \quad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \lambda\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} , \quad \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varepsilon_{\theta\theta}) . \tag{17.15}\]

We look for the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) by Ritz's method from the weak form. What is the weak form for the tube, and what does it tell us about \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) before any computation?

Lemma 17.17 Ritz solution for the tube (Ritzova rešitev za cev)

Let the tube as above, \(W\) the space of continuous, piecewise continuously differentiable functions \(w(r)\) on \([a, b]\) (no essential conditions in \(r\), the piecewise linear \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is in it too), and

\[B(u, w) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(u)\,w' + \sigma_{\theta\theta}(u)\,\frac{w}{r}\big)\,r\,\mathrm{d}r , \qquad \ell(w) = p\,a\,w(a) , \tag{17.16}\]

where the stresses \(\sigma(u)\) follow from equation (17.15) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\) and \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\). Then:

  1. The Lamé displacement \(u\) solves the weak form \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) for every \(w \in W\) (definition 6.6).
  2. \(B(w, w) > 0\) for every displacement \(w \ne 0\).
  3. For every mesh \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(a) \le u(a)\). If every mesh halves the elements of the previous one, \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(a)\) does not decrease.
Proof

We must show the weak form by integration over the pieces, the positivity of the form, and then, from the theorem on the Ritz energy, the sign of the error.

  1. The Lamé stresses (theorem 11.28) depend on \(r\) only, there are no shear components and no body force, so the first equation (11.32) (corollary 11.25) reads \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\). We multiply it by \(r\): \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). This is the equilibrium of the piece of the wall in the radial direction: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) acts on the outer face of the piece over a longer arc than on the inner one, and the difference is balanced by \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) on the side faces, which are inclined towards each other.
  2. Let \(w \in W\). The function \(g = r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,w\) is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, on every piece with the derivative \(g' = (r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})'\,w + r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,w' = \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,w' + \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,w/r\big)\,r\) by step 1. This is the integrand of the form \(B(u, w)\).
  3. By lemma 6.12 \(B(u, w) = g(b) - g(a) = b\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b)\,w(b) - a\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\,w(a)\). The outer surface is free, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\), and the bore carries the pressure, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) (theorem 11.28), so \(B(u, w) = p\,a\,w(a) = \ell(w)\). The right-hand side has the meaning of a work: the pressure \(p\) acts on the inner wall, which at the radius \(a\) has the circumference \(2\pi a\) and so the area \(2\pi a\) per unit length of the tube, so \(2\pi\,p\,a\,w(a)\) is its work on the displacement \(w(a)\), and divided by \(2\pi\) this is \(\ell(w)\).
  4. With \(x = w'\) and \(y = w/r\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}w' + \sigma_{\theta\theta}w/r = (\lambda + 2\mu)(x^2 + y^2) + 2\lambda xy \ge (\lambda + 2\mu - |\lambda|)(x^2 + y^2)\), because \(2|xy| \le x^2 + y^2\). For \(\lambda \ge 0\) we have \(\lambda + 2\mu - |\lambda| = 2\mu\), for \(\lambda < 0\) it is \(2(\lambda + \mu) = 2(K + \mu/3)\) with the bulk modulus \(K\).
  5. By theorem 3.64 \(K > 0\) and \(\mu > 0\), so the factor \(c > 0\) and \(B(w, w) \ge c \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (w'^2 + w^2/r^2)\,r\,\mathrm{d}r \ge 0\). If \(B(w, w) = 0\), the last integral is zero, so by lemma 6.10 (point 3) its integrand is zero on every piece, hence \(w = 0\).
  6. There are no essential conditions, so \(\psi_0 = 0\) in theorem 6.34 and by its point 2 \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{h}}) \le \ell(u)\), that is \(p\,a\,u_{\mathrm{h}}(a) \le p\,a\,u(a)\). The displacement of the inner edge from a mesh therefore does not exceed the true one. For nested meshes the space of the coarse mesh is part of the space of the fine one, so by theorem 6.35 the energy \(\Pi(u_{\mathrm{h}}) = -\tfrac12\,\ell(u_{\mathrm{h}})\) does not increase, hence \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{h}})\) does not decrease.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. It rests on the theorems next to it.

Figure 17.34 puts three columns of the table on number lines. On the displacement axis in part (a) the points \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(a)\) approach the true value from the left and none passes it, as the lemma requires. The distance to the true value is \(0{,}181\) µm at \(n = 2\) and \(0{,}047\) µm at \(n = 4\), almost four times less. So the displacement is fine. On the hoop stress axis in part (b) the values of element 1 go towards \(78{,}00\) MPa much more slowly: the distance is \(1{,}22\) MPa at \(n = 16\) and \(0{,}62\) MPa at \(n = 32\), so it only halves when \(n\) doubles. The same happens on the radial stress axis in part (c), where the values creep towards the prescribed \(-p = -30\) MPa, and at \(n = 16\) there are still \(2{,}88\) MPa missing. The circles and the dashed distance below the axes (b) and (c) are used a few paragraphs further down, at the extrapolation. Why are the stresses so much worse than the displacement? Figure 17.35, which shows the stresses at \(n = 4\), helps.

We draw the axes: radius \(r\) in mm and stress \(\sigma\) in MPa. Into them we put the Lamé curves, against which we measure the error. Then from the solution \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) we compute for every element the stresses by equation (17.15) and draw them in blackwhite. Within an element the stress is not constant, because \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u_{\mathrm{h}}/r\) changes with the radius: \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) falls along the element while \(r\) grows (across the whole wall the displacement falls from \(16{,}87\) to \(13{,}37\) µm while the radius grows from \(40\) to \(60\) mm). At a node where two elements meet, however, each element states its own value, so the blackwhite lines break: at \(n = 4\) the jump is at most \(16{,}71\) MPa in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(7{,}16\) MPa in \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), at \(n = 32\) it is \(2{,}82\) and \(1{,}21\) MPa. The breaks measure how coarse the mesh is. Because \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) is continuous at the nodes, a break arises only in the derivative, not in the displacement. Now we look at the middle of each element: the dots lie almost on the curves, at \(n = 4\) at most \(0{,}046\) MPa away. Finally the circles at the edge, the values of element 1 at \(r = a\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -19{,}32\) instead of \(-30\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 82{,}33\) instead of \(78{,}00\) MPa, the errors are \(10{,}68\) and \(4{,}33\) MPa. The stress is therefore good in the middle of an element and poor at its edge, as figure 17.32 predicts. That this is no accident of the picture we show first on a bar, where the computation is entirely in our hands.

Take the bar with a linear element from chapter 7. If the nodal values are exact (for a bar with \(EA\) constant on every element this is ensured by theorem 6.38 and, for a bar fixed at both ends, by lemma 7.21), the strain of an element \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) of length \(h\) is the slope of the chord through the nodes,

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{u(x_{\mathrm{b}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{a}})}{h} , \tag{17.17}\]

and the stress of the element is \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\). The assumption of exact nodal values carries the whole proof that follows: where it fails, as for the tube, the error of the nodal values adds to the error of the chord's slope, and only measured numbers remain there. At which point of the element is this the true stress?

Corollary 17.18 Exact strain in an element (točna deformacija v elementu)

Let \(u\) be continuously differentiable on \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\) and let \(u(x_{\mathrm{a}})\), \(u(x_{\mathrm{b}})\) be the exact values at the nodes of the element. Then there is \(\xi \in (x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}})\) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = u'(\xi)\), and if \(u\) has a continuous second derivative with \(M_2 = \max|u''|\), then \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(x)| \le h\,M_2\) for every \(x \in [x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\).

Proof

We must show that the slope of the chord is the true strain at some point, and then bound how far it is from it at another point.

  1. The field \(u\) is continuous and differentiable, so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) there is \(\xi \in (x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}})\) with \(u'(\xi) = (u(x_{\mathrm{b}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{a}}))/h = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\). In figure 17.32 this is the point at which the tangent is parallel to the chord.
  2. The bound \(|u'(x) - (Iu)'| \le h\,M_2\) is point 1 of lemma 6.40 (which calls \(M_2\) by the name \(\mu\)), because \((Iu)' = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) is the slope of the chord. In the figure this is the difference between the slopes of the chord and of any tangent, for example the one at the edge.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Step 2 is lemma 6.40, which has the same mean value theorem.

The corollary is honest but does not say where \(\xi\) lies. Can we find it? For fields with a continuous third derivative Taylor's theorem gives a sharper answer.

Instead of the displacement we draw its slope (figure 17.36, part (a)). Into the axes we put the true slope \(u'\) along the element. The slope of the chord is the mean of the slope \(u'\) over the element, so \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a horizontal line at the mean height of the curve. Through the point at the midpoint we draw the straight line with slope \(u''(m)\), the straight part of the curve \(u'\). Left of \(m\) this line is as far above the height \(u'(m)\) as it is below it on the right: the two triangles are equal and cancel in the mean. The mean \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) therefore differs from \(u'(m)\) only because of the curved remainder between the curve and the line, and that is a tiny gap. At the edge \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\), however, the whole height of the triangle, about \(\tfrac12\,|u''(m)|\,h\), separates it from the true slope. The lemma says the same with bounds.

Lemma 17.19 Element strain at the midpoint and at the end (deformacija elementa v sredini in na robu)

Let \(u\) have a continuous third derivative on \([x_{\mathrm{a}}, x_{\mathrm{b}}]\), \(M_3 = \max|u'''|\), \(m = (x_{\mathrm{a}} + x_{\mathrm{b}})/2\) the midpoint and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}}\) from equation (17.17) with exact nodal values. Then

  1. \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(m)| \le \tfrac124\,M_3\,h^2\),
  2. \(|\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(x_{\mathrm{a}})| \ge \tfrac12\,|u''(m)|\,h - \tfrac16\,M_3\,h^2\), and the same for \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\).
Proof

We must show that the chord differs from the tangent at the midpoint by order \(h^2\) and from the tangent at the end by order \(h\).

  1. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac1h\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the horizontal line at the mean height of the curve \(u'\).
  2. Let \(R(x) = u'(x) - u'(m) - u''(m)\,(x - m)\). By Taylor's theorem (theorem 8.68) for \(u'\) we have \(R(x) = \tfrac12\,u'''(c)\,(x - m)^2\) for some \(c\), so \(|R(x)| \le \tfrac12\,M_3\,(x - m)^2\). \(R\) is the height of the area between the curve and the straight line.
  3. The integral \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} (x - m)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) by symmetry about \(m\), so \(h\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(m)) = \int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} R\,\mathrm{d}x\). These are the two equal triangles, one above the height \(u'(m)\) and one below it.
  4. Since \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_\mathrm{a}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}} (x - m)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = h^3/12\), the properties of the integral (lemma 6.10) give \(\big|\int R\,\mathrm{d}x\big| \le \tfrac12\,M_3\,h^3/12\). Division by \(h\) gives point 1, the gap at the midpoint.
  5. For point 2 we put the point \(x_{\mathrm{a}}\) into Taylor's theorem for \(u'\), with \(x_{\mathrm{a}} - m = -h/2\): \(u'(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = u'(m) - \tfrac12\,h\,u''(m) + \tfrac18\,h^2\,u'''(c')\). Hence \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(x_{\mathrm{a}}) = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} - u'(m)) + \tfrac12\,h\,u''(m) - \tfrac18\,h^2\,u'''(c')\). By the triangle inequality and point 1 its absolute value is at least \(\tfrac12\,h\,|u''(m)| - \tfrac1{24}\,M_3\,h^2 - \tfrac18\,M_3\,h^2\), which is point 2, the distance at the edge.

Source: COOK, section 9.9 (PDF p. 335): stresses and strains in an element are most accurate at the Gauss points of a low-order rule. The proof for the bar is not from the sources.

The proof showed why. For a linear element the one-point Gauss rule has its point at the midpoint of the element (definition 7.24, \(q = 1\), point \(0\)), and there the stress error is of order \(h^2\). At the edge of an element the error is of order \(h\) when \(u'' \ne 0\), that is when the stress varies along the element. The cause is the shape of the element: a constant strain cannot follow a derivative that changes. The lemma requires a continuous third derivative of the displacement on the element. A bar has it where the stiffness and the load are smooth along the element. It fails across a step or across a point force inside an element, which is why we put a node there. Without this assumption only the bound \(h\,M_2\) of corollary 17.18 remains, which guarantees no more than order \(h\) at the midpoint too. We test this on a bar for which the third derivative is zero.

The steel hanging bar with a step from section 6.7.3 has in its upper half the stress \(\sigma = \rho g\,(6{,}25 - x)\) with depth \(x\) in metres and \(\rho g = 76\,518\) Pa/m, linear in \(x\) (part (b) of figure 17.36 with the depth \(x\) and the stress \(\sigma\) on the axes, the orange line). So \(u''' = 0\) and \(M_3 = 0\). The nodal values are exact (theorem 6.38, \(EA\) is constant on every element), so lemma 17.19 gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = u'(m)\) exactly. At \(n = 8\) the first element, \(0 \le x \le 1{,}25\) m, has the stress \(E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{h}} = 0{,}43041\) MPa and the true stress at its midpoint \(x = 0{,}625\) m is \(76\,518 \cdot 5{,}625\) Pa \(= 0{,}43041\) MPa (NumPy), equal in all digits given. At the edge of the element, \(x = 0\), the true stress is \(0{,}47824\) MPa, an error of \(0{,}0478\) MPa. This is exactly \(\rho g\,h/2\): in point 2 of the lemma equality holds, because \(M_3 = 0\) and \(E\,|u''| = \rho g\). The error is 11 % of the element stress. At \(n = 16\) the error at the edge is \(0{,}0239\) MPa, half as large, and at the midpoint again zero.

For the tube lemma 17.19 cannot be used as it stands. The nodal values there are not exact, because the coefficients \(r\) and \(1/r\) of the form (17.16) vary along an element: at \(n = 16\) the value \(u_{\mathrm{h}}(a)\) is \(0{,}0030\) µm below the true one (the points just below the true value). The proof therefore does not cover the tube, it covers bars with piecewise constant stiffness. For the tube we can only report numbers. The largest error of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) at element midpoints falls with order \(1{,}88\) to \(1{,}98\) (between \(n = 4\) and \(32\)), at element edges with order \(0{,}89\) to \(0{,}98\) (NumPy). COOK says the same for plane problems: element stresses have convergence rate \(O(h^p)\) with \(p\) the degree of the highest complete polynomial of the field (COOK, section 9.9, PDF p. 339), so \(p = 1\) for linear elements.

The mechanism at the tube's edge is the same as for the bar. Near \(r = a\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,u' + \lambda\,u/r\). The part \(\lambda\,u/r\) the element shows correctly, but the part \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,u'\) is constant, while the true \(u'\) changes by \(u''\) per unit radius. The error at the edge is therefore about \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,|u''|\,h/2\). At \(n = 16\), with \(u''\) at the midpoint of element 1, this is \(2{,}82\) MPa and the actual error of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a)\) is \(2{,}88\) MPa, a difference of \(2\) % (at \(n = 32\) it is \(1{,}44\) against \(1{,}46\) MPa). The condition \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) is a natural boundary condition (section 6.4.2), it is not built into \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\), so the solution satisfies it only in the weak form. The Ritz solution satisfies the weak form of lemma 17.17 for the test functions of its own space. With the hat function of the node \(r = a\) as \(w\), which has \(w(a) = 1\), we get \(B(u_{\mathrm{h}}, w) = p\,a\), the equation for the force at the node, in which a weighted mean of the stress over element 1 appears, not the value at \(r = a\). As \(h \to 0\) the mean approaches the value at the edge and the condition is met in the limit, with order 1.

What about the extrapolation from Gauss points that COOK proposes for the stress at the edge (COOK, section 9.9, PDF p. 335)? The straight line through the values at the two Gauss points of element 1, extrapolated to \(r = a\), gives at \(n = 16\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -27{,}13\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 79{,}19\) MPa. This differs from the element values (\(-27{,}12\) and \(79{,}22\) MPa) by \(0{,}03\) MPa. Both are built from the same constant slope \(u_{\mathrm{h}}'\) of the element and miss the same part of the derivative, so the extrapolation does not help here. Richardson's extrapolation with a known order does (theorem 17.14): for \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\) from element 1 the observed order from \(n = 16, 32, 64\) is \(0{,}96\) (definition 17.11), and with \(q = 1\) from \(n = 16\) and \(32\) we get \(2\cdot 78{,}621 - 79{,}218 = 78{,}024\) MPa (the circle right at the true value), an error of \(0{,}024\) MPa. The wrong order \(q = 2\) gives \((4 \cdot 78{,}621 - 79{,}218)/3 = 78{,}422\) MPa, an error of \(0{,}42\) MPa, about 18 times larger, and without any warning: the value looks fine. The order must therefore be measured or derived, not guessed. Stresses converge one order more slowly than displacements, because they are a derivative of the displacement (lemma 6.40: displacement error \(\tfrac18 h^2 M_2\), derivative error \(h\,M_2\)).

At nodes where two elements meet, programs therefore often print the average of the two values, which COOK calls nodal averaging (COOK, section 9.9, PDF p. 335). The average is natural when the field is smooth. It is also a trap, as the bar with a step shows.

Take the bar of section 6.1.4, \(x\) is the depth below the support. We draw the axes. We draw the true stress: the axial force \(N\) is continuous, \(7{,}512\) N at the step, but the area drops to a quarter, so the stress \(N/A\) jumps from \(0{,}0956\) MPa (above) to \(0{,}3826\) MPa (below). Each element lies on one side of the step, because there is a node at the step (section 6.7.3), so corollary 17.18 and lemma 17.19 hold for each separately. The element stresses are therefore good: each horizontal line is at the true stress at the midpoint of its element. The node at the step, however, belongs to two elements, one above and one below, and the average of their stresses is \(0{,}2391\) MPa, between the true values \(0{,}0956\) and \(0{,}3826\) MPa (the blackwhite dots). This is the stress nowhere in the bar. A finer mesh does not reduce the error: at \(n = 2, 4, 8\) and \(16\) the average at the step is always \(0{,}2391\) MPa (NumPy), since the two elements at the step approach the true values on their own side, while their average stays midway between them. In the thin part, where the stress is 4 times higher, the value \(0{,}2391\) MPa would underestimate the stress by \(37{,}5\) %. COOK therefore warns against averaging across physically valid discontinuities such as a change of material or thickness (COOK, section 9.9, PDF p. 335). Averaging is allowed only across element boundaries where the field is really continuous, and there it works: at the node \(x = 1{,}25\) m (\(n = 8\)) the average is \(0{,}38259\) MPa and the true value is also \(0{,}38259\) MPa, because the stress there is linear.

We still have to test what this means for the equivalent stress. The engineer wants to know whether the tube holds, and for that compares the stress on the inner wall with a limit one. We have no source yet in the book for the limit stress of steel, so we make no comparison with a limit here and compute only the equivalent stress itself. Figure 17.38 shows the principal stresses on the wall.

The top row shows the true state on the wall, with a blackwhite arrow for the difference \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and an orange one for the equivalent stress. The principal stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -30\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta}) = 14{,}40\) (plane strain, corollary 11.27 with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 78\) MPa, in this order by size: \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\), \(\sigma_3 = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\). By theorem 2.93 \(2\,\tau_{\max} = \sigma_1 - \sigma_3 = 108{,}00\) MPa, which is Tresca's criterion. The equivalent stress is by definition 2.107 and lemma 2.100 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sqrt{\tfrac12\big((\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2 + (\sigma_2 - \sigma_3)^2 + (\sigma_3 - \sigma_1)^2\big)} = 94{,}02\) MPa. The bottom row is the same computation with the values of element 1 at \(n = 16\): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -27{,}12\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 15{,}63\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 79{,}22\) MPa, a difference of \(106{,}34\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 92{,}68\) MPa. Both are too low, by \(1{,}54\) % and \(1{,}43\) %, and they fall with order 1: at \(n = 64\) the errors are \(-0{,}39\) % and \(-0{,}36\) %. Why too low? The difference \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) has two errors. The stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is \(2{,}88\) MPa too high (closer to zero than \(-30\)), which reduces the difference by \(2{,}88\) MPa. The stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is \(1{,}22\) MPa too high, which increases the difference. Together the difference is \(2{,}88 - 1{,}22 = 1{,}66\) MPa too small. Most of the error is therefore contributed by exactly the stress that the boundary condition prescribes, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\). Is this enough to reject a mesh? At \(n = 16\) the error of the equivalent stress is \(1{,}4\) %. For a tolerance of 5 % \(n = 8\) already suffices (\(-2{,}8\) %), for 1 % we need \(n = 32\). The equivalent stress \(94{,}02\) MPa is the stress of the plane strain model. Whether this model is the right choice for a tube with closed ends is a question of modelling error, which a mesh does not settle (later in the chapter we compare it with the discretization error on the same tube).

One last thought: how does a program say where the mesh is too coarse when it does not know the true solution? From figure 17.35: where the breaks of the blackwhite lines are large. This idea has a name.

From history

The error estimate that uses the difference between the element stresses and smoothed stresses is called the ZZ (or Z2) estimate, after Zienkiewicz and Zhu (COOK, PDF p. 340). It carries the name because they proposed it in a paper of 1987 and in the second part of a paper on stress recovery of 1992 (COOK, PDF p. 705). The estimate needs only the difference between raw and smoothed stresses, that is what we computed in this section, and so it does not require knowing the true solution. The procedure they suggested is often used for adaptive meshing: after every computation the mesh is refined where the error estimate is large (COOK, PDF p. 343).

Back to the stresses of this section. The ZZ idea is exactly the break at a node: a smoothed stress, for example from averages of nodal values, differs from the element stress by the break, and the difference, combined over the elements into an energy norm, is the error estimate (COOK, section 9.10, PDF pp. 340-341). The largest break of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is \(16{,}71\) MPa at \(n = 4\), then \(9{,}87\), \(5{,}39\) and \(2{,}82\) MPa at \(n = 8, 16, 32\), so when \(n\) doubles it drops almost to half, as is fitting for a stress error of order 1. The estimate has the same trap as averaging: one must not smooth across a step, because the break there is not an error (COOK, section 9.10, PDF p. 342).

What the section showed. The stress of an element is good in the middle of the element (error of order \(h^2\), lemma 17.19) and poor at its edge (order \(h\)), so it is one order less accurate than the displacement and satisfies the natural boundary condition only in the limit. Averaging across nodes helps where the field is smooth and misleads across a step. The equivalent and Tresca stresses from the elements on the tube wall are \(1{,}4\) % and \(1{,}5\) % too low at \(n = 16\). With the order measured from three meshes Richardson's extrapolation corrects the value, as we showed for \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)\). All this is discretization error. For plane and solid elements (chapter 15) the same holds in other terms, but there is no proof here: the citation from COOK stays (section 9.9, PDF p. 339).
