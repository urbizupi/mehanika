Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.12 Connections

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19.

  • The string, separation of variables and the Fourier series: the solution of the wave equation by theorems 13.7 and 13.11 is a sum of products of a function of place \(\varphi(x)\) and a function of time \(T(t)\), and the functions of place are sines, which are complete by lemma 13.12 and theorem 9.119. The same procedure solves heat conduction in a bar (VT, AK, chapter 14), except that the time part there is a decaying exponential and not an oscillation. In belts, which are a string with an axial velocity \(v\), the standing wave of section 13.2 is no longer a solution (lemma 26.11, DSK, chapter 26). Why? A standing wave, like every motion of the string, is the sum of two waves with the same speed \(c\) in opposite directions (theorem 13.11). A moving belt, however, carries the wave along its motion with the speed \(c + v\) and the wave against it with the speed \(c - v\), so their sum is no longer a shape that oscillates everywhere at once. The string of this chapter is the case \(v = 0\), where the two speeds are equal.
  • Bar and shaft: the exact frequencies of theorem 13.24 are the yardstick for finite element models (AK, chapter 7). The shaft of length \(L\) with a disk at its end from section 13.3 is the simplest model of torsional vibration of a drive shaft (the disk has the moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{d}}\) and turns by the angle \(\varphi\)), which chapter 23 continues for several disks (DSK). For a circular shaft the torsional stiffness is \(G I_{\mathrm{p}}\) with the polar second moment of the section \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) (as in section 13.3), and for other sections it is derived in chapter 16 (MK, VD). The critical speeds of a rotating shaft, where bending couples with rotation, are treated in chapter 24 (DSK).
  • Elastodynamics: equations (11.3) with the inertia term from theorem 13.33 have, by lemma 13.34, two speeds, longitudinal \(c_{\mathrm{L}}\) and transverse \(c_{\mathrm{T}}\). The speed \(c = \sqrt{E/\rho}\) is the speed of the bar model (lemma 13.35), valid for waves long compared with the cross-section. Section 13.4.3 compares it with the longitudinal speed of the body, \(c = 0{,}862\,c_{\mathrm{L}}\) for steel. Why is the bar slower? The sides of the bar are free and contract laterally when it is stretched, so the stiffness of the bar is \(E\). In a body, however, the neighbouring material holds back the lateral contraction and the stiffness is larger, \(\lambda + 2\mu\). At \(\nu = 0\) the sides do not contract and the two speeds are equal. The same section also estimates the lateral inertia that the model neglects. A body in several dimensions requires continuum elements (AK, chapter 15). The wave speed also explains the critical step of central differences in time stepping: with a lumped mass it is close to the number \(h/c\) of section 10.13, and that is the time a wave of speed \(c\) needs to cross one element of length \(h\) (section 13.3.4). When a one-dimensional model is no longer enough is a question of modelling (AK, chapter 17).
  • Hamilton's principle for the beam: theorem 13.37, from an action with the energies \(T\) and \(U\), gives the equation of motion and the natural boundary conditions together. The action is assigned to every motion \(w(x, t)\) of the beam, and the figure shows its midspan deflection \(q(t)\). The true motion is the one at which the action is stationary: its first variation is zero for every change of the motion that leaves the position at the instants \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) unchanged. Theorem 8.118 did the same for the bar. The same procedure with the energy of a plate gives the plate equation and its natural conditions on the edge (MK, AK, chapter 18), and for shells the energy is the sum of a membrane and a bending part (MK, AK, chapter 22).
  • Modes and orthogonality: theorem 13.70 is the orthogonality part of theorem 10.21, with an integral in place of a sum, and the expansion in modes of theorem 13.72 is the modal superposition of chapter 10 with infinitely many terms, proved for the sine families, for the string by theorem 13.11. The beam modes of section 13.6 are the basis of a shortened model that keeps only the first few modes (DSK, chapter 21). In flexible links of robots the cantilever shapes of section 13.6 are the chosen shapes of motion (DSK, chapter 28), and for a beam on an elastic foundation the stiffness of the foundation is added as a term to the equation of motion (DSK, chapter 25).
  • The Ritz upper bound: theorem 13.82 proves that, for a bar fixed at both ends or at one end and for a simply supported beam, no frequency of a model with a conforming space lies below the exact frequency with the same number, in the figure for the bar fixed at one end. For the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends this holds under the completeness assumption of the modes (equation (13.87)). This proves the upper bound that chapters 10 and 12 only explained. For the meshes of chapters 7 and 12 (AK) it means that no frequency has a negative error, and by corollaries 10.60 (bar) and 12.46 (beam) the error does not grow when the mesh is halved. For a lumped mass the theorem does not hold, because the lumped mass matrix is not the matrix of the mass form \(\mathcal{M}\). For a uniform bar fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) with equal elements the underestimate is proved (corollary 10.55): with two elements the first frequency is \(0{,}9745\) times the exact one, the second \(0{,}7842\) times. For the beam, where the difference of the matrices is indefinite (section 12.9.2), the underestimate is only a property of the example.
  • Rotary inertia and shear: theorem 13.89 and example 13.90 show that for a simply supported slender beam (\(L = 1\) m, section \(20 \times 40\) mm with the height \(40\) mm in the plane of bending) the first frequency is lower by \(0{,}066\) % (the Rayleigh model) and by \(0{,}270\) % (the Timoshenko model), and for a short one (\(L = 80\) mm) by \(8{,}93\) % and by \(24{,}89\) %. Why such a difference? The slender beam is \(25\) times longer than its height, the short one only twice. The sections turn as the beam vibrates, and the ratio of the kinetic energy of this rotation to the kinetic energy of translation is \(\varepsilon = (\pi r/L)^2\) with the radius of gyration of the section \(r = 11{,}547\) mm, while the ratio of the shear to the bending energy is \(\kappa\varepsilon\) with \(\kappa = 3{,}12\) (section 13.10.4). For the slender beam \(\varepsilon = 0{,}0013\), for the short one \(0{,}21\). For the slender beam the fall by Rayleigh is therefore about \(\varepsilon/2 = 0{,}066\) %, by Timoshenko \((1 + \kappa)\,\varepsilon/2 = 0{,}27\) %. For the short beam this approximation no longer holds, because \(\varepsilon\) is not small. Mindlin plates carry the same idea into two dimensions (MK, AK, chapter 18), and in rotors the rotary inertia of a disk affects the coupling of the transverse motions (DSK, chapter 24).
  • Damping: the equations of this chapter have no damping. If the damping is a combination of mass and stiffness, it would follow from theorem 13.70 (orthogonality in the mass and stiffness forms) that the mode shapes are kept and the equations by modes separate as for several degrees of freedom. We do not derive this here (DSK, chapter 20). More general damping, where the modes couple, is in the same chapter.
  • The column: theorem 13.96 proves that the second variation of the column with a fixed and a pinned end is positive for every \(P\) below the limit \(20{,}19\,EI/L^2\). A positive second variation means that every small bent shape has more energy than the straight position, so the straight column is stable below the limit (section 8.14). This closes the gap between \(\pi^2 EI/L^2\) and the limit left in chapters 8 and 12. Linear analysis still assumes a perfect column and small displacements, and section 8.14 listed the limits of these idealizations. Imperfections and nonlinear buckling are in chapter 17 (AK).
  • Limits of the chapter: the completeness of the modes is proved for the families of sines: the string, the bar fixed at both ends or at one end, and the simply supported beam. For other beam supports, the cantilever and both ends fixed, completeness is cited from sources (GR, CK) and the upper bound is proved under it. The general existence of solutions of elastodynamics is not proved. Damping, plates and nonlinear vibration are in chapters 20, 18 and 17.
