Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.13 Timoshenko beam (Timošenkov nosilec)

In the Euler-Bernoulli beam (Euler-Bernoullijev nosilec) the shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is zero by assumption, yet the shear force (prečna sila) \(V\) exists all the same. We obtained it from the equilibrium of a slice, not from Hooke's law (assumption (c) in section 5.3.2). The model therefore contradicts itself: a material that carries a shear force must deform in shear. How large is this deformation and how much does it add to the deflection of the beam? For our cantilever made of a flat steel bar of length \(L = 1\) m and height \(h = 40\) mm, that is, at the ratio \(L/h = 25\), we shall see that it adds very little, 0,125 % of the deflection. For the same section and a length of 80 mm (\(L/h = 2\)) the contribution is 19,5 %. This section tells when that matters and what changes in the equations.

The assumptions are the same as in section 5.3.2, except for one. The beam lies in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane, the section is symmetric about the \(y\) axis, the material is linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic (definition 3.3), and displacements and slopes are small. We drop only the requirement that sections stay perpendicular to the axis.

From history

A. Duleau, a graduate of the polytechnic school, published in 1820 an extensive series of tests of iron and iron structures (TIMO, PDF p. 89). His bending tests of rectangular beams agreed very well with theory (TIMO, PDF p. 89). For built-up beams of two parts joined by bolts, the measured deflections were always somewhat larger than the calculated ones, and the difference grew with the distance \(h_1\) between the parts (TIMO, PDF p. 90). In his calculation Duleau did not account for the effect of the shear force on the deflection (TIMO, PDF p. 90). This effect grows with the distance \(h_1\) while the total deflection decreases, so the part of the deflection due to shear becomes relatively more and more important (TIMO, PDF p. 90).

Poncelet (1788 to 1867) lectured at the Sorbonne and was the first to introduce the effect of the shear force into the formulas for the deflection of beams (TIMO, PDF p. 95, 97). For a rectangular cantilever of length \(l\), width \(b\) and depth \(h\) under a uniform load he wrote a formula for the largest deflection and observed that the second term in the bracket, which stands for the effect of the shear force, is of practical importance only in comparatively short beams (TIMO, PDF p. 97). Rankine and Grashof wrote this correction in an elementary form. Horace Lamb, with a plane calculation of an infinite beam of narrow rectangular cross section loaded at equal intervals by equal forces alternately up and down, showed two things. The elementary Bernoulli theory of bending is accurate enough if the depth of the beam is small compared with its length. The correction for the shear force by Rankine and Grashof is somewhat exaggerated and must be reduced to about 0{,}75 of its value (TIMO, PDF p. 280).

For the vibration of shafts and beams, simple cases were known in the eighteenth century. For beams whose cross-section dimensions are not very small compared with the length, and for higher modes of vibration, a more complete differential equation had to be derived, one that accounts for the effect of shear forces on the deflection.

Let us return to the question of the section: shear stresses deform the material, so the cross sections do not stay perpendicular to the axis, and we ask how much this adds to the deflection and when it matters. Duleau's tests and Poncelet's formula show that the addition matters for built-up beams with a large distance between the parts and for short beams. Lamb showed that for beams whose depth is small compared with the length the Bernoulli theory is accurate enough.

5.13.1 A section not perpendicular to the axis

In the Euler-Bernoulli beam the section turns as much as the axis, by the slope \(v'\), because it stays perpendicular to it. What if it turns by another number? Let the section stay plane, but let it turn by a number \(\theta(x)\) that we do not tie to the slope of the axis \(v'(x)\). A point at height \(y\) above the axis then moves in the direction \(x\) by \(u - y\,\theta\), where \(u\) is the displacement of the axis, and in the direction \(y\) by the displacement of the axis \(v\). The number \(\theta\) is a ratio (displacement in the direction \(-x\) per unit height above the axis) and has no unit. For small displacements it equals the angle of rotation of the section in radians, measured counterclockwise.

Definition 5.84 Timoshenko beam model (Timošenkov model nosilca)

The beam (definition 5.1) lies in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane. The functions \(u\), \(v\) and \(\theta\) are twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and have real values. The displacement field of the point \((x, y)\) of a section is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = u(x) - y\,\theta(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = v(x) . \tag{5.74}\]

The number \(\theta(x)\) is the tilt of the section (nagib prereza), the number \(\gamma(x) = v'(x) - \theta(x)\) is the shear strain of the beam (strižna deformacija nosilca). The Euler-Bernoulli model is the special case \(\theta = v'\).

What does this mean for the strains? By equations (1.63)

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} = u' - y\,\theta' , \qquad 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} = -\theta + v' = \gamma . \tag{5.75}\]

The second equation is the engineering shear strain of section 1.17.4, the change of the right angle between the axis and the section (figure 5.73). It does not depend on \(y\), because all fibres tilt equally. The other components are zero, as in the Euler-Bernoulli beam. By Hooke's law \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) (uniaxial stress, lemma 3.6) and by equation (3.10) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\).

The internal forces are, by definition, integrals of the stress over the section (section 5.2). The axis \(x\) passes through the centroids of the sections, hence \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) (lemma 5.22), and \(I = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Lemma 5.85 Axial force and bending moment in the Timoshenko model (osna sila in upogibni moment v Timošenkovem modelu)

In the Timoshenko model (definition 5.84) with constant \(E\), area \(A\) and moment of inertia \(I\) and with stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\),

\[N = E A\,u' , \qquad M = E I\,\theta' ,\]

where \(N = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M = -\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Proof

We insert the stress from equation (5.75) and integrate over the section.

  1. By equation (5.75), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,\theta')\). The functions \(u'\) and \(\theta'\) do not depend on \(y\), so we take them out of the integral over the section.
  2. \(N = E\,u' \int \mathrm{d}A - E\,\theta' \int y\,\mathrm{d}A = E A\,u'\), because \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).
  3. \(M = -E\,u' \int y\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,\theta' \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = E I\,\theta'\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. EP (2.5.25), (2.5.26), p. 77, with the axis \(z\) upward and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = -\theta\), their moment is \(M_\mathrm{EP} = -M\), converted to our signs.

Let us compare with the Euler-Bernoulli model. There \(M = E I\,v''\), here \(M = E I\,\theta'\), the same equation in which \(\theta\) replaces the slope of the axis. There are now two unknowns, \(v\) and \(\theta\).

The Timoshenko section stays plane, although the true section warps because of the shear stresses. In the plane elasticity solution for the cantilever (chapter 11, EP example 2.5.1, pp. 79 to 80) the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) contains a term \((2 + \nu)\,F y^3/(6EI)\), which is not linear in \(y\). At \(y = 20\) mm at the edge of the section it is 0,073 µm, while the turn of the section at the free end moves the same edge by 239 µm. The warping of the section is thus 0,03 % of this displacement and the plane section is a good assumption. The error is elsewhere: the tilt of the section is not the slope of the axis.

5.13.2 Shear correction factor

What is the shear force in this model? By the definition of the shear force (definition 5.7), \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), without a sign. If we insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\), equal over the whole section, then \(V = G A\,\gamma\). This is a consequence of the model, but it is not true. On the upper and lower faces of the beam there is no load, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\) there, and by the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.36) also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). A uniform stress \(G\,\gamma\) cannot satisfy this boundary condition. We found the true distribution in section 5.6: by theorem 5.39

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(y) = \frac{V\,S(y)}{I\,b(y)} .\]

For a rectangle this is a parabola, zero at the edges and largest on the axis, \(3V/(2A)\). For our cantilever \(V = -500\) N, the stress on the axis is \(-0{,}9375\) MPa, the average \(V/A = -0{,}625\) MPa, so 1,5 times less (figure 5.74).

What shall we do about the error? We are not interested in the stresses themselves but in how much the beam deforms in shear, and this depends on the energy that the shear stores. We keep the shear strain \(\gamma\) constant over the section and relate the force \(V\) to it by a coefficient \(k\), chosen so that the energy of shear strain equals the energy of the true stresses. In the model the energy per unit length is \(\tfrac12 V\gamma = V^2/(2kGA)\). For the true distribution the energy density is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\) (definition 3.44, where \(\tfrac12(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\)), and its integral over the section is, by lemma 5.80 (equation (5.71)), \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\,V^2/(2GA)\). Equating the two energies gives \(1/k = f_{\mathrm{s}}\).

Definition 5.86 Shear correction factor (strižni korekcijski faktor)

A section has area \(A\), moment of inertia \(I\), width \(b(y)\) at height \(y\) and first moment of area \(S(y)\) of the part above the height \(y\) (definition 5.38). The shear correction factor of the section is the number

\[k = \frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{s}}} , \qquad f_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{A}{I^2}\int \Big(\frac{S}{b}\Big)^2 \mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.76}\]

without a unit, depending only on the shape of the section. The product \(k\,G\,A\) is the shear stiffness of the section (strižna togost prereza), in newtons.

Lemma 5.87 The shear correction factor does not exceed one (strižni korekcijski faktor ne presega ena)

Let a section have area \(A\), the axis \(x\) through the centroid, width \(b(y) > 0\) and moment of inertia \(I\), and let \(V \neq 0\). For the shear stress \(\tau = V S/(I b)\) from theorem 5.39, \(f_{\mathrm{s}} \ge 1\), hence \(0 < k \le 1\). Equality holds exactly when \(\tau\) is constant over the section.

Proof

We show that \(\frac{V^2}{A}(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1)\) is the integral of a square. First we need \(\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A\).

  1. Let the section occupy the heights from \(-c_1\) to \(c_2\), \(\mathrm{d}A = b\,\mathrm{d}y\) and \(S(y) = \int_{\mathrm{y}}^{c_2} s\,b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\). Then \(S' = -y\,b\), \(S(c_2) = 0\) and \(S(-c_1) = \int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).
  2. Integration by parts: \(\int_{-\mathrm{c}_1}^{c_2} S\,\mathrm{d}y = [y\,S]_{-\mathrm{c}_1}^{c_2} + \int y^2\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 + I\), because the boundary terms are zero by step 1.
  3. Therefore \(\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{V}{I}\int S\,\mathrm{d}y = V\). By the definition of \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\int \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}A = V^2 f_{\mathrm{s}}/A\).
  4. We expand the square: \(\int (\tau - V/A)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}A - 2\,\frac{V}{A}\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A + \frac{V^2}{A^2}\,A = \frac{V^2}{A}\,(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1)\).
  5. The integral of a square is nonnegative, so \(f_{\mathrm{s}} \ge 1\) for \(V \neq 0\). It equals zero only if \(\tau = V/A\) everywhere.

Source: The proof is not from a source. ONATE2 (PDF p. 72) states only \(k_{\mathrm{z}} \le 1\) without proof.

Now we understand the number \(6/5\) for the rectangle (lemma 5.80). For it \(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1 = 0{,}2\): the deviation of the parabola from the average, from \(+0{,}5\,V/A\) on the axis to \(-V/A\) at the edge, contributes 20 % of the energy of the average stress \(V^2/(2GA)\). Hence \(k = 5/6\). For steel \(G A = 6{,}031 \cdot 10^7\) N and the shear stiffness is \(k G A = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N.

The factor \(5/6\) is not general. In another section the shear travels differently. For the I-section of section 5.4 (height 200 mm, flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm, web \(180 \times 6\) mm), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 2{,}610\) and \(k = 0{,}383\), close to the share of the web area, \(180 \cdot 6/3080 = 0{,}351\). The shear is carried almost only by the web (section 5.6.2), so the web area determines the shear stiffness. ONATE2 also gives \(k = 6/7\) for a circle and \(k = 0{,}5\) for a thin-walled round tube (figure 2.3, PDF p. 73).

The value of \(k\) depends on what we compare. The energy comparison, as we made it, gives \(k = 5/6\) for a rectangle (ONATE2 §2.2.3.1, PDF p. 72, EP (3.2.43), p. 118). In example 2.5.1 (p. 80) EP compares the deflection of the cantilever with the plane elasticity solution, fixed so that at the centre of the clamped section the displacements and the rotation are zero, and gets \(K_{\mathrm{s}} = 4/3\). This is not an energy factor, since by lemma 5.87 \(k\) does not exceed 1. The shear part of the cantilever deflection is then \(3FL/(4GA) = 0{,}0062\) mm, and for \(k = 5/6\) it is \(FL/(kGA) = 0{,}0099\) mm. The difference of 0,0037 mm is 0,05 % of the deflection 7,97 mm. For a slender beam it does not matter, but it shows that the correction factor depends on the clamping.

The shear force in the model is now \(V = k\,G\,A\,\gamma\). The sign follows from the definition in section 5.2 and from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\): the shear force has the same sign as the shear strain, because \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) without a minus. All three relations are

\[N = E A\,u' , \qquad M = E I\,\theta' , \qquad V = k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta) . \tag{5.77}\]

EP (2.5.27), p. 77, with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = -\theta\) and the axis \(z\) upward, has the same formula, its shear force equals ours. ONATE2 (2.2) and (2.4a), PDF pp. 69 to 70, with the axis \(z\) upward has the same signs as we do: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = -z\,\theta'\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = w' - \theta\), \(M = -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(Q = \int \tau_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(M\) and \(Q\) are our \(M\) and \(V\).

5.13.3 Equations of the Timoshenko beam

The connections between forces and displacements are (5.77). The equilibrium of a slice, however, we derived in section 5.2 only from the definitions of the forces as integrals of stress, without kinematics, and it therefore holds here too: by corollary 5.11, \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The minus in \(V = -M'\) has a reason. The slice between \(x\) and \(x + \mathrm{d}x\) is in moment equilibrium about the centroid of the left section. The force \(V\) on the right section acts on the lever arm \(\mathrm{d}x\) and gives the moment \(+V\,\mathrm{d}x\), the moment of the right section is \(M(x + \mathrm{d}x)\), that of the left one \(-M(x)\), hence \(M(x + \mathrm{d}x) - M(x) + V\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). We insert (5.77).

Theorem 5.88 Timoshenko beam equations (enačbe Timošenkovega nosilca)

A beam of length \(L\) has constant \(E\), \(G\), \(A\), \(I\), \(k\) and a distributed transverse load \(q\), continuous on \([0, L]\) (\(q\) positive upward), without distributed couples (\(m = 0\)) and without point forces and couples in the interior. The supports are arbitrary. The displacement field is the Timoshenko one (definition 5.84), and \(V\) and \(M\) follow equations (5.77). At an end \(\xi \in \{0, L\}\) let a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(C^\xi\) act, either may be zero, and let \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\), \(n_0 = -1\) (as in section 5.10). At each end there are the pairs \((v, V)\) and \((\theta, M)\), as for the beam in section 5.7.2: in each pair the support prescribes exactly one member.

  1. Slice equilibrium, \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\), holds exactly when
    \[\big(k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta)\big)' = -q , \qquad \big(E I\,\theta'\big)' + k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta) = 0 . \tag{5.78}\]
  2. At each end \(\xi\): if the support does not prescribe \(v(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\), and if it does not prescribe \(\theta(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\), that is
    \[n_\xi\,k\,G\,A\,\big(v'(\xi) - \theta(\xi)\big) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi , \qquad n_\xi\,E I\,\theta'(\xi) = C^\xi .\]
  3. For every pair of virtual displacements \(\delta v\) and \(\delta\theta\), continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), which at each end are zero where the support prescribes \(v\) or \(\theta\) respectively,
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L \big(M\,\delta\theta' + V\,(\delta v' - \delta\theta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + C^\xi\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.79}\]
    holds exactly when (5.78) and the conditions of item 2 at both ends hold.
Proof

Items 1 and 2 follow by substitution, item 3 by integration by parts and the fundamental lemma.

Items 1 and 2.

  1. Slice equilibrium is \(V' = -q\) and \(M' + V = 0\) (corollary 5.11). We insert \(V = k G A\,(v' - \theta)\) and \(M = E I\,\theta'\) from equations (5.77). The first relation gives the first equation (5.78), the second the second. Since the substitutions are two-sided, the converse holds too.
  2. By section 5.7.2, at the end \(\xi\) where the corresponding displacement is not prescribed, \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) and \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\). Into these conditions we insert (5.77).

Item 3.

  1. Let \(\delta v\), \(\delta\theta\) be a pair from item 3. The functions \(V\) and \(M\) have continuous derivatives, because \(v\) and \(\theta\) are twice continuously differentiable. Integration by parts gives, with \(f(L) - f(0) = \sum_\xi n_\xi f(\xi)\), \(\int_0^L M\,\delta\theta' = \sum_\xi n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi) - \int_0^L M'\,\delta\theta\) and \(\int_0^L V\,(\delta v' - \delta\theta) = \sum_\xi n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) - \int_0^L V'\,\delta v - \int_0^L V\,\delta\theta\).
  2. The left side of equation (5.79) is thus
    \[-\int_0^L V'\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L (M' + V)\,\delta\theta\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\xi} \Big(n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) + n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) .\]
  3. If (5.78) and the conditions of item 2 hold, then \(-V' = q\) and \(M' + V = 0\). At each end \(n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi)\), either by item 2 or because \(\delta v(\xi) = 0\) where \(v\) is prescribed, and likewise \(n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi) = C^\xi\,\delta\theta(\xi)\). The expression of step 4 is therefore equal to the right side of (5.79).
  4. Conversely, let (5.79) hold for every such pair. By step 4 we then have
    \[\begin{aligned} &\int_0^L (-V' - q)\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L (M' + V)\,\delta\theta\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi} \Big(\big(n_\xi V(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\big)\,\delta v(\xi) + \big(n_\xi M(\xi) - C^\xi\big)\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) = 0 . \end{aligned}\]
  5. Take \(\delta\theta = 0\) and \(\delta v = \psi\) with \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\). The pair is admissible for any supports, because it is zero at both ends. The function \(-V' - q\) is continuous, hence by lemma 4.77 it is zero. With \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta\theta = \psi\) likewise \(M' + V = 0\).
  6. The integrals in step 6 are now zero. Let \(v(\xi)\) not be prescribed and let \(\xi'\) be the other end. Take \(\delta\theta = 0\) and \(\delta v(x) = (x - \xi')/(\xi - \xi')\), which is 1 at \(\xi\) and 0 at \(\xi'\). The pair is admissible for any supports, because it is zero at \(\xi'\) and \(v\) is not prescribed at \(\xi\). Step 6 gives \(n_\xi V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\). Likewise with \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta\theta(x) = (x - \xi')/(\xi - \xi')\), when \(\theta(\xi)\) is not prescribed, we get \(n_\xi M(\xi) = C^\xi\).

Source: EP (2.5.33), (2.5.34), p. 78, EP example 3.5.4, pp. 149 to 150 (natural boundary conditions), ONATE2 (2.8), (2.9), PDF pp. 73 to 74 (virtual work). The signs are converted to ours, not copied from the sources. The proof is not from a source.

What have we obtained? The same equation by two routes. The route through slice equilibrium is short and says what \(V\) and \(M\) are. The route through virtual work shows what the Euler-Bernoulli model hides: the internal virtual work has, besides the term \(M\,\delta\theta'\), the term \(V\,\delta\gamma\) with \(\delta\gamma = \delta v' - \delta\theta\), so the shear force does internal work (section 5.10). Equation (5.79) contains only first derivatives \(\delta v'\) and \(\delta\theta'\), so for the functions continuity is enough, not a continuous derivative (ONATE2, PDF p. 74). This will matter in the finite element computation (chapter 12).

How does this agree with the Euler-Bernoulli equation? If \(kGA\) is very large, \(\gamma = V/(kGA)\) is small and \(\theta \approx v'\). The second equation (5.78) gives \(V = -E I\,\theta'' \approx -E I\,v'''\), the first \(V' = -q\), hence \(E I\,v'''' = q\). The Timoshenko equations are two equations of second order, together of order four, but for two functions and with the boundary pairs \((v, V)\) and \((\theta, M)\).

Statically determinate beam. When \(V\) and \(M\) are already known from equilibrium, the equations need not be solved. From \(M = E I\,\theta'\) we have \(\theta' = M/(EI)\), and from \(V = kGA\,(v' - \theta)\) we have \(v' = \theta + V/(kGA)\). Two successive integrals, no new mathematics, and the clamped end or the supports determine the constants.

Corollary 5.89 Unit load method with the shear term (enotska sila s strižnim členom)

Let the beam have the properties of theorem 5.88 and let \(M\), \(V\), \(v\), \(\theta\) be the true ones under the given load. Let the supports not yield: at each end every prescribed \(v\) or \(\theta\) is zero under the true load. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be a point of the beam, an end or an interior point, at which \(v\) is not prescribed. Let \(m(x)\) and \(v_1(x)\) be the moment and the shear force, divided by \(1\) N, of an imagined system consisting of a force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports (with the same signs as for the true forces). The true \(v\) and \(\theta\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) as in the theorem. Then the deflection \(\Delta = v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) equals

\[\Delta = \int_0^L \Big( \frac{m\,M}{EI} + \frac{v_1\,V}{kGA} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.80}\]
Proof

We must show that the computation of steps 3 and 4 of the proof of theorem 5.88, in which the imagined system replaces the forces and the true displacements the virtual ones, gives \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), when written on the pieces \([0, x_{\mathrm{P}}]\) and \([x_{\mathrm{P}}, L]\) (at an end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) there is only one piece).

  1. On each piece the imagined system has no load along the length, so there \(v_1' = 0\) and \(m' = -v_1\) (corollary 5.11). On a piece \(v_1\) is constant, \(m\) is linear and both are continuously differentiable on the closed piece.
  2. At an interior point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) a point force \(1\) N acts without a couple. By lemma 5.12, \(v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^+) - v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^-) = -1\) and \(m\) is continuous at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\).
  3. On each piece \([a, b]\), integration by parts as in step 3 of the proof of the theorem, with \(m\), \(v_1\) in place of \(M\), \(V\) and with the true \(v\), \(\theta\), gives that \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(m\,\theta' + v_1\,(v' - \theta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\) equals \(\big[m\,\theta + v_1\,v\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (m' + v_1)\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b v_1'\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\), where the last two integrals are zero by step 1.
  4. We add the two pieces. At an interior point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), \(v\), \(\theta\) and \(m\) are continuous, so the terms with \(m\,\theta\) cancel and what remains is \(\big(v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^-) - v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^+)\big)\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) by step 2. This is the term of the force \(1\) N times the displacement, as in corollary 5.64.
  5. At each end \(\xi\) the term \(n_\xi\big(m(\xi)\,\theta(\xi) + v_1(\xi)\,v(\xi)\big)\) is zero where \(v\) or \(\theta\) is prescribed, because the true displacement is zero. Elsewhere the imagined system has no load at the end, except the force \(1\) N when \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is that end. So \(n_\xi v_1(\xi) = 1\) at the end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), otherwise \(n_\xi v_1(\xi) = 0\), and \(n_\xi m(\xi) = 0\) wherever \(\theta\) is not prescribed. At the end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) the sum over the ends is thus \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), at an interior point it is zero, and together with step 4 the total is \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) in both cases.
  6. For the true beam \(\theta' = M/(EI)\) and \(v' - \theta = V/(kGA)\) (equations (5.77)). We insert into the left side of step 3, summed over both pieces.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same shear term \(F L/(K_{\mathrm{s}}\,GA)\) for the cantilever is in EP example 4.2.3 (pp. 177-178).

The formula thus holds for an interior point as well. We test it on the simply supported beam: \(L = 2\) m, force \(F = 1\) kN downward at mid-span, the same \(20 \times 40\) mm bar, \(E I = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(k = 5/6\), \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa, \(kGA = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N. The true ones are \(M = 500\,x\) N m and \(V = -500\) N on the left half and \(M = 500\,(L - x)\) N m and \(V = +500\) N on the right half. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 1\) m gives two reactions \(1/2\) N downward, so \(m = -x/2\) and \(v_1 = +1/2\) on the left, \(m = -(L - x)/2\) and \(v_1 = -1/2\) on the right, and the jump of \(v_1\) is \(-1\) as in step 2. By symmetry the mid-span deflection is twice the left part:

\[\Delta = 2\int_0^1 \frac{(-x/2)\,(500\,x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x + 2\int_0^1 \frac{(1/2)\,(-500)}{kGA}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{F L^3}{48\,EI} - \frac{F L}{4\,kGA} .\]

The bending part is \(F L^3/(48 EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, the shear part \(F L/(4 kGA) = 500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m}/(5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\ \text{N}) = 0{,}00995\) mm, together \(-7{,}982\) mm, downward, as it must be for a downward force. The shear part is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending part. The ratio is \(12\,EI/(kGA\,L^2)\), so \(3\,EI/(kGA)\) at \(L = 2\) m, and it equals that of the cantilever of length \(1\) m (\(3\,EI/(kGA\,L^2)\)) by coincidence. SymPy confirms the numbers.

Without the shear term the formula is theorem 5.78, for \(kGA \to \infty\).

5.13.4 Cantilever by Timoshenko

Let us test this on the cantilever of section 5.1.3: a flat bar \(b = 20\) mm, \(h = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), a force \(F = 500\) N downward at the end. We know \(V = -500\) N everywhere and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\). Data: \(E I = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(kGA = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N. Conditions by item 2 of the theorem: at \(x = 0\) both \(v\) and \(\theta\) are prescribed, \(v(0) = \theta(0) = 0\), and at the free end \(x = L\) we have \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and \(M(L) = 0\).

  1. \(\theta' = M/(EI) = -F\,(L - x)/(EI)\) and \(\theta(0) = 0\), hence \(\theta(x) = -F\,(L x - x^2/2)/(EI)\). At the end \(\theta(L) = -F L^2/(2EI) = -0{,}011958\).
  2. \(v' = \theta + V/(kGA) = \theta - F/(kGA)\). The shear strain is constant, \(\gamma = -F/(kGA) = -9{,}949 \cdot 10^{-6}\), because \(V\) is constant.
  3. From \(v(0) = 0\) it follows that \(v(x) = -F\,(L x^2/2 - x^3/6)/(EI) - F\,x/(kGA)\) and at the end \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI) - F L/(kGA)\).

SymPy confirms that this solution satisfies both equations (5.78): \(\gamma\) is constant, so \((kGA\,\gamma)' = 0 = -q\), and \((EI\,\theta')' = M' = F\), which is \(-V\). The result agrees with EP, p. 80 (cantilever), and with EP example 4.2.3, pp. 177 to 178.

The bending part is \(F L^3/(3EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, the shear part \(F L/(kGA) = 0{,}00995\) mm, together \(7{,}982\) mm. The shear part is 0,125 % of the bending part. The slope of the axis at the end is \(v'(L) = \theta(L) + \gamma = -0{,}011968\), the tilt of the section \(-0{,}011958\). The difference is \(\gamma\) and shows only in the fourth significant digit.

The same shear part is given by corollary 5.89. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at the end gives \(m = L - x\) and \(v_1 = 1\), the true ones are \(M = -F\,(L - x)\) and \(V = -F\), so \(\Delta = -F L^3/(3EI) - F L/(kGA) = -7{,}972 - 0{,}00995 = -7{,}982\) mm. The shear part is \(F L/(kGA) = 500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m}/(5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\ \text{N}) = 0{,}00995\) mm, that is \(0{,}125\) % of \(7{,}972\) mm, as above.

Why 0,125 % and when does it grow? The ratio of the shear and the bending part is

\[\frac{F L/(kGA)}{F L^3/(3EI)} = \frac{3 E I}{k\,G\,A\,L^2} = \frac{3 E\,b\,h^3/12}{k\,G\,b\,h\,L^2} = \frac{E}{4 k G}\,\frac{h^2}{L^2} = \frac{1 + \nu}{2k}\,\frac{h^2}{L^2} ,\]

because \(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\) (equation (3.10)). For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(k = 5/6\), \((1 + \nu)/(2k) = 0{,}78\). The cause is how the stiffnesses grow with \(h\): the bending stiffness \(E I = E b h^3/12\) with the third power, the shear stiffness \(kGA = kGbh\) with the first. If we halve the height, the bending compliance grows 8 times, the shear compliance only 2 times, and their ratio is \(\tfrac14\) of the former. For the length it is the other way round: the bending compliance is proportional to \(L^3\), the shear one to \(L\), so a length shorter by a factor of 5 reduces the bending part 125 times and the shear part 5 times, and the ratio grows 25 times. The numbers for three lengths of the same bar (SymPy):

\(L\) \(L/h\) bending part shear part shear share
1 m 25 7,972 mm 0,00995 mm 0,125 %
0,2 m 5 0,06378 mm 0,00199 mm 3,12 %
80 mm 2 0,004082 mm 0,000796 mm 19,5 %

The share falls with the square of the ratio \(h/L\) (figures 5.75 and 5.76): for a bar five times shorter it is 25 times larger, \(0{,}125 \cdot 25 = 3{,}12\).

Simply supported beam with a force at the midspan. Is it the same with the other support? Take \(L = 2\) m (\(L/h = 50\)) with a force \(F = 1\) kN downward at the midspan. By section 5.1.3, \(M = 500\) N m at the midspan and \(V = -500\) N in the left half. By symmetry the midspan does not turn, \(\theta(L/2) = 0\), so the left half behaves like a cantilever of length 1 m, clamped at the midspan, with a force of 500 N at the end. The two integrals from the paragraph on the statically determinate beam give (SymPy) \(v(L/2) = -F L^3/(48EI) - F L/(4kGA) = -7{,}972 - 0{,}00995 = -7{,}982\) mm. The shear part is the same as for the cantilever, because \(500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m}\) equals \(1000\ \text{N} \cdot 2\ \text{m}/4\). The rotation of the section at the support, \(\theta(0) = -F L^2/(16EI) = -0{,}011958\), is likewise equal to the rotation of the cantilever end, for the same reason.

Uniform load. For \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m and \(L = 2\) m the shear part of the midspan deflection is \(q_0 L^2/(8kGA) = 0{,}00995\) mm, the bending part \(5 q_0 L^4/(384 E I) = 9{,}965\) mm, the ratio 0,0998 %. In general the ratio is \(8\,(1 + \nu)/(5k)\,(h/L)^2 = 2{,}496\,(h/L)^2\), so 2,5 % at \(L/h = 10\). The same number 0,00995 mm as for the cantilever is a coincidence: \(q_0 L^2/8\) and \(F L\) are both 500 N m.

How does this agree with plane elasticity? SADD (8.1.37), PDF p. 177, gives for a simply supported beam with a uniform load the midspan deflection \(\frac{5 q_0 L^4}{384 E I}\big[1 + \tfrac{12}{5}\big(\tfrac45 + \tfrac\nu2\big)\tfrac{h^2}{L^2}\big]\), where \(2c\) is the height and \(2l\) the length of the beam (\(c/l = h/L\)). For \(\nu = 0\) the correction is \(1{,}92\,(h/L)^2\). The Timoshenko model at \(\nu = 0\) and \(k = 5/6\) gives \(8/(5k) = 1{,}92\), exactly the same. For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the plane correction is \(2{,}28\,(h/L)^2\) and the Timoshenko one \(2{,}496\,(h/L)^2\). The difference lies in the term with \(\nu\): the Timoshenko model gets it only through the shear modulus \(G\) (\(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\)), while plane elasticity gets it also through the transverse strain, which the beam model neglects (assumption (b) in section 5.3.2). SADD (PDF p. 579) writes that the effect of shear on the deflection is important only for very short beams with a ratio of length to height below 10.

5.13.5 Which theory when

At \(L/h = 10\) the shear share of the deflection is between 0,78 % (cantilever with an end force) and 2,5 % (simply supported beam with a uniform load), at \(L/h = 20\) between 0,2 % and 0,62 %, at \(L/h = 5\) between 3,1 % and 10 %. Rule: the Euler-Bernoulli model suffices when \(L/h\) is at least 10 and we want an accuracy of a few percent. Below this limit the shear part exceeds a few percent and the Timoshenko model captures it. At \(L/h = 2\) the shear part is 19,5 %, but for such a short beam it is itself uncertain: the way of clamping from the remark above changes it from 19,5 % to 12,2 % of the bending part, that is, by 7 percentage points of the bending part. For such a beam no beam theory is enough and we need plane elasticity (EP example 2.5.1, p. 80, where both beam models differ from it mainly because of the Poisson effect).

The price of the model: the Euler-Bernoulli model has one unknown \(v\) and an equation of fourth order, the Timoshenko model two unknowns, \(v\) and \(\theta\), and two equations of second order. In the finite element computation (chapter 12) continuous functions without a continuous derivative suffice for the Timoshenko model. For slender beams, however, shear locking (strižno zaklepanje) can appear: the finite element solution is too stiff, because the shear term \(V\,\delta\gamma\) dominates (ONATE2 §2.4, PDF pp. 78 to 85). The cause and the remedy are described in chapter 12. In the vibration of beams the rotary inertia of the section (vztrajnost vrtenja prereza) joins the Timoshenko model (CK §13.4, PDF pp. 418 to 419), which chapter 13 treats.
