Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.13 Timoshenko beam (Timošenkov nosilec)

In the Euler-Bernoulli beam (Euler-Bernoullijev nosilec) the shear strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is zero by assumption (c) of section 5.3.2, yet the shear force (prečna sila) \(V\) exists all the same. We obtained it from equilibrium, not from Hooke's law. The end of the cantilever, cut off at the section at \(x\) (figure 5.210), carries the force \(F = 500\) N downward. For the piece to be in equilibrium, a vertical force of \(500\) N upward must act on it at the cut face, and with it a moment that keeps it from turning. The cut face of this piece looks toward the negative \(x\) axis, so this force is \(-V\), and \(V = -500\) N. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, the modulus \(G\) from equation (3.22)) the shear stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) it would follow that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and with it \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\): the cut face would carry only horizontal normal stresses, and the force \(F\) would stay unbalanced. The model therefore contradicts itself.

A material that carries a shear force must therefore deform in shear, and the section tilts against the axis, as the angle \(\gamma\) in figure 5.211 will show. How large is this deformation and how much does it add to the deflection of the beam? For our cantilever made of a flat steel bar of length \(L = 1\) m and height \(h = 40\) mm, that is, at the ratio \(L/h = 25\), we shall see that it adds very little, 0,125 % of the bending part of the deflection. For the same section and a length of 80 mm (\(L/h = 2\)) the shear part of the deflection is 19,5 % of the bending part, that is 16,3 % of the total deflection. This section tells when that matters and what changes in the equations.

The assumptions are the same as in section 5.3.2, except for one. The beam lies in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane, the section is symmetric about the \(y\) axis, the material is linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic (definition 3.9), and displacements and slopes are small. We drop only the requirement that sections stay perpendicular to the axis.

From history

A. Duleau, a graduate of the polytechnic school, published in 1820 an extensive series of tests of iron and iron structures (TIMO, PDF p. 89). His bending tests of rectangular beams agreed very well with theory (TIMO, PDF p. 89). For built-up beams of two parts joined by bolts, the measured deflections were always somewhat larger than the calculated ones, and the difference grew with the distance \(h_1\) between the parts (TIMO, PDF p. 90). In his calculation Duleau did not account for the effect of the shear force on the deflection (TIMO, PDF p. 90). This effect grows with the distance \(h_1\) while the total deflection decreases, so the part of the deflection due to shear becomes relatively more and more important (TIMO, PDF p. 90), as the share of the shear part in figure 5.219 does when the beam gets shorter.

Poncelet (1788 to 1867) lectured at the Sorbonne and was the first to introduce the effect of the shear force into the formulas for the deflection of beams (TIMO, PDF p. 95, 97). For a rectangular cantilever of length \(l\), width \(b\) and depth \(h\) under a uniform load he wrote a formula for the largest deflection and observed that the second term in the bracket, which stands for the effect of the shear force, is of practical importance only in comparatively short beams (TIMO, PDF p. 97). Rankine and Grashof wrote this correction in an elementary form. Horace Lamb, with a plane calculation of an infinite beam of narrow rectangular cross section loaded at equal intervals by equal forces alternately up and down, showed two things. The elementary Bernoulli theory of bending is accurate enough if the depth of the beam is small compared with its length. The correction for the shear force by Rankine and Grashof is somewhat exaggerated and must be reduced to about 0{,}75 of its value (TIMO, PDF p. 280).

For the vibration of shafts and beams, simple cases were known in the eighteenth century (TIMO, PDF p. 357). For beams whose cross-section dimensions are not very small compared with the length, and for higher modes of vibration, a more complete differential equation had to be derived, one that accounts for the effect of shear forces on the deflection.

Let us return to the question of the section: shear stresses deform the material, so the cross sections do not stay perpendicular to the axis. Poncelet's finding that the addition to the deflection matters only for short beams will become a number at the end of the section, the share of the shear part of the deflection at different \(L/h\).

5.13.1 A section not perpendicular to the axis

In the Euler-Bernoulli beam the section turns as much as the axis, because it stays perpendicular to it. Let us draw this for a small piece of the beam around the point \(P\) on the axis (figure 5.211, top). The axis of the beam tilts against the horizontal \(x\) by the angle \(v'\). The section, which before loading was vertical, turns by the same angle and stays perpendicular to the axis. What if it turns by another number? In the middle the section stays straight, but it turns by a number \(\theta(x)\) that we do not tie to the slope of the axis \(v'(x)\). So the perpendicular to the section does not lie on the axis. The point \(Q\) of the section at height \(y\) above the axis then moves in the direction \(x\) by \(u - y\,\theta\), where \(u\) is the displacement of the axis: the turn of the section carries it back by \(y\,\theta\). In the direction \(y\) it moves by the displacement of the axis \(v\). The number \(\theta\) is a ratio (displacement in the direction \(-x\) per unit height above the axis) and has no unit. For small displacements it is approximately the angle of rotation \(\varphi\) of the section in radians, measured counterclockwise: for a rigid rotation \(\theta = \sin\varphi\) and the relative difference \((\varphi - \sin\varphi)/\varphi\) is below \(\varphi^2/6\).

Definition 5.87 Timoshenko beam model (Timošenkov model nosilca)

The beam (definition 5.1) lies in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane. The functions \(u\), \(v\) and \(\theta\) are twice continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) and have real values. The displacement field of the point \((x, y)\) of a section is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = u(x) - y\,\theta(x) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = v(x) . \tag{5.81}\]

The number \(\theta(x)\) is the tilt of the section (nagib prereza), the number \(\gamma(x) = v'(x) - \theta(x)\) is the shear strain of the beam (strižna deformacija nosilca). The Euler-Bernoulli model is the special case \(\theta = v'\).

What does this mean for the strains? By equations (1.71)

\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} = u' - y\,\theta' , \qquad 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} = -\theta + v' = \gamma . \tag{5.82}\]

The second equation is the engineering shear strain of section 1.17.4, the change of the right angle between the axis and the section (figure 5.211). It does not depend on \(y\): the section stays a straight line, so every small piece of it turns by the same \(\theta\), and every horizontal fibre tilts by the same \(v'\), because all points of the section move in the direction \(y\) by the same \(v(x)\). The other components are zero, as in the Euler-Bernoulli beam. The model assumes that every fibre is under uniaxial stress, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (lemma 3.12), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). This is not consistent with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), but the model neglects the transverse strain (assumption (b) in section 5.3.2). The transverse strain left out in this way is below \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) at the clamp of the cantilever (section 5.3.4). It does not change the displacement of the axis \(v\), since it only stretches or shrinks the section along its height about the axis. By equation (3.22) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\).

The internal forces are, by definition, integrals of the stress over the section (section 5.2). The axis \(x\) passes through the centroids of the sections, hence \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\) (lemma 5.22), and \(I = \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Lemma 5.88 Axial force and bending moment in the Timoshenko model (osna sila in upogibni moment v Timošenkovem modelu)

In the Timoshenko model (definition 5.87) with constant \(E\), area \(A\) and moment of inertia \(I\) and with stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\),

\[N = E A\,u' , \qquad M = E I\,\theta' ,\]

where \(N = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) and \(M = -\int y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A\).

Proof

We insert the stress from equation (5.82) and integrate over the section.

  1. By equation (5.82), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\,(u' - y\,\theta')\). The functions \(u'\) and \(\theta'\) do not depend on \(y\), so we take them out of the integral over the section.
  2. \(N = E\,u' \int \mathrm{d}A - E\,\theta' \int y\,\mathrm{d}A = E A\,u'\), because \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The forces of the upper and the lower piece of the linear part are therefore equal and opposite and only the uniform part gives the force \(N\).
  3. \(M = -E\,u' \int y\,\mathrm{d}A + E\,\theta' \int y^2\,\mathrm{d}A = E I\,\theta'\). The first term is zero, because the uniform part has no moment about the centroid, only the linear part gives the moment \(M\). The height \(y\) appears twice, because the stress of the strip at height \(y\) is proportional to \(y\) and its lever arm is \(y\) again (the strips of the section and their contributions in figure 5.70).

Source: The proof is not from a source. EP (2.5.25), (2.5.26), p. 77, with the axis \(z\) upward and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = -\theta\), their moment is \(M_\mathrm{EP} = -M\), converted to our signs.

Figure 5.212 shows steps 2 and 3 of the proof. The total stress is a straight line over the height. We split it: the total is the sum of the uniform part \(E\,u'\) and the linear part \(-E\,y\,\theta'\), which is zero at the centroid. The forces of the upper and the lower piece of the linear part are equal and opposite, because \(\int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The linear part therefore gives no axial force, only a couple with the moment \(M = E I\,\theta'\). The uniform part gives the force \(N = E A\,u'\) at the centroid and zero moment. Each part carries exactly one internal force.

Let us compare with the Euler-Bernoulli model. There \(M = E I\,v''\), here \(M = E I\,\theta'\), the same equation in which the tilt of the section \(\theta\) replaces the slope of the axis. There are now two unknowns, \(v\) and \(\theta\).

The Timoshenko section stays plane, although the true section warps because of the shear stresses. Take the end section of the cantilever (figure 5.211, bottom). The model turns it as a straight line, while the true section is slightly curved. In the plane elasticity solution for the cantilever (chapter 11, EP example 2.5.1, pp. 79 to 80) the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) contains a term \((2 + \nu)\,F y^3/(6EI)\), which is not linear in \(y\). At \(y = 20\) mm at the edge of the section it is 0,073 µm, while the turn of the section at the free end moves the same edge by 239 µm. The warping of the section is thus 0,03 % of this displacement and the plane section is a good assumption. The ratio of the two displacements is

\[\frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,(h/2)^3/(6EI)}{\big(F L^2/(2EI)\big)\,(h/2)} = \frac{(2 + \nu)\,h^2}{12\,L^2} ,\]

so \(3{,}1 \cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(L/h = 25\), but already 4,8 % for a short beam with \(L/h = 2\), because it grows with the square of \(h/L\). The larger error is elsewhere: the tilt of the section is not the slope of the axis.

5.13.2 Shear correction factor

What is the shear force in this model? Let us draw the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) horizontally and the height of the section \(h\) vertically (figure 5.213). By the definition of the shear force (definition 5.7), \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), without a sign. If we insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\), equal over the whole section, then \(V = G A\,\gamma\). This is a consequence of the model, but it is not true. On the upper and lower faces of the beam there is no tangential load, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\) there, and by the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41) also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). A uniform stress \(G\,\gamma\) cannot satisfy this boundary condition. We found the true distribution in section 5.6: by theorem 5.39

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(y) = \frac{V\,S(y)}{I\,b(y)} .\]

For a rectangle this is a parabola, zero at the edges and largest on the axis, \(3V/(2A)\). The two shapes have the same area, since the area of a shape times the width \(b\) is exactly the shear force \(V\). So the parabola, which is zero at the edges, must be higher than the straight line in the middle. For our cantilever \(V = -500\) N, the stress on the axis is \(-0{,}9375\) MPa, the average \(V/A = -0{,}625\) MPa, so 1,5 times less.

What shall we do about the error? We are not interested here in the stresses themselves but in how much the beam deforms in shear and how much this adds to the deflection. The answer comes from the energy that the shear stores. The idea is the same as in Castigliano's second theorem (theorem 5.84), where the displacement under a force is the derivative of the energy with respect to that force: a model that has the same energy as the true beam at the same force gives the same displacement. So we keep the shear strain \(\gamma\) constant over the section, as the straight line, and relate the force \(V\) to it by a coefficient \(k\), chosen so that the energy of shear strain equals the energy of the true stresses, the parabola. In the model the energy per unit length is \(\tfrac12 V\gamma = V^2/(2kGA)\). For the true distribution the energy density is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\) (definition 3.59, where \(\tfrac12(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}) = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2/(2G)\)), and its integral over the section is, by lemma 5.81 (equation (5.76)), \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\,V^2/(2GA)\). Equating the two energies gives \(1/k = f_{\mathrm{s}}\).

Definition 5.89 Shear correction factor (strižni korekcijski faktor)

A section has area \(A\), moment of inertia \(I\), width \(b(y)\) at height \(y\) and first moment of area \(S(y)\) of the part above the height \(y\) (definition 5.38). The shear correction factor of the section is the number

\[k = \frac{1}{f_{\mathrm{s}}} , \qquad f_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{A}{I^2}\int \Big(\frac{S}{b}\Big)^2 \mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.83}\]

without a unit, depending only on the shape of the section. The product \(k\,G\,A\) is the shear stiffness of the section (strižna togost prereza), in newtons. In the lectures \(\kappa = f_{\mathrm{s}} = 1/k\): for the rectangle \(\kappa = 6/5\), our \(k = 5/6\) (section 5.12.3).

Lemma 5.90 The shear correction factor is less than one (strižni korekcijski faktor je manjši od ena)

Let a section of area \(A\) with the axis \(x\) through the centroid occupy the heights from \(-c_1\) to \(c_2\), with width \(b(y) > 0\), piecewise continuous on \([-c_1, c_2]\), and moment of inertia \(I\), and let \(V \neq 0\). For the shear stress \(\tau = V S/(I b)\) from theorem 5.39, \(f_{\mathrm{s}} > 1\), hence \(0 < k < 1\). At the upper edge, namely, \(S(c_2) = 0\), so there \(\tau = 0 \ne V/A\) and \(\tau\) is not constant on any section.

Proof

We show that \(\frac{V^2}{A}(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1)\) is the integral of the square of the deviation of the parabola from the straight line. First we need \(\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A\).

  1. Let the section occupy the heights from \(-c_1\) to \(c_2\). By theorem 4.9 the integral over the section is an integral over \(y\) with the factor \(b(y)\), the width of the section at height \(y\). Let \(S(y) = \int_{\mathrm{y}}^{c_2} s\,b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), the first moment of area of the part above the height \(y\). Then \(S' = -y\,b\), \(S(c_2) = 0\) and \(S(-c_1) = \int y\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\).
  2. Integration by parts: \(\int_{-\mathrm{c}_1}^{c_2} S\,\mathrm{d}y = [y\,S]_{-\mathrm{c}_1}^{c_2} + \int y^2\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = 0 + I\), because the boundary terms are zero by step 1.
  3. Therefore \(\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A = \frac{V}{I}\int S\,\mathrm{d}y = V\), the area under the parabola times the width. By the definition of \(f_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\int \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}A = V^2 f_{\mathrm{s}}/A\).
  4. We expand the square into three terms and insert step 3: \(\int (\tau - V/A)^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}A - 2\,\frac{V}{A}\int \tau\,\mathrm{d}A + \frac{V^2}{A^2}\,A = \frac{V^2}{A}\,f_{\mathrm{s}} - 2\,\frac{V^2}{A} + \frac{V^2}{A}\). The sum is \(\frac{V^2}{A}\,(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1)\).
  5. The square is nowhere negative, so \(f_{\mathrm{s}} \ge 1\). The strict inequality comes from the upper edge. There \(S(c_2) = 0\) and \(b(c_2) > 0\), so \(\tau\) tends to 0 as \(y\) tends to \(c_2\). On some band below the edge we therefore have \((\tau - V/A)^2 > V^2/(4A^2)\) and the integral of the square is already positive on this band. Hence \(f_{\mathrm{s}} > 1\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. ONATE2 (PDF p. 72) states only \(k_{\mathrm{z}} \le 1\) without proof.

Figure 5.214 shows steps 4 and 5 for a rectangle. On the left, above the zero line, are the three terms of the expanded square in units of \(V^2/A\): \(\int \tau^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 1{,}2\), the mixed term \(-2\) by step 3 and the average term \(+1\). What remains is \(0{,}2 = f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1\). On the right is the square of the deviation \((\tau - V/A)^2\) over the height, which is nowhere negative. On the band below each edge, from 16,3 mm to 20 mm, it is larger than \(0{,}25\,(V/A)^2\), so the integral cannot be zero.

Now we understand the number \(6/5\) for the rectangle (lemma 5.81). For it \(f_{\mathrm{s}} - 1 = 0{,}2\), by step 4 of lemma 5.90 the energy of the deviation of the parabola from the average. On the axis the parabola is \(3V/(2A) - V/A = 0{,}5\,V/A\) above the average, and at the edge it is \(V/A\) below it. This deviation contributes 20 % of the energy of the average stress \(V^2/(2GA)\). Hence \(k = 5/6\). For steel \(G A = 6{,}031 \cdot 10^7\) N and the shear stiffness is \(k G A = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N.

The factor \(5/6\) is not general. In another section the shear travels differently. For the I-section of section 5.4 (height 200 mm, flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm, web \(180 \times 6\) mm), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 2{,}610\) and \(k = 0{,}383\), close to the share of the web area, \(180 \cdot 6/3080 = 0{,}351\). The shear is carried almost only by the web (section 5.6.2, figure 5.101), so the web area determines the shear stiffness. For a solid circle our definition gives \(S/b = (R^2 - y^2)/3\), \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 10/9\) and \(k = 9/10\) (SymPy). ONATE2 gives \(k = 6/7\) for a circle (figure 2.3, PDF p. 73), because its values come from other methods and not from Jourawski. For a thin-walled round tube it gives \(k = 0{,}5\). Our definition gives \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 3/2\) and \(k = 2/3\) for a thin tube, because it counts only the vertical component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). In a thin wall the shear runs along the wall, almost horizontally (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\)) at the top and the bottom, and with the energy of both components \(f_{\mathrm{s}} = 2\) and \(k = 0{,}5\) as in ONATE2.

The value of \(k\) depends on what we compare. The energy comparison of the parabola and the straight line, as we made it, gives \(k = 5/6\) for a rectangle (ONATE2 §2.2.3.1, PDF p. 72, EP (3.2.43), p. 118). In example 2.5.1 (p. 80) EP compares the deflection of the cantilever with the plane elasticity solution, fixed so that at the centre of the clamped section the displacements and the rotation are zero, and gets \(K_{\mathrm{s}} = 4/3\). This is not an energy factor, since by lemma 5.90 \(k\) does not exceed 1. The shear part of the cantilever deflection is then \(3FL/(4GA) = 0{,}0062\) mm, and for \(k = 5/6\) it is \(FL/(kGA) = 0{,}0099\) mm. The difference of 0,0037 mm is 0,05 % of the deflection 7,97 mm. For a slender beam it does not matter, but it shows that the correction factor depends on the clamping.

The shear force in the model is now \(V = k\,G\,A\,\gamma\). The sign follows from the definition in section 5.2 and from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\,\gamma\): the shear force has the same sign as the shear strain, because \(V = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) without a minus. All three relations are

\[N = E A\,u' , \qquad M = E I\,\theta' , \qquad V = k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta) . \tag{5.84}\]

EP (2.5.27), p. 77, with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}} = -\theta\) and the axis \(z\) upward, has the same formula, its shear force equals ours. ONATE2 (2.2) and (2.4a), PDF pp. 69 to 70, with the axis \(z\) upward has the same signs as we do: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = -z\,\theta'\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = w' - \theta\), \(M = -\int z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), \(Q = \int \tau_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A\), so \(M\) and \(Q\) are our \(M\) and \(V\).

5.13.3 Equations of the Timoshenko beam

The connections between forces and displacements are (5.84). The equilibrium of a slice, however, we derived in section 5.2 only from the definitions of the forces as integrals of stress, without kinematics, and it therefore holds here too: by corollary 5.11, \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\). The minus in \(V = -M'\) has a reason. Take the slice between \(x\) and \(x + h\) of figure 5.38 (there its length is labelled \(\Delta x\)). By lemma 5.10, in the plane case without distributed couples, \(M(x + h) - M(x) = -\int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x+h} V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). Since \(V\) is continuous, for small \(h\) the integral is close to \(V(x)\,h\). This is the picture: the force \(V\) on the right section acts on the lever arm \(h\) about the centroid of the left section and gives the moment \(V\,h\), which the difference of the moments of the left and the right section balances. Dividing by \(h\) and letting \(h \to 0\) gives \(M' = -V\), which corollary 5.11 proves. We insert (5.84).

Theorem 5.91 Timoshenko beam equations (enačbe Timošenkovega nosilca)

A beam of length \(L\) has constant \(E\), \(G\), \(A\), \(I\), \(k\) and a distributed transverse load \(q\), continuous on \([0, L]\) (\(q\) positive upward), without distributed couples (\(m = 0\)) and without point forces and couples in the interior. The supports are arbitrary. The displacement field is the Timoshenko one (definition 5.87), and \(V\) and \(M\) follow equations (5.84). At an end \(\xi \in \{0, L\}\) let a force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) across the axis and a couple with moment \(C^\xi\) act, either may be zero, and let \(n_{\mathrm{L}} = 1\), \(n_0 = -1\) (as in section 5.10). At each end there are the pairs \((v, V)\) and \((\theta, M)\), as for the beam in section 5.7.2: in each pair the support prescribes exactly one member (figure 5.116: the clamp prescribes both displacements, the free end both forces, and for the Timoshenko beam \(\theta\) replaces \(v'\)).

  1. Equilibrium of the slice, \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\), holds exactly when
    \[\big(k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta)\big)' = -q , \qquad \big(E I\,\theta'\big)' + k\,G\,A\,(v' - \theta) = 0 . \tag{5.85}\]
  2. At each end \(\xi\): if the support does not prescribe \(v(\xi)\) (as at the free end), then \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\), and if it does not prescribe \(\theta(\xi)\), then \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\), that is
    \[n_\xi\,k\,G\,A\,\big(v'(\xi) - \theta(\xi)\big) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi , \qquad n_\xi\,E I\,\theta'(\xi) = C^\xi .\]
  3. The equation
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_0^L \big(M\,\delta\theta' + V\,(\delta v' - \delta\theta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x &= \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi \in \{0, \mathrm{L}\}} \Big(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi) + C^\xi\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{5.86}\]
    holds for every pair of virtual displacements \(\delta v\) and \(\delta\theta\), continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\), which at each end are zero where the support prescribes \(v\) or \(\theta\) respectively (as at the clamp), exactly when (5.85) and the conditions of item 2 at both ends hold.
Proof

Items 1 and 2 follow by substitution, item 3 by integration by parts and the fundamental lemma.

Items 1 and 2.

  1. Equilibrium of the slice is \(V' = -q\) and \(M' + V = 0\) (corollary 5.11). We insert \(V = k G A\,(v' - \theta)\) and \(M = E I\,\theta'\) from equations (5.84). The first relation gives the first equation (5.85), the second the second. Since the substitutions are two-sided, the converse holds too.
  2. By section 5.7.2, at the end \(\xi\) where the corresponding displacement is not prescribed, \(n_\xi\,V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) and \(n_\xi\,M(\xi) = C^\xi\). Such an end is the free end, at a clamp both displacements are prescribed and there is no condition on the forces. Into these conditions we insert (5.84).

Item 3.

  1. Let \(\delta v\), \(\delta\theta\) be a pair from item 3. The functions \(V\) and \(M\) have continuous derivatives, because \(v\) and \(\theta\) are twice continuously differentiable. Integration by parts gives, with \(f(L) - f(0) = \sum_\xi n_\xi f(\xi)\), \(\int_0^L M\,\delta\theta' = \sum_\xi n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi) - \int_0^L M'\,\delta\theta\) and \(\int_0^L V\,(\delta v' - \delta\theta) = \sum_\xi n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) - \int_0^L V'\,\delta v - \int_0^L V\,\delta\theta\).
  2. The left side of equation (5.86) is thus
    \[-\int_0^L V'\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L (M' + V)\,\delta\theta\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\xi} \Big(n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) + n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) .\]
  3. If (5.85) and the conditions of item 2 hold, then \(-V' = q\) and \(M' + V = 0\). At each end \(n_\xi V(\xi)\,\delta v(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\,\delta v(\xi)\), in both cases. Where \(v\) is not prescribed, \(n_\xi V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) by item 2. Where \(v\) is prescribed, \(\delta v(\xi) = 0\) and both sides are zero. Likewise \(n_\xi M(\xi)\,\delta\theta(\xi) = C^\xi\,\delta\theta(\xi)\). The expression of step 4 is therefore equal to the right side of (5.86).
  4. Conversely, let (5.86) hold for every such pair. By step 4 we then have
    \[\begin{aligned} &\int_0^L (-V' - q)\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L (M' + V)\,\delta\theta\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &\quad + \sum_{\xi} \Big(\big(n_\xi V(\xi) - F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\big)\,\delta v(\xi) + \big(n_\xi M(\xi) - C^\xi\big)\,\delta\theta(\xi)\Big) = 0 . \end{aligned}\]
  5. Take \(\delta\theta = 0\) and \(\delta v = \psi\) with \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\). The pair is admissible for any supports, because it is zero at both ends. The function \(-V' - q\) is continuous, hence by lemma 4.77 it is zero. With \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta\theta = \psi\) likewise \(M' + V = 0\).
  6. The integrals in step 6 are now zero. Let \(v(\xi)\) not be prescribed (as at the free end) and let \(\xi'\) be the other end. Take \(\delta\theta = 0\) and \(\delta v(x) = (x - \xi')/(\xi - \xi')\), which is 1 at \(\xi\) and 0 at \(\xi'\). The pair is admissible for any supports, because it is zero at \(\xi'\) and \(v\) is not prescribed at \(\xi\) (at the clamp it would be, so \(\xi\) is the other end there). Step 6 gives \(n_\xi V(\xi) = F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\). Likewise with \(\delta v = 0\) and \(\delta\theta(x) = (x - \xi')/(\xi - \xi')\), when \(\theta(\xi)\) is not prescribed, we get \(n_\xi M(\xi) = C^\xi\).

Source: EP (2.5.33), (2.5.34), p. 78, EP example 3.5.4, pp. 149 to 150 (natural boundary conditions), ONATE2 (2.8), (2.9), PDF pp. 73 to 74 (virtual work). The signs are converted to ours, not copied from the sources. The proof is not from a source.

Figure 5.215 shows both cases of step 5. Where \(v\) is prescribed, the admissible virtual displacement must be zero there, \(\delta v(\xi) = 0\), so both sides of the equality are zero whatever \(V(\xi)\) is. Where \(v\) is not prescribed, the virtual displacement of the end is arbitrary, and the force \(F_{\mathrm{y}}^\xi\) at the end is by item 2 equal to the internal force \(V(\xi)\).

What have we obtained? The same equation by two routes. The route through slice equilibrium is short and says what \(V\) and \(M\) are. The route through virtual work shows what the Euler-Bernoulli model hides: the internal virtual work has, besides the term \(M\,\delta\theta'\), the term \(V\,\delta\gamma\) with \(\delta\gamma = \delta v' - \delta\theta\), so the shear force does internal work on the change of the angle \(\gamma\) between the axis and the normal to the section (section 5.10). Equation (5.86) contains only first derivatives \(\delta v'\) and \(\delta\theta'\), so for the functions continuous functions with a piecewise continuous derivative suffice, the derivative may jump at nodes (ONATE2, PDF p. 74). This will matter in the finite element computation (chapter 12).

How does this agree with the Euler-Bernoulli equation? The second equation (5.85) gives \(V = -E I\,\theta''\), the first \(V' = -q\), hence \(E I\,\theta''' = q\). From the first it also follows that \(v'' = \theta' - q/(kGA)\). For a twice differentiable \(q\) we therefore have \(E I\,v'''' = q - (E I/(kGA))\,q''\). For \(q'' = 0\), for example a constant load, this is the Euler-Bernoulli equation \(E I\,v'''' = q\), otherwise it differs by a term that tends to zero as \(kGA \to \infty\), which is the shear angle tending to zero in rigid shear, so the normal coincides with the axis. The end conditions for \(\theta\) still differ. The Timoshenko equations are two equations of second order, together of order four, but for two functions and with the boundary pairs \((v, V)\) and \((\theta, M)\).

Statically determinate beam. When \(V\) and \(M\) are already known from equilibrium, the equations need not be solved. From \(M = E I\,\theta'\) we have \(\theta' = M/(EI)\), and from \(V = kGA\,(v' - \theta)\) we have \(v' = \theta + V/(kGA)\): the slope of the axis is the sum of the rotation of the section and the shear angle. Two successive integrals, no new mathematics, and the clamped end or the supports determine the constants.

Corollary 5.92 Unit load method with the shear term (enotska sila s strižnim členom)

Let the beam have the properties of theorem 5.91, except that point forces may also act in the interior, and let \(M\), \(V\), \(v\), \(\theta\) be the true ones under the given load. Let the supports not yield: at each end every prescribed \(v\) or \(\theta\) is zero under the true load. Let \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) be a point of the beam, an end or an interior point, at which \(v\) is not prescribed. Let \(m(x)\) and \(v_1(x)\) be the moment and the shear force, divided by \(1\) N, of an imagined system consisting of a force \(1\) N in the direction \(y\) at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) and the reactions of the supports (with the same signs as for the true forces). The true \(v\) and \(\theta\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and twice continuously differentiable on each piece between the points of true point forces (there \(V\) jumps, \(v\) and \(\theta\) stay continuous). Then the deflection \(\Delta = v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) equals

\[\Delta = \int_0^L \Big( \frac{m\,M}{EI} + \frac{v_1\,V}{kGA} \Big)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{5.87}\]
Proof

We must show that the computation of steps 3 and 4 of the proof of theorem 5.91, in which the imagined system replaces the forces and the true displacements the virtual ones, gives \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), when written on the pieces \([0, x_{\mathrm{P}}]\) and \([x_{\mathrm{P}}, L]\) (in figure 5.217 left and right of the force), divided further at the points of true point forces (at an end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) there is only one piece).

  1. On each piece the imagined system has no load along the length, so there \(v_1' = 0\) and \(m' = -v_1\) (corollary 5.11). On a piece \(v_1\) is constant, \(m\) is linear and both are continuously differentiable on the closed piece.
  2. At an interior point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) a point force \(1\) N acts without a couple. By lemma 5.12, \(v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^+) - v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^-) = -1\) and \(m\) is continuous at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\).
  3. On each piece \([a, b]\), integration by parts as in step 3 of the proof (\(m\), \(v_1\) for \(M\), \(V\), true \(v\), \(\theta\)) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(m\,\theta' + v_1\,(v' - \theta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\) equal to \(\big[m\,\theta + v_1\,v\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (m' + v_1)\,\theta\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b v_1'\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\), the last two integrals being zero by step 1.
  4. We add the pieces. At a point of a true force \(v\), \(\theta\), \(m\) and \(v_1\) are continuous, so all terms cancel there. At an interior point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), \(v\), \(\theta\) and \(m\) are continuous, so the terms with \(m\,\theta\) cancel and what remains is \(\big(v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^-) - v_1(x_{\mathrm{P}}^+)\big)\,v(x_{\mathrm{P}}) = v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) by step 2: the jump of \(v_1\) times the deflection under the force. This is the term of the force \(1\) N times the displacement, as in corollary 5.65.
  5. At each end \(\xi\) we split the term \(n_\xi\big(m(\xi)\,\theta(\xi) + v_1(\xi)\,v(\xi)\big)\) into two products. The product \(v_1 v\) is zero where \(v\) is prescribed (at the supports), the product \(m\,\theta\) where \(\theta\) is prescribed, because the true displacement is zero. Elsewhere the imagined system has no load at the end, except the force \(1\) N when \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is that end. So \(n_\xi v_1(\xi) = 1\) at the end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), otherwise \(n_\xi v_1(\xi) = 0\), and \(n_\xi m(\xi) = 0\) wherever \(\theta\) is not prescribed. At the end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) the sum over the ends is thus \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\), at an interior point it is zero, and together with step 4 the total is \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\) in both cases.
  6. For the true beam \(\theta' = M/(EI)\) and \(v' - \theta = V/(kGA)\) (equations (5.84)). We insert into the left side of step 3, summed over both pieces.

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same shear term \(F L/(K_{\mathrm{s}}\,GA)\) for the cantilever is in EP example 4.2.3 (pp. 177-178).

Figure 5.216 shows the four kinds of end from step 5. At a clamp both true displacements are zero. At a pin the true \(v\) is zero, and the imagined system has no couple at a pin, so \(m = 0\). At a free end without the imagined force \(v_1\) and \(m\) are zero. Only at the free end \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) does \(v_1 = 1\) remain, and the term is the true deflection \(v(x_{\mathrm{P}})\).

The formula thus holds for an interior point as well. We test it on the simply supported beam of figure 5.217.

On the left is the true beam. The axis before loading is dashed, \(L = 2\) m long, with the supports at the ends. At mid-span a force \(F = 1\) kN acts downward, so the supports answer with reactions of \(500\) N upward. The bar is the same, \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(E I = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(k = 5/6\), \(G = 75{,}38\) GPa, \(kGA = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N. The axis bends and the mid-span sinks by \(\Delta\), which we are looking for. Below the beam are the true internal forces: \(V = -500\) N on the left half and \(V = +500\) N on the right, and the moment is \(M = 500\,x\) N m on the left and \(M = 500\,(L - x)\) N m on the right, with the peak \(500\) N m under the force.

On the right is the same beam with an imagined force of \(1\) N upward at \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 1\) m. The supports balance it with reactions of \(1/2\) N downward. So \(v_1 = +1/2\) on the left and \(v_1 = -1/2\) on the right, the jump of \(v_1\) under the force is \(-1\) as in step 2 of the proof, and the moment is \(m = -x/2\) on the left and \(m = -(L - x)/2\) on the right. By symmetry the left and the right half contribute equally, so the mid-span deflection is twice the left part:

\[\Delta = 2\int_0^1 \frac{(-x/2)\,(500\,x)}{EI}\,\mathrm{d}x + 2\int_0^1 \frac{(1/2)\,(-500)}{kGA}\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac{F L^3}{48\,EI} - \frac{F L}{4\,kGA} .\]

The first integral multiplies the triangle \(m\) by the triangle \(M\), the second the rectangle \(v_1\) by the rectangle \(V\). The bending part is \(F L^3/(48 EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, the shear part \(F L/(4 kGA) = 500\ \text{N} \cdot 1\ \text{m}/(5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\ \text{N}) = 0{,}00995\) mm, together \(\Delta = -7{,}982\) mm, downward, as it must be for a downward force. The shear part is \(0{,}125\) % of the bending part. The ratio is \(12\,EI/(kGA\,L^2)\), so \(3\,EI/(kGA \cdot 1\ \text{m}^2)\) at \(L = 2\) m. SymPy confirms the numbers.

Where does the assumption that the supports do not yield enter, and what does it cost? In step 5 of the proof: there the term \(n_\xi\,v_1(\xi)\,v(\xi)\) drops out because the true deflection at a support is zero. The assumption holds when the ground is much stiffer than the beam, for example a steel bar resting on a wall. If a support settles (a footing in soft soil), the term stays. The number \(n_\xi\,v_1(\xi)\) is the reaction of the imagined system at that support, divided by \(1\) N and positive upward, here \(-1/2\). If the right support settles by \(1\) mm, so \(v(L) = -1\) mm, then \(\Delta = \int_0^L (\dots)\,\mathrm{d}x - (-\tfrac12)\cdot(-1\ \text{mm})\), and the mid-span sinks by a further \(0{,}5\) mm. This is as expected: the beam turns as a rigid body about the left support and the mid-span moves half as far as the right end.

Without the shear term the formula is theorem 5.79, for \(kGA \to \infty\).

5.13.4 Cantilever by Timoshenko

Let us test this on the cantilever of section 5.1.3: a flat bar \(b = 20\) mm, \(h = 40\) mm, \(L = 1\) m, clamped at \(x = 0\), a force \(F = 500\) N downward at the end. We know \(V = -500\) N everywhere and \(M(x) = -F\,(L - x)\). Data: \(E I = 20\,906{,}7\) N m², \(kGA = 5{,}026 \cdot 10^7\) N. Conditions by item 2 of the theorem: at \(x = 0\) both \(v\) and \(\theta\) are prescribed, \(v(0) = \theta(0) = 0\), and at the free end \(x = L\) we have \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N and \(M(L) = 0\).

Let us draw what we expect. The axis before loading is horizontal, \(L = 1\) m long, with the clamp on the left and the force \(F = 500\) N pressing on the right end. If the section were rigid in shear, the axis would bend into the thin line, which we know from section 5.9.2. Shear tilts every small piece of the axis a little more, so the axis after loading lies lower. The end section turns by \(\theta\), while the axis tilts by \(v'\), which is larger. We now compute all three.

  1. \(\theta' = M/(EI) = -F\,(L - x)/(EI)\) and \(\theta(0) = 0\), hence \(\theta(x) = -F\,(L x - x^2/2)/(EI)\). At the end \(\theta(L) = -F L^2/(2EI) = -0{,}011958\), the section turns clockwise.
  2. \(v' = \theta + V/(kGA) = \theta - F/(kGA)\). The shear strain is constant, \(\gamma = -F/(kGA) = -9{,}949 \cdot 10^{-6}\), because \(V\) is constant. Every small piece of the axis is therefore tilted by the same small angle more than under bending alone.
  3. From \(v(0) = 0\) it follows that \(v(x) = -F\,(L x^2/2 - x^3/6)/(EI) - F\,x/(kGA)\) and at the end \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI) - F L/(kGA)\). The first term is the drop of the thin line, the second the drop from it to the orange axis. The second term is the straight line \(-F\,x/(kGA)\): the constant tilt \(\gamma\) over the length \(x\).

SymPy confirms that this solution satisfies both equations (5.85): \(\gamma\) is constant, so \((kGA\,\gamma)' = 0 = -q\), and \((EI\,\theta')' = M' = F\), which is \(-V\). The result agrees with EP, p. 80 (cantilever), and with EP example 4.2.3, pp. 177 to 178.

The bending part is \(F L^3/(3EI) = 7{,}972\) mm, the shear part \(F L/(kGA) = 0{,}00995\) mm, together \(7{,}982\) mm. The shear part is 0,125 % of the bending part. The slope of the axis at the end is \(v'(L) = \theta(L) + \gamma = -0{,}011968\), the tilt of the section \(-0{,}011958\). The difference is \(\gamma\) and shows only in the fourth significant digit.

The same shear part is given by corollary 5.92. An imagined force \(1\) N upward at the end gives \(m = L - x\) and \(v_1 = 1\), the true ones are \(M = -F\,(L - x)\) and \(V = -F\), so \(\Delta = -F L^3/(3EI) - F L/(kGA) = -7{,}972 - 0{,}00995 = -7{,}982\) mm, the same two parts as above.

Why 0,125 % and when does it grow? The ratio of the shear and the bending part is

\[\frac{F L/(kGA)}{F L^3/(3EI)} = \frac{3 E I}{k\,G\,A\,L^2} = \frac{3 E\,b\,h^3/12}{k\,G\,b\,h\,L^2} = \frac{E}{4 k G}\,\frac{h^2}{L^2} = \frac{1 + \nu}{2k}\,\frac{h^2}{L^2} ,\]

because \(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\) (equation (3.22)). For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(k = 5/6\), \((1 + \nu)/(2k) = 0{,}78\). The cause is how the stiffnesses that set the bending and the shear part grow with \(h\): the bending stiffness \(E I = E b h^3/12\) with the third power, the shear stiffness \(kGA = kGbh\) with the first. If we halve the height, the bending compliance grows 8 times, the shear compliance only 2 times, and their ratio is \(\tfrac14\) of the former. For the length it is the other way round: the bending compliance is proportional to \(L^3\), the shear one to \(L\), so a length shorter by a factor of 5 reduces the bending part 125 times and the shear part 5 times, and the ratio grows 25 times. The numbers for three lengths of the same bar (SymPy):

\(L\) \(L/h\) bending part shear part shear share
1 m 25 7,972 mm 0,00995 mm 0,125 %
0,2 m 5 0,06378 mm 0,00199 mm 3,12 %
80 mm 2 0,004082 mm 0,000796 mm 19,5 %

The share falls with the square of the ratio \(h/L\) (figures 5.219 and 5.220): for a bar five times shorter, the middle column, the share is 25 times larger, \(0{,}1248 \cdot 25 = 3{,}12\).

In the figure there are three columns for three lengths of the bar, labelled by the ratio \(L/h\). The height of each is the total deflection on the axis, scaled to the same height so that only the ratio of the parts is seen. It contains the blackwhite bending part and the blue shear part, and under the column is their share.

In the figure, on a double logarithmic scale, the horizontal axis is the ratio \(L/h\) and the vertical is the shear share of the deflection in percent. The solid line is the cantilever with an end force, the dashed one the simply supported beam with a uniform load, the horizontal lines mark 1 % and 5 %.

Simply supported beam with a force at the midspan. Why is the shear part \(0{,}00995\) mm of the beam of figure 5.217 the same as for the cantilever? The beam, the supports and the force are mirror images about the midspan. There is only one solution, so it is a mirror image too. The mirror image of a rotation \(\theta\) is \(-\theta\), so at the midspan \(\theta = -\theta\) must hold, that is \(\theta(L/2) = 0\): the midspan does not turn. A section that does not turn holds half of the beam as a clamp does, only that it has sunk with it by \(\Delta\). The left half is therefore a cantilever of length \(1\) m, clamped at the midspan, with a force of \(500\) N upward at its end, that is the cantilever of figure 5.218 turned upside down. The two integrals from the paragraph on the statically determinate beam give (SymPy) the same \(v(L/2) = -7{,}982\) mm as the unit load. The rotation of the section at the support, \(\theta(0) = -F L^2/(16EI) = -0{,}011958\), equals the rotation of the cantilever end for the same reason.

Uniform load. For \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m and \(L = 2\) m (the beam of figure 5.192) the shear part of the midspan deflection is \(q_0 L^2/(8kGA) = 0{,}00995\) mm, the bending part \(5 q_0 L^4/(384 E I) = 9{,}965\) mm, the ratio 0,0998 %. In general the ratio is \(8\,(1 + \nu)/(5k)\,(h/L)^2 = 2{,}496\,(h/L)^2\), the dashed line, so 2,5 % at \(L/h = 10\). The same number 0,00995 mm as for the cantilever is a coincidence: \(q_0 L^2/8\) and \(F L\) are both 500 N m.

How does this agree with plane elasticity? SADD (8.1.37), PDF p. 177, gives for a simply supported beam with a uniform load the midspan deflection \(\frac{5 q_0 L^4}{384 E I}\big[1 + \tfrac{12}{5}\big(\tfrac45 + \tfrac\nu2\big)\tfrac{h^2}{L^2}\big]\), where \(2c\) is the height and \(2l\) the length of the beam (\(c/l = h/L\)). For \(\nu = 0\) the correction is \(1{,}92\,(h/L)^2\). The Timoshenko model at \(\nu = 0\) and \(k = 5/6\) gives \(8/(5k) = 1{,}92\), exactly the same. For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the plane correction is \(2{,}28\,(h/L)^2\) and the Timoshenko one \(2{,}496\,(h/L)^2\). The difference lies in the term with \(\nu\): the Timoshenko model gets it only through the shear modulus \(G\) (\(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\)), while plane elasticity gets it also through the transverse strain, which the beam model neglects (assumption (b) in section 5.3.2). SADD (PDF p. 579) writes that the effect of shear on the deflection is important only for very short beams with a ratio of length to height below 10.

5.13.5 Which theory when

In the cases we computed, the shear share of the deflection at \(L/h = 10\) is between 0,78 % (cantilever with an end force) and 2,5 % (simply supported beam with a uniform load), at \(L/h = 20\) between 0,2 % and 0,62 %, at \(L/h = 5\) between 3,1 % and 10 %. For a beam clamped at both ends with a uniform load the ratio is \(48\,EI/(kGA\,L^2) = 8\,(1 + \nu)/k\,(h/L)^2 = 12{,}48\,(h/L)^2\) (SymPy, from the Timoshenko equations), so 12,5 % at \(L/h = 10\). The ratio is thus \(c\,(h/L)^2\), where \(c\) runs from 0,78 to 12,5 in the cases computed, depending on supports and load. In figure 5.221 these are three parallel lines: \(c\) only moves a line up or down, the slope \(-2\) stays. Rule for the cantilever and the simply supported beam: the Euler-Bernoulli model suffices when \(L/h\) is at least 10 and we want an accuracy of a few percent (SADD, PDF p. 579, gives the limit 10, and in figure 5.220 both lines lie below the 5 % line there). For clamping at both ends the share is below 3 % when \(12{,}48\,(h/L)^2 < 0{,}03\), that is \(L/h > \sqrt{12{,}48/0{,}03} = 20{,}4\). Below the limit 10 the shear share grows quickly, because it falls with the square of \(h/L\), and the Timoshenko model captures it. For a cantilever with \(L/h = 2\) the shear part is 19,5 % of the bending part (16,3 % of the total deflection), but for such a short beam it is itself uncertain: the way of clamping from the remark in section 5.13.2 changes it from 19,5 % to 12,2 % of the bending part, that is, by 7 percentage points of the bending part. For such a beam no beam theory is enough and we need plane elasticity (EP example 2.5.1, p. 80, where both beam models differ from it mainly because of the Poisson effect).

The price of the model: the Euler-Bernoulli model has one unknown \(v\) and an equation of fourth order, the Timoshenko model two unknowns, the deflection \(v\) with the slope of the axis \(v'\) and the rotation of the section \(\theta\), and two equations of second order. In the finite element computation (chapter 12) continuous functions with a piecewise continuous derivative suffice for the Timoshenko model. For slender beams, however, shear locking (strižno zaklepanje) can appear: the finite element solution is too stiff, because the shear term \(V\,\delta\gamma\) dominates (ONATE2 §2.4, PDF pp. 78 to 85). The cause and the remedy are described in chapter 12. In the vibration of beams the rotary inertia of the section (vztrajnost vrtenja prereza) joins the Timoshenko model (CK §13.4, PDF pp. 418 to 419), which chapter 13 treats.
