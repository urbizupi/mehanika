Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.6 Galerkin method and weighted residuals (Galerkinova metoda in uteženi ostanki)

The Ritz method of section 6.4 chooses the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) so that the total potential energy is smallest. For this it needs an energy. The weak form (šibka oblika) of section 6.1.2, however, is an equation that holds for every test function (preizkusna funkcija) and does not mention energy. Can we look for an approximation directly from it? And while we are at it: in how many ways can we demand that the error of an approximation be small, and how do these ways differ?

We look for the answer on the hanging bar that we know from chapter 4 and section 6.4.2. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m, cross-sectional area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and modulus of elasticity \(E = 196\) GPa is fixed at \(x = 0\), the axis \(x\) points downward, and the lower end is free. It is loaded by its own weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m per unit length, \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N. The true field is \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), the lower end moves by \(19{,}52\) µm and the middle by \(14{,}64\) µm. The trial field (poskusno polje) throughout this section is the cubic function

\[u_1(x) = c\,\psi(x) , \qquad \psi(x) = 3L^2 x - x^3 . \tag{6.27}\]

We chose it because it respects the fixing, \(\psi(0) = 0\), and because \(\psi'(L) = 3(L^2 - L^2) = 0\), so the field has zero axial force at the free end, as the true field has. The second derivative \(\psi'' = -6x\) is not zero, so we will also be able to try the ways that need a second derivative. And the true function is not among the fields \(c\,\psi\), because it is quadratic and \(\psi\) is cubic, so the ways will give different results. Here \(c\) is a number with the unit \(\text{m}^{-2}\), since \(\psi\) is in \(\text{m}^3\) and \(u_1\) in m.

6.6.1 Galerkin method (Galerkinova metoda)

The true solution \(u\) satisfies the weak form, \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\), for every test function \(w\) (definition 6.6). The trial field \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) cannot satisfy it for all \(w\), since it would otherwise be the true field. But we can satisfy it for \(n\) test functions, as many as there are unknown numbers. Which ones should they be? The simplest choice is to take the very functions from which the trial field is built. Where this idea comes from is told in the next paragraph.

From history

Around the turn from the nineteenth to the twentieth century, Rayleigh, Ritz and Galerkin were among the prominent contributors to approximate variational methods (EP, pp. 3 to 4). Galerkin published his work in 1915 (EP, pp. 701 and 705). The work of Rayleigh and Ritz, together with Galerkin's weighted-residual approach, formed a consistent framework in which a continuous problem with infinitely many degrees of freedom is replaced by a discrete one (GR, PDF p. 354). In their original form these methods were not flexible enough for systems of complex geometry with arbitrary boundary conditions and loading (GR, PDF p. 354). Galerkin's idea is to make the residual of the differential equation orthogonal to the functions from which the approximation is built (EP, p. 344). The name is often used also for an expression in which integration by parts has reduced the order of the derivatives, but this turns Galerkin's original idea into the Ritz method (EP, p. 345). The method in which the weights are the same functions as in the approximation is called more precisely after Bubnov and Galerkin (COOK, PDF p. 196).

Let us return to the weak form of the bar. The equation \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) must be true for every test function. Which test functions do we take once we restrict the trial field to the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.32)? Galerkin's answer is this.

Definition 6.42 Galerkin method (Galerkinova metoda)

Let the problem be written in the weak form \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) for every test function \(w\), with a bilinear form \(B\) and a linear form \(\ell\) (definition 6.6). Let \(\psi_0\) be an admissible field, \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) independent test functions and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) the set of all their linear combinations (definition 6.32). The Galerkin approximation is a function \(u_{\mathrm{n}} \in \psi_0 + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) for which

\[B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) = \ell(w) \quad \text{for every } w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}} . \tag{6.28}\]

The trial and the test functions are from the same family.

Source: EP (6.4.12), (6.4.13), p. 344, COOK §5.1, PDF pp. 193 to 196 ("a Galerkin statement incorporates differential equations in their weak form", PDF p. 193).

How many equations is this? The demand holds for each of infinitely many fields \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\), but the equations for \(w = \psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) already suffice, and they are linear in the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Lemma 6.43 Galerkin equations (Galerkinove enačbe)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) be as in definition 6.42. Then equation (6.28) holds for every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) exactly when \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\). These equations are the system \(K\,c = f\) with \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).

Proof

We must show that the difference \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) - \ell(w)\) for each \(w\) splits into the differences for the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. Every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(w = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}} \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(B\) is linear in the second argument and \(\ell\) is linear, \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) - \ell(w) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}}\,\big(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\big)\).
  2. If all terms in the brackets are zero, then by step 1 the sum is zero for every choice of the numbers \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the equation holds for every \(w\).
  3. If the equation holds for every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\), it also holds for \(w = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\), since \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\), the other \(d\) zero). These are the equations \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  4. Since \(B\) is linear in the first argument, \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). The equation \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\), row \(i\) of the system \(K\,c = f\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same step is in the proof of lemma 6.36, steps 2 and 3.

The matrix \(K\) and the right-hand side \(f\) are the same as in the Ritz method (definition 6.32). The only difference is that in the Galerkin method \(B\) need not be symmetric, and therefore the matrix \(K\) need not be either.

A bar with the cubic field. Let us write out the definition for the field (6.27). The essential condition is \(u(0) = 0\), so \(\psi_0 = 0\), \(n = 1\), \(\psi_1 = \psi\), and equation (6.28) is \(B(c\,\psi, \psi) = \ell(\psi)\) with \(B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\). The numbers \(B(\psi, \psi) = \tfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\) and \(\ell(\psi) = \tfrac54\,q\,L^4\) are the matrix and the right side of the Ritz equation of section 6.4.2, so the Galerkin equation \(\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\,c = \tfrac54\,q\,L^4\) is the same and gives \(c = \tfrac{25}{96}\,q/(EA\,L)\). The field has \(20{,}33\) µm at the end, \(4{,}2\ \%\) too much, and \(13{,}98\) µm in the middle, \(4{,}5\ \%\) too little, while the work of the weight is \(2{,}3\ \%\) smaller than the true one, as theorem 6.34 says (section 6.4.4). By the weak form for \(u\) and the Galerkin equation, \(B(u - u_1, \psi) = \ell(\psi) - B(u_1, \psi) = 0\) (lemma 6.36), so the error is orthogonal to \(\psi\) in the energy inner product.

Is this a coincidence? For a bar \(B\) is symmetric, and the two methods coincide for every symmetric \(B\).

Theorem 6.44 Galerkin and Ritz methods coincide (Galerkinova in Ritzova metoda sovpadata)

Let \(B\) be a symmetric bilinear form, \(B(v, w) = B(w, v)\) for all admissible \(v\), \(w\), let \(\ell\) be a linear form and \(\Pi(w) = \tfrac12\,B(w, w) - \ell(w)\) the total potential energy. Let \(\psi_0, \psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the functions of definition 6.32. Then the Galerkin equations \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\) are exactly the equations \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) of the Ritz method, that is, the same system \(K\,c = f\) with the same solution \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the energy with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals the Galerkin expression.

  1. Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By bilinearity and symmetry, \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_0) = B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{j}})\), we have \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) = B(\psi_0, \psi_0) + 2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  2. By linearity of \(\ell\), \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \ell(\psi_0) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\). The energy \(\Pi\) is therefore a polynomial of second order in \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. The term \(c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the derivative \(\delta_{\mathrm{ik}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} + c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{jk}}\) with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) (product rule). Hence the derivative of the double sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\), which by symmetry is \(2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  4. From steps 1 to 3, \(\dfrac{\partial \Pi}{\partial c_{\mathrm{k}}} = \tfrac12\,\big(2B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) + 2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\big) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\), where the derivative of \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\) with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\), was taken from step 2 and the last equality follows from bilinearity.
  5. By step 4 and lemma 6.43, \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{kj}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} - f_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(K_{\mathrm{kj}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\). The Ritz method takes the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) from \(K\,c = f\) (definition 6.32), that is, from the condition \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for every \(k\), as it must be at the lowest point (section 4.6.6). By step 4 the same condition is \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) for every \(k\), and by lemma 6.43 these are the Galerkin equations.

Source: EP p. 345 ("The Ritz and Galerkin methods yield the same set of algebraic equations for the following three cases"), COOK §5.1, PDF p. 196 (the same for the choice of the same family). The proof is not from a source.

We have shown that the Galerkin equations are the condition that the derivative of the energy with respect to each \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) is zero, written with the bilinear form and work instead of a derivative. For the bar with the cubic field this can be seen: \(\Pi(c) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA L^5 c^2 - \tfrac54\,qL^4 c\) has the derivative \(\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA L^5 c - \tfrac54\,qL^4\), which is the Galerkin equation above. The Ritz calculation in section 6.4.2 and the Galerkin calculation above have the same number, \(c = 25/96\ q/(EAL)\), and the energy \(\Pi = -381{,}87\) µJ.

Why then do we need a new name at all? Because the Galerkin method does not need an energy. The bilinear form \(B\) and the right-hand side \(\ell\) of the weak form are enough. In some problems, for example in fluid flows, we have only a differential equation and boundary conditions, and a functional similar to the potential energy cannot be written or has no clear physical meaning (COOK, PDF p. 193). Then we write the weak form from the equation itself, as COOK (PDF pp. 194 to 195) does for a bar, and as a nonstructural example it names steady heat conduction (COOK, PDF p. 194), to which chapter 14 is devoted. The Galerkin method works there, the Ritz method does not. Also most of what we proved for the Ritz method in section 6.5 remains. The proof of lemma 6.36 uses only the weak form for \(u\) (step 1) and the equations \(K\,c = f\) (steps 2 and 3), which by lemma 6.43 are the Galerkin equations, and it does not need symmetry, so it holds for every \(B\) and every Galerkin approximation. The steps of the proof of theorem 6.39 for the constant \(M/m\) use only this orthogonality and the estimates \(|B(v, w)| \le M \int |v'|\,|w'|\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(B(w, w) \ge m \int w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and they do not need symmetry either. Hence the same steps give Cea's estimate with the constant \(M/m\) also for an unsymmetric \(B\) that has these two estimates. The estimate with the constant \(\sqrt{M/m}\) and theorem 6.37, however, need symmetry, because the splitting \(B(e + d, e + d) = B(e, e) + 2B(e, d) + B(d, d)\) in the proof of theorem 6.37 combines two mixed terms into \(2B(e, d)\). We do not develop unsymmetric problems further, since the book does not need them in this chapter.

6.6.2 Weighted residuals (uteženi ostanki)

The Galerkin method is the weak form with a particular choice of test functions. But what if we take the differential equation directly, without integration by parts? We insert the trial field into the equilibrium equation of the bar, and how much the equation fails is told by the residual.

Definition 6.45 Residual of a trial field (ostanek poskusnega polja)

Let a bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) and axial load \(q\) be as in definition 6.1, and let \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a trial field for which \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\) is a differentiable function. The residual of the trial field is the function

\[R(x) = \big(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(x)\big)' + q(x) , \tag{6.29}\]

a number with the unit N/m at every point \(x \in [0, L]\). The residual is zero at every point exactly when the field satisfies the differential equation of equilibrium.

Source: EP (6.4.3), (6.4.5), pp. 338 and 340, COOK (5.1-2), PDF p. 194.

For the field (6.27) we compute the residual in three steps.

  1. The derivative is \(u_1' = 3c\,(L^2 - x^2)\).
  2. The axial force \(EA\,u_1' = 3c\,EA\,(L^2 - x^2)\) has the derivative \(-6c\,EA\,x\).
  3. The residual is \(R(x) = q - 6c\,EA\,x\), a straight line in \(x\) that equals the load \(q\) at \(x = 0\) and falls with a slope determined by \(c\).

The residual cannot be zero everywhere: that would require \(q = 6c\,EA\,x\) for every \(x\), which holds for no \(c\). We must demand something weaker. We choose \(n\) functions and demand that the residual be orthogonal to them, which means that the integral of the product is zero (inner product of functions, definition 6.16).

Definition 6.46 Weighted-residual method (metoda uteženih ostankov)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) be a trial field of a bar with the residual \(R\) (definition 6.45) that meets the essential conditions and the natural condition \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\). Let \(w_1, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\) be independent continuous functions on \([0, L]\), the weight functions (utežne funkcije), which may differ from the \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The weighted-residual method determines the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) from the \(n\) equations

\[\int_0^L R(x)\,w_{\mathrm{i}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 , \qquad i = 1, \dots, n . \tag{6.30}\]

If the trial field does not meet the natural condition, the residual at the boundary must be added to the equations.

Source: EP §6.4.2, (6.4.4) to (6.4.6), pp. 339 to 340 ("which, in general, are different from the approximation functions", p. 340), COOK §5.2, PDF pp. 196 to 197.

For a linear problem the equations (6.30) are linear in \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), just like the Galerkin ones. We wrote the method for a bar, for a general differential equation it is written by EP (6.4.4) to (6.4.6). Why must the trial field also meet the natural condition? In the weak form this condition is already in the equation, in the equation with the residual it is not (EP, pp. 340 to 341), so the field itself must ensure it. The field (6.27) meets it, since \(u_1'(L) = c\,\psi'(L) = 0\) and \(F = 0\).

The methods differ in the choice of the weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\) (EP (6.4.11), p. 341).

  1. Galerkin, \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The residual must be orthogonal to the trial functions themselves.
  2. Petrov-Galerkin, the weights are functions other than \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\), for example \(w_1 = x\). We separate the families of trial and test functions (EP, p. 270, example 6.4.1, pp. 342 to 344, COOK PDF p. 196). With the weight \(x\) we will obtain below the exact displacement of the end.
  3. Least squares (najmanjši kvadrati): we minimize \(I(c) = \int_0^L R^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The derivative \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}c_{\mathrm{i}}} = 2\int_0^L R\,\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) must be zero at the minimum, so the residual is orthogonal to \(\partial R/\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is the weighted-residual method with the weight \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial R/\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}\) (EP §6.4.4, (6.4.14) to (6.4.18), p. 349, COOK (5.2-4), (5.2-5), PDF p. 197). EP also adds to the functional a term with the boundary residual, which we omit because our field meets the natural condition (EP, pp. 349 to 350).
  4. Collocation (kolokacija): the residual is zero at chosen points, \(R(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) (COOK, PDF p. 197, EP §6.4.5, p. 356). The weight is concentrated at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\). EP (6.4.29) writes it with the Dirac delta, a function concentrated at a point, which we will not need.
  5. Subdomain (podobmočje): we divide the bar into parts \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) and demand that the integral of the residual over each part is zero, \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{i}}} R\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The weight is \(1\) on this part and \(0\) elsewhere (EP §6.4.6, (6.4.34) to (6.4.36), pp. 358 to 359, COOK PDF p. 197).

With the Galerkin choice the connection with the weak form appears. With integration by parts from chapter 3 we can turn the residual with the weight \(\psi\) into the weak form.

Lemma 6.47 Weighted residual and weak form (utežni ostanek in šibka oblika)

Let a bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) that is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) be fixed at \(x = 0\), loaded by a continuous axial load \(q\) and by a force \(F\) at the end \(x = L\). Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a field with a continuous second derivative, \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\), and \(\psi\) a continuously differentiable function with \(\psi(0) = 0\). Then

\[\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell(\psi) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi) , \tag{6.31}\]

where \(B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,w(L)\).

Proof

We must show that integration by parts turns the residual into the weak form.

  1. By the definition of the residual, \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}')'\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L q\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. The functions \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\) and \(\psi\) are continuously differentiable, so by corollary 3.41 \(\int_0^L (EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}')'\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi\big]_0^L - \int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi'\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. At \(x = 0\) we have \(\psi(0) = 0\), at \(x = L\) we have \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\), so the square bracket equals \(F\,\psi(L)\).
  4. From steps 1 to 3, \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = F\,\psi(L) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi) + \int_0^L q\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell(\psi) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi)\).

Source: COOK §5.1, (5.1-5) to (5.1-7), PDF pp. 194 to 195 (the same step). The proof in steps is not from the source.

We have shown that the equation with the weight \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is exactly the Galerkin equation \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) when the field meets the natural condition. The Galerkin method as EP (p. 344) describes it and its weak form therefore give the same, the difference is only whether we have already carried out the integration by parts. In the first form the field must have a second derivative and meet the natural condition, in the second a first derivative and the essential condition are enough.

Let us also compute the equations for the field (6.27). The residual of step 3 is \(R = q - 6c\,EA\,x\), its derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(\partial R/\partial c = -6\,EA\,x\).

  1. Galerkin: \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac54\,qL^4 - 6c\,EA\,\int_0^L x\,(3L^2 x - x^3)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac54\,qL^4 - \tfrac{24}{5}\,c\,EA\,L^5\), because \(\int_0^L (3L^2 x^2 - x^4)\,\mathrm{d}x = L^5\,(1 - \tfrac15)\). The equation gives \(c = \tfrac{25}{96}\,q/(EA\,L)\), which by lemma 6.47 is the same number as for the weak form.
  2. Petrov-Galerkin with \(w = x\) and least squares: \(\int_0^L R\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,qL^2 - 6c\,EA\,\tfrac13 L^3 = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac14\,q/(EA\,L)\). The weight of least squares is \(-6\,EA\,x\), proportional to \(x\), so its equation is the same.
  3. Collocation at \(x_1 = L/2\): \(R(L/2) = q - 3c\,EA\,L = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac13\,q/(EA\,L)\).
  4. Subdomain, one whole bar \([0, L]\): \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = qL - 3c\,EA\,L^2 = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac13\,q/(EA\,L)\).

Figure 6.22 shows the residuals for the obtained numbers and the weights that gave them. All the residuals are straight lines that start at \(R = q\) at \(x = 0\). With collocation the residual is zero in the middle of the bar and \(-q\) at the end, so by absolute value it is largest there. With Galerkin, \(R(L/2) = \tfrac{7}{32}\,q = 1{,}315\) N/m and \(R(L) = -\tfrac{9}{16}\,q\). The residual is zero only at \(x = \tfrac{16}{25}\,L = 6{,}4\) m, but the integral of its product with \(\psi\) is zero, and Galerkin demands no more.

What about the field from the chapter on energy? In section 4.6.8 we took \(u = c\,x\) and with the energy obtained \(c = aL\) and the exact displacement of the end. Its residual is \(R = q = 6{,}010\) N/m for every \(c\), as we saw for the strong form in section 6.1.1. For a weight that is positive everywhere, such as \(\psi\), \(x\) or \(1\), \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\), so the equation cannot be solved for any \(c\). Moreover, the field does not meet the natural condition, \(EA\,u'(L) = EA\,c \ne 0\), which the weak form does not demand. The weighted-residual method gives nothing with this field, while the cubic field (6.27) has a nonzero second derivative and is therefore suitable. In the methods with a residual the trial field must have a second derivative that can follow the equation, while the weak form needs only the first, which EP (p. 323) describes for the weak form as "weaker (i.e., lesser) differentiability".

6.6.3 Comparison of the methods (primerjava metod)

Which of the ways is best? The answer depends on what we measure. Let us compare them on the trial field (6.27), which separates them. Figure 6.23 shows the fields and the energies of the errors. The numbers differ for four reasons.

Galerkin has the smallest energy of the error. This is theorem 6.37: among all the fields \(c\,\psi\) the field with the Ritz and Galerkin number has the error with the smallest strain energy. For \(c = \kappa\,q/(EA\,L)\) the energy of the error is \(9{,}165 + 5631\,(\kappa - \tfrac{25}{96})^2\) µJ (the table in section 6.5.1), the second term being the energy of the difference from the Galerkin field. So collocation has an energy of error \(39{,}10/9{,}165 = 4{,}3\) times larger than Galerkin. The number \(1/3\) is \(7/96\) away from \(25/96\), the number \(1/4\) only \(1/96\), and the second term grows with the square of the distance, so for collocation it is \(49\) times larger than for least squares, \(29{,}94\) against \(0{,}611\) µJ.

Least squares and \(w = x\) give the exact displacement of the end. For the weight \(w = x\) we have \(w' = 1\), so \(B(u_1, x) = \int_0^L EA\,u_1'\,\mathrm{d}x = EA\,u_1(L)\), and \(\ell(x) = \int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,qL^2\). The equation \(\int_0^L R\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) is by lemma 6.47 (the field meets the natural condition, \(F = 0\)) equal to \(B(u_1, x) = \ell(x)\), so \(EA\,u_1(L) = \tfrac12\,qL^2\). The weak form with \(w = x\) says the same for the true field, so \(u_1(L) = u(L)\). This weight is \(EA\) times the field \(G = x/EA\), which is the field of a unit force at the end of the bar, and for every admissible field \(v\) we have \(B(G, v) = \int_0^L v'\,\mathrm{d}x = v(L)\), which is exactly the reason why the equation reads off the displacement of the end. In theorem 6.38 this is the field \(G_{\mathrm{L}}\), which must lie in the trial family for the displacement to be exact. In the Galerkin method the test family is the same as the trial family, so \(G_{\mathrm{L}}\) must lie in it, while in the Petrov-Galerkin method we choose the test function ourselves and choose \(x\). In least squares the weight is proportional to \(x\), because \(\partial R/\partial c = -6\,EA\,x\), and the equation is the same.

Collocation at \(L/2\) is the same as subdomain. The residual is a straight line, \(R = q - 6c\,EA\,x\). The mean of a straight line over an interval is its value in the middle, \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = L\,R(L/2)\), so the equations \(R(L/2) = 0\) and \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) give the same number.

Which measure is the right one? The energy of the error is smallest for Galerkin. The displacement of the end is best for least squares. In the middle the error of Galerkin is \(-4{,}5\ \%\), of least squares \(-8{,}3\ \%\) and of collocation \(+22\ \%\). The theorem holds only for the energy: Galerkin is best in this sense for every trial space (theorem 6.37), while the exact displacement of the end in least squares is a consequence of the weight being proportional to \(x\), which is the field of the force at the end of the bar, and not a property of the method. If we were interested in the displacement of the middle, nothing of this would be guaranteed.

Matrices. In the Ritz method the matrix \(K\) is symmetric, because \(B\) is symmetric, and so it is in the Galerkin method with a symmetric \(B\) (theorem 6.44). In least squares the matrix \(\int_0^L (EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}')'\,(EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}')'\,\mathrm{d}x\) is symmetric already because of the commutativity of the product, and as a matrix of inner products of the functions \((EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}')'\) it is positive definite if these functions are independent (lemma 6.28). EP (p. 350) writes that this is "the only other method, in addition to the Ritz method, that is based on the minimization of a functional and results in a symmetric coefficient matrix". In collocation and Petrov-Galerkin the matrix is in general unsymmetric (EP, p. 340), and we do not have the positive definiteness that guarantees a single solution (theorem 6.29). For \(n = 1\) the matrix is \(1 \times 1\) and always symmetric, so our example cannot show this. In collocation the choice of points matters: EP (p. 358) shows that for a simply supported beam with two parameters the points \(L/4\) and \(3L/4\) give the same relations between the numbers \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), so no solution follows from them.

Finite differences (končne razlike). We can use collocation in one more way. We divide the bar into \(n\) equal parts of length \(h = L/n\) with grid points \(x_{\mathrm{i}} = i h\) and demand that the residual be zero at the points \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\). The unknowns are not coefficients of trial functions but the values \(u_{\mathrm{i}} \approx u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and the second derivative is replaced by a difference. The central quotient of section 2.7.4 gives \(u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} + h/2) \approx (u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - u_{\mathrm{i}})/h\) and \(u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} - h/2) \approx (u_{\mathrm{i}} - u_{\mathrm{i}-1})/h\), and with it applied to \(u'\) we obtain

\[u''(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \approx \frac{u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} + h/2) - u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} - h/2)}{h} \approx \frac{u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1}}{h^2} .\]

For constant \(EA\) the equation at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is then \(EA\,(u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1})/h^2 + q = 0\), at \(x_0 = 0\) we have \(u_0 = 0\), and the natural condition \(u'(L) = 0\) we write with the central quotient around \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), \((u_{\mathrm{n}+1} - u_{\mathrm{n}-1})/(2h) = 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{n}+1} = u_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) (the point \(x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) is auxiliary, outside the bar). For \(n = 2\) and \(n = 4\) we solved the system with NumPy. We obtain exactly the true values: \(14{,}64\) and \(19{,}52\) µm for \(n = 2\), and \(8{,}540\), \(14{,}64\), \(18{,}30\) and \(19{,}52\) µm for \(n = 4\). Why exactly? The true field is quadratic, and the central quotient of a quadratic function is exact (section 2.7.4), so the true values satisfy the difference equations. The system has a single solution: the homogeneous system gives \(u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1} = 0\) for \(i < n\) and \(u_0 = 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{i}} = i s\) for some number \(s\), and the last equation \(u_{\mathrm{n}-1} - u_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) gives \(s = 0\). The exactness is therefore a consequence of the quadratic true field and not a property of the method, for other fields the central quotient is not exact and the values are not hit exactly. This paragraph is not from the sources. We do not treat the boundary element method (metoda robnih elementov) in the book.

The eigenvalues that the Ritz method looks for in vibration (the Rayleigh quotient) need mass and motion, so we treat them in chapter 13.

With this we have answered the two questions from the start of the section. We can use the weak form directly, without energy: the Galerkin method with the same functions for the trial and the test fields gives for a symmetric \(B\) the same equations as the Ritz method, and the orthogonality of the error and Cea's estimate hold. We can also make the error small with weighted residuals, and the five ways of choosing the weights give for the same cubic field an energy of error between \(9{,}165\) and \(39{,}10\) µJ. The theorem on the best approximation guarantees this only for the Galerkin choice, so the next section builds the trial fields from pieces of straight lines, which this theorem covers.
