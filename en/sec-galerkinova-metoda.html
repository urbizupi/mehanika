Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.6 Galerkin method and weighted residuals (Galerkinova metoda in uteženi ostanki)

The Ritz method of section 6.4 chooses the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) so that the total potential energy is smallest. For this it needs an energy. The weak form (šibka oblika) of section 6.1.3, however, is an equation that holds for every test function (preizkusna funkcija) and does not mention energy. Can we look for an approximation directly from it? And while we are at it: in how many ways can we demand that the error of an approximation be small, and how do these ways differ?

Figure 6.101 sorts the problems. The outer region is the problems that we can write in the weak form, the inner one those that also have an energy \(\Pi\) whose lowest point is the solution. In the inner region is the bar, outside it are heat conduction, fluid flow and the nonsymmetric form \(B\), for which there is no energy.

We look for the answer on the hanging bar that we know from chapter 4 and section 6.4.2. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m, cross-sectional area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and modulus of elasticity \(E = 196\) GPa is fixed at \(x = 0\), the axis \(x\) points downward, and the lower end is free. On the right of the figure the axes are \(x\) and the displacement \(u\). It is loaded by its own weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m per unit length, \(EA = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^7\) N. The true field is \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), the lower end moves by \(19{,}52\) µm and the middle by \(14{,}64\) µm. The trial field (poskusno polje) throughout this section is the cubic function

\[u_1(x) = c\,\psi(x) , \qquad \psi(x) = 3L^2 x - x^3 . \tag{6.27}\]

We chose it because it respects the fixing, \(\psi(0) = 0\), and because \(\psi'(L) = 3(L^2 - L^2) = 0\), so the field has zero axial force at the free end, as the true field has. The second derivative \(\psi'' = -6x\) is not zero, so we will also be able to try the ways that need a second derivative. And the true function is not among the fields \(c\,\psi\), because it is quadratic and \(\psi\) is cubic, so the ways will give different results. Here \(c\) is a number with the unit \(\text{m}^{-2}\), since \(\psi\) is in \(\text{m}^3\) and \(u_1\) in m. At \(x = 5\) m the true field is at the middle of the bar \(14{,}64\) µm, and the cubic field \(13{,}98\) µm.

6.6.1 Galerkin method (Galerkinova metoda)

The true solution \(u\) satisfies the weak form, \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\), for every test function \(w\) (definition 6.6). If the true field is not in the family, the trial field \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) cannot satisfy it for all \(w\), since it would otherwise be the true field. But we can satisfy it for \(n\) test functions, as many as there are unknown numbers. Which ones should they be? The simplest choice is to take the very functions from which the trial field is built. Where this idea comes from is told in the next paragraph.

From history

Around the turn from the nineteenth to the twentieth century, Rayleigh, Ritz and Galerkin were among the prominent contributors to approximate variational methods (EP, pp. 3 to 4). Galerkin published his work in 1915 (EP, pp. 701 and 705). The work of Rayleigh and Ritz, together with Galerkin's weighted-residual approach, formed a consistent framework in which a continuous problem with infinitely many degrees of freedom is replaced by a discrete one (GR, PDF p. 354). In their original form these methods were not flexible enough for systems of complex geometry with arbitrary boundary conditions and loading (GR, PDF p. 354). Galerkin's idea is to make the residual of the differential equation orthogonal to the functions from which the approximation is built (EP, p. 344). The name is often used also for an expression in which integration by parts has reduced the order of the derivatives, but this turns Galerkin's original idea into the Ritz method (EP, p. 345). The method in which the weights are the same functions as in the approximation is called more precisely after Bubnov and Galerkin (COOK, PDF p. 196).

Figure 6.103 shows the space of test functions. The plane is the set \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) of all linear combinations, spanned by the functions \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\). Each function \(w\) in it is the sum of two parts, \(d_1\psi_1 + d_2\psi_2\), and the function outside the plane is not in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\). On the right are the residuals \(R(w) = B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) - \ell(w)\). For the Galerkin approximation \(R(\psi_1)\) and \(R(\psi_2)\) are zero, so the residual at \(w\) is also \(d_1 \cdot 0 + d_2 \cdot 0 = 0\), but for the function outside the plane the residual is in general not zero.

Let us return to the weak form of the bar. The equation \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) must be true for every test function. Which test functions do we take once we restrict the trial field to the family \(\psi_0 + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.32)? Galerkin's answer is this.

Definition 6.42 Galerkin method (Galerkinova metoda)

Let the problem be written in the weak form \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) for every test function \(w\), with a bilinear form \(B\) and a linear form \(\ell\) (definition 6.6). Let \(\psi_0\) be an admissible field, \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) independent test functions and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) the set of all their linear combinations (definition 6.32). The Galerkin approximation is a function \(u_{\mathrm{n}} \in \psi_0 + \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) for which

\[B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) = \ell(w) \quad \text{for every } w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}} . \tag{6.28}\]

The trial and the test functions are from the same family.

Source: EP (6.4.12), (6.4.13), p. 344, COOK §5.1, PDF pp. 193 to 196 ("a Galerkin statement incorporates differential equations in their weak form", PDF p. 193).

How many equations is this? The demand holds for each of infinitely many fields \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\), but the equations for \(w = \psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) already suffice, and they are linear in the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Lemma 6.43 Galerkin equations (Galerkinove enačbe)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) be as in definition 6.42. Then equation (6.28) holds for every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) exactly when \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\). These equations are the system \(K\,c = f\) with \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).

Proof

We must show that the difference \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) - \ell(w)\) for each \(w\) splits into the differences for the functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\).

  1. Every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(w = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}} \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(B\) is linear in the second argument and \(\ell\) is linear, \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, w) - \ell(w) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d_{\mathrm{i}}\,\big(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\big)\).
  2. If all terms in the brackets are zero, then by step 1 the sum is zero for every choice of the numbers \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\), so the equation holds for every \(w\).
  3. If the equation holds for every \(w \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\), it also holds for \(w = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\), since \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(d_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\), the other \(d\) zero). These are the equations \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  4. Since \(B\) is linear in the first argument, \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). The equation \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\,c_{\mathrm{j}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\), row \(i\) of the system \(K\,c = f\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same step is in the proof of lemma 6.36, steps 2 and 3.

Figure 6.104 shows the system. In the matrix \(K\) row \(i\) holds the numbers \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). They are multiplied by the column of unknowns \(c\), and the sum is the right side \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\).

The matrix \(K\) and the right-hand side \(f\) are the same as in the Ritz method (definition 6.32). The only difference is that in the Galerkin method \(B\) need not be symmetric, and therefore the matrix \(K\) need not be either: in a nonsymmetric matrix \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{ji}}\) differ, in a symmetric one they are equal.

A bar with the cubic field. Let us write out the definition for the field (6.27). The essential condition is \(u(0) = 0\), so \(\psi_0 = 0\), \(n = 1\), \(\psi_1 = \psi\), and equation (6.28) is \(B(c\,\psi, \psi) = \ell(\psi)\) with \(B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\). The numbers \(B(\psi, \psi) = \tfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\) and \(\ell(\psi) = \tfrac54\,q\,L^4\) are the matrix and the right side of the Ritz equation of section 6.4.2, so the Galerkin equation \(\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA\,L^5\,c = \tfrac54\,q\,L^4\) is the same and gives \(c = \tfrac{25}{96}\,q/(EA\,L)\). The field has \(20{,}33\) µm at the end, \(4{,}2\ \%\) too much, and \(13{,}98\) µm in the middle, \(4{,}5\ \%\) too little, while the work of the weight is \(2{,}3\ \%\) smaller than the true one, as theorem 6.34 says (section 6.4.4). By the weak form for \(u\) and the Galerkin equation, \(B(u - u_1, \psi) = \ell(\psi) - B(u_1, \psi) = 0\) (lemma 6.36), so the error is orthogonal to \(\psi\) in the energy inner product.

Is this a coincidence? For a bar \(B\) is symmetric, and the two methods coincide for every symmetric \(B\).

Theorem 6.44 Galerkin and Ritz methods coincide (Galerkinova in Ritzova metoda sovpadata)

Let \(B\) be a symmetric bilinear form, \(B(v, w) = B(w, v)\) for all admissible \(v\), \(w\), let \(\ell\) be a linear form and \(\Pi(w) = \tfrac12\,B(w, w) - \ell(w)\) the total potential energy. Let \(\psi_0, \psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the functions of definition 6.32. Then the Galerkin equations \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\) are exactly the equations \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) of the Ritz method, that is, the same system \(K\,c = f\) with the same solution \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\).

Proof

We must show that the derivative of the energy with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals the Galerkin expression.

  1. Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By bilinearity and symmetry, \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_0) = B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{j}})\), we have \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}})\) quadratic in \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), in one \(c\) a parabola: \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, u_{\mathrm{n}}) = B(\psi_0, \psi_0) + 2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\).
  2. By linearity of \(\ell\), \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}}) = \ell(\psi_0) + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\). The energy \(\Pi\) is therefore a polynomial of second order in \(c_1, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}}\), a bowl with a lowest point.
  3. The term \(c_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the derivative \(\delta_{\mathrm{ik}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} + c_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{jk}}\) with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) (product rule). Hence the derivative of the double sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\), which by symmetry is \(2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  4. From steps 1 to 3 the slope of the curve is \(\dfrac{\partial \Pi}{\partial c_{\mathrm{k}}} = \tfrac12\,\big(2B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) + 2\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\big) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) - \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\), where the derivative of \(\ell(u_{\mathrm{n}})\) with respect to \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\), was taken from step 2 and the last equality follows from bilinearity.
  5. By step 4 and lemma 6.43, \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{kj}}\,c_{\mathrm{j}} - f_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(K_{\mathrm{kj}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}) - B(\psi_0, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\). The Ritz method takes the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) from \(K\,c = f\) (definition 6.32), that is, from the condition \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for every \(k\), as it must be at the lowest point (section 4.6.6). By step 4 the same condition is \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{k}})\) for every \(k\), and by lemma 6.43 these are the Galerkin equations.

Source: EP p. 345 ("The Ritz and Galerkin methods yield the same set of algebraic equations for the following three cases"), COOK §5.1, PDF p. 196 (the same for the choice of the same family). The proof is not from a source.

We have shown that the Galerkin equations are the condition that the derivative of the energy with respect to each \(c_{\mathrm{k}}\) is zero, written with the bilinear form and work instead of a derivative. For the bar with the cubic field this can be seen: the energy \(\Pi(c) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA L^5 c^2 - \tfrac54\,qL^4 c\) has the derivative \(\tfrac{24}{5}\,EA L^5 c - \tfrac54\,qL^4\), which is the Galerkin equation above. The Ritz calculation in section 6.4.2 and the Galerkin calculation above have the same number, \(c = 25/96\ q/(EAL)\), and the energy \(\Pi = -381{,}87\) µJ.

Symmetry is needed in the theorem. On \([0, 1]\) take the nonsymmetric \(B(u, w) = \int_0^1 (u'\,w' + u'\,w)\,\mathrm{d}x\) from the example at definition 6.4, \(\ell(w) = \int_0^1 w\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(\psi_0 = 0\) and \(\psi_1 = x\), \(\psi_2 = x^2\). The test functions are piecewise continuously differentiable on \([0, 1]\) with \(w(0) = 0\), the end \(x = 1\) is free. The matrix has elements \(K_{\mathrm{kj}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}})\): \(K_{11} = \int_0^1 (1 + x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac32\), \(K_{12} = B(x^2, x) = \tfrac53\), \(K_{21} = B(x, x^2) = \tfrac43\) and \(K_{22} = \int_0^1 (4x^2 + 2x^3)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{11}{6}\). The right side is \(f = (\ell(x), \ell(x^2)) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac13)\).

Figure 6.105 shows the calculation. On the left are the matrix \(K\), whose off-diagonal elements differ, \(\tfrac53 \ne \tfrac43\), and its symmetric part \(K_{\mathrm{s}}\), where they are equal. In the middle, in the plane \((c_1, c_2)\), are the level curves of the energy with the lowest point and beside it the Galerkin point. On the right are the corresponding fields: the true field, the Galerkin field and the field of least energy.

  1. Galerkin solves \(K\,c = f\), so \(c = (\tfrac{13}{19}, -\tfrac{6}{19})\).
  2. Least energy \(\Pi(c) = \tfrac12\,c^{\mathsf T} K c - f \cdot c\): since \(c^{\mathsf T} K c = c^{\mathsf T} K_{\mathrm{s}} c\) for the symmetric part \(K_{\mathrm{s}} = \tfrac12 (K + K^{\mathsf T})\), which has the off-diagonal elements \(\tfrac12 (\tfrac53 + \tfrac43) = \tfrac32\), the condition \(\partial \Pi/\partial c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) is the equation \(K_{\mathrm{s}}\,c = f\) and gives \(c = (\tfrac56, -\tfrac12)\). The matrix \(K_{\mathrm{s}}\) is positive definite (\(\tfrac32 > 0\), \(\det K_{\mathrm{s}} = \tfrac12 > 0\)), so this is the lowest point.
  3. The solutions differ. In step 3 of the proof of the theorem the derivative of the double sum is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{k}}) + \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}} B(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}})\). Without symmetry these are two different expressions, since \(K_{12}\) and \(K_{21}\) differ, together \(\big((K + K^{\mathsf T})\,c\big)_{\mathrm{k}} = 2\,(K_{\mathrm{s}}\,c)_{\mathrm{k}}\), and not \(2\,(K c)_{\mathrm{k}}\).

The weak form \(B(u, w) = \ell(w)\) has here the true solution \(u = x - e^{x-1} + e^{-1}\) with \(u(1) = 0{,}3679\) (SymPy). The Galerkin approximation gives \(u_2(1) = \tfrac{7}{19} = 0{,}3684\), and least energy gives \(\tfrac13 = 0{,}3333\). Galerkin's method does find an approximation of the weak form, whereas least energy does not suit a nonsymmetric \(B\), because \(u\) is not the minimum point of the functional \(\Pi\).

Why then do we need a new name at all? Because the Galerkin method does not need an energy. The bilinear form \(B\) and the right-hand side \(\ell\) of the weak form are enough. In some problems, for example in fluid flows, we have only a differential equation and boundary conditions, and a functional similar to the potential energy cannot be written or has no clear physical meaning (COOK, PDF p. 193). Then we write the weak form from the equation itself, as COOK (PDF pp. 194 to 195) does for a bar, and as a nonstructural example it names steady heat conduction (COOK, PDF p. 194), to which chapter 14 is devoted. The Galerkin method works there, the Ritz method does not. Also most of what we proved for the Ritz method in section 6.5 remains. The proof of lemma 6.36 uses only the weak form for \(u\) (step 1) and the equations \(K\,c = f\) (steps 2 and 3), which by lemma 6.43 are the Galerkin equations, and it does not need symmetry, so it holds for every \(B\) and every Galerkin approximation, since the error is orthogonal in the sense of \(B\) to every \(w\) whatever the energy. The steps of the proof of theorem 6.39 for the constant \(M/m\) use only this orthogonality and the estimates \(|B(v, w)| \le M \int |v'|\,|w'|\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(B(w, w) \ge m \int w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and they do not need symmetry either. Hence the same steps give Cea's estimate with the constant \(M/m\) also for an unsymmetric \(B\) that has these two estimates. The estimate with the constant \(\sqrt{M/m}\) and theorem 6.37, however, need symmetry, because the splitting \(B(e + d, e + d) = B(e, e) + 2B(e, d) + B(d, d)\) in the proof of theorem 6.37 combines two mixed terms into \(2B(e, d)\). We do not develop unsymmetric problems further, since the book does not need them in this chapter.

6.6.2 Weighted residuals (uteženi ostanki)

The Galerkin method is the weak form with a particular choice of test functions. But what if we take the differential equation directly, without integration by parts? We insert the trial field into the equilibrium equation of the bar, and how much the equation fails is told by the residual.

Definition 6.45 Residual of a trial field (ostanek poskusnega polja)

Let a bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) and axial load \(q\) be as in definition 6.1, and let \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a trial field for which \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\) is a differentiable function. The residual of the trial field is the function

\[R(x) = \big(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(x)\big)' + q(x) , \tag{6.29}\]

a number with the unit N/m at every point \(x \in [0, L]\). The residual is zero at every point exactly when the field satisfies the differential equation of equilibrium.

Source: EP (6.4.3), (6.4.5), pp. 338 and 340, COOK (5.1-2), PDF p. 194.

Figure 6.106 shows how the residual arises. At the top is the parabola of the axial force \(EA\,u_1'\), zero at \(x = L\), and a tangent to it: its slope is the derivative of the force. Below, on the axes, is the residual: it starts at \(q\), falls and at the end reaches \(-0{,}5625\,q\), while the zero line is equilibrium.

For the field (6.27) we compute the residual in three steps.

  1. The derivative is \(u_1' = 3c\,(L^2 - x^2)\), multiplied by \(EA\) it is the parabola of the axial force, which is zero at \(x = L\).
  2. The axial force \(EA\,u_1' = 3c\,EA\,(L^2 - x^2)\) has the derivative \(-6c\,EA\,x\), which is the slope of the tangent to the force curve.
  3. The residual is \(R(x) = q - 6c\,EA\,x\), a straight line in \(x\) that equals the load \(q\) at \(x = 0\) and falls with a slope determined by \(c\).

The residual cannot be zero everywhere: that would require \(q = 6c\,EA\,x\) for every \(x\), which holds for no \(c\), since the line crosses the zero line at one point only. We must demand something weaker. We choose \(n\) functions and demand that the residual be orthogonal to them, which means that the integral of the product is zero, because the positive and the negative area cancel (figure 6.107, inner product of functions, definition 6.16).

In the figure there are five rows with the same residual, the grey line. Each row has its own axis for \(x\), running to \(L\). The first has the weight \(\psi\) and the areas of the product \(R\,\psi\) above and below the axis, which are equal for the Galerkin \(c\). The second has the weight \(x\), the third the weight of least squares, proportional to \(x\), and the area of the squared residual, which we minimize. The fourth has the impulse of collocation at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), the fifth the subdomain weight, which is \(1\) on a part of the bar and \(0\) elsewhere.

Definition 6.46 Weighted-residual method (metoda uteženih ostankov)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \psi_0 + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}} \psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) be a trial field of a bar with the residual \(R\) (definition 6.45) that meets the essential conditions and the natural condition \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\). Let \(w_1, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\) be independent piecewise continuous functions on \([0, L]\), the weight functions (utežne funkcije), which may differ from the \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The weighted-residual method determines the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) from the \(n\) equations

\[\int_0^L R(x)\,w_{\mathrm{i}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 , \qquad i = 1, \dots, n . \tag{6.30}\]

If the trial field does not meet the natural condition, the residual at the boundary must be added to the equations.

Source: EP §6.4.2, (6.4.4) to (6.4.6), pp. 339 to 340 ("which, in general, are different from the approximation functions", p. 340), COOK §5.2, PDF pp. 196 to 197.

Figure 6.108 shows what the natural condition means. At the top, on the axes, is the axial force \(EA\,u'\) along the hanging bar. At the end there is no force, \(F = 0\). For the cubic field the axial force falls to zero exactly there, for the field \(u = c\,x\) it is constant and leaves a difference \(EA\,c\) at the end. Below, on the axes, is the residual of this field, the constant \(q\), and the area of its product with the weight \(x\).

For a linear problem the equations (6.30) are linear in \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), just like the Galerkin ones. We wrote the method for a bar, for a general differential equation it is written by EP (6.4.4) to (6.4.6). Why must the trial field also meet the natural condition? In the weak form this condition is already in the equation, in the equation with the residual it is not (EP, pp. 340 to 341), so the field itself must ensure it. The field (6.27) meets it, since \(u_1'(L) = c\,\psi'(L) = 0\) and \(F = 0\).

The methods differ in the choice of the weights \(w_{\mathrm{i}}\) (EP (6.4.11), p. 341).

  1. Galerkin, \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\). The residual must be orthogonal to the trial functions themselves.
  2. Petrov-Galerkin, the weights are functions other than \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\), for example \(w_1 = x\). We separate the families of trial and test functions (EP, p. 270, example 6.4.1, pp. 342 to 344, COOK PDF p. 196). With the weight \(x\) we will obtain below the exact displacement of the end.
  3. Least squares (najmanjši kvadrati): we minimize \(I(c) = \int_0^L R^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The derivative \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}c_{\mathrm{i}}} = 2\int_0^L R\,\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) must be zero at the minimum, so the residual is orthogonal to \(\partial R/\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is the weighted-residual method with the weight \(w_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial R/\partial c_{\mathrm{i}}\) (EP §6.4.4, (6.4.14) to (6.4.18), p. 349, COOK (5.2-4), (5.2-5), PDF p. 197). EP also adds to the functional a term with the boundary residual, which we omit because our field meets the natural condition (EP, pp. 349 to 350).
  4. Collocation (kolokacija): the residual is zero at chosen points, \(R(x_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\) (COOK, PDF p. 197, EP §6.4.5, p. 356). The weight is concentrated at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\). Such a weight is not a function. Collocation is the limit of the weight \(1/(2h)\) on the interval \([x_{\mathrm{i}} - h, x_{\mathrm{i}} + h]\), since the mean of a continuous \(R\) over this interval tends to \(R(x_{\mathrm{i}})\) as \(h \to 0\). EP (6.4.29) writes it with the Dirac delta, which we will not need.
  5. Subdomain (podobmočje): we divide the bar into parts \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) and demand that the integral of the residual over each part is zero, \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{i}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{i}}} R\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The weight is \(1\) on this part and \(0\) elsewhere (EP §6.4.6, (6.4.34) to (6.4.36), pp. 358 to 359, COOK PDF p. 197).

With the Galerkin choice the connection with the weak form appears. With integration by parts from chapter 3 we can turn the residual with the weight \(\psi\) into the weak form, as figure 6.109 shows.

Figure 6.109 shows what the proof computes. At the top, on the axes, is the axial force \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\), which at \(x = L\) equals the prescribed force \(F\), and the test function \(\psi\), which is zero at the fixed end. Below, on the axes, is their product, zero at \(x = 0\) and equal to \(F\,\psi(L)\) at \(x = L\). The difference of these two values is the square bracket.

Lemma 6.47 Weighted residual and weak form (utežni ostanek in šibka oblika)

Let a bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) that is continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) be fixed at \(x = 0\), loaded by a continuous axial load \(q\) and by a force \(F\) at the end \(x = L\). Let \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) be a field with a continuous second derivative, \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\), and \(\psi\) a continuously differentiable function with \(\psi(0) = 0\). Then

\[\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell(\psi) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi) , \tag{6.31}\]

where \(B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,w(L)\).

Proof

We must show that integration by parts turns the residual into the weak form.

  1. By the definition of the residual, \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L (EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}')'\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L q\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\). The left integral is the area of the product \(R\,\psi\).
  2. The functions \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\) and \(\psi\) are continuously differentiable, so by corollary 3.56 \(\int_0^L (EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}')'\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi\big]_0^L - \int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi'\,\mathrm{d}x\). The square bracket is the difference of the product \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi\) at the ends.
  3. At \(x = 0\) we have \(\psi(0) = 0\), at \(x = L\) we have \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\), so the square bracket equals \(F\,\psi(L)\).
  4. From steps 1 to 3, \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = F\,\psi(L) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi) + \int_0^L q\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \ell(\psi) - B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi)\). The term \(F\,\psi(L)\) is the value of the product at the end.

Source: COOK §5.1, (5.1-5) to (5.1-7), PDF pp. 194 to 195 (the same step). The proof in steps is not from the source.

We have shown that the equation with the weight \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is exactly the Galerkin equation \(B(u_{\mathrm{n}}, \psi_{\mathrm{i}}) = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{i}})\) when the field meets the natural condition. The Galerkin method as EP (p. 344) describes it and its weak form therefore give the same, the difference is only whether we have already carried out the integration by parts. In the first form the field must have a second derivative and meet the natural condition, in the second a first derivative and the essential condition are enough.

The natural condition is necessary in the lemma. Take the hanging bar (\(F = 0\)) and the trial field \(u = c\,x\), which has \(u'' = 0\) but \(EA\,u'(L) = EA\,c \ne 0\) for \(c \ne 0\). The residual is \(R = (EA\,c)' + q = q\) for every \(c\). With the weight \(\psi = x\) we have \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = q\,L^2/2 = 300{,}5\) N m, and the equation \(300{,}5 = 0\) does not contain \(c\) at all and has no solution. The weak form with the same function, \(\ell(\psi) - B(u, \psi) = 300{,}5 - EA\,c\,L = 0\), gives \(c = q\,L/(2EA) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) (section 6.1.3). The equations differ by \(EA\,c\,L\), which is the square bracket \([EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'\,\psi]_0^L\) from step 2 of the proof, which step 3 replaces by \(F\,\psi(L)\) only if \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{n}}'(L) = F\). Without this condition the residual and the weak form do not agree.

Let us also compute the equations for the field (6.27). The residual of step 3 is \(R = q - 6c\,EA\,x\), its derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(\partial R/\partial c = -6\,EA\,x\).

  1. Galerkin: \(\int_0^L R\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac54\,qL^4 - 6c\,EA\,\int_0^L x\,(3L^2 x - x^3)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac54\,qL^4 - \tfrac{24}{5}\,c\,EA\,L^5\), because \(\int_0^L (3L^2 x^2 - x^4)\,\mathrm{d}x = L^5\,(1 - \tfrac15)\). The equation gives \(c = \tfrac{25}{96}\,q/(EA\,L)\), which by lemma 6.47 is the same number as for the weak form.
  2. Petrov-Galerkin with \(w = x\) and least squares: \(\int_0^L R\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,qL^2 - 6c\,EA\,\tfrac13 L^3 = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac14\,q/(EA\,L)\). The weight of least squares is \(-6\,EA\,x\), proportional to \(x\), so its equation is the same.
  3. Collocation at \(x_1 = L/2\): \(R(L/2) = q - 3c\,EA\,L = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac13\,q/(EA\,L)\).
  4. Subdomain, one whole bar \([0, L]\): \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = qL - 3c\,EA\,L^2 = 0\) gives \(c = \tfrac13\,q/(EA\,L)\).

Figure 6.110 shows the residuals for the obtained numbers and the weights that gave them. At the top are the axes for \(x\) and \(R/q\), with the residual of Galerkin ending at \(-0{,}5625\), the residual of least squares and Petrov-Galerkin ending at \(-0{,}5\) and the residual of collocation and subdomain ending at \(-1\) and zero at \(x = 5\) m (dot). Below, on the axes, are the weights: the weight \(\psi\), the weight \(x\), the subdomain weight \(1\) and the impulse \(\delta\) at \(x = 5\) m. All the residuals are straight lines that start at \(R = q\) at \(x = 0\). With collocation the residual is zero in the middle of the bar and \(-q\) at the end, so by absolute value it is largest there. With Galerkin, \(R(L/2) = \tfrac{7}{32}\,q = 1{,}315\) N/m and \(R(L) = -\tfrac{9}{16}\,q\). The residual is zero only at \(x = \tfrac{16}{25}\,L = 6{,}4\) m, but the integral of its product with \(\psi\) is zero, and Galerkin demands no more.

What about the field from the chapter on energy? In section 4.6.8 we took \(u = c\,x\) and with the energy obtained \(c = aL\) and the exact displacement of the end. Its residual is \(R = q = 6{,}010\) N/m for every \(c\), as we saw for the strong form in section 6.1.1. For a weight that is positive on \((0, L]\), such as \(\psi\), \(x\) or \(1\), \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x > 0\), so the equation cannot be solved for any \(c\). Moreover, the field does not meet the natural condition, \(EA\,u'(L) = EA\,c \ne 0\), which the weak form does not demand. The weighted-residual method gives nothing with this field, while the cubic field (6.27) has a nonzero second derivative and is therefore suitable. In the methods with a residual the trial field must have a second derivative that can follow the equation, while the weak form needs only the first, which EP (p. 323) describes for the weak form as "weaker (i.e., lesser) differentiability".

6.6.3 Comparison of the methods (primerjava metod)

Which of the ways is best? The answer depends on what we measure. Let us compare them on the trial field (6.27), which separates them. Figure 6.111 shows the fields and the energies of the errors. On the left are the axes for \(x\) and the displacement \(u\), the true field is the thick blackwhite curve, the Galerkin field has \(20{,}33\) µm at the end, the field with the weight \(x\) \(19{,}52\) µm and the field of collocation and subdomain \(26{,}03\) µm. The vertical dashed lines are at \(x = 5\) m and \(x = 10\) m. On the right, on the axes, are the energies of the errors in µJ, the bars are labelled with the weights: \(\psi\), \(x\), \(\delta\) and \(1\). The numbers differ for four reasons.

Galerkin has the smallest energy of the error. This is theorem 6.37: among all the fields \(c\,\psi\) the field with the Ritz and Galerkin number has the error with the smallest strain energy. For \(c = \kappa\,q/(EA\,L)\) the energy of the error is \(9{,}165 + 5631\,(\kappa - \tfrac{25}{96})^2\) µJ (the table in section 6.5.1), the second term being the energy of the difference from the Galerkin field. So the bar of collocation is \(39{,}10/9{,}165 = 4{,}3\) times as high as that of Galerkin. The number \(1/3\) is \(7/96\) away from \(25/96\), the number \(1/4\) only \(1/96\), and the second term grows with the square of the distance, so for collocation it is \(49\) times larger than for least squares, \(29{,}94\) against \(0{,}611\) µJ. This is why the bar of the weight \(x\) barely exceeds that of Galerkin, \(9{,}776\) against \(9{,}165\) µJ.

Least squares and \(w = x\) give the exact displacement of the end. For the weight \(w = x\) we have \(w' = 1\), so \(B(u_1, x) = \int_0^L EA\,u_1'\,\mathrm{d}x = EA\,u_1(L)\), and \(\ell(x) = \int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12\,qL^2\). The equation \(\int_0^L R\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) is by lemma 6.47 (the field meets the natural condition, \(F = 0\)) equal to \(B(u_1, x) = \ell(x)\), so \(EA\,u_1(L) = \tfrac12\,qL^2\). The weak form with \(w = x\) says the same for the true field, so \(u_1(L) = u(L)\): the orange curve meets the true field on the right vertical line at \(x = 10\) m, at \(19{,}52\) µm. This weight is \(EA\) times the field \(G = x/EA\), which is the field of a unit force at the end of the bar (a force \(1\) at the end gives the axial force \(1\) in every cross-section, so \(EA\,G' = 1\) and \(G = x/EA\)), and for every admissible field \(v\) we have \(B(G, v) = \int_0^L v'\,\mathrm{d}x = v(L)\), which is exactly the reason why the equation reads off the displacement of the end. In theorem 6.38 this is the field \(G_{\mathrm{L}}\): if it lies in the trial family, the end displacement is guaranteed to be exact. In the Galerkin method the test family is the same as the trial family, so for this guarantee \(G_{\mathrm{L}}\) must lie in it, while in the Petrov-Galerkin method we choose the test function ourselves and choose \(x\). In least squares the weight is proportional to \(x\), because \(\partial R/\partial c = -6\,EA\,x\), and the equation is the same.

Collocation at \(L/2\) is the same as subdomain. The residual is a straight line, \(R = q - 6c\,EA\,x\). The mean of a straight line over an interval is its value in the middle, \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = L\,R(L/2)\), so the equations \(R(L/2) = 0\) and \(\int_0^L R\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) give the same number. In figure 6.111 they therefore have one common dashed curve and equally high bars \(\delta\) and \(1\).

Which measure is the right one? The energy of the error is smallest for Galerkin. The displacement of the end is best for least squares. In the middle, on the left vertical line at \(x = 5\) m, the error of Galerkin is \(-4{,}5\ \%\), of least squares \(-8{,}3\ \%\) and of collocation \(+22\ \%\). The theorem holds only for the energy: Galerkin is best in this sense for every trial space (theorem 6.37), while the exact displacement of the end in least squares is a consequence of the weight being proportional to \(x\), which is the field of the force at the end of the bar, and not a property of the method. If we were interested in the displacement of the middle, nothing of this would be guaranteed.

Matrices. In the Ritz method the matrix \(K\) is symmetric, because \(B\) is symmetric, and so it is in the Galerkin method with a symmetric \(B\) (theorem 6.44). In least squares the matrix \(\int_0^L (EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}')'\,(EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{j}}')'\,\mathrm{d}x\) is symmetric already because of the commutativity of the product, and as a matrix of inner products of the functions \((EA\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}')'\) it is positive definite if these functions are independent (lemma 6.28). EP (p. 350) writes that this is "the only other method, in addition to the Ritz method, that is based on the minimization of a functional and results in a symmetric coefficient matrix". In collocation and Petrov-Galerkin the matrix is in general unsymmetric (EP, p. 340), and we do not have the positive definiteness that guarantees a single solution (theorem 6.29). For \(n = 1\) the matrix is \(1 \times 1\) and always symmetric, so our example cannot show this. In collocation the choice of points matters: EP (p. 358) shows that for a simply supported beam with two parameters the points \(L/4\) and \(3L/4\) give the same relations between the numbers \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), so no solution follows from them.

Figure 6.112 shows the difference. The Ritz and Galerkin matrix has equal mirror elements across the diagonal, so does the least-squares matrix, while in collocation and Petrov-Galerkin the mirror elements differ.

Finite differences (končne razlike). We can use collocation in one more way. We divide the bar into \(n\) equal parts of length \(h = L/n\) with grid points \(x_{\mathrm{i}} = i h\) and demand that the residual be zero at the points \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\). Figure 6.113 shows this for \(n = 4\): on the axes are the depth \(x\) and the displacement \(u\), the dashed vertical line is the lower end of the bar. The unknowns are not coefficients of trial functions but the values \(u_{\mathrm{i}} \approx u(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), and the second derivative is replaced by a difference. The central quotient of section 2.7.7 gives \(u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} + h/2) \approx (u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - u_{\mathrm{i}})/h\) and \(u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} - h/2) \approx (u_{\mathrm{i}} - u_{\mathrm{i}-1})/h\). These are the slopes of the chords to the right and to the left of the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), drawn in the figure at \(x_2\), each assigned to the middle of its chord. With the central quotient applied once more, to \(u'\), we obtain

\[u''(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \approx \frac{u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} + h/2) - u'(x_{\mathrm{i}} - h/2)}{h} \approx \frac{u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1}}{h^2} .\]

For constant \(EA\) the equation at the point \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is then \(EA\,(u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1})/h^2 + q = 0\), at \(x_0 = 0\) we have \(u_0 = 0\), and the natural condition \(u'(L) = 0\) we write with the central quotient around \(x_{\mathrm{n}}\), \((u_{\mathrm{n}+1} - u_{\mathrm{n}-1})/(2h) = 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{n}+1} = u_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) (the point \(x_{\mathrm{n}+1}\) is auxiliary, outside the bar, in the figure the point \(x_5\), and the chord from \(u_3\) to \(u_5\) is horizontal, which is the slope zero at \(x_4 = L\)). For \(n = 2\) and \(n = 4\) we solved the system with NumPy. We obtain exactly the true values: \(14{,}64\) and \(19{,}52\) µm for \(n = 2\), and \(8{,}540\), \(14{,}64\), \(18{,}30\) and \(19{,}52\) µm for \(n = 4\). Why exactly? The true field is quadratic, and the central quotient of a quadratic function is exact (section 2.7.7), so the true values satisfy the difference equations. The system has a single solution: the homogeneous system gives \(u_{\mathrm{i}+1} - 2u_{\mathrm{i}} + u_{\mathrm{i}-1} = 0\) for \(i < n\) and \(u_0 = 0\), so \(u_{\mathrm{i}} = i s\) for some number \(s\), and the last equation \(u_{\mathrm{n}-1} - u_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) gives \(s = 0\). The exactness is therefore a consequence of the quadratic true field and not a property of the method, for other fields the central quotient is not exact and the values are not hit exactly. As an example we also solved with NumPy the same bar with the load \(q\,x/L\), which grows from zero at the top to \(q\) at the bottom. The true field is then cubic, \(u = q\,(3L^2 x - x^3)/(6\,EA\,L)\), with the displacement of the end \(q L^2/(3EA) = 13{,}02\) µm, while finite differences give \(14{,}64\) µm for \(n = 2\) (\(+12{,}5\ \%\)) and \(13{,}42\) µm for \(n = 4\) (\(+3{,}1\ \%\)). The error comes from the condition at the end: there the central quotient over \(2h\) is no longer exact for a cubic field. This paragraph is not from the sources.

We do not treat the boundary element method (metoda robnih elementov) in the book.

The eigenvalues that the Ritz method looks for in vibration (the Rayleigh quotient) need mass and motion, so we treat them in chapter 13.

With this we have answered the two questions from the start of the section. We can use the weak form directly, without energy: the Galerkin method with the same functions for the trial and the test fields gives for a symmetric \(B\) the same equations as the Ritz method, and the orthogonality of the error and Cea's estimate hold. We can also make the error small with weighted residuals, and the five ways of choosing the weights give for the same cubic field an energy of error between \(9{,}165\) and \(39{,}10\) µJ. The theorem on the best approximation guarantees this only for the Galerkin choice, so the next section builds the trial fields from pieces of straight lines, which this theorem covers.
