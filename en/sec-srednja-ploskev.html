Chapter 22 Shells

22.1 The middle surface of a shell

The wall of a boiler is a piece of iron with a length and a width, and a thickness that is much smaller. How do we determine a point of this piece? With two numbers we say where on the wall it is, and with a third how deep in the wall. What is "the wall" if it has two surfaces, an inner and an outer one? We take the surface that lies halfway between them and measure everything else from it.

Let us draw a piece of the wall as seen obliquely from above. We have the inner and the outer surface with their edges. Between them is the iron, which along the top edge and at the right end we see in section as a grey area of thickness \(h\). Between them we draw the middle surface and divide it with two families of lines, counted by the parameters \(q_1\) and \(q_2\). A surface described by two parameters we know from chapter 4 (definition 4.61), with points \(\vek r(q_1, q_2)\) and normal vector \(\vek N\) ((4.33)). At a chosen point of the middle surface we draw the normal, the perpendicular to the surface with unit vector \(\vek n = \vek N/|\vek N|\) (definition 2.17). A point of the shell is thus on the normal through a point of the middle surface, at the distance \(|z|\) from it, with \(z > 0\) on the side to which \(\vek n\) points, and the thickness \(h\) is the distance between the surfaces measured along the normal, so \(-h/2 \le z \le h/2\). We write this as a definition.

Definition 22.1 Shell and its middle surface (lupina in njena srednja ploskev)

Let \(\vek r\) be a description of a surface \(S\) on a set \(M\) of the parameter plane \((q_1, q_2)\) (definition 4.61), which has continuous partial derivatives of the first and second order on an open set \(U \supseteq M\), and \(\vek n = \vek N/|\vek N|\) the unit normal of the surface. Let \(h > 0\) be a number with the unit of length. The map

\[\vek x(q_1, q_2, z) = \vek r(q_1, q_2) + z\,\vek n(q_1, q_2) , \qquad (q_1, q_2) \in M , \quad -\frac{h}{2} \le z \le \frac{h}{2} , \tag{22.1}\]

has a triple of numbers as input and a point of space as output. Let it be injective on this set of triples. The set of its points is a shell (lupina), \(S\) is its middle surface (srednja ploskev), \(h\) its thickness (debelina), and the points with \(z = \pm h/2\) form its faces (površini). A shell is thin if \(h\) is much smaller than every other dimension of the middle surface (radii, lengths, distances between edges).

Source: VK §11.1 (PDF p. 308) for a surface given by two parameters, and §12.1 (PDF p. 330) for the thickness and the requirement that the shell is thin in comparison with its other dimensions. The form \(\vek x = \vek r + z\,\vek n\) and the requirement of injectivity are ours: VK does not write them so.

The name middle surface is understandable: it lies halfway between the faces. The word shell for a thin curved wall is the same as for an egg. The definition is not as simple as it looks, in two places. First, we require the second derivative of the map \(\vek r\), because the normal \(\vek n\) must change continuously differentiably with \((q_1, q_2)\), as we will measure in section 22.2 how it turns. Second, injectivity is not always satisfied. The normals of a curved surface approach each other on one side, and if \(h/2\) is larger than the distance at which they meet, the shell overlaps itself. For a circular tube of radius \(R\) the normals meet on the axis, at a distance \(R\) from the surface, so injectivity holds for \(h/2 < R\). The tube of chapter 11 with radii 40 mm and 60 mm has a mean radius of 50 mm and \(h = 20\) mm, so \(h/(2R) = 0{,}2\) and the tube is still a shell by the definition, but not thin: \(h/R = 0{,}4\) is not "much smaller". The thin tube of the example in the introduction has \(h/R = 2/50 = 0{,}04\). How precisely "thin" is meant in a calculation, we fix in section 22.3 with the bound \(h/(2R)\) and with numbers. What matters here is only that we will describe the shell everywhere by its middle surface and its thickness.

What remains is to describe the middle surface alone: how it changes when we move along it. We need directions and lengths on it.

On the surface we take a net of lines, in which once the parameter \(q_2\) is constant and once \(q_1\), and look at one point. If we move along a line \(q_2 = \) const. with the parameter \(q_1\), the point \(\vek r(q_1, q_2)\) moves along a curve, its velocity is the vector \(\partial\vek r/\partial q_1\) and points along the line. Such a vector is the first tangent vector \(\vek a_1\) at the point. The same along a line \(q_1 = \) const. gives the second, \(\vek a_2\). Through the chosen point they span a plane that touches the surface. The word tangent is from the Latin tangere, to touch. The vector product \(\vek a_1 \times \vek a_2\) is perpendicular to both (lemma 2.31, item 4) and is the normal vector of the surface, which we know from (4.33). Between the vectors there is an angle \(\gamma\), and here we see that the net need not be rectangular.

Definition 22.2 Tangent vectors of a surface (tangentna vektorja ploskve)

Let \(\vek r\) be a description of a surface \(S\) (definition 4.61). The vectors

\[\vek a_1 = \frac{\partial\vek r}{\partial q_1} , \qquad \vek a_2 = \frac{\partial\vek r}{\partial q_2} \tag{22.2}\]

are the tangent vectors (tangentna vektorja) of the surface at the point \(\vek r(q_1, q_2)\), functions with a pair of parameters as input and a vector with the unit of length per unit of parameter as output. Their linear span is the tangent plane (tangentna ravnina). The normal vector is \(\vek N = \vek a_1 \times \vek a_2 \neq \vek 0\), so the vectors are linearly independent, and the normal \(\vek n = \vek N/|\vek N|\) is perpendicular to the tangent plane. If \(\vek a_1 \cdot \vek a_2 = 0\) at every point of \(M\), the coordinate lines are perpendicular at every point (theorem 2.10) and the coordinates \(q_1\), \(q_2\) are orthogonal on the surface.

Source: VK §11.1 (11.5) (PDF p. 309): the tangent vectors \(\vek r_{,\alpha}\) and \(\vek r_{,\beta}\) along the coordinate lines. The letters \(q_1\), \(q_2\) are ours, because in chapter 11 the coordinates are \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Every curve on the surface has its velocity in the tangent plane, which we prove a little further on, in the lemma on length, angle and area on a surface. But how do we compute from the tangent vectors the length of a curve that runs along the surface? In the plane of parameters \((q_1, q_2)\) the curve moves with velocity \((q_1', q_2')\), and the surface maps this pair to a vector. We need the numbers \(\vek a_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek a_{\mathrm{j}}\), which tell how far in space the point moves when it moves in the parameter plane.

Definition 22.3 First fundamental form of a surface (prva osnovna forma ploskve)

Let \(\vek a_1\) and \(\vek a_2\) be the tangent vectors of a surface \(S\) with description \(\vek r\) (definition 22.2). The first fundamental form (prva osnovna forma) is the function with a pair \((q_1, q_2)\) of \(M\) as input and a symmetric matrix with two rows and two columns as output

\[\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{12} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix} , \qquad a_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot \vek a_{\mathrm{j}} . \tag{22.3}\]

If \(\mat F\) is the matrix with three rows and two columns whose columns are \(\vek a_1\) and \(\vek a_2\) (the matrix of derivatives of the map \(\vek r\)), then \(\mat A = \mat F^\T\mat F\). For a pair of numbers \(\vek u = (u_1, u_2)\), \(\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek u = |u_1\vek a_1 + u_2\vek a_2|^2\) is a quadratic function of both numbers, called a quadratic form.

Source: VK §11.3 (11.10), (11.11) (PDF p. 313) writes \(a_{\mathrm{ik}} = \vek r_{,\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek r_{,\mathrm{k}}\) and the "first quadratic form" \(ds^2 = a_{\mathrm{ik}}\,d\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}\,d\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\). Instead of the arc length with differentials we use it on the velocity of a curve in lemma 22.5 (rule 47). The notation \(\mat A = \mat F^\T\mat F\) is ours.

The name is composed like this: the form is called quadratic because \(\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek u\) is quadratic in the numbers \(u_1\) and \(u_2\), namely \(\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek u = a_{11}u_1^2 + 2a_{12}u_1u_2 + a_{22}u_2^2\). It is fundamental because from it we compute everything that we measure on a surface with a ruler and a protractor: lengths, angles and areas. It is first because in section 22.2 we will need a second one, which measures how the surface curves in space and which the first does not know. Before we use it, let us say what the matrix \(\mat A\) certainly has.

Lemma 22.4 Determinant of the first form and change of description (determinanta prve forme in zamenjava opisa)

Let \(\vek r\) be a description of a surface \(S\) and \(\mat A\) its first fundamental form (definition 22.3).

  1. \(\det\mat A = a_{11}a_{22} - a_{12}^2 = |\vek N|^2 > 0\).
  2. Let \(\vek r_2 = \vek r\circ\tau\) be another description of the same surface, as in corollary 4.64, and \(\mat J\) the matrix of derivatives of the map \(\tau\) with two rows and columns. Then the first form of the description \(\vek r_2\) at the point \((\tilde q_1, \tilde q_2)\) is
    \[\mat A_2 = \mat J^\T\,\mat A\big(\tau(\tilde q_1, \tilde q_2)\big)\,\mat J . \tag{22.4}\]
Proof

We must show that the determinant equals the square of the normal vector and that under a change of description the matrix transforms with the matrix of derivatives of the change.

  1. By lemma 2.31, item 5, \(|\vek a_1\times\vek a_2|^2 = |\vek a_1|^2|\vek a_2|^2 - (\vek a_1\cdot\vek a_2)^2\), and the right side is \(a_{11}a_{22} - a_{12}^2\).
  2. Since \(\vek N \neq \vek 0\) by definition 4.61, \(|\vek N|^2 > 0\).
  3. The matrix of derivatives of the composite map \(\vek r\circ\tau\) is by corollary 4.44 the product of the matrices of derivatives: \(\mat F_2 = \mat F\,\mat J\), where \(\mat F\) is evaluated at the point \(\tau(\tilde q_1, \tilde q_2)\).
  4. Hence \(\mat A_2 = \mat F_2^\T\mat F_2 = \mat J^\T\mat F^\T\mat F\,\mat J = \mat J^\T\mat A\,\mat J\), because the order of a product is reversed under transposition.

Source: Item 1 is Lagrange's identity from lemma 2.31. Item 2 is our proof with the chain rule. VK §11.3 (PDF p. 313) does not treat a change of description.

By item 1, \(\sqrt{\det\mat A} = |\vek N|\), which is the factor in the surface integral (4.34): the area of a surface follows from the first form. Now we can say what we compute from it.

Lemma 22.5 Length, angle and area on a surface (dolžina, kot in ploščina na ploskvi)

Let \(\vek r\) be a description of a surface \(S\) with first form \(\mat A\), and let \(q_1(t)\) and \(q_2(t)\) be continuously differentiable functions on an interval \([\alpha, \beta]\) with values in \(M\). A curve on the surface is \(\vek c(t) = \vek r\big(q_1(t), q_2(t)\big)\). Then:

  1. \(\vek c'(t) = q_1'(t)\,\vek a_1 + q_2'(t)\,\vek a_2\), every velocity of a curve on the surface lies in the tangent plane, and \(|\vek c'(t)|^2 = \vek q'(t)^\T\mat A\,\vek q'(t)\) for \(\vek q' = (q_1', q_2')\).
  2. The length of the curve, defined as the integral of the speed \(|\vek c'|\) (as in definition 8.4, with three components instead of two), is
    \[L = \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{\vek q'(t)^\T\mat A\,\vek q'(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{22.5}\]
  3. Let a second curve on the surface have at the same point the velocity \(\vek d' = w_1\vek a_1 + w_2\vek a_2\) and the first \(\vek c' = u_1\vek a_1 + u_2\vek a_2\). The angle \(\gamma\) between the curves is the angle between these vectors, and
    \[\cos\gamma = \frac{\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek w}{\sqrt{\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek u}\,\sqrt{\vek w^\T\mat A\,\vek w}} . \tag{22.6}\]
  4. The area of the surface \(S\) is \(\int_{\mathrm{M}}\sqrt{\det\mat A}\,\mathrm{d}A\).
  5. Length, angle and area do not depend on the description of the surface: for a description \(\vek r_2 = \vek r\circ\tau\) from lemma 22.4 they give the same numbers.
Proof

We must show that the velocity of a curve is expressed with the tangent vectors, that all formulas follow from this, and that a change of description does not alter them.

  1. The components of the function \(\vek r\) are continuously differentiable, and so are the functions \(q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\), so by the chain rule (lemma 4.43, the version with an interval) \(\vek c'(t) = q_1'\,\partial\vek r/\partial q_1 + q_2'\,\partial\vek r/\partial q_2\) for each component.
  2. The square of the length of the velocity is \(|\vek c'|^2 = \vek c'\cdot\vek c' = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} q_{\mathrm{i}}'q_{\mathrm{j}}'\,\vek a_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q'^\T\mat A\,\vek q'\) by the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12). This proves item 1 and (22.5).
  3. The angle between nonzero vectors is the angle whose cosine is the scalar product divided by the product of the lengths (chapter 2). The scalar product \(\vek c'\cdot\vek d' = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}u_{\mathrm{i}}w_{\mathrm{j}}\,a_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek w\) as in step 2, and the lengths are the roots of \(\vek u^\T\mat A\,\vek u\) and \(\vek w^\T\mat A\,\vek w\).
  4. By (4.34) with \(f = 1\) the area is \(\int_{\mathrm{M}} |\vek N|\,\mathrm{d}A\), and \(|\vek N| = \sqrt{\det\mat A}\) by item 1 of lemma 22.4.
  5. The function \(\vek c(t)\), which gives the point of the curve in space, is the same whichever description of the surface we write it with, so it has the same velocity \(\vek c'(t)\). If \(\vek r_2 = \vek r\circ\tau\) and \(\vek q = \tau(\tilde{\vek q})\), then \(\vek c(t) = \vek r_2(\tilde{\vek q}(t))\) with exactly the same velocity, and the formula (22.5), which is a computation of this velocity, gives the same number. For the angle the velocities of both curves are the same vectors. For the area this is stated by corollary 4.64. The matrix \(\mat A_2 = \mat J^\T\mat A\,\mat J\) is only another way of writing the same value: \(\tilde{\vek q}'^\T\mat A_2\,\tilde{\vek q}' = (\mat J\tilde{\vek q}')^\T\mat A\,(\mat J\tilde{\vek q}') = \vek q'^\T\mat A\,\vek q'\), because \(\vek q' = \mat J\tilde{\vek q}'\) by the chain rule.

Source: VK §11.3 (PDF p. 313-314): the first quadratic form for lengths, areas and angles. VK uses the arc length with differentials \(ds\) and \(d\alpha\), we use the velocity of a curve. The extension of the definition of the length of a curve from the plane to space is ours: the justification with segments between points does not use the plane. We also do not have a proof that polygonal lines converge to this integral, even in the plane (see definition 8.4).

The lemma says that the first form is all we need about a surface to measure on it, and that this does not depend on the chosen description. We took the length of a curve in space as the integral of the speed, as in the plane: this is a simplification that the book makes also for a plane curve, and the full treatment (the limit of polygonal lines) is in KREYSZIG §9.5 (PDF p. 411).

When the coordinate lines are perpendicular at every point, \(a_{12} = 0\) and the first form has only two numbers, the diagonal entries \(a_{11}\) and \(a_{22}\). Their roots get a name of their own.

Definition 22.6 Scale factors of a surface (skalirna faktorja ploskve)

Let \(a_{12} = 0\) on \(M\) (orthogonal coordinates on the surface, definition 22.2). The scale factors (skalirna faktorja) of the surface are the functions \(A_1 = \sqrt{a_{11}} = |\vek a_1|\) and \(A_2 = \sqrt{a_{22}} = |\vek a_2|\), with the unit of length per unit of parameter, and \(\mat A = \mathrm{diag}(A_1^2, A_2^2)\). The unit vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{i}}/A_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(\vek n\) form a local frame (as in lemma 11.14).

Source: VK §11.3 (11.12), (11.13) (PDF p. 313) calls them the Lamé parameters \(A\) and \(B\). We write \(A_1\), \(A_2\) so as not to clash with the thickness \(h\) and with the scale factors \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) of definition 11.13, which are in space, not on a surface.

The scale factor \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) is like \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) of chapter 11: it tells how much straight length falls on a unit of the parameter. From section 22.3 on we will always choose the coordinates \(q_1\), \(q_2\) of the shell so that \(a_{12} = 0\). That such a choice is possible is not self-evident.

Let us first look at three surfaces that we will use most in the chapter, and for which the coordinate lines are perpendicular.

We draw a dome, made by rotating a plane curve about the axis. The position of the curve in one of the planes through the axis is a meridian, as on a globe, where the meridians connect the poles and all places on one meridian have noon at the same time. Every point of the curve describes in the rotation a circle that is parallel to the others, and we call it a parallel, like a parallel on the globe. Through the point \(P\) we take one meridian, to which \(\vek a_1\) is the tangent, and one parallel with the tangent \(\vek a_2\). At \(P\) we also draw the normal \(\vek n\), perpendicular to both vectors. The point \(P\) is at the distance \(\rho\) from the axis, the radius of the parallel. On the right is the meridian plane in section with the arc of the meridian and the point \(P\). In it is the angle \(\varphi\) between the axis and the line from the centre to \(P\), of length \(R\). The distance \(\rho\) is the leg opposite the angle \(\varphi\), so \(\rho = R\sin\varphi\). Since the meridian and the parallel are perpendicular at \(P\), the net is not oblique as in the previous figure.

Example 22.7 Surfaces of revolution: cylinder, sphere, cone (vrtenine: valj, krogla, stožec)

Let the meridian be a curve given by the functions \(\rho(q_1)\) (distance from the axis \(z\)) and \(\zeta(q_1)\) (height), and let \(\theta\) be the angle of rotation about the axis. A surface of revolution (vrtenina) has the description \(\vek r(q_1, \theta) = \big(\rho\cos\theta,\ \rho\sin\theta,\ \zeta\big)\). Then

\[\vek a_1 = (\rho'\cos\theta,\ \rho'\sin\theta,\ \zeta') , \quad \vek a_2 = \rho\,(-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0) , \quad \mat A = \begin{pmatrix} \rho'^2 + \zeta'^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \rho^2 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{22.7}\]

\(\vek N = \rho\,(-\zeta'\cos\theta,\ -\zeta'\sin\theta,\ \rho')\) and \(|\vek N| = \rho\sqrt{\rho'^2 + \zeta'^2}\). Perpendicularity follows from \(a_{12} = 0\). The scale factors (definition 22.6) are \(A_1 = \sqrt{\rho'^2 + \zeta'^2}\) and \(A_2 = \rho\). They tell how much length in space falls on a unit of the parameter along a coordinate line.

  1. Cylinder of radius \(R = 50\) mm: \(\rho = R\), \(\zeta = q_1 = z\), so \(A_1 = 1\), \(A_2 = 50\) mm, and \(\vek N = -R\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\) points toward the axis.
  2. Sphere of radius \(R = 50\) mm with the angle \(\varphi = q_1\) from the axis: \(\rho = R\sin\varphi\), \(\zeta = R\cos\varphi\), so \(A_1 = R = 50\) mm, \(A_2 = R\sin\varphi\), at \(\varphi = 60^\circ\) \(A_2 = 43{,}30\) mm, and \(\vek N = R^2\sin\varphi\,(\sin\varphi\cos\theta, \sin\varphi\sin\theta, \cos\varphi)\) points outward.
  3. Cone with half-angle \(\alpha = 30^\circ\) at the apex, \(s\) measured along the generator from the apex: \(\rho = s\sin\alpha\), \(\zeta = s\cos\alpha\), so \(A_1 = 1\), \(A_2 = s\sin\alpha\), at \(s = 20\) mm \(A_2 = 10\) mm and at \(s = 80\) mm \(40\) mm.

For the cylinder and the cone \(q_1\) is a length along the meridian, for the sphere an angle, so \(A_1\) equals 1 and \(R\) respectively. The direction of the normal depends on the order of the coordinates and on the direction of the meridian: for the cylinder with \(z\) upward it points toward the axis, for the sphere with \(\varphi\) from the axis outward (SymPy, num-01.py). Swapping the order of \(q_1\), \(\theta\) or reversing the meridian reverses the normal, as in definition 4.61.

For the cylinder the test with the first form is simple: the meridian is a straight line parallel to the axis, and \(\vek a_1\) is the unit vector along it. An arc of a parallel with the angle \(\Delta\theta\) has the length \(R\,\Delta\theta\), so \(A_2 = R\). For the sphere an arc of a meridian with the angle \(\Delta\varphi\) has the length \(R\,\Delta\varphi\), and an arc of a parallel with the angle \(\Delta\theta\) the length \(R\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta\), which is less the closer the parallel is to the axis. That the net of meridians and parallels is not uniform is shown by \(A_2 = R\sin\varphi\): at \(\varphi = 60^\circ\) the parallel is \(2\pi \cdot 43{,}30 = 272{,}07\) mm long, at \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) it is \(314{,}16\) mm.

All three surfaces of revolution have \(a_{12} = 0\), so the coordinate lines are perpendicular. Let us take one more example that does not have this property, and see what the first form tells about an oblique net.

We draw a cylinder of radius \(R\) and on it two families of helices. One family is blue and climbs with a rise \(c\) per radian, the other orange and descends equally steeply, with a rise \(-c\). Through the chosen point goes one helix of each family, the first with the tangent vector \(\vek a_1\), the second with the vector \(\vek a_2\). On the right we cut the cylinder along a generator and unroll it into a rectangle of width \(2\pi R\), because that is the circumference. Unrolling does not change lengths on the cylinder, and the helices of both families become straight lines. The two helices through the point become a rising and a falling straight line through the same point, with the vectors \(\vek a_1\) and \(\vek a_2\). The angle \(\gamma\) between them is the angle between the helices. One turn of a helix is the hypotenuse of a triangle with the horizontal leg \(2\pi R\) and the vertical leg \(2\pi c\).

Example 22.8 A helical grid on the cylinder (vijačna mreža na valju)

Let the cylinder be the one of example 22.7 with radius \(R = 50\) mm and let the description be \(\vek r(u, v) = \big(R\cos(u + v),\ R\sin(u + v),\ c\,(u - v)\big)\) with \(c = 25\) mm and angles \(u\), \(v\) in radians. The line \(v = \) const. is a helix with a rise \(c\) per radian, the line \(u = \) const. a helix with a rise \(-c\), so of the other hand. The tangent vectors are \(\vek a_1 = (-R\sin(u + v),\ R\cos(u + v),\ c)\) and \(\vek a_2 = (-R\sin(u + v),\ R\cos(u + v),\ -c)\), and

\[\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} R^2 + c^2 & R^2 - c^2 \\ R^2 - c^2 & R^2 + c^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3125 & 1875 \\ 1875 & 3125 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{mm}^2 . \tag{22.8}\]
  1. The coordinates are not orthogonal (definition 22.6): \(a_{12} = 1875\ \text{mm}^2 \neq 0\). The test of the definition fails, although the surface is the same cylinder as in example 22.7, where \(a_{12} = 0\).
  2. The angle between the lines by (22.6) with \(\vek u = (1, 0)\) and \(\vek w = (0, 1)\) is \(\cos\gamma = a_{12}/\sqrt{a_{11}a_{22}} = 1875/3125 = 0{,}6\), \(\gamma = 53{,}13^\circ\). Each helix climbs at the angle \(\arctan(c/R) = 26{,}57^\circ\) to the parallel, and \(2 \cdot 26{,}57^\circ = 53{,}13^\circ\), as it must be.
  3. The length of one turn of the first helix (\(u\) from 0 to \(2\pi\), \(v = 0\)) is by (22.5) \(\sqrt{3125}\cdot 2\pi = 55{,}90 \cdot 6{,}283 = 351{,}24\) mm. On the developed cylinder (right side of the figure) this is the hypotenuse of the triangle with legs \(2\pi R = 314{,}16\) mm and \(2\pi c = 157{,}08\) mm, \(\sqrt{314{,}16^2 + 157{,}08^2} = 351{,}24\) mm, the same number.
  4. The description \(\vek r_2(\theta, z) = (R\cos\theta, R\sin\theta, z)\), which is the description of the cylinder of example 22.7 with the order of the coordinates swapped, has \(\theta = u + v\), \(z = c(u - v)\), the matrix of derivatives \(\mat J = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ c & -c\end{pmatrix}\) with determinant \(-2c = -50\) mm and \(\mat A_2 = \mathrm{diag}(R^2, 1)\) in the order \((\theta, z)\). By lemma 22.4 \(\mat J^\T\mat A_2\,\mat J = \mat A\), which SymPy confirms. The area of the square \([0, \pi/2]^2\) in \((u, v)\) is by lemma 22.5 \(\sqrt{\det\mat A}\,(\pi/2)^2 = 2Rc\,(\pi/2)^2 = 6168{,}50\ \text{mm}^2\) and in \((\theta, z)\), where the image of the square is a parallelogram of area \(|\det\mat J|\,(\pi/2)^2 = 2c\,(\pi/2)^2\) and where \(\sqrt{\det\mat A_2} = R\), it is \(R\cdot 2c\,(\pi/2)^2\), again \(6168{,}50\ \text{mm}^2\).
  5. Since \(\det\mat J < 0\), the normal in the description \((u, v)\) points toward the axis, in the description \((\theta, z)\) outward. Lengths and areas do not change, the direction of the normal does, as corollary 4.64 says.

What we found: the first form distinguishes the two nets on the same surface, but each net gives the same length and area. For later expressions we need an orthogonal net, because \(\mat A\) will then be diagonal.

The last example is special. The net of meridians and parallels on the sphere is orthogonal, but the description fails at two points, the poles.

Example 22.9 The poles of a sphere and the apex of a cone (pola krogle in vrh stožca)

For the sphere of example 22.7 \(|\vek N| = R^2\sin\varphi\) (SymPy, num-01.py), so at \(\varphi = 0\), at the pole, where all meridians meet, \(\vek N = \vek 0\) and the description there does not satisfy definition 4.61. The parallel with \(\varphi = 0\) is a point: \(\vek a_2 = \vek 0\). The same holds for the apex of the cone at \(s = 0\) (\(A_2 = s\sin\alpha = 0\)). For the sphere this is a fault of the description, not of the surface: the sphere has a normal at the pole, pointing along the axis, and another description gives it, for example the graph \(z = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}\), as with the description \(\vek r(s, t) = (s^3, t, 0)\) of a flat square in chapter 4, which fails on the line \(s = 0\), although the plane has a normal there. For the cone it is different: at the apex there is no tangent plane, because it would have to contain the directions of all generators through the apex, and these do not lie in any plane, and a shell reaching to the apex has no unit normal of the middle surface. So we will always treat conical shells as a truncated cone and not take them up to the apex.

A shell is thus described by its middle surface, which at every point has two tangent vectors, a normal and a first form, and by its thickness \(h\). On three surfaces of revolution we computed the scale factors, on the helical grid we saw that the first form distinguishes a non-orthogonal net from an orthogonal one, and at the poles of the sphere that a description can fail where the surface itself is not at fault. One thing is missing: the tangent vectors say how a surface is stretched, but not how it curves in space. The cylinder with the description \(\vek r_2(\theta, z)\) has the first form \(\mathrm{diag}(R^2, 1)\), and exactly the same one has the flat rectangle with the description \((\theta, z) \mapsto (R\theta, z, 0)\), which is the cylinder unrolled into the plane. In space the two surfaces differ, but the first form does not tell them apart. The next section therefore introduces the second fundamental form and the curvature.

Notation in this chapter. The thickness of a shell is the total \(h\), not the half as in the generalized plane stress state of chapter 11 (\(-h \le z \le h\)) and not the scale factor \(h_{\mathrm{i}}\) (there, in space, here we write \(A_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the factors of the surface and \(H_{\mathrm{i}}\) for the factors in the space of the shell). The normal vector of the surface \(\vek N\) is bold, the internal forces of the shell in section 22.3 have indices (\(N_1\), \(N_2\)). The letter \(K\) is not used for the bulk modulus of chapter 11 but for the Gaussian curvature, and \(D\) is the bending stiffness of the shell, which we introduce in section 22.3, and is not the same as other letters \(D\) of the earlier chapters.
