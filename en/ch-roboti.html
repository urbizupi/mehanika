We start this chapter with machines whose joints are moved by motors: an industrial arm that welds, moves or assembles. We build on chapter 8, where we described a system of rigid bodies with constraints, degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates, and derived the equations of motion from virtual work. From chapter 2 we have the rotations of coordinate axes written as matrices, and the determinant. This chapter applies that knowledge to one family of machines.

The chapter's name has three words. The word robot comes from the Czech word robota, which means a worker. The Czech playwright Karel Čapek introduced it in his play Rossum's Universal Robots in 1920 (SPONG, introductory chapter). Today it names a machine that works with some degree of autonomy under computer control. Kinematics (kinematika) is the description of positions and motion of bodies without forces: it asks where the end of the arm is and how fast it moves, not what drives it. Dynamics (dinamika) adds forces and moments: it asks what torques the motors must give so that the arm moves as we want. The name therefore gives the order of work, positions first, then forces.

Why does this matter? A robot that welds a car body must bring the tip of the torch to the seam without any link hitting the workpiece. Whoever knows only the motor rotations does not know where the tip is. Whoever knows only the wanted point does not know what rotations it requires. There are also positions in which the arm can no longer move its end in some direction at all, however strong the motors are. The motors also decide what torques we must design for, and we compute these from the equations of motion.

The chapter promises five things. First, we will be able to say how many numbers fix the position of the arm and which points the end of the arm can reach at all. Second, we will write the position of every link with matrices, so that the position of the end of the arm is a product of matrices that we can read link by link. Third, we will solve the reverse task, which joint rotations give a given point, and show when a solution exists and how many there are. Fourth, we will connect the joint speeds with the speed of the end of the arm, then the forces at the end with the torques needed in the joints, and so find the positions where mobility is lost. Fifth, from chapter 8 we will derive the equations of motion of the arm and show which properties they always have.
