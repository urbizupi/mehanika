Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.4 The plane triangle: displacements and stiffness

In heat conduction the triangle had one unknown at each node, the temperature. Now we use the same triangle for displacements. Each node has two unknowns, the horizontal and the vertical displacement, and the question is the same as for the bar in chapter 7: what stiffness matrix does one element have, and what does that matrix say about it? The model is a thin steel plate (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) loaded in its own plane, that is plane stress (definition 11.32), or a long prism of constant cross-section, that is plane strain (definition 11.30). The idealizations are named: the material is linearly elastic and isotropic, strains are small (strain is measured by the small strain tensor, definition 1.115), and we look for the displacements in a family of fields fixed by the nodes (chapter 6). We take the triangle with nodes \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\) and \((10, 30)\) mm from section 15.1 and thickness \(t = 10\) mm. Its area is \(A = 600\ \text{mm}^2\).

15.4.1 Displacement in a triangle

We draw the triangle before loading. We number its nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) counterclockwise, so that the signed area \(\Delta\) of equation (15.2), half the determinant of the matrix with columns \((x_2 - x_1, y_2 - y_1)\) and \((x_3 - x_1, y_3 - y_1)\), is positive and equals the area \(A\) of the triangle (lemma 15.2, definition 15.3). The load moves each node, horizontally by \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and vertically by \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the values of the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the displacement field at node \(i\). We draw the horizontal and the vertical part of each displacement as a distance with end bars. So we get six numbers, the column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (u_1, v_1, u_2, v_2, u_3, v_3)^\T\). The new position of the triangle is fixed by the new nodes.

What happens between the nodes? For the displacement field we take the same as for temperature, a field whose sides stay straight, since on each side the displacement is a linear function of the position along it and two nodes fix it (lemma 15.7). By definition 15.5, with the functions \(L_1\), \(L_2\), \(L_3\),

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = L_1\,u_1 + L_2\,u_2 + L_3\,u_3 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = L_1\,v_1 + L_2\,v_2 + L_3\,v_3 . \tag{15.17}\]

Each function \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is an affine function of \(x\) and \(y\) (it equals \(1\) at node \(i\) and \(0\) at the other two, definition 15.3), so each displacement component is also affine, of the form \(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1\,x + \alpha_2\,y\). We want the strain of this field, since stress and energy follow from it.

Definition 15.21 Strain-displacement matrix of the triangle (matrika deformacij trikotnika)

Let the triangle have vertices \((x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\) numbered counterclockwise and area \(A > 0\). Write

\[b_1 = y_2 - y_3 , \quad b_2 = y_3 - y_1 , \quad b_3 = y_1 - y_2 , \qquad c_1 = x_3 - x_2 , \quad c_2 = x_1 - x_3 , \quad c_3 = x_2 - x_1 . \tag{15.18}\]

The strain vector in the plane is the column \(\{\varepsilon\} = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}})^\T\) with the engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), that is the Voigt vector of definition 3.18 without the places \(zz\), \(yz\) and \(xz\). The strain-displacement matrix (matrika deformacij) of the triangle is the \(3 \times 6\) matrix \(\mat B\),

\[\mat B = \frac{1}{2A}\begin{bmatrix} b_1 & 0 & b_2 & 0 & b_3 & 0 \\ 0 & c_1 & 0 & c_2 & 0 & c_3 \\ c_1 & b_1 & c_2 & b_2 & c_3 & b_3 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{15.19}\]

Source: ONATE1 (4.39) (PDF p. 160), COOK (3.4-10) (PDF p. 107). The symbols \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are as in ONATE1.

The matrix \(\mat B\) is in bold, like the matrix \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the bar in chapter 7, which is a row of two numbers there. Its role is the same: it multiplies the column of nodal displacements and gives the strain.

Lemma 15.22 Strain of the linear triangle (deformacija linearnega trikotnika)

Let the field (15.17) on a triangle of positive area \(A\) have the column of nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then:

  1. \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = b_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\), and \(b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 0\).
  2. The strain of the field is \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Every component is constant on the triangle.
Proof

We must show that the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) have exactly these derivatives, and put them into the definition of strain.

  1. By lemma 15.4, claim 1, \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(x, y) = (a_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\,x + c_{\mathrm{i}}\,y)/(2A)\), where \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are exactly the numbers (15.18) (there \(b_2\), \(c_2\), \(b_3\), \(c_3\) are obtained by the cyclic change of indices \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\)). Differentiating with respect to \(x\) and to \(y\) gives \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = b_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\).
  2. The sum \(b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = (y_2 - y_3) + (y_3 - y_1) + (y_1 - y_2) = 0\), and the same for \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). This is claim 1.
  3. By definition 1.115, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{i}}\,u_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} (c_{\mathrm{i}}\,u_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}})/(2A)\). These are the three rows of the product \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). The coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are numbers that depend only on the vertices, so the strains are constant.

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.2 (PDF p. 158-160), COOK §3.4 (PDF p. 107). The proof of claim 1 is not from the source.

We try it on our triangle. Here \(b = (-30,\ 30,\ 0)\) mm, \(c = (-30,\ -10,\ 40)\) mm and \(2A = 1200\ \text{mm}^2\), so

\[\mat B = \frac{1}{1200\ \text{mm}}\begin{bmatrix} -30 & 0 & 30 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -30 & 0 & -10 & 0 & 40 \\ -30 & -30 & -10 & 30 & 40 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Take the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), which the nodes give as \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (0,\ 0,\ 0{,}04,\ 0,\ 0{,}01,\ 0)^\T\) mm. The product \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 30 \cdot 0{,}04/1200 = 0{,}001\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), which is the strain of the field we chose. Since the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) give every affine field from its nodal values (lemma 15.8), the result is expected. The test shows that \(\mat B\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are arranged correctly.

Since the strain on the triangle is constant (lemma 15.22), the linear triangle for displacements is called the constant strain triangle (trikotnik s stalno deformacijo, abbreviation CST, COOK PDF p. 105). The element has the same strain at every point, so it cannot show how the strain changes across it. This will show in bending.

15.4.2 The stiffness matrix of the triangle

For the energy we need the relation between stress and strain in the plane. Chapter 11 gives the same form for both states, equation (11.43), with constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\) that are \(E\), \(\nu\) in plane stress and \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\), \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) in plane strain (corollary 11.34). We write it as a matrix.

Lemma 15.23 In-plane material stiffness matrix (ravninska togostna matrika materiala)

Let the material be isotropic with constants \(E > 0\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). In plane stress let \(E'\), \(\nu'\) equal \(E\), \(\nu\), in plane strain \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\). Let \(\{\sigma\} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})^\T\). Then:

  1. \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\) with the matrix
    \[\mat C = \frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \nu' & 0 \\ \nu' & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12\,(1 - \nu') \end{bmatrix} . \tag{15.20}\]
  2. The strain energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\).
  3. \(\mat C\) is symmetric and positive definite: \(\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} > 0\) for every \(\{\varepsilon\} \ne \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show that (15.20) inverts equation (11.43), that the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) loses terms, and that the quadratic form is positive.

  1. Equation (11.43) gives \(E'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(E'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We multiply the first by \(1\) and the second by \(\nu'\) and add: \(E'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) = (1 - \nu'^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Likewise \(E'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}) = (1 - \nu'^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). For shear, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{E'}{2\,(1 + \nu')}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\cdot\frac{1 - \nu'}{2} = \frac{E'}{2\,(1 + \nu')}\). These are the rows of (15.20).
  2. The energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 3.44). In plane stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), in plane strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (theorems 11.33 and 11.31), so the terms that remain are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \{\sigma\}^\T\{\varepsilon\}\).
  3. Symmetry can be read off (15.20). For positivity we write \(\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = \frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\big(\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 + (1 - \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2) + \tfrac12\,(1 - \nu')\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\big)\), which follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + 2\nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 = \nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 + (1 - \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\). The constants satisfy \(-1 < \nu' < 1\): in plane stress from \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), in plane strain from \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\), which for \(\nu \in (-1, \tfrac12)\) lies between \(-\tfrac12\) and \(1\). For \(\nu' \ge 0\) all terms are \(\ge 0\) and the sum is zero only for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), since \(1 - \nu' > 0\). For \(\nu' < 0\), \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \le 2\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\), because the difference is \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \ge 0\), and since \(\nu'\) is negative, \(\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \ge 2\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\). The first two terms together are then \(\ge (1 + \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2) > 0\) unless \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.5)-(4.7) (PDF p. 144-145), COOK (3.1-3) (PDF p. 108). The proof of positivity is not from the source.

For steel in plane stress \(E' = 196\) GPa and \(\nu' = 0{,}3\), so \(\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} 215{,}385 & 64{,}615 & 0 \\ 64{,}615 & 215{,}385 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 75{,}385 \end{bmatrix}\) GPa. In plane strain \(E^* = 215{,}385\) GPa, \(\nu^* = 0{,}4286\) and

\[\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda + 2\mu & \lambda & 0 \\ \lambda & \lambda + 2\mu & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \mu \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 263{,}846 & 113{,}077 & 0 \\ 113{,}077 & 263{,}846 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 75{,}385 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{GPa} ,\]

which agrees with definition 3.10 (\(\lambda = 113{,}077\) GPa, \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa) and with matrix (3.22), which for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) has exactly this block. In this chapter the matrix is written \(\mat C\) as in chapter 3, the sources (ONATE1, COOK) write \(\mat D\) or \([\mat E]\). The other symbols: bold \(\mat B\) is the strain-displacement matrix, \(t\) the thickness (\(t\) is time in chapter 14, there is no clash in this section), \(A\) the area of the triangle (\(A\) is a cross-section in chapter 7). By claim 1 of the lemma the stress \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) on the triangle is also a single value, constant like the strain, so the element cannot show how the stress changes across it either.

Lemma 15.24 Stiffness matrix of the constant strain triangle (togostna matrika trikotnika s stalno deformacijo)

Let a triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) counterclockwise, area \(A\) and thickness \(t\) (in plane strain the length of a slice of the prism) be made of a material with matrix \(\mat C\) (lemma 15.23). Then:

  1. The strain energy of the element is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = t\,A\,\mat B^\T\,\mat C\,\mat B . \tag{15.21}\]
  2. The \(6 \times 6\) matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15).
  3. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the stiffness matrix of the element (definition 7.4): the forces with which the surroundings hold the element in the state \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
Proof

We must show that the energy is the integral of a constant function and then read off the properties of the matrix.

  1. The body of the element is the prism \(T \times [0, t]\), with \(T\) the triangle. The map \(\Phi(s, \lambda, z) = (P_1 + s\,\vek u + \lambda\,\vek v,\ z)\) with \(\vek u = P_2 - P_1\) and \(\vek v = P_3 - P_1\) is injective, has the constant Jacobian determinant \(2A \ne 0\) and maps the prism \(T_0 \times [0, t]\) over the triangle \(T_0\) of the proof of lemma 15.2 onto \(T \times [0, t]\). The prism \(T_0 \times [0, t]\) is a body between the graphs \(0 \le \lambda \le 1 - s\) over the rectangle \(0 \le s \le 1\), \(0 \le z \le t\) (definition 4.15). So for a continuous function \(f\) that does not depend on \(z\), by theorems 4.59 and 4.33, in space and then in the plane, \(\int_{\mathrm{T} \times [0, \mathrm{t}]} f\,\mathrm{d}V = 2A\,t\int_{\mathrm{T}_0} f\big(P_1 + s\,\vek u + \lambda\,\vek v\big)\,\mathrm{d}A = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\), as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 15.9. By definition 4.85, therefore, \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \int w\,\mathrm{d}V = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} w\,\mathrm{d}A\). By lemma 15.22 and lemma 15.23, \(w = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is constant on \(T\), so the integral equals the value times the area \(A\) (lemma 4.32), and \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,(t A\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The transpose of a product of matrices is the product of the transposes in reverse order, so \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^\T = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C^\T\mat B = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), since \(\mat C\) is symmetric. For every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} \ge 0\) with \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(tA > 0\) (lemma 15.23, claim 3).
  3. By theorem 4.92 the force in the direction of the displacement \(d_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\partial U_{\mathrm{e}}/\partial d_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{j}}\), since \(U_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a quadratic function and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric, as for the bar (lemma 7.5).

Source: ONATE1 (4.52), (4.61)-(4.63) (PDF p. 162-165), COOK §3.4 (PDF p. 107).

We compute the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) for our triangle with SymPy and NumPy from equations (15.19) and (15.20) for plane stress (unit \(10^6\) N/mm, rows and columns \(u_1, v_1, u_2, v_2, u_3, v_3\)):

\[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}0904 & 0{,}5250 & -0{,}7135 & -0{,}2019 & -0{,}3769 & -0{,}3231 \\ 0{,}5250 & 1{,}0904 & -0{,}1481 & -0{,}0135 & -0{,}3769 & -1{,}0769 \\ -0{,}7135 & -0{,}1481 & 0{,}8391 & -0{,}1750 & -0{,}1256 & 0{,}3231 \\ -0{,}2019 & -0{,}0135 & -0{,}1750 & 0{,}3724 & 0{,}3769 & -0{,}3590 \\ -0{,}3769 & -0{,}3769 & -0{,}1256 & 0{,}3769 & 0{,}5026 & 0 \\ -0{,}3231 & -1{,}0769 & 0{,}3231 & -0{,}3590 & 0 & 1{,}4359 \end{bmatrix} .\]

We test it on the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,x\). The stress is \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = (215{,}385,\ 64{,}615,\ 0)^\T\) MPa, since by \(\mat C\) transverse tensions \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 64{,}6\) MPa are needed for a strain in the \(x\) direction alone, as for the steel in chapter 3. The forces of the surroundings are \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (-32\,308,\ -9692,\ 32\,308,\ -3231,\ 0,\ 12\,923)^\T\) N. Their sum in the \(x\) direction and in the \(y\) direction is zero and the sum of moments about the origin is zero, as it must be for a body in equilibrium (Newton). The energy \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 646{,}15\) N mm equals \(\tfrac12\,t A\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = \tfrac12 \cdot 6000\ \text{mm}^3 \cdot 215\,385\ \text{N/mm}^2 \cdot 10^{-6} = 646{,}15\) N mm, since \(\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = C_{11}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2\).

We put the element into a structure as a bar. Node \(m\) has the unknowns \(2m - 1\) and \(2m\). A triangle with nodes \(5\), \(9\) and \(12\) has the row \((9, 10, 17, 18, 23, 24)\) of the connection table (definition 7.10), and the matrix \(\mat K\) is the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (theorem 7.13). That the solution of the equilibrium equations is exactly the solution of the Ritz method on the family of fields made of triangles is stated by corollary 15.16. The corollary is written for a body in space. In plane strain the plane energy is exactly the energy in space of a slice of thickness \(t\). In plane stress the argument goes the same way with the matrix (15.20), which by lemma 15.23 is symmetric and positive definite.

15.4.3 Rigid motions and what the triangle can do

The properties of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) mean physical properties of the element. What costs the element no energy? Anyone who has held a plate in the hands knows: if we move it or turn it as a whole we do not deform it, so there is no stress and no force is needed (a free body in equilibrium, Newton). The lemma says that the element has zero energy exactly in these three motions and in no other.

Lemma 15.25 Rigid motions of the constant strain triangle (togi gibi trikotnika s stalno deformacijo)

Let \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the matrix (15.21) of a triangle with area \(A > 0\). Then:

  1. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly when the numbers \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are the values at the nodes of a field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a_1 - \omega\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = a_2 + \omega\,x\), for some real \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(\omega\).
  2. There are three independent such columns (a translation in the \(x\) direction, a translation in the \(y\) direction and a rotation), so the kernel of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) has dimension \(3\) and only three of the six columns of the matrix are independent.
  3. Let \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) be prescribed and the component of the displacement of node \(2\) perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\) be zero. Then \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} \ne \vek 0\) that satisfies these three conditions, so the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive definite on the remaining three unknowns.
Proof

We must show that zero energy means zero strain and that the fields with zero strain are exactly these three.

  1. By lemma 7.16 and claim 2 of lemma 15.24, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = 0\). Since \(\mat C\) is positive definite, this holds exactly when \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\).
  2. The field (15.17) is affine, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + \alpha_2 y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 y\). Its strain is \((\alpha_1,\ \beta_2,\ \alpha_2 + \beta_1)\), so it is zero exactly for \(\alpha_1 = \beta_2 = 0\) and \(\alpha_2 = -\beta_1\). With \(\omega = \beta_1\) the field is \((\alpha_0 - \omega\,y,\ \beta_0 + \omega\,x)\), which is claim 1. Conversely a field of this form is affine and has strain zero.
  3. By lemma 15.8 the map that gives an affine field its values at the three nodes is a bijection between affine fields and columns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (six numbers on each side). The fields \((a_1 - \omega\,y,\ a_2 + \omega\,x)\) form a three-dimensional subspace, so the kernel is three-dimensional. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a symmetric \(6 \times 6\) matrix, so exactly \(6 - 3 = 3\) of its columns are independent.
  4. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) satisfy the three conditions of claim 3 and let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\). By lemma 7.16, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), so by steps 1 and 2 \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a rigid motion with some \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(\omega\). The displacement of node \(2\) is the displacement of node \(1\) plus \(\omega\,(-(y_2 - y_1),\ x_2 - x_1)\), so for \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) it is exactly \(\omega\,(-(y_2 - y_1),\ x_2 - x_1)\). This vector is perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\) and has length \(|\omega|\,\ell\), with \(\ell\) the length of the side. Since its component perpendicular to the side is prescribed as zero, \(\omega = 0\). Then the displacement of every node equals that of node \(1\), so it is zero, and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\). Hence \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) for every nonzero \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the three conditions (claim 2 of lemma 15.24), which is the positive definiteness of the matrix on the remaining three unknowns.

Source: The proof is not from the source. The same for a space truss is stated by lemma 7.35.

A translation, the other translation and a rotation about node \(1\) change neither the lengths of the sides nor the angles (figure 15.15, all motions are drawn too large so that they can be seen). The fourth picture shows the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = e\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), where the side along \(x\) becomes longer: this is a deformation and its energy is not zero. For our triangle the field with \(e = 0{,}001\) has energy \(646{,}15\) N mm, as we saw.

The lemma says what the supports must do: they must prevent all three motions. Otherwise the matrix of the structure is not positive definite and the system has no single solution. For our triangle with \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) and \(v_2 = 0\) prescribed (the displacement of node \(2\) perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\), which lies on the \(x\) axis) the matrix on \(u_2\), \(u_3\), \(v_3\) has the eigenvalues \(0{,}447\), \(0{,}750\) and \(1{,}580\cdot 10^6\) N/mm, all positive. With the wrong choice \(u_1 = v_1 = u_2 = 0\), where we prescribed the component along the side, one eigenvalue on \(v_2\), \(u_3\), \(v_3\) is zero: the rotation about node \(1\) moves node \(2\) perpendicular to the side and the prescription \(u_2 = 0\) does not catch it. The hypothesis of the lemma that the prescribed component is not along the side therefore cannot be dropped.

The condition \(A > 0\) is needed by the lemma for \(\mat B\). If all three nodes lie on a line, \(A = 0\) and \(\mat B\) does not exist. An almost such triangle is already bad: with node \(3\) at the height \(30\), \(3\) and \(0{,}3\) mm the ratio of the largest and the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is \(2{,}5\), \(172\) and \(1{,}7\cdot 10^{4}\) in turn. A thin triangle therefore makes the matrix badly conditioned (mesh quality is a subject of chapter 17).

15.4.4 Consistent forces on the triangle

The element has forces only at the nodes, but the load is distributed, as for the bar (definition 7.7). On the triangle we meet the same demand, that the forces do the same work as the load on every field of the family.

Lemma 15.26 Consistent nodal forces on the triangle (konsistentne sile na trikotniku)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the field (15.17) of a triangle of thickness \(t\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) its column. The consistent nodal forces of a load are the numbers \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = (f_{\mathrm{ix}}, f_{\mathrm{iy}})\) at node \(i\) for which the work of the load on \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \vek f_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot (u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then:

  1. A body force \(\vek b\) (force per unit volume, constant) gives \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = \tfrac13\,t A\,\vek b\) at every node. The sum of the forces is the whole weight \(t A\,\vek b\).
  2. A load \(\vek p\) (force per unit area of the lateral surface) with a constant value on the side \(1\)-\(2\) of length \(\ell\) gives \(\vek f_1 = \vek f_2 = \tfrac12\,t\,\ell\,\vek p\) and \(\vek f_3 = \vek 0\). If \(\vek p\) changes linearly from \(\vek p_1\) at node \(1\) to \(\vek p_2\) at node \(2\), then \(\vek f_1 = t\,\ell\,(2\vek p_1 + \vek p_2)/6\) and \(\vek f_2 = t\,\ell\,(\vek p_1 + 2\vek p_2)/6\).
  3. A point force \(\vek F\) at the point \(P\) of the triangle gives \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\,\vek F\).
Proof

We must show that the work of the load splits into a sum over the nodes and compute the integrals of the shape functions.

  1. The work of the body force is \(\int \vek b\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}V = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} \vek b\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} t\,\vek b\cdot(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (step 1 of the proof of lemma 15.24, linearity of the integral). By lemma 15.9 with exponents \((1, 0, 0)\), \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 1!\cdot 2A/3! = A/3\). So \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = tA\,\vek b/3\).
  2. On the side \(1\)-\(2\), \(L_3 = 0\) (lemma 15.7), \(L_1 = 1 - s/\ell\) and \(L_2 = s/\ell\) with \(s \in [0, \ell]\) (affine functions with the values \(1\) and \(0\) at the ends). The work is \(t\int_0^\ell \vek p\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}s\), which for constant \(\vek p\) gives by the fundamental theorem of calculus \(f_1 = t\,\vek p\int_0^\ell (1 - s/\ell)\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,t\,\ell\,\vek p\), and the same for \(f_2\). The linear case is the same computation as in equation (7.9) for the bar, with \(t\,\vek p\) in place of \(q\).
  3. The work of the force \(\vek F\) on the field is \(\vek F\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\,\vek F\cdot(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.64)-(4.70) (PDF p. 166-167), COOK §3.11 (3.11-2) (PDF p. 126-127). Claims 1 and 2 are as in ONATE1, claim 3 as in COOK. The proof is not from the source.

The weight of the element for steel (\(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\), \(g = 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2\)) is \(\rho g\,t A = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 6\cdot 10^{-6}\) N \(= 0{,}459\) N. Each node gets a third, \(0{,}153\) N downward, since \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) is one third of the area. On the right is the same triangle with a constant pressure \(p = 1\) MPa that pushes upward on its lower side of length \(\ell = 40\) mm. The total force is \(p\,t\,\ell = 1\cdot 10\cdot 40 = 400\) N, and the nodes get \(200\) N each. For a pressure that grows linearly from \(0\) at node \(1\) to \(1\) MPa at node \(2\), node \(1\) gets \(66{,}67\) N and node \(2\) gets \(133{,}3\) N, a sum of \(200\) N, since the mean pressure is \(0{,}5\) MPa. The node on the side of the larger pressure gets more, as for the bar.

The forces of the surroundings on the sides that two neighbours share do not appear in the assembled structure, because they cancel (theorem 15.15). So we compute only with the loads on the outer boundary. ONATE1 states the same (PDF p. 164).

15.4.5 Temperature on the triangle

In plane stress, by chapter 3 the total strain is the sum of the elastic and the thermal strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon^{\mathrm{el}}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (theorem 3.15). For the bar we called \(\varepsilon_0 = \alpha\,\Delta T\) the initial strain (definition 7.48). We do the same on the triangle.

Lemma 15.27 Heated triangle (segret trikotnik)

Let the triangle be heated by \(\Delta T\), uniformly, relative to a reference temperature, and let the material have the coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\). Let \(\{\varepsilon_0\} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) in plane stress and \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) in plane strain. Then:

  1. The stress in the plane is \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not change, the right-hand side gets the column \(\vek f_0 = t A\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\), whose sum of components in the \(x\) direction and in the \(y\) direction is zero.
  3. In plane stress, for a triangle with node \(1\) at the origin and the side \(1\)-\(2\) on the \(x\) axis, the triangle with only \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) and \(v_2 = 0\) prescribed has the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,y\) and zero stress.
  4. If all six nodal displacements are zero, the stress is \(-\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - \nu)\) in plane stress and \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\) in plane strain.
Proof

We must show how temperature enters the relation between stress and strain and compute the energy.

  1. In plane stress the equations for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) in theorem 3.15 are the same as in equation (11.43) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) on the left. From lemma 15.23 therefore \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T)\).
  2. In plane strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and from theorem 3.15, \(0 = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), as in chapter 11. We put this into \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\) and get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big) + (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) (checked with SymPy). The shear equation does not change. This is (11.43) with \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) and the extra term \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) in the normal components.
  3. The energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})^\T\mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})\), since this is the elastic part. Multiplied out, \(w = \tfrac12\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} + \text{const.}\) By lemma 15.22, \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and everything is constant, so the energy of the element is \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek f_0 + \text{const.}\) with \(\vek f_0 = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\). The term \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek f_0\) is like the work of forces on the right-hand side, so we add \(\vek f_0\) to the right-hand side, as for the bar (lemma 7.49).
  4. Let \(\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the column with ones at the places \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and zeros at the places \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\). These are the nodal values of the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), which is a rigid motion (lemma 15.25), so \(\mat B\,\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\) and the sum of the components of \(\vek f_0\) in the \(x\) direction is \(\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}}^\T\vek f_0 = tA\,(\mat B\,\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}})^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} = 0\). The same in the \(y\) direction.
  5. The field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,y\) is affine, its nodal values \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) have by lemma 15.22 the strain \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \{\varepsilon_0\}\) (in plane stress), so \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon_0\} - \{\varepsilon_0\}) = \vek 0\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} = \vek f_0\). This \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is therefore a solution and has \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) (the origin) and \(v_2 = 0\) (\(y_2 = 0\)). The reduced matrix is positive definite by lemma 15.25, so the solution is unique.
  6. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), \(\{\varepsilon\} = \vek 0\) and \(\{\sigma\} = -\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\). In plane stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - \nu)\) and the same for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) (the shear is zero). In plane strain \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(\lambda + 2\mu + \lambda)\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -2\,(\lambda + \mu)\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), and since by definition 3.10 \(\lambda + \mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.17)-(4.18), (4.71)-(4.73) (PDF p. 147, 167), Examples 4.2 and 4.3 (PDF p. 150-154). The proof is not from the source.

The numbers. For our triangle with \(\Delta T = 30\) K and \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), in plane stress \(\{\varepsilon_0\} = 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) and the forces are \(\vek f_0 = (-18\,900,\ -18\,900,\ 18\,900,\ -6300,\ 0,\ 25\,200)^\T\) N, which have the sum zero in both directions. For the free triangle with \(u_1 = v_1 = v_2 = 0\) the displacement of node \(2\) is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\cdot 40\ \text{mm} = 18\ \mu\)m and that of node \(3\) is \((4{,}5,\ 13{,}5)\ \mu\)m, that is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the stress is zero. If we hold all the nodes, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -126{,}0\) MPa in plane stress and \(-220{,}5\) MPa in plane strain, where also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -220{,}5\) MPa. We compare with the bar between walls, \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -88{,}2\) MPa. Why is the stress in the plate larger? The bar cannot extend in the \(x\) direction, but in the \(y\) direction it widens freely, by \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 5{,}85\cdot 10^{-4}\). A plate with two held directions can widen neither in the \(x\) nor in the \(y\) direction, so it is compressed in both. Through \(\nu\) the compression in the \(y\) direction widens it in the \(x\) direction, so the compression in the \(x\) direction must be even larger: in plane stress by the factor \(1/(1 - \nu) = 1{,}43\). In plane strain the third direction \(z\) is also held, the plate is compressed in all three directions and the factor is \(1/(1 - 2\nu) = 2{,}5\).

The free triangle loses its stress because the thermal strain \(\{\varepsilon_0\}\) belongs to a displacement field that is in the family (an affine one): the triangle expands as a whole, like the bar in chapter 7. Also for a temperature that changes linearly over a free plate, \(\Delta T = c_0 + c_1\,x + c_2\,y\), the exact stress is zero. The strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) is then the strain of the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,(c_0\,x + c_1\,(x^2 - y^2)/2 + c_2\,x\,y)\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,(c_0\,y + c_1\,x\,y + c_2\,(y^2 - x^2)/2)\), which we check by differentiation. The constant strain triangle, however, does not capture a linearly changing \(\{\varepsilon_0\}\) exactly, since it has a single strain over the whole element. Thermal stresses for a temperature that changes over the body are treated in chapter 14.

15.4.6 The triangle cannot bend

We try the triangle on a task where the strain is not constant: the cantilever of chapter 11 (\(L = 1\) m, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(F = 500\) N), \(I = 106\,667\ \text{mm}^4\). At the clamp the moment is \(500\) N m and the curvature is \(\kappa = M/(EI) = 0{,}02392\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The exact strain in pure bending is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa\,y\), linear in height, at the edge \(y = 20\) mm it is \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) and the stress is \(93{,}75\) MPa. The triangle gives a constant value. What does that mean on one small piece?

Take a cell \(100 \times 20\) mm next to the clamp, \(0 \le x \le 100\) mm and \(0 \le y \le 20\) mm. The exact field of pure bending is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \kappa\,x\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\kappa\,(x^2 + \nu\,y^2)/2\) (its strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa\,y\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\kappa\,y\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \kappa\,x - \kappa\,x = 0\), so the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\kappa\,y\) and the others zero). We cut the cell into two triangles. The exact field curves the upper and lower edges, and the sides stay straight. Take the triangle \((0, 0)\), \((a, 0)\), \((0, h)\) with \(a = 100\) mm and \(h = 20\) mm and give it the nodal values of the exact field. Node \(1\) stays, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \kappa\,x\,y\) is zero at all three nodes (at each one \(x = 0\) or \(y = 0\)), node \(2\) drops by \(v_2 = -\kappa\,a^2/2 = -0{,}1196\) mm and node \(3\) by \(v_3 = -\kappa\,\nu\,h^2/2 = -0{,}0014\) mm. The field of the triangle with these nodal values therefore has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = v_3/h = -7{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = v_2/a = -1{,}196\cdot 10^{-3}\), while the exact \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The bending strain has disappeared in the nodal values, because all three \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and a shear has appeared that the exact field does not have. It is \(7{,}5\) times the exact value of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the centroid of the triangle (\(1{,}59\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(y = h/3\)), the corresponding stress \(G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is \(90{,}1\) MPa, almost as large as the bending stress \(93{,}75\) MPa at the edge of the cell.

The thickness of the cell is the width of the cantilever's cross-section, \(t = 20\) mm. The energy of the cell given by the two triangles is \(2626\) N mm, the exact one \(298{,}9\) N mm, a ratio of \(8{,}8\) (the same for both diagonals). The shear part \(\tfrac12\,tA\,G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\) of the two triangles together is about \(2160\) N mm, more than four fifths, so the cell bends as if it were almost nine times as stiff. The reason is twofold: a constant strain cannot follow \(\kappa\,y\), and the nodal values give, instead of the bending strain, a shear that uses up energy. COOK describes the same on the element at the lower left edge (PDF p. 108): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = u_2/a\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = v_2/a\), "the element displays transverse shear strain, which should not be present". For its mesh COOK gives displacements and stresses about one quarter of the true values (PDF p. 108).

The whole cantilever of chapter 11, cut into \(10 \times 2\) cells, each cut along a diagonal into two triangles, has \(60\) unknowns (the displacement at the clamp is prescribed by the exact solution). The clamp is at the left, the force at the right end acts as a parabolic shear stress, which we replace for the elements by consistent forces. The exact displacement of the free end by Airy is \(-7{,}9719\) mm, the mesh gives \(-0{,}914\) mm, which is \(0{,}115\) of the exact value. The figure shows the exact deformed axis and the deformed mesh of triangles, both enlarged \(20\) times.

With finer cells the mesh is better but slowly: with \(20 \times 4\) cells (\(200\) unknowns) the ratio is \(0{,}341\), with \(40 \times 8\) (\(720\) unknowns) \(0{,}673\). With each doubling of the number of cells in both directions there are three to four times as many unknowns, and the difference from the exact value falls only from \(0{,}885\) to \(0{,}659\) and to \(0{,}327\). COOK (PDF p. 107) says the same: correct results are approached as a mesh is refined, but slowly. What an element needs in order to converge under refinement is shown by the patch test (section 15.7), and we do not give a proof of convergence here. For plane strain of an almost incompressible material (\(\nu \to \tfrac12\)) a mesh of triangles can lock (COOK, PDF p. 108): that is the subject of the section on locking.
