Chapter 15 Continuum finite elements

15.4 The plane triangle: displacements and stiffness

In heat conduction the triangle had one unknown at each node, the temperature. Now we use the same triangle for displacements. Each node has two unknowns, the horizontal and the vertical displacement, and the question is the same as for the bar in chapter 7: what stiffness matrix does one element have, and what does that matrix say about it? The model is a thin steel plate (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) loaded in its own plane, that is plane stress (definition 11.36), or a long prism of constant cross-section, that is plane strain (definition 11.32). We take the triangle with nodes \((0, 0)\), \((40, 0)\) and \((10, 30)\) mm from section 15.1 and thickness \(t = 10\) mm. Its area is \(A = 600\ \text{mm}^2\).

The model rests on four assumptions. For each we say why we may make it, when it fails and what it does to the result.

  1. The material is linearly elastic and isotropic. For steel at small strains measurements show this (chapter 3). The assumption fails when the material deforms permanently, and for materials with a marked direction, such as wood. Effect: the stress is the strain multiplied by one matrix of numbers, so the stiffness matrix of the element is constant and we compute it once.
  2. Strains are small and we measure them by the small strain tensor (definition 1.121). In our test the strain will be \(0{,}001\): the side of length \(40\) mm becomes longer by \(0{,}04\) mm. The small strain tensor drops the quadratic term, which for this stretch is \(0{,}001^2/2 = 5\cdot 10^{-7}\), one two-thousandth of the strain. The assumption fails for large stretches and rotations (a rubber band, a bent ruler), where the quadratic terms are no longer small.
  3. The state is plane. Plane stress holds for a plate that is thin compared with its sizes in the plane and loaded only in that plane, plane strain for a long prism far from its ends. For a thick plate and near the ends of the prism the assumption fails and an element in space is needed (section 15.10). Effect: a node has two unknowns instead of three.
  4. We look for the displacements only among the fields fixed by the nodes (chapter 6). This assumption is not physical but computational, and it has the largest effect. Where the true field is of this form the result is exact. Where it is not, the structure is too stiff in the computation: the cantilever made of triangles at the end of this section gives a deflection of \(0{,}914\) mm instead of the true \(7{,}97\) mm.

15.4.1 Displacement in a triangle

We draw the triangle before loading. We number its nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) counterclockwise, so that the signed area \(\Delta\) of equation (15.2), half the determinant of the matrix with columns \((x_2 - x_1, y_2 - y_1)\) and \((x_3 - x_1, y_3 - y_1)\), is positive and equals the area \(A\) of the triangle (lemma 15.2, definition 15.3). The load moves each node, horizontally by \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and vertically by \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the values of the components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) of the displacement field at node \(i\). We draw the horizontal and the vertical part of each displacement as a distance with end bars. So we get six numbers, the column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (u_1, v_1, u_2, v_2, u_3, v_3)^\T\). The new position of the triangle is fixed by the new nodes.

What happens between the nodes? For the displacement field we take the same as for temperature, a field whose sides stay straight, since on each side the displacement is a linear function of the position along it and two nodes fix it (lemma 15.7). By definition 15.5, with the functions \(L_1\), \(L_2\), \(L_3\),

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y) = L_1\,u_1 + L_2\,u_2 + L_3\,u_3 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y) = L_1\,v_1 + L_2\,v_2 + L_3\,v_3 . \tag{15.17}\]

Each function \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is an affine function of \(x\) and \(y\) (it equals \(1\) at node \(i\) and \(0\) at the other two, definition 15.3), so each displacement component is also affine, of the form \(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1\,x + \alpha_2\,y\). We want the strain of this field, since stress and energy follow from it.

First we write in one line what we are going to do with the field. The strain comes from the displacement by differentiation (definition 1.121): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\). We arrange these derivatives in a matrix \(\boldsymbol\partial\) with three rows and two columns. Its entries are not numbers but the instructions "differentiate with respect to \(x\)" and "differentiate with respect to \(y\)", which act on the displacement components to its right:

\[\{\varepsilon\} = \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} \\ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} \\ \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & 0 \\ 0 & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u_{\mathrm{x}} \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} \end{bmatrix} = \boldsymbol\partial\,\vek u . \tag{15.18}\]

The field (15.17) can be written just as briefly. We multiply the six numbers \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) by the matrix of shape functions \(\mat N\) of size \(2 \times 6\) (definition 7.2):

\[\vek u = \begin{bmatrix} u_{\mathrm{x}} \\ u_{\mathrm{y}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} L_1 & 0 & L_2 & 0 & L_3 & 0 \\ 0 & L_1 & 0 & L_2 & 0 & L_3 \end{bmatrix} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat N\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{15.19}\]

The first row gives \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) from the horizontal displacements \(u_1\), \(u_2\), \(u_3\), the second gives \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) from the vertical ones \(v_1\), \(v_2\), \(v_3\). We put the second equation into the first:

\[\{\varepsilon\} = \boldsymbol\partial\,\vek u = \boldsymbol\partial\,\mat N\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} , \qquad \mat B = \boldsymbol\partial\,\mat N . \tag{15.20}\]

The numbers \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) do not depend on \(x\) and \(y\), so the derivatives act only on the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\). The matrix \(\mat B\) is therefore the matrix \(\mat N\) in which each function \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is replaced by its derivative: in the first row the derivative with respect to \(x\), in the second with respect to \(y\), in the third both, in swapped order. The definition and the lemma below compute these derivatives for the triangle. The line \(\mat B = \boldsymbol\partial\,\mat N\) holds for every plane element, only the functions in \(\mat N\) are different (the quadrilateral in section 15.5). The sources write it the same way (ONATE1 (4.2) and (4.36) to (4.38), PDF pp. 143 and 159 to 160, COOK (3.1-9), PDF p. 94, and PDF p. 117). COOK writes the matrix of derivatives \([\partial]\). Another common symbol for it is \(\mat L\), which is taken here, since \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the area coordinates.

Why does the strain column hold \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and not \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\)? Because of the energy. In the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the shear appears twice, as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and as \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\). The two terms are equal and together give \(2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). With \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) in the column, the work of the stress is therefore simply the product \(\{\sigma\}^\T\{\varepsilon\}\), without a factor two that we would otherwise carry through every equation. The quantity \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) also has a picture: it is the whole angle by which the right angle between the \(x\) and \(y\) directions changes, like the angle \(\gamma\) at node \(1\) of the triangle we shall meet in bending.

Definition 15.21 Strain-displacement matrix of the triangle (matrika deformacij trikotnika)

Let the triangle have vertices \((x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\) numbered counterclockwise and area \(A > 0\). Write

\[b_1 = y_2 - y_3 , \quad b_2 = y_3 - y_1 , \quad b_3 = y_1 - y_2 , \qquad c_1 = x_3 - x_2 , \quad c_2 = x_1 - x_3 , \quad c_3 = x_2 - x_1 . \tag{15.21}\]

The strain vector in the plane is the column \(\{\varepsilon\} = (\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}, \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}, \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}})^\T\) with the engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), that is the Voigt vector of definition 3.27 without the places \(zz\), \(yz\) and \(xz\). The strain-displacement matrix (matrika deformacij) of the triangle is the \(3 \times 6\) matrix \(\mat B\),

\[\mat B = \frac{1}{2A}\begin{bmatrix} b_1 & 0 & b_2 & 0 & b_3 & 0 \\ 0 & c_1 & 0 & c_2 & 0 & c_3 \\ c_1 & b_1 & c_2 & b_2 & c_3 & b_3 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{15.22}\]

Source: ONATE1 (4.39) (PDF p. 160), COOK (3.4-10) (PDF p. 107). The symbols \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are as in ONATE1.

The matrix \(\mat B\) is in bold, like the matrix \(\mat b_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the bar in chapter 7, which is a row of two numbers there. Its role is the same: it multiplies the column of nodal displacements and gives the strain.

Look at the function \(L_1\) as a surface over the triangle. The plane rises from \(0\) on the side \(2\)-\(3\) to \(1\) at node \(1\). Its slope in the \(x\) direction and in the \(y\) direction is the derivative that the lemma computes. All three planes together give the horizontal plane of height \(1\).

The numbers \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) of equation (15.21) have a simple picture. The side opposite node \(i\), walked in the direction \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\), moves by \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) in the \(x\) direction and by \(-b_{\mathrm{i}}\) in the \(y\) direction. The side from node \(2\) to \(3\), for example, is \((x_3 - x_2,\ y_3 - y_2) = (-30, 30)\) mm, which is \((c_1, -b_1)\). Whoever walks around the triangle comes back to the start, so the steps in the \(x\) direction add up to zero, and so do the steps in the \(y\) direction.

Lemma 15.22 Strain of the linear triangle (deformacija linearnega trikotnika)

Let the field (15.17) on a triangle of positive area \(A\) have the column of nodal displacements \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then:

  1. The slope of the plane \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) is \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = b_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) in the \(x\) direction and \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) in the \(y\) direction. Since the sum of the three planes is the horizontal plane of height \(1\), \(b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 0\).
  2. The strain of the field is \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Every component is constant on the triangle, because a plane has the same slope everywhere.
Proof

We must show that the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) have exactly these derivatives, and put them into the definition of strain.

  1. By lemma 15.4, claim 1, \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(x, y) = (a_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\,x + c_{\mathrm{i}}\,y)/(2A)\), where \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are exactly the numbers (15.21), differences of the coordinates of the vertices (there \(b_2\), \(c_2\), \(b_3\), \(c_3\) are obtained by the cyclic change of indices \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\)). Differentiating with respect to \(x\) and to \(y\) gives \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x = b_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\partial L_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y = c_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\).
  2. The sum \(b_1 + b_2 + b_3 = (y_2 - y_3) + (y_3 - y_1) + (y_1 - y_2) = 0\), since every \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) appears once with plus and once with minus, and the same for \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). These are the steps in \(y\) and in \(x\) of the walk around the triangle, which returns to its start. This is claim 1.
  3. By definition 1.121, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} b_{\mathrm{i}}\,u_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}}/(2A)\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} (c_{\mathrm{i}}\,u_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{i}}\,v_{\mathrm{i}})/(2A)\). These are the three rows of the product \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), since the nodal displacements \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) are numbers and the derivative acts only on \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\). The coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) are numbers that depend only on the vertices, so the strains are constant: the sides of the triangle stay straight, nothing curves anywhere.

Source: ONATE1 §4.3.2 (PDF p. 158-160), COOK §3.4 (PDF p. 107). The proof of claim 1 is not from the source.

We try it on our triangle. Here \(b = (-30,\ 30,\ 0)\) mm, \(c = (-30,\ -10,\ 40)\) mm and \(2A = 1200\ \text{mm}^2\), so

\[\mat B = \frac{1}{1200\ \text{mm}}\begin{bmatrix} -30 & 0 & 30 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -30 & 0 & -10 & 0 & 40 \\ -30 & -30 & -10 & 30 & 40 & 0 \end{bmatrix} .\]

Take the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\): we move every point to the right by one thousandth of its coordinate \(x\). Node \(1\) at the origin stays. Node \(2\) at \(x = 40\) mm moves by \(u_2 = 0{,}04\) mm, node \(3\) at \(x = 10\) mm by \(u_3 = 0{,}01\) mm. We draw the triangle before the displacement grey dashed and after it orange. The column of nodal displacements is \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (0,\ 0,\ 0{,}04,\ 0,\ 0{,}01,\ 0)^\T\) mm. The product \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 30 \cdot 0{,}04/1200 = 0{,}001\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), which is the strain of the field we chose. We read the same from the figure without a matrix: the side \(1\)-\(2\) of length \(40\) mm becomes longer by \(0{,}04\) mm, and \(0{,}04/40 = 0{,}001\). Since the functions \(L_{\mathrm{i}}\) give every affine field from its nodal values (lemma 15.8), the result is expected. The test shows that \(\mat B\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are arranged correctly.

The right half of the figure shows the stresses and forces too. The triangle is under the constant stress \((215{,}4,\ 64{,}6,\ 0)\) MPa, shown as a small square. On side \(1\)-\(2\) the force is vertical and equals \(64{,}6 \cdot 40 \cdot 10 / 1000 = 25{,}8\) kN. On side \(2\)-\(3\) the force is \((64{,}6,\ 19{,}4)\) kN, and on side \(3\)-\(1\) the force is \((-64{,}6,\ 6{,}5)\) kN. The force at a node is half the sum of the forces of the two sides that meet there: at node \(1\) it is \((-32{,}3,\ -9{,}7)\) kN, at node \(2\) \((32{,}3,\ -3{,}2)\) kN and at node \(3\) \((0,\ 12{,}9)\) kN. Their sum is zero, as equilibrium requires.

Since the strain on the triangle is constant (lemma 15.22), the linear triangle for displacements is called the constant strain triangle (trikotnik s stalno deformacijo, abbreviation CST, COOK PDF p. 105). The element has the same strain at every point, so it cannot show how the strain changes across it. This will show in bending.

15.4.2 The stiffness matrix of the triangle

For the energy we need the relation between stress and strain in the plane. Chapter 11 gives the same form for both states, equation (11.50), with constants \(E'\), \(\nu'\) that are \(E\), \(\nu\) in plane stress and \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\), \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) in plane strain (corollary 11.38). We write it as a matrix.

The figure shows what the matrix says. Tension in the \(x\) direction stretches the square in \(x\) and contracts it across, which the constant \(\nu'\) captures. Shear tilts its sides. Energy is the area of the triangle under the stress line, half the product of stress and strain. Because of this half, the energy in two strains is a bowl with its bottom at zero.

Lemma 15.23 In-plane material stiffness matrix (ravninska togostna matrika materiala)

Let the material be isotropic with constants \(E > 0\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\). In plane stress let \(E'\), \(\nu'\) equal \(E\), \(\nu\), in plane strain \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\). Let \(\{\sigma\} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})^\T\). Then:

  1. The relation of stress and strain is \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\) with the matrix
    \[\mat C = \frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \nu' & 0 \\ \nu' & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12\,(1 - \nu') \end{bmatrix} . \tag{15.23}\]
  2. The strain energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\).
  3. \(\mat C\) is symmetric and positive definite: \(\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} > 0\) for every \(\{\varepsilon\} \ne \vek 0\).
Proof

We must show that (15.23) inverts equation (11.50), that the sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) loses terms, and that the quadratic form is positive.

  1. Equation (11.50) (lengthwise stretch and transverse contraction) gives \(E'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(E'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} - \nu'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). We multiply the first by \(1\) and the second by \(\nu'\) and add: \(E'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}) = (1 - \nu'^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). Likewise \(E'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}) = (1 - \nu'^2)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). For shear, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{E'}{2\,(1 + \nu')}\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\cdot\frac{1 - \nu'}{2} = \frac{E'}{2\,(1 + \nu')}\). These are the rows of (15.23).
  2. The energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 3.59). In plane stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), in plane strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) (theorems 11.37 and 11.33), so the terms that remain are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \{\sigma\}^\T\{\varepsilon\}\).
  3. Symmetry can be read off (15.23). For positivity we write \(\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = \frac{E'}{1 - \nu'^2}\big(\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 + (1 - \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2) + \tfrac12\,(1 - \nu')\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\big)\), which follows from \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + 2\nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 = \nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 + (1 - \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\). The constants satisfy \(-1 < \nu' < 1\): in plane stress from \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\), in plane strain from \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\), which for \(\nu \in (-1, \tfrac12)\) lies between \(-\tfrac12\) and \(1\). For \(\nu' \ge 0\) all terms are \(\ge 0\) and the sum is zero only for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), since \(1 - \nu' > 0\). For \(\nu' < 0\), \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \le 2\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\), because the difference is \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \ge 0\), and since \(\nu'\) is negative, \(\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}})^2 \ge 2\nu'\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\). The first two terms together are then \(\ge (1 + \nu')\,(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2)\) \(> 0\) unless \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.5)-(4.7) (PDF p. 144-145), COOK (3.1-3) (PDF p. 93), for plane strain (3.4-11) (PDF p. 108). The proof of positivity is not from the source.

The figure shows the positivity of claim 3 in two cases. The bottom row says which bar is which term: first term, second and the whole expression. The constant \(\nu'\) lies between \(-1\) and \(1\) in both states, so \(1 - \nu'\) and \(1 + \nu'\) are positive. For \(\nu' = 0{,}3\) both terms point up, their sum is \(0{,}675 + 0{,}875 = 1{,}55\), which is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}^2 + 2\nu'\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}^2 = 1 + 0{,}3 + 0{,}25\). For \(\nu' = -0{,}5\) the first term points down, \(-1{,}125\). It cannot go below the line \(-1{,}25\), because the square of the sum is not longer than twice the sum of squares: the difference is the square of the difference \((1 - 0{,}5)^2 = 0{,}25\). The second term \(1{,}875\) therefore wins, and the sum \(1 - 0{,}5 + 0{,}25 = 0{,}75\) stays above the line \(0{,}625\), which is positive unless \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) are both zero.

In the lectures the column carries \(\varepsilon_{12}\), here \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so there \(1 - \nu\), here \(\tfrac12(1 - \nu)\) (equations (11.51), (11.55)).

For steel in plane stress \(E' = 196\) GPa and \(\nu' = 0{,}3\), so \(\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} 215{,}385 & 64{,}615 & 0 \\ 64{,}615 & 215{,}385 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 75{,}385 \end{bmatrix}\) GPa. The first column, multiplied by the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0{,}001\) of our test, is the stresses \(215{,}4\) MPa and \(64{,}6\) MPa shown by the square in the figure. In plane strain \(E^* = 215{,}385\) GPa, \(\nu^* = 0{,}4286\) and

\[\mat C = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda + 2\mu & \lambda & 0 \\ \lambda & \lambda + 2\mu & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \mu \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 263{,}846 & 113{,}077 & 0 \\ 113{,}077 & 263{,}846 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 75{,}385 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{GPa} ,\]

which agrees with definition 3.18 (\(\lambda = 113{,}077\) GPa, \(\mu = 75{,}385\) GPa) and with matrix (3.40), which for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) has exactly this block. In this chapter the matrix is written \(\mat C\) as in chapter 3, the sources (ONATE1, COOK) write \(\mat D\) or \([\mat E]\). The other symbols: bold \(\mat B\) is the strain-displacement matrix, \(t\) the thickness (\(t\) is time in chapter 14, there is no clash in this section), \(A\) the area of the triangle (\(A\) is a cross-section in chapter 7).

We picture the energy of the element as a valley over the nodal displacements. On the axes we plot the displacements \(d_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(d_{\mathrm{b}}\), and vertically \(2U_{\mathrm{e}}\). Since it is never negative, the surface never goes below the zero plane, but it is not a bowl with a point at the bottom: the floor is a straight line of displacements that cost the element no energy.

Lemma 15.24 Stiffness matrix of the constant strain triangle (togostna matrika trikotnika s stalno deformacijo)

Let a triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) counterclockwise, area \(A > 0\) and thickness \(t > 0\) (in plane strain the length of a slice of the prism) be made of a material with matrix \(\mat C\) (lemma 15.23). Then:

  1. The strain energy of the element at the nodal displacements is \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with
    \[\mat k_{\mathrm{e}} = t\,A\,\mat B^\T\,\mat C\,\mat B . \tag{15.24}\]
  2. The \(6 \times 6\) matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite (definition 7.15).
  3. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the stiffness matrix of the element (definition 7.4): the forces with which the surroundings hold the element in the state \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are \(\vek F_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
Proof

We must show that the energy is the integral of a constant function and then read off the properties of the matrix.

  1. The body of the element is the prism \(T \times [0, t]\), with \(T\) the triangle, which we look at along the thickness \(t\). The map \(\Phi(s, \lambda, z) = (P_1 + s\,\vek u + \lambda\,\vek v,\ z)\) with \(\vek u = P_2 - P_1\) and \(\vek v = P_3 - P_1\) is injective, has the constant Jacobian determinant \(2A \ne 0\) and maps the prism \(T_0 \times [0, t]\) over the triangle \(T_0\) of the proof of lemma 15.2 onto \(T \times [0, t]\). The prism \(T_0 \times [0, t]\) is a body between the graphs \(0 \le \lambda \le 1 - s\) over the rectangle \(0 \le s \le 1\), \(0 \le z \le t\) (definition 4.15). So for a continuous function \(f\) that does not depend on \(z\), by theorems 4.59 and 4.33, in space and then in the plane, \(\int_{\mathrm{T} \times [0, \mathrm{t}]} f\,\mathrm{d}V = 2A\,t\int_{\mathrm{T}_0} f\big(P_1 + s\,\vek u + \lambda\,\vek v\big)\,\mathrm{d}A = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} f\,\mathrm{d}A\), as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 15.9. By definition 4.85, therefore, \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \int w\,\mathrm{d}V = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} w\,\mathrm{d}A\). By lemma 15.22 and lemma 15.23, \(w = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is constant on \(T\), so the integral equals the value times the area \(A\) (lemma 4.32), and \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,(t A\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\mat B)\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The transpose of a product of matrices is the product of the transposes in reverse order, so \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^\T = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C^\T\mat B = \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\), since \(\mat C\) is symmetric. For every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (a point on the surface), \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 2U_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} \ge 0\) with \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(tA > 0\) (lemma 15.23, claim 3).
  3. By theorem 4.92 the force in the direction of the displacement \(d_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\partial U_{\mathrm{e}}/\partial d_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{j}}\), since \(U_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a quadratic function and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric, as for the bar (lemma 7.5).

We put the element into a structure as a bar. Node \(m\) has the unknowns \(2m - 1\) and \(2m\). A triangle with nodes \(5\), \(9\) and \(12\) has the row \((9, 10, 17, 18, 23, 24)\) of the connection table (definition 7.10), and the matrix \(\mat K\) is the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (theorem 7.13). That the solution of the equilibrium equations is exactly the solution of the Ritz method on the family of fields made of triangles is stated by corollary 15.16. The corollary is written for a body in space. In plane strain the plane energy is exactly the energy in space of a slice of thickness \(t\). In plane stress the argument goes the same way with the matrix (15.23), which by lemma 15.23 is symmetric and positive definite.

15.4.3 Stress from the nodal displacements

The solution of the system is the nodal displacements. The designer, however, wants the stress, since the stress decides whether the part holds. The way to it leads back through the same matrices: from the column of all displacements we take the six numbers \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) of one element, the matrix \(\mat B\) turns them into the strain, and the matrix \(\mat C\) turns the strain into the stress. This step is called stress recovery (izračun napetosti).

Lemma 15.25 Stress in the constant strain triangle (napetost v trikotniku s stalno deformacijo)

Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the column of nodal displacements of a triangle with area \(A > 0\) made of a material with the matrix \(\mat C\) (lemma 15.23), without initial strain. Then:

  1. The stress of the field (15.17) is
    \[\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} . \tag{15.25}\]
  2. All three components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) are constant on the triangle.
  3. Let the triangles \(\mathrm{I}\) and \(\mathrm{II}\) of the same material share a side and let the columns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{I}}\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{II}}\) hold the same displacements at the two shared nodes (as in an assembled mesh). The displacement is continuous across this side. The stress is \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{I}}\) on one side of it and \(\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{II}}\) on the other, and the two columns are equal exactly when the strains of the two triangles are equal, \(\mat B_{\mathrm{I}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{I}} = \mat B_{\mathrm{II}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{II}}\).
Proof

We must show that the matrices multiply to a constant column and that equal stress requires equal strain.

  1. By lemma 15.22, claim 2, \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). By lemma 15.23, claim 1, \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}\). Together \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat B\) holds only differences of the coordinates of the vertices (equation (15.22)), the matrix \(\mat C\) only the constants of the material (equation (15.23)), and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are the nodal displacements. None of these numbers depends on \(x\) and \(y\), so neither does the product.
  3. On the shared side the displacement of each triangle is a linear function of the position along the side, fixed by the displacements of its two ends (lemma 15.7). The ends are shared, so the two displacements are equal: the displacement is continuous.
  4. If the strains are equal, the stresses are equal by step 1 on both sides of the common side. Conversely let \(\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{I}} = \mat C\,\{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{II}}\). For the difference \(\{e\} = \{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{I}} - \{\varepsilon\}_{\mathrm{II}}\) we then have \(\mat C\,\{e\} = \vek 0\) and so \(\{e\}^\T\mat C\,\{e\} = 0\). Since \(\mat C\) is positive definite (lemma 15.23, claim 3), \(\{e\} = \vek 0\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.41) (PDF p. 160) and §9.8 (PDF pp. 352-353), COOK (3.12-1) (PDF p. 129) and §3.12 (PDF p. 130). The proof of claim 3 is not from a source.

By claim 2 the element has a single stress, so it cannot show how the stress changes across it. Where the stress changes quickly we therefore need a finer mesh (ONATE1, PDF p. 160). Claim 3 says that the stress in two neighbours is in general different. Let us see this on numbers.

To our triangle, which we now call \(\mathrm{I}\), we add the neighbour \(\mathrm{II}\) with nodes \(2\), \(4\), \(3\), where node \(4\) is at the point \((40, 30)\) mm. We draw both before the displacement grey dashed. They share the side \(2\)-\(3\). We move the nodes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) as before, \(u_2 = 0{,}04\) mm and \(u_3 = 0{,}01\) mm, and node \(4\) by \(u_4 = 0{,}05\) mm to the right. All vertical displacements are zero. We draw both triangles after the displacement orange. In the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}001\,x\) node \(4\) would move by only \(0{,}04\) mm, so triangle \(\mathrm{II}\) is stretched more than \(\mathrm{I}\). Equation (15.25) gives (NumPy)

\[\{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{I}} = \begin{bmatrix} 215{,}4 \\ 64{,}6 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{MPa} , \qquad \{\sigma\}_{\mathrm{II}} = \mat C\,\mat B_{\mathrm{II}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{II}} = \mat C \begin{bmatrix} 1{,}333 \\ 0 \\ 0{,}333 \end{bmatrix} 10^{-3} = \begin{bmatrix} 287{,}2 \\ 86{,}2 \\ 25{,}1 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{MPa} .\]

These are the column in triangle \(\mathrm{I}\) and the column in triangle \(\mathrm{II}\). On the shared side \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) changes by \(287{,}2 - 215{,}4 = 71{,}8\) MPa.

Where does the jump come from? We go along the horizontal line \(y = 15\) mm, which crosses the shared side at \(x = 25\) mm. The displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) along it is one line with a kink: in triangle \(\mathrm{I}\) it grows with the slope \(0{,}001\), in \(\mathrm{II}\) with the slope \(0{,}001333\), and on the side the two pieces meet, because both triangles read the displacement there from the same two nodes. The strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the slope of this line, and a line with a kink has two slopes at the kink, the left one and the right one. So \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is a step with a jump of \(71{,}8\) MPa. The jump is therefore not an error of the computation but the price of the family of fields: we asked it for a continuous displacement, not for a continuous derivative. With mesh refinement the jumps become smaller (ONATE1, PDF p. 353), so a large jump warns that the mesh is too coarse at that place (COOK, PDF p. 130).

Which value should we give to a node where several elements meet? Programs usually compute the mean of the stresses of all elements at the node. This is called nodal averaging (povprečenje v vozliščih, COOK §3.12, PDF p. 130, ONATE1 §9.8, PDF p. 353). At our nodes \(2\) and \(3\) we would get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = (215{,}4 + 287{,}2)/2 = 251{,}3\) MPa, the middle of the step. We do not develop the procedure here. We keep two things in mind. The picture of stress after averaging is smooth and hides the jumps, which are a warning (COOK, PDF pp. 28 and 130). And across the boundary of two materials or two thicknesses we do not average, because there the stress is discontinuous in reality too (COOK, PDF p. 130).

15.4.4 Rigid motions and what the triangle can do

The properties of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) mean physical properties of the element. What costs the element no energy? Anyone who has held a plate in the hands knows: if we move it or turn it as a whole we do not deform it, so there is no stress and no force is needed (a free body in equilibrium, Newton). The lemma says that the element has zero energy exactly in these three motions and in no other.

Look at the supports in the figure. The correct supports hold node \(1\) and node \(2\) perpendicular to the side, so the rotation about node \(1\) is impossible. The wrong supports hold node \(2\) along the side and do not catch the rotation.

Lemma 15.26 Rigid motions of the constant strain triangle (togi gibi trikotnika s stalno deformacijo)

Let \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the matrix (15.24) of a triangle with area \(A > 0\) and thickness \(t > 0\). Then:

  1. \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly when the numbers \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) are the values at the nodes of a small rigid motion (to first order), a field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = a_1 - \omega\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = a_2 + \omega\,x\), for some real \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(\omega\).
  2. There are three independent such columns (a translation in the \(x\) direction, a translation in the \(y\) direction and a rotation), so the kernel of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) has dimension \(3\) and only three of the six columns of the matrix are independent.
  3. Let \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) be prescribed and the component of the displacement of node \(2\) perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\) be zero. Then \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} \ne \vek 0\) that satisfies these three conditions, so the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is positive definite on the remaining three unknowns.
Proof

We must show that zero energy means zero strain and that the fields with zero strain are exactly these three.

  1. By lemma 7.16 and claim 2 of lemma 15.24, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\) exactly when \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} = 0\). Since \(\mat C\) is positive definite, this holds exactly when \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), that is when the triangle does not deform as in the fourth picture.
  2. The field (15.17) is affine, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + \alpha_2 y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 y\). Its strain is \((\alpha_1,\ \beta_2,\ \alpha_2 + \beta_1)\), so it is zero exactly for \(\alpha_1 = \beta_2 = 0\) and \(\alpha_2 = -\beta_1\). With \(\omega = \beta_1\) the field is \((\alpha_0 - \omega\,y,\ \beta_0 + \omega\,x)\), the sum of a translation \(\alpha_0\) in the \(x\) direction, a translation \(\beta_0\) in the \(y\) direction and a rotation \(\omega\), which is claim 1. Conversely a field of this form is affine and has strain zero.
  3. By lemma 15.8 the map that gives an affine field its values at the three nodes is a bijection between affine fields and columns \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) (six numbers on each side). The fields \((a_1 - \omega\,y,\ a_2 + \omega\,x)\) form a three-dimensional subspace, so the kernel is three-dimensional. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a symmetric \(6 \times 6\) matrix, so exactly \(6 - 3 = 3\) of its columns are independent.
  4. Let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) satisfy the three conditions of claim 3 and let \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = 0\). By lemma 7.16, \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), so by steps 1 and 2 \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is a rigid motion with some \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(\omega\). The displacement of node \(2\) is the displacement of node \(1\) plus \(\omega\,(-(y_2 - y_1),\ x_2 - x_1)\), so for \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) it is exactly \(\omega\,(-(y_2 - y_1),\ x_2 - x_1)\). This vector is perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\) and has length \(|\omega|\,\ell\), with \(\ell\) the length of the side. Since its component perpendicular to the side is prescribed as zero, \(\omega = 0\). Then the displacement of every node equals that of node \(1\), so it is zero, and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\). Hence \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} > 0\) for every nonzero \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) with the three conditions (claim 2 of lemma 15.24), which is the positive definiteness of the matrix on the remaining three unknowns.

Source: The proof is not from the source. The same for a space truss is stated by lemma 7.35.

A translation, the other translation and a rotation about node \(1\) change neither the lengths of the sides nor the angles to first order: a side of length \(\ell\) gets the length \(\ell\sqrt{1 + \omega^2}\), relatively \(5\cdot 10^{-7}\) at \(\omega = 0{,}001\), and the small strain tensor drops this quadratic term (assumption 2) (figure 15.44, all motions are drawn too large so that they can be seen). The fourth picture shows the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = e\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), where the side along \(x\) becomes longer: this is a deformation and its energy is not zero. For our triangle the field with \(e = 0{,}001\) has energy \(646{,}15\) N mm, as we saw.

The lemma says what the supports must do: they must prevent all three motions. Otherwise the matrix of the structure is not positive definite and the system has no single solution. For our triangle with \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) and \(v_2 = 0\) prescribed (the displacement of node \(2\) perpendicular to the side \(1\)-\(2\), which lies on the \(x\) axis) the matrix on \(u_2\), \(u_3\), \(v_3\) has the eigenvalues \(0{,}447\), \(0{,}750\) and \(1{,}580\cdot 10^6\) N/mm, all positive. With the wrong choice \(u_1 = v_1 = u_2 = 0\), where we prescribed the component along the side, one eigenvalue on \(v_2\), \(u_3\), \(v_3\) is zero: the rotation about node \(1\) moves node \(2\) perpendicular to the side and the prescription \(u_2 = 0\) does not catch it. The hypothesis of the lemma that the prescribed component is not along the side therefore cannot be dropped.

The condition \(A > 0\) is needed by the lemma for \(\mat B\). If all three nodes lie on a line, \(A = 0\) and \(\mat B\) does not exist. An almost such triangle is already bad: with node \(3\) at the height \(30\), \(3\) and \(0{,}3\) mm the ratio of the largest and the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is \(2{,}5\), \(172\) and \(1{,}7\cdot 10^{4}\) in turn. A thin triangle therefore makes the matrix badly conditioned (mesh quality is a subject of chapter 17).

15.4.5 Consistent forces on the triangle

The element has forces only at the nodes, but the load is distributed, as for the bar (definition 7.7). On the triangle we meet the same demand, that the forces do the same work as the load on every field of the family.

For a point force the picture is simple. The triangle with nodes \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) is the same as above. The force acts at the point \(P\). The shares are the areas of the three triangles that \(P\) cuts off opposite the nodes, divided by \(A\), which is \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\). The nodal forces are these shares of the force \(\vek F\).

Lemma 15.27 Consistent nodal forces on the triangle (konsistentne sile na trikotniku)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the field (15.17) of a triangle of thickness \(t\) and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) its column. The consistent nodal forces of a load are the numbers \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = (f_{\mathrm{ix}}, f_{\mathrm{iy}})\) at node \(i\) for which the work of the load on \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \vek f_{\mathrm{i}} \cdot (u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\) for every \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then:

  1. A body force \(\vek b\) (force per unit volume, constant) gives \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = \tfrac13\,t A\,\vek b\) at every node. The sum of the forces is the whole weight \(t A\,\vek b\).
  2. A load \(\vek p\) (force per unit area of the lateral surface) with a constant value on the side \(1\)-\(2\) of length \(\ell\) gives \(\vek f_1 = \vek f_2 = \tfrac12\,t\,\ell\,\vek p\) and \(\vek f_3 = \vek 0\). If \(\vek p\) changes linearly from \(\vek p_1\) at node \(1\) to \(\vek p_2\) at node \(2\), then \(\vek f_1 = t\,\ell\,(2\vek p_1 + \vek p_2)/6\) and \(\vek f_2 = t\,\ell\,(\vek p_1 + 2\vek p_2)/6\).
  3. A point force \(\vek F\) at the point \(P\) of the triangle gives \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\,\vek F\): node \(i\) gets the share \(L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\) of the force.
Proof

We must show that the work of the load splits into a sum over the nodes and compute the integrals of the shape functions.

  1. The work of the body force is \(\int \vek b\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}V = t\int_{\mathrm{T}} \vek b\cdot\sum_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\,\mathrm{d}A = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} t\,\vek b\cdot(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (step 1 of the proof of lemma 15.24, linearity of the integral). By lemma 15.9 with exponents \((1, 0, 0)\), \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 1!\cdot 2A/3! = A/3\). So \(\vek f_{\mathrm{i}} = tA\,\vek b/3\), one third at each node.
  2. On the side \(1\)-\(2\), \(L_3 = 0\) (lemma 15.7), \(L_1 = 1 - s/\ell\) and \(L_2 = s/\ell\) with \(s \in [0, \ell]\) (affine functions with the values \(1\) and \(0\) at the ends). The work is \(t\int_0^\ell \vek p\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}\,\mathrm{d}s\), which for constant \(\vek p\) gives by the fundamental theorem of calculus \(f_1 = t\,\vek p\int_0^\ell (1 - s/\ell)\,\mathrm{d}s = \tfrac12\,t\,\ell\,\vek p\), and the same for \(f_2\) (the forces at nodes \(1\) and \(2\)). The linear case is the same computation as in equation (7.9) for the bar, with \(t\,\vek p\) in place of \(q\).
  3. The work of the point force \(\vek F\) on the field is \(\vek F\cdot u_{\mathrm{h}}(P) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} L_{\mathrm{i}}(P)\,\vek F\cdot(u_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{i}})\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.64)-(4.70) (PDF p. 166-167), COOK §3.11 (3.11-2) (PDF p. 126-127). Claims 1 and 2 are as in ONATE1, claim 3 as in COOK. The proof is not from the source.

The weight of the element for steel (\(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\), \(g = 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2\)) is \(\rho g\,t A = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 \cdot 6\cdot 10^{-6}\) N \(= 0{,}459\) N. Each node gets a third, \(0{,}153\) N downward, since \(\int_{\mathrm{T}} L_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) is one third of the area. On the right is the same triangle with a constant pressure \(p = 1\) MPa that pushes upward on its lower side of length \(\ell = 40\) mm. The total force is \(p\,t\,\ell = 1\cdot 10\cdot 40 = 400\) N, and the nodes get \(200\) N each. For a pressure that grows linearly from \(0\) at node \(1\) to \(1\) MPa at node \(2\), node \(1\) gets \(66{,}67\) N and node \(2\) gets \(133{,}3\) N, a sum of \(200\) N, since the mean pressure is \(0{,}5\) MPa. The node on the side of the larger pressure gets more, as for the bar.

The forces of the surroundings on the sides that two neighbours share, such as the side \(2\)-\(3\) of the two triangles, do not appear in the assembled structure, because they cancel (theorem 15.15). So we compute only with the loads on the outer boundary. ONATE1 states the same (PDF p. 164).

15.4.6 Temperature on the triangle

In plane stress, by chapter 3 the total strain is the sum of the elastic and the thermal strain, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \varepsilon^{\mathrm{el}}_{\mathrm{ij}} + \alpha\,\Delta T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (theorem 3.23). For the bar we called \(\varepsilon_0 = \alpha\,\Delta T\) the initial strain (definition 7.48). We do the same on the triangle, which expands freely when heated or is held by supports.

Lemma 15.28 Heated triangle (segret trikotnik)

Let the triangle be heated by \(\Delta T\), uniformly, relative to a reference temperature, and let the material have the coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\). Let \(\{\varepsilon_0\} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) in plane stress and \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) in plane strain. Then:

  1. The stress in the plane is \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})\). In free expansion \(\{\varepsilon\} = \{\varepsilon_0\}\) and the in-plane stress is zero. In plane strain \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) remains, because stretching in \(z\) is still prevented.
  2. The matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) does not change, the right-hand side gets the column \(\vek f_0 = t A\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\), whose sum of components in the \(x\) direction and in the \(y\) direction is zero.
  3. In plane stress, for a triangle with node \(1\) at the origin and the side \(1\)-\(2\) on the \(x\) axis, the triangle with only \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) and \(v_2 = 0\) prescribed has the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,y\) and zero stress.
  4. If all six nodal displacements are zero, the stress is \(-\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - \nu)\) in plane stress and \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\) in plane strain.
Proof

We must show how temperature enters the relation between stress and strain and compute the energy.

  1. In plane stress, where temperature alone expands the triangle, the equations for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) in theorem 3.23 are the same as in equation (11.50) with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} - \alpha\,\Delta T\) on the left. From lemma 15.23 therefore \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T)\).
  2. In plane strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and from theorem 3.23, \(0 = (\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}) - E\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), as in chapter 11. We put this into \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}))/E + \alpha\,\Delta T\) and get \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{E}\big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} - \frac{\nu}{1 - \nu}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\big) + (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) (checked with SymPy). The shear equation does not change. This is (11.50) with \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) and the extra term \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\) in the normal components.
  3. The energy density is \(w = \tfrac12\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})^\T\mat C\,(\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon_0\})\), since this is the elastic part (in plane strain up to the constant \(E\,\alpha^2\Delta T^2/2\) from \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), which does not depend on the displacements): from the total strain we subtract the part that temperature makes alone. Multiplied out, \(w = \tfrac12\,\{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon\} - \{\varepsilon\}^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} + \text{const.}\) By lemma 15.22, \(\{\varepsilon\} = \mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and everything is constant, so the energy of the element is \(\tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek f_0 + \text{const.}\) with \(\vek f_0 = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\). The term \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\vek f_0\) is like the work of forces on the right-hand side, so we add \(\vek f_0\) to the right-hand side, as for the bar (lemma 7.49).
  4. Let \(\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the column with ones at the places \(u_{\mathrm{i}}\) and zeros at the places \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\). These are the nodal values of the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 1\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), which is a rigid motion (lemma 15.26), so \(\mat B\,\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\) and the sum of the components of \(\vek f_0\) in the \(x\) direction is \(\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}}^\T\vek f_0 = tA\,(\mat B\,\vek 1_{\mathrm{x}})^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} = 0\). The same in the \(y\) direction.
  5. The field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,x\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,\Delta T\,y\) is affine, its nodal values \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) have by lemma 15.22 the strain \(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \{\varepsilon_0\}\) (in plane stress), so \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\{\varepsilon_0\} - \{\varepsilon_0\}) = \vek 0\) and \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = tA\,\mat B^\T\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\} = \vek f_0\). This \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is therefore a solution and has \(u_1 = v_1 = 0\) (the origin) and \(v_2 = 0\) (\(y_2 = 0\)). The reduced matrix is positive definite by lemma 15.26, so the solution is unique.
  6. For \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek 0\), \(\{\varepsilon\} = \vek 0\) and \(\{\sigma\} = -\mat C\,\{\varepsilon_0\}\). In plane stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -\frac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - \nu)\) and the same for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) (the shear is zero). In plane strain \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(\lambda + 2\mu + \lambda)\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -2\,(\lambda + \mu)\,(1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T\), and since by definition 3.18 \(\lambda + \mu = E/\big(2\,(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\big)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T/(1 - 2\nu)\).

Source: ONATE1 (4.17)-(4.18), (4.71)-(4.73) (PDF p. 147, 167), Examples 4.2 and 4.3 (PDF p. 150-154). The proof is not from the source.

By claim 1 of the lemma, the stress recovery of equation (15.25) reads \(\{\sigma\} = \mat C\,(\mat B\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} - \{\varepsilon_0\})\) for a heated element (COOK (3.12-1), PDF p. 129): from the strain we first subtract the part that the temperature makes by itself and that causes no stress.

The numbers. For our triangle with \(\Delta T = 30\) K and \(\alpha = 15\cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}\), in plane stress \(\{\varepsilon_0\} = 4{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) and the forces are \(\vek f_0 = (-18\,900,\ -18\,900,\ 18\,900,\ -6300,\ 0,\ 25\,200)^\T\) N, which have the sum zero in both directions. First we support the triangle before heating so that it can expand freely: the two supports prescribe only \(u_1 = v_1 = v_2 = 0\). The heated triangle is an enlarged copy of the original one. Node \(2\) moves by \(\alpha\,\Delta T\cdot 40\ \text{mm} = 18\ \mu\)m and node \(3\) by \((4{,}5,\ 13{,}5)\ \mu\)m, that is \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,(x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\), and the stress is zero. Now we hold the same triangle at all three nodes. It cannot expand, so it is compressed: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = -126{,}0\) MPa in plane stress and \(-220{,}5\) MPa in plane strain, where also \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -220{,}5\) MPa. We compare with the bar between walls, \(-E\,\alpha\,\Delta T = -88{,}2\) MPa. Why is the stress in the plate larger? The bar cannot extend in the \(x\) direction, but in the \(y\) direction it widens freely, by \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,\Delta T = 5{,}85\cdot 10^{-4}\). A plate with two held directions can widen neither in the \(x\) nor in the \(y\) direction, so it is compressed in both. Through \(\nu\) the compression in the \(y\) direction widens it in the \(x\) direction, so the compression in the \(x\) direction must be even larger: in plane stress by the factor \(1/(1 - \nu) = 1{,}43\). In plane strain the third direction \(z\) is also held, the prism is compressed in all three directions and the factor is \(1/(1 - 2\nu) = 2{,}5\).

The free triangle loses its stress because the thermal strain \(\{\varepsilon_0\}\) belongs to a displacement field that is in the family (an affine one): the triangle expands as a whole, like the bar in chapter 7. Also for a temperature that changes linearly over a free plate, \(\Delta T = c_0 + c_1\,x + c_2\,y\), the exact stress is zero. The strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\,(1, 1, 0)^\T\) is then the strain of the field \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \alpha\,(c_0\,x + c_1\,(x^2 - y^2)/2 + c_2\,x\,y)\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = \alpha\,(c_0\,y + c_1\,x\,y + c_2\,(y^2 - x^2)/2)\), which we check by differentiation. The constant strain triangle, however, does not capture a linearly changing \(\{\varepsilon_0\}\) exactly, since it has a single strain over the whole element. Thermal stresses for a temperature that changes over the body are treated in chapter 14.

15.4.7 The triangle cannot bend

We try the triangle on a task where the strain is not constant: the cantilever of chapter 11 (\(L = 1\) m, cross-section \(20 \times 40\) mm, \(F = 500\) N), \(I = 106\,667\ \text{mm}^4\). At the clamp the moment is \(500\) N m and the curvature is \(\kappa = M/(EI) = 0{,}02392\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The exact strain in pure bending is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa\,y\), linear in height, at the edge \(y = 20\) mm it is \(4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) and the stress is \(93{,}75\) MPa. The triangle gives a constant value. What does that mean on one small piece?

Take a cell \(100 \times 20\) mm next to the clamp, \(0 \le x \le 100\) mm and \(0 \le y \le 20\) mm. The exact field of pure bending is \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \kappa\,x\,y\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\kappa\,(x^2 + \nu\,y^2)/2\) (its strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \kappa\,y\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\kappa\,y\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \kappa\,x - \kappa\,x = 0\), so the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = E\kappa\,y\) and the others zero). We cut the cell into two triangles. The exact field curves the upper and lower edges, and the sides stay straight. Take the triangle \((0, 0)\), \((a, 0)\), \((0, h)\) with \(a = 100\) mm and \(h = 20\) mm and give it the nodal values of the exact field. Node \(1\) stays, \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = \kappa\,x\,y\) is zero at all three nodes (at each one \(x = 0\) or \(y = 0\)), node \(2\) drops by \(v_2 = -\kappa\,a^2/2 = -0{,}1196\) mm and node \(3\) by \(v_3 = -\kappa\,\nu\,h^2/2 = -0{,}0014\) mm, which is \(83\) times less and cannot be seen in the figure. The field of the triangle with these nodal values therefore has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = v_3/h = -7{,}2\cdot 10^{-5}\) and the shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = v_2/a = -1{,}196\cdot 10^{-3}\): the side \(1\)-\(2\) tilts, the side \(1\)-\(3\) stays vertical, so the right angle at node \(1\) opens. The exact \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is zero. The bending strain has disappeared in the nodal values, because all three \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and a shear has appeared that the exact field does not have. It is \(7{,}5\) times the exact value of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) at the centroid of the triangle (\(1{,}59\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(y = h/3\)), the corresponding stress \(G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is \(90{,}1\) MPa, almost as large as the bending stress \(93{,}75\) MPa at the edge of the cell.

The thickness of the cell is the width of the cantilever's cross-section, \(t = 20\) mm. The energy of the cell given by the two triangles is \(2626\) N mm, the exact one \(298{,}9\) N mm, a ratio of \(8{,}8\) (the same for both diagonals). The shear part \(\tfrac12\,tA\,G\,\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2\) of the two triangles together is about \(2160\) N mm, more than four fifths. What does too high an energy mean? The energy is the work of the moment that curves the cell. For the same curvature the moment must do \(8{,}8\) times as much work, so it must be \(8{,}8\) times as large. The cell therefore bends as if it were almost nine times as stiff. The reason is twofold: a constant strain cannot follow \(\kappa\,y\), and the nodal values give, instead of the bending strain, a shear that uses up energy. COOK describes the same on the element at the lower left edge (PDF p. 108): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{x}} = u_2/a\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\), \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = v_2/a\), "the element displays transverse shear strain, which should not be present". For its mesh COOK gives displacements and stresses about one quarter of the true values (PDF p. 108).

The whole cantilever of chapter 11, cut into \(10 \times 2\) cells, each cut along a diagonal into two triangles, has \(60\) unknowns. We prescribe the displacement at the clamp by the exact solution, so that the clamp itself adds no error and the number measures the element alone. If we hold the nodes at the clamp at zero instead, the mesh gives \(0{,}1145\) instead of \(0{,}1147\) of the exact deflection, so the choice hardly changes the result. The clamp is at the left, the force at the right end acts as a parabolic shear stress, which we replace for the elements by consistent forces. The exact displacement of the free end by Airy is \(-7{,}9719\) mm, the mesh gives \(-0{,}914\) mm, which is \(0{,}115\) of the exact value. The figure shows the exact deformed axis and the deformed mesh of triangles, both enlarged \(20\) times.

With finer cells the mesh is better but slowly: with \(20 \times 4\) cells (\(200\) unknowns) the ratio is \(0{,}341\), with \(40 \times 8\) (\(720\) unknowns) \(0{,}673\). With each doubling of the number of cells in both directions there are three to four times as many unknowns, and the difference from the exact value falls only from \(0{,}885\) to \(0{,}659\) and to \(0{,}327\). COOK (PDF p. 107) says the same: correct results are approached as a mesh is refined, but slowly. What an element needs in order to converge under refinement is shown by the patch test (section 15.7), and we do not give a proof of convergence here. For plane strain of an almost incompressible material (\(\nu \to \tfrac12\)) a mesh of triangles can lock (COOK, PDF p. 108): that is the subject of the section on locking.
