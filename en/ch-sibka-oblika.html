The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

In this chapter we start computing displacements of bodies that we cannot compute by hand. It builds on chapters 4 and 5, where we obtained the displacement of a bar and the deflection of a beam by integrating the equation of equilibrium, and on chapter 3, which linked stress to deformation. This worked because the shapes and loads were simple: the hanging bar has a constant cross section, the cantilever one force. For a bar with a step the writing already gets complicated, for a beam whose cross section changes along its length and which carries many loads the integration becomes long, and for a body with holes we cannot write the true displacement at all. A computer must give displacements all the same.

The words of the title tell the route. The strong form (močna oblika) of equilibrium is an equation at every point, which asks the unknown function for as many derivatives as the order of the equation: two for the bar, four for the beam. The weak form (šibka oblika) is an equality of integrals that asks less, and is called weak for that reason: it also admits functions with kinks, such as approximations made of straight pieces. The Ritz and Galerkin methods are named after Ritz and Galerkin, who were among the major contributors to approximate variational methods (EP, pp. 3 to 4). The word method means a way (Greek methodos): the trial field has as many free numbers as we like, and they follow from equations written in advance.

This matters because without this route there is no computer calculation of displacements. The chapter promises to show how more free numbers give an ever better approximation, how good it is and how fast it approaches the true field.

The question of the chapter is: how do we find an approximate displacement when we cannot write the true one, and how do we find out how good it is?

We have already seen an answer in a small way. For the hanging bar we chose the straight line \(c\,x\) in section 4.6.8 and looked for the number \(c\) at which the total potential energy is smallest. We got \(c = a L\) (figure 4.261): the displacement of the end was exact, 19,52 µm, at the middle of the bar the line gave 9,760 µm instead of the 14,64 µm of the true field, and the energy was \(-293{,}27\) µJ instead of \(-391{,}03\) µJ. For the cantilever in section 5.11.6 the parabola \(a\,x^2\) gave the end of the beam as \(-5{,}979\) mm instead of \(-7{,}972\) mm, that is three quarters of the true value. Both times the energy came out higher than the true one, never lower, and both times there was only one free number.

The route is this. First we write equilibrium in a form that asks less of the unknown function than an equation at every point, and we check what of the computation in chapters 3 and 4 remains for functions with kinks. Then we collect the mathematics for the equations of the free numbers and write two methods: the first finds the free numbers from the smallest energy, the second directly from the equation of equilibrium. In between we say how good the approximation is and why it is the best in its family. At the end we take trial fields made of straight pieces, with which the finite element method of chapter 7 begins.
