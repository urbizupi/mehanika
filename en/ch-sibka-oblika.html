The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

In chapters 4 and 5 we computed the displacement of a bar and the deflection of a beam by integrating the equation of equilibrium. This worked because the loads and the shapes were simple. The hanging bar has a constant cross section and a load that is the same everywhere, the cantilever has a constant bending stiffness and one force. For a bar with a step in the middle the writing already gets complicated, for a beam whose cross section changes along its length and which carries many loads the integration becomes long, and for a body that is a machine part with holes we do not know how to write the true displacement at all. A computer must give displacements all the same. The question of the chapter is: how do we find an approximate displacement when we cannot write the true one, and how do we find out how good it is?

We have already seen an answer in a small way. For the hanging bar we chose the straight line \(c\,x\) in section 4.6.8 and looked for the number \(c\) at which the total potential energy is smallest. We got \(c = a L\), the displacement of the end was exact, 19,52 µm, at the middle of the bar the line gave 9,760 µm instead of 14,64 µm, and the energy was \(-293{,}27\) µJ instead of \(-391{,}03\) µJ. For the cantilever in section 5.11.6 the parabola \(a\,x^2\) gave the end of the beam as \(-5{,}979\) mm instead of \(-7{,}972\) mm, that is three quarters of the true value. Both times the energy came out higher than the true one, never lower, and both times there was only one free number. The idea of the chapter is to turn this trying into a method: the trial field gets as many free numbers as we like, and the numbers follow from equations that we write in advance and that a computer can solve. We also want to know whether an approximation with more numbers really approaches the true field and how fast.

The route is this. In the first section we write equilibrium in a form that asks less of the unknown function than an equation at every point, and we test this form on a bar, on a bar with a step, on a beam and on a body. Trial fields made of straight pieces have kinks, so in the second section we check what of the computation in chapters 3 and 4 remains for functions with kinks. In the third section we collect the mathematics we need for the equations for the free numbers: the scalar product, carried over from vectors to functions, and systems of linear equations that have exactly one solution. Then we write the first method, in which the free numbers are found from the smallest energy, and in the fifth section we say how good its approximation is and why it is the best among all fields of the same family. The sixth section shows a route that needs no energy, because it starts directly from the equation of equilibrium, and compares it with the first. The last section takes trial fields made of straight pieces, which can be spread as densely as we like along the bar. This choice is the beginning of the finite element method, which we develop in chapter 7.
