The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

We begin the chapter on how to compute a real structure with finite elements (končni elementi). The previous chapters gave us the tools: elements for bars (chapter 7) and beams (chapter 12) and an estimate of how fast the error falls with a finer mesh (theorem 6.41). For a bar and a beam with a stiffness that is constant on each element and with consistent forces we also proved that the values at the nodes are exact (theorems 6.38 and 12.14). Every computation so far had a model underneath, which we chose at the start and never questioned again. A program divides the model into elements and returns a number, and the question is what that number says about the real structure.

Let us look at the words in the title. A model is a simplified picture of a thing: we keep what affects the answer and leave out the rest. For the cantilever in the figure the model keeps the length and the cross-section of the bar, the clamp and the force of the weight, and leaves out the bolts, the weld and the hook. Modeling (modeliranje) is therefore the choice of what to keep from the real thing and what to leave out. The abbreviation FEM (MKE) says that the computation is done with the finite element method, which divides the model into finite elements. The words "in practice" say that we do not prove theorems about one element, but answer when the number from the computer can be trusted and what to do when it cannot.

This decides the outcome for every machine and beam we compute. A steel beam with a clamped end, a pressurized pipe or a joint of two parts each give a number that can be wrong for several reasons. The first is a mesh that is too coarse: the true displacement \(u\) of the bar is a curve, while two linear elements meet it only at the nodes and replace it between them with straight pieces. The second is a wrong support or load: the model has a rigid clamp, while the real clamp yields a little, as if there were a spring that twists between the wall and the bar. The edge of the bar therefore turns by \(\theta_0\) and the end goes down by \(\delta'\) instead of \(\delta\). The third is that the linear computation is no longer correct for this structure: a thin strip bends by a visible part of its length, so the lever arm \(a\) of the force is shorter than \(L\), while a computation on the unbent shape takes the lever arm \(L\).

The chapter promises three things. First, we split the error of a result into parts, each with its own remedy, and show how a structure is turned into a model with supports and loads. Then we show how the error falls with the mesh and how to estimate it from the numbers alone, how to obtain stresses from the result, when a mesh is poor, how large the systems of equations are and what spoils the computation in them. Finally we show when the linear model fails, in statics and in motion in time, and tie everything together in a plan of analysis on the example of a pipe. We test everything with numbers on bodies from the previous chapters.
