The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

We begin the chapter on how to compute a real structure with finite elements (končni elementi). The previous chapters gave us the tools: elements for bars (chapter 7) and beams (chapter 12), the proof that the values at the nodes are exact, and an estimate of how fast the error falls with a finer mesh. Every computation so far had a model underneath, which we chose at the start and never questioned again. A program returns a number, and the question is what that number says about the real structure.

Let us look at the words in the title. A model is a simplified picture of a thing: we keep what affects the answer and leave out the rest. Modeling (modeliranje) is therefore the choice of what to keep from the real thing and what to leave out. The phrase "FEM" says that the computation is done with finite elements. The words "in practice" say that we do not prove theorems about one element, but answer when the number from the computer can be trusted and what to do when it cannot.

This decides the outcome for every machine and beam we compute. A steel beam with a clamped end, a pressurized pipe or a joint of two parts each give a number that can be wrong for several reasons: the mesh is too coarse, the support or the load is wrong, or the linear computation is no longer correct for this structure.

The chapter promises three things. First, we split the error of a result into parts, each with its own remedy, and show how a structure is turned into a model with supports and loads. Then we show how the error falls with the mesh and how to estimate it from the numbers alone, how to obtain stresses from the result, when a mesh is poor, how large the systems of equations are and what spoils the computation in them. Finally we show when the linear model fails, in statics and in motion in time, and tie everything together in a plan of analysis on the example of a pipe. We test everything with numbers on bodies from the previous chapters.
