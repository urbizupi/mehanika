Chapter 3 Constitutive equations

3.5 Mathematics: the integral

The next section will ask how much work a force does when it slowly stretches a steel bar, and where that work is stored. The school formula for work is not enough here. It says that a force that stays the same all the time and points in the direction of motion does work equal to the force times the distance travelled. The force on the end of a bar in the tension test (section 3.1), however, does not stay the same. At the start it is zero, and then it grows together with the elongation. Which force should we multiply by the distance?

We need the same tool for a second question. In chapter 1 we took the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), as given and computed its strain with the derivative, \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\). In chapter 2 we promised to compute the displacement from the stress. From the stress we get the strain by dividing by Young's modulus (section 3.1). But how do we get the displacement itself from the strain, which is the derivative of the displacement? We need a procedure that undoes differentiation.

From history

The ancient Greeks already computed the area of a region with a curved boundary. They compared it with the areas of polygons inscribed in the region or circumscribed about it, because as the number of sides grew, the polygon came ever closer to the region (CAJORI, PDF p. 40-41). The true value was then fixed by contradiction. In this way they proved that the areas of two circles are in the ratio of the squares of their diameters. Suppose that this ratio held for the area of the larger circle and some wrong, smaller value in place of the area of the smaller circle. An inscribed polygon approaches the smaller circle as closely as we like, so it would exceed this value, but from the ratio of the squares of the diameters it would follow that the similar polygon inscribed in the larger circle is larger than that circle, which is impossible. A wrong larger value is treated in the same way (CAJORI, PDF p. 41). This procedure, called the method of exhaustion (izčrpavanje), was rigorous but cumbersome (CAJORI, PDF p. 40-41).

Archimedes found his results first in a different way. He cut regions and solids in thought into infinitely narrow strips and weighed them, taking the strips that he weighed together equally thick. He took such strips as a good enough hint for a theorem, but not as a rigorous proof (CAJORI, PDF p. 51). His text on this method was found only in 1906 in Constantinople by Heiberg (CAJORI, PDF p. 51).

Bonaventura Cavalieri in 1635 built regions from lines and added the lines (CAJORI, PDF p. 177). He cut a cone into circles parallel to the base (figure 3.41). The radius of a circle grows in proportion to the distance from the apex and the area of the circle with the square of the radius, so the circles at distances \(1, 2, \dots, n\) from the apex have areas in the ratio \(1^2 : 2^2 : \dots : n^2\). All sections of a cylinder with the same base and height equal the base, that is, the farthest circle of the cone, so on the same scale they have area \(n^2\). The sum of squares is \(1^2 + 2^2 + \dots + n^2 = n\,(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6\), and the sum of \(n\) sections of the cylinder is \(n \cdot n^2 = n^3\). The ratio \((1 + 1/n)(2 + 1/n)/6\) approaches \(1/3\) as \(n\) grows, so the cone is one third of the cylinder with the same base and height (CAJORI, PDF p. 177). The calculation was correct but had no foundation, since a line without width gives no area, however many of them we add (CAJORI, PDF p. 177).

Let us return to the two questions about the work of a changing force and about the displacement sought from the strain. Both questions are answered by a procedure akin to Cavalieri's but with a foundation. We cut the path or the bar into short pieces, estimate the quantity on each piece as if it were constant, and add up the estimates. The shorter the pieces, the better the estimates. In this section we show when the estimates determine exactly one number. The route is as follows. We estimate the work done by a changing force from below and from above with sums of areas of rectangles. From such sums we build the number that the estimates determine, and the completeness axiom appears again on the way. Then we show that this number exists for every function that is continuous on a closed interval. For this we need a stronger form of continuity, in which the same band serves all points at once, and we prove it with the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57. Then come the rules for computing with these numbers and a theorem that connects them with the derivative through the mean value theorem 1.84 and answers both questions. At the end we derive from the rule for the derivative of a product a formula that moves a derivative from one function to another. We will need it in chapter 4.

About numbers we assume the same as in section 1.8.1: the arithmetic rules from school, the properties of the integers and the completeness axiom 1.32. Everything else we derive from the theorems of chapter 1, so the chain of proofs from the completeness axiom to the end of the section is closed.

3.5.1 The work done by a changing force

Take the steel bar of chapter 2: length \(L = 10\) m, a round cross section with diameter 10 mm and area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\). The left end is held by the fixed jaw of a tension testing machine. The machine pulls the right end along the axis of the bar with a force that it slowly increases from zero to 10 kN (figure 3.42). We neglect the weight of the bar as in chapter 2, where it was only 0,6 % of the force 10 kN. The distance by which the right end has moved up to a given moment we denote by \(e\). Since the left end does not move, \(e\) is the current elongation of the bar. Slowly means that the bar is in equilibrium at every moment and the force equals the force that the tension test measures at the elongation \(e\). Why this assumption matters we will see in section 3.6.

At the force 10 kN the stress is 127,3 MPa (section 2.2), which is below the yield stress 220 MPa. The bar therefore stays in the linear part of the tension test the whole time, where the stress is proportional to the strain, \(\sigma = E\,\varepsilon\), with Young's modulus \(E = 196\) GPa (definition 3.1). The stress is the force divided by the cross-section area, \(\sigma = F/A\), and the strain is the elongation divided by the length, \(\varepsilon = e/L\). From \(F/A = E\,e/L\) we get

\[F(e) = k\,e , \qquad k = \frac{EA}{L} = \frac{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa} \cdot 78{,}54 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2}{10\ \text{m}} = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} .\]

The force is proportional to the elongation. The number \(k\) says how much force is needed for every metre of elongation, so 1539 N for a millimetre. The final elongation at the force 10 kN is \(\Delta L = 10\,000\ \text{N}/k = 6{,}496\) mm.

The school formula holds for a force that stays the same, and ours changes. So let us cut the path of the right end, from \(e = 0\) to \(e = \Delta L\), into four pieces of equal length. On each piece the force changes less than on the whole path, and on each piece we know it at least between two bounds. Since the force grows with the elongation, it is not smaller than its value at the start of the piece and not larger than its value at the end of the piece. Can we estimate the work from this? We can, if we demand of the work two properties that the school formula has.

  1. The work on the whole path is the sum of the works on consecutive pieces of the path.
  2. If the force on some piece of the path is nowhere smaller than a number \(m\), the work on that piece is at least \(m\) times the length of the piece. If it is nowhere larger than a number \(M\), the work on the piece is at most \(M\) times the length of the piece.

For a constant force both properties hold, because the work is force times length and the lengths of the pieces add up to the length of the path. For a force that changes, the school formula does not determine the work. We therefore take properties 1 and 2 as requirements that every sensible notion of work must meet. We will see that the two requirements determine the work exactly. Here the force points in the direction of motion, as for our bar. When a force points at an angle to the direction of motion, the work is different, and we will need such a case already in section 3.6.2. EP too defines the work done by a changing force with the procedure we are about to build (EP (3.1.2), p. 97).

A piece of the path has length \(h = \Delta L/4\). On the piece from \((i - 1)\,h\) to \(i\,h\), for \(i = 1, 2, 3, 4\), the force lies between \(k\,(i - 1)\,h\) and \(k\,i\,h\), so by property 2 the work on this piece lies between \(k\,(i-1)\,h \cdot h\) and \(k\,i\,h \cdot h\). By property 1 the work on the whole path lies between the sums

\[(0 + 1 + 2 + 3)\,k h^2 = 6\,k h^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (1 + 2 + 3 + 4)\,k h^2 = 10\,k h^2 .\]

The number \(k\,\Delta L\) is the final force \(F = 10\,000\) N, so \(k\,\Delta L^2 = F\,\Delta L = 10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 64{,}96\) J and \(k h^2 = k\,\Delta L^2/16 = 4{,}060\) J. The work therefore lies between 24,36 J and 40,60 J.

On a graph that shows the force against the elongation, the work of a constant force is the area of a rectangle with sides force and length of the path. Both sums are therefore areas of staircases of rectangles (figure 3.43). The lower staircase lies below the line \(F = k\,e\), the upper one reaches above it. The estimate is crude, since the two sums differ by almost a factor of 2. Shorter pieces improve it. With \(n\) equal pieces of length \(h = \Delta L/n\) the sums are

\[(0 + 1 + \dots + (n - 1))\,k h^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (1 + 2 + \dots + n)\,k h^2 .\]

We compute the sum \(1 + 2 + \dots + n\) with an old trick. We write it forwards and backwards, \(1 + 2 + \dots + n\) and \(n + (n - 1) + \dots + 1\), and add the two terms that stand one under the other. Each such pair gives \(n + 1\), there are \(n\) pairs, so twice the sum equals \(n\,(n + 1)\) and

\[1 + 2 + \dots + n = \frac{n\,(n + 1)}{2} .\]

In the same way \(0 + 1 + \dots + (n - 1) = (n - 1)\,n/2\). Since \(h = \Delta L/n\), we have \(k h^2 = k\,\Delta L^2/n^2\), and the sums are

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{k\,\Delta L^2}{n^2} \cdot \frac{(n - 1)\,n}{2} &= \frac{k\,\Delta L^2}{2}\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big) , \\ \frac{k\,\Delta L^2}{n^2} \cdot \frac{n\,(n + 1)}{2} &= \frac{k\,\Delta L^2}{2}\Big(1 + \frac1n\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{3.30}\]

For our bar \(k\,\Delta L^2 = 64{,}96\) J, as computed above. The table shows the two sums for a few numbers of pieces, rounded to a hundredth of a joule.

\(n\) lower estimate (J) upper estimate (J) difference (J)

By equation (3.30) the difference of the estimates is \(k\,\Delta L^2/n\). When we double the number of pieces, it halves. We see this in figure 3.43 too. The difference between the upper and the lower staircase is a row of \(n\) grey rectangles, each of width \(h\) and height \(k h\), so their total area is \(n \cdot k h \cdot h = k\,\Delta L^2/n\). Both estimates approach the number \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2 = 32{,}48\) J, the lower one from below, the upper one from above. Is this the work? By properties 1 and 2 the work must lie between the lower and the upper estimate for every \(n\). The number \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\) lies between them, because \(1 - 1/n < 1 < 1 + 1/n\). In section 3.5.3 we will show that it is the only such number. So the work is

\[\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2 = \tfrac12\,F\,\Delta L = \tfrac12 \cdot 10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 6{,}496 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 32{,}48\ \text{J} ,\]

where we used again that \(k\,\Delta L = F\) is the final force 10 kN. Why half the product of the final force and the elongation? The force grows evenly from zero to its final value, so the average force is half the final one. On the graph the work is the area of the triangle under the line, which is half of the rectangle with sides \(F\) and \(\Delta L\).

For the line we computed the sums with the formula for the sum of the numbers \(1 + 2 + \dots + n\). For a force whose graph is not a line, for example for a bar pulled beyond the yield stress, where the relation between stress and strain bends (figure 2.11), there is no such trick. We need a general definition.

3.5.2 Lower and upper sums

Let us write the estimates of the previous section for an arbitrary function \(f\) whose input is a number \(x\) from a closed interval \([a, b]\) and whose output is a number \(f(x)\). The force, for example, has as input the elongation \(e\) from the interval \([0, \Delta L]\) and as output the force \(F(e)\). We cut the interval into pieces. The pieces need not be of equal length, since where the function changes quickly we may want shorter ones.

Definition 3.31 Partition of an interval (delitev intervala)

Let \(a < b\) be real numbers. A partition (delitev) of the closed interval \([a, b]\) is a finite set of numbers \(D = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\}\) such that

\[a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = b .\]

The intervals \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n\) are the pieces of the partition, and the numbers \(\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} = x_{\mathrm{i}} - x_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) their lengths. A partition \(D'\) of \([a, b]\) is a refinement (zgostitev) of the partition \(D\) if it contains all the points of \(D\), that is, \(D \subseteq D'\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 5.1.1 and 5.1.6 (PDF pp. 181 and 183). LEBL1 denotes a partition by \(P\), we by \(D\), because \(P\) is a point in this book.

A partition is thus a set of points that cuts the interval into \(n\) pieces. For our force, \(D = \{0, h, 2h, 3h, 4h\}\) with \(h = \Delta L/4\) is a partition of the interval \([0, \Delta L]\) into four equal pieces. The partition into eight equal pieces refines it, because it contains all its points and also the midpoints of the pieces. The partition into \(n\) equal pieces has the points \(x_{\mathrm{i}} = a + i\,(b - a)/n\). A refinement adds new points and cuts pieces, but it removes no point.

A sum of \(n\) numbers \(c_1 + c_2 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}}\) is written more briefly with the sign \(\sum\), which we met in section 1.16, as \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{i}}\). We read it "the sum of the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i\) from \(1\) to \(n\)". The lengths of the pieces of a partition add up to the length of the interval:

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n \Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} = (x_1 - x_0) + (x_2 - x_1) + \dots + (x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_{\mathrm{n}-1}) = x_{\mathrm{n}} - x_0 = b - a .\]

Each point in between is added once and subtracted once, and only the first and the last remain. A sum in which consecutive terms cancel in this way is called a telescoping sum (teleskopska vsota), because it collapses like a telescope. We will meet it again in section 3.5.7.

For the force, which grows, the smallest value on a piece was at its start and the largest at its end. For a general function this is not so. It may take its smallest value in the middle of a piece (figure 3.44), and a function with a jump may not take its smallest or largest value at all. The function that equals \(x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(0\) on \([1, 2]\) has on the piece \([0, 2]\) values that come arbitrarily close to the number \(1\) but do not reach it. So instead of the smallest and the largest value we take the infimum and the supremum of the values on the piece (definition 1.30). These exist for every bounded function: the supremum by the completeness axiom 1.32, the infimum by theorem 1.33 on the properties of the supremum and infimum, item 1.

Definition 3.32 Lower, upper and Riemann sum (spodnja, zgornja in Riemannova vsota)

Let \(f\) be a function defined on a closed interval \([a, b]\) that is bounded, that is, the set of its values \(\{f(x) : a \le x \le b\}\) is bounded (definition 1.29). Let \(D = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\}\) be a partition of \([a, b]\), and for every piece \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) let

\[m_{\mathrm{i}} = \inf\{f(x) : x_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{i}}\} , \qquad M_{\mathrm{i}} = \sup\{f(x) : x_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le x \le x_{\mathrm{i}}\} .\]

The lower sum (spodnja vsota) and the upper sum (zgornja vsota) of \(f\) for the partition \(D\) are the numbers

\[s(f, D) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad S(f, D) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n M_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{3.31}\]

If on every piece we choose a point \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(x_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le \xi_{\mathrm{i}} \le x_{\mathrm{i}}\), the number \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a Riemann sum (Riemannova vsota) of \(f\) for the partition \(D\) and the chosen points.

Source: LEBL1 Def. 5.1.1 (PDF p. 181), where the sums are written \(L(P, f)\) and \(U(P, f)\) and called the lower and upper Darboux sums. Riemann sums appear in LEBL1 Exercises 5.1.7 and 5.1.13 (PDF pp. 189-190). We write \(s\) and \(S\), because \(L\) is the length of the bar.

The lower and upper sums are thus numbers that depend on the function and on the partition. Figure 3.44 shows what they measure for a nonnegative function. The lower sum is the area of rectangles that lie under the graph and have heights \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\), the upper sum the area of rectangles that reach above the graph and have heights \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\). A Riemann sum takes the height of the graph at a chosen point of the piece. This height lies between \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a lower and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) an upper bound of the values on the piece. Where the function is negative, the products \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) are negative and count the area below the axis with a minus sign. For a force that points against the direction of motion, the work is negative too (EP, p. 97).

For a continuous function, \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) are the smallest and the largest value of the function on the piece. By theorem 1.73 on the largest and the smallest value, a continuous function on a closed piece takes both values, and the value that is the largest is the supremum: it is an upper bound of the values on the piece and is itself one of these values, so no upper bound is smaller than it. For our force, \(F\) is increasing: for \(e \le e'\) we have \(k\,e \le k\,e'\), because \(k > 0\). On the piece \([(i - 1)\,h, i\,h]\) the smallest value is therefore at the start, \(m_{\mathrm{i}} = k\,(i - 1)\,h\), and the largest at the end, \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = k\,i\,h\). The two estimates of equation (3.30) are exactly \(s(F, D_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(S(F, D_{\mathrm{n}})\), where \(D_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the partition of the interval \([0, \Delta L]\) into \(n\) equal pieces.

In the table of the previous section, doubling the number of pieces raised the lower estimate and lowered the upper one. Is this always so? When we add a point to a partition, one piece is split in two. The values of the function on each of the two parts are only some of the values on the whole piece. The greatest lower bound of the smaller set therefore cannot be lower, and the least upper bound cannot be higher (figure 3.45).

Lemma 3.33 Sums under refinement (vsote pri zgostitvi delitve)

Let \(f\) be a bounded function on a closed interval \([a, b]\).

  1. If the partition \(D'\) is a refinement of the partition \(D\), then \(s(f, D) \le s(f, D')\) and \(S(f, D') \le S(f, D)\).
  2. For any two partitions \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) of \([a, b]\), \(s(f, D_1) \le S(f, D_2)\).
Proof

We must show that a refinement does not decrease the lower sum and does not increase the upper sum, and that no lower sum is larger than any upper sum.

1. Refinement.

  1. First let \(D'\) have only one point more than \(D\), a point \(c\) in the piece \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\), so \(x_{\mathrm{j}-1} < c < x_{\mathrm{j}}\). The partition \(D'\) has the same pieces as \(D\), only the piece \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\) is split into \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, c]\) and \([c, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\).
  2. Let \(m'\) and \(m''\) be the infima of the values of \(f\) on \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, c]\) and on \([c, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\). The number \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\) is a lower bound of all the values on the whole piece, so also of the values on the part \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, c]\). The infimum \(m'\) is the greatest lower bound of these values, so \(m_{\mathrm{j}} \le m'\). Likewise \(m_{\mathrm{j}} \le m''\).
  3. The piece \([x_{\mathrm{j}-1}, x_{\mathrm{j}}]\) contributes to the lower sum for \(D\) the term \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{j}} = m_{\mathrm{j}}\,(c - x_{\mathrm{j}-1}) + m_{\mathrm{j}}\,(x_{\mathrm{j}} - c)\), and its two parts contribute to the lower sum for \(D'\) the terms \(m'\,(c - x_{\mathrm{j}-1}) + m''\,(x_{\mathrm{j}} - c)\). The lengths \(c - x_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{j}} - c\) are positive, so by step 2 the second contribution is not smaller than the first.
  4. The contributions of the other pieces are the same in both sums. Hence \(s(f, D) \le s(f, D')\).
  5. For the upper sum the argument is the same with the inequalities reversed. The number \(M_{\mathrm{j}}\) is an upper bound of the values on the whole piece, so also on each part, and therefore the suprema \(M'\) and \(M''\) on the parts are not larger than \(M_{\mathrm{j}}\). By the same computation as in step 3, \(S(f, D') \le S(f, D)\).
  6. A general refinement \(D'\) has finitely many points more than \(D\), say \(r\). We obtain it by adding the points one after another, which gives a row of partitions \(D = D_0, D_1, \dots, D_{\mathrm{r}} = D'\), each with one point more than the one before. By steps 1 to 5, \(s(f, D_{\mathrm{q}-1}) \le s(f, D_{\mathrm{q}})\) and \(S(f, D_{\mathrm{q}}) \le S(f, D_{\mathrm{q}-1})\) for every \(q\). The inequalities chain as in lemma 1.50 on the terms of a monotone sequence, so \(s(f, D) \le s(f, D')\) and \(S(f, D') \le S(f, D)\). For \(r = 0\) the partitions are equal and the inequalities are equalities.

2. A lower sum is not larger than an upper sum.

  1. Let \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) be partitions of \([a, b]\). The set \(D = D_1 \cup D_2\) is finite, contains \(a\) and \(b\), and all its points lie in \([a, b]\). Ordered by size, \(D\) is a partition of \([a, b]\). It contains all the points of \(D_1\) and all the points of \(D_2\), so it is a refinement of both.
  2. On every piece of \(D\), \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le M_{\mathrm{i}}\). Indeed, for any value on the piece, for example \(f(x_{\mathrm{i}})\), we have \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le f(x_{\mathrm{i}}) \le M_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a lower and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) an upper bound of the values on the piece. Hence \(s(f, D) \le S(f, D)\).
  3. By item 1, applied to \(D_1\) and its refinement \(D\) and to \(D_2\) and its refinement \(D\), the inequalities chain:
    \[s(f, D_1) \le s(f, D) \le S(f, D) \le S(f, D_2) .\]

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 5.1.7 and part of the proof of Prop. 5.1.8 (PDF pp. 183-185). The source leaves the inequality for the upper sum in item 1 to the reader.

Item 1 says that with a finer partition the lower sums grow and the upper sums shrink, as in the table. Item 2 says something the table does not show. The lower estimate with 32 pieces is not only larger than the lower estimate with 4 pieces, but also not larger than any upper estimate, not even the upper estimate for a partition with pieces of different lengths. No lower sum lies to the right of any upper sum (figure 3.46). The two can be equal, for example for a constant function, where every lower and every upper sum equals the value of the function times the length of the interval.

3.5.3 The integral

By requirements 1 and 2 of section 3.5.1 the work lies between all the lower and all the upper sums of the force. Which numbers lie there? The set of all lower sums of a function \(f\) is nonempty and, by lemma 3.33 on sums under refinement, item 2, bounded above by every upper sum. By the completeness axiom 1.32 it therefore has a supremum. The set of all upper sums is nonempty and bounded below by every lower sum, so by theorem 1.33, item 1, it has an infimum. The supremum of the lower sums is the smallest number that no lower sum exceeds. The infimum of the upper sums is the largest number below which no upper sum falls. If the two are equal, exactly one number lies between all the lower and all the upper sums.

From history

From 1672 to 1676 Leibniz lived mostly in Paris and computed areas as sums (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 225). Following Cavalieri, he wrote the sum as omn., for the Latin omnia, all (CAJORI, PDF p. 223). In a manuscript dated 29 October 1675, however, he wrote that it will be useful to write \(\int\) instead of omn., that is, a sum (CAJORI, PDF p. 223, CAJNOT2, PDF p. 225-227). He printed the sign only in 1686, eleven years later (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 266).

For a long time there was also a second view. Euler understood the quantity that the sums seek as the reverse of differentiation, that is, as a function whose derivative is the given function. Cauchy again introduced Leibniz' original idea, a number as the limit of sums (CAJORI, PDF p. 385). Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann (1826 to 1866, CAJORI, PDF p. 437), however, rejected Cauchy's definition because of its arbitrariness, gave a new one, and asked when a number that the sums determine exists at all (CAJORI, PDF p. 392).

Let us return to the lower and upper sums, between which lies a single number when the supremum of the lower sums and the infimum of the upper sums are equal. The following definition names this number.

Definition 3.34 Integral (integral)

Let \(f\) be a bounded function on a closed interval \([a, b]\). The lower integral (spodnji integral) of \(f\) on \([a, b]\) is the supremum of the set of all its lower sums, and the upper integral (zgornji integral) the infimum of the set of all its upper sums:

\[\begin{aligned} \underline{I} &= \sup\{s(f, D) : D \text{ is a partition of } [a, b]\} , \\ \overline{I} &= \inf\{S(f, D) : D \text{ is a partition of } [a, b]\} . \end{aligned}\]

Both exist, because by lemma 3.33, item 2, every upper sum is an upper bound of the lower sums and every lower sum a lower bound of the upper sums. The function \(f\) is integrable (integrabilna) on \([a, b]\) if \(\underline{I} = \overline{I}\). This common number is then the integral (integral) of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\), written

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \underline{I} = \overline{I} .\]

We also agree that \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^a f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 5.1.3 and 5.1.9 (PDF pp. 182 and 185), the convention on PDF p. 188. LEBL1 calls this integral the Riemann integral and builds it with lower and upper (Darboux) sums, which gives the same integral as Riemann sums and is technically simpler (PDF p. 181).

The integral is thus a number that depends on the function \(f\) and on the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\). The sign \(\int\) is an elongated letter S, the initial of the Latin word summa, sum. It was introduced by Leibniz (LEBL1 Remark 5.1.15, PDF p. 188). The word integral was first used in print by Jakob Bernoulli. Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli finally agreed on the name integral calculus and on Leibniz' sign (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 206). The notation \(f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) recalls the terms \(f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) of a Riemann sum, but \(\mathrm{d}x\) is not a number. It only says which variable runs over the interval. The name of this variable does not matter: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) are the same number, just as \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^n c_{\mathrm{j}}\) are the same sum. We will use this whenever the letter \(x\) is already taken. The convention \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^a f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) says that a force does no work on a path of length zero. We will need it in section 3.5.7. For a nonnegative function the integral is the area of the region between the graph and the axis over the interval \([a, b]\). For regions with a curved boundary we may take the integral as the definition of the area, since school geometry knows only the areas of regions made of rectangles and triangles, and of the circle.

Why does the definition demand that the lower and the upper integral be equal? The next lemma says that then the integral and no other number lies between all the lower and all the upper sums, and that for integrability it is enough to find partitions with a small difference of the sums.

Lemma 3.35 The integral between the lower and upper sums (integral med spodnjimi in zgornjimi vsotami)

Let \(f\) be a bounded function on a closed interval \([a, b]\), \(\underline{I}\) its lower and \(\overline{I}\) its upper integral.

  1. \(\underline{I} \le \overline{I}\).
  2. A number \(I\) lies between all the lower and all the upper sums, that is, \(s(f, D) \le I \le S(f, D)\) for every partition \(D\), if and only if \(\underline{I} \le I \le \overline{I}\). If \(f\) is integrable, its integral is therefore the only such number.
  3. If for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a partition \(D\) with \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\), then \(f\) is integrable.
Proof

We must show all three items. All follow from the definition of the supremum and infimum and from lemma 3.33 on sums under refinement.

1. The lower integral is not larger than the upper one.

  1. Let \(D_2\) be any partition. By lemma 3.33, item 2, \(s(f, D_1) \le S(f, D_2)\) for every partition \(D_1\), so \(S(f, D_2)\) is an upper bound of the set of lower sums.
  2. The supremum is not larger than any upper bound (definition 1.30), so \(\underline{I} \le S(f, D_2)\).
  3. This holds for every partition \(D_2\), so \(\underline{I}\) is a lower bound of the set of upper sums. The infimum is not smaller than any lower bound, so \(\underline{I} \le \overline{I}\).

2. The numbers between all the sums.

  1. Let \(s(f, D) \le I \le S(f, D)\) for every partition \(D\). The left inequality says that \(I\) is an upper bound of the set of lower sums, so \(\underline{I} \le I\). The right one says that \(I\) is a lower bound of the set of upper sums, so \(I \le \overline{I}\).
  2. Let \(\underline{I} \le I \le \overline{I}\) and let \(D\) be any partition. The supremum is an upper bound of the lower sums, so \(s(f, D) \le \underline{I}\). The infimum is a lower bound of the upper sums, so \(\overline{I} \le S(f, D)\). The inequalities chain:
    \[s(f, D) \le \underline{I} \le I \le \overline{I} \le S(f, D) .\]
  3. Let \(f\) be integrable, that is, \(\underline{I} = \overline{I}\). By step 1 every number \(I\) that lies between all the sums satisfies \(\underline{I} \le I \le \underline{I}\), so \(I = \underline{I}\), which is the integral. By step 2 the integral does lie between all the sums.

3. A small difference of the sums is enough.

  1. We choose an arbitrary error \(\eta > 0\) and a partition \(D\) with \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\).
  2. As in step 2 of item 2, \(s(f, D) \le \underline{I}\) and \(\overline{I} \le S(f, D)\), and by item 1 \(\underline{I} \le \overline{I}\). So
    \[s(f, D) \le \underline{I} \le \overline{I} \le S(f, D) .\]
  3. The difference of the inner two numbers is not larger than the difference of the outer two, so \(0 \le \overline{I} - \underline{I} \le S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\).
  4. We chose the error arbitrarily. By lemma 1.34 on an inequality tested with all positive numbers, therefore \(\overline{I} - \underline{I} = 0\), and \(f\) is integrable.

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 5.1.8 and 5.1.13 (PDF pp. 184-185 and 187). Item 2 is not from the source.

Now we can finish the computation of the work from section 3.5.1. The function \(F(e) = k\,e\) on the interval \([0, \Delta L]\) has the elongation as input and the force as output, with \(k = 1{,}539 \cdot 10^6\) N/m and \(\Delta L = 6{,}496\) mm. We must show two things: that it is integrable, and that its integral is \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\).

  1. For the partition \(D_{\mathrm{n}}\) into \(n\) equal pieces the lower and upper sums are those of equation (3.30), \(s(F, D_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\,(1 - 1/n)\) and \(S(F, D_{\mathrm{n}}) = \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\,(1 + 1/n)\), as we saw in section 3.5.2.
  2. Their difference is \(S(F, D_{\mathrm{n}}) - s(F, D_{\mathrm{n}}) = k\,\Delta L^2/n\). We choose an error \(\eta > 0\). By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a natural number \(n\) with \(n\,\eta > k\,\Delta L^2\), that is, with \(k\,\Delta L^2/n < \eta\). By lemma 3.35, item 3, \(F\) is integrable.
  3. The number \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\) lies between \(s(F, D_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \(S(F, D_{\mathrm{n}})\) for every \(n\), because \(1 - 1/n < 1 < 1 + 1/n\). By item 2 of the same lemma the integral \(I = \int_0^{\Delta L} k\,e\,\mathrm{d}e\) lies between them too.
  4. Two numbers in the same interval are at most its length apart, so \(|I - \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2| \le k\,\Delta L^2/n\) for every \(n\).
  5. For a given error \(\eta > 0\), by step 2 the right side is smaller than \(\eta\) for \(n\) large enough. So \(|I - \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2| < \eta\) for every \(\eta > 0\), and by lemma 1.34 the difference is zero:
    \[\int_0^{\Delta L} k\,e\,\mathrm{d}e = \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2 .\]

The work that the machine does while it slowly raises the force from zero to 10 kN is therefore 32,48 J. The argument holds for every force that points in the direction of motion and is an integrable function of the elongation. By requirements 1 and 2 the work lies between all the lower and upper sums of the force, and by item 2 of the lemma the integral is the only such number. The work done by a force along a straight path is therefore the integral of the force over the path. The result \(\tfrac12 F\,\Delta L\), however, holds only as long as the force is proportional to the elongation. Above the yield stress the graph of the force bends, the work is still the area under the graph, but it is no longer half of the rectangle.

Is every bounded function integrable? No. Take the function on \([0, 1]\) that equals \(1\) at the fractions and \(0\) at the numbers that are not fractions. Every piece of a partition contains both. That a piece \([c, d]\) contains a fraction \(q\) with \(c < q < d\) is LEBL1 Thm. 1.2.4 (ii) (PDF p. 31). That it also contains a number that is not a fraction we see as follows. By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a natural number \(n\) with \(n\,(d - q) > \sqrt 3\), where \(\sqrt 3 > 0\) is the number with square \(3\) (theorem 1.36 on the existence of square roots). Since \(0 < \sqrt 3/n < d - q\), the number \(q + \sqrt 3/n\) lies between \(q\) and \(d\). It is not a fraction. If it were, then \(\sqrt 3 = n\,\big((q + \sqrt 3/n) - q\big)\) would be a fraction too, because a difference and a multiple of fractions are again fractions, but by lemma 1.27 no rational number has square 3. So on every piece \(m_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and \(M_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\). Every lower sum is \(0\), every upper sum \(1\), and the lower integral \(0\) differs from the upper integral \(1\) (LEBL1 Example 5.1.4, PDF p. 183). Such a function does not occur in mechanics, but it shows that integrability cannot be taken for granted. Which functions are integrable is the subject of section 3.5.5. Before that we need a new form of continuity.

3.5.4 Uniform continuity

For the force \(F(e) = k\,e\) we showed integrability by computing the sums. For a general function we have no such computation. Item 3 of lemma 3.35 on the integral between the sums, however, tells us what is enough: a partition for which the difference of the sums

\[S(f, D) - s(f, D) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n (M_{\mathrm{i}} - m_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\]

is small. The difference \(M_{\mathrm{i}} - m_{\mathrm{i}}\) says how much the values of the function on the piece differ from each other. If it were smaller than \(\eta/(b - a)\) on every piece, the difference of the sums would be smaller than \(\eta/(b - a)\) times the sum of the lengths of the pieces, \(b - a\), so smaller than \(\eta\). Can we choose the pieces that short for a continuous function? Continuity says something similar, but not enough. A function \(f\) is continuous on a set \(I\) of real numbers if it is continuous at every point \(c \in I\): for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(x) - f(c)| < \eta\) for every \(x \in I\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) (definition 1.58). The band \(\delta\), however, may be different at every point \(c\). A partition has pieces at all places of the interval, and we need one single length of the pieces that is enough everywhere.

That this is not hair-splitting is shown by the function \(f(x) = 1/x\) on the half-open interval \((0, 1]\). It is continuous at every point \(c \in (0, 1]\). Let us show this from the definition and look at the band \(\delta\) we get.

  1. We choose \(\eta > 0\). We write the difference over a common denominator: \(\dfrac1x - \dfrac1c = \dfrac{c - x}{x\,c}\), so \(\Big|\dfrac1x - \dfrac1c\Big| = \dfrac{|x - c|}{x\,c}\).
  2. If \(|x - c| < c/2\), then \(x > c - c/2 = c/2\), so the denominator is \(x\,c > c^2/2\) and \(\Big|\dfrac1x - \dfrac1c\Big| \le \dfrac{2\,|x - c|}{c^2}\). The inequality is not strict, because at \(x = c\) both sides are zero.
  3. We take \(\delta\) as the smaller of the numbers \(c/2\) and \(c^2\eta/2\). For \(|x - c| < \delta\) step 2 applies and \(2\,|x - c|/c^2 < 2\delta/c^2 \le \eta\). Hence \(|1/x - 1/c| < \eta\), and \(f\) is continuous at \(c\).

The band we get shrinks as \(c\) approaches zero. For \(\eta = 1\) it is \(0{,}5\) at \(c = 1\), only \(0{,}005\) at \(c = 0{,}1\), and \(0{,}00005\) at \(c = 0{,}01\). Is this only the fault of the crude estimate in step 2? No. To the left of \(c\) the value \(1/x\) grows, and the condition \(1/x - 1/c < \eta\) holds exactly for \(x > c/(1 + c\eta)\). The band to the left can therefore reach at most the distance

\[c - \frac{c}{1 + c\eta} = \frac{c^2\eta}{1 + c\eta} ,\]

which for \(c = 0{,}1\) and \(\eta = 1\) equals \(0{,}0091\) and shrinks like \(c^2\) for \(c\) close to zero. The closer the point is to zero, the steeper the graph, and the narrower the band must be (figure 3.47). For a function like the displacement of the hanging bar, on the other hand, one single band is enough for all points. We give such functions a name.

Definition 3.36 Uniform continuity (enakomerna zveznost)

Let \(f\) be a function defined on a set \(I\) of real numbers, for example on an interval. The function \(f\) is uniformly continuous (enakomerno zvezna) on \(I\) if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(x) - f(y)| < \eta\) for all numbers \(x, y \in I\) with \(|x - y| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.4.1 (PDF p. 138).

Let us compare with continuity. For continuity on \(I\) we first choose a point \(c\) and an error \(\eta\), and only then look for the band, which may therefore depend on both. For uniform continuity we choose only the error \(\eta\), and the band must then serve all pairs of points at once. The word uniformly says that the band is the same over the whole set. Every uniformly continuous function is continuous, since in the definition we may take \(y = c\). The converse does not hold, as we will see for \(1/x\).

Let us show that the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), is uniformly continuous on \([0, L]\). Recall that the bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points down from the top, and \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). First we write the definition out for this case. We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|a\,(2Lx - x^2) - a\,(2Ly - y^2)| < \eta\) for all numbers \(x, y \in [0, L]\) with \(|x - y| < \delta\).

  1. We choose an arbitrary error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. We rewrite the difference. Since \(x^2 - y^2 = (x - y)(x + y)\),
    \[\begin{aligned} a\,(2Lx - x^2) - a\,(2Ly - y^2) &= a\,\big(2L\,(x - y) - (x - y)(x + y)\big) \\ &= a\,(x - y)(2L - x - y) . \end{aligned}\]
  3. For \(x, y \in [0, L]\) we have \(0 \le x + y \le 2L\), so \(0 \le 2L - x - y \le 2L\). Step 2 gives \(|u(x) - u(y)| = a\,|x - y|\,(2L - x - y) \le 2aL\,|x - y|\).
  4. We take \(\delta = \eta/(2aL)\), which is a positive number. For \(|x - y| < \delta\) step 3 gives \(|u(x) - u(y)| \le 2aL\,|x - y| < 2aL\,\delta = \eta\).
  5. The band \(\delta\) does not depend on \(x\) and \(y\), only on \(\eta\). The displacement \(u\) is therefore uniformly continuous on \([0, L]\).

The number \(2aL = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) is the largest strain of the bar, the one at the top. It is the one-sided derivative \(u'(0)\), the limit of the difference quotient from the right, since the top is an end of the bar (definition 1.9). The displacements of two points therefore differ by at most this strain times the distance between the points. For the error \(\eta = 1\ \mu\text{m}\) the band is \(\delta = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}/(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}) = 0{,}2561\) m. Wherever on the bar we take two points closer than 25,61 cm, their displacements differ by less than a micrometre.

For \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) no such band exists. That the function \(f(x) = 1/x\) is not uniformly continuous on \((0, 1]\) means: there is an error \(\eta > 0\) for which no band works, that is, for every \(\delta > 0\) there are \(x, y \in (0, 1]\) with \(|x - y| < \delta\) and \(|f(x) - f(y)| \ge \eta\). Let us take \(\eta = 1\) and an arbitrary \(\delta > 0\).

  1. Let \(x\) be the smaller of the numbers \(1\) and \(\delta\), and \(y = x/2\). Both numbers are in \((0, 1]\).
  2. The distance is \(|x - y| = x/2 \le \delta/2 < \delta\).
  3. The difference of the values is \(\dfrac1y - \dfrac1x = \dfrac2x - \dfrac1x = \dfrac1x \ge 1\), because \(x \le 1\).

So for the error \(1\) no band works, and \(1/x\) is not uniformly continuous on \((0, 1]\), although it is continuous at every point.

For \(1/x\) the points where the band fails crowd together at the endpoint \(0\), which is not in the interval. On a closed interval this cannot happen.

Theorem 3.37 A continuous function on a closed interval is uniformly continuous (zvezna funkcija na zaprtem intervalu je enakomerno zvezna)

Let \(f\) be a continuous function on a closed interval \([a, b]\). Then \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \([a, b]\).

Proof

We must show that for every error there is one band for all pairs of points. We argue by contradiction.

  1. Suppose that \(f\) is not uniformly continuous. Then there is an error \(\eta > 0\) for which no band works: for every \(\delta > 0\) there are numbers \(x, y \in [a, b]\) with \(|x - y| < \delta\) and \(|f(x) - f(y)| \ge \eta\).
  2. We use this with the band \(\delta = 1/n\) for every natural number \(n\). We get numbers \(x_{\mathrm{n}}, y_{\mathrm{n}} \in [a, b]\) with \(|x_{\mathrm{n}} - y_{\mathrm{n}}| < 1/n\) and \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(y_{\mathrm{n}})| \ge \eta\). This gives two sequences \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) and \((y_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  3. The sequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}})\) is bounded, because all its terms are in \([a, b]\). By the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem 1.57 it has a convergent subsequence \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) (definition 1.52). We denote its limit by \(c\).
  4. Since \(a \le x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} \le b\) for every \(k\), lemma 1.48, that limits preserve non-strict inequalities, item 2, gives \(a \le c \le b\) too. Here we need the interval to be closed.
  5. The sequence \((y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) also converges to \(c\). By the triangle inequality for numbers \(|p + q| \le |p| + |q|\) (lemma 1.6, item 1),
    \[|y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| \le |y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}| + |x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| < \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{k}}} + |x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| .\]
  6. By lemma 1.53 on the indices of a subsequence, \(n_{\mathrm{k}} \ge k\), so \(1/n_{\mathrm{k}} \le 1/k\).
  7. We choose \(\eta' > 0\). By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a \(K_1\) such that \(1/k < \eta'/2\) for \(k \ge K_1\). Since \((x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(c\), there is a \(K_2\) such that \(|x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| < \eta'/2\) for \(k \ge K_2\). For \(k\) not smaller than the larger of \(K_1\) and \(K_2\), steps 5 and 6 give \(|y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}} - c| < \eta'\). We chose the error \(\eta'\) arbitrarily, so \((y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(c\) (definition 1.38).
  8. The function \(f\) is continuous at the point \(c\), which by step 4 lies in \([a, b]\). By lemma 1.61 on continuity through sequences, therefore, \(f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(f(c)\) and \(f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}})\) converges to \(f(c)\).
  9. By the definition of the limit of a sequence 1.38, for the error \(\eta/2\) there are places for both sequences of step 8. Let \(K\) be larger than both. Then \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - f(c)| < \eta/2\) and \(|f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}) - f(c)| < \eta/2\) for every \(k \ge K\). By the triangle inequality, for \(k = K\),
    \[|f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{K}}) - f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{K}})| \le |f(x_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{K}}) - f(c)| + |f(c) - f(y_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{K}})| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  10. This contradicts step 2, by which \(|f(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - f(y_{\mathrm{n}})| \ge \eta\) for every \(n\), also for \(n = n_{\mathrm{K}}\). The assumption of step 1 is false, and \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \([a, b]\).

Source: LEBL1 Thm. 3.4.4 (PDF p. 139). The proof follows the source, steps 7 and 9 are written out in more detail.

The proof used both properties of a closed interval. Boundedness gave a convergent subsequence in step 3, closedness made sure in step 4 that its limit lies in the interval, where \(f\) is continuous. For \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) the numbers \(x_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/n\) and \(y_{\mathrm{n}} = 1/(2n)\) would be a pair at a distance below \(1/n\) with a difference of values \(n \ge 1\). They crowd together at \(0\), where the function is not even defined. So step 4 fails, because the limit \(0\) is not in the interval, and with it step 8, which needs continuity at the limit.

3.5.5 Continuous functions are integrable

Now let us prove what we announced at the start of section 3.5.4. Uniform continuity gives one band \(\delta\) for the whole function. On pieces shorter than \(\delta\) the values differ little, and the difference of the sums is small.

Theorem 3.38 A continuous function is integrable (zvezna funkcija je integrabilna)

Let \(f\) be a continuous function on a closed interval \([a, b]\).

  1. For every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\) for every partition \(D\) of \([a, b]\) whose pieces are all shorter than \(\delta\).
  2. The function \(f\) is integrable on \([a, b]\).
  3. For the same \(\eta\) and \(\delta\) as in item 1,
    \[\Big|\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big| < \eta\]
    for every partition \(D = \{x_0, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\}\) with pieces shorter than \(\delta\) and for every choice of points \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(x_{\mathrm{i}-1} \le \xi_{\mathrm{i}} \le x_{\mathrm{i}}\).
Proof

We must show that for pieces short enough the lower and upper sums are close, and derive from this integrability and the estimate of Riemann sums.

1. Short pieces give a small difference of the sums.

  1. The function \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), so by lemma 1.72 it is bounded, and the sums exist.
  2. We choose \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 3.37 \(f\) is uniformly continuous, so for the error \(\eta/(b - a)\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|f(x) - f(y)| < \eta/(b - a)\) for all numbers \(x, y \in [a, b]\) with \(|x - y| < \delta\).
  3. Let \(D\) be a partition whose pieces are all shorter than \(\delta\), and \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) one of its pieces. The function \(f\) is continuous on this piece too, since continuity at a point of the piece checks only some of the points that continuity on \([a, b]\) checks. By theorem 1.73 on the largest and the smallest value it therefore takes its largest value on the piece at some point \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) and its smallest at some point \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  4. The largest value \(f(p_{\mathrm{i}})\) is an upper bound of the values on the piece and is itself one of them, so no upper bound is smaller than it, and \(f(p_{\mathrm{i}}) = M_{\mathrm{i}}\). Likewise \(f(q_{\mathrm{i}}) = m_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  5. The points \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) lie in a piece shorter than \(\delta\), so \(|p_{\mathrm{i}} - q_{\mathrm{i}}| < \delta\). By step 2, \(M_{\mathrm{i}} - m_{\mathrm{i}} = f(p_{\mathrm{i}}) - f(q_{\mathrm{i}}) < \eta/(b - a)\).
  6. We multiply by the positive length \(\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and add over all pieces. The lengths add up to \(b - a\) (the telescoping sum of section 3.5.2), so
    \[S(f, D) - s(f, D) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n (M_{\mathrm{i}} - m_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} < \frac{\eta}{b - a} \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n \Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} = \eta .\]

2. Integrability.

  1. A partition with pieces shorter than \(\delta\) exists. By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a natural number \(n\) with \(n\,\delta > b - a\), and the pieces of the partition into \(n\) equal pieces have length \((b - a)/n < \delta\).
  2. By item 1, \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\) for this partition. We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily, so by lemma 3.35, item 3, \(f\) is integrable.

3. Riemann sums are close to the integral.

  1. Let \(D\) be a partition with pieces shorter than \(\delta\), and \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) points in the pieces. Since \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a lower and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) an upper bound of the values on the piece, \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}) \le M_{\mathrm{i}}\). We multiply by the positive length \(\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and add: \(s(f, D) \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} \le S(f, D)\).
  2. By lemma 3.35, item 2, the integral also lies between \(s(f, D)\) and \(S(f, D)\).
  3. Two numbers in the same interval are at most its length apart, so the Riemann sum and the integral differ by at most \(S(f, D) - s(f, D)\), which by item 1 is smaller than \(\eta\).

Source: LEBL1 Lemma 5.2.7 (PDF p. 195) for items 1 and 2 with equal pieces. Item 3 is a stronger form of LEBL1 Exercise 5.1.7 (PDF p. 189), which asks for only one partition. The form with pieces shorter than \(\delta\) is LEBL1 Exercise 5.1.13 (PDF p. 190), which also says that the integral defined with Riemann sums has this form in other books.

Since the force \(F(e) = k\,e\) is continuous, the theorem says once more that it is integrable, this time without computing sums. Item 3 says how to compute an integral approximately: we take the pieces short enough and on each piece the value of the function at any point. The methods by which computers compute integrals rest on this idea. The simplest is the rectangular rule (KREYSZIG §19.5, PDF pp. 853-854), more accurate ones weigh the values on a piece differently. The pieces need not be of equal length, it is enough that they are all shorter than \(\delta\).

The theorem needs continuity on the whole closed interval. Bounded functions with finitely many jumps are integrable too, for example the axial force of a bar with a weight hung at its middle (section 1.12), but we will not need this and do not prove it (LEBL1 Thm. 5.2.9, PDF p. 196).

3.5.6 Properties of the integral

When we compute work we expect a few rules. The work of two forces that act on the same point at the same time is the sum of their works. The work on a path made of two parts is the sum of the works on the two parts. A larger force on the same path does at least as much work. We now prove these rules for continuous functions. For continuous ones, because theorem 3.38 gives us partitions with an arbitrarily small difference of the sums, which shortens the proofs. All the rules hold for all integrable functions too (LEBL1 Prop. 5.1.10, 5.2.2, 5.2.4 and 5.2.6, PDF pp. 185 and 192-194), but in the book we will integrate only continuous ones. One idea builds the proofs: two numbers that lie in the same interval of arbitrarily small length are equal.

Lemma 3.39 Properties of the integral (lastnosti integrala)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be continuous functions on a closed interval \([a, b]\) and \(\alpha\) a real number.

  1. Linearity: \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (f(x) + g(x))\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \alpha f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \alpha \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. Additivity: if \(a < c < b\), then \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^c f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_{\mathrm{c}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. Bounds: if \(m\) and \(M\) are numbers with \(m \le f(x) \le M\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\), then \(\displaystyle m\,(b - a) \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \le M\,(b - a)\). In particular \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \alpha\,\mathrm{d}x = \alpha\,(b - a)\).
  4. Monotonicity: if \(f(x) \le g(x)\) for every \(x \in [a, b]\), then \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\).
Proof

We must show four items. For the first two we show that both sides of the equation lie in the same interval, which can be arbitrarily short. The third follows from the partition with a single piece, the fourth from the first and the third.

1. Linearity.

  1. The function \(f + g\) is continuous by corollary 1.64 on the sum and product of continuous functions, so by theorem 3.38 it is integrable. We denote the integrals of \(f\), \(g\) and \(f + g\) by \(I_{\mathrm{f}}\), \(I_{\mathrm{g}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{f}+\mathrm{g}}\).
  2. Let \(D\) be a partition and \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) one of its pieces, on which \(f\) and \(g\) have the infima \(m_{\mathrm{i}}(f)\) and \(m_{\mathrm{i}}(g)\). For every \(x\) on the piece, \(f(x) + g(x) \ge m_{\mathrm{i}}(f) + m_{\mathrm{i}}(g)\). The number \(m_{\mathrm{i}}(f) + m_{\mathrm{i}}(g)\) is therefore a lower bound of the values of \(f + g\) on the piece and is not larger than its infimum \(m_{\mathrm{i}}(f + g)\). Likewise the supremum \(M_{\mathrm{i}}(f + g)\) is not larger than \(M_{\mathrm{i}}(f) + M_{\mathrm{i}}(g)\).
  3. We multiply by the lengths of the pieces and add:
    \[s(f, D) + s(g, D) \le s(f + g, D) \le S(f + g, D) \le S(f, D) + S(g, D) .\]
  4. By lemma 3.35, item 2, \(I_{\mathrm{f}+\mathrm{g}}\) lies between \(s(f + g, D)\) and \(S(f + g, D)\), so by step 3 between \(s(f, D) + s(g, D)\) and \(S(f, D) + S(g, D)\). The number \(I_{\mathrm{f}} + I_{\mathrm{g}}\) lies there too, as we get by adding the inequalities \(s(f, D) \le I_{\mathrm{f}} \le S(f, D)\) and \(s(g, D) \le I_{\mathrm{g}} \le S(g, D)\).
  5. We choose \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 3.38, item 1, there is a number \(\delta_{\mathrm{f}} > 0\) such that the difference of the sums of \(f\) is smaller than \(\eta/2\) for every partition with pieces shorter than \(\delta_{\mathrm{f}}\), and a number \(\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\) with the same property for \(g\). By the Archimedean property 1.35 there is a partition into equal pieces shorter than both numbers. For it, \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta/2\) and \(S(g, D) - s(g, D) < \eta/2\).
  6. The numbers \(I_{\mathrm{f}+\mathrm{g}}\) and \(I_{\mathrm{f}} + I_{\mathrm{g}}\) therefore lie in an interval shorter than \(\eta\), and \(|I_{\mathrm{f}+\mathrm{g}} - (I_{\mathrm{f}} + I_{\mathrm{g}})| < \eta\). We chose the error arbitrarily, so by lemma 1.34 they are equal.
  7. The function \(\alpha f\) is continuous by the same corollary, so it is integrable. Let \(D\) be a partition. If \(\alpha \ge 0\), multiplying by \(\alpha\) does not reverse the inequalities \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le f(x) \le M_{\mathrm{i}}\): \(\alpha m_{\mathrm{i}} \le \alpha f(x) \le \alpha M_{\mathrm{i}}\). So \(\alpha m_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a lower bound of the values of \(\alpha f\) on the piece and \(\alpha M_{\mathrm{i}}\) an upper one, and after adding, \(\alpha\,s(f, D) \le s(\alpha f, D) \le S(\alpha f, D) \le \alpha\,S(f, D)\). Multiplying \(s(f, D) \le I_{\mathrm{f}} \le S(f, D)\) by \(\alpha\) we see that \(\alpha I_{\mathrm{f}}\) also lies between \(\alpha\,s(f, D)\) and \(\alpha\,S(f, D)\).
  8. If \(\alpha < 0\), multiplication reverses the inequalities: \(\alpha M_{\mathrm{i}} \le \alpha f(x) \le \alpha m_{\mathrm{i}}\). As in step 7 we find that the integral of \(\alpha f\) and the number \(\alpha I_{\mathrm{f}}\) lie between \(\alpha\,S(f, D)\) and \(\alpha\,s(f, D)\).
  9. In both cases the interval has length \(|\alpha|\,(S(f, D) - s(f, D))\). By theorem 3.38 there is a partition with \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta/(|\alpha| + 1)\), for which the interval is shorter than \(\eta\). As in step 6, the integral of \(\alpha f\) and \(\alpha I_{\mathrm{f}}\) are equal.

2. Additivity.

  1. The function \(f\) is continuous on \([a, c]\) and on \([c, b]\) too, so both integrals on the right exist.
  2. Let \(D_1\) be a partition of \([a, c]\) and \(D_2\) a partition of \([c, b]\). The set \(D = D_1 \cup D_2\) is a partition of \([a, b]\) that contains \(c\), and its pieces are the pieces of \(D_1\) and \(D_2\). Hence \(s(f, D) = s(f, D_1) + s(f, D_2)\) and \(S(f, D) = S(f, D_1) + S(f, D_2)\).
  3. By lemma 3.35, item 2, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\) lies between \(s(f, D)\) and \(S(f, D)\). The sum \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^c f + \int_{\mathrm{c}}^b f\) lies between \(s(f, D_1) + s(f, D_2)\) and \(S(f, D_1) + S(f, D_2)\), so by step 2 between the same two numbers.
  4. We choose \(\eta > 0\). By theorem 3.38, applied on \([a, c]\) and on \([c, b]\), there are partitions \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) for which the difference of the sums is smaller than \(\eta/2\). Then \(S(f, D) - s(f, D) < \eta\), and as in step 6 of item 1 the two sides of the equation are equal.

3. Bounds.

  1. We take the partition \(D_0 = \{a, b\}\) with a single piece \([a, b]\). The number \(m\) is a lower bound of all the values of the function, and their infimum \(m_1\) is the greatest lower bound, so \(m \le m_1\) and \(m\,(b - a) \le m_1\,(b - a) = s(f, D_0)\).
  2. By lemma 3.35, item 2, \(s(f, D_0) \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\), so \(m\,(b - a) \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\).
  3. Likewise \(M_1 \le M\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f \le S(f, D_0) = M_1\,(b - a) \le M\,(b - a)\).
  4. For a constant function with value \(\alpha\) we take \(m = M = \alpha\) and get \(\alpha\,(b - a) \le \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \alpha\,\mathrm{d}x \le \alpha\,(b - a)\).

4. Monotonicity.

  1. The function \(g - f = g + (-1)\,f\) is continuous by corollary 1.64 and nonnegative, because \(f(x) \le g(x)\).
  2. By item 3 with \(m = 0\), \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (g - f) \ge 0\).
  3. By item 1, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (g - f) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g + (-1)\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\). Hence \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f \ge 0\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 5.2.4, 5.2.2, 5.1.10 and 5.2.6 (PDF pp. 193, 192, 185 and 194). The source proves the items for all integrable functions and leaves the first item for \(\alpha < 0\) and for the sum to the reader. The proofs for continuous functions through theorem 3.38 are not from the source.

Let us test the properties on the hanging bar (figure 3.48). A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a round cross section of area \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) and density \(\rho = 7800\ \text{kg/m}^3\) hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre of the top face. What is the weight of the part of the bar below the cross section at depth \(x\)? In chapter 2 we computed it as density times gravitational acceleration times volume, \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\) (equation (2.3)). Now let us compute it with the integral. Since the letter \(x\) is taken by the depth of the cross section, we denote the points of the lower part by \(t\), \(x \le t \le L\). We cut the lower part into pieces with a partition \(x = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_{\mathrm{n}} = L\). The piece \([t_{\mathrm{i}-1}, t_{\mathrm{i}}]\) is a cylinder of length \(\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\), volume \(A\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\) and weight \(\rho g A\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{i}}\). The weights of the pieces add up, as the works on the pieces of a path did. The weight per unit length is the same everywhere, \(\rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, so the lower and the upper sum for every partition equal \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\), and by item 3

\[\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L \rho g A\,\mathrm{d}t = \rho g A\,(L - x) .\]

At \(x = 5\) m this is \(30{,}05\) N, half the weight of the whole bar. Additivity says that the weights of the upper and the lower part add up to the weight of the whole bar,

\[\int_0^x \rho g A\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^L \rho g A\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^L \rho g A\,\mathrm{d}t = \rho g A L = 60{,}10\ \text{N} .\]

For this bar the integral brought nothing new, because the weight per unit length is constant. For a bar whose cross section narrows toward the bottom, however, the cross-section area is a function \(A(t)\), and the weight of the part below the cross section is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^L \rho g A(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). This we can no longer compute with the school formula for the volume of a cylinder.

3.5.7 The fundamental theorem of calculus

We computed the integral \(\tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2\) with the sum \(1 + 2 + \dots + n\) and the Archimedean property. For a force whose graph is not a line such a computation would be much harder. But let us recall what we did with the hanging bar in chapter 1. From the displacement \(u(x)\) we obtained with the derivative the strain \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\), the elongation per unit length close to a point. Does it also go backwards? Let us cut the bar into pieces with a partition \(0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\). The elongation of the piece \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\) is \(u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{i}-1})\), and the sum of the elongations of all pieces is a telescoping sum, equal to the elongation of the whole bar, \(u(L) - u(0)\). The elongation of a piece is approximately the strain times the length. The mean value theorem 1.84 says more: for every piece there is a point \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) at which this is not an approximation but an exact equality,

\[u(x_{\mathrm{i}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{i}-1}) = u'(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\]

(equation (1.31)). The sum of the right sides is a Riemann sum of the strain \(u'\), and the sum of the left sides is exactly the elongation of the bar. The elongation of the bar therefore lies between the lower and the upper sum of the strain for every partition, and by lemma 3.35 it is its integral (figure 3.49). This argument holds for every function with a continuous derivative. Let us state it precisely.

From history

In 1599 Edward Wright published a table that, for navigation with Mercator's map, gave the length of an arc of a meridian. He computed it by successively adding the secants (sekans) of successive angles, that is, the reciprocals of their cosines (CAJORI, PDF p. 205). Around 1645 Henry Bond noticed by inspection that this is a table of logarithms of tangents (CAJORI, PDF p. 205). A sum of values of one function thus gave values of another. Newton, by his own account, conceived in 1665 and 1666 the method of fluxions (section 1.3.3) and applied it to the areas of curves. He treated the area under a curve as a quantity growing in proportion to the length of the ordinate, that is, the vertical segment from the axis to the curve (CAJORI, PDF p. 208). This is exactly the statement that the rate at which the area grows equals the value of the function. In 1668 James Gregory proved Bond's observation (CAJORI, PDF p. 205-206). In 1669 Newton placed a tract on his method in the hands of his teacher Isaac Barrow (CAJORI, PDF p. 208), and in 1670 Barrow gave his own proof as well (CAJORI, PDF p. 205-206).

In a manuscript of 1673 Leibniz noticed that the inverse problem of tangents, which asks for a curve whose tangents are prescribed, leads to finding areas under curves (CAJORI, PDF p. 223). He used a small triangle along the curve that Barrow had already used and that Leibniz, in his own words, took from Pascal (CAJORI, PDF p. 223). When he introduced the sign for a sum on 29 October 1675 (section 3.5.3), he added that a sum raises the dimension, since the sum of lines \(l\) is a region of area \(y\,a\), so the opposite calculus, the calculus of differences, must lower it (CAJORI, PDF p. 223-224). In 1676 Newton wrote in a letter, in which he hid his method in an anagram, that from an equation between changing quantities one is to find the fluxions, and conversely (CAJORI, PDF p. 228-229).

Let us return to figure 3.49, where the areas of the rectangles under the derivative \(f\) give the change of the function \(G\). Sum and difference are inverse operations, which this section states exactly in theorem 3.40 with the integral and the derivative.

Theorem 3.40 Fundamental theorem of calculus (osnovni izrek analize)
  1. Let \(G\) be a function that is continuous on a closed interval \([a, b]\) and differentiable at every point of the open interval \((a, b)\), and let \(f\) be a function, continuous on \([a, b]\), for which \(G'(x) = f(x)\) for every \(x \in (a, b)\). Then
    \[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(b) - G(a) . \tag{3.32}\]
  2. Let \(f\) be a continuous function on a closed interval \([a, b]\), and let \(G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) for \(x \in [a, b]\). Then \(G\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable at every point \(x \in [a, b]\), at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), and \(G'(x) = f(x)\).
Proof

We must show both items. The first follows from the mean value theorem on every piece of a partition, the second from the bounds of the integral over a short interval.

1. The integral of a derivative.

  1. Let \(D = \{x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\}\) be any partition of \([a, b]\).
  2. On the piece \([x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}}]\), \(G\) is continuous and differentiable at every point of the open interval \((x_{\mathrm{i}-1}, x_{\mathrm{i}})\). By the mean value theorem 1.84 there is a point \(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(x_{\mathrm{i}-1} < \xi_{\mathrm{i}} < x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and
    \[G(x_{\mathrm{i}}) - G(x_{\mathrm{i}-1}) = G'(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} = f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} .\]
  3. We add over \(i\) from \(1\) to \(n\). On the left is a telescoping sum, in which all the values in between cancel:
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n \big(G(x_{\mathrm{i}}) - G(x_{\mathrm{i}-1})\big) = G(x_{\mathrm{n}}) - G(x_0) = G(b) - G(a) .\]
  4. On the right is the Riemann sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\). Since \(m_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a lower and \(M_{\mathrm{i}}\) an upper bound of the values on the piece, \(m_{\mathrm{i}} \le f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}}) \le M_{\mathrm{i}}\). We multiply by the positive length \(\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}}\) and add, which gives \(s(f, D) \le \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^n f(\xi_{\mathrm{i}})\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{i}} \le S(f, D)\). By step 3 therefore \(s(f, D) \le G(b) - G(a) \le S(f, D)\).
  5. The partition \(D\) was arbitrary, so the number \(G(b) - G(a)\) lies between all the lower and all the upper sums. The function \(f\) is continuous, so by theorem 3.38 it is integrable, and by lemma 3.35, item 2, the integral is the only such number. So \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(b) - G(a)\).

2. The derivative of an integral.

  1. The function \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), so by lemma 1.72 it is bounded. There are therefore numbers \(m\) and \(M_0\) with \(m \le f(t) \le M_0\) for every \(t \in [a, b]\). For \(M\) we take the largest of the numbers \(|m|\), \(|M_0|\) and \(1\). Then \(M > 0\) and \(-M \le f(t) \le M\) for every \(t \in [a, b]\).
  2. For \(a \le x < y \le b\), \(G(y) - G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}}^y f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). For \(x = a\) this is the definition of the number \(G(y)\), since \(G(a) = 0\) by the convention of definition 3.34. For \(x > a\) it follows from additivity (lemma 3.39, item 2) on the interval \([a, y]\) with the point \(x\) in between.
  3. By the bounds (lemma 3.39, item 3) on the interval \([x, y]\), \(-M\,(y - x) \le G(y) - G(x) \le M\,(y - x)\), so \(|G(y) - G(x)| \le M\,|y - x|\). The inequality does not change if we swap \(x\) and \(y\), and for \(x = y\) both sides are zero. So it holds for any two points of the interval \([a, b]\).
  4. Let \(c \in [a, b]\) and \(\eta > 0\). We take \(\delta = \eta/M\). For \(x \in [a, b]\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\) step 3 gives \(|G(x) - G(c)| \le M\,|x - c| < \eta\). The function \(G\) is therefore continuous at every point of the interval \([a, b]\) (definition 1.58).
  5. Now let \(x \in [a, b]\). For \(r\) we take the smaller of the numbers \(x - a\) and \(b - x\) when \(x \in (a, b)\), and the number \(b - a\) when \(x = a\) or \(x = b\). By the definition of the derivative 1.9 we must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) with \(\delta \le r\) such that
    \[\Big|\frac{G(x + h) - G(x)}{h} - f(x)\Big| < \eta\]
    for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) for which \(x + h\) lies in \([a, b]\). For \(x \in (a, b)\) these are, by the choice of the number \(r\), all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) (definition 1.5). For \(x = a\) these are the \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\), which is the condition for the limit from the right (definition 1.8), and for \(x = b\) the \(h\) with \(-\delta < h < 0\), which is the condition for the limit from the left.
  6. We choose \(\eta > 0\). The function \(f\) is continuous at \(x\), so there is a \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|f(t) - f(x)| < \eta/2\) for \(t \in [a, b]\) with \(|t - x| < \delta_1\). For \(\delta\) we take the smaller of the numbers \(\delta_1\) and \(r\).
  7. Let \(x < b\) and \(0 < h < \delta\). Then \(x + h < x + r \le b\), so \(x + h\) lies in \([a, b]\). By step 2, \(G(x + h) - G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x+h} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). For \(t \in [x, x + h]\) we have \(|t - x| \le h < \delta_1\), so \(f(x) - \eta/2 < f(t) < f(x) + \eta/2\). By the bounds on an interval of length \(h\),
    \[\big(f(x) - \tfrac{\eta}{2}\big)\,h \le G(x + h) - G(x) \le \big(f(x) + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\big)\,h .\]
  8. We divide by the positive number \(h\) and subtract \(f(x)\) from all sides. We get \(-\eta/2 \le (G(x + h) - G(x))/h - f(x) \le \eta/2\), so the difference is at most \(\eta/2 < \eta\) in absolute value.
  9. Let \(x > a\) and \(-\delta < h < 0\). Then \(x + h > x - r \ge a\), so \(x + h\) lies in \([a, b]\). By step 2 now \(G(x) - G(x + h) = \int_{\mathrm{x}+\mathrm{h}}^{x} f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), over an interval of length \(-h > 0\). As in step 7, \(\big(f(x) - \tfrac{\eta}{2}\big)(-h) \le G(x) - G(x + h) \le \big(f(x) + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\big)(-h)\). We divide by the positive number \(-h\). Since \((G(x) - G(x + h))/(-h) = (G(x + h) - G(x))/h\), we get the same estimate as in step 8.
  10. We chose the error \(\eta\) arbitrarily. For \(x \in (a, b)\) steps 8 and 9 hold for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\), so the limit of the difference quotient equals \(f(x)\) (definition 1.5). For \(x = a\) step 8 holds for all \(h\) with \(0 < h < \delta\), so \(f(a)\) is the limit from the right (definition 1.8). For \(x = b\) step 9 holds for all \(h\) with \(-\delta < h < 0\), so \(f(b)\) is the limit from the left. In all three cases therefore \(G'(x) = f(x)\) by the definition of the derivative 1.9.

Source: LEBL1 Thm. 5.3.1 (PDF pp. 200-201) and Thm. 5.3.3 (PDF p. 202), which takes the derivative at the endpoints of the interval as one-sided. In item 1 LEBL1 asks only that \(f\) be integrable instead of continuous. We ask for continuity, which we will always have and which gives integrability by theorem 3.38.

The first item says that we compute the integral of a function \(f\) without sums if we know a function \(G\) whose derivative is \(f\). Books call such a function an antiderivative (primitivna funkcija). The second item says that every continuous function has one, namely \(G(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). Integration and differentiation are thus inverse procedures. The derivative of the integral up to \(x\) gives back the function \(f\), and the integral of a derivative gives the difference of the values at the endpoints. Figure 3.50 shows why the second item holds. When \(x\) grows by a small \(h\), the area under the graph grows by a narrow strip of width \(h\), which is approximately \(f(x)\) high. The change of the area divided by \(h\) is therefore approximately \(f(x)\), and step 7 of the proof wrote this approximation down exactly.

Let us first apply the theorem to the force that pulls the bar. The function \(G(e) = \tfrac12 k\,e^2\) is a polynomial, so it is continuous and differentiable everywhere. By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81), \((e^2)' = 2e\) and \(G'(e) = \tfrac12 k \cdot 2e = k\,e\), which is the force \(F(e)\). By item 1,

\[\int_0^{\Delta L} k\,e\,\mathrm{d}e = G(\Delta L) - G(0) = \tfrac12 k\,\Delta L^2 - 0 = 32{,}48\ \text{J} .\]

We obtained the result of section 3.5.3 in one line. The second item adds a physical picture. The work that the force does up to the elongation \(e\) is, by item 1 on the interval \([0, e]\), equal to \(\int_0^e k\,t\,\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac12 k\,e^2\), and by item 2 its derivative with respect to the elongation is the force \(k\,e\). The force thus says how fast the work grows with the elongation. At the final force 10 kN the next tenth of a millimetre of elongation adds approximately \(10\,000\ \text{N} \cdot 0{,}0001\ \text{m} = 1\) J of work. Exactly it is \(1{,}008\) J, because the force still grows a little over this distance, to \(10{,}15\) kN. We will use this relation between work and force in chapter 4.

Now let us keep the promise of chapter 2 and compute the displacement of the hanging bar from the stress. Recall the setting. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points down from the top, and the top of the bar does not move, \(u(0) = 0\). The stress at depth \(x\) is \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\) (equation (2.5)). We get the strain by dividing the stress by Young's modulus (section 3.1),

\[\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\rho g\,(L - x)}{E} = 2a\,(L - x) , \qquad a = \frac{\rho g}{2E} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The strain is the derivative of the displacement, \(u'(x) = \varepsilon(x)\) (definition 1.10). The displacement itself we do not know. We assume only that the bar has no cracks. As for all displacements in this book, let the displacement \(u\) be continuously differentiable at the interior points of the bar (definition 1.90), and in addition continuous at both ends. So \(u\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) and differentiable at the interior points. We need the continuity at the ends because item 1 of the fundamental theorem asks for a function that is continuous on a closed interval.

  1. Let \(0 < x \le L\). By item 1 of the fundamental theorem on the interval \([0, x]\), with \(G = u\) and the continuous function \(f = \varepsilon\),
    \[u(x) - u(0) = \int_0^x 2a\,(L - t)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  2. We compute the integral with the same item of the theorem, this time with the function \(H(t) = a\,(2Lt - t^2)\). By the rules of differentiation \(H'(t) = 2aL - 2at = 2a\,(L - t)\), so the integral equals \(H(x) - H(0) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\).
  3. Since \(u(0) = 0\), we get \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). At \(x = 0\) this holds too, because both sides are zero.

This is exactly the displacement that we took as given in chapter 1 (figure 3.51). The route is now closed: from the weight we get the axial force and the stress (chapter 2), from the stress with Young's modulus the strain, from the strain with the integral the displacement. The computation tells us one more thing. The strains determine the displacement only together with the displacement of one point, here the top, \(u(0) = 0\). If we added the same number to the displacements of all points, the bar would move as a rigid body, and the strains would stay the same, because a constant has derivative zero. The elongation of the whole bar is

\[u(L) - u(0) = \int_0^L 2a\,(L - t)\,\mathrm{d}t = a\,L^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 100\ \text{m}^2 = 19{,}52\ \mu\text{m} .\]

In figure 3.51 this is the area of the triangle under the graph of the strain, with base \(L\) and height \(2aL\), so \(\tfrac12 \cdot L \cdot 2aL = aL^2\).

Where does the theorem fail? Item 1 requires \(G\) to be continuous on \([a, b]\). In a bar with a crack at some depth, the displacement jumps there. The elongation of the bar is then no longer the integral of the strains, since the jump of the displacement contributes nothing to the integral of the strains. Item 2 requires a continuous function \(f\). Where \(f\) has a jump, the graph of \(G\) has a kink, and \(G\) has no derivative there (LEBL1, PDF p. 203).

Let us look at a bar with a crack, with numbers. On \([0, 1]\) let the function be \(G(x) = 0\) for \(x < \tfrac12\) and \(G(x) = 1\) for \(x \ge \tfrac12\): the displacement jumps by \(1\) at the crack at \(x = \tfrac12\). Item 1 requires three things: \(G\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\), differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, 1)\), and \(G'(x) = f(x)\) at every such point for a continuous function \(f\). Let us check them.

  1. At \(x = \tfrac12\) the function \(G\) is discontinuous: the value to the left of it is \(0\) and the value at it is \(1\). The first requirement fails.
  2. Because of the same jump, \(G\) is not differentiable at \(\tfrac12\). The difference quotient \(\big(G(\tfrac12 + h) - G(\tfrac12)\big)/h\) equals \((1 - 1)/h = 0\) for \(h > 0\) and \((0 - 1)/h = -1/h\) for \(h < 0\), which grows beyond all bounds as \(h \to 0^-\). There is no limit, so the second requirement fails as well.
  3. Everywhere else \(G' = 0\), so the only possibility would be \(f = 0\), which is continuous. But \(G'(\tfrac12) = f(\tfrac12)\) cannot hold, because \(G'(\tfrac12)\) does not exist, and the third requirement fails.
  4. The calculation \(\int_0^1 f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^1 0\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\) is therefore not equal to \(G(1) - G(0) = 1 - 0 = 1\). The conclusion (3.32) fails.

Two requirements therefore fail at once, and the jump of the displacement does not show up in any value of \(f\): the derivative is zero everywhere it exists. Continuity on the closed interval is also needed on its own. For \(G(x) = 0\) for \(0 \le x < 1\) and \(G(1) = 1\), \(G\) is differentiable at every point of the interval \((0, 1)\) with \(G' = f = 0\), it is discontinuous only at the endpoint \(1\), and again \(\int_0^1 f\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \ne 1 = G(1) - G(0)\).

3.5.8 Integration by parts

In chapter 4 we will integrate products of two functions, one of which is a derivative, and we will want to move the derivative onto the other function. The tool for this comes from the rule for the derivative of a product, \((f g)' = f' g + f g'\) (lemma 1.81), and from the fundamental theorem of calculus.

From history

In Paris Leibniz divided regions with ordinates into elements. Three days before the manuscript in which he first wrote the sign for a sum, on 26 October 1675, he wrote a theorem saying that the sum of the products \(x\,w\), where the \(w\) are small parts of horizontal lines and \(x\) is a changing height, equals the last \(x\) times the sum of all \(w\), reduced by the sum of the sums of \(w\) (CAJNOT2, PDF p. 225-226).

Back to Leibniz's theorem on the sum of products \(x\,w\). We understand the sum of sums as the sum of the partial sums, and check the theorem on three numbers, where \(x\) is successively 1, 2 and 3 and \(w\) is 5, 2 and 4. The sum of the products is \(1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 21\). The last \(x\) is 3 and the sum of all \(w\) is 11, the product is 33. The partial sums are 5 and \(5 + 2 = 7\), together 12, and \(33 - 12 = 21\). Each \(w\) is counted three times in the product 33, and the partial sums take away as many as needed for each to remain as many times as its \(x\) requires: \(w = 5\) is in both partial sums and remains once, \(w = 2\) is in one and remains twice, \(w = 4\) is in none and remains three times. The corollary below writes the same idea for an integral and a derivative instead of sums and differences, and we derive it from the product rule.

Corollary 3.41 Integration by parts (integracija po delih)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be functions that are differentiable at every point of the closed interval \([a, b]\), at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) with a one-sided derivative (definition 1.9), and let their derivatives \(f'\) and \(g'\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,g'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = f(b)\,g(b) - f(a)\,g(a) - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f'(x)\,g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{3.33}\]
Proof

We must show that the integrals on the two sides differ by the difference of the values of the product \(f g\) at the endpoints.

  1. Let \(G(x) = f(x)\,g(x)\) for \(x \in [a, b]\). The functions \(f\) and \(g\) are defined on the open interval \((a, b)\) and differentiable at each of its points. By the rule for the derivative of a product (lemma 1.81, item 3) with \(I = (a, b)\), \(G\) is therefore differentiable at every point \(x \in (a, b)\) and \(G'(x) = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)\).
  2. The functions \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at every point of the interval \([a, b]\), one-sidedly at the endpoints, so they are continuous there (lemma 1.79). By corollary 1.64 on sums and products of continuous functions the product \(G\) is therefore continuous too.
  3. The functions \(f' g\) and \(f g'\) are continuous on \([a, b]\) as products of continuous functions (corollary 1.64), their sum is continuous too, and by step 1 this sum equals \(G'(x)\) at every point \(x \in (a, b)\).
  4. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, item 1, with the function \(G\) and the continuous function \(f' g + f g'\),
    \[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \big(f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(b) - G(a) = f(b)\,g(b) - f(a)\,g(a) .\]
  5. By linearity (lemma 3.39, item 1) the left side is the sum of the integrals \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f' g\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f g'\). We subtract \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f' g\) on both sides and get equation (3.33).

Source: LEBL1 Exercise 5.3.5 (PDF p. 205).

Integration by parts thus moves the derivative from the function \(g\) to the function \(f\), at the price of the term \(f(b)\,g(b) - f(a)\,g(a)\), which contains only values at the endpoints. The name says that we split the integral into parts: the product at the endpoints and a new integral. At the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) the corollary asks only for a one-sided derivative, the limit of the difference quotient from one side, because the functions may not be defined outside the interval \([a, b]\). The displacement of a bar is such a function, known only from \(x = 0\) to \(x = L\). LEBL1 states the same formula, for functions that are continuously differentiable on \([a, b]\), with the derivative at an endpoint as in Def. 4.1.1 (LEBL1 Exercise 5.3.5, PDF p. 205, Def. 4.1.1, PDF p. 155). Polynomials are differentiable everywhere and meet the condition.

Let us test the formula on the hanging bar, on which we will see its meaning in chapter 4. The bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points down from the top, and the top does not move. The axial force is \(N(x) = \rho g A\,(L - x)\), the displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). Take \(f = N\) and \(g = u\), both polynomials. Their derivatives are \(N'(x) = -\rho g A\) and \(u'(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\). Equation (3.33) gives

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = N(L)\,u(L) - N(0)\,u(0) + \int_0^L \rho g A\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

The term at the endpoints is zero, and for two physical reasons. The lower end is free, so there is no axial force there, \(N(L) = 0\). The upper end does not move, \(u(0) = 0\). What remains is

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L \rho g A\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x .\]

On the left are the axial forces times the strains, on the right the weights of the pieces per unit length times the displacements. Let us check the equality with the fundamental theorem of calculus. On the left the integrand is \(\rho g A\,(L - x) \cdot 2a\,(L - x) = 2a\,\rho g A\,(L^2 - 2Lx + x^2)\). The function \(L^2 x - L x^2 + x^3/3\) has the derivative \(L^2 - 2Lx + x^2\), so

\[\int_0^L N(x)\,u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 2a\,\rho g A\,\Big(L^3 - L^3 + \frac{L^3}{3}\Big) = \frac23\,a\,\rho g A\,L^3 .\]

On the right the integrand is \(\rho g A\,a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The function \(L x^2 - x^3/3\) has the derivative \(2Lx - x^2\), so

\[\int_0^L \rho g A\,u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \rho g A\,a\,\Big(L^3 - \frac{L^3}{3}\Big) = \frac23\,a\,\rho g A\,L^3 .\]

With \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), \(\rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m and \(L^3 = 1000\ \text{m}^3\) both sides equal \(7{,}821 \cdot 10^{-4}\) J. Why this equality holds and what it means we will see in chapter 4.

With the integral we can now compute the work done by a changing force along a straight path, when the force changes continuously, and the displacement of a bar from its strains. The next section will ask where the work goes that the machine does on the bar when it slowly stretches it.
