Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.5 Inverse kinematics

So far we chose the joint angles and computed where the end effector is (definition 27.31). A robot, however, gets its task the other way round: "grasp the object at the point \(P\)", and it has to find the joint angles itself, because motors set them. Since we have turned the direction of the map around, this task is called inverse kinematics (inverzna kinematika, from Latin inversus, turned around, and Greek kinema, motion, kinematics describing position and motion without forces). We ask three questions. Can the angles for a given point be found at all? How many sets of angles reach it? And do the angles change smoothly when the point moves? On the two-link arm of example A we settle all three by calculation, and for more general arms we say what is known and cite the source.

The model is the one of section 27.1: a planar arm with two rigid links of lengths \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, with ideal joints without friction or backlash (definition 27.2). The angle \(q_1\) is the angle of link 1 with the axis \(x_0\), and \(q_2\) the angle between the extension of link 1 and link 2, both in radians. The tip (konica) of the arm is the point at the end of link 2 where the end effector sits (definition 27.1). We want only the position of the tip and not the orientation of the end effector. A point of the plane has two numbers and the arm has two joint variables, so we may hope for finitely many solutions. The tip is at the point

\[\Phi(q_1, q_2) = \big(l_1\cos q_1 + l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2),\ \ l_1\sin q_1 + l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2)\big) . \tag{27.17}\]

Link 1 makes the angle \(q_1\) with the axis \(x_0\) and link 2 the angle \(q_1 + q_2\), because the angles of rotations add (lemma 27.12 for two rotations about the same axis, written with matrices as \(\mat R(q_1)\,\mat R(q_2) = \mat R(q_1 + q_2)\) by the addition formulas (8.11)). The tip lies at the vector of link 1 plus the vector of link 2, which is (27.17). The function \(\Phi\) takes two numbers in and gives a point of the plane out, so it is a map in the sense of definition 4.42. At \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) (example A1) we get \(\Phi = (0{,}8660,\ 1{,}0000)\) m, and this is the point we choose for \(P\).

We build the figure as one would draw it on a board. The axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) belong to the fixed frame, joint 1 sits at the origin \(O\) and the tip has to be at the point \(P\). First we draw the posture we already know, the blue arm: link 1 of length \(l_1\) at the angle \(q_1 = 30^\circ\) up to the elbow \(o_1\), and link 2 of length \(l_2\) from there to \(P\), straight up. The elbow is joint 2, because the arm bends there like a human arm. The angle \(q_2 = 60^\circ\) is measured from the dashed extension of link 1 to link 2. Then we ask whether another posture exists. The two links and the segment from \(O\) to \(P\) form a triangle with sides \(l_1 = 1\) m, \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m and \(r = \sqrt{0{,}866^2 + 1^2} = 1{,}3229\) m. A triangle with three given sides is fixed up to reflection (the SSS congruence theorem), so the second posture arises by reflecting the elbow in the segment \(OP\): the orange arm has its elbow \(o_1'\) at \((0{,}3712,\ 0{,}9286)\) m, the mirror image of \(o_1 = (0{,}8660,\ 0{,}5000)\) m (computation num-05.out, C9). Its angles are \(q_1' = 68{,}213^\circ\) and \(q_2' = -60^\circ\). We tell the two elbows apart by their position relative to the line \(OP\), looking with the axis \(y_0\) upward: in the blue arm the elbow is below it (elbow down, \(q_2 > 0\)), in the orange arm above it (elbow up, \(q_2 < 0\)).

Both idealizations of the model enter right here. Rigid links mean that the sides \(l_1\) and \(l_2\) of the triangle have the same length in every posture, otherwise we could not compute with three sides. A joint without clearance means that the angle at the joint is exactly the one the motor sets. How much this matters is told by the distance \(r\): if joint 1 has a clearance of \(0{,}1^\circ = 0{,}00175\) rad, the tip moves along an arc by \(r\cdot 0{,}00175 = 1{,}3229\cdot 0{,}00175\) m \(= 2{,}3\) mm. The model is therefore good as long as the accuracy demanded of the tip position is coarse compared with such a shift, and it fails for tasks where tenths of a millimetre count.

Reflection is the idea, and the theorem turns it into a calculation valid for every point and every pair of link lengths. The matrix \(\mat R(q_1)\) appears in it, the matrix of a planar rotation from lemma 8.41. Its columns are the axes of the frame of link 1 in the base frame, so it is the orientation matrix \(\mat R^0_1\) of section 27.2 in the planar case. It is the transpose of the rotation matrix of definition 2.44, as stated after lemma 8.41.

The proof will run on figure 27.42, so we build it first. The axes \(x_0\), \(y_0\) and joint 1 at \(O\) are the same as before. We first lay the arm so that link 1 lies on the axis \(x_0\), that is \(q_1 = 0\), and leave the elbow bent by \(q_2 = 60^\circ\): this is the grey dashed arm. We call its tip \(\vek w\). Its components can be read straight off the figure: along the axis \(x_0\) the whole of link 1 and the projection of link 2, \(w_1 = l_1 + l_2\cos q_2 = 1 + 0{,}5\cdot 0{,}5 = 1{,}25\) m, and vertically only the height of link 2, \(w_2 = l_2\sin q_2 = 0{,}5\cdot 0{,}866 = 0{,}433\) m. The segment from \(O\) to \(\vek w\) has the length \(r\) and makes the angle \(\beta = 19{,}107^\circ\) with the axis \(x_0\). Now we turn the whole grey arm as one rigid piece about \(O\) by \(q_1 = 30^\circ\): the tip travels along the blue arc of the circle of radius \(r\) and lands at the point \(P\), and the arm becomes the orange one. A turn about \(O\) changes no distance from \(O\), so the segment \(OP\) is as long as the segment from \(O\) to \(\vek w\), and its angle with the axis is \(\varphi = \beta + q_1 = 19{,}107^\circ + 30^\circ = 49{,}107^\circ\) (NumPy). These two observations are the whole proof: the length \(r\) knows nothing about \(q_1\), so it fixes \(q_2\), and the angle \(\varphi\) then fixes \(q_1 = \varphi - \beta\). At the elbow of the grey arm the two angles are marked that we shall need for the law of cosines: \(q_2\) is measured from the extension of link 1, and the angle of the triangle between the links is \(\pi - q_2\).

Theorem 27.34 Inverse kinematics of the two-link planar arm (inverzna kinematika dvočlenske ravninske roke)

Let \(l_1 > 0\) and \(l_2 > 0\) be the link lengths and \(\Phi\) the map (27.17). Let \((x, y)\) be a point of the plane other than the origin, \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) and

\[c = \frac{r^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2\,l_1 l_2} . \tag{27.18}\]

Let \(\varphi\) be an angle with \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (x, y)/r\). Then:

  1. A pair of angles \((q_1, q_2)\) with \(\Phi(q_1, q_2) = (x, y)\) exists if and only if \(|c| \le 1\), that is \(|l_1 - l_2| \le r \le l_1 + l_2\).
  2. For every such pair \(\cos q_2 = c\), and \(q_1 = \varphi - \beta\) up to multiples of \(2\pi\), where \(\beta\) is an angle with \(r\,(\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = (l_1 + l_2\cos q_2,\ l_2\sin q_2)\).
  3. For \(|c| < 1\) there are exactly two solutions up to multiples of \(2\pi\), with \(q_2 = \theta\) and \(q_2 = -\theta\), where \(\theta \in (0, \pi)\) and \(\cos\theta = c\). For \(c = 1\) there is one solution, with \(q_2 = 0\), for \(c = -1\) one, with \(q_2 = \pi\).
Proof

We have to show that the tip \(\Phi\) arises from a vector \(\vek w\) by a rotation through \(q_1\), that a rotation preserves length and so determines \(\cos q_2\), and that the angle \(q_1\) can then be chosen.

  1. Let \(\vek w = (w_1, w_2)\) with \(w_1 = l_1 + l_2\cos q_2\) and \(w_2 = l_2\sin q_2\). By the addition formulas (8.11), \(l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) = l_2\cos q_1\cos q_2 - l_2\sin q_1\sin q_2\) and \(l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2) = l_2\sin q_1\cos q_2 + l_2\cos q_1\sin q_2\). From (27.17) we therefore get \(\Phi = (w_1\cos q_1 - w_2\sin q_1,\ w_1\sin q_1 + w_2\cos q_1) = \mat R(q_1)\,\vek w\) with the matrix \(\mat R\) of lemma 8.41. In figure 27.42, \(\vek w\) is the tip of the grey arm with the components \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), and multiplying by \(\mat R(q_1)\) is the blue turn that carries it into the orange arm.
  2. A rotation preserves length, the segments from \(O\) to \(\vek w\) and from \(O\) to \(P\) both have the length \(r\): \(|\mat R(q_1)\vek w|^2 = (w_1\cos q_1 - w_2\sin q_1)^2 + (w_1\sin q_1 + w_2\cos q_1)^2 = (w_1^2 + w_2^2)(\cos^2 q_1 + \sin^2 q_1) = w_1^2 + w_2^2\), because the mixed terms cancel and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) by lemma 8.11, item 1. From \(\Phi = (x, y)\) we therefore get \(r^2 = |\vek w|^2 = l_1^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos q_2 + l_2^2\,(\cos^2 q_2 + \sin^2 q_2) = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos q_2\). This is \(\cos q_2 = c\), the first part of item 2.
  3. By lemma 8.11, item 1, \(|\cos q_2| \le 1\), so \(|c| \le 1\) must hold. The condition \(c \le 1\) is \(r^2 \le (l_1 + l_2)^2\), the condition \(c \ge -1\) is \(r^2 \ge (l_1 - l_2)^2 = |l_1 - l_2|^2\). The numbers \(r\), \(l_1 + l_2\) and \(|l_1 - l_2|\) are nonnegative, so lemma 1.29 turns the squares into \(|l_1 - l_2| \le r \le l_1 + l_2\). This proves necessity in item 1.
  4. Conversely let \(|c| \le 1\). By item 1 of definition 8.10 there is \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) with \(\cos\theta = c\). For \(q_2 = \theta\), step 2 read backwards gives \(|\vek w| = r > 0\), so \(\vek w/r\) is a point of the unit circle. By the construction in step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.41 there is an angle \(\beta\) with \(\vek w = r\,(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)\), and an angle \(\varphi\) with \((x, y) = r\,(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\). For \(q_1 = \varphi - \beta\) step 1 and the addition formulas give \(\Phi = \mat R(q_1)\,\vek w = r\,(\cos(q_1 + \beta),\ \sin(q_1 + \beta)) = r\,(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (x, y)\). This is existence in item 1.
  5. Two angles \(a\) and \(b\) with the same \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) differ by a multiple of \(2\pi\). By the addition formula, \(\cos(a - b) = \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b = \cos^2 a + \sin^2 a = 1\), and by definition 8.10 the cosine equals \(1\) only at multiples of \(2\pi\) (on \([0, \pi]\) it is strictly decreasing by lemma 8.11, item 3, and \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\)). For a given \(q_2\), from \(\mat R(q_1)\vek w = (x, y)\), step 4 and the addition formulas, \((\cos(q_1 + \beta), \sin(q_1 + \beta)) = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\), so \(q_1 = \varphi - \beta\) up to a multiple of \(2\pi\). This is the second part of item 2.
  6. The number of solutions. By step 2, \(\cos q_2 = c\). On \([0, \pi]\) the cosine, being strictly decreasing, takes the value \(c\) at exactly one \(\theta\), and on \([-\pi, 0)\) we have \(\cos q_2 = \cos(-q_2)\), hence \(q_2 = -\theta\). Up to multiples of \(2\pi\) only \(q_2 = \theta\) and \(q_2 = -\theta\) are therefore possible (lemma](27.42:pred) 8.11, item 4). For \(|c| < 1\) we have \(0 < \theta < \pi\), so \(\sin\theta > 0\), and \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) also differ by something other than a multiple of \(2\pi\), because otherwise \(\sin\theta = \sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\) and \(\sin\theta = 0\). For each of them step 5 gives one \(q_1\), two solutions in all. For \(c = 1\) we have \(\theta = 0\) and for \(c = -1\) we have \(\theta = \pi\) (item 2 of lemma 8.11), \(\theta\) and \(-\theta\) agree up to \(2\pi\), one solution. In figure 27.42, \(q_2 = \theta\) is the grey arm above the axis \(x_0\) and \(q_2 = -\theta\) its mirror image below the axis, and [a turn by its own \(q_1\) carries each to the same point \(P\).

Source: SPONG (5.24) and (5.25), PDF p. 173, state the same conclusion for an elbow arm by the law of cosines, \(\cos\theta_3 = D\) and \(\theta_3 = \operatorname{Atan2}(D, \pm\sqrt{1 - D^2})\), and point to equations (1.10) and (1.11) for the two-link arm. The proof by rotation and addition formulas is not from the sources.

The theorem says that \(\cos q_2\) is determined by the distance \(r\), which is the same in both postures, so the postures are mirror images of each other. Equation (27.18) is the law of cosines for the triangle with sides \(l_1\), \(l_2\) and \(r\), which we see in figure 27.41. The sign in front of \(2l_1l_2\cos q_2\) is plus and not minus, because the angle of the triangle at the elbow is \(\pi - q_2\) (\(q_2\) is measured from the extension of link 1) and \(\cos(\pi - q_2) = -\cos q_2\). For example A at the point \(P\) we have \(c = (1{,}75 - 1 - 0{,}25)/1 = 0{,}5\), so \(\theta = 60^\circ\), and the two solutions are \((30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) and \((68{,}213^\circ, -60^\circ)\) (example A4, num-05.out, C2). We read the angles \(q_1\) off figure 27.42: the segment \(OP\) has the angle \(\varphi = 49{,}107^\circ\), for elbow down \(\beta = 19{,}107^\circ\) and \(q_1 = \varphi - \beta = 30^\circ\). For elbow up the grey arm is mirrored below the axis \(x_0\), so \(\beta = -19{,}107^\circ\) and \(q_1' = \varphi + 19{,}107^\circ = 68{,}213^\circ\) (NumPy, from unrounded values). Both solutions were checked with \(\Phi\) and give \(P\). The theorem does not treat the point \((0, 0)\). Only an arm with \(l_1 = l_2\) and \(q_2 = \pi\) reaches it, and then \(q_1\) is arbitrary, as at a configuration that we shall call singular in section 27.7.

The condition \(|c| \le 1\) is the workspace of lemma 27.7, and the theorem now also tells us which sets of angles we get. We test it on points near the edge.

The workspace is an annulus (kolobar), a plane figure between two circles, like a wheel with a hole, centred at joint 1 \(O\) between the circles of radii \(l_1 - l_2 = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_1 + l_2 = 1{,}5\) m, marked with their radii. On the axis \(x_0\) we first take the point \(E = (1{,}5,\ 0)\) m on the outer circle. There \(c = (2{,}25 - 1{,}25)/1 = 1\) and \(\theta = 0\), so the only solution is \(q = (0, 0)\), the arm stretched out as the blackwhite segment in the figure. Why only one? The triangle with sides \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m and the third side \(r = 1{,}5\) m is flattened into a segment, and the mirror image of a segment in itself is the same segment. Next we look at the point \(Q = (1{,}6,\ 0)\) m just beyond the edge. There \(c = (2{,}56 - 1{,}25)/1 = 1{,}31 > 1\) and no number \(q_2\) has cosine \(1{,}31\) (lemma 8.11, item 1). The arm reaches \(l_1 + l_2 = 1{,}5\) m and \(Q\) is \(0{,}1\) m farther. On the inner side the story is the same. The point \(H = (0{,}5,\ 0)\) m has \(c = (0{,}25 - 1{,}25)/1 = -1\) and \(q_2 = \pi\), the arm folded back, while the point \(W = (0{,}4,\ 0)\) m in the hole has \(c = -1{,}09\) and no solution. Inside the annulus, for instance at the point \(P\) of figure 27.41, there are two solutions. The last values were checked in num-05.out, C3.

At the edge, then, two solutions merge into one. This raises the third question: what happens to a solution when the point moves? At the point \(P\) the sets of angles are \((30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) and \((68{,}213^\circ, -60^\circ)\), and we expect both to change continuously if \(P\) is moved a little. This holds at every configuration where the matrix of partial derivatives of the map \(\Phi\) is invertible, and that we must prove. We compute it from (27.17) with the theorem on the derivatives of sine and cosine (theorem 8.13) and the rules of differentiation:

\[\mat F_\Phi(q_1, q_2) = \begin{pmatrix} -l_1\sin q_1 - l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2) & -l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2) \\ l_1\cos q_1 + l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) & l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{27.19}\]

The entries are continuous, so \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable (definition 4.42). In section 27.6 we shall meet this matrix as the Jacobian matrix of the robot, here it is only the matrix of partial derivatives of a map.

Corollary 27.35 Local inverse of the two-link arm (lokalna inverzna preslikava dvočlenske roke)

Let \(\Phi\) be the map (27.17) and \(\vek q_* = (q_{1*}, q_{2*})\) a set of angles with \(\sin q_{2*} \neq 0\). Then \(\det\mat F_\Phi(\vek q) = l_1l_2\sin q_2\), and there is a ball \(B\) centred at \(\vek q_*\) such that \(\Phi\) is injective on \(B\), \(V = \Phi(B)\) is open, and the inverse map \(\Psi\) of \(\Phi\) on \(B\) is continuously differentiable on \(V\) with

\[\mat F_\Psi(\Phi(\vek q)) = \mat F_\Phi(\vek q)^{-1} \quad\text{for every } \vek q \text{ in } B . \tag{27.20}\]
Proof

We have to show that the matrix of partial derivatives at \(\vek q_*\) has two pivots, so that theorem 8.34 can be used.

  1. By theorem 8.13 and the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82), \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable on the plane of all pairs \((q_1, q_2)\), with the matrix of partial derivatives (27.19).
  2. By definition (2.72) the determinant is \((-l_1\sin q_1 - l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2))\,l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) + l_2\sin(q_1 + q_2)\,(l_1\cos q_1 + l_2\cos(q_1 + q_2))\). The terms with \(l_2^2\) cancel, leaving \(l_1l_2\,(\sin(q_1 + q_2)\cos q_1 - \sin q_1\cos(q_1 + q_2)) = l_1l_2\sin q_2\) by the addition formula (8.11) with \(\alpha = q_1 + q_2\) and \(\beta \to -q_1\) (evenness and oddness, lemma 8.11, item 4).
  3. For \(\sin q_{2*} \neq 0\) we have \(\det\mat F_\Phi(\vek q_*) \neq 0\). By lemma 4.49 the matrix \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek q_*)\) has an inverse, so \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek q_*)\vek w = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek w = \mat F_\Phi(\vek q_*)^{-1}\vek 0 = \vek 0\), and by lemma 8.22 the matrix has two pivots.
  4. Theorem 8.34 with \(n = 2\), \(D\) the plane of all pairs and \(\vek x_0 = \vek q_*\) gives the ball \(B\), the openness of \(V\), the injectivity and continuous differentiability of \(\Psi\), and (27.20).

Source: SPONG PDF p. 163, in the introduction to chapter 5, mentions that the Jacobian inverse method is similar to a Newton-Raphson search, but does not state the corollary. The proof is not from the sources.

The corollary says that near every configuration with \(\sin q_2 \neq 0\) a smooth inverse exists. For example A this does not cover every configuration: \(\sin q_2 = 0\) means \(q_2 = 0\) or \(q_2 = \pi\), a stretched or completely folded arm, and these are exactly the two edges of the annulus in figure 27.43. The determinant also has a picture. In figure 27.42 the triangle made of the two links and the segment \(r\) lies on the axis \(x_0\): its base is link 1 of length \(l_1\), its height above the base is \(w_2 = l_2\sin q_2\). The area of the triangle is half the base times the height, \(\frac12\,l_1l_2\sin q_2\), and the turn does not change it. The determinant \(l_1l_2\sin q_2\) is therefore twice the area of the triangle of the arm, for example A \(1\cdot 0{,}433 = 0{,}433\) m\(^2\), with the sign plus for elbow down and minus for elbow up. To the calculation in steps 2 and 3 of the proof this adds a view by eye: the determinant is zero exactly when the triangle flattens into a segment, and exactly then the two mirror postures merge into one. The condition of the corollary and the number of solutions in theorem 27.34 are the same picture.

A calculation on example A. At \(\vek q_* = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) we have \(\mat F_\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -0{,}5 \\ 0{,}866 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) m, \(\det = 0{,}4330\) m\(^2\) \(= l_1l_2\sin 60^\circ\) and

\[\mat F_\Phi^{-1} = \frac{1}{0{,}4330}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0{,}5 \\ -0{,}866 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1{,}1547 \\ -2{,}0 & -2{,}3094 \end{pmatrix} \ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

By (27.20) these are the partial derivatives of the local inverse map: \(\partial q_1/\partial x = 0\), \(\partial q_1/\partial y = 1{,}1547\) rad/m, \(\partial q_2/\partial x = -2{,}0\) rad/m and \(\partial q_2/\partial y = -2{,}3094\) rad/m. We check by computer: difference quotients with steps \(h = 10^{-6}\) m around \(P\), computed from the formulas of theorem 27.34 on the elbow-down branch, give the columns \((0,\ -2{,}0)\) and \((1{,}1547,\ -2{,}3094)\) (num-05.out, C4 and C5). On the elbow-up branch \(\sin q_2 = \sin(-60^\circ) < 0\), the determinant is \(-0{,}4330\) m\(^2\) and the inverse is \(\begin{pmatrix} -1{,}1429 & -0{,}1650 \\ 2{,}0 & 2{,}3094 \end{pmatrix}\) m\(^{-1}\). The point \(P\) therefore has two local inverse maps, each around its own set of angles. The corollary guarantees each separately, and theorem 27.34 says there are no others.

What these numbers mean we show on the first column, \(\partial q_1/\partial x = 0\) and \(\partial q_2/\partial x = -2{,}0\) rad/m. The axes and joint 1 at \(O\) are as before, the grey dashed arm is the posture \((30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) with link 2 straight above the elbow \(o_1\). If we turn joint 2 alone, the tip travels along the circle of radius \(l_2\) round the elbow. At the point \(P\) this circle runs horizontally, because the tangent of a circle is perpendicular to the radius, and the radius here is the vertical link 2. A small horizontal shift of the tip is therefore done by joint 2 alone and \(q_1\) stays at rest. Hence the zero in the matrix. By how much must joint 2 turn? The length of an arc is the radius times the angle in radians, so a shift \(\Delta x\) needs the angle \(\Delta x/l_2\), and with the lever \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m this is \(1/0{,}5 = 2\) rad per metre. The sign is minus, because the tip goes to the right when link 2 turns clockwise, that is, when \(q_2\) decreases.

A derivative is a statement about small shifts, so we test it on a large one. We move the tip by \(0{,}1\) m to the point \(P' = (0{,}966,\ 1{,}0)\) m. The derivatives predict \(\Delta q_1 = 0\) and \(\Delta q_2 = -2{,}0\cdot 0{,}1 = -0{,}2\) rad \(= -11{,}46^\circ\). Theorem 27.34 gives for \(P'\) exactly the orange arm with \(q = (30{,}766^\circ,\ 46{,}905^\circ)\): the elbow angle decreases by \(13{,}09^\circ\), and link 1 rises by \(0{,}77^\circ\) (NumPy). The prediction for \(q_2\) is off by \(1{,}63^\circ\), because the circle is not straight: after a turn by \(0{,}2\) rad the tip on the arc is already \(l_2\,(1 - \cos 0{,}2) = 0{,}01\) m below the height of the point \(P\), and both joints have to correct this. For a shift ten times smaller, \(0{,}01\) m, the elbow angle decreases by \(1{,}159^\circ\) against the predicted \(1{,}146^\circ\), so the error \(0{,}013^\circ\) is about a hundred times smaller. The linear prediction with derivatives holds for small shifts, and its error falls roughly with the square of the shift.

What happens when the condition \(\sin q_2 \neq 0\) is dropped? It turns out that the conclusion of the corollary fails.

Take the configuration \(\vek q_* = (0, 0)\), where the arm is stretched and \(\sin q_2 = 0\). The blue arm has its elbow \(o_1\) on the axis \(x_0\) and a small angle \(q_2 = 0{,}6\) rad at the elbow. Its tip is at the point \(P = (1{,}4127,\ 0{,}2823)\) m, a little inside the outer edge. By theorem 27.34 there is another solution there (\(c = 0{,}8253\)), the orange arm with \(q_1' = 0{,}3945\) rad and \(q_2' = -0{,}6\) rad. Each posture is near \(\vek q_*\), since \(|q_2| \le 0{,}6\) rad and \(q_1 \in \{0,\ 0{,}3945\}\). With a smaller \(q_2\) the postures come closer still: at \(q_2 = 0{,}1\) rad the posture \((0, 0{,}1)\) has the same tip \((1{,}4975,\ 0{,}0499)\) m as the posture \((0{,}0666, -0{,}1)\), and at \(q_2 = 0{,}01\) rad the posture \((0, 0{,}01)\) has the same tip as \((0{,}0067, -0{,}01)\) (num-05.out, C7).

That this holds for every small angle and not only for three numbers follows from step 1 of the proof of theorem 27.34. For \(0 < t < \pi\) we have \(\Phi(0, t) = \vek w(t) = (l_1 + l_2\cos t,\ l_2\sin t)\). Let \(\beta\) be the angle of item 2 of the theorem, \(\vek w(t) = |\vek w(t)|\,(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)\). Both components are positive, \(l_1 + l_2\cos t \ge l_1 - l_2 = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_2\sin t > 0\) (lemma 8.11, item 3), so we choose \(\beta\) between \(0\) and \(\pi/2\). The cosine is even and the sine odd (the same lemma, item 4), so \(\vek w(-t) = |\vek w(t)|\,(\cos\beta, -\sin\beta)\), the mirror image of \(\vek w(t)\) in the axis \(x_0\). By step 1 and the addition formulas, \(\Phi(2\beta, -t) = \mat R(2\beta)\,\vek w(-t) = |\vek w(t)|\,(\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = \vek w(t)\). The set \((0, t)\) and the set \((2\beta, -t)\) are therefore different and have the same tip. At \(t = 0{,}1\) rad we get \(2\beta = 0{,}0666\) rad, exactly the \(q_1'\) above (NumPy). It remains to estimate that both are close to \((0, 0)\). From \(|\vek w(t)|^2 = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos t \ge (l_1 - l_2)^2\) (step 2 of the proof and \(\cos t \ge -1\)) we get \(|\vek w(t)| \ge l_1 - l_2\) (lemma 1.29). With \(\sin t \le t\) (lemma 8.20, step 2 of the proof) this gives \(\sin\beta = l_2\sin t/|\vek w(t)| \le l_2\,t/(l_1 - l_2) = t\). By the same lemma \(\beta - \sin\beta \le \beta^3/6 \le \beta\,\pi^2/24\), because \(\beta < \pi/2\), so \(\beta \le \sin\beta/(1 - \pi^2/24) < 1{,}7\,t\). Both sets are thus closer to \((0, 0)\) than \(2\cdot 1{,}7\,t + t = 4{,}4\,t\), which for small enough \(t\) is less than the radius of any ball around \((0, 0)\). In every ball around \((0, 0)\) there are therefore two different sets of angles with the same tip, and \(\Phi\) is not injective on any ball. A local inverse map does not exist, although \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable everywhere. The condition of the corollary is missing: \(\det\mat F_\Phi = l_1l_2\sin q_2\) is only \(0{,}0499\) m\(^2\) at \(q_2 = 0{,}1\) rad, \(0{,}005\) m\(^2\) at \(0{,}01\) rad and zero at \(0\).

We see the same if we follow how fast the angle \(q_2\) changes with the distance of the point from the edge. On the axis \(x_0\) we have \(r = x\). From (27.18), \(1 - c = ((l_1 + l_2)^2 - r^2)/(2l_1l_2) = (1{,}5 - r)(1{,}5 + r)\) (for example A, \(2l_1l_2 = 1\) m\(^2\)) and \(1 - c = 2\sin^2(q_2/2)\), because \(\cos q_2 = 1 - 2\sin^2(q_2/2)\) by the addition formula, For small angles \(\sin(q_2/2)\) is about \(q_2/2\) (lemma 8.20), so \(2\sin^2(q_2/2)\) is about \(q_2^2/2\), and near the edge \(1{,}5 + r\) is about \(3\) m. Hence \(q_2^2 \approx 6\,(1{,}5 - r)\) with the distance in metres and the angle in radians: \(|q_2|\) grows like the square root of the distance to the edge. At \(0{,}1\) m from the edge this is \(\sqrt{0{,}6} = 0{,}775\) rad \(= 44{,}4^\circ\), at \(0{,}01\) m it is \(\sqrt{0{,}06} = 0{,}245\) rad \(= 14{,}0^\circ\). Our own arm feels this: a stretched arm has to bend by as much as \(14^\circ\) at the elbow before the reach shortens by a single centimetre, because a link that starts to bend at first hardly shortens along the arm. The derivative of a square root, however, is unbounded at zero. Computed at points on the axis \(x_0\) (num-05.out, C6):

Figure 27.46 draws the columns of the table. Horizontally the distance \(r\) of the tip from joint 1, vertically the angle \(|q_2|\), the dashed vertical is the edge \(r = l_1 + l_2 = 1{,}5\) m. The blue curve is \(|q_2|\) from (27.18): towards the edge it falls ever more steeply and meets the axis at the edge vertically. At \(r = 1{,}4\) m, that is \(0{,}1\) m from the edge, the angle is \(44{,}77^\circ\) and the orange tangent has slope \(-3{,}98\) rad/m. At \(r = 1{,}49\) m, \(0{,}01\) m from the edge, the angle is still \(14{,}05^\circ\), while the tangent with slope \(-12{,}28\) rad/m is already three times steeper.

When the distance to the edge is ten times smaller, the derivative is about \(\sqrt{10} = 3{,}16\) times larger. The conclusion of the corollary (the derivative of the inverse is continuous in a neighbourhood of the point \(\Phi(\vek q_*)\), hence bounded there) therefore fails when the condition fails. For a robot this means: near the edge of the workspace a small motion of the tip demands a much faster rotation of the elbow. In section 27.7 we shall call the same configuration singular and see what it means for velocities and forces.

Corollary 27.35 says only that a local inverse exists, not how to find it. For the two-link arm we have theorem 27.34, which gives it in closed form. For a general arm with \(n\) joints the task is given by \(\mat T^0_{\mathrm{n}}(\vek q) = \mat H\) (the product of 27.31 and a given homogeneous transformation \(\mat H\), definition 27.20), which is twelve nonlinear equations for \(n\) unknowns, SPONG (5.2) and (5.4), PDF p. 163-164. It does not always have a solution, the solution is not always unique, and we cannot always write it in closed form (SPONG PDF p. 165). For robots with six joints whose wrist has three axes through one point, SPONG splits it into two tasks, for position and for orientation: the position of the wrist centre \(\vek o_{\mathrm{c}}\) depends by (5.10), PDF p. 166, only on the first three angles, and the orientation of the wrist by (5.13), PDF p. 167, on the last three. We do not prove this here, because it lies beyond the subject of the chapter.

We build the figure on the board. The first three links stand on the base and carry the wrist. The end of the third link is the wrist centre \(o_{\mathrm{c}}\), where the three wrist axes meet. On the wrist is the gripper. The first three angles move \(o_{\mathrm{c}}\), while the last three only turn the gripper about \(o_{\mathrm{c}}\) and do not move \(o_{\mathrm{c}}\), so the task splits into position and orientation.

For the first three angles of such a robot the calculation we have made suffices, because links 2 and 3 move in a plane (SPONG, PDF p. 172-173). We look at it on arm B of section 27.4, an elbow arm with a turning base and two links turning about horizontal axes: \(d_1 = 0{,}4\) m, \(a_2 = 0{,}5\) m, \(a_3 = 0{,}4\) m (our values, the layout is from SPONG, example 4.9, PDF p. 147-150).

Example 27.36 Inverse kinematics of the elbow arm B (inverzna kinematika komolčne roke B)

Let \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 45^\circ, -60^\circ)\), which by example B1 gives the tip \(T = (0{,}6408,\ 0{,}3700,\ 0{,}6500)\) m. We find all sets of angles that give this point.

  1. Links 2 and 3 lie in the vertical plane that \(q_1\) turns away from the axis \(x_0\). The tip is therefore \(T = (\rho\cos q_1,\ \rho\sin q_1,\ d_1 + a_2\sin q_2 + a_3\sin(q_2 + q_3))\) with \(\rho = a_2\cos q_2 + a_3\cos(q_2 + q_3)\) (checked with the product of DH matrices, num-05.out, D1). In this plane \(\rho\) is the abscissa of the tip and \(s = z - d_1\) the ordinate, and links 2 and 3 are the planar two-link arm of theorem 27.34 with \(l_1 = a_2\), \(l_2 = a_3\) and the angles \(q_2\) and \(q_3\).
  2. Let \(T = (0{,}6408,\ 0{,}3700,\ 0{,}6500)\) m. The horizontal distance from the axis \(z_0\) is \(r = \sqrt{0{,}6408^2 + 0{,}3700^2} = 0{,}7399\) m, the height above the shoulder is \(s = 0{,}65 - 0{,}4 = 0{,}25\) m, and \(r^2 + s^2 = 0{,}61\) m\(^2\). By (27.18), \(\cos q_3 = (0{,}61 - 0{,}25 - 0{,}16)/(2\cdot 0{,}5\cdot 0{,}4) = 0{,}5\), so \(q_3 = \pm 60^\circ\).
  3. The first two components of the tip give \((\rho\cos q_1, \rho\sin q_1) = (x, y)\), so \(\rho^2 = \rho^2(\cos^2 q_1 + \sin^2 q_1) = x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) (lemma 8.11, item 1) and \(\rho = r\) or \(\rho = -r\). For \(\rho = r\) we get \((\cos q_1, \sin q_1) = (x, y)/r = (\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ)\), so \(q_1 = 30^\circ\). For \(\rho = -r\) we get \((\cos q_1, \sin q_1) = -(\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ) = (\cos 210^\circ, \sin 210^\circ)\), so \(q_1 = 210^\circ\). The cosine and the sine fix an angle up to a multiple of \(2\pi\) (step 5 of the proof of theorem 27.34), so there are no other angles \(q_1\). For each \(\rho\), theorem 27.34 for the point \((\rho, s)\) gives two values \(q_3 = \pm 60^\circ\) and one \(q_2\) for each. In all there are four sets (num-05.out, D3): \((30^\circ, -7{,}659^\circ, 60^\circ)\), \((30^\circ, 45^\circ, -60^\circ)\), \((210^\circ, 135^\circ, 60^\circ)\) and \((210^\circ, -172{,}341^\circ, -60^\circ)\). Each gives the tip \(T\) by equation (27.16).

We build the figure on the board. The axis \(z_0\) is the vertical line through the base. Link 1 of length \(d_1\) is vertical up to the shoulder \(S\). From the shoulder we draw the blue links 2 and 3 with the elbow \(45^\circ\) above the horizontal and a bend downward by \(60^\circ\) towards the tip \(T\), and the orange ones with the elbow below the horizontal. Both end at the same point \(T\), at the distance \(r\) and height \(s\). Comparing solutions 2 and 3 we see that they have the same elbow and tip: the links lie on the same segments, but the sets of angles differ, because at \(q_1 = 210^\circ\) the arm is turned by half a turn about the axis \(z_0\) and \(q_2\) is raised past the vertical. The four sets of angles therefore give only two positions of the segments, which for ideal links without extent cannot be seen (the idealization of section 27.1).

We see the same from above. The axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) are the axes of the base, the axis \(z_0\) points at the reader. The point \(T\) is the projection of the tip, and the distance \(r\) from the axis \(z_0\) to it is the same in both solutions. For \(\rho = r\), links 2 and 3 lie in the direction \(q_1 = 30^\circ\) and reach \(T\). For \(\rho = -r\) the abscissa of the tip in the plane of the links is negative, so the base is turned half a turn to \(q_1 = 210^\circ\) and the links reach backwards over the axis to the same point \(T\).

The example does not behave like this everywhere. If the tip is on the axis \(z_0\), \(x = y = 0\), step 3 of the example does not determine the angle \(q_1\): from \((\rho\cos q_1, \rho\sin q_1) = (0, 0)\) we only get \(\rho = 0\), and \(q_1\) can be any angle.

The left side of the figure shows the posture in which links 2 and 3 have equal and opposite horizontal projections: \(\rho = a_2\cos 45^\circ + a_3\cos(45^\circ + 107{,}1144^\circ) = 0{,}3536 - 0{,}3536 = 0\) (num-05.out, D4). The tip is on the axis at \(z = 0{,}9406\) m and rotating the base does not move it. Every angle \(q_1\) gives the same point, as the view from above shows with the elbow going round a circle for different \(q_1\). There are infinitely many solutions, and this is the shoulder singularity that SPONG mentions at (5.18) and (5.19), PDF p. 170, and in figure 5.5, PDF p. 171. What fails is step 3 of the example, not theorem 27.34. The theorem holds for the point \((\rho, s) = (0,\ 0{,}5406)\) m in the plane of links 2 and 3, which is not the origin of that plane, and gives two pairs \((q_2, q_3)\), \((45^\circ,\ 107{,}1144^\circ)\) and \((135^\circ,\ -107{,}1144^\circ)\) with \(\cos q_3 = -0{,}2943\) (NumPy). For every \(q_1\) there are therefore two solutions. They are infinitely many because \(r = 0\), and step 3 cannot divide by \(r\) to get \((\cos q_1, \sin q_1)\) from \((x, y)\).
