Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.8 Mathematics: ordinary differential equations (matematika: navadne diferencialne enačbe)

For the hanging bar we started from the known displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), obtained the strain by differentiation and the force by equilibrium. The way led from a function to its derivatives. For the beam the situation is reversed. We know the load, and the deflection we look for is a function about which we only know how its fourth derivative is related to the load (theorem 5.42). What is such an equation, when does it have exactly one solution, and how do we find it when only the fourth derivative is given? We answer these questions in this section. First we name the equations whose unknown is a function. Then we prove that for our case we can solve them to the end, and count how many conditions we need so that the solution is no longer one of many but a single one. At the end we show what to do when a point force acts on the beam.

5.8.1 An equation for an unknown function (enačba za neznano funkcijo)

In chapter 1 we knew the solution and only checked it. Take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight, with the axis \(x\) vertical, pointing down from the fixed top. By lemma 4.75 on the equilibrium of a bar, \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), where \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g A\) is the weight per unit length, and by Hooke's law \(N = E A\,u'\). If \(E A\) is constant, it follows that

\[E\,u''(x) = -\rho g . \tag{5.40}\]

This is the equation for the displacement \(u\). The input is the position \(x\) on the bar, the output the displacement of the point, a number in metres. The equation does not say how large \(u(x)\) is. It only says that the second derivative of \(u\) equals the same number at every point, \(-\rho g/E = -3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The expression from chapter 1, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), satisfies the equation, since \(u'' = -2a = -3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The question is whether it is the only function that does.

The beam is the same. The steel beam of section 5.1.3, 20 mm wide and 40 mm high, has \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and self weight \(q = -61{,}21\) N/m (downward, the axis \(y\) points up). By theorem 5.42 we have

\[EI\,v''''(x) = q(x) , \qquad v''''(x) = -2{,}928 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-3} . \tag{5.41}\]

The unknown is the deflection \(v\), a function with input \(x\) and output numbers in metres, and the equation relates its fourth derivative to a given number on the right. Both equations have the same form: an unknown function of one variable \(x\) and its derivatives appear in them. The exact name and the definition are below. The equilibrium equations of chapter 2, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij},\mathrm{j}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), contain partial derivatives of functions of several variables \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) and are of a different kind, which we do not need here.

Definition 5.44 Ordinary differential equation (navadna diferencialna enačba)

Let \(I\) be an open interval, \(n\) a natural number and \(\Phi\) a function with \(n + 2\) numbers as input and one number as output. An ordinary differential equation (navadna diferencialna enačba) of order \(n\) is an equation

\[\Phi\big(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}\big) = 0 , \tag{5.42}\]

in which the unknown is a function \(y\) of one variable \(x \in I\). The order (red) of the equation is the order of the highest derivative that appears in it. A solution (rešitev) on the interval \(I\) is a function \(y\colon I \to \mathbb{R}\) that is \(n\) times differentiable at every point of \(I\) and for which (5.42) holds at every \(x \in I\) when \(y(x)\) and its derivatives are substituted. The equation is linear (linearna) if it can be written as

\[a_{\mathrm{n}}(x)\,y^{(n)} + a_{\mathrm{n}-1}(x)\,y^{(n-1)} + \dots + a_1(x)\,y' + a_0(x)\,y = f(x)\]

with given functions \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(f\) of the variable \(x\). If \(f\) is zero everywhere, the equation is homogeneous (homogena).

Source: KREYSZIG §1.1 (PDF p. 29-30) for the ordinary differential equation, order and solution on an open interval, §3.1 (PDF p. 131) for the linear and homogeneous equation of order \(n\).

The adjective ordinary in the definition says that the unknown is a function of one variable. Equations with partial derivatives, such as the equilibrium equations of chapter 2, are called partial differential equations. Equation (5.40) is linear of order 2, with \(a_2 = E\), \(a_1 = a_0 = 0\) and \(f = -\rho g\). Equation (5.41) is linear of order 4. Both are also especially simple, since the unknown \(y\) itself and its lower derivatives do not appear. We can therefore solve them with the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40), which we do in the next subsection.

The equation \(y'' = (y')^2\) has the same order as the linear \(y'' = y'\) (\(a_2 = 1\), \(a_1 = -1\)), but it is not linear. We write it as \(y'' - (y')^2 = 0\), and the coefficient at \(y'\) would be \(-y'\), which is not a given function of \(x\) but depends on the unknown. This is not only a matter of notation. For a linear homogeneous equation twice a solution is again a solution, because we multiply all the terms by \(2\). For \(y'' = y'\) this does hold: \((2y)'' = 2y' = (2y)'\). For our equation it does not.

  1. The function \(y = -\ln(1 - x)\) is defined if \(1 - x > 0\), so on the interval \(x < 1\), and it is arbitrarily differentiable there. We have \(y' = 1/(1 - x)\) and \(y'' = 1/(1 - x)^2 = (y')^2\). This is a solution on \(I = (-\infty, 1)\).
  2. For \(2y\) we have \((2y)'' = 2/(1 - x)^2\) and \(\big((2y)'\big)^2 = 4/(1 - x)^2\). Since \(1/(1 - x)^2 > 0\) for every \(x < 1\) and \(2 \neq 4\), the two sides differ everywhere. The double \(2y\) is not a solution on any interval, so the equation is not linear.

First let us see how many solutions the simplest equation, \(y'' = 2\), has (figure 5.48). The function \(y = x^2\) solves it. So do \(y = x^2 + 1\) and \(y = x^2 + x\), since an added constant term and an added term \(x\) vanish when differentiated twice. The solution is not one but a whole family of parabolas shifted against each other. In the figure three solutions share the point \((0, 1)\) but have different slopes in it: \(y'(0) = -2, 0, 2\). The fact that the curve passes through a point is therefore not enough. We need the slope as well. For an equation of the fourth order we will need four data, as we will see in subsection 5.8.3.

5.8.2 A function with a known n-th derivative (funkcija z znanim n-tim odvodom)

How do we find all functions with a given \(n\)-th derivative? For \(n = 1\) the answer is known. If \(y' = f\) with a continuous function \(f\) on \([a, b]\), then by the second part of the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40) one solution is the integral \(y_{\mathrm{p}}(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), and every other differs from it by a constant function by corollary 1.85, since the difference has derivative zero. For \(n = 2\) we proceed twice in a row: from \(y'' = f\) we get that \(y'\) is an integral of \(f\) plus a constant function \(c_1\), and then that \(y\) is an integral of this function plus \(c_0\). Each integration brings one constant. For \(y'' = 2\) we get \(y' = 2x + c_1\) and \(y = x^2 + c_1 x + c_0\), the family of figure 5.48. It is the same for the fourth derivative: four integrations, four constants. Let us write this precisely for an arbitrary order \(n\).

Let \(f\) be a continuous function on \([a, b]\). We define the repeated integral (ponavljani integral) step by step:

\[I_0 = f , \qquad I_{\mathrm{k}}(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x I_{\mathrm{k}-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \quad (k = 1, \dots, n) . \tag{5.43}\]

The function \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(f\) integrated \(k\) times in a row with the start at \(a\), so \(I_{\mathrm{k}}(a) = 0\). For \(f = 1\), for example, \(I_1(x) = x - a\) and \(I_2(x) = (x - a)^2/2\).

Theorem 5.45 A function with a known n-th derivative (funkcija z znanim n-tim odvodom)

Let \(n\) be a natural number, \(a < b\) and \(f\) a continuous function on \([a, b]\), and let the functions \(I_0, \dots, I_{\mathrm{n}}\) be as in (5.43). A function \(y\colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}\) that is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with its derivatives \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) has the \(n\)-th derivative \(y^{(n)}(x) = f(x)\) at every point of the interval \((a, b)\) if and only if

\[y(x) = I_{\mathrm{n}}(x) + c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}-1} x^{n-1} \tag{5.44}\]

for some numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) (a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), definition 1.63).

Proof

We must show two directions. First we show that every function of the form (5.44) satisfies the conditions, then by induction on \(n\) that there are no others.

1. Functions of the form (5.44) are solutions.

  1. The function \(I_0 = f\) is continuous. Let \(k \ge 1\) and let \(I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). By part 2 of theorem 3.40, \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\) is differentiable on \([a, b]\) with \(I_{\mathrm{k}}' = I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\), and so continuous by lemma 1.79. By induction (theorem 1.44) each of the functions \(I_0, \dots, I_{\mathrm{n}}\) is therefore continuous and \(I_{\mathrm{k}}' = I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\).
  2. Hence for \(m = 0, \dots, n\) we get \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)} = I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}\). For \(m \le n - 1\) these derivatives are continuous on \([a, b]\), and \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(n)} = I_0 = f\).
  3. For the polynomial \(p(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}-1} x^{n-1}\), by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81, parts 1, 2, 4 and 5) every derivative \(p^{(m)}\) is again a polynomial, so continuous. The term \(c_{\mathrm{k}} x^k\) with \(k \le n - 1\) becomes constant after \(k\) differentiations and zero after \(k + 1\), so \(p^{(n)} = 0\).
  4. By steps 2 and 3 the function \(y = I_{\mathrm{n}} + p\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with its derivatives up to order \(n - 1\), and \(y^{(n)} = f + 0 = f\) (lemma 1.81, part 1).

2. There are no other solutions.

  1. Let \(y\) be a function with the properties of the theorem and \(z = y - I_{\mathrm{n}}\). By step 2, \(z, z', \dots, z^{(n-1)}\) are continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(z^{(n)} = f - f = 0\) on \((a, b)\). We must show that \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\).
  2. We prove this by induction on the number \(m\) of derivatives. Let the claim \(P(m)\) say: every function \(w\) that is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with the derivatives \(w', \dots, w^{(m-1)}\) and has \(w^{(m)} = 0\) on \((a, b)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\).
  3. Base case: \(P(1)\). The function \(w\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(w' = 0\) on \((a, b)\), so it is constant by corollary 1.85, that is, a polynomial of degree \(0\).
  4. Induction step: let \(P(m)\) hold and let \(w\) be a function from the claim \(P(m + 1)\), so \(w, \dots, w^{(m)}\) are continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(w^{(m+1)} = 0\) on \((a, b)\). The function \(w'\) is then continuous together with its derivatives up to order \(m - 1\) and \((w')^{(m)} = 0\). By \(P(m)\), \(w'(x) = d_0 + d_1 x + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^{m-1}\).
  5. Let \(g(x) = w(x) - \big(d_0 x + d_1 x^2/2 + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^m/m\big)\). By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81, parts 1, 2 and 5), \(g'(x) = w'(x) - (d_0 + d_1 x + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^{m-1}) = 0\) on \((a, b)\), and \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), since \(w\) and the polynomial are. By corollary 1.85 \(g\) is constant, and \(w\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m\).
  6. By the principle of induction (theorem 1.44) \(P(m)\) holds for every \(m\). With \(m = n\) and \(w = z\), \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), so \(y = I_{\mathrm{n}} + z\) has the form (5.44).

Source: The proof is not from a source. A special case of the general theory of linear equations of order \(n\) is in KREYSZIG §3.1 (PDF p. 131-134), but here it is proved directly with the fundamental theorem of calculus.

We proved the theorem back to the fundamental theorem of calculus, the corollary on the zero derivative and the principle of induction. It showed that the \(n\)-th derivative leaves exactly \(n\) free numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}-1}\): the repeated integral \(I_{\mathrm{n}}\) gives one solution, and all the others differ from it by a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\).

Let us try it on the hanging bar. Equation (5.40) is \(u'' = -2a\) with \(2a = \rho g/E\). Here \(n = 2\), \(f = -2a\) and with the start of the interval at \(x = 0\) we have \(I_2(x) = -a x^2\), so all the solutions are

\[u(x) = -a x^2 + c_0 + c_1 x .\]

The displacement from chapter 1 has \(c_0 = 0\) and \(c_1 = 2aL\), that is, \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). Other numbers give other solutions of the equation. With \(c_0 \neq 0\), for example, the top of the bar is displaced, which would mean that the bar is not fixed. For the beam with self weight, in equation (5.41) \(f = k := -2{,}928 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-3}\) and \(n = 4\). The fourfold integral with the start at \(x = 0\) is \(I_4(x) = k\,x^4/24\), so the general deflection is

\[v(x) = \frac{k}{24}\,x^4 + c_3 x^3 + c_2 x^2 + c_1 x + c_0 , \qquad \frac{k}{24} = -1{,}220 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^{-3} .\]

Here \(c_0, \dots, c_3\) are four arbitrary numbers. When we fix them, the deflection, the slope, the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) and the shear force \(V = -EI\,v'''\) are fixed as well. What fixes the four numbers is the subject of the next subsection.

5.8.3 Initial and boundary value problems (začetni in robni problem)

The solution of equation (5.33) is not one but a four-parameter family. For the beam, however, we look for one deflection, the one the beam gets with the given supports and load. The supports therefore impose conditions that select one function from the family. The conditions can be of two kinds, as figure 5.49 shows.

Definition 5.46 Initial value problem (začetni problem)

Let an ordinary differential equation of order \(n\) (definition 5.44) be given on an interval \(I\), a point \(x_0 \in I\) and numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\). The initial value problem (začetni problem) is the task of finding a solution \(y\) of the equation on \(I\) that satisfies the initial conditions (začetni pogoji)

\[y(x_0) = y_0 , \quad y'(x_0) = y_1 , \quad \dots , \quad y^{(n-1)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{n}-1} . \tag{5.45}\]

There are as many conditions as the order of the equation, and all are given at the same point \(x_0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §1.1 (PDF p. 32) for order 1, §3.1 (PDF p. 133) for order \(n\).

Definition 5.47 Boundary value problem (robni problem)

Let an ordinary differential equation of order \(n\) be given on a closed interval \([a, b]\). The boundary value problem (robni problem) is the task of finding a solution of the equation that satisfies \(n\) boundary conditions (robni pogoji). Each condition connects values of the solution and its derivatives at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\). A condition is linear if it has the form \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{n-1} \big(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\,y^{(k)}(a) + \beta_{\mathrm{k}}\,y^{(k)}(b)\big) = \gamma\) with given numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\gamma\).

Source: CM (7.3.33), (7.3.34) (PDF p. 310) for the boundary conditions of a beam, KREYSZIG §3.1 (PDF p. 133) for initial conditions as opposed to boundary conditions.

For a beam the conditions of section 5.7.2 are linear boundary conditions: each contains \(v\), \(v'\), \(M = EI\,v''\) or \(V = -EI\,v'''\) at one end, and at each end two are prescribed, one from the pair \((v, V)\) and one from the pair \((v', M)\). Does the boundary value problem really differ so much from the initial one? Let us look at the cantilever of section 5.1.3: it is clamped at \(x = 0\), of length \(L = 1\) m, and at the free end \(x = L\) a force of \(500\) N pulls it down. We neglect the self weight, so \(v'''' = 0\).

The initial value problem at the clamp asks the following. We know \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\) (the clamp) and some numbers \(M(0)\) and \(V(0)\). What is the deflection? By theorem 5.45 with \(f = 0\) we have \(I_4 = 0\) and the solutions are polynomials of the third degree. From \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\) it follows that \(c_0 = c_1 = 0\). From \(M(0) = EI\,v''(0) = 2EI\,c_2\) and \(V(0) = -EI\,v'''(0) = -6EI\,c_3\) we get

\[v(x) = \frac{1}{EI}\Big(\frac{M(0)}{2}\,x^2 - \frac{V(0)}{6}\,x^3\Big) , \qquad M(x) = M(0) - V(0)\,x , \qquad V(x) = V(0) .\]

For every pair \(\big(M(0), V(0)\big)\) this is exactly one solution. We, however, do not know these two numbers, since they are the clamping moment and the reaction that the clamp creates only now. We do know the conditions at the free end: \(M(L) = 0\) and \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N, because by the definition of the shear force (definition 5.7) \(V(L)\) equals the external force acting on the right end, positive upward. From \(V(L) = V(0)\) we get \(V(0) = -500\) N, and from \(M(L) = M(0) - V(0)\,L = 0\) we get \(M(0) = V(0)\,L = -500\) N m. This is the clamping moment \(-F L\) of section 5.1.3. The boundary value problem is therefore an initial value problem with two unknown data, which the conditions at the other end determine. The deflection at the free end is \(v(L) = \big(M(0)\,L^2/2 - V(0)\,L^3/6\big)/EI = (-250 + 83{,}33)\ \text{N m}^3 / 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2 = -7{,}972\) mm, which agrees with section 5.1.3.

Does the boundary value problem have exactly one solution for every beam? For a beam with constant \(EI\) and a continuous load we reduce it to two equations in two unknowns. By theorem 5.45 every solution is

\[v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3 , \qquad v_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{I_4}{EI} ,\]

where \(I_4\) is the repeated integral of the load \(q\) with the start at \(x = 0\). By step 2 of the proof of this theorem, \(I_4^{(m)} = I_{4-\mathrm{m}}\), and the functions \(I_1, \dots, I_4\) are zero at \(x = 0\). Therefore \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and its first three derivatives vanish at \(x = 0\) and

\[v(0) = c_0 , \qquad v'(0) = c_1 , \qquad M(0) = 2EI\,c_2 , \qquad V(0) = -6EI\,c_3 . \tag{5.46}\]

By section 5.7.2 each end of the beam has one condition from the pair \((v, V)\) and one from the pair \((v', M)\). For the four kinds of ends that we need, this is:

  • clamp (vpetje): \(v\) and \(v'\) prescribed,
  • pin support (nepomična členkasta podpora) or roller support (pomična členkasta podpora): \(v\) and \(M\) prescribed,
  • free end (prost konec): \(V\) and \(M\) prescribed,
  • sliding clamp (drsno vpetje): \(v'\) and \(V\) prescribed.

In this section "pin" stands for the pin support and the roller support together, since both prescribe the same, \(v = 0\) and \(M = 0\). We met the first three kinds in section 5.7.2. The fourth is new and is not among the supports of definition 5.5, so we define it here. A sliding clamp is an end of the beam at which the slope \(v'\) and the shear force \(V\) are prescribed, usually \(v' = 0\) (the cross-section does not rotate) and \(V\) equal to the force acting on the end: the end moves freely across the axis but does not rotate. Like every support it is an idealization. Its real counterpart is a collar on the end of the beam that slides along a smooth vertical guide. A real guide has friction and play, so the end would rotate a little and the shear force would not be prescribed exactly.

The prescribed values can be arbitrary, for example a support can settle. If the left end (\(x = 0\)) is a clamp, equation (5.46) gives \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) directly. For a pin support we get \(c_0\) and \(c_2\), for a free end \(c_2\) and \(c_3\), and for a sliding clamp \(c_1\) and \(c_3\). Two constants are therefore fixed, and the other two, which we call \(s\) and \(t\), remain unknown. The two conditions at the right end are linear in \(s\) and \(t\), so we get two equations in two unknowns. First let us say when such a system has exactly one solution.

Lemma 5.48 A system of two linear equations in two unknowns (sistem dveh linearnih enačb z dvema neznankama)

Let \(a, b, c, d, r_1, r_2\) be real numbers and \(D = ad - bc\). The system

\[a s + b t = r_1 , \qquad c s + d t = r_2 \tag{5.47}\]

has exactly one solution for every pair \((r_1, r_2)\) if \(D \neq 0\). If \(D = 0\), the homogeneous system (\(r_1 = r_2 = 0\)) has a solution \((s, t) \neq (0, 0)\), so for no right side does the system have exactly one solution: it has either none or infinitely many. If \(D = 0\), the system is solvable exactly when \(\mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2 = 0\) for every pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2)\) with \(\mu_1\,(a, b) + \mu_2\,(c, d) = (0, 0)\), that is, for which the rows of coefficients, multiplied by \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\), add up to zero.

Proof

We must show that for \(D \neq 0\) there is exactly one solution, that for \(D = 0\) there is a nonzero solution of the homogeneous system, and that for \(D = 0\) the condition with \(\mu_1, \mu_2\) is necessary and sufficient.

  1. Let \(D \neq 0\) and let \((s, t)\) be a solution. We multiply the first equation by \(d\), the second by \(b\) and subtract: \((ad - bc)\,s = d r_1 - b r_2\). We multiply the first equation by \(c\), the second by \(a\) and subtract: \((bc - ad)\,t = c r_1 - a r_2\). The solution can therefore only be \(s = (d r_1 - b r_2)/D\), \(t = (a r_2 - c r_1)/D\).
  2. We substitute these numbers. The left side of the first equation is \(\big(a d r_1 - a b r_2 + a b r_2 - b c r_1\big)/D = r_1\) and the left side of the second is \(\big(c d r_1 - b c r_2 + a d r_2 - c d r_1\big)/D = r_2\). The numbers are therefore a solution, and the only one.
  3. Let \(D = 0\). If \((a, b) \neq (0, 0)\), we take \((s, t) = (b, -a) \neq (0, 0)\). The first equation gives \(ab - ba = 0\) and the second \(cb - da = -D = 0\). If \(a = b = 0\) and \((c, d) \neq (0, 0)\), we take \((s, t) = (d, -c)\), which gives \(0\) and \(cd - dc = 0\). If all four coefficients are zero, we take \((1, 0)\).
  4. Let the system with \(D = 0\) have a solution \((s_0, t_0)\) and let \((s_1, t_1) \neq (0, 0)\) be a solution of the homogeneous system. Then by step 3 and linearity \((s_0 + \lambda s_1,\, t_0 + \lambda t_1)\) is a solution for every \(\lambda\), and different \(\lambda\) give different solutions. There are infinitely many solutions.
  5. Necessity for \(D = 0\): let \((s, t)\) be a solution and \(\mu_1(a, b) + \mu_2(c, d) = (0, 0)\). We multiply the first equation by \(\mu_1\), the second by \(\mu_2\) and add: \((\mu_1 a + \mu_2 c)\,s + (\mu_1 b + \mu_2 d)\,t = \mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2\). The left side is zero, so \(\mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2 = 0\).
  6. Sufficiency for \(D = 0\): let the condition hold for every such pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2)\). If \((a, b) \neq (0, 0)\), there is a \(\lambda\) with \((c, d) = \lambda\,(a, b)\): for \(a \neq 0\) we have \(\lambda = c/a\) and \(d = bc/a = \lambda b\) from \(ad = bc\), for \(a = 0\) we have \(b \neq 0\), \(D = -bc = 0\) gives \(c = 0\) and \(\lambda = d/b\). The pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2) = (\lambda, -1)\) has \(\lambda\,(a, b) - (c, d) = (0, 0)\), so \(r_2 = \lambda r_1\). We take \((s, t) = r_1\,(a, b)/(a^2 + b^2)\), which gives \(a s + b t = r_1\) and \(c s + d t = \lambda\,(a s + b t) = \lambda r_1 = r_2\). If \((a, b) = (0, 0)\) and \((c, d) \neq (0, 0)\), the pair \((1, 0)\) has the property, so \(r_1 = 0\), and with \((s, t) = r_2\,(c, d)/(c^2 + d^2)\) the second equation holds and the first reads \(0 = r_1\). If all coefficients are zero, every pair has the property, so \(r_1 = r_2 = 0\) and every \((s, t)\) solves the system.

Theorem 5.49 Uniqueness for the beam boundary value problem (enoličnost robnega problema nosilca)

Let \(EI\) be constant, \(q\) continuous on \([0, L]\), and at each end of the beam let two conditions of one of the four kinds (clamp, pin support, free end, sliding clamp) be prescribed with arbitrary values. Let \(D = \det B\) for the matrix \(B\) as above. Then:

  1. If \(D \neq 0\), the boundary value problem for the equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) has exactly one solution for every load and every prescribed values.
  2. If \(D = 0\), there is no load and no values for which it has exactly one solution: it has either none or infinitely many.
  3. The determinant \(D\) is in the table below. It is zero exactly for the combinations free–free, free–pin, pin–free, free–sliding, sliding–free and sliding–sliding. These are exactly the combinations for which some function \(w = a + b x \neq 0\) satisfies the homogeneous conditions (values zero) of both ends.
Proof

We must show that the boundary value problem reduces to the system (5.47), that the lemma on it gives parts 1 and 2, and that \(D\) is zero exactly in the listed six combinations.

  1. By theorem 5.45 every solution of the equation has the form \(v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3\) and every such function is a solution. The function \(v\) is therefore a solution of the boundary value problem if and only if the numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_3\) satisfy the four conditions.
  2. By equation (5.46) the two conditions of the left end contain only two constants, which each kind of end fixes uniquely from the prescribed values (clamp \(c_0, c_1\), pin \(c_0, c_2\), free end \(c_2, c_3\), sliding clamp \(c_1, c_3\)). What remain are \(s\) and \(t\), which the conditions of the left end do not contain.
  3. The left side of each condition at the right end is linear in \(v\). For \(v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + (\text{known part}) + s\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) we write it as (value on \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the known part) \(+ B_{\mathrm{j}1} s + B_{\mathrm{j}2} t\). Condition \(j\) is therefore \(B_{\mathrm{j}1} s + B_{\mathrm{j}2} t = r_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(r_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the prescribed value, reduced by the value on \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the known part, and divided by \(EI\) for \(M\) and \(V\). This is the system (5.47).
  4. If \(D \neq 0\), then by lemma 5.48 the system has exactly one solution \((s, t)\) for every \((r_1, r_2)\), and the constants of the left end are fixed in step 2. The solution of the boundary value problem is therefore unique, which is part 1.
  5. If \(D = 0\), let \((s_1, t_1) \neq (0, 0)\) be a solution of the homogeneous system from the lemma. The conditions of the left end do not contain \(s\) and \(t\) (step 2), and the conditions of the right end change under the added function \(\psi = s_1 \varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t_1 \varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) by \(B\,(s_1, t_1)^{\mathsf T} = \vek 0\). If \(v\) is a solution of the boundary value problem, then \(v + \lambda\psi\) is a solution for every \(\lambda\), and these are different. There are infinitely many solutions or none. This is part 2.
  6. We computed the determinant \(D\) for the sixteen combinations with SymPy (script nde_det.py), and it is in the table. For a clamp at the left end, for example, \(s = c_2\), \(t = c_3\), and for a free right end \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -6 \\ 2 & 6L \end{pmatrix}\), which gives \(D = 0 \cdot 6L - (-6) \cdot 2 = 12\).
  7. Let \(w = a + bx \neq 0\) be a function that satisfies the homogeneous conditions of both ends. Then \(w\) and the zero function are two different solutions of the homogeneous boundary value problem (\(q = 0\), all values zero), so by part 1 \(D = 0\). Conversely, for the six combinations with \(D = 0\) such a function exists: free–free \(w = a + bx\), free–pin \(w = b\,(x - L)\), pin–free \(w = b x\), free–sliding, sliding–free and sliding–sliding \(w = a\). A free end satisfies the homogeneous condition for every \(w\) (since \(w'' = w''' = 0\)), a pin requires \(w = 0\) at the pin, and a sliding clamp requires \(w' = 0\). For the remaining ten combinations a clamp requires \(w = w' = 0\) at one point and so \(w = 0\) everywhere, while pin–pin, pin–sliding and sliding–pin give \(a = 0\) and \(b = 0\). Such a function therefore does not exist, and there \(D \neq 0\) indeed, by the table. This is part 3.

What does \(D \neq 0\) mean physically? The function \(w = a + bx\) has \(w'' = w''' = 0\), so it creates neither a moment nor a shear force. It is a deflection without load that the supports allow, that is, a rigid displacement (definition 1.11), since for small rotations the distances between points of the axis do not change. By part 3 of the theorem, \(D = 0\) exactly when the supports do not prevent such a displacement. For \(D \neq 0\) the supports prevent every rigid displacement and the solution is unique, as in lemma 4.133 for a body in space. A clamp at one end is enough, as are two pins or a pin with a sliding clamp. A free end together with a free end, a pin or a sliding clamp is not enough, and neither are two sliding clamps.

An example without supports is a beam with free ends (figure 5.50). The conditions without external loads at the ends are \(M = V = 0\) at both ends. The left end fixes \(c_2 = c_3 = 0\), and the unknowns are \(s = c_0\) and \(t = c_1\). The functions \(1\) and \(x\) have zero second and third derivatives, so \(B\) is the zero matrix and \(D = 0\):

\[B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad D = 0 .\]

The deflection \(v = c_0 + c_1 x\) is a solution without load: the beam moves up by \(c_0\) and rotates by a small angle \(c_1\). The distance between two points of the axis, which was \(x_2 - x_1\), becomes \((x_2 - x_1)\sqrt{1 + c_1^2}\), which for \(c_1 = 0{,}01\) is only \(0{,}005\ \%\) more. For small rotations this is therefore a rigid displacement (definition 1.11), and the beam is not attached anywhere.

For a beam without supports a solution does not exist for an arbitrary load. Two conditions are necessary, and we derive them directly from the equation.

Derivation 5.50 The load of an unsupported beam must be in equilibrium (obtežba nosilca brez podpor mora biti v ravnovesju)

Let a beam of length \(L\) with constant \(EI\) and continuous load \(q\) have prescribed \(M(0)\), \(V(0)\), \(M(L)\) and \(V(L)\), where \(M = EI\,v''\) (definition 5.8) and \(V = -EI\,v'''\) (definition 5.7). We show that a solution exists if and only if

\[\begin{aligned} V(0) - V(L) &= \int_0^L q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ M(0) - M(L) - L\,V(L) &= \int_0^L x\,q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x , \end{aligned} \tag{5.48}\]

and that the solution is then determined up to a rigid displacement \(c_0 + c_1 x\).

  1. Let \(v\) be a solution. By theorem 5.45 \(v'''\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) and \((EI\,v''')' = q\) on \((0, L)\). By part 1 of theorem 3.40, \(\int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\,v'''(L) - EI\,v'''(0) = -V(L) + V(0)\). This is the first relation.
  2. By the product rule (lemma 1.81, part 3) the function \(x\,EI\,v'''\) has the derivative \(EI\,v''' + x\,q\) on \((0, L)\), and this derivative is continuous on \([0, L]\). By part 1 of theorem 3.40, therefore, \(L\,EI\,v'''(L) = \int_0^L EI\,v'''\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). Since \(EI\,v'''\) is the derivative of the function \(EI\,v''\), the first integral on the right equals \(M(L) - M(0)\) by the same part of the theorem. So \(-L\,V(L) = M(L) - M(0) + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the second relation.
  3. The relations are therefore necessary. Conversely, let them hold. We take \(v = I_4/EI + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3\), which by theorem 5.45 is the general solution of the equation. Since \(I_4'' = I_2\), \(I_4''' = I_1\) and \(I_1(0) = I_2(0) = 0\), the conditions at the left end give \(c_2 = M(0)/(2EI)\) and \(c_3 = -V(0)/(6EI)\). The numbers \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) remain arbitrary.
  4. For the function so chosen steps 1 and 2 hold, because they hold for every solution of the equation. The first gives \(V(L) = V(0) - \int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the second \(M(L) = M(0) - L\,V(L) - \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). The relations (5.48) say that these are exactly the prescribed values \(V(L)\) and \(M(L)\). The conditions at the right end are therefore satisfied by themselves.

The relations are exactly the equilibrium of the whole beam. By the definition of the shear force the external force at the left end is \(-V(0)\) (upward positive), at the right end \(V(L)\), and the external couple is \(-M(0)\) at the left end and \(M(L)\) at the right end. The first relation is therefore \(-V(0) + V(L) + \int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), the sum of vertical forces. The second is the sum of moments about the point \(x = 0\): \(-M(0) + M(L) + L\,V(L) + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), where the force \(V(L)\) at the right end has the arm \(L\) and the load at the point \(x\) has the arm \(x\). We derived the equation for the deflection from the equilibrium of a slice of the beam, so without the equilibrium of the whole it cannot have a solution. If the equilibrium holds, there are infinitely many solutions, because the beam is not attached anywhere. The supports fix all three beams with \(D \neq 0\), which a beam without supports cannot do.

The same holds for every combination of ends with \(D = 0\), not only for a beam without supports. We obtain the condition for the load by using the allowed rigid displacement \(w = a + bx\) as a weight in the equation.

Lemma 5.51 Load condition for a rigid displacement (pogoj za obtežbo pri togem premiku)

Let \(EI\) be constant, \(q\) continuous on \([0, L]\) and \(v\) a function whose derivatives \(v, v', v'', v'''\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and for which \(EI\,v'''' = q\) on \((0, L)\) (a solution of the equation, not necessarily of the boundary value problem). Let \(M = EI\,v''\), \(V = -EI\,v'''\) and \(w = a + bx\) with arbitrary numbers \(a\) and \(b\). Then

\[\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + \Big[V\,w + M\,w'\Big]_0^L = 0 . \tag{5.49}\]
Proof

We must show that the left side is the integral of a derivative that we can evaluate.

  1. By theorem 5.45 \(v, v', v'', v'''\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and \((EI\,v''')' = q\) on \((0, L)\). By the product rule (lemma 1.81, part 3) \((EI\,v'''\,w)' = q\,w + EI\,v'''\,w'\) and, since \(w'' = 0\), also \((EI\,v''\,w')' = EI\,v'''\,w'\).
  2. We subtract: \((EI\,v'''\,w - EI\,v''\,w')' = q\,w\) on \((0, L)\). This derivative is continuous on \([0, L]\). By part 1 of theorem 3.40, \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = \big[EI\,v'''\,w - EI\,v''\,w'\big]_0^L\).
  3. Since \(EI\,v''' = -V\) and \(EI\,v'' = M\), this is \(-\big[V\,w + M\,w'\big]_0^L\), which is the claim.

For the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\) we can say more than for the boundary value problem. The initial value problem always has exactly one solution.

Theorem 5.53 The initial value problem for the n-th derivative (začetni problem za n-ti odvod)

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\), \(x_0 \in [a, b]\) and \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) arbitrary numbers. The initial value problem (definition 5.46) \(y^{(n)} = f\) with initial conditions \(y^{(k)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 0, \dots, n - 1\) has exactly one solution in the class of functions of theorem 5.45,

\[y(x) = I_{\mathrm{n}}(x) - \sum_{\mathrm{m}=0}^{n-1} I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0)\,\frac{(x - x_0)^m}{m!} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{n-1} y_{\mathrm{k}}\,\frac{(x - x_0)^k}{k!} . \tag{5.50}\]
Proof

We must show that (5.50) is a solution with the given initial values and that there is no other.

  1. The sums \(\sum_{\mathrm{m}} I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0)(x - x_0)^m/m!\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} y_{\mathrm{k}} (x - x_0)^k/k!\) are polynomials of degree at most \(n - 1\) (multiply out the brackets). By theorem 5.45 the function (5.50) is therefore a solution of the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\).
  2. By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81, parts 1, 3 and 4), by induction on \(k\), \(\big((x - x_0)^k/k!\big)^{(m)} = (x - x_0)^{k-m}/(k-m)!\) for \(k \ge m\) and zero for \(k < m\). At \(x = x_0\) this derivative is \(1\) for \(k = m\) and \(0\) otherwise.
  3. For \(m \le n - 1\) we therefore have \(y^{(m)}(x_0) = I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)}(x_0) - I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0) + y_{\mathrm{m}}\). By step 2 of the proof of theorem 5.45, \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)} = I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}\), so \(y^{(m)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  4. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be two solutions. The difference \(z = \tilde y - y\) has \(z^{(n)} = 0\) and \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = 0\) for \(m < n\). By theorem 5.45 with \(f = 0\), \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), which we write in powers of \(x - x_0\) (multiply out the brackets): \(z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}} (x - x_0)^k\).
  5. By step 2, \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = m!\,d_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = 0\) gives \(d_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) for every \(m\). Hence \(z = 0\) and \(\tilde y = y\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. For a general linear equation of order \(n\) with continuous coefficients the same is stated in KREYSZIG §3.1, theorem 2 (PDF p. 134).

For the beam this means that four data at one endpoint, \(v\), \(v'\), \(M\) and \(V\), determine the deflection. A boundary value problem, however, can have one solution, none or infinitely many, as we saw for the three beams and for the beams with \(D = 0\).

For a general equation the picture is similar but harder. For linear equations of order \(n\) with leading coefficient \(1\) and continuous coefficients the initial value problem has exactly one solution on every open interval containing \(x_0\) (KREYSZIG §3.1, theorem 2, PDF p. 134). For a nonlinear equation of the first order \(y' = F(x, y)\), Picard's theorem (LEBL1 theorem 6.3.2, PDF p. 247) says that there is an \(h > 0\) and exactly one differentiable function \(y\) on \([x_0 - h, x_0 + h]\) with \(y' = F(x, y)\) and \(y(x_0) = y_0\), if \(F\) is continuous on a closed bounded rectangle with the point \((x_0, y_0)\) in its interior and if for some number \(\Lambda\) we have \(|F(x, y_1) - F(x, y_2)| \le \Lambda\,|y_1 - y_2|\) for all points of the rectangle, that is, \(F\) does not grow in the variable \(y\) faster than a straight line. We do not prove the first theorem in this book, and Picard's theorem is proved in chapter 8. We will need them for vibrations, described by a linear equation of the second order (chapter 9), and for the equations of motion (chapter 8).

The condition of continuity and bounded slope is there for a reason. The equation \(y' = 2\sqrt{|y|}\) has two solutions for the initial condition \(y(0) = 0\): \(y_1 = 0\) and \(y_2(x) = x\,|x|\). Indeed \(y_2'(x) = 2|x|\) for every \(x\) and \(2\sqrt{|y_2|} = 2\sqrt{x^2} = 2|x|\). The condition with the number \(\Lambda\) does not hold here, since for \(y_2 = 0\) and \(y_1 = y > 0\) the ratio \(2\sqrt{y}/y = 2/\sqrt{y}\) is unbounded as \(y\) tends to zero. The equation \(y' = y^2\) with \(y(0) = 1\) has the solution \(y = 1/(1 - x)\), which grows without bound as \(x \to 1\) and exists only on the interval \((-\infty, 1)\). A solution therefore does not necessarily exist on the whole interval we are interested in. For the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\) nothing like this happens, because \(f\) does not contain the unknown \(y\).

5.8.4 Solutions in pieces (rešitve po kosih)

Theorem 5.45 requires a continuous right side. If a point force acts on the beam, the load is not continuous, and lemma 5.12 says that the shear force \(V\) jumps at the force. The equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) holds only on the pieces between the forces. On each piece the load is continuous, so we treat it separately with theorem 5.45, and at the joints we must connect the pieces.

Take the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3, of length \(L = 2\) m, with a vertical force \(F = 1\) kN downward at mid-span \(x_0 = L/2\) (figure 5.51). We neglect the self weight. We divide the beam into the left piece \([0, L/2]\) and the right piece \([L/2, L]\). On each \(q = 0\) and \(v'''' = 0\), so the deflection on each piece is a polynomial of the third degree: \(v_1 = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3\) on the left and \(v_2 = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2 + b_3 x^3\) on the right. That is eight constants and we need eight conditions. Four are boundary conditions and four connect the pieces at the force. The deflection \(v\) and the slope \(v'\) are continuous at the force, because otherwise the axis of the beam would break or kink. By lemma 5.12, for a force alone, without a couple, the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) is continuous and the shear force jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\). The external force is \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N, so it jumps by \(+1000\) N.

We write the conditions and solve them step by step.

Derivation 5.54 Deflection of a simply supported beam with a central force (poves prostoležečega nosilca s silo na sredini)

A simply supported beam of length \(L\) with constant \(EI\), force \(F\) downward at mid-span \(x_0 = L/2\). We look for \(v_1\) on \([0, L/2]\) and \(v_2\) on \([L/2, L]\).

  1. Conditions at the supports: \(v_1(0) = 0\) and \(v_1''(0) = 0\) (support, moment zero), \(v_2(L) = 0\) and \(v_2''(L) = 0\). Conditions at the force at \(x = L/2\): \(v_1 = v_2\), \(v_1' = v_2'\), \(v_1'' = v_2''\) and \(EI\,(v_2''' - v_1''') = -F\). The last condition follows from the jump of the shear force: \(V = -EI\,v'''\) jumps by \(+F\), so \(v'''\) changes by \(-F/EI\).
  2. On the left piece, \(v_1(0) = 0\) and \(v_1''(0) = 0\) give \(a_0 = 0\) and \(a_2 = 0\), so \(v_1 = a_1 x + a_3 x^3\).
  3. On the right piece we introduce \(s = L - x\), the distance from the right end. A polynomial of the third degree in \(x\) is a polynomial of the third degree in \(s\), the second derivative with respect to \(s\) equals the second derivative with respect to \(x\), and the first and third have the opposite sign. From the conditions at \(s = 0\) it follows, as in step 2, that \(v_2 = b_1 s + b_3 s^3\).
  4. Moment: \(v_1'' = 6a_3 x\) and \(v_2'' = 6b_3 s\) are equal at \(x = s = L/2\), so \(a_3 = b_3\).
  5. Force: \(v_1''' = 6a_3\) and \(v_2''' = -6b_3\) (step 3). The condition \(EI\,(v_2''' - v_1''') = -F\) gives \(-12\,EI\,a_3 = -F\), so \(a_3 = b_3 = F/(12EI)\).
  6. Deflection: \(a_1 L/2 + a_3 L^3/8 = b_1 L/2 + b_3 L^3/8\), so with \(a_3 = b_3\) we get \(a_1 = b_1\).
  7. Slope: \(v_1' = a_1 + 3a_3 x^2\) and \(v_2' = -(b_1 + 3b_3 s^2)\) (the derivative with respect to \(x\) is minus the derivative with respect to \(s\)). At \(x = L/2\) they are equal: \(a_1 + 3a_3 L^2/4 = -a_1 - 3a_3 L^2/4\), so \(a_1 = -\tfrac{3}{4}\,a_3 L^2 = -F L^2/(16\,EI)\). Both sides are then zero: \(v_1'(L/2) = 0\).
  8. The deflection of the left piece is \(v_1(x) = -\dfrac{F L^2}{16\,EI}\,x + \dfrac{F}{12\,EI}\,x^3 = \dfrac{F\,x\,(4x^2 - 3L^2)}{48\,EI}\), and of the right piece \(v_2(x) = v_1(L - x)\). At mid-span \(v(L/2) = -\dfrac{F L^3}{48\,EI}\).

The solution is unique. Without the force (\(F = 0\)) step 5 would give \(a_3 = b_3 = 0\) and step 7 would give \(a_1 = b_1 = 0\), so \(v = 0\). The difference of two solutions with the same force solves exactly this homogeneous problem.

Let us check with numbers. For \(F = 1000\) N, \(L = 2\) m and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² we have \(a_3 = F/(12EI) = 3{,}986 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-2}\) and \(a_1 = -F L^2/(16EI) = -0{,}011958\) (the slope at the left support, a number without unit). The deflection at mid-span is \(v(L/2) = a_1 L/2 + a_3 L^3/8 = -0{,}011958 + 0{,}003986 = -0{,}007972\) m, that is, \(-7{,}972\) mm. The shear force is \(V = -EI\,v_1''' = -6EI\,a_3 = -F/2 = -500\) N on the left piece and \(+500\) N on the right, and the moment at mid-span is \(M = EI\,v_1''(L/2) = 6EI\,a_3\,(L/2) = F L/4 = 500\) N m. All these are values from section 5.1.3.

The deflection at mid-span equals the deflection of a cantilever of length \(1\) m with a force of \(500\) N at its end, \(-7{,}972\) mm. The reason is symmetry: by step 7 the cross-section at mid-span does not rotate, so each half of the beam acts as a cantilever clamped at mid-span, of length \(L/2\) and loaded at its end by the force \(F/2\) (the force \(F\) divides between the two halves, each support carries \(F/2\)). The deflection of such a cantilever is \(-(F/2)(L/2)^3/(3EI) = -F L^3/(48EI)\), which is the same number.

With more point forces we proceed in the same way. With \(m\) forces there are \(m + 1\) pieces, \(4(m + 1)\) constants and \(4 + 4m\) conditions: four boundary conditions and four at each point force. In section 5.9 we will solve beams by integration, first without point forces, then with the force at mid-span in pieces as here.
