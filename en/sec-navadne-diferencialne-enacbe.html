Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.8 Mathematics: ordinary differential equations (matematika: navadne diferencialne enačbe)

For the hanging bar we started from the known displacement \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), obtained the strain, the steepness of the graph, by differentiation and the force by equilibrium. The way led from a function to its derivatives. For the beam the situation is reversed. We know the load, and the deflection we look for is a function about which we only know how its fourth derivative is related to the load (theorem 5.42). What is such an equation, when does it have exactly one solution, and how do we find it when only the fourth derivative is given? We answer these questions in this section. First we name the equations whose unknown is a function. Then we prove that for our case we can solve them to the end, and count how many conditions we need so that the solution is no longer one of many but a single one. At the end we show what to do when a point force acts on the beam.

5.8.1 An equation for an unknown function (enačba za neznano funkcijo)

In chapter 1 we knew the solution and only checked it. Take again the steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight, with the axis \(x\) vertical, pointing down from the fixed top. By lemma 4.75 on the equilibrium of a bar, \(N' + q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), where \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g A\) is the weight per unit length, and by Hooke's law \(N = E A\,u'\). The bar is made of one steel and has the same cross section everywhere, so \(E A\) is constant, and from \((E A\,u')' = -\rho g A\) it follows that

\[E\,u''(x) = -\rho g . \tag{5.43}\]

This is the equation for the displacement \(u\). The input is the position \(x\) on the bar, the output the displacement of the point, a number in metres. The equation does not say how large \(u(x)\) is. It only says that the second derivative of \(u\) equals the same number at every point, \(-\rho g/E = -3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The second derivative is the change of the steepness per unit length. On the graph of the displacement the steepness falls from \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the top to \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the middle, so by \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) over \(5\) m, which is \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\) per metre. The expression from chapter 1, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), satisfies the equation, since \(u'' = -2a = -3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\). The question is whether it is the only function that does.

The beam is the same. The steel beam of section 5.1.3, 20 mm wide and 40 mm high, has \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² and self weight \(q = -61{,}21\) N/m (downward, the axis \(y\) points up). By theorem 5.42 we have

\[EI\,v''''(x) = q(x) , \qquad v''''(x) = -2{,}928 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-3} . \tag{5.44}\]

The unknown is the deflection \(v\), a function with input \(x\) and output numbers in metres, and the equation relates its fourth derivative to a given number on the right. Both equations have the same form: an unknown function of one variable \(x\) and its derivatives appear in them. The exact name and the definition are below. The equilibrium equations of chapter 2, for example \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), contain partial derivatives of functions of several variables \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) and are of a different kind, which we do not need here.

Definition 5.45 Ordinary differential equation (navadna diferencialna enačba)

Let \(I\) be an open interval, \(n\) a natural number and \(\Phi\) a function with \(n + 2\) numbers as input and one number as output. An ordinary differential equation (navadna diferencialna enačba) of order \(n\) is an equation

\[\Phi\big(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}\big) = 0 , \tag{5.45}\]

in which the unknown is a function \(y\) of one variable \(x \in I\). The order (red) of the equation is the order of the highest derivative that appears in it. A solution (rešitev) on the interval \(I\) is a function \(y\colon I \to \mathbb{R}\) that is \(n\) times differentiable at every point of \(I\) and for which (5.45) holds at every \(x \in I\) when \(y(x)\) and its derivatives are substituted. The equation is linear (linearna) if it can be written as

\[a_{\mathrm{n}}(x)\,y^{(n)} + a_{\mathrm{n}-1}(x)\,y^{(n-1)} + \dots + a_1(x)\,y' + a_0(x)\,y = f(x)\]

with given functions \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(f\) of the variable \(x\), where \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not zero everywhere (otherwise the order would be lower). If \(f\) is zero everywhere, the equation is homogeneous (homogena).

Source: KREYSZIG §1.1 (PDF p. 29-30) for the ordinary differential equation, order and solution on an open interval, §3.1 (PDF p. 131) for the linear and homogeneous equation of order \(n\).

The adjective ordinary in the definition says that the unknown is a function of one variable. Equations with partial derivatives, such as the equilibrium equations of chapter 2, are called partial differential equations. Equation (5.43) is linear of order 2 (the second derivative is the change of the slope of the graph), with \(a_2 = E\), \(a_1 = a_0 = 0\) and \(f = -\rho g\). Equation (5.44) is linear of order 4 (from load to deflection there are four derivatives). Both are also especially simple, since the unknown \(y\) itself and its lower derivatives do not appear. We can therefore solve them with the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55), which we do in the next subsection.

The equation \(y'' = (y')^2\) has the same order as the linear \(y'' = y'\) (\(a_2 = 1\), \(a_1 = -1\)), but it is not linear. We write it as \(y'' - (y')^2 = 0\), and the coefficient at \(y'\) would be \(-y'\), which is not a given function of \(x\) but depends on the unknown. This is not only a matter of notation. For a linear homogeneous equation twice a solution is again a solution, because we multiply all the terms by \(2\). For \(y'' = y'\) this does hold: \((2y)'' = 2y'' = 2y' = (2y)'\). For our equation it does not.

  1. The function \(y = -\ln(1 - x)\) is defined if \(1 - x > 0\), so on the interval \(x < 1\) (in the figure from \(-1\) to \(0{,}8\)), and it is arbitrarily differentiable there. We have \(y' = 1/(1 - x)\) and \(y'' = 1/(1 - x)^2 = (y')^2\). This is a solution on \(I = (-\infty, 1)\). At \(x = 0\) both sides equal \(1\), the tangent has the slope \(1\) there.
  2. For \(2y\) we have \((2y)'' = 2/(1 - x)^2\) and \(\big((2y)'\big)^2 = 4/(1 - x)^2\). At \(x = 0\) the tangent has the slope \(2\), the left side is \(2\) and the right side \(2^2 = 4\). Since \(1/(1 - x)^2 > 0\) for every \(x < 1\) and \(2 \neq 4\), the two sides differ everywhere. The double \(2y\) is not a solution on any interval, so the equation is not linear. A linear form would have \(f = 0\), because the constant function \(y = 0\) solves \(y'' = (y')^2\), so it would be homogeneous, and twice a solution would again be a solution.

First let us see how many solutions the simplest equation, \(y'' = 2\), has (figure 5.122). The function \(y = x^2\) solves it. So do \(y = x^2 + 1\) and \(y = x^2 + x\), since an added constant term and an added term \(x\) vanish when differentiated twice. The solution is not one but a whole family of parabolas shifted against each other. In the figure three solutions are drawn on the axes \(x\) and \(y\). They share the point \((0, 1)\) but have different slopes in it: \(y'(0) = -2, 0, 2\). The fact that the curve passes through a point is therefore not enough. We need the slope as well. For an equation of the fourth order we will need four data, as we will see in subsection 5.8.3.

5.8.2 A function with a known n-th derivative (funkcija z znanim n-tim odvodom)

How do we find all functions with a given \(n\)-th derivative? For \(n = 1\) the answer is known. If \(y' = f\) with a continuous function \(f\) on \([a, b]\), then by the second part of the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) one solution is the integral \(y_{\mathrm{p}}(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\), and every other differs from it by a constant function by corollary 1.86, since the difference has derivative zero. For \(n = 2\) we proceed twice in a row: from \(y'' = f\) we get that \(y'\) is an integral of \(f\) plus a constant function \(c_1\), and then that \(y\) is an integral of this function plus \(c_0\). Each integration brings one constant. For \(y'' = 2\) we get \(y' = 2x + c_1\) and \(y = x^2 + c_1 x + c_0\), the family of the figure. It is the same for the fourth derivative: four integrations, four constants. Let us write this precisely for an arbitrary order \(n\).

Let \(f\) be a continuous function on \([a, b]\). We define the repeated integral (ponavljani integral) step by step:

\[I_0 = f , \qquad I_{\mathrm{k}}(x) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^x I_{\mathrm{k}-1}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \quad (k = 1, \dots, n) . \tag{5.46}\]

The function \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(k \ge 1\) is \(f\) integrated \(k\) times in a row with the start at \(a\). On the chain figure each \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a box, and integration is the arrow from the box \(I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) to the box \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\). For \(f = 1\), \(I_1(x)\) is the area of the rectangle under \(f\) from \(a\) to \(x\), that is \(x - a\), and the graph of \(I_1\) is a straight line. \(I_2(x)\) is the area of the triangle under this line, \((x - a)\cdot(x - a)/2 = (x - a)^2/2\), and this is the graph of \(I_2\). Every graph starts with zero at \(a\), \(I_{\mathrm{k}}(a) = 0\), since the area from \(a\) to \(a\) has no width. The same zeros are written below the boxes of the chain.

Theorem 5.46 A function with a known n-th derivative (funkcija z znanim n-tim odvodom)

Let \(n\) be a natural number, \(a < b\) and \(f\) a continuous function on \([a, b]\), and let the functions \(I_0, \dots, I_{\mathrm{n}}\) be as in (5.46). A function \(y\colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}\) that is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with its derivatives \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) has the \(n\)-th derivative \(y^{(n)}(x) = f(x)\) at every point of the interval \((a, b)\) if and only if

\[y(x) = I_{\mathrm{n}}(x) + c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}-1} x^{n-1} \tag{5.47}\]

for some numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) (a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), definition 1.64). In the figure for \(n = 2\) and \(f = 1\): the graph of \(I_2\) is one solution, all others are shifted or tilted copies, since the polynomial \(c_0 + c_1 x\) adds a constant shift and a slope.

Proof

We must show two directions. First we show that every function of the form (5.47) satisfies the conditions, then by induction on \(n\) that there are no others. For the second direction the key is that two functions with the same slope have the same gap everywhere.

1. Functions of the form (5.47) are solutions.

  1. The function \(I_0 = f\) is continuous. Let \(k \ge 1\) and let \(I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) be continuous on \([a, b]\). By part 2 of theorem 3.55, \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\) is differentiable on \([a, b]\) with \(I_{\mathrm{k}}' = I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) (when we move \(x\) a little to the right, the area grows by a narrow strip of height \(I_{\mathrm{k}-1}(x)\)), and so continuous by lemma 1.80. By induction (theorem 1.45) each of the functions \(I_0, \dots, I_{\mathrm{n}}\) is therefore continuous and \(I_{\mathrm{k}}' = I_{\mathrm{k}-1}\).
  2. Hence for \(m = 0, \dots, n\) we get \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)} = I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}\): every differentiation undoes one integration, as the steepness of the parabola \(I_2\) is the straight line \(I_1\) and the steepness of the line \(I_1\) is the constant \(f = 1\). For \(m \le n - 1\) these derivatives are continuous on \([a, b]\), and \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(n)} = I_0 = f\).
  3. For the polynomial \(p(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}-1} x^{n-1}\), by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, parts 1, 2, 4 and 5) every derivative \(p^{(m)}\) is again a polynomial, so continuous. The term \(c_{\mathrm{k}} x^k\) with \(k \le n - 1\) becomes constant after \(k\) differentiations and zero after \(k + 1\), so \(p^{(n)} = 0\).
  4. By steps 2 and 3 the function \(y = I_{\mathrm{n}} + p\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with its derivatives up to order \(n - 1\), and \(y^{(n)} = f + 0 = f\) (lemma 1.82, part 1).

2. There are no other solutions.

  1. Let \(y\) be a function with the properties of the theorem and \(z = y - I_{\mathrm{n}}\). By step 2, \(z, z', \dots, z^{(n-1)}\) are continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(z^{(n)} = f - f = 0\) on \((a, b)\). We must show that \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\).
  2. We prove this by induction on the number \(m\) of derivatives. Let the claim \(P(m)\) say: every function \(w\) that is continuous on \([a, b]\) together with the derivatives \(w', \dots, w^{(m-1)}\) and has \(w^{(m)} = 0\) on \((a, b)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\).
  3. Base case: \(P(1)\). The function \(w\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(w' = 0\) on \((a, b)\), so it is constant by corollary 1.86, that is, a polynomial of degree \(0\). Its graph is horizontal, as the graph of \(w' = d_0\) at the bottom left is horizontal.
  4. Induction step: let \(P(m)\) hold and let \(w\) be a function from the claim \(P(m + 1)\), so \(w, \dots, w^{(m)}\) are continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(w^{(m+1)} = 0\) on \((a, b)\). The function \(w'\) is then continuous together with its derivatives up to order \(m - 1\) and \((w')^{(m)} = 0\). By \(P(m)\), \(w'(x) = d_0 + d_1 x + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^{m-1}\). For \(m = 1\) this is the constant function \(w' = d_0\).
  5. Let \(g(x) = w(x) - \big(d_0 x + d_1 x^2/2 + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^m/m\big)\). By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, parts 1, 2 and 5), \(g'(x) = w'(x) - (d_0 + d_1 x + \dots + d_{\mathrm{m}-1} x^{m-1}) = 0\) on \((a, b)\), and \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), since \(w\) and the polynomial are. By corollary 1.86 \(g\) is constant, and \(w\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m\). For \(m = 1\), \(w\) and \(d_0 x\) have the same slope \(d_0\) everywhere, so the difference \(g\) between them is the same everywhere.
  6. By the principle of induction (theorem 1.45) \(P(m)\) holds for every \(m\). With \(m = n\) and \(w = z\), \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), so \(y = I_{\mathrm{n}} + z\) has the form (5.47).

Source: The proof is not from a source. A special case of the general theory of linear equations of order \(n\) is in KREYSZIG §3.1 (PDF p. 131-134), but here it is proved directly with the fundamental theorem of calculus.

We proved the theorem back to the fundamental theorem of calculus, the corollary on the zero derivative and the principle of induction. It showed that the \(n\)-th derivative leaves exactly \(n\) free numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_{\mathrm{n}-1}\): the repeated integral \(I_{\mathrm{n}}\) gives one solution, and all the others differ from it by a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), as the parabolas of figure 5.122 differ by \(c_1 x + c_0\). Different numbers give different solutions: if \(c_0 + c_1 x + \dots + c_{\mathrm{n}-1} x^{n-1} = 0\) on \([a, b]\), the \((n-1)\)-th derivative gives \((n-1)!\,c_{\mathrm{n}-1} = 0\), then the \((n-2)\)-th gives \(c_{\mathrm{n}-2} = 0\), and so on down to \(c_0 = 0\).

Let us try it on the hanging bar with the axis \(x\) downward from the top. Equation (5.43) is \(u'' = -2a\) with \(2a = \rho g/E\). Here \(n = 2\), \(f = -2a\) and with the start of the interval at \(x = 0\) we have \(I_2(x) = -a x^2\), so all the solutions are

\[u(x) = -a x^2 + c_0 + c_1 x .\]

The displacement from chapter 1 has \(c_0 = 0\) and \(c_1 = 2aL\), that is, \(u = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The two numbers are fixed by the ends. The top is fixed, \(u(0) = 0\), so \(c_0 = 0\). The lower end is free, \(N(L) = EA\,u'(L) = 0\), so \(u'(L) = -2aL + c_1 = 0\). Other numbers give other solutions of the equation and each belongs to a bar supported in another way. The same bar standing on the floor has a free top, \(N(0) = 0\) and so \(c_1 = 0\), and its lower end on the floor, \(u(L) = 0\) and so \(c_0 = aL^2\). Its displacement is \(u = a\,(L^2 - x^2)\), on the graph with the axis \(x\) downward as for the bars. The hanging bar gets longer and its lower end descends by \(aL^2 = 0{,}0195\) mm. The standing bar gets shorter and its top descends by the same distance, since in both bars the cross section at the distance \(s\) from the free end carries the weight of \(s\) metres of the bar. The equation is the same for both bars, and the displacements differ by \(aL^2 - 2aL\,x\), a polynomial of degree 1. For the beam with self weight, in equation (5.44) \(f = k := -2{,}928 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-3}\) and \(n = 4\). The fourfold integral with the start at \(x = 0\) is \(I_4(x) = k\,x^4/24\), so the general deflection is

\[v(x) = \frac{k}{24}\,x^4 + c_3 x^3 + c_2 x^2 + c_1 x + c_0 , \qquad \frac{k}{24} = -1{,}220 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^{-3} .\]

Here \(c_0, \dots, c_3\) are four arbitrary numbers. When we fix them, the deflection, the slope, the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) and the shear force \(V = -EI\,v'''\) are fixed as well. What fixes the four numbers is the subject of the next subsection.

5.8.3 Initial and boundary value problems (začetni in robni problem)

The solution of equation (5.33) is not one but a four-parameter family. For the beam, however, we look for one deflection, the one the beam gets with the given supports and load. The supports therefore impose conditions that select one function from the family. The conditions can be of two kinds, as figure 5.126 shows. On the left (initial value problem): on the axis of the beam between \(0\) and \(L\) all four data are given at the left end. On the right (boundary value problem): on the axis there are two data at the left end and two at the right end.

Definition 5.47 Initial value problem (začetni problem)

Let an ordinary differential equation of order \(n\) (definition 5.45) be given on an interval \(I\), a point \(x_0 \in I\) and numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\). The initial value problem (začetni problem) is the task of finding a solution \(y\) of the equation on \(I\) that satisfies the initial conditions (začetni pogoji)

\[y(x_0) = y_0 , \quad y'(x_0) = y_1 , \quad \dots , \quad y^{(n-1)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{n}-1} . \tag{5.48}\]

There are as many conditions as the order of the equation, and all are given at the same point \(x_0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §1.1 (PDF p. 32) for order 1, §3.1 (PDF p. 133) for order \(n\).

Definition 5.48 Boundary value problem (robni problem)

Let an ordinary differential equation of order \(n\) be given on a closed interval \([a, b]\). The boundary value problem (robni problem) is the task of finding a solution of the equation that satisfies \(n\) boundary conditions (robni pogoji). Each condition connects values of the solution and its derivatives at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\). A condition is linear if it has the form \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{n-1} \big(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\,y^{(k)}(a) + \beta_{\mathrm{k}}\,y^{(k)}(b)\big) = \gamma\) with given numbers \(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\gamma\).

Source: CM (7.3.33), (7.3.34) (PDF p. 310) for the boundary conditions of a beam, KREYSZIG §3.1 (PDF p. 133) for initial conditions as opposed to boundary conditions.

For a beam the conditions of section 5.7.2 are linear boundary conditions: each contains \(v\), \(v'\), \(M = EI\,v''\) or \(V = -EI\,v'''\) at one end, and at each end two are prescribed, one from the pair \((v, V)\) and one from the pair \((v', M)\). Does the boundary value problem really differ so much from the initial one? Let us look at the cantilever of section 5.1.3 (figure 5.127).

The cantilever is clamped in a wall at \(x = 0\) and is \(L = 1\) m long. We draw its axis before loading grey and dashed. At the free end \(x = L\) a force of \(500\) N pulls it down. We neglect the self weight, so \(v'''' = 0\). We may do so because the weight is small: the beam weighs \(61{,}21\) N per metre, so \(61{,}21\) N in all, which is about one eighth of the force at the end. Since the weight acts along the whole length, on average at the middle and not at the end, it increases the drop of the end by only \(0{,}366\) mm, that is by \(4{,}6\ \%\) (the computation is in section 5.9.2). For a long slender beam with a small force at the end the weight would be comparable to the force and we could not neglect it.

The initial value problem at the clamp asks the following. We know \(v(0) = 0\) and \(v'(0) = 0\) (the clamp, filled dots) and some numbers \(M(0)\) and \(V(0)\) (empty dots). What is the deflection? By theorem 5.46 with \(f = 0\) we have \(I_4 = 0\) and the solutions are polynomials of degree at most \(3\). From \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\) it follows that \(c_0 = c_1 = 0\). From \(M(0) = EI\,v''(0) = 2EI\,c_2\) and \(V(0) = -EI\,v'''(0) = -6EI\,c_3\) we get

\[v(x) = \frac{1}{EI}\Big(\frac{M(0)}{2}\,x^2 - \frac{V(0)}{6}\,x^3\Big) , \qquad M(x) = M(0) - V(0)\,x , \qquad V(x) = V(0) .\]

For every pair \(\big(M(0), V(0)\big)\) this is exactly one solution, and every pair gives another curve. We, however, do not know these two numbers, since they are the clamping moment and the reaction that the clamp creates only now. We do know the conditions at the free end: \(M(L) = 0\) and \(V(L) = F_{\mathrm{y}} = -500\) N, because by equation (5.39) \(V(L)\) equals the external force acting on the right end, positive upward. From \(V(L) = V(0)\) we get \(V(0) = -500\) N, and from \(M(L) = M(0) - V(0)\,L = 0\) we get \(M(0) = V(0)\,L = -500\) N m. This is the same \(M(0) = -F L = -500\) N m as in equation (5.6), and the reaction couple at the clamp in section 5.1.3 is its opposite, \(+500\) N m. The boundary value problem is therefore an initial value problem with two unknown data, which the conditions at the other end determine: out of all the curves leaving the clamp they select exactly one. The deflection at the free end is \(v(L) = \big(M(0)\,L^2/2 - V(0)\,L^3/6\big)/EI = (-250 + 83{,}33)\ \text{N m}^3 / 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2 = -7{,}972\) mm, which we will also obtain in section 5.9.2.

Does the boundary value problem have exactly one solution for every beam? For a beam with constant \(EI\) and a continuous load we reduce it to two equations in two unknowns. By theorem 5.46 every solution is

\[v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3 , \qquad v_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{I_4}{EI} ,\]

where \(I_4\) is the repeated integral of the load \(q\) with the start at \(x = 0\). By step 2 of the proof of this theorem, \(I_4^{(m)} = I_{4-\mathrm{m}}\), and the functions \(I_1, \dots, I_4\) are zero at \(x = 0\). Therefore \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and its first three derivatives vanish at \(x = 0\) and

\[v(0) = c_0 , \qquad v'(0) = c_1 , \qquad M(0) = 2EI\,c_2 , \qquad V(0) = -6EI\,c_3 . \tag{5.49}\]

The four data at the clamp of the cantilever are therefore exactly the four constants. By section 5.7.2 each end of the beam has one condition from the pair \((v, V)\) and one from the pair \((v', M)\). The four kinds of ends that we need are in figure 5.128.

At each end we draw the axis before loading grey and dashed and the axis after it orange, and the prescribed values are zero. What is prescribed:

  • clamp (vpetje): \(v\) and \(v'\) prescribed, the end stays in place and the axis leaves it horizontally,
  • pin support (nepomična členkasta podpora) or roller support (pomična členkasta podpora): \(v\) and \(M\) prescribed, the end stays in place but rotates,
  • free end (prost konec): \(V\) and \(M\) prescribed, the end moves and rotates,
  • sliding clamp (drsno vpetje): \(v'\) and \(V\) prescribed, the end moves but does not rotate.

In this section "pin" stands for the pin support and the roller support together, since both prescribe the same, \(v = 0\) and \(M = 0\). We met the first three kinds in section 5.7.2. The fourth is new and is not among the supports of definition 5.5, so we define it here. A sliding clamp is an end of the beam at which the slope \(v'\) and the shear force \(V\) are prescribed, usually \(v' = 0\) (the cross-section does not rotate) and \(V\) determined by the force acting on the end (at the right end \(V(L)\) is this force, at the left end \(V(0)\) is its opposite, section 5.7.2): the end moves freely across the axis but does not rotate. Like every support it is an idealization. Its picture is a collar on the end of the beam that slides along a smooth vertical guide. The two rollers between the collar and the guide mean that the collar slides along the guide without friction. A real guide has friction and play, so the end would rotate a little and the shear force would not be prescribed exactly.

The prescribed values can be arbitrary, for example a support can settle. If the left end (\(x = 0\)) is a clamp, equation (5.49) gives \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) directly. For a pin support we get \(c_0\) and \(c_2\), for a free end \(c_2\) and \(c_3\), and for a sliding clamp \(c_1\) and \(c_3\). Two constants are therefore fixed, and the other two, which we call \(s\) and \(t\), remain unknown. The two conditions at the right end are linear in \(s\) and \(t\), so we get two equations in two unknowns. First let us say when such a system has exactly one solution. Each of the two equations is a line in the plane with the coordinates \(s\) and \(t\), and the solution is a point that lies on both (figure 5.129).

The equation \(a s + b t = r_1\) is a line: all points \((s, t)\) that satisfy it. The row of coefficients \((a, b)\) is an arrow perpendicular to the line. For if we move along the line by \((\Delta s, \Delta t)\), the value \(a s + b t\) does not change, so \(a\,\Delta s + b\,\Delta t = 0\). The scalar product of \((a, b)\) with the move is zero, so the arrow \((a, b)\) is perpendicular to the line (theorem 2.10). The solution of the system is the common point of the two lines. If the rows are not parallel, the lines meet in exactly one point. If they are parallel, \((c, d) = \lambda\,(a, b)\), which is just \(D = ad - bc = 0\), the lines are parallel too. Then they have no common point or they are the same line, and every point of it is a solution. The lemma says the same by a computation that also holds when both rows are zero and there are no lines.

Lemma 5.49 A system of two linear equations in two unknowns (sistem dveh linearnih enačb z dvema neznankama)

Let \(a, b, c, d, r_1, r_2\) be real numbers and \(D = ad - bc\). The system

\[a s + b t = r_1 , \qquad c s + d t = r_2 \tag{5.50}\]

has exactly one solution for every pair \((r_1, r_2)\) if \(D \neq 0\). If \(D = 0\), the homogeneous system (\(r_1 = r_2 = 0\)) has a solution \((s, t) \neq (0, 0)\), the direction of coinciding lines, so for no right side does the system have exactly one solution: it has either none or infinitely many. If \(D = 0\), the system is solvable exactly when \(\mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2 = 0\) for every pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2)\) with \(\mu_1\,(a, b) + \mu_2\,(c, d) = (0, 0)\), that is, for which the rows of coefficients, multiplied by \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\), add up to zero.

Proof

We must show that for \(D \neq 0\) there is exactly one solution, that for \(D = 0\) there is a nonzero solution of the homogeneous system, and that for \(D = 0\) the condition with \(\mu_1, \mu_2\) is necessary and sufficient.

  1. Let \(D \neq 0\) and let \((s, t)\) be a solution. We multiply the first equation by \(d\), the second by \(b\) and subtract: \((ad - bc)\,s = d r_1 - b r_2\). We multiply the first equation by \(c\), the second by \(a\) and subtract: \((bc - ad)\,t = c r_1 - a r_2\). The solution can therefore only be \(s = (d r_1 - b r_2)/D\), \(t = (a r_2 - c r_1)/D\), one single point.
  2. We substitute these numbers. The left side of the first equation is \(\big(a d r_1 - a b r_2 + a b r_2 - b c r_1\big)/D = r_1\) and the left side of the second is \(\big(c d r_1 - b c r_2 + a d r_2 - c d r_1\big)/D = r_2\). The numbers are therefore a solution, and the only one.
  3. Let \(D = 0\). If \((a, b) \neq (0, 0)\), we take \((s, t) = (b, -a) \neq (0, 0)\). The first equation gives \(ab - ba = 0\) and the second \(cb - da = -D = 0\). If \(a = b = 0\) and \((c, d) \neq (0, 0)\), we take \((s, t) = (d, -c)\), which gives \(0\) and \(cd - dc = 0\). If all four coefficients are zero, we take \((1, 0)\). The direction \((b, -a)\) is the direction of the line, since it is perpendicular to the row \((a, b)\).
  4. Let the system with \(D = 0\) have a solution \((s_0, t_0)\) and let \((s_1, t_1) \neq (0, 0)\) be a solution of the homogeneous system. Then by step 3 and linearity \((s_0 + \lambda s_1,\, t_0 + \lambda t_1)\) is a solution for every \(\lambda\), and different \(\lambda\) give different solutions. There are infinitely many solutions, all the points of the line.
  5. Necessity for \(D = 0\): let \((s, t)\) be a solution and \(\mu_1(a, b) + \mu_2(c, d) = (0, 0)\). We multiply the first equation by \(\mu_1\), the second by \(\mu_2\) and add: \((\mu_1 a + \mu_2 c)\,s + (\mu_1 b + \mu_2 d)\,t = \mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2\). The left side is zero, so \(\mu_1 r_1 + \mu_2 r_2 = 0\). Otherwise the lines would be parallel and different.
  6. Sufficiency for \(D = 0\): let the condition hold for every such pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2)\). If \((a, b) \neq (0, 0)\), there is a \(\lambda\) with \((c, d) = \lambda\,(a, b)\): for \(a \neq 0\) we have \(\lambda = c/a\) and \(d = bc/a = \lambda b\) from \(ad = bc\), for \(a = 0\) we have \(b \neq 0\), \(D = -bc = 0\) gives \(c = 0\) and \(\lambda = d/b\). The pair \((\mu_1, \mu_2) = (\lambda, -1)\) has \(\lambda\,(a, b) - (c, d) = (0, 0)\), so \(r_2 = \lambda r_1\). We take \((s, t) = r_1\,(a, b)/(a^2 + b^2)\), which gives \(a s + b t = r_1\) and \(c s + d t = \lambda\,(a s + b t) = \lambda r_1 = r_2\). The second equation is then the first multiplied by \(\lambda\), and the lines are the same. If \((a, b) = (0, 0)\) and \((c, d) \neq (0, 0)\), the pair \((1, 0)\) has the property, so \(r_1 = 0\), and with \((s, t) = r_2\,(c, d)/(c^2 + d^2)\) the second equation holds and the first reads \(0 = r_1\). If all coefficients are zero, every pair has the property, so \(r_1 = r_2 = 0\) and every \((s, t)\) solves the system.

Theorem 5.50 Uniqueness for the beam boundary value problem (enoličnost robnega problema nosilca)

Let \(EI\) be constant, \(q\) continuous on \([0, L]\), and at each end of the beam let two conditions of one of the four kinds (clamp, pin support, free end, sliding clamp) be prescribed with arbitrary values. Let \(D = \det B\) for the matrix \(B\) as above. Then:

  1. If \(D \neq 0\), the boundary value problem for the equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) has exactly one solution for every load and every prescribed values, one solution.
  2. If \(D = 0\), there is no load and no values for which it has exactly one solution: it has either none or infinitely many.
  3. The determinant \(D\) is in the table below. It is zero exactly for the six combinations free–free, free–pin, pin–free, free–sliding, sliding–free and sliding–sliding. These are exactly the combinations for which some function \(w = a + b x \neq 0\) satisfies the homogeneous conditions (values zero) of both ends.
Proof

We must show that the boundary value problem reduces to the system (5.50), that the lemma on it gives parts 1 and 2, and that \(D\) is zero exactly in the listed six combinations.

  1. By theorem 5.46 every solution of the equation has the form \(v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3\) and every such function is a solution. The function \(v\) is therefore a solution of the boundary value problem if and only if the numbers \(c_0, \dots, c_3\) satisfy the four conditions.
  2. By equation (5.49) the two conditions of the left end contain only two constants, which each kind of end fixes uniquely from the prescribed values (clamp \(c_0, c_1\), pin \(c_0, c_2\), free end \(c_2, c_3\), sliding clamp \(c_1, c_3\)). What remain are \(s\) and \(t\), which the conditions of the left end do not contain.
  3. The left side of each condition at the right end is linear in \(v\). For \(v = v_{\mathrm{p}} + (\text{known part}) + s\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) we write it as (value on \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the known part) \(+ B_{\mathrm{j}1} s + B_{\mathrm{j}2} t\). Condition \(j\) is therefore \(B_{\mathrm{j}1} s + B_{\mathrm{j}2} t = r_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(r_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the prescribed value, reduced by the value on \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the known part, and divided by \(EI\) for \(M\) and \(V\). This is the system (5.50), two lines in the plane \((s, t)\).
  4. If \(D \neq 0\), then by lemma 5.49 the system has exactly one solution \((s, t)\) for every \((r_1, r_2)\), and the constants of the left end are fixed in step 2. The lines meet in one point. The solution of the boundary value problem is therefore unique, which is part 1.
  5. If \(D = 0\), let \((s_1, t_1) \neq (0, 0)\) be a solution of the homogeneous system from the lemma. The conditions of the left end do not contain \(s\) and \(t\) (step 2), and the conditions of the right end change under the added function \(\psi = s_1 \varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t_1 \varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) by \(B\,(s_1, t_1)^{\mathsf T} = \vek 0\). If \(v\) is a solution of the boundary value problem, then \(v + \lambda\psi\) is a solution for every \(\lambda\), and these are different. There are infinitely many solutions or none. This is part 2.
  6. We computed the determinant \(D\) for the sixteen combinations with SymPy and it is in the table. For a clamp at the left end, which fixes \(c_0\) and \(c_1\), for example, \(s = c_2\), \(t = c_3\), and for a free right end \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -6 \\ 2 & 6L \end{pmatrix}\), which gives \(D = 0 \cdot 6L - (-6) \cdot 2 = 12\).
  7. Let \(w = a + bx \neq 0\) be a function that satisfies the homogeneous conditions of both ends. Then \(w\) and the zero function are two different solutions of the homogeneous boundary value problem (\(q = 0\), all values zero), so by part 1 \(D = 0\). Conversely, for the six combinations with \(D = 0\) such a function exists: free–free \(w = a + bx\), free–pin \(w = b\,(x - L)\), pin–free \(w = b x\), free–sliding, sliding–free and sliding–sliding \(w = a\). A free end satisfies the homogeneous condition for every \(w\) (since \(w'' = w''' = 0\)), a pin requires \(w = 0\) at the pin, and a sliding clamp requires \(w' = 0\). For the remaining ten combinations a clamp requires \(w = w' = 0\) at one point and so \(w = 0\) everywhere, while pin–pin, pin–sliding and sliding–pin give \(a = 0\) and \(b = 0\). Such a function therefore does not exist, and there \(D \neq 0\) indeed, by the table. This is part 3.

Where did the two assumptions enter the theorem and what do they cost? The constant \(EI\) enters in step 1 through theorem 5.46: only then is every solution \(v_{\mathrm{p}}\) plus a cubic polynomial, and only then are the rows of the matrix \(B\) the powers \(x^j\), \(x^k\) and their derivatives at \(x = L\). The force at the end of the cantilever, however, does not spoil the continuity, because it is not a part of the load \(q\) but the prescribed value \(V(L)\).

What does \(D \neq 0\) mean physically? The function \(w = a + bx\) has \(w'' = w''' = 0\), so it creates neither a moment nor a shear force. It is a deflection without load that the supports allow, that is, a small rigid displacement: the distances between points of the axis change only by the factor \(\sqrt{1 + b^2}\), that is, by less than \(b^2/2\) (\(0{,}5\ \%\) for \(|b| \le 0{,}1\)), so only in second order in \(b\), which we neglect (definition 1.11 demands exactly equal distances). Below, "rigid displacement" means this small rigid displacement. By part 3 of the theorem, \(D = 0\) exactly when the supports do not prevent such a displacement. All six such combinations are in figure 5.130.

Two free ends allow a shift and a rotation, since nothing holds the beam. A free end with a pin on the right allows a rotation about the pin, \(w = b\,(x - L)\), which is zero at \(x = L\). A pin on the left with a free end allows a rotation about the left pin, \(w = bx\). A sliding clamp does not let the end rotate but lets it shift across the axis, so the shift is allowed with a free end on the right, with a free end on the left or with a second sliding clamp: in all three cases the beam shifts by \(w = a\). For \(D \neq 0\) the supports prevent every rigid displacement and the solution is unique, as in lemma 4.134 for a body in space. A clamp at one end is enough, as are two pins or a pin with a sliding clamp.

An example without supports is a beam with free ends (figure 5.130). Below it is the axis \(x\) with the end points \(0\) and \(L\). The conditions without external loads at the ends are \(M = V = 0\) at both ends. The left end fixes \(c_2 = c_3 = 0\), and the unknowns are \(s = c_0\) and \(t = c_1\). The functions \(1\) and \(x\) have zero second and third derivatives, so \(B\) is the zero matrix and \(D = 0\):

\[B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad D = 0 .\]

The deflection \(v = c_0 + c_1 x\) is a solution without load: the beam moves up by \(c_0\) and rotates by a small angle \(c_1\), so its right end moves by \(c_0 + c_1 L\).

For a beam without supports a solution does not exist for every load but only for a load in equilibrium. Figure 5.131 shows why.

Let us draw the whole beam as a free body. There are no supports, so only the load \(q\) along the length and the forces and couples at the two ends act on it. We draw everything in the positive direction, forces upward, couples counterclockwise. By the definition of the shear force the external force at the left end is \(-V(0)\) and at the right end \(V(L)\), and the external couple is \(-M(0)\) at the left end and \(M(L)\) at the right end. The beam is at rest, so by Newton's laws the sum of forces and the sum of moments are zero. The sum of vertical forces is \(-V(0) + V(L) + \int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\). The sum of moments about the left end \(x = 0\) is \(-M(0) + M(L) + L\,V(L) + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), where the force \(V(L)\) has the arm \(L\) and the load at the point \(x\) has the arm \(x\). We derive the same two relations directly from the equation.

Derivation 5.51 The load of an unsupported beam must be in equilibrium (obtežba nosilca brez podpor mora biti v ravnovesju)

Let a beam of length \(L\) (figure 5.131) with constant \(EI\) and continuous load \(q\) have prescribed \(M(0)\), \(V(0)\), \(M(L)\) and \(V(L)\), where \(M = EI\,v''\) (theorem 5.35) and \(V = -EI\,v'''\) (by corollary 5.11 from \(M' = -V\)). We show that a solution exists if and only if

\[\begin{aligned} V(0) - V(L) &= \int_0^L q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x , \\ M(0) - M(L) - L\,V(L) &= \int_0^L x\,q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x , \end{aligned} \tag{5.51}\]

and that the solution is then determined up to a rigid displacement \(c_0 + c_1 x\).

  1. Let \(v\) be a solution. By theorem 5.46 \(v'''\) is continuous on \([0, L]\) and \((EI\,v''')' = q\) on \((0, L)\). By part 1 of theorem 3.55, \(\int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\,v'''(L) - EI\,v'''(0) = -V(L) + V(0)\). This is the first relation, the sum of vertical forces.
  2. By the product rule (lemma 1.82, part 3) the function \(x\,EI\,v'''\) has the derivative \(EI\,v''' + x\,q\) on \((0, L)\), and this derivative is continuous on \([0, L]\). By part 1 of theorem 3.55, therefore, \(L\,EI\,v'''(L) = \int_0^L EI\,v'''\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). Since \(EI\,v'''\) is the derivative of the function \(EI\,v''\), the first integral on the right equals \(M(L) - M(0)\) by the same part of the theorem. So \(-L\,V(L) = M(L) - M(0) + \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). This is the second relation, the sum of moments about \(x = 0\).
  3. The relations are therefore necessary. Conversely, let them hold. We take \(v = I_4/EI + c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3\), which by theorem 5.46 is the general solution of the equation. Since \(I_4'' = I_2\), \(I_4''' = I_1\) and \(I_1(0) = I_2(0) = 0\), the conditions at the left end give \(c_2 = M(0)/(2EI)\) and \(c_3 = -V(0)/(6EI)\). The numbers \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) remain arbitrary, which is the rigid displacement.
  4. For the function so chosen steps 1 and 2 hold, because they hold for every solution of the equation. The first gives \(V(L) = V(0) - \int_0^L q\,\mathrm{d}x\) and the second \(M(L) = M(0) - L\,V(L) - \int_0^L x\,q\,\mathrm{d}x\). The relations (5.51) say that these are exactly the prescribed values \(V(L)\) and \(M(L)\). The conditions at the right end are therefore satisfied by themselves, because the equilibrium of the whole already contains them.

Why did the derivation from the equation give exactly the equilibrium of the whole? We derived the equation for the deflection from the equilibrium of a short piece of the beam. When we add the pieces up into the whole beam, the forces and couples between neighbouring pieces cancel, because by Newton's third law they act on the two pieces equal and opposite. Only the external forces remain, so without the equilibrium of the whole the equation cannot have a solution. If the equilibrium holds, there are infinitely many solutions, because the beam is not attached anywhere and we can add a rigid displacement to it.

The same holds for every combination of ends with \(D = 0\), not only for a beam without supports. We obtain the condition for the load by using the allowed rigid displacement \(w = a + bx\) as a weight in the equation. What a weight means is shown in the lower part of figure 5.131. We put the beam before the displacement into a rigid displacement. The point \(x\) where the load acts moves by \(w(x)\), the ends by \(w(0) = a\) and \(w(L)\), and the cross-sections rotate by \(w' = b\). We multiply every force by the displacement of its point, every couple by the rotation, and add everything up. Since \(w = a + bx\), this sum is \(a\) times the sum of forces plus \(b\) times the sum of moments about \(x = 0\), so zero at equilibrium. The lemma says this by a computation that needs no figure.

Let \(EI\) be constant, \(q\) continuous on \([0, L]\) and \(v\) a function whose derivatives \(v, v', v'', v'''\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and for which \(EI\,v'''' = q\) on \((0, L)\) (a solution of the equation, not necessarily of the boundary value problem). Theorem 5.53 Existence and uniqueness up to a rigid displacement (obstoj in enoličnost do togega premika)

Let \(EI\) be constant, \(q\) continuous on \([0, L]\), and at the ends of the beam let two conditions of the four kinds of theorem 5.50 be prescribed with arbitrary values. Let \(D = 0\), that is, let the combination of ends be one of the six in the table, and let \(W\) be the set from the table (the rigid displacements the combination allows). Then:

  1. The boundary value problem has a solution exactly when equation (5.52) holds for every \(w \in W\) with the prescribed values of \(V\) and \(M\) at the ends (for free–free for \(w = 1\) and \(w = x\), for the other five for the one function \(w\) of the table).
  2. If a solution \(v_0\) exists, all solutions are \(v_0 + w\) with \(w \in W\), and different \(w\) give different solutions. The solution is unique up to the allowed rigid displacement.
Proof

We must show that the condition from the lemma on the system of two equations, for the system of the right end, is exactly equation (5.52), that these conditions are given by the functions of \(W\), and that the difference of two solutions lies in \(W\).

  1. By steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 5.50 let \(u_0\) be the function \(v_{\mathrm{p}} + (\text{constants of the left end})\), which solves the equation \(EI\,u'''' = q\) and satisfies the two conditions of the left end with the prescribed values. The functions that do this are exactly \(u(s, t) = u_0 + s\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\). Let \(\rho_1, \rho_2\) be the left sides of the conditions of the right end, divided by \(EI\) for \(M\) and \(V\) (so \(\rho_1(u) = u(L)\) or \(-u'''(L)\), \(\rho_2(u) = u'(L)\) or \(u''(L)\)), \(g_1, g_2\) the prescribed values divided in the same way, and \(\rho(u) = (\rho_1(u), \rho_2(u))\). Since \(\rho\) is linear, \(\rho\big(u(s, t)\big) = \rho(u_0) + B\,(s, t)^{\mathsf T}\), and \(u(s, t)\) solves the boundary value problem exactly when \(B\,(s, t)^{\mathsf T} = \vek r\) with \(\vek r = g - \rho(u_0)\).
  2. By lemma 5.49, for \(D = 0\) this system is solvable exactly when \(\mu\cdot\vek r = 0\) for every pair \(\mu = (\mu_1, \mu_2)\) with \(\mu_1\,(\text{first row of } B) + \mu_2\,(\text{second row of } B) = \vek 0\), that is, with \(\mu B = \vek 0\). Let \(N\) be the set of these pairs. When the two rows are not both zero, this says that the lines of the system coincide and are not parallel and different.
  3. Let \(w \in W\) and \(\kappa\) as in the table. For every \((s, t)\) the function \(u(s, t)\) has all the necessary continuous derivatives (theorem 5.46) and solves the equation, so by lemma 5.52 (the work of the forces on the rigid displacement is zero) \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x - \Lambda_0 + \big(V w + M w'\big)\big|_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}} = 0\), where \(V = -EI\,u'''\) and \(M = EI\,u''\) for \(u = u(s, t)\) and \(\Lambda_0 = \big(V w + M w'\big)\big|_{\mathrm{x} = 0}\).
  4. The term \(\Lambda_0\) at the left end does not depend on \((s, t)\): \(u(s, t)\) satisfies the conditions of the left end, and in each product \(V(0)\,w(0)\), \(M(0)\,w'(0)\) either the first factor is prescribed or the second is zero, because \(w\) satisfies the homogeneous conditions.
  5. The term at the right end is \(EI\,\kappa\cdot\rho(u)\). If \(v\) is prescribed, \(w(L) = 0\) and the term \(V w\) is zero, which is also \(\kappa_1 = 0\). If \(V\) is prescribed, \(V(L) = EI\,\rho_1(u)\) and the term \(V w\) is \(EI\,\kappa_1 \rho_1(u)\) with \(\kappa_1 = w(L)\). The same holds for \(M w'\) and \(\kappa_2\).
  6. By steps 3 to 5 (the work of the forces on a rigid displacement is zero), \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x - \Lambda_0 + EI\,\kappa\cdot\big(\rho(u_0) + B\,(s, t)^{\mathsf T}\big) = 0\) for every \((s, t)\). At \((s, t) = (0, 0)\) we get \(\int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x - \Lambda_0 + EI\,\kappa\cdot\rho(u_0) = 0\). We subtract: \(EI\,\kappa B\,(s, t)^{\mathsf T} = 0\) for every \((s, t)\), so \(\kappa B = \vek 0\) and \(\kappa \in N\).
  7. From step 6, \(\kappa\cdot\rho(u_0) = \big(\Lambda_0 - \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\big)/EI\). The term \(\Lambda_0\) is the left end, the term in \(\rho\) the right end. Therefore \(EI\,\kappa\cdot\vek r = EI\,\kappa\cdot g - EI\,\kappa\cdot\rho(u_0) = EI\,\kappa\cdot g + \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x - \Lambda_0\). Here \(EI\,\kappa\cdot g = \big(V w + M w'\big)\big|_{\mathrm{x} = \mathrm{L}}\) with the prescribed values (step 5 with \(\rho(u)\) replaced by \(g\)), and \(\Lambda_0\) is taken with the prescribed values of the left end, so \(EI\,\kappa\cdot\vek r\) is exactly the left side of equation (5.52) with the prescribed values. We denote it by \(E_{\mathrm{w}}\).
  8. The set \(N\) in the plane \((\mu_1, \mu_2)\) is spanned by the pairs \(\kappa\) of the functions of \(W\). If \(\mu, \mu' \in N\), then also \(\alpha\mu + \beta\mu' \in N\), because \((\alpha\mu + \beta\mu')B = \alpha\,\mu B + \beta\,\mu' B = \vek 0\). Two non-parallel pairs in \(N\) would therefore give all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and if \(N\) is not all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), it is at most a line through \((0, 0)\). For free–free, \(B = 0\), so \(N\) is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and the pairs \(\kappa = (1, 0)\) for \(w = 1\) and \((L, 1)\) for \(w = x\) are independent and span it. For the other five combinations, by the table \(B \neq 0\), so \(N\) is not all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (in it both rows would be zero) and is at most a line. The pair \(\kappa\) of the table is nonzero and by step 6 lies in \(N\), so it spans \(N\).
  9. Part 1: by step 2 the system is solvable exactly when \(\mu\cdot\vek r = 0\) for every \(\mu \in N\), by step 8 exactly when \(\kappa\cdot\vek r = 0\) for the \(\kappa\) of every function \(w \in W\), by step 7 exactly when \(E_{\mathrm{w}} = 0\) for every rigid displacement \(w \in W\) (since \(EI > 0\)). A solvable system means a solution of the boundary value problem by step 1.
  10. Part 2: let \(v_0\) and \(\tilde v\) be two solutions. Their difference \(\psi\) solves the same problem with \(q = 0\) and zero values, has zero constants at the left end and is therefore \(\psi = s\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} + t\varphi_{\mathrm{t}}\) with \(B\,(s, t)^{\mathsf T} = \vek 0\) (step 1 with \(u_0 = 0\)). We find the solutions of this system from the table for each of the six combinations. Free–free: \(B = 0\), every \((s, t)\), \(\psi = s + t x\) \(\in W\). Free–pin: \(s + L t = 0\), so \(\psi = t\,(x - L)\) \(\in W\). Pin–free: \(-6t = 0\), so \(t = 0\) and \(\psi = s x\) \(\in W\). For the remaining three (free–sliding, sliding–free, sliding–sliding) the second row gives \(t = 0\) and \(\psi = s\) \(\in W\). In all six cases \(\psi \in W\). Conversely, for every \(w \in W\) the function \(v_0 + w\) is again a solution, because \(w\) satisfies the homogeneous conditions at both ends and \(w'''' = 0\), and different \(w\) give different functions.

The theorem showed that for \(D = 0\) the equilibrium of the load on every allowed rigid displacement is a necessary and sufficient condition for a solution to exist, and that the solution is then determined up to this displacement. For the beam without supports treated in derivation 5.51 the first row of the table holds.

For the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\) we can say more than for the boundary value problem. The initial value problem always has exactly one solution. Figure 5.133 shows why: each of the \(n\) data at the point \(x_0\) has its own polynomial, which sets it and does not change the other data.

Let us draw all four around the point \(x_0\). The constant \(1\) has the value \(1\) at \(x_0\), and all its derivatives are zero. The line \(x - x_0\) passes through \(x_0\) with the value zero and the slope \(1\). The parabola \((x - x_0)^2/2\) has value and slope zero at \(x_0\) and the second derivative \(1\). The cubic curve \((x - x_0)^3/6\) passes through \(x_0\) horizontally and does not bend there: value, slope and second derivative are zero, and the third derivative is \(1\). The datum \(y_{\mathrm{k}}\) thus multiplies its own polynomial \((x - x_0)^k/k!\) and does not spoil the other data. This is the last sum in the equation of the theorem below.

Theorem 5.54 The initial value problem for the n-th derivative (začetni problem za n-ti odvod)

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\), \(x_0 \in [a, b]\) and \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) arbitrary numbers. The initial value problem (definition 5.47) \(y^{(n)} = f\) with initial conditions \(y^{(k)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 0, \dots, n - 1\) has exactly one solution in the class of functions of theorem 5.46,

\[y(x) = I_{\mathrm{n}}(x) - \sum_{\mathrm{m}=0}^{n-1} I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0)\,\frac{(x - x_0)^m}{m!} + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{n-1} y_{\mathrm{k}}\,\frac{(x - x_0)^k}{k!} . \tag{5.53}\]
Proof

We must show that (5.53) is a solution with the given initial values and that there is no other.

  1. The sums \(\sum_{\mathrm{m}} I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0)(x - x_0)^m/m!\) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} y_{\mathrm{k}} (x - x_0)^k/k!\) are polynomials of degree at most \(n - 1\) (multiply out the brackets). By theorem 5.46 the function (5.53) is therefore a solution of the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\).
  2. By the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.82, parts 1 to 4), by induction on \(k\), \(\big((x - x_0)^k/k!\big)^{(m)} = (x - x_0)^{k-m}/(k-m)!\) for \(k \ge m\) and zero for \(k < m\). At \(x = x_0\) this derivative is \(1\) for \(k = m\) and \(0\) otherwise: each polynomial sets one single datum.
  3. For \(m \le n - 1\) we therefore have \(y^{(m)}(x_0) = I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)}(x_0) - I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}(x_0) + y_{\mathrm{m}}\). By step 2 of the proof of theorem 5.46, \(I_{\mathrm{n}}^{(m)} = I_{\mathrm{n}-\mathrm{m}}\), so \(y^{(m)}(x_0) = y_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  4. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be two solutions. The difference \(z = \tilde y - y\) has \(z^{(n)} = 0\) and \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = 0\) for \(m < n\). By theorem 5.46 with \(f = 0\), \(z\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 1\), which we write in powers of \(x - x_0\) (multiply out the brackets): \(z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}} (x - x_0)^k\), a sum of the polynomials of the figure multiplied by numbers.
  5. By step 2, \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = m!\,d_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(z^{(m)}(x_0) = 0\) gives \(d_{\mathrm{m}} = 0\) for every \(m\): each polynomial would change its own datum, so none may appear. Hence \(z = 0\) and \(\tilde y = y\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. For a general linear equation of order \(n\) with continuous coefficients and a continuous right side the same follows from KREYSZIG §3.1, theorem 2 (PDF p. 134, for the homogeneous equation), together with §3.3 (PDF p. 142, for the right side).

For the beam this means that four data at one endpoint, \(v\), \(v'\), \(M\) and \(V\), determine the deflection, as for the cantilever. A boundary value problem, however, can have one solution, none or infinitely many, as we saw for the three beams and for the beams with \(D = 0\).

For a general equation the picture is similar but harder. For linear equations of order \(n\) with leading coefficient \(1\), continuous coefficients and a continuous right side the initial value problem has exactly one solution on every open interval containing \(x_0\) (KREYSZIG §3.1, theorem 2, PDF p. 134, for the homogeneous equation, and §3.3, PDF p. 142, for the right side). For a nonlinear equation of the first order \(y' = F(x, y)\), Picard's theorem (LEBL1 theorem 6.3.2, PDF p. 247) says that there is an \(h > 0\) and exactly one differentiable function \(y\) on \([x_0 - h, x_0 + h]\) with \(y' = F(x, y)\) and \(y(x_0) = y_0\), if \(F\) is continuous on a closed bounded rectangle with the point \((x_0, y_0)\) in its interior and if for some number \(\Lambda\) we have \(|F(x, y_1) - F(x, y_2)| \le \Lambda\,|y_1 - y_2|\) for all points of the rectangle, that is, \(F\) does not change in the variable \(y\) faster than a straight line of slope \(\Lambda\) (on the middle tile of figure 5.134 the graph lies in a double wedge of lines with slopes \(\Lambda\) and \(-\Lambda\)). We do not prove the first theorem in this book. We prove Picard's theorem in chapter 8 (theorem 8.107) with a somewhat stronger condition: \(F\) and \(\partial F/\partial y\) are continuous, which guarantees the condition with the number \(\Lambda\) on every closed rectangle in the region. We will need both theorems for vibrations, described by a linear equation of the second order (chapter 9), and for the equations of motion (chapter 8).

The condition of continuity and bounded slope is there for a reason. The equation \(y' = 2\sqrt{|y|}\) (left) has two solutions for the initial condition \(y(0) = 0\): \(y_1 = 0\) and \(y_2(x) = x\,|x|\). Indeed \(y_2'(x) = 2|x|\) for every \(x\) and \(2\sqrt{|y_2|} = 2\sqrt{x^2} = 2|x|\). The condition with the number \(\Lambda\) does not hold here (middle), since for \(z_1 = y > 0\) and \(z_2 = 0\) the ratio \(2\sqrt{y}/y = 2/\sqrt{y}\) is unbounded as \(y\) tends to zero (exit from the wedge): the curve \(2\sqrt{y}\) is above every line \(\Lambda y\) near zero, for \(\Lambda = 8\) up to \(y = 1/16\). Even when both conditions hold, the theorem guarantees a solution only on \([x_0 - h, x_0 + h]\). The equation \(y' = y^2\) (right) satisfies both conditions on every bounded rectangle, since \(|y_1^2 - y_2^2| = |y_1 + y_2|\,|y_1 - y_2| \le 2R\,|y_1 - y_2|\) for \(|y_1|, |y_2| \le R\) (wedge on the middle tile), and with \(y(0) = 1\) has the solution \(y = 1/(1 - x)\), which grows without bound as \(x \to 1\) and exists only on the interval \((-\infty, 1)\). A solution therefore does not necessarily exist on the whole interval we are interested in. For the equation \(y^{(n)} = f\) nothing like this happens, because \(f\) does not contain the unknown \(y\).

5.8.4 Solutions in pieces (rešitve po kosih)

Theorem 5.46 requires a continuous right side. If a point force acts on the beam, the load is not continuous, and lemma 5.12 says that the shear force \(V\) jumps at the force. A short piece around the force shows why: however short it is, the force on it stays whole, so the forces of the neighbouring parts must balance it, and the shear force left and right of the force differs by exactly the force. The equation \(EI\,v'''' = q\) holds only on the pieces between the forces. On each piece the load is continuous, so we treat it separately with theorem 5.46, and at the joints we must connect the pieces.

Take the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3, of length \(L = 2\) m, with a vertical force \(F = 1\) kN downward at mid-span \(x_0 = L/2\) (figure 5.136, distances \(L/2\)). We neglect the self weight: in section 5.9 we will compute that by itself it lowers the middle by \(0{,}610\) mm, which is 7,7 % of the deflection due to the force. We divide the beam into the left piece \([0, L/2]\) and the right piece \([L/2, L]\). On each \(q = 0\) and \(v'''' = 0\), so the deflection on each piece is a polynomial of degree at most \(3\): \(v_1 = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3\) on the left and \(v_2 = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2 + b_3 x^3\) on the right. That is eight constants and we need eight conditions. Four are boundary conditions and four connect the pieces at the force. The deflection \(v\) and the slope \(v'\) are continuous at the force by lemma 5.44. If the deflection jumped, the beam would tear under the force. If the slope jumped, the cross sections just left and just right of the force would rotate differently, and the material would open a gap on one side of the axis and overlap on the other. By lemma 5.12, for a force alone, without a couple, the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) is continuous and the shear force jumps by \(-F_{\mathrm{y}}\). The external force is \(F_{\mathrm{y}} = -1000\) N, so it jumps by \(+1000\) N.

We write the conditions and solve them step by step.

Derivation 5.55 Deflection of a simply supported beam with a central force (poves prostoležečega nosilca s silo na sredini)

A simply supported beam of length \(L\) with constant \(EI\), force \(F\) downward at mid-span \(x_0 = L/2\). We look for \(v_1\) on \([0, L/2]\) and \(v_2\) on \([L/2, L]\).

  1. Conditions at the supports: \(v_1(0) = 0\) and \(v_1''(0) = 0\) (support, moment zero), \(v_2(L) = 0\) and \(v_2''(L) = 0\). Conditions at the point of the force at \(x = L/2\): \(v_1 = v_2\), \(v_1' = v_2'\), \(v_1'' = v_2''\) and \(EI\,(v_2''' - v_1''') = -F\). The last condition follows from the jump of the shear force: \(V = -EI\,v'''\) jumps by \(+F\), so \(v'''\) changes by \(-F/EI\).
  2. On the left piece, \(v_1(0) = 0\) and \(v_1''(0) = 0\) give \(a_0 = 0\) and \(a_2 = 0\), so \(v_1 = a_1 x + a_3 x^3\).
  3. On the right piece we introduce \(s = L - x\), the distance from the right end. A polynomial of degree at most \(3\) in \(x\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(3\) in \(s\), the second derivative with respect to \(s\) equals the second derivative with respect to \(x\), and the first and third have the opposite sign. From the conditions at \(s = 0\) it follows, as in step 2, that \(v_2 = d_1 s + d_3 s^3\), where \(d_1\) and \(d_3\) are the coefficients at \(s\) and \(s^3\) (not the same as \(b_1\) and \(b_3\) at \(x\)).
  4. Moment: \(v_1'' = 6a_3 x\) and \(v_2'' = 6d_3 s\) are equal at \(x = s = L/2\), so \(a_3 = d_3\).
  5. Force: \(v_1''' = 6a_3\) and \(v_2''' = -6d_3\) (step 3). The condition \(EI\,(v_2''' - v_1''') = -F\) gives \(-12\,EI\,a_3 = -F\), so \(a_3 = d_3 = F/(12EI)\).
  6. Deflection at the force: \(a_1 L/2 + a_3 L^3/8 = d_1 L/2 + d_3 L^3/8\), so with \(a_3 = d_3\) we get \(a_1 = d_1\).
  7. Slope: \(v_1' = a_1 + 3a_3 x^2\) and \(v_2' = -(d_1 + 3d_3 s^2)\) (the derivative with respect to \(x\) is minus the derivative with respect to \(s\)). At \(x = L/2\) they are equal: \(a_1 + 3a_3 L^2/4 = -a_1 - 3a_3 L^2/4\), so \(a_1 = -\tfrac{3}{4}\,a_3 L^2 = -F L^2/(16\,EI)\). Both sides are then zero: \(v_1'(L/2) = 0\), the horizontal tangent in the figure.
  8. The deflection of the left piece is \(v_1(x) = -\dfrac{F L^2}{16\,EI}\,x + \dfrac{F}{12\,EI}\,x^3 = \dfrac{F\,x\,(4x^2 - 3L^2)}{48\,EI}\), and of the right piece \(v_2(x) = v_1(L - x)\). At mid-span \(v(L/2) = -\dfrac{F L^3}{48\,EI}\).

The solution is unique. Without the force (\(F = 0\)) step 5 would give \(a_3 = d_3 = 0\) and step 7 would give \(a_1 = d_1 = 0\), so \(v = 0\), and the beam would stay straight. The difference of two solutions with the same force solves exactly this problem without the force, because all the conditions are linear: in the subtraction the jump \(-F\) cancels by itself. The difference is therefore zero and the two solutions are equal.

Let us check with numbers. For \(F = 1000\) N, \(L = 2\) m and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² we have \(a_3 = F/(12EI) = 3{,}986 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-2}\) and \(a_1 = -F L^2/(16EI) = -0{,}011958\) (the slope at the left support, a number without unit). The deflection at mid-span is \(v(L/2) = a_1 L/2 + a_3 L^3/8 = -0{,}011958 + 0{,}003986 = -0{,}007972\) m, that is, \(-7{,}972\) mm. The shear force is \(V = -EI\,v_1''' = -6EI\,a_3 = -F/2 = -500\) N on the left piece and \(+500\) N on the right, and the moment at mid-span is \(M = EI\,v_1''(L/2) = 6EI\,a_3\,(L/2) = F L/4 = 500\) N m. The reactions, the shear force and the moment are values from section 5.2.2, and the deflection \(-7{,}972\) mm from section 5.7.2.

The deflection at mid-span equals the deflection of a cantilever of length \(1\) m with a force of \(500\) N at its end, \(-7{,}972\) mm. The reason is symmetry: by step 7 the cross section at mid-span does not rotate, just like the end in a clamp. So the right half acts as a cantilever clamped at mid-span, of length \(L/2\), which the support pushes up at its free end with the force \(F/2\) (the force \(F\) divides between the two supports, each carries \(F/2\)). The same holds for the left half. The end of such a cantilever moves away from the horizontal tangent at the clamp by \((F/2)(L/2)^3/(3EI) = F L^3/(48EI)\). This is the descent of the end of a cantilever of length \(\ell\) with a force \(P\) at its end, \(P\ell^3/(3EI)\), at \(P = F/2\) and \(\ell = L/2\). We obtained it in section 5.8.3 as \(\big(M(0)\,\ell^2/2 - V(0)\,\ell^3/6\big)/EI\) with \(V(0) = -P\) and \(M(0) = V(0)\,\ell = -P\ell\), which gives \((-P\ell^3/2 + P\ell^3/6)/EI = -P\ell^3/(3EI)\). The support is higher than the middle by exactly this much, so \(v(L/2) = -F L^3/(48EI)\).

With more point forces we proceed in the same way. With our one force there are two pieces and eight constants. With \(m\) forces there are \(m + 1\) pieces, \(4(m + 1)\) constants and \(4 + 4m\) conditions: four boundary conditions and four at each point force. In section 5.9 we will solve beams by integration, first without point forces, then the result of this derivation for the force at mid-span.
