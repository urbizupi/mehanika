Chapter 26 Belts as axially moving continua

26.3 Modes of the moving string

A steel band for a band saw, 50 mm wide and 0,9 mm thick, runs at 40 m/s through two fixed eyelets (očesci) 1 m apart and is pulled with a stress of 100 MPa. These dimensions are our choice, not data from a source. We pluck the band. What frequency do we hear, and how does it change when the band runs faster? Do all points of the band oscillate together, as on a string at rest? In section 13.2 (chapter 13) we found for the string at rest the standing wave \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), and the chapter promised that for belts this shape is no longer a solution. Here we prove it and find what replaces it.

The model is the moving string of section 26.1: the deflection \(w(x, t)\) of the point of the string that lies at \(x \in [0, L]\) at time \(t\) is transverse, \(x\) is measured from the first eyelet in the direction of motion, and the transport speed \(v\) is constant and positive. The letter \(v\) here is the transport speed in m/s, and the deflection is \(w\) as in chapter 13 (in the chapters on beams \(v\) is a deflection). The string has tension \(T\) and mass per length \(\mu\) as in definition 13.2, \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) is the wave speed of definition 13.4, and everything below holds for subcritical speed \(0 \le v < c\). The equation of motion of theorem 26.4 and the two ends are

\[w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + (v^2 - c^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0 , \qquad w(0, t) = w(L, t) = 0 . \tag{26.10}\]

The first term is the local acceleration at the place \(x\), the second is the Coriolis term, the third, \((v^2 - c^2)\,w_{\mathrm{xx}}\), is the centripetal acceleration \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) together with the tension force per unit mass \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) moved to the left side. The second is named after Coriolis because it is the acceleration of a body that moves along a path that itself moves: a particle of the string runs at speed \(v\) along a curve whose slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}}\) changes in time, and the transverse velocity \(w_{\mathrm{t}} + v w_{\mathrm{x}}\) of the particle therefore changes also through \(v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\). The part \(v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is centripetal because it is the acceleration of a particle that runs at speed \(v\) along a curve of curvature \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and points to the centre of curvature, as in a car in a bend. Section 26.1 derived both from the chain rule. For \(v = 0\) the wave equation of theorem 13.3 remains.

Where is the difficulty? As for the string at rest, we try a motion in which every point oscillates with the same frequency \(\omega\): \(w = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) with a real function \(\varphi\). The terms \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are then proportional to \(\cos\omega t\), but the mixed derivative \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -\omega\varphi'\sin\omega t\) is proportional to \(\sin\omega t\). The functions \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\sin\omega t\) are not proportional: at \(t = 0\) the first is \(1\) and the second \(0\), at \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the other way round. So the part of the equation with each of them must vanish separately. The following lemma shows this, and it is also the proof that was promised.

Lemma 26.11 A standing wave is not a solution (stoječe valovanje ni rešitev)

Let \(L > 0\), \(c > 0\), \(0 < v < c\) and \(\omega > 0\), and let \(\varphi : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) be twice continuously differentiable. If \(w(x, t) = \varphi(x)\cos\omega t\) solves equation (26.10) for every \(t \ge 0\), then \(\varphi = 0\). In particular \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega t)\) is not a solution for any \(\omega > 0\).

Proof

We must show that the equation forces two different conditions on \(\varphi\) at two times.

  1. For \(w = \varphi\cos\omega t\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = -\omega^2\varphi\cos\omega t\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -\omega\varphi'\sin\omega t\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varphi''\cos\omega t\) (theorem on derivatives of sine and cosine, 8.13).
  2. At \(t = 0\) we have \(\cos 0 = 1\) and \(\sin 0 = 0\), so equation (26.10) gives \(-\omega^2\varphi + (v^2 - c^2)\varphi'' = 0\). This is the instant of largest deflection, when the string is at rest and its slope is not changing.
  3. At \(t = \pi/(2\omega)\) we have \(\cos = 0\) and \(\sin = 1\), so the equation gives \(-2v\omega\varphi'(x) = 0\) for every \(x\). Since \(v\) and \(\omega\) are positive, \(\varphi' = 0\). This is the instant when the string is straight and \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are zero everywhere, while the slope changes fastest: only the Coriolis term is left in the equation and nothing balances it.
  4. A function with zero derivative is constant (corollary 1.86), and \(\varphi(0) = 0\) by the boundary condition, so \(\varphi = 0\). The function \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) is not zero everywhere, so by this it is not a solution.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. WM1990 (PDF p. 4) and CHEN2005 (PDF p. 2) state only the result that the eigenfunctions of the moving string change.

What do we use instead of \(\cos\omega t\)? Every point still oscillates with \(\omega\), but not all at once: the point \(x\) reaches its crest with a delay that depends on \(x\). We say the point \(x\) has amplitude \(|\varphi(x)|\) and phase shift \(\arg\varphi(x)\), and we write this with one complex function, as for systems with one degree of freedom in chapter 9: \(w(x, t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big)\) (definition 9.33). If \(\varphi = |\varphi|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\), then \(w = |\varphi|\cos(\omega t + \theta)\). The word phase means the stage a point has reached in its oscillation: two points with different \(\theta\) are not at the same stage and do not reach their crests at the same time.

Definition 26.12 Mode of the moving string (način nihanja gibajoče se strune)

Let \(L > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(0 \le v < c\). Let \(\omega > 0\) be a number and \(\varphi : [0, L] \to \mathbb{C}\) a complex function that is not zero everywhere and whose real and imaginary parts are twice continuously differentiable. The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode (način nihanja) of the string with equation (26.10) if the real function

\[w(x, t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big) = \mathrm{Re}\,\varphi(x)\cos\omega t - \mathrm{Im}\,\varphi(x)\sin\omega t\]

solves equation (26.10) for all \(t \ge 0\), boundary conditions included. The number \(\omega\) is the natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca), \(\varphi\) is the complex mode shape (kompleksna oblika načina). A point \(x\) with \(\varphi(x) = 0\) is a node (vozel).

Source: WM1990 section 3 (PDF pp. 2-3) and CHEN2005 section 2.2 (PDF pp. 2-3) treat complex eigenfunctions of the moving string. The notation with \(\mathrm{Re}(\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\) is not from the sources. For \(v = 0\) and real \(\varphi\) this is the mode of definition 13.6.

Which pairs \((\omega, \varphi)\) exist? We look for them in three moves. First we insert \(\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) into the equation and get an ordinary equation with complex coefficients. Then we remove the term with the first derivative by writing \(\varphi\) as the product \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\psi\) with a phase \(kx\) that grows with position. The equation of section 13.2 remains. Why a phase? The term with the first derivative carries the factor \(\mathrm{i}\), and multiplying by \(\mathrm{i}\) only turns a pointer, it does not lengthen it (definition 9.33): so this term changes the phase of the points, not their amplitude, and the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\), which has size \(1\), can take it over.

The assumption \(v < c\) enters in the second move, where \(k = v\omega/(c^2 - v^2)\). It is justified here because the band runs at \(40\) m/s while waves run along it at \(c = 113{,}23\) m/s. It fails for \(v \ge c\): there the wave against the motion stands still relative to the eyelets or the band carries it backwards. At \(v = c\) the number \(k\) is not defined and there are no modes of this form. For \(v > c\) the equation again has solutions of this form, but we do not treat supercritical speeds here (section 26.4). In the result it leaves the factor \(1 - v^2/c^2\), with which all frequencies fall to zero at \(v = c\), for the band this factor is \(0{,}8752\).

Theorem 26.13 Modes of the moving string (načini nihanja gibajoče se strune)

Let \(L > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(0 \le v < c\). The pair \((\omega, \varphi)\) is a mode of the string with equation (26.10) if and only if there are a natural number \(n\) and a complex number \(C \ne 0\) with

\[\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{n\pi c}{L}\Big(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\Big) , \qquad \varphi(x) = C\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}} x}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} , \qquad k_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{n\pi v}{cL} . \tag{26.11}\]

For \(C = |C|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\) the real form of the mode is

\[w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t) = |C|\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\cos\big(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + k_{\mathrm{n}} x + \theta\big) . \tag{26.12}\]

For \(v = 0\) we have \(k_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi c/L\) and \(\varphi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\), which is theorem 13.7.

Proof

We must show that the condition for a mode is equivalent to an ordinary equation with end conditions, and solve it. We write \(\varphi' = \mathrm{d}\varphi/\mathrm{d}x\), and derivatives of complex functions are taken by components (lemma 9.35).

  1. For \(w = \mathrm{Re}(\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\) theorem 9.34, item 3, gives \(w_{\mathrm{t}} = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\omega\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\), \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = \mathrm{Re}(-\omega^2\varphi\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\), \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = \mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}\omega\varphi'\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = \mathrm{Re}(\varphi''\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t})\). Equation (26.10) is then \(\mathrm{Re}(P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}) = 0\) with the complex function \(P = -\omega^2\varphi + 2\mathrm{i}v\omega\varphi' + (v^2 - c^2)\varphi''\).
  2. If \(\mathrm{Re}(P(x)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}) = 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\), then \(t = 0\) gives \(\mathrm{Re}\,P = 0\) and \(t = \pi/(2\omega)\) gives \(\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{i}P) = -\mathrm{Im}\,P = 0\). So \(P = 0\), that is
    \[(c^2 - v^2)\,\varphi'' - 2\mathrm{i}v\omega\,\varphi' + \omega^2\varphi = 0 . \tag{26.13}\]
    Conversely \(P = 0\) gives the equation for every \(t\). The same argument (at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\)) shows that the boundary conditions hold if and only if \(\varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0\).
  3. Let \(k = v\omega/(c^2 - v^2)\) (a defined number, since \(v < c\)) and \(\psi(x) = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx}\varphi(x)\). By the rules for complex functions (lemma 9.35, item 1) and the derivative \((\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx})' = \mathrm{i}k\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\) we get \(\varphi' = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}(\psi' + \mathrm{i}k\psi)\) and \(\varphi'' = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}(\psi'' + 2\mathrm{i}k\psi' - k^2\psi)\).
  4. We insert into (26.13) and divide by \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx} \ne 0\) (theorem 9.34, item 2). The coefficient of \(\psi'\) is \(2\mathrm{i}k(c^2 - v^2) - 2\mathrm{i}v\omega = 0\) by the choice of \(k\). The coefficient of \(\psi\) is
    \[-(c^2 - v^2)k^2 + 2v\omega k + \omega^2 = \frac{-v^2\omega^2 + 2v^2\omega^2}{c^2 - v^2} + \omega^2 = \frac{\omega^2 c^2}{c^2 - v^2} .\]
    What remains is \((c^2 - v^2)\psi'' + \dfrac{\omega^2c^2}{c^2 - v^2}\psi = 0\), that is \(\psi'' = -\kappa^2\psi\) with \(\kappa = \omega c/(c^2 - v^2) > 0\). The boundary conditions are \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\), since \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx} \ne 0\).
  5. The real and imaginary parts of \(\psi\) are real, twice continuously differentiable, and each solves \(\psi'' = -\kappa^2\psi\) with zeros at \(0\) and \(L\) (the coefficient \(-\kappa^2\) is real). Since \(\psi\) is not zero everywhere, at least one of them is not. For that part steps 3 to 5 of the proof of theorem 13.7 hold, with \(\kappa\) in place of the number \(k = \omega/c\) used there: \(\sin\kappa L = 0\), so \(\kappa L = n\pi\) with a natural number \(n\), and the part is a multiple of \(\sin\kappa x\). The same holds for the other part if it is not zero, so \(\psi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) with a complex \(C \ne 0\).
  6. From \(\kappa = n\pi/L\) we get \(\omega = \kappa(c^2 - v^2)/c = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(k = v\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(c^2 - v^2) = n\pi v/(cL) = k_{\mathrm{n}}\). So \(\varphi = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}} x}\psi = C\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}}x}\sin(n\pi x/L)\). This is the direction "only if".
  7. Conversely, let \(\omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\varphi\) from (26.11). Then \(\kappa = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} c/(c^2 - v^2) = n\pi/L\) and \(\psi = C\sin(n\pi x/L)\) solves \(\psi'' = -\kappa^2\psi\) with zeros at \(0\) and \(L\). Steps 4 and 3 in reverse give (26.13), and step 2 shows that \(w\) is a solution with the boundary conditions.
  8. The real part: \(\mathrm{Re}\big(|C|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}}x}\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t}\big) = |C|\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + k_{\mathrm{n}}x + \theta)\), because \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha} = \cos\alpha + \mathrm{i}\sin\alpha\) (definition 9.33) and \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) is real. The first factor is the envelope, the cosine with the phase \(k_{\mathrm{n}}x\) gives the shape in the right column.

Source: WM1990 (27a) and (27b) (PDF p. 4): \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi(1 - \nu^2)\) and the eigenfunction with components \(\sin(n\pi x)\cos(n\pi\nu x)\) and \(\sin(n\pi x)\sin(n\pi\nu x)\) in dimensionless quantities, where \(\nu = v/c\) and not Poisson's ratio. CHEN2005 (5) (PDF p. 2): \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = (n\pi/l)(1 - \rho c^2/P)\sqrt{P/\rho}\), where \(c\) means the transport speed. The proof by removing the first derivative with the phase \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\) is not from the sources, it is checked with SymPy (outlines/ch-jermeni/num-03.out, lines 1 and 2).

The theorem is short, its meaning is in the shape, and figure 26.19 builds it. We draw first the two eyelets in each row, with the string of length \(L\) between them, and the horizontal position of the string at rest. Each row is one time in the oscillation, from top to bottom \(\omega t = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2\). Into it we put the shape of the string at time \(\omega t = 0\) and the grey dashed envelope \(\pm\sin(\pi x/L)\), which is the largest deflection a point reaches. The left column is the string at rest, \(v = 0\): at \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the string is straight, at \(\omega t = \pi\) it is inverted, and at every time the shape is symmetric about the middle. In the right column \(v/c = \tfrac12\) and \(k_1 = \pi/(2L)\): at \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the string is not straight, because the factor \(\cos(\omega t + k_1x)\) differs from point to point, and at none of the four times is the shape symmetric. The place of largest deflection, the orange dot, is at \(0{,}39\,L\) at \(\omega t = 0\), at \(0{,}61\,L\) at \(\pi/2\), at \(0{,}39\,L\) again at \(\pi\) and at \(0{,}61\,L\) at \(3\pi/2\) (computed in outlines/ch-jermeni/num-03.out, lines 5). The envelope and the eyelets stay as they were.

Corollary 26.14 Nodes and amplitudes of the mode (vozli in amplitude načina)

Let \((\omega_{\mathrm{n}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\) be a mode from theorem 26.13. The amplitude of the point \(x\) is \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x)| = |C|\,|\sin(n\pi x/L)|\), as for the string at rest. Mode \(n\) has \(n - 1\) nodes in the interior, at the points \(x = jL/n\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\). For \(v > 0\) there is no time at which the string is straight everywhere.

Proof

We must show three claims. The theorem gives the amplitude and the nodes, straightness needs one more argument.

  1. By theorem 9.34, item 2, \(|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}}x}| = 1\), so \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}| = |C|\,|\sin(n\pi x/L)|\). This is the envelope, the same in the left and the right column.
  2. A node is a point with \(\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}(x) = 0\), that is \(\sin(n\pi x/L) = 0\), since \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mathrm{n}}x} \ne 0\) and \(C \ne 0\). The zeros of the sine are the multiples of \(\pi\) (step 5 of the proof of theorem 13.7), so \(x = jL/n\), and the condition \(0 < x < L\) gives \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\).
  3. At time \(t\) we have \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t) = |C|\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + k_{\mathrm{n}}x + \theta)\). For \(x \in (0, L)\) the first factor is nonzero except at the nodes, and the second is zero only where \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + k_{\mathrm{n}}x + \theta\) is an odd multiple of \(\pi/2\). For \(v > 0\) we have \(k_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\) and the argument grows with \(x\) by \(k_{\mathrm{n}}L > 0\), so the cosine has at most finitely many zeros on \((0, L)\). Hence \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(\cdot, t)\) is not zero everywhere. In the right column at \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the factor \(\cos(\pi/2 + \pi x/(2L)) = -\sin(\pi x/(2L))\) has no zero between the eyelets, so the shape lies wholly below the axis, while in the left column it is straight at the same time.

Source: WM1990 (PDF p. 4): the nodes are the same as for the string at rest, but the axial motion distorts the shapes strongly (figure 2, PDF p. 4). The proof is not from the sources.

So every point has its own phase. Where this phase comes from is shown by a second view: the mode as two travelling waves.

By the addition formulas (corollary 8.16) the product \(\sin A\cos B\) is half the sum \(\sin(A + B) + \sin(A - B)\). With \(A = n\pi x/L\) and \(B = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + k_{\mathrm{n}}x\) from mode \(n\) we get the sum of two waves of the kind that lemma 26.6 of section 26.2 knows.

Corollary 26.15 A mode as a sum of two travelling waves (način kot vsota dveh potujočih valov)

Let \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) be from equation (26.12) with \(\theta = 0\) and \(|C| = 1\). Then

\[w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t) = \tfrac12\sin\!\Big(\frac{n\pi(c - v)}{cL}\,x - \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t\Big) + \tfrac12\sin\!\Big(\frac{n\pi(c + v)}{cL}\,x + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t\Big) . \tag{26.14}\]

The first wave has the form \(F(x - (c + v)t)\) and moves in the direction of motion with speed \(c + v\). The second has the form \(G(x + (c - v)t)\) and moves against the direction of motion with speed \(c - v\). Both speeds are measured relative to the fixed eyelets. The waves have the same frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and wavelengths \(2Lc/(n(c - v))\) and \(2Lc/(n(c + v))\).

Proof

We must show the equality and, for each wave, its speed and wavelength.

  1. By the addition formulas (corollary 8.16), \(\sin(A + B) + \sin(A - B) = 2\sin A\cos B\). With \(A = n\pi x/L\) and \(B = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t + k_{\mathrm{n}}x\) we have \(\sin A\cos B = \tfrac12\sin(A + B) + \tfrac12\sin(A - B)\), where \(A + B = (n\pi/L + k_{\mathrm{n}})x + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t\) and \(A - B = (n\pi/L - k_{\mathrm{n}})x - \omega_{\mathrm{n}}t\). Since \(n\pi/L \pm k_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi(c \pm v)/(cL)\), this is (26.14).
  2. The first term is \(\tfrac12\sin(q(x - st))\) with \(q = n\pi(c - v)/(cL)\) and \(s = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/q = (c^2 - v^2)/(c - v) = c + v\). This is the form \(F(x - (c + v)t)\), the blue wave. The wavelength is \(2\pi/q = 2Lc/(n(c - v))\).
  3. The second term is \(\tfrac12\sin(q'(x + s't))\) with \(q' = n\pi(c + v)/(cL)\) and \(s' = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/q' = c - v\), wavelength \(2\pi/q' = 2Lc/(n(c + v))\). This is the orange wave, whose half wavelength for \(n = 1\) and \(v/c = \tfrac12\) is \(\tfrac23 L\).

Source: CHEN2005 (PDF p. 2): Skutch determined the natural frequencies by superposing two waves in opposite directions. The derivation from the mode is not from the sources, it is checked in outlines/ch-jermeni/num-03.out, lines 2.

Figure 26.20 builds this for the first mode at \(v/c = \tfrac12\) at \(\omega t = \pi/3\). In each row there are the two eyelets a distance \(L\) apart and between them the horizontal position of the string at rest. Then we draw the first row, the wave \(\tfrac12\sin(\pi x/(2L) - \omega_1t)\), which the arrow \(c + v\) moves in the direction of motion. Its wavelength is \(4L\), four times the length of the string, so the length \(L\) holds exactly a quarter of the wave. The second row is the wave \(\tfrac12\sin(3\pi x/(2L) + \omega_1t)\) with speed \(c - v\) against the motion, wavelength \(\tfrac43 L\): from crest to trough, the orange dots, is half a wavelength, \(\tfrac23 L\). In the third row is the sum, which is the shape of figure 26.19. For \(v = 0\) the waves have the same wavelength and are mirror images, so the sum is a standing wave. For \(v > 0\) the string carries both waves along at speed \(v\), so the first runs at \(c + v\) and the second at \(c - v\). At the same frequency they therefore have different wavelengths, they are no longer mirror images, and the sum is not a standing wave.

The two waves traverse the length of the string and are reflected at the eyelet. How long does it take for the motion to repeat? Figure 26.21 follows one place of the wave, a crest for example, in the plane of position and time, with the numbers of the band of example 26.17 at \(v = 40\) m/s. We draw the axes: position \(x\) horizontally, time \(t\) vertically upwards. The eyelets are at rest, so they are the vertical lines at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), and the arrow \(v\) shows where the band runs. The crest of the wave in the direction of motion travels at speed \(c + v\) along the blue line and reaches the second eyelet after the time \(L/(c + v) = 6{,}526\) ms. There it is reflected and returns against the motion at speed \(c - v\) along the orange line, which is steeper because the slower wave needs more time for the same distance: \(L/(c - v) = 13{,}656\) ms. Together this is the round-trip time \(\tau = 20{,}182\) ms. The grey dashed path is the same round trip at \(v = 0\), symmetric and \(2L/c = 17{,}664\) ms long. The motion of the band lengthens the round trip by \(2{,}519\) ms: the way forward becomes shorter by \(2{,}306\) ms, but the way back becomes longer by \(4{,}824\) ms, because the band holds the slower wave back for longer than it carries the faster one. The ratio of the two round trips is \(1/(1 - v^2/c^2) = 1{,}1426\), and a longer round trip means a lower frequency: this is where the factor \(1 - v^2/c^2\) of theorem 26.13 comes from.

Why does the motion repeat after one round trip? The eyelet holds the string, so the deflection there is zero at all times. When a wave arrives at the eyelet, the wave that leaves it must cancel the arriving one exactly at that place, so it leaves with the opposite sign: a crest returns as a trough (lemma 26.10). These are the dots marked \(\cdot(-1)\). One round trip has two reflections and \((-1)\cdot(-1) = 1\): the crest is a crest again, at the same place and travelling in the same direction as at the start. By the same lemma the width of the wave is multiplied by \((c - v)/(c + v)\) at the first reflection and by \((c + v)/(c - v)\) at the second, together by \(1\). This holds for every place of the wave, so after the time \(\tau\) the whole string is in the same state as at the start, and the same state under the same equation gives the same continuation. The theorem gives the same number without this picture.

Corollary 26.16 Period and round-trip time (perioda in čas obhoda)

The period of the first mode equals the round-trip time,

\[\tau = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = \frac{L}{c + v} + \frac{L}{c - v} = \frac{2Lc}{c^2 - v^2} , \tag{26.15}\]

and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\tau = 2\pi n\), so every mode is periodic with period \(\tau\).

Proof

We must show the equality of the three expressions and the multiple.

  1. \(\omega_1 = (\pi c/L)(1 - v^2/c^2) = \pi(c^2 - v^2)/(cL)\) by equation (26.11), so \(2\pi/\omega_1 = 2cL/(c^2 - v^2)\). This is \(\tau\).
  2. The time of the leg in the direction of motion is \(L/(c + v)\), the time of the leg back is \(L/(c - v)\). With the same denominator, \(L/(c + v) + L/(c - v) = L\big((c - v) + (c + v)\big)/(c^2 - v^2) = 2Lc/(c^2 - v^2)\).
  3. \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\omega_1\), so \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\tau = 2\pi n\), and \(k_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not depend on time, so \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t + \tau) = w_{\mathrm{n}}(x, t)\).

Source: CHEN2005 (PDF p. 2): Skutch's route by two waves. The calculation is not from the sources. The reflection at a fixed end is treated in more detail in section 26.2.

Example 26.17 Frequencies of the band at different speeds (frekvence traku pri različnih hitrostih)

The band from the start of the section has \(c = \sqrt{\sigma/\rho} = 113{,}23\) m/s with \(\sigma = 100\) MPa and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ (steel from BOWER, table 3.1, PDF p. 100, as in section 26.1). By theorem 26.13 we have \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(2\pi) = n\,\dfrac{c}{2L}\,(1 - v^2/c^2)\), so:

One more step: we show in numbers that the standing wave of section 13.2 is indeed not a solution, and why. Lemma 26.11 says that there is no real standing mode, but not how large the imbalance is. We take \(\sin(\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_1t)\) with the correct frequency \(\omega_1\) of the band and look at the eyelet.

Example 26.18 The standing wave at the first eyelet (stoječe valovanje na prvem očescu)

At \(x = 0\) we have \(w = 0\) at every time, so \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} = 0\) there. For \(w = \sin(\pi x/L)\cos\omega_1t\) also \(w_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(\pi/L)^2 w = 0\) at \(x = 0\). The mixed derivative, however, is \(w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -(\pi/L)\omega_1\sin\omega_1t\), equal to \(-\pi\omega_1/L\) at \(\omega_1t = \pi/2\). The left side of equation (26.10), which should be zero, is then \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -2\pi v\omega_1/L\). At \(v = 40\) m/s and \(\omega_1 = 311{,}32\) 1/s this is \(-78\,244\) m/s² per unit amplitude. As a scale we take the tension term \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) in the middle of the string at \(\omega_1 t = 0\), where it is largest: \(c^2(\pi/L)^2 = 126\,533\) m/s² per unit amplitude. The imbalance is thus \(2g(1 - g^2) = 62\,\%\) of this size, with \(g = v/c = 0{,}353\). For small \(v\) it is about \(2g\), that is linear in the speed.

Source: The calculation is ours (outlines/ch-jermeni/num-03.out, line 3). WM1990 (PDF p. 4) says only that the axial motion distorts the shapes.

Why is it so? Figure 26.23 shows what happens to a particle that runs through the eyelet. We draw the eyelet and the taut string on the left. The shape of the string at \(\omega t = 0\) has slope \(w_{\mathrm{x}} = \pi A/L\) at the eyelet (\(A\) is the drawn deflection in the middle), at \(\omega t = \pi\) the opposite one, and at \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the string is straight. The particle running through the eyelet has a velocity along the tangent, that is the horizontal component \(v\) and the transverse component \(v\,w_{\mathrm{x}}\). The velocity vectors differ at the three times: the transverse component goes from \(v\pi A/L\) through zero to \(-v\pi A/L\). The transverse velocity of the particles running through the eyelet thus changes in time. The transverse acceleration of a particle is \(w_{\mathrm{tt}} + 2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} + v^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) by equation (26.2), and since \(w_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) are zero at the eyelet, the Coriolis term \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}}\) remains. By Newton's law something must act on it. At the eyelet itself the eyelet also pushes on the particle, so we look for the contradiction just past it. At \(\omega_1 t = \pi/2\) the string is straight everywhere and the force \(c^2 w_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is zero everywhere, but the acceleration \(2v\,w_{\mathrm{xt}} = -2v\omega_1(\pi/L)\cos(\pi x/L)\) at \(x = 0{,}05\,L\) is still \(98{,}8\,\%\) of its value at the eyelet. An acceleration without a force is a contradiction, so the standing wave cannot be a motion of the string.

Having found the modes, we ask whether they are enough: can every free motion of the string be written as a sum of modes? Section 26.6 treats this with the orthogonality of complex modes. First, however, the real limit demands attention, the critical speed \(v = c\), at which all \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) vanish: section 26.4.
