Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.4 Mathematics: linear equations with constant coefficients

System A, the cantilever with a mass, vibrates freely after a blow, and its motion is described by the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = 0\) with \(m = 50\) kg, \(c = 35{,}4175\) N s/m and \(k = 62\,720\) N/m. The trial \(q = \mathrm{e}^{st}\) gave \(m s^2 + c s + k = 0\) in section 9.3, with roots \(s_{1,2} = -0{,}35418 \pm 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\), and the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) are solutions. Every combination \(C_1 \mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} + C_2 \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) of them is again a solution, because derivatives are linear (lemma 9.6). Two questions arise here. Is every motion of the cantilever such a combination? And does the start, that is the position \(q(0)\) and the velocity \(\dot q(0)\) at the moment of the blow, determine the motion exactly? The start is the point from which the motion leaves, and the question is whether the curve that goes on from there is unique. If not, the model predicts nothing: the same cantilever with the same start could vibrate in several ways.

The same question will return for a beam on soft ground (chapter 25) and for vibrating beams (chapter 13), where four derivatives appear in the equation. We therefore settle it at once for every order \(n\). The idea is this: we split the equation of order \(n\) into a chain of \(n\) equations, each of which has only a first derivative, which we can solve with one formula, and we feed the solution of each link of the chain into the next. The splitting is possible if we know the roots of the polynomial that the trial with the exponential function gives, and we shall need nothing more than that. We shall factor all polynomials of the book explicitly: the quadratic one with the square root of a complex number from section 9.3, and the polynomial of degree four from the example at the end of the section with a difference of squares.

9.4.1 The equation, its polynomial and the trial with the exponential function

We call the variable \(t\), because we shall solve equations mainly for time. A beam on a foundation has the same equation with the variable \(x\), and the letter does not affect the result. Coefficients and function values may be complex, because the roots will be complex (section 9.3), and a complex function of a real variable is differentiated by its real and imaginary parts (definition 9.32).

Definition 9.38 Characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom)

Let \(n \ge 1\) be a natural number, \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) complex numbers with \(a_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\) and \(f\) a complex function of the real variable \(t\). The order \(n\) is the order of the highest derivative in the equation. Lemma 9.40 will show that it is also the number of frames of the chain, in which every frame contributes one derivative. A linear equation of order \(n\) with constant coefficients (linearna enačba reda \(n\) s stalnimi koeficienti) for an unknown complex function \(y\) of a real variable is

\[a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_1 y' + a_0 y = f(t) . \tag{9.39}\]

The equation is homogeneous (homogena) if \(f = 0\). The polynomial

\[P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_1 s + a_0 \tag{9.40}\]

in a complex variable \(s\) is the characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom) of the equation, and the equation \(P(s) = 0\) is the characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba). We write the left side of equation (9.39) as \(P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\).

Source: KREYSZIG §2.2 (3) (PDF p. 80) for order \(2\), §3.2 (2) (PDF p. 137-138) for order \(n\), CK (3.9) (PDF p. 75). The notation \(P(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t)\) is ours.

For the equation of system A we have \(n = 2\), \(a_2 = m = 50\), \(a_1 = c = 35{,}4175\), \(a_0 = k = 62\,720\) and \(f = 0\). The root \(s_1\) gives the decaying vibration that figure 9.41 shows after the blow. The trial from section 9.3 can now be written as a computation. By theorem 9.34 we have \((\mathrm{e}^{st})^{(j)} = s^j \mathrm{e}^{st}\) for complex \(s\), hence

\[P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = \big(a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_0\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = P(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} .\]

Since \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) is never zero, \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) is a solution of the homogeneous equation exactly when \(P(s) = 0\). That is all the trial says: every root of the polynomial gives a solution. It does not say whether these are all the solutions, nor how many there are for a given initial state. The answer needs a proof.

9.4.2 The equation of first order

We begin with \(n = 1\). We look for a solution of the equation \(y' - s y = f\) on an interval \([0, T]\), where \(T > 0\) is an arbitrary number. A force \(f\) is often only piecewise continuous in vibration, for example a force that is switched on or off at some moment, so we allow such \(f\) too (definition 6.8). Figure 9.37 shows an example that we shall compute for the lemma below. Both graphs have a horizontal time axis. At the top is the right side \(f\) of a constant force on a mass with a damper, which we switch off at \(t_1 = 3\) s, so \(f\) jumps to zero there. The solution \(y\), here the velocity \(v\), is then continuous and does not jump at the point \(t_1\): the force is finite, and by Newton's second law it changes the momentum \(m v\) in a short time by at most the largest force times this time, which goes to zero with the time. The derivative does jump, because \(y' = s y + f\) and \(f\) jumps. So the graph has a kink at \(t_1\): the slope changes there by exactly the jump of the right side \(f\), that is \(0{,}4\) m/s\(^2\). So \(y\) has a derivative only outside finitely many break points. There \(y' = s y + f\), which on every piece of the partition equals the function \(s y + f_{\mathrm{k}}\), continuous on the closed piece (\(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the continuous function that \(f\) agrees with on the piece). So \(y\) is piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). We require the equation at all points of the open interval \((0, T)\) that are not break points of the function \(y\) or \(f\). For a continuous \(f\) there may be no break points at all.

Lemma 9.39 The first-order linear equation with a piecewise continuous right-hand side (linearna enačba prvega reda z odsekoma zvezno desno stranjo)

Let \(T > 0\), \(s\) a complex number, \(f\) a piecewise continuous complex function on \([0, T]\) and \(y_0\) a complex number. In figure 9.37, \(f\) and \(y\) are the right-hand side and the solution. The equation \(y' - s y = f\) with the initial condition \(y(0) = y_0\) has exactly one piecewise continuously differentiable solution on \([0, T]\), namely

\[y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{st}\Big(y_0 + \int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-su} f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) . \tag{9.41}\]

If \(f\) is continuous, \(y\) is differentiable everywhere and the equation holds at every point.

Proof

We must show two things: that the function (9.41) solves the equation, and that there is no other solution. Both follow from multiplying by \(\mathrm{e}^{-st}\), because \((\mathrm{e}^{-st} y)' = \mathrm{e}^{-st}(y' - s y)\). We draw the steps in figure 9.38 for real \(s\) and real \(f\). By definition the complex case is computed by real and imaginary parts, and each of them is such a graph.

1. The formula is a solution.

  1. Let \(g(u) = \mathrm{e}^{-su} f(u)\). On every closed piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) of the partition of \(f\), \(g\) is a product of continuous functions (theorem 9.34 and definition 6.8), so \(g\) is piecewise continuous with the same partition and jumps at most at the break points, such as \(x_1\).
  2. Let \(G(t) = \int_0^t g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) (definition 6.9, the complex integral by real and imaginary part), that is the area under the graph of \(g\) from \(0\) to \(t\). On the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) this definition gives \(G(t) = G(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^t g_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\). By part 2 of the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55), \(G\) is continuous on this piece and differentiable in its interior with \(G' = g_{\mathrm{k}}\). The value \(G(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) is the same from both sides of a break point, since its definition is a sum over pieces, so \(G\) is continuous on \([0, T]\) and piecewise continuously differentiable, with \(G' = g\) outside the break points. In the figure: when \(t\) moves across the jump by \(\delta\), the area changes only by a narrow strip, which vanishes with \(\delta\), so the graph of \(G\) does not jump at \(x_1\). Its slope is the height of \(g\), which jumps at \(x_1\), so the graph has a kink there.
  3. The function (9.41) is \(y = \mathrm{e}^{st}(y_0 + G)\). By the product rule (lemma 9.35, point 1), outside the break points \(y' = s\,\mathrm{e}^{st}(y_0 + G) + \mathrm{e}^{st} g = s y + f\). Here \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\,\mathrm{e}^{-st} = 1\) by theorem 9.34. The function \(y\) is continuous as a product of continuous functions, and its derivative \(s y + f\) equals, on every open piece \((x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}})\), the function \(s y + f_{\mathrm{k}}\), which is continuous on the closed piece. So \(y\) is piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). Also \(y(0) = y_0\), because \(G(0) = 0\). If \(f\) is continuous, then \(g\) is continuous and \(G' = g\) everywhere, so \(y\) is differentiable everywhere and the equation holds at every point. Where \(f\) jumps, \(G\) has a kink, and with it \(y\) gets one too, as the velocity at the switch-off has.

2. There is no other solution.

  1. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be solutions with the same initial value and \(w = y - \tilde y\). Let \(b_0 = 0 < b_1 < \dots < b_{\mathrm{r}} = T\) be all the break points of both solutions and of the function \(f\) together with the end points. Outside them \(w' - s w = f - f = 0\), \(w(0) = 0\) and \(w\) is continuous.
  2. The function \(h = \mathrm{e}^{-st} w\) is continuous on \([0, T]\) and on every open piece \((b_{\mathrm{i}-1}, b_{\mathrm{i}})\) it has derivative \(h' = \mathrm{e}^{-st}(w' - s w) = 0\) (step 3). By corollary 1.86, applied to the real and imaginary parts, \(h\) is constant on every closed piece \([b_{\mathrm{i}-1}, b_{\mathrm{i}}]\). This statement alone would also allow a staircase, with a different value on each piece.
  3. Neighbouring pieces share the point \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(h\) is continuous there, so there is no staircase and \(h\) is constant on all of \([0, T]\) and equal to \(h(0) = w(0) = 0\). Since \(\mathrm{e}^{st} \ne 0\), we get \(w = 0\).

The proof showed where the formula comes from: the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-st}\) is the one that turns the left side, once multiplied, into the derivative of a product, \((\mathrm{e}^{-st} y)'\), and that we know how to integrate. Let us test it on system A with the spring removed, that is on the example of figure 9.37. A mass \(m = 50\) kg with a damper \(c = 35{,}4175\) N s/m, acted on by a constant force \(20\) N from \(t = 0\), has a velocity \(v\) with \(m\dot v + c v = 20\) N, that is \(\dot v - s v = f\) with \(s = -c/m = -0{,}70835\) 1/s and \(f = 20/m = 0{,}4\) m/s\(^2\). Formula (9.41) with \(v(0) = 0\) gives

\[v(t) = \mathrm{e}^{st}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-su}\cdot 0{,}4\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{0{,}4}{-s}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{st}\big) = 0{,}5647\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\,\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0{,}70835\,t}\big) .\]

The velocity approaches the value \(20/c = 0{,}5647\) m/s, at which the damper force equals the applied force, and it approaches it with the time constant \(1/0{,}70835 = 1{,}412\) s: the tangent at the start, with slope \(f = 0{,}4\) m/s\(^2\), reaches the limit \(0{,}5647\) m/s exactly at this time. Both are expected, which means the formula is not meaningless. At the switch-off at \(t_1 = 3\) s the integrand is zero for \(u > 3\), the integral stops at \(3\), and the same formula gives for \(t \ge 3\)

\[v(t) = \mathrm{e}^{st}\int_0^{3} \mathrm{e}^{-su}\cdot 0{,}4\,\mathrm{d}u = 0{,}5647\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\,\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{3s}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{s(t - 3)} = 0{,}4973\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}70835\,(t - 3)} .\]

The velocity decays with the same time constant, but it starts from the value \(0{,}4973\) m/s that it had at the switch-off, so it has no jump. For \(f = 0\) the same computation gives \(y = y_0\mathrm{e}^{st}\), which is lemma 9.19 from section 9.2.

9.4.3 Factorization of the operator

A mapping that assigns to a function \(y\) the function \(P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\) is called an operator (operator): its input is a function and its output is a function. A polynomial \(P\) has roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\) if we can write it as \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\). A root that appears several times in the factorization is listed several times. The factorization is nothing other than the statement that the left side of equation (9.39) is written as a chain of differentiations, each of which subtracts a multiple of the function.

Lemma 9.40 Factorization of the operator (razcep operatorja)

Let \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_0 = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) with complex numbers \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\). The right side is a chain of \(n\) frames, each with one derivative. For every \(n\) times differentiable complex function \(y\),

\[a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_0 y = a_{\mathrm{n}} \Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1\Big)\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_2\Big)\cdots\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}}\Big)\,y , \tag{9.42}\]

where we first compute \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\), then from this function the derivative minus \(s_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) times this function, and so on. The order of the factors can be changed arbitrarily.

Proof

We must show that when factors are composed, derivatives and coefficients combine in the same way as coefficients do in the multiplication of polynomials. The main step is that differentiation and multiplication by a constant commute.

  1. For a polynomial \(Q(s) = b_0 + b_1 s + \dots + b_{\mathrm{l}} s^l\) and an \(l\) times differentiable function \(y\) let \(Q(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,y = b_0 y + b_1 y' + \dots + b_{\mathrm{l}} y^{(l)}\). We write the terms of the operator \(Q\) one below the other as the rows of a grid. Row \(i\) differentiates a function \(i\) times and multiplies it by \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. Let also \(R(s) = c_0 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{p}} s^p\) and let \(y\) have \(l + p\) derivatives. We write the terms of the function \(R(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,y\) side by side as the columns. Since the derivative is linear and a constant passes through the derivative (lemma 9.35, point 1), every row acts on every column separately. Row \(i\) on column \(j\) gives the term \(b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,y^{(i+j)}\) in cell \((i, j)\), and the operator is the sum of all cells:
    \[Q\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\Big(R\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\Big) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{l} b_{\mathrm{i}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{p} c_{\mathrm{j}} y^{(j)}\Big)^{(i)} = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\, y^{(i+j)} .\]
  3. Cells with the same \(i + j = q\) lie on the same diagonal and carry the same derivative \(y^{(q)}\), so they collect into \(\big(\sum_{\mathrm{i} + \mathrm{j} = \mathrm{q}} b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,y^{(q)}\), in the figure the diagonal \(q = 2\). When we multiply the polynomials \(Q\) and \(R\), the same cell holds \(b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\,s^{i+j}\) and the same diagonal collects the coefficient of \(s^q\), which is therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{i} + \mathrm{j} = \mathrm{q}} b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\). The right side of step 2 is thus exactly \((QR)\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\). Composing operators is multiplying polynomials.
  4. The factor \(s - s_{\mathrm{j}}\) gives \((s - s_{\mathrm{j}})\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y = y' - s_{\mathrm{j}} y\). For two factors this is the grid of four cells, whose middle diagonal collects \(-(s_1 + s_2)\,y'\), just as \((s - s_1)(s - s_2) = s^2 - (s_1 + s_2)\,s + s_1 s_2\). By induction on the number of factors, using step 3, we obtain (9.42), which is the chain of differentiations. The coefficients of a product of polynomials are sums of products of complex numbers, and these commute (lemma 9.25), so the product of polynomials commutes and the result is the same for every order of the factors. Swapping two factors mirrors the grid across the main diagonal, and every diagonal goes into itself.

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §3.2 (PDF pp. 140-141) writes the left side with an operator \(L[y]\) and the polynomial in product form, but does not prove the link between the factorization of the polynomial and the composition of differentiations.

For system A we have \(P(s) = 50\,(s - s_1)(s - s_2)\) and the left side \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q\) of the equation can be written \(50\,(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_2)\,q\). Let \(u_1 = \dot q - s_2 q\). Then the equation of the cantilever is equivalent to the pair

\[\dot u_1 - s_1 u_1 = 0 , \qquad \dot q - s_2 q = u_1 ,\]

two equations of first order that we know how to solve. For an equation of order \(n\) the way is the same with \(n\) equations (figure 9.40).

The figure shows order \(n = 4\). Every box is one first-order equation \(u_{\mathrm{j}}' - s_{\mathrm{j}} u_{\mathrm{j}} = u_{\mathrm{j}-1}\), with its root \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) written inside. The chain starts at the function \(u_0 = f/a_{\mathrm{n}}\), that is the right side of the equation divided by \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\). The solution of each box, the function \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\), goes on the right into the next box as its right side. The last solution is \(u_4 = y\), the function we seek. Lemma 9.39 solves each box only once we also know the initial value \(u_{\mathrm{j}}(0)\), which comes from below. For system A there are only two boxes, with \(u_0 = 0\) and \(u_2 = q\).

9.4.4 Existence and uniqueness of the solution of order \(n\)

What does a solution of order \(n\) mean? As in first order, we allow a piecewise continuous force \(f\). A solution of order \(n\) on \([0, T]\) is a function \(y\) that has continuous derivatives \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) (for \(n = 1\) the function \(y\) itself is continuous), whose derivative \(y^{(n-1)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable, and which satisfies equation (9.39) at every point of the open interval \((0, T)\) that is not a break point of the function \(y^{(n-1)}\) or \(f\). The initial values are the numbers \(y(0), y'(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\) (definition 5.47). For \(n = 1\) this is the velocity at the switch-off: continuous, with a slope that jumps because \(f\) jumps.

Theorem 9.41 Existence and uniqueness of the solution (obstoj in enoličnost rešitve)

Let \(T > 0\), \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) the characteristic polynomial of equation (9.39) with \(a_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\), and \(f\) a piecewise continuous complex function on \([0, T]\). The solution is the result of a chain of frames with initial values from below. For every choice of complex numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), equation (9.39) has exactly one solution of order \(n\) on \([0, T]\) with \(y^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n - 1\).

Proof

We prove it by induction on the order \(n\), namely the statement for every factorization of the polynomial at once. For \(n = 1\) the statement is the lemma on the first order. For \(n \ge 2\) we remove the last factor of the factorization: the function \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\) is a solution of an equation of order \(n - 1\) with fewer roots, and its initial values are determined by those of the function \(y\).

1. Order 1.

  1. The equation \(a_1 y' + a_0 y = f\) is \(a_1 (y' - s_1 y) = f\), so \(y' - s_1 y = f/a_1\). The function \(f/a_1\) is piecewise continuous and lemma 9.39 gives exactly one solution with \(y(0) = y_0\). The chain has a single box here.

2. Step from \(n - 1\) to \(n\). Let \(n \ge 2\) and let the statement hold for order \(n - 1\) for every set of roots. Let \(\tilde P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}-1})\) be the polynomial without the last factor. It belongs to the first \(n - 1\) frames of the chain.

  1. Let \(y\) be a solution of order \(n\) and \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\). The derivatives \(z^{(j)} = y^{(j+1)} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(j)}\) are continuous for \(j \le n - 2\). The function \(z^{(n-2)} = y^{(n-1)} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n-2)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable, because \(y^{(n-1)}\) is, and \(y^{(n-2)}\) has a continuous derivative.
  2. At every point where the equation holds, \(y\) is \(n\) times differentiable on the open piece of the partition around the point, so lemma 9.40 gives \(\tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = a_{\mathrm{n}} (\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)\cdots(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}})\,y = f\). So \(z\), in the figure the function \(u_3\), is a solution of order \(n - 1\) of the equation with polynomial \(\tilde P\) and right side \(f\), that is of the chain of the first \(n - 1\) boxes, and its initial values \(z^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}+1} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n - 2\) are determined by the numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\).
  3. Uniqueness. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be solutions with the same initial values. By step 3 their functions \(z\) and \(\tilde z\) solve the same equation of order \(n - 1\) with the same initial values, so they are equal by the induction hypothesis. Then \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) solve the same first-order equation \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\) with the same initial value \(y_0\) and are equal by lemma 9.39.
  4. Existence. By the induction hypothesis there is a solution \(z\) of order \(n - 1\) of the equation \(\tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = f\) with initial values \(z^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}+1} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}}\). Since \(z\) is continuous, lemma 9.39 in the last box gives an everywhere differentiable function \(y\) with \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\) and \(y(0) = y_0\).
  5. Differentiating the relation \(y' = s_{\mathrm{n}} y + z\) of the last frame gives in turn \(y^{(j)} = s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(j-1)} + z^{(j-1)}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\), because \(z^{(j-1)}\) for \(j - 1 \le n - 2\) exists and is continuous. Therefore \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) are continuous and \(y^{(n-1)} = s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n-2)} + z^{(n-2)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable. The initial values are \(y^{(j)}(0) = s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}-1} + (y_{\mathrm{j}} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}-1}) = y_{\mathrm{j}}\), by induction on \(j\).
  6. By lemma 9.40, \(P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,y = a_{\mathrm{n}}(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)\cdots(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}-1})\,z = \tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = f\) outside the break points. So the function \(y\) is a solution of order \(n\), the output of the whole chain.

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §3.1 theorem 2 (PDF p. 134) states the same for homogeneous linear equations of order \(n\) with continuous coefficients, but leaves the proof of existence to the literature. Here the constant case is proved.

The theorem answered the question about the start: the \(n\) numbers \(y(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\), which give the initial values of the boxes, determine the solution exactly, and for order \(2\) these are \(q(0)\) and \(\dot q(0)\). It also answered whether a solution exists for every start, since the proof itself is the computation of the chain. Because uniqueness holds on every \([0, T]\), solutions on \([0, T]\) and on \([0, T']\) agree on the common part, so we also obtain a solution on \([0, \infty)\), which we shall have in the next sections. Chapter 5 stated the theorem for equations of order \(n\) without proof. For constant coefficients, which we need in vibration and for a beam on a foundation, it is proved here, namely for every polynomial whose factorization we know (that a factorization always exists is the fundamental theorem of algebra at the end of the section).

If the coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\), the force \(f\) and the initial values \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) are real, the solution is real. By lemma 9.37 the real part \(\operatorname{Re} y\) solves the same equation with the right side \(\operatorname{Re} f = f\), and its initial values are \(\operatorname{Re} y_{\mathrm{j}} = y_{\mathrm{j}}\). By uniqueness, therefore, \(y = \operatorname{Re} y\). Such is the curve of the motion after the blow.

The first use is a hammer blow on system A. Let \(q(0) = 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s (an impulse of \(5\) N s on a mass of \(50\) kg). In figure 9.41, which has time on the horizontal axis and the displacement on the vertical one, the motion starts at equilibrium with slope \(0{,}1\) m/s. Let \(s_1 = -0{,}35418 + 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(s_2 = \overline{s_1}\), both in 1/s. The first link of the chain is \(u_1 = \dot q - s_2 q\) with initial value \(u_1(0) = \dot q(0) - s_2 q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s. By lemma 9.39, \(u_1(t) = 0{,}1\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and

\[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-s_2 u}\cdot 0{,}1\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 u}\,\mathrm{d}u = 0{,}1\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}}{s_1 - s_2} .\]

The difference of the roots is \(s_1 - s_2 = 2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 35{,}41574\) 1/s, and the numerator is, by the definition of the complex exponential function (definition 9.33), \(\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\,(2\mathrm{i}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\). The numerator is the vertical segment between the number \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and its mirror image \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) across the real axis, so it is purely imaginary, like the denominator \(2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\). The quotient is therefore real:

\[q(t) = \frac{0{,}1}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot \mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t .\]

The complex chain gave a real motion with amplitude \(0{,}1/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 2{,}8236\) mm, which the envelope slowly reduces. With the undamped frequency it would be \(0{,}1/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2{,}8235\) mm, which is \(0{,}005\ \%\) less, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) and \(\zeta = 0{,}01\). The correct one is \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), because the difference of the roots \(2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) appears in \(q\). SymPy checked that the function solves the equation and that \(q(0) = 0\), \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s. Up to here we have used no formula for vibration, only the chain.

Why exactly two initial numbers is shown by another view, step by step in time. The equation \(m\ddot q = -c\dot q - k q\) gives the acceleration once we know the position and the velocity. At \(t = 0\) we know the position \(q(0) = 0\) and the velocity, the slope of the tangent, and the equation gives the acceleration \(\ddot q(0) = -c\,\dot q(0)/m\), which says how the curve bends away from the tangent. After a short time the position and the velocity are known again and we repeat the step. The equation itself computes the acceleration, but not the position and the velocity, so the start must give them. For order \(n\) the equation gives the \(n\)-th derivative, but not the \(n\) lower ones. With critical damping \(c\) is a hundred times larger and so is the initial deceleration, so the orange curve bends away from the same tangent much sooner than the blackwhite one. This view is not a proof, because the steps give only approximations that must be taken to a limit. That work was done by the chain.

9.4.5 All solutions of the homogeneous equation

What happens if a root repeats? For a double root the two functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) would coincide and only one would remain, while there are two initial conditions. Let us see what the chain does. Take the same system A with critical damping, \(c = c_\mathrm{kr} = 2\sqrt{km}\) (section 9.1), so \(s_1 = s_2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = -35{,}4175\) 1/s. The chain is \(\dot u_1 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} u_1 = 0\) and \(\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q = u_1\). The first gives \(u_1 = u_1(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), and the second

\[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(q(0) + \int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\,u_1(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\big(q(0) + u_1(0)\,t\big) .\]

The exponential functions in the integrand cancel, because the roots are equal, and the constant \(u_1(0)\) remains. Its integral from \(0\) to \(t\) is the area of the rectangle of height \(u_1(0)\) and width \(t\), that is \(u_1(0)\,t\). So a polynomial of degree one appears in front of \(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). For a triple root the same would happen once more, with the integral of a line, which is the area of a triangle and grows like \(t^2\).

The proof will need two lemmas on the product of a polynomial and an exponential function.

Lemma 9.42 The operator on a product with an exponential (operator na zmnožku z eksponentno funkcijo)

Let \(s\) be a complex number, \(m \ge 1\) a natural number and \(u\) an \(m\) times differentiable complex function. Then \(\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s\big)\big(\mathrm{e}^{st} u\big) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\) and therefore \(\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s\big)^m\big(\mathrm{e}^{st} u\big) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m)}\). If \(u\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\), the right side is zero. An example is the area of the rectangle \(u_1(0)\,t\), a polynomial of degree one: one derivative gives the constant \(u_1(0)\), a second gives zero.

Proof

We must show the first equality, the other two follow from it.

  1. By the product rule (lemma 9.35, point 1) and theorem 9.34, \((\mathrm{e}^{st} u)' = s\,\mathrm{e}^{st} u + \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\), so \((\mathrm{e}^{st} u)' - s\,\mathrm{e}^{st} u = \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\). We draw the curve \(\mathrm{e}^{st} u\) and pick a point at \(t_0\) on it. If \(u\) stayed at the value \(u(t_0)\), the curve \(u(t_0)\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) would pass through this point with the slope \(s\,\mathrm{e}^{s t_0} u(t_0)\), which is the first term. The true tangent has in addition the second term \(\mathrm{e}^{s t_0} u'(t_0)\), which the growth of \(u\) brings. After the time \(h\) this term is the vertical distance between the tangents. The operator subtracts the first term and leaves the second. The figure is drawn for a real \(s\), but the computation holds for every complex \(s\).
  2. By induction on \(m\): if \((\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s)^{m-1}(\mathrm{e}^{st} u) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m-1)}\), applying step 1 to the function \(u^{(m-1)}\) gives \(\mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m)}\). In the figure the result of the first factor is the function \(\mathrm{e}^{st} u'\), whose height at every \(t\) equals the difference of the slopes on the left. The second factor does the same to it, with \(u'\) in the role of the function \(u\).
  3. A polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\) has value zero after \(m\) derivatives (lemma 1.82, the derivative of the term \(t^l\) is \(l\,t^{l-1}\)). This is the reverse of the area of the triangle, where the integral raises the degree by one: each derivative lowers it by one. In the critical hammer blow \(u' = 0{,}1\) m/s is a constant, so the tangent of the frozen curve coincides with the true one and the second factor gives zero, \((\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\,q = 0\).

Lemma 9.43 Integral of a polynomial times an exponential (integral zmnožka polinoma in eksponentne funkcije)

For \(s = 0\) the integral of a constant is the area of a rectangle, and the integral of a line is the area of a triangle. Let \(s \ne 0\) be a complex number and \(p\) a polynomial of degree \(d\) with complex coefficients. Then \(r = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j)}/s^{j+1}\) is a polynomial of degree \(d\) with \((r\,\mathrm{e}^{st})' = p\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\), hence

\[\int_0^t p(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{su}\,\mathrm{d}u = r(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} - r(0) . \tag{9.43}\]

For \(s = 0\), \(\int_0^t p(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) is a polynomial of degree \(d + 1\).

Proof

We must show that \(r' + s r = p\), because then \((r\,\mathrm{e}^{st})' = (r' + s r)\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\).

  1. The derivative of the polynomial \(r\) is \(r' = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\), and the sum \(s r = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j)}/s^{j}\). We draw the terms of both sums as bars, for \(p(t) = t^2\), \(s = 2\) and the value at \(t = 2\), and we place every term above the number \(k\) of times \(p\) is differentiated in it. We draw the terms of the sum \(s r\) in blue, \(p = 4\), \(-p'/s = -2\) and \(p''/s^2 = 0{,}5\). We draw the terms of the sum \(r'\) in orange, \(p'/s = 2\), \(-p''/s^2 = -0{,}5\) and \(p'''/s^3 = 0\). Since differentiating term \(j\) adds one derivative to \(p\), term \(j\) of the sum \(r'\) stands one column further right than term \(j\) of the sum \(s r\).
  2. Term \(j\) of the first sum, \((-1)^j p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\), cancels against term \(j + 1\) of the second, \((-1)^{j+1} p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\). These are two bars of equal height and opposite sign above the same \(k\). What remains is \(p\) from term \(j = 0\) of the second sum, the bar at \(k = 0\), because the last term of the first sum is \(p^{(d+1)}/s^{d+1} = 0\). In the figure the sum of all bars is \(p(2) = 4\).
  3. The degree of the polynomial \(r\) is \(d\), because the term \(j = 0\), that is \(p/s\), contains the power \(t^d\) and the derivatives lower it. Equality (9.43) follows from part 1 of the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55). For \(s = 0\) the integral of the term \(t^l\) is \(t^{l+1}/(l+1)\), which is a rectangle for \(l = 0\) and a triangle for \(l = 1\).

For the theorem we need one more notion. The multiplicity (večkratnost) of a root \(s_*\) of the polynomial \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}}(s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) is the number of indices \(j\) for which \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = s_*\). The roots in figure 9.47 are four distinct ones, each of multiplicity one. The polynomial \(P(s) = (s + 1)^2 (s + 3)\) in figure 9.44, however, has the factorization with \(s_1 = s_2 = -1\) and \(s_3 = -3\). In the complex plane the root \(-1\) carries the mark \(\times 2\), because it occurs twice in the factorization and has multiplicity two, while the root \(-3\) occurs once. We denote the distinct roots by \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) and their multiplicities by \(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{r}}\), so that \(m_1 + \dots + m_{\mathrm{r}} = n\), in this example \(\sigma_1 = -1\), \(m_1 = 2\), \(\sigma_2 = -3\), \(m_2 = 1\) and \(2 + 1 = 3\).

The multiplicity does not depend on the choice of factorization. Suppose that \(P(s) = (s - s_*)^{m}\,Q(s) = (s - s_*)^{m'}\,Q'(s)\) with polynomials \(Q\) and \(Q'\), with \(Q(s_*) \ne 0 \ne Q'(s_*)\) and \(m < m'\). For real \(h \ne 0\) we insert \(s = s_* + h\) and cancel the factor \(h^m \ne 0\), which gives \(Q(s_* + h) = h^{m'-m}\,Q'(s_* + h)\). In the example of the figure, where \(s_* = -1\), \(m = 2\) and \(Q(s) = s + 3\), the left side is the line \(Q(-1 + h) = 2 + h\) above the axis \(h\). Both sides are complex functions of the real variable \(h\), built from sums and products, hence differentiable (lemma 9.35, point 1) and therefore continuous (lemma 1.80 for each component). Since they agree at every \(h \ne 0\), they have the same limit as \(h \to 0\), and by lemma 1.60 this limit is the value at \(h = 0\). The left side tends to \(Q(s_*)\), in the figure \(Q(-1) = 2\). The right side tends to \(0^{m'-m}\,Q'(s_*) = 0\), because the factor \(h^{m'-m}\) with \(m' - m \ge 1\) tends to zero, while \(Q'(s_* + h)\) stays close to \(Q'(s_*)\). Its absolute values therefore lie in a band above the axis that narrows towards \(h = 0\), in the figure in the wedge \(|w| \le 4|h|\), drawn for \(|Q'(s_* + h)| \le 4\) at \(|h| \le 1\), where \(|h|^{m'-m} \le |h|\). Theorem 9.44 All solutions of the homogeneous equation (vse rešitve homogene enačbe)

Let \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) be the characteristic polynomial of the homogeneous equation \(a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_0 y = 0\) with distinct roots \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) and multiplicities \(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{r}}\). At critical damping there is a polynomial of degree one in front of the exponential, and at four distinct roots only constants. A function \(y\) is a solution on \([0, T]\) exactly when

\[y(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{r} p_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} \tag{9.44}\]

for polynomials \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) of degree at most \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\) with complex coefficients. The coefficients of the polynomials are \(m_1 + \dots + m_{\mathrm{r}} = n\) complex numbers. For every initial values \(y(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\) they can be chosen so that the function (9.44) has these initial values.

Proof

We must show three things: that every such function is a solution, that every solution has this form, and that with the constants we reach every start. The first follows from the lemma on the operator, the second from the chain, the third from the theorem on uniqueness.

1. The functions (9.44) are solutions.

  1. We order the factors in equation (9.42) (lemma 9.40) so that the last frames of the chain are those with \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\). The function \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} p_{\mathrm{k}}\) passes through all \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) factors \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) into zero (lemma 9.42, \(\deg p_{\mathrm{k}} \le m_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\)), and the remaining factors do not change zero, because they are linear. So every term is a solution.
  2. Since the operator \(P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\) is linear (lemma 9.35, point 1), the sum of the terms is a solution too.

2. Every solution has this form. We prove it by induction on \(n\), for every set of roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\) at once.

  1. For \(n = 1\), by lemma 9.39 with \(f = 0\), the only solution in the only frame with start \(y(0)\) is the function \(y(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\), which is the form (9.44) with one root and \(m_1 = 1\).
  2. Let \(n \ge 2\) and let the statement hold for order \(n - 1\). Let \(y\) be a solution and \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\). As in step 3 of the proof of theorem 9.41, \(z\) is a solution of the homogeneous equation of order \(n - 1\) with roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), that is of the chain without the last box, so by the induction hypothesis \(z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the distinct roots of this set and \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) are polynomials of degree below their multiplicity in it. We denote the multiplicity of the root \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the full set by \(m\), in the shorter set it is \(m - 1\). In figure 9.45, \(n = 3\) with roots \(-1\), \(-2\), \(-2\), so \(s_{\mathrm{n}} = -2\) and \(m = 2\). Each column is one root, each row one power of \(t\). The function \(z = 3\,\mathrm{e}^{-t} + 2\,\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\) solves the chain with roots \(-1\) and \(-2\), and its cells hold \(q_1 = 3\) and \(q_2 = 2\), both of degree zero, because in the shorter set each root has multiplicity one.
  3. The function \(y\) solves \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\), the equation of the last box, in figure 9.45 \(y' + 2y = z\) with \(y(0) = 1\), so by lemma 9.39
    \[y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(y(0) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \int_0^t q_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} - s_{\mathrm{n}})u}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) .\]
  4. A term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} \ne s_{\mathrm{n}}\) enters the last frame: lemma 9.43 gives the integral \(r_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} - s_{\mathrm{n}})t} - r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\) with a polynomial \(r_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the same degree as \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\). After multiplying by \(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) there remains \(r_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} - r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). The multiplicity of the root \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the full set equals that in the shorter set, so the degree of the polynomial \(r_{\mathrm{k}}\) is still below it. In the figure \(\sigma_1 = -1\), and the lemma gives for \(p = 3\) and \(s = -1 - (-2) = 1\) the polynomial \(r_1 = 3\), and the term becomes \(3\,\mathrm{e}^{-t} - 3\,\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\): the coefficient \(3\) stays in its cell, while \(-r_1(0) = -3\) goes to the column of the root \(-2\).
  5. The term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} = s_{\mathrm{n}}\) (there is none if \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not among the roots of the shorter set, that is for \(m = 1\)): the polynomial \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) has degree at most \(m - 2\), and its integral \(\int_0^t q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\) (lemma 9.43, for \(s = 0\)). For \(m = 2\), \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a constant and the integral is the area of a rectangle. In figure 9.45 this is the coefficient \(2\), which the integral \(\int_0^t 2\,\mathrm{d}u = 2t\) raises from the row \(t^0\) to the row \(t^1\).
  6. In front of \(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) in \(y\) stand the initial value of the last frame, that is \(y(0)\), and the numbers \(-r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\) and the integral from step 7. Together they form a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\), for \(m = 1\) a constant. All terms therefore have the form (9.44) with the multiplicities of the full set. In figure 9.45 the cell \(t^0\) of the root \(-2\) holds the initial value \(y(0) = 1\) and \(-3\), together \(-2\), and above it stands \(2\) at \(t^1\). 9.4.6 Real solutions

The cantilever is real, its motion is real, and the coefficients are real. Yet the solution \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) is complex. The measured curve is real, and a root is a point in the complex plane. How do we obtain the solutions with real values that we measure?

Corollary 9.45 Real solutions of a linear equation with real coefficients (realne rešitve linearne enačbe z realnimi koeficienti)

Let the coefficients \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) of the homogeneous equation be real and \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) a factorization of its characteristic polynomial. The non-real roots of the polynomial \(P\) occur in conjugate pairs \(\sigma \ne \overline{\sigma}\) with the same multiplicity. In figure 9.47 the roots \(\beta(-1 \pm \mathrm{i})\) are such a pair, and their real solutions are the decaying cosine and sine. The real solutions of the equation are exactly the linear combinations with real coefficients of the functions \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) for real roots \(s\) and \(j < m\), and of the functions \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\cos\omega t\) and \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\sin\omega t\) for pairs of roots \(\alpha \pm \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega > 0\) and \(j < m\), where \(m\) is the multiplicity of the root.

Proof

We must show three things: that the conjugate root belongs to a pair with the same multiplicity, that the listed functions are real solutions, and that every real solution is their combination. The second and third follow from the theorem on the general solution and from the fact that the real and imaginary parts of a complex solution are real solutions.

  1. Since the \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) are real, \(\overline{P(\overline{s})} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}} s^j = P(s)\) (conjugation preserves sums and products, lemma 9.27, point 2). From \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) it therefore follows that \(P(s) = \overline{P(\overline{s})} = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - \overline{s_1})\cdots(s - \overline{s_{\mathrm{n}}})\), because \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\) is real. This is a factorization with conjugate roots, the mirror images of the original ones across the real axis. The multiplicity does not depend on the factorization (the paragraph before theorem 9.44), so \(\overline{\sigma}\) has the same multiplicity as \(\sigma\).
  2. The function \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\) is a complex solution (theorem 9.44). By lemma 9.37 its real and imaginary parts are real solutions. For \(\sigma = \alpha + \mathrm{i}\omega\) these are, by the definition of the complex exponential function (definition 9.33), \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\cos\omega t\) and \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\sin\omega t\), in the figure \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos t\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin t\). For real \(\sigma\) the function itself is real.
  3. Let \(y\) be a real solution. By theorem 9.44, \(y = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} p_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\), and since \(y = \operatorname{Re} y\), \(y = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \operatorname{Re}\big(p_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\). Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}'} = \overline{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}}\) be the conjugate root. Since \(\operatorname{Re} w = \operatorname{Re}\overline{w}\) and \(\overline{\mathrm{e}^{z}} = \mathrm{e}^{\overline{z}}\) (cosine is even, sine is odd, definition 9.33), \(\operatorname{Re}\big(p_{\mathrm{k}'}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}'} t}\big) = \operatorname{Re}\big(\overline{p_{\mathrm{k}'}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\), so the conjugate term together with the \(k\)-th gives \(\operatorname{Re}\big((p_{\mathrm{k}} + \overline{p_{\mathrm{k}'}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\) with a polynomial of the same bounded degree (step 1). We see the equality \(\operatorname{Re} w = \operatorname{Re}\overline{w}\) in the figure: the arrow \(w\) and its mirror image \(\overline{w}\) across the real axis cast the same shadow on the real axis.
  4. In each pair we choose the term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} = \alpha + \mathrm{i}\omega\), \(\omega > 0\), in the figure the upper root of the pair. For a polynomial with coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i}\,d_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (c_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i}\,d_{\mathrm{j}})\,t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}(\cos\omega t + \mathrm{i}\sin\omega t)\big) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} t^j \mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}(c_{\mathrm{j}}\cos\omega t - d_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\omega t)\). In the figure the product \((c_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i}\,d_{\mathrm{j}})(\cos\omega t + \mathrm{i}\sin\omega t)\) is the blackwhite arrow, made of the arrow \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) and the arrow \(\mathrm{i}\,d_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\), which is a quarter turn ahead. The shadow of the first on the real axis is \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\cos\omega t\), the shadow of the second \(-d_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\omega t\), and together they are exactly the bracket on the right. 9.4.7 An example of order four: a beam on soft ground

Now we pay the promise from chapter 5. There, theorem 5.70 gave, for a beam on an elastic foundation, the equation \(EI\,v'''' - c\,v'' + k\,v = q\), where the term \(k\,v\) describes a foundation of stiffness \(k\) (force per unit length per unit deflection), and for \(c = 0\) there is no axial force. It did not solve the equation. Without load and without axial force this is \(EI\,v'''' + k\,v = 0\) with constant \(EI\). Theorem 9.41 with the variable \(x\) in place of \(t\) says that the four values \(v, v', v'', v'''\) at the point \(x = 0\) determine exactly one solution, and theorem 9.44 says how to find it. A beam has conditions at both ends, not only at \(x = 0\): we choose the four constants of the solution so that we satisfy them (chapter 25). The roots of the characteristic polynomial that we shall find lie in the complex plane, as four points in figure 9.47.

Take the cantilever from chapter 5 with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and a foundation of stiffness \(k = 1{,}0\cdot 10^{6}\) N/m\(^2\) (our choice for illustration). The equation is \(v'''' + 4\beta^4 v = 0\) with \(4\beta^4 = k/EI = 47{,}832\) 1/m\(^4\), so \(\beta = 1{,}8596\) 1/m. The characteristic polynomial is \(P(s) = s^4 + 4\beta^4\). It has no real roots, because it is positive for real \(s\). We factor it with a difference of squares:

\[s^4 + 4\beta^4 = (s^2 + 2\beta^2)^2 - (2\beta s)^2 = (s^2 + 2\beta s + 2\beta^2)(s^2 - 2\beta s + 2\beta^2)\]

(SymPy checked the equality). The quadratic formula with discriminant \(4\beta^2 - 8\beta^2 = -4\beta^2\) gives roots \(-\beta \pm \mathrm{i}\beta\) for the first factor and \(\beta \pm \mathrm{i}\beta\) for the second (section 9.3). The four roots

\[s = \beta\,(\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})\]

are simple. By corollary 9.45 the real solutions are combinations of the four functions (figure 9.47)

\[\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\cos\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\sin\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x .\]

The measure of change is \(1/\beta = 0{,}538\) m: the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\) of the decaying solutions, which bounds their cosine and sine, drops over this distance, in the figure at \(t = \beta x = 1\), to \(1/\mathrm{e}\) of its initial value, and over the half-wavelength of the oscillation \(\pi/\beta = 1{,}689\) m, in the figure at \(t = \pi\), to \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432\) of its initial value.

On the left of the figure is the complex plane, the real part of a root horizontal and the imaginary part vertical. The point \(\beta\) is marked on the real axis. All four roots have the modulus \(|\beta(\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})| = \sqrt{2}\,\beta\), so they lie on the circle of this radius. The roots with negative real part, \(\beta(-1 + \mathrm{i})\) and \(\beta(-1 - \mathrm{i})\), give the solutions with the decaying factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\), \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cos t\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\sin t\) at the top right. The roots with positive real part, \(\beta(1 + \mathrm{i})\) and \(\beta(1 - \mathrm{i})\), give the solutions with the growing factor \(\mathrm{e}^{t}\) at the bottom right, drawn multiplied by \(\mathrm{e}^{-3}\). Beside the decaying cosine the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-t}\) alone is drawn grey dashed. It bounds the cosine from above and touches it at the start, because there \(\cos t = 1\).

The same procedure gives for the polynomial \(s^4 - \beta^4 = (s - \beta)(s + \beta)(s - \mathrm{i}\beta)(s + \mathrm{i}\beta)\) the roots \(\pm\beta\) (in the figure the point \(\beta\) on the real axis) and \(\pm\mathrm{i}\beta\) and the solutions \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\), \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\), \(\cos\beta x\) and \(\sin\beta x\) (in place of the first two we may choose their half sum and half difference, which we shall do in section 9.5 for \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\omega_* t}\) with \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) in the overdamped case of theorem 9.48). Chapter 13 will have the equation \(v'''' - \beta^4 v = 0\) for a vibrating beam, here we only name it.

Two tools are only named here. The fundamental theorem of algebra (LEBL2 theorem 11.5.5, PDF p. 170) says that every non-constant complex polynomial has a root, so a factorization always exists. Theorem 9.44 does not need it, because we factored all polynomials explicitly. The Laplace transform (KREYSZIG chapter 6, CK §5.6, PDF pp. 154-160) solves the same equations with another tool. It would require infinite complex integrals and an inversion theorem, while factorization and convolution (section 9.10) give the same results with tools the book already has.
