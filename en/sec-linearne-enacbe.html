Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.4 Mathematics: linear equations with constant coefficients

System A, the cantilever with a mass, vibrates freely after a blow, and its motion is described by the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = 0\) with \(m = 50\) kg, \(c = 35{,}4175\) N s/m and \(k = 62\,720\) N/m. The trial \(q = \mathrm{e}^{st}\) gave \(m s^2 + c s + k = 0\) in section 9.3, with roots \(s_{1,2} = -0{,}35418 \pm 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\), and the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) are solutions. Every combination \(C_1 \mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} + C_2 \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) of them is again a solution, because derivatives are linear. Two questions arise here. Is every motion of the cantilever such a combination? And does the start, that is the position \(q(0)\) and the velocity \(\dot q(0)\) at the moment of the blow, determine the motion exactly? If not, the model predicts nothing: the same cantilever with the same start could vibrate in several ways.

The same question will return for a beam on soft ground (chapter 25) and for vibrating beams (chapter 13), where four derivatives appear in the equation. We therefore settle it at once for every order \(n\). The idea is this: we split the equation of order \(n\) into a chain of \(n\) equations, each of which has only a first derivative, which we can solve with one formula, and we feed each link of the chain into the next. The splitting is possible if we know the roots of the polynomial that the trial with the exponential function gives, and we shall need nothing more than that. We shall factor all polynomials of the book explicitly: the quadratic one with the square root of a complex number from section 9.3, and the polynomial of degree four from the example at the end of the section with a difference of squares.

9.4.1 The equation, its polynomial and the trial with the exponential function

We call the variable \(t\), because we shall solve equations mainly for time. A beam on a foundation has the same equation with the variable \(x\), and the letter does not affect the result. Coefficients and function values may be complex, because the roots will be complex (section 9.3), and a complex function of a real variable is differentiated by its real and imaginary parts (definition 9.29).

Definition 9.33 Characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom)

Let \(n \ge 1\) be a natural number, \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) complex numbers with \(a_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\) and \(f\) a complex function of the real variable \(t\). A linear equation of order \(n\) with constant coefficients (linearna enačba reda \(n\) s stalnimi koeficienti) for an unknown complex function \(y\) of a real variable is

\[a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_1 y' + a_0 y = f(t) . \tag{9.31}\]

The equation is homogeneous (homogena) if \(f = 0\). The polynomial

\[P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_1 s + a_0 \tag{9.32}\]

in a complex variable \(s\) is the characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom) of the equation, and the equation \(P(s) = 0\) is the characteristic equation (karakteristična enačba). We write the left side of equation (9.31) as \(P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\).

Source: KREYSZIG §2.2 (3) (PDF p. 80) for order \(2\), §3.2 (PDF p. 140) for order \(n\), CK (3.9) (PDF p. 75). The notation \(P(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t)\) is ours.

For the equation of system A we have \(n = 2\), \(a_2 = m = 50\), \(a_1 = c = 35{,}4175\), \(a_0 = k = 62\,720\) and \(f = 0\). The trial from section 9.3 can now be written as a computation. By theorem 9.28 we have \((\mathrm{e}^{st})^{(j)} = s^j \mathrm{e}^{st}\) for complex \(s\), hence

\[P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = \big(a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_0\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = P(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} .\]

Since \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) is never zero, \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) is a solution of the homogeneous equation exactly when \(P(s) = 0\). That is all the trial says: every root of the polynomial gives a solution. It does not say whether these are all the solutions, nor how many there are for a given initial state. The answer needs a proof.

9.4.2 The equation of first order

We begin with \(n = 1\). We look for a solution of the equation \(y' - s y = f\) on an interval \([0, T]\), where \(T > 0\) is an arbitrary number. A force \(f\) is often only piecewise continuous in vibration, for example a force that is switched on at some moment, so we allow such \(f\) too (definition 6.8). The function \(y\) is then continuous and has a derivative only outside finitely many break points, so it is piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). We require the equation at all points of the open interval \((0, T)\) that are not break points of the function \(y\) or \(f\). For a continuous \(f\) there may be no break points at all.

Lemma 9.34 The first-order equation (enačba prvega reda)

Let \(T > 0\), \(s\) a complex number, \(f\) a piecewise continuous complex function on \([0, T]\) and \(y_0\) a complex number. The equation \(y' - s y = f\) with the initial condition \(y(0) = y_0\) has exactly one piecewise continuously differentiable solution on \([0, T]\), namely

\[y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{st}\Big(y_0 + \int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-su} f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) . \tag{9.33}\]

If \(f\) is continuous, \(y\) is differentiable everywhere and the equation holds at every point.

Proof

We must show two things: that the function (9.33) solves the equation, and that there is no other solution. Both follow from multiplying by \(\mathrm{e}^{-st}\), because \((\mathrm{e}^{-st} y)' = \mathrm{e}^{-st}(y' - s y)\).

1. The formula is a solution.

  1. Let \(g(u) = \mathrm{e}^{-su} f(u)\). On every closed piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) of the partition of \(f\), \(g\) is a product of continuous functions (theorem 9.28 and definition 6.8), so \(g\) is piecewise continuous with the same partition.
  2. Let \(G(t) = \int_0^t g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) (definition 6.9, the complex integral by real and imaginary part). On the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) this definition gives \(G(t) = G(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}) + \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^t g_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\). By part 2 of the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40), \(G\) is continuous on this piece and differentiable in its interior with \(G' = g_{\mathrm{k}}\). The value \(G(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) is the same from both sides of a break point, since its definition is a sum over pieces, so \(G\) is continuous on \([0, T]\) and piecewise continuously differentiable, with \(G' = g\) outside the break points.
  3. The function (9.33) is \(y = \mathrm{e}^{st}(y_0 + G)\). By the product rule (lemma 1.81, applied to the real and imaginary parts), outside the break points \(y' = s\,\mathrm{e}^{st}(y_0 + G) + \mathrm{e}^{st} g = s y + f\). Here \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\,\mathrm{e}^{-st} = 1\) by theorem 9.28. Also \(y(0) = y_0\), because \(G(0) = 0\). If \(f\) is continuous, then \(g\) is continuous and \(G' = g\) everywhere.

2. There is no other solution.

  1. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be solutions with the same initial value and \(w = y - \tilde y\). Let \(b_0 = 0 < b_1 < \dots < b_{\mathrm{r}} = T\) be all the break points of both solutions and of the function \(f\) together with the end points. Outside them \(w' - s w = f - f = 0\), \(w(0) = 0\) and \(w\) is continuous.
  2. The function \(h = \mathrm{e}^{-st} w\) is continuous on \([0, T]\) and on every open piece \((b_{\mathrm{i}-1}, b_{\mathrm{i}})\) it has derivative \(h' = \mathrm{e}^{-st}(w' - s w) = 0\) (step 3). By corollary 1.85, applied to the real and imaginary parts, \(h\) is constant on every closed piece \([b_{\mathrm{i}-1}, b_{\mathrm{i}}]\).
  3. Neighbouring pieces share the point \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(h\) is continuous there, so \(h\) is constant on all of \([0, T]\) and equal to \(h(0) = w(0) = 0\). Since \(\mathrm{e}^{st} \ne 0\), we get \(w = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §1.5 (PDF p. 54) solves the same first-order equation with an integrating factor, which here is \(\mathrm{e}^{-st}\).

The proof showed where the formula comes from: the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-st}\) is the one that turns the left side, once multiplied, into the derivative of a product, \((\mathrm{e}^{-st} y)'\), and that we know how to integrate. Let us test it on system A with the spring removed. A mass \(m = 50\) kg with a damper \(c = 35{,}4175\) N s/m, acted on by a constant force \(20\) N from \(t = 0\), has a velocity \(v\) with \(m\dot v + c v = 20\) N, that is \(\dot v - s v = f\) with \(s = -c/m = -0{,}70835\) 1/s and \(f = 20/m = 0{,}4\) m/s\(^2\). Formula (9.33) with \(v(0) = 0\) gives

\[v(t) = \mathrm{e}^{st}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-su}\cdot 0{,}4\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{0{,}4}{-s}\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{st}\big) = 0{,}5647\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\,\big(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0{,}70835\,t}\big) .\]

The velocity approaches the value \(20/c = 0{,}5647\) m/s, at which the damper force equals the applied force, and it approaches it with the time constant \(1/0{,}70835 = 1{,}412\) s. Both are expected, which means the formula is not meaningless. For \(f = 0\) the same computation gives \(y = y_0\mathrm{e}^{st}\), which is lemma 9.14 from section 9.2.

9.4.3 Factorization of the operator

A mapping that assigns to a function \(y\) the function \(P\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\) is called an operator (operator): its input is a function and its output is a function. A polynomial \(P\) has roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\) if we can write it as \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\). A root that appears several times in the factorization is listed several times. The factorization is nothing other than the statement that the left side of equation (9.31) is written as a chain of differentiations, each of which subtracts a multiple of the function.

Lemma 9.35 Factorization of the operator (razcep operatorja)

Let \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} s^n + \dots + a_0 = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) with complex numbers \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\). For every \(n\) times differentiable complex function \(y\),

\[a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_0 y = a_{\mathrm{n}} \Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1\Big)\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_2\Big)\cdots\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}}\Big)\,y , \tag{9.34}\]

where we first compute \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\), then from this function the derivative minus \(s_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) times this function, and so on. The order of the factors can be changed arbitrarily.

Proof

We must show that when factors are composed, derivatives and coefficients combine in the same way as coefficients do in the multiplication of polynomials. The main step is that differentiation and multiplication by a constant commute.

  1. For a polynomial \(Q(s) = b_0 + b_1 s + \dots + b_{\mathrm{l}} s^l\) and an \(l\) times differentiable function \(y\) let \(Q(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,y = b_0 y + b_1 y' + \dots + b_{\mathrm{l}} y^{(l)}\).
  2. Let also \(R(s) = c_0 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{p}} s^p\) and let \(y\) have \(l + p\) derivatives. Since the derivative is linear and a constant passes through the derivative (lemma 1.81),
    \[Q\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\Big(R\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\Big) = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{l} b_{\mathrm{i}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{p} c_{\mathrm{j}} y^{(j)}\Big)^{(i)} = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\, y^{(i+j)} .\]
  3. The coefficient of \(s^q\) in the product \(QR\) is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i} + \mathrm{j} = \mathrm{q}} b_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\), so the right side of step 2 is exactly \((QR)\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y\). Composing operators is therefore multiplying polynomials.
  4. The factor \(s - s_{\mathrm{j}}\) gives \((s - s_{\mathrm{j}})\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big)\,y = y' - s_{\mathrm{j}} y\). By induction on the number of factors, using step 3, we obtain (9.34). The coefficients of a product of polynomials are sums of products of complex numbers, and these commute (lemma 9.20), so the product of polynomials commutes and the result is the same for every order of the factors.

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §3.2 (PDF pp. 140-141) writes the left side with an operator \(L[y]\) and the polynomial in product form, but does not prove the link between the factorization of the polynomial and the composition of differentiations.

For system A we have \(P(s) = 50\,(s - s_1)(s - s_2)\) and the left side \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q\) of the equation can be written \(50\,(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_2)\,q\). Let \(u_1 = \dot q - s_2 q\). Then the equation of the cantilever is equivalent to the pair

\[\dot u_1 - s_1 u_1 = 0 , \qquad \dot q - s_2 q = u_1 ,\]

two equations of first order that we know how to solve. For an equation of order \(n\) the way is the same with \(n\) equations (figure 9.11).

9.4.4 Existence and uniqueness of the solution of order \(n\)

What does a solution of order \(n\) mean? As in first order, we allow a piecewise continuous force \(f\). A solution of order \(n\) on \([0, T]\) is a function \(y\) that has continuous derivatives \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) (for \(n = 1\) the function \(y\) itself is continuous), whose derivative \(y^{(n-1)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable, and which satisfies equation (9.31) at every point of the open interval \((0, T)\) that is not a break point of the function \(y^{(n-1)}\) or \(f\). The initial values are the numbers \(y(0), y'(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\) (definition 5.46).

Theorem 9.36 Existence and uniqueness of the solution (obstoj in enoličnost rešitve)

Let \(T > 0\), \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) the characteristic polynomial of equation (9.31) with \(a_{\mathrm{n}} \ne 0\), and \(f\) a piecewise continuous complex function on \([0, T]\). For every choice of complex numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), equation (9.31) has exactly one solution of order \(n\) on \([0, T]\) with \(y^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n - 1\).

Proof

We prove it by induction on the order \(n\), namely the statement for every factorization of the polynomial at once. For \(n = 1\) the statement is the lemma on the first order. For \(n \ge 2\) we remove the last factor of the factorization: the function \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\) is a solution of an equation of order \(n - 1\) with fewer roots, and its initial values are determined by those of the function \(y\).

1. Order 1.

  1. The equation \(a_1 y' + a_0 y = f\) is \(a_1 (y' - s_1 y) = f\), so \(y' - s_1 y = f/a_1\). The function \(f/a_1\) is piecewise continuous and lemma 9.34 gives exactly one solution with \(y(0) = y_0\).

2. Step from \(n - 1\) to \(n\). Let \(n \ge 2\) and let the statement hold for order \(n - 1\) for every set of roots. Let \(\tilde P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}-1})\) be the polynomial without the last factor.

  1. Let \(y\) be a solution of order \(n\) and \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\). The derivatives \(z^{(j)} = y^{(j+1)} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(j)}\) are continuous for \(j \le n - 2\). The function \(z^{(n-2)} = y^{(n-1)} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n-2)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable, because \(y^{(n-1)}\) is, and \(y^{(n-2)}\) has a continuous derivative.
  2. At every point where the equation holds, lemma 9.35 gives \(\tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = a_{\mathrm{n}} (\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)\cdots(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}})\,y = f\). So \(z\) is a solution of order \(n - 1\) of the equation with polynomial \(\tilde P\) and right side \(f\), and its initial values \(z^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}+1} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n - 2\) are determined by the numbers \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\).
  3. Uniqueness. Let \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) be solutions with the same initial values. By step 3 their functions \(z\) and \(\tilde z\) solve the same equation of order \(n - 1\) with the same initial values, so they are equal by the induction hypothesis. Then \(y\) and \(\tilde y\) solve the same first-order equation \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\) with the same initial value \(y_0\) and are equal by lemma 9.34.
  4. Existence. By the induction hypothesis there is a solution \(z\) of order \(n - 1\) of the equation \(\tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = f\) with initial values \(z^{(j)}(0) = y_{\mathrm{j}+1} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}}\). Since \(z\) is continuous, lemma 9.34 gives an everywhere differentiable function \(y\) with \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\) and \(y(0) = y_0\).
  5. Differentiating the relation \(y' = s_{\mathrm{n}} y + z\) gives in turn \(y^{(j)} = s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(j-1)} + z^{(j-1)}\) for \(j = 1, \dots, n - 1\), because \(z^{(j-1)}\) for \(j - 1 \le n - 2\) exists and is continuous. Therefore \(y', \dots, y^{(n-1)}\) are continuous and \(y^{(n-1)} = s_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n-2)} + z^{(n-2)}\) is piecewise continuously differentiable. The initial values are \(y^{(j)}(0) = s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}-1} + (y_{\mathrm{j}} - s_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{j}-1}) = y_{\mathrm{j}}\), by induction on \(j\).
  6. By lemma 9.35, \(P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,y = a_{\mathrm{n}}(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_1)\cdots(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s_{\mathrm{n}-1})\,z = \tilde P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\,z = f\) outside the break points. So the function \(y\) is a solution of order \(n\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §3.1 theorem 2 (PDF p. 134) states the same for linear equations of order \(n\) with continuous coefficients, but leaves the proof of existence to the literature. Here the constant case is proved.

The theorem answered the question about the start: the \(n\) numbers \(y(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\) determine the solution exactly, and for order \(2\) these are \(q(0)\) and \(\dot q(0)\). It also answered whether a solution exists for every start, since the proof itself is the computation of the chain. Because uniqueness holds on every \([0, T]\), solutions on \([0, T]\) and on \([0, T']\) agree on the common part, so we also obtain a solution on \([0, \infty)\), which we shall have in the next sections. Chapter 5 stated the theorem for equations of order \(n\) without proof (KREYSZIG §3.1 theorem 2, PDF p. 134, with continuous coefficients). For constant coefficients, which we need in vibration and for a beam on a foundation, it is proved here.

If the coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\), the force \(f\) and the initial values \(y_{\mathrm{j}}\) are real, the solution is real. By lemma 9.32 the real part \(\operatorname{Re} y\) solves the same equation with the right side \(\operatorname{Re} f = f\), and its initial values are \(\operatorname{Re} y_{\mathrm{j}} = y_{\mathrm{j}}\). By uniqueness, therefore, \(y = \operatorname{Re} y\).

The first use is a hammer blow on system A. Let \(q(0) = 0\) and \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s (an impulse of \(5\) N s on a mass of \(50\) kg). Let \(s_1 = -0{,}35418 + 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(s_2 = \overline{s_1}\), both in 1/s. The first link of the chain is \(u_1 = \dot q - s_2 q\) with initial value \(u_1(0) = \dot q(0) - s_2 q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s. By lemma 9.34, \(u_1(t) = 0{,}1\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and

\[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-s_2 u}\cdot 0{,}1\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 u}\,\mathrm{d}u = 0{,}1\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}}{s_1 - s_2} .\]

The difference of the roots is \(s_1 - s_2 = 2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) with \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 35{,}41574\) 1/s, and the numerator is, by the definition of the complex exponential function (definition 9.27), \(\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\,(2\mathrm{i}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\). The quotient is real:

\[q(t) = \frac{0{,}1}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot \mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t .\]

The complex chain gave a real motion with amplitude \(0{,}1/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 2{,}8236\) mm. With the undamped frequency it would be \(0{,}1/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2{,}8235\) mm, which is \(0{,}005\ \%\) less, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) and \(\zeta = 0{,}01\). The correct one is \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), because the difference of the roots \(2\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) appears in \(q\). SymPy checked that the function solves the equation and that \(q(0) = 0\), \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s. Up to here we have used no formula for vibration, only the chain.

9.4.5 All solutions of the homogeneous equation

What happens if a root repeats? For a double root the two functions \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\) would coincide and only one would remain, while there are two initial conditions. Let us see what the chain does. Take the same system A with critical damping, \(c = c_\mathrm{kr} = 2\sqrt{km}\) (section 9.1), so \(s_1 = s_2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = -35{,}4175\) 1/s. The chain is \(\dot u_1 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} u_1 = 0\) and \(\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q = u_1\). The first gives \(u_1 = u_1(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), and the second

\[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(q(0) + \int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\,u_1(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\big(q(0) + u_1(0)\,t\big) .\]

The exponential functions in the integrand cancel, because the roots are equal, and the constant \(u_1(0)\) remains, whose integral is \(t\). So a polynomial of degree one appears in front of \(\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). For a triple root the same would happen once more and give \(t^2\).

The proof will need two lemmas on the product of a polynomial and an exponential function.

Lemma 9.37 The operator on a product with an exponential (operator na zmnožku z eksponentno funkcijo)

Let \(s\) be a complex number and \(u\) a sufficiently often differentiable complex function. Then \(\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s\big)\big(\mathrm{e}^{st} u\big) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\) and therefore \(\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s\big)^m\big(\mathrm{e}^{st} u\big) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m)}\). If \(u\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\), the right side is zero.

Proof

We must show the first equality, the other two follow from it.

  1. By the product rule (lemma 1.81, applied to the real and imaginary parts, section 9.3) and theorem 9.28, \((\mathrm{e}^{st} u)' = s\,\mathrm{e}^{st} u + \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\), so \((\mathrm{e}^{st} u)' - s\,\mathrm{e}^{st} u = \mathrm{e}^{st} u'\).
  2. By induction on \(m\): if \((\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - s)^{m-1}(\mathrm{e}^{st} u) = \mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m-1)}\), applying step 1 to the function \(u^{(m-1)}\) gives \(\mathrm{e}^{st} u^{(m)}\).
  3. A polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\) has value zero after \(m\) derivatives (lemma 1.81, the derivative of the term \(t^l\) is \(l\,t^{l-1}\)).

Source: The proof is not from a source. KREYSZIG §3.2 (PDF pp. 140-141) derives the same claim, that \(x^j\mathrm{e}^{\lambda x}\) passes through \(m\) factors, differently, by differentiating with respect to a parameter \(\lambda\).

Lemma 9.38 Integral of a polynomial times an exponential (integral zmnožka polinoma in eksponentne funkcije)

Let \(s \ne 0\) be a complex number and \(p\) a polynomial of degree \(d\) with complex coefficients. Then \(r = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j)}/s^{j+1}\) is a polynomial of degree \(d\) with \((r\,\mathrm{e}^{st})' = p\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\), hence

\[\int_0^t p(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{su}\,\mathrm{d}u = r(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{st} - r(0) . \tag{9.35}\]

For \(s = 0\), \(\int_0^t p(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) is a polynomial of degree \(d + 1\).

Proof

We must show that \(r' + s r = p\), because then \((r\,\mathrm{e}^{st})' = (r' + s r)\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\).

  1. The derivative of the polynomial \(r\) is \(r' = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\), and the sum \(s r = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=0}^{d} (-1)^j\,p^{(j)}/s^{j}\).
  2. In the sum \(r' + s r\) all terms of the second sum except the first cancel against terms of the first: term \(j\) of the first sum is \((-1)^j p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\), and term \(j + 1\) of the second is \((-1)^{j+1} p^{(j+1)}/s^{j+1}\). What remains is \(p\) from term \(j = 0\) of the second sum, because \(p^{(d+1)} = 0\).
  3. The degree of the polynomial \(r\) is \(d\), because the term \(j = 0\), that is \(p/s\), contains the power \(t^d\) and the derivatives lower it. Equality (9.35) follows from part 1 of the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40). For \(s = 0\) the integral of the term \(t^l\) is \(t^{l+1}/(l+1)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The computation is repeated integration by parts, written as a check.

For \(s = 0\) the integral of a polynomial is a polynomial one degree higher, which in the example above gave \(t\).

For the theorem we need one more notion. The multiplicity (večkratnost) of a root \(s_*\) of the polynomial \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}}(s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) is the number of indices \(j\) for which \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = s_*\). We denote the distinct roots by \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) and their multiplicities by \(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{r}}\), so that \(m_1 + \dots + m_{\mathrm{r}} = n\). The multiplicity does not depend on the choice of factorization: if \(P(s) = (s - s_*)^{m}\,Q(s) = (s - s_*)^{m'}\,Q'(s)\) with \(Q(s_*) \ne 0 \ne Q'(s_*)\) and \(m < m'\), then after cancelling the factor \((s - s_*)^m\) at \(s \ne s_*\) the polynomials \(Q(s)\) and \((s - s_*)^{m'-m} Q'(s)\) agree at infinitely many points, so they have the same coefficients, and at \(s = s_*\) we get \(Q(s_*) = 0\), a contradiction.

Theorem 9.39 All solutions of the homogeneous equation (vse rešitve homogene enačbe)

Let \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) be the characteristic polynomial of the homogeneous equation \(a_{\mathrm{n}} y^{(n)} + \dots + a_0 y = 0\) with distinct roots \(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\) and multiplicities \(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{r}}\). A function \(y\) is a solution on \([0, T]\) exactly when

\[y(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{r} p_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} \tag{9.36}\]

for polynomials \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\) of degree at most \(m_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\) with complex coefficients. The coefficients of the polynomials are \(m_1 + \dots + m_{\mathrm{r}} = n\) complex numbers. For every initial values \(y(0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(0)\) they can be chosen so that the function (9.36) has these initial values.

Proof

We must show three things: that every such function is a solution, that every solution has this form, and that with the constants we reach every start. The first follows from the lemma on the operator, the second from the chain, the third from the theorem on uniqueness.

1. The functions (9.36) are solutions.

  1. We order the factors in equation (9.34) (lemma 9.35) so that the last ones are those with \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\). The function \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} p_{\mathrm{k}}\) passes through all \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) factors \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} - \sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) into zero (lemma 9.37, \(\deg p_{\mathrm{k}} \le m_{\mathrm{k}} - 1\)), and the remaining factors do not change zero, because they are linear. So every term is a solution.
  2. Since the operator \(P(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\) is linear (lemma 1.81), the sum of the terms is a solution too.

2. Every solution has this form. We prove it by induction on \(n\), for every set of roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}}\) at once.

  1. For \(n = 1\), by lemma 9.34 with \(f = 0\), the only solution with start \(y(0)\) is the function \(y(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\), which is the form (9.36) with one root and \(m_1 = 1\).
  2. Let \(n \ge 2\) and let the statement hold for order \(n - 1\). Let \(y\) be a solution and \(z = y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y\). As in step 3 of the proof of theorem 9.36, \(z\) is a solution of the homogeneous equation of order \(n - 1\) with roots \(s_1, \dots, s_{\mathrm{n}-1}\), so by the induction hypothesis \(z = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} q_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the distinct roots of this set and \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) are polynomials of degree below their multiplicity in it. We denote the multiplicity of the root \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the full set by \(m\), in the shorter set it is \(m - 1\).
  3. The function \(y\) solves \(y' - s_{\mathrm{n}} y = z\), so by lemma 9.34
    \[y(t) = \mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\Big(y(0) + \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \int_0^t q_{\mathrm{k}}(u)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} - s_{\mathrm{n}})u}\,\mathrm{d}u\Big) .\]
  4. A term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} \ne s_{\mathrm{n}}\): lemma 9.38 gives the integral \(r_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} - s_{\mathrm{n}})t} - r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\) with a polynomial \(r_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the same degree as \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\). After multiplying by \(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) there remains \(r_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t} - r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). The multiplicity of the root \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\) in the full set equals that in the shorter set, so the degree of the polynomial \(r_{\mathrm{k}}\) is still below it.
  5. The term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} = s_{\mathrm{n}}\) (there is none if \(s_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not among the roots of the shorter set, that is for \(m = 1\)): the polynomial \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) has degree at most \(m - 2\), and its integral \(\int_0^t q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\) (lemma 9.38, for \(s = 0\)).
  6. In front of \(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) in \(y\) stand the numbers \(y(0)\) and \(-r_{\mathrm{k}}(0)\) and the integral from step 7. Together they form a polynomial of degree at most \(m - 1\), for \(m = 1\) a constant. All terms therefore have the form (9.36) with the multiplicities of the full set.

3. Initial values.

  1. By theorem 9.36, for every set \(y_0, \dots, y_{\mathrm{n}-1}\) there is exactly one solution. By part 2 it has the form (9.36) with some choice of coefficients of the polynomials \(p_{\mathrm{k}}\), so they can be chosen to give the given start.

Source: KREYSZIG §3.2 (7) (PDF p. 140) lists the functions \(x^j\mathrm{e}^{\lambda x}\), independence for real roots is shown on PDF p. 141, and §2.2 (PDF pp. 80-83) treats order \(2\). The proof is ours, with the chain in place of differentiation with respect to a parameter and of the Wronskian determinant.

The proof showed more than the theorem says: it showed where the polynomial in front of the exponential function comes from. Every repeated root adds an integral of a constant to the chain, and this raises the degree of the polynomial by one. We do not need the independence of the functions \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\): the theorem guarantees that the constants exist for every start, and their computation for concrete starts will be carried out for order \(2\) in section 9.5.

For critically damped system A we have \(r = 1\), \(\sigma_1 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(m_1 = 2\). The solution is \((C_1 + C_2 t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), which are exactly the functions that arose in the chain above, with \(C_1 = q(0)\) and \(C_2 = u_1(0) = \dot q(0) + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q(0)\).

9.4.6 Real solutions

The cantilever is real, its motion is real, and the coefficients are real. Yet the solution \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) is complex. How do we obtain the solutions with real values that we measure?

Corollary 9.40 Real solutions of the differential equation (realne rešitve)

Let the coefficients \(a_0, \dots, a_{\mathrm{n}}\) of the homogeneous equation be real. The non-real roots of the polynomial \(P\) occur in conjugate pairs \(\sigma \ne \overline{\sigma}\) with the same multiplicity. The real solutions of the equation are exactly the linear combinations with real coefficients of the functions \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) for real roots \(s\) and \(j < m\), and of the functions \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\cos\omega t\) and \(t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\sin\omega t\) for pairs of roots \(\alpha \pm \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega > 0\) and \(j < m\), where \(m\) is the multiplicity of the root.

Proof

We must show three things: that the conjugate root belongs to a pair with the same multiplicity, that the listed functions are real solutions, and that every real solution is their combination. The second and third follow from the theorem on the general solution and from the fact that the real and imaginary parts of a complex solution are real solutions.

  1. Since the \(a_{\mathrm{j}}\) are real, \(\overline{P(\overline{s})} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}} s^j = P(s)\) (conjugation preserves sums and products, lemma 9.20). From \(P(s) = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - s_1)\cdots(s - s_{\mathrm{n}})\) it therefore follows that \(P(s) = \overline{P(\overline{s})} = a_{\mathrm{n}} (s - \overline{s_1})\cdots(s - \overline{s_{\mathrm{n}}})\), because \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\) is real. This is a factorization with conjugate roots. The multiplicity does not depend on the factorization (the paragraph before theorem 9.39), so \(\overline{\sigma}\) has the same multiplicity as \(\sigma\).
  2. The function \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\) is a complex solution (theorem 9.39). By lemma 9.32 its real and imaginary parts are real solutions. For \(\sigma = \alpha + \mathrm{i}\omega\) these are, by the definition of the complex exponential function (definition 9.27), \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\cos\omega t\) and \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}\sin\omega t\). For real \(\sigma\) the function itself is real.
  3. Let \(y\) be a real solution. By theorem 9.39, \(y = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} p_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\), and since \(y = \operatorname{Re} y\), \(y = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \operatorname{Re}\big(p_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\). Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}'} = \overline{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}}\) be the conjugate root. Since \(\operatorname{Re} w = \operatorname{Re}\overline{w}\) and \(\overline{\mathrm{e}^{z}} = \mathrm{e}^{\overline{z}}\) (cosine is even, sine is odd, definition 9.27), \(\operatorname{Re}\big(p_{\mathrm{k}'}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}'} t}\big) = \operatorname{Re}\big(\overline{p_{\mathrm{k}'}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\), so the conjugate term together with the \(k\)-th gives \(\operatorname{Re}\big((p_{\mathrm{k}} + \overline{p_{\mathrm{k}'}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\big)\) with a polynomial of the same bounded degree (step 1).
  4. In each pair we choose the term with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} = \alpha + \mathrm{i}\omega\), \(\omega > 0\). For a polynomial with coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i} b_{\mathrm{j}}\) we have \(\operatorname{Re}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} (a_{\mathrm{j}} + \mathrm{i} b_{\mathrm{j}})\,t^j\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}(\cos\omega t + \mathrm{i}\sin\omega t)\big) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} t^j \mathrm{e}^{\alpha t}(a_{\mathrm{j}}\cos\omega t - b_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\omega t)\). This is a combination of the listed functions with real coefficients. For real \(\sigma_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\operatorname{Re}(p_{\mathrm{k}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\) is a combination of the functions \(t^j\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{\mathrm{k}} t}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §2.2 (PDF p. 83) for order \(2\) and §3.2 (PDF p. 141) for multiple complex roots. The proof is ours.

For system A with \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the polynomial \(P\) has real coefficients and roots \(-0{,}35418 \pm 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\), so its real solutions are \(\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\,(C_1\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + C_2\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\) with real \(C_1\), \(C_2\). For the hammer blow \(C_1 = 0\) and \(C_2 = 0{,}1/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) m, as the chain gave. For the double root of the previous section the roots are real, so the solution \((C_1 + C_2 t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is already of real form.

9.4.7 An example of order four: a beam on soft ground

Now we pay the promise from chapter 5. There, theorem 5.69 gave, for a beam on an elastic foundation, the equation \(EI\,v'''' - c\,v'' + k\,v = q\), where the term \(k\,v\) describes a foundation of stiffness \(k\) (force per unit length per unit deflection), and for \(c = 0\) there is no axial force. It did not solve the equation. Without load and without axial force this is \(EI\,v'''' + k\,v = 0\) with constant \(EI\). Theorem 9.36 with the variable \(x\) in place of \(t\) says that the four values \(v, v', v'', v'''\) at the point \(x = 0\) have exactly one solution, and theorem 9.39 says how to find it. A beam has conditions at both ends, not only at \(x = 0\): we choose the four constants of the solution so that we satisfy them (chapter 25).

Take the cantilever from chapter 5 with \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\) and a foundation of stiffness \(k = 1{,}0\cdot 10^{6}\) N/m\(^2\) (our choice for illustration). The equation is \(v'''' + 4\beta^4 v = 0\) with \(4\beta^4 = k/EI = 47{,}832\) 1/m\(^4\), so \(\beta = 1{,}8596\) 1/m. The characteristic polynomial is \(P(s) = s^4 + 4\beta^4\). It has no real roots, because it is positive for real \(s\). We factor it with a difference of squares:

\[s^4 + 4\beta^4 = (s^2 + 2\beta^2)^2 - (2\beta s)^2 = (s^2 + 2\beta s + 2\beta^2)(s^2 - 2\beta s + 2\beta^2)\]

(SymPy checked the equality). The quadratic formula with discriminant \(4\beta^2 - 8\beta^2 = -4\beta^2\) gives roots \(-\beta \pm \mathrm{i}\beta\) for the first factor and \(\beta \pm \mathrm{i}\beta\) for the second (section 9.3). The four roots

\[s = \beta\,(\pm 1 \pm \mathrm{i})\]

are simple. By corollary 9.40 the real solutions are combinations of the four functions (figure 9.12)

\[\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\cos\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\sin\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\cos\beta x , \quad \mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\sin\beta x .\]

The measure of change is \(1/\beta = 0{,}538\) m: the decaying solutions shrink by a factor \(\mathrm{e}\) over this distance, and over the half-wavelength of the oscillation \(\pi/\beta = 1{,}689\) m by a factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi} = 0{,}0432\).

The same procedure gives for the polynomial \(s^4 - \beta^4 = (s - \beta)(s + \beta)(s - \mathrm{i}\beta)(s + \mathrm{i}\beta)\) the roots \(\pm\beta\) and \(\pm\mathrm{i}\beta\) and the solutions \(\mathrm{e}^{\beta x}\), \(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta x}\), \(\cos\beta x\) and \(\sin\beta x\) (in place of the first two we may choose their half sum and half difference, which we shall do for \(\mathrm{e}^{\pm\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) in section 9.5). Chapter 13 will have the equation \(v'''' - \beta^4 v = 0\) for a vibrating beam, here we only name it.

Two tools are only named here. The fundamental theorem of algebra (LEBL2 theorem 11.5.5, PDF p. 170) says that every non-constant complex polynomial has a root, so a factorization always exists. Theorem 9.39 does not need it, because we factored all polynomials explicitly. The Laplace transform (KREYSZIG chapter 6, CK §5.6, PDF pp. 154-160) solves the same equations with another tool. It would require infinite complex integrals and an inversion theorem, while factorization and convolution (section 9.10) give the same results with tools the book already has.
