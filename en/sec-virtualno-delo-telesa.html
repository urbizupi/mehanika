Chapter 4 Energy principles and virtual work

4.5 The principle of virtual displacements for a body

In section 4.4 we wrote the equilibrium of a bar in a new form. The bar was fixed at the end \(x = 0\), a force \(q(x)\) per unit length acted along it in the direction of the axis, and a force \(F\) acted at the free end \(x = L\). Instead of the equation \(N' + q = 0\) at every point and the condition \(N(L) = F\) at the end we wrote a single equation,

\[\int_0^L N\,\delta u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,\delta u\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L) ,\]

which holds for every virtual displacement of the bar \(\delta u\), that is, for every function with a continuous derivative and with \(\delta u(0) = 0\) (theorem 4.76 on the principle of virtual displacements for a bar). On the left is the work of the axial forces on the virtual elongations of the pieces of the bar, on the right the work of the external forces on the virtual displacements. The converse holds too: if the equation holds for every such \(\delta u\), the bar is in equilibrium (theorem 4.78).

The goal of this section is the same for a body of any shape in space. In chapter 2 we described the equilibrium of a body by the three equilibrium equations (2.31) at every interior point, by the symmetry of stress (2.37) and by the condition that on the loaded part of the surface the traction vector equals the force per unit area with which the surroundings act on the body. We will replace all of this by a single equation that must hold for every virtual displacement the supports allow. Why would we want that? For three reasons. The new equation contains no derivative of the stresses, only first partial derivatives of the virtual displacement, and the finite element method of chapters 6 and 7 stands on it. Its derivation needs no property of the material, so the equation holds for every material. And from it we will derive the theorems on energy in the next sections.

The route is as follows. First we set up the model precisely: the body, its supports and loads, and the assumptions. Then we say which virtual displacements are admissible. With a picture of a body built from small boxes we explain why the work of the stresses on the virtual strains must equal the work of the external forces on the virtual displacements. Then we prove it with the divergence theorem, which plays the role of integration by parts in space. The converse follows: if the equation holds for all virtual displacements the supports allow, the equilibrium equations and the condition on the surface hold. At the end we test the principle on the cube in pure shear and on the hanging bar.

4.5.1 The body, its supports and its loads

Take a body that before loading occupies the set of points \(V\), with surface \(S\), and a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with axes \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) (figure 4.64). We must say how the body is held and what acts on it. We distinguish three kinds of forces.

  1. Supports. On a part of the surface, which we denote by \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the displacement is prescribed. Such a part is for example the bottom face of a cube glued to a table, like the sheared cube in section 1.6.2. There the displacement is zero. In general a displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) is prescribed on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), which need not be zero, for example at a support that settles by a known distance. The forces with which the supports act on the body are not known in advance. They are exactly what is needed for the points of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) to move by the prescribed displacement.
  2. Prescribed surface forces. On the rest of the surface, which we denote by \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), we know the force per unit area with which the surroundings act on the body. We denote it by \(\bar{\vek t}\). Examples are the pressure of water on a wall or the even pull on the top face of the hanging bar. Where nothing acts on the surface, \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\), and this too is a prescribed force. The bar over the letter says here that the quantity is prescribed (predpisan), and has nothing to do with the bar for turned axes in section 2.9.
  3. Body force. A force with density \(\vek b\) acts on every bit of the material (definition 2.26), for example the weight with density \(\rho g\).

The parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) do not overlap and together make up the whole surface (SADD §5.2, PDF p. 104, EP §4.2.2, p. 168). At every point of the surface we therefore know either the displacement or the force per unit area, not both. This is a simplification. For a body that slides along a smooth rigid wall, at the same point the component of the displacement perpendicular to the wall is prescribed, and so is the component of the force along the wall, which is zero (SADD §5.2, PDF p. 104). We do not treat such supports in this section.

The model makes four more assumptions. For each we say why it is justified and where we will use it.

  1. Small deformations. The body deforms so little that we do not distinguish its shape before and after loading. We therefore write all integrals over the body \(V\) and the surface \(S\) before loading, with the outward normal of the surface before loading. In steel structures the dimensions change by less than a thousandth (section 1.17), and we already proceeded this way for the equilibrium equations in section 2.7. For large deformations the forces act on the body in its deformed shape, and we would have to write the integrals over the deformed body or use the stresses referred to the original shape from section 2.13 (BOWER §2.4.5, PDF pp. 88-89).
  2. Equilibrium. The body is at rest. At every interior point the stress satisfies the equilibrium equations (2.31), at every point it is symmetric (theorem 2.36 on the symmetry of stress), and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the traction vector given by Cauchy's formula equals the prescribed force, \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) (equation (2.42)), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal. These are the conditions we will rewrite as one equation.
  3. No jumps. The stress components are continuously differentiable (definition 1.90) on an open set that contains the body together with its surface. So the stress never changes with a jump, not even right next to the surface. The body force density \(\vek b\) is continuous on the body, the prescribed force \(\bar{\vek t}\) on each outer face of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) separately. On an edge where two faces with different normals meet it may have two values, as on the edge between the top face of the cube in pure shear, which we push to the right, and its right face, which we pull up (section 2.6.6). We need this so that the integrals exist and so that we may use the divergence theorem. Point forces and the contact of two materials are thereby left out (section 1.15). The assumption excludes two more things. First, a load that acts on part of a flat face only and drops to zero at the edge of that part. The normal is the same on both sides of such an edge, so a continuous stress gives the same traction \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n\) on both sides, and \(\bar{\vek t}\) cannot jump there. Second, a re-entrant corner (vbočeno oglišče), where two faces meet at an angle larger than \(180^\circ\), measured through the body. There the stress of the true solution may be unbounded (BARBER §11.2.3, PDF p. 175). In the figures of this chapter we draw loads on part of a face as an idealization. An exact treatment would replace them by loads that fall continuously to zero at the edge of the patch, as we will write them with weights in section 4.8.5.
  4. Shape. For the proof that we write out in full, let the body consist of cells of one grid, that is, let it be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), and let \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) consist of whole outer faces of these cells. A box is the simplest such body. For bodies made of boxes we have proved the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14). For bodies cut into pieces between graphs, like the round hanging bar, we proved it in section 4.2.12, and for bodies cut into pieces between graphs and curved boxes, like a ball and a thick spherical shell, in section 4.3.7. At the end of section 4.5.4 we say what changes in the proofs for them. All the bodies of our examples are of this kind.

We have assumed nothing about the material. We will nowhere need the relation between stresses and strains from chapter 3, nor that the stresses come from the displacements of the body.

Two bodies we already know will accompany the section. The first is the steel cube with edge \(h = 10\) mm in pure shear from section 2.6.6, glued to a table with its bottom face, as in section 3.2.5. The origin is at the bottom front left corner \(A\), the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(y\) into the depth and the axis \(z\) up. The bottom face is \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), the displacement is zero there. We push the top face to the right, pull the right face up and the left face down, all with the force \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa per unit area. The front and back faces are free. These five faces are \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). The stress is the same at all points, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\), the other components are zero, and we neglect the weight, because it changes the stress by only \(765\) Pa, as we saw in section 2.7. On the top face with normal \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) Cauchy's formula gives \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is indeed the prescribed force pushing to the right. On the bottom face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) it gives \(-\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). This is the force of the glue, which pulls to the left. In this example we know it because we know the stress, but in general we do not know the force of a support in advance.

The second body is the steel hanging bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a round cross section of diameter 10 mm, with the axis \(x\) vertically down from the centre \(O\) of the top face. It is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: a force spread evenly over the top face pulls it up, and the point \(O\) is fixed, with the material right next to it not turning, only so that the bar cannot move or turn as a whole (CM, PDF p. 306). The weight is carried by the pull on the top face, so in this model there is no force at the point \(O\). The whole surface is therefore \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\): on the top face \(\bar{\vek t} = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the lateral surface and the bottom face are free, \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\). The part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty. The body force is the weight, \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the only stress component that is not zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). On the top face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) Cauchy's formula gives \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(0) \cdot (-1), 0, 0) = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the prescribed upward pull.

4.5.2 Admissible virtual displacements

In section 4.4 a virtual displacement was an imagined small displacement that the supports allow, during which the forces stay as they are (definition 4.70). The sign \(\delta\) in front of a quantity means a virtual quantity, as we agreed in section 4.4.1. For a body a virtual displacement is a field. Its input is the three coordinates of a point of the body before loading, its output three numbers \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\delta u_{\mathrm{z}}\), the components of the vector by which we imagine the point to move. Which fields do we allow?

Definition 4.79 Kinematically admissible virtual displacement (kinematično dopusten virtualni pomik)

Let a body occupy the set of points \(V\) before loading, and let the displacement be prescribed on the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of its surface. A kinematically admissible virtual displacement (kinematično dopusten virtualni pomik) is a vector field \(\delta\vek u = (\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}, \delta u_{\mathrm{y}}, \delta u_{\mathrm{z}})\) that is continuously differentiable (definition 1.90) on an open set containing the body together with its surface, and equal to the zero vector at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). Its virtual strain (virtualna deformacija) at a point of the body is the symmetric matrix \(\delta\ten\varepsilon\) with entries

\[\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac12\Big(\frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big) ,\]

where the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), and \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the coordinate named \(j\): \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\).

Source: SADD §6.5 (PDF p. 137), EP §4.2.2 (pp. 168-169), BOWER §2.4.4 (PDF p. 85) and §2.4.6 (PDF pp. 89-90).

The word kinematically says that the condition is about displacements only, not about forces. BOWER writes that kinematically admissible is just a complicated way of saying that the field is continuous, differentiable and zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) (BOWER, PDF p. 85). Why exactly these two conditions? We explain each by what we will use it for.

Why must the virtual displacement be zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\)? We do not know the forces of the supports. If the points of \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) do not move during the virtual displacement, these forces do no work, and they do not appear in the equation we will derive. We will get an equation that contains only known forces. On \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), on the other hand, the virtual displacement can be anything. What if a displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\) that is not zero is prescribed on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\)? The virtual displacement is zero there all the same. A virtual displacement is an imagined change from the true state, and on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) the support allows no change of the displacement.

Why must it be continuously differentiable? From its partial derivatives we compute the virtual strain, which we need at every point of the body, and the continuity of the partial derivatives is needed by the divergence theorem, which we will use in the proof. Physically the condition says that the material does not tear anywhere during the imagined displacement.

The virtual strain is the small strain tensor (equation (1.51)) computed from the field \(\delta\vek u\) instead of from the displacement field \(\vek u\). If \(\delta\mat H\) is the displacement gradient of the field \(\delta\vek u\) (definition 1.109), with entries \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}\), then \(\delta\ten\varepsilon = \tfrac12\,(\delta\mat H + \delta\mat H^\T)\). Since we think of the virtual displacement as small, by section 1.17 this is the strain that the imagined displacement would cause, up to negligible terms.

Must the virtual displacement be small? The definition does not require it. In the equation we will derive, every term contains a component of the field \(\delta\vek u\) or its partial derivative exactly once. If we multiply the field by a number, both sides are therefore multiplied by the same number. The size does not matter. We need smallness only for the mental picture: during a small imagined displacement the shape of the body and the forces on it do not change noticeably.

Let us test the definition on the glued cube (figure 4.65). The part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the bottom face, where \(z = 0\).

  • The field \(\delta\vek u = (\gamma z, 0, 0)\), where \(\gamma\) is a number, is continuously differentiable, because its components are polynomials, and it is zero at \(z = 0\). It is therefore kinematically admissible. The only partial derivative that is not zero is \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = \gamma\), so \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tfrac12\,(\gamma + 0) = \gamma/2\), and all other virtual strains are zero. For \(\gamma = 0{,}001\) this field has the same shape as the true field of the sheared cube (1.16), only here we merely imagine it.
  • The field \(\delta\vek u = (\delta a, 0, 0)\) moves the whole cube by \(\delta a\) in the direction of the axis \(x\). It is continuously differentiable, all its partial derivatives are zero, so the virtual strain is zero. But it is not kinematically admissible, because it also moves the glued bottom face.
  • A field that moves the upper half of the cube by \(\delta a\) and the lower half not at all is not continuous, because it has a jump on the plane \(z = h/2\). It is not kinematically admissible, because the material would tear there.

For the hanging bar \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty, so every continuously differentiable virtual displacement is admissible. Moving the whole bar down by \(\delta a\) is admissible too. The point \(O\) is fixed only against a motion of the bar as a whole and carries no force in the model, so moving the point \(O\) adds no work.

Among the admissible fields, those that only move or turn the body as if it were rigid are especially simple. For a move by a constant vector all partial derivatives are zero. For a small rotation about the axis \(y\), for example \(\delta\vek u = \theta\,(z, 0, -x)\) with a number \(\theta\), we have \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z = \theta\) and \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x = -\theta\), so \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \tfrac12\,(\theta - \theta) = 0\), and all other virtual strains are zero too. These fields do not deform the body. For the cube they are not admissible, because they move the bottom face, for the hanging bar they are.

4.5.3 Why the internal work equals the external work

When the body moves by an admissible virtual displacement, the forces on it do virtual work. A force \(\vek F\) whose point of application has the virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\) does the virtual work \(\vek F\cdot\delta\vek u\) (definition 4.71). The body force on a small piece of volume \(\Delta V\) is approximately \(\vek b\,\Delta V\), and when the piece moves by \(\delta\vek u\), it does work of approximately \(\vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\Delta V\). The prescribed force on a small patch of area \(\Delta A\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) does work of approximately \(\bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\Delta A\). The forces of the supports do no work, because on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) the virtual displacement is zero. When we add up over all pieces and patches and make them smaller, we get integrals. The stresses, in turn, do work on the virtual strain. In section 3.6 we computed that the forces on the faces of a small box of volume \(\Delta V\) do the work \(\Delta V \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) when the strain of the box changes slightly by \(\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) while the stress stays nearly the same. A move and a rotation of the box as a whole did no work. Here the change of strain is the virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). For the bar we called the work of the axial forces on the virtual elongations the internal virtual work and the work of the loads the external virtual work (section 4.4). For a body we define them as follows.

Definition 4.80 Internal and external virtual work of a body (notranje in zunanje virtualno delo telesa)

Let a body occupy the set of points \(V\), let there be supports on the part \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) of its surface, let a force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\) be prescribed on the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), let a body force with density \(\vek b\) act on the material, and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) be the stresses in the body. Let \(\delta\vek u\) be a kinematically admissible virtual displacement with virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 4.79). The internal virtual work (notranje virtualno delo) is the number

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V ,\]

and the external virtual work (zunanje virtualno delo) is the number

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A .\]

The indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Source: SADD (6.5.1), (6.5.3) (PDF pp. 137-138), EP (4.2.2), (4.2.3) (p. 169).

The double sum has nine terms. Since the stress and the virtual strain are symmetric matrices, the terms with swapped indices are equal, for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yx}}\), and the sum has six different terms:

\[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} + 2\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}} .\]

SADD writes it with engineering shear strains, where the factor two hides in \(\delta\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (SADD (6.5.3), PDF p. 138). The internal virtual work has the unit J, like every work: a stress in Pa times a virtual strain without a unit times a volume in m³ gives N·m.

Why is there no factor one half, as in the strain energy density \(w = \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\varepsilon\) of the steel bar in section 3.6? There the stress grew from zero to its final value during loading, together with the strain, and the average stress was half the final one. During a virtual displacement, however, the stresses do not change. They are the true stresses in equilibrium, and the virtual strain is added to them without changing them. So the work is simply the stress times the virtual strain (SADD, PDF p. 138).

Now the question the whole section answers. Why should the internal virtual work equal the external one? The answer is shown by a picture of a body built from small boxes like a wall from bricks (figure 4.66). Every box is in equilibrium. Let us add up the virtual work of all forces on all boxes, and do it in two ways.

First, face by face. Take the shared face of two neighbouring boxes. The force with which the right box acts on the left one across this face is, by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, opposite to the force with which the left box acts on the right one. But during the virtual displacement the face moves in the same way for both boxes, because the field \(\delta\vek u\) is continuous. The works of the two forces are therefore opposite numbers and cancel in the sum. What remains are only the forces on faces that lie on the surface of the body, and the body forces. Their work is the external virtual work, since the forces of the supports do no work.

Second, box by box. The virtual displacement of a small box is close to a sum of four simple parts (figure 4.67): a move of the box as a whole, a rotation of the box as a whole, an elongation along an axis, and a shear. In the move all points of the box move in the same way, and the work of all forces on the box is the scalar product of their resultant with this move. The resultant is zero, because the box is in equilibrium. In the rotation the work of all forces is the scalar product of the sum of their moments with the rotation vector, by theorem 4.73 on the principle of virtual displacements for a rigid body, and the sum of the moments is zero, because the box is in equilibrium. What remains is the work on the deformation of the box, which by section 3.6 is approximately \(\Delta V \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\). The sum over the boxes is the internal virtual work.

Both ways add up the same work, so the two sums are equal: the external virtual work equals the internal one. In this argument we used both conditions of equilibrium of every box, zero resultant and zero sum of moments, and the fact that two neighbouring boxes move in the same way on their shared face. This is the idea, not a proof. It contains too many approximations: the boxes have a finite size, the virtual displacement on a box is only approximately made of four parts, the stress is not the same everywhere in a box. The exact route is given by the divergence theorem. In it the integral takes over the role of the sum over boxes, and the cancelling of the forces on inner faces is exactly what we saw in the proof of the divergence theorem for a body made of boxes in section 4.2.

4.5.4 The principle of virtual displacements

First let us prove an equality that we will need twice, for the principle and for its converse. For the bar, integration by parts (corollary 3.41),

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,g'\,\mathrm{d}x = f(b)\,g(b) - f(a)\,g(a) - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f'\,g\,\mathrm{d}x ,\]

was the tool that moved the derivative from the function \(g\) to the function \(f\) and gave terms at the end points in exchange. In space the stress plays the role of the function \(f\), the virtual displacement the role of the function \(g\), and the surface of the body replaces the end points of the interval.

Lemma 4.81 Internal virtual work through the surface (notranje virtualno delo prek površine)

Let \(V\) be a body made of boxes (definition 4.11) with surface \(S\) and outward unit normal \(\vek n\). Let the stress components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and the components of the field \(\delta\vek u\) be continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body together with its surface, and let the stress be symmetric at every point, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\). Let \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \tfrac12\,(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}})\). Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t(\vek n)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V , \tag{4.50}\]

where \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is the traction vector at the points of the surface, and the indices \(i\) and \(j\) run over \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).

Proof

We must show that the internal virtual work splits into the work of the traction vector on the surface and a volume integral with derivatives of the stresses. Using the symmetry of stress and the product rule, we first write the integrand on the left as a sum of partial derivatives of some field plus a remainder, then we apply the divergence theorem to the sum of partial derivatives.

  1. By the definition of the numbers \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and by multiplying out the product with the sum, at every point
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + \frac12 \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  2. In the first double sum we swap the names of the indices, writing \(i\) instead of \(j\) and \(j\) instead of \(i\). The sum runs over the same nine pairs, so it does not change, and it equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\,\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\). By symmetry \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\), so the first double sum equals the second one, and
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
    For the pair of indices \(x\), \(y\) this means \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\big(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\big) = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x\), which are the terms of the right-hand sum with the pairs \((i, j) = (y, x)\) and \((x, y)\).
  3. For every pair of indices \(i\), \(j\) the expression \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\) is at every point a product of two functions. If we hold fixed the coordinates other than \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), it is a function of one variable, and by the product rule (lemma 1.81, item 3)
    \[\frac{\partial\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}})}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} = \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\frac{\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}} .\]
  4. For \(i = x, y, z\) let \(v_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\). So for example \(v_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot\delta\vek u\) by (2.23). We sum the equation of step 3 over \(j\) and over \(i\). The partial derivative of a sum is the sum of the partial derivatives (lemma 1.81, item 1), so from steps 2 and 3 we get at every point
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z} - \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} .\]
  5. The field \(\vek v = (v_{\mathrm{x}}, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\) is continuously differentiable on the open set of the hypothesis. A continuously differentiable function is continuous (lemma 1.91). By step 3 the partial derivatives of the components of \(\vek v\) are sums of products of continuous functions, so they are continuous by corollary 1.69 on the continuity of sums, products and polynomials in space, item 1. For the same reason all functions in step 4 are continuous.
  6. We integrate the equality of step 4 over \(V\). The integral over a body made of boxes is the sum of the integrals over the cells (definition 4.11), and on every cell the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals (lemma 4.10 on the properties of the integral over a box, item 1). We get
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \Big(\frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}V - \int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
  7. By the divergence theorem 4.14, which we may use because by step 5 \(\vek v\) is continuously differentiable,
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \Big(\frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{z}}}{\partial z}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{S}} (v_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
  8. At every point of the surface
    \[v_{\mathrm{x}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} .\]
    By Cauchy's formula in components (2.24), \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,n_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the component \(j\) of the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), for example \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). Therefore \(v_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{x}} + v_{\mathrm{y}} n_{\mathrm{y}} + v_{\mathrm{z}} n_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek t(\vek n)\cdot\delta\vek u\), and because of the symmetry \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) (equation (2.42)).
  9. Inserting steps 7 and 8 into step 6 we get (4.50).

Source: SADD (6.5.2) (PDF p. 137), EP (4.2.3) (p. 169), BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86). The sources do these steps for a general body and do not separate them into a lemma.

The lemma is integration by parts in space. On the left the components of the virtual displacement are differentiated, on the right the components of the stress, and the surface integral plays the role of the terms at the end points. We used the symmetry of stress only in step 2. Without it, the left side would contain the partial derivatives \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\) instead of the virtual strain. The field \(\vek v\) has a simple meaning: \(v_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the work of the force per unit area on the face with normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the virtual displacement, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot\delta\vek u\), and likewise \(v_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Now the principle itself follows. We get it from the lemma with the equilibrium equations and the two conditions on the surface.

Theorem 4.82 Principle of virtual displacements (načelo virtualnih pomikov)

Let the body before loading occupy a set of points \(V\) that is a body made of boxes (definition 4.11), and let its surface \(S\) be divided into parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) that do not overlap and consist of whole outer faces of cells. Let the displacement be prescribed on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), a force per unit area \(\bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) that is continuous on each outer face of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and let a body force with density \(\vek b\), continuous on \(V\), act on the material. Assume:

  1. the stress components are continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body together with its surface,
  2. at every interior point of the body the equilibrium equations (2.31) hold,
  3. at every point of the body the stress is symmetric, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}\),
  4. at every point of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal.

Then for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement \(\delta\vek u\) with virtual strain \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) (definition 4.79) the internal virtual work equals the external one (definition 4.80):

\[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A . \tag{4.51}\]
Proof

We must show that in the equality (4.50) the volume integral on the right turns into the work of the body force and the surface integral into the work of the prescribed force.

  1. By assumption 1 the stress components are continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body with its surface, by definition 4.79 so are the components of the field \(\delta\vek u\), and by assumption 3 the stress is symmetric. By lemma 4.81 on the internal virtual work through the surface, (4.50) therefore holds.
  2. With indices the equilibrium equations (2.31) read \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(j = x, y, z\). For \(j = x\) this is the first equation, \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z + b_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). By assumption 2, at every interior point
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -\sum_{\mathrm{j}} b_{\mathrm{j}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = -\vek b\cdot\delta\vek u .\]
  3. The equality also holds at the points of the surface. Let \(Q\) be a point of the surface and \(C\) the centre of a cell of the body that contains \(Q\). The points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QC}\) for \(n = 2, 3, \dots\) lie in the interior of this cell, because each of their coordinates is strictly between the bounds of the cell, and they converge to \(Q\), because \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}} Q| = |QC|/n\) (definition 1.65). Both sides of the equality of step 2 are continuous functions of the point, the first by step 5 of the proof of the lemma, the second because \(\vek b\) is continuous. By lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points, the values of both sides at the points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) converge to the values at \(Q\). The values are equal at every point \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\), so by the uniqueness of the limit of a sequence (lemma 1.41) they are equal at \(Q\) too.
  4. The function under the second volume integral in (4.50) and \(-\vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\) are therefore equal at every point of the body, so their integrals are equal too:
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \Big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big)\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V = -\int_{\mathrm{V}} \vek b\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
  5. The surface integral is the sum of the integrals over the outer faces (definition 4.11, item 3). Every outer face belongs to \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) or to \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), so the surface integral is the sum of the integrals over \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). On \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(\delta\vek u = \vek 0\) by definition 4.79, so there \(\vek t(\vek n)\cdot\delta\vek u = 0\), and the integral over \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is zero. On \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\vek t(\vek n) = \ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) by assumption 4. Therefore
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}} \vek t(\vek n)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A .\]
  6. We insert steps 4 and 5 into (4.50). The minus in front of the volume integral and the minus of step 4 give a plus, and we get (4.51).

Source: SADD (6.5.1), (6.5.2) (PDF p. 137), EP (4.2.2), (4.2.3) (p. 169), BOWER §2.4.4.1 (PDF p. 86) and §2.4.6 (PDF pp. 89-90). The sources prove the theorem for a general body with the divergence theorem. Step 3 is not from the sources.

This is the central result of the chapter, so let us remember it. The proof showed how equilibrium turns into an equality of works. The lemma moved the derivative from the virtual displacement to the stress. The equilibrium equations turned the volume integral with derivatives of the stress into the work of the body force. On the surface two conditions acted: on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) the virtual displacement is zero, so the forces of the supports do no work, and on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the traction vector equals the prescribed force. The second condition of equilibrium, the symmetry of stress, entered in step 2 of the lemma, where it allowed us to write the virtual strain instead of the partial derivatives of the virtual displacement. This is the exact form of the argument with boxes: a rotation of a box does no work, because the sum of the moments is zero.

What is not in the theorem? There is no true displacement field \(\vek u\) and no relation between stresses and strains. The stress need only be in equilibrium, and the virtual displacement is an arbitrary admissible field that has no connection with the stress. That is why the principle holds for every material, linear elastic, nonlinear elastic or one that deforms permanently. EP stresses this by saying that the principle is independent of any constitutive law, that is, of any relation between stresses and strains (EP §4.2.2, p. 169).

What about a body with a curved surface, for example the round hanging bar? The proof of the lemma used the divergence theorem in step 7 and the linearity of the integral over the body in step 6, while all other steps hold at every point separately. For a body cut into pieces between graphs (definition 4.21) we have both: the linearity by lemma 4.22 and the divergence theorem by corollary 4.40. The lemma therefore holds for it too, with steps 1 to 9 unchanged. The surface integral in it is then the sum of the integrals over the outer pieces of the surface (definition 4.36). The proof of the principle used the cells of the body in two more places. In step 3 it reached every point of the surface by a sequence of interior points, and in step 5 it split the surface into whole outer faces. For a body cut into pieces we therefore also require that \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) consist of whole outer pieces and that every point of the body is the limit of a sequence of its interior points. With these the principle holds too.

For the round bar we check the second requirement as follows. Let \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\) be a point of the bar and \(M = (L/2, 0, 0)\) a point on its axis. The point \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QM}\) has the coordinates \(\big((1 - \tfrac1n)\,x_{\mathrm{Q}} + \tfrac{L}{2n},\ (1 - \tfrac1n)\,y_{\mathrm{Q}},\ (1 - \tfrac1n)\,z_{\mathrm{Q}}\big)\). Its coordinate \(x\) lies between \(L/(2n)\) and \(L - L/(2n)\), so strictly between \(0\) and \(L\), and the sum \(y^2 + z^2\) is \((1 - \tfrac1n)^2\,(y_{\mathrm{Q}}^2 + z_{\mathrm{Q}}^2) < r^2\). The functions \(x\) and \(y^2 + z^2\) are continuous functions of the point (corollary 1.69). Continuity at the point \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 1.67), written with an error smaller than the distance to the bound, gives a band around \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) in which the strict inequalities still hold. The points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}}\) are thus interior points of the bar, and they converge to \(Q\), because \(|Q_{\mathrm{n}} Q| = |QM|/n\). For the bar \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) is the whole surface. The lemma and the principle of virtual displacements therefore hold also for the round hanging bar, cut into the pieces \(C\), \(D\) and \(E\) of section 4.2.6.

The same holds for a body cut into pieces between graphs and curved boxes (section 4.3.7). The linearity of the integral over it is given by lemma 4.32, item 1, the divergence theorem by corollary 4.68, the surface integral is the sum of the fluxes and integrals over the outer pieces of the surface, and the requirements on the parts \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) and on interior points stay the same. For a ball with the centre \(O\) and the radius \(R\) the second requirement is met by the points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QO}\), which are at the distance \((1 - \tfrac1n)\,|\overrightarrow{OQ}| < R\) from \(O\) and are therefore, by the same argument as for the bar, interior points of the ball. For a thick spherical shell with the inner radius \(R_1\) and the outer radius \(R_2\) we take for \(M\) the point on the ray from \(O\) through \(Q\) at the distance \((R_1 + R_2)/2\) from \(O\). The points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QM}\) lie on the same ray, at the distance \((1 - \tfrac1n)\,|\overrightarrow{OQ}| + \tfrac1n\,(R_1 + R_2)/2\) from \(O\), which is strictly between \(R_1\) and \(R_2\). For the ring of section 4.3.7, the set of the points with \(40 \le \rho \le 60\) and \(|z| \le c\), where \(\rho\) is the distance from the \(z\) axis in millimetres, we take for \(M\) the point \((50\,x_{\mathrm{Q}}/\rho_{\mathrm{Q}},\ 50\,y_{\mathrm{Q}}/\rho_{\mathrm{Q}},\ 0)\), which is \(50\) mm from the axis in the same direction as \(Q\). The points \(Q_{\mathrm{n}} = Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QM}\) have \(|z| = (1 - \tfrac1n)\,|z_{\mathrm{Q}}| < c\), and their distance from the axis is \((1 - \tfrac1n)\,\rho_{\mathrm{Q}} + \tfrac1n \cdot 50\), which lies strictly between \(40\) and \(60\), so by the same argument as for the bar they are interior points of the ring. The lemma and the principle therefore hold also for the ball, the thick spherical shell and the ring.

4.5.5 The converse of the principle

In the finite element method we do not know the stresses. We look for them by requiring the equation (4.51) for many virtual displacements. Is the body then really in equilibrium? For the bar we showed in section 4.4 that it is, with the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations (lemma 4.77). It says that a continuous function whose integral with every admissible function is zero is itself zero. In space we need the same, only with a bump in a box instead of on an interval. The idea is simple. If a continuous function \(g\) is positive at some point \(P\), it is positive in a small box around \(P\) too. Take a function that is positive in this box and zero outside it. The integral of the product with \(g\) is then positive, not zero.

We build the bump from three bumps of the kind we used in the proof of the fundamental lemma 4.77. For numbers \(\alpha < \beta\) let

\[p_{\alpha\beta}(t) = (t - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - t)^2 \quad \text{for } \alpha \le t \le \beta , \qquad p_{\alpha\beta}(t) = 0 \quad \text{for other } t .\]

Between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) both factors are positive, so \(p_{\alpha\beta}\) is positive there, and at the end points and outside it is zero (figure 4.68, left). The squares in the factors make the graph touch the axis without a kink, with zero derivative. The box \(K = [\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\) is the set of points with \(\alpha_1 \le x \le \beta_1\), \(\alpha_2 \le y \le \beta_2\) and \(\alpha_3 \le z \le \beta_3\). The product \(p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,p_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\) is positive in the interior of the box \(K\) and zero outside it (figure 4.68, right).

Lemma 4.83 Fundamental lemma for boxes and rectangles (osnovna lema za kvadre in pravokotnike)

For numbers \(\alpha < \beta\) let \(p_{\alpha\beta}(t) = (t - \alpha)^2\,(\beta - t)^2\) for \(\alpha \le t \le \beta\) and \(p_{\alpha\beta}(t) = 0\) for other \(t\).

  1. The function \(p_{\alpha\beta}\) is differentiable at every real number and its derivative is continuous. Therefore, for every box \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\), the function \(\varphi(x, y, z) = p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,p_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\) is continuously differentiable in all of space.
  2. Let \(g\) be a function of three variables, continuous on an open set \(D\). If for every box \(K = [\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\) that lies in \(D\)
    \[\int_{\mathrm{K}} g(x, y, z)\,p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,p_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\,\mathrm{d}V = 0 ,\]
    then \(g = 0\) at every point of \(D\).
  3. Let \(R\) be the rectangle \([a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2]\) in the plane \(z = c\) and \(f\) a function of the point, continuous on \(R\). If for all numbers \(a_1 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < b_1\) and \(a_2 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < b_2\)
    \[\int_{\alpha_1}^{\beta_1} \int_{\alpha_2}^{\beta_2} f(x, y, c)\,p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 ,\]
    then \(f = 0\) at every point of \(R\).
Proof

We must show three items. In the first we rely on the proof of the fundamental lemma, in the other two we derive a contradiction from a point where the function is not zero, with a bump around this point.

1. Derivative of the bump.

  1. The function \(p_{\alpha\beta}\) is the function \(\psi\) of step 4 of the proof of the fundamental lemma 4.77. In steps 5 to 7 of that proof we showed that it is differentiable at every real number and that its derivative is continuous. On the interval \((\alpha, \beta)\) the derivative equals \(2\,(t - \alpha)\,(\beta - t)\,(\alpha + \beta - 2t)\), at the points \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) and outside the interval \([\alpha, \beta]\) it is zero.
  2. The partial derivative of the function \(\varphi\) with respect to \(x\) is \(p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}'(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,p_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\), because in differentiating with respect to \(x\) the other two factors are constant (lemma 1.81, item 2). Each of the three factors is a continuous function of one coordinate and therefore a continuous function of the point, since a coordinate of the point \(Q\) differs from the coordinate of the point \(P\) by at most the distance \(|PQ|\). By corollary 1.69, item 1, the partial derivative is therefore a continuous function of the point. The same holds for the partial derivatives with respect to \(y\) and \(z\). The function \(\varphi\) is therefore continuously differentiable in all of space (definition 1.90).

2. Box.

  1. Suppose that \(g(P) \ne 0\) at some point \(P\) of the set \(D\), say \(g(P) > 0\). If \(g(P) < 0\), we proceed in the same way with the function \(-g\), which satisfies the hypothesis too, because the integral is multiplied by \(-1\) when the function is multiplied by \(-1\).
  2. The function \(g\) is continuous at \(P\). By definition 1.67 with the error \(\eta = g(P)/2\) there is \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(Q) - g(P)| < g(P)/2\), so \(g(Q) > g(P)/2\), for every point \(Q\) of the set \(D\) with \(|PQ| < \delta_1\). Since \(D\) is open, there is \(r_1 > 0\) such that all points at a distance less than \(r_1\) from \(P\) are in \(D\). Let \(r\) be smaller than the numbers \(\delta_1\) and \(r_1\).
  3. Let \(K\) be the box with centre \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) and edge \(r\), so \(\alpha_1 = x_{\mathrm{P}} - r/2\), \(\beta_1 = x_{\mathrm{P}} + r/2\) and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). Every point \(Q\) of the box \(K\) is at a distance of at most \(\sqrt{3\,(r/2)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,r < r\) from \(P\) by (1.10). The box therefore lies in \(D\), and at every one of its points \(g(Q) > g(P)/2\).
  4. Let \(\varphi\) be the product of the three bumps of the box \(K\), as in item 1. In \(K\), \(\varphi \ge 0\), so \(g\,\varphi \ge \tfrac12\,g(P)\,\varphi\) at every point of \(K\).
  5. The integral over a box is an iterated integral (definition 4.8), and each of the three iterated integrals is monotone (lemma 3.39, item 4), so
    \[\int_{\mathrm{K}} g\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}V \ge \frac{g(P)}{2} \int_{\mathrm{K}} \varphi\,\mathrm{d}V .\]
  6. We integrate a product of three functions, each depending on a single coordinate, step by step. In integrating over \(x\) the factors depending on \(y\) and \(z\) are constant and we take them out (lemma 3.39, item 1), and likewise for \(y\). Therefore
    \[\int_{\mathrm{K}} \varphi\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\alpha_1}^{\beta_1} p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \cdot \int_{\alpha_2}^{\beta_2} p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y \cdot \int_{\alpha_3}^{\beta_3} p_{\alpha_3\beta_3}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z .\]
  7. Each of the three integrals is positive. Let \(\ell = \beta - \alpha\). On the middle half of the interval, from \(\alpha + \ell/4\) to \(\beta - \ell/4\), both factors \(t - \alpha\) and \(\beta - t\) are at least \(\ell/4\), so there \(p_{\alpha\beta}(t) \ge (\ell/4)^4\). Elsewhere on \([\alpha, \beta]\), \(p_{\alpha\beta} \ge 0\). By additivity and the bound of the integral (lemma 3.39, items 2 and 3) we therefore have \(\int_\alpha^\beta p_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathrm{d}t \ge \tfrac{\ell}{2}\,(\ell/4)^4 > 0\). The exact value is \(\ell^5/30\).
  8. By steps 5 to 7, \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} g\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}V > 0\). The box \(K\) lies in \(D\), so by the hypothesis this integral is zero. This is a contradiction, so \(g(P) = 0\).

3. Rectangle.

  1. Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, c)\) be a point of the rectangle \(R\) that does not lie on its edge, so \(a_1 < x_{\mathrm{P}} < b_1\) and \(a_2 < y_{\mathrm{P}} < b_2\), and suppose that \(f(P) > 0\). As in steps 2 and 3 of item 2 we find a square with centre \(P\) and side \(r\) that lies in \(R\) and does not reach its edge, and on which \(f > f(P)/2\). Here the distance of the corners from \(P\) is \(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\,r < r\).
  2. As in steps 4 to 8 of item 2, only with two factors instead of three, the integral of the hypothesis for this square is positive, which is a contradiction. Therefore \(f = 0\) at every point of \(R\) that does not lie on the edge.
  3. Let \(Q\) be a point on the edge of \(R\) and \(C\) the centre of the rectangle. The points \(Q + \tfrac1n\,\overrightarrow{QC}\) for \(n = 2, 3, \dots\) do not lie on the edge and converge to \(Q\). At them \(f = 0\) by step 2, so by lemma 1.68 on continuity through sequences of points \(f(Q) = 0\) too.

Source: EP Lemma 3.1 and (3.5.22), (3.5.23) (pp. 140-141) for functions of one variable, with a different proof. The bump in a box and the proof are not from the sources.

For each box the lemma needed only a single bump, the product of three functions \(p_{\alpha\beta}\). A single one was enough, because we chose the box so small that the function \(g\) was positive everywhere in it, while the bump was zero outside it. Item 1 will ensure that a virtual displacement made from the bump is continuously differentiable, and so kinematically admissible.

Theorem 4.84 Converse of the principle of virtual displacements (obrat načela virtualnih pomikov)

Let the body \(V\), the parts of the surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the prescribed force \(\bar{\vek t}\) and the body force \(\vek b\) be as in theorem 4.82. Let the stress components be continuously differentiable on an open set containing the body together with its surface, and let the stress be symmetric at every point. If the equation (4.51) holds for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement, then

  1. the equilibrium equations (2.31) hold at every interior point of the body,
  2. on every outer face that belongs to the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\), where \(\vek n\) is the outward unit normal of that face.
Proof

We must show two items. We compare the equation (4.51) with lemma 4.81 and get an integral that is zero for every admissible virtual displacement. Then we choose virtual displacements that are not zero only in a small box, first in the interior of the body and then next to the surface.

1. Equilibrium equations.

  1. For \(j = x, y, z\) let \(g_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} + b_{\mathrm{j}}\). These are the left-hand sides of the equilibrium equations (2.31), and the functions \(g_{\mathrm{j}}\) are continuous on the body, because the partial derivatives of the stress are continuous and \(\vek b\) is continuous.
  2. Let \(\delta\vek u\) be a kinematically admissible virtual displacement. By lemma 4.81 on the internal virtual work through the surface, (4.50) holds, and by the hypothesis (4.51) holds. The left side is the same in both equations, so the right sides are equal. We split the surface integral into the integral over \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\), which is zero because \(\delta\vek u = \vek 0\) there, and the integral over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). Moving all terms to one side we get
    \[\int_{\mathrm{V}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} g_{\mathrm{j}}\,\delta u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{d}V + \int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \big(\bar{\vek t} - \ten\sigma\,\vek n\big)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]
    This holds for every kinematically admissible virtual displacement.
  3. Let \(K = [\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [\alpha_3, \beta_3]\) be a box that lies in the set of interior points of the body, and \(\varphi\) the product of its three bumps from lemma 4.83. Take \(\delta\vek u = (\varphi, 0, 0)\). By item 1 of the lemma this field is continuously differentiable in all of space. Outside \(K\) it is zero, and the points of \(K\) are interior points of the body, so \(\delta\vek u\) is zero at every point of the surface, in particular on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\). It is therefore a kinematically admissible virtual displacement, and the integral over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) of step 2 is zero for it.
  4. We add the planes \(x = \alpha_1\), \(x = \beta_1\), \(y = \alpha_2\), \(y = \beta_2\), \(z = \alpha_3\) and \(z = \beta_3\) to the grid of the body. By lemma 4.12 the integral over the body does not change, and in the new grid the box \(K\) is a union of cells. On the cells outside \(K\), \(\varphi = 0\), so the integrals over them are zero, and by lemma 4.10, item 3, the integrals over the cells in \(K\) add up to the integral over \(K\). Step 2 therefore reads \(\int_{\mathrm{K}} g_{\mathrm{x}}\,\varphi\,\mathrm{d}V = 0\).
  5. The set of interior points of the body is open by lemma 1.89 on the neighbourhood of an interior point, since every point close to an interior point is again interior, and \(g_{\mathrm{x}}\) is continuous on it. Step 4 holds for every box in it, so by lemma 4.83, item 2, \(g_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) at every interior point of the body.
  6. In the same way, with \(\delta\vek u = (0, \varphi, 0)\) and \(\delta\vek u = (0, 0, \varphi)\), we get \(g_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\) and \(g_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The equations \(g_{\mathrm{x}} = g_{\mathrm{y}} = g_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) are the equilibrium equations (2.31).

2. The condition on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\).

  1. By item 1, \(g_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) at every interior point, and, as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 4.82, by continuity also at every point of the surface. The volume integral of step 2 of item 1 is therefore zero for every admissible virtual displacement, and what remains is
    \[\int_{\mathrm{S}_\mathrm{t}} \big(\bar{\vek t} - \ten\sigma\,\vek n\big)\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}A = 0 .\]
  2. Let \(R = [a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2]\) in the plane \(z = c\) be one of the outer faces that belong to the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), and let its outward normal be \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so the body lies below it. For faces with another normal we proceed in the same way, only with the roles of the coordinates swapped or with a sign. Choose numbers \(a_1 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < b_1\) and \(a_2 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < b_2\). Since the body consists of finitely many boxes and there is no material above \(R\), there is \(d > 0\) such that the box \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [c - d, c]\) lies in the body, while the box \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [c, c + d]\) lies outside the body except for its bottom face (figure 4.69).
  3. For a chosen \(j\) let \(\delta u_{\mathrm{j}} = p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,\chi(z)\), where \(\chi(z) = p_{\mathrm{c}-\mathrm{d},\,\mathrm{c}+\mathrm{d}}(z)/d^4\), and let the other two components be zero. By item 1 of lemma 4.83 this field is continuously differentiable in all of space. It is different from zero only in the interior of the box \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2] \times [c - d, c + d]\). This box meets the surface of the body only in the rectangle \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2]\) in the plane \(z = c\), which lies in \(R\), so in \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). The field is therefore zero on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and is a kinematically admissible virtual displacement.
  4. On all other outer faces of the part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) the virtual displacement is zero by step 3, and on the face \(R\) it is different from zero only inside the rectangle \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2]\). When we cut \(R\) with the lines \(x = \alpha_1\), \(x = \beta_1\), \(y = \alpha_2\) and \(y = \beta_2\), the integral of step 1 therefore equals, by lemma 4.10, item 3, for a rectangle, the integral over this rectangle. In the plane \(z = c\), \(\chi(c) = d^2 \cdot d^2/d^4 = 1\), so
    \[\int_{\alpha_1}^{\beta_1} \int_{\alpha_2}^{\beta_2} \big(\bar t_{\mathrm{j}} - (\ten\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\,p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 .\]
  5. The function \(\bar t_{\mathrm{j}} - (\ten\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{j}}\) is continuous on \(R\), because \(\bar{\vek t}\) and the stress are continuous. Step 4 holds for all numbers of step 2, so by lemma 4.83, item 3, \(\bar t_{\mathrm{j}} = (\ten\sigma\,\vek n)_{\mathrm{j}}\) at every point of \(R\). This holds for \(j = x, y, z\), so \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on all of \(R\).

Source: SADD (6.5.7)-(6.5.9) (PDF pp. 138-139), EP (4.2.4)-(4.2.6) (p. 169), BOWER §2.4.4.2 (PDF pp. 86-88) and §2.4.6 (PDF p. 90). The sources conclude that the expressions under the integrals are zero because the virtual displacement is arbitrary. The proof with bumps is not from the sources.

The converse does not give the symmetry of stress, it assumes it. Only the virtual strain, which is symmetric, appears in the internal virtual work, so the equation does not see the antisymmetric part of the stress at all. BOWER too assumes the symmetry before writing the principle (BOWER, PDF p. 85). The second condition of equilibrium is thus built into the principle through the fact that the virtual strain stands in it, while the first condition follows from the equation. For a body with a curved surface the sources prove the converse differently. BOWER, for example, chooses a virtual displacement that inside the body is proportional to the left-hand sides of the equilibrium equations and zero on the surface, which also needs second derivatives of the stress (BOWER, PDF pp. 87-88).

4.5.6 Two tests

The cube in pure shear. Let us restate the setting. A steel cube with edge \(h = 10\) mm is glued to a table with its bottom face. The origin is at the corner \(A\) at the bottom front left, the axis \(x\) points to the right, the axis \(y\) into the depth, the axis \(z\) up. We push the top face to the right, pull the right face up and the left face down, all with the force \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa per unit area, and the front and back faces are free. The stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\) everywhere, the other components are zero, and we do not take the weight into account (figure 4.70, left). As the virtual displacement take the admissible field \(\delta\vek u = (\gamma z, 0, 0)\), which moves the top face by \(\gamma h\) to the right and the bottom face not at all.

  1. The virtual strain is \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} = \delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = \gamma/2\), the others are zero, as we computed in section 4.5.2. In the double sum only the terms with the pair \(x\), \(z\) remain:
    \[\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zx}} = 2\,\tau_0\,\frac{\gamma}{2} = \tau_0\,\gamma .\]
  2. This number is the same at all points of the cube, so the internal virtual work equals this number times the volume of the cube, \(\tau_0\,\gamma\,h^3\) (lemma 4.10 on the properties of the integral over a box, item 2).
  3. There is no body force, so the external virtual work is done only by the prescribed forces on the five faces of \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). On the top face, where \(z = h\), \(\bar{\vek t} = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\delta\vek u = (\gamma h, 0, 0)\), so \(\bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u = \tau_0\,\gamma h\) at every point of the face, and the integral over the face of area \(h^2\) is \(\tau_0\,\gamma\,h^3\).
  4. On the right face \(\bar{\vek t} = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), on the left one \(-\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The virtual displacement, however, has only an \(x\) component, so \(\bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u = 0\) on both. On the front and back faces \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\).
  5. The external virtual work is therefore \(\tau_0\,\gamma\,h^3\), equal to the internal one.

With numbers, for \(\gamma = 0{,}001\): the force on the top face is \(\tau_0\,h^2 = 50 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa} \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 = 5000\) N, the face moves by \(\gamma h = 10\) µm, and the work is \(5000\ \text{N} \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m} = 0{,}05\) J. That is also \(\tau_0\,\gamma\,h^3 = 50 \cdot 10^6 \cdot 0{,}001 \cdot 10^{-6}\) J. The work of the force pushing the top face goes into the work of the stresses on the virtual shear of every piece of the cube. We notice one more thing. This field has the shape of the true field of the sheared cube, but the stress of \(50\) MPa is not the stress that would cause such a displacement. By section 3.2.5 that would be \(75{,}38\) MPa. The principle does not care, because in it the stress and the virtual displacement are not connected.

What goes wrong with a field that is not admissible? Take the move of the whole cube, \(\delta\vek u = (\delta a, 0, 0)\) (figure 4.70, right). The virtual strain is zero, so the internal virtual work is zero. The external virtual work over \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\), however, is the work of the force on the top face, \(\tau_0\,h^2\,\delta a\), and on the right and left faces the work is zero. For \(\delta a = 1\) µm this is \(5000\ \text{N} \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 0{,}005\) J, not zero, and the equation (4.51) does not hold. Missing is the work of the force of the glue, which pulls the bottom face to the left with the force \(\tau_0\,h^2\) and does \(-\tau_0\,h^2\,\delta a = -0{,}005\) J. With it the sum would be zero, which is the first condition of equilibrium of the whole cube in the direction of the axis \(x\). Since in general we do not know the forces of the supports, we remove them by not allowing virtual displacements to move anything on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\).

The hanging bar. Let us restate the setting. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m with a round cross section of area \(A = 78{,}54\) mm² hangs under its own weight, the axis \(x\) points down from the centre \(O\) of the top face. A force spread evenly over the top face, \(\rho g L\) per unit area, pulls it up, which is an idealization, the point \(O\) is fixed only against a motion of the bar as a whole, and the lateral surface and the bottom face are free. The only stress component that is not zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\), the body force is \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and \(\rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, while the weight of the bar is \(W = \rho g A L = 60{,}10\) N. As the virtual displacement take

\[\delta\vek u = (\delta_0 + \kappa x^2,\ 0,\ 0) , \qquad \delta_0 = 1\ \text{µm} , \qquad \kappa = 10^{-8}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The top face moves down by \(\delta_0 = 1\) µm, the bottom face by \(\delta_0 + \kappa L^2 = 2\) µm (figure 4.71). Since \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty, the field is admissible.

  1. The only partial derivative that is not zero is \(\partial\,\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = 2\kappa x\), so \(\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2\kappa x\), and the other virtual strains are zero. Of the nine terms of the double sum a single one remains: \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 2\rho g\kappa\,x\,(L - x)\).
  2. This function depends only on \(x\). The integral over the bar is therefore the area of the cross section times the integral over \(x\). For a bar with a rectangular cross section, as treated by CM (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305), the bar is a box, and this follows from theorem 4.9, because integrating a constant function over \(y\) and \(z\) gives the area of the cross section. For the round bar we compute the integral with the cut into the pieces \(C\), \(D\) and \(E\) of section 4.2.6 (definition 4.21). In every piece \(x\) is the outer variable, so the two inner integrals of a function that depends only on \(x\) give this function times the area of the cross section of the piece, and the areas of the pieces add up to \(A\) (section 4.2.8).
  3. By the fundamental theorem of calculus 3.40, with the function \(L x^2/2 - x^3/3\), whose derivative is \(L x - x^2\), the internal virtual work is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} A \int_0^L 2\rho g\kappa\,(L x - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x &= 2\rho g\kappa A \Big(\frac{L^3}{2} - \frac{L^3}{3}\Big) = \frac{\rho g\kappa A L^3}{3} \\ &= \frac{6{,}010 \cdot 10^{-8} \cdot 1000}{3}\ \text{J} = 2{,}003 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} . \end{aligned}\]
  4. The work of the body force is
    \[\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho g\,(\delta_0 + \kappa x^2)\,\mathrm{d}V &= \rho g A \Big(\delta_0 L + \frac{\kappa L^3}{3}\Big) \\ &= 6{,}010 \cdot \big(10^{-5} + 3{,}333 \cdot 10^{-6}\big)\ \text{J} = 8{,}013 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} . \end{aligned}\]
  5. On the top face, where \(x = 0\), \(\bar{\vek t} = -\rho g L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\delta\vek u = (\delta_0, 0, 0)\), so \(\bar{\vek t}\cdot\delta\vek u = -\rho g L\,\delta_0\) at every point of the face. The integral over the face of area \(A\) is \(-\rho g A L\,\delta_0 = -W\,\delta_0 = -6{,}010 \cdot 10^{-5}\) J. On the lateral surface and on the bottom face \(\bar{\vek t} = \vek 0\).
  6. The external virtual work is \(8{,}013 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} - 6{,}010 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{J} = 2{,}003 \cdot 10^{-5}\) J, equal to the internal one.

The pattern in the numbers deserves an explanation. The virtual displacement has two parts. The part \(\delta_0\) moves the whole bar in the same way. For it the virtual strain is zero, and the work of the weight, \(W\,\delta_0\), exactly cancels the work of the pull on the top face, \(-W\,\delta_0\). This is the first condition of equilibrium of the whole bar: the upward pull equals the weight. The part \(\kappa x^2\) does not move the top face, so the pull does no work there, and the work of the weight on this part, \(\rho g\kappa A L^3/3\), is exactly the internal virtual work. Why is the function in the figure largest in the middle? The stress \(\rho g\,(L - x)\) is largest at the top, where it carries the whole bar, and the virtual strain \(2\kappa x\) at the bottom, where the stretching of the imagined field is largest. Their product is zero at both ends and largest in between, at \(x = L/2\).

What is three-dimensional about the bar? Of the nine products in the internal virtual work only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\delta\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) remained, because the other stress components are zero. If we added components \(\delta u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\delta u_{\mathrm{z}}\) to the virtual displacement, nothing would change. In the internal work no stress sees them, the body force has no \(y\) and \(z\) components, and the lateral surface is free. When \(\delta u_{\mathrm{x}}\) depends only on \(x\), the integral over the cross section, \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \rho g A\,(L - x)\), is the axial force \(N(x)\), and the internal virtual work is \(\int_0^L N\,\delta u'\,\mathrm{d}x\), exactly the left side of the principle for the bar from section 4.4. The principle for the bar is thus a special case of the principle for a body.

4.5.7 What the principle says

We now have the equilibrium of a body written in two equivalent ways, which theorems 4.82 and 4.84 connect. The first way is the equations at every point: the equilibrium equations in the interior and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \bar{\vek t}\) on \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\). The second way is one equation for all admissible virtual displacements, the equality of internal and external virtual work. Books call the second the weak form (šibka oblika) of equilibrium (BOWER §8.1.2, PDF p. 496). The word weak says that it asks less differentiability of the solution: there are no derivatives of the stress in the equation, only first partial derivatives of the virtual displacement (EP §6.3.6.2, p. 323). BOWER adds that for a computer this is exactly what matters, because derivatives are hard to compute accurately while integrals are easy (BOWER, PDF p. 87). The finite element method requires the virtual work equation only for finitely many chosen virtual displacements and gets from it a system of equations for the unknown displacements. We will see this in chapters 6 and 7.

Where does the principle in this form not hold? Where one of the assumptions from the beginning of the section fails.

  • For large deformations we may not identify the shapes before and after loading. BOWER writes the principle with stresses referred to the original shape, of the kind we met in section 2.13, and with integrals over the body before loading, where the virtual strain is replaced by a virtual change of the deformation gradient or of the Green-Lagrange tensor (BOWER §2.4.5, PDF pp. 88-89).
  • For a body that accelerates, the equilibrium equations are no longer satisfied. The right side of the equilibrium equations then holds the density times the acceleration (section 2.7), and the virtual work equation gets the additional term \(\int_{\mathrm{V}} \rho\,\vek a\cdot\delta\vek u\,\mathrm{d}V\), where \(\vek a\) is the acceleration (BOWER, PDF p. 86). We can think of the force \(-\rho\,\vek a\) per unit volume as one more body force, which is d'Alembert's form of dynamics (GR (1.1), PDF p. 32). We will use this in chapter 8.
  • Where the stress is not continuously differentiable, for example at a point force or at the contact of two materials, we may not use the divergence theorem in our form on the whole body.
  • The stress must be symmetric. Bodies with distributed moments that are not moments of forces were already excluded in section 2.8.

The principle holds for every material, because we took the stress as given. How, then, do we find the stresses and displacements? Only when we express the stresses by the displacements, with the relation between stresses and strains from chapter 3, does the virtual work equation become an equation for the unknown displacement field. For the ideal elastic material of section 3.6, in the next section we will take as the virtual displacement the difference of two displacement fields that respect the supports and get from the principle a number that is smallest for the true displacement field.
