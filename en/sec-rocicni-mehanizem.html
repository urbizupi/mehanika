Chapter 23 Reciprocating engines: dynamics, balancing and vibration isolation

23.2 The slider-crank mechanism and the motion of the piston

The previous section (23.1) treated a rigid rotor turning about a fixed axis. The crankshaft of a piston engine is such a rotor, but it is driven by pistons that move along straight lines, and together with the shaft they form a mechanism. The forces and moments of the following sections are mass times acceleration (theorem 8.74), so we must first know how the piston moves when the shaft turns. The question of this section has two parts: where the piston is at every shaft angle, and how to split this position into the oscillations we know from chapter 9.

We treat one cylinder. Engine \(E\), which we use throughout the chapter, is our model and not data of an existing engine: crank radius \(a = 50\) mm, conrod length \(l = 200\) mm and speed \(1500\) 1/min, that is \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s, as for machine \(B\) in chapter 9. The model has five idealizations.

  1. The crank and the conrod are rigid bodies, their lengths do not change. A real conrod deforms elastically under load (chapter 1), and we do not estimate its size here.
  2. The joints are frictionless and have no clearance, so they are points.
  3. The cylinder guides the piston exactly along a line through the axis of the crankshaft. A cylinder whose axis misses this point is treated by example 23.13.
  4. The motion is planar.
  5. The crank turns with constant angular speed \(\Omega\), so the angle is \(\theta = \Omega t\). The assumption matters only where we differentiate with respect to time. The position of the piston as a function of the angle \(\theta\) does not depend on it. The speed of a real engine oscillates about its mean, which section 23.5 treats.

We draw the mechanism part by part (figure 23.3). We begin with the cylinder, a round tube in which the piston slides and in which the gas presses on it. We draw its axis horizontally and put on it the point \(O\), the axis of the crankshaft, which is perpendicular to the plane of the drawing and turns in a bearing in the frame. About \(O\) turns the crank, a rigid rod of length \(a\) whose free end is the crank pin \(A\). The pin travels on the circle of radius \(a\). The rod is called a crank because of the arm of the force in a moment: a force on the pin turns the shaft with a moment whose arm is at most \(a\).

To the pin the conrod is attached by a joint, a rigid rod of length \(l\). It is so called because it connects the crank with the piston. Its other end is at the point \(B\), where it is joined by a joint to the piston. The piston is a body that slides in the cylinder, and the cylinder holds it so that \(B\) stays on the axis. The position of the mechanism is given by the angle \(\theta\) from the cylinder axis to the crank, counterclockwise, in radians as in chapter 8. The conrod is inclined towards the axis by the angle \(\varphi\), and the position of the piston is the distance \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) from \(O\) to \(B\) along the axis. All other quantities of the mechanism will be functions of the angle \(\theta\).

Definition 23.7 Slider-crank mechanism (ročični mehanizem)

A slider-crank mechanism is a planar mechanism made of a frame with a bearing at the point \(O\) and a cylinder, and of three moving bodies.

  1. The crank (ročica) is a rigid rod \(OA\) of length \(a > 0\) that turns about \(O\). Its other end \(A\) is the crank pin (čep).
  2. The conrod (ojnica, connecting rod) is a rigid rod \(AB\) of length \(l > a\), jointed at \(A\) and \(B\).
  3. The piston (bat) is a body with a joint at \(B\), which the cylinder guides so that \(B\) stays on the line through \(O\), the cylinder axis (os valja).

The axis \(x\) is the cylinder axis, directed from \(O\) towards the cylinder head (glava valja), the angle \(\theta\) is the angle of the crank from the axis \(x\) counterclockwise, and \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is the coordinate of the point \(B\). The number \(\lambda = a/l < 1\) is the crank-to-conrod ratio (razmerje ročice in ojnice).

A dead position (mrtva lega) is a position in which \(O\), \(A\) and \(B\) are on one line. If \(A\) is between \(O\) and \(B\), it is the top dead centre (TDC, zgornja mrtva lega), the position at \(\theta = 0\). If \(O\) is between \(A\) and \(B\), it is the bottom dead centre (BDC, spodnja mrtva lega) at \(\theta = \pi\). The stroke (hod) is the difference of the values of \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) at the top and at the bottom dead position.

Source: NORTON, PDF p. 701 (the basic mechanism of crank, conrod and piston, figure 13-3) and 702 (top and bottom dead centre, TDC and BDC). The notation is ours: NORTON writes \(r\) for the crank radius, but \(r\) in this book means the frequency ratio (chapter 9), so the crank radius is \(a\). The letter \(\lambda\) has another meaning in chapters 10 and 8, an eigenvalue and a multiplier, and here it is only the ratio \(a/l\). The toggle mechanism \(D\) of chapter 8 is a special case with \(\lambda = 1\) and does not belong here (example 23.9). Practical values of \(\lambda\) are, by NORTON, PDF p. 709, between \(1/5\) and \(1/3\). At \(r/l = 1/2\) the transmission angle \(\pi/2 - \varphi\) would be too small and the mechanism would not run smoothly, so the upper limit is about \(1/3\).

Where is the piston when the crank is at the angle \(\theta\)? The pin \(A\) is on a circle of radius \(a\), and the conrod has length \(l\). The point \(B\) is therefore on the axis at the distance \(l\) from \(A\). The first questions are how many such points there are and whether the angle \(\theta\) is a good coordinate of the mechanism in every position.

Lemma 23.8 Position of the piston and the coordinate of the mechanism (lega bata in koordinata mehanizma)

Let \(\lambda = a/l < 1\) and let the origin be at \(O\).

  1. At every angle \(\theta\) the point \(B\) has two different positions on the axis, at the distance \(l\) from the pin \(A = (a\cos\theta,\ a\sin\theta)\), namely \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\cos\theta \pm \sqrt{l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta}\). A continuous motion keeps the same sign. We take the sign \(+\), the conrod looks towards the cylinder head:
    \[x_{\mathrm{B}}(\theta) = a\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta} . \tag{23.5}\]
  2. The function \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is infinitely differentiable, even and \(2\pi\)-periodic. Its root part \(s(\theta) = \sqrt{l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta}\) is an even function with period \(\pi\).
  3. The conrod is inclined by the angle \(\varphi\) with \(\sin\varphi = \lambda\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\varphi = s/l > 0\), so \(|\varphi| \le \arcsin\lambda\). The mechanism has one degree of freedom and the angle \(\theta\) is a generalized coordinate in every position (definition 8.45).
Proof

We must first show that the length \(l\) of the conrod really determines two positions of \(B\), and then that the position is a smooth function of the angle \(\theta\) and that \(\theta\) is a coordinate.

  1. By the definition of the angle \(\theta\) we have \(A = (a\cos\theta,\ a\sin\theta)\) and the point on the axis is \(B = (x, 0)\).
  2. The length \(AB\) is \(l\), so \((x - a\cos\theta)^2 + (0 - a\sin\theta)^2 = l^2\), from which \((x - a\cos\theta)^2 = l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta\).
  3. Since \(\sin^2\theta \le 1\) (lemma 8.10, \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)) and \(a < l\), the right side is at least \(l^2 - a^2 = l^2(1 - \lambda^2) > 0\). The equation therefore has two different real roots, \(x = a\cos\theta \pm s(\theta)\), which differ by \(2s(\theta) \ge 2\sqrt{l^2 - a^2} > 0\). In a continuous motion the difference \(x - a\cos\theta\) is a continuous function of time with values \(+s\) or \(-s\), whose absolute value is at least \(\sqrt{l^2 - a^2} > 0\). If it changed its sign, by theorem 2.57 it would take the value \(0\) in between, which is impossible. A continuous motion therefore cannot jump from one root to the other. This is point 1.
  4. The radicand \(p(\theta) = l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta\) is infinitely differentiable (theorem 8.12 and the rules of differentiation, lemma 1.81) with values in \([l^2 - a^2,\ l^2] \subset (0, \infty)\). Let \(w = 1/\sqrt p\). By lemma 8.5 \(s' = \tfrac12 p'\,w\), and by lemma 4.69 (written for one variable) \(w' = -\tfrac12 w^3 p'\). Every derivative of \(s\) is therefore a sum of products of derivatives of \(p\) and powers of \(w\), and the derivative of such a product is, by the product rule (lemma 1.81), again such a sum. Hence \(s = \sqrt p\) is infinitely differentiable and so is \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\cos\theta + s\).
  5. The cosine is even, \(\sin^2(-\theta) = \sin^2\theta\), and by corollary 8.15 (with \(\beta = \pi\), \(\sin\pi = 0\), \(\cos\pi = -1\)) we have \(\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta\), so \(\sin^2(\theta + \pi) = \sin^2\theta\). Hence \(s\) is even with period \(\pi\) and \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is even with period \(2\pi\). This is point 2.
  6. From \(B - A = (x_{\mathrm{B}} - a\cos\theta,\ -a\sin\theta) = l\,(\cos\varphi,\ -\sin\varphi)\) it follows that \(\cos\varphi = s/l > 0\) and \(\sin\varphi = (a/l)\sin\theta\) and so \(|\sin\varphi| \le \lambda\). Since \(\cos\varphi > 0\), the angle \(\varphi\) lies between \(-\pi/2\) and \(\pi/2\), where the sine strictly increases (its derivative \(\cos\) is positive there, theorem 8.12), so \(|\varphi| \le \arcsin\lambda\). This is the first part of point 3.
  7. We describe the configuration as in example 8.48: the crank \((x_1, y_1, \varphi_1)\) and the conrod \((x_2, y_2, \varphi_2)\) with centres of mass at the midpoints, \(N = 6\). The constraints are \(f_1 = x_1 - \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_1 = 0\), \(f_2 = y_1 - \tfrac a2\sin\varphi_1 = 0\), \(f_3 = x_2 - a\cos\varphi_1 - \tfrac l2\cos\varphi_2 = 0\), \(f_4 = y_2 - a\sin\varphi_1 - \tfrac l2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\) (the pin \(A\) is the end of both) and \(f_5 = y_2 + \tfrac l2\sin\varphi_2 = 0\) (\(B\) is on the axis).
  8. The matrix of derivatives \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) (\(5 \times 6\)) has pivots in rows \(1\) to \(4\) in the columns \(x_1\), \(y_1\), \(x_2\), \(y_2\). We subtract row \(4\) from row \(5\) and are left with only \(a\cos\varphi_1\) in the column \(\varphi_1\) and \(l\cos\varphi_2\) in the column \(\varphi_2\). At \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi\) we have \(l\cos\varphi_2 = s(\theta) > 0\) by step 3, so the matrix has five pivots in every position. The constraints are independent, \(m = 5\) and \(n = 6 - 5 = 1\).
  9. The components \(\vek X(\theta)\) are \((\tfrac a2\cos\theta,\ \tfrac a2\sin\theta,\ \theta,\ x_2,\ y_2,\ -\varphi)\), where \((x_2, y_2) = (a\cos\theta + s/2,\ \tfrac a2\sin\theta)\) is the midpoint of \(AB\). The first five functions are continuously differentiable by step 4. The last one is \(-\varphi = -h(\lambda\sin\theta)\), where \(h\) is the inverse function of the sine on \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\). By lemma 8.8 \(h\) is differentiable on \((-1, 1)\) with the derivative \(1/\cos h\), which is continuous, and \(|\lambda\sin\theta| \le \lambda < 1\), so \(-\varphi\) is continuously differentiable by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, point 3). This is condition 1 of the definition. The constraints \(f_1\) to \(f_5\) are satisfied for every \(\theta\) by steps 1 to 6 (condition 2). The component \(\varphi_1 = \theta\) has derivative \(1\), so the column \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\) has a pivot (condition 3, \(n = 1\)).

Source: Equation ((23.5)) is NORTON (13.1d), PDF p. 707, which derives it from \(\sin\varphi = (r/l)\sin\omega t\) and \(x = r\cos\omega t + l\cos\varphi\) (13.1a to c). The proof is ours: the distance instead of the angle \(\varphi\), differentiability and pivots. Checked by computation (SymPy): the \(5 \times 6\) matrix has five pivots at \(\theta = 0\), \(\pi/6\), \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\).

The condition \(\lambda < 1\) is essential in the lemma. Let us see what happens without it, at both neighbours (figure 23.4).

Example 23.9 Conrod not longer than the crank (ojnica, ki ni daljša od ročice)

The hypothesis \(\lambda < 1\) supports lemma 23.8 in step 3. Let us look at both neighbours.

  1. Let \(a = 50\) mm and \(l = 40\) mm, so \(\lambda = 1{,}25\) (figure 23.4). The crank turns about the point \(O\), and the pin \(A\) is on the circle of radius \(a\). The point \(B\) is on the axis, so the pin \(A\) must be at most \(l\) above or below the axis, \(|a\sin\theta| \le l\). Hence \(|\sin\theta| \le l/a = 0{,}8\), so \(|\theta| \le \arcsin 0{,}8 = 53{,}13°\) (or mirrored about \(\pi\)). These are the two thick arcs of the circle. At a larger angle the radicand \(l^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta\) becomes negative and \(B\) does not exist. In the limiting position at the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) the conrod of length \(l\) is vertical and \(B\) is below \(A\). The crank therefore cannot turn round, it only swings between \(\pm 53{,}13°\). The conclusion of the lemma, that \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is determined at every \(\theta\), fails.
  2. Let \(a = l = 200\) mm, so \(\lambda = 1\). The radicand is \(l^2\cos^2\theta \ge 0\) and the root is \(s = l|\cos\theta|\). At \(\theta = \pm\pi/2\) we have \(s = 0\): both roots \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\cos\theta \pm s\) meet and \(B\) is at \(O\), the conrod is folded onto the crank. The function \(s = l|\cos\theta|\) has a corner there with slopes \(-0{,}200\) and \(+0{,}200\) m/rad, it is not differentiable, and the motion from there on is not determined by \(\theta\), because the conrod can continue along both paths. For \(\cos\theta \ge 0\) we have \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = 2a\cos\theta\), which is exactly the toggle mechanism \(D\) of example 8.48, which has \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = 2a\cos\theta\) and the angle \(\theta\) only in \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\).
  3. At \(\lambda = 1/4\) (engine \(E\)) this does not happen: the radicand is at least \(l^2(1 - \lambda^2) = 0{,}9375\,l^2\).

Source: The example is ours (NORTON, PDF p. 709, only mentions that at \(r/l\) near \(1\) the piston would hit the crank axis).

The mechanism is therefore sound for \(\lambda < 1\). But the piston has a special role in two positions. Where does it turn round, and how far does it travel between two turns?

Lemma 23.10 Stroke and dead positions of the piston (hod in mrtve lege bata)

Let \(\lambda < 1\).

  1. The derivative of the piston position is \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'(\theta) = -a\sin\theta\,\Big(1 + \dfrac{\lambda\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2\sin^2\theta}}\Big)\) and the bracket is positive at every \(\theta\).
  2. The function \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) strictly decreases on \((0, \pi)\) and strictly increases on \((\pi, 2\pi)\). Its maximum is \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(0) = l + a\) and its minimum \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(\pi) = l - a\), so the stroke equals \(2a\).
  3. \(x_{\mathrm{B}}' = 0\) exactly at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\) (up to the period \(2\pi\)), that is in the dead positions: only in them are \(O\), \(A\), \(B\) on one line.
Proof

We must show the sign of the derivative and connect it with the position.

  1. By lemma 8.5 \(s' = p'/(2s)\) with \(p' = -2a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\), so \(s' = -a^2\sin\theta\cos\theta/s\). Together with \((a\cos\theta)' = -a\sin\theta\) (theorem 8.12) we get \(x_{\mathrm{B}}' = -a\sin\theta\,(1 + a\cos\theta/s)\). Since \(s = l\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2\sin^2\theta}\), we have \(a\cos\theta/s = \lambda\cos\theta/\sqrt{1 - \lambda^2\sin^2\theta}\).
  2. The absolute value of this fraction is less than \(1\): \(\lambda|\cos\theta| < \sqrt{1 - \lambda^2\sin^2\theta}\) is after squaring (both sides are nonnegative) equivalent to \(\lambda^2\cos^2\theta < 1 - \lambda^2\sin^2\theta\), that is \(\lambda^2 < 1\) by \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\). Hence the bracket is positive. This is point 1.
  3. On \((0, \pi)\) we have \(\sin\theta > 0\) (lemma 8.10) and so \(x_{\mathrm{B}}' < 0\), on \((\pi, 2\pi)\) we have \(x_{\mathrm{B}}' > 0\). A function whose derivative has a constant sign is strictly monotonic, because by theorem 1.84 the difference of its values at two points equals the derivative at some intermediate point times the distance of the points.
  4. The values are \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(0) = a + \sqrt{l^2} = l + a\) and \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(\pi) = -a + l\). The stroke is the difference \(2a\). This is point 2.
  5. The derivative is zero exactly where \(\sin\theta = 0\) (the bracket is positive), that is at \(\theta = 0, \pi\). Since \(O \ne B\) (\(x_{\mathrm{B}} \ge l - a > 0\)) and \(O\), \(B\) are on the axis, \(O\), \(A\), \(B\) are on one line exactly when \(A\) is on the axis, that is \(a\sin\theta = 0\). This is point 3.

Source: The proof is not from a source. NORTON, PDF p. 702, mentions the piston returning from the bottom to the top dead centre.

Why do we call them dead? The gas pushes the piston along the axis. A conrod with negligible mass and inertia has, by theorem 8.74, the sum of forces and the sum of moments about \(A\) equal to zero, because the left side of the equations of motion is zero. The force at \(B\) therefore passes through \(A\) and the forces in the joints are opposite, so the conrod pushes the pin \(A\) along \(AB\). The moment of this force about \(O\) is the vector product \(\vek{OA}\times\vek F\) (definition 2.32). When \(OA\) and \(AB\) are on one line, as in the top and bottom position in figure 23.3, the vectors are parallel and the product is zero. The gas then cannot turn the crank, the crank is "dead". NORTON, PDF p. 702, says it by stating that the arm of the gas force is zero in these positions, and adds that the shaft must therefore store some rotational kinetic energy to pass the dead positions. This will be the topic of section 23.5. The distance between the two positions of the piston is the stroke, by lemma 23.10 exactly \(2a\).

The travel of the piston between the top and the bottom dead position is called a stroke (takt, in the sense of a part of the cycle). By lemma 23.10 a stroke is half a turn, \(\Delta\theta = \pi\), because the piston turns round only in the dead positions. The cycle of an engine is a sequence of strokes that repeats. In a four-stroke engine (Otto) it consists of intake, compression, power stroke and exhaust, four strokes, so \(4\pi\), that is two turns or 720° (NORTON, PDF p. 702 and 703). A two-stroke engine (Clerk) has two strokes and one turn. The cycles are named after inventors of the nineteenth century (NORTON, PDF p. 702), we do not give years. The length of the cycle will matter for the gas torque (section 23.4).

Example 23.11 Engine \(E\): position, stroke and acceleration of the piston (motor \(E\): lega, hod in pospešek bata)

Engine \(E\) is our model. With \(a = 50\) mm and \(l = 200\) mm we have \(\lambda = 0{,}25\) and the speed is \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s.

  1. The top dead centre is \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(0) = l + a = 250\) mm, the bottom one \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(\pi) = l - a = 150\) mm, the stroke is \(100\) mm, which is exactly \(2a\) by lemma 23.10 (NumPy: \(250{,}000\), \(150{,}000\), \(100{,}000\) mm).
  2. The largest inclination of the conrod is \(\arcsin\lambda = 14{,}48°\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\) by lemma 23.8, point 3.
  3. The bracket in lemma 23.10 equals \(1 + \lambda = 1{,}25\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(1 - \lambda = 0{,}75\) at \(\theta = \pi\) (NumPy: minimum \(0{,}75\), maximum \(1{,}25\) over a turn). At the same small angle from the dead position the speed of the piston near the top dead centre is therefore about \(1{,}25/0{,}75 = 1{,}67\) times larger than near the bottom one. The approximation is the better the smaller the angle: at \(5°\) the ratio is \(1{,}663\), at \(10°\) it is \(1{,}654\) (NumPy).
  4. The acceleration, which we will decompose in theorem 23.12, has the scale \(a\Omega^2 = 1233{,}70\) m/s\(^2\). At \(\theta = \Omega t\) with constant \(\Omega\) the chain rule gives \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = \Omega^2 x_{\mathrm{B}}''(\theta)\). We denote the bracket of lemma 23.10 by \(k(\theta)\), so that \(x_{\mathrm{B}}' = -a\sin\theta\,k(\theta)\). By the product rule \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'' = -a\cos\theta\,k(\theta) - a\sin\theta\,k'(\theta)\), and in the dead positions, where \(\sin\theta = 0\), what remains is \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'' = -a\cos\theta\,k(\theta)\), that is \(-a(1 + \lambda)\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(a(1 - \lambda)\) at \(\theta = \pi\). At the top dead centre the acceleration is therefore \(-1{,}25\,a\Omega^2 = -1542{,}1\) m/s\(^2\) (towards the crank, 157,2 times the acceleration of gravity) and at the bottom one \(+0{,}75\,a\Omega^2 = 925{,}3\) m/s\(^2\) (94,3 times).

Source: Engine \(E\) is our model and not data from a source. A comparison of the order of magnitude: NORTON, PDF p. 711, gives for another engine (stroke 89 mm) an acceleration of 747 times the acceleration of gravity at 3400 1/min.

Why the angle \(\theta\) and not simply \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) for the coordinate? In example 8.48 we saw for the toggle mechanism that a coordinate fails where two positions belong to it. For the piston this happens in the dead positions (figure 23.5).

The figure shows the position of the piston over a whole turn, which turns round gently at both ends. The enlargement near the top dead centre (enlargement) shows what this means for the coordinate. By lemma 23.10 \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'(0) = 0\), and by example 23.11, point 4, \(x_{\mathrm{B}}''(0) = -a\,(1 + \lambda) = -62{,}5\) mm/rad\(^2\). By theorem 8.60 we therefore have for a small \(\theta\) approximately \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(\theta) \approx (l + a) - 31{,}25\,\theta^2\) mm, because \(x_{\mathrm{B}}''(c) \approx x_{\mathrm{B}}''(0)\) at \(c\) near \(0\). For a position \(1\) mm below the top dead centre \(\theta_{\mathrm{c}} = \pm 0{,}179\) rad, that is \(\pm 10{,}27°\) (exactly \(0{,}17926\) rad, NumPy), and hence two positions of the mechanism with the same \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\). For \(0{,}1\) mm we have \(\theta_{\mathrm{c}} = 0{,}0566\) rad and for \(0{,}01\) mm \(0{,}0179\) rad: when the distance from the dead position is ten times smaller, the angle is only \(\sqrt{10} = 3{,}16\) times smaller. The dependence of the angle on \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) is a square root in the dead position, not linear. The pivot condition says this exactly: if we add to the \(5 \times 6\) matrix of lemma 23.8 the derivatives of \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) as a sixth row, there are six pivots exactly when \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'(\theta) \ne 0\). The constraints allow only displacements in the direction of the column \(\partial\vek X/\partial\theta\), and the sixth row multiplied by this column gives \(x_{\mathrm{B}}'(\theta)\). If this is zero, the new row does not forbid that displacement and there stay five pivots. At \(\theta = \pi/6\) and \(\pi/2\) it is six, at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\pi\) only five (SymPy). The coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) fails in the dead positions, \(\theta\) does not.

Now we look for the oscillations of which the motion of the piston is composed, that is, which harmonics occur and which do not. The answer will later tell which forces the counterweights cancel and which they do not. The function \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) depends on \(\theta = \Omega t\) and by definition 9.88 it has Fourier coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\) with the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) (section 9.11). The term \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\cos n\Omega t + b_{\mathrm{n}}\sin n\Omega t\) is the \(n\)-th harmonic (harmonik). The first harmonic repeats once per turn and NORTON (PDF p. 710) calls it the primary component (primarna komponenta), the second repeats twice per turn and is the secondary component (sekundarna komponenta). The name tells how many times faster than the shaft the term oscillates.

Theorem 23.12 Harmonics of the position and the acceleration of the piston (harmoniki lege in pospeška bata)

Let \(\lambda = a/l < 1\) and \(\theta = \Omega t\) with constant \(\Omega\). The position \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(t) = x_{\mathrm{B}}(\Omega t)\) and the acceleration \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}}(t)\) from lemma 23.8 are continuous \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\)-periodic functions with Fourier series for which

  1. all sine coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\) are zero,
  2. the coefficients \(a_{\mathrm{n}}\) of odd orders \(n \ge 3\) are zero,
  3. the coefficient of the first order is \(a\) for the position and \(-a\Omega^2\) for the acceleration, so the first harmonic comes only from the crank,
  4. the series converge uniformly to the functions (theorem 9.95), so that
    \[x_{\mathrm{B}}(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + a\cos\Omega t + \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{\infty}a_{2\mathrm{k}}\cos 2k\Omega t . \tag{23.6}\]
Proof

We must show that the root part loses all odd harmonics because of two symmetries, and then carry this over to \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) and to the acceleration.

  1. We write \(x_{\mathrm{B}}(t) = a\cos\Omega t + g(t)\) with \(g(t) = s(\Omega t)\) and the acceleration \(\ddot x_{\mathrm{B}} = -a\Omega^2\cos\Omega t + \ddot g\) (constant \(\Omega\)). By lemma 23.8, point 2, \(g\) is infinitely differentiable, even and has the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). The derivative of an even function is odd, the second derivative is even, and the derivative of a periodic function has the same period. Hence \(g\) and \(\ddot g\) are both even, differentiable and with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\).
  2. Lemma on even functions: let \(h\) be even, \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\)-periodic and piecewise continuous. Then \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\int_{-\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{p}/2}^{T_{\mathrm{p}}/2}h(t)\sin n\Omega t\,\mathrm{d}t\) (lemma 9.86, point 3). The integrand \(q(t) = h(t)\sin n\Omega t\) is odd, \(q(-t) = -q(t)\), and by reflection (lemma 9.86, point 2, \(c = 0\)) \(\int_{-\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{p}/2}^{T_{\mathrm{p}}/2}q = \int_{-\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{p}/2}^{T_{\mathrm{p}}/2}q(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int_{-\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{p}/2}^{T_{\mathrm{p}}/2}q\), so the integral is zero.
  3. Lemma on the half period: let \(h\) have the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\). We split the interval \([0, T_{\mathrm{p}}]\) into \([0, T_{\mathrm{p}}/2]\) and \([T_{\mathrm{p}}/2, T_{\mathrm{p}}]\). On the second we substitute \(t = u + T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\) (lemma 9.86, point 1), \(h(u + T_{\mathrm{p}}/2) = h(u)\) and \(\cos(n\Omega u + n\pi) = (-1)^n\cos n\Omega u\) by the addition theorem (corollary 8.15, \(\sin n\pi = 0\), \(\cos n\pi = (-1)^n\)). Hence \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{2}{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\big(1 + (-1)^n\big)\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}/2}h(u)\cos n\Omega u\,\mathrm{d}u\), which is zero for odd \(n\).
  4. By step 1, \(g\) and \(\ddot g\) are even and have the period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\), so by steps 2 and 3 they have only cosine terms of even orders.
  5. By orthogonality (lemma 9.87) the function \(\cos\Omega t\) has coefficient \(1\) at \(n = 1\) and zero at the others. The coefficient of the first order is therefore \(a + 0 = a\) for \(x_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(-a\Omega^2 + 0\) for the acceleration, odd \(n \ge 3\) are zero, \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). These are points 1 to 3.
  6. The functions are continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable (lemma 23.8, point 2), so theorem 9.95 applies and we get point 4.

Source: NORTON, PDF pp. 709 to 710 expands the root with the binomial theorem and obtains (13.3) with the terms \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\cos 2\omega t\), higher harmonics are left out. The book has no binomial series, so the proof is ours (the symmetries), and it says that all odd harmonics except the first vanish exactly. The coefficients of higher orders are computed (NumPy), in closed form they are not proved.

The theorem says that all \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\) are zero and that the odd orders above the first vanish. It holds because the cylinder is through \(O\) and because \(\lambda < 1\). What if the cylinder does not pass through \(O\)?

Example 23.13 Offset cylinder (premaknjen valj)

Let us move the cylinder so that its axis is the distance \(e\) above the shaft axis through the point \(O\) (figure 23.7). The crank of length \(a\) turns about \(O\) by the angle \(\theta\), the crank pin \(A\) runs on the circle as before, and the conrod of length \(l\) connects it with the point \(B\) on the piston. The point \(B\) is now at the height \(e\). The same computation as in lemma 23.8, step 2, gives \((x_{\mathrm{B}} - a\cos\theta)^2 + (e - a\sin\theta)^2 = l^2\) and

\[x_{\mathrm{B}}(\theta) = a\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - (a\sin\theta - e)^2} .\]

Let \(e = 10\) mm (engine \(E\) with another cylinder). The radicand contains \(-(a\sin\theta - e)^2 = -a^2\sin^2\theta + 2ae\sin\theta - e^2\) and so has the term \(2ae\sin\theta\), which is odd and has not the period \(\pi\) but \(2\pi\). The root part is \(s = l\sqrt{1 - u}\) with \(u = (a\sin\theta - e)^2/l^2 \le (a + e)^2/l^2 = 0{,}09\). For such a small \(u\) theorem 8.60 gives \(\sqrt{1 - u} \approx 1 - u/2\) (we estimate the error in lemma 23.14 below), so \(s \approx l - \frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{2l} + \frac{ae}{l}\sin\theta - \frac{e^2}{2l}\). The term with \(\sin\theta\) has the amplitude \(ae/l = \lambda e = 2{,}5\) mm, and on double differentiation it gives a sine term with coefficient \(-e/l = -0{,}05\) in units of \(a\Omega^2\). The root part \(s\) is therefore no longer even and no longer half-periodic: by the approximation \(s(\theta + \pi) - s(\theta) \approx -2\lambda e\sin\theta\), at most \(2\lambda e = 5\) mm, and by the exact computation \(5{,}17\) mm (NumPy).

The computed coefficients of the acceleration in units of \(a\Omega^2\) (NumPy): cosine \(-1{,}000000\), \(-0{,}255045\), \(0\), \(+0{,}004181\), \(0\), \(-0{,}000078\) (orders \(1\) to \(6\)), and sine \(b_1 = -0{,}051291\), \(b_2 = 0\), \(b_3 = +0{,}003759\), \(b_4 = 0\), \(b_5 = -0{,}000128\). Theorem 23.12 fails in both points: the sine coefficients are not zero and the odd orders 3 and 5 differ from zero. Figure 23.6 shows at the bottom the amplitudes \(A_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{a_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{n}}^2}\) of the harmonics of the acceleration on a logarithmic scale. The orange bars at \(n = 3\) and \(5\) stand above the blue ones, which are zero there. The blue and the orange bars differ only a little at \(n = 2\) and \(4\).

The dead positions also move. The velocity of \(B\) is zero exactly when \(OA\) and \(AB\) are parallel: the length \(AB\) is constant, so \((\dot{\vek A} - \dot{\vek B})\cdot(\vek A - \vek B) = 0\), and with \(\dot{\vek B} = \vek 0\) we get \(\dot{\vek A}\perp(\vek A - \vek B)\). Since \(\dot{\vek A}\perp\vek{OA}\) and \(\dot{\vek A} \ne \vek 0\), the lines \(OA\) and \(AB\) coincide. Conversely, if they are on one line, then \(\dot{\vek A}\cdot(\vek A - \vek B) = 0\) and so \(\dot{\vek B}\cdot(\vek A - \vek B) = 0\). The velocity \(\dot{\vek B}\) lies on the cylinder axis, and the conrod is not perpendicular to it, because the vertical difference is \(|a\sin\theta - e| \le a + e < l\), so \(\dot{\vek B} = \vek 0\). Since \(O\), \(A\), \(B\) are on one line, \(\sin\theta = e/(l + a)\) in the top and \(\theta = \pi + \arcsin(e/(l - a))\) in the bottom position, that is \(2{,}29°\) and \(183{,}82°\), and not \(0\) and \(\pi\). The stroke is \(100{,}134\) mm, not \(100\) mm, and the peaks of the acceleration are \(-1{,}25136\) and \(+0{,}75991\), not \(-1{,}25\) and \(0{,}75\).

Source: The example is ours (NumPy). NORTON, PDF pp. 706 and 707, treats only the slider-crank mechanism without offset ("nonoffset"), because the cylinder runs through the crank axis.

Now we know when the theorem holds: for a cylinder through \(O\) and \(\lambda < 1\) the piston has only the first harmonic from the crank and the even higher ones. One more question remains. Computing with the complete function, which has infinitely many harmonics, is too hard if we are interested in only the first few. How large is the error if we keep only the first two terms?

Lemma 23.14 Two-term approximation of the piston position (dvočlenski približek lege bata)

Let \(\lambda < 1\). For every angle \(\theta\)

\[x_{\mathrm{B}}(\theta) = l\Big(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{4}\Big) + a\Big(\cos\theta + \frac{\lambda}{4}\cos 2\theta\Big) + R(\theta) , \qquad |R(\theta)| \le \frac{l\,\lambda^4}{8\,(1 - \lambda^2)^{3/2}} . \tag{23.7}\]
Proof

We must show that the root part \(s\) up to the term with \(\lambda^2\) is a polynomial in \(\sin^2\theta\), and estimate the remainder with Taylor's theorem.

  1. We write \(s = l\sqrt{1 - u}\) with \(u = \lambda^2\sin^2\theta \in [0, \lambda^2] \subset [0, 1)\).
  2. Let \(f(u) = \sqrt{1 - u}\). The derivative \(f'(u) = -\tfrac12(1 - u)^{-1/2}\) is continuous on \([0, \lambda^2]\) and \(f''(u) = -\tfrac14(1 - u)^{-3/2}\) exists on \((0, \lambda^2)\) (lemma 8.5). By theorem 8.60 at \(x_0 = 0\) we have \(f(u) = 1 - \tfrac u2 + \tfrac12 f''(c)\,u^2\) with some point \(c \in (0, u)\).
  3. Since \((1 - c)^{-3/2}\) increases with \(c\) and \(c < \lambda^2\), we have \(|\tfrac12 f''(c)| \le \tfrac18(1 - \lambda^2)^{-3/2}\). Since \(u \le \lambda^2\), we have \(\big|f(u) - (1 - \tfrac u2)\big| \le \tfrac18\lambda^4(1 - \lambda^2)^{-3/2}\).
  4. We multiply by \(l\): \(s = l - \tfrac{l u}{2} + R\) with \(|R| \le l\lambda^4/(8(1 - \lambda^2)^{3/2})\).
  5. With \(\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)\) (corollary 8.15, \(\cos 2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta\)) we have \(\tfrac{lu}{2} = \tfrac{l\lambda^2}{2}\sin^2\theta = \tfrac{l\lambda^2}{4}(1 - \cos 2\theta)\).
  6. So \(s = l - \tfrac{l\lambda^2}{4} + \tfrac{l\lambda^2}{4}\cos 2\theta + R\). With \(l\lambda^2/4 = a\lambda/4\) and \(x_{\mathrm{B}} = a\cos\theta + s\) we obtain equation ((23.7)).

Source: NORTON (13.3), PDF p. 710, which keeps the first two terms of the binomial expansion and says that the error is "only a fraction of one percent". The estimate of the remainder is ours (theorem 8.60 instead of the binomial series).

How large is this estimate? For engine \(E\) we have \(\lambda^4 = 1/256\) and \((1 - \lambda^2)^{3/2} = 0{,}9077\), so \(|R| \le 107{,}58\) µm, that is \(0{,}108\) % of the stroke (NumPy). The true largest deviation over a turn is \(100{,}83\) µm at \(\theta = 90°\). The two-term approximation thus gives the position of the piston to about a tenth of a millimetre at a stroke of \(100\) mm. Why is the error so small? The remainder is quadratic in \(u = \lambda^2\sin^2\theta \le 1/16 = 0{,}0625\): by step 3 of the proof it is at most \(l\,u^2/(8(1 - \lambda^2)^{3/2})\), and \(u^2 \le 1/256\). At \(\lambda = 1/3\) the estimate is \(0{,}184\) % of \(l\), that is \(0{,}28\) % of the stroke, at \(\lambda = 1/5\) it is \(0{,}053\) % of the stroke (NumPy), which agrees with NORTON's sentence about a fraction of a percent for the upper limit of \(\lambda\).

The acceleration from the two-term approximation is \(-a\Omega^2(\cos\Omega t + \lambda\cos 2\Omega t)\) (a finite sum differentiated twice). The Taylor estimate is for the position, not for the derivatives, and for the acceleration another computation replaces it. By theorem 9.95 the error of the acceleration is at most the sum of the absolute differences of the coefficients: \(|0{,}254025 - 0{,}25| + 0{,}004098 + 0{,}000074 + \dots = 0{,}00820\,a\Omega^2\), which is also the computed largest deviation (at \(\theta = 90°\), NumPy: exactly \(0{,}25820\), two-term \(0{,}25000\)). It is therefore \(0{,}66\) % of the largest acceleration \(1{,}25\,a\Omega^2\). The first two harmonics thus give the acceleration of engine \(E\) to better than one percent, which the difference, enlarged fifty times, in figure 23.6 also shows.

The section showed where the piston is at every angle (equation (23.5)), that the angle \(\theta\) is the coordinate of the mechanism in every position (lemma 23.8), that the stroke is exactly \(2a\) (lemma 23.10) and that in the acceleration of the piston, for a cylinder through \(O\), there are only the first and the even harmonics (theorem 23.12), the first two of which suffice to an eighth of a percent in the position (lemma 23.14). These results will come back. The acceleration \(-a\Omega^2(\cos\theta + \lambda\cos 2\theta)\) we will multiply by the mass in section 23.3. The harmonics of theorem 23.12 we will add over several cylinders in section 23.7 and see that for some arrangements of the cranks whole harmonics vanish, because their vectors add up to zero. The primary harmonic has the scale \(a\Omega^2\) and the secondary \(\lambda a\Omega^2\), which for engine \(E\) at \(1500\) 1/min gives \(1233{,}70\) and \(308{,}4\) m/s\(^2\).
