Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.3 Mathematics: implicit functions and Lagrange multipliers (matematika: implicitna funkcija in Lagrangeevi množitelji)

The toggle mechanism of section 8.1 (figure 8.2) has two rigid bars AB and BC of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, a pin at point A, a hinge at point B, a slider C in a smooth horizontal guide through A, a horizontal spring between A and C and a vertical load \(P\) at B. We described its position with a single angle \(\theta\), the angle of bar AB above the horizontal, because the mechanism can move in only one way. For other mechanisms we do not know in advance how many numbers we need and which ones. The question is therefore general: how many numbers can we choose freely, and when do they determine all the others? We start the answer by counting on a figure that shows the bars apart on the left and the assembled mechanism on the right.

Let us count. In the plane we describe every rigid body with three numbers: the two coordinates of its centre and an angle (rigid body, definition 1.11). That these three numbers determine the position of all points of the body we prove in section 8.4.1, here we only use them for an example. Take the bars apart, as if nothing connected them. Two free bars give six numbers, which we write as the column \(\vek x = (x_1, y_1, \theta_1, x_2, y_2, \theta_2)\). The coordinates \(x_1\), \(y_1\) give the centre \(S_1\) of bar AB, \(\theta_1\) is its angle above the horizontal, and \(x_2\), \(y_2\) and \(\theta_2\) are the same for bar BC, where \(\theta_2\) is the angle of the direction from B to C. In the figure bar BC falls from B towards C, so \(\theta_2\) is measured clockwise and is negative. Angles are in radians (section 8.2). Now we assemble the bars into the mechanism. Point A is at the origin, and half a bar has length \(a/2\). In the assembled mechanism bar AB and bar BC with the centres \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) and the angles \(\theta_1 = \theta\) and \(\theta_2\) are drawn in the axes \(x\) and \(y\). The connections between the bars and to the surroundings impose conditions that these six numbers must satisfy:

\[\begin{aligned} f_1 &= x_1 - \tfrac{a}{2}\cos\theta_1 = 0 , \\ f_2 &= y_1 - \tfrac{a}{2}\sin\theta_1 = 0 , \\ f_3 &= x_1 + \tfrac{a}{2}\cos\theta_1 \\ &\quad - x_2 + \tfrac{a}{2}\cos\theta_2 = 0 , \\ f_4 &= y_1 + \tfrac{a}{2}\sin\theta_1 \\ &\quad - y_2 + \tfrac{a}{2}\sin\theta_2 = 0 , \\ f_5 &= y_2 + \tfrac{a}{2}\sin\theta_2 = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.14}\]

The first two equations say that the end of bar AB that lies \(a/2\) from its centre towards the pin is at the origin. The next two say that the end of bar AB and the start of bar BC, point B, agree in both coordinates. In the left picture these are still two different points named B, and the equations \(f_3 = 0\) and \(f_4 = 0\) glue them into one. The last one says that end C is on the horizontal line through A. These are five equations in six unknowns. We expect them to leave one free number, because each equation ties one number: the free bars have six numbers, the pin ties two of them, the hinge two and the guide one, and \(6 - 5 = 1\) is left. Why one equation ties one number is shown by the linear case below. The count is only an expectation, though: in the straight position (subsection 8.3.4) some numbers cannot be free, although there are still five equations. We name such conditions in section 8.4. Here we only need the mathematics that tells when five equations leave exactly one free number.

The question has a second side. We must look for the equilibrium among the positions of the mechanism that satisfy the equations. In section 8.5 we will see that we find the equilibrium as a point where the energy on this set does not change to first order. How do we find the smallest value of a function of six variables if the variables may only be such that equations (8.14) hold? The answer the section finds also gives the forces of the connections, which we do not see in the calculation with the angle \(\theta\): for a load of \(100\) N we will find at the end of the section that the guide pushes the slider upwards with a force of \(50\) N. The section is mathematical. The mechanism returns in the examples at its end, and we continue with it in section 8.4.

8.3.1 The linear case (linearni primer)

We start with equations that are linear. Later, in a small neighbourhood, we replace nonlinear equations by linear ones (how much this misses we measure on a circle in subsection 8.3.3). Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix with \(m\) rows and \(N\) columns, \(N > m\), and we look for columns \(\vek x\) with \(\mat A\vek x = \vek 0\). In section 6.3.4 we carried out Gaussian elimination without row exchanges, because for a positive definite matrix the pivot on the diagonal is never zero (theorem 6.29). For the matrix \(\mat A\) this is not guaranteed: in the column whose turn it is, the entry in the pivot position may be zero, or the remaining rows of that column may contain only zeros. We therefore describe the elimination precisely and then try it on a figure. In the figure 8.33 the column without a pivot is skipped.

Definition 8.21 Matrix with a given number of pivots (matrika z danim številom pivotov)

Let \(\mat A\) be a matrix of size \(m \times N\). Gaussian elimination with row exchanges treats the rows in turn, \(k = 1, 2, \dots\). In step \(k\) it chooses the first column to the right of the column of the previous pivot that has a number different from zero in one of the rows \(k, \dots, m\). If necessary it exchanges row \(k\) with such a row, and this number in row \(k\) is the pivot of row \(k\). Then it subtracts from the rows below such multiples of row \(k\) that there are only zeros below the pivot. If there is no such column, the elimination stops. The matrix \(\mat A\) has \(m\) pivots (ima \(m\) pivotov) if the elimination, for some choice of exchanged rows, finds a pivot in every row. The columns in which the pivots lie are the pivot columns. In the figure 8.33 the pivots are marked, and the skipped column is not a pivot column.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.3 (PDF pp. 300-302) for elimination, the pivot and the exchange of rows (partial pivoting), PDF p. 305 for the row echelon form, in which the first nonzero number of each row lies to the right of the first one in the previous row.

Let us watch the elimination on a matrix with three rows and five columns, whose columns belong to the unknowns \(x_1\) to \(x_5\). In the first step the first column of the first row holds \(1\), this is the pivot of the first row. We make zeros below it: from the second row we subtract twice the first, and from the third the first, where we write row \(k\) as \(v_{\mathrm{k}}\). In the second step it is the turn of the second column, but it has only zeros in the second and third rows. A row exchange does not help here, so the elimination skips the column and finds the pivot of the second row in the third column. When we subtract the second row from the third, only a \(1\) in the fifth column is left in it, the pivot of the third row. The pivots form a staircase that descends one row at a time, while to the right it may jump over several columns. The final matrix has three pivots, and columns \(1\), \(3\) and \(5\) are the pivot columns.

Let \(\mat A\) have \(m\) pivots (definition 8.21), like the matrix in the figure. The unknowns in the pivot columns are determined from the others by back substitution, so we call them dependent, and the other \(N - m\) unknowns independent or free. In the figure \(x_2\) and \(x_4\) are free: we choose them at will, then the last row gives \(x_5 = 0\), the second \(x_3 = -x_4\) and the first \(x_1 = -2x_2 - x_4\). Every row with a pivot is one equation that ties one unknown, which is the reason for the count in figure 8.32. We number the unknowns so that the dependent ones come last (the order of the unknowns does not affect the equations). We write \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek z)\), where \(\vek y\) is the column of the \(N - m\) free and \(\vek z\) the column of the \(m\) dependent unknowns, and \(\mat A = [\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\ \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}]\), where \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the square matrix of order \(m\) formed by the pivot columns.

\(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) also has \(m\) pivots. The same elimination steps are an elimination of the matrix \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\): in step \(k\) the pivot column is the first column of \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) to the right of the previous one and has a number different from zero in row \(k\). In figure 8.33 \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) consists of columns \(1\), \(3\) and \(5\), and the same steps find the pivots on its diagonal. We need the conclusion that \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) is therefore invertible. We prove this for every order \(n\), because the book has determinants only up to order \(3\).

Lemma 8.22 Square matrix with a full set of pivots (kvadratna matrika s polnim številom pivotov)

Let \(\mat A\) be a square matrix of order \(n\). The matrix \(\mat A\) has \(n\) pivots (definition 8.21) if and only if \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek w = \vek 0\). In this case every elimination of \(\mat A\) finds \(n\) pivots, the system \(\mat A\vek w = \vek b\) has exactly one solution for every column \(\vek b\), the matrix \(\mat A\) has the inverse matrix \(\mat A^{-1}\) (definition 1.110), and the matrix \(\mat A^\T\) also has \(n\) pivots and the inverse matrix \((\mat A^\T)^{-1} = (\mat A^{-1})^\T\). The lower matrix in the figure 8.33 has too few pivots and a nonzero solution.

Proof

We must show three things: that the pivots give a unique solution, that the statement about \(\vek w = \vek 0\) follows from this, and that the pivots follow from it for every elimination. Then we assemble the inverse matrix from solutions.

  1. Every elimination step, exchanging two rows or subtracting a multiple of one row from another, gives a system with the same solutions, because the step is undone by the reverse step. Let the elimination find \(n\) pivots. Each pivot column lies to the right of the previous one and there are \(n\) columns, so the pivot of row \(k\) is in column \(k\). Below the pivots there are zeros, so we obtain an upper triangular system \(\mat U\vek w = \vek c\) with \(U_{\mathrm{kk}} \neq 0\) for every \(k\). In the figure these are the steps that turn the matrix into echelon form.
  2. The last equation \(U_{\mathrm{nn}}w_{\mathrm{n}} = c_{\mathrm{n}}\) determines \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) uniquely. Once \(w_{\mathrm{k}+1}, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\) are determined, equation \(k\) gives \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = (c_{\mathrm{k}} - \sum_{\mathrm{j} > \mathrm{k}} U_{\mathrm{kj}}w_{\mathrm{j}})/U_{\mathrm{kk}}\), uniquely. Hence \(\mat A\vek w = \vek b\) has exactly one solution for every \(\vek b\). In the figure we solve from the last row upwards.
  3. For \(\vek b = \vek 0\), \(\vek w = \vek 0\) is a solution, and by step 2 the only one. So the statement about \(\vek w = \vek 0\) follows from \(n\) pivots. With full pivots there is no free unknown, the opposite is the lower matrix with a nonzero solution.
  4. Conversely, let \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\) imply \(\vek w = \vek 0\), and suppose that some elimination does not find \(n\) pivots. Then some column is not a pivot column. Let \(k\) be the smallest such column. Columns \(1, \dots, k - 1\) are the pivot columns of rows \(1, \dots, k - 1\), and the elimination did not choose column \(k\) in step \(k\), so this column has only zeros in rows \(k\) to \(n\). Rows \(k\) to \(n\) then have zeros in columns \(1\) to \(k\). We choose \(w_{\mathrm{k}} = 1\) and \(w_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \dots = w_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), and \(w_{\mathrm{k}-1}, \dots, w_1\) by back substitution from the first \(k - 1\) rows, which have their pivots on the diagonal. Rows \(k\) to \(n\) are satisfied, because they multiply only zeros in columns \(1\) to \(k\) and the unknowns \(w_{\mathrm{k}+1}, \dots, w_{\mathrm{n}}\), which are zero. By step 1 this is a nonzero solution of \(\mat A\vek w = \vek 0\), a contradiction with the hypothesis. Hence every elimination finds \(n\) pivots. In figure 8.33, bottom, the same steps as above turn the square matrix into a matrix with only two pivots. The second column has no pivot, so \(k = 2\), the third row is zero, and \(\vek w = (-1/2,\ 1,\ 0)\) with \(w_2 = 1\), \(w_3 = 0\) and \(w_1\) from the first row is a nonzero solution.
  5. Let \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}\) be the solution of the system \(\mat A\vek b_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) (\(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) is column \(j\) of the identity matrix) and \(\mat B\) the matrix with columns \(\vek b_{\mathrm{j}}\). Then \(\mat A\mat B = \mat I\). For every \(\vek w\), by lemma 1.100, item 1, and lemma 1.109, \(\mat A(\mat B\mat A\vek w) = (\mat A\mat B)(\mat A\vek w) = \mat A\vek w\), so by step 2 \(\mat B\mat A\vek w = \vek w\). For \(\vek w = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) we find that column \(j\) of \(\mat B\mat A\) equals \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), hence \(\mat B\mat A = \mat I\) and \(\mat B = \mat A^{-1}\). The product \(\mat A\mat B\) is step \(\mat B\) and then step \(\mat A\), as in the figure 1.370.
  6. By lemma 1.102, \(\mat A^\T(\mat A^{-1})^\T = (\mat A^{-1}\mat A)^\T = \mat I\) and \((\mat A^{-1})^\T\mat A^\T = (\mat A\mat A^{-1})^\T = \mat I\), because \(\mat I^\T = \mat I\). The matrix \(\mat A^\T\) therefore has the inverse matrix \((\mat A^{-1})^\T\). From \(\mat A^\T\vek w = \vek 0\) it follows that \(\vek w = (\mat A^{-1})^\T\mat A^\T\vek w = \vek 0\), and by step 4 \(\mat A^\T\) has \(n\) pivots. Transposing reverses the order of the steps of the product.

Source: KREYSZIG §7.3 (PDF pp. 300-302) for elimination, the pivot and back substitution, Theorem 1 (PDF p. 303) that systems related by elimination steps have the same solutions. The proof for any order is not from a source.

The lemma is proved: a square matrix that has all its pivots is invertible, and conversely. So \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) is invertible, and the equation \(\mat A\vek x = \vek 0\), that is \(\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\vek y + \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\vek z = \vek 0\), is solved for every \(\vek y\) by exactly one \(\vek z = -\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\vek y\). This is the linear version of what we will prove for nonlinear equations. In the figure 8.33 the dependent unknowns are in the pivot columns, and the free ones are chosen arbitrarily.

8.3.2 Earlier results in \(n\) variables (rezultati prejšnjih poglavij v \(n\) spremenljivkah)

For nonlinear equations we need theorems of analysis from chapters 1 and 4: the theorem on the change of a field over a short step, the chain rule, the extreme value theorem and the inverse function theorem. There we proved them for points of space with three coordinates, for points of the plane or for numbers. The mechanism has six coordinates, and the systems in the rest of the chapter have even more. The number of coordinates appears in these proofs in three kinds of places. The first are sums over coordinates and the counting of bands, where we replace the number \(3\) by \(n\). The second are coordinates written out with the names \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). The third are the determinant and the cross product, which we do not have for \(n\) coordinates. For each result we therefore say which places its proof has. Where it has only the first kind, the proof holds word for word, and where it has the second or third kind, we write the steps again. In this subsection \(n\) is the number of coordinates, so we write the sequence index, which is \(n\) in the earlier chapters, as \(l\). The subsection brings no new idea: we drew every proof in the chapter where we carried it out, and the steps here point at the same figures, only with \(n\) axes instead of three. A reader who wants to follow the mechanism first can continue at subsection 8.3.3 and return here when the theorem needs its proof.

Definition 8.23 Points with any number of coordinates (točke s poljubnim številom koordinat)

Let \(n\) be a natural number. A point with \(n\) coordinates (točka z \(n\) koordinatami) is a column \(\vek x = (x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}})\) with \(n\) entries (definition 1.94), which we call its coordinates. We add columns and multiply them by a number entry by entry (definition 1.95). The column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the number \(1\) in place \(j\) and \(0\) elsewhere. The dot product of the columns \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) is the number \(\vek x\cdot\vek y = x_1 y_1 + \dots + x_{\mathrm{n}} y_{\mathrm{n}}\), the length of a column is \(|\vek x| = \sqrt{\vek x\cdot\vek x}\), and the distance between the points \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) is \(|\vek x - \vek y|\). With them we define: For \(n = 3\) this is a point among the coordinate axes.

  1. The ball (krogla) with centre \(\vek x_0\) and radius \(r > 0\) is the set of points \(\vek x\) with \(|\vek x - \vek x_0| < r\), and the closed ball is the set of points with \(|\vek x - \vek x_0| \le r\). A set \(D\) is open if for each of its points it contains some ball with centre at that point. For three coordinates the ball with its radius is as in the figure 1.265, and an open set has a ball around each of its points.
  2. The limit of a sequence of points, a closed and bounded set and a continuous function of a point are defined by the texts of definitions 1.66, 1.75 and 1.68, in which \(|PQ|\) is the distance of points with \(n\) coordinates and the origin \(O\) is the column \(\vek 0\). For three coordinates they are shown by the ball with a sequence, the closed set with a limit and the ball around a point with an error.
  3. The partial derivative of a function \(f\) that is defined in some ball with centre \(\vek x\), with respect to the coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\), is the limit
    \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(\vek x) = \lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{f(\vek x + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}) - f(\vek x)}{h} ,\]
    if this limit is defined (definition 1.26). Second partial derivatives are partial derivatives of partial derivatives (definition 1.130). The column \(\nabla f(\vek x)\) with entries \(\partial f/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek x)\) is the gradient (gradient) of the function \(f\) at the point \(\vek x\). The step \(h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) goes along axis \(j\) and changes no other coordinate.
  4. A continuously differentiable function on an open set is defined by the text of definition 1.91, with \(n\) partial derivatives. A map that assigns points with \(k\) coordinates to points with \(n\) coordinates, its components \(\Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_{\mathrm{k}}\), its continuous differentiability and its matrix of partial derivatives \(\mat F_\Phi\) of size \(k \times n\) are defined by the text of definition 4.42, and a one-to-one map and the inverse map by the text of definition 4.45. For three coordinates the map \(\Phi\) and its inverse \(\Psi\) are shown in the figure 4.83.

Source: LEBL2 Def. 8.2.1 and the text before Thm. 8.2.2 (PDF p. 20) for the length in \(\mathbb R^n\), Def. 8.3.8 (PDF p. 39) for the partial derivative and Def. 8.4.5 (PDF p. 48) for continuous differentiability of functions of \(n\) variables, LEBL1 Def. 7.2.4 (PDF p. 264) for an open set.

For \(n = 3\) these are the coordinates, the dot product and the distance of chapter 2, and the definitions agree with those of chapters 1 and 4. First we check that the distance with \(n\) coordinates has the properties the proofs use. The figure 1.267 shows that the distance is the diagonal of a box with sides that are the coordinate differences.

Lemma 8.24 Distance and a matrix bound for any number of coordinates (razdalja in ocena matrike za poljubno število koordinat)

For all columns \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\), \(\vek z\) with \(n\) entries and every number \(h\): Item 1 is shown by the rectangles in the plane of the two columns, items 2 and 3 by the box, and item 4 by the ellipse into which the matrix maps the unit circle.

  1. \(|\vek x\cdot\vek y| \le |\vek x|\,|\vek y|\) (Cauchy-Schwarz inequality), The figure 8.34 shows that the scalar product is below the bound and equal to it only for parallel columns.
  2. \(|\vek x + \vek y| \le |\vek x| + |\vek y|\) and \(|\vek x - \vek z| \le |\vek x - \vek y| + |\vek y - \vek z|\), Item 2 says that the straight path is shorter than the path through an intermediate point.
  3. \(|h\,\vek x| = |h|\,|\vek x|\) and \(|x_{\mathrm{i}}| \le |\vek x| \le |x_1| + \dots + |x_{\mathrm{n}}|\) for every \(i\), Item 3 is shown by the diagonal, the each edge and the path along the edges.
  4. if every entry of a matrix \(\mat A\) of size \(k \times n\) is at most \(g\) in absolute value, then \(|\mat A\vek x| \le c\,g\,|\vek x|\), where \(c\) is the larger of the numbers \(k\) and \(n\). For a square matrix \(c = n\). The figure 4.85 shows for two coordinates how the length \(|\mat A\vek v|\) is bounded by the bound.
Proof

We must show four items for any number of coordinates. We derive all of them from the properties of the dot product, as in chapters 2 and 6.

The columns \(\vek x\) and \(\vek y\) lie in a plane, so we can see item 1 in a figure although \(n\) is not limited. We read the figure as for three coordinates in chapter 2. We draw the columns as arrows from the origin and the angle \(\vartheta\) between them. Projecting the arrow \(\vek y\) perpendicularly onto the direction of the arrow \(\vek x\) gives the projection of length \(|\vek y|\cos\vartheta\). The scalar product is then the rectangle with sides \(|\vek x|\) and \(|\vek y|\cos\vartheta\). It lies in the rectangle with sides \(|\vek x|\) and \(|\vek y|\), because \(|\cos\vartheta| \le 1\). The rectangles are equal only when the arrows are parallel. The figure shows the meaning of the inequality, the proof is in lemma 6.19.

  1. By lemma 1.96 the columns with \(n\) entries form a vector space (definition 1.16). The dot product of columns has properties 1 to 3 of definition 6.16, because every term \(x_{\mathrm{i}} y_{\mathrm{i}}\) of the sum has them by the rules of arithmetic with numbers. It also has property 4, because \(\vek x\cdot\vek x = x_1^2 + \dots + x_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) is a sum of nonnegative numbers, which is positive when some \(x_{\mathrm{i}} \neq 0\). By lemma 6.19 item 1 therefore holds. For two coordinates item 1 is shown by the figure 8.34: the scalar product is a rectangle that lies in the bound rectangle.
  2. By properties 1 to 3, \(|\vek x + \vek y|^2 = |\vek x|^2 + 2\,\vek x\cdot\vek y + |\vek y|^2\). By item 1 this is at most \(|\vek x|^2 + 2\,|\vek x|\,|\vek y| + |\vek y|^2 = (|\vek x| + |\vek y|)^2\), and by lemma 1.29 the first inequality of item 2 follows. The second is the first one for the columns \(\vek x - \vek y\) and \(\vek y - \vek z\), whose sum is \(\vek x - \vek z\). In the figure of the triangle \(|\vek x + \vek y|\) is the straight path and the sum of the lengths is the path through the intermediate point.
  3. We have \(|h\,\vek x|^2 = h^2\,(x_1^2 + \dots + x_{\mathrm{n}}^2) = (|h|\,|\vek x|)^2\), and since both numbers are nonnegative, they are equal by lemma 1.29. We have \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^2 \le x_1^2 + \dots + x_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = |\vek x|^2\), so by the same lemma \(|x_{\mathrm{i}}| \le |\vek x|\). The square of the sum \(s = |x_1| + \dots + |x_{\mathrm{n}}|\) is the sum of all products \(|x_{\mathrm{i}}|\,|x_{\mathrm{j}}|\), among which are the squares \(x_{\mathrm{i}}^2\), while the others are not negative. Hence \(s^2 \ge |\vek x|^2\) and \(s \ge |\vek x|\). These are steps 2 and 3 of the proof of lemma 1.67 with \(n\) coordinates. With three coordinates this is visible on a box: the diagonal is longer than every edge and shorter than the path along three edges. With \(n\) coordinates there are \(n\) edges.
  4. Let \(\vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\) be row \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A\), written as a column with \(n\) entries. By (1.39), \((\mat A\vek x)_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek p_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\vek x\), and by item 1 and lemma 1.29, \((\mat A\vek x)_{\mathrm{i}}^2 \le |\vek p_{\mathrm{i}}|^2\,|\vek x|^2 \le n\,g^2\,|\vek x|^2\), because a row has \(n\) entries with square at most \(g^2\). The sum over the \(k\) rows gives \(|\mat A\vek x|^2 \le k\,n\,g^2\,|\vek x|^2 \le c^2 g^2\,|\vek x|^2\), and by lemma 1.29 \(|\mat A\vek x| \le c\,g\,|\vek x|\). These are steps 1 to 5 of the proof of lemma 4.46 with \(k\) rows and \(n\) columns. Each row gives one component of the image, bounded by the bound.

Lemma 8.25 Sequences and continuity for any number of coordinates (zaporedja in zveznost za poljubno število koordinat)

For points with \(n\) coordinates (definition 8.23): For three coordinates the figure 1.265 shows a sequence and a ball around the limit.

  1. A sequence of points \((\vek x_{\mathrm{l}})\) converges to a point \(\vek x\) if and only if for every \(i\) the sequence of the coordinates \(i\) of the points \(\vek x_{\mathrm{l}}\) converges to \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\). For three coordinates this is the projection of the sequence onto an axis.
  2. A function \(f\) defined on a set \(D\) is continuous at a point \(\vek x \in D\) if and only if for every sequence of points of \(D\) that converges to \(\vek x\), the sequence of values of \(f\) converges to \(f(\vek x)\). The figure 1.270 shows the points of the sequence, their values and the value at the limit.
  3. The sum, a multiple and the product of functions that are continuous at a point are continuous at that point. Every polynomial in the variables \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}\) is continuous at every point. It is shown by the error in a band around the value, which is followed by the ball around the point.
  4. Let \(f\) be continuous at every point and \(c\) a number. The sets of points with \(f(\vek x) = c\), with \(f(\vek x) \le c\) and with \(f(\vek x) \ge c\) are closed. The set of points that lie in two closed sets is closed. The figure 1.296 shows the sets \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) and a sequence whose limit stays in the set.

Theorem 8.28 Change of a function of several variables over a short step (sprememba funkcije več spremenljivk na kratkem koraku)

We divide the error by \(n\) because in step 6 we will add \(n\) such errors.
  2. Let \(\delta\) be the smallest of the numbers \(r, \delta_1, \dots, \delta_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\vek q\) a point with \(0 < |\vek q - \vek p| < \delta\). By item 3 of lemma 8.24, \(|\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}| \le |\vek\Delta|\) for every \(j\).

We go from \(\vek p\) to \(\vek q\) in \(n\) steps along the axes, through the points \(\vek a_0 = \vek p\) and \(\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), so that \(\vek a_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek q\). The point \(\vek a_{\mathrm{j}-1} + t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(t\) between \(0\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) has the first \(j - 1\) coordinates equal to those of \(\vek q\), coordinate \(j\) equal to \(p_{\mathrm{j}} + t\), and the others equal to those of \(\vek p\). Its distance from \(\vek p\) is \(\sqrt{\Delta_1^2 + \dots + \Delta_{\mathrm{j}-1}^2 + t^2} \le |\vek\Delta|\), because \(t^2 \le \Delta_{\mathrm{j}}^2\) (lemma 1.29). All points of the path are therefore in the ball with centre \(\vek p\) and radius \(\delta\).

  6. By steps 4 and 5 and by (8.15), \(R(\vek q) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(\vek t_{\mathrm{j}}) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}}(\vek p)\big)\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}\). By step 3 the point \(\vek t_{\mathrm{j}}\) is less than \(\delta \le \delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) away from \(\vek p\), so by step 1 each bracket is less than \(\eta/n\) in absolute value. By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6, items 1 and 2) and step 2, \(|R(\vek q)| \le n \cdot \frac{\eta}{n}\,|\vek\Delta| = \eta\,|\vek q - \vek p|\). This is item 1.
  7. Item 2 is lemma 1.92. Its proof uses the theorem on the change of a field, which item 1 replaces, and the bound \(|\Delta_{\mathrm{j}}| \le |\vek\Delta|\) from item 3 of lemma 8.24. The number \(M\) is the sum of \(n\) absolute values of partial derivatives instead of three. Otherwise the proof holds word for word and gives \(|f(\vek q) - f(\vek p)| \le (M + 1)\,|\vek q - \vek p|\) for points \(\vek q\) close to \(\vek p\). The figure 1.340 shows the step \(PQ\) in a ball of radius \(\delta\).

Lemma 8.29 Derivative along a segment for any number of coordinates (odvod vzdolž daljice za poljubno število koordinat)

Lemma 8.32 Differentiating an integral with respect to several parameters (odvod integrala po več parametrih)

Let \(W\) be a closed interval \([\alpha, \beta]\) or a set with volume (definition 4.27), \(U\) an open set of points with \(n\) coordinates (definition 8.23) and \(f\) a function that assigns a number \(f(t, \vek s)\) to a point \(t\) of \(W\) and a point \(\vek s\) of \(U\). The point \((t, \vek s)\) has \(1 + n\) or \(3 + n\) coordinates. Suppose that for every \(t\) in \(W\) the function \(\vek s \mapsto f(t, \vek s)\) has at every point of \(U\) the partial derivatives \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial s_{\mathrm{i}}}(t, \vek s)\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), and that \(f\) and these partial derivatives are continuous on the set of points \((t, \vek s)\) with \(t \in W\) and \(\vek s \in U\). Then the function \(G(\vek s) = \int_{\mathrm{W}} f(t, \vek s)\,\mathrm{d}t\) is continuously differentiable on \(U\) and

\[\frac{\partial G}{\partial s_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek s) = \int_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial s_{\mathrm{i}}}(t, \vek s)\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{8.19}\]

For a set with volume, \(t\) is a point of space and \(\mathrm{d}t\) means \(\mathrm{d}V\). The figure 4.40 shows the gained and the lost part of the area under the integrand when the parameter changes.

Proof

We must show the existence of the partial derivatives and their continuity. We follow the first part of the proof of lemma 4.19, except that the parameter runs over a ball and that we integrate over \(W\).

  1. Let \(\vek s_0 \in U\) and \(i\) an index. The set \(U\) is open, so there is \(\rho > 0\) such that the closed ball \(K\) with centre \(\vek s_0\) and radius \(\rho\) lies in \(U\) (half the radius of the ball from the definition of an open set). Let \(V_{\mathrm{W}}\) be the length \(\beta - \alpha\) or the volume of the set \(W\). The closed ball \(K\) is like the ball around a point \(P\) inside a body.
  2. The set of points \((t, \vek s)\) with \(t \in W\) and \(\vek s \in K\) is closed and bounded. If its points \((t_{\mathrm{l}}, \vek s_{\mathrm{l}})\) converge to \((t, \vek s)\), then by item 1 of lemma 8.25 \(t_{\mathrm{l}}\) converges to \(t\) and \(\vek s_{\mathrm{l}}\) to \(\vek s\). The interval is closed by lemma 1.49, a set with volume by definition, and the ball \(K\) by item 4 of lemma 8.25, because \(|\vek s - \vek s_0|^2\) is a polynomial. Hence \(t \in W\) and \(\vek s \in K\). The set is bounded, because the square of the distance of the point \((t, \vek s)\) from the origin is the sum of the squares of the distances of the points \(t\) and \(\vek s\) from the origin, which are bounded. 8.3.3 The implicit function theorem (izrek o implicitni funkciji)

We test the idea on the simplest equations, on a small ball on a rod, which we draw together with the circle along which it moves.

A small ball on a light rigid rod of length \(l = 1\) m rotates about a pin at the origin. The coordinates of the ball are \((x, y)\), and the equation \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\) holds: the ball is on a circle. One equation in two unknowns should leave one free number. At the point \(T = (0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\) m, \(\partial f/\partial y = 2y = 1{,}6\) m \(\neq 0\). Near it, in the disc \(W\), the piece of the circle is the graph of the function \(y = g(x) = \sqrt{l^2 - x^2}\) over the interval \(Y\): to every \(x\) of \(Y\) belongs exactly one point of the piece. The derivative is \(g'(x) = -x/y = -0{,}75\) (checked: the difference quotient \((g(0{,}6 + h) - g(0{,}6 - h))/2h\) at \(h = 10^{-6}\) is \(-0{,}7500\)), and this is the slope of the tangent at the point \(T\).

Why is it \(\partial f/\partial y\) that decides? Near the point \(T\) the circle is almost the same as its tangent, and the tangent is the line \(f_{\mathrm{x}}\,\delta x + f_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta y = 0\) with \(f_{\mathrm{x}} = 2x = 1{,}2\) m and \(f_{\mathrm{y}} = 1{,}6\) m. The line determines \(\delta y\) from \(\delta x\) exactly when \(f_{\mathrm{y}} \neq 0\), that is, when it is not vertical. This is the linear case of subsection 8.3.1 with the matrix \([f_{\mathrm{x}}\ \ f_{\mathrm{y}}]\) and the pivot \(f_{\mathrm{y}}\). Replacing the circle by its tangent is an assumption that has a price. Over a step \(h\) in the direction \(x\) the tangent drops by \(\tfrac34 h\), the circle a little more, and the difference \(e\) is already \(0{,}139\) m at \(h = 0{,}3\) m. At \(h = 0{,}1\) m, \(e = 0{,}0109\) m, and at \(h = 0{,}01\) m only \(0{,}0001\) m: a step ten times shorter gives an error about a hundred times smaller, because the error is proportional to the square of the step. The tangent therefore tells the right direction, but it describes the piece of the circle only in a small neighbourhood of the point \(T\). This is why the theorem we are proving holds only in a neighbourhood.

At the point \((l, 0)\), \(\partial f/\partial y = 0\): there the tangent of the circle is vertical. In the disc about this point the vertical line at \(x = 0{,}95\) m meets the circle twice, at \(y = \pm 0{,}312\) m, and for \(x > l\) not at all. So \(y\) is not a function of the number \(x\) there, however small a disc we take. The roles of the unknowns swap: the horizontal line at \(y = 0{,}15\) m meets the circle in the disc only once, at \(x = 0{,}989\) m, and \(x = \sqrt{l^2 - y^2}\) works, because there \(\partial f/\partial x = 2l \neq 0\). The criterion that separates free numbers from dependent ones is therefore the invertibility of the derivative with respect to the dependent unknown. This is exactly the condition on pivots of subsection 8.3.1, applied to the matrix of partial derivatives of the map \(f\).

For the six coordinates of the mechanism and five equations we prove this for all cases at once. So as not to solve the equations every time, we use a trick: we complete the number of equations to the number of unknowns and use the inverse function theorem. Theorem 4.51 assumes that the map is one-to-one on the whole set and that its Jacobian is different from zero at every point. We need it for \(n\) coordinates and in a form that concludes from the condition on pivots at a single point that the map is invertible near that point.

Theorem 8.35 Implicit function theorem (izrek o implicitni funkciji)

  2. For every \(\vek y\) of \(Y\) the point \((\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) is in \(W\), the matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek z\) is invertible at it, and the matrix of partial derivatives of the map \(\vek g\) is

The map \(\Phi\) is continuously differentiable on \(U\) and has points with \(N\) coordinates as images.
  2. By (1.39) the matrix of partial derivatives at a point \(\vek x\) of \(U\) assigns to a column \(\vek w = (\vek h, \vek k)\) the column \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\,\vek w = \big(\vek h,\ \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek y}\vek h + \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek z}\vek k\big)\). Let \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x_0)\,\vek w = \vek 0\). Then \(\vek h = \vek 0\) and \(\frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek z}(\vek x_0)\,\vek k = \vek 0\), so by lemma 8.22 \(\vek k = \vek 0\). By the same lemma \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x_0)\) has \(N\) pivots. The first \(N - m\) coordinates stay, because vertical lines stay vertical.
  3. By theorem 8.34 there is a ball \(W \subseteq U\) with centre \(\vek x_0\) on which \(\Phi\) is one-to-one and has an invertible matrix of partial derivatives at every point, the image \(V = \Phi(W)\) is open, the inverse map \(\Psi\) is continuously differentiable on \(V\), and \(\mat F_\Psi(\Phi(\vek x)) = \mat F_\Phi(\vek x)^{-1}\) for \(\vek x\) of \(W\).
  4. The first \(N - m\) components of the map \(\Phi\) are \(\vek y\), so the point \(\Psi(\vek y, \vek s)\) has its first \(N - m\) coordinates equal to \(\vek y\) (vertical lines stay vertical). We write \(\Psi(\vek y, \vek s) = (\vek y, \vek h(\vek y, \vek s))\), where the components of the map \(\vek h\) are the last \(m\) components of the map \(\Psi\), continuously differentiable on \(V\).
  5. Let \(Y\) be the set of those \(\vek y\) for which \((\vek y, \vek 0)\) is in \(V\), and \(\vek g(\vek y) = \vek h(\vek y, \vek 0)\) for \(\vek y\) of \(Y\). The point \(\Phi(\vek x_0) = (\vek y_0, \vek 0)\) is in \(V\), so \(\vek y_0 \in Y\), and \(\Psi(\vek y_0, \vek 0) = \vek x_0\) gives \(\vek g(\vek y_0) = \vek z_0\). The set \(Y\) is open: if the ball with radius \(\rho\) around \((\vek y, \vek 0)\) lies in \(V\), then for \(|\vek y' - \vek y| < \rho\) the point \((\vek y', \vek 0)\) is also in it, because \(|(\vek y', \vek 0) - (\vek y, \vek 0)| = |\vek y' - \vek y|\). The partial derivative of the component \(g_{\mathrm{s}}\) with respect to \(y_{\mathrm{i}}\) is by definition the partial derivative of the component \(h_{\mathrm{s}}\) with respect to \(y_{\mathrm{i}}\) at the point \((\vek y, \vek 0)\), because a step \(t\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) in \(\vek y\) does not change \(\vek s = \vek 0\). It is continuous at \(\vek y\) by lemma 8.26, so \(\vek g\) is continuously differentiable on \(Y\).
  6. Let \(\vek y \in Y\). The point \(\Psi(\vek y, \vek 0) = (\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) is in \(W\), and \(\Phi\) maps it to \((\vek y, \vek 0)\), so \(\vek f(\vek y, \vek g(\vek y)) = \vek 0\).
  7. Conversely, let \((\vek y, \vek z)\) of \(W\) have \(\vek f(\vek y, \vek z) = \vek 0\). Then \(\Phi(\vek y, \vek z) = (\vek y, \vek 0)\) is in \(V\), so \(\vek y \in Y\), and \((\vek y, \vek z) = \Psi(\vek y, \vek 0) = (\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\), hence \(\vek z = \vek g(\vek y)\). Together with step 6 this is item 1.
  8. Let \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) with \(\vek y \in Y\), and write \(\mat A_{\mathrm{y}} = \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek y}(\vek x)\) and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek z}(\vek x)\). By step 3 the matrix \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\) has an inverse matrix, so \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\,\vek w = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek w = \mat F_\Phi(\vek x)^{-1}\vek 0 = \vek 0\). If \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\vek k = \vek 0\), then by step 2 \(\mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\,(\vek 0, \vek k) = \vek 0\), hence \(\vek k = \vek 0\), and by lemma 8.22 \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) is invertible. The figure 8.36 shows the map \(\Phi\) around the point, which is invertible.

By step 2 and lemmas 1.98 and 1.100, item 1, for every \(\vek w = (\vek h, \vek k)\)
    \[\mat C\,\mat F_\Phi(\vek x)\,\vek w = \big(\vek h,\ -\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\vek h + \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}(\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\vek h + \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\vek k)\big) = (\vek h, \vek k) = \vek w .\]

8.3.5 Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji)

Now the second side of the question. Let the function \(V\) give the energy of the system. The energy of a linear spring is \(\tfrac12 k e^2\) at an extension \(e\) (section 3.6), and the energy of a load \(P\) that we lower by \(\Delta y\) decreases by \(P\,\Delta y\) (section 4.1.1). In section 8.5 we will see that an equilibrium of the system corresponds to a point where the energy on the set of allowed positions does not change to first order. With one free number \(\theta\) we find it from the derivative with respect to \(\theta\) set equal to zero, as in chapter 5. For \(N\) coordinates and \(m\) equations we would first have to solve the equations for the dependent unknowns and substitute them into \(V\), which is often impossible or laborious. We need a condition that we write directly with the derivatives of the functions \(V\) and \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The figure 8.39 shows the wire, the circles of constant energy and the gradient of the energy.

The gradient \(\nabla V\) of the function \(V\) is the column of its \(N\) partial derivatives (definition 8.23, item 3), \((\nabla V)_{\mathrm{i}} = \partial V/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and likewise \(\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the gradient of the function \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The matrix of partial derivatives \(\mat A\) of the map \(\vek f\) therefore has the rows \((\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}})^\T\). The idea is geometric (figure 8.39). On the left the set \(f = 0\) is a circle with centre \(O\), on the right a straight wire. If the function \(V\) has its smallest value on the set \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at the point \(\vek x_0\), \(V\) does not change to first order for any displacement along the set (a displacement \(\delta\vek x\) with \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\), a tangential displacement). This means \(\nabla V\cdot\delta\vek x = 0\) for every such displacement. Displacements that are perpendicular to all rows of the matrix \(\mat A\) are also perpendicular to \(\nabla V\), so \(\nabla V\) is a linear combination of the rows. This is the theorem we now prove, first its linear part.

Lemma 8.36 Lemma on linear multipliers (lema o linearnih množiteljih)

Let the matrix \(\mat A\) with \(m\) rows and \(N > m\) columns have \(m\) pivots, and let \(\vek a\) be a column with \(N\) entries for which \(\vek a\cdot\delta\vek x = 0\) for every column \(\delta\vek x\) with \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \vek 0\). Then there is exactly one column \(\vek\lambda\) with \(m\) entries such that \(\vek a = \mat A^\T\vek\lambda\). The figure 8.39 shows the gradient of the energy at an extremum, perpendicular to the tangent of the wire and hence parallel to the gradient of the constraint.

Proof

We must show that \(\vek a\) is a linear combination of the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\) and that the combination is unique. We split the unknowns into free and dependent ones and turn the free part of the coefficients into an equality for all free displacements. In figure 8.39, left, the matrix \(\mat A\) has a single row, the gradient \(\nabla f\). The columns perpendicular to all tangent displacements are there exactly the multiples of this row, and the lemma says the same for \(m\) rows.

  1. We number the columns of the matrix \(\mat A\) so that the pivot columns come last, \(\mat A = [\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\ \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}]\), and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) has \(m\) pivots (subsection 8.3.1). Correspondingly we write \(\vek a = (\vek a_{\mathrm{y}}, \vek a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\delta\vek x = (\delta\vek y, \delta\vek z)\). The renumbering permutes the entries of the columns \(\vek a\), \(\delta\vek x\) and \(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda\) in the same way, so it changes neither the hypothesis nor the statement. By lemma 8.22, \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\) is invertible. The figure 8.33 shows the dependent and the free unknowns.
  2. For every column \(\delta\vek y\), \(\delta\vek z = -\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\vek y\) is a solution of the equation \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\delta\vek y + \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\delta\vek z = \vek 0\). Let \(\vek u = \vek a_{\mathrm{y}} - \mat A_{\mathrm{y}}^\T(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1})^\T\vek a_{\mathrm{z}}\). By hypothesis \(0 = \vek a\cdot\delta\vek x = \vek a_{\mathrm{y}}\cdot\delta\vek y + \vek a_{\mathrm{z}}\cdot\delta\vek z = \vek u\cdot\delta\vek y\) (we move the matrix \(-\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\) to the other side of the dot product by transposition, lemma 1.102). For chosen free unknowns the dependent ones are determined.
  3. The equality \(\vek u\cdot\delta\vek y = 0\) holds for every \(\delta\vek y\), hence also for \(\delta\vek y = \vek u\). Then \(|\vek u|^2 = 0\) and \(\vek u = \vek 0\), which means \(\vek a_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{y}}^\T\vek\lambda\) with \(\vek\lambda = (\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1})^\T\vek a_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  4. From the definition of \(\vek\lambda\) and lemma 8.22, \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^\T\vek\lambda = \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^\T(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1})^\T\vek a_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek a_{\mathrm{z}}\). Together with step 3, \(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda = (\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}^\T\vek\lambda,\ \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^\T\vek\lambda) = \vek a\).
  5. Uniqueness. If \(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda = \vek 0\), then \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^\T\vek\lambda = \vek 0\), so by lemma 8.22 \(\vek\lambda = \vek 0\). Two columns \(\vek\lambda\) that give the same \(\vek a\) have a difference that gives \(\vek 0\), so they are equal. The lower matrix in the figure 8.33 has a nonzero solution, which is impossible with full pivots.

Theorem 8.37 Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji)

Let \(U\) be an open set of points with \(N\) coordinates (definition 8.23), \(V\) a function that is continuously differentiable on \(U\), and \(\vek f = (f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}})\) a map that is continuously differentiable on \(U\), with \(m < N\). Let \(\vek f(\vek x_0) = \vek 0\) at a point \(\vek x_0\) of \(U\), and let the matrix of partial derivatives \(\mat A = \mat F_{\vek{f}}(\vek x_0)\) have \(m\) pivots (definition 8.21). Let \(V\) have a local extremum on the set \(\vek f = \vek 0\) at the point \(\vek x_0\): there is a ball \(B\) with centre \(\vek x_0\) such that \(V(\vek x) \ge V(\vek x_0)\) for all points \(\vek x\) of \(B \cap U\) with \(\vek f(\vek x) = \vek 0\), or such that \(V(\vek x) \le V(\vek x_0)\) for all such points. Then there is exactly one \(m\)-tuple of numbers \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) for which

\[\nabla V(\vek x_0) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{m}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek x_0) . \tag{8.23}\]

The numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji). The figure 8.39 shows the wire on which the extremum lies, touching a level of the energy.

Proof

We must show that \(\nabla V(\vek x_0)\) is perpendicular to all tangential displacements, and use lemma 8.36. We obtain the perpendicularity from the fact that along the set of solutions \(V\) is a function of the free numbers that has an extremum.

  1. We number the coordinates so that the pivot columns of the matrix \(\mat A\) come last, \(\vek x = (\vek y, \vek z)\) and \(\mat A = [\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\ \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}]\). The renumbering permutes the entries of all gradients in the same way, so it does not change equation (8.23). The matrix \(\mat A_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{\partial\vek f}{\partial\vek z}(\vek x_0)\) has \(m\) pivots (subsection 8.3.1). By theorem 8.35 there are open sets \(Y\) and \(W\) and a map \(\vek g\), continuously differentiable on \(Y\), with the properties of the theorem. In figure 8.39, left, this is the piece of the circle about the point \(T\), which is the graph of a function of one coordinate.
  2. Let \(\rho\) be the radius of the ball \(B\). The components of the map \(\vek g\) are continuous at \(\vek y_0\) (theorem 8.28, item 2), and \(Y\) is open, so there is \(\delta\) with \(0 < \delta \le \rho/2\) such that for \(|\vek y - \vek y_0| < \delta\) the point \(\vek y\) is in \(Y\) and \(|\vek g(\vek y) - \vek z_0| < \rho/2\). For such \(\vek y\) the square of the distance of the point \((\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) from \(\vek x_0\) is the sum of the squares of the numbers \(|\vek y - \vek y_0|\) and \(|\vek g(\vek y) - \vek z_0|\), so the distance is less than \(\rho\). The point lies in \(W \subseteq U\) and satisfies \(\vek f = \vek 0\) (theorem 8.35, items 1 and 2), so the bound for \(V\) from the hypothesis holds for it. The figure 8.35 shows the band around the point and the graph of \(\vek g\) in it.
  3. Let \(\varphi(\vek y) = V(\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) for \(\vek y \in Y\). The map \(\vek y \mapsto (\vek y, \vek g(\vek y))\) is continuously differentiable on \(Y\) and has its images in \(W\), so by lemma 8.30 the function \(\varphi\) is continuously differentiable and
    \[\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek y_0) = \frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek x_0) + \sum_{\mathrm{s}} \frac{\partial V}{\partial z_{\mathrm{s}}}(\vek x_0)\,\frac{\partial g_{\mathrm{s}}}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek y_0) .\]
  4. For every \(i\) the function \(h \mapsto \varphi(\vek y_0 + h\,\vek e_{\mathrm{i}})\) is defined on the interval \((-\delta, \delta)\) and by step 2 has its largest or smallest value on it at \(h = 0\). By lemma 1.83 its derivative at \(0\), which by definition is \(\partial\varphi/\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}(\vek y_0)\), is therefore zero. In figure 8.39, right, \(\varphi\) is the energy along the wire: it decreases from the point \(S\) towards the point \(T\), at the point \(T\) it is smallest and does not change to first order there.
  5. Let \(\delta\vek x = (\delta\vek y, \delta\vek z)\) be a tangential displacement, \(\mat A\,\delta\vek x = \mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\delta\vek y + \mat A_{\mathrm{z}}\delta\vek z = \vek 0\). Then \(\delta\vek z = -\mat A_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat A_{\mathrm{y}}\,\delta\vek y\), which by (8.21) at the point \(\vek y_0\) equals \(\frac{\partial\vek g}{\partial\vek y}(\vek y_0)\,\delta\vek y\). By steps 3 and 4, therefore
    \[\nabla V(\vek x_0)\cdot\delta\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}\delta y_{\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial z_{\mathrm{s}}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \frac{\partial g_{\mathrm{s}}}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}\delta y_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{\mathrm{i}}}(\vek y_0)\,\delta y_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 .\]
  6. By lemma 8.36 with \(\vek a = \nabla V(\vek x_0)\) there is exactly one \(\vek\lambda\) with \(\nabla V(\vek x_0) = \mat A^\T\vek\lambda\). The columns of the matrix \(\mat A^\T\) are \(\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek x_0)\), so \(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek x_0)\), which is (8.23). In figure 8.39, right, the gradients \(\nabla V\) and \(\nabla f\) are parallel at the point \(T\), and \(\nabla V = \lambda\nabla f\) with a single number \(\lambda\).

Source: The theorem is not from a source: none of the project's mathematics books (LEBL1, LEBL2, KREYSZIG) states it. The mechanical form is in GR §1.6, equation (1.79) (PDF p. 63): "It is called a Lagrange multiplier", and in EP §5.4 (5.4.11) (p. 257).

The theorem only says where to look: at a point of extremum the gradient of the energy is a linear combination of the gradients of the equations. The converse does not hold, just as for a function of one variable a zero derivative does not mean that the point is an extremum. Equations (8.23) together with the \(m\) equations \(\vek f = \vek 0\) give \(N + m\) equations for \(N\) coordinates and the \(m\) numbers \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). Without solving the equations for the dependent unknowns we obtained a system in which all coordinates are equally treated. The figure 8.39 shows the contact of the wire and the energy level, where the gradients are parallel.

8.3.6 Example: a ring on a wire (zgled: obroč na žici)

First the simplest calculation, where we know the answer. A ring moves freely along the straight wire \(0{,}6\,x + 0{,}8\,y - 1 = 0\) (in metres, the wire is 1 m from the origin), and a spring with zero natural length and stiffness \(k = 100\) N/m pulls it towards the origin, so the energy of the spring is \(V = \tfrac12 k (x^2 + y^2)\). The ring comes to rest where the energy on the wire is smallest, that is, at the point of the wire closest to the origin: \(T = (0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\) m, at distance 1 m, with \(V = 50\) J. We know this from geometry. Theorem 8.37 tells where to look for this point without geometry. Equation (8.23): \(\nabla V = (kx, ky) = \lambda\,(0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\), with the wire \(0{,}6x + 0{,}8y = 1\). Three equations, unknowns \(x\), \(y\), \(\lambda\). From the first two \(x = 0{,}6\lambda/k\) and \(y = 0{,}8\lambda/k\), substituted into the third: \(\lambda/k\,(0{,}36 + 0{,}64) = 1\), hence \(\lambda = k = 100\) N and \((x, y) = (0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\) m. The equations have a single solution, and it is the point that geometry gave. At other points of the wire, for example at the point \(S\), the gradients \(\nabla V\) and \(\nabla f\) are not parallel, so equation (8.23) does not hold there, and the energy along the wire decreases towards the point \(T\). The condition on pivots holds, because \(\nabla f = (0{,}6,\ 0{,}8) \neq \vek 0\) and the matrix with one row has a pivot. The multiplier has a physical meaning. The spring pulls the ring with the force \(-\nabla V = (-60,\ -80)\) N, 100 N long towards the origin, and the wire must hold it with a force perpendicular to the wire (the wire is smooth, that is, without friction. With friction the wire could also give a force along itself, the ring would also stay at rest somewhat away from \(T\), and \(\lambda\nabla f\) would not be the whole force of the wire), with components \((60,\ 80) = \lambda\nabla f\) N. The force of the wire is therefore \(\lambda\nabla f\), and the multiplier \(\lambda = 100\) N is the magnitude of the force on the ring, because \(\nabla f\) is a unit vector. Why exactly \(\lambda\nabla f\) is the force of the connection we will prove in section 8.5, here it is a calculation that we check on the mechanism.

8.3.7 Example: the mechanism with a guide (zgled: mehanizem z vodilom)

We load the mechanism from the start of the section with a load \(P = 100\) N at hinge B and a spring with \(k = 10\) kN/m that is free of tension in the position \(\theta_0 = 30°\), so it has the natural length \(L_0 = 2a\cos\theta_0 = 0{,}8660\) m. We neglect the weights of the bars, so that the calculation stays with two forces. This is a choice of model and not a small error: bars with a mass of 1 kg each would weigh \(9{,}81\) N each, and by the moments about A the force of the guide would grow from \(50\) N to \(P/2 + 9{,}81 = 59{,}81\) N. The procedure stays the same, only the weight at the centres \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) joins the energy. The energy is the sum of the energy of the spring and the energy of the load,

\[\begin{aligned} V &= \tfrac12 k\,(x_{\mathrm{C}} - L_0)^2 + P\,y_{\mathrm{B}} , \\ x_{\mathrm{C}} &= x_2 + \tfrac{a}{2}\cos\theta_2 , \quad y_{\mathrm{B}} = y_1 + \tfrac{a}{2}\sin\theta_1 , \end{aligned} \tag{8.24}\]

as a function of all six coordinates. By theorem 8.37 every local extremum of the energy on the set (8.14) at which the matrix of partial derivatives has five pivots satisfies equation (8.23). Without eliminating angles: equation (8.23) in six components and the five equations (8.14) give eleven equations for eleven unknowns \(\vek x\) and \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_5\). We solve them numerically (SymPy nsolve, starting from the position at \(\theta \approx 0{,}5\)). The result is \(\theta_1 = 0{,}50523\) rad \(= 28{,}95°\), \(\theta_2 = -\theta_1\), \(x_1 = 0{,}2188\), \(y_1 = 0{,}1210\), \(x_2 = 0{,}6563\), \(y_2 = 0{,}1210\) m and the numbers

\[\lambda_1 = 90{,}40\ \text{N} , \quad \lambda_2 = 50{,}00\ \text{N} , \quad \lambda_3 = -90{,}40\ \text{N} , \quad \lambda_4 = 50{,}00\ \text{N} , \quad \lambda_5 = 50{,}00\ \text{N} .\]

The angle \(28{,}95°\) agrees with the one we obtain with a single angle (the derivative of the function \(V(\theta) = \tfrac12 k(2a\cos\theta - L_0)^2 + Pa\sin\theta\) is zero at \(28{,}95°\), also computed with SymPy). In this position the elimination of the first five columns of the matrix of partial derivatives, by the same steps as at \(30°\), gives the pivots \(1\), \(1\), \(-1\), \(-1\) and \(a\cos\theta_1 = 0{,}4375\), so the condition on pivots of theorem 8.37 holds.

The multipliers are forces of the connections. Let us draw them (figure 8.40). At the top is the whole mechanism at the angle \(\theta = 28{,}95°\). We have removed the pin, the guide and the spring and drawn in their place the forces with which they act on the mechanism. The arrows show the true directions and are \(1\) cm long for \(50\) N. The fifth multiplier belongs to the guide: \(\lambda_5 = 50\) N upwards on slider C. We check this with the equilibrium of the mechanism (definition 2.3, moments about A). The force of the pin with components \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) acts at A, and the force of the spring \(F_{\mathrm{k}}\) at C lies on the horizontal line through A, so neither has a moment about A. The load \(P\) at B has the lever arm \(a\cos\theta\), the force of the guide at C the lever arm \(2a\cos\theta\), twice as long, so \(P\,a\cos\theta = N_{\mathrm{C}}\,2a\cos\theta\) and \(N_{\mathrm{C}} = P/2 = 50\) N. The first two multipliers belong to pin A: the force \((90{,}40,\ 50{,}00)\) N on bar AB. At the bottom the bars are drawn apart, bar AB on the left and bar BC with the slider on the right, each with the forces that act on it. Only the forces at its ends B and C act on bar BC, so by lemma 4.5 they point along the bar. The vertical component of the force of the bar on the slider must balance the force of the guide \(50\) N, so the compressive force in the bar is \(N = P/(2\sin\theta) = 103{,}3\) N, and its horizontal component \(N\cos\theta = 90{,}4\) N equals the force of the spring \(k(x_{\mathrm{C}} - L_0) = 90{,}4\) N, because the slider is in equilibrium in the horizontal direction too. The third and fourth multipliers, \((-90{,}40,\ 50{,}00)\) N, are the components of the force of bar BC on bar AB at hinge B. Since \(\lambda_3\) is negative, the horizontal component points to the left and is labelled in the figure with its magnitude \(-\lambda_3\), and the vertical one, \(\lambda_4\), points upwards. The magnitude of the force is \(\sqrt{90{,}40^2 + 50{,}00^2} = 103{,}3\) N, and the angle with the horizontal \(\arctan(50/90{,}4) = 28{,}95°\) is exactly the angle of the bar. On bar BC the equal and opposite force acts at B, and its resultant is the force \(N\) along the bar. The multipliers also balance bar AB by itself: horizontally \(\lambda_1\) \(+\) \(\lambda_3\) \(= 90{,}40 - 90{,}40 = 0\), and vertically \(\lambda_2\) \(+\) \(\lambda_4\) \(-\) \(P\) \(= 50 + 50 - 100 = 0\). All the calculations (from the multipliers, from the moments about A, from the equilibrium of bar BC and from the equilibrium of bar AB) agree. The five multipliers are the five components of the forces of the connections, two at the pin, two at the hinge and one at the guide. In the calculation with a single angle they did not appear, because that calculation has the connections already built in.

In section 8.5 we will show that this is general: where we keep a connection as an equation, the multiplier is the force of the connection. In the figure 8.40 this is the force of the guide.

Summary of the section. A square matrix is invertible if and only if it has all its pivots, which we showed with elimination and back substitution (lemma 8.22). We carried the theorems of analysis of the earlier chapters over to \(n\) coordinates by finding in each proof the places where the number of coordinates appears and writing the changed steps again. Five equations in six unknowns leave one free number near a solution when the matrix of partial derivatives has five pivots. This is theorem 8.35, which we proved with the inverse function theorem for \(n\) coordinates, in which the condition on pivots replaces the determinant. For the mechanism the condition holds at \(30°\) for every choice of the free number, but in the straight position it fails for the coordinates \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) and for the distance AC. An extremum of the energy on this set has a gradient that is a linear combination of the gradients of the equations (theorem 8.37, proved with the implicit function and lemma 8.36), and for the mechanism the multipliers equal the components of the forces of the connections, which we checked with equilibrium. The figures 8.33, 8.36 and 8.39 show the pivots, the flattened curve and the contact of the wire with the level.
