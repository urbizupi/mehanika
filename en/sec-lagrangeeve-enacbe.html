Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.9 Lagrange's equations of the second kind (Lagrangeeve enačbe druge vrste)

In section 8.7 we turned motion into equilibrium. For every virtual displacement that the constraints allow, the work of the applied forces equals the work of the inertia forces, that is, the work done by \(-m\vek a\) on every small piece of the system. For the pendulum we therefore had to write the acceleration of the mass in components, and only in the projection on the tangent did the rod force drop out. For a rigid body that rotates and translates we would have to write the acceleration of each of its points, and for mechanism D the accelerations of the points of both bars and of the slider. In comparison, the kinetic energy \(T\) (definition 8.79) is a single number, which section 8.8 writes with generalized coordinates and their time derivatives. The question of this section is: can we obtain the equations of motion from \(T\) and from the generalized forces, in the coordinates we have chosen, without the accelerations of individual points and without the forces in the constraints?

The answer is yes, and the way to it goes through one observation about differentiation. Throughout the section the model of section 8.1 stays in force. The system consists of finitely many material points with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and rigid bodies, connected by ideal constraints (definition 8.2). We describe the position by generalized coordinates \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}})\) (definition 8.45), and the position of point \(k\) is \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek X_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) with a twice continuously differentiable map \(\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}\). For a rigid body, \(k\) labels a point of the body and the sum over points becomes an integral over the body with density \(\rho\), as in section 8.8. A motion is a twice differentiable function \(t \mapsto \vek q(t)\). Point \(k\) then has velocity \(\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\) and acceleration \(\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\).

We write the velocity of a point with the coordinates by the chain rule for several variables (lemma 8.29):

\[\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{n} \frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial t} . \tag{8.79}\]

On the right stands a function of \(2n + 1\) variables \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(t\), which we write \(\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t)\). The coefficients of \(\dot q_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the last term are functions of \(\vek q\) and \(t\), not of \(\dot{\vek q}\). So in the function \(\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}\) the velocity \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) appears only linearly, and we can differentiate the expression with respect to \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) as with respect to an independent variable. The kinetic energy is \(T(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t) = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} \tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{k}}\,|\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t)|^2\) (theorem 8.86).

In the inertia part we will have the product of the acceleration and \(\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). We want to write it as a derivative of the kinetic energy, and for that we must move the dots in the derivatives of the position from one place to another. The next lemma gives the two equalities that allow this.

Lemma 8.87 Moving the dot in derivatives of position (zamenjava pike v odvodih lege)

Let \(\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) be a twice continuously differentiable map and \(\vek q(t)\) a twice differentiable motion. For every \(s = 1, \dots, n\),

\[\frac{\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} , \tag{8.80}\]
\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big) = \frac{\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} , \tag{8.81}\]

where in the second equation the functions are evaluated at \(\vek q(t)\), \(\dot{\vek q}(t)\) and \(t\).

Proof

We must show that the dot disappears when we differentiate with respect to the velocity and that it exchanges places with the time derivative.

  1. The right side of equation (8.79) is linear in \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and the coefficient of \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), which does not depend on the velocities. The derivative with respect to \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) at constant \(\vek q\), \(t\) and the other velocities is therefore exactly this coefficient. This is (8.80).
  2. The function \(\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuously differentiable. Its time derivative along the motion is, by the chain rule (lemma 8.29),
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \frac{\partial^2\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\partial^2\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial t\,\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} .\]
  3. The derivative of equation (8.79) with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) at constant \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(t\) is
    \[\frac{\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \frac{\partial^2\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial q_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\partial^2\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\partial t} .\]
  4. The second derivatives are continuous, so the mixed derivatives are equal (theorem 8.30). The right sides in steps 2 and 3 agree, and this is (8.81).

Source: SHABANA (3.87) and (3.88) (PDF p. 129), where the equalities are written with the words "interchanging the differentiation with respect to t and q j" and with the derivative of \(\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{i}}\) with respect to \(\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\), GR (1.24) (PDF p. 42) for the expression of the velocity. The steps are the same as in SHABANA, their notation is ours.

We test the lemma on pendulum A, a mass \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid light rod of length \(l = 1\) m that turns about a fixed point O. The angle \(\theta\) is measured from the downward vertical, the axis \(y\) points up, and the coordinate is \(q_1 = \theta\). The position of the mass is \(x = l\sin\theta\), \(y = -l\cos\theta\). The time derivative of \(x\) is \(\dot x = l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\). At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) we have \(\partial x/\partial\theta = l\cos\theta = 0{,}8660\) m and also \(\partial\dot x/\partial\dot\theta = l\cos\theta = 0{,}8660\) m, which is (8.80). The time derivative of the function \(\partial x/\partial\theta = l\cos\theta\) is \(-l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\), and exactly the same is \(\partial\dot x/\partial\theta = -l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\), which is (8.81). We took the derivatives of the sine and cosine from theorem 8.12.

Now we can prove the main theorem. The idea is that the inertia work \(\sum m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) can, by the lemma, be written as a derivative of a single function \(T\). For the virtual displacements we take \(\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with arbitrary independent numbers \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 8.47). The generalized forces \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are those of definition 8.50, that is, the forces that do not come from the constraints.

Theorem 8.88 Lagrange's equations of the second kind (Lagrangeeve enačbe druge vrste)

Let a system of finitely many points and rigid bodies with ideal holonomic constraints be described by generalized coordinates \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) and maps \(\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) that are twice continuously differentiable. Let \(T(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t)\) be its kinetic energy and \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}\) the generalized force of the applied forces. A twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) satisfies d'Alembert's principle (corollary 8.72) at every time and for every virtual displacement that the constraints allow if and only if, for \(s = 1, \dots, n\),

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}} . \tag{8.82}\]
Proof

We must show that the inertia work equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} I_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\), and that d'Alembert's principle for arbitrary \(\delta \vek q\) is exactly \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} (Q_{\mathrm{s}} - I_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\).

  1. D'Alembert's principle says that at every time \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} (\vek F_{\mathrm{k}} - m_{\mathrm{k}}\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}})\cdot\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) for every compatible virtual displacement, where \(\vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the applied forces. The forces in ideal constraints do not appear in the sum (definition 8.2).
  2. The virtual displacements are \(\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with arbitrary numbers \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 8.47). By definition 8.50 the work of the applied forces is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} Q_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The inertia work is \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} I_{\mathrm{s}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with
    \[I_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} . \tag{8.83}\]
  3. By the product rule, \(\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big) - \dot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big)\).
  4. In the first term we replace \(\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) by \(\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (equation (8.80)), and in the second the time derivative by \(\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (equation (8.81)). Since \(\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\partial\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial\big(\tfrac12|\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\big)/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and the same with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\), we obtain
    \[m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\ddot{\vek x}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{k}}|\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\Big) - \frac{\partial}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big(\tfrac12 m_{\mathrm{k}}|\dot{\vek X}_{\mathrm{k}}|^2\Big) .\]
  5. The sum over \(k\) is \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\). For a rigid body \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the integral \(\int\rho\,\mathrm{d}V\), and the derivatives with respect to \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(t\) go under the integral (derivative of an integral with respect to a parameter, lemma 8.31).
  6. By steps 1, 2 and 5, d'Alembert's principle reads \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} (Q_{\mathrm{s}} - I_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\). If (8.82) holds, every \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} - I_{\mathrm{s}}\) is zero and the sum is zero for every \(\delta \vek q\). If the sum vanishes for every \(\delta \vek q\), we take \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) and all others zero. Every \(\delta \vek q\) is allowed here, because by lemma 8.47 the coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) are independent. So we obtain \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} - I_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for every \(s\).

Source: GR (1.17), (1.29) (PDF pp. 39, 43), SHABANA (3.94), (3.95) (PDF p. 129), CK (8.9) (PDF p. 241). GR and CK derive (1.29) and (8.9) from Hamilton's principle, SHABANA from d'Alembert's, as here. The notation in steps 3 to 6 follows SHABANA PDF 129, the equivalence in both directions is ours.

We have shown that each of the \(n\) equations (8.82) is the projection of d'Alembert's principle on the direction in which one coordinate changes. For the acceleration it suffices to differentiate two functions, \(\partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). We need the forces in the constraints nowhere, because we chose the coordinates so that the constraints are satisfied automatically.

Example 8.89 Pendulum A by Lagrange (nihalo A po Lagrangeu)

Pendulum A: mass \(m = 1\) kg, rod \(l = 1\) m, angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\).

  1. The velocity of the mass is \((l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta,\ l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta)\) and \(T = \tfrac12 m\,l^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 = \tfrac12 m\,l^2\dot\theta^2\) (by lemma 8.10, item 1, \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)).
  2. The weight \((0, -mg)\) has the generalized force \(Q_\theta = (0, -mg)\cdot(l\cos\theta, l\sin\theta) = -m\,g\,l\sin\theta\). The force in the rod is a force in an ideal constraint and we do not need it.
  3. Derivatives: \(\partial T/\partial\dot\theta = m\,l^2\dot\theta\), its time derivative is \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta\), and \(\partial T/\partial\theta = 0\). Equation (8.82) is \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta = -m\,g\,l\sin\theta\), so
    \[\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{l}\sin\theta = 0 . \tag{8.84}\]

This is the equation that we obtained in section 8.7 by projecting the acceleration, here without the acceleration in components and without the rod force. We do not know how to write the solution with the functions that the book has, so we compute it numerically (the Runge-Kutta method of fourth order, 4000 steps over two periods). We do not explain the method, and we check the correctness of the computation with the energy. Figure 8.27 shows three motions from rest, with angles \(10^\circ\), \(90^\circ\) and \(170^\circ\). The curve that we have for small angles is \(\theta_0\cos(\omega_0 t)\) with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l} = 3{,}1321\) s\(^{-1}\) and period \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2{,}0061\) s. Why the solutions differ, we see from the equation: the restoring acceleration is \((g/l)\sin\theta\), and the linear equation replaces it with \((g/l)\,\theta\). At \(90^\circ = 1{,}5708\) rad, \(\sin\theta/\theta = 0{,}6366\), so the restoring acceleration in the equation with the sine is \(36{,}3\,\%\) smaller than in the linear one, and the oscillation is therefore slower. The periods are \(2{,}0099\) s at \(10^\circ\) (\(0{,}19\,\%\) longer than \(T_0\)), \(2{,}3678\) s at \(90^\circ\) (\(18{,}0\,\%\) longer) and \(4{,}8935\) s at \(170^\circ\) (2,44 times longer), where \(\sin\theta/\theta = 0{,}0585\) and the restoring acceleration at the start is very small. At \(10^\circ\) the two curves hardly separate (in the figure they overlap), because \(\sin\theta\) at \(0{,}1745\) rad is smaller than \(\theta\) by \(0{,}51\,\%\) (lemma 8.19: \(|\sin\theta - \theta| \le |\theta|^3/6\)).

The energy \(\tfrac12 m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 - m\,g\,l\cos\theta\) must be conserved during the motion (we prove this in section 8.11), so its drift during the computation is a measure of the error. After two periods the largest deviation of the energy from the initial one is \(1\cdot 10^{-14}\) J at \(10^\circ\), \(7\cdot 10^{-12}\) J at \(90^\circ\) and \(5\cdot 10^{-10}\) J at \(170^\circ\), with oscillation energy \(m g l\,(1 - \cos\theta_0)\) equal to \(0{,}149\) J, \(9{,}81\) J and \(19{,}47\) J.

8.9.1 Conservative forces and existence of the motion (konservativne sile in obstoj gibanja)

If the applied forces are conservative (definition 8.53), then \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) for a potential energy \(V(\vek q)\) of the system, a continuously differentiable function of the coordinates. The potential energy does not depend on the velocities, so \(\partial V/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\). If there are in addition some nonconservative forces, for example friction or a force given by a motor, we write their generalized force \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\). Then we can move the term \(-\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) to the left side, and both energies are in one function. We write the Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) as \(\mathcal{L}\), because \(L\) is a length in this book.

Theorem 8.90 Lagrange's equations with a potential (Lagrangeeve enačbe s potencialom)

In the setting of theorem 8.88 let \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) with a continuously differentiable \(V(\vek q)\). With \(\mathcal{L} = T - V\), a twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) is a motion of the system if and only if, for \(s = 1, \dots, n\),

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big) - \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} . \tag{8.85}\]
Proof

We must show that equation (8.82), with the split of \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}\), can be rewritten as (8.85).

  1. Since \(V\) does not depend on \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. Since we differentiate \(T - V\) with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - \partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  3. We insert into (8.82): \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\). Since all steps are equalities, the converse holds too.

Source: GR (1.29) with (1.21) (PDF pp. 41, 43), EP (5.2.17) (p. 246) for the nonconservative part, where \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) are generalized forces. The proof in three steps is ours.

For pendulum A, \(V = -m\,g\,l\cos\theta\) and \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\). Equation (8.85) gives \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta + m\,g\,l\sin\theta = 0\), so the same as before, only that we did not have to compute \(Q_\theta\). If in the pivot of the pendulum there also acts a friction moment proportional to the angular velocity and opposing it, \(Q_\theta^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\,\dot\theta\) with \(c = 0{,}1\) N m s (our model, at \(\dot\theta = 1\) s\(^{-1}\) the moment is \(0{,}1\) N m), the equation reads \(\ddot\theta + \dfrac{c}{m\,l^2}\,\dot\theta + \dfrac{g}{l}\sin\theta = 0\). The numerical solution from rest at \(10^\circ\) reaches the next largest deviation in the same direction after \(2{,}01\) s and it is \(9{,}04^\circ\), so the deviation decreases in one period to \(0{,}904\) of the original. How the energy changes in the process is shown by section 8.11.

We met a rheonomic constraint in section 8.4 in a pendulum whose pivot moves. Let the pivot of pendulum A move vertically with constant acceleration \(a = 5\) m/s\(^2\) upward from rest, so its height is \(a t^2/2\) (GR example 1.6, PDF p. 44). The position of the mass is \(x = l\sin\theta\), \(y = -l\cos\theta + a t^2/2\), and the velocity is \((l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta,\ l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta + a t)\). The kinetic energy has three parts (theorem 8.86):

\[T = \underbrace{\tfrac12 m\,l^2\dot\theta^2}_{\mathrm{T}_2} + \underbrace{m\,a\,t\,l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta}_{\mathrm{T}_1} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 m\,a^2 t^2}_{\mathrm{T}_0} .\]

The part \(T_1\) depends on \(\dot\theta\) linearly and on time, and \(T_0\) does not depend on \(\theta\). The potential energy of the weight is \(m\,g\,y\), its derivative with respect to \(\theta\) is \(m\,g\,l\sin\theta\), because the second term depends only on time. The derivatives are \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot\theta} = m\,l^2\ddot\theta + m\,a\,l\sin\theta + m\,a\,t\,l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\) and \(\partial T/\partial\theta = m\,a\,t\,l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\). The terms \(m\,a\,t\,l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\) cancel in the subtraction, what remains is \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta + m\,a\,l\sin\theta = -m\,g\,l\sin\theta\), so

\[\ddot\theta + \frac{g + a}{l}\sin\theta = 0 .\]

This is the equation of a pendulum in which the weight is increased by the acceleration of the pivot. At \(a = 5\) m/s\(^2\) the angular frequency of small oscillations is \(\sqrt{(g + a)/l} = 3{,}8484\) s\(^{-1}\) instead of \(3{,}1321\) s\(^{-1}\). If the pivot moved with constant velocity \(u\), we would have \(T_1 = m\,u\,l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\), its contribution \(m\,u\,l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\) would cancel with \(\partial T/\partial\theta\), and the pendulum would oscillate as with a pivot at rest (GR p. 45).

The second example is a rigid body on two springs.

Example 8.91 Rigid bar on two springs by Lagrange (toga palica na dveh vzmeteh po Lagrangeu)

Bar C: a uniform bar of length \(L = 2\) m, \(m = 30\) kg, \(J_{\mathrm{G}} = m L^2/12 = 10\) kg m\(^2\), vertical springs \(k_1 = 20\) kN/m at the left and \(k_2 = 30\) kN/m at the right end, weight. A guide prevents horizontal motion (an idealization, our model). Coordinates: \(y\), the vertical displacement of the center of mass G upward, and \(\varphi\), the rotation counterclockwise. We write \(a = L/2 = 1\) m. The springs stay vertical, and their ends move horizontally together with the ends of the bar (our model).

  1. The center of mass moves vertically with velocity \(\dot y\), the angular velocity is \(\dot\varphi\). By theorem 8.82, \(T = \tfrac12 m\,\dot y^2 + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{G}}\,\dot\varphi^2\). The matrix \(\mat M\) from theorem 8.86 is \(\mathrm{diag}(30, 10)\) in kg and kg m\(^2\).
  2. The vertical displacements of the ends are \(y \mp a\sin\varphi\). The potential energy is
    \[V = \tfrac12 k_1\,(y - a\sin\varphi)^2 + \tfrac12 k_2\,(y + a\sin\varphi)^2 + m\,g\,y .\]
  3. Derivatives with respect to \(y\) and \(\varphi\) (SymPy checks every term): \(\partial V/\partial y = k_1(y - a\sin\varphi) + k_2(y + a\sin\varphi) + m g\) and \(\partial V/\partial\varphi = a\cos\varphi\,\big[(k_2 - k_1)\,y + (k_1 + k_2)\,a\sin\varphi\big]\).
  4. Equations (8.85) are the exact nonlinear equations:
    \[\begin{aligned} m\ddot y + (k_1 + k_2)\,y + (k_2 - k_1)\,a\sin\varphi + m g &= 0 , \\ J_{\mathrm{G}}\ddot\varphi + a\cos\varphi\,\big[(k_2 - k_1)\,y + (k_1 + k_2)\,a\sin\varphi\big] &= 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{8.86}\]
  5. For a small \(\varphi\) we take \(\sin\varphi \approx \varphi\) and \(\cos\varphi \approx 1\) (lemma 8.19). What remains is the linear form \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K \vek q = \vek f\) with \(\vek q = (y, \varphi)\), \(\vek f = (-m g, 0)\) and
    \[\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} k_1 + k_2 & (k_2 - k_1)\,a \\ (k_2 - k_1)\,a & (k_1 + k_2)\,a^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 50\,000 & 10\,000 \\ 10\,000 & 50\,000 \end{pmatrix} .\]

We check the linearization with numbers. The equilibrium of the linear system is \(y = -6{,}1313\) mm, \(\varphi = 1{,}2263\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad. The exact system (8.86) gives for \(\ddot{\vek q} = 0\) the same number \(\varphi\), the difference is relatively \(2{,}5\cdot 10^{-7}\), which is below the bound \(\varphi^2/2 = 7{,}5\cdot 10^{-7}\) for \(1 - \cos\varphi\) (lemma 8.19). In motion the difference is larger: if we release the bar from the equilibrium height with a rotation \(\varphi_0\), the solutions of the exact and the linear system differ in \(\varphi\) up to the time \(0{,}2\) s by at most \(0{,}11\,\%\) of \(\varphi_0\) at \(\varphi_0 = 0{,}02\) rad, by \(3{,}1\,\%\) at \(0{,}1\) rad and by \(28\,\%\) at \(0{,}3\) rad. The difference is larger than the neglected terms of the equation (\(\varphi^2/6 = 0{,}17\,\%\) for the sine and \(\varphi^2/2 = 0{,}5\,\%\) for the cosine at \(\varphi = 0{,}1\) rad), because the error accumulates: at \(\varphi_0 = 0{,}1\) rad the largest difference is \(0{,}42\,\%\) up to the time \(0{,}05\) s, \(1{,}1\,\%\) up to \(0{,}1\) s and \(3{,}1\,\%\) up to \(0{,}2\) s. The linear system is therefore usable for rotations below about \(0{,}1\) rad and for times that are not much longer than the period.

The third example is mechanism D, in which the mass changes with position.

Example 8.92 Toggle mechanism D by Lagrange (kolenasti mehanizem D po Lagrangeu)

Mechanism D (section 8.1): bars AB and BC of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, mass \(2\) kg each (uniform), slider C of mass \(1\) kg, horizontal spring \(k = 10\) kN/m, unstretched at \(\theta_0 = 30^\circ\), vertical load \(P = 100\) N at B. The coordinate \(\theta\) is the angle of bar AB above the horizontal.

  1. By section 8.8 the kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 m(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2\) with \(m(\theta) = \tfrac13 + 2\sin^2\theta\) kg m\(^2\). At \(0^\circ\), \(m = 0{,}3333\), and at \(30^\circ\), \(m = 0{,}8333\) kg m\(^2\).
  2. The potential energy from section 8.5 is \(V = \tfrac12 k\,(2a)^2(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)^2 + P\,a\sin\theta\) and \(V'(\theta) = -5000\sin 2\theta + 5000\sqrt3\,\sin\theta + 0{,}5\,P\cos\theta\) in N m (SymPy checks the derivative).
  3. Derivatives: \(\partial T/\partial\dot\theta = m\,\dot\theta\), its time derivative is \(m\,\ddot\theta + m'\,\dot\theta^2\) (product rule and chain rule for \(m(\theta(t))\), lemma 8.29, item 3), \(\partial T/\partial\theta = \tfrac12 m'\,\dot\theta^2\).
  4. Equation (8.85) is
    \[m(\theta)\,\ddot\theta + \tfrac12 m'(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 + V'(\theta) = 0 , \qquad m'(\theta) = 4\sin\theta\cos\theta . \tag{8.87}\]

The term \(\tfrac12 m'\dot\theta^2\) is the one that "mass times acceleration" misses. The inertia term is the time derivative of the quantity \(\partial T/\partial\dot\theta = m(\theta)\,\dot\theta\). This changes because of the acceleration and because the mass changes with the angle, which gives \(m\ddot\theta + m'\dot\theta^2\). But the kinetic energy, at the same \(\dot\theta\), also changes with position, because \(m\) depends on position, and this part, \(\partial T/\partial\theta = \tfrac12 m'\dot\theta^2\), is subtracted from the first term in the equation. Half is subtracted. What remains is \(\tfrac12 m'\dot\theta^2\). Let us test how large this term is. We release the mechanism from rest at \(\theta = 0{,}65\) rad \(= 37{,}2^\circ\), the equilibrium is at \(0{,}50523\) rad. The motion oscillates between \(0{,}2997\) rad and \(0{,}65\) rad with period \(0{,}1258\) s and largest velocity \(8{,}81\) s\(^{-1}\). The largest value of the term \(\tfrac12 m'\dot\theta^2\) is \(63{,}9\) N m (at \(\theta = 0{,}494\) rad, \(m = 0{,}783\) kg m\(^2\), \(m' = 1{,}670\) kg m\(^2\), \(\dot\theta = 8{,}75\) s\(^{-1}\)), and the largest value of \(|V'|\) is \(463{,}1\) N m, so the term is \(14\,\%\) of the other. If we omit it, the energy \(T + V\) changes by \(10{,}3\) J, while \(V\) oscillates between \(24{,}6\) and \(54{,}7\) J, the period is \(0{,}1217\) s instead of \(0{,}1258\) s, and at \(t = 0{,}6\) s we have \(\theta = 0{,}635\) rad instead of \(0{,}494\) rad. With it the energy is conserved to \(6\cdot 10^{-10}\) J. Figure 8.28 shows \(m(\theta)\) and the three terms of equation (8.87) during one period.

If we took \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) instead of \(\theta\) as the coordinate, the mass would be \(m(\theta)/(4a^2\sin^2\theta)\), at \(\theta = 0{,}1\) rad \(35{,}4\) kg and unbounded as \(\theta\) tends to zero. This is the same reason as in section 8.4: at the dead position \(x_{\mathrm{C}}\) is not a coordinate, and the matrix \(\mat M\) is not positive definite.

In all three examples we could solve the equations for the accelerations, because the mass \(m\), respectively the matrix \(\mat M\), is positive, respectively positive definite (theorem 8.86). This holds in general and says that the motion exists and is unique if we know the position and velocity at some time. The proof needs the theorem that a system of differential equations containing only first derivatives of the unknowns has a solution and that it is unique. Chapter 5 stated it without proof and announced that we would need it for the equations of motion, so we prove it here. The idea is Picard's: the solution is the limit of approximations, each of which arises from the previous one by an integral, and the errors of the approximations decrease as fast as the terms \(x^k/k!\).

Theorem 8.93 Picard's theorem for systems (Picardov izrek za sisteme)

Let \(D\) be an open set of points \((t, \vek y)\) with \(t \in \mathbb R\) and \(\vek y \in \mathbb R^N\), and \(\vek G = (G_1, \dots, G_{\mathrm{N}})\) a map whose components \(G_{\mathrm{i}}\) and partial derivatives \(\partial G_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y_{\mathrm{j}}\) are continuous on \(D\). Then:

  1. For every point \((t_0, \vek y_0)\) of \(D\) there exist \(\delta > 0\) and a differentiable function \(\vek y\) on \([t_0 - \delta, t_0 + \delta]\), whose graph lies in \(D\), with \(\vek y'(t) = \vek G(t, \vek y(t))\) and \(\vek y(t_0) = \vek y_0\).
  2. If \(\vek y\) and \(\vek z\) are differentiable functions with graphs in \(D\) on an interval \(J\) that contains \(t_0\), with \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y)\), \(\vek z' = \vek G(t, \vek z)\) and \(\vek y(t_0) = \vek z(t_0)\), then \(\vek y = \vek z\) on \(J\).
Proof

We must show existence (item 1) and uniqueness (item 2). We reduce both to an integral equation, on which we use an estimate with a Lipschitz constant \(L\), which says that \(\vek G\) changes with \(\vek y\) at most \(L\) times as fast as \(\vek y\).

Preparation.

  1. For a column \(\vek v\) with components \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) let \(\|\vek v\| = |v_1| + \dots + |v_{\mathrm{N}}|\). By the triangle inequality for numbers (lemma 1.6), \(\|\vek u + \vek v\| \le \|\vek u\| + \|\vek v\|\). Let \(\vek g\) be a continuous column of functions on \([t_0, t]\). The functions \(|g_{\mathrm{i}}|\) are continuous, because \(||a| - |b|| \le |a - b|\) by the triangle inequality. From \(-|g_{\mathrm{i}}| \le g_{\mathrm{i}} \le |g_{\mathrm{i}}|\) it follows by linearity and monotonicity of the integral (lemma 3.39, items 1 and 4) that \(|\int g_{\mathrm{i}}| \le \int |g_{\mathrm{i}}|\), and summing over \(i\) gives \(\big\|\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \vek g\,\mathrm{d}s\big\| \le \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \|\vek g\|\,\mathrm{d}s\).
  2. Since \(D\) is open, there exist \(a > 0\) and \(r > 0\) such that the set \(B = \{(t, \vek y) : |t - t_0| \le a,\ \|\vek y - \vek y_0\| \le r\}\) lies in \(D\). The set \(B\) is closed and bounded, so the continuous functions \(\|\vek G\| = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} |G_{\mathrm{i}}|\) and \(|\partial G_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y_{\mathrm{j}}|\) attain largest values on \(B\) (theorem 8.26). Let \(M\) be the largest value of \(\|\vek G\|\), \(c_{\mathrm{ij}}\) the largest value of \(|\partial G_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial y_{\mathrm{j}}|\), and \(L = \max_{\mathrm{j}} \sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  3. The Lipschitz estimate: for \((t, \vek y)\) and \((t, \vek z)\) in \(B\), \(\|\vek G(t, \vek z) - \vek G(t, \vek y)\| \le L\,\|\vek z - \vek y\|\). The points \(\vek y + \tau(\vek z - \vek y)\) for \(\tau \in [0, 1]\) have \(\|\cdot - \vek y_0\| \le (1 - \tau)\|\vek y - \vek y_0\| + \tau\|\vek z - \vek y_0\| \le r\), so the segment lies in \(B\). The function \(\varphi(\tau) = G_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek y + \tau(\vek z - \vek y))\) is differentiable by lemma 8.28 with \(\varphi'(\tau) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial G_{\mathrm{i}}}{\partial y_{\mathrm{j}}}(\cdots)\,(z_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{j}})\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.84), \(G_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek z) - G_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek y) = \varphi'(\tau_{\mathrm{i}})\) for some \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}} \in (0, 1)\), so \(|G_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek z) - G_{\mathrm{i}}(t, \vek y)| \le \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{ij}}|z_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{j}}|\). We sum over \(i\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{ij}}|z_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{j}}| = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{ij}}\big)|z_{\mathrm{j}} - y_{\mathrm{j}}| \le L\|\vek z - \vek y\|\).
  4. The integral equation: if \(\vek y\) is differentiable with graph in \(D\) and \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y)\), then \(G_{\mathrm{i}}(s, \vek y(s))\) is continuous as a composite function (lemma 8.25), so by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.40, item 1)
    \[\vek y(t) = \vek y_0 + \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \vek G(s, \vek y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s . \tag{8.88}\]
    Conversely, a continuous function \(\vek y\) with graph in \(D\) that satisfies (8.88) is, by the fundamental theorem (item 2), differentiable with \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y)\) and \(\vek y(t_0) = \vek y_0\).
  5. It suffices to treat \(t \ge t_0\). If \(\vek y\) is a solution for \(t \le t_0\), then \(\tilde{\vek y}(\tau) = \vek y(2t_0 - \tau)\) is a solution for \(\tau \ge t_0\) of the equation with the map \(\tilde{\vek G}(\tau, \vek y) = -\vek G(2t_0 - \tau, \vek y)\), which has the same properties, the same \(M\) and \(L\) on the reflected set \(B\), and conversely. If we have solutions on both sides of \(t_0\), they have the same one-sided derivative \(\vek G(t_0, \vek y_0)\) at \(t_0\), so the joined function is differentiable at \(t_0\) (one-sided derivatives as in chapter 1).
  6. An auxiliary estimate: let \(x \ge 0\) and \(a_{\mathrm{k}} = x^k/k!\). Let \(k_0\) be a natural number with \(k_0 \ge 2x\) (Archimedean property, theorem 1.35). For \(k \ge k_0\), \(a_{\mathrm{k}+1}/a_{\mathrm{k}} = x/(k + 1) \le \tfrac12\), so \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \le a_{\mathrm{k}_0}\,2^{-(k - k_0)}\). From \(2^j \ge j + 1\) (induction) it follows that \(2^{-j}\) tends to zero, so \(a_{\mathrm{k}} \to 0\). The sums \(\sum_{\mathrm{k} \le \mathrm{m}} a_{\mathrm{k}}\) grow with \(m\) and are bounded by \(\sum_{\mathrm{k} < \mathrm{k}_0} a_{\mathrm{k}} + 2a_{\mathrm{k}_0}\), because \(1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + 2^{-j} < 2\).

Existence.

  1. Let \(\delta = \min\{a,\ r/(M + 1)\}\) and \(I = [t_0, t_0 + \delta]\). The Picard iterates (Picardovi približki) are \(\vek y_0(t) = \vek y_0\) and \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t) = \vek y_0 + \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \vek G(s, \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\). By induction, every \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous on \(I\) and \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t) - \vek y_0\| \le M(t - t_0) \le M\delta < r\). For \(k = 0\) this holds. If it holds for \(k\), then \((s, \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s))\) is in \(B\), the function \(\vek G(s, \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s))\) is continuous (as in step 4) and bounded by \(M\), so \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is continuous (fundamental theorem, item 2) and by step 1 \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t) - \vek y_0\| \le M(t - t_0)\).
  2. By induction on \(k\), \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\| \le M L^k (t - t_0)^{k+1}/(k + 1)!\). For \(k = 0\) this is \(\|\vek y_1 - \vek y_0\| \le M(t - t_0)\) from step 7. If it holds for \(k\), then by step 3, step 1 and monotonicity of the integral (the integral of a power from the fundamental theorem, theorem 3.40),
    \[\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+2}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t)\| \le \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t L\,\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(s) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s)\|\,\mathrm{d}s \le \frac{M L^{k+1}}{(k + 1)!}\int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t (s - t_0)^{k+1}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{M L^{k+1}(t - t_0)^{k+2}}{(k + 2)!} .\]
    Hence \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\| \le c_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = M L^k \delta^{k+1}/(k + 1)! \le M\delta\,a_{\mathrm{k}}\) at \(x = L\delta\) from step 6.
  3. By step 6 the series \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}\) converges: the partial sums are increasing and bounded, so they converge by the monotone convergence theorem (theorem 1.51). Let \(S\) be its sum and \(\rho_{\mathrm{k}} = S - \sum_{\mathrm{j} < \mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\). Then \(\rho_{\mathrm{k}} \to 0\) (lemma 1.60) and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}=\mathrm{k}}^{m-1} c_{\mathrm{j}} \le \rho_{\mathrm{k}}\) for every \(m > k\).
  4. For given \(t \in I\) and \(i\) let \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = y_{\mathrm{j}+1,\mathrm{i}}(t) - y_{\mathrm{j},\mathrm{i}}(t)\), so \(|d_{\mathrm{j}}| \le c_{\mathrm{j}}\). Let \(u_{\mathrm{j}} = \max(d_{\mathrm{j}}, 0)\) and \(w_{\mathrm{j}} = \max(-d_{\mathrm{j}}, 0)\), so that \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = u_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(0 \le u_{\mathrm{j}}, w_{\mathrm{j}} \le c_{\mathrm{j}}\). The partial sums of \(\sum u_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\sum w_{\mathrm{j}}\) are increasing and bounded by \(S\), so they converge (theorem 1.51), and \(y_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{i}}(t) = y_{0,\mathrm{i}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}<\mathrm{k}}(u_{\mathrm{j}} - w_{\mathrm{j}})\) converges (lemma 1.60). We denote the limit \(y_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) and \(\vek y(t) = (y_1(t), \dots, y_{\mathrm{N}}(t))\).
  5. For \(k < m\) the triangle inequality gives \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{m}}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\| \le \sum_{\mathrm{j}=\mathrm{k}}^{m-1}\|\vek y_{\mathrm{j}+1}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{j}}(t)\| \le \rho_{\mathrm{k}}\). As \(m \to \infty\), by lemma 1.60 (a finite sum over components) and lemma 1.48 we obtain \(\|\vek y(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\| \le \rho_{\mathrm{k}}\) for every \(t \in I\), so with the same \(\rho_{\mathrm{k}}\) for all \(t\). From step 7, by the same lemma, \(\|\vek y(t) - \vek y_0\| \le M\delta < r\), so the graph of \(\vek y\) lies in \(B\).
  6. The function \(\vek y\) is continuous: for given \(t_1 \in I\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\) we choose \(k\) with \(\rho_{\mathrm{k}} < \varepsilon/3\) (step 9) and \(\eta > 0\) such that \(\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t_1)\| < \varepsilon/3\) for \(|t - t_1| < \eta\) (\(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous). Then \(\|\vek y(t) - \vek y(t_1)\| \le \|\vek y(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t)\| + \|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t) - \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t_1)\| + \|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(t_1) - \vek y(t_1)\| < \varepsilon\).
  7. The function \(\vek y\) satisfies (8.88). For \(t \in I\), \(\vek y_{\mathrm{k}+1}(t) = \vek y_0 + \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \vek G(s, \vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\), so by the triangle inequality and steps 1, 3, 11
    \[\Big\|\vek y(t) - \vek y_0 - \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t \vek G(s, \vek y(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\| \le \rho_{\mathrm{k}+1} + \int_{\mathrm{t}_0}^t L\,\|\vek y_{\mathrm{k}}(s) - \vek y(s)\|\,\mathrm{d}s \le \rho_{\mathrm{k}+1} + L\delta\,\rho_{\mathrm{k}} .\]
    The left side does not depend on \(k\), the right side tends to zero, so by lemma 1.48 the left side is zero. By step 4, \(\vek y\) is differentiable with \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y)\) and \(\vek y(t_0) = \vek y_0\) on \(I\). With step 5 we obtain a solution on \([t_0 - \delta, t_0 + \delta]\). This is item 1.

Uniqueness.

  1. First locally. Let \(\vek y\) and \(\vek z\) be solutions on an interval \(J\) that contains \(t_1\), with \(\vek y(t_1) = \vek z(t_1) = \vek y_1\), and let \(B\) from step 2 be built at \((t_1, \vek y_1)\). Since they are continuous, there exists \(h \in (0, a]\) such that for \(t \in J \cap [t_1, t_1 + h]\) we have \(\|\vek y(t) - \vek y_1\| \le r\) and \(\|\vek z(t) - \vek y_1\| \le r\). Let \(e(t) = \|\vek y(t) - \vek z(t)\|\), a continuous function with \(e \le 2r\). By step 4, \(\vek y(t) - \vek z(t) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^t (\vek G(s, \vek y) - \vek G(s, \vek z))\,\mathrm{d}s\), so by steps 1 and 3 \(e(t) \le L\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^t e\,\mathrm{d}s\). By induction on \(k\), \(e(t) \le 2r\,(L(t - t_1))^k/k!\): for \(k = 0\) this is \(e \le 2r\), and in the step from \(k\) to \(k + 1\), \(e(t) \le L\cdot 2r\,L^k\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^t (s - t_1)^k/k!\,\mathrm{d}s\). The right side is \(\le 2r\,a_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(x = Lh\) from step 6, and this tends to zero, so \(e(t) \le 0\) and \(\vek y = \vek z\) on \(J \cap [t_1, t_1 + h]\). To the left of \(t_1\) it is the same by step 5.
  2. Now on the whole interval. Let \(\vek y\) and \(\vek z\) be as in item 2 and \(P = \{t \in J,\ t \ge t_0 : \vek y = \vek z \text{ on } [t_0, t]\}\). The set \(P\) contains \(t_0\). Let \(t^* = \sup P\). If \(t^* < \sup J\), then \(t^* \in J\), and by continuity \(\vek y(t^*) = \vek z(t^*)\). By step 14 at \(t_1 = t^*\) the functions agree also on \([t^*, t^* + h]\), which contradicts \(t^* = \sup P\). So \(t^* = \sup J\), and every point \(t \in J\) with \(t \ge t_0\) lies below some element of \(P\) or equals \(t^*\) (then we use continuity), so \(\vek y(t) = \vek z(t)\). For \(t \le t_0\) we use step 5.

Corollary 8.94 Existence and uniqueness of the motion (obstoj in enoličnost gibanja)

In the setting of theorem 8.90 let the maps that give the position of the system (\(\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}\), for rigid bodies the position and rotation of the body) be three times continuously differentiable, \(V\) twice continuously differentiable and \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) continuously differentiable functions of \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(t\), all on an open set. For every time \(t_0\) and initial values \(\vek q(t_0) = \vek q^0\), \(\dot{\vek q}(t_0) = \vek v^0\) from this set there exists a motion \(\vek q(t)\) on some interval around \(t_0\), and any two such motions agree on a common interval.

Proof

We must show that (8.85) is a first order system with a right side that has continuous partial derivatives, and apply theorem 8.93.

  1. By theorem 8.86, \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q} + \vek b\cdot\dot{\vek q} + T_0\), where \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\) and \(T_0\) depend on \(\vek q\) and \(t\), not on \(\dot{\vek q}\). So \(\partial T/\partial\dot{\vek q} = \mat M\dot{\vek q} + \vek b\) and by the product rule and the chain rule (lemma 8.29) \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mat M\dot{\vek q} + \vek b) = \mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q} + \dot{\vek b}\), where \(\dot{\mat M} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (\partial\mat M/\partial q_{\mathrm{r}})\,\dot q_{\mathrm{r}} + \partial\mat M/\partial t\) and the same for \(\dot{\vek b}\), functions of \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\) and \(t\).
  2. The elements of \(\mat M\) and \(\vek b\) and \(T_0\) are sums of products of first derivatives of the maps of position and of the tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q, t)\) (steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 8.86). The maps of position are three times continuously differentiable, so these elements are twice continuously differentiable. Equation (8.85) is \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \vek h(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t)\) with \(\vek h = \partial T/\partial\vek q - \partial V/\partial\vek q + \vek Q^{\mathrm{nc}} - \dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q} - \dot{\vek b}\). The terms \(\partial T/\partial\vek q\) and \(\dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q}\) contain first derivatives of \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\), \(T_0\), which are continuously differentiable, so \(\vek h\) is a continuously differentiable function of \(\vek q\), \(\dot{\vek q}\), \(t\).
  3. We express the accelerations with the implicit function theorem (theorem 8.34). Let \(\vek F(\vek q, \vek v, t, \vek p) = \mat M(\vek q, t)\,\vek p - \vek h(\vek q, \vek v, t)\), continuously differentiable in all variables, with the unknown \(\vek p\) (the acceleration). The matrix \(\mat M\) is positive definite (theorem 8.86), so \(\mat M\vek w = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek w^\T\mat M\vek w = 0\) and \(\vek w = \vek 0\). By lemma 8.21, \(\mat M\) has \(n\) pivots and the system \(\mat M\vek p = \vek h\) has exactly one solution \(\vek p = \vek a(\vek q, \vek v, t)\). The matrix \(\partial\vek F/\partial\vek p = \mat M\) has \(n\) pivots at every point, so the implicit function theorem gives in a neighborhood of every point \((\vek q, \vek v, t)\) a continuously differentiable function \(\vek g\) with \(\vek F(\vek q, \vek v, t, \vek g) = \vek 0\). By uniqueness of the solution, \(\vek g = \vek a\) in this neighborhood. So \(\vek a\) is continuously differentiable everywhere.
  4. With \(\vek y = (\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) equation (8.85) is equivalent to the system \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y) = (\dot{\vek q}, \vek a(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}, t))\), whose right side has continuous partial derivatives. If \(\vek q\) is a twice differentiable motion, then by step 1 \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \vek h\), so \(\ddot{\vek q} = \vek a\) and \(\vek y\) solves the system. If \(\vek y = (\vek y_1, \vek y_2)\) solves the system, then \(\vek y_1' = \vek y_2\) and \(\vek y_2' = \vek a\), so \(\vek q = \vek y_1\) is twice differentiable, satisfies (8.85) and by theorem 8.90 is a motion. Existence and uniqueness of the motion with the given initial values are therefore items 1 and 2 of theorem 8.93 with \(N = 2n\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Theorem 8.93 is our proof after LEBL1 theorem 6.3.2 (PDF p. 247) and KREYSZIG §4.2, theorem 1 (PDF p. 163), which treat the initial value problem only for one equation, respectively without proof.

Let us also see what the corollary does not claim. It proves the existence of the motion on some interval around \(t_0\) and uniqueness, not existence for all times: the interval may be shorter than the time that interests us (for the equation \(y' = y^2\) from chapter 5 the solution does not exist for all time), and it does not compute the numbers of the motion. For a motion on an arbitrarily long interval we would need the continuation theorem for solutions: a solution of the system \(\vek y' = \vek G(t, \vek y)\) of theorem 8.93 whose graph stays, on an interval \([t_0, t_1)\) with finite \(t_1\), in a closed bounded set lying in \(D\) can be continued beyond \(t_1\). We do not prove this theorem and did not find it in the sources of the project. Where later we need a motion on a given interval or for all times (sections 8.12 and 8.13), we therefore state its existence as an assumption. For pendulum A the right side of (8.84) is smooth and independent of time, so for every initial angle and initial angular velocity there exists exactly one motion on some interval, and this is the curve that figure 8.27 drew.

8.9.2 Constraints as equations (vezi kot enačbe)

Until now we have chosen the coordinates so that the constraints are satisfied automatically, and we did not see the forces in the constraints. Sometimes we need them: the force in the rod of the pendulum decides whether the rod holds, the force in the guide of a slider whether the guide wears out. In section 8.5 we did this for equilibrium by keeping the constraint as an equation and using a multiplier. We do the same for motion. Let us take pendulum A in Cartesian coordinates \(\vek q = (x, y)\), which are two numbers, although the pendulum has one degree of freedom. The constraint is \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\), its matrix of derivatives \(\mat A = (2x,\ 2y)\) has one pivot, because one row is nonzero (the end of the rod is never at O). The virtual displacements that the constraint allows are those \(\delta \vek q = (\delta x, \delta y)\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 2x\,\delta x + 2y\,\delta y = 0\), that is, displacements along the tangent of the circle (figure 8.29).

In general, let the system be described by \(n\) numbers \(\vek q\) that satisfy \(m\) constraints \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q, t) = 0\) (\(r = 1, \dots, m\)) with the matrix \(A_{\mathrm{rs}} = \partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (\(m \times n\)) that has \(m\) pivots (definition 8.42). For nonholonomic constraints (definition 8.43) \(A_{\mathrm{rs}}(\vek q, t)\,\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} + b_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q, t) = 0\) the matrix \(\mat A\) is the matrix of coefficients of the velocities. In both cases the virtual displacements are those \(\delta \vek q\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\). For holonomic constraints this follows from the first order expansion \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q + \delta \vek q, t) = f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q, t)\) at constant time, for nonholonomic ones it is the convention for virtual displacements under such constraints (GR PDF p. 37).

Theorem 8.95 Lagrange's equations with multipliers (Lagrangeeve enačbe z množitelji)

Let the system be described by coordinates \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) that satisfy the constraints \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek q, t) = 0\) (or \(\mat A\dot{\vek q} + \vek b = 0\)) with a matrix \(\mat A\) with \(m\) pivots, \(m < n\). Let \(T\), \(V\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) be as in theorem 8.90, computed for all \(n\) coordinates. A twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) that satisfies the constraints satisfies d'Alembert's principle for every virtual displacement \(\delta \vek q\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\) if and only if there exists exactly one function \(\vek\lambda(t) = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}})\) of time for which

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}}\Big) - \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{m}\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,A_{\mathrm{rs}} , \qquad s = 1, \dots, n , \tag{8.89}\]

together with the constraints. These are \(n + m\) equations for \(n + m\) unknowns \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Proof

We must show that the difference between inertia and forces is perpendicular to all \(\delta \vek q\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\) if and only if it is a linear combination of the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\).

  1. The compatible virtual displacements are \(\delta\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (\partial\vek X_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(\delta \vek q\) that satisfies \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\) (the convention above). Steps 3 to 5 of the proof of theorem 8.88 are equalities for every \(\delta \vek q\) and every twice differentiable motion, they do not use the independence of the numbers \(\delta q_{\mathrm{s}}\). So d'Alembert's principle (steps 1 and 2 there), for every \(\delta \vek q\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\), reads \(\vek a\cdot\delta \vek q = 0\), where \(a_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}} - I_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(I_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\).
  2. By lemma 8.35, \(\vek a\cdot\delta \vek q = 0\) for every \(\delta \vek q\) with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\) if and only if \(\vek a = \mat A^\T\vek\mu\) for exactly one \(\vek\mu\) at each time. We write \(\vek\lambda = -\vek\mu\), so \(I_{\mathrm{s}} - Q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\).
  3. With \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) and steps 1 to 3 of the proof of theorem 8.90 we obtain (8.89). The steps are equalities, so the converse holds too.
  4. Uniqueness: the matrix \(\mat A^\T\) has \(m\) pivots, so \(\mat A^\T\vek\mu = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek\mu = \vek 0\) and \(\vek\lambda\) is determined at each time.

Source: GR (1.77)-(1.82) (PDF pp. 62-63), SHABANA (3.114), (3.115) (PDF p. 134), EP (5.4.7) and (5.4.11) (pp. 255-257). The proof is after SHABANA PDF 134 and GR PDF 63, where the multipliers are chosen so that the last rows cover the dependent variables. Here we use lemma 8.35. The same steps hold for nonholonomic constraints, because we use only the condition \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\) (SHABANA PDF 134: "can be applied to both holonomic and nonholonomic systems").

The multipliers are the forces in the constraints. The constraint is ideal, so its forces do no work at any virtual displacement with \(\mat A\,\delta \vek q = 0\). The generalized constraint force \(\vek R\) is therefore perpendicular to all such displacements, and by lemma 8.35 \(\vek R = \mat A^\T\vek\mu'\), which is exactly the right term in (8.89). The equation is thus Newton's second law, in which, besides the applied forces, there is also the constraint force. This is the meaning of the multipliers: \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,A_{\mathrm{rs}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the generalized force of constraint \(r\) (GR (1.79), PDF p. 63).

Example 8.96 Pendulum A in Cartesian coordinates with a multiplier (nihalo A v kartezičnih koordinatah z množiteljem)

Pendulum A with \(m = 1\) kg, \(l = 1\) m, coordinates \(\vek q = (x, y)\), \(y\) upward, \(x = l\sin\theta\), \(y = -l\cos\theta\).

  1. The kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)\), the potential energy \(V = m g y\), the constraint \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\) and \(\mat A = (2x,\ 2y)\).
  2. Equations (8.89) are \(m\ddot x = 2\lambda x\) and \(m\ddot y = -m g + 2\lambda y\).
  3. We denote the force of the rod on the mass by \(N\), positive as a pull: it points toward O and is \(-N\,(x, y)/l\). This is the generalized constraint force \(\lambda\mat A = 2\lambda(x, y)\), so \(N = -2l\lambda\).
  4. We multiply the radial unit vector \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = (\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)\) with both equations: \(m(\ddot x\sin\theta - \ddot y\cos\theta) = 2\lambda l(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) + m g\cos\theta = 2\lambda l + m g\cos\theta\). With \(\ddot x = l(\ddot\theta\cos\theta - \dot\theta^2\sin\theta)\) and \(\ddot y = l(\ddot\theta\sin\theta + \dot\theta^2\cos\theta)\) the radial component of the acceleration is \(\ddot x\sin\theta - \ddot y\cos\theta = -l\dot\theta^2\), because the terms with \(\ddot\theta\) cancel. So \(-m l\dot\theta^2 = -N + m g\cos\theta\) and
    \[N = m\,(g\cos\theta + l\dot\theta^2) . \tag{8.90}\]
  5. We multiply the tangential unit vector \(\vek e_\theta = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) with both equations. The term with \(\lambda\) becomes \(2\lambda(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta) = 2\lambda l(\sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta) = 0\), and what remains is \(m l\ddot\theta = -m g\sin\theta\), so (8.84). The multiplier does not influence the motion along the circle.

The rod force depends on the velocity, not only on the position. For the pendulum released from rest at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) we multiply (8.84) by \(\dot\theta\): \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2\big) = -\tfrac{g}{l}\sin\theta\,\dot\theta = \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\tfrac{g}{l}\cos\theta\big)\), so \(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2 - \tfrac{g}{l}\cos\theta\) is constant, and with the initial values \(\dot\theta = 0\), \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (where \(\cos\theta = 0\)) we have \(\dot\theta^2 = 2(g/l)\cos\theta\). We insert into (8.90): \(N = 3\,m\,g\cos\theta\). At release \(N = 0\), because for a horizontal rod the weight is perpendicular to it and the velocity is zero, and at the bottom \(N = 3\,m g = 29{,}43\) N at angular velocity \(\dot\theta = \sqrt{2g/l} = 4{,}4294\) s\(^{-1}\). Why three times the weight? The weight \(m g = 9{,}81\) N must be balanced, and in addition a force \(m l\dot\theta^2 = 19{,}62\) N toward O is needed, which bends the path of the mass. Both together give \(29{,}43\) N. Figure 8.30 shows \(N(\theta)\).

We check (8.90) independently on the Cartesian system: we obtain the multiplier from the constraint differentiated twice with respect to time, \(x\ddot x + y\ddot y + \dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = 0\), into which we insert \(\ddot x\) and \(\ddot y\) from step 2: \(\lambda = m\,(g\,y - \dot x^2 - \dot y^2)/(2l^2)\). We then integrate the system for \(x\) and \(y\) numerically. We take the step \(10^{-4}\) s and release from rest at \(90^\circ\). At every step \(N\) from the multiplier agrees with \(3\,m\,g\cos\theta\) at the same angle to within \(2\cdot 10^{-13}\) N, at \(\theta = 60^\circ\) it is \(14{,}715\) N, at \(30^\circ\) \(25{,}487\) N, at \(0^\circ\) \(29{,}43\) N, and the constraint is satisfied after \(1{,}5\) s to within \(2\cdot 10^{-14}\) m\(^2\).

We close the section with the kinds of terms that Lagrange's equations contain, which we only name here. In coordinates the kinetic energy has three parts \(T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0\), in which \(T_2\) is the quadratic, \(T_1\) the linear and \(T_0\) the zeroth power of the velocities (theorem 8.86). The inertia part of the equations is, after GR, divided into transport forces (those that remain at \(\dot{\vek q} = 0\)), relative forces (from \(T_2\), among them the term \(\tfrac12 m'\dot\theta^2\) of mechanism D) and complementary forces, which are linear in the velocities with skew-symmetric coefficients and therefore do no work, called Coriolis or gyroscopic (GR (1.36)-(1.40), PDF pp. 46-47, figure 1.11, PDF p. 57). For scleronomic constraints, as for D, only \(T_2\) remains. Transport and gyroscopic forces come into play in rotating systems, which chapter 24 treats.
