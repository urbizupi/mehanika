Chapter 20 Damping models

20.8 First-order form and complex modes

For frame A (figure 20.65) we put a single dashpot under floor \(1\) in section 10.10.4 and saw that the modes are no longer uncoupled: the matrix \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\mat C\mat\Phi\) is not diagonal, theorem 10.66 demands a diagonal one, and we obtained the roots \(-0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02493 \pm 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s with a complex ratio between the floors. There we computed them with NumPy from the "first-order form", which we only named, and promised that this section tells it to the end. Now we must answer three questions. What is the first-order form and why is it equivalent to the equation of motion? What remains of the modes when the matrix \(\mat C\) is not classical? And does it still hold that every motion is a sum of modes that we compute one by one?

The idea is short. For a system with one degree of freedom we solved in chapter 9 the equation of second order by breaking it into a chain of equations of first order (theorem 9.41). Here we do the same with a vector: we take the velocities for additional unknowns. We get \(2n\) equations of first order, written with two matrices. The matrix in front of the derivative, however, is no longer positive definite, so the spectral theorem of section 10.3 does not apply to it and we will prove orthogonality and expansion once more. (figure 20.65)

Model and assumptions. A system with \(n\) coordinates \(\vek q(t)\) has the equation of motion \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q(t)\) (theorem 10.3). The matrices \(\mat M\), \(\mat K\) and \(\mat C\) are real and symmetric, \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are positive definite (definition 6.27) and \(\mat C\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), as with several viscous dashpots (lemma 9.2). Since \(\mat K\) is positive definite, the system cannot move as a whole without deforming (rigid-body modes are not treated, definition 10.33). The force \(\vek Q\) is continuous, which the integral in the expansion at the end of the section needs. The running example is frame A with one dashpot: masses \(m = 500\) kg, stiffness of one storey \(k = 501\,760\) N/m and a dashpot \(c_{\mathrm{d}} = 1416{,}70\) N s/m under floor \(1\) (section 10.10.4).

Let us draw the frame as we will need it. First the ground, which stays at rest. On it we put the columns, two in each storey. The columns of one storey together act as a spring of stiffness \(k\), because the force they give is proportional to the difference of the displacements of their ends. On top of the columns we lay the floors, each of mass \(m\). The displacements of the floors are measured from the rest position, so we mark them by the distances \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) between the dashed and the solid position. Under floor \(1\) we fix the dashpot with coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\), which connects only floor \(1\) to the ground, floor \(2\) does not feel it. Hence \(\mat C = c_{\mathrm{d}}\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 0)\), as in equation (10.97).

On the right is floor \(1\) as a free body. Newton's law for it is the same as in section 10.1, except that a third force is added: the dashpot pulls back with \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\dot q_1\) (orange arrow below), the lower columns pull back with \(k q_1\) (blue arrow upper left) and the upper ones forward with \(k(q_2 - q_1)\) (blue arrow right). All three pull, so they start at the edge of the body.

20.8.1 First-order form

What should we take for unknowns so that the equation is of first order? For one degree of freedom the initial conditions \(q(0)\) and \(\dot q(0)\) determined the solution (theorem 9.41, order \(2\)). Also with \(n\) coordinates we need \(2n\) numbers, positions and velocities, for the future to be determined. We call these numbers the state (stanje), because they contain everything we must know about the system at this moment to be able to compute its further course. (figure 20.65)

Definition 20.44 State and first-order form (stanje in zapis prvega reda)

Let \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C\dot{\vek q} + \mat K\vek q = \vek Q(t)\) be the equation of motion of a system with \(n\) coordinates with the assumptions above. The column with \(2n\) elements

\[\vek z(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \vek q(t) \\ \dot{\vek q}(t) \end{pmatrix} \tag{20.37}\]

is the state, the matrices with \(2n\) rows and columns

\[\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} \mat C & \mat M \\ \mat M & \mat 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat B = \begin{pmatrix} \mat K & \mat 0 \\ \mat 0 & -\mat M \end{pmatrix} \tag{20.38}\]

are the state matrices (the blocks are \(n \times n\)), and the equation

\[\mat A\,\dot{\vek z} + \mat B\,\vek z = \vek F(t) , \qquad \vek F = \begin{pmatrix} \vek Q \\ \vek 0 \end{pmatrix} \tag{20.39}\]

is the first-order form.

Source: CK (10.91) to (10.95) (PDF p. 325-326), the same form. GR (3.65) to (3.68) (PDF p. 192-193) have the same equation with the names \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) exchanged: their \(\mat A\) is our \(\mat B\) and conversely, and the form \(\mat A\vek r + \mat B\dot{\vek r} = \vek s\). GR writes that the form is not unique, and that the chosen one is symmetric (PDF p. 193).

The name "first order" is because the highest derivative in equation (20.39) is the first: only \(\dot{\vek z}\) appears. (figure 20.65)

Does equation (20.39) really say the same as the equation of motion? This is not self-evident, so let us draw it in blocks and read it row by row.

For now we write the state as \(\vek z = (\vek x, \vek y)\) and do not assume that \(\vek y\) is the velocity. The matrix \(\mat A\) has four blocks of size \(n \times n\), and so has the matrix \(\mat B\). The columns \(\dot{\vek z}\) and \(\vek z\) have two blocks of \(n\) elements each, \(\vek x\) above and \(\vek y\) below. On the right is the column \(\vek F\) with the force \(\vek Q\) above and zero below. A block equation is read by rows, just like an ordinary matrix equation: the upper row multiplies the upper blocks of both matrices by the blocks of the columns and gives \(\mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat M\dot{\vek y} + \mat K\vek x = \vek Q\), the lower row gives \(\mat M\dot{\vek x} - \mat M\vek y = \vek 0\). The lower row has no right-hand side. So it must express that the lower part of the state really is the velocity of the upper, and the lemma below shows that it does.

Why does \(\mat M\) stand in the lower row? The relation \(\vek y = \dot{\vek x}\) could also be written without it, as \(\dot{\vek x} - \vek y = \vek 0\). Then the identity matrix \(\mat I\) would stand at the lower left of \(\mat A\), with \(\mat M\) still at the upper right. The blocks that are mirror images across the diagonal would no longer be equal (for frame A the number \(1\) against \(500\) kg) and \(\mat A\) would not be symmetric. We shall need symmetry for the orthogonality of the modes further down in this section. So we multiply the lower row by \(\mat M\): this does not change the equation, because \(\mat M\) is invertible, and the two matrices become symmetric.

Lemma 20.45 Equivalence of the two forms (enakovrednost zapisov)

Let the assumptions of definition 20.44 hold.

  1. A differentiable function \(\vek z = (\vek x, \vek y)\) with values in \(\mathbb R^{2n}\) or \(\mathbb C^{2n}\) solves equation (20.39) exactly when \(\vek y = \dot{\vek x}\) and \(\vek x\) is a solution of \(\mat M\ddot{\vek x} + \mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x = \vek Q\). Then \(\vek z = (\vek x, \dot{\vek x})\).
  2. For a complex number \(s\) and a column \(\vek w = (\vek w_1, \vek w_2)\) with \(2n\) complex elements, \((s\mat A + \mat B)\vek w = \vek 0\) holds exactly when \(\vek w_2 = s\vek w_1\) and \((s^2\mat M + s\mat C + \mat K)\vek w_1 = \vek 0\).
  3. The matrices \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) are symmetric, \(\mat A\) is invertible, and neither of them is positive semidefinite.
Proof

We must show that both rows agree with the equation of motion and with the relation between velocity and position, and that the block with zeros does not spoil the properties of the matrices.

  1. The first row of equation (20.39) is \(\mat C\dot{\vek x} + \mat M\dot{\vek y} + \mat K\vek x = \vek Q\), the second is \(\mat M\dot{\vek x} - \mat M\vek y = \vek 0\).
  2. The matrix \(\mat M\) is positive definite, hence invertible: \(\mat M\vek v = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek v^{\T}\mat M\vek v = 0\), so \(\vek v = \vek 0\). The second row therefore gives \(\vek y = \dot{\vek x}\), and then \(\dot{\vek y} = \ddot{\vek x}\), so that the first row becomes the equation of motion. Conversely, if \(\vek y = \dot{\vek x}\) and the equation of motion hold, both rows are satisfied. This proves point 1.
  3. Point 2 follows in the same way, with \(s\) in place of the derivative: the second row \(s\mat M\vek w_1 - \mat M\vek w_2 = \vek 0\) gives \(\vek w_2 = s\vek w_1\), and the first row \(s\mat C\vek w_1 + s\mat M\vek w_2 + \mat K\vek w_1\) becomes \((s^2\mat M + s\mat C + \mat K)\vek w_1\).
  4. Symmetry: transposing a block matrix transposes every block and exchanges the off-diagonal blocks. The matrices \(\mat C\), \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) are symmetric, so transposing the blocks changes nothing. Off the diagonal, \(\mat M\) stands at both places in \(\mat A\) and \(\mat 0\) at both places in \(\mat B\), so the exchange changes nothing either.
  5. Invertibility of \(\mat A\): \(\mat A(\vek x, \vek y) = \vek 0\) means in the second row \(\mat M\vek x = \vek 0\), so \(\vek x = \vek 0\), and then in the first row \(\mat M\vek y = \vek 0\), so \(\vek y = \vek 0\). A square matrix with only the zero kernel is invertible (lemma 8.22).
  6. For matrix \(\mat A\) take, for every \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) and number \(\varepsilon > 0\), the columns \(\vek z_\pm = (\vek u, \pm\varepsilon\vek u)\). Their quadratic form is \(\vek z_\pm^{\T}\mat A\vek z_\pm = \vek u^{\T}\mat C\vek u \pm 2\varepsilon\,\vek u^{\T}\mat M\vek u\). For large enough \(\varepsilon\) it has a positive value for \(\vek z_+\) and a negative one for \(\vek z_-\), because \(\vek u^{\T}\mat M\vek u > 0\). So \(\mat A\) is neither positive nor negative semidefinite. For \(\mat B\) we take \(\vek z = (\vek 0, \vek v)\) with the value \(-\vek v^{\T}\mat M\vek v < 0\), so \(\mat B\) is not positive semidefinite (the value at \((\vek u, \vek 0)\) is \(\vek u^{\T}\mat K\vek u > 0\), so it is not negative semidefinite either).

Source: CK (10.94) to (10.95) (PDF p. 326): the same form and a remark that the matrices are symmetric. Points 2, 5 and 6 are not from the source.

For frame A (figure 20.65) the matrices are

\[\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 1416{,}70 & 0 & 500 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 500 \\ 500 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 500 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat B = \begin{pmatrix} 1\,003\,520 & -501\,760 & 0 & 0 \\ -501\,760 & 501\,760 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -500 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -500 \end{pmatrix} \tag{20.40}\]

and \(\det\mat A = 6{,}25 \cdot 10^{10} = (\det\mat M)^2\). For \(\vek u = (1, 0)\) and \(\varepsilon = 10^3\) we get \(\vek z_+^{\T}\mat A\vek z_+ = 1{,}0014 \cdot 10^6\) and \(\vek z_-^{\T}\mat A\vek z_- = -9{,}9858 \cdot 10^5\). So the matrix \(\mat A\) cannot be used as a mass matrix in the eigenproblem of chapter 10: that needed positive definiteness for orthogonality (theorem 10.21).

20.8.2 Complex modes and their roots

For one degree of freedom we looked for the solution in the form \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) (theorem 9.34). Let us try the same with the state: \(\vek z = \vek w\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\) in the homogeneous equation \(\mat A\dot{\vek z} + \mat B\vek z = \vek 0\) gives \((s\mat A + \mat B)\vek w\,\mathrm{e}^{st} = \vek 0\). Before we know \(s\), we know the form of \(\vek w\): by point 2 of lemma 20.45, \(\vek w = (\vek u, s\vek u)\) and \(\vek u\) solves equation (10.99) of section 10.10.4. (figure 20.67)

Definition 20.46 Eigenpair of the first-order form and complex mode (lastni par zapisa prvega reda in kompleksni način)

A complex number \(s\) and a nonzero column \(\vek w \in \mathbb C^{2n}\) are an eigenpair of the first-order form if \((s\mat A + \mat B)\vek w = \vek 0\). By point 2 of lemma 20.45, then \(\vek w = (\vek u, s\vek u)\) with \(\vek u \ne \vek 0\) and \((s^2\mat M + s\mat C + \mat K)\vek u = \vek 0\). The motion \(\vek q(t) = \mathrm{Re}\,(\vek u\,\mathrm{e}^{st})\) is a complex mode of the system. For \(s = -\alpha + \mathrm{i}\omega\) with \(\omega > 0\), \(\omega\) is the damped angular frequency and \(\zeta = \alpha/|s|\) the damping ratio of the mode, as in definition 9.5. At one degree of freedom \(s = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} + \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) (theorem 9.48), so \(|s| = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\alpha/|s| = \zeta\).

Source: CK (10.96) to (10.98) (PDF p. 326): \(\vek z = \boldsymbol\theta\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\) and "complex modes" (PDF p. 327), (10.111) and (10.112) (PDF p. 328): frequency and ratio from the real and imaginary part. GR (3.69) and (3.70) (PDF p. 193-194).

The name comes from the numbers in \(\vek u\). For an undamped or classically damped system \(\vek u\) can be chosen real, and all parts of the structure reach their extreme position at the same time. If \(\vek u\) cannot be chosen real, each of its elements has its own modulus and its own argument, that is its own amplitude and its own phase, and the parts move with shifted phases. For frame A, \(u_2/u_1 = 1{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\), which is a phase of \(1{,}417^\circ\) (section 10.10.4). (figure 20.67)

What can we say about the numbers \(s\) before we compute them? For one degree of freedom the roots were \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 9.48), lying in the left half-plane and meeting on the real axis at \(\zeta = 1\). Is there again no root with \(\mathrm{Re}\,s > 0\) (decay instead of growth)?

Lemma 20.47 The eigenvalues lie in the left half-plane in conjugate pairs (koreni so v levi polravnini in v konjugiranih parih)

Let the assumptions of definition 20.44 hold.

  1. If \((s, \vek w)\) is an eigenpair, then \((\bar s, \bar{\vek w})\) is also an eigenpair. The eigenvalues are therefore real or in pairs \(s\), \(\bar s\).
  2. For every eigenpair with \(\vek w = (\vek u, s\vek u)\) let \(m_{\mathrm{u}} = \bar{\vek u}^{\T}\mat M\vek u\), \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = \bar{\vek u}^{\T}\mat C\vek u\) and \(k_{\mathrm{u}} = \bar{\vek u}^{\T}\mat K\vek u\). These numbers are real, \(m_{\mathrm{u}} > 0\), \(k_{\mathrm{u}} > 0\), \(c_{\mathrm{u}} \ge 0\), and \(s\) is a root of the equation \(m_{\mathrm{u}} s^2 + c_{\mathrm{u}} s + k_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\).
  3. If \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 < 4 m_{\mathrm{u}} k_{\mathrm{u}}\), then \(s\) is non-real and \(\mathrm{Re}\,s = -c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2 m_{\mathrm{u}}) \le 0\). If \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 \ge 4 m_{\mathrm{u}} k_{\mathrm{u}}\), then \(s\) is real and negative. Hence \(\mathrm{Re}\,s \le 0\) for all eigenvalues, and \(\mathrm{Re}\,s = 0\) only for \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\).
Proof

We must show that the reality of the numbers and the sign of the coefficients follow from symmetry and definiteness, and what remains is a quadratic equation with real coefficients.

  1. The matrices \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) are real because \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\) and \(\mat K\) are real (definition 20.44). Conjugating the equation \((s\mat A + \mat B)\vek w = \vek 0\) gives \((\bar s\mat A + \mat B)\bar{\vek w} = \vek 0\) and \(\bar{\vek w} \ne \vek 0\). In the complex plane conjugation mirrors across the real axis, so the two roots of a pair lie one above the other. This proves point 1.
  2. Multiply the equation \((s^2\mat M + s\mat C + \mat K)\vek u = \vek 0\) (lemma 20.45, point 2) from the left by \(\bar{\vek u}^{\T}\). We get \(m_{\mathrm{u}} s^2 + c_{\mathrm{u}} s + k_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\).
  3. Let \(\mat S\) be a real symmetric matrix and \(\vek u = \vek a + \mathrm{i}\vek b\) with real columns. Then \(\bar{\vek u}^{\T}\mat S\vek u = \vek a^{\T}\mat S\vek a + \vek b^{\T}\mat S\vek b + \mathrm{i}(\vek a^{\T}\mat S\vek b - \vek b^{\T}\mat S\vek a)\), and the bracket is zero, because \(\vek a^{\T}\mat S\vek b\) is a scalar equal to its transpose \(\vek b^{\T}\mat S\vek a\). So \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{u}}\) are real, \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{u}}\) positive because \(\vek a\) or \(\vek b\) is nonzero, \(c_{\mathrm{u}} \ge 0\).
  4. If \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 < 4m_{\mathrm{u}}k_{\mathrm{u}}\), the roots of the quadratic equation are \((-c_{\mathrm{u}} \pm \mathrm{i}\sqrt{4m_{\mathrm{u}}k_{\mathrm{u}} - c_{\mathrm{u}}^2})/(2m_{\mathrm{u}})\), the real part is \(-c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2m_{\mathrm{u}})\), so \(c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2m_{\mathrm{u}})\) is the distance of the root from the vertical axis. If \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 \ge 4m_{\mathrm{u}}k_{\mathrm{u}}\), the roots are real with product \(k_{\mathrm{u}}/m_{\mathrm{u}} > 0\) and sum \(-c_{\mathrm{u}}/m_{\mathrm{u}} \le 0\). Equal signs with a positive product and a non-positive sum mean that both are negative (a sum of zero would mean \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\) and a negative discriminant).

Source: CK, PDF p. 327: real or conjugate pairs and the conjugate vector. GR, PDF p. 194: "conjugate eigensolutions", and PDF p. 195-196: multiplying by the conjugate vector gives a quadratic equation with real coefficients and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) for positive definite \(\mat C\). The proof with a positive semidefinite \(\mat C\) is not from the source.

Let us inspect the lemma on figure 20.67. We draw the complex plane, horizontally the real part \(\mathrm{Re}\,s\) and vertically \(\mathrm{Im}\,s\), and shade the left half-plane, where by point 3 of the lemma all roots fall. The roots of frame A are \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) and their conjugates, which point 1 of the lemma places mirror-wise across the real axis. Both pairs are shown as a dashed vertical segment between \(s\) and \(\bar s\), which the axis bisects. How far to the left a root lies is told by point 3 of the lemma: its distance from the vertical axis is \(c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2m_{\mathrm{u}})\), for the first mode \(0{,}392\) 1/s and for the second \(1{,}025\) 1/s (drawn at the two lower roots). The roots are very close to the vertical axis, because the damping ratios are \(0{,}02\). That is why the real axis in the figure is stretched \(20\) times.

Where do these two distances come from? The numbers of point 2 of the lemma for the two modes, for \(\vek u\) with \(u_1 = 1\), are:

mode \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) [kg] \(c_{\mathrm{u}}\) [N s/m] \(k_{\mathrm{u}}\) [N/m] \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 - 4m_{\mathrm{u}}k_{\mathrm{u}}\) \(\mathrm{Re}\,s = -c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2m_{\mathrm{u}})\) [1/s]
\(1\) \(1808{,}08\) \(1416{,}70\) \(693\,552{,}6\) \(-5{,}014 \cdot 10^9\) \(-0{,}39177\)
\(2\) \(691{,}12\) \(1416{,}70\) \(1\,814\,444{,}4\) \(-5{,}014 \cdot 10^9\) \(-1{,}02493\)

For both, \(c_{\mathrm{u}}^2 - 4m_{\mathrm{u}}k_{\mathrm{u}} < 0\), so the roots are non-real, and the real parts are the distances \(0{,}392\) and \(1{,}025\), as the NumPy roots give. The difference between the modes is not in the dashpot: \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = c_{\mathrm{d}}\,|u_1|^2 = c_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the same in both rows, because the dashpot feels only floor \(1\). The difference is in how much mass moves: in the first mode both floors move in the same direction, \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) is larger and the damping per unit mass smaller.

On the right are the axes and on them the semicircle of roots of cantilever A (chapter 9, theorem 9.48) for growing \(\zeta\). It starts at \(\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(\zeta = 0\), no damping, \(\mathrm{Re}\,s = 0\) by point 3 of the lemma with \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\)), for every \(\zeta < 1\) it lies on the circle of radius \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (because \(|s|^2 = (\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(1 - \zeta^2) = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\)) and at \(\zeta = 1\) the two ends meet in the double root \(-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). For \(\zeta > 1\) two real roots \(-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(\zeta \pm \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\) leave it, negative by point 3 of the lemma. We will need the double root at the end of this section as a near miss.

A near miss for the lemma is a dashpot with a negative coefficient, so that \(\mat C\) is not positive semidefinite. For frame A with \(-c_{\mathrm{d}}\) instead of \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) the roots are \(+0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(+1{,}02493 \pm 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s, mirrored across the vertical axis into the right half-plane (hollow circles). The real parts are positive, because \(c_{\mathrm{u}} = -1416{,}70 < 0\) and \(\mathrm{Re}\,s = -c_{\mathrm{u}}/(2m_{\mathrm{u}}) > 0\): the amplitude of mode \(1\) doubles every \(\ln 2/0{,}39177 = 1{,}77\) s and does not decay, as it decays with the dashpot \(c_{\mathrm{d}}\) to \(1/\mathrm{e}\) every \(2{,}55\) s. Point 3 of the lemma therefore needs \(c_{\mathrm{u}} \ge 0\), that is a positive semidefinite \(\mat C\).

20.8.3 Orthogonality of complex modes

The goal is to expand every motion in complex modes, as we did in real ones (theorem 10.66). For real modes the key was orthogonality with respect to \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) (theorem 10.21). What about here? Let us try the same idea as there: multiply the eigen-equations of two modes by the other mode and subtract.

Theorem 20.48 Orthogonality of complex modes (pravokotnost kompleksnih načinov)

Let the assumptions of definition 20.44 hold and let \((s_{\mathrm{i}}, \vek w_{\mathrm{i}})\) and \((s_{\mathrm{j}}, \vek w_{\mathrm{j}})\) be eigenpairs of the first-order form with \(s_{\mathrm{i}} \ne s_{\mathrm{j}}\). Then

\[\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\,\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 , \qquad \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\,\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 , \tag{20.41}\]

where \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\) is the transposed column without conjugation. For every eigenpair with \(a = \vek w^{\T}\mat A\vek w\) and \(b = \vek w^{\T}\mat B\vek w\) we have \(s\,a + b = 0\).

Proof

We must show that in the difference of the two eigen-equations, each multiplied by the other column, the term with \(\mat B\) cancels and only the term with the factor \(s_{\mathrm{i}} - s_{\mathrm{j}}\) remains.

  1. Multiplying the equation \((s_{\mathrm{i}}\mat A + \mat B)\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\) from the left by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\): \(s_{\mathrm{i}}\,\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  2. Multiplying the equation \((s_{\mathrm{j}}\mat A + \mat B)\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\) from the left by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^{\T}\) and transposing the scalar: \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\), because \(\mat A\) and \(\mat B\) are symmetric (lemma 20.45, point 3). We get \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\,\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  3. The difference of steps 1 and 2 is \((s_{\mathrm{i}} - s_{\mathrm{j}})\,\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). Since \(s_{\mathrm{i}} \ne s_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), and step 1 gives also \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  4. For \(i = j\) step 1 gives \(s\,a + b = 0\).

Source: CK (10.100) to (10.107) (PDF p. 327): the same procedure, equations (10.106) and (10.107). GR, PDF p. 194, equation (3.71). The last claim (\(s\,a + b = 0\)) is CK (10.110) (PDF p. 328).

The proof showed why we need symmetry: both matrices must be symmetric so that the products \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\) agree, and so do the products \(\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\). In the form (20.38) both are symmetric. The proof needs no definiteness, only symmetry and distinct eigenvalues, so it holds also for an indefinite \(\mat A\).

Two conditions of the theorem are essential. The first is \(s_{\mathrm{i}} \ne s_{\mathrm{j}}\). The left plane in Figure 20.68 shows what happens without it. Take two equal undamped oscillators, \(\mat M = \mat I\) kg, \(\mat C = \mat 0\), \(\mat K = 100\,\mat I\) N/m, which have the double eigenvalue \(s = 10\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s. The eigenpairs \(\vek w_{\mathrm{a}} = (\vek u_{\mathrm{a}}, 10\mathrm{i}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{a}})\) and \(\vek w_{\mathrm{b}} = (\vek u_{\mathrm{b}}, 10\mathrm{i}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{b}})\) with \(\vek u_{\mathrm{a}} = (1, 0)\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{b}} = (0, 1)\) give \(\vek w_{\mathrm{a}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\), but with \(\vek u_{\mathrm{a}} = (1, 1)\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{b}} = (1, 2)\) they give \(\vek w_{\mathrm{a}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{b}} = 60\mathrm{i} \ne 0\). For an equal eigenvalue orthogonality is therefore not guaranteed, we must choose it.

The second is transposing without conjugating. For frame A we multiply all four eigencolumns in pairs and put the numbers into a grid.

The rows and columns of both grids are the four roots \(s_1\), \(\bar s_1\), \(s_2\), \(\bar s_2\). In the left grid the cell in row \(j\) and column \(i\) holds the number \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\), as in theorem 20.48. On the diagonal are the numbers \(a_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\), namely \(a_1 = -2531{,}6 + 70\,696{,}2\,\mathrm{i}\), \(a_2 = 2531{,}6 + 70\,696{,}2\,\mathrm{i}\) and their conjugates, all four with modulus \(70\,741\). Off the diagonal, \(|\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}|\) is at most \(10^{-15}\,|a_{\mathrm{r}}|\), that is zero within rounding, as equation (20.41) says. In the right grid we replace the transpose by the conjugate transpose, \(\bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{i}}\). The diagonal is now zero (modulus below \(10^{-10}\)), and the numbers \(a_{\mathrm{r}}\) have moved to the cells where the row and the column are a conjugate pair.

The reason is short. The column \(\bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the eigencolumn of the root \(\bar s_{\mathrm{r}}\) (lemma 20.47, point 1), and for a non-real \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) the root \(\bar s_{\mathrm{r}}\) differs from \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\). The number \(\bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) is therefore a product of two different modes, which by the theorem is zero. Conjugation only exchanges the two rows of each pair. The computation says the same: for \(\vek w = (\vek u, s\vek u)\) and non-real \(s\), \(\bar{\vek w}^{\T}\mat A\vek w = c_{\mathrm{u}} + (s + \bar s)\,m_{\mathrm{u}} = c_{\mathrm{u}} + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,s\;m_{\mathrm{u}} = c_{\mathrm{u}} - c_{\mathrm{u}} = 0\) by point 3 of lemma 20.47. With the number zero we cannot normalize the expansion, since we would divide by zero. The expansion therefore makes sense only with transposing without conjugating.

20.8.4 Expansion in complex modes

Orthogonality leads us to the main theorem of the section. If we have \(2n\) eigenpairs with distinct \(s\), we will show that their columns are independent, so every state has the form of a sum of their multiples. By theorem 20.48 they are orthogonal to one another, so we find the coefficients as for a Fourier series (lemma 9.109): we multiply by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\) and a single term remains.

Theorem 20.49 Expansion in complex modes (razvoj po kompleksnih načinih)

Let the assumptions of definition 20.44 hold and \(T > 0\). Let the first-order form have \(2n\) distinct eigenvalues \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) with eigencolumns \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = (\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}, s_{\mathrm{r}}\vek u_{\mathrm{r}})\), \(r = 1, \dots, 2n\) (there cannot be more, step 1 of the proof). By lemma 20.47, for every \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) also \(\bar s_{\mathrm{r}}\) is an eigenvalue, so it equals \(s_{\bar{r}}\) for some \(\bar r \in \{1, \dots, 2n\}\). We choose the eigencolumns so that \(\vek w_{\bar{r}} = \bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the sum in equation (20.42) does not change under another choice, because \(p_{\mathrm{r}}\) is multiplied by the reciprocal value). Let \(a_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\).

  1. The columns \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) are linearly independent (definition 6.22), form a basis of \(\mathbb C^{2n}\) (every column is a linear combination of them in exactly one way) and \(a_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\).
  2. For all real \(\vek q_0\) and \(\vek v_0\) and continuous \(\vek Q\) on \([0, T]\) the equation of motion with \(\vek q(0) = \vek q_0\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0) = \vek v_0\) has exactly one twice differentiable solution, namely the first \(n\) elements of the column \(\vek z(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} p_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\,\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) with
    \[p_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = \mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{r}} t}\Big( c_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{r}}}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{-s_{\mathrm{r}}\tau}\,\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau \Big) , \qquad c_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat A\,\vek z_0}{a_{\mathrm{r}}} , \tag{20.42}\]
    where \(\vek z_0 = (\vek q_0, \vek v_0)\). For \(\vek Q = \vek 0\), \(\vek q(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{r}} t}\,\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the sum is real.
Idea

With independence we expand \(\vek z_0\) and the force in the columns \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\), orthogonality (theorem 20.48) extracts the coefficients, each coefficient is solved by an equation of first order (lemma 9.39), and uniqueness we get from energy, not from the expansion.

Proof

We must show four things: independence, that the sum solves the equation with the given initial conditions, that it is real and that the solution is unique.

  1. Independence. Let \(\mat N = -\mat A^{-1}\mat B\) (lemma 20.45, point 3). From \((s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat A + \mat B)\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) it follows \(\mat N\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\). Suppose a dependent set exists and choose the smallest, \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{k}}\) with \(k \ge 2\) (one column is nonzero), so that \(\sum_{\mathrm{r} \le \mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) with all \(d_{\mathrm{r}} \ne 0\). Applying \(\mat N - s_{\mathrm{k}}\mat I\) gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{r} < \mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{r}}(s_{\mathrm{r}} - s_{\mathrm{k}})\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). Because the roots are distinct, \(s_{\mathrm{r}} - s_{\mathrm{k}} \ne 0\). This is a dependent set of \(k - 1\) columns with nonzero coefficients, which contradicts the choice of the smallest. There are no other eigenvalues: if \((s, \vek w)\) is an eigenpair with \(s \ne s_{\mathrm{r}}\) for all \(r\), then \(\vek w = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\), applying \(\mat N - s\mat I\) gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}(s_{\mathrm{r}} - s)\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\), by independence all \(d_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and \(\vek w = \vek 0\), which is not an eigencolumn. By independence the matrix \(\mat W\) with columns \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) has only the zero kernel, so by lemma 8.22 it has an inverse and \(\mat W\vek c = \vek z_0\) has exactly one solution for every \(\vek z_0\) (elimination uses only the four arithmetic operations, so it holds for complex numbers too). The columns \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) are therefore a basis.
  2. Coefficients. Let \(\vek z_0 = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) (basis). Multiplying by \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\) and theorem 20.48 (the point \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) for \(r \ne j\)) gives \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\vek z_0 = c_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\). If \(a_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\), the row \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\) would be orthogonal to the whole basis, so \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A = \vek 0\). By symmetry \(\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = (\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A)^{\T} = \vek 0\), by invertibility \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\), which is not an eigencolumn. So \(a_{\mathrm{j}} \ne 0\) and \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) is given by equation (20.42).
  3. Expansion of the force. We expand \(\mat A^{-1}\vek F(t) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\). The same procedure as in step 2 gives \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\mat A\,\mat A^{-1}\vek F = d_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{j}}\), so \(d_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek w_{\mathrm{j}}^{\T}\vek F/a_{\mathrm{j}}\) and \(\vek F = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  4. Solution. From \((s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat A + \mat B)\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\), \(\mat B\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = -s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\). The function \(p_{\mathrm{r}}\) of equation (20.42) solves \(p_{\mathrm{r}}' - s_{\mathrm{r}} p_{\mathrm{r}} = d_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(p_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = c_{\mathrm{r}}\) (lemma 9.39, continuous \(d_{\mathrm{r}}\)). For \(\vek z = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} p_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\), the row equation \(\mat A\dot{\vek z} + \mat B\vek z = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} (p_{\mathrm{r}}' - s_{\mathrm{r}} p_{\mathrm{r}})\,\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} d_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat A\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek F\) by step 3, and \(\vek z(0) = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek z_0\). By lemma 20.45, point 1, the first \(n\) elements of the column \(\vek z\) are a solution of the equation of motion with \(\vek q(0) = \vek q_0\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(0) = \vek v_0\).
  5. Reality. Conjugating the equation for \(p_{\mathrm{r}}\) (conjugate roots) gives, because \(\vek F\) and \(\vek z_0\) are real, \(\bar p_{\mathrm{r}}' - \bar s_{\mathrm{r}}\bar p_{\mathrm{r}} = \bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F/\bar a_{\mathrm{r}}\) with initial value \(\bar c_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the equation for \(p_{\bar{r}}\), because \(\vek w_{\bar{r}} = \bar{\vek w}_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(a_{\bar{r}} = \bar a_{\mathrm{r}}\). By uniqueness in lemma 9.39, \(p_{\bar{r}} = \bar p_{\mathrm{r}}\). The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} p_{\mathrm{r}}\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) has its terms in conjugate pairs and is real. For real \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) we choose \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}\) real, and \(p_{\mathrm{r}}\) is real by the same reasoning.
  6. Uniqueness. Let \(\vek q\) and \(\tilde{\vek q}\) be twice differentiable solutions with the same initial conditions and \(\vek d = \vek q - \tilde{\vek q}\). Then \(\mat M\ddot{\vek d} + \mat C\dot{\vek d} + \mat K\vek d = \vek 0\), \(\vek d(0) = \dot{\vek d}(0) = \vek 0\). Let \(E = \tfrac12\dot{\vek d}^{\T}\mat M\dot{\vek d} + \tfrac12\vek d^{\T}\mat K\vek d \ge 0\). By the symmetry of \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\), \(E' = \dot{\vek d}^{\T}(\mat M\ddot{\vek d} + \mat K\vek d) = -\dot{\vek d}^{\T}\mat C\dot{\vek d} \le 0\). Since \(E(0) = 0\) and \(E \ge 0\), \(E \equiv 0\), so \(\vek d \equiv \vek 0\) (definiteness of \(\mat K\)).

Source: Orthogonality and the modal numbers \(a_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{r}}\): CK, PDF p. 327-328. The expansion of the state in eigencolumns is only announced in GR (3.76) (PDF p. 196) and CK (PDF p. 326: "mode-superposition solutions become possible"). The proof of the expansion with a force, of independence, of reality and of uniqueness by energy is not from the source. The existence of the eigenpairs is assumed.

The proof showed where the theorem needs what it needs. The distinct eigenvalues gave a basis in step 1, orthogonality gave the coefficients and the nonzero numbers \(a_{\mathrm{r}}\) in step 2, and the definiteness of \(\mat K\) and the semidefiniteness of \(\mat C\) are needed only by uniqueness in step 6. The existence of \(2n\) eigenpairs the theorem assumes and does not prove.

Let us test it on frame A with one dashpot from the state \(\vek q_0 = (0, 1\text{ mm})\), \(\vek v_0 = \vek 0\). The four eigenvalues are distinct, so the theorem holds. We normalize the columns \(\vek u_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(u_{\mathrm{r}1} = 1\) and compute the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) by equation (20.42):

root \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) [1/s] \(u_{\mathrm{r}2}\) \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) [m]
\(s_1 = -0{,}39177 + 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) \(1{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\) \(2{,}2389 \cdot 10^{-4} + 2{,}002 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{i}\)
\(s_2 = -1{,}02493 + 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) \(-0{,}61696 + 0{,}04000\,\mathrm{i}\) \(-2{,}2389 \cdot 10^{-4} + 2{,}002 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{i}\)

At the conjugate roots \(\bar s_1\) and \(\bar s_2\), \(u_{\mathrm{r}2}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the conjugates.

Figure 20.70 shows the consequence. We draw the axes, time \(t\) horizontally and \(q_2\) vertically in millimetres. Floor \(2\) starts at \(1\) mm. The sum of all four terms is the blue curve. We split it into two pairs: the pair of the first mode (\(s = -0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\)), which is \(2\,\mathrm{Re}(c_1 u_{12}\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t})\), and the pair of the second mode, which has a higher frequency and decays faster. The dashed curves are the envelopes given by the modulus \(2|c_{\mathrm{r}}u_{\mathrm{r}2}|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{Re}\,s_{\mathrm{r}} t}\). The first envelope at \(t = 0\) is \(0{,}7243\) mm, the second \(0{,}2769\) mm. The pairs themselves are slightly lower at \(t = 0\), \(0{,}7239\) mm and \(0{,}2761\) mm, because \(c_{\mathrm{r}}u_{\mathrm{r}2}\) is complex and the term is then not at the peak of its oscillation. These two distances are stacked one on the other left of the axis and reach exactly the mark \(1\): the sum of the pairs, \(1{,}0000\) mm, is the initial displacement. Finally the circles are an independent computation: numerical solution of the equation of motion in time by the Runge-Kutta method of fourth order (RK4), \(6000\) steps over \(1{,}2\) s, which does not use modes. The largest difference between the expansion and RK4 at the circles is \(4{,}7 \cdot 10^{-10}\) mm. At \(t = 0{,}5\) s, \(\vek q = (-0{,}58145, -0{,}39629)\) mm by both routes (the second element, \(-0{,}396\) mm, is read off the curve in the figure), and the imaginary part of the sum is below \(10^{-18}\) mm, that is zero within rounding. The energy \(E = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^{\T}\mat M\dot{\vek q} + \tfrac12\vek q^{\T}\mat K\vek q\) falls \(0{,}25088\), \(0{,}20716\), \(0{,}18065\), \(0{,}14954\), \(0{,}13104\), \(0{,}11528\) J at \(t = 0\), \(0{,}1\), \dots, \(0{,}5\) s: it never grows, as step 6 demands.

The theorem admits also a force. For a constant force \(\vek Q = (0, 100)\) N on floor \(2\) from rest (equation (20.42) with \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) and constant \(\vek F\): the integral is \((1 - \mathrm{e}^{-s_{\mathrm{r}}t})/s_{\mathrm{r}}\) times \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F\), so \(p_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = (\mathrm{e}^{s_{\mathrm{r}}t} - 1)/s_{\mathrm{r}}\) times \(\vek w_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek F/a_{\mathrm{r}}\)), \(\vek q(0{,}5) = (0{,}39914, 0{,}67742)\) mm, and RK4 gives the same to eight digits. The static value \(\mat K^{-1}\vek Q = (0{,}19930, 0{,}39860)\) mm is smaller: the displacement of floor \(2\) at \(t = 0{,}5\) s is \(1{,}70\) times the static one, because the force acts suddenly and the system overshoots, as with a step (theorem 9.81). (figure 20.70)

There are two near misses of the theorem. The first is the hypothesis of distinct eigenvalues, which critical damping violates. Take cantilever A, \(m = 50\) kg, \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, \(\zeta = 1\), so \(s_1 = s_2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = -35{,}4175\) 1/s, the double root in which the two ends of the semicircle meet. By point 2 of lemma 20.45 every eigencolumn has the form \((u, -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} u)\), that is a single independent column: the matrix \(s\mat A + \mat B\) at \(s = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) has singular values \(6{,}277\cdot 10^4\) and \(0\) (NumPy gives \(7\cdot 10^{-15}\) through rounding), so its kernel has dimension \(1\). Two independent eigencolumns therefore do not exist. The initial state \((0, v_0)\) is not a multiple of \((1, -\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\): the first element \(0\) would demand the multiple \(0\), and the second element is \(v_0 \ne 0\). The expansion therefore does not exist. The true motion is \(q = v_0\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}t}\) (equation (9.49) with \(q_0 = 0\)), which contains \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\), and functions of the form \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\vek u\) do not give it. For \(v_0 = 0{,}1\) m/s it reaches a maximum of \(1{,}0387\) mm at \(t = 1/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 28{,}2\) ms. CK calls this a defective matrix, which does not have \(N\) independent eigencolumns, and needs generalized eigencolumns and the Jordan form (PDF p. 334-335). We only quote this.

The second is nearly critical damping, where the expansion holds but the columns are almost dependent. As \(\zeta\) grows, the roots \(s\) and \(\bar s\) approach the double root along the semicircle, at \(\zeta = 0{,}9\) and \(0{,}99\) we can still tell them apart, at \(\zeta = 0{,}999\) they are only \(1{,}6\) 1/s away from it. With them the columns \((1, s)\) and \((1, \bar s)\) approach each other. For cantilever A and \(v_0 = 0{,}1\) m/s with columns \((1, s)\) the coefficients have size \(|c_{\mathrm{r}}| = v_0/(2\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\), so \(3{,}24\), \(10{,}0\) and \(31{,}6\) mm for \(\zeta = 0{,}9\), \(0{,}99\) and \(0{,}999\), the condition number of the matrix of eigencolumns is \(81\), \(251\) and \(793\) (at \(\zeta = 1\) the two columns coincide and the matrix is singular), and the largest displacement in the first \(0{,}5\) s is \(1{,}113\), \(1{,}046\) and \(1{,}039\) mm. The terms of \(31{,}6\) mm thus cancel to \(1{,}04\) mm, a factor of \(30\) smaller, and in double precision we lose \(\log_{10}30 \approx 1{,}5\) digits. The expansion is correct, but numerically ill-conditioned.

What have we obtained? To the equation of motion with an arbitrary symmetric positive semidefinite \(\mat C\) we assigned \(2n\) complex modes. When their eigenvalues are distinct, every motion is their sum, the coefficients are computed one by one by equation (20.42), as for real modes. The price is the eigenproblem of the non-symmetric matrix \(-\mat A^{-1}\mat B\) of order \(2n\) instead of the symmetric one of order \(n\). For frame A with one dashpot the roots are \(-0{,}39177 \pm 19{,}58141\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02493 \pm 51{,}2281\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s, and with classical damping with the same diagonal \(\mat\Phi^{\T}\mat C\mat\Phi\), that is with ratios \(0{,}02\), \(-0{,}39157 \pm 19{,}5744\,\mathrm{i}\) and \(-1{,}02513 \pm 51{,}2464\,\mathrm{i}\) (section 10.10.4): the difference is in the fourth digit. The shape changes, the ratio between the floors is complex. Section 10.10.4 estimated the error of the classical solution on the example of the response to a force, here is the exact theory that replaces it for estimation when the error is not acceptable. Truncation of modes and the effective modal mass are in chapter 21. (figure 20.70)
