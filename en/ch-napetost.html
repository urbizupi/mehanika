In chapter 1 we described how a body changes its shape: how far each of its points moves and how much the material close to it is stretched. But we did not ask why this happens. A bar gets longer because something pulls it. A hanging bar stretches because the Earth pulls it down and the ceiling holds it. Most of the deformations we study in this book are caused by forces, and in this chapter we turn to them. A body also deforms when it is heated (BOWER §3.2.1, PDF p. 96), and we will treat that in chapter 3.

For a structure the most important question is whether it holds. Let us pull a steel bar harder and harder. At some force it no longer returns to its original length after unloading, and at a still larger force it breaks. But this force is not a property of steel. A thin steel wire breaks under a force that a thick steel bar hardly notices. So how heavily the material of a bar is loaded depends not only on the force but also on how much material shares it. We will look for a number that tells this, and compare it with what steel can bear.

The route will be similar to that of chapter 1. First we will say exactly what a force is and when the forces on a body balance. Then we will cut the bar in thought and compute with what force one of its parts pulls the other. From this force we will get a number that measures how heavily the material is loaded. When we cut the bar obliquely, we will need new mathematics that splits a vector into a part in a given direction and a part perpendicular to that direction. At the end we will describe the force acting across a cut at each point of the cut separately.

2.1 Force in a bar

What is a force? In everyday language it is what we pull or push something with. A rope pulls a bar, the ceiling holds a hanging bar, the Earth pulls every bit of matter down. Two things count for each such force: how strongly it pulls and in which direction. A bar pulled along its axis gets longer. If we hold it firmly at one end and pull equally strongly across it at the other, it bends. So a force is a quantity with a size and a direction, and in mechanics exactly such quantities are described by vectors (section 1.5.5).

Definition 2.1 Force as a vector (sila kot vektor)

A force (sila) is a vector that describes how one body pulls or pushes another. The length of this vector, the magnitude of the force, tells how strongly, and its direction tells in which direction. The magnitude of a force is measured in newtons (newton), N. A force acts on a definite part of a body: on a part of its surface or on all of its material.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (PDF p. 381), NORTON §1.10 (PDF pp. 44-45), SADD §3.1 (PDF pp. 63-64).

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system we write a force vector with three components, like every vector, only the components have the unit newton instead of metre. When a force acts on a very small part of a surface, like the tip of a pencil on paper, we often imagine this part as a point and say that the force acts at a point. A force distributed over a larger part of the surface or over all the material, like the weight, is the sum of many small forces, one on each bit of that part.

When several forces act at the same point of a body, they have the same effect as a single force, their vector sum (KREYSZIG §9.1, PDF p. 383, where the sum of two forces is drawn as the diagonal of a parallelogram). In figure 2.1 on the left two ropes pull a ring, and a single rope pulling it with the force \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2\) would have the same effect. For a force, though, not only the arrow matters but also where it acts. We open a door if we push it near the handle, but the same force near the hinges does not move it (figure 2.1, right).

How large is a newton? The Earth pulls every body down with a force called the weight (teža) of the body. Its magnitude is proportional to the mass (masa) of the body, that is, to how many kilograms of matter it contains:

\[W = m\,g , \qquad g = 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 . \tag{2.1}\]

The number \(g\) is the gravitational acceleration (težni pospešek) at the surface of the Earth. It tells by how many metres per second the speed of a body falling without air resistance grows every second (NORTON (1.2), PDF p. 45, where the same equation is written as \(m = W/g\)). A body with a mass of 1 kg weighs \(9{,}81\) N, an apple with a mass of 0,1 kg about 1 N, and a person with a mass of 70 kg \(686{,}7\) N. So a newton is a kilogram times a metre per second squared, \(1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}/\text{s}^2\) (NORTON §1.10, PDF pp. 44-45). A thousand newtons is a kilonewton, kN, which is the weight of about 102 kg.

Take the hanging steel bar from chapter 1, of length \(L = 10\) m. Let it be round, with diameter \(d = 10\) mm. The area (ploščina) of its cross section is, by the school formula for a circle,

\[A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi\,(10\ \text{mm})^2}{4} = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2 .\]

Its volume is the area of the cross section times the length, \(A L = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3\). We get the mass by multiplying the volume by the density (gostota) \(\rho\), the mass of one cubic metre of the material. For mild steel (navadno konstrukcijsko jeklo) \(\rho = 7{,}8\) Mg/m³ (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). A megagram, Mg, is 1000 kg (BOWER, PDF p. 100), so this is 7800 kg/m³. So the bar has the mass and, by (2.1), the weight

\[m = \rho A L = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3 = 6{,}126\ \text{kg} , \qquad W = m\,g = 60{,}10\ \text{N} .\]

Several forces usually act on a body at once. On the hanging bar the weight and the ceiling act, on a bar that we pull two ropes, one at each end. For the question whether a body stays at rest the sum of all the forces acting on it will matter, so it gets its own name.

Definition 2.2 Resultant (rezultanta)

The resultant (rezultanta) of the forces \(\vek F_1, \vek F_2, \dots, \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\) acting on a body is their vector sum \(\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (PDF p. 383), SADD §3.7 (PDF p. 77).

When does a body stay at rest under such forces? The answer comes from Newton's law of motion. For a body that moves as a whole, without turning and without changing its shape, it reads: the resultant of the forces on the body equals the mass of the body times its acceleration, that is, times how fast its velocity changes (EP (2.2.2), p. 50, where the law is written for a particle of matter). A body that is at rest and stays at rest does not accelerate, so the resultant of the forces on it is the zero vector. How the law reads for a body that turns or changes its shape we will see later in this chapter.

That is not yet enough. Push a ruler lying on a table along the axis \(x\) with the force \((0, 5, 0)\) N at one end and with the force \((0, -5, 0)\) N at the other. The resultant is \((0, 0, 0)\) N, and yet the ruler turns (figure 2.2, top). So for rest one more condition must hold: the forces must not turn the body (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77). The two conditions together have a name.

Definition 2.3 Equilibrium of a body (ravnovesje telesa)

A body is in equilibrium (ravnovesje or ravnotežje) when the resultant of the forces acting on it is the zero vector,

\[\vek F_1 + \vek F_2 + \dots + \vek F_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek 0 , \tag{2.2}\]

and the forces also do not tend to turn the body.

Source: SADD §3.7 (PDF p. 77), BOWER §2.3.1, §2.3.2 (PDF pp. 77-78).

So equilibrium is a state of a body that we check on the forces acting on it. A body at rest is in equilibrium (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77). But a body in equilibrium need not be at rest. A body that moves with constant velocity and does not turn also has no acceleration, so the resultant of the forces on it is the zero vector (EP (2.2.2), p. 50). Equation (2.2) is a vector equation, so it means three equations for numbers, one for each component (definition 1.11). The second part of the condition, that the forces do not tend to turn the body, we will write with numbers later. In this chapter all the forces on a bar will act along its axis, as on a ruler pulled at both ends along its length (figure 2.2, bottom), and such forces do not turn the bar.

Pull a bar lying along the axis \(x\) at its right end with a force of 10 kN in the direction of the axis \(x\), and at its left end with an equally large force in the opposite direction. The forces are \((10, 0, 0)\) kN and \((-10, 0, 0)\) kN, their resultant is \((0, 0, 0)\) kN, and both act along the axis of the bar. The bar is in equilibrium. For the hanging bar we point the axis \(x\) vertically down, as in figure 1.20. The weight points in the direction of the axis \(x\), so its vector is \(W\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) with \(W = 60{,}10\) N. The ceiling holds the bar with the force \(-W\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the resultant is again the zero vector.

Now we look inside the bar. What holds the left and the right part of the bar together? Cut the bar, pulled with the force \(F\) at both ends, in thought with a cross section at the distance \(x\) from the left end (figure 2.3). The cut is only imagined, the bar stays whole. But we ask which forces act on each of the two parts. On the left part acts the force at the left end, which pulls it to the left. If this were the only force, the left part would not stay at rest. Since it is at rest, the right part must pull it to the right across the face of the cut. We made this argument already for the piece of the bar in figure 1.6. Books use it everywhere: if the whole body is in equilibrium, every part of it is in equilibrium too, and across the cut the forces of the rest of the body act on each part (SADD §3.7, PDF p. 77, BOWER §2.2.2, PDF p. 67).

Let us compute these forces. Let the axis \(x\) lie along the axis of the bar, pointing from the left end to the right end. We call the force with which the right part acts on the left part across the cut \(\vek R\), and the force with which the left part acts on the right part \(\vek R'\). We neglect the weight of the bar. For a steel bar with diameter 10 mm and length 10 m it is 60 N, which is 0,6 % of the force 10 kN.

  1. On the left part act the force \(-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the left end and the force \(\vek R\) across the cut.
  2. The left part is in equilibrium, so by (2.2)
    \[-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R = \vek 0 .\]
  3. We add \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) to both sides. By the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.13) we may reorder the terms of a sum, and the sum \(-F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the zero vector. What remains is
    \[\vek R = F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]
  4. On the right part act the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the right end and the force \(\vek R'\) across the cut. From the equilibrium of the right part, \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R' = \vek 0\), we get in the same way \(\vek R' = -F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  5. So \(\vek R' = -\vek R\).

The right part pulls the left one to the right, the left part pulls the right one to the left, and both forces are equally large. That they are opposite is no accident. When one body acts on another with a force, the other acts on the first with an equal and opposite force. This is Newton's law of action and reaction (akcija in reakcija) (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). For the bar we obtained it from the equilibrium of the two parts.

We chose the distance \(x\) freely, so in every cross section one part of the bar pulls the other with the same force of magnitude \(F\). We do not see this force until we cut the body in thought. So it gets its own name.

Definition 2.4 Internal force (notranja sila)

The internal force (notranja sila) in a cut of a body is the resultant of the forces with which the part of the body on one side of the cut acts on the part on the other side.

Source: EP §2.2.1 (p. 50), SADD §3.1 (PDF p. 63), BOWER §2.2.2 (PDF p. 67).

The force at the end of the bar and the weight, by contrast, are external forces (zunanje sile), because they act on the body from outside, from the rope and from the Earth (EP §2.2.1, p. 50). The internal force is in general distributed over the whole face of the cut and does not necessarily pull in one direction only. Take a bar that is clamped into a wall at one end and pushed down at its free end (figure 2.4). The bar bends. In the cut the upper part of the material pulls the neighbouring part towards itself, the lower part pushes it, and in addition the part at the wall holds the rest of the bar up. So the forces across the cut also tend to turn the neighbouring part. When everything happens in one plane, books describe them by three quantities: a force along the axis of the bar, a force across it, and a number that measures how strongly the forces tend to turn the neighbouring part (EP (2.5.6), p. 74). We will define them precisely in chapter 5. In a bar pulled along its axis the internal force points along the axis, and one number is enough for it.

Definition 2.5 Axial force (osna sila)

Let the axis \(x\) lie along the axis of the bar. The axial force (osna sila) \(N\) in a cross section of the bar is the component \(x\) of the internal force with which the part of the bar on the side of larger \(x\) acts on the part on the side of smaller \(x\).

Source: EP (2.5.6) (p. 74), ONATE1 (PDF p. 38).

The axial force is a number. A positive axial force means that the part on the side of larger \(x\) pulls the other one towards itself, so the two parts pull each other. This is called tension (nateg). A negative axial force means that the parts push each other, as in the compression of chapter 1 (figure 2.5). The part on the side of smaller \(x\) acts on the other one with the opposite force, so it does not matter which part we look at. In the bar pulled with the force \(F\) at both ends, by step 3 above, \(N = F\) in every cross section. For \(F = 10\) kN, \(N = 10\) kN.

In the hanging bar the axial force is not the same everywhere. We point the axis \(x\) vertically down, with the origin at the upper end, as in figure 1.20. Cut the bar in thought at the distance \(x\) from the ceiling and look at the lower part, from the cut to the lower end at \(L\) (figure 2.6).

  1. On the lower part act its weight and the force \(\vek R\) with which the upper part pulls it across the cut. The lower end is free, so there are no other forces.
  2. The lower part has the length \(L - x\), its volume is \(A\,(L - x)\), its mass \(\rho A\,(L - x)\) and its weight, by (2.1), \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\). The weight points in the direction of the axis \(x\), so its vector is \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The weight acts on every bit of the lower part separately, but for equilibrium only the sum of all these forces counts, the weight of the whole part.
  3. The lower part is in equilibrium, so \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R = \vek 0\) and \(\vek R = -\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The upper part pulls the lower one up.
  4. By the law of action and reaction the lower part pulls the upper one with the opposite force \(\rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), downwards. The lower part is on the side of larger \(x\), so the component \(x\) of this force is the axial force:
    \[N(x) = \rho g A\,(L - x) . \tag{2.3}\]

So the axial force in the hanging bar is the weight of the part of the bar below the cut. It is positive, which means tension, as we expect for a bar that hangs. For our bar it is \(N(0) = 60{,}10\) N at the top, the weight of the whole bar, \(N(5\ \text{m}) = 30{,}05\) N at the middle, and zero at the lower end. Exactly this argument explained in section 1.2 why the material at the middle of the bar is stretched half as much as at the top.

We get the same if we look at the upper part, from the ceiling to the cut. On it act the ceiling, its weight \(\rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the force of the lower part, which we call \(\vek Q\). The ceiling holds the whole bar, so by the equilibrium of the whole bar it acts with the force \(-\rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The equilibrium of the upper part, \(-\rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek Q = \vek 0\), gives

\[\vek Q = \rho g A L\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - \rho g A x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho g A\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

the same as in step 4, this time without the law of action and reaction.

The axial force in our hanging bar falls from 60 N at the top to zero at the bottom, and in the bar that we pull it is 10 kN everywhere. Is 10 kN a lot for this bar? That depends on how much material carries the force, and this is the subject of the next section.

2.2 Stress in a bar

The axial force tells with what force one part of the bar pulls the other. But it does not yet tell how heavily the material is loaded. Take two equal thin steel bars with a cross section of area \(A\) and put them side by side. At both ends we fix them to a rigid plate and pull the plates apart with the force \(F\) (figure 2.7, left). How much does each bar carry?

  1. We call the axial forces of the bars \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). On the right plate act the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the forces of the bars, which pull it back, \(-N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The plate is in equilibrium, so \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\). The component \(x\) of this equation is \(F - N_1 - N_2 = 0\), so \(N_1 + N_2 = F\).
  3. The bars are equal and equally fixed, and the force pulls the plate in the middle between them. Nothing distinguishes one bar from the other, so they carry the same, \(N_1 = N_2\).
  4. From step 2, therefore, \(2N_1 = F\) and \(N_1 = N_2 = F/2\).

Now glue the bars together lengthwise, so that we get one bar with a cross section of area \(2A\) (figure 2.7, right). When we pull it with the force \(F\), both halves stretch as before, so the glue between them has nothing to carry, and each half still carries \(F/2\). The thick bar carries twice the force of one thin bar, but it also has twice the cross section. The material of both is loaded equally, since in both the same force falls on a unit of cross-section area, \((F/2)/A = F/(2A)\). We could also cut the thick bar into ten or a hundred thin lengthwise bars, and each would carry a share of the force proportional to its area. So for the material what counts is the force per unit area of the cross section.

Definition 2.6 Stress in a bar (napetost v palici)

Let \(N\) be the axial force in a cross section of a bar and \(A\) the area of this cross section before loading. The stress (napetost) in the bar at this cross section is the number

\[\sigma = \frac{N}{A} . \tag{2.4}\]

Source: CM §4.1 (PDF p. 180), ONATE1 (1.3) (PDF p. 38), where it is written as \(N = A\sigma\).

Stress is force per unit area (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180). The Greek letter \(\sigma\) (sigma) is its usual symbol. Stress has the sign of the axial force: positive means tension, negative compression.

The definition has two restrictions. First: the stress \(N/A\) is an average over the cross section. It tells how much force falls on a unit of area if the force is distributed evenly over the cross section. Near the ends of the bar, where grips or a rope hold it, this is not true. But books state Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo): two differently applied loadings with the same resultant and the same tendency to turn the body distribute the force almost equally far from the places where they act (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). Figure 2.8 shows this schematically on a bar pulled by pins at the middle of both ends. How far is far, the principle does not say. SADD itself points out that far and almost equally are only descriptive words without an exact bound. Far from the ends of the bar the force is therefore distributed over the cross section as it would be if we pulled evenly over the whole cross section at the ends, that is, evenly, and \(\sigma\) is the force per unit area at every point of the cross section. How to describe the force per unit area at a point when it is not even, we will see in section 2.5.

Second: for \(A\) we take the area of the cross section before loading. When a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about 0,3 thousandths (section 1.6), and the area of the cross section by 0,06 %. For steel the difference is negligible, for rubber it is not. Rubber strongly resists a change of volume (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121). When we stretch it by half its length, its volume, the product of the cross-section area and the length, stays almost the same, so the area decreases to \(A/1{,}5 = 0{,}667\,A\), by a third. Books therefore distinguish the force per unit area before loading and after it (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180). BOWER calls the force per unit area after loading the true stress (prava napetost), and the force per unit area before loading the nominal stress (nominalna napetost) (BOWER §2.2.4, PDF pp. 71-72). So our stress \(N/A\) is nominal.

Stress has the unit of force divided by area, newton per square metre, N/m². This unit is called the pascal (paskal), Pa (NORTON, unit conversion table, PDF p. 47). The pascal is a small unit. A force of 1 N, about the weight of an apple, spread over a square metre gives a stress of 1 Pa. Stresses in steel are millions of times larger, so we use the megapascal, \(1\ \text{MPa} = 10^6\ \text{Pa}\). Since \(1\ \text{mm} = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\), \(1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\), and

\[1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} = \frac{1\ \text{N}}{10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 10^6\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = 1\ \text{MPa} .\]

So a stress in megapascals is the number of newtons per square millimetre. BOWER writes it in MN/m², meganewtons per square metre, which by the same computation is again MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). For still larger numbers we use the gigapascal, \(1\ \text{GPa} = 10^9\ \text{Pa}\).

We pull the steel bar with diameter 10 mm with the force 10 kN. The area of the cross section is \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) (section 2.1), the axial force \(N = 10\,000\) N, and the stress is

\[\sigma = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2} = 127{,}3\ \text{MPa} .\]

What does this number mean? We must compare it with what steel can bear. In section 1.1 we saw that after too large a stretch steel no longer returns to its original length. This happens when the stress exceeds a certain value, which is a property of the material.

Definition 2.7 Yield stress (meja tečenja)

The yield stress (meja tečenja) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) of a material is the stress in a bar of this material above which the bar remains permanently longer after unloading.

Source: BOWER §3.7.1 (PDF p. 142), Table 3.9 (PDF p. 156).

The yield stress is a number, a property of the material and not of the bar. Below it the bar returns to its original length after unloading, above it the bar stays permanently longer (figure 2.9). The name says that above it the material begins to yield, that is, to flow: it changes its shape permanently, somewhat like a thick liquid. BOWER warns that the yield stress is often hard to pin down exactly, because the relation between stress and strain bends only gradually, and that the values in its table are only rough (BOWER, PDF pp. 142 and 156). For mild steel BOWER gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). We defined the yield stress with a bar that we pull. When a material yields under loading in several directions at once, we will treat later (BOWER §3.7, PDF pp. 142-156).

The stress of 127,3 MPa in our bar is 58 % of the yield stress. The bar holds and returns to its original length after unloading. The largest force that does not yet make this bar permanently longer is

\[F = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} A = 220\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 17\,280\ \text{N} ,\]

about the weight of 1,76 t. A bar with half the diameter, 5 mm, has a quarter of the area, \(19{,}63\ \text{mm}^2\), and under the same force of 10 kN the stress \(509{,}3\) MPa, more than twice the yield stress. This is the thin wire from the beginning of the chapter: the same force, but four times less material carrying it.

For the hanging bar the axial force is (2.3), and the stress is

\[\sigma(x) = \frac{N(x)}{A} = \frac{\rho g A\,(L - x)}{A} = \rho g\,(L - x) . \tag{2.5}\]

The area has cancelled. The stress in a hanging bar does not depend on its thickness (BARBER (1.33), PDF p. 32). Why? A bar with twice the cross-sectional area is twice as heavy, so each of its cross sections carries twice the weight, but it also has twice the area to carry that weight (figure 2.10). This is the same argument as for the two thin bars that together make a thick one.

At the top of a bar of length 10 m

\[\sigma(0) = \rho g L = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9{,}81\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 765\,180\ \text{Pa} = 0{,}765\ \text{MPa} ,\]

which is 288 times less than the yield stress. The units agree because \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 1\ \text{kg}/(\text{m}\,\text{s}^2)\). How long would a steel bar have to be so that under its own weight it just starts to stretch permanently at the top? The stress at the top, \(\rho g L\), must reach \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\), so

\[L = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}}{\rho g} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2} = 2875\ \text{m} ,\]

almost three kilometres, whatever the thickness.

In section 1.1 we wrote that steel stretched by more than about a thousandth of its length no longer returns to its original length. This limit and the yield stress are connected. For the same steel BOWER also gives the number 196 GPa, which tells how strongly steel resists stretching (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). The quotient

\[\frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 0{,}00112\]

is about this thousandth. The same number 196 GPa also connects the stress in the hanging bar with its strain. At the top the stress is \(0{,}765\) MPa, and

\[\frac{0{,}76518\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{0{,}76518 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} ,\]

which is exactly the strain at the top of the hanging bar from section 1.2. It agrees elsewhere too: at the middle the stress is half as large, and so is the strain. Why the quotient of the stress and the number 196 GPa gives the strain, we will see in chapter 3, when we connect stress with strain. There we will also compute from the stress (2.5) the displacement \(u(x)\) of the hanging bar, which in chapter 1 we took as given.

2.3 Mathematics: the scalar product

So far we have cut the bar only perpendicular to its axis. What if we cut it obliquely? The force with which one part of the bar pulls the other still points along the axis, but the face of the cut is tilted. Part of this force pulls the two parts of the bar apart, perpendicular to the face of the cut. The other part tries to shift one part along the other, along the face, as the cards slide in the deck of figure 1.4. We will see that it is exactly this second part that decides when steel yields. So we want to know how large each of them is. The mathematical question is this: how do we split a vector into a part that points in a given direction and a part perpendicular to that direction?

In this section all vectors are written with components in the same chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system.

For the direction of the axis \(x\) the answer is simple. The vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the sum \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) (figure 2.11). The first part points in the direction of the axis \(x\). The second part has the component \(x\) equal to zero, so it lies in the plane of the axes \(y\) and \(z\), which is perpendicular to the axis \(x\). So the part in the direction of the axis is the component times the unit vector of the axis. But for a direction that is not the direction of one of the axes, we need a way to check with components whether two arrows are perpendicular.

The Pythagorean theorem helps. Draw the vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) from the same point \(O\), to the points \(A\) and \(B\) (figure 2.12). The third side of the triangle \(OAB\) is the vector from \(A\) to \(B\), that is, \(\vek b - \vek a\). From school we know the Pythagorean theorem and its converse: the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when the square of the length of the side \(AB\) equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the sides \(OA\) and \(OB\). By how much do these two numbers differ? Let us compute with components. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek b - \vek a\) is \(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and its square is

\[(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

The same holds for \(y\) and \(z\). When we add all three squares, we get by the formula for the length (1.10)

\[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

The square of the length of the side \(AB\) differs from the sum of the squares of the other two sides by \(-2\) times the number \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when this number is zero. The number is so important that it gets a name.

Definition 2.8 Scalar product (skalarni produkt)

The scalar product (skalarni produkt) of the vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\), written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, is the number

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{2.6}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (2) (PDF p. 387). CM (2.2.6) (PDF p. 41) defines it with the two lengths and the angle.

The dot between the vectors is not a multiplication of numbers. On both sides of the dot stands a vector, and the result is a number. A number that is not a vector is called a scalar (skalar) in physics, hence the name (KREYSZIG §9.2, PDF p. 387). Books also call it the inner product (notranji produkt) or the dot product (pikčasti produkt) (KREYSZIG §9.2, PDF p. 387, CM §2.2.2.1, PDF p. 41).

Let us try it. The flight of the fly from chapter 1 was \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\). For \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\)

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-1) + (-2) \cdot 0 = 0 ,\]

so the angle between them is a right angle. We check with the Pythagorean theorem: \(\vek b - \vek a = (1, -3, 2)\), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = 1 + 9 + 4 = 14\) and \(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 = 9 + 5 = 14\). But for the vector \(\vek c = (2, 0, 0)\), by which the fly crawled along the floor after landing, \(\vek a\cdot\vek c = 2\), and \(|\vek c - \vek a|^2 = |(1, -2, 2)|^2 = 9\) is \(2 \cdot 2 = 4\) less than \(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek c|^2 = 13\). The angle between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek c\) is not a right angle.

The formula (2.6) holds only for components in a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. We derived it from the Pythagorean theorem and from the formula for the length, which holds only there (section 1.5.4). KREYSZIG goes the other way. It defines the scalar product with the lengths of the vectors and the angle between them, \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\cos\gamma\), and derives the formula with components from the axes being perpendicular (KREYSZIG §9.2 (1), (2), PDF pp. 387-389).

First let us write the computation with the triangle as a statement, and add a similar formula for the sum, which we will need.

Lemma 2.9 Square of the length of a difference and of a sum (kvadrat dolžine razlike in vsote)

For any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\)

\[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b , \qquad |\vek a + \vek b|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 + 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b . \tag{2.7}\]
Proof

We write both sides with components and compare them.

  1. The difference of vectors \(\vek b - \vek a\) has the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} - a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} - a_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.12).
  2. By the school formula for the square of a difference, \((b_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 - 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. By (1.10), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2\) is the sum of these three squares. We arrange the terms in three groups:
    \[|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = (a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2) + (b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{z}}^2) - 2\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]
  4. By (1.10) the first group is \(|\vek a|^2\) and the second \(|\vek b|^2\), and by (2.6) the bracket in the third is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b\). This proves the first formula.
  5. For the sum we repeat steps 1 to 4 with the components \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{z}}\) and the school formula \((a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}})^2 = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + 2\,a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The only difference is the sign in front of \(2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. Adding the two equalities gives the parallelogram equality in KREYSZIG §9.2 (8) (PDF p. 389).

The lemma also answers a question that soon comes up about the scalar product. The components of a vector depend on how we place the axes (section 1.5.5). Does the scalar product therefore depend on it too? It does not. From the first formula of the lemma we express it:

\[\vek a\cdot\vek b = \tfrac12\left(|\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - |\vek b - \vek a|^2\right) .\]

On the right stand only the lengths of the three sides of the triangle \(OAB\) in figure 2.12. But the distance between two points is one and the same, and the formula for the length (1.10) gives it in every rectangular Cartesian coordinate system with the same unit of length. So the formula (2.6) also gives the same number in every such system. Let us try it on the fly. In axes turned by a quarter turn about the axis \(z\) (the new axis \(x\) points in the direction of the old axis \(y\), the new axis \(y\) in the direction opposite to the old axis \(x\)), its flight has the components \((2, -1, -2)\), and the vector \(\vek b = (2, -1, 0)\) the components \((-1, -2, 0)\). The scalar product is \(2 \cdot (-1) + (-1) \cdot (-2) + (-2) \cdot 0 = 0\), as in the old axes. For mechanics this matters. The stresses we will compute with the scalar product will not depend on how we placed the axes, only on the loading of the body and on the surface on which we compute them.

Now we can say exactly what the scalar product means.

Theorem 2.10 Scalar product and the right angle (skalarni produkt in pravi kot)

Nonzero vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) drawn from the same point form a right angle exactly when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Proof

We must show that a right angle gives \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\) and that \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\) gives a right angle. We get both directions at once from the Pythagorean theorem and its converse.

  1. We draw the arrows from the point \(O\) to the points \(A = O + \vek a\) and \(B = O + \vek b\). Since the vectors are not zero, \(A \neq O\) and \(B \neq O\).
  2. First the case when the points \(O\), \(A\) and \(B\) lie on one line. Then \(\vek b = k\,\vek a\) for some number \(k \neq 0\), the arrows point in the same or in opposite directions, and they do not form a right angle. On the other hand, by (2.6) \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2) = k\,|\vek a|^2\), which is not zero, because \(k \neq 0\) and \(|\vek a| > 0\). Both statements are false, so they are equivalent.
  3. Otherwise \(OAB\) is a triangle. The sides at the vertex \(O\) have the lengths \(|\vek a|\) and \(|\vek b|\), and the third side the length \(|\vek b - \vek a|\), because by (1.8) \(\vek b - \vek a\) is the vector from \(A\) to \(B\).
  4. By the Pythagorean theorem and its converse from school, the angle at \(O\) is a right angle exactly when \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2\).
  5. By the formula for the square of the length of a difference (lemma 2.9), \(|\vek b - \vek a|^2 = |\vek a|^2 + |\vek b|^2 - 2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b\). So the condition of step 4 is \(-2\,\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\), which holds exactly when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2, Theorem 1 (PDF p. 388), CM §2.2.2.1, item 2 (PDF p. 42). The route through the Pythagorean theorem is not from the sources.

The proof showed that the scalar product measures by how much a triangle made of two arrows differs from a right triangle. The theorem gives a computational test for a right angle that needs neither a picture nor a protractor. So we take it as the definition.

Definition 2.11 Perpendicular vectors (pravokotna vektorja)

The vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\) are perpendicular (pravokotna) or orthogonal (ortogonalna) when \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = 0\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (PDF p. 388), CM §2.2.2.1 (PDF p. 42).

For nonzero vectors the definition, by theorem 2.10, means exactly what it meant at school: the arrows form a right angle. But it also covers the zero vector, which is perpendicular to every vector, since \(\vek 0\cdot\vek b = 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{y}} + 0 \cdot b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). We cannot give the zero vector an angle, because it has no direction. But when we split vectors, the part perpendicular to a given direction will sometimes be the zero vector, and even then we will want to say that it is perpendicular. The unit vectors of the axes are pairwise perpendicular, for example \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 0\), which agrees with the axes being perpendicular to each other.

To compute with long expressions, we also need rules that say how the scalar product works together with addition and multiplication by a number.

Lemma 2.12 Properties of the scalar product (lastnosti skalarnega produkta)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) written in a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and all numbers \(k\):

  1. \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = \vek b\cdot\vek a\),
  2. \(\vek a\cdot(\vek b + \vek c) = \vek a\cdot\vek b + \vek a\cdot\vek c\),
  3. \((k\,\vek a)\cdot\vek b = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b) = \vek a\cdot(k\,\vek b)\),
  4. \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = |\vek a|^2\), so \(\vek a\cdot\vek a \ge 0\), and \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = 0\) only for \(\vek a = \vek 0\).
Proof

We write each rule with components by (2.6) and reduce it to rules for numbers.

1. Symmetry.

  1. The left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}\), the right side \(b_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{y}} a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{z}} a_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. In multiplying numbers the order does not matter, \(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} = b_{\mathrm{x}} a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). The sums agree term by term.

2. Splitting a sum.

  1. The sum of vectors \(\vek b + \vek c\) has the components \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}}\) (definition 1.12), so the left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}}(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}) + a_{\mathrm{y}}(b_{\mathrm{y}} + c_{\mathrm{y}}) + a_{\mathrm{z}}(b_{\mathrm{z}} + c_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  2. By the rule for multiplying a sum by a number, \(a_{\mathrm{x}}(b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}}) = a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the same for \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. We rearrange the six terms so that the products with \(\vek b\) are together and the products with \(\vek c\) are together: \((a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) + (a_{\mathrm{x}} c_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} c_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} c_{\mathrm{z}})\). This is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b + \vek a\cdot\vek c\).

3. Taking out a number.

  1. The vector \(k\,\vek a\) has the components \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(k\,a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(k\,a_{\mathrm{z}}\), so \((k\,\vek a)\cdot\vek b = (k\,a_{\mathrm{x}})\,b_{\mathrm{x}} + (k\,a_{\mathrm{y}})\,b_{\mathrm{y}} + (k\,a_{\mathrm{z}})\,b_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  2. In multiplying three numbers it does not matter which two we multiply first, so the terms equal \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  3. We take out the common factor \(k\) and get \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}} b_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}} b_{\mathrm{z}}) = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\).
  4. In the same way \(\vek a\cdot(k\,\vek b) = a_{\mathrm{x}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{x}}) + a_{\mathrm{y}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{y}}) + a_{\mathrm{z}}\,(k\,b_{\mathrm{z}}) = k\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\).

4. The product of a vector with itself.

  1. By (2.6) \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2\), and by (1.10) this is \(|\vek a|^2\).
  2. Every square of a real number is nonnegative, so their sum is nonnegative too.
  3. A sum of three nonnegative numbers is zero only if each of them is zero. So \(\vek a\cdot\vek a = 0\) gives \(a_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{y}} = a_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), that is, \(\vek a\) is the zero vector \(\vek 0\) (definition 1.11).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (3), (5), (5a*) (PDF pp. 388-389), CM (2.2.7), (2.2.8) (PDF p. 42). The proofs are not from the sources.

The proof again showed the principle from chapter 1: we check a statement about vectors on the components, where it becomes a rule for numbers. With properties 1 to 3 we may compute with the scalar product as with the multiplication of numbers: expand brackets, swap factors and take out numbers. Property 4 says that the scalar product contains the length, \(|\vek a| = \sqrt{\vek a\cdot\vek a}\) (KREYSZIG §9.2 (3), PDF p. 388).

Exactly these properties are what mathematics takes as the definition of a general scalar product. Take a vector space in which elements are multiplied by real numbers (section 1.5.3), and a rule whose input is two elements of the space and whose output is one number. The rule is called a scalar product when it splits sums and multiples as our product does (properties 2 and 3), when it is symmetric (property 1), and when the product of every element with itself is positive, except for the zero element, where it is zero (the second part of property 4). A space with such a rule is an inner product space (prostor s skalarnim produktom) (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF pp. 337-338). Such a space gets the length of an element from the scalar product, as property 4 gives it for us. We will need only the scalar product (2.6).

Now we can answer the question from the beginning of the section. Let the unit vector \(\vek e\) give a direction. We want to write the vector \(\vek a\) as \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\), where \(p\,\vek e\) is the part in the direction \(\vek e\) and the vector \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\). How large must \(p\) be? The remainder is \(\vek r = \vek a - p\,\vek e\), and the condition that it is perpendicular to \(\vek e\) is \(\vek e\cdot(\vek a - p\,\vek e) = 0\). By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product the left side equals \(\vek e\cdot\vek a - p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e)\), and by item 4 \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = |\vek e|^2 = 1\). So the condition is \(\vek e\cdot\vek a - p = 0\), and by item 1 \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\). So the number \(p\) is the scalar product of the vector with the unit vector of the direction.

Definition 2.13 Projection of a vector onto a direction (projekcija vektorja na smer)

Let \(\vek e\) be a unit vector. The projection (projekcija) of the vector \(\vek a\) onto the direction \(\vek e\) is the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek e\). It is also called the component in the direction of \(\vek e\) (komponenta v smeri \(\vek e\)). The vector \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is the part of the vector \(\vek a\) in the direction \(\vek e\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (12) (PDF p. 391), CM §2.2.2.1, item 4 (PDF p. 42).

The projection is a number that can also be negative. Then the part in the direction \(\vek e\) points opposite to \(\vek e\). For the direction of the axis \(x\) the projection is just the component, \(\vek a\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 1 + a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which justifies the name component in the direction. For a direction given by a vector \(\vek b\) that is not a unit vector, we take the unit vector in the direction of the vector \(\vek b\), and the projection is \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/|\vek b|\) (KREYSZIG §9.2 (11), PDF p. 391). The next lemma says that the split we were looking for really exists, that there is only one, and how lengths behave in it.

Lemma 2.14 Splitting a vector into a part along a direction and a perpendicular part (razstavitev vektorja na del v smeri in pravokotni del)

Let \(\vek e\) be a unit vector and \(\vek a\) any vector. Then:

  1. The vector \(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\), and
    \[\vek a = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e + \big(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\big) . \tag{2.8}\]
  2. If \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\) for some number \(p\) and a vector \(\vek r\) perpendicular to \(\vek e\), then \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\).
  3. \(|\vek a|^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 + |\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e|^2\).
Proof

We compute all three statements with the properties of the scalar product. The key is that \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = |\vek e|^2 = 1\) by item 4 of the properties, because \(\vek e\) is a unit vector.

1. The perpendicular part.

  1. We write \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\) and \(\vek r = \vek a - p\,\vek e\).
  2. By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek e\cdot\vek r = \vek e\cdot\vek a - p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) = \vek e\cdot\vek a - p\).
  3. By item 1 \(\vek e\cdot\vek a = \vek a\cdot\vek e = p\), so \(\vek e\cdot\vek r = 0\), and \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\).
  4. The equality (2.8) holds by the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.13): \(p\,\vek e + (\vek a - p\,\vek e) = \vek a + (p\,\vek e - p\,\vek e) = \vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\).

2. Uniqueness.

  1. We take the scalar product of both sides of the equality \(\vek a = p\,\vek e + \vek r\) with \(\vek e\). By items 1 to 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) + \vek r\cdot\vek e\).
  2. Here \(\vek e\cdot\vek e = 1\), and \(\vek r\cdot\vek e = 0\), because \(\vek r\) is perpendicular to \(\vek e\). What remains is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = p\).

3. The Pythagorean theorem for the split.

  1. Let \(\vek u = p\,\vek e\) with \(p = \vek a\cdot\vek e\), and \(\vek r = \vek a - \vek u\). By item 1 \(\vek a = \vek u + \vek r\).
  2. By item 3 of the properties and item 1 of this lemma \(\vek u\cdot\vek r = p\,(\vek e\cdot\vek r) = 0\).
  3. By the formula for the square of the length of a sum (lemma 2.9) \(|\vek a|^2 = |\vek u|^2 + |\vek r|^2 + 2\,\vek u\cdot\vek r = |\vek u|^2 + |\vek r|^2\).
  4. By items 3 and 4 of the properties \(|\vek u|^2 = (p\,\vek e)\cdot(p\,\vek e) = p^2\,(\vek e\cdot\vek e) = p^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (10)-(12), Fig. 181 (PDF p. 391), CM §2.2.2.1, item 4 (PDF p. 42), CM (4.2.16) (PDF p. 184). The proof is not from the sources.

The lemma says three things. Every vector can be split into a part in the direction \(\vek e\) and a part perpendicular to it. There is only one such split, so the projection is the only number that makes it possible. And the lengths of the two parts add up by the Pythagorean theorem, like the legs of a right triangle with the hypotenuse \(\vek a\). We will use exactly this triangle for the force on an oblique cut.

Let us try it with numbers. Let \(\vek a = (5, 10, 5)\) and \(\vek e = (0, \tfrac35, \tfrac45)\). The vector \(\vek e\) is a unit vector, since \(|\vek e|^2 = 0 + \tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 1\). The projection is \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = 0 + 6 + 4 = 10\), the part in the direction \(\vek e\) is \(10\,\vek e = (0, 6, 8)\), and the perpendicular part is \((5, 10, 5) - (0, 6, 8) = (5, 4, -3)\). Indeed \((5, 4, -3)\cdot\vek e = 0 + \tfrac{12}{5} - \tfrac{12}{5} = 0\). Lengths: \(|\vek a|^2 = 25 + 100 + 25 = 150\), and \(10^2 + (25 + 16 + 9) = 100 + 50 = 150\), as item 3 requires.

What does the projection have to do with the angles from school? In figure 2.13 the arrows \(\vek a\), \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) and \(\vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) form a right triangle. The hypotenuse has the length \(|\vek a|\), and the right angle is between the last two, which are perpendicular by lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector. Let the angle \(\gamma\) between \(\vek a\) and \(\vek e\) be acute, so that \(\vek a\cdot\vek e > 0\). Then the leg at the angle \(\gamma\) has the length \(\vek a\cdot\vek e\), since the square of the length of the vector \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\) is \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\), as we computed in step 4 of the proof of item 3. The cosine of an acute angle in a right triangle is, from school, the ratio of the adjacent leg to the hypotenuse, so

\[\vek a\cdot\vek e = |\vek a|\cos\gamma .\]

KREYSZIG introduces the projection with this formula (§9.2 (10), PDF p. 391). In our example \(\cos\gamma = 10/\sqrt{150} = 0{,}816\) and \(\gamma = 35{,}3^\circ\).

Item 3 of the lemma gives one more thing. The number \(|\vek a|^2\) is the sum of \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\) and the square of a length, which is not negative, so the projection never exceeds the length of the vector in absolute value. For any two vectors this inequality reads as follows.

Corollary 2.15 Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (Cauchy-Schwarzeva neenakost)

For any vectors \(\vek a\) and \(\vek b\), \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b| \le |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).

Proof

We reduce the inequality to the projection onto the direction of the vector \(\vek b\).

  1. If \(\vek b = \vek 0\), then \(\vek a\cdot\vek b = a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{y}} \cdot 0 + a_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = 0\) and \(|\vek b| = 0\). Both sides are zero, and the inequality holds.
  2. Let \(\vek b \neq \vek 0\). Then \(\vek e = \vek b/|\vek b|\) is the unit vector in the direction of a vector, here of \(\vek b\) (lemma 1.16).
  3. By item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, \(|\vek a|^2 = (\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 + |\vek r|^2\) with \(\vek r = \vek a - (\vek a\cdot\vek e)\,\vek e\). The square \(|\vek r|^2\) is not negative, so \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2 \le |\vek a|^2\).
  4. The numbers \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e|\) and \(|\vek a|\) are nonnegative. Of two nonnegative numbers the larger one has the larger square. So if \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e|\) were larger than \(|\vek a|\), then \((\vek a\cdot\vek e)^2\) would be larger than \(|\vek a|^2\), which contradicts step 3. So \(|\vek a\cdot\vek e| \le |\vek a|\).
  5. By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product \(\vek a\cdot\vek e = \vek a\cdot\big(\tfrac{1}{|\vek b|}\,\vek b\big) = \tfrac{1}{|\vek b|}\,(\vek a\cdot\vek b)\), so \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b|/|\vek b| \le |\vek a|\).
  6. We multiply the inequality by the positive number \(|\vek b|\) and get \(|\vek a\cdot\vek b| \le |\vek a|\,|\vek b|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.2 (6) (PDF p. 389). The proof is not from the sources.

For two nonzero vectors the number \(\vek a\cdot\vek b/(|\vek a|\,|\vek b|)\) therefore always lies between \(-1\) and \(1\), and from school we know that exactly one angle \(\gamma\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) has this cosine. KREYSZIG takes it as the angle between the vectors (KREYSZIG §9.2 (4), PDF p. 388):

\[\cos\gamma = \frac{\vek a\cdot\vek b}{|\vek a|\,|\vek b|} .\]

We will not need the angle between arbitrary vectors. For the oblique cut we will know the angle from school trigonometry already, and the scalar product will split the force.

2.4 Stress on an oblique cut

Let us return to the bar of section 2.2. Let it lie along the axis \(x\) and have a rectangular cross section of width \(b\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) and height \(h\) in the direction of the axis \(z\), with the area \(A = b h\). We pull it with the force \(N\) at both ends, so that the axial force in every cross section is \(N\) and the stress \(\sigma = N/A\). We put the origin on the axis of the bar, far from both ends.

Now cut the bar obliquely. Let the plane face of the cut pass through the origin, contain the direction of the axis \(z\), and be tilted from the cross section by the angle \(\theta\) (theta) about the axis \(z\), as figure 2.16 shows, and figure 2.14 in space. At \(\theta = 0\) this is the cross section. The cross section contains the directions \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The tilted face contains the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and instead of the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) the direction

\[\vek d = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0) ,\]

which is tilted from \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) by the angle \(\theta\). The components come from school trigonometry. A unit vector in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\) that deviates from the direction of the axis \(y\) by the angle \(\theta\) towards the negative direction of the axis \(x\) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with length 1. The leg at the angle \(\theta\) lies along the axis \(y\) and has the length \(\cos\theta\), so the component \(y\) is \(\cos\theta\). The opposite leg has the length \(\sin\theta\) and points in the negative direction of the axis \(x\), so the component \(x\) is \(-\sin\theta\). The vector \(\vek d\) is a unit vector because \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\), which is the Pythagorean theorem for a right triangle with hypotenuse 1. So the points of the face of the cut are the points \(O + s\,\vek d + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), where \(s\) and \(w\) are numbers for which the point still lies in the bar.

The direction in which a face looks is described by a vector perpendicular to it.

Definition 2.16 Unit normal (enotska normala)

The unit normal (enotska normala) of a plane face is a unit vector that is perpendicular to every vector between two points of the face. When a plane face arises from a cut of a body, for the part of the body we are looking at we choose the unit normal that points out of this part, and call it the outward normal (zunanja normala).

Source: CM §4.2.1 (PDF p. 180), SADD §3.2 (PDF p. 65).

If \(\vek n\) is a unit normal, so is \(-\vek n\), since \((-\vek n)\cdot\vek v = -(\vek n\cdot\vek v) = 0\) for every vector \(\vek v\) between points of the face. One points to one side of the face, the other to the other side. So the part of the body on one side of the cut has the outward normal \(\vek n\), and the part on the other side \(-\vek n\). A curved surface, for example the surface of a pipe, has no single normal. There every point has its own, perpendicular to the surface close to the point, and we will define it when we need it.

Let us show that

\[\vek n = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta,\ 0)\]

is the outward normal of the oblique cut for the left part of the bar.

  1. Length: \(|\vek n|^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta + 0 = 1\), so \(\vek n\) is a unit vector.
  2. The vector between two points of the cut, \(O + s_1\vek d + w_1\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(O + s_2\vek d + w_2\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), is by (1.8) and the rules of vector arithmetic equal to \((s_2 - s_1)\,\vek d + (w_2 - w_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. By (2.6) \(\vek n\cdot\vek d = -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta + 0 = 0\) and \(\vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = 0 + 0 + 0 \cdot 1 = 0\).
  4. By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product, therefore,
    \[\vek n\cdot\big((s_2 - s_1)\,\vek d + (w_2 - w_1)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\big) = (s_2 - s_1)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek d) + (w_2 - w_1)\,(\vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0 .\]
    The vector \(\vek n\) is perpendicular to every vector between two points of the cut.
  5. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek n\) is \(\cos\theta > 0\) for \(0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ\), so \(\vek n\) points towards larger \(x\), out of the left part of the bar. For the right part the outward normal is \(-\vek n\).

At \(\theta = 0\), \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\): the normal of the cross section points along the axis of the bar. The normal is tilted from the axis \(x\) by the same angle \(\theta\) as the cut from the cross section, since \(\vek n\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\).

The next question is the area of the oblique cut. We assume that the bar is long enough for the cut not to reach its ends.

  1. The point \(O + s\,\vek d + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) has the coordinates \(y = s\cos\theta\) and \(z = w\).
  2. The bar reaches from \(-b/2\) to \(b/2\) in the direction of the axis \(y\) and from \(-h/2\) to \(h/2\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). A point of the cut is in the bar when \(-b/2 \le s\cos\theta \le b/2\) and \(-h/2 \le w \le h/2\). Since \(\cos\theta > 0\), the first condition is the same as \(-b/(2\cos\theta) \le s \le b/(2\cos\theta)\).
  3. So the cut is a rectangle with sides in the directions \(\vek d\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (figure 2.14), which are unit vectors and perpendicular, \(\vek d\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The side in the direction \(\vek d\) is \(b/\cos\theta\) long, and the side in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is \(h\) long.
  4. The area of a rectangle is the product of its sides:
    \[A_\theta = \frac{b}{\cos\theta}\,h = \frac{A}{\cos\theta} . \tag{2.9}\]

At \(\theta = 60^\circ\), \(\cos\theta = 1/2\), and the oblique cut has twice the area of the cross section. The same ratio holds for every shape of cross section, not only for a rectangle (figure 2.15). Cut the cross section into narrow strips in the direction of the axis \(y\). On the oblique cut each strip gets longer by the factor \(1/\cos\theta\), and its width in the direction of the axis \(z\) stays the same. So the area of each strip, and with it the area of the whole cut, grows by the same factor.

What force acts across the oblique cut? On the left part act the force \(-N\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at the left end and the force \(\vek R\) with which the right part acts on the left one across the oblique cut. The equilibrium of the left part, \(-N\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + \vek R = \vek 0\), gives, as for the cross section,

\[\vek R = N\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

The cut is oblique, but the force across it is the same as across the cross section, since the right part must hold the left one against the same force at the left end. As for the cross section, we assume that this force is distributed evenly over the face of the cut. So the force per unit area of the cut is the vector

\[\vek t = \frac{1}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \frac{N\cos\theta}{A}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} . \tag{2.10}\]

It points along the axis of the bar, like the force \(\vek R\). But its magnitude \(\sigma\cos\theta\) is smaller than \(\sigma\), because the same force is spread over a larger area.

The part of the vector \(\vek t\) in the direction of the normal pulls the two parts of the bar apart, and the rest, which lies in the face of the cut, shifts them along each other. We name both parts for every plane face.

Definition 2.17 Normal and shear stress (normalna in strižna napetost)

Let a force act on a plane face with the unit normal \(\vek n\), equal per unit area to the vector \(\vek t\). The normal stress (normalna napetost) on this face is the projection of the vector \(\vek t\) onto the direction \(\vek n\), the number

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n . \tag{2.11}\]

The vector \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\) is the normal part (normalni del) of the vector \(\vek t\), and the vector \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n\) its shear part (strižni del). The length of the shear part

\[\tau = |\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n| \tag{2.12}\]

is the shear stress (strižna napetost).

Source: CM (4.2.16)-(4.2.18) (PDF p. 184), SADD (3.4.4) (PDF p. 70).

The name normal says that the first part points in the direction of the normal, perpendicular to the face. The shear part is perpendicular to \(\vek n\) by lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector, so it lies in the face and tries to shift the material on one side of the face relative to the material on the other side, as in the shear strain of the deck of cards. A positive normal stress means that the force per unit area pulls a part of the body in the direction of its outward normal, towards the other part, that is, tension. A negative one means that it pushes it. The shear stress is a length and so is never negative, and the direction of the shear part is given by the vector \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n\). By item 3 of the lemma on splitting a vector \(\tau^2 = |\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), and in this form CM (4.2.18) and SADD (3.4.4) write the shear stress. SADD says it in words: the normal part is the projection of the vector onto the unit normal, and the shear part follows from the Pythagorean theorem (SADD, PDF p. 70). From the same formula \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}| \le |\vek t|\): the normal stress never exceeds the magnitude of the force per unit area. CM writes them \(t_{\mathrm{nn}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{ns}}\), SADD \(N\) and \(S\) (CM (4.2.17), (4.2.18), SADD (3.4.4)). We write \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\tau\), because for us \(N\) is the axial force.

The stress in the bar of section 2.2 is the normal stress on the cross section, where \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\vek t = \sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma\) and \(\tau = 0\). That is why books also call it a normal stress (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).

Now we split the force per unit area (2.10) on the oblique cut of the bar.

  1. By (2.11) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n\). We insert \(\vek t = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and take out the number by item 3 of the properties of the scalar product: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma\cos\theta\,(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek n)\).
  2. By (2.6) \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek n = 1 \cdot \cos\theta + 0 \cdot \sin\theta + 0 \cdot 0 = \cos\theta\), so
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \sigma\cos^2\theta . \tag{2.13}\]
  3. The shear part is \(\vek t - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\vek n = \sigma\cos\theta\,(1, 0, 0) - \sigma\cos^2\theta\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\). By components this is \(\sigma\cos\theta\,(1 - \cos^2\theta,\ -\cos\theta\sin\theta,\ 0)\).
  4. From \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\) we get \(1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta\). So the shear part is
    \[\sigma\cos\theta\,(\sin^2\theta,\ -\sin\theta\cos\theta,\ 0) = \sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(\sin\theta,\ -\cos\theta,\ 0) .\]
    The vector \((\sin\theta, -\cos\theta, 0)\) is the opposite of the vector \(\vek d = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)\), so the shear part equals \(-\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\vek d\). It points along the face of the cut, in the direction \(\vek d\) or the opposite one.
  5. For a number \(k\) the length of the vector \(k\,\vek d\) is by (1.10) \(\sqrt{k^2\,(d_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + d_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + d_{\mathrm{z}}^2)} = \sqrt{k^2} = |k|\), because \(\vek d\) is a unit vector. So the shear stress is
    \[\tau = |\sigma\sin\theta\cos\theta| . \tag{2.14}\]
  6. Check with item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + \tau^2 = \sigma^2\cos^4\theta + \sigma^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = \sigma^2\cos^2\theta\,(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = \sigma^2\cos^2\theta\), which is \(|\vek t|^2\) by (2.10).

We derived the formulas (2.13) and (2.14) ourselves, from the equilibrium of the left part and from the scalar product. The table shows how both stresses change with the tilt of the cut. The numbers were computed with NumPy.

The table shows three patterns, and the graph in figure 2.17 shows them for all angles. First, the normal stress falls with the tilt from \(\sigma\) to zero. The same force \(\vek R\) is spread over an ever larger area, and an ever smaller part of the force per unit area points in the direction of the normal. Second, the shear stress is the same at \(30^\circ\) and at \(60^\circ\). Why? At the angle \(90^\circ - \theta\) the sine and the cosine swap roles, \(\sin(90^\circ - \theta) = \cos\theta\) and \(\cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta\) (school trigonometry), so the product \(\sin\theta\cos\theta\) does not change. Third, the normal stresses at \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\) add up to \(\sigma\), \(0{,}75 + 0{,}25 = 1\). The reason is the same: \(\cos^2 30^\circ + \cos^2 60^\circ = \cos^2 30^\circ + \sin^2 30^\circ = 1\).

In the table the shear stress is largest at \(45^\circ\), where it equals \(\sigma/2\). The table shows only five angles, so let us show this for all of them. The idea is simple: the square of a number is never negative, and the square of the difference of the sine and the cosine gives a bound for their product.

Lemma 2.18 Largest shear stress in a bar (največja strižna napetost v palici)

In a bar with the stress \(\sigma\) in the cross section, the shear stress on an oblique cut tilted by the angle \(\theta\) with \(0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ\) is at most \(|\sigma|/2\). If \(\sigma \neq 0\), it reaches this value at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) and at no other angle.

Proof

We must show that \(\sin\theta\cos\theta \le 1/2\) and that equality holds only at \(45^\circ\).

  1. For \(0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ\) both \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) are nonnegative, so by (2.14) \(\tau = |\sigma|\sin\theta\cos\theta\).
  2. The square of a number is not negative: \((\sin\theta - \cos\theta)^2 \ge 0\).
  3. We expand the square: \(\sin^2\theta - 2\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta \ge 0\).
  4. Since \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\), this is \(1 - 2\sin\theta\cos\theta \ge 0\), so \(\sin\theta\cos\theta \le 1/2\).
  5. We multiply the inequality by the nonnegative number \(|\sigma|\) and by step 1 get \(\tau \le |\sigma|/2\).
  6. Equality in step 4 holds exactly when the square in step 2 is zero, that is, when \(\sin\theta = \cos\theta\). Between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) the sine increases and the cosine decreases, so they agree at one angle only, at \(45^\circ\), where both equal \(\sqrt2/2\) (school trigonometry). So for \(\sigma \neq 0\), \(\tau = |\sigma|/2\) only at \(\theta = 45^\circ\).

Source: The lemma and the proof are not from the sources.

For our bar with diameter 10 mm under the force 10 kN, \(\sigma = 127{,}3\) MPa. On the cross section this is the whole stress, and there is no shear stress there. But on the cut at \(45^\circ\) the normal and the shear stress are both \(63{,}7\) MPa, and on the cut at \(30^\circ\) the normal stress is \(95{,}5\) MPa and the shear stress \(55{,}1\) MPa. So the same bar under the same force has different stresses on differently tilted faces through the same point. Which of them is dangerous for the material depends on the material. For metals such as steel, the start of yielding is decided approximately by the largest shear stress. This rule is called the Tresca yield criterion (Trescov pogoj tečenja) (BOWER §3.7.3, PDF p. 146). BOWER adds that tests agree somewhat better with another criterion, which we will meet later. So it matters that we can compute the shear stress also where there is none on the cross section.

Where does the derivation stop holding? We assumed that the force is distributed evenly over the cut, which by section 2.2 holds far from the ends of the bar. We also assumed that the cut does not reach the ends of the bar. As the angle approaches \(90^\circ\) the cut becomes ever longer, and at \(90^\circ\) it is no longer a cut across the bar at all but a lengthwise cut, which divides the bar into two halves side by side. Then each half carries half the force at its ends, like the thin bars in figure 2.7, and nothing has to be carried across the lengthwise cut. The formulas (2.13) and (2.14) give exactly this at \(90^\circ\), zero, although we did not derive them there.

2.5 Stress at a point

So far the force over each cut was distributed evenly, and the force per unit area was the same at all points of the cut. In the hanging bar the stress changed along the bar, from 0,765 MPa at the top to zero at the bottom, but over each cross section it was still the same. In a general body it is not the same even over a cut. Near the ends of a bar, where grips hold it, the force is concentrated on a smaller part of the cross section (section 2.2). So we need the force per unit area at a single point of a cut.

The same approach as for the strain at a point in chapter 1 helps. There we looked at ever shorter pieces of the bar around a point. Here we do the same with the face of the cut. Cut the body in thought with a plane face through the point \(P\) (figure 2.18). Let \(\vek n\) be the outward normal of the face for the part of the body we are looking at. Around the point \(P\) on the face of the cut we choose a small piece of the face, a patch (zaplata) with the area \(\Delta A\). The part of the body on the other side of the cut, the one into which \(\vek n\) points, acts on our part across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). The quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\), that is, the vector \(\Delta\vek F\) multiplied by the number \(1/\Delta A\), is the average force per unit area on the patch. As we shrink the patch around the point \(P\), the quotient approaches a vector that describes the force per unit area at the point \(P\).

Definition 2.19 Traction vector (vektor napetosti)

The traction vector (vektor napetosti), also called the stress vector, \(\vek t(\vek n)\) at a point \(P\) of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\) is the limit

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{A} \to 0} \frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} , \tag{2.15}\]

where \(\Delta\vek F\) is the force with which the part of the body on the side into which \(\vek n\) points acts on the part on the other side across a patch of the face with the area \(\Delta A\) around the point \(P\).

Source: CM (4.2.1) (PDF p. 180), SADD (3.2.1) (PDF p. 65), BOWER §2.2.2 (PDF p. 67).

The traction vector is a vector whose components have the unit of stress, Pa. The notation \(\vek t(\vek n)\) stresses that it depends on the normal of the face. Of course it also depends on the point \(P\). SADD stresses that it depends on exactly these two things, on the point and on the normal (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). We wrote the definition for a plane cut. The force across a curved surface, for example across the surface of a pipe, is described in the same way, with the normal at each point of the surface, which we will show when we need such a surface. The same concept also describes a force acting on the surface of a body from outside, for example the pressure of water on a wall. Then \(\Delta\vek F\) is the force of the surroundings on a patch of the surface (SADD §3.1, PDF pp. 63-64).

What exactly does the limit in (2.15) mean? The definition of the limit 1.5 speaks about a function whose input is one number \(h\) and whose output is one number. Here, though, the input is not a number but a patch, and the output is a vector. We remove the first difficulty by choosing how to shrink the patch. Take, for example, squares with side \(h\) that lie in the face of the cut and have their centre at \(P\) (figure 2.19). Then \(\Delta A = h^2\), and the quotient is a function of one number, \(h\). The side of a square is positive, so in the definition of the limit we require \(0 < h < \delta\) instead of \(0 < |h| < \delta\). Such a limit is called a right-hand limit (limita z desne) (LEBL1 Def. 3.1.16, PDF p. 119). We remove the second difficulty with components. That the limit is the vector \(\vek t\) means that each of the three components of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) approaches the corresponding component of the vector \(\vek t\), each by the definition of the limit. CM and SADD only write the limit down, but they do not say what shape the patches we shrink around \(P\) have (CM (4.2.1), PDF p. 180, SADD (3.2.1), PDF p. 65). In this way they silently assume that the limit exists and is the same for all shapes of patches. This is an assumption about how forces are distributed over a continuum, and we cannot derive it from our definition of the limit.

Let us test the definition on bars. In a bar pulled by the axial force \(N\) the force across an oblique cut is distributed evenly. So a patch with the area \(\Delta A\) receives the share \(\Delta A/A_\theta\) of the force \(\vek R = N\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F}{\Delta A} = \frac{1}{\Delta A}\,\frac{\Delta A}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \frac{1}{A_\theta}\,\vek R = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\]

for every patch. The quotient is the same vector for all patches, so its limit is this vector too. So the traction vector at every point of the oblique cut is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force per unit area (2.10). The definition of the normal and shear stress 2.17 therefore carries over to a point: at a point \(P\) of a face with the unit normal \(\vek n\) we take for \(\vek t\) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\).

In the hanging bar with the axis \(x\) pointing down, take the cross section at the distance \(x\) from the ceiling. For the upper part the outward normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), since it points down, into the lower part. The lower part pulls the upper one down, the force is distributed evenly over the cross section, and by (2.5)

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} .\]

At the top this is \(0{,}765\) MPa in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For the lower part the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the upper part pulls it up, and \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\rho g\,(L - x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

In both cases the traction vectors on the two sides of the same cut are opposite. This is not peculiar to bars. The idea is simple: the two parts act on each other across the same patch with opposite forces, and opposite forces give opposite quotients (figure 2.18).

Lemma 2.20 Traction on the opposite side of a cut (vektor napetosti na nasprotni strani reza)

If at a point of a plane face of a cut with the unit normal \(\vek n\) the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) exists, then \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) exists too, and

\[\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n) . \tag{2.16}\]
Proof

We must show that the limit that defines \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the opposite vector of the limit that defines \(\vek t(\vek n)\). We take the same patch twice, once as part of the face of the left part and once as part of the face of the right part. For the left part the outward normal is \(\vek n\), for the right part \(-\vek n\).

  1. The right part acts on the left one across the patch with the force \(\Delta\vek F\). By definition 2.19 of the traction vector, for the left part \(\vek t(\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\).
  2. The left part acts on the right one with the opposite force \(-\Delta\vek F\), by the law of action and reaction (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).
  3. So for the right part, whose outward normal is \(-\vek n\), \(\vek t(-\vek n)\) is the limit of the quotients \((-\Delta\vek F)/\Delta A = -(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A)\).
  4. Let \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) be the component \(x\) of the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) and \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\) the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\). The component \(x\) of the opposite quotient is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the distance \(|(-q_{\mathrm{x}}) - (-t_{\mathrm{x}})| = |q_{\mathrm{x}} - t_{\mathrm{x}}|\) is the same. So when \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(t_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}\) approaches \(-t_{\mathrm{x}}\), with the same bands \(\delta\) for every error \(\eta\). The same holds for the components \(y\) and \(z\).
  5. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.6) each component has only one limit. The proof of the lemma holds equally for the right-hand limit, since the number \(h\) that the proof chooses in step 4 is positive. So \(\vek t(-\vek n) = -\vek t(\vek n)\).

Source: CM §4.2.1 (PDF p. 181), SADD §3.2 (PDF p. 65). The proof by components is not from the sources.

So the traction vector on one side of a cut already determines the traction vector on the other side. CM and SADD write this as a consequence of the law of action and reaction (CM §4.2.1, PDF p. 181, SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65).

At the same point of the bar we got a different traction vector for every tilt of the cut, \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.20). So the stress at a point is not a single vector. It is a function whose input is the unit normal \(\vek n\), three numbers that tell how the cut is tilted, and whose output is the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), three numbers that give the force per unit area. For the bar we computed this function from equilibrium. But how does the traction vector depend on the normal in a general body, where the forces are not distributed so simply? Do we have to know \(\vek t(\vek n)\) separately for each of the infinitely many directions \(\vek n\)? This is the question of the next section.

2.6 Stress on a face of any tilt

At the end of the previous section we asked whether we have to know the traction vector at a point separately for each of the infinitely many tilts of the face. The answer is no. Three traction vectors are enough, on three faces through the point that are perpendicular to the axes of the coordinate system. From them every other traction vector is computed with a simple formula. First we will notice the formula in the bar and see on a small wedge cut from it why it holds. Then we will cut a small tetrahedron out of an arbitrary body and show from its equilibrium that the formula holds in every body. At the end we will write the three vectors with nine numbers and try the formula on three examples.

2.6.1 A hint from the bar

Take the bar from section 2.4, pulled by the axial force \(N\). The axis \(x\) lies along the bar, the axis \(y\) points up and the axis \(z\) towards us, as in figure 2.14. The stress in the cross section is \(\sigma = N/A\). Let the point \(P\) be far from the ends of the bar. At it we know the traction vectors on three faces that are perpendicular to the axes.

  1. On the cross section the unit normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and by (2.10) at \(\theta = 0\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the lengthwise cut at \(\theta = 90^\circ\), which divides the bar into an upper and a lower half. At the end of section 2.4 we saw that nothing has to be carried across such a cut, so \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\).
  3. The face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) is the lengthwise cut that divides the bar into a front and a back half. The argument is the same: each half carries half of the force at its ends, like the thin bars in figure 2.7, and nothing has to be carried across the cut. So also \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0\).

On the oblique cut with the normal \(\vek n = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), though, the traction vector is \(\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (section 2.5). The component \(x\) of the normal is \(n_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\), so we can write this vector with the three vectors from the list:

\[\vek t(\vek n) = \sigma\cos\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek 0 + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek 0 = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]

The traction vector on the oblique cut is the sum of the traction vectors on three faces perpendicular to the axes, and each of them is multiplied by the corresponding component of the normal. Is this a coincidence that holds only for the bar?

What holds this sum together is shown by a small wedge that we cut in thought out of the bar at the point \(P\) (figure 2.21). The wedge is bounded by five plane faces: the cross section through \(P\), the lengthwise cut through \(P\) with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), the oblique cut with the normal \(\vek n\) a little away from \(P\), and two faces perpendicular to the axis \(z\), at the front and at the back. A piece of a body with such faces, two equal triangles and three rectangles, is called a triangular prism (tristrana prizma). Which forces act on the wedge?

  1. The wedge lies on the side of larger \(x\) of the cross section, so the outward normal of the wedge on this face is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). By lemma 2.20 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, the traction vector there is \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The face has the area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\), the force is distributed over it evenly, and the rest of the bar acts on it with the force \(-\sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points to the left.
  2. On the oblique face with the area \(\Delta A\) the outward normal is \(\vek n\), and the rest of the bar acts on it with the force \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points to the right.
  3. On the lengthwise cut and on the faces at the front and at the back the traction vectors are zero, so there are no forces there. We neglect the weight of the wedge, as for the whole bar.
  4. The wedge is part of a bar in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself. The forces from steps 1 and 2 are therefore equal and opposite, \(\sigma\cos\theta\,\Delta A = \sigma\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\), and
    \[\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\,\Delta A = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A .\]

This is exactly the ratio of areas we found in (2.9), where the cross section with the area \(A\) is smaller than the oblique cut with the area \(A_\theta\) by the factor \(\cos\theta\). The cross-sectional face of the wedge is the shadow of the oblique face cast by light directed along the axis \(x\), and the shadow has an area smaller by the factor \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\) (figure 2.15). So the equilibrium of the wedge says this. The force on the oblique face must balance the forces on the other faces. These forces are traction vectors times areas, and the areas are the area of the oblique face times components of the normal. When we divide the force on the oblique face by its area, we get the traction vectors multiplied by components of the normal. This argument does not need the bar. It needs only a small piece of a body with faces in the right ratio, and equilibrium. Let us carry it out for every body.

2.6.2 A small tetrahedron

In a general body we do not know in advance what the traction vectors are, nor whether they are the same over the faces. So we take a piece of the body that is so small that the traction vector over each of its faces differs only a little from the traction vector at the point \(P\). In the bar the normal \(\vek n\) lay in the plane of the axes \(x\) and \(y\), so besides the oblique face the wedge had only two faces perpendicular to the axes. For a normal with three components different from zero we need three such faces. The piece of the body is then a tetrahedron (tetraeder), a body with four plane triangular faces, or in other words a pyramid with a triangular base.

Let the body be in equilibrium and \(P\) a point in its interior, that is, a point around which there is still some material of the body in all directions. We put the origin of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system at \(P\). Let \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) be a unit vector that has, for now, all three components positive. We choose a small positive number \(h\) and on the axes the points

\[A = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad B = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad C = P + \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{z}}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{2.17}\]

The points \(P\), \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are the vertices (oglišča) of the tetrahedron \(PABC\), and its faces are the triangles \(PBC\), \(PCA\), \(PAB\) and \(ABC\) (figure 2.22). The first three lie in planes perpendicular to the axes. The triangle \(PBC\) lies in the plane \(x = 0\), because all three vertices have the coordinate \(x\) equal to zero, \(PCA\) in the plane \(y = 0\) and \(PAB\) in the plane \(z = 0\). Why did we put the vertices \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) exactly there? Because then \(\vek n\) is the normal of the fourth face \(ABC\), as we will show below. And because all distances between the vertices are proportional to \(h\). When we make \(h\) smaller, the tetrahedron shrinks towards \(P\) while its shape stays the same.

For the equilibrium we will need the areas of the faces and the volume of the tetrahedron. The next lemma expresses them with the area of the face \(ABC\) and with the components of the normal. The idea of the proof is simple: we compute the volume of the tetrahedron in two ways, once with the base \(ABC\), once with the base \(PBC\), and set the two values equal.

Lemma 2.21 Faces and volume of the tetrahedron (ploskve in prostornina tetraedra)

Let \(P\) be the origin of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) a unit vector with three positive components and \(h > 0\). For the tetrahedron \(PABC\) with the vertices (2.17):

  1. \(\vek n\) is a unit normal of the face \(ABC\).
  2. The height of the tetrahedron on the face \(ABC\), the distance from \(P\) to the plane of this face, is \(h\), and \(\vek n\) points away from \(P\), out of the tetrahedron.
  3. The areas of the faces \(PBC\), \(PCA\) and \(PAB\) are
    \[\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A , \qquad \Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A , \qquad \Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\Delta A , \tag{2.18}\]
    where \(\Delta A\) is the area of the face \(ABC\).
  4. The volume of the tetrahedron is \(\Delta V = h\,\Delta A/3\).
Proof

We compute items 1 and 2 with the scalar product, and items 3 and 4 from the school formula for the volume of a pyramid, which we use twice.

1. The normal of the face \(ABC\).

  1. By (1.8) the vectors from \(A\) to \(B\) and from \(A\) to \(C\) are \(B - A = (-h/n_{\mathrm{x}},\ h/n_{\mathrm{y}},\ 0)\) and \(C - A = (-h/n_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0,\ h/n_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  2. By (2.6), \(\vek n\cdot(B - A) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(-h/n_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}} \cdot 0 = -h + h = 0\). Likewise \(\vek n\cdot(C - A) = -h + h = 0\).
  3. The points of the plane through \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are the points \(A + s\,(B - A) + w\,(C - A)\) for arbitrary numbers \(s\) and \(w\), just as the points of the oblique cut of the bar were the points \(O + s\,\vek d + w\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) (section 2.4). The triangle \(ABC\) consists of those among them for which \(s \ge 0\), \(w \ge 0\) and \(s + w \le 1\).
  4. The vector between the points of the plane at the numbers \(s_1, w_1\) and \(s_2, w_2\) is, by the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.13), \((s_2 - s_1)\,(B - A) + (w_2 - w_1)\,(C - A)\). By items 2 and 3 of the properties of the scalar product (lemma 2.12) and by step 2, its scalar product with \(\vek n\) is \((s_2 - s_1) \cdot 0 + (w_2 - w_1) \cdot 0 = 0\).
  5. The vector \(\vek n\) is a unit vector and perpendicular to every vector between two points of the face \(ABC\), so it is a unit normal of this face (definition 2.16).

2. The height and the direction of the normal.

  1. Let \(F = P + h\,\vek n\). We show that \(F\) is a point of the plane from step 3 of item 1, namely at \(s = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\) and \(w = n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). The component \(x\) of the point \(A + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\,(B - A) + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\,(C - A)\) is
    \[\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} - n_{\mathrm{y}}^2\,\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} - n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\,\frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}} = \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\big(1 - n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 - n_{\mathrm{z}}^2\big) = \frac{h}{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 = h\,n_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]
    because \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = |\vek n|^2 = 1\). The component \(y\) is \(n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 \cdot h/n_{\mathrm{y}} = h\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the component \(z\) is \(n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 \cdot h/n_{\mathrm{z}} = h\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\). So the point is \(P + h\,\vek n = F\).
  2. Since \(s + w = n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = 1 - n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 < 1\), the point \(F\) even lies on the triangle \(ABC\).
  3. The vector from \(F\) to \(P\) is \(-h\,\vek n\). By item 3 of the properties of the scalar product and by step 4 of item 1 it is perpendicular to every vector between points of the plane, so the segment \(PF\) is perpendicular to the plane of the face \(ABC\). Its length is \(|{-h\,\vek n}| = h\,|\vek n| = h\). This is the distance from \(P\) to the plane, the height of the tetrahedron on the face \(ABC\).
  4. From \(P = F - h\,\vek n\) we see that we reach \(P\) if we move from a point of the face by \(h\) in the direction opposite to the normal. So the normal \(\vek n\) points away from \(P\), out of the tetrahedron.

3. and 4. The areas and the volume.

  1. A tetrahedron is a pyramid with a triangular base. The volume of a pyramid is, from school, one third of the product of the area of the base and the height, the distance from the opposite vertex to the plane of the base. CM (4.2.5) writes exactly this formula for our tetrahedron.
  2. For the base \(ABC\) the opposite vertex is \(P\), and by item 2 the height is \(h\). So \(\Delta V = \tfrac13\,h\,\Delta A\). This is item 4.
  3. For the base \(PBC\) in the plane \(x = 0\) the opposite vertex is \(A\). The vector from \(P\) to \(A\) is \((h/n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). Every vector between two points of the plane \(x = 0\) has the component \(x\) equal to zero, so it has the form \((0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}})\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(0, v_{\mathrm{y}}, v_{\mathrm{z}}) = 0\). The segment \(PA\) is therefore perpendicular to the plane \(x = 0\), and the height is its length \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\). So \(\Delta V = \tfrac13\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  4. The two values of the volume are equal, \(\tfrac13\,h\,\Delta A = \tfrac13\,\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\,h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\). We multiply both sides by the positive number \(3n_{\mathrm{x}}/h\) and get \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A\).
  5. We do the same with the base \(PCA\), which has the height \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\), and with the base \(PAB\), which has the height \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}}\). We get \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A\) and \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\Delta A\).

Source: CM (4.2.4), (4.2.5) (PDF pp. 181-182), BOWER, Appendix E.1 (PDF pp. 792-793). CM gets the ratios of the areas from a theorem on closed surfaces, BOWER with the vector product. The proof that computes the volume twice is not from the sources.

The proof used only the scalar product, the fact that \(\vek n\) is a unit vector, and the school formula for the volume of a pyramid. Item 3 is the ratio we noticed on the wedge. If we light the tetrahedron along the axis \(x\), the face \(ABC\) casts a shadow on the plane \(x = 0\): the vertex \(A\) falls onto \(P\), and the vertices \(B\) and \(C\) stay where they are. The shadow is the face \(PBC\), and its area is smaller by the factor \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\).

Let us test the lemma on numbers. Let \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), which is a unit vector since \(\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac49 = 1\), and \(h = 1\) mm. The vertices are at the distances \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}} = 3\) mm, \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}} = 1{,}5\) mm and \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}} = 1{,}5\) mm from \(P\). The face \(PBC\) is a right triangle with legs of \(1{,}5\) mm, so it has the area \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 1{,}5/2 = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^2\), and the faces \(PCA\) and \(PAB\) have \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = \Delta A_{\mathrm{z}} = 1{,}5 \cdot 3/2 = 2{,}25\ \text{mm}^2\). By item 3, \(\Delta A = \Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/n_{\mathrm{x}} = 3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\), and indeed \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}/n_{\mathrm{y}} = 2{,}25 \cdot \tfrac32 = 3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\) too. By item 4 the volume is \(\Delta V = 1 \cdot 3{,}375/3 = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^3\), which is also one third of the product of \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = 1{,}125\ \text{mm}^2\) and the height \(|PA| = 3\) mm.

Now let us write the equilibrium of the tetrahedron (figure 2.23). Four forces across the faces and the weight act on it. We denote by \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}\) the force with which the rest of the body acts on the tetrahedron across the face \(ABC\). The forces across the faces \(PBC\), \(PCA\) and \(PAB\), whose outward normals are \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), we denote by \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}\). The index tells the outward normal of the face. Let the material have the density \(\rho\). The mass of the tetrahedron is then \(\rho\,\Delta V\), and by (2.1) its weight is a vector \(\Delta\vek W\) of size \(\rho g\,\Delta V\), pointing down. The tetrahedron is part of a body in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself (section 2.1), and by (2.2)

\[\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}} + \Delta\vek W = \vek 0 . \tag{2.19}\]

What happens when we shrink the tetrahedron towards \(P\)? The forces across the faces are roughly traction vectors times areas, and the areas are proportional to \(h^2\). The weight is proportional to the volume, and the volume to \(h^3\). For a steel tetrahedron with the normal \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\) and \(\rho g = 7800 \cdot 9{,}81 = 76\,518\ \text{N/m}^3\) we computed this with NumPy:

\(h\) \(\Delta A\) \(\Delta V\) \(\vert\Delta\vek W\vert\) \(\vert\Delta\vek W\vert/\Delta A\)
1 mm \(3{,}375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(1{,}125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-5}\) N \(25{,}51\) Pa
0,1 mm \(0{,}03375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(0{,}001125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N \(2{,}551\) Pa
0,01 mm \(0{,}0003375\ \text{mm}^2\) \(0{,}000001125\ \text{mm}^3\) \(8{,}608 \cdot 10^{-11}\) N \(0{,}2551\) Pa

When we make \(h\) smaller by the factor 10, the area becomes smaller by the factor 100, but the volume and the weight by the factor 1000. The weight per unit area of the slanted face is, by item 4 of the lemma, \(\rho g\,\Delta V/\Delta A = \rho g h/3\), and it falls towards zero as \(h\) gets smaller, from \(25{,}51\) Pa at \(h = 1\) mm to \(0{,}2551\) Pa at \(h = 0{,}01\) mm. Stresses in steel are measured in millions of pascals. The forces across the faces per unit area, however, do not get smaller, because they are close to the traction vectors at \(P\). For a small tetrahedron the weight therefore no longer counts, and the equilibrium relates only the forces across the faces. This is the heart of the derivation. As with the derivative, two quantities get smaller together, but at different rates, and their ratio is what decides.

To write this precisely, we divide equation (2.19) by the area \(\Delta A\). We write the force \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\) as \((\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A)\,(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}})\), and by (2.18), \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A = n_{\mathrm{x}}\). Likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). We get

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}}{\Delta A} + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}} + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}} + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}}{\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}}} + \frac{\Delta\vek W}{\Delta A} = \vek 0 . \tag{2.20}\]

Each of the first four quotients is the force across a face divided by the area of that face, that is, the average force per unit area on the face, like the quotient \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) in definition 2.19 of the traction vector.

Where do these quotients go as \(h\) approaches zero? We expect each of them to approach the traction vector at \(P\) for the outward normal of its face: \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}/\Delta A\) the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and so on. We cannot derive this from the definition of the traction vector. The definition speaks about square patches centred at \(P\), but the faces of the tetrahedron are triangles, three of them have \(P\) at a vertex, and the face \(ABC\) does not contain the point \(P\) at all. The expectation rests on the assumption that the traction vector changes continuously from point to point, without jumps. Then the force per unit area at every point of a small face near \(P\) is close to the one at \(P\), and so is the average. CM assumes that in a continuum all quantities, stresses included, vary continuously (CM §1.1, PDF p. 30), and then simply shrinks the tetrahedron to a point (CM, PDF p. 182). We too will take this as an assumption.

2.6.3 Mathematics: the limit of a sum

In the last step we will let \(h\) approach zero and conclude from equation (2.20), which holds for every \(h\), an equation between limits. On the left stands a sum of five vectors, and four of them are multiplied by numbers. So we need the rule that the limit of such a sum equals the sum of the limits, multiplied by the same numbers. We compute the limit of a vector by components (section 2.5), so it is enough to prove the rule for functions whose input is a number \(h\) and whose output is one number.

The idea is the same as for the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.6). The difference between the sum and the sum of the limits is a sum of differences, each of which is small. If there are \(k\) differences, we allow each of them a \(k\)-th part of the error.

Lemma 2.22 Limit of a sum (limita vsote)

Let the functions \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{\mathrm{k}}\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and let \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g_{\mathrm{i}}(h) = d_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, k\). Then for all numbers \(c_1, c_2, \dots, c_{\mathrm{k}}\)

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \big(c_1\,g_1(h) + c_2\,g_2(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}}\,g_{\mathrm{k}}(h)\big) = c_1 d_1 + c_2 d_2 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}} . \tag{2.21}\]

The same holds for the right-hand limit.

Proof

We write the definition of the limit 1.5 for the sum and the number \(d = c_1 d_1 + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} d_{\mathrm{k}}\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|c_1 g_1(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. We choose an arbitrary error \(\eta > 0\).
  2. For each \(i\) we take the error \(\eta_{\mathrm{i}} = \eta/\big(k\,(|c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1)\big)\). The added one makes sure that we do not divide by zero when \(c_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). Since \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the limit of \(g_{\mathrm{i}}(h)\), there is a band \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) such that \(|g_{\mathrm{i}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{i}}| < \eta_{\mathrm{i}}\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  3. For \(\delta\) we take the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1, \dots, \delta_{\mathrm{k}}\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) all inequalities from step 2 hold at once.
  4. We rearrange the difference between the sum and the number \(d\) by the rules of arithmetic with numbers: \(c_1 g_1(h) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}} g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d = c_1\,(g_1(h) - d_1) + \dots + c_{\mathrm{k}}\,(g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{k}})\).
  5. By the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\) (step 6 of the proof of lemma 1.6 on the uniqueness of the limit), used \(k - 1\) times, and by the rule \(|c\,a| = |c|\,|a|\), the absolute value of this sum is at most \(|c_1|\,|g_1(h) - d_1| + \dots + |c_{\mathrm{k}}|\,|g_{\mathrm{k}}(h) - d_{\mathrm{k}}|\).
  6. By step 2 each term is at most \(|c_{\mathrm{i}}|\,\eta_{\mathrm{i}} = \dfrac{|c_{\mathrm{i}}|}{|c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1} \cdot \dfrac{\eta}{k}\), which is less than \(\eta/k\), because \(|c_{\mathrm{i}}| < |c_{\mathrm{i}}| + 1\).
  7. There are \(k\) terms, so the sum is less than \(k \cdot \eta/k = \eta\). The condition of the definition of the limit is satisfied.
  8. For the right-hand limit we repeat all the steps with the band \(0 < h < \delta\) instead of \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Cor. 3.1.12 (PDF p. 118), for two functions and without the numbers \(c_{\mathrm{i}}\). The source leaves the proof to the reader. The proof that divides the error is not from the source.

The lemma holds for each component of a vector separately, so it also holds for a sum of vectors multiplied by numbers. We will also need that a function which is zero for every \(h\) has the limit zero. The definition of the limit confirms it: the difference \(|0 - 0| = 0\) is smaller than every error \(\eta\), for every band \(\delta\).

2.6.4 Cauchy's formula

Now we can state and prove the main result of the section.

Theorem 2.23 Cauchy's formula (Cauchyjeva formula)

Let a body be in equilibrium, \(P\) a point in its interior and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the unit vectors of the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. Assume that the average force per unit area on a plane face approaches the traction vector at \(P\) for the outward normal of this face when we shrink the face so that all its points come ever closer to \(P\). Then for every unit normal \(\vek n = (n_{\mathrm{x}}, n_{\mathrm{y}}, n_{\mathrm{z}})\) at the point \(P\)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) . \tag{2.22}\]
Proof

We prove the formula first for a normal with three positive components, then for a normal with negative components, and finally for a normal with some component equal to zero.

1. Three positive components.

  1. For every \(h > 0\) we take the tetrahedron \(PABC\) with the vertices (2.17). The point \(P\) is in the interior of the body, so for a small enough \(h\) the whole tetrahedron lies in the body. It is in equilibrium, and (2.19) holds.
  2. We divide the equation by the area \(\Delta A\) and use the ratios of areas (2.18). We get (2.20), which holds for every small enough \(h > 0\).
  3. By item 4 of lemma 2.21 on the faces and volume of the tetrahedron, the last term has the length \(\rho g\,\Delta V/\Delta A = \rho g h/3\). Each component of a vector is in absolute value at most its length, since for example \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 \le a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2\). For an error \(\eta > 0\) we take \(\delta = 3\eta/(\rho g)\). For \(0 < h < \delta\) each component of the last term is in absolute value less than \(\rho g\,\delta/3 = \eta\), so the right-hand limit of the last term is the zero vector.
  4. All points of the tetrahedron are at most as far from \(P\) as the farthest vertex, that is, at most the largest of the numbers \(h/n_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h/n_{\mathrm{z}}\). As \(h\) approaches zero, all points of all four faces therefore come ever closer to \(P\). By the assumption the quotients approach the traction vectors at \(P\): \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{n}}/\Delta A\) the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}/\Delta A_{\mathrm{z}}\) the vector \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  5. By lemma 2.22 on the limit of a sum, used on each component separately, the left side of (2.20) approaches the vector \(\vek t(\vek n) + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) + \vek 0\).
  6. The left side is the zero vector for every \(h\), so its limit is also the zero vector. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.6), which also holds for the right-hand limit (step 5 of the proof of lemma 2.20 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut), the limits from steps 5 and 6 are equal:
    \[\vek t(\vek n) + n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0 .\]
  7. By lemma 2.20 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). We substitute and move three terms to the right side. We get (2.22).

2. Negative components, none of them zero.

  1. Let, for example, \(n_{\mathrm{x}} < 0\). We take the coordinate system with the same origin and the same axes, only on the axis \(x\) we choose the opposite positive direction. This is again a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system, since definition 1.10 requires only that a positive direction is chosen on each axis. Its unit vector of the axis \(x\) is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The component of the vector \(\vek n\) along the new axis \(x\) is its projection onto \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 2.13), that is, \(\vek n\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -n_{\mathrm{x}} > 0\). The other two components stay \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. In the same way we reverse every axis along which \(\vek n\) has a negative component. In the new system \(\vek n\) has all three components positive, and by item 1, for example with the reversed axis \(x\),
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = (-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) .\]
  4. By the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \((-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = (-n_{\mathrm{x}})\,\big({-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})}\big) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\). Likewise for every other reversed axis. So in the old system the formula has the form (2.22).

3. Components that are zero.

  1. If two components are zero, \(\vek n\) is \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) or \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). For \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the formula reads \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), and for \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it reads \(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), which is the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut. Likewise for \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. Let only one component be zero, for example \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), and let the other two be positive by item 2. Then the plane through \(A\) and \(B\) with the normal \(\vek n\) does not meet the axis \(z\), and there is no tetrahedron. Instead of it we take a triangular prism, like the wedge in figure 2.21. One of its equal triangular faces is the triangle \(PAB\) in the plane \(z = 0\), with \(A\) and \(B\) as in (2.17), the other is the same triangle moved by \(h\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  3. By the Pythagorean theorem the side \(AB\) has the length \(\sqrt{h^2/n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + h^2/n_{\mathrm{y}}^2} = h\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{x}}^2}/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}}) = h/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\), because \(n_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{y}}^2 = 1\). The slanted face is a rectangle with the sides \(h/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(h\), so \(\Delta A = h^2/(n_{\mathrm{x}} n_{\mathrm{y}})\). The normal \(\vek n\) is perpendicular to \(B - A\) as in item 1 of the lemma and to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so it is a unit normal of this face.
  4. The face in the plane \(x = 0\) is a rectangle with the sides \(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(h\), so \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{x}} = h^2/n_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\Delta A\). Likewise \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{y}} = n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\Delta A\). The triangular faces have the area \(\tfrac12\,(h/n_{\mathrm{x}})(h/n_{\mathrm{y}}) = \tfrac12\,\Delta A\), and the volume of the prism is the area of the triangle times \(h\), that is, \(h\,\Delta A/2\).
  5. We divide the equilibrium of the prism by \(\Delta A\) and repeat steps 3 to 6 of item 1. The triangular faces have the outward normals \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), so in the limit they contribute \(\tfrac12\,\big(\vek t(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) + \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\big)\), which by the lemma on the traction on the opposite side of a cut is the zero vector. What remains is \(\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\), which is (2.22) at \(n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\).

Source: CM (4.2.2)-(4.2.7) (PDF pp. 181-182), SADD (3.2.5) (PDF p. 66), BOWER §2.2.2-2.2.3 (PDF pp. 68-70). The sources prove the formula only for the tetrahedron. Items 2 and 3 of the proof are not from the sources.

This is one of the results on which all of continuum mechanics stands, so let us remember it. The proof showed why it holds. The faces of a small tetrahedron at \(P\) are shadows of the slanted face, so their areas are the area of the slanted face times components of the normal. The equilibrium of the tetrahedron relates the forces on the four faces, and the weight does not count for a small tetrahedron, because it shrinks faster than the areas. What remains is a relation between traction vectors at a single point.

We derived the formula for a body in equilibrium. What about a body that moves? Then by Newton's law the resultant of the forces on the tetrahedron equals its mass times its acceleration, and on the right side of (2.19) stands \(\rho\,\Delta V\) times the acceleration instead of the zero vector. CM carries out the derivation exactly this way (CM (4.2.2), PDF p. 181). The mass is proportional to the volume, so after division by \(\Delta A\) this term vanishes just like the weight (CM (4.2.6), (4.2.7), PDF p. 182). The same holds for every other force that acts on all the material and is proportional to the volume. Cauchy's formula therefore holds also in a body that moves or changes its shape.

2.6.5 Stress components

Cauchy's formula says that the stress at a point is described by three vectors, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\). In a chosen coordinate system each of them has three components. So the stress at a point is described by nine numbers.

Definition 2.24 Stress components (komponente napetosti)

Let \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) be the traction vectors at the point \(P\) on the faces that are perpendicular to the axes of a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. The stress components (komponente napetosti) at \(P\) are nine numbers, the components of these three vectors:

\[\begin{aligned} \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) , \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}) , \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) &= (\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}},\ \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{2.23}\]

The first index tells the face, perpendicular to the axis of that name, and the second index the component of the traction vector on it.

Source: SADD (3.2.2), (3.2.3) (PDF pp. 65-66), BOWER §2.2.3 (PDF pp. 70-71), EP §1.3.1 (pp. 28-29).

Why two indices? For the strain in chapter 1 the indices of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) told two directions, between which we measure the change of a right angle. Here the first index selects the face and the second the component of the force on it. The number \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is the component \(y\) of the force per unit area on the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

What do the numbers mean? On the face with the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the normal stress is, by definition 2.17, the projection of the traction vector onto the normal, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The shear part is \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (0, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}})\). It lies in the face, because its component \(x\) is zero, and its length is the shear stress on this face, \(\tau = \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2}\). Likewise on the other two faces. The three numbers with equal indices, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), are therefore the normal stresses on the three faces. The six numbers with different indices are the components of the shear parts, so we call them shear components (strižne komponente). Figure 2.24 shows them on a small cube around the point \(P\).

What are the signs? A positive component on the face with the outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) points in the positive direction of its axis, as figure 2.24 shows. On the opposite face of the cube the outward normal is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the traction vector is, by lemma 2.20 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(-\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\), so there positive components point in the negative directions of the axes (SADD, PDF p. 66). A positive normal stress on both faces pulls outward from the cube, so it means tension, as in the bar.

Books write the components differently. SADD writes the normal components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}\) and the shear ones \(\tau_{\mathrm{xy}}\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and so on (SADD (3.2.5), PDF p. 66). BOWER and EP write the indices as we do, the first for the face and the second for the direction (BOWER, PDF p. 71, EP, pp. 28-29). CM swaps the indices: for it \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) means the component in the direction of the axis \(i\) on the face perpendicular to the axis \(j\) (CM, PDF p. 183). When reading CM, the indices must therefore be swapped.

Let us write Cauchy's formula with components.

  1. Into (2.22) we substitute the vectors from (2.23):
    \[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}) + n_{\mathrm{y}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}) + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) .\]
  2. We compute multiples and the sum of vectors by components (definition 1.12). The component \(x\) we get from the components \(x\) of all three vectors, the component \(y\) from the components \(y\), the component \(z\) from the components \(z\):
    \[\begin{aligned} t_{\mathrm{x}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{y}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} , \\ t_{\mathrm{z}}(\vek n) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,n_{\mathrm{x}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,n_{\mathrm{z}} . \end{aligned} \tag{2.24}\]

This is how SADD (3.2.5) (PDF p. 66) and BOWER (PDF p. 70) write it. The nine numbers are easier to remember in a table in which each row is one traction vector:

\[\begin{array}{c|ccc} & x & y & z \\ \hline \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} \\ \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) & \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} \end{array}\]

The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek t(\vek n)\) we get from the column \(x\): we multiply the first number by \(n_{\mathrm{x}}\), the second by \(n_{\mathrm{y}}\), the third by \(n_{\mathrm{z}}\), and add the products. Likewise for the columns \(y\) and \(z\).

Books call these nine numbers the stress tensor (napetostni tenzor) at the point \(P\) (SADD §3.2, PDF pp. 64-66, CM §4.2.3, PDF pp. 182-183). What the word tensor means we will say later, when we see how the nine numbers change when we turn the axes. Until then the stress components are for us nine numbers in a chosen coordinate system.

In all the examples that follow, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\). This is no coincidence. We will show that it follows from the second condition of equilibrium, that the forces do not turn the body, which we have not yet written with numbers. For Cauchy's formula we did not use this condition, so the formula does not need the equality. CM too stresses that its derivation does not assume it (CM, PDF p. 183).

2.6.6 Three examples

A bar, any face. In a bar pulled by an axial force, by the hint at the beginning of the section, \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0\) and \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \vek 0\). The only nonzero stress component is therefore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma\), and by (2.24)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = (\sigma\,n_{\mathrm{x}},\ 0,\ 0)\]

for every face, not only for the faces tilted about the axis \(z\) that we treated in section 2.4. Take the face with the normal \(\vek n = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)\), which is not tilted about any axis. Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = (\sigma/3, 0, 0)\), the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = \sigma/3 \cdot 1/3 = \sigma/9\), and the shear stress, by item 3 of lemma 2.14 on splitting a vector,

\[\tau = \sqrt{|\vek t|^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{9} - \frac{\sigma^2}{81}} = \frac{2\sqrt2}{9}\,\sigma = 0{,}3143\,\sigma .\]

For our bar with \(\sigma = 127{,}3\) MPa, \(|\vek t| = 42{,}43\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = 14{,}14\) MPa and \(\tau = 40{,}01\) MPa. Without Cauchy's formula this face would be hard to treat, since it does not pass through the bar as simply as the cuts in figure 2.16.

Pure shear. We load a cube only with forces along its faces (figure 2.25, left). Let the axis \(x\) point to the right and the axis \(z\) up. We label the corners of the front face as in section 1.6.2: \(A\) at the bottom left, \(B\) at the bottom right, \(C\) at the top right and \(D\) at the top left. We pull the top face to the right and the bottom face to the left, as with the deck of cards in figure 1.4. If only these two forces acted, they would turn the cube, like the forces on the ruler in figure 2.2. So we pull the right face up and the left face down, and these two forces turn the cube the other way. Let all four forces per unit area be equally large, \(\tau_0\), and the cube is in equilibrium. Why exactly at equal sizes, we will show when we write the second condition of equilibrium with numbers. The front and the back face are not loaded. As in the bar, we assume that the stress is the same at all points of the cube. Then

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = \vek 0 , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = \tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\), and all other components are zero. Such a state is called pure shear (čisti strig). On the faces of the cube there are only shear stresses, no normal ones. But what about the faces that cut the cube along a diagonal? By (2.22)

\[\vek t(\vek n) = n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} + n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\tau_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \tau_0\,(n_{\mathrm{z}},\ 0,\ n_{\mathrm{x}}) .\]
  1. The face perpendicular to the diagonal \(AC\) has the normal \(\vek n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2 = \tau_0\,\vek n\). The traction vector points in the direction of the normal, so the shear part is zero, and the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \tau_0\,(\vek n\cdot\vek n) = \tau_0\), tension.
  2. The face perpendicular to the diagonal \(BD\) has the normal \(\vek n = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\vek t(\vek n) = \tau_0\,(1, 0, -1)/\sqrt2 = -\tau_0\,\vek n\), the shear part is again zero, and the normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = -\tau_0\), compression.

So shear on the faces of the cube is the same as tension and compression on the diagonal faces, depending only on which faces we look at. At \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa the material is pulled with 50 MPa in the direction of one diagonal and pressed with 50 MPa in the direction of the other. We saw the same pattern for the sheared cube in section 1.6.2: the diagonal \(AC\) got longer, the diagonal \(BD\) shorter. How stress and strain are related we will see in chapter 3. Let us also check the equilibrium of the lower left triangular piece \(ABD\) of the cube with side \(s\), cut off by the diagonal face (figure 2.25, right). On the bottom face acts the force \(-\tau_0 s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) per unit depth, on the left one \(-\tau_0 s\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and on the diagonal face of length \(s\sqrt2\) the force \(\tau_0\,\vek n\,s\sqrt2 = \tau_0 s\,(1, 0, 1)\). The sum is \(\tau_0 s\,\big((-1, 0, 0) + (0, 0, -1) + (1, 0, 1)\big) = \vek 0\).

A fluid at rest. A fluid (tekočina), such as water or air, differs from a solid in that it cannot resist shear. A shear force deforms a fluid continuously, so a fluid at rest carries only pressure and no shear stress (CM §8.1.1, PDF p. 384). At a point of still water the shear part of the traction vector is therefore zero on every face. On the faces perpendicular to the axes the traction vector points along the normal and towards the face, because the water pushes:

\[\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -p_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}) = -p_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} , \qquad \vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = -p_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} ,\]

where \(p_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{z}}\) are positive numbers, the pressures (tlaki) on the three faces. Are these pressures equal? Cauchy's formula says they must be.

  1. Take the face with the normal \(\vek n = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2\). By (2.22), \(\vek t(\vek n) = -(p_{\mathrm{x}}, p_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)/\sqrt2\).
  2. The normal stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek t\cdot\vek n = -(p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}})/2\), and the shear part is
    \[\vek t(\vek n) - \sigma_{\mathrm{n}}\,\vek n = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(-p_{\mathrm{x}} + \frac{p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}}}{2},\ -p_{\mathrm{y}} + \frac{p_{\mathrm{x}} + p_{\mathrm{y}}}{2},\ 0\right) = \frac{p_{\mathrm{y}} - p_{\mathrm{x}}}{2\sqrt2}\,(1, -1, 0) .\]
  3. On this face too the shear part must be zero, so \(p_{\mathrm{x}} = p_{\mathrm{y}}\).
  4. In the same way the face with the normal \((0, 1, 1)/\sqrt2\) gives \(p_{\mathrm{y}} = p_{\mathrm{z}}\). All three pressures are equal, and we denote them by \(p\).
  5. For every face therefore \(\vek t(\vek n) = -p\,n_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - p\,n_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} - p\,n_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = -p\,\vek n\).

In a fluid at rest the pressure pushes perpendicular to every face and is the same in all directions (figure 2.26). That is why water presses on the wall of a dam perpendicular to it, however the wall is tilted, and why a diver feels the same pressure from all sides. CM writes \(\vek t = -p\,\vek n\) directly (CM (8.1.1), PDF p. 384). That \(p\) is the same in all directions we have derived here from Cauchy's formula. The stress components are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\), and the shear components are zero.

We have described the stress at a point with nine numbers in a chosen coordinate system. Three questions remain open. How do these nine numbers change when we turn the axes? On which face through the point is the normal stress largest, and on which the shear stress? For the bar we found the answer only among the faces tilted about the axis \(z\), and in pure shear we saw that faces with normal stresses only exist even where the faces of the cube have none. And how does the second condition of equilibrium relate \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\)? For the first two questions we need new mathematics, computing with tables of numbers, which are called matrices.
