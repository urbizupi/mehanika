Chapter 19 Symmetry, periodicity and constraints

19.1 Mirror symmetry

For a spatial cantilever (lemma 7.36) and for a bridge (lemma 7.38) chapter 7 proved that a symmetric structure under a symmetric load gives a symmetric response. Each of the two proofs held for its own structure. Here we prove the same idea once, for every body and every mesh model. Why is this worth it? Because half a structure means half the unknowns, but only if we know when we may use the symmetry. This is why both theorems of this section will also have an example in which the structure looks symmetric and the response is not.

Imagine a T-shaped bracket welded to a wall. In the middle of the stem, where the bracket is narrowest, we place a flat mirror, perpendicular to the wall and to the plane of the page, so that we see it from the side as a line. The plane of the mirror is called the plane of reflection (ravnina zrcaljenja) and we denote it by \(\Pi\), the Greek capital pi. In the mirror we see the whole bracket: the right half appears as the left one and the reverse. Each point \(\vek x\) on the shoulder has its image \(Z\vek x\) on the left shoulder, which is as far behind the mirror as \(\vek x\) is in front of it distance. The point and its image lie on the same perpendicular to the mirror, that is on a line with the normal \(\vek m\) of the plane, which we take to be a unit vector. Let us write this with a matrix. If we choose the origin of coordinates on the plane, the displacement from the point to its image is \(-2\,(\vek x\cdot\vek m)\,\vek m\), twice the orthogonal projection of the point on \(\vek m\) with the sign reversed, and we get \(Z\vek x = \vek x - 2\,(\vek m\cdot\vek x)\,\vek m\).

Definition 19.1 Reflection in a plane (zrcaljenje čez ravnino)

Let \(\Pi\) be a plane in the space \(\mathbb{R}^3\) through the origin of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and \(\vek m\) a unit vector perpendicular to it. The reflection in the plane \(\Pi\) (zrcaljenje čez ravnino \(\Pi\)) is the map \(\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3\), \(\vek x \mapsto \mat Z\vek x\), with the matrix of order \(3\)

\[\mat Z = \mat I - 2\,\vek m\,\vek m^\T , \tag{19.1}\]

that is \(\mat Z\vek x = \vek x - 2\,(\vek m\cdot\vek x)\,\vek m\). The point \(\mat Z\vek x\) is the mirror image (zrcalna slika) of the point \(\vek x\), the set \(\mat Z(V)\) the mirror image of the set \(V\) of points.

Source: COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69) describes mirror symmetry as a property with respect to a plane that the reflection maps "onto itself". The matrix (19.1) is our notation for this map. We use the letter \(\mat Z\) because the letter \(S\) is already taken in the book: the surfaces \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) in chapter 11 and the geometric stiffness in chapter 12.

What does a reflection preserve? We shall need this in every proof of this chapter, so we prove it at once.

Lemma 19.2 Properties of reflection (lastnosti zrcaljenja)

For the reflection \(\mat Z\) in the plane \(\Pi\) with the normal \(\vek m\) (definition 19.1) the following holds:

  1. \(\mat Z^\T = \mat Z\) and \(\mat Z^2 = \mat I\), so \(\mat Z\) is an orthogonal matrix, \(\mat Z^\T\mat Z = \mat I\).
  2. \(\mat Z\vek x = \vek x\) for every point \(\vek x\) on the plane \(\Pi\) and \(\mat Z\vek m = -\vek m\).
  3. The distance between two points and the distance between their mirror images are equal: \(|\mat Z\vek x - \mat Z\vek y| = |\vek x - \vek y|\). The midpoint of the segment between \(\vek x\) and \(\mat Z\vek x\) lies on the plane \(\Pi\).
  4. In a coordinate system with the axis \(z\) along \(\vek m\) we have \(\mat Z = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\) and \(\det\mat Z = -1\). This is the matrix \(\mat Q\) from the proof of lemma 3.21. In the original system, too, the determinant of the matrix (19.1) is \(-1\).
Proof

We must show that the properties follow from \(\vek m\cdot\vek m = 1\) and from the form (19.1).

  1. Transposition: \((\mat I - 2\,\vek m\vek m^\T)^\T = \mat I - 2\,\vek m\vek m^\T\), because \((\vek m\vek m^\T)^\T = \vek m\vek m^\T\). Square: \(\mat Z^2 = \mat I - 4\,\vek m\vek m^\T + 4\,\vek m\,(\vek m^\T\vek m)\,\vek m^\T = \mat I - 4\,\vek m\vek m^\T + 4\,\vek m\vek m^\T = \mat I\), since \(\vek m^\T\vek m = 1\). Hence \(\mat Z^\T\mat Z = \mat Z^2 = \mat I\).
  2. The point \(\vek x\) lies on \(\Pi\) if \(\vek m\cdot\vek x = 0\) (a plane through the origin, perpendicular to \(\vek m\)). Then \(\mat Z\vek x = \vek x\). For \(\vek m\) we have \(\mat Z\vek m = \vek m - 2\,(\vek m\cdot\vek m)\,\vek m = -\vek m\).
  3. By step 1, \(|\mat Z\vek w|^2 = \vek w^\T\mat Z^\T\mat Z\vek w = |\vek w|^2\) for every \(\vek w\). With \(\vek w = \vek x - \vek y\) and linearity, \(\mat Z\vek x - \mat Z\vek y = \mat Z\vek w\), we get the first claim. The midpoint of the segment is \(\tfrac12(\vek x + \mat Z\vek x) = \vek x - (\vek m\cdot\vek x)\,\vek m\), and its scalar product with \(\vek m\) is \(\vek m\cdot\vek x - \vek m\cdot\vek x = 0\).
  4. Let \(\vek e_1\) and \(\vek e_2\) be perpendicular unit vectors on the plane \(\Pi\), \(\vek e_3 = \vek m\). By step 2, \(\mat Z\vek e_1 = \vek e_1\), \(\mat Z\vek e_2 = \vek e_2\) and \(\mat Z\vek e_3 = -\vek e_3\). The columns of the matrix in this basis are thus \((1, 0, 0)\), \((0, 1, 0)\) and \((0, 0, -1)\), and the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of the diagonal, \(-1\). For the original system let \(\mat R\) be the rotation matrix (definition 2.37) with the rows \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\) and \(\vek m\). By lemma 2.38 a point \(\vek x\) has the components \(\mat R\vek x\) in the new axes, and from the new components we get the old ones with \(\mat R^\T\). So in the original system \(\mat Z = \mat R^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\,\mat R\), and by theorem 2.52 \(\det\mat Z = \det\mat R^\T\cdot(-1)\cdot\det\mat R = -\det(\mat R^\T\mat R) = -\det\mat I = -1\).

Source: The lemma is ours. Every rotation matrix of definition 2.37 is orthogonal (lemma 2.40), and the definition includes mirrored axes. The determinant tells the two cases apart (section 2.10): \(+1\) for a rotation, \(-1\) for a reflection.

A reflection is thus like a rotation, except that it has determinant \(-1\): it preserves lengths and angles but not orientation. A right-handed system of axes turns into a left-handed one in the mirror. For a rotation, chapter 2 told us how the components of tensors change. For a reflection the same rule, with \(\mat Z\) in place of \(\mat Q\), does the same job.

Now we load the bracket. On both shoulders we put the same vertical force downward, one on each shoulder: the mirror shows on the left shoulder exactly the force that we see on the right shoulder in reality, so the image of the load is the load itself. What we see in the mirror is the same bracket with the same wall. We say that the load is symmetric. If, however, we turn the left force upward, so that it pulls the shoulder, the mirror image of the right force, that is a force downward on the left shoulder, is not the load that is there but its opposite. We say that the load is antisymmetric (protisimetrična obremenitev): the prefix anti- is Greek and means against, antisymmetric is what would become symmetric if we reversed its sign. The mirror image of the same bracket is again the same bracket, only the load is the opposite.

For a body that is not just a bracket we must say this for all data of the boundary value problem from chapter 11: for the body, for the part of the surface with a prescribed displacement, for the part with a prescribed force, for the material and for three kinds of load, body force, surface force and prescribed displacement.

Definition 19.3 Symmetric and antisymmetric problem (simetričen in protisimetričen problem)

Let a boundary value problem of elasticity be given (definition 11.1) with a body \(V\), a part of the surface \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) with a prescribed displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\), a part \(S_{\mathrm{t}}\) with a prescribed surface force \(\bar{\vek t}\), a body force \(\vek b\) and a homogeneous material (definition 3.5). Let \(\mat Z\) be the reflection in the plane \(\Pi\) (definition 19.1).

The structure (body, supports and material) is mirror symmetric with respect to the plane \(\Pi\) (zrcalno simetrična glede na ravnino \(\Pi\)) if:

  1. \(\mat Z(V) = V\), \(\mat Z(S_{\mathrm{u}}) = S_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(\mat Z(S_{\mathrm{t}}) = S_{\mathrm{t}}\),
  2. \(\Pi\) is a plane of symmetry of the material (definition 3.20).

The load is symmetric (simetrična obremenitev) if for every point \(\vek x\)

\[\mat Z\,\vek b(\mat Z\vek x) = \vek b(\vek x) , \qquad \mat Z\,\bar{\vek t}(\mat Z\vek x) = \bar{\vek t}(\vek x) \ \ (\vek x \in S_{\mathrm{t}}) , \qquad \mat Z\,\bar{\vek u}(\mat Z\vek x) = \bar{\vek u}(\vek x) \ \ (\vek x \in S_{\mathrm{u}}) , \tag{19.2}\]

and antisymmetric (antisymmetric load) if the same holds with a minus sign on the right side of all three equations. The boundary value problem is a symmetric (antisymmetric) problem if the structure is mirror symmetric and the load is symmetric (antisymmetric).

Source: COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69): symmetry of geometry, supports and elastic properties with respect to a plane, an antisymmetric load is symmetric "except that the loads must be reversed". ONATE1 §9.6.1 (PDF p. 346): an antisymmetric load is a system of forces such that reflecting the structure with the load and reversing the sign of all forces gives a match. BARBER §5.2.3 (PDF p. 81): the load is split into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part. The notation with the matrix \(\mat Z\) is ours.

What does the second requirement for the structure mean? For an isotropic material every plane is a plane of symmetry. By theorem 3.11, Hooke's law (3.13) holds with the same \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) in every rectangular Cartesian system, so also in the system with the axis \(z\) along the normal of the plane. When we reverse this axis, only the components with one index \(z\) change sign (lemma 3.21), the trace \(\tr\ten\varepsilon\) stays the same, and in the equations \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 2\mu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yz}}\) the sign changes on both sides at once. The law therefore has the same constants in both systems. For an orthotropic material (definition 3.23, section 3.4.4) there are three such planes, perpendicular to the axes of the material. A material with inclined fibres has no plane perpendicular to the axis \(y\).

Now we come to the question for which we wrote all this. What can we say about displacements if the problem is symmetric? Let us see what would happen if we read the solution \(\vek u\) off the mirror: at the point \(\vek x\) we take the displacement that the solution has at the point \(\mat Z\vek x\) and reflect it, \(\mat Z\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\). The mirror changes nothing that matters for equilibrium: not distances, not the material, not the load, and a piece of a body in equilibrium under forces is, once reflected, in equilibrium under the reflected forces, since the laws of equilibrium are the same in a left-handed and a right-handed system of axes. So the reflected displacement should solve the same problem. The solution of a boundary value problem is unique by theorem 11.4, so the mirror image of the solution must be the solution itself. For an antisymmetric load the calculation is the same with a reversed sign. We now carry out this idea on components.

Theorem 19.4 Symmetry of the solution of the boundary value problem (simetrija rešitve robnega problema)

Let the boundary value problem of elasticity be symmetric or antisymmetric with respect to the plane \(\Pi\) with the reflection \(\mat Z\) (definition 19.3). Let the material be linearly elastic (definition 3.3) with a strain energy density that is positive for every small strain that is not zero, and let the only small rigid motion that vanishes on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) be the field \(\vek 0\). Let \(\vek u\) be the solution. Then:

  1. for a symmetric problem \(\vek u(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\), for an antisymmetric one \(\vek u(\vek x) = -\mat Z\,\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\), for every point \(\vek x\) of the body,
  2. strains and stresses have the same property: \(\ten\varepsilon(\vek x) = \pm\mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\) and \(\ten\sigma(\vek x) = \pm\mat Z\,\ten\sigma(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\), with the plus sign for a symmetric and the minus sign for an antisymmetric problem,
  3. at a point \(\vek x\) of the plane \(\Pi\) we have \(\vek u(\vek x)\cdot\vek m = 0\) for a symmetric problem, and for an antisymmetric one \(\vek u(\vek x)\) is parallel to \(\vek m\).

Item 2 holds also without the hypothesis on rigid motions, that is also when some small rigid motion other than \(\vek 0\) vanishes on \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\).

Proof

We must show that the field \(\tilde{\vek u}(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\) solves the boundary value problem with the data \((\pm\vek b, \pm\bar{\vek t}, \pm\bar{\vek u})\), and use uniqueness.

  1. The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is defined on \(V\), because \(\mat Z(V) = V\), and is continuously differentiable as a composition of \(\vek u\) with a linear map. By the chain rule \(\partial\tilde u_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{c}} Z_{\mathrm{ia}}\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{a}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{c}})(\mat Z\vek x)\,Z_{\mathrm{cj}}\), which we write \(\nabla\tilde{\vek u}(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\nabla\vek u(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\).
  2. Since \(\mat Z^\T = \mat Z\) (lemma 19.2), \((\mat Z\,\mat G\,\mat Z)^\T = \mat Z\,\mat G^\T\mat Z\) for every matrix \(\mat G\). Hence the tensor of small strains, the symmetric part of the gradient, is \(\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\).
  3. We choose a coordinate system with the axis \(z\) along \(\vek m\), in which \(\mat Z = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\) (lemma 19.2, item 4). By lemma 3.21 the components of strain and stress in the system with the axis \(z\) reversed are \(\mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Z\) and \(\mat Z\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Z\). By definition 3.20 the material has the same elastic constants in both systems. Let \(\mathcal{C}\) denote the map \(\ten\varepsilon \mapsto \ten\sigma\) of Hooke's law. The same constants mean: \(\ten\sigma = \mathcal{C}(\ten\varepsilon)\) implies \(\mat Z\,\ten\sigma\,\mat Z = \mathcal{C}(\mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Z)\). This is an equation between tensors, so it holds in every system, and at every point, because the material is homogeneous. The stresses of the field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) at the point \(\vek x\) are therefore \(\mathcal{C}(\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}(\vek x)) = \mathcal{C}(\mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z) = \mat Z\,\ten\sigma(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\).
  4. Equilibrium. The derivative of the stress \(\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek x) = \sum_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}} Z_{\mathrm{ia}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}(\mat Z\vek x)\,Z_{\mathrm{jb}}\) with respect to \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the sum over \(j\) give \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \partial\tilde\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b},\mathrm{c}} Z_{\mathrm{ia}}\,(\partial_{\mathrm{c}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}})(\mat Z\vek x)\,\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Z_{\mathrm{cj}}Z_{\mathrm{jb}} = \sum_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}} Z_{\mathrm{ia}}\,(\partial_{\mathrm{b}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}})(\mat Z\vek x)\), since \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Z_{\mathrm{cj}}Z_{\mathrm{jb}} = (\mat Z^2)_{\mathrm{cb}} = \delta_{\mathrm{cb}}\). Because \(\vek u\) satisfies the equations of equilibrium at the point \(\mat Z\vek x\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{b}}\partial_{\mathrm{b}}\sigma_{\mathrm{ab}}(\mat Z\vek x) = -b_{\mathrm{a}}(\mat Z\vek x)\), so \(\operatorname{div}\tilde{\ten\sigma}(\vek x) + \mat Z\,\vek b(\mat Z\vek x) = \vek 0\). The field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) has the body force \(\tilde{\vek b}(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\vek b(\mat Z\vek x)\).
  5. Surface force. A reflection is an isometry that maps the body onto the body, so it maps the outward normal at the point \(\vek x\) of the surface onto the outward normal at the point \(\mat Z\vek x\), \(\vek n(\mat Z\vek x) = \mat Z\,\vek n(\vek x)\) (the normal is perpendicular to the tangent plane and points out of the body, and an isometry preserves both). For \(\vek x\in S_{\mathrm{t}}\), by step 3 and by Cauchy's formula for \(\vek u\) at the point \(\mat Z\vek x\in S_{\mathrm{t}}\), the traction vector of the field \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is \(\tilde{\ten\sigma}(\vek x)\,\vek n(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\ten\sigma(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\,\vek n(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\ten\sigma(\mat Z\vek x)\,\vek n(\mat Z\vek x) = \mat Z\,\bar{\vek t}(\mat Z\vek x)\).
  6. Displacement. For \(\vek x\in S_{\mathrm{u}}\) we have \(\mat Z\vek x\in S_{\mathrm{u}}\), so \(\tilde{\vek u}(\vek x) = \mat Z\,\bar{\vek u}(\mat Z\vek x)\).
  7. By steps 4, 5 and 6, \(\tilde{\vek u}\) is the solution for the data \((\mat Z\vek b(\mat Z\,\cdot), \mat Z\bar{\vek t}(\mat Z\,\cdot), \mat Z\bar{\vek u}(\mat Z\,\cdot))\), that is by equation (19.2) for \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\) in the symmetric and for \((-\vek b, -\bar{\vek t}, -\bar{\vek u})\) in the antisymmetric problem. In the symmetric problem \(\tilde{\vek u}\) and \(\vek u\) are solutions of the same data, so by item 3 of theorem 11.4 \(\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\). In the antisymmetric problem \(-\tilde{\vek u}\) is by corollary 11.3 with \(\alpha = -1\) the solution of the data \((\vek b, \bar{\vek t}, \bar{\vek u})\), so \(-\tilde{\vek u} = \vek u\). This is item 1.
  8. Item 2: from \(\vek u = \pm\tilde{\vek u}\) and steps 2 and 3 follow \(\ten\varepsilon(\vek x) = \pm\mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon(\mat Z\vek x)\,\mat Z\) and the same for stresses.
  9. Item 3: for \(\vek x\in\Pi\) we have \(\mat Z\vek x = \vek x\) (lemma 19.2, item 2), so \(\vek u = \pm\mat Z\vek u\). With the plus sign \(\vek u = \vek u - 2\,(\vek m\cdot\vek u)\,\vek m\), so \(\vek m\cdot\vek u = 0\). With the minus sign \(\vek u = -\vek u + 2\,(\vek m\cdot\vek u)\,\vek m\), so \(\vek u = (\vek m\cdot\vek u)\,\vek m\).
  10. Without the hypothesis on rigid motions, by step 7 \(\tilde{\vek u}\) and \(\vek u\) are still solutions for the same data in a symmetric problem, and \(-\tilde{\vek u}\) and \(\vek u\) in an antisymmetric one. Item 1 of theorem 11.4 does not need this hypothesis and gives both solutions the same strains and stresses. The strains of the field \(-\tilde{\vek u}\) are \(-\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}\) by corollary 11.3, so \(\ten\varepsilon = \pm\tilde{\ten\varepsilon}\), and the same for stresses. With steps 2 and 3 this is item 2.

Source: The theorem is ours, the proof follows the idea of lemma 7.38 (the mirror image of the solution solves the same problem, uniqueness). COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69): a symmetric load on a symmetric structure gives symmetric results, without proof. Item 3 is the content of the rules S1 and A1 of COOK (PDF p. 69), which we prove in full in section 19.2.

The proof showed what the theorem needs: that the mirror maps onto itself everything that determines the problem, and that the solution is unique. Item 3 already says what happens on the plane: in symmetry the points of the plane do not leave the plane, and in antisymmetry they move only perpendicular to it. We treat this in detail in section 19.2.

What if one of the conditions is not met? Let us see that the second requirement is really needed. The composite strip that we pulled obliquely to the fibres in section 3.4.6 (figure 3.39) is a rectangle that we pull at both ends with the same stress \(\sigma = 10\) MPa, as described there: the stress is uniform along the strip, as it is further from the jaws (SADD §5.6, PDF pp. 114-115). The shape and the load are symmetric with respect to the plane that is perpendicular to the plane of the strip and runs along its middle, that is perpendicular to the axis \(\bar y\). The first requirement of the definition is met, the load is symmetric. The strip is not fixed anywhere, so it is a traction problem, \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\) is empty, and the hypothesis of the theorem on rigid motions does not hold: the strip can move as a whole. Item 2, however, holds by the theorem also without this hypothesis. The fibres are inclined (direction cosines \(c = 0{,}8\) and \(s = 0{,}6\)), so by section 3.4.6 the plane perpendicular to the axis \(\bar y\) is not a plane of symmetry of the material: the constant \(k = \bar\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\bar\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 5{,}362\cdot 10^{-5}\) MPa\(^{-1}\) has the opposite sign in the reflected system.

If the theorem held, the strain at a point of the middle plane would by item 2 be \(\ten\varepsilon = \mat Z\,\ten\varepsilon\,\mat Z\) with \(\mat Z = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, 1)\) in the axes of the strip. The component \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) has one index \(y\), so by lemma 3.21, which holds for every axis, it changes sign, \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), so \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). The solution of section 3.4.6, however, has everywhere \(\bar\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 5{,}362\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(10\) MPa, so \(\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2{,}681\cdot 10^{-4} \ne 0\). The conclusion of item 2 does not hold, and since this item does not need the hypothesis on rigid motions, the only hypothesis that fails here is the one on the material. The mirror image of the strip has fibres inclined to the other side and a shear \(-5{,}362\cdot 10^{-4}\): the mirror image of the solution is the solution of another strip, not of the same one. A symmetric shape is therefore not enough for symmetry.

For a mesh model, instead of the field \(\vek u\) we get a column of unknowns \(\vek d\). We write the same idea for a column and test it on the frame of chapter 12. Take a portal frame of steel \(E = 196\) GPa with a section \(20 \times 40\) mm, where the height of the section in the plane of the frame is \(40\) mm, so \(A = 800\) mm\(^2\) and \(I = 106\,667\) mm\(^4\), \(EA = 1{,}568\cdot 10^{8}\) N and \(EI = 20\,907\) N m\(^2\). The columns \(AB\) and \(EC\) are \(1\) m high and clamped at \(A\) and \(E\), the beam \(BC\) is \(1\) m long. We divide the columns into four elements each (definition 12.17) and the beam into eight, four on each half \(BD\) and \(DC\), and the nodes include \(B\), \(C\) and the middle of the beam \(D\). In all there are \(17\) nodes and \(51\) unknowns, of which \(6\) are prescribed and \(45\) are free.

Each node has three unknowns as in chapter 12: the horizontal displacement \(u\), the vertical displacement \(v\) and the rotation \(\varphi\) counterclockwise. We place the mirror through the middle of the beam, vertically, and the node \(B\) goes into \(C\). Let the node \(B\) move by \(u_{\mathrm{B}}\) to the right, by \(v_{\mathrm{B}}\) upward and rotate by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\). Where does its mirror image, the node \(C\), move? We reflect the displacement as a point: the horizontal component reverses direction, the vertical one stays. We reflect the rotation as a turning: a wheel that turns counterclockwise in front of the mirror turns clockwise in the mirror, because the mirror reverses orientation. The image of the node \(B\) therefore has the displacement \((-u_{\mathrm{B}}, v_{\mathrm{B}})\) and the rotation \(-\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\). Each node \(i\) has its mirror node \(\bar i\), a node on the plane has \(\bar i = i\), and there the same formula prescribes the mirror image of the displacement. We collect these rules in a matrix and state them precisely.

Definition 19.5 Mirror matrix of a frame model (zrcalna matrika modela okvirja)

Let a plane frame model (chapter 12) have nodes \(1, \dots, n\) with coordinates \((x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\), and let the plane \(\Pi\) be perpendicular to the axis \(x\), \(x = x_0\). For the node \(i\) let \(\bar i\) be the node with coordinates \((2x_0 - x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\), if it exists. The model is mirror symmetric with respect to \(\Pi\) (zrcalno simetričen glede na \(\Pi\)) if:

  1. every node has a mirror node, \(i \mapsto \bar i\),
  2. every element between the nodes \(i\) and \(j\) has a mirror element between \(\bar i\) and \(\bar j\) with the same length, \(EA\) and \(EI\), and no element crosses the plane (an element with the nodes \(i\) and \(\bar i\) is not allowed, a node must be placed on the plane),
  3. an unknown of the node \(i\) is prescribed exactly when the same unknown of the node \(\bar i\) is prescribed, and the prescribed values are zero.

The mirror matrix (zrcalna matrika) \(\mat P\) of order \(3n\) is the matrix whose \(3\times 3\) block in the row of the node \(i\) and the column of the node \(\bar i\) is \(\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, -1)\), all other blocks are zero. For a column of unknowns \(\vek d\) we thus have \((\mat P\vek d)_{\mathrm{i}} = (-u_{\bar{i}},\ v_{\bar{i}},\ -\varphi_{\bar{i}})\). It is a permutation matrix (definition 7.45) with signs: every row and every column has exactly one nonzero value, \(\pm 1\).

Source: The definition is ours, a generalization of the matrix of reflection of a node from lemma 7.36. Chapter 7 denoted the matrix by the letter \(S\), which in chapter 12 is the geometric stiffness, so we write \(\mat P\) here. For a plane \(\Pi\) in another position the blocks are orthogonal instead of diagonal, and \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\) holds in the same way. The rule \(\varphi \mapsto -\varphi\) as the reflection of a rotation is COOK §2.11 (PDF p. 69), rule S2: the rotation vector has no component in the plane of symmetry.

The matrix \(\mat P\) is symmetric and \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\). For \(\mat P^\T\) the block \((i, \bar i)\) comes from the block \((\bar i, i)\), which is the same diagonal matrix, so \(\mat P^\T = \mat P\). For \(\mat P^2\) the block \((i, i)\) is \(\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, -1)^2 = \mat I\) and all others are zero, because two reflections return a node to its place. Hence \(\mat P^\T = \mat P^{-1} = \mat P\), as for \(\mat Z\) in lemma 19.2.

In lemma 7.36 chapter 7 proved \(\mat S\,\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\mat S = \mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) for a cantilever of three bars by calculation and concluded the response from it. For an arbitrary mesh model we cannot check this by calculation for every case separately, so we first prove that \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) follows from the symmetry of the elements, and then the response.

Theorem 19.6 Symmetry of the solution of a frame model (simetrija rešitve mrežnega modela okvirja)

Let a plane frame model be mirror symmetric with respect to the plane \(\Pi\) (definition 19.5) and let theorem 12.20 have valid hypotheses, that is, let the reduced system have exactly one solution. Let \(\mat K\) be the matrix of the frame, \(\mat P\) the mirror matrix, and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) and \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\) the rows and columns of the free unknowns. Then:

  1. \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) and \(\mat P\mat K = \mat K\mat P\),
  2. \(\mat P\) maps free unknowns to free ones, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is a permutation matrix with signs, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}^2 = \mat I\) and \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\),
  3. if the column of loads of the free unknowns \(\vek f_{\mathrm{f}}\) is symmetric, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek f_{\mathrm{f}} = \vek f_{\mathrm{f}}\), the solution \(\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\) is symmetric, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\). If the load is antisymmetric, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek f_{\mathrm{f}} = -\vek f_{\mathrm{f}}\), the solution is antisymmetric, \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}} = -\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\).
Proof

We must show that reflection does not change the energy of any element, hence \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\), and then use the uniqueness of the solution, as in the proof of lemma 7.36.

  1. Let the element \(e\) lie between the nodes \(i\) and \(j\), with the unit tangent \(\vek t\) and the normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Q_{90}\,\vek t\), where \(\mat Q_{90}\) rotates by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise (local axes from lemma 12.18). The mirror element \(\bar e\) between \(\bar i\) and \(\bar j\) has the tangent \(\mat Z_2\vek t\), where \(\mat Z_2 = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1)\) is the reflection of the plane, and the normal \(\mat Q_{90}\mat Z_2\vek t = -\mat Z_2\,\mat Q_{90}\,\vek t = -\mat Z_2\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\), because the reflection reverses orientation.
  2. For the column \(\mat P\vek d\) the node \(i\) has the displacement \(\mat Z_2(u_{\bar{i}}, v_{\bar{i}})\) and the rotation \(-\varphi_{\bar{i}}\). The local components of the element \(e\) are then: along the tangent \(\vek t\cdot\mat Z_2\vek w = (\mat Z_2\vek t)\cdot\vek w\), so equal to the axial component of the element \(\bar e\) for \(\vek d\). Along the normal \(\vek n_{\mathrm{e}}\cdot\mat Z_2\vek w = (\mat Z_2\vek n_{\mathrm{e}})\cdot\vek w = -\vek n_{\bar{e}}\cdot\vek w\), so opposite to the transverse component of the element \(\bar e\). The rotation is the opposite rotation. The local column \(\bar{\vek d}_{\mathrm{e}}\) of the element \(e\) for \(\mat P\vek d\) is thus \(\mat Y\,\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}}\) with \(\mat Y = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, 1, -1, -1)\) and the local column \(\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}}\) of the element \(\bar e\) for \(\vek d\).
  3. The local stiffness matrix \(\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\) (lemma 12.18) is the same for \(e\) and \(\bar e\) (same length, \(EA\) and \(EI\)), and the axial part \(\{1, 4\}\) is not coupled to the bending part \(\{2, 3, 5, 6\}\). The matrix \(\mat Y\) reverses the sign of all bending components at once, so \(\mat Y\,\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Y = \bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\): a nonzero entry of the row \(a\) and column \(b\) gets the factor \(Y_{\mathrm{aa}}Y_{\mathrm{bb}}\), which is \(1\cdot 1\) in the axial part and \((-1)(-1)\) in the bending part, and there are no mixed entries.
  4. The energy of the element \(e\) for \(\mat P\vek d\) is \(\tfrac12\,\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}}^\T\,\mat Y\,\bar{\mat k}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Y\,\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}} = \tfrac12\,\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}}^\T\,\bar{\mat k}_{\bar{e}}\,\bar{\vek d}_{\bar{e}}\), which is the energy of the element \(\bar e\) for \(\vek d\). The map \(e \mapsto \bar e\) is a bijection of the elements, so \((\mat P\vek d)^\T\mat K\,(\mat P\vek d) = \vek d^\T\mat K\vek d\) by theorem 7.13 (the energy is the sum of the energies of the elements).
  5. The matrix \(\mat A = \mat P\mat K\mat P - \mat K\) is symmetric and \(\vek d^\T\mat A\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek d\) by step 4 and \(\mat P^\T = \mat P\). For a column \(\vek d\) with a one at the place \(i\) and zeros elsewhere we get \(A_{\mathrm{ii}} = 0\), and for a column with ones at the places \(i\) and \(j\) we get \(A_{\mathrm{ii}} + 2A_{\mathrm{ij}} + A_{\mathrm{jj}} = 2A_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\), so \(\mat A = \mat 0\). From \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) and \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\) follows \(\mat P\mat K = \mat K\mat P\). This is item 1.
  6. By item 3 of the definition an unknown is prescribed exactly when its mirror unknown is prescribed, so \(\mat P\) maps free unknowns to free ones and prescribed to prescribed. Hence \(\mat P\) has no blocks between free and prescribed unknowns, the rows and columns of the free unknowns from \(\mat P\mat K = \mat K\mat P\) give \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\), from which \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}} = \mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}^2\), and from \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\) follows \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}^2 = \mat I\). The matrix \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is a permutation matrix with signs, because it maps the set of free unknowns into itself. This is item 2.
  7. Let \(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek f_{\mathrm{f}} = \pm\vek f_{\mathrm{f}}\). Then \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,(\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}) = \mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}} = \mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek f_{\mathrm{f}} = \pm\vek f_{\mathrm{f}}\), so \(\pm\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\) solves the same reduced system as \(\vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\). By theorem 12.20 the solution is unique, so \(\pm\mat P_{\mathrm{ff}}\vek d_{\mathrm{f}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{f}}\). This is item 3.

Source: The theorem is ours. It generalizes lemma 7.36 and lemma 7.38 from one case to an arbitrary frame model. The energy argument of steps 4 and 5 is the same as in step 2 of lemma 7.38.

The proof showed where the symmetry of the elements enters: at every element and its image the axial and the bending stiffness are equal. If any element lacks this property, step 3 and with it the whole proof has a gap.

We test the theorem on the frame of chapter 12. We draw the portal frame with the clamps at \(A\) and \(E\), as before. Through the middle of the beam we draw the plane of reflection. At \(D\) on the plane we hang a weight, that is, we press on the frame with the force \(F = 500\) N downward. The force lies on the plane, so the load is symmetric by definition 19.3. The deformed shape is a mirror image. The section of both columns, under the feet seen along the column, is \(20 \times 40\) mm, the longer side in the plane of the frame. A calculation in NumPy for the \(45\) free unknowns confirms \(\mat P^2 = \mat I\), \(\mat P\mat K\mat P = \mat K\) (the largest difference of the entries is \(0\)) and \(\mat P\vek f = \vek f\). The solution: \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}2508\) mm, \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\) up to the rounding error of the computer (of the order of \(10^{-15}\) mm), \(u_{\mathrm{B}} = +0{,}00020\) mm and \(u_{\mathrm{C}} = -0{,}00020\) mm, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = -0{,}000498\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = +0{,}000498\). The mirror nodes have opposite horizontal displacements and opposite rotations, as \(\mat P\vek d = \vek d\) demands: the new number of the node \(B\) is the old number \(C\) and \((-u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}}, -\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}) = (u_{\mathrm{B}}, v_{\mathrm{B}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{B}})\). At \(D\) we have \(\bar i = i\), so \((-u_{\mathrm{D}}, v_{\mathrm{D}}, -\varphi_{\mathrm{D}}) = (u_{\mathrm{D}}, v_{\mathrm{D}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{D}})\) and \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{D}} = 0\). The largest difference between \(\mat P\vek d\) and \(\vek d\) is \(4{,}5\cdot 10^{-18}\) (in metres and radians), which is the error of the computer. The reaction at \(A\) has the horizontal component \(62{,}475\) N, the vertical \(250{,}00\) N and the moment \(-20{,}817\) N m, at \(E\) the horizontal component and the moment are opposite, \(-62{,}475\) N and \(+20{,}817\) N m, and the vertical one is the same: the mirror image of a force and a moment, as in the reflection of a displacement and a rotation.

Now we draw the same frame once more, on the right, with the same plane of reflection and the same force at \(D\). Let us turn the right column by a quarter turn about its axis, which is the same as if it had been installed sideways by mistake during manufacture. The outer shape of the frame is still mirror symmetric, only the section of the right column is now \(40 \times 20\) mm instead of \(20 \times 40\) mm. For \(40 \times 20\) mm the second moment of area in the plane of the frame is \(I = 40\cdot 20^3/12 = 26\,667\) mm\(^4\), which is four times less than before (\(106\,667/26\,667 = 4{,}0\)), and \(EI\) of the right column is \(5\,227\) N m\(^2\) instead of \(20\,907\) N m\(^2\). The mirror element of the column no longer has the same \(EI\): step 3 of the proof of the theorem does not hold, \(\mat P\mat K\mat P \ne \mat K\) (the entries \(12EI/h^3\) of the left and the right column differ by a factor \(4\)), and the solution is not symmetric. The deformed shape shows this: \(v_{\mathrm{D}} = -0{,}3227\) mm, \(1{,}29\) times more than before, and \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = +0{,}2134\) mm sideways, which is a distance of \(0{,}66\) of the deflection, not a small correction. The rotations of the beam are \(\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} = -0{,}000741\) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = +0{,}000831\), so no longer opposite. With the largest rotation \(0{,}00083\) and the largest displacement \(0{,}33\) mm on an element of length \(1\) m (ratio below \(4\cdot 10^{-4}\)) the calculation stays within the limits of the linear model, so the loss of symmetry is a property of the frame and not an error of the model.

Why does the frame move sideways? Let us look at the equilibrium of the beam as a body. Horizontally only the tops of the two columns act on it, since the force \(F\) is vertical, so the horizontal forces on the beam must add up to zero (Newton's law). By the law of action and reaction the beam acts on the top of a column with the opposite force, so the sum of the horizontal forces \(H\) that the beam exerts on the two tops is zero too, \(H_{\mathrm{L}} + H_{\mathrm{R}} = 0\). A column of length \(h = 1\) m is clamped at the bottom. By item 3 of theorem 12.20 the field in its four elements is the exact displacement of the frame. The column carries no load, neither along it nor at its interior nodes, so the shear force in it is constant over the whole height and the moment is linear, and the deflection, whose second derivative is by (5.24) the moment divided by \(EI\), is a single cubic polynomial over the whole height \(h\). The column therefore behaves as one element of length \(h\), and the force at its top for a displacement \(u\) and a rotation \(\varphi\) of the top is, by the matrix of lemma 12.18 (row 5, we look at the column from the clamp upward, the local transverse axis points in \(-x\)), \(H = 12EI\,u/h^3 + 6EI\,\varphi/h^2\). The beam is almost inextensible: \(EA\,h^2/EI = 7\,500\) and its shortening is below \(0{,}0004\) mm, so the tops of the columns have the same displacement, \(u_{\mathrm{B}} \approx u_{\mathrm{C}} = u\). From \(H_{\mathrm{L}} + H_{\mathrm{R}} = 0\) follows

\[u = -\frac{h}{2}\,\frac{EI_{\mathrm{L}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} + EI_{\mathrm{R}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}}{EI_{\mathrm{L}} + EI_{\mathrm{R}}} . \tag{19.3}\]

For equal columns and opposite rotations, \(\varphi_{\mathrm{C}} = -\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}\), we have \(u = 0\). For the turned right column \(u = -\tfrac12\,\big(20\,907\cdot(-0{,}000741) + 5\,227\cdot 0{,}000831\big)/26\,134\ \text{m} = 0{,}2133\) mm, which agrees with the calculation, \(0{,}2134\) mm. The softer right column holds the top of the beam back less, so \(|\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}| > |\varphi_{\mathrm{B}}|\), the sum \(EI_{\mathrm{L}}\varphi_{\mathrm{B}} + EI_{\mathrm{R}}\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}\) is no longer zero and the frame moves sideways. The displacement is therefore a consequence of the different stiffnesses of the columns, not of an error of the calculation.

Theorem 19.6 is thus usable only when the model is truly mirror symmetric in all three points of the definition, and it fails if the elements are not equal, as the frame with the turned column showed. In the following sections we use it for three things: the conditions on the plane (section 19.2), the split of an arbitrary load (section 19.3) and vibration, where the symmetry tells what the half model misses (section 19.4).
