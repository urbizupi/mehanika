Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.9 The bearing in the finite element model

So far we have described the bearing by a law between force and approach (section 25.7) and by its stiffness (section 25.8). Now we put it into the finite element model of a shaft from chapter 12. The difficulty is that the force of the bearing is a power of the approach, \(F = \kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,\delta^{3/2}\), so the equations of equilibrium are no longer linear and the matrix equation \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek F\) cannot simply be inverted. The section takes two steps. The first is statics: where the shaft comes to rest in the bearings. There we use Newton's method (Newtonov postopek), which replaces a nonlinear equation by a sequence of linear ones. The second is small vibration about this state: we replace the bearing by a tangent spring (tangentna vzmet) and obtain the natural frequencies. We test both steps on a rotor whose outcome we know.

25.9.1 The rotor as a model

Take a steel shaft of diameter \(40\) mm and length \(L = 0{,}8\) m, with a disk of mass \(m_{\mathrm{d}} = 50\) kg at its middle and a bearing with nine balls from section 25.8 at each end (\(D = 12{,}7\) mm, zero clearance). The steel is the book's, \(E = 196\) GPa and density \(7800\) kg/m³. The cross section gives \(EI = 24\,630{,}1\) N m² and the mass per length \(\rho A = 9{,}802\) kg/m, so the mass of the shaft is \(7{,}84\) kg. The total weight of shaft and disk is \(W = (50 + 7{,}84)\cdot 9{,}81 = 567{,}42\) N.

We name the idealizations before we use them. The shaft is an Euler-Bernoulli beam in a vertical plane, divided into four equal elements of length \(h = 0{,}2\) m (definition 12.3). The disk is a point mass at the middle node, without rotary inertia. The shaft does not spin, so there are no gyroscopic terms, which chapter 24 treats. The bearing acts only on the vertical displacement of the end node and carries no moment. The bearing supports are rigid and the bearing has no internal degrees of freedom. FPGL (PDF p. 214 to 215) also names such a model of a shaft with rigid foundations a shaft-line model. A real bearing does carry some moment, and FPGL (PDF p. 203) gives extended stiffness matrices of the bearing (Lim and Singh), which we do not use here.

We draw the shaft as a thick horizontal line of length \(0{,}8\) m, since the beam lies along it. On it we place the nodes \(1\) to \(5\) at a spacing of \(0{,}2\) m, because the finite element method of chapter 12 needs them. The distance between two neighbouring nodes is the length of an element \(h\). At the middle, in node \(3\), we fix the disk of mass \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\), because the disk sits at the middle of the shaft. Under each end we draw a bearing as a nonlinear spring: the zigzag line means a spring and the arrow across it means that its force is not proportional to the displacement. The bearing is under the shaft, because the shaft presses on it downward.

The lower picture shows what happens under the weight. The deflection curve is an orange curve below the grey straight line of the undeformed shaft. We have enlarged it vertically \(300\) times relative to the horizontal scale \(1:10\), because the deflection at the middle is only \(0{,}238\) mm, which is \(0{,}71\) cm in the drawing. Forces act on the shaft: the weight \(W\) at the middle downward and the forces \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) of the two bearings at the ends upward. Each arrow lies on the line along which the force acts, and the upward ones end at the point of action, because the bearing pushes the shaft up. The vertical displacement \(w_{\max}\) is measured from the straight line to the lowest point. The ends of the shaft move down by \(4{,}98\) µm, which is not visible at this enlargement: \(4{,}98\) µm is \(2{,}09\,\%\) of the deflection at the middle, in the drawing \(0{,}015\) cm. Why this small number nevertheless counts is shown by the section on vibration.

The unknowns are the nodal displacements and rotations \(\vek d = (v_1, \theta_1, \dots, v_5, \theta_5)^\T\), in all \(N = 10\). We measure the displacement \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) downward, because we are interested in how the shaft presses on the bearing. By theorem 12.9 the stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) is the sum of the element matrices of lemma 12.4. Since the shaft is not clamped, \(\mat K\) is singular, because moving the whole shaft does not deform it. The right-hand side \(\vek F\) consists of the nodal forces of the weight, namely by theorem 12.9 for the uniform load \(q = \rho A\,g\) on an element the force \(q h/2\) and the moment \(\pm q h^2/12\) at its two nodes, and the weight of the disk \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\,g\) at node \(3\). The bearing forces we write for each bearing separately. For the bearing of section 25.8 with zero clearance and \(n = 3/2\) we have by theorem 25.35 \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}(\tfrac12)\,K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^{3/2}\), which we write as

\[F_{\mathrm{r}}(\delta) = \kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,\delta^{3/2} , \qquad \kappa_{\mathrm{b}} = Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}\big(\tfrac12\big)\,K_{\mathrm{n}} = 2{,}549\cdot 10^{10}\ \text{N/m}^{3/2} . \tag{25.43}\]

The constant \(\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the constant \(c = Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}\,K_{\mathrm{n}}\) of section 25.8, there in units N and µm (\(c = 25{,}49\) N/µm\(^{3/2}\)), computed with Palmgren's formula (25.37) (HK (8.1), PDF p. 179). Here we write \(\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\), because we need the letter \(c\) for other quantities. Check: at \(\delta = 11{,}54\) µm equation (25.43) gives \(999{,}4\) N, which is the \(1000\) N of example 25.41. The law holds only for \(\delta > 0\), since the bearing does not pull (definition 25.19). So the vector of bearing forces \(\vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d)\) is zero everywhere except in two components, \(v_1\) and \(v_5\), where it is \(\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_{\mathrm{i}}, 0)^{3/2}\). The equilibrium of the rotor is then

\[\mat K\,\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d) = \vek F . \tag{25.44}\]

The law \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,\delta^{3/2}\) is the model with a continuous angle (theorem 25.35). At \(Z = 9\) the largest ball load differs by about \(10\,\%\) with the position of the cage (\(4{,}385\) and \(3{,}969\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\), section 25.7), so the law (25.43) has that much uncertainty too. The rotor is a test of the method, not an exact computation of a bearing.

The equilibrium (25.44) has one property that does not depend on the law of the bearing, and we prove it before we solve the nonlinear part.

Lemma 25.45 The bearing forces add up to the weight (vsota sil ležajev je teža)

Let \(\vek d\) be a solution of equation (25.44). Then the sum of the forces of the two bearings equals the total weight of the rotor, \(\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_1, 0)^{3/2} + \kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_5, 0)^{3/2} = W\).

Proof

We must show that we move the whole shaft without deforming it, and that this turns the equation of equilibrium into a sum of forces. Let \(\vek t\) be the column with ones at the places \(v_{\mathrm{i}}\) and zeros at the places \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\), that is, the displacement of the whole shaft by \(1\) downward.

  1. For every element \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(1, 0, 1, 0)^\T = \vek 0\): by lemma 12.4 each row of the matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) gives the sum \(12 - 12 = 0\), \(6h - 6h = 0\), \(-12 + 12 = 0\), \(6h - 6h = 0\). By theorem 12.9 therefore \(\mat K\,\vek t = \vek 0\), and since \(\mat K\) is symmetric, also \(\vek t^\T\mat K = \vek 0^\T\).
  2. We multiply equation (25.44) by the row \(\vek t^\T\): \(\vek t^\T\mat K\,\vek d + \vek t^\T\vek f_{\mathrm{b}} = \vek t^\T\vek F\). The first term is zero by step 1.
  3. The sum \(\vek t^\T\vek F\) is the sum of the nodal forces. Each element contributes \(q h/2 + q h/2 = q h\) (moments do not count, since they stand at the places with a zero in \(\vek t\)), four elements \(q L\), the disk \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\,g\), so \(\vek t^\T\vek F = \rho A\,g\,L + m_{\mathrm{d}}\,g = W\).
  4. The vector \(\vek f_{\mathrm{b}}\) has nonzero components only at \(v_1\) and \(v_5\), so \(\vek t^\T\vek f_{\mathrm{b}} = f_{\mathrm{b},1} + f_{\mathrm{b},5}\). The claim follows from step 2.

Source: The proof is ours, with the help of theorem 12.9. FPGL (PDF p. 214, equation 5.109) writes the assembled equations of motion of the shaft-line model, whose static part with linear bearings has the form of equation (25.44).

The lemma says that the two bearings together carry the weight whatever their stiffness. In our case the load and the supports are symmetric, so each bearing carries \(W/2 = 283{,}71\) N (section 25.8 gives the approach \(4{,}99\) µm at \(284\) N). From equation (25.43) follows \(\delta^* = (W/(2\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}))^{2/3} = 4{,}9847\) µm and the tangent stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\sqrt{\delta^*} = 8{,}5375\cdot 10^7\) N/m. So we have obtained the solution in closed form. This is a consequence of the shaft on two bearings being statically determinate and is not general. On two bearings the balance of forces and the balance of moments already fix both forces, also when the bearings are not equal. With a third bearing there are three unknown forces and only two equations of equilibrium, so we do not know the force of each bearing until we know the bending of the shaft and the displacements of the bearings. The method that can do this we now develop on the example with a known outcome.

25.9.2 Newton's method

We solve a nonlinear equation \(g(x) = 0\) like someone walking along a slope: at the current point he replaces the curve by its tangent and looks where the tangent cuts the axis. In a rotor with two equal bearings the displacements of both bearings are equal, so there is one unknown, the displacement \(x\). The bearings together carry the weight \(W\) (lemma 25.45), so \(x\) is the solution of the equation

\[g(x) = 2\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,x^{3/2} - W = 0 , \qquad x > 0 . \tag{25.45}\]

The function \(g(x)\) is the difference between the force that the two bearings give at the displacement \(x\) and the weight. The zero \(x^* = \delta^*\) we know from the previous section, so we shall see the error of the method. As at a board we draw first the axes: horizontally \(x\) in µm, vertically \(g\) in N. On them we lay the curve \(g\), which equals \(-W\) at \(x = 0\) and grows with \(x\) as the power \(3/2\). Its zero is the root \(x^*\).

Definition 25.46 Newton's method (Newtonov postopek)

Let \(\vek r\colon \mathbb R^N \to \mathbb R^N\) be a differentiable vector function whose solution \(\vek r(\vek u) = \vek 0\) we seek. We call the function \(\vek r(\vek u)\) the residual (ostanek), because it is what remains of the equation when we insert the approximation \(\vek u\). The Jacobian matrix (matrika odvodov) \(\mat J(\vek u)\) is the square matrix of order \(N\) with entries \(J_{\mathrm{ij}} = \partial r_{\mathrm{i}}/\partial u_{\mathrm{j}}\). Newton's method with the start \(\vek u_0\) is the sequence

\[\vek u_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek u_{\mathrm{k}} - \mat J(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}})^{-1}\,\vek r(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}) , \tag{25.46}\]

that is, \(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \vek u_{\mathrm{k}} + \vek\Delta_{\mathrm{k}}\), where \(\vek\Delta_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the solution of the linear system \(\mat J(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}})\,\vek\Delta_{\mathrm{k}} = -\vek r(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}})\), if \(\mat J(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}})\) is invertible. For \(N = 1\) we have \(r = g\), \(\mat J = g'\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - g(x_{\mathrm{k}})/g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})\).

Source: KREYSZIG (PDF p. 827 to 828, algorithm Newton and table 19.1) for one unknown, COOK (PDF p. 611 to 612) for a system. KREYSZIG (PDF p. 827) also says that the method is called Newton-Raphson as well, after Raphson, who published a similar method.

The geometric meaning is the tangent: \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is the intersection of the tangent to the curve at the point \((x_{\mathrm{k}}, g(x_{\mathrm{k}}))\) with the axis \(x\). The tangent has the equation \(y = g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) + g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})(x - x_{\mathrm{k}})\) and its zero we get from \(0 = g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) + g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} - x_{\mathrm{k}})\), which is the definition for \(N = 1\). The same notation holds for a system, because the linear approximation of a vector function is \(\vek r(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}}) + \mat J(\vek u_{\mathrm{k}})(\vek u - \vek u_{\mathrm{k}})\). In the rotor \(\vek r(\vek d) = \mat K\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d) - \vek F\) and, since each bearing has only one component, \(\mat J = \mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d)\), where \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the diagonal matrix with the tangent stiffness of the bearing \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_{\mathrm{i}}, 0)^{1/2}\) at the places \(v_1\) and \(v_5\) (the tangent stiffness matrix of the bearing from definition 25.39 and theorem 25.40). The function \(\max(v, 0)^{3/2}\) has the derivative \(\tfrac32\max(v, 0)^{1/2}\) everywhere, also at \(v = 0\), where it is zero.

Let us take the steps on figure 25.34. We start at a very small displacement \(x_0 = 0{,}1\) µm, where the two bearings together carry only \(1{,}6\) N. At \(x_0\) the residual is \(g(x_0) = -565{,}8\) N, so the first tangent meets the curve below the axis and is very shallow, because \(g'(x_0) = 3\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\sqrt{x_0} = 2{,}42\cdot 10^7\) N/m is small. A shallow tangent cuts the axis far away, at \(x_1 = 23{,}50\) µm, which is \(4{,}7\) times farther from the origin than the root. There the second tangent is much steeper and cuts the axis at \(x_2 = 9{,}36\) µm, the third at \(x_3 = 5{,}546\) µm. Then \(x_4 = 4{,}9991\) µm (\(0{,}3\,\%\) above the root), \(x_5 = 4{,}9847\) and \(x_6 = 4{,}9847\) µm. The start at \(20\) µm gives \(8{,}3257\), \(5{,}3465\), \(4{,}9909\), \(4{,}9847\) µm, the start at \(2\) µm gives \(5{,}9129\), \(5{,}0221\), \(4{,}9847\) µm (NumPy, num-09.py). Everywhere the iterates fall from the first one on, from the right toward the root. This is no accident.

Theorem 25.47 Newton's method for a convex increasing function (Newtonov postopek za konveksno naraščajočo funkcijo)

Let \(g\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on the interval \((0, \infty)\) with \(g'(x) > 0\) and \(g''(x) > 0\) for every \(x > 0\), and let it have a zero \(x^* > 0\). Let \(x_0 > 0\) be arbitrary and \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - g(x_{\mathrm{k}})/g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})\). Let \(e_{\mathrm{k}} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - x^*\). Then:

  1. The zero \(x^*\) is the only one.
  2. For every \(k \ge 0\) we have \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} > 0\) and \(e_{\mathrm{k}+1} \ge 0\), equality only at \(e_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\).
  3. For \(k \ge 1\) we have \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le x_{\mathrm{k}}\), the sequence \((x_{\mathrm{k}})\) converges and its limit is \(x^*\).
  4. Let \(m = g'(x^*)\) and \(M\) the largest value of \(g''\) on \([x^*, x_1]\). For \(k \ge 1\) we have \(0 \le e_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le \dfrac{M}{2m}\,e_{\mathrm{k}}^2\).
Idea

The tangent of a convex curve lies below the curve, so its intersection with the axis cannot be to the left of the zero. From there on the residual is nonnegative, and so every step only decreases \(x\). The square in the error estimate comes from the second derivative in Taylor's theorem.

Proof
  1. Since \(g' > 0\), by theorem 1.84 for \(a < b\) the value \(g(b) - g(a) = g'(\xi)(b - a) > 0\), so \(g\) is strictly increasing and has at most one zero.
  2. Let \(x_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}} \ne x^*\). By theorem 8.60 for \(g\) about \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) there is \(\xi\) between \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(x^*\) such that \(0 = g(x^*) = g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) + g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})(x^* - x_{\mathrm{k}}) + \tfrac12 g''(\xi)(x^* - x_{\mathrm{k}})^2\). So \(g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) + g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})(x^* - x_{\mathrm{k}}) = -\tfrac12 g''(\xi)\,e_{\mathrm{k}}^2\). We divide by \(g'(x_{\mathrm{k}}) > 0\) and use \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - g(x_{\mathrm{k}})/g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})\): \(x^* - x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = -\dfrac{g''(\xi)}{2\,g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})}\,e_{\mathrm{k}}^2 < 0\), so \(e_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \dfrac{g''(\xi)}{2\,g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})}\,e_{\mathrm{k}}^2 > 0\). Hence \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} > x^* > 0\). At \(x_{\mathrm{k}} = x^*\) we have \(g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) = 0\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. For \(k \ge 1\) step 2 gives \(x_{\mathrm{k}} \ge x^*\), so \(g(x_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge g(x^*) = 0\) (step 1) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - g(x_{\mathrm{k}})/g'(x_{\mathrm{k}}) \le x_{\mathrm{k}}\). The sequence is therefore decreasing from \(k = 1\) on and bounded below by \(x^*\), and by theorem 1.51 it converges to a limit \(L \ge x^* > 0\). The functions \(g\) and \(g'\) are continuous at \(L\) and \(g'(L) > 0\), so from \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - g(x_{\mathrm{k}})/g'(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) in the limit follows \(L = L - g(L)/g'(L)\), so \(g(L) = 0\) and by step 1 \(L = x^*\).
  4. For \(k \ge 1\) we have \(\xi \in (x^*, x_{\mathrm{k}}) \subset [x^*, x_1]\), so \(g''(\xi) \le M\). Since \(g'' > 0\), by theorem 1.84 \(g'\) is increasing, so \(g'(x_{\mathrm{k}}) \ge g'(x^*) = m\). Step 2 gives \(e_{\mathrm{k}+1} \le M\,e_{\mathrm{k}}^2/(2m)\).

Source: The theorem and the proof are ours. The convergence of Newton's method is proved in KREYSZIG (PDF p. 829 to 830, theorem 2) as quadratic convergence near the zero by a different route.

What did the theorem show? If the curve is convex and increasing, Newton's method lands to the right of the zero after one step whatever the start \(x_0\), then falls monotonically, and the error is squared at every step. This is the reason for the sequence of figure 25.34: \(x_1 = 23{,}50\) is to the right of the root and falls from there without turning back. In the rotor \(g'' = \tfrac32\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,x^{-1/2}\) is decreasing, so \(M = g''(x^*)\) and the constant is \(M/(2m) = g''(x^*)/(2g'(x^*)) = 1/(4x^*) = 0{,}0502\) per µm. The measured ratios \(e_{\mathrm{k}+1}/e_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) from the start \(0{,}1\) µm are, at \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\), \(0{,}0128\), \(0{,}0293\), \(0{,}0459\), \(0{,}0500\) per µm, all below the bound \(0{,}0502\), and approach \(0{,}0502\) with each step: the errors \(e_1, \dots, e_5\) in µm are \(18{,}511\), \(4{,}378\), \(0{,}561\), \(0{,}01443\), \(0{,}00001\) (the initial error \(e_0 = -4{,}885\) µm is to the left of the root). So each step is not merely a few times smaller, the number of correct digits roughly doubles, as the estimate predicts. The estimate holds for the one-dimensional case. For a system of equations we do not prove it: COOK (PDF p. 612) writes that the method is not guaranteed to converge for all nonlinear problems, but in practice the residuals decrease.

The theorem requires \(x_0 > 0\) and \(g'' > 0\). Each requirement has a case where we remove it. Let us look first at a start that is not in \((0, \infty)\).

Example 25.48 A start at zero (začetek v ničli)

Let \(x_0 = 0\), which the theorem does not allow. At \(x = 0\) the tangent stiffness of the bearing is \(g'(0) = 3\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\sqrt 0 = 0\), the tangent is horizontal and never cuts the axis, and the step \(x_1 = x_0 - g(x_0)/g'(x_0)\) divides by zero. KREYSZIG says the same (PDF p. 828): if it happens that the derivative is zero at some \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) (line 2 of the algorithm), we try another start. In the system the matrix \(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek 0)\) equals \(\mat K\) and is singular, since the displacement of the whole shaft is not a deformation (lemma 25.45, step 1), so the system \(\mat J\,\vek\Delta = -\vek r\) cannot be solved. Physically: a bearing without load and without preload has no stiffness, and the shaft in such a model is supported nowhere.

So we start with a linear guess. In the rotor we take bearings of linear stiffness \(k_0 = 1{,}0\cdot 10^8\) N/m, solve \((\mat K + \mat K_0)\,\vek d_0 = \vek F\) (\(\mat K_0\) has \(k_0\) at \(v_1\) and \(v_5\)) and obtain the end of the shaft at \(2{,}8371\) µm. This is the start \(\vek d_0\).

Example 25.49 Newton's method on the rotor (Newtonov postopek na rotorju)

The rotor of section 25.9.1, \(N = 10\), residual \(\vek r = \mat K\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d) - \vek F\) and \(\mat J = \mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d)\). The start: linear bearings with \(k_0 = 1{,}0\cdot 10^8\) N/m. The displacement of the end of the shaft \(v_1\) and the norm of the residual \(\|\vek r\|\) by steps (NumPy, num-09.py). The start is a guess with linear bearings, because a start at zero fails (example 25.48):

\(k\) \(v_1\) (µm) \(\|\vek r\|\) (N)
0 \(2{,}83712\) \(228{,}9\)
1 \(5{,}35049\) \(44{,}97\)
2 \(4{,}99099\) \(0{,}7640\)
3 \(4{,}98467\) \(2{,}421\cdot 10^{-4}\)
4 \(4{,}98467\) \(2{,}44\cdot 10^{-11}\)

The quotient \(\|\vek r_{\mathrm{k}+1}\|/\|\vek r_{\mathrm{k}}\|^2\) is from the second step on \(3{,}8\cdot 10^{-4}\), \(4{,}1\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(4{,}2\cdot 10^{-4}\) per N, which is nearly constant and shows quadratic convergence also for a system with ten unknowns. The first step overshoots the solution (\(5{,}35\) against \(4{,}98\) µm), as theorem 25.47 predicts for one unknown, and then it approaches from the right. The displacement of the end of the shaft \(4{,}98467\) µm agrees with the closed solution \(\delta^* = 4{,}9847\) µm of lemma 25.45, which is a check that the system solves the right equation. The deflection at the middle is \(0{,}2382\) mm. Independently we get it as the sum of the deflections of a simply supported beam: the force of the disk \(490{,}5\) N gives \(F L^3/(48 EI) = 0{,}2124\) mm (equation (5.57)), the weight of the shaft \(q L = 76{,}93\) N gives \(5qL^4/(384 EI) = 0{,}0208\) mm (equation (5.38)), and the bearings lower the whole shaft by another \(0{,}0050\) mm. The sum \(0{,}2382\) mm agrees. The Jacobian matrix \(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) agrees with numerical differences: the largest relative difference is \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-9}\).

The theorem requires \(g'' > 0\). If we remove it, convergence stops, as the next example shows.

Example 25.50 A concave function: the cube root (konkavna funkcija: kubični koren)

On the axes we draw the curve \(g(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) with the zero \(x^* = 0\). It is increasing (\(g' = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3} > 0\)) but concave, \(g'' = -\tfrac29 x^{-5/3} < 0\) for \(x > 0\). The step is \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - \sqrt[3]{x_{\mathrm{k}}}\big/\big(\tfrac13\,x_{\mathrm{k}}^{-2/3}\big) = x_{\mathrm{k}} - 3\,x_{\mathrm{k}} = -2\,x_{\mathrm{k}}\). From \(x_0 = 0{,}1\) we come to \(x_1 = -0{,}2\) along the first tangent, to \(x_2 = 0{,}4\) along the second and to \(x_3 = -0{,}8\) along the third: each step doubles the distance from the zero and changes side (NumPy, num-09.py: \(0{,}1,\ -0{,}2,\ 0{,}4,\ -0{,}8,\ 1{,}6\)). In theorem 25.47 step 2 fails, because \(g''(\xi) < 0\) and so \(e_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) is not nonnegative: the tangent of a concave curve lies above the curve and cuts the axis on the other side of the zero. The example also removes the condition \(x^* > 0\), since the zero of the cube root is at \(0\). That concavity alone already causes the failure is shown by \(g(x) = \ln x\) on \((0, \infty)\), which fulfils every condition of the theorem except \(g'' > 0\): \(g' = 1/x > 0\), \(g'' = -1/x^2 < 0\), zero \(x^* = 1\). The step is \(x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = x_{\mathrm{k}} - \ln x_{\mathrm{k}}/(1/x_{\mathrm{k}}) = x_{\mathrm{k}}(1 - \ln x_{\mathrm{k}})\), and with the start \(x_0 = 3\) it gives \(x_1 = 3(1 - \ln 3) = -0{,}296\), which is no longer in \((0, \infty)\), where \(\ln\) is defined. Point 2 of the theorem fails and the method stops (NumPy). Without the condition \(g'' > 0\) convergence does not follow from \(g' > 0\) and a zero. Our bearing fulfils this condition, since \(g''(x) = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,x^{-1/2} > 0\).

For the bearing \(g'' > 0\) is proved, so we use Newton's method with a start that is not zero. For a system we have no proof, so we check each step with the residual.

25.9.3 Small vibration about equilibrium

Once we know the static state \(\vek d_0\), we are interested in the natural frequencies of the rotor about it. The equations of motion are Lagrange's equations of a system with a potential: the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\) with the mass of the shaft (the consistent mass matrix by lemma 12.43) and with the mass of the disk at the place \(v_3\). The potential is

\[V(\vek d) = \tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d + \sum_{\mathrm{b}}\tfrac25\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_{\mathrm{b}}, 0)^{5/2} - \vek F^\T\vek d , \tag{25.47}\]

the sum runs over the components of the bearings \(v_1\) and \(v_5\). The first term is the strain energy of the shaft (theorem 12.9), the second the energy of the two bearings, since its derivative with respect to \(v_{\mathrm{b}}\) is exactly \(\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\max(v_{\mathrm{b}}, 0)^{3/2}\), the third the work of the weight. The derivative \(\partial V/\partial\vek d = \mat K\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}} - \vek F\) is the left side of equation (25.44) without its right side, so the equilibrium is a stationary point of the energy.

Let us ask what this means for small departures. The bearing is not linear, so it does not behave like one spring. In statics the secant (sekanta), \(F/\delta\), applies, in small vibration the tangent (tangenta), as in figure 25.30 of section 25.8. The next theorem says exactly when and how much.

Theorem 25.51 Linearization of the bearing and the natural frequencies of the rotor (linearizacija ležaja in lastne frekvence rotorja)

Let the rotor of section 25.9.1 have a symmetric stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) and a symmetric positive definite mass matrix \(\mat M\) of order \(N\), with bearings with the law (25.43) at the components \(v_1\) and \(v_5\). Let \(\vek d_0\) be an equilibrium (25.44) with \(v_{1,0} > 0\) and \(v_{5,0} > 0\). Let \(k_{\mathrm{b}}(v) = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\sqrt v\) and \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) the diagonal matrix with \(k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{1,0})\) and \(k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{5,0})\) at \(v_1\) and \(v_5\) and zeros elsewhere. Then:

  1. The equilibrium \(\vek d_0\) is a stationary point of the potential (25.47), its matrix of second derivatives at \(\vek d_0\) is \(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\), and the motion \(\vek x = \vek d - \vek d_0\) satisfies \(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek x} + (\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}})\,\vek x = \vek R\) with a remainder \(\vek R\) that has nonzero components only at the bearings.
  2. For \(|x_{\mathrm{b}}| \le v_{\mathrm{b},0}/2\) we have \(|R_{\mathrm{b}}| \le \dfrac{3\sqrt 2}{8}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,v_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1/2}\,x_{\mathrm{b}}^2\), so relative to the tangent term \(k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0})\,x_{\mathrm{b}}\) at most \(\dfrac{\sqrt 2}{4}\,|x_{\mathrm{b}}|/v_{\mathrm{b},0}\).
  3. The matrix \(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is symmetric and positive definite. By theorem 10.13 the eigenproblem \((\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}})\,\vek\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\,\vek\varphi\) has \(N\) positive eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the natural circular frequencies of the linear model are \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} = \sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}\).
  4. Let the bearings be replaced by springs of stiffness \(c\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\) and let \(0 < c_1 \le c_2\). Then every natural frequency at \(c_2\) is at least as large as at \(c_1\).
Idea

The equations of motion follow from the potential (theorem 10.2), which is smooth enough as long as the displacements of the bearings are positive. We subtract the equilibrium from the motion and what remains is the difference of the bearing force, which we estimate with Taylor's theorem in one variable, because each bearing depends on one component. The positivity of the matrix we show by the energy.

Proof
  1. Let \(U\) be the set of columns \(\vek d\) with \(v_1 > 0\) and \(v_5 > 0\). It is open, and on it \(\max(v_{\mathrm{b}}, 0)^{5/2} = v_{\mathrm{b}}^{5/2}\), which is three times continuously differentiable at \(v_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\). The potential \(V\) is therefore three times continuously differentiable on \(U\), and the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek d}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek d}\) has a constant matrix. By theorem 10.2 (point 2, constant \(\mat M\), no dampers, \(\vek Q = \vek 0\) because the weight is in the potential) the motion in \(U\) is a solution of \(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek d} + \mat K\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d) = \vek F\), since \(\partial V/\partial\vek d = \mat K\vek d + \vek f_{\mathrm{b}} - \vek F\). At \(\vek d_0\) this derivative is zero. The matrix of second derivatives of the potential is \(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\), since \(\partial^2 V/\partial v_{\mathrm{b}}^2 = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{b}}}\) and the mixed second derivatives of the bearing terms are zero. For \(\vek d = \vek d_0 + \vek x\) we subtract the equilibrium: \(\mat M\,\ddot{\vek x} + \mat K\vek x + \big(\vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d_0 + \vek x) - \vek f_{\mathrm{b}}(\vek d_0)\big) = \vek 0\). For each bearing the difference is \(f_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0} + x_{\mathrm{b}}) - f_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0}) = k_{\mathrm{b}}\,x_{\mathrm{b}} - R_{\mathrm{b}}\) with \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0})\) and \(R_{\mathrm{b}} = k_{\mathrm{b}}\,x_{\mathrm{b}} - \big(f_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0} + x_{\mathrm{b}}) - f_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{\mathrm{b},0})\big)\) for \(f_{\mathrm{b}}(v) = \kappa_{\mathrm{b}} v^{3/2}\). This is the equation of the claim.
  2. By theorem 8.60 we have \(R_{\mathrm{b}} = -\tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{b}}''(c)\,x_{\mathrm{b}}^2\) with \(c\) between \(v_{\mathrm{b},0}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{b},0} + x_{\mathrm{b}}\). At \(|x_{\mathrm{b}}| \le v_{\mathrm{b},0}/2\) we have \(c \ge v_{\mathrm{b},0}/2\). From \(f_{\mathrm{b}}'' = \tfrac34\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,v^{-1/2}\) follows \(|R_{\mathrm{b}}| \le \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,(v_{\mathrm{b},0}/2)^{-1/2}x_{\mathrm{b}}^2 = \tfrac{3\sqrt 2}{8}\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,v_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1/2}x_{\mathrm{b}}^2\). We divide by \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\,|x_{\mathrm{b}}| = \tfrac32\,\kappa_{\mathrm{b}}\,v_{\mathrm{b},0}^{1/2}|x_{\mathrm{b}}|\): the quotient is \(\tfrac{3\sqrt 2/8}{3/2}\,|x_{\mathrm{b}}|/v_{\mathrm{b},0} = \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{4}\,|x_{\mathrm{b}}|/v_{\mathrm{b},0}\).
  3. Symmetry follows from the symmetry of \(\mat K\) and the diagonality of \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\). The energy is \(\vek y^\T(\mat K + \mat K_{\mathrm{b}})\vek y = \int_0^L EI\,(v_{\mathrm{h}}'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x + k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{1,0})\,y_1^2 + k_{\mathrm{b}}(v_{5,0})\,y_5^2 \ge 0\) for every column \(\vek y\) (point 3 of theorem 12.9, the field \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) from \(\vek y\)). If it is zero, then \(v_{\mathrm{h}}'' = 0\), so \(v_{\mathrm{h}}\) is a linear function, and \(y_1 = y_5 = 0\) gives \(v_{\mathrm{h}}(0) = v_{\mathrm{h}}(L) = 0\), so \(v_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\). The Hermite functions are linearly independent (definition 12.3), so \(\vek y = \vek 0\). The matrix is therefore positive definite, and by theorem 10.13 the eigenvalues are real and, by the minimum of the quotient, positive.
  4. The difference \((\mat K + c_2\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}) - (\mat K + c_1\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}) = (c_2 - c_1)\,\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is diagonal with nonnegative entries, so positive semidefinite, and therefore by corollary 10.30 (point 1) no eigenvalue decreases. A frequency is the root of an eigenvalue, so every frequency is nondecreasing as well.

Source: The theorem is ours, from theorems 10.2, 8.60 and 10.13. FPGL (PDF p. 214 to 215) describes adding the stiffness of a bearing to the assembled matrix, and FPGL (PDF p. 216) says that bearing stiffnesses may depend on the spin speed, which we do not consider here.

What did the theorem show? In vibration about a state the bearing is replaced by a spring with the tangent stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\), and the error of this replacement is estimated by the departure \(x_{\mathrm{b}}\) relative to the static displacement \(v_{\mathrm{b},0}\), whatever the frequency. For example: a departure of \(10\,\%\) of the static displacement, that is, \(0{,}5\) µm at \(4{,}98\) µm, gives an error of at most \(3{,}5\,\%\) of the change of force. The computation: the force of the bearing at \(v_0 = 4{,}98\) µm is \(283{,}71\) N, at a \(10\,\%\) larger displacement \(327{,}32\) N, and the linear approximation \(326{,}27\) N, so \(1{,}05\) N of error at a change of \(42{,}56\) N, that is, \(2{,}5\,\%\). At \(50\,\%\) the error of the approximation is \(-4{,}74\,\%\) of the force, which is \(11{,}6\,\%\) of the change, and the estimate of the theorem is \(17{,}7\,\%\). The bound was exceeded in no case. If in vibration the bearing departs upward by more than the static displacement, \(x_{\mathrm{b}} < -v_{\mathrm{b},0}\), the bearing lets the shaft go (it does not pull) and the vector \(\vek d\) leaves the set \(U\) of the theorem: the linear model does not hold there.

We test the order of magnitude of the frequency with a model with one mass. Let a massless shaft carry the disk \(m_{\mathrm{d}} = 50\) kg at the middle. Under a force \(P\) at the middle the shaft on rigid pins (togi členki) bends by \(P/k\) with the stiffness \(k = 48EI/L^3 = 2{,}309\cdot 10^6\) N/m (equation (5.57)). The bearings add compliance: each carries \(P/2\) and yields by \(P/(2k_{\mathrm{b}})\), so the compliance of the whole is \(1/k + 1/(2k_{\mathrm{b}})\). The frequency \(\sqrt{k_{\text{c}}/m_{\mathrm{d}}}/(2\pi)\) with the stiffness \(k_{\text{c}} = k\) for pins and \(k_{\text{c}} = \big(1/k + 1/(2k_{\mathrm{b}})\big)^{-1}\) for bearings gives \(34{,}20\) Hz for the pins and \(33{,}97\) Hz for the bearings (num-09.py). The mass of the shaft (\(7{,}84\) kg) is missing in this model, so the numbers are higher than the \(32{,}97\) and \(32{,}74\) Hz of the finite element model below, but the difference between pins and bearings is \(0{,}7\,\%\) in both. So the first frequency is determined mainly by the bending stiffness of the shaft, and the bearings lower it only a little.

Example 25.52 Natural frequencies of the rotor (lastne frekvence rotorja)

The rotor of section 25.9.1, static state \(v_1 = v_5 = 4{,}9847\) µm, \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 8{,}5375\cdot 10^7\) N/m, which is \(37\) times more than \(48EI/L^3 = 2{,}309\cdot 10^6\) N/m. NumPy solves the eigenproblem of theorem 25.51.

We draw the first mode. The horizontal grey line is the shaft at rest, the blue curve above it its shape at the largest displacement, normalized to \(1\) at the middle. The disk moves the most, the ends of the shaft hardly at all, and the nodal displacements are \(-0{,}014,\ -0{,}693,\ -1{,}000,\ -0{,}693,\ -0{,}014\) (the sign is arbitrary). The frequency beside the curve is \(32{,}74\) Hz. The grey dashed curve is the same mode with rigid pins, where the ends of the shaft stay at zero, and has \(32{,}97\) Hz, which is \(0{,}7\,\%\) more. In the second mode the middle is at rest and the two halves of the shaft move in opposite directions, the nodal displacements are \(-0{,}137,\ -1{,}000,\ 0,\ 1{,}000,\ 0{,}137\). Rigid pins (dashed curve) give \(494{,}07\) Hz, the bearings \(472{,}24\) Hz, so \(4{,}4\,\%\) less. Here the ends of the shaft move noticeably at the bearings, in the first mode they do not: an elastic bearing allows a displacement at the support that a rigid pin prevents, and the second mode uses it more (\(0{,}137\)) than the first (\(0{,}014\)). So the bearing lowers the second frequency more than the first.

tangent stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) \(32{,}74\) \(472{,}24\)

With four elements the frequencies are \(32{,}74\) and \(472{,}24\) Hz, with eight \(32{,}74\) and \(470{,}62\) Hz (rigid pins \(32{,}97\) and \(492{,}26\) Hz). The higher mode drops by \(0{,}3\,\%\) with the finer mesh. This is expected, because the strain energy of the shaft and the energy of the springs are terms of the potential, so the Ritz solution is an upper bound, as in corollary 13.56 for a beam without springs. At \(0{,}01\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(0{,}5\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(1{,}5\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\) the frequencies grow, as theorem 25.51 (point 4) requires.

The last row of the table shows what the theorem does not promise. The tangent stiffness depends on the load: a bearing with \(1000\) N instead of \(283{,}7\) N has \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 1{,}2993\cdot 10^8\) N/m, which is \(52\,\%\) more, and the second frequency \(479{,}65\) instead of \(472{,}24\) Hz. It moves the first frequency by only \(0{,}08\) Hz. So the linear model is not independent of statics: it always requires a previously computed static state. When the bearing is without load and without preload, \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 0\) and the bearing does not support the shaft, as at the start at zero (example 25.48). The row \(0{,}01\,k_{\mathrm{b}}\) is not that case, it only illustrates how fast the frequencies fall (\(f_2\) to \(125{,}97\) Hz) when the bearing softens.

The two steps of the section are therefore the following. Statics (equation (25.44)) is nonlinear, and we solve it by Newton's method (definition 25.46), in which the stiffness matrix of the bearing enters as the tangent term, and each step is a linear system of the same look as in chapter 12. Vibration about the solution (theorem 25.51) uses the same tangent matrix and gives the eigenproblem of chapter 10. Statics with bearings that have no closed solution, and vibration with gyroscopic terms and spin speeds we do not solve here, but chapter 24 will carry them over to the same matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{b}}\).
