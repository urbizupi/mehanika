Chapter 28 Flexible robot links

28.3 Kinematics of the link

In section 28.2 we wrote the position of every point of a flexible link with three parts: the origin \(\vek o\) of the floating frame, the angle \(\theta\) of its direction, and the elastic coordinates \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) that carry the bending. Now we ask how fast such a point moves and what accelerates it. We need the answer three times. The kinetic energy of the link (next section) is a square of the velocity. Lagrange's equations (theorem 8.88) turn it into accelerations by differentiating in time, so the equations of motion will contain the same terms as here. And whoever reads the equations of motion has to know which term belongs to which kind of motion.

We use two examples. The first is the beam of SHABANA, examples 5.1 and 5.2 (PDF pp. 204-211), because it gives numbers that we can compare with the source. The second is our running link of section 28.1: a steel strip of length \(L = 1\) m and cross-section \(40 \times 6\) mm, driven on a hub by a torque (link A). The model is planar: the link moves in the plane \(xy\). A spatial position would need three angles or four rotation parameters, which the book does not prove (SHABANA, section 5.1, PDF pp. 207-208, and section 6.6, PDF pp. 303-308, only cited here). Link A is an Euler-Bernoulli beam (definition 5.14) with transverse elastic displacement only, and its axis does not lengthen in the elastic coordinates. This is an idealization, and we name it because we will correct it in the section on constant spin.

The symbols have several meanings, so we separate them from the others in the book at first meeting. We write \(\theta\) for the angle of the floating frame. Chapter 8 writes the angle of position in equation (8.26) with \(\varphi\) and uses \(\theta\) in the kinematics of planar motion of a rigid body, as in this section. Chapter 5 writes \(\theta\) for the rotation of the cross-section of a Timoshenko beam, which we do not use in this chapter. The quarter turn is the matrix

\[\mat J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat J\,\vek y = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} \times \vek y \ \text{ for every vector } \vek y \text{ in the plane,} \tag{28.13}\]

which is not a moment of inertia. We write the moments of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{h}}\) (hub) and \(J_{\mathrm{link}}\) (link) so that they cannot be confused. The subscript \(d\) of \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) means "deformation". We do not use the letter \(f\), because in chapter 7 it marks the free unknowns (\(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\)).

The position of a point of the link from section 28.2 (definition 28.4) is written as

\[\vek r = \vek o + \mat R(\theta)\,\bar{\vek p} , \qquad \bar{\vek p} = \bar{\vek r}_0 + \bar{\vek u} = \bar{\vek r}_0 + \mat\Psi\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} . \tag{28.14}\]

Here \(\vek r\) is the position of the point in the fixed axes, \(\vek o\) the origin of the floating frame, \(\mat R(\theta)\) the rotation of equation (8.26), \(\bar{\vek r}_0\) the position of this point in the undeformed link in the axes of the floating frame (attached to the point, so independent of time), \(\mat\Psi\) the \(2 \times n\) matrix of shape functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{k}}\) (which are the trial functions of Ritz's method, definition 6.32), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}(t)\) the column of \(n\) elastic coordinates. The vector \(\bar{\vek p}\) is the position of the point in the deformed link, measured in the axes of the floating frame, and \(\bar{\vek u} = \mat\Psi\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) is its elastic displacement of equation (28.9). The position gets its own letter \(p\) (for position) so that we do not confuse it with the displacement \(\bar{\vek u}\). A bar over a letter always means "in the axes of the floating frame".

We build figure 28.5 together. First the fixed axes \(x\) and \(y\), in which we measure the position. At the point \(\vek o\) the floating frame stands and its axes \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\) are turned by \(\theta = 30^\circ\) against the fixed ones. We attached the frame to the link so that the axis \(\bar x\) points along the axis of the link at the clamp, which is the condition of section 28.2. We draw the link twice: dashed the undeformed one, which lies on the axis \(\bar x\) and has length \(l = 0{,}5\) m, and solid the deformed one, described by the shape \(\psi_2(\xi) = 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3\) with \(\xi = x/l\). The point \(A\) at the end of the link is given by the vector \(\bar{\vek p}\) from \(\vek o\) to \(A\) in the deformed link. For example 5.1,

\[\mat\Psi(\xi) = \begin{bmatrix} \xi & 0 \\ 0 & 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}001 \\ 0{,}01 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{m} , \qquad \bar{\vek p}_{\mathrm{A}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \mat\Psi(1)\,\vek q_{\mathrm{d}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}501 \\ 0{,}010 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{m} ,\]

and by equation (28.14) the point \(A\) lies at \(\vek r_{\mathrm{A}} = (1{,}4289;\ 0{,}7592)\) m, which agrees with the source (SHABANA, PDF p. 206). The first shape function describes the extension and the second the bending, so \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) has two coordinates in example 5.1. Definition 28.4 has the first row of the matrix \(\mat\Psi\) equal to zero, while this example has an axial part. The two lemmas below hold for every \(2 \times n\) matrix \(\mat\Psi(x)\), also with an axial part.

Now we ask how fast \(A\) moves. In equation (28.14) three things change in time. The point \(\vek o\) moves, the angle \(\theta\) grows, and the elastic coordinates \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) change. We build an expectation from the mechanics of a rigid body. If the link were frozen in its present shape, lemma 8.78 would apply: the velocity is \(\dot{\vek o}\) plus a turn about \(\vek o\) with the rate \(\dot\theta\), which is a vector perpendicular to \(\vek r - \vek o\) of length \(\dot\theta\,|\bar{\vek p}|\). In addition the point travels along the link, because the bend changes. Do these two parts simply add? We show that they do.

Lemma 28.8 Velocity of a point of a flexible link (hitrost točke podajnega člena)

Let \(\vek o(t)\), \(\theta(t)\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}(t)\) be differentiable, \(\mat\Psi\) a matrix of shape functions that depends only on the place in the link, and \(\bar{\vek r}_0\) the position of the point in the undeformed link. For the point with the position (28.14),

\[\dot{\mat R} = \dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J , \qquad \dot{\vek r} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p} + \mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} . \tag{28.15}\]

The first two terms \(\dot{\vek o} + \dot\theta\,\mat J\,(\vek r - \vek o)\) are the velocity of the same point with the link frozen (lemma 8.78 with \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\)), the third term is the velocity relative to the floating frame.

Proof

We have to show the derivative of the rotation and then the derivative of the sum (28.14).

  1. The elements of the matrix \(\mat R(\theta)\) are \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) with \(\theta = \theta(t)\). By theorem 8.12 and the rule for the derivative of a composite function, \(\dot{\mat R} = \dot\theta\begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta & -\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \end{bmatrix}\).
  2. The product is \(\mat R\,\mat J = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta & -\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \end{bmatrix}\), which is the matrix of step 1. So \(\dot{\mat R} = \dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\). The same computation in the other order gives \(\mat J\,\mat R = \mat R\,\mat J\).
  3. By the product rule (lemma 1.81, applied to every element), (28.14) gives \(\dot{\vek r} = \dot{\vek o} + \dot{\mat R}\,\bar{\vek p} + \mat R\,\dot{\bar{\vek p}}\).
  4. The vector \(\bar{\vek r}_0\) and the matrix \(\mat\Psi\) do not depend on time, so \(\dot{\bar{\vek p}} = \mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  5. We insert step 2 and step 4 into step 3 and obtain (28.15). By step 2, \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p} = \dot\theta\,\mat J\,\mat R\,\bar{\vek p} = \dot\theta\,\mat J\,(\vek r - \vek o)\), which is \(\vek\omega \times (\vek r - \vek o)\) with \(\vek\omega = \dot\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) by equation (28.13). For \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\) the equation therefore agrees with lemma 8.78.

Source: SHABANA (5.13)-(5.19) and the explanation of the terms on PDF p. 207, where the central term is written as \(\mat B\,\dot{\vek\theta}\) with \(\mat B = (\partial\mat A/\partial\theta)\,\bar{\vek u}\) (the source writes \(\bar{\vek u}\) for our position \(\bar{\vek p}\)). The notation with the matrix \(\mat J\) and the proof in five steps are not from the source.

Every term of (28.15) has its own meaning. The velocity of the point \(\vek o\) is the speed with which the floating frame moves as a whole. The turning term \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) is perpendicular to \(\bar{\vek p}\) (because \(\mat J\) turns a vector by a quarter turn) and of length \(\dot\theta\,|\bar{\vek p}|\), like a point on a wheel at the distance \(|\bar{\vek p}|\) from the axle. We call it the turning term because it comes from the turning of the frame. The elastic term \(\mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the velocity seen by an observer standing on the floating frame, carried into the fixed axes by \(\mat R\), and it vanishes when the bend does not change. The sum of these three arrows in figure 28.5 is the velocity we are after.

Example 28.9 Velocity of the tip of the SHABANA beam (hitrost konca nosilca iz SHABANA)

By example 5.2 (SHABANA, PDF pp. 209-210) the beam of figure 28.5 has \(\dot{\vek o} = (0{,}1;\ 1{,}0)\) m/s, \(\dot\theta = 5\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = (2;\ 3)\) m/s. At \(\theta = 30^\circ\) and \(\bar{\vek p}_{\mathrm{A}} = (0{,}501;\ 0{,}010)\) m we have \(\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}_{\mathrm{A}} = (-0{,}25916;\ 0{,}42888)\) m and \(\mat R\,\mat\Psi(1)\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = (0{,}2321;\ 3{,}5981)\) m/s. Equation (28.15) gives

\[\dot{\vek r}_{\mathrm{A}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}1 \\ 1{,}0 \end{bmatrix} + 5\begin{bmatrix} -0{,}25916 \\ 0{,}42888 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0{,}2321 \\ 3{,}5981 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}9638 \\ 6{,}7425 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{m/s} .\]

The source gives \((-0{,}96380;\ 6{,}74235)\) m/s (PDF p. 210). The source computes with \(\cos 30^\circ = 0{,}8660\) (the matrix \(\mat A\mat S\) on PDF p. 210). With this value we get \((-0{,}96380;\ 6{,}74233)\) m/s, so the first component of the source exactly and the second to within two units in the fifth place. We also checked numerically that the time derivative of the position \(\vek r_{\mathrm{A}}(t)\) at these values is \((-0{,}96375;\ 6{,}74247)\) m/s (script num-03.py). The turning term has length \(2{,}5\) m/s, the elastic term \(3{,}6\) m/s because the large velocity \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) drives it, and the velocity of the point \(\vek o\) \(1{,}0\) m/s. None of the three may be left out.

In our link A the ratio is different, and that is the reason why the turning term must not be forgotten. Let \(\theta = 30^\circ\), \(\dot\theta = 2\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and \(\dot{\vek o} = \vek 0\) (the hub stays in its place), and let the first mode (section 13.6, \(\psi_1(L) = 1\)) show the end displaced by \(q_{\mathrm{d}} = 10\) mm, moving away at \(0{,}1\) m/s. The position of the end in the axes of the floating frame is \(\bar{\vek p} = (1;\ 0{,}010)\) m. In the axes of the floating frame the turning term has the components \(2\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p} = (-0{,}020;\ 2{,}000)\) m/s, the elastic term \((0;\ 0{,}100)\) m/s, together \((-0{,}020;\ 2{,}100)\) m/s, and in the fixed axes \((-1{,}067;\ 1{,}809)\) m/s. The turning contributes twenty times more than the bending. The first component \(-0{,}020\) m/s is the turning term acting on the sideways offset: \(\dot\theta\,\mat J\,(0;\ q_{\mathrm{d}}) = (-\dot\theta\,q_{\mathrm{d}};\ 0)\), which is \(2 \cdot 0{,}010\) m/s towards the hub.

What would happen if we made the mistake that is the easiest to imagine at first sight? The observer on the floating frame sees the point move with the velocity \(\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\). So we would conclude that the velocity in the fixed axes is simply \(\dot{\vek o} + \mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), which is equation (28.15) without the turning term. We try it on the rigid turning of the link about the point \(\vek o\), which stands still (\(\dot{\vek o} = \vek 0\)), with \(\dot\theta = 1\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\). At the instant drawn the link makes the angle \(30^\circ\) with the fixed axis \(x\). The link does not bend, \(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\), so the computation from the components alone gives zero everywhere: every point of the link would stand still although the link turns. The correct speed of a point is \(\dot\theta\) times the distance of the point from the axle (lemma 8.78), and points perpendicular to the link: for the end \(A\) of figure 28.5 this is \(|\dot\theta\,\bar{\vek p}_{\mathrm{A}}| = 0{,}5011\) m/s, which is \(|\bar{\vek p}_{\mathrm{A}}|\). The error is that the floating frame, in which the observer stands, turns together with the point, so \(\mat R\) is not a constant when we differentiate. Without the turning term, example 5.2 would lose the vector \((-1{,}296;\ 2{,}144)\) m/s of length \(2{,}505\) m/s, which is \(37\,\%\) of the true velocity \(6{,}811\) m/s.

We get the acceleration with the second derivative. In doing so every part gets its name and its reason.

Lemma 28.10 Acceleration of a point of a flexible link (pospešek točke podajnega člena)

Let \(\vek o\), \(\theta\) and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) be twice differentiable, the rest as in lemma 28.8. Then

\[\ddot{\vek r} = \ddot{\vek o} + \ddot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p} - \dot\theta^2\,\mat R\,\bar{\vek p} + 2\,\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} . \tag{28.16}\]
Proof

We differentiate (28.15) term by term, each by the product rule (lemma 1.81).

  1. The derivative of \(\dot{\vek o}\) is \(\ddot{\vek o}\).
  2. The derivative of the term \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) has three parts: \(\ddot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) from the derivative of the rate, \(\dot\theta\,\dot{\mat R}\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) from the derivative of the rotation and \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\dot{\bar{\vek p}}\) from the derivative of the vector.
  3. By lemma 28.8 \(\dot{\mat R} = \dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\), and \(\mat J^2 = -\mat I\) (the product \(\mat J\mat J\) of (28.13) is \(-\mat I\)). So the second part of step 2 is \(\dot\theta^2\,\mat R\,\mat J^2\,\bar{\vek p} = -\dot\theta^2\,\mat R\,\bar{\vek p}\).
  4. By lemma 28.8 \(\dot{\bar{\vek p}} = \mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), so the third part of step 2 equals \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  5. The derivative of the term \(\mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) is \(\dot{\mat R}\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} + \mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\).
  6. We add: the terms \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) of steps 4 and 5 are equal and give \(2\,\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\). This is (28.16).

Source: SHABANA (5.37)-(5.38) (PDF p. 209), where the same acceleration is written with the vector products \(\vek\omega \times (\vek\omega \times \vek u)\), \(\vek\alpha \times \vek u\) and \(2\,\vek\omega \times (\mat A\dot{\bar{\vek p}})\). The notation with the matrix \(\mat J\) and the proof are not from the source.

We name the terms of equation (28.16) and give their reasons on figure 28.7. It shows the deformed link with the origin \(\vek o\) and the end \(A\) in the same position as for the velocity, and the arrows are the parts of the acceleration of the end, drawn as a chain that starts at \(A\). The translational part \(\ddot{\vek o}\) is the acceleration of the point \(\vek o\) with which the floating frame moves as a whole, and it does not depend on the shape of the link. The tangential part \(\ddot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) is the turning with an accelerating angle: it is perpendicular to \(\bar{\vek p}\), so tangent to the circle that the point would describe about \(\vek o\) if the link were frozen, hence the name tangential, and of length \(\ddot\theta\,|\bar{\vek p}|\). The centripetal part \(-\dot\theta^2\,\mat R\,\bar{\vek p}\) points from the point \(A\) to \(\vek o\) along the line \(A\vek o\), which means it points to the centre (Latin "seeking the centre"), and has length \(\dot\theta^2\,|\bar{\vek p}|\), like a point on a wheel that needs an acceleration towards the axle to stay on its circle. This part exists also at uniform turning, when \(\ddot\theta = 0\).

The Coriolis part \(2\,\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the one that a rigid body does not have. It is made of two equal contributions, from steps 4 and 5 of the proof. The first arises because the vector \(\bar{\vek p}\) changes with the velocity \(\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\), so the turning part \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\) of the velocity is no longer constant. The second arises because the elastic velocity \(\mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) turns together with the floating frame at the rate \(\dot\theta\). The factor \(2\) is therefore the sum of two equal contributions and not a convention. The name Coriolis part is the usual name of this term in the sources (SHABANA, PDF p. 209, SPONG). Why it is called so, the sources of the book do not say, and we write no history here. The part is zero if the hub stands still (\(\dot\theta = 0\)) or if the link does not bend (\(\dot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek 0\)), so a rigid-link argument does not notice it. It is perpendicular to the elastic velocity, because \(\mat J\) turns the vector by a quarter turn. The relative part \(\mat R\,\mat\Psi\,\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the acceleration seen by the observer on the floating frame. We call it relative because it is measured against the frame. The sum of all parts is \(\ddot{\vek r}\).

Example 28.11 Acceleration of the tip of the SHABANA beam (pospešek konca nosilca iz SHABANA)

Example 5.2 (PDF pp. 209-211) with \(\dot{\vek o}\) and \(\dot\theta\) as above, \(\ddot{\vek o} = (2;\ 0)\) m/s\(^2\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\) and \(\ddot{\vek q}_{\mathrm{d}} = (10;\ 20)\) m/s\(^2\). The parts of equation (28.16) in m/s\(^2\) are: translational \((2;\ 0)\), tangential \((0;\ 0)\), centripetal \((-10{,}722;\ -6{,}479)\), Coriolis \((-35{,}981;\ 2{,}321)\), relative \((-1{,}340;\ 22{,}321)\). The sum is \((-46{,}042;\ 18{,}162)\) m/s\(^2\) and the source gives \((-46{,}042;\ 18{,}161)\) (PDF p. 211). The source separates also the sum of the translational and relative parts, \((0{,}66;\ 22{,}32)\), and the rest \((-46{,}702;\ -4{,}159)\), which is the sum of the centripetal and Coriolis parts, in our computation \((-46{,}703;\ -4{,}158)\). The numerical second derivative of \(\vek r_{\mathrm{A}}(t)\) at these values gives the same result. The Coriolis part, \(36{,}1\) m/s\(^2\), is here the largest of all, and for a rigid link we would leave it out.

In link A the shares are as follows. At \(\theta = 30^\circ\), \(\dot\theta = 2\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\), \(\ddot\theta = 10\ \mathrm{s}^{-2}\) (the hub acceleration of section 28.1), \(q_{\mathrm{d}} = 10\) mm, \(\dot q_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}1\) m/s and \(\ddot q_{\mathrm{d}} = 5\) m/s\(^2\), the components of the acceleration of the end in the axes of the floating frame, in m/s\(^2\), are: tangential \((-0{,}1;\ 10{,}0)\), centripetal \((-4{,}0;\ -0{,}04)\), Coriolis \((-0{,}4;\ 0)\), relative \((0;\ 5{,}0)\), together \((-4{,}5;\ 14{,}96)\). The Coriolis part points along the axis of the link towards the hub and is \(8\,\%\) of the relative part. Because it points along the axis, it would couple the transverse motion with an axial one, which the inextensible axis of link A does not know. We will leave this coupling out in the section on constant spin and name it there.

What remains is the question of how accurate the transverse field \(\bar{\vek u} = (0;\ \psi(x)\,q_{\mathrm{d}})\) is for link A. Its axis does not lengthen in bending, so it should shorten at the end: by steps 1 to 3 of the proof of lemma 8.133, which do not use the end values of \(v\), the shortening of the axis is approximately \(\tfrac12\int_0^\ell v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) (an integral over the projection of the axis, \(\ell\) is its length) with a relative error of at most \(v'^2_{\max}/4\). For the end displaced by \(10\) mm in the first mode the slope of the end is \(0{,}0138\), the shortening \(0{,}058\) mm (that is \(5{,}8 \cdot 10^{-5}\,L\)) and the error of the estimate at most \(5 \cdot 10^{-5}\). The shortening changes the position of the end by \(0{,}058\) mm and the centripetal part \(4{,}0\) m/s\(^2\) by \(2 \cdot 10^{-4}\) m/s\(^2\), so in the kinematics it does not count. In the elastic energy under an axial force it does count: the work of the axial force \(\tfrac N2\int v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is quadratic in \(q_{\mathrm{d}}\), of the same order as the bending energy. The section on constant spin deals with this.

We have closed the kinematics: the velocity (28.15) and the acceleration (28.16) of every point are expressed by the coordinates \(\vek o\), \(\theta\), \(\vek q_{\mathrm{d}}\) and their derivatives. In the next section we square the velocity (28.15) and integrate over the link to get the kinetic energy and the mass matrix of the link. The term \(\dot\theta\,\mat R\,\mat J\,\bar{\vek p}\), which the wrong computation overlooked, will there tie the turning of the hub to the bending coordinates.
