Chapter 20 Damping models

20.2 Equivalent viscous damping

At resonance, cantilever \(A\) of section 9.1 with viscous damping \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) has amplitude \(X = 15{,}944\) mm and loses \(1{,}0018\) J in every period (theorem 9.61). In example 9.110 we added dry friction of force \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N. What is the amplitude at resonance now? The equation of motion

\[m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q + F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q) = Q_0\cos\Omega t\]

is not linear, so the methods of chapter 9 do not solve it: they relied on the sum of two solutions being a solution again, and on the complex form of the harmonic response (theorem 9.52). Here we look for another way. In steady vibration the exciting force supplies as much energy as the damping removes (theorem 9.61), so we do not need the shape of the damping force, only how much energy it removes in a period. If we know that number we can take it as the measure and replace the damper by a viscous one that removes the same amount.

The model is system \(A\) of section 9.1: a mass \(m = 50\) kg on a spring \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, the coordinate \(q\) is the displacement of the mass. Besides the spring force and the exciting force \(Q = Q_0\cos\Omega t\), the mass is acted on by a damping force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}(t)\) (N), produced by any mechanism: a viscous damper (\(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c\,\dot q\), definition 9.1), friction, or something else. The letter \(c\) in this section always means the coefficient of a dashpot as in chapter 9, and never the wave speed of chapter 13. The assumption, which we name because it cannot be justified in advance, is that the motion is (approximately) harmonic, \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) with amplitude \(X > 0\), circular frequency \(\Omega > 0\) and period \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\). Section 20.2.2 below says how close this is to the truth, with numbers.

How much energy does the mechanism remove? We multiply the equation of motion \(m\,\ddot q + k\,q = Q + f_{\mathrm{D}}\) by the velocity, as in lemma 9.59. There the equation held \(-c\,\dot q\), here the right side holds a general force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\), and we get \(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}/\mathrm{d}t = Q\,\dot q + f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\) for the stored energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 m\,\dot q^2 + \tfrac12 k\,q^2\). The term \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\) is the power of the damping force (definition 8.54), its integral over a period is the work. The energy lost in a period is therefore

\[W_{\mathrm{izg}} = -\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}} f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{20.4}\]

The sign is chosen so that \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} > 0\) for every real damping that slows the motion. What does this integral mean on a plot of the force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\) against the displacement \(q\)? By lemma 20.2 (section 20.1), \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the area of the loop that the point \((q, f_{\mathrm{D}})\) traces in a period. We draw the loop (figure 20.4).

On the horizontal axis we plot the displacement \(q\), on the vertical axis the damping force \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\), and with the marks \(\pm X\) we mark the extreme positions between which the mass moves. In dry friction the force has the constant magnitude \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\) and always points against the velocity. When the mass moves to the right (\(\dot q > 0\)), \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\), so this is the lower side of the rectangle from \(-X\) to \(X\). At the right end the velocity reverses and the force jumps to \(+F_{\mathrm{t}}\) while the mass moves to the left, this is the upper side. The point travels around the rectangle counterclockwise, like around the ellipse of the viscous damper in figure 9.27. The area of the rectangle is the width \(2X\) times the height \(2F_{\mathrm{t}}\),

\[W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 2X\cdot 2F_{\mathrm{t}} = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\,X ,\]

since in every period the mass covers the distance \(4X\) (\(2X\) to the right and \(2X\) back) against a force of constant magnitude \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\). At \(X = 13{,}914\) mm this is \(0{,}1113\) J.

For a viscous damper with coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) and the same motion, the loop is the ellipse with the same horizontal semi-axis \(X\), since the displacement is the same, and vertical semi-axis \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\), since \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\dot q\) and \(\dot q\) has amplitude \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\) (at resonance \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\)). Its area is \(\pi\,X\cdot c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\) (the area of an ellipse with semi-axes \(a\) and \(b\) is \(\pi a b\), as used in figure 9.27). We choose \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) so that the areas agree: \(\pi\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2 = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi = 2{,}5465\) N. The vertical semi-axis of the ellipse is therefore \(2{,}5465\) N and exceeds \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N: the ellipse sticks out of the rectangle at \(q = 0\) and does not reach it at the corners, and the two loops enclose the same area. The direction of travel on the ellipse is the same as on the rectangle.

This is the rule that we introduce as a definition. The name has a reason: the damper is "equivalent" by what it removes (\(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) in a period), and "viscous" because its force is proportional to the velocity, so the equation of motion stays linear. For a viscous damper the loss is \(\pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\) by theorem 9.61, so we get \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) by setting this loss equal to the real one.

Definition 20.6 Equivalent viscous damping (enakovredno viskozno dušenje)

Let \(q(t) = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) be a motion with amplitude \(X > 0\) and circular frequency \(\Omega > 0\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\), and let \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\) be a piecewise continuous function of time with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) (the damping force in this motion). Let the energy lost in a period \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) be equation (20.4). The equivalent viscous damping coefficient is the number

\[c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{W_{\mathrm{izg}}}{\pi\,\Omega\,X^2} \quad (\mathrm{N\,s/m}) \tag{20.5}\]

and the equivalent damping ratio is \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = c_{\mathrm{eq}}/(2\sqrt{km})\) (definition 9.5). A viscous damper with coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) in the same motion loses exactly \(W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) in a period.

Source: CK section 4.7 (PDF p. 125 to 128): equation (4.75) (PDF p. 127) writes \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}}/(\pi\,\Omega\,U^2)\), where \(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{D}} = -W_{\mathrm{izg}}\) is the work of the damping force in a cycle and \(U\) is our \(X\). The equivalent damping ratio is in equations (4.76) to (4.78) (PDF p. 128). Our notation uses the letter \(q\) and \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} > 0\) as in definition 20.1.

The number \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is not a property of the mechanism but of the mechanism in a given motion: it depends on \(X\) and in general on \(\Omega\). For an actual viscous damper (\(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -c\,\dot q\)) theorem 9.61 gives \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = c\) for every \(X\) and \(\Omega\), since then \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\). Is equating the energies the only sensible choice, or could we take something else, say the same peak force? The answer is in the next theorem. We draw the friction force over a period (figure 20.5).

We label the horizontal axis with \(\theta = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), an angle that grows by \(2\pi\) in a period, and the displacement is \(q = X\sin\theta\) while its velocity is proportional to \(\cos\theta\). The dry friction force is piecewise constant: \(-F_{\mathrm{t}}\) while \(\cos\theta > 0\) (from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\) and from \(3\pi/2\) to \(2\pi\)), and \(+F_{\mathrm{t}}\) in between, when the velocity is negative. The viscous force \(-c\,\dot q = -c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\theta\) is a cosine of amplitude \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\), the blue curve for the chosen \(c = c_{\mathrm{eq}}\). Which multiple of the velocity fits friction best? We measure the difference of the two forces, the remainder \(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c\,\dot q\), by the integral of its square over a period, \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c\,\dot q)^2\,\mathrm{d}t\), which is \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) times the mean square. We take the square because it does not depend on the sign of the deviation and squares are easy to work with (the proof below is a single completed square). The theorem says that the best \(c\) is exactly \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

Theorem 20.7 The energy rule is the best viscous fit (pravilo energije je najboljši viskozni približek)

Let \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) with \(X > 0\), \(\Omega > 0\), \(T_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\pi/\Omega\) and let \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\) be a piecewise continuous function with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\). For a real number \(c\) let

\[R(c) = \int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c\,\dot q\bigr)^2\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

Then:

  1. \(R(c) = R(c_{\mathrm{eq}}) + \pi\,\Omega X^2\,(c - c_{\mathrm{eq}})^2\) with \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) from definition 20.6. The function \(R\) therefore has exactly one minimum, at \(c = c_{\mathrm{eq}}\).
  2. The remainder \(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\dot q\) is orthogonal to the velocity: \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\dot q\bigr)\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). So the remainder does no work in a period.
Proof

We must show that \(R\) is a quadratic function with leading coefficient \(\pi\,\Omega X^2\) and vertex at \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

  1. We expand the square: \(R(c) = \int f_{\mathrm{D}}^2\,\mathrm{d}t + 2c\int f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t + c^2\int \dot q^2\,\mathrm{d}t\), all integrals over \([0, T_{\mathrm{p}}]\). The integrals exist because the integrands are piecewise continuous.
  2. From \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) we get \(\dot q = -\Omega X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\) (theorem 8.12), and by lemma 9.60 (item 2 with \(a = -\varphi\)) \(\int\dot q^2\,\mathrm{d}t = \Omega^2X^2\,T_{\mathrm{p}}/2 = \pi\,\Omega X^2\).
  3. By equation (20.4), \(\int f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = -W_{\mathrm{izg}}\), and by equation (20.5) \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\pi\,\Omega X^2\). With \(A = \pi\,\Omega X^2 > 0\) we therefore have \(R(c) = \int f_{\mathrm{D}}^2\,\mathrm{d}t - 2c\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}A + c^2A\).
  4. We complete the square: \(c^2A - 2c\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}A = A\,(c - c_{\mathrm{eq}})^2 - A\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}^2\), so \(R(c) = A\,(c - c_{\mathrm{eq}})^2 + \bigl(\int f_{\mathrm{D}}^2\,\mathrm{d}t - A\,c_{\mathrm{eq}}^2\bigr)\). The binomial in the bracket does not contain \(c\) and equals \(R(c_{\mathrm{eq}})\) (insert \(c = c_{\mathrm{eq}}\)). Since \(A > 0\) and \((c - c_{\mathrm{eq}})^2 \ge 0\) with equality exactly at \(c = c_{\mathrm{eq}}\), this is item 1.
  5. Item 2: \(\int(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\dot q)\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = -W_{\mathrm{izg}} + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\pi\,\Omega X^2 = 0\) by steps 2 and 3.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. CK (4.75) (PDF p. 127) gives the energy rule, not the property of the best fit.

The proof showed that the two rules are one and the same: whoever equates the energy lost in a period also finds the viscous force that is closest to the real one in the mean square. The reason is item 2: the remainder is orthogonal to the velocity, its work is zero, so the real force and the substitute do the same work in a period. We read figure 20.5 like this: the blue curve is the projection of the orange one onto the direction of the velocity (like the shadow of a vector on a line, hence the name "projection"), and the grey curve is what remains of the projection. For friction the square root of the mean square of the remainder is \(\sqrt{1 - 8/\pi^2} = 0{,}4352\) times the square root of the mean square of the friction force itself, that is \(43{,}5\ \%\). The number comes from item 2: since the remainder is orthogonal to the velocity, the mean square of the force splits into the mean square of the remainder and the mean square of the blue curve, as in Pythagoras' theorem. The friction force has mean square \(F_{\mathrm{t}}^2\), the blue cosine curve of amplitude \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi\) has \((4F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi)^2/2 = 8F_{\mathrm{t}}^2/\pi^2\) (lemma 9.60), so \(F_{\mathrm{t}}^2(1 - 8/\pi^2)\) is left for the remainder. As a force the remainder is therefore large, but in a harmonic motion it does not affect the energy in a period, and in describing the motion we neglect it. Section 20.2.2 shows what we neglect and how little it contributes.

The theorem holds for every mechanism. We test it on two that we already know: dry friction, and the quadratic drag of air or a liquid.

Corollary 20.8 Equivalent damping of dry friction (enakovredno dušenje suhega trenja)

For \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\) and \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) we have \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\,X\) and

\[c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{4F_{\mathrm{t}}}{\pi\,\Omega\,X} , \qquad \zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{2F_{\mathrm{t}}}{\pi\,k\,X}\,\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}{\Omega} . \tag{20.6}\]
Proof

We must show the energy loss and from it \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

  1. Piece by piece, \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\,\dot q = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\,|\dot q|\), so \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = F_{\mathrm{t}}\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}|\dot q|\,\mathrm{d}t\) by equation (20.4).
  2. \(|\dot q| = \Omega X\,|\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)|\) has period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\), so we may take the integral over \([0, T_{\mathrm{p}}]\) over \([\varphi/\Omega, \varphi/\Omega + T_{\mathrm{p}}]\) instead (lemma 9.86, item 3), where \(\Omega t - \varphi\) runs from \(0\) to \(2\pi\). The sine is nonnegative on the first half of this interval and nonpositive on the second, each half lasts \(T_{\mathrm{p}}/2\), and the integral of the magnitude of the sine over a half is \(2/\Omega\) (fundamental theorem 3.40, since \(\bigl(-\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)/\Omega\bigr)' = \sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\)). Hence \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}|\dot q|\,\mathrm{d}t = \Omega X\cdot 2\cdot 2/\Omega = 4X\), which is exactly the path of the mass in a period, from \(-X\) to \(X\) and back.
  3. \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\), and by equation (20.5) \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X/(\pi\,\Omega X^2) = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi\,\Omega X)\).
  4. \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = c_{\mathrm{eq}}/(2m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) (since \(\sqrt{km} = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\)) and \(m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = k\) give \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(2\pi\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\Omega X) = (2F_{\mathrm{t}}/(\pi\,k\,X))(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\Omega)\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The loss \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\) is the geometric work of friction on the path \(4X\), CK (3.45) to (3.50) (PDF p. 89) gives the equation of motion with friction.

For \(A\) at resonance and \(X = 13{,}914\) mm, \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = 4\cdot 2/(\pi\cdot 35{,}4175\cdot 0{,}013914) = 5{,}167\) N s/m, that is \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = 0{,}00146\). The viscous damping of system \(A\) is \(c = 35{,}418\) N s/m (\(\zeta = 0{,}01\)), so friction adds only about a seventh (\(14{,}6\ \%\) of the coefficient). We note that the \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) of friction falls with frequency and with amplitude. The loss \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\) does not depend on the frequency at all and grows only in proportion to \(X\), while the loss \(\pi\,c\,\Omega X^2\) of a viscous damper grows with \(\Omega\) and with \(X^2\), so at a larger \(\Omega\) or \(X\) the coefficient that gives the same loss must be smaller.

Corollary 20.9 Equivalent damping of quadratic drag (enakovredno dušenje kvadratnega upora)

For \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -a\,|\dot q|\,\dot q\) with \(a > 0\) (N s²/m²) and \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) we have \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \tfrac{8}{3}\,a\,\Omega^2X^3\) and

\[c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{8}{3\pi}\,a\,\Omega\,X . \tag{20.7}\]
Proof

We must show the energy loss and from it \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

  1. From \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\,\dot q = -a\,|\dot q|^3\), equation (20.4) gives \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = a\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}|\dot q|^3\,\mathrm{d}t = a\,\Omega^3X^3\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{p}}}|\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)|^3\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  2. With \(\theta = \Omega t - \varphi\) the integral over a period is \((1/\Omega)\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin\theta|^3\,\mathrm{d}\theta = (2/\Omega)\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\) (the interval \([-\varphi, 2\pi - \varphi]\) is shifted to \([0, 2\pi]\) by lemma 9.86, item 3), since \(|\sin|\) is periodic with period \(\pi\) (lemma 8.10).
  3. Because \(\sin^3\theta = \sin\theta\,(1 - \cos^2\theta)\) we have \(\bigl(-\cos\theta + \tfrac13\cos^3\theta\bigr)' = \sin\theta - \cos^2\theta\sin\theta = \sin^3\theta\). By the fundamental theorem 3.40, \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = \bigl(1 - \tfrac13\bigr) - \bigl(-1 + \tfrac13\bigr) = \tfrac43\).
  4. So the integral over a period is \((2/\Omega)\cdot\tfrac43 = 8/(3\Omega)\) and \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \tfrac83\,a\,\Omega^2X^3\).
  5. By equation (20.5), \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \tfrac83\,a\,\Omega^2X^3/(\pi\,\Omega X^2) = \tfrac{8}{3\pi}\,a\,\Omega\,X\).

Source: CK example 4.4 (PDF p. 127 to 128) treats the same drag and obtains \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = 8\,a\,\Omega\,U/(3\pi)\) (p. 128, the numerator 8 stands in the text of the page). Steps 1 to 5 are ours.

A check with SymPy and NumPy: for \(a = 1\) N s²/m², \(X = 10\) mm and \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the numerical projection of theorem 20.7 gives \(0{,}300633\) N s/m, and formula (20.7) gives \(8\cdot 35{,}4175\cdot 0{,}01/(3\pi) = 0{,}300633\) N s/m as well. For friction the same check gives \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = 5{,}1674\) N s/m from least squares and \(5{,}1674\) from formula (20.6). For quadratic drag \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) grows with the amplitude, for friction it falls: the first removes proportionally more energy at large displacements, the second at small ones.

A real joint usually has several mechanisms at once: a viscous damper, friction in the connections, air drag. Must we compute each one separately, or can we add them into one number?

Corollary 20.10 Sum of mechanisms (vsota mehanizmov)

Let \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = f_1 + f_2 + \dots + f_{\mathrm{N}}\) be a sum of piecewise continuous functions with period \(T_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(q = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\). If \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the equivalent coefficient of the force \(f_{\mathrm{j}}\) in this motion, then

\[W_{\mathrm{izg}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N W_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N c_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}=1}^N \zeta_{\mathrm{j}} . \tag{20.8}\]
Proof

We must show that the energy is linear in \(f_{\mathrm{D}}\).

  1. By equation (20.4), \(W_{\mathrm{izg}} = -\int(f_1 + \dots + f_{\mathrm{N}})\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\bigl(-\int f_{\mathrm{j}}\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t\bigr) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} W_{\mathrm{j}}\) by linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39).
  2. Division by \(\pi\,\Omega X^2\) by equation (20.5) gives \(c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{j}}\), and division by \(2\sqrt{km}\) gives \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\zeta_{\mathrm{j}}\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. CK (§4.7, PDF p. 125 to 128) treats each mechanism separately.

The corollary holds for the same motion: we add the \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the same \(X\) and \(\Omega\). The sum is the number \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) that we use in the formulas of chapter 9. For \(A\) the viscous part is \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) and the friction adds \(0{,}00146\) at \(X = 13{,}914\) mm, so \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = 0{,}01146\). At another amplitude the addition would be different, so the correct order is: first solve for \(X\) from the energy balance, then compute \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) from it. The theorem below does this.

20.2.1 Resonance with friction

We return to the opening question. At resonance, \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), a viscous system has the phase lag \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) by theorem 9.52 (\(\cos\varphi = 0\) because \(k - m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 0\)), and so does the system with equivalent viscous damping, so \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and the force \(Q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) is in phase with the velocity \(\dot q = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). The exciting force therefore always points in the same direction as the velocity and supplies the largest possible energy in a period. How much is this, and when do friction and viscosity use it up?

Theorem 20.11 Resonance with dry friction (resonanca s trenjem)

Let a system with mass \(m > 0\) and spring \(k > 0\) (\(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\)) have a viscous coefficient \(c \ge 0\), friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} > 0\) and a force \(Q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) (\(Q_0 > 0\)). Let \(T = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then:

  1. Every solution \(q\) with period \(T\) and a continuous velocity satisfies
    \[\int_0^T Q_0\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^T\bigl(c\,\dot q^2 + F_{\mathrm{t}}\,|\dot q|\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  2. For the trial motion \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) with \(X > 0\) (a harmonic approximation that does not solve the equation of motion exactly) the two sides of this equation evaluate to \(\pi\,Q_0\,X = \pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2 + 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\,X\). For \(c > 0\) this equality holds exactly for
    \[X = \frac{\pi\,Q_0 - 4F_{\mathrm{t}}}{\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}} , \tag{20.9}\]
    which requires \(4F_{\mathrm{t}} < \pi\,Q_0\).
  3. For \(c = 0\) and \(4F_{\mathrm{t}} \ne \pi\,Q_0\) the equality of item 2 holds for no \(X > 0\): no trial motion \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) satisfies the energy balance.
Proof

We must show first the energy balance over a period, then evaluate it for \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\).

  1. We multiply the equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q + F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q) = Q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) by the velocity. As in lemma 9.59, \(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}/\mathrm{d}t = Q_0\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\,\dot q - c\,\dot q^2 - F_{\mathrm{t}}\,|\dot q|\), since \(\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\,\dot q = |\dot q|\).
  2. Because \(q\) is periodic with period \(T\), so is \(\dot q\), and hence \(\mathcal{E}(T) = \mathcal{E}(0)\). By the fundamental theorem 3.40, \(0 = \int_0^T \mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}/\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}t\), and this is item 1.
  3. With \(q = X\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) we have \(\dot q = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). By lemma 9.60 (item 2 with \(a = 0\)), \(\int_0^T Q_0\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\,\dot q\,\mathrm{d}t = Q_0\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\,T/2 = \pi\,Q_0X\) and \(\int_0^T c\,\dot q^2\,\mathrm{d}t = \pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2\). By step 2 of corollary 20.8, \(\int_0^T F_{\mathrm{t}}|\dot q|\,\mathrm{d}t = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\).
  4. Division by \(X > 0\) gives \(\pi\,Q_0 = \pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X + 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\), so equation (20.9). Positivity of \(X\) requires \(\pi\,Q_0 > 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\) when \(c > 0\).
  5. For \(c = 0\) the equation becomes \(\pi\,Q_0X = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}X\), that is \(\pi\,Q_0 = 4F_{\mathrm{t}}\) after division by \(X \ne 0\). If this condition is violated, the equation has no solution \(X > 0\), which is item 3.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. CK treats resonance only with viscous damping (see theorem 9.54). The comparison with RK4 time stepping is in num-02.py.

For \(A\), \(\pi Q_0 = 62{,}832\) N and \(4F_{\mathrm{t}} = 8\) N, so \(X = (62{,}832 - 8)/(\pi\cdot 35{,}418\cdot 35{,}4175) = 13{,}914\) mm. Viscous damping alone, \(4F_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\), gives \(20/(35{,}418\cdot 35{,}4175) = 15{,}944\) mm as in chapter 9. Friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N, a tenth of the exciting force \(Q_0 = 20\) N, reduces the amplitude by \(12{,}7\ \%\). In terms of energy: in a period the force supplies \(\pi Q_0 X = \pi\cdot 20\cdot 0{,}013914 = 0{,}8742\) J, of which viscosity takes \(\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2 = 0{,}7629\) J and friction \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X = 0{,}1113\) J, together \(0{,}8742\) J, equal to the energy supplied. We get the same with the replacement: the total coefficient is \(c + c_{\mathrm{eq}} = 35{,}418 + 5{,}167 = 40{,}585\) N s/m and the resonance formula of chapter 9, \(X = Q_0/(c_{\mathrm{total}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), gives \(20/(40{,}585\cdot 35{,}4175) = 13{,}914\) mm.

The trial motion is not an exact solution: with it \(m\ddot q + k q = 0\), and the equation of motion would require \(F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t) = (Q_0 - c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), which fails for \(F_{\mathrm{t}} > 0\) because the sign function is not a multiple of the cosine. The theorem therefore does not say whether the motion really settles at \(X = 13{,}914\) mm. We check this by time stepping (fourth-order Runge-Kutta, 200 steps per period, smoothed sign function, 400 periods, computed in num-02.py): the peak in period 400 is \(13{,}9138\) mm, the fundamental component \(13{,}9138\) mm and the energy formula \(13{,}9138\) mm, all agreeing to four decimals. Figure 20.6 shows how the amplitude settles.

On the horizontal axis we plot the number of periods \(n\), on the vertical axis the peak displacement \(X\). The system starts from rest, at \(n = 0\) we have \(X = 0\). Without damping, the dashed line, the peak grows by \(\pi Q_0/k = 1{,}0018\) mm per period, because the force supplies \(\pi Q_0 X\) in every period and \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 kX^2\) (we derive the increment \(\pi Q_0/k\) two paragraphs below, with \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\)). The grey curve, given by viscosity alone, is stopped by the damper at \(15{,}944\) mm, when the loss \(\pi\,c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X^2\) reaches the energy supplied. The blue curve is damping with friction: it settles lower, at \(13{,}914\) mm, and has \(6{,}376\) mm after \(n = 10\) periods, \(9{,}893\) mm after \(20\), \(12{,}769\) mm after \(40\) and \(13{,}821\) mm after \(80\).

Why is there no settling with friction alone? This is the near miss of the theorem, item 3.

Take friction without viscosity (\(c = 0\)). At resonance with \(Q_0 = 20\) N and \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N the energy supplied is \(\pi Q_0X = 62{,}83\,X\) (J, \(X\) in m) and the loss of friction is \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}X = 8\,X\). The difference \((\pi Q_0 - 4F_{\mathrm{t}})\,X = 54{,}83\,X\) is positive for every \(X\): friction never catches up with the force. The loss and the energy supplied are both proportional to \(X\), so their ratio does not depend on the amplitude and there is no \(X\) at which they would be equal. For a viscous damper or for quadratic drag the loss grows with a higher power of \(X\) (\(X^2\) or \(X^3\)), so the line of the energy supplied and the curve of the loss intersect at a finite \(X\).

How much does the amplitude grow in each period? The energy at a turning point is \(\tfrac12 k X^2\), and in a period it grows by approximately \((\pi Q_0 - 4F_{\mathrm{t}})\,\bar X\) with \(\bar X\) the mean peak in that period (we compute the energy supplied and the friction loss as if the motion in that period were harmonic with amplitude \(\bar X\)). From \(\tfrac12 k(X_{\mathrm{n}+1}^2 - X_{\mathrm{n}}^2) = \tfrac12 k\,(X_{\mathrm{n}+1} - X_{\mathrm{n}})(X_{\mathrm{n}+1} + X_{\mathrm{n}}) = k\,\bar X\,(X_{\mathrm{n}+1} - X_{\mathrm{n}})\) with \(\bar X = (X_{\mathrm{n}} + X_{\mathrm{n}+1})/2\) we get \(X_{\mathrm{n}+1} - X_{\mathrm{n}} \approx (\pi Q_0 - 4F_{\mathrm{t}})/k = 0{,}8742\) mm: the growth is a straight line, not an exponential curve. Time stepping confirms: after \(5\), \(10\) and \(20\) periods the peak is \(4{,}159\), \(8{,}530\) and \(17{,}272\) mm, with a mean increment from the fifth period on of \(0{,}8742\) mm per period (figure 20.6, the orange line). Without friction the increment would be \(1{,}0018\) mm. Friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\) N reduces the increment to \(0{,}8726\) of its former value but does not stop the growth. For \(F_{\mathrm{t}} > \pi Q_0/4 = 15{,}708\) N the loss would exceed the energy supplied at every amplitude and a harmonic motion could not last. What happens instead (the mass stops and starts again) the harmonic model does not say.

20.2.2 When the replacement is allowed

Replacing a damper by an equivalent viscous one rests on the assumption that the motion is harmonic. Theorem 20.7 tells us what we neglect: the remainder \(f_{\mathrm{D}} + c_{\mathrm{eq}}\dot q\) of figure 20.5 has a root mean square of \(43{,}5\ \%\) of the root mean square of the friction force, and it consists of higher harmonic components. How large a deviation do they cause? The dry friction force \(f_{\mathrm{D}} = -F_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\cos\theta)\) has, by lemma 9.87 (orthogonality of the trigonometric system), an expansion in \(\cos n\theta\). The coefficient is \(a_{\mathrm{n}} = (1/\pi)\int_0^{2\pi}f_{\mathrm{D}}\cos n\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = -(F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi)\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm{sgn}(\cos\theta)\cos n\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\). By the symmetries \(\theta \to -\theta\) and \(\theta \to \pi - \theta\) the integral is \(4\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos n\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 4\sin(n\pi/2)/n\) for odd \(n\), so \(a_1 = -4F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi\) (this is exactly the blue \(-c_{\mathrm{eq}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\cos\theta\)) and \(a_3 = +4F_{\mathrm{t}}/(3\pi) = 0{,}8488\) N. The third-harmonic force with amplitude \(0{,}8488\) N drives the system at frequency \(3\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), where by theorem 9.52 the amplitude of the response is \(0{,}8488\,|H(3\omega_{\mathrm{n}})|\) with \(|H| = 1/\sqrt{(k - 9m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2)^2 + (3c\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2}\). For \(A\), \(k - 9m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = -8k = -501\,760\) N/m and \(3c\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 3763\) N/m, so \(|H| = 1{,}993\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N and the response is \(1{,}69\cdot 10^{-3}\) mm, that is \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-4}\) of the amplitude \(X = 13{,}914\) mm. This deviation is almost exactly \(-1{,}69\cdot 10^{-3}\cos 3\theta\) mm (the phase shift is only \(3c\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(8k) = 0{,}0075\) rad), and at the peak of the fundamental component, at \(\theta = \pi/2\), \(\cos 3\theta = 0\). The peak and the fundamental component therefore differ in time stepping by less than \(10^{-4}\) mm, and formula (20.9) gives the true amplitude to four decimals. The smallness has a reason: at resonance the system is not in resonance with the third harmonic, its response is \(1/8\) of the static one (\(8k\) in the denominator), and the friction force is already a small part of the excitation (\(4F_{\mathrm{t}}/\pi = 2{,}5465\) N against \(Q_0 = 20\) N).

The replacement is therefore valid when three conditions hold:

  1. The motion is close to harmonic: the higher components of the damping force are small and the corresponding response smaller still. For \(A\) at resonance with friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}1\,Q_0\) this share is \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-4}\). With larger friction or a weaker excitation, where the mass stops in between (sticks), the motion is no longer close to harmonic and the replacement fails.
  2. The damping is light, the ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is small (at resonance \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = 0{,}0115\) for \(A\)). The resonance peak is then narrow, the half-power band is \(2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) wide (theorem 9.62), and the system already filters the harmonic components outside this band.
  3. The replacement holds for one amplitude and one frequency: \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is a number at \(X\) and \(\Omega\), and if \(X\) changes, \(c_{\mathrm{eq}}\) changes too. Free vibration shows this last point.

Free vibration with friction has a known answer: by theorem 9.109 the amplitude falls linearly, by \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}/k = 0{,}12755\) mm per full vibration, and in example 9.110 the mass starting at \(5\) mm stops after \(39\) vibrations. What would we get if we computed \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) from the first vibration and used it as a fixed number? By equation (20.6) \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2u_{\mathrm{D}}/(\pi X)\) with \(u_{\mathrm{D}} = F_{\mathrm{t}}/k = 0{,}03189\) mm, at \(X = 5\) mm this is \(0{,}00406\). With it the decay would be exponential with a peak ratio \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}} = 0{,}9748\) per vibration and after \(39\) vibrations the peak would still be \(5\cdot 0{,}3697 = 1{,}85\) mm (theorem 9.107), whereas the real motion stops, its peak at \(39\) vibrations is \(5 - 39\cdot 0{,}12755 = 0{,}025\) mm. The reason: \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}} \propto 1/X\) and so it grows as the amplitude falls. At \(X = 1\) mm it is \(0{,}0203\), at \(0{,}5\) mm \(0{,}0406\) and at \(0{,}1531\) mm \(0{,}1326\). The decrement that we measure between the last two peaks (\(0{,}2806\) and \(0{,}1531\) mm) gave \(\zeta = 0{,}0960\) in example 9.110, and this is close to the equivalent ratio at the middle of these two peaks, \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}(0{,}217\ \mathrm{mm}) = 0{,}094\) (figure 9.57). The number \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) of friction is therefore a reading at a single amplitude.

Summary: the damping force of any mechanism is replaced by a viscous one that removes the same energy in a period (definition 20.6). This is at the same time the best viscous fit in the mean square (theorem 20.7). So for resonance with friction (theorem 20.11) we obtained an amplitude that friction reduces by \(12{,}7\ \%\) without solving a nonlinear equation. The replacement holds for one amplitude and one frequency: for friction \(\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}\) falls as the amplitude or the frequency grows. Hysteretic damping, whose loss is by construction \(\pi\gamma kX^2\) and independent of frequency, is treated in section 20.3.
