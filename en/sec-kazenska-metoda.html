Chapter 12 Beam and frame finite elements

12.6 Constraints: multipliers and the penalty method

In section 12.4.5 we treated the roller support at \(C\) (definition 5.5) by prescribing one unknown, \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\). This works when the support closes the direction of a single coordinate, and chapter 7 showed how a prescribed unknown is eliminated (theorem 7.18). But what if the roller support at \(C\) does not stand on a horizontal base but on a base inclined at \(30°\) to the horizontal? By definition 5.5 the support moves freely along the base, but it cannot move in the direction of the normal \(\vek n = (-\sin 30°,\ \cos 30°)\) of the base. Only a combination of the displacements \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) is forbidden, \(-\sin 30°\,u_{\mathrm{C}} + \cos 30°\,v_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\), and no coordinate is prescribed by itself. Chapter 7 announced that prescribed values can also be imposed by a penalty (kazenska metoda) and by Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji), the penalty for frames in this chapter, while chapter 8 supplied the multipliers. This section carries both routes over to the finite element method and proves them in general form, for prescribed unknowns and for such combinations, because later in this chapter we shall meet a penalty that nobody added on purpose.

12.6.1 Linear constraints

We collect the unknowns of the system, the displacements and rotations of the nodes of the mesh, in a column \(\vek d\) with \(n\) entries. A condition like the ban on displacement in the direction of the normal is a linear equation for the entries of the column \(\vek d\). We write several such equations together.

Definition 12.25 Linear constraints and the constraint matrix (linearne vezi in matrika vezi)

Let \(\vek d\) be a column of \(n\) unknowns. Linear constraints (linearne vezi) are the equations

\[\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g , \tag{12.25}\]

where \(\mat C\) is a real matrix with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, \(m \le n\), that has \(m\) pivots (definition 8.21), and \(\vek g\) is a column of \(m\) given numbers. The set of solutions is, in the space of columns, a line or a plane. The matrix \(\mat C\) is called the constraint matrix (matrika vezi).

Source: COOK §13.1, equation (13.1-1) (PDF p. 503), where the constraint is written as \([C]\{D\} - \{Q\} = \{0\}\), and PDF p. 504: "[C] has more columns than rows", while the dependent unknowns are chosen so that their block is "square and nonsingular". The requirement of \(m\) pivots is our form of this condition, so that we can use lemma 8.36.

The condition on the pivots ensures two things, which we prove in theorem 12.26 (steps 1 and 3 of the proof): the constraints do not exclude each other, because the system (12.25) has a solution for every \(\vek g\), and no row is a combination of the others. The letter \(\mat C\) is the damping matrix in chapter 10, here it is the constraint matrix. Let us look at three examples on the L-frame of section 12.4.5, which after the clamp at \(A\) still has nine unknowns \(u_{\mathrm{B}}, v_{\mathrm{B}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{B}}, u_{\mathrm{D}}, v_{\mathrm{D}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{D}}, u_{\mathrm{C}}, v_{\mathrm{C}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{C}}\). The support in the direction \(\vek n\) is one constraint, \(m = 1\), with a row that has the numbers \(-\sin 30°\) and \(\cos 30°\) at the places of \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\), zero elsewhere, and \(g = 0\). A prescribed unknown \(d_{\mathrm{k}} = \bar d\) is a constraint with the row \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\) (the column \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\) has \(1\) at place \(k\) and zero elsewhere) and \(g = \bar d\). The clamp at \(A\) is three such rows with \(\bar d = 0\). A rigid link (toga palica) between the nodes \(B\) and \(C\) does not allow their distance to change. The points \(B\) and \(C\) lie on the same horizontal line, so for small displacements their distance changes by \(u_{\mathrm{C}} - u_{\mathrm{B}}\) and the rigid link is the constraint \(u_{\mathrm{B}} - u_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\) with a row that has the numbers \(1\) and \(-1\) at the places of \(u_{\mathrm{B}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\). This is where the assumption of small displacements enters. Exactly, the new distance is \(\sqrt{(L + u_{\mathrm{C}} - u_{\mathrm{B}})^2 + (v_{\mathrm{C}} - v_{\mathrm{B}})^2}\) with \(L = 1\) m, and the difference of the vertical displacements \(v_{\mathrm{C}} - v_{\mathrm{B}}\) lengthens it by about \((v_{\mathrm{C}} - v_{\mathrm{B}})^2/(2L)\) more. For a difference of \(1\) mm this is \(1^2/(2 \cdot 1000) = 0{,}0005\) mm, two thousand times less than the difference itself, so we drop this term and the constraint stays a linear equation. For displacements comparable with the length this no longer works: a difference of \(100\) mm already gives \(100^2/(2 \cdot 1000) = 5\) mm and the constraint is no longer linear. Figure 12.54 shows the frame with the support on the inclined base. The thick line is the L-frame with the node \(B\) at the corner and the node \(D\) at the middle of the horizontal part. The lower end of the frame is clamped at \(A\), and at \(D\) the force \(F\) acts on it. The right end stands in the roller support \(C\) on the base inclined at \(30°\). The blue arrow is the normal \(\vek n\), the direction in which \(C\) cannot move. It makes the angle \(30°\) with the vertical, because it is perpendicular to the base and is therefore turned by the same angle as the base. The arrow \(\lambda\,\vek n\) is the force of the support on the frame, which we determine in section 12.6.2.

The condition on \(m\) pivots excludes rows that repeat the same requirement. Figure 12.55 shows three cases in the plane \((d_1, d_2)\), where each constraint is a line. In the left case the constraints \(d_1 + d_2 = 0\) and \(d_1 - d_2 = 0\) are independent and the lines meet in one point. Take the constraint \(d_1 + d_2 = 0\) and write it twice, the second time multiplied by \(2\): \(\mat C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), \(m = 2\). The second row is twice the first, so \(\mat C\) has one pivot and not two, and the condition of the definition does not hold. Let us see what breaks. For \(\vek g = (0, 0)\) the system has a solution, for example \(\vek d = \vek 0\), but \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda = (\lambda_1 + 2\lambda_2)\,(1, 1)^\T\), so the equation \(\mat K\,\vek d^* = \vek f + \mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) determines only the sum \(\lambda_1 + 2\lambda_2\) and not \(\vek\lambda\) (step 3 of the proof of theorem 12.26 fails). For \(\vek g = (0, 1)\) the constraints demand \(d_1 + d_2 = 0\) and \(2(d_1 + d_2) = 1\), which exclude each other, and the set of constraints is empty (step 1 fails). This matrix differs from a support that is a single row in one thing: the same constraint is written twice.

How do we include such constraints in the system \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f\)? A route that stays within chapter 7 is local axes at the node: instead of \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) we take as unknowns the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = \vek n\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) in the direction of the normal and the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{t}} = \vek t\cdot\vek u_{\mathrm{C}}\) along the base, \(\vek t = (\cos 30°, \sin 30°)\). The constraint is then the prescribed unknown \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). This route works when the constraint concerns one node. For a rigid link between two nodes we have to express one unknown by the others, which is the general route that the proof of theorem 12.26 will use. Multipliers and the penalty instead add the constraints to the system without changing the unknowns, and they work for every constraint matrix.

12.6.2 Multipliers

The support in the direction \(\vek n\) acts on the frame with the force \(\lambda\,\vek n\) with an unknown number \(\lambda\), which the frame needs for equilibrium. Why exactly along the normal \(\vek n\)? The roller of the support rolls on the base, so it can neither push the frame along it nor hold it back, and only a force perpendicular to the base is left. This is the assumption of a support without friction (an ideal constraint). For a roller it is justified. For a slider with a friction coefficient of \(0{,}1\) the force would also have a component along the base, of size up to \(0{,}1\,\lambda\), a tenth of the force along the normal, which is not a multiple of the constraint row, and the theorem below does not hold for it. In the load column the force \(\lambda\,\vek n\) has the numbers \(-\lambda\sin 30°\) and \(\lambda\cos 30°\) at the places of \(u_{\mathrm{C}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\) and zero elsewhere, which is \(\lambda\) times the constraint row. Chapter 8 said this for every ideal constraint: the load of the constraint forces is a linear combination of the rows of the constraint matrix, \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) (corollary 8.67). The unknowns are now \(\vek d\) and \(\vek\lambda\), and the equations are equilibrium \(\mat K\,\vek d = \vek f + \mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) and the constraints \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\). The sign is the sign of chapter 8: \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) is the load that the constraints add to the external load \(\vek f\). We get the same system from energy: we seek the smallest value of the potential energy \(\Pi(\vek d) = \tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d - \vek f^\T\vek d\) among the columns that satisfy the constraints. This is the linear case of theorem 8.37 with the gradient \(\mat K\,\vek d - \vek f\) of the function \(\Pi\) (symmetric \(\mat K\)) and with the rows of the matrix \(\mat C\) as the gradients of the constraints. The theorem tells when this route gives a single answer, and proves it without the implicit function theorem, because the constraints are linear.

Figure 12.56 shows the smallest example, \(n = 2\) and \(m = 1\). The line is the set of columns with \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\). On it lie the point \(\vek d_0\) and the direction \(\mat T\vek y\) along which the constraints stay satisfied. The ellipses are lines of constant energy \(\Pi\). The line crosses all ellipses except one, which it touches at the point \(\vek d^*\): there the energy on the line is smallest. At the touching point the gradient \(\mat K\,\vek d^* - \vek f\) is perpendicular to the line, hence parallel to the row of the matrix \(\mat C\), and this is the constraint force \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\).

Figure 12.57 shows the matrix of the system (12.26) made of blocks. The matrix of the system has \(n + m\) rows and columns. Top left is the block \(\mat K\), top right and bottom left are the blocks \(-\mat C^\T\) and \(-\mat C\), and bottom right is the zero block. The first \(n\) steps of elimination give \(n\) positive pivots and change the lower rows, so that the zero block turns into the block \(\mat S\). Its pivots are \(m\) negative pivots.

Theorem 12.26 Minimum of the energy under linear constraints and multipliers (najmanjša energija pri linearnih vezeh in množitelji)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix of order \(n\) (definition 7.15), \(\vek f\) a column with \(n\) entries, \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\) linear constraints (definition 12.25), and \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d > 0\) for every column \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\) with \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek 0\). Then:

  1. The set of columns with \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\) is not empty, and the function \(\Pi(\vek d) = \tfrac12\,\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d - \vek f^\T\vek d\) has exactly one smallest value on it, at the column \(\vek d^*\).
  2. There is exactly one column \(\vek\lambda\) of \(m\) numbers with \(\mat K\,\vek d^* = \vek f + \mat C^\T\vek\lambda\). The column \(\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) is the load of the constraint forces (corollary 8.67).
  3. The system
    \[\begin{pmatrix} \mat K & -\mat C^\T \\ -\mat C & \mat 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \vek d \\ \vek\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \vek f \\ -\vek g \end{pmatrix} \tag{12.26}\]
    is symmetric and has exactly one solution \((\vek d^*, \vek\lambda)\).
  4. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, Gaussian elimination of the matrix of the system (12.26) without row exchanges has first \(n\) positive pivots, then \(m\) negative ones. After \(n\) steps the block \(\mat S = -\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T\) remains. The matrix of the system is therefore not positive definite (lemma 8.70).
Proof

We have to show that the constraint set is not empty and that the energy on it is a strictly convex function of the free numbers, and then we obtain the multipliers from the lemma on linear multipliers. For part 4 we follow the elimination step by step.

  1. By definition 8.21 and the text after it we number the unknowns so that the pivot columns of the matrix \(\mat C\) come last, \(\vek d = (\vek y, \vek z)\), \(\mat C = [\mat C_{\mathrm{y}}\ \mat C_{\mathrm{z}}]\), and \(\mat C_{\mathrm{z}}\) is a square matrix of order \(m\) with \(m\) pivots, hence invertible (lemma 8.22). The numbering shuffles the entries of the columns \(\vek d\) and \(\vek f\) and the rows and columns of the matrix \(\mat K\) in the same way, so it changes neither the values of the function \(\Pi\) nor the assumptions nor the claims (as in step 1 of the proof of lemma 8.36). The equation \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\) is equivalent to \(\vek z = \mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}(\vek g - \mat C_{\mathrm{y}}\vek y)\). The constraint set is not empty: it contains \(\vek d_0 = (\vek 0, \mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\vek g)\). For \(m = n\) there is no column \(\vek y\), \(\mat C = \mat C_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the constraint set is the single point \(\mat C^{-1}\vek g\). For \(m < n\) each of its points is \(\vek d_0 + \mat T\vek y\) with \(\mat T = \begin{pmatrix}\mat I \\ -\mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat C_{\mathrm{y}}\end{pmatrix}\) and an arbitrary \(\vek y\). We have \(\mat C\,\mat T = \mat C_{\mathrm{y}} - \mat C_{\mathrm{z}}\mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^{-1}\mat C_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat 0\), and \(\mat T\vek y \ne \mat T\vek y'\) for \(\vek y \ne \vek y'\), because \(\mat T\) has the unit matrix on top.
  2. For \(m = n\) part 1 holds with \(\vek d^* = \mat C^{-1}\vek g\). Let \(m < n\). Multiplying out the products as in steps 1 to 3 of the proof of lemma 6.30, which use only the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat K\), gives \(\Pi(\vek d_0 + \mat T\vek y) = \tfrac12\,\vek y^\T\mat M\,\vek y - \vek h^\T\vek y + \Pi(\vek d_0)\) with \(\mat M = \mat T^\T\mat K\,\mat T\) and \(\vek h = \mat T^\T(\vek f - \mat K\vek d_0)\). The matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric, and for \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek y^\T\mat M\,\vek y = (\mat T\vek y)^\T\mat K\,(\mat T\vek y) > 0\) by assumption, because \(\mat T\vek y \ne \vek 0\) and \(\mat C\,\mat T\vek y = \vek 0\). The matrix \(\mat M\) is therefore positive definite. By lemma 6.30 the function has exactly one smallest value, at the solution \(\vek y^*\) of the system \(\mat M\vek y = \vek h\) (the constant \(\Pi(\vek d_0)\) does not affect this), and since different \(\vek y\) give different points of the constraint set, \(\Pi\) has exactly one smallest value on the constraint set, at \(\vek d^* = \vek d_0 + \mat T\vek y^*\). This proves part 1.
  3. From \(\mat C^\T\vek x = \vek 0\) follows \(\vek x = \vek 0\). Indeed, the last \(m\) entries of the column \(\mat C^\T\vek x\) are \(\mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^\T\vek x\), and the matrix \(\mat C_{\mathrm{z}}^\T\) is invertible (lemma 8.22).
  4. Let \(\vek\delta\) be a column with \(\mat C\,\vek\delta = \vek 0\) and \(t\) any number. The column \(\vek d^* + t\vek\delta\) satisfies the constraints, so by the multiplied-out form of step 2 \(0 \le \Pi(\vek d^* + t\vek\delta) - \Pi(\vek d^*) = t\,a + \tfrac12\,t^2 b\) with \(a = (\mat K\vek d^* - \vek f)^\T\vek\delta\) and \(b = \vek\delta^\T\mat K\,\vek\delta \ge 0\). For \(a \ne 0\) and \(t = -a/(b + 1)\) the right side equals \(-a^2/(b + 1) + \tfrac12\,a^2 b/(b + 1)^2 = -a^2\,(b/2 + 1)/(b + 1)^2 < 0\), which is a contradiction. Hence \((\mat K\vek d^* - \vek f)\cdot\vek\delta = 0\) for every \(\vek\delta\) with \(\mat C\,\vek\delta = \vek 0\). For \(m < n\), by lemma 8.36 (with the column \(\mat K\vek d^* - \vek f\)) there is a \(\vek\lambda\) with \(\mat K\vek d^* - \vek f = \mat C^\T\vek\lambda\). For \(m = n\) such a \(\vek\lambda\) is \((\mat C^\T)^{-1}(\mat K\vek d^* - \vek f)\) (lemma 8.22). Two such \(\vek\lambda\) have a difference \(\vek x\) with \(\mat C^\T\vek x = \vek 0\), so by step 3 they are equal. This is part 2.
  5. The system (12.26) is symmetric, because \(\mat K\) is symmetric and \((-\mat C^\T)^\T = -\mat C\). Its rows are \(\mat K\vek d - \mat C^\T\vek\lambda = \vek f\) and \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\). The pair \((\vek d^*, \vek\lambda)\) satisfies them by step 4. Let \((\vek d, \vek\lambda')\) satisfy them too. For every \(\vek\delta\) with \(\mat C\,\vek\delta = \vek 0\), by the multiplied-out form of step 2,
    \[\begin{aligned} \Pi(\vek d + \vek\delta) - \Pi(\vek d) &= (\mat K\vek d - \vek f)^\T\vek\delta + \tfrac12\,\vek\delta^\T\mat K\,\vek\delta \\ &= \vek\lambda'^\T\mat C\,\vek\delta + \tfrac12\,\vek\delta^\T\mat K\,\vek\delta = \tfrac12\,\vek\delta^\T\mat K\,\vek\delta \ge 0 \end{aligned}\]
    and every point of the constraint set has the form \(\vek d + \vek\delta\). Hence \(\Pi\) has its smallest value on the constraint set at \(\vek d\), by step 2 \(\vek d = \vek d^*\), then \(\mat C^\T(\vek\lambda' - \vek\lambda) = \vek 0\) and by step 3 \(\vek\lambda' = \vek\lambda\). This is part 3.
  6. Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite. Steps 6 to 9 hold for the original numbering of the unknowns, since step 3 does not depend on the order of the entries of the column \(\mat C^\T\vek x\). From \(\mat K\vek w = \vek 0\) follows \(\vek w^\T\mat K\,\vek w = 0\) and \(\vek w = \vek 0\), so \(\mat K\) is invertible and \((\mat K^{-1})^\T = (\mat K^\T)^{-1} = \mat K^{-1}\) (lemma 8.22). For \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek w = \mat K^{-1}\vek y \ne \vek 0\) and \(\vek y^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek y = (\mat K\vek w)^\T\vek w = \vek w^\T\mat K\,\vek w > 0\). The matrix \(\mat K^{-1}\) is positive definite.
  7. Let \(\mat A\) be the matrix of the system (12.26). Step \(k\) of elimination without row exchanges subtracts from each row below row \(k\) the multiple of row \(k\) that makes a zero below pivot \(k\) (step 4 of the proof of theorem 6.29). In the first \(n\) steps the rows \(1, \dots, n\) change only by subtraction of multiples of rows \(1, \dots, n\), and the multipliers are ratios of entries of the upper left block of order \(n\). This block therefore changes exactly as in the elimination of the matrix \(\mat K\), and the pivots of the first \(n\) steps are the pivots of the matrix \(\mat K\), all positive by theorem 6.29.
  8. After the first \(n\) steps each row \(n + r\) is the original row \(n + r\) of the matrix \(\mat A\) minus a combination of the original rows \(1, \dots, n\), since by step 7 the changed rows \(1, \dots, n\) are also combinations of the original ones. The original rows \(1, \dots, n\) are \([\mat K\ \ {-\mat C^\T}]\), and the rows \(n + 1, \dots, n + m\) are \([-\mat C\ \ \mat 0]\). The lower rows are therefore \([-\mat C - \mat X\mat K\ \ \ \mat X\mat C^\T]\) for some matrix \(\mat X\) with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns. The elimination has made zeros below the first \(n\) pivots, and later steps keep them, because they subtract rows that already have zeros in the earlier columns. Hence \(-\mat C - \mat X\mat K = \mat 0\), by multiplication with \(\mat K^{-1}\) from the right \(\mat X = -\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\), and the remaining block is \(\mat X\mat C^\T = -\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T = \mat S\).
  9. The matrix \(\mat S\) is symmetric by step 6 and lemma 1.102. For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek x^\T(-\mat S)\,\vek x = (\mat C^\T\vek x)^\T\mat K^{-1}(\mat C^\T\vek x) > 0\) by steps 3 and 6, so \(-\mat S\) is positive definite and its elimination without row exchanges has \(m\) positive pivots (theorem 6.29). The elimination of the matrix \(\mat S\) has the same multipliers and in every step the opposite numbers, hence \(m\) negative pivots. The steps \(n + 1, \dots, n + m\) of the elimination of the matrix \(\mat A\) are the elimination of the block \(\mat S\), as in step 4 of the proof of theorem 6.29. Since some pivots are negative, the matrix \(\mat A\) is not positive definite by lemma 8.70. This is part 4.

Source: COOK §13.2 (PDF pp. 506-507, read): multipliers as constraint forces, equations (13.2-2), "The null submatrix in Eq. 13.2-2 becomes nonzero in the course of a Gauss elimination", "the solution process proceeds normally if [K] is by itself positive definite", the sign of the multiplier is not significant. The sign is ours, following chapter 8. The proof is not from a source.

The proof has shown that the constraints leave us a space \(\mat T\vek y\) of free numbers on which the energy is strictly convex, and that the multiplier is exactly the number that the lemma on linear multipliers requires. Steps 1 and 2 are also a computational procedure: we express the dependent unknowns by the free ones and solve the system \(\mat T^\T\mat K\,\mat T\,\vek y = \mat T^\T(\vek f - \mat K\vek d_0)\) of order \(n - m\) with a positive definite matrix. For prescribed unknowns \(\mat C_{\mathrm{y}} = \mat 0\), \(\mat T\) picks the free unknowns and the system is the reduced system of chapter 7 (COOK, PDF p. 504), while for constraints between several nodes we have to rearrange the matrix, which is laborious in programs (COOK, PDF p. 503). Part 4 tells what has happened to the matrix of the system (12.26): it is symmetric but no longer positive definite, so theorem 6.29 does not apply to it. With a positive definite matrix \(\mat K\) elimination without row exchanges still succeeds by part 4. With a singular matrix \(\mat K\) (a structure without supports, the supports being rows of the constraint matrix) it can fail: for our frame with all twelve unknowns, three rows of the clamp and the row of the normal, a zero pivot appears in the tenth step (NumPy), so rows have to be exchanged.

Let us test the theorem on the frame of section 12.4.5. First the roller support on a horizontal base at \(C\): the constraint \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\), the row \(\vek c^\T = \vek e_{\mathrm{v}_\mathrm{C}}^\T\), \(g = 0\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is the matrix of the nine free unknowns after the clamp at \(A\), positive definite. The system (12.26) gives \(\lambda = 226{,}59\) N. This is the reaction \(R = 226{,}590\) N of section 12.4.5: the reaction of a prescribed unknown was the value of \((\mat K\,\vek d - \vek f)\) in its row, which is \((\mat C^\T\vek\lambda)_{\mathrm{v}_\mathrm{C}} = \lambda\). By part 4 of the theorem the last pivot of the matrix of the system is the block \(\mat S = -\vek c^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek c\), computed as \(-6{,}378 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m/N, while the first nine pivots are positive, the smallest \(1{,}045 \cdot 10^4\). The number \(s = \vek c^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek c = 6{,}378 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m/N is the flexibility of the frame without the support at \(C\) in the direction \(v_{\mathrm{C}}\): \(\mat K^{-1}\vek c\) are the displacements due to a unit vertical force at \(C\), and \(\vek c^\T\) picks the vertical displacement of the point \(C\) from them. We computed the same frame with the clamp at \(A\) and the force \(500\) N at \(C\) in section 12.4.3, \(v_{\mathrm{C}} = -31{,}89\) mm, which is \(500\,s\). Let us draw what the multiplier does (figure 12.58). In panel (a) there is no support at \(C\). We draw the unloaded frame grey dashed and orange the frame under the force \(500\) N at \(D\), with the shape only approximate and the displacements enlarged about twenty times. The point \(C\) drops by \(14{,}45\) mm. In panel (b) we remove the support and replace it by its force. The multiplier \(\lambda\) is the vertical force at \(C\) that lifts the point \(C\) back by exactly this amount: \(\lambda\,s = 14{,}45\) mm, \(\lambda = 14{,}45\ \text{mm}/s = 226{,}59\) N, and the frame has \(C\) at its original height again. The multiplier is thus the unknown that chapter 5 chose as the reaction \(R\) of the statically indeterminate frame, and the equation for \(\lambda\) is its condition that \(C\) does not move, except that the system now sets up the unknown and the condition by itself.

The hypothesis \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d > 0\) on the kernel of the matrix \(\mat C\) is not just a matter of style. Its negation is: there is \(\vek d \ne \vek 0\) with \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek 0\) and \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = 0\). Take \(n = 2\), \(\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (semidefinite, as the theorem allows), \(\mat C = (1\ \ 0)\), \(g = 1\) and \(\vek f = (0, 1)^\T\). The constraint determines \(d_1 = 1\), the kernel of \(\mat C\) consists of the columns \((0, t)^\T\), and for all of them \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = 0\), so the hypothesis does not hold. The energy on the set of constraints is \(\Pi(1, t) = 1 - t\), which decreases without bound, so there is no smallest value. The unknown \(d_2\) is held neither by a spring (\(K_{22} = 0\)) nor by a constraint, like a beam with a single hinge in corollary 12.11. For \(\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0{,}01 \end{pmatrix}\) the hypothesis holds, because \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = 0{,}01\,t^2 > 0\) for \(t \ne 0\), and \(\Pi(1, t) = 1 + t^2/200 - t\) has its smallest value at \(t = 100\), \(\Pi = -49\).

Multipliers are exact and do not change the matrix \(\mat K\), but they have a price: the system has \(n + m\) unknowns and its matrix is not positive definite. A second way adds the constraints to the system in a way that keeps the size, the symmetry and positive definiteness.

12.6.3 The penalty method

Imagine that the support at \(C\) is not rigid, but that the node \(C\) is held by a very stiff spring of stiffness \(\alpha\) in the direction \(\vek n\), which is stretched by \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) when the node moves by \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the direction \(\vek n\). At \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\) the spring does not act, otherwise it stores the energy \(\tfrac12\,\alpha\,u_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). If the spring is stiff enough, \(u_{\mathrm{n}}\) will be small. For a general constraint \(\mat C\,\vek d - \vek g\) is the column of the stretches of \(m\) springs, one for each row, and the energy of all springs is \(\tfrac12\,\alpha\,|\mat C\,\vek d - \vek g|^2\) (figure 12.60). COOK describes this on two bars: the spring has in effect added to the structure a bar of axial stiffness \(\alpha k\) ("has in effect added a bar of axial stiffness \(\alpha k\)", COOK, PDF p. 508), where \(k\) is the axial stiffness of a bar of the structure and \(\alpha\) a number without unit.

In figure 12.60 we look only at the surroundings of the node \(C\). The base lies obliquely, the unit vector \(\vek t\) points along it and the unit vector \(\vek n\) perpendicular away from it. The spring stands between the base and the end of the frame at the node \(C\). The hollow circle is the position of the node with the spring unstressed, that is at \(u_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\). The frame pushes the node toward the base and compresses the spring, so the node is at the point \(C\), moved by the distance \(-u_{\mathrm{n}}\) in the direction \(-\vek n\). For a rigid support this penetration is zero, for the spring it is small but not zero: the constraint is met only nearly.

Definition 12.27 Penalty method and penalty number (kazenska metoda in kazensko število)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\vek f\) and the constraints \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek g\) be as in theorem 12.26 and let \(\alpha > 0\) be a real number, the penalty number (kazensko število). The penalty function is

\[\Pi_\alpha(\vek d) = \Pi(\vek d) + \tfrac12\,\alpha\,|\mat C\,\vek d - \vek g|^2 . \tag{12.27}\]

The penalty method (kazenska metoda) takes, instead of the solution \(\vek d^*\) of theorem 12.26, the penalty solution (kazenska rešitev) \(\vek d_\alpha\), the solution of the penalty system (kazenski sistem)

\[(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\,\vek d = \vek f + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\vek g . \tag{12.28}\]

The penalty forces (kazenske sile) are \(\vek\lambda_\alpha = -\alpha\,(\mat C\,\vek d_\alpha - \vek g)\), since (12.28) gives \(\mat K\,\vek d_\alpha = \vek f + \mat C^\T\vek\lambda_\alpha\), equilibrium with the spring forces in the form of theorem 12.26.

Source: COOK §13.3 (PDF pp. 507-508, read): (13.3-2) and (13.3-3) with a diagonal matrix of penalty numbers \([\alpha]\), we take a single number. The penalty forces are our notation.

We expand \(\tfrac12\,\alpha\,|\mat C\,\vek d - \vek g|^2 = \tfrac12\,\vek d^\T(\alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\,\vek d - \alpha\,(\mat C^\T\vek g)^\T\vek d + \tfrac12\,\alpha\,|\vek g|^2\), so \(\Pi_\alpha\) is a quadratic function with the matrix \(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C\) and the right side \(\vek f + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\vek g\), and its smallest value solves (12.28) if this matrix is positive definite (lemma 6.30). The letter \(\alpha\) is the coefficient of thermal expansion in chapters 3 and 7, here it is the penalty number. For a prescribed unknown \(d_{\mathrm{k}} = \bar d\) with the row \(\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\) we have \(\alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C = \alpha\,\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}\vek e_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\): the penalty system adds \(\alpha\) to the diagonal entry \(K_{\mathrm{kk}}\) and \(\alpha\,\bar d\) to the right side of row \(k\), and changes no other number. This is the penalty by which, as chapter 7 announced (section 7.3.3), prescribed values can be imposed. How close is \(\vek d_\alpha\) to the true solution \(\vek d^*\), and how close are the penalty forces to the multipliers? The theorem gives the answer in general, and part 5 gives it for one constraint in closed form, which we then test on the frame.

Figure 12.61 shows the same example with a penalty. The line is the set of columns that satisfy the constraint. Its point \(\vek d^*\) is the true solution, while the penalty solution \(\vek d_\alpha\) lies beside it, not on it. The strip along the line, of width \(|\vek\lambda|/\alpha\), is the region where the penalty solution may be found. The arrow from \(\vek d^*\) to \(\vek d_\alpha\) is the error \(\vek e\). The ellipse centred at \(\vek d_\alpha\) is a line of constant penalty energy and passes through \(\vek d^*\). Below are the multiplier \(\vek\lambda\) and the penalty force \(\vek\lambda_\alpha\) as arrows, the second one shorter.

Theorem 12.28 Error of the penalty method (napaka kazenske metode)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\vek f\), \(\mat C\), \(\vek g\) satisfy the hypotheses of theorem 12.26, and let \(\vek d^*\) and \(\vek\lambda\) be the solution and the multipliers of that theorem. Then the matrix \(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C\) is positive definite, its smallest eigenvalue \(\mu\) is positive, and:

  1. For every \(\alpha > 0\) the penalty system (12.28) has exactly one solution \(\vek d_\alpha\), which is the only minimum of the function \(\Pi_\alpha\).
  2. For every \(\alpha > 0\), \(|\mat C\,\vek d_\alpha - \vek g| \le |\vek\lambda|/\alpha\).
  3. For every \(\alpha > 0\), \((\vek d_\alpha - \vek d^*)^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)(\vek d_\alpha - \vek d^*) \le |\vek\lambda|^2/\alpha\), and for \(\alpha \ge 1\) also \(|\vek d_\alpha - \vek d^*|\) \le |\vek\lambda|/\sqrt{\mu,\alpha}$.
  4. The penalty forces approach the multipliers: \(|\vek\lambda_\alpha - \vek\lambda| \le q\,|\vek\lambda|/\sqrt{\alpha}\) for \(\alpha \ge 1\), where \(q = n\,K_{\max}/\sqrt{\mu\,\nu}\), \(K_{\max}\) is the largest absolute value of the entries of the matrix \(\mat K\) and \(\nu\) is the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat C\,\mat C^\T\), which is positive.
  5. If \(\mat K\) is positive definite, then for every \(\alpha > 0\) the error \(\vek d_\alpha - \vek d^* = -\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T(\mat I + \alpha\,\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T)^{-1}\vek\lambda\). For one constraint with the row \(\vek c^\T\), \(s = \vek c^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek c\) and \(\lambda \ne 0\)
    \[\begin{aligned} \vek d_\alpha - \vek d^* &= -\frac{\lambda}{1 + \alpha s}\,\mat K^{-1}\vek c , \\ &\qquad \lambda_\alpha = \lambda\,\frac{\alpha s}{1 + \alpha s} , \\ &\qquad \frac{\lambda_\alpha - \lambda}{\lambda} = -\frac{1}{1 + \alpha s} . \end{aligned} \tag{12.29}\]
Proof

We have to show first that the penalty system has exactly one solution, and then estimate the constraints, the energy and the forces from the equation for the error. The error \(\vek e = \vek d_\alpha - \vek d^*\) solves the system with the penalty matrix and the right side \(-\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\).

  1. The matrix \(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C\) is symmetric and \(\vek d^\T(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C)\vek d = \vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d + |\mat C\,\vek d|^2 \ge 0\). If the sum is zero, both nonnegative terms are zero, \(\mat C\,\vek d = \vek 0\) and \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = 0\), so by hypothesis \(\vek d = \vek 0\). The matrix is positive definite, its smallest eigenvalue \(\mu\) is positive and \(\vek d^\T(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C)\vek d \ge \mu\,|\vek d|^2\) (theorem 10.42 with \(\mat M = \mat I\)). For \(\alpha \ge 1\), \(\vek d^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\vek d = \vek d^\T(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C)\vek d + (\alpha - 1)\,|\mat C\,\vek d|^2 \ge \mu\,|\vek d|^2\), and for \(0 < \alpha < 1\) it is at least \(\alpha\,\vek d^\T(\mat K + \mat C^\T\mat C)\vek d\), because \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d \ge 0\). In both cases the penalty matrix is positive definite. By theorem 6.29 the system has exactly one solution, and by lemma 6.30 it is the smallest value of the function \(\Pi_\alpha\) (expansion below definition 12.27).
  2. For the error \(\vek e\) we subtract \(\mat K\,\vek d^* = \vek f + \mat C^\T\vek\lambda\) from (12.28) and use \(\mat C\,\vek d^* = \vek g\): \(\mat K\,\vek e + \alpha\,\mat C^\T(\mat C\,\vek d_\alpha - \vek g) = -\mat C^\T\vek\lambda\), and \(\mat C\,\vek d_\alpha - \vek g = \mat C\,\vek e\). Hence
    \[(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\,\vek e = -\mat C^\T\vek\lambda . \tag{12.30}\]
  3. We multiply (12.30) by \(\vek e^\T\): \(\vek e^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\vek e = -(\mat C\,\vek e)\cdot\vek\lambda \le |\mat C\,\vek e|\,|\vek\lambda|\) (Cauchy-Schwarz, lemma 8.24, part 1). Since \(\vek e^\T\mat K\,\vek e \ge 0\), the left side is at least \(\alpha\,|\mat C\,\vek e|^2\), hence \(\alpha\,|\mat C\,\vek e|^2 \le |\mat C\,\vek e|\,|\vek\lambda|\) and \(|\mat C\,\vek e| \le |\vek\lambda|/\alpha\). For \(\mat C\,\vek e = \vek 0\) this holds as well. Since \(\mat C\,\vek e = \mat C\,\vek d_\alpha - \vek g\), this is part 2.
  4. With the estimate of step 3, \(\vek e^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\vek e\) \(\le |\mat C\,\vek e|\,|\vek\lambda| \le |\vek\lambda|^2/\alpha\), the first estimate of part 3. By step 1, for \(\alpha \ge 1\) the left side is at least \(\mu\,|\vek e|^2\), hence \(|\vek e|^2 \le |\vek\lambda|^2/(\mu\,\alpha)\). This is part 3.
  5. From (12.28) and (12.30), \(\mat C^\T(\vek\lambda_\alpha - \vek\lambda)\) \(= \mat K\,\vek d_\alpha - \mat K\,\vek d^* = \mat K\,\vek e\). By lemma 8.24, part 4, \(|\mat K\,\vek e| \le n\,K_{\max}\,|\vek e|\). The matrix \(\mat C\,\mat C^\T\) is symmetric and for \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\vek z^\T\mat C\,\mat C^\T\vek z = |\mat C^\T\vek z|^2 > 0\) by step 3 of the proof of theorem 12.26, so it is positive definite and its smallest eigenvalue \(\nu\) is positive. By theorem 10.42 with \(\mat M = \mat I\), \(|\mat C^\T\vek z|^2 \ge \nu\,|\vek z|^2\). For \(\vek z = \vek\lambda_\alpha - \vek\lambda\) and \(\alpha \ge 1\), by step 4 \(\sqrt{\nu}\,|\vek z| \le n\,K_{\max}\,|\vek e| \le n\,K_{\max}\,|\vek\lambda|/\sqrt{\mu\,\alpha}\), which is part 4.
  6. Let \(\mat K\) be positive definite and \(\mat W = \mat I + \alpha\,\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T\). The matrix \(\mat W\) is symmetric and \(\vek z^\T\mat W\vek z = |\vek z|^2 + \alpha\,(\mat C^\T\vek z)^\T\mat K^{-1}(\mat C^\T\vek z) > 0\) for \(\vek z \ne \vek 0\) (\(\mat K^{-1}\) is symmetric and positive definite by step 6 of the proof of theorem 12.26). The matrix \(\mat W\) is positive definite, hence invertible (theorem 6.29 and lemma 8.22). We have \((\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T = \mat C^\T + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C\,\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T = \mat C^\T\mat W\). Therefore \(\vek e = -\mat K^{-1}\mat C^\T\mat W^{-1}\vek\lambda\) is a solution of equation (12.30), and the only one, because the penalty matrix is invertible. For \(m = 1\), \(\mat C = \vek c^\T\), \(\mat W = 1 + \alpha s\) is a number, and \(\lambda_\alpha - \lambda = -\alpha\,(\mat C\,\vek e) - \lambda = \alpha\,\lambda\,s/(1 + \alpha s) - \lambda = -\lambda/(1 + \alpha s)\), which gives (12.29).

Source: COOK §13.3 (PDF pp. 507-508, read): the example of two bars with the solution (13.3-6), in which the error is proportional to \(1/(1 + \alpha)\). The proof is not from a source, the estimates 2 to 4 do not appear in COOK.

The proof has shown three things. The violation of the constraints at the penalty number \(\alpha\) is at most \(|\vek\lambda|/\alpha\): the larger the force the constraint needs, the larger the stretch of the spring. The energy of the error measured with the penalty matrix, \(\tfrac12\,\vek e^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C)\vek e\), falls like \(1/\alpha\). The estimate of the distance to the true solution falls only like \(1/\sqrt\alpha\), because we bound the energy from below with the smallest eigenvalue, that is for the worst direction. The estimate of part 4 tells only that the penalty forces converge to the multipliers, and its constant is crude: for our frame \(q = 9{,}7 \cdot 10^7\) (mixed units). For one constraint and a positive definite matrix \(\mat K\) the distance and the error of the force are truly proportional to \(1/(1 + \alpha s)\), so for a large \(\alpha s\) they fall like \(1/\alpha\), as formula (12.29) tells, in which \(\alpha s\) is the ratio of the penalty stiffness \(\alpha\) to the stiffness \(1/s\) of the structure in the direction of the constraint: the error is small when the spring is much stiffer than the structure in that direction.

Where \(1 + \alpha s\) comes from is shown by a second view, without matrices, with the two springs of panel (c) of figure 12.58. The first spring is the frame itself: a force of \(1\) N at \(C\) moves the point \(C\) by \(s\), and without the support \(C\) is \(\lambda s\) below its original height (panel (a)). The second is the penalty spring under \(C\). It is compressed by \(-v_{\mathrm{C}}\), so it pushes up with the force \(\lambda_\alpha = \alpha\,(-v_{\mathrm{C}})\), and this force lifts \(C\) by \(\lambda_\alpha s\). The position of the point \(C\) is therefore

\[v_{\mathrm{C}} = -\lambda s + \lambda_\alpha s = -\lambda s - \alpha s\,v_{\mathrm{C}} , \qquad v_{\mathrm{C}} = -\frac{\lambda s}{1 + \alpha s} , \qquad \lambda_\alpha = \lambda\,\frac{\alpha s}{1 + \alpha s} ,\]

which is formula (12.29). In the panel \(\alpha s = 1\), the two springs are equally stiff, so \(C\) stops halfway, \(7{,}226\) mm below its original height, and the penalty spring carries half of the force, \(113{,}29\) N instead of \(226{,}59\) N. The proof of the theorem gives the same for every matrix \(\mat K\), and this view adds the reason: the frame and the spring share the drop according to their stiffnesses, and a stiffer spring leaves the frame a smaller share.

Up to \(\alpha s = 10^8\) both curves lie on the line \(1/(1 + \alpha s)\) and the formula holds to the digits given. For larger numbers the error is larger than predicted. The computer stores every number with about \(16\) decimal digits (double precision, dvojna natančnost) and rounds it at every operation by about \(10^{-16}\) relative (chapter 6), so for a large \(\alpha\) it loses digits, and at \(\alpha s = 10^{16}\) the error is already \(0{,}5\) %. The error of the method falls like \(1/(\alpha s)\), while the rounding error grows with \(\alpha\), so the sum is smallest in between: for \(\alpha s\) between \(10^8\) and \(10^{10}\), with an error of about \(10^{-8}\). If the rounding error grew like \(10^{-16}\,\alpha s\), the sum \(1/(\alpha s) + 10^{-16}\,\alpha s\) would be smallest at \(\alpha s = 10^8\), with the value \(2 \cdot 10^{-8}\). The measured rounding error is smaller (at \(\alpha s = 10^{12}\) it is \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\) instead of \(10^{-4}\)) and does not grow evenly, so this is only an estimate of the order of magnitude. COOK states a rule (PDF p. 513, read): if the penalty matrix is singular and the computer stores about \(d\) decimal digits, the penalty number should not exceed \(10^{d/2}\), because then the entries of the matrix \(\mat K\) still influence the last \(d/2\) digits of the stored entries of the matrix \(\mat K + \alpha\,\mat C^\T\mat C\), and in double precision \(10^{d/2}\) is according to COOK typically \(10^6\) or \(10^7\), which corresponds to \(d = 12\) to \(14\) and not to \(16\) digits. The penalty matrix \(\alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T\) is singular here, because \(\mat C\) has fewer rows than columns (COOK, PDF p. 509). COOK writes the constraint so that \(\alpha\) is a number without unit (PDF p. 508, the row of the constraint is multiplied by the square root of the axial stiffness \(k\) of a bar), so his number is the ratio of the penalty stiffness to the stiffness of a bar, while our \(\alpha s\) is the ratio of the penalty stiffness to the stiffness of the structure in the direction of the constraint. At \(\alpha s = 10^6\) to \(10^7\) the error on the blue curve is \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-7}\) and no rounding is visible yet, so for this frame the rule is one to two orders of magnitude more cautious than the smallest error.

The condition number drops from \(2{,}76 \cdot 10^5\) to \(3{,}0 \cdot 10^4\), because the spring at \(C\) stiffens the softest mode of the frame, in which \(C\) moves, and the smallest eigenvalue rises from \(3410\) to about \(3{,}1 \cdot 10^4\) (mixed units), while the largest stays \(9{,}4 \cdot 10^8\). Once \(\alpha\) exceeds the largest eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat K\), the condition number grows like \(\alpha\). That it grows at least in proportion to \(\alpha\) holds for every positive definite matrix \(\mat K\) and every constraint, as the next lemma shows.

Figure 12.63 shows a line of constant energy of the matrix \(\mat K + \alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T\) in the plane. The ellipse is very elongated. The direction \(\vek c\), in which the penalty spring acts, is the direction of the short semi-axis, because the matrix is very stiff there. The direction \(\vek v_0\), perpendicular to \(\vek c\), does not feel the spring, so the long semi-axis there is what \(\mat K\) gives. The square of the ratio of the semi-axes is the condition number, which grows with \(\alpha\).

Lemma 12.29 Growth of the condition number with the penalty number (rast števila občutljivosti s kazenskim številom)

Let \(\mat K\) be a positive definite matrix of order \(n\), \(\vek c \ne \vek 0\) a column with \(n\) entries and \(\vek v_0 \ne \vek 0\) a column with \(\vek c^\T\vek v_0 = 0\). Then for every \(\alpha > 0\)

\[\kappa(\mat K + \alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T) \ge \frac{\alpha\,|\vek c|^2}{\rho_0} , \qquad \rho_0 = \frac{\vek v_0^\T\mat K\,\vek v_0}{|\vek v_0|^2} . \tag{12.31}\]
Proof

We have to show a lower bound for the largest and an upper bound for the smallest eigenvalue, both with the Rayleigh quotient (theorem 10.42 with \(\mat M = \mat I\)).

  1. The matrix \(\mat K + \alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T\) is symmetric and \(\vek v^\T(\mat K + \alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T)\vek v = \vek v^\T\mat K\,\vek v + \alpha\,(\vek c^\T\vek v)^2 > 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\), so it is positive definite and has a condition number. Its largest eigenvalue is at least the quotient at \(\vek v = \vek c\): \((\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c + \alpha\,|\vek c|^4)/|\vek c|^2 \ge \alpha\,|\vek c|^2\), because \(\vek c^\T\mat K\,\vek c > 0\).
  2. Its smallest eigenvalue is at most the quotient at \(\vek v_0\): \((\vek v_0^\T\mat K\,\vek v_0 + \alpha\,(\vek c^\T\vek v_0)^2)/|\vek v_0|^2 = \rho_0\), because \(\vek c^\T\vek v_0 = 0\), and \(\rho_0\) does not depend on \(\alpha\).
  3. The ratio of the two (definition 6.31) is at least \(\alpha\,|\vek c|^2/\rho_0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. COOK (PDF p. 508) mentions "ill-conditioning" as a phenomenon.

With the choice \(\vek v_0 = \vek d^*\) (it satisfies \(\vek c^\T\vek d^* = 0\)) we get \(\rho_0 = 5{,}24 \cdot 10^4\) for the horizontal base and \(1{,}30 \cdot 10^5\) for the inclined one, both with \(|\vek c| = 1\), hence at \(\alpha s = 10^8\) the lower bounds \(3{,}0 \cdot 10^7\) and \(1{,}1 \cdot 10^7\) (the computed condition numbers are \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^7\) and \(4{,}4 \cdot 10^7\)). The lemma tells that the condition number grows at least like \(\alpha\), but not what effect this has on the solution. The horizontal base is an example without effect: the error \((\lambda_\alpha - \lambda)/\lambda\) stays below \(3 \cdot 10^{-14}\) up to \(\alpha s = 10^{20}\), although the condition number grows to \(5 \cdot 10^{19}\). For the inclined base rounding already overwhelms the formula at \(\alpha s = 10^{10}\). The difference lies in where the large number goes. For the horizontal base the penalty matrix is \(\alpha\,\vek e_{\mathrm{v}_\mathrm{C}}\vek e_{\mathrm{v}_\mathrm{C}}^\T\), so \(\alpha\) is added to one diagonal entry. For the inclined one it is \(\alpha\,\vek c\,\vek c^\T\) with the off-diagonal entries \(-\alpha\sin 30°\cos 30° = -0{,}433\,\alpha\) and the diagonal entries \(\alpha\sin^2 30° = 0{,}25\,\alpha\) and \(\alpha\cos^2 30° = 0{,}75\,\alpha\). COOK says for a similar case (PDF p. 319, read) that a very stiff spring in an oblique direction, if the unknowns \(u_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{A}}\) are kept, introduces large off-diagonal entries and ill-conditioning with an error in the subtraction of nearly equal numbers, but that the same spring causes no trouble if we take as the unknowns of the node the displacements along the spring and perpendicular to it, because the large number is then only on the diagonal. This is the route of local axes from the beginning of the section. Our computation agrees with this, but this is an observation, not a proof.

Let us summarize the routes. Elimination of dependent unknowns, that is the partition of chapter 7, local axes at the node or in general steps 1 and 2 of the proof of theorem 12.26, is exact and gives a smaller system with a positive definite matrix, but it requires a choice of the dependent unknowns and a rearrangement of the matrix, which is laborious for constraints between several nodes. Multipliers are exact for every constraint matrix and do not change the matrix \(\mat K\), but they add \(m\) unknowns and the matrix of the system is not positive definite. The penalty method keeps the size of the system and positive definiteness and works for every constraint matrix, but it is approximate, for one constraint with the relative error of the force \(1/(1 + \alpha s)\), and it requires a choice of \(\alpha s\), for our frame between \(10^4\) and \(10^8\), where the error of the method and the rounding error are below \(10^{-4}\).

A penalty, however, sometimes arises without anybody adding it. The Timoshenko beam of chapter 5 has the shear stiffness \(kGA\), which for a slender element of length \(h\) is much larger than \(EI/h^2\), and COOK (PDF pp. 509-510, read) says that shear locking is an instance of a penalty constraint. Later in this chapter we shall show that the finite element system of such a beam is indeed the penalty system of definition 12.27 with \(\alpha = kGA\), and theorem 12.28 will tell what happens as \(\alpha\) grows.
