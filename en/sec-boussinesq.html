Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.4 A point force on a half-space: the Boussinesq solution

In section 25.1 every point of the foundation acted on its own: under the beam, a displacement \(v\) produced a force \(k\,v\) per unit length, and neighbouring points did not know each other. A real foundation, the ground under a footing or the steel of the ring on which a ball rests, does not act like that. If we press on one point only, its surroundings sink as well, as a mattress sinks around a single fist. The question of this section is therefore: by how much does the surface of an elastic body sink at a distance \(r\) from the point where a force \(F\) presses, and what stresses does the force cause inside? We need the answer for the contact of two bodies, where we must know how an elastic body gives way under a pressure on a small part of its surface.

The body we take is a half-space (polprostor): all the points with \(z \geq 0\), with the plane \(z = 0\) as its surface. The name says that we cut space in two by a plane and kept one half. The half-space is a model of a body that is much larger than the region where we press: the distances at which we want the field are much smaller than the dimensions of the body, so the far edge of the body has no influence (for a force of \(1000\) N the normal stress at a depth of \(10\) mm below the force is only \(4{,}8\) MPa, as we shall compute below). The material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, with modulus \(E\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) (theorem 3.11), the strains are small and the surface is smooth and frictionless. Let us take the steel of the book, \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), and a force \(F = 1000\) N.

Let us draw figure 25.13, the left panel first. The surface is the horizontal line, the body is below it, and we measure the distance from the axis of the force with \(r\) along the surface. The force \(F\) presses downward at the point \(O\), so the arrow ends at \(O\), like any push. The axis \(z\) goes from \(O\) into the body, and \(R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\) is the distance of a point from \(O\). The letter \(R\) has another role later in the chapter, the radius of curvature, but in this section it is only a distance. We write the force \(F\), not \(P\), because we kept \(P\) for the load of the beam (section 25.1).

We shall first state and check the solution, not derive it, because the derivation with potentials (BARBER §23.2.1, PDF p. 372-374) goes beyond what the book has. Checking is an honest proof: we look for a displacement field that meets all the requirements, and verifying them is a calculation. We shall not prove uniqueness, see below the theorem.

Theorem 25.15 Boussinesq solution (Boussinesqova rešitev)

Let a half-space \(z \geq 0\) be made of a homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material with \(E\) and \(\nu \neq -1\), \(\nu \neq \tfrac12\) (shear modulus \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\), definition 3.9), let \(F > 0\) and \(R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\). The displacement field

\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{F}{4\pi G}\Big(\frac{x z}{R^3} - \frac{(1 - 2\nu)\,x}{R\,(R + z)}\Big) , \quad u_{\mathrm{y}} = \frac{F}{4\pi G}\Big(\frac{y z}{R^3} - \frac{(1 - 2\nu)\,y}{R\,(R + z)}\Big) , \quad u_{\mathrm{z}} = \frac{F}{4\pi G}\Big(\frac{2(1 - \nu)}{R} + \frac{z^2}{R^3}\Big) \tag{25.11}\]

has continuous second derivatives at every point with \(R > 0\) and \(z \geq 0\), and:

  1. it satisfies the Navier-Lamé equations (11.3) without body force,
  2. it has the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\dfrac{3F\,x z^2}{2\pi R^5}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = -\dfrac{3F\,y z^2}{2\pi R^5}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\dfrac{3F\,z^3}{2\pi R^5}\), so on every plane \(z = c > 0\) the vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}})\) points along a line through \(O\) and has the length \(3F c^2/(2\pi R^4)\),
  3. the surface \(z = 0\) outside \(O\) is free of load, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\),
  4. on every plane \(z = c > 0\) the resultant of the stresses on the disc of radius \(\rho\) centred on the \(z\) axis has the components \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), and its component \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) tends to \(-F\) as \(\rho \to \infty\),
  5. the displacements of the surface are \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \dfrac{F\,(1 - \nu^2)}{\pi E\,r}\) and in the radial direction \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = -\dfrac{(1 - 2\nu)(1 + \nu)\,F}{2\pi E\,r}\) (towards \(O\)), \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\),
  6. the displacements go to zero like \(1/R\) and the stresses like \(1/R^2\) as \(R \to \infty\).
Proof

We must show six claims. We compute the derivatives with the chain rule with \(\partial R/\partial x = x/R\), \(\partial R/\partial y = y/R\), \(\partial R/\partial z = z/R\) and simplify the expressions with \(R^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\), which is a calculation in SymPy (outlines/ch-kontakt-lezaji/num-04.py). One more relation is important, \((R + z)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + 2z^2 + 2Rz\).

  1. The fields (25.11) are made of powers of \(R\) and quotients with \(R + z\), which is positive on \(z \geq 0\) at \(R > 0\). So they have continuous second derivatives. The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}\vek u = -\dfrac{(1 - 2\nu)\,F\,z}{2\pi G R^3}\) and its derivatives are \(\dfrac{\partial\operatorname{div}\vek u}{\partial x} = \dfrac{3(1 - 2\nu)F\,x z}{2\pi G R^5}\), the same for \(y\), and \(\dfrac{\partial\operatorname{div}\vek u}{\partial z} = -\dfrac{(1 - 2\nu)F\,(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2)}{2\pi G R^5}\).
  2. The Laplace operators (definition 11.5) of the components are \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{x}} = -\dfrac{3F\,x z}{2\pi G R^5}\), the same for \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with \(y\), and \(\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{z}} = \dfrac{F\,(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2)}{2\pi G R^5}\).
  3. By theorem 11.6 we must show \(\mu\,\nabla^2 u_{\mathrm{i}} + (\lambda + \mu)\,\partial(\operatorname{div}\vek u)/\partial x_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) with \(\mu = G\) and \(\lambda + \mu = G/(1 - 2\nu)\) (definition 3.10, \(\lambda = 2G\nu/(1 - 2\nu)\)). Component \(x\): \(G\cdot\Big(-\dfrac{3F\,x z}{2\pi G R^5}\Big) + \dfrac{G}{1 - 2\nu}\cdot\dfrac{3(1 - 2\nu)F\,x z}{2\pi G R^5} = -\dfrac{3F\,x z}{2\pi R^5} + \dfrac{3F\,x z}{2\pi R^5} = 0\), the same for \(y\). Component \(z\): \(\dfrac{F\,(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2)}{2\pi R^5} - \dfrac{F\,(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2)}{2\pi R^5} = 0\). This is claim 1. We repeated the calculation exactly, with rational numbers, at the points \((1, 2, 2)\), \((2, 3, 6)\), \((4, 4, 7)\) and \((2, 6, 9)\), where \(R\) is the integer \(3\), \(7\), \(9\), \(11\), and got zero everywhere.
  4. We get the stresses from (11.4): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \lambda\operatorname{div}\vek u + 2G\,\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\,(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial z + \partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial x)\). After differentiating and simplifying, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\dfrac{3F\,z^3\,(R + z)^2}{2\pi R^5 (R + z)^2}\), because the numerator is \(2Rz + x^2 + y^2 + 2z^2 = (R + z)^2\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\dfrac{3F\,x z^2}{2\pi R^5}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) the same. The vector \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}) = -\dfrac{3F z^2}{2\pi R^5}\,(x, y, z)\) is proportional to \((x, y, z)\), so it points along a line through \(O\), and on the plane \(z = c\) its length is \(\dfrac{3F c^2}{2\pi R^5}\,R = \dfrac{3F c^2}{2\pi R^4}\). This is claim 2.
  5. At \(z = 0\) all three stresses have the factor \(z^2\) and \(R = r > 0\), so they are zero. This is claim 3.
  6. For the plane \(z = c\) we use cylindrical coordinates (lemma 11.15, \(J = r\)) and theorem 4.59. On a disc of radius \(\rho\), \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -\dfrac{3F c^3}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\rho} \dfrac{r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{(r^2 + c^2)^{5/2}} = -3F c^3\Big[-\dfrac{1}{3(r^2 + c^2)^{3/2}}\Big]_0^{\rho} = -F\Big(1 - \dfrac{c^3}{(\rho^2 + c^2)^{3/2}}\Big)\), which tends to \(-F\) as \(\rho \to \infty\) (definition 9.16). For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) we have \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 0\), the same for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) with \(\sin\theta\). This is claim 4.
  7. At \(z = 0\) we have \(R = r\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = \dfrac{F}{4\pi G}\cdot\dfrac{2(1 - \nu)}{r} = \dfrac{F(1 - \nu)}{2\pi G r}\). Since \(1/G = 2(1 + \nu)/E\), this is \(\dfrac{F(1 - \nu^2)}{\pi E r}\). The radial component: \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = -\dfrac{F(1 - 2\nu)\,x}{4\pi G\,r^2}\), the same for \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) with \(y\), so \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = -\dfrac{F(1 - 2\nu)}{4\pi G\,r} = -\dfrac{(1 - 2\nu)(1 + \nu)F}{2\pi E r}\). This is claim 5.
  8. Every term of (25.11) is divided by \(t\) when we replace \((x, y, z)\) by \((t x, t y, t z)\) with \(t > 0\), so \(\vek u(t\vek x) = \vek u(\vek x)/t\) (SymPy). Differentiating this equation with respect to \(x\) gives \(t\,\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial x}(t\vek x) = \dfrac{1}{t}\,\dfrac{\partial\vek u}{\partial x}(\vek x)\), so the derivatives are divided by \(t^2\), likewise with respect to \(y\) and \(z\), and the stresses with them. For \(t = 1/R\) we get \(\vek u(\vek x) = \vek u(\vek x/R)/R\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek x) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}(\vek x/R)/R^2\). The point \(\vek x/R\) lies on the hemisphere \(R = 1\), \(z \geq 0\), which is a closed and bounded set, and there \(R + z \geq 1\). So the displacements and stresses are continuous on it and by theorem 1.78 bounded by some constant \(M\). Hence \(|u_{\mathrm{i}}| \leq M/R\) and \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}| \leq M/R^2\). This is claim 6.

Source: BARBER §23.2.1, (23.14), (23.17), (23.18) (PDF p. 373-374): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) in the form \(3z^3/R^5\) with a potential of another normalisation, the surface displacements with proportionality \(1/r\) and the same factors \((1 - 2\nu)\) and \((1 - \nu)\). TIMO (PDF p. 268-269): the direction of the stress resultant towards the point of loading, the dependence \(1/R^2\) and \(w = P(1 - \nu^2)/(\pi E r)\). We checked the field (25.11) in SymPy, the proof is a verification, it is not from the source. We do not prove uniqueness of the solution (see the text).

The proof showed that the field (25.11) solves the equations in the interior, that the surface is free of load everywhere except at the point \(O\), and that every plane below the surface carries the whole force \(F\). The last is what we expect of a point load: we cut off the part of the body above the plane \(z = c\) and the force \(F\) acts downward on it, so by the equilibrium of a part of a body (corollary 4.41, used here without proof on an unbounded part containing the point \(O\), so only as an expectation, the proof is step 6) the lower part must act with \(F\) upward, which with the normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek t = \ten\sigma\,\vek n\) is exactly \(\int \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = -F\).

We do not prove uniqueness. Theorem 11.4 holds for bounded bodies and smooth data, while the half-space is unbounded and a force at a point is not a smooth load (we do not prove uniqueness of the contact problem of an interference fit either, section 11.7.6, there because the contact pressure is not given). For the chosen solution it helps that the displacements and stresses decay (claim 6), and that the displacements depend on \(1/r\), which also follows from a dimensional argument (BARBER PDF p. 374), but this is not a proof of uniqueness.

From history

Joseph Valentin Boussinesq (1842-1929) was the most distinguished pupil of Saint-Venant (TIMO, PDF p. 267). In 1867 he took his doctorate and at about the same time wrote a treatise on the theory of elasticity, which Saint-Venant reviewed and at once recognised the young author as exceptional (TIMO, PDF p. 267). In 1873 he became a professor in Lille (TIMO, PDF p. 268). His most important works are collected in a book on the application of potentials to the equilibrium and motion of elastic bodies, published in Paris in 1885 (TIMO, PDF p. 268). Timoshenko writes that among his solutions the most important for practice are those of the stresses and deformations in a semi-infinite body due to known forces on the boundary plane (TIMO, PDF p. 268). In the simplest case the force acts perpendicular to the plane. Boussinesq found that the resultant of the stress on a horizontal plane points along the line through the loaded point and is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from it (TIMO, PDF p. 268-269). For the vertical displacement of the boundary plane he obtained \(w = P(1 - \nu^2)/(\pi E r)\), so the product \(w r\) is constant and the lines from the origin on the boundary turn into hyperbolas with the asymptotes \(Or\) and \(Oz\) after deformation (TIMO, PDF p. 269).

Let us return to the field (25.11): the historical result is claim 5 of the theorem, and the corollary is its special case.

Corollary 25.16 Deflection of the surface of a half-space (poves površja polprostora)

Under the conditions of theorem 25.15, for every \(r > 0\) the deflection of the surface is \(w(r) = u_{\mathrm{z}}(r, 0) = \dfrac{F\,(1 - \nu^2)}{\pi E\,r}\), so the product \(w\,r = \dfrac{F\,(1 - \nu^2)}{\pi E}\) is constant, the graph of \(w\) over \(r\) is a hyperbola with the asymptotes the \(r\)-axis and the \(z\)-axis, and \(w \to \infty\) as \(r \to 0\).

Proof

By claim 5 of theorem 25.15, \(u_{\mathrm{z}}(r, 0) = F(1 - \nu^2)/(\pi E r)\). If \(w(r_1) = C/r_1\) and \(w(r_2) = C/r_2\), then \(w(r_1)\,r_1 = C = w(r_2)\,r_2\) for any two distances, so the rectangles \(r_1 \times w_1\) and \(r_2 \times w_2\) with the common vertex \(O\) and sides on the axes are not similar, but they have the same area \(C\). As \(r \to 0\) the denominator has the limit zero and the numerator is constant, so \(w\) grows beyond every bound.

Source: TIMO (PDF p. 269), BARBER (23.18) (PDF p. 374). The proof is a corollary of the theorem.

Let us look at this on the graph of the deflection in the left panel of figure 25.13: the blue curve is \(w = C/r\) and its value is read by the scale downward. Two rectangular areas are the same number \(C\): at \(r_1 = 1\) the deflection \(w_1\) is four times larger than the deflection \(w_2\) at \(r_2 = 4\), because \(r_2 = 4r_1\) and \(w r\) is constant. For our steel and the force \(1000\) N, \(C = F(1 - \nu^2)/(\pi E) = 1{,}478 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m\(^2\), so \(w = 1{,}478\ \mu\text{m}\) at \(r = 1\) mm, \(0{,}148\ \mu\text{m}\) at \(10\) mm and \(14{,}78\ \mu\text{m}\) at \(0{,}1\) mm (SymPy, num-04.py). The radial displacement is towards \(O\) and is \(0{,}422\ \mu\text{m}\) at \(r = 1\) mm, so smaller than the deflection by the factor \((1 - 2\nu)/(2(1 - \nu)) = 0{,}286\).

It is also worth seeing how such a deflection arises. For the foundation of section 25.1 the displacement of a point of the foundation is the force per unit length with which the beam presses on the foundation at that point, divided by \(k\), so it depends only on that force. Here the deflection at the distance \(r\) depends on the force acting at the distance \(r\) away, and it decays only like \(1/r\), more slowly than for any foundation with only local stiffness. So for the half-space we shall have to add up the influence of the pressure from the whole region of contact.

Let us look at what the force does in the interior. The middle panel of figure 25.13 is a section of the body in true proportions. It shows the same surface and the same force \(F\) at the point \(O\) as on the left. The plane \(z = c\) is at the depth \(c\) below \(O\). We draw the dashed rays from \(O\) to chosen points of the plane, because by claim 2 of theorem 25.15 the stress resultant points along such lines. The orange arrows are the stresses that the part of the body above the plane transmits to the lower part. Each ends on the plane, on its own line, because they push the lower part downward, and their length falls with \((c/R)^4\), so quickly as we move away from the axis: at \(r = c\) the length is \(0{,}25\) of the length on the axis, and at \(r = 2c\) it is \(0{,}04\) (by \(3F c^2/(2\pi R^4)\), SymPy). The right panel shows only the vertical component: horizontally \(r/c\), vertically \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}|\) divided by its value on the axis. The bell is \((1 + (r/c)^2)^{-5/2}\), the stress \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}| = \dfrac{3F}{2\pi c^2}\big(1 + (r/c)^2\big)^{-5/2}\) divided by its value on the axis. When we rotate the area under the bell for the undivided stress about the axis, the solid obtained has by claim 4 the volume \(F\). At \(c = 5\) mm and \(F = 1000\) N, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) on the axis is \(-19{,}10\) MPa, at \(r = 5\) mm \(-3{,}376\) MPa, at \(10\) mm \(-0{,}342\) MPa and at \(15\) mm \(-0{,}060\) MPa. So at the depth \(c\) the load spreads over a disc of radius a few times \(c\). We trust the field of a point force as a stand-in for a pressure on a small region only at a distance much larger than the size of the region (at a distance of two radii of a loaded disc the difference is \(3{,}5\ \%\), at six radii \(0{,}35\ \%\), as we shall see below).

How close to \(O\) we may trust linear theory itself is told by the size of the derivatives of the displacements. On the surface the largest are \(|\partial u_{\mathrm{z}}/\partial r| = |\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial z| = C/r^2\), the others are smaller or zero (SymPy). At \(r = 1\) mm this is \(1{,}5\cdot 10^{-3}\), at \(r = 0{,}3\) mm it is \(1{,}6\cdot 10^{-2}\) and at \(r = 0{,}1\) mm it is \(0{,}148\). Linear theory assumes that all derivatives of the displacements are below about \(0{,}001\) in size (section 3.2.1, assumption 4), so for our force it holds for \(r \gtrsim 1{,}2\) mm, \(r = \sqrt{C/0{,}001}\).

Near the point of loading. As \(r \to 0\) the deflection goes beyond every bound (corollary 25.16), and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) on the axis goes as \(1/z^2\) as \(z \to 0\). This is not a mistake of the solution but a measure of the model: a force at one point means infinite pressure, because pressure is force over area and the area is zero. No steel bears such a pressure, so the model stops being used long before infinity is put into the calculation, at the yield limit. Timoshenko writes that we can help ourselves by cutting out the material near the point with a hemispherical surface of small radius and replacing the force by statically equivalent forces distributed over this surface (TIMO, PDF p. 269). The second way out, which we shall use, means the same: the force acts on a finite region of the surface, as a pressure.

25.4.1 Pressure on a finite region

Let the force act not at a point but as a pressure \(p\) on a region \(A\) of the surface. Pressure (tlak) is a function of a point of the surface: at the point \(s \in A\) the number \(p(s)\) is the force per unit area in N/m\(^2\) (in MPa), and the force on a part \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\) of the region is the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p\,\mathrm{d}A\) over a set with an area (definition 4.30). The sum over the parts is the whole force \(F = \int_{\mathrm{A}} p\,\mathrm{d}A\). We know how the surface sinks only for a point force, so we cut the region into finitely many parts, replace the force of each part by a point force and add the fields by the principle of superposition. Does the sum settle as the parts become ever smaller? Figure 25.14 (left) shows what we draw for the proof.

First the disc of radius \(a\) and centre \(O\): the region on which the pressure acts. In it is the point \(x\), at which we look for the deflection. If \(x\) lies outside the region, the function \(1/|x - s|\) is continuous on it, which we shall use in step 2. If it lies inside, the function is unbounded as \(s \to x\). So from \(x\) we draw a sector between two rays, where the distance \(\rho = |x - s|\) varies from zero to the edge, and write the integral in polar coordinates around \(x\), where the factor \(\rho\) from the Jacobian determinant just cancels \(1/\rho\). The length of this ray up to the edge is the chord \(\ell(\varphi)\) at the angle \(\varphi\).

Lemma 25.17 Deflection under a pressure (poves pod tlakom)

The material and model as in theorem 25.15. Let \(A\) be a closed convex set with an area on the surface \(z = 0\) (a disc, an ellipse, a rectangle) of diameter \(D\) (the diameter of a set is the largest distance between two of its points), and \(p \geq 0\) a continuous pressure function on \(A\), \(p \leq p_{\max}\), \(F = \int_{\mathrm{A}} p\,\mathrm{d}A\). For a point \(x\) of the surface we denote

\[w(x) = \frac{1 - \nu^2}{\pi E}\int_{\mathrm{A}} \frac{p(s)}{|x - s|}\,\mathrm{d}A(s) . \tag{25.12}\]
  1. If \(x\) has the distance \(d > 0\) from \(A\) and we cut \(A\) into parts \(A_1, \dots, A_{\mathrm{n}}\) of diameter at most \(h\), with points \(s_{\mathrm{j}} \in A_{\mathrm{j}}\) and forces \(F_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p\,\mathrm{d}A\), the deflection of the surface at \(x\) due to the point forces \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) at \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x) = \dfrac{1 - \nu^2}{\pi E}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \dfrac{F_{\mathrm{j}}}{|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|}\) and \(\big|w_{\mathrm{n}}(x) - w(x)\big| \leq \dfrac{1 - \nu^2}{\pi E}\cdot\dfrac{F\,h}{d^2}\).
  2. If \(x\) lies in \(A\), the integrand is unbounded at \(s = x\), so there the integral (25.12) means the limit of the integrals over \(A\) without the disc of radius \(\varepsilon\) around \(x\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). This limit exists and in polar coordinates around \(x\) it is \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\ell(\varphi)} p\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\), where \(\ell(\varphi) \leq D\) is the length of the ray to the edge, and hence \(w(x) \leq \dfrac{1 - \nu^2}{\pi E}\,2\pi D\,p_{\max}\).
Proof

We must show an estimate for a point outside and finiteness for a point inside the region.

  1. The field of the point force \(F_{\mathrm{j}}\) at \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the field (25.11), shifted to \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\). By theorem 25.15 it satisfies the equations, and by corollary 11.3 (its proof uses only the linearity of derivatives and of Hooke's law, so it holds for the half-space as well) the sum of the fields is a field with displacements and stresses that are the same sums. On the surface, by corollary 25.16, the displacement is \(F_{\mathrm{j}}(1 - \nu^2)/(\pi E\,|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|)\), which gives \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  2. For every \(j\) the difference \(\int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p/|x - s|\,\mathrm{d}A - F_{\mathrm{j}}/|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|\) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p(s)\big(\dfrac{1}{|x - s|} - \dfrac{1}{|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|}\big)\mathrm{d}A\), because \(F_{\mathrm{j}} = \int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p\,\mathrm{d}A\). Since \(|x - s|\) and \(|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|\) are both at least \(d\) and differ by at most \(|s - s_{\mathrm{j}}| \leq h\), \(\Big|\dfrac{1}{|x - s|} - \dfrac{1}{|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|}\Big| = \dfrac{\big||x - s_{\mathrm{j}}| - |x - s|\big|}{|x - s|\,|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|} \leq \dfrac{h}{d^2}\). So the difference is \(|\int_{\mathrm{A}_\mathrm{j}} p/|x - s|\,\mathrm{d}A - F_{\mathrm{j}}/|x - s_{\mathrm{j}}|| \leq F_{\mathrm{j}}\,h/d^2\). We add over \(j\) and use \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} F_{\mathrm{j}} = F\) (additivity of the integral over a set). This is the estimate of claim 1. As \(h \to 0\) the right side tends to zero, so \(w_{\mathrm{n}}(x)\) tends to \(w(x)\).
  3. For \(x \in A\) we take \(A\) without the disc of radius \(\varepsilon\) around \(x\). Since \(A\) is convex, the set of points of \(A\) on the ray at the angle \(\varphi\) from \(x\) is a segment \(0 \leq \rho \leq \ell(\varphi)\) with \(\ell \leq D\). In polar coordinates around \(x\), \(J = \rho\) (theorem 4.59, \(\Phi(\rho, \varphi) = x + \rho\,(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\)), so \(\int p/|x - s|\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_\varepsilon^{\ell(\varphi)} (p/\rho)\,\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\), where \(\rho\) cancels \(1/\rho\) (where \(\ell(\varphi) < \varepsilon\), the inner integral is zero).
  4. The integrand \(p\) is continuous and bounded by \(p_{\max}\), and \(\ell(\varphi) \leq D\), so the integral \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\ell(\varphi)} p\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\) exists and is at most \(2\pi D\,p_{\max}\). It differs from the integral of step 3 by the integral over \(0 \leq \rho \leq \varepsilon\), which is at most \(2\pi\varepsilon\,p_{\max}\) and tends to zero as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). So the limit exists and equals this integral. This is claim 2.

Source: TIMO (PDF p. 269): Boussinesq solves a load distributed over a part of the boundary plane by the principle of superposition. The proof is not from the source. For a point inside the region we do not prove that the value of the integral is the value of the true solution for a pressure, but take it as a definition and check it against TIMO on the example of a disc below (cited, not proved).

The proof showed two things: for points outside the region the sum of point forces does settle at the integral, with an error estimate proportional to \(h\), and for points inside the integral is finite although the integrand has a singularity. This is exactly what the point force lacks.

Uniform pressure on a disc. Let \(p = q\) be constant on a disc of radius \(a\). For \(x\) at the centre, \(\ell(\varphi) = a\) for every \(\varphi\) and by claim 2, \(\int_0^{2\pi} a\,\mathrm{d}\varphi = 2\pi a\), so \(w(0) = \dfrac{1 - \nu^2}{\pi E}\,q\,2\pi a = \dfrac{2(1 - \nu^2)\,q\,a}{E}\). For \(x\) on the edge, with \(\varphi\) measured from the direction towards the centre, \(\ell(\varphi) = 2a\cos\varphi\) for \(|\varphi| < \pi/2\) and zero elsewhere, so \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 2a\cos\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi = 4a\) and \(w(a) = \dfrac{4(1 - \nu^2)\,q\,a}{\pi E} = \dfrac{2}{\pi}\,w(0)\). TIMO (PDF p. 269) gives the same two numbers: \(w_0 = 2(1 - \nu^2)\,q\,a/E\) at the centre and \(2w_0/\pi\) at the edge. Our calculation gives them without quoting, so this is a verification of that claim (SymPy and NumPy, num-04.py). Let us take \(F = 1000\) N on a disc of radius \(a = 1\) mm, \(q = F/(\pi a^2) = 318{,}3\) MPa: \(w(0) = 2{,}956\ \mu\text{m}\), \(w(a) = 1{,}882\ \mu\text{m}\), and the point force would give \(1{,}478\ \mu\text{m}\) at the distance \(a\). The right panel of figure 25.14 shows the course of the deflection divided by the deflection at the centre, over \(r/a\): the blue curve is the deflection of the disc, at the edge it is \(2/\pi = 0{,}637\) of the deflection at the centre, and the orange curve is the deflection of the point force at the centre. At \(r = 2a\) the disc is \(3{,}5\ \%\) above the point force (\(0{,}7645\) against \(0{,}7389\ \mu\text{m}\)), and at \(r = 6a\) only \(0{,}35\ \%\), as we expect: from far away we cannot see whether the force acts at a point or on a small disc.

Sum and integral. Let us check the estimate of claim 1 at \(x\) at the distance \(2a\) from the centre, where \(d = a\), with \(a = 1\) mm and \(F = 1000\) N. We divide the plane into small squares of side \(2a/n\). The part \(A_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the intersection of a square with the disc, its force is \(F_{\mathrm{j}} = q\) times the area of the part, the point \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the centroid of the part, and the forces add up to \(F\). The exact value is \(w(2a) = 0{,}7645\ \mu\text{m}\) and the deviation of the sum \(w_{\mathrm{n}}\) at \(n = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\) is \(-1{,}49\ \%\), \(-0{,}33\ \%\), \(-0{,}081\ \%\), \(-0{,}021\ \%\), \(-0{,}0049\ \%\), \(-0{,}0013\ \%\) (NumPy, areas and centroids approximately from \(200 \times 200\) points in each square). The estimate of claim 1 gives at most \(8{,}5\ \%\) at \(n = 64\) (the diameter of a part is at most \(h = \sqrt{2}\cdot 2a/64\)), while the actual deviation \(0{,}0013\ \%\) is much smaller: it falls by about a factor of four at each halving of the side, the estimate only by a factor of two. The estimate is therefore reliable but coarse. It falls with \(h\), which is what we need.

The point force is not a limit. Let us take the same force \(F = 1000\) N on an ever smaller disc: \(a = 1\) mm gives \(w(0) = 2{,}956\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(q = 318\) MPa, \(a = 0{,}1\) mm gives \(29{,}56\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(q = 31\,830\) MPa, \(a = 0{,}01\) mm gives \(295{,}6\ \mu\text{m}\) and \(q = 3{,}18\cdot 10^6\) MPa. The product \(w(0)\,a = 2(1 - \nu^2)F/(\pi E) = 2C\) is constant, so \(w(0) = 2C/a \to \infty\) as \(a \to 0\), as for a point force. So the deflection has no finite limit as \(a \to 0\), and we must not regard the point force as the limiting case of contact. Contact requires a finite region, which we do not know in advance, since it depends on the deflection. This will be the task of the following sections.
