Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.9 The rotating disk

So far the disk of the rotor has been a rigid body for us, with a mass \(m\) and two moments of inertia (sections 24.1 and 24.3). A rigid body is an idealization: a disk that we spin is also stretched, and the rigid-disk model does not tell us what it suffers. There are three questions. What are the stresses in the steel disk of rotor R1 at 3000 revolutions per minute, and where are they largest? What does a hole in the middle of the disk, the hole in which the shaft sits, do to them? And how far can we trust the formulas of the books if we check them on an example that the book itself works out? Chapter 11 already computed the rotating ring and remained in debt for the disk. Now we pay it.

The path is short. First we say what we assume about the disk and how the forces that stretch it follow from Newton's law. Then from the equilibrium equation and Hooke's law we derive the stresses of the solid and the annular disk, test them on the disk R1, show that a hole doubles the stress, estimate what the thickness of the disk changes, and compute an example of the book FPGL in which a printing error sits.

24.9.1 The disk and the forces on it

A disk (Slovenian: disk, a round plate) is here a flat round plate of constant thickness \(h\) made of steel, which turns about an axis through its centre, perpendicular to the plate. The disk of rotor R1 from section 24.1 has the radius \(b = 200\) mm and the thickness \(h = 50\) mm, so \(h/b = 0{,}25\). The steel has the density \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m\(^3\) and Poisson's ratio \(\nu = 0{,}3\). It turns with a constant angular velocity \(\Omega\), which is here the spin speed (hitrost vrtenja) as in section 24.2. Chapter 11 wrote the same quantity as \(\omega\) for the ring. In this section \(r\) is the distance of a point of the disk from the axis (the frequency ratio of chapter 9 is not needed here), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the radial and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) the hoop stress (obodna napetost) in the cylindrical coordinates of chapter 11.

Let us draw what a teacher would draw on the board. First the disk with its axis and the spin \(\Omega\). Then let us take from it a piece, bounded by two circles and two rays from the centre. Its centre of mass is at the radius \(r\) from the axis. Because the disk turns with a constant \(\Omega\), every point of the piece moves on a circle of this radius, and by chapter 11 (section 11.7.7) its acceleration is \(\vek a = -\Omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\): the acceleration points towards the axis and has the magnitude \(\Omega^2 r\). By Newton's law a force \(\rho\,\vek a\) per unit volume must act on the piece, so towards the axis. There is no weight and no external force. Who supplies it? Only the neighbouring material, through the stresses on the four faces of the piece. The radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) pulls the piece outwards on the outer face and inwards on the inner face. The hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) pulls the piece away from itself on both side faces, each along the tangent to the circle. The tangents on the two side faces are turned by the angle between the faces, so the two pulls together point inwards, just as a taut rope wound around a post presses on the post. So the hoop pull alone already gives a force towards the axis, and the radial pull corrects it by the difference between the outer and the inner face. That the sum really gives \(\rho\,\vek a\) is written by corollary 11.24 with the body force \(\rho\,\Omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (\(-\rho\vek a\) by d'Alembert's principle, theorem 8.71). This equation will be the first step of the proof.

The model is an idealization and we name it.

  1. The disk is thin: plane stress (definition 11.32), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) through the whole thickness. At \(h/b = 0{,}25\) this is an approximation, which we estimate in section 24.9.5. FPGL (PDF p. 306) says that the stresses do not depend on the thickness if it is small against the diameter.
  2. The speed \(\Omega\) is constant, so the points have no tangential acceleration.
  3. The disk is axisymmetric: homogeneous, of constant thickness, the axis of rotation passes through the centre. The unbalance of section 24.2 (\(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm against \(b = 200\) mm, \(0{,}025\,\%\)) is another load and we neglect it here.
  4. The shaft is not in the model: the edge of the hole is free. Pressing the shaft into the disk (shrink fit) and its bending would add stresses that we do not compute (FPGL §6.12.2, PDF pp. 307-308, computes the bending stress of the shaft, not of the disk).
  5. The material is linearly elastic and isotropic, chapter 3.

24.9.2 Stresses in the rotating disk

The radial equilibrium equation has two unknown stresses, Hooke's law connects them with the displacement \(u(r)\), and we get an ordinary differential equation for \(u\). Its general solution has two constants, which the two boundary conditions determine. For a disk without a hole one condition is at the radius \(b\), and the other is that the displacement at the centre stays bounded.

Theorem 24.60 Stresses in the rotating disk (napetosti v vrtečem se disku)

Let the disk be a flat plate of constant thickness made of an isotropic homogeneous material with the density \(\rho\), Young's modulus \(E\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.48), with the inner radius \(a\) and the outer radius \(b\), \(0 \le a < b\). Let it turn with a constant angular velocity \(\Omega\) about an axis through the centre, perpendicular to the plate, and let its state of stress be plane stress (definition 11.32) with the displacement \(\vek u = u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Let the outer edge be free, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\), and for \(a > 0\) also the edge of the hole, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = 0\). For \(a = 0\) let the displacement be bounded. Then:

  1. For \(a > 0\)
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\Big(a^2 + b^2 - \frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2} - r^2\Big) , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\Big((3 + \nu)\Big(a^2 + b^2 + \frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2}\Big) - (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\Big) . \end{aligned} \tag{24.54}\]
  2. For \(a = 0\)
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\,(b^2 - r^2) , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \frac{\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\,\big((3 + \nu)\,b^2 - (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\big) , \tag{24.55}\]
    and for \(\nu \ge -\tfrac13\) (steel has \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) the largest stress is at the centre, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = (3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 b^2/8\).
  3. For \(a > 0\) the radial stress is largest at \(r = \sqrt{ab}\) and there equals \((3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 (b - a)^2/8\), and the hoop stress is largest at the edge of the hole, \(r = a\).
  4. The stresses do not depend on \(E\) and grow with the square of \(\Omega\).
Proof

We must show that the displacement \(u(r)\) satisfies the equilibrium equation and that the two boundary conditions determine the constants. There are three unknowns: \(u\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\).

  1. A point at the radius \(r\) moves on a circle, its acceleration is \(-\Omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (section 11.7.7), and the equation of motion \(\mathrm{div}\,\ten\sigma = \rho\,\vek a\) is the equilibrium equation with the body force \(b_{\mathrm{r}} = \rho\,\Omega^2 r\) (theorem 8.71).
  2. The strains of an axisymmetric displacement are by corollary 11.21 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u'\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r\). Hooke's law of plane stress, solved for the stresses: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\big(u' + \nu\,\dfrac{u}{r}\big)\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\big(\dfrac{u}{r} + \nu\,u'\big)\).
  3. The first equilibrium equation in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.24): \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r + \rho\,\Omega^2 r = 0\). The other two hold because the stresses do not depend on \(\theta\) and \(z\) and the shear stresses are zero.
  4. We insert step 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = \dfrac{E}{1 - \nu^2}\big(u'' + \nu\,(\tfrac{u'}{r} - \tfrac{u}{r^2})\big)\) and \(\dfrac{\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta}}{r} = \dfrac{E\,(1 - \nu)}{1 - \nu^2}\,\big(\tfrac{u'}{r} - \tfrac{u}{r^2}\big)\). The sum has the coefficient \(\nu + (1 - \nu) = 1\) at \(\tfrac{u'}{r} - \tfrac{u}{r^2}\), so
    \[u'' + \frac{u'}{r} - \frac{u}{r^2} = -\frac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{E}\,r . \tag{24.56}\]
  5. For \(u = q\,r^3\) the left side is \((6 + 3 - 1)\,q\,r = 8\,q\,r\), so \(u_{\mathrm{p}} = -k\,r^3\) with \(k = \dfrac{(1 - \nu^2)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{8\,E}\) is a solution. The difference of two solutions solves the homogeneous equation, whose general solution is \(C_1 r + C_2/r\) (theorem 11.27, step 5 of the proof). Hence \(u = u_{\mathrm{p}} + C_1 r + C_2/r\) is the general solution.
  6. We insert \(u\) into step 2. With \(c = (3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2/8\), \(A = E\,C_1/(1 - \nu)\) and \(B = E\,C_2/(1 + \nu)\) we have \(u' + \nu u/r = -(3 + \nu)\,k\,r^2 + (1 + \nu)\,C_1 - (1 - \nu)\,C_2/r^2\) and \(u/r + \nu u' = -(1 + 3\nu)\,k\,r^2 + (1 + \nu)\,C_1 + (1 - \nu)\,C_2/r^2\) with \(k\) from step 5, so
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A - \frac{B}{r^2} - c\,r^2 , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = A + \frac{B}{r^2} - \frac{(1 + 3\nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\,r^2 .\]
  7. For \(a > 0\) we insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\): \(A - B/a^2 = c\,a^2\) and \(A - B/b^2 = c\,b^2\). Subtracting: \(B\,(b^2 - a^2)/(a^2 b^2) = c\,(b^2 - a^2)\), so \(B = c\,a^2 b^2\) and \(A = c\,(a^2 + b^2)\). Inserted into step 6 this is equation (24.54), item 1.
  8. For \(a = 0\) the displacement is bounded by assumption. The term \(C_2/r\) is unbounded as \(r \to 0\), so \(C_2 = 0\) and \(B = 0\). From \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\) follows \(A = c\,b^2\). This is item 2. At the centre \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta} = c\,b^2\). The radial stress falls with the radius because \(c > 0\). The hoop stress falls because for \(\nu \ge -\tfrac13\) the coefficient \(1 + 3\nu\) is nonnegative. For \(\nu < -\tfrac13\) the hoop stress would grow with the radius and would be larger at the rim, \((2 - 2\nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 b^2/8\), than at the centre, \(c\,b^2\), because then \(2 - 2\nu > 3 + \nu\).
  9. For \(a > 0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = 2c\,(a^2 b^2/r^3 - r) = 0\) at \(r^4 = a^2 b^2\), so \(r = \sqrt{ab}\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = c\,(a^2 + b^2 - 2ab) = c\,(b - a)^2\), and this is the maximum, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}'\) is positive at smaller \(r\) and negative at larger \(r\). Hoop stress: from step 6 with \(B = c\,a^2 b^2\) we get \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) - \sigma_{\theta\theta}(r) = \dfrac{\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\,\dfrac{r^2 - a^2}{r^2}\,\big((3 + \nu)\,b^2 + (1 + 3\nu)\,r^2\big)\). For \(a \le r \le b\) we have \(b^2 \ge r^2\), so the last bracket is at least \((4 + 4\nu)\,r^2 > 0\) and the difference is not negative. The hoop stress is therefore largest at \(r = a\), for every \(\nu > -1\). This is item 3. Item 4 follows from the formulas, because \(E\) enters only in \(A\) and \(B\) together with \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), which the boundary conditions determine.

Source: SADD (8.4.81) (PDF p. 221) for the solid disk with the stress function (8.4.75) to (8.4.80) (PDF pp. 220-221), with the remark that the solution can also be obtained from displacements (PDF p. 221). FPGL (6.78), (6.79) (PDF p. 306) for the disk with a hole, (6.82) to (6.84) (PDF p. 307) for the solid disk, from Fenner (1989). The proof by displacements, step by step, is not from the sources. We do not prove uniqueness in the plane model: in the class of fields \(u(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) the constants are fixed by the boundary conditions and the solution is one, but whether other fields exist, theorem 11.4 (the three-dimensional problem) does not say.

The proof gave more than we asked: the formulas contain no Young's modulus. Two disks with the same density and the same \(\nu\) but different \(E\) have the same stresses at the same speed and different displacements. The reason is in step 6: the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{p}}\) is proportional to \(1/E\), and by Hooke's law the stresses multiply the displacement by \(E\), so \(E\) cancels. What matters are the density and the peripheral speed \(v = \Omega b\) (obodna hitrost, the speed of a point on the rim of the disk): the central stress of a solid disk is \((3 + \nu)\,\rho\,v^2/8\). The disk R1 has \(v = 62{,}83\) m/s.

24.9.3 Test: the disk of rotor R1

Let us take the solid disk R1 with \(b = 200\) mm at \(3000\) revolutions per minute, \(\Omega = 3000\cdot 2\pi/60 = 314{,}16\) 1/s. The product \(\rho\,\Omega^2 b^2 = 30{,}79\) MPa. By equation (24.55) the central stress is \((3{,}3/8)\cdot 30{,}79 = 12{,}70\) MPa, at the edge \(r = b\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) = \rho,\Omega^2 b^2,(2 - 2\nu)/8 = 5{,}39$ MPa. In between (SymPy and NumPy, num-09.py):

\(r\) in mm 0 50 100 150 200
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) in MPa 12,70 11,91 9,53 5,56 0
\(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) in MPa 12,70 12,25 10,87 8,59 5,39

Let us look at the left diagram. The hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) falls from \(12{,}70\) MPa at the centre to \(5{,}39\) MPa at the edge, and the radial one \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) from \(12{,}70\) MPa to zero. Two things need an explanation. The first: why is the largest stress at the centre, when the force per unit volume \(\rho\,\Omega^2 r\) is zero there and largest at the edge? Let us write the radial equation of step 3 of the proof as \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta} - \rho\,\Omega^2 r^2\) and integrate from \(r\) to \(b\) with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\):

\[r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r) = \int_{\mathrm{r}}^b \big(\rho\,\Omega^2 s^2 - \sigma_{\theta\theta}(s)\big)\,\mathrm{d}s .\]

What does this equation say? Let us think of the disk cut into thin rings that do not touch each other. In each of them \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\), and by step 3 the ring at the radius \(s\) would need the hoop stress \(\rho\,\Omega^2 s^2\) to give by itself the force towards the axis that keeps it on its circle. The integral therefore adds up by how much the rings between \(r\) and \(b\) carry less hoop pull in the disk than they would carry alone. For the solid disk, by equation (24.55),

\[\rho\,\Omega^2 s^2 - \sigma_{\theta\theta}(s) = \frac{(3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2}{8}\,(3 s^2 - b^2) .\]

Outside the radius \(s = b/\sqrt3 = 115{,}5\) mm the difference is positive (at the edge \(30{,}79\) against \(5{,}39\) MPa). There the rings carry less hoop pull than they need, and the rest of the force towards the axis is given to them by the inner material through a radial pull, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} > 0\). Inside \(b/\sqrt3\) the difference is negative. There the rings carry more hoop pull than their own rotation requires, because the outer material pulls them outward through the radial pull. At the centre there is no own pull, \(\rho\,\Omega^2 s^2 = 0\), and all \(12{,}70\) MPa are the pull of the surrounding material. It pulls the centre equally to all sides, no direction is special, so there \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). That the central stress is the largest is said by equation (24.55): both stresses are the central value \((3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 b^2/8\) reduced by a term that grows with \(r^2\) (step 8 of the proof). The second: at a free edge \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) must be zero, because outside it there is no material that could pull. Hooke's law of step 2 gives at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) the relation \(u' = -\nu\,u/b\) and hence \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = E\,u(b)/b\). By steps 5 to 8, \(u(b) = (1 - \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 b^3/(4E) > 0\), the edge widens during the rotation, so the hoop pull remains, \(5{,}39\) MPa.

The stresses grow with the square of \(\Omega\) (item 4 of the theorem): at \(6000\) revolutions per minute they are \(50{,}81\) MPa at the centre and \(21{,}56\) MPa at the edge. The equivalent stress by von Mises (definition 2.76) at the centre, where the two principal stresses are equal and the third is zero, is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = 12{,}70\) MPa, equal to the stress itself. A steel with the yield strength \(220\) MPa, as in section 11.7.7, would start to yield there at \(3000\sqrt{220/12{,}70} = 12\,485\) revolutions per minute, which is \(4{,}16\) times faster than \(3000\). At the first critical speed of the rotor (\(1483\) rev/min, section 24.1) the disk has only \(12{,}70\,(1483/3000)^2 = 3{,}10\) MPa, while the bending stress of the shaft in resonance is by section 24.2 \(58{,}8\) MPa. For rotor R1, then, it is not the spinning of the disk that limits, it is the bending of the shaft. For large disks it is different, as the last example will show.

24.9.4 A hole doubles the stress

The disk R1 sits on a shaft, so it has a bore (izvrtina), a hole made by drilling, of diameter \(40\) mm. What does it do to the stresses? The first conclusion would be that it reduces them: the most stressed material is at the centre, and we drill that centre out. Let us see whether this holds. The right diagram shows the disk with the bore \(a = 20\) mm: the hoop stress at the edge of the bore is \(25{,}46\) MPa and is more than twice the central stress of the solid disk, the dashed \(12{,}70\) MPa. Contrary to our conclusion, the bore has increased the stress.

Corollary 24.61 A hole doubles the maximum stress (luknja podvoji največjo napetost)

Let the disk satisfy the conditions of theorem 24.60 with \(0 < a < b\). Let \(\sigma_0 = (3 + \nu)\,\rho\,\Omega^2 b^2/8\) be the largest stress of the solid disk of the same radius and the same speed. Then:

  1. The hoop stress at the edge of the hole is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 2\,\sigma_0\,\Big(1 + \dfrac{1 - \nu}{3 + \nu}\,\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}\Big)\).
  2. The ratio \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)/\sigma_0\) is larger than \(2\) for every \(a > 0\) and tends to \(2\) as \(a \to 0\). For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(a/b \le 0{,}1\) it is smaller than \(2{,}0043\).
  3. The radial stress decreases at the bore: \((3 + \nu)\rho\Omega^2(b - a)^2/8 < \sigma_0\).
Proof

We must show that \(r = a\) in equation (24.54) gives this formula.

  1. We insert \(r = a\) into \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) of theorem 24.60: \(\tfrac{\rho\Omega^2}{8}\big((3 + \nu)\,(a^2 + b^2 + b^2) - (1 + 3\nu)\,a^2\big) = \tfrac{\rho\Omega^2}{8}\big(2(3 + \nu)\,b^2 + 2(1 - \nu)\,a^2\big)\), because \((3 + \nu) - (1 + 3\nu) = 2 - 2\nu\). We factor out \(2\sigma_0 = (3 + \nu)\rho\Omega^2 b^2/4\).
  2. The term in the bracket is larger than \(1\) for \(a > 0\) and tends to \(1\) as \(a \to 0\). For \(\nu = 0{,}3\) we have \((1 - \nu)/(3 + \nu) = 0{,}2121\), so for \(a/b = 0{,}1\) we get \(2\,(1 + 0{,}2121\cdot 0{,}01) = 2{,}0042\).
  3. Item 3 of theorem 24.60 and \(b - a < b\).

Source: SADD (PDF p. 221): for a small hole \(a \ll b\) the largest stress is about twice the stress of the solid disk (Exercise 8.53), FPGL (6.81) (PDF p. 307) for the hoop stress at the edge of the hole. The formula of item 1 is ours.

The numbers of the disk R1: at \(a = 20\) mm the ratio is \(2{,}0042\), so \(25{,}46\) MPa, with a bore of diameter \(2\) mm (\(a = 1\) mm) \(25{,}40\) MPa, that is \(2{,}00001\,\sigma_0\). The hole can be as small as we like and the largest stress still doubles. At every fixed \(r > 0\) the term \(a^2 b^2/r^2\) vanishes as \(a \to 0\) and the stresses tend to those of the solid disk. At the edge of the hole, \(r = a\), however, \(2\sigma_0\) remains, and the solid disk (item 2 of the theorem) has no such edge. Therefore the largest stress with a hole does not tend, as \(a \to 0\), to the largest stress without a hole.

Why exactly twice? Let us look at the centre of the solid disk, shown by figure 24.33. There is an equal pull \(\sigma_0 = 12{,}70\) MPa in all directions in the plane of the plate, as we saw. A small hole in this field is exactly the case of biaxial tension of corollary 11.52: at the edge of the hole the hoop stress is \(2\sigma_0\) in every direction, the stress concentration factor (faktor koncentracije napetosti) is \(K_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\). Our theorem gives the same number. Chapter 11 gave the factor \(3\) for uniaxial tension (theorem 11.51). Here the tension is not uniaxial, because the whole surroundings pull the centre equally, so the factor \(3\) does not hold.

The right diagram shows that the agreement is good also away from the edge: the exact hoop stress at the bore of \(2\) mm and the Kirsch field differ at radii from \(1\) to \(20\) mm by at most \(0{,}07\) MPa, that is \(0{,}6\,\%\) of the stress \(\sigma_0\). The Kirsch field holds for an infinite plate with a uniform field. Here the field changes over the length \(b = 200\) mm, while the hole has the radius \(1\) mm, so \(0{,}5\,\%\) of this length. At a bore of \(40\) mm (\(a/b = 0{,}1\)) the field already changes noticeably at the hole and the factor is \(2{,}0042\), so it differs from the Kirsch number \(2\) by \(0{,}2\,\%\) (item 2 of the corollary).

A consequence for the disk R1: with the \(40\) mm bore the hoop stress at the edge of the bore is \(25{,}46\) MPa, at the outer edge \(5{,}64\) MPa (more than for the solid disk, \(5{,}39\) MPa) and the largest radial stress is \(10{,}29\) MPa at \(r = \sqrt{20\cdot 200} = 63{,}2\) mm (item 3 of the theorem and of the corollary). At the edge of the bore the radial stress is zero, so the state there is uniaxial and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). The disk starts to yield at \(3000\sqrt{220/25{,}46} = 8819\) revolutions per minute. This is \(70{,}6\,\%\) of the speed of the solid disk, \(1/\sqrt{2{,}0042}\). The pressure with which the shaft acts on the edge of the bore during shrink fitting or thermal expansion is not in the model (item 4 of the model) and can change the stress.

24.9.5 A thick disk: plane strain

The model of plane stress requires a thin disk. At \(h/b = 0{,}25\) this is doubtful, and for a long cylinder whose ends are held at rest (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)) the other extreme model holds, plane strain (definition 11.30). We take the solution from the exchange of constants.

Corollary 24.62 A long rotating cylinder (vrteči se dolg valj)

Let a cylinder with the axis of rotation \(z\) and a cross-section of constant radii \(a\) and \(b\) along its length have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (plane strain, definition 11.30). Then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are given by equations (24.54) and (24.55), in which \(\nu\) is replaced by \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\). The axial stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})\) with the original \(\nu\).

Proof

We must show that the system for plane strain becomes the system of the theorem.

  1. By corollary 11.34, item 1, plane strain with \(E\), \(\nu\) solves the same plane system as plane stress with \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\), with the same body force \(\rho\,\Omega^2 r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the same conditions in the plane.
  2. Steps 2 to 9 of the proof of theorem 24.60 use only this system and the conditions in the plane, so they hold with \(E^*\), \(\nu^*\) in place of \(E\), \(\nu\). Young's modulus does not enter the stresses (item 4 of the theorem), so only \(\nu \to \nu^*\) remains.
  3. Hooke's law at \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) gives \(0 = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} - \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})\).

Source: SADD (PDF p. 221): the solution is obtained by the exchange \(\nu \to \nu/(1 - \nu)\). FPGL (PDF p. 307): the same for a thickness much larger than the diameter. The proof by corollary 11.34 is not from the sources.

For the disk R1 we have \(\nu^* = 0{,}4286\). The central stress is \(13{,}20\) MPa instead of \(12{,}70\) MPa (\(+3{,}9\,\%\)), the hoop stress at the edge \(4{,}40\) MPa instead of \(5{,}39\) MPa (\(-18{,}4\,\%\)), the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 7{,}92\) MPa at the centre and \(1{,}32\) MPa at the edge. The two idealizations above, a thin plate with free faces and a long cylinder with ends at rest, thus differ by \(3{,}9\,\%\) in the largest stress, at the centre. This is a measure of how much the choice of model matters for the disk R1, not a bound on the true answer: the real disk with free faces and \(h/b = 0{,}25\) is a three-dimensional problem that we do not solve here. For the ring of chapter 11 plane stress differed from the exact cylinder with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) by \(2\) to \(3\,\%\), which is the same order of magnitude.

24.9.6 The FPGL example and its error

FPGL (Example 6.12.1, PDF p. 307, printed p. 289) takes a steel disk with the outer diameter \(1{,}6\) m turning at \(3000\) rev/min, with \(\rho = 7850\) kg/m\(^3\) and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), and computes the highest hoop and radial stresses (a) with a bore of diameter \(0{,}1\) m and (b) without a bore. Let us compute it with theorem 24.60, \(b = 0{,}8\) m, \(a = 0{,}05\) m.

  1. \(\Omega = 3000\cdot 2\pi/60 = 314{,}16\) 1/s and \(\rho\,\Omega^2\,(3 + \nu)/8 = 7850\cdot 314{,}16^2\cdot 3{,}3/8 = 319{,}6\) MN/m\(^4\).
  2. Solid disk (b): \(\sigma_{\max} = 319{,}6\cdot 0{,}8^2 = 204{,}5\) MPa at the centre.
  3. With the bore (a): the largest radial stress is \(319{,}6\,(b - a)^2 = 319{,}6\cdot 0{,}75^2 = 179{,}8\) MPa at \(r = \sqrt{ab} = 0{,}2\) m. The largest hoop stress is at the edge of the bore, \(2\cdot 319{,}6\,\big(0{,}8^2 + \tfrac{1 - \nu}{3 + \nu}\,0{,}05^2\big) = 409{,}4\) MPa (item 1 of corollary 24.61).

FPGL prints different values: \(426{,}1\) MN/m\(^4\) for the coefficient and \(239{,}7\), \(545{,}9\) and \(272{,}7\) MPa. All three are \(4/3\) of ours (\(1{,}3334\), \(1{,}3334\), \(1{,}3333\)), and the coefficient is exactly \(\rho\,\Omega^2 (3 + \nu)/6 = 426{,}1\) MN/m\(^4\). So in the example the denominator of the coefficient is \(6\) instead of \(8\). The error is in the number of the example, not in the formula of the book, since equation (6.84) on the same page (PDF p. 307) says \((3 + \nu)\rho\Omega^2 R_2^2/8\), and the same is said by SADD (8.4.81) (PDF p. 221) and by our theorem. The computation is checked independently also with the peripheral speed \(v = \Omega b\). For the disk R1 we have \(v = 62{,}83\) m/s and \(\sigma_0 = 12{,}70\) MPa, for the FPGL disk \(v = 251{,}3\) m/s, so the ratio must be \((251{,}3/62{,}83)^2\cdot 7850/7800 = 16{,}10\), and indeed \(204{,}5/12{,}70 = 16{,}10\). Our values are the correct ones, not the printed ones.

These numbers matter for a rotor too. A disk with \(v = 251\) m/s and \(204{,}5\) MPa at the centre is already close to the yield strength of steel, \(220\) MPa, and with a bore the stress is \(409{,}4\) MPa, which exceeds the yield strength \(220\) MPa. FPGL (PDF p. 306) therefore says that these stresses matter more for disks of large diameter than for sections of the shaft. The small disk R1 at \(3000\) rev/min has \(12{,}70\) MPa, and the stress is decided by the peripheral speed \(v\), not by the mass of the disk.

24.9.7 What we have shown

Theorem 24.60 gave the stresses of the rotating disk from the equilibrium equation with the d'Alembert force and from Hooke's law, as plane stress: for \(\nu \ge -\tfrac13\) the solid disk has its largest stress \((3 + \nu)\,\rho\,v^2/8\) at the centre and Young's modulus is not in the formula. Corollary 24.61 showed that every hole at the centre doubles the stress, because there is biaxial tension and the concentration factor is \(2\), and that therefore the largest stress with a small hole does not tend to the largest stress without a hole. Corollary 24.62 solved the same for a long cylinder by the exchange \(\nu \to \nu/(1 - \nu)\), the difference for the disk R1 is \(3{,}9\,\%\) at the centre. The FPGL example with the corrected coefficient \(319{,}6\) instead of \(426{,}1\) MN/m\(^4\) gave \(204{,}5\), \(409{,}4\) and \(179{,}8\) MPa. We did not compute the shrink fit of the shaft, the temperature, the bending of the disk during whirl or the three-dimensional field of a disk with \(h/b = 0{,}25\).
