Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.12 Connections

The abbreviations of subjects are the same as in section 1.19. In this chapter we computed stresses from the temperature and carried the temperature into finite elements by the weak form. Now we ask what of this is not special to heat. Two things hold for all the following subjects: the equation this chapter solved appears in other fields, and the decay of modes, known from the copper bar, separates heat conduction from vibration.

14.12.1 The same equation in other fields

For a steady field the derivative in time is zero. The equation of theorem 14.8 is then \(\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + Q = 0\)\((k\,T_{,\mathrm{i}})_{,\mathrm{i}} + Q = 0\), and for constant conductivity \(k\) it is \(k\,\nabla^2 T + Q = 0\)\(k\,T_{,\mathrm{ii}} + Q = 0\), where \(\nabla^2\) is the Laplace operator of definition 11.5 and \(Q\) is the power of the source per unit volume. The deflection of a stretched membrane under pressure and the stress function in torsion have the same form, and chapter 16 will treat them (MK, VD). The source (COOK, §12.5, PDF p. 483, read) writes the equation for a variable \(\varphi\) and lists, among others, ground water (hydraulic head), the stretched membrane (lateral deflection), elastic torsion (warping function or Prandtl stress function), electrostatics and potential flow. For the membrane \(k\) is the surface tension (\(N\) in chapter 16) and \(Q\) the lateral pressure (\(p\)), for the warping function \(k\) is one and \(Q\) is zero. Fields described by the same equation are called analogous, because the solution of one tells the shape of the solutions of the others. The word says that the equations agree, not the physical quantities.

Why do such different things have the same equation? The answer is given by a piece we cut out, as we cut a piece out of the bar when deriving the equation of conduction (section 14.2). Take a long strip between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\) with \(\varphi = 0\) on both edges and \(Q/k = 1\). Since the strip is long, \(\varphi\) does not depend on \(y\), and the solution is \(\varphi = x(1-x)/2\): its second derivative is \(-1 = -Q/k\), and it is zero at the edges. On the left we draw it as the temperature \(T\) over the axis \(x\), on the right as the deflection \(\zeta\) of a stretched membrane, which the pressure bulges upward. The two curves are the same. From both we cut out the piece between \(x_1 = 0{,}6\) and \(x_2 = 0{,}85\).

For heat, heat flows from warmer to colder, here towards the edge \(x = 1\). By Fourier's law (definition 14.3) the flux is \(q = -k\,T'\), proportional to the slope of the curve. At \(x_1\) the slope is \(-0{,}10\), so a flux \(0{,}10\,k\) enters the piece. At \(x_2\) the slope is \(-0{,}35\), so \(0{,}35\,k\) leaves it. The difference \(0{,}25\,k\) is supplied by the source in the piece, \(Q\,(x_2 - x_1) = 0{,}25\,Q\), and since \(Q = k\), the balance closes.

For the membrane the rest of the membrane pulls the piece at both ends with the tension \(N\) along the tangent. For equilibrium in the vertical direction only the vertical component of this force counts, which for a small slope is \(N\) times the slope. At \(x_1\) it pulls up with \(0{,}10\,N\), because the tangent rises towards the left, at \(x_2\) down with \(0{,}35\,N\). The difference \(0{,}25\,N\) downward is balanced by the pressure on the piece, \(p\,(x_2 - x_1) = 0{,}25\,p\), and since \(p = N\), the piece is at rest.

The two balances are the same calculation. What flows or pulls through the two ends of the piece is \(k\) or \(N\) times the slope, and the difference between the ends is supplied by what the piece receives along its length. Written out: \(k\,\varphi'(x_2) - k\,\varphi'(x_1) + Q\,(x_2 - x_1) = 0\), and the same for the membrane with \(N\) in place of \(k\) and \(p\) in place of \(Q\). We divide by \(x_2 - x_1\) and let \(x_2\) go to \(x_1\). The quotient of the difference of the slopes and the length tends to \(\varphi''(x_1)\) by the definition of the derivative, so \(k\,\varphi'' + Q = 0\) and \(N\,\zeta'' + p = 0\). In the plane the flux passes through the whole boundary of the piece, the slope becomes the gradient and the difference of the fluxes the divergence, and so we get \(k\nabla^2\varphi + Q = 0\) (theorem 14.8, for the membrane theorem 16.33). The fields are therefore analogous because they are all the same balance: something flows or pulls in proportion to the slope, and the difference between what enters and what leaves is supplied by a source.

For the membrane there is an assumption in this. The vertical component of the tension along the tangent is exactly \(N\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle of the tangent with the horizontal, while we took \(N\tan\theta = N\zeta'\). The sine and the tangent of a small angle hardly differ, their ratio is \(\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt{1 + s^2}\) at the slope \(s\) (lemma 16.36). On the piece in the figure the slope at \(x_2\) is \(0{,}35\) and the ratio \(0{,}944\), an error of almost \(6\) percent. With a pressure ten times smaller the slope is \(0{,}035\) and the error \(0{,}06\) percent. The analogy with the membrane therefore holds for shallow membranes, a deeply bulged membrane has another shape (a circular arc instead of a parabola, the same lemma). For heat there is no such approximation, because Fourier's law is itself linear in the slope.

Let us see what carries over. Take a square region \(\Omega\) of side \(1\) and an edge where \(\varphi = 0\). For heat this condition is a prescribed temperature, for the membrane a clamped edge. Let the source have \(Q/k = 1\). On a rectangle we solved by a series of sines only the equation without a source, with a nonzero temperature on one edge (theorem 14.35, section 14.6). With a source and a zero edge we take a double series of sines. Each term \(\sin m\pi x \sin n\pi y\) is zero on the whole edge, and \(\nabla^2\) multiplies it by \(-\pi^2(m^2 + n^2)\). We expand the constant \(1\) in a series of the same terms. The coefficients are the products of the sine coefficients of the function \(1\) in \(x\) and in \(y\) (formula (13.8)), that is \(\frac{4}{m\pi}\cdot\frac{4}{n\pi} = \frac{16}{\pi^2 m n}\) for odd \(m\) and \(n\) and zero for even ones. We divide these coefficients by \(\pi^2(m^2 + n^2)\), so that \(-\nabla^2\varphi\) equals this series. The series \(\varphi(x, y) = \sum_{\mathrm{m},\mathrm{n}\ \mathrm{odd}} \frac{16}{\pi^4 m n (m^2 + n^2)} \sin m\pi x \sin n\pi y\) gives \(\varphi = 0{,}0737\) at the centre (NumPy, num-12.out). We differentiated the series twice term by term, which we do not prove here. This is not self-evident, because the twice differentiated terms fall off only like \(1/(mn)\). So we check the central value numerically: with \(20\), \(30\) and \(200\) odd terms in each direction it is the same to four places (num-12-teach.out).

Its contours at \(0{,}2\), \(0{,}4\), \(0{,}6\) and \(0{,}8\) of the central value are closed curves that crowd towards the edge, because \(\varphi\) is steepest at the edge. Why at the edge? The whole source in the square, \(Q \cdot 1\), must leave through the edge of length \(4\), so there the mean flux is \(Q/4\) and the mean slope \(Q/(4k) = 0{,}25\), at the middle of a side \(0{,}34\). Through the contour \(0{,}8\) only the source inside it flows out, so the slope there at the middle is only \(0{,}13\). The cut through the middle \(y = 1/2\) is drawn on the right over the axis \(x\) with the height \(\varphi\). This profile is a dome with its top at \(0{,}0737\). We compare it with the solution on the long strip between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\), which we cut into pieces above. It is the parabola \(\varphi = \frac{Q}{2k}\,x(1-x)\) with the central value \(0{,}1250\). The square has its top lower by \(0{,}0513\), because part of the source flows out towards the edges \(y = 0\) and \(y = 1\), while in the strip all of it flows out in the direction \(x\). The shape, however, is almost the same: at \(x = 1/4\) the ratio to the centre is \(0{,}750\) for the strip and \(0{,}778\) for the square.

The theorems carry over with the equation. By theorem 14.17 (point 2), with \(Q = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\) on the whole edge the solution is zero. A membrane without pressure stays flat, and a stress function without a source is zero. The equation alone therefore does not fix the shape, the solution is awakened only by a source or a nonzero edge. The same theorem with the same proof (the energy \(\int k|\nabla w|^2\), step 8 of the proof) holds for all the fields of this family, so a solution with the boundary conditions of section 14.3 is unique everywhere under the conditions of point 2. The weak form (theorem 14.50) and the functional (lemma 14.52) have the same form for all these fields. The Ritz and Galerkin methods are therefore equal for all of them by theorem 6.44, and the element matrix of lemma 14.54 is for them what the stiffness matrix is for the bar (AK, chapter 15). The source warns here that there may be subtle but important differences in how the equations are used in different fields, so great care is needed when another field is solved by software for heat (COOK, PDF p. 483).

14.12.2 Modes that decay

For the string in chapter 13 and for the bar in section 14.5 the spatial shapes are the same sines \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\). Only the time part differs. For the string mode \(n\) varies in time as \(A\cos(n\omega_1 t) + B\sin(n\omega_1 t)\) with frequency \(n\omega_1\), that is \(n\) times the first (theorem 13.7, point 2, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi c/L\), where \(c\) is the wave speed on the string, not the specific heat). For heat it varies as \(e^{-n^2\lambda_1 t}\) with the decay rate \(n^2\lambda_1\), where \(\lambda_1 = \kappa\pi^2/L^2\) (theorem 14.26, equation (14.27)). The ratio \(n^2\) instead of \(n\) is no accident. For the string the equation has a second derivative in time, so \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = c^2 (n\pi/L)^2\) and the frequency is a square root. For heat the derivative is of first order, so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = \kappa (n\pi/L)^2\) and the root disappears. Both equations have the square \((n\pi/L)^2\) for the same reason: mode \(n\) has \(n\) half waves on the same length, so at the same height it is \(n\) times steeper and \(n^2\) times more curved (section 14.5). For heat the curvature sets the rate of change, for the string the acceleration.

Take the copper bar of example 14.27 (length \(L = 80\) cm, \(\kappa = 1{,}1576\) cm²/s, KREYSZIG, §12.6, PDF pp. 587-588), where \(\lambda_1 = 1{,}7852\cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/s. The time in which the amplitude of a mode falls to half is \(\ln 2/(n^2\lambda_1)\). On the left are the amplitudes of heat over time measured in such times of the first mode, on the right the amplitudes of the string over time measured in periods of the first mode. On the left the blue curve \(n = 1\) falls to one half at time \(1\), that is after \(388{,}3\) s. The orange one \(n = 2\) falls to it already at a quarter of that time, after \(97{,}1\) s, the grey one \(n = 3\) at a ninth, after \(43{,}1\) s. On the right all three curves oscillate about zero, and \(n = 3\) has three times as many oscillations as \(n = 1\). Let all modes start with the same amplitude. The ratio of mode \(n\) to the first is then \(e^{-(n^2 - 1)\lambda_1 t}\), and at time \(388{,}3\) s we have \(\lambda_1 t = \ln 2\). Then the first mode is at \(1/2\) and the second at \(1/16\) of the initial amplitude, so the second is at \(2^{-3} = 0{,}125\) of the amplitude of the first, the third at \(2^{-8} = 0{,}0039\) and the fourth at \(2^{-15} = 0{,}00003\). When the first mode is at one half, the second is therefore only an eighth of the first, and the third and higher ones are below \(0{,}4\) percent of the first. For heat a shortened model with a few first modes (DSK, chapter 21) therefore loses nothing that would last, and the higher modes vanish on their own. For an undamped string every mode still oscillates with full amplitude after that time, so a dropped mode changes the solution for good.

The decoupling into modes also works for finite elements, where theorem 10.21 gives it. For heat this is theorem 14.60, in which each mode decays with \(e^{-\lambda t}\). A consequence is a difference in time stepping. An explicit step for heat must satisfy \(\Delta t \le 2/\lambda_{\max}\) (theorem 14.64). For a bar of equal elements of length \(h\) with lumped capacity, \(\lambda_{\max} < 4\kappa/h^2\), and on a fine mesh it is close to this bound (lemma 14.61, for eight elements and \(L = \kappa = 1\) we have \(\lambda_{\max} = 246{,}26\) against the bound \(256\)). The critical step is therefore slightly above \(h^2/(2\kappa)\) and roughly proportional to \(h^2\). For waves the critical step of central differences with lumped mass is just above \(h/c\) (section 10.13, figure 10.143), roughly proportional to \(h\). When the mesh is refined, the thermal step therefore shrinks faster. For a bar with \(L = \kappa = c = 1\) and lumped capacity or mass, with sixteen elements instead of eight the thermal step is \(0{,}24\) of the previous one, about a quarter, and the wave step \(0{,}49\) of the previous one, about a half (num-12-teach.out).

14.12.3 Other connections

  • Thermal stresses: first the temperature by sections 14.2 to 14.6, then the stresses by theorem 14.39 (VT, MK, chapter 11). This is the uncoupled problem (definition 14.38). For bodies of complicated shape we solve it by finite elements: the temperature as a scalar field on a triangle (COOK, §1.3, PDF p. 21, read), the stresses from the initial strain \(\alpha\,\Delta T\) by definition 7.48 and lemma 7.49 (AK, chapter 15). An example in the source (COOK, §12.6, PDF pp. 483-485) computes the steady temperature field of a flanged pipe joint before going on to the stress analysis. The source also notes that the assumption of an insulated contact surface is pessimistic for the stresses, because it increases the nearby temperature gradients, and large gradients are often accompanied by large stresses.
  • Thermal bending: theorem 5.37 of chapter 5 holds only for a temperature linear in height, and lemma 14.42 extends it to an arbitrary temperature (VT, MK). Plates and shells (chapters 18 and 22) are not treated in this section.
  • Weak form and the Ritz method: the Galerkin method does not need a functional, the Ritz method does. Steady conduction has one (lemma 14.52 in section 14.9), so by theorem 6.44 the two agree (AK, VT, chapter 6).
  • Limits of the model: the chapter worked with a rigid conductor at rest, isotropic, with constant \(k\) and \(c\) (section 14.2). Radiation has a heat flux proportional to the fourth power of the absolute temperature and gives a highly nonlinear problem (COOK, §12.3, PDF pp. 476-478, read), and a temperature-dependent conductivity does too (COOK, PDF p. 469). The coupling term that connects temperature and deformation is small for most materials under static or quasi-static loading (SADD, PDF p. 379, read), but the chapter has no quantitative estimate. Fluid flow in contact with a hot body, for example in internal combustion engines and solar collectors, is regarded by the source (CM, §8.3.5, PDF p. 410, read) as a coupled problem outside its scope. When a model is still enough is a question of modelling (AK, chapter 17).
  • The infinite bar: the Fourier integral replaces the series when the bar has no ends and only the initial temperature is given (KREYSZIG, §12.7, PDF pp. 594-599, read). Chapter 9 only named the Fourier integral, its proof is not in the book.
  • Limits of the chapter: proved are uniqueness, the series solutions for the bar and the rectangle, the thermal stresses from the temperature, the weak form and the elements. Cited but not proved are radiation, the dependence of \(k\) on temperature, the size of the coupling term, the Fourier integral, inelastic residual stresses after unequal cooling, the convergence of the plane series up to an edge where the temperature jumps (only in the mean), and the convergence of the double sine series for the square with a source. The book has no values of \(k\) and \(c\) for steel, so the steel examples are in dimensionless numbers.

From history

William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, took Fourier's book out of the library on 1 May 1840, at the age of sixteen, and in a fortnight had gone right through it. Maxwell called it a great mathematical poem (CAJORI, PDF p. 287). Kelvin wrote of Fourier's work on heat that it is difficult to say whether its original quality, its transcendent interest or its perennially important instructiveness for physical science is most to be praised (CAJORI, PDF p. 287).

A small part of this instructiveness we saw above: the same equation, the same theorems and the same methods, different physics.
