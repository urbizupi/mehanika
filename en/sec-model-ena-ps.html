Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.1 The single-degree-of-freedom model (model z eno prostostno stopnjo)

The cantilever with a weight has infinitely many points and each moves its own way. When does one number describe its motion, and which equation does that number obey? The answer has two parts. The first is the idea of the Ritz method (section 6.4): if we prescribe one shape that the cantilever may have, one free number remains, and it is the height of the free end. The second is the calculation with the energies of chapter 8, which gives the equation of motion from the kinetic and the potential energy, without writing the forces at every point.

9.1.1 The cantilever with a weight as a system with one coordinate (konzola z utežjo kot sistem z eno koordinato)

Take the cantilever of chapter 5: steel with \(E = 196\) GPa, length \(L = 1\) m, cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm standing on its edge, so \(I = 1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) and \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m\(^2\). A weight of mass \(m = 50\) kg is rigidly attached to the free end. We chose the mass ourselves, because its weight \(m g = 490{,}5\) N is close to the force 500 N that we used in chapter 5. The body moves in the vertical plane in which the beam was bent. Let the coordinate \(q\) be the displacement of the free end upwards, so \(q = v(L, t)\) with the displacement \(v\) of theorem 5.56.

How do we get the displacement of all points from the displacement of the end? By theorem 5.56 a force \(F\) on the free end gives the shape \(v(x) = -F\,x^2 (3L - x)/(6EI)\) and \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI)\). The quotient of the two is the function

\[\psi(x) = \frac{x^2\,(3L - x)}{2L^3} , \qquad \psi(0) = 0 , \quad \psi'(0) = 0 , \quad \psi(L) = 1 . \tag{9.1}\]

We choose the displacement of the beam at every time to be

\[v(x, t) = q(t)\,\psi(x) . \tag{9.2}\]

So at every time the beam has the shape that a force on the end causes statically, and only the height of the end changes (figure 9.1). The function \(\psi\) satisfies the clamping, \(\psi(0) = 0\) and \(\psi'(0) = 0\), so every displacement \(q\) allows this choice.

This is a choice and not a theorem. The motion of the cantilever is never exactly this shape, because the beam has other modes of motion, and the choice neglects three things: other shapes, the shear deformation of the beam and the rotation of the weight. The choice is good when the mass of the beam is small against the mass of the weight and the motion is slow. Then the only important force that loads the beam is the inertia of the weight on the end, and the beam bends under this force as under a static force on the end. The mass of the beam is \(m_{\mathrm{b}} = \rho A L = 7800 \cdot 8 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 1 = 6{,}240\) kg, which is 12,5 % of the mass of the weight. Shear deformation adds 0,125 % to the deflection (section 5.13). A weight with the largest dimension \(a\) has a moment of inertia of at most \(m a^2\), and its rotation, because of \(\psi'(L) = 3/(2L)\), adds to the kinetic energy at most the part \((9/4)(a/L)^2\) of the energy of its motion. At \(a = 0{,}1\) m this is at most 2,25 %.

We have the energy of the beam from chapter 5. A transverse load causes no axial force, so \(u' = 0\) and from definition 5.75 there remains \(U = \tfrac12 EI \int_0^L (v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). From (9.1), \(\psi''(x) = 3\,(L - x)/L^3\), so \(\int_0^L \psi''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L 9\,(L - x)^2/L^6\,\mathrm{d}x = 3/L^3\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.40). With \(v'' = q\,\psi''\),

\[U = \frac12\,\frac{3EI}{L^3}\,q^2 = \frac12\,k\,q^2 , \qquad k = \frac{3EI}{L^3} = 62\,720\ \text{N/m} . \tag{9.3}\]

The number \(k\) is the stiffness of the cantilever, the force on the free end per unit of displacement, since \(v(L) = -F L^3/(3EI)\) gives \(F = -k\,v(L)\). The weight of the load is a force \(m g\) downwards at a point at height \(q\), so its potential energy is \(m g q\) (definition 8.53), and the potential is

\[V(q) = \tfrac12\,k\,q^2 + m g\,q .\]

The weight has velocity \(\dot q\), because \(\psi(L) = 1\), and if the beam has no mass, \(T = \tfrac12 m\,\dot q^2\). We include the mass of the beam as follows: the point of the beam at \(x\) has velocity \(\dot q\,\psi(x)\), by definition 8.79 the kinetic energy is the integral of \(\tfrac12\rho\,|\vek v|^2\) over the volume of the beam. All points of the cross-section at \(x\) have the same velocity, so the integral over the cross-section gives the factor \(A\) (theorem 4.9 on the order of integration) and the kinetic energy of the beam is \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,\rho A\,(\dot q\,\psi)^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), where \(\rho A\) is the mass per unit length. Hence

\[\begin{aligned} T_{\mathrm{b}} &= \tfrac12\,\rho A \int_0^L \psi^2\,\mathrm{d}x\ \dot q^2 \\ &= \tfrac12\,\frac{33}{140}\,m_{\mathrm{b}}\,\dot q^2 , \\ \int_0^L \psi^2\,\mathrm{d}x &= \frac{1}{4L^6}\int_0^L x^4 (3L - x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x \\ &= \frac{L}{4}\Big(\frac95 - 1 + \frac17\Big) = \frac{33\,L}{140} . \end{aligned} \tag{9.4}\]

The kinetic energy is therefore \(T = \tfrac12 M \dot q^2\) with \(M = m + \tfrac{33}{140}\,m_{\mathrm{b}} = 51{,}4709\) kg. We count the mass of the beam with the weight \(33/140 = 0{,}236\) and not in full, because only the end of the beam moves with the velocity \(\dot q\), elsewhere it moves more slowly, and at the clamp not at all.

What remains is the question whether we may use Lagrange's equations for such a system with a prescribed shape. In chapter 8 we derived them for systems of finitely many rigid bodies. We proved Hamilton's principle in the book for a system of such bodies (theorem 8.102) and for a bar (theorem 8.103), but not for a bending beam. Our step is therefore a modelling step and not a theorem: we assume that for motions of the form (9.2) the action \(\int (T - V)\,\mathrm{d}t\) is a functional of one function \(q(t)\), and that its stationary points are, by theorem 8.98 (for \(n = 1\)), the solutions of Lagrange's equation with the energies \(T(q, \dot q)\) and \(V(q)\). This is the Ritz idea applied in time. A full treatment would first have to prove Hamilton's principle for a beam, that is for a function \(v(x, t)\) of two variables, and derive from it the equation of motion of the beam. That is the task of chapter 13. Here we test the correctness of the choice with a number in example 9.7.

9.1.2 The viscous damper (viskozni dušilnik)

After a blow the cantilever really does come to rest. Which force causes this, and how do we write it so that the equation can still be solved to the end? A real system loses energy in several ways: friction in joints, losses in the material at every deformation, air resistance. These effects differ in how the force depends on the motion. For a piston in oil the force is roughly proportional to the velocity of the piston, for dry sliding surfaces it has constant size and points against the velocity. For now we take the model in which the force is proportional to the velocity, because it gives a linear equation that we know how to solve. Other models come when we need them (dry friction in section 9.13, losses in the material in chapter 20).

Definition 9.1 Viscous damper (viskozni dušilnik)

A viscous damper with damping coefficient \(c \ge 0\) is a massless element that connects two points \(A\) and \(B\) of a system at distance \(\ell > 0\) and acts on each with a force along the line \(AB\), opposite to the velocity with which the distance changes. Let \(\vek e\) be the unit vector from \(A\) towards \(B\) and \(\dot\ell\) the time derivative of the distance \(\ell\). On the point \(B\) acts the force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}} = -c\,\dot\ell\,\vek e\), and on the point \(A\) the opposite force \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}} = -\vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\). The unit of the coefficient \(c\) is N s/m.

Source: CK (3.1) (PDF p. 74): the term \(c\,\dot u\) in the equation of the system with one degree of freedom, where the damping force is proportional to the velocity. The definition with two points and a line is not from the source.

The damper is an idealization: in reality \(c\) and the force are only approximately constant, and what the model assigns to one damper may be the sum of several effects. If the distance grows, \(\dot\ell > 0\), the force on \(B\) points towards \(A\) and the damper tries to reduce the distance, like a piston in oil (figure 9.2). The two forces lie on the line and are opposite, so together they have no resultant and no moment about any point of the line.

What does a damper give in Lagrange's equations? The points \(A\) and \(B\) are part of a system with one coordinate \(q\). Their positions are functions of the coordinate, \(\vek x_{\mathrm{A}}(q)\) and \(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}(q)\), continuously differentiable. The distance \(\ell(q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}|\) is a function of the coordinate.

Lemma 9.2 Generalized force of a damper (posplošena sila dušilnika)

Let \(\vek x_{\mathrm{A}}(q)\) and \(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}(q)\) be continuously differentiable functions of the coordinate \(q\), \(\ell(q) = |\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}| > 0\), and let there be a damper with coefficient \(c\) between the points \(A\) and \(B\) by definition 9.1. Then the distance \(\ell\) is a differentiable function of \(q\), \(\dot\ell = \ell'(q)\,\dot q\), and the generalized force of the damper (definition 8.50) is

\[Q_{\mathrm{d}} = -c\,\ell'(q)^2\,\dot q = -\frac{\partial\mathcal D}{\partial\dot q} , \qquad \mathcal D = \tfrac12\,c\,\ell'(q)^2\,\dot q^2 . \tag{9.5}\]

We call the function \(\mathcal D\) the dissipation function of the damper. The power received by the two forces of the damper is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\vek v_{\mathrm{A}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = -c\,\dot\ell^2 = -2\mathcal D \le 0\).

Proof

We must show that the virtual work of the damper forces equals \(Q_{\mathrm{d}}\,\delta q\) with the stated \(Q_{\mathrm{d}}\), and that the power is \(-c\,\dot\ell^2\). The velocities at a given moment are frozen, since we compute the generalized force for each time separately.

  1. The number \(\ell^2 = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}})\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}})\) is positive and differentiable in \(q\), so \(\ell\) is differentiable, and by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1) \(2\ell\,\ell' = 2\,(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}})\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}' - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}')\). With \(\vek e = (\vek x_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}})/\ell\) this is \(\ell' = \vek e\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}' - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}')\).
  2. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 3, componentwise) the velocities of the points are \(\vek v_{\mathrm{A}} = \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}'\,\dot q\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = \vek x_{\mathrm{B}}'\,\dot q\), so by step 1 \(\dot\ell = \ell'\,\dot q = \vek e\cdot(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek v_{\mathrm{A}})\).
  3. By definition 8.50, \(Q_{\mathrm{d}} = \vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\vek x_{\mathrm{A}}' + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}'\). With \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}} = c\,\dot\ell\,\vek e\) and \(\vek F_{\mathrm{B}} = -c\,\dot\ell\,\vek e\) we get \(Q_{\mathrm{d}} = -c\,\dot\ell\,\vek e\cdot(\vek x_{\mathrm{B}}' - \vek x_{\mathrm{A}}') = -c\,\dot\ell\,\ell'\) by step 1.
  4. From step 2, \(\dot\ell = \ell'\,\dot q\), so \(Q_{\mathrm{d}} = -c\,\ell'^2\,\dot q\). The derivative \(\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot q = c\,\ell'^2\,\dot q\) gives the same number with the opposite sign.
  5. The power is \(\vek F_{\mathrm{A}}\cdot\vek v_{\mathrm{A}} + \vek F_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} = -c\,\dot\ell\,\vek e\cdot(\vek v_{\mathrm{B}} - \vek v_{\mathrm{A}}) = -c\,\dot\ell^2\) by step 2, and \(2\mathcal D = c\,\ell'^2\,\dot q^2 = c\,\dot\ell^2\).

Source: GR (1.53) (PDF p. 53) defines the dissipation function for dampers with force \(C_{\mathrm{k}} f_{\mathrm{k}}\), (1.54) the generalized force \(Q_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The proof for a damper between two points is not from the source.

The lemma says two things. A damper gives a generalized force that in the coordinate \(q\) is proportional to the velocity \(\dot q\), and \(c(q) = c\,\ell'(q)^2\) is the coefficient that the coordinate feels. The power is negative: the damper takes mechanical energy from the system, and \(-c\,\dot\ell^2\) is the rate at which it loses it. For several dampers we add the generalized forces. A rotary damper that connects two bodies turning in the same plane acts on the second body with a moment \(-c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{rel}}\) against the rate of the relative rotation \(\varphi_{\mathrm{rel}}\) and by the same calculation has \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{rel}}^2\). The unit of the coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{t}}\) is N m s.

9.1.3 The equation of motion (gibalna enačba)

Now we have all the forces: the potential, the dampers and the force that the outside world brings to the system and that changes with time. Which equation does the system obey, and when may we simplify this equation to a linear one? The answer is a theorem. First the exact equation, then the equilibrium about which the motion stays small.

Theorem 9.3 Equation of motion of a system with one degree of freedom (gibalna enačba sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

Let a system have one degree of freedom with generalized coordinate \(q\) on an open interval \(U \subset \mathbb R\) (definition 8.45), ideal holonomic scleronomic constraints and kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12 M(q)\,\dot q^2\) with continuously differentiable \(M(q) > 0\) (theorem 8.86). Let the applied forces be conservative with a three times continuously differentiable potential \(V(q)\) (definition 8.53), dampers by definition 9.1 with the sum of dissipation functions \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12 c(q)\,\dot q^2\) with continuously differentiable \(c(q) \ge 0\), and an outer force with generalized force \(Q(t)\), a continuous function of time. A twice continuously differentiable motion \(q(t)\) is a motion of the system if and only if

\[M(q)\,\ddot q + \tfrac12\,M'(q)\,\dot q^2 + c(q)\,\dot q + V'(q) = Q(t) . \tag{9.6}\]

Along the motion, \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(T + V) = Q\,\dot q - c(q)\,\dot q^2\). Let \(q_0 \in U\) be an equilibrium, \(V'(q_0) = 0\), and let \(m = M(q_0)\), \(c_0 = c(q_0)\) and \(k = V''(q_0)\). For the displacement \(x = q - q_0\) there exist numbers \(\rho > 0\) and \(C\) such that at every time at which \(|x| \le \rho\),

\[m\,\ddot x + c_0\,\dot x + k\,x = Q(t) + R , \qquad |R| \le C\,\big(x^2 + \dot x^2 + |x|\,|\dot x| + |x|\,|\ddot x|\big) . \tag{9.7}\]
Proof

We must show that Lagrange's equations for the given energies and forces give (9.6), and then bound the terms that are not linear in the displacement and its derivatives by the remainder \(R\).

  1. By lemma 9.2 and by assumption, the forces that are not in the potential have the generalized force \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c(q)\,\dot q + Q(t)\). By theorem 8.90 a motion is a motion of the system if and only if, for \(\mathcal L = T - V\), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q} = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\).
  2. Since \(V\) does not depend on \(\dot q\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = M\,\dot q\), and by the product rule and the chain rule for a function of one variable (lemmas 1.81 and 8.29, item 3) \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(M\dot q) = M\,\ddot q + M'\,\dot q^2\). The derivative with respect to the coordinate is \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q = \tfrac12 M'\,\dot q^2 - V'\).
  3. We insert step 2 into step 1: \(M\ddot q + M'\dot q^2 - \tfrac12 M'\dot q^2 + V' = -c\,\dot q + Q\). We move the term \(c\,\dot q\) to the left and get (9.6). All steps are equivalences, so the equivalence of the theorem also holds.
  4. Theorem 8.106 says that \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(T + V) = Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\dot q\), and with \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\) from step 1 this is \(Q\dot q - c\,\dot q^2\).
  5. The displacement \(x = q - q_0\) changes the coordinate by a constant, so \(\dot x = \dot q\), \(\ddot x = \ddot q\), and the derivatives of the functions \(M\), \(c\), \(V\) with respect to \(x\) are the same as with respect to \(q\). Let \(\rho\) be so small that the interval \([q_0 - \rho, q_0 + \rho]\) lies in \(U\). The functions \(V'''\), \(M'\) and \(c'\) are continuous on the closed interval, so they have largest absolute values \(c_{\mathrm{V}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{M}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{c}}\) there (theorem 1.73).
  6. The function \(V'\) is differentiable on the interval with a continuous derivative \(V''\), which has the derivative \(V'''\). By theorem 8.60, \(V'(q) = V'(q_0) + V''(q_0)\,x + \tfrac12\,V'''(\xi)\,x^2\) for some \(\xi\) between \(q_0\) and \(q\). Since \(V'(q_0) = 0\), \(V'(q) = k\,x + r\) with \(|r| \le \tfrac12\,c_{\mathrm{V}}\,x^2\). At \(q = q_0\), \(r = 0\).
  7. By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.84), \(M(q) - m = M'(\eta)\,x\) and \(c(q) - c_0 = c'(\eta')\,x\) for some points between \(q_0\) and \(q\), so \(|M(q) - m| \le c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|x|\) and \(|c(q) - c_0| \le c_{\mathrm{c}}\,|x|\). Also \(|M'(q)| \le c_{\mathrm{M}}\) by step 5.
  8. In (9.6) we insert \(M = m + (M - m)\), \(c = c_0 + (c - c_0)\) and step 6. We get \(m\,\ddot x + c_0\,\dot x + k\,x = Q + R\) with \(R = -(M - m)\,\ddot x - \tfrac12 M'\,\dot x^2 - (c - c_0)\,\dot x - r\). By steps 6 and 7, \(|R| \le c_{\mathrm{M}}\,|x|\,|\ddot x| + \tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{M}}\,\dot x^2 + c_{\mathrm{c}}\,|x|\,|\dot x| + \tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{V}}\,x^2\). The inequality (9.7) holds with \(C = \max\big(c_{\mathrm{M}},\ c_{\mathrm{c}},\ \tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{V}}\big)\).

Source: GR (1.53) and (1.54) (PDF p. 53) and (1.59) (PDF p. 54): Lagrange's equations with a potential, a dissipation function and an outer force. CK (3.1) (PDF p. 74): the linear equation \(m\ddot u + c\dot u + k u = p(t)\). The exact equation (9.6) and the bound of the remainder are not from the source.

The theorem says what the model is. The exact equation is nonlinear, the square of the velocity appears in it because the mass \(M\) changes with the coordinate (the pendulum, the crank mechanism, chapter 8), and \(V'(q)\) is not linear. The linear equation is its approximation for a small displacement from equilibrium, and the remainder has, by the theorem, all terms of second order in \((x, \dot x, \ddot x)\). What the remainder means we see in the examples below. The system then has three numbers that describe every such system: the mass \(m\), the damping coefficient \(c\) and the stiffness \(k\). For a coordinate that is a length, the units are kg, N s/m and N/m. For a coordinate that is an angle, the units are kg m\(^2\), N m s and N m. We call them the generalized mass (posplošena masa), the generalized damping coefficient (posplošeni koeficient dušenja) and the generalized stiffness (posplošena togost). With \(Q(t)\) we denote the force that we shall call the excitation (vzbujanje) in this chapter, and the motion \(x(t)\) the response (odziv). From now on we write the displacement from equilibrium again as \(q\), as in chapter 8, and the equation as

\[m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q(t) . \tag{9.8}\]

The last part of the theorem says two more things. The energy \(T + V\) grows by the power of the outer force and falls by \(c\,\dot q^2\), which is the loss in the damper. For \(Q = 0\) the energy therefore does not grow. If \(k > 0\), \(V\) has a strict local minimum at \(q_0\) (theorem 8.63), and for \(Q = 0\) the equilibrium is stable by theorem 8.115, because the power of the damper is \(-c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\) and the theorem allows dissipative forces. For \(k < 0\) the equilibrium is not a minimum of the energy, and without damping it is unstable (theorem 8.117). An example is the inverted rod with a spring of chapter 8, where \(k = k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L\) can also be negative.

9.1.4 Natural frequency and damping ratio (lastna frekvenca in razmernik dušenja)

The equation (9.8) has three numbers, \(m\), \(c\) and \(k\). How do we gather them into fewer numbers that tell what the system will do? For \(c = 0\) and \(Q = 0\) this is the equation of theorem 8.122, its solution for \(k > 0\) is \(q(0)\cos\omega t + (\dot q(0)/\omega)\sin\omega t\) with \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) and period \(2\pi/\omega\). The number \(\omega\) is the natural circular frequency of a system with one degree of freedom (definition 8.124 for \(n = 1\)): the number by which time is multiplied in \(\cos\omega t\), in radians per second (definition 8.7).

Definition 9.4 Natural frequency and natural period of the system (lastna frekvenca in lastni nihajni čas sistema)

Let \(m > 0\) be the generalized mass and \(k > 0\) the generalized stiffness of the system (9.8). The undamped natural circular frequency (lastna krožna frekvenca) is the number that definition 8.124 gives for \(n = 1\),

\[\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} , \tag{9.9}\]

with the unit 1/s. The natural period (lastni nihajni čas) is \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the natural frequency (lastna frekvenca) is \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}/(2\pi) = 1/T_{\mathrm{n}}\) with the unit hertz (Hz), the number of vibrations per second. The subscript \(n\) of the symbol \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) separates this number from the frequency of the excitation and from the frequency of the damped vibration, which we introduce later.

Source: CK (3.4a) (PDF p. 74) and the text after it (PDF p. 75), which calls \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) the "undamped circular natural frequency".

It is natural because the system chooses it by itself: for \(c = 0\) and \(Q = 0\) it vibrates with this frequency whatever the initial displacement and velocity, because theorem 8.122 says that the period does not depend on the initial values. For a system with \(c > 0\) the frequency of the vibration will be somewhat different (section 9.5), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) keeps its name as the natural frequency without damping.

How large is \(c\)? We must compare it with something that has the same unit. Take a vibration with circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and amplitude \(X\). The largest force of the spring is \(k X\) and the largest force of the damper is \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\), because the largest velocity is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} X\). Their ratio is \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/k = c/(m\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\), because \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). The number \(c/(m\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) has no unit, because the unit of \(m\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) is kg/s, which is the unit of \(c\). We multiply it by two, because then it appears in the standard form of the equation without an extra factor, as we shall see.

Definition 9.5 Critical damping and damping ratio (kritično dušenje in razmernik dušenja)

Let \(m > 0\) and \(k > 0\), \(c \ge 0\). The critical damping coefficient (kritični koeficient dušenja) is

\[c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\sqrt{k\,m} , \tag{9.10}\]

with the unit N s/m, and the damping ratio (razmernik dušenja) is the number without a unit

\[\zeta = \frac{c}{c_{\mathrm{kr}}} = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{k\,m}} . \tag{9.11}\]

Source: CK (3.4b) and (3.4c) (PDF p. 75).

Why \(c_{\mathrm{kr}}\) carries the name critical we explain in section 9.5: it is the boundary between a motion that oscillates and a motion that only returns towards equilibrium. For now it holds that the ratio of the largest forces is \(c\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/k = 2\zeta\), because \(c = 2\zeta\,m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(k = m\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). At \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the largest force of the damper is therefore 2 % of the largest force of the spring. We divide the equation (9.8) by \(m\). The term at \(\dot q\) is \(c/m = \zeta\,c_{\mathrm{kr}}/m = 2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), and the term at \(q\) is \(k/m = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), so

\[\ddot q + 2\zeta\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,q = \frac{Q(t)}{m} . \tag{9.12}\]

We call this form the standard form. Every system with \(k > 0\) has in it only the numbers \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta\) (besides the force \(Q/m\)), and these two will determine all our results: two systems with the same \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta\) move equally under the same force \(Q/m\) and the same initial values, even if they have very different masses. The number \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) determines how fast things happen, the number \(\zeta\) how fast the energy is lost.

9.1.5 Adding solutions (seštevanje rešitev)

For the equation (9.8) we shall look for solutions piece by piece: first without a force, then with simple forces, and then we put them together. What does this allow? That the equation is linear in \(q\) and its derivatives means the following.

Lemma 9.6 Adding solutions (seštevanje rešitev)

Let \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) be twice differentiable functions on an interval \(I\) that solve the equation \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = Q_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) with forces \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\), and let \(a\), \(b\) be any numbers. Then:

  1. The function \(a\,q_1 + b\,q_2\) solves the equation with the force \(a\,Q_1 + b\,Q_2\).
  2. If \(Q_1 = Q_2\), the function \(q_1 - q_2\) solves the equation without a force, \(m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = 0\).
Proof

We must show that differentiation is linear, so the left side of the equation takes over a linear combination.

  1. By lemma 1.81, \((a\,q_1 + b\,q_2)' = a\,q_1' + b\,q_2'\), and the same for the second derivative. The left side for \(a\,q_1 + b\,q_2\) is therefore \(a\,(m\ddot q_1 + c\dot q_1 + k q_1) + b\,(m\ddot q_2 + c\dot q_2 + k q_2) = a\,Q_1 + b\,Q_2\).
  2. Item 2 is item 1 with \(a = 1\), \(b = -1\) and \(Q_1 = Q_2\), because then \(a\,Q_1 + b\,Q_2 = 0\).

Source: The lemma is not from the source. CK (3.5) (PDF p. 75) says the same for the sum of the forced and the free motion.

Item 2 says that two solutions with the same force differ by a solution without a force. Every motion under a given force is therefore one known solution to which we add a solution without a force, whose two initial values we adapt to the initial values. Item 1 says that forces that we split into simple pieces we solve each separately. The whole second part of the chapter will rest on this: harmonic pieces (section 9.6), impulses (section 9.10) and periodic forces (section 9.12).

9.1.6 The systems of the chapter (sistemi poglavja)

We test the theorem and the definitions on three systems (figure 9.3).

Example 9.7 System A: the cantilever with a weight, equation and numbers (sistem A: konzola z utežjo, enačba in števila)

By equations (9.3) and (9.4) the cantilever with a weight has \(V = \tfrac12 k q^2 + m g q\) and \(T = \tfrac12 M\dot q^2\) with constant \(M\). The equilibrium is \(V'(q_0) = k q_0 + m g = 0\), so

\[q_0 = -\frac{m g}{k} = -\frac{490{,}5}{62\,720}\ \text{m} = -7{,}8205\ \text{mm} ,\]

the deflection of the end under the weight of the load (in chapter 5 the deflection under the force 500 N is 7,972 mm). The second derivative is \(V'' = k\), \(M\) does not depend on \(q\), and neither does \(c\). In theorem 9.3 all derivatives \(M'\), \(c'\) and \(V'''\) are therefore zero and the remainder \(R = 0\): within the model the linear equation is exact. The weight does not change the equation, it only shifts the equilibrium. The linearity is guaranteed by the model of the beam (small displacements, chapter 5), not by the theorem.

System A is the cantilever with a weight without the mass of the beam, so \(m = 50\) kg (this is what we call it in the whole chapter, unless said otherwise explicitly), and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{62\,720/50} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5{,}637\) Hz, \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}1774\) s. We get the same from the deflection: \(\sqrt{g/\delta_{\mathrm{st}}}\) with \(\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = 7{,}8205\) mm is also 35,4175 1/s, because \(m g = k\,\delta_{\mathrm{st}}\) and so \(k/m = g/\delta_{\mathrm{st}}\). With the mass of the beam, \(m = M = 51{,}4709\) kg and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 34{,}9078\) 1/s, 1,44 % less. We test the choice of the shape with a more accurate calculation that takes all modes of the beam into account (this calculation belongs to chapter 13, here we quote only its result): it gives \(34{,}9074\) 1/s, so the model with one shape and the mass \(33/140\,m_{\mathrm{b}}\) is only 0,0012 % away from it, and the model without the mass of the beam 1,46 %. The added mass \(\tfrac{33}{140} m_{\mathrm{b}} = 1{,}4709\) kg is 2,9 % of the mass of the weight, and its effect on the frequency is half of this, because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \propto m^{-1/2}\). The weight of the beam, \(\rho A g\int_0^L\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac38\,m_{\mathrm{b}} g\) (because \(\int_0^L\psi\,\mathrm{d}x = 3L/8\)), lowers the equilibrium by another \(0{,}366\) mm.

The damping in this model is not a drawn damper but the sum of losses in the material and at the clamp. We choose \(\zeta = 0{,}01\): CK states that the damping of structures, if it is not added on purpose, is usually in the range from 0,5 % to 5 % (PDF p. 82), and FPGL (PDF p. 46) gives 0,5 % as typical of metallic structures. Our choice lies in this range, and section 9.13 will show how to measure it from a record. Then by (9.10) the critical coefficient is \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{62\,720 \cdot 50} = 3541{,}75\) N s/m and \(c = \zeta\,c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 35{,}4175\) N s/m.

Where does the model stop being valid? The stress at the clamp under a force \(F\) on the end is \(M y/I\) with \(M = F L\) and \(y = 20\) mm (theorem 5.35). Under the weight of the load, \(F = 490{,}5\) N and \(\sigma = 490{,}5 \cdot 1 \cdot 0{,}02/(1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}) = 91{,}97\) MPa. The yield strength of the steel of chapter 5 is 220 MPa. At displacement \(q\) from equilibrium the force on the end changes by \(k\,q\), so the stress by \(k q L \cdot 0{,}02/I\). The limit is reached at \(q = (220 - 91{,}97) \cdot 10^6 \cdot 1{,}0667 \cdot 10^{-7}/(62\,720 \cdot 0{,}02) = 10{,}887\) mm. Above this amplitude the linear elastic model of chapter 5 no longer holds, and the results of the chapter hold only below it.

Example 9.8 A machine on mounts (stroj na podložkah)

A rigid machine with total mass \(M = 250\) kg (frame and rotor) stands on mounts that we model with a spring of stiffness \(k\) and a damper with coefficient \(c\). We guide the machine so that it moves only vertically (this is a simplification that we state, because real mounts also allow tilting). The coordinate \(x\) is the vertical displacement. The rotor has an unbalanced mass, and its effect is a vertical force \(Q(t) = m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\) on the machine, where \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}02\) kg m is the product of the unbalanced mass and its arm, and \(\Omega\) is the angular velocity of the rotor. At 1500 revolutions per minute \(\Omega = 157{,}08\) 1/s and the amplitude of the force is \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\Omega^2 = 493{,}48\) N. We derive this force from Lagrange's equations with a rheonomic constraint in section 9.8, here we only write it down, so that the equation of the machine then has the form (9.8) with \(m = M\), since the unbalanced mass is part of \(M\).

We choose the mounts so that the natural frequency is \(f_{\mathrm{n}} = 5\) Hz. Then \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi \cdot 5 = 31{,}416\) 1/s and \(k = M\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 250 \cdot 31{,}416^2 = 246\,740\) N/m, on four mounts 61,685 N/m each. The static deflection under the weight is \(g/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 9{,}940\) mm. The critical damping is \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{kM} = 15\,708\) N s/m and at \(\zeta = 0{,}05\), \(c = 785{,}4\) N s/m.

Example 9.9 A pendulum with a rotary damper (nihalo z vrtljivim dušilnikom)

The pendulum of chapter 8, a point mass \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid rod of length \(l = 1\) m, has \(T = \tfrac12 m l^2\,\dot\theta^2\) and \(V = -m g l\cos\theta\). Let there be a rotary damper with coefficient \(c_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}1\) N m s at the pivot, so \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12 c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\theta^2\). The exact equation from theorem 9.3 is \(m l^2\,\ddot\theta + c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\theta + m g l\sin\theta = 0\), because \(M = m l^2\) is constant. The equilibrium is \(\theta_0 = 0\) and \(k = V''(0) = m g l = 9{,}81\) N m. By the theorem the remainder is \(R = m g l\,(\theta - \sin\theta)\), and by lemma 8.19 its size relative to the retained term \(m g l\,\theta\) is smaller than \(\theta^2/6\), at \(10^\circ\) so smaller than \(0{,}51\) %. The linear equation is \(m l^2\,\ddot\theta + c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\theta + m g l\,\theta = 0\). The natural frequency is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{9{,}81/1} = 3{,}1321\) 1/s, \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2{,}0061\) s, the critical damping \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{9{,}81 \cdot 1} = 6{,}2642\) N m s, and the damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}1/6{,}2642 = 0{,}0160\).

The inverted rod with a spring that we treated in chapter 8 has \(m L^2\,\ddot\theta + (k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L)\,\theta = 0\), so \(k = k_{\mathrm{r}} - P L\). For a load \(P\) above \(P_{\mathrm{kr}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}/L\), \(k < 0\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) is not defined and neither is the damping ratio. What then happens to the motion is told by section 9.5.

The same equation also describes torsional vibration. A disk with moment of inertia \(J\) on a shaft, which turns through an angle \(\varphi\) if a moment acts on it, and with a rotary damper \(c_{\mathrm{t}}\), has \(J\,\ddot\varphi + c_{\mathrm{t}}\,\dot\varphi + k_{\mathrm{t}}\,\varphi = M_{\mathrm{t}}(t)\) with the moment \(M_{\mathrm{t}}(t)\) on the disk and with \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) the moment of the shaft per unit of rotation (the torsional stiffness of the shaft). The equation is the same as (9.8) with \(m \to J\), \(c \to c_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(k \to k_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(Q \to M_{\mathrm{t}}\). We shall compute the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{t}}\) of a round shaft in chapter 16. Everything that follows holds for it when we read \(q\) as the rotation, \(m\) as the moment of inertia and \(Q\) as the moment.

There are three systems and one equation. Now it has to be solved, first without a force and with damping, then with a force. When damping makes the amplitude shrink, it is no longer a cosine of constant amplitude. Which function has an equal quotient of values in equal steps of time, we ask in section 9.2.
