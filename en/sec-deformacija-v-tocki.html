Chapter 1 Strain

1.4 Strain at a point

Let us return to the bar and draw a piece (figure 1.45, upper left). The blackwhite line is the bar before loading, the thickened part between the points \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is the piece. The orange line is the same bar after loading, the thickened part is the same piece. The blue measuring lines show the displacements of the ends, \(u(x)\) and \(u(x + \Delta x)\), because each end moved by its own displacement. The grey dashed lines carry the positions of the ends from the blackwhite line to the orange one. The difference of the displacements, \(\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\), is the elongation of the piece, because the piece is that much longer after loading than before. The strain of the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is the difference quotient of the displacement, and as the length of the piece goes to zero it approaches the derivative of the displacement. This derivative is the measure we were looking for.

Definition 1.10 Strain at a point (deformacija v točki)

Let \(u(x)\) be the displacement of the point of the bar that was at distance \(x\) from a chosen end before loading. The strain (deformacija) of the bar at the point \(x\) is the derivative of the displacement, the quotient of the elongation \(\Delta u\) and the length of the piece \(\Delta x\) as the latter goes to zero:

\[\varepsilon(x) = u'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.7}\]

Source: BARBER (1.35), (1.36) (PDF p. 33), ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF p. 66). In books it is often written \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) or \(e_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the displacement in the direction \(x\) (why two indices, see section 1.1). The sign \(\partial\) writes the derivative when the displacement depends not only on one distance \(x\) but on the position of the point in space. We will meet it when we describe strain in space.

So the strain at a point is, like the strain of a bar (1.1), a number without a unit, an elongation per unit length. The difference is that we do not compute it for the whole bar, but for shorter and shorter pieces around the point, and take the number that their strains approach. In figure 1.45, upper right, the pieces around the same point have the lengths \(\Delta x\), \(\Delta x / 2\) and \(\Delta x / 4\), each shorter than the one before. The blue arrow shows the number \(\varepsilon(x)\) that their strains approach.

So the name strain at a point is a shorthand. The point \(x\), the blue dot on all three lines, has no length, so it cannot stretch itself. The strain at a point describes the material close to the point, in pieces that can be as short as we like. For the hanging bar we saw this in the pieces at the top with lengths from 1 m to 0.001 m: their strains are \(3{,}709\), \(3{,}885\), \(3{,}902\) and \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) and approach the same number.

For a bar that stretches the same everywhere, the two strains are equal. Let us look at the graph of the displacement \(u(x) = \varepsilon_0\,x\) with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\) (figure 1.45, below). The blackwhite line is the graph, a line through the crossing of the axes, because the displacement grows in proportion to \(x\). The two blue triangles show two pieces with the same \(\Delta x\) at different places. Their vertical sides \(\Delta u\) are equal, because the line is equally steep everywhere, and the slope \(\Delta u / \Delta x\) is the strain of the piece.

We compute the same with numbers, step by step. The displacement at both ends of the piece is:

\[u(x) = \varepsilon_0\,x , \qquad u(x + \Delta x) = \varepsilon_0\,(x + \Delta x) .\]

We get the difference of the displacements by subtraction. We expand the bracket, multiply \(\varepsilon_0\) by each term, and the terms \(\varepsilon_0\,x\) cancel:

\[\Delta u = \varepsilon_0\,x + \varepsilon_0\,\Delta x - \varepsilon_0\,x = \varepsilon_0\,\Delta x .\]

We divide the difference by \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta x\) cancels:

\[\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x} = \frac{\varepsilon_0\,\Delta x}{\Delta x} = \varepsilon_0 .\]

The difference quotient is \(\varepsilon_0\) for every \(\Delta x\), as the constant slope of the line shows, so its limit is \(\varepsilon_0\) too.

For the whole bar we put \(x = L\) and \(x = 0\) into the displacement:

\[u(L) - u(0) = \varepsilon_0\,L - 0 = \varepsilon_0\,L .\]

This is the total elongation, the measuring line at \(x = L\) on the graph. We divide it by \(L\) and get \(\Delta L / L = \varepsilon_0\), the same number as the strain at a point.

For the hanging bar we have already computed the derivative:

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

We computed it for the formula \(a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), which is defined for every number \(x\). The displacement of the bar, however, is defined only for the points of the bar, for \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\). At a point between the two ends of the bar this changes nothing. For all \(\Delta x\) for which the point \(x + \Delta x\) stays on the bar, the quotient of the displacement equals the quotient of the formula, and we may always take the band so narrow that these are all the \(\Delta x\) in the band (section 1.3.3). At the ends of the bar, however, the points of the bar lie on one side only (figure 1.46).

The hatched horizontal line at the top is the ceiling. From it hangs the bar, drawn as an upright rectangle. The arrow to the left of it is the axis \(x\). It points down, because we measure the distance \(x\) from the ceiling downward. At the top, at \(x = 0\), which the blue dot shows, the bar is only below the point. The band for \(0 < \Delta x < \delta\) is the thick blue line just under the dot, its width \(\delta\) is shown by the measuring line with end ticks to the right of the bar. The band lies on one side, so only \(\Delta x > 0\) is possible there, as written to the right of the measuring line. At the bottom, at \(x = L\), where there is again a blue dot, the bar is only above the point. A grey dashed horizontal line there joins the bar to the axis, so that we see that \(x = L\) is indeed the end of the bar. The band for \(-\delta < \Delta x < 0\) is the thick blue line above the dot, that is, on the other side, and only \(\Delta x < 0\) is possible there. The strain there is the one-sided derivative of definition 1.9: the limit from the right at the top and the limit from the left at the bottom. The condition of the one-sided limit is only part of the condition that we checked in section 1.3.4, in steps 7 to 11, for all \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\), that is, for both sides at once. What holds for both sides holds for one side as well, for example for \(\Delta x > 0\). So at the ends too \(\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\).

We draw the strain \(2a\,(L - x)\) as a graph (figure 1.47).

On the horizontal axis \(x\) is the distance from the top in metres, from \(0\) to \(10\), and on the vertical axis \(\varepsilon\) is the strain. The thick blackwhite line is the graph of \(\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x)\). It is a straight line, because \(\varepsilon\) is a linear function of \(x\), and it falls, because \(x\) has a minus sign in front. The blue dots mark three points that we compute below. At the top, at \(x = 0\), the strain is \(2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 10 = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). At the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, where a grey dashed vertical line leads from the point on the line to the axis, it is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the lower end it is zero. These are exactly the numbers that the columns of the table approached. The strain falls from the top to the bottom evenly, as the weight of the bar that a single cross section carries falls: the top carries the whole bar, the middle half, the lower end nothing.

The total elongation of the bar is the displacement of the lower end, \(\Delta L = u(L) - u(0) = aL^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and the average strain is \(\Delta L / L = aL = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), which is the orange horizontal line on the graph. This is exactly the strain at the middle. It is so because \(\varepsilon(L/2) = 2a\,(L - L/2) = aL\). This is a property of a straight line, not of every strain. The most stretched material at the top, however, is stretched twice as much as the average. If we judged by the average whether the bar holds, we would be wrong by a factor of two.

One more simple but important observation. Suppose the ceiling sags by 1 mm for some reason. We still measure the displacement from the original position of the points, so the top of the bar is now no longer at \(u(0) = 0\) but at \(u(0) = 0{,}001\) m. Every point of the bar moves by an extra millimetre, and the new displacement is \(u(x) + 0{,}001\) m. The strain does not change. In the difference quotient the extra millimetre cancels, \((u(x + \Delta x) + 0{,}001) - (u(x) + 0{,}001) = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). A displacement that is the same for all points does not deform the body, it only moves it as a whole. To give such moves a name, we imagine a body that cannot be deformed at all.

Definition 1.11 Rigid body and rigid-body displacement (togo telo in togi premik)

A rigid body (togo telo) is a body in which the distance between any two points stays the same, whatever forces act on it. A displacement of a body that does not change the distance between any two of its points is called a rigid-body displacement (togi premik).

Source: CM §3.3.1 (PDF p. 118), CM §3.8.1.1 (PDF p. 154), BARBER §1.1.6 (PDF p. 32).

No real body is rigid, since every body changes its shape under a force, as we saw at the start of the chapter. A rigid body is the picture of a body that deforms so little that its change of shape does not matter for the question at hand. The name rigid-body displacement says that this is the only kind of displacement a rigid body can undergo (BARBER §1.1.6, PDF p. 32). The extra millimetre from the sagging ceiling is a rigid-body displacement: it moves all points of the bar by the same distance, so the distances between them stay the same. But a rigid-body displacement need not be the same for all points. A body can also be turned as a whole without any distance changing, and we will meet this in section 1.6 (CM §3.8.1.1, PDF p. 156).

Let us draw all three cases with the same body, a rectangle. In each, the blackwhite rectangle is the body before the move, the orange one after it. On the body we choose two points, \(P\) and \(Q\), and join them with a grey segment. After the move they are at \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\), joined by an orange dashed segment. The blue arrows show where each point has moved. In the left case the displacement is the same for all points: the arrows are equally long and parallel, so the body is only carried along, and the segment \(P_1Q_1\) is as long as \(PQ\). Under each case it is written how long the segments are, in the same arbitrary unit, here \(5\) and \(5\). In the middle case we turn the body about the bottom left corner. Again the blackwhite rectangle is the body before the turn, the orange one after it, \(P\) and \(Q\) are the chosen points and \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\) their new positions. The arrows are now of different lengths and point in different directions, because a point that is farther from the corner moves farther. The segment is only turned, so the lengths are again equal. In the right case we stretch the body in the horizontal direction, the left edge stays. The blackwhite rectangle is the body before the stretching, the orange one is longer, \(P\) and \(Q\) are the chosen points, \(P_1\) and \(Q_1\) their new positions. The arrows are of different lengths, because \(Q\), which is farther from the left edge, moves farther than \(P\), and the lengths differ: \(5\) and about \(7\).

Since a displacement that is the same for all points does not change the strain, we can never compute the displacement completely from the strain: we can always add such a displacement. For the hanging bar it is fixed by the condition that the top is attached to the ceiling, \(u(0) = 0\) (BARBER (1.39)). That this is the only freedom, that is, that two displacements with the same strain at every point differ only by such a constant shift, we will prove in section 1.14.6.

So far the bar has been just a line: every point moved along the same straight line, and the displacement was a single number. But the bar is a body, and like every body it has a length, a width and a height. A point in a body can move in any direction, and a small piece of the body can at the same time stretch in one direction, shorten in another, and twist. If we bend a straight strip, for example, its points move along the strip and sideways at the same time, and one number does not describe that. One number is not enough to describe displacements in space. We need quantities that have a size and a direction, and we start the next section with them.
