Chapter 20 Damping models

20.4 Viscoelastic models

We stretch a steel bar by one thousandth of its length and hold it. The force that pulls it stays practically the same. A nylon bar behaves differently: if we stretch it and hold it at that length, the force slowly falls, and if we hang a constant weight on it, it keeps getting longer long after we hung the weight. For steel we neglected this in chapter 3 and wrote \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\) (definition 3.1). For nylon that does not work. We called such materials viscoelastic (viskoelastični) and promised to treat them in this chapter. How do we write a law in which the stress depends not only on the strain at this moment, and what follows from it for damping?

We test the material in two ways, which BOWER gives (§3.6.1.1, PDF p. 132). In the first we quickly stretch the bar at time \(t = 0\) to the strain \(\varepsilon_0\) and hold it. The stress we need for this falls. This phenomenon is called relaxation (relaksacija), which means loosening: the stress loosens although we do not change the strain. In the second we load the bar at time \(t = 0\) with the stress \(\sigma_0\) and hold it. The strain keeps increasing. This phenomenon is called creep (lezenje), because the strain slowly creeps on. From these two tests BOWER defines the relaxation modulus and the creep compliance, and we will turn them into definitions.

We compute under three assumptions. The strains are small, BOWER (PDF p. 130) gives less than \(0{,}5\ \%\), so as in chapter 3 we may compute stress and strain on the original cross-section and length. The temperature is constant, because the response of polymers depends strongly on it (BOWER, PDF p. 131-132). The load is uniaxial, as in the tensile test. What happens in the material? For metals we looked at this in section 20.1: heat flows between parts of a crystal and between grains, so the path of loading differs from the path of unloading. For polymers BOWER distinguishes two kinds (PDF p. 133). Heavily cross-linked polymers show retarded elastic behaviour (zakasnjeno elastično obnašanje): under constant stress the strain settles at a final value and returns after unloading. Uncross-linked polymers show steady-state creep (lezenje s stalno hitrostjo): under constant stress the strain does not settle. We will meet both kinds in the models.

From history

In 1849 the Russian government founded the Central Laboratory of Weights and Measures and installed A. T. Kupffer (1799-1865) as its director (TIMO, PDF p. 193). Kupffer studied the properties of metals because they can affect standards of measurement (TIMO, PDF p. 193). In torsional vibration he investigated damping and showed that only a part of it comes from air resistance and the rest from the viscosity of the material (TIMO, PDF p. 194). In steel bars he found that the deflection does not vanish at once after unloading but keeps decreasing for several days, which TIMO calls elastic afterstrain (elastična naknadna deformacija). This effect also gives rise to damping of vibrations (TIMO, PDF p. 194). Kupffer also showed that the damping due to the afterstrain grows with temperature, and for his work on the influence of temperature he won a prize of the Royal Society of Göttingen in 1855 (TIMO, PDF p. 194).

The story is here for two reasons. Separating air damping from damping in the material is the question of section 20.1. The slow return of the strain over several days is creep, which the Kelvin-Voigt element and the standard linear solid in this section describe.

Back to the bar that we stretch and hold, and to the question of how to write a law in which the stress does not depend only on the strain at this moment. The model needs two properties at once: it must respond at once, like a spring, and yet stress and strain must loosen with time, as with a dashpot. We know both elements. The spring is a linear elastic material, \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\) (definition 3.3). The dashpot we know from chapter 9: the force with which it resists stretching is proportional to the rate of stretching (definition 9.1). We draw them on the bar and assemble them like a circuit.

We begin with the bar: each model is a bar pulled by forces at its ends. We pull it with the stress \(\sigma\), so with the force \(\sigma A\) on the cross-section \(A\). The force is drawn on the axis of the bar and starts at the point that we pull. Next we draw the spring with modulus \(E\) as a coiled wire, because it stretches under a force, and by Hooke's law its stress is proportional to the strain, \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\). Then the dashpot: a piston in a cylinder of oil, which flows past the piston and resists its motion, so the stress is proportional to the rate of strain. A dashpot with coefficient \(c\) from chapter 9 resists stretching with the force \(c\,\dot\ell\) if the distance between the ends changes at the rate \(\dot\ell\). On a bar of length \(L\) and cross-section \(A\) the force is \(F = \sigma A\) and the elongation \(\Delta\ell = \varepsilon L\), so \(\sigma = (c L/A)\,\dot\varepsilon\). We call the number \(\eta = cL/A\) the viscosity (viskoznost) of the dashpot, its unit is Pa s, because \(\sigma\) is in Pa and \(\dot\varepsilon\) in 1/s. CM (PDF p. 426) uses the same letter. In chapter 10 \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the modal coordinate, but there are no modal coordinates here, so the two meanings do not meet. Likewise \(\tau\) is the shear stress elsewhere in the book, in this chapter there is no shear stress and we measure time with \(\tau\).

We connect two elements in two ways. In series the spring and the dashpot lie one after the other on the axis, the same force flows through both and they have the same stress \(\sigma\), while their elongations \(\varepsilon_1\) and \(\varepsilon_2\) add up to the total strain \(\varepsilon\). In parallel they lie beside each other on the axis between two rigid plates that the force pulls. The elongation is the same for both, \(\varepsilon\), and the stresses add. The third model combines both connections: the spring \(E_\infty\) is parallel to a branch in which the spring \(E_1\) and the dashpot \(\eta\) are in series.

Definition 20.19 Linear viscoelastic material (linearni viskoelastični material)

Let a bar be under uniaxial stress \(\sigma(t)\) with small strain \(\varepsilon(t)\) at constant temperature, as in chapter 3. A spring element (elastični člen) with modulus \(E > 0\) has \(\sigma = E\varepsilon\). A dashpot element (viskozni člen) with viscosity \(\eta > 0\) has \(\sigma = \eta\,\dot\varepsilon\), its strain is a continuous function of time also at a jump of the stress, because at a jump of its strain the dashpot would need an infinite stress. In series the elements have the same stress and their strains add. In parallel they have the same strain and their stresses add. A material is a linear viscoelastic material if \(\sigma(t)\) and \(\varepsilon(t)\) are related by the equation \(\sum_{\mathrm{m}=0}^{M} p_{\mathrm{m}}\,\sigma^{(m)} = \sum_{\mathrm{n}=0}^{N} q_{\mathrm{n}}\,\varepsilon^{(n)}\) with constant coefficients \(p_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(q_{\mathrm{n}}\), where \(\sigma^{(m)}\) is the \(m\)-th time derivative of the stress and \(\varepsilon^{(n)}\) the \(n\)-th derivative of the strain. This is the equation of every model made of springs and dashpots. For \(M = N = 1\) the material is a first-order linear viscoelastic material (linearni viskoelastični material prvega reda) and the equation is

\[p_0\,\sigma + p_1\,\dot\sigma = q_0\,\varepsilon + q_1\,\dot\varepsilon \tag{20.15}\]

with constant coefficients \(p_0, p_1, q_0, q_1\). At a jump of \(\sigma\) or \(\varepsilon\) the equation (20.15) holds at every point where \(\sigma\) and \(\varepsilon\) are differentiable, and at the jump the relations of the elements above hold.

Source: CM (9.1.1)-(9.1.2) (PDF p. 419), the general form, CM §9.2 (PDF p. 425-433), equation (9.2.12) for the case \(M = N = 1\), and BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 137-138). The statement at a jump is from this section. Higher orders (four-element models, CM §9.2.5, PDF p. 433-435) are not treated here.

The three models of the figure are special cases: for the Maxwell element \(q_0 = 0\), for the Kelvin-Voigt element \(p_1 = 0\), for the standard linear solid all four coefficients are non-zero. We derive each from its elements in the theorem on its response.

This is a simplification and it has to be said. The spring and dashpot together have a single time, the ratio \(\eta/E\), whereas a real polymer, by BOWER (PDF p. 139), has a response that three elements do not hit over a long time. CM (PDF p. 419) writes that such models give a qualitative picture and not a satisfactory quantitative description of any real material. For a better description we take several Maxwell elements at once (Prony series, BOWER §3.6.4, PDF p. 139), which is the subject of section 20.5.

Now we turn the tests into numbers. In relaxation the strain jumps at \(t = 0\) to \(\varepsilon_0\), before that the bar was at rest.

Definition 20.20 Relaxation modulus (relaksacijski modul)

Let a bar of linear viscoelastic material be at rest until time \(t = 0\) and let \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0\) for all \(t \ge 0\). Let the stress be bounded on every interval \((0, T]\). The relaxation modulus (relaksacijski modul) is the function \(E_{\mathrm{rel}} : (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\),

\[E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = \frac{\sigma(t)}{\varepsilon_0} , \tag{20.16}\]

in Pa. If they exist, \(E_0 = E_{\mathrm{rel}}(0+)\) is the instantaneous modulus (trenutni modul) and \(E_\infty = \lim_{\mathrm{t}\to\infty} E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\) the long-time modulus (dolgotrajni modul). For a linear material \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) does not depend on \(\varepsilon_0\), which we will see for each model.

Source: CM §9.2.1 (PDF p. 425), where the relaxation modulus is written \(Y(t)\), and BOWER §3.6.1.1 (PDF p. 132), where \(G(t) = \tau(t)/2\Delta\varepsilon\) for shear. It is written \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\) here so that it stays connected with the modulus \(E\) of chapter 3.

In creep the stress jumps at \(t = 0\) to \(\sigma_0\).

Definition 20.21 Creep compliance (podajnost lezenja)

Let a bar of linear viscoelastic material be at rest until time \(t = 0\) and let \(\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\) for all \(t \ge 0\). The creep compliance (podajnost lezenja) is the function \(J_{\mathrm{lez}} : (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\),

\[J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t) = \frac{\varepsilon(t)}{\sigma_0} , \tag{20.17}\]

in 1/Pa. The value \(J_{\mathrm{lez}}(0+)\) is the instantaneous compliance and \(\lim_{\mathrm{t}\to\infty} J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t)\) the long-time compliance, if they exist.

Source: CM §9.2.1 (PDF p. 425), where the compliance is written \(J(t)\), and BOWER §3.6.1.1 (PDF p. 132). The letter \(J\) is also the Jacobian matrix of the chapters on deformation, hence the index.

The word compliance says how much the material gives way per unit stress, and it is the reciprocal of stiffness. For a spring \(J_{\mathrm{lez}} = 1/E\) and \(E_{\mathrm{rel}} = E\) are constants. For a viscoelastic material they are functions of time.

For each model we will ask two questions. First: does the strain settle under constant stress, as it must in a solid that keeps its shape? Second: does the material respond to a jump of strain with a finite stress, like a spring? We begin with the model that fails the first. A dashpot in series with a spring is the Maxwell element, which the figure shows in the top row.

Definition 20.22 Maxwell element (Maxwellov člen)

The Maxwell element is the series connection of a spring element with modulus \(E > 0\) and a dashpot element with viscosity \(\eta > 0\). The number \(\tau = \eta/E\) is the relaxation time (relaksacijski čas) and has the unit second.

Source: CM §9.2.2 (PDF p. 426), BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 137-138).

The number \(\tau\) is called the relaxation time by what it means in the test: the theorem below shows that at a jump of strain the stress falls to \(1/\mathrm{e}\) of its initial value in the time \(\tau\).

Theorem 20.23 Response of a Maxwell element (odziv Maxwellovega člena)

Let a Maxwell element have \(E\), \(\eta\) and \(\tau = \eta/E\).

  1. Stress and strain satisfy \(\sigma + \tau\dot\sigma = \eta\,\dot\varepsilon\).
  2. At a jump of the strain to \(\varepsilon_0\) at \(t = 0\), \(\sigma(t) = E\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) for \(t > 0\), so \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = E\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\), \(E_0 = E\) and \(E_\infty = 0\).
  3. At a jump of the stress to \(\sigma_0\) at \(t = 0\), \(\varepsilon(t) = \sigma_0\,(1/E + t/\eta)\) for \(t \ge 0\), so \(J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t) = 1/E + t/\eta\), which grows beyond every bound as \(t \to \infty\).
  4. If the stress \(\sigma_0\) is removed after a time \(T > 0\), the strain first jumps back by \(\sigma_0/E\) and then stays at the value \(\sigma_0 T/\eta\), which is not zero.
Proof

We must show four claims. In all of them we use both elements and the connection of definition 20.19, in point 2 also lemma 9.34.

  1. Let \(\varepsilon_1\) be the strain of the spring and \(\varepsilon_2\) the strain of the dashpot, \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2\), the stress \(\sigma\) is the same (series). The elements give \(\varepsilon_1 = \sigma/E\) and \(\dot\varepsilon_2 = \sigma/\eta\).
  2. We differentiate the first equation, \(\dot\varepsilon_1 = \dot\sigma/E\). The sum is \(\dot\varepsilon = \dot\sigma/E + \sigma/\eta\). We multiply by \(\eta\) and use \(\eta/E = \tau\): \(\eta\,\dot\varepsilon = \tau\dot\sigma + \sigma\). This is point 1.
  3. Relaxation: the stress is bounded, so \(\varepsilon_2(t) = \int_0^t \sigma(u)/\eta\,\mathrm{d}u\) is continuous and \(\varepsilon_2(0) = 0\) (fundamental theorem, theorem 3.40). So the spring takes the whole jump, \(\varepsilon_1(0+) = \varepsilon_0\) and \(\sigma(0+) = E\varepsilon_0\).
  4. For \(t > 0\), \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\) is constant, \(\dot\varepsilon = 0\), and point 1 gives \(\tau\dot\sigma + \sigma = 0\), that is \(\dot\sigma - (-1/\tau)\,\sigma = 0\). By lemma 9.34 with \(s = -1/\tau\), \(f = 0\) and \(y_0 = E\varepsilon_0\) this equation has exactly one solution, \(\sigma(t) = E\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\). This is point 2.
  5. Creep: for \(t \ge 0\), \(\sigma = \sigma_0\), so \(\varepsilon_1 = \sigma_0/E\) is constant. The dashpot gives \(\dot\varepsilon_2 = \sigma_0/\eta\) with \(\varepsilon_2(0) = 0\) as in step 3. By the fundamental theorem \(\varepsilon_2(t) = \sigma_0 t/\eta\) and \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 = \sigma_0(1/E + t/\eta)\). This is point 3.
  6. Unloading: for \(t > T\), \(\sigma = 0\), so \(\dot\varepsilon_2 = 0\) and \(\varepsilon_2\) stays at the value \(\sigma_0 T/\eta\) that it had at \(T\) (continuous as in step 3). The spring has \(\varepsilon_1 = 0\) and has jumped back by \(\sigma_0/E\). The final strain is \(\sigma_0 T/\eta\). This is point 4.

Source: CM (9.2.12)-(9.2.19) (PDF p. 426-428) and BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 137-138). The proof is with lemma 9.34, CM uses the Laplace transform.

Let us see this in numbers. We take a Maxwell element with \(E = 1\) and \(\tau = 1\), dimensionless, a chosen model and not a material. At \(t/\tau = 0{,}5;\ 1;\ 2;\ 5\) we have \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}/E = 0{,}6065;\ 0{,}3679;\ 0{,}1353;\ 0{,}0067\) and it goes to zero, while the compliance \(E J_{\mathrm{lez}}\) is \(1{,}5;\ 2;\ 3;\ 6\). If we load the element with \(\sigma_0 = 1\) until \(T = 2\tau\) and unload it, the strain \(2\) remains: of the \(3\) that it had reached, the spring returned \(1\).

The figure shows what the theorem says. We draw the left graph: the stress at a jump of strain by \(1\) in units of \(E\). First the Maxwell element. It starts at \(1\), because the spring takes the whole jump, and falls towards zero, because the dashpot slowly lengthens and the spring unloads. Then the right graph, the strain at a jump of stress. The Maxwell curve goes up as a straight line without bound, so under constant stress the Maxwell element never finds equilibrium. The first question fails for a solid: the element behaves like a liquid that flows under constant stress (CM, PDF p. 419, which calls the Maxwell model the model of a viscoelastic fluid). After unloading the strain remains, the opposite of a steel bar, which returns. For polymers in which the permanent strain dominates this is fitting: BOWER (PDF p. 138) says that the Maxwell model gives steady-state creep and represents an uncross-linked polymer.

In parallel it is the other way round. In the Kelvin-Voigt element of the middle row the spring has the same strain as the dashpot, so the dashpot holds the spring back.

Definition 20.24 Kelvin-Voigt element (Kelvin-Voigtov člen)

The Kelvin-Voigt element is the parallel connection of a spring element with modulus \(E > 0\) and a dashpot element with viscosity \(\eta > 0\). Stress and strain satisfy \(\sigma = E\varepsilon + \eta\,\dot\varepsilon\). The number \(\tau_\varepsilon = \eta/E\) is the retardation time (čas zakasnitve) and has the unit second.

Source: CM §9.2.3 (PDF p. 429, equation (9.2.22)), BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 137-138).

The equation in the definition is the sum of the stress of the spring \(E\varepsilon\) and the stress of the dashpot \(\eta\dot\varepsilon\), which have the same strain. The time \(\tau_\varepsilon\) is called the retardation time because the strain follows the stress with a delay: at a jump of stress it does not jump, it approaches the new value in a time of this size, as shown below.

Theorem 20.25 Response of a Kelvin-Voigt element (odziv Kelvin-Voigtovega člena)

Let a Kelvin-Voigt element have \(E\), \(\eta\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = \eta/E\).

  1. At a jump of the stress to \(\sigma_0\) at \(t = 0\), \(\varepsilon(t) = (\sigma_0/E)\,(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon})\) for \(t \ge 0\), so \(J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t) = (1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon})/E\). It is bounded by \(1/E\) and approaches it as \(t \to \infty\).
  2. If the stress is removed after a time \(T\), \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon(T)\,\mathrm{e}^{-(t - T)/\tau_\varepsilon}\) for \(t \ge T\). The strain decreases towards zero: after every time \(\tau_\varepsilon\) it is \(\mathrm{e}\) times smaller.
  3. Let the strain grow uniformly in a time \(h > 0\) from \(0\) to \(\varepsilon_0\), \(\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon_0 t/h\) for \(0 \le t \le h\). Then \(\sigma(t) = \varepsilon_0\,(E t + \eta)/h\) on this interval and the mean value of the stress is \(\eta\varepsilon_0/h + E\varepsilon_0/2\). For \(h \to 0\) it exceeds every bound.
Proof

We must show three claims. The first two we solve with lemma 9.34, the third with the fundamental theorem.

  1. At constant stress \(\sigma_0\) for \(t \ge 0\) the equation of the element gives \(\eta\,\dot\varepsilon + E\varepsilon = \sigma_0\), that is \(\dot\varepsilon - (-1/\tau_\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon = \sigma_0/\eta\).
  2. The strain is continuous at \(t = 0\), because otherwise the force of the dashpot \(\eta\dot\varepsilon\) would be infinite: \(\dot\varepsilon = (\sigma_0 - E\varepsilon)/\eta\) must be bounded. So \(\varepsilon(0) = 0\).
  3. By lemma 9.34 with \(s = -1/\tau_\varepsilon\), \(f = \sigma_0/\eta\) and \(y_0 = 0\), \(\varepsilon(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\int_0^t \mathrm{e}^{u/\tau_\varepsilon}\,\sigma_0/\eta\,\mathrm{d}u\). The function \(\tau_\varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{u/\tau_\varepsilon}\) has the derivative \(\mathrm{e}^{u/\tau_\varepsilon}\), so by the fundamental theorem the integral equals \((\sigma_0/\eta)\,\tau_\varepsilon\,(\mathrm{e}^{t/\tau_\varepsilon} - 1) = (\sigma_0/E)(\mathrm{e}^{t/\tau_\varepsilon} - 1)\), since \(\tau_\varepsilon/\eta = 1/E\). Hence point 1. Since \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon} \to 0\), \(\varepsilon \to \sigma_0/E\).
  4. For \(t \ge T\), \(\sigma = 0\) and the equation is \(\dot\varepsilon - (-1/\tau_\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon = 0\) with start at time \(T\) and \(y_0 = \varepsilon(T)\). Lemma 9.34, applied with the start at \(T\) instead of \(0\), gives point 2.
  5. Point 3: on \(0 < t < h\), \(\dot\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0/h\) is constant, and \(\sigma = E\varepsilon + \eta\dot\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(Et + \eta)/h\). The mean value is \(\frac1h\int_0^h \sigma\,\mathrm{d}t = \varepsilon_0(Eh/2 + \eta)/h\) (fundamental theorem), which is \(\eta\varepsilon_0/h + E\varepsilon_0/2\). The first term grows beyond every bound as \(h \to 0\).

Source: CM (9.2.22)-(9.2.26) (PDF p. 429-430), CM (9.2.54) (PDF p. 434), where the Dirac delta shows that the model has no impact response, and BOWER (PDF p. 138), which says that the Kelvin-Voigt model has no well-defined relaxation modulus. Points 2 and 3 are written in this section, not from the source.

Let us look at the numbers for \(E = 1\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = 1\): \(E J_{\mathrm{lez}} = 0{,}3935;\ 0{,}6321;\ 0{,}8647;\ 0{,}9933\) at \(t/\tau_\varepsilon = 0{,}5;\ 1;\ 2;\ 5\). This is the curve on the right graph, which rises and settles. After the time \(\tau_\varepsilon\) it reaches the fraction \(1 - 1/\mathrm{e} = 0{,}6321\) of the final value, which explains the name. The first question is passed here: the strain settles at \(\sigma_0/E\) and returns after unloading, which BOWER (PDF p. 138) calls the retarded elastic behaviour of a cross-linked polymer, similar to the afterstrain that Kupffer described, except that the model returns it in the time \(\tau_\varepsilon\). The second question fails. If we increase the strain from \(0\) to \(\varepsilon_0\) in the time \(h = 0{,}01\,\tau_\varepsilon\), the mean stress by point 3 is more than \(\eta\varepsilon_0/h = 100\,E\varepsilon_0\), and a smaller \(h\) gives an even larger one. The Kelvin-Voigt element does not bear a jump of strain: it would need an infinite stress, which the arrow at the axis of the left graph shows. So the relaxation modulus (definition 20.20) is not defined, as BOWER also says.

The first model was left without boundedness, the second without an instantaneous response. Each has what the other lacks, so we combine them. The third model, the bottom row, has the spring \(E_\infty\) in parallel with a Maxwell branch.

Definition 20.26 Standard linear solid (standardni trdnik)

The standard linear solid is the parallel connection of a spring element with modulus \(E_\infty > 0\) and a Maxwell element with modulus \(E_1 > 0\) and viscosity \(\eta > 0\). We write \(E_0 = E_\infty + E_1\), \(\tau = \eta/E_1\) (the relaxation time of the branch) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau E_0/E_\infty\) (the retardation time).

Source: CM §9.2.4 (PDF p. 431-433), Fig. 9.2.9(b), which calls it the three-element model, and Example 9.2.1 (PDF p. 435), the standard linear solid. BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 138) calls it the three-parameter model.

The theorem below explains the names \(\tau\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon\): \(\tau\) determines how fast the stress loosens at a jump of strain, \(\tau_\varepsilon\) how fast the strain settles at a jump of stress. The ratio \(\tau_\varepsilon/\tau = E_0/E_\infty > 1\) says that they differ.

Theorem 20.27 Response of the standard linear solid (odziv standardnega trdnika)

Let a standard linear solid have \(E_\infty\), \(E_1\), \(\eta\) and \(E_0\), \(\tau\), \(\tau_\varepsilon\) from definition 20.26.

  1. Stress and strain satisfy \(\sigma + \tau\dot\sigma = E_\infty\varepsilon + \tau E_0\,\dot\varepsilon\).
  2. The relaxation modulus is \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t) = E_\infty + (E_0 - E_\infty)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\), so it goes from \(E_0\) at \(t = 0+\) to \(E_\infty > 0\).
  3. The creep compliance is \(J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t) = 1/E_\infty - (1/E_\infty - 1/E_0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\), so it goes from \(1/E_0\) to \(1/E_\infty\).
  4. If the stress \(\sigma_0\) is removed after a time \(T\), the strain first jumps back by \(\sigma_0/E_0\) and then decreases towards zero: after every time \(\tau_\varepsilon\) it is \(\mathrm{e}\) times smaller.
Proof

We must show four claims. The branches are parallel and have the same strain \(\varepsilon\), the stress is the sum \(\sigma = \sigma_\infty + \sigma_{\mathrm{M}}\) with \(\sigma_\infty = E_\infty\varepsilon\) and the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{M}}\) of the Maxwell branch.

  1. By theorem 20.23, point 1, with \(E_1\) in place of \(E\) and \(\eta = \tau E_1\), the Maxwell branch satisfies \(\sigma_{\mathrm{M}} + \tau\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{M}} = \tau E_1\,\dot\varepsilon\).
  2. Point 1 of the theorem: \(\sigma + \tau\dot\sigma = E_\infty\varepsilon + \tau E_\infty\dot\varepsilon + (\sigma_{\mathrm{M}} + \tau\dot\sigma_{\mathrm{M}}) = E_\infty\varepsilon + \tau E_\infty\dot\varepsilon + \tau E_1\dot\varepsilon = E_\infty\varepsilon + \tau E_0\dot\varepsilon\).
  3. Relaxation: by theorem 20.23, point 2 (with \(E_1\)), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{M}} = E_1\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) for \(t > 0\), and \(\sigma_\infty = E_\infty\varepsilon_0\). The sum is \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_\infty + E_1\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau})\) with \(E_1 = E_0 - E_\infty\). This is point 2.
  4. Creep: for \(t \ge 0\), \(\sigma = \sigma_0\). The dashpot does not move at \(t = 0\) (its strain \(\varepsilon_2\) is continuous and \(\varepsilon_2(0) = 0\)), so the strain of the spring of the Maxwell branch is \(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon\) and \(\sigma_0 = E_\infty\varepsilon(0+) + E_1\varepsilon(0+) = E_0\,\varepsilon(0+)\).
  5. For \(t > 0\), \(\dot\sigma = 0\) and point 1 gives \(\tau E_0\,\dot\varepsilon + E_\infty\varepsilon = \sigma_0\), that is \(\dot\varepsilon - (-1/\tau_\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon = \sigma_0/(\tau E_0)\) with \(\tau_\varepsilon = \tau E_0/E_\infty\). Lemma 9.34 with \(y_0 = \sigma_0/E_0\) gives \(\varepsilon = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\big(\sigma_0/E_0 + \tfrac{\sigma_0}{\tau E_0}\,\tau_\varepsilon(\mathrm{e}^{t/\tau_\varepsilon} - 1)\big)\) (the integral as in theorem 20.25). Since \(\tau_\varepsilon/(\tau E_0) = 1/E_\infty\), \(\varepsilon = \sigma_0\big(1/E_\infty - (1/E_\infty - 1/E_0)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_\varepsilon}\big)\). This is point 3.
  6. Unloading at \(T\): the dashpot does not move, so \(\sigma = E_\infty\varepsilon + E_1(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2)\) is linear in \(\varepsilon\) with the same \(\varepsilon_2\). The drop of \(\sigma\) from \(\sigma_0\) to \(0\) is thus \(-\sigma_0 = E_0\,\Delta\varepsilon\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon = -\sigma_0/E_0\). For \(t > T\), \(\sigma = 0\) and point 1 gives \(\dot\varepsilon - (-1/\tau_\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon = 0\), and by lemma 9.34 \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon(T+)\,\mathrm{e}^{-(t - T)/\tau_\varepsilon}\). This is point 4.

Source: CM §9.2.4 and Example 9.2.1 (PDF p. 431-433, 435), BOWER §3.6.3 (PDF p. 138). The split into a spring and a Maxwell branch is written in this section, not from the source.

We take \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\), so \(E_1 = E_\infty\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = 2\tau\). This is a chosen model and not a material, the numbers are dimensionless. For \(t/\tau = 0{,}5;\ 1;\ 2;\ 5\) the values are in the table.

\(t/\tau\) \(0{,}5\) \(1\) \(2\) \(5\)
\(E_{\mathrm{rel}}/E_\infty\) \(1{,}6065\) \(1{,}3679\) \(1{,}1353\) \(1{,}0067\)
\(E_\infty J_{\mathrm{lez}}\) \(0{,}6106\) \(0{,}6967\) \(0{,}8161\) \(0{,}9590\)

The curve of the standard linear solid falls from \(2\) to \(1\) on the left graph and rises from \(0{,}5\) to \(1\) on the right. The first question is passed, because the strain settles at \(\sigma_0/E_\infty\). The second is passed, because the bar responds to a jump of stress at once with \(\varepsilon(0+) = \sigma_0/E_0\), and at a jump of strain it gets the finite stress \(E_0\varepsilon_0\). This is the smallest model that passes both tests. We checked the values with an independent computation: numerical integration (RK4, step \(0{,}001\,\tau\)) of the equation for the strain of the dashpot, \(\dot\varepsilon_2 = (\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2)/\tau\), gives the same four values in both rows, and SymPy shows that both functions satisfy the equation of point 1.

Two mistakes are easy to make with this model. The first is to take the same time for relaxation and for creep. If in the compliance we take \(\tau\) in place of \(\tau_\varepsilon\), at \(t = \tau\) the value is \(E_\infty J_{\mathrm{lez}} = 0{,}8161\) instead of \(0{,}6967\), so creep would look twice as fast. The true reason that \(\tau_\varepsilon > \tau\) is seen on the dashpot. Its strain \(\varepsilon_2\) catches up with the strain \(\varepsilon\) at the rate \(\dot\varepsilon_2 = (\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2)/\tau\) (step 1 of theorem 20.23 with \(E_1\)). At a jump of strain \(\varepsilon\) is fixed and the difference \(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2\) falls at the rate \(1/\tau\). At a jump of stress \(\varepsilon\) moves with the dashpot: from \(\sigma_0 = E_\infty\varepsilon + E_1(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2)\) we get \(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_2 = (\sigma_0 - E_\infty\varepsilon_2)/E_0\), so the difference falls \(E_\infty/E_0\) times slower, at the rate \(1/\tau_\varepsilon\). The second mistake is to think that \(J_{\mathrm{lez}}(t) = 1/E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\). At \(t = 0+\) and as \(t \to \infty\) it holds, \(E_0 J_{\mathrm{lez}}(0+) = 1\) and \(E_\infty J_{\mathrm{lez}}(\infty) = 1\). In between the product \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}\,J_{\mathrm{lez}}\) at \(t/\tau = 1;\ 2;\ 5\) equals \(0{,}9530;\ 0{,}9265;\ 0{,}9654\) and is not \(1\). The true relation is a relation between the Laplace transforms (CM (9.2.9), PDF p. 425), which we do not prove here and only cite.

What have we got? Three models, each derived from a spring and a dashpot, have shown that stress and strain in a viscoelastic material move with a delay and that this is what we need for losses. The Maxwell element fails the first question, the Kelvin-Voigt element the second, the standard linear solid passes both. The next section (20.5) writes the stress for any history of strain as a sum of loosenings, and section 20.6 shows how much energy the standard linear solid loses per cycle.
