Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.6 Shear stresses in bending

By lemma 5.9 the shear force \(V\) is the integral of the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) over the cross section, \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (for a rectangle, and by corollary 5.19 for a figure made of rectangles). Take the cantilever of section 5.1.3, a flat steel beam with a \(20 \times 40\) mm cross section and length \(L = 1\) m, loaded at its free end by a force \(F = 500\) N downwards. Here \(V = -500\) N in every cross section. Some shear stress must therefore exist in the cross section. The beam model, however, assumed that cross sections stay perpendicular to the axis, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (section 5.3.2), and by Hooke's law (theorem 3.11) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). Hooke's law and the shear force contradict each other. We resolve this by not computing \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from the deformation but from equilibrium. In this section we answer the questions how the shear stress is distributed over the height of the cross section, how large it is and how good this computation is.

From history

Coulomb pointed out the shear stresses in a cantilever and wrote that they matter only in short beams (TIMO, PDF p. 149). Young wrote that solid bodies differ from liquids in resisting shear. Vicat gave many cases in which this resistance is decisive and criticized the theory of bending because it ignores shear stresses (TIMO, PDF p. 149). The first edition of Navier's book in 1826 says nothing about them. The second edition in 1833 has an article on the bending of short beams, in which the shear stress at the built-in end is taken as an average over the cross section, and the way to combine it with the longitudinal bending stresses is unsatisfactory (TIMO, PDF p. 149). The exact solution was given by Saint-Venant in his memoir on bending, but only for a few of the simplest cross-section shapes. For more complicated shapes engineers had to use the approximate, simple solution presented by D. J. Jourawski (1821 to 1891) (TIMO, PDF p. 149).

He developed the theory of shear stresses in rectangular beams while designing wooden bridges for the railway between St. Petersburg and Moscow between 1844 and 1850, and presented it in 1854 to the Russian Academy of Sciences, which awarded him the Demidov prize for the work (TIMO, PDF p. 149, footnote). He graduated in 1842 from the Institute of Engineers of Ways of Communication in St. Petersburg and immediately got work on building this railway (TIMO, PDF p. 149-150). In 1844 he was given the design and construction of a bridge over the river Werebia (spelling as in the source), with nine spans, a total length of 180 feet, 170 feet above the water (TIMO, PDF p. 150). For this bridge he had to use wooden beams of great depth and built-up wooden beams. Wood is very weak in shear along the fibres, so Jourawski correctly concluded that shear stresses in such beams must not be neglected (TIMO, PDF p. 150). The literature had nothing on calculating them, so he had to work them out himself (TIMO, PDF p. 150).

He started with the simplest case, a rectangular cantilever with a load at the free end. He reasoned that the normal stresses across the cross section at the built-in end tend to cause shear in the neutral plane \(00\) (TIMO, PDF p. 150). The shear force \(T\) in this plane equals the resultant of the normal stresses of the upper half of the cross section at the built-in end. The normal stress is largest at the edge, \(\sigma_{\max} = 6 Q l/(b h^2)\), and falls to zero toward the neutral plane. The resultant in the upper half is therefore \(\tfrac12\sigma_{\max}\cdot\tfrac12 b h = 3 Q l/(2h)\), where \(Q\) is the load, \(l\) the length of the bar, \(b\) the width and \(h\) the depth of the cross section. The plane \(00\) has area \(l b\), so the shear stress is \(T/(l b) = 3 Q/(2 b h)\) (TIMO, PDF p. 151). He calculated the stresses in any horizontal plane \(ss\) above the plane \(00\) in a similar way (TIMO, PDF p. 151). For a uniformly distributed load he showed that the shear stress along the horizontal plane is no longer constant but grows with the distance from the free end (TIMO, PDF p. 151). With the same idea he calculated the forces on each connecting element of built-up wooden beams and from them the required dimensions of the elements (TIMO, PDF p. 151). He also calculated the rivet spacing in built-up iron beams and in the Conway and Britannia tubular bridges. He showed that the number of rivets could be reduced considerably, because the transverse force on the tube falls from the ends toward the middle of the span, and the rivet spacing in the middle can therefore be larger (TIMO, PDF p. 151). Saint-Venant praised his approximate method and adapted it to a rectangular beam whose depth is much larger than its width (TIMO, PDF p. 151).

Let us return to the question of the section: how the shear force \(V\) is distributed over the cross section as shear stress. Jourawski's reasoning, the equilibrium of the part of the beam above a horizontal cut, we shall now derive for an arbitrary cross section. For a rectangular cross section our result must give in the neutral plane the same value as Jourawski's, \(3 Q/(2 b h)\) for a load \(Q = |V|\), and we shall indeed obtain it in section 5.6.2.

5.6.1 Equilibrium of part of a slice

Let us first look at the simplest case in which shear stress appears. Two equal boards, each \(20 \times 20\) mm, are laid one on top of the other and loaded as a beam. If they are not glued, each bends on its own and at the ends they slide along each other, like cards in a deck (figure 1.5). If we glue them, the glue prevents the sliding by pulling them together with a stress that lies in the horizontal plane of the joint and points along the axis \(x\). The glued and the free beam also do not bend alike: the second moment of area of the \(20 \times 40\) mm cross section is \(1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\), and the sum of the second moments of the two free boards is \(2{,}667\cdot 10^{-8}\) m\(^4\). At the same curvature the glued beam therefore carries a moment four times as large. A beam made of one piece is like a glued one in every horizontal plane, since the material does not slide there, and so there is a shear stress in every such plane.

How large this stress is we compute by cutting the beam in thought also along a horizontal plane. Take a straight prismatic beam (the cross section does not change along the axis) whose cross section has the axis \(y\) as an axis of symmetry, with the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections and with the load in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), as in the previous sections. We use the assumptions of theorem 5.35, and we take the axial load to be zero: the load on the surface and the body force have no component in the direction \(x\), so \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Otherwise the equilibrium of forces in the direction \(x\) would contain one more term for the load. Equilibrium of a piece of the beam tells us how large the stress must be. Imagine a slice between the cross sections at \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) and cut it with the plane \(y = y_0\). We consider the part of the slice above this plane (figure 5.38). The longitudinal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) act on the faces of this part. Since the bending moment changes along the beam, the stresses on the right face (\(x + \Delta x\)) are not the same as on the left one (\(x\)), and their difference is not balanced. It is balanced by the shear stress on the lower face of the part, that is, on the cut.

For the computation we need the first moment of the part of the cross section above the cut. We shall use it in the examples too, so we introduce it with a definition. The first moment of the whole cross section about an axis through the centroid is zero (lemma 5.22), but the first moment of a part of the cross section is not.

Definition 5.38 First moment of a partial area (statični moment dela prereza)

Let \(A\) be the cross section of a beam in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) with the axis \(x\) through the centroid, and let \(y_0\) be the height at which we cut the cross section by a horizontal line. Denote by \(A^*(y_0)\) the part of the cross section above this line, that is, the points of the cross section with \(y \ge y_0\). The first moment of a partial area (statični moment dela prereza) is the number

\[S(y_0) = \int_{\mathrm{A}^*(\mathrm{y}_0)} y\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.27}\]

with unit m\(^3\). The width of the cross section \(b(y_0)\) is the total length of that part of the line \(y = y_0\) which lies in the cross section.

Source: SADD (D.9) and the text before it (PDF p. 580), EP (2.5.19), (2.5.20) (p. 76). Both books write this number \(Q\), in this book it is \(S\) as in section 5.4.2.

The first moment of a partial area is the product of the area of the part and the height of its centroid, \(S(y_0) = A^*\,y_{\mathrm{T}}^*\), where \(y_{\mathrm{T}}^*\) is the coordinate \(y\) of the centroid of the part \(A^*\) (definition 5.21). How does \(S\) change with \(y_0\)? For \(y_0\) at the top of the cross section the part is empty and \(S = 0\). For \(y_0\) at the bottom \(A^*\) is the whole cross section and \(S = 0\), because the whole cross section has first moment zero. When we increase \(y_0\) by \(\Delta y_0\), we lose a strip of height \(\Delta y_0\) and width \(b(y_0)\) at height \(y_0\), which contributes \(y_0\,b(y_0)\,\Delta y_0\), so \(\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}y_0 = -y_0\,b(y_0)\). Below the axis \(y_0 < 0\) and \(S\) grows, above the axis it falls. Therefore \(S \ge 0\) for all \(y_0\) and it is largest at the axis, \(y_0 = 0\). Figure 5.39 shows this for a rectangle.

Equilibrium of a part of the slice gives us only the total force across the cut, that is, the integral of the shear stress over the width of the cut. We obtain the stress from it only with an additional assumption, that it is uniform across the width of the cut \(b(y_0)\), and we use it only to replace the integral by the product \(b\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The assumption does not hold exactly: near a free side face the stress may change. In section 5.6.3 we shall show how large the error is for our beam.

Theorem 5.39 Jourawski's formula for shear stress (Jourawskijeva enačba za strižno napetost)

Let the beam be straight and prismatic with a plane of symmetry \(x\)-\(y\), the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections and the load in this plane. Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold. The load on the surface and the body force have no component in the direction \(x\), so \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Let \(N\) and \(M\) be differentiable functions of \(x\) on the interval considered and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) continuous in \(x\). Let \(b(y_0) > 0\) be the width of the cross section at height \(y_0\) and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be uniform across the width along this cut. Then at every point of the cross section at height \(y_0\)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, y_0) = \frac{V(x)\,S(y_0)}{I\,b(y_0)} , \tag{5.28}\]

where \(S(y_0)\) is the first moment of the part of the cross section above \(y_0\) (definition 5.38), \(I\) is the second moment of area (definition 5.26) and the signs hold by the convention by which \(V\) and \(M\) mean the forces on the face with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 5.7).

Idea

The part of the slice above the cut is in equilibrium. The longitudinal stresses on its two faces differ, because the moment differs, and the difference must be balanced by the shear stress on the cut. We know the stresses on the faces from theorem 5.35, so from the difference we obtain the stress on the cut.

Proof

We must show that the shear stress on the cut equals \(V S/(I b)\). We write the equilibrium of forces in the direction \(x\) for the part of the slice above the cut and divide it by \(\Delta x\).

  1. Let \(P\) be the part of the slice between the cross sections \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) above the plane \(y = y_0\). It is a part of a body in equilibrium, so by definition 2.3 the sum of the forces on it is zero. The force across a face is the stress vector times the area, and by Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.24) we obtain the stress vector from the stress components. For a face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the component \(x\) of the stress vector is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and for a face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) (definition 2.25).
  2. The faces of the part \(P\) are: the right face at \(x + \Delta x\) with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the left face at \(x\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the cut at \(y_0\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), because it points out of the part \(P\), which lies above it, downwards, the upper face of the beam and the two side faces. By assumption the load on the surface and the body force have no component \(x\), so these faces and the volume of the part \(P\) contribute nothing in the direction \(x\).
  3. The sum of the components \(x\) is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x + \Delta x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A \\ &\quad - \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x + \Delta x}\!\int \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0, z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.29}\]
    The first term has the normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the second the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the third has a minus sign because of the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the inner integral runs over the width of the cut.
  4. By the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.36) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and by assumption this is uniform across the width. The inner integral is therefore \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0)\). Denote \(\Phi(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A\). Equation (5.29) then says
    \[\Phi(x + \Delta x) - \Phi(x) = b(y_0)\int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x + \Delta x} \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0)\,\mathrm{d}\xi .\]
  5. We divide by \(\Delta x\) and let \(\Delta x \to 0\). By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.40, part 2) the right-hand side approaches \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(x, y_0)\), because by assumption \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is continuous in \(x\). The left-hand side therefore approaches it too, so \(\Phi\) is differentiable and
    \[\Phi'(x) = b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(x, y_0) .\]
  6. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M y/I\) from theorem 5.35. Since the cross section is the same at every \(x\), \(A^*\), \(A\), \(I\) and \(S\) do not depend on \(x\), and
    \[\Phi(x) = \frac{N(x)}{A}\,A^* - \frac{M(x)}{I}\,S(y_0) , \qquad \Phi'(x) = \frac{N'}{A}\,A^* - \frac{M'}{I}\,S(y_0) .\]
  7. By corollary 5.11 \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(M' = -V\) (a vertical load on the surface has no component \(x\), so it gives no couple about the axis, \(m = 0\)). Therefore \(\Phi'(x) = V\,S(y_0)/I\).
  8. Steps 5 and 7 give \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = V S/I\), and since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) (step 4), this is equation (5.28).
Explanation

The sign follows from the convention, not from the source: \(M' = -V\) is a consequence of \(V\) being the force that the part at larger \(x\) exerts on the part at smaller \(x\) (section 5.2.3). Since \(S \ge 0\) for all \(y_0\) (section 5.6.1), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) has the sign of the force \(V\) in the whole cross section, so on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it points in the direction \(y\) just as \(V\) itself does. The assumption of uniformity across the width is the one that the beam model cannot derive. How well it holds we shall see in section 5.6.3.

Source: SADD (D.9) (PDF pp. 579-580), EP (2.5.19) (p. 76). The sign is derived from the convention of this book. SADD has \(V\) with the opposite sign (its \(V\) is \(-V_{\mathrm{y}}\) in ours), so its \(\tau = V_{\mathrm{SADD}}\,Q/(I t)\) has the opposite sign to our \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the same magnitude. EP has the axis \(z\) upwards and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\). The name after Jourawski: TIMO PDF pp. 149-150.

We check the equation on the cantilever. At \(V = -500\) N, \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) and \(b = 20\) mm the first moment of the part of the cross section above the axis (\(y_0 = 0\)) is \(S(0) = 20 \cdot 20 \cdot 10 = 4000\) mm\(^3 = 4\cdot 10^{-6}\) m\(^3\) (an area \(20 \times 20\) mm\(^2\) with its centroid 10 mm above the axis). By equation (5.28)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(0) = \frac{-500 \cdot 4\cdot 10^{-6}}{1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 0{,}02}\ \text{Pa} = -0{,}9375\ \text{MPa} .\]

The minus sign means that on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the shear stress points downwards. This is correct, since the part at larger \(x\) must act on the part at smaller \(x\) downwards to be in equilibrium with the force of \(500\) N downwards at the free end.

We call equation (5.28) Jourawski's formula. The syllabus of the course (item MK 9) calls it Grashof's equation (Grashof equation), but none of the sources of this book supports this name. Timoshenko ascribes the approximate elementary solution for shear stresses in beams to Jourawski (TIMO PDF pp. 149-150). His account of Grashof's book on elasticity and strength (TIMO PDF pp. 141-142) lists the bending and torsion of prismatic bars, the longitudinal tensile force in the bending of a beam with two pins, shells and plates, but it does not mention the equation for shear stress.

5.6.2 Rectangular cross section

How is the shear stress distributed over the height? Equation (5.28) depends on the height \(y_0\) only through \(S(y_0)\) and \(b(y_0)\), and for a rectangle \(b\) is constant. So we check how \(S(y_0)\) changes.

Corollary 5.40 Shear stress in a rectangular cross section (strižna napetost v pravokotnem prerezu)

For a rectangular cross section of width \(b\) and height \(h\) with the axis \(x\) through the centroid, under the conditions of theorem 5.39

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, y_0) = \frac{V(x)}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) = \frac{3V(x)}{2A}\Big(1 - \frac{4 y_0^2}{h^2}\Big) , \qquad |y_0| \le \frac h2 , \tag{5.30}\]

where \(A = b h\). The stress is a parabola over the height, zero at the top and bottom of the cross section, with its largest magnitude on the axis, \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, 0)| = 3|V|/(2A)\).

Proof

We must show the shape of the stress and its largest value. We compute \(S(y_0)\) and insert it into equation (5.28).

  1. The part of the cross section above \(y_0\) is a rectangle of width \(b\) and height \(h/2 - y_0\). By definition 5.38 and by Fubini's theorem (theorem 4.9)
    \[S(y_0) = \int_{\mathrm{y}_0}^{h/2} y\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac b2\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) .\]
  2. The width is \(b(y_0) = b\) and \(I = b h^3/12\) (lemma 5.27). Equation (5.28) gives
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{V}{I b}\cdot\frac b2\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) = \frac{V}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) ,\]
    which is the first form. The second follows, because \(\dfrac{1}{2I}\cdot\dfrac{h^2}{4} = \dfrac{6}{b h^3}\cdot\dfrac{h^2}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2 b h}\).
  3. The expression \(h^2/4 - y_0^2\) is a quadratic function, which is zero at \(y_0 = \pm h/2\) and largest at \(y_0 = 0\), where it equals \(h^2/4\). There \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}| = 3|V|/(2A)\).

Source: SADD (D.10), (D.11) (PDF p. 581), EP (2.5.20), (2.5.21) (p. 76). EP denotes the height of the cross section by \(2h\), so it has \(h^2 - z^2\) and \(3V/(4bh)\), where this book has \(h^2/4 - y_0^2\) and \(3V/(2bh)\). The proof is not from the source.

Why is the distribution a parabola? The first moment \(S\) is the area of the part above the cut, \(b\,(h/2 - y_0)\), times the height of its centroid, \((h/2 + y_0)/2\). Both are linear in \(y_0\), and the product of two linear functions is a quadratic function. The area is zero at the top, and at the bottom the centroid falls on the axis, so \(S\) is zero at both end points. Figure 5.40 shows the distribution.

The values for our cantilever (\(V = -500\) N, \(h = 40\) mm) by equation (5.30) at \(y_0 = 0\), \(5\), \(10\), \(15\) and \(20\) mm are \(-0{,}9375\), \(-0{,}8789\), \(-0{,}7031\), \(-0{,}4102\) and \(0\) MPa. Let us also check that this is a distribution of the shear force. By lemma 5.9 we must have \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = V\). The integral is

\[\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \frac{V}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y^2\Big)\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{V b}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^3}{4} - \frac{h^3}{12}\Big) = \frac{V b}{2I}\cdot\frac{h^3}{6} = V ,\]

since \(I = b h^3/12\). The force carried by the shear stress is indeed \(V\), as it must be.

How large is this stress compared with the bending stress? At the clamp \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|_{\max} = 93{,}75\) MPa (section 5.5.3) and \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}|_{\max} = 0{,}9375\) MPa, so exactly 1 %. This is no coincidence. For a cantilever with a force \(F\) at the end, at the clamp \(M = FL\) and \(V = F\), so

\[\frac{|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}|_{\max}}{|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|_{\max}} = \frac{3F/(2bh)}{6FL/(b h^2)} = \frac{h}{4L} , \tag{5.31}\]

at \(L/h = 25\) that is \(1/100\). Why is the ratio equal to the height divided by the length? The bending stress is proportional to the moment, and this to the lever arm \(L\), whereas the shear stress is proportional to the force alone and has no lever arm. A longer beam has the same \(V\) and a larger \(M\), so the shear stress relatively disappears. For the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 with load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m the largest shear stress at the support is \(1{,}875\) MPa (there \(V = 1\) kN, the moment is zero), the bending stress at mid-span is \(93{,}75\) MPa, the ratio is \(h/L = 0{,}02\), but it is reached at two different places.

A beam in the shape of the letter I has the same \(V\) distributed differently. Take the cross section of section 5.4.3, of height 200 mm, with flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm and a web \(180 \times 6\) mm, and \(V = 1\) kN. The section is a figure made of three rectangles, so \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) holds for it too (corollary 5.19). By equation (5.28) on the axis \(S = 100\cdot 10\cdot 95 + 6\cdot 90\cdot 45 = 119\,300\) mm\(^3\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 1000 \cdot 119\,300/(2{,}098\cdot 10^7 \cdot 6)\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 0{,}948\) MPa. At the edge of the web, where it meets the flange, \(S = 100\cdot 10\cdot 95 = 95\,000\) mm\(^3\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0{,}755\) MPa, and in the flange at the same \(S\) and \(b = 100\) mm only \(0{,}045\) MPa. The stress jumps, because at the same \(S\) the width \(b\) changes abruptly from 6 mm to 100 mm. The average in the web, \(V/(6 \cdot 180\ \text{mm}^2) = 0{,}926\) MPa, is close to the largest value \(0{,}948\) MPa, and the web carries \(95{,}4\) % of the shear force, which we obtain by integrating the stress over the web. This is why for I-beams we compute as if the web carried the shear force with uniform stress and the flanges carried the moment. Figure 5.41 shows the distribution.

Where are the longitudinal and the shear stress present at the same point? At the point of the cantilever at the clamp at height \(y = 10\) mm, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 46{,}875\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}703\) MPa, and there are no other components in the plane. The principal stresses are the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & 0\end{pmatrix}\) (definition 2.60), \(46{,}886\) and \(-0{,}011\) MPa, and the principal direction is rotated by \(0{,}86^\circ\) from the axis \(x\). The shear stress therefore hardly changes the principal stresses. On the axis, however, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}9375\) MPa, that is, pure shear, with principal stresses \(\pm 0{,}9375\) MPa in directions rotated by \(45^\circ\). The largest shear stress is on the axis and the largest bending stress at the edge of the cross section, and nowhere are both large at the same time.

5.6.3 How accurate the formula is

Equation (5.28) follows from equilibrium and from the assumption of uniformity across the width, and it contains the beam model with plane cross sections. How good is it? SADD has three cases.

In section 8.1 of SADD, in example 8.3 (PDF pp. 173-175), the plane stress state of a beam with uniform transverse load is computed, and the shear stresses given by elasticity are equal to those of equation (5.28). The longitudinal stresses differ, the difference at the outer fibres is \(w/5\), where \(w\) is the load per unit area of the upper face, and it does not depend on the dimensions of the beam. At a ratio of span to height of 4 the difference is about 1 %. In example 9.9 (PDF pp. 279-280) the rectangular cross section is treated in three dimensions. Besides the term of equation (5.28), elasticity gives a correction with the factor \(\nu/(1 + \nu)\). At \(\nu = 0\) the correction vanishes, and so it does (relative to the elementary term) for a very narrow or a very wide rectangle, where the elastic solution passes into the result of equation (5.28). For our cross section with half-width \(a = 10\) mm and half-height \(c = 20\) mm equation (9.9.19) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) gives a correction on the axis of \(-1{,}9\) % of the elementary value \(|V|\,c^2/(2I)\). We computed the sum in the equation on a computer, its terms fall quickly because of \(\operatorname{sech}(n\pi c/a)\). Equation (9.9.18) also tells how the correction changes across the width: at the side edge, \(x = \pm a\), it is \(+3{,}8\) % and on the axis \(-1{,}9\) %, so the assumption of uniformity across the width does not hold exactly, the difference is about 6 % of the elementary value. At \(\nu = 1/2\) SADD calls the differences sizeable (p. 280). In example 9.8 (PDF pp. 277-278) the cross section is a circle, and the differences are small according to SADD. For our beam at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the equation is therefore wrong by about 2 % on the axis and by about 4 % at the edge, and this is the price of the assumption of uniformity across the width.

The shear stress has one more consequence that must be stated. The deformations are now no longer zero. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.11, definition 3.9) the shear strain is \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\). On the axis of the cantilever \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75{,}38\ \text{GPa} = 1{,}24\cdot 10^{-5}\), which is 2,6 % of the longitudinal strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) at the edge at the clamp. The shear strain is a parabola over the height, at the edge it is zero. The plane cross sections that the beam assumes therefore do not stay plane: the cross section warps (figure 5.43). Let us derive by how much.

Derivation 5.41 Warping of the cross section of a cantilever (izbočenje prereza konzole)

Goal: find the displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}})\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) for a cantilever with a force \(F\) downwards at the end, whose stresses are those computed by theorem 5.35 and equation (5.28), and from it the warping of the cross section. We assume that the field lies in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) and that we obtain the displacements by integrating the strains. The fibres are in uniaxial stress (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), so Hooke's law (theorem 3.11, equation (3.12)) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). With this we give up assumption (b) of section 5.3.2 about transversals of equal length, because the transversals stretch because of the Poisson effect.

1
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{F (L - x)\,y}{E I} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{G} = -\frac{F}{2 G I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y^2\Big) .\]
Hint

From \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M y/I\) with \(M = -F(L - x)\) (section 5.2.2) and from theorem 3.11 with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The shear strain is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\) by definition 3.9 and by equation (5.30) with \(V = -F\).

2
\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{F y}{E I}\Big(L x - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big) + f(y) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{\nu F (L - x)\,y^2}{2 E I} + g(x) .\]
Hint

We integrate \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) over \(x\) and \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) over \(y\). The integration constants are functions of the other variables, \(f(y)\) and \(g(x)\).

3
\[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
Hint

The definition of the engineering shear strain, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (chapter 1).

4
\[\underbrace{g'(x) + \frac{F}{E I}\Big(L x - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big)}_{\text{only } \mathrm{x}} = -\underbrace{\Big[ f'(y) - \frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,y^2}{2 E I} + \frac{(1 + \nu)\,F h^2}{4 E I}\Big]}_{\text{only } \mathrm{y}} .\]
Explanation

We insert steps 1 and 2 into step 3 and collect the terms that depend only on \(x\) on one side and those that depend only on \(y\) on the other. Here we use \(1/G = 2(1 + \nu)/E\).

5
\[f(y) = \frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,y^3}{6 E I} - \Big[\frac{(1 + \nu)\,F h^2}{4 E I} + c_0\Big]\,y + c_1 .\]
Explanation

The left side is a function of \(x\) only and the right of \(y\) only, and they are equal for all \(x\) and \(y\), so each is a constant. We denote the left side by the constant \(c_0\), and the right side is then \(-c_0\). From the right side we obtain \(f'(y)\) and integrate. The constants \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) are determined by the clamp (how the beam is attached), and the whole linear part of \(f(y)\) only means a rotation of the cross section as a whole.

What warps is therefore the term \((2 + \nu)\,F y^3/(6 E I)\), which is not linear in \(y\). The linear part rotates the cross section, the cubic one bends it. All steps are possible, so the displacement field exists and the strains derived from it are compatible by the definition of compatible strains. This is the field that EP computes for a cantilever with a point force at the end (example 2.5.1).

Source: EP example 2.5.1 (pp. 78-80), field (p80-b9) in the axes of the book EP (\(x_3\) upwards). The derivation is in our axes and with signs by the convention of this book.

For our beam (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\)) the cubic term at the edge of the cross section (\(y = 20\) mm) is \(0{,}073\ \mu\text{m}\). The deviation of the cross section from the plane through its two edges (\(y = \pm 20\) mm) is largest at \(y = \pm h/(2\sqrt3) = \pm 11{,}5\) mm, \(0{,}028\ \mu\text{m}\). The displacement of the edge of the cross section at the free end due to rotation is \(0{,}239\) mm, so the cubic term at the edge is \(0{,}03\) % of this displacement. The flatness of the cross sections therefore holds very well, because the beam is long: the shear strain is proportional to \(V\), which does not depend on \(L\), whereas the longitudinal displacement due to rotation is proportional to \(L\). For a short and thick beam the ratio is different, which the Timoshenko beam takes into account by including the shear strain in the model (section 5.13).
