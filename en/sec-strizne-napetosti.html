Chapter 5 Beam theory (teorija nosilcev)

5.6 Shear stresses in bending

By lemma 5.9 the shear force \(V\) is the integral of the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) over the cross section, \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) (for a rectangle, and by corollary 5.19 for a figure made of rectangles). For the cantilever of section 5.1.3 with the force \(F = 500\) N downward at the free end, \(V = -500\) N in every cross section. Some shear stress must therefore exist in the cross section. The beam model, however, assumed that cross sections stay perpendicular to the axis, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) (section 5.3.2), and by Hooke's law (theorem 3.19) it follows that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2G\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). Hooke's law and the shear force contradict each other. We resolve this by not computing \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from the deformation but from equilibrium. In this section we answer the questions how the shear stress is distributed over the height of the cross section, how large it is and how good this computation is.

From history

Coulomb pointed out the shear stresses in a cantilever and wrote that they matter only in short beams (TIMO, PDF p. 149). Young wrote that solid bodies differ from liquids in resisting shear. Vicat gave many cases in which this resistance is decisive and criticized the theory of bending because it ignores shear stresses (TIMO, PDF p. 149). The first edition of Navier's book in 1826 says nothing about them. The second edition in 1833 has an article on the bending of short beams, in which the shear stress at the built-in end is taken as an average over the cross section, and the way to combine it with the longitudinal bending stresses is unsatisfactory (TIMO, PDF p. 149). The exact solution was given by Saint-Venant in his memoir on bending, but only for a few of the simplest cross-section shapes. For more complicated shapes engineers had to use the approximate, simple solution presented by D. J. Jourawski (1821 to 1891) (TIMO, PDF p. 149).

He developed the theory of shear stresses in rectangular beams while designing wooden bridges for the railway between St. Petersburg and Moscow between 1844 and 1850, and presented it in 1854 to the Russian Academy of Sciences, which awarded him the Demidov prize for the work (TIMO, PDF p. 149, footnote). He graduated in 1842 from the Institute of Engineers of Ways of Communication in St. Petersburg and immediately got work on building this railway (TIMO, PDF p. 149-150). In 1844 he was given the design and construction of a bridge over the river Werebia (spelling as in the source), which according to the source had nine spans, a length of 180 feet and a height of 170 feet above the water (the source does not say whether this is the length of the bridge or of one span) (TIMO, PDF p. 150). For this bridge he had to use wooden beams of great depth and built-up wooden beams. Wood is very weak in shear along the fibres, so Jourawski correctly concluded that shear stresses in such beams must not be neglected (TIMO, PDF p. 150). The literature had nothing on calculating them, so he had to work them out himself (TIMO, PDF p. 150).

He started with the simplest case, a rectangular cantilever with a load at the free end. He reasoned that the normal stresses across the cross section at the built-in end tend to cause shear in the neutral plane \(00\) (TIMO, PDF p. 150). The shear force \(T\) in this plane equals the resultant of the normal stresses of the upper half of the cross section at the built-in end. The normal stress is largest at the edge, \(\sigma_{\max} = 6 Q l/(b h^2)\), and falls to zero toward the neutral plane. The resultant in the upper half is therefore \(\tfrac12\sigma_{\max}\cdot\tfrac12 b h = 3 Q l/(2h)\), where \(Q\) is the load, \(l\) the length of the bar, \(b\) the width and \(h\) the depth of the cross section. The plane \(00\) has area \(l b\), so the shear stress is \(T/(l b) = 3 Q/(2 b h)\) (TIMO, PDF p. 151). He calculated the stresses in any horizontal plane \(ss\) above the plane \(00\) in a similar way (TIMO, PDF p. 151). For a uniformly distributed load he showed that the shear stress along the horizontal plane is no longer constant but grows with the distance from the free end (TIMO, PDF p. 151). With the same idea he calculated the forces on each connecting element of built-up wooden beams and from them the required dimensions of the elements (TIMO, PDF p. 151). He also calculated the rivet spacing in built-up iron beams and in the Conway and Britannia tubular bridges. He showed that the number of rivets could be reduced considerably, because the transverse force on the tube falls from the ends toward the middle of the span, and the rivet spacing in the middle can therefore be larger (TIMO, PDF p. 151). Saint-Venant praised his approximate method and adapted it to a rectangular beam whose depth is much larger than its width (TIMO, PDF p. 151).

Let us return to the question of the section: how the shear force \(V\) is distributed over the cross section as shear stress. Jourawski's reasoning, the equilibrium of the part of the beam above a horizontal cut, we shall now derive for an arbitrary cross section. For a rectangular cross section our result must give in the neutral plane the same value as Jourawski's, \(3 Q/(2 b h)\) for a load \(Q = |V|\), and we shall indeed obtain it in section 5.6.2.

5.6.1 Equilibrium of part of a slice

Let us first look at the simplest case in which shear stress appears: two boards laid one on top of the other (figure 5.94).

Take two equal steel boards with a \(20 \times 20\) mm cross section, 1 m long, lay them one on top of the other and clamp them in a wall at one end, like the cantilever of section 5.1.3. As they are before loading, we draw them grey and dashed. On the free end we press down with a force \(F = 500\) N. If the boards are not glued (we neglect the friction between them), each bends on its own, and we draw both bent boards in orange. At the free end they are offset: the lower board slides \(0{,}96\) mm further out than the upper one, as the cards of a deck slide (figure 1.12). Why the lower one? When a cantilever bends downwards, the upper fibres of each board are stretched and the lower ones shortened (section 5.5.3). At the joint, then, the bottom of the upper board gets shorter and the top of the lower one longer, and the difference adds up from the wall to the end.

If we glue the boards, clamp them the same way and load them with the same force, they bend as one beam and there is no sliding. The glue holds the two faces of the joint together and gives each a force along the axis \(x\), the upper board towards the free end, the lower one towards the wall. This force per unit area of the joint is a stress that lies in the horizontal plane of the joint and points along the axis \(x\), that is, a shear stress. The glued and the loose beam also do not bend alike. The loose boards deflect by \(31{,}9\) mm, the glued ones only by \(8{,}0\) mm, four times less (how the deflection of a cantilever is computed we shall show in section 5.9.2). The reason is the second moment of area, which for a rectangle is \(I = b h^3/12\) (lemma 5.27). The glued \(20 \times 40\) mm cross section has \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\), the two loose \(20 \times 20\) mm squares together \(2{,}667\cdot 10^{-8}\) m\(^4\). Why four times? Gluing doubles the height, so \(h^3\) grows eightfold, while there are only two boards. At the same curvature the glued beam therefore carries a moment four times as large (\(M = EI\,v''\), section 5.5.3). A beam made of one piece is like a glued one in every horizontal plane, since the material does not slide there, and so there is a shear stress in every such plane, like the one the glue carries.

How large this stress is we compute by cutting the beam in thought also along a horizontal plane. Take a straight prismatic beam (the cross section does not change along the axis) whose cross section has the axis \(y\) as an axis of symmetry, with the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections and with the load in the plane \(x\)-\(y\), as in the previous sections. We use the assumptions of theorem 5.35. Equilibrium of a piece of the beam tells us how large the stress must be (figure 5.95).

From the beam we cut out a slice of width \(\Delta x\) between the cross sections at \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). We cut it once more with a horizontal plane at height \(y_0\), which we draw as a grey dashed cut. We consider only the part of the slice above the cut, which we call \(P\). The rest of the beam below it and the neutral axis \(y = 0\) we only sketch, grey and dashed, and the axes \(x\) and \(y\) are as before. On the left and right face of the part \(P\) the longitudinal stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) act. We draw them as arrows that are the longer the further they are from the neutral axis, because by theorem 5.35 the stress grows linearly with the distance from it. Why are the two faces loaded differently? The moment changes along the beam because the shear force is not zero (\(M' = -V\), corollary 5.11). In the cantilever it falls in magnitude from \(500\) N m at the wall to zero at the free end (figure 5.27). So the arrows on the left are longer than the arrows on the right, and their difference, which nothing balances, pulls the part \(P\) towards the wall. It is balanced by the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) on the cut, which lies in the plane of the cut and is therefore drawn just below it.

The sum of the forces in the direction \(x\) on the part \(P\) could also contain a load. Let us assume that the load on the surface and the body force have no component in the direction \(x\), so \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), and the upper face of the part \(P\) is not loaded in the direction \(x\). Why may we? The loads of our beams, the force at the end of the cantilever, the uniform load of the simply supported beam and the weight of a horizontal beam, all point vertically. When does it fail? A wheel braking on a bridge pulls the upper face along the axis by friction. What would that change? The same equilibrium as in the proof below, together with \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}}\) and with the couple \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\,y_{\max}\) that the friction on the upper edge \(y_{\max}\) gives about the axis (corollary 5.11), then gives

\[b\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{V S}{I} + q_{\mathrm{x}}\Big(1 - \frac{A^*}{A} - y_{\max}\,\frac{S}{I}\Big) .\]

At the upper edge the extra term equals \(q_{\mathrm{x}}\) (there the shear stress is just the friction per unit area), on the axis of a rectangular cross section it is \(-q_{\mathrm{x}}/4\), so relative to the main term \(q_{\mathrm{x}} h/(6|V|)\): at \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}1\) kN/m, a tenth of the load of the simply supported beam, at the support with \(|V| = 1\) kN only \(0{,}07\,\%\).

For the computation we need the first moment of the part of the cross section above the cut. Why the first moment? By theorem 5.35 the longitudinal stress is, apart from the constant part \(N/A\), proportional to the height \(y\), so the force that the stresses give to the left face of the part \(P\) is a sum of the heights \(y\) over the part of the cross section above the cut, each weighted by its area. We shall use it in the examples too, so we introduce it with a definition. The first moment of the whole cross section about an axis through the centroid is zero (lemma 5.22), but the first moment of a part of the cross section is not (figure 5.96).

Let us draw the cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm of our beams with the axis \(z\) through the centroid and the axis \(y\) upwards. At height \(y_0\) we cut it with a horizontal line. We colour the part above it blue and denote it by \(A^*\), its height \(h/2 - y_0\), and the width of the cross section at the cut by \(b\).

Definition 5.38 First moment of a partial area (statični moment dela prereza)

Let \(A\) be the cross section of a beam (a figure in the plane \(y\)-\(z\)) with the axis \(z\) through the centroid, and let \(y_0\) be the height at which we cut the cross section by a horizontal line. Denote by \(A^*(y_0)\) the part of the cross section above this line, that is, the points of the cross section with \(y \ge y_0\). The first moment of a partial area (statični moment dela prereza) is the number

\[S(y_0) = \int_{\mathrm{A}^*(\mathrm{y}_0)} y\,\mathrm{d}A , \tag{5.27}\]

with unit m\(^3\). The width of the cross section \(b(y_0)\) is the total length of that part of the line \(y = y_0\) which lies in the cross section.

Source: SADD (D.9) and the text before it (PDF p. 580), EP (2.5.19), (2.5.20) (p. 76). Both books write this number \(Q\), in this book it is \(S\) as in section 5.4.2.

The first moment of a partial area is the product of the area of the part and the height of its centroid, \(S(y_0) = A^*\,y_{\mathrm{T}}^*\), where \(y_{\mathrm{T}}^*\) is the coordinate \(y\) of the centroid of the part \(A^*\) (definition 5.21). For the cut drawn at \(y_0 = 8\) mm, \(A^* = 20 \cdot 12 = 240\) mm\(^2\) and \(y_{\mathrm{T}}^* = 14\) mm, so \(S = 3360\) mm\(^3\). How does \(S\) change with \(y_0\)? This is shown by the graph on the right, where \(y_0\) is vertical at the same heights as the cross section. For \(y_0\) at the top of the cross section the part is empty and \(S = 0\). For \(y_0\) at the bottom \(A^*\) is the whole cross section and again \(S = 0\), because the whole cross section has first moment zero: above the axis the heights are positive, below it negative, and since the axis goes through the centroid, the contributions cancel exactly. By Fubini \(S(y_0) = \int_{\mathrm{y}_0}^{y_{\max}} y\,b(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\), where \(y_{\max}\) is the top edge of the cross section, since the area of a strip between \(y\) and \(y + \Delta y\) is for continuous \(b\) approximately \(b(y)\,\Delta y\). Where \(b\) is continuous, the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, point 2) gives \(\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}y_0 = -y_0\,b(y_0)\), and where \(b\) jumps (the letter T or I) the derivative is one-sided. Below the axis \(y_0 < 0\) and \(S\) grows, above the axis it falls, as the curve shows. Therefore \(S \ge 0\) for all \(y_0\) and it is largest at the axis, \(y_0 = 0\), for our cross section \(4000\) mm\(^3\).

Equilibrium of a part of the slice gives us only the total force across the cut, that is, the integral of the shear stress over the width of the cut. We obtain the stress from it only with an additional assumption, that it is uniform across the width of the cut \(b(y_0)\), and we use it only to replace the integral by the product \(b\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\). Why is it reasonable? For a cross section much narrower than it is high, the elastic solution approaches a uniform stress (SADD, PDF p. 280). When does it fail? Near a free side face the stress changes, and the more so the wider the cross section is compared with its height. What does it cost? By the solution SADD (9.9.18) (PDF p. 280), for our \(20 \times 40\) mm cross section and \(\nu = 0{,}3\) the stress on the axis is \(3{,}8\,\%\) larger at the side edge and \(1{,}9\,\%\) smaller in the middle of the width than the uniform one, and for a square cross section already \(14{,}6\,\%\) larger at the edge. Section 5.6.3 treats this in more detail.

Theorem 5.39 Jourawski's formula for shear stress (Jourawskijeva enačba za strižno napetost)

Let the beam be straight and prismatic with a plane of symmetry \(x\)-\(y\), the axis \(x\) through the centroids of the cross sections and the load in this plane. Let the assumptions of theorem 5.35 hold. The load on the surface and the body force have no component in the direction \(x\), so \(q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\). Let there be no point forces and couples on the interval considered and let the load \(q\) be continuous (then \(N\) and \(M\) are differentiable, corollary 5.11), and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be continuous in \(x\). Let \(b(y_0) > 0\) be the width of the cross section at height \(y_0\) and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) be uniform across the width along this cut. Then at every point of the cross section at height \(y_0\)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, y_0) = \frac{V(x)\,S(y_0)}{I\,b(y_0)} , \tag{5.28}\]

where \(S(y_0)\) is the first moment of the part of the cross section above \(y_0\) (definition 5.38), \(I\) is the second moment of area (definition 5.26) and the signs hold by the convention by which \(V\) and \(M\) mean the force and the moment on the face with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) (definition 5.7).

Idea

The part of the slice above the cut is in equilibrium. The longitudinal stresses on its two faces, the left and the right, differ, because the moment differs, and the difference must be balanced by the shear stress on the cut. We know the stresses on the faces from theorem 5.35, so from the difference we obtain the stress on the cut.

Proof

We must show that the shear stress on the cut equals \(V S/(I b)\). We write the equilibrium of forces in the direction \(x\) for the part of the slice above the cut and divide it by \(\Delta x\).

  1. Let \(P\) be the part of the slice between the cross sections \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) above the plane \(y = y_0\). It is a part of a body in equilibrium, so by definition 2.3 the sum of the forces on it is zero. The force across a face is the stress vector times the area, and by Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) we obtain the stress vector from the stress components. For a face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the component \(x\) of the stress vector is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and for a face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) it is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) (definition 2.27).
  2. The faces of the part \(P\) are: the right face at \(x + \Delta x\) with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the left face at \(x\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the cut at \(y_0\) with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), because it points out of the part \(P\), which lies above it, downwards, the upper face of the beam and the two side faces, which are the planes of the drawing in front and behind. By assumption the load on the surface and the body force have no component \(x\), so these faces and the volume of the part \(P\) contribute nothing in the direction \(x\).
  3. The sum of the components \(x\) is therefore
    \[\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x + \Delta x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A - \int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A \\ &\quad - \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x + \Delta x}\!\int \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0, z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.29}\]
    The first term is the force of the right face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the second the force of the left face with normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the third, the force of the cut, has a minus sign because of the normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), and the inner integral runs over the width of the cut.
  4. By the symmetry of stress (theorem 2.41) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), and by assumption this is uniform across the width. The inner integral is therefore \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0)\). Denote \(\Phi(x) = \int_{\mathrm{A}^*} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x, y, z)\,\mathrm{d}A\), the sum of the longitudinal stresses over the part of the cross section \(A^*\) at \(x\), which the right face feels at \(x + \Delta x\) with a plus sign and the left face at \(x\) with a minus sign. Equation (5.29) then says
    \[\Phi(x + \Delta x) - \Phi(x) = b(y_0)\int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x + \Delta x} \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(\xi, y_0)\,\mathrm{d}\xi .\]
  5. We divide by \(\Delta x\) and let \(\Delta x \to 0\). The right-hand side divided by \(\Delta x\) is \(b(y_0)\) times the mean of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) over the band from \(x\) to \(x + \Delta x\). As the band narrows, by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, part 2) the mean approaches the value \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(x, y_0)\), because by assumption \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) is continuous in \(x\). So the right-hand side approaches \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(x, y_0)\). The left-hand side divided by \(\Delta x\) is the slope of the secant of the graph of \(\Phi\) between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). The left-hand side equals the right-hand side, so the slope of the narrower secants approaches the same value. Therefore the graph of \(\Phi\) has a tangent at \(x\), \(\Phi\) is differentiable and
    \[\Phi'(x) = b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}(x, y_0) .\]
  6. We insert \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M y/I\) from theorem 5.35. The stress on the part \(A^*\) is the sum of a uniform part \(N/A\) and a linear part \(-M y/I\), so \(\Phi\) is a sum of two forces too. The equals and plus signs say so. The rest of the face below the cut does not contribute to \(\Phi\). The uniform part gives \(N/A\) times the area \(A^*\). The linear part gives \(-M/I\) times \(\int_{\mathrm{A}^*} y\,\mathrm{d}A\), and this integral is \(S(y_0)\) (definition 5.38). Since the cross section is the same at every \(x\), \(A^*\), \(A\), \(I\) and \(S\) do not depend on \(x\), and
    \[\Phi(x) = \frac{N(x)}{A}\,A^* - \frac{M(x)}{I}\,S(y_0) , \qquad \Phi'(x) = \frac{N'}{A}\,A^* - \frac{M'}{I}\,S(y_0) .\]
  7. By corollary 5.11 \(N' = -q_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\) and \(M' = -V\) (a vertical load on the surface has no component \(x\), so it gives no couple about the axis, \(m = 0\)). Therefore \(\Phi'(x) = V\,S(y_0)/I\).
  8. Steps 5 and 7 give \(b(y_0)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = V S/I\), and since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) (step 4), this is equation (5.28).
Explanation

The sign follows from the convention, not from the source: \(M' = -V\) is a consequence of \(V\) being the force that the part at larger \(x\) exerts on the part at smaller \(x\) (section 5.2.3, force of the cross section on the cut-off part of the cantilever). Since \(S \ge 0\) for all \(y_0\) (section 5.6.1), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) has the sign of the force \(V\) in the whole cross section, so on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it points in the direction \(y\) just as \(V\) itself does. The assumption of uniformity across the width is the one that the beam model cannot derive. How well it holds we shall see in section 5.6.3.

Source: SADD (D.9) (PDF pp. 579-580), EP (2.5.19) (p. 76). The sign is derived from the convention of this book. SADD has \(V\) with the opposite sign (its \(V\) is \(-V_{\mathrm{y}}\) in ours), so its \(\tau = V_{\mathrm{SADD}}\,Q/(I t)\) has the opposite sign to our \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and the same magnitude. EP has the axis \(z\) upwards and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\). The name after Jourawski: TIMO PDF pp. 149-150.

We check the equation on the cantilever. The force of \(500\) N downwards at the free end gives \(V = -500\) N along the whole length (section 5.2.2). At \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) and \(b = 20\) mm the first moment of the part of the cross section above the axis (\(y_0 = 0\)) is \(S(0) = 20 \cdot 20 \cdot 10 = 4000\) mm\(^3\) \(= 4\cdot 10^{-6}\) m\(^3\) (an area \(20 \times 20\) mm\(^2\) with its centroid 10 mm above the axis). By equation (5.28)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(0) = \frac{-500 \cdot 4\cdot 10^{-6}}{1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 0{,}02}\ \text{Pa} = -0{,}9375\ \text{MPa} .\]

The minus sign means that on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) the shear stress points downwards. Is that right? The cut-off part at larger \(x\) receives from the part at smaller \(x\) an upward force of the cross section, which balances the force of \(500\) N downwards at the end. By Newton's third law the part at larger \(x\) therefore acts on the part at smaller \(x\) downwards, and this is exactly the force on the face with normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).

We call equation (5.28) Jourawski's formula. The syllabus of the course (item MK 9) calls it Grashof's equation (Grashof equation), but none of the sources of this book supports this name. Timoshenko ascribes the approximate elementary solution for shear stresses in beams to Jourawski (TIMO PDF pp. 149-150). His account of Grashof's book on elasticity and strength (TIMO PDF pp. 141-142) lists the bending and torsion of prismatic bars, the longitudinal tensile force in the bending of a beam with two pins, shells and plates, but it does not mention the equation for shear stress.

At a point where the normal stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) from bending and the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) from this equation act together, the yield condition is equation (2.133), \(\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2 = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}^2\) with \(\sigma = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\tau = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).

5.6.2 Rectangular cross section

How is the shear stress distributed over the height? Equation (5.28) depends on the height \(y_0\) only through \(S(y_0)\) and \(b(y_0)\), and for a rectangle \(b\) is constant. The stress therefore changes like \(S(y_0)\).

Corollary 5.40 Shear stress in a rectangular cross section (strižna napetost v pravokotnem prerezu)

For a rectangular cross section of width \(b\) and height \(h\) with the axis \(x\) through the centroid, under the conditions of theorem 5.39

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, y_0) = \frac{V(x)}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) = \frac{3V(x)}{2A}\Big(1 - \frac{4 y_0^2}{h^2}\Big) , \qquad |y_0| \le \frac h2 , \tag{5.30}\]

where \(A = b h\). The stress is a parabola over the height, zero at the top and bottom of the cross section, with its largest magnitude on the axis, \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(x, 0)| = 3|V|/(2A)\).

Proof

We must show the shape of the stress and its largest value. We compute \(S(y_0)\) and insert it into equation (5.28).

  1. The part of the cross section above \(y_0\) is a rectangle of width \(b\) and height \(h/2 - y_0\). By definition 5.38 and by Fubini's theorem (theorem 4.9)
    \[S(y_0) = \int_{\mathrm{y}_0}^{h/2} y\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac b2\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) .\]
  2. The width is \(b(y_0) = b\) and \(I = b h^3/12\) (lemma 5.27). Equation (5.28) gives
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{V}{I b}\cdot\frac b2\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) = \frac{V}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y_0^2\Big) ,\]
    which is the first form. The second follows, because \(\dfrac{1}{2I}\cdot\dfrac{h^2}{4} = \dfrac{6}{b h^3}\cdot\dfrac{h^2}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2 b h}\).
  3. The expression \(h^2/4 - y_0^2\) is a quadratic function, which is zero at \(y_0 = \pm h/2\) (top and bottom) and largest at \(y_0 = 0\), where it equals \(h^2/4\). There \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}| = 3|V|/(2A)\).

Source: SADD (D.10), (D.11) (PDF p. 581), EP (2.5.20), (2.5.21) (p. 76). EP denotes the height of the cross section by \(2h\), so it has \(h^2 - z^2\) and \(3V/(4bh)\), where this book has \(h^2/4 - y_0^2\) and \(3V/(2bh)\). The proof is not from the source.

Why is the distribution a parabola? The first moment \(S\) is the area of the part above the cut, \(b\,(h/2 - y_0)\) with the height of the part \(h/2 - y_0\), times the height of its centroid, \((h/2 + y_0)/2\), which is halfway between the cut and the top. Both are linear in \(y_0\), and the product of two linear functions is a quadratic function. The area is zero at the top, and at the bottom the centroid falls on the axis, so \(S\) is zero at both end points, at the top and at the bottom. Figure 5.99 shows the distribution of the stress.

Next to the cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm we draw on the right a vertical axis of the graph, on which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\). We carry the heights from the cross section over to the graph horizontally, as the line through the axis of the cross section shows. For every height \(y_0\) we mark the value of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) by equation (5.30) for our cantilever (\(V = -500\) N, \(h = 40\) mm), to the left of the axis, because it is negative, and we obtain the parabola. At \(y_0 = 0\), \(5\), \(10\) and \(15\) mm the values are \(-0{,}9375\), \(-0{,}8789\), \(-0{,}7031\) and \(-0{,}4102\) MPa, and at \(20\) mm, at the top of the cross section, the parabola touches the axis, \(0\) MPa.

Let us also check that this is a distribution of the shear force. By lemma 5.9 we must have \(\int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = V\), that is, the area between the parabola and the axis, times the width \(b\), must equal \(V\). The integral is

\[\int_{-\mathrm{h}/2}^{h/2} \frac{V}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y^2\Big)\,b\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{V b}{2I}\Big(\frac{h^3}{4} - \frac{h^3}{12}\Big) = \frac{V b}{2I}\cdot\frac{h^3}{6} = V ,\]

since \(I = b h^3/12\). The force carried by the shear stress is indeed \(V\), as it must be.

How large is this stress compared with the bending stress? Next to the cross section at the clamp of the cantilever we draw both in the same scale, the bending stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) on the left and the shear stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) on the right, with the scale of \(20\) MPa below. At the clamp \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|_{\max} = 93{,}75\) MPa (section 5.5.3) and \(|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}|_{\max} = 0{,}9375\) MPa, so exactly 1 %, which is why the parabola is barely visible in this scale. This is no coincidence. For a cantilever with a force \(F\) at the end, at the clamp in magnitude \(M = FL\) and \(V = F\), so

\[\frac{|\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}|_{\max}}{|\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}|_{\max}} = \frac{3F/(2bh)}{6FL/(b h^2)} = \frac{h}{4L} , \tag{5.31}\]

at \(L/h = 25\) that is \(1/100\). Why is the ratio proportional to the height divided by the length? The bending stress is proportional to the moment, and this to the lever arm \(L\) of the force \(F\), whereas the shear stress is proportional to the force alone and has no lever arm. A longer beam has the same \(V\) and a larger \(M\), so the shear stress relatively disappears. For the simply supported beam of section 5.1.3 with load \(q_0 = 1\) kN/m the largest shear stress at the support is \(1{,}875\) MPa (there \(V = 1\) kN, the moment is zero), the bending stress at mid-span is \(93{,}75\) MPa (there the moment is largest, \(500\) N m), the ratio is \(h/L = 0{,}02\), but it is reached at two different places.

Where are the longitudinal and the shear stress present at the same point? Take the point of the cantilever at the clamp at height \(y = 10\) mm. There \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 46{,}875\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}703\) MPa, and there are no other components in the plane. A small square of material around this point feels a large tension on its vertical faces and a small shear on all four. The principal stresses are the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} & \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} & 0\end{pmatrix}\) (definition 2.82), \(46{,}886\) and \(-0{,}011\) MPa, and the principal direction is rotated by \(0{,}86^\circ\) clockwise from the axis \(x\). The shear stress therefore hardly changes the principal stresses. On the axis, however, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -0{,}9375\) MPa, that is, pure shear, with principal stresses \(\pm 0{,}9375\) MPa in directions rotated by \(45^\circ\): the material on the axis is stretched along one diagonal and compressed along the other. The largest shear stress is on the axis, where the bending stress is zero, and the largest bending stress at the edge of the cross section, where the shear stress is zero, so for a rectangular cross section the two largest values never occur at the same point.

A beam in the shape of the letter I has the same \(V\) distributed differently. Take the cross section of section 5.4.3, of height 200 mm, with flanges \(100 \times 10\) mm and a web \(180 \times 6\) mm, and \(V = 1\) kN. The section is a figure made of three rectangles, so \(V = \int_{\mathrm{A}} \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A\) holds for it too (corollary 5.19). Next to the cross section we draw the graph with the axis \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), and the line through the axis of the cross section carries the heights over. For a cut on the axis the part above it is the upper half of the cross section: the flange with its centroid \(95\) mm above the axis and the half of the web with its centroid \(45\) mm above the axis. By equation (5.28) on the axis therefore \(S = 100\cdot 10\cdot 95 + 6\cdot 90\cdot 45 = 119\,300\) mm\(^3\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 1000 \cdot 119\,300/(2{,}098\cdot 10^7 \cdot 6)\ \text{N/mm}^2 = 0{,}948\) MPa. At the edge of the web, where it meets the flange, \(S = 100\cdot 10\cdot 95 = 95\,000\) mm\(^3\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0{,}755\) MPa, and in the flange at the same \(S\) and \(b = 100\) mm only \(0{,}045\) MPa. This is only the average over the width \(b = 100\) mm: theorem 5.39 does not give the stress at a point of the flange, because uniformity across the width does not hold there (the underside of the flange beside the web is a free surface, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}} = 0\)). The stress jumps, because at the same \(S\) the width \(b\) changes abruptly from 6 mm to 100 mm. The stress the web would have if it carried the whole shear force, \(V/(6 \cdot 180\ \text{mm}^2) = 0{,}926\) MPa, is only \(2{,}3\) % below the largest value \(0{,}948\) MPa. The web carries \(95{,}4\) % of the shear force, which we obtain by integrating the stress over the web, so the actual average in the web is \(0{,}883\) MPa. This is why for I-beams we compute as if the web carried the shear force with uniform stress and the flanges carried the moment, because they are furthest from the axis, where the longitudinal stress is largest (figure 5.89).

5.6.3 How accurate the formula is

Equation (5.28) follows from equilibrium and from the assumption of uniformity across the width, and it contains the beam model with plane cross sections. How good is it? SADD has three cases.

In section 8.1 of SADD, in example 8.3 (PDF pp. 173-175), the plane stress state of a beam with uniform transverse load is computed, and the shear stresses given by elasticity are equal to those of equation (5.28). On the right, the vertical axis is the height \(y\) and the horizontal one the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The longitudinal stresses differ over the height, the difference at the outer fibres is \(w/5\), where \(w\) is the load per unit area of the upper face, and it does not depend on the dimensions of the beam. At a ratio of span to height of 4 the difference is about \(1{,}7\,\%\) (SADD, PDF p. 175, writes about 1 %: \(w/5\) against the elementary maximum \(3 w l^2/(4 c^2) = 12 w\) gives \(1/60\)).

In example 9.9 (PDF pp. 279-280) the rectangular cross section is treated in three dimensions. Besides the term of equation (5.28), elasticity gives a correction with the factor \(\nu/(1 + \nu)\). On the horizontal axis is \(a/c\), on the vertical the correction in percent. At \(\nu = 0\) the correction vanishes, and so it does (relative to the elementary term) for a very narrow rectangle, where the width is much smaller than the height and the elastic solution passes into the result of equation (5.28). For a wide rectangle the relative correction grows: by (9.9.18) at the edge for \(\nu = 0{,}3\) it equals \(+3{,}8\,\%\) for \(a/c = 0{,}5\), \(+14{,}6\,\%\) for \(a/c = 1\) and \(+46\,\%\) for \(a/c = 2\). The sentence of SADD on p. 280 that the result is similar also for \(a \gg b\) is therefore wrong. At \(\nu = 1/2\) SADD calls the differences sizeable (p. 280). In example 9.8 (PDF pp. 277-278) the cross section is a circle, and the differences are small according to SADD.

Let us look at example 9.9 on our cross section (figure 5.104). On the left is the cross section \(20 \times 40\) mm with the axes \(y\) and \(z\), the half-width is \(a = 10\) mm and the half-height \(c = 20\) mm. We watch the shear stress along the neutral axis, from one side edge to the other. On the right, above the axis \(z\), is drawn what the two computations say about it. Equation (5.28) assumes the same value \(|V|\,c^2/(2I)\) everywhere. Equation (9.9.18) of SADD gives at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) an arc: on the axis of the cross section the stress is \(1{,}9\) % smaller, which equation (9.9.19) gives too, and at the side edges it is \(3{,}8\) % larger (the side edges are at \(z = \pm a\), in the axes of SADD at \(x = \pm a\)). We computed the sum in the equation on a computer, its terms fall quickly because of \(\operatorname{sech}(n\pi c/a)\). The correction is proportional to \(\nu/(1 + \nu)\): it is brought by the lateral contraction that Poisson's ratio measures, so at \(\nu = 0\) it is absent.

So the assumption of uniformity across the width does not hold exactly, the difference between the edge and the axis is about 6 % of the elementary value. For our beam at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) equation (5.28) is wrong by about 2 % on the axis and by about 4 % at the edge, and this is the price of that assumption. It is usable for cross sections narrower than their height (for \(a/c \le 0{,}5\) the error at the edge is at most about 4 %), and no longer for wide ones (for \(a/c = 2\) it is \(+46\) %).

The shear stress has one more consequence that must be stated. The deformations are now no longer zero. By Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, definition 3.17) the shear strain is \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\). On the axis of the cantilever its magnitude is \(\gamma_0 = 0{,}9375\ \text{MPa}/75{,}38\ \text{GPa} = 1{,}24\cdot 10^{-5}\), which is 2,6 % of the longitudinal strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = 4{,}78\cdot 10^{-4}\) at the edge at the clamp. The shear strain is a parabola over the height, at the edge it is zero. The plane cross sections that the beam assumes therefore do not stay plane: the cross section warps. Why? The shear strain is the change of the right angle between a fibre and the cross section (chapter 1). On the axis this angle changes by \(\gamma_0\), at the upper and the lower edge by nothing, so the cross section meets the fibres on the axis at another angle than the fibres at the edge. A straight line meets parallel fibres at the same angle everywhere, so the cross section cannot stay straight. Let us derive by how much it bends.

In the middle of figure 5.105 is the piece of the cantilever with the axes \(x\) and \(y\) and the axis of the beam. Its right cross section is, as the model assumes it, straight. The shear strain turns it into the orange curve, which we derive below. The grey line passes through its two edges, and the offset of the curve from this line is the warping. On the right is the magnitude of the shear strain over the height above the graph axis, with the largest value \(\gamma_0\) on the axis. On the left, for comparison, is the cross section at the free end, before the load and rotated, as the model with plane cross sections gives it.

Derivation 5.41 Warping of the cross section of a cantilever (izbočenje prereza konzole)

Goal: find the displacement field \((u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}})\) in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) for a cantilever with a force \(F\) downwards at the end, whose stresses are those computed by theorem 5.35 and equation (5.28), and from it the warping of the cross section. We assume that the field lies in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) and that we obtain the displacements by integrating the strains. The normal stresses across the fibres are zero (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)), so Hooke's law (theorem 3.19, equation (3.27)) gives \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). This is justified here because there is no load on the upper and the lower face of the cantilever (the force acts only at the end). With a load \(w\) on the upper face \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) is of the size of \(w\), as in example 8.3 above. With this we give up assumption (b) of section 5.3.2 about transversals of equal length, because the transversals stretch because of the Poisson effect. In the result this changes \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) (the term with \(\nu\) in step 2) and the coefficient of the warping from \(2\) to \(2 + \nu\), at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) by 15 %.

1
\[\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \frac{F (L - x)\,y}{E I} , \qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} , \qquad \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{G} = -\frac{F}{2 G I}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4} - y^2\Big) .\]
Hint

From \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -M y/I\) with \(M = -F(L - x)\) (section 5.2.2) and from theorem 3.19 with \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) we have \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\). The shear strain is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/G\) by definition 3.17 and by equation (5.30) with \(V = -F\), its magnitude is the parabola on the right of the figure.

2
\[u_{\mathrm{x}} = \frac{F y}{E I}\Big(L x - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big) + f(y) , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}} = -\frac{\nu F (L - x)\,y^2}{2 E I} + g(x) .\]
Hint

In the beam on the left we integrate \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) over \(x\), along each horizontal line \(y = \text{const.}\) separately. Each line starts with its own value \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(0, y)\), so the integration constant is not one number but a function \(f(y)\), the shape of the cross section at \(x = 0\). In the beam on the right, in the same way we integrate \(\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial y = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\) over \(y\) along vertical lines, which start on the axis with the values \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, 0) = g(x)\), that is, with the deflection of the axis.

3
\[\frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x} = \gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} .\]
Hint

The definition of the engineering shear strain, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) (chapter 1).

4
\[\underbrace{g'(x) + \frac{F}{E I}\Big(L x - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big)}_{\text{only } \mathrm{x}} = -\underbrace{\Big[ f'(y) - \frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,y^2}{2 E I} + \frac{(1 + \nu)\,F h^2}{4 E I}\Big]}_{\text{only } \mathrm{y}} .\]
Explanation

We insert steps 1 and 2 into step 3 and collect the terms that depend only on \(x\) on one side and those that depend only on \(y\) on the other. Here we use \(1/G = 2(1 + \nu)/E\).

5
\[f(y) = \frac{(2 + \nu)\,F\,y^3}{6 E I} - \Big[\frac{(1 + \nu)\,F h^2}{4 E I} + c_0\Big]\,y + c_1 .\]
Explanation

The left side is a function of \(x\) only and the right of \(y\) only, and they are equal for all \(x\) and \(y\). In the beam is the point \((x_1, y_1)\). When we move horizontally, only \(x\) changes, the right side stays the same, so the left side does not change either. When we move vertically, only \(y\) changes, the left side stays the same, so the right side does not change either. Each side is therefore a constant. We denote the left side by the constant \(c_0\), and the expression in square brackets is then \(-c_0\). From the bracket we obtain \(f'(y)\) and integrate. From the left side we also get

\[g(x) = c_0 x - \frac{F}{E I}\Big(\frac{L x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{6}\Big) + c_2 .\]

The constants \(c_0\), \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are determined by the clamp (how the beam is attached). The terms with them are a rigid motion: \(-c_0 y\) in \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(c_0 x\) in \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) are a rotation by the angle \(c_0\), and \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are translations in the directions \(x\) and \(y\). The whole linear part of \(f(y)\) only means a rotation of the cross section as a whole, as the left of the figure shows it.

What warps is therefore the term \((2 + \nu)\,F y^3/(6 E I)\), which is not linear in \(y\): this is the orange curve in the middle of figure 5.105. The linear part rotates the cross section, the cubic one bends it. All steps are possible, so the displacement field exists and the strains derived from it are compatible in the plane \(x\)-\(y\) by the definition of compatible strains. The three-dimensional strains (with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\)) are not compatible (EP, p. 78). This is the field that EP computes for a cantilever with a point force at the end (example 2.5.1).

Source: EP example 2.5.1 (pp. 78-80), field (p80-b9) in the axes of the book EP (\(x_3\) upwards). The derivation is in our axes and with signs by the convention of this book.

For our beam (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(I = 1{,}0667\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\)) the cubic term at the edge of the cross section (\(y = 20\) mm) is \(0{,}073\ \mu\text{m}\). The deviation of the cross section from the plane through its two edges (\(y = \pm 20\) mm) is largest at \(y = \pm h/(2\sqrt3) = \pm 11{,}5\) mm, \(0{,}028\ \mu\text{m}\). The displacement of the edge of the cross section at the free end due to rotation is \(0{,}239\) mm, so the cubic term at the edge is \(0{,}03\) % of this displacement. The flatness of the cross sections therefore holds very well, because the beam is long: the shear strain is proportional to \(V\), which does not depend on \(L\), whereas the longitudinal displacement due to rotation is proportional to \(L^2\) (the rotation of the end is \(-F L^2/(2EI)\)). For a short and thick beam the ratio is different, which the Timoshenko beam takes into account by including the shear strain in the model (section 5.13).
