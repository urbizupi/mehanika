Chapter 7 Basics of FEM for bars and trusses

7.2 Assembly (sestavljanje)

In section 7.1 we computed, for one element, the stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the consistent nodal forces \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). A bar has many elements, each has its own two displacements at its nodes, and neighbouring elements share one displacement. How do we get the matrix of the whole bar from the matrices of the elements? And why is it the same matrix that we obtained in chapter 6 from integrals of hat functions over the whole bar? First we write down which displacements of the whole bar belong to which element. Then we add the element matrices at the right places and show that the sum is exactly the Ritz matrix of chapter 6. At the end we compute the sum for the hanging bar and see what it still lacks.

The whole section computes the same bar as chapters 6, 4 and the previous section: a steel hanging bar of length \(L = 10\) m and diameter 10 mm, axial stiffness \(EA = 1{,}5394 \cdot 10^7\) N, self weight \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}0097\) N/m per unit length, axis \(x\) pointing down. We do not support the bar in this section. All nodal displacements are unknowns, and we add the supports in section 7.3.

7.2.1 Connectivity table (tabela povezav)

The mesh \(0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{n}} = L\) (definition 6.24) has elements \([x_{\mathrm{e}-1}, x_{\mathrm{e}}]\) for \(e = 1, \dots, n\). The unknowns of the whole bar are the displacements of all nodes \(d_0, \dots, d_{\mathrm{n}}\), collected in a column \(\vek d\) of \(n + 1\) numbers, where \(d_{\mathrm{I}}\) is the displacement of node \(x_{\mathrm{I}}\). Element \(e\) has its own two displacements, which we write as a column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = (d^e_1, d^e_2)\) with local numbers \(1\) and \(2\) (first the left node, then the right one, as for the shape functions \(\psi^e_1\) and \(\psi^e_2\) of definition 7.2). For a bar the connection is simple: \(d^e_1 = d_{\mathrm{e}-1}\) and \(d^e_2 = d_{\mathrm{e}}\). For the truss that we compute later, however, the numbering cannot be guessed from the mesh, so we write the connection into a table.

Definition 7.10 Connectivity table (tabela povezav)

A structure has \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) unknowns, numbered with global numbers (globalne številke), and \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) elements, and element \(e\) has \(m\) displacements with local numbers \(i = 1, \dots, m\) (for the linear bar element \(m = 2\)). The connectivity table (tabela povezav) is a table with \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) rows and \(m\) columns in which row \(e\) and column \(i\) contain the global number \(g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) of the unknown that is the \(i\)-th displacement of element \(e\). Two numbers in the same row are different, because two displacements of an element are two different unknowns.

Source: FELIPPA §3.5.1 (PDF p. 46 to 47, Element Freedom Table), ONATE1 §2.3.3 (PDF p. 74, "global number"), COOK §2.5 (PDF p. 46 to 50). The Slovenian name is ours, FELIPPA calls the table Element Freedom Table.

For a bar with four elements, \(n = 4\), \(h = 2{,}5\) m, the unknowns are \(d_0, \dots, d_4\) (\(n_{\mathrm{u}} = 5\)) and the table has rows \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 3)\), \((3, 4)\) (figure 7.4). Row \(e = 2\) says that the first displacement of the second element is unknown \(1\) and the second is unknown \(2\), so \(g_{21} = 1\) and \(g_{22} = 2\). The numbering is a choice. If we numbered the nodes from the bottom up, the rows would be \((4, 3)\), \((3, 2)\), \((2, 1)\), \((1, 0)\), and the matrix of the whole bar would have the same values with rows and columns exchanged. The table says everything about the numbering that we need when we add the element matrices.

From the table we build a matrix that picks the column of unknowns of one element out of the column of all unknowns.

Definition 7.11 Selection matrix of an element (izbirna matrika elementa)

Let a structure have \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) unknowns and let \(g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) be its connectivity table (definition 7.10). The selection matrix (izbirna matrika) of element \(e\) is the matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(m\) rows and \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) columns whose entry in row \(i\) and column \(I\) is

\[(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{iI}} = \begin{cases} 1 & I = g_{\mathrm{ei}} , \\ 0 & \text{elsewhere.} \end{cases}\]

Each row therefore has one entry equal to 1, in the column of the number that the table gives for this local displacement.

Source: ONATE1 (2.85) (PDF p. 98, "operator for the global assembly"), FELIPPA §3.5.1 (PDF p. 47, EFT). The matrix form is ours.

For element \(e = 2\) of the bar with four elements (the columns are numbered \(0, \dots, 4\))

\[\mat Z_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \mat Z_2 \vek d = \begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \end{pmatrix} = \vek d_2 .\]

The matrix \(\mat Z_2\) does pick the displacements of nodes 1 and 2 out of \(\vek d\). The transposed matrix works the other way: it puts the column \(\vek d_2\) at places 1 and 2 and writes zeros at the other places. This is what the lemma says, which we need in the proof.

Lemma 7.12 Properties of the selection matrix (lastnosti izbirne matrike)

Let \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) be the selection matrix of element \(e\), \(\vek d\) a column of \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) unknowns, \(\mat k\) an \(m \times m\) matrix and \(\vek f\) a column of \(m\) numbers. Then:

  1. \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), that is \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d)_{\mathrm{i}} = d_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ei}}}\).
  2. The entry \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{IJ}}\) of the \(n_{\mathrm{u}} \times n_{\mathrm{u}}\) matrix is \(k_{\mathrm{ij}}\) if \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) and \(J = g_{\mathrm{ej}}\), and zero if \(I\) or \(J\) is not in row \(e\) of the table.
  3. The entry \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \vek f)_{\mathrm{I}}\) is \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) if \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\), and zero if \(I\) is not in row \(e\) of the table.
Proof

We must show three sums by the definition of the product of matrices (definition 1.98). In each of them at most one term is different from zero, because each row of the matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) has a single entry equal to 1.

  1. By the definition of the product \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d)_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} (\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{iI}}\,d_{\mathrm{I}}\). The only nonzero term is at \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) and it is \(1 \cdot d_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ei}}}\). The displacement \(d_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ei}}}\) is by the definition of the table the \(i\)-th displacement of element \(e\), that is \(d^e_{\mathrm{i}}\).
  2. By the definition of the product and lemma 1.101, \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{IJ}} = \sum_{\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}} (\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{aI}}\,k_{\mathrm{ab}}\,(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{bJ}}\), where \(a\) and \(b\) run over the local numbers. A term is nonzero only for \(a\) with \(g_{\mathrm{ea}} = I\) and for \(b\) with \(g_{\mathrm{eb}} = J\). Because the numbers in a row of the table are different, such an \(a\) exists exactly when \(I\) is in the row, and then it is unique, \(a = i\) with \(g_{\mathrm{ei}} = I\). The same holds for \(b = j\). The sum is therefore \(k_{\mathrm{ij}}\) if both \(I\) and \(J\) are in row \(e\), and otherwise empty, that is zero.
  3. By the same definition \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \vek f)_{\mathrm{I}} = \sum_{\mathrm{a}} (\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{aI}}\,f_{\mathrm{a}}\). Only the term with \(g_{\mathrm{ea}} = I\) is nonzero, which gives \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) at \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\), and otherwise zero.

Source: The proof is not from a source.

The matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) is thus a large \(n_{\mathrm{u}} \times n_{\mathrm{u}}\) matrix that has the entries of the matrix \(\mat k\) at four places \((g_{\mathrm{ei}}, g_{\mathrm{ej}})\) and zeros everywhere else. We will use this: assembly will be the addition of such large matrices.

7.2.2 The structure matrix as a sum (matrika konstrukcije kot vsota)

In chapter 6 an entry of the Ritz matrix \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = \int_0^L EA\,\psi'_{\mathrm{J}}\,\psi'_{\mathrm{I}}\,\mathrm{d}x\) was an integral over the whole bar. The hat function of a node lives on two elements only and is zero outside them, so the integral splits into pieces by elements, and on each element only the functions of the two nodes of that element are nonzero. We guess that the piece of element \(e\) is exactly the entry of its matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) at the place where the table puts its two displacements. The theorem says that the guess is true, and more. Adding the element matrices at the places given by the table makes sense for every structure made of elements, also for a truss, where a node has two or three unknowns. The energy of the structure is then the sum of the energies of the elements, and the force that an unknown needs is the sum of the forces of the elements that contain this unknown. We therefore state the theorem generally, and the bar is its first example. The sum over elements is exactly what we will later do on a computer.

Item 3 of the theorem holds for the bar of section 6.1.2, with axial stiffness \(EA > 0\) and load \(q\) that are continuous on every closed element of the mesh (with one-sided values at the ends), which holds if they have jumps only at nodes, as for the step in section 6.7.3. Besides the distributed load \(q\) we allow point forces \(P_{\mathrm{I}}\) in the direction of the axis \(x\) at nodes. For unknown \(I\), \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) is the hat function of the node whose displacement is this unknown (definition 6.25). We also include the hat function of the first node, although it was not among the trial functions in chapter 6, because its value at \(x = 0\) is not zero. The bilinear form \(B\) and the linear form \(\ell\) can be computed with it as well:

\[B(u, w) = \int_0^L EA\,u'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \ell(w) = \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x + \sum_{\mathrm{K}} P_{\mathrm{K}}\,w(x_{\mathrm{K}}) .\]

For element \(e\) with shape functions \(\psi^e_1\), \(\psi^e_2\) (definition 7.2) let

\[(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{e}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{e}}} EA\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{j}})'\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}})'\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad (\vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{e}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{e}}} q\,\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x\]

be the stiffness matrix of the element (lemma 7.5, which for constant \(EA\) on the element gives \(\tfrac{EA}{h}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\)) and the consistent nodal forces of the element (definition 7.7).

Theorem 7.13 Assembly from elements (sestavljanje po elementih)

Let a structure have \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) unknowns and \(n_{\mathrm{e}}\) elements with a connectivity table \(g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) and selection matrices \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) (definitions 7.10 and 7.11). Let element \(e\) have \(m\) displacements, a stiffness matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) of order \(m\) (definition 7.4) and a column of nodal forces \(\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\) with \(m\) numbers, and let \(\vek P\) be the column of point forces \(P_{\mathrm{I}}\) in the directions of the unknowns. The matrix \(\mat K\) and the right-hand side \(\vek f\) of the structure are

\[\begin{aligned} \mat K &= \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} , \\ \vek f &= \sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\vek f_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek P . \end{aligned} \tag{7.12}\]

Then for every column \(\vek d\) with \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) numbers and \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d\):

  1. \((\mat K\,\vek d)_{\mathrm{I}} = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} (\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\), where the sum runs over the elements \(e\) whose row of the table contains \(I\), and \(i\) is the local number with \(g_{\mathrm{ei}} = I\). The force that unknown \(I\) needs is the sum of the forces that the elements need for it.
  2. \(\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek d^\T \vek f = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} + \vek d^\T \vek P\).
  3. For the bar of section 6.1.2 with hat functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) and the element matrices and forces from the integrals above, \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\) and \(f_{\mathrm{I}} = \ell(\psi_{\mathrm{I}})\), where \(I\) and \(J\) are numbers of unknowns. For the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\), \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12 \int_0^L EA\,(u_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\vek d^\T \vek f = \ell(u_{\mathrm{h}})\), where \(U_{\mathrm{e}} = \tfrac12\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the strain energy of element \(e\).
Proof

Items 1 and 2 follow from lemma 7.12 and hold for every structure. For item 3 we must show that each entry \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\) and \(\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{I}})\) splits into pieces by elements, and that the piece of element \(e\) has exactly the value that \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), respectively \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\), puts at the right place.

  1. By item 1 of lemma 7.12 \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), so \(\mat K\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})\). By item 3 of the same lemma, applied to the column \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), entry \(I\) of term \(e\) equals \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) at \(g_{\mathrm{ei}} = I\) and zero if \(I\) is not in row \(e\). This is item 1.
  2. By lemma 1.101 \(\vek d^\T\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d = (\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d)^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d) = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) and \(\vek d^\T \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = (\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d)^\T \vek f_{\mathrm{e}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\). The sum over \(e\) gives item 2.
  3. The derivatives \(\psi'_{\mathrm{I}}\) and \(\psi'_{\mathrm{J}}\) are piecewise constant and \(EA\) is continuous on every element, so the integrand is continuous on every closed element. By lemma 6.10 (item 1, independence of the subdivision) the integral over \([0, L]\) is the sum of the integrals over the elements: \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{e}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{e}}} EA\,\psi'_{\mathrm{J}}\,\psi'_{\mathrm{I}}\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. Let \(I\) not be in row \(e\) of the table. By the definition of the hat function (definition 6.25) \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) is zero at both ends of element \(e\) and runs along a straight line on it. A straight line with zero values at both ends is zero, so \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}} = 0\) on element \(e\).
  5. Let \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\). The function \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) is a straight line on element \(e\) that equals 1 at node \(i\) and 0 at the other node of the element. Two values determine a straight line, so this is the shape function \(\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}}\) (definition 7.2).
  6. By steps 4 and 5 the piece of element \(e\) in step 3 equals \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\) if \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) and \(J = g_{\mathrm{ej}}\), and zero if \(I\) or \(J\) is not in row \(e\) (the derivative of the zero function is zero). By item 2 of lemma 7.12 the matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) has the same value at the same place \((I, J)\). The sum over \(e\) in step 3 is therefore the sum of these matrices, entry by entry, so \(B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = K_{\mathrm{IJ}}\).
  7. In the same way we split \(\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{e}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{e}}} q\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\,\mathrm{d}x + P_{\mathrm{I}}\) (lemma 6.10, item 1). By steps 4 and 5 the piece of element \(e\) equals \((\vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) at \(I = g_{\mathrm{ei}}\) and zero elsewhere, which by item 3 of lemma 7.12 is the entry \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{I}}\). The term with point forces is \(\sum_{\mathrm{K}} P_{\mathrm{K}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}(x_{\mathrm{K}}) = P_{\mathrm{I}}\), because \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) is 1 at its own node and 0 at the other nodes. So \(\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = f_{\mathrm{I}}\).
  8. By steps 4 and 5 the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) on element \(e\) equals \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} d^e_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}}\), where by item 1 of lemma 7.12 \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d\). The square of the derivative is \((u_{\mathrm{h}}')^2 = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} d^e_{\mathrm{i}}\,d^e_{\mathrm{j}}\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}})'\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{j}})'\), and its integral with \(EA\) over the element is \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}} d^e_{\mathrm{i}}\,(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\,d^e_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). This is \(2U_{\mathrm{e}}\).
  9. By item 2 and step 8 \(\vek d^\T \mat K \vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} 2U_{\mathrm{e}}\), and the sum of the integrals over the elements is by lemma 6.10 the integral over the bar. The last equality, \(\vek d^\T \vek f = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\,\ell(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}) = \ell(u_{\mathrm{h}})\), follows from step 7 and the linearity of the form \(\ell\) (lemma 6.10, item 2).

Source: ONATE1 (2.23) and (2.24) (PDF p. 74, sum of the element contributions at a node) and (2.85) (PDF p. 98), COOK (2.5-10) (PDF p. 50, assembled matrix of a three-bar truss), EP (8.2.36) to (8.2.39) (pp. 540 to 542, two neighbouring elements add up at the common node). The sources assemble from the equilibrium of forces at a node, we from the splitting of the integral, so the proof in this form is not from a source.

The proof showed that the matrix that we computed in chapter 6 with integrals over the whole bar is nothing new. It is a sum of small pieces, each belonging to one element and computed once, no matter where on the bar the element lies. The same holds for the right-hand side and for the energy. All statements of chapter 6 about the Ritz solution therefore hold also for the system that we assemble from elements. Their use is in section 7.3, when we support the bar. Items 1 and 2 are not tied to the bar, so we will use them for trusses as well. Item 3 uses only that the function of a node is, on every element, the shape function of this node (steps 4 and 5). For elements with three nodes we will show this separately when we introduce them.

We test the theorem on the bar with a step from section 6.7.3, where the two elements differ. Two elements of length \(h = 5\) m, the first with stiffness \(EA\) and weight \(q\) per unit length, the second with stiffness \(EA/4\) and weight \(q/4\). The element matrices are \(\mat k_1 = \tfrac{EA}{h}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(\mat k_2 = \tfrac{EA}{4h}\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the element forces \(\vek f_1 = \tfrac{qh}{2}(1, 1)\) and \(\vek f_2 = \tfrac{qh}{8}(1, 1)\). The connectivity table is \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\). Equation (7.12) gives

\[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 + \tfrac14 & -\tfrac14 \\ 0 & -\tfrac14 & \tfrac14 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f = qh \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 + \tfrac18 \\ \tfrac18 \end{pmatrix} = qh \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac58 \\ \tfrac18 \end{pmatrix} .\]

For unknowns \(1\) and \(2\) this is chapter 6: \(K_{11} = EA/h + EA/(4h)\), \(K_{12} = -EA/(4h)\), \(K_{22} = EA/(4h)\), \(f_1 = \tfrac58 qh\), \(f_2 = \tfrac18 qh\). A computation with SymPy from the integrals of the hat functions over the whole bar, without elements, gives the same matrix and the same right-hand side. The sum of the forces at all nodes is \(qh\,(\tfrac12 + \tfrac58 + \tfrac18) = \tfrac54\,qh\), which is the weight of the bar: \(qh\) for the upper piece and \(qh/4\) for the lower one.

We check the energy on the field of the Ritz solution \(n = 2\) for the uniform bar, \(d_0 = 0\), \(d_1 = 14{,}640\) µm, \(d_2 = 19{,}520\) µm (chapter 6). With \(EA/h = 3{,}0788 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(U_1 = \tfrac12 (EA/h)(d_1 - d_0)^2 = 329{,}93\) µJ and \(U_2 = \tfrac12 (EA/h)(d_2 - d_1)^2 = 36{,}659\) µJ. The sum \(366{,}59\) µJ equals \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d\) and also \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T \vek f\), which is the last equality of the theorem and the equality from chapter 6, where \(\Pi = -391{,}03 + 97{,}76/n^2\) µJ, so for \(n = 2\) it is \(-366{,}59\) µJ. The first element carries almost all the energy: it has the larger elongation (\(14{,}64\) µm against \(4{,}88\) µm), and the energy grows with the square of the elongation, \((14{,}64/4{,}88)^2 = 9\) (the ratio \(329{,}93 / 36{,}659 = 9{,}0\)).

Three properties of the matrix follow from the theorem, and we will need them.

Corollary 7.14 Structure matrix (matrika konstrukcije)

Let \(\mat K\) be the matrix of theorem 7.13 and let every element matrix \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) be symmetric with \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}} \ge 0\) for every column \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\). Then \(\mat K\) is symmetric, \(\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d \ge 0\) for every column \(\vek d\), and the entry \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}}\) is zero if the unknowns \(I\) and \(J\) do not stand in the same row of the connectivity table. The elements of the bar of item 3 of theorem 7.13 satisfy the hypothesis.

Proof

We must show three properties and then the hypothesis for the bar.

  1. Symmetry. By lemma 1.101 \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})^\T = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}} = \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}} \mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), because \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric. A sum of symmetric matrices is symmetric.
  2. Nonnegativity. By item 2 of theorem 7.13 \(\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d = \sum_{\mathrm{e}} \vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\), a sum of nonnegative numbers.
  3. Zeros. Let \(I\) and \(J\) not stand in the same row. Then for every element \(e\) at least one of them is not in row \(e\), so by item 2 of lemma 7.12 the entry \((\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{IJ}}\) is zero. The sum over \(e\) is therefore zero.
  4. The bar. The entry \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is the integral of the product \((\psi^e_{\mathrm{j}})'\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}})'\), which equals the product \((\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}})'\,(\psi^e_{\mathrm{j}})'\), so \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) is symmetric. By step 8 of the proof of the theorem \(\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}^\T \mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\vek d_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the integral of \(EA\,(u_{\mathrm{h}}')^2\) over the element for the field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} d^e_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi^e_{\mathrm{i}}\). The integrand is nonnegative because \(EA > 0\), so the integral is nonnegative (lemma 6.10, item 3).

Source: EP p. 541 ("the global stiffness coefficient K_IJ is zero if global nodes I and J do not belong to the same element"), COOK §2.6 (PDF p. 51, Sparsity). The proof in steps is not from a source.

For the bar with four elements of figure 7.4 the unknowns \(I\) and \(J\) are in the same row only if \(|I - J| \le 1\), so the matrix has nonzero entries only on the main diagonal and two neighbouring diagonals (13 of 25 entries):

\[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f = \frac{qh}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

With \(EA/h = 6{,}1575 \cdot 10^6\) N/m and \(qh/2 = 7{,}512\) N the forces at the nodes are in turn \(7{,}512\) N, \(15{,}024\) N, \(15{,}024\) N, \(15{,}024\) N and \(7{,}512\) N. An inner node carries the weight of half of each of the two neighbouring elements, so twice the number, the end nodes only that of one element. The sum \(60{,}097\) N is the weight of the bar \(qL\). The zeros in the matrix follow from each hat function living on two elements only (chapter 6). In section 7.8 we will see what these zeros bring us.

The theorem determines what the structure matrix is. In a computation, however, we must not form every large matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\), since it has \((n+1)^2\) entries, almost all of them zero. Lemma 7.12 says that adding such matrices is the same as adding the entries of \(\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\) directly at the places that the table gives. The algorithm is this.

  1. Take a matrix \(\mat K\) of order \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) and a column \(\vek f\) of \(n_{\mathrm{u}}\) numbers, all zeros.
  2. For every element \(e = 1, \dots, n_{\mathrm{e}}\) and every pair of local numbers \(i, j\) add \((\mat k_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{ij}}\) to the entry \(K_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ei}}, \mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ej}}}\), and for every \(i\) add \((\vek f_{\mathrm{e}})_{\mathrm{i}}\) to the entry \(f_{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{ei}}}\). For element \(e\) this is \(m^2 + m\) additions (6 for a bar) instead of \(n_{\mathrm{u}}^2 + n_{\mathrm{u}}\).
  3. Finally add the point forces: \(f_{\mathrm{I}} \to f_{\mathrm{I}} + P_{\mathrm{I}}\).

By lemma 7.12 (items 2 and 3) every step 2 adds exactly the matrix \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\mat k_{\mathrm{e}}\,\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}\) and the column \(\mat Z_{\mathrm{e}}^\T\,\vek f_{\mathrm{e}}\), so by theorem 7.13 the result is the same as the sum. FELIPPA says that the element matrices are not expanded ("member stiffness matrices are not expanded", PDF p. 47). The procedure in which we add the coefficients of the elements at the places dictated by the numbering of the nodes of the structure is called by COOK (PDF p. 50) the direct stiffness method (metoda neposredne togosti). FELIPPA (PDF p. 28) uses the same name for the whole procedure from the elements to the structure and says that all major commercial programs for the finite element method are based on it.

We carry out the algorithm for a bar with two elements, \(h = 5\) m, \(EA/h = 3{,}0788 \cdot 10^6\) N/m, \(qh/2 = 15{,}024\) N (figure 7.5). The connectivity table is \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), and we start with the zero matrix \(3 \times 3\).

  • Element 1 (row \((0, 1)\)): the pairs \((i, j)\) go to the places \((g_{1\mathrm{i}}, g_{1\mathrm{j}})\), that is \((0, 0)\), \((0, 1)\), \((1, 0)\), \((1, 1)\). After this
    \[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f = \frac{qh}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]
  • Element 2 (row \((1, 2)\)): the places \((1, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 1)\), \((2, 2)\). At place \((1, 1)\) there is already \(1\), we add \(1\) and get \(2\). After this
    \[\mat K = \frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 + 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek f = \frac{qh}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 + 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The inner node 1 belongs to both elements, so it gets two contributions. In mechanical terms: the first element alone would hold stiffness \(EA/h\) against a displacement of this node, the second one the same, together \(2EA/h\). The end nodes belong to one element and have \(EA/h\).

From history

Aircraft engineers made remarkable progress in the early to mid-1950s, although some of the work was not published until much later because of company policies (COOK, PDF p. 24). Early in this period, equations from conventional methods of analysis were solved on the small computers of the time (COOK, PDF p. 24). The problem was wings of low aspect ratio: in the United States conventional methods proved inadequate for them, so Turner devised a three-node triangular element to model the wing skin (COOK, PDF p. 24). FELIPPA (PDF p. 203) says that continuum finite elements were invented in the aircraft industry for plane stress in the analysis of delta wing panels, and adds in a note the company Boeing and the period 1952 to 1953 (FELIPPA, PDF p. 203, note 1). In Germany, Argyris included finite element concepts in a set of influential papers about matrix procedures (COOK, PDF p. 24).

The year of Argyris's work differs between the sources: GR (PDF p. 354) writes that in 1954 he published a famous monograph on matrix structural analysis, ONATE1 (PDF p. 469) lists his book with Kelsey with the year 1960, which appeared in a series of articles in 1954 to 1955.

In 1956 Martin, Turner, Clough and Topp published a paper with two continuum elements, which according to FELIPPA (PDF p. 218, note 1) is widely regarded as the start of the present finite element method. According to GR (PDF p. 354) Clough and his colleagues then formulated the finite element method as a natural extension of matrix structural analysis. According to GR (PDF p. 354) the real development of the method was triggered by the needs of the aerospace industry and by the concurrent progress in computers and in methods of matrix computation. Clough coined the name "finite element" in 1960 (COOK, PDF p. 24, ONATE1, PDF p. 10 and 63). Many new elements for stress analysis soon followed, developed largely by intuition and physical argument (COOK, PDF p. 24). In 1963 it was recognized that the finite element method is a form of the Rayleigh-Ritz method, a classical approximation technique. Thus, according to COOK (PDF p. 24), it was seen not only as a special trick for stress analysis but as a widely applicable method with a sound mathematical basis. This is the road that we took in chapter 6.

Let us return to the hanging bar and the assembly of its matrix. We now have the matrix of the bar with two elements and know that it equals the Ritz matrix with hat functions. Let us look at it once more in full.

7.2.3 The hanging bar, assembled (viseča palica, sestavljena)

The bar is the same, \(L = 10\) m, diameter 10 mm, \(EA = 1{,}5394 \cdot 10^7\) N, \(q = 6{,}0097\) N/m, axis \(x\) down, a mesh with two elements (\(h = 5\) m), and no unknown is yet prescribed. The assembled system \(\mat K \vek d = \vek f\) for the unknowns \(d_0, d_1, d_2\) is

\[\frac{EA}{h} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} d_0 \\ d_1 \\ d_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 15{,}024 \\ 30{,}049 \\ 15{,}024 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{N} , \qquad \frac{EA}{h} = 3{,}0788 \cdot 10^6\ \text{N/m} .\]

The matrix is symmetric and the sum of the entries of each row is zero. For displacements \(d_0 = d_1 = d_2 = c\) we therefore have \(\mat K \vek d = \vek 0\) for every \(c\). The field \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = c\,(\psi_0 + \psi_1 + \psi_2) = c\) is constant and has derivative zero, so by theorem 7.13 its energy is \(\tfrac12\,\vek d^\T \mat K\,\vek d = 0\). The bar moves as a whole by \(c\) and is not stretched. The eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat K/(EA/h)\) are \(0\), \(1\) and \(3\) (SymPy), one of them is zero.

We therefore cannot solve the system. Let us add all three equations. On the left the terms cancel, because the sum of each column is zero, and on the right we get \(15{,}024 + 30{,}049 + 15{,}024 = 60{,}097\) N, the weight of the bar \(qL\). We get \(0 = 60{,}097\) N, which holds for no \(\vek d\). The cause is in the model. A bar without a support is a body on which the force of gravity acts and no other, so it cannot be in equilibrium, and our computation assumes equilibrium. The hat function \(\psi_0\) was left out in chapter 6 precisely because it does not satisfy \(w(0) = 0\). Now we included it and forgot the condition \(u(0) = 0\).

What is missing is therefore the force with which the support holds the bar. Chapter 6 built the condition \(d_0 = 0\) into the choice of trial functions and left out the function \(\psi_0\), which in the matrix means that it left out the row and the column of unknown \(0\). The first equation thereby disappeared, and since the force of the support is the only unknown force in it, chapter 6 did not need it. How we build the condition into the assembled system, how we get the force of the support from the omitted equation, and whether the solution of the shortened system also solves the original one, is shown in section 7.3.
