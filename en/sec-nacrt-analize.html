Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.9 Planning an analysis and the tube case study

The earlier sections of this chapter gave the parts: what a model is (section 17.1), how a body becomes a model (section 17.2), how we check that a result settles down as the mesh is refined (section 17.3), what to do with stresses (section 17.4), when a mesh is bad (section 17.5) and when a system of equations is hard (section 17.6). Whoever carries out an analysis needs an order: what to decide before the first computation, what after it, and when to stop. We call this order the plan of an analysis (načrt analize). In everyday use a plan is a drawing or a sequence of work made before the building starts, and that is what it is here: the decisions we make before the computation so that the computation answers the question we asked.

The plan does not treat only one of the three errors of definition 17.2. It leads us through all three: modelling error (the model against the real body), discretization error (the finite element solution against the exact solution of the model) and numerical error (round-off and stopped iterations). Reducing one does not reduce the others, so the plan separates them and gives each its own step. The steps follow COOK §10.12 to §10.15 (PDF pp. 375-382) and ONATE1 §1.4.4 (PDF pp. 31-33). We test them on the tube of chapter 11, because we know its exact solution and can see what each step tells us and what it does not.

The model is this tube: steel, inner radius \(a = 40\) mm and outer radius \(b = 60\) mm, modulus \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\), oil pressure \(p = 30\) MPa in the bore and a free outer surface (theorem 11.27). The idealizations are the same as there: the material is homogeneous, isotropic and linearly elastic, and the pressure is uniform over the bore. The ends of the tube are not fixed by the problem. Chapter 11 computed three possibilities, open ends, closed ends and plane strain (definition 11.30), and at \(p = 30\) MPa the von Mises stresses (definition 2.76) at the bore are \(96{,}56\), \(93{,}53\) and \(94{,}02\) MPa. The yield pressure \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) is the pressure at which this stress equals the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa of section 2.12.5 of chapter 2, so \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 30 \cdot 220/\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\). We carry out the task as if we did not know the exact solution, and compare with it at the end.

We build the figure step by step, as one solves the task. First the question: from it follows which quantity we compute and how accurately we need it. For the tube the question is when it starts to yield, and the quantity is the von Mises stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}(a)\) at the bore, because by chapter 11 it is largest there. We choose the accuracy ourselves, \(2\,\%\) for the example, because an error in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) changes \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) by the same fraction. This is our choice for the example and not a datum from a source. For another task the requirement may differ, but it must be written down before the computation, since without it we do not know when the mesh is fine enough.

17.9.1 Question and hand estimate

The second step is the hand calculation, before we open the program. COOK demands it with a good reason: since we find it hard to give up a result that took much work, an approximate result should be ready beforehand, and even a rough calculation detects a displacement in the wrong direction, a strange shape of the field or an error of orders of magnitude (COOK §10.12, PDF p. 375). For the tube the calculation is already in section 11.7.1 of chapter 11. Half the tube per unit length is in equilibrium if the pressure resultant \(2\,p\,a\) equals the sum of the forces in the two cuts (figure 11.18), so

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = p\,a , \tag{17.44}\]

(equation (11.32)), and the mean hoop stress is \(p\,a/(b - a) = 60\) MPa. With the mean radius in place of \(a\) we get \(75\) MPa (equation (11.33)). The true value at the bore is known from theorem 11.27, \(78\) MPa, and the first two numbers are \(23\,\%\) and \(4\,\%\) below it. The hand calculation here gives the order of magnitude and the sign of the stress, not the stress to \(2\,\%\). Since the two numbers differ from each other by \(25\,\%\), it would catch a factor of \(10\) or a wrong sign, but not finer errors.

The hand calculation and the computer result must refer to the same physical case (COOK §10.15, PDF p. 381). If we compare \(78\) MPa with a mesh that has another pressure or another bore radius, the difference must not be put down to the mesh.

17.9.2 Sequence of models and meshes

The third step is the choice of model. COOK advises a sequence of models from simple to detailed, each correcting the faults of the previous one, because this is faster than one large model that turns out wrong when we look at its result (COOK §10.12, PDF p. 376). For the tube the sequence is: the thin-wall formula (step 2), the Lamé solution, and finally the finite element model. One question remains in the model, the ends of the tube. By theorem 11.27 the constants \(A\) and \(B\), and with them \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), do not depend on the axial stress \(S\), which enters only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). So the mesh computes the same \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) for all three ends, and the difference of \(3{,}2\,\%\) between \(96{,}56\) and \(93{,}53\) MPa is wholly modelling error. The mesh does not reduce it (definition 17.2), only knowledge of how the tube is really closed decides it.

The computational model is the radial problem of the tube in plane strain with linear elements along the radius, as section 17.4 derives it from the weak form (lemma 17.17). The fourth step is a sequence of meshes with \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\) equal elements. Doubling is not arbitrary: theorem 17.14 needs three nested meshes, each differing from the previous one by the same factor. Example 17.46 collects the results.

First the axes: horizontally the number of elements \(n\) in doublings, vertically the error in per cent on a logarithmic scale, so a straight line means that the error falls as a power of the element size \(h \sim 1/n\). The blackwhite line is the error of the displacement \(u(a)\): at every doubling it falls to a quarter, which is second order, as section 17.3 predicts for displacements. The horizontal dashed line at \(2\) is the chosen tolerance: the blue line crosses it between \(n = 8\) (\(2{,}80\,\%\)) and \(n = 16\) (\(1{,}43\,\%\)). The blue line is still above it at \(n = 32\), at \(0{,}72\,\%\). The vertical dashed line at \(n = 16\) says one more thing: at this mesh the displacement at the bore is exact to \(0{,}017\,\%\) and the stress to \(1{,}43\,\%\), worse by a factor of \(82\) (from the unrounded errors \(1{,}427\,\%\) and \(0{,}0175\,\%\)). COOK writes this as advice to use a finer mesh for stresses than for displacements (COOK §10.12, PDF p. 376), and the table shows how much finer.

The error has a sign. All the values of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\) from the mesh are below the exact one, so the yield pressure \(p_{\mathrm{Y}}\) computed from them is above the true one: at \(n = 16\) it is \(71{,}21\) against \(70{,}20\) MPa. For the question about yielding this is an error on the unsafe side, which is the reason not to stop the mesh at the first number that is close.

17.9.3 Checks and mistakes

The fifth step is the checks of the result. Section 7.10.2 of chapter 7 collected eight of them. For the truss the first is the sum of reactions that balances the load (lemma 7.20). The tube has no supports, so its equivalent check is in half the tube: for the exact field equation (17.44) holds. Does the solution from the mesh satisfy it too?

Lemma 17.47 Half-tube equilibrium for the finite element solution (ravnovesje polovice cevi pri rešitvi iz mreže)

Let \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) be the solution of the radial problem of the tube \(a \le r \le b\) of lemma 17.17 on a mesh \(a = r_0 < r_1 < \dots < r_{\mathrm{n}} = b\) with hat functions \(\psi_0, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{n}}\) (definition 6.25), all nodes free, pressure \(p_{\mathrm{a}}\) at \(r = a\) and a free outer surface. Let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr},\mathrm{g}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}}\) be the stresses from \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) by Hooke's law of plane strain at the Gauss point \(g\), and \(w_{\mathrm{g}} = h_{\mathrm{e}}/2\) the weight of the two-point Gauss rule (definition 7.24) in the element \(e\) of length \(h_{\mathrm{e}}\). Let the system matrix be computed with the same rule, as in the computation of example 17.46. Then for every mesh

\[\sum_{\mathrm{g}} w_{\mathrm{g}}\,\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}} = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,a , \tag{17.45}\]

the sum running over all Gauss points of all elements.

Proof

We show that the sum of the rows of the system of equations for \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\) gives exactly equation (17.45).

  1. The system is \(K\,\vek d = \vek f\) with \(f_0 = p_{\mathrm{a}} a\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) for \(i \ge 1\) (lemma 17.17: the work of the pressure on a virtual displacement \(v\) is \(p_{\mathrm{a}} a\,v(a)\), and the hat function \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is one only at node \(i\)). By hypothesis the matrix is a sum over Gauss points, \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} = \sum_{\mathrm{g}} w_{\mathrm{g}}\,r_{\mathrm{g}}\,\big(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'(r_{\mathrm{g}})\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}[\psi_{\mathrm{j}}] + \psi_{\mathrm{i}}(r_{\mathrm{g}})\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}[\psi_{\mathrm{j}}]/r_{\mathrm{g}}\big)\), where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}[\psi_{\mathrm{j}}]\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}[\psi_{\mathrm{j}}]\) are the stresses of the field \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). By linearity, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} K_{\mathrm{ij}} d_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{g}} w_{\mathrm{g}}\,r_{\mathrm{g}}\,\big(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr},\mathrm{g}} + \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}}/r_{\mathrm{g}}\big)\).
  2. On every element only two hat functions are nonzero, both linear with sum \(1\) (definition 6.25), so \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{n}\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = 1\) on the whole interval and \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \psi_{\mathrm{i}}' = 0\).
  3. We add the equations for \(i = 0, \dots, n\). By step 2 the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}'\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr},\mathrm{g}}\) is zero, the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} \psi_{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}}/r_{\mathrm{g}}\) is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}}/r_{\mathrm{g}}\), and the factor \(r_{\mathrm{g}}\) cancels. The left side is \(\sum_{\mathrm{g}} w_{\mathrm{g}}\,\sigma_{\theta\theta,\mathrm{g}}\).
  4. The right side of the sum is \(f_0 = p_{\mathrm{a}} a\). Equating gives equation (17.45). \(\square\)

Source: The proof is not from the sources. The check follows the sum of reactions: COOK §10.15 (PDF p. 381).

The lemma says more than it seems. The sum is the Gauss sum for \(\int\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r\), so the mesh satisfies the same half-tube equilibrium as the exact field. If the matrix is integrated exactly, the same proof with integrals in place of sums gives \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = p_{\mathrm{a}}\,a\) for the stress from \(u_{\mathrm{h}}\). The proof uses only that the constant \(1\) lies in the space of hat functions (step 2), just as for the truss, where the reactions balance the load because a rigid displacement lies in the space. The check therefore depends on the space and not on the quality of the mesh. We test it on three mistakes, each introduced into the computation of example 17.46.

  • A coarse mesh \(n = 1\). The sum is \(1{,}2\) MN/m \(= p\,a\) up to round-off, although the hoop stress at the bore is \(88{,}45\) MPa instead of \(78{,}00\), an error of \(+13{,}4\,\%\) (example 17.46). The check does not find the discretization error and cannot, because it holds on every mesh. It is found by comparison with the Lamé solution or by refining the mesh (check (f) of section 7.10.2, figure 17.29).
  • A wrong pressure in the data, \(p = 3\) MPa instead of \(30\) MPa. The sum is \(0{,}12\) MN/m, which is \(p\,a\) with the entered pressure, and it agrees with itself. If we compare it with \(p\,a\) at the pressure the task demands, the error is \(-90\,\%\). The check finds the mistake only when the computer has the true datum from the task, not from the input. Refining the mesh does not find this mistake, because the relative errors at \(p = 3\) MPa are the same as at \(30\) MPa (the computation is linear in \(p\)).
  • A wrong modulus, \(E = 19{,}6\) GPa instead of \(196\) GPa. The sum and all the stresses are unchanged (\(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 79{,}218\) MPa in both cases, because \(E\) does not enter the stresses of this force problem), but the displacement is \(168{,}71\ \mu\)m instead of \(16{,}87\ \mu\)m. Only comparing the displacement with an approximate or exact calculation (check (d)) or a review of units and data (check (g)) finds it. The stress check is blind to this mistake.

The list does not say that the real model is now verified. It says only which step catches which mistake. Section 7.10.2 did the same for the truss, and COOK lists mistakes that a program does not detect by itself: elements of the wrong type, a coarse mesh, supports wrong in position, kind or direction, a misplaced decimal point, and an element defined twice that we do not see on a plot of the mesh (COOK §10.13, PDF pp. 377-378). A program checks element distortion and unconnected nodes, but it cannot know whether the problem we wanted is the one defined, whether the material data are right, or whether the supports are in the right places (COOK §10.14, PDF p. 380). Since the program does not know this, the analyst must keep a record of the sources of data, assumptions, tests and the version of the program, so that after an interruption the work can be resumed or reviewed by someone else (COOK §10.13, PDF pp. 378-379).

17.9.4 Critique of the result and the decision

The sixth step is the critique. First the question whether the result looks strange, and then the quantities we already know (COOK §10.15, PDF pp. 380-381). COOK warns that stresses computed from displacements are less accurate than the displacements, so we do not trust them if the displacements are doubtful (COOK §10.15, PDF p. 382). In our tube the displacements are trustworthy but the stresses do not reach the boundary condition. At the bore \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -p = -30\) MPa must hold (theorem 11.27, step 7 of its proof), and the element at the bore gives \(-27{,}12\) MPa at \(n = 16\), which is \(9{,}59\,\%\) too little, and \(-28{,}54\) MPa at \(n = 32\), \(4{,}86\,\%\) too little. This deviation is an estimate of the error that needs no exact solution, because we know the condition from the task. The hoop stress is wrong by \(1{,}56\,\%\) and \(0{,}80\,\%\) at these two meshes, so the deviation of the boundary condition is six times larger than the error it estimates (\(6{,}15\) and \(6{,}11\)). The factor six is an observation on this tube, not a law: it says that the estimate is here on the safe side, and on another task it would have to be measured again.

If the critique finds a mistake, the dashed arrow sends the analysis back to the model and the mesh. The seventh step is the decision. The mesh with \(n = 16\) is within the \(2\,\%\) tolerance (\(1{,}43\,\%\)), but the error is one-sided and the deviation at the boundary says that the stress at the bore has not yet settled. So we use theorem 17.14. The order given by the two triples of meshes is close to \(1\) (\(0{,}93\) and \(0{,}96\)), so we take \(q = 1\), and from the values \(92{,}68\) and \(93{,}34\) MPa at \(n = 16\) and \(32\) we get \(2 \cdot 93{,}3449 - 92{,}6803 = 94{,}01\) MPa. The exact value is \(94{,}02\) MPa, an error of \(-0{,}013\,\%\), and \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 30 \cdot 220/94{,}01 = 70{,}21\) MPa. Had we taken the wrong \(q = 2\), which is right for the displacement, we would get \((4 \cdot 93{,}34 - 92{,}68)/3 = 93{,}57\) MPa, an error of \(-0{,}48\,\%\): within the tolerance, but more than thirty times larger than with the observed order (section 17.3).

The decision has a remainder that computing does not reduce. The discretization error at \(n = 16\) is \(1{,}43\,\%\), the difference between plane strain and closed ends is \(0{,}53\,\%\) and the difference between open and closed ends is \(3{,}2\,\%\). So we first decide what ends the tube really has (modelling error). Only then does it make sense to refine the mesh, since at \(1{,}43\,\%\) plane strain and closed ends (\(0{,}53\,\%\)) are not yet distinguished. For open ends \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 68{,}35\) MPa, the lowest of the three, and whoever does not know the ends designs by it.

17.9.5 Verification and validation

What the tables of example 17.46 do not cover is the question whether the model is the right one at all. ONATE1 has two terms for this and keeps them apart.

Definition 17.48 Verification and validation of a computational model (preverjanje in potrjevanje računskega modela)

Let a physical system be given, its mathematical model (equations and data) and a computational model (the solution of that model by finite elements). Verification is the process of determining that the computational model accurately represents the mathematical model and its solution, whether or not the model corresponds to reality. It has two parts:

  1. code verification: we gain confidence that the program solves the equations of the model correctly. It is done by comparison with simple cases that have an exact solution.
  2. calculation verification: for this computational case we estimate the discretization error, by refining the mesh or by raising the order of the elements.

Validation is the assessment whether the mathematical and the computational model represent the physical system for the intended purpose: by comparison with measurements on a model or on a real structure. A large deviation for an already verified program means that the chosen model is not suitable.

Source: ONATE1 §1.4.4 (PDF pp. 31-33): verification shows that we solve the problems accurately, validation that we solve the right problem.

The names say what they establish. To verify means in everyday use to look whether something holds, and verification here looks whether the solution holds for the model we wrote down. To validate means to establish by a measurement or a witness that something is so, and validation here compares the model with a measurement on the real system. For the tube, the first part, code verification, is the comparison of \(u(a)\) and the stresses with the Lamé solution (theorem 11.27): the exact solution of the mathematical model is the yardstick (the table in example 17.46, displacement \(0{,}004\,\%\) at \(n = 32\)). The second part, calculation verification, is the estimate from this section: from three meshes with Richardson's extrapolation and from the deviation of the boundary condition. For a tube with a groove, for which no exact solution exists, this would be the only part of verification we have for the computation itself, while the program would still be verified on the tube without the groove. ONATE1 also mentions manufactured solutions: we choose an arbitrary displacement field, compute from the equations the load that produces it, and check whether the program finds the field (ONATE1 §1.4.4, PDF pp. 33-35). Here we only name this way.

We have no validation for our tube. The book has no measured strains on a real tube, so the answer \(p_{\mathrm{Y}} = 70{,}2\) MPa (plane strain) holds for the model: a homogeneous isotropic linearly elastic material, the yield stress \(220\) MPa and the von Mises condition. It is verified and not validated. Among the unvalidated idealizations are these three: the material is homogeneous, yielding is described by the von Mises stress, and the ends of the tube are fixed as one of the three possibilities.

17.9.6 Software

The plan needs a tool, a program. Programs are not written by the average analyst but by researchers, students and companies, their development is expensive, and users stay loyal to the program they already know. For this reason COOK does not compare products, which change quickly, and neither do we name any. The documentation is part of the system we test, and it must be read when choosing a program (COOK, PDF p. 384). Code verification of definition 17.48 applies to a program we buy just as to one we write: the tube with the Lamé solution is a test that can be run on every program in which a pressure on the inner edge can be prescribed.

17.9.7 Plate with a hole: a plan without numbers

Chapter 11 left the question how large the stress at a hole in a finite plate is, and wrote it as the yardstick for a numerical result: Kirsch's factor \(3\) (theorem 11.51). The plate is \(200 \times 200\) mm with a hole of radius \(a = 10\) mm and a tensile load \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa, the largest stress is \(3\sigma_0 = 150\) MPa, first yielding is at \(\sigma_0 = 73{,}33\) MPa, and the error of the load at the edge due to the finite width is \(2{,}5\,\%\) (section 11.13.6). Let us see what the plan would demand for a finite element computation, and what we can already say from the book without a single mesh result.

Plane elements are in chapter 15, but the book does not carry out the computation of this plate with them, so there are no mesh numbers here. What we can compute is a yardstick for the mesh. Figure 17.30 shows how the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) along the \(y\) axis falls from the edge of the hole (equation (11.69)). First the axes: horizontally the distance from the centre of the hole in radii \(r/a\), vertically \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\sigma_0\). The curve starts at the peak at \(3\) and falls to \(1{,}93\) at \(r = 1{,}25\,a\), \(1{,}52\) at \(1{,}5\,a\), \(1{,}22\) at \(2\,a\) and \(1{,}07\) at \(3\,a\), towards the value \(1\) far from the hole. So the stress changes over a length of the size of the hole radius, and from here follows how fine the mesh must be.

An element in which the stress is constant cannot give the peak. The mean of a function over an interval is smaller than its largest value when the function is smaller than it everywhere except at one end, and the curve of the figure has its peak at \(r = a\). The orange segment shows an element of size \(h = a\) at the hole: the mean of the exact stress over it is \(1{,}6875\,\sigma_0\) (SymPy, outlines/ch-modeliranje/num-09.out), \(56\,\%\) of the peak. The blue segment at \(h = a/4\) gives \(2{,}376\,\sigma_0\), \(79\,\%\), and at \(h = a/8\) still only \(2{,}635\,\sigma_0\), \(88\,\%\). For small \(h\) the mean is about \(3\,\sigma_0 - 3{,}5\,\sigma_0\,h/a\): half the slope of the curve at the peak (\(-7\,\sigma_0/a\)), because the mean over an interval of length \(h\) is about the value at its middle. At \(h = a/8\) this approximation gives \(2{,}56\,\sigma_0\) instead of \(2{,}635\,\sigma_0\). The shortfall is therefore proportional to \(h/a\), and for \(2\,\%\) of the peak (\(0{,}06\,\sigma_0\)) a constant stress in the element would need \(h\) of about \(a/58\) (the exact mean gives \(a/57\), SymPy). This is why the plan for the plate uses higher-order elements or stress recovery (section 17.4), and refines the mesh towards the hole and not everywhere. The conclusion holds for elements with constant stress and does not say how much error the elements of chapter 15 would have.

The remaining steps of the plan follow the tube: a plane stress model of a quarter of the plate with mirror conditions on the axis planes (the idea of lemma 7.39, in general chapter 19), the question (the largest stress at a point at the hole, hence a mesh as fine as for stresses, section 17.9.2), the hand calculation (Kirsch's factor \(3\), the correction for the finite width is not computed, section 11.13.6), the check (the resultant of the forces on the edge \(x = \pm 100\) mm is \(\sigma_0 \cdot 200\ \text{mm} \cdot t\), like the reactions of the truss), the sequence of meshes and theorem 17.14, and the critique (the deviation of the value at the edge of the hole from the free-edge condition, as for the tube). Whoever gets a value near \(150\) MPa from the computation must not conclude that the mesh error is small: the difference from Kirsch's value also contains the effect of the finite width, which the book estimates only for the load on the edge (\(2{,}5\,\%\)), not for the stress at the hole. So we judge the mesh from three meshes and not from the comparison with \(3\sigma_0\).

17.9.8 What we have shown

The plan of an analysis is seven steps: question and tolerance, hand calculation, model, sequence of meshes, check, critique, decision (figure 17.28). On the tube we showed what each gives. The hand calculation hit the order of magnitude (\(60\) and \(75\) against \(78\) MPa), not \(2\,\%\) (with equation (17.44) and the Lamé field). The displacement and the stress converge at different speeds, \(0{,}017\,\%\) and \(1{,}43\,\%\) at \(n = 16\) (comparison of the columns of the table, figure 17.29). The check of the half-tube equilibrium holds on every mesh (lemma 17.47, proof with hat functions) and therefore does not find a coarse mesh, but it does find a wrong pressure if we compare it with the task (computed cases). The deviation of the boundary condition is six times larger than the error of the hoop stress (a computed case, not a law). Extrapolation with the observed order \(1\) gives \(94{,}01\) MPa against the exact \(94{,}02\) MPa. Larger than the error of \(1{,}43\,\%\) is the difference between open and closed ends, \(3{,}2\,\%\), and the mesh does not reduce it. The model is verified, not validated.

For the plate with a hole the plan is written without mesh numbers, because the book does not carry out the computation of this plate with the plane elements of chapter 15. The yardstick for the mesh, the mean of the exact stress over an element at the hole (\(56\,\%\) of the peak at \(h = a\)), is computed from Kirsch's solution.
