The lecture of this chapter (figures built as it is explained)

This chapter continues the vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom. In chapter 10 we wrote the equation of motion with a mass and a stiffness matrix, found the eigenpairs and showed that the response expands in modes of vibration (načini nihanja). In chapters 7 and 12 we built such matrices for bars and beams with finite elements. Such models can have thousands of coordinates, and the full solution is expensive for every new load.

The word reduction means making smaller: we replace the large model by a small one that keeps what interests us. What is large here and what is small is shown in figure 21.1 on the smallest example, bar C from section 10.8: a steel bar with modulus of elasticity \(E = 196\) GPa, length \(L = 1\) m and cross-section \(A = 100\) mm². The left end is clamped in a wall, and a force of \(1\) kN pulls at the free end. We divide the bar into sixteen equal pieces, and the dots are their joints, the nodes. Before loading, the end of the bar is at the grey dashed line, after it the bar is orange and its end has moved by \(FL/(EA) = 51{,}02\) µm, strongly magnified in the figure so that it can be seen. Every node moves, and the displacement of each is one coordinate of the model. We draw them above the axis of position \(x/L\): sixteen dots, from \(q_1\) at the wall to \(q_{16}\) at the end, and the last dot is the same \(51{,}02\) µm. This is the large model: sixteen unknowns here, thousands in a real machine.

The word modal says that the small model is built from modes of vibration, that is, from the shapes in which the system can vibrate (definition 8.140). Every position of the sixteen dots can be written as a sum of these shapes, each multiplied by one number (corollary 10.22): the first shape \(\boldsymbol\varphi_1\) with the number \(c_1\), the second with \(c_2\) and all higher ones together. Why does this work? There are as many shapes as coordinates, sixteen, and none of them can be put together from the others, so the sixteen numbers \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) say the same as the sixteen displacements \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\), only sorted differently. Solving the eigenproblem (lastni problem) is computing these modes and their frequencies. Each mode we keep is one unknown, one number \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) instead of all the node displacements, so the computation becomes shorter, but we cut something off and must know how much.

How much we may cut off depends on the load. Two cases are common, a force on the structure and a shaking support, and the figure shows the first. For the bar of the figure, the first mode alone gives \(41{,}39\) µm at the end, which is only \(81{,}12\) % of the static displacement of the end, so it matters to know where the rest goes. In the figure it has not vanished: \(4{,}63\) µm comes from the second mode and \(5{,}00\) µm from all higher ones together, \(41{,}39 + 4{,}63 + 5{,}00 = 51{,}02\).

Why does the first mode carry the most? We draw the answer in figure 21.2. On the axes, the position \(x/L\) is horizontal and the displacement vertical. On them we draw the first shape and the second shape, each multiplied by such a number that the end of the bar moves by the same length \(1\) in both. This is the shape \(\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)\), where \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)\) is the last component of the column \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), the displacement of the end. The first shape rises all the way from the wall to the end. The second first goes down and turns upwards at \(x \approx L/3\).

Below the two shapes we draw by how much each of the sixteen pieces stretches, as a bar: upwards when the piece lengthens, downwards when it shortens. The elongation of a piece is the difference of the displacements of its two nodes, so the elongations of all pieces from the wall to the end add up to the displacement of the end, which is \(1\) in both shapes. In the first shape all bars point upwards and their sum is \(1\). In the second, the first five pieces shorten, together by \(0{,}995\), and the other eleven lengthen by \(1{,}995\), so that the difference is again \(1\). The second shape therefore stretches and compresses the pieces by \(0{,}995 + 1{,}995 = 2{,}99\) in total, almost three times as much as the first.

A piece that stretches more resists more. By equation (7.5) a piece with elongation \(\Delta\) has the strain energy \(EA\,\Delta^2/(2h)\), where \(h = L/16\) is the length of the piece, so a piece with three times the elongation has nine times the energy. We add the energies of the pieces into the energy of the bar. Twice the energy of the shape with end \(1\) is

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{EA}{h}\sum_{\mathrm{e}=1}^{16}\Delta_{\mathrm{e}}^2 ,\]

where \(\Delta_{\mathrm{e}}\) is the elongation of piece \(e\), and it is \(1{,}233\) for the first and \(11{,}02\) for the second shape, in units of \(EA/L\). The ratio is \(8{,}94\), close to \(3^2 = 9\).

The share of mode \(r\) in the displacement of the end under a slow force is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2F/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) (lemma 10.69, section 10.10). In two steps we write it with the energy of the figure:

\[\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2F}{\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}} = \frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2F}{\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}} = \frac{F}{\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}} .\]

The first equality holds because the mode is \(\mat M\)-normalized, \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\) (theorem 10.21), so \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). The second holds because \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}\), and so \(\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}(L)^2\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\mat K\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}_{\mathrm{r}}\). Since \(F/(EA/L) = 51{,}02\) µm, the share is the static displacement of the end divided by the number of the figure. On the bars at the bottom right this is \(51{,}02/1{,}233 = 41{,}39\) µm for the first and \(51{,}02/11{,}02 = 4{,}63\) µm for the second mode, the same parts as above. The first mode carries the most because its shape stretches the pieces least and therefore has the least energy.

This split holds for a force that changes slowly compared with the vibration of the bar, that is, with a frequency far below the first natural frequency of the model with sixteen elements, \(f_1 = 1253{,}70\) Hz. Only then does every mode respond with its static part. Under a harmonic force with angular frequency \(\Omega\), the undamped response of mode \(r\) is larger by the factor \(1/(1 - \Omega^2/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2)\), where \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) is its natural angular frequency (section 10.10). At \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\) this is \(1/(1 - 0{,}25) = 1{,}333\) for the first mode, and the share grows to \(55{,}19\) µm. For the second, \(\omega_2^2 = 9{,}058\,\omega_1^2\), the factor is \(1/(1 - 0{,}25/9{,}058) = 1{,}028\), and the share grows only to \(4{,}76\) µm. For \(\Omega\) close to \(\omega_1\) the factor grows without bound, and there the response is set by damping. How we correct the cut-off modes under a faster force is told in section 21.1. For a machine on a vibrating support, for example a frame on a floor shaken by an earthquake or a neighbouring machine, we must know how much mass each mode really moves, so that we know which modes we may cut off (section 21.2).

The chapter promises three things. First, we will show how to correct the cut-off modes with a small computation: with accelerations and with an added mass at the support, and how to measure how much mass the modes capture. Then we will shrink the model without modes, by keeping chosen coordinates and by dividing a structure into parts that we then join again. Finally we will show three procedures for computing the modes themselves, from the simplest to the one used by large programs, and how damping changes in a shortened model. For every procedure we will say where it fails.
