Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.2 Stress in a bar

The axial force tells with what force one part of the bar pulls the other. But it does not yet tell how heavily the material is loaded. Take two equal thin steel bars with a cross section of area \(A\) and put them side by side. At both ends we fix them to a rigid plate and pull the plates apart with the force \(F\) (figure 2.7, left). How much does each bar carry?

  1. We call the axial forces of the bars \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). On the right plate act the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the forces of the bars, which pull it back, \(-N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The plate is in equilibrium, so \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\). The component \(x\) of this equation is \(F - N_1 - N_2 = 0\), so \(N_1 + N_2 = F\).
  3. The bars are equal and equally fixed, and the force pulls the plate in the middle between them. Nothing distinguishes one bar from the other, so they carry the same, \(N_1 = N_2\).
  4. From step 2, therefore, \(2N_1 = F\) and \(N_1 = N_2 = F/2\).

Now glue the bars together lengthwise, so that we get one bar with a cross section of area \(2A\) (figure 2.7, right). When we pull it with the force \(F\), both halves stretch as before, so the glue between them has nothing to carry, and each half still carries \(F/2\). The thick bar carries twice the force of one thin bar, but it also has twice the cross section. The material of both is loaded equally, since in both the same force falls on a unit of cross-section area, \((F/2)/A = F/(2A)\). We could also cut the thick bar into ten or a hundred thin lengthwise bars, and each would carry a share of the force proportional to its area. So for the material what counts is the force per unit area of the cross section.

Definition 2.6 Stress in a bar (napetost v palici)

Let \(N\) be the axial force in a cross section of a bar and \(A\) the area of this cross section before loading. The stress (napetost) in the bar at this cross section is the number

\[\sigma = \frac{N}{A} . \tag{2.4}\]

Source: CM §4.1 (PDF p. 180), ONATE1 (1.3) (PDF p. 38), where it is written as \(N = A\sigma\).

Stress is force per unit area (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180). The Greek letter \(\sigma\) (sigma) is its usual symbol. Stress has the sign of the axial force: positive means tension, negative compression.

The definition has two restrictions. First: the stress \(N/A\) is an average over the cross section. It tells how much force falls on a unit of area if the force is distributed evenly over the cross section. Near the ends of the bar, where grips or a rope hold it, this is not true. But books state Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo): two differently applied loadings with the same resultant and the same tendency to turn the body distribute the force almost equally far from the places where they act (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). SADD states the principle for a body that returns to its original shape after unloading, and warns that its routine use is not always justified for materials that respond differently in different directions or at different places (SADD, PDF pp. 113-114). Figure 2.8 shows this schematically on a bar pulled by pins at the middle of both ends. How far is far, the principle does not say. SADD itself points out that far and almost equally are only descriptive words without an exact bound. Far from the ends of the bar the force is therefore distributed over the cross section as it would be if we pulled evenly over the whole cross section at the ends, that is, evenly, and \(\sigma\) is the force per unit area at every point of the cross section. Why evenly? When the ends are pulled evenly, every piece of the bar between two cross sections is in equilibrium if an evenly distributed force of the same size pulls it at both ends in opposite directions and nothing acts on its side surface. SADD computes exactly this distribution for a long flat bar pulled evenly at its ends and uses it as an approximation far from the ends where the force is applied differently (SADD Example 8.1, (8.1.10), PDF p. 169). How to describe the force per unit area at a point when it is not even, we will see in section 2.5.

Second: for \(A\) we take the area of the cross section before loading. When a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about 0,3 thousandths (section 1.6), and the area of the cross section by 0,06 %. For steel the difference is negligible, for rubber it is not. Rubber strongly resists a change of volume (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121). When we stretch it by half its length, its volume, the product of the cross-section area and the length, stays almost the same, so the area decreases to \(A/1{,}5 = 0{,}667\,A\), by a third. Books therefore distinguish the force per unit area before loading and after it (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180). BOWER calls the force per unit area after loading the true stress (prava napetost), and the force per unit area before loading the nominal stress (nominalna napetost) (BOWER §2.2.4, PDF pp. 71-72). So our stress \(N/A\) is nominal.

Stress has the unit of force divided by area, newton per square metre, N/m². This unit is called the pascal (paskal), Pa (CM, PDF p. 188). The pascal is a small unit. A force of 1 N, about the weight of an apple, spread over a square metre gives a stress of 1 Pa. Stresses in steel are millions of times larger, so we use the megapascal, \(1\ \text{MPa} = 10^6\ \text{Pa}\). Since \(1\ \text{mm} = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\), \(1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\), and

\[1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} = \frac{1\ \text{N}}{10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 10^6\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = 1\ \text{MPa} .\]

So a stress in megapascals is the number of newtons per square millimetre. BOWER writes it in MN/m², meganewtons per square metre, which by the same computation is again MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). For still larger numbers we use the gigapascal, \(1\ \text{GPa} = 10^9\ \text{Pa}\).

We pull the steel bar with diameter 10 mm with the force 10 kN. The area of the cross section is \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) (section 2.1), the axial force \(N = 10\,000\) N, and the stress is

\[\sigma = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2} = 127{,}3\ \text{MPa} .\]

What does this number mean? We must compare it with what steel can bear. In section 1.1 we saw that after too large a stretch steel no longer returns to its original length. This happens when the stress exceeds a certain value, which is a property of the material.

Definition 2.7 Yield stress (meja tečenja)

The yield stress (meja tečenja) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) of a material is the stress in a bar of this material above which the bar remains permanently longer after unloading.

Source: BOWER §3.7.1 (PDF p. 142), Table 3.9 (PDF p. 156).

The yield stress is a number, a property of the material and not of the bar. Below it the bar returns to its original length after unloading, above it the bar stays permanently longer (figure 2.9). The name says that above it the material begins to yield, that is, to flow: it changes its shape permanently, somewhat like a thick liquid. BOWER warns that the yield stress is often hard to pin down exactly, because the relation between stress and strain bends only gradually, and that the values in its table are only rough (BOWER, PDF pp. 142 and 156). For mild steel BOWER gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). We defined the yield stress with a bar that we pull. Two criteria that tell when a material yields under loading in several directions at once we will write down in section 2.12.5 (BOWER §3.7, PDF pp. 140-161).

The stress of 127,3 MPa in our bar is 58 % of the yield stress. The bar holds and returns to its original length after unloading. The largest force that does not yet make this bar permanently longer is

\[F = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} A = 220\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 17\,280\ \text{N} ,\]

about the weight of 1,76 t. A bar with half the diameter, 5 mm, has a quarter of the area, \(19{,}63\ \text{mm}^2\), and under the same force of 10 kN the stress \(509{,}3\) MPa, more than twice the yield stress. This is the thin wire from the beginning of the chapter: the same force, but four times less material carrying it.

For the hanging bar the axial force is (2.3), and the stress is

\[\sigma(x) = \frac{N(x)}{A} = \frac{\rho g A\,(L - x)}{A} = \rho g\,(L - x) . \tag{2.5}\]

The area has cancelled. The stress in a hanging bar does not depend on its thickness (BARBER (1.33), PDF p. 32). Why? A bar with twice the cross-sectional area is twice as heavy, so each of its cross sections carries twice the weight, but it also has twice the area to carry that weight (figure 2.10). This is the same argument as for the two thin bars that together make a thick one.

At the top of a bar of length 10 m

\[\sigma(0) = \rho g L = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9{,}81\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 765\,180\ \text{Pa} = 0{,}765\ \text{MPa} ,\]

which is 288 times less than the yield stress. The units agree because \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 1\ \text{kg}/(\text{m}\,\text{s}^2)\). How long would a steel bar have to be so that under its own weight it just starts to stretch permanently at the top? The stress at the top, \(\rho g L\), must reach \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\), so

\[L = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}}{\rho g} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2} = 2875\ \text{m} ,\]

almost three kilometres, whatever the thickness.

In section 1.1 we wrote that steel stretched by more than about a thousandth of its length no longer returns to its original length. This limit and the yield stress are connected. For the same steel BOWER also gives the number 196 GPa, which tells how strongly steel resists stretching (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). The quotient

\[\frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 0{,}00112\]

is about this thousandth. The same number 196 GPa also connects the stress in the hanging bar with its strain. At the top the stress is \(0{,}765\) MPa, and

\[\frac{0{,}76518\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{0{,}76518 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} ,\]

which is exactly the strain at the top of the hanging bar from section 1.2, \(u'(0) = 2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The top, \(x = 0\), is an end of the bar, so this is a one-sided derivative, the limit of the difference quotient from the right (definition 1.9). It agrees elsewhere too: at the middle the stress is half as large, and so is the strain. Why the quotient of the stress and the number 196 GPa gives the strain, we will see in chapter 3, when we connect stress with strain. There we will also compute from the stress (2.5) the displacement \(u(x)\) of the hanging bar, which in chapter 1 we took as given.
