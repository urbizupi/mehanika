Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.2 Stress in a bar

The axial force tells with what force one part of the bar pulls the other. But it does not yet tell how heavily the material is loaded. Take two equal thin steel bars with a cross section of area \(A\) and put them side by side. At both ends we fix them to a rigid plate and pull the plates apart with the force \(F\) (figure 2.14, left). How much does each bar carry?

  1. We call the axial forces of the bars \(N_1\) and \(N_2\). On the right plate act the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the forces of the bars, which pull it back, \(-N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(-N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\).
  2. The plate is in equilibrium, so the force \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) equals in size the sum of the forces of both bars: \(F\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_1\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} - N_2\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \vek 0\). The component \(x\) of this equation is \(F - N_1 - N_2 = 0\), so \(N_1 + N_2 = F\).
  3. The bars are equal and equally fixed, and the force pulls the plate in the middle between them. Nothing distinguishes one bar from the other, so they carry the same, \(N_1 = N_2\).
  4. From step 2, therefore, for both bars \(2N_1 = F\) and \(N_1 = N_2 = F/2\).

Now glue the bars together lengthwise, so that we get one bar with a cross section of area \(2A\) (figure 2.14, right). When we pull it with the force \(F\), applied evenly over the whole cross section at both ends, as the plates did before, each half gets \(F/2\) at its end and stretches as before. So the glue between them has nothing to carry, and each half still carries \(F/2\). If we apply the force differently, for example only at the middle of the end, this does not hold near the end, but far from it it holds again, as we will see with Saint-Venant's principle below. The thick bar carries twice the force of one thin bar, but it also has twice the cross section. The material of both is loaded equally, since in both the same force falls on a unit of cross-section area, \((F/2)/A = F/(2A)\). We could also cut the thick bar into ten or a hundred thin lengthwise bars, and each would carry a share of the force proportional to its area. So for the material what counts is the force per unit area of the cross section.

From history

Engineers of the eighteenth century sought, by experiment and calculation, formulas for the force at which a structure fails (TIMO, PDF p. 82). In 1807, in his lectures on natural philosophy, Thomas Young was the first to write down a number that describes how a substance resists pulling and pressing (TIMO, PDF pp. 82 and 100). He gave it by means of a column of the same substance, standing on the ground and having the height \(H\) (figure 2.15). The weight of the column is as many times greater than the weight that shortens the bar as the length of the bar is greater than its shortening (TIMO, PDF p. 100). For a thicker bar this weight is greater, since a thicker bar, as we saw with two bars (figure 2.14), carries a greater force for the same change of shape. Young's weight is therefore proportional to the cross section of the bar (TIMO, PDF p. 100). Navier, in his book on strength of materials, which we know from chapter 1, put the question differently. He was interested in the limit up to which a structure after unloading returns completely to its original shape. Within it the change of shape is proportional to the force, so simple formulas can be derived, but beyond it the relation between force and change of shape becomes very complicated (TIMO, PDF p. 82). To describe a material it is therefore not enough to say what load it can still carry. One must also know the ratio of the load per unit of cross-sectional area to the elongation per unit of length (TIMO, PDF p. 82). For this ratio very small elongations have to be measured, and Navier could take very little from earlier experiments, so he made them himself, on the iron he used in building the Pont des Invalides in Paris (TIMO, PDF p. 82). Navier's definition of this ratio has remained in use (TIMO, PDF p. 82).

The number that describes a material must therefore not depend on the thickness of the bar. So it contains the force per unit of area, which is the same in the thin and the thick bar. How this idea is extended to every cut through every point of a body we shall see in section 2.5, and the number that describes the material in chapter 3.

Definition 2.6 Stress in a bar (napetost v palici)

Let \(N\) be the axial force in a cross section of a bar and \(A\) the area of this cross section before loading. The stress (napetost) in the bar at this cross section is the number

\[\sigma = \frac{N}{A} . \tag{2.4}\]

Source: CM §4.1 (PDF p. 180), ONATE1 (1.3) (PDF p. 38), where it is written as \(N = A\sigma\).

Stress is force per unit area (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180), for the thin bar \(F/(2A)\), and for the thick one the same. The Greek letter \(\sigma\) (sigma) is its usual symbol. Stress has the sign of the axial force: positive means tension, negative compression.

The definition has two restrictions. First: the stress \(N/A\) is an average over the cross section. It tells how much force falls on a unit of area if the force is distributed evenly over the cross section. Near the ends of the bar, where grips or a rope hold it, this is not true. But books state Saint-Venant's principle (Saint-Venantovo načelo): two differently applied loadings with the same resultant and the same tendency to turn the body distribute the force almost equally far from the places where they act (SADD §5.6, PDF p. 114). SADD states the principle for a body that returns to its original shape after unloading, and warns that its routine use is not always justified for materials that respond differently in different directions or at different places (SADD, PDF pp. 113-114). Figure 2.16 shows this schematically on a bar pulled by pins at the middle of both ends. In it, right at the end the force is gathered near the middle of the cross section, a little farther it is already more even, and far from the end it is almost even. The principle does not say how far is far: SADD itself points out that far and almost equally are only descriptive words without an exact bound. For a flat bar, however, we compute the distance in section 11.11.2, under the conditions listed there: of the part of the difference between the two distributions that decays most slowly with the distance from the end, at most about \(5\,\%\) of its largest value at the end remains at a distance of one cross-section height from the end. Far from the ends of the bar, when the resultant of the end force lies on the axis of the bar, as with the pins at the middle of the ends, the force is therefore distributed over the cross section approximately as it would be if we pulled evenly over the whole cross section at the ends, that is, approximately evenly, and \(\sigma\) is approximately the force per unit area at every point of the cross section.

What if the resultant does not lie on the axis? We draw two equal flat bars with the axis through the middle of the cross section. We pull the left bar by a pin on the axis, and far from the end the force is distributed evenly over the cross section, the stress is \(N/A\) everywhere. The right bar we grip off the axis, \(h/10\) above it, where \(h\) is the height of the cross section. The force then acts on a line above the axis, so the end load also has a tendency to turn the bar. An even distribution does not have this tendency, but the cross section far from the end must carry it, so there the stress is larger on the side of the grip than on the other: \(1{,}6\,N/A\) at the top edge, \(0{,}4\,N/A\) at the bottom one, and linear in between (equation (5.25) in chapter 5). An offset of a tenth of the cross-section height thus changes the stress at the edges by 60 %.

Why evenly? Far from the ends we cut a piece out of the bar between two cross sections. An even distribution is possible in equilibrium: the piece is in equilibrium if an evenly distributed force of the same size pulls it at both ends in opposite directions and nothing acts on its side surface. Equilibrium alone, however, shows only that this distribution is possible, not that it is the only one, since the same piece is in equilibrium under any other distribution that is the same on both sections. Every arrow on the left section has a partner of the same length on the same line on the right, so the two forces of a pair cancel and do not turn the piece. The proof comes only with the solution of the equations of elasticity, which needs the relation between stress and strain from chapter 3. SADD computes this distribution for a long flat bar pulled evenly at its ends and uses it as an approximation far from the ends where the force is applied differently (SADD Example 8.1, (8.1.10), PDF p. 169). How to describe the force per unit area at a point when it is not even, we will see in section 2.5.

Second: for \(A\) we take the area of the cross section before loading. We stretch a steel bar so that it gets longer by a thousandth of its length. Its thickness then decreases by about 0,3 thousandths (section 1.6). The cross section shrinks in both transverse directions by the same factor \(1 - 0{,}0003\), so the area decreases by \(1 - (1 - 0{,}0003)^2 \approx 0{,}0006\), that is, by \(0{,}06\,\%\). On a scale from 0 to 40 % this is a barely visible stroke. For steel the difference is negligible, for rubber it is not.

Rubber strongly resists a change of volume (BOWER §3.5, PDF pp. 120-121). When we pull a piece of rubber of length \(L\) with a cross section of area \(A\) so that it stretches by half its length, its volume, the product of the cross-section area and the length, stays almost the same, so the area decreases to \(A/1{,}5 = 0{,}667\,A\), by a third. Books therefore distinguish the force per unit area before loading and after it (CM §4.1, PDF p. 180). The force per unit area after loading is called the true stress (prava napetost) in elementary strength-of-materials courses (BOWER, PDF p. 71), and the force per unit area before loading BOWER calls the nominal stress (nominalna napetost) (BOWER §2.2.4, PDF p. 72). So our stress \(N/A\) is nominal.

Stress has the unit of force divided by area, newton per square metre, N/m². This unit is called the pascal (paskal), Pa (CM, PDF p. 188). The pascal is a small unit. A force of 1 N, about the weight of an apple, spread over a square metre gives a stress of 1 Pa. The same force on a square millimetre gives a million times larger stress, 1 MPa, as follows below. The vertical line in the middle separates the two scales, which are not the same. Stresses in steel are a hundred thousand to a few billion times larger, so we use the megapascal, \(1\ \text{MPa} = 10^6\ \text{Pa}\). Since \(1\ \text{mm} = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\), \(1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2\), and

\[1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} = \frac{1\ \text{N}}{10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} = 10^6\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = 1\ \text{MPa} .\]

So a stress in megapascals is the number of newtons per square millimetre. BOWER writes it in MN/m², meganewtons per square metre, which by the same computation is again MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). For still larger numbers we use the gigapascal, \(1\ \text{GPa} = 10^9\ \text{Pa}\).

We pull the steel bar with diameter 10 mm with the force 10 kN. The area of the cross section is \(A = 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2\) (section 2.1), the axial force \(N = 10\,000\) N, and the stress is

\[\sigma = \frac{10\,000\ \text{N}}{78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2} = 127{,}3\ \text{MPa} .\]

What does this number mean? We must compare it with what steel can bear. In section 1.1 we saw that after too large a stretch steel no longer returns to its original length. This happens when the stress exceeds a certain value, which is a property of the material.

Definition 2.7 Yield stress (meja tečenja)

The yield stress (meja tečenja) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\) of a material is the stress in a bar of this material above which the bar, pulled for the first time from an unloaded state with no permanent deformation, remains permanently longer after unloading.

Source: BOWER §3.7.1 (PDF p. 142), PDF p. 155, where it is called the initial yield stress (začetna meja tečenja), Table 3.9 (PDF p. 156).

The yield stress is a number, a property of the material and not of the bar. Below it the bar returns to its original length after unloading, above it the bar stays permanently longer (figure 2.23). If we unload the bar from the point \(B\) above the yield stress, it returns along a line parallel to the first one, and at zero stress a permanent strain remains. If the bar is then loaded again, it rises along the same parallel line and becomes permanently longer again only when the stress reaches the stress at point \(B\) (BOWER, PDF p. 142). So the stress above which the bar remains permanently longer depends on how the bar was loaded before, which is why the definition speaks of a bar pulled for the first time. The name says that above it the material begins to yield, that is, to flow: it changes its shape permanently, somewhat like a thick liquid. BOWER warns that the yield stress is often hard to pin down exactly, because the relation between stress and strain bends only gradually, and that the values in its table are only rough (BOWER, PDF pp. 142 and 156). For mild steel BOWER gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} = 220\) MPa (BOWER, Table 3.9, PDF p. 156). We defined the yield stress with a bar that we pull. Two criteria that tell when a material yields under loading in several directions at once we will write down in section 2.12.6 (BOWER §3.7, PDF pp. 140-161).

The stress of 127,3 MPa in our bar is 58 % of the yield stress. It lies on the straight line below the yield stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\), so the bar holds and after unloading returns approximately along the same line to its original length. Far from the ends of the bar, the largest force that does not yet make it permanently longer is

\[F = \sigma_{\mathrm{Y}} A = 220\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{mm}^2} \cdot 78{,}54\ \text{mm}^2 = 17\,280\ \text{N} ,\]

about 17 kN, the weight of 1,76 t. A bar with half the diameter, 5 mm, has a quarter of the area, \(19{,}63\ \text{mm}^2\), and under the same force of 10 kN the stress \(509{,}3\) MPa, more than twice the yield stress. This is the thin wire from the beginning of the chapter: the same force, but four times less material carrying it.

For the hanging bar the axial force is (2.3), and the stress is

\[\sigma(x) = \frac{N(x)}{A} = \frac{\rho g A\,(L - x)}{A} = \rho g\,(L - x) . \tag{2.5}\]

The area has cancelled, the depth \(x\) and the length \(L\) remain. The stress in a hanging bar does not depend on its thickness (BARBER (1.33), PDF p. 32). Why? A bar with twice the cross-sectional area is twice as heavy, so each of its cross sections carries twice the weight, but it also has twice the area to carry that weight (figure 2.24). This is the same argument as for the two thin bars that together make a thick one. At the middle of the length only half of the bar hangs below the cross section, so the axial force and the stress there are half of those at the top.

At the top of a bar of length 10 m

\[\sigma(0) = \rho g L = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9{,}81\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 765\,180\ \text{Pa} = 0{,}765\ \text{MPa} ,\]

which is 288 times less than the yield stress. The units agree because \(1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N}/\text{m}^2 = 1\ \text{kg}/(\text{m}\,\text{s}^2)\). How long would a steel bar have to be so that under its own weight it just starts to stretch permanently at the top? The stress at the top, \(\rho g L\), must reach \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}\), so

\[L = \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{Y}}}{\rho g} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{7800\ \text{kg/m}^3 \cdot 9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2} = 2875\ \text{m} ,\]

almost three kilometres, whatever the thickness.

In section 1.1 we wrote that steel stretched by more than about a thousandth of its length no longer returns to its original length. This limit and the yield stress are connected. For the same steel BOWER also gives the number 196 GPa, which tells how strongly steel resists stretching (Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). The quotient

\[\frac{220\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{220 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 0{,}00112\]

is about this thousandth. The same number 196 GPa also connects the stress in the hanging bar with its strain. At the top the stress is \(0{,}765\) MPa, and

\[\frac{0{,}76518\ \text{MPa}}{196\ \text{GPa}} = \frac{0{,}76518 \cdot 10^6\ \text{Pa}}{196 \cdot 10^9\ \text{Pa}} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} ,\]

which is exactly the strain at the top of the hanging bar from section 1.2, \(u'(0) = 2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). The top, \(x = 0\), is an end of the bar, so this is a one-sided derivative, the limit of the difference quotient from the right (definition 1.9). It agrees elsewhere too: at the middle the stress is half as large, and so is the strain. Why the quotient of the stress and the number 196 GPa gives the strain, we will see in chapter 3, when we connect stress with strain. There we will also compute from the stress (2.5) the displacement \(u(x)\) of the hanging bar, which in chapter 1 we took as given.
