Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.10 Connections

The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19. The chapter followed one path: the pose of the links (sections 27.2 to 27.4), velocity as its derivative (section 27.6), joint forces as the transfer along the same matrix (section 27.7) and the equations of motion from the kinetic energy (sections 27.8 and 27.9). Below, for each notion we say where it comes back and what stays outside.

  • Lagrange's equations and the rigid body of chapter 8 (DSK 13): the equations of motion \(\mat M(\vek q)\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\dot{\vek q} + \vek g(\vek q) = \vek\tau\) of theorem 27.56 are theorem 8.101 with kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12 \dot{\vek q}^\mathsf{T}\mat M\dot{\vek q}\), where by theorem 27.52 the mass matrix is the sum of the contributions of the links from theorem 8.97. For arm A in the pose \((30°, 60°)\) with the velocities \(\dot{\vek q} = (1;\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s the contribution of the first link is \(1{,}667\) J, of the second \(3{,}906\) J, and the sum \(5{,}573\) J is exactly \(\tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\mathsf{T}\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) with the mass matrix of section 27.9 (SymPy). On this route only the weight enters, through the potential, and the motor torques, as generalized forces. The force with which the links hold each other at the joint \(o_1\) does not appear, because the joint is an ideal constraint and the sum of the virtual works of its forces is zero (definition 8.3). This keeps the promise of chapter 8 that manipulators are treated with Lagrange's equations. For each link separately, the two equations of theorem 8.82 on the equations of motion of a rigid body would give the same. Since the theorem counts the constraint forces among the forces on the body, we then have to write the force and the torque at the joint on both links with opposite signs. This is the idea of the Newton-Euler procedure. The source derives it link by link and joins it into a recursion (SPONG, section 6.6, PDF pp. 220 to 230), but it only states that it is equivalent to Lagrange's equations (SPONG, PDF p. 236). We do not prove it.
  • Closed chains: lemma 27.4 holds only for a chain in which every joint adds one independent variable. In a closed chain, such as mechanism D in example 8.49 with three joint angles and one degree of freedom, the joint angles are not coordinates and the equations of motion of this chapter cannot be copied. Why not is seen when we open the chain at \(C\) and turn the second angle from \(-60°\) to \(30°\): the end goes to \(C'\), which is \(0{,}683\) m above the guide, and the mechanism cannot go there, because the slider keeps \(C\) on the guide. So the angle cannot be chosen freely, and a coordinate is exactly a number that can. Crank mechanisms, which are closed chains, are treated in chapter 23 (DSK).
  • Rotation matrices and frames: the orientation matrix \(\mat R^i{}_{\mathrm{j}}\) of definition 27.8 is by lemma 27.9 the transpose of the rotation matrix of definition 2.44, because it holds the axes of frame \(j\) in its columns and not in its rows. For a rotation by \(30°\) the first column is the pair of numbers \(0{,}866\) and \(0{,}5\) that the perpendiculars from the tip of the axis \(x_1\) cut off on the axes of frame 0, and the second column is \(-0{,}5\) and \(0{,}866\) for the axis \(y_1\). This is the promise of chapter 1 that matrices describing the rotations of robot links come back. The same matrix for the rotation of a bar and a beam element is in lemma 7.29 and lemma 12.23 (AK, chapters 7 and 12). Letters have several meanings in the book. Bold \(\mat R\) is the orientation here and the polar factor in chapter 2, \(\mat T\) is the homogeneous matrix while the slanted \(T\) stays the kinetic energy. Bold \(\mat J\) is the Jacobian matrix here, and the inertia tensor of a link is written \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) with the index \(G\). Bold \(\mat C\) is the matrix of Christoffel symbols here and the damping matrix in chapter 10. Without arguments \(g\) is the gravitational acceleration \(9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\), with an argument \(\vek g(\vek q)\) is the column of gravity torques.
  • Inverting a map: the local inverse of corollary 27.35 is theorem 8.34 applied to the forward map of the arm pose, and holds where the determinant of the Jacobian matrix is not zero. For the two-link arm this determinant is \(l_1 l_2 \sin q_2\) (lemma 27.48). The determinant is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns of the matrix, the velocities of the tip when a single joint turns. In the pose \((30°, 60°)\) the columns point in different directions and the area is \(1 \cdot 0{,}5 \cdot \sin 60° = 0{,}433\) m\(^2\). With the arm stretched out, \(q_2 = 0\), we have \(\vek j_1 = 3\,\vek j_2\), the parallelogram flattens into a segment of area zero, and the tip cannot move along the arm. There is no inverse there, because it would have to assign a turn of the joints to a small move of the tip in this direction, and no such turn exists. The general inverse, the arm with a spherical wrist and the pseudoinverse for redundant arms stay cited (SPONG, sections 5.2 to 5.4, PDF pp. 165 to 177, and section 4.11, PDF pp. 153 and 154).
  • Flexible links: chapter 28 (DSK) takes from this chapter the matrices \(\mat T^{i-1}{}_{\mathrm{i}}\) of section 27.4, the link Jacobian matrices of section 27.6, the equations of theorem 27.56 and the skew symmetry of theorem 27.60. Here the links are rigid (definition 1.11), the first idealization of the robot model: a tube that sags by \(1{,}9\) mm under a load of \(50\) N stays straight in the model, and the gripper is that far from where the model puts it. What changes when a link is not rigid is answered in that chapter.
  • Mass matrix and eigenvalues: lemma 27.64 applies theorems 10.21 and 8.27 to a mass matrix that is symmetric and positive definite at every configuration. For arm A both eigenvalues lie at every angle \(q_2\) between the bounds \(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}1661\) kg m\(^2\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{M}} = 11{,}5006\) kg m\(^2\). For a small oscillation about a rest configuration \(\vek q^*\) we would also need a stiffness, which we do not derive here, and the resulting system of chapter 10 is shortened by modes in chapter 21 (DSK).
  • Spinning bodies: the rotation of a body about its own axis and gyroscopic forces are treated in chapter 24 (DSK). In this chapter every link turns about the joint axis and is a rigid body. The term \(\mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\dot{\vek q}\) alone does work in general, since by theorem 27.60 its power is \(\dot{\vek q}^\mathsf{T}\mat C\dot{\vek q} = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\mathsf{T}\dot{\mat M}\dot{\vek q}\). Only the difference \(\big(\mat C - \tfrac12\dot{\mat M}\big)\dot{\vek q}\) does no work, and so by corollary 27.61 the power of the motors is exactly the derivative of the total energy. For arm A this is read off the figure: the power at time \(0{,}5\) s is \(155{,}96\) W, and the tangent to the energy curve at the same time has slope \(155{,}96\) J/s. In a rotor the same property is simpler: the gyroscopic matrix itself is skew symmetric, so the gyroscopic forces do no work (lemma 24.19).
  • Damping and friction: the model contains no joint friction and no inertia of the motor behind its gear, which with a ratio of \(100 : 1\) would add \(10^4\,J_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the inertia of the link (section 27.1). Damping models are treated in chapter 20 (DSK), the joint stiffness with an elastic transmission is in the source (SPONG, example 6.2 in section 6.1.1, PDF p. 191).
  • Control and path planning: from theorem 27.56 we get the torques for a given motion (inverse dynamics, the example of arm A in section 27.8). How to choose the motion and how to provide the torques is covered by the chapters of the source from the seventh on (SPONG) and not by this book. Corollary 27.61 is the property on which the passivity rests that the source uses in control (SPONG, section 6.5, PDF p. 217): the motors cannot take more energy from the robot than \(\beta\), the energy it has at the start above the lower bound of the potential.
  • Limits of the chapter: proved are the existence and uniqueness of the DH parameters, the columns of the Jacobian matrix, joint forces from virtual work, the equations of motion, skew symmetry and the bounds of the mass matrix. Cited without proof are the roll-pitch-yaw and axis-angle formulas, the general inverse kinematics, the analytical Jacobian, the pseudoinverse, manipulability ellipsoids, linearity in the inertia parameters and the Newton-Euler procedure with its equivalence. The masses of the links in the examples (10 kg and 5 kg) are ours and not from the source.
