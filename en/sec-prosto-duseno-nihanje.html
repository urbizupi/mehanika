Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.5 Free damped vibration

We strike the cantilever with the weight, system A of section 9.1, with a hammer. The weight gets the velocity \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s (an impulse of 5 N s on a mass of 50 kg, the blow itself is the subject of section 9.9), and from then on no external force acts on it any more. It starts to vibrate and the vibration slowly dies out. How fast (the envelope), with which period, and how much damping is enough for the vibration to disappear altogether? A motion without an external force, determined only by the initial displacement and the initial velocity, is called free motion (prosto gibanje).

Let us first look at the answer as a picture, the calculation comes after it (figure 9.42). On the horizontal axis is the time \(t\) in seconds, on the vertical one the displacement \(q\) of the weight in millimetres. The curve is the motion of the weight after the blow, as the calculation of this section will give it: the weight swings to and fro, and each swing is a little smaller than the one before. We must explain two things that the picture shows: the distance between neighbouring peaks, which is the period, and the dashed curves between which the swings get smaller.

Why does the weight swing at all instead of returning straight to the equilibrium? We follow it at three instants (figure 9.43). We draw the cantilever, clamped in the wall, schematically and the displacement strongly magnified. We move the weight up by \(q_0\) above the equilibrium position and release it. The weight is already balanced in the equilibrium position (example 9.9), so the sum of the force of the cantilever and the weight is the force \(k q_0\) towards the equilibrium, proportional to the displacement. The weight accelerates downwards. When it reaches the equilibrium, this force is zero, because the displacement is zero. But there the weight has a velocity, and the largest one, because the force accelerated it all the way there. A body on which no force acts does not change its velocity (Newton's first law), so the weight does not stop at the equilibrium but goes through it. On the other side the cantilever pulls it back up and slows it down until the weight stops. From there everything repeats in the opposite direction. This is vibration.

Why does the vibration decay? The damper acts with the force \(c\,|\dot q|\) against the velocity, and it does so during the whole motion, so it takes energy from the weight on its way. The weight therefore turns at the displacement \(q_1\), smaller than \(q_0\). If the damper is strong enough, the weight reaches the equilibrium so slowly that it does not pass it any more. Where this limit lies is the third question of the section.

In the linear model (9.8) (theorem 9.3) the free motion, \(Q = 0\), is a solution of the equation

\[m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = 0 , \qquad q(0) = q_0 , \quad \dot q(0) = \dot q_0 , \tag{9.45}\]

with \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\) and \(c \ge 0\). With the natural circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and the damping ratio \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\) (definitions 9.4 and 9.5) we write it as \(\ddot q + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,q = 0\). The term \(k\,q\) is the force of the cantilever, the term \(c\,\dot q\) the force of the damper. For system A, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{km} = 3541{,}75\) N s/m, and at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) we have \(c = 35{,}418\) N s/m.

The linear model has two assumptions here. The first is small displacements of the beam: within the beam model the remainder \(R\) of theorem 9.3 is zero for system A (example 9.9), and the largest displacement \(2{,}82\) mm that we will get is \(0{,}28\ \%\) of the length \(1\) m of the cantilever. At displacements comparable with the length, the beam model and with it this section no longer hold. The second is a damper with a constant \(c\) (definition 9.1). From it comes the fact that the swing gets smaller by the same fraction in every period, as we will show. With damping that is not proportional to the velocity this fraction is not constant, and the formulas of this section hold only approximately. How well, a measurement tells in section 9.14.

Sections 9.2 to 9.4 prepared three tools. The trial \(q = \mathrm{e}^{st}\) gives for the number \(s\) the characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom), which after division by \(m\) we write as

\[s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,s + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = (s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,(\zeta^2 - 1) \tag{9.46}\]

(we get the right-hand side by completing the square). The zeros are therefore \(s = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\). Let us draw them as points of the complex plane, divided by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (figure 9.44): on the horizontal axis is the real part, on the vertical one the imaginary part. The sign of the expression \(\zeta^2 - 1\) separates three cases. For \(\zeta < 1\) the root of a negative number is purely imaginary, \(\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} = \mathrm{i}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), because \(\mathrm{i}^2 = -1\) (section 9.3), and the zeros are complex conjugate numbers, points above the real axis and below it. For \(\zeta = 1\) there is a single zero, a double one, for \(\zeta > 1\) both are real. For system A at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) we have \(s = -0{,}35418 \pm 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s: the real part \(-0{,}35418\) is negative, so the motion dies out, and the imaginary part \(35{,}41574\) is a circular frequency, so it vibrates. In the figure these are the points \(-0{,}01 \pm 1{,}00\,\mathrm{i}\), just to the left of the imaginary axis. What remains is the question whether this trial captures every motion and how to choose the two constants for a given start. The answer is given by theorems 9.44 and 9.41.

For \(\zeta > 1\) the zeros are real and the solution is a sum of two exponential functions \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{x} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\) with \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\,t\). The constants \(a\) and \(b\) follow more easily from the initial values if we write the sum with two functions that have the values \(1\) and \(0\) at \(0\) and differentiate into each other, just as cosine and sine do for \(\zeta < 1\). We define these two functions as follows.

Definition 9.46 Hyperbolic cosine and sine (hiperbolični kosinus in sinus)

For every real number \(x\) the hyperbolic cosine (hiperbolični kosinus) and the hyperbolic sine (hiperbolični sinus) are the real numbers

\[\operatorname{cosh} x = \frac{\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x}}{2} , \qquad \operatorname{sinh} x = \frac{\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x}}{2} . \tag{9.47}\]

Source: CK (3.39), PDF p. 83, where the functions are used without a definition. The definition by the exponential function (definition 9.17) is not from the source.

What these two functions are is shown by figure 9.45. On the horizontal axis is \(x\), on the vertical one the values of the functions. We draw \(\mathrm{e}^{x}\), which grows, and \(\mathrm{e}^{-x}\), which falls. At \(x = 1\) they have the values \(2{,}718\) and \(0{,}368\), these are the ends of the vertical segment. The hyperbolic cosine is their midpoint, \(1{,}543\), and the hyperbolic sine is half the distance between them, \(1{,}175\). This gives the curve \(\operatorname{cosh}\), which runs everywhere between the two exponential curves, and the curve \(\operatorname{sinh}\), which passes through the origin. Conversely, \(\mathrm{e}^{x}\) is the midpoint plus half the distance and \(\mathrm{e}^{-x}\) the midpoint minus half the distance. Therefore every sum \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{x} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\) can be written with \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\), which is point 4 of the lemma below. At \(x = 0\) the functions have the values \(1\) and \(0\), just as cosine and sine do. This is where the words cosine and sine in the name come from. The word hyperbolic comes from the fact that \(\operatorname{cosh}^2 x - \operatorname{sinh}^2 x = (\operatorname{cosh} x + \operatorname{sinh} x)(\operatorname{cosh} x - \operatorname{sinh} x) = \mathrm{e}^{x}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x} = 1\): the point \((\operatorname{cosh} x, \operatorname{sinh} x)\) lies on the curve \(X^2 - Y^2 = 1\), which is called a hyperbola, just as the point \((\cos x, \sin x)\) lies on the circle \(X^2 + Y^2 = 1\).

Lemma 9.47 Properties of the hyperbolic functions (lastnosti hiperboličnih funkcij)

For every real number \(x\) and all real numbers \(a\), \(b\):

  1. The derivative of \(\operatorname{cosh}\) is \(\operatorname{sinh}\): \((\operatorname{cosh})'(x) = \operatorname{sinh} x\) and \((\operatorname{sinh})'(x) = \operatorname{cosh} x\).
  2. At \(x = 0\) we have \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = 0\).
  3. \(\operatorname{cosh} x > 0\), and for \(x \ge 0\) we have \(\operatorname{sinh} x \ge 0\).
  4. The middle and half the distance give every sum: \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{x} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x} = (a + b)\operatorname{cosh} x + (a - b)\operatorname{sinh} x\).
Proof

We must show four points. The first follows from the derivative of the exponential function, the second from \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\), the third from the exponential function being increasing, the fourth from expanding.

  1. By theorem 9.18 (point 2), \((\mathrm{e}^x)' = \mathrm{e}^x\). By the chain rule for a function of one variable (lemma 8.30, item 3) with the inner function \(x \mapsto -x\), \((\mathrm{e}^{-x})' = -\mathrm{e}^{-x}\). Hence \((\operatorname{cosh})' = (\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x})/2 = \operatorname{sinh}\) and \((\operatorname{sinh})' = (\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x})/2 = \operatorname{cosh}\). In the figure this is seen as follows: \(\operatorname{cosh}\) falls where \(\operatorname{sinh}\) is negative and grows where it is positive, while \(\operatorname{sinh}\) grows everywhere, because \(\operatorname{cosh}\) is positive everywhere.
  2. By theorem 9.18 (point 1), \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\), so \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = (1 + 1)/2 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = (1 - 1)/2 = 0\), the two points on the vertical axis.
  3. The exponential function has positive values (definition 9.17), so \(\operatorname{cosh} x > 0\): the midpoint of two positive numbers is positive. For \(x \ge 0\) we have \(-x \le x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-x} \le \mathrm{e}^x\), because the exponential function is increasing (theorem 9.18, point 1), so \(\operatorname{sinh} x \ge 0\): to the right of the origin the curve \(\mathrm{e}^{x}\) lies above the curve \(\mathrm{e}^{-x}\).
  4. The sum \((a + b)(\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x}) + (a - b)(\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x})\) equals \(2a\,\mathrm{e}^x + 2b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\). By definition the first expression equals \(2(a + b)\operatorname{cosh} x + 2(a - b)\operatorname{sinh} x\), so the identity follows after division by \(2\). For \(a = 1\), \(b = 0\) this is the midpoint plus half the distance, \(\mathrm{e}^x = \operatorname{cosh} x + \operatorname{sinh} x\).

Source: The lemma is not from the source. CK (3.39) uses the same notation, without deriving the properties.

Theorem 9.48 Free damped vibration (prosto dušeno nihanje)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\) and \(c \ge 0\) be real numbers, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\). Let \(q(t)\) be a solution of equation (9.45) with the initial values \(q(0) = q_0\) and \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\). This solution exists and is unique for every \(t \ge 0\).

  1. Underdamping (podkritično dušenje), \(\zeta < 1\). With the damped circular frequency (dušena krožna frekvenca) \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\),
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\left( q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \frac{\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t \right) . \tag{9.48}\]
  2. Critical damping (kritično dušenje), \(\zeta = 1\):
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl( q_0 + (\dot q_0 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)\,t \bigr) . \tag{9.49}\]
  3. Overdamping (nadkritično dušenje), \(\zeta > 1\). With \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) and the functions of definition 9.46,
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\left( q_0\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + \frac{\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0}{\omega_*}\,\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t \right) . \tag{9.50}\]

For \(\zeta = 0\), where the zeros lie on the imaginary axis, we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and (9.48) is \(q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + (\dot q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), the solution of theorem 8.138. The damped period (dušeni nihajni čas) is \(T_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\).

Proof

We must show that each formula solves the equation and fits the start. Uniqueness is given by theorem 9.41, so the solution we find is the only one.

  1. From \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) we get \(2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = (c/\sqrt{km})\sqrt{k/m} = c/m\), so after division by \(m\) we obtain \(\ddot q + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 q = 0\) with the characteristic polynomial (9.46).
  2. For \(\zeta < 1\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} > 0\) and the polynomial is \((s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2\), with the zeros \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) (because \((\pm\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}})^2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2\)). By corollary 9.45 all real solutions have the form \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + b\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\) with real constants \(a\) and \(b\). Its derivative is \(\dot q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl((-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + \omega_{\mathrm{d}} b)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - (\omega_{\mathrm{d}} a + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} b)\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\bigr)\). At \(t = 0\) we have \(q = a\) and \(\dot q = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + \omega_{\mathrm{d}} b\). The requirement \(q(0) = q_0\), \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\) gives \(a = q_0\) and \(b = (\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), which is (9.48).
  3. For \(\zeta = 1\) the polynomial is \((s + \omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\) with the double zero \(-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). By theorem 9.44 and corollary 9.45 all real solutions are \(q = (a + b\,t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) with real \(a\) and \(b\). At \(t = 0\) we have \(q = a\) and \(\dot q = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + b\), so \(a = q_0\) and \(b = \dot q_0 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0\). This is (9.49).
  4. For \(\zeta > 1\) we have \(\omega_* > 0\) and the polynomial \((s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 - \omega_*^2\) has the two distinct real zeros \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \omega_*\). By theorem 9.44 and corollary 9.45, \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(a\,\mathrm{e}^{\omega_* t} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_* t})\) with real \(a\) and \(b\). By lemma 9.47 (point 4) we write this as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(A\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + B\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t)\) with \(A = a + b\) and \(B = a - b\). By the same lemma (points 1 and 2), \((\operatorname{cosh})' = \operatorname{sinh}\), \((\operatorname{sinh})' = \operatorname{cosh}\), \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = 0\). At \(t = 0\) we therefore have (the derivative with respect to \(t\) gives the factor \(\omega_*\)) \(q = A\) and \(\dot q = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} A + \omega_* B\), so \(A = q_0\) and \(B = (\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)/\omega_*\). This is (9.50).
  5. Each formula, that is each of the three curves, is a solution of the equation with the given start. By theorem 9.41 it is the only such solution.

Source: Formulas: CK (3.26) to (3.39), PDF pp. 80 to 83 (the critical case (3.36) on PDF p. 83, the overdamped case (3.39) in the form with \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\), \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) by (3.37), checked on the images of the formulas), KREYSZIG §2.4, PDF pp. 91 to 93. The proof by steps is not from the source: CK and KREYSZIG derive the formulas with the trial \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\), without showing that it captures every solution.

The theorem showed that every free motion is one of three forms that are determined only by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta\), and the start enters only through two constants (in front of the cosine and sine, in front of \(1\) and \(t\), or in front of \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\)). The underdamped case tells us what we see in system A: a cosine and a sine with the damped circular frequency, multiplied by a decreasing exponential function. In the other two cases there is no vibration, because a solution that is not \(0\) all the time passes through \(0\) at most once. For \(\zeta = 1\) the decreasing exponential function is multiplied by the polynomial \(a + b\,t\), which has at most one zero. For \(\zeta > 1\) the bracket equals \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{\omega_* t} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_* t}\) (lemma 9.47, point 4) and is zero exactly when \(\mathrm{e}^{2\omega_* t} = -b/a\), which holds for at most one \(t\) because the exponential function is increasing.

Example 9.49 Hammer blow on system A (udarec s kladivom na sistemu A)

The start is \(q_0 = 0\), \(\dot q_0 = 0{,}1\) m/s, the damping ratio is \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) (CK gives for structures "0,5 % to 5 %", PDF p. 82, the value 0,01 is our choice, how to measure it is shown in section 9.14). By (9.48),

\[q(t) = \frac{\dot q_0}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\,\sin(35{,}4157\,t) ,\]

with the time \(t\) in seconds. First we look at what the damping changed in the frequency. The damped frequency is \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 35{,}4157\) 1/s, the undamped one \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, the difference is \(0{,}005\ \%\). In the figure the damped period \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the distance between the first two peaks. The reason is that \(\zeta\) enters only as a square, \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), and at \(\zeta^2 = 10^{-4}\) we have \(1 - \sqrt{1 - 10^{-4}} = 0{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\). The damped period is \(T_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}177412\) s, the undamped one \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}177403\) s. The difference \(0{,}009\) ms is much smaller in the figure than the thickness of the line. For weak damping the formula therefore shows almost the same frequency as the undamped pendulum. Damping acts on the amplitude, not on the frequency.

The amplitude is bounded by the envelope (section 9.2) \(\pm 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\): the curve touches it when \(\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = \pm 1\) (KREYSZIG PDF p. 93), in between it lies below it (figure 9.42). The constant \(2{,}82\) mm is \(\dot q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\). The first peak is at \(2{,}78\) mm, a little below it, because by a quarter of a period the envelope has already fallen somewhat. The envelope falls to one half when \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = 1/2\), that is after \(t = \ln 2/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}69315/0{,}35418 = 1{,}957\) s, or after \(1{,}957/0{,}177412 = 11{,}03\) periods. It falls below \(1\ \%\) after \(\ln 100/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 13{,}00\) s, that is after 73 periods. The figure shows the first two seconds, up to there it is six and a half of its lengths. The blow therefore dies out only after 13 s, when more than 70 periods have passed since it.

Free motion in underdamping therefore has two components: vibration with the damped frequency and decay with an exponential envelope. We compare three values of \(\zeta\) for the start \(q_0 = 1\) mm and \(\dot q_0 = 0\), that is, a displaced weight that we release.

The next corollary answers three questions at once: by how much the vibration decays in one period, where the energy goes, and why \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{km}\) is the smallest damping at which the weight, starting from rest, does not pass the equilibrium.

Corollary 9.51 Decay, energy and critical damping (pojemanje, energija in kritično dušenje)

Let \(q\) be a free motion as in theorem 9.48.

  1. For \(\zeta < 1\) and for any two neighbouring peaks and every \(t\),
    \[q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,q(t) , \tag{9.51}\]
    so the ratio of the values one damped period apart is independent of the start and of \(t\). The same ratio holds for \(\dot q\).
  2. The energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2 + \tfrac12 k q^2\), which the damper takes away, has the derivative \(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}/\mathrm{d}t = -c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\). For \(\zeta < 1\), \(\mathcal{E}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \mathrm{e}^{-4\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,\mathcal{E}(t)\).
  3. Let \(q_0 > 0\) and \(\dot q_0 = 0\). For \(\zeta < 1\), \(q(t) = \dfrac{q_0}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha)\), where \(\alpha \in [0, \pi/2)\) is the angle with \(\sin\alpha = \zeta\). The motion crosses the equilibrium at \(t = (\alpha + \pi/2)/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), and its first minimum, the first overshoot, is \(q(\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}) = -q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\). For \(\zeta \ge 1\), \(q(t) > 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\), the curves stay above the axis.
  4. The slower part of the response (the zero closer to the imaginary axis) decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta < 1\), as \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta = 1\) and as \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta > 1\). The decay exponent \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) grows with \(\zeta\) up to \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) at \(\zeta = 1\), and above that \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\omega_{\mathrm{n}} < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and it falls.
Proof

We show points 1 and 2 from the form of the solution and from the equation, point 3 with a form that we get from the formulas of theorem 9.48, point 4 from the exponents.

  1. On the record the shift by \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) is the step from one peak to the next. Cosine and sine have the period \(2\pi\) (lemma 8.11, point 4), so \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + 2\pi) = \cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\), and the same for sine. The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is multiplied by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{d}}}\) under a shift by \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 9.18, \(\mathrm{e}^{a + b} = \mathrm{e}^a\mathrm{e}^b\)). Because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{d}}\zeta = 2\pi\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), (9.51) follows. Because \(q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r\,q(t)\) holds for every \(t\) with the same number \(r\), differentiating both sides (chain rule for a function of one variable, lemma 8.30, item 3, with the inner function \(t \mapsto t + T_{\mathrm{d}}\)) gives also \(\dot q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r\,\dot q(t)\).
  2. The derivative of the energy is \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = m\dot q\ddot q + k q\dot q = \dot q\,(m\ddot q + k q)\). By equation (9.45), \(m\ddot q + k q = -c\dot q\), so \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\): the force of the damper \(c\,|\dot q|\) points against the velocity, so its power is force times velocity with a minus sign. This is equation (8.133) of theorem 8.121 with the non-conservative force \(-c\dot q\), which has the power \(-c\dot q^2\). For \(\zeta < 1\), by point 1 both terms of the energy are multiplied by \(r^2\), so \(\mathcal{E}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r^2\mathcal{E}(t)\) with \(r^2 = \mathrm{e}^{-4\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\).
  3. We write the underdamped curve as a cosine with a phase \(\alpha\). The angle \(\alpha \in [0, \pi/2)\) exists by theorem 2.79, because \(\sin 0 = 0 \le \zeta < 1 = \sin(\pi/2)\), and \(\cos\alpha = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} > 0\) (lemma 8.11). By the addition formulas (corollary 8.16), \(\cos(x - \alpha) = \cos\alpha\cos x + \sin\alpha\sin x = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\cos x + \zeta\sin x\). For \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\) we therefore have \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\dfrac{q_0}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,q_0\bigl(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \tfrac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\bigr)\), which is (9.48) with \(\dot q_0 = 0\), because \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\).
  4. For \(t \ge 0\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha \ge -\alpha > -\pi/2\), so \(\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha) > 0\) as long as \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha < \pi/2\). At \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha = \pi/2\) the cosine is zero and changes sign there, this is the first passage through the equilibrium. The derivative of the solution is, by the formula in the proof of theorem 9.48, step 2, with \(\dot q_0 = 0\) and \(a = q_0\), \(b = \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\): \(\dot q = -\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(q_0\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\), because \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2 + \zeta^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). It has zeros at \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = j\pi\), the first at \(t = \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), and there \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}(\cos\pi + (\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\sin\pi) = -q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\). The derivative is negative on \((0, \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) and positive on \((\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}, 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\), so this is the first minimum, the first overshoot.
  5. For \(\zeta = 1\), \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(1 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t) > 0\) for \(t \ge 0\) ((9.49) with \(\dot q_0 = 0\)), a curve that stays above the axis.
  6. For \(\zeta > 1\), \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl(\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + (\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_*)\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t\bigr)\) ((9.50)). By lemma 9.47 (point 3), for \(t \ge 0\) we have \(\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t > 0\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t \ge 0\), the coefficient \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_*\) is positive, so the sum in the bracket is positive, this curve also stays above the axis.
  7. For \(\zeta > 1\) we have \(\omega_* < \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(\omega_*^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(\zeta^2 - 1) < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\zeta^2\). The terms in (9.50) decay as \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} - \omega_*)t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} + \omega_*)t}\), the first one is slower with the exponent \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Because \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})(\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}) = \zeta^2 - (\zeta^2 - 1) = 1\), we have \(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} = 1/(\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\). The denominator is larger than \(1\) and grows with \(\zeta\), so the quotient is smaller than \(1\) and falls: this is the zero that returns along the real axis towards the origin, at \(\zeta = 2\) it is at \(-0{,}268\). For \(\zeta < 1\) the exponent is \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and it grows with \(\zeta\). This is point 4.

The plane \((q, \dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) is called the phase plane (fazna ravnina): each point is a state determined by the displacement and the velocity, and a motion is the path of the point. In it the same decay is seen from another side (figure 9.48). Both paths in the figure start at the point \((q_0, 0)\), a displacement without velocity. In the undamped system the energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 k\bigl(q^2 + (\dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\bigr)\) stays constant (theorem 8.138, step 5), which is the equation of a circle, since \(\tfrac12 m\dot q^2 = \tfrac12 k(\dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\). The point \((q, \dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) moves along the circle. With damping, by point 2 of corollary 9.51, \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -c\dot q^2 \le 0\), so the point crosses the circles inwards, and the path is a spiral.

So far we had \(c \ge 0\) and \(k > 0\). Section 8.12 showed that damping does not endanger stability, and section 8.13 found for \(k < 0\) a motion that moves away from the equilibrium. But what about \(c < 0\) or \(k = 0\)? Negative \(c\) is a model of a force that supports the motion instead of braking it, for example an air flow that supplies energy to a vibrating wing. CK says that avoiding such a growing vibration (flutter) is essential in the design of all airplanes (PDF p. 86). One statement treats all combinations of the signs of \(c\) and \(k\). For it we distinguish a rest to which the motion returns from a rest that the motion merely does not leave. The second is the stability of section 8.12, the first gets its name in the next definition.

Definition 9.52 Asymptotically stable equilibrium (asimptotično stabilno ravnovesje)

Let \(\vek q_0\) be a stable equilibrium of a system with \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) by definition 8.130. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable (asimptotično stabilno) if there is a number \(\delta_0 > 0\) such that for every motion that is defined for every \(t \ge 0\) and has \(|\vek q(0) - \vek q_0| < \delta_0\) and \(|\dot{\vek q}(0)| < \delta_0\) we have \(\vek q(t) \to \vek q_0\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(t) \to \vek 0\) as \(t \to \infty\).

Source: The definition is not from the source. CK §3.3 (PDF p. 85) classifies the motion of the equation \(a\ddot u + b\dot u + \dots\) by the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation and says about asymptotically stable motion: "If both roots of the characteristic equation lie in the left half-plane ... with time the motion will die out".

Equation (9.45) is the Lagrange equation of a system with the kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2\), the potential \(V = \tfrac12 k q^2\) and the generalized force \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\dot q\), also when \(c < 0\) or \(k < 0\) (theorem 8.101). The constant \(q = 0\) is its solution, thus an equilibrium, and definitions 8.130 and 9.52 judge it with \(n = 1\) and \(\vek q_0 = 0\). By theorem 9.41 the solutions exist for all times, so the conditions "on every \([0, \tau]\)" and "for every \(t \ge 0\)" mean the same.

Theorem 9.53 Stability of a linear system with one degree of freedom (stabilnost linearnega sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

Let \(m > 0\) and let \(c\) and \(k\) be arbitrary real numbers. The equilibrium \(q = 0\) of the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = 0\) is

  1. asymptotically stable exactly when \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\),
  2. stable but not asymptotically stable exactly when \(c = 0 < k\) or \(k = 0 < c\). In the first case the motion is a bounded vibration that does not decay, in the second \(q(t)\) tends to the constant \(q_0 + m\dot q_0/c\), which depends on the start,
  3. unstable in all other cases: for \(k < 0\), for \(c < 0\) and \(k \ge 0\), and for \(c = k = 0\). For \(k < 0\) the polynomial \(m s^2 + c s + k\) has a positive zero \(s_1\) and a negative one \(s_2\), and every solution with \(\dot q_0 \ne s_2 q_0\) grows exponentially without vibration (divergence, divergenca). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 < 4mk\) every solution that is not \(0\) all the time is a vibration with an exponentially growing amplitude (flutter), for \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 \ge 4mk\) an exponential growth without vibration. For \(c < 0 = k\) every solution with \(\dot q_0 \ne 0\) grows exponentially, while the solution with \(\dot q_0 = 0\) is the constant \(q_0\). For \(c = k = 0\) we have \(q = q_0 + \dot q_0 t\).
Proof

Idea: every motion is a combination of two special solutions \(u\) and \(v\), so only these two decide about stability. Then we compute them case by case, as separated by the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation.

  1. Let \(u\) be the solution with \(u(0) = 1\), \(\dot u(0) = 0\) and \(v\) the solution with \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v(0) = 1\) (they exist for every \(t \ge 0\) by theorem 9.41). The solution with \(q(0) = q_0\), \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\) is \(q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\), because by lemma 9.6 this function is a solution and has the same start, and uniqueness holds by theorem 9.41. For \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\) we know both: \(u\) is the released weight, \(v\) is the weight after the blow, divided by the initial velocity. Hence \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\) and \(\dot q = q_0\dot u + \dot q_0\dot v\).
  2. The equilibrium is stable exactly when \(u\), \(\dot u\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) are bounded on \([0, \infty)\). On the record the bound \(M\) is a horizontal line below which the envelope stays. If they are bounded by \(M \ge 1\), for a given \(\varepsilon\) we take \(\delta = \varepsilon/(2M)\): for a start with \(|q_0| < \delta\), \(|\dot q_0| < \delta\) we have \(|q| \le M(|q_0| + |\dot q_0|) < 2M\delta = \varepsilon\) and the same for \(\dot q\). Conversely, if the equilibrium is stable, we take \(\varepsilon = 1\) and the corresponding \(\delta\). The motion with the start \((\delta/2, 0)\) has \(|\delta u(t)/2| < 1\) and \(|\delta\dot u(t)/2| < 1\) for every \(t \ge 0\), so \(u\) and \(\dot u\) are bounded. The same with the start \((0, \delta/2)\) for \(v\) and \(\dot v\).
  3. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable exactly when \(u\), \(\dot u\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) tend to zero, like the envelope. If they do, they are bounded, so the equilibrium is stable, and for every start \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v \to 0\) and \(\dot q \to 0\). Conversely, the starts \((\delta_0/2, 0)\) and \((0, \delta_0/2)\) give that \(u\), \(\dot u\) and \(v\), \(\dot v\) tend to zero.
  4. Let \(s_1\), \(s_2\) be the zeros of the polynomial \(P(s) = m s^2 + c s + k = m(s - s_1)(s - s_2)\). By expanding the product, \(s_1 + s_2 = -c/m\) and \(s_1 s_2 = k/m\). For \(c^2 < 4mk\) the zeros are conjugate, \(\sigma \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega\) with \(\sigma = -c/(2m)\) and \(\omega > 0\), and \(k > 0\) holds automatically. For \(c^2 \ge 4mk\) they are real. We claim: both zeros have a negative real part, so they lie to the left of the imaginary axis, exactly when \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\). In the complex case the real part \(-c/(2m)\) is negative exactly when \(c > 0\), and \(k > 0\) already holds. In the real case both zeros are negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product positive (equal signs, the sum tells which), that is \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\).
  5. The case \(c > 0\), \(k > 0\), region \(1\). By step 3 we must show that \(u\), \(v\) and their derivatives tend to zero, and the zeros have a negative real part by step 4. By theorem 9.44 and corollary 9.45, \(u\) and \(v\) are linear combinations of the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\cos\omega t\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\sin\omega t\), or \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\), or \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) and \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\), with the real part of the zero negative. The derivative of each of these functions is a combination of the same functions (the rules for the derivative of a product and the derivative of sine and cosine, theorem 8.13). All tend to zero, because \(|\cos| \le 1\), \(|\sin| \le 1\) and \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-at} \to 0\) for \(a > 0\) (lemma 9.20). The equilibrium is asymptotically stable.
  6. The case \(c = 0 < k\), the vertical half-axis \(2\). Let \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\). By theorem 8.138, \(u = \cos\omega t\) and \(v = (\sin\omega t)/\omega\), with the derivatives \(-\omega\sin\omega t\) and \(\cos\omega t\). All are bounded, the equilibrium is stable (step 2). Because \(u(2\pi j/\omega) = 1\) for every integer \(j\), \(u\) does not tend to zero, and the equilibrium is not asymptotically stable (step 3).
  7. The case \(k = 0 < c\), the horizontal half-axis \(2\). The zeros are \(0\) and \(-c/m\). The functions \(u = 1\) and \(v = (m/c)\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-ct/m}\bigr)\) are solutions: for \(v\) we have \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v = \mathrm{e}^{-ct/m}\) with \(\dot v(0) = 1\), and \(m\ddot v + c\dot v = -c\,\mathrm{e}^{-ct/m} + c\,\mathrm{e}^{-ct/m} = 0\). The functions \(u\), \(\dot u = 0\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) are bounded, the equilibrium is stable. Because \(u = 1\) does not tend to zero, it is not asymptotically stable. The solution \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\) tends to \(q_0 + m\dot q_0/c\).
  8. The unstable cases, region \(3\). We show that \(v\) is not bounded, and by step 2 the equilibrium is not stable. For \(k < 0\) we have \(s_1 s_2 < 0\), so the polynomial has a positive zero \(s_1\) and a negative one \(s_2\). The function \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t})/(s_1 - s_2)\) has \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v(0) = (s_1 - s_2)/(s_1 - s_2) = 1\), it is a solution by theorem 9.44, and it tends to \(\infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 < 4mk\) we have \(v = \mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}(\sin\omega t)/\omega\) with \(\sigma > 0\) (we check the start as in step 2 of the proof of theorem 9.48) and at \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = (\pi/2 + j\pi)/\omega\) we have \(|v| = \mathrm{e}^{\sigma t_{\mathrm{j}}}/\omega \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 > 4mk\) the zeros are distinct and positive (\(s_1 s_2 > 0\), \(s_1 + s_2 > 0\)), and \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t})/(s_1 - s_2) \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 = 4mk\) there is a double zero \(s = -c/(2m) > 0\) and \(v = t\,\mathrm{e}^{st} \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 = k\) the zeros are \(0\) and \(s = -c/m > 0\), \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{st} - 1)/s \to \infty\). For \(c = k = 0\) we have \(v = t\) and \(u = 1\). The description of all solutions in point 3: for \(k < 0\) we have \(q = A\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} + B\,\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\), from \(A + B = q_0\) and \(s_1 A + s_2 B = \dot q_0\) it follows that \(A = (\dot q_0 - s_2 q_0)/(s_1 - s_2)\), and for \(A \ne 0\) we have \(|q| \ge |A|\,\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - |B| \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) both zeros have a positive real part (steps 4 and 8), so every nonzero term of the solution grows. For \(c < 0 = k\) we have \(m\ddot q + c\dot q = 0\), thus \(\dot q = \dot q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-ct/m}\), which grows exactly when \(\dot q_0 \ne 0\).
  9. The pairs \((c, k)\) split into the three exhausted groups of steps 5, 6 and 7 (\(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\), \(c = 0 < k\), \(k = 0 < c\)) and all others: \(k < 0\) for every \(c\), \(c < 0 \le k\) and \(c = k = 0\). All pairs of the second group are in step 8, which is point 3. Region \(1\), the half-axes \(2\) and region \(3\) together cover the whole plane.

The proof showed that the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation decides stability, not the start (figure 9.44). The figure shows the path of the zeros \(s/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) for \(k > 0\) as \(\zeta\) grows from \(0\) to \(2\). The underdamped pair lies on the unit circle, because by (9.46) \(|s/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}|^2 = \zeta^2 + (1 - \zeta^2) = 1\). At \(\zeta = 1\) the zeros meet at \(-1\), and the overdamped zeros separate along the real axis. Both zeros in the left half-plane (\(\operatorname{Re} s < 0\)) mean asymptotic stability, a zero in the right half-plane (\(\operatorname{Re} s > 0\)) means instability. On the imaginary axis the multiplicity also decides: a conjugate pair \(\pm\mathrm{i}\,\omega\) and a simple zero \(0\) with the other zero in the left half-plane give stability, but a double zero \(0\) (\(c = k = 0\)) does not, because the solution \(q_0 + \dot q_0 t\) runs away.

Example 9.54 Inverted bar above the critical force (obrnjena palica nad kritično silo)

Example 8.74 of section 8.6: a massless rigid bar of length \(L = 1\) m, pinned at the bottom, a torsional spring \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 2000\) N m, a vertical force \(P\) downwards at the top, \(\theta\) measured from the vertical upwards. In that chapter it was a model of a column, here we add motion to it. By theorem 9.44, \(\theta = a\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta t} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t}\). The linear equation holds only while \(\theta\) is small: at \(0{,}1\) rad, \(\sin\theta\) is smaller than \(\theta\) by \(0{,}17\ \%\) (lemma 8.20), later the model does not hold and the motion is governed by the nonlinear equation (section 8.6 found the curved equilibria). 9.5.1 Self-excited vibration and the limit cycle

Theorem 9.53 says that for \(c < 0 < k\) the vibration grows without bound (region \(3\) at the top left of the map). No real pendulum does this. A metronome with a wound spring starts to swing after a slight push, its swing grows for a while, and then it swings with always the same swing as long as the spring is wound. Such a vibration is called self-excited (samovzbujeno), because its energy is supplied by its own motion and not by an external force with a rhythm of its own. A constant negative \(c\) describes only the beginning, the growth. For the growth to stop, the supply of energy must weaken at large swings and the loss must prevail. There are two questions: at which swing does the growth stop, and does this swing depend on the start.

The simplest model with this property is a damping coefficient that depends on the angle (figure 9.51, left). Take the pendulum of example 9.55 with the angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, and instead of the constant \(c\) write

\[c(\theta) = c_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{\theta^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2} - 1\right) , \qquad c_{\mathrm{s}} > 0 , \quad \theta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0 . \tag{9.53}\]

On the horizontal axis we plot the angle divided by the threshold angle, on the vertical one the coefficient divided by \(c_{\mathrm{s}}\). The graph of this coefficient is a parabola. At \(\theta = 0\) it has the value \(-c_{\mathrm{s}}\). Between the angles \(-\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) it is negative: there the moment \(-c(\theta)\,\dot\theta\) points in the direction of the velocity and drives the pendulum. Outside this band it is positive: there it is an ordinary damper that brakes the pendulum. We call the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) the threshold angle (mejni kot), because it is the border between driving and braking. With theorem 8.101 and the non-conservative generalized force \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c(\theta)\,\dot\theta\) we get for small angles the equation

\[M\,\ddot\theta + c_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\frac{\theta^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2} - 1\right)\dot\theta + k\,\theta = 0 , \tag{9.54}\]

with \(M = m\ell^2\) and \(k = m g\ell\) as in example 9.55. It is called the van der Pol equation (van der Polova enačba, KREYSZIG (10), PDF p. 184, FPGL (2.143), PDF p. 87). It first appeared in the study of electrical circuits with vacuum tubes (KREYSZIG PDF p. 184).

The model has three assumptions, each with its own reach. The first is a small angle, \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) in the restoring moment: at the angle \(0{,}2\) rad, which we will reach, \(\sin\theta\) is smaller than \(\theta\) by \(0{,}67\ \%\) (lemma 8.20), and at larger angles the period gets longer. The second is the smooth coefficient (9.53). We chose it because it is the simplest function that is negative inside the band and positive outside it. A real metronome gets its energy in short kicks of the escapement, so the model tells at which swing the supply and the loss balance, but not how the velocity changes within one period. The third is that the source of energy does not run out: when the spring has unwound, \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and an ordinary damped pendulum remains.

In the lectures the angle is denoted by \(\varphi\) and the threshold angle by \(\varphi_0\). In the form KREYSZIG (10), \(y'' - \mu(1 - y^2)\,y' + y = 0\), we have \(y = \theta/\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), the time is multiplied by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\mu = c_{\mathrm{s}}/(M\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). In the form FPGL (2.143), \(m\ddot x + c(1 - \gamma x^2)\dot x + k x = 0\) with a negative \(c\), we have \(c = -c_{\mathrm{s}}\) and \(\gamma = 1/\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2\).

Where the energy goes is told by the same calculation as in point 2 of corollary 9.51, only that the coefficient now has two signs. The swing at which the supply and the loss balance is given by the second point of the next lemma.

Lemma 9.56 Power and work of the van der Pol term (moč in delo van der Polovega člena)

Let \(M > 0\), \(k > 0\), \(c_{\mathrm{s}} > 0\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\) be real numbers and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/M}\).

  1. Along every solution of equation (9.54) the energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 M\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12 k\theta^2\) has the derivative
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}}{\mathrm{d}t} = c_{\mathrm{s}}\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\right)\dot\theta^2 . \tag{9.55}\]
    For \(\dot\theta \ne 0\) it is positive when \(|\theta| < \theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and negative when \(|\theta| > \theta_{\mathrm{m}}\).
  2. For the function \(\theta(t) = A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) with \(A \ge 0\), which need not be a solution of the equation, the integral of the right-hand side of (9.55) over one period \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) equals
    \[W(A) = \int_0^{T_{\mathrm{n}}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\right)\dot\theta^2\,\mathrm{d}t = \pi\,c_{\mathrm{s}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,A^2\left(1 - \frac{A^2}{4\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\right) . \tag{9.56}\]
    Hence \(W(A) > 0\) for \(0 < A < 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(W(2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}) = 0\) and \(W(A) < 0\) for \(A > 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\).
Proof

We must show the formula for the derivative of the energy and compute the integral for the given function.

  1. The derivative of the energy is \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = M\dot\theta\ddot\theta + k\theta\dot\theta = \dot\theta\,(M\ddot\theta + k\theta)\), as in step 2 of the proof of corollary 9.51. By equation (9.54), \(M\ddot\theta + k\theta = -c(\theta)\,\dot\theta\) with the coefficient \(c(\theta)\) of (9.53), so \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -c(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2\), which is (9.55).
  2. The factor \(\dot\theta^2\) is positive for \(\dot\theta \ne 0\), so \(\dot{\mathcal{E}}\) has the opposite sign to \(c(\theta)\): it is positive in the band \(|\theta| < \theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and negative outside it. This is point 1.
  3. For \(\theta = A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), by theorem 8.13 and the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 3), \(\dot\theta = -A\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\). This is the swing between \(-A\) and \(A\). With the notation \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) the integrand is
    \[c_{\mathrm{s}} A^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\sin^2 x - \frac{c_{\mathrm{s}} A^4\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\,\cos^2 x\,\sin^2 x .\]
  4. By the addition formulas (corollary 8.16), \(\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x\) and \(\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x\). With \(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1\) (lemma 8.11) it follows that \(\sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x)\). In the same way \(\cos^2 x\,\sin^2 x = \tfrac14\sin^2 2x = \tfrac18(1 - \cos 4x)\).
  5. The function \(\sin(2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)/(2\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) has the derivative \(\cos 2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and the value zero at \(t = 0\) and at \(t = T_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(\sin 0 = \sin 4\pi = 0\). By theorem 3.55 therefore \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\cos 2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\), and in the same way \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\cos 4\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). With step 4, \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\sin^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\mathrm{d}t = T_{\mathrm{n}}/2 = \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\int_0^{T_{\mathrm{n}}}\cos^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\sin^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\,\mathrm{d}t = T_{\mathrm{n}}/8 = \pi/(4\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\).
  6. Steps 3 and 5 give \(W(A) = c_{\mathrm{s}} A^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{\omega_{\mathrm{n}}} - \dfrac{c_{\mathrm{s}} A^4\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2}{\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{4\omega_{\mathrm{n}}} = \pi c_{\mathrm{s}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} A^2\left(1 - \dfrac{A^2}{4\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2}\right)\), this is the curve of the work.
  7. The factor \(\pi c_{\mathrm{s}}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} A^2\) is positive for \(A > 0\). The bracket is positive exactly when \(A^2 < 4\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2\), that is for \(A < 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) (the blue part), zero at \(A = 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and negative for \(A > 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) (the orange part). This is point 2.

Source: The lemma is not from the source. KREYSZIG §4.5, example 5 (PDF p. 184) says in words that the damping is negative for small oscillations and positive for large ones, FPGL (PDF pp. 87 to 88) says the same. The calculation of the work in one period is ours (checked with SymPy).

The lemma gives the steady swing with one consideration (figure 9.51, right). On the horizontal axis is the swing \(A\) divided by the threshold angle, on the vertical one the work in one period in millijoules. If \(c_{\mathrm{s}}\) is small, the swing hardly changes in one period, so the motion within one period is almost \(A\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and the energy changes in it by about \(W(A)\). For \(A < 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) the work is positive, the energy and with it the swing grow. For \(A > 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) it is negative, the swing falls. Among the swings \(A > 0\) only \(A = 2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) does not change, and every other positive swing approaches it, from below and from above. For small \(\mu\) the steady swing thus does not depend on the start, nor on \(c_{\mathrm{s}}\), \(M\) or \(k\), only on the threshold angle.

Why twice the threshold angle and not the threshold angle itself? A pendulum with the swing \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) would be in the band where energy is supplied all the time, so the swing would still grow. For the supply and the loss to balance, the swing must reach across the border. It must reach well across, because the velocity is largest in the middle of the swing, where energy is supplied, and small at the turning points, where it is taken, and the power is proportional to the square of the velocity ((9.55)).

How well does the assumption hold that the motion within one period is almost harmonic? The measure is the dimensionless number \(\mu = c_{\mathrm{s}}/(M\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\). The work \(W(A)\) relative to the energy \(\tfrac12 k A^2\) equals \(2\pi\mu\,(1 - A^2/(4\theta_{\mathrm{m}}^2))\), so for small \(\mu\) the energy changes little in a period. A computation with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (step \(0{,}002\) of dimensionless time) gives the steady swing \(2{,}0001\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(\mu = 0{,}1\), \(2{,}0016\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(\mu = 0{,}4\), \(2{,}009\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(\mu = 1\) and \(2{,}023\,\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(\mu = 3\). The zero of the work at \(2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) therefore predicts the swing to within \(1{,}2\ \%\) even where the assumption no longer holds. It no longer holds, however, for the period and the shape of the motion: for \(\mu = 1\) the period is \(6\ \%\) longer than \(T_{\mathrm{n}}\), for \(\mu = 3\) it is longer by \(41\ \%\), and the motion no longer resembles a cosine.

The motion that all others approach has a name of its own.

Definition 9.57 Limit cycle and self-excited vibration (limitni cikel in samovzbujeno nihanje)

Let \(\ddot q = f(q, \dot q)\) be the equation of motion of a system with one coordinate \(q\), in which time does not appear, with a continuously differentiable function \(f\). Let \(q_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\) be a periodic solution of it that is not a constant, and \(\Gamma\) its path in the phase plane \((q, \dot q)\), a closed curve. The curve \(\Gamma\) is a limit cycle (limitni cikel) if there is a number \(\delta > 0\) such that for every solution whose initial point \((q(0), \dot q(0))\) is closer to \(\Gamma\) than \(\delta\), the distance of the point \((q(t), \dot q(t))\) from \(\Gamma\) tends to zero as \(t \to \infty\). The motion along a limit cycle is called self-excited vibration (samovzbujeno nihanje, also self-sustained oscillation).

Source: KREYSZIG §4.5, example 5 (PDF pp. 184 to 185): a limit cycle is a closed path in the phase plane that other paths approach, in which it differs from a closed path around a center. FPGL PDF pp. 87 to 88: "self-excited system", "limit cycle". The form with the distance and the number \(\delta\) is ours.

The name says both things. A cycle is a path that closes on itself, and it is a limit cycle because it is the limit of other paths. For now the definition covers only a cycle that nearby paths approach. In the literature a closed path from which nearby paths move away carries the same name, and it is called an unstable limit cycle. We already know an example that does not meet the definition: the circle of the undamped pendulum is the closed path of a periodic solution, but a motion that starts a little outside it moves along its own, slightly larger circle and never approaches the first one (KREYSZIG PDF p. 185). The undamped pendulum remembers its swing from the start, the self-excited one forgets it.

Three things therefore separate self-excited vibration from free damped vibration. The system chooses the swing itself, the limit cycle, the start does not determine it (from inside and from outside we arrive at the same cycle). The equilibrium is unstable, so any disturbance sets the vibration off. And the energy that the system loses is replaced from a source as it goes, in the band. The next example shows what happens when two such systems stand on the same base.

9.5.2 Two metronomes on a common base

Let us put two equal metronomes on a board that lies on two rollers, and start them at different instants (figure 9.53). The pendulums can swing in the same direction, oppositely, or with any shift in between. Each one alone would swing with its own swing \(2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\) and keep the shift with which we started it. But the board can move, and each pendulum pushes it in the opposite direction as it swings. Do the pendulums fall into step through the board, and if they do, how? The phenomenon that the pendulums fall into step by themselves, without an external beat, is called spontaneous synchronization (spontana sinhronizacija).

The model is this. The base is a rigid body of mass \(M_{\mathrm{b}}\) (the board with the housings of both metronomes) that rolls on the rollers over the ground without resistance. Its horizontal displacement from the position at rest is \(x\). Each metronome is a point mass \(m\) on a light bar of length \(\ell\) hung on the base, with the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) from the vertical. In each joint the moment \(-c(\theta_{\mathrm{i}})\,\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the coefficient (9.53) acts between the base and the bar. There are four idealizations. A real metronome is a bar with two weights and not a point mass, which changes the numbers \(m\) and \(\ell\) but not the form of the equations. The rollers have no mass and no resistance, we look at the effect of resistance at the end. The drive is smooth, as in the previous subsection. The angles are small: at the swing \(0{,}2\) rad, \(\cos\theta = 0{,}980\) and \(\sin\theta\) is smaller than \(\theta\) by \(0{,}67\ \%\), so the linear equations miss the natural frequencies by about a percent.

We get the equations from the energies in four steps.

  1. Mass \(i\) has the horizontal velocity \(\dot x + \ell\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and the vertical one \(\ell\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\), because it moves with the base and turns about the joint. For small angles we keep the terms of second order in \((x, \theta_1, \theta_2)\) and their velocities, that is \(\cos\theta_{\mathrm{i}} \approx 1\) in the velocity and \(-\cos\theta_{\mathrm{i}} \approx -1 + \tfrac12\theta_{\mathrm{i}}^2\) in the height:
    \[T = \tfrac12 (M_{\mathrm{b}} + 2m)\,\dot x^2 + m\ell\,\dot x\,(\dot\theta_1 + \dot\theta_2) + \tfrac12 m\ell^2\,(\dot\theta_1^2 + \dot\theta_2^2) , \qquad V = \tfrac12 m g\ell\,(\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2) .\]
  2. The coordinate \(x\) does not appear in \(T\) and \(V\), it is cyclic (section 8.11.2), and no non-conservative force acts on it, because the moment in the joint does no work when the base is displaced. The Lagrange equation for it (theorem 8.101) is
    \[(M_{\mathrm{b}} + 2m)\,\ddot x + m\ell\,(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2) = 0 . \tag{9.57}\]
    It says that the horizontal momentum of the whole is conserved, as in example 8.129: when the masses go to the right, the base goes to the left.
  3. The Lagrange equation for the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) with the non-conservative force \(-c(\theta_{\mathrm{i}})\,\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is
    \[m\ell^2\,\ddot\theta_{\mathrm{i}} + m\ell\,\ddot x + m g\ell\,\theta_{\mathrm{i}} = -c(\theta_{\mathrm{i}})\,\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad i = 1, 2 . \tag{9.58}\]
    The term \(m\ell\,\ddot x\) is the only thing through which a pendulum feels the other pendulum: the acceleration of the base.
  4. From (9.57) we express \(\ddot x = -\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\,\ell\,(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2)\) with the mass ratio
    \[\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{m}{M_{\mathrm{b}} + 2m} , \qquad 0 < \mu_{\mathrm{r}} < \tfrac12 , \tag{9.59}\]
    insert it into (9.58) and divide by \(m\ell^2\). With \(\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}\) we get
    \[\ddot\theta_{\mathrm{i}} - \mu_{\mathrm{r}}\,(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2) + \omega^2\,\theta_{\mathrm{i}} = -\frac{c(\theta_{\mathrm{i}})}{m\ell^2}\,\dot\theta_{\mathrm{i}} , \qquad i = 1, 2 . \tag{9.60}\]
    The mass ratio \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\) is small for a heavy base, which hardly moves, and tends to \(\tfrac12\) when the base has no mass.

First we look at the pair without the drive. We ask which motions (in phase and in antiphase) are such that both pendulums swing with the same frequency.

Lemma 9.59 In-phase and antiphase motion of two pendulums on a common base (nihanje dveh nihal na skupni podlagi v fazi in v protifazi)

Let \(m > 0\), \(M_{\mathrm{b}} > 0\), \(\ell > 0\) and \(g > 0\) be real numbers, \(\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\) as in (9.59). For equations (9.57) and (9.58) with \(c(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0\):

  1. For every real number \(a\), \(\theta_1 = a\cos\omega t\), \(\theta_2 = -a\cos\omega t\), \(x = 0\) is a solution. We call it motion in antiphase (nihanje v protifazi). The base is at rest, the circular frequency is \(\omega\).
  2. For every real number \(a\), \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{f}} t\), \(x = -2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\,\ell\,a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{f}} t\) is a solution with the circular frequency
    \[\omega_{\mathrm{f}} = \frac{\omega}{\sqrt{1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}} > \omega . \tag{9.61}\]
    We call it motion in phase (nihanje v fazi). The base swings against the pendulums with the amplitude \(2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\,\ell\,a\).
  3. The sum \(s = \theta_1 + \theta_2\) and the difference \(d = \theta_1 - \theta_2\) of every solution satisfy the equations \((1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ddot s + \omega^2 s = 0\) and \(\ddot d + \omega^2 d = 0\).
Proof

We must show that both motions solve the equations, and derive the equations for the sum and the difference. We start with point 3, because it gives the other two.

  1. We add equations (9.60) for \(i = 1\) and \(i = 2\) with a zero right-hand side: \(\ddot s - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\,\ddot s + \omega^2 s = 0\), that is \((1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ddot s + \omega^2 s = 0\). We subtract them: the term \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2)\) is the same in both and drops out, what remains is \(\ddot d + \omega^2 d = 0\). This is point 3.
  2. In the motion in antiphase, \(s = 0\) and \(d = 2a\cos\omega t\). The first equation of step 1 is satisfied by \(s = 0\), the second by theorem 8.138, because \(\cos\omega t\) is a solution of the equation \(\ddot d + \omega^2 d = 0\). From (9.57), \(\ddot x = -\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\ell\,\ddot s = 0\), so \(x = 0\) is a solution: the forces of the pendulums on the base are opposite and cancel. This is point 1.
  3. In the motion in phase, \(d = 0\) and \(s = 2a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{f}} t\). We divide the equation \((1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ddot s + \omega^2 s = 0\) by \(1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is positive because \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} < \tfrac12\). By theorem 8.138 it is solved by the cosine with the circular frequency \(\omega/\sqrt{1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}}\), which is (9.61).
  4. From (9.57), \(\ddot x = -\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\ell\,\ddot s\). The function \(x = -\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\ell\,s = -2\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\ell\,a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{f}} t\) satisfies this, it is the displacement of the base in the opposite direction. This is point 2.

Source: The lemma is not from the source. It is derived from theorem 8.101 and theorem 8.138, checked with SymPy.

Why is the motion in phase faster? When both masses go to the right, the base goes to the left, and the joints with it. Relative to the ground the mass is displaced by \(x + \ell\theta = (1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ell\,\theta\), less than relative to the joint. So it moves as if it hung on a shorter bar of length \((1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ell\) with a joint at rest, and a shorter pendulum swings faster. In the motion in antiphase the base is at rest and each pendulum swings as if the other were not there.

With the drive both motions remain possible, each with the swing \(2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\). In antiphase \(\ddot x = 0\) and each of equations (9.58) is exactly the van der Pol equation (9.54). In phase \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = \theta\) and (9.60) becomes \((1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}})\,\ddot\theta + c(\theta)\,\dot\theta/(m\ell^2) + \omega^2\theta = 0\), again a van der Pol equation, only with a smaller mass, and by lemma 9.56 the steady swing does not depend on the mass (the zero of the work). Which of the two motions happens, the lemma does not tell. A computation shows it.

Example 9.60 Two metronomes: in phase or in antiphase (dva metronoma: v fazi ali v protifazi)

The numbers are our choice: \(m = 0{,}03\) kg, \(\ell = 0{,}25\) m, so \(\omega = 6{,}2642\) 1/s and the period is \(1{,}0030\) s. The base has \(M_{\mathrm{b}} = 0{,}24\) kg, so \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}03/0{,}30 = 0{,}1\). The threshold angle is \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = 0{,}1\) rad and \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 1{,}1745\cdot 10^{-3}\) N m s, which is \(c_{\mathrm{s}}/(m\ell^2\omega) = 0{,}1\): small vibrations grow as if the pendulum had the damping ratio \(-0{,}05\). Lemma 9.59 predicts the period \(1{,}0030\) s in antiphase and \(1{,}0030\cdot\sqrt{0{,}8} = 0{,}8971\) s in phase, and at the swing \(0{,}2\) rad the amplitude of the base \(2\cdot 0{,}1\cdot 0{,}25\cdot 0{,}2\ \text{m} = 10\) mm.

We solve equations (9.57) and (9.58) numerically (fourth-order Runge-Kutta, step \(0{,}004\) s). At \(t = 0\): \(\theta_1 = 0{,}05\) rad, \(\dot\theta_1 = 0\), \(\theta_2 = 0{,}05\cos\Delta\varphi_0\) rad, \(\dot\theta_2 = -0{,}05\,\omega\sin\Delta\varphi_0\) rad/s (the second pendulum leads the first by \(\Delta\varphi_0\)), and \(\dot x\) is such that the total momentum is zero. The border is between \(100^\circ\) and \(105^\circ\) (for this start, if the second pendulum lags, it lies between \(95^\circ\) and \(98^\circ\)), two almost equal starts give different ends. The border is not at \(90^\circ\). A reason that agrees with the computation is that a small motion in phase grows faster: in its equation the same drive is divided by the smaller factor \(1 - 2\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}8\) in front of the acceleration. The periods agree with the lemma to within \(0{,}08\ \%\). The difference comes from the drive, which makes the period a little longer, as in example 9.58.

Why does one motion push out the other? Each pendulum has one drive with one threshold angle. When a pendulum swings as the sum of both motions, it reaches across the threshold angle farther than each motion alone would, so the drive is still enough for the stronger motion but no longer for the weaker one. The stronger one grows to the swing \(2\theta_{\mathrm{m}}\), the weaker one dies out. We do not derive this claim, the computation in the table supports it.

A base with resistance. Let us add to the base a damper against the ground, that is the term \(-c_{\mathrm{b}}\,\dot x\) on the right-hand side of (9.57). In the motion in antiphase the base is at rest and the resistance does nothing. The motion in phase moves the base and loses energy on it. A computation from the start \(\Delta\varphi_0 = 20^\circ\), almost in phase: for \(c_{\mathrm{b}} = 0{,}1\) N s/m the motion in phase remains with the swing \(0{,}189\) rad, for \(0{,}3\) N s/m with the swing \(0{,}166\) rad, and for \(1\) N s/m the pendulums pass into antiphase after \(12{,}7\) s with the swing \(0{,}2\) rad. On a base that is hard to move, antiphase wins.

The mass ratio. The base is the only path by which the pendulums act on each other, and its displacement is proportional to \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\) (lemma 9.59, point 2). For equal pendulums and the start \(\Delta\varphi_0 = 60^\circ\) without resistance we get:

The heavier the base, the less it moves and the longer the pendulums take to fall into step: for \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}01\) almost four minutes. The mass ratio also decides whether pendulums that are not equal fall into step. If the second pendulum is \(1\ \%\) shorter (\(\ell_2 = 0{,}2475\) m, its natural frequency higher by \(0{,}5\ \%\)), then for \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}1\) and \(0{,}05\) they lock to the same frequency with the constant shift \(2{,}5^\circ\) and \(10{,}6^\circ\), but for \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}02\) and less they do not: the shift keeps running and each pendulum keeps its own frequency. The coupling through the base, which moves the two natural frequencies apart by a fraction of about \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\) ((9.61)), must be a few times larger than the difference of the natural frequencies of the pendulums.

The reach of the example. The equations are linear in the angles, the drive is smooth, the rollers have no mass. With the exact terms \(\sin\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\cos\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) the same starts \(60^\circ\) and \(150^\circ\) also end in phase and in antiphase (a computation with the same step). For a real metronome with the kicks of an escapement we did not check this.

The answer to the question of this subsection is therefore: equal pendulums with a drive of their own fall into step by themselves on a movable base, either in phase or in antiphase. Which of the two prevails is decided by the start and by the losses of the base, how fast, by the mass ratio \(\mu_{\mathrm{r}}\). A linear system cannot do this: in it, by lemma 9.59, both motions would live side by side with swings that the start determines. Falling into step is a consequence of a drive that depends on the swing.

With this we leave motion without an external force. But what if a force acts on the weight of system A all the time? Section 9.6 shows that the free motion, which dies out after \(13\) s, is replaced by the motion under the force, and that what remains depends on the frequency of the force.
