Chapter 9 Vibration of a system with one degree of freedom (nihanje sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

9.5 Free damped vibration

We strike the cantilever with the weight, system A of section 9.1, with a hammer. The weight gets the velocity \(\dot q(0) = 0{,}1\) m/s (an impulse of 5 N s on a mass of 50 kg, the blow itself is the subject of section 9.9), and from then on no external force acts on it any more. It starts to vibrate and the vibration slowly dies out. How fast, with which period, and how much damping is enough for the vibration to disappear altogether? A motion without an external force, determined only by the initial displacement and the initial velocity, is called free motion (prosto gibanje).

By theorem 9.3 the free motion is a solution of the equation

\[m\,\ddot q + c\,\dot q + k\,q = 0 , \qquad q(0) = q_0 , \quad \dot q(0) = \dot q_0 , \tag{9.37}\]

with \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\) and \(c \ge 0\). With the natural circular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and the damping ratio \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\) (definitions 9.4 and 9.5) we write it as \(\ddot q + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,q = 0\). For system A, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{km} = 3541{,}75\) N s/m, and at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) we have \(c = 35{,}418\) N s/m.

Sections 9.2 to 9.4 prepared three tools. The trial \(q = \mathrm{e}^{st}\) gives for the number \(s\) the characteristic polynomial (karakteristični polinom), which after division by \(m\) we write as

\[s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,s + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = (s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 - \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,(\zeta^2 - 1) \tag{9.38}\]

(we get the right-hand side by completing the square). The zeros are therefore \(s = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\). The sign of the expression \(\zeta^2 - 1\) separates three cases. For \(\zeta < 1\) the root of a negative number is purely imaginary, \(\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} = \mathrm{i}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), because \(\mathrm{i}^2 = -1\) (section 9.3), and the zeros are complex conjugate numbers. For \(\zeta = 1\) there is a single zero, a double one, for \(\zeta > 1\) both are real. For system A at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) we have \(s = -0{,}35418 \pm 35{,}41574\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s: the real part \(-0{,}35418\) is negative, so the motion dies out, and the imaginary part \(35{,}41574\) is a circular frequency, so it vibrates. What remains is the question whether this trial captures every motion and how to choose the two constants for a given start. The answer is given by theorems 9.39 and 9.36.

For \(\zeta > 1\) the zeros are real and the solution is a sum of two exponential functions \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{x} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\) with \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\,t\). The constants \(a\) and \(b\) follow more easily from the initial values if we write the sum with two functions that have the values \(1\) and \(0\) at \(0\) and differentiate into each other, just as cosine and sine do for \(\zeta < 1\). We define these two functions as follows.

Definition 9.41 Hyperbolic cosine and sine (hiperbolični kosinus in sinus)

For every real number \(x\) the hyperbolic cosine (hiperbolični kosinus) and the hyperbolic sine (hiperbolični sinus) are the real numbers

\[\operatorname{cosh} x = \frac{\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x}}{2} , \qquad \operatorname{sinh} x = \frac{\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x}}{2} . \tag{9.39}\]

Source: CK (3.39), PDF p. 83, where the functions are used without a definition. The definition by the exponential function (definition 9.12) is not from the source.

Lemma 9.42 Properties of the hyperbolic functions (lastnosti hiperboličnih funkcij)

For every real number \(x\) and all real numbers \(a\), \(b\):

  1. \((\operatorname{cosh})'(x) = \operatorname{sinh} x\) and \((\operatorname{sinh})'(x) = \operatorname{cosh} x\).
  2. \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = 0\).
  3. \(\operatorname{cosh} x > 0\), and for \(x \ge 0\) we have \(\operatorname{sinh} x \ge 0\).
  4. \(a\,\mathrm{e}^{x} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x} = (a + b)\operatorname{cosh} x + (a - b)\operatorname{sinh} x\).
Proof

We must show four points. The first follows from the derivative of the exponential function, the second from \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\), the third from the exponential function being increasing, the fourth from expanding.

  1. By theorem 9.13 (point 2), \((\mathrm{e}^x)' = \mathrm{e}^x\). By the chain rule for a function of one variable (lemma 8.29, item 3) with the inner function \(x \mapsto -x\), \((\mathrm{e}^{-x})' = -\mathrm{e}^{-x}\). Hence \((\operatorname{cosh})' = (\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x})/2 = \operatorname{sinh}\) and \((\operatorname{sinh})' = (\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x})/2 = \operatorname{cosh}\).
  2. By theorem 9.13 (point 1), \(\mathrm{e}^0 = 1\), so \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = (1 + 1)/2 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = (1 - 1)/2 = 0\).
  3. The exponential function has positive values (definition 9.12), so \(\operatorname{cosh} x > 0\). For \(x \ge 0\) we have \(-x \le x\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-x} \le \mathrm{e}^x\), because \(\mathrm{e}\) is increasing (theorem 9.13, point 1), so \(\operatorname{sinh} x \ge 0\).
  4. The sum \((a + b)(\mathrm{e}^x + \mathrm{e}^{-x}) + (a - b)(\mathrm{e}^x - \mathrm{e}^{-x})\) equals \(2a\,\mathrm{e}^x + 2b\,\mathrm{e}^{-x}\). By definition the first expression equals \(2(a + b)\operatorname{cosh} x + 2(a - b)\operatorname{sinh} x\), so the identity follows after division by \(2\).

Source: The lemma is not from the source. CK (3.39) uses the same notation, without deriving the properties.

Theorem 9.43 Free damped vibration (prosto dušeno nihanje)

Let \(m > 0\), \(k > 0\) and \(c \ge 0\) be real numbers, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) and \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\). Let \(q(t)\) be a solution of equation (9.37) with the initial values \(q(0) = q_0\) and \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\). This solution exists and is unique for every \(t \ge 0\).

  1. Underdamping (podkritično dušenje), \(\zeta < 1\). With the damped circular frequency (dušena krožna frekvenca) \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\),
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\left( q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \frac{\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t \right) . \tag{9.40}\]
  2. Critical damping (kritično dušenje), \(\zeta = 1\):
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl( q_0 + (\dot q_0 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)\,t \bigr) . \tag{9.41}\]
  3. Overdamping (nadkritično dušenje), \(\zeta > 1\). With \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) and the functions of definition 9.41,
    \[q(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\left( q_0\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + \frac{\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0}{\omega_*}\,\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t \right) . \tag{9.42}\]

For \(\zeta = 0\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and (9.40) is \(q_0\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + (\dot q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), the solution of theorem 8.120. The damped period (dušeni nihajni čas) is \(T_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\).

Proof

We must show that each formula solves the equation and fits the start. Uniqueness is given by theorem 9.36, so the solution we find is the only one.

  1. From \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\) we get \(2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = (c/\sqrt{km})\sqrt{k/m} = c/m\), so after division by \(m\) we obtain \(\ddot q + 2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\dot q + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 q = 0\) with the characteristic polynomial (9.38).
  2. For \(\zeta < 1\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} > 0\) and the polynomial is \((s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2\), with the zeros \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\) (because \((\pm\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{d}})^2 = -\omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2\)). By corollary 9.40 all real solutions have the form \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(a\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + b\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t)\) with real constants \(a\) and \(b\). Its derivative is \(\dot q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl((-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + \omega_{\mathrm{d}} b)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - (\omega_{\mathrm{d}} a + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} b)\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\bigr)\). At \(t = 0\) we have \(q = a\) and \(\dot q = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + \omega_{\mathrm{d}} b\). The requirement \(q(0) = q_0\), \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\) gives \(a = q_0\) and \(b = (\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), which is (9.40).
  3. For \(\zeta = 1\) the polynomial is \((s + \omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\) with the double zero \(-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). By theorem 9.39 and corollary 9.40 all real solutions are \(q = (a + b\,t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) with real \(a\) and \(b\). At \(t = 0\) we have \(q = a\) and \(\dot q = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a + b\), so \(a = q_0\) and \(b = \dot q_0 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0\). This is (9.41).
  4. For \(\zeta > 1\) we have \(\omega_* > 0\) and the polynomial \((s + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2 - \omega_*^2\) has the two distinct real zeros \(-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \omega_*\). By theorem 9.39 and corollary 9.40, \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(a\,\mathrm{e}^{\omega_* t} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_* t})\) with real \(a\) and \(b\). By lemma 9.42 (point 4) we write this as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(A\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + B\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t)\) with \(A = a + b\) and \(B = a - b\). By the same lemma (points 1 and 2), \((\operatorname{cosh})' = \operatorname{sinh}\), \((\operatorname{sinh})' = \operatorname{cosh}\), \(\operatorname{cosh} 0 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{sinh} 0 = 0\). At \(t = 0\) we therefore have (the derivative with respect to \(t\) gives the factor \(\omega_*\)) \(q = A\) and \(\dot q = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} A + \omega_* B\), so \(A = q_0\) and \(B = (\dot q_0 + \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0)/\omega_*\). This is (9.42).
  5. Each formula is a solution of the equation with the given start. By theorem 9.36 it is the only such solution.

Source: Formulas: CK (3.26) to (3.39), PDF pp. 80 to 83 (the critical case (3.36) on PDF p. 83, the overdamped case (3.39) in the form with \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\), \(\omega_* = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\) by (3.37), checked on the images of the formulas), KREYSZIG §2.4, PDF pp. 91 to 93. The proof by steps is not from the source: CK and KREYSZIG derive the formulas with the trial \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\), without showing that it captures every solution.

The theorem showed that every free motion is one of three forms that are determined only by \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\zeta\), and the start enters only through two constants in front of the sine and cosine. The underdamped case tells us what we see in system A: a cosine and a sine with the damped circular frequency, multiplied by a decreasing exponential function. In the other two cases there is no vibration, because \(\omega_*\) appears in the functions \(\operatorname{cosh}\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\), which do not oscillate.

Example 9.44 Hammer blow on system A (udarec s kladivom na sistemu A)

The start is \(q_0 = 0\), \(\dot q_0 = 0{,}1\) m/s, the damping ratio is \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) (CK gives for structures "0,5 % to 5 %", PDF p. 82, the value 0,01 is our choice, how to measure it is shown in section 9.13). By (9.40),

\[q(t) = \frac{\dot q_0}{\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-0{,}35418\,t}\,\sin(35{,}4157\,t) ,\]

with the time \(t\) in seconds. First we look at what the damping changed in the frequency. The damped frequency is \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 35{,}4157\) 1/s, the undamped one \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, the difference is \(0{,}005\ \%\). The reason is that \(\zeta\) enters only as a square, \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), and at \(\zeta^2 = 10^{-4}\) we have \(1 - \sqrt{1 - 10^{-4}} = 0{,}5\cdot 10^{-4}\). The damped period is \(T_{\mathrm{d}} = 0{,}177412\) s, the undamped one \(T_{\mathrm{n}} = 0{,}177403\) s. For weak damping the formula therefore shows almost the same frequency as the undamped pendulum. Damping acts on the amplitude, not on the frequency.

The amplitude is bounded by the envelope (section 9.2) \(\pm 2{,}8236\ \text{mm}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\): the curve touches it when \(\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = \pm 1\) (KREYSZIG PDF p. 93), in between it lies below it (figure 9.13). The constant \(2{,}82\) mm is \(\dot q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\). The envelope falls to one half when \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = 1/2\), that is after \(t = \ln 2/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}69315/0{,}35418 = 1{,}957\) s, or after \(1{,}957/0{,}177412 = 11{,}03\) periods. It falls below \(1\ \%\) after \(\ln 100/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 13{,}00\) s, that is after 73 periods. The blow therefore dies out only after 13 s, when more than 70 periods have passed since it.

Free motion in underdamping therefore has two components: vibration with the damped frequency and decay with an exponential envelope. Let us see what happens if we give the same system the same start with different dampers. We compare three values of \(\zeta\) for the start \(q_0 = 1\) mm and \(\dot q_0 = 0\), that is, a displaced weight that we release.

Example 9.45 Three kinds of damping from the same start (tri vrste dušenja iz istega začetka)

system A with \(m = 50\) kg, \(k = 62\,720\) N/m, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 35{,}4175\) 1/s, the start \(q_0 = 1\) mm, \(\dot q_0 = 0\). The formulas of theorem 9.43 give (time \(t\) in seconds, \(q\) in millimetres):

\(\zeta\) \(c\) (N s/m) zeros \(s\) (1/s)
0,1 354,2 \(-3{,}5418 \pm 35{,}2400\,\mathrm{i}\)
1 3541,8 \(-35{,}4175\) (double)
2 7083,5 \(-9{,}4901\) and \(-132{,}1799\)
  • \(\zeta = 0{,}1\): \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-3{,}5418\,t}\,(\cos 35{,}2400\,t + 0{,}10050\,\sin 35{,}2400\,t)\)
  • \(\zeta = 1\): \(q = (1 + 35{,}4175\,t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-35{,}4175\,t}\)
  • \(\zeta = 2\): \(q = 1{,}0774\,\mathrm{e}^{-9{,}4901\,t} - 0{,}0774\,\mathrm{e}^{-132{,}1799\,t}\)

For \(\zeta = 0{,}1\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 35{,}2400\) 1/s, the coefficient \(0{,}10050 = \zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\). The weight passes through the equilibrium at \(0{,}0474\) s and moves to the other side up to \(-0{,}729\) mm at \(0{,}0891\) s. For \(\zeta = 1\) and \(\zeta = 2\) the weight approaches the equilibrium from one side and does not pass it (figure 9.14). For \(\zeta = 2\) the zeros are very different. The fast term \(\mathrm{e}^{-132{,}18\,t}\) falls in \(0{,}03\) s to \(2\ \%\) of its initial value, then the slow term \(1{,}0774\,\mathrm{e}^{-9{,}4901\,t}\) remains. It is slow because a strong damper holds the motion back: at the displacement 1 mm the spring pulls with the force \(62{,}7\) N, and the damper with \(c = 7083{,}5\) N s/m allows at this force only a velocity of about \(62{,}7/7083{,}5 = 8{,}9\) mm/s, which is \(k/c = 8{,}85\) 1/s times the displacement, close to the slow zero \(9{,}49\) 1/s. The weight reaches \(1\ \%\) of the initial displacement (and stays there) after \(1{,}265\) s for \(\zeta = 0{,}1\), after \(0{,}187\) s for \(\zeta = 1\) and after \(0{,}493\) s for \(\zeta = 2\).

The table shows that at \(\zeta = 1\) there is no vibration any more. Where exactly is this limit and why is it called critical? The next corollary answers three questions at once: by how much the vibration decays in one period, where the energy goes, and why \(c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 2\sqrt{km}\) is the smallest damping at which the weight, starting from rest, does not pass the equilibrium.

Corollary 9.46 Decay, energy and critical damping (pojemanje, energija in kritično dušenje)

Let \(q\) be a free motion as in theorem 9.43.

  1. For \(\zeta < 1\) and every \(t\),
    \[q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,q(t) , \tag{9.43}\]
    so the ratio of the values one damped period apart is independent of the start and of \(t\). The same ratio holds for \(\dot q\).
  2. The energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2 + \tfrac12 k q^2\) has the derivative \(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}/\mathrm{d}t = -c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\). For \(\zeta < 1\), \(\mathcal{E}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \mathrm{e}^{-4\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,\mathcal{E}(t)\).
  3. Let \(q_0 > 0\) and \(\dot q_0 = 0\). For \(\zeta < 1\), \(q(t) = \dfrac{q_0}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha)\), where \(\alpha \in (0, \pi/2)\) is the angle with \(\sin\alpha = \zeta\). The motion crosses the equilibrium at \(t = (\alpha + \pi/2)/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), and its first minimum is \(q(\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}) = -q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\). For \(\zeta \ge 1\), \(q(t) > 0\) for every \(t \ge 0\).
  4. The slower part of the response decays as \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta < 1\), as \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta = 1\) and as \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) for \(\zeta > 1\). The decay exponent \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) grows with \(\zeta\) up to \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) at \(\zeta = 1\), and above that \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\omega_{\mathrm{n}} < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and it falls.
Proof

We show points 1 and 2 from the form of the solution and from the equation, point 3 with a form that we get from the formulas of theorem 9.43, point 4 from the exponents.

  1. Cosine and sine have the period \(2\pi\) (lemma 8.10, point 4), so \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = \cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + 2\pi) = \cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\), and the same for sine. The factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is multiplied by \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{d}}}\) under a shift by \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\) (theorem 9.13, \(\mathrm{e}^{a + b} = \mathrm{e}^a\mathrm{e}^b\)). Because \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} T_{\mathrm{d}}\zeta = 2\pi\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = 2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), (9.43) follows. Because \(q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r\,q(t)\) holds for every \(t\) with the same number \(r\), differentiating both sides (chain rule for a function of one variable, lemma 8.29, item 3, with the inner function \(t \mapsto t + T_{\mathrm{d}}\)) gives also \(\dot q(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r\,\dot q(t)\).
  2. The derivative of the energy is \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = m\dot q\ddot q + k q\dot q = \dot q\,(m\ddot q + k q)\). By equation (9.37), \(m\ddot q + k q = -c\dot q\), so \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -c\,\dot q^2 \le 0\). This is equation (8.98) of theorem 8.104 with the non-conservative force \(-c\dot q\), which has the power \(-c\dot q^2\). For \(\zeta < 1\), by point 1 both terms of the energy are multiplied by \(r^2\), so \(\mathcal{E}(t + T_{\mathrm{d}}) = r^2\mathcal{E}(t)\) with \(r^2 = \mathrm{e}^{-4\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\).
  3. The angle \(\alpha\) exists by theorem 2.57, because \(\sin 0 = 0\) and \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\), and \(\cos\alpha = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} > 0\) (lemma 8.10). By the addition formulas (corollary 8.15), \(\cos(x - \alpha) = \cos\alpha\cos x + \sin\alpha\sin x = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\cos x + \zeta\sin x\). For \(x = \omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\) we therefore have \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,\dfrac{q_0}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,q_0\bigl(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t + \tfrac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\bigr)\), which is (9.40) with \(\dot q_0 = 0\), because \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}} = \zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\).
  4. For \(t \ge 0\) we have \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha \ge -\alpha > -\pi/2\), so \(\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha) > 0\) as long as \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha < \pi/2\). At \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t - \alpha = \pi/2\) the cosine is zero and changes sign there. The derivative of the solution is, by the formula in the proof of theorem 9.43, step 2, with \(\dot q_0 = 0\) and \(a = q_0\), \(b = \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} q_0/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\): \(\dot q = -\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\,(q_0\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\,\sin\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t\), because \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}^2 + \zeta^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\). It has zeros at \(\omega_{\mathrm{d}} t = j\pi\), the first at \(t = \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}\), and there \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}}(\cos\pi + (\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\sin\pi) = -q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\). The derivative is negative on \((0, \pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\) and positive on \((\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}}, 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{d}})\), so this is the first minimum.
  5. For \(\zeta = 1\), \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}(1 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t) > 0\) for \(t \ge 0\) ((9.41) with \(\dot q_0 = 0\)).
  6. For \(\zeta > 1\), \(q = q_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\bigl(\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t + (\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_*)\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t\bigr)\) ((9.42)). By lemma 9.42 (point 3), for \(t \ge 0\) we have \(\operatorname{cosh}\omega_* t > 0\) and \(\operatorname{sinh}\omega_* t \ge 0\), the coefficient \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\omega_*\) is positive, so the sum in the bracket is positive.
  7. For \(\zeta > 1\) we have \(\omega_* < \zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), because \(\omega_*^2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(\zeta^2 - 1) < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\zeta^2\). The terms in (9.42) decay as \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} - \omega_*)t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} + \omega_*)t}\), the first one is slower with the exponent \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Because \((\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})(\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}) = \zeta^2 - (\zeta^2 - 1) = 1\), we have \(\zeta - \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} = 1/(\zeta + \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1})\). The denominator is larger than \(1\) and grows with \(\zeta\), so the quotient is smaller than \(1\) and falls. For \(\zeta < 1\) the exponent is \(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} < \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and it grows with \(\zeta\). This is point 4.

Source: CK §3.2 and §3.5 (PDF pp. 80 to 83, 93 to 94), KREYSZIG §2.4 (PDF pp. 91 to 93, on critical damping: the solution passes through the equilibrium at most once, p. 92). The proof is not from the source. CK states that with critical damping the amplitude decays faster than with underdamping and overdamping (PDF p. 80). Step 7 says in which sense this holds (the exponent of the final decay), while the time to a given tolerance can be shorter for non-critical damping, as the table shows.

For system A at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) point 1 gives the ratio \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\cdot 0{,}01/\sqrt{0{,}9999}} = 0{,}93910\): in one period the amplitude falls to \(93{,}9\ \%\). By point 2, after one period \(0{,}93910^2 = 0{,}88191\) of the energy is kept, we lose \(11{,}8\ \%\). For the blow of example 9.44 the initial energy is \(\tfrac12\cdot 50\cdot 0{,}1^2 = 0{,}25\) J, so in the first period \(0{,}0295\) J is lost. For \(\zeta = 0{,}1\) of example 9.45, item 3 of the corollary gives the first overshoot (prenihaj) \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\cdot 0{,}1/\sqrt{0{,}99}} = 0{,}7292\), as the table shows. Point 3 answers the name: \(c_{\mathrm{kr}}\) is the limit between a motion that passes the equilibrium and one that does not. For \(c < c_{\mathrm{kr}}\) (underdamping) it passes it, for \(c \ge c_{\mathrm{kr}}\) it does not.

Point 4 says something else that one would expect differently: at critical damping the exponent of the final decay is the largest. We would therefore expect the weight to settle fastest at \(\zeta = 1\). The calculation refutes this if we measure the time to a given deviation. We compute the time after which \(|q| \le 0{,}01\,q_0\) for ever, for \(q_0 = 1\) mm at rest (NumPy):

\(\zeta\) 0,5 0,7 0,83 1 2
time to \(1\ \%\) (s) 0,2479 0,1856 0,1195 0,1874 0,4931
first overshoot (point 3) \(16{,}3\ \%\) \(4{,}6\ \%\) \(0{,}93\ \%\) 0 0

For \(\zeta = 0{,}7\) it passes the equilibrium by \(4{,}6\ \%\), which is more than the allowed \(1\ \%\), so it enters the band only after the overshoot, at \(0{,}1856\) s. For \(\zeta = 0{,}83\) the overshoot is \(0{,}93\ \%\), thus smaller than \(1\ \%\), and the band is reached already at the first passage, at \(0{,}1195\) s. For \(\zeta = 1\) there is no overshoot, but the term \((1 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is slower, because \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) first grows before it falls. We denote the allowed deviation by \(\eta\) (here \(\eta = 0{,}01\)) and by \(\zeta_*\) the damping ratio at which the overshoot is exactly \(\eta\). The fastest damping is just above \(\zeta_*\). By point 3 of the corollary the overshoot is \(\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} = \eta\) at \(\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = \lambda/\pi\) with \(\lambda = \ln(1/\eta)\), so \(\zeta_*^2 = (\lambda/\pi)^2/\bigl(1 + (\lambda/\pi)^2\bigr)\). For \(\eta = 0{,}01\) we have \(\lambda = 4{,}6052\) and \(\zeta_* = 0{,}8261\). Below \(\zeta_*\) the overshoot exceeds the band and the time jumps to the later entry (at \(\zeta = 0{,}826\) it is \(0{,}1589\) s), above \(\zeta_*\) the time grows with \(\zeta\), because the decay is slower (at \(\zeta = 0{,}91\) it is \(0{,}1489\) s), just above \(\zeta_*\) it is \(0{,}1184\) s. For \(\eta = 0{,}05\) we have \(\zeta_* = 0{,}6901\) with \(0{,}0807\) s, and for \(\eta = 0{,}001\) we have \(\zeta_* = 0{,}9103\), the closer to critical damping the stricter the tolerance. Critical damping is therefore the fastest by the exponent of the final decay and by not passing the equilibrium, but not by the time to a given tolerance. We can therefore speak of the fastest damping only once we state the tolerance (all times are computed with NumPy).

The plane \((q, \dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) is called the phase plane (fazna ravnina): each point is a state determined by the displacement and the velocity, and a motion is the path of the point. In it the same decay is seen from another side (figure 9.15). In the undamped system the energy \(\mathcal{E} = \tfrac12 k\bigl(q^2 + (\dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\bigr)\) stays constant (theorem 8.120, step 5), which is the equation of a circle, since \(\tfrac12 m\dot q^2 = \tfrac12 k(\dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})^2\). The point \((q, \dot q/\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) moves along the circle. With damping, by point 2 of corollary 9.46, \(\dot{\mathcal{E}} = -c\dot q^2 \le 0\), so the point crosses the circles inwards, and the path is a spiral.

So far we had \(c \ge 0\) and \(k > 0\). Section 8.12 showed that damping does not endanger stability, and section 8.13 found for \(k < 0\) a motion that moves away from the equilibrium. But what about \(c < 0\) or \(k = 0\)? Negative \(c\) is a model of a force that supports the motion instead of braking it, for example an air flow that supplies energy to a vibrating wing. CK says that avoiding such a growing vibration (flutter) is essential in the design of all airplanes (PDF p. 86). One statement treats all combinations of the signs of \(c\) and \(k\). For it we distinguish a rest to which the motion returns from a rest that the motion merely does not leave. The second is the stability of section 8.12, the first gets its name in the next definition.

Definition 9.47 Asymptotically stable equilibrium (asimptotično stabilno ravnovesje)

Let \(\vek q_0\) be a stable equilibrium of a system with \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) by definition 8.112. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable (asimptotično stabilno) if there is a number \(\delta_0 > 0\) such that for every motion that is defined for every \(t \ge 0\) and has \(|\vek q(0) - \vek q_0| < \delta_0\) and \(|\dot{\vek q}(0)| < \delta_0\) we have \(\vek q(t) \to \vek q_0\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(t) \to \vek 0\) as \(t \to \infty\).

Source: The definition is not from the source. CK §3.3 (PDF p. 85) classifies the motion of the equation \(a\ddot u + b\dot u + \dots\) by the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation and says about asymptotically stable motion: "If both roots of the characteristic equation lie in the left half-plane ... with time the motion will die out".

Equation (9.37) is the Lagrange equation of a system with the kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2\), the potential \(V = \tfrac12 k q^2\) and the generalized force \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\dot q\), also when \(c < 0\) or \(k < 0\) (theorem 8.88). The constant \(q = 0\) is its solution, thus an equilibrium, and definitions 8.112 and 9.47 judge it with \(n = 1\) and \(\vek q_0 = 0\). By theorem 9.36 the solutions exist for all times, so the conditions "on every \([0, \tau]\)" and "for every \(t \ge 0\)" mean the same.

Theorem 9.48 Stability of a linear system with one degree of freedom (stabilnost linearnega sistema z eno prostostno stopnjo)

Let \(m > 0\) and let \(c\) and \(k\) be arbitrary real numbers. The equilibrium \(q = 0\) of the equation \(m\ddot q + c\dot q + k q = 0\) is

  1. asymptotically stable exactly when \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\),
  2. stable but not asymptotically stable exactly when \(c = 0 < k\) or \(k = 0 < c\). In the first case the motion is a bounded vibration that does not decay, in the second \(q(t)\) tends to the constant \(q_0 + m\dot q_0/c\), which depends on the start,
  3. unstable in all other cases: for \(k < 0\), for \(c < 0\) and \(k \ge 0\), and for \(c = k = 0\). For \(k < 0\) the solution grows exponentially without vibration (divergence, divergenca). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 < 4mk\) it is a vibration with an exponentially growing amplitude (flutter), for \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 \ge 4mk\) an exponential growth without vibration. For \(c < 0 = k\) the growth is exponential, for \(c = k = 0\) we have \(q = q_0 + \dot q_0 t\).
Proof

Idea: every motion is a combination of two special solutions \(u\) and \(v\), so only these two decide about stability. Then we compute them case by case, as separated by the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation.

  1. Let \(u\) be the solution with \(u(0) = 1\), \(\dot u(0) = 0\) and \(v\) the solution with \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v(0) = 1\) (they exist for every \(t \ge 0\) by theorem 9.36). The solution with \(q(0) = q_0\), \(\dot q(0) = \dot q_0\) is \(q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\), because by lemma 9.6 this function is a solution and has the same start, and uniqueness holds by theorem 9.36. Hence \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\) and \(\dot q = q_0\dot u + \dot q_0\dot v\).
  2. The equilibrium is stable exactly when \(u\), \(\dot u\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) are bounded on \([0, \infty)\). If they are bounded by \(M \ge 1\), for a given \(\varepsilon\) we take \(\delta = \varepsilon/(2M)\): for a start with \(|q_0| < \delta\), \(|\dot q_0| < \delta\) we have \(|q| \le M(|q_0| + |\dot q_0|) < 2M\delta = \varepsilon\) and the same for \(\dot q\). Conversely, if the equilibrium is stable, we take \(\varepsilon = 1\) and the corresponding \(\delta\). The motion with the start \((\delta/2, 0)\) has \(|\delta u(t)/2| < 1\) and \(|\delta\dot u(t)/2| < 1\) for every \(t \ge 0\), so \(u\) and \(\dot u\) are bounded. The same with the start \((0, \delta/2)\) for \(v\) and \(\dot v\).
  3. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable exactly when \(u\), \(\dot u\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) tend to zero. If they do, they are bounded, so the equilibrium is stable, and for every start \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v \to 0\) and \(\dot q \to 0\). Conversely, the starts \((\delta_0/2, 0)\) and \((0, \delta_0/2)\) give that \(u\), \(\dot u\) and \(v\), \(\dot v\) tend to zero.
  4. Let \(s_1\), \(s_2\) be the zeros of the polynomial \(P(s) = m s^2 + c s + k = m(s - s_1)(s - s_2)\). By expanding the product, \(s_1 + s_2 = -c/m\) and \(s_1 s_2 = k/m\). For \(c^2 < 4mk\) the zeros are conjugate, \(\sigma \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega\) with \(\sigma = -c/(2m)\) and \(\omega > 0\), and \(k > 0\) holds automatically. For \(c^2 \ge 4mk\) they are real. We claim: both zeros have a negative real part exactly when \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\). In the complex case the real part \(-c/(2m)\) is negative exactly when \(c > 0\), and \(k > 0\) already holds. In the real case both zeros are negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product positive (equal signs, the sum tells which), that is \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\).
  5. The case \(c > 0\), \(k > 0\). By step 3 we must show that \(u\), \(v\) and their derivatives tend to zero, and the zeros have a negative real part by step 4. By theorem 9.39 and corollary 9.40, \(u\) and \(v\) are linear combinations of the functions \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\cos\omega t\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}\sin\omega t\), or \(\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t}\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{s_2 t}\), or \(\mathrm{e}^{st}\) and \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{st}\), with the real part of the zero negative. The derivative of each of these functions is a combination of the same functions (the rules for the derivative of a product and the derivative of sine and cosine, theorem 8.12). All tend to zero, because \(|\cos| \le 1\), \(|\sin| \le 1\) and \(t\,\mathrm{e}^{-at} \to 0\) for \(a > 0\) (lemma 9.15). The equilibrium is asymptotically stable.
  6. The case \(c = 0 < k\). Let \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\). By theorem 8.120, \(u = \cos\omega t\) and \(v = (\sin\omega t)/\omega\), with the derivatives \(-\omega\sin\omega t\) and \(\cos\omega t\). All are bounded, the equilibrium is stable (step 2). Because \(u(2\pi j/\omega) = 1\) for every integer \(j\), \(u\) does not tend to zero, and the equilibrium is not asymptotically stable (step 3).
  7. The case \(k = 0 < c\). The zeros are \(0\) and \(-c/m\). The functions \(u = 1\) and \(v = (m/c)\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-ct/m}\bigr)\) are solutions: for \(v\) we have \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v = \mathrm{e}^{-ct/m}\) with \(\dot v(0) = 1\), and \(m\ddot v + c\dot v = -c\,\mathrm{e}^{-ct/m} + c\,\mathrm{e}^{-ct/m} = 0\). The functions \(u\), \(\dot u = 0\), \(v\), \(\dot v\) are bounded, the equilibrium is stable. Because \(u = 1\) does not tend to zero, it is not asymptotically stable. The solution \(q = q_0 u + \dot q_0 v\) tends to \(q_0 + m\dot q_0/c\).
  8. The unstable cases. We show that \(v\) is not bounded, and by step 2 the equilibrium is not stable. For \(k < 0\) we have \(s_1 s_2 < 0\), so the polynomial has a positive zero \(s_1\) and a negative one \(s_2\). The function \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t})/(s_1 - s_2)\) has \(v(0) = 0\), \(\dot v(0) = (s_1 - s_2)/(s_1 - s_2) = 1\), it is a solution by theorem 9.39, and it tends to \(\infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 < 4mk\) we have \(v = \mathrm{e}^{\sigma t}(\sin\omega t)/\omega\) with \(\sigma > 0\) (we check the start as in step 2 of the proof of theorem 9.43) and at \(t_{\mathrm{j}} = (\pi/2 + j\pi)/\omega\) we have \(|v| = \mathrm{e}^{\sigma t_{\mathrm{j}}}/\omega \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 > 4mk\) the zeros are distinct and positive (\(s_1 s_2 > 0\), \(s_1 + s_2 > 0\)), and \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{s_1 t} - \mathrm{e}^{s_2 t})/(s_1 - s_2) \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 < k\) and \(c^2 = 4mk\) there is a double zero \(s = -c/(2m) > 0\) and \(v = t\,\mathrm{e}^{st} \to \infty\). For \(c < 0 = k\) the zeros are \(0\) and \(s = -c/m > 0\), \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{st} - 1)/s \to \infty\). For \(c = k = 0\) we have \(v = t\) and \(u = 1\).
  9. The pairs \((c, k)\) split into the three exhausted groups of steps 5, 6 and 7 (\(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\), \(c = 0 < k\), \(k = 0 < c\)) and all others: \(k < 0\) for every \(c\), \(c < 0 \le k\) and \(c = k = 0\). All pairs of the second group are in step 8, which is point 3.

Source: CK §3.3, (3.40) to (3.44), PDF pp. 84 to 87 (page 85 read: the classification by the position of the zeros in the left half-plane, on the imaginary axis and in the right half-plane, figure 3.10, flutter and divergence on p. 86). The proof by steps is not from the source, CK classifies the motions by the position of the zeros and does not prove it.

The proof showed that the position of the zeros of the characteristic equation decides stability, not the start (figure 9.16). Both zeros in the left half-plane (\(\operatorname{Re} s < 0\)) mean asymptotic stability, a zero in the right half-plane (\(\operatorname{Re} s > 0\)) means instability. On the imaginary axis the multiplicity also decides: a conjugate pair \(\pm\mathrm{i}\,\omega\) and a simple zero \(0\) with the other zero in the left half-plane give stability, but a double zero \(0\) (\(c = k = 0\)) does not, because the solution \(q_0 + \dot q_0 t\) runs away.

The condition \(c > 0\) and \(k > 0\) can be drawn as a quarter of the parameter plane (figure 9.17). The parabola \(c^2 = 4mk\) separates vibration from non-vibration.

Let us look at two more examples, one with a negative stiffness and one with a damper on the pendulum of section 8.13.

Example 9.49 Inverted bar above the critical force (obrnjena palica nad kritično silo)

Example 8.64 of section 8.6: a massless rigid bar of length \(L = 1\) m, pinned at the bottom, a torsional spring \(k_{\mathrm{r}} = 2000\) N m, a vertical force \(P\) downwards at the top, \(\theta\) measured from the vertical upwards. In that chapter it was a model of a column, here we add motion to it. The body with the mass \(m = 10\) kg at the top is our addition to the model (the bar remains massless): we count its weight (98 N) into \(P\), and the force \(P = 2200\) N is a constant vertical force (for example the pressure of a hydraulic cylinder). The critical force of section 8.6 is \(P_{\mathrm{kr}} = k_{\mathrm{r}}/L = 2000\) N, so \(P = 1{,}1\,P_{\mathrm{kr}}\). The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12 m L^2\dot\theta^2\), \(V = \tfrac12 k_{\mathrm{r}}\theta^2 + PL\cos\theta\) and by theorem 8.118 the linearized equation is \(m L^2\ddot\theta + (k_{\mathrm{r}} - PL)\,\theta = 0\), that is \(10\,\ddot\theta - 200\,\theta = 0\). The stiffness is \(k = k_{\mathrm{r}} - PL = -200\) N m \(< 0\), and \(c = 0\). By point 3 of theorem 9.48 the equilibrium of the linearized equation is unstable. Section 8.13 showed only the linear estimate \(|\theta| \ge \varepsilon\,\sqrt{|k|/(mL^2)}\,t\).

Now we get the exact solution. The characteristic polynomial \(10 s^2 - 200\) has the zeros \(\pm\beta\), \(\beta = \sqrt{20} = 4{,}4721\) 1/s. By theorem 9.39, \(\theta = a\,\mathrm{e}^{\beta t} + b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta t}\). With \(\theta(0) = \theta_0\) and \(\dot\theta(0) = 0\) it follows from \(a + b = \theta_0\) and \(\beta(a - b) = 0\) that \(a = b = \theta_0/2\) and

\[\theta(t) = \theta_0\operatorname{cosh}\beta t . \tag{9.44}\]

The solution does not vibrate: it grows with the exponential function. The time in which it grows by the factor \(\mathrm{e}\) is \(1/\beta = 0{,}2236\) s. With \(\theta_0 = 0{,}001\) rad it reaches \(0{,}1\) rad when \(\operatorname{cosh}\beta t = 100\), that is after \(t = 1{,}1847\) s. For \(P = 1800\) N, that is at \(0{,}9\,P_{\mathrm{kr}}\) instead of \(1{,}1\,P_{\mathrm{kr}}\), we have \(k = +200\) N m and \(\theta = \theta_0\cos(4{,}4721\,t)\) with the period \(1{,}405\) s (figure 9.18). The force changed by \(18\ \%\), and the motion jumped from vibration to an exponential escape. The linear equation holds only while \(\theta\) is small: at \(0{,}1\) rad, \(\sin\theta\) is smaller than \(\theta\) by \(0{,}17\ \%\) (lemma 8.19), later the model does not hold and the motion is governed by the nonlinear equation (section 8.6 found the curved equilibria). The same solution is given by CK in example 3.1 (PDF pp. 87 to 88) for an inverted pendulum with springs, for \(2k/m - g/L < 0\).

Example 9.50 Pendulum with a damper (nihalo z dušilnikom)

The pendulum of section 8.11: a point mass \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid light bar of length \(\ell = 1\) m, in the joint a damping moment \(-c\dot\theta\) with \(c = 0{,}2\) N m s (example 8.105). Around the lower position \(\theta = 0\), by theorem 9.3 (with \(M = m\ell^2\), \(V'' = m g\ell\) and a constant \(c\)) the linearized equation is \(m\ell^2\,\ddot\theta + c\dot\theta + m g\ell\,\theta = 0\), that is \(\ddot\theta + 0{,}2\,\dot\theta + 9{,}81\,\theta = 0\) with the numbers \(m\ell^2 = 1\) kg m\(^2\) and \(m g\ell = 9{,}81\) J. Dirichlet's criterion (theorem 8.113) gave only stability: \(V'' = 9{,}81\) J, so the motion stays close. By point 1 of theorem 9.48, now that \(c = 0{,}2 > 0\) and \(k = 9{,}81 > 0\), the linearized equilibrium is asymptotically stable: the pendulum returns to the equilibrium. The numbers: \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 3{,}1321\) 1/s, \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km}) = 0{,}03193\), the zeros \(-0{,}1 \pm 3{,}1305\,\mathrm{i}\) 1/s, the ratio after a period \(0{,}8182\), the time to half amplitude \(\ln 2/0{,}1 = 6{,}93\) s. The theorem for the linearized equation holds for small deflections. That the real, nonlinear pendulum also settles can be seen in example 8.105 from the RK4 computation (the energy falls towards \(-9{,}81\) J), we do not give a proof of it.

The inverted pendulum at \(\theta = \pi\) has \(k = -m g\ell = -9{,}81\) J and also \(c = 0{,}2\). Point 3 says that it is unstable even with the damper: the zeros are \((-0{,}2 \pm \sqrt{0{,}04 + 39{,}24})/2 = 3{,}0337\) and \(-3{,}2337\) 1/s, and \(v = (\mathrm{e}^{3{,}0337\,t} - \mathrm{e}^{-3{,}2337\,t})/6{,}2674\) grows. A damper cannot replace stiffness, because it cannot restore a position: its force depends only on the velocity.

For system A at \(\zeta = 0{,}01\) the answer to the initial question is now complete. The period hardly changes (\(0{,}177412\) s against \(0{,}177403\) s), the amplitude falls to one half in \(1{,}957\) s (11 periods) and to \(1\ \%\) in \(13{,}00\) s. There would be no vibration at all for \(c \ge c_{\mathrm{kr}} = 3541{,}75\) N s/m, that is for a hundred times larger damping, and the weight settles fastest to \(1\ \%\) just above \(\zeta_* = 0{,}8261\) (in \(0{,}118\) s), not at critical damping (\(0{,}187\) s). But what if a force acts on the weight all the time? Section 9.6 shows that the free motion, which dies out after \(13\) s, is replaced by the motion under the force, and that what remains depends on the frequency of the force.
