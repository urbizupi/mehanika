Chapter 6 Weak form, the Ritz and Galerkin method (šibka oblika, Ritzova in Galerkinova metoda)

6.2 Mathematics: functions with kinks

Four graphs share the \(x\) axis from \(0\) to \(10\) m. The stiffness \(EA\) jumps at \(x = 5\) m by a factor of \(4\). The axial force \(N\) is continuous. So the derivative \(u' = N/EA\) must jump, and the displacement \(u\) has a kink. We cut the integral into two pieces, on each the derivative is continuous. The trial fields of the finite element method are built from straight pieces with kinks.

The displacement of the stepped bar (section 6.1.4) has a kink. At \(x = 5\) m the stiffness \(EA\) changes abruptly by a factor of \(4\), while the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) is continuous there, so the derivative \(u'\) must jump. Such a displacement has no second derivative and not even a derivative at every point. The trial fields of the finite element method (chapter 7) will be built from straight pieces that meet in kinks, so a kink is not a rare exception but the general rule. Chapter 4, however, proved the principle of virtual displacements and the principle of minimum energy for a bar for fields whose derivative is continuous on the whole bar. Do the two theorems stay true when the derivative jumps?

The answer is yes and it is short, because we cut the integral into pieces. First we say what a function that we differentiate piece by piece is and what its integral is. Then we prove the fundamental theorem of calculus and integration by parts for such functions, and at the end we repeat the proofs of chapter 4 for the bar and of chapter 5 for the beam. The general mathematical setting, in which all this is written without pieces, we only name.

6.2.1 Piecewise continuously differentiable functions

We met a field with kinks in section 1.15.5: the graph breaks along a line and the derivative does not exist there. For a function of one variable a kink is a point where the graph breaks and has different slopes to the left and to the right of it. Away from the kinks the function is smooth. So we describe it piece by piece. First we describe functions that are continuous piece by piece, then those that are continuously differentiable piece by piece.

Definition 6.8 Piecewise continuous function (odsekoma zvezna funkcija)

Let \([a, b]\) be a closed interval. A function \(f\), which takes a number \(x\) as input and gives the number \(f(x)\) as output, is piecewise continuous (odsekoma zvezna) on \([a, b]\) if there is a partition of the interval (definition 3.46) \(a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = b\) and for each \(k = 1, \dots, m\) a function \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\), continuous on the closed interval \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\), such that \(f(x) = f_{\mathrm{k}}(x)\) for every \(x\) in the open interval \((x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}})\). The points \(x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{m}-1}\) are called break points (delilne točke).

Source: KREYSZIG §6.1 (PDF p. 235): a function is piecewise continuous if the interval can be divided into finitely many subintervals, on each of which the function is continuous and has finite limits as the endpoints are approached from the interior. The notation with the functions \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) is ours.

The function \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) is determined by \(f\), since its values at the ends of the piece (the filled dots in figure 6.31 on the right) are the limits of the values of \(f\) as the end is approached from inside the piece (lemma 1.81). The values of \(f\) at the break points do not interest us, so we may leave them undefined. If a function is piecewise continuous with some partition, it is piecewise continuous with every finer partition too, since we only restrict the functions \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) to subintervals. For finitely many piecewise continuous functions there is therefore a common partition, whose break points are the union of the break points of all of them. We will always take such functions on a common partition.

The requirement that \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous on the closed piece is essential. Take \(f(x) = 1/\sqrt{x}\) on \((0, 1]\) (we do not need the value at \(0\), figure 6.31 left). The function is continuous on every closed interval \([c, 1]\) with \(c > 0\), but it is not piecewise continuous. It would be piecewise continuous if for some partition there were a function \(f_1\), continuous on the closed interval \([0, x_1]\), that equals \(f\) on \((0, x_1)\). Such a function does not exist:

  1. A continuous function on a closed interval attains its greatest and least value (theorem 1.74), so it is bounded, \(|f_1| \le M\) for some number \(M > 0\). Its whole graph would have to stay below the horizontal line at height \(M\).
  2. We take \(x\) with \(0 < x < x_1\) and \(x < 1/(M+1)^2\). Then \(f_1(x) = f(x) = 1/\sqrt{x} > M + 1\), which contradicts step 1. Since this holds for every partition, \(f\) is not piecewise continuous.

The function with a jump, \(f = 0\) on \((0, \tfrac12)\) and \(f = 1\) on \((\tfrac12, 1)\), passes the test with \(f_1 = 0\) and \(f_2 = 1\). The jump is therefore not an obstacle, since every piece has its own continuous function up to the endpoint: at the break point \(\tfrac12\) each piece has its own value, \(f_1(\tfrac12) = 0\) and \(f_2(\tfrac12) = 1\). The obstacle is unboundedness at the endpoint.

A piecewise continuous function is integrable on every piece, because it is continuous there (theorem 3.53). The integral over the whole interval is therefore the sum of the integrals over the pieces, just as the area under the axial force is the sum of the areas on the two halves.

Definition 6.9 Integral of a piecewise continuous function (integral odsekoma zvezne funkcije)

Let \(f\) be a piecewise continuous function on \([a, b]\) with the partition \(a = x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = b\) and the functions \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) of definition 6.8. Its integral (integral) is the number

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^m \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} f_{\mathrm{k}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x , \tag{6.5}\]

where each term is the Riemann integral of a continuous function (definition 3.49). That the number does not depend on the choice of the partition is shown by item 1 of the next lemma.

Source: The definition is ours, since the books only use integrals of piecewise continuous functions. For a continuous function the partition may be just \(x_0 = a\), \(x_1 = b\) and the definition gives the integral of definition 3.49.

Figure 6.32 shows what a common partition is. The first row has the partition \(D\), the second the partition \(D'\), the third their union, which has all the points of both. Below the rows is the graph of \(f\): on every piece of the common partition both partitions gave the same function.

Figure 6.33 shows linearity on one piece. The functions \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\) are drawn with their areas, and below them the sum \(f_{\mathrm{k}} + g_{\mathrm{k}}\), whose area is the sum of the two areas. The function \(\alpha f_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a stretched \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) and has an area \(\alpha\) times larger. On each row the horizontal axis has the ends \(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the piece.

Figure 6.34 shows a nonnegative function \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) on one piece. The graph lies above the axis and the area under it is not negative. If the value at the point \(x_0\) is positive, then by continuity the whole band \([c, d]\) lies above the half height, and under it is a rectangle of area \(\tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{k}}(x_0)(d - c)\). The rest of the piece is the left and right part.

Lemma 6.10 Properties of the integral of piecewise continuous functions (lastnosti integrala odsekoma zveznih funkcij)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be piecewise continuous functions on \([a, b]\) and \(\alpha\) a real number.

  1. The integral does not depend on the partition: if two partitions are given with which \(f\) is piecewise continuous, equation (6.5) gives the same number for both.
  2. Linearity (area under the sum, under a multiple): \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (f + g)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x + \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\mathrm{d}x\) and \(\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \alpha f\,\mathrm{d}x = \alpha \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. Nonnegativity: if on a common partition \(f_{\mathrm{k}}(x) \ge 0\) for every \(x \in [x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) and every \(k\), then \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b f\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\). If in addition the integral is zero, then \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\) on every closed piece.
Proof

We must show three items. The first and the second follow from additivity and linearity of the integral on a piece, the third is an argument from the proof of corollary 4.93, written for a piece.

1. Independence of the partition.

  1. Let \(c\) be a point of the open piece \((x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}})\). By additivity (lemma 3.54, item 2), \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} f_{\mathrm{k}} = \int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{c} f_{\mathrm{k}} + \int_{\mathrm{c}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} f_{\mathrm{k}}\). So the sum over pieces does not change when a break point is added. A numerical case is in figure 6.42 on the left: the area under the axial force \(N\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 60{,}10 \cdot 10 = 300{,}5\) N m, and with an added break point at \(5\) m the sum of \(225{,}4\) and \(75{,}1\) is again \(300{,}5\) N m.
  1. Two given partitions \(D\) and \(D'\) have a common finer partition, obtained by uniting their points. We reach it from \(D\) by adding finitely many points and likewise from \(D'\). The vertical guide lines connect the same points in all rows. On a piece of the common partition the functions given by \(D\) and \(D'\) are equal: both are continuous on the closed piece and equal to \(f\) on the open piece, so they agree at the ends too (as for the function \(f_{\mathrm{k}}\) above). By step 1, \(D\) and \(D'\) give the same sum as the common partition.

2. Linearity.

  1. We take the common partition of the functions \(f\) and \(g\). The ends of a piece, \(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\), are on the horizontal axis. On every closed piece \(f_{\mathrm{k}} + g_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\alpha f_{\mathrm{k}}\) are continuous (corollary 1.65) and equal to \(f + g\) and \(\alpha f\) on the open piece. By linearity of the integral on a piece (lemma 3.54, item 1) the equality holds on every piece: the areas under \(f\) and \(g\) add up to the area under the sum. We sum over the pieces.

3. Nonnegativity.

  1. By monotonicity on a piece (lemma 3.54, item 4, with the zero function) every term of the sum is nonnegative, hence so is the sum.
  2. Let the sum be zero. Since every term is nonnegative, every term is zero. Suppose that \(f_{\mathrm{k}}(x_0) > 0\) at some point \(x_0\) of the piece, \(x_0 \in [x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) (figure 6.37). By continuity (definition 1.59) with the allowed error \(f_{\mathrm{k}}(x_0)/2\) there is a closed interval from \(c\) to \(d\), \([c, d] \subseteq [x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) with \(c < d\), which contains \(x_0\) and on which \(f_{\mathrm{k}} > f_{\mathrm{k}}(x_0)/2\).
  3. By additivity the integral over the piece is the sum of the integrals over \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, c]\), \([c, d]\) and \([d, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) (if \(c = x_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) or \(d = x_{\mathrm{k}}\), the corresponding term drops out, because the integral over an interval of length zero is zero). The first and the third are not negative by step 4, and the middle one is by the bound (lemma 3.54, item 3) not smaller than \(\tfrac12 f_{\mathrm{k}}(x_0)\,(d - c) > 0\), the area of the rectangle that lies under the graph. This contradicts the integral over the piece being zero. So \(f_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. Item 3 is the same argument as in steps 6 and 7 of the proof of corollary 4.93.

Now we can say what a function is that we may differentiate piece by piece.

Definition 6.11 Piecewise continuously differentiable function (odsekoma zvezno odvedljiva funkcija)

Let \([a, b]\) be a closed interval. A function \(u\), which takes a number \(x\) as input and gives the number \(u(x)\) as output, is piecewise continuously differentiable (odsekoma zvezno odvedljiva) on \([a, b]\) if:

  1. \(u\) is continuous on \([a, b]\),
  2. there is a partition \(a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}} = b\), and on every open piece \((x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}})\) the function \(u\) is differentiable and its derivative \(u'\) is there equal to a function \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\), continuous on the closed piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\).

The derivative \(u'\) is defined everywhere except at the break points and is a piecewise continuous function by definition 6.8. At a break point \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) the function \(u\) has the left derivative \(g_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) and the right derivative \(g_{\mathrm{k}+1}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\), which may be different.

Source: The definition is ours, we met a kink in section 1.15.5. KREYSZIG §6.1 (PDF p. 235) has only piecewise continuous functions, not their derivatives.

Requirement 1 is essential, because the derivative on the pieces does not register a jump: a function that jumps at a break point does not satisfy it (the example in the remark below lemma 6.12). A function we know from chapter 4, that is a function differentiable at every point of the interval with a continuous derivative, is piecewise continuously differentiable with a single partition, \(m = 1\). The stepped bar is the first example with two pieces.

Requirement 2 excludes functions that are continuous and differentiable in the interior but have an unbounded derivative at an endpoint. Take \(w = \sqrt{x}\) on \([0, 1]\). The function is continuous, \(w(0) = 0\), it is differentiable on \((0, 1)\) and \(w' = 1/(2\sqrt{x})\). It differs from the function with a kink only at the endpoint \(x = 0\), where its graph has a vertical tangent (figure 6.38), whereas the derivative of the function with a kink is bounded everywhere.

  1. For every partition, \(w'\) on \((0, x_1)\) would have to equal a function \(g_1\) continuous on \([0, x_1]\). Such a function is bounded (theorem 1.74), \(|g_1| \le M\). The slope of the graph on this piece would therefore never be allowed to exceed \(M\).
  2. For \(x\) with \(0 < x < x_1\) and \(x < 1/\big(4(M+1)^2\big)\) we have \(w'(x) = 1/(2\sqrt{x}) > M + 1\). The graph is there steeper than every bound, because near \(x = 0\) it fits the vertical tangent. This contradicts step 1, so \(w\) is not piecewise continuously differentiable.

We see the consequence in the energy. For \(EA = 1\) we have \(\int_\varepsilon^1 w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_\varepsilon^1 \tfrac{1}{4x}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac14 \ln(1/\varepsilon)\), the area under the graph of \(w'^2\) to the right of the vertical line at \(\varepsilon\). As we move the line towards zero the area grows: for \(\varepsilon = 10^{-4}\) this is \(2{,}30\), for \(\varepsilon = 10^{-8}\) it is \(4{,}61\), and as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) the value grows beyond all bounds. The function \(w\) therefore does not have a finite energy, such as the function with a kink has, since its derivative is bounded.

Definition 6.2 writes the same class differently, with a function that is differentiable on every closed piece with a continuous derivative, one-sided at the ends. The two classes are equal. Let \(u\) be piecewise continuously differentiable and \(x\) a point of the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) to the left of \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) \(\big(u(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - u(x)\big)/(x_{\mathrm{k}} - x) = g_{\mathrm{k}}(\xi)\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\). As \(x\) tends to \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\xi\) tends to \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(g_{\mathrm{k}}(\xi)\) to \(g_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\), because \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuous. The left derivative of \(u\) at \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) is therefore \(g_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}})\), likewise the right derivative at \(x_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) is \(g_{\mathrm{k}}(x_{\mathrm{k}-1})\), so \(u\) is differentiable on the closed piece with the continuous derivative \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\). For the stepped bar in figure 6.39 these are the slopes of the grey segments at the kink, the left derivative \(g_1(5) = 4{,}880 \cdot 10^{-7}\) and the right derivative \(g_2(5) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the values at the open circles. Conversely, a function of definition 6.2 satisfies both items of the definition, with \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\) its derivative on the closed piece. The admissible functions of a bar are therefore exactly the piecewise continuously differentiable functions \(w\) with \(w(0) = 0\).

The stepped bar (section 6.1.4, figure 6.39): the upper \(5\) m have a diameter of \(10\) mm, the cross-sectional area is \(A\), and the lower \(5\) m have a diameter of \(5\) mm and area \(A/4\). It hangs under its own weight, fixed at the top. The axial force is \(N(5) = 7{,}512\) N on both sides of the step, because it is the weight of the lower part. The derivative of the displacement \(u' = N/EA\) is therefore at \(x = 5\) m \(4{,}880 \cdot 10^{-7}\) from above and \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\) from below, four times larger, because \(EA\) below is four times smaller. The displacement is continuous, \(u(5) = 7{,}320\) µm from both sides, but its derivative is not: at the kink the grey segments show the slope before the point and after it. This is a piecewise continuously differentiable function with the partition \(0, 5, 10\), \(g_1 = N/(EA)\) on the upper piece and \(g_2 = N/(E A/4)\) on the lower one.

The simplest example of a kink is a function \(w\) in the shape of a roof on an interval with points \(x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < x_3\), which is zero at \(x_0\) and from \(x_2\) on, has the value \(1\) at \(x_1\) and is a straight line between these points (figure 6.40). The points are equally spaced, \(x_1 - x_0 = x_2 - x_1 = 3/2\), so the slopes are \(2/3\) and \(-2/3\). The derivative is constant on three pieces, \(2/3\), \(-2/3\) and \(0\), and at the break points \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) it jumps by \(-4/3\) and \(2/3\). Such a function is the basic building block of trial fields made of straight pieces, which we will use in the rest of the chapter and in chapter 7.

The first theorem of analysis that we need is the fundamental theorem. It says that for a piecewise continuously differentiable function the values at the break points cancel when we sum over the pieces.

Lemma 6.12 Fundamental theorem of calculus for a function with kinks (osnovni izrek analize za funkcijo s pregibi)

Let \(u\) be a piecewise continuously differentiable function on \([a, b]\). Then the difference at the ends

\[u(b) - u(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b u'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{6.6}\]
Proof

We must show that the differences of the values of the function \(u\) over the pieces add up to the difference over the whole interval. On every piece we use the fundamental theorem of calculus, and the intermediate values cancel because \(u\) is continuous.

  1. Let \(x_0 < \dots < x_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the break points of definition 6.11. On the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}}]\) the function \(u\) is continuous (item 1 of the definition), differentiable at every point of the open piece, and its derivative there equals the continuous function \(g_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the closed piece (item 2).
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55, item 1, with \(G = u\) and \(f = g_{\mathrm{k}}\) on the piece) \(\int_{\mathrm{x}_{\mathrm{k}-1}}^{x_{\mathrm{k}}} g_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mathrm{d}x = u(x_{\mathrm{k}}) - u(x_{\mathrm{k}-1})\). This is the increment of the function over piece \(k\), marked \(\Delta_{\mathrm{k}}\) in figure 6.41.
  3. We sum over \(k = 1, \dots, m\). By equation (6.5) the left side is the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b u'\,\mathrm{d}x\). On the right every intermediate term \(u(x_{\mathrm{k}})\) appears twice, once with a plus sign as the end of piece \(k\) and once with a minus sign as the beginning of piece \(k+1\). Since \(u\) is continuous, both values are the same number. What remains is \(u(x_{\mathrm{m}}) - u(x_0) = u(b) - u(a)\) (figure 6.41). In the figure \(u\) is made of three pieces, and the values at the break points are carried to the right along the dashed lines. There the increments of the pieces are stacked on each other and their sum is \(u(b) - u(a)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The statement is LEBL1 exercise 5.3.3 (PDF p. 205), there for a function that is differentiable everywhere except at finitely many points. The fundamental theorem is theorem 3.55 of chapter 3.

Let us test the theorem on the stepped bar. The increments over the pieces are \(\int_0^5 u'\,\mathrm{d}x = 7{,}320\) µm and \(\int_5^{10} u'\,\mathrm{d}x = 4{,}880\) µm, the areas under the derivative in figure 6.39, and their sum is \(12{,}20\) µm, which is exactly \(u(10) - u(0)\), because \(u(0) = 0\). Piece by piece it is the same computation as in section 6.1.4.

The continuity of \(u\) is necessary in step 3. The function that is \(0\) on \([0, 1/2]\) and \(1\) on \((1/2, 1]\) (figure 6.31 on the right) has derivative zero wherever it is differentiable, so \(\int_0^1 u'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\), while \(u(1) - u(0) = 1\). The jump of the function disappears, because the derivative on the pieces does not see it.

From the fundamental theorem follows integration by parts, which we need for the theorem on virtual displacements.

Lemma 6.13 Integration by parts for functions with kinks (integracija po delih za funkcije s pregibi)

Let \(N\) and \(w\) be piecewise continuously differentiable functions on \([a, b]\) on a common partition. Then

\[\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b N(x)\,w'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = N(b)\,w(b) - N(a)\,w(a) - \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b N'(x)\,w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x . \tag{6.7}\]
Proof

We must show that the two integrals differ by the difference of the product \(N w\) at the ends. The product is again piecewise continuously differentiable, so we use lemma 6.12.

  1. Let \(G = N w\) on \([a, b]\). The functions \(N\) and \(w\) are continuous by item 1 of definition 6.11, so their product is continuous too (corollary 1.65). In figure 6.42 on the left these are the axial force \(N\) and the roof \(w\), both without a jump.
  2. On the open piece \((x_{\mathrm{k}-1}, x_{\mathrm{k}})\) the functions \(N\) and \(w\) are differentiable, so by the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3) \(G' = N'\,w + N\,w'\). On the closed piece the right side equals the continuous function \(g_{\mathrm{k}}^{N}\,w + N\,g_{\mathrm{k}}^{w}\), where \(g_{\mathrm{k}}^N\) and \(g_{\mathrm{k}}^w\) are the continuous functions of the definition for \(N\) and \(w\), and a product and a sum of continuous functions are continuous (corollary 1.65). So the function \(G\) is piecewise continuously differentiable.
  3. By lemma 6.12, \(G(b) - G(a) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (N'\,w + N\,w')\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. By linearity (lemma 6.10, item 2) the right side is the sum of the integrals \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b N'\,w\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b N\,w'\). We subtract the first on both sides and obtain equation (6.7).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The same theorem without kinks is corollary 3.56 of chapter 3 (LEBL1 exercise 5.3.5, PDF p. 205).

What did the lemma show? Along every piece integration by parts works as before, and the terms at the break points cancel in the sum if \(N\) and \(w\) are continuous at the break point. Let us test this on the hanging bar of chapter 3: \(L = 10\) m, \(\rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m, axial force \(N(x) = \rho g A\,(L - x)\) and a function \(w\) in the shape of a roof with a peak of \(1\) mm at the middle, which is \(0\) at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) and a straight line between these points (partition \(0, 5, 10\) m, figure 6.42 on the left). The derivative is \(w' = 2 \cdot 10^{-4}\) on the upper half and \(-2 \cdot 10^{-4}\) on the lower, \(N' = -\rho g A\). The left side is

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^{L} N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x &= 2 \cdot 10^{-4} \Big(\int_0^{5} N\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_5^{10} N\,\mathrm{d}x\Big) \\ &= 2 \cdot 10^{-4}\,(225{,}375 - 75{,}125)\ \text{N m} = 0{,}03005\ \text{N m} . \end{aligned}\]

The numbers in the brackets are the areas under the graph of the axial force, \(225{,}4\) N m on the upper half and \(75{,}1\) N m on the lower. The right side is \(N(L)\,w(L) - N(0)\,w(0) - \int_0^{L} N'\,w\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 - 0 + \rho g A \int_0^{L} w\,\mathrm{d}x = 6{,}010 \cdot 5 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{N m} = 0{,}03005\ \text{N m}\), because the area of the triangle under the graph of \(w\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 10\ \text{m} \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 5 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2\). The two sides are equal. The terms at the ends are zero because \(w(0) = w(L) = 0\), and the term at the break point \(x = 5\) m cancels because \(N\) and \(w\) are continuous there.

What would happen if \(N\) were discontinuous at the break point? Take without units \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\), \(N = 2\) on \([0, \tfrac12]\) and \(N = 1\) on \((\tfrac12, 1]\), and a function \(w\) in the shape of a roof with a peak of \(1\) at \(x = \tfrac12\) (figure 6.42 on the right). The derivative is \(w' = 2\) on the left half and \(-2\) on the right. The left side is \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 1 \cdot (-2) \cdot \tfrac12 = 1\). On the right \(N'= 0\) on the pieces and \(w(0) = w(1) = 0\), so \(0\). The difference is \(1 = (2 - 1) \cdot w(\tfrac12)\), the jump of the function \(N\) at the break point times the value of \(w\) there. In step 1 of the proof of the lemma a continuous product is therefore required: at a jump of \(N\) the function \(G\) would jump and lemma 6.12 would not hold. Hence the lemma requires a continuous \(N\). For the stepped bar this condition is met, because the axial force is continuous by equilibrium (theorem 6.3, item 2(b)), even though \(u'\) is discontinuous.

6.2.2 The principle of minimum energy with kinks

Now we have the tool to repeat the proofs of chapter 4, that is theorem 4.76 on virtual displacements and corollary 4.93 on the minimum energy of a bar, for functions with kinks. The difference is twofold. The trial fields may have kinks, and the stiffness \(EA\) may be piecewise constant or piecewise continuous, as in the stepped bar. The continuity of the axial force across the break points is an assumption of the theorem, which equilibrium requires as well: equilibrium of the piece \([x_{\mathrm{k}} - h, x_{\mathrm{k}} + h]\) around the step \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) gives a difference of the axial forces at its ends that is at most \(2h \max|q|\) in absolute value, the size of the load on the piece, and tends to zero as \(h \to 0\), so the one-sided values of the axial force at \(x_{\mathrm{k}}\) are equal. The strain \(u' = N/EA\) does jump, because \(EA\) jumps.

We do the same for the beam, where we have the same problem: for a beam \(EI\) may jump, and so the curvature \(v''\) jumps, while the moment \(M = EI\,v''\) is continuous. The trial fields of a beam may have kinks of the second kind: \(v\) and \(v'\) are continuous and \(v''\) jumps (why \(v'\) must not jump is explained in the paragraph after the proof).

For the bar we draw the wall at \(x = 0\), the pieces with their own stiffness \(EA_{\mathrm{k}}\), the load \(q\) and the force \(F\) at the end. Below it are the true field \(u\), the trial field \(\tilde u\) and the virtual field \(\delta u\), all with kinks at the break points and zero at \(x = 0\), and at the bottom the axial force \(N\), continuous across the break points too.

For the beam we draw the beam, clamped at \(x = 0\), divided into pieces with their own stiffness \(EI_{\mathrm{k}}\), with the load \(q\) and the load at the end with a force \(f_{\mathrm{L}}\) and a moment \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}}\). Below it are the true field \(v\), the trial field \(\tilde v\) and the virtual field \(\delta v\), all with zero displacement and slope at the clamp, and at the bottom the shear force \(V\) and moment \(M\), both continuous.

Corollary 6.14 Virtual displacements and minimum energy with kinks (virtualni pomiki in najmanjša energija s pregibi)

Bar. The bar lies along the \(x\) axis from \(0\) to \(L\) and is fixed at \(x = 0\), as in theorem 4.76. Let the stiffness \(EA\) be piecewise continuous and positive on every closed piece, the axial load \(q\) piecewise continuous, and at the end \(x = L\) let a force \(F\) act in the direction of the axis, all independent of the deformation. An admissible field is an admissible function of a bar of the weak form (definition 6.2), that is a piecewise continuously differentiable function \(u\) on \([0, L]\) with \(u(0) = 0\) (definition 6.11). For an admissible field let

\[\Pi(u) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,u'^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,u\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,u(L) , \tag{6.8}\]

with the integrals of definition 6.9. Let \(u\) be an admissible field for which the axial force \(N = EA\,u'\) (on the open pieces) is a piecewise continuously differentiable function, continuous on \([0, L]\), with \(N' + q = 0\) on the open pieces and \(N(L) = F\). Then:

  1. for every admissible field \(\delta u\), \(\displaystyle\int_0^L N\,\delta u'\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,\delta u\,\mathrm{d}x + F\,\delta u(L)\), the left side is the work of the axial force, the right side the work of the load and the force \(F\),
  2. for every admissible field \(\tilde u\), \(\displaystyle\Pi(\tilde u) - \Pi(u) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,(\tilde u' - u')^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), because the integrand is nonnegative on every piece, with equality only for \(\tilde u = u\).

Beam. The beam lies along the \(x\) axis from \(0\) to \(L\) in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane with a piecewise continuous and positive stiffness \(EI\) and a piecewise continuous load \(q\) across the axis, signs as in chapter 5. An admissible field is a function \(v\) for which \(v\) and \(v'\) are continuous on \([0, L]\), \(v'\) is piecewise continuously differentiable and \(v\) satisfies the essential conditions of the supports. Let \(v\) be an admissible field for which \(M = EI\,v''\) and \(V\) are continuous on \([0, L]\) and piecewise continuously differentiable, with \(V' + q = 0\) and \(M' + V = 0\) on the open pieces, and at every end one of the possibilities holds: the support prohibits the corresponding component (\(v\) or \(v'\)), or the prescribed load equals the internal force, so at the end \(x = L\) we have \(f_{\mathrm{L}} = V(L)\) and \(\mu_{\mathrm{L}} = M(L)\), and at the end \(x = 0\) we have \(f_0 = -V(0)\) and \(\mu_0 = -M(0)\). Then:

  1. for every admissible field \(\delta v\), \(\displaystyle\int_0^L M\,\delta v''\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^L q\,\delta v\,\mathrm{d}x + \Big[V\,\delta v + M\,\delta v'\Big]_0^L\), and the last term is the work of the loads at the ends, where the support does not prohibit the corresponding component,
  2. for \(\Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,v\,\mathrm{d}x - f_0\,v(0) - \mu_0\,v'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,v(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,v'(L)\) and every admissible field \(\tilde v\), \(\displaystyle\Pi(\tilde v) - \Pi(v) = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x \ge 0\), if the only admissible variation of the form \(a + b\,x\) is the zero function (the supports exclude a rigid motion). Equality holds only for \(\tilde v = v\).
Proof

We must show four items. Items 1 and 3 are the proofs of theorems 4.76 and 5.63, in which lemma 6.12 replaces the fundamental theorem, and items 2 and 4 are the decomposition of corollaries 4.93 and 5.75, in which the integral of a square that is zero is handled by item 3 of lemma 6.10.

1. Virtual displacements of the bar.

One picture serves the bar and the beam: the function \(G\) is continuous and has kinks at the break points, the increments over the pieces add up to the difference of the values \(G(0)\) and \(G(L)\).

  1. Let \(G = N\,\delta u\) on \([0, L]\). The functions \(N\) and \(\delta u\) are continuous, so the product \(G\) is continuous too (corollary 1.65). On the open pieces, by the product rule and because \(N' = -q\), \(G' = N'\,\delta u + N\,\delta u' = -q\,\delta u + N\,\delta u'\), which is a continuous function on the closed piece. The function \(G\) is therefore piecewise continuously differentiable, as in step 2 of the proof of lemma 6.13.
  2. By lemma 6.12 and linearity (lemma 6.10, item 2) \(\int_0^L (N\,\delta u' - q\,\delta u)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0)\), which is the sum of the increments over the pieces. The value \(G(0)\) is zero, because \(\delta u(0) = 0\), and the value \(G(L)\) is \(F\,\delta u(L)\), because \(N(L) = F\). This is item 1.

2. Minimum energy of the bar.

The difference \(w = \tilde u - u\) is zero at \(x = 0\) and has kinks at the break points. Its energy density \(\tfrac12\,EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,w'^2\) is nonnegative on every piece. If its integral is zero, \(w\) is constant on every piece, and since it is continuous, the pieces agree at the joint.

  1. Let \(w = \tilde u - u\). This function is piecewise continuously differentiable on a common partition, \(w(0) = 0\) and \(w' = \tilde u' - u'\) on the open pieces (rules of differentiation, lemma 1.82, items 1 and 2). So it is an admissible field.
  2. As in steps 2 and 3 of the proof of corollary 4.93 we expand \(\tfrac12\,EA\,\tilde u'^2 = \tfrac12\,EA\,u'^2 + N\,w' + \tfrac12\,EA\,w'^2\) and with linearity of the integral we obtain
    \[\Pi(\tilde u) = \Pi(u) + \Big[\int_0^L N\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,w\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,w(L)\Big] + \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x .\]
  3. The square bracket is zero by item 1 with \(\delta u = w\).
  4. The function \(\tfrac12\,EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,(g_{\mathrm{k}}^{w})^2\) is continuous and nonnegative on every closed piece, so by item 3 of lemma 6.10 the last integral is nonnegative. This is the equation of item 2.
  5. Let the last integral be zero. By the same item \(\tfrac12\,EA_{\mathrm{k}}\,(g_{\mathrm{k}}^{w})^2 = 0\) on every closed piece, because \(EA_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\), so \(w' = 0\) on every open piece. By corollary 1.86 (\(w\) is continuous on the closed piece and differentiable on the open one) \(w\) is constant on every piece. Neighbouring pieces share a break point, at which \(w\) is continuous, so they have the same constant, and \(w(x) = w(0) = 0\) everywhere. Hence \(\tilde u = u\).

3. Virtual displacements of the beam.

  1. Let \(G = V\,\delta v + M\,\delta v'\) on \([0, L]\). The functions \(V\), \(M\), \(\delta v\) and \(\delta v'\) are continuous, so \(G\) is continuous too. On an open piece \(G' = V'\,\delta v + V\,\delta v' + M'\,\delta v' + M\,\delta v''\), and with \(V' = -q\) and \(M' = -V\) the terms \(V\,\delta v'\) and \(M'\,\delta v' = -V\,\delta v'\) cancel: \(G' = -q\,\delta v + M\,\delta v''\). This is a continuous function on the closed piece, so \(G\) is piecewise continuously differentiable.
  2. By lemma 6.12 \(\int_0^L (M\,\delta v'' - q\,\delta v)\,\mathrm{d}x = G(L) - G(0)\) (sum of the increments), which is item 3. That this is the work of the end loads, where the support does not prohibit the component, follows from the end conditions as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 5.63: at the end \(x = L\) we have \(V\,\delta v + M\,\delta v' = f_{\mathrm{L}}\,\delta v + \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,\delta v'\), at the end \(x = 0\) we have \(-G(0) = f_0\,\delta v + \mu_0\,\delta v'\), and the term of a component that the support prohibits is zero.

4. Minimum energy of the beam.

If the last integral is zero, \(w''\) is zero on every piece, so \(w'\) is constant on every piece, and since it is continuous, it is the same constant \(b\) everywhere. The function \(w - b\,x\) has derivative zero, so it is constant, and \(w = a + b\,x\) is a line. If the support prohibits \(w\) and \(w'\), this line is the zero function.

  1. Let \(w = \tilde v - v\). Both fields satisfy the same essential condition, so \(w\) is zero where the support prohibits \(v\) or \(v'\). So it is an admissible field.
  2. As in step 2 of the proof of corollary 5.75, \(\tfrac12\,EI\,\tilde v''^2 = \tfrac12\,EI\,v''^2 + M\,w'' + \tfrac12\,EI\,w''^2\), and with linearity \(\Pi(\tilde v) = \Pi(v) + [\int_0^L M\,w'' - \int_0^L q\,w - f_0\,w(0) - \mu_0\,w'(0) - f_{\mathrm{L}}\,w(L) - \mu_{\mathrm{L}}\,w'(L)] + \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,w''^2\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. The square bracket is zero: by item 3 with \(\delta v = w\), \(\int_0^L M\,w'' - \int_0^L q\,w = [V\,w + M\,w']_0^L\), and every term at the ends cancels with the corresponding term in the bracket (by the end conditions), or is zero because \(w\) or \(w'\) is zero there.
  4. The last integral is nonnegative by item 3 of lemma 6.10. If it is zero, \(w'' = 0\) on the open pieces. By corollary 1.86 with \(w'\), \(w'\) is constant on every piece, and since \(w'\) is continuous, it is the same constant \(b\) on all pieces. The function \(w - b\,x\) has derivative zero on every piece, so it is constant on a piece, and since it is continuous, it is the same constant \(a\) everywhere. The variation \(w = a + b\,x\) is admissible, so by the assumption on the supports it is the zero function.

Source: The proof is not from a source. Bar: EP §4.3.1 (pp. 179-183) and EP (3.3.3), (3.3.4) (p. 124) for the total potential energy, beam: the same sources as corollary 5.75.

What did the proof show? Both principles stay valid, because the terms at the break points cancel thanks to continuity, and because a nonnegative continuous function with zero integral is zero everywhere on every piece. Continuity is essential in two places: the function \(u\) (or for the beam \(v\) and \(v'\)) and the force \(N\) (or \(V\) and \(M\)) must not jump. A jump of \(N\) would give an extra term at the break point, as in the example below lemma 6.13. A jump of \(v'\) would give a term equal to the moment \(M\) times the jump, which for \(M \neq 0\) would not vanish.

Let us test the principle on the stepped bar (section 6.1.4), drawn in figure 6.50. On the left are the axes of \(x\) and \(u\), 10 m long, and on the right the axes of \(x\) and \(u'\). The true strain falls from \(2{,}440\) to \(0{,}488\), jumps at the step to \(1{,}952\) and falls to \(0\), and the line has a constant \(1{,}366\). As a trial field take the straight line \(\tilde u = c\,x\), which chapter 4 used as the first trial field. The function \(\tilde u\) has one piece here and \(u\) has two (step at \(x = 5\) m), but the difference \(\tilde u - u\) is piecewise continuously differentiable with the break point \(x = 5\) m. The best \(c\) from the minimum of \(\Pi(c\,x)\) gives the end displacement \(\tilde u(L) = 13{,}66\) µm instead of \(12{,}20\) µm. The energy is \(\Pi(\tilde u) = -89{,}81\) µJ, and that of the true field \(\Pi(u) = -106{,}92\) µJ. The difference is \(17{,}11\) µJ, in the figure the shaded difference of the strains per piece. The integral \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,(\tilde u' - u')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\) is a sum over pieces, \(\int_0^5 \dots\) (\(12{,}59\) µJ) \(+\) \(\int_5^{10} \dots\) (\(4{,}52\) µJ), and gives the same number \(17{,}11\) µJ, as item 2 requires. Item 1 with \(\delta u = x\) gives on the left \(\int_0^L N\,\mathrm{d}x = 131{,}46\) N m and on the right \(\int_0^L q\,x\,\mathrm{d}x = 131{,}46\) N m, where \(q\) is the weight per unit length, \(\rho g A\) above and \(\rho g A/4\) below.

For the beam take a cantilever of length \(L = 1\) m with a vertical force of \(500\) N downward at the free end, as in chapter 5, but with a bending stiffness that is reduced at the middle: \(EI = 20\,906{,}7\) N m² for \(x < 0{,}5\) m and half of that value for \(x > 0{,}5\) m (step in the cross-section, which the model idealizes as a jump, as for the bar). At the top are the wall and the beam, below them the axis of \(M\), then the axis of \(v''\) with the true curvature, and at the bottom the axis of \(v\). The moment \(M = -500\ \text{N}\,(L - x)\) is continuous, so the curvature \(v'' = M/EI\) jumps at \(x = 0{,}5\) m from \(-0{,}011\,958\ \text{m}^{-1}\) to \(-0{,}023\,916\ \text{m}^{-1}\), and \(v'\) is continuous. The true end displacement is \(-8{,}968\) mm, the energy \(\Pi(v) = -2{,}242\) J. The trial parabola \(\tilde v = a\,x^2\) has a constant curvature and the best \(a = -7{,}972 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-1}\), end displacement \(-7{,}972\) mm and \(\Pi(\tilde v) = -1{,}993\) J. The end displacement of the parabola is \(-7{,}972\) mm. The difference \(0{,}249\) J equals the integral \(\int_0^L \tfrac12\,EI\,(\tilde v'' - v'')^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). We compute it piece by piece, the left piece gives \(0{,}083\) J and the right piece \(0{,}166\) J, and the sum is \(0{,}249\) J, as item 4 requires.

6.2.3 The general setting

Everything we proved holds for functions that are continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, and for the Riemann integral. The mathematics that treats these problems in general uses a larger class of functions. The space \(L^2\) (space of square integrable functions, prostor kvadratno integrabilnih funkcij) consists of the functions whose square has a finite integral (EP (6.2.6), (6.2.7), p. 274, BATHE PDF p. 253). The Sobolev space \(H^1\) (Sobolev space, Sobolevov prostor) consists of the functions whose square and whose derivative's square have a finite integral (EP (6.2.22), p. 279, BATHE PDF p. 254). For a bar with positive stiffness this is the class of functions with finite energy \(\tfrac12\int EA\,u'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). BATHE counts in it also the trial fields of the finite element method (BATHE, PDF p. 256, "Of course, \(V_{\mathrm{h}} \subset V\)"). Every piecewise continuously differentiable function also has finite energy, since its derivative is continuous and therefore bounded on every piece.

Why do mathematicians need it? In this chapter we proved the minimum energy for fields that we know how to write down, and for the true field we had in every case a formula that integration gave. A general theorem must show that a minimum of the energy exists at all. The distance between two fields is the square root of twice the energy of their difference, \(\sqrt{\int EA\,(\tilde u' - u')^2\,\mathrm{d}x}\) (EP (6.2.43), p. 287), where the energy of the difference has the form of item 2 of corollary 6.14. A space is complete (poln) if every sequence of fields whose terms come ever closer to each other by this distance has a limit in the space. EP says that the space \(H_{\mathrm{A}}\) consisting of the fields with finite energy is complete with respect to this distance, that is a Hilbert space (EP pp. 287 and 281). The potential energy \(\Pi\), which assigns a number to a field, extends to the whole space and attains its minimum there at exactly one function. The proofs go beyond EP, which leaves them to the literature (EP p. 287). The space of continuously differentiable functions is not complete for the distance that takes into account the square of the function and the square of the derivative, and its completion is the Sobolev space (EP p. 278). In general, for example for a body with a re-entrant corner (BARBER §11.2.3, PDF p. 175), the minimum may be attained only in a larger class.

In this book we do not need this, because for bars and beams we obtain the true field by integration (chapters 4 and 5), so the existence of the minimum energy is already proved, for the bar directly from the formula \(u' = N/EA\) with piecewise continuous \(N\). For a beam with variable stiffness, integration leaves four constants in a system of four linear equations whose homogeneous problem has only the zero solution by lemma 6.7 (item 2), so the system has exactly one solution. The Sobolev space needs the Lebesgue integral, which the book does not have, so we only name it. For bodies in the plane and in space the existence of a solution does not follow from integration. Each time we state that the claim holds if a solution exists, and we leave existence to chapter 11.
