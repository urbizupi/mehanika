Chapter 20 Damping models

20.10 Connections

The course abbreviations are the same as in section 1.19. The chapter treated damping step by step, from the energy that one period removes to matrices and continuous systems. For each model we say where we need it again and where it stops being valid, since the limit of a model matters more for damping than for stiffness. Each paragraph points at the figure of the chapter that belongs to it.

  • Energy per period and the loss factor: by lemma 20.2 the work of the damping force over one period equals minus the area of the force-displacement loop, and the loss factor \(\gamma\) of definition 20.3 is this area divided by \(2\pi U\). With this one measure we compare all mechanisms of the chapter, and we read the measurement of section 9.13 with it: the damping ratio \(\zeta\) and the factor \(\gamma\) are the same datum at resonance, \(\gamma = 2\zeta\) (lemma 20.4 and corollary 9.63). For the mounts of machines (DSK, chapter 23) it is the measure by which a mount is chosen.
  • Equivalent viscous damping: by theorem 20.7 the energy rule is also the best fit in the mean square, so friction, quadratic drag and viscous damping add up to one \(\zeta\) (corollary 20.10). The replacement is valid for one amplitude: for cantilever A with dry friction \(F_{\mathrm{t}} = 2\ \text{N}\) the amplitude at resonance settles at \(13{,}914\) mm (theorem 20.11), while without the viscous part it would grow by \(0{,}874\) mm per period, since no balance exists. In free vibration with friction the amplitude falls linearly by theorem 9.109, by \(4F_{\mathrm{t}}/k = 0{,}12755\) mm per cycle, and not exponentially, so the logarithmic decrement of section 9.13.2 is not constant. Nonlinear systems, where the replacement fails, are in chapter 17 (AK).
  • Hysteretic damping: \(k(1 + \mathrm{i}\gamma)\) is a model of steady harmonic motion (definition 20.12). In frequency-domain analyses, which theorem 10.53 of chapter 10 derives, it can be put into the stiffness matrix (section 20.7). In the time domain it must not be used, because by lemma 20.18 the characteristic polynomial has a root with a positive real part and the free solution grows (figure): for the mounts of machine \(B\) with \(\gamma = 0{,}04\) the growing part of the solution grows by a factor of \(\mathrm{e}\) in \(1{,}592\) s. Time stepping (AK, chapter 17) therefore uses the viscous matrix of section 20.7 or the Prony internal variables of section 20.5 (lemma 20.31).
  • Viscoelastic elements and the hereditary integral: the relaxation modulus and the creep compliance (definitions 20.20 and 20.21) fill in what chapter 3 announced about polymers under the assumption of slow loading: the modulus of definition 3.1 is no longer a number but a function of time or of frequency. The standard linear solid (theorem 20.27) combines an instantaneous response and a bounded creep, while the Maxwell element has only the first and the Kelvin-Voigt element only the second. The hereditary integral (theorem 20.30) is proved along the same path as Duhamel's theorem (theorem 9.79), with the term \(E_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)\,\varepsilon(0)\) for the jump at the start, without which the formula would give zero stress in a bar stretched by a step (section 20.5.4). For loads that last long or change fast this matters in every calculation with rubber or plastic (VT, MK, chapter 3).
  • Complex modulus: \(\hat E = E_{\mathrm{shr}} + \mathrm{i}E_{\mathrm{izg}}\) (definition 20.32) is the response to steady harmonic strain (theorem 20.33). By lemma 20.38 every Prony material with \(E_\infty > 0\) has a loss factor that tends to zero as \(\Omega \to 0\) and as \(\Omega \to \infty\). A constant \(\gamma\) is therefore possible in a material only approximately and only over a band, as the Prony fits for the target \(\gamma = 0{,}1\) on the band \(1 \le \Omega \le 100\) show, with three terms (\(0{,}0952\) to \(0{,}1047\)) and with five (\(0{,}0952\) to \(0{,}1018\)). For the standard solid with \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\), \(\gamma\) peaks at \(0{,}35355\) at \(\Omega\tau = 0{,}70711\) (corollary 20.36). The Kelvin-Voigt element has \(\gamma = \Omega\tau\) (lemma 20.37), the material form of stiffness-proportional damping of definition 10.45.
  • Damping matrices: the element damping matrix of lemma 20.39, built from the element stiffness and mass matrices (section 12.9), is assembled in the same way as the stiffness matrices by theorem 7.13. If all elements have the same \(\tau_\varepsilon\) and the same \(a_0\), the damping is Rayleigh with \(a_1 = \tau_\varepsilon\) and classical (theorem 20.40 and lemma 10.46), so by theorem 10.49 it can be treated mode by mode (AK, VD, DSK). If only one storey is viscous, as in the middle frame A of the figure, where only the lower storey has a damper, the modes couple and the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T \mat C \mat\Phi\) is not diagonal (section 20.7). In a structure that is not fixed to the ground, \(a_0 \mat M\) also damps the motion of the body as a whole (figure: for the wagons with \(a_0 = 0{,}56668\) 1/s the velocity halves in \(\ln 2/a_0 = 1{,}223\) s), which is a property of the model and not of the material (lemma 20.41).
  • First-order form and complex modes: what section 10.9.3 of chapter 10 showed for frame A with one damper, the chapter proves in general (theorems 20.48 and 20.49, the response by complex modes agrees with step-by-step computation). The form with \(2n\) equations is the basis for reducing models with non-classical damping (DSK, chapter 21). At critical damping the expansion fails because the eigenvalue is double and has only one independent eigenvector, while the motion contains \(t\mathrm{e}^{st}\) (section 20.8).
  • Continuous systems: by theorem 20.51 a beam with a Kelvin-Voigt material or an external dashpot keeps the modes of theorem 13.43, and each mode is an oscillator of chapter 9 with the ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}} = \tau_\varepsilon\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/2 + c_{\mathrm{z}}/(2\rho A\omega_{\mathrm{n}})\) (equation (20.45)). For the cantilever with \(\zeta_1 = 0{,}01\) internal damping, which is stiffness-proportional, gives \(\zeta_2 = 0{,}0627\), and external damping, which is mass-proportional, gives \(0{,}0016\), so for the flexible links of robots (DSK, chapter 28), where the first modes matter, it matters which mechanism dominates. The theorem needs the same damper everywhere: with dampers only on the right half of the cantilever the modes couple (example 20.53).
  • Notation: the loss factor \(\gamma\) is not the shear strain \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) of chapter 1 nor a closed curve \(\gamma\) (section 20.1 says so at first use), and the time \(\tau\) (relaxation time) is not the shear stress of chapter 2 (this chapter has none). The letter \(\eta\) means the viscosity in section 20.4 and in section 20.7 the modal coordinate of chapter 10, so the loss factor does not use that letter.

20.10.1 Planned in other chapters

The chapter does not treat the following because it belongs elsewhere. The chapters are drafted in parallel, so we name them by chapter only.

  • Effective modal mass and model reduction with damping: the ratio \(\zeta_{\mathrm{n}}\) of theorem 20.51 and the matrix \(\mat\Phi^\T\mat C\mat\Phi\) of section 20.7 are the data that a reduced model needs (DSK, chapter 21).
  • Internal damping of rotors and the instability it causes: in a rotating shaft the damping of the material can drive the vibration instead of suppressing it, which chapter 24 (DSK) treats with the first-order form of section 20.8. This chapter only shows that a non-rotating system with a symmetric positive semidefinite \(\mat C\) has no eigenvalue with \(\mathrm{Re}\,s > 0\) (lemma 20.47, point 3).
  • Numerical damping of schemes and nonlinear damping that an equivalent linear damping does not capture: chapter 17 (AK).
  • Elastic mounts of engines and the forces they pass to the foundation: chapter 23 (DSK), which uses the ratio \(\zeta\) and the factor \(\gamma\) of this chapter.
  • The viscoelastic boundary-value problem and the correspondence principle (CM §9.3.3 and §9.3.4, PDF 441-444), fractional models, GHM models and aging of materials: the chapter only names them because of its length.

20.10.2 Not proved

The following is cited from the sources and not proved in this book.

  • Boltzmann's superposition principle for a general linear material (CM §9.3.1, PDF 436-439): it is proved only for materials described by a sum of first-order relaxations (a Prony series), by theorem 20.30.
  • Causality of the hysteretic model (GR, PDF 181, footnote 3): lemma 20.18 shows a root with a positive real part, which is why the model fails in the time domain, but causality itself needs the Fourier integral, which chapter 9 only names.
  • Existence of the eigenvalues of the first-order form: the fundamental theorem of algebra, which the book only names.
  • Expansion in complex modes at a double eigenvalue, with generalized eigenvectors and the Jordan form (CK, PDF 334-335): theorem 20.49 is proved only for \(2n\) distinct eigenvalues, for the free and the forced response.
  • Completeness of the cantilever modes in theorem 20.51: cited from chapter 13 (GR, CK).
  • Fitting the standard solid and the Prony series to a real material: the numbers of the viscoelastic models in the chapter are dimensionless ratios (for example \(E_0/E_\infty = 2\)), no data of a particular polymer is used.
  • The use of internal variables in the time step of FE programs: named, not derived. Only the step for a bar with a prescribed strain is derived (lemma 20.31).
