Take a rubber band and pull it by both ends. It gets longer, and we can see it. Now take a steel bar and pull it with the same force. It looks as if nothing has happened. Yet the steel bar has also become longer, only by so little that our eyes cannot notice it. This is true of every object: when a force acts on it, it changes its shape. When we step onto a bridge, the bridge bends a little. When we sit on a chair, the legs of the chair get a little shorter. When a motor drives a shaft, the shaft twists a little, one end more than the other.

The band, the bar, the bridge, the chair and the shaft have something in common. They are all made of material, and under a force they all change their shape. So mechanics calls them by one name.

Definition 1.1 Body (telo)

A body (telo) is an object made of material whose motion and change of shape we study. We imagine the material distributed continuously, without gaps, so that every point of the space the body occupies is a point of the material. So a body is made of points. This picture of matter is called a continuum (kontinuum).

Source: CM §3.2.1 (PDF p. 111), CM §1.1 (PDF p. 30).

In reality matter is made of molecules with empty space between them. But molecules are so small that for a bridge, a shaft or a bar only pieces containing an enormous number of molecules matter. So we may treat matter as if it filled space without gaps (CM §3.1, PDF p. 110). When we speak of a point of a body, we mean a point of this continuous material.

From history

Until the end of 1820 nobody had tried to derive general equations of rest and vibration for bodies that return to their original shape after unloading. Only separate theories were known, for bending and twisting and for the vibration of bars and plates, and even these were incomplete (LOVE, PDF p. 23). From the eighteenth century the picture of Isaac Newton prevailed, that bodies are made of small particles that act on one another with forces (LOVE, PDF p. 23). Navier was the first to try to derive general equations from it. He read his memoir to the Paris Academy in May 1821 (LOVE, PDF p. 23). He took the molecules as points, and the force between two of them as one that changes when their distance changes slightly (figure 1.1, LOVE, PDF p. 23). In his view the response of a body to a change of shape arose from these changes of the forces (LOVE, PDF p. 23). To obtain the equation for one molecule he had to add up the forces of all the other molecules on it. This is a triple sum, which he replaced by continuous summation over the volume, that is, by matter without gaps (LOVE, PDF p. 24). We take the same step: there are enormously many molecules in a small piece and they are small, so we replace the sum over them by summation over continuous matter. Navier's contemporaries, however, did not grant him this without objection. The validity of the procedure was disputed and his derivations were not generally accepted (LOVE, PDF p. 24). Siméon Denis Poisson and Augustin-Louis Cauchy followed Navier's example, and according to Augustus Edward Hough Love their molecular theories differ greatly from today's picture of molecules (LOVE, PDF p. 24).

Changes of shape of bodies under a force are mostly small, but they decide whether a structure holds. A structure (konstrukcija) is anything built to carry forces, such as a bridge, the frame of a building or a machine part. It holds if under the forces it does not break and does not bend or stretch so much that it can no longer do its job. A bridge that bends a few centimetres under a truck is fine. A bridge that bends a metre is not. So we begin the book with the question of how to describe a change of shape with numbers. We will build the answer step by step, starting from the simplest case. Where we need new mathematics, we will stop and develop it completely, and then return to mechanics.
