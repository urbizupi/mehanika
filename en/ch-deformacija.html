Take a rubber band and pull it by both ends. It gets longer, and we can see it. Now take a steel bar and pull it with the same force. It looks as if nothing has happened. Yet the steel bar has also become longer, only by so little that our eyes cannot notice it. This is true of every object: when a force acts on it, it changes its shape. When we step onto a bridge, the bridge bends a little. When we sit on a chair, the legs of the chair get a little shorter. When a motor drives a shaft, the shaft twists a little, one end more than the other.

The band, the bar, the bridge, the chair and the shaft have something in common. They are all made of material, and under a force they all change their shape. So mechanics calls them by one name.

Definition 1.1 Body (telo)

A body (telo) is an object made of material whose motion and change of shape we study. We imagine the material distributed continuously, without gaps, so that every point of the space the body occupies is a point of the material. So a body is made of points. This picture of matter is called a continuum (kontinuum).

Source: CM §3.2.1 (PDF p. 111), CM §1.1 (PDF p. 30).

In reality matter is made of molecules with empty space between them. But molecules are so small that for a bridge, a shaft or a bar only pieces containing an enormous number of molecules matter. So we may treat matter as if it filled space without gaps (CM §3.1, PDF p. 110). When we speak of a point of a body, we mean a point of this continuous material.

These changes are mostly small, but they decide whether a structure holds. A structure (konstrukcija) is anything built to carry forces, such as a bridge, the frame of a building or a machine part. It holds if under the forces it does not break and does not bend or stretch so much that it can no longer do its job. A bridge that bends a few centimetres under a truck is fine. A bridge that bends a metre is not. So we begin the book with the question of how to describe a change of shape with numbers. We will build the answer step by step, starting from the simplest case. Where we need new mathematics, we will stop and develop it completely, and then return to mechanics.

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar (palica), a body that is much longer than it is thick. If we cut the bar perpendicular to its length, we get a face called a cross section (prečni prerez). The axis of the bar is the straight line along the bar through the centres of its cross sections. We pull the bar at both ends along its axis. The forces that act on a body are called its loading (obremenitev), so we speak of the bar before loading and after it. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Is the elongation a good measure of how strongly the material of the bar is stretched? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end (figure 1.3). The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves, so each half stretches as much as the short bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

What matters for the material is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.2 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language "deformation" means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is called the strain. Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Other books also call this number the relative elongation or the engineering strain, and often write it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(x\) is the direction of the axis of the bar. Why two indices when there is only one direction? For a bar one would indeed be enough. In a body that is not a thin bar, however, we will describe the strain of the material around a point by several numbers, and each of them will be fixed by two directions. When the two directions are the same, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the number measures how strongly the material is stretched in that direction. When they differ, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the number describes how the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. A deck of cards pushed sideways at the top leans over: the cards keep their length, but the right angles in the side face of the deck become slanted (figure 1.4). So the index \(xx\) says that the number measures a change of length in the direction \(x\) and not a change of angle. We will meet these numbers properly when we describe strain in space (SADD §2.2, PDF pp. 41-46).

The deck of cards shows that the material close to a point can change its shape in two ways. Its lengths can change: in some direction the material gets longer when we pull it, or shorter when we push it together, which is called compression (stiskanje). Or the right angles between directions can change, as in the leaning deck, where the cards slide past each other. This is called shear (strig). Each of the numbers with two indices measures one of these two changes.

Definition 1.3 Normal and shear strain (normalna in strižna deformacija)

A normal strain (normalna deformacija) in a direction is the change of length of a short piece of material lying in that direction, divided by its original length. It is positive when the material gets longer and negative when it gets shorter. A shear strain (strižna deformacija) of two perpendicular directions describes how the right angle between them changes.

Source: SADD §2.2 (PDF pp. 42-43).

Three normal strains, one for each axis, and three shear strains, one for each pair of axes, together describe completely how the material close to a point is deformed, as long as the deformation is small (SADD §2.2, PDF p. 43). We will derive them when we describe strain in space.

Which of the two changes we see depends on the directions we look along. In the leaning deck the cards keep their length, and only the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. But a line drawn on the side face of the deck from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner gets longer, and the line between the other two corners gets shorter. Along these two directions the same deformation is stretching and compression. We will check this with numbers on a sheared cube in section 1.6.2. Books also show that for every small deformation there are three mutually perpendicular directions in which there is no shear at all, only stretching or compression (SADD §2.4, PDF p. 49).

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}0005 ,\]

five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0{,}5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer. This bound follows from the data for steel in BOWER (Tables 3.1 and 3.9, PDF pp. 100 and 156). We will see how to compute it once we know how the strain of a bar depends on the force that pulls it. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\). This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

For a compressed bar \(\Delta L\) is negative, so the strain is negative too. A positive strain means lengthening, a negative one shortening.

The number \(\Delta L / L\) is not the only measure of stretching, only the simplest. Books use others too, and we will meet them when we need them. The stretch (razmerje dolžin) \(\lambda = (L + \Delta L)/L\) compares the new length with the old one and equals \(1 + \varepsilon\). The Green-Lagrange strain \(\big((L + \Delta L)^2 - L^2\big)/(2L^2)\) compares the squares of the lengths and equals \(\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2/2\) (CM (3.4.15), PDF p. 131). The logarithmic strain is the logarithm of the ratio \((L + \Delta L)/L\) (BOWER, PDF p. 589). For \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\) the Green-Lagrange strain is \(0{,}0010005\) and the logarithmic strain \(0{,}0009995\), both differ from \(\varepsilon\) by half a per mille of its value. For the rubber band with \(\varepsilon = 0{,}5\) they are \(0{,}625\) and \(0{,}405\). So for small strains all the measures give almost the same number, for large strains they differ a lot, and then we must say which measure we use. Until we say otherwise, the strain of a bar means \(\Delta L / L\).

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?

1.2 When a bar does not stretch the same everywhere

Hang a steel bar from the ceiling and let it hang. Apart from the ceiling that holds its top end, only its own weight acts on it. The top part of the bar has to carry the weight of the whole bar below it. The bottom end carries almost nothing. So we expect the top centimetres to stretch more than the bottom ones. If we measure the total elongation \(\Delta L\) and divide it by \(L\), we get some average, which does not tell us how the material is stretched at the top and how at the bottom. We want to know how the material is stretched close to each point of the bar, separately for every point.

Figure 1.6 shows the difference. Before loading we draw marks on the bar at equal distances, like the marks on a ruler. If we pull the bar by a force at its lower end, and its weight is negligible compared with the force, every piece between two neighbouring marks is pulled by the same force, and all the gaps grow equally. Under its own weight the top gap grows the most, because the piece at the top carries the whole bar below it, and every gap lower down grows a little less. The lowest piece carries only its own weight and hardly gets longer. The two loaded bars in the figure have the same total elongation, so the same average \(\Delta L / L\), and yet the material in them is stretched very differently.

Why is the pull the same everywhere in the first bar but not in the second? Let us cut out one piece between two marks in thought, as drawn to the right of each loaded bar. The part of the bar above the piece pulls it up, the part below pulls it down. The piece is at rest, so the forces on it balance (EP (2.5.3)). In the bar pulled at its end nothing else acts on the piece, so the pull from above is exactly as large as the pull from below. This holds for every piece, and the lowest one is pulled by the force at the end, so every piece is pulled by that same force. In the hanging bar the piece also has its own weight. The pull from above must carry both the pull from below and the weight of the piece, so it is larger by exactly this weight. Going up the bar, the pull grows by the weight of one piece at every mark, from zero at the bottom end to the weight of the whole bar at the top (BARBER (1.33)). So the difference lies in where the load acts. The force at the end acts at one place only, the weight acts on every piece of the material. Books call a force that acts on every bit of the material, like weight, a body force (prostorninska sila), and a force that acts on the surface of a body, like the pull at the end, a surface force (površinska sila) (SADD §3.1, PDF p. 63). We will write the balance of forces exactly in chapter 2. The forces in the figure are drawn to scale: the force at the end is half the weight of the bar. The pull in the hanging bar falls evenly from the weight of the whole bar at the top to zero at the bottom, so on average it is half the weight. If a piece stretches in proportion to the force that pulls it, as we will see for steel, both bars therefore stretch by the same total amount.

How do we describe that? We follow the points of the bar. We label a point by its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, measured before the bar is hung. Books that distinguish the position before loading from the position after it often write this distance with a capital \(X\) and the new position with a small \(x\). The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), the bottom end at \(x = L\). When the bar hangs, every point moves down a little. How far the point that was at distance \(x\) moves, we write as \(u(x)\) and call the displacement (pomik) of that point. So the displacement is the difference between the new and the old position of the point. The top of the bar is fixed to the ceiling, so it does not move: \(u(0) = 0\). Points lower down move more, because the elongations of all the parts of the bar above them add up.

Now pick a short piece of the bar between the points \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) (figure 1.7). Its initial length is \(\Delta x\). The top end of the piece moves by \(u(x)\), the bottom end by \(u(x + \Delta x)\). If the bottom end moves more than the top end, the piece has become longer, by the difference of the two displacements:

\[\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) .\]

The piece is a small bar, so we can use the formula (1.1) for it. Its strain is the elongation \(\Delta u\) divided by the length \(\Delta x\):

\[\varepsilon \approx \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.2}\]

The sign \(\approx\) means "approximately equal". Even within the piece the strain changes a little from top to bottom, so this is still an average, only over a shorter part of the bar. The shorter the piece, the less the strain changes within it, and the better the number describes how the material is stretched close to the point \(x\). The point itself has no length, so it cannot be stretched. Only the material near it can, and the shorter the piece we take, the closer to the point we look.

Let us try this with numbers. In chapters 2 and 3 we will compute that a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight stretches so that the displacement of the point at distance \(x\) from the ceiling is

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2), \qquad a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The constant \(a\) depends only on how heavy steel is and how strongly it resists stretching (BARBER (1.39) with the data for mild steel from BOWER, Table 3.1). We do not need to understand it now, we will only compute with it. The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), where \(u(0) = 0\), as it must be. The bottom end moves by \(u(10\ \text{m}) = a \cdot 100\ \text{m}^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, two hundredths of a millimetre. A ten-metre steel bar stretches under its own weight by less than the thickness of a sheet of paper.

Let us compute the strain of the piece (1.2) at the top of the bar (\(x = 0\)) and at the middle (\(x = 5\) m) for shorter and shorter pieces:

length of the piece \(\Delta x\) at the top at the middle
1 m \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}757 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,1 m \(3{,}884 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}932 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,01 m \(3{,}902 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}950 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,001 m \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\)

The numbers settle. At the top they approach about \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), at the middle about \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), exactly half. The material at the top is stretched twice as much as at the middle, which agrees with our expectation that the top part carries more weight. Why exactly half? Every cross section of the bar carries the weight of the part of the bar below it. The cross section at the top carries the whole bar, the one at the middle only the lower half, so half the weight. The bar is equally thick everywhere, and in steel the elongation of a short piece is proportional to the force that pulls it, as long as we do not stretch it beyond about \(0{,}001\), the value from section 1.1: half the force gives half the elongation. We will learn this when we connect the force in a bar with its strain, and the formula for \(u(x)\) above is derived from it (BARBER (1.39)). The same reasoning predicts more. The weight carried by a cross section decreases evenly from the top to the bottom, in proportion to the length of the bar below the cross section, and at the bottom end it is zero. The strain should change in the same way. We will confirm this by calculation in section 1.4.

So it seems that every point has its own strain, the number that the strains of shorter and shorter pieces approach. But what exactly does "approach" mean? A table cannot be the answer. It shows only a few rows, and from them we do not know for certain how the numbers behave further on. And we cannot shrink the piece to nothing, because in the formula (1.2) we would get \(0/0\), a division by zero. We need an exact meaning of the words "the number that the ratio approaches as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller and smaller", and a way to compute that number without tables. This is a job for mathematics, and the next section does it.

1.3 Mathematics: limit and derivative

In this section we forget the bar for a moment. We have a rule that assigns a number \(u(x)\) to every number \(x\), and we ask how fast \(u(x)\) changes as we change \(x\). The answer will be a new number. Once we can compute it, we return to the bar.

When computing we use the school rules for real numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero number, and working with inequalities. The absolute value \(|a|\) of a number \(a\) is the distance of the number from zero: \(|3| = 3\), \(|-3| = 3\). So the distance between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(|a - b|\).

1.3.1 Function

Definition 1.4 Function (funkcija)

A function (funkcija) \(f\) is a rule that assigns to every element \(x\) of some set exactly one element \(f(x)\) of another set. The set whose elements the rule assigns something to is the domain (definicijsko območje) of the function, and \(f(x)\) is the value of the function at \(x\). For now both sets will be sets of numbers: the function assigns to every number \(x\) from some set of numbers exactly one number \(f(x)\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 0.3.11 (PDF p. 14), EP §6.2.

The displacement \(u(x)\) is a function. For the hanging bar its domain is the set of all \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\), that is, of all points of the bar, and the value \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the point \(x\). Why does the definition ask for exactly one number? So that the symbol \(f(x)\) means one definite number that we can calculate with. In the next section we will calculate the difference \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). These are the displacements of two different points, \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), and the difference makes sense only because each of the two symbols is one number. A rule that assigned to the number \(4\) both numbers whose square is \(4\), that is, \(2\) and \(-2\), would not be a function: the symbol \(f(4)\) would not say which number is meant, and we could not calculate with it. For the displacement the requirement holds by itself, because after loading every point of the bar is in one place only.

Functions whose input is one number and whose output is one number, like the displacement \(u(x)\), are only the simplest kind. The elements of the two sets can be anything: points, triples of numbers or even other functions (LEBL1 Example 0.3.12). Soon we will describe the position of every point of a body by three numbers, and section 1.5.1 explains how. The displacement of a point in space will also be given by three numbers, which say how far the point moves in each of three directions. The rule whose input is the three numbers that give the position of a point and whose output is the three numbers that give its displacement will be a function too, only both of its sets will be sets of triples of numbers. The general definition asks only that every element of the first set gets exactly one element of the second. Mathematicians even write a function as a set of pairs (input, value) (LEBL1 Def. 0.3.11), which we will not need.

1.3.2 Ratio of changes

The ratio we computed in the table has a general form. In mathematics the numbers at the input of a function are often called points, because we picture them as points on a straight line. For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a small change \(\Delta x \neq 0\),

\[\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \tag{1.3}\]

is the change of the value divided by the change of the input \(x\). It tells us by how much \(f\) changed on average per unit change of \(x\). We call it the difference quotient (diferenčni količnik). For the bar it is the strain of the piece. For a car journey, where \(f(t)\) is the distance covered up to the time \(t\), it is the average speed over the time \(\Delta t\).

We can also draw the quotient. We draw a function by placing, above every point \(x\) of a horizontal line, a point at the height \(f(x)\). These points form a curve, called the graph (graf) of the function. In figure 1.8 the points of the graph above \(x\) and above \(x + \Delta x\) are joined by a straight line. On the way from the first to the second we move \(\Delta x\) horizontally and \(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\) vertically. The difference quotient is the ratio of these two paths, so it says how much the line rises per unit of horizontal path. This number is called the slope (strmina) of the line. If the line falls, the slope is negative.

For \(\Delta x = 0\) the quotient is not defined, because we would divide by zero. So we treat it as a function of the change \(\Delta x\), defined for all small \(\Delta x\) except \(\Delta x = 0\). We ask what it approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero.

1.3.3 Limit

What does it mean that some function \(g(h)\) approaches a number \(d\) as \(h\) approaches zero? It means that \(g(h)\) is as close to \(d\) as we wish, provided \(h\) is close enough to zero. Set an allowed error, say \(0{,}001\). Is there a band around zero so narrow that for every \(h\) in the band, except \(h = 0\), the value \(g(h)\) differs from \(d\) by less than \(0{,}001\)? If there is one for the error \(0{,}001\), for the error \(0{,}000001\), and for every other error we can think of, then \(g(h)\) really approaches \(d\). For a smaller error the band may be narrower, but it must exist.

We write this exactly. We call the allowed error \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta) and the distance the band reaches on each side of zero \(\delta\) (delta). In mathematics books the allowed error is usually written \(\varepsilon\). We keep \(\varepsilon\) for strain.

Definition 1.5 Limit of a function (limita funkcije)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit (limita) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d , \tag{1.4}\]

if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3 (PDF p. 114), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < |h|\) leaves out the point \(h = 0\), where \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient. The limit tells what \(g\) approaches, not what it is at the point itself. The number \(r\) makes sure that the points the condition talks about really are in the domain. How? The condition talks about the points \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\). If \(\delta \le r\), every such point is also among the points with \(0 < |h| < r\), where \(g\) is defined by assumption, so the value \(g(h)\) exists. And we can always take the band that narrow. If the condition holds for some \(\delta\), it also holds for every smaller \(\delta\), because a narrower band only checks some of the same points. So we may always take \(\delta \le r\). The number \(r\) ensures one more thing: every band around \(0\), however narrow, contains points where \(g\) is defined. We will need this in step 4 of the proof below that the limit is unique, where we must actually pick such a point.

We have defined the limit only at the point \(0\), because the difference quotient needs it only there. This is a narrowing of a more general notion. The limit of a function \(f\) at any point \(c\) is defined in the same way, with the distance \(|x - c|\) between \(x\) and \(c\) in place of \(|h|\) (LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3). LEBL1 also allows functions that are not defined everywhere near \(c\), only at some points that come arbitrarily close to \(c\). The definition uses only distances, \(|g(h) - d|\) and \(|h|\), so it works wherever we can measure distances, for example between points of space. The most general form is the limit of a function between sets in which a distance between the elements is given, called metric spaces (LEBL1 Def. 7.5.15).

Let us try the definition on the simplest example. Let \(g(h) = 5 - h\). We look for its limit at the point \(0\), that is, the number that \(g(h)\) approaches as \(h\) approaches zero. We expect it to be \(5\), since for small \(h\) the value \(5 - h\) is close to \(5\). The function \(g\) is defined for every \(h\), so all the points the condition talks about are in the domain, however wide the band is.

First we write the definition out for this case, with \(g(h) = 5 - h\) and \(d = 5\). We must show: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|(5 - h) - 5| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  2. Simplify the difference that the condition bounds. The fives cancel, \((5 - h) - 5 = -h\), so \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |{-h}|\).
  3. The numbers \(-h\) and \(h\) are equally far from zero, so \(|{-h}| = |h|\). So the condition we must reach is \(|h| < \eta\).
  4. We choose the band so that this condition holds: we take \(\delta = \eta\). It is a positive number, as the definition requires.
  5. Check. For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(|h| < \delta = \eta\), so by steps 2 and 3, \(|(5 - h) - 5| = |h| < \eta\).
  6. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of \(g\) at the point \(0\) is \(5\), \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} (5 - h) = 5\).

For the error \(0{,}001\) the band is \(0{,}001\) wide on each side, for the error \(0{,}000001\) only \(0{,}000001\). A smaller allowed error needs a narrower band, as we expected.

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Otherwise the word "limit" could mean two different numbers. Let us check that this is so.

The idea is simple. If \(g(h)\) approached two numbers at once, then for small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) would be close to both, and so the two numbers would be close to each other. But only equal numbers can be closer to each other than every error.

Lemma 1.6 Uniqueness of the limit (enoličnost limite)

A function has at most one limit at the point \(0\).

Proof

We have to show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) that both satisfy the definition of the limit are equal.

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \(d_1\) is a limit, the definition gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a band \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We take half the error because in step 6 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \(d_2\) is a limit, for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a band \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\).
  4. Take an \(h\) that is nonzero and smaller than both bands, for example half of the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\). For this \(h\) both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold.
  5. Write the difference \(d_1 - d_2\) through the value \(g(h)\), which we know: \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - g(h)) + (g(h) - d_2)\).
  6. The distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) is not larger than the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\) plus the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). This is the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\). It holds because every number lies between its absolute value and the negative of it, \(-|a| \le a \le |a|\) and \(-|b| \le b \le |b|\). Adding the inequalities gives \(-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|\), and a number that lies between \(-c\) and \(c\) has absolute value at most \(c\). With \(a = d_1 - g(h)\) and \(b = g(h) - d_2\) we therefore get
    \[|d_1 - d_2| \le |d_1 - g(h)| + |g(h) - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  7. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2|\) is smaller than every positive number.
  8. A nonnegative number that is smaller than every positive number is zero: if it were positive, it would be smaller than itself. So \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\) and \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.1.4 (PDF pp. 114-115). The argument for the triangle inequality in step 6 is not from the source.

The proof showed a pattern that we will meet many times: when we want a sum of two errors to be small, we allow each of them half.

1.3.4 Derivative

Now we can say what the number is that the strain of the piece approached in the table.

Definition 1.7 Derivative (odvod)

A function \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(x\) if the difference quotient (1.3) has a limit as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. This limit is the derivative (odvod) of the function \(f\) at the point \(x\):

\[f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.5}\]

Source: LEBL1 Def. 4.1.1 (PDF p. 155). The notation with \(\Delta x\) follows BARBER (1.35)-(1.36), where it is \(\delta x\).

So the derivative is a rate of change: by how much \(f\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when the change is very small. We read \(f'(x)\) as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)". In books you will also find the derivative written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}\). This notation recalls the quotient \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) from which the derivative comes.

This is the derivative of a function whose input is one number, \(x\), and whose output is one number, \(f(x)\), like the displacement \(u(x)\) of the bar. For now this is the only kind we need. In a body, however, a point is given by three numbers, which say where it lies, and its displacement is given by three numbers too, which say how far it moves in each of three directions. There we will look at how one of the output numbers changes when only one of the three input numbers changes and the other two stay fixed. This is called a partial derivative (parcialni odvod) (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.8). The most general derivative, however, is no longer a single number. Take a function whose input is a group of \(n\) numbers and whose output is a group of \(m\) numbers. For the displacement in a body \(n = 3\) and \(m = 3\): the input is three numbers that give the position of the point, the output three numbers that give its displacement. The derivative of such a function at a point is a rule that tells, for every small change of the \(n\) input numbers, approximately how much each of the \(m\) output numbers changes (LEBL2 Def. 8.3.1). We will need such a derivative of the displacement when we describe strain in space.

On the graph the derivative has a clear picture. As \(\Delta x\) gets smaller, the point of the graph above \(x + \Delta x\) moves along the graph towards the point above \(x\), and the lines through the two points turn towards the line that has the slope \(f'(x)\) and passes through the point of the graph above \(x\) (figure 1.10). This line is called the tangent (tangenta) of the graph at the point \(x\). The slopes of the lines through two points are difference quotients, and their limit, the derivative, is the slope of the tangent.

Let us compute the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a\) and \(L\) are given numbers. The way has two parts. First we simplify the difference quotient so that \(\Delta x\), which we must not set to zero, disappears from the denominator. Then we find the limit of the simplified expression from the definition.

  1. The value at \(x + \Delta x\): \(u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2)\).
  2. Expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). So
    \[u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x - x^2 - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  3. Subtract \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
    \[u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) = a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  4. All three terms in the bracket have the factor \(\Delta x\), so we take it out: \(a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  5. Divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
    \[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]

The first part of the way is done. Before we take the limit, let us see what the equation of step 5 tells us. It holds for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), but its two sides behave differently at \(\Delta x = 0\).

On the left side the numerator would be \(u(x + 0) - u(x) = u(x) - u(x) = 0\), and the denominator \(0\):

\[\frac{u(x + 0) - u(x)}{0} = \frac{0}{0} .\]

This is not a number. The quotient \(c/b\) is the number that gives \(c\) when multiplied by \(b\). For \(0/0\) it would have to be a number that gives \(0\) when multiplied by \(0\). Every number does that, so there is no single number we could call \(0/0\). The left side has no value at \(\Delta x = 0\), and that is why we could not shrink the piece to nothing in section 1.2.

On the right side there is no division any more. It disappeared in step 5, when the factor \(\Delta x\) cancelled. So we may put \(\Delta x = 0\) into the right side:

\[a\,(2L - 2x - 0) = a\,(2L - 2x) .\]

This is an ordinary number, which we can compute for every point \(x\).

Now we split the right side of step 5 into this number and the rest. We multiply the bracket by \(a\) and group the first two terms again: \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\). So the strain of the piece is

\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x . \tag{1.6}\]

The first term does not depend on the length \(\Delta x\) of the piece. It is the same for long and short pieces. The second term, \(a\,\Delta x\), is all that separates the quotient from the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and it is proportional to the length of the piece: a piece ten times shorter is ten times closer to the number. Because \(a\,\Delta x\) is subtracted, the strains of pieces with a positive \(\Delta x\) are smaller than the number and grow towards it as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller.

This explains the table of section 1.2, where \(a = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\) and \(L = 10\) m. At the top, at \(x = 0\), the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) is \(a \cdot 20\ \text{m} = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(a \cdot 10\ \text{m} = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). These are the two numbers at which the columns of the table settled. For a piece 1 m long the difference is \(a\,\Delta x = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 1\ \text{m} = 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and indeed \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} - 0{,}1952 \cdot 10^{-6} = 3{,}7088 \cdot 10^{-6}\), rounded to four digits \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), the first number in the table. In every following row the piece is ten times shorter, so the difference is ten times smaller: \(0{,}1952\), \(0{,}01952\), \(0{,}001952\) and \(0{,}0001952\), each times \(10^{-6}\). The table shows four digits, and the last one stands for \(0{,}001 \cdot 10^{-6}\). In the last row the difference is too small to show in the rounded last digit, so the table shows \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) itself. So the numbers settle when the difference falls below the last digit shown.

The difference \(a\,\Delta x\) does not contain \(x\), so it is the same at the top and at the middle. The table shows this too: in each row both numbers lie equally far below their final values, for example \(1{,}952 - 1{,}757 = 0{,}195\) at the middle and \(3{,}904 - 3{,}709 = 0{,}195\) at the top. The difference comes from the term \(-a\,\Delta x^2\) of step 2, the part of the square \((x + \Delta x)^2\) that does not contain \(x\). After the division by \(\Delta x\) in step 5 it becomes \(-a\,\Delta x\).

So the difference \(a\,\Delta x\) goes to zero as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, and we expect the limit to be the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). But a table, as we said in section 1.2, shows only a few rows. The equation (1.6) has exactly the form of the example \(g(h) = 5 - h\) of section 1.3.3: the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) stands in place of \(5\), the change \(\Delta x\) in place of \(h\), and the subtracted term has an extra factor \(a\). So we show the limit with the same steps as there. Because of the factor \(a\), the band will be \(\delta = \eta / a\) instead of \(\delta = \eta\).

  1. Let us show from the definition of the limit 1.5 that the limit of the quotient is \(d = a\,(2L - 2x)\). The definition, written out for the quotient of step 5 as a function of the change \(\Delta x\) (which plays the role of \(h\)) and for this number \(d\), demands: for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) - a\,(2L - 2x)| < \eta\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\). The quotient is defined for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\), so all the points the condition talks about are in the domain.
  2. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). For it we must find a band \(\delta\).
  3. Simplify the difference. By the equation (1.6), \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) = a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\), so the difference is \(a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x - a\,(2L - 2x) = -a\,\Delta x\).
  4. The numbers \(-a\,\Delta x\) and \(a\,\Delta x\) are equally far from zero, and since \(a > 0\), \(|a\,\Delta x| = a\,|\Delta x|\). So \(|{-a\,\Delta x}| = a\,|\Delta x|\), and the condition we must reach is \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\).
  5. We divide the inequality by the positive number \(a\), so it does not turn around: the condition holds exactly when \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\). So we take the band \(\delta = \eta / a\), which is positive.
  6. Check. For every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\) we have \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\), so \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\), and by steps 8 and 9 this is the difference between the quotient and \(d\).
  7. We picked the error freely and found a band for it. The condition of the definition holds, so the limit of the quotient is \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and
    \[u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

Step 5 was the heart of the computation. As \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, both the numerator \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) and the denominator \(\Delta x\) go to zero. If only one of them did, the outcome would be clear. A numerator going to zero over a fixed denominator gives quotients that go to zero. A fixed nonzero numerator over a denominator going to zero gives quotients whose size grows beyond every bound. When both go to zero together, the quotient can approach an ordinary number, and which number depends on how fast the numerator shrinks compared with the denominator. Step 4 showed how fast: the numerator is \(\Delta x\) times \(a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\), so it shrinks as fast as \(\Delta x\) itself, and the factor that remains approaches \(a\,(2L - 2x)\). In the bar the numerator and the denominator are the elongation and the length of the piece. At the top of the bar the piece of 1 m stretches by \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) m, the piece of 0,001 m only by \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\) m. The elongation of a piece is its length times its strain from the table:

\[3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{m} \cdot 3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6} , \qquad 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 0{,}001\ \text{m} \cdot 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6} .\]

The lengths differ by a factor of 1000, the strains only a little, so the elongation of the long piece is \(1000 \cdot 3{,}709 / 3{,}904 = 950\) times larger, almost a thousand times. The strains differ a little because the long piece reaches from the top down to \(x = 1\) m, and lower down the material is stretched less. By step 12, very short pieces at \(x = 1\) m have strains close to \(a\,(2L - 2x) = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} \cdot 18\ \text{m} = 3{,}514 \cdot 10^{-6}\), less than \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) at the top. The strain of the long piece, \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\), lies between the two, exactly halfway, \((3{,}904 + 3{,}514)/2 = 3{,}709\), because the strain falls evenly along the piece. The shorter the piece, the less its strain differs from the one at the top, and the closer the ratio of the elongations comes to the ratio of the lengths. The elongation and the length vanish together, and their ratio, the strain, approaches a definite number. With patient algebra we cancelled the common factor \(\Delta x\) in step 5 and turned the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and after that the limit was easy. We will compute derivatives this way later as well, until we derive rules that do this work once and for all.

1.4 Strain at a point

Let us return to the bar. The strain of the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is the difference quotient of the displacement, and as the length of the piece goes to zero it approaches the derivative of the displacement. This derivative is the measure we were looking for.

Definition 1.8 Strain at a point (deformacija v točki)

Let \(u(x)\) be the displacement of the point of the bar that was at distance \(x\) from a chosen end before loading. The strain (deformacija) of the bar at the point \(x\) is the derivative of the displacement:

\[\varepsilon(x) = u'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.7}\]

Source: BARBER (1.35), (1.36) (PDF p. 33), ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF p. 66). In books it is often written \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) or \(e_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the displacement in the direction \(x\) (why two indices, see section 1.1). The sign \(\partial\) writes the derivative when the displacement depends not only on one distance \(x\) but on the position of the point in space. We will meet it when we describe strain in space.

So the strain at a point is, like the strain of a bar (1.1), a number without a unit, an elongation per unit length. The difference is that we do not compute it for the whole bar, but for shorter and shorter pieces around the point, and take the number that their strains approach. So the name strain at a point is a shorthand. It does not mean that the point itself stretches, since it has no length. It describes the material close to the point, in pieces that can be as short as we like. For a bar that stretches the same everywhere, the two strains are equal. Indeed, if the displacement is \(u(x) = \varepsilon_0\,x\) with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\), the difference quotient is \(\varepsilon_0\,\Delta x / \Delta x = \varepsilon_0\) for every \(\Delta x\), its limit is \(\varepsilon_0\), and the total elongation is \(u(L) - u(0) = \varepsilon_0 L\), so \(\Delta L / L = \varepsilon_0\).

For the hanging bar we have already computed the derivative:

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

At the top, at \(x = 0\), the strain is \(2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 10 = 3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\). At the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the bottom end zero. These are exactly the numbers the columns of the table approached. The strain falls evenly from top to bottom, just as the weight carried by each cross section falls: the top carries the whole bar, the middle half of it, the bottom end nothing.

The total elongation of the bar is the displacement of the bottom end, \(\Delta L = u(L) - u(0) = aL^2 = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and the average strain is \(\Delta L / L = aL = 1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\). This is exactly the strain at the middle. When the strain changes evenly along the bar, the average really is the value at the middle. But the most stretched material, at the top, is stretched twice as much as the average. If we judged by the average whether the bar holds, we would be wrong by a factor of two.

One more simple but important observation. Suppose the ceiling sags by 1 mm for some reason. Then every point of the bar moves by an extra millimetre, and the new displacement is \(u(x) + 0{,}001\) m. The strain does not change. In the difference quotient the extra millimetre cancels, \((u(x + \Delta x) + 0{,}001) - (u(x) + 0{,}001) = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). A displacement that is the same for all points does not deform the body, it only moves it as a whole. To give such moves a name, we imagine a body that cannot be deformed at all.

Definition 1.9 Rigid body and rigid-body displacement (togo telo in togi premik)

A rigid body (togo telo) is a body in which the distance between any two points stays the same, whatever forces act on it. A displacement of a body that does not change the distance between any two of its points is called a rigid-body displacement (togi premik).

Source: CM §3.3.1 (PDF p. 118), CM §3.8.1.1 (PDF p. 154), BARBER §1.1.6 (PDF p. 32).

No real body is rigid, since every body changes its shape under a force, as we saw at the start of the chapter. A rigid body is the picture of a body that deforms so little that its change of shape does not matter for the question at hand. The name rigid-body displacement says that this is the only kind of displacement a rigid body can undergo (BARBER §1.1.6, PDF p. 32). The extra millimetre from the sagging ceiling is a rigid-body displacement: it moves all points of the bar by the same distance, so the distances between them stay the same. But a rigid-body displacement need not be the same for all points. A body can also be turned as a whole without any distance changing, and we will meet this in section 1.6 (CM §3.8.1.1, PDF p. 156).

Since a displacement that is the same for all points does not change the strain, we can never compute the displacement completely from the strain: we can always add such a displacement. For the hanging bar it is fixed by the condition that the top is attached to the ceiling, \(u(0) = 0\) (BARBER (1.39)).

So far the bar has been just a line: every point moved along the same straight line, and the displacement was a single number. But the bar is a body, and like every body it has a length, a width and a height. A point in a body can move in any direction, and a small piece of the body can at the same time stretch in one direction, shorten in another, and twist. One number is not enough to describe displacements in space. We need quantities that have a size and a direction, and we start the next section with them.

1.5 Mathematics: points and vectors

Once again we put mechanics aside for a moment. To describe displacements in space we need two things. First we must say with numbers where a point is. Then we must say with numbers where and how far it has moved. We will build both from an experience everyone has, measuring a room with a tape measure.

1.5.1 Coordinates

How do we tell someone over the phone where a lamp hangs in a room? We can say: start in the corner of the room, walk 2 m along one wall, then 3 m along the other wall, and then 2 m up. Three numbers, \(2\), \(3\) and \(2\), fix the position of the lamp exactly, provided we have agreed on three things beforehand: at which point we start, in which three directions we walk, and in what order we list the numbers. We choose the directions perpendicular to each other, like the edges where the floor and the two walls meet in the corner of the room, and we measure distances along all three with the same tape. Such an agreement has a name of its own. It is the simplest of several kinds of such agreements, and we will talk about the others at the end of this subsection.

Definition 1.10 Rectangular Cartesian coordinate system and coordinates (pravokotni kartezični koordinatni sistem in koordinate)

A rectangular Cartesian coordinate system (pravokotni kartezični koordinatni sistem) consists of a chosen point \(O\), called the origin (izhodišče), and three mutually perpendicular straight lines through \(O\), the coordinate axes (koordinatne osi) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). On each axis a positive direction is chosen, and lengths are measured in the same unit on all three axes. The position of a point \(P\) is described by three numbers, its coordinates (koordinate) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\): we reach \(P\) if, starting at the origin, we walk the distance \(x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\), then the distance \(y\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), and then the distance \(z\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). A negative number means walking in the opposite direction. We write \(P = (x, y, z)\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 167 (PDF p. 382).

So coordinates are numbers, and the coordinate system is the agreement that gives these numbers their meaning. The lamp of our example is the point \((2, 3, 2)\) if the axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie along the walls, the axis \(z\) points vertically up and the units are metres (figure 1.12). The order matters: the point \((3, 2, 2)\) is a different point, 3 m along the first wall and 2 m along the second. That is why we say that coordinates are an ordered triple (urejena trojica) of numbers. It matters which number stands in which place, because each place belongs to its own axis.

In another coordinate system the same lamp has other coordinates. If we start in the opposite corner of the room, all three numbers change, while the lamp stays where it is. So coordinates always belong to a point together with a coordinate system. In each problem we will choose the system once and keep it. Other books often write coordinates as \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\) or, for the position before loading, \(X_1\), \(X_2\), \(X_3\) (CM (3.3.14)), because with numbered axes the calculations that repeat for all three directions are easier to write. As long as we can, we will stay with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

The system we have described is the simplest, but not the only one. It is called Cartesian because its axes are straight and each carries a fixed unit, and rectangular because the axes are perpendicular and the unit is the same on all three (EP §1.2, p. 16). The axes can also be oblique, and then too every point gets its own triple of numbers (EP §1.2). But coordinates need not be distances along straight axes at all. For a pipe it is more natural to say how far a point is from the axis of the pipe, at what angle around the axis it lies and how high it is. These are cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\). For a ball we give the distance from the centre and two angles, much like latitude and longitude on the Earth. These are spherical coordinates (SADD (1.9.1), (1.9.2), PDF p. 29).

All these systems share a single requirement. A coordinate system in some part of our space is a rule that assigns to every point exactly one triple of numbers, different triples to different points, and nearby triples to nearby points (CM (2.4.7), (2.4.8), PDF pp. 70-71, SADD §1.9). Cylindrical coordinates break this requirement in two places. On the axis \(z\) the distance from the axis is zero, and every angle \(\theta\) describes the same point. Where the angle completes a full turn and starts again at zero, two neighbouring points on either side get very different angles. That is why a coordinate system often covers only a part of space.

We need three numbers because our space has three dimensions, not because coordinates have to come in threes. The same requirement works with any number of coordinates. On a line one number is enough, the distance from a chosen point. On a curved surface, where there are no straight axes at all, two are enough, like latitude and longitude on the Earth (VK §11.1, p. 308). The number of numbers a space needs is called its dimension (razsežnost). KREYSZIG §7.4 (PDF p. 312) gives the precise definition. The set of all ordered \(n\)-tuples of real numbers is written \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF p. 335). So a coordinate system gives every point of a space with \(n\) dimensions its own element of \(\mathbb{R}^n\): our space corresponds to \(\mathbb{R}^3\), a plane to \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Nothing stops at three. \(\mathbb{R}^4\), the set of all ordered quadruples of numbers, is as good a set as \(\mathbb{R}^3\), only we cannot draw it. In mechanics such spaces turn up by themselves. The position of a mechanism with several moving parts is described by as many numbers as it has independent ways to move, its degrees of freedom. These numbers are called generalized coordinates (posplošene koordinate) (GR §1.2.3, PDF p. 38). A pendulum swinging in a plane needs only one, its angle (GR, Figure 1.5). The positions of a mechanism with \(n\) degrees of freedom form a space with \(n\) dimensions (SPONG §3.1, PDF p. 28), and it needs \(n\) coordinates, also when \(n\) is larger than three. We will meet them in chapter 8. The most general form of a coordinate system, for spaces with any number of dimensions and also for curved spaces, is called a chart (karta) in mathematics, and it is the starting point of differential geometry.

What we derive next, the move from one point to another as the differences of their coordinates and the formula that tells from the coordinates how far apart two points are, holds only in a rectangular Cartesian system. In cylindrical coordinates, for example, two points with the same difference of angles are further apart the further from the axis they lie, so the distance does not come from the differences of the coordinates in the same way (SADD (1.9.4)). Until we say otherwise, a coordinate system in this book means a rectangular Cartesian system.

1.5.2 Vector

Now let a point move. A fly sits on the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\). It flies off and lands on the floor at the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\). How do we say with numbers where it has moved? Let us compare the coordinates. The coordinate \(x\) has increased by \(1\), the coordinate \(y\) by \(2\), and the coordinate \(z\) has decreased by \(2\). The triple of changes \((1, 2, -2)\) tells where the fly ended its flight relative to where it began: 1 m further in the direction of the axis \(x\), 2 m further in the direction of the axis \(y\) and 2 m lower. It does not tell where the fly started. A fly that flew from the point \((0, 0, 2)\) to the point \((1, 2, 0)\) would have the same triple of changes, although it flew somewhere else. Nor does the triple tell along which path the fly flew, only where it ended. Such a triple is a new kind of object. It does not describe a position but a move.

Definition 1.11 Vector (vektor)

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system we write a vector (vektor) \(\vek a\) as an ordered triple of real numbers, \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). The numbers \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the components (komponente) of the vector. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components. The vector \(\vek 0 = (0, 0, 0)\) is the zero vector (ničelni vektor).

The vector from the point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) to the point \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\) has the differences of the coordinates as its components:

\[\overrightarrow{PQ} = (x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}},\ y_{\mathrm{Q}} - y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}) . \tag{1.8}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (1) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

For the fly, \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (3 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (1, 2, -2)\). So a vector, like a point, is an ordered triple of numbers, but it means something else. A point tells where something is. A vector tells how to get from one point to another. Bold type, \(\vek a\), distinguishes vectors from numbers. On paper we write a vector with an arrow over the letter, \(\vec a\).

We draw a vector as an arrow from the point \(P\) to the point \(Q\). The same vector can be drawn from any starting point, just as both flies in the example above had the same vector (figure 1.13): the two arrows start at different places, but both point 1 m in the direction \(x\), 2 m in the direction \(y\) and 2 m down. The vector from the origin \(O = (0, 0, 0)\) to the point \(P\) has the coordinates of \(P\) as its components, because the coordinates of the origin are zero. Books call it the position vector (krajevni vektor) of the point \(P\).

The components of a vector depend on the coordinate system, just like the coordinates of points. The arrow itself, however, does not depend on the coordinate system. The fly made the same flight however we placed the axes. The triple of numbers is only how the arrow is written in the chosen system, just as the coordinates are how the position of a point is written, and if we turn the axes, the same vector has other components. So when we say that a vector is a triple of numbers, we always mean how it is written in the chosen system. Other books also write the components as \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(a_3\) (CM) or \([a_1, a_2, a_3]\) (KREYSZIG (1)).

1.5.3 Calculating with vectors

On the floor the fly crawls a little further, 2 m in the direction of the axis \(x\), that is, by the vector \((2, 0, 0)\). Where is it now? Each coordinate changes by the corresponding component, and from \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) it gets to the point \((5, 5, 0)\). From the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) to there the vector is \((5 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (3, 2, -2)\). We get the same vector if we add the components of the two parts of the path: \(1 + 2 = 3\), \(2 + 0 = 2\) and \(-2 + 0 = -2\). So two moves one after the other have the same effect as a single move whose components are the sums of the components. This is the model for adding vectors. The difference and the multiple are defined in the same way.

Definition 1.12 Sum, difference and multiple of vectors (vsota, razlika in večkratnik vektorjev)

For vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) and a number \(k\), the sum (vsota), the difference (razlika) and the multiple (večkratnik) are the vectors

\[\begin{aligned} \vek a + \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ \vek a - \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} - b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} - b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} - b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ k\,\vek a &= (k\,a_{\mathrm{x}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{y}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{z}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.9}\]

A vector is added to a point in the same way: for a point \(P = (x, y, z)\), \(P + \vek a = (x + a_{\mathrm{x}},\ y + a_{\mathrm{y}},\ z + a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the point we reach from \(P\) by the move \(\vek a\). The difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) of (1.8).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (3), (5) (PDF pp. 383-384), CM §2.2.1.1 and §2.2.1.2 (PDF p. 40).

All these operations are calculated component by component: the component \(x\) of the result is obtained from the components \(x\) only, and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). We draw the sum (figure 1.14) by attaching the arrow of the vector \(\vek b\) to the tip of the arrow of the vector \(\vek a\). The sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second. If we draw both arrows from the same point, the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram that the two arrows span (KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 172, PDF p. 383).

The multiple \(2\,\vek a\) is the move we get if we make the move \(\vek a\) twice in a row, since component by component \(\vek a + \vek a = 2\,\vek a\). The multiple \((-1)\,\vek a\) undoes the move \(\vek a\), since \(\vek a + (-1)\,\vek a = \vek 0\). It is the move back. The difference \(\vek a - \vek b\) is the vector we must add to the vector \(\vek b\) to get \(\vek a\), and the difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector we must add to the point \(P\) to reach the point \(Q\).

We will calculate with vectors as we do with numbers: move the terms of a sum around, shift brackets and factor out numbers. Are we allowed to? The operations are defined component by component, and the components are numbers, for which we know these rules. So we expect the rules to hold for vectors too. Let us check.

Lemma 1.13 Rules of vector arithmetic (računska pravila za vektorje)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and all numbers \(k\), \(m\):

  1. \(\vek a + \vek b = \vek b + \vek a\),
  2. \((\vek a + \vek b) + \vek c = \vek a + (\vek b + \vek c)\),
  3. \(\vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\) and \(\vek a - \vek a = \vek 0\),
  4. \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b) = k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) and \((k + m)\,\vek a = k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\),
  5. \(k\,(m\,\vek a) = (k\,m)\,\vek a\) and \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\).
Proof

We must show that for each rule the vectors on the left and on the right are equal.

  1. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components (definition 1.11). So we check each rule separately for the components \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. All operations in the rules are defined component by component ((1.9)), so the component \(x\) of each side is calculated only from the components \(x\) of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and from the numbers \(k\), \(m\).
  3. Rule 1. The component \(x\) of the left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal, because the order of adding numbers does not change the sum.
  4. Rule 2. The component \(x\) of the left side is \((a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}) + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + (b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}})\). The two numbers are equal, because when adding three numbers it does not matter which two we add first.
  5. Rule 3. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a + \vek 0\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\). The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a - \vek a\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), which is the component \(x\) of the zero vector.
  6. Rule 4. The component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b)\) is \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + k\,b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. The component \(x\) of the vector \((k + m)\,\vek a\) is \((k + m)\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). These two numbers are equal by the same rule.
  7. Rule 5. The component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(m\,\vek a)\) is \(k\,(m\,a_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \((k\,m)\,\vek a\) is \((k\,m)\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal, because when multiplying three numbers it does not matter which two we multiply first. The component \(x\) of the vector \(1\,\vek a\) is \(1 \cdot a_{\mathrm{x}} = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\).
  8. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same, with \(x\) replaced everywhere by \(y\) or \(z\).
  9. The left and the right side of each rule agree in all three components, so by step 1 they are equal.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (4), (6) (PDF p. 384), CM §2.2.1.1 (PDF p. 40).

The proof has shown a principle we will use all the time: we check an equality of two vectors by checking three equalities of numbers, one for each component. Every rule for adding, subtracting and multiplying by a number that we will need will be checked in this way.

These rules matter more than they seem to. Mathematics turns them around. It takes any set in which we can add two elements and multiply an element by a real number so that the result is again an element of the set. Then it requires that all the rules of vector arithmetic of lemma 1.13 hold. Only in rule 3 no difference is given in advance, so instead of \(\vek a - \vek a = \vek 0\) it requires that every element has an opposite element which, added to it, gives the zero element. Such a set together with its addition and its multiplication by numbers is called a vector space (vektorski prostor), and its elements are called vectors (LEBL2 Def. 8.1.1, PDF p. 8, KREYSZIG §7.9, PDF pp. 335-336). So whether something is a vector does not depend on that object alone. It depends on the set in which we look at it, and on how we add and multiply by numbers in that set. If only one rule fails, for example \(1\,\vek a = \vek a\), the set is not a vector space.

Arrows in space are only one example. Vectors in this sense are also functions on an interval, which we add by adding their values (LEBL2 Example 8.1.4), and tables of numbers of size \(2 \times 2\) (KREYSZIG §7.9, Example 1). So whatever we derive from these rules alone will hold for all such objects at once. In mechanics, however, the word vector says more than this. What more, we will see in section 1.5.5, when we look at how the components change if we turn the axes.

1.5.4 Length of a vector

How far did the fly travel when it flew from the lamp to the floor by the vector \((1, 2, -2)\)? Not \(1 + 2 + 2 = 5\) m, because that would be the path along the axes: first 1 m in the direction \(x\), then 2 m in the direction \(y\) and then 2 m down. But the fly flies straight. We work out the distance in two steps (figure 1.16).

First we look only at the floor. The point on the floor directly below the fly moves \(1\) m in the direction \(x\) and \(2\) m in the direction \(y\). These two paths are the legs of a right triangle, since the axes are perpendicular, and the straight path of the point on the floor is its hypotenuse. By the Pythagorean theorem from school, the square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the squares of the legs, \(1^2 + 2^2 = 5\), so the point on the floor moves \(\sqrt 5\) m.

Then we add the height. The fly has come down \(2\) m vertically, and the vertical is perpendicular to every direction on the floor. So the straight path of the point on the floor and the vertical descent are the legs of a second right triangle, whose hypotenuse is the path of the fly. The square of the fly's path is \(5 + 2^2 = 9\), and the path itself is \(\sqrt 9 = 3\) m.

The two steps together say that the square of the distance is the sum of the squares of all three components, \(1^2 + 2^2 + (-2)^2 = 9\). The sign of a component makes no difference, because the square of a negative number is positive.

Definition 1.14 Length of a vector and distance (dolžina vektorja in razdalja)

The length (dolžina) or magnitude (velikost) of the vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the number

\[|\vek a| = \sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2} . \tag{1.10}\]

The distance (razdalja) between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) is the length of the vector between them, \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (2) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

So the length is a number that is never negative, and it has the same unit as the components. We do not prove the Pythagorean theorem in this book. We measure coordinates with the same tape along three mutually perpendicular axes, and for such coordinates school geometry gives us the formula (1.10), as we saw with the fly. From now on this formula is what the word length means, and everything we claim about lengths we will derive from it. It holds only for components in a rectangular Cartesian system, where the axes are perpendicular and the units equal.

Length too has a more general form. Mathematics calls a norm (norma) any rule that assigns a number to a vector so that three requirements hold: the number is never negative and is zero only for the zero vector, for a multiple of a vector it is multiplied by the absolute value of the number, and the norm of a sum is not larger than the sum of the norms (LEBL2 Def. 8.2.1, PDF p. 20). Our length is an example of a norm, called the Euclidean norm (LEBL2, PDF p. 20). More general still is a distance between the elements of any set, which must meet similar requirements (LEBL1 Def. 7.1.1, PDF p. 255).

For a vector with a single nonzero component, for example \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\), the length is \(\sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\), the nonnegative number whose square is \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2\). This is exactly the absolute value \(|a_{\mathrm{x}}|\), the distance of the number \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) from zero from section 1.3. That is why we use the same sign for the length of a vector as for the absolute value. The length of a vector measures distances in space as the absolute value measures them on the number line.

The distance between the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) and the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) on the floor is the length of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (1, 2, -2)\), that is, \(3\) m. This is exactly what we will need in section 1.6: the distance between two points of a body before and after loading.

1.5.5 Unit vectors and steps along the axes

With the length we can separate the two things an arrow carries: how long it is and where it points. The flight of the fly \(\vek a = (1, 2, -2)\) is \(3\) m long. If we multiply it by \(1/3\), we get the vector \(\tfrac13 (1, 2, -2) = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, -\tfrac23)\). It points the same way, and its length is \(\sqrt{\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac49} = 1\). Such a vector carries only the direction.

Definition 1.15 Unit vector (enotski vektor)

A unit vector (enotski vektor) is a vector of length \(1\).

Source: CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

Every vector except the zero vector can be shortened or lengthened into a unit vector that points the same way.

Lemma 1.16 Unit vector in the direction of a vector (enotski vektor v smeri vektorja)

For every vector \(\vek a \neq \vek 0\) the vector

\[\hat{\vek a} = \frac{1}{|\vek a|}\,\vek a \tag{1.11}\]

is a unit vector, and \(\vek a = |\vek a|\,\hat{\vek a}\).

Proof

We compute the length of \(\hat{\vek a}\) from its components.

  1. Since \(\vek a \neq \vek 0\), at least one component of \(\vek a\) is not zero, since two vectors are equal only if they agree in all components (definition of a vector 1.11). Its square is positive and the squares of the other two are not negative, so \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2 > 0\) and \(|\vek a| > 0\). So we may divide by \(|\vek a|\).
  2. By the definition of the multiple ((1.9)), \(\hat{\vek a} = (a_{\mathrm{x}}/|\vek a|,\ a_{\mathrm{y}}/|\vek a|,\ a_{\mathrm{z}}/|\vek a|)\).
  3. The sum of the squares of these components is \((a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2)/|\vek a|^2\), because the square of a quotient is the quotient of the squares.
  4. By (1.10) the numerator is \(|\vek a|^2\), so the sum of the squares is \(1\), and by the same formula \(|\hat{\vek a}| = \sqrt 1 = 1\).
  5. By rule 5 of the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.13), \(|\vek a|\,\hat{\vek a} = (|\vek a| \cdot \tfrac{1}{|\vek a|})\,\vek a = 1\,\vek a = \vek a\).

Source: CM (2.2.1), (2.2.2) (PDF p. 39).

So every vector except the zero vector is its length times a unit vector that gives its direction. The hat over the letter is the usual mark of a unit vector (CM (2.2.1)). For the fly, \(\hat{\vek a} = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, -\tfrac23)\) and \(\vek a = 3\,\hat{\vek a}\).

We described the flight of the fly \((1, 2, -2)\) in words as three moves: 1 m in the direction of the axis \(x\), 2 m in the direction of the axis \(y\) and 2 m down. With the sum and the multiple we can now write this with vectors. The unit vector in the positive direction of the axis \(x\) is \((1, 0, 0)\): it points along the axis \(x\), and its length is \(\sqrt{1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2} = 1\). In the same way \((0, 1, 0)\) is the unit vector along the axis \(y\) and \((0, 0, 1)\) along the axis \(z\). The flight of the fly is one such step in the direction of the axis \(x\), two in the direction of the axis \(y\) and two against the direction of the axis \(z\):

\[1\,(1, 0, 0) + 2\,(0, 1, 0) + (-2)\,(0, 0, 1) = (1, 0, 0) + (0, 2, 0) + (0, 0, -2) = (1, 2, -2) .\]

So the components of a vector are the numbers of steps along the individual axes from which the vector is made. We will need these three vectors often, so they get a name.

Definition 1.17 Unit vectors of the axes (enotski vektorji osi)

In a chosen rectangular Cartesian coordinate system the unit vectors of the axes (enotski vektorji osi) are the vectors

\[\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (1, 0, 0) , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, 1, 0) , \quad \vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1) . \tag{1.12}\]

Each of them is the unit vector in the positive direction of its axis.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (8), (9) (PDF p. 385), SADD §1.2 (PDF p. 12).

Every vector can be made from such steps, and in only one way.

Lemma 1.18 A vector as a sum of steps along the axes (vektor kot vsota korakov vzdolž osi)

For every vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\)

\[\vek a = a_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} . \tag{1.13}\]

If \(\vek a = k\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + n\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) for some numbers \(k\), \(m\) and \(n\), then \(k = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(m = a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n = a_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Proof

First we calculate the right side, then we compare components.

  1. By the definition of the multiple ((1.9)), \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = (a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 1,\ a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0,\ a_{\mathrm{x}} \cdot 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\).
  2. In the same way, \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, a_{\mathrm{z}})\).
  3. By the definition of the sum, \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0) + (0, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}} + 0,\ 0 + a_{\mathrm{y}},\ 0 + 0) = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, 0)\).
  4. We add \((0, 0, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and get \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\), that is, \(\vek a\). We write no brackets in the sum of three vectors, because by rule 2 of the rules of vector arithmetic (lemma 1.13) it does not matter which two we add first. This proves the first claim.
  5. For the second claim we repeat steps 1 to 4 with the numbers \(k\), \(m\), \(n\) in place of \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) and get \(k\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} + n\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = (k, m, n)\).
  6. By assumption this vector equals the vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components (definition 1.11), so \(k = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(m = a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(n = a_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (8) (PDF p. 385).

The lemma says two things. The components are exactly the numbers of steps along the axes that make up the vector. And there are no other numbers that give the same vector, so the components in the chosen system tell everything about the vector and nothing more. Many books write the unit vectors of the axes as \(\vek i\), \(\vek j\), \(\vek k\), and the flight of the fly there would be \(\vek i + 2\,\vek j - 2\,\vek k\) (KREYSZIG (8), PDF p. 385). Others write them \(\vek e_1\), \(\vek e_2\), \(\vek e_3\) (SADD §1.2, PDF p. 12), and so will we, once we need digits instead of the letters \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).

Now let us show what we only claimed after the definition of a vector 1.11: the arrow is one, but its components depend on the coordinate system. Let us turn the axes about the vertical axis \(z\) by a quarter turn, so that the new axis \(x\) points where the old axis \(y\) pointed, the new axis \(y\) points opposite to the old axis \(x\), and the axis \(z\) stays as it was. We mark the unit vectors of the new axes with a bar on top. Written in the old system they are

\[\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} = (0, 1, 0) , \quad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} = (-1, 0, 0) , \quad \bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 0, 1) .\]

The flight of the fly is one step along the old axis \(x\), two along the old axis \(y\) and two down. The old axis \(y\) is now the new axis \(x\), so two steps along the old axis \(y\) are two steps along the new axis \(x\). The old axis \(x\) points opposite to the new axis \(y\), so one step along the old axis \(x\) is one step back along the new axis \(y\). The vertical part does not change. Let us check:

\[2\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{x}} + (-1)\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{y}} + (-2)\,\bar{\vek e}_{\mathrm{z}} = (0, 2, 0) + (1, 0, 0) + (0, 0, -2) = (1, 2, -2) .\]

So in the turned system the same flight has the components \((2, -1, -2)\). The arrow is the same, its length \(\sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-2)^2} = 3\) m is the same, but the numbers are different. How the components change under any turn of the axes we will derive when we need it.

Nor are the unit vectors of the axes the only triple from which every vector can be made. Any three vectors that, drawn from one point, do not lie in one plane serve equally well, for example vectors along axes that are not perpendicular. Every vector is in exactly one way a sum of their multiples, and the numbers in this sum are the components of the vector with respect to these three vectors (CM (2.2.30), PDF p. 48). The unit vectors of rectangular axes are the simplest choice, because the components with respect to them are simply the differences of coordinates of (1.8). Until we say otherwise, a component of a vector always means a component with respect to the unit vectors of the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system.

Now we can also answer the question that the vector space left open: is every triple of numbers a vector? In the mathematical sense yes, since we add triples and multiply them by numbers component by component, and all the rules hold. In mechanics, however, the word vector says more. Take the masses of three parts of a machine, for example \((2, 5, 1)\) kg. Two such machines together have \((4, 10, 2)\) kg, so it makes sense to add such triples and multiply them by numbers. But they are not arrows. When we turn the axes, the components of the fly's flight change from \((1, 2, -2)\) to \((2, -1, -2)\), while the masses of the machine parts stay as they are. A vector in mechanics is a quantity with a size and a direction, an arrow, whose components change in a definite way when the axes are turned, while the arrow itself stays the same (SADD (1.4.6), PDF p. 17, BARBER §1.1.4, PDF p. 26). So three numbers that come out of some calculation may or may not be the components of a vector. We decide by how they change when we turn the axes (BOWER Appendix B.1.3, PDF p. 758).

1.6 Displacement in space

Let us return to mechanics, where we left it at the end of section 1.4: to the bar and to bodies with three dimensions. When we load a body, every point of it moves from its old position to a new one. For the bar we described this move by a single number, because the points moved only along the bar. In a body a point can move in any direction, so we describe its move by a vector.

Definition 1.19 Displacement vector (vektor pomika)

If a point of a body moves under loading from the position \(P\) to a new position, its displacement vector (vektor pomika) \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the vector from the old position to the new one. So the new position is \(P + \vek u\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) say how far the point moves in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Source: CM (3.2.7) (PDF p. 117), BARBER (1.32) (PDF pp. 31-32), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

How does the bar of the first sections fit into this? There we labelled a point of the bar by its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, measured before loading, and its displacement by a single number \(u(x)\), how far the point moved down. To write this displacement as a vector, we choose a coordinate system that fits the bar. We put the origin at the top end of the bar and point the axis \(x\) down along the bar. Then the coordinate \(x\) of a point on the axis of the bar is exactly its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, and a move down by \(u(x)\) is a move in the direction of the axis \(x\). So the displacement vector of this point is

\[\vek u = \big(u(x),\ 0,\ 0\big) .\]

Its component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the number \(u(x)\) of the previous sections. The components \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are zero, because this point moves only along the axis \(x\).

This holds for the points on the axis of the bar, that is, for the line we have looked at so far. A bar that we stretch also gets a little thinner. When a steel bar gets longer by a thousandth of its length, its thickness decreases by about \(0{,}3\) thousandths of the thickness (BOWER §3.2, PDF p. 102, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). So the points off the axis of the bar also move a little sideways, toward the axis, and for them \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) are not zero. We have not taken this into account so far. In a body all three components of the displacement are in general different from zero.

Can we explain the thinning by the volume? Suppose the material kept its volume exactly. A cube of the material with edge \(1\) becomes a block with the edge \(1{,}001\) along the axis and the edges \(1 - d\) across it, and its volume is \(1{,}001\,(1 - d)^2\) (BARBER §1.3.2, PDF p. 40). This equals \(1\) only for \(d = 1 - 1/\sqrt{1{,}001} \approx 0{,}0005\): the thickness would decrease by half a thousandth. Steel gets thinner by less, \(0{,}0003\), so its volume grows a little, \(1{,}001 \cdot 0{,}9997^2 \approx 1{,}0004\), that is, by about \(0{,}04\) %. So steel does not keep its volume exactly. Keeping the volume is a limiting case: a material that keeps its volume exactly has the ratio of the relative decrease of thickness to the relative increase of length equal to \(0{,}5\) (BOWER §3.2, PDF p. 102), and rubber, with \(0{,}49\), comes very close (BOWER, Table 3.1, PDF p. 100). If the ratio were \(0\), the bar would not get thinner at all (BOWER p. 102). For most materials it lies between these two cases, typically between \(0{,}2\) and \(0{,}49\), and around \(0{,}3\) for metals (BOWER p. 102). How much a material gets thinner is therefore a property of the material, which we measure in a test. Books call this ratio Poisson's ratio (Poissonovo število), and we will meet it in chapter 3.

Books also write the components of the displacement vector as \(u_1\), \(u_2\), \(u_3\) (CM) or \(u\), \(v\), \(w\) (SADD §2.1, PDF p. 39). In this book the index says the direction, as with the components of every vector.

Every point of the body has its own displacement vector, and different points move differently. For the hanging bar the displacement depended on the position \(x\). In a body it depends on the position of the point, that is, on three coordinates.

Definition 1.20 Displacement field (polje pomikov)

The displacement field (polje pomikov) of a body is the rule that assigns to every point \((x, y, z)\) of the body, taken in its position before loading, its displacement vector

\[\vek u(x, y, z) = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z)\big) . \tag{1.14}\]

Source: CM (3.2.8) (PDF p. 117), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

The displacement field is a rule of the same kind as a function of definition 1.4, except that it has three numbers at the input and three at the output. At the input are the coordinates of the point before loading, at the output the components of its displacement. Each component on its own, for example \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\), is a rule whose input is three numbers and whose output is one number. The numbers at the input are called variables (spremenljivke), so such a rule is called a function of three variables (funkcija treh spremenljivk). The word field comes from a picture: if we draw at every point of the body the arrow of its displacement, the body is covered with arrows like a field with blades of grass.

How do we tell from the displacement field whether the body is deformed? If the displacement is the same vector at all points, all points move equally, and the body only moves as a whole. This is the rigid-body displacement of section 1.4, now in space. But the body can also be turned as a whole without being deformed. Then the points move differently, while the distances between them stay the same, so this too is a rigid-body displacement. Every rigid-body displacement is a move of all points by the same vector, a turn of the body, or both one after the other (CM (3.8.12), PDF p. 156, SADD (2.2.10), PDF p. 45). So displacements that differ from point to point do not yet mean deformation. Deformation is a change of the distances between points, and that is why we will measure it with lengths.

1.6.1 Fibres

For the bar we looked at a small piece between two points. In a body we do the same, except that the piece can lie in any direction.

Definition 1.21 Material fibre (vlakno)

A material fibre (vlakno) is a straight segment between two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body, that is, a straight line that starts at \(P\) and ends at \(Q\), which we imagine drawn into the material so that its points move together with the material. Before loading the fibre is described by the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\).

Source: CM §3.3.1 (PDF pp. 118-119, material line element), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42, fibers).

A fibre is like a very thin bar inside the body. So we calculate its strain as the strain of a bar (1.1): the elongation divided by the length before loading. The length before loading is \(L_0 = |\overrightarrow{PQ}|\), the length after loading \(L_1\) is the distance between the new positions of the ends, and the elongation is \(L_1 - L_0\). After loading a fibre is in general no longer perfectly straight. A short fibre bends only a little, so we take the distance between the new positions of the ends as the new length. How accurate this is we will see when we shorten the fibre towards zero, as we did with the piece of the bar. For the cube below the fibres stay straight, and there it is exact.

Where does the fibre go? The end \(P\) moves by \(\vek u(P)\), which means the displacement vector at the point \(P\), and the end \(Q\) by \(\vek u(Q)\). The new positions of the ends are \(P_1 = P + \vek u(P)\) and \(Q_1 = Q + \vek u(Q)\). The index \(1\), as in \(L_1\), means the state after loading. We calculate the vector of the fibre after loading, \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\), component by component, the way we checked the rules of vector arithmetic in the proof of lemma 1.13. Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\).

  1. The coordinate \(x\) of the point \(Q_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)\), and that of the point \(P_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\), because a vector is added to a point in the same way as in the sum of vectors, by adding to each coordinate the corresponding component (definition 1.12).
  2. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\) is the difference of these two coordinates ((1.8)), \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)) - (x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\).
  3. We remove the brackets and rearrange the terms by the rules for numbers: \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) + (u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\). The first bracket is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) ((1.8)), the second the component \(x\) of the difference \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\) ((1.9)).
  4. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same. So the two vectors agree in all three components, and by definition 1.11 that is what equality of vectors means, so
    \[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) . \tag{1.15}\]

So a fibre changes only by the difference of the displacements of its ends (figure 1.22). This is the same difference as \(\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) for the piece of the bar, except that it is now a vector. If the displacement is the same at all points, the difference is the zero vector, and every fibre stays as it was.

1.6.2 A sheared cube

Let us try all this on one simple body. We glue a steel cube with an edge of 10 mm to a table by its bottom face and push its top face sideways, so that it moves by 10 µm (figure 1.23). A micrometre, µm, is a thousandth of a millimetre, so the shift is a thousandth of the edge. The cube leans over like the deck of cards of section 1.1: every horizontal layer slides a little relative to the layer below it. Books call such a deformation simple shear (enostavni strig) (CM (3.3.14), PDF p. 123). The word shear describes exactly this, layers sliding past each other like the blades of scissors.

We place the coordinate system at the bottom front left corner of the cube: the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(y\) along the other bottom edge, and the axis \(z\) vertically up. We measure lengths in micrometres, so the edge of the cube is \(10\,000\) µm. How far does each point move? The bottom layer is glued and does not move at all. The top layer, at the height \(10\,000\) µm, moves by \(10\) µm. A layer in between moves in proportion to its height, by a thousandth of it: the layer at the height \(5\,000\) µm by \(5\) µm, the layer at the height \(z\) by \(0{,}001\,z\) (figure 1.24). All points move only in the direction \(x\). So the displacement field is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = 0{,}001\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) = 0 . \tag{1.16}\]

At the bottom face, where \(z = 0\), the displacement is zero, as it must be, since the face is glued down. The field does not depend on \(x\) and \(y\): all points of one layer move equally.

Is it reasonable that the layers move only sideways and not also up or down? One reason is that this way the cube keeps its volume. The front face \(ABCD\) becomes a parallelogram with the same base \(AB\) and the same height, \(10\,000\) µm, and the area of a parallelogram is its base times its height, so the area of the face does not change. The depth of the cube stays the same as well, so its volume does not change either. This holds although the slanted edges get a little longer, as we will compute for the edge \(AD\) below. A deformation that keeps the volume of every piece of a body is called an isochoric deformation (izohorna deformacija), and simple shear is one (CM §3.3.2, PDF pp. 121-122). For small deformations this holds in general: the change of volume of a small piece depends only on its normal strains, while shear changes only the shape, not the volume (BOWER §2.1.9, PDF pp. 51-52).

To see how the material is stretched, we follow four fibres on the front face of the cube, where \(y = 0\) (figure 1.25). Its corners are \(A = (0, 0, 0)\), \(B = (10\,000, 0, 0)\), \(C = (10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) and \(D = (0, 0, 10\,000)\). The fibres are the bottom edge \(AB\), the vertical edge \(AD\), and the two diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\). The bottom corners do not move, \(\vek u(A) = \vek u(B) = \vek 0\), and the top ones move by \(\vek u(C) = \vek u(D) = (10, 0, 0)\). For each fibre we calculate its vector after loading by (1.15), its two lengths by (1.10) and its strain by (1.1).

Let us show the calculation for the vertical edge \(AD\). Before loading \(\overrightarrow{AD} = (0, 0, 10\,000)\), with length \(L_0 = 10\,000\) µm. The difference of the displacements of the ends is \(\vek u(D) - \vek u(A) = (10, 0, 0)\), so the vector after loading is \((0, 0, 10\,000) + (10, 0, 0) = (10, 0, 10\,000)\). Its length is

\[L_1 = \sqrt{10^2 + 0^2 + 10\,000^2} = \sqrt{100\,000\,100} = 10\,000{,}005\ \text{µm} ,\]

and the strain is \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0 = 0{,}005/10\,000 = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The other fibres are calculated in the same way. Before loading both diagonals are \(\sqrt{2 \cdot 10\,000^2} = 14\,142{,}136\) µm long, after loading \(AC\) is \(14\,149{,}208\) µm long and \(BD\) is \(14\,135{,}066\) µm long.

fibre vector before loading vector after loading strain
bottom edge \(AB\) \((10\,000, 0, 0)\) \((10\,000, 0, 0)\) \(0\)
vertical edge \(AD\) \((0, 0, 10\,000)\) \((10, 0, 10\,000)\) \(5{,}0 \cdot 10^{-7}\)
diagonal \(AC\) \((10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) \((10\,010, 0, 10\,000)\) \(+5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
diagonal \(BD\) \((-10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) \((-9\,990, 0, 10\,000)\) \(-4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

The table shows three things that we could not see with the bar.

  1. The diagonal \(AC\) has become longer by five ten-thousandths, and the diagonal \(BD\) shorter by almost exactly as much (figure 1.27). The same material and the same loading, and yet the material is stretched in one direction and shortened in another. How much the material is stretched depends on the direction in which we measure it.
  2. The edges have hardly changed. The bottom edge has stayed the same, and the vertical one has become longer by only \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), a thousand times less than the diagonals.
  3. The vertical edge has tilted, since its top has moved sideways and its bottom has not. So the right angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) at the corner \(A\) has become a little smaller. This is the change of a right angle that a shear strain describes (definition 1.3), here between the directions \(x\) and \(z\). The front face, which was a square, has become a parallelogram, a leaning four-sided figure with parallel opposite sides.

What happens if we push the top face twice as far, by 20 µm? The displacement field is then \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}002\,z\), and when we repeat the calculation, the diagonals get the strains \(+1{,}0005 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(-0{,}9995 \cdot 10^{-3}\), twice as much as before. The vertical edge, however, gets \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\), four times as much as before. The strain of the diagonals grows in proportion to the shift of the top face, and the strain of the vertical edge with the square of the shift.

These numbers show two patterns that need an explanation. The two diagonals change by the same amount with opposite signs, namely by half the ratio between the shift of the top face and the height of the cube: \(0{,}0005\) for the ratio \(10/10\,000 = 0{,}001\). The vertical edge changes a thousand times less, and when the shift doubles, its strain grows four times, so it grows with the square of the shift. Where does this come from? We call the height of the cube \(h\) and the shift of the top face \(s\), so \(h = 10\,000\) µm and \(s = 10\) µm. The ratio \(s/h\) is a ratio of two lengths, a number without a unit.

What gets in the way of the calculation is the square root in the lengths. We get rid of it with one move that we will need again.

  1. We multiply and divide the strain \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) by \(L_1 + L_0\). We may, because \(L_1 + L_0 > 0\), and the value does not change.
  2. The numerator becomes \((L_1 - L_0)(L_1 + L_0)\). When we multiply out the brackets, the terms \(L_1 L_0\) and \(-L_0 L_1\) cancel, and \(L_1^2 - L_0^2\) remains. So
    \[\varepsilon = \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)} . \tag{1.17}\]
    This equality is exact. The squares of the lengths are sums of squares of components ((1.10)), so there are no roots in the numerator.
  3. The denominator of (1.17), \(L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)\), still contains the new length \(L_1\), which we compute with a square root. We would like to get rid of this root too. If the length of the fibre did not change, we would have \(L_1 = L_0\) and the denominator \(L_0\,(L_0 + L_0) = 2L_0^2\). We check how far the true denominator is from this value. From \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) we get \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\), so \(L_1 + L_0 = L_0\,(2 + \varepsilon)\), and the true denominator is \(L_0 \cdot L_0\,(2 + \varepsilon) = 2L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon/2)\). So it differs from \(2L_0^2\) only by the factor \(1 + \varepsilon/2\). For strains smaller than a thousandth in size, \(|\varepsilon| < 0{,}001\), this factor differs from \(1\) by less than half a thousandth. So if we replace the denominator \(L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)\) by \(2L_0^2\), we are wrong by less than half a thousandth of the calculated value:
    \[\varepsilon \approx \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_0^2} .\]
    So the strain is about half the relative change of the square of the length. Why half? The new length is \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\), so its square is \(L_1^2 = L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon)^2\). The square \((1 + \varepsilon)^2\) is just the product \((1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \varepsilon)\) written out: multiplying every term of the first bracket by every term of the second gives \(1 + \varepsilon + \varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\). So the relative change of the square of the length is \((L_1^2 - L_0^2)/L_0^2 = 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\). The last term is the product \(\varepsilon \cdot \varepsilon\), and for \(|\varepsilon| < 0{,}001\) it is smaller than a thousandth of \(\varepsilon\) in size, for example \(\varepsilon^2 = 0{,}000001\) for \(\varepsilon = 0{,}001\). Without it, the relative change of the square is \(2\varepsilon\), twice the strain, and the strain is half of it.
  4. The bottom end of each of our three fibres does not move, and the top end moves sideways by \(s\). So the vertical part of each fibre stays \(h\), and its horizontal part changes by \(s\).
  5. The vertical edge \(AD\) has no horizontal part before loading and gets the horizontal part \(s\). The square of its length grows from \(h^2\) to \(h^2 + s^2\), and step 3 gives \(\varepsilon \approx s^2/(2h^2)\). For our cube this is \(10^2/(2 \cdot 10\,000^2) = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\).
  6. The diagonal \(AC\) has the horizontal part \(h\), which grows to \(h + s\). The square of its length grows from \(h^2 + h^2 = 2h^2\) to \((h + s)^2 + h^2 = 2h^2 + 2hs + s^2\), so by \(2hs + s^2\). The part \(s^2\) is only \(s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\) times the part \(2hs\), so we leave it out, and \(\varepsilon \approx 2hs/(2 \cdot 2h^2) = s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\). The diagonal \(BD\) points the other way, its horizontal part shrinks from \(h\) to \(h - s\), the square of its length shrinks by about \(2hs\), and \(\varepsilon \approx -s/(2h) = -0{,}0005\).

Let us put the three fibres side by side.

fibre change of the square of the length strain
edge \(AD\) \(s^2\) \(\approx s^2/(2h^2) = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\)
diagonal \(AC\) \(2hs + s^2\) \(\approx s/(2h) = 5 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
diagonal \(BD\) \(-2hs + s^2\) \(\approx -s/(2h) = -5 \cdot 10^{-4}\)

The changes are made of two kinds of terms: terms with the shift \(s\) itself, namely \(\pm 2hs\), and terms with its square, \(s^2\). The term \(s^2\) is \(s/(2h) = 0{,}0005\) times the term \(2hs\), so it is 2000 times smaller. Where there is a term with \(s\), as for the diagonals, we may leave out the term \(s^2\), and we are wrong by less than a thousandth. Where there is no term with \(s\), as for the vertical edge, which only tilts, the term \(s^2\) is all that is left. That is why the change of the edge is a thousand times smaller than that of the diagonals and grows with the square of the shift: twice the shift, four times the strain. The term \(s^2\) that we left out and the small error of step 3 cause the small difference between \(5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in the table of the exactly computed strains.

We will use this rule all the time with small deformations. Next to a term with a small number itself, a term with its square is smaller roughly by the factor of that small number, as long as the numbers in front of the two terms are of similar size, as here. So we may leave it out.

Is the front face special? It is not. The difference of the displacements of two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of the cube is \(\big(0{,}001\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}),\ 0,\ 0\big)\). It depends only on how much higher \(Q\) lies than \(P\), not on where in the cube the two points are. By (1.15), two fibres with the same vector before loading therefore also have the same vector after loading, and the same strain, wherever in the cube they lie (figure 1.29). The whole cube is deformed in the same way at every point, and what we saw on the front face holds everywhere in it. Such a deformation is called a homogeneous deformation (homogena deformacija) (CM §3.3.2, PDF p. 122).

The cube has taught us two things. First, how the material is stretched close to a point is not described by a single number. Fibres through the same point are stretched in one direction, shortened in another, almost unchanged in a third, and the angles between them change too. Second, we can calculate all of this from the displacement field. For the cube the field was very clear, since the displacement was only horizontal and proportional to the height. In a general body, however, the displacement changes from point to point in a complicated way, as for the hanging bar, but now in three directions and depending on three coordinates. For the bar we helped ourselves with a piece that we shortened towards zero, and with the derivative. In space we will do the same. For this we will need derivatives of functions of three variables, and a way of writing that gives the strain of a fibre for every direction. We continue with them in the next section.
