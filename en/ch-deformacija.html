Take a rubber band and pull it by both ends. It gets longer, and we can see it. Now take a steel bar and pull it with the same force. It looks as if nothing has happened. Yet the steel bar has also become longer, only by so little that our eyes cannot notice it. This is true of every body: when a force acts on it, it changes its shape. When we step onto a bridge, the bridge bends a little. When we sit on a chair, the legs of the chair get a little shorter. When a motor drives a shaft, the shaft twists a little, one end more than the other.

These changes are mostly small, but they decide whether a structure holds. A bridge that bends a few centimetres under a truck is fine. A bridge that bends a metre is not. So we begin the book with the question of how to describe a change of shape with numbers. We will build the answer step by step, starting from the simplest case. Where we need new mathematics, we will stop and develop it completely, and then return to mechanics.

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar that we pull along its axis. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Is the elongation a good measure of how hard the bar is loaded? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end. The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves, so each half stretches as much as the short bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

What matters for the material is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.1 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language "deformation" means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is called the strain. Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Other books also call this number the relative elongation or the engineering strain, and often write it \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(x\) is the direction of the axis of the bar. Why two indices when there is only one direction? For a bar one would indeed be enough. In a body that is not a thin bar, however, we will describe the strain of the material around a point by several numbers, and each of them will be fixed by two directions. When the two directions are the same, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), the number measures the stretch in that direction. When they differ, as in \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\), the number describes how the right angle between the directions \(x\) and \(y\) changes. A deck of cards pushed sideways at the top leans over: the cards keep their length, but the right angles in the side face of the deck become slanted. So the index \(xx\) says that the number is a stretch in the direction \(x\) and not a change of angle. We will meet these numbers properly when we describe strain in space (SADD §2.2, PDF pp. 41-46).

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0{,}001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0{,}0005 ,\]

five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0{,}5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer. This bound follows from the data for steel in BOWER (Tables 3.1 and 3.9, PDF pp. 100 and 156). We will see how to compute it once we know how the force in a bar is related to its strain. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0{,}001\). This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

For a compressed bar \(\Delta L\) is negative, so the strain is negative too. A positive strain means lengthening, a negative one shortening.

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?

1.2 When a bar does not stretch the same everywhere

Hang a steel bar from the ceiling and let it hang. No force acts on it except its own weight. The top part of the bar has to carry the weight of the whole bar below it. The bottom end carries almost nothing. So we expect the top centimetres to stretch more than the bottom ones. If we measure the total elongation \(\Delta L\) and divide it by \(L\), we get some average, which does not tell us how the material is stretched at the top and how at the bottom. We want to know how the material is stretched close to each point of the bar, separately for every point.

How do we describe that? We follow the points of the bar. We label a point by its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, measured before the bar is hung. Books that distinguish the position before loading from the position after it often write this distance with a capital \(X\) and the new position with a small \(x\). The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), the bottom end at \(x = L\). When the bar hangs, every point moves down a little. How far the point that was at distance \(x\) moves, we write as \(u(x)\) and call the displacement (pomik) of that point. So the displacement is the difference between the new and the old position of the point. The top of the bar is fixed to the ceiling, so it does not move: \(u(0) = 0\). Points lower down move more, because the elongations of all the parts of the bar above them add up.

Now pick a short piece of the bar between the points \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). Its initial length is \(\Delta x\). The top end of the piece moves by \(u(x)\), the bottom end by \(u(x + \Delta x)\). If the bottom end moves more than the top end, the piece has become longer, by the difference of the two displacements:

\[\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) .\]

The piece is a small bar, so we can use the formula (1.1) for it. Its strain is the elongation \(\Delta u\) divided by the length \(\Delta x\):

\[\varepsilon \approx \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.2}\]

The sign \(\approx\) means "approximately equal". Even within the piece the strain changes a little from top to bottom, so this is still an average, only over a shorter part of the bar. The shorter the piece, the less the strain changes within it, and the better the number describes how the material is stretched close to the point \(x\). The point itself has no length, so it cannot be stretched. Only the material near it can, and the shorter the piece we take, the closer to the point we look.

Let us try this with numbers. In the chapter on the forces in a bar we will compute that a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight stretches so that the displacement of the point at distance \(x\) from the ceiling is

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2), \qquad a = 1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The constant \(a\) depends only on how heavy and how stiff steel is (BARBER (1.39) with the data for steel from BOWER, Table 3.1). We do not need to understand it now, we will only compute with it. The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), where \(u(0) = 0\), as it must be. The bottom end moves by \(u(10\ \text{m}) = a \cdot 100\ \text{m}^2 = 1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, two hundredths of a millimetre. A ten-metre steel bar stretches under its own weight by less than the thickness of a sheet of paper.

Let us compute the strain of the piece (1.2) at the top of the bar (\(x = 0\)) and at the middle (\(x = 5\) m) for shorter and shorter pieces:

length of the piece \(\Delta x\) at the top at the middle
1 m \(3{,}709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}757 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,1 m \(3{,}884 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}932 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,01 m \(3{,}902 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}950 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0,001 m \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\)

The numbers settle. At the top they approach about \(3{,}904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), at the middle about \(1{,}952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), exactly half. The material at the top is stretched twice as much as at the middle, which agrees with our expectation that the top part carries more weight. Why exactly half? Every cross section of the bar carries the weight of the part of the bar below it. The cross section at the top carries the whole bar, the one at the middle only the lower half, so half the weight. The bar is equally thick everywhere, and in steel the elongation of a short piece is proportional to the force that pulls it, as long as we do not stretch it beyond about \(0{,}001\), the value from section 1.1: half the force gives half the elongation. We will learn this when we connect the force in a bar with its strain, and the formula for \(u(x)\) above is derived from it (BARBER (1.39)). The same reasoning predicts more. The weight carried by a cross section decreases evenly from the top to the bottom, in proportion to the length of the bar below the cross section, and at the bottom end it is zero. The strain should change in the same way. We will confirm this by calculation in section 1.4.

So it seems that every point has its own strain, the number that the strains of shorter and shorter pieces approach. But what exactly does "approach" mean? A table cannot be the answer. It shows only a few rows, and from them we do not know for certain how the numbers behave further on. And we cannot shrink the piece to nothing, because in the formula (1.2) we would get \(0/0\), a division by zero. We need an exact meaning of the words "the number that the ratio approaches as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller and smaller", and a way to compute that number without tables. This is a job for mathematics, and the next section does it.

1.3 Mathematics: limit and derivative

In this section we forget the bar for a moment. We have a rule that assigns a number \(u(x)\) to every number \(x\), and we ask how fast \(u(x)\) changes as we change \(x\). The answer will be a new number. Once we can compute it, we return to the bar.

When computing we use the school rules for real numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero number, and working with inequalities. The absolute value \(|a|\) of a number \(a\) is the distance of the number from zero: \(|3| = 3\), \(|-3| = 3\). So the distance between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(|a - b|\).

1.3.1 Function

Definition 1.2 Function (funkcija)

A function (funkcija) \(f\) is a rule that assigns to every number \(x\) from some set of numbers exactly one number \(f(x)\). The set of numbers \(x\) for which the rule is given is the domain (definicijsko območje) of the function, and the number \(f(x)\) is the value of the function at \(x\).

Source: LEBL1 §0.3 (PDF pp. 18-19), EP §6.2.

The displacement \(u(x)\) is a function. For the hanging bar its domain is the set of all \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\), that is, of all points of the bar, and the value \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the point \(x\). Why does the definition ask for exactly one number? So that the symbol \(f(x)\) means one definite number that we can calculate with. In the next section we will calculate the difference \(u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). These are the displacements of two different points, \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\), and the difference makes sense only because each of the two symbols is one number. A rule that assigned to the number \(4\) both numbers whose square is \(4\), that is, \(2\) and \(-2\), would not be a function: the symbol \(f(4)\) would not say which number is meant, and we could not calculate with it. For the displacement the requirement holds by itself, because after loading every point of the bar is in one place only.

1.3.2 Ratio of changes

The ratio we computed in the table has a general form. For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a small change \(\Delta x \neq 0\),

\[\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \tag{1.3}\]

is the change of the value divided by the change of the argument. It tells us by how much \(f\) changed on average per unit change of \(x\). We call it the difference quotient (diferenčni količnik). For the bar it is the strain of the piece. For a car journey, where \(f(t)\) is the distance covered up to the time \(t\), it is the average speed over the time \(\Delta t\).

For \(\Delta x = 0\) the quotient is not defined, because we would divide by zero. So we treat it as a function of the change \(\Delta x\), defined for all small \(\Delta x\) except \(\Delta x = 0\). We ask what it approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero.

1.3.3 Limit

What does it mean that some function \(g(h)\) approaches a number \(d\) as \(h\) approaches zero? It means that \(g(h)\) is as close to \(d\) as we wish, provided \(h\) is close enough to zero. Set an allowed error, say \(0{,}001\). Is there a band around zero so narrow that for every \(h\) in the band, except \(h = 0\), the value \(g(h)\) differs from \(d\) by less than \(0{,}001\)? If there is one for the error \(0{,}001\), for the error \(0{,}000001\), and for every other error we can think of, then \(g(h)\) really approaches \(d\). For a smaller error the band may be narrower, but it must exist.

We write this exactly. We call the allowed error \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta) and the width of the band around zero \(\delta\) (delta). In mathematics books the allowed error is usually written \(\varepsilon\). We keep \(\varepsilon\) for strain.

Definition 1.3 Limit of a function (limita funkcije)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit (limita) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d , \tag{1.4}\]

if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3 (PDF p. 114), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < |h|\) leaves out the point \(h = 0\), where \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient. The limit tells what \(g\) approaches, not what it is at the point itself. The number \(r\) makes sure that the points the condition talks about really are in the domain.

Let us try the definition on the simplest example. Let \(g(h) = 5 - h\). We expect \(g(h)\) to approach \(5\). Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The difference is \(|g(h) - 5| = |{-h}| = |h|\). If we take \(\delta = \eta\), then for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) the difference is \(|h| < \eta\). So for every error we have found a band, and the limit is \(5\). For the error \(0{,}001\) the band is \(0{,}001\) wide on each side, for the error \(0{,}000001\) only \(0{,}000001\).

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Otherwise the word "limit" could mean two different numbers. Let us check that this is so.

The idea is simple. If \(g(h)\) approached two numbers at once, then for small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) would be close to both, and so the two numbers would be close to each other. But only equal numbers can be closer to each other than every error.

Lemma 1.4 Uniqueness of the limit (enoličnost limite)

A function has at most one limit at the point \(0\).

Proof

We have to show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) that both satisfy the definition of the limit are equal.

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \(d_1\) is a limit, the definition gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a band \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We take half the error because in step 6 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \(d_2\) is a limit, for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a band \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\).
  4. Take an \(h\) that is nonzero and smaller than both bands, for example half of the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\). For this \(h\) both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold.
  5. Write the difference \(d_1 - d_2\) through the value \(g(h)\), which we know: \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - g(h)) + (g(h) - d_2)\).
  6. The distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) is not larger than the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\) plus the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). This is the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\). It holds because every number lies between its absolute value and the negative of it, \(-|a| \le a \le |a|\) and \(-|b| \le b \le |b|\). Adding the inequalities gives \(-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|\), and a number that lies between \(-c\) and \(c\) has absolute value at most \(c\). With \(a = d_1 - g(h)\) and \(b = g(h) - d_2\) we therefore get
\[|d_1 - d_2| \le |d_1 - g(h)| + |g(h) - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  1. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2|\) is smaller than every positive number.
  2. A nonnegative number that is smaller than every positive number is zero: if it were positive, it would be smaller than itself. So \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\) and \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.1.4 (PDF pp. 114-115). The argument for the triangle inequality in step 6 is not from the source.

The proof showed a pattern that we will meet many times: when we want a sum of two errors to be small, we allow each of them half.

1.3.4 Derivative

Now we can say what the number is that the strain of the piece approached in the table.

Definition 1.5 Derivative (odvod)

A function \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(x\) if the difference quotient (1.3) has a limit as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. This limit is the derivative (odvod) of the function \(f\) at the point \(x\):

\[f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.5}\]

Source: LEBL1 Def. 4.1.1 (PDF p. 155). The notation with \(\Delta x\) follows BARBER (1.35)-(1.36), where it is \(\delta x\).

So the derivative is a rate of change: by how much \(f\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when the change is very small. We read \(f'(x)\) as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)". In books you will also find the derivative written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}\). This notation recalls the quotient \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) from which the derivative comes.

Let us compute the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a\) and \(L\) are given numbers. The way has two parts. First we simplify the difference quotient so that \(\Delta x\), which we must not set to zero, disappears from the denominator. Then we find the limit of the simplified expression from the definition.

  1. The value at \(x + \Delta x\): \(u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2)\).
  2. Expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). So
\[u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x - x^2 - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  1. Subtract \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
\[u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) = a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  1. All three terms in the bracket have the factor \(\Delta x\), so we take it out: \(a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  2. Divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]

This holds for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\). At \(\Delta x = 0\) the left side would be \(0/0\), but the right side is a perfectly good number. Now we see why the numbers in the table settled: the quotient differs from the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) by exactly \(a\,\Delta x\). 6. Let us show from the definition of the limit that the limit of the quotient is \(d = a\,(2L - 2x)\). Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The difference between the quotient and \(d\) is \(|a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) - a\,(2L - 2x)| = |{-a\,\Delta x}| = a\,|\Delta x|\), since \(a > 0\). 7. We want \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\), which holds exactly when \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\). So we take the band \(\delta = \eta / a\). For every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\) the difference is smaller than \(\eta\). 8. We have found a band for every error, so the limit of the quotient is \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and

\[u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

Step 5 was the heart of the computation. With patient algebra we turned the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and after that the limit was easy. We will compute derivatives this way later as well, until we derive rules that do this work once and for all.

1.4 Strain at a point

Let us return to the bar. The strain of the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is the difference quotient of the displacement, and as the length of the piece goes to zero it approaches the derivative of the displacement. This derivative is the measure we were looking for.

Definition 1.6 Strain at a point (deformacija v točki)

Let \(u(x)\) be the displacement of the point of the bar that was at distance \(x\) from a chosen end before loading. The strain (deformacija) of the bar at the point \(x\) is the derivative of the displacement:

\[\varepsilon(x) = u'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.6}\]

Source: BARBER (1.35), (1.36) (PDF p. 33), ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF p. 66). In books it is often written \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) or \(e_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the displacement in the direction \(x\) (why two indices, see section 1.1). The sign \(\partial\) writes the derivative when the displacement depends not only on one distance \(x\) but on the position of the point in space. We will meet it when we describe strain in space.

So the strain at a point is, like the strain of a bar (1.1), a number without a unit, an elongation per unit length. The difference is that we do not compute it for the whole bar, but for shorter and shorter pieces around the point, and take the number that their strains approach. So the name strain at a point is a shorthand. It does not mean that the point itself stretches, since it has no length. It describes the material close to the point, in pieces that can be as short as we like. For a bar that stretches the same everywhere, the two strains are equal. Indeed, if the displacement is \(u(x) = \varepsilon_0\,x\) with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\), the difference quotient is \(\varepsilon_0\,\Delta x / \Delta x = \varepsilon_0\) for every \(\Delta x\), its limit is \(\varepsilon_0\), and the total elongation is \(u(L) - u(0) = \varepsilon_0 L\), so \(\Delta L / L = \varepsilon_0\).

For the hanging bar we have already computed the derivative:

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

At the top, at \(x = 0\), the strain is \(2aL = 2 \cdot 1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 10 = 3{,}90 \cdot 10^{-6}\). At the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the bottom end zero. These are exactly the numbers the columns of the table approached. The strain falls evenly from top to bottom, just as the weight carried by each cross section falls: the top carries the whole bar, the middle half of it, the bottom end nothing.

The total elongation of the bar is the displacement of the bottom end, \(\Delta L = u(L) - u(0) = aL^2 = 1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and the average strain is \(\Delta L / L = aL = 1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-6}\). This is exactly the strain at the middle. When the strain changes evenly along the bar, the average really is the value at the middle. But the most stretched material, at the top, is stretched twice as much as the average. If we judged the strength of the bar by the average, we would be wrong by a factor of two.

One more simple but important observation. Suppose the ceiling sags by 1 mm for some reason. Then every point of the bar moves by an extra millimetre, and the new displacement is \(u(x) + 0{,}001\) m. The strain does not change. In the difference quotient the extra millimetre cancels, \((u(x + \Delta x) + 0{,}001) - (u(x) + 0{,}001) = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). A displacement that is the same for all points does not deform the body, it only moves it as a whole. Such a displacement is called a rigid-body displacement (togi premik), because it is the only displacement a perfectly rigid body can undergo (BARBER p. 32). So we can never compute the displacement completely from the strain: we can always add a rigid-body displacement. For the hanging bar it is fixed by the condition that the top is attached to the ceiling, \(u(0) = 0\) (BARBER (1.39)).

So far the bar has been just a line: every point moved along the same straight line, and the displacement was a single number. A real body has three dimensions. A point in it can move in any direction, and a small piece of the body can at the same time stretch in one direction, shorten in another, and twist. One number is not enough to describe displacements in space. We need quantities that have a size and a direction, and we start the next section with them.

1.5 Mathematics: points and vectors

Once again we put mechanics aside for a moment. To describe displacements in space we need two things. First we must say with numbers where a point is. Then we must say with numbers where and how far it has moved. We will build both from an experience everyone has, measuring a room with a tape measure.

1.5.1 Coordinates

How do we tell someone over the phone where a lamp hangs in a room? We can say: start in the corner of the room, walk 2 m along one wall, then 3 m along the other wall, and then 2 m up. Three numbers, \(2\), \(3\) and \(2\), fix the position of the lamp exactly, provided we have agreed on three things beforehand: at which point we start, in which three directions we walk, and in what order we list the numbers. We choose the directions perpendicular to each other, like the edges where the floor and the two walls meet in the corner of the room. Such an agreement has a name of its own.

Definition 1.7 Coordinate system and coordinates (koordinatni sistem in koordinate)

A coordinate system (koordinatni sistem) consists of a chosen point \(O\), called the origin (izhodišče), and three mutually perpendicular straight lines through \(O\), the coordinate axes (koordinatne osi) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). On each axis a positive direction is chosen, and lengths are measured in the same unit on all three axes. The position of a point \(P\) is described by three numbers, its coordinates (koordinate) \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\): we reach \(P\) if, starting at the origin, we walk the distance \(x\) in the direction of the axis \(x\), then the distance \(y\) in the direction of the axis \(y\), and then the distance \(z\) in the direction of the axis \(z\). A negative number means walking in the opposite direction. We write \(P = (x, y, z)\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 167 (PDF p. 382).

So coordinates are numbers, and the coordinate system is the agreement that gives these numbers their meaning. The lamp of our example is the point \((2, 3, 2)\) if the axes \(x\) and \(y\) lie along the walls, the axis \(z\) points vertically up and the units are metres. The order matters: the point \((3, 2, 2)\) is a different point, 3 m along the first wall and 2 m along the second. That is why we say that coordinates are an ordered triple (urejena trojica) of numbers. It matters which number stands in which place, because each place belongs to its own axis.

In another coordinate system the same lamp has other coordinates. If we start in the opposite corner of the room, all three numbers change, while the lamp stays where it is. So coordinates always belong to a point together with a coordinate system. In each problem we will choose the system once and keep it. Other books often write coordinates as \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\) or, for the position before loading, \(X_1\), \(X_2\), \(X_3\) (CM (3.3.14)), because with numbered axes the calculations that repeat for all three directions are easier to write. As long as we can, we will stay with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

1.5.2 Vector

Now let a point move. A fly sits on the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\). It flies off and lands on the floor at the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\). How do we say with numbers where it has moved? Let us compare the coordinates. The coordinate \(x\) has increased by \(1\), the coordinate \(y\) by \(2\), and the coordinate \(z\) has decreased by \(2\). The triple of changes \((1, 2, -2)\) tells where the fly ended its flight relative to where it began: 1 m further in the direction of the axis \(x\), 2 m further in the direction of the axis \(y\) and 2 m lower. It does not tell where the fly started. A fly that flew from the point \((0, 0, 2)\) to the point \((1, 2, 0)\) would have the same triple of changes, although it flew somewhere else. Nor does the triple tell along which path the fly flew, only where it ended. Such a triple is a new kind of object. It does not describe a position but a move.

Definition 1.8 Vector (vektor)

A vector (vektor) \(\vek a\) is an ordered triple of real numbers, \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\). The numbers \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(a_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the components (komponente) of the vector. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components. The vector \(\vek 0 = (0, 0, 0)\) is the zero vector (ničelni vektor).

The vector from the point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) to the point \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\) has the differences of the coordinates as its components:

\[\overrightarrow{PQ} = (x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}},\ y_{\mathrm{Q}} - y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}) . \tag{1.7}\]

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (1) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

For the fly, \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (3 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (1, 2, -2)\). So a vector, like a point, is an ordered triple of numbers, but it means something else. A point tells where something is. A vector tells how to get from one point to another. Bold type, \(\vek a\), distinguishes vectors from numbers. On paper we write a vector with an arrow over the letter, \(\vec a\).

We draw a vector as an arrow from the point \(P\) to the point \(Q\). The same vector can be drawn from any starting point, just as both flies in the example above had the same vector: the two arrows start at different places, but both point 1 m in the direction \(x\), 2 m in the direction \(y\) and 2 m down. The vector from the origin \(O = (0, 0, 0)\) to the point \(P\) has the coordinates of \(P\) as its components, because the coordinates of the origin are zero. Books call it the position vector (krajevni vektor) of the point \(P\).

The components of a vector depend on the coordinate system, just like the coordinates of points. Other books also write the components as \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(a_3\) (CM) or \([a_1, a_2, a_3]\) (KREYSZIG (1)).

1.5.3 Calculating with vectors

On the floor the fly crawls a little further, 2 m in the direction of the axis \(x\), that is, by the vector \((2, 0, 0)\). Where is it now? Each coordinate changes by the corresponding component, and from \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) it gets to the point \((5, 5, 0)\). From the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) to there the vector is \((5 - 2,\ 5 - 3,\ 0 - 2) = (3, 2, -2)\). We get the same vector if we add the components of the two parts of the path: \(1 + 2 = 3\), \(2 + 0 = 2\) and \(-2 + 0 = -2\). So two moves one after the other have the same effect as a single move whose components are the sums of the components. This is the model for adding vectors. The difference and the multiple are defined in the same way.

Definition 1.9 Sum, difference and multiple of vectors (vsota, razlika in večkratnik vektorjev)

For vectors \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) and \(\vek b = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) and a number \(k\), the sum (vsota), the difference (razlika) and the multiple (večkratnik) are the vectors

\[\begin{aligned} \vek a + \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} + b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} + b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ \vek a - \vek b &= (a_{\mathrm{x}} - b_{\mathrm{x}},\ a_{\mathrm{y}} - b_{\mathrm{y}},\ a_{\mathrm{z}} - b_{\mathrm{z}}) , \\ k\,\vek a &= (k\,a_{\mathrm{x}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{y}},\ k\,a_{\mathrm{z}}) . \end{aligned} \tag{1.8}\]

A vector is added to a point in the same way: for a point \(P = (x, y, z)\), \(P + \vek a = (x + a_{\mathrm{x}},\ y + a_{\mathrm{y}},\ z + a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the point we reach from \(P\) by the move \(\vek a\). The difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) of (1.7).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (3), (5) (PDF pp. 383-384), CM §2.2.1.1 and §2.2.1.2 (PDF p. 40).

All these operations are calculated component by component: the component \(x\) of the result is obtained from the components \(x\) only, and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\). In a picture we draw the sum by attaching the arrow of the vector \(\vek b\) to the tip of the arrow of the vector \(\vek a\). The sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second. If we draw both arrows from the same point, the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram that the two arrows span (KREYSZIG §9.1, Fig. 172, PDF p. 383).

The multiple \(2\,\vek a\) is the move we get if we make the move \(\vek a\) twice in a row, since component by component \(\vek a + \vek a = 2\,\vek a\). The multiple \((-1)\,\vek a\) undoes the move \(\vek a\), since \(\vek a + (-1)\,\vek a = \vek 0\). It is the move back. The difference \(\vek a - \vek b\) is the vector we must add to the vector \(\vek b\) to get \(\vek a\), and the difference of points \(Q - P\) is the vector we must add to the point \(P\) to reach the point \(Q\).

We will calculate with vectors as we do with numbers: move the terms of a sum around, shift brackets and factor out numbers. Are we allowed to? The operations are defined component by component, and the components are numbers, for which we know these rules. So we expect the rules to hold for vectors too. Let us check.

Lemma 1.10 Rules of vector arithmetic (računska pravila za vektorje)

For all vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and all numbers \(k\), \(m\):

  1. \(\vek a + \vek b = \vek b + \vek a\),
  2. \((\vek a + \vek b) + \vek c = \vek a + (\vek b + \vek c)\),
  3. \(\vek a + \vek 0 = \vek a\) and \(\vek a - \vek a = \vek 0\),
  4. \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b) = k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) and \((k + m)\,\vek a = k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\).
Proof

We must show that for each rule the vectors on the left and on the right are equal.

  1. Two vectors are equal if they agree in all three components (definition 1.8). So we check each rule separately for the components \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).
  2. All operations in the rules are defined component by component ((1.8)), so the component \(x\) of each side is calculated only from the components \(x\) of the vectors \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\), \(\vek c\) and from the numbers \(k\), \(m\).
  3. Rule 1. The component \(x\) of the left side is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(b_{\mathrm{x}} + a_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal, because the order of adding numbers does not change the sum.
  4. Rule 2. The component \(x\) of the left side is \((a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}}) + c_{\mathrm{x}}\), of the right side \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + (b_{\mathrm{x}} + c_{\mathrm{x}})\). The two numbers are equal, because when adding three numbers it does not matter which two we add first.
  5. Rule 3. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a + \vek 0\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} + 0 = a_{\mathrm{x}}\), which is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a\). The component \(x\) of the vector \(\vek a - \vek a\) is \(a_{\mathrm{x}} - a_{\mathrm{x}} = 0\), which is the component \(x\) of the zero vector.
  6. Rule 4. The component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,(\vek a + \vek b)\) is \(k\,(a_{\mathrm{x}} + b_{\mathrm{x}})\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + k\,\vek b\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + k\,b_{\mathrm{x}}\). The two numbers are equal by the rule for multiplying a sum by a number. The component \(x\) of the vector \((k + m)\,\vek a\) is \((k + m)\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the component \(x\) of the vector \(k\,\vek a + m\,\vek a\) is \(k\,a_{\mathrm{x}} + m\,a_{\mathrm{x}}\). These two numbers are equal by the same rule.
  7. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same, with \(x\) replaced everywhere by \(y\) or \(z\).
  8. The left and the right side of each rule agree in all three components, so by step 1 they are equal.

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (4), (6) (PDF p. 384), CM §2.2.1.1 (PDF p. 40).

The proof has shown a principle we will use all the time: we check an equality of two vectors by checking three equalities of numbers, one for each component. Every rule for adding, subtracting and multiplying by a number that we will need will be checked in this way.

1.5.4 Length of a vector

How far did the fly travel when it flew from the lamp to the floor by the vector \((1, 2, -2)\)? Not \(1 + 2 + 2 = 5\) m, because that would be the path along the axes: first 1 m in the direction \(x\), then 2 m in the direction \(y\) and then 2 m down. But the fly flies straight. We work out the distance in two steps.

First we look only at the floor. The point on the floor directly below the fly moves \(1\) m in the direction \(x\) and \(2\) m in the direction \(y\). These two paths are the legs of a right triangle, since the axes are perpendicular, and the straight path of the point on the floor is its hypotenuse. By the Pythagorean theorem from school, the square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the squares of the legs, \(1^2 + 2^2 = 5\), so the point on the floor moves \(\sqrt 5\) m.

Then we add the height. The fly has come down \(2\) m vertically, and the vertical is perpendicular to every direction on the floor. So the straight path of the point on the floor and the vertical descent are the legs of a second right triangle, whose hypotenuse is the path of the fly. The square of the fly's path is \(5 + 2^2 = 9\), and the path itself is \(\sqrt 9 = 3\) m.

The two steps together say that the square of the distance is the sum of the squares of all three components, \(1^2 + 2^2 + (-2)^2 = 9\). The sign of a component makes no difference, because the square of a negative number is positive.

Definition 1.11 Length of a vector and distance (dolžina vektorja in razdalja)

The length (dolžina) or magnitude (velikost) of the vector \(\vek a = (a_{\mathrm{x}}, a_{\mathrm{y}}, a_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the number

\[|\vek a| = \sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{y}}^2 + a_{\mathrm{z}}^2} . \tag{1.9}\]

The distance (razdalja) between the points \(P\) and \(Q\) is the length of the vector between them, \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|\).

Source: KREYSZIG §9.1 (2) (PDF p. 382), CM §2.2.1 (PDF p. 39).

So the length is a number that is never negative, and it has the same unit as the components. We do not prove the Pythagorean theorem in this book. We measure coordinates with the same tape along three mutually perpendicular axes, and for such coordinates school geometry gives us the formula (1.9), as we saw with the fly. From now on this formula is what the word length means, and everything we claim about lengths we will derive from it.

For a vector with a single nonzero component, for example \((a_{\mathrm{x}}, 0, 0)\), the length is \(\sqrt{a_{\mathrm{x}}^2}\), the nonnegative number whose square is \(a_{\mathrm{x}}^2\). This is exactly the absolute value \(|a_{\mathrm{x}}|\), the distance of the number \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) from zero from section 1.3. That is why we use the same sign for the length of a vector as for the absolute value. The length of a vector measures distances in space as the absolute value measures them on the number line.

The distance between the lamp \(P = (2, 3, 2)\) and the point \(Q = (3, 5, 0)\) on the floor is the length of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = (1, 2, -2)\), that is, \(3\) m. This is exactly what we will need in the next section: the distance between two points of a body before and after loading.

1.6 Displacement in space

Let us return to the body. Under loading every point of the body moves from its old position to a new one. For the bar this move was a single number, now it is a vector.

Definition 1.12 Displacement vector (vektor pomika)

If a point of a body moves under loading from the position \(P\) to a new position, its displacement vector (vektor pomika) \(\vek u = (u_{\mathrm{x}}, u_{\mathrm{y}}, u_{\mathrm{z}})\) is the vector from the old position to the new one. So the new position is \(P + \vek u\). The components \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) say how far the point moves in the direction of the axis \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\).

Source: CM (3.2.7) (PDF p. 117), BARBER (1.32) (PDF pp. 31-32), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

For the bar the displacement was the number \(u(x)\). All points moved along the axis of the bar. If we lay the axis \(x\) along the bar, the displacement of a point of the bar is the vector \((u(x), 0, 0)\): the displacement of the previous sections is the component \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), and the other two components are zero, because we looked only at moves along the axis. Books also write the components of the displacement as \(u_1\), \(u_2\), \(u_3\) (CM) or \(u\), \(v\), \(w\) (SADD §2.1, PDF p. 39).

Every point of the body has its own displacement vector, and different points move differently. For the hanging bar the displacement depended on the position \(x\). In a body it depends on the position of the point, that is, on three coordinates.

Definition 1.13 Displacement field (polje pomikov)

The displacement field (polje pomikov) of a body is the rule that assigns to every point \((x, y, z)\) of the body, taken in its position before loading, its displacement vector

\[\vek u(x, y, z) = \big(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z),\ u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z)\big) . \tag{1.10}\]

Source: CM (3.2.8) (PDF p. 117), SADD §2.1 (PDF p. 39).

The displacement field is a rule of the same kind as a function of definition 1.2, except that it has three numbers at the input and three at the output. At the input are the coordinates of the point before loading, at the output the components of its displacement. Each component on its own, for example \(u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z)\), is a rule that assigns one number to three numbers. The numbers at the input are called variables (spremenljivke), so such a rule is called a function of three variables (funkcija treh spremenljivk). The word field comes from a picture: if we draw at every point of the body the arrow of its displacement, the body is covered with arrows like a field with blades of grass.

How do we tell from the displacement field whether the body is deformed? If the displacement is the same vector at all points, all points move equally, and the body only moves as a whole. This is the rigid-body displacement of section 1.4, now in space. But the body can also be rotated as a whole without being deformed. Then the points move differently, while the distances between them stay the same, as in every rotation of a rigid object. So displacements that differ from point to point do not yet mean deformation. Deformation is a change of the distances between points, and that is why we will measure it with lengths.

1.6.1 Fibres

For the bar we looked at a small piece between two points. In a body we do the same, except that the piece can lie in any direction.

Definition 1.14 Material fibre (vlakno)

A material fibre (vlakno) is a straight line between two points \(P\) and \(Q\) of a body, which we imagine drawn into the material so that its points move together with the material. Before loading the fibre is described by the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\).

Source: CM §3.3.1 (PDF pp. 118-119, material line element), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42, fibers).

A fibre is like a very thin bar inside the body. So we calculate its strain as the strain of a bar (1.1): the elongation divided by the length before loading. The length before loading is \(L_0 = |\overrightarrow{PQ}|\), the length after loading \(L_1\) is the distance between the new positions of the ends, and the elongation is \(L_1 - L_0\). After loading a fibre is in general no longer perfectly straight. For a short fibre the curvature is small, so we take the distance between the new positions of the ends as the new length. How accurate this is we will see when we shorten the fibre towards zero, as we did with the piece of the bar. For the cube below the fibres stay straight, and there it is exact.

Where does the fibre go? The end \(P\) moves by \(\vek u(P)\), which means the displacement vector at the point \(P\), and the end \(Q\) by \(\vek u(Q)\). The new positions of the ends are \(P_1 = P + \vek u(P)\) and \(Q_1 = Q + \vek u(Q)\). The index \(1\), as in \(L_1\), means the state after loading. We calculate the vector of the fibre after loading, \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\), component by component, the way we checked the rules of vector arithmetic in the proof of lemma 1.10. Let \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) and \(Q = (x_{\mathrm{Q}}, y_{\mathrm{Q}}, z_{\mathrm{Q}})\).

  1. The component \(x\) of the point \(Q_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)\), and that of the point \(P_1\) is \(x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P)\), because the sum of a point and a vector is calculated component by component, like the sum of vectors (definition 1.9).
  2. The component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1}\) is the difference of these two coordinates ((1.7)), \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q)) - (x_{\mathrm{P}} + u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\).
  3. We remove the brackets and rearrange the terms by the rules for numbers: \((x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) + (u_{\mathrm{x}}(Q) - u_{\mathrm{x}}(P))\). The first bracket is the component \(x\) of the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) ((1.7)), the second the component \(x\) of the difference \(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\) ((1.8)).
  4. For the components \(y\) and \(z\) the calculation is the same. So the two vectors agree in all three components, and by definition 1.8 that is what equality of vectors means, so
\[\overrightarrow{P_1 Q_1} = \overrightarrow{PQ} + \big(\vek u(Q) - \vek u(P)\big) . \tag{1.11}\]

So a fibre changes only by the difference of the displacements of its ends. This is the same difference as \(\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\) for the piece of the bar, except that it is now a vector. If the displacement is the same at all points, the difference is the zero vector, and every fibre stays as it was.

1.6.2 A sheared cube

Let us try all this with numbers. We glue a steel cube with an edge of 10 mm to a table by its bottom face and push the top face sideways, so that it moves by 10 µm. A micrometre, µm, is a thousandth of a millimetre. The cube leans over like the deck of cards of section 1.1: every horizontal layer slides relative to the layer below it, and the layer at height \(z\) moves in proportion to that height. Books call such a deformation simple shear (enostavni strig) (CM (3.3.14), PDF p. 123). The word shear describes exactly this, layers sliding past each other like the blades of scissors.

We place the coordinate system in the bottom front left corner of the cube: the axis \(x\) points in the direction of the push, the axis \(y\) along the other bottom edge, and the axis \(z\) vertically up. We measure lengths in micrometres, so the edge of the cube is \(10\,000\) µm. The top face at height \(10\,000\) µm moves by \(10\) µm, and the layer at height \(z\) by a thousandth of its height. So the displacement field is

\[u_{\mathrm{x}}(x, y, z) = 0{,}001\,z , \qquad u_{\mathrm{y}}(x, y, z) = 0 , \qquad u_{\mathrm{z}}(x, y, z) = 0 . \tag{1.12}\]

The bottom face, where \(z = 0\), does not move, as it must not, since it is glued down.

Let us look at the front face of the cube, where \(y = 0\), with the corners \(A = (0, 0, 0)\), \(B = (10\,000, 0, 0)\), \(C = (10\,000, 0, 10\,000)\) and \(D = (0, 0, 10\,000)\). The bottom corners do not move, \(\vek u(A) = \vek u(B) = \vek 0\). The top ones move by \(\vek u(C) = \vek u(D) = (10, 0, 0)\). Take four fibres: the bottom edge \(AB\), the vertical edge \(AD\), and the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\). For each we calculate the vector after loading by (1.11), both lengths by (1.9) and the strain by (1.1).

Let us show the calculation for the vertical edge \(AD\). Before loading \(\overrightarrow{AD} = (0, 0, 10\,000)\), with length \(L_0 = 10\,000\) µm. The difference of the displacements of the ends is \(\vek u(D) - \vek u(A) = (10, 0, 0)\), so the vector after loading is \((0, 0, 10\,000) + (10, 0, 0) = (10, 0, 10\,000)\). Its length is

\[L_1 = \sqrt{10^2 + 0^2 + 10\,000^2} = \sqrt{100\,000\,100} = 10\,000{,}005\ \text{µm} ,\]

and the strain is \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0 = 0{,}005/10\,000 = 5 \cdot 10^{-7}\). The other fibres are calculated in the same way. Before loading both diagonals are \(\sqrt{2 \cdot 10\,000^2} = 14\,142{,}136\) µm long, after loading \(AC\) is \(14\,149{,}208\) µm long and \(BD\) is \(14\,135{,}066\) µm long.

The table tells us something we could not see with the bar. The diagonal \(AC\) has become longer by five ten-thousandths, and the diagonal \(BD\) has become shorter by almost exactly as much. The same material and the same loading, and yet the answer to the question of how much the material is stretched depends on the direction in which we measure it. The front face, which was a square, has become a leaning four-sided figure with parallel opposite sides, a parallelogram, with one diagonal longer and the other shorter.

The edges, however, have hardly changed. The bottom edge has stayed the same, and the vertical one has become longer by \(5 \cdot 10^{-7}\), a thousand times less than the change of the diagonals. Above all, the vertical edge has tilted, since its top has moved 10 µm sideways. So the right angle between the edges \(AB\) and \(AD\) at the corner \(A\) has become a little smaller. This is the change of angle that we announced with the indices of \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}\) in section 1.1, here between the directions \(x\) and \(z\).

What happens if we push the top face twice as far, by 20 µm? The displacement field is then \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = 0{,}002\,z\), and when we repeat the calculation, the diagonals get the strains \(+1{,}0005 \cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(-0{,}9995 \cdot 10^{-3}\), twice as much as before. The vertical edge, however, gets \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\), four times as much as before. The strain of the diagonals grows in proportion to the shift of the top face, and the strain of the vertical edge with the square of the shift.

These numbers show three patterns that need an explanation. The two diagonals change by almost exactly the same amount, only with opposite signs. This change is half the ratio between the shift of the top face and the height of the cube, \(0{,}0005\) for the ratio \(0{,}001\). And the vertical edge grows with the square of the shift. Where does this come from? We repeat the calculation with letters. We call the edge of the cube \(h\) and the ratio between the shift of a layer and its height \(k\), so that \(u_{\mathrm{x}} = k\,z\). For our cube \(h = 10\,000\) µm and \(k = 0{,}001\).

What gets in the way of the calculation is the square root in the lengths. We get rid of it with one move that we will need again.

  1. We multiply and divide the strain \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) by \(L_1 + L_0\). We may, because \(L_1 + L_0 > 0\), and the value does not change.
  2. The numerator becomes \((L_1 - L_0)(L_1 + L_0)\). When we multiply out the brackets, the terms \(L_1 L_0\) and \(-L_0 L_1\) cancel, and \(L_1^2 - L_0^2\) remains. So
\[\varepsilon = \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{L_0\,(L_1 + L_0)} . \tag{1.13}\]

This equality is exact. The squares of the lengths are sums of squares of components ((1.9)), so there are no roots in the numerator. 3. We compare the denominator with \(2L_0^2\). From \(\varepsilon = (L_1 - L_0)/L_0\) we get \(L_1 = L_0\,(1 + \varepsilon)\), so \(L_1 + L_0 = L_0\,(2 + \varepsilon)\), and the denominator is \(L_0^2\,(2 + \varepsilon) = 2L_0^2\,(1 + \varepsilon/2)\). For strains smaller than a thousandth, the factor \(1 + \varepsilon/2\) differs from \(1\) by less than half a thousandth. So if we replace the denominator by \(2L_0^2\), we are wrong by less than half a thousandth of the calculated value:

\[\varepsilon \approx \frac{L_1^2 - L_0^2}{2L_0^2} .\]

So the strain is about half the relative change of the square of the length. The half is there because the square of a length changes about twice as much as the length itself: \((1 + \varepsilon)^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\), and \(\varepsilon^2\) is negligible for small \(\varepsilon\). 4. The vertical edge \(AD\) has the vector \((0, 0, h)\) before loading and \((kh, 0, h)\) after. The squares of the lengths are \(L_0^2 = h^2\) and \(L_1^2 = k^2h^2 + h^2\), the difference is \(k^2h^2\), and the strain is about \(k^2h^2/(2h^2) = k^2/2\). 5. The diagonal \(AC\) has the vector \((h, 0, h)\) before loading and \((h + kh, 0, h)\) after. The squares are \(L_0^2 = 2h^2\) and \(L_1^2 = (h + kh)^2 + h^2 = 2h^2 + 2kh^2 + k^2h^2\), the difference is \(2kh^2 + k^2h^2\), and the strain is about \((2kh^2 + k^2h^2)/(4h^2) = k/2 + k^2/4\). 6. The diagonal \(BD\) has the vector \((-h, 0, h)\) before loading and \((-h + kh, 0, h)\) after. The squares are \(L_0^2 = 2h^2\) and \(L_1^2 = (-h + kh)^2 + h^2 = 2h^2 - 2kh^2 + k^2h^2\), the difference is \(-2kh^2 + k^2h^2\), and the strain is about \(-k/2 + k^2/4\).

Now all three patterns are explained. For the diagonals the term \(k/2\) with its sign dominates. The horizontal component of the diagonal \(AC\) points in the direction of the shift, so its size grows from \(h\) to \(h + kh\). The horizontal component of the diagonal \(BD\) points against the shift, so its size shrinks from \(h\) to \(h - kh\). In both cases the square of the length changes by \(2kh^2\), once up and once down, and this gives the equal but opposite strains. The vertical edge has no horizontal component before loading. The shift gives it the horizontal component \(kh\), which appears in the square of the length only as \(k^2h^2\), and that is why its strain grows with the square of the shift. The ratio between the strains of the edge and of a diagonal is \((k^2/2)/(k/2) = k\), which for \(k = 0{,}001\) is the factor of a thousand we saw in the table. The terms \(k^2/4\) and the error of step 3 are more than a thousand times smaller than \(k/2\) for \(k = 0{,}001\). They cause the small difference between \(5{,}001 \cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(4{,}999 \cdot 10^{-4}\) in the table.

So for small shifts the effect that grows with the square of the shift is negligible compared with the one that grows in proportion to the shift. This observation will matter when we look for simpler formulas for small strains.

The cube is special in this respect. The difference of the displacements of two of its points has the component \(x\) equal to \(0{,}001\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}})\), and the other two components are zero. So it depends only on the vector \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\), not on where in the cube the fibre lies. By (1.11), two fibres with the same vector before loading therefore have the same vector after loading too, and the same strain, wherever in the cube they lie. What we saw on the front face holds at every point of the cube.

The cube has taught us two things. First, how the material is stretched close to a point is not described by a single number. Fibres through the same point are stretched in one direction, shortened in another, almost unchanged in a third, and the angles between them change too. Second, we can calculate all of this from the displacement field. For the cube the field was very clear, since the displacement was only horizontal and proportional to the height. In a general body, however, the displacement changes from point to point in a complicated way, as for the hanging bar, but now in three directions and depending on three coordinates. For the bar we helped ourselves with a piece that we shortened towards zero, and with the derivative. In space we will do the same. For this we will need derivatives of functions of three variables, and a way of writing that gives the strain of a fibre for every direction. We continue with them in the next section.
