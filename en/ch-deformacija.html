Take a rubber band and pull it by both ends. It gets longer, and we can see it. Now take a steel bar and pull it with the same force. It looks as if nothing has happened. Yet the steel bar has also become longer, only by so little that our eyes cannot notice it. This is true of every body: when a force acts on it, it changes its shape. When we step onto a bridge, the bridge bends a little. When we sit on a chair, the legs of the chair get a little shorter. When a motor drives a shaft, the shaft twists a little, one end more than the other.

These changes are mostly small, but they decide whether a structure holds. A bridge that bends a few centimetres under a truck is fine. A bridge that bends a metre is not. So we begin the book with the question of how to describe a change of shape with numbers. We will build the answer step by step, starting from the simplest case. Where we need new mathematics, we will stop and develop it completely, and then return to mechanics.

1.1 A bar that stretches

The simplest case is a straight bar that we pull along its axis. Before loading it has length \(L\). When we pull it, it has length \(L + \Delta L\). The number \(\Delta L\) is the elongation (raztezek) of the bar, that is, the change of its length. In this book the Greek letter \(\Delta\) (delta) always means a change: we read \(\Delta L\) as "the change of the length \(L\)". If we compress the bar, it gets shorter, and the elongation is negative.

Is the elongation a good measure of how hard the bar is loaded? Take two bars of the same steel with the same cross section, one 1 m long and the other 2 m, and pull them with the same force. The longer one stretches twice as much. This is no surprise. We can think of the longer bar as two shorter ones placed end to end. The force that pulls the longer bar pulls each of the two halves, so each half stretches as much as the short bar, and together they stretch twice as much. But the material in the two bars is loaded the same: every centimetre of the longer bar stretches exactly as much as every centimetre of the shorter one. So the elongation does not tell us how hard the material is loaded. Mostly it tells us how long the bar is.

What matters for the material is the stretch of every centimetre, of every metre, that is, the elongation per unit length. We get it by dividing the elongation by the length.

Definition 1.1 Strain of a bar (deformacija palice)

A bar with initial length \(L\) that stretches by \(\Delta L\) has the strain (deformacija)

\[\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} . \tag{1.1}\]

Source: BARBER §1.2.1 (PDF p. 32), EP Example 2.3.1(d) (p. 60), SADD §2.2 (PDF p. 42).

In everyday language "deformation" means a change of shape. In mechanics the number that measures this change is called the strain. Strain is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit: the metres cancel. For the 1 m bar and the 2 m bar of our example we get the same strain, which is what we wanted. Other books also call this number the relative elongation, the engineering strain, or the strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}\), where the index \(xx\) says that it is a stretch in the direction of the \(x\) axis.

How large are strains? A steel bar 2 m long that stretches by 1 mm has the strain

\[\varepsilon = \frac{0.001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 0.0005 ,\]

five ten-thousandths. A rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 15 cm has the strain \(5\ \text{cm} / 10\ \text{cm} = 0.5\), a thousand times more. For structures one more number matters. If ordinary structural steel is stretched by more than about one thousandth of its length, it does not return to its original length when we let go, but stays permanently longer. This bound follows from the data for steel in BOWER (Tables 3.1 and 3.9, PDF pp. 100 and 156). We will see how to compute it once we know how the force in a bar is related to its strain. So the strains in steel structures are almost always smaller than \(0.001\). This is one of the most important facts in this book, because many simplifications will rest on it.

For a compressed bar \(\Delta L\) is negative, so the strain is negative too. A positive strain means lengthening, a negative one shortening.

The formula \(\varepsilon = \Delta L / L\) has a hidden assumption. When we divided the longer bar into two halves, we assumed that both stretch the same. More than that, we assumed that every centimetre of the bar stretches the same as every other. What if this is not so?

1.2 When a bar does not stretch the same everywhere

Hang a steel bar from the ceiling and let it hang. No force acts on it except its own weight. The top part of the bar has to carry the weight of the whole bar below it. The bottom end carries almost nothing. So we expect the top centimetres to stretch more than the bottom ones. If we measure the total elongation \(\Delta L\) and divide it by \(L\), we get some average, which does not tell us how the material is stretched at the top and how at the bottom. We want to know how the material is stretched at each point of the bar separately.

How do we describe that? We follow the points of the bar. We label a point by its distance \(x\) from the ceiling, measured before the bar is hung. Books that distinguish the position before loading from the position after it often write this distance with a capital \(X\) and the new position with a small \(x\). The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), the bottom end at \(x = L\). When the bar hangs, every point moves down a little. How far the point that was at distance \(x\) moves, we write as \(u(x)\) and call the displacement (pomik) of that point. So the displacement is the difference between the new and the old position of the point. The top of the bar is fixed to the ceiling, so it does not move: \(u(0) = 0\). Points lower down move more, because the elongations of all the parts of the bar above them add up.

Now pick a short piece of the bar between the points \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). Its initial length is \(\Delta x\). The top end of the piece moves by \(u(x)\), the bottom end by \(u(x + \Delta x)\). If the bottom end moves more than the top end, the piece has become longer, by the difference of the two displacements:

\[\Delta u = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) .\]

The piece is a small bar, so we can use the formula (1.1) for it. Its strain is the elongation \(\Delta u\) divided by the length \(\Delta x\):

\[\varepsilon \approx \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.2}\]

The sign \(\approx\) means "approximately equal". Even within the piece the strain changes a little from top to bottom, so this is still an average, only over a shorter part of the bar. The shorter the piece, the less the strain changes within it, and the better the number describes how the material is stretched at the point \(x\).

Let us try this with numbers. In the chapter on the forces in a bar we will compute that a steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hanging under its own weight stretches so that the displacement of the point at distance \(x\) from the ceiling is

\[u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2), \qquad a = 1.95 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1} .\]

The constant \(a\) depends only on how heavy and how stiff steel is (BARBER (1.39) with the data for steel from BOWER, Table 3.1). We do not need to understand it now, we will only compute with it. The top of the bar is at \(x = 0\), where \(u(0) = 0\), as it must be. The bottom end moves by \(u(10\ \text{m}) = a \cdot 100\ \text{m}^2 = 1.95 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, two hundredths of a millimetre. A ten-metre steel bar stretches under its own weight by less than the thickness of a sheet of paper.

Let us compute the strain of the piece (1.2) at the top of the bar (\(x = 0\)) and at the middle (\(x = 5\) m) for shorter and shorter pieces:

length of the piece \(\Delta x\) at the top at the middle
1 m \(3.709 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1.757 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0.1 m \(3.884 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1.932 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0.01 m \(3.902 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1.950 \cdot 10^{-6}\)
0.001 m \(3.904 \cdot 10^{-6}\) \(1.952 \cdot 10^{-6}\)

The numbers settle. At the top they approach about \(3.904 \cdot 10^{-6}\), at the middle about \(1.952 \cdot 10^{-6}\), exactly half. The material at the top is stretched twice as much as at the middle, which agrees with our expectation that the top part carries more weight.

So it seems that every point has its own strain, the number that the strains of shorter and shorter pieces approach. But what exactly does "approach" mean? A table cannot be the answer. It shows only a few rows, and from them we do not know for certain how the numbers behave further on. And we cannot shrink the piece to nothing, because in the formula (1.2) we would get \(0/0\), a division by zero. We need an exact meaning of the words "the number that the ratio approaches as \(\Delta x\) gets smaller and smaller", and a way to compute that number without tables. This is a job for mathematics, and the next section does it.

1.3 Mathematics: limit and derivative

In this section we forget the bar for a moment. We have a rule that assigns a number \(u(x)\) to every number \(x\), and we ask how fast \(u(x)\) changes as we change \(x\). The answer will be a new number. Once we can compute it, we return to the bar.

When computing we use the school rules for real numbers: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division by a nonzero number, and working with inequalities. The absolute value \(|a|\) of a number \(a\) is the distance of the number from zero: \(|3| = 3\), \(|-3| = 3\). So the distance between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is \(|a - b|\).

1.3.1 Function

Definition 1.2 Function (funkcija)

A function (funkcija) \(f\) is a rule that assigns to every number \(x\) from some set of numbers exactly one number \(f(x)\). The set of numbers \(x\) for which the rule is given is the domain (definicijsko območje) of the function, and the number \(f(x)\) is the value of the function at \(x\).

Source: LEBL1 §0.3 (PDF pp. 18-19), EP §6.2.

The displacement \(u(x)\) is a function. For the hanging bar its domain is the set of all \(x\) between \(0\) and \(L\), that is, of all points of the bar, and the value \(u(x)\) is the displacement of the point \(x\). The words "exactly one" matter: a point of the bar moves by one distance only, not by two at once.

1.3.2 Ratio of changes

The ratio we computed in the table has a general form. For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a small change \(\Delta x \neq 0\),

\[\frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \tag{1.3}\]

is the change of the value divided by the change of the argument. It tells us by how much \(f\) changed on average per unit change of \(x\). We call it the difference quotient (diferenčni količnik). For the bar it is the strain of the piece. For a car journey, where \(f(t)\) is the distance covered up to the time \(t\), it is the average speed over the time \(\Delta t\).

For \(\Delta x = 0\) the quotient is not defined, because we would divide by zero. So we treat it as a function of the change \(\Delta x\), defined for all small \(\Delta x\) except \(\Delta x = 0\). We ask what it approaches as \(\Delta x\) approaches zero.

1.3.3 Limit

What does it mean that some function \(g(h)\) approaches a number \(d\) as \(h\) approaches zero? It means that \(g(h)\) is as close to \(d\) as we wish, provided \(h\) is close enough to zero. Set an allowed error, say \(0.001\). Is there a band around zero so narrow that for every \(h\) in the band, except \(h = 0\), the value \(g(h)\) differs from \(d\) by less than \(0.001\)? If there is one for the error \(0.001\), for the error \(0.000001\), and for every other error we can think of, then \(g(h)\) really approaches \(d\). For a smaller error the band may be narrower, but it must exist.

We write this exactly. We call the allowed error \(\eta\) (the Greek letter eta) and the width of the band around zero \(\delta\) (delta). In mathematics books the allowed error is usually written \(\varepsilon\). We keep \(\varepsilon\) for strain.

Definition 1.3 Limit of a function (limita funkcije)

Let the function \(g\) be defined for all \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < r\) for some \(r > 0\). The function \(g\) has the limit (limita) \(d\) at the point \(0\), written

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d , \tag{1.4}\]

if for every \(\eta > 0\) there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d| < \eta\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\).

Source: LEBL1 Def. 3.1.3 (PDF p. 114), written for the point \(0\) and with \(\eta\) instead of \(\varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < |h|\) leaves out the point \(h = 0\), where \(g\) may not be defined at all, like our quotient. The limit tells what \(g\) approaches, not what it is at the point itself. The number \(r\) makes sure that the points the condition talks about really are in the domain.

Let us try the definition on the simplest example. Let \(g(h) = 5 - h\). We expect \(g(h)\) to approach \(5\). Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The difference is \(|g(h) - 5| = |{-h}| = |h|\). If we take \(\delta = \eta\), then for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) the difference is \(|h| < \eta\). So for every error we have found a band, and the limit is \(5\). For the error \(0.001\) the band is \(0.001\) wide on each side, for the error \(0.000001\) only \(0.000001\).

The notation \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} g(h) = d\) claims that there is only one limit. Otherwise the word "limit" could mean two different numbers. Let us check that this is so.

The idea is simple. If \(g(h)\) approached two numbers at once, then for small \(h\) the value \(g(h)\) would be close to both, and so the two numbers would be close to each other. But only equal numbers can be closer to each other than every error.

Lemma 1.4 Uniqueness of the limit (enoličnost limite)

A function has at most one limit at the point \(0\).

Proof

We have to show that two numbers \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) that both satisfy the definition of the limit are equal.

  1. Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). We will show that \(|d_1 - d_2| < \eta\).
  2. Since \(d_1\) is a limit, the definition gives for the error \(\eta/2\) a band \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_1| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\). We take half the error because in step 6 we will add two such errors.
  3. Since \(d_2\) is a limit, for the same error \(\eta/2\) there is a band \(\delta_2 > 0\) such that \(|g(h) - d_2| < \eta/2\) for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta_2\).
  4. Take an \(h\) that is nonzero and smaller than both bands, for example half of the smallest of the numbers \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) and \(r\). For this \(h\) both inequalities of steps 2 and 3 hold.
  5. Write the difference \(d_1 - d_2\) through the value \(g(h)\), which we know: \(d_1 - d_2 = (d_1 - g(h)) + (g(h) - d_2)\).
  6. The distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) is not larger than the distance from \(d_1\) to \(g(h)\) plus the distance from \(g(h)\) to \(d_2\). This is the triangle inequality for numbers, \(|a + b| \le |a| + |b|\). It holds because every number lies between its absolute value and the negative of it, \(-|a| \le a \le |a|\) and \(-|b| \le b \le |b|\). Adding the inequalities gives \(-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|\), and a number that lies between \(-c\) and \(c\) has absolute value at most \(c\). With \(a = d_1 - g(h)\) and \(b = g(h) - d_2\) we therefore get
\[|d_1 - d_2| \le |d_1 - g(h)| + |g(h) - d_2| < \tfrac{\eta}{2} + \tfrac{\eta}{2} = \eta .\]
  1. We picked the error \(\eta\) freely, so \(|d_1 - d_2|\) is smaller than every positive number.
  2. A nonnegative number that is smaller than every positive number is zero: if it were positive, it would be smaller than itself. So \(|d_1 - d_2| = 0\) and \(d_1 = d_2\).

Source: LEBL1 Prop. 3.1.4 (PDF pp. 114-115). The argument for the triangle inequality in step 6 is not from the source.

The proof showed a pattern that we will meet many times: when we want a sum of two errors to be small, we allow each of them half.

1.3.4 Derivative

Now we can say what the number is that the strain of the piece approached in the table.

Definition 1.5 Derivative (odvod)

A function \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(x\) if the difference quotient (1.3) has a limit as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. This limit is the derivative (odvod) of the function \(f\) at the point \(x\):

\[f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.5}\]

Source: LEBL1 Def. 4.1.1 (PDF p. 155). The notation with \(\Delta x\) follows BARBER (1.35)-(1.36), where it is \(\delta x\).

So the derivative is a rate of change: by how much \(f\) changes per unit change of \(x\) when the change is very small. We read \(f'(x)\) as "\(f\) prime of \(x\)". In books you will also find the derivative written as \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}\). This notation recalls the quotient \(\Delta f / \Delta x\) from which the derivative comes.

Let us compute the derivative of the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), where \(a\) and \(L\) are given numbers. The way has two parts. First we simplify the difference quotient so that \(\Delta x\), which we must not set to zero, disappears from the denominator. Then we find the limit of the simplified expression from the definition.

  1. The value at \(x + \Delta x\): \(u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2L(x + \Delta x) - (x + \Delta x)^2)\).
  2. Expand the square, \((x + \Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\,\Delta x + \Delta x^2\), and multiply out \(2L(x + \Delta x) = 2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x\). So
\[u(x + \Delta x) = a\,(2Lx + 2L\,\Delta x - x^2 - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  1. Subtract \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\). The terms \(2Lx\) and \(-x^2\) cancel, and what remains is
\[u(x + \Delta x) - u(x) = a\,(2L\,\Delta x - 2x\,\Delta x - \Delta x^2) .\]
  1. All three terms in the bracket have the factor \(\Delta x\), so we take it out: \(a\,\Delta x\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x)\).
  2. Divide by \(\Delta x \neq 0\). The factor \(\Delta x\) cancels:
\[\frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} = a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) .\]

This holds for every \(\Delta x \neq 0\). At \(\Delta x = 0\) the left side would be \(0/0\), but the right side is a perfectly good number. Now we see why the numbers in the table settled: the quotient differs from the number \(a\,(2L - 2x)\) by exactly \(a\,\Delta x\). 6. Let us show from the definition of the limit that the limit of the quotient is \(d = a\,(2L - 2x)\). Pick any error \(\eta > 0\). The difference between the quotient and \(d\) is \(|a\,(2L - 2x - \Delta x) - a\,(2L - 2x)| = |{-a\,\Delta x}| = a\,|\Delta x|\), since \(a > 0\). 7. We want \(a\,|\Delta x| < \eta\), which holds exactly when \(|\Delta x| < \eta / a\). So we take the band \(\delta = \eta / a\). For every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta\) the difference is smaller than \(\eta\). 8. We have found a band for every error, so the limit of the quotient is \(a\,(2L - 2x)\), and

\[u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

Step 5 was the heart of the computation. With patient algebra we turned the difference quotient into an expression that is defined at \(\Delta x = 0\) too, and after that the limit was easy. We will compute derivatives this way later as well, until we derive rules that do this work once and for all.

1.4 Strain at a point

Let us return to the bar. The strain of the piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\) is the difference quotient of the displacement, and as the length of the piece goes to zero it approaches the derivative of the displacement. This derivative is the measure we were looking for.

Definition 1.6 Strain at a point (deformacija v točki)

Let \(u(x)\) be the displacement of the point of the bar that was at distance \(x\) from a chosen end before loading. The strain (deformacija) of the bar at the point \(x\) is the derivative of the displacement:

\[\varepsilon(x) = u'(x) = \lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} \frac{u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} . \tag{1.6}\]

Source: BARBER (1.35), (1.36) (PDF p. 33), ONATE1 §2.2 (PDF p. 66). In books it is often written \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{x}} / \partial x\) or \(e_{\mathrm{xx}}\).

So the strain at a point is, like the strain of a bar (1.1), a number without a unit, an elongation per unit length. The difference is that we do not compute it for the whole bar, but for shorter and shorter pieces around the point, and take the number that their strains approach. For a bar that stretches the same everywhere, the two strains are equal. Indeed, if the displacement is \(u(x) = \varepsilon_0\,x\) with a constant \(\varepsilon_0\), the difference quotient is \(\varepsilon_0\,\Delta x / \Delta x = \varepsilon_0\) for every \(\Delta x\), its limit is \(\varepsilon_0\), and the total elongation is \(u(L) - u(0) = \varepsilon_0 L\), so \(\Delta L / L = \varepsilon_0\).

For the hanging bar we have already computed the derivative:

\[\varepsilon(x) = 2a\,(L - x) .\]

At the top, at \(x = 0\), the strain is \(2aL = 2 \cdot 1.95 \cdot 10^{-7} \cdot 10 = 3.90 \cdot 10^{-6}\). At the middle, at \(x = 5\) m, it is \(1.95 \cdot 10^{-6}\), and at the bottom end zero. These are exactly the numbers the columns of the table approached. The strain falls evenly from top to bottom, just as the weight carried by each cross section falls: the top carries the whole bar, the middle half of it, the bottom end nothing.

The total elongation of the bar is the displacement of the bottom end, \(\Delta L = u(L) - u(0) = aL^2 = 1.95 \cdot 10^{-5}\) m, and the average strain is \(\Delta L / L = aL = 1.95 \cdot 10^{-6}\). This is exactly the strain at the middle. When the strain changes evenly along the bar, the average really is the value at the middle. But the most stretched material, at the top, is stretched twice as much as the average. If we judged the strength of the bar by the average, we would be wrong by a factor of two.

One more simple but important observation. Suppose the ceiling sags by 1 mm for some reason. Then every point of the bar moves by an extra millimetre, and the new displacement is \(u(x) + 0.001\) m. The strain does not change. In the difference quotient the extra millimetre cancels, \((u(x + \Delta x) + 0.001) - (u(x) + 0.001) = u(x + \Delta x) - u(x)\). A displacement that is the same for all points does not deform the body, it only moves it as a whole. Such a displacement is called a rigid-body displacement (togi premik), because it is the only displacement a perfectly rigid body can undergo (BARBER p. 32). So we can never compute the displacement completely from the strain: we can always add a rigid-body displacement. For the hanging bar it is fixed by the condition that the top is attached to the ceiling, \(u(0) = 0\) (BARBER (1.39)).

So far the bar has been just a line: every point moved along the same straight line, and the displacement was a single number. A real body has three dimensions. A point in it can move in any direction, and a small piece of the body can at the same time stretch in one direction, shorten in another, and twist. One number is not enough to describe displacements in space. We need quantities that have a size and a direction, and we start the next section with them.
