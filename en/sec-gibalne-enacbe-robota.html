Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.8 Equations of motion of a robot

Until now the arm was at rest or we prescribed its velocities. Section 27.6 connected the joint velocities with the velocity of the tip, and section 27.7 connected the motor torques with the load when the arm is at rest: \(\boldsymbol\tau = \vek g(\vek q) - \mat J^\T\vek F\) (theorem 27.50). In motion something else happens. Arm A of section 27.1 holds the configuration \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) without a load with the torques \((84{,}957,\ 0)\) N m. If the first link turns with angular acceleration \(2\) rad/s\(^2\) and angular velocity \(1\) rad/s, and the second with \(0{,}5\) rad/s and acceleration \(-1\) rad/s\(^2\), the motor of the first joint needs \(102{,}562\) N m and the motor of the second \(2{,}749\) N m, which is more than for holding (the sum is computed step by step in example 27.58). What torques do the motors need for a prescribed motion \(\vek q(t)\), and conversely, how does the arm move if we prescribe the torques of the motors? We call this inverse and direct dynamics (obratna in neposredna dinamika). In the inverse problem we compute the torques from the motion, in the direct problem the motion from the torques.

In figure 27.68 each motor gets its own axis and its own scale. For the first motor the grey dashed holding torque is \(84{,}957\) N m, the orange moving torque is \(102{,}562\) N m, and the blue difference of \(17{,}6\) N m is what the motion adds to holding. For the second motor (axis) holding is zero, because the weight does not turn the second joint in this configuration, while moving needs \(2{,}749\) N m, so the whole difference is \(2{,}749\) N m. The arm needs not only the torque to hold itself but also the torque to accelerate.

The idea is not new. The joint variables are generalized coordinates of the chain (lemma 27.4), the links are rigid bodies (definition 1.11) and the joints are ideal constraints (definition 8.3), so the arm is a system of chapter 8. Lagrange's equations hold for it (theorem 8.101), and they need the kinetic energy \(T\) and the potential energy \(V\) as functions of \(\vek q\) and \(\dot{\vek q}\) and the generalized forces of the remaining forces, here of the motors and the load. The work of this section is therefore to write \(T\) and \(V\) with the matrices we already have and to carry the differentiation through to the end.

The model has four idealizations (idealizacije), and a real arm violates each of them.

  1. The links are rigid (definition 1.11). Flexible links belong to chapter 28.
  2. The joints are ideal and each has one degree of freedom, without friction and without play. A real joint has friction, and the equation we shall derive does not contain it. Friction always opposes the motion of the joint, so for the same motion the motor needs more torque than the equation predicts, and at rest it holds within limits the model does not know.
  3. A motor gives between links \(i - 1\) and \(i\) a torque (revolute joint) or a force (prismatic joint) \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\). We do not model the inertia of the motor and of the gearbox. For one link with a gearbox of ratio \(r : 1\), \(\theta_{\mathrm{m}} = r\theta_\ell\), the motor adds \(r^2 J_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the inertia of the link, with the moment of inertia of the motor \(J_{\mathrm{m}}\) (SPONG, PDF pp. 189-190), so at \(r = 100\) it is \(10^4\,J_{\mathrm{m}}\) and is not small.
  4. Apart from the load the arm is free: only the weight, which is constant, and the torques of the motors act on the links.

Arm A has the same links as before: lengths \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, uniform thin rods with masses \(m_1 = 10\) kg and \(m_2 = 5\) kg (the masses are ours, not from the source), centres of mass \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) in the middle of the links, so at distances \(l_{\mathrm{c}1} = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}25\) m from the joint, and moments of inertia about the centre of mass \(m_{\mathrm{k}}l_{\mathrm{k}}^2/12\) (equation (8.91)), that is \(0{,}8333\) and \(0{,}1042\) kg m\(^2\).

27.8.1 Kinetic energy of a robot

Theorem 8.92 requires the velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity of every link. Take arm A in the configuration \((30^\circ, 60^\circ)\) (figure 27.71) and ask how fast the centre of mass of the second link moves.

We start with frame 0, the axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) at the base, which stays at rest. In it we place both links as thick lines: the first goes from the joint at \(O\) to the joint at \(o_1\), the second from \(o_1\) to the tip. The angles are the same as in section 27.1: \(q_1\) from the axis \(x_0\) to the first link and \(q_2\) from the extension of the first link to the second. Every link has a centre of mass, the point in which theorem 8.92 gathers all the mass for the translational motion: \(G_1\) in the middle of the first and \(G_2\) in the middle of the second link. Their positions are \(\vek c_1 = (0{,}433,\ 0{,}25)\) m and \(\vek c_2 = (0{,}866,\ 0{,}75)\) m.

The centre of mass \(G_2\) moves because of both joints. If only the first one turns, with \(\dot q_1 = 1\) rad/s, the whole arm turns like one body about \(O\) and by equation (8.87) of lemma 8.87 (the point \(O\) is at rest, \(\vek\omega = \dot q_1\vek z_0\)) the velocity of the centre of mass is \(\vek z_0\times(\vek c_2 - \vek o_0)\). This is the arrow \(\vek j_1\), perpendicular to the radius \(OG_2\) of length \(|OG_2| = 1{,}1456\) m, so \(\vek j_1 = (-0{,}75,\ 0{,}866)\) m. If only the second joint turns, only the second link turns about \(o_1\), and the velocity of \(G_2\) is \(\vek z_0\times(\vek c_2 - \vek o_1) = (-0{,}25,\ 0)\) m, which is the arrow \(\vek j_2\), perpendicular to the radius \(o_1G_2\), whose length is \(l_{\mathrm{c}2} = 0{,}25\) m. When both turn at once the velocities add, \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G}2} = \dot q_1\vek j_1 + \dot q_2\vek j_2\). This is the product of a matrix and a vector (definition 1.97) with the matrix \(\mat A_2\) made of the columns \(\vek j_1\) and \(\vek j_2\).

The same holds for every arm, as theorem 27.42 says. Its proof speaks about a point of the last link that is fixed in the axis of this link, so it holds also for the centre of mass \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}\) of link \(k\) with the chain of the first \(k\) joints. Column \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) of size \(3 \times n\) is for a revolute joint \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\times(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}} - \vek o_{\mathrm{i}-1})\), for a prismatic joint \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\), and column \(i\) of the matrix \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) (angular velocity) is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) or \(\vek 0\) respectively. For \(i > k\) both columns are zero, because link \(k\) does not move if only the joints after it turn. So \(\vek v_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\dot{\vek q}\) and \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\dot{\vek q}\) are exactly the velocities of step 1 of the proof of theorem 8.97. For arm A

\[\mat A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}25 & 0 \\ 0{,}433 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{ m}, \quad \mat A_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}75 & -0{,}25 \\ 0{,}866 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\text{ m}, \quad \mat W_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mat W_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{27.33}\]

The second column of the matrix \(\mat A_1\) is zero, because the first link does not move if only the second joint turns.

The inertia tensor of link \(k\) is constant in an axis attached to the link, so in frame \(k\) (lemma 8.90, point 3), and we write it \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\). The letter \(G\) separates it from the matrix \(\mat J\) of section 27.6. From it we get the tensor in frame 0, in which the velocities are written, and this is the last component of the energy.

Theorem 27.52 Mass matrix of a robot (masna matrika robota)

Let the robot be an open chain of \(n\) joints with ideal joints and rigid links \(k = 1, \dots, n\) with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}} > 0\) and inertia tensors \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) about the centre of mass in the axis of link \(k\) (section 27.1). Let \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R^0_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\) be the orientation matrix of link \(k\) (definition 27.8), \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) the matrices of size \(3 \times n\) from the description above. The kinetic energy of the robot is

\[T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(\vek q)\,\dot{\vek q} , \qquad \mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{n}\Big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\Big) , \tag{27.34}\]

where \(\mat M(\vek q)\) is a symmetric positive definite matrix of size \(n \times n\) with continuously differentiable elements that depend only on \(\vek q\). We call it the mass matrix of the robot (masna matrika).

Proof

We must show that the robot satisfies the assumptions of theorem 8.97 and that the tensor of the link is written with \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\).

  1. The joint variables are generalized coordinates of a chain with \(n\) degrees of freedom (lemma 27.4). The constraints do not contain time, because the arm moves only by its motors and not by a prescribed law, so \(\vek b = \vek 0\) and \(T_0 = 0\) in theorem 8.97. The position of the centre of mass \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\) and the orientation \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(\vek q\) (products and sums of sines and cosines of the joint angles, lemma 27.25).
  2. By step 3 of the proof of theorem 8.97, \(\mat M = \sum_{\mathrm{k}}\big(m_{\mathrm{k}}\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat A_{\mathrm{k}} + \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\big)\), where \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}}\) is the inertia tensor of link \(k\) in frame 0 and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) is exactly the matrix \(\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial\vek q\) (step 1 of the same proof).
  3. Let \(\vek r^0\) be the vector from the centre of mass to a point of link \(k\) in frame 0 and \(\vek r^k\) the same vector in frame \(k\). By lemma 27.9, \(\vek r^0 = \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\vek r^k\). An orthogonal matrix preserves lengths, \(|\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\vek r^k|^2 = |\vek r^k|^2\), and \(\vek r^0(\vek r^0)^\T = \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\vek r^k(\vek r^k)^\T\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\). Hence \(|\vek r^0|^2\mat I - \vek r^0(\vek r^0)^\T = \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\big(|\vek r^k|^2\mat I - \vek r^k(\vek r^k)^\T\big)\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\), and by definition 8.89, taking \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\) out of the integral, \(\mat J_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\). The tensor \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) is constant, because the vector \(\vek r^k\) is constant (the link is a rigid body).
  4. Equation (27.34) follows from steps 2 and 3. Symmetry, positive definiteness and continuous differentiability follow from theorem 8.97, since \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\) are continuously differentiable and \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\) is constant. The dependence on \(\vek q\) only is a consequence of step 1.

Source: SPONG (6.50)-(6.53) (PDF pp. 202-203), where \(\mat M\) is denoted \(D\) and positive definiteness is left as an exercise (problem 6-6, PDF p. 203). Here they follow from theorem 8.97. The proof by steps is not from the source.

The name mass matrix has a reason. For one point of mass \(m\) on a line \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2\), so \(\mat M = m\) and the equation we shall derive has \(m\ddot q\) on the left, which is the force by Newton. For a robot the mass is a matrix because the accelerations of different joints are not independent: an acceleration of the first joint in general requires a torque also on the second.

We test the theorem on arm A. For a planar arm the angular velocity has only a component along \(z_0\), and \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\) is a rotation about this axis, so \(\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}}\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = \omega_{\mathrm{k}}^2\,J_{\mathrm{zz},\mathrm{k}} = I_{\mathrm{k}}\omega_{\mathrm{k}}^2\) with the moment \(I_{\mathrm{k}} = m_{\mathrm{k}}l_{\mathrm{k}}^2/12\). The second part of the sum is therefore \(I_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek w_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek w_{\mathrm{k}}\) with the row \(\vek w_{\mathrm{k}}\) of the component \(z\) of the matrix \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\).

Example 27.53 Mass matrix of arm A (masna matrika roke A)

Arm A at \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)\), matrices (27.33), \(m_1 = 10\) kg, \(m_2 = 5\) kg, \(I_1 = 0{,}8333\) kg m\(^2\), \(I_2 = 0{,}1042\) kg m\(^2\).

  1. First link, translation: \(m_1\mat A_1^\T\mat A_1 = 10\begin{pmatrix} 0{,}0625 + 0{,}1875 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2{,}5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m\(^2\), since \(0{,}25^2 + 0{,}433^2 = 0{,}25\).
  2. Second link, translation: \(m_2\mat A_2^\T\mat A_2 = 5\begin{pmatrix} 0{,}75^2 + 0{,}866^2 & 0{,}75\cdot 0{,}25 \\ 0{,}75\cdot 0{,}25 & 0{,}25^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6{,}5625 & 0{,}9375 \\ 0{,}9375 & 0{,}3125 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m\(^2\).
  3. Rotation: \(I_1\vek w_1^\T\vek w_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0{,}8333 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(I_2\vek w_2^\T\vek w_2 = 0{,}1042\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), since \(\vek w_1 = (1,\ 0)\) and \(\vek w_2 = (1,\ 1)\).
  4. The sum of the four parts is
    \[\mat M(30^\circ, 60^\circ) = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 1{,}0417 \\ 1{,}0417 & 0{,}4167 \end{pmatrix}\text{ kg m}^2 .\]
  5. For an arbitrary \(\vek q\) the same computation with SymPy (written in num-08.py) gives
    \[\mat M(\vek q) = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{35}{4} + \tfrac52\cos q_2 & \tfrac{5}{12} + \tfrac54\cos q_2 \\ \tfrac{5}{12} + \tfrac54\cos q_2 & \tfrac{5}{12} \end{pmatrix}\text{ kg m}^2 . \tag{27.35}\]

Source: The computation is ours (SymPy). SPONG, equation (6.86) (PDF p. 209) gives the same formula for the same kind of arm in its own notation.

The matrix does not depend on \(q_1\): turning the whole arm about \(O\) changes neither the distances of the centres of mass from the axis nor the moments of inertia. It does depend on \(q_2\), which changes the distance of the centre of mass \(G_2\) from \(O\) (figure 27.74).

The axes show the angle \(q_2\) from \(0^\circ\) to \(360^\circ\) and the values of the elements, the marked values are the three configurations we shall need. The element \(M_{22}\) is constant, \(0{,}4167\) kg m\(^2\): the second link turns about \(o_1\) regardless of the position of the first, and its moment of inertia about \(o_1\) is \(m_2l_{\mathrm{c}2}^2 + I_2\). The element \(M_{11}\) is largest for the stretched arm, \(11{,}25\) kg m\(^2\) at \(q_2 = 0^\circ\), and smallest for the folded one, \(6{,}25\) kg m\(^2\) at \(q_2 = 180^\circ\). The reason is the distance of the centre of mass \(G_2\) from \(O\): \(1{,}25\) m for the stretched and \(0{,}75\) m for the folded arm, the squares are \(1{,}5625\) and \(0{,}5625\) m\(^2\), the difference \(1\) m\(^2\), times \(m_2 = 5\) kg is \(5\) kg m\(^2\), and indeed \(11{,}25 - 6{,}25 = 5\). For the folded arm the second link is closer to the axis and the first link turns more easily with it. By equation (27.34) the element \(M_{12}\) equals \(m_2(\vek c_2 - \vek o_0)\cdot(\vek c_2 - \vek o_1) + I_2\), since the columns of \(\mat A_2\) are the vectors \(\vek z_0\times(\vek c_2 - \vek o_0)\) and \(\vek z_0\times(\vek c_2 - \vek o_1)\), and the scalar product and the length are the same after a rotation by a right angle (lemma 8.38). This is the scalar product of the radii of figure 27.71, and it is negative when they point in opposite directions: for the folded arm \(\vek c_2 - \vek o_0\) is \(0{,}75\) m long in the direction of the first link and \(\vek c_2 - \vek o_1\) is \(0{,}25\) m long in the opposite one, the product is \(5\cdot(-0{,}1875) + 0{,}1042 = -0{,}8333\) kg m\(^2\). It is zero at \(\cos q_2 = -\tfrac13\), that is at \(q_2 = 109{,}47^\circ\) and \(q_2 = 250{,}53^\circ\).

The mass matrix is invertible in every configuration, also in a singular one. At \(q_2 = 0\) we have \(\det\mat J = l_1l_2\sin q_2 = 0\) (lemma 27.48), yet \(\det\mat M = 11{,}25\cdot 0{,}4167 - 1{,}6667^2 = 1{,}9097\) kg\(^2\) m\(^4\). Singularity of the matrix \(\mat J\) and invertibility of the matrix \(\mat M\) are two different things. The first says that some velocity of the tip is impossible. The second says that by theorem 27.52 every motion of the joints is connected with a nonzero kinetic energy.

27.8.2 Potential energy and gravity

The only force with a potential is the weight (idealization 4). Let \(\vek e\) be the unit vector in frame 0 that points upward, against the weight, and \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\) the gravitational acceleration. For arm A frame 0 lies so that \(\vek e = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) (figure 27.71).

Lemma 27.54 Gravity of a robot (težnost robota)

Let the robot have links with masses \(m_{\mathrm{k}}\) and centres of mass \(\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\), and let only the weight act on the links. Its potential energy (definition 8.58) is

\[V(\vek q) = g\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{n} m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e\cdot\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) . \tag{27.36}\]

The column \(\vek g(\vek q) = \partial V/\partial\vek q\) equals

\[\vek g(\vek q) = g\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{n} m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek e , \tag{27.37}\]

which is the same column as in equation (27.30).

Proof

We must show that the force of gravity has the potential (27.36), and differentiate it.

  1. The weight acts on every part of link \(k\), per unit volume with the force \(-g\rho\,\vek e\). We divide the link into finitely many parts and choose a point in each. The sum of definition 8.51 for the weights of these parts is a Riemann sum, which tends to an integral as the division gets finer, so the generalized force of the weight of link \(k\) is \(Q_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{s}} = -g\int\rho\,\vek e\cdot\dfrac{\partial\vek x}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\mathrm{d}V\).
  2. A point of the link is \(\vek x = \vek c_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q) + \mat R_{\mathrm{k}}(\vek q)\,\vek r\), where \(\vek r\) is the vector from the centre of mass in the axis of the link and is constant (the link is a rigid body). Hence \(\partial\vek x/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} + (\partial\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\,\vek r\), and by linearity of the integral \(Q_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{s}} = -g\,m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e\cdot\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - g\,\vek e\cdot(\partial\mat R_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}})\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\). The integral \(\int\rho\,\vek r\,\mathrm{d}V\) is zero by the definition of the centre of mass (definition 8.81). Since \(\vek e\) is constant, \(Q_{\mathrm{k},\mathrm{s}} = -\partial(g\,m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e\cdot\vek c_{\mathrm{k}})/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\). The sum over the links is \(-\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) with \(V\) from (27.36), which is therefore the potential energy of the weight by definition 8.58.
  3. By step 2, \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = g\sum_{\mathrm{k}} m_{\mathrm{k}}\,\vek e\cdot\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and \(\partial\vek c_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is column \(s\) of the matrix \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) (step 2 of the proof of theorem 27.52). Hence \(\vek e\cdot(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}) = (\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\vek e)_{\mathrm{s}}\) and (27.37) follows.

Source: SPONG (6.54)-(6.55), (6.64) (PDF pp. 203, 205): the potential energy of the chain is the sum of the potential energies of the links, in which all the mass is in the centre of mass. The proof by steps is not from the source.

For arm A, \(\vek c_1\cdot\vek e = l_{\mathrm{c}1}\sin q_1\) and \(\vek c_2\cdot\vek e = l_1\sin q_1 + l_{\mathrm{c}2}\sin(q_1 + q_2)\), so

\[V = (m_1l_{\mathrm{c}1} + m_2l_1)\,g\sin q_1 + m_2l_{\mathrm{c}2}\,g\sin(q_1 + q_2) , \qquad \vek g(\vek q) = \begin{pmatrix} 98{,}1\cos q_1 + 12{,}2625\cos(q_1 + q_2) \\ 12{,}2625\cos(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix}\text{ N m} , \tag{27.38}\]

since \((m_1l_{\mathrm{c}1} + m_2l_1)g = (5 + 5)\cdot 9{,}81 = 98{,}1\) and \(m_2l_{\mathrm{c}2}g = 5\cdot 0{,}25\cdot 9{,}81 = 12{,}2625\). At \((30^\circ, 60^\circ)\), \(\vek g = (84{,}957,\ 0)\) N m, the second torque is zero because the second link points vertically and its centre of mass \(G_2\) lies on the vertical through \(o_1\) (the radius \(o_1G_2\) in figure 27.71), so the lever arm of the weight about \(o_1\) is zero. At \((0^\circ, 60^\circ)\), \(\vek g = (104{,}231,\ 6{,}131)\) N m.

27.8.3 Equations of motion

Now we have \(T\) and \(V\). Lagrange's equation for the \(k\)-th coordinate (theorem 8.101) requires the time derivative of \(\partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\) and the derivative \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}\). For \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) the first gives \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q}\) and also terms with the derivative of \(\mat M\), that is the derivative with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) multiplied by the velocity \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the second gives terms with the derivatives of \(\mat M\) with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\). All these terms contain a product of two velocities. Since the product \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\), we can simplify the coefficient next to it by replacing it with its symmetric part. We obtain numbers that we name.

Definition 27.55 Coefficients of the velocity terms (koeficienti hitrostnih členov)

Let \(\mat M(\vek q) = (d_{\mathrm{kj}})\) be a symmetric matrix of size \(n \times n\) with continuously differentiable elements on an open set in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The coefficients of the velocity terms (Christoffel symbols of the first kind, Christoffelovi simboli) are the numbers

\[c_{\mathrm{ijk}} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} + \frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ki}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\Big) , \qquad i, j, k = 1, \dots, n , \tag{27.39}\]

functions of \(\vek q\). For a column \(\dot{\vek q}\) of \(n\) numbers, \(\mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) is the matrix of size \(n \times n\) with elements \(C_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{n} c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Source: SPONG (6.63) and (6.67) (PDF p. 205), where the \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\) are called Christoffel symbols of the first kind, with no reason for the name.

The source does not explain the name after a person, so we take it only as a label. Our name has a reason: the symbols stand next to the products of velocities in the equation of motion. From equation (27.39) it follows that \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}} = c_{\mathrm{jik}}\), because \(d_{\mathrm{ij}} = d_{\mathrm{ji}}\) and the first two terms are exchanged under \(i \leftrightarrow j\). By the definition the matrix \(\mat C\) depends on \(\vek q\) and is linear in \(\dot{\vek q}\). Two symbols are used elsewhere in the book: \(\mat C\) is here the matrix of the velocity terms, not the damping matrix of chapter 10, and \(g\) without an argument is the gravitational acceleration \(9{,}81\) m/s\(^2\), while \(\vek g(\vek q)\) with an argument is the column of lemma 27.54.

Theorem 27.56 Equations of motion of a robot (gibalne enačbe robota)

Let the robot be as in theorem 27.52 and lemma 27.54, let the motors give the torques and forces \(\boldsymbol\tau\) and let a load \(\vek F\) (possibly \(\vek 0\)) also act on the last link. A twice differentiable motion \(\vek q(t)\) is a motion of the robot if and only if for every time

\[\mat M(\vek q)\,\ddot{\vek q} + \mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q})\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek g(\vek q) = \boldsymbol\tau + \mat J(\vek q)^\T\vek F , \tag{27.40}\]

where \(\mat M\) is from theorem 27.52, \(\mat C\) from definition 27.55 with the elements \(d_{\mathrm{kj}}\) of the matrix \(\mat M\), and \(\vek g\) from lemma 27.54. The matrix \(\mat M\) is invertible, so \(\ddot{\vek q} = \mat M^{-1}(\boldsymbol\tau + \mat J^\T\vek F - \mat C\dot{\vek q} - \vek g)\).

Proof

We must show that the \(k\)-th Lagrange equation is exactly the \(k\)-th row of equation (27.40). For the \(k\)-th coordinate we compute both derivatives of the energy.

  1. The robot satisfies the assumptions of theorem 8.101: the joint variables are generalized coordinates (lemma 27.4), the joints are ideal, \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M\dot{\vek q}\) (theorem 27.52) and the force of gravity has the potential \(V\) (lemma 27.54).
  2. Motor \(i\) acts with the moment \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) on link \(i\) and with the opposite moment on link \(i - 1\). The virtual rotation of link \(k\) is \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\,\delta\vek q\) (definition 8.51), where column \(s\) of the matrix \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}}\) is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{s}-1}\) for a revolute joint \(s \le k\) and \(\vek 0\) otherwise. For a revolute joint \(i\) the matrices \(\mat W_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\mat W_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) differ only in column \(i\), which is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\), so \(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - \delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1} = \vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The work of the pair is \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\cdot(\delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - \delta\boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1}) = \tau_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\). For a prismatic joint the forces \(\pm\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) act at the same point of both links, which separate by \(\vek z_{\mathrm{i}-1}\delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\) along the coordinate \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the work is again \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The generalized force of motor \(i\) (definition 8.51) is therefore \(\tau_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the generalized force of the load is \((\mat J^\T\vek F)_{\mathrm{k}}\) (steps 2 to 4 of the proof of theorem 27.50). The nonconservative generalized force is thus \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}_{\mathrm{k}} = \tau_{\mathrm{k}} + (\mat J^\T\vek F)_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  3. By theorem 8.101, and since \(V\) does not depend on \(\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\), \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}} - \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}} + \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}} = \tau_{\mathrm{k}} + (\mat J^\T\vek F)_{\mathrm{k}}\).
  4. The derivative with respect to velocity is \(\partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{kj}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\), since \(\mat M\) is symmetric. By the product rule and the chain rule (lemma 8.30) its time derivative is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{kj}}\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\dfrac{\partial d_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  5. The derivative with respect to position is \(\partial T/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}} = \tfrac12\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}\dfrac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\), because \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\) does not depend on \(q_{\mathrm{k}}\) (\(T\) is a function of the \(2n\) variables \(\vek q\) and \(\dot{\vek q}\) as in section 8.9, where we differentiate with respect to one and hold the others).
  6. Their difference is \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{kj}}\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}a_{\mathrm{ij}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\) with \(a_{\mathrm{ij}} = \dfrac{\partial d_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} - \tfrac12\dfrac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\).
  7. The sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}a_{\mathrm{ij}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\) does not change if \(a_{\mathrm{ij}}\) is replaced by \(\tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{ij}} + a_{\mathrm{ji}})\): by renaming indices, \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}a_{\mathrm{ji}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}a_{\mathrm{ij}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\), and the sum of both is twice the wanted one. Since \(d_{\mathrm{ij}} = d_{\mathrm{ji}}\), \(\tfrac12(a_{\mathrm{ij}} + a_{\mathrm{ji}}) = \tfrac12\big(\frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{kj}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{i}}} + \frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ki}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{j}}} - \frac{\partial d_{\mathrm{ij}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{k}}}\big) = c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\).
  8. So \(\sum_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\big)\dot q_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{kj}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}}\). Insert into step 3 and \(\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{k}} = g_{\mathrm{k}}\) (lemma 27.54): \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} d_{\mathrm{kj}}\ddot q_{\mathrm{j}} + \sum_{\mathrm{j}} C_{\mathrm{kj}}\dot q_{\mathrm{j}} + g_{\mathrm{k}} = \tau_{\mathrm{k}} + (\mat J^\T\vek F)_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\), which is (27.40).
  9. The steps are equalities, so the converse holds as well. The matrix \(\mat M\) is invertible: \(\mat M\vek x = \vek 0\) implies \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x = 0\), and by positive definiteness \(\vek x = \vek 0\).

Source: SPONG (6.56)-(6.68) (PDF pp. 203-205): the same derivation, in which equality (6.62) is left as an exercise (problem 6-7, PDF p. 204). The proof by steps 1, 2, 7 and 9 is ours.

What did the theorem say? The term \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q}\) is the part of the torque that is linear in the acceleration, and \(\vek g\) says what the arm needs for holding. The remaining term \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) contains only velocities and arises because the mass matrix changes with the configuration: the coefficients \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\) are made of derivatives of \(\mat M\). If \(\dot{\vek q} = \vek 0\) it vanishes, and for \(\ddot{\vek q} = \vek 0\) what remains is \(\vek g = \boldsymbol\tau + \mat J^\T\vek F\), which is exactly theorem 27.50. The terms with a squared velocity, \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}^2\), are according to SPONG (PDF p. 205) called centrifugal, from the Latin centrum and fugere, to flee from the centre: a body that moves in a circle departs from the circle by inertia. The terms with a product of two different velocities are according to the same source called Coriolis. They are named after a person, the reason for the name SPONG does not give, so we call them mixed terms.

27.8.4 Tests and use

Example 27.57 Equations of motion of arm A (gibalne enačbe roke A)

Arm A, \(\mat M\) from equation (27.35) and \(\vek g\) from equation (27.38).

  1. The matrix \(\mat M\) depends only on \(q_2\), so all derivatives with respect to \(q_1\) are zero. The derivatives with respect to \(q_2\) are \(\partial d_{11}/\partial q_2 = -\tfrac52\sin q_2 = 2h\), \(\partial d_{12}/\partial q_2 = -\tfrac54\sin q_2 = h\) and \(\partial d_{22}/\partial q_2 = 0\) with \(h = -m_2l_1l_{\mathrm{c}2}\sin q_2 = -1{,}25\sin q_2\) kg m\(^2\).
  2. By equation (27.39) the nonzero symbols are \(c_{121} = c_{211} = c_{221} = h\) and \(c_{112} = -h\). For example \(c_{112} = \tfrac12(\partial d_{21}/\partial q_1 + \partial d_{21}/\partial q_1 - \partial d_{11}/\partial q_2) = -\tfrac12\cdot 2h = -h\) and \(c_{221} = \tfrac12(\partial d_{12}/\partial q_2 + \partial d_{12}/\partial q_2 - \partial d_{22}/\partial q_1) = h\).
  3. The matrix \(C_{\mathrm{kj}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}}c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is
    \[\mat C(\vek q, \dot{\vek q}) = h\begin{pmatrix} \dot q_2 & \dot q_1 + \dot q_2 \\ -\dot q_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{27.41}\]
  4. The equations by components, with \(\vek F = \vek 0\):
    \[\begin{aligned} \tau_1 &= \big(\tfrac{35}{4} + \tfrac52\cos q_2\big)\ddot q_1 + \big(\tfrac5{12} + \tfrac54\cos q_2\big)\ddot q_2 + h\,(2\dot q_1\dot q_2 + \dot q_2^2) + 98{,}1\cos q_1 + 12{,}2625\cos(q_1 + q_2) , \\ \tau_2 &= \big(\tfrac5{12} + \tfrac54\cos q_2\big)\ddot q_1 + \tfrac5{12}\ddot q_2 - h\,\dot q_1^2 + 12{,}2625\cos(q_1 + q_2) . \end{aligned} \tag{27.42}\]

Source: The computation is ours (SymPy, num-08.py). SPONG (6.90)-(6.91) (PDF p. 210) gives the same equations in its own notation.

In the second equation stands \(-h\dot q_1^2 = 1{,}25\sin q_2\,\dot q_1^2\), the centrifugal term: when the first link turns with \(\dot q_1\), the mass of the second link moves away from \(O\) by inertia and would turn the second link about \(o_1\), so the torque of the motor of the second joint must balance this moment. In the first equation are the mixed term \(2h\dot q_1\dot q_2\) and the centrifugal term \(h\dot q_2^2\).

A test with the double pendulum of chapter 8. The equations contain more terms than can be checked by hand, so we compare the computation with an independent computation of the same system. The geometry is the same as in figure 27.71, except that frame 0 is turned and the masses are in the joint and at the tip. Take point masses on light rods with \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m as in example 8.50, \(m_1 = 10\) kg in the joint and \(m_2 = 5\) kg at the tip (the masses are ours, the example does not give them). Turn frame 0 so that the axis \(x_0\) points downward, and take \(q_1 = \theta_1\) and \(q_2 = \theta_2 - \theta_1\) (the relative angle, step 3 of example 8.50). At \(\theta_1 = 30^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 = 60^\circ\), so \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 30^\circ)\), the points are \(P_1 = (0{,}866,\ 0{,}5)\) m and \(P_2 = (1{,}116,\ 0{,}933)\) m in these axes, that is \((0{,}5000,\ -0{,}8660)\) m and \((0{,}9330,\ -1{,}1160)\) m in the axes of the example. The centres of mass are now in the joint and at the tip, \(\mat J_{\mathrm{G},\mathrm{k}} = \mat 0\), \(\vek e = -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}_0}\). Theorem 27.52 gives

\[\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 16{,}25 + 5\cos q_2 & 1{,}25 + 2{,}5\cos q_2 \\ 1{,}25 + 2{,}5\cos q_2 & 1{,}25 \end{pmatrix}\text{ kg m}^2 , \qquad \vek g = \begin{pmatrix} 147{,}15\sin q_1 + 24{,}525\sin(q_1 + q_2) \\ 24{,}525\sin(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix}\text{ N m} .\]

At \(q_2 = 30^\circ\), \(\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 20{,}580 & 3{,}415 \\ 3{,}415 & 1{,}25 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\vek g = (94{,}814,\ 21{,}239)\) N m and with \(h = -m_2l_1l_2\sin q_2 = -1{,}25\) kg m\(^2\) equation (27.41) (the same form) at \(\dot{\vek q} = (1,\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s gives \(\mat C = \begin{pmatrix} -0{,}625 & -1{,}875 \\ 1{,}25 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) kg m\(^2\)/s. For \(\boldsymbol\tau = \vek 0\) equation (27.40) gives the accelerations \(\ddot{\vek q} = (-2{,}8275,\ -10{,}2664)\) rad/s\(^2\). The same system in the angles \(\theta_1\), \(\theta_2\) of chapter 8: from \(T\) and \(V\) by theorem 8.101, without equation (27.40), the equations are (SymPy)

\[\begin{aligned} 15\ddot\theta_1 + 2{,}5\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\ddot\theta_2 + 2{,}5\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_2^2 + 147{,}15\sin\theta_1 &= 0 , \\ 2{,}5\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\ddot\theta_1 + 1{,}25\,\ddot\theta_2 - 2{,}5\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1^2 + 24{,}525\sin\theta_2 &= 0 . \end{aligned}\]

At \(\dot\theta = (1,\ 1{,}5)\) rad/s and accelerations \(\ddot\theta_1 = \ddot q_1 = -2{,}8275\) and \(\ddot\theta_2 = \ddot q_1 + \ddot q_2 = -13{,}0940\) rad/s\(^2\) (from \(\theta_2 = q_1 + q_2\)) the left sides are \(0{,}0007\) and \(0{,}0001\), so zero within rounding, and in SymPy to \(10^{-8}\). The matrices \(\mat M\), \(\mat C\) and \(\vek g\) of the theorem therefore give a motion that the independently derived equation in other coordinates confirms.

A case without the term \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\). Arm A shows that the velocity terms exist. Does every robot have them? Look at a robot whose links only slide, each along its own line. In figure 27.79 the guide is vertical and link 1 of mass \(m_1 = 10\) kg is threaded on it and slides up and down. Along it slides link 2 of mass \(m_2 = 5\) kg horizontally. The displacements \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are the joint variables. The motors act with the forces \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) along the guides, and on both centres of mass acts the weight. Both joints are prismatic, so \(\mat W_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat 0\) (the angular velocity is zero for a prismatic joint), and the columns of the matrices \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) are the joint axes: \(\vek z_0\) vertically upward and \(\vek z_1\) horizontal. So \(\mat A_1 = (\vek z_0,\ \vek 0)\) (the first link moves only vertically) and \(\mat A_2 = (\vek z_0,\ \vek z_1)\). Since \(\vek z_0\) and \(\vek z_1\) are perpendicular unit vectors, \(\mat A_1^\T\mat A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat A_2^\T\mat A_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Theorem 27.52 gives a constant matrix

\[\mat M = m_1\mat A_1^\T\mat A_1 + m_2\mat A_2^\T\mat A_2 = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 + m_2 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 15 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\text{ kg} .\]

All derivatives of \(\mat M\) are zero, so by equation (27.39) all \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}} = 0\) and \(\mat C = \mat 0\). By equation (27.37) with \(\vek e = \vek z_0\) the weight is \(\vek g = ((m_1 + m_2)g,\ 0) = (147{,}15,\ 0)\) N. The equations are \((m_1 + m_2)(\ddot q_1 + g) = f_1\) and \(m_2\ddot q_2 = f_2\). At \(\ddot{\vek q} = (2,\ -1)\) m/s\(^2\) we get \(f_1 = 177{,}15\) N and \(f_2 = -5\) N, which is exactly Newton. For this robot a forgotten term \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) would go unnoticed, because there is none. The reason is written in the formula: the \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\) are made of derivatives of \(\mat M\). Here \(\mat M\) is constant because the velocity of the centre of mass for each \(\dot q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the same in every configuration, so the \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) are constant. For a revolute joint the direction of the velocity turns with the configuration and \(\mat A_{\mathrm{k}}\) depends on \(\vek q\): for arm A the matrix \(\mat M\) is a function of \(q_2\) (figure 27.74).

The error that a forgotten term brings. Arm A at \(\vek q = (30^\circ, 60^\circ)\), \(\dot{\vek q} = (1,\ 0{,}5)\) rad/s, \(\ddot{\vek q} = (2,\ -1)\) rad/s\(^2\) (inverse dynamics, SymPy).

Example 27.58 Inverse dynamics of arm A (obratna dinamika roke A)

We look for the torques that give the stated motion, without a load.

  1. The mass matrix from example 27.53: \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 1{,}0417 \\ 1{,}0417 & 0{,}4167 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = (18{,}958,\ 1{,}667)\) N m.
  2. The velocity term: \(h = -1{,}25\sin 60^\circ = -1{,}0825\) kg m\(^2\), \(\mat C = h\begin{pmatrix} 0{,}5 & 1{,}5 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mat C\dot{\vek q} = h\,(0{,}5 + 0{,}75,\ -1) = (-1{,}353,\ 1{,}083)\) N m.
  3. The weight from equation (27.38): \(\vek g = (84{,}957,\ 0)\) N m.
  4. The sum: \(\boldsymbol\tau = (18{,}958 - 1{,}353 + 84{,}957,\ 1{,}667 + 1{,}083 + 0) = (102{,}562,\ 2{,}749)\) N m. If the arm rests in the same configuration, \(\boldsymbol\tau = \vek g = (84{,}957,\ 0)\) N m.
  5. The error if we leave out the term \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\) is exactly this term, \((-1{,}353,\ 1{,}083)\) N m, on the second motor \(1{,}083\) of \(2{,}749\) N m, that is \(39\) %. The matrix \(\mat C\) is linear in the velocity, so at \(s\) times the velocities \(\mat C(\vek q, s\dot{\vek q})\,s\dot{\vek q} = s^2\,\mat C\dot{\vek q}\): at twice the velocities the error is four times larger, at four times sixteen times larger: the length of the error, \(1{,}733\) N m, grows to \(1{,}733\cdot 16 = 27{,}7\) N m.

Source: The computation is ours (SymPy, num-08.py).

The velocity term is thus small in slow motion and grows quickly with the square of the velocity. So in fast motions we must not leave it out.

Let us also test a motion along a long path (figure 27.81). We turn the first link of arm A from \(0\) to \(90^\circ\) in one second and stop it at the start and at the end, and hold the second link at \(q_2 = 60^\circ\). A polynomial that goes from \(0\) to \(\tfrac\pi2\) in one second with zero velocity at both ends must satisfy four conditions. A quadratic cannot, because its velocity is linear and is zero at \(t = 0\) and \(t = 1\) only if it is zero everywhere. The lowest degree is therefore three, \(q_1(t) = \tfrac\pi2\,(3t^2 - 2t^3)\) with \(t\) in seconds. Its velocity is \(\dot q_1 = 3\pi\,t(1 - t)\) and its acceleration \(\ddot q_1 = 3\pi(1 - 2t)\) rad/s\(^2\). We compute the torques from equation (27.42) with \(\dot q_2 = \ddot q_2 = 0\) (SymPy, num-08.py).

The upper axes show time and the torque of the first motor. The blue curve shows \(\tau_1\), the dashed one the gravity part \(g_1\). At \(t = 0\), \(\tau_1 = 198{,}479\) N m: the arm is still at rest, so the velocity term is zero, and this is \(10\cdot 3\pi + 104{,}231\) (\(M_{11}\ddot q_1\) and \(g_1(0^\circ, 60^\circ)\)). The gravity part is thus only a good half. At the end \(\tau_1 = -104{,}87\) N m: the motor brakes, \(-94{,}25\) N m for the acceleration \(-3\pi\) rad/s\(^2\) and \(-10{,}62\) N m the gravity part at \(q_1 = 90^\circ\). The lower axes show time and the torque of the second motor. The second link does not turn, yet \(\tau_2\) is not zero, because the first one turns: \(\tau_2\) contains \(M_{21}\ddot q_1\), \(g_2\) and the centrifugal term \(-h\dot q_1^2\). The full curve is \(\tau_2\), highest \(15{,}949\) N m at \(t = 0\) and lowest \(-20{,}437\) N m at \(t = 1\). The dashed orange curve is \(\tau_2'\) without the term \(-h\dot q_1^2\). They differ most at \(t = 0{,}5\), where \(\ddot q_1 = 0\), \(\dot q_1 = 2{,}356\) rad/s, and \(\tau_2 = 2{,}84\) N m, \(\tau_2' = -3{,}17\) N m: the difference is \(-h\dot q_1^2 = 1{,}0825\cdot 5{,}55 = 6{,}01\) N m. So at \(t = 0{,}5\) the torques \(\tau_2\) and \(\tau_2'\) have opposite signs. A model that omitted the term would give the wrong direction.

The torques obtained in this way are exactly those that the motors must give if the model holds (idealizations 1 to 4). For direct dynamics we would solve from equation (27.40) the linear system for \(\ddot{\vek q}\) at a given \(\boldsymbol\tau\) (theorem 27.56) and integrate it step by step, which will serve us in section 27.9 for checking the equations at the free fall of the arm. By theorem 8.108 of chapter 8 the equation has for continuously differentiable \(\boldsymbol\tau(t)\) a solution on some interval around \(t_0\) that is unique for given \(\vek q(t_0)\) and \(\dot{\vek q}(t_0)\).

Equation (27.40) is Lagrange's equation. The other way is the Newton-Euler recursion (Newton-Euler formulation): for each link separately it writes the equations of motion of a rigid body (theorem 8.82) and computes the forces in the joints from the tip toward the base (SPONG, section 6.6, PDF pp. 220-230). It does not use the kinetic and potential energy (SPONG, PDF p. 220). That the two methods are equivalent SPONG states without proof (PDF p. 236, reference to source [152]), so we do not prove it.

Two properties of the equation we prove in section 27.9. The first is that the matrix \(\dot{\mat M} - 2\mat C\) is antisymmetric, which holds for \(\mat C\) made of the symbols \(c_{\mathrm{ijk}}\) and not for every matrix with the same column \(\mat C\dot{\vek q}\). The second are the bounds of the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat M\) for a robot with revolute joints only, because of which the inertia of such an arm never becomes infinite or zero.
