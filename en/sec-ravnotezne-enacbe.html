Chapter 2 Stress and equilibrium

2.7 Equilibrium of a small box

All three questions with which section 2.6 ended are about the stress at a single point. First, though, we must look at how the stresses at neighbouring points are related to each other. We will need this in every body in which we look for the stresses, and on the same small piece of a body we will answer the third question in the next section.

Can the stress change from point to point in any way at all? No. Let us cut a small piece out of a body in thought. The body is in equilibrium, so this piece is in equilibrium too (section 2.1). On it act the forces across its faces, which are set by the traction vectors on the faces, and the forces that act on all of its matter, like the weight. If the traction vectors on two opposite faces were exactly opposite, the forces across these two faces would balance each other, and nothing would carry the weight of the piece. So the stress must change from one face to the opposite one by exactly as much as is needed for the difference of the forces to carry the weight.

On 14 May 1821 Navier presented to the Paris Academy a memoir on the equilibrium and motion of solid bodies that return to their original shape after unloading (TIMO, PDF p. 113). He saw the body, as we said in chapter 1, as a set of particles with forces acting between them (figure 1.1). These are proportional to the change of distance between two particles and directed along the line joining them (TIMO, PDF p. 113). He added up the forces of all the particles in the neighbourhood of one particle and wrote the condition that together with an external force, for example weight, they are in equilibrium (TIMO, PDF p. 113 and 114). LOVE counts Navier's general equations of 1821 as one of the two greatest landmarks of the theory, besides Hooke's proportionality of force and extension (chapter 1), since with them all questions about small changes of shape were reduced to calculation (LOVE, PDF p. 19). Cauchy read Navier's memoir and began to work on the same question (TIMO, PDF p. 116). Instead of forces between particles he took the pressure on a surface, which he knew from hydrodynamics, and allowed that in a solid it need not be perpendicular to the surface (chapter 1, TIMO, PDF p. 116). He presented his work to the Academy on 30 September 1822 (TIMO, PDF p. 116). By the autumn of that year he had derived, for a small box, the equations of equilibrium that connect the stresses with the force per unit volume (LOVE, PDF p. 25, TIMO, PDF p. 117). On this path the forces between particles need not be known, the forces across the faces of a piece suffice. We shall follow the same path.

The goal of this section is to write this condition with numbers. For every interior point of a body we will derive three equations between the partial derivatives of the stress components and the force per unit volume that acts on the matter. The route is similar to the one for Cauchy's formula. First we write the condition for a slice of the hanging bar, where everything is simple and exact. Then we say exactly what a force that acts on all the matter of a body is. Then we cut a small cube out of a general body, write its equilibrium, divide it by the volume of the cube and let the cube shrink to a point. For this step we will need one more small tool from mathematics. Finally we test the equations on the hanging bar, on pure shear and on water at rest.

We will use only the first condition of equilibrium from definition 2.3, that the resultant of the forces is the zero vector. The second condition, that the forces do not turn the body, comes in the next section. As so far in this chapter, we do not distinguish the shape of the body before and after loading. In steel, which deforms by less than a thousandth, an edge of a cube with side 1 mm changes under loading by less than 1 µm. CM derives these equations in the same way and assumes small deformations explicitly (CM §4.5, PDF p. 198). What changes for large deformations we will see at the end of the chapter.

2.7.1 A slice of the hanging bar

Let us start with an example we know. The steel hanging bar has the length \(L = 10\) m, a round cross section with diameter 10 mm and area \(A = 7{,}854 \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2\), and the density of steel is \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³. The bar hangs under its own weight and is held in the idealized way of section 1.7.1: only the centre of the top face is fixed, and the weight is carried by a force that pulls the top face up evenly. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from the centre of the top face. In section 2.2 we found from the equilibrium of the lower part of the bar the stress in the cross section at the depth \(x\),

\[\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x) ,\]

the same at all points of the cross section (equation (2.5)).

Now let us go the other way: we do not know the stress, we know only the weight. We assume only, as everywhere for bars, that on the cross section at the depth \(x\) the traction vector for the normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is \(\sigma(x)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) at all points of the cross section, but we do not know the function \(\sigma\). We cut the bar in thought by the cross sections at the depths \(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\) and \(x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\) and look at the slice (rezina) between them, of thickness \(\Delta x\), with its centre at the depth \(x\) (figure 2.30). Every slice of the bar can be described in this way by its centre and its thickness. Which forces act on it?

  1. Across the top face the part of the bar above pulls it. The outward normal of the slice is \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) there, so by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut the traction vector there is \(-\sigma(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). It is the same at all points of the face, so the force is the area times it, \(-\sigma(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})\,A\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). It points up.
  2. Across the bottom face, with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), the part of the bar below pulls it with the force \(\sigma(x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})\,A\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points down.
  3. The mantle of the slice, its side surface, is part of the surface of the bar, on which nothing acts.
  4. The slice has the volume \(A\,\Delta x\), the mass \(\rho A\,\Delta x\) and by (2.1) the weight \(\rho g A\,\Delta x\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which points down.
  5. The slice is in equilibrium, so by (2.2) the sum of these forces is the zero vector. All the forces point along the axis \(x\), so we write only the component \(x\):
    \[-\sigma(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})\,A + \sigma(x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})\,A + \rho g A\,\Delta x = 0 .\]
  6. We divide the equation by the volume of the slice \(A\,\Delta x\):
    \[\frac{\sigma(x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}) - \sigma(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2})}{\Delta x} + \rho g = 0 . \tag{2.25}\]

The equation says that the stress on the bottom face of the slice is smaller than on the top face, and that the difference, divided by the thickness of the slice, is exactly the weight per unit volume, \(\rho g\). Why? The part of the bar above the slice must carry everything that the part below carries, and the weight of the slice besides. We made this argument already for the piece of a bar in figure 1.12. For steel

\[\rho g = 7800\ \frac{\text{kg}}{\text{m}^3} \cdot 9{,}81\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 76\,518\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^3} ,\]

so the stress falls by 76 518 Pa, that is by \(0{,}0765\) MPa, for every metre of depth. Over the 10 m from the top to the bottom this gives \(0{,}765\) MPa, exactly the stress at the top from section 2.2. Figure 2.30 shows the forces to scale for the slice from the depth \(2L/5\) to the depth \(3L/5\). It is pulled up by the force \(36{,}06\) N and down by \(24{,}04\) N, its weight is \(12{,}02\) N, and indeed \(36{,}06 = 24{,}04 + 12{,}02\).

Let us check the equation with the stress \(\sigma(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\). The numerator in (2.25) is

\[\sigma(x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}) - \sigma(x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}) = \rho g\,(L - x - \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}) - \rho g\,(L - x + \tfrac{\Delta x}{2}) = -\rho g\,\Delta x ,\]

and divided by \(\Delta x\) it gives \(-\rho g\). Equation (2.25) holds for every thickness of the slice, not only for a small one, because in the bar the stress is the same over every cross section and nothing acts on the mantle. So it even gives us the stress itself. Take the slice from any depth \(x_1\) to the lower end of the bar. The bottom face of this slice is the free end of the bar, where \(\sigma(L) = 0\). By (2.25) the difference of the stresses divided by the thickness is \(-\rho g\):

\[\frac{0 - \sigma(x_1)}{L - x_1} = -\rho g , \qquad \text{so} \qquad \sigma(x_1) = \rho g\,(L - x_1) .\]

This is the same calculation as in section 2.1, only written with the stress.

And when the slice is very thin? The first term of (2.25) is the difference of the values of the function \(\sigma\) at two points that lie symmetrically about \(x\), divided by the distance between them. Below we will show that such a quotient approaches the derivative \(\sigma'(x)\) as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero. The equation then reads \(\sigma'(x) + \rho g = 0\): the rate at which the stress changes with depth and the weight per unit volume add up to zero. This form no longer speaks about a slice but about a single depth \(x\). We will get exactly this form in a general body. There, however, is more work. The stress changes also over the faces of a piece, forces act across all of its faces, not only across two, and they do not point only along one axis. Before we take this on, let us say exactly what a force is that acts, like the weight, on all the matter.

2.7.2 Body force

The weight of the slice was proportional to its volume: \(\rho g\) times the volume. The weight acts on every bit of matter separately, not only on the surface of the body. A force with this property has its own name in books, as we mentioned already in section 1.2. A force that acts only on the surface of a body, for example the pull of a rope or the push of water on a wall, is a surface force (površinska sila) (SADD §3.1, PDF p. 63). A surface force at a point of the surface is described by the traction vector, the force per unit area (section 2.5). A force that acts on all the matter is described in a similar way, by the force per unit volume. For the traction vector we shrank patches around the point, here we will shrink cubes. Figure 2.31 shows both kinds of force.

Definition 2.26 Body force (prostorninska sila)

A body force (prostorninska sila) is a force with which something outside a body acts on all of its matter, on every bit of it separately. Let \(P\) be an interior point of the body and \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. For \(h > 0\) let \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}\) be the body force on the matter in the cube with centre \(P\), side \(h\) and edges parallel to the axes. The body force density (gostota prostorninske sile) at the point \(P\) is the vector

\[\vek b(P) = \lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}}{h^3} , \tag{2.26}\]

if this limit from the right (definition 1.8) exists. The limit of a vector is taken component by component.

Source: SADD §3.1, (3.1.1) (PDF p. 63), BOWER §2.2.1 (PDF pp. 66-67), CM §5.3.1.1, (5.3.4) (PDF pp. 226-227). BOWER writes the force per unit mass with a limit but does not give the shape of the pieces. SADD and CM introduce the density as a function from which the force on a part of the body follows by summation over its volume. The shrinking cubes are not from the sources.

The number \(h^3\) is the volume of the cube, so \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}/h^3\) is the average force per unit volume in the cube, just as \(\Delta\vek F/\Delta A\) was the average force per unit area on a patch. As we shrink the cube around \(P\), the average approaches the force per unit volume at the point \(P\). The unit is N/m³. The point must be interior so that for small \(h\) the whole cube lies in the body. We chose cubes because we will need them in the equilibrium below. BOWER only writes the limit down and does not say what shape the pieces have that it shrinks around the point (BOWER, PDF p. 67). SADD and CM do not define the density by a limit but as a function from which the force on a part of the body follows by exact summation over its volume (SADD (3.1.1), CM (5.3.4)). We will meet such summation in chapter 4.

Let us test the definition on the weight. Let the body have the same density \(\rho\) everywhere. A cube with side \(h\) has the mass \(\rho h^3\) and by (2.1) a weight of magnitude \(\rho g h^3\), pointing down. If the axis \(x\) points down, as for the hanging bar, then \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}} = \rho g h^3\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and

\[\frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}}{h^3} = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\]

for every \(h\). The quotient is the same vector for all cubes, so its limit is this vector too: \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). For steel its magnitude is \(76\,518\) N/m³. A cube of steel with side 1 cm has the volume \(10^{-6}\) m³ and weighs \(0{,}0765\) N. For a body with the same density everywhere we would get the same vector also if we shrank pieces of another shape. Other body forces are electric and magnetic forces (BOWER §2.2.1, PDF p. 66).

Books write the body force in different ways. SADD writes the force per unit volume, as we do, with the letter \(\vek F\) (SADD (3.1.1), PDF p. 63). CM and BOWER write the force per unit mass, \(\vek f\) and \(\vek b\), and multiply it by the density (CM, PDF p. 226, BOWER, PDF p. 67). So our \(\vek b\) is \(\rho\,\vek f\) in CM and \(\rho\,\vek b\) in BOWER. Where there is no doubt, we also call \(\vek b\) simply the body force.

2.7.3 Forces on a small cube

Now take a general body in equilibrium and a point \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) in its interior, in a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. Around \(P\) we cut out in thought a cube with centre \(P\), side \(h\) and edges parallel to the axes (figure 2.32). Since \(P\) is an interior point, for small enough \(h\) the whole cube lies in the body. The title of the section speaks of a rectangular box (kvader), a body bounded by six rectangles of which each two opposite ones are equal and parallel. A cube is a box with equal edges. CM takes a box with three different edges (CM (4.5.1), PDF p. 198). We take a cube because its size is given by a single number \(h\). When we shrink it, we will therefore compute a limit of a function of one number, as definition 1.5 knows it. The point \(P\) is the centre of the cube so that the cube shrinks towards \(P\) equally from all sides.

The cube has six faces, each perpendicular to one of the axes (figure 2.32). The face on the side of larger \(x\) has the outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the centre \(Q_{+\mathrm{x}} = P + \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The opposite face has the outward normal \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and the centre \(Q_{-\mathrm{x}} = P - \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the same way \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{y}} = P \pm \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{z}} = P \pm \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) are the centres of the faces with outward normals \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). We denote the force with which the rest of the body acts on the cube across the face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) by \(\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}\), the force across the opposite face by \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}\), and likewise \(\Delta\vek F_{\pm \mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Delta\vek F_{\pm \mathrm{z}}\). The index gives the outward normal of the face, as for the tetrahedron in section 2.6.2. On the matter in the cube acts also the body force \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}\). The cube is part of a body in equilibrium, so it is in equilibrium itself, and by (2.2)

\[\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}} + \Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{y}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}} + \Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{z}} + \Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}} + \Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}} = \vek 0 . \tag{2.27}\]

How large are these forces? Let us first compute them in the hanging bar, where we can (figure 2.33). The bar is made of steel, 10 m long, with diameter 10 mm, it hangs under its own weight and is held in the idealized way, the axis \(x\) points down from the centre of the top face. Take the point \(P = (5\ \text{m}, 4\ \text{mm}, 0)\) from section 1.7.1, at the middle of the length and 1 mm below the surface of the bar, and the cube with centre \(P\) and side \(h = 1\) mm. In the idealized model the only stress component different from zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) (CM Example 7.3.2, equation (2), PDF p. 305, where \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \rho g\,z\) with the axis \(z\) pointing up from the lower end, so \(z = L - x\)). CM takes a bar with a rectangular cross section. The shape of the section does not enter the solution: the stress does not depend on the dimensions of the section, and on the side surface of the bar, where the normal has the component \(x\) equal to zero, Cauchy's formula gives a zero traction vector for every shape of the section. So the solution holds for our round bar too. The traction vectors on the faces perpendicular to the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are therefore zero, and there are no forces across the side faces of the cube. On the top face, at the depth \(x_{\mathrm{P}} - h/2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is the same at all points of the face, so the part of the bar above the cube pulls the cube up with a force of magnitude \(h^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{-\mathrm{x}})\). The part of the bar below the cube pulls it down with a force of magnitude \(h^2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}})\). The weight of the cube is \(\rho g h^3\). With NumPy we computed:

\(h\) force on the top face difference of the forces on the top and the bottom face weight of the cube difference divided by the force on the top face
1 mm \(0{,}3826\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-5}\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-5}\) N \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
0,1 mm \(3{,}826 \cdot 10^{-3}\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-8}\) N \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-5}\)
0,01 mm \(3{,}826 \cdot 10^{-5}\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-11}\) N \(7{,}652 \cdot 10^{-11}\) N \(2{,}0 \cdot 10^{-6}\)

The forces on the top and the bottom face are almost equal and opposite (figure 2.33, right). Their difference is, for \(h = 1\) mm, only two ten-thousandths of each of them, and this difference is exactly the weight of the cube. When we make \(h\) smaller by a factor of 10, the forces on the faces become smaller by a factor of 100, because they are proportional to the area \(h^2\). The difference and the weight, however, become smaller by a factor of 1000. Why the difference? The two faces are a distance \(h\) apart, the stress changes between them by \(\rho g h\), and so the difference of the forces is \(\rho g h \cdot h^2 = \rho g h^3\). The weight is proportional to the volume, \(h^3\). Here lies the essential difference from the tetrahedron in section 2.6.2. There the weight was proportional to \(h^3\) and the forces across the faces to \(h^2\), so the weight disappeared for a small tetrahedron. For the cube the two forces across opposite faces almost cancel, what remains is a difference proportional to \(h^3\), and it is as large as the weight. When we divide the equilibrium by the volume \(h^3\), both will remain.

In a general body the traction vectors at different points are different. So far we wrote the traction vector as \(\vek t(\vek n)\) and left the point out, because we computed everything at one point. Now we compare traction vectors at different points, so we write the point: \(\vek t(Q, \vek n)\) is the traction vector at the point \(Q\) on a face with unit normal \(\vek n\). SADD also stresses that the traction vector depends on the point and on the normal (SADD §3.2, PDF p. 65). The stress components at every point \(Q\) of the body are nine numbers, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q)\) and so on. As the point \(Q\) runs through the body, we get nine functions of the point, which, like the components of the displacement, we call fields. By (2.23), for example,

\[\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = \big(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q),\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q),\ \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(Q)\big) .\]

Figure 2.34 shows the components \(x\) of the forces on all six faces of the cube as they would be if the traction vector over the whole face were equal to the one at its centre. On the faces with outward normals \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) they are the normal components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) times the area \(h^2\). On the faces with outward normals \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) they are the shear components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), the components \(x\) of the traction vector on the face perpendicular to the axis \(y\). On the faces with outward normals \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) they are the shear components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\). On the faces with outward normals \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) the components point the opposite ways, because there the traction vector is opposite by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut.

But how large is the force across a face really? If the traction vector were the same at all points of the face, the force would be the area of the face times it, as for the cross section of a bar. In a general body, however, the traction vector changes a little from point to point of the face. Let us first see what happens when it changes linearly, that is, so that a component, for example \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), changes by \(w\) times a fixed number \(k\) when we move along the face by the distance \(w\) in a chosen direction (figure 2.35, left). Take the face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and centre \(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}\). Two points of the face that lie symmetrically about the centre then have the values \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) + k w\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - k w\). Together they carry as much as two points with the value \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}})\) would, since \((\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) + k w) + (\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - k w) = 2\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}})\). The face splits into such pairs of small patches, symmetric about the centre. So the force is exactly the area times the value at the centre. As much as one half of the face carries more, the other half carries less.

A field that does not change linearly is, however, linear near the point \(P\) up to a small remainder (figure 2.35, right). Let us assume that the stress components are continuously differentiable fields (definition 1.90), that is, that they have partial derivatives which change from point to point without jumps. Physically this means that the stress in the body changes smoothly. Where this assumption fails we saw for displacements in section 1.15: at a concentrated force, at the tip of a crack and at the interface of two materials. By theorem 1.88 on the change of a field over a short step we then have for the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\)

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q) &= \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(P) + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x}(P)\,(x_{\mathrm{Q}} - x_{\mathrm{P}}) \\ &\quad + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial y}(P)\,(y_{\mathrm{Q}} - y_{\mathrm{P}}) + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial z}(P)\,(z_{\mathrm{Q}} - z_{\mathrm{P}}) + R(Q) , \end{aligned}\]

where \(Q\) is a point near \(P\), and for every chosen error \(\eta > 0\) the remainder is at most \(\eta\,|PQ|\) when \(Q\) is close enough to \(P\). The sum of the first four terms changes linearly with the coordinates of the point \(Q\). Of the points of the cube, the corners are farthest from \(P\), for example \(P + (\tfrac h2, \tfrac h2, \tfrac h2)\), which by (1.10) is at the distance \(\sqrt{(\tfrac h2)^2 + (\tfrac h2)^2 + (\tfrac h2)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,h = 0{,}866\,h\). This is less than \(h\), so in the cube the remainder is less than \(\eta h\). The linear part gives on every face exactly its value at the centre, as we saw above. The remainder changes the average over the face by less than \(\eta h\) and the value at the centre of the face by less than \(\eta h\). So the average force per unit area differs from the traction vector at the centre of the face by less than \(2\eta h\) in each component. Since \(\eta\) is arbitrarily small, the difference is much smaller than \(h\).

This argument added up the force on a face from the forces on small patches into which we cut the face. The book does not have such exact adding up yet (SADD (3.1.2), PDF p. 64, BOWER, PDF p. 66). We will meet it for functions of one variable in chapter 3 and over a surface and over a body in chapter 4, section 4.2. Until then we take the argument as an assumption on the forces on a small cube. For the face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) it reads

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac1h \left( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} - \vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) \right) = \vek 0 , \tag{2.28}\]

and the same for the other five faces, each with its outward normal and its centre. The limit is a limit from the right, since \(h > 0\), and we take it component by component. The vector \(\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}/h^2\) is the average force per unit area on the face, and the assumption says that it differs from the traction vector at the centre of the face by much less than \(h\).

Why exactly by much less than \(h\)? Because the difference between the traction vectors on opposite faces is itself of the size \(h\): the faces are a distance \(h\) apart, and in the hanging bar \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) changed between them by \(\rho g h\). An error comparable with \(h\) could hide this difference completely. CM takes for the force on each face of the box the stress on the face times the area and says nothing about the error (CM (4.5.1), PDF p. 198). SADD derives the equations by exact adding up over a part of the body and over its surface, and leaves the derivation with a small box to the exercises (SADD §3.7, PDF pp. 77-78).

Now we divide equation (2.27) by the volume of the cube \(h^3\). We group the forces on opposite faces in pairs and write each pair with the two average forces per unit area:

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac1h \Big( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} + \frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} \Big) + \frac1h \Big( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{y}}}{h^2} + \frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{y}}}{h^2} \Big) \\ &\qquad + \frac1h \Big( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{z}}}{h^2} + \frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{z}}}{h^2} \Big) + \frac{\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}}{h^3} = \vek 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{2.29}\]

The first term says by how much the average forces per unit area on the opposite faces perpendicular to the axis \(x\) fail to cancel, divided by the distance \(h\) between the faces. The second and the third term say the same for the faces perpendicular to the axes \(y\) and \(z\). The last term is the average body force per unit volume in the cube, which by definition 2.26 approaches \(\vek b(P)\).

Where does the first term go as \(h\) approaches zero? By the assumption (2.28) it is close to the vector \(\big(\vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + \vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\big)/h\), and by lemma 2.21 this equals \(\big(\vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) - \vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\big)/h\). Its component \(x\) is

\[\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x_{\mathrm{P}} + \tfrac h2,\ y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{P}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(x_{\mathrm{P}} - \tfrac h2,\ y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{P}})}{h} ,\]

of the same form as the first term of (2.25). It is the difference of the values of a function at two points that lie symmetrically about \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\), divided by the distance between them.

2.7.4 Mathematics: the central difference quotient

We defined the derivative with the difference quotient (1.3), which compares the value of a function at the point \(x\) and at the point \(x + \Delta x\) beside it. For the slice and for the cube, however, the point we care about lies in the middle between the points at which we compare the values. Does such a quotient also approach the derivative? The idea is simple. We split the step from \(x - \tfrac h2\) to \(x + \tfrac h2\) into a step back from \(x\) and a step forward from \(x\). Each gives an ordinary difference quotient, which approaches the derivative, and our quotient is their average.

In his Methodus incrementorum directa et inversa, which appeared in the years 1715 to 1717, Brook Taylor created the calculus of finite differences (račun končnih razlik), that is the calculus with differences of function values at points a finite distance apart (CAJORI, PDF p. 242). Among other things he used it for the form of motion of a vibrating string, which he was the first to try to reduce to mechanical principles (CAJORI, PDF p. 242). Euler developed the calculus of finite differences in the first chapters of his book Institutiones calculi differentialis, which appeared in 1755 (CAJORI, PDF p. 249 and 254-255). From it he derived the calculus with derivatives (CAJORI, PDF p. 255). For Euler the derivative arose from differences, as in this section we obtain it from the quotient.

Lemma 2.27 Central difference quotient (centralni diferenčni količnik)

Let the function \(f\) of one variable be differentiable at the point \(x\). Then the central difference quotient (centralni diferenčni količnik) approaches the derivative:

\[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} \frac{f(x + \tfrac h2) - f(x - \tfrac h2)}{h} = f'(x) . \tag{2.30}\]
Proof

We must show that the quotient on the left approaches the number \(f'(x)\). We will write it as the average of an ordinary difference quotient with a step \(\tfrac h2\) forward and an ordinary difference quotient with a step \(\tfrac h2\) back.

  1. The function \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), so the limit of the difference quotient exists (definition 1.9). By the definition of the limit 1.5 the quotient \(q(\Delta x) = \big(f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)\big)/\Delta x\) is therefore defined for all \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < r\) for some \(r > 0\), and \(\lim_{\Delta \mathrm{x} \to 0} q(\Delta x) = f'(x)\). So for \(0 < |h| < 2r\) both values \(f(x + \tfrac h2)\) and \(f(x - \tfrac h2)\) are defined.
  2. We subtract and add \(f(x)\) in the numerator of the central quotient and split the fraction into two:
    \[\frac{f(x + \tfrac h2) - f(x - \tfrac h2)}{h} = \frac{f(x + \tfrac h2) - f(x)}{h} + \frac{f(x) - f(x - \tfrac h2)}{h} .\]
  3. In the denominator of the first fraction \(h = 2 \cdot \tfrac h2\), so the first fraction equals \(\tfrac12 \cdot \big(f(x + \tfrac h2) - f(x)\big)/\tfrac h2 = \tfrac12\,q(\tfrac h2)\).
  4. We multiply the numerator and the denominator of the second fraction by \(-1\). The numerator becomes \(f(x - \tfrac h2) - f(x)\) and the denominator \(-h = 2 \cdot (-\tfrac h2)\). So the second fraction is \(\tfrac12 \cdot \big(f(x - \tfrac h2) - f(x)\big)/(-\tfrac h2) = \tfrac12\,q(-\tfrac h2)\). The central quotient is therefore \(\tfrac12\,q(\tfrac h2) + \tfrac12\,q(-\tfrac h2)\), the average of the quotients with the step forward and back.
  5. Let us show that \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} q(\tfrac h2) = f'(x)\). We choose an error \(\eta > 0\). By step 1 there is a band \(\delta' > 0\) such that \(|q(\Delta x) - f'(x)| < \eta\) for every \(\Delta x\) with \(0 < |\Delta x| < \delta'\). We take \(\delta = 2\delta'\). For every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) we have \(0 < |\tfrac h2| < \delta'\), so \(|q(\tfrac h2) - f'(x)| < \eta\).
  6. In the same way \(\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0} q(-\tfrac h2) = f'(x)\), since for \(0 < |h| < \delta\) also \(0 < |{-\tfrac h2}| < \delta'\).
  7. By lemma 2.23 on the limit of a sum with the numbers \(c_1 = c_2 = \tfrac12\) the central quotient approaches the number \(\tfrac12\,f'(x) + \tfrac12\,f'(x) = f'(x)\).

Source: CHK (6.2) (PDF p. 104), where the central difference quotient is an approximation of a partial derivative. The proof is not from the source. The error orders \(h\) and \(h^2\) quoted for the lemma are stated by CHK (PDF pp. 103-104) without a condition, and LEBL1 thm. 4.3.2 (PDF p. 172) proves them with the remainder if the third derivative is bounded. The lemma itself asks for less, only differentiability at \(x\), and also claims less, since it speaks only of the limit.

Let us test the lemma on the displacement of the hanging bar, \(u(x) = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\), whose derivative is \(u'(x) = a\,(2L - 2x)\) (section 1.3.4). The numerator of the central quotient is

\[u(x + \tfrac h2) - u(x - \tfrac h2) = a\,\big(2L\,h - (x + \tfrac h2)^2 + (x - \tfrac h2)^2\big) ,\]

because the terms \(2Lx\) cancel and \(2L \cdot \tfrac h2 + 2L \cdot \tfrac h2 = 2L\,h\) remains. We write out the squares as products of brackets: \((x + \tfrac h2)^2 = x^2 + x\,h + \tfrac{h^2}{4}\) and \((x - \tfrac h2)^2 = x^2 - x\,h + \tfrac{h^2}{4}\). Their difference is \(2x\,h\), so the numerator is \(a\,(2L\,h - 2x\,h)\), and divided by \(h\) it gives

\[\frac{u(x + \tfrac h2) - u(x - \tfrac h2)}{h} = a\,(2L - 2x) = u'(x)\]

for every \(h\), not only in the limit. The ordinary difference quotient, by (1.6), was \(a\,(2L - 2x) - a\,\Delta x\) and differed from the derivative by \(a\,\Delta x\). Why is the central quotient exact here? For a parabola the chord between two points that lie symmetrically about \(x\) is parallel to the tangent at \(x\) (figure 2.36, left). For other functions it is not quite parallel, but its slope approaches the slope of the tangent as \(h\) gets smaller, as the lemma says (figure 2.36, right). Because the error of the central quotient shrinks faster than the error of the ordinary one, numerical methods use it as an approximation of the derivative. For functions with a bounded third derivative near \(x\), the error of the ordinary quotient falls like \(h\), the error of the central one like \(h^2\). CHK states this without a condition on the derivatives: the error is \(O(h)\) and \(O(h^2)\) (CHK, text before and after (6.2), PDF pp. 103-104). The condition comes from Taylor's theorem with remainder (LEBL1 thm. 4.3.2, PDF p. 172). If \(|f'''| \le M\) near \(x\), it gives an error of at most \(M h^2/24\) for the central quotient, and for the ordinary quotient a bounded second derivative is enough, \(|f''| \le M_2\), with an error of at most \(M_2 |\Delta x|/2\). For a function that is only differentiable at \(x\), the lemma says only that the error goes to zero, not how fast.

The converse does not hold. The function \(f(x) = |x|\) has at the point \(0\) the central quotient \(\big(|\tfrac h2| - |{-\tfrac h2}|\big)/h = 0\) for every \(h\), so it approaches zero. But it has no derivative at \(0\), because the ordinary difference quotient \(|\Delta x|/\Delta x\) equals \(1\) for \(\Delta x > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(\Delta x < 0\). This is why the lemma requires that the derivative exists.

Two more consequences matter for us. First: a partial derivative is the derivative of the function of one variable that we get when we hold the other two variables fixed (definition 1.25). If a function of three variables \(f\) has a partial derivative with respect to \(x\) at the point \(P\), then by the lemma

\[\frac{f(x_{\mathrm{P}} + \tfrac h2,\ y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{P}}) - f(x_{\mathrm{P}} - \tfrac h2,\ y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{P}})}{h}\]

approaches \(\partial f/\partial x\) at \(P\), and likewise with respect to \(y\) and to \(z\). Second: the lemma speaks of the limit as \(h\) goes to zero from both sides. The condition for \(0 < |h| < \delta\) contains the condition for \(0 < h < \delta\), so with the same band the quotient approaches the derivative also from the right. We need this for the cube, where \(h > 0\). For the slice of the hanging bar the lemma says that the first term of (2.25) approaches \(\sigma'(x)\) as \(\Delta x\) goes to zero, when \(\sigma\) is differentiable at \(x\). So by lemma 2.23 on the limit of a sum the left side of (2.25) approaches \(\sigma'(x) + \rho g\). But it is zero for every \(\Delta x\), so by the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7) \(\sigma'(x) + \rho g = 0\).

2.7.5 The equilibrium equations

Now we can let \(h\) approach zero in (2.29). The idea of the proof is this. Each pair of opposite faces gives a central difference quotient of the components of a traction vector, which by lemma 2.27 approaches their partial derivatives at \(P\). The error that the assumption on the forces on the faces allows disappears on the way. The last term approaches the body force \(\vek b(P)\). The left side is the zero vector for every \(h\), so the sum of the limits is the zero vector too. The theorem lists exactly the assumptions that the proof uses. Assumption 1 holds when the stress components are continuously differentiable fields, assumption 2 holds for the weight, and assumption 3 we justified by the argument about the linear part of the field, which we will make fully exact only in chapter 4.

Theorem 2.28 Equilibrium equations (ravnotežne enačbe)

Let a body be in equilibrium, \(P = (x_{\mathrm{P}}, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\) a point in its interior and \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) the axes of a rectangular Cartesian coordinate system. For \(h > 0\) we consider the cube with centre \(P\), side \(h\) and edges parallel to the axes. Assume:

  1. The stress components have partial derivatives with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) at the point \(P\).
  2. The body force density \(\vek b(P) = (b_{\mathrm{x}}, b_{\mathrm{y}}, b_{\mathrm{z}})\) exists at the point \(P\) (definition 2.26).
  3. On every face of the cube the average force per unit area, that is, the force across the face divided by the area \(h^2\), differs from the traction vector at the centre of the face by much less than \(h\). For the face with outward normal \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) this means
    \[\lim_{\mathrm{h} \to 0^+} \frac1h \left( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} - \vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) \right) = \vek 0 ,\]
    where \(\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}\) is the force with which the rest of the body acts on the cube across this face, \(Q_{+\mathrm{x}} = P + \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the centre of the face, and \(\vek t(Q, \vek n)\) is the traction vector at the point \(Q\) on a face with unit normal \(\vek n\). The limit is a limit from the right, taken component by component. The same holds for the faces with outward normals \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\pm\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and centres \(Q_{-\mathrm{x}} = P - \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{y}} = P \pm \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{z}} = P \pm \tfrac h2\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\).

Then at the point \(P\)

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{y}} &= 0 , \\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{z}} &= 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{2.31}\]

where all the partial derivatives are taken at the point \(P\).

Proof

We must show three equalities at the point \(P\). We get them all at once, as the three components of one vector equality. We divide the equilibrium of the cube by its volume and let \(h\) approach zero.

  1. The point \(P\) is in the interior of the body, so for small enough \(h\) the whole cube lies in the body. The cube is part of a body in equilibrium, so the resultant of the forces on it is the zero vector, and (2.27) holds. We use only this, the first part of the condition of equilibrium in definition 2.3.
  2. We divide the equation by the volume of the cube \(h^3\) and group the forces on opposite faces in pairs. We get (2.29), which holds for every small enough \(h > 0\).
  3. In the first term of (2.29) we subtract and add \(\vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\) to the vector \(\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}/h^2\), and \(\vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\) to the vector \(\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}/h^2\). By the rules of calculation with vectors (lemma 1.15) the first term equals
    \[\frac{\vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) + \vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})}{h} + \frac1h \Big( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{+\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} - \vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) \Big) + \frac1h \Big( \frac{\Delta\vek F_{-\mathrm{x}}}{h^2} - \vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) \Big) .\]
  4. By assumption 3 the last two vectors of step 3 approach the zero vector as \(h\) approaches zero.
  5. By lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, \(\vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, -\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) = -\vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\). So the first vector of step 3 is \(\big(\vek t(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}}) - \vek t(Q_{-\mathrm{x}}, \vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\big)/h\). By (2.23) its components are
    \[\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(Q_{-\mathrm{x}})}{h} , \qquad \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}(Q_{-\mathrm{x}})}{h} , \qquad \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(Q_{+\mathrm{x}}) - \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(Q_{-\mathrm{x}})}{h} .\]
  6. The points \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{x}} = (x_{\mathrm{P}} \pm \tfrac h2,\ y_{\mathrm{P}},\ z_{\mathrm{P}})\) differ from \(P\) only in the coordinate \(x\). Each component of step 5 is therefore the central difference quotient of a function of one variable, for example \(s \mapsto \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}(s, y_{\mathrm{P}}, z_{\mathrm{P}})\), at the point \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\). The derivative of this function at \(x_{\mathrm{P}}\) is the partial derivative \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x\) at \(P\) (definition 1.25), which exists by assumption 1. By lemma 2.27 on the central difference quotient the components therefore approach \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x\), \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\) and \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x\) at \(P\). The lemma speaks of the limit from both sides, so it holds also for the right-hand limit.
  7. By lemma 2.23 on the limit of a sum, used on each component separately, the first term of (2.29) therefore approaches, by steps 3 to 6, the vector
    \[\Big( \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x} \Big) .\]
  8. We treat the second and the third term in the same way. The points \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{y}}\) differ from \(P\) only in the coordinate \(y\), the points \(Q_{\pm \mathrm{z}}\) only in the coordinate \(z\), and the components of the vectors \(\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{y}})\) and \(\vek t(Q, \vek e_{\mathrm{z}})\) are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). So the second term approaches the vector \((\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y,\ \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial y,\ \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y)\) and the third the vector \((\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z,\ \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}/\partial z,\ \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z)\).
  9. The last term \(\Delta\vek F_{\mathrm{V}}/h^3\) approaches the vector \(\vek b(P)\) by assumption 2 and definition 2.26.
  10. By the lemma on the limit of a sum the left side of (2.29) approaches the sum of the four vectors of steps 7, 8 and 9. The left side is the zero vector for every small enough \(h\), so its limit is the zero vector. By the uniqueness of the limit (lemma 1.7), which also holds for the limit from the right (step 5 of the proof of lemma 2.21), the two limits are equal:
    \[\Big( \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}}{\partial x} \Big) + \Big( \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}}{\partial y} \Big) + \Big( \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}}{\partial z} \Big) + \vek b(P) = \vek 0 .\]
  11. Vectors are added component by component (definition 1.14). The component \(x\) of this equality is the first equation of (2.31), the component \(y\) the second, the component \(z\) the third.

Source: CM (4.5.1)-(4.5.5) (PDF p. 198), with the indices swapped, SADD (3.7.4), (3.7.5) (PDF p. 78), BOWER §2.3.1 (PDF pp. 76-77). Assumption 3 and the proof with the central difference quotient are not from the sources.

The proof showed where each term comes from. The pair of opposite faces perpendicular to the axis \(x\) gives the rate at which the traction vector \(\vek t(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}})\) changes in the direction of the axis \(x\), since the two faces are a distance apart in this direction. Likewise for the axes \(y\) and \(z\). The body force on the matter of the cube, divided by the volume, gives \(\vek b\). Besides the new lemma on the central quotient we used only the two tools that we needed already for Cauchy's formula: the lemma on the limit of a sum and the uniqueness of the limit.

What do the equations mean? Each is the first condition of equilibrium, written per unit volume at a single point. The sum of the first three terms is the force with which the neighbouring matter acts on a small cube around \(P\), divided by the volume of the cube. It is the part of the forces on opposite faces that does not cancel. The fourth term is the body force per unit volume. So the stress cannot change from point to point in any way at all. Where there is no body force, the changes in the three directions must balance each other: if, for example, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) grows in the direction of the axis \(x\), then \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) must fall in the direction of the axis \(y\) or \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) in the direction of the axis \(z\).

The pattern of the indices is worth noticing. In the equation for the component \(x\) all the stress components have the second index \(x\), because they are the components \(x\) of the traction vectors on the three faces. Each is differentiated with respect to the coordinate named by its first index, that is, in the direction of the normal of its face. With the indices \(i\) and \(j\) running over \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\), and with \(x_{\mathrm{j}}\) for the coordinate named \(j\) (\(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\), \(x_{\mathrm{z}} = z\)), as in section 1.17.1, all three equations can be written as

\[\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ji}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{j}}} + b_{\mathrm{i}} = 0 , \qquad i = x, y, z ,\]

where \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\) is, as in section 1.16.2, the sum of three terms, one for each of \(j = x\), \(j = y\) and \(j = z\). This is how SADD writes the equilibrium equations, with the letter \(F_{\mathrm{i}}\) for \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) (SADD (3.7.4), (3.7.5), PDF p. 78), and how BOWER writes them, with the force per unit mass (BOWER, PDF pp. 76-77). CM has the indices swapped, \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{j}} + \rho_0 f_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), because in CM \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}}\) means the component \(i\) on the face perpendicular to the axis \(j\) (CM (4.5.5), PDF p. 198). When we show in the next section that \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\), this difference will disappear.

Where do the equations hold? At interior points of a body that is at rest or moves without acceleration, where the assumptions of the theorem hold. At a concentrated force, at the tip of a crack or at the interface of two materials the stress components are not continuously differentiable, and the equations do not hold there in this form. On the surface of the body we do not write them, because a cube around a point of the surface does not lie in the body. There the traction vector is set by the force of the surroundings per unit area (section 2.5). Let us also say what the equations cannot do. For nine stress components there are only three equations, and after the next section only three for six different components. So on their own they do not determine the stress. The missing equations come from the properties of the material, which relate stresses and strains (chapter 3), and from the requirement that the strains fit together (section 1.18).

2.7.6 Three tests

The hanging bar. A steel bar of length \(L = 10\) m hangs under its own weight, held in the idealized way, the axis \(x\) points down from the centre of the top face. The only stress component that is not zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\) (CM Example 7.3.2, equation (2), PDF p. 305), and the body force is the weight, \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). In the first equation of (2.31), \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x = -\rho g\), since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is a linear function of \(x\) with slope \(-\rho g\). The components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\) are zero, so their derivatives are zero too. The first equation reads \(-\rho g + 0 + 0 + \rho g = 0\) and holds. In the second and the third equation all the stress components are zero and \(b_{\mathrm{y}} = b_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\), so they read \(0 = 0\). So the stress in the hanging bar satisfies the equilibrium equations at every interior point.

The equations can do more than check. CM finds the stress in the hanging bar with them (CM Example 7.3.2, PDF p. 305). Let us assume only, as CM does in its assumption (7.3.17), that the only stress component that is not zero is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and that it depends only on the depth, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = S(x)\). Let the function \(S\) be continuous on \([0, L]\) and differentiable at every point of \((0, L)\).

  1. The second and the third equation hold, because all their terms are zero.
  2. The first equation reads \(S'(x) + \rho g = 0\) for every \(x\) in \((0, L)\).
  3. The function \(x \mapsto S(x) + \rho g\,x\) has, by the rules of differentiation (lemma 1.81), the derivative \(S'(x) + \rho g\), which by step 2 is zero at every point of \((0, L)\). The function is continuous on \([0, L]\), because \(S\) and \(\rho g\,x\) are continuous there (corollary 1.64).
  4. By corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative, therefore, \(S(x) + \rho g\,x = S(L) + \rho g L\) for every \(x\) in \([0, L]\).
  5. The lower end of the bar is free, so the stress there is zero, \(S(L) = 0\).
  6. From \(S(x) + \rho g\,x = \rho g L\) follows \(S(x) = \rho g\,(L - x)\).

This is the stress of section 2.2. There we got it from the equilibrium of the part of the bar below the cut, here from the equilibrium of every small cube.

Pure shear. Take the cube of section 2.6.6, in which \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zx}} = \tau_0\) at every point and all other stress components are zero. There we did not take the weight into account, so \(\vek b = \vek 0\). All the stress components are the same at all points, so all their partial derivatives are zero, and each of the three equations reads \(0 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 0\). The forces on opposite faces of a small cube cancel exactly here (figure 2.28). And if we do not neglect the weight? With the axis \(z\) pointing up, \(\vek b = -\rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), and the third equation requires \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yz}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z = \rho g\). The stress then cannot be the same everywhere. If only \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) changed, then on a steel cube with side 10 mm it would have to change from the bottom to the top face by \(\rho g \cdot 0{,}01\ \text{m} = 765\) Pa. This is \(1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) of the stress \(\tau_0 = 50\) MPa, so neglecting the weight was justified.

Water at rest. Water is at rest in a container shaped like a box. The axis \(x\) points vertically down from the water surface, the axes \(y\) and \(z\) are horizontal. Water has the density \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m³ (VK appendix A.4, PDF p. 637), and the body force is the weight, \(\vek b = \rho g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). In section 2.6.6 we saw that at every point of a fluid at rest the pressure \(p\) is the same in all directions, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -p\), and the shear components are zero. The pressure can, however, be different at different points, \(p = p(Q)\). Let us assume that it is continuously differentiable inside the water. The equilibrium equations read

\[-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \rho g = 0 , \qquad -\frac{\partial p}{\partial y} = 0 , \qquad -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = 0 .\]

So the pressure does not change in the horizontal direction and grows downwards at the rate \(\rho g\). What is it, then?

  1. The function \(f = p - \rho g\,x\) has the partial derivatives \(\partial f/\partial x = \partial p/\partial x - \rho g = 0\), \(\partial f/\partial y = 0\) and \(\partial f/\partial z = 0\) at every interior point of the water.
  2. On a segment in the water parallel to the axis \(x\), \(f\) is a function of one variable with zero derivative. Since it is differentiable, it is also continuous (lemma 1.79), so by corollary 1.85 on a function with zero derivative it is constant on the segment. Likewise on segments parallel to the axes \(y\) and \(z\).
  3. In a container shaped like a box, any two interior points of the water can be joined by three such segments, first along the axis \(x\), then \(y\), then \(z\), which do not leave the water. So \(f\) has the same value \(c\) at all interior points of the water.
  4. At the surface, at \(x = 0\), the air presses on the water with the pressure \(p_0\). If the pressure changes continuously up to the surface, then \(c = p_0\).
  5. Therefore
    \[p = p_0 + \rho g\,x .\]

This is the law of hydrostatics: the pressure in water at rest grows linearly with depth. The term \(\rho g\,x\) is the weight of the column of water of height \(x\) above a unit area, since such a column has the volume \(x\) times the unit area. CM writes, for a fluid in which the stress is \(-p\) in all directions, the equations of motion in a form which for a fluid at rest gives the same three equations (CM (5.3.14), PDF p. 228). Figure 2.37 shows why it is so. The water pushes up on the bottom face of a small cube of water harder than it pushes down on the top face, and the difference is exactly the weight of the cube. On the side faces it pushes equally. For water \(\rho g = 1000 \cdot 9{,}81 = 9810\) N/m³. At the depth 10 m the pressure is \(98\,100\) Pa higher than at the surface, which is about as much as the pressure of the air, about 100 kPa (VK appendix A.4, PDF p. 637). So every 10 m of water adds about one atmosphere of pressure. Let us compare with the hanging bar. There \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \rho g\,(L - x)\), here \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = -(p_0 + \rho g\,x)\). In both cases \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\) falls with depth at the rate \(\rho g\): in the bar the tension gets smaller, in the water the pressure gets larger. The only difference between them is where the stress is known. In the bar it is zero at the free lower end, in the water it is \(-p_0\) at the surface.

A body that moves. When a body accelerates, different bits of the cube need not have the same acceleration. By Newton's law for a collection of bits of matter, the resultant of the forces on the cube is the sum of the masses of the bits times their accelerations (section 2.6.4, CM (5.3.6), (5.3.9), PDF p. 227). The mass of the cube is \(\rho h^3\) if the matter near \(P\) has the density \(\rho\). If the acceleration changes continuously from point to point, this sum is, for a small cube, close to \(\rho h^3\) times the acceleration of the point \(P\). After dividing by \(h^3\), on the right side instead of zero there remains \(\rho\) times the acceleration of the point \(P\). The equilibrium equations then become the equations of motion (enačbe gibanja), for example

\[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}}{\partial z} + b_{\mathrm{x}} = \rho\,a_{\mathrm{x}} ,\]

where \(a_{\mathrm{x}}\) is the component \(x\) of the acceleration (BOWER §2.3.1, PDF p. 76, CM (5.3.12), PDF p. 228). We will need them for vibrations of bodies.

Equilibrium of a finite part of a body. We got the equilibrium equations from the equilibrium of every small cube. Does the converse hold too, that from the equations at every point follows the equilibrium of the whole body or of any of its parts? We can picture a part of the body as built from small cubes, like a wall from bricks. The two forces across the common face of two neighbouring cubes are opposite by lemma 2.21 on the traction on the opposite side of a cut, so they cancel in the sum. What remains are the forces on the outer surface of the part and the body forces. To make this sum exact we need exact adding up over a surface and over a body, which we will meet in chapter 4, and there we will also show this, in section 4.2.13. SADD goes along this route in the opposite direction, from the equilibrium of any part of the body to the equations at a point (SADD (3.7.1)-(3.7.4), PDF pp. 77-78).

The equilibrium equations are the first condition of equilibrium, written at every point of the body. The second condition, that the forces do not turn the body, is still waiting. We will write it on the same small cube in the next section, once we say with numbers how strongly a force tries to turn a body. It will answer the third question from the end of section 2.6: whether \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) equals \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}\).
