Chapter 16 Torsion

16.5 The rectangular section (pravokotni prerez)

In chapter 12 we put the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm into the L-frame and took the torsion constant \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\) as given (equation (12.17)). With the ordinary polar moment \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) the displacement of the top of the frame would be \(26\) % too small (figure 12.16). Where does the number \(73\,178\) come from, and why is it nearly half of \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\)? This section answers both, for a rectangle, which the theory of the circular shaft does not cover.

The model is that of definition 16.1: a straight prism, isotropic linear elastic material (steel, \(G = 75{,}385\) GPa), free lateral surface, loaded only by the torque \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about the axis \(x\). The section \(S\) is a rectangle with side \(2a\) along \(y\) and \(2b\) along \(z\), chosen so that \(a \le b\), with the origin at its centre. For the strip \(a = 10\) mm and \(b = 20\) mm. The twist per unit length is \(\alpha\). The centre of the rectangle is the centre of twist, because the section has two axes of symmetry, as we assumed already in chapter 12, and the reasons are in the section on the shear centre later in the chapter.

By theorem 16.16 the Prandtl function (Prandtlova funkcija) \(\Phi(y, z)\) (definition 16.14) must satisfy \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\) in the section and be zero on the boundary, and the stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial\Phi/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\partial\Phi/\partial y\). By lemma 16.17 such a function is unique if it is twice continuously differentiable on an open set containing \(S\). The function of the rectangle will not be so at the corners, so we show its uniqueness in the proof of the theorem below with the same idea. So we do not have to derive the solution in its only possible form: it is enough to build one, check the conditions and appeal to uniqueness.

A polynomial that is zero on all four sides is the first try. The product \((a^2 - y^2)(b^2 - z^2)\) does that, but its Laplacian is not constant (section 16.3, where it returns as a trial function). So we go another way and build the solution in parts on figure 16.19.

We draw the section with the half width \(a\) along \(y\) and the half height \(b\) along \(z\). The same construction works for every \(a \le b\), the square included. First we look for a function that has the right Laplacian and is zero on the long sides \(y = \pm a\). A function of \(y\) alone does: \(\Phi_{\mathrm{p}} = G\alpha\,(a^2 - y^2)\) has \(\partial^2\Phi_{\mathrm{p}}/\partial y^2 = -2G\alpha\), so \(\nabla^2\Phi_{\mathrm{p}} = -2G\alpha\), and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{p}}(\pm a, z) = 0\). This is exactly the parabola that appears for a long strip (the membrane of section 16.4 is curved across the narrow side like a parabola). For the rectangle it has an error on the short sides \(z = \pm b\): there \(\Phi_{\mathrm{p}} = G\alpha(a^2 - y^2)\), not zero.

We must cancel the error with a function \(\Phi_{\mathrm{h}}\) that has the value \(-G\alpha(a^2 - y^2)\) on the short sides, zero on the long ones, and \(\nabla^2\Phi_{\mathrm{h}} = 0\), so that the sum keeps the right Laplacian. A term of the form \(\cos(\lambda y)\cosh(\lambda z)\) is harmonic, because \(\partial^2_{\mathrm{y}}\cos(\lambda y) = -\lambda^2\cos(\lambda y)\) and \(\partial^2_{\mathrm{z}}\cosh(\lambda z) = +\lambda^2\cosh(\lambda z)\) (lemma 9.42, item 1). Zero on \(y = \pm a\) requires \(\cos(\lambda a) = 0\), so \(\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi/(2a)\) with odd \(n\). On the profile at the short side we must therefore expand the parabola \(a^2 - y^2\) in a series of cosines \(\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\). This is the sine series of lemma 13.10, shifted by \(a\): we take \(h(s) = s(2a - s)\) on \([0, 2a]\) and \(s = y + a\).

Lemma 16.29 The parabola as a cosine series (parabola kot vrsta kosinusov)

Let \(a > 0\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = n\pi/(2a)\) and

\[c_{\mathrm{n}} = \frac{32\,a^2}{\pi^3 n^3}\,(-1)^{(n-1)/2} \qquad (n = 1, 3, 5, \dots) . \tag{16.29}\]

The series \(\sum c_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\) over odd \(n\) converges uniformly on \([-a, a]\) and has the sum \(a^2 - y^2\). Also \(\sum |c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = 2a\).

Proof

We must show that \(a^2 - y^2\) is a sine series by lemma 13.10 and that the coefficients are as stated. Only the odd ones are nonzero, and \(\sum|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a known series of reciprocal squares.

  1. Let \(h(s) = s(2a - s)\) on \([0, 2a]\). It is a polynomial, hence continuously differentiable, and \(h(0) = h(2a) = 0\). By lemma 13.10 with \(L = 2a\), \(h(s) = \sum_{\mathrm{m} \ge 1}h_{\mathrm{m}}\sin(m\pi s/2a)\) uniformly, with sine coefficients \(h_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac1a\int_0^{2a}h(s)\sin\frac{m\pi s}{2a}\,\mathrm{d}s\) (equation (13.8)).
  2. Let \(\kappa = m\pi/(2a)\). Integrating by parts twice (lemma 1.81, product rule): \(\int_0^{2a}h\sin\kappa s\,\mathrm{d}s = \big[-h\cos\kappa s/\kappa\big]_0^{2a} + \frac1\kappa\int_0^{2a}h'\cos\kappa s\,\mathrm{d}s\). The first term is zero because \(h(0) = h(2a) = 0\). The second is \(\frac1{\kappa}\big(\big[h'\sin\kappa s/\kappa\big]_0^{2a} - \frac1\kappa\int_0^{2a}h''\sin\kappa s\,\mathrm{d}s\big)\). Its first term is again zero because \(\sin 0 = \sin m\pi = 0\), and \(h'' = -2\).
  3. What remains is \(\frac{2}{\kappa^2}\int_0^{2a}\sin\kappa s\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{2}{\kappa^3}\big(1 - (-1)^m\big)\). This is zero for even \(m\) and \(4/\kappa^3 = 32a^3/(\pi^3m^3)\) for odd \(m\), so \(h_{\mathrm{m}} = 32a^2/(\pi^3 m^3)\) for odd \(m\) (SymPy: \(h_1/a^2 = 1{,}03205\)).
  4. With \(s = y + a\) and odd \(m\), \(\sin(m\pi s/2a) = \sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} y + m\pi/2) = (-1)^{(m-1)/2}\cos\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} y\), since \(\cos(m\pi/2) = 0\) and \(\sin(m\pi/2) = (-1)^{(m-1)/2}\). So \(h_{\mathrm{m}}\sin(m\pi s/2a) = c_{\mathrm{m}}\cos\lambda_{\mathrm{m}} y\) and \(h(y + a) = a^2 - y^2\). This is the first claim.
  5. By lemma 9.83 (reciprocal squares) \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}1/n^2\) converges, and \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = 16a/(\pi^2 n^2)\). The sum is \(\frac{16a}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8} = 2a\) (the known sum of odd reciprocal squares, which follows from the pointwise convergence of the triangle wave in example 9.96, where \(\frac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}\frac1{n^2} = \frac12\)).

Source: BARBER section 16.4 (PDF p. 258-259, equations (16.45) to (16.49)) finds the coefficients by multiplying with a cosine and integrating, SADD (9.5.7) to (9.5.9) (PDF p. 264) with Fourier coefficients. The proof through the sine series lemma is not from the sources.

The lemma gives us the coefficients and the uniform convergence that we need for differentiation. One term is already close to the parabola: at \(y = 0\), \(c_1 = 103{,}2\) mm\(^2\) instead of \(a^2 = 100\) mm\(^2\), which is \(3{,}2\) %, and the largest deviation on \([-a, a]\) is \(4{,}3\) mm\(^2\). The first term is the blue curve on figure 16.19, the first two terms the orange dashed one, with \(c_1 + c_3 = 99{,}4\) mm\(^2\) at \(y = 0\) and a largest deviation of \(1{,}2\) mm\(^2\) (SymPy). Going from the side \(z = b\) inwards, term \(n\) shrinks by the factor \(\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\). For the strip at \(z = 0\) this factor is \(1/\cosh\pi = 0{,}0863\) for \(n = 1\) and \(1/\cosh 3\pi = 1{,}6\cdot10^{-4}\) for \(n = 3\). The higher terms nearly vanish in the interior.

Theorem 16.30 Torsion of the rectangular section (torzija pravokotnega prereza)

Let \(S = (-a, a) \times (-b, b)\) be a rectangle with \(0 < a \le b\) and let \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) be as in lemma 16.29. For the twist \(\alpha\) per unit length:

  1. The series
    \[\Phi(y, z) = G\alpha\Big[a^2 - y^2 - \sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}c_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,\frac{\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)}{\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)}\Big] \tag{16.30}\]
    and the series of its first derivatives converge term by term uniformly on \(\bar S\). The function \(\Phi\) is continuous on \(\bar S\), zero on the boundary, has continuous second derivatives in the interior and \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\). The stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial\Phi/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\partial\Phi/\partial y\) are in equilibrium in the interior, the lateral surface is free and the resultant force of the section is zero. The function \(\Phi\) is the only one that is continuously differentiable on \(\bar S\), twice continuously differentiable in the interior, zero on the boundary and has \(\nabla^2\Phi = -2G\alpha\), and it is the Prandtl function of the rectangle.
  2. The torque is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = G J\alpha\) with
    \[J = \frac{16\,a^3 b}{3} - \frac{1024\,a^4}{\pi^5}\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^5}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a} . \tag{16.31}\]
    This is the same equation as (12.17).
Proof

We must first show that the series satisfies Prandtl's equation (16.22), then compute the torque. The function is harmonic term by term, the boundary conditions come from lemma 16.29, differentiation term by term from the uniform convergence of the derivatives. Theorem 16.16 and lemma 16.17 do not hold at the corners, so we show their conclusions for the rectangle directly. The torque comes from integration by parts and then term by term.

  1. Let \(r_{\mathrm{n}}(z) = \cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\) for \(|z| \le b\). Because \(\cosh\) is even and increasing on \([0, b]\) (lemma 9.42, items 1 and 3, and the mean value theorem 1.84), \(0 < r_{\mathrm{n}} \le 1\) and \(r_{\mathrm{n}}(\pm b) = 1\). The term \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}(z)\) has absolute value at most \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\), and \(\sum|c_{\mathrm{n}}| < \infty\) (lemma 16.29). By the Weierstrass criterion (theorem 9.85) the series converges uniformly on \(\bar S\) and its sum is continuous.
  2. The first derivatives of a term are \(-c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}'/\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(|r_{\mathrm{n}}'/\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}| = |\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)|/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b) \le 1\). The absolute value is at most \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), and \(\sum|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} = 2a\) (lemma 16.29). So both series of derivatives converge uniformly on \(\bar S\) (theorem 9.85).
  3. Let \(0 < \delta < b\) and \(|z| \le b - \delta\). Because \(\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z) \le \mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}|z|}\) and \(\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b) \ge \mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b}/2\), \(r_{\mathrm{n}}(z) \le 2\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\delta} \le 2q^n\) with \(q = \mathrm{e}^{-\pi\delta/(2a)} < 1\), and the same holds for \(|\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)|/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\). For the strip and \(\delta = 4\) mm, \(q = 0{,}533\). Every second derivative of a term is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) times \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) times a sine or cosine times \(r_{\mathrm{n}}\) or \(\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\), so its absolute value is at most \(2|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}^2q^n = \frac{16}{\pi n}\,q^n \le \frac{16}{\pi}q^n\), since \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = 8/(\pi n)\). The geometric series \(\sum q^n\) converges (lemma 9.83), so the series of second derivatives converge uniformly on the band \(|z| \le b - \delta\) (Weierstrass criterion).
  4. By the lemma on differentiating a uniformly convergent series (lemma 14.25, applied first in one variable and then in the other, chapter 14) \(\Phi\) has continuous second derivatives on the band, equal to the sums of the derivatives of the terms. As \(\delta\) is arbitrary, this holds at every interior point.
  5. Every term has \(\partial^2_{\mathrm{y}} + \partial^2_{\mathrm{z}} = (-\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}^2 + \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}^2)\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}(z) = 0\) (lemma 9.42, item 1, and the theorem on derivatives of sine and cosine 8.12). So \(\nabla^2\Phi = G\alpha\,\nabla^2(a^2 - y^2) = -2G\alpha\).
  6. On \(y = \pm a\), \(\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a) = \cos(n\pi/2) = 0\) for odd \(n\), so all terms of the series are zero, and \(a^2 - y^2 = 0\). On \(z = \pm b\), \(r_{\mathrm{n}} = 1\), so \(\Phi = G\alpha\big[a^2 - y^2 - \sum c_{\mathrm{n}}\cos\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y\big] = 0\) by lemma 16.29. The function \(\Phi\) is zero on the boundary.
  7. Theorem 16.16 and lemma 16.17 require the function to be twice continuously differentiable on an open set containing \(S\). Our \(\Phi\) is not so at the corners. If it were, then at the corner \((a, b)\) we would have \(\Phi_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), because \(\Phi\) is zero along the side \(z = b\), and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), because it is zero along the side \(y = a\), so \(\nabla^2\Phi = 0\) instead of \(-2G\alpha\). So we check the conclusions of both for the rectangle directly.
  8. Equilibrium and free lateral surface. In the interior \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}/\partial z = \Phi_{\mathrm{zy}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\), since by step 4 both mixed derivatives are sums of the same derivatives of the terms \(-c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}'(z)\). On the side \(y = a\) we have \(\Phi(a, z) = 0\) for all \(z\), and by step 2 and lemma 14.25 \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}(a, z)\) is the derivative of this function, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \Phi_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and with the normal \((1, 0)\) we get \(t_{\mathrm{x}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}n_{\mathrm{y}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}n_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\). The same holds on \(y = -a\), and on \(z = \pm b\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} = 0\). At the corners both components are zero. The warping function \(\psi\) with \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = z + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}/(G\alpha)\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = -y - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}/(G\alpha)\), which gives these stresses by equation (16.12), is the series of lemma 16.32 below. The lemma uses only the series of derivatives of step 2, not this theorem. The stresses are therefore stresses of Saint-Venant torsion and \(\Phi\) is the Prandtl function (definition 16.14).
  9. Uniqueness. Let \(\tilde\Phi\) be another function, continuously differentiable on \(\bar S\), twice continuously differentiable in the interior, with \(\nabla^2\tilde\Phi = -2G\alpha\) in the interior and \(\tilde\Phi = 0\) on the boundary, and let \(w = \Phi - \tilde\Phi\). We use the idea of lemma 16.17 on the smaller rectangle \(S_\varepsilon = [-a + \varepsilon, a - \varepsilon] \times [-b + \varepsilon, b - \varepsilon]\), around which \(w\) is twice continuously differentiable: \(\int_{\mathrm{S}_\varepsilon}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \oint_{\partial \mathrm{S}_\varepsilon}w\,\partial w/\partial n\,\mathrm{d}s\). By theorem 8.26, \(|\nabla w| \le K\) on \(\bar S\) for some number \(K\). A point of the boundary of \(S_\varepsilon\) lies at distance \(\varepsilon\) from the boundary of \(S\), where \(w = 0\), so by the mean value theorem 1.84 \(|w| \le \varepsilon K\) there. The right-hand side is therefore at most \(\varepsilon K^2(4a + 4b)\) in absolute value. The left-hand side differs from \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) by the integral over a frame of area less than \(\varepsilon(4a + 4b)\), so by at most \(\varepsilon K^2(4a + 4b)\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) we get \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\), and then as in the lemma: \(\nabla w = 0\), \(w\) is constant and zero on the boundary, so \(w = 0\). This proves the first item.
  10. Torque and resultant. The moment of the stresses of the end section about the axis \(x\) is \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = \int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} - z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}})\,\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\mathrm{S}}(y\,\Phi_{\mathrm{y}} + z\,\Phi_{\mathrm{z}})\,\mathrm{d}A\) (theorem 16.10, step 5). We integrate by parts at fixed \(z\): \(\int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}y\,\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\,\mathrm{d}y = \big[y\,\Phi\big]_{\mathrm{y}=-\mathrm{a}}^{a} - \int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}y = -\int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}y\), since \(\Phi(\pm a, z) = 0\), and the same in \(z\). By theorem 4.9, \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A\), which is equation (16.23), here without theorem 16.16. Likewise \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}\big[\Phi\big]_{\mathrm{z}=-\mathrm{b}}^{b}\,\mathrm{d}y = 0\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}\,\mathrm{d}A = 0\). The series converges uniformly, so we integrate it term by term. \(\int_{\mathrm{S}}(a^2 - y^2)\,\mathrm{d}A = 2b\cdot\frac{4a^3}{3} = \frac{8a^3b}{3}\).
  11. For a term, \(\int_{-\mathrm{a}}^{a}\cos\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{2\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}} = \frac{4a}{n\pi}(-1)^{(n-1)/2}\) and \(\int_{-\mathrm{b}}^{b}r_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{2\tanh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}} = \frac{4a}{n\pi}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a}\). The product with \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(\frac{32a^2}{\pi^3n^3}\cdot\frac{16a^2}{\pi^2n^2}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a} = \frac{512\,a^4}{\pi^5n^5}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a}\), because \((-1)^{(n-1)} = 1\).
  12. So \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 2G\alpha\big[\frac{8a^3b}{3} - \frac{512a^4}{\pi^5}\sum\frac{1}{n^5}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a}\big] = G\alpha J\) with \(J\) from equation (16.31).

Source: SADD example 9.4 (PDF p. 262-265, equations (9.5.10) to (9.5.12)) and BARBER section 16.4 (PDF p. 258-259) give the same series and the same \(J\), SADD with other letters: his \(\phi\) is our \(\Phi\), his \(T\) our \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\), his axes \(x\), \(y\) are our \(y\), \(z\). The proof through the sine series lemma and uniqueness is not from the sources.

The proof showed four things. Every term of the series is harmonic, so the Laplacian stayed the right one that the parabola gave. The boundary condition on the short sides is just the cosine expansion of the parabola, and because the coefficients fall like \(1/n^3\) the series dies out quickly in the interior. And integration by parts moved the torque from the derivatives onto \(\Phi\) itself, and we obtained it with the integral of the series. Since \(\Phi\) has no second derivatives at the corners, we showed equilibrium, the free side and uniqueness for the rectangle directly, with the same idea as in section 16.3.

Let us compute for the strip \(20 \times 40\) mm (num-05.py). The first term of the series in \(J\) is \(1{,}00000\) times \(33\,337{,}2\) mm\(^4\), the second is \(0{,}00413\) and the third \(0{,}00032\) times as much, which gives

\[J = 106\,666{,}7 - 33\,488{,}5 = 73\,178{,}1 \text{ mm}^4 ,\]

and \(G J = 5516{,}5\) N m\(^2\). Direct numerical integration gives the same, \(2\int_{\mathrm{S}}\Phi\,\mathrm{d}A = 73\,178{,}137\) mm\(^4\), and \(\nabla^2\Phi/(G\alpha) = -2{,}0000\) at three points (finite difference check). At \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m, \(\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/(GJ) = 0{,}00906\) rad/m. So the number that chapter 12 took as given is now computed.

Let us see where the stress is.

We draw the boundary of the section and on it the level lines of \(\Phi\), like the contour lines of a map of a hill: each line joins points with the same value, at the lines \(30\), \(60\) and \(90\) there is a number. The summit \(91{,}10\) mm\(^2\) is at the centre. By lemma 16.20 the stress is tangent to the level line and its magnitude is \(|\nabla\Phi|\), so the closer the level lines the larger the stress. At the middle of the long side the level lines are closest, the stress is largest there, and it points along the side. At the middle of the short side the level lines are farther apart and the stress is smaller. At the corner the two sides meet at a right angle. There \(\Phi\) is zero on both sides, so both tangential derivatives vanish and by equation (16.19) both stress components are zero.

Corollary 16.31 Stresses on the boundary of the rectangle (napetosti na robu pravokotnika)

Under the conditions of theorem 16.30 the magnitude of the stress \(\tau = \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}^2}\) at the middle of the long side \((\pm a, 0)\) is

\[\tau(a, 0) = 2a\,G\alpha\Big[1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^2\cosh(n\pi b/2a)}\Big] = k_\tau\,\frac{M_{\mathrm{x}}}{J} , \qquad k_\tau = 2a\Big[1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^2\cosh(n\pi b/2a)}\Big] , \tag{16.32}\]

and the stress there is along the axis \(z\). At the corners \(\tau = 0\).

Proof

We must show the value of the derivative at one point and at the corners. We get the values by differentiating the series term by term, which the uniform convergence of theorem 16.30, item 1, allows.

  1. At the point \((a, 0)\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \partial\Phi/\partial z = 0\), because \(\partial_{\mathrm{z}}\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z) = \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z) = 0\) at \(z = 0\).
  2. The component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = -\partial\Phi/\partial y = G\alpha\big[2y - \sum c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}(z)\big]\). At \(y = a\) and \(z = 0\), \(\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}\), so \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a) = \frac{16a}{\pi^2n^2}\) and \(r_{\mathrm{n}}(0) = 1/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\). With the sum \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}1/n^2 = \pi^2/8\) (lemma 16.29, step 5), \(2a = \frac{16a}{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{n^2}\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}}(a, 0) = G\alpha\cdot 2a\big[1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum\frac{1}{n^2\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)}\big]\).
  3. By theorem 16.30, item 2, \(G\alpha = M_{\mathrm{x}}/J\), which gives the second form.
  4. At the corner \((a, b)\), \(\Phi\) has a zero on both sides that meet there. The tangential derivative along the side \(y = a\) is \(\partial\Phi/\partial z = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) and along the side \(z = b\) it is \(\partial\Phi/\partial y = -\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = 0\), since \(\Phi\) is zero on the boundary (theorem 16.30, item 1, continuous first derivatives on \(\bar S\)). Both stress components are therefore zero.

Source: SADD (9.5.13) (PDF p. 264-265) for the largest stress at the middle of the long side, ONATE2 figure 4.11 (PDF p. 201) the same with a fitted formula. The proof is not from the sources.

For the strip \(k_\tau = 18{,}601\) mm, or \(0{,}9301\) of \(2a\). At \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m,

\[\tau(a, 0) = \frac{50\,000 \text{ N mm}}{73\,178 \text{ mm}^4}\cdot 18{,}601 \text{ mm} = 12{,}71 \text{ MPa} ,\]

and ONATE2 with a fitted formula (\(J = 73\,242\) mm\(^4\), \(+0{,}09\) %) gives \(12{,}74\) MPa. At the middle of the short side \(\tau(0, b)/(G\alpha) = 14{,}79\) mm, which is \(0{,}795\) of the largest, or \(10{,}10\) MPa (num-05.py). At \(500\) N m the stress would be ten times larger, \(127{,}1\) MPa, above the shear yield by the Tresca criterion (\(110\) MPa), which is why we took \(50\) N m. That this value is the largest over the whole section is not proved. We checked it on a grid of \(41 \times 81\) points of a quarter of the section (the largest value at the middle of the long side, num-05.py, item 15), and SADD (PDF p. 264) and the membrane analogy (corollary 16.26: the largest slope of the membrane is at the edge nearest the centre) predict it.

Let us also see what happens if we treat the rectangle like a circle. This is the last try that lemma 16.6 works out: plane sections, so \(\psi = 0\) and \(J = I_{\mathrm{p}}\). For the strip \(I_{\mathrm{p}} = I_{\mathrm{y}} + I_{\mathrm{z}} = 26\,667 + 106\,667 = 133\,333\) mm\(^4\) (lemma 5.27) and at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m the twist is \(\alpha = 4{,}97\cdot10^{-3}\) rad/m instead of \(9{,}06\cdot10^{-3}\) rad/m, a twist \(45{,}1\) % too small (conversely \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(82{,}2\) % larger than \(J\) and the true twist \(82{,}2\) % larger). By the circle formula \(\tau = G\alpha r\) the stress would be \(3{,}75\) MPa at the middle of the long side (\(r = a\)) instead of \(12{,}71\) MPa, and \(8{,}39\) MPa at the corners (\(r = 22{,}36\) mm), where the true value is zero. It is wrong in two directions at once: too small where the stress is largest, and a stress where there is none. This is the same number the L-frame shows in figure 12.16: the twisting of the column contributes \(4{,}97\) mm instead of \(9{,}06\) mm to the displacement of the top, because the ratio is \(J/I_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}5488\). Why is \(J\) smaller than \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\)? Lemma 16.12 gives \(I_{\mathrm{p}} - J = \int_{\mathrm{S}}|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}A\) for \(\psi\) twice continuously differentiable on an open set containing \(S\). The warping of the rectangle is not so at the corners, so here we check the equality numerically. With the warping function of the lemma below the integral is \(60\,155{,}2\) mm\(^4\) (\(60 \times 60\) point quadrature, num-05.py), \(45{,}1\) % of \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\), which is exactly \(1 - J/I_{\mathrm{p}}\). Warping thus lets the section twist under a smaller torque than a section that stays plane would need.

We already have the warping function (definition 16.7), because the Prandtl function carries it. By lemma 16.8, \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z)\) and \(\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y)\), and by Hooke's law \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\gamma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\gamma_{\mathrm{xz}}\) (theorem 3.11).

Lemma 16.32 Warping of the rectangular section (zvitje pravokotnega prereza)

Under the conditions of theorem 16.30 the warping function, determined up to an additive constant that is a rigid axial shift, is

\[\psi(y, z) = y z - \sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}c_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,\frac{\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)}{\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)} . \tag{16.33}\]

It is odd in \(y\) and in \(z\) (so zero on the axis \(y\) and on the axis \(z\)), harmonic in the interior, and \(\psi(a, b) = ab - \frac{32a^2}{\pi^3}\sum_{\mathrm{n}\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^3}\tanh\frac{n\pi b}{2a}\).

Proof

We must show that the series satisfies the relation of stresses and warping. We compute the derivatives of the series term by term and compare them with \(\Phi\).

  1. From \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = G\alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} - z) = \partial\Phi/\partial z\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = G\alpha(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} + y) = -\partial\Phi/\partial y\) it follows that \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = z + \Phi_{\mathrm{z}}/(G\alpha)\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = -y - \Phi_{\mathrm{y}}/(G\alpha)\).
  2. With the expressions for \(\Phi_{\mathrm{y}}\) and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{z}}\) (step 2 of theorem 16.30), \(\psi_{\mathrm{y}} = z - \sum c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\cos(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{z}} = y - \sum c_{\mathrm{n}}\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\,r_{\mathrm{n}}(z)\). Both series converge uniformly (the same terms as in the theorem, the bound \(|c_{\mathrm{n}}|\lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\)).
  3. The function (16.33) has these two derivatives (lemma 14.25), so \(\psi\) is the warping function sought, up to a constant. It is odd in \(y\) (sine, \(y\)) and in \(z\) (\(\sinh\), \(z\)), so it is zero on both axes.
  4. The term \(\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} y)\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} z)\) is harmonic like \(\cos\cosh\), and so is \(y z\), because \(\partial^2_{\mathrm{y}}(yz) = \partial^2_{\mathrm{z}}(yz) = 0\). We replace the second derivatives by the sums of derivatives of the terms on the band \(|z| \le b - \delta\) as in step 3 of theorem 16.30, so \(\nabla^2\psi = 0\).
  5. At \((a, b)\), \(\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}\) and \(\sinh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)/\cosh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b) = \tanh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\), so \(c_{\mathrm{n}}\sin(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} a)\tanh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b) = \frac{32a^2}{\pi^3n^3}\tanh(\lambda_{\mathrm{n}} b)\).

Source: SADD (9.5.14) (PDF p. 265) gives the displacement after integrating (9.3.5), with figure 9.13 (PDF p. 265) of contour lines. The proof through the relation of \(\psi\) and \(\Phi\) is not from the sources.

For the strip \(\psi(a, b) = 91{,}83\) mm\(^2\) and at \(M_{\mathrm{x}} = 50\) N m the axial displacement of the corner is \(\alpha\psi(a, b) = 0{,}832\) µm. This is very little: \(4{,}2\cdot10^{-5}\) of the section width \(2a\). The displacement is largest not at the corners but on the long side at \(z = \pm 16{,}63\) mm, where \(\psi = 105{,}1\) mm\(^2\), which is \(0{,}953\) µm (fine division of the side, num-05.py). Figure 16.21 shows what it looks like when we enlarge the displacement strongly.

On the left we draw the section and on it the axes on which \(\psi = 0\) (lemma 16.32, oddness). The axes divide it into four quarters. In the quarters with \(y z > 0\), \(\psi > 0\), the quarters where the term \(y z\) wins, and we mark them with the signs, in the others it is negative. In every quarter we draw level lines of \(\psi\). On the right we take the end of the bar, seen obliquely, with the axis \(x\) along the oblique axis. The undeformed section is dashed. The edge of the warped section lies now above the dashed rectangle, now below it, as \(\alpha\psi\) carries it, and the arrow from corner to corner shows the displacement along the axis \(x\): the corners \((a, b)\) and \((-a, -b)\) are in front, the corners \((a, -b)\) and \((-a, b)\) behind. The sections therefore do not stay plane, as we guessed for the circle.

The rectangle is now solved. The findings:

  • The torsion constant of the strip is \(J = 73\,178\) mm\(^4\), which is \(0{,}549\,I_{\mathrm{p}}\), and this very computation explains the number of chapter 12 (figure 12.16).
  • The stress is largest at the middle of the long side (\(12{,}71\) MPa at \(50\) N m) and zero at the corners.
  • The section warps, the axial displacement is \(0{,}832\) µm at the corners and \(0{,}953\) µm at most.
  • The circle formula with plane sections is therefore wrong: \(I_{\mathrm{p}}\) is \(82{,}2\) % larger than \(J\), and the stress \(3{,}75\) against \(12{,}71\) MPa.

For thin strips of length \(h\) and thickness \(t\) the series in \(J\) reduces to its first term, which leads to \(J \approx h t^3/3\). This is the subject of the section on thin sections, where we return to \(b/a \to \infty\) with a quantitative error.
