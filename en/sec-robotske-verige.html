Chapter 27 Robot kinematics and dynamics

27.1 Robot chains, joints and workspace (robotske verige, zglobi in delovni prostor)

An industrial arm consists of a few rigid bars that motors turn against each other, with a tool or a gripper at the end. The robot must know where this tool is when it knows the motor angles, and the other way round, what angles a given point demands. Three questions stand before us. How many numbers do we need to say in what position the whole arm is? Where is the end of the arm when we know these numbers? Which points can the end of the arm reach at all? The first two are questions of kinematics (kinematika), the description of the positions and motion of bodies without forces. The third is a question about the extent of the motion. In this section we answer the first and the third for a planar arm, and we finish the second in the later sections of this chapter, where the positions of bodies are written with matrices. Forces and the equations of motion come later still.

The chapter builds on chapter 8: configuration, constraints, degrees of freedom and generalized coordinates are derived there, and here we only use them.

Model. The model of the arm has idealizations, which we list in advance, each with what it would spoil in a real arm. Figure 27.2 shows each in its own tile: top left, top right, bottom left and bottom right.

  1. The links are rigid (definition 1.11): they do not bend and do not vibrate. For short thick links this is fine, for long thin ones it is not. An aluminium tube \(1\) m long with a diameter of \(50\) mm and a wall of \(3\) mm, clamped at one end, sags under a load of \(50\) N at the other end by \(Fl^3/(3EI) = 1{,}9\) mm (equation (5.57) with \(E = 70\) GPa and \(I = 1{,}23 \cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\), the numbers are our choice, NumPy), while the rigid model gives \(0\) mm. By that much the gripper is elsewhere than the model says. Flexible links are treated in chapter 28.
  2. The joints are ideal (definition 8.3), hence without friction. Besides, they have no backlash, and the rotation is not limited. A real joint has a limited angle, and its backlash gives an error at the gripper.
  3. The motors give only forces and moments in the joints. Their own masses and inertias are not in the model. How much this matters is told by section 27.8: a motor behind a gear with the ratio \(r : 1\) adds the inertia \(r^2 J_{\mathrm{m}}\) to the link, where \(J_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the moment of inertia of the motor, so at \(r = 100\) it is \(10^4\,J_{\mathrm{m}}\), which is not small.
  4. The base stands still (so the robot is not on a vehicle), and the chain of links is open, as the definition below says.

We first picture the arm in space. Figure 27.3 shows its scheme.

We start at the floor. The base (podlaga) stands and never moves, we call it link \(0\). On it sits the first joint (zglob), where the first link (člen) can turn against the base. At the other end of the first link is joint \(2\), which carries link \(2\), and so on to joint \(3\) and link \(3\). At the end of the last link is the end effector (prijemalo). There the robot acts on an object, which is why it is called an end effector. We know the word link from a chain: a link of a chain holds its neighbour, and every link of the arm is joined to its two neighbours in a joint, so the structure is called a chain. A joint is a place where two links touch and can move, as in an elbow. The base is called so because it carries everything else.

Figure 27.3 is the scheme of every arm in which each link holds only the next one. An arm in which the links close into a loop appears in example 27.5.

Definition 27.1 Serial manipulator (serijski robot)

Let \(n \ge 1\) be an integer. A serial manipulator (serijski robot) with \(n\) joints is a system of \(n + 1\) rigid bodies (definition 1.11), the links \(0, 1, \dots, n\), and \(n\) joints \(1, \dots, n\) with the following properties:

  1. Link \(0\), the base (podlaga), stands still.
  2. Joint \(i\) connects link \(i - 1\) to link \(i\) and to no other link.
  3. The end effector (prijemalo) is a chosen point of link \(n\), which we denote \(E\).

The chain is open (open chain), because each link is connected only to the previous and the next one, link \(0\) only to the next, link \(n\) only to the previous. The links do not form a loop.

Source: SPONG section 3.1 (PDF p. 98): "A robot manipulator with \(n\) joints will have \(n + 1\) links", the links are numbered from \(0\) and joint \(i\) connects link \(i - 1\) to link \(i\). The base stands still, same page. The notion of the end effector is in SPONG section 1.1.4 (PDF p. 29). The wording "open chain" and the requirement of links without a loop are our explanation.

What are the joints like? Figure 27.5 shows two kinds. A human elbow has one motion, like a door on hinges: two parts turn about one axis, this is a revolute joint. We know another one from a telescopic aerial or a drawer that is pulled out: a displacement along one line, this is a prismatic joint.

The shoulder, however, we move in three directions, like a ball in a bowl. SPONG section 3.1 (PDF p. 98) shows that this is nothing new: a ball joint with two degrees of freedom and a wrist with three can be thought of as a succession of joints with one degree of freedom and links of length zero between them. The arrow with the equals sign between the two halves says that the right picture shows the same motions as the left. So in this chapter we always assume joints with one degree of freedom. Two kinds suffice.

On the left of figure 27.5 is the revolute joint. Link \(i - 1\) carries the axis of the joint. We look along it, so it is drawn as a circle with a dot. As long as the joint is not turned, link \(i\) lies in the extension of link \(i - 1\), dashed grey. When it turns, it is orange. Every point of link \(i\), for example the point \(P\), then moves on a circle about the axis, and we measure the angle \(\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) between the lines of the two links, which is zero in the initial position. On the right is the prismatic joint. The guide is part of link \(i - 1\). The slider of link \(i\) is dashed grey in the initial position. When it slides along the guide, it is orange, and it does not turn. We measure the displacement \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) between the mark on the guide and the mark on the slider, which were at the same place in the initial position.

Definition 27.2 Revolute and prismatic joint (vrtljivi in drsni zglob)

Let joint \(i\) connect links \(i - 1\) and \(i\) of a serial manipulator (definition 27.1). The joint is revolute (vrtljivi zglob, symbol R) if every point of link \(i\) moves relative to link \(i - 1\) on a circle about the same line, the joint axis (os zgloba), which is fixed to link \(i - 1\). The joint is prismatic (drsni zglob, symbol P) if every point of link \(i\) moves relative to link \(i - 1\) on a line parallel to the same line, the joint axis, and link \(i\) does not turn. Both joints are ideal (definition 8.3), hence without friction. Besides, they have no backlash, and the angle or displacement is not limited. A joint has one degree of freedom, because one number determines the position of link \(i\) relative to link \(i - 1\), an angle or a displacement, which the next definition calls the joint variable.

Source: SPONG section 1.1.1 (PDF p. 27) and figure 1.4 (PDF p. 28): the revolute joint is like a hinge and allows a relative rotation, the prismatic joint allows a linear relative motion, symbols R and P. The statement that all points move on circles or lines is ours. The source gives no reasons for the names: revolute is from "revolve" (to turn), for prismatic we explain the name by the prismatic shape of a slider that stays aligned in its guide, which is our explanation and not from the source.

The names have a reason: the revolute joint revolves, in Slovenian vrtljivi, the prismatic one slides, drsni. An angle or a displacement is written as a number. The next definition says how.

Definition 27.3 Joint variable (spremenljivka zgloba)

Let joint \(i\) be a revolute or prismatic joint of a serial manipulator. For a revolute joint we choose on the two links lines perpendicular to the joint axis that are parallel and equally directed in some reference position of the joint, and we choose the direction in which the angle counts as positive. The joint variable (spremenljivka zgloba) \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = \theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) is then the angle, in radians (definition 8.8), by which the line of link \(i\) turns relative to the line of link \(i - 1\) from the reference position. For a prismatic joint we choose marks on the two links on the axis that are at the same place in a reference position, and a direction of the axis. The variable \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = d_{\mathrm{i}}\) is then the displacement of the mark of link \(i\) from the mark of link \(i - 1\) along the axis, in the chosen direction, in metres. The numbers are collected in a column \(\vek q = (q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}) \in \mathbb{R}^n\), which we also call the configuration of the robot in joint coordinates.

Source: SPONG section 1.1.2 (PDF p. 28) and equation (3.1) of section 3.1 (PDF p. 98): \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = \theta_{\mathrm{i}}\) for a revolute and \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = d_{\mathrm{i}}\) for a prismatic joint, and the vector \(\vek q\) determines the position of every point of the rigid links. The choice of the reference lines, which fixes the zero and the sign, is our wording. In the section on the Denavit-Hartenberg notation the frame of the link will fix the zero and the sign.

In this section the letter \(d_{\mathrm{i}}\) always means the displacement of a joint and the letter \(\theta\) the angle of a joint. In chapter 8 the letter \(\theta\) is the angle of a pendulum, here it is the angle in a joint. The variables \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) have the same name and the same role as the generalized coordinates of chapter 8. For a planar chain with revolute joints lemma 27.4 will show this. For chains with prismatic joints and for chains in space we quote it from SPONG section 1.1.2 (PDF p. 28), where the number of joints determines the number of degrees of freedom, and we do not prove it here.

For a planar arm we can see everything in one picture. Arm \(A\) is a planar arm with two revolute joints whose axes are perpendicular to the plane (an RR arm). Its links have the lengths \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m, like the double pendulum of example 8.50. The masses of the links, which we shall need at the equations of motion, are our choice and not from a source: \(10\) kg and \(5\) kg, the links are homogeneous thin bars.

Let us draw the arm as we see it. We draw the axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) through a chosen point of the base, they are the axes of a fixed coordinate system labelled \(0\), because it is fixed to link \(0\). The base is below them. At the origin is joint \(O\), the first revolute joint. From it goes link \(1\) of length \(l_1\), which is turned from the axis \(x_0\) by the angle \(q_1\). This angle is also the variable of joint \(1\), because the base is at rest and the line of link \(0\) has the direction of the axis \(x_0\). At the end of the link is joint \(B\), and from it goes link \(2\) of length \(l_2\). Its angle \(q_2\) is measured from the extension of link \(1\), drawn dashed, because that is the line of link \(1\) at joint \(2\) from which the joint counts the rotation. At the end of link \(2\) is the end effector \(E\). Where is it? The position of a point on a link we know from lemma 8.41: a point of a rigid body with the initial vector \(\vek r_0\) from the point \(O\) of the body is, after a rotation \(\varphi\), at the position \(\vek o + \mat R(\varphi)\vek r_0\).

Link \(1\) has its point \(O\) at the origin and the rotation \(\varphi_1 = q_1\), the point \(B\) is at the vector \(\vek r_0 = (l_1, 0)\) from it. Link \(2\) has the point \(B\) and the rotation \(\varphi_2 = q_1 + q_2\), because link \(2\) turns by \(q_2\) against link \(1\), which is already turned by \(q_1\). The end effector is at \(\vek r_0 = (l_2, 0)\) from \(B\). So

\[\vek b = l_1\begin{pmatrix} \cos q_1 \\ \sin q_1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vek e = \vek b + l_2\begin{pmatrix} \cos(q_1 + q_2) \\ \sin(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{27.1}\]

For \(q_1 = 30°\) and \(q_2 = 60°\) we get \(\vek b = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}500)\) m and \(\vek e = (0{,}866;\ 1{,}000)\) m: the second link has the rotation \(\varphi_2 = 90°\) and points vertically up, as the figure shows, which was drawn from exactly these numbers. The end effector is at the coordinates \(0{,}866\) m from the axis \(y_0\) and \(1{,}0\) m from the axis \(x_0\) (numbers from NumPy).

Did we compute the same as for the double pendulum? Let us check on figure 27.7.

For the double pendulum (example 8.50) the links hang from the point \(O\). The angles are measured from the downward vertical, \(\theta_1 = \pi/6\) and \(\theta_2 = \pi/3\). In the axes of the pendulum, \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up, the point \(B\) is at \((0{,}5000;\ -0{,}8660)\) m and the end \(E\) at \((0{,}9330;\ -1{,}1160)\) m. To read the pendulum as an arm we turn the axes: let the axis \(x_0\) point down and the axis \(y_0\) to the right. Together with the perpendicular axis \(z\) out of the plane of the drawing they form a right-handed system. The horizontal coordinate of the pendulum is then the coordinate \(y_0\), and the vertical one is \(-x_0\). The pendulum angles in this system are the rotations of the links from the axis \(x_0\), that is \(\varphi_1 = 30°\) and \(\varphi_2 = 60°\), and the joint variables are \(q_1 = 30°\) and \(q_2 = \varphi_2 - \varphi_1 = 30°\), the angle from the dashed extension of the first link. Equation (27.1) gives \(\vek b = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}500)\) m and \(\vek e = (1{,}116;\ 0{,}933)\) m, these are the distances of the point \(B\) and the point \(E\), read along the axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\). In the axes of the pendulum these are \((0{,}500;\ -0{,}866)\) m and \((0{,}933;\ -1{,}116)\) m, as in chapter 8 (NumPy). The relative angle \(q_2 = \theta_2 - \theta_1\) is chosen there as the second generalized coordinate (step 3 of example 8.50), here it is the variable of the second joint.

Why are the two numbers \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) enough to write the position of every point of the arm? Let us count on figure 27.8.

On the left are the links of arm \(A\) without joints, free in the plane \(xy\). Link \(1\) needs three numbers: the coordinates \(x_1\), \(y_1\) of the point \(O_1\) and the rotation \(\varphi_1\). Link \(2\) needs three as well. Together that is \(3 + 3 = 6\) numbers. A joint is a pin that holds two points together. The first pin fixes \(O_1\) to the origin: the gap between the origin and \(O_1\) has a horizontal part \(f_1\) and a vertical part \(f_2\), and both must be zero. The second pin fixes \(O_2\) to the end of link \(1\): this gap too has two parts, \(f_3\) and \(f_4\). Each pin takes two numbers, because a gap in the plane has two directions, and a pin does not hinder the rotation. What remains is \(6 - 2 - 2 = 2\). On the right the arm is assembled: the pins \(O_1\) and \(O_2\) are inserted, only the angles \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are still free, and from them we get the rotation of the second link \(\varphi_2 = q_1 + q_2\). The lemma says the same for \(n\) links and adds what the counting alone does not show: that no pin equation repeats another and that the angles can really be chosen freely.

Lemma 27.4 Joint variables of an open planar chain are generalized coordinates (spremenljivke zglobov odprte ravninske verige so posplošene koordinate)

Let a chain consist of \(n\) rigid links moving in the plane \(xy\) and \(n\) revolute joints with axes perpendicular to this plane, open by definition 27.1. The point \(O_{\mathrm{i}}\) of link \(i\) is the centre of joint \(i\). The point \(O_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) is the point of link \(i\) at the distance \(l_{\mathrm{i}} > 0\) from \(O_{\mathrm{i}}\) that lies, in the position with the angle \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\), on the axis \(x\) from \(O_{\mathrm{i}}\). Link \(0\) stands still, its point \(O_1\) is at the origin. Let \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) be the rotation of link \(i\) by lemma 8.41, \(\varphi_0 = 0\), so that the joint variable is \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1}\). Then:

  1. The configuration of the chain (definition 8.42) has \(N = 3n\) numbers, the constraints of the joints are \(m = 2n\) independent scleronomic holonomic constraints (definition 8.43), and the number of degrees of freedom is \(n\).
  2. The joint variables \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) are generalized coordinates of the chain (definition 8.46) at every \(\vek q \in \mathbb{R}^n\), not only near a chosen position, with the map \(\vek X\) given by equation (27.2) below.
Proof

We must show that the constraints of the joints give \(2n\) independent equations, and that the angles \(q\) are mapped to a configuration by a map whose columns have \(n\) pivots.

  1. Link \(i\) has by definition 8.42 three numbers, \((x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{i}})\), where \((x_{\mathrm{i}}, y_{\mathrm{i}})\) is the position of the point \(O_{\mathrm{i}}\) (left in the figure for \(n = 2\)). Link \(0\) stands still and is not in the configuration. So \(N = 3n\).
  2. Joint \(1\) holds \(O_1\) at the origin: \(f_1 = x_1 = 0\) and \(f_2 = y_1 = 0\) (gap to the origin). Joint \(i + 1\) (\(i = 1, \dots, n - 1\)) holds \(O_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) at a point of link \(i\). By lemma 8.41 this point is at the position \(\vek o + \mat R(\varphi)\vek r_0\) with \(\vek r_0 = (l_{\mathrm{i}}, 0)\), so \(f_{2\mathrm{i}+1} = x_{\mathrm{i}+1} - x_{\mathrm{i}} - l_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\) and \(f_{2\mathrm{i}+2} = y_{\mathrm{i}+1} - y_{\mathrm{i}} - l_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\). For \(i = 1\) these are the parts of the gap between the end of link \(1\) and \(O_2\). Altogether there are \(m = 2n\) constraints, none contains time.
  3. We number the coordinates so that \(x_1, y_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}, y_{\mathrm{n}}\) are the first columns of the \(2n \times 3n\) matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\). Of these, the constraint \(f_{2\mathrm{i}+1}\) contains only \(x_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) with the coefficient \(1\) and \(x_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the constraint \(f_{2\mathrm{i}+2}\) only \(y_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) and \(y_{\mathrm{i}}\), and all nonzero entries are on the diagonal or below it. The matrix of these \(2n\) columns is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal. The reason can be seen in the figure: each pin attaches the new point \(O_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) to the link before it, so its gap contains the new coordinates \(x_{\mathrm{i}+1}\), \(y_{\mathrm{i}+1}\) and besides them only the numbers of the previous link. Elimination of the lower rows gives the identity matrix, so it has \(2n\) pivots (definition 8.21) and the constraints are independent (definition 8.43).
  4. By definition 8.46 the number of degrees of freedom is \(n = N - m = 3n - 2n\) (for \(n = 2\) these are the angles \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) that remain of the six numbers). This is point 1.
  5. Let \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} = q_1 + \dots + q_{\mathrm{i}}\) (which follows from \(q_{\mathrm{i}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{i}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{i}-1}\) and \(\varphi_0 = 0\), for arm \(A\) this is \(\varphi_2 = q_1 + q_2\)) and
    \[x_1 = y_1 = 0 , \quad x_{\mathrm{i}+1} = x_{\mathrm{i}} + l_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} , \quad y_{\mathrm{i}+1} = y_{\mathrm{i}} + l_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\varphi_{\mathrm{i}} . \tag{27.2}\]
    The map \(\vek X(\vek q) = (x_1, y_1, \varphi_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{n}}, y_{\mathrm{n}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}})\) is continuously differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (sums and derivatives of sine and cosine, theorem 8.13) and satisfies the constraints by step 2: we insert \(x_{\mathrm{i}+1} - x_{\mathrm{i}} = l_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\) into \(f_{2\mathrm{i}+1}\) and get zero.
  6. The matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has in the rows of the angles \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) (for arm \(A\) the row of \(\varphi_2 = q_1 + q_2\) has two ones) a square lower triangular matrix \(\mat L\) with \(L_{\mathrm{ij}} = 1\) for \(j \le i\) and \(L_{\mathrm{ij}} = 0\) otherwise. We put these rows at the top (a row swap is allowed by definition 8.21). In step \(k\) of the elimination row \(k\) equals row \(k\) of the identity matrix: at the start it has ones in the columns \(1, \dots, k\), and in each step \(j < k\) we subtract from it row \(j\), which is then row \(j\) of the identity matrix. Its one in column \(k\) is the pivot. So the elimination finds a pivot in each of the \(n\) columns, as in step 4 of the proof of lemma 8.47. Since the square matrix \(\mat L\) has \(n\) pivots by the same steps, it is invertible by lemma 8.22. This is the third requirement of definition 8.46, the first two hold by step 5. This is point 2, at every \(\vek q\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources. SPONG section 1.1.2 (PDF p. 28) says that the configuration of a robot is given by the vector of joint variables and that the number of joints determines the number of degrees of freedom. The coordinates of chapter 8 are example 8.50, where \(n = 2\).

What did we show? For an open chain we can choose all \(n\) joint variables freely, and every choice gives an admissible position, with no hidden constraint between them. Why are the joint variables \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) relative angles and not absolute angles \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\)? The motor in joint \(i\) turns link \(i\) against link \(i - 1\), and the sensor in the joint also tells only \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\). The same chain could be described by the \(\varphi_{\mathrm{i}}\), since \(\mat L\) is invertible, but the motor does not grip these angles. For arm \(A\) we have \(n = 2\), \(N = 6\) and \(m = 4\). The matrix \(\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x\) has four pivots at \(\vek q = (30°, 60°)\) and also at \(q_1 = 0\), the matrix \(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q\) has two, and the product \((\partial\vek f/\partial\vek x)(\partial\vek X/\partial\vek q)\) is the zero matrix, as lemma 8.48 demands (SymPy).

The lemma says "open", and that condition is used. Let us see what happens when it is missing.

Example 27.5 Closed chain: toggle mechanism D (zaprta veriga: mehanizem D)

Take mechanism \(D\) of example 8.49: bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) of length \(a = 0{,}5\) m, joint \(A\) is fixed, the end \(C\) slides on a horizontal guide. On the left of the figure is the mechanism. The chain has four links: the base with the guide, the bar \(AB\), the bar \(BC\) and the slider. The joints are the revolute joint \(A\) between the base and \(AB\), the revolute joint \(B\), the revolute joint \(C\) between \(BC\) and the slider, and the prismatic joint between the slider and the base. The chain returns to the base, it is closed, and definition 27.1 excludes it.

  1. The joint variables by definition 27.3 are: \(q_1\), the angle of the bar \(AB\) from the guide, \(q_2\), the angle of the bar \(BC\) against the extension of \(AB\) (\(q_2\)), \(q_3\), the angle of the bar \(BC\) from the guide, and \(d = x_{\mathrm{C}}\). Step 4 of example 8.49 gives \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1\) and \(x_2 = \tfrac32 a\cos\theta\), hence \(x_{\mathrm{C}} = x_2 + \tfrac a2\cos\varphi_2 = 2a\cos\theta\), and so \(q_1 = \theta\), \(q_2 = \varphi_2 - \varphi_1 = -2\theta\), \(q_3 = -\theta\) and \(d = 2a\cos\theta\). At \(\theta = 30°\) we have \((q_1, q_2, q_3, d) = (30°, -60°, -30°, 0{,}866\ \text{m})\), at \(\theta = 20°\) it is \((20°, -40°, -20°, 0{,}940\ \text{m})\).
  2. These are four joint variables but one single degree of freedom: step 5 of example 8.49 found five independent constraints on six configuration numbers. The variables cannot be coordinates, because by definition 8.46 there are \(n = 6 - 5 = 1\) of them, not four. Lemma 27.4 says nothing here, because the chain is not open. We can choose only one, \(q_1\), the others depend on it.
  3. Where in the proof of the lemma does this show? The bars \(AB\) and \(BC\) are an open chain with two joints, and the lemma gives it the coordinates \((q_1, q_2)\), since at a free end \(C\) everything is possible. The end \(C\) must lie on the guide, which is an additional constraint \(g(q_1, q_2) = a\sin q_1 + a\sin(q_1 + q_2) = 0\) (the height of the point \(C\)). This constraint is not one of the joint constraints of step 2 of the proof, it links the angles of the two joints. Because of it the constraints are no longer triangular as in step 3.
  4. At \(q = (30°, -60°)\) we have \(g = 0{,}25 - 0{,}25 = 0\), the end is at \((0{,}866;\ 0)\) m, on the guide. At \(q = (30°, 30°)\) we have \(g = 0{,}25 + 0{,}433 = 0{,}683\) m, the end \(C'\) is at \((0{,}683;\ 0{,}683)\) m and is not on the guide. At \(q_1 = 30°\) the constraint \(g = 0\) has only the solution \(q_2 = -60°\) (besides the folded position \(q_2 = 180°\), which we leave out, as in chapter 8), so \(q_2\) is not free.
  5. The matrix of derivatives of the constraint is the row \(\partial g/\partial\vek q = (a\cos q_1 + a\cos(q_1 + q_2),\ a\cos(q_1 + q_2)) = (0{,}866;\ 0{,}433)\) m at \(q = (30°, -60°)\), and it has one pivot. One independent constraint, the end \(C\) on the guide, so \(2 - 1 = 1\) degree of freedom, as in chapter 8.

Source: The mechanism and its numbers are from example 8.49. The conclusion about the closed chain and the constraint \(g\) are ours.

The example says what "open" means in the model. In a closed chain, such as the mechanism of a piston engine, the joint variables are not all independent, and we treat this in chapter 23. An open planar chain with \(n\) revolute joints has exactly \(n\) independent variables (lemma 27.4). That this also holds with prismatic joints and in space we quote from SPONG section 1.1.2 (PDF p. 28) without proof.

Now we can answer the third question from the start of the section: how far does the end of the arm reach?

Definition 27.6 Workspace (delovni prostor)

Let \(E\) be the end effector of a serial manipulator whose joint variables \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) can take values from sets \(S_{\mathrm{i}} \subseteq \mathbb{R}\), for an ideal revolute joint from all real numbers, for a prismatic joint from an interval limited by the stroke of the guide. The workspace (delovni prostor) is the set of positions of the point \(E\) that we get when \(\vek q\) runs through all values of \(S_1 \times \dots \times S_{\mathrm{n}}\). The position of \(E\) is a function of the column \(\vek q\), as equation (27.1) is for arm \(A\).

Source: SPONG section 1.1.4 (PDF p. 29): the workspace is the total volume swept out by the end effector when the manipulator executes all possible motions, constrained by the geometry of the manipulator and the mechanical constraints on the joints. SPONG splits it into the reachable workspace and the dexterous workspace, the points that the end effector can reach with an arbitrary orientation. We use the reachable workspace and do not define the dexterous one, because we write the orientation of a link only in the next section.

The name workspace has a reason: it is the space in which the robot can work. For arm \(A\) we compute it completely, it is the annulus in the figure below.

The answer and the way to it we read on two figures. Figure 27.10 shows the answer, the blue annulus around the joint \(O\). Figure 27.11 shows how the arm reaches one chosen point of the annulus.

In the figure the arm \(A\) is in the axes \(x_0\), \(y_0\) with its end at the point \(\vek p\). The blue segment is the distance \(r = |\vek p|\) of the end from \(O\). With the links it forms the triangle \(OBE\), which has at \(B\) the angle \(q_2\) between the extension of the first link and the second link. The proof reaches the point in two moves, as if we set the arm with our hands. First it chooses the \(q_2\) that gives the right distance: the distance of the end from \(O\) depends only on how much the arm is bent at the joint \(B\), not on where the arm points. The dashed grey arm has this \(q_2\) and \(q_1 = 0\). Its end \(\vek w\) is already at the distance \(r\), but in the wrong direction, at the angle \(\alpha\) instead of the angle \(\psi\) of the point \(\vek p\). Then it turns the whole arm by \(q_1 = \psi - \alpha\), and the end arrives at \(\vek p\).

Lemma 27.7 Workspace of the RR arm (delovni prostor roke RR)

Let an RR arm have the lengths \(l_1 > l_2 > 0\), revolute joints with no limit on the angle, and the end effector \(E\) at the end of the second link. Then its workspace is the closed annulus

\[\{\,\vek p \in \mathbb{R}^2 : l_1 - l_2 \le |\vek p| \le l_1 + l_2\,\} ,\]

where \(|\vek p|\) is the distance of the point from the joint \(O\). For \(l_1 = 1\) m and \(l_2 = 0{,}5\) m this is the annulus between \(0{,}5\) m and \(1{,}5\) m with the area \(2\pi = 6{,}2832\) m\(^2\).

Proof

We must show two things: that the position of the end effector always lies in the annulus, and that every point of the annulus is reached by some choice of \(q_1\), \(q_2\). For the first we compute the distance from equation (27.1), for the second we find the angles.

  1. Let \(\vek e(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)\). By equation (27.1), \(\vek p = l_1\vek e(q_1) + l_2\vek e(q_1 + q_2)\), the path from \(O\) along both links to the end.
  2. The squared distance is \(|\vek p|^2 = l_1^2(\cos^2 q_1 + \sin^2 q_1) + l_2^2(\cos^2(q_1 + q_2) + \sin^2(q_1 + q_2)) + 2l_1l_2\big(\cos q_1\cos(q_1 + q_2) + \sin q_1\sin(q_1 + q_2)\big)\). The first two brackets are \(1\) (lemma 8.11, point 1). The third is \(\cos q_2\): the addition formulas (corollary 8.16) with \(\alpha = q_1 + q_2\) and \(\beta = -q_1\), together with the oddness of sine and the evenness of cosine (lemma 8.11, point 4), give \(\cos q_2 = \cos(q_1 + q_2)\cos q_1 + \sin(q_1 + q_2)\sin q_1\). Hence
    \[|\vek p|^2 = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos q_2 . \tag{27.3}\]
    The angle \(q_1\) dropped out: the distance \(r\) depends only on the angle \(q_2\).
  3. Since \(-1 \le \cos q_2 \le 1\) (lemma 8.11, point 1), we have \((l_1 - l_2)^2 \le |\vek p|^2 \le (l_1 + l_2)^2\). Both sides are squares of nonnegative numbers, because \(l_1 > l_2\), so lemma 1.29 gives \(l_1 - l_2 \le |\vek p| \le l_1 + l_2\). In the figure the largest distance is at the extended arm, the smallest at the folded one.
  4. Conversely. Let \(\vek p\) be a point with \(r = |\vek p|\) and \(l_1 - l_2 \le r \le l_1 + l_2\), so \(r > 0\). Let \(c = (r^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2)/(2l_1l_2)\). From \(r^2 \ge (l_1 - l_2)^2\) it follows that \(c \ge -1\), and from \(r^2 \le (l_1 + l_2)^2\) that \(c \le 1\). By definition 8.10, point 1, there is \(q_2 \in [0, \pi]\) with \(\cos q_2 = c\).
  5. Let \(\vek w = (l_1 + l_2\cos q_2,\ l_2\sin q_2)\), the end of the arm with this \(q_2\) and \(q_1 = 0\). With the addition formulas (corollary 8.16), \(l_1\vek e(q_1) + l_2\vek e(q_1 + q_2) = \mat R(q_1)\vek w\) for every \(q_1\), where \(\mat R(q_1)\) is the matrix of lemma 8.41. By equation (27.3) for this \(q_2\), \(|\vek w|^2 = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2c = r^2\), so \(|\vek w| = r > 0\).
  6. By step 2 of the proof of lemma 8.41 there are angles \(\psi\) and \(\alpha\) with \(\vek e(\psi) = \vek p/r\) and \(\vek e(\alpha) = \vek w/r\). Let \(q_1 = \psi - \alpha\). Then \(\mat R(q_1)\vek w = r\,\mat R(\psi - \alpha)\vek e(\alpha) = r\,\vek e(\psi) = \vek p\) (addition formulas). The point \(\vek p\) is reached.

Source: The proof is not from the sources. SPONG section 3.3.1 (PDF p. 109-110) treats the same planar two-link arm with Denavit-Hartenberg matrices, it does not compute the workspace there. The proof and the cosine formula are ours.

The proof also shows how a point is reached. For \(\vek p = (0{,}866;\ 1{,}000)\) m we have \(r = 1{,}3229\) m, \(c = 0{,}5\), so \(q_2 = 60°\). The vector \(\vek w = (1{,}25;\ 0{,}433)\) m has the polar angle \(19{,}1°\), the point \(\vek p\) has the polar angle \(49{,}1°\), so \(q_1 = 49{,}1° - 19{,}1° = 30°\), as at the start (SymPy and NumPy).

Where did the assumption enter that the joint rotation is not limited? In step 4, which takes \(q_2\) anywhere between \(0\) and \(180°\), and in step 6, which takes an arbitrary \(q_1\). A real arm has a limited angle. If joint \(B\) allows only \(q_2\) between \(-150°\) and \(150°\), then by equation (27.3) the smallest distance is \(\sqrt{1 + 0{,}25 + \cos 150°} = 0{,}620\) m instead of \(0{,}5\) m (NumPy): the hole in the middle is larger, but the outer edge stays. The axes \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) cross at \(O\), from where both radii are measured.

In figure 27.10 the annulus around joint \(O\) is the workspace, and the two radii are the distances \(l_1 - l_2 = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_1 + l_2 = 1{,}5\) m. The outer edge is reached by the stretched arm with \(q_2 = 0\) (by equation (27.3) \(r = l_1 + l_2\)), the inner edge by the folded arm with \(q_2 = 180°\).

The annulus is not a disc. What is in the middle? Let us look at a point that lies near \(O\) but is not on it. The point \(U\) at the distance \(0{,}3\) m from \(O\) is unreachable: it would be reached if \(\cos q_2 = (0{,}09 - 1 - 0{,}25)/1 = -1{,}16\), which is less than \(-1\), so no such position exists. The inner radius \(0{,}5\) m is therefore a real obstacle: an arm with a longer first link cannot fold the second link so that the end comes closer than \(l_1 - l_2\). If the links were equally long, \(l_1 = l_2 = 1\) m, then by equation (27.3) \(|\vek p|^2 = 2 + 2\cos q_2\) and \(|\vek p|\) would reach zero at \(q_2 = 180°\): the hole disappears, the workspace is a disc of radius \(2\) m and area \(4\pi = 12{,}566\) m\(^2\). Step 6 of the proof would then fail at \(r = 0\), because the angle \(\alpha\) does not exist, but in that case the point \(O\) is reached at every \(q_1\) with \(q_2 = 180°\). For \(l_1 < l_2\) the hole stays too: by equation (27.3) \(|\vek p|^2 \ge l_1^2 + l_2^2 - 2l_1l_2 = (l_2 - l_1)^2 > 0\), and the same proof with \(l_2 - l_1\) in place of \(l_1 - l_2\) gives the annulus between \(l_2 - l_1\) and \(l_1 + l_2\). For \(l_1 = 0{,}5\) m and \(l_2 = 1\) m the end comes no closer to the point \(O\) than \(0{,}5\) m (NumPy). So there is a hole exactly when the two lengths differ, and its radius is \(|l_1 - l_2|\). The condition \(l_1 > l_2\) in the lemma only simplifies the notation, it does not cause the hole.

We checked the two radii of the annulus by tabulation: on a \(361 \times 361\) grid of the angles \(q_1\), \(q_2\) the smallest distance of the end is \(0{,}5000\) m and the largest \(1{,}5000\) m (NumPy).

What we have. An open planar chain of \(n\) rigid links with \(n\) revolute joints has \(n\) degrees of freedom, and its joint variables \(q_1, \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}\) are generalized coordinates of chapter 8 (lemma 27.4). For prismatic joints and chains in space we quoted this from SPONG without proof. For the planar RR arm we computed the position of the end, checked it with the double pendulum and determined the workspace. We used three things: the position of a rigid body in the plane (lemma 8.41), the independence of constraints by pivots (definition 8.21) and the addition formulas. In space the position of a link is not written with one angle. The next section therefore writes the rotation of a link with a matrix, called the orientation matrix, and then the position of a link together with a point, since we build the position of the end of the arm from the positions of the links. The idealizations of joints and links that we named at the start of the section hold for all sections of this chapter until we say otherwise.

Notation. \(n\) is the number of joints and the number of degrees of freedom (as in chapter 8), \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) the joint variables and generalized coordinates, \(\vek q\) their column. The letter \(\theta\) is the angle of a revolute joint, \(d\) the displacement of a prismatic one, \(l_{\mathrm{i}}\) the distance between the axes of joints \(i\) and \(i + 1\). The letters \(\mat R\), \(\mat T\), \(\mat J\), \(\mat C\), \(g\) and \(\tau\) have in this chapter a different meaning from earlier in the book, and we shall say it for each in the section where it first appears: in this section \(\mat R(\varphi)\) is still the matrix of the planar rotation of lemma 8.41.
