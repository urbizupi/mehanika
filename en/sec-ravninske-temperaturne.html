Chapter 14 Heat conduction

14.8 Plane temperature fields and stresses

In the previous section we computed stresses from temperature by a route called the uncoupled problem: first the field \(T\) from the conduction equation (theorem 14.8), then Hooke's law with temperature (theorem 3.23) as a body force and a boundary load (theorem 14.39). Here we ask two things. When does a body, heated with a temperature distribution that no longer changes, stay free of stress? And how large are the stresses when it does not, in the tube of chapter 11 that we loaded there with oil?

The starting point is the U-shaped piece of chapter 4. The steel piece stands free and nothing loads it. We heat it so that it is cold at the foot and warmer higher up, by 1 K per millimetre of height. The figure shows the isotherms (lines of constant temperature, izoterme), horizontal at the heights 10, 20 and 30 mm, labelled with \(T\) in kelvin above the temperature of the foot: neighbouring isotherms are 10 mm apart and differ by 10 K. Heat flows from the warmer to the colder part, so down the arms, as the flux arrows \(\vek q\) show (definition 14.3). Such a field does not change with time if the tops of the arms and the floor of the slot between them take in heat and the foot gives it off, the same amount through every square millimetre, while the other faces let no heat through. We call a field like this steady (stacionarno). In chapter 4 we used it only as the strain field \(\alpha\,g\,y\) and tested the sufficiency theorem for the compatibility equations with it. Now we know where the field comes from and can ask whether the piece is really free of stress. The answer will show what is special about temperature: the stretch \(\alpha\,T\) is the same in every direction, so for a given temperature there are far fewer ways to turn it into stress than for an arbitrary load.

Notation. The coefficient \(\kappa\) of the U piece in chapter 4 is written here as the product \(\alpha\,g\), because in this chapter \(\kappa\) is the thermal diffusivity.

14.8.1 When a heated body stays free of stress

We state the theorem for two cases. The first is a body in which the temperature is a linear function of all three coordinates, like the U piece. The second is a long prism with cross-section \(R\) in which the temperature is a function of \(x\) and \(y\) only and has a vanishing Laplace operator (definition 11.5), \(\nabla^2 T = T_{\mathrm{xx}} + T_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\). With constant conductivity and no source this is exactly the steady equation by theorem 14.8: from \(\rho c\,T_{\mathrm{t}} = k\,\nabla^2 T + Q\) with \(T_{\mathrm{t}} = 0\) and \(Q = 0\) we get \(\nabla^2 T = 0\). For the prism we also need a model, which we call plane strain with temperature: the displacement along the axis \(z\) is zero, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) (definition 11.32). This is an idealization, and we name it: neighbouring cross-sections cannot shift relative to one another because a rigid wall holds them. Why may we take it? It is right where the prism is clamped between much stiffer bodies, and in the middle part of a very long prism, where by symmetry the cross-sections cannot bulge. It does not hold at free ends: there is no axial force there and the solution is a different one (for the tube it is given by theorem 14.47 with the axial strain \(\varepsilon_0\)). It changes the result in two ways. The wall brings the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\), which for the steel of chapter 3 and \(T = 100\) K is \(-196\,000 \cdot 15 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 100 = -294\) MPa, and it raises the stretch in the plane of the cross-section by the factor \(1 + \nu = 1{,}3\) (part d of figure 14.79 below). What happens when the cross-section is not simply connected is shown by lemma 14.46.

Why is a heated body ever free of stress, and why for exactly these two temperatures? We build the answer on figure 14.79, from a small piece of material up to the whole body.

In thought we cut the body into small square cells of side \(a\). Take one, free, and heat it by \(T\). Nothing holds it, so it is free of stress, and by Hooke's law with temperature (theorem 3.23) it grows the same in every direction, each side by \(\alpha\,T\,a\) (part a). For steel and 100 K this is \(15 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 100 = 0{,}0015\) of the side, the figure shows much more. The cell stays a square, only a larger one.

The question is whether the heated cells fit together again into a whole body, without gaps and without overlap. If they do, no cell needs a force from its neighbour and the body stays free of stress. Part b shows a grid of two rows of cells, the lower at the temperature \(T_1\) and the upper at the higher \(T_2\). The upper row grows more, and the rows fit into an arch, the longer one on the outside. They fit because with a linear temperature the length of a row grows in proportion to its height, exactly as the length of a circular arc grows in proportion to its distance from the centre. In part c one cell is warmer than all its neighbours. It would grow beyond its place: the lightly filled band is space that two cells would occupy at once. That cannot be, so the neighbours squeeze it and it pushes them apart, and this is stress. Such a temperature has a hump, in the hot cell \(T_{\mathrm{xx}} < 0\) and \(T_{\mathrm{yy}} < 0\).

The calculation says the same. The field of stretches \(\alpha\,T\) in all directions is the strain of a whole body only if it satisfies the compatibility equations (theorem 1.133), which are exactly the condition that the cells fit. The first equation, for the plane \(x\), \(y\), is \(\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial y^2 + \partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}/\partial x^2 = 2\,\partial^2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}}/\partial x\,\partial y\)\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx},\mathrm{yy}} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy},\mathrm{xx}} = 2\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy},\mathrm{xy}}\). With \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \alpha\,T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) it says \(\alpha\,(T_{\mathrm{yy}} + T_{\mathrm{xx}}) = 0\). This is the Laplace condition: a hump in one direction is allowed if a trough in the other balances it exactly, while the hot cell with a hump in both directions is not. For the full three-dimensional field the second equation gives in addition \(\alpha\,(T_{\mathrm{yy}} + T_{\mathrm{zz}}) = 0\) and the third \(\alpha\,(T_{\mathrm{zz}} + T_{\mathrm{xx}}) = 0\), and all six equations together require all second derivatives of \(T\) to vanish, that is \(T\) must be linear as in part b.

For the prism the only difference comes from the walls (part d). The prism is held by rigid walls that do not let it grow along \(z\). So they press on its ends with the stress \(E\,\alpha\,T\), that is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\). Squeezed material with \(\nu > 0\) widens sideways, like any compressed bar (Poisson's ratio \(\nu\)), so the cross-section grows by \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,T\) instead of \(\alpha\,T\), as the blue measure shows (step 2 of the proof below). For it the same first equation holds with \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\) in place of \(\alpha\), and the others hold because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), there are no shear strains and nothing depends on \(z\). That is why the theorem has a linear temperature in the first case and a plane one with a vanishing Laplace operator in the second.

Theorem 14.44 Steady temperature without stress (stacionarna temperatura brez napetosti)

Let the body be isotropic, homogeneous and linearly elastic with \(E > 0\), \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) (theorem 3.64) and a temperature expansion coefficient \(\alpha\) independent of temperature. Let \(T\) be the change of temperature from the temperature at which the body is free of stress. There is no body force and the surface of the body is free, in case 2 below only the lateral surface (no force acts on it). A solution of the thermoelastic problem is a displacement field \(\vek u\), continuously differentiable with continuous second derivatives, whose stresses by equation (3.32) satisfy the equilibrium equations and the given boundary conditions. Then:

  1. If the body is simply connected (definition 1.142) and \(T\) is a linear function of \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), all stresses are zero in every solution, and the solution is unique up to a small rigid motion.
  2. If the body is a prism \(R \times (-l, l)\) with simply connected cross-section \(R\), \(T = T(x, y)\) has a vanishing Laplace operator, there is no body force, no force on the lateral surface, and the force \(\mp E\,\alpha\,T\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) on the ends \(z = \pm l\) (which a rigid wall holding \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) supplies), then in every solution \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\), and the solution is unique up to a small rigid motion.

In both cases the strains have this form: in the first \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha\,T\) and all shear strains zero\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}} = \alpha\,T\,\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), in the second \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \hat\alpha\,T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all shear strains zero, where \(\hat\alpha = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\).

Proof

We must show that the stresses of the theorem, together with the strains they give by Hooke's law, belong to a displacement field that satisfies all conditions, and that this is the only solution. The route is: stresses, strains, compatibility, displacement, uniqueness.

1. Stresses and strains.

  1. Case 1: all stresses are zero. By equation (3.31), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}} = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \alpha\,T\) and the shear strains are zero. This is the free cell of part a.
  2. Case 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = 0\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\). By equation (3.31), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \tfrac1E\,(0 - \nu\,(0 - E\,\alpha\,T)) + \alpha\,T = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,T\), the same for \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{yy}}\), and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \tfrac1E\,(-E\,\alpha\,T) + \alpha\,T = 0\). This is the prism between the walls of part d.

2. Compatibility.

  1. Case 1: all second derivatives of the strains of step 1 are zero, because second derivatives of a linear function are zero, so all six equations (1.76) hold as \(0 = 0\). The cells fit into the arch.
  2. Case 2 (prism): equations 2 to 6 hold, because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\), all shear strains are zero and nothing depends on \(z\) (every term contains a derivative in \(z\) or a component that is zero). Equation 1 is \(\hat\alpha\,(T_{\mathrm{yy}} + T_{\mathrm{xx}}) - 0 = \hat\alpha\,\nabla^2 T = 0\).

3. Displacement.

  1. The set \(D\) is the interior of the body in case 1, and the prism \(R \times (-l, l)\) in case 2. In case 2 it is simply connected: for a closed curve \((\gamma(s), z(s))\) in \(D\) we shrink the first part in \(R\) by a shrinking to a point (because \(R\) is simply connected) and the second part by \((1 - t)\,z(s)\), and all intermediate stages stay in \(D\).
  2. By theorem 4.120 there is a displacement field \(\vek u\), continuously differentiable with continuous second derivatives, whose strains are those of step 1: in case 1 the cells fit together into the body, in case 2 the prism between the walls.
  3. By theorem 3.23, equations (3.31) and (3.32) are inverse to one another, so the same strains give the same stresses. For the strains of step 1 these are the stresses of the theorem: zero in the free cell and the push of the walls in the prism. In case 1 this is also the computation \(\ten\sigma = \lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,T\,\mat I)\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \alpha\,T\,\mat I) = \mat 0\) from \(\ten\varepsilon = \alpha\,T\,\mat I\).

4. Equilibrium and boundary conditions.

  1. In case 1 the stresses are zero, so the equilibrium equations, \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}/\partial x + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{yx}}/\partial y + \partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}/\partial z = 0\) and the same for the directions \(y\) and \(z\)\(\sigma_{\mathrm{ji},\mathrm{j}} = 0\), hold. In case 2 the only nonzero component is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T(x, y)\) and the equilibrium \(\partial\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}/\partial z = 0\) holds, because \(T\) does not depend on \(z\), the same on every section of the prism.
  2. On the lateral surface the normal \(\vek n\) has only \(x\) and \(y\) components. By Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26), \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = \vek 0\), since \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zy}}\) are zero. At the end \(z = l\) the normal is \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) and \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), at the end \(z = -l\) it is \(\ten\sigma\,(-\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}) = +E\,\alpha\,T\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). This is the force of the theorem, the arrows of the walls in part d.

5. Uniqueness.

  1. Let \(\tilde{\vek u}\) be another solution. By equation (3.32) the difference of the stresses is \(\lambda\,\tr(\ten\varepsilon - \tilde{\ten\varepsilon})\,\mat I + 2\mu\,(\ten\varepsilon - \tilde{\ten\varepsilon})\), in which \(T\) cancels. This is the stress of the field \(\vek u - \tilde{\vek u}\) by the ordinary Hooke's law, and it is in equilibrium with no forces on the surface. By theorem 11.4, item 2, the difference has zero stress and strain, so \(\vek u\) and \(\tilde{\vek u}\) have the same stresses and differ by a small rigid motion.

In the figure the body before heating is grey dashed, solution \(\vek u\) orange and the other solution \(\tilde{\vek u}\) blue. Both are the same body, only moved: the difference of displacements is at every point the same arrow, a rigid motion, and the stress of the difference is zero, because the body does not deform in it.

Source: BARBER §14.3 (PDF p. 236): a steady temperature field without a source causes no stress in a free, simply connected plane plate. SADD §12.5 (PDF p. 386): the same for the plane problem, and for multiply connected bodies a steady temperature field normally gives rise to in-plane stresses. The proof by compatibility, theorem 4.120 and uniqueness is not from the sources. BARBER gives only the plane problem. We split it into two cases, because the first holds in three dimensions and the second only with a rigid wall at the ends.

The proof showed three things. First, the reason is not in the physics of heat but in geometry: the heated cells must fit together, as the arch does and the hot cell does not. Second, simple connectedness of the body makes compatibility sufficient (theorem 4.120). Third, the uniqueness of chapter 11 says there is no other solution. In case 1 there is therefore no stress, however we look for it. In case 2 there is no stress in the plane of the cross-section, and the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\) is carried by the rigid wall.

Let us test the theorem on the U piece. The temperature \(T = g\,y\) is linear, so case 1 applies, and the piece is free of stress. Its strain \(\alpha\,g\,y\,\mat I\) is the same as in chapter 4, and the displacement of the point \(B\) relative to \(A\) computed there, \(\vek u(B) = (0{,}0105,\ -0{,}0030,\ 0)\) mm, is now the displacement of the free heated piece. We draw the piece before heating grey and dashed, and over it in orange the piece after heating, with the displacement magnified 400 times so that the eye can see it. The points \(A\) and \(B\) on the arms are grey dots before heating and orange dots after it. The arms move apart: the distance between \(A\) and \(B\) grows by \(\Delta = 0{,}0105\) mm, half of it at each arm, which at this magnification is \(4{,}2\) mm. The foot arches upward: its middle stays above the dashed chord between its ends. Why so? The top is warmer than the foot, so the horizontal fibres above are longer than those below, and the piece closes up into an arch like the cells in part b of figure 14.79. The piece deforms and there is no stress. The everyday impression that a piece with an uneven temperature must be under stress is therefore wrong: stress does not depend on the temperature being uneven but on whether the stretch \(\alpha\,T\) is compatible.

The theorem has one condition that we must test with an example where it fails. What happens when the temperature is not steady? Take the beam of chapter 5 and raise its top face by 20 K at once and hold it. The temperature along the height is then not linear (\(T_{\mathrm{t}} \ne 0\), hence also \(\nabla^2 T = T_{\mathrm{yy}} \ne 0\)): the layer under the hot face is the hot cell of part c, squeezed by its colder surroundings. The stress at the top face is \(-53{,}04\) MPa at \(\mathrm{Fo} = \kappa t/h^2 = 0{,}0005\) (section 14.7, the example of the heated beam). Only after a long time, when the temperature along the height evens out into a linear field, does just the curvature of chapter 5 remain. The theorem therefore holds for the steady state and not for the way to it. A source in the body (\(Q \ne 0\)) or dependence on \(z\) in a thin plate also break it: in a plate with free faces \(z = \pm h\) a \(T(x, y)\) with nonzero \(T_{\mathrm{yy}}\) does not fit the second compatibility equation of the full three-dimensional strain, so plane stress (definition 11.36) is a model for a thin plate and not a solution (chapter 11). Such a model keeps the condition \(\nabla^2 T = 0\) for the plane and the in-plane stresses are zero (BARBER, §14.3, PDF p. 236), but we do not prove more precise statements here.

14.8.2 Edge curvature and heat flux (Dundurs' theorem)

The U piece bent. Can the bending be predicted from the heat flux, without computing displacements? In the piece the foot is cold and the arms warm. Does the curvature depend on the heat that flows through the edge? This is the content of the next corollary. It carries Dundurs' name because he was the first to prove it (BARBER, §14.3.1, PDF p. 237), so the name is the author's and not the subject's.

First let us see by hand why a heat flux curves. From the piece we cut a horizontal strip of length \(L\) and height \(h\). Heat flows down through it, so it is warmer above, at the temperature \(T_2\), and colder below, at \(T_1\). Every fibre grows by its own temperature: the top one to \(L\,(1 + \alpha\,T_2)\), the bottom one to \(L\,(1 + \alpha\,T_1)\). A strip with a longer top fibre is an arc. The end sections, which were parallel, turn towards each other by the angle \(\varphi\). The length of an arc is the angle times the radius, and the radii of the two fibres differ by \(h\), so the difference of the fibre lengths is \(h\,\varphi\):

\[\varphi = \frac{L\,\alpha\,(T_2 - T_1)}{h} , \qquad \frac1R = \frac{\varphi}{L} = \alpha\,\frac{T_2 - T_1}{h} .\]

The curvature \(1/R\) is therefore \(\alpha\) times the drop of temperature per unit height, and by Fourier's law that drop is set by the flux, \((T_2 - T_1)/h = |q_{\mathrm{y}}|/k\) (definition 14.3). We insert the foot of the U piece:

quantity computation value
length and height of the strip \(L = 20\) mm, \(h = 10\) mm
temperature difference \(1\ \text{K/mm} \cdot 10\) mm \(T_2 - T_1 = 10\) K
the top fibre is longer by \(15 \cdot 10^{-6} \cdot 10 \cdot 20\) mm \(0{,}003\) mm
angle between the end sections \(0{,}003/10\) \(\varphi = 3{,}0 \cdot 10^{-4}\)
radius of curvature \(20\ \text{mm}/(3{,}0 \cdot 10^{-4})\) \(R = 66{,}7\) m
rise of the middle above the chord \(L^2/(8R)\) \(f = 7{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) mm

The angle \(\varphi\) is the same as the rotation \(\omega_{\mathrm{xy}}(B) = 3{,}0 \cdot 10^{-4}\) of chapter 4. The rise \(f\) of the middle above the chord follows from Pythagoras' theorem for half the chord, \((L/2)^2 + (R - f)^2 = R^2\), where we neglect \(f^2\) beside \(2Rf\): the error is in the ratio \(f/(2R) = 6 \cdot 10^{-9}\). In the figure the angle is \(0{,}8\) rad, about 2700 times larger than the true one, otherwise we would not see the arc.

The picture holds for a strip on its own. The corollary says the same for every point of the body and with exact conditions.

Corollary 14.45 Dundurs' theorem on edge curvature (Dundursov izrek o ukrivljenosti roba)

Let case 1 or case 2 of theorem 14.44 hold, let \(\hat\alpha\) be as in the theorem (equal to \(\alpha\) in case 1) and \(k\) the thermal conductivity (definition 14.3). Then at every point of the body the second derivative of the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{y}}\) in \(x\), at fixed \(y\) and \(z\), is

\[\frac{\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}}{\partial x^2} = -\hat\alpha\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} = \frac{\hat\alpha}{k}\,q_{\mathrm{y}} , \tag{14.44}\]

where \(q_{\mathrm{y}}\) is the component of the heat flux in the direction \(y\).

Proof

We must show that the strains of theorem 14.44 give equation (14.44).

  1. By theorem 14.44, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\), so \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial y + \partial u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x = 0\) (definition 1.121). We differentiate in \(x\): \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x^2 = -\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\,\partial y\), where the order of differentiation can be exchanged because the second derivatives are continuous (theorem 1.131). In figure 14.81 \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) means that the sections stay perpendicular to the fibres.
  2. By the theorem, \(\partial u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{xx}} = \hat\alpha\,T\). The derivative of this equality in \(y\) gives \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{x}}/\partial x\,\partial y = \hat\alpha\,T_{\mathrm{y}}\). This is the difference of the stretches of the top and the bottom fibre per unit height. From step 1 follows the first equality in (14.44).
  3. By Fourier's law \(q_{\mathrm{y}} = -k\,T_{\mathrm{y}}\) (definition 14.3), so \(-\hat\alpha\,T_{\mathrm{y}} = \hat\alpha\,q_{\mathrm{y}}/k\), which is the flux through the strip.

Source: BARBER §14.3.1 (14.20), (14.21) (PDF p. 237) states the result under the name Dundurs' theorem and \(\delta = \alpha\,(1 + \nu)/k\) for the thermal distortivity. The proof is ours, since the book does not show it.

The derivative \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x^2\) is, to first order, the curvature of a line that was parallel to the axis \(x\) before heating (for small slopes the curvature is the second derivative, as for the beam in chapter 5). The corollary therefore says: an edge through which heat flows in the direction \(+y\) gets the curvature \(\hat\alpha\,q_{\mathrm{y}}/k\), and an edge without flux stays straight. On the U piece \(T_{\mathrm{y}} = g = 1\) K/mm and \(\alpha = 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K, so \(\partial^2 u_{\mathrm{y}}/\partial x^2 = -\alpha\,g = -1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-5}\) 1/mm. This is \(-1/R\) of the table above, with \(R\) \(= 66{,}7\) m. A negative second derivative means that the edge arches upward, like the bottom fibre of the strip and like the foot of the piece. In chapter 4 the displacement \(u_{\mathrm{y}}(B) = -0{,}0030\) mm includes the rotation of the piece as a whole, so it is not directly comparable with this number. The beam of chapter 5 has the same relation \(\alpha g\): at \(g = 500\) K/m its curvature is \(v'' = -\alpha g = -7{,}5 \cdot 10^{-3}\) 1/m (section 5.5.4). There we obtained it from the stretch of the fibres, now it is expressed by the heat flux \(k\,g\) through the beam.

Where does Dundurs' theorem fail? Equation (14.44) is written for the field that theorem 14.44 allows. The next section shows what happens when the cross-section has a hole.

14.8.3 A ring with a hole: the jump of displacement at a cut

Now we remove what the theorem requires. Take the ring of chapters 4 and 11, radii \(a = 40\) mm and \(b = 60\) mm, with the inner wall 100 K warmer than the outer, no force on the surfaces. The ring is exactly the body that by corollary 4.114 is not simply connected. Heat flows outward through the wall, as the arrows \(\vek q\) show, and the isotherms are circles. From the Laplace operator in polar coordinates (corollary 11.18) it follows that the field \(T(r) = A + B\,\ln r\) is steady and without a source, since \(T'' + T'/r = -B/r^2 + B/r^2 = 0\). With our boundary condition \(T = 100\,\ln(b/r)/\ln(b/a)\), so \(B = -100/\ln 1{,}5 = -246{,}63\) K, and \(T = 100\) K at \(r = 40\) mm, \(70{,}95\) K at \(45\) mm, \(44{,}97\) K at \(50\) mm, \(21{,}46\) K at \(55\) mm and \(0\) at \(60\) mm. The flux through every circle per unit thickness is the same, \(2\pi\,k\,|B| = k \cdot 1549{,}6\) K, because \(q_{\mathrm{r}} = -k\,B/r\) and the circumference is \(2\pi\,r\).

Is the ring free of stress nevertheless? Theorem 14.44 does not say, because the ring is not simply connected. Let us see what would happen if it were free of stress, and make the cut \(S\) that turns the ring into a simply connected body.

First by eye. The cut ring is simply connected, so it may heat up freely without stress, every small piece by its own temperature, by \(\hat\alpha\,T\) in the plane (the wall holds \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\)). Do the faces of the cut still touch after heating? They would if every circle stayed closed, that is if its circumference and its radius grew in the same ratio: the displacement would be \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = \hat\alpha\,T\,r\). But then the radial fibres would stretch by

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{\mathrm{r}}}{\mathrm{d}r} = \hat\alpha\,T + \hat\alpha\,r\,T' = \hat\alpha\,T + \hat\alpha\,B ,\]

and not by \(\hat\alpha\,T\), as the temperature requires (here we used \(r\,T' = B\)). The difference \(\hat\alpha\,B\) is the same at every radius, with our data \(1{,}95 \cdot 10^{-5} \cdot (-246{,}63) = -0{,}00481\). So both cannot hold at once. The free cut ring gives the radial fibres what they require, so every circle lacks the share \(\hat\alpha\,|B| = 0{,}00481\) of the circumference that its new radius needs: the ring opens at the cut by the angle \(w = 2\pi \cdot 0{,}00481 = 0{,}03022\) rad. Where does the difference come from? The inner wall is warmer, so towards the axis every piece grows more than its outer neighbour, and the circumferences do not keep up with the radii. The lemma says this exactly.

Lemma 14.46 A ring with a logarithmic temperature field (obroč z logaritemskim temperaturnim poljem)

Let the ring \(a \le r \le b\), \(|z| < c\) be as in lemma 1.137, with the material of theorem 14.44, and \(T = A + B\,\ln r\) with \(B \ne 0\). Let \(\hat\alpha = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\) and \(S\) the cut, the set of points of the ring with \(y = 0\) and \(x > 0\). Then:

  1. the displacement field \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = \hat\alpha\,r\,(T - B)\), \(u_\theta = \hat\alpha\,B\,r\,\theta\) for \(0 < \theta < 2\pi\), \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) has on the cut ring the strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \hat\alpha\,T\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and all shear strains zero, which are the strains of theorem 14.44, case 2,
  2. the jump of the displacement across the cut is \(\vek u^+ - \vek u^- = -2\pi\,\hat\alpha\,B\,r_{\mathrm{C}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at a point of the cut at distance \(r_{\mathrm{C}}\) from the axis, so it is different from zero, and its size grows linearly with the radius,
  3. on the whole ring there is no displacement field with these strains, and therefore no solution of the thermoelastic problem on the ring with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) has zero stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\).
Proof

We must show that the field of item 1 gives the stated strains, then compute the jump and use lemma 4.121. The steps run on the right part of figure 14.82.

  1. Strains in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.21): \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r = \hat\alpha\,(T - B) + \hat\alpha\,r\,T' = \hat\alpha\,(T - B) + \hat\alpha\,B = \hat\alpha\,T\), because \(r\,T' = B\). Further \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u_{\mathrm{r}}/r + (1/r)\,\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta = \hat\alpha\,(T - B) + \hat\alpha\,B = \hat\alpha\,T\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\,(\partial u_\theta/\partial r - u_\theta/r + (1/r)\,\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta) = \tfrac12\,(\hat\alpha\,B\,\theta - \hat\alpha\,B\,\theta + 0) = 0\). The remaining three components are zero, since \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and nothing depends on \(z\). This is item 1: every small piece of the cut ring grows in the plane by \(\hat\alpha\,T\) as if it were free.
  2. At a point of the cut \(C = (r_{\mathrm{C}}, 0)\), \(\theta \to 0\) for neighbours above the cut (\(C^+\)) and \(\theta \to 2\pi\) for neighbours below it (\(C^-\)). The displacement above the cut has \(u_\theta = 0\), below it \(u_\theta = 2\pi\,\hat\alpha\,B\,r_{\mathrm{C}}\), and \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the same on both sides. Since \(\vek e_\theta = \vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = 2\pi\), \(\vek u^+ - \vek u^- = (0 - 2\pi\,\hat\alpha\,B\,r_{\mathrm{C}})\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), which is the gap \(w\,r\) between \(C^-\) and \(C^+\). This equals \(\vek a + \mat W\,\vek r_{\mathrm{C}}\) of lemma 4.121 with \(\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(W_{\mathrm{yx}} = -2\pi\,\hat\alpha\,B \ne 0\).
  3. Lemma 4.121 needs strains with continuous first and second derivatives that satisfy the compatibility equations at every point of the whole ring. The strains of item 1 are \(\hat\alpha\,(A + B\,\ln r)\) or zero, at \(r \ge a > 0\) they have continuous derivatives of all orders, and they are the strains of case 2 of theorem 14.44 with \(T = A + B\,\ln r\), which has a vanishing Laplace operator (corollary 11.18). By step 4 of the proof of the theorem they therefore satisfy all six compatibility equations at every point of the ring, on the cut too, since that computation does not need simple connectedness. By lemma 4.121, item 3, a displacement field on the whole ring with strains \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ij}}\) exists exactly when \(\vek a = \vek 0\) and \(\mat W = \mat 0\). Here \(\mat W \ne \mat 0\), so there is no such field. A solution with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = 0\) would by Hooke's law with temperature have the strains of item 1 (step 2 of the proof of theorem 14.44), hence a displacement field on the whole ring.

Source: BARBER §14.3 (PDF p. 236) and SADD §12.5 (PDF p. 386) state that for multiply connected bodies stresses arise, and BARBER connects them with a dislocation in the hole. The derivation with the displacement of item 1 and the jump from lemma 4.121 are ours.

Let us see what the jump says. The cut ring after heating is drawn in orange, and the grey dashed circles show the ring before heating. If we cut the ring while rigid walls hold its faces \(z = \pm c\) (\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) as in the prism), the faces of the cut turn relative to one another by the angle \(w = 2\pi\,\hat\alpha\,|B| = 0{,}03022\) rad (\(1{,}73°\)). The gap between the points \(C^+\) and \(C^-\) at the radius \(r\) is therefore \(w\,r\), at the radius 50 mm \(1{,}511\) mm. This is with \(\hat\alpha = (1 + \nu)\,\alpha\). For a free thin ring (plane stress) the in-plane stretch would be \(\alpha\,T\) and we would get \(0{,}02324\) rad and \(1{,}162\) mm. The factor \(1 + \nu = 1{,}3\) between the numbers comes from the axial strain: in the prism with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) the opposing \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) raises the in-plane strain from \(\alpha\,T\) to \((1 + \nu)\,\alpha\,T\), in a plate with free faces there is no such stress. In the figure the gap is drawn magnified 12 times, the shape of the rest of the ring is not to scale, and only the angle between the faces is computed.

Why, then, can the whole ring not be free of stress? The whole ring has no cut, so it must close the gap, and that needs a force that pulls the faces of the cut together. The ring therefore gets stresses from a steady heat flow through the wall, even if no force acts from outside. Theorem 14.44 was not broken, but its assumption of simple connectedness is essential. The link with the flux is that \(2\pi\,B\) is exactly the flux through a closed curve around the hole divided by \(-k\): in a simply connected body this flux is zero by the divergence theorem (theorem 4.14, theorem 14.8, \(\nabla^2 T = 0\)), around a hole it is not, because heat enters through the inner wall and leaves through the outer (the arrows). For a hole through which no flux flows \(B = 0\) and there would be no stress. So much for the reason. How large the stresses are we compute in the next section.

14.8.4 A tube with a steady radial temperature

We know the temperature of the tube. The question is what stresses it causes. The tube of chapter 11, \(a = 40\) mm, \(b = 60\) mm, was loaded there with oil at 30 MPa, which gave a hoop stress of 78 MPa at the inner wall. Now the tube has no pressure and its inner wall is warmer than the outer. We shall compare the stress we get with the stress from the oil, and, more interesting, add them.

What do we expect before we compute? Figure 14.83 shows on the left the cross-section of the wall, 100 K warmer inside than outside. In thought we divide it in the middle into an inner and an outer half. The inner one is warmer and, if free, would grow more than the outer one, beyond the place where the outer one begins: both would occupy the lightly filled band at once, as with the hot cell in figure 14.79. The outer half does not allow that. It squeezes the inner half along the circumference and is itself stretched along the circumference. So we expect a compressive hoop stress at the inner wall and a tensile one at the outer. How much, the calculation tells.

From history

The thermal stresses in a cylinder and a sphere, where the temperature is a function only of the distance from the axis or the centre, were investigated by Duhamel and Neumann, whom chapter 3 already met. Timoshenko writes that C. W. Borchardt, among Neumann's pupils, contributed to the theory of thermal stresses: for both cases he gave solutions in the form of integrals for any temperature distribution (TIMO, PDF p. 286). This is the reason why for the tube we are interested in a general \(T(r)\) and not in one special case: the solution we shall write down contains an integral of the temperature over the radius, so it has that form.

We return to the tube without pressure whose inner wall is warmer than the outer. Take a general temperature \(T(r)\) and write the solution as an integral. Let \(J(r) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^r s\,T(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) (here \(s\) is the auxiliary variable of the integral), \(\zeta = \alpha\,(1 + \nu)/(1 - \nu)\) and \(\Gamma = E\,\alpha/(1 - \nu)\). Then \(J' = r\,T\) by the fundamental theorem (theorem 3.55), because \(T\) is continuous.

Theorem 14.47 A tube with a steady radial temperature (cev s stacionarno radialno temperaturo)

Let the body be the tube \(a \le r \le b\), \(-l \le z \le l\) as in theorem 11.28, with a material that has \(E\), \(\nu\) and \(\alpha\) independent of temperature, no body force and no pressure on either lateral surface. The temperature \(T = T(r)\) is a continuously differentiable function on \([a, b]\) (change from the stress-free temperature). Let \(\varepsilon_0\) be a constant axial strain. Let

\[A_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{\Gamma\,J(b)}{b^2 - a^2} , \qquad B_{\mathrm{T}} = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,a^2 . \tag{14.45}\]

Then:

  1. the displacement field
    \[\vek u = \Big(\zeta\,\frac{J(r)}{r} + \Big(\frac{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\,A_{\mathrm{T}}}{E} - \nu\,\varepsilon_0\Big)\,r + \frac{(1 + \nu)\,B_{\mathrm{T}}}{E}\,\frac1r\Big)\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \varepsilon_0\,z\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}} \tag{14.46}\]
    is a solution of the thermoelastic problem, with stresses in local axes
    \[\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A_{\mathrm{T}} - \frac{B_{\mathrm{T}}}{r^2} - \Gamma\,\frac{J(r)}{r^2} , \\ &\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A_{\mathrm{T}} + \frac{B_{\mathrm{T}}}{r^2} + \Gamma\,\Big(\frac{J(r)}{r^2} - T(r)\Big) , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2\nu\,A_{\mathrm{T}} + E\,\varepsilon_0 - \Gamma\,T(r) , \\ &\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{14.47}\]
    and the force on the ends \(z = \pm l\) is \(\pm\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\),
  2. the radial and hoop stresses are independent of \(\varepsilon_0\), and every solution of this problem has the same stresses and strains and differs by a small rigid motion,
  3. at \(\varepsilon_0 = 0\) (the ends held by a rigid wall) this is plane strain, and at \(\varepsilon_0 = 2\,(1 - \nu)\,A_{\mathrm{T}}/E\) the resultant axial force is zero, \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,2\pi\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = 0\), and on both lateral surfaces \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\).
Proof

We must show that the field (14.46) satisfies the three requirements of a solution (definition 11.1, with Hooke's law with temperature), and then use uniqueness. The route is that of theorem 11.28: strains, stresses, equilibrium, boundary conditions. We use the constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) (definition 3.18) and \(\beta = (3\lambda + 2\mu)\,\alpha = E\,\alpha/(1 - 2\nu)\).

1. Strains and stresses.

  1. The field (14.46) is \(u = \zeta\,J/r + C_1\,r + C_2/r\) with \(C_1 = (1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)\,A_{\mathrm{T}}/E - \nu\,\varepsilon_0\) and \(C_2 = (1 + \nu)\,B_{\mathrm{T}}/E\). By corollary 11.21, \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = u' = \zeta\,(T - J/r^2) + C_1 - C_2/r^2\) (since \(J' = rT\)), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = u/r = \zeta\,J/r^2 + C_1 + C_2/r^2\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = \varepsilon_0\) and the remaining three components are zero as in step 1 of the proof of theorem 11.28.
  2. Hooke's law with temperature, equation (3.32), gives \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} + \lambda\,(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}}) - \beta\,T\) and similarly for \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\). The terms with \(T\) in \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) are \((\lambda + 2\mu)\,\zeta\,T - \beta\,T = 0\), because \(\zeta = \beta/(\lambda + 2\mu)\), which we check from \(\lambda + 2\mu = E\,(1 - \nu)/((1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu))\). The terms with \(J/r^2\) are \(-(\lambda + 2\mu)\,\zeta\,J/r^2 + \lambda\,\zeta\,J/r^2 = -2\mu\,\zeta\,J/r^2\), and since \(2\mu\,\zeta = E\,\alpha/(1 - \nu) = \Gamma\) we get \(-\Gamma\,J/r^2\). The remaining terms make up \(A_{\mathrm{T}} - B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^2\) with \(A_{\mathrm{T}} = 2(\lambda + \mu)\,C_1 + \lambda\,\varepsilon_0\) and \(B_{\mathrm{T}} = 2\mu\,C_2\) (SymPy, as in step 9 of the proof of theorem 11.28). So \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the one of (14.47).
  3. Likewise: the terms with \(T\) in \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) are \(\lambda\,\zeta\,T - \beta\,T = (\lambda - \lambda - 2\mu)\,\zeta\,T = -\Gamma\,T\), the terms with \(J/r^2\) are \(-\lambda\,\zeta\,J/r^2 + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\zeta\,J/r^2 = \Gamma\,J/r^2\), and the rest is \(A_{\mathrm{T}} + B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^2\). In \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) the terms with \(T\) are \(\lambda\,\zeta\,T - \beta\,T = -\Gamma\,T\), the rest \(2\lambda\,C_1 + (\lambda + 2\mu)\,\varepsilon_0\) is constant and equals \(2\nu\,A_{\mathrm{T}} + E\,\varepsilon_0\) (from \(\lambda/(\lambda + \mu) = 2\nu\) and \(\mu\,(3\lambda + 2\mu)/(\lambda + \mu) = E\), as in step 6 of the proof of theorem 11.28). The shear stresses are \(2\mu\) times zero shear strains. In figure 14.83 the term \(-\Gamma\,T\) is the squeeze of the warmer inner half: where the material is warmer, the hoop stress is lower.

2. Equilibrium and boundary conditions.

  1. The radial equilibrium equation (corollary 11.25, \(\vek b = \vek 0\)) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\). The derivative from (14.47) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = 2B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^3 - \Gamma\,T/r + 2\Gamma\,J/r^3\) (since \(J' = rT\)), and the difference is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta} = -2B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^2 - 2\Gamma\,J/r^2 + \Gamma\,T\). The sum \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r\) is \(2B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^3 - \Gamma\,T/r + 2\Gamma\,J/r^3 - 2B_{\mathrm{T}}/r^3 - 2\Gamma\,J/r^3 + \Gamma\,T/r = 0\). The second and third equilibrium equations are as in step 3 of the proof of theorem 11.28, because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) does not depend on \(z\). The radial equation says that the radial stress falls where the hoop stress is lower than it, and rises where it is higher. So it has its lowest point where the two curves cross, for the logarithmic temperature below at \(r = 48{,}3\) mm.
  2. The lateral surfaces are free. At \(r = a\), \(J = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = A_{\mathrm{T}} - B_{\mathrm{T}}/a^2 = A_{\mathrm{T}} - A_{\mathrm{T}} = 0\). At \(r = b\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = A_{\mathrm{T}} - A_{\mathrm{T}}\,a^2/b^2 - \Gamma\,J(b)/b^2 = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,(b^2 - a^2)/b^2 - \Gamma\,J(b)/b^2 = 0\) by the definition of \(A_{\mathrm{T}}\). Cauchy's formula (theorem 2.26) gives zero force on both lateral surfaces, because the shear stresses are zero. In figure 14.83 the radial stress therefore starts and ends at zero.
  3. At the ends, by Cauchy's formula the force is \(\pm\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\). The field is thus a solution of the problem in which the force at the ends is this one.

3. Uniqueness and the axial condition.

  1. The difference of two solutions has by equation (3.32) the stress of the ordinary Hooke's law and is in equilibrium with no forces on the surface (as in step 10 of the proof of theorem 14.44). By theorem 11.4, item 2, they have the same stresses and strains. Steps 2 and 3 show that \(\varepsilon_0\) does not enter \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) (the constants \(A_{\mathrm{T}}\), \(B_{\mathrm{T}}\) do not contain it). This is item 2.
  2. The integral of the axial stress: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (2\nu A_{\mathrm{T}} + E\,\varepsilon_0 - \Gamma\,T)\,2\pi\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = \pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\,(2\nu A_{\mathrm{T}} + E\,\varepsilon_0) - 2\pi\,\Gamma\,J(b)\). With \(\Gamma\,J(b) = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,(b^2 - a^2)\) this is \(\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)\,(2\nu A_{\mathrm{T}} + E\,\varepsilon_0 - 2A_{\mathrm{T}})\), which is zero at \(\varepsilon_0 = 2\,(1 - \nu)\,A_{\mathrm{T}}/E\). At this value \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 2A_{\mathrm{T}} - \Gamma\,T\). At \(r = a\), by (14.47) (\(J = 0\), \(B_{\mathrm{T}} = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,a^2\)) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(a) = 2A_{\mathrm{T}} - \Gamma\,T(a)\), and at \(r = b\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(b) = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,(1 + a^2/b^2) + \Gamma\,J(b)/b^2 - \Gamma\,T(b) = 2A_{\mathrm{T}} - \Gamma\,T(b)\), because \(\Gamma\,J(b)/b^2 = A_{\mathrm{T}}\,(1 - a^2/b^2)\). That is why the curves of the hoop and the axial stress meet at the inner and at the outer wall. This is item 3.

Source: SADD §12.7 (PDF pp. 388-392) for a thin ring (plane stress), equations (12.7.8) to (12.7.14), and example 12.2 (PDF p. 391) for comparing a number. TIMO (PDF p. 286) for the history. The proof by guessing the field and checking is ours, after the model of theorem 11.28. SymPy checked the equations of steps 2 to 4 and the equilibrium.

The proof showed that the solution for any radial temperature has the same two "Lamé" terms as for pressure, \(A_{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{T}}\), plus an additional term with the integral of the temperature. Let us test it on a constant temperature: \(T = T_0\) gives \(J = T_0\,(r^2 - a^2)/2\), \(A_{\mathrm{T}} = \Gamma\,T_0/2\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) of (14.47) are zero (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \tfrac{\Gamma T_0}{2}\,(1 - a^2/r^2 - (r^2 - a^2)/r^2) = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac{\Gamma T_0}{2}\,(1 + a^2/r^2 + (r^2 - a^2)/r^2 - 2) = 0\)), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) is zero at \(\varepsilon_0 = \alpha\,T_0\), the free thermal strain. A uniformly heated tube is therefore free of stress, like the body of case 1 of theorem 14.44. The theorem itself may not be used here, because the tube is not simply connected. But lemma 14.46 applies: a constant temperature has \(B = 0\), so there is no jump at the cut and the hole does no harm.

Now we insert the logarithmic temperature of the previous section, which falls from \(100\) K at the inner wall to \(0\) at the outer. From the computation (SymPy, num-08.py): \(J(b) = 43\,315\) K mm², for the steel of chapter 3 (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), \(\alpha = 15 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K) \(\Gamma = E\,\alpha/(1 - \nu) = 4{,}2\) MPa/K and \(A_{\mathrm{T}} = 90{,}96\) MPa. The stresses are in the table. The tube here is open (\(\varepsilon_0 = 2(1 - \nu)\,A_{\mathrm{T}}/E = 6{,}497 \cdot 10^{-4}\)): the resultant axial force at the ends is zero, but the stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) at the ends is not, since it goes from \(-238{,}08\) to \(181{,}92\) MPa. The difference from a free end with no force at all is a self-equilibrated load, so this solution describes a free end only far from the end, by Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11).

The axes of the lower right part of the figure show radius and stress. The hoop stress goes from \(-238\) MPa at the hot inner wall to \(+182\) MPa at the cold outer wall, the radial is compressive everywhere and is lowest, \(-21{,}1\) MPa, at \(r = 48{,}3\) mm, and the axial behaves like the hoop stress. The signs are as we expected: the inner half is squeezed, the outer one stretched. The radial stress is compressive because the two halves press on each other, and it is small because it is zero on both lateral surfaces. In plane strain (\(\varepsilon_0 = 0\)) the hoop and radial stresses are the same as for open ends (item 2 of the theorem), and the axial stress is \(-365{,}42\) MPa at the inner and \(54{,}58\) MPa at the outer wall. For a thin plate (plane stress, \(\Gamma\) replaced by \(E\,\alpha\)) the hoop stress is \(-166{,}65\) MPa at \(r = 40\) mm and \(127{,}35\) MPa at 60 mm, which is a factor \(1 - \nu = 0{,}7\) less than in plane strain, because the expressions contain \(E\,\alpha\) instead of \(\Gamma = E\,\alpha/(1 - \nu)\). Comparison with the source: SADD example 12.2 (PDF p. 391), a plate with \(b/a = 3\), \(E = 200\) GPa, \(\alpha = 13 \cdot 10^{-6}\) 1/K and \(100\) K, gives \(-174{,}17\) MPa by the same procedure, and the source writes "about 174 MPa".

The solution can be compared with the oil of chapter 11. The thermoelastic law is linear and the solution is unique, so the stress with pressure and temperature together is the sum of the stresses of the two separate problems. In figure 14.83 the hoop stress from oil is drawn blue and dashed, with \(78\) MPa at the inner wall, and we add it to the \(-238\) MPa from heating. The stresses at the inner wall with open ends (the axial stress from oil is zero), in megapascals, the last column by von Mises (chapter 2):

load \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\) von Mises
oil 30 MPa \(-30\) \(78\) \(0\) \(96{,}56\)
inner wall 100 K warmer \(0\) \(-238{,}08\) \(-238{,}08\) \(238{,}08\)
oil and inner wall 100 K warmer \(-30\) \(-160{,}08\) \(-238{,}08\) \(182{,}07\)
oil and inner wall 100 K colder \(-30\) \(316{,}08\) \(238{,}08\) \(314{,}42\)

Oil and a warmer inner wall give \(182{,}07\) MPa by von Mises, which is below the yield strength of 220 MPa. At the outer wall the hoop stress is then \(48 + 181{,}92 = 229{,}92\) MPa and by von Mises \(210{,}08\) MPa, only \(4{,}5\,\%\) below yield. If the inner wall is 100 K colder (cooling under pressure, for example), von Mises gives \(314{,}42\) MPa, which is above yield, where the linearly elastic model no longer holds. Oil alone gives only \(96{,}56\) MPa by von Mises, so the stresses from the 100 K difference between the walls are, with these data, much larger than the stresses from the oil. Depending on the sign of the difference they subtract in one case and add in the other.

Is the model reasonable? The total strain from temperature is \(\alpha\,\Delta T = 1{,}5 \cdot 10^{-3}\) at 100 K, and the elastic strain at the largest stress is \(238{,}08/196\,000 = 1{,}2 \cdot 10^{-3}\). Both are slightly above the bound \(0{,}001\) that we set for small strains in chapter 4, but they are still \(0{,}15\,\%\) and \(0{,}12\,\%\) of a length, and the model is linear in \(\Delta T\), so at 60 K (\(\alpha\,\Delta T = 9 \cdot 10^{-4}\)) all results scale with \(0{,}6\). Two more idealizations that we name: constant conductivity \(k\) and elastic constants independent of temperature (section 14.2), and the uncoupled treatment, where the deformation does not act back on the temperature (definition 14.38). Constant conductivity gives the logarithmic profile of the temperature. If \(k\) changed with temperature, only this profile would change, and theorem 14.47 holds for every continuously differentiable \(T(r)\), so the same integral \(J\) would give the stresses. Elastic constants that depend on temperature no longer fit the theorem. How much \(k\), \(E\) and \(\alpha\) of steel change over 100 K the project's books do not say, so we give no number for this correction. The last bound is yield. At the inner wall the hoop and axial stresses are \(-238{,}08\) MPa and the radial one is zero, which by von Mises is \(238{,}08\) MPa, above the yield strength of 220 MPa. Ends held by a rigid wall give the axial stress \(-365{,}42\) MPa instead of \(-238{,}08\) MPa and by von Mises \(321{,}27\) MPa. The stresses are linear in \(\Delta T\), so the elastic result holds with open ends only up to a difference of \(100 \cdot 220/238{,}08 = 92\) K, with a rigid wall up to \(100 \cdot 220/321{,}27 = 68\) K. At 100 K the inner wall already yields, and the linearly elastic model no longer holds there. So the order of loading matters too: oil and an inner wall 100 K warmer give together only \(182{,}07\) MPa, but if we first heat the tube and only then fill it with oil, the inner wall exceeds yield on the way.

What have we shown? Two results. A simply connected body with a linear temperature stays free of stress. A prism with a steady plane temperature stays free of stress in the plane of the cross-section, and the axial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = -E\,\alpha\,T\) is carried by the rigid wall at the ends. We showed both with compatibility and uniqueness. For a ring with heat flowing through the wall stresses arise, because the hole demands a jump of displacement that a field without stress cannot satisfy (the lemma on the jump at a cut). For the tube we computed them with a guessed displacement field that we checked: for the steel of chapter 3 and 100 K between the walls they reach a hoop stress of \(-238{,}08\) MPa, three times the \(78\) MPa that oil at 30 MPa gives, and by von Mises they already exceed yield.
