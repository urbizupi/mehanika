Chapter 17 FEM modeling in practice

17.1 The hierarchy of models and three errors (Hierarhija modelov in tri napake)

This section answers the question of what we actually compute when we compute a real structure. The name of the section says that models form a ladder in which every higher one keeps more detail than the one below, and that we sort the errors of a result into three groups.

Take a steel bar of rectangular section \(20 \times 40\) mm and length \(L = 80\) mm. The height of the section in the plane of bending is \(H = 40\) mm. (In chapter 5 the section height is \(h\), here we write \(H\) because \(h\) means the element size, as in definition 6.24.) We cannot hold the bar in the air, so we clamp its left end firmly into a rigid wall. At the free end we hang a weight of mass \(m = 50{,}97\) kg. The weight pulls the bar down with its weight, that is with the force \(F = m g = 500\) N, where \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s² is the gravitational acceleration. The end of the bar therefore sinks by \(\delta\). For steel this deflection is only a few thousandths of a millimetre, so it is magnified in the figure. If we measured it, we would obtain the number against which every calculation must be compared.

This story already holds two assumptions, so we state them here, where they enter. The first: the wall is rigid, so the edge of the bar at the wall neither moves nor turns. It is justified when the wall is much larger and stiffer than the bar and the bar is long compared with the height of its section. For our short bar it does not hold up: we shall see that merely the way the rigid clamp is written in the equations changes the tip deflection by \(24{,}4\ \%\), and for a bar with \(L/H = 25\) only by \(0{,}16\ \%\). The second: the displacements are small, so we write equilibrium on the bar as it is before loading. Here it is justified with a wide margin, because the deflection is about \(5\ \mu\)m on a length of \(80\) mm, the slope of the tip is \(7{,}65 \cdot 10^{-5}\) and its square \(5{,}9 \cdot 10^{-9}\) (num-01.py, part B). It fails when the slope is no longer small against \(1\), for example for a thin strip that bends by a visible part of its length: then the lever arm of the force \(F\) about the clamp shortens and a calculation on the unbent shape gives too large a moment.

The bar is at rest, so by Newton's law of equilibrium the sum of forces and the sum of moments on it must be zero. To see what this means, we remove the wall and replace it by its action, as in a free-body diagram (diagram prostega telesa). The diagram has this name because we remove from it everything that is not the observed body and draw the body free, without its surroundings. The weight still pulls down with the force \(F\). The sum of forces is zero only if the wall holds the bar with the force \(R\) upwards, so \(R = F = 500\) N. The sum of moments about the clamp is zero only if the wall adds a couple \(M_0\) (a couple is a pair of equal opposite forces whose moment does not depend on the point about which it is taken). The force \(F\) turns the bar about the clamp clockwise with the moment \(F L\), the force \(R\) has zero moment about the clamp, so the couple \(M_0\) must act the other way and \(M_0 = F L = 500\ \text{N} \cdot 0{,}08\ \text{m} = 40\) N m.

What is \(\delta\)? There is no single calculation for this question. The same bar can be treated as a three-dimensional elastic body (chapter 11), as a plane body (section 11.9), as a Timoshenko beam or as an Euler-Bernoulli beam (chapter 5). Each gives its own number. We must therefore choose a model, not only a program, and to do so deliberately we need words for what a model is.

Definition 17.1 Physical system, mathematical model and computational model (fizikalni sistem, matematični model in računski model)

A physical system is a real structure with real loads, supports and material, as we can measure it. A mathematical model is a list of idealizations (idealizacije), that is assumptions about geometry, material, supports and loads, together with the equations that follow from them: the sought fields, which are functions of position and where needed of time, the equations these fields satisfy, and the boundary conditions. Its solution, the exact solution of the model (točna rešitev modela), is the set of these fields. A computational model turns the mathematical model into a finite list of numbers: a mesh and a kind of element, an integration rule and a way of solving with an allowed residual. Its solution is a vector of numbers, the nodal values, and the fields computed from it.

Source: The definition is ours. COOK §1.2 (PDF p. 17-18): a geometric model becomes a mathematical one when chosen differential equations and boundary conditions describe it, and is an idealization. ONATE1 §1.4.2 (PDF p. 28-29): a conceptual model, a structural (mathematical) model with geometry, basic laws and properties of material and loads, and a computational model with the element, mesh size and data. Our mathematical model joins ONATE1's conceptual and structural model.

In our story the physical system is the bar with the weight. The new word in the definition is idealization. It is an assumption that simplifies the matter and is not exactly true: the material of the bar is homogeneous and linearly elastic, the clamp is rigid, the load acts at a point. COOK lists them: the material is usually different, a load on a small surface cannot act at a point, no support is perfectly rigid (COOK §1.2, PDF p. 18). The computational model is what the earlier chapters built: a mesh of bar elements (chapter 7) or beam elements (chapter 12).

What mathematical models does our bar have available? Chapter 5 divided structures by shape into line, surface and solid ones (section 5.1.1). That is a property of the body. A model has its own dimension. The number of spatial variables on which the sought fields of the model depend we call the dimension of the model (razsežnost modela). The same body has several models of different dimensions, as figure 17.4 shows.

Let us begin with the richest. In the solid model we take the bar as a three-dimensional body and each of its points receives three displacement components, which are functions of three coordinates. In the plane model we assume that everything happens in the plane \(xy\), and each point of that plane has two displacement components, \(u\) and \(v\), functions of two coordinates (section 11.9). A line model replaces the section by a point on the axis, so the sought fields depend only on \(x\). In the Timoshenko model these are two functions, the deflection of the axis \(v(x)\) and the rotation of the section \(\theta(x)\) (definition 5.87). The section stays flat in the model, the axis curves, and because the section is not perpendicular to the axis, there is a shear angle \(\gamma\) between them. In the Euler-Bernoulli model the section is flat and stays perpendicular to the curved axis, so the rotation of the section is already fixed by the slope of the axis, and only one function \(v(x)\) is sought (definition 5.14).

The hierarchy is formed by what each step takes away (figure 17.4). The plane model follows from the solid one by the assumption of a plane state (section 11.9). The Timoshenko model follows from the plane one by the assumption that sections stay flat (definition 5.87). The Euler-Bernoulli model follows from the Timoshenko one by the assumption \(\gamma = 0\), which means that the shear stiffness is infinite, \(kGA \to \infty\) (corollary 5.92: without the shear term the formula is that of the Euler-Bernoulli beam, since this is the case \(kGA \to \infty\)). The word hierarchy here means only an ordering by steps. It does not mean that a result of a higher step is always better, because even in this ladder the choice of the clamp is part of the model. Figure 17.5 shows the reason.

What each assumption of the ladder costs can be read from the tip deflection of our bar in part (a) of figure 17.5 (the numbers are derived in lemma 17.3 below). The wall holds the left end, the dashed rectangle and the dashed axis are the bar before loading with length \(L\) and height \(H\), the force \(F\) acts at the free end. The thick orange curve is the axis by the Euler-Bernoulli model: the tip sinks by \(4{,}082\ \mu\)m, and this is the deflection due to bending. The thin orange curve is the axis by the Timoshenko model, which allows the shear angle \(\gamma\): the tip sinks by a further \(0{,}796\ \mu\)m, in total by \(4{,}878\ \mu\)m. The assumption \(\gamma = 0\) therefore removes exactly this piece from the deflection, that is \(16{,}32\ \%\) of the Timoshenko deflection. It is justified for a slender bar, because the shear piece grows as \(L\) and the bending piece as \(L^3\): at \(L/H = 25\) the omitted piece is only \(0{,}12\ \%\). The dashed orange curve is the plane model in which the rigid clamp is written by holding the vertical element at the middle of the clamp (label (ii) from chapter 11): the deflection is \(5{,}077\ \mu\)m. If we hold the horizontal element instead (label (i)), the same plane model gives exactly the Euler-Bernoulli deflection. The difference between the two ways of writing the clamp is \(0{,}995\ \mu\)m, more than the whole shear piece. So a higher step of the ladder is not automatically better: for a short bar the way the clamp is written decides the deflection more than whether the model knows shear. The assumption of a plane state, on the other hand, is justified here, because the bar is loaded in the plane \(xy\) and its side faces are free (when it holds and what it leaves out is told in section 11.9.7).

What it means to hold the horizontal or the vertical element at the middle of the clamp, and why this moves the tip of the bar, we look at in figure 17.6.

In both parts of the figure the bar before loading is dashed grey, and the force \(F\) acts at the free end. The plane model does not know the wall as a body, so we write the rigid clamp at one point, the middle of the clamp \(O\), where we prescribe both displacements to be zero. This does not yet stop the bar from turning about \(O\) as a whole, so at \(O\) we also hold one short element of material. In the circle we enlarge the neighbourhood of the point \(O\) and draw two such elements, a horizontal one along the axis and a vertical one downwards, at a right angle before loading, in dashed grey. The force \(F\) causes a shear stress in the section at the wall, which at the middle of the section is \(3F/(2A) = 3 \cdot 500\ \text{N}/(2 \cdot 800\ \text{mm}^2) = 0{,}9375\) MPa (derivation 11.46). Divided by \(G = 75\,385\) MPa it gives the shear strain \(\gamma_0 = 1{,}244\cdot 10^{-5}\), and by this much the right angle between the elements closes, whichever element we hold. With clamp (i) we hold the horizontal element, so the vertical one tilts by the angle \(\gamma_0\), and the axis leaves the wall horizontally and the tip sinks by the Euler-Bernoulli deflection \(4{,}082\ \mu\)m. With clamp (ii) we hold the vertical element, so the horizontal one tilts by the same angle. The stresses and strains are the same for both clamps, the two differ only by a rigid rotation: the axis (ii) is the axis (i) turned about \(O\) by \(\gamma_0\), as the turned tangent shows. The tip of the axis is at the distance \(L\) from \(O\), so with (ii) it is lower by \(L\gamma_0 = 80\ \text{mm} \cdot 1{,}244\cdot 10^{-5} = 0{,}995\ \mu\)m. This is the difference between the two ways of writing the clamp from figure 17.5.

The ladder of our bar is one case of a general rule, and this rule is the view from the top down on everything the book has so far built from the bottom up. Every structural element is the same boundary value problem of elasticity (definition 11.1, fifteen unknown functions of three coordinates), reduced by one assumption about how the body deforms, because it is thin in two directions or in one. Figure 17.7 shows six such bodies, and the table below it tells four things for each: the assumption, the unknown function, the equation and the number of boundary conditions.

The bar (a) (palica) is pulled along its axis, so every section moves as a whole by \(u(x)\) and the bar becomes longer. The shaft (b) (gred) is twisted by the moment \(M_{\mathrm{x}}\) about its axis, so the section turns by \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}(x)\), as the line on the section on the right shows. The beam (c) (nosilec) carries a load across, the axis sinks by \(v(x)\) and the section turns by \(\theta(x)\). The plane-stress panel (d) is a thin flat plate loaded in its own plane, and its point \((x, y)\) moves by \(u\) and \(v\) in that plane. In the lectures this body is called stena, in the book it is a body in plane stress (definition 11.36), and it is not the wall into which our bar is clamped. The plate (e) (plošča) is the same body loaded perpendicular to its plane, so the middle plane sinks by \(w(x, y)\). The shell (f) (lupina) is curved, so it can carry the pressure \(p\) already by the forces \(N\) in its surface, moving by \(w\) along the normal.

We read the table by columns. The assumption is most often a statement about what happens to the section or to the normal: it stays rigid, straight, perpendicular. For the wall and the membrane shell it is a statement about stresses: those that a thin body cannot carry are zero. Each such statement turns three coordinates into one or two and fifteen unknown functions into at most three, but it holds only as long as the body really is thin. For the models that seek displacements the number of boundary conditions is half the order of the equation: an equation of second order (\(u''\), \(\varphi_{\mathrm{x}}''\)) needs one per end, an equation of fourth order (\(v''''\), \(\nabla^4 w\)) two, the Mindlin system of sixth order three, the general shell of eighth order four. In each pair we prescribe either the displacement or the force that does work on it, never both (section 5.7.2). The rows with two models, beam and plate, are the same step as for our bar: the model that allows shear has one more unknown function and passes into the simpler one when the shear stiffness goes to infinity. How and why this passage is made step by step on a beam is in section A.2.2.

One more word. Different questions are possible for the same model. The tip deflection, the largest stress at the clamp or a natural frequency are different sought quantities and each needs a different model. Before we choose a model we must therefore say what we seek, and in the definition below this quantity has a name.

Definition 17.2 Quantity of interest and three kinds of error (iskana količina in tri vrste napak)

The quantity of interest \(Q\) is the number that we need from the analysis, for example the tip deflection, the largest stress or a frequency. Let \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) be its value on the physical system (measured), \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) its value for the exact solution of the mathematical model, \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) its value for the solution of the computational model, if we computed without rounding and with all iterations to the end, and \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\) the number returned by the program. The errors are three numbers:

\[e_{\mathrm{mod}} = Q_{\mathrm{m}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}} , \qquad e_{\mathrm{dis}} = Q_{\mathrm{r}} - Q_{\mathrm{m}} , \qquad e_{\mathrm{num}} = Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{r}} . \tag{17.1}\]

The first is the modelling error (napaka modela), the second the discretization error (napaka diskretizacije), the third the numerical error (numerična napaka).

Source: The division is from COOK §1.2 (PDF p. 18) and §9.1 (PDF p. 314): the modelling error is the difference between the physical system and the mathematical model, the discretization error the difference between the solution of the computational model and the exact solution of the mathematical model, the numerical error comes from the finite precision of numbers. The notation with numbers \(Q\) is ours.

The names have a reason. Discretization comes from the Latin discretus, separate: we replace a continuous field by finitely many separate numbers, the nodal values. The error is numerical because it results from the computer calculating with numbers of finite length and from iterations being stopped at some point. The modelling error arises at the choice of idealizations, before the computer starts, and is therefore the only one that the mesh does not reduce.

The errors add up. On the axis we take the physical system \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) as the starting point: from it the step \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) leads to the mathematical model \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), from there the step \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) to the computational model \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) and from there the step \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) to the number from the program \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\). If in \(Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) we add and subtract \(Q_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), we obtain

\[Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}} = (Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{r}}) + (Q_{\mathrm{r}} - Q_{\mathrm{m}}) + (Q_{\mathrm{m}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}}) = e_{\mathrm{num}} + e_{\mathrm{dis}} + e_{\mathrm{mod}} .\]

On the figure the sum of the steps is the distance from \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\) to \(Q_{\mathrm{p}}\). The lower axis shows the same steps with the numbers of our bar and one beam element: the three points \(Q_{\mathrm{m}} = Q_{\mathrm{r}} = Q_{\mathrm{p}}\) coincide and the whole gap is \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\), which we compute at the end of the section. The signs may differ. If \(e_{\mathrm{mod}} = -e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) is small, the total error equals \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) and is small although both are large. A small total error therefore does not prove that the parts are small, and so we estimate each separately. Each part has its source: \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) depends on the idealizations, \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\) on the mesh and the element, \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) on the numbers and the solution. Chapter 7 (section 7.10.3) distinguished after ONATE1 five sources of error. By our definition the error of the geometry approximation and the error of the element integrals belong to \(e_{\mathrm{dis}}\), since they are properties of the computational model, the error of solving the system belongs to \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\), and the error of the constitutive equation belongs to \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\), since the choice of material is an idealization.

The modelling error \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) can be computed only if we know \(Q_{\mathrm{f}}\). We have no measurement, so in this chapter we measure \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\) against a richer model from the ladder. BATHE calls the richer model a very-comprehensive mathematical model: its solution is what we compare with to judge the reliability of a simpler model, and in practice it is usually not solved but judged by experience or by another, more refined model (BATHE, PDF p. 26-27). This tells what a simplification leaves out, but not how good the richer model is against the metal.

Let us see how large the modelling error is for our bar. The condition for the Euler-Bernoulli model is slenderness (vitkost), the ratio \(L/H\). Chapter 5 has already computed the shear part of the deflection of the Timoshenko model for three lengths of the same bar (section 5.13.4). Here we do the same calculation as a function of slenderness and compare it also with the plane model of chapter 11.

Lemma 17.3 Tip deflection of a cantilever in the models of the ladder (poves konzole v modelih lestvice)

A cantilever of length \(L\) with a flat rectangular section of width \(b\) and height \(H\) is made of a homogeneous isotropic linearly elastic material with modulus of elasticity \(E\), Poisson's ratio \(\nu\) and shear modulus \(G = E/(2(1 + \nu))\). It is clamped at \(x = 0\), at the free end it is loaded by a force \(F\) transverse to the axis, displacements are small. Let \(|v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\), \(|v_{\mathrm{T}}|\) be the tip deflection by the Euler-Bernoulli model (definition 5.14) and by the Timoshenko model with shear factor \(k\) (definition 5.87), and \(|v_{(\mathrm{i})}|\) and \(|v_{(\mathrm{ii})}|\) the deflection of the plane model with a prescribed rotation of the horizontal or the vertical element at the middle of the clamp (derivation 11.46 of chapter 11). Then:

  1. \(|v_{\mathrm{T}}|\) \(= |v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\,(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{T}})\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = \dfrac{1 + \nu}{2k}\Big(\dfrac{H}{L}\Big)^2\).
  2. The Euler-Bernoulli deflection is below the Timoshenko one by the fraction \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}}/(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{T}})\).
  3. \(|v_{(\mathrm{i})}| = |v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\) and \(|v_{(\mathrm{ii})}| = |v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\,(1 + \eta_{(\mathrm{ii})})\) with \(\eta_{(\mathrm{ii})} = \dfrac{3\,(1 + \nu)}{4}\Big(\dfrac{H}{L}\Big)^2\), and \(\eta_{(\mathrm{ii})}/\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = 3k/2\).
Proof

We must show that the relation between forces and displacements gives the ratio of the shear and bending parts of the deflection, the same for every \(L\), and that the same fraction gives the plane model.

  1. By corollary 5.92 with an imagined force of \(1\) N upwards at the free end we have \(m = L - x\) and \(v_1 = 1\), the real ones are \(M = -F\,(L - x)\) and \(V = -F\) (as in section 5.13.4). Hence \(v_{\mathrm{T}} = -F L^3/(3EI) - F L/(kGA)\). The first term is the bending part of the deflection, the second the shear part.
  2. By theorem 5.57 (equation (5.57)) the first term is \(v_{\mathrm{EB}} = -F L^3/(3EI)\), that is the end of the thick curve. The ratio of the second term to the first, that is of the short blue distance to the long one, is \(3EI/(kGA\,L^2) = \eta_{\mathrm{T}}\) and the first item follows from \(v_{\mathrm{T}} = v_{\mathrm{EB}}\,(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{T}})\).
  3. For a rectangle \(I = b H^3/12\) and \(A = b H\), so \(3EI/(kGA\,L^2) = E\,H^2/(4kG\,L^2)\). From \(E/G = 2(1 + \nu)\) (equation (3.22)) it follows that \(E/(4kG) = (1 + \nu)/(2k)\) and the written form of \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}}\).
  4. Item 2: \(|v_{\mathrm{T}}| - |v_{\mathrm{EB}}| = |v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\,\eta_{\mathrm{T}}\), that is the shear part, divided by the whole Timoshenko deflection \(|v_{\mathrm{T}}| = |v_{\mathrm{EB}}|\,(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{T}})\).
  5. By derivation 11.46, \(v(L, 0) = -F L^3/(3EI)\) for the clamp (i) and \(-F L^3/(3EI) - (1 + \nu)\,F c^2 L/(EI)\) for the clamp (ii), where \(c = H/2\) is half the height. The first term is \(v_{\mathrm{EB}}\), the second is the difference between the clamps. Since \((1 + \nu)/E = 1/(2G)\) and \(c^2/I = 3/A\), the second term is \(L \cdot 3F/(2GA) = L\gamma_0\), the turn of the axis by the shear angle at \(O\) at the distance \(L\). The ratio of the second term to the first is \(3(1 + \nu)\,c^2/L^2 = 3(1 + \nu)\,H^2/(4L^2) = \eta_{(\mathrm{ii})}\).
  6. The quotient: \(\eta_{(\mathrm{ii})}/\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = \dfrac{3(1 + \nu)/4}{(1 + \nu)/(2k)} = \dfrac{3k}{2}\). For \(k = 5/6\) this is \(1{,}25\): the difference between the clamps is a quarter longer than the shear part, \(0{,}995/0{,}796 = 1{,}25\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The shear term \(F L/(kGA)\) of the cantilever is in EP example 4.2.3 (p. 177-178), the plane deflections (i) and (ii) are from BARBER §9.1.1 (PDF p. 139) as in derivation 11.46.

New is the connection with slenderness: \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}}\) falls with the square of \(H/L\), so the Euler-Bernoulli model is below the Timoshenko one by less than \(5\ \%\) at \(L/H \ge 3{,}85\) and by less than \(1\ \%\) at \(L/H \ge 8{,}79\) (from \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}}/(1 + \eta_{\mathrm{T}}) = 0{,}05\) and \(0{,}01\)). This holds for \(\nu = 0{,}3\) and \(k = 5/6\) and for a rectangle. The material moves the coefficient \((1 + \nu)/(2k) = 0{,}78\) little: at \(\nu = 0{,}2\) it is \(0{,}72\), at \(\nu = 0{,}35\) it is \(0{,}81\). The section moves it strongly. For the I-section of section 5.13.2 (height \(200\) mm, \(k = 0{,}383\), \(I/A = 6812{,}6\) mm²) we get \(\eta_{\mathrm{T}} = 3EI/(kGA\,L^2) = 3{,}47\,(H/L)^2\), that is \(4{,}4\) times more than for the rectangle, because almost only the thin web carries the shear. The threshold \(5\ \%\) therefore moves from \(L/H = 3{,}85\) to \(L/H = 8{,}1\) and the threshold \(1\ \%\) from \(8{,}79\) to \(18{,}5\).

How do we read this on the figure? The horizontal axis is the slenderness \(L/H\), the vertical one the percentage by which the Euler-Bernoulli deflection is below the other model. The blue curve is below the Timoshenko model, the orange one below the plane model with the clamp (ii). Both rise steeply at small \(L/H\), because \(\eta\) grows as \((H/L)^2\). The dashed lines are the thresholds \(5\ \%\) and \(1\ \%\) from the calculation above. The orange curve lies above the blue one by the constant factor \(3k/2 = 1{,}25\) in \(\eta\), and this factor does not depend on \(\nu\), as the third item of the lemma says.

Now let us try a case where the lemma holds but the conclusion that the Timoshenko model is better than the Euler-Bernoulli one does not. Take \(L/H = 2\), our bar with \(L = 80\) mm. The tip deflection is on figure 17.5: by Euler-Bernoulli and by the plane model with the clamp (i) \(4{,}082\ \mu\)m, by Timoshenko \(4{,}878\ \mu\)m and by the plane model with the clamp (ii) \(5{,}077\ \mu\)m (num-01.py, part B). The Euler-Bernoulli model is below the Timoshenko one by the shear part \(0{,}796\ \mu\)m, that is \(16{,}32\ \%\), which we could take for the modelling error of definition 17.2. But the Timoshenko model is itself below the plane model (ii) by \(0{,}199\ \mu\)m, that is \(3{,}92\ \%\). The plane models (i) and (ii), which have the same equations and differ only in how the clamp is written, differ by \(0{,}995\ \mu\)m, that is \(\eta_{(\mathrm{ii})} = 0{,}24375\) or \(24{,}4\ \%\) of the deflection. At \(L/H = 25\) this difference is \(0{,}16\ \%\).

Why does the clamp have such an effect for a short bar? Figure 17.6 showed that the difference between (ii) and (i) equals \(L\) times the shear strain at the middle of the clamp, because (i) holds the horizontal element of the axis and (ii) the vertical one. This shear strain is for the same force and the same section equal for every \(L\) (the shear stress \(3F/(2A)\) divided by \(G\)), while the bending part grows as \(L^3\), so their ratio falls as \((H/L)^2\).

The second reason is Saint-Venant's principle: statically equivalent loads differ only near the place of loading. Chapter 11 computed for a strip that the difference falls within a distance of about one to two section heights, under the conditions of fully developed decay and a semi-infinite strip (section 11.11). Part (b) of figure 17.5 draws this region on both bars. On the long bar with \(L/H = 25\) the distances \(H\) and \(2H\) are a short piece at the wall, \(4\) to \(8\ \%\) of the length. On the short bar with \(L/H = 2\) the same distances reach to the middle and to the end, that is \(50\) to \(100\ \%\) of the length: the whole bar lies in the region where we do not know the stress distribution at the wall. The conclusion that the Timoshenko deflection is a better approximation of the true one is no longer justified here. A real wall would decide the deflection by how much the clamp deforms, and neither model contains that.

What this means for the choice is shown by BATHE. It replaces the bracket that it treats by a beam that also accounts for shear deformation (BATHE, PDF p. 22), that is by a Timoshenko model. Such a model is reliable if we seek the bending moment (to within \(1\ \%\)) and the deflection only to within \(20\ \%\), but not if we seek the largest stress, because the beam model completely neglects the stress increase at the fillets. The difference in the deflection comes from the beam model assuming a rigid clamp at the left end and neglecting the deformation between the end of the beam and the bolts (BATHE, PDF p. 26). This is the same cause as for our bar: the Timoshenko model does not contain the deformation of the clamp either. The choice of model depends on the question we put to nature (BATHE, PDF p. 26): the same model is reliable for one question and wrong for another.

The same appears in vibration. For a bar in a uniaxial state of stress, chapter 13 obtained the wave equation with speed \(\sqrt{E/\rho}\), and estimated how much of the kinetic energy the model neglects when it does not count the motion by which the free sides contract transversely: the ratio of the kinetic energy of transverse motion to the axial one is \(3{,}7 \cdot 10^{-5}\) at wavelength \(4\) m and \(0{,}09\) at wavelength \(8\) cm (section 13.4.3). The model is the same, the question is different (a short wave instead of the first natural shape), and the model is no longer good. A model is therefore not good or bad in itself, but for something we ask.

One last claim of the section remains: reducing one error does not reduce the others. We test it on the bar with \(L/H = 2\), as the lower axis of figure 17.8 shows. Take the Euler-Bernoulli model and a single beam element from chapter 12. By theorem 12.14 the deflection and slope at a node under a nodal load are exact, so \(Q_{\mathrm{r}} = Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{dis}} = 0\). With NumPy we obtain from \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\,\vek d = \vek f\) with the matrix \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}} = (EI/L^3)\begin{pmatrix} 12 & -6L \\ -6L & 4L^2 \end{pmatrix}\) the tip deflection \(4{,}0816\ \mu\)m, which differs from \(F L^3/(3EI)\) by \(4 \cdot 10^{-16}\) relative. The condition number \(\kappa(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}) = 1881\) (with \(L\) in metres, definition 6.31) is small, so \(e_{\mathrm{num}}\) is of the order of rounding. The total error against the plane model (ii) is therefore almost all \(e_{\mathrm{mod}}\): \(Q_{\mathrm{p}} - Q_{\mathrm{f}} \approx e_{\mathrm{mod}} = 4{,}082 - 5{,}077 = -0{,}995\ \mu\)m, which is \(19{,}6\ \%\) of the deflection. More elements would fix nothing here, because the discretization error is already zero. The only remedy is a better model: the Timoshenko one (the error against (ii) falls to \(3{,}92\ \%\)) or the plane one in which we draw the clamp as it is.

The errors and the remedies are therefore these. We reduce the modelling error by a better model, chosen according to the quantity of interest, and this is the subject of this section. We reduce the discretization error by a well chosen mesh and shall estimate it later in the chapter. We reduce the numerical error by better solving of the equations and better conditioning, and this also comes later. The steps of an analysis, the eight checks and the division by sources from chapter 7 (sections 7.10.1 and 7.10.2) stay as they were: the checks work inside the model and do not check the model itself (section 7.10.3).
