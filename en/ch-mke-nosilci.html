The whole chapter on one page (loads more slowly)

Chapter 5 treated beams and frames by hand. We obtained displacements with the unit load and Castigliano's theorem, each frame separately, and every time we had to choose ourselves which quantities to find from equilibrium and which from continuity. Chapter 6 showed that for a beam the hat functions of the bar fail, because a deflection with a kink has no finite energy, and it announced polynomials of the third degree. Chapter 7 wrote the computation for bars and trusses in a form a computer can carry out: we compute the matrix of each element once, obtain the matrix of the structure by adding, and introduce the supports only at the end. How do we extend this computation to beams and frames with arbitrary supports and loads, in the plane and in space, and how do we use the same matrix to find how a frame vibrates and at which force it buckles?

The route is as follows. First we build a single piece of beam that has a displacement and a rotation at each end, so that the slope is shared by neighbouring pieces, and compute its stiffness matrix. Then we join the pieces into a beam and replace the loads along the beam by forces and couples at the nodes. Next comes a question we could not ask for the bar: are the displacements and rotations at the nodes exact, and how do we get the true internal forces from the result? Then we assemble frames in the plane, which chapter 5 solved by hand, and frames in space, where besides bending in two planes the twisting of the cross-section about the axis of the beam appears. Then we treat constraints that do not run along a single unknown, for example a support sliding on an inclined plane, and two ways to enforce them: with the Lagrange multipliers (Lagrangeevi množitelji) of chapter 8 and with a penalty term in the energy. The next step is an element that takes shear deformation into account (the Timoshenko beam of chapter 5) and the reason why it locks in shear for a thin beam (shear locking, strižno zaklepanje). At the end we use the same family of elements for the mass matrix of a vibrating frame and for the matrix that describes the influence of the axial force on bending, that is, for buckling.

What is new in this chapter is therefore an element whose field has a continuous slope, the reason why its values at the nodes are exact, frames in the plane and in space, constraints and their enforcement, an element with shear deformation, with locking, and the mass and geometric stiffness of beams. Everything else comes from earlier chapters: assembly and the reduced system from chapter 7, the Ritz method from chapter 6, internal forces and frames from chapter 5.

The goal of the chapter is that for a frame with any number of beams, in the plane or in space, we can compute displacements, internal forces, natural frequencies and the critical buckling force, and that for every result we know when it is exact and when only an approximation. We compare the computations with the beams and frames of chapter 5, the columns of chapter 8 and the frame of chapter 10, which already have a known answer. Where we prove a result, the proof is in the text, where we do not, we say so.
