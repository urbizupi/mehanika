Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.6 Critical speeds of a rotor with several masses

For the Jeffcott rotor we found the critical speed as the peak of the response to unbalance (definition 24.8): at \(1483\) rev/min the motion of the disk centre is \(25\) times the eccentricity \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\). A rotor with an off-centre disk or with several masses has several such speeds, and the definition by a peak has three weaknesses (FPGL, §6.7, PDF p. 293). The first is that the peak depends on where the unbalance sits on the rotor, and we do not know that in advance because it arises in manufacture. The second is that it depends on which quantity we measure (displacement of the disk, displacement of the bearing, force in the bearing), because different quantities peak at different speeds. The third is that a local peak may lie at a speed that is not a property of the rotor and never harms the machine. We therefore want a definition that does not depend on the unbalance or on the measured quantity, and a calculation that finds all critical speeds of the rotor from the matrices we already have from sections 24.3 and 24.5. The section answers four questions: which speeds are critical (theorem 24.33), what shape the shaft has at each (definition 24.36), how the critical speeds change when the bearings get softer (theorem 24.37), and what happens when the rotor is run through a critical speed quickly (lemma 24.38).

The name critical speed (kritična hitrost) comes from the fact that at it a small unbalance can cause a motion that is critical for the machine, that is, so large that it damages it (FPGL, PDF p. 293 speaks of "problematic levels" of motion). Figure 24.20 shows the example this section treats: the response of rotor \(R_2\) (disk at \(0{,}25\) m from the left bearing, section 24.4) to unbalance, at all speeds from zero to \(9000\) rev/min.

24.6.1 Which speed is critical

A rotor has an excitation whose frequency depends on the spin speed \(\Omega\), and natural frequencies that by section 24.4 also depend on \(\Omega\). A critical speed is therefore the intersection of two curves that both move with \(\Omega\), and not merely the natural frequency of some stationary system (FPGL, PDF p. 294). First we name the excitation we have and the natural frequency.

Definition 24.32 Critical speed, formal definition (kritična hitrost, formalna definicija)

A rotor turns at a constant speed \(\Omega > 0\) (1/s) and carries an excitation that is a sum of components with frequencies \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}(\Omega)\) (each component is sinusoidal with its own frequency, for unbalance it is \(\Omega\)). A natural frequency of an axisymmetric rotor on bearings of equal stiffness in both planes at speed \(\Omega\) is a real number \(\omega\) at which the equation of motion without excitation has a nonzero solution \(\vek p(t) = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) (the whirl is forward for \(\omega > 0\) and backward for \(\omega < 0\), definition 24.3). The speed \(\Omega\) is critical if at it the frequency of one excitation component equals one natural frequency of the rotor at the same speed: \(\omega_{\mathrm{f}}(\Omega) = \omega\).

Source: FPGL §6.7 (PDF p. 293-294): the critical speed is "the speed of rotation of the machine at which one of the frequency components of the excitation coincides with a natural frequency of the system". As a property of the system the definition does not depend on the distribution of the excitation or on the measured quantity.

The definition removes all three weaknesses, because only two frequencies enter it, the excitation and the natural one, and not the amplitudes of the excitation, the place of the unbalance or the measured quantity. We pay for this on the other side: the definition also finds speeds that do not harm the machine, as we will see at the second critical speed of rotor \(R_1\) in section 24.6.4 (FPGL, PDF p. 293).

For the unbalance of an axisymmetric rotor (section 24.2) there is a single excitation component, a force of frequency \(\Omega\) that turns in the same direction as the shaft (FPGL, PDF p. 294). In the complex notation \(p = v + \mathrm{i}w\) this is \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), so the critical speed here means the natural frequency \(\omega = +\Omega\): synchronous forward whirl. For bearings with different stiffness in the two planes we get the natural frequencies from the equation of each plane separately, and the Jeffcott rotor then has two critical speeds, \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{w}}\) (lemma 24.11).

24.6.2 The rotor in complex form

The model is the same as in sections 24.3 to 24.5, with the same named idealizations (section 24.5): the shaft and the disks are written with a finite number of degrees of freedom, the shaft axis is \(x\), at every node there are a displacement and a slope in the two planes \(v\) and \(w\), the spin speed \(\Omega\) is constant, the axisymmetric rotor rests on bearings of equal stiffness in both planes, and there is no damping yet (section 24.7 adds it). Let \(\vek v\) be the column of displacements and slopes in the plane \(v\) and \(\vek w\) the same column in the plane \(w\). Assembly by theorem 24.29 gives matrices of the same order \(n\): the mass matrix \(\mat M\) (symmetric, positive definite, it also contains the rotary inertia of the shaft and the transverse moment of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) of the disks), the matrix of polar inertias \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) (symmetric, positive semidefinite, on the slopes it has the polar moments of inertia \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) of the disks, on the shaft twice the rotary inertia, because \(\rho I_{\mathrm{p}} = 2\rho I\)) and the stiffness matrix \(\mat K\) (symmetric, positive definite, because the bearings hold the rotor). The disk is as in theorem 24.17: in the plane \(v\) the term is \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\,\ddot v' + \Omega J_{\mathrm{p}}\,\dot w'\), and in the plane \(w\) it is \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\,\ddot w' - \Omega J_{\mathrm{p}}\,\dot v'\). For all nodes together we get two real equations

\[\mat M\ddot{\vek v} + \Omega\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\,\dot{\vek w} + \mat K\vek v = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} , \qquad \mat M\ddot{\vek w} - \Omega\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\,\dot{\vek v} + \mat K\vek w = \vek f_{\mathrm{w}} . \tag{24.33}\]

We multiply the second equation by \(\mathrm{i}\) and add the two. For the complex column \(\vek p = \vek v + \mathrm{i}\,\vek w\) we have \(\dot{\vek w} - \mathrm{i}\,\dot{\vek v} = -\mathrm{i}\,(\dot{\vek v} + \mathrm{i}\,\dot{\vek w}) = -\mathrm{i}\,\dot{\vek p}\), hence

\[\mat M\ddot{\vek p} - \mathrm{i}\,\Omega\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\,\dot{\vek p} + \mat K\vek p = \vek f_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}\,\vek f_{\mathrm{w}} . \tag{24.34}\]

The unbalance of each disk \(j\) is a mass \(m_{\mathrm{j}}\) with eccentricity \(a_{\mathrm{u},\mathrm{j}}\) at an angle \(\delta_{\mathrm{j}}\) to the plane \(v\) and we write it as the complex number \(u_{\mathrm{j}} = m_{\mathrm{j}} a_{\mathrm{u},\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\delta_{\mathrm{j}}}\) (definition 24.6). Its force on the disk is by section 24.2 \(\Omega^2 u_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) in the displacement of the \(j\)-th disk, so the right-hand side of equation (24.34) is the column \(\Omega^2\,\vek u\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) with components \(u_{\mathrm{j}}\) at the displacements of the disks and zeros elsewhere.

Free whirl with frequency \(\omega\) is a solution \(\vek p = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) of equation (24.34) without the right-hand side. Inserting it gives \(\big(-\omega^2\mat M + \omega\,\Omega\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}} + \mat K\big)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\). The sign of \(\omega\) gives the direction of the whirl: for \(\omega > 0\) the shaft whirls forward (in the sense of the spin), for \(\omega < 0\) backward. For \(\omega = \Omega\) we have in addition \(-\Omega^2\mat M + \Omega^2\mat G_{\mathrm{p}} = -\Omega^2(\mat M - \mat G_{\mathrm{p}})\), and it is this difference that decides the critical speeds. We denote it \(\mat D = \mat M - \mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\). It is symmetric, but it need not have a sign: a disk that is thin (\(J_{\mathrm{p}} > J_{\mathrm{t}}\)) has \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}} < 0\) on the slope.

On notation (D15): \(\Omega\) is the spin speed, the same as the forcing frequency in chapter 9, \(\omega\) is the whirl frequency (the bold \(\boldsymbol\omega\) in chapter 8 is the angular velocity vector and does not appear here), \(p = v + \mathrm{i}w\) is the complex displacement (not the pressure of chapter 11), \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the matrix of polar inertias (the shear modulus is the italic \(G\)), and \(J_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}}\) are the polar and transverse moments of inertia of the disk.

24.6.3 Critical speeds as an eigenproblem

Now we can compute the critical speeds. From equation (24.34) with \(\omega = \Omega\) it follows that \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), a generalized eigenproblem, but with a difficulty: \(\mat D\) need not have a sign. In the usual eigenproblem (theorem 10.13) the mass matrix is positive definite, all eigenvalues are \(\omega^2 > 0\) and each gives a frequency. Now the role of the mass is taken by the positive definite \(\mat K\) and the role of the stiffness by \(\mat D\), which may be indefinite. Let us test the swap of roles.

Theorem 24.33 Synchronous critical speeds of a rotor (sinhrone kritične hitrosti rotorja)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\mat K\) be real symmetric matrices of order \(n\), \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K\) positive definite and \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) positive semidefinite, and \(\mat D = \mat M - \mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\). Then there exist real numbers \(s_1 \le \dots \le s_{\mathrm{n}}\) (in s\(^2\)) and real columns \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) with \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) otherwise and \(\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and for a speed \(\Omega > 0\):

  1. Equation (24.34) without the right-hand side has a solution \(\vek p = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) with \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\) (synchronous forward whirl) if and only if \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), and this holds if and only if \(\Omega^2 = 1/s_{\mathrm{r}}\) for some \(r\) with \(s_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\).
  2. Hence the critical speeds for unbalance (definition 24.32) are the numbers \(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{r}}}\) for all \(r\) with \(s_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\), and their number is the number of distinct positive \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\). If \(\mat D\) is positive definite, all \(s_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) and each gives a critical speed.
Proof

We must show that the equation \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) splits along the eigenvectors of the pair \((\mat D, \mat K)\) into \(n\) scalar equations from which we read the critical speeds.

  1. The spectral theorem (theorem 10.13) holds for a pair of a symmetric matrix and a positive definite matrix. Take it with \(\mat D\) in the role of its \(\mat K\) and with our \(\mat K\) in the role of its \(\mat M\). It gives numbers \(s_1 \le \dots \le s_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\mat K\)-orthonormal \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) with \(\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). These are the numbers and columns of the theorem.
  2. The columns \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are linearly independent, since from \(\sum c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) multiplication by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat K\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\). Every column \(\vek\varphi\) is therefore a combination \(\vek\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\).
  3. We insert into \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) and use \(\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\): \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}(1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{r}})\,\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). Multiplication by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{j}}(1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for every \(j\).
  4. A nonzero solution \(\vek\varphi\) therefore exists if and only if \(1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for some \(j\), that is \(s_{\mathrm{j}} = 1/\Omega^2 > 0\). Then the solutions are the combinations of the columns with the same \(s_{\mathrm{j}}\). This is the first claim in both directions.
  5. A solution \(\vek p = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) of equation (24.34) without the right-hand side satisfies after insertion \((-\Omega^2\mat M + \Omega^2\mat G_{\mathrm{p}} + \mat K)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), that is \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\). Conversely every solution of this equation gives a solution of the free motion, because the derivation is reversible. By definition 24.32 the speed is critical if and only if \(\omega = \Omega\) coincides with a natural frequency, which is the second claim.
  6. If \(\mat D\) is positive definite, then \(s_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) for every \(r\) (from \(\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) after multiplication by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\)), so every \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) gives a critical speed.

Source: The proof is not from the source. FPGL §6.8.1 (PDF p. 295): in synchronous whirl the transverse inertia of the disk becomes \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\) and the rotary inertia of the shaft becomes \(-\rho I\).

We proved the theorem only for a pair of matrices, and all the conclusions are arithmetic with the numbers \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\). Each \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) carries one critical speed if it is positive and none if it is negative or zero. Let us test it on rotor \(R_2\).

Rotor \(R_2\), four-coordinate model. The disk has mass \(m = 49{,}009\) kg, \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}9802\) kg m\(^2\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}5003\) kg m\(^2\) and sits at \(0{,}25\) m from the left bearing of a steel shaft with \(EI = 24\,630\) N m\(^2\) and \(L = 1\) m. The shaft is massless, the coordinates are the displacement \(v\) and the slope \(v'\) of the disk (section 24.4). The stiffness matrix at the disk is \(\mat K = \begin{bmatrix} 4{,}9041\cdot10^6 & -1{,}0509\cdot10^6 \\ -1{,}0509\cdot10^6 & 3{,}9408\cdot10^5 \end{bmatrix}\) (N/m, N, N m), the mass matrix is \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(49{,}009,\ 0{,}5003)\) and the polar one is \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{diag}(0,\ 0{,}9802)\). So \(\mat D = \mathrm{diag}(49{,}009,\ -0{,}4799)\): the first entry is the mass, the second is \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\), negative. The eigenproblem \(\mat D\vek\varphi = s\mat K\vek\varphi\) gives (computed with Python, NumPy, num-06.py) \(s_1 = -1{,}3037\cdot10^{-6}\) s\(^2\) and \(s_2 = 2{,}1780\cdot10^{-5}\) s\(^2\). By theorem 24.33 there is one critical speed, \(\Omega = 1/\sqrt{s_2} = 214{,}28\) 1/s, that is \(2046{,}2\) rev/min, and \(s_1 < 0\) gives none.

Figure 24.20 shows the response of rotor \(R_2\) to the unbalance force acting on the disk, without damping (blue curve). Vertically is the displacement \(|v|\) of the disk in mm on a logarithmic axis, horizontally the spin speed \(n\) in rev/min. At \(2046\) rev/min it has a vertical dashed line at which the response grows beyond all bounds (equation of theorem 24.34 below). Everywhere else it is finite.

The figure also shows what the theorem promises: of the four speeds at which something coincides for \(R_2\) (\(1771\), \(1906\), \(5501\) and \(8793\) rev/min), none is critical. Two are dashed grey lines, two are dotted grey lines. Each of them is also a test of the theorem, which says when a natural frequency is not a critical speed, and we treat each separately (rule 48). First the response by modes, which we need for both.

Theorem 24.34 Unbalance response by modes (odziv na neuravnoteženost po oblikah)

In the setting of theorem 24.33 let \(\Omega > 0\) and \(\Omega^2 \ne 1/s_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\) with \(s_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\). Let \(\vek u\) be the complex unbalance column of section 24.6.2. Then equation (24.34) with the right-hand side \(\Omega^2\vek u\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) has exactly one solution of the form \(\vek p = \vek P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) (circular forward whirl with frequency \(\Omega\)), namely

\[\vek P = \Omega^2\sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{n}\frac{\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek u}{1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} . \tag{24.35}\]

A term with \(s_{\mathrm{r}} \le 0\) has the denominator \(1 + \Omega^2|s_{\mathrm{r}}| \ge 1\) and is finite at every speed. For \(s_{\mathrm{r}} < 0\) its coefficient is smaller in absolute value than \(|\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek u|/|s_{\mathrm{r}}|\) at all speeds, for \(s_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) it grows like \(\Omega^2\).

Proof

We must show that the expression is a solution and that it is the only one. Insertion of \(\vek p = \vek P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) gives \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek P = \Omega^2\vek u\) (the same calculation as in step 5 of theorem 24.33, only with the right-hand side).

  1. We expand \(\vek P = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) (theorem 24.33, step 2). The complex \(c_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the only unknowns, because the \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) are real and independent.
  2. Multiplication of the equation by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\) and \(\mat D\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = s_{\mathrm{r}}\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) give, as in step 3 of the theorem, \(c_{\mathrm{j}}(1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{j}}) = \Omega^2\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\vek u\).
  3. Since \(1 - \Omega^2 s_{\mathrm{j}} \ne 0\) by assumption for \(s_{\mathrm{j}} > 0\) and \(\ge 1\) for \(s_{\mathrm{j}} \le 0\), \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\) is uniquely determined, and this is (24.35). For \(s_{\mathrm{j}} < 0\) we have \(\Omega^2/(1 + \Omega^2|s_{\mathrm{j}}|) < 1/|s_{\mathrm{j}}|\), which gives the bound of the coefficient.

Source: The proof is not from the source. The form is the same as the response by modes of theorem 10.49, but with the matrix \(\mat D\) without sign instead of a positive definite mass matrix.

The theorem says how the unbalance of an individual disk excites an individual mode: with the weight \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek u\). For \(R_2\) these two numbers are \(1{,}0315\cdot10^{-7}\) and \(1{,}6871\cdot10^{-6}\) kg\(^{1/2}\) m s (the unit follows from the normalization \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\), for \(\vek u = (m a_{\mathrm{u}},\ 0)\) with \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm) and the response at \(1800\) rev/min is the sum of the term with \(s_2 > 0\), which gives \(0{,}1825\) mm, and the term with \(s_1 < 0\), which gives \(0{,}0001\) mm, together \(0{,}1827\) mm. Direct solution of the \(2\times 2\) system gives the same value \(0{,}182653\) mm (calculation, num-06.out).

Test 1: a natural frequency at rest is not always a critical speed. Without spin \(R_2\) has the natural frequencies \(1906\) and \(8793\) rev/min (\(31{,}77\) and \(146{,}55\) Hz). If we neglect the gyroscopic term (\(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0\) in \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\), \(\mat D = \mat M\)), these are also the critical speeds. With the gyroscopic term only one remains, \(2046\) rev/min. The reason is that \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}9802 > J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}5003\): the difference \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\) is negative. By the spectral theorem (theorem 10.13) for the pair \((\mat D, \mat K)\), \(s_1\) is the smallest and \(s_2\) the largest value of the quotient \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x/\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x\). The column \(\vek x = (0,\ 1)\) (slope only) gives \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x = -0{,}4799 < 0\), so \(s_1 < 0\). The column \((1,\ 0)\) (displacement only) gives \(49{,}009 > 0\), so \(s_2 > 0\). As figure 24.20 says, the response at \(8793\) rev/min is not unbounded and there is not even a peak (dotted line on the right). For a disk at midspan (\(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 0\), section 24.4) displacement and slope do not couple and the equation of pure tilt \(-J_{\mathrm{t}}\omega^2 + J_{\mathrm{p}}\Omega\omega + k_{\mathrm{R}} = 0\) shows the same: at \(\omega = \Omega\) it gives \(\Omega^2(J_{\mathrm{p}} - J_{\mathrm{t}}) = -k_{\mathrm{R}}\), which for \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > J_{\mathrm{t}}\) and \(k_{\mathrm{R}} > 0\) has a negative right-hand side and a positive left-hand side, so there is no solution. For \(R_2\) we have \(k_{\mathrm{C}} \ne 0\) and only the conclusion from \(s_1 < 0\) holds: the upper forward curve of frequencies never reaches the line \(\omega = \Omega\), because a crossing would give a second critical speed, and it grows with \(\Omega\) faster than this line (\(146{,}55\) Hz at rest, \(271{,}04\) Hz at \(6000\) rev/min, section 24.4, lemma 24.26).

Let us compare with a disk for which the theorem promises two speeds. A cylinder of the same mass \(49{,}009\) kg and length \(0{,}4\) m has radius \(70{,}7\) mm, \(J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}1225\) kg m\(^2\) and \(J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}7147\) kg m\(^2\), so \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}5922 > 0\) and \(\mat D\) is positive definite. We idealize the cylinder as a rigid body attached at one point of the shaft at \(0{,}25\) m: the massless shaft stays the same and the cylinder does not stiffen it over its length of \(0{,}4\) m. The natural frequencies at rest are \(1876\) and \(7476\) rev/min. The eigenvalues of the pair are \(s = 1{,}3770\cdot10^{-6}\) and \(2{,}5447\cdot10^{-5}\) s\(^2\), the critical speeds are \(1893\) and \(8138\) rev/min. Both natural frequencies at rest therefore have their partner in synchronous forward whirl. For the thinner disk they do not.

Test 2: backward whirl is not excited by unbalance. The unbalance turns forward, but it is not necessary that the whirl backward would coincide with it as well.

Corollary 24.35 Unbalance does not excite backward whirl (neuravnoteženost ne vzbuja vrtinčenja nazaj)

In the setting of theorem 24.33 equation (24.34) without the right-hand side has a solution \(\vek p = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) with \(\vek\varphi \ne \vek 0\) (synchronous backward whirl) if and only if \(\big(\mat K - \Omega^2(\mat M + \mat G_{\mathrm{p}})\big)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\). If at such a speed \(\Omega^2 \ne 1/s_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r\) with \(s_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\), the response to unbalance at it is finite, as equation (24.35) says.

Proof

We must show the first claim by insertion, the second is theorem 24.34.

  1. Insertion of \(\vek p = \vek\varphi\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) gives \(\ddot{\vek p} = -\Omega^2\vek p\) and \(\dot{\vek p} = -\mathrm{i}\Omega\,\vek p\). The term \(-\mathrm{i}\Omega\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\dot{\vek p}\) is therefore \(-\mathrm{i}\Omega\cdot(-\mathrm{i}\Omega)\,\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\vek p = -\Omega^2\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\vek p\).
  2. The equation becomes \((-\Omega^2\mat M - \Omega^2\mat G_{\mathrm{p}} + \mat K)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\), which is the claim.
  3. The response to unbalance has the form \(\vek P\,\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) and the matrix in it is \(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D\), not \(\mat K - \Omega^2(\mat M + \mat G_{\mathrm{p}})\). If its determinant is not zero, \(\vek P\) is finite by theorem 24.34.

Source: FPGL §6.7 (PDF p. 294): in an axisymmetric rotor on isotropic bearings unbalance does not excite the modes that whirl backward, although their natural frequencies are computed as critical speeds.

For \(R_2\) we have \(\mat M + \mat G_{\mathrm{p}} = \mathrm{diag}(49{,}009,\ 1{,}4805)\) and the backward whirl coincides with the speed at \(1771{,}1\) and \(5501{,}0\) rev/min (grey dashed lines in figure 24.20). The response to unbalance is \(0{,}1594\) mm at \(1771\) rev/min, \(0{,}0621\) mm at \(1500\) rev/min and \(0{,}3340\) mm at \(1900\) rev/min, rising smoothly towards the peak at \(2046\) rev/min. The reason lies in the work of the force: the unbalance force \(F = F_{\mathrm{v}} + \mathrm{i}F_{\mathrm{w}}\) on a circular motion with \(\vek p = P\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) has the power \(F_{\mathrm{v}}\dot v + F_{\mathrm{w}}\dot w = \mathrm{Re}(\bar F\dot p)\) and for \(F \propto \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) this is proportional to \(\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\), its average over a revolution is zero, so the force supplies no energy to the backward motion. For forward motion this power is constant. The condition is that the rotor is axisymmetric and the bearings have equal stiffness in both directions: for different stiffnesses \(k_{\mathrm{v}} \ne k_{\mathrm{w}}\) we found in section 24.2 ellipses and backward orbits under forward excitation. Here the assumption of isotropy thus supports the claim.

Circular orbits and the fact that unbalance does not excite backward whirl are properties of the model, not rules: for an unsymmetric rotor or bearings there are no circular orbits and a backward mode can be excited as well (FPGL, §6.9, PDF p. 299).

24.6.4 Shapes at the critical speeds

A critical speed only says at which speed a peak occurs. For design we must also know what shape the shaft has then: where the motion is largest and where it stands still, and what the slope of the disk is. We name it by what it shows: the shape the shaft takes (mode shape), as with the mode shapes of chapter 10.

Definition 24.36 Mode shape at a critical speed (oblika pri kritični hitrosti)

Let \(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{r}}}\) be a critical speed from theorem 24.33 and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) a real column with \((\mat K - \Omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\mat D)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) (a column of \(n\) numbers, displacements and slopes of the nodes, determined up to multiplication by a nonzero number). The mode shape at a critical speed is this column together with the motion \(\vek p(t) = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} t}\) that it describes: every point of the shaft has displacements \(v = \varphi\cos\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) and \(w = \varphi\sin\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) with one real number \(\varphi\) per point.

Source: FPGL §6.9 (PDF p. 299): the mode shape at a critical speed is obtained by setting the spin speed to the critical one and computing the right eigenvector.

Because \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is real, all points of the shaft have the same phase: the displacement of a point is \(\varphi(x)\) in a plane that is turned about the bearing axis by the angle \(\Omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\). The curve \(\varphi(x)\) (between the nodes it is interpolated with Hermite functions, definition 12.1) is thus a plane curve that turns as a whole with the spin speed, and each of its points describes a circle of radius \(|\varphi(x)|\). Only the points with \(\varphi(x) = 0\) (nodes) stand still.

Figure 24.21 shows four shapes obtained with the finite element model (\(n = 8\) elements, section 24.5) and with the mass of the shaft: rotor \(R_1\) (disk at midspan) and \(R_2\) (disk at \(0{,}25\) m), each at the first and second forward critical speed. Let us draw it as an eigenvector: first the bearing axis (dashed), at the ends the bearings (triangles), then the shape curve (blue), on which the disk (thick line, drawn perpendicular to the tangent of the curve, because the disk stays perpendicular to the shaft axis), and where the curve crosses the axis, the node (circle).

For \(R_1\) the first shape (top left, \(1416\) rev/min) is nearly the shape of the shaft in one half wave \(\sin(\pi x/L)\) (the largest difference is \(0{,}018\) of the largest displacement), the disk at midspan does not tilt (slope \(0\)). So the gyroscopic term of the disk does not act on it. The gyroscopic term of the shaft remains (lemma 24.27) and raises it from \(1415{,}8\) rev/min at rest (section 24.5) to \(1416{,}0\) rev/min, by \(0{,}008\) %. The second shape (top right, \(28\,384\) rev/min) has a node at midspan, where the disk sits, and the disk tilts there with slope \(0{,}836\) per metre of normalized displacement. It is just this slope that lifts it from \(7002\) rev/min at rest to \(28\,384\) rev/min, because the disk is thin and \(J_{\mathrm{p}} > J_{\mathrm{t}}\) (section 24.5, numbers from that section). This is also an example of a critical speed that does not harm the machine. An unbalance on the disk gives a column \(\vek u\) with a component only at the displacement of the disk, which is zero in the second shape, so \(\vek\varphi^\T\vek u = 0\) for this shape and its term in equation (24.35) is zero at every speed. The response around \(28\,384\) rev/min therefore stays bounded, although this speed is critical by definition 24.32. For \(R_2\) the disk is markedly tilted already at the first shape (bottom left, \(1896{,}2\) rev/min, slope \(2{,}09\) per metre): the disk is not at midspan, so it is at a place where the curve has a slope. At the second shape (bottom right, \(12\,817\) rev/min) the shaft has a node at \(0{,}295\) m, just right of the disk, the disk has displacement \(-0{,}059\) and slope \(0{,}635\) per metre.

The four-coordinate model estimated the first critical speed of \(R_2\) at \(2046{,}2\) rev/min, the finite element model with the mass of the shaft at \(1896{,}2\) rev/min, \(7{,}3\) % lower. The difference is the mass and inertias of the shaft, which has \(20{,}0\) % of the mass of the disk: for \(R_1\) the same shaft lowered the frequency by \(4{,}53\) % (section 24.1), for \(R_2\) by more. In the first shape of \(R_2\) the disk moves only by \(0{,}80\) of the largest displacement, for \(R_1\) by the whole largest displacement. So for \(R_2\) the shaft carries a larger share of the moving mass.

24.6.5 The map of critical speeds

The stiffness of the bearings of a machine is often not known exactly: it depends on how the bearings are made and also on how the machine is fixed (FPGL, §6.10, PDF p. 299-300). The designer wants to know how the uncertainty of this stiffness affects the critical speeds, and whether a critical speed falls into the working range for an unknown stiffness. The answer is the critical speed map (zemljevid kritičnih hitrosti): the critical speeds as functions of the stiffness. First a theorem that says in which direction the first one goes.

Theorem 24.37 Support stiffness and the first critical speed (togost podpor in prva kritična hitrost)

Let \(\mat M\), \(\mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\) and \(\mat K\) be as in theorem 24.33 and let there be a column \(\vek y\) with \(\vek y^\T\mat D\vek y > 0\) for \(\mat D = \mat M - \mat G_{\mathrm{p}}\). Then the first (lowest) critical speed is \(\Omega_1\) and

\[\Omega_1^2 = \min\left\{\frac{\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x}{\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x} : \vek x^\T\mat D\vek x > 0\right\} . \tag{24.36}\]

If we add stiffness: \(\mat K' = \mat K + \Delta\mat K\) with \(\Delta\mat K\) symmetric and positive semidefinite (for example springs of the bearings), the first critical speed is \(\Omega_1' \ge \Omega_1\).

Proof

We must show that the first critical speed is read off the largest \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\), and that this follows from the largest Rayleigh quotient, from which the dependence on \(\mat K\) is visible at once.

  1. We apply the spectral theorem (theorem 10.13) to the pair \((-\mat D, \mat K)\): the smallest eigenvalue is the smallest value of \(-\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x\) among the columns with \(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x = 1\). It is \(-s_{\max}\), where \(s_{\max}\) is the largest of the numbers \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) of theorem 24.33. So for every \(\vek x\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x \le s_{\max}\,\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x\) (both sides are homogeneous of degree \(2\)) and equality holds at the corresponding \(\vek\varphi\).
  2. For \(\vek y\) from the assumption, \(0 < \vek y^\T\mat D\vek y \le s_{\max}\,\vek y^\T\mat K\vek y\), so \(s_{\max} > 0\). By theorem 24.33 the lowest critical speed is \(\Omega_1 = 1/\sqrt{s_{\max}}\).
  3. For \(\vek x\) with \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x > 0\) the quotient \(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x/\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x \ge 1/s_{\max}\) by step 1, and equality holds at \(\vek\varphi\) with \(s_{\max}\). So \(1/s_{\max} = \Omega_1^2\) is the minimum, which is (24.36).
  4. For \(\mat K' = \mat K + \Delta\mat K\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat K'\vek x = \vek x^\T\mat K\vek x + \vek x^\T\Delta\mat K\vek x \ge \vek x^\T\mat K\vek x\) for every \(\vek x\), because \(\Delta\mat K\) is positive semidefinite. For every \(\vek x\) with \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x > 0\) the quotient with \(\mat K'\) is therefore at least as large as with \(\mat K\), and so is its minimum. So \(\Omega_1'^2 \ge \Omega_1^2\).

Source: The proof and the minimum principle of the quotient for critical speeds are not from the source. Critical speeds as an eigenvalue problem: FPGL §6.8 (PDF p. 295).

For \(R_1\) with bearing springs, each of stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\), we get the first critical speed from the finite element model (\(n = 8\), with the mass of the shaft, num-06.py): \(519{,}4\), \(1108{,}6\), \(1373{,}8\), \(1411{,}6\), \(1415{,}5\) rev/min at \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 10^5\), \(10^6\), \(10^7\), \(10^8\), \(10^9\) N/m, with the limit \(1416{,}0\) for rigid bearings. The theorem says that they grow, but not how fast. The answer is given by an estimate with the mass of the disk, without the mass of the shaft: the shaft of stiffness \(k = 1{,}1822\cdot10^6\) N/m is in series with two bearing springs that act in parallel (\(2k_{\mathrm{b}}\)), so the total stiffness is \(1/(1/k + 1/(2k_{\mathrm{b}}))\) and the critical speed is reduced by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1 + k/(2k_{\mathrm{b}})}\) against the value with rigid bearings. At \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 10^7\) N/m we have \(k/(2k_{\mathrm{b}}) = 0{,}059\) and the factor is \(0{,}972\). Applied to \(1416{,}0\) rev/min with rigid bearings it gives \(1376\) rev/min, the finite elements give \(1373{,}8\) rev/min. The bearings are therefore essential when \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) is comparable to \(k/2 = 5{,}9\cdot10^5\) N/m, and unimportant when \(k_{\mathrm{b}} \gg k\): at \(3{,}16\cdot10^7\) N/m the first critical speed is already \(1402{,}2\) rev/min, within \(1\,\%\) of the limit.

Figure 24.22 shows the map for \(R_1\): horizontally the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{b}}\) of each bearing spring, vertically the critical speed \(n\), both on logarithmic axes. The first curve (blue) grows from \(519\) to \(1416\) rev/min and for \(k_{\mathrm{b}} \gtrsim 10^7\) N/m levels off at the dashed limit for rigid bearings. The second and third curves are different at low stiffness: at \(k_{\mathrm{b}} = 10^5\) N/m the second is \(3440\) rev/min, the third \(7607\) rev/min, and at \(10^9\) N/m they are \(28\,300\) and \(30\,249\) rev/min, and even there they are still \(0{,}3\) % and \(0{,}3\) % below \(28\,384\) and \(30\,339\) rev/min. The second stays sensitive even at large stiffnesses, as its growth from \(27\,543\) at \(10^8\) N/m to \(28\,300\) at \(10^9\) N/m shows. At low stiffness the second has an understandable reason: the rotor tilts as a rigid body on the bearing springs. The estimate from the rotation of a rigid rotor about midspan, \(\Omega^2 = k_{\mathrm{b}} L^2/(2J)\) with \(J = m_{\mathrm{shaft}}L^2/12 + J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}} = 0{,}3369\) kg m\(^2\) (shaft mass \(9{,}802\) kg, difference \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}} = -0{,}4799\) kg m\(^2\) from section 24.6.3), gives at \(10^5\) N/m \(3679\) rev/min against \(3440\) rev/min from finite elements, \(7\) % more, because the estimate does not know the bending of the shaft, which lowers the frequency. The estimate of the first, \(\sqrt{2k_{\mathrm{b}}/(m + m_{\mathrm{shaft}})}\), is the quotient (24.36) for the rigid translation \(\vek x\) (all displacements equal to \(1\), slopes zero, the shaft does not bend: \(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x = 2k_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(\vek x^\T\mat D\vek x = m + m_{\mathrm{shaft}}\)). By theorem 24.37 it is therefore an upper bound: at \(10^5\) N/m \(556{,}9\) rev/min against \(519{,}4\) rev/min, \(7\) % more.

We proved the theorem only for the first critical speed. That all three curves grow is shown by the figure, but a proof for the second and third needs a theorem on maxima over subspaces, which the book does not yet have. The difference \(J_{\mathrm{t}} - J_{\mathrm{p}}\) is negative for \(R_1\), and still the rotor has the critical speeds \(n_2\) and \(n_3\). Only one \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) is negative: with rigid bearings the finite element model has \(16\) degrees of freedom in one plane, and of the \(16\) numbers \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(15\) are positive (computed with Python). For soft bearings the estimate above shows the reason: the transverse inertia of the shaft \(m_{\mathrm{shaft}}L^2/12 = 0{,}817\) kg m\(^2\) is larger than \(J_{\mathrm{p}} - J_{\mathrm{t}} = 0{,}4799\) kg m\(^2\), so \(J = 0{,}3369\) kg m\(^2 > 0\).

24.6.6 Run-up through a critical speed

A rotor in operation runs above the first critical speed, so on its way from rest it must pass through it. For \(R_1\) the steady peak is \(a_{\mathrm{u}}/(2\zeta) = 1{,}250\) mm at \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), but this holds only for a rotor that runs at the critical speed for a long time. In run-up the speed rises and the amplitude may not have time to grow to the steady value. Section 9.8.3 tested this for a machine on pads with steady values in a slow run-up, now we ask how slow it must be for that to be true (FPGL, §6.11, PDF p. 302-305).

We write the equation of motion. The spin speed rises with a constant angular acceleration \(\dot\Omega\) (1/s\(^2\)), the angle of rotation of the shaft is \(\gamma(t)\) with \(\dot\gamma = \Omega(t)\) and \(\ddot\gamma = \dot\Omega\).

Lemma 24.38 Equation of run-up of the Jeffcott rotor (enačba zagona Jeffcottovega rotorja)

A Jeffcott rotor (definition 24.1) with disk mass \(m\), stiffness \(k\), external damping \(c\) and unbalance \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) turns with the angle \(\gamma(t)\) and the angular velocity \(\Omega = \dot\gamma\). The disk centre has the complex displacement \(p = v + \mathrm{i}w\), the centre of mass of the disk is at distance \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) from the centre in the direction of the angle \(\gamma\). Then

\[m\ddot p + c\dot p + k\,p = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\,(\Omega^2 - \mathrm{i}\dot\Omega)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma} . \tag{24.37}\]

For \(\dot\Omega = 0\) this is the equation of section 24.2.

Proof

We must show the equation of motion of the centre of mass of the disk. We neglect the weight as in section 24.2 (the linear model adds it as a constant displacement).

  1. The centre of mass of the disk has the position \(z = p + a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma}\) (the centre and a turn by \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) in the direction \(\gamma\)).
  2. The second derivative in time is \(\ddot z = \ddot p + a_{\mathrm{u}}(\mathrm{i}\ddot\gamma - \dot\gamma^2)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma}\), because \(\dot{(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma})} = \mathrm{i}\dot\gamma\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma}\).
  3. In the plane of rotation the disk is acted on only by the force of the shaft, which is at the centre (\(-kp - c\dot p\): elastic and damping). Newton's law for the centre of mass of a rigid body (theorem 8.74) says \(m\ddot z = -kp - c\dot p\).
  4. We insert step 2: \(m\ddot p + m a_{\mathrm{u}}(\mathrm{i}\dot\Omega - \Omega^2)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma} = -kp - c\dot p\), which gives (24.37).

Source: The proof is not from the source. FPGL §6.11 (PDF p. 302) gives equation (24.37) in dimensionless form for a constant acceleration.

The additional term \(-\mathrm{i}\dot\Omega\) is small at our accelerations: the ratio \(\dot\Omega/\Omega^2\) at \(\Omega = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and \(\dot\Omega = 100\) 1/s\(^2\) is \(0{,}0041\), so \(0{,}4\) % of the main term \(\Omega^2\). We integrated the equation for \(R_1\) (\(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 155{,}32\) 1/s, \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm) numerically (Runge-Kutta of fourth order, step \(1/80\) of the period) from \(0{,}5\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) to \(1{,}5\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) with constant acceleration and a start from the steady state at \(0{,}5\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is a numerical observation, not a theorem.

Figure 24.23 shows all three runs: horizontally the ratio \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), vertically \(|p|\) in units of the steady peak. We drew first the steady curve (grey, peak \(1{,}250\) mm, drawn as \(1\) on the dashed horizontal), for which the rotor at each moment is in the steady state at the current speed. Then the three run-ups: the slow one (\(\dot\Omega = 5\) 1/s\(^2\), \(48\) rev/min per second) is still close to the steady curve and reaches \(96\) % of the peak, the middle one (\(\dot\Omega = 20\), \(191\) rev/min per second) \(84\) %, the fast one (\(\dot\Omega = 100\), \(955\) rev/min per second) only \(59\) %. A faster run-up lowers the peak.

We notice two things and each needs an explanation. The first is that the peak is below the steady one. The amplitude of a system with damping \(\zeta\) follows a changing force with a delay of the order of the time constant \(1/(\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}) = 0{,}322\) s (after that time the amplitude of a free oscillation falls by the factor \(\mathrm{e}\), chapter 9). The resonance peak has a width in frequency \(2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 6{,}21\) 1/s (width at half power, theorem 9.62). At acceleration \(\dot\Omega\) the rotor crosses this width in the time \(2\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}}/\dot\Omega\), and the ratio of this time to the time constant is \(2\zeta^2\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2/\dot\Omega\): for \(\dot\Omega = 5\) it is \(3{,}86\), for \(20\) it is \(0{,}96\), for \(100\) it is \(0{,}19\). In the first the amplitude has time to grow, in the last it does not, and falls to \(59\) %. The ratios are numbers for this rotor, not a law: the explanation is an estimate of the order of magnitude, not a proof.

The second is that the peak is at a higher speed than \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) (\(1{,}016\), \(1{,}044\), \(1{,}118\)), to the right of the dashed vertical \(r = 1\). The amplitude never grows at the moment when the speed is at \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), but keeps climbing and reaches the peak when the speed is already higher: the difference is the way the rotor covers in the time of the delay. After the peak the amplitude oscillates (ripples to the right of the peak in the figure), because the free oscillation with frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) that remains from the transient adds to the forced oscillation with frequency \(\Omega\) and their difference of frequencies grows with time (beating, chapter 9). On the axis \(r\) the ripples are sparser at higher acceleration. The beat phase gathered since the crossing is \(\dot\Omega t^2/2 = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2(r - 1)^2/(2\dot\Omega)\), where \(t = (\Omega - \omega_{\mathrm{n}})/\dot\Omega\) is the time since the crossing: at the same speed less time has passed since the crossing and the phase is smaller. At \(\dot\Omega = 5\) 1/s\(^2\) there are no ripples, because the free part dies out meanwhile.

A practical rule (FPGL, §6.11, PDF p. 302-305): the run-up through a critical speed should be as fast as possible, because the peak is reduced and the rotor spends fewer revolutions at a high amplitude, and the critical speed should be well damped. For very large machines, such as power plant turbines, the run-up takes about \(20\) minutes and the response is almost steady, which is computed in the frequency domain (FPGL, PDF p. 305). For our rotor the steady peak \(1{,}25\) mm and the \(0{,}96\) from the table at \(\dot\Omega = 5\) 1/s\(^2\) show that in a slow run-up the steady calculation is enough, and in a fast one it overestimates the peak by \(1/0{,}59 - 1 = 69\) %.

24.6.7 What we have shown

We connected two definitions of the critical speed: the peak of the response from section 24.2 is, for light damping, near the speed at which the excitation and natural frequencies coincide (definition 24.32), and the latter does not depend on the unbalance. For the unbalance of an axisymmetric rotor on isotropic bearings it equals the eigenproblem \((\mat K - \Omega^2\mat D)\vek\varphi = \vek 0\) (theorem 24.33, proved from the spectral theorem), and only positive \(s_{\mathrm{r}}\) give critical speeds: for a thin disk there are fewer of them than natural frequencies, as \(R_2\) and the cylinder showed. Backward whirl is not excited by unbalance (corollary 24.35), but only for an isotropic rotor. The shape at a critical speed is a plane curve that turns with the rotor (definition 24.36, figure 24.21), the first critical speed does not fall with larger bearing stiffness (theorem 24.37, figure 24.22), and run-up lowers the peak by the ratio of the time of crossing the resonance to the time constant (numerical observation, figure 24.23).
