Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.10 Hamilton's principle (Hamiltonovo načelo)

In section 8.7 we obtained the equation of motion from equilibrium at each instant separately: at the instant \(t\) the work of the applied and inertial forces is zero for every virtual displacement that the constraints allow. In section 8.9 we obtained from this the equations for the coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\). In chapter 5 we saw something different: the equilibrium of a beam is a stationary point of a functional, that is, of one number that we assign to the whole deflection curve. Does motion also have such a number? We look for a rule that assigns one number to the whole motion from the instant \(t_1\) to the instant \(t_2\), such that the motion the system really performs is its stationary point.

The answer is yes, and it is given by a principle (Hamiltonovo načelo) that we prove in this section. First we test it on the pendulum, then we prepare the mathematics it needs, and finally we prove it and apply it to a bar.

8.10.1 An experiment on the pendulum (poskus na nihalu)

Take pendulum A of section 8.9: a point mass \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid massless rod of length \(l = 1\) m, at the angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, with gravitational acceleration \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s². Its Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) is \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\), that is, the kinetic energy minus the potential energy (theorem 8.90). We release it from rest at \(\theta = 90°\) and observe for \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s, that is, from \(t_1 = 0\) to \(t_2 = \tau\). The equation of motion \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\) (RK4, as in section 8.9) gives at \(t_2\) the angle \(\theta = 0{,}4018\) rad, that is, \(23{,}0°\).

Now take other motions that begin and end in the same positions at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\): \(\theta(t) + \alpha\,\eta(t)\) with the function \(\eta(t) = 4\,t\,(\tau - t)/\tau^2\), which is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) and has the value \(1\) in the middle of the interval, and with a number \(\alpha\) in radians (figure 8.30). These are not motions the pendulum really performs but paths that we only compare. To each path we assign the number

\[\mathcal S = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \mathcal L\,\mathrm{d}t ,\]

which has the unit J s. The true path gives \(\mathcal S = 3{,}5515\) J s. For the other paths (the integral by Simpson's rule with \(20\,000\) steps) the difference from this value is

\(\alpha\) \(-0{,}2\) \(-0{,}1\) \(0{,}1\) \(0{,}2\)
\(\mathcal S - 3{,}5515\) J s \(+0{,}1934\) \(+0{,}0487\) \(+0{,}0494\) \(+0{,}1989\)

The difference is positive on both sides and almost equal for \(\alpha\) and \(-\alpha\). If \(\alpha\) appeared linearly, the difference would be negative on one side. Hence the derivative of \(\mathcal S\) with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is zero (numerically \(3\cdot 10^{-9}\) J s/rad, which is the integration error): the true path is a stationary point. Here it is even the smallest, since all the differences are positive. The quadratic term \(\tfrac12\,\alpha^2\,m\,l^2\int_0^\tau\big(\dot\eta^2 - (g/l)\cos\theta\,\eta^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}t = 4{,}902\,\alpha^2\) J s (we define the second variation later in this chapter) equals \(0{,}0490\) J s at \(\alpha = \pm 0{,}1\), close to the computed \(0{,}0487\) and \(0{,}0494\) J s.

For a longer window the picture is different. If we observe the pendulum for \(\tau = 2\) s, it ends at \(t_2\) at \(\theta = 0{,}9167\) rad, \(\mathcal S = 19{,}690\) J s, and the differences for \(\alpha = -0{,}2\), \(-0{,}1\), \(0{,}1\), \(0{,}2\) are \(-0{,}0315\), \(-0{,}0089\), \(-0{,}0109\) and \(-0{,}0476\) J s. The derivative at \(\alpha = 0\) is again zero (numerically \(-10^{-8}\) J s/rad), but the path is now not the smallest but larger than the compared ones. Stationarity remains, the minimum does not. Hence the principle will speak of a stationary point. Why the sign turns over we show later in this chapter, in the section on buckling of a column, on a pendulum with small oscillations: there the second variation is positive as long as the window is not longer than half the oscillation period, and the same inequality decides buckling.

The experiment shows that the motion is a stationary point of the number \(\mathcal S\). What does this mean for the equations that the motion satisfies, and does it hold for every system? For the answer we need the calculus of variations for several functions at once, since a system has \(n\) coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\), and for functions that must satisfy constraints.

8.10.2 Mathematics: several functions and constraints (matematika: več funkcij in vezi)

In chapter 5 we treated one functional of one function \(v(x)\) with derivatives up to the second order (definition 5.66, theorem 5.69). Here we need three extensions. The first are functionals that depend on several functions of one variable \(t\) and contain only first derivatives. The second are constraints between the functions that must not be violated. The third is a function of two variables \((x, t)\), which the bar needs. The tools stay the same: differentiation under the integral, integration by parts and the fundamental lemma.

Definition 8.97 Functional of several functions (funkcional več funkcij)

Let \(t_1 < t_2\) be numbers, \(n\) a natural number and \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) columns of \(n\) numbers. An admissible function (dopustna funkcija) is a column \(\vek q(t) = (q_1(t), \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}(t))\) of functions that have a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) (one-sided at the end points) and satisfy \(\vek q(t_1) = \vek a\), \(\vek q(t_2) = \vek b\). Let \(F(t, \vek q, \vek p)\) be a function whose input is a number \(t\) and two columns \(\vek q\), \(\vek p\) of \(n\) numbers each and whose output is a number, and let \(F\) and all its partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) be continuous on \([t_1, t_2] \times \mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R^n\). A functional (funkcional) on admissible functions is the rule

\[\mathcal S(\vek q) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} F\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{8.91}\]

which assigns one real number to each admissible function. An admissible variation (dopustna variacija) is a column \(\vek\eta = (\eta_1, \dots, \eta_{\mathrm{n}})\) of functions with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\vek\eta(t_1) = \vek\eta(t_2) = \vek 0\). The first variation (prva variacija) of the functional at \(\vek q\) in the direction \(\vek\eta\) is

\[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\,\mathcal S(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} ,\]

if the derivative exists. An admissible function \(\vek q\) is a stationary point (stacionarna točka) of the functional if \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\).

Source: EP §3.5.6 (pp. 143 to 146: the Euler equation for one function and a functional of two dependent functions with two Euler equations, (3.5.41) to (3.5.46), the statement for \(p\) functions is ours), SHABANA (3.175) to (3.180) (PDF pp. 147 to 148) for a vector of functions with fixed end points. An extension of definitions 5.66 and 5.67 to \(n\) functions. The same as the directional derivative in section 4.6.6.

The function \(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta\) is admissible for every \(\alpha\), since it has a continuous derivative and the same values at the end points as \(\vek q\). The letter \(F\) here is the integrand as in chapter 5 and not a force. For the pendulum \(n = 1\), \(q_1 = \theta\) and \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,p^2 + m\,g\,l\cos q\).

Theorem 8.98 Euler-Lagrange equations for a system (Euler-Lagrangeove enačbe za sistem)

Let \(\mathcal S\) be the functional (8.91) of definition 8.97 and \(\vek q\) an admissible function for which, for every \(s = 1, \dots, n\), the function

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}(t) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\]

is continuously differentiable on \([t_1, t_2]\). Then \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\mathcal S\) if and only if for every \(s = 1, \dots, n\)

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0 \quad \text{on } [t_1, t_2] , \tag{8.92}\]

where \(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}(t) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\). For every admissible variation

\[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t , \qquad E_{\mathrm{s}} = \hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} . \tag{8.93}\]
Proof

We must show that the first variation is the integral of the products \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\). Then we choose, one after another, variations that change only one function and use the fundamental lemma. Steps 1 to 3 are steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 5.69 with several coordinates and without second derivatives.

  1. Let \(\vek\eta\) be an admissible variation and \(c < d\) arbitrary numbers. For \(t \in [t_1, t_2]\) and real \(\alpha\) let \(f(t, \alpha) = F\big(t, \vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta}\big)\), where \(\vek q\), \(\vek\eta\) and the derivatives are taken at \(t\). For constant \(t\) this is a composite function: the map \(\alpha \mapsto (\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta})\) with derivative \((\vek\eta, \dot{\vek\eta})\), then \(F\) as a function of \(2n\) variables with continuous partial derivatives. By the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1)
    \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\alpha}(t, \alpha) = \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n}\big(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} + F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big) ,\]
    where the derivatives of \(F\) are taken at the point \(\big(t, \vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta}\big)\). The functions \(f\) and \(\partial f/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on \([t_1, t_2] \times [c, d]\) as composite functions of continuous functions (lemma 8.25 and corollary 1.64).
  2. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the function \(\alpha \mapsto \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} f(t, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}t = \mathcal S(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta)\) is differentiable on \((c, d)\) with a derivative that is the integral of the derivative. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so this holds for every \(\alpha\), and at \(\alpha = 0\)
    \[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n}\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} + \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  3. For every \(s\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) (by assumption) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\) are continuously differentiable. Integration by parts (corollary 3.41) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}{}'\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\). The terms in the square bracket are zero, because \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}}(t_1) = \eta_{\mathrm{s}}(t_2) = 0\). The first variation is therefore the integral (8.93).
  4. If (8.92) holds for all \(s\), every \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and by step 3 \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation. The function \(\vek q\) is a stationary point.
  5. Conversely, let \(\vek q\) be a stationary point. Choose \(s\) and a function \(\psi\) with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\). The column \(\vek\eta\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = \psi\) and the other components \(0\) is an admissible variation, so by step 3 \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). The function \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous: \(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\) is a composite function of continuous functions (lemma 8.25) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}{}'\) is continuous by assumption. Since \(\psi\) is arbitrary, by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) on \([t_1, t_2]\). This holds for every \(s\).

Source: SHABANA (3.179) to (3.185) (PDF p. 148) and EP (5.2.12), (5.2.13) (p. 245) for the equations. The sources integrate by parts and then use the fundamental lemma, without continuity. Differentiation under the integral is lemma 4.19 (LEBL2 theorem 9.1.1, PDF pp. 65 to 66, as in chapter 5), the fundamental lemma is lemma 4.77. The proof in steps is not from the source.

We proved the theorem by varying one function at a time. Since all variations are free except at the end points, stationarity demands \(n\) equations, one for each function. For the pendulum, \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,p^2 + m\,g\,l\cos q\), we have \(F_{\mathrm{q}} = -m\,g\,l\sin q\) and \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = m\,l^2\,p\), so equation (8.92) becomes \(-m\,g\,l\sin\theta - m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta = 0\), that is, \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\). The motion in the experiment at the beginning of the section gave the same equation, so the numerical result there, the derivative zero, is exactly this.

What if the functions are not independent of each other? Take the same pendulum in Cartesian coordinates \((x, y)\) with the centre at the bearing and the axis \(y\) pointing up. The mass is on a rod of length \(l\), so \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\) at every instant. Only paths that satisfy this are admissible. What does stationarity demand if we cannot vary \(x\) and \(y\) independently?

For constraints \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q) = 0\) the displacement \(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta\) does not preserve the constraints unless they are linear. Imagine a curve \(\vek q_\alpha(t)\) that satisfies the constraints for every \(\alpha\) and equals \(\vek q\) at \(\alpha = 0\). Its derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is a tangent displacement \(\vek\eta\), and from \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q_\alpha(t)) = 0\) it follows that \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} \frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\), which we write as \(\mat A(t)\,\vek\eta(t) = \vek 0\) with the matrix \(A_{\mathrm{rs}} = \partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 8.47). Exactly such displacements we called virtual displacements in section 8.4, so we define stationarity under constraints with them. That for every such \(\vek\eta\) a curve satisfying the constraints really exists could be shown with the implicit function theorem (theorem 8.34) and we do not need it here.

The proof of the theorem on constraints will need the matrix \((\mat A\mat A^\T)^{-1}\) to be a continuously differentiable function of time if the entries of \(\mat A\) are continuously differentiable. The book has the formula for the inverse by cofactors only up to order \(3\) (lemma 4.49, theorem 2.54), and there may be more constraints. The matrix \(\mat A\mat A^\T\) is positive definite (definition 6.27), and a positive definite system is solved by elimination with positive pivots (theorem 6.29). Each step of elimination is an arithmetic operation in which we divide only by a positive pivot. The solution is therefore a continuously differentiable function of the entries, which we write as a lemma for any order.

Lemma 8.99 Smooth solution of a system with a positive definite matrix (gladka rešitev sistema s pozitivno definitno matriko)

Let \(I\) be an interval, \(m\) a natural number, \(\mat K(t)\) a matrix of order \(m\) and \(\vek d(t)\) a column of \(m\) entries whose entries are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\) on \(I\) (one-sided at an end point). Let \(\mat K(t)\) be positive definite for every \(t \in I\). Then the system \(\mat K(t)\,\vek c = \vek d(t)\) has exactly one solution \(\vek c(t)\) for every \(t\), the entries of the column \(\vek c(t)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\), the matrix \(\mat K(t)\) is invertible and the entries of the matrix \(\mat K(t)^{-1}\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\).

Proof

We must show that each step of elimination from the proof of theorem 6.29 turns continuously differentiable functions into such functions again. The statement \(P(m)\) reads: the lemma holds for order \(m\). We prove it by induction (theorem 1.44). The entries that are denoted \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) there are denoted \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) and \(d_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) here.

  1. Rules. A sum and a product of continuously differentiable functions are continuously differentiable (lemma 1.81, points 1 and 3, and corollary 1.64 for the continuity of the derivative). A quotient \(f/g\) of continuously differentiable functions with \(g(t) \neq 0\) for every \(t\) is continuously differentiable (lemma 4.108, point 3), and its derivative (4.74) is continuous, because it is composed of continuous functions with the denominator \(g^2 \neq 0\) (point 2 of the same lemma and corollary 1.64). Point 3 of the lemma is written for an open interval, at an end point the same computation holds with one-sided derivatives.
  2. Basis, \(m = 1\). The matrix is the number \(K_{11}(t)\), which is positive because \(c\,K_{11}\,c > 0\) for \(c = 1\). The solution \(c(t) = d_1(t)/K_{11}(t)\) is by step 1 continuously differentiable, unique, and so is \(K_{11}^{-1} = 1/K_{11}\).
  3. Induction step. Let \(P(m - 1)\) hold and let \(m \ge 2\). We write \(\mat K = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} K_{11} & \vek b^\T \\ \vek b & \mat K' \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) as in step 2 of the proof of theorem 6.29. By its step 3, \(K_{11}(t) = \vek e_1^\T\mat K(t)\,\vek e_1 > 0\) for every \(t\).
  4. The first step of elimination (step 4 of that proof) gives the matrix \(\mat K'' = \mat K' - \vek b\,\vek b^\T/K_{11}\) with entries \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} - K_{\mathrm{i}1}K_{1\mathrm{j}}/K_{11}\) and the right side with entries \(d_{\mathrm{i}} - K_{\mathrm{i}1}\,d_1/K_{11}\) for \(i, j = 2, \dots, m\). By step 1 and because \(K_{11} > 0\), all these entries are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\).
  5. By steps 5 and 6 of that proof, \(\mat K''(t)\) is symmetric and positive definite of order \(m - 1\) for every \(t\). By \(P(m - 1)\) the system \(\mat K''\,\vek c'' = \vek d''\) has for every \(t\) one solution \(\vek c''(t)\) and its entries are continuously differentiable.
  6. By step 7 of that proof, \(c_1 = (d_1 - \vek b^\T\vek c'')/K_{11}\) and \((c_1, \vek c'')\) is a solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek d\). By step 1, \(c_1\) is continuously differentiable, because \(d_1\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(c''_{\mathrm{i}}\) are continuously differentiable and \(K_{11} > 0\). Uniqueness is by theorem 6.29. The first and second claims of the lemma are proved for order \(m\).
  7. By theorem 6.29 elimination has all \(m\) pivots for every \(t\), so by lemma 8.21 the matrix \(\mat K(t)\) is invertible. Column \(j\) of \(\mat K^{-1}\) is the solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek x = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) with a constant right side, so by step 6 it is continuously differentiable. This proves \(P(m)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The steps of elimination are from the proof of theorem 6.29 (KREYSZIG §7.3, PDF pp. 300 to 302), the derivatives from lemmas 1.81 and 4.108.

Theorem 8.100 Euler-Lagrange equations with constraints (Euler-Lagrangeove enačbe z vezmi)

Let \(\mathcal S\) be the functional (8.91) and \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}}\) functions that have continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \([t_1, t_2] \times \mathbb R^n\), with \(m < n\). Let \(\vek q\) be an admissible function with \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q(t)) = 0\) for every \(r\) and every \(t\), for which the \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) are continuously differentiable. Let the matrix \(\mat A(t)\) with entries \(A_{\mathrm{rs}}(t) = \frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t)\big)\) have \(m\) pivots for every \(t\) (as in theorem 8.95). We say that \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints (stacionarna točka pod vezmi) if \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\) for which \(\mat A(t)\,\vek\eta(t) = \vek 0\) for every \(t\). Then \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints if and only if there exist continuous functions \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) on \([t_1, t_2]\) that together with \(\vek q\) satisfy for every \(s\) the equations

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{m} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0 , \qquad f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q) = 0 . \tag{8.94}\]

The first are the equations of the Euler-Lagrange system (8.92) for the function \(F + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The functions \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are unique.

Proof

We must show both directions. One is a computation with the first variation. In the other, a projection onto tangent displacements gives variations that are continuously differentiable, and from the fundamental lemma we conclude that the column \(\vek E\) of theorem 8.98 is at every instant a linear combination of the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\).

  1. Let \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) be as in theorem 8.98. Step 3 of its proof holds for every admissible variation, so \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \vek E\cdot\vek\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\) for the column \(\vek E = (E_1, \dots, E_{\mathrm{n}})\). Equation (8.94) says \(\vek E = -\mat A^\T\vek\lambda\) for the column \(\vek\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}})\), since the derivative of the function \(F + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} f_{\mathrm{r}}\) with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and its derivative with respect to \(p_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\), because \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\) does not contain \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. Let (8.94) hold and let \(\mat A\,\vek\eta = \vek 0\). Then \(\vek E\cdot\vek\eta = -(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda)\cdot\vek\eta = -\vek\lambda\cdot(\mat A\,\vek\eta) = 0\) at every instant, so \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\). The function \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints.
  3. Conversely, let \(\vek q\) be stationary under the constraints. Let \(\mat K = \mat A\mat A^\T\), a matrix of order \(m\) for every \(t\). It is symmetric, because \((\mat A\mat A^\T)^\T = \mat A\mat A^\T\) (lemma 1.101). It is also positive definite: for \(\vek z \neq \vek 0\) we have \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z = (\mat A^\T\vek z)\cdot(\mat A^\T\vek z) = |\mat A^\T\vek z|^2 \ge 0\), and if \(\mat A^\T\vek z = \vek 0\) held, uniqueness in lemma 8.35 (for the column \(\vek a = \vek 0\), the matrix \(\mat A\) has \(m\) pivots) would give \(\vek z = \vek 0\). Hence \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z > 0\).
  4. The matrix \(\mat K\) is invertible and \(\mat K^{-1}\) is also a continuously differentiable function of time. The entries \(A_{\mathrm{rs}}(t) = \partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}(t, \vek q(t))\) are continuously differentiable by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1), because \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\) is twice continuously differentiable and \(\vek q\) is continuously differentiable. The entries of the matrix \(\mat K\) are sums of products of these entries, so they are continuously differentiable (lemma 1.81). By lemma 8.99 the entries of the matrix \(\mat K^{-1}\) are therefore continuously differentiable.
  5. Let \(\mat P = \mat I - \mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A\), a matrix of size \(n \times n\) for every \(t\). We have \(\mat A\mat P = \mat A - \mat A\mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A = \mat A - \mat K\mat K^{-1}\mat A = \mat 0\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric, so \(\mat K^{-1}\) is symmetric as well (lemma 1.117) and \(\mat P^\T = \mat P\) (lemma 1.101). The entries of the matrix \(\mat P\) are sums of products of continuously differentiable functions, so \(\mat P\) is continuously differentiable.
  6. Choose \(s\) and a function \(\psi\) with a continuous derivative and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\). The column \(\vek\eta = \psi\,\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\) has by step 5 a continuous derivative, is zero at the end points, and \(\mat A\,\vek\eta = \psi\,\mat A\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek 0\). It is therefore an admissible tangent variation, and by step 1 \(0 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \psi\,\vek E\cdot\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \psi\,(\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\), since \(\mat P\) is symmetric.
  7. The function \((\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous, because \(\mat P\) and \(\vek E\) are continuous (\(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 8.98). By the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \((\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) on \([t_1, t_2]\) for every \(s\), so \(\mat P\vek E = \vek 0\), that is, \(\vek E = \mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A\vek E\).
  8. Let \(\vek\lambda = -\mat K^{-1}\mat A\vek E\). It is continuous as a product of continuous functions, and by step 7 \(\vek E = -\mat A^\T\vek\lambda\), which is (8.94). If \(\vek\lambda'\) were another solution, we would have \(\mat A^\T(\vek\lambda - \vek\lambda') = \vek 0\), so by uniqueness in lemma 8.35 \(\vek\lambda = \vek\lambda'\).

Source: GR (1.77) to (1.82) (PDF pp. 62 to 63), EP (5.4.7), (5.4.11) (pp. 256, 257): the books choose the multipliers so that the coefficients of the dependent variations vanish, and state the ansatz without a proof of continuity. The proof by projection is not from a source. The algebraic statement that the column \(\vek E\) is orthogonal to all \(\vek\eta\) with \(\mat A\vek\eta = \vek 0\) if and only if \(\vek E = \mat A^\T\vek\lambda\) is lemma 8.35 at each instant separately. The projection only ensures that \(\vek\eta\) is continuously differentiable.

The sign of the multipliers is a convention: with \(F - \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} f_{\mathrm{r}}\) we would get \(-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). We chose \(+\) so that three sections agree. In section 8.3, \(\nabla V = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\) holds (equation (8.21)). In theorem 8.95, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} - \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\). For \(F = \mathcal L\) the left side is \(-E_{\mathrm{s}}\), so this is equation (8.94) with \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\). At rest \(\mathcal L = -V\), so \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and the same equation gives \(\nabla V = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The multiplier \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is thus everywhere the intensity of the reaction of constraint \(r\).

Let us test it on the pendulum with \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,(p_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + p_{\mathrm{y}}^2) - m\,g\,y\) (the kinetic energy minus the potential energy) and \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\). The matrix \(\mat A = (2x,\ 2y)\) has one pivot, since the row is not zero: \(x^2 + y^2 = l^2 > 0\). Equations (8.94) are

\[-m\,\ddot x + 2\lambda\,x = 0 , \qquad -m\,g - m\,\ddot y + 2\lambda\,y = 0 .\]

We obtain the multiplier without solving the motion. The derivative of the constraint \(x\dot x + y\dot y = 0\) differentiated once more gives \(x\ddot x + y\ddot y = -(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)\). We multiply the first equation by \(x\), the second by \(y\) and add: \(m\,(x\ddot x + y\ddot y) = 2\lambda\,(x^2 + y^2) - m\,g\,y\). With \(x = l\sin\theta\), \(y = -l\cos\theta\) and \(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = l^2\dot\theta^2\) this is \(-m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 = 2\lambda\,l^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\), so

\[\lambda = -\frac{m}{2\,l}\,\big(l\,\dot\theta^2 + g\cos\theta\big) = -\frac{N}{2\,l} , \qquad N = m\,\big(g\cos\theta + l\,\dot\theta^2\big) .\]

The reaction of the constraint on the mass is \(2\lambda\,(x, y)\), a vector of magnitude \(2|\lambda|\,l = N\) that points towards the bearing when \(\lambda < 0\). \(N\) is thus the force in the rod, the same as in section 8.9. In a descent from rest at \(90°\) we have at the start \(\dot\theta = 0\), \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\lambda = 0\). At the bottom \(\dot\theta = 4{,}4294\) 1/s, so \(N = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot(9{,}81 + 1\ \text{m}\cdot 19{,}62\ \text{1/s}^2)\ \text{m/s}^2 = 29{,}43\) N and \(\lambda = -14{,}715\) N/m. The rod pulls, as it must.

For the bar we also need the fundamental lemma for functions of two variables \((x, t)\) on a rectangle. In section 4.2.2 we proved it as point 3 of lemma 4.83, for a rectangle in a plane of space. The name of the second variable does not matter in the statement, so we use it with \(t\) in place of \(y\) and need no new lemma.

8.10.3 The principle (načelo)

Now we have the tools for the answer. Let us write the number from the experiment as a definition. Let the system be described by \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) (section 8.4), with kinetic energy \(T(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) (definition 8.79) and potential energy \(V(\vek q)\) from which the applied forces arise (definition 8.53).

Definition 8.101 Action integral (akcija)

Let \(\mathcal L(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q}) = T - V\) be the Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) of the system. The action (akcija) of the system on a path \(\vek q\), which is an admissible function of definition 8.97 on \([t_1, t_2]\), is the number

\[\mathcal S(\vek q) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \mathcal L\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

Source: GR (1.27) (PDF p. 42), EP (5.2.10) (p. 245). For \(F = \mathcal L\) this is the functional (8.91).

What is admissible? For \(F = \mathcal L\) we need \(\mathcal L\) and its partial derivatives to be continuous, that is, \(T\) and \(V\) must have continuous partial derivatives with respect to \(t\), \(\vek q\) and \(\dot{\vek q}\). The kinetic energy is a polynomial of second degree in the velocities, \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(t, \vek q)\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek b(t, \vek q)\cdot\dot{\vek q} + T_0\) (theorem 8.86), so this is true when \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\), \(T_0\) and \(V\) are continuously differentiable in \(t\) and \(\vek q\).

Theorem 8.102 Hamilton's principle (Hamiltonovo načelo)

Let a system have generalized coordinates \(\vek q\), ideal holonomic constraints solved in \(\vek q\) (definition 8.2), and kinetic energy and potential energy with continuous partial derivatives, as above. Let \(\vek q\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on \([t_1, t_2]\).

  1. If all applied forces are conservative, \(\vek q\) is a motion of the system, that is, it satisfies d'Alembert's principle (corollary 8.72), if and only if \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the action \(\mathcal S\) among paths with the same end points \(\vek q(t_1)\), \(\vek q(t_2)\).
  2. If in addition to the conservative forces there are non-conservative ones with generalized forces \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q})\), continuous functions, \(\vek q\) is a motion if and only if for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\)
    \[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n} Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = 0 . \tag{8.95}\]
Proof

We must show that the Euler-Lagrange equations of the action are exactly the Lagrange equations of section 8.9. Then the principle is another way of writing the same statement.

  1. The function \(F = \mathcal L\) has continuous partial derivatives. For a twice continuously differentiable \(\vek q\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuously differentiable: \(M_{\mathrm{sk}}\) and \(b_{\mathrm{s}}\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\) and \(\vek q\), which is continuously differentiable, so by the chain rule (lemma 8.29, item 1) they are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\), and \(\dot q_{\mathrm{k}}\) is continuously differentiable. The assumptions of theorem 8.98 are satisfied.
  2. By theorem 8.98, \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the action if and only if \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for every \(s\), that is,
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0 .\]
  3. These are the Lagrange equations of a conservative system without non-conservative forces (theorem 8.90), which are by theorem 8.88 equivalent to d'Alembert's principle. This proves point 1.
  4. For point 2 take an admissible variation \(\vek\eta\). By equation (8.93), \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) with \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\), so the left side of equation (8.95) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}})\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  5. If \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\) for all \(s\), equation (8.95) holds for every \(\vek\eta\). If it holds for every \(\vek\eta\), we choose, as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 8.98, a variation that changes only one component, and from the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77, the function \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) is continuous) we get \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\), that is, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} - \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\). These are the Lagrange equations with non-conservative forces (theorem 8.90).

Source: GR (1.27) (PDF p. 42, read), EP (5.2.8) (p. 244) for point 1, SHABANA (3.188) (PDF p. 149) for point 2. The proof is not from a source, the sources use the steps of integration by parts directly.

The idea of the theorem is that d'Alembert's principle at each instant, integrated over time, equals stationarity. Integration by parts in time moves the derivative from the inertial force \(m\,\ddot{\vek x}\) to the virtual displacement and turns the work of the inertial forces into a variation of the kinetic energy. This is step 3 of the proof of theorem 8.98. GR (PDF p. 41) therefore says that the principle is only the time-integrated form of the principle of virtual work.

Hamilton's principle thus does not give us a new equation of motion but the same one, in another way. The advantage is that the action is one number, independent of the coordinates: in whatever generalized coordinates we write it, we get the same equations. For pendulum A the action is \(\mathcal S = \int \big(\tfrac12\,m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\) and theorem 8.98 gave \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\). The experiment from the beginning of the section is therefore a test of point 1: the derivative of the action with respect to \(\alpha\) was zero on the true path. With a non-conservative force, for example angular damping with the moment \(-c\,\dot\theta\), point 2 gives \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta + c\,\dot\theta + m\,g\,l\sin\theta = 0\), because \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\,\dot\theta\).

The theorem says that the motion is a stationary point, not that it is the smallest value. The experiment showed this: at \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s the action on the true path is smaller than on the neighbouring paths, at \(\tau = 2\) s larger. The exact condition is shown by the second variation, which we will estimate later in this chapter in the section on buckling of a column.

8.10.4 The bar (palica)

So far the system had finitely many coordinates. The bar of chapters 3 and 7 has one coordinate for each cross section: the displacement \(u(x, t)\) of the cross section at the point \(x\) at time \(t\). Does the principle hold here too, and what does it give for the equation and the condition at the free end?

Take a bar of length \(L\) with constant \(A\), \(E\) and \(\rho\) (density), fixed at \(x = 0\), with the axis \(x\) along the bar. The load per unit length \(q(x, t)\) and the force \(F(t)\) at the end \(x = L\) point in the direction of increasing \(x\) and are continuous. The end has no mass and is free except for the force \(F\). All parts of the cross section move by \(u(x, t)\), so every point of the cross section at \(x\) has the velocity \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\). By definition 8.79 the kinetic energy of the bar is the integral with the density \(\rho\) over the volume \(A\,L\) of the bar, which by theorem 4.9 becomes an integral over \(x\) with the factor \(A\), \(T = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The potential energy is the total potential energy of chapter 4 (equation (4.62)) at time \(t\), because the bar is linearly elastic: \(V = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,u\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,u(L, t)\). The action of the bar is therefore

\[\mathcal S(u) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \bigg[\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + q\,u\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x + F(t)\,u(L, t)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{8.96}\]

The admissible functions are \(u\) on the rectangle \(\Omega = [0, L] \times [t_1, t_2]\) that have continuous \(u\), \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) there, satisfy \(u(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\) and have prescribed displacements \(u(x, t_1)\) and \(u(x, t_2)\) (the positions of the bar at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)). An admissible variation is a function \(\eta\) on \(\Omega\) with continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\) (and hence continuous \(\eta\)) and \(\eta(0, t) = 0\), \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\). The first variation \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta)\) is the derivative of \(\mathcal S(u + \alpha\,\eta)\) with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\), as in definition 8.97.

Theorem 8.103 Hamilton's principle for a bar (Hamiltonovo načelo za palico)

Let \(u\) be an admissible function. Then \(u\) is a stationary point of the action (8.96) if and only if

\[\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}} = EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} + q \quad \text{on } \Omega , \tag{8.97}\]

and at the free end for every \(t \in [t_1, t_2]\)

\[EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = F(t) . \tag{8.98}\]

Equation (8.98) is called the dynamic boundary condition (dinamični robni pogoj).

Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral over the rectangle and an integral over the end of the bar. Then we separate the equation with bumps, and the condition with a variation that is not zero at the end.

  1. The integrand in (8.96) at \(u + \alpha\,\eta\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\) with coefficients that are continuous functions on \(\Omega\). Hence \(\mathcal S(u + \alpha\,\eta) = \mathcal S(u) + \alpha\,B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2\) with
    \[B_1 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \big(\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}} - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}} + q\,\eta\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} F\,\eta(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t ,\]
    \[B_2 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EA\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
    The integrals exist, because the integrands are continuous (theorem 3.38), and the splitting follows from the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.39). The derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(0\) is \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) = B_1\). Equations (8.97) and (8.98) will follow from \(B_1 = 0\).
  2. For every \(x\), \(\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}(x, \cdot)\) and \(\eta(x, \cdot)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\) (continuous \(u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\)). Integration by parts (corollary 3.41) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \big[\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\), and the term in the square bracket is zero, because \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\). We may exchange the order of integration (theorem 4.9, the version for a rectangle), so the first term in \(B_1\) equals \(-\int\!\int \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. For every \(t\), \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(\cdot, t)\) and \(\eta(\cdot, t)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(x\) (continuous \(u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\)). Integration by parts gives \(-\int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,\eta(L, t) + \int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\), where the term at \(x = 0\) drops out because \(\eta(0, t) = 0\).
  4. Substituting steps 2 and 3 into \(B_1\) we obtain for every admissible variation
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(F(t) - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\big)\,\eta(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned} \tag{8.99}\]
    where \(R = -\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}} + EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} + q\) is a continuous function on \(\Omega\).
  5. If (8.97) and (8.98) hold, \(R = 0\) and \(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\), so by step 4 \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation. The function \(u\) is a stationary point.
  6. Conversely, let \(u\) be a stationary point. For numbers \(0 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < L\) and \(t_1 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < t_2\) let \(\eta(x, t) = p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\) (lemma 4.83, point 1). It has continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\), is zero at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t = t_1\) and \(t = t_2\), so it is an admissible variation with \(\eta(L, t) = 0\). By step 4, \(\int\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). Since \(\eta\) is zero outside \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2]\), this is an integral over this rectangle, and by theorem 4.9 we may write it with the inner integral over \(t\). For all such \(\alpha_1, \beta_1, \alpha_2, \beta_2\) the assumption of point 3 of lemma 4.83 holds for the rectangle \(\Omega\) with coordinates \(x\) and \(y = t\) and the continuous function \(f(x, y) = R(x, y)\), so \(R = 0\) on \(\Omega\). This is equation (8.97).
  7. Let \(\psi\) be a function with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\), and \(\eta(x, t) = (x/L)\,\psi(t)\). It has continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = \psi/L\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}} = (x/L)\,\dot\psi\), is zero at \(x = 0\), \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), so it is admissible, and \(\eta(L, t) = \psi(t)\). By step 6 the first integral in (8.99) is zero, so \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\big)\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). The function \(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, \cdot)\) is continuous, and by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) it is zero on \([t_1, t_2]\). This is (8.98).

Source: EP example 5.3.1 (pp. 251 to 252), CM §7.7.3 (7.7.30) (PDF p. 367). The sources derive the equations from \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) by integration by parts and the conclusion that the coefficients are zero, without determining which variations are admissible. The proof in steps is not from a source.

Equation (8.97) is the equilibrium of a slice of the bar (section 4.4.4) to which the inertial force \(-\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) is added (section 8.7): \(N_{\mathrm{x}} + q = \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) for the axial force \(N = EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\). The dynamic boundary condition is the natural boundary condition of theorem 5.69, now at every instant, not only at rest: the force in the bar at \(x = L\) at time \(t\) equals the force \(F(t)\) acting on the end. We did not prescribe the condition. The action is sensitive to it only because the displacement at \(x = L\) is not prescribed, and so the principle chooses it by itself (figure 8.31). At \(x = 0\) we prescribe the displacement, so there is no natural condition.

Let us test it on the hanging bar of chapters 4 to 7. A steel bar, \(L = 10\) m, diameter 10 mm, \(A = 78{,}54\) mm², \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, axis \(x\) pointing down, fixed at \(x = 0\), lower end free, \(F = 0\). Its only load is its weight, \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m. The bar rests in the equilibrium position \(u_{\mathrm{s}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^{-1}\), and the lower end is displaced by \(u_{\mathrm{s}}(L) = a\,L^2 = 19{,}52\) µm. This position satisfies (8.97), since \(u_{\mathrm{tt}} = 0\) and \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{s}}'' + q = -2aEA + \rho g A = 0\), and (8.98), since \(u_{\mathrm{s}}'(L) = 2a\,(L - L) = 0 = F/EA\). The motion we study is a departure from this position.

Let us look at the first variation in the window \(t_1 = 0\), \(t_2 = 10\) ms, with the variation \(\eta = (x/L)\,\psi(t)\), where \(\psi(t) = 16\,t^2\,(t_2 - t)^2/t_2^4\) is a dimensionless function with maximum value \(1\) (in what follows \(\alpha\) is in metres). The end of the bar thus has the value \(\psi\) in \(\eta\), which is exactly the variation that in step 7 of the proof touches the dynamic condition. By formula (8.99), \(\delta\mathcal S(u_{\mathrm{s}}, \eta) = 0\) (SymPy: \(10^{-16}\) N s, which is rounding). Now try the field \(u_{\mathrm{s}} + c\,x\) with \(c = 10^{-6}\). It satisfies equation (8.97) (the term \(c\,x\) is linear in \(x\) and constant in time), but not the condition: \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = EA\,c = 15{,}39\) N instead of \(0\). The first variation is \(\delta\mathcal S = -EA\,c\int_0^{t_2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = -15{,}39\ \text{N}\cdot 5{,}333\ \text{ms} = -0{,}0821\) N s, which agrees with the SymPy computation. The field would be a motion only for a bar with a force \(F = 15{,}39\) N at the end. Without it, it is not stationary: the condition at the end is not an unnecessary addition but the content of the principle.

Now the second variation. For \(\eta = (x/L)\,\psi\) we have \(B_2 = \tfrac12\,\rho A\,\tfrac{L}{3}\int\dot\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}t - \tfrac12\,\tfrac{EA}{L}\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}t\). For our \(\psi\), \(\int\dot\psi^2/\int\psi^2 = 12/\tau^2\) on a window of length \(\tau = t_2 - t_1\), so \(B_2 > 0\) if and only if \(\tau < 2L\sqrt{\rho/E} = 3{,}990\) ms, which follows from \(\rho L^2/(3E) \cdot 12/\tau^2 > 1\). At \(\tau = 1\) ms \(B_2 = +4666\) N s/m, at \(\tau = 10\) ms \(B_2 = -2630\) N s/m. For this variation the action therefore increases in \(\alpha^2\) for a short window and decreases for a longer one. For the longer window the stationary position is thus not the smallest action, as for the pendulum. For the short window we tested only one variation. The frequencies of the bar and the solution of equation (8.97) belong to chapter 13.

Hamilton's principle thus gave, for the pendulum, for a system with kinetic energy and for the bar, the same structure: stationarity of the action means equations in the interior and natural conditions where the displacement is not prescribed. For a system of points this structure is theorem 8.102, for the bar theorem 8.103. Both times the action is stationary, not the smallest. The equations of motion obtained are the same as in section 8.9, so we look at what follows from them: section 8.11 finds out why the pendulum conserves energy and which numbers remain constant.
