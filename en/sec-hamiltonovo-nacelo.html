Chapter 8 Analytical dynamics (analitična dinamika)

8.10 Hamilton's principle (Hamiltonovo načelo)

In section 8.7 we obtained the equation of motion from equilibrium at each instant separately: at the instant \(t\) the work of the applied and inertial forces is zero for every virtual displacement that the constraints allow. In section 8.9 we obtained from this the equations for the coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\). In chapter 5 we saw something different: the equilibrium of a beam is a stationary point of a functional, that is, of one number that we assign to the whole deflection curve. Does motion also have such a number? We look for a rule that assigns one number to the whole motion from the instant \(t_1\) to the instant \(t_2\), such that the motion the system really performs is its stationary point. For the pendulum the time axis \(t\) plays the role that the axis \(x\) plays for the beam.

The answer is yes, and it is given by a principle (Hamiltonovo načelo) that we prove in this section. First we test it on the pendulum, then we prepare the mathematics it needs, and finally we prove it and apply it to a bar.

8.10.1 An experiment on the pendulum (poskus na nihalu)

Take pendulum A of section 8.9 and draw it (figure 8.181, left).

At the bearing \(O\) hangs a point mass \(m = 1\) kg on a rigid massless rod of length \(l = 1\) m. The position is measured by the angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, and the gravitational acceleration is \(g = 9{,}81\) m/s². The Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) of the pendulum is \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,\dot\theta^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\), that is, the kinetic energy minus the potential energy (theorem 8.101). We release it from rest at \(\theta = 90°\), a position we draw grey and dashed, and observe it for \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s, that is, from \(t_1 = 0\) to \(t_2 = \tau\). The equation of motion \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\) (RK4, as in section 8.9) gives at \(t_2\) the angle \(\theta = 0{,}4018\) rad, that is, \(23{,}0°\), and we draw this position orange. In this time the pendulum turns by \(67{,}0°\).

Let us also draw the same motion as a graph of the angle against time (figure 8.182, left).

The horizontal axis is the time from \(t_1 = 0\) to \(t_2 = \tau\), the vertical axis the angle \(\theta\) in radians. The blackwhite curve is the motion the pendulum really performs: it begins at \(1{,}5708\) rad, that is, \(90°\), and ends at \(0{,}4018\) rad. Now take other motions that begin and end in the same positions at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\): \(\theta(t) + \alpha\,\eta(t)\) with the function \(\eta(t) = 4\,t\,(\tau - t)/\tau^2\), which is zero at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) and has the value \(1\) in the middle of the interval, and with a number \(\alpha\) in radians. The blue curves are drawn for \(\alpha = \pm 0{,}2\) rad, so in the middle they are \(0{,}2\) rad away from the blackwhite one. These are not motions the pendulum really performs but paths that we only compare. What they mean for the pendulum itself is shown in the right part of figure 8.181: at the time \(t = \tau/2\) the pendulum on the true path is at \(72{,}5°\), and on the compared paths it would be \(\alpha = 0{,}2\) rad, that is, \(11{,}5°\), higher, at \(83{,}9°\), or lower, at \(61{,}0°\). To each path we assign the number

\[\mathcal S = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \mathcal L\,\mathrm{d}t ,\]

which has the unit J s. The true path gives \(\mathcal S = 3{,}5515\) J s. For the other paths (the integral by Simpson's rule with \(20\,000\) steps) the difference from this value is

\(\alpha\) \(-0{,}2\) \(-0{,}1\) \(0{,}1\) \(0{,}2\)
\(\mathcal S - 3{,}5515\) J s \(+0{,}1934\) \(+0{,}0487\) \(+0{,}0494\) \(+0{,}1989\)

In the right part of figure 8.182 these are the blackwhite dots, and the numbers written beside them are in units of \(10^{-2}\) J s: the difference \(0{,}1934\) J s is the dot labelled \(19{,}34\).

The difference is positive on both sides and almost equal for \(\alpha\) and \(-\alpha\). What this says about the derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) is shown in figure 8.183.

The difference has a quadratic term \(c\,\alpha^2\) with \(c = 4{,}902\) J s, which we compute below, and this term has a horizontal tangent at \(\alpha = 0\). If the difference also had a linear term \(b\,\alpha\), it would have the slope \(b\) at \(\alpha = 0\) and would be negative on one side, between \(0\) and \(-b/c\). This band is narrow: with \(b = 0{,}45\) J s/rad the differences at \(\alpha = \pm 0{,}1\) are still both positive, \(0{,}004\) and \(0{,}094\) J s. The positive differences of the table therefore only say that \(|b| < c \cdot 0{,}1 = 0{,}49\) J s/rad. We obtain the slope at \(\alpha = 0\) more precisely from the symmetric difference quotient \(\big(\mathcal S(\varepsilon) - \mathcal S(-\varepsilon)\big)/(2\varepsilon)\), in which the quadratic term cancels while the linear one remains. At \(\varepsilon = 10^{-3}\) rad it is \(3{,}5\cdot 10^{-7}\) J s/rad, at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-4}\) rad it is \(3{,}5\cdot 10^{-9}\) J s/rad. With a ten times smaller \(\varepsilon\) it is a hundred times smaller, as a term in \(\alpha^3\) gives it, while a linear term \(b\) would stay the same. The derivative of \(\mathcal S\) with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is therefore zero: the true path is a stationary point. Along this family of paths it is even the smallest, since all the differences are positive. The quadratic term \(\tfrac12\,\alpha^2\,m\,l^2\int_0^\tau\big(\dot\eta^2 - (g/l)\cos\theta\,\eta^2\big)\,\mathrm{d}t = 4{,}902\,\alpha^2\) J s (we define the second variation later in this chapter) equals \(0{,}0490\) J s at \(\alpha = \pm 0{,}1\), close to the computed \(0{,}0487\) and \(0{,}0494\) J s.

For a longer window the picture is different. If we observe the pendulum for \(\tau = 2\) s, it ends at \(t_2\) at \(\theta = 0{,}9166\) rad, \(\mathcal S = 19{,}690\) J s, and the differences for \(\alpha = -0{,}2\), \(-0{,}1\), \(0{,}1\), \(0{,}2\) are \(-0{,}0315\), \(-0{,}0089\), \(-0{,}0109\) and \(-0{,}0476\) J s, in the figure the blue dots with the labels \(-3{,}15\) and \(-4{,}76\). The derivative at \(\alpha = 0\) is again zero (the difference quotient at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-4}\) rad is \(-1{,}0\cdot 10^{-8}\) J s/rad, again from the term in \(\alpha^3\)), but the path is now not the smallest but larger than the compared ones. Stationarity remains, the minimum does not. Hence the principle will speak of a stationary point.

Why does the sign turn over? The quadratic term has two parts with opposite signs, and at \(\alpha = 1\) they are, in J s,

\[\underbrace{\tfrac12\,m\,l^2\int_0^\tau \dot\eta^2\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{velocity}} - \underbrace{\tfrac12\,m\,g\,l\int_0^\tau \cos\theta\,\eta^2\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{position}} = \begin{cases} 5{,}333 - 0{,}431 = 4{,}902 & \text{for } \tau = 0{,}5\ \text{s} , \\ 1{,}333 - 2{,}328 = -0{,}995 & \text{for } \tau = 2\ \text{s} . \end{cases}\]

The first part measures how much the compared path changes the velocity. The bump \(\eta\) always has the height \(1\), and stretched over a longer window it is flatter: at the start it has the slope \(4/\tau\), \(8\) 1/s for \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s and \(2\) 1/s for \(\tau = 2\) s. The slope is everywhere proportional to \(1/\tau\), while the window is \(\tau\) long, so \(\int_0^\tau\dot\eta^2\,\mathrm{d}t = 16/(3\tau)\) and this part falls with the length of the window, from \(5{,}333\) to \(1{,}333\). The second part measures how much the compared path changes the potential energy. Its integrand \(\cos\theta\,\eta^2\) is nowhere negative, because the pendulum released at \(90°\) never rises above the horizontal position. So the area under it accumulates during the whole window, and this part grows from \(0{,}431\) to \(2{,}328\). For the short window the first part wins, for the long one the second. The exact limit we show later in this chapter, in the section on buckling of a column, on a pendulum with small oscillations: there the second variation is positive as long as the window is shorter than half the oscillation period, and the same inequality decides buckling.

The experiment shows that the motion is a stationary point of the number \(\mathcal S\). But \(\mathcal S\) is one number for the whole path, while the equation of motion says what happens at each instant separately. How does the first determine the second? We see it when we simplify the path to a polygon (figure 8.185). This view adds a reason to the experiment: it shows where the acceleration and the force in stationarity come from.

We divide the window from \(t_1 = 0\) to \(t_2 = 0{,}5\) s into five slices of length \(h = 0{,}1\) s. Let the path be a polygon through the angles \(\theta_0, \dots, \theta_5\) at the borders of the slices, with the end points \(\theta_0 = 1{,}5708\) rad and \(\theta_5 = 0{,}4018\) rad as before. In each slice the angular velocity is constant, equal to the slope of the segment \((\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \theta_{\mathrm{k}})/h\). We replace the integral in \(\mathcal S\) by a sum over the slices. The kinetic energy is taken with the slope of the segment, the potential energy at the nodes (the trapezoidal rule):

\[\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}=0}^{4} h\,\tfrac12\,m\,l^2\Big(\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{k}+1} - \theta_{\mathrm{k}}}{h}\Big)^2 + h\,m\,g\,l\,\Big(\tfrac12\cos\theta_0 + \cos\theta_1 + \cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_3 + \cos\theta_4 + \tfrac12\cos\theta_5\Big) .\]

Now \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}}\) is an ordinary function of the four numbers \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_4\), and a stationary point is where all four partial derivatives are zero (section 4.6.6).

Let us move one single node, \(\theta_2\). Only the segments to its left and right change, that is, only two slices, all the others stay as they are. In \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}}\) the angle \(\theta_2\) therefore appears in three terms only: in the kinetic energy of two slices and in \(\cos\theta_2\). The derivative is

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}}}{\partial\theta_2} = m\,l^2\,\frac{\theta_2 - \theta_1}{h} - m\,l^2\,\frac{\theta_3 - \theta_2}{h} - h\,m\,g\,l\sin\theta_2 .\]

We set it equal to zero and divide by \(h\,m\,l^2\):

\[\frac{1}{h}\Big(\frac{\theta_3 - \theta_2}{h} - \frac{\theta_2 - \theta_1}{h}\Big) = -\frac{g}{l}\sin\theta_2 .\]

On the left is the change of the slope at the node divided by \(h\), that is, the difference quotient that approaches the angular acceleration as \(h \to 0\). It is not the angular acceleration itself, since inside a slice the polygon has no acceleration at all. On the right is the moment of the weight divided by \(m\,l^2\). Stationarity with respect to one node is thus the equation of motion at that instant, written with differences. This answers the question: a number for the whole path knows every instant, because moving the path at one instant changes only the slices beside it.

Let us check with numbers. We solve the four equations, for \(k = 1, \dots, 4\), by Newton's method (NumPy) and get the nodes of the blackwhite polygon:

\(\theta_1\) \(\theta_2\) \(\theta_3\) \(\theta_4\)
polygon (rad) \(1{,}5228\) \(1{,}3769\) \(1{,}1347\) \(0{,}8035\)
true motion at the same time (rad) \(1{,}5218\) \(1{,}3748\) \(1{,}1322\) \(0{,}8016\)

The difference is at most \(0{,}0025\) rad, and \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}} = 3{,}5496\) J s is close to \(\mathcal S = 3{,}5515\) J s. At the node \(\theta_2 = 1{,}3769\) rad the slope changes from \(-1{,}460\) to \(-2{,}422\) 1/s, that is, by \(-0{,}9626\) 1/s in the time \(h = 0{,}1\) s, so the difference quotient is \(-9{,}626\) 1/s². The right side is \(-(g/l)\sin 1{,}3769 = -9{,}81 \cdot 0{,}9813 = -9{,}626\) 1/s², the same. The true motion has at \(t = 0{,}2\) s the angular acceleration \(-(g/l)\sin 1{,}3748 = -9{,}622\) 1/s². The polygon misses it by \(0{,}004\) 1/s², because its nodes are up to \(0{,}0025\) rad away from the true motion.

Now move the node \(\theta_2\) up by \(0{,}2\) rad. The left segment becomes flatter, the right one steeper, and since the kinetic energy grows with the square of the slope, the steeper one prevails: the kinetic part of the sum grows by \(0{,}5925\) J s. The potential part decreases by \(0{,}1950\) J s. Altogether \(\mathcal S_{\mathrm{h}}\) grows by \(0{,}3975\) J s. When the node is moved down by \(0{,}2\) rad, it grows by \(0{,}3950\) J s. The change is almost the same on both sides, as in the table of the experiment: there is no linear term.

The polygon showed what stationarity means, on six points and for one pendulum. Does the same hold for smooth paths and for every system? For the answer we need the calculus of variations for several functions at once, since a system has \(n\) coordinates \(q_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\), and for functions that must satisfy constraints.

8.10.2 Mathematics: several functions and constraints (matematika: več funkcij in vezi)

In chapter 5 we treated one functional of one function \(v(x)\) with derivatives up to the second order (definition 5.67, theorem 5.70). Here we need three extensions. The first are functionals that depend on several functions of one variable \(t\) and contain only first derivatives. The second are constraints between the functions that must not be violated. The third is a function of two variables \((x, t)\), which the bar needs. The tools stay the same: differentiation under the integral, integration by parts and the fundamental lemma.

Definition 8.112 Functional of several functions (funkcional več funkcij)

Let \(t_1 < t_2\) be numbers, \(n\) a natural number and \(\vek a\), \(\vek b\) columns of \(n\) numbers. An admissible function (dopustna funkcija) is a column \(\vek q(t) = (q_1(t), \dots, q_{\mathrm{n}}(t))\) of functions that have a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) (one-sided at the end points) and satisfy \(\vek q(t_1) = \vek a\), \(\vek q(t_2) = \vek b\). Let \(F(t, \vek q, \vek p)\) be a function whose input is a number \(t\) and two columns \(\vek q\), \(\vek p\) of \(n\) numbers each and whose output is a number, and let \(F\) and all its partial derivatives \(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\), \(F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) be continuous on \([t_1, t_2] \times \mathbb R^n \times \mathbb R^n\). A functional (funkcional) on admissible functions is the rule

\[\mathcal S(\vek q) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} F\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t , \tag{8.123}\]

which assigns one real number to each admissible function. An admissible variation (dopustna variacija) is a column \(\vek\eta = (\eta_1, \dots, \eta_{\mathrm{n}})\) of functions with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\vek\eta(t_1) = \vek\eta(t_2) = \vek 0\). The first variation (prva variacija) of the functional at \(\vek q\) in the direction \(\vek\eta\) is

\[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\,\mathcal S(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta)\Big|_{\alpha = 0} ,\]

if the derivative exists. An admissible function \(\vek q\) is a stationary point (stacionarna točka) of the functional if \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\).

An extension of definitions 5.67 and 5.68 to \(n\) functions. The same as the directional derivative in section 4.6.6.

For two functions, \(n = 2\), all this is in figure 8.186.

An admissible function is two curves, \(q_1(t)\) above and \(q_2(t)\) below, with prescribed values at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\). An admissible variation moves the first by \(\alpha\,\eta_1\) and the second by \(\alpha\,\eta_2\), and neither at the end points. The function \(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta\) is therefore admissible for every \(\alpha\): it has a continuous derivative and the same values at the end points as \(\vek q\). The prescribed end points are an assumption that the proof below uses in step 3, where the boundary term drops out because of it. The letter \(F\) here is the integrand as in chapter 5 and not a force. For the pendulum \(n = 1\), \(q_1 = \theta\) and \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,p^2 + m\,g\,l\cos q\).

Theorem 8.113 Euler-Lagrange equations for a system (Euler-Lagrangeove enačbe za sistem)

Let \(\mathcal S\) be the functional (8.123) of definition 8.112 and \(\vek q\) an admissible function for which, for every \(s = 1, \dots, n\), the function

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}(t) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\]

is continuously differentiable on \([t_1, t_2]\). Then \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the functional \(\mathcal S\) if and only if for every \(s = 1, \dots, n\)

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0 \quad \text{on } [t_1, t_2] , \tag{8.124}\]

where \(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}(t) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\). For every admissible variation

\[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t , \qquad E_{\mathrm{s}} = \hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} . \tag{8.125}\]
Proof

We must show that the first variation is the integral of the products \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\). Then we choose, one after another, variations that change only one function and use the fundamental lemma. Steps 1 to 3 are steps 1 to 4 of the proof of theorem 5.70 with several coordinates and without second derivatives.

  1. Let \(\vek\eta\) be an admissible variation and \(c < d\) arbitrary numbers. For \(t \in [t_1, t_2]\) and real \(\alpha\) let \(f(t, \alpha) = F\big(t, \vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta}\big)\), where \(\vek q\), \(\vek\eta\) and the derivatives are taken at \(t\). For constant \(t\) this is a composite function: the map \(\alpha \mapsto (\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta})\) with derivative \((\vek\eta, \dot{\vek\eta})\), then \(F\) as a function of \(2n\) variables with continuous partial derivatives. By the chain rule (lemma 8.30, item 1)
    \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial\alpha}(t, \alpha) = \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n}\big(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} + F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big) ,\]
    where the derivatives of \(F\) are taken at the point \(\big(t, \vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta, \dot{\vek q} + \alpha\,\dot{\vek\eta}\big)\). The functions \(f\) and \(\partial f/\partial\alpha\) are continuous on \([t_1, t_2] \times [c, d]\) as composite functions of continuous functions (lemma 8.26 and corollary 1.65).
  2. By lemma 4.19, point 1, the function \(\alpha \mapsto \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} f(t, \alpha)\,\mathrm{d}t = \mathcal S(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta)\) is differentiable on \((c, d)\) with a derivative that is the integral of the derivative. The numbers \(c < d\) are arbitrary, so this holds for every \(\alpha\), and at \(\alpha = 0\)
    \[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n}\big(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} + \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
  3. For every \(s\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) (by assumption) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\) are continuously differentiable. Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\dot\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \big[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}{}'\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\). The terms in the square bracket are zero, because \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}}(t_1) = \eta_{\mathrm{s}}(t_2) = 0\): here we use the prescribed end points. The first variation is therefore the integral (8.125).
  4. If (8.124) holds for all \(s\), every \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) and by step 3 \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation. The function \(\vek q\) is a stationary point.
  5. Conversely, let \(\vek q\) be a stationary point. Choose \(s\) and a function \(\psi\) with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\). The column \(\vek\eta\) with \(\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = \psi\) and the other components \(0\) is an admissible variation, so by step 3 \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). The function \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous: \(\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}}\) is a composite function of continuous functions (lemma 8.26) and \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}{}'\) is continuous by assumption. Since \(\psi\) is arbitrary, by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) on \([t_1, t_2]\). This holds for every \(s\).

Source: SHABANA (3.179) to (3.185) (PDF p. 148) and EP (5.2.12), (5.2.13) (p. 245) for the equations. The sources integrate by parts and then use the fundamental lemma, without continuity. Differentiation under the integral is lemma 4.19 (LEBL2 theorem 9.1.1, PDF pp. 65 to 66, as in chapter 5), the fundamental lemma is lemma 4.77. The proof in steps is not from the source.

We proved the theorem by varying one function at a time. Since all variations are free except at the end points, stationarity demands \(n\) equations, one for each function. For the pendulum, \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,l^2\,p^2 + m\,g\,l\cos q\), we have \(F_{\mathrm{q}} = -m\,g\,l\sin q\) and \(F_{\mathrm{p}} = m\,l^2\,p\), so equation (8.124) becomes \(-m\,g\,l\sin\theta - m\,l^2\,\ddot\theta = 0\), that is, \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\). The hypothesis of the theorem that \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = m\,l^2\,\dot\theta\) is continuously differentiable here demands a continuous angular acceleration \(\ddot\theta\). The true path has it, since on it \(\ddot\theta = -(g/l)\sin\theta\) is a continuous function of time. For a path with a continuous angular velocity whose acceleration jumps somewhere the hypothesis fails: the integration by parts in step 3 is not available for it and the theorem says nothing about it. The motion in the experiment at the beginning of the section gave the same equation, so the numerical result there, the derivative zero, is exactly this.

What if the functions are not independent of each other? Take the same pendulum in Cartesian coordinates \((x, y)\) (figure 8.187).

The origin is at the bearing \(O\), the axis \(y\) points up. The mass is on a rod of length \(l\), so at every instant it is on the circle \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - l^2 = 0\). Only paths that stay on it are admissible. What does stationarity demand if we cannot vary \(x\) and \(y\) independently?

For constraints \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q) = 0\) the displacement \(\vek q + \alpha\,\vek\eta\) does not preserve the constraints unless they are linear. From a position \(\vek q\) on the circle a displacement straight along the tangent takes us off the circle. For \(l = 1\) m and a displacement of length \(0{,}1\) m the new point is \(\sqrt{1^2 + 0{,}1^2} = 1{,}005\) m from the bearing, so it is \(5\) mm outside.

On the horizontal axis on the left is the length of the tangent displacement \(\alpha\,|\vek\eta|\), on the vertical axis the gap, for \(l = 1\) m. The curve is the gap \(\sqrt{l^2 + \alpha^2|\vek\eta|^2} - l\), and at a displacement of \(0{,}1\) m it is \(5\) mm, as computed above. At the origin the curve touches the horizontal axis: for small displacements the gap is roughly \((\alpha\,|\vek\eta|)^2/(2\,l)\) and grows with the square of the displacement. At \(0{,}1\) m the approximation gives \(5{,}000\) mm, the exact value is \(4{,}988\) mm. The derivative of the gap with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is the limit of the quotient \(\big(\sqrt{l^2 + \alpha^2|\vek\eta|^2} - l\big)/\alpha\) as \(\alpha\) goes to zero. The quotient is \(|\vek\eta|\) times the slope of the secant from the origin to the point of the curve. On the right are the slopes of the three secants: \(0{,}099\) at a displacement of \(0{,}2\) m, \(0{,}050\) at \(0{,}1\) m and \(0{,}025\) at \(0{,}05\) m. At half the displacement the slope is nearly half, so it goes to zero. The gap therefore contributes nothing to the derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\), and this is the reason why tangent displacements will suffice below.

Imagine a curve \(\vek q_\alpha(t)\) that satisfies the constraints for every \(\alpha\) and equals \(\vek q\) at \(\alpha = 0\). Its derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\) is a tangent displacement \(\vek\eta\), and from \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q_\alpha(t)) = 0\) it follows that \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} \frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\), which we write as \(\mat A(t)\,\vek\eta(t) = \vek 0\) with the matrix \(A_{\mathrm{rs}} = \partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 8.48). A row of the matrix \(\mat A\) is the gradient of a constraint, for the circle \((2x,\ 2y)\), which points outward from the bearing, and \(\mat A\,\vek\eta = \vek 0\) says that \(\vek\eta\) is perpendicular to it, so it lies on the tangent. Exactly such displacements we called virtual displacements in section 8.4, so we define stationarity under constraints with them. That for every such \(\vek\eta\) a curve satisfying the constraints really exists could be shown with the implicit function theorem (theorem 8.35) and we do not need it here.

The proof of the theorem on constraints will need the matrix \((\mat A\mat A^\T)^{-1}\) to be a continuously differentiable function of time if the entries of \(\mat A\) are continuously differentiable. The book has the formula for the inverse by cofactors only up to order \(3\) (lemma 4.49, theorem 2.76), and there may be more constraints. The matrix \(\mat A\mat A^\T\) is positive definite (definition 6.27), and a positive definite system is solved by elimination with positive pivots (theorem 6.29). Each step of elimination is an arithmetic operation in which we divide only by a positive pivot. The solution is therefore a continuously differentiable function of the entries, which we write as a lemma for any order. One step of elimination is shown in figure 8.189.

The matrix \(\mat K(t)\) of order \(m\) is split into the pivot \(K_{11}\), the rest of the first row \(\vek b^\T\), the rest of the first column \(\vek b\) and the block \(\mat K'\) of order \(m - 1\), and the right side into \(d_1\) and the rest \(\vek d'\). The step of elimination divides only by \(K_{11}\) and gives the smaller matrix \(\mat K''\) with the right side \(\vek d''\). The solution \(\vek c''\) of the smaller system then gives the first unknown \(c_1\), again with a division by \(K_{11}\). The whole way from the entries of the matrix to the solution thus consists of sums, products and divisions by a positive number. For the pendulum with one constraint the order is \(m = 1\) and \(\mat K = \mat A\mat A^\T = 4x^2 + 4y^2 = 4\,l^2\), a single positive number.

Lemma 8.114 Smooth solution of a system with a positive definite matrix (gladka rešitev sistema s pozitivno definitno matriko)

Let \(I\) be an interval, \(m\) a natural number, \(\mat K(t)\) a matrix of order \(m\) and \(\vek d(t)\) a column of \(m\) entries whose entries are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\) on \(I\) (one-sided at an end point). Let \(\mat K(t)\) be positive definite for every \(t \in I\). Then the system \(\mat K(t)\,\vek c = \vek d(t)\) has exactly one solution \(\vek c(t)\) for every \(t\), the entries of the column \(\vek c(t)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\), the matrix \(\mat K(t)\) is invertible and the entries of the matrix \(\mat K(t)^{-1}\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\).

Proof

We must show that each step of elimination from the proof of theorem 6.29 turns continuously differentiable functions into such functions again. The statement \(P(m)\) reads: the lemma holds for order \(m\). We prove it by induction (theorem 1.45). The entries that are denoted \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(f_{\mathrm{i}}\) there are denoted \(K_{\mathrm{ij}}(t)\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) and \(d_{\mathrm{i}}(t)\) here.

  1. Rules. A sum, a product and a quotient \(f/g\) (\(g \neq 0\)) of continuously differentiable functions are continuously differentiable, and the derivative of the quotient (4.80) is continuous (lemmas 1.82, 4.109, corollary 1.65). At an end point the derivatives are one-sided.
  2. Basis, \(m = 1\). The matrix is the number \(K_{11}(t)\), which is positive because \(c\,K_{11}\,c > 0\) for \(c = 1\). The solution \(c(t) = d_1(t)/K_{11}(t)\) is by step 1 continuously differentiable, unique, and so is \(K_{11}^{-1} = 1/K_{11}\).
  3. Induction step. Let \(P(m - 1)\) hold and let \(m \ge 2\). We write \(\mat K = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} K_{11} & \vek b^\T \\ \vek b & \mat K' \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) as in step 2 of the proof of theorem 6.29. By its step 3, \(K_{11}(t) = \vek e_1^\T\mat K(t)\,\vek e_1 > 0\) for every \(t\).
  4. The first step of elimination (step 4 of that proof) gives the matrix \(\mat K'' = \mat K' - \vek b\,\vek b^\T/K_{11}\) with entries \(K_{\mathrm{ij}} - K_{\mathrm{i}1}K_{1\mathrm{j}}/K_{11}\) and the right side with entries \(d_{\mathrm{i}} - K_{\mathrm{i}1}\,d_1/K_{11}\) for \(i, j = 2, \dots, m\). By step 1 and because \(K_{11} > 0\), all these entries are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\).
  5. By steps 5 and 6 of that proof, \(\mat K''(t)\) is symmetric and positive definite of order \(m - 1\) for every \(t\). By \(P(m - 1)\) the system \(\mat K''\,\vek c'' = \vek d''\) has for every \(t\) one solution \(\vek c''(t)\) and its entries are continuously differentiable.
  6. By step 7 of that proof, \(c_1 = (d_1 - \vek b^\T\vek c'')/K_{11}\) and \((c_1, \vek c'')\) is a solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek c = \vek d\). By step 1, \(c_1\) is continuously differentiable, because \(d_1\), \(b_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(c''_{\mathrm{i}}\) are continuously differentiable and \(K_{11} > 0\). Uniqueness is by theorem 6.29. The first and second claims of the lemma are proved for order \(m\).
  7. By theorem 6.29 elimination has all \(m\) pivots for every \(t\), so by lemma 8.22 the matrix \(\mat K(t)\) is invertible. Column \(j\) of \(\mat K^{-1}\) is the solution of the system \(\mat K\,\vek x = \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\) with a constant right side, so by step 6 it is continuously differentiable. This proves \(P(m)\).

Source: The proof is not from a source. The steps of elimination are from the proof of theorem 6.29 (KREYSZIG §7.3, PDF pp. 300 to 302), the derivatives from lemmas 1.82 and 4.109.

Theorem 8.115 Euler-Lagrange equations with constraints (Euler-Lagrangeove enačbe z vezmi)

Let \(\mathcal S\) be the functional (8.123) and \(f_1, \dots, f_{\mathrm{m}}\) functions that have continuous partial derivatives up to the second order on \([t_1, t_2] \times \mathbb R^n\), with \(m < n\). Let \(\vek q\) be an admissible function with \(f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q(t)) = 0\) for every \(r\) and every \(t\), for which the \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\) are continuously differentiable. Let the matrix \(\mat A(t)\) with entries \(A_{\mathrm{rs}}(t) = \frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}}\big(t, \vek q(t)\big)\) have \(m\) pivots for every \(t\) (as in theorem 8.109). We say that \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints (stacionarna točka pod vezmi) if \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\) for which \(\mat A(t)\,\vek\eta(t) = \vek 0\) for every \(t\). Then \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints if and only if there exist continuous functions \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) on \([t_1, t_2]\) that together with \(\vek q\) satisfy for every \(s\) the equations

\[\hat F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{m} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0 , \qquad f_{\mathrm{r}}(t, \vek q) = 0 . \tag{8.126}\]

The first are the equations of the Euler-Lagrange system (8.124) for the function \(F + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The functions \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are unique.

Proof

We must show both directions. One is a computation with the first variation. In the other, a projection onto tangent displacements gives variations that are continuously differentiable, and from the fundamental lemma we conclude that the column \(\vek E\) of theorem 8.113 is at every instant a linear combination of the rows of the matrix \(\mat A\), for the pendulum along the gradient.

  1. Let \(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) be as in theorem 8.113. Step 3 of its proof holds for every admissible variation, so \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \vek E\cdot\vek\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\) for the column \(\vek E = (E_1, \dots, E_{\mathrm{n}})\). Equation (8.126) says \(\vek E = -\mat A^\T\vek\lambda\) for the column \(\vek\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{\mathrm{m}})\), since the derivative of the function \(F + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} f_{\mathrm{r}}\) with respect to \(q_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(F_{\mathrm{q}_\mathrm{s}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\partial f_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\) and its derivative with respect to \(p_{\mathrm{s}}\) is \(F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\), because \(f_{\mathrm{r}}\) does not contain \(\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. Let (8.126) hold and let \(\mat A\,\vek\eta = \vek 0\). Then \(\vek E\cdot\vek\eta = -(\mat A^\T\vek\lambda)\cdot\vek\eta = -\vek\lambda\cdot(\mat A\,\vek\eta) = 0\) at every instant, because a column along the gradient is perpendicular to every tangent displacement, so \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = 0\). The function \(\vek q\) is stationary under the constraints.
  3. Conversely, let \(\vek q\) be stationary under the constraints. Let \(\mat K = \mat A\mat A^\T\), a matrix of order \(m\) for every \(t\). It is symmetric, because \((\mat A\mat A^\T)^\T = \mat A\mat A^\T\) (lemma 1.102). It is also positive definite: for \(\vek z \neq \vek 0\) we have \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z = (\mat A^\T\vek z)\cdot(\mat A^\T\vek z) = |\mat A^\T\vek z|^2 \ge 0\), and if \(\mat A^\T\vek z = \vek 0\) held, uniqueness in lemma 8.36 (for the column \(\vek a = \vek 0\), the matrix \(\mat A\) has \(m\) pivots) would give \(\vek z = \vek 0\). Hence \(\vek z^\T\mat K\vek z > 0\). For the pendulum \(\mat K\) is the square of the length of the gradient, \(4\,l^2 > 0\).
  4. The entries \(A_{\mathrm{rs}}(t)\) are continuously differentiable (lemma 8.30, item 1), the entries of \(\mat K\) are sums of their products (lemma 1.82), so by lemma 8.114 \(\mat K\) is invertible and \(\mat K^{-1}\) is continuously differentiable.
  5. Let \(\mat P = \mat I - \mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A\), a matrix of size \(n \times n\) for every \(t\). We have \(\mat A\mat P = \mat A - \mat A\mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A = \mat A - \mat K\mat K^{-1}\mat A = \mat 0\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric, so \(\mat K^{-1}\) is symmetric as well (lemma 1.123) and \(\mat P^\T = \mat P\) (lemma 1.102). The entries of the matrix \(\mat P\) are sums of products of continuously differentiable functions, so \(\mat P\) is continuously differentiable. The matrix \(\mat P\) is the projection onto tangent displacements: from the column \(\vek e_1\) it cuts off the part along the gradient and leaves the part on the tangent, \(\mat P\,\vek e_1\). For the pendulum \(\mat P = \mat I - \vek n\,\vek n^\T\) with the unit column \(\vek n = (x, y)/l\).
  1. Choose \(s\) and a function \(\psi\) with a continuous derivative and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\). The column \(\vek\eta = \psi\,\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\) has by step 5 a continuous derivative, is zero at the end points, and \(\mat A\,\vek\eta = \psi\,\mat A\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek 0\). Figure 8.190 shows it for the pendulum of the experiment, \(s = 1\) and the bump \(\psi = 4\,t\,(\tau - t)/\tau^2\) with \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s. The mass moves along the circle from \(90°\) at \(t_1 = 0\) to \(23{,}0°\) at \(t_2 = 0{,}5\) s. At every position \(\vek\eta\) lies on the tangent, since \(\mat P\,\vek e_1 = \cos\theta\,(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\), and the column \((\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\) is a unit column tangent to the circle. The length \(|\vek\eta| = \psi\cos\theta\) is the product of the bump \(\psi\) and the factor \(\cos\theta\) and changes continuously with time. At \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) it is zero, since \(\psi = 0\) there. The column \(\vek\eta\) is therefore an admissible tangent variation, and by step 1 \(0 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \psi\,\vek E\cdot\mat P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \psi\,(\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\), since \(\mat P\) is symmetric.
  2. The function \((\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous, because \(\mat P\) and \(\vek E\) are continuous (\(E_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuous as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 8.113). By the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) \((\mat P\vek E)_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) on \([t_1, t_2]\) for every \(s\), so \(\mat P\vek E = \vek 0\), that is, \(\vek E = \mat A^\T\mat K^{-1}\mat A\vek E\): the column \(\vek E\) has no part on the tangent.
  3. Let \(\vek\lambda = -\mat K^{-1}\mat A\vek E\). It is continuous as a product of continuous functions, and by step 7 \(\vek E = -\mat A^\T\vek\lambda\), which is (8.126). If \(\vek\lambda'\) were another solution, we would have \(\mat A^\T(\vek\lambda - \vek\lambda') = \vek 0\), so by uniqueness in lemma 8.36 \(\vek\lambda = \vek\lambda'\).

Source: GR (1.77) to (1.82) (PDF pp. 62 to 63), EP (5.4.7), (5.4.11) (pp. 256, 257): the books choose the multipliers so that the coefficients of the dependent variations vanish, and state the ansatz without a proof of continuity. The proof by projection is not from a source. The algebraic statement that the column \(\vek E\) is orthogonal to all \(\vek\eta\) with \(\mat A\vek\eta = \vek 0\) if and only if \(\vek E = \mat A^\T\vek\lambda\) is lemma 8.36 at each instant separately. The projection only ensures that \(\vek\eta\) is continuously differentiable.

The sign of the multipliers is a convention: with \(F - \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} f_{\mathrm{r}}\) we would get \(-\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). We chose \(+\) so that three sections agree. In section 8.3, \(\nabla V = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\) holds (equation (8.23)). In theorem 8.109, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} - \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} + \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} A_{\mathrm{rs}}\). For \(F = \mathcal L\) the left side is \(-E_{\mathrm{s}}\), so this is equation (8.126) with \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\). At rest \(\mathcal L = -V\), so \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = -\partial V/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}\), and the same equation gives \(\nabla V = \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}}\). The multiplier \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is thus everywhere the intensity of the reaction of constraint \(r\).

The assumption of \(m\) pivots of the matrix \(\mat A\) enters in step 3 of the proof, where it makes \(\mat K = \mat A\mat A^\T\) positive definite. It says that the constraints are independent of each other: no gradient is a combination of the others.

When does the assumption fail? Let us write the constraint of the pendulum twice, \(f_1 = f_2 = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\), and look at the pendulum at the bottom. The gradients of the two constraints are equal, so the matrix \(\mat A\) has two equal rows \((2x,\ 2y)\) and a single pivot, and \(\mat K\) has all four entries equal to \(4\,l^2\) and determinant zero. The motion stays the same, since both constraints demand the same, but the multipliers are no longer unique. The force of constraint \(r\) on the mass is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\nabla f_{\mathrm{r}} = 2\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,(x, y)\), as the comparison with theorem 8.109 above says, and since the gradients are equal, only the sum \(2\,(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\,(x, y)\) acts on the mass. Equations (8.126) therefore determine only the sum \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2\). For the pendulum of the experiment, \(m = 1\) kg and \(l = 1\) m, released from rest at \(90°\), the force of the rod at the bottom is \(29{,}43\) N, three times the weight (section 8.9). The constraints can share it half and half, \(14{,}715\) N each, the first can carry all of it, or the first pulls with \(44{,}145\) N and the second pushes with \(14{,}715\) N. The sum is \(29{,}43\) N every time, and the equations choose none of the three ways.

Let us test it on the pendulum with \(F = \tfrac12\,m\,(p_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + p_{\mathrm{y}}^2) - m\,g\,y\) (the kinetic energy minus the potential energy) and \(f = x^2 + y^2 - l^2\). The matrix \(\mat A = (2x,\ 2y)\) has one pivot, since the row is not zero: \(x^2 + y^2 = l^2 > 0\). Equations (8.126) are

\[-m\,\ddot x + 2\lambda\,x = 0 , \qquad -m\,g - m\,\ddot y + 2\lambda\,y = 0 .\]

Equations of this form, written in dependent coordinates, with multipliers and together with the equations of the constraints, are called Lagrange's equations of the first kind (Lagrangeeve enačbe prve vrste). The equations in independent generalized coordinates of theorem 8.99 are Lagrange's equations of the second kind (druge vrste). We obtain the multiplier without solving the motion. The derivative of the constraint \(x\dot x + y\dot y = 0\) differentiated once more gives \(x\ddot x + y\ddot y = -(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)\). We multiply the first equation by \(x\), the second by \(y\) and add: \(m\,(x\ddot x + y\ddot y) = 2\lambda\,(x^2 + y^2) - m\,g\,y\). With \(x = l\sin\theta\), \(y = -l\cos\theta\) and \(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = l^2\dot\theta^2\) this is \(-m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 = 2\lambda\,l^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\), so

\[\lambda = -\frac{m}{2\,l}\,\big(l\,\dot\theta^2 + g\cos\theta\big) = -\frac{N}{2\,l} , \qquad N = m\,\big(g\cos\theta + l\,\dot\theta^2\big) .\]

The reaction of the constraint on the mass is \(2\lambda\,(x, y)\), a vector along the gradient of magnitude \(2|\lambda|\,l = N\) that points towards the bearing when \(\lambda < 0\). \(N\) is thus the force in the rod, the same as in section 8.9. We release the pendulum of the experiment of section 8.10.1, \(m = 1\) kg and \(l = 1\) m, from rest at \(90°\). At the start \(\dot\theta = 0\), \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(\lambda = 0\). At the bottom \(\dot\theta = \sqrt{2\,g/l} = 4{,}4294\) 1/s, given by the constant \(\tfrac12\,\dot\theta^2 - (g/l)\cos\theta\) as in section 8.9, the velocity of the mass is \(l\,\dot\theta = 4{,}43\) m/s, so \(N = 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\big(9{,}81\ \text{m/s}^2 + 1\ \text{m}\cdot 19{,}62\ \text{1/s}^2\big) = 29{,}43\) N and \(\lambda = -14{,}715\) N/m. This is three times the weight \(9{,}81\) N: one weight holds the mass, two turn it into the circle, because here \(l\,\dot\theta^2 = 19{,}62\) m/s² \(= 2\,g\). The rod pulls, as it must.

For the bar we also need the fundamental lemma for functions of two variables \((x, t)\) on a rectangle. In section 4.2.2 we proved it as point 3 of lemma 4.83, for a rectangle in a plane of space. The name of the second variable does not matter in the statement, so we use it with \(t\) in place of \(y\) and need no new lemma.

8.10.3 The principle (načelo)

Now we have the tools for the answer. Let us write the number from the experiment as a definition. Let the system be described by \(n\) generalized coordinates \(\vek q\) (section 8.4), with kinetic energy \(T(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q})\) (definition 8.88) and potential energy \(V(\vek q)\) from which the applied forces arise (definition 8.58).

Definition 8.116 Action integral (akcija)

Let \(\mathcal L(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q}) = T - V\) be the Lagrangian (Lagrangeeva funkcija) of the system. The action (akcija) of the system on a path \(\vek q\), which is an admissible function of definition 8.112 on \([t_1, t_2]\), is the number

\[\mathcal S(\vek q) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \mathcal L\big(t, \vek q(t), \dot{\vek q}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t .\]

Source: GR (1.27) (PDF p. 42), EP (5.2.10) (p. 245). For \(F = \mathcal L\) this is the functional (8.123).

What is admissible? For \(F = \mathcal L\) we need \(\mathcal L\) and its partial derivatives to be continuous, that is, \(T\) and \(V\) must have continuous partial derivatives with respect to \(t\), \(\vek q\) and \(\dot{\vek q}\). The kinetic energy is a polynomial of second degree in the velocities, \(T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vek q}^\T\mat M(t, \vek q)\,\dot{\vek q} + \vek b(t, \vek q)\cdot\dot{\vek q} + T_0\) (theorem 8.97), so this is true when \(\mat M\), \(\vek b\), \(T_0\) and \(V\) are continuously differentiable in \(t\) and \(\vek q\).

Take the pendulum A with damping. The mass moves on a circular arc, the velocity points along the tangent, and the damping force \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\) points the opposite way. The variation \(\vek\eta\) moves the mass along the tangent, and the work of the force on this displacement is \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\eta\). This force does not come from a potential \(V\), so this work does not enter the action and stands separately in the theorem.

Theorem 8.117 Hamilton's principle (Hamiltonovo načelo)

Let a system have generalized coordinates \(\vek q\), ideal holonomic constraints solved in \(\vek q\) (definition 8.3), and kinetic energy and potential energy with continuous partial derivatives, as above. Let \(\vek q\) be a twice continuously differentiable function on \([t_1, t_2]\).

  1. If all applied forces are conservative, \(\vek q\) is a motion of the system, that is, it satisfies d'Alembert's principle (corollary 8.80), if and only if \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the action \(\mathcal S\) among paths with the same end points \(\vek q(t_1)\), \(\vek q(t_2)\).
  2. If in addition to the conservative forces there are non-conservative ones with generalized forces \(Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}(t, \vek q, \dot{\vek q})\), continuous functions, \(\vek q\) is a motion if and only if for every admissible variation \(\vek\eta\)
    \[\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}=1}^{n} Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t = 0 . \tag{8.127}\]
Proof

We must show that the Euler-Lagrange equations of the action are exactly the Lagrange equations of section 8.9. Then the principle is another way of writing the same statement.

  1. For a twice continuously differentiable \(\vek q\), \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}} = \partial T/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = \sum_{\mathrm{k}} M_{\mathrm{sk}}\,\dot q_{\mathrm{k}} + b_{\mathrm{s}}\) is continuously differentiable (lemma 8.30, item 1). \(F = \mathcal L\) has continuous partial derivatives, so theorem 8.113 applies.
  2. By theorem 8.113, \(\vek q\) is a stationary point of the action if and only if \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for every \(s\), that is,
    \[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_{\mathrm{s}}} = 0 .\]
  3. These are the Lagrange equations of a conservative system without non-conservative forces (theorem 8.101), which are by theorem 8.99 equivalent to d'Alembert's principle, that is, to equilibrium at each instant separately, as we obtained it at one node of the polygon. This proves point 1.
  4. For point 2 take an admissible variation \(\vek\eta\). By equation (8.125), \(\delta\mathcal S(\vek q, \vek\eta) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}} E_{\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) with \(E_{\mathrm{s}} = \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}}\), so the left side of equation (8.127) equals \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \sum_{\mathrm{s}} (E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}})\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  5. If \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\) for all \(s\), equation (8.127) holds for every \(\vek\eta\). If it holds for every \(\vek\eta\), we choose, as in step 5 of the proof of theorem 8.113, a variation that changes only one component, and from the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77, the function \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\) is continuous) we get \(E_{\mathrm{s}} + Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\), that is, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_{\mathrm{s}} - \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_{\mathrm{s}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\). These are the Lagrange equations with non-conservative forces (theorem 8.101).

Source: GR (1.27) (PDF p. 42, read), EP (5.2.8) (p. 244) for point 1, SHABANA (3.188) (PDF p. 149) for point 2. The proof is not from a source, the sources use the steps of integration by parts directly.

The idea of the theorem is that d'Alembert's principle at each instant, integrated over time, equals stationarity. Integration by parts in time moves the derivative from the inertial force \(m\,\ddot{\vek x}\) to the virtual displacement and turns the work of the inertial forces into a variation of the kinetic energy. This is step 3 of the proof of theorem 8.113. On the polygon the same step is seen without computation: the angle \(\theta_2\) appears in the slopes of two neighbouring segments, and the derivative with respect to it gives the difference of the slopes, which divided by the step \(h\) is a difference quotient that approaches the acceleration as \(h \to 0\). GR (PDF p. 41) therefore says that the principle is only the time-integrated form of the principle of virtual work.

Hamilton's principle thus does not give us a new equation of motion but the same one, in another way. The advantage is that the action is one number, independent of the coordinates: in whatever generalized coordinates we write it, we get equations for the same motion. For pendulum A the action is \(\mathcal S = \int \big(\tfrac12\,m\,l^2\dot\theta^2 + m\,g\,l\cos\theta\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\) and theorem 8.113 gave \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\). The experiment from the beginning of the section is therefore a test of point 1: the derivative of the action with respect to \(\alpha\) was zero on the true path. With a non-conservative force, for example angular damping with the moment \(-c\,\dot\theta\), point 2 gives \(m\,l^2\ddot\theta + c\,\dot\theta + m\,g\,l\sin\theta = 0\), because \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\,\dot\theta\).

The theorem says that the motion is a stationary point, not that it is the smallest value. The experiment showed this: at \(\tau = 0{,}5\) s the action on the true path is smaller than on the compared paths, at \(\tau = 2\) s larger. The exact condition is shown by the second variation, which we will estimate later in this chapter in the section on buckling of a column. In the literature the principle also has the name principle of least action (načelo najmanjše akcije): EP (§1.1, p. 3) states that a principle with this name was published by Euler in 1744, and SPONG (§6.1, PDF p. 188) writes "Hamilton's principle of least action". The name is historical and we read it as the principle of stationary action: the motion is a stationary point of the action, and the action is the smallest only for a short enough window.

The theorem has three assumptions. For each let us say where it enters, why we may assume it, when it fails and what it does to the result.

  1. The end points are prescribed: the compared paths start and end in the same positions as the true one. It enters in step 3 of the proof of theorem 8.113, where the boundary term \(\big[\hat F_{\mathrm{p}_\mathrm{s}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\) drops out because of it. Without it the true path would not be stationary. For the pendulum \(\hat F_{\mathrm{p}} = m\,l^2\,\dot\theta\), at \(t_2 = 0{,}5\) s equal to \(1\ \text{kg m}^2 \cdot (-4{,}249\ \text{1/s}) = -4{,}249\) J s. Take the path \(\theta + \alpha\,t/\tau\), which starts in the same position as the true one but ends higher by \(\alpha\). The boundary term now does not drop out, so its derivative of the action with respect to \(\alpha\) is \(-4{,}249\) J s and not zero: at \(\alpha = 0{,}1\) rad the action changes by \(-0{,}420\) J s, at \(\alpha = -0{,}1\) rad by \(+0{,}430\) J s (NumPy). This is \(8{,}5\) and \(8{,}8\) times more than for the bump \(\eta\) of the table of the experiment. For the bump the change is positive for both signs of \(\alpha\), the curve has its bottom at \(\alpha = 0\), here it changes sign with \(\alpha\) like a line through the origin. The assumption does not describe how we usually look for a motion, since then we know the initial position and the initial velocity, but not the final position. The principle is therefore not a recipe for computing a motion from initial conditions, but a source of the equations of motion and a test of whether a given path is a motion.
  2. In point 1 all applied forces have a potential \(V\). It enters already in definition 8.116: without \(V\) there is no function \(\mathcal L\) whose integral would be the action. It fails for damping and friction. Then point 2 remains, where the work of the non-conservative forces stands separately as the integral \(\int \sum_{\mathrm{s}} Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{d}t\) and the statement is no longer the stationarity of one number. How much this is, is shown by pendulum A with angular damping \(c = 0{,}1\) N m s, released as in the experiment, with the paths \(\theta + \alpha\,\eta\) around its true motion and the bump \(\eta\) of the experiment (the right graph). Without damping the curve has a horizontal tangent at \(\alpha = 0\). With damping the tangent is tilted: the derivative of the action with respect to \(\alpha\) is \(-0{,}078\) J s/rad and not zero (NumPy), so the difference at \(\alpha = 0{,}1\) rad is only \(4{,}16\cdot 10^{-2}\) J s, but at \(\alpha = -0{,}1\) rad it is \(5{,}65\cdot 10^{-2}\) J s. The integral of the work of the damping force \(\int_0^\tau Q^{\mathrm{nc}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\) with \(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = -c\,\dot\theta\) is \(+0{,}078\) J s/rad on this motion and exactly balances the derivative, so equation (8.127) holds (sum \(10^{-14}\), NumPy).
  3. The constraints are ideal and holonomic and solved in the coordinates \(\vek q\). It enters in step 3 of the proof, where we use theorem 8.99, which demands it. Because of it the variations of all coordinates are free and the forces of the constraints do not appear in the action. If we do not solve the constraints, theorem 8.115 holds and multipliers appear in the equations. What the constraint brings is shown by the pendulum in the coordinates \(x\), \(y\): the multiplier gives the force in the rod, which at the lowest position is \(29{,}43\) N, three times the weight of the mass. If we dropped the constraint and varied \(x\), \(y\) freely, this force would be missing from the equations and the stationary path would be free fall. Constraints on velocities that are not holonomic (section 8.4.3) are not covered by this theorem.

8.10.4 The bar (palica)

So far the system had finitely many coordinates. Let us look at the bar of chapters 3 and 7 (figure 8.194).

The bar has one coordinate for each cross section: the displacement \(u(x, t)\) of the cross section at the point \(x\) at time \(t\). Does the principle hold here too, and what does it give for the equation and the condition at the free end?

Take a bar of length \(L\) with constant \(A\), \(E\) and \(\rho\) (density), fixed at \(x = 0\), with the axis \(x\) along the bar. The load per unit length \(q(x, t)\) and the force \(F(t)\) at the end \(x = L\) point in the direction of increasing \(x\) and are continuous. The end has no mass and is free except for the force \(F\).

The model of the bar has an assumption that enters the kinetic energy here: all parts of the cross section move by the same \(u(x, t)\) along the axis, so every point of the cross section at \(x\) has the velocity \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\). Why may we assume it, and when not? When the bar stretches it also gets thinner (Poisson's ratio \(\nu\), definition 3.8), so the particles also move sideways: a particle at the distance \(r\) from the axis moves by \(u\) along the axis and by \(-\nu\,r\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\) sideways, so it has the lateral velocity \(-\nu\,r\,u_{\mathrm{xt}}\). We neglect the kinetic energy of this motion. How much of it is there? Per unit length we get it by an integral over the cross section, and for a round cross section of diameter \(d\) we have \(\int r^2\,\mathrm{d}A = A\,d^2/8\):

\[\underbrace{\tfrac12\,\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}^2}_{\text{longitudinal}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\rho A\,\nu^2\,\frac{d^2}{8}\,u_{\mathrm{xt}}^2}_{\text{lateral}} .\]

For a wave of wavelength \(\Lambda\) along the bar the mean of \(u_{\mathrm{xt}}^2\) is \((2\pi/\Lambda)^2\) times the mean of \(u_{\mathrm{t}}^2\), so the ratio of the lateral and the longitudinal energy is \(\nu^2\,(d^2/8)\,(2\pi/\Lambda)^2\), that is \((\nu^2\pi^2/2)\,(d/\Lambda)^2\), which depends only on the ratio \(\Lambda/d\). For the steel bar of the test below, \(d = 10\) mm, \(\nu = 0{,}30\), and a wave of length \(\Lambda = 40\) m, four times the length of the bar, this is \(0{,}09 \cdot 1{,}25\cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2 \cdot 0{,}0247\ \text{m}^{-2} = 2{,}8\cdot 10^{-8}\), at \(\Lambda/d = 4000\). For long waves the assumption is thus excellent. It fails for waves that are not long compared with the diameter: the ratio reaches \(1\,\%\) at \(\Lambda = 67\) mm, just under seven diameters. The lateral term was written down by LOVE (§278, PDF p. 447), with the radius of gyration of the cross section about the axis of the bar, which for a round cross section is \(d/\sqrt 8\). The second assumption is that \(A\), \(E\) and \(\rho\) are constant along the bar. Without it the equation would have \((EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}})_{\mathrm{x}}\) in place of \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}}\), and the argument would run the same way.

By definition 8.88 the kinetic energy of the bar is the integral with the density \(\rho\) over the volume \(A\,L\) of the bar, which by theorem 4.9 becomes an integral over \(x\) with the factor \(A\), \(T = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x\). The potential energy is the total potential energy of chapter 4 (equation (4.63)) at time \(t\), because the bar is linearly elastic: \(V = \int_0^L \tfrac12\,EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}^2\,\mathrm{d}x - \int_0^L q\,u\,\mathrm{d}x - F\,u(L, t)\). The action of the bar is therefore

\[\mathcal S(u) = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \bigg[\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}^2 + q\,u\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x + F(t)\,u(L, t)\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}t . \tag{8.128}\]

Where the functions we compare live is shown in figure 8.196.

The admissible functions are \(u\) on the rectangle \(\Omega = [0, L] \times [t_1, t_2]\): horizontally the position \(x\) along the bar, vertically time. They have continuous \(u\), \(u_{\mathrm{x}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{t}}\), \(u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) there, satisfy \(u(0, t) = 0\) for every \(t\) and have prescribed displacements \(u(x, t_1)\) and \(u(x, t_2)\) (the positions of the bar at \(t_1\) and \(t_2\)). An admissible variation is a function \(\eta\) on \(\Omega\) with continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\) (and hence continuous \(\eta\)) and \(\eta(0, t) = 0\), \(\eta(x, t_1) = 0\) and \(\eta(x, t_2) = 0\). On three edges the variation is thus zero, because the displacement is prescribed there. On the fourth edge, \(x = L\), nothing is prescribed, so \(\eta\) is free there, and it is this edge that will give the dynamic condition. The first variation \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta)\) is the derivative of \(\mathcal S(u + \alpha\,\eta)\) with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(\alpha = 0\), as in definition 8.112.

Theorem 8.118 Hamilton's principle for a bar (Hamiltonovo načelo za palico)

Let the bar be as above, with constant \(A\), \(E\) and \(\rho\) and continuous \(q\) and \(F\), and let \(u\) be an admissible function. Then \(u\) is a stationary point of the action (8.128) if and only if

\[\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}} = EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} + q \quad \text{on } \Omega , \tag{8.129}\]

and at the free end for every \(t \in [t_1, t_2]\)

\[EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = F(t) . \tag{8.130}\]

Equation (8.130) is called the dynamic boundary condition (dinamični robni pogoj).

Proof

We must show that the first variation is the sum of an integral over the rectangle and an integral over the end of the bar. Then we separate the equation with bumps, and the condition with a variation that is not zero at the end.

  1. The integrand in (8.128) at \(u + \alpha\,\eta\) is a polynomial of second degree in \(\alpha\) with coefficients that are continuous functions on \(\Omega\). Hence \(\mathcal S(u + \alpha\,\eta) = \mathcal S(u) + \alpha\,B_1 + \alpha^2 B_2\) with
    \[B_1 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \big(\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}} - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}} + q\,\eta\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} F\,\eta(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t ,\]
    \[B_2 = \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L \Big(\tfrac12\,\rho A\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}^2 - \tfrac12\,EA\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}^2\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t .\]
    The integrals exist, because the integrands are continuous (theorem 3.53), and the splitting follows from the linearity of the integral (lemma 3.54). The derivative with respect to \(\alpha\) at \(0\) is \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) = B_1\). Equations (8.129) and (8.130) will follow from \(B_1 = 0\).
  2. For every \(x\), \(\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}(x, \cdot)\) and \(\eta(x, \cdot)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(t\) (continuous \(u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\)). Integration by parts (corollary 3.56) along the vertical segment at constant \(x\) gives \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \big[\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{t}}\,\eta\big]_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} - \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}t\), and the term in the square bracket is zero, because \(\eta(x, t_1) = \eta(x, t_2) = 0\) on the lower and upper edges. We may exchange the order of integration (theorem 4.9, the version for a rectangle), so the first term in \(B_1\) equals \(-\int\!\int \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. For every \(t\), \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(\cdot, t)\) and \(\eta(\cdot, t)\) are continuously differentiable functions of \(x\) (continuous \(u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\)). Integration by parts along the horizontal segment at constant \(t\) gives \(-\int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x = -EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\,\eta(L, t) + \int_0^L EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}}\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\), because the term at \(x = 0\) drops out since \(\eta(0, t) = 0\). The term at \(x = L\) remains, because \(\eta\) is not zero there.
  4. Substituting steps 2 and 3 into \(B_1\) we obtain for every admissible variation an integral over the rectangle and an integral over the edge \(x = L\)
    \[\begin{aligned} \delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) &= \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^L R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \\ &\quad + \int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(F(t) - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\big)\,\eta(L, t)\,\mathrm{d}t , \end{aligned} \tag{8.131}\]
    where \(R = -\rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}} + EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}} + q\) is a continuous function on \(\Omega\).
  5. If (8.129) and (8.130) hold, \(R = 0\) and \(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t) = 0\), so by step 4 \(\delta\mathcal S(u, \eta) = 0\) for every admissible variation. The function \(u\) is a stationary point.
  6. Conversely, let \(u\) be a stationary point. For numbers \(0 < \alpha_1 < \beta_1 < L\) and \(t_1 < \alpha_2 < \beta_2 < t_2\) let \(\eta(x, t) = p_{\alpha_1\beta_1}(x)\,p_{\alpha_2\beta_2}(t)\) (lemma 4.83, point 1, which also gives the letters \(\alpha_1\) and \(\alpha_2\) for the bounds of the bump, not the parameter \(\alpha\) of step 1), a bump that is positive only in the interior of a small rectangle. It has continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}}\), is zero at \(x = 0\), \(x = L\), \(t = t_1\) and \(t = t_2\), so it is an admissible variation with \(\eta(L, t) = 0\). By step 4, \(\int\!\int R\,\eta\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). Since \(\eta\) is zero outside \([\alpha_1, \beta_1] \times [\alpha_2, \beta_2]\), this is an integral over this rectangle, and by theorem 4.9 we may write it with the inner integral over \(t\). For all such \(\alpha_1, \beta_1, \alpha_2, \beta_2\) the assumption of point 3 of lemma 4.83 holds for the rectangle \(\Omega\) with coordinates \(x\) and \(y = t\) and the continuous function \(f(x, y) = R(x, y)\), so \(R = 0\) on \(\Omega\). This is equation (8.129).
  7. Let \(\psi\) be a function with a continuous derivative on \([t_1, t_2]\) and \(\psi(t_1) = \psi(t_2) = 0\), and \(\eta(x, t) = (x/L)\,\psi(t)\), which grows linearly from the wall to the free end. It has continuous \(\eta_{\mathrm{x}} = \psi/L\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{t}} = (x/L)\,\dot\psi\), is zero at \(x = 0\), \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), so it is admissible, and \(\eta(L, t) = \psi(t)\). By step 6 the first integral in (8.131) is zero, so \(\int_{\mathrm{t}_1}^{t_2} \big(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, t)\big)\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = 0\). The function \(F - EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L, \cdot)\) is continuous, and by the fundamental lemma (lemma 4.77) it is zero on \([t_1, t_2]\). This is (8.130).

Source: EP example 5.3.1 (pp. 251 to 252), CM §7.7.3 (7.7.30) (PDF p. 367). The sources derive the equations from \(\delta\mathcal S = 0\) by integration by parts and the conclusion that the coefficients are zero, without determining which variations are admissible. The proof in steps is not from a source.

We also get equation (8.129) from the equilibrium of a piece of the bar (section 4.4.4) when we add the inertial force to it (section 8.7). Let us cut out of the bar the piece between the cross sections \(x\) and \(x + h\). The left part of the bar pulls it with the axial force \(N(x)\) toward \(-x\), the right part with \(N(x + h)\) toward \(+x\), where \(N = EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}\). The load on it is \(\int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x+h} q\,\mathrm{d}s\). The piece moves with the acceleration \(u_{\mathrm{tt}}\), so we add the inertial force \(-\int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x+h} \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\,\mathrm{d}s\), opposite to the acceleration, which is close to \(-\rho A h\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) for a small \(h\). The sum of the forces is zero:

\[N(x + h) - N(x) + \int_{\mathrm{x}}^{x+h} \big(q - \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s = 0 .\]

We divide by \(h\) and let \(h \to 0\). The difference of the axial forces divided by \(h\) is a difference quotient and approaches \(N_{\mathrm{x}}(x, t)\). The integral divided by \(h\) is the mean of the continuous function \(q - \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\) on \([x, x + h]\) and approaches its value at \(x\). What remains is \(N_{\mathrm{x}} + q = \rho A\,u_{\mathrm{tt}}\), which with \(N_{\mathrm{x}} = EA\,u_{\mathrm{xx}}\) is exactly equation (8.129). The principle thus gave the same equation as Newton's law on the piece.

The dynamic boundary condition is the natural boundary condition of theorem 5.70, now at every instant, not only at rest: the force in the bar at \(x = L\) at time \(t\) equals the force \(F(t)\) acting on the end. We did not prescribe the condition. The action is sensitive to it only because the displacement at \(x = L\) is not prescribed, and so the principle chooses it by itself: in the plane \((x, t)\) this is the only edge on which the variation is free. At \(x = 0\) we prescribe the displacement, so there is no natural condition.

Let us test it on the hanging bar of chapters 4 to 7 (figure 8.198).

A steel bar, \(L = 10\) m, diameter 10 mm, \(A = 78{,}54\) mm², \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(EA = 1{,}539\cdot 10^7\) N, axis \(x\) pointing down, fixed at \(x = 0\), lower end free, \(F = 0\). Its only load is its weight, \(q = \rho g A = 6{,}010\) N/m. The bar rests in the equilibrium position \(u_{\mathrm{s}} = a\,(2Lx - x^2)\) with \(a = \rho g/(2E) = 1{,}952\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^{-1}\), and the lower end is displaced by \(u_{\mathrm{s}}(L) = a\,L^2 = 19{,}52\) µm. This position satisfies (8.129), since \(u_{\mathrm{tt}} = 0\) and \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{s}}'' + q = -2aEA + \rho g A = 0\), and (8.130), since \(u_{\mathrm{s}}'(L) = 2a\,(L - L) = 0 = F/EA\), the tangent at the end is horizontal, as for the blackwhite curve of figure 8.194. The motion we study is a departure from this position.

Let us look at the first variation in the window \(t_1 = 0\), \(t_2 = 10\) ms, with the variation \(\eta = (x/L)\,\psi(t)\), where \(\psi(t) = 16\,t^2\,(t_2 - t)^2/t_2^4\) is a dimensionless function with maximum value \(1\) (in what follows \(\alpha\) is in metres). The end of the bar thus has the value \(\psi\) in \(\eta\), which is exactly the variation that in step 7 of the proof touches the dynamic condition. By formula (8.131), \(\delta\mathcal S(u_{\mathrm{s}}, \eta) = 0\) (SymPy: \(10^{-16}\) N s, which is rounding). Now try the field \(u_{\mathrm{s}} + c\,x\) with \(c = 10^{-6}\), for which the lower end is lower by a further \(c\,L = 10\) µm. It satisfies equation (8.129) (the term \(c\,x\) is linear in \(x\) and constant in time), but not the condition: \(EA\,u_{\mathrm{x}}(L) = EA\,c = 15{,}39\) N instead of \(0\). The first variation is \(\delta\mathcal S = -EA\,c\int_0^{t_2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}t = -15{,}39\ \text{N}\cdot 5{,}333\ \text{ms} = -0{,}0821\) N s, which agrees with the SymPy computation. The field would be a motion only for a bar with a force \(F = 15{,}39\) N at the end. Without it, it is not stationary: the condition at the end is not an unnecessary addition but the content of the principle.

Now the second variation. For \(\eta = (x/L)\,\psi\) we have \(B_2 = \tfrac12\,\rho A\,\tfrac{L}{3}\int\dot\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}t - \tfrac12\,\tfrac{EA}{L}\int\psi^2\,\mathrm{d}t\). For our \(\psi\), \(\int\dot\psi^2/\int\psi^2 = 12/\tau^2\) on a window of length \(\tau = t_2 - t_1\), so \(B_2 > 0\) if and only if \(\tau < 2L\sqrt{\rho/E} = 3{,}990\) ms, which follows from \(\rho L^2/(3E) \cdot 12/\tau^2 > 1\). At \(\tau = 1\) ms \(B_2 = +4666\) N s/m, at \(\tau = 10\) ms \(B_2 = -2630\) N s/m. For this variation the action therefore increases in \(\alpha^2\) for a short window and decreases for a longer one. For the longer window the stationary position is thus not the smallest action, as for the pendulum. For the short window we tested only one variation. The frequencies of the bar and the solution of equation (8.129) belong to chapter 13.

Hamilton's principle thus gave, for the pendulum, for a system with kinetic energy and for the bar, the same structure: stationarity of the action means equations in the interior and natural conditions where the displacement is not prescribed. For a system of points this structure is theorem 8.117, for the bar theorem 8.118. Both times the action is stationary, not necessarily the smallest. For a system of points the equations of motion obtained are the same as in section 8.9, so we look at what follows from them: section 8.11 finds out why the pendulum conserves energy and which numbers remain constant.
