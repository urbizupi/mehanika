Chapter 13 Vibration of continuous systems: string, shaft and beam

13.9 Rayleigh quotient and the Ritz upper bound for continuous systems

Chapters 10 and 12 listed for the consistent mass frequencies that were always above the exact ones, and did not prove why. Take steel with \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³ and a bar of length \(L = 1\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\). Its exact lowest frequency is \(f_1 = 1253{,}2\) Hz (theorem 13.24). A model of \(n = 1\), \(2\), \(4\) and \(8\) equal elements with the consistent mass (lemma 10.50) gives \(1381{,}9\), \(1285{,}6\), \(1261{,}3\) and \(1255{,}2\) Hz in turn (example 13.25), and so does the cantilever in chapter 12 (section 12.9.4) and in example 13.67. What is proved is only that a finer mesh does not raise the frequencies of the coarse one (corollaries 10.60 and 12.46), not that every frequency of the model is above the frequency of the continuous bar or the continuous beam. Theorem 10.59 is about a system with \(n\) coordinates, whose modes expand every column of \(n\) numbers (corollary 10.22). A continuous bar has infinitely many modes, so we first have to answer whether a field that is not a mode really expands in the modes in such a way that neither mass nor energy is lost.

The path is as follows. First we say which fields are admissible and show that a mode satisfies the weak equation with every admissible field (lemma 13.78). Then we introduce the quotient of the energy and the mass of a field (the Rayleigh quotient), and the expansion in the modes that we need is shown for the sine families of sections 13.3 and 13.6 (lemma 13.80). The theorems on the minimum of the quotient and on the Ritz values follow, and at the end a corollary for finite element meshes and numbers for the bar and the beam. The story of the Ritz method is told in section 6.4.1.

13.9.1 Model and admissible fields

Figure 13.124 shows what we will call an admissible field, on the left for a bar and on the right for a beam. Under each is the axis \(x\) from \(0\) to \(L\), along which we measure the position of a cross-section.

We treat two models, both with idealizations that we name. The bar is a straight bar with axial stiffness \(EA\) and mass per length \(\rho A\), both constant along the bar, in which the cross-sections move only along the axis (chapter 8, theorem 8.118). The beam is an Euler-Bernoulli beam (definition 5.14) with constant \(EI\) and \(\rho A\). We neglect the rotary inertia of the cross-sections and the shear deformation. This is justified for a slender beam and low modes: for a simply supported steel beam with a \(20 \times 40\) mm section and length \(L = 1\) m the first frequency of a beam that includes both is only \(0{,}27\) % lower than the frequency of our model, the third \(2{,}3\) %, and at a length of \(80\) mm the first is already a quarter lower (section 13.10). The bounds of this section therefore compare the frequencies of the computational model with the exact frequencies of the Euler-Bernoulli beam, not with measured ones. The supports are ideal: a clamp allows neither displacement nor rotation, a pin allows rotation without friction. A real support is softer than the ideal one, because the wall and the bolt that hold the end also deform a little under the force. For a system with finitely many coordinates an additional constraint does not lower the eigenvalues (corollary 10.45), so we expect the real frequencies to be somewhat lower than the computed ones. For a continuous system we do not prove this here. The supports must prevent every rigid motion. We treat a bar fixed at both ends or fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), and a beam simply supported at both ends, clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) (a cantilever) or clamped at both ends. These are the supports whose modes we computed in sections 13.3 and 13.6. A beam clamped at one end and simply supported at the other prevents rigid motion as well, but we have not computed its modes, so we leave it out here.

A mode with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is a solution of a differential equation, four times differentiable for the beam, and satisfies all boundary conditions. The trial fields of the Ritz method have no such properties: a hat function has a kink, a Hermite function (definition 12.1) a jump of the second derivative, both at the nodes of the elements. So we introduce fields that satisfy only what we need.

Definition 13.76 Admissible field of vibration (dopustno polje nihanja)

Let a bar of length \(L\) with constant \(EA\) and \(\rho A\) be given, fixed at both ends or fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), or a beam of length \(L\) with constant \(EI\) and \(\rho A\), simply supported at both ends, clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), or clamped at both ends. An admissible field (dopustno polje) is a function \(v : [0, L] \to \mathbb{R}\) that satisfies two conditions. The blue curves are examples.

  1. For a bar, \(v\) is piecewise continuously differentiable (definition 6.11). For a beam, \(v\) is continuously differentiable and its derivative \(v'\) is piecewise continuously differentiable.
  2. The field satisfies the geometric boundary conditions (geometrijski robni pogoji, called essential conditions in section 13.5): for a bar \(v = 0\) at every fixed end, for a beam \(v = 0\) at every clamped or simply supported end and \(v' = 0\) at every clamped end.

We do not require of the admissible field the conditions at a free end or the conditions on the moment and the shear force (natural boundary conditions, naravni pogoji). For admissible fields \(v\) and \(w\) let

\[\begin{aligned} B(v, w) &= \int_0^L EA\,v'\,w'\,\mathrm{d}x \ \text{(bar)} , \\ B(v, w) &= \int_0^L EI\,v''\,w''\,\mathrm{d}x \ \text{(beam)} , \\ \mathcal{M}(v, w) &= \int_0^L \rho A\,v\,w\,\mathrm{d}x \end{aligned} \tag{13.84}\]

be the energy form and the mass form (energijska in masna forma). A trial space (definition 6.32) all of whose fields are admissible is conforming (skladen).

Source: CK (PDF p. 416), where the function \(\psi\) has to satisfy the conditions of an "admissible function" of section 2.5, which we have not read. EP (p. 261): the field has to be differentiable enough and satisfy the geometric boundary conditions. The definition is ours, the form \(B\) is the same as in lemma 12.8 and theorem 12.9.

For the hat functions (definition 6.25) of the free nodes and for the Hermite functions of the free unknowns (lemma 12.8) every combination is an admissible field, since the prescribed unknowns are zero, and the trial space is conforming. This means that a kink and a jump of the second derivative are no obstacle. Are these two forms well defined, and what sign do they have?

Figure 13.125 shows what the next lemma will prove. The left part adds two fields. The field \(v\) and the field \(w\) are two admissible fields of a bar fixed at \(x = 0\), both broken lines with the value zero at the support. The sum \(v + w\) is again a broken line, its kinks are the kinks of the two fields. In the middle is the square of the derivative \(v'^2\), a step that never falls below the axis \(x\), and its area is \(B(v, v)\) up to the factor \(EA\). On the right is the square of the field \(v^2\), which never falls below the axis either, and its area is \(\mathcal{M}(v, v)\) up to the factor \(\rho A\).

Lemma 13.77 Energy form and mass form (energijska in masna forma)

The admissible fields of a bar or a beam form a vector space (definition 1.16). The forms \(B\) and \(\mathcal{M}\) of equation (13.84) are symmetric bilinear forms on it (definition 6.4), \(B(v, v) \ge 0\) for every admissible field, and \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) > 0\) for every admissible field \(v \ne 0\).

Proof

We have to show that a sum and a multiple remain admissible fields, that the forms are bilinear and symmetric, and that they have the sign we claim.

  1. Let \(v\) and \(w\) be admissible fields and \(\alpha\) a number. The function \(v + w\) is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable on the common partition of the two fields (for a beam the same for \(v'\) and \(w'\)), and so is \(\alpha v\). The boundary conditions are linear (\(v = 0\), \(v' = 0\)), so \(v + w\) and \(\alpha v\) satisfy them as well.
  2. The integrands \(EA\,v'w'\), \(EI\,v''w''\) and \(\rho A\,vw\) are piecewise continuous, since a product of two piecewise continuous functions is piecewise continuous on the common partition. The integrals exist (definition 6.9) and are linear in each field by item 2 of lemma 6.10. The forms are symmetric, because a product of numbers does not depend on the order.
  3. The integrand \(EA\,v'^2\) (for a beam \(EI\,v''^2\)) is nonnegative, so \(B(v, v) \ge 0\) by item 3 of lemma 6.10.
  4. Let \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) = 0\). The integrand \(\rho A\,v^2\) is nonnegative, so by item 3 of the same lemma it is zero on every closed piece of the partition. Hence \(v = 0\). For \(v \ne 0\) we therefore have \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) > 0\).

Source: The proof is not from the sources, it follows theorem 12.9 and lemma 12.43.

The next lemma is the bridge between the equation of a mode and the admissible fields, and figure 13.126 shows why the boundary terms vanish. In theorem 13.37 the double integration by parts of the variation gave the equation of motion and the natural conditions, here we use it the other way round: from the equation and the conditions we get an equality of integrals.

Figure 13.126 shows which factor of the boundary term is zero at each end. On the left is the bar with the mode \(\varphi\) and the trial field \(v\), both drawn above the axis \(x\). At the fixed end \(v = 0\). At the free end the mode has a horizontal tangent, \(\varphi' = 0\), so the axial force \(N = EA\,\varphi'\) is zero, although \(v\) is not. The boundary term \(N\,v\,\big|_0^L\) is therefore zero. On the right is the cantilever with its axis. At the clamped end \(v = 0\) and \(v' = 0\). At the free end \(\varphi'' = 0\) (moment) and \(\varphi''' = 0\) (shear force). So the boundary term of the beam is zero as well.

Lemma 13.78 A mode satisfies the weak equation (način zadošča šibki enačbi)

Let \((\omega, \varphi)\) be a mode of a bar or of a beam (definition 13.6) and \(\lambda = \omega^2\): \(\varphi \ne 0\) satisfies the equation of the mode \(-(EA\,\varphi')' = \lambda\rho A\,\varphi\) for a bar and \((EI\,\varphi'')'' = \lambda\rho A\,\varphi\) for a beam, and all boundary conditions, geometric and natural (\(\varphi' = 0\) at a free end of a bar, \(\varphi'' = 0\) at a simply supported end of a beam, \(\varphi'' = 0\) and \(\varphi''' = 0\) at a free end of a beam). Then

\[B(\varphi, v) = \lambda\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi, v) \tag{13.85}\]

for every admissible field \(v\). In particular \(B(\varphi, \varphi) = \lambda\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi, \varphi)\).

Proof

We have to show that the boundary terms of the integration by parts vanish because of the geometric conditions of the field \(v\) and the natural conditions of the mode \(\varphi\).

  1. Bar. The functions \(N = EA\,\varphi'\) and \(v\) are piecewise continuously differentiable, so by lemma 6.13 \(B(\varphi, v) = \int_0^L N\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x =\) \(N\,v\,\big|_0^L\) \(- \int_0^L N'\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. At every end \(v = 0\) (fixed end, geometric condition) or \(\varphi' = 0\) (free end, natural condition), so \(N\,v\,\big|_0^L = 0\). By the equation of the mode \(-N' = \lambda\rho A\,\varphi\), so \(B(\varphi, v) = \lambda\int_0^L\rho A\,\varphi\,v\,\mathrm{d}x = \lambda\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi, v)\).
  3. Beam. The function \(\varphi\) is four times continuously differentiable, \(v'\) is piecewise continuously differentiable. Lemma 6.13 with \(N = EI\,\varphi''\) and \(w = v'\) gives \(\int_0^L EI\,\varphi''\,v''\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\,\varphi''\,v'\,\big|_0^L - \int_0^L EI\,\varphi'''\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  4. Once more with \(N = EI\,\varphi'''\) and \(w = v\) (both continuously differentiable): \(\int_0^L EI\,\varphi'''\,v'\,\mathrm{d}x = EI\,\varphi'''\,v\,\big|_0^L - \int_0^L EI\,\varphi''''\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\). Together \(B(\varphi, v) =\) \(\big[EI\,\varphi''\,v' - EI\,\varphi'''\,v\big]_0^L\) \(+ \int_0^L (EI\,\varphi'')''\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  5. At every end \(\varphi'' = 0\) (simply supported or free end) or \(v' = 0\) (clamped end), and \(\varphi''' = 0\) (free end) or \(v = 0\) (clamped or simply supported end). The boundary term is therefore zero. By the equation of the mode the remaining integral is \(\lambda\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi, v)\).
  6. For \(v = \varphi\) the mode \(\varphi\) is an admissible field (it satisfies the geometric conditions and is smooth), so \(B(\varphi, \varphi) = \lambda\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi, \varphi)\). This agrees with lemma 13.77: \(B(\varphi, \varphi) \ge 0\), \(\mathcal{M}(\varphi, \varphi) > 0\) and \(\lambda = \omega^2 > 0\).

Source: CK (13.44) (PDF p. 421): double integration by parts and "all of the boundary terms ... vanish due to either geometric boundary conditions or natural boundary conditions", for a beam with arbitrary supports. The proof in steps is not from the sources, fields with kinks follow from lemma 6.13.

13.9.2 Rayleigh quotient of a continuous system

Figure 13.127 shows a bar vibrating with the shape \(v\). In both halves of the figure the bar is fixed at \(x = 0\), the upper axis \(x\) carries the deflection, the lower one the energy density. At \(t = 0\) the deflection is largest, the velocity is zero and all the energy is strain energy, its density is the square of the derivative \(v'^2\). At \(\omega t = \pi/2\) the deflection is zero, the velocity \(\omega v\) is largest and all the energy is kinetic, its density is \(\omega^2 v^2\). If \(w(x, t) = v(x)\cos\omega t\) is a solution of the equations of motion without losses, the largest strain energy \(\tfrac12 B(v, v)\) (theorems 8.118 and 13.37) is equal to the largest kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\omega^2\,\mathcal{M}(v, v)\) (definition 8.88), because energy is conserved, and therefore \(\omega^2 = B(v, v)/\mathcal{M}(v, v)\). This is the reason for the next definition. For a field that is not a mode, a motion of such a shape is not a solution, and the quotient is only a number that we compute from the same formula. What it tells us is shown by the two theorems below.

Definition 13.79 Rayleigh quotient of a continuous system (Rayleighov količnik zveznega sistema)

Let \(v \ne 0\) be an admissible field of a bar or a beam (definition 13.76). The Rayleigh quotient (Rayleighov količnik) of the field \(v\) is the number

\[R(v) = \frac{B(v, v)}{\mathcal{M}(v, v)} = \frac{\int_0^L EI\,v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_0^L \rho A\,v^2\,\mathrm{d}x} \ \text{(beam)} , \tag{13.86}\]

for a bar the numerator is \(\int_0^L EA\,v'^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), and the denominator is the area under \(\rho A\,v^2\) without the factor \(\omega^2\). The function \(R\) assigns one number with the unit \(\text{1/s}^2\) to every admissible field except the zero field. The denominator is positive (lemma 13.77).

Source: CK (13.26) (PDF pp. 416 and 417), EP (5.5.4) (p. 261), where the quotient is written for a bar. CK calls the condition that \(v\) is an admissible field an "admissible function", and EP says that the field has to be differentiable enough and satisfy the geometric boundary conditions.

Figure 13.128 shows the fields for which we compute the quotient, each above its own axis \(x\). Bottom right a field and its multiple are drawn next to a clamp. The quotient of the field \(c\,v\) with \(c \ne 0\) equals that of the field \(v\) (\(c^2\) cancels), so the quotient depends only on the shape of the field, like the quotient of definition 10.41. By lemma 13.78 \(R(\varphi) = \lambda\) for every mode. The simply supported beam bottom left with the field \(v = \sin(\pi x/L)\) therefore gives the quotient \(EI(\pi/L)^4/(\rho A) = \omega_1^2\) exactly, because the field is a mode (EP, p. 262). Top left is the bar of the introduction of this section (steel, \(L = 1\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\)), and next to the field \(v = x\) its first mode \(\varphi_1\) is drawn for comparison. The field \(v = x\) has the numerator \(EA\,L\) and the denominator \(\rho A\,L^3/3\), so \(R = 3\,EA/(\rho A\,L^2) = 3\,c^2/L^2\) with \(c^2 = E/\rho\). The frequency is \(\sqrt{3}\,c/(2\pi L) = 1381{,}9\) Hz, which is \(10{,}27\) % above the exact one (\(1253{,}2\) Hz), and is exactly the result of the model with one element. For the cantilever top right (a beam clamped at \(x = 0\)) EP (p. 261) takes the field that is the deflection of the beam under its own weight, \(v = x^4 - 4x^3L + 6x^2L^2\). For \(L = 1\) we have \(\int_0^1 v''^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^1 (12x^2 - 24x + 12)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 144/5\) and \(\int_0^1 v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = 104/45\) (SymPy). For another \(L\) each derivative gets a factor \(1/L\), so \(R = \tfrac{162}{13}\,\tfrac{EI}{\rho A L^4} = 12{,}4615\,\tfrac{EI}{\rho A L^4}\). The exact value is \(\lambda_1 = (\beta L)^4\,\tfrac{EI}{\rho A L^4} = 12{,}3624\,\tfrac{EI}{\rho A L^4}\) with \(\beta L = 1{,}8751\) (section 13.6), so the quotient is \(0{,}80\) % above the exact eigenvalue and the frequency \(0{,}40\) % above the exact one. For both fields that are not modes the quotient is above the exact value. Does this hold for every field?

It does not hold when the field is not admissible. A field is not admissible when at least one of the two conditions of definition 13.76 fails. Figure 13.129 shows three such cases, on the left, in the middle and on the right, each above its own axis \(x\). Take the same bar, fixed at \(x = 0\), and the field \(v = 0{,}1\,L + x\), which differs from the field \(v = x\) in one thing: \(v(0) = 0{,}1\,L\) instead of \(0\). Condition 1 holds, because the field is smooth. Condition 2 fails, because \(v = 0\) is required at the fixed end. We can still compute the quotient from the same formula. The derivative is \(v' = 1\) as before, so the numerator is again \(EA\,L\). The denominator is \(\rho A\int_0^L (0{,}1\,L + x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \rho A\,L^3\,(0{,}01 + 0{,}1 + 1/3) = \rho A\,L^3 \cdot 133/300\), which is more than \(\rho A\,L^3/3\), because the field shifts the whole bar by \(0{,}1\,L\) and so adds mass without adding energy. The quotient is therefore \(R = (300/133)\,c^2/L^2 = 2{,}2556\,c^2/L^2\), which is \(8{,}6\) % below \(\lambda_1 = (\pi/2)^2\,c^2/L^2 = 2{,}4674\,c^2/L^2\). The frequency is \(1198{,}2\) Hz, which is \(4{,}4\) % below the exact \(1253{,}2\) Hz, whereas the field \(v = x\) gives \(1381{,}9\) Hz, \(10{,}27\) % above it (SymPy). The inequality "the quotient is not below the exact value" is therefore not a property of every field but of admissible ones, and condition 2 is the one that rejects the field \(0{,}1\,L + x\).

The same holds for the beam. Take the cantilever (\(L = 1\), clamped at \(x = 0\)) and the field \(v = x^4 - 4x^3 + 6x^2 + 0{,}1\,x\), that is the field taken by EP with a rotation \(0{,}1\,x\) added. Condition 2 requires \(v'(0) = 0\), but here \(v'(0) = 0{,}1\). The linear term has no second derivative, so the numerator is \(144/5\) as before. The denominator grows from \(104/45 = 2{,}3111\) to \(2239/900 = 2{,}4878\), because the field is larger. The quotient is \(R = 25\,920/2239 = 11{,}5766\), which is below the exact \(\lambda_1 = 12{,}3624\) (\(f = 31{,}34\) Hz against \(32{,}39\) Hz, \(3{,}2\) % below), whereas the admissible field of EP gives \(12{,}4615\). For the beam condition 1 matters just as much. The field \(v = 0\) for \(x \le L/2\) and \(v = x - L/2\) for \(x \ge L/2\) is admissible for the bar but not for the beam, because its derivative jumps at \(x = L/2\) from \(0\) to \(1\) and \(v\) is not continuously differentiable. If we computed the second derivative piece by piece, it would be zero on each piece, the numerator would be zero and the quotient zero, below every eigenvalue. The result is nonsense, because a sum computed piece by piece does not see the point of the kink. We smooth the kink as the part of the figure below the right one shows. We replace the sharp kink with a rounded one over a piece of length \(\delta\) around \(x = L/2\), so that the derivative rises evenly from \(0\) to \(1\) on it. The smoothed field is admissible for the beam. Its second derivative, drawn above the lower axis, is \(1/\delta\) on that piece and zero elsewhere, since the derivative rises by \(1\) over the length \(\delta\). The area under the second derivative is therefore always \(1\), while the numerator integrates its square: \(EI\,\delta\,(1/\delta)^2 = EI/\delta\). For half the \(\delta\) the second derivative is twice as high, its square four times, while the piece is only half as wide, so the numerator doubles. For \(\delta = 0{,}1\,L\), \(0{,}01\,L\) and \(0{,}001\,L\) the numerator is \(10\,EI/L\), \(100\,EI/L\) and \(1000\,EI/L\) (SymPy). As \(\delta\) tends to zero, the numerator grows beyond every bound, so no finite bending energy can be assigned to the kink.

13.9.3 Completeness of the modes

Why can the quotient of an admissible field not fall below \(\lambda_1\)? Before we prove it, let us look at a picture that shows the answer at one glance.

For matrices the quotient was a weighted mean of the eigenvalues (proof of theorem 10.42), and a weighted mean is the balance point of a seesaw. The upper part of figure 13.130 draws this. We lay a board along the axis of eigenvalues and mark on it the positions \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \lambda_3 < \lambda_4\). We divide the field among the modes and put at the position \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) the weight \(w_{\mathrm{s}}\), the share of the field's mass that is in the shape of mode \(s\). We support the board where it is in balance. That position is the weighted mean \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} w_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\). Now let us draw the vertical line through \(\lambda_1\). All the weights stand on it or to its right, because \(\lambda_1\) is the smallest eigenvalue. If we put the support to the left of this line, every weight would turn the board about the support in the same direction, the right end down. The moments would add up and could not cancel, while a board at rest must have zero total moment. So the support is on the line or to its right, \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} w_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_1\). Item 1 of theorem 13.81 will show that the quotient \(R(v)\) is not below this mean, and together we will have \(R(v) \ge \lambda_1\).

For matrices the seesaw came from the expansion \(\vek v = \sum c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the equality \(\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v = \sum c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) (proof of theorem 10.42). For a continuous system we need the same equality for admissible fields: for \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}, v)\) let (the weights of the modes are the shares of \(c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\) in \(\mathcal{M}(v, v)\), their sum shall be \(1\))

\[\sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{\infty} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2 = \mathcal{M}(v, v) . \tag{13.87}\]

We call this property completeness of the modes (polnost načinov). Without it we could imagine a field that has a part of its mass in a shape that is not among the modes. That part would not be on the board, the weights would add up to less than \(1\), \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} w_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) would no longer be a mean and could lie below \(\lambda_1\). The theorem on the expansion in modes (theorem 13.72) says that the sine series converges uniformly to every admissible field of the sine families, also one with a kink, but it does not give equation (13.87) for the mass. For the sine families we show it with Parseval's equality (lemma 13.12), and for other supports we assume it.

Figure 13.131 shows why Parseval's equality holds for these fields. In cases (a) and (c) the field \(v\) is zero at both ends, and lemma 13.12 applies to it directly. Its proof rests on lemma 13.10, which reflects the field through the origin and repeats it with period \(2L\), so that it gets a function that is continuous on the whole axis. In case (b) the field is not zero at the end \(x = L\), so we first mirror it across \(x = L\). The extension is zero at both ends of the interval \([0, 2L]\), and the lemma extends it as an odd function and repeats it with period \(4L\).

Lemma 13.80 Completeness of the sine modes (polnost sinusnih načinov)

Let the system be one of three: (a) a bar fixed at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\), (b) a bar fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\), (c) a simply supported beam. The normalized modes, in increasing order of eigenvalues, are \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{2/(\rho A L)}\,\sin(k\pi x/L)\) for (a) and (c) and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}} = \sqrt{2/(\rho A L)}\,\sin\big((2k - 1)\pi x/(2L)\big)\) for (b), \(k = 1, 2, \dots\) (sections 13.3 and 13.6). Then equation (13.87) holds for every admissible field \(v\).

Proof

We have to show that Parseval's equality (lemma 13.12) holds for the field or for its mirror extension, and that this equality is equation (13.87).

  1. Cases (a) and (c). The field \(v\) is piecewise continuously differentiable on \([0, L]\) (for a beam even continuously differentiable) and \(v(0) = v(L) = 0\). These are the conditions of lemma 13.12 with \(h = v\). With the sine coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L v\,\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm{d}x\) the lemma gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), so \(\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{L}{2}\sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}}^2\).
  2. Case (b). On \([0, 2L]\) let \(\tilde v(x) = v(x)\) for \(x \le L\) and \(\tilde v(x) = v(2L - x)\) for \(x \ge L\). The function \(\tilde v\) is continuous at \(x = L\), where it is \(v(L)\) from both sides, piecewise continuously differentiable, because it is so on each half, and \(\tilde v(0) = \tilde v(2L) = v(0) = 0\). Lemma 13.12 on the interval \([0, 2L]\) with the coefficients \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac{1}{L}\int_0^{2L}\tilde v\,\sin(n\pi x/(2L))\,\mathrm{d}x\) gives \(\sum_{\mathrm{n}} b_{\mathrm{n}}^2 = \tfrac{1}{L}\int_0^{2L}\tilde v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x\), because the integrals over the two halves are equal (substitution \(x \mapsto 2L - x\)).
  3. Because of \(\tilde v(2L - x) = v(x)\) and \(\sin\big(n\pi(2L - x)/(2L)\big) = -(-1)^n\sin(n\pi x/(2L))\) the integral over \([L, 2L]\) in \(b_{\mathrm{n}}\) is \(-(-1)^n\) times the integral \(\int_0^L v\,\sin(n\pi x/(2L))\,\mathrm{d}x\). For even \(n\) the two integrals cancel and \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = 0\), for odd \(n\) we have \(b_{\mathrm{n}} = \tfrac{2}{L}\int_0^L v\,\sin(n\pi x/(2L))\,\mathrm{d}x\). By step 2, therefore, \(\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{L}{2}\sum b_{\mathrm{n}}^2\) over odd \(n\).
  4. The expansion coefficient is \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = \mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{k}}, v) = \sqrt{2\rho A/L}\int_0^L v\,\sin(\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{\rho A L/2}\ b_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}\), where \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = k\) for (a) and (c) and \(n_{\mathrm{k}} = 2k - 1\) for (b). Hence \(\sum_{\mathrm{k}} c_{\mathrm{k}}^2 = \tfrac{\rho A L}{2}\sum b_{\mathrm{n}_\mathrm{k}}^2 = \rho A\int_0^L v^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \mathcal{M}(v, v)\) by steps 1 and 3.

The lemma covers a bar fixed at both ends and a bar fixed at one end and free at the other, which is the bar of chapter 10, and a simply supported beam. For the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends the modes are not sines. CK (PDF p. 422) states an expansion theorem: every function \(V\) that satisfies the same boundary conditions as the modes and has a continuous \((EI\,V'')''\) is the sum of an absolutely and uniformly convergent series in the modes. The theorem holds for smooth fields with all (also natural) conditions, while our admissible fields may have a jump of the second derivative and need not satisfy the natural conditions. To pass from one set of fields to the other we would have to show that every admissible field can be approximated by smooth fields that satisfy all conditions, so that the integral of the square of the difference tends to zero. We do not carry this out here. For the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends we therefore assume equation (13.87) (the completeness assumption) and point this out wherever we use it. Everything that follows is proved for the sine families, and for the other supports proved under this assumption and checked with numbers.

Theorem 13.81 The Rayleigh quotient of a continuous system has its minimum at a mode (Rayleighov količnik zveznega sistema ima najmanjšo vrednost pri načinu)

Let the system be a bar or a beam of definition 13.76 and \(\varphi_1, \varphi_2, \dots\) all its normalized modes, \(\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{s}}) = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) otherwise (theorem 13.70), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\), \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \dots\), each with a single mode up to a multiple (section 13.7.3). For an admissible field \(v \ne 0\) let \(c_{\mathrm{r}} = \mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}, v)\). Then:

  1. \(B(v, v) \ge \sum_{\mathrm{r}} \lambda_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}^2\).
  2. If completeness (13.87) holds (for the sine families by lemma 13.80), then \(R(v) \ge \lambda_1\).
  3. If completeness holds and \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(s = 1, \dots, r - 1\), then \(R(v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). So \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is the smallest value of the quotient on the admissible fields that are \(\mathcal{M}\)-orthogonal to \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{\mathrm{r}-1}\), and the field \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) attains it.
Proof

Item 1 is Bessel's inequality for the energy form, which does not need completeness. Items 2 and 3 combine item 1 with completeness, as in the proof of theorem 10.42.

  1. The modes \(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) are smooth and satisfy the geometric conditions, so they are admissible fields, and so is \(S_{\mathrm{N}} = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \le \mathrm{N}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) (lemma 13.77, step 1). By lemma 13.78 and symmetry \(B(v, \varphi_{\mathrm{s}}) = B(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, v) = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, v) = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. By bilinearity and theorem 13.70 \(B(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{t}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{t}}\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, \varphi_{\mathrm{t}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\delta_{\mathrm{st}}\), so \(B(S_{\mathrm{N}}, S_{\mathrm{N}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \le \mathrm{N}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) and \(B(v, S_{\mathrm{N}}) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \le \mathrm{N}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) by step 1.
  3. By lemma 13.77 \(0 \le B(v - S_{\mathrm{N}}, v - S_{\mathrm{N}})\) (the difference between \(v\) and \(S_3\) is a field, its energy is not negative) \(= B(v, v) - 2B(v, S_{\mathrm{N}}) + B(S_{\mathrm{N}}, S_{\mathrm{N}}) =\) \(B(v, v) - \sum_{\mathrm{s} \le \mathrm{N}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). All terms \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) are nonnegative (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} = \omega_{\mathrm{s}}^2 > 0\) by definition 13.6), the partial sums increase and are bounded by \(B(v, v)\), so the series converges to a sum that is not larger than \(B(v, v)\). This is item 1.
  4. Let completeness hold and \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(s < r\). By completeness \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) = \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\). By item 1 and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(s \ge r\) we have \(B(v, v) \ge \sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2 \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{s} \ge \mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2 = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathcal{M}(v, v)\).
  5. We divide by \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) > 0\) and get \(R(v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is item 3. For \(r = 1\) there are no conditions on \(c_{\mathrm{s}}\), this is item 2. The field \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) satisfies the conditions of item 3 by theorem 13.70 and has \(R(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}) = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) by lemma 13.78, so \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) is indeed the smallest value.

Source: CK (PDF p. 418): "It will be shown in Section 13.5 that the Rayleigh Method always gives an upper bound to the exact fundamental frequency", which CK only announces. The proof in steps is not from the sources, it follows theorem 10.42.

Theorem 13.82 Ritz values of a continuous system are upper bounds (Ritzove vrednosti zveznega sistema so zgornje meje)

Let the system be a bar or a beam of definition 13.76 for which completeness of the modes holds ((13.87), proved by lemma 13.80 for a bar fixed at both ends or at one, and for a simply supported beam). Let \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) be linearly independent admissible fields (definition 6.22), that is a conforming trial space, and let

\[\tilde K_{\mathrm{ij}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) , \qquad \tilde M_{\mathrm{ij}} = \mathcal{M}(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}}) \tag{13.88}\]

be matrices of order \(m\). Let \(\tilde\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the eigenvalues of the reduced problem \(\tilde{\mat K}\,\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\tilde{\mat M}\,\vek a\) (as in definition 10.58). Then:

  1. The matrix \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is symmetric, and \(\tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric and positive definite, so the \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are real numbers.
  2. \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r = 1, \dots, m\), where the \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the eigenvalues of the system.
  3. If the conforming trial space with \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_{\mathrm{m}}\) is contained in a conforming trial space with \(m' \ge m\) fields and \(\tilde\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\) are its Ritz values, then \(\tilde\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, m\).
Proof

We have to show that the Ritz columns form fields with orthonormal forms \(B\) and \(\mathcal{M}\), that their space contains a field that is \(\mathcal{M}\)-orthogonal to the first \(r - 1\) modes and has a quotient of at most \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and then use theorem 13.81. Item 3 follows from theorem 10.59.

  1. The matrix \(\tilde{\mat K}\) is symmetric because \(B\) is symmetric (lemma 13.77). The same for \(\tilde{\mat M}\). For \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\) the field \(v = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} a_{\mathrm{i}}\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is nonzero (independence), so \(\mathcal{M}(v, v) > 0\), and also \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat M}\vek a = \mathcal{M}(v, v) > 0\) by lemma 13.77 and \(\vek a^\T\tilde{\mat K}\vek a = B(v, v)\) by bilinearity. Item 1 is proved, the reality of the values follows from theorem 10.21 for the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \tilde{\mat M})\).
  2. By theorem 10.21 there are columns \(\vek a_1, \dots, \vek a_{\mathrm{m}}\) with \(\vek a_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\tilde{\mat M}\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(\tilde{\mat K}\vek a_{\mathrm{j}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\tilde{\mat M}\vek a_{\mathrm{j}}\). The fields \(v_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} (\vek a_{\mathrm{j}})_{\mathrm{i}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are admissible and by step 1 \(\mathcal{M}(v_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{j}}) = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\) and \(B(v_{\mathrm{i}}, v_{\mathrm{j}}) = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}}\delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\).
  3. The fields \(v_1, \dots, v_{\mathrm{r}}\) are linearly independent: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}} v_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) it follows, after multiplying with \(\mathcal{M}(v_{\mathrm{i}}, \cdot)\) and by step 2, that \(t_{\mathrm{i}} = 0\).
  4. Let \(v = \sum_{\mathrm{j} \le \mathrm{r}} t_{\mathrm{j}} v_{\mathrm{j}}\). By step 2 \(B(v, v) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}}\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}^2 = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mathcal{M}(v, v)\), so \(R(v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(v \ne 0\).
  5. The conditions \(\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, v) = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} t_{\mathrm{j}}\,\mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, v_{\mathrm{j}}) = 0\) for \(s = 1, \dots, r - 1\) are \(r - 1\) homogeneous linear equations for the \(r\) numbers \(t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}\) (none for \(r = 1\)). By lemma 10.16 they have a solution \((t_1, \dots, t_{\mathrm{r}}) \ne \vek 0\), and \(v \ne 0\) by step 3.
  6. The field of step 5 is admissible and has \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = \mathcal{M}(\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}, v) = 0\) for \(s < r\). By item 3 of theorem 13.81 \(R(v) \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and by step 4 \(R(v) \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is item 2.
  7. Item 3. Every \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) is a combination \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} P_{\mathrm{ji}}\psi'_{\mathrm{j}}\) with a matrix \(\mat P\) with \(m'\) rows and \(m\) columns. The columns of \(\mat P\) are independent because the \(\psi_{\mathrm{i}}\) are. By bilinearity \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat P^\T\tilde{\mat K}'\mat P\) and \(\tilde{\mat M} = \mat P^\T\tilde{\mat M}'\mat P\). By item 1 \(\tilde{\mat K}'\) is symmetric and \(\tilde{\mat M}'\) symmetric positive definite, so item 1 of theorem 10.59 with \(\mat\Psi = \mat P\) gives \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \tilde\lambda'_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Corollary 13.83 Frequencies of a consistent-mass model are not below the exact ones (frekvence konsistentnega modela niso pod pravimi)

Let a bar with constant \(EA\), \(\rho A\), fixed at \(x = 0\) and fixed or free at \(x = L\), be divided into \(n\) elements, or a beam with constant \(EI\), \(\rho A\) into \(n\) Hermite elements (definition 12.3), simply supported at both ends, clamped at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) (cantilever) or clamped at both ends. Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be the matrices of the free unknowns with the consistent mass, computed with exact integrals (theorems 7.13 and 12.9, lemmas 10.50 and 12.43, equation (12.50)), and \(\lambda^h_1 \le \dots \le \lambda^h_{\mathrm{N}}\) the eigenvalues of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) of order \(N\). Then \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for every \(r = 1, \dots, N\), where the \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are the eigenvalues of the continuous bar or the continuous beam. For a bar and a simply supported beam this is proved, for the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends under the completeness assumption ((13.87)).

Proof

We have to show that the functions of the free unknowns form a conforming trial space and that the matrices are the Ritz matrices \(\tilde{\mat K}\) and \(\tilde{\mat M}\), then use theorem 13.82.

  1. The functions of the free nodes \(\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\) of a bar (hat functions, definition 6.25) are continuous and piecewise linear, so piecewise continuously differentiable. The functions \(H_{\mathrm{I}}\) of a beam (lemma 12.8) are continuously differentiable and piecewise polynomials of degree three, so \(H_{\mathrm{I}}'\) is piecewise continuously differentiable. The function of a free node has the value zero at all prescribed unknowns, so it satisfies the geometric conditions. So all are admissible fields and the trial space is conforming.
  2. The fields are linearly independent: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\psi_{\mathrm{I}} = 0\) it follows that the value (for a beam also the slope) of the field at every node is zero, and these values are, by lemma 12.8 (item 2) or by the definition of the hat function, exactly the \(d_{\mathrm{I}}\).
  3. By item 3 of theorem 7.13 (for a beam by item 2 of theorem 12.9) \(K_{\mathrm{IJ}} = B(\psi_{\mathrm{J}}, \psi_{\mathrm{I}})\), so \(\mat K = \tilde{\mat K}\) of equation (13.88). By lemma 10.50 and equation (12.50) \(\vek d^\T\mat M\vek d = \mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{h}}, u_{\mathrm{h}})\) for \(u_{\mathrm{h}} = \sum_{\mathrm{I}} d_{\mathrm{I}}\psi_{\mathrm{I}}\). The matrix \(\tilde{\mat M}\) gives the same quadratic form, \(\vek d^\T\tilde{\mat M}\vek d = \mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{h}}, u_{\mathrm{h}})\) by bilinearity, and the difference \(\mat S = \mat M - \tilde{\mat M}\) is symmetric with \(\vek d^\T\mat S\vek d = 0\) for every \(\vek d\), so \(\mat S = \mat 0\) (we take \(\vek d = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}} + \vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), as in step 4 of the proof of corollary 10.60).
  4. By theorem 13.82 item 2 \(\lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} = \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) for \(r = 1, \dots, N\).

Source: COOK (PDF pp. 396 and 397): "if ... elements are compatible ... and mass matrices are consistent, then computed natural frequencies are upper bounds to exact frequencies of the mathematical model". The proof in steps is not from the sources, it follows corollary 10.60.

The conditions of the corollary are those of COOK: conformity holds because the fields of the node functions are admissible, and exact integrals make sure that the matrices do not soften the energy (with incomplete integration \(\mat K\) would not be the matrix of the form \(B\)). The lumped mass matrix is not the matrix of the form \(\mathcal{M}\) and the theorem does not hold for it. For a uniform bar fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) with equal elements, corollary 10.55 proves that the lumped frequency is below the consistent one and below the exact one, but this theorem does not. Together with corollary 10.60, for nested meshes \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda^h_{\mathrm{r}} \le \lambda^H_{\mathrm{r}}\), where \(h\) is the finer mesh than \(H\).

A test with numbers. The bar (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(L = 1\) m, fixed at \(x = 0\)) with the consistent mass has, for \(n = 1\), \(2\), \(4\) and \(8\) elements, its first two frequencies and their errors in example 13.25. The third frequency (\(6266{,}0\) Hz) is \(7227{,}7\) Hz (\(+15{,}35\) %) at \(n = 4\) and \(6519{,}6\) Hz (\(+4{,}05\) %) at \(n = 8\), and does not exist at \(n = 1\) and \(2\) because there are fewer than three free unknowns.

The cantilever of the same material, section \(20 \times 40\) mm (height \(40\) mm in the plane of bending), \(L = 1\) m, has the exact frequencies \(32{,}391\), \(202{,}989\) and \(568{,}376\) Hz (\(\beta L = 1{,}8751\), \(4{,}6941\), \(7{,}8548\)). The frequencies and errors of Hermite elements with \(n = 1\), \(2\), \(4\), \(8\) are in example 13.67 and are not repeated here.

Figure 13.137 shows the errors of examples 13.25 and 13.67. On the left is the bar, on the right the cantilever. Horizontally is the mode number, vertically the frequency error in percent on a logarithmic axis. Each series of dots is one model: grey \(n = 1\), blue \(n = 2\), orange \(n = 4\) and blackwhite \(n = 8\) elements. The series are higher at higher \(r\) and lower with more elements. For \(n = 8\) all \(8\) frequencies of the bar and all \(16\) frequencies of the beam are above the exact ones, and so for every \(n\) of examples 13.25 and 13.67 (NumPy, the computation compares every \(r \le N\)). The cantilever with \(n = 1\) has the trial space \(\xi^2\), \(\xi^3\) (admissible fields with \(v(0) = v'(0) = 0\)). The quotient of the field \(3\xi^2 - \xi^3\), which is the shape of the deflection of a cantilever under a concentrated force at the free end, is \(140/11 = 12{,}7273\) (in the unit \(EI/(\rho A L^4)\)), the frequency \(32{,}865\) Hz (\(+1{,}47\) %). The smallest Ritz value of the space \(\xi^2\), \(\xi^3\) is \(12{,}4802\) (\(32{,}545\) Hz, \(+0{,}475\) %) and is below the quotient of the field \(3\xi^2 - \xi^3\), as item 3 of the theorem requires, because that field lies in this space. The quotient of the EP field, \(12{,}4615\) (\(+0{,}40\) % in frequency), is lower than \(12{,}4802\), but that field is not in the space \(\xi^2\), \(\xi^3\) (it has a term \(\xi^4\)), so item 3 does not apply to it. All three quotients are above the exact value \(12{,}3624\).

The ratio of the errors on doubling the number of elements is \(4{,}02\) and \(4{,}01\) for the bar (first frequency, \(n = 2 \to 4 \to 8\)), and \(14{,}8\) and \(15{,}7\) for the beam. On a logarithmic axis the same ratio is the same distance, however high on the axis it lies. So the bars of the bar are equally long, and the bars of the beam are about twice as long, because \(16 = 4 \cdot 4\) and the distance for the ratio \(16\) is made of two distances for the ratio \(4\). According to COOK (PDF p. 397), with the consistent mass the frequency error is reduced by a factor of \(4\) for bars and \(16\) for beams on doubling the number of elements, because the error is of order \(h^{2(p+1-m)}\) with \(h\) the element length, \(p\) the degree of the polynomial of the field and \(2m\) the order of the equation. For a linear bar \(p + 1 - m = 1\), for a cubic beam \(2\). This is not proved here, but the numbers agree. For higher modes the reduction is smaller, because a mesh with few elements does not describe the shorter waves of the higher modes well enough: for the beam the ratio at \(r = 2\) between \(n = 2\) and \(4\) is only \(7{,}3\) (\(0{,}849\) % against \(0{,}117\) %).

13.9.6 What is proved

The section kept the promises of chapters 10 and 12 and the promise of chapter 6 that the Ritz method is stated for natural frequencies.

  • Proved without assumptions: for a bar fixed at both ends or fixed at one end and free at the other, and for a simply supported beam, every natural frequency of the consistent model with a conforming field and exact integrals is not below the exact one, and the Rayleigh quotient of every admissible field is not below \(\lambda_1\) (theorem 13.82, corollary 13.83).
  • Proved under the completeness assumption (equation (13.87)): the same for the cantilever and the beam clamped at both ends. The assumption is stated in CK (PDF p. 422) for smooth fields and is not proved for admissible fields. The numbers above confirm it, they do not prove it.
  • Not proved and not true in general: the lumped mass (for a uniform bar fixed at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\) with equal elements the lumped frequency is below the consistent one and below the exact one by corollary 10.55, for other supports and meshes this is not proved, for a beam the difference of the matrices is indefinite), incomplete integration of the stiffness (COOK, PDF p. 397: the bound is not guaranteed), a nonconstant \(EI\) inside an element (the matrices do not belong to the form \(B\) with the exact coefficients) and a model with a different idealization from the one that has the exact frequency (for example a Timoshenko beam against an Euler-Bernoulli beam).
  • The size of the error is not given: the theorem guarantees only the sign, not the size. The size is of order \(h^2\) for a bar and \(h^4\) for a beam by COOK and the numbers above, but this is not derived.
