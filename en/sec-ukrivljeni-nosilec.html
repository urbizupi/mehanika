Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.14 The curved beam

The ring of chapter 4 is a steel ring with inner radius \(a = 40\) mm, outer radius \(b = 60\) mm and thickness \(t = 2\) mm. There we cut it with a plane through the axis and showed that the cut ring is simply connected (lemma 4.110). Now we attach one couple to each of the two faces of the cut and look at what happens to the ring.

On the left of figure 11.174 is the ring before loading, drawn grey dashed, with the cut on the right. To the faces of the cut we attach couples of opposite sense: the couple on the face above the cut turns anticlockwise, the couple below it clockwise. The ring after loading is drawn orange. The faces of the cut turn away from each other by the angle \(\Delta\varphi\) about the centre \(O\), so the cut opens into a wedge, the ring spreads and its radius grows a little. The changes are drawn strongly exaggerated: for the couples of example 11.61 \(\Delta\varphi\) is only \(6{,}0\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad. Couples of the reversed sense would press the faces of the cut against each other. The piece of a ring on the right of the figure, however, is closed by such couples: the piece curves more.

This gives a curved beam (ukrivljeni nosilec): a body whose axis is a circular arc. The piece of the ring in figure 11.177 has inner radius \(a = 40\) mm. The axis runs midway between the inner and the outer edge, that is along the radius \(c = (a + b)/2 = 50\) mm, and the height of the section is \(h = b - a = 20\) mm. Chapter 5 treated straight beams and named, as a limit of its theory, the exact plane solutions of elasticity that this chapter provides. For a circular edge the polar form of the Airy function from section 11.12 is suitable.

Does the formula \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = N/A - M\,y/I\) of theorem 5.35 hold for this beam? Let us see in figure 11.175 what the formula assumes.

On the left is a piece of a straight beam between two sections. All fibres between the sections have the same length \(\ell\). We hold the left section in place. The right section before loading is drawn grey dashed and after loading orange: it stays plane and turns by the angle \(\Delta\omega\) about its middle. So the bottom fibre is lengthened and the top one shortened, both by \(\delta\), and every fibre in between by a displacement proportional to its distance from the middle. Since all fibres have the same length, the strain, that is the displacement divided by the length \(\ell\), is also proportional to the distance from the middle.

On the right is a piece of our curved beam between sections that enclose the angle \(\Delta\theta\) (in radians, definition 8.8). The inner fibre (\(r = a = 40\) mm) is \(40\,\Delta\theta\) mm long and the outer one (\(r = b = 60\) mm) is \(60\,\Delta\theta\) mm long, that is half as long again. Let the right section turn by the same angle \(\Delta\omega\) about the fibre at radius \(r_0\) that does not change its length. Then the elongation of the inner fibre \(\delta_{\mathrm{a}}\) and the shortening of the outer fibre \(\delta_{\mathrm{b}}\) are of similar size, but we divide them by different lengths, so the strain of the inner fibre is larger. So we expect the formula to be wrong for this beam, and the question is by how much. The answer comes from the exact solution of the plane problem, not from a model of the section. We compute it with the Airy function in polar coordinates and compare it with the formula.

11.14.1 Internal forces of a curved beam

First we agree on what the force and the moment on a section of a curved beam are, so that the comparison with chapter 5 has the same sign. A curved beam is a piece of an annulus \(a \le r \le b\), \(\theta_1 \le \theta \le \theta_2\) with \(\theta_2 - \theta_1 < 2\pi\) (figure 11.177), in plane stress (definition 11.36). The section at angle \(\theta\) is the radial segment \(\theta = \text{const}\), \(a \le r \le b\). Chapter 5 defined the internal forces of a straight beam as the resultant and the moment of the stress vector on the face with outward normal \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), that is as what the part at larger \(x\) exerts on the part at smaller \(x\) (lemma 5.9). We take the same rule here, only the direction of the axis of the beam is \(\vek e_\theta\) instead of \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\). The stress vector on the face with normal \(+\vek e_\theta\) is \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). Its component in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) is, by definition 11.19, \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta = \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\). Since the stress matrix is symmetric, this is the same number as \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{r}}\), where the first index gives the face and the second the direction of the force. The centroid of the section is at the point \(r = c\) (definition 5.21). The point of the section with radius \(r\) has the position vector \((r - c)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) relative to the centroid.

We compute the moment of the stress vector of this point in figure 11.176. On the left is the section, drawn as a strip from \(r = a\) to \(r = b\), with the basis vectors \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) to the right and \(\vek e_\theta\) up. In the middle of the strip is the centroid \(C\), and from it to the point with radius \(r\) the lever arm \(r - c\). At the point acts the stress vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\), which we split into the component \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta\) and the component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). The component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies on the line through \(C\) and the point, so its moment about \(C\) is zero. The component \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta\) is perpendicular to the lever arm, and since \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\times\vek e_\theta = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) in the right-handed system \((r, \theta, z)\), the \(z\) component of the moment of this point is \((r - c)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), positive when it turns anticlockwise. The shear force \(V\) is the component of the resultant in the direction of the axis \(y\) of chapter 5. Since this axis points here towards the centre \(O\) (\(\vek e_\theta\times(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), as \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) for a straight beam), it is the component in the direction \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). We compute the plane problem per unit thickness, so the integrals run over \(r\) only.

Plane stress is a model of a thin plate, and it is suitable here because the thickness \(t = 2\) mm is a tenth of the height of the section \(h = 20\) mm. The faces \(z = \pm t/2\) are free, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}}\) and \(\sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}}\) are zero on them, and the model assumes that they are zero through the whole thickness, which is good for a thin plate but not exact: for the cantilever of section 11.9.7 the error is \(0{,}06\,\%\) of the largest stress. The stresses of this section are therefore stresses of the model, and by theorem 11.39 they are averages over the thickness of the exact three-dimensional solution when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\). The model does not hold for a thick ring, say for a long curved piece of a pipe with thickness \(t\) much larger than \(h\). Such a piece is closer to plane strain, and it is exactly in plane strain when we hold it between smooth rigid walls (section 11.9.7). What does this change in the results? The piece of the annulus is simply connected, carries no body force, and only forces are prescribed on its boundary, so by corollary 11.43 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) are the same in both models. What differs is the axial stress, which in plane strain is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \nu\,(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})\), and the displacements, in which \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2)\) and \(\nu^* = \nu/(1 - \nu)\) stand in place of \(E\) and \(\nu\) (theorem 11.33, items 2 and 3). The rotation of the sections relative to each other, which we compute in item 5 of theorem 11.60, is inversely proportional to \(E\) and contains no other elastic constant. In plane strain the larger \(E^* = E/(1 - \nu^2) = E/0{,}91\) at \(\nu = 0{,}3\) stands in place of \(E\), so the rotation is \(9\,\%\) smaller there.

Definition 11.59 Internal forces of a curved beam (notranje količine ukrivljenega nosilca)

Let a curved beam be a piece of an annulus \(a \le r \le b\), \(\theta_1 \le \theta \le \theta_2\) with \(\theta_2 - \theta_1 < 2\pi\) (figure 11.177), of an isotropic, homogeneous, linearly elastic material in plane stress, and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) be continuous functions on it. Let \(c = (a + b)/2\) be the radius of the centroids of the sections and \(t\) the thickness of the beam. The internal forces of the section at \(\theta\) are the axial force, the shear force and the bending moment per unit thickness,

\[\begin{aligned} N(\theta) &= \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r , \\ V(\theta) &= -\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r , \\ M(\theta) &= \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (r - c)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r . \end{aligned} \tag{11.93}\]

These are the component \(\vek e_\theta\) and the component \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the direction of the axis \(y\)) of the resultant and the component \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) of the moment about the centroid of the stress vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) on the section, per unit thickness. At thickness \(t\) the force and the moment of the beam are \(t\,N\), \(t\,V\) and \(t\,M\).

Source: The definition is ours, by the same rule as lemma 5.9. SADD (8.4.58) (PDF p. 211, crop read) writes \(\int\sigma_\theta\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = -M\), so with the opposite sign to this definition, by which \(\int\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = M\) at zero axial force. BARBER (10.9) (PDF p. 150, crop read) has \(\int\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = M_0\), like us.

What does the sign mean? At \(M > 0\) we have \(\int (r - c)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r > 0\), so the hoop stress on the outer half of the section (\(r > c\)) is larger, on average weighted by the distance from the centroid, than on the inner half: the outer fibres are more in tension, the inner ones more in compression, and the couples close the piece of the annulus, that is they increase its curvature. For the straight beam of chapter 5 it is the same: at \(M > 0\) we have \(v'' > 0\), the beam bends towards the positive direction of the axis \(y\) and the fibres on that side are compressed (figure 5.86). In the curved beam the axis \(y\), which forms a right-handed system with the axis of the beam \(\vek e_\theta\) (\(\vek e_\theta\times\vek e_{\mathrm{y}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\)), points towards the centre \(O\), so \(y = c - r\), and \(M = -\int y\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r\) is the same expression as in lemma 5.9. The elementary formula (5.25) at \(A = h\) and \(I = h^3/12\) gives for a beam that would be straight

\[\sigma_{\theta\theta} \approx \frac{N}{h} + \frac{12\,M}{h^3}\,(r - c) , \tag{11.94}\]

and this is the object of comparison in this section. On the right of figure 11.176 the formula at \(N = 0\) and \(M > 0\) is drawn over the radius \(r\). It is a straight line that passes through zero at the centroid, at \(r = c\). Its largest absolute value is at both edges, \(r - c = \pm h/2\): \(12\,|M|\,(h/2)/h^3 = 6\,|M|/h^2\), at the inner edge as compression and at the outer edge as tension.

11.14.2 Pure bending

First take a beam loaded at its ends by couples only, without force. The part of the beam between the end \(\theta_1\) and the section at \(\theta_0\) is in equilibrium: the field on it is the solution of the boundary value problem for this part with the data that it itself gives. In figure 11.178 we cut this part off from the rest of the beam at the angle \(\theta_0\). On it are the load of the end \(\theta_1\), the free arcs \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) and the stress vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) on the section at \(\theta_0\), with which the rest pulls or pushes the cut-off part. By lemma 11.2 the sum of the forces is zero. The load of the end has no force resultant, so \(N(\theta_0) = V(\theta_0) = 0\) on every section. With zero resultant the moment does not depend on the point about which we measure it (lemma 2.39). The end \(\theta_1\) has the outward normal \(-\vek e_\theta\), its stress vector is therefore \(-\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) and its moment is \(-M(\theta_1)\). The sum of this moment and the moment \(M(\theta_0)\) on the section is zero, so \(M(\theta_0) - M(\theta_1) = 0\): the moment is the same on every section, \(M(\theta_0) = M\). Since every section carries the same moment and no force and the beam is the same at every angle, we look for stresses that do not depend on the angle \(\theta\), that is for an Airy function \(\varphi(r)\). By lemma 11.51 such functions are combinations of the functions \(\ln r\), \(r^2\ln r\) and \(r^2\), that is the function of theorem 11.54. There the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\) is forbidden in a whole ring, because its displacement \(u_\theta = 4B\,r\,\theta/E\) is not equal to the initial one after a circuit around the hole. Our beam is a piece of an annulus with \(\theta_2 - \theta_1 < 2\pi\), where the points at \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) are different, so there is no jump here and the term is allowed.

Why is this term forbidden in a whole ring? After a circuit the angle \(\theta\) has grown by \(2\pi\) and the displacement \(u_\theta\) of the same point would have changed by \(8\pi B\,r/E\). The same point of the material would then have to be in two places at once, and between them a gap would open or the material would overlap. In a ring with a cut, however, the points at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = 2\pi\) are two different points, one on each face of the cut. The difference \(8\pi B\,r/E\) is there exactly the wedge into which the cut opens. Since it is proportional to \(r\), it is a rotation of one face against the other about the centre \(O\) by \(\Delta\varphi = 8\pi B/E\). The term that a whole ring forbids is thus the very one that bends a ring with a cut.

That the stresses do not depend on \(\theta\) was a guess. The guess is confirmed only by the theorem below, since item 4 says that the field found is the only one when the ends carry exactly the stresses \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(r)\) of this solution. In figure 11.179 these stresses at the end are drawn for the couple of example 11.61: tensile near the inner edge, compressive near the outer one, and not on a line. A couple at an end is usually applied differently, say by jaws that grip the end. In the figure such a load is simplified to two uniform bands at the edges that give the same moment. The difference of the two loads has zero resultant and zero moment, so it is self-equilibrated, and by Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) it dies out near the end. In a straight strip an antisymmetric difference, such as a couple gives, falls to about \(2\,\%\) already at a distance of half the height from the end (BARBER, PDF p. 98). A symmetric difference dies out more slowly, as \(\mathrm{e}^{-2{,}1\,x/(h/2)}\) at the distance \(x\) from the end (BARBER, PDF p. 99), so it falls to \(2\,\%\) only at \(x = (h/2)\ln 50/2{,}1 = 0{,}93\,h\). In our beam with the height \(h = 20\) mm this is about \(10\) mm, which along the axis of radius \(50\) mm is an angle of \(0{,}2\) rad, or about \(19\) mm and \(0{,}37\) rad. Farther from the ends the solution holds also for a couple applied in this way, closer to the ends it does not.

Theorem 11.60 Pure bending of a curved beam (čisti upogib ukrivljenega nosilca)

Let the curved beam be as in definition 11.59 (figure 11.177), of an isotropic, homogeneous, linearly elastic material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) in plane stress, without body force. Let \(M\) be a real number, \(L = \ln(b/a)\) and

\[\Delta = (b^2 - a^2)^2 - 4\,a^2 b^2 L^2 .\]

Then:

  1. \(\Delta > 0\) and the function \(\varphi = A\ln(r/a) + B\,r^2\ln(r/a) + C\,r^2\) with
    \[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{4M\,a^2 b^2 L}{\Delta} , \\ B &= \frac{2M\,(b^2 - a^2)}{\Delta} , \\ C &= \frac{M\,(a^2 - b^2 - 2b^2 L)}{\Delta} \end{aligned} \tag{11.95}\]
    is the only function of this form for which the faces \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) are free and \(M(\theta) = M\),
  2. the stresses given by \(\varphi\) are
    \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac{4M}{\Delta}\Big[\frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2}\,L + b^2\ln\frac rb \\ &\quad + a^2\ln\frac ar\Big] , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \frac{4M}{\Delta}\Big[-\frac{a^2 b^2}{r^2}\,L + b^2\ln\frac rb \\ &\quad + a^2\ln\frac ar + b^2 - a^2\Big] , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{11.96}\]
    do not depend on \(\theta\) (in figure 11.183 the hoop stress is a function of the radius only) and satisfy equilibrium,
  3. on every section \(N(\theta) = 0\), \(V(\theta) = 0\) and \(M(\theta) = M\), the faces \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) are free,
  4. the stress field (11.96) is the only stress field of the beam whose arcs are free and whose ends carry the stress vector \(\mp\sigma_{\theta\theta}(r)\,\vek e_\theta\) from item 2, and the displacement is unique up to a rigid motion,
  5. the sections \(\theta = \text{const}\) stay plane to first order (figure 11.175, right): the displacement \(u_\theta\) is a linear function of \(r\) on every section, the section at \(\theta\) turns by \(4B\,\theta/E + W\) with some rigid rotation \(W\), so the sections at \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) turn against each other by \(4B\,(\theta_2 - \theta_1)/E\).
Proof

We have to show that the constants (11.95) solve the three conditions, that the stresses satisfy equilibrium and that the displacement field exists. For the displacements we use theorem 11.54.

We write the logarithms with \(a\) (first step):

\[\ln r = \ln(r/a) + \ln a , \qquad r^2\ln r = r^2\ln(r/a) + r^2\ln a .\]

The function of theorem 11.54, with \(C_0\) at \(r^2\), is thus \(\varphi + A\ln a\), \(C = C_0 + B\ln a\) (second step). Stresses are derivatives (lemma 11.50), so \(A\ln a\) drops out, and by the theorem (third step)

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A/r^2 + B\,(1 + 2\ln(r/a)) + 2C , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = -A/r^2 + B\,(3 + 2\ln(r/a)) + 2C , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0 .\]
  1. By lemma 11.50, for \(\varphi(r)\) the stress is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \varphi'/r\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \varphi''\). The arcs \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) have the outward normal \(\mp\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the stress vector \(\mp(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_\theta)\), so they are free exactly when \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\) (the shear stress is zero everywhere). This is \(\varphi'(a) = \varphi'(b) = 0\), a horizontal tangent of the curve \(\varphi\) at both ends.
  2. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) the axial force on the section is \(N = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\varphi''\,\mathrm{d}r = \varphi'(b) - \varphi'(a) = 0\), the change of slope between the horizontal tangents. For the integral \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\,\varphi''\,\mathrm{d}r\) we have \((r\varphi')' = \varphi' + r\varphi''\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\varphi''\,\mathrm{d}r = \big[r\varphi'\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b - \big[\varphi\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b = \varphi(a) - \varphi(b)\). At \(N = 0\) the moment about the centroid is \(M(\theta) = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (r - c)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r - c\,N = \varphi(a) - \varphi(b)\). Free arcs and zero axial force therefore follow from the form of the function, and the condition remains that the difference in height of the ends \(\varphi(a) - \varphi(b)\) equals \(M\).
  3. The conditions are three linear equations for \(A\), \(B\), \(C\). From \(\varphi' = A/r + 2B\,r\ln(r/a) + B\,r + 2C\,r\) the condition \(\varphi'(a) = 0\) is \(A/a^2 + B + 2C = 0\) and \(\varphi'(b) = 0\) is \(A/b^2 + B\,(1 + 2L) + 2C = 0\). The difference of the two is \(A\,(1/b^2 - 1/a^2) + 2BL = 0\), so \(A = 2BL\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\). The first equation gives \(C = -\tfrac12\,(A/a^2 + B)\).
  4. The function has \(\varphi(a) = C\,a^2\) and \(\varphi(b) = A L + B\,b^2 L + C\,b^2\), so \(\varphi(a) - \varphi(b) = -AL - B\,b^2 L - C\,(b^2 - a^2)\). We insert \(C\) from step 3: \(M = A\big[\frac{b^2 - a^2}{2a^2} - L\big] + B\big[\frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} - b^2 L\big]\). With \(A\) from step 3 the first term is \(B L b^2 - 2BL^2 a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2)\), and the sum of both terms is \(M = B\big[\frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} - \frac{2a^2 b^2 L^2}{b^2 - a^2}\big] = \frac{B\,\Delta}{2\,(b^2 - a^2)}\).
  5. \(\Delta > 0\): we factor \(\Delta = (b^2 - a^2 - 2abL)(b^2 - a^2 + 2abL)\). The second factor is positive, because \(b > a\) and \(L > 0\). At \(s = b/a > 1\) the first factor is \(a^2 s\,k(s)\) with the function \(k(s) = s - 1/s - 2\ln s\), which has \(k(1) = 0\) and \(k'(s) = 1 + 1/s^2 - 2/s = (1 - 1/s)^2 > 0\) for \(s > 1\). By the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) \(k(s) - k(1) = k'(\xi)\,(s - 1) > 0\), so \(k(s) > 0\) and \(\Delta > 0\). Therefore step 4 gives \(B = 2M\,(b^2 - a^2)/\Delta\), step 3 gives \(A = 4M\,a^2 b^2 L/\Delta\) and \(C = M\,(a^2 - b^2 - 2b^2 L)/\Delta\), and all steps were equivalent transformations. This is item 1.
  6. We insert into the stresses. For \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) the sum is
    \[\begin{gathered} \frac{M}{\Delta}\big[\frac{4a^2 b^2 L}{r^2} + 2(b^2 - a^2)\,(1 + 2\ln\frac ra) + 2(a^2 - b^2 - 2b^2 L)\big] \\ = \frac{4M}{\Delta}\big[\frac{a^2 b^2 L}{r^2} + (b^2 - a^2)\ln\frac ra - b^2 L\big] \end{gathered}\]
    and the last two terms are \(b^2\ln\frac rb + a^2\ln\frac ar\), because \(\ln\frac ra - L = \ln\frac rb\). For \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) the sum is \(3B + 2C = \frac{M}{\Delta}\,(4(b^2 - a^2) - 4b^2 L)\), logarithms likewise. This is (11.96). Equilibrium and \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) hold by item 1 of theorem 11.54.
  7. Item 3: \(N = 0\) and \(M(\theta) = M\) by step 2, \(V = 0\) because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). The arcs are free by step 1.
  8. Item 4. The stresses are continuous, they satisfy equilibrium without body force (step 6), the arcs are free and the ends carry the prescribed stress vector, so the field is a solution of the traction problem (definition 11.1 with empty \(S_{\mathrm{u}}\)). If any solution has the same data, it has by theorem 11.4 the same stresses, and the displacement differs by a small rigid motion. That the displacement exists is shown by theorem 11.54, item 2: the field (11.83) satisfies all three strain equations identically (checked with SymPy) and on a piece with \(\theta_2 - \theta_1 < 2\pi\) it is a function of the point.
  9. Item 5. From (11.83) the displacement on the section \(\theta = \theta_0\) is \(u_\theta = (4B\,\theta_0/E + W)\,r - Q\sin\theta_0 + P\cos\theta_0\), a linear function of \(r\) (the terms with \(Q\) and \(P\) are constant at given \(\theta_0\)). The point with radius \(r\) therefore moves by \(u_{\mathrm{r}}(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + u_\theta(r)\,\vek e_\theta\). The radial part moves it only along the section, and the tangential part \(u_\theta\) is a linear function of the original \(r\). Expressed with the new radius \(r + u_{\mathrm{r}}\) it differs from a linear function by a product of two small quantities (the rotation and \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\)), which we neglect to first order. For the ring of example 11.61 the rotation is at most \(6{,}0\cdot 10^{-3}\) rad and \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) about \(0{,}047\) mm, and their product \(3\cdot 10^{-4}\) mm is a thousandth of the displacement \(u_\theta\), which is up to \(0{,}36\) mm there. So the section stays plane and turns by \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r = 4B\,\theta_0/E + W\). The difference of the rotations at \(\theta_2\) and \(\theta_1\) is \(4B\,(\theta_2 - \theta_1)/E\).

BARBER §10.1.1, (10.1)-(10.16) (PDF pp. 149 to 151). The form (11.96) with logarithms was checked with SymPy (the difference of the two forms is zero) and with the form from TIMO in rbe.py part J. The sign of \(M\) is ours (definition 11.59), SADD has \(\int\sigma_\theta\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = -M\), BARBER (10.9) has \(\int\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = M_0\) as we do. The proof showed three things. The first two are shown by figure 11.180 for the ring of example 11.61. On the horizontal axis is the radius \(r\) from \(a = 40\) to \(b = 60\) mm, on the vertical one the difference \(\varphi(r) - \varphi(a)\) in N. The curve has a horizontal tangent at both ends, these are the conditions \(\varphi'(a) = \varphi'(b) = 0\) of the free arcs from step 1. The axial force \(N = \varphi'(b) - \varphi'(a)\) of step 2 is the change of slope from one end to the other. Both tangents are horizontal, so \(N = 0\) without a new condition. The moment gives only the difference in height of the ends, \(\varphi(a) - \varphi(b) = M\). For \(M = -2500\) N the curve rises by \(2500\) N from \(a\) to \(b\). The second derivative \(\varphi''\) is the hoop stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\). It is tensile near the inner edge, so the curve bends upwards to the left of \(r_0\), and compressive near the outer edge, so it bends downwards to the right of \(r_0\). At the inflection at \(r_0 = 49{,}33\) mm we have \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 0\).

The third thing is that the exact solution keeps the sections plane like the elementary theory, but the distribution of stress is different. Step 9 of the proof shows this in figure 11.181. The section before loading is a row of points at radii from \(a\) to \(b\), \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) points along it, \(\vek e_\theta\) perpendicular to it. The tangential displacement \(u_\theta\) is a linear function of \(r\), so the tips of the blue arrows lie on a line inclined by the rotation \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r\). Then the radial displacement \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) moves each point along \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is horizontally, and takes it away from the inclined line. For a point on the outer edge the departure is the short side of a thin triangle whose long side is \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and whose slope is \(\partial u_\theta/\partial r\), so the departure is \((\partial u_\theta/\partial r)\,u_{\mathrm{r}}\). If \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) were the same everywhere, all points would be the same distance aside from the line and the section after loading would again be plane. Because \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) changes a little over the section, the section bends, but at most by this product. In the figure both small quantities are strongly enlarged, so the bending is visible. In the ring of example 11.61 the product is \(6{,}0\cdot 10^{-3}\cdot 0{,}047\) mm, about \(3\cdot 10^{-4}\) mm, a thousandth of the displacement \(u_\theta\), so we neglect it to first order.

Why is the inner fibre loaded more? We now measure the idea from the start of the section. On the right of figure 11.175 the sections enclose an angle \(\Delta\theta\). Since they stay plane (item 5 of the theorem), the displacement of the points of the section along the axis varies linearly with the radius, so the fibre at radius \(r\) is lengthened by \(\Delta\omega\,(r - r_0)\), where \(\Delta\omega\) is the relative rotation of the sections and \(r_0\) the radius of the fibre without elongation. The length of the fibre before loading is \(r\,\Delta\theta\), so the strain is

\[\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} \approx \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta\theta}\,\frac{r - r_0}{r} .\]

In a straight beam the denominator would be constant, \(\ell\), here it is \(r\), so the elongation of the inner fibre (\(r = 40\)) is spread over a shorter length than the shortening of the outer one (\(r = 60\)). In the exact solution \(r\,\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = r\,(\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E\) is a linear function of \(r\) to within \(1{,}5\,\%\) of its range (SymPy, at \(\nu = 0{,}3\)), so the idea holds. The rotation of the sections is therefore almost exact in the elementary theory, but the stress at the edge is not.

The radial stresses, which the elementary formula does not contain, have their cause in equilibrium. The first equilibrium equation in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.25) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) and independence of \(\theta\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\), which is \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\). At the inner edge \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\). As long as \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} > 0\) (inner fibres, tension), \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) grows, that is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is tensile. A fibre in hoop tension is curved and pulls towards the centre of curvature \(O\), so it separates from the layer outside it, which is radial tension.

Why does a curved fibre in tension pull towards the centre? On the left of figure 11.184 is a straight piece of fibre, pulled at both ends by a force \(T\) along the fibre. The two forces lie on the same line, are opposite and cancel, so the piece feels no transverse force. In the middle of the figure is a short piece of a curved fibre between radii that enclose the angle \(\Delta\theta\). The forces \(T\) at its ends point along the fibre, that is perpendicular to the radii, and differ in direction by \(\Delta\theta\). When we add them in the triangle of forces, we get a sum that points towards the centre \(O\) and has the size \(2T\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\), at small \(\Delta\theta\) approximately \(T\,\Delta\theta\). The sum pulls the piece inwards, so the layer outside it must hold it with a radial tensile stress. This is the content of the equation \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\), which we now show without approximations. Let the piece of fibre lie between the radii \(r\) and \(r + \Delta r\). At each end the stress \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) acts along the fibre, so the force \(T\) per unit thickness of the plate is \(T = \int_{\mathrm{r}}^{r+\Delta r}\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\), the same at both ends, because the stress does not depend on \(\theta\). On the face at radius \(r\) acts the radial stress \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\), the same along the whole arc of length \(r\,\Delta\theta\). The component of its sum along the bisector of the angle is \(\int r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\cos(\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{s}})\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\), where \(\theta_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the direction of the bisector and the integral runs over the angle \(\Delta\theta\) around it, while the transverse components from the two halves of the arc cancel. Equilibrium of the piece along the bisector is therefore \(2T\sin(\Delta\theta/2) = 2\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\,\big[(r + \Delta r)\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r + \Delta r) - r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(r)\big]\): the inward sum of the forces \(T\) equals the difference of the radial forces on the outer and the inner face. The factor \(2\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\) cancels, so the equality holds exactly at every angle, not only at a small one. We divide it by \(\Delta r\) and let \(\Delta r\) go to zero: by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55) the left side tends to \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}(r)\), the right side to \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})'\). In a straight beam the forces cancel, so the elementary theory needs no radial stress.

The equation \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) at the inner edge say, by the fundamental theorem of calculus (theorem 3.55), that \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) at the radius \(r\) is the area under the curve of the hoop stress from \(40\) mm to \(r\). In figure 11.185 the top shows the hoop stress of the exact solution over the radius axis, and the bottom the curve \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), which the lower vertical axis measures in N per mm of thickness. As long as the hoop stress is tensile, the area under it grows, so \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) grows. At \(r_0 = 49{,}33\) mm, where the hoop stress changes sign, \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \int_{40}^{r_0}\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = 187{,}45\) N per mm of thickness. From there on the hoop stress is compressive and its area is subtracted. At the outer edge the sum of the two areas is \(\int_{40}^{60}\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = N = 0\) (step 2 of the proof), so there \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is zero again. A free outer edge is therefore, with a free inner edge, the same condition as \(N = 0\). The peak \(187{,}45\) N/mm is the largest value of \(r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is largest a little earlier because of the division by \(r\), \(3{,}84\) MPa at \(r = 48{,}3\) mm (SymPy), which is \(8{,}9\,\%\) of the largest hoop stress. For a couple of opposite sign \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is compressive everywhere. In the elementary theory the fibres are in uniaxial stress (assumption 6 of chapter 5), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\).

From history

Navier and Bresse calculated displacements and stresses of curved bars by formulas derived for straight prismatic bars. TIMO says that this way is adequate when the dimensions of the cross section are small in comparison with the radius of curvature of the axis, and that in hooks, rings, chains and links this condition is not fulfilled, so the formulas for straight bars are not reliable enough (TIMO, PDF p. 161). Winkler published his paper on curved bars soon after finishing his studies, in the year 1858 according to a footnote in TIMO (TIMO, PDF p. 160), and his main contribution to strength of materials is the theory of bending of curved bars (TIMO, PDF p. 161). Winkler kept the assumption that cross sections stay plane in bending, but took into account that, because of the initial curvature, the longitudinal fibres between two sections have different lengths, so the stresses are no longer proportional to the distance from the neutral axis and this axis does not pass through the centroid of the section (TIMO, PDF p. 161). He applied his theory to stresses in hooks, rings of various sections and chain links and showed that the elementary bending formula for straight beams loses its value when the dimensions of the section are not small in comparison with the radius \(r\), and that the new theory is then needed (TIMO, PDF p. 162). Winkler therefore kept plane sections and assumed that the longitudinal fibres do not press upon each other (TIMO, PDF p. 161), so in his theory there are no radial stresses. Golovin, however, was interested in the deformations and stresses of circular arches of constant thickness, treated the problem as a two-dimensional problem of the theory of elasticity and solved it for pure bending and for bending by a force at the end (TIMO, PDF p. 290, figure 209). In pure bending he found that the cross sections stay plane, as the elementary theory assumes, but that his stress distribution differs from the elementary one, because the elementary theory assumes that the longitudinal fibres are in simple tension or compression only, while Golovin shows that there are also radial stresses (TIMO, PDF p. 290). The work appeared in 1880 and 1881 (TIMO, PDF p. 290, footnote). For a force at the end there are, besides normal stresses, also shear stresses on every section and their distribution is not parabolic, as the elementary theory assumes (TIMO, PDF p. 290). Golovin also computed the deflection of arches and with it solved a statically indeterminate arch with built-in ends, and for usual proportions of arches the elementary theory is accurate enough for practice (TIMO, PDF p. 290), which we will see in this section in numbers for the ratio \(b/a\). TIMO calls Golovin's work the first attempt to use the theory of elasticity in the stress analysis of arches (TIMO, PDF p. 290).

We asked whether the formula \(\sigma = -My/I\) holds for the curved beam made of the ring with \(a = 40\) mm and \(b = 60\) mm. In pure bending it does not hold exactly: at the same moment the stress at the inner edge is \(15{,}5\,\%\) larger, at the outer one \(11{,}7\,\%\) smaller, the neutral fibre moves by \(0{,}67\) mm towards the centre and a radial stress of up to \(3{,}84\) MPa appears. The sections, however, stay plane and the rotation is almost the one that the formula gives. We now test the same on a beam loaded by a force.

11.14.3 A cantilever with a force at its end

Pure bending has the same moment on every section and no force. A curved beam with a force at its end has on the section also an axial and a shear force, and the moment is proportional to the distance of the centroid of the section from the line of the force. Take a quarter of the annulus \(a \le r \le b\), \(0 \le \theta \le \pi/2\). At the end \(\theta = 0\) acts a force in the direction of the radius that passes through the centre \(O\), at the other end the beam is clamped (figure 11.190). Let the force per unit thickness be \(P\) and let it point in the direction \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (\(P > 0\) pulls the end away from \(O\)).

What must the section carry? In figure 11.186 is the piece of the beam between the end \(\theta = 0\) and the section at angle \(\theta\), the rest up to the clamp is drawn grey dashed. On the end acts the force \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) along the line through \(O\). The piece is at rest, so the sum of the forces on it is zero (lemma 11.2). The arcs are free, so the section must carry the resultant \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\), which we draw at the centroid of the section \(C\). In the basis \((\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek e_\theta)\) this vector has the components \(-P\cos\theta\) and \(P\sin\theta\), because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \cos\theta\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = -\sin\theta\). These are the axial force \(N = P\sin\theta\) along \(\vek e_\theta\) and the shear force \(V = P\cos\theta\) in the direction \(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), towards the centre. The sum of the moments on the piece is zero as well. The line of the force passes the centroid \(C\) at the distance \(c\sin\theta\), so the force has about \(C\) a moment of size \(c\,P\sin\theta\). This moment turns anticlockwise, about \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\), because the force pulls to the right below the centroid \(C\). The section must balance it with a couple that turns clockwise. By definition 11.59 \(M\) is the \(\vek e_{\mathrm{z}}\) component of this couple, so \(M = -c\,P\sin\theta\) is negative. Such a couple opens the piece, since a couple with \(M > 0\) closes it. At \(\theta = 0\) the lever arm is zero and the section carries only the shear force, at \(\theta = \pi/2\) the lever arm is the whole \(c\) and there is no shear force. We therefore expect stresses that depend on the angle as \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\). By lemma 11.51, item 2, the functions \(r^3\sin\theta\), \(r^{-1}\sin\theta\), \(r\sin\theta\) and \(r\ln r\,\sin\theta\) are biharmonic, so we look for the solution among their combinations.

Theorem 11.62 Curved cantilever with an end force (ukrivljena konzola s silo na koncu)

Let the curved beam be as in definition 11.59 with \(\theta_1 = 0\) and \(\theta_2 = \pi/2\), in plane stress, without body force. Let \(P\) be a real number, \(L = \ln(b/a)\) and \(K = (b^2 - a^2) - (a^2 + b^2)\,L\). Then:

  1. \(K < 0\) and the function \(\varphi = (A\,r^3 + B/r + D\,r\ln r)\sin\theta\) with
    \[A = \frac{P}{2K} , \qquad B = -\frac{P\,a^2 b^2}{2K} , \qquad D = -\frac{P\,(a^2 + b^2)}{K} \tag{11.97}\]
    is biharmonic and is the only function of this form for which the arcs \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) are free and the resultant of the stress vector on the end \(\theta = 0\) is \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\),
  2. the stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = g(r)\sin\theta\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = h(r)\sin\theta\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -g(r)\cos\theta\) with \(g(r) = 2A\,r - 2B/r^3 + D/r\) and \(h(r) = 6A\,r + 2B/r^3 + D/r\),
  3. the end \(\theta = 0\) carries only the radial stress vector \(g(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the internal forces of the section at every \(\theta\) are (definition 11.59)
    \[N(\theta) = P\,\sin\theta , \qquad V(\theta) = P\,\cos\theta , \qquad M(\theta) = -c\,P\,\sin\theta , \tag{11.98}\]
  4. the field is the only stress field of the beam with free arcs that has on the end \(\theta = 0\) the stress vector \(g(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and on the end \(\theta = \pi/2\) the stress vector \(\ten\sigma\,\vek e_\theta\) from item 2, and the displacement is unique up to a rigid motion.
Proof

We have to show that the constants (11.97) solve the three conditions and that we get exactly these stresses and section quantities.

  1. Each of the functions \(r^3\sin\theta\), \(r^{-1}\sin\theta\) and \(r\ln r\,\sin\theta\) is biharmonic (lemma 11.51, item 2). The function \(r\sin\theta = y\) has all second derivatives zero and gives no stresses, so it is omitted.
  2. Let \(f(r) = A\,r^3 + B/r + D\,r\ln r\) and \(\varphi = f(r)\sin\theta\). By lemma 11.50 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = (f'/r - f/r^2)\sin\theta\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = f''\sin\theta\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -(f/r)'\cos\theta\). From \(f' = 3Ar^2 - B/r^2 + D\ln r + D\) we get
    \[\begin{gathered} f'/r - f/r^2 = (3Ar - B/r^3 + D\ln r/r + D/r) - (Ar + B/r^3 + D\ln r/r) \\ = 2Ar - 2B/r^3 + D/r = g . \end{gathered}\]
    From \(f'' = 6Ar + 2B/r^3 + D/r\) we get \(h\) and from \(f/r = Ar^2 + B/r^2 + D\ln r\) we get \((f/r)' = 2Ar - 2B/r^3 + D/r = g\). This is item 2.
  3. The arcs \(r = a\) and \(r = b\) are free exactly when the stress vector \(\mp(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_\theta) = \mp g\,(\sin\theta\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} - \cos\theta\,\vek e_\theta)\) is zero there. Both components are proportional to \(g\) on the arc, so this is exactly \(g(a) = g(b) = 0\). If we draw \(g\) over the radius from \(a\) to \(b\), these are the zeros of the curve at both edges. The end \(\theta = 0\) has the outward normal \(-\vek e_\theta\) and the stress vector \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = g(r)\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), because \(\sin 0 = 0\) and \(\cos 0 = 1\). The resultant \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) means \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\mathrm{d}r = P\), that is the area under the curve \(g\).
  4. The conditions \(g(a) = 0\), \(g(b) = 0\) and \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b g\,\mathrm{d}r = P\) are, after multiplying the first two by \(a\) and \(b\), the equations \(2Aa^2 - 2B/a^2 + D = 0\), \(2Ab^2 - 2B/b^2 + D = 0\) and \(A\,(b^2 - a^2) + B\,(1/b^2 - 1/a^2) + DL = P\). The difference of the first two is \(2A\,(a^2 - b^2) = 2B\,(1/a^2 - 1/b^2)\), so \(B = -A\,a^2 b^2\), and from the first \(D = -2Aa^2 + 2B/a^2 = -2A\,(a^2 + b^2)\). The third gives \(B\,(1/b^2 - 1/a^2) = A\,(b^2 - a^2)\) and \(P = 2A\,(b^2 - a^2) - 2A\,(a^2 + b^2)\,L = 2A\,K\).
  5. \(K < 0\): at \(s = b/a > 1\) the inequality \(K < 0\) is \(\ln s > (s^2 - 1)/(s^2 + 1)\). The function \(m(s) = \ln s - (s^2 - 1)/(s^2 + 1)\) has \(m(1) = 0\) and \(m'(s) = 1/s - 4s/(s^2 + 1)^2 = (s^2 - 1)^2/(s\,(s^2 + 1)^2) > 0\) for \(s > 1\), so by the mean value theorem (theorem 1.85) \(m(s) > 0\). Since \(K \ne 0\), the system of step 4 has exactly one solution, which is (11.97), and all steps were equivalent transformations. This proves item 1.
  6. Item 3. The field satisfies equilibrium without body force (theorem 11.40), so the piece of the beam from the end \(\theta = 0\) to the section at \(\theta_0\) is in equilibrium and by lemma 11.2 the stress vector on its surface has resultant zero and moment about \(O\) zero. The arcs are free, the end \(\theta = 0\) has the stress vector \(g\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) with resultant \(P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and moment about \(O\) zero, because the position vector of the point is parallel to \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (the line of the force passes through \(O\)). The section at \(\theta_0\) therefore has resultant \(-P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) and moment about \(O\) zero. The components of the resultant are \(N = -P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot\vek e_\theta = P\sin\theta_0\) and \(V = -P\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\cdot(-\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}) = P\cos\theta_0\). The moment about \(O\) is the \(z\) component of \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\times(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\vek e_\theta + \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}})\,\mathrm{d}r = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b r\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = 0\). By definition 11.59 \(M = \int_{\mathrm{a}}^b (r - c)\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = 0 - c\,N = -cP\sin\theta_0\).
  7. Item 4 like item 4 of theorem 11.60: the field is a solution of the traction problem (free arcs, prescribed ends) that corresponds to the equilibrium of the whole body (step 6), and by theorem 11.4 it is the only one up to a rigid motion.

Source: SADD §8.4.12, (8.4.62)-(8.4.64) (PDF pp. 212 to 215, crops (8.4.62) and (8.4.64) read). SADD writes the conditions at the ends with the forces \(P\) at \(\theta = 0\) and \(-P\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(\int\tau_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(r, 0)\,\mathrm{d}r = P\), and we reversed the sign of \(P\) so that \(P\) points in the direction \(+\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) (our convention, all stress fields agree up to the sign of \(P\), checked in rbe.py part J2). BARBER §10.1.2 (PDF pp. 152 to 153). Step 6 (section quantities from the equilibrium of the whole piece) and step 5 (\(K < 0\)) are ours.

Here too the zeros \(g(a) = g(b) = 0\) for the free arcs mean two conditions for three constants, and the area under \(g\), which is the force on the end, gives the third. The moment of the section is not a condition but a consequence of equilibrium (step 6): the couple \(M\) balances the moment of the force with the lever arm \(c\sin\theta\).

As in pure bending, the solution is exact for one loading of the ends only: the end \(\theta = 0\) must carry the force exactly as the shear stress \(-g(r)\), and the clamped section exactly the stresses of item 2. So at the clamp too the theorem prescribes forces, not displacements.

But the force is usually applied differently. In figure 11.188, top left, over the radius from \(40\) to \(60\) mm is drawn the exact distribution \(g(r)\), and next to it a uniform shear stress \(P/h = 2{,}5\) MPa, which carries the same force \(50\) N/mm. Both act in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\) on the end, which lies on the line \(y = 0\) through \(O\), so the moment of both about \(O\) is zero. Their difference \(g - P/h\) therefore has moment zero and resultant zero, since the area above the axis, \(9{,}9\) N/mm, equals the sum of the areas below it, \(3{,}3 + 6{,}6\) N/mm. The difference is thus self-equilibrated. A real clamp, which prevents the displacements, also gives at the clamp stresses different from item 2. The difference is self-equilibrated there too, because the equilibrium of the whole beam prescribes to the clamp a resultant and a moment that are the same for both loads of the end. By Saint-Venant's principle (section 11.11) both differences die out near the ends. Since the peak of the exact curve is not at the middle of the section and the two areas below the axis are not equal, the difference is not mirror symmetric about the middle of the section, so it decays at both rates of section 11.14.2. The faster part falls to \(2\,\%\) within about \(10\) mm, that is \(0{,}2\) rad along the axis of radius \(50\) mm, the slower one only within about \(19\) mm, that is \(0{,}37\) rad, both at both ends. The quarter of the annulus spans \(\pi/2 \approx 1{,}57\) rad, so there remains a middle arc of length \(1{,}57 - 2\cdot 0{,}37 = 0{,}83\) rad, more than half of the beam, on which the solution holds no matter how the ends are loaded in detail. The numbers for the clamped section in the example below are therefore numbers for a section that carries the axial force \(P\) and the moment \(-c\,P\), not a prediction for the material right at a real clamp. Let us test it on the ring.

11.14.4 How far the elementary formula holds

So when may we use the formula of the straight beam? The answer depends on the ratio \(b/a\) or, equivalently, on the ratio of the radius of the axis to the height, \(c/h\). For pure bending at every \(b/a\) we compute the stress at the inner and the outer edge from theorem 11.60 and divide it by the elementary value \(6\,|M|/h^2\). The quotients \(f_{\mathrm{a}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}(a)/(6|M|/h^2)\) and \(f_{\mathrm{b}} = \sigma_{\theta\theta}(b)/(6|M|/h^2)\) (in absolute value, the inner one in tension and the outer one in compression at \(M < 0\)) are equal to \(1\) in the elementary formula. For the ring (\(b/a = 1{,}5\)) of example 11.61 they are \(1{,}155\) and \(0{,}883\). Figure 11.191 shows \(f_{\mathrm{a}}\) and \(f_{\mathrm{b}}\) on the vertical axis against \(b/a\) from \(1{,}1\) to \(4\) on the horizontal one.

The elementary formula at the inner edge falls below \(5\,\%\) deviation if \(b/a \le 1{,}153\), that is \(c/h \ge 7{,}0\), below \(10\,\%\) at \(b/a \le 1{,}31\) (SymPy, bisection). At the outer edge the limits are \(1{,}17\) and \(1{,}40\). BARBER says that the elementary theory deviates significantly from the exact solution when \(a/b < 0{,}5\), that is \(b/a > 2\), and that the theory of curved beams, which keeps plane sections but takes into account the different lengths of the fibres, is much better (BARBER, PDF p. 151). At \(b/a = 2\) the elementary formula at the inner edge is, by our table, \(29\,\%\) too small. At \(b/a = 4\) SADD compares the exact solution with the theory of curved beams from strength of materials in its appendix D.4, which keeps plane sections and has a nonlinear distribution of stress, and says that the differences are very small (SADD, PDF pp. 212 and 581 to 583). This theory is not the formula of the straight beam with which we compare: the exact solution gives at \(b/a = 4\) the quotients \(1{,}727\) and \(0{,}719\) relative to the formula of the straight beam.

Hooks and chain links have a large ratio \(b/a\): a hook with inner radius equal to the height of the section (\(a = h\), \(b/a = 2\)) has at the inner edge a stress \(29\,\%\) larger than the elementary one, a chain link with \(a = h/2\) (\(b/a = 3\)) one \(53\,\%\) larger. This is why TIMO names precisely hooks, rings, chains and links as examples where the formulas of the straight beam do not suffice (TIMO, PDF p. 161). For the cantilever of example 11.63 the deviation of the hoop stress at the clamp (by \(15{,}3\,\%\) and \(12{,}2\,\%\)) is similar to pure bending. The largest shear stress agrees with the elementary one to within \(1{,}5\,\%\), but its distribution differs from the parabola by up to \(17\,\%\).

This section solved the curved beam with the Airy function in polar coordinates. Pure bending gives with \(\varphi(r)\) stresses independent of the angle, determined by the free arcs and the moment, and the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\), which uniqueness of the displacement forbids in a whole ring, is allowed in a piece of an annulus (theorem 11.60). The cantilever with an end force gives stresses that vary as \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) (theorem 11.62). For the ring with \(a = 40\) mm and \(b = 60\) mm the elementary formula at the inner edge underestimates the stress by \(15{,}5\,\%\) in bending and by \(15{,}3\,\%\) under the force and overestimates the outer edge by \(11{,}7\,\%\) and \(12{,}2\,\%\), a radial stress of up to \(3{,}84\) MPa in bending and up to \(3{,}80\) MPa under the force appears, and the formula gets the rotation of the sections right to within \(0{,}54\,\%\). The formula holds to within \(5\,\%\) at \(b/a \le 1{,}15\). The answer to the question from the beginning is therefore: not exactly, but plane sections are a good model of the deformation, and at small radii of curvature we must compute the stress from the exact solution or from the theory of curved beams.
