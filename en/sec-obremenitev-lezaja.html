Chapter 25 Hertz contact and rolling bearings

25.7 Load on a rolling bearing

A shaft supported by a bearing presses on it with a force. In a bearing with nine steel balls of diameter \(D = 12{,}7\) mm let the shaft press with a force \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N perpendicular to the axis. Does every ball carry a ninth of this force, that is \(111{,}1\) N? The balls on the upper side of the shaft cannot carry it: a ball can only support the shaft, it cannot pull it down. So only some balls will carry force, most of all the one under the shaft. How much exactly the most loaded ball carries decides which contact we must compute by section 25.6 and how stiffly the bearing supports the shaft (section 25.8). This section therefore first builds a model of the bearing, then derives from the contact of two bodies the law between the force of a ball and its displacement, and at the end computes the distribution of the force among the balls from the equilibrium of the ring.

Let us draw the bearing as we see it when cut perpendicular to the axis. First the outer ring. It is fixed in the housing and stands still. Its inner side is the raceway (steza), because the balls run along it like runners on a track. Between the rings are nine balls. They roll on the raceways, so friction is small, and this is why the bearing is called rolling (kotalni, from kotaliti, to roll). The balls must not collide. So a cage (kletka) holds them, placing them at equal angular spacings and leaving them free to roll. It is named for the way it closes the balls in like bars. We do not draw the cage in the figure, since it only keeps the spacings. From the middle the balls are touched by the inner ring, on which the shaft is mounted. The bearing (ležaj) is named for the shaft lying (ležati) on it and resting against it.

The shaft acts on it with the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), which we draw from the centre of the inner ring downward, because the shaft pushes the ring there. The load (obremenitev) is the force with which the bearing is loaded from outside, named for the burden (breme). The index \(r\) says that the force is radial (radialna), that is perpendicular to the axis. Under this force the rings are in equilibrium (ravnovesje), that is, the sum of all forces on them is zero and they do not accelerate. We will use the equilibrium below.

Under the force the inner ring shifts downward by a distance \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) toward the outer one, drawn with edge marks between the centres of the two rings. The grey dashed circle is its outer edge before the shift, concentric with the outer ring. The ball under the axis is squeezed between the rings, and the ball at the top gets room. For each ball we need the angle \(\psi\) that its axis measures from the vertical along which the force acts. The balls in the load zone are squeezed, in the figure from \(-\psi_{\mathrm{l}}\) to \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}}\).

Before we write the definition, let us draw one ball on the line of the force between the raceways, which we draw as straight lines, and show what the clearance is. On the left is the unloaded bearing: the ball lies between the raceways, and the gaps above and below it have together the width \(P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). This is half of the clearance, since the diametral clearance \(P_{\mathrm{d}}\) divides over two balls at opposite ends of a diameter. In the middle the raceway shifts by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} < P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), the gap narrows, but the ball is not yet pressed. On the right the shift is larger than \(P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\): the gap is used up and the raceways press on the ball with the approach \(\delta_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{r}} - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\).

Definition 25.32 Radial rolling-element bearing (radialni kotalni ležaj)

A radial ball bearing has an inner and an outer ring, between them \(Z\) equal balls of diameter \(D\) and a cage that places the balls at equal angular spacings \(2\pi/Z\). The rings have raceways along which the balls roll. In an unloaded bearing in which the axes of both rings coincide, the diametral clearance (diametralna zračnost) \(P_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 0\) is the difference between the diameter of the outer raceway and the sum of the diameter of the inner raceway and two ball diameters. The angle \(\alpha\) between the radial plane and the line along which the ball pushes on the rings is the contact angle (stični kot). For a radial bearing \(\alpha = 0\). The force \(Q\) with which a ring presses on a ball at the point of contact and perpendicular to the surface is the rolling-element load (obremenitev kroglice). The force with which the shaft loads the bearing perpendicular to the axis is the radial load \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the force along the axis is the axial load (osna obremenitev) \(F_{\mathrm{a}}\).

Source: The notions follow FPGL section 5.5.3 (PDF p. 201-202), which lists the rings, raceways, balls and cage, and HK section 7.1 (PDF p. 155). The clearance and the contact angle follow HK (PDF p. 156 and 160). The claims are not proved, the definition only fixes the names.

The model we use has a number of simplifications, which we name so that they do not drop out of sight.

  • The rings are rigid: they do not deform, they move as a whole. Real rings bend. This neglects the compliance of the housing and the ring. The assumption is good when the inner ring sits on a solid shaft and the outer ring in a massive housing, which support them along the whole circumference. The standards for bearing ratings assume such a mounting too (HK, PDF p. 270). It fails in light housings, where the deflection of the housing under the most loaded ball is comparable with the shift of the ring, which for our bearing at \(1000\) N is \(11{,}54\) µm (section 25.8). Then the load zone widens and the largest ball load is smaller than the model gives. Our sources do not give the size of this difference: HK (PDF p. 271) lists flexible supports of the rings among the conditions treated only in the second volume.
  • There is no friction and no inertia of the balls: the forces in the contact are perpendicular to the surface, the balls are in equilibrium. HK (PDF p. 155) justifies this by noting that rotation speeds are usually not so high that the inertial forces of the balls would affect the distribution. In a fast bearing the centrifugal force would change the load on the outer raceway, which chapter 24 treats. How large is this force in our bearing? A ball of mass \(m\) that circles the axis with angular speed \(\omega_{\mathrm{k}}\) at the radius \(R_{\mathrm{p}}\) presses on the outer raceway with the additional centrifugal force \(m R_{\mathrm{p}}\,\omega_{\mathrm{k}}^2\). Our steel ball (\(D = 12{,}7\) mm, density \(7800\) kg/m\(^3\)) has \(m = 8{,}37\) g, and we take the radius of the circle of ball centres \(R_{\mathrm{p}} = 23\) mm (assumed). At \(\omega_{\mathrm{k}} = 100\) rad/s, that is \(955\) turns of the cage per minute, the force is \(1{,}9\) N, which is \(0{,}4\,\%\) of the largest ball load \(485{,}6\) N computed below, and we neglect it. The force grows with the square of the speed: at ten times the speed it is \(192\) N and the assumption no longer holds. For friction forces, too, HK (PDF p. 155) finds that they usually do not change the distribution noticeably. Their size is estimated by the friction torque of the bearing due to load, which HK gives after Palmgren's measurements as \(M_{\mathrm{l}} = f_1 F_\beta d_{\mathrm{m}}\) (equations (10.18) to (10.20), PDF pp. 204 and 205). For a radial ball bearing \(F_\beta = F_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(d_{\mathrm{m}} = 2R_{\mathrm{p}} = 46\) mm is the diameter of the circle of ball centres, and \(f_1 \le 0{,}0006\) as long as the load does not exceed the static rating of the bearing. The friction forces at the inner raceway give this torque at the radius \(R_{\mathrm{p}} - D/2 = 16{,}65\) mm, so when they all resist the rotation they add up to \(M_{\mathrm{l}}/16{,}65\ \text{mm} \le 0{,}0006 \cdot 46/16{,}65\,F_{\mathrm{r}} = 0{,}17\,\%\) of \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), that is \(1{,}7\) N at \(1000\) N, and they do not change the equilibrium of the ring noticeably.
  • The contact of a ball and a raceway is a non-conforming contact by definition 25.18, its stresses and stiffness are described by the Hertz solution of section 25.6. Lubrication in the contact, which is outside this chapter, does not appear in the model, and friction is estimated by the previous item.
  • The load is static: the shaft is at rest or turns slowly enough that we can compute the equilibrium. The measure of "slowly enough" is in the second item: the centrifugal force of a ball must be small beside its load.

A ball in the bearing has two contacts, with the inner and with the outer raceway. Before we compute what force it carries, we must know which force corresponds to which displacement. This is the force-approach law for one contact.

Let us draw a ball of radius \(D/2\) and squeeze it between two walls. The lower inner raceway approaches the ball by \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\), the outer one by \(\delta_{\mathrm{o}}\). Both displacements are measured from the place where the walls touched, so the total approach of the two raceways is the sum \(\delta = \delta_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta_{\mathrm{o}}\). The ball is in equilibrium under two forces. There is no friction, so each of the two forces is perpendicular to the surface of the sphere and passes through its centre. In equilibrium the sum of the forces is zero, so the forces are equal in size and opposite in direction. Since both pass through the centre, they lie on the same line through both points of contact. This is the same force \(Q\) in both contacts.

What is the law of a single contact? For a spring we know \(F = kx\). For a Hertz contact the force is not proportional to the approach, since at a larger force the contact area is also larger and so the contact is stiffer. Let us look at this in numbers before we write the general statement.

Let us draw a ball \(D = 12{,}7\) mm pressed on a flat steel plate, with the numbers of section 25.6. On the left is the state at the force \(1000\) N: below, the contact area \(\Omega\) seen from above, a circle of radius \(a = 353{,}6\) µm, and above it the pressure along a diameter with the peak \(p_0 = 3818\) MPa. Now we double the approach, from \(19{,}69\) µm to \(39{,}39\) µm. On the right is the new state: the area does not double, it stretches by \(\sqrt2\), to the radius \(500{,}1\) µm. Why \(\sqrt2\)? The gap grows with the square of the distance from the first point of touch, so twice the gap lies only \(\sqrt2\) times farther out. The pressure also grows by \(\sqrt2\), to \(5400\) MPa. The grey dashed outline is the old state, so that we see the difference. Every point \(\vek x'\) of the old area moves to the point \(\sqrt2\,\vek x'\) of the new one, and every small piece of the area has twice the area, because it stretches by \(\sqrt2\) in two directions. The force is the pressure summed over the area, so it grows by \(\sqrt2 \cdot 2 = 2^{3/2} = 2{,}828\), to \(2828\) N. Twice the approach thus needs almost three times the force, where a spring would need twice. The lemma shows that the same holds for every factor \(\lambda\) in place of \(2\) and for every contact whose gap before loading is a quadratic function, without knowing the Hertz solution:

Lemma 25.33 Force-approach law of a contact (zakon sila-pomik stika)

Let two bodies touch in a non-conforming contact (definition 25.18) under the Hertz assumptions, and let them be linearly elastic and isotropic with effective modulus \(E^*\). Let the gap before loading in the principal axes of the contact be \(g_0(x, y) = Ax^2 + By^2\) with \(A, B > 0\) (definition 25.30), and let the approach (približevanje) of the bodies, which we wrote \(h\) in theorem 25.27, be written \(\delta\) in this section.

  1. If the contact problem (the conditions of definition 25.19, written in the plane of contact with given \(\delta\) at the start of the proof) has exactly one solution at every \(\delta > 0\), the force in the contact is
    \[Q = K\,\delta^{3/2} \tag{25.28}\]
    with a number \(K > 0\) independent of \(\delta\).
  2. If the ball is loaded only by the two contact forces (no friction, inertia or cage force) and the two approaches add (contact radius small against the ball radius), a ball touching two raceways with the numbers \(K_{\mathrm{i}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{o}}\) from item 1 carries the same force \(Q\) at both contacts, their total approach is \(\delta = \delta_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) and \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^{3/2}\) holds with
    \[K_{\mathrm{n}} = \Big( K_{\mathrm{i}}^{-2/3} + K_{\mathrm{o}}^{-2/3} \Big)^{-3/2} . \tag{25.29}\]
Idea

If we double the approach, the contact area widens and the pressure rises. We show that a stretching of space in the ratio \(\sqrt\lambda\) maps the solution for \(\delta\) to a solution for \(\lambda\delta\), which determines how the force changes. The second item we get from the equilibrium of the ball.

Proof

Denote by \(\Omega\) the contact region, which is to be closed and convex, and by \(p \ge 0\) a continuous pressure, which is zero outside \(\Omega\). By lemma 25.17 and theorem 25.15, added for both bodies with the effective modulus of definition 25.21, the total displacement of both surfaces toward the interior in the model, measured from far away, at the point \(\vek x\) of the surface is

\[u(\vek x) = \frac{1}{\pi E^*} \int_\Omega \frac{p(\vek s)}{|\vek x - \vek s|}\,\mathrm{d}A_{\vek{s}} .\]

The contact problem at a given \(\delta > 0\) consists of the conditions of definition 25.19 written in the plane of contact: the gap is zero on \(\Omega\), \(g_0(\vek x) + u(\vek x) - \delta = 0\), outside \(\Omega\) we have \(g_0 + u - \delta \ge 0\), and the pressure is non-negative. The approach \(\delta\) is given, and the force \(Q = \int_\Omega p\,\mathrm{d}A\) is unknown.

  1. Let the triple \((\Omega, p, \delta)\) solve the contact problem. For \(\lambda > 0\) define \(\Omega_\lambda = \sqrt\lambda\,\Omega\), \(p_\lambda(\vek x) = \sqrt\lambda\,p(\vek x/\sqrt\lambda)\) and \(\delta_\lambda = \lambda\delta\). We must show that the triple \((\Omega_\lambda, p_\lambda, \delta_\lambda)\) solves the same problem. In figure 25.65 \(\lambda = 2\): the first triple is on the left, the stretched one on the right, the approach is doubled.
  2. Substitute \(\vek s = \sqrt\lambda\,\vek s'\) and \(\vek x = \sqrt\lambda\,\vek x'\) (the points in the figure). Then \(|\vek x - \vek s| = \sqrt\lambda\,|\vek x' - \vek s'|\) and \(p_\lambda(\vek s) = \sqrt\lambda\,p(\vek s')\). The map \(\vek s' \mapsto \sqrt\lambda\,\vek s'\) stretches the plane by \(\sqrt\lambda\) in each of two directions, its Jacobian determinant is \(\lambda\) (a small piece of the area has \(\lambda\) times the area), and by theorem 4.59 the integral gets the factor \(\lambda\). Hence
    \[u_\lambda(\vek x) = \frac{1}{\pi E^*} \int_{\Omega} \frac{\sqrt\lambda\,p(\vek s')}{\sqrt\lambda\,|\vek x' - \vek s'|}\,\lambda\,\mathrm{d}A_{\vek{s}'} = \lambda\,u(\vek x') .\]
  3. The function \(g_0\) is homogeneous quadratic: \(g_0(\vek x) = \lambda\,g_0(\vek x')\). Hence \(g_0(\vek x) + u_\lambda(\vek x) - \delta_\lambda = \lambda\big(g_0(\vek x') + u(\vek x') - \delta\big)\). This is zero on \(\Omega_\lambda\) and non-negative outside it, since \(\vek x' \in \Omega\) exactly when \(\vek x \in \Omega_\lambda\). The pressure \(p_\lambda\) is non-negative and continuous, and \(\Omega_\lambda\) is closed and convex. So \((\Omega_\lambda, p_\lambda, \delta_\lambda)\) is a solution (definition 25.19).
  4. The force is the integral of the pressure over \(\Omega_\lambda\): \(Q_\lambda = \int_{\Omega_\lambda} p_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}A = \sqrt\lambda \cdot \lambda \int_\Omega p\,\mathrm{d}A = \lambda^{3/2}\,Q\). The factor \(\lambda\) is the Jacobian determinant of step 2 (theorem 4.59), and \(\sqrt\lambda\) is the factor of the pressure.
  5. Since the solution at each approach is unique (the assumption of item 1), \(Q(\lambda\delta) = \lambda^{3/2}\,Q(\delta)\) for all \(\lambda > 0\) and \(\delta > 0\). With \(\lambda = \delta/\delta_1\) at a chosen \(\delta_1 > 0\) we get \(Q(\delta) = Q(\delta_1)\,\delta_1^{-3/2}\,\delta^{3/2}\), and \(K = Q(\delta_1)\,\delta_1^{-3/2}\) does not depend on \(\delta\). At the point \(\vek x = 0\) we have \(g_0(0) = 0\), so the gap condition gives \(u(0) \ge \delta_1 > 0\). The pressure is therefore not zero everywhere, and since it is continuous and non-negative, \(Q(\delta_1) = \int_\Omega p\,\mathrm{d}A > 0\), so \(K > 0\). This is item 1.
  6. We cut the ball out of the bearing and draw all forces on it. It touches only two bodies and there is no friction, so only two forces act on it. Equilibrium requires that their sum is zero, so they are equal in size and opposite in direction. The same force \(Q\) therefore acts at both contacts.
  7. We measure both approaches to the centre of the ball, which we take as far from both contacts (at \(1000\) N the contact radius is \(5{,}6\,\%\) of the ball radius, see below), so the deformation at one contact does not move the other and the approaches add. The assumption fails when the contact radius is no longer small beside the ball radius. What it costs for our ball is estimated by the Boussinesq solution (theorem 25.15, \(u_{\mathrm{z}}\) at \(x = y = 0\)). The centre of the ball lies at the depth \(D/2 = 6{,}35\) mm below the contact, where the ball as a half-space of the book's steel (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\)) under the force \(1000\) N still moves by \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = F(1 + \nu)(3 - 2\nu)/(2\pi E\,D/2) = 0{,}40\) µm. This is \(2{,}0\,\%\) of \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} = 19{,}69\) µm from the test below. By this much the approach to the centre is smaller than the Hertz one, so the force at a given total approach is about \(3\,\%\) larger than \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^{3/2}\). By item 1, \(Q = K_{\mathrm{i}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{i}}^{3/2} = K_{\mathrm{o}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{o}}^{3/2}\), so \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}} = (Q/K_{\mathrm{i}})^{2/3}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{o}} = (Q/K_{\mathrm{o}})^{2/3}\). The sum is \(\delta = Q^{2/3}\big(K_{\mathrm{i}}^{-2/3} + K_{\mathrm{o}}^{-2/3}\big)\). Inverting gives \(Q = \delta^{3/2}\big(K_{\mathrm{i}}^{-2/3} + K_{\mathrm{o}}^{-2/3}\big)^{-3/2}\). This is item 2.

Source: Item 2 follows HK (7.5) to (7.7) (PDF p. 156), which writes the series of two contacts with \(\delta_{\mathrm{n}} = \delta_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta_{\mathrm{o}}\). The exponent \(3/2\) in item 1 follows HK (7.2) (PDF p. 155) and BOWER (PDF p. 331), where it is obtained from the Hertz solution. The proof by scaling is not from the source. The book does not prove the uniqueness of the contact problem (theorem 11.4 holds for bounded bodies and regular data, not for contact).

Let us test the law on a ball \(D = 12{,}7\) mm between two flat steel plates of the book (\(E = 196\) GPa, \(\nu = 0{,}3\), so \(E^* = E/(2(1-\nu^2)) = 107\,692\) MPa and \(R = 6{,}35\) mm). By theorem 25.27 \(F = \tfrac43 E^*\sqrt R\,h^{3/2}\), so \(K = \tfrac43 E^*\sqrt R = 361\,835\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\). At \(Q = 1000\) N, in figure 25.64 \(\delta_{\mathrm{i}}\) \(=\) \(\delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) \(= 19{,}69\) µm, together \(\delta = 39{,}39\) µm, and \(K_{\mathrm{n}} = 1000/\delta^{3/2} = 127\,928\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\). By equation (25.29), \(K_{\mathrm{n}} = \big(2K^{-2/3}\big)^{-3/2} = K/2^{3/2} = 127\,928\) N/mm\(^{3/2}\), the same number, since \(2^{-3/2} = 0{,}3536\). Two contacts in series are thus softer than one. The scaling at \(\lambda = 2\) we saw in figure 25.65: the force grows by \(2{,}828 = 2^{3/2}\). At \(\lambda = 10\) the ratio is \(Q(10h)/Q(h) = 31{,}62 = 10^{3/2}\).

Is it sensible to use the lemma for our balls? It requires the Hertz assumptions, among them that the contact area is small. At \(Q = 1000\) N the radius of the contact circle is \(a = 353{,}6\) µm (section 25.6, the example with a ball), which is \(5{,}6\,\%\) of the ball radius \(6{,}35\) mm, so the quadratic form of the gap \(Ax^2 + By^2\) near the contact is accurate. The lemma does not hold for cylinders: in a line contact the area of the contact line is not small relative to the size of the bodies, and the displacement of the plane in the Flamant solution (section 25.3) contains terms \(\ln r\) that depend on the size of the bodies. For a line contact the law with the power \(3/2\) therefore cannot be derived. For roller bearings HK gives (7.3) \(Q = K_{\mathrm{l}}\,\delta^{10/9}\) (PDF p. 155 and 156) as a law from measurements and computations, which the book does not prove (lemma 25.25). It also fails for a contact where the surfaces fit closely (a conforming contact, which definition 25.18 excludes), since the contact area is no longer small relative to the radii of curvature and the half-space is not a good model. In both cases the law \(Q = K\delta^n\) will have another exponent \(n\) or another constant. For ball bearings \(n = 3/2\), for roller bearings \(n = 10/9\) (HK (7.4), PDF p. 156), in what follows we write in general \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^n\), and the proofs shown hold for every \(n \ge 1\).

Now we compute how the force is distributed. Let us write down what the shift of the ring means for an individual ball, and introduce the variables.

Definition 25.34 Radial shift of the ring and the load zone (radialni pomik obroča in območje obremenitve)

For a bearing of definition 25.32 let the outer ring be fixed and let the inner ring shift as a whole by a number \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) in the direction of the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). The balls are at the angles \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = \psi_0 + 2\pi j/Z\) \((j = 0, \dots, Z - 1)\), measured from the direction of the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) (figure 25.62), where \(\psi_0\) gives the position of the cage. For \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} > P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) we define the load-zone parameter (parameter obremenitvenega območja)

\[\chi = \frac12\Big(1 - \frac{P_{\mathrm{d}}}{2\delta_{\mathrm{r}}}\Big) \tag{25.30}\]

and the limit angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}} = \arccos\big(P_{\mathrm{d}}/(2\delta_{\mathrm{r}})\big) = \arccos(1 - 2\chi)\). The balls at the angles \(|\psi_{\mathrm{j}}| < \psi_{\mathrm{l}}\) are in the load zone (območje obremenitve).

Source: By HK (7.10) to (7.13) (PDF p. 156-157). HK writes the parameter \(\varepsilon\), the book uses the letter \(\varepsilon\) for strain, so we write \(\chi\). The sum introduced in the next theorem follows HK (7.16) and (7.17) (PDF p. 158).

In an unloaded bearing with clearance \(P_{\mathrm{d}}\) and concentric rings, at the place of each ball the sum of the gaps between the ball and the two raceways is \(P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) (the clearance \(P_{\mathrm{d}}\) divides over two balls at opposite ends of a diameter). The inner ring shifts under the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the gap under it closes. The ball under the axis truly touches when \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} > P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). So \(\chi > 0\) exactly when the bearing is really loaded, and \(\chi \le 1/2\) because \(P_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 0\). At zero clearance \(\chi = 1/2\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}} = 90^\circ\): the whole lower half is loaded.

For each ball we must know two things: how the distance between the raceways changes when the ring shifts, and what force this causes. The first is given by geometry, the second by lemma 25.33. Then we write the equilibrium of the inner ring.

We read the geometry from figure 25.62. The point \(P\) of the inner raceway at the ball at the angle \(\psi = 40^\circ\) moves down by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) with the whole ring, since the ring is rigid. This shift is drawn enlarged on the right, in the bearing of the introduction it measures \(11{,}54\) µm. The ball is squeezed only by the part of the shift that pushes the point \(P\) toward the outer raceway, that is along the radial line through the centre of the ball. So we split the shift into the two legs of a right triangle with the angle \(\psi\) at \(P\). The radial leg \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi = 8{,}84\) µm reduces the distance between the raceways. The leg along the raceway \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\sin\psi\) carries the point past the ball and does not squeeze it (up to a small term bounded in item 1 of the theorem). The ball under the shaft (\(\psi = 0\)) feels the whole shift, the ball at \(80^\circ\) only \(2{,}00\) µm, the ball at \(90^\circ\) nothing.

We draw the inner ring alone, as a ring, and replace everything that acts on it by forces. The shaft pushes it with the downward force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). The ring is carried by the balls, which push it toward the centre at the points of contact. We draw each of these forces of the balls on its own radial line and each arrow ends at the point of contact with the ring, because the force is a push. The ball under the shaft carries the most, the neighbours each less, so the arrows are shorter. The values written in are those that example 25.36 will give at \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N: \(Q_0 = 485{,}6\) N under the shaft, \(Q_1 = 325{,}6\) N at \(\pm 40^\circ\) and \(Q_2 = 35{,}1\) N at \(\pm 80^\circ\). The grey dashed arrow is a ninth of the force, \(111{,}1\) N, which a ball would carry if the force were shared equally. An oblique force carries \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) only with its part along \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\): for the ball at \(40^\circ\) this is \(Q_1\cos 40^\circ = 249{,}4\) N. The parts across of the left and the right ball, \(209{,}3\) N each, are opposite and cancel. Since all the forces of the balls pass through the centre of the ring, they give no moment. For the equilibrium of the ring what remains is that the sum of forces along \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is zero: the forces of the balls, projected on this direction, carry \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\).

A sum over the balls is awkward, because it depends on where the balls stand. The theorem will therefore replace it by the area under a curve. Figure 25.67 shows the idea at zero clearance.

Let us draw the axes: the angle \(\psi\) over a whole turn on the horizontal, and on the vertical the share \(f(\psi)\) of the largest load \(Q_{\max}\) that the ball at the angle \(\psi\) contributes in the direction of \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). The blue curve is this share for a ball at any angle: \(\cos^{3/2}\psi\), because it is squeezed less, times \(\cos\psi\), because it pushes obliquely, and zero outside the lower half. The orange bars are the real balls at \(0^\circ\), \(\pm 40^\circ\) and \(\pm 80^\circ\) with the values \(1\), \(0{,}5136\) and \(0{,}0126\). Their sum \(2{,}0524\) is \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/Q_{\max}\). We give each bar a cell of width \(\Delta = 40^\circ\), the spacing of the balls. The area of the rectangles is then the sum of the bars times \(\Delta\), and the area under the curve is the integral. A rectangle differs from the curve above its cell nowhere by more than the oscillation \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\), the difference between the largest and the smallest value of the curve on the cell. In one turn the curve rises once from \(0\) to \(1\) and falls back once, so all the oscillations together do not exceed \(2\). At this cage position the peak of the curve is on the edge of a cell, and the oscillations \(0{,}0126\), \(0{,}5010\) and \(0{,}4864\) on each side add up to exactly \(2\).

Why the sum does not exceed \(2\) also when the peak falls inside a cell is shown by the cage turned by \(20^\circ\). On the same axes let us draw the same curve and the cells with left edges at \(-100^\circ\), \(-60^\circ\), \(-20^\circ\), \(20^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\). On each cell we draw the oscillation as a vertical segment from the lowest to the highest value of the curve on it: \(0{,}1768\), \(0{,}6792\), \(0{,}1440\), \(0{,}6792\) and \(0{,}1768\). On the other cells the curve is zero, and so is the oscillation. Left of the peak the curve only rises, so the oscillation there is just the rise of the curve on the cell. The rises, stacked on each other, reach from \(0\) to \(1\), since that is the whole climb of the curve. In the same way the falls right of the peak reach \(1\). The cell with the peak has the oscillation \(1 - 0{,}8560 = 0{,}1440\), while its rise up to the peak and its fall after it are \(0{,}1440\) each, \(0{,}2880\) together. In the columns it is therefore counted twice, in the sum of the oscillations once. The sum of the oscillations is \(1{,}8560\) and does not exceed the height of both columns together, \(2\). This is the content of item 4 of the theorem.

Theorem 25.35 Distribution of a radial load among the balls (porazdelitev radialne obremenitve med kroglice)

Let there be a bearing of definition 25.32 with rigid rings, without friction and inertial forces, with \(Z \ge 3\) balls and clearance \(P_{\mathrm{d}} \ge 0\), at radial load \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and radial shift \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} > P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) (definition 25.34). Let a ball in contact with both raceways follow the law \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^n\) with the same \(n \ge 1\) and \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) for all balls (for balls from lemma 25.33 with \(n = 3/2\), for rollers HK (7.4) with \(n = 10/9\)). Let \(Q_{\max} = K_{\mathrm{n}}(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2)^n\).

  1. The approach of the raceways at the ball at the angle \(\psi\) is \(\delta_\psi = \delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\)\( - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\), up to a term of second order in \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/(2R_{\mathrm{p}})\), where \(R_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the radius of the circle of ball centres, and the ball is loaded exactly when \(\delta_\psi > 0\), that is \(|\psi| < \psi_{\mathrm{l}}\).
  2. The load of the ball at the angle \(\psi\) in the load zone is
    \[Q_\psi = Q_{\max}\Big[1 - \frac{1 - \cos\psi}{2\chi}\Big]^n , \tag{25.31}\]
    and outside it is zero.
  3. If the cage stands so that the direction of the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) lies in a symmetry line of the bearing (one ball on the direction of the force or between two balls), then
    \[F_{\mathrm{r}} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} . \tag{25.32}\]
  4. Let
    \[J_{\mathrm{r}}(\chi) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\psi_\mathrm{l}}^{\psi_{\mathrm{l}}} \Big[1 - \frac{1 - \cos\psi}{2\chi}\Big]^n\cos\psi\;\mathrm{d}\psi . \tag{25.33}\]
    Then for every cage position \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = Z\,Q_{\max}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}(\chi) + \eta\) with \(|\eta| \le 2\,Q_{\max}\). At a cage position of item 3 the left side equals \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), in other words \(\big|F_{\mathrm{r}}/(Z\,Q_{\max}) - J_{\mathrm{r}}(\chi)\big| \le 2/Z\).
Idea

The shift of the ring determines the approach of each ball by geometry, the contact law gives the force, and the equilibrium of the ring gives the sum. We replace the sum over the balls by an integral over the angle, which is a Riemann sum, and estimate it.

Proof
  1. When the ring shifts by \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) in the direction \(\psi = 0\), the distance between the centres of the raceways at the ball at the angle \(\psi\) changes by the projection of the shift on the radial line, \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi\), with an error of second order in \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), which is at most \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/(2R_{\mathrm{p}})\). At \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = 11{,}54\) µm (the shift of the bearing of the introduction at \(F_{\mathrm{r}} = 1000\) N, computed in section 25.8) and a radius of the circle of ball centres \(R_{\mathrm{p}} = 23\) mm (assumed, a bearing for balls \(D = 12{,}7\) mm) this term is \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}^2/(2R_{\mathrm{p}}) = 2{,}9\) nm, \(0{,}025\,\%\) of the shift, so we neglect it. The sum of the gaps between the ball and the two raceways before loading is \(P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\) (by definition 25.32 the diameter \(P_{\mathrm{d}}\) divides over two balls at opposite ends of a diameter), which is subtracted, and \(\delta_\psi = \delta_{\mathrm{r}}\cos\psi - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2\). If \(\delta_\psi \le 0\), the ball does not touch both raceways. The contact does not pull (definition 25.19, pressure \(\ge 0\)), so \(Q = 0\). It is therefore loaded exactly for \(\cos\psi > P_{\mathrm{d}}/(2\delta_{\mathrm{r}})\), that is \(|\psi| < \psi_{\mathrm{l}}\).
  2. By the assumed contact law, \(Q_\psi = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta_\psi^n\). At \(\psi = 0\), \(\delta_0 = \delta_{\mathrm{r}} - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2 = 2\chi\,\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) (equation (25.30)), so \(Q_{\max} = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta_0^n\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} = \delta_0/(2\chi)\). Hence \(\delta_\psi = \delta_0 + \delta_{\mathrm{r}}(\cos\psi - 1) = \delta_0\big[1 - (1 - \cos\psi)/(2\chi)\big]\), and substituting into \(Q_\psi = K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta_\psi^n\) gives equation (25.31).
  3. The inner ring is in equilibrium under the force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the forces of the balls (figure 25.66). The force of ball \(j\) on the ring is \(Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\) along the radial line at the angle \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\), directed toward the centre. Its component against the direction in which \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) acts is \(Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\). The perpendicular component of the force of a pair of balls at \(\pm\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) cancels by symmetry about the direction of \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), because the loads are equal, and what remains is the sum along \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), which must balance \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is equation (25.32). The forces pass through the centre of the ring, so there is no moment.
  4. Define \(f(\psi) = [1 - (1 - \cos\psi)/(2\chi)]^n\cos\psi\) on \([-\psi_{\mathrm{l}}, \psi_{\mathrm{l}}]\) and \(f(\psi) = 0\) elsewhere on \([-\pi, \pi)\), then extend it with period \(2\pi\) (the blue curve for \(\chi = 1/2\) and \(n = 3/2\)). For \(\chi \le 1/2\), \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}} \le \pi/2\), and \(f\) is non-negative, continuous (\(f(\pm\psi_{\mathrm{l}}) = 0\)), even, and decreasing on \([0, \pi]\) (on \([0, \psi_{\mathrm{l}}]\) it is a product of two non-negative decreasing functions, \(\cos\psi\) and the bracket to the power \(n\), and for \(\psi > \psi_{\mathrm{l}}\), \(f = 0\)), \(f(0) = 1\). By theorem 3.53, \(J_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f\,\mathrm{d}\psi\) is well defined. The integral of a periodic function is the same over every interval of length \(2\pi\), so also \(J_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\psi_0}^{\psi_0 + 2\pi} f\,\mathrm{d}\psi\), and this interval is covered by the cells of step 5.
  5. By step 2, \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}/Q_{\max} = \sum_{\mathrm{j}} f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\), since \(f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\) is the value \([\dots]^n\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) for the balls in the zone and zero for the others. The angular spacing is \(\Delta = 2\pi/Z\). So \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}/(Z\,Q_{\max}) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\mathrm{j}} f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\,\Delta\) (a Riemann sum with the left edges of the cells, the bars and rectangles of the figure). We compare it with the integral over the cells \([\psi_{\mathrm{j}}, \psi_{\mathrm{j}} + \Delta]\).
  6. On each cell \(c_{\mathrm{j}}\), \(|f(\psi) - f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})| \le v_{\mathrm{j}}\), where \(v_{\mathrm{j}} = \max_{\mathrm{c}_\mathrm{j}} f - \min_{\mathrm{c}_\mathrm{j}} f\) is the oscillation of \(f\) on the cell (a continuous \(f\) has a largest and a smallest value on a closed cell). Hence \(\big|\int_{\mathrm{c}_\mathrm{j}} f\,\mathrm{d}\psi - f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\,\Delta\big| \le v_{\mathrm{j}}\,\Delta\) (lemma 3.50 gives a bound for the integral, the estimate is direct from \(|f - f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})| \le v_{\mathrm{j}}\)). In one period \(f\) rises from \(0\) to \(1\) and falls back to \(0\). On a cell where \(f\) is monotone, \(v_{\mathrm{j}}\) is the change of \(f\) on it, and these changes add up to at most \(1\) on the rising part and at most \(1\) on the falling part. The cell with the peak contributes at most the rise to the left of the peak plus the fall to its right. So \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} v_{\mathrm{j}} \le 2\) (the columns in figure 25.68).
  7. Adding up the errors of the cells: \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} f(\psi_{\mathrm{j}})\,\Delta - \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f\,\mathrm{d}\psi\big| \le 2\Delta\). Dividing by \(2\pi\): \(\big|\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\psi_\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}/(Z\,Q_{\max}) - J_{\mathrm{r}}\big| \le 2\Delta/(2\pi) = 2/Z\), and multiplying by \(Z\,Q_{\max}\) gives \(|\eta| \le 2\,Q_{\max}\). At a cage position of item 3 the sum equals \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) by equation (25.32).

Source: The geometry and equations (25.31) to (25.33) follow HK (7.10) to (7.20) (PDF p. 156-158). HK calls the integral \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) the radial integral and tabulates it (table 7.1, PDF p. 158). The estimate \(2/Z\) of the error of the sum is not from the source. The neglect of the side force at cage positions that are not symmetric is described in the text.

What has the theorem shown? For a given load we know how \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is distributed if we know a single parameter, \(\chi\). The load falls from the ball under the shaft toward the edge of the zone, as the blue curve in figure 25.69 shows, where for zero clearance (\(\chi = 1/2\), \(n = 3/2\)) \(Q_\psi/Q_{\max} = \cos^{3/2}\psi\) for \(|\psi| < 90^\circ\). The equilibrium gives what we are looking for: from \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\chi\) the largest force \(Q_{\max}\), and from it the shift \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Let us draw an axis, the angle \(\psi\) from \(-90^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\) on the horizontal and the load in units of \(Q_{\max}\) on the vertical. The load of a ball that would be on the direction of the force, in the continuous curve, falls as \(\cos^{3/2}\psi\). The balls, which really are at separate angles, we draw as bars: with nine balls the angular spacings are \(40^\circ\), and when one ball is on the direction of the force, the bars are at \(0^\circ\), \(\pm 40^\circ\) and \(\pm 80^\circ\) with heights \(1\), \(0{,}6705\) and \(0{,}0724\) (\(Q_{\max}\) times \(\cos^{3/2}\) at these angles). When the cage turns by \(20^\circ\), the balls are at other angles, \(\pm 20^\circ\) and \(\pm 60^\circ\), with heights \(0{,}9109\) and \(0{,}3536\). The tallest bar at the second cage position is \(9\,\%\) lower than the largest value of the curve, because no ball is at the place of the maximum.

If we neglect the error \(\eta\) of item 4 of the theorem, we replace the sum of the columns by the area under the curve and get the integral model, which we use for the computation:

\[F_{\mathrm{r}} \approx Z\,Q_{\max}\,J_{\mathrm{r}}(\chi) , \qquad Q_{\max} = K_{\mathrm{n}}(\delta_{\mathrm{r}} - P_{\mathrm{d}}/2)^n . \tag{25.34}\]

For a given \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) the unknown is \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\) (and with it \(\chi\)), since \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a tabulated function of \(\chi\). HK solves the equation by trial: he chooses \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), computes \(\chi\) by equation (25.30), reads off \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) and checks the equation (PDF p. 159).

Example 25.36 A bearing with nine balls (ležaj z devetimi kroglicami)

For the bearing of the introduction, \(Z = 9\), \(D = 12{,}7\) mm, zero clearance (\(\chi = 1/2\), \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}} = 90^\circ\)) and \(n = 3/2\), \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2) = 0{,}2288\) (numerical quadrature of equation (25.33), equal to HK table 7.1, PDF p. 158). By the integral model (25.34), \(Q_{\max} = F_{\mathrm{r}}/(Z\,J_{\mathrm{r}}) = 1000/(9 \cdot 0{,}2288) =\) \(485{,}6\) N, not \(111{,}1\) N. When a ball is on the direction of the force, by equation (25.31) the forces at \(0^\circ\), \(\pm 40^\circ\), \(\pm 80^\circ\) are \(485{,}6\) N, \(325{,}6\) N and \(35{,}1\) N, and the other four balls do not carry. Check of the equilibrium by equation (25.32): \(485{,}6 + 2 \cdot 325{,}6\cos 40^\circ + 2 \cdot 35{,}1\cos 80^\circ = 996{,}7\) N. Up to \(1000\) N, \(0{,}33\,\%\) is missing, which is the error of the integral model, bounded by \(2/Z\) in item 4 of the theorem.

The largest load that any of the balls carries at a given force \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\) is, at the two positions of the cage (for a ball on the direction of the force this is \(Q_{\max}\) of the theorem, for the position between two balls it is less, since there is no ball at the angle \(0^\circ\)):

With the approximation \(Q_{\max} = 5\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/(Z\cos\alpha)\) for clearance (equation (7.24), PDF p. 159), HK expressly warns that it does not suffice for light loads. The model does not capture this (section 25.9 shows where the elasticity of the rings does not appear in the model).

Corollary 25.37 Maximum ball load at zero clearance (največja obremenitev kroglice pri ničelni zračnosti)

For a bearing with rigid rings, zero clearance (\(\chi = 1/2\)), \(Z\) balls and radial load \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\), in the model of theorem 25.35 with \(n = 3/2\) and at a cage position of its item 3

\[\lim_{\mathrm{Z} \to \infty} \frac{Z\,Q_{\max}}{F_{\mathrm{r}}} = \frac{1}{J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2)} = 4{,}370 , \tag{25.35}\]

and for a roller bearing with \(n = 10/9\) this limit is \(4{,}085\). At finite \(Z\), \(Q_{\max} \approx 4{,}370\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\) is the approximation of the integral model (25.34), and the table before the corollary shows its error.

Proof
  1. At \(\chi = 1/2\), \(P_{\mathrm{d}} = 0\) by equation (25.30), \(\psi_{\mathrm{l}} = \pi/2\) and the bracket in equation (25.31) is \(1 - (1 - \cos\psi) = \cos\psi\). So \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2)\) \(= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^{n+1}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\psi\).
  2. For \(n = 3/2\) numerical quadrature gives \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2) = 0{,}2288\), for \(n = 10/9\) it gives \(0{,}2448\). HK table 7.1 (PDF p. 158) has \(0{,}2453\) for line contact, because it computes with \(n = 1{,}1\), the rounded \(10/9\): with \(n = 1{,}1\) the values of the column at \(\chi = 0{,}1\), \(0{,}3\) and \(0{,}5\) (\(0{,}1268\), \(0{,}2055\), \(0{,}2453\)) agree to all four digits, with \(n = 10/9\) they do not (\(0{,}1264\), \(0{,}2050\), \(0{,}2448\)). Our value \(0{,}2448\) is correct for \(n = 10/9\).
  3. By item 4 of theorem 25.35, at a cage position of item 3, \(\big|F_{\mathrm{r}}/(Z\,Q_{\max}) - J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2)\big| \le 2/Z\), and the right side tends to zero as \(Z \to \infty\). So \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/(Z\,Q_{\max})\) tends to \(J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2) > 0\) and its reciprocal \(Z\,Q_{\max}/F_{\mathrm{r}}\) to \(1/J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2)\): \(1/0{,}2288 = 4{,}370\) and \(1/0{,}2448 = 4{,}085\).

Source: HK (7.23) and (7.25) (PDF p. 159), where for a ball bearing \(Q_{\max} = 4{,}37\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\) is given and attributed to Stribeck, and for a roller bearing \(4{,}08\,F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\) (that is \(1/0{,}2453\), with \(n = 1{,}1\)). The computation and the limit are ours.

The number \(4{,}37\) is directly usable: from the radial force and the number of balls we get the force with which we recompute the contact by section 25.6 and the service life. The number is not small. If the balls carried equally, each would carry \(F_{\mathrm{r}}/Z\), and the most loaded one carries \(4{,}37\) times as much. Why just this much? There are two factors. Only the lower half of the balls carries, because the upper ones cannot pull, and this is the factor \(2\). In the lower half a ball contributes on average only \(0{,}4577\) of the largest load in the direction of \(F_{\mathrm{r}}\). This is the mean height of the blue curve over the lower half, \(2\,J_{\mathrm{r}}(1/2)\): a ball away from the direction of the force is squeezed less (\(\cos^{3/2}\psi\), mean \(0{,}5564\)) and on top of that pushes obliquely (\(\cos\psi\)). This is the factor \(1/0{,}4577 = 2{,}185\), together \(2 \cdot 2{,}185 = 4{,}370\).

With an axial load it is different. In a bearing that carries the shaft along the axis (a thrust bearing) the shift happens along the axis and all balls are squeezed equally.

Let us draw the bearing viewed along the axis. The axis is a dot in the centre, around it on a circle are nine balls at equal spacings \(2\pi/Z = 40^\circ\). If we rotate the bearing by one spacing, every ball comes to the place of its neighbour and the new bearing is equal to the old. On the right is the axial section through two opposite balls. The ring shifts along the axis and both balls are squeezed equally, by the same approach \(\delta\).

Let us draw a ball in axial section. The rings press on it, each at the end of a line at the contact angle \(\alpha\) from the horizontal, so the forces \(Q\) act along this line. Their axial component \(Q\sin\alpha\) is what is decisive for the equilibrium of the ring along the axis.

The proof of the lemma and the eccentric load after it need two facts about vectors in the plane perpendicular to the axis. On the left we draw a vector \(\vek s\), for example the transverse shift of the ring, and the same vector turned by \(40^\circ\), the spacing of the balls. The turned vector points elsewhere, so the rotation preserves only a vector of length zero. On the right we draw nine unit vectors in the directions of the balls, \(0^\circ\), \(40^\circ\) and so on up to \(320^\circ\), one after another, each starting at the end of the previous one. Their sum is the arrow from the start of the chain to its end, and the chain ends at the starting point. Why? If we turn all nine vectors by \(40^\circ\), we get the same vectors in a different order, so the sum does not change. By the left picture, a sum the rotation preserves is zero. So the sum of the horizontal components \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} \cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) is zero too.

Lemma 25.38 Centric thrust load (središčna osna obremenitev)

Let a bearing be built as in definition 25.32, but with a contact angle \(0 < \alpha \le 90^\circ\) in the loaded state, with rigid rings, without friction, with \(Z \ge 2\) equal balls at equal angular spacings, and let the axial force \(F_{\mathrm{a}}\) be centric (its line is the axis of the bearing). Let the position of the ring at a given \(F_{\mathrm{a}}\) be unique up to a turn about its own axis (an assumption we do not prove). Such a turn changes no gap, since the ring is round. Then all balls carry the same force

\[Q = \frac{F_{\mathrm{a}}}{Z\sin\alpha} , \tag{25.36}\]

where \(\alpha\) is the contact angle of the loaded bearing.

Idea

The loaded bearing maps to the same state under a rotation by the spacing \(2\pi/Z\). So the displacement of the ring must be the same for all balls, and so must the force.

Proof
  1. A rotation by the angle \(2\pi/Z\) about the axis maps the bearing with its load (a centric force does not change direction) to itself, since the spacing of the balls is equal and the balls are equal. So it maps an equilibrium position of the ring to an equilibrium position, and since that position is unique up to a turn about the ring's own axis (the assumption), to a position that differs from the first at most by such a turn. That turn changes neither the transverse shift nor the tilt of the ring. The transverse shift is a vector in the plane perpendicular to the axis, the tilt too (the axis of the tilt, as long as the tilt angle), and the rotation by \(2\pi/Z\) therefore maps both to themselves. A vector in the plane that a rotation by \(2\pi/Z\) (\(Z \ge 2\)) preserves is zero: the rotated vector points elsewhere unless its length is zero. The transverse shift and the tilt of the ring are therefore zero, and the ring moves only along the axis.
  2. For a shift only along the axis the displacement at each ball is equal and so is the approach \(\delta\). By lemma 25.33, \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\,\delta^{3/2}\) is equal for all balls.
  3. The axial component of the force of one ball on the ring is \(Q\sin\alpha\) (figure 25.72). The equilibrium of the ring along the axis: \(F_{\mathrm{a}} = Z\,Q\sin\alpha\), so equation (25.36).

Source: HK (7.26) (PDF p. 160). The proof by symmetry is not from the source.

For \(F_{\mathrm{a}} = 1000\) N and \(Z = 9\), at \(\alpha = 90^\circ\) (a thrust bearing with flat raceways) \(Q = 111{,}1\) N, at \(\alpha = 60^\circ\) of figure 25.72 \(128{,}3\) N, because only the part \(Q\sin\alpha\) carries along the axis, and at \(\alpha = 45^\circ\) \(157{,}1\) N. At the same force the ball of a radial bearing is thus under the largest load, \(485{,}6\) N, \(4{,}37\) times as loaded as a ball of a thrust bearing. The contact angle of the loaded bearing is larger than that of the unloaded one (HK, PDF p. 160), so \(\alpha\) in equation (25.36) is not a datum of the bearing but is computed from equilibrium, which HK does by trial (PDF p. 161-163) and this chapter does not. Centricity is a condition without which this does not hold. Let the axial force be shifted from the axis by \(e \ne 0\) (an eccentric load) and let us measure the angles \(\psi_{\mathrm{j}}\) of the balls from the direction of the shift. Equilibrium of moments about the line through the centre perpendicular to this direction requires \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} Q_{\mathrm{j}}\sin\alpha\,R_{\mathrm{p}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = F_{\mathrm{a}}\,e\), where \(R_{\mathrm{p}}\) is the radius of the circle of ball centres. If all \(Q_{\mathrm{j}}\) were equal, the left side would be \(Q\sin\alpha\,R_{\mathrm{p}}\sum_{\mathrm{j}}\cos\psi_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\), since the sum of \(Z \ge 2\) equally spaced unit vectors is zero. Equal loads are therefore impossible at \(e \ne 0\): the ring tilts and the balls are not equally loaded, their distribution is given by HK in section 7.4.3 (PDF p. 163-165) with an integral similar to \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\).

Notation of this section. The letter \(Q\) is here the force of a ball, in the chapter on heat conduction a heat source. The letter \(Z\) is the number of balls, not the set of integers \(\mathbb{Z}\). The angle \(\alpha\) is the contact angle, not the thermal expansion of the chapter on constitutive equations. The exponent of the contact law is \(n\), and the index of the vibration modes is \(j\). The letter \(\delta\) is the approach of bodies and also the radial shift of the ring \(\delta_{\mathrm{r}}\), not a virtual displacement. There is no matrix in \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\): \(K_{\mathrm{n}}\) is a number, the contact constant of equation (25.29), and \(K\) without an index is the constant of one contact. The parameter \(\chi\) is HK's \(\varepsilon\), since \(\varepsilon\) in the book means strain. The effective modulus \(E^*\) is a scalar, as used by section 25.6, and is not the Euler-Almansi tensor.

We have computed how the bearing carries the force. The next section (25.8) computes how the force changes when the ring shifts by a small displacement, that is the stiffness of the bearing. There \(J_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the law \(Q = K_{\mathrm{n}}\delta^n\) of this section will be needed, and one more consideration will show itself: an unloaded bearing has no force, so it has no stiffness.
