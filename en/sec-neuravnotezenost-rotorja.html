Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.2 Rotor unbalance and the critical speed

A car wheel that has a little more mass at one place on its rim a little more mass than elsewhere starts to shake the steering wheel at one road speed and calms down again at a higher one. That is why the garage clips a small weight to the rim (figure 24.14). The same happens in a turbine, a fan or an electric motor, except that there a shaft turns thousands of times a minute and rests on two bearings. Why does a small fault in the distribution of mass cause a force on the bearings, why is this force especially large at one speed, and why does the shaft settle down again above that speed?

The model is the Jeffcott rotor (Jeffcottov rotor) of section 24.1 (definition 24.1): a rigid disk of mass \(m\) at midspan of a massless shaft that rests in two rigid short bearings. Our running example R1 is a steel shaft of diameter \(40\) mm with the bearings \(L = 1\) m apart, carrying a disk of diameter \(400\) mm and thickness \(50\) mm. The disk has mass \(m = 49{,}009\) kg. By the deflection curve of a simply supported beam under a central force (equation (5.60)) the stiffness of the shaft at midspan is \(k = 48EI/L^3 = 1{,}1822 \cdot 10^6\) N/m. The second moment of area \(I = \pi d^4/64\) holds about every axis through the centre of the circle, because a circle is symmetric about every diameter (definition 5.26). The natural circular frequency of free vibration is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m} = 155{,}32\) 1/s, which is \(1483\) revolutions per minute (rev/min). The disk sits at midspan, where the slope of the deflection curve is zero by symmetry (figure 5.143), so it does not tilt when the shaft bends. The tilt of the disk comes in section 24.3. The shaft turns at a constant spin speed \(\Omega\) (chapter 9: this is what we called the frequency of the forcing), kept up by a motor. This holds while the motor can supply the power to keep the shaft turning: at run-up and shutdown \(\Omega\) is not constant, the force gets a further term with \(\dot\Omega\), and the theorem below does not apply, so such passages are treated in section 24.6.

So far the disk moved only because of bending. Now we give it a fault that makes the shaft shake even though the only cause is the turning. We look at the disk in a plane perpendicular to the shaft, along the shaft.

We draw the axes \(v\) and \(w\) through the point \(O\) where the bearing axis, the line through the centres of the two bearings, pierces the plane of the disk. At rest the shaft would be straight and the centre of the disk would lie at \(O\). When the shaft turns it bends and the disk centre \(S\) moves from \(O\) by the displacement \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) (section 24.1, complex number: definition 9.24). The vector from \(O\) to \(S\) is \(p\). The centre of the disk is the point where the axis of the shaft pierces the disk, so in this plane it is also the centre of the shaft. The centre of mass \(G\) of the disk may lie elsewhere. If the disk is not perfectly round or homogeneous (cast metal with a bubble, a keyway, a bolt on the rim), it is not at \(S\). From \(S\) to \(G\) we draw the vector of length \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\). The disk is a rigid body that turns with the shaft at angular velocity \(\Omega\), so this vector turns with it, and its angle \(\Omega t\) is measured from the direction of the axis \(v\). The turning goes from the axis \(v\) towards the axis \(w\). The centre of mass is then at \(p + a_{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) and the dashed line from \(O\) to \(G\) shows its position relative to the bearing axis.

Why does the position of the centre of mass matter, and not the centre? By Newton's second law the sum of the external forces on a body equals its mass times the acceleration of its centre of mass. If we held the centre \(S\) at \(O\) (as for a rigid shaft), the centre of mass \(G\) would go round a circle of radius \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) with angular velocity \(\Omega\), with acceleration \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) towards \(O\) (figure 24.16). For this acceleration the shaft must act on the disk with a force \(m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\), and by the law of action and reaction the disk acts with such a force on the shaft and through it on the bearings. The force turns with the disk. For the disk R1 with \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm at \(3000\) rev/min, which is \(\Omega = 314{,}16\) 1/s, it is \(49{,}009 \cdot 0{,}05 \cdot 10^{-3} \cdot 314{,}16^2 = 241{,}8\) N (NumPy), about half the weight of the disk, \(mg = 480{,}8\) N. We made the same calculation for a machine with an unbalanced mass in example 9.76, where only a small mass \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\) at radius \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) was unbalanced. Here the whole disk is unbalanced, so its whole mass enters the product.

Definition 24.6 Unbalance (neuravnoteženost)

Let a rigid disk of mass \(m\) be fixed on a shaft that turns about its axis at constant angular velocity \(\Omega > 0\) in the sense of increasing angle from the axis \(v\) towards the axis \(w\). Let \(S\) be the point where the axis of the shaft pierces the plane of the disk and \(G\) the centre of mass of the disk. The vector \(\overrightarrow{SG}\) lies in the plane of the disk and turns with it. The disk is unbalanced (neuravnotežen) if \(G \ne S\). The number

\[a_{\mathrm{u}} = |SG| \ge 0 \tag{24.5}\]

is the eccentricity (izsrednost) and the vector

\[\vek U = m\,\overrightarrow{SG} , \qquad U = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}} \tag{24.6}\]

is the unbalance of the disk. It is a vector in the plane of the disk, measured in kg m (in practice g mm), and the disk carries it round. The disk is balanced (uravnotežen) if \(\vek U = \vek 0\). Here \(m\) is the total mass of the turning part. If a mass \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\) is added at distance \(a\) from the axis of a balanced disk, the unbalance of the whole is \(U = m_{\mathrm{d}}\,a\) (example after this block).

Source: FPGL section 6.2.1 (PDF p. 248-250): there the eccentricity is \(\varepsilon = |SG|\) (figure 6.2) and the equivalent added small mass \(m_0\) has \(m_0 a = m\varepsilon\) (PDF p. 250). FPGL has the axis of turning as z, ours is the shaft axis \(x\), as in chapter 5. The notation \(U = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\) is the product \(m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}\) of example 9.76 (chapter 9), here with the whole disk as \(m_{\mathrm{u}}\).

Notation. The spin speed \(\Omega\) is the forcing frequency \(\Omega\) of chapter 9, and the same number for unbalance. The whirl frequency will be written \(\omega\) (the bold \(\boldsymbol\omega\) of chapter 8 is the angular velocity vector and does not appear here). The letter \(p\) is the complex displacement \(v + \mathrm{i}\,w\). The ratio \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) is the frequency ratio of chapter 9. \(G\) is the centre of mass in this section (the shear modulus is the italic \(G\) elsewhere).

The word balanced says that the masses are distributed around the axis so that their effects on the axis cancel, as the weights on the two arms of a balance cancel. With \(G = S\) the acceleration of the centre of mass is zero while the centre stays still, and the bearings receive no force from the turning. With \(G \ne S\) this condition is lost. Let the disk R1 be balanced and let us bolt a mass \(m_{\mathrm{d}} = 14\) g on it at radius \(a = 175\) mm, inside the disk of radius \(200\) mm (figure 24.17). The centre of mass of everything is then \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\,a/(m + m_{\mathrm{d}}) = 14 \cdot 175/49\,023\) mm \(= 0{,}04998\) mm from \(S\), and the total unbalance is \((m + m_{\mathrm{d}})\cdot 0{,}04998\) mm \(= 2450\) g mm, which is the product \(m_{\mathrm{d}}\,a = 2450\) g mm. Unbalance is thus a product of a mass and an arm, and neither factor alone fixes the force. For that reason we describe the rotor in every calculation by one number \(U\) and a direction, that is by \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = U/m\). For R1 we take \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm, \(U = 2450{,}4\) g mm. This is very little: the ratio of \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) to the radius of the disk is \(2{,}5 \cdot 10^{-4}\), and yet the force will be large, because \(\Omega\) enters squared.

Now we compute how the shaft responds. The centre \(S\) moves with the bending of the shaft, so with two coordinates \(v\) and \(w\) (figure 24.15), and the centre of mass \(G\) is away from it by the turning vector \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\). The kinetic energy of the disk therefore depends on the velocity of the point \(G\). We derive the equations by Lagrange, as for the machine in example 9.76, but in two directions.

Theorem 24.7 Response of the Jeffcott rotor to unbalance (odziv Jeffcottovega rotorja na neuravnoteženost)

Let the Jeffcott rotor of definition 24.1 be a disk of mass \(m\) at midspan of a massless shaft that has the same stiffness \(k\) at the disk in both planes \(v\) and \(w\), and two rigid short bearings. Let the disk have unbalance with eccentricity \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) (definition 24.6), a constant spin speed \(\Omega > 0\) and an external viscous damping with coefficient \(c \ge 0\): a force \(-c\,\dot p\) acts on the disk centre, opposite to its velocity, as for the damper of definition 9.1, only equally in every direction of the plane. Notation: \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\), \(\zeta = c/(2\sqrt{km})\), \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). Then:

  1. The equations of motion in the real coordinates \(v\) and \(w\) are
    \[\begin{aligned} m\ddot v + c\dot v + k v &= m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\cos\Omega t , \\ m\ddot w + c\dot w + k w &= m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\sin\Omega t , \end{aligned} \tag{24.7}\]
    and as one complex equation for \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\)
    \[m\ddot p + c\dot p + k p = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} . \tag{24.8}\]
  2. If \(c > 0\) or \(r \ne 1\), one solution is \(p(t) = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\) with the complex number
    \[P = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\frac{r^2}{1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta r} , \qquad |P| = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,r^2 D(r) , \tag{24.9}\]
    where \(D\) is the dynamic factor (definition 9.64). The disk centre goes round a circle of radius \(|P|\) along it, once per revolution of the shaft, in the sense of the turning of the shaft, that is in forward whirl (definition 24.3).
  3. If \(\zeta > 0\), every solution of equation (24.8) approaches the solution of item 2 as time goes on (figure 24.19).
Proof

We must show three things: the equations from Lagrange's equations, the solution of item 2 from theorem 9.62 in each plane separately, and their joining into the complex form.

  1. The centre of the disk is at the point \((v, w)\) of the plane of the disk. The centre of mass is away from it by \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) in the direction of angle \(\Omega t\) (definition 24.6), so \(x_{\mathrm{G}} = v + a_{\mathrm{u}}\cos\Omega t\) and \(y_{\mathrm{G}} = w + a_{\mathrm{u}}\sin\Omega t\).
  2. The disk is a rigid body (chapter 8). By theorem 8.92 its kinetic energy is the sum of the energy of the centre of mass, \(\tfrac12 m(\dot x_{\mathrm{G}}^2 + \dot y_{\mathrm{G}}^2)\), and the energy of the turning about the centre of mass. It does not tilt (section 24.1) and turns about its own axis with constant \(\Omega\), so the energy of the turning is constant and does not enter the derivatives with respect to \(v\) and \(\dot v\). The angle \(\Omega t\) is prescribed (a rheonomic constraint, as in example 9.76). So
    \[T = \tfrac12 m\big[(\dot v - a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin\Omega t)^2 + (\dot w + a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\cos\Omega t)^2\big] + \text{const.}\]
  3. By the split of theorem 8.97, \(T_2 = \tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2)\), \(T_1 = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega(-\dot v\sin\Omega t + \dot w\cos\Omega t)\) and \(T_0 = \tfrac12 m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}^2\Omega^2 + \text{const.}\) The coordinates \(v\) and \(w\) themselves do not enter \(T\).
  4. The force of the shaft on the disk is \(-k\,v\) in the direction \(v\) and \(-k\,w\) in the direction \(w\): the shaft has no mass, so it acts on the disk as a spring, and by equation (5.60) the deflection under a force \(F\) at midspan is \(F/k\), the same in each plane because \(I\) is the same about every axis through the centre of the section, and by lemma 5.60 the displacements in the two planes add. The potential energy is \(\tfrac12 k(v^2 + w^2)\). The weight of the disk only moves the equilibrium position by \(mg/k = 0{,}41\) mm (R1), and \(v\) and \(w\) are measured from it, as in example 9.76, step 3.
  5. The damping force on the disk centre has the components \(-c\,\dot v\) and \(-c\,\dot w\). The position of the centre is \((v, w)\), so its derivative with respect to \(v\) is the unit vector of the \(v\) axis and with respect to \(w\) the unit vector of the \(w\) axis. By definition 8.51 the generalized forces are \(Q_{\mathrm{v}} = -c\,\dot v\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{w}} = -c\,\dot w\).
  6. By theorem 8.101 for the coordinate \(v\): \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\partial T/\partial\dot v\big) - \partial T/\partial v + k\,v = -c\,\dot v\). Here \(\partial T/\partial\dot v = m\dot v - m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\sin\Omega t\), its time derivative is \(m\ddot v - m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\), and \(\partial T/\partial v = 0\). The torque that keeps up \(\Omega t\) does no virtual work (example 9.76, step 5). This gives the first equation (24.7).
  7. For \(w\), \(\partial T/\partial\dot w = m\dot w + m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega\cos\Omega t\), its derivative is \(m\ddot w - m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\sin\Omega t\), and we get the second equation (24.7). SymPy confirms steps 6 and 7.
  8. We multiply the second equation by \(\mathrm i\) and add it to the first. Since \(\cos\Omega t + \mathrm i\sin\Omega t = \mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}\) (definition 9.33), this is equation (24.8). This is item 1.
  9. The first equation (24.7) is equation (9.64) with \(Q_0 = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\). For \(c > 0\) or \(r \ne 1\), by theorem 9.62 (item 1) one solution is \(v_{\mathrm{p}} = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi)\) with \(X = Q_0|H(\Omega)|\) and \(\varphi = \arg(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm i\,c\Omega)\) (in the theorem this is the \(\arg\) of the polynomial at \(\mathrm i\Omega\), not our amplitude \(P\)).
  10. The second equation has the same left side and the force \(Q_0\sin\Omega t = Q_0\cos(\Omega t - \pi/2)\), which is the force of the first equation delayed by \(\tau = \pi/(2\Omega)\) (figure 24.20). The coefficients are constant, so \(w_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\) \(= v_{\mathrm{p}}(t - \tau) = X\cos(\Omega t - \varphi - \pi/2) = X\sin(\Omega t - \varphi)\).
  11. So \(p_{\mathrm{p}} = v_{\mathrm{p}} + \mathrm i\,w_{\mathrm{p}} = X\,\mathrm e^{\mathrm i(\Omega t - \varphi)}\) (definition 9.33): a circle of radius \(X\) that the point runs round with angular velocity \(\Omega > 0\), so in the sense of the turning of the shaft.
  12. The radius is \(X = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2|H| = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,(m\Omega^2/k)\,k|H| = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,r^2 D(r)\), because \(m\Omega^2/k = r^2\) and \(D = k|H|\) (definition 9.64). By step 4 of the proof of theorem 9.62 the complex number \(P = X\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\varphi}\) equals \(m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\,H(\Omega)\) with \(H = 1/(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm i\,c\Omega) = 1/\big(k(1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r)\big)\), since \(c\Omega/k = 2\zeta r\). This proves item 2.
  13. The remainder \(p - p_{\mathrm{p}}\) solves the free equation \(m\ddot p + c\dot p + k p = 0\), that is the free equations of both real coordinates (theorem 9.62, item 2), and for \(\zeta > 0\) it tends to zero (item 3 of the same theorem). This is item 3.

Source: FPGL section 6.2.1 (PDF p. 248-250) gets equations (6.5) from the free-vibration equations (3.63) by substituting the displacement of the centre of mass, section 6.2.2 (PDF p. 252-253) gives equation (6.16) and the complex amplitude \(r_0\) from (6.17) to (6.19), which is \(P\) in equation (24.9), and section 6.2.3 (PDF p. 259) states that (6.16) holds for the Jeffcott rotor too. The derivation from Lagrange's equations in steps 1 to 8 is ours, as in example 9.76. The radius \(|P|\) is \(|r_0|\) of FPGL (figure 6.5, PDF p. 254).

The theorem says that under unbalance the disk centre goes round (figure 24.15). It goes round a circle because in the plane \(w\) the force is the same as in the plane \(v\) but delayed by a quarter of a period (step 10), and always in the sense of the turning of the shaft, because the force itself turns in that sense. The radius of the circle is \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\,r^2 D(r)\). In chapter 9 we obtained the same formula for a machine with an unbalanced mass, \(X = (m_{\mathrm{u}} a_{\mathrm{u}}/M)\,r^2 D(r)\) (equation (9.74)), only with a mass ratio. Here the whole disk is unbalanced and the mass ratio is \(1\).

We read the upper graph from the left. On the horizontal axis is the ratio \(r = \Omega/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), on the vertical one the radius \(|p|\) in units of the eccentricity, shown by the blue curve. At low speed, \(r = 0{,}5\), \(|p| = 0{,}3332\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), because the force \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) is small and the spring holds it. The radius grows and at \(r = 1\) reaches \(25{,}000\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), which is \(1/(2\zeta)\), because at \(r = 1\) the spring force \(k|P|\) and the inertia force \(m\Omega^2|P|\) on the disk cancel (\(k = m\Omega^2\)) and the force \(m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) is opposed only by the damping \(c\Omega|P|\). From \(c\Omega|P| = m\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) we get \(|P| = a_{\mathrm{u}}m\Omega/c = a_{\mathrm{u}}/(2\zeta)\), and indeed only the term \(2\mathrm i\zeta r\) is left in the denominator of equation (24.9). The peak of the curve, the orange dot, lies a little to the right of \(r = 1\) and a little higher, which we compute exactly below. At \(r = 0{,}9\) it is still \(4{,}1886\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) and at \(r = 1{,}1\) it is \(5{,}6394\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) (SymPy). After that the radius falls. At \(r = 2\) it is \(1{,}3329\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), at \(r = 5\) it is \(1{,}0416\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), and it tends to the value \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\). The vertical axis is logarithmic because the values \(0{,}1\) and \(25\) differ by more than two orders of magnitude. The lag \(\varphi\) is near zero for small \(r\) (at \(r = 0{,}5\) it is \(1{,}5^\circ\)), at \(r = 1\) it is \(90^\circ\) and for large \(r\) it is near \(180^\circ\). What that means in the plane of the disk is shown by figure 24.22.

We take three snapshots. In each we take the moment when the vector from \(S\) to \(G\) points along the axis \(v\). The centre \(S\) goes round the blue circle, the centre of mass \(G\) round the dashed orange one. At \(r = 0{,}5\) the points \(S\) and \(G\) lie almost in the same direction from \(O\) and the centre of mass is farther from the axis than the centre: \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = 1{,}3331\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) against \(|p| = 0{,}3332\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\). The disk goes round with a small radius and the centre of mass outside it. At \(r = 1\) the point \(S\) is below the axis and \(G\) is \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) to the right of it, a distance \(|SG| = a_{\mathrm{u}}\) that is barely visible against the radius \(25\) times larger (in this panel \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) is only \(0{,}08\) cm). At \(r = 2\) the point \(S\) is to the left of \(O\) and \(G\) lies between \(S\) and \(O\), so the centre of mass is closer to the axis: \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = 0{,}3343\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\). The centre of mass is now inside the orbit of the centre. At \(r = 5\) it would be \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = 0{,}0425\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\).

Definition 24.8 Critical speed (kritična hitrost)

For a Jeffcott rotor with unbalance, the critical speed (kritična hitrost) \(\Omega_{\mathrm{c}} > 0\) is the spin speed at which the radius \(|P|\) of the circular orbit of theorem 24.7, as a function of \(\Omega\), is largest, if such a largest value exists. We write \(r_{\mathrm{c}} = \Omega_{\mathrm{c}}/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), the position of the peak on the horizontal axis of figure 24.21, and usually give \(\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\) in rev/min. A more general definition for several masses is in section 24.6.

Source: FPGL section 6.2.2 (PDF p. 254): "When the amplitude of whirl is a maximum, the corresponding rotor speed is called the critical speed." The more general definition is in section 6.7 (PDF p. 293), not used here.

The word critical says that at such a speed turning is no longer safe. FPGL (PDF p. 247) writes that if a machine runs continuously at a critical speed, damage can occur very quickly, and (PDF p. 254) that it is unlikely that the rotor can be operated safely at a steady speed near it. Where is this speed? Without damping the denominator of equation (24.9) is zero at \(r = 1\). There is no circular solution there, and as \(r\) approaches \(1\) the radius \(|P| = a_{\mathrm{u}}r^2/|1 - r^2|\) grows beyond every bound. With little damping the peak is somewhere near \(r = 1\), as the orange dot of figure 24.21 shows for \(\zeta = 0{,}02\). The theorem gives the exact answer.

Theorem 24.9 Critical speed of the Jeffcott rotor (kritična hitrost Jeffcottovega rotorja)

In the setting of theorem 24.7 with \(\zeta \ge 0\) the following holds for the curve \(|P|\):

  1. If \(0 < \zeta < 1/\sqrt2\), then \(|P|\) as a function of \(r > 0\) has exactly one largest peak at
    \[r_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}} , \qquad |P|_{\max} = \frac{a_{\mathrm{u}}}{2\zeta\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} . \tag{24.10}\]
  2. If \(\zeta \ge 1/\sqrt2\), then \(|P|\) is strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\) and tends to \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) without reaching it: there is no critical speed.
  3. If \(\zeta = 0\), then \(|P| \to \infty\) as \(r \to 1\) (figure 24.23).
Proof

We must show where \(|P|\) is largest. We write it as \(a_{\mathrm{u}}/\sqrt{g}\) with one function \(g\) and find the smallest value of \(g\) by completing the square (figure 24.24).

  1. By equation (24.9), \((|P|/a_{\mathrm{u}})^2 = r^4/\big((1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2r^2\big)\). We divide numerator and denominator by \(r^4\) and introduce \(u = 1/r^2 \in (0, \infty)\): \((|P|/a_{\mathrm{u}})^2 = 1/g(u)\) with \(g(u) = (u - 1)^2 + 4\zeta^2u\).
  2. With \(b = 1 - 2\zeta^2\) we have \(g(u) = u^2 - 2bu + 1 = (u - b)^2 + 1 - b^2\) and \(1 - b^2 = (1 - b)(1 + b) = 2\zeta^2\cdot 2(1 - \zeta^2) = 4\zeta^2(1 - \zeta^2)\).
  3. Since \(u = 1/r^2\) is smaller at larger \(r\), \(|P|\) is largest where \(g\) is smallest. If \(b > 0\) (that is \(\zeta < 1/\sqrt2\)), \(u = b\) is admissible and \(g_{\min} = 4\zeta^2(1 - \zeta^2)\) at \(r_{\mathrm{c}}^2 = 1/b\). This is also the only minimum of \(g\), because the square \((u - b)^2\) is positive for \(u \ne b\). Hence \(|P|_{\max}\) of equation (24.10) for \(\zeta > 0\). This is item 1.
  4. If \(b \le 0\) (that is \(\zeta \ge 1/\sqrt2\)), then for every \(u > 0\) \(g(u) - 1 = u(u - 2b) > 0\) and \(g\) is increasing on \((0, \infty)\), because \((u - b)^2\) is increasing there. So \(|P|/a_{\mathrm{u}} = g^{-1/2}\) is decreasing in \(u\), that is increasing in \(r\), and \(g \to 1\) as \(u \to 0\) (that is \(r \to \infty\)), hence \(|P| < a_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(|P| \to a_{\mathrm{u}}\). This is item 2.
  5. For \(\zeta = 0\), \(g(u) = (u - 1)^2 \to 0\) as \(u \to 1\) and \(|P| \to \infty\). This is item 3.

Source: FPGL section 6.2.2 (PDF p. 253): from \(|r_0|\) in equation (6.20) the maximum is found by differentiating \(|r_0|^2\) with respect to \(R^2\), with the result \(R^2 = 1/(1 - 2\zeta^2)\) in the unnumbered equation below it. The proof by completing the square is not from the source.

So the critical speed \(\Omega_{\mathrm{c}}\) does not coincide exactly with the natural frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). For R1 with \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), \(r_{\mathrm{c}} = 1{,}00040\), that is \(\Omega_{\mathrm{c}} = 1483{,}75\) rev/min against \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = 1483{,}16\) rev/min, and the peak is \(25{,}005\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) against \(25{,}000\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) at \(r = 1\). So we may equate the critical speed with the natural frequency, but only for small damping. At \(\zeta = 0{,}1\) it is \(r_{\mathrm{c}} = 1{,}0102\) and at \(\zeta = 0{,}3\) already \(1{,}1043\). In chapter 9 the peak of the dynamic factor \(D(r)\) was at \(r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} < 1\) (theorem 9.66, item 1), here it is at \(r > 1\). The reason is the factor \(r^2\) in theorem 24.7: the force \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\) grows with speed, so the response keeps growing even where \(D\) already falls. At slow turning the force is small, at \(r = 0{,}5\) it is only a quarter of the force at \(r = 1\).

A critical speed is not a property of every rotor. At damping \(\zeta = 0{,}6\) the theorem (item 1) gives \(r_{\mathrm{c}} = 1{,}8898\) with a peak \(1{,}0417\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\), barely above \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\), and the peak has almost gone (figure 24.21 shows the case \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), where it is still large). At \(\zeta = 0{,}8\), by item 2, \(|P| = 0{,}9647\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) at \(r = 3\), \(0{,}9972\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) at \(r = 10\) and \(0{,}99997\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) at \(r = 100\), always less than \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) and with no peak (SymPy, NumPy). Above the limit \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt2\) there is no critical speed. For R1, \(\zeta = 0{,}02\), far below the limit \(1/\sqrt2 \approx 0{,}707\), so R1 has a sharp peak at the critical speed.

Where does the centre of mass go? Figure 24.21 has also the blackwhite curve: the distance of the centre of mass from the bearing axis, \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}|\). The centre of mass is at \(p_{\mathrm{G}} = p + a_{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t} = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,(1 + H_{\mathrm{u}})\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}\) with \(H_{\mathrm{u}} = P/a_{\mathrm{u}} = r^2/(1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r)\). For large \(r\) the curve goes to zero, for small \(r\) to \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\), because there \(|p|\) is small and the centre of mass is almost \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) from the centre.

Corollary 24.10 Self-centring (samocentriranje)

In the setting of theorem 24.7 the distance of the centre of mass from the bearing axis is

\[|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = a_{\mathrm{u}}\,\frac{\sqrt{1 + 4\zeta^2r^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2r^2}} . \tag{24.11}\]

As \(r \to \infty\), \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| \to 0\) and \(|p| \to a_{\mathrm{u}}\). For \(\zeta = 0\) and \(r \ne 1\), \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = a_{\mathrm{u}}/|1 - r^2|\) and \(|p| = a_{\mathrm{u}}r^2/|1 - r^2|\), so \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = |p|/r^2\). Without damping the centre of mass is thus farther from the bearing axis than the disk centre for \(r < 1\) and nearer for \(r > 1\).

Proof

We must show the formula that follows from the theorem, and the limits.

  1. Let \(H_{\mathrm{u}} = P/a_{\mathrm{u}} = r^2/(1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r)\). By theorem 24.7, \(p = a_{\mathrm{u}}H_{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}\). The centre of mass is \(p_{\mathrm{G}} = p + a_{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}\) \(= a_{\mathrm{u}}(1 + H_{\mathrm{u}})\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}\) (definition 24.6).
  2. From \(1 + H_{\mathrm{u}} = (1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r + r^2)/(1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r) = (1 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r)/(1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm i\zeta r)\) and \(|\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\Omega t}| = 1\) we get equation (24.11).
  3. As \(r \to \infty\) the numerator \(\sqrt{1 + 4\zeta^2r^2} \sim 2\zeta r\) for \(\zeta > 0\) (and \(1\) for \(\zeta = 0\)), while the denominator grows as \(r^2\), so the quotient tends to zero. The radius \(|p| = a_{\mathrm{u}}r^2D(r)\) has, after division by \(r^2\), the denominator \(\sqrt{(1/r^2 - 1)^2 + 4\zeta^2/r^2} \to 1\), hence \(|p| \to a_{\mathrm{u}}\).
  4. For \(\zeta = 0\), \(H_{\mathrm{u}} = r^2/(1 - r^2)\) and \(1 + H_{\mathrm{u}} = 1/(1 - r^2)\). From this \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}|\) and \(|p|\) and their ratio \(r^2\). The ratio \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}|/|p| = 1/r^2\) is larger than \(1\) exactly for \(r < 1\).

Source: FPGL section 6.2.2 (PDF p. 254-256, figures 6.6 and 6.7) describes the same as the figure: below the critical speed the heavy side is outside the orbit of the centre, above it inside, and at a very high speed the rotor turns about the heavy side. The proof is ours.

The reason for self-centring (figure 24.22, the panel \(r = 2\), where the centre of mass is inside the orbit of the centre) is Newton's second law, as at the start of the section. Without damping the only force on the disk is the force of the shaft \(-k\,p\). The centre of mass goes round with radius \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}|\) and angular velocity \(\Omega\), so its acceleration is \(\Omega^2|p_{\mathrm{G}}|\), hence \(m\,\Omega^2|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = k|p|\) and \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = |p|/r^2\), as the corollary says. For large \(r\) the number \(r^2\) is large, so \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}|\) must be small. Indeed the spring is stretched only by \(|p| \le |p_{\mathrm{G}}| + a_{\mathrm{u}}\), because \(S\) is \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) away from \(G\). From \(r^2|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = |p| \le |p_{\mathrm{G}}| + a_{\mathrm{u}}\) we get for \(r > 1\) the bound \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| \le a_{\mathrm{u}}/(r^2 - 1)\). A spring stretched this little gives no force for a large acceleration, so the centre of mass hardly moves and the disk turns about its centre of mass. At \(r = 3\), \(|p_{\mathrm{G}}| = 0{,}1259\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) with damping \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) and \(0{,}1250\,a_{\mathrm{u}}\) without it.

Let us see what this means for the shaft and the bearings. The rotor R1 has \(a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0{,}05\) mm and \(\zeta = 0{,}02\). At \(r = 1\) the radius of the circle is \(|P| = a_{\mathrm{u}}/(2\zeta) = 1{,}250\) mm. The force of the shaft on the disk is \(k|P| = 1{,}1822\cdot10^6 \cdot 1{,}250\cdot10^{-3} = 1478\) N and points towards the bearing axis (theorem 24.7, step 4), and the disk acts with such a force \(F\) on the shaft.

We draw the shaft, bent under this force, and the bearing axis dashed. The shaft has no mass, so the equilibrium of forces and moments on it is the same as for a static load: at midspan the disk acts with the force \(F\) (away from the axis, by the law of action and reaction), and each of the bearings holds it with a force \(F/2\) in the opposite direction (symmetry, the sum of the forces on the shaft is zero). So each bearing carries \(F/2 = 739\) N, which is \(3{,}07\) times the static force under the weight of the disk (\(mg/2 = 240{,}4\) N). The moment at midspan is \(FL/4 = 369{,}5\) N m (figure 5.118, a beam with a force at midspan) and the bending stress in the fibre on the outer side of the bend by theorem 5.35 is \(\sigma = M\,(d/2)/I = 58{,}8\) MPa, while the weight alone gives \(19{,}13\) MPa.

The fibres of the shaft are not fatigued by alternating bending here. In the four pictures below the centre \(S\) of the section of the shaft is each time at another place on the dashed path round \(O\), but by theorem 24.7 the angle from \(O\) to \(S\) is \(\Omega t - \varphi\) and a fibre on the surface of the shaft has the angle \(\theta_0 + \Omega t\). Their difference \(\theta_0 + \varphi\) is independent of time, so the fibre is always on the same side of the bend (the drawn fibre on the outer side), and the stress in it is constant. With alternating bending it would swing between tension and compression. FPGL (PDF p. 265) writes that elliptical orbits cause alternating stresses and forward circular ones do not. The reason for ellipses follows in the lemma below.

The theorem assumes the same stiffness in both planes \(v\) and \(w\). With rigid bearings this assumption is met, because the cross-section of the shaft is round (step 4 of the proof of the theorem). In reality a bearing on a pedestal is not equally stiff in all directions, the horizontal stiffness is usually lower than the vertical one (FPGL, PDF p. 263). What then happens to the orbit? We replace the idealization of rigid bearings by bearings that have springs in series with the shaft. Let the disk R1 with the same unbalance lie on bearings with stiffness \(2\cdot10^6\) N/m in the direction \(v\) and \(6\cdot10^6\) N/m in the direction \(w\), each. Under a force \(F\) at midspan the shaft bends by \(F/k\) and each support sinks by \((F/2)/k_{\mathrm{b}}\) (figure 24.26), so the midspan sinks by \(F/k + F/(2k_{\mathrm{b}})\) (lemma 5.60: the displacement of a rigid body and the bending add). The stiffness at midspan is therefore \(1/k_{\mathrm{v}} = 1/k + 1/(2k_{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{v}})\), numerically \(k_{\mathrm{v}} = 9{,}1253\cdot10^5\) N/m and \(k_{\mathrm{w}} = 1{,}0762\cdot10^6\) N/m. The natural circular frequencies are \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}} = 136{,}45\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{w}} = 148{,}19\) 1/s, that is \(1303{,}0\) and \(1415{,}1\) rev/min. We neglect damping to keep the calculation clear.

Lemma 24.11 Unbalance with different stiffnesses (neuravnoteženost pri različnih togostih)

Let the Jeffcott rotor without damping of theorem 24.7 have stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{v}}\) at the disk in the plane \(v\) and \(k_{\mathrm{w}}\) in the plane \(w\), \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{v}}/m} < \omega_{\mathrm{w}} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{w}}/m}\), and let \(\Omega \ne \omega_{\mathrm{v}}, \omega_{\mathrm{w}}\). Then:

  1. One solution is \(v = A_{\mathrm{v}}\cos\Omega t\), \(w = A_{\mathrm{w}}\sin\Omega t\) with \(A_{\mathrm{v}} = a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2/(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}^2 - \Omega^2)\) and \(A_{\mathrm{w}} = a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2/(\omega_{\mathrm{w}}^2 - \Omega^2)\). The disk centre moves on an ellipse with semi-axes \(|A_{\mathrm{v}}|\) and \(|A_{\mathrm{w}}|\) along the axes.
  2. The orbit is in forward whirl if \(A_{\mathrm{v}}A_{\mathrm{w}} > 0\) and in backward whirl if \(A_{\mathrm{v}}A_{\mathrm{w}} < 0\). The latter holds exactly for \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}} < \Omega < \omega_{\mathrm{w}}\).
  3. As \(\Omega \to \omega_{\mathrm{v}}\) and as \(\Omega \to \omega_{\mathrm{w}}\) one semi-axis tends to infinity: there are two critical speeds.
Proof

We must show the solution by checking it, the shape of the orbit and the sign of the turning.

  1. Steps 1 to 7 of the proof of theorem 24.7 with \(c = 0\) and potential energy \(\tfrac12(k_{\mathrm{v}}v^2 + k_{\mathrm{w}}w^2)\) give \(m\ddot v + k_{\mathrm{v}}v = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\) and \(m\ddot w + k_{\mathrm{w}}w = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\sin\Omega t\).
  2. For \(v = A\cos\Omega t\), \(\ddot v = -\Omega^2 v\) and \((k_{\mathrm{v}} - m\Omega^2)A = m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2\), so \(A = a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2/(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}^2 - \Omega^2)\). Likewise for \(w\) with a sine. So \(A_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{w}}\) are those of item 1. Every other solution differs from it by a free vibration in each plane (theorem 9.62, item 2), which does not decay without damping. This solution is the one that remains under however small a damping once the free vibration has died out: by the same theorem, with damping \(c\) the amplitude in the plane \(v\) is \(m a_{\mathrm{u}}\Omega^2/|k_{\mathrm{v}} - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm i\,c\Omega|\), which tends to \(|A_{\mathrm{v}}|\) as \(c \to 0\), because \(k_{\mathrm{v}} - m\Omega^2 \ne 0\). The same holds in the plane \(w\).
  3. From \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) it follows that \((v/A_{\mathrm{v}})^2 + (w/A_{\mathrm{w}})^2 = 1\), an ellipse with semi-axes \(|A_{\mathrm{v}}|\) and \(|A_{\mathrm{w}}|\).
  4. Let \(\vartheta(t)\) be the angle from the \(v\) axis to the centre, so \(v = |p|\cos\vartheta\) and \(w = |p|\sin\vartheta\). Here \(|p| > 0\), because an ellipse with nonzero semi-axes does not pass through \(O\). By the product rule (lemma 1.82, item 3) and the chain rule (lemma 4.43), \(\dot v = \dot{|p|}\cos\vartheta - |p|\,\dot\vartheta\sin\vartheta\) and \(\dot w = \dot{|p|}\sin\vartheta + |p|\,\dot\vartheta\cos\vartheta\), so \(v\dot w - w\dot v = |p|^2\dot\vartheta\,(\cos^2\vartheta + \sin^2\vartheta) = |p|^2\dot\vartheta\). Here \(v\dot w - w\dot v = A_{\mathrm{v}}\cos\Omega t\cdot A_{\mathrm{w}}\Omega\cos\Omega t + A_{\mathrm{w}}\sin\Omega t\cdot A_{\mathrm{v}}\Omega\sin\Omega t = A_{\mathrm{v}}A_{\mathrm{w}}\Omega\), a constant. The angle \(\vartheta\) of the centre therefore grows (\(\dot\vartheta > 0\), turning in the sense of the shaft, forward whirl, definition 24.3) exactly when \(A_{\mathrm{v}}A_{\mathrm{w}} > 0\).
  5. The product \(A_{\mathrm{v}}A_{\mathrm{w}} = a_{\mathrm{u}}^2\Omega^4/\big((\omega_{\mathrm{v}}^2 - \Omega^2)(\omega_{\mathrm{w}}^2 - \Omega^2)\big)\) is negative exactly when the two factors in the denominator have opposite signs, that is for \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}^2 < \Omega^2 < \omega_{\mathrm{w}}^2\), since \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}} < \omega_{\mathrm{w}}\).
  6. If \(\Omega \to \omega_{\mathrm{v}}\), then \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}^2 - \Omega^2 \to 0\) and \(|A_{\mathrm{v}}| \to \infty\). The same for \(\omega_{\mathrm{w}}\) and \(A_{\mathrm{w}}\).

Source: FPGL sections 6.2.5 and 6.2.6 (PDF p. 263-266): the orbit of a rigid rotor on anisotropic bearings is an ellipse and backward whirl occurs in speed ranges that, for damped bearings, are roughly between the two critical speeds (example 6.2.3, figure 6.13, PDF p. 265-266), and damping can remove it. The proof for the undamped model is ours.

Let us look at the three speeds on the horizontal axis \(\Omega\) of the previous figure.

At \(1042\) rev/min, below both natural frequencies, \(A_{\mathrm{v}} = 0{,}0887\) mm and \(A_{\mathrm{w}} = 0{,}0592\) mm are positive and the orbit is an ellipse in forward whirl. At \(1359\) rev/min, between \(1303\) and \(1415\) rev/min, \(A_{\mathrm{v}} = -0{,}6199\) mm and \(A_{\mathrm{w}} = +0{,}5935\) mm, and the orbit is almost a circle of large radius with backward whirl. The reason is physical: the plane \(v\) is already above its resonance, where the response opposes the force, while the plane \(w\) is still below it, where the response follows the force, and the two motions combine into the opposite sense. At \(1698\) rev/min, above both, both \(A\) are negative (\(-0{,}1216\) and \(-0{,}1637\) mm) and the orbit is forward again, but with the longer axis along \(w\). Theorem 24.7 therefore relies on equal stiffness in both planes. Without it there is no circle, no constant stress in a fibre (the centre moves unevenly while the fibre of the shaft turns evenly, so the angle between them changes) and no single critical speed.

We got to know the response to unbalance on the simplest model and saw that, as a function of the ratio \(r\), two numbers determine it, \(a_{\mathrm{u}}\) and \(\zeta\).
