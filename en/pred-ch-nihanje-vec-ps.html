Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

Chapter 10Lecture: Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

1 A mode: when the floors move in step

First we draw the ground. Out of it rise steel columns, which we draw grey dashed, as they stand at rest. On the columns there are two rigid floors (stropa), the lower and the upper one, each with a mass of 500 kg. This is frame A of the book. Now we take hold of both floors, move them to the right and let go. What happens? The floors start to swing, and in general they swing in a tangled way: one runs ahead, the other lags behind.

The question of this hour is: is there a starting shape from which both floors swing like a single pendulum? So that they pass through zero at the same instant and reach their largest displacement together, for ever?

The answer is yes, and there are exactly two such shapes. In the first the columns bend into the shape of a letter S, the lower floor moves by \(1\), and the upper floor by \(1{,}618\), in the same direction. The frame then swings at 3,116 Hz. In the second the columns bend differently: the lower floor again moves by \(1\), the upper one by \(0{,}618\) in the opposite direction. This shape swings at 8,158 Hz. The displacements are greatly enlarged in the figure, the real ones are a few millimetres.

The claim we explain in this hour is about these two shapes: only in them is the spring force on each floor the same multiple of mass times displacement, and so both floors run on the same clock. Such a shape is called a mode shape (način nihanja). The name says that it is a way, a mode, in which the frame can swing by itself, without us.

The claim contains five things we have not explained yet. Each is its own why. Let us go in order.

1.1 Why do we talk about a shape and not only about a frequency?

The claim names a shape and a frequency. Would it not be enough to say how fast the frame swings? What does the shape say that the frequency does not? Let us draw how the frame moves in time in the first mode. On the horizontal axis we put time, on the vertical one the displacement of a floor. We release the lower floor from 5 mm and the upper one from \(5 \cdot 1{,}618 = 8{,}09\) mm, that is, in the shape of the first mode. Both swing as a cosine. Look at when they pass through zero: both together, first at 0,0802 s, a quarter of the period \(T_1 = 0{,}321\) s.

What does every instant of this motion have in common? The ratio \(q_2/q_1\). When the lower floor is at half of its displacement, the upper one is at half of its own. So we can write the motion as one shape times one function of time:

\[\vek q(t) = \boldsymbol\varphi\, g(t) , \qquad \boldsymbol\varphi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1{,}618 \end{bmatrix} .\]

The column \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) is the shape, which does not change, \(g(t)\) is one clock for both floors. "In step" therefore means: one shape and one clock. The frequency alone does not say this, since it only says how fast the clock runs, not whether it is the same clock for both floors. In the book: 10.2, equation (10.20).

Here we took the first assumption: nothing damps the frame and no external force acts on it. Why is it right here? We ask how the frame swings by itself. The damping the book takes in the third part, 2 % in each mode, lowers the frequency only by the factor \(\sqrt{1 - 0{,}02^2} = 0{,}9998\). When does it not hold? When we shake the frame or when something damps it strongly. What does it do to the result? Without damping the swinging in the figure goes on for ever with the same amplitudes. With that damping the shape stays the same, and the amplitude drops by about 12 % in each swing.

1.2 Why do the floors not swing in step from every shape?

What decides how fast one mass on one spring swings? Chapter 9 answers with the number \(\omega^2 = k/m\): the spring force per unit displacement, divided by the mass. A larger force for the same displacement means a faster clock, a larger mass a slower one. The frame has two floors. If they are to run on the same clock, each floor must "ask for" the same number: the force acting on it, divided by its displacement and by its mass. Can every shape do that? Let us test it. We draw the ground and the frame at rest, grey dashed.

We move the floors into a shape close to the first mode but not equal to it: we draw the displaced frame orange, the lower floor by \(1\), the upper one by \(1{,}5\). The columns resist and pull the floors back towards equilibrium. On the lower floor acts a force \(0{,}5\,k\), on the upper one also \(0{,}5\,k\). Where these two forces come from we see in the next step.

What does each floor ask for? The lower one has the force \(0{,}5\,k\) on the displacement \(1\) and the mass \(m\). With \(k/m = 1003{,}52\) 1/s\(^2\) this gives \(501{,}76\) 1/s\(^2\). The upper one has the same force, but on the displacement \(1{,}5\), so it asks only for \(334{,}51\) 1/s\(^2\). Two different numbers mean two different clocks. The lower floor would want to swing faster than the upper one, so it runs ahead and the shape \((1;\ 1{,}5)\) falls apart.

Let us try the shape of the first mode: the lower floor by \(1\), the upper one by \(1{,}618\). The forces are now different, \(0{,}382\,k\) on the lower floor, \(0{,}618\,k\) on the upper one. Dividing by the displacements we get \(383{,}31\) for the lower one and \(383{,}31\) for the upper one as well, in 1/s\(^2\). The same number, the same clock. The upper floor has a larger force, but also a larger displacement, and the ratio comes out exactly.

The book writes this condition as one equation for both floors at once, row by row, one row per floor:

\[\mat K\boldsymbol\varphi = \lambda\,\mat M\boldsymbol\varphi .\]

On the left is the spring force on each floor, on the right the same number \(\lambda\), times mass, times displacement. A shape \(\boldsymbol\varphi\) for which such a \(\lambda\) exists is a mode shape, and \(\omega = \sqrt\lambda\) is its circular frequency. In the book: 10.2.1 and equation (10.21).

1.3 Where do the forces on the floors come from?

We said that the columns pull each floor back with the force \(0{,}5\,k\). Who exactly pulls which floor, and with what force? Let us draw the frame once more, now in more detail. The ground, the columns at rest dashed. The displaced columns we draw orange: the lower pair goes from the ground to the lower floor, the upper pair from the lower floor to the upper one. On them sit the lower floor and the upper floor, each with mass \(m = 500\) kg. The lower floor moves by \(q_1\), the upper one by \(q_2\), both measured from equilibrium.

Now we cut each floor out and replace the columns by the forces they exert on it. This is the free-body diagram (diagram prostega telesa). First the lower floor alone. The lower pair of columns has one end in the ground and the other in the floor, and the ends are offset by \(q_1\). The pair pulls the floor back, to the left with the force \(k\,q_1\). The upper pair has its ends offset by \(q_2 - q_1\) and pulls the lower floor to the right with the force \(k\,(q_2 - q_1)\). Then the upper floor. Only the upper pair acts on it, to the left with the force \(k\,(q_2 - q_1)\). An external force \(Q_2\) is absent this hour, \(Q_2 = 0\).

Let us check the trial of the previous figure, \(q_1 = 1\), \(q_2 = 1{,}5\):

\[\text{lower: } -k \cdot 1 + k\,(1{,}5 - 1) = -0{,}5\,k , \qquad \text{upper: } -k\,(1{,}5 - 1) = -0{,}5\,k .\]

These are the two arrows \(0{,}5\,k\), both to the left. Why does the lower floor have two terms and the upper one only one? Because the lower floor is held by two storeys (etaži), the upper one by only one. That is why the first row of the stiffness matrix has \(2k\) and the second only \(k\):

\[\mat K = k\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} , \qquad \mat M = m\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} .\]

In the book: 10.1.1, equation (10.19).

Two assumptions entered here. The first: the columns have no mass. Why is it right here? The two columns of a storey weigh 12,48 kg together, 2,5 % of the mass of a floor. When does it not hold? For light floors and long, heavy columns. What does it do to the result? With the masses counted both frequencies would be slightly lower, an error of the order of one percent. The second: the displacements are small and the force is proportional to the offset. Why is it right? At a few millimetres per metre of column the shortening of the column along its axis is of second order and we do not count it. When not? For a large swing, when the columns get noticeably shorter. What does it do? Without it the equation would not be linear, and everything that follows would hold only approximately.

1.4 Why is a storey a spring with stiffness 501 760 N/m?

A column is a steel bar, not a coil spring. Why then does it behave as a spring? And how much force does it take to move its top by a given displacement? Take a single column. At the bottom it is clamped in the ground. Its axis before loading we draw dashed, and the column is \(h = 1\) m long. At the top it is clamped in the floor. We move the floor, and the top end moves by \(v\), but does not rotate, since the floor holds it level. What force does this take? On the end acts a force \(F\) in the direction of the displacement, and also a couple \(F h/2\), by which the floor keeps the end parallel to the original axis. So the axis bends into the shape of a letter S: the lower half to one side, the upper half to the other.

The ground is a measured fact: for small bending the displacement is proportional to the force. How far the top of a cantilever moves under a force and how far under a couple we know from chapter 5. Since the displacement is proportional to the load, we may treat the force and the couple each on its own and add the displacements. Let us draw this on the board.

Again the ground and the axis of the column dashed, three times. On the left column we push the top with the force \(F\) alone. The column bends like a cantilever, the top moves by \(F h^3/(3EI)\) and tilts as it does. On the middle column acts only the couple \(F h/2\), which turns the top back. It moves the top the other way, by \(F h^3/(4EI)\). On the right column both act at once. The tilts cancel, so the top stays level, as the floor holds it, and the displacements subtract: what is left is \(F h^3/(3EI) - F h^3/(4EI) = F h^3/(12EI)\). So

\[k_{\mathrm{s}} = \frac{12\,EI}{h^3} = \frac{12 \cdot 20\,906{,}7\ \text{N m}^2}{(1\ \text{m})^3} = 250\,880\ \text{N/m} , \qquad k = 2\,k_{\mathrm{s}} = 501\,760\ \text{N/m} .\]

A storey has two columns, their forces add, so the stiffness of the storey is twice as large. Without the couple the column would be a free cantilever with \(3EI/h^3 = 62\,720\) N/m, four times softer. In the book: 10.1.1, the steps with the equation of the end of a cantilever.

Adding the displacements rests on the assumption of small displacements, which we already took for the forces on the floors: the displacement of the top is a few millimetres per metre of length. For large bending the stiffness \(k_{\mathrm{s}}\) would depend on the displacement.

Two more assumptions entered here. The first: the floor is rigid and the column does not rotate in it. Why is it right? In the model the floor is a rigid body, while the columns are slender, 20 mm \(\times\) 40 mm over 1 m of length, so it is mainly the columns that bend. When not? A real floor has beams that bend a little and soften the clamping. What does it do? The real stiffness is smaller than 501 760 N/m. By how much at most? If the end could rotate freely, the couple would vanish and only the left column would be left, with 62 720 N/m. The real stiffness lies between the two bounds. The second: the weight of the floors on the columns is not counted. Why is it right? Each lower column carries 4905 N of compression, and this compression lowers the stiffness of the lower storey by only 2,35 %, of the upper one by 1,17 %. When not? In a tall building with thin columns, where the compression is large. What does it do? The two frequencies of frame A drop by 1,0 % and 0,75 %.

1.5 Why exactly 1,618?

Why do the clocks agree exactly at \(1{,}618\) and not at \(1{,}5\) or at \(2\)? Could we have computed this number in advance, without trying? Now we have everything for the answer. Take the shape \((1;\ r)\), the lower floor displaced by \(1\), the upper one by \(r\), and look again at the free-body diagram. On the upper floor acts the force \(k\,(r - 1)\). On the lower floor act \(k \cdot 1\) to the left and \(k\,(r - 1)\) to the right, together \(k\,(2 - r)\) to the left.

The floors have equal masses. If they are to ask for the same clock, the ratio of the forces must equal the ratio of the displacements, that is \(r\):

\[\frac{k\,(r - 1)}{k\,(2 - r)} = r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r - 1 = 2r - r^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^2 - r - 1 = 0 .\]

This is the equation of the golden ratio (zlati rez). A quadratic equation has two roots, and this counting is what tells us why there are exactly two modes:

\[r_1 = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} = 1{,}618 , \qquad r_2 = \frac{1 - \sqrt5}{2} = -0{,}618 .\]

The first we see on the left of the first figure, the second on the right. From \(r - 1 = 1/r\) one more detail follows: in the first mode the upper storey drifts by \(0{,}618\), which is \(0{,}618\) times the drift of the lower one.

The clock of each mode is the number the lower floor asks for, \(\lambda = (2 - r)\,k/m\):

\[\lambda_1 = 0{,}382 \cdot 1003{,}52 = 383{,}31\ \tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2} \;\Rightarrow\; f_1 = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_1}}{2\pi} = 3{,}116\ \text{Hz} , \qquad \lambda_2 = 2{,}618 \cdot 1003{,}52 = 2627{,}25\ \tfrac{1}{\text{s}^2} \;\Rightarrow\; f_2 = 8{,}158\ \text{Hz} .\]

These are the two frequencies of the first figure.

Here the last assumption entered: equal masses. Why is it right? Both floors have 500 kg. When not? With a heavier lower floor, for example. What does it do? The ratio of the forces must then equal the ratio of the masses times \(r\), the equation stays quadratic and there are still two modes, only the golden ratio no longer appears.

1.6 Back up

Now we walk the way from the ground up, in four steps:

  1. A column that the floor holds without rotation is a spring, a storey has stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m.
  2. So the forces on the floors are \(k\,q_1\) and \(k\,(q_2 - q_1)\).
  3. So each floor asks for its own clock, force per displacement and mass.
  4. The two clocks agree only at \(r = 1{,}618\) and \(r = -0{,}618\), with 3,116 Hz and 8,158 Hz.

And back to the first figure. In the first mode the floors move together, both storeys share the bending and the frame swings slowly. In the second the floors move against each other, the upper storey drifts much more and the frame swings fast. Why fast, the last of the perspectives below tells.

1.7 The perspective of forces

This is the one minute explanation. In a mode each floor on its own is a pendulum with one mass and one spring. In the first mode the lower floor feels the force \(0{,}382\,k = 191\,655\) N/m times its displacement. The upper one feels \(310\,105\) N/m times the displacement of the lower floor, which is again \(191\,655\) N/m times its own displacement \(1{,}618\). Both floors therefore hang on an equally stiff spring and have the same mass, so both give \(383{,}31\). What does this view add? A mode is nothing mysterious: two equal pendulums with the same clock, which the frame carries at once.

1.8 The perspective of time

What does a motion look like that is not a mode? Take a sum: the first mode with 5 mm and the second with 2 mm on the lower floor. On the right half we see the lower floor, which first passes through zero at 0,0593 s, and the upper one, which passes through it only at 0,0835 s. Not together, so not in step. What does this view add? What "not a mode" looks like, and why the modes are special solutions. That every motion is a sum of modes is shown by the second part.

1.9 The perspective of algebra

A computer does not try shapes. It writes the demand of both rows for the same \(\lambda\) as the condition that the system \((\mat K - \lambda\mat M)\boldsymbol\varphi = \vek 0\) has a nonzero solution, that is, that the determinant is zero:

\[\det(\mat K - \lambda\mat M) = m^2\lambda^2 - 3km\,\lambda + k^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = \frac{3 \mp \sqrt5}{2}\,\frac{k}{m} .\]

This gives the same \(383{,}31\) and \(2627{,}25\) 1/s\(^2\). What does this view add? A route without trying. Its limit: we can compute the determinant only for two coordinates, for more the book finds the eigenvalues another way. In the book: (10.23) and 10.3.

1.10 The perspective of hand and eye

Why is the second mode faster? Take the frame by hand. In the first mode the lower storey drifts by \(1\) and the upper one by \(1{,}618 - 1 = 0{,}618\). In the second the lower one again by \(1\), but the upper one by \(1 + 0{,}618 = 1{,}618\), since the floors move apart. For the same displacement of the lower floor the upper columns in the second mode are bent much more than in the first, the forces are larger, and the masses are the same. A larger force on the same mass means a faster clock: \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 6{,}854\), and the frequencies are in the ratio \(2{,}618\). What does this view add? We can estimate the frequency of a mode just from how much it bends the columns.

2 Every motion is a sum of modes

Last time we found two special shapes of frame A, the modes (načina), in which both floors swing in step. Today we start from a shape that is not a mode. We push the upper floor with a force until it has moved by 10 mm. The lower floor then moves by 5 mm. Then we suddenly take the force away. How do the floors move?

Let us draw a diagram, time runs to the right, the displacement from the equilibrium position upwards. First the lower floor, which starts at 5 mm, then the upper floor, which starts at 10 mm. Does anything repeat? The peaks are not equally high, the shape of the curve changes from swing to swing. What is such a motion made of?

Let us give the answer in one sentence and then explain it. This tangle is two modes swinging at once, each as its own one-degree oscillator on its own clock, and the share of each is fixed at the start and never passes to the other. Let us draw a second diagram with the same time axis. In it are the share of the first mode, a pure cosine with period \(T_1 = 0{,}32093\) s, and the share of the second mode, a small pure cosine with period \(T_2 = 0{,}12258\) s.

The claim uses four things we have not shown yet. That every starting shape can be written as a sum of modes. That each share swings by itself. How we compute a share. And that there are neither more nor fewer modes than floors. Let us take them in turn.

2.1 Why can we write the starting shape as a sum of two modes?

The starting shape is not a mode. Can we still build it out of the two modes? Let us try. Let us draw the frame in the equilibrium position, columns vertical. Then let us draw it under the force on the upper floor: the lower floor moved by 5 mm, the upper one by 10 mm. Why exactly twice as much? The force on the upper floor goes through the upper storey and then through the lower storey. Both storeys carry the same force and are equally stiff, so each drifts by 5 mm. The upper floor gets both drifts, \(5 + 5 = 10\) mm.

Now we take the two modes out of the same shape. Let us draw the contribution of the first mode, \((5{,}854,\ 9{,}472)\) mm, with the upper floor at 9,472 mm. It is almost our starting shape already. Let us draw the contribution of the second mode, \((-0{,}854,\ 0{,}528)\) mm, small, with the lower floor in the opposite direction. Let us add them floor by floor:

\[5{,}854 - 0{,}854 = 5\ \text{mm} , \qquad 9{,}472 + 0{,}528 = 10\ \text{mm} .\]

We get the starting shape.

Why does this always work? Two floors mean two numbers, so a point in a plane. Let us draw it.

For the axes we take the displacement of the upper floor \(y_1 = q_2\) and the drift of the upper storey \(y_2 = q_2 - q_1\). Our shape is the point \(\vek y = (10,\ 5)\) mm. Through the origin we draw the lines of both modes. From the tip of \(\vek y\) we draw the parallels to the modes and get a parallelogram. Its sides are the share of the first mode, \((9{,}472,\ 3{,}618)\) mm, and the share of the second, \((0{,}528,\ 1{,}382)\) mm. That is the whole ground: in a plane we reach every point by moving along two directions that do not lie on one line. Anyone can check this with a ruler.

Assumption: the two frequencies are distinct. Why it holds here: 3,116 Hz and 8,158 Hz from the previous part, and modes with different frequencies lie on two different lines. When not: with two equal frequencies every direction in the plane is a mode, so the choice of modes is not unique. What it does to the result: the splitting still exists, the list of frequencies is the same, only the picture of the modes is not a single one. In the book: 10.22.

2.2 Why does each share move by itself?

Why does the first share not disturb the second, although the same frame carries both? The answer is in the forces. Where does the force on a floor come from? From the drift of the storeys: a storey is a spring that pushes back in proportion to its drift, with stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m. Let us draw three frames side by side.

First the contribution of the first mode. The lower storey drifts by 5,854 mm, the upper one by 3,618 mm, that is the difference \(9{,}472 - 5{,}854\). We add the contribution of the second mode: the lower storey drifts by \(-0{,}854\) mm, in the opposite direction, the upper one by 1,382 mm. The sum is the starting shape with the drifts 5 mm and 5 mm. So the drifts add, and since the force of a storey is \(k\) times its drift, the forces add too. For the lower storey we write:

\[k \cdot 5{,}854\ \text{mm} + k \cdot (-0{,}854\ \text{mm}) = 2937{,}3\ \text{N} - 428{,}5\ \text{N} = 2508{,}8\ \text{N} ,\]

which is \(k\) times the drift 5 mm, exactly the force with which we pushed. Newton's law for a floor is therefore the sum of two Newton's laws. If the first contribution obeys Newton by itself and the second does too, their sum obeys as well.

But each contribution is a mode, and from the previous part we know how a mode swings: in step, with its own cosine. So the motion of the floors is the sum of two cosines,

\[\vek q(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 5{,}854 \\ 9{,}472 \end{bmatrix}\cos(19{,}578\,t)\ \text{mm} + \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}854 \\ 0{,}528 \end{bmatrix}\cos(51{,}257\,t)\ \text{mm} ,\]

and these are exactly the first and the second cosine of the lower diagram. The idea of the proof is just this: we write the equation in the shares and each row contains a single share. In the book: 10.28, 10.31, (10.49).

Assumption: small displacements, force proportional to the drift. Why it holds: the drifts are a few millimetres per metre of storey height, the columns stay in the range where the force is proportional to the displacement. When not: in a large sway the stiffness changes with the displacement. What it does: the sum of two solutions is no longer a solution and the whole splitting into modes falls apart.

Assumption: no damping. Why we take it: so we see the modes clean, both cosines keep their height. When not: every real frame swings less and less. What it does: with so-called classical damping each share dies out by itself and the shares still do not mix, with other damping they do. In the book: 10.10.1, 10.10.4.

2.3 Why do we not get the share by a perpendicular projection?

How would we find the share of the first mode with a ruler? Perhaps by dropping a perpendicular from the tip onto the line of the mode? Look once more at the lines of the modes. Are they perpendicular? They are not, the angle between them is \(48{,}2^\circ\). If we dropped a perpendicular from the tip of \(\vek y\) onto the line of the first mode, we would get a wrong share. The right shares are the sides of the parallelogram, parallel to the modes.

So what is zero between the modes, if not the ordinary dot product? The kinetic energy. If the frame moves with velocities in the shape of the first mode and at the same time of the second, the kinetic energy is the sum of the energies of the two motions, there is no mixed term. Written down this is \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_2 = 0\), and we call it \(\mat M\)-orthogonality (\(\mat M\)-ortogonalnost). So we get a share by one multiplication:

\[c_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q(0) , \qquad c_1 = 0{,}24899 , \quad c_2 = -0{,}022451\ \text{m kg}^{1/2} .\]

The unusual unit comes from our choice, we normalised the modes by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 1\). The share as a displacement, \(c_1\vek\varphi_1\) and \(c_2\vek\varphi_2\), does not depend on this choice. In the book: 10.27, (10.42).

A near case that misleads: in the coordinates \(q_1\), \(q_2\) and with equal masses the modes \((1,\ 1{,}618)\) and \((1,\ -0{,}618)\) are perpendicular in the ordinary sense, since \(1 \cdot 1 + 1{,}618 \cdot (-0{,}618) = 0\). This comes from the coordinates and the equal masses, it is not a general rule. On the parallelogram we see that in other coordinates perpendicularity disappears, while \(\mat M\)-orthogonality stays.

Assumption: the matrix \(\mat M\) is symmetric and positive definite. Why it holds: every motion in which something moves has a kinetic energy greater than zero, here the floors of 500 kg move. When not: a coordinate without mass, for example a point we model as massless. What it does: the normalisation \(\vek\varphi^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 1\) is no longer possible and the formula for the share fails.

2.4 Why are the modes M-orthogonal, and why are there exactly as many as floors?

So far we took the two modes from the previous part. But who guarantees that modes exist for every frame, that there are exactly as many as floors, and that there is no mixed energy between them? The answer is one picture. Let us draw the origin and the same axes as before, the displacement of the upper floor and the drift of the upper storey. In them we draw an ellipse: these are all shapes that, read as velocities, have the same kinetic energy. Among them we look for the shape that strains the storeys least. Curves of equal strain energy are ellipses around the origin, larger for larger energy. Take one that crosses the ellipse. At a crossing we can move along the ellipse to the side where the energy is smaller, so the smallest value is not there.

We shrink the curve until it touches the ellipse from inside, at the value 383,311 1/s\(^2\). The point of contact is the first mode \(\vek\varphi_1\), and the value is exactly \(\lambda_1\) from the previous part. At the largest curve that still touches the ellipse from outside, 2627,249 1/s\(^2\), we get the second mode \(\vek\varphi_2\). The ground is an observation anyone makes by hand and eye: the lowest point on a curve is where another curve just touches it.

With \(n\) floors there is no plane any more, but the idea stays. The first mode is the smallest value on the set of shapes with the same kinetic energy. The second is the smallest value among the shapes that are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to the first. And so on, one direction fewer each time, until the directions run out, that is after \(n\) steps. So there are \(n\) modes and they are \(\mat M\)-orthogonal by the way we found them. In the book: 10.3.1, 10.21.

Assumption: the matrix \(\mat K\) is symmetric. Why it holds: we get it from the strain energy of the springs. Its sign may be anything though: \(\lambda = 0\) is a motion in which no spring is stretched (10.5), \(\lambda < 0\) an equilibrium from which the frame runs away. For frame A both values, on the blue and on the orange curve, are positive, so both modes swing.

2.5 Why does the motion never repeat?

When would the motion have to repeat? Perhaps after the time \(T_1\), when the first mode has finished its swing? Let us see where the second one is then. Let us picture each mode as a clock. We draw the clock of the first mode with its hand. The share of the mode is the horizontal projection of the hand. In the time \(T_1\) the hand makes exactly one turn and is back where it started. Now we draw the clock of the second mode with its hand at the start. This clock runs faster. In the same time \(T_1\) it makes 2,618 turns and stops here, at an angle of \(222{,}49^\circ\) from the start. Its horizontal projection is \(-0{,}737\) of the starting one.

What does this mean for the frame? We draw the equilibrium position and the starting shape, at 5 mm and 10 mm. After the time \(T_1\) the first share is whole again, the second is multiplied by \(-0{,}737\):

\[\begin{bmatrix} 5{,}854 \\ 9{,}472 \end{bmatrix} - 0{,}737 \begin{bmatrix} -0{,}854 \\ 0{,}528 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6{,}484 \\ 9{,}083 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{mm} .\]

This is a new shape, 6,484 mm and 9,083 mm, not the starting one.

Would the motion repeat some time later? We would need an instant at which both hands are at the start together, so a whole number of turns of the first clock and a whole number of turns of the second. The ratio of the speeds of the clocks would have to be a fraction. But it is \((3 + \sqrt5)/2 = 2{,}618034\), and \(\sqrt5\) is not a fraction. The idea of the proof: if \(\sqrt5 = a/b\), then \(a^2 = 5b^2\), so both \(a\) and \(b\) would be divisible by 5, and the fraction could be reduced without end, which is impossible. In the book: 10.4.5.

2.6 Back up

Now we build the answer from the ground. From the curves touching the ellipse we get two modes with no mixed kinetic energy. So the starting shape is exactly one sum of two shares. Since the forces are proportional to the drifts, each share swings by itself, with its own cosine. The clocks run in the ratio 1 : 2,618, so the sum never repeats. That is the tangle at the start, made of two pure cosines.

2.7 The view through shapes, in a few minutes

Whoever has only a few minutes should keep the starting shape in mind: it is almost the first mode and a little of the second. Why so little of the second? We moved the frame with a force, and each mode moves under the force as much as it is compliant. The first mode is 6,854 times softer, since \(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = 2627{,}25/383{,}31 = 6{,}854\). Besides, the force acts on the upper floor, where the first mode has a large displacement and the second one smaller by a factor 1,618. The two causes multiply:

\[6{,}854 \cdot 1{,}618 = 11{,}09 = \frac{0{,}24899}{0{,}022451} .\]

What does this view add? Without computing the motion it tells which mode will dominate: the one that is soft and has a large displacement where we push.

2.8 The view through energy

That the shares do not mix can be measured with energy. We draw the time axis and on it the energy of the first mode \(E_1 = 11{,}882\) J, a horizontal line. Then the energy of the second \(E_2 = 0{,}662\) J, horizontal too. Their sum \(E = 12{,}544\) J is exactly the work the force did while it pushed the upper floor:

\[\tfrac12 \cdot 2508{,}8\ \text{N} \cdot 0{,}010\ \text{m} = 12{,}544\ \text{J} .\]

The first mode has 94,72 % of the energy, the second 5,28 %, and the lines stay horizontal. The potential energy of the whole frame swings. So energy flows between strain and motion inside each mode, and between the modes nothing. What does this view add? The claim "the shares do not mix" is no longer only a computation but two straight lines.

2.9 The view in the coordinates of the modes

Let us go back to the lower diagram. If we take as coordinates the shares instead of the displacements of the floors, the modal coordinates (modalne koordinate), each obeys its own equation

\[\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q(t) , \qquad r = 1, 2 ,\]

where \(\vek Q\) is the external force, zero today. In these coordinates the frame is one oscillator and another oscillator that know nothing of each other. What does this view add? With 16 or 100 coordinates a computer does not solve a coupled system but 16 or 100 equations of one degree of freedom, each by itself. The coupling was a property of the coordinates, not of the frame. In the book: (10.43), 10.28.

3 A force that swings: peaks and valleys

Let us go back to our frame. First the ground and the frame at rest, grey dashed. To the upper floor we bolt a shaker (stresalnik), a machine that pushes the floor back and forth with the force \(100\ \text{N}\cdot\cos\Omega t\). The frame starts to swing. We draw it orange at some instant: the columns of the lower storey, a spring of stiffness \(k = 501\,760\) N/m, bend, the lower floor of mass \(m = 500\) kg moves by \(q_1\), the columns of the upper storey with the same stiffness \(k\) carry the upper floor, again \(500\) kg, which moves by \(q_2\).

We turn the shaker's frequency slowly up from \(0\) to \(9\) Hz. At which frequencies does the frame swing wildly? And is there a frequency at which the pushed floor stands still, although we push it with \(100\) N?

Assumption: steady state. We measure only once the motion of the floors has settled. Why may we? At switch-on the free motion of the previous part adds to the steady response, but damping makes it die out (In the book: 10.72, item 4). So it holds when we turn the frequency slowly and wait. It does not hold just after switch-on, where we would see the sum of both motions. How long is that? The free motion of the first mode decays with the factor \(\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_1 t}\) and drops to \(37\ \%\) (the factor \(1/\mathrm{e}\)) in \(1/(\zeta\omega_1) = 1/(0{,}02\cdot 19{,}578\ \text{1/s}) = 2{,}55\) s. After ten seconds it is almost gone.

What do we measure? We draw the frequency \(f\) in Hz horizontally and vertically how many micrometres the floor moves per newton of force. Both axes are logarithmic, so that we see very large and very small motions at once. The upper floor moves calmly at low frequencies, then twice jumps to a peak, at \(3{,}12\) Hz and at \(8{,}16\) Hz, and in between drops into a narrow valley. For comparison we also draw the lower floor: the same two peaks, but only a shallow minimum between them.

The claim of this part is a single one. Each mode answers the shaker as an oscillator with one degree of freedom, so there is a peak at each natural frequency. Between the peaks, at \(7{,}13\) Hz, there is a valley where only the lower floor swings and the force of the upper storey on the upper floor cancels the shaker.

What does the frame look like? At \(0\) Hz it leans, the lower floor by \(0{,}20\) mm, the upper one by \(0{,}40\) mm. At \(3{,}12\) Hz it swings in the shape of the first mode, by \(5{,}83\) and \(9{,}44\) mm. At \(5{,}04\) Hz both floors move alike, by \(0{,}20\) mm. At \(7{,}13\) Hz only the lower floor moves, the upper one by just \(0{,}01\) mm. At \(8{,}16\) Hz it swings in the second mode, the floors against each other. That is the explanation in five drawings. Now let us ask why, step by step.

3.1 Why are the peaks exactly at \(3{,}12\) and \(8{,}16\) Hz?

Why does the frame swing wildly at exactly these two frequencies and at no other? What is special about them? From the previous part we know that every motion is a sum of modes and that each share lives on its own. A force does not spoil this. For the share \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\) of mode \(r\)

\[\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + 2\zeta_{\mathrm{r}}\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \omega_{\mathrm{r}}^2\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \boldsymbol\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^{\T}\vek Q(t) .\]

This is the equation of one mass on one spring. On the right stands only the part of the force of the shaker that matches the shape of the mode. In the book: 10.66, (10.92).

Let us draw what this means for the upper floor. Horizontally the frequency, vertically the motion of the upper floor per newton of force, this time with its sign: above the axis the floor moves with the force, below the axis against it. The first mode alone is an oscillator as we know it from chapter 9. Below its frequency it follows the force, at \(3{,}12\) Hz the response grows beyond all bounds, above it it moves against the force. Why beyond all bounds? As with a swing pushed in time: every push adds energy. The second mode does the same at \(8{,}16\) Hz. The floor sees the sum of both, so it has a peak wherever a single mode has one.

What is the shape at a peak? At \(3{,}12\) Hz the ratio of the motions is \(5{,}83 : 9{,}44 = 0{,}618\), which is almost exactly the ratio of the first mode, \(1 : 1{,}618\) from the first part. Near a resonance the sum is dominated by the term of one mode, and that term has the shape of the mode, wherever the shaker stands. In the book: 10.73, item 2.

Assumption: the linear frame of the first part. Columns without mass, floors that do not rotate, the weight of the floors not counted. Why may we? The columns weigh \(2{,}5\ \%\) of a floor, the compressive force lowers the stiffness of the lower storey by \(2{,}35\ \%\). It fails for light floors and long columns. Effect: the weight of the floors lowers the natural frequencies, and so the peaks, by \(1{,}0\ \%\) and \(0{,}75\ \%\), the mass of the columns by a similar small amount. The picture stays the same.

3.2 Why is nothing special about slow shaking?

And what if we turn the shaker very slowly? Does the frame notice at all that the force swings? At \(0\) Hz the force does not swing, it only presses slowly. The frame leans as under a steady force. Why exactly \(0{,}20\) and \(0{,}40\) mm? Each storey carries all \(100\) N. The lower one therefore drifts by \(100\ \text{N}/k = 0{,}19930\) mm, the upper one once more as much. On the figure of the contributions we read this at the left edge: the first mode gives \(3{,}78\), the second \(0{,}21\), together \(3{,}99\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N. Times \(100\) N this is \(0{,}399\) mm. Slow shaking is thus only the static flexibility, the frame is a plain spring.

3.3 Why does the upper floor stand still at \(7{,}13\) Hz?

How can a floor that we push with \(100\) N stand still? Who holds it? This is the heart of this part. Suppose the upper floor really stands still, and let us see what follows by Newton. For now without damping. We draw the ground and the rest position of the columns and the lower floor. We leave the upper floor where it is, and the shaker is just pushing it to the right with \(100\) N. What must the lower floor do? It moves to the left, so the lower columns and the upper columns bend. The displacement of the lower floor is \(q_1 = -0{,}1993\) mm. Where does this number come from? Let us look at the forces on the upper floor.

We cut out the upper floor and replace the removed surroundings with forces. On one side the shaker, \(100\) N to the right. On the other the upper storey, which the lower floor pulls to the left, with the force \(k\cdot 0{,}1993\) mm \(= 100\) N. That is why exactly \(0{,}1993\) mm: this much is needed for the upper storey to pull with \(100\) N. The sum of the forces is zero, and a body on which no net force acts does not accelerate. The upper floor stands still.

But the lower floor must really move like this. Can it? We also cut out the lower floor. The lower storey pulls it back towards its rest position with \(k\,|q_1| = 100\) N, the upper storey likewise with \(100\) N, because the upper floor is at rest. So the lower floor feels two springs at once, a stiffness of \(2k\) together. A mass on a spring \(2k\) swings with \(\Omega^2 = 2k/m = 2007{,}04\) 1/s\(^2\), that is \(\Omega = 44{,}80\) 1/s or \(7{,}130\) Hz. Exactly there is the valley of the response figure and the shape with the upper floor standing.

So the idea of the proof is: the valley of coordinate \(2\) is the natural frequency of the system in which coordinate \(2\) is held. The lower floor swings as if free on both storeys, and with the force of the upper storey it exactly balances the shaker. In the book: 10.76, 10.77, item 2.

Assumption: equal masses and equal storeys. In frame A, \(k_{11} = 2k\) and \(m_1 = m\), hence \(2k/m\). With other floors the picture stays the same, the valley is at \(k_{11}/m_1\), and the theorem says it always lies between the two peaks.

3.4 Why does the valley not reach zero and the peak not infinity?

On the figure of the contributions without damping the peaks go beyond all bounds and the valley down to zero. Is it so on the measured curve too? Let us go back to it. The dot at the bottom of the valley is not at zero, the upper floor still moves by \(0{,}01496\) mm, and the peaks are finite. What limits them?

Damping. The frame has the damping ratio \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) in both modes. Damping takes some energy every swing, because the lower floor moves. The shaker must supply this energy. But the shaker supplies energy only if the point it pushes moves: work is force times path. If the upper floor stood completely still, the shaker would give nothing. So it must move a little. The numbers in the valley and at the peaks:

place \(\vert H_{22}\vert\) (\(10^{-6}\) m/N) upper floor at \(100\) N
static, \(0\) Hz \(3{,}986\) \(0{,}399\) mm
peak, \(3{,}116\) Hz \(94{,}39\) \(9{,}44\) mm, \(23{,}7\) times static
valley, \(7{,}130\) Hz \(0{,}1496\) \(0{,}015\) mm, \(3{,}8\ \%\) of static
peak, \(8{,}158\) Hz \(5{,}31\) \(0{,}53\) mm

The same damping limits the peak. At \(\omega_1\) the term of the first mode is \(\varphi_{21}^2/(2\zeta\lambda_1) = 0{,}0014472/(2\cdot 0{,}02\cdot 383{,}31) = 9{,}4389\cdot 10^{-5}\) m/N. This one term alone gives the peak with an error of \(0{,}03\ \%\). In the book: the paragraph after 10.73.

Assumption: classical damping, the same \(\zeta = 0{,}02\) in both modes. Why? Only then do the modes stay separate oscillators and the figure of the contributions holds. When not: a single damper at one floor (In the book: 10.10.4). Effect: without damping the valley would be at zero and the peaks infinite, with it the valley is \(3{,}8\ \%\) of the static value and the peak \(23{,}7\) times it.

3.5 Why does the lower floor have only a shallow valley between the peaks?

Could the lower floor ever stand still too, if we shake the upper one? The orange curve of the lower floor says no, between the peaks it has only a shallow minimum. Why, Newton tells again, on the diagram of the lower floor. If the lower floor stands still, the lower storey does not pull it. For the sum of the forces to be zero, the upper storey must not pull it either, so the upper floor stands still too. But the shaker pushes the upper floor and nothing balances it. The lower floor therefore cannot stand still at any frequency. In the book: 10.76, item 2 (the numerator \(-k_{12}\) is constant).

And at \(5{,}04\) Hz, where the floors move alike? The upper storey then has no drift, the upper floor feels only the shaker. If we put the shaker on the lower floor instead, the lower floor would stand still at this same frequency. A valley is thus not a property of the frequency but of the coordinate that carries the force. In the book: 10.77, items 1 and 3.

3.6 Back up

Now we put the story back together. The modes are separate oscillators, each with a peak at its own frequency. Far below the first peak the frame is a plain spring. Between the peaks the two contributions have opposite signs and cancel at the frequency of the held subsystem. Damping rounds both. So we read the curve of the upper floor from left to right as a map of the frame: low frequencies are the static flexibility, peaks are the modes, valleys are the subsystems with one coordinate held.

3.7 By modes: the valley as a cancellation

Let us look at the same valley from the modes. On the figure of the contributions we mark the band between the two natural frequencies. In it the first mode is already above its frequency and moves against the force, the second is still below its own and follows it. At \(7{,}13\) Hz the contribution of the first is \(-0{,}89\), of the second \(+0{,}89\cdot 10^{-6}\) m/N, the sum is zero. Why exactly there? The contribution of mode \(r\) without damping is \(\varphi_{2\mathrm{r}}^2/(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \Omega^2)\) (In the book: (10.106) at \(\zeta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\)), and the equality

\[\frac{0{,}0014472}{\Omega^2 - 383{,}31} = \frac{0{,}00055277}{2627{,}25 - \Omega^2}\]

gives \(\Omega^2 = 2007{,}04\) 1/s\(^2\), the same as the diagram of forces. What does this view add? The valley is not special to two floors. With more degrees of freedom the response of the pushed coordinate without damping passes through zero between every two neighbouring peaks in whose modes this coordinate moves. The book does not prove this for more than two coordinates, it cites a source (In the book: the paragraph after 10.76). With many coordinates we keep only the lowest modes, and the ones cut off contribute almost only a static part (In the book: 10.10.5).

3.8 As a vibration absorber

On the diagram of forces a part of the structure, tuned to the shaker's frequency, stops the pushed floor. If the shaker stood on the lower floor, the upper storey would take over this role at \(5{,}04\) Hz. This is the idea of the tuned vibration absorber (uglašen dušilnik nihanja). In the book: 10.12.

3.9 By phase: how to tell a peak from a valley

In a measurement we see only the curve, we do not know what is where. The lag helps. We draw the frequency horizontally and the lag of the displacement behind the force vertically, in degrees. The lag of the upper floor grows by \(180^\circ\) at the first natural frequency, at the frequency itself it is halfway, \(89{,}85^\circ\). In the valley it falls back, down to \(31^\circ\) at \(7{,}62\) Hz, and at the second natural frequency it grows again. The lower floor has no valley, so its lag only grows, to more than \(350^\circ\). A peak shows as a jump up, a valley as a fall.
