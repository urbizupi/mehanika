Chapter 10 Vibration of systems with several degrees of freedom and modal analysis (nihanje sistemov z več prostostnimi stopnjami in modalna analiza)

10.5 Zero frequencies and rigid-body modes (ničelne frekvence in togi načini)

So far every system has had supports that prevent every displacement without deformation. What if the supports are missing? Take bracket B (konzola B) from section 10.2: bar AC is horizontal and 4 m long, bar BC is 5 m long and points from the wall to C in the direction \((4, -3)/5\), that is 4 m to the right and 3 m down, both have axial stiffness \(EA = 19{,}6\) MN, and the pin C carries a weight of mass \(m = 1000\) kg (the truss of section 4.8.1, the stiffness matrix from section 4.6.7). We remove bar AC, so we draw it grey and dashed (figure 10.54). The pin C can now leave its former position without bar BC lengthening, at least to first order: the right part of the figure shows the bar before and after the pin moves sideways, and the tiny difference of the lengths, to which we return with numbers. What does the theory say about a motion that costs no energy?

First we compute the stiffness matrix. The pin C is displaced by \(\vek d = (u, v)\). By lemma 7.28 bar BC lengthens by the scalar product \(\vek n\cdot\vek d\) with the unit vector \(\vek n = (0{,}8,\ -0{,}6)\) along the bar, and its energy is \(\tfrac12 k\,(\vek n\cdot\vek d)^2\) with bar stiffness \(k = EA/L = 19{,}6\cdot 10^6\ \text{N}/5\ \text{m} = 3{,}92\) MN/m. Hence \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = k\,(\vek n\cdot\vek d)^2\), so

\[\mat K = k\,\vek n\,\vek n^\T = 10^6\begin{pmatrix} 2{,}5088 & -1{,}8816 \\ -1{,}8816 & 1{,}4112 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{N/m} . \tag{10.52}\]

This is the matrix of definition 7.4 without the contribution of bar AC, since the matrix of the whole truss has the entry \((1, 1)\) equal to \(7{,}4088\cdot 10^6\) N/m, bar AC contributes \(EA/4\ \text{m} = 4{,}9\cdot 10^6\) N/m to this entry, and \(2{,}5088\cdot 10^6\) N/m remains. The matrix is symmetric and \(\vek d^\T\mat K\,\vek d = k\,(\vek n\cdot\vek d)^2 \ge 0\), so it is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15). The mass matrix is still \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\), positive definite. For the column \(\vek t = (0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\) we have \(\vek n\cdot\vek t = 0{,}48 - 0{,}48 = 0\), hence \(\mat K\,\vek t = k\,\vek n\,(\vek n\cdot\vek t) = \vek 0\). For \(\vek n\) itself \(\mat K\,\vek n = k\,\vek n\), since \(\vek n\cdot\vek n = 1\). So we have two eigenpairs of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) (definition 10.13):

\[\lambda_1 = 0 ,\ \vek\varphi_1 \parallel \vek t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lambda_2 = \frac{k}{m} = 3920\ \text{s}^{-2} ,\ \vek\varphi_2 \parallel \vek n ,\]

the second corresponds to the circular frequency \(\omega_2 = 62{,}610\) s\(^{-1}\) and the frequency \(9{,}9647\) Hz. Since \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\), \(\mat M\)-orthogonal is the same as perpendicular, and indeed \(\vek t\cdot\vek n = 0\). In the mode \(\vek\varphi_2\) the pin C moves along the bar and stretches and shortens it, in the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\) it moves perpendicular to it and the bar does no work.

The spectral theorem (theorem 10.21) does not exclude this example. It assumed only that \(\mat K\) is symmetric and \(\mat M\) positive definite, and the eigenvalue zero is allowed. A second question opens. The expansion in theorem 10.31 divides by \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\), because for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) the solution of the modal equation is \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t + \eta_{\mathrm{r}}'(0)\,\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t)/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\). As \(\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) tends to zero, \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t\) tends to \(1\) and \(\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{r}} t)/\omega_{\mathrm{r}}\) tends to \(t\). So we expect a solution \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = a + bt\): a pin that we give a velocity in the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\) keeps moving on with that velocity, since no force holds it back. By definition 8.130 the equilibrium of the pin C is therefore unstable: for an initial velocity in the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\), however small, the displacement grows linearly with time and exceeds every bound. The growth differs from that for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < 0\) in chapter 8, however: the shape stays the same, the strain energy is zero at all times, and no modal coordinate grows like \(\cosh\) or \(\sinh\). The following definition names such modes.

Definition 10.33 Zero-frequency mode, rigid-body mode and mechanism (način z ničelno frekvenco, togi način in mehanizem)

Let \((\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}})\) be an eigenpair of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) from definition 10.13, where \(\mat K\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite and \(\mat M\) is positive definite. If \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), then \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a zero-frequency mode (način z ničelno frekvenco). If in addition \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) is a rigid-body displacement of the system (definition 1.11), it is a rigid-body mode (togi način), otherwise a mechanism (mehanizem) as for the truss in definition 7.32. A mode with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) is an elastic mode (prožni način). In figure 10.54, the rigid-body mode moves the pin along the tangent and the elastic mode stretches the bar.

Source: CK §9.4 (PDF p. 285): "Rigid-body modes have a corresponding natural frequency of zero", "An eigenvalue problem that results in one or more zero eigenvalues ... is called a semidefinite eigenvalue problem" (PDF p. 286). The distinction between a rigid-body mode and a mechanism is from definition 7.32 (COOK, PDF p. 53), CK does not separate mechanisms.

The bracket without AC has one zero-frequency mode. Is it a rigid-body mode or a mechanism? We put the origin at B, so that the pin C is at \((4, -3)\) m. By definition 7.32 a rotation of the truss by a small angle \(\theta\) about the origin leaves the point B in place and moves the pin C by \(\theta\,(3, 4)\) m \(= 5\theta\,(0{,}6,\ 0{,}8)\) m, which is exactly the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\). So to first order the mode is a rigid-body displacement of bar BC together with the weight, hence a rigid-body mode and not a mechanism. By the same definition this means that the truss lacks a support, the one that bar AC provided. A mechanism is found, for example, in the two bars on one line of section 7.5.3.

We must say what the first-order model claims, since it is the assumption on which the zero frequency stands. The model with the matrix \(\mat K\) says that the pin C travels along the tangent. The real motion is a rotation of bar BC about B, that is, motion along a circle of radius \(L = 5\) m. The right part of the figure shows the difference between the two, greatly magnified: the bar before is grey and dashed, we move the pin by a step \(s\) along the tangent, perpendicular to the bar, and the bar after is orange. The old bar, the step and the new bar form a right triangle with legs \(L\) and \(s\), so by the Pythagorean theorem the new length is \(\sqrt{L^2 + s^2}\). The circle through the old C marks how far the length \(L\) reaches, and the blue distance from it to the new end is the lengthening of the bar.

Take a displacement \(s = 10\) mm along \(\vek t\). The bar lengthens by

\[\sqrt{L^2 + s^2} - L = 9{,}99999\cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{m} , \qquad \frac{s^2}{2L} = 10^{-5}\ \text{m} ,\]

and the two values differ by only a millionth. The figure is drawn with the step \(s = L/3\) instead of \(L/500\), since the true lengthening, a hundredth of a millimetre on five metres, would be far below the thickness of a line. The energy of the bar is \(\tfrac12 k\,(10^{-5}\ \text{m})^2 = 1{,}96\cdot 10^{-4}\) J. The same displacement along the bar would cost \(\tfrac12 k\,s^2 = 196\) J, a million times more: for a displacement along the tangent the lengthening is in the ratio \(s/(2L) = 0{,}001\) to the lengthening for a displacement along the bar, and the energy is proportional to the square of the lengthening.

The first-order assumption is thus justified here by the ratio \(s/(2L)\): as long as it is small compared with \(1\) (for \(s = 10\) mm it is \(0{,}001\)), the first-order rigid-body mode is a good model, and it leaves out of the computation only the energy \(1{,}96\cdot 10^{-4}\) J next to the \(196\) J of the elastic mode. It does not hold for a displacement comparable to \(L\). At a step \(s = 1\) m the bar lengthens by \(\sqrt{26}\ \text{m} - 5\ \text{m} = 99{,}0\) mm and stores \(\tfrac12 k\,(0{,}0990\ \text{m})^2 = 19{,}2\) kJ, while the model still claims that the motion costs nothing. There it is replaced by a rotation about B, which chapter 8 treats with the angle as a free coordinate.

What is certain about zero-frequency modes for every system is stated by a theorem. Its first part says when a frequency is zero, the second how many such modes there are, the third how they lie relative to the elastic modes, the fourth how their modal coordinate changes.

The figure shows the axis of eigenvalues \(\lambda\). On its left lie the zeros, each one a zero-frequency mode, and to their right the positive values of the elastic modes. Below the axis is the plane \(\mathcal N\) of columns of zero energy and on it the right angle to an elastic mode. On the right is the curve \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\), shaped by the double integral of the force.

Theorem 10.34 Zero frequencies of a positive semidefinite stiffness matrix (ničelne frekvence pri pozitivno semidefinitni togostni matriki)

Let \(\mat K\) be a symmetric positive semidefinite and \(\mat M\) a symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Let \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (definition 10.17) \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the eigenvalues and modes of theorem 10.21, \(\mathcal N\) the set of columns \(\vek w\) with \(\mat K\,\vek w = \vek 0\), and \(\vek Q(t)\) a continuous column. Then: The eigenvalues lie on the \(\lambda\) axis.

  1. All eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\), and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) exactly when \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). In the figure the zeros lie on the left, the positive values to their right.
  2. A column \(\vek w\) lies in \(\mathcal N\) exactly when it has zero energy, \(\vek w^\T\mat K\,\vek w = 0\). The number \(z\) of zero eigenvalues equals the largest number of linearly independent columns in \(\mathcal N\), and every column of \(\mathcal N\) is a linear combination of the modes \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{z}}\). The columns of zero energy form the plane spanned by the two modes with zero eigenvalue.
  3. Every column of \(\mathcal N\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to every elastic mode, \(\vek w^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) for \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} > 0\). The right angle shows the \(\mat M\)-orthogonality to the elastic mode.
  4. For \(r \le z\) the modal coordinate (definition 10.27) of the solution of \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t)\) equals
    \[\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t) = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,t + \int_0^t\!\!\int_0^s \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}s , \tag{10.53}\]
    where \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q(0)\) and \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0)\). The curve is a line bent by the double integral of the force, with no oscillation.
Proof

We must show four points. The first follows from the energy of a mode, the second from lemma 7.16 and the expansion in modes, the third from the symmetry of the matrix \(\mat K\), the fourth from the modal equation. We always use \(\mat M\)-orthonormality, \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = 1\) for \(r = s\) and \(0\) otherwise. The steps that have a picture point at the bracket of figure 10.54, where \(n = 2\), \(z = 1\) and \(\mathcal N\) is the line through \(\vek\varphi_1\).

  1. From \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\) we get, multiplying by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive semidefinite (definition 7.15), hence \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\). The eigenvalue is thus twice the strain energy of the \(\mat M\)-normalized mode: the mode \(\vek\varphi_2\) stretches the bar and has \(\lambda_2 = k/m > 0\), the mode \(\vek\varphi_1\) does not stretch it and has \(\lambda_1 = 0\).
  2. If \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), then by step 1 \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\), so by lemma 7.16 \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\). If \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\), then \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\) by step 1. This is the first point. The first sentence of the second point is lemma 7.16: a motion without energy is a motion that no force holds, like the displacement of the pin along the tangent, which does not load the bar.
  3. Let \(\vek w \in \mathcal N\) and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} > 0\). From \(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\mat M\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) and the symmetry of \(\mat K\), \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}(\mat K\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}})^\T = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\). Hence \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w = \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat K\,\vek w = 0\). This is the third point. For the bracket \(\mat M = m\,\mat I\) and the claim says that \(\vek\varphi_1\) is perpendicular to \(\vek\varphi_2\).
  4. By corollary 10.22, \(\vek w = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek w)\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). By step 3 the terms with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} > 0\) vanish, so \(\vek w\) is a combination of modes with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} = 0\). Since \(\lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\mathrm{n}}\), these are the first \(z\) modes \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{z}}\).
  5. The modes \(\vek\varphi_1, \dots, \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{z}}\) lie in \(\mathcal N\) (step 2) and are linearly independent: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{r}} c_{\mathrm{r}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) we get, multiplying by \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}^\T\mat M\), \(c_{\mathrm{s}} = 0\) (definition 6.22).
  6. Let \(\vek w_1, \dots, \vek w_{\mathrm{p}} \in \mathcal N\) be linearly independent. By step 4, \(\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}=1}^{z} c_{\mathrm{rj}}\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\). If \(p > z\), the homogeneous system \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{rj}}x_{\mathrm{j}} = 0\) for \(r = 1, \dots, z\) would by lemma 10.16 have a nonzero solution \((x_1, \dots, x_{\mathrm{p}})\), and \(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} x_{\mathrm{j}}\vek w_{\mathrm{j}} = \sum_{\mathrm{r}}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{j}} c_{\mathrm{rj}}x_{\mathrm{j}}\big)\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek 0\) would contradict independence. So \(p \le z\), and step 5 shows that \(p = z\) is attained. This completes the second point. The plane \(\mathcal N\) has only \(z\) independent directions.
  7. By theorem 10.28, \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q(t)\), so for \(r \le z\) we get \(\ddot\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = f(t)\) with the continuous \(f = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\vek Q\): the term \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\eta_{\mathrm{r}}\), which would pull the coordinate back, has vanished, just as no force pulls the pin C back in the direction \(\vek\varphi_1\). By theorem 9.41 with characteristic polynomial \(s^2\) (the root \(0\) twice) this equation has exactly one solution of order 2 for given \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\) and \(\dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\). The initial values are \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\vek q(0)\) and \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0)\) from the relation \(\eta = \mat\Phi^\T\mat M\,\vek q\) (definition 10.27).
  8. The function \(f\) is continuous, so by theorem 3.55 (point 2) the function \(g(s) = \int_0^s f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\) is differentiable with \(g' = f\). By the same point of the theorem, applied to the continuous function \(g\), the function \(\eta(t) = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + \dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\,t + \int_0^t g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) is differentiable with \(\eta'(t) = \dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0) + g(t)\), and this is differentiable with \(\eta'' = g' = f\). The function satisfies the equation and the initial values \(\eta(0) = \eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\), \(\eta'(0) = \dot\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(0)\), so by step 7 it is that solution. This is equation (10.53). The double integral gives the curve \(\eta_{\mathrm{r}}(t)\).

Source: CK (11.54) and (11.55) (PDF p. 371): the coordinates of rigid-body modes are obtained by double integration. CK §9.4 (PDF pp. 285-286), CK Example 10.2 (PDF pp. 305-306, "Since det(K) = 0, K is a singular matrix"). The proof is not from the source.

The theorem showed that the whole theory of the previous sections accepts positive semidefiniteness, except that at zero eigenvalues straight-line motion replaces oscillation, as for the pin C in the direction \(\vek\varphi_1\). The last point also says how many times the force must be integrated: twice, as for a freely moving particle. Each hypothesis of the theorem entered at one place. The positive semidefiniteness of \(\mat K\) gave \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} \ge 0\) in step 1. Without it there would be a direction of negative energy with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} < 0\), in which the displacement grows like \(\cosh\) (chapter 8), no longer linearly. The positive definiteness of \(\mat M\) gave the spectral theorem and the normalization. Without mass in some direction there is no equation of motion there, only a condition of equilibrium. The continuity of the force \(\vek Q\) was needed in step 8 to differentiate the integral. For a blow that changes the velocity in an instant we split the computation into the time before the blow and after it, with a new initial velocity. For \(z \ge 2\) the zero-frequency modes are not determined uniquely: any \(\mat M\)-orthonormal basis of the space \(\mathcal N\) is allowed (CK, PDF p. 311, "these two rigid-body modes are not unique"). The elastic modes, however, are by the third point all \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to the whole of \(\mathcal N\).

Let us test it on an example that has a rigid-body mode. Two railway wagons of masses \(m_1 = 20\) t and \(m_2 = 30\) t, joined by a buffer of stiffness \(k = 2\cdot 10^6\) N/m, move on a straight track without friction. The displacements of the wagons along the track from the position with the unstretched buffer are \(q_1\) and \(q_2\). The buffer is an ideal spring that pushes the wagons apart when it is compressed and pulls them together when it is stretched. A real buffer only pushes and the coupling (spenjača) pulls, so our spring stands for both. This holds as long as the wagons are coupled. Uncoupled wagons separate at the first stretching of the buffer and each moves on at its own speed. The wagons are point masses, which is right as long as a wagon itself deforms much less than the buffer. There is no friction. If a resisting force braked the wagons, then by Newton's law for both wagons together their centre of mass would slow down with a deceleration equal to the resistance divided by the total mass: a resistance of \(1\) kN would give \(1000\ \text{N}/50\,000\ \text{kg} = 0{,}02\) m/s\(^2\), so the rigid-body mode would no longer run at constant speed. The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12(m_1\dot q_1^2 + m_2\dot q_2^2)\), the potential energy \(\tfrac12 k\,(q_2 - q_1)^2\), so by theorem 10.3

\[\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{pmatrix} ,\qquad \mat K = k\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .\]

The energy \(k\,(q_2 - q_1)^2\) is zero exactly when \(q_1 = q_2\), so \(\mathcal N\) is the line of columns \(c\,(1, 1)\), the wagons move by the same distance, and this is a rigid-body displacement (definition 1.11). By theorem 10.34, \(z = 1\), \(\lambda_1 = 0\) and \(\vek\varphi_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{m_1 + m_2} = 0{,}0044721\,(1, 1)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\). By the third point the second mode must be \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to it, \(m_1 q_1 + m_2 q_2 = 0\), so \(q_2/q_1 = -m_1/m_2 = -2/3\): the wagons move in opposite directions, the lighter wagon moves \(3/2\) times as far as the heavier one, because the sum \(m_1 q_1 + m_2 q_2\) must be zero. The column \(\vek q = (m_2, -m_1)\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek\varphi_1\), since \(m_2 m_1 - m_1 m_2 = 0\). By corollary 10.22, \(\vek q = c_1\vek\varphi_1 + c_2\vek\varphi_2\) with \(c_1 = \vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\,\vek q = 0\), so \(\vek q = c_2\vek\varphi_2\), and by the first point of the theorem (in step 1 of the proof \(\vek\varphi_2^\T\mat K\,\vek\varphi_2 = \lambda_2\) with \(\vek\varphi_2^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi_2 = 1\))

\[\lambda_2 = \frac{\vek q^\T\mat K\,\vek q}{\vek q^\T\mat M\,\vek q} = \frac{k\,(m_1 + m_2)^2}{m_1 m_2^2 + m_2 m_1^2} = \frac{k\,(m_1 + m_2)}{m_1 m_2} = \frac{2\cdot 10^6\cdot 5\cdot 10^4}{6\cdot 10^8}\ \text{s}^{-2} = 166{,}67\ \text{s}^{-2} ,\]

where \(c_2^2\) cancels. Let us also check the eigenvalue equation: the first row of \(\mat K\,\vek q = \lambda_2\mat M\,\vek q\) is \(k\,(m_2 + m_1) = \lambda_2 m_1 m_2\), which is exactly the expression above, and the second row gives the same. The circular frequency is \(12{,}910\) s\(^{-1}\), the frequency \(2{,}0547\) Hz, the period \(0{,}4867\) s. The \(\mat M\)-normalized mode is \(\vek\varphi_2 = (m_2, -m_1)/\sqrt{m_1 m_2\,(m_1 + m_2)} = (0{,}0054772,\ -0{,}0036515)\) kg\(^{-1/2}\).

Figure 10.56 shows both modes, the rigid-body mode at the top and the elastic one at the bottom. In the elastic mode a pattern appears: the centre of mass, where \(m_1 q_1 + m_2 q_2 = 0\), is at rest. This is no accident, the cause is the third point of the theorem: the condition of \(\mat M\)-orthogonality to the rigid-body mode \((1, 1)\) is exactly the condition that the sum \(m_1 q_1 + m_2 q_2\) be zero.

The zero frequency has another view, which says where it comes from and why we may call it a frequency. Take the same wagons on the same track and draw them three times (figure 10.57). At the top we tie wagon 1 to a wall with a weak spring of stiffness \(k_0\). Now no displacement is free any more and the system has two true frequencies. The lower one belongs to the mode in which the wagons move almost together, with the period \(T_1\). We then weaken the spring \(k_0\):

\(k_0\) (N/m) \(T_1\) (s) \(2\pi\sqrt{(m_1 + m_2)/k_0}\) (s) \(q_2/q_1\) in the first mode \(\omega_2\) (1/s)
\(2\cdot 10^5\) \(3{,}199\) \(3{,}142\) \(1{,}0614\) \(13{,}146\)
\(2\cdot 10^4\) \(9{,}952\) \(9{,}935\) \(1{,}0060\) \(12{,}933\)
\(2\cdot 10^3\) \(31{,}42\) \(31{,}42\) \(1{,}0006\) \(12{,}912\)

As \(k_0\) tends to zero, the shape of the first mode approaches the rigid-body displacement \((1, 1)\), and its period grows beyond every bound, like \(2\pi\sqrt{(m_1 + m_2)/k_0}\): the two wagons together are one mass \(m_1 + m_2\) on the spring \(k_0\). The second frequency settles at \(12{,}910\) s\(^{-1}\), which we computed without the wall. The zero frequency is thus the limit of an ever slower oscillation: an oscillation that lasts infinitely long never turns back, and that is the straight-line motion \(a + bt\) of the fourth point of the theorem. This view also adds a practical rule: a structure hung on very soft springs behaves at its elastic frequencies like a free one.

Two initial states show what the fourth point says. The first is in the middle row of the figure: wagon 1 moves at \(1\) m/s towards the resting wagon 2, the buffer is unstretched, \(\vek q(0) = \vek 0\), \(\dot{\vek q}(0) = (1, 0)\) m/s. We get \(\dot\eta_1(0) = \vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0) = m_1\cdot 1/\sqrt{m_1 + m_2} = 89{,}44\) m kg\(^{1/2}\)/s and \(\eta_1 = \dot\eta_1(0)\,t\), so the rigid part is \(\vek\varphi_1\eta_1 = 0{,}4\,t\,(1, 1)\) m: the centre of mass, and with it on average both wagons, moves at the speed \(m_1\cdot 1/(m_1 + m_2) = 0{,}4\) m/s. The elastic part has \(\dot\eta_2(0) = \vek\varphi_2^\T\mat M\,\dot{\vek q}(0) = 109{,}54\) m kg\(^{1/2}\)/s and \(\eta_2 = \dot\eta_2(0)\sin(\omega_2 t)/\omega_2\). The buffer shortens and stretches between \(\pm 77{,}46\) mm, which is exactly the relative speed \(1\) m/s divided by \(\omega_2 = 12{,}910\) s\(^{-1}\). The largest force in the buffer is \(154{,}9\) kN, the stiffness \(k\) multiplied by \(77{,}46\) mm, and when the buffer is compressed it pushes the wagons apart. Of the initial kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 m_1\cdot 1^2 = 10\,000\) J, \(\tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)\,0{,}4^2 = 4000\) J is in the rigid part (a share \(m_1/(m_1 + m_2) = 40\ \%\)) and \(6000\) J in the oscillation.

The second is in the bottom row: a locomotive pulls wagon 2 with a constant force \(F = 60\) kN, \(\vek Q = (0, F)\), from rest. The static computation \(\mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q\) has no solution, since \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat K\,\vek q = (\mat K\,\vek\varphi_1)^\T\vek q = 0\) for every \(\vek q\) while \(\vek\varphi_1^\T\vek Q = F/\sqrt{m_1 + m_2}\) is not zero: a force with a component in the rigid-body mode accelerates the whole system. By equation (10.53), \(\ddot\eta_1 = \vek\varphi_1^\T\vek Q\), so \(\vek\varphi_1\eta_1 = \tfrac12 a\,t^2\,(1, 1)\) with the acceleration \(a = F/(m_1 + m_2) = 1{,}2\) m/s\(^2\): in \(1\) s the centre of mass moves by \(0{,}6\) m. The elastic part \(\ddot\eta_2 + \lambda_2\eta_2 = \vek\varphi_2^\T\vek Q = -219{,}09\) m kg\(^{1/2}\)/s\(^2\) has the constant solution \(\eta_2 = -219{,}09/166{,}67 = -1{,}3145\) m kg\(^{1/2}\), so the displacements are \(\vek\varphi_2\eta_2 = (-7{,}2,\ 4{,}8)\) mm: the buffer is stretched by \(12\) mm and pulls wagon 1 with the force \(k\cdot 0{,}012\ \text{m} = 24\) kN, which is exactly \(m_1 a = 20\,000\cdot 1{,}2\) N, the force wagon 1 needs for the same acceleration. The elastic part is thus the static stretch that the acceleration demands, and its shape is the elastic mode. Since the wagons start from rest, \(\eta_2 = -1{,}3145\,(1 - \cos\omega_2 t)\) m kg\(^{1/2}\) and the stretch of the buffer oscillates between \(0\) and \(24\) mm about the mean value \(12\) mm. A computation for the full system in NumPy (fourth-order Runge-Kutta) confirms the centre of mass at \(0{,}6000\) m at \(1\) s.

For the wagons the centre of mass is at rest in the elastic mode and moves by Newton's law in the rigid-body mode. Does this also hold for several point masses on a line? The answer is a corollary of the theorem.

Corollary 10.35 The centre of mass is at rest in the elastic modes (težišče miruje v prožnih načinih)

Let \(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{n}}\) be the masses of points on a line, \(q_{\mathrm{i}}\) the displacement of the \(i\)-th point along the line, \(\mat M = \mathrm{diag}(m_1, \dots, m_{\mathrm{n}})\), \(\mat K\) a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix with \(\mat K\,\vek s = \vek 0\) for \(\vek s = (1, \dots, 1)\) (all points move by the same distance), and \(\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \mat K\,\vek q = \vek Q(t)\), where \(Q_{\mathrm{i}}\) is the force on the \(i\)-th point. The displacement of the centre of mass (definition 8.81, for point masses a sum instead of an integral) is \(q_{\mathrm{T}} = \vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek q/m_\mathrm{c}\) with the total mass \(m_\mathrm{c} = \vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek s = \sum m_{\mathrm{i}}\). Then: An example is the two wagons with their centre of mass.

  1. \(m_\mathrm{c}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{T}} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{i}}\): the centre of mass moves like a single point of mass \(m_\mathrm{c}\) under the sum of the external forces. In the figure the centre of mass has an acceleration under the external force.
  2. For every elastic mode \(\vek\varphi\) (definition 10.33), \(\vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 0\): the centre of mass is at rest in such an oscillation. In the figure the buffer oscillates while the centre of mass travels on uniformly and does not feel the oscillation.
Proof

We must show two equations, the first from the equation of motion, the second from the third point of theorem 10.34.

  1. Multiply the equation of motion by \(\vek s^\T\) from the left: \(\vek s^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} + \vek s^\T\mat K\,\vek q = \vek s^\T\vek Q\). By symmetry \(\vek s^\T\mat K = (\mat K\,\vek s)^\T = \vek 0^\T\), so \(\vek s^\T\mat M\ddot{\vek q} = \sum_{\mathrm{i}} Q_{\mathrm{i}}\), and the left side is \(m_\mathrm{c}\,\ddot q_{\mathrm{T}}\). For the wagons this is the force \(F\), which gives the centre of mass the acceleration \(F/(m_1 + m_2)\), while the forces of the buffer cancel in the sum, because they act on the two wagons in opposite directions.
  2. The column \(\vek s\) lies in \(\mathcal N\) of theorem 10.34, since \(\mat K\,\vek s = \vek 0\). By the third point of the theorem, \(\vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 0\) for every elastic mode \(\vek\varphi\), as in the elastic mode of the wagons, in which the centre of mass is at rest.

Source: CK §9.4 (PDF p. 286): "there is a node point (i.e., a point that remains stationary)", for the two-mass system of Example 9.7 (PDF p. 285-286), not for wagons. The centre of mass is ours. The general form is from theorem 10.34, the proof is not from the source.

The centre of mass thus follows only the rigid-body part of the motion, and all internal oscillations are in elastic modes, in which it is at rest. For a mass matrix that is not diagonal, for example for a bar with finite elements (section 10.8), the same conclusion \(\vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek\varphi = 0\) still holds by the third point of the theorem, except that \(\vek s^\T\mat M\,\vek q\) is no longer a sum of masses multiplied by the displacements of single points, and the centre of mass is explained separately there.

Zero frequencies return in later sections. A bar with finite elements that has both ends free (section 10.8) has \(\mat K\,(1, \dots, 1)^\T = \vek 0\), so a zero frequency with a rigid-body mode in which all nodes move by the same distance, and elastic modes in which the centre of mass is at rest by corollary 10.35. For a truss with supports that prevent every rigid-body displacement and every mechanism, \(\mat K_{\mathrm{ff}}\) is positive definite (theorem 7.33) and all frequencies are greater than zero.
