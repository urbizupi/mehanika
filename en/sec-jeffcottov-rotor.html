Chapter 24 Rotordynamics

24.1 The Jeffcott rotor and free whirl

Take a slender steel shaft held by two bearings, with a thick disk at its middle: a turbine wheel, a pump rotor, a fan. If we knock the disk sideways, the shaft vibrates like the beam of chapter 5 with a mass at the middle. But the shaft usually also turns. The disk can then vibrate in one plane, or it can circle around the line joining the two bearings, and we ask whether the turning changes the frequency and the shape of this motion. The word rotor (rotor) comes from the Latin verb rotare, to turn, and in mechanics it means any body that turns about its axis, here the shaft with the disk. We start with the simplest model that still poses the question and solve it to the end. Each further ingredient of a rotor comes in its own section.

24.1.1 The model

Figure 24.1 shows the model we will compute. First the two bearings at the ends, a distance \(L\) apart (the length \(L\) is drawn below them). The bearings are short and rigid: we take them as pinned supports, so the shaft can tilt in them but cannot move sideways. Through the middle of both bearings runs the bearing axis, which we call the axis \(x\), as in chapter 5. When the disk is at rest and weight is ignored, the shaft lies on this axis. Under load the shaft is curved along a curve that we compute below. It is steel, has a circular cross section and is massless in the model. At the middle sits the disk of mass \(m\), drawn from the side. In the side view the disk centre is displaced from the axis by \(v\). The force of the shaft on the disk \(k\,v\) starts at the disk centre and points toward the axis, because the bent shaft tries to straighten (the arrow lies on the line along which the force acts).

The disk can leave the axis in two directions at once, so on the right half of the figure we look along the axis. We draw the plane perpendicular to the axis, with the axes \(v\) and \(w\) and the point \(O\) where the bearing axis pierces the plane. The axes are turned so that \(x\), \(v\), \(w\) form a right-handed system, so the sense that turns counterclockwise in this view is positive about the axis \(x\). We draw the disk as a circle with centre \(S\), the centre of the shaft in this plane, and we turn it with a constant spin speed \(\Omega\), positive counterclockwise. The symbol \(\Omega\) is the excitation frequency of chapter 9: in the machine of example 9.64 it was the speed with which the unbalanced mass turns, and here it is the speed of the shaft. The centre \(S\) is displaced from \(O\) by the vector \(p\), which has the components \(v\) and \(w\). While vibrating, the point \(S\) circles around \(O\), and we write \(\omega\) for the angular velocity with which it circles. We separate the two symbols: \(\Omega\) is the turning of the disk about its own axis, \(\omega\) is the circling of its centre about the bearing axis.

Notation. The whirl frequency \(\omega\) is related to the natural angular frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) of chapter 9. The bold \(\boldsymbol\omega\) of chapter 8 is the angular velocity vector of a body and does not appear in this section. The letter \(p\) is the complex displacement \(v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) (in chapter 11 \(p\) is a pressure, not used here). The stiffness \(k\) at the disk is the stiffness of the shaft under a force at the middle, not the stiffness of the foundation in chapter 9.

Now we write the model as a definition.

Definition 24.1 Jeffcott rotor (Jeffcottov rotor)

A Jeffcott rotor is a rigid disk of mass \(m\) fixed at the middle between the bearings on a shaft with a circular cross section. The shaft is massless, linearly elastic and lies in two rigid short bearings that hold it as pinned supports (chapter 5), a distance \(L\) apart. The disk turns with a constant spin speed \(\Omega\) about its own axis, which is the tangent of the shaft at the point of attachment. The lateral displacements of the disk centre from the equilibrium position are \(v\) and \(w\), perpendicular to the axis \(x\) and to each other (figure 24.1).

Source: FPGL section 3.9 (PDF pp. 131-132): rigid short bearings that treat the shaft as simply supported, one disk and a small shaft mass compared with the disk, which is therefore neglected, and the disk at midspan as the Jeffcott rotor. In FPGL the axis of rotation is \(z\), here it is \(x\), as in chapter 5.

Three assumptions are named here and each has its limit. The first is the massless shaft. In example R1 (below) the shaft has a mass equal to 20 % of the disk mass, so the model overestimates the frequency by 4.5 %, which we compute in example 24.5. The second is small slopes of the shaft: by the deflection curve the slope at the bearing is \(3\,|p|/L\) (step 2 of the proof of theorem 24.2), at \(|p| = 3{,}33\) mm it equals \(0{,}01\), the square of this slope is \(10^{-4}\), and by that much (up to a factor \(3/2\)) the true curvature term differs from the linear \(v''\), so the linear stiffness \(k\) is good enough here. The third is the constant \(\Omega\), which the motor maintains and the model takes as prescribed, as in example 9.64.

24.1.2 Equations of free motion

We look for the equations by which the disk centre moves if we release it. The disk is at the middle of the shaft, where the slope of the shaft under symmetric loading is zero, so the disk will only translate and not tilt. We prove this in the first steps, and it is the reason why the spin does not change the equations.

Theorem 24.2 Equations of motion of the Jeffcott rotor (gibalne enačbe Jeffcottovega rotorja)

Let the Jeffcott rotor of definition 24.1 be undamped and balanced (the centre of mass of the disk is on the shaft axis), with a constant spin speed \(\Omega\). Let the disk have zero tilt and zero tilt rate at the start. Let the bearing axis be horizontal, and let \(v\) and \(w\) be measured from the equilibrium position under weight. Let \(k = 48EI/L^3\) be the stiffness of the shaft under a force at the middle (chapter 5) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m}\). Then:

  1. The disk does not tilt during the motion: the slope of the shaft at the middle is zero, in both planes, and the shaft gives the disk no moment.
  2. The equations of motion in the real coordinates \(v\) and \(w\) are
    \[m\ddot v + k\,v = 0 , \qquad m\ddot w + k\,w = 0 , \tag{24.1}\]
    and in the complex coordinate \(p = v + \mathrm{i}\,w\) (definition 9.19) one equation
    \[m\ddot p + k\,p = 0 . \tag{24.2}\]
  3. The spin speed \(\Omega\) does not appear in the equations, so \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) does not depend on it either.
Proof

We must show that the middle of the shaft stays without slope, that the equations are Lagrange's equations for the energies \(T\) and \(V\), and that we combine them into the complex form.

  1. If a force \(F\) (the disk at the middle) acts at the middle of a simply supported shaft in the plane \(x v\), equation (5.57) gives \(v(x) = -F\,x\,(3L^2 - 4x^2)/(48EI)\) on \([0, L/2]\). The derivative is \(v'(x) = -F\,(3L^2 - 12x^2)/(48EI)\) and at \(x = L/2\) we get \(3L^2 - 3L^2 = 0\). So the slope of the shaft at the middle is zero and the force at the middle needs no moment. The same holds in the plane \(x w\), because \(I\) about every axis through the centre of a circle is the same (definition 5.26), and by lemma 5.59 the two loadings add. A disk that only translates and turns with a constant \(\Omega\) about its own axis needs only a force from the shaft: by equation (8.62) its moment about the centre of mass is zero. The part of the acceleration that equals the acceleration of the centre of mass for all particles gives no moment, because by the definition of the centre of mass the sum of the masses times their positions relative to it is zero, and two particles on opposite sides of the disk axis have opposite accelerations toward the axis and their moments cancel. A force at the middle, in turn, causes no slope. So the motion without tilt satisfies both equations (8.62), and since the disk has zero tilt and zero tilt rate at the start, by the uniqueness of the solution (theorem 8.93) it is the only motion. This is point 1.
  2. The displacement under a force \(F\) at the middle is \(F/k\) with \(k = 48EI/L^3\), the same in both planes. By the same equation the slope at the bearing is \(v'(0) = -FL^2/(16EI) = -3(F/k)/L\), that is \(3|p|/L\) at the displacement \(|p| = F/k\). The shaft is massless and acts on the disk as a spring with the forces \(-k\,v\) and \(-k\,w\). The potential energy is \(V = \tfrac12 k\,(v^2 + w^2)\). The weight of the disk is a constant force on a linear spring, so it only shifts the equilibrium position, by \(mg/k = 0{,}41\) mm (R1, below), and vertically downward, because the stiffness is the same in every direction across the axis. We measure the coordinates from it (as in example 9.64, step 3).
  3. The disk is a rigid body (chapter 8). By theorem 8.82 its kinetic energy is the sum of the energy of the centre of mass, \(\tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2)\), and the energy of rotation about the centre of mass. The axis of the disk keeps its direction (point 1) and the spin speed \(\Omega\) is constant, so the energy of rotation is constant and does not appear in the derivatives with respect to \(v\), \(w\), \(\dot v\), \(\dot w\). So \(T = \tfrac12 m(\dot v^2 + \dot w^2) + \text{const.}\) and \(\Omega\) is only in the constant.
  4. By theorem 8.90 (\(Q^{\mathrm{nc}} = 0\)), for the coordinate \(v\): \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\partial T/\partial\dot v\big) - \partial T/\partial v + \partial V/\partial v = 0\). Here \(\partial T/\partial\dot v = m\dot v\), \(\partial T/\partial v = 0\) and \(\partial V/\partial v = k\,v\). We get \(m\ddot v + k\,v = 0\). For \(w\) the calculation is the same and gives the second equation (24.1).
  5. We multiply the second equation by \(\mathrm{i}\) and add it to the first: \(m(\ddot v + \mathrm{i}\,\ddot w) + k\,(v + \mathrm{i}\,w) = 0\). This is equation (24.2), because \(\ddot p = \ddot v + \mathrm{i}\,\ddot w\) (derivative by components, definition 9.27).
  6. In steps 2 to 5 there is no term with \(\Omega\), which is point 3.

Source: FPGL section 3.9 (PDF pp. 131-133): there are four coordinates \(u\), \(v\), \(\theta\), \(\psi\) and a relation with the stiffness matrix. For a disk at the distances \(a\) and \(b\) from the bearings, equation (3.85) (PDF p. 133) gives the coupling coefficient \(k_{\mathrm{C}} = 3EI(a^2 - b^2)/(a^2b^2)\), which is zero for a disk at the middle (\(a = b\)), so the equations for the two displacements separate from those for the two tilts, and \(k_{\mathrm{T}} = 3EI(a^3 + b^3)/(a^3b^3)\), which at \(a = b = L/2\) equals our \(k = 48EI/L^3\). Our step 1 shows this split with the deflection curve of chapter 5.

The theorem holds only if the disk really sits at the middle. That is the condition without which the conclusion that \(\Omega\) is not in the equations fails. Take the same shaft R1 and a disk at \(x = L/4\). A force \(F\) there gives, by the deflection curve of a simply supported beam (SymPy confirms the calculation), the displacement \(3FL^3/(256EI)\) and the slope \(FL^2/(32EI)\), that is a slope equal to \(8/3\) times the displacement divided by \(L\). At a displacement of \(1\) mm and \(L = 1\) m the slope is \(0{,}00267\), not zero. Such a disk also tilts when it moves, and while it tilts its spinning contributes a moment that depends on \(\Omega\) and that this section does not yet contain. So a disk away from the middle has frequencies that change with \(\Omega\) (section 24.3). The free whirl that follows belongs only to a disk at the middle.

24.1.3 Whirl and circular orbits

Equation (24.2) is the equation of the undamped oscillator in a complex variable, from chapter 9. Solutions of this kind are complex exponential functions. Before we write them down, we name the motions that we get.

Definition 24.3 Whirl and orbit (vrtinčenje in orbita)

The orbit is the curve that the disk centre \(p(t) = v(t) + \mathrm{i}\,w(t)\) traces in the plane \(v w\). Whirl is the motion of the disk centre about the bearing axis along an orbit. The motion \(p(t) = P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\) with a complex \(P \ne 0\) and a real \(\omega \ne 0\) is a circular whirl with radius \(|P|\) and angular velocity \(|\omega|\) (theorem 9.29, point 2). For \(\omega > 0\) the centre circles in the sense of positive rotation about the axis \(x\), that is the sense in which the disk turns at \(\Omega > 0\), and the whirl is forward (vrtinčenje naprej, prednje vrtinčenje). For \(\omega < 0\) it circles the opposite way and the whirl is backward (vrtinčenje nazaj, obratno vrtinčenje).

Source: FPGL section 3.6.1 (PDF pp. 117-118): the direction of whirl from the phase between \(u\) and \(v\), and the words forward and backward for the sense of the shaft's spin or the opposite, circular orbits with amplitudes \(r_{\mathrm{u}} = r_{\mathrm{v}}\) and phase \(\pm\pi/2\). Our \(\omega\) is a signed frequency that already contains this direction.

The word whirl comes from a whirlpool, in which particles of water circle about a centre, as the disk centre circles about the bearing axis. Both whirls have the same centre of circling, the point \(O\), and differ only in direction. Whirl is not the turning of the disk: the disk turns with the speed \(\Omega\) about its own axis, while whirl is the motion of its centre. The relation between \(\omega\) and \(\Omega\) shows itself with unbalance in section 24.2.

Lemma 24.4 Free motion is the sum of a forward and a backward circular orbit (prosto gibanje je vsota krožnih orbit naprej in nazaj)

Let \(p(t)\) be a solution of equation (24.2). Then:

  1. There are exactly determined complex numbers \(A\) and \(B\) such that
    \[p(t) = A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} + B\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} , \qquad A = \tfrac12\Big(p(0) + \frac{\dot p(0)}{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\Big) , \quad B = \tfrac12\Big(p(0) - \frac{\dot p(0)}{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}}\Big) , \tag{24.3}\]
    and every such sum is a solution.
  2. The first term is a circular forward whirl with radius \(|A|\), the second a circular backward whirl with radius \(|B|\), both with angular velocity \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\).
  3. If \(A\) and \(B\) are not both zero, the orbit of the sum is an ellipse with semi-axes \(|A| + |B|\) and \(\big||A| - |B|\big|\), which the centre traverses forward if \(|A| > |B|\), backward if \(|A| < |B|\), and which is a line segment if \(|A| = |B|\). If \(B = 0\) or \(A = 0\) it is a circle. The period is \(T = 2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) in all cases.
Proof

We must show that the sum solves the equation and has the given initial condition, that there is no other solution, and that its orbit is an ellipse with the stated properties.

  1. By theorem 9.29 (point 3), \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = \pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) and the second derivative is \(-\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\). So \(\ddot p + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 p = 0\) for every \(A\) and \(B\), which is equation (24.2) divided by \(m\).
  2. At \(t = 0\), \(p(0) = A + B\) and \(\dot p(0) = \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(A - B)\). We solve the system for \(A\) and \(B\) and get equation (24.3). So for every \(p(0)\) and \(\dot p(0)\) there is a sum with one pair of numbers \(A\), \(B\).
  3. The characteristic polynomial of the equation \(\ddot p + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2 p = 0\) is \(s^2 + \omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2\), with leading coefficient \(1 \ne 0\). By theorem 9.36 the equation has exactly one solution for given \(p(0)\) and \(\dot p(0)\). So every solution is the sum of step 2, and \(A\), \(B\) are determined by the initial values. This is point 1.
  4. By definition 24.3 the term \(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) is a circular whirl with radius \(|A|\) (theorem 9.29, point 2) and with \(\omega = +\omega_{\mathrm{n}} > 0\), so forward. The term \(B\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\) has \(\omega = -\omega_{\mathrm{n}} < 0\), radius \(|B|\), so backward. If \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\) one term remains. This is point 2.
  5. Write \(A = |A|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\) and \(B = |B|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta}\) and take \(\gamma = (\alpha + \beta)/2\) and \(\varphi = \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t + (\alpha - \beta)/2\). Then \(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = |A|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\gamma + \varphi)}\) and \(B\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t} = |B|\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\gamma - \varphi)}\), since the exponents \(\gamma + \varphi = \alpha + \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and \(\gamma - \varphi = \beta - \omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) agree with the given ones. So
    \[p(t) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma}\Big[(|A| + |B|)\cos\varphi + \mathrm{i}\,(|A| - |B|)\sin\varphi\Big] , \tag{24.4}\]
    because \(\cos\varphi + \mathrm{i}\sin\varphi = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\) and \(\cos\varphi - \mathrm{i}\sin\varphi = \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi}\) (definition 9.28).
  6. The expression in the bracket is the point \((a\cos\varphi,\ b\sin\varphi)\) with \(a = |A| + |B| > 0\) and \(b = |A| - |B|\). Its orbit is an ellipse with semi-axes \(a\) and \(|b|\) along the coordinate axes (a line segment at \(b = 0\)), and multiplication by \(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\gamma}\) turns the ellipse by the angle \(\gamma\) about \(O\). Since \(\dot\varphi = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), the velocity of the point is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}(-a\sin\varphi,\ b\cos\varphi)\). The cross product of position and velocity in the plane, the first component of the position times the second of the velocity minus the second times the first, is \(a\cos\varphi \cdot \omega_{\mathrm{n}} b\cos\varphi - b\sin\varphi \cdot (-\omega_{\mathrm{n}} a\sin\varphi) = \omega_{\mathrm{n}}\,a\,b\), and the turn by \(\gamma\) does not change it. For \(b > 0\) it is positive and the point circles counterclockwise (forward), for \(b < 0\) the opposite way (backward). Because \(\varphi\) grows with \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\), the period is \(2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\). This is point 3.

Source: FPGL section 3.6.1 (PDF pp. 117-118): the orbit of \(u\) and \(v\) with a phase difference is an ellipse, the semi-axes from the eigenvalues of the matrix \(H = TT^{\mathsf T}\), the direction from the sign of the phase difference, a circle at \(r_{\mathrm{u}} = r_{\mathrm{v}}\) and phase \(\pm\pi/2\). Our proof comes from the complex form and is shorter because equation (24.2) has one frequency.

Two cases show what the lemma says. First we ask whether the ellipse that the disk centre traces for a general initial condition is a third kind of whirl besides forward and backward. It is not. Take example R1 with \(v(0) = 1\) mm, \(w(0) = 0\), \(\dot v(0) = 0\) and \(\dot w(0) = 0{,}2\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) mm, so \(p(0) = 1\) mm. Then \(\dot p(0) = \mathrm{i}\cdot 0{,}2\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) mm. By equation (24.3), \(A = \tfrac12(1 + 0{,}2) = 0{,}6\) mm and \(B = \tfrac12(1 - 0{,}2) = 0{,}4\) mm, both real. The orbit is an ellipse with semi-axes \(|A| + |B| = 1{,}0\) mm and \(|A| - |B| = 0{,}2\) mm, and because \(|A| > |B|\) the centre traverses it forward. SymPy confirms the same result: \(v = \cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) and \(w = 0{,}2\sin\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) (in mm) solve both equations (24.1) with the given initial conditions (both residuals are zero). Figure 24.2 shows an early moment, \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = 50^\circ\).

We build the figure step by step. We draw the axes \(v\) and \(w\) through \(O\). The first term, \(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), is an arrow from \(O\) of length \(|A|\) (0.6 mm, 1.8 cm at the scale of the figure) and angle \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) counterclockwise: a centre that moved only along it would circle forward. At its end we attach the second term, \(B\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t}\), an arrow of length \(|B|\) (0.4 mm, 1.2 cm) that turns the opposite way, clockwise. The sum of these two arrows is the point \(p\) we look for, at \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t = 50^\circ\) at \(0{,}643 + 0{,}153\,\mathrm{i}\) mm. As time runs, the point \(p\) traces the ellipse, which at every time is the sum of two turning arrows, and its semi-axes are the sum and the difference of their lengths: when the arrows are parallel the distance from \(O\) is largest, \(|A| + |B| = 1{,}0\) mm, when they are opposite it is smallest, \(|A| - |B| = 0{,}2\) mm. Both arrows have the same frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), so their relative position repeats every \(2\pi/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) and the orbit is a closed curve.

The same initial position with another initial velocity gives other whirls. At \(\dot w(0) = 0\) we have \(\dot p(0) = 0\) and \(A = B = 0{,}5\) mm: the two arrows are equally long, the sum is \(\cos\omega_{\mathrm{n}} t\) on the real axis, and the orbit is a line segment from \(-1\) mm to \(1\) mm on the axis \(v\), that is vibration in one plane (\(w \equiv 0\)), which is the sum of both whirls with equal radii. At \(\dot w(0) = -0{,}2\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) mm we have \(A = 0{,}4\) mm and \(B = 0{,}6\) mm: the ellipse is the same, but the centre traverses it backward. The first ellipse is thus a sum and not a third whirl, and a sum of two circular orbits differs from a single circle only in that both \(A\) and \(B\) are nonzero. The reason that the circular orbits are solutions and have the same frequency is the equal stiffness in both planes. With unequal bearing stiffness the vibrations in the planes \(v\) and \(w\) have different frequencies \(\omega_{\mathrm{v}}\) and \(\omega_{\mathrm{w}}\) (section 24.2), the circular orbits are no longer solutions, and the free orbit is in general no longer an ellipse.

24.1.4 Example R1: numbers and a check of the massless shaft

Example 24.5 Steel shaft with disk R1 (jeklena gred z diskom R1)

The shaft has diameter \(d = 40\) mm and length between the bearings \(L = 1\) m, the disk has diameter \(D = 400\) mm and thickness \(h = 50\) mm. The steel has \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³. We look for the stiffness, the natural frequency and the effect of the shaft mass.

  1. The second moment of area of the section is \(I = \pi d^4/64 = 1{,}2566\cdot 10^{-7}\) m\(^4\) (definition 5.26) and \(EI = 24\,630{,}1\) N m\(^2\).
  2. The stiffness at the middle is by equation (5.57) \(k = 48EI/L^3 = 1{,}1822\cdot 10^{6}\) N/m. Check: a cantilever of length \(L/2\), clamped at the middle, with a force \(F/2\) at its end, has the deflection \((F/2)(L/2)^3/(3EI) = F L^3/(48EI)\), because \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac18\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac1{48}\). This is the same value.
  3. The mass of the disk is \(m = \rho\,\pi D^2 h/4 = 49{,}009\) kg, the mass of the shaft \(m_{\mathrm{s}} = \rho\,\pi d^2 L/4 = 9{,}802\) kg, so \(20{,}0\) % of the disk mass.
  4. The natural angular frequency is \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}} = \sqrt{k/m} = 155{,}32\) 1/s. That is \(f = 24{,}719\) Hz, period \(T = 40{,}45\) ms and \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\cdot 60/(2\pi) = 1483{,}2\) rev/min.
  5. The static sag under weight is \(mg/k = 0{,}407\) mm. The slope of the shaft at the bearing is \(3|p|/L\) (theorem 24.2, step 2), for \(|p| = 3{,}33\) mm that is \(0{,}01\).
  6. Check of the massless shaft with the Rayleigh quotient. The static deflection curve on one half, \(v = p\,(3\xi - 4\xi^3)\) with \(\xi = x/L\) and \(p\) the displacement of the middle, is an admissible field of the simply supported beam (definition 13.49, chapter 13). The derivative at \(\xi = 0\) is \(3p/L\), which gives the slope at the bearing of step 5. The kinetic energy of the shaft is \(\tfrac12\rho A L\,\dot p^2\cdot 2\int_0^{1/2}(3\xi - 4\xi^3)^2\mathrm{d}\xi\). The integral is \(17/70\), times two \(17/35\) (SymPy), so it is proportional to \(\tfrac{17}{35}\,m_{\mathrm{s}}\), not to the whole mass. The strain energy of this shape is \(\tfrac12 k\,p^2\). The quotient is \(\omega^2 = k/(m + \tfrac{17}{35}m_{\mathrm{s}})\), which gives \(\omega = 148{,}28\) 1/s or \(1416{,}0\) rev/min.
  7. Theorem 13.54 says that the quotient with an admissible field is at least \(\omega_1^2\), but it is proved for a beam without a disk. For the shaft with the disk we therefore check the bound with the frequency equation. By equation (13.31) with \(q = 0\), the shape of a vibration with frequency \(\omega\) on the half \([0, L/2]\) is \(a\sin\beta x + c\sinh\beta x\) with \(\beta^4 = \rho A\,\omega^2/(EI)\), because the displacement and the moment at the bearing are zero. The disk moves only in symmetric shapes, since antisymmetric ones have zero displacement at the middle, so at the middle the slope is zero, and the shear forces on both sides of the disk together accelerate the disk: \(-2EI\,v'''(L/2) = m\,\omega^2 v(L/2)\), with the derivative taken from the left. For a massless shaft this condition with the shape \(3\xi - 4\xi^3\) gives exactly \(\omega^2 = k/m\). The smallest \(\omega\) at which both conditions have a solution \((a, c) \ne (0, 0)\) is \(\omega_1 = 148{,}27\) 1/s (NumPy), just below the quotient \(148{,}28\) 1/s, as the bound requires. The frequency of the massless model is larger than the true one: \((155{,}32 - 148{,}27)/155{,}32 = 0{,}045\). The model of a massless shaft overestimates the frequency by \(4{,}5\) % of its value.
  8. By lemma 24.4, at \(p(0) = 1\) mm and \(\dot p(0) = 0\) the motion is a line segment, at \(\dot p(0) = \mathrm{i}\cdot 0{,}2\,\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\) mm it is an ellipse with semi-axes \(1{,}0\) and \(0{,}2\) mm, in both cases with the frequency \(1483{,}2\) rev/min. What happens if the shaft turns at exactly this speed is shown in section 24.2.

The Rayleigh quotient of step 6 is thus only \(0{,}006\) % above the true frequency of step 7, because the static deflection curve is close to the shape of the first mode. For a quick check the quotient is therefore enough, and the frequency equation shows that in this example the bound also holds for the shaft with the disk. A shaft with \(20\) % of the disk mass is not negligible, and the numbers of the massless model must be read with this deviation.

From history

The model is named after Jeffcott and De Laval: FPGL writes that it is called a Jeffcott (Jeffcott, 1919) or a De Laval rotor in honour of Henry Jeffcott and Carl De Laval, who conducted some of the earliest studies of the dynamics of flexible rotors (FPGL, PDF p. 131). Jeffcott's work is listed in the references of FPGL as The lateral vibration of loaded shafts in the neighbourhood of a whirling speed: The effects of want of balance, in the journal Philosophical Magazine (FPGL, PDF p. 528). The title shows why the name belongs to this model: the paper dealt with the lateral vibration of a loaded shaft near the speed at which it whirls, and with the effect of unbalance, which is the question of the next section. For De Laval FPGL gives no dates, so we write none.

The main finding is therefore this. The Jeffcott rotor has two independent vibrations with the same frequency \(\omega_{\mathrm{n}}\), in the planes \(v\) and \(w\), and the complex coordinate \(p\) combines them into equation (24.2). Every free motion is the sum of a circular forward and a circular backward whirl, and the spin speed does not appear in the equations. When the disk gets an unbalance, the spin speed will enter as an excitation, and in section 24.2 we ask at which speed the shaft bends most.
