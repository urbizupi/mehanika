Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.8 The Lanczos method (Lanczosov postopek)

In section 21.6 we obtained the first mode from one column by repeatedly solving \(\mat K\vek x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \mat M\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\), and in section 21.7 the first \(q\) modes from \(q\) columns. In both we threw away the intermediate columns \(\vek x_0, \vek x_1, \dots\) and kept only the last one or the last block. Yet each of them cost one solution with the factorized matrix \(\mat K\) (lemma 7.47), so the discarded work is lost. In this section we keep everything. If the \(m\) columns that we obtained with \(m\) solutions are independent, they span a space, and by theorem 10.42 the Ritz values from any such space are upper bounds of the eigenvalues. The question is how much more work we need to obtain from these columns a small problem that we can solve. The answer is surprising: if we \(\mat M\)-orthogonalize the columns as we go, each new column needs only the two previous ones, and the reduced matrix is tridiagonal.

The procedure is called the Lanczos method (Lanczosov postopek) after the author who introduced it. CK says of it only that it was introduced in 1950, and since the source does not explain the history of the name, we do not give it here. The source (CK, PDF p. 507) also says something that determines how we treat the method in this section. In rounding arithmetic the recurrence is unstable because of the loss of orthogonality, which at first held the method back, and so for some time the Householder method was the method of choice. With various ways of reorthogonalization, that is, of correcting the vectors as we go so that they stay orthogonal, however, the Lanczos method again became a competitor for large sparse problems of the kind that arise in structural dynamics. We therefore first prove the theorem for computation without rounding, and then on the cantilever of example 21.40 we measure what happens if we run the recurrence without reorthogonalization.

After this section we will be able to build an \(\mat M\)-orthonormal basis from one starting shape with a three-term recurrence (theorem 21.37), to say why the Ritz values from it are upper bounds and never worse than those of inverse iteration with the same starting shape (lemma 21.38), to see that a start at the static solution \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\) gives a basis that contains this solution (corollary 21.41, a continuation of section 21.1), and to measure when and why the method without reorthogonalization goes wrong.

21.8.1 Krylov vectors

Let \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite, hence invertible (lemma 8.21), and \(\mat A\vek x\) is exactly the result of one step of inverse iteration: one multiplication by \(\mat M\) and one solution with the factorization \(\mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\). An eigenpair \((\lambda, \vek\varphi)\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) has \(\mat A\vek\varphi = \vek\varphi/\lambda\), since we multiply \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\vek\varphi\) by \(\lambda^{-1}\mat K^{-1}\). The eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat A\) are therefore \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) with the same modes \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the lowest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) belongs to the largest \(\theta_1\). This explains why repeated multiplication by \(\mat A\) emphasizes the first mode.

Definition 21.36 Krylov subspace (Krilov podprostor)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) and \(\vek r \ne \vek 0\) a column with \(n\) entries. The Krylov vectors (Krilovi vektorji) are \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat A^i\vek r\) for \(i = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), that is \(\vek v_0 = \vek r\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}+1} = \mat A\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}\). The Krylov subspace (Krilov podprostor) of order \(m\) is the set of all linear combinations of the first \(m\) Krylov vectors,

\[\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r) = L(\vek v_0, \vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{m}-1}) = \Big\{ \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{m-1}c_{\mathrm{i}}\mat A^i\vek r \Big\} . \tag{21.46}\]

Source: CK section 15.5.1 (PDF p. 508), where the \(j\)-th Krylov subspace is \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek r)\) with the Krylov vectors \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat A^i\vek r\). The name Krylov is from the source. The notation with \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is ours, because it matches inverse iteration from section 21.6.

Since \(\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the expansion in modes (corollary 10.14) and \(\mat A^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), we have \(\mat A^i\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\). The elements of the Krylov subspace are therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\,p(\theta_{\mathrm{s}})\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) for any polynomial \(p\) of degree less than \(m\). The polynomial can be chosen large at the largest \(\theta_1\) and small at the others, and so the Krylov subspace quickly gathers the first few modes. This is a description, not a proof, and we do not prove the speed in this book. We measure it in example 21.40.

Let us look at the shape of the Krylov vectors on bar C from section 10.9.4 (steel, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(A = 100\) mm², \(L = 1\) m, sixteen elements, clamped at \(x = 0\), force \(1\) kN at the free end) and start at the static solution, \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\).

On the left are the \(x\) axis and the vertical axis with the unit height \(1\), and on them the three Krylov vectors. The first vector \(\vek v_0\) is the static shape of the bar under the end force, a straight line. The second \(\vek v_1\) and the third \(\vek v_2\) have barely separated from it. The \(\mat M\)-cosine of the angle between \(\vek v_0\) and \(\vek v_1\) is \(0{,}99409\), between \(\vek v_1\) and \(\vek v_2\) it is \(0{,}99994\), and the shape \(\vek v_2\) has the cosine \(0{,}999999\) with the first mode (in absolute value, the sign of a mode is arbitrary). Each next vector is thus almost parallel to the previous one, as in inverse iteration from section 21.6: repeated multiplication by \(\mat A\) emphasizes the first mode. For a basis this is a poor choice, because \(\vek v_0, \vek v_1, \vek v_2\) are almost dependent, and so we orthogonalize them. On the right are the \(x\) axis and the vertical axis with the same scale, and on them \(\vek q_1\), \(\vek q_2\) and \(\vek q_3\), which span the same space but are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal. We see that \(\vek q_1\) has no zero crossing, \(\vek q_2\) has one and \(\vek q_3\) two, like the first three modes of the bar. The numbers of zero crossings of \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_4\) are computed (\(0\), \(1\), \(2\), \(3\)), and we do not prove them.

21.8.2 The three-term recurrence

How do we get \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) from the previous ones? The usual Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization would subtract from \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) its parts along all \(j\) previous vectors, but because \(\mat A\) is symmetric with respect to the inner product \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek y\), all terms except the last two are zero. This cannot be guessed, it must be proved.

Theorem 21.37 Lanczos recurrence (Lanczosova rekurenca)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) and \(\vek r \ne \vek 0\) be as in definition 21.36. Set \(\vek q_0 = \vek 0\), \(\beta_1 = 0\) and \(\vek q_1 = \vek r/\sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\), and for \(j = 1, 2, \dots\)

\[\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} , \qquad \beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \sqrt{\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \frac{\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}}{\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}} , \tag{21.47}\]

where the last step is carried out only if \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} > 0\). Let \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\) and let \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the matrix with \(n\) rows and the columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{m}}\). Then:

  1. The columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{m}}\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (definition 10.10) and span \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r)\).
  2. \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat A\,\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), where \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the symmetric tridiagonal matrix of order \(m\) with \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\) on the diagonal and \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}}\) immediately above and below it.
  3. If \(\beta_{\mathrm{m}+1} = 0\), then \(\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), and every eigenvalue \(\theta\) of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat A\), so \(1/\theta\) is an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\).
  4. Let \(\theta_1^{(m)} \ge \dots \ge \theta_{\mathrm{m}}^{(m)}\) be the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} = 1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\). Then \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) for \(s = 1, \dots, m\).
Proof

We proceed in steps. The first two steps prepare the symmetry, the third to sixth give point 1, the seventh point 2, the eighth point 3 and the ninth to eleventh point 4.

  1. For columns \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek y = \vek x^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek y\). The matrix \(\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is symmetric (lemma 1.101, since \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K^{-1}\) are symmetric), so \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek y = \vek y^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek x = (\mat A\vek x)^\T\mat M\,\vek y\). The matrix \(\mat K^{-1}\) is positive definite, because \(\vek y^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek y = (\mat K^{-1}\vek y)^\T\mat K\,(\mat K^{-1}\vek y) > 0\) for \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\), and so \(\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is positive definite too: \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek x = (\mat M\vek x)^\T\mat K^{-1}(\mat M\vek x) > 0\) for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), because \(\mat M\vek x \ne \vek 0\).
  2. From the definition, \(\mat A\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), because \(\mat A\) maps \(\mat A^i\vek r\) to \(\mat A^{i+1}\vek r\).
  3. Induction on \(j\) for the statement \(P(j)\): the vectors \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal and span \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}}\). For \(j = 1\), \(\vek q_1 \in \mathcal K_1\) by definition and \(\vek q_1^\T\mat M\vek q_1 = 1\). Let \(P(j)\) hold for \(j < m\).
  4. Let \(P(j)\) hold for some \(j \le m\). We show that \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) (this holds also if \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\)). For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} = \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - 0 = 0\). For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) (when \(j \ge 2\)): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) by step 1. The previous step of the recurrence gives \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{j}-1}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \beta_{\mathrm{j}-1}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-2}\), so \((\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the whole expression is zero. For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(i \le j-2\): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{i}})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\), and \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{i}+1} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}-1} = L(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})\) by step 2 and \(P(j)\), while \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all of these, so the expression is zero.
  5. For \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} > 0\) the vector \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \vek z_{\mathrm{j}}/\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) is therefore \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and has \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 1\). By step 2, \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), so \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\).
  6. A set of \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns is linearly independent: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek q_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\), multiplication by \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\). So \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) are independent columns in the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) (definition 6.22). We show that they span it. Let \(\vek w \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). Each of the \(j+2\) columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}, \vek w\) is a combination of the \(j+1\) columns \(\vek v_0, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\). Finding numbers \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{j}+2}\), not all zero, with which the combination of these \(j+2\) columns gives \(\vek 0\) is therefore a homogeneous system of \(j+1\) equations (the coefficients of \(\vek v_0, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\)) for \(j+2\) unknowns, which by lemma 10.9 has a nonzero solution. In it the number at \(\vek w\) is nonzero, because the \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\) are independent, so \(\vek w\) is a combination of the columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). This is \(P(j+1)\) and point 1.
  7. Point 2: the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\). From the recurrence \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek z_{\mathrm{k}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \beta_{\mathrm{k}}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and step 4 (for \(i \le k\) we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\), and for \(k < m\) we have \(\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = \beta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\)) this entry is \(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(i = k\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(i = k-1\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for \(i = k+1 \le m\) and \(0\) for \(i > k+1\) or \(i < k-1\). The symmetry of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) follows from step 1.
  8. Point 3: for \(\beta_{\mathrm{m}+1} = 0\) we have \(\vek z_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{m}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\vek q_{\mathrm{m}} + \beta_{\mathrm{m}}\vek q_{\mathrm{m}-1}\), and together with the previous steps of the recurrence \(\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\). If \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \theta\vek a\) with \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\), then \(\mat A(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a) = \theta\,\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\), and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a \ne \vek 0\) because the columns are independent. By the start of the section, \(\theta = 1/\lambda\) for an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\).
  9. Point 4: set \(\mat K^\sharp = \mat M\) and \(\mat M^\sharp = \mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\). Both are symmetric positive definite (step 1). The problem \(\mat K^\sharp\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M^\sharp\vek\varphi\) is \(\mat M\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek\varphi\), so after multiplication by \(\mat M^{-1}\) and by \(\mat K\) it is again \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\vek\varphi\). The pair \((\mat K^\sharp, \mat M^\sharp)\) therefore has the same eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) as the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\).
  10. With the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) (linearly independent columns by step 6) the reduced matrices of the pair \((\mat K^\sharp, \mat M^\sharp)\) are, by lemma 10.40, \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat I\) (point 1) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) (point 2). The reduced eigenproblem of definition 10.41 is \(\mat I\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\), that is \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \tilde\lambda^{-1}\vek a\), so the Ritz values are exactly \(1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\).
  11. By theorem 10.42, therefore, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\).

Source: CK sections 15.5.1 and 15.5.2 (PDF pp. 507 to 511, equations 15.38 to 15.46 and table 15.4): the three-term recurrence for a \(\mat B\)-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspaces of a \(\mat B\)-symmetric matrix, the tridiagonal matrix \(\mat T\) and the Ritz approximation from it. CK writes a general matrix \(\mat B\), we write \(\mat B = \mat M\) and \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\). The proof is written out by us (CK derives the steps with inner products and does not repeat them), and point 3 and the use of theorem 10.42 in point 4 are not from the source.

The theorem says that for each step \(j\) we have one product with \(\mat M\), one solution with the factorization \(\mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\) and two inner products, while the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is tridiagonal and its eigenvalues are cheap (the order \(m\) is small, a tridiagonal matrix has \(3m-2\) numbers). All \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) must be stored as well, because we need them to assemble the Ritz shapes \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\).

The letter \(\alpha\) is already in the book the coefficient of the penalty method of definition 12.27 and the thermal expansion coefficient, here it carries the index \(j\) and means a diagonal entry of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\). The matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is not the column of generalized forces \(\vek Q(t)\) of chapter 10: it has the index \(m\) and \(m\) columns that do not depend on time.

Figure 21.28 checks point 2 of the theorem on the cantilever from section 21.4 with fifty elements (\(n = 100\) free coordinates, \(\mathrm{cond}\,\mat K = 1{,}47\cdot 10^8\)). We start with the vector whose entries are all \(1\). The grid has eight rows and eight columns, the numbers at its edge are the indices \(i\) and \(j\). On the three diagonals there are dots: these are the entries that the theorem allows. All the other entries are marked with small circles and are smaller in absolute value than \(2{,}1\cdot 10^{-11}\) of the geometric mean of their diagonal pair, so below the bound \(10^{-10}\) of the caption. The largest entry outside the three diagonals has the size \(3{,}1\cdot 10^{-12}\) of the largest entry of the matrix. The theorem is for computation without rounding, and the computation with reorthogonalization (described below) confirms it to \(10^{-11}\). The diagonal entries and those next to the diagonal decrease quickly: \(\alpha_1 = 5{,}06\cdot 10^{-2}\), \(\alpha_2 = 3{,}13\cdot 10^{-2}\), \(\alpha_3 = 1{,}09\cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(\alpha_8 = 5{,}99\cdot 10^{-6}\), \(\beta_2 = 3{,}87\cdot 10^{-2}\), \(\beta_3 = 1{,}24\cdot 10^{-3}\) and \(\beta_8 = 3{,}9\cdot 10^{-6}\) (in units in which \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) have \(E I = \rho A = L = 1\)). A small \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) means that \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is already almost in the space of the previous vectors, and this is a sign that the Krylov subspace is almost saturated.

21.8.3 No worse than inverse iteration

The Ritz values from \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) are upper bounds by theorem 21.37. How good are they? We compare them with two other procedures on the same space: with the Ritz approximation of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) on the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) (definition 10.41) and with inverse iteration from section 21.6, which from the start \(\vek r\) gives after \(m-1\) steps \(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}-1} = \mat A^{m-1}\vek r\) and from it a Rayleigh quotient.

Lemma 21.38 The Lanczos estimate is no worse than the Ritz estimate and inverse iteration (Lanczosova ocena ni slabša od Ritzove in od inverzne iteracije)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A\), \(\vek r\), \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) be as in theorem 21.37, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} = 1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\) the Lanczos values and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) the Ritz values of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) let \(\theta(\vek x) = \vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x/\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x\). Then:

  1. \(1/\theta(\vek x) \le R(\vek x)\) for every \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), where \(R\) is the Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.25) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\).
  2. \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) for \(s = 1, \dots, m\).
  3. \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}-1})\) and \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\) for every \(k \le m-1\), where \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat A^k\vek r\).
Proof

Point 1 is an inequality between the two quotients, point 2 follows from it by the minimax theorem, point 3 from point 2.

  1. Let \(\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) be the expansion in modes (corollary 10.14). Then \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), \(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), and because \(\mat A\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) also \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  2. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for two sums, \(\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\big)^2 = \big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}}\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}}\,c_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot c_{\mathrm{s}}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}}\big)^2 \le \big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\big)\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\), gives \(1/\theta(\vek x) = \sum c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum(c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}) \le \sum\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum c_{\mathrm{s}}^2 = R(\vek x)\). This is point 1.
  3. For \(\vek x = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x = \vek a^\T\vek a\) and \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x = \vek a^\T\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\) (theorem 21.37, points 1 and 2), so \(\vek a^\T\vek a/\vek a^\T\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = 1/\theta(\vek x)\). The matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is symmetric and positive definite as a reduced matrix (lemma 10.40, step 10 of the proof of theorem 21.37), so the Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\mat I, \mat T_{\mathrm{m}})\) at \(\vek a\) equals \(1/\theta(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a)\). The Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \mat I)\) with \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) at \(\vek a\) is \(R(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a)\). Because \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) has independent columns, the \(s\)-dimensional subspaces of the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) are exactly the images \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal S'\) of the \(s\)-dimensional subspaces \(\mathcal S'\) of the space of all columns \(\vek a\) with \(m\) entries.
  4. By theorem 10.28, point 2, applied to the reduced pairs, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L}\) is the smallest value, over all \(s\)-dimensional subspaces \(\mathcal S \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), of the largest value of \(1/\theta(\vek x)\) on \(\mathcal S\), and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) is the same with \(R(\vek x)\). By step 2, \(1/\theta \le R\) everywhere, so on every \(\mathcal S\) the largest value of the first function is less than or equal to the largest value of the second, and hence \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\). The inequality \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L}\) is point 4 of theorem 21.37. This is point 2.
  5. Point 3: inverse iteration (definition 21.26) divides each step by a number \(t_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), which does not change the Rayleigh quotient, so its \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals \(\mat A^k\vek r\) up to a factor. This vector is in \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{k}+1} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(k \le m-1\). By theorem 10.28, point 1, at \(s = 1\) we have \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R} = \min R\) on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\), and by point 2, \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R}\).

Source: The inequalities in the proof are not from the source. CK section 15.5.2 (PDF p. 511) writes the Ritz approximation from \(\mat T\), it does not compare it with the Ritz approximation of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) or with inverse iteration. The comparison is ours.

The lemma says that the Lanczos value is never worse than the Ritz value on the same space and than the Rayleigh quotient of inverse iteration with the same number of columns. Unlike the Ritz value from \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), however, it also uses \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{m}}\), which is one more solution (the last value \(\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\)). So the lemma does not say whether Lanczos with \(m\) solutions is better than inverse iteration with \(m\) steps, since \(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\). The measurements in example 21.39 compare the two.

21.8.4 Convergence of several modes at once

Inverse iteration gives one mode at a time, Lanczos gives all Ritz values \(\tilde\lambda_1, \dots, \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) from the same basis. Let us see how quickly they approach.

21.8.5 What happens without reorthogonalization

The theorem holds for computation without rounding. What if we run the recurrence (21.47) in ordinary double precision, without any measures? The figure shows that the matter is not harmless.

The top panel shows how well the vectors \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) are orthonormal. The axes show \(m\) from \(0\) to \(40\) and the deviation from \(10^{-16}\) to \(1\). The orange curve is the computation without any measure: the deviation is \(6{,}7\cdot 10^{-16}\) at \(m = 1\), already \(2{,}3\cdot 10^{-10}\) at \(m = 3\), \(1{,}3\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(m = 5\), \(0{,}50\) at \(m = 6\) and \(1{,}0\) at \(m = 20\). Vectors that should be perpendicular are no longer even approximately perpendicular. The blue curve is the computation with reorthogonalization: the deviation stays at \(10^{-15}\) throughout, at \(m = 40\) it is \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-15}\). Reorthogonalization is the measure that CK gives (table 15.6, PDF p. 512): after each step we subtract from the new vector its projections on all previous \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\vek z \leftarrow \vek z - \sum_{\mathrm{i} \le \mathrm{j}}(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z)\,\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\). In our computation we do this twice in a row, so that only rounding remains. The cost in step \(j\) is \(j\) inner products and \(j\) additions of columns of length \(n\), done twice. It needs no extra memory, because we store all \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) anyway (\(m\,n = 4000\) numbers at \(m = 40\) and \(n = 100\)).

The bottom panel shows what the loss of orthogonality does to the values. The axes show \(m\) and the number of eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) that agree with \(\omega_1\) to \(10^{-6}\). The blue curve (with reorthogonalization) is \(1\) throughout: one eigenvalue, as it should be. The orange curve (without it) is \(1\) up to \(m = 6\), jumps to \(2\) at \(m = 7\) and then grows: after \(m = 12\) steps it is \(3\), after \(m = 20\) it is \(5\), after \(m = 30\) it is \(7\), and after \(m = 40\) there are \(10\) copies of the same frequency. The second copy appears at \(m = 7\), when the deviation reaches \(0{,}61\) (at \(m = 6\) it is \(0{,}50\) and there is no copy yet). At \(m = 8\) the list of the lowest five is \(3{,}516\), \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), \(120{,}902\) (exact \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), \(120{,}902\), \(199{,}860\)): the true values are there, but the first is doubled. At \(m = 20\) the first five computed values are all \(3{,}516\), and the true \(\omega_2 = 22{,}034\) is not among them. Someone who counted the eigenvalues would conclude at \(m = 8\) that \(\omega_1\) is double, and at \(m = 40\) that it is tenfold.

Why does this happen? CK does not derive the reasons and only names the loss of orthogonality (PDF p. 507). We measure it: the deviation grows when the first frequency has already been computed to \(5{,}6\cdot 10^{-9}\), since then the rounding error in the direction \(\vek\varphi_1\) does not vanish from the computation, but multiplication by \(\mat A\) amplifies it as in inverse iteration, and the vector \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) again receives the first mode, which is already in the basis. This explanation is our conjecture and we do not prove it here. What we know for certain is only what the computation shows: without reorthogonalization the vectors are not \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (deviation up to \(1{,}0\)) and the copies of the Ritz values are spurious. Theorem 21.37 claims nothing about such a computation. The Ritz values from \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) in such a computation are still eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), but not Ritz values of an \(\mat M\)-orthonormal basis, so point 4 of the theorem is not proved for them.

Let us add one more experiment, which reaches back into section 21.6. If we start with a vector from which we have removed the \(\vek\varphi_1\) part (\(|\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek r| = 1{,}6\cdot 10^{-16}\)), with reorthogonalization and \(m = 8\), we still find \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), with an error of \(5{,}6\cdot 10^{-9}\) in the first. In computation without rounding this would not happen: with \(c_1 = 0\) every \(\mat A^i\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) is without \(\vek\varphi_1\), so the Krylov subspace does not contain the first mode. The result shows that the remainder \(10^{-16}\) and the rounding of every step were amplified enough in eight steps for the method to find the first mode (we inferred the cause from the result, we did not prove it), just as inverse iteration with a start without the first mode found it in section 21.6. Reorthogonalization does not prevent this, because it removes only directions that are already in the basis, not new ones that rounding creates.

21.8.6 Starting at the static solution

So far we started at an arbitrary vector. If we compute the response to a known load, one choice of start is particularly useful: \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\).

COOK has such a start too (section 11.8, PDF pp. 412 to 414): the first Ritz vector is the static solution, normalized with the mass, and each next one arises from the static solution for the inertia force that the previous shape produces, that is, with the same multiplication by \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\). By this description the span is the same as \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), and we do not compare the orthogonalization equations of COOK here. COOK writes that the static correction is thus automatically in the basis and that the Ritz vector method »spans the spatial distribution of loading but approximates its frequency content«, which is the opposite of the modal method.

Corollary 21.41 Load-dependent Ritz vectors (Ritzovi vektorji obremenitve)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A\) be as before, \(\vek F \ne \vek 0\) a column of forces, \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\), \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) from theorem 21.37 with \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\), and \(\Omega \ge 0\) such that \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} - \Omega^2\mat I\) is an invertible matrix. Let \(\vek a\) be the solution of the reduced system

\[\big(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} - \Omega^2\mat I\big)\,\vek a = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F \tag{21.48}\]

and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\). Then:

  1. \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F = \sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\;\vek q_1\) is in the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r)\).
  2. For \(\Omega = 0\), \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\) for every \(m \ge 1\).
  3. If \(\Omega^2 < \lambda_1\), then \(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M\) is a positive definite matrix and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the one among all \(\vek y \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) at which the expression \((\vek u - \vek y)^\T(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)(\vek u - \vek y)\) has its smallest value, where \(\vek u = (\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)^{-1}\vek F\) is the exact solution. The error in this norm does not increase as \(m\) increases.
Proof

Point 1 follows from the definition, point 2 from point 1, point 3 from the Galerkin condition.

  1. By definition \(\vek q_1 = \vek r/\sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\) and \(\vek r = \vek v_0 \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(m \ge 1\). This is point 1.
  2. For \(\Omega = 0\) we have \(\vek r = c\,\vek q_1\) with \(c = \sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\), so \(\vek r = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(c\,\vek e_1)\) with \(\vek e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\). Hence \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(c\,\vek e_1) = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\vek r = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F\), and \(c\,\vek e_1\) solves (21.48). The solution is unique, because \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is positive definite (lemma 10.40), so \(\vek a = c\,\vek e_1\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = c\,\vek q_1 = \vek r\).
  3. By theorem 10.26, \(\vek v^\T(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)\vek v = (R(\vek v) - \Omega^2)\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v \ge (\lambda_1 - \Omega^2)\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v > 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\). So \(\mat G = \mat K - \Omega^2\mat M\) is positive definite.
  4. Let \(\vek e = \vek u - \vek u_{\mathrm{m}}\). Since \(\mat G\vek u = \vek F\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F\) (equation (21.48), in which \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat I\)), we have \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\vek e = \vek 0\). For every \(\vek y = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek b\) we have \(\vek u - \vek y = \vek e + \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b)\) and \(\vek e^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b) = 0\), so \((\vek u - \vek y)^\T\mat G(\vek u - \vek y) = \vek e^\T\mat G\vek e + (\vek a - \vek b)^\T\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b) \ge \vek e^\T\mat G\vek e\), with equality exactly for \(\vek b = \vek a\) (the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is positive definite, because \(\mat G\) is positive definite and the columns are independent).
  5. Since \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\), the smallest value on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\) is not larger than the smallest value on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\).

Source: COOK section 11.8 (PDF pp. 412 to 414): load-dependent Ritz vectors, the first is the static solution normalized with the mass, and the static correction is included automatically. The proof of points 2 and 3 is not from the source, point 3 is a standard property of the Galerkin approximation and is written out by us.

Point 2 is the same as for the mode-acceleration correction (theorem 21.4, point 2): at \(\Omega = 0\) the solution is exact. The difference is in the cost. The mode-acceleration correction needs the modes, the Krylov basis does not: since the start \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\) contains the static shape already in the first column, \(m\) solutions with the already computed factorization are enough.

The axes show \(\Omega/\omega_1\) from \(0\) to \(3\) and the error from \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{2}\) %, only the spikes at the resonances reach above the last tick. The two dashed verticals are at \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\). The orange curve, the mode-displacement solution, is \(9{,}8\) % too small at \(\Omega = 0\) and \(7{,}7\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\), which is the known error of truncated modes from section 21.1. The blackwhite curve (mode acceleration) is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) and has an error of \(0{,}037\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\). The blue curve is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) as well and has an error of \(2\cdot 10^{-4}\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\). At \(\Omega = 1{,}5\,\omega_1\) the blue one is at \(+2{,}2\) %, the blackwhite one at \(+1{,}08\) % and the orange one at \(+24\) %. At \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\) the blue one is at \(-44{,}2\) %, the blackwhite one at \(-5{,}9\) % and the orange one at \(-45{,}0\) %. Where \(\Omega\) is above \(\omega_1\), so that the condition of point 3 of the corollary no longer holds, the Krylov solution with two vectors is still close to the exact one at \(1{,}5\,\omega_1\) (\(2{,}2\) %), but much worse than the mode-acceleration correction at \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\).

Now the numbers for more vectors. The end errors at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\) are \(-1{,}13\) %, \(-2\cdot 10^{-4}\) % and less than \(10^{-4}\) % with \(m = 1, 2, 3\) Lanczos vectors, and \(-15{,}1\) %, \(-7{,}7\) % and \(-5{,}1\) % with \(N = 1, 2, 3\) modes. The end errors with \(m = 1, 2, 3\) vectors are \(-142{,}60\) %, \(-57{,}17\) % and \(-4{,}86\) %. Corollary 21.41 thus does not claim that the error is small at every frequency. It claims that the solution is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) and the best in its subspace for \(\Omega^2 < \lambda_1\). The mode-acceleration correction with \(N = 2\) modes has the errors \(-5{,}9\) % and \(-1{,}7\) % at \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\) and \(2{,}9\,\omega_1\), the Krylov solution with \(m = 2\) has \(-44{,}2\) % and \(-79{,}8\) %: in the mode-acceleration correction the second resonance appears at its exact frequency, not at a Ritz frequency.

21.8.7 Other methods

For the choice of a solver for the eigenproblem CK (§15.1, PDF pp. 488 to 489) gives four guidelines after Bathe and Wilson and after Golub and Van Loan. First: Householder-QR-inverse iteration is the most efficient when we need all eigenpairs of a matrix that has a large bandwidth or is full. Second: the determinant search is very effective for the lowest eigenpairs of systems with a small bandwidth. Third: subspace iteration is very effective for the lowest eigenpairs of systems with a large bandwidth that are too large for fast memory. Fourth: the Lanczos method can be efficient for a few extremal eigenvalues of a large sparse problem. The QR method, Householder's method and Sturm sequences are only named in this book, because we do not need them here and they are not proved. We use the Lanczos method for a few lowest frequencies of a large structure with a sparse matrix: the cost is \(m\) solutions, \(m\) stored vectors of length \(n\) and the eigenproblem of a tridiagonal matrix of order \(m\).

The Krylov basis is not only a way of finding natural frequencies. We started with the same idea as Guyan and Craig-Bampton reduction from sections 21.4 and 21.5: we choose a matrix \(\mat\Psi\) and reduce by definition 10.41. In Guyan reduction the columns are given by static shapes, in Craig-Bampton reduction also by the modes of the substructures, in Lanczos the columns are given by the load and the matrix \(\mat A\). For all of them theorem 10.42 holds with the same line of proof, and all are Ritz approximations. In the next section (21.9) we look at what happens to such an approximation when we add damping to it.
