Chapter 21 Modal reduction and eigenvalue solvers

21.8 The Lanczos method (Lanczosov postopek)

In section 21.6 we obtained the first mode from one column by repeatedly solving \(\mat K\vek x_{\mathrm{k}+1} = \mat M\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\), and in section 21.7 the first \(q\) modes from \(q\) columns. In both we threw away the intermediate columns \(\vek x_0, \vek x_1, \dots\) and kept only the last one or the last block. Yet each of them cost one solution with the factorized matrix \(\mat K\) (lemma 7.47), so the discarded work is lost. In this section we keep everything. If the \(m\) columns that we obtained with \(m\) solutions are independent, they span a space, and by theorem 10.59 the Ritz values from any such space are upper bounds of the eigenvalues. The question is how much more work we need to obtain from these columns a small problem that we can solve. The answer is surprising: if we \(\mat M\)-orthogonalize the columns as we go, each new column needs only the two previous ones, and the reduced matrix is tridiagonal. In the figure the discarded columns of inverse iteration are grey and the kept ones blue. The rows from the top are inverse iteration, subspace iteration and Lanczos, and the dots mean that the vectors go on up to \(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}}\). Each is the result of one solve, and in the Lanczos method each new vector needs only two previous ones.

The procedure is called the Lanczos method (Lanczosov postopek) after the author who introduced it. It was introduced in 1950 (CK, PDF p. 507). The source also says something that determines how we treat the method in this section. In rounding arithmetic the recurrence is unstable because of the loss of orthogonality, which at first held the method back, and so for some time the Householder method was the method of choice. With various ways of reorthogonalization, that is, of correcting the vectors as we go so that they stay orthogonal, however, the Lanczos method again became a competitor for large sparse problems of the kind that arise in structural dynamics. We therefore first prove the theorem for computation without rounding, and then on the cantilever of example 21.40 we measure what happens if we run the recurrence without reorthogonalization.

After this section we will be able to build an \(\mat M\)-orthonormal basis from one starting shape with a three-term recurrence (theorem 21.37), to say why the Ritz values from it are upper bounds and never worse than those of inverse iteration with the same starting shape (lemma 21.38), to see that a start at the static solution \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\) gives a basis that contains this solution (corollary 21.41, a continuation of section 21.1), and to measure when and why the method without reorthogonalization goes wrong.

21.8.1 Krylov vectors

Let \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\). The matrix \(\mat K\) is positive definite, hence invertible (lemma 8.22), and \(\mat A\vek x\) is exactly the result of one step of inverse iteration: one multiplication by \(\mat M\) and one solution with the factorization \(\mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\). An eigenpair \((\lambda, \vek\varphi)\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) has \(\mat A\vek\varphi = \vek\varphi/\lambda\), since we multiply \(\mat K\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\vek\varphi\) by \(\lambda^{-1}\mat K^{-1}\). The eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat A\) are therefore \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) with the same modes \(\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the lowest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) belongs to the largest \(\theta_1\). This explains why repeated multiplication by \(\mat A\) emphasizes the first mode. In the figure the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\) are mapped by inversion to the values \(\theta_{\mathrm{r}} = 1/\lambda_{\mathrm{r}}\), and the smallest \(\lambda_1\) belongs to the largest \(\theta_1\).

Definition 21.36 Krylov subspace (Krilov podprostor)

Let \(\mat K\) and \(\mat M\) be symmetric positive definite matrices of order \(n\), \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) and \(\vek r \ne \vek 0\) a column with \(n\) entries. The Krylov vectors (Krilovi vektorji) are \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat A^i\vek r\) for \(i = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), that is \(\vek v_0 = \vek r\) and \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}+1} = \mat A\vek v_{\mathrm{i}}\). The Krylov subspace (Krilov podprostor) of order \(m\) is the set of all linear combinations of the first \(m\) Krylov vectors,

\[\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r) = L(\vek v_0, \vek v_1, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{m}-1}) = \Big\{ \sum_{\mathrm{i}=0}^{m-1}c_{\mathrm{i}}\mat A^i\vek r \Big\} . \tag{21.48}\]

In the figure the Krylov vectors form a chain in which \(\mat A\) maps each vector to the next, and the Krylov subspace is the space that they span.

Source: CK section 15.5.1 (PDF p. 508), where the \(j\)-th Krylov subspace is \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}}(\vek r)\) with the Krylov vectors \(\vek v_{\mathrm{i}} = \mat A^i\vek r\). The name Krylov is from the source. The notation with \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is ours, because it matches inverse iteration from section 21.6.

Since \(\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) is the expansion in modes (corollary 10.22) and \(\mat A^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\), we have \(\mat A^i\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\). The elements of the Krylov subspace are therefore \(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\,p(\theta_{\mathrm{s}})\,\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) for any polynomial \(p\) of degree less than \(m\). The polynomial can be chosen large at the largest \(\theta_1\) and small at the others, and so the Krylov subspace quickly gathers the first few modes. This is a description, not a proof, and we do not prove the speed in this book. We measure it in example 21.40.

Let us look at the shape of the Krylov vectors on bar C from section 10.10.5 (steel, \(E = 196\) GPa, \(\rho = 7800\) kg/m³, \(A = 100\) mm², \(L = 1\) m, sixteen elements, clamped at \(x = 0\), force \(1\) kN at the free end) and start at the static solution, \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\).

On the left are the \(x\) axis and the vertical axis with the unit height \(1\), and on them the three Krylov vectors. The first vector \(\vek v_0\) is the static shape of the bar under the end force, a straight line. The second \(\vek v_1\) and the third \(\vek v_2\) have barely separated from it. The \(\mat M\)-cosine of the angle between \(\vek v_0\) and \(\vek v_1\) is \(0{,}99409\), between \(\vek v_1\) and \(\vek v_2\) it is \(0{,}99994\), and the shape \(\vek v_2\) has the cosine \(0{,}999999\) with the first mode (in absolute value, the sign of a mode is arbitrary). Each next vector is thus almost parallel to the previous one, as in inverse iteration from section 21.6: repeated multiplication by \(\mat A\) emphasizes the first mode. For a basis this is a poor choice, because \(\vek v_0, \vek v_1, \vek v_2\) are almost dependent, and so we orthogonalize them. On the right are the \(x\) axis and the vertical axis with the same scale, and on them \(\vek q_1\), \(\vek q_2\) and \(\vek q_3\), which span the same space but are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal. We see that \(\vek q_1\) has no zero crossing, \(\vek q_2\) has one and \(\vek q_3\) two, like the first three modes of the bar. The numbers of zero crossings of \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_4\) are computed (\(0\), \(1\), \(2\), \(3\)), and we do not prove them.

21.8.2 The three-term recurrence

Before we write the general rule, we do the procedure by hand on the frame with three floors from section 21.6.1, where everything can be seen. In the units \(k = \bar m = 1\) we have \(\mat M = \mat I\), so \(\mat M\)-orthogonality is here an ordinary right angle and the columns can be drawn as arrows in the space of the three floor displacements. The matrix \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is simply \(\mat K^{-1}\) with the rows \((1, 1, 1)\), \((1, 2, 2)\) and \((1, 2, 3)\). The floor displacements are written here as entries of a column, and the letter \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) means a Lanczos vector in this section.

We start at \(\vek r = (1, 1, 1)^\T\), all floors displaced equally. We divide by the length \(\sqrt 3\) and get the first vector \(\vek q_1 = (1, 1, 1)^\T/\sqrt 3\). One step of inverse iteration maps it to \(\mat A\vek q_1 = (3, 5, 6)^\T/\sqrt 3\), which is the static shape from section 21.6.1. This arrow no longer points along \(\vek q_1\). How much of it lies along \(\vek q_1\) is given by the inner product \(\alpha_1 = \vek q_1^\T\mat A\vek q_1 = (3 + 5 + 6)/3 = 14/3 = 4{,}667\), and we subtract this part \(\alpha_1\vek q_1\). What remains is \(\vek z_1 = (3, 5, 6)^\T/\sqrt 3 - \tfrac{14}{3}(1, 1, 1)^\T/\sqrt 3 = (-5, 1, 4)^\T/(3\sqrt 3)\), the part that stands at a right angle to \(\vek q_1\). Its length is \(\beta_2 = \sqrt{42}/(3\sqrt 3) = \sqrt{14}/3 = 1{,}247\), and its direction is the second vector \(\vek q_2 = (-5, 1, 4)^\T/\sqrt{42}\). The arrows \(\vek q_1\) and \(\vek q_2\) span the plane \(\mathcal K_2\), and \(\mat A\vek q_1 = \alpha_1\vek q_1 + \beta_2\vek q_2\) lies in it.

The second step: \(\mat A\vek q_2 = (0, 5, 9)^\T/\sqrt{42}\). We subtract the part along \(\vek q_2\), \(\alpha_2 = \vek q_2^\T\mat A\vek q_2 = (0 + 5 + 36)/42 = 41/42 = 0{,}976\), and the part along \(\vek q_1\), which is \(\vek q_1^\T\mat A\vek q_2 = (0 + 5 + 9)/(\sqrt 3\sqrt{42}) = \sqrt{14}/3\), so \(\beta_2\) again. The remainder has the length \(\beta_3 = \sqrt 3/14 = 0{,}124\) and the direction \(\vek q_3 = (1, -3, 2)^\T/\sqrt{14}\), which stands at a right angle to the plane.

The third step shows what is essential in the method. \(\mat A\vek q_3 = (0, -1, 1)^\T/\sqrt{14}\), its part along \(\vek q_3\) is \(\alpha_3 = (0 + 3 + 2)/14 = 5/14 = 0{,}357\) and along \(\vek q_2\) again \(\beta_3\). Its part along \(\vek q_1\), however, is \(\vek q_1^\T\mat A\vek q_3 = (0 - 1 + 1)/(\sqrt 3\sqrt{14}) = 0\). So from \(\mat A\vek q_3\) we subtract only two parts, not three. We put all these inner products into a table, the number \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) into row \(i\) and column \(j\): on the diagonal are \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\), next to it \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\), in the corners two zeros.

Why is the corner zero? The zero is not an accident of the numbers. We ask how much of \(\mat A\vek q_3\) lies along \(\vek q_1\). Because \(\mat A\) is symmetric here, this is the same as asking how much of \(\mat A\vek q_1\) lies along \(\vek q_3\): \(\vek q_1^\T\mat A\vek q_3 = (\mat A\vek q_1)^\T\vek q_3\). The arrow \(\mat A\vek q_1\) lies in the plane, and \(\vek q_3\) stands at a right angle to it, so the answer is zero. In general it is the same: \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\) lies in the space of the first \(i + 1\) vectors, and every later vector from \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}+2}\) on is at a right angle to that space. This is the whole reason for the three-term recurrence, the theorem only writes it down with all its conditions.

The eigenvalues of the table give approximations to the eigenvalues of the frame \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\). From the upper left piece of order \(1\) we get \(1/\alpha_1 = 3/14 = 0{,}2143\), from the piece of order \(2\) the values \(0{,}19807\) and \(1{,}6829\), and from the whole table exactly \(0{,}198062\), \(1{,}554958\) and \(3{,}246980\). After three steps the remainder is \(\vek z_3 = \vek 0\): in a space of three dimensions there is no fourth perpendicular direction.

The rows are \(m = 1\), \(m = 2\) and \(m = 3\): in row \(m\) are the Ritz values from the table \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\). In the figure, at the top, are the eigenvalues \(\theta\) of the table \(\mat T_3\) on the \(\theta\) axis, which the inversion \(1/\theta\) maps to the \(\lambda\) axis. There the eigenvalues of the frame are blackwhite. The Ritz value from \(\mat T_1\) is \(0{,}2143\), from \(\mat T_2\) they are \(0{,}19807\) and \(1{,}6829\), and from \(\mat T_3\) all three are exact. Each lies to the right of its eigenvalue or on it: this is point 4 of the theorem that we write below.

How do we get \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) from the previous ones in general? The usual Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization would subtract from \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) its parts along all \(j\) previous vectors, but because \(\mat A\) is symmetric with respect to the inner product \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek y\), all terms except the last two are zero. On the frame we saw this in numbers, for every pair of matrices it must be proved.

Theorem 21.37 Lanczos recurrence (Lanczosova rekurenca)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\) and \(\vek r \ne \vek 0\) be as in definition 21.36. Set \(\vek q_0 = \vek 0\), \(\beta_1 = 0\) and \(\vek q_1 = \vek r/\sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\), and for \(j = 1, 2, \dots\)

\[\alpha_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} , \qquad \vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} , \qquad \beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \sqrt{\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}} , \qquad \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \frac{\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}}{\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}} , \tag{21.49}\]

where the last step is carried out only if \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} > 0\). On the frame \(\vek q_1\) is the start, \(\alpha_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) the part that we subtract, \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}\) the remainder, perpendicular to the previous ones, of length \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) its direction. Let \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\) and let \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) be the matrix with \(n\) rows and the columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{m}}\). Then:

  1. The columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{m}}\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (definition 10.17) and span \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r)\). On the frame these are \(\vek q_1\), \(\vek q_2\) and \(\vek q_3\), mutually perpendicular, and the first two span the plane.
  2. \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat A\,\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), where \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the symmetric tridiagonal matrix of order \(m\) with \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\) on the diagonal and \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}}\) immediately above and below it. On the frame this is the table with the diagonal, the two neighbours and the zeros in the corners.
  3. If \(\beta_{\mathrm{m}+1} = 0\), then \(\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), and every eigenvalue \(\theta\) of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is an eigenvalue of the matrix \(\mat A\), so \(1/\theta\) is an eigenvalue of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). On the frame the Ritz values from \(\mat T_3\) are the exact eigenvalues at \(m = 3\).
  4. Let \(\theta_1^{(m)} \ge \dots \ge \theta_{\mathrm{m}}^{(m)}\) be the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} = 1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\). Then \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) for \(s = 1, \dots, m\). On the frame each Ritz value lies to the right of the eigenvalue or on it.
Proof

We proceed in steps. The first two steps prepare the symmetry, the third to sixth give point 1, the seventh point 2, the eighth point 3 and the ninth to eleventh point 4.

  1. For columns \(\vek x\), \(\vek y\) we have
    \[\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek y = \vek x^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\,\vek y .\]
    The matrix \(\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is symmetric (lemma 1.102, since \(\mat M\) and \(\mat K^{-1}\) are symmetric), so
    \[\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek y = \vek y^\T\mat M\mat A\,\vek x = (\mat A\vek x)^\T\mat M\,\vek y .\]
    The matrix \(\mat K^{-1}\) is positive definite, because for \(\vek y \ne \vek 0\)
    \[\vek y^\T\mat K^{-1}\vek y = (\mat K^{-1}\vek y)^\T\mat K\,(\mat K^{-1}\vek y) > 0 .\]
    So \(\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\) is positive definite too, because for \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) we have \(\mat M\vek x \ne \vek 0\) and
    \[\vek x^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek x = (\mat M\vek x)^\T\mat K^{-1}(\mat M\vek x) > 0 .\]
  2. From the definition, \(\mat A\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), because \(\mat A\) maps \(\mat A^i\vek r\) to \(\mat A^{i+1}\vek r\). On the frame: \(\mat A\vek q_1\) lies in the plane \(\mathcal K_2\).
  3. Induction on \(j\) for the statement \(P(j)\): the vectors \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) are \(\mat M\)-orthonormal and span \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}}\). For \(j = 1\), \(\vek q_1 \in \mathcal K_1\) by definition and \(\vek q_1^\T\mat M\vek q_1 = 1\). Let \(P(j)\) hold for \(j < m\). On the frame \(P(1)\) is the vector \(\vek q_1\) and \(P(2)\) the plane \(\mathcal K_2\) with the vectors \(\vek q_1\) and \(\vek q_2\).
  4. Let \(P(j)\) hold for some \(j \le m\). We show that \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) (this holds also if \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\)). For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} = \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{j}} - 0 = 0\). For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}\) (when \(j \ge 2\)): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} - \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) by step 1. The previous step of the recurrence gives \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{j}-1}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} + \beta_{\mathrm{j}-1}\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-2}\), so \((\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} = \beta_{\mathrm{j}}\) and the whole expression is zero. For \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\) with \(i \le j-2\): \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} = (\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{i}})^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\), and \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{i}} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{i}+1} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}-1} = L(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1})\) by step 2 and \(P(j)\), while \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to all of these, so the expression is zero. On the frame this is the right angle between the remainder \(\vek z_1\) and \(\vek q_1\), and between \(\vek q_3\) and the plane.
  5. For \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} > 0\) the vector \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = \vek z_{\mathrm{j}}/\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) is therefore \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) and has \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}^\T\mat M\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} = 1\). By step 2, \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), and \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}-1} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\), so \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1} \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). On the frame this is \(\vek q_3\), which stands perpendicular to the plane.
  6. A set of \(\mat M\)-orthonormal columns is linearly independent: from \(\sum_{\mathrm{i}} c_{\mathrm{i}}\vek q_{\mathrm{i}} = \vek 0\), multiplication by \(\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}^\T\mat M\) gives \(c_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\). So \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) are independent columns in the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) (definition 6.22). We show that they span it. Let \(\vek w \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). Each of the \(j+2\) columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}, \vek w\) is a combination of the \(j+1\) columns \(\vek v_0, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\). Finding numbers \(d_1, \dots, d_{\mathrm{j}+2}\), not all zero, with which the combination of these \(j+2\) columns gives \(\vek 0\) is therefore a homogeneous system of \(j+1\) equations (the coefficients of \(\vek v_0, \dots, \vek v_{\mathrm{j}}\)) for \(j+2\) unknowns, which by lemma 10.16 has a nonzero solution. In it the number at \(\vek w\) is nonzero, because the \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\) are independent, so \(\vek w\) is a combination of the columns \(\vek q_1, \dots, \vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\). This is \(P(j+1)\) and point 1. On the frame \(\vek q_3\) is not in the plane, so it is not a combination of \(\vek q_1\) and \(\vek q_2\).
  7. Point 2: the entry \((i, k)\) of the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{k}}\). From the recurrence \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} = \vek z_{\mathrm{k}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}} + \beta_{\mathrm{k}}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}-1}\) and step 4 (for \(i \le k\) we have \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = 0\), and for \(k < m\) we have \(\vek z_{\mathrm{k}} = \beta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\vek q_{\mathrm{k}+1}\)) this entry is \(\alpha_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(i = k\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}}\) for \(i = k-1\), \(\beta_{\mathrm{k}+1}\) for \(i = k+1 \le m\) and \(0\) for \(i > k+1\) or \(i < k-1\). The symmetry of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) follows from step 1. On the frame these are the diagonal, the numbers next to it and the zeros in the corners.
  8. Point 3: for \(\beta_{\mathrm{m}+1} = 0\) we have \(\vek z_{\mathrm{m}} = \vek 0\) and \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{m}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\vek q_{\mathrm{m}} + \beta_{\mathrm{m}}\vek q_{\mathrm{m}-1}\), and together with the previous steps of the recurrence \(\mat A\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\). If \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \theta\vek a\) with \(\vek a \ne \vek 0\), then \(\mat A(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a) = \theta\,\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\), and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a \ne \vek 0\) because the columns are independent. By the start of the section, \(\theta = 1/\lambda\) for an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). On the frame this happens at \(m = 3\): \(\beta_4 = 0\) and the eigenvalues of the table \(\mat T_3\) are the exact eigenvalues of the frame.
  9. Point 4: set \(\mat K^\sharp = \mat M\) and \(\mat M^\sharp = \mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\), both symmetric positive definite (step 1). The eigenproblem of the pair \((\mat K^\sharp, \mat M^\sharp)\) is
    \[\mat M\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek\varphi .\]
    Multiply by \(\mat M^{-1}\):
    \[\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat K^{-1}\mat M\vek\varphi .\]
    Multiply by \(\mat K\):
    \[\mat K\vek\varphi = \lambda\mat M\vek\varphi .\]
    This is the original problem, so the two pairs have the same eigenvalues \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\).
  10. With the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) (linearly independent columns by step 6) the reduced matrices of the pair \((\mat K^\sharp, \mat M^\sharp)\) are, by lemma 10.57, \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat I\) (point 1) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat K^{-1}\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) (point 2). The reduced eigenproblem of definition 10.58 is \(\mat I\vek a = \tilde\lambda\,\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\), that is \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \tilde\lambda^{-1}\vek a\), so the Ritz values are exactly \(1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\). In the figure these are the values that the inversion maps from the \(\theta\) axis to the \(\lambda\) axis.
  11. By theorem 10.59, therefore, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \ge \lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\). In the figure this is the step to the right from each eigenvalue to the Ritz value.

Source: CK sections 15.5.1 and 15.5.2 (PDF pp. 507 to 511, equations 15.38 to 15.46 and table 15.4): the three-term recurrence for a \(\mat B\)-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspaces of a \(\mat B\)-symmetric matrix, the tridiagonal matrix \(\mat T\) and the Ritz approximation from it. CK writes a general matrix \(\mat B\), we write \(\mat B = \mat M\) and \(\mat A = \mat K^{-1}\mat M\). The proof is written out by us (CK derives the steps with inner products and does not repeat them), and point 3 and the use of theorem 10.59 in point 4 are not from the source.

The theorem rests on three assumptions, and each has its price. The first: \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1} > 0\), because we divide by it. It fails when the remainder is \(\vek z_{\mathrm{j}} = \vek 0\), that is, when \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) already lies in the space of the previous vectors. On the frame this happens after the third step, because there is no fourth perpendicular direction, and it happens earlier if the start is made of fewer modes: for \(\vek r = \vek\varphi_1\) we have \(\mat A\vek q_1 = \vek q_1/\lambda_1\) and \(\beta_2 = 0\) already in the first step. No harm is done, by point 3 the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) are then exact. But this start gives no new modes, and for the next ones we need a new starting column, \(\mat M\)-orthogonal to those we have. The second: \(\mat K\) is positive definite, so the structure is supported in such a way that it cannot move without deforming. For a free structure, such as an aircraft in flight, \(\mat K\) has zero eigenvalues, \(\mat K^{-1}\) does not exist and \(\mat A\) is not defined. There we take, in place of \(\mat K\), the matrix \(\mat K - \lambda_0\mat M\) with a negative shift \(\lambda_0\) from section 21.6.7, which is positive definite, and the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) are then approximations to \(1/(\lambda_{\mathrm{r}} - \lambda_0)\). The third: we compute without rounding. This assumption does not hold in a computer and its effect is the largest: on the cantilever with fifty elements the deviation from orthonormality is already \(0{,}50\) after six steps and the first frequency is doubled at \(m = 7\) (section 21.8.5).

The theorem says that for each step \(j\) we have one product with \(\mat M\), one solution with the factorization \(\mat L\mat D\mat L^\T\) and two inner products, while the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is tridiagonal and its eigenvalues are cheap (the order \(m\) is small, a tridiagonal matrix has \(3m-2\) numbers). All \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) must be stored as well, because we need them to assemble the Ritz shapes \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\).

The letter \(\alpha\) is already in the book the coefficient of the penalty method of definition 12.27 and the thermal expansion coefficient, here it carries the index \(j\) and means a diagonal entry of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\). The matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is not the column of generalized forces \(\vek Q(t)\) of chapter 10: it has the index \(m\) and \(m\) columns that do not depend on time.

A small \(\beta_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) means that \(\mat A\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) is already almost in the space of the previous vectors, and this is a sign that the Krylov subspace is almost saturated.

21.8.3 No worse than inverse iteration

The Ritz values from \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) are upper bounds by theorem 21.37. How good are they? We compare them with two other procedures on the same space: with the Ritz approximation of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) on the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) (definition 10.58) and with inverse iteration from section 21.6, which from the start \(\vek r\) gives after \(m-1\) steps \(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}-1} = \mat A^{m-1}\vek r\) and from it a Rayleigh quotient.

First a view that says why the estimate \(1/\theta\) is better than the Rayleigh quotient, on the frame with three floors and the shape \(\vek x_0 = (1, 1, 1)^\T\).

On the axis we mark the three eigenvalues of the frame, \(\lambda_1 = 0{,}198\), \(\lambda_2 = 1{,}555\) and \(\lambda_3 = 3{,}247\). The axis is logarithmic, so that the lower end can be told apart. The shape \(\vek x_0\) is a sum of three modes, and the bars show what share \(c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum c^2\) belongs to each: \(0{,}914\), \(0{,}075\) and \(0{,}011\). The Rayleigh quotient is the weighted arithmetic mean of the eigenvalues with these shares, \(R(\vek x_0) = 0{,}914\cdot 0{,}198 + 0{,}075\cdot 1{,}555 + 0{,}011\cdot 3{,}247 = 1/3\). The small shares of the high modes pull it far to the right, because large numbers multiply them. The quotient \(1/\theta\) is the weighted harmonic mean with the same shares: we average the reciprocals \(1/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) and invert the result, \(1/\theta(\vek x_0) = 1/(0{,}914/0{,}198 + 0{,}075/1{,}555 + 0{,}011/3{,}247) = 3/14 = 0{,}214\). Here the high modes contribute small reciprocals and hardly move the mean, so it stays right next to \(\lambda_1\). The gap between the two means is the content of point 1 of the lemma below: the harmonic mean of positive numbers is never larger than the arithmetic one, and they are equal only when all the numbers with a nonzero share are equal, that is, when the shape is already a mode. Both means lie to the right of \(\lambda_1\), because no mean is smaller than the smallest number.

Lemma 21.38 The Lanczos estimate is no worse than the Ritz estimate and inverse iteration (Lanczosova ocena ni slabša od Ritzove in od inverzne iteracije)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A\), \(\vek r\), \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\), \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) be as in theorem 21.37, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} = 1/\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^{(m)}\) the Lanczos values and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) the Ritz values of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) with the basis \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\). For \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\) let \(\theta(\vek x) = \vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x/\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x\). Then: In the figure these are the Lanczos and Ritz value and the harmonic and arithmetic mean of the shape \(\vek x\).

  1. \(1/\theta(\vek x) \le R(\vek x)\) for every \(\vek x \ne \vek 0\), where \(R\) is the Rayleigh quotient (definition 10.41) of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\). In the figure this is the harmonic mean to the left of the arithmetic one.
  2. \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) for \(s = 1, \dots, m\). On bar C this is the Lanczos curve below the Ritz one.
  3. \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{m}-1})\) and \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\) for every \(k \le m-1\), where \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}} = \mat A^k\vek r\). In the figure the Lanczos curve lies below the inverse iteration curve.
Proof

Point 1 is an inequality between the two quotients, point 2 follows from it by the minimax theorem, point 3 from point 2.

  1. Let \(\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) be the expansion in modes (corollary 10.22). Then \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), \(\vek x^\T\mat K\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\), and because \(\mat A\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}} = \vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\) also \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\). The squares \(c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\) are the bars in the figure.
  2. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for two sums, \(\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\big)^2 = \big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}}\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}}\,c_{\mathrm{s}}\cdot c_{\mathrm{s}}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}}\big)^2 \le \big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}}\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2\big)\big(\sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}\big)\), gives \(1/\theta(\vek x) = \sum c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum(c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}) \le \sum\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^2/\sum c_{\mathrm{s}}^2 = R(\vek x)\). This is point 1. In the figure this is the gap between the harmonic and the arithmetic mean.
  3. For \(\vek x = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\) we have \(\vek x^\T\mat M\vek x = \vek a^\T\vek a\) and \(\vek x^\T\mat M\mat A\vek x = \vek a^\T\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\) (theorem 21.37, points 1 and 2), so \(\vek a^\T\vek a/\vek a^\T\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = 1/\theta(\vek x)\). The matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) is symmetric and positive definite as a reduced matrix (lemma 10.57, step 10 of the proof of theorem 21.37), so the Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\mat I, \mat T_{\mathrm{m}})\) at \(\vek a\) equals \(1/\theta(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a)\). The Rayleigh quotient of the pair \((\tilde{\mat K}, \mat I)\) with \(\tilde{\mat K} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) at \(\vek a\) is \(R(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a)\). Because \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) has independent columns, the \(s\)-dimensional subspaces of the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) are exactly the images \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal S'\) of the \(s\)-dimensional subspaces \(\mathcal S'\) of the space of all columns \(\vek a\) with \(m\) entries. On the frame the plane \(\mathcal K_2\) is the image of the plane of all columns \(\vek a\) with two elements.
  4. By theorem 10.44, point 2, applied to the reduced pairs, \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L}\) is the smallest value, over all \(s\)-dimensional subspaces \(\mathcal S \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), of the largest value of \(1/\theta(\vek x)\) on \(\mathcal S\), and \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\) is the same with \(R(\vek x)\). By step 2, \(1/\theta \le R\) everywhere, so on every \(\mathcal S\) the largest value of the first function is less than or equal to the largest value of the second, and hence \(\tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm R}\). The inequality \(\lambda_{\mathrm{s}} \le \tilde\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm L}\) is point 4 of theorem 21.37. This is point 2. On bar C this is the order of the curves: the Lanczos curve lies below the Ritz curve.
  5. Point 3: inverse iteration (definition 21.26) divides each step by a number \(t_{\mathrm{k}+1}\), which does not change the Rayleigh quotient, so its \(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}}\) equals \(\mat A^k\vek r\) up to a factor. This vector is in \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{k}+1} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(k \le m-1\). By theorem 10.44, point 1, at \(s = 1\) we have \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R} = \min R\) on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), so \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R} \le R(\vek x_{\mathrm{k}})\), and by point 2, \(\tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm L} \le \tilde\lambda_1^{\mathrm R}\). The curve of inverse iteration therefore lies above both.

Source: The inequalities in the proof are not from the source. CK section 15.5.2 (PDF p. 511) writes the Ritz approximation from \(\mat T\), it does not compare it with the Ritz approximation of the pair \((\mat K, \mat M)\) or with inverse iteration. The comparison is ours.

The lemma says that the Lanczos value is never worse than the Ritz value on the same space and than the Rayleigh quotient of inverse iteration with the same number of columns. 21.8.5 What happens without reorthogonalization

The theorem holds for computation without rounding. What if we run the recurrence (21.49) in ordinary double precision, without any measures? The figure shows that the matter is not harmless.

The top panel shows how well the vectors \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) are orthonormal. The axes show \(m\) from \(0\) to \(40\) and the deviation from \(10^{-16}\) to \(1\). The orange curve is the computation without any measure: the deviation is \(6{,}7\cdot 10^{-16}\) at \(m = 1\), already \(2{,}3\cdot 10^{-10}\) at \(m = 3\), \(1{,}3\cdot 10^{-4}\) at \(m = 5\), \(0{,}50\) at \(m = 6\) and \(1{,}0\) at \(m = 20\). Vectors that should be perpendicular are no longer even approximately perpendicular. The blue curve is the computation with reorthogonalization: the deviation stays at \(10^{-15}\) throughout, at \(m = 40\) it is \(1{,}2\cdot 10^{-15}\). Reorthogonalization is the measure that CK gives (table 15.6, PDF p. 512): after each step we subtract from the new vector its projections on all previous \(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\), \(\vek z \leftarrow \vek z - \sum_{\mathrm{i} \le \mathrm{j}}(\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}^\T\mat M\vek z)\,\vek q_{\mathrm{i}}\). In our computation we do this twice in a row, so that only rounding remains. The cost in step \(j\) is \(j\) inner products and \(j\) additions of columns of length \(n\), done twice. It needs no extra memory, because we store all \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}}\) anyway (\(m\,n = 4000\) numbers at \(m = 40\) and \(n = 100\)).

The bottom panel shows what the loss of orthogonality does to the values. The axes show \(m\) and the number of eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) that agree with \(\omega_1\) to \(10^{-6}\). The blue curve (with reorthogonalization) is \(1\) throughout: one eigenvalue, as it should be. The orange curve (without it) is \(1\) up to \(m = 6\), jumps to \(2\) at \(m = 7\) and then grows: after \(m = 12\) steps it is \(3\), after \(m = 20\) it is \(5\), after \(m = 30\) it is \(7\), and after \(m = 40\) there are \(10\) copies of the same frequency. The second copy appears at \(m = 7\), when the deviation reaches \(0{,}61\) (at \(m = 6\) it is \(0{,}50\) and there is no copy yet). At \(m = 8\) the list of the lowest five is \(3{,}516\), \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), \(120{,}902\) (exact \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), \(120{,}902\), \(199{,}860\)): the true values are there, but the first is doubled. At \(m = 20\) the first five computed values are all \(3{,}516\), and the true \(\omega_2 = 22{,}034\) is not among them. Someone who counted the eigenvalues would conclude at \(m = 8\) that \(\omega_1\) is double, and at \(m = 40\) that it is tenfold.

Why does this happen? CK does not derive the reasons and only names the loss of orthogonality (PDF p. 507). We measure it: the deviation grows when the first frequency has already been computed to \(5{,}6\cdot 10^{-9}\), since then the rounding error in the direction \(\vek\varphi_1\) does not vanish from the computation, but multiplication by \(\mat A\) amplifies it as in inverse iteration, and the vector \(\vek q_{\mathrm{j}+1}\) again receives the first mode, which is already in the basis. This explanation is our conjecture and we do not prove it here. What we know for certain is only what the computation shows: without reorthogonalization the vectors are not \(\mat M\)-orthonormal (deviation up to \(1{,}0\)) and the copies of the Ritz values are spurious. Theorem 21.37 claims nothing about such a computation. The Ritz values from \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) in such a computation are still eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\), but not Ritz values of an \(\mat M\)-orthonormal basis, so point 4 of the theorem is not proved for them.

Let us add one more experiment, which reaches back into section 21.6. If we start with a vector from which we have removed the \(\vek\varphi_1\) part (\(|\vek\varphi_1^\T\mat M\vek r| = 1{,}6\cdot 10^{-16}\)), with reorthogonalization and \(m = 8\), we still find \(3{,}516\), \(22{,}034\), \(61{,}697\), with an error of \(5{,}6\cdot 10^{-9}\) in the first. In computation without rounding this would not happen: with \(c_1 = 0\) every \(\mat A^i\vek r = \sum_{\mathrm{s}} c_{\mathrm{s}}\theta_{\mathrm{s}}^i\vek\varphi_{\mathrm{s}}\) is without \(\vek\varphi_1\), so the Krylov subspace does not contain the first mode. The result shows that the remainder \(10^{-16}\) and the rounding of every step were amplified enough in eight steps for the method to find the first mode (we inferred the cause from the result, we did not prove it), just as inverse iteration with a start without the first mode found it in section 21.6. Reorthogonalization does not prevent this, because it removes only directions that are already in the basis, not new ones that rounding creates. In the figure the axes show \(j\) and the share of the first mode, which starts at \(10^{-16}\). In computation without rounding it would stay zero, but in the computation it grows until the procedure finds it after eight steps.

21.8.6 Starting at the static solution

So far we started at an arbitrary vector. If we compute the response to a known load, one choice of start is particularly useful: \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\). On bar C this is the static shape \(\vek v_0\), the start of all Krylov vectors.

COOK has such a start too (section 11.8, PDF pp. 412 to 414): the first Ritz vector is the static solution, normalized with the mass, and each next one arises from the static solution for the inertia force that the previous shape produces, that is, with the same multiplication by \(\mat K^{-1}\mat M\). By this description the span is the same as \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\), and we do not compare the orthogonalization equations of COOK here. COOK writes that the static correction is thus automatically in the basis and that the Ritz vector method »spans the spatial distribution of loading but approximates its frequency content«, which is the opposite of the modal method. On bar C the first vector is the static shape and the second is the static shape for the inertial force that it triggers.

Point 3 of the corollary below is a perpendicular projection, which we see in the figure. The space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) is a plane, the exact solution \(\vek u\) stands above it. The Galerkin solution \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the foot, the error \(\vek e\) stands at a right angle to the plane, as measured by the inner product with \(\mat G\). Every other \(\vek y\) in the plane is farther: \(\vek u - \vek y\) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the legs \(\vek e\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} - \vek y\). On the right \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) is contained in \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\) and the foot in the larger space is no farther.

Corollary 21.41 Load-dependent Ritz vectors (Ritzovi vektorji obremenitve)

Let \(\mat K\), \(\mat M\), \(\mat A\) be as before, \(\vek F \ne \vek 0\) a column of forces, \(\vek r = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\), \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) and \(\mat T_{\mathrm{m}}\) from theorem 21.37 with \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_{\mathrm{m}} > 0\), and \(\Omega \ge 0\) such that \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} - \Omega^2\mat I\) is an invertible matrix. Let \(\vek a\) be the solution of the reduced system

\[\big(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} - \Omega^2\mat I\big)\,\vek a = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F \tag{21.50}\]

and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a\). On bar C, \(\vek r\) is the static shape and \(\vek q_1\) its normalized shape. Then:

  1. \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F = \sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\;\vek q_1\) is in the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}(\mat A, \vek r)\). On bar C, \(\vek r\) is the first Krylov vector and has the shape of \(\vek q_1\).
  2. For \(\Omega = 0\), \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat K^{-1}\vek F\) for every \(m \ge 1\). At \(\Omega = 0\) the blue curve has no error.
  3. If \(\Omega^2 < \lambda_1\), then \(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M\) is a positive definite matrix and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}}\) is the one among all \(\vek y \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) at which the expression \((\vek u - \vek y)^\T(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)(\vek u - \vek y)\) has its smallest value, where \(\vek u = (\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)^{-1}\vek F\) is the exact solution. The error in this norm does not increase as \(m\) increases. The condition holds to the left of the first dashed line, where the blue curve is the Galerkin solution.
Proof

Point 1 follows from the definition, point 2 from point 1, point 3 from the Galerkin condition.

  1. By definition \(\vek q_1 = \vek r/\sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\) and \(\vek r = \vek v_0 \in \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) for \(m \ge 1\). This is point 1. On bar C, \(\vek r = \vek v_0\) has the same shape as \(\vek q_1\).
  2. For \(\Omega = 0\) we have \(\vek r = c\,\vek q_1\) with \(c = \sqrt{\vek r^\T\mat M\vek r}\), so \(\vek r = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(c\,\vek e_1)\) with \(\vek e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\). Hence \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(c\,\vek e_1) = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\vek r = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F\), and \(c\,\vek e_1\) solves (21.50). The solution is unique, because \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat K\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is positive definite (lemma 10.57), so \(\vek a = c\,\vek e_1\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} = c\,\vek q_1 = \vek r\). On bar C, \(\vek r\) and \(\vek q_1\) lie on the same curve and differ by the factor \(c\).
  3. By theorem 10.42, \(\vek v^\T(\mat K - \Omega^2\mat M)\vek v = (R(\vek v) - \Omega^2)\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v \ge (\lambda_1 - \Omega^2)\,\vek v^\T\mat M\vek v > 0\) for \(\vek v \ne \vek 0\). So \(\mat G = \mat K - \Omega^2\mat M\) is positive definite. The condition \(\Omega^2 < \lambda_1\) is the region to the left of the first dashed line in figure 21.73.
  4. Let \(\vek e = \vek u - \vek u_{\mathrm{m}}\). Since \(\mat G\vek u = \vek F\) and \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek a = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\vek F\) (equation (21.50), in which \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat M\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}} = \mat I\)), we have \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\vek e = \vek 0\). For every \(\vek y = \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\vek b\) we have \(\vek u - \vek y = \vek e + \mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b)\) and \(\vek e^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b) = 0\), so \((\vek u - \vek y)^\T\mat G(\vek u - \vek y) = \vek e^\T\mat G\vek e + (\vek a - \vek b)^\T\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}(\vek a - \vek b) \ge \vek e^\T\mat G\vek e\), with equality exactly for \(\vek b = \vek a\) (the matrix \(\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}^\T\mat G\mat Q_{\mathrm{m}}\) is positive definite, because \(\mat G\) is positive definite and the columns are independent). This is the right angle: \(\vek u - \vek y\) is the hypotenuse with the legs \(\vek e\) and \(\vek u_{\mathrm{m}} - \vek y\).
  5. Since \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}} \subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\), the smallest value on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}+1}\) is not larger than the smallest value on \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\). In the figure this is the space \(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{m}}\) inside a larger one, where the foot is no farther.

Source: COOK section 11.8 (PDF pp. 412 to 414): load-dependent Ritz vectors, the first is the static solution normalized with the mass, and the static correction is included automatically. The proof of points 2 and 3 is not from the source, point 3 is a standard property of the Galerkin approximation and is written out by us.

Point 2 is the same as for the mode-acceleration correction (theorem 21.4, point 2): at \(\Omega = 0\) the solution is exact, and in figure 21.73 the blue and the blackwhite curve have no error there. The difference is in the cost. The mode-acceleration correction needs the modes, the Krylov basis does not: since the start \(\mat K^{-1}\vek F\) contains the static shape already in the first column, \(m\) solutions with the already computed factorization are enough.

Figure 21.73 compares three solutions with two columns (two modes or two Lanczos vectors). The axes show \(\Omega/\omega_1\) from \(0\) to \(3\) and the error from \(10^{-6}\) to \(10^{2}\) %, only the spikes at the resonances reach above the last tick. The two dashed verticals are at \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\). The orange curve, the mode-displacement solution, is \(9{,}8\) % too small at \(\Omega = 0\) and \(7{,}7\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\), which is the known error of truncated modes from section 21.1. The blackwhite curve (mode acceleration) is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) and has an error of \(0{,}037\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\). The blue curve is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) as well and has an error of \(2\cdot 10^{-4}\) % at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\). At \(\Omega = 1{,}5\,\omega_1\) the blue one is at \(+2{,}2\) %, the blackwhite one at \(+1{,}08\) % and the orange one at \(+24\) %. At \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\) the blue one is at \(-44{,}2\) %, the blackwhite one at \(-5{,}9\) % and the orange one at \(-45{,}0\) %. Where \(\Omega\) is above \(\omega_1\), so that the condition of point 3 of the corollary no longer holds, the Krylov solution with two vectors is still close to the exact one at \(1{,}5\,\omega_1\) (\(2{,}2\) %), but much worse than the mode-acceleration correction at \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\).

Now the numbers for more vectors. The end errors at \(\Omega = 0{,}5\,\omega_1\) are \(-1{,}13\) %, \(-2\cdot 10^{-4}\) % and less than \(10^{-4}\) % with \(m = 1, 2, 3\) Lanczos vectors, and \(-15{,}1\) %, \(-7{,}7\) % and \(-5{,}1\) % with \(N = 1, 2, 3\) modes. Here \(\Omega^2 = 0{,}25\,\lambda_1\), the condition of point 3 holds, and the error falls with the second vector by more than a factor of \(10^3\).

What about near the second resonance? Take \(\Omega = 0{,}9\,\omega_2 = 2{,}71\,\omega_1\) (the same condition as in the example of section 21.1). The end errors with \(m = 1, 2, 3\) vectors are \(-142{,}60\) %, \(-57{,}17\) % and \(-4{,}86\) %. The cause lies in the Lanczos values of the same computation as in example 21.39: the error of \(\omega_2\) is still \(7{,}3\cdot 10^{-2}\) at \(m = 2\) and \(1{,}95\cdot 10^{-3}\) at \(m = 3\), so the second resonance of the model is shifted or not captured until we take at least three vectors. Corollary 21.41 thus does not claim that the error is small at every frequency. It claims that the solution is exact at \(\Omega = 0\) and the best in its subspace for \(\Omega^2 < \lambda_1\). The mode-acceleration correction with \(N = 2\) modes has the errors \(-5{,}9\) % and \(-1{,}7\) % at \(2{,}5\,\omega_1\) and \(2{,}9\,\omega_1\), the Krylov solution with \(m = 2\) has \(-44{,}2\) % and \(-79{,}8\) %: in the mode-acceleration correction the second resonance appears at its exact frequency, not at a Ritz frequency.

21.8.7 Other methods

For the choice of a solver for the eigenproblem CK (§15.1, PDF pp. 488 to 489) gives four guidelines after Bathe and Wilson and after Golub and Van Loan. First: Householder-QR-inverse iteration is the most efficient when we need all eigenpairs of a matrix that has a large bandwidth or is full. Second: the determinant search is very effective for the lowest eigenpairs of systems with a small bandwidth. Third: subspace iteration is very effective for the lowest eigenpairs of systems with a large bandwidth that are too large for fast memory. Fourth: the Lanczos method can be efficient for a few extremal eigenvalues of a large sparse problem. The QR method, Householder's method and Sturm sequences are only named in this book, because we do not need them here and they are not proved. We use the Lanczos method for a few lowest frequencies of a large structure with a sparse matrix: the cost is \(m\) solutions, \(m\) stored vectors of length \(n\) and the eigenproblem of a tridiagonal matrix of order \(m\). In the figure Householder, the determinant search, subspace iteration and the Lanczos method each have their own cell: the row says what the matrix is like, the column how many eigenpairs we want.

The Krylov basis is not only a way of finding natural frequencies. We started with the same idea as Guyan and Craig-Bampton reduction from sections 21.4 and 21.5: we choose a matrix \(\mat\Psi\) and reduce by definition 10.58. In Guyan reduction the columns are given by static shapes, in Craig-Bampton reduction also by the modes of the substructures, in Lanczos the columns are given by the load and the matrix \(\mat A\). For all of them theorem 10.59 holds with the same line of proof, and all are Ritz approximations. In the next section (21.9) we look at what happens to such an approximation when we add damping to it.
