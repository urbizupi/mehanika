Chapter 11 The boundary value problem of linear elasticity

11.12 The Airy function in polar coordinates

In section 11.10 we wrote the three stresses of the plane problem, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\), with one function \(\varphi(x, y)\) that satisfies \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\). For beams this form is suitable, because the edges are the lines \(x = \text{constant}\) and \(y = \text{constant}\). The tube of section 11.7, the ring of chapter 4 and a plate with a hole have a circle for an edge. The condition of a free edge on a circle is complicated in Cartesian coordinates, because the normal changes from point to point. In the coordinates \(r\), \(\theta\) it is simple: on the circle \(r = a\) we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) at every \(\theta\). So there are three questions. How are the stresses and the equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) written with \(r\) and \(\theta\)? Which functions solve the equation? And which of them may we use in a ring, that is, in a body that surrounds a hole?

Throughout this section the plane problem without body force holds, as in theorem 11.36: the stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y\).

11.12.1 Stresses and the equation in polar coordinates

Polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) are cylindrical coordinates without the coordinate \(z\) (lemma 11.15). The stresses in the local axes (krajevne osi) \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\vek e_\theta\) are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) (definition 11.19), the first index gives the normal of the face, as in chapter 2. The component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the normal stress on the face \(r = \text{constant}\), that is, on the circle. The component \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is the normal stress on the face \(\theta = \text{constant}\), that is, on the radius. The component \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) is the shear stress between them (figure 11.33, left).

How do the stresses from the same function \(\varphi\) read in these axes? In Cartesian axes \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2\) is the curvature of the surface \(z = \varphi(x, y)\) in the direction \(y\), that is, perpendicular to the normal of the face on which the stress acts. The same holds for \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\). The shear stress is \(-\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y\). In polar axes we expect the same: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) is the curvature along the circle and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) the curvature along the radius. The following lemma says this exactly, and its proof also shows why the rule holds in every rotated system.

Lemma 11.46 Airy stresses in polar coordinates (Airyjeve napetosti v polarnih koordinatah)

Let \(\varphi\) be a four times continuously differentiable function in a region of plane polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) (lemma 11.15), written as a function of the point (lemma 11.16, item 3), and let \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial y^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x^2\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}} = -\partial^2\varphi/\partial x\,\partial y\) be the stresses of a plane problem without body force. Then the local components of the stress are

\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial r} \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial\theta^2} , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= \frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial r^2} , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big(\frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\theta}\Big) , \end{aligned} \tag{11.59}\]

and the equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) reads

\[\Big(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\Big)\Big(\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial r^2} + \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial\theta^2}\Big) = 0 . \tag{11.60}\]
Proof

We must show that the stresses \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xy}}\) can be written with \(\varphi\) independently of the axes, and that their local components are computed from the derivatives of \(\varphi\) with respect to \(r\) and \(\theta\).

  1. Let \(\mat H\) be the matrix of second derivatives of \(\varphi\) (the Hessian matrix, Hessova matrika), \(H_{\mathrm{kl}} = \partial^2\varphi/\partial x_{\mathrm{k}}\,\partial x_{\mathrm{l}}\) with \(x_{\mathrm{x}} = x\), \(x_{\mathrm{y}} = y\). It is symmetric, because the second derivatives are continuous (theorem 1.125). The stress matrix is
    \[\ten\sigma = \begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{\mathrm{yy}} & -\varphi_{\mathrm{xy}} \\ -\varphi_{\mathrm{xy}} & \varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\end{bmatrix} = (\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varphi_{\mathrm{yy}})\,\mat I - \mat H = (\nabla^2\varphi)\,\mat I - \mat H ,\]
    which we check element by element: the element \(xx\) of the right-hand side is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} + \varphi_{\mathrm{yy}} - \varphi_{\mathrm{xx}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{yy}}\), the element \(yy\) is \(\varphi_{\mathrm{xx}}\) and the element \(xy\) is \(-\varphi_{\mathrm{xy}}\). The form contains no axes, so it holds in every orthonormal system.
  2. By definition 11.19 \(\sigma_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\), and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat I\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}} = \delta_{\mathrm{ij}}\), because the local basis is orthonormal. From step 1 it follows that
    \[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \nabla^2\varphi - H_{\mathrm{rr}} , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \nabla^2\varphi - H_{\theta\theta} , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -H_{\mathrm{r}\theta} ,\]
    where \(H_{\mathrm{ij}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mat H\,\vek e_{\mathrm{j}}\).
  3. The matrix \(\mat H\) is the matrix of derivatives of the field \(\vek v = \nabla\varphi\), \(H_{\mathrm{kl}} = \partial v_{\mathrm{k}}/\partial x_{\mathrm{l}}\), and the field \(\vek v\) is continuously differentiable. Its local components are, by lemma 11.16, item 1, \(v_{\mathrm{r}} = \partial\varphi/\partial r\) and \(v_\theta = (1/r)\,\partial\varphi/\partial\theta\). Because \(\mat H\) is symmetric, \(\mat H = \tfrac12(\mat H + \mat H^\T)\), and this is the strain matrix of the field \(\vek v\) if we take \(\vek v\) as a displacement field (definition 1.115). Corollary 11.21 (without derivatives with respect to \(z\)) therefore gives
    \[H_{\mathrm{rr}} = \frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial r^2} , \qquad H_{\theta\theta} = \frac1r\Big(v_{\mathrm{r}} + \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial\theta}\Big) = \frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial\theta^2} ,\]
    \[H_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac1r\,\frac{\partial v_{\mathrm{r}}}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial r} - \frac{v_\theta}{r}\Big) = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{2}{r}\,\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial r\,\partial\theta} - \frac{2}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\theta}\Big) = \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\Big(\frac1r\,\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\theta}\Big) ,\]
    in the last one by the rule for the derivative of a quotient \(\partial_{\mathrm{r}}(\varphi_\theta/r) = \varphi_{\mathrm{r}\theta}/r - \varphi_\theta/r^2\), and the second derivatives are interchanged by theorem 1.125.
  4. By corollary 11.18 (without derivatives with respect to \(z\)) \(\nabla^2\varphi = \varphi_{\mathrm{rr}} + \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/r + \varphi_{\theta\theta}/r^2\). Inserting into step 2: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/r + \varphi_{\theta\theta}/r^2\) (the term \(\varphi_{\mathrm{rr}}\) cancels), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \varphi_{\mathrm{rr}}\) (\(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/r + \varphi_{\theta\theta}/r^2\) cancels) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\partial_{\mathrm{r}}(\varphi_\theta/r)\). This is (11.59).
  5. The function \(\psi = \nabla^2\varphi\) is twice continuously differentiable, because \(\varphi\) is four times, and it is a function of the point. Corollary 11.18 applied to \(\psi\) gives \(\nabla^4\varphi = \nabla^2\psi = \psi_{\mathrm{rr}} + \psi_{\mathrm{r}}/r + \psi_{\theta\theta}/r^2\), which is (11.60).

Source: SADD (7.6.7) and (7.6.8) (PDF p. 161-162, crop (7.6.7) read). SADD writes the components \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(\sigma_\theta\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\), while here each has two indices, the first is the normal of the face. The derivation through \(\ten\sigma = (\nabla^2\varphi)\mat I - \mat H\) is ours, SADD refers to Exercise 3.3 and the chain rule.

Let us test the lemma on a field we already know. Uniform tension \(\sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} = \sigma_0\), everything else zero, has \(\varphi = \sigma_0 y^2/2\), since \(\varphi_{\mathrm{yy}} = \sigma_0\) and the other second derivatives are zero. With \(y = r\sin\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta = (1 - \cos 2\theta)/2\) (corollary 8.15) we get \(\varphi = (\sigma_0/4)\,r^2\,(1 - \cos 2\theta)\). The lemma gives

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\,(1 - \cos 2\theta) + \sigma_0\cos 2\theta = \sigma_0\cos^2\theta , \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\,(1 - \cos 2\theta) = \sigma_0\sin^2\theta , \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -\tfrac{\sigma_0}{2}\sin 2\theta .\]

So we obtained \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\) from \(\varphi_{\mathrm{r}}/r = (\sigma_0/2)(1 - \cos 2\theta)\) and \(\varphi_{\theta\theta}/r^2 = \sigma_0\cos 2\theta\), and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\) from \(\varphi_\theta/r = (\sigma_0/2)\,r\sin 2\theta\). Directly from definition 11.19, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\ten\sigma\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} = \sigma_0\cos^2\theta\), because \(\ten\sigma = \sigma_0\,\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}\vek e_{\mathrm{x}}^\T\) and \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\cdot\vek e_{\mathrm{x}} = \cos\theta\). Similarly \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \sigma_0\cos\theta\,(-\sin\theta)\). At \(\sigma_0 = 50\) MPa and \(\theta = 30^\circ\) we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 37{,}5\) MPa, \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 12{,}5\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = -21{,}65\) MPa, and the sum of the normal stresses is \(50\) MPa, as it must be (\(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta} = \sigma_{\mathrm{xx}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{yy}}\), the trace of a matrix does not change under a rotation of the axes). We will use the same field at a circular hole as the stress far from it. With a computer we also checked the lemma on three other functions (\(r^3\cos\theta\), \(r^4\cos 2\theta + r^2\sin 3\theta\) and \(r^2\ln r\cos\theta + r^{-1}\sin 2\theta\)): at the point with \(x = 0{,}7\) and \(y = -1{,}3\) the stresses from the rotation of the Cartesian stresses and from (11.59) agree to \(10^{-14}\).

The lemma says that in polar axes it is the same as in Cartesian ones: a normal stress is the curvature of the surface \(\varphi\) in the perpendicular direction, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = H_{\theta\theta}\) (curvature along the circle) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = H_{\mathrm{rr}}\) (curvature along the radius), and the shear stress is \(-H_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\). For the tube of section 11.7, \(\varphi\) is a function of \(r\) only and figure 11.33 (right) shows it: the slope of the curve divided by \(r\) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\), and its curvature is \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\).

11.12.2 Biharmonic functions in polar coordinates

A function is biharmonic (biharmonična) if \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\). Which functions of \(r\) and \(\theta\) are such? One way is to try functions in which \(r\) and \(\theta\) are separated. The second derivative of \(\cos n\theta\) with respect to \(\theta\) is \(-n^2\cos n\theta\), and the Laplace operator lowers the power \(r^k\) by \(2\). So we expect that the functions \(r^k\cos n\theta\) and \(r^k\sin n\theta\) have a simple Laplace operator and that the condition on \(k\) will be a polynomial. We take integer \(n\) so that the stresses are equal again after going around the angle \(2\pi\), since they are at the same point.

Lemma 11.47 Biharmonic functions in polar coordinates (biharmonične funkcije v polarnih koordinatah)

For \(r > 0\) the following functions are biharmonic.

  1. \(1\), \(\ln r\), \(r^2\) and \(r^2\ln r\) (\(n = 0\)). Every four times continuously differentiable biharmonic function that depends only on \(r\) is, on the interval \(r > 0\), a linear combination of them.
  2. \(r^3\), \(r^{-1}\), \(r\) and \(r\ln r\), each multiplied by \(\cos\theta\) or \(\sin\theta\) (\(n = 1\)).
  3. \(r^n\), \(r^{n+2}\), \(r^{-n}\) and \(r^{2-n}\), each multiplied by \(\cos n\theta\) or \(\sin n\theta\), for every integer \(n \ge 2\). For \(n = 2\) these are \(r^2\), \(r^4\), \(r^{-2}\) and \(1\), multiplied by \(\cos 2\theta\) or \(\sin 2\theta\).
Proof

We must show that \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) holds for the listed functions, and that there are no others among the functions of \(r\) alone. For the Laplace operator we use corollary 11.18 without derivatives with respect to \(z\).

  1. Let \(\varphi = r^k\cos n\theta\) with real \(k\) and integer \(n \ge 0\). By corollary 11.18 \(\nabla^2\varphi = \big(k(k-1) + k - n^2\big)\,r^{k-2}\cos n\theta = (k^2 - n^2)\,r^{k-2}\cos n\theta\), since the second derivative of \(\cos n\theta\) with respect to \(\theta\) is \(-n^2\cos n\theta\). The same computation with \(\sin n\theta\) gives the same product. Applying it again to the function \(r^{k-2}\cos n\theta\) gives
    \[\begin{gathered} \nabla^4\varphi = (k^2 - n^2)\,\big((k-2)^2 - n^2\big)\,r^{k-4}\cos n\theta \\ = (k - n)(k + n)(k - 2 - n)(k - 2 + n)\,r^{k-4}\cos n\theta . \end{gathered}\]
    This expression is zero for \(r > 0\) exactly when \(k \in \{n,\ -n,\ n + 2,\ 2 - n\}\). For \(n \ge 2\) these are four different values, which is item 3. For \(n = 1\) the values are \(1, -1, 3, 1\), so we get \(r\), \(r^{-1}\) and \(r^3\), and the value \(1\) is double. For \(n = 0\) the values are \(0, 0, 2, 2\), so we get \(1\) and \(r^2\), each twice.
  2. The double values give one function more each, which we check directly. For \(n = 0\): \(\nabla^2\ln r = \frac1r\,(r\cdot\frac1r)' = 0\), so \(\nabla^4\ln r = 0\). Next \(\nabla^2(r^2\ln r) = \frac1r\,\big(r\,(2r\ln r + r)\big)' = \frac1r\,(2r^2\ln r + r^2)' = 4\ln r + 4\), which is the sum of a harmonic function and a constant, so \(\nabla^4(r^2\ln r) = 0\). For \(n = 1\) and \(f(r)\cos\theta\), corollary 11.18 gives \(\nabla^2\big(f\cos\theta\big) = (f'' + f'/r - f/r^2)\cos\theta\). For \(f = r\ln r\) we have \(f' = \ln r + 1\), \(f'' = 1/r\) and the expression is \(\frac1r + \frac{\ln r + 1}{r} - \frac{\ln r}{r} = \frac2r\). Next \(\nabla^2(r\ln r\cos\theta) = \frac2r\cos\theta\), and for \(f = 2/r\) we have \(f' = -2/r^2\), \(f'' = 4/r^3\) and the expression \(\frac{4}{r^3} - \frac{2}{r^3} - \frac{2}{r^3} = 0\). So \(\nabla^4(r\ln r\cos\theta) = 0\), and the same with \(\sin\theta\). Thus the functions \(\ln r\), \(r^2\ln r\) and \(r\ln r\cos\theta\), \(r\ln r\sin\theta\) are added to those from step 1, which gives items 1 and 2. All twenty functions of items 1 to 3 at \(n \le 2\) were also checked with SymPy.
  3. Let \(\varphi\) be a function of \(r\) only with four continuous derivatives on an interval and \(\psi = \nabla^2\varphi\). By corollary 11.18 \(\psi = \varphi'' + \varphi'/r = \frac1r\,(r\varphi')'\), and \(\nabla^4\varphi = \frac1r\,(r\psi')'\). From \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) we get \((r\psi')' = 0\), so by corollary 1.85 \(r\psi' = c_1\) and \(\psi' = c_1/r\). Because \((\ln r)' = 1/r\) (definition 9.10), the difference \(\psi - c_1\ln r\) is constant, \(\psi = c_1\ln r + c_2\). Then \((r\varphi')' = r\psi = c_1\,r\ln r + c_2\,r\). Because \(\big(\tfrac{r^2}{2}\ln r - \tfrac{r^2}{4}\big)' = r\ln r\), by the same corollary \(r\varphi' = c_1\big(\tfrac{r^2}{2}\ln r - \tfrac{r^2}{4}\big) + \tfrac{c_2}{2}\,r^2 + c_3\), so \(\varphi' = c_1\big(\tfrac r2\ln r - \tfrac r4\big) + \tfrac{c_2}{2}\,r + \tfrac{c_3}{r}\). Because \(\big(\tfrac{r^2}{4}\ln r - \tfrac{r^2}{4}\big)' = \tfrac r2\ln r - \tfrac r4\), applying the corollary again gives
    \[\varphi = \frac{c_1}{4}\,r^2\ln r + \frac{c_2 - c_1}{4}\,r^2 + c_3\ln r + c_4 ,\]
    which is a linear combination of the four functions of item 1.

Source: SADD (8.3.1)-(8.3.7) (PDF p. 186-187, crop (8.3.6) read). The listed functions are terms of the general solution (8.3.6), which SADD credits to Michell. SADD adds terms with \(\theta\) as a factor (\(\theta\), \(\theta\ln r\), \(r\theta\cos\theta\), \(r\theta\sin\theta\) and others), which are not periodic and are not used in this chapter. BARBER §8.4 (PDF pp. 132-133) gives the same solution in a table. That Michell's list is complete, that is, that every periodic biharmonic function follows from it, we quote from SADD (PDF p. 187) and do not prove here. The proof of items 1 to 3 is ours.

The lemma tells us where to look. By it, Michell's series \(\varphi = \sum_{\mathrm{n}} f_{\mathrm{n}}(r)\cos n\theta + g_{\mathrm{n}}(r)\sin n\theta\) (\(f_{\mathrm{n}}\), \(g_{\mathrm{n}}\) suitable combinations of the listed powers) solves the equation at every \(n\) separately, and the boundary conditions on the circles give for every \(n\) only a few linear equations for the constants. For a circular hole in a plate we will see that \(n = 0\) and \(n = 2\) suffice, for a beam in the shape of an arc (section 11.14) \(n = 0\) and \(n = 1\). Now let us take the simplest case, \(n = 0\).

11.12.3 The axisymmetric stress function

Let an annulus (kolobar) be loaded equally around the whole circumference. We expect stresses that do not depend on \(\theta\), so \(\varphi = \varphi(r)\). By item 1 of lemma 11.47 such functions are combinations of \(1\), \(\ln r\), \(r^2\) and \(r^2\ln r\). A constant gives no stress, since all second derivatives are zero, so three remain.

Theorem 11.48 Axisymmetric Airy function (osnosimetrična Airyjeva funkcija)

Let the annulus \(a \le r \le b\) (\(0 < a < b\)) be made of an isotropic, homogeneous, linearly elastic material with \(E > 0\) and \(-1 < \nu < \tfrac12\) in a state of plane stress (definition 11.32), without body force, and let \(\varphi = A\ln r + B\,r^2\ln r + C\,r^2\) with real \(A\), \(B\), \(C\). Then:

  1. the stresses that \(\varphi\) gives by lemma 11.46 are
    \[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} &= \frac{A}{r^2} + B\,(1 + 2\ln r) + 2C , \\ \sigma_{\theta\theta} &= -\frac{A}{r^2} + B\,(3 + 2\ln r) + 2C , \\ \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} &= 0 , \end{aligned} \tag{11.61}\]
    and satisfy equilibrium without body force and \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\),
  2. every displacement field on the part of the annulus with \(\theta_0 < \theta < \theta_0 + 2\pi\) whose strains are \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (\sigma_{\theta\theta} - \nu\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})/E\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) has the local components
    \[\begin{aligned} u_{\mathrm{r}} &= \frac1E\Big[-\frac{(1+\nu)A}{r} + 2(1-\nu)B\,r\ln r \\ &\quad - (1+\nu)B\,r + 2(1-\nu)C\,r\Big] \\ &\quad + Q\cos\theta + P\sin\theta , \\ u_\theta &= \frac{4B\,r\,\theta}{E} - Q\sin\theta + P\cos\theta \\ &\quad + W\,r \end{aligned} \tag{11.62}\]
    with constants \(P\), \(Q\) and \(W\) that belong to a rigid motion (a displacement \((Q, P)\) and a rotation \(W\) about \(O\)),
  3. if \(B \ne 0\), a displacement field with the strains of item 2 does not exist on the whole annulus, because \(u_\theta\) changes by \(8\pi B\,r/E\) on going around the hole. If \(B = 0\), the stresses are Lamé's (theorem 11.27, item 1, at \(S = 0\)) with the constants \(A_{\mathrm{L}} = 2C\) and \(B_{\mathrm{L}} = -A\) of that theorem, and the displacements are those of that theorem up to a rigid motion, so they are unique.
Proof

We must show that the stresses satisfy equilibrium, that the displacements are obtained by integrating the strains, and that \(u_\theta\) is single-valued only at \(B = 0\). We start from lemma 11.46 and corollaries 11.26 and 11.21.

1. Stresses.

  1. For \(\varphi = \varphi(r)\) all derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) in (11.59) are zero, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = \varphi'/r\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = \varphi''\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\). From \(\varphi' = A/r + 2B\,r\ln r + B\,r + 2C\,r\) we get \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A/r^2 + 2B\ln r + B + 2C\), and from \(\varphi'' = -A/r^2 + 2B\ln r + 2B + B + 2C\) we get \(\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) of (11.61).
  2. The first equation of equilibrium in cylindrical coordinates (corollary 11.24, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\), without derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) and \(z\) and without body force) is \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\). By step 1, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' = \varphi''/r - \varphi'/r^2\) and \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = \varphi'/r^2 - \varphi''/r\), and the sum is zero. The second equation is \(0 = 0\) and the third holds because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0\). The equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) holds by item 1 of lemma 11.47.

2. Displacements.

  1. Hooke's law in local axes (corollary 11.26) at \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{rz}} = \sigma_{\theta \mathrm{z}} = 0\) gives the strains of item 2 of the theorem. The relation between strains and displacements is, by corollary 11.21 with \(u_{\mathrm{z}} = 0\) and nothing depending on \(z\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rr}} = \partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r\), \(\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = (u_{\mathrm{r}} + \partial u_\theta/\partial\theta)/r\) and \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac1r\,\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial\theta + \partial u_\theta/\partial r - u_\theta/r\big)\).
  2. The function \(U(r) = \frac1E\big[-(1+\nu)A/r + 2(1-\nu)B\,r\ln r - (1+\nu)B\,r + 2(1-\nu)C\,r\big]\) has the derivative \(U' = (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\), which we check by differentiating: the derivative of the first term is \((1+\nu)A/(E r^2)\), of the second \(2(1-\nu)B(\ln r + 1)/E\), of the third \(-(1+\nu)B/E\) and of the fourth \(2(1-\nu)C/E\), and the sum is \(\big[(1+\nu)A/r^2 + B\,(2(1-\nu)\ln r + 2(1-\nu) - (1+\nu)) + 2(1-\nu)C\big]/E\), and this is \((\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \nu\sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\) by (11.61). From \(\partial u_{\mathrm{r}}/\partial r = U'\) and corollary 1.85 on every radius we get \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = U(r) + f(\theta)\) with a function \(f\) of the angle.
  3. From the second strain, \(\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta = r\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} - u_{\mathrm{r}}\). By (11.61) \(r\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} = \frac1E\big[-(1+\nu)A/r + B\,r\,(3 + 2\ln r - \nu - 2\nu\ln r) + 2(1-\nu)C\,r\big]\). The difference \(r\varepsilon_{\theta\theta} - U\) has equal terms at \(A\) and \(C\), and at \(B\) it is \(B\,r\,\big[3 + 2\ln r - \nu - 2\nu\ln r - 2(1-\nu)\ln r + (1+\nu)\big]/E = 4B\,r/E\). So \(\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta = 4B\,r/E - f(\theta)\) and \(u_\theta = 4B\,r\,\theta/E - F(\theta) + g(r)\), where \(F' = f\) and \(g\) is a function of the radius.
  4. The third strain \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{r}\theta} = 0\) gives \(\frac1r f'(\theta) + \frac{4B\theta}{E} + g'(r) - \frac1r\big(\frac{4Br\theta}{E} - F(\theta) + g(r)\big) = 0\). The terms with \(\theta\) cancel. After multiplying by \(r\) what remains is \(f'(\theta) + F(\theta) = g(r) - r\,g'(r)\). The left side is a function of \(\theta\) only and the right side of \(r\) only, so both are constant (corollary 1.85 on each side with the other variable fixed), \(= c\). From \(g - r\,g' = c\) it follows that \((g/r)' = (r g' - g)/r^2 = -c/r^2\), so \(g/r = c/r + W\) and \(g = c + W\,r\). The function \(F\) has the second derivative \(F'' = f'\) and \(F'' + F = c\), so \(G = F - c\) satisfies \(G'' + G = 0\). The functions \(W_1 = G'\cos\theta + G\sin\theta\) and \(W_2 = G\cos\theta - G'\sin\theta\) have, by theorem 8.12, the derivatives \(W_1' = (G'' + G)\cos\theta = 0\) and \(W_2' = -(G'' + G)\sin\theta = 0\), so they are constant (corollary 1.85), say \(W_1 = Q\) and \(W_2 = -P\). From \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\) we get \(G = W_1\sin\theta + W_2\cos\theta = Q\sin\theta - P\cos\theta\). Then \(f = F' = Q\cos\theta + P\sin\theta\) and \(u_\theta = 4B\,r\,\theta/E - F + g = 4B\,r\,\theta/E - Q\sin\theta + P\cos\theta - c + c + W\,r\). This is (11.62). The field \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = Q\cos\theta + P\sin\theta\), \(u_\theta = -Q\sin\theta + P\cos\theta\) is the displacement \((Q, P)\) in local components, and \(u_\theta = W\,r\) is a rotation about \(O\) by \(W\).

3. Uniqueness.

  1. The displacement of the whole annulus is a function of the point, so at the point with the same \(r\) and \(\theta\) and at \(\theta + 2\pi\) it has the same value. The local components \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\), \(u_\theta\) then also return after going around, because \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) are functions of the point. All terms of (11.62) except \(4B\,r\,\theta/E\) are periodic in \(\theta\) with period \(2\pi\). This one changes by \(8\pi B\,r/E\) after going around, which for \(r > 0\) differs from zero if \(B \ne 0\). So for \(B \ne 0\) there is no displacement field on the whole annulus.
  2. For \(B = 0\) the stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A/r^2 + 2C\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -A/r^2 + 2C\), so the form \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A_{\mathrm{L}} - B_{\mathrm{L}}/r^2\), \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = A_{\mathrm{L}} + B_{\mathrm{L}}/r^2\) of theorem 11.27 with \(A_{\mathrm{L}} = 2C\) and \(B_{\mathrm{L}} = -A\). At \(B = 0\) we have \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac1E\big[-(1+\nu)A/r + 2(1-\nu)C\,r\big] + \dots\), which is \(u_{\mathrm{r}} = \frac{(1-\nu)A_{\mathrm{L}}}{E}\,r + \frac{(1+\nu)B_{\mathrm{L}}}{E}\,\frac1r\), the displacement of theorem 11.27 at \(S = 0\), and \(u_\theta = W r + P\cos\theta - Q\sin\theta\) is a rigid motion.

Source: SADD (8.3.7)-(8.3.9) (PDF p. 187-188, crops (8.3.7), (8.3.8), (8.3.9) read). SADD calls the constants of the function \(\varphi\) \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(a_3\) (here \(A\), \(C\), \(B\)) and the constants of the rigid motion \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) (here \(P\), \(Q\) and \(W\)), but because the letters \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are already taken here, we renamed them. On p. 188 SADD says that the term with \(a_3\) in \(u_\theta\) leads to multivalued displacements if a closed curve can be drawn around the origin inside the body. BARBER (PDF p. 132) says the same for the terms \(n = 0\) and \(n = 1\). The integration with the functions \(f\) and \(g\) and the solution for \(F\) are ours, and SymPy checked that \(u_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(u_\theta\) of (11.62) satisfy all three strain equations identically.

The theorem showed three things. The three constants \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) give an axisymmetric stress field that satisfies equilibrium and the equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\). Displacements are obtained only by integrating the strains, because the stresses alone do not say whether the ring has a displacement field. So single-valuedness of the displacement is a separate condition, as we already saw for the ring in chapter 4 (corollary 4.113, lemma 4.120): the compatibility equations are not enough for a body with a hole. And in a whole ring this condition removes the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\), while in a ring with a cut it leaves it.

From history

Michell investigated two-dimensional problems in a paper on the direct determination of stress in an elastic solid (On the direct determination of stress in an elastic solid), published in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society (BARBER, PDF p. 132, TIMO, PDF p. 292). BARBER attributes to him the general solution with stresses in the form of a Fourier series in \(\theta\) (definition 9.88) and calls it the Michell solution (BARBER, PDF p. 132). SADD says that Michell originally developed this solution in 1899 and that he restricts it to solutions periodic in \(\theta\), because then arbitrary boundary conditions can be treated with the Fourier method (SADD, PDF p. 187). LOVE, in the appendix on Volterra's theory of dislocations, says that Michell explicitly treated the possibility that the stress function is many-valued if the displacements are single-valued, and gives the year 1900 (LOVE, PDF p. 240). The years do not agree: 1899 (TIMO, BARBER, SADD) or 1900 (LOVE). Which year is right we cannot decide from these sources. LOVE justifies single-valued displacements like this: an actual displacement appears to be necessarily single-valued, but in a body that surrounds a hole, for example in a hollow cylinder, a many-valued displacement is also physically possible. From the cylinder we remove a thin slice between two axial planes, and the new surfaces are pressed together and joined. The body formed in this way is in an initial stress, which we obtain if the ratio of the displacement, resolved at right angles to the axial plane through the point, to the distance from the axis is many-valued with a cyclic constant, and we adjust the constant so that the corresponding points of the two axial planes coincide after the displacement (LOVE, PDF p. 240). The possibility of such an interpretation was, according to LOVE, first indicated by Weingarten in 1901, Timpe treated it in more detail for two-dimensional systems, among them the plane circular ring, and Volterra developed a more general theory in a series of notes and presented it comprehensively in 1907. Volterra calls the kind of deformations to which the theory applies "distorsioni", and Love says he ventured to call them dislocations (dislocations) (LOVE, PDF p. 240).

We asked what the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\) does in a ring. By the theorem it is absent in a whole ring, and the history tells how a many-valued displacement was given a physical meaning: a body from which a wedge is cut out and which is joined again. Let us now compute such rings, first a ring in which \(B = 0\).

Example 11.49 The tube from the Airy function (cev iz Airyjeve funkcije)

The tube of section 11.7 has inner radius \(a = 40\) mm, outer radius \(b = 60\) mm, pressure \(p = 30\) MPa in the bore, a free outer surface and free ends. This time we do not guess the displacements. We take \(\varphi = A\ln r + C\,r^2\), so \(B = 0\), because the tube surrounds a hole (theorem 11.48, item 3).

  1. On the circle \(r = a\) the outer normal of the body is \(\vek n = -\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\). The force per unit area there is \(-p\,\vek n = p\,\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and must equal \(\ten\sigma\,\vek n = -\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\vek e_{\mathrm{r}} - \sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}\vek e_\theta\), so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(a) = 0\). At \(r = b\) we have \(\vek n = \vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and the force is zero, so \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}\theta}(b) = 0\). The shear stress is zero everywhere by the theorem, two equations remain by the expression (11.61):
    \[\frac{A}{a^2} + 2C = -p , \qquad \frac{A}{b^2} + 2C = 0 .\]
  2. The difference of the equations is \(A\,(1/a^2 - 1/b^2) = -p\), so \(A = -p\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2) = -30 \cdot 1600 \cdot 3600/2000 = -86\,400\) MPa mm\(^2\). From the second equation \(2C = -A/b^2 = 86\,400/3600 = 24\) MPa, so \(C = 12\) MPa.
  3. The stresses are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = 24 - 86\,400/r^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 24 + 86\,400/r^2\) in MPa with \(r\) in mm. At \(r = 40\), \(50\), \(60\) mm we have \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = -30\), \(-10{,}56\), \(0\) MPa and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 78\), \(58{,}56\), \(48\) MPa. These are the same results as in theorem 11.27 with \(A_{\mathrm{L}} = 24\) MPa and \(B_{\mathrm{L}} = 86\,400\) MPa mm\(^2\).
  4. Because \(B = 0\), the displacements of item 3 of theorem 11.48 are Lamé's and single-valued. Because \(\sigma_{\mathrm{zz}} = 0\) at the free ends of the tube, the stresses are the same as in theorem 11.27 at \(S = 0\). There the field is shown to be an exact three-dimensional solution of the boundary problem, and by theorem 11.4 the only one up to a rigid motion.

Without the condition of single-valuedness the two boundary conditions do not determine the solution. For \(B \neq 0\) the same two conditions \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = -p\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\) would give different \(A\) and \(C\) for every \(B\). For \(B = 10\) MPa (in the expressions we write \(\ln(r/a)\) instead of \(\ln r\), which changes only \(C\), because \(r^2\ln r = r^2\ln(r/a) + r^2\ln a\)) we get \(A = -63\,045\) MPa mm\(^2\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = 68{,}81\), \(59{,}08\), \(55{,}03\) MPa at \(r = 40\), \(50\), \(60\) mm, instead of \(78\), \(58{,}56\), \(48\) MPa. This field too is in equilibrium and satisfies the boundary conditions, but by item 3 of the theorem it has a many-valued displacement, so it is not the field of a tube loaded by pressure alone. The choice \(B = 0\) is determined by the single-valuedness of the displacement, not by a boundary condition.

The stresses of Lamé's tube do not contain \(E\) and \(\nu\). This agrees with Michell's condition from section 11.10.4: for a region with a hole the stresses are independent of the moduli if the resultant of the forces on each edge is zero. For the tube the resultant of the pressure on each of the two circles is zero by symmetry, and in that section we only stated the condition, we did not prove it.

For a loading with forces that gives an axisymmetric field, \(B = 0\) therefore always holds in a ring that surrounds a hole. But what if the ring is not loaded by forces and instead we prescribe a jump of the displacement across a cut, like the ring with shortened fibres in chapter 4?

Example 11.50 A ring with a wedge removed (obroč z izrezanim klinom)

Take the ring of chapter 4: a thin steel ring with inner radius \(a = 40\) mm, outer radius \(b = 60\) mm, thickness \(2\) mm, modulus \(E = 196\) GPa and \(\nu = 0{,}3\). There we shortened all the fibres around the hole by \(k = 0{,}001\) and computed that the sides of the cut move apart by \(2\pi k\,r\), that is, by \(0{,}251\) mm at \(r = 40\) mm and \(0{,}377\) mm at \(r = 60\) mm (lemma 4.120). Now we do the opposite. From a ring without stress we cut out a wedge with the angle \(\alpha = 2\pi k = 0{,}006283\) (\(0{,}36^\circ\)) with its apex at \(O\). The wedge is \(\alpha r\) wide, that is, \(0{,}251\) mm and \(0{,}377\) mm at the edges, like the gap in chapter 4. We press the sides of the cut together and weld them (figure 11.34, left). What stresses remain in a ring that no external force loads? The ring is thin and lies freely in the plane, so we take plane stress.

  1. We treat the ring with \(\varphi = A\ln(r/a) + B\,r^2\ln(r/a) + C'\,r^2\) by item 3 of lemma 11.47 (the expression \(\ln(r/a) = \ln r - \ln a\) adds only a term \(r^2\ln a\), which is part of \(C\), and changes \(A\) by a \(\ln a\) multiple of a constant). The stresses by theorem 11.48 are \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} = A/r^2 + B\,(1 + 2\ln(r/a)) + 2C'\) and \(\sigma_{\theta\theta} = -A/r^2 + B\,(3 + 2\ln(r/a)) + 2C'\).
  2. Before welding the ring occupies the angles \(\alpha/2 \le \theta \le 2\pi - \alpha/2\). The point \(P^+\) on the radius \(r\) at \(\theta = \alpha/2\) and the point \(P^-\) on the same radius at \(\theta = 2\pi - \alpha/2\) are apart by \(2r\sin(\alpha/2) \approx \alpha r\) in the direction \(\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\), in which \(\vek e_\theta\) points on both sides to first order in \(\alpha\). After welding they must be at the same point, \(P^+ + \vek u^+ = P^- + \vek u^-\), so \(\vek u^- - \vek u^+ = \alpha r\,\vek e_{\mathrm{y}}\) must hold, and in local components \(u_\theta(2\pi - \alpha/2) - u_\theta(\alpha/2) = \alpha r\) (the computation is first order in \(\alpha\): we neglect the deviations of the bases \(\vek e_{\mathrm{r}}\) and \(\vek e_\theta\) between the two sides, which are of order \(\alpha = 0{,}006\), and the products of displacements with them, so the relative error is of order \(\alpha\), that is below \(1\,\%\)). By expression (11.62) the left side is \(4B\,r\,(2\pi - \alpha)/E \approx 8\pi B\,r/E\), since the rigid motion with \(P\), \(Q\) and \(W\) moves both sides equally. So \(B = E\alpha/(8\pi) = E k/4 = 49\) MPa. The jump of the displacement is thus \(2\pi k\,r\), as in chapter 4, only with the opposite sign: there the sides move apart, here we close them.
  3. The remaining constants are computed from the free surfaces, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(a) = 0\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}(b) = 0\):
    \[\frac{A}{a^2} + B + 2C' = 0 , \qquad \frac{A}{b^2} + B\,\big(1 + 2\ln\tfrac ba\big) + 2C' = 0 .\]
    The difference is \(A\,(1/a^2 - 1/b^2) = 2B\ln(b/a)\), so \(A = 2B\ln(b/a)\,a^2 b^2/(b^2 - a^2) = 114\,438\) MPa mm\(^2\), and \(2C' = -A/a^2 - B = -71{,}52 - 49 = -120{,}52\) MPa. The stresses in MPa at \(r\) in mm (figure 11.34, right):

The hoop stress is compressive at the inner edge and tensile at the outer one, and is zero at \(r = 49{,}33\) mm. The radial stress is compressive everywhere and at most \(3{,}99\) MPa in absolute value at \(r = 48{,}3\) mm, which a ring without a hole and without a wedge would not have.

  1. The resultant of the hoop stresses on a cross section is zero. From the first equation of equilibrium in cylindrical axes, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}' + (\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} - \sigma_{\theta\theta})/r = 0\), we get \((r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}})' = \sigma_{\theta\theta}\), so \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}r = \big[r\,\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}}\big]_{\mathrm{a}}^b = 0\) by the free surfaces. The moment of the hoop stresses about \(O\) is not zero: \(\int_{\mathrm{a}}^b\sigma_{\theta\theta}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r = 2599\) N mm per millimetre of thickness, for the thickness \(2\) mm it is \(5{,}198\) N m. Every cross section thus carries a moment without a force, compression at the inner and tension at the outer edge. We will meet the same form of \(\varphi\) in section 11.14, in a beam in the shape of an arc, where the moment is prescribed at the ends of the beam and the cut stays open.

The stresses are proportional to \(E\), because \(B = E\alpha/(8\pi)\) and \(A\) and \(C'\) are proportional to \(B\). They do not contain \(\nu\), because \(\nu\) appears neither in \(B\) nor in the equations for \(A\) and \(C'\): the datum is a jump of displacement, not a force. This is not in contradiction with Michell's independence of the moduli, which holds for a loading with forces with zero resultant on each edge. The term \(4B/E\) in \(\partial u_\theta/\partial\theta\) equals \(\alpha/(2\pi) = k\), that is, the relative shortening of the circumference by the wedge, \(\alpha r/(2\pi r)\). Because \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{zz}} = -\nu(\sigma_{\mathrm{rr}} + \sigma_{\theta\theta})/E\) here depends on \(r\) (from \(6{,}9\cdot10^{-5}\) at \(r = 40\) mm to \(-5{,}3\cdot10^{-5}\) at \(60\) mm), the model of plane stress does not satisfy all the compatibility equations in three dimensions (theorem 11.33), so the field holds as an average over the thickness of the thin ring (theorem 11.35).

The section gave the polar form of the Airy stresses (lemma 11.46), the powers and the logarithm that solve the equation \(\nabla^4\varphi = 0\) (lemma 11.47), and the axisymmetric case with the single-valuedness that removes the term \(B\,r^2\ln r\) in a whole ring (theorem 11.48). With it we computed the tube once more, this time from the stresses, and computed a ring with a wedge removed, in which the same term describes stresses without an external force. We saved the terms with \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\) of lemma 11.47: with \(n = 2\) we will solve in section 11.13 the plane problem with a round hole, and with \(n = 0\) and \(n = 1\) in section 11.14 a beam in the shape of an arc.
